# Joseph-Marie Jacquard (1752-1834): research report

*Compiled 2026-08-12 for the Atlas of Journeys. Queue brief: "the loom that put writing into
punched cards; mobbed by weavers, then a statue woven in silk."*

**Legend.** **[A]** attested, source named in the bracket · **[R]** reconstruction, tradition,
or a claim resting on a source later historians distrust. Contradictions are flagged and **not**
adjudicated; unverifiable things are written down as gaps.

**Two structural warnings.** The reachable literature is thin and circular: two Wikipedias, the
1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, and timeline summaries, all drawing on one nineteenth-century
French secondary layer. No archival document was read. And one name inside that layer, the
historian **Charles Ballot**, is flagged by English Wikipedia itself as unreliable and
rumor-repeating; Ballot is the origin of the most cinematic material in the life, the
enlistment with the son and the son shot down at his father's side. Anything traceable to him
is tagged [R] here even where an encyclopedia states it flatly.

## 1. Birth, family, name (1747-1765)

Born **Joseph Marie Charles**, **7 July 1752, Lyon**, into the silk trade; "Jacquard" was a
family nickname later adopted formally **[A: en.wiki, "Joseph Marie Jacquard"]**. It
distinguished one branch of a large Charles kindred at **Couzon-au-Mont-d'Or**, a Saone-side
village north of Lyon; the grandfather is recorded as **Bartholomew Charles dit Jacquard**
**[A: en.wiki]**. Couzon lived on limestone quarrying and the barge trade in cut stone
downriver **[A: fr.wiki, "Couzon-au-Mont-d'Or"]**.

**Parentage is a live disagreement.** en.wiki names the father **Jean Charles**, a master
weaver. fr.wiki names him **Jean-Charles Jacquard**, a master silk *manufacturer*, and adds a
mother, **Antoinette Rive**, a **reader of woven patterns** from **Saint-Maurice-de-Rotherens**
in Savoie **[A both; contradiction flagged]**. Both agree the household worked silk. Weaver
versus manufacturer is not trivial in a life about who owns looms and who works them, and is
left open.

Saint-Maurice-de-Rotherens was in the **Duchy of Savoy**, not France, in 1752; French
occupation began only in 1792 **[A: fr.wiki, commune article]**. The mother of the man France
would decorate was, at his birth, a foreign subject.

Sister **Clemence**, born **7 November 1747**, was among the few of nine children to reach
adulthood; the **mother died in 1762**; he was **illiterate until thirteen**, taught letters
afterward by his brother-in-law the printer-bookseller **Jean-Marie Barret** **[A: en.wiki]**.

**Gap.** No street, parish, or baptismal register for the birth in any source consulted.

## 2. Trades and inheritance (1765-1778)

Apprenticed first to a **bookbinder**, then to a **maker of printers' type** **[A: en.wiki;
trade list also fr.wiki]**; also recorded working a **quarry** **[A: fr.wiki]**. As a boy he
had helped at his father's loom and the work "proved too arduous", which is the whole of what
the record says **[R: en.wiki, that phrase and nothing more]**.

**Location contradiction, flagged.** fr.wiki puts the quarry work in the **Bugey**; en.wiki
locates the inherited quarry at **Couzon-au-Mont-d'Or**. These are different limestone regions
about 60 km apart, one on the Saone and one in Ain/Savoie. Both recorded, neither preferred;
the Couzon holding is the one tied to the inheritance.

**Anachronism flagged.** Croix-Rousse became the weavers' quarter only in the **early
nineteenth century**, when the new looms' tall frames drove the *canuts* uphill into
purpose-built high-ceilinged flats; a 1760s family loom stood in the older **Presqu'ile**
district **[A by inference from fr.wiki on the quarter's formation; the workshop's address is
a gap]**. Any account putting the child Jacquard at a Croix-Rousse loom has run the geography
backwards through his own invention.

**Father died 1772**, leaving house, looms, workshop, vineyard, quarry; he then dabbled
unsuccessfully in real estate **[A: en.wiki]**.

## 3. Marriage, debt, hardship (1778-1793)

**26 July 1778**: married **Claudine Boichon**, a Lyon widow with property and a dowry; the
marriage record lists him as **master weaver and silk merchant** **[A: en.wiki]**. Son **Jean
Marie** born **19 April 1779** **[A for the date; the *place* is [R], unstated in the
sources]**.

A failed figure-weaving venture put him in debt; he was taken to court, sold the inheritance,
and used his wife's dowry to settle **[A: en.wiki]**. The **Oullins** house stayed in
**Claudine's name** and became the family's anchor for life **[A: en.wiki]**.

In hardship he reportedly burned lime in the **Bresse** while Claudine **plaited straw** in
Lyon **[R: 1911 Britannica via Wikisource; the kiln site unnamed]**. Blanqui, lecturing at the
Conservatoire in 1837, called him flatly **"un simple ouvrier en chapeaux de paille"** whose
youth passed in the most obscure condition **[A as printed text: Blanqui, *Cours d'economie
industrielle*, dix-septieme lecon, French Wikisource; the characterization is his, not a
document]**.

## 4. Revolution and the Rhine (1793-1797)

After the **1793** siege and suppression of the Lyon revolt he **joined the revolutionary army
with his son** **[A as stated by en.wiki; the underlying authority is Ballot, so [R] in the
journey]**, reportedly in the **Rhone-et-Loire battalion of the Army of the Rhine** under
**Pichegru** **[R: en.wiki citing Ballot]**. He **fought in the 1795 Rhine campaign** and his
son **was killed outside Heidelberg** **[A per en.wiki and the 1911 Britannica; [R] in
substance, both routing to Ballot]**.

**Gap.** No source places him at a named bastion at Lyon or a named crossing on the Rhine. The
Kehl pin is campaign theater, not a documented position, and is tagged as such.

He then returned to Lyon and **found factory work, developing loom ideas in his spare time**
through the later 1790s **[A: 1911 Britannica]**.

## 5. The inventions (1800-1804)

- **1800**: an improved **treadle loom**; it worked badly and found no market **[A: en.wiki]**.
- **1801**: exhibited at the **Exposition des produits de l'industrie francaise**, Paris,
  **bronze medal** **[A: fr.wiki]**. **Venue corrected:** the an IX edition was held in the
  **Cour carree du Louvre**, 229 exhibitors; the Champ-de-Mars hosted the **first** edition of
  1798 and the Invalides esplanade the 1806 edition **[A: fr.wiki, exposition article]**. A
  Champ-de-Mars pin for 1801 is about 4 km wrong.
- **1803**: a loom for **weaving fishing nets** **[A: en.wiki]**.
- **1803**: **summoned to Paris**, attached to the **Conservatoire des Arts et Metiers**, where
  a loom by **Jacques de Vaucanson** was stored **[A: en.wiki]**.
- **1804**: urged on by the fabric maker **Gabriel Dutillieu** he studied that loom; by 1805 he
  abandoned an earlier **paper-strip** mechanism and returned to a **punched-card chain
  descended from Falcon's design**, reaching the final form **[A: en.wiki]**.
- **1804**: mechanism **patented**, drawing on **Basile Bouchon (1725)** and **Jean-Baptiste
  Falcon (1728)** **[A: en.wiki, "Jacquard machine"]**.

**Date contradiction, flagged, unresolved.** fr.wiki dates the programmable card-controlled
loom to **1801**; other summaries date the patented mechanism to **1804**. Whether the machine
shown in the Cour carree already selected by cards is not settled by anything reachable. This
decides whether the invention preceded or followed his study of Vaucanson's machine.

**Vaucanson date corrected.** "1740" appears only in en.wiki's "Jacquard machine". fr.wiki
dates his automated looms **1745-1755**, and fr.wiki "Metier Jacquard" says "as early as 1745"
**[A: fr.wiki, both]**. The name carries the particle: **Jacques de Vaucanson**.

**Jacquard's own words**, from the 1809 *Notice*: the machine "is simple, costs virtually
nothing to maintain, and only requires being kept clean" **[A as text in English Wikiquote's
sourced entry; the French original was not located, a gap]**.

## 6. The Emperor and the city (1805-1806)

- **12 April 1805**: **Napoleon and Josephine** view the loom at Lyon **[A: en.wiki,
  fr.wiki]**.
- **15 April 1805**: the **patent is granted to the city of Lyon** **[A: en.wiki]**.
- **1805-1811**: **pension of 3,000 francs** for life plus **50 francs royalty per machine**
  **[A: en.wiki]**.
- **27 August 1805**: the **Academy of Lyon's inventor's prize** **[A: fr.wiki; [R] in the
  journey, one article only]**.
- **1806**: the government **declares the loom public property** **[A: 1911 Britannica]**.

**Contradiction, flagged.** A compressed reading of en.wiki collapses two events into
**15 April 1805**, the patent grant *and* the public-property declaration. The 1911 Britannica
dates public property to **1806**. Kept apart here.

**Second contradiction, flagged.** No source says where the 15 April act issued. The imperial
party was in **Lyon** on 12 April, so Lyon is the plausible reading; the journey pins it there
and carries the doubt in the confidence field.

Blanqui records a sentence attributed to Napoleon: **"C'est donc vous qui luttez avec le bon
Dieu, et pretendez faire des noeuds sur une corde tendue ?"** So it is you who wrestle with
God, and claim to make knots on a taut rope. **[A as printed in the 1837 lecture; the
attribution itself [R], unconfirmed beyond this retelling.]**

**Improvement by another hand.** **Jean-Antoine Breton** improved the mechanism in **1806** and
again in **1817** **[A: fr.wiki]**. en.wiki notes real sales growth followed **Breton's fix
after 1815**, and that the much-quoted **11,000 looms by 1812** is disputed by historians
**[A: en.wiki]**. The invention carrying one man's name sold once a second man had repaired it.

## 7. The machine broken: legend against record

The queue brief's hinge, and the least secure passage in the life.

**Supported.** Weavers **opposed** the loom **[A: 1911 Britannica, which records opposition and
no destruction]**. en.wiki says the 1840 statue stood "on the site where his **1801 exhibit
loom was destroyed**", a passive phrase with **no agent, no date, no place** **[A only as that
phrase]**. fr.wiki "Metier Jacquard" records **machine-breaking by canuts** with **no year**
**[A]**. The **1831 canut uprising** blamed automation for unemployment and is a distinct,
later, far better documented conflict **[A: fr.wiki]**.

**Not supported by anything read here:** an angry crowd, the year 1801, a Croix-Rousse location,
an attack on his person, or wood and iron thrown into the Rhone. All circulate freely; none was
confirmable.

**What one 1837 source does say**, closer to the event than any encyclopedia: **"on voulut le
lapider, et son metier fut brise en place publique par ordre des prud'hommes ; le bois et le
fer qui le composaient furent vendus comme materiaux de rebut"** **[A as printed text:
Blanqui, dix-septieme lecon]**. They wanted to stone him, and his loom was broken in the public
square **by order of the prud'hommes**, the guild court, wood and iron sold as scrap.

Note what that does to the popular story. It is not a spontaneous mob but a **judicial or
corporate act**, with the material auctioned afterward. The stoning is a wish ("on voulut"),
not an event. And there is **no river**: the metal was sold, not drowned. The Rhone detail, the
most retold image of the whole life, is unsupported in every source read here.

Blanqui's coda, same source, same tag: only later, when foreign competition began to be felt
and to become formidable, did the ungrateful Lyonnais come back from their unjust prejudice and
adopt the loom at last. Lyon accepted it when refusing it became expensive.

**Gap.** No sourced date for the destruction, no corroboration of the river, no primary
document. The web budget ran out before a dedicated search could be made, which is part of why
the gap stands.

## 8. Honours, retirement, death (1817-1834)

- **17 November 1819**: **Knight of the Legion of Honour** **[A: fr.wiki]**.
- **1826**: **municipal councilor of Oullins**, where he had retired **[A: fr.wiki]**.
- **1831**: the **canut uprising** **[A: fr.wiki]**.
- **7 August 1834**: **died at Oullins**, aged 82 **[A: en.wiki, fr.wiki]**.
- **Buried in the cemetery of Oullins**, tomb said to be still visible **[R: fr.wiki only;
  en.wiki silent on burial]**.

**Coordinate contradiction in the pool, flagged.** Two pairs circulate for the grave,
45.7157/4.8007 and 45.7147/4.8065, 460 m apart, neither from a cemetery record; the journey
uses the first and marks the pin approximate. **Gap:** plot and tomb description unverified.

## 9. Afterlife: silk, bronze, stone, cards

**The woven portrait.** **1839**, five years after his death: a **portrait woven in silk** on a
Jacquard loom, roughly **24,000 punched cards**, a surviving example in the **Science Museum,
London**, called "the most famous image in the early history of computing" **[A: en.wiki,
"Joseph Marie Jacquard" and "Jacquard loom"]**. The commissioning firm is commonly given
elsewhere as **Didier Petit et Cie**; that was **not** in the pages fetched, so it is a gap.

**Babbage.** A copy reached **Charles Babbage**, who cited the card-driven cloth as an
inspiration for the **Analytical Engine** **[A: en.wiki]**. *Passages from the Life of a
Philosopher* (1864) describes it: **"The portrait of Jacquard was, in fact, a sheet of woven
silk, framed and glazed, but looking so perfectly like an engraving, that it had been mistaken
for such by two members of the Royal Academy"** (ch. XI). Chapter VIII adds that the loom "is
capable of weaving any design which the imagination of man may conceive", and that the Engine
can be grasped by "those who are acquainted with the principles of the Jacquard loom" **[A:
Wikisource text, both chapters, read directly]**.

**Lovelace.** Note A to her translation of Menabrea (1843): **"We may say most aptly, that the
Analytical Engine weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and
leaves"** **[A: confirmed via the fourmilab reproduction and the Wikisource listing]**. The
sentence is Lovelace's own English, her added note, not translated Menabrea.

**The card after the loom.**

- **1886-1887**: the woven silk **Livre de Prieres**, card estimates **106,000 to 500,000**
  depending on source **[A as a range: en.wiki, "Jacquard loom"; the spread is the finding]**.
- **1890**: **Herman Hollerith** adapts the punched card to the **United States census**,
  launching the industry later dominated by IBM **[A: Jacquard-machine timeline summary]**.
- **1983**: the first successful **electronic** Jacquard machine debuts at **ITMA, Milan**, by
  **Bonas Textile Machinery NV** **[A: same summary]**. The cards last 179 years from the
  patent.

## Contradictions, collected and not adjudicated

1. Father's trade: master weaver (en.wiki) vs master silk manufacturer (fr.wiki).
2. Mother: named with occupation and origin by fr.wiki, absent from en.wiki.
3. Quarry region: Bugey (fr.wiki) vs Couzon-au-Mont-d'Or (en.wiki).
4. Card mechanism: 1801 (fr.wiki) vs the 1804 patent, which also decides whether the invention
   preceded the study of Vaucanson's loom.
5. Vaucanson's looms: 1740 (en.wiki) vs 1745-1755 (fr.wiki).
6. 15 April 1805: patent alone vs patent plus public property, against the Britannica's 1806.
7. Where that act issued: unstated everywhere; Lyon is inference.
8. Destruction of the machine: mob (popular) vs order of the prud'hommes (Blanqui) vs no
   destruction at all (1911 Britannica); year unfixed anywhere.
9. Statue relocation: 1898 vs an 1901 inauguration, two fr.wiki articles.
10. Statue site: destroyed-loom site (en.wiki) vs no such claim (fr.wiki).
11. Livre de Prieres cards: 106,000 to 500,000.
12. Oullins grave: two coordinate pairs 460 m apart inside the pool.
13. 11,000 looms by 1812: repeated everywhere, disputed per en.wiki.

## Gaps stated as gaps

No archival or primary document was read; everything rests on encyclopedias, the 1911
Britannica, Blanqui's 1837 lecture, Babbage's 1864 memoir, and Lovelace's 1843 note. Unknown:
the birth street and parish; the boyhood workshop's address, whose usual Croix-Rousse
attribution is anachronistic; the French original of the 1809 *Notice*; the Science Museum
object page for the woven portrait, whose fetch failed; Didier Petit et Cie as the 1839
commissioning firm; any date, agent, or river for the destruction of the machine; the lime kiln
site in the Bresse; Jacquard's position at the Lyon siege or on the Rhine, the pins there being
theater and not evidence.
