# Salomon Trismosin (fl. 1473, claimed): research report

*The name attached to the Splendor Solis and to a first-person alchemical travel narrative printed at Rorschach in 1598. Reputed teacher of Paracelsus. Compiled 2026-08-08 for the Atlas of Journeys.*

Legend: **[A]** = attested, source named. **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition, or inference. Contradictions between sources are flagged and left open; they are not adjudicated here.

A structural warning first. This subject differs in kind from the rest of the alchemical wing. Trithemius left charters and a grave; Paracelsus left a printed corpus and a tomb at Salzburg. Trismosin left a name on a title page. There is no birth record, no death record, no burial, no municipal or guild or university trace of a person so called **[R: absence across en.wikipedia "Salomon Trismosin", alchemywebsite.com, and the 1920 Kohn edition]**, and modern scholarship treats the name as a pseudonym **[A: en.wikipedia]**.

The journey therefore has two layers and the file keeps them apart. Layer one is what the autobiography says happened: well attested *as text*, unverifiable *as event*. Layer two is what the manuscripts and printings did: attested as event, unconnected to any biography. Between them sits the meeting with Paracelsus, which belongs to neither and is carried as legend.

---

## 1. The primary text, and which edition this rests on

Everything in layer one comes from one document: *Trismosin's Alchemical Wanderings and Adventures in Search of the Philosopher's Stone*, translated from the *Aureum Vellus* (Rorschach, 1598) and printed as an appendix in *Splendor Solis: Alchemical Treatises of Solomon Trismosin*, trans. and ed. J.K. (Julius Kohn), London: Kegan Paul, Trench, Trübner & Co., 1920, pp. 82-88 **[A: https://archive.org/stream/splendorsolisalc00tris/splendorsolisalc00tris_djvu.txt]**.

**Pagination corrections applied.** The gathering pass mis-cited five of the ten quotations; all ten were re-checked verbatim and the wording was exact in every case. The 1473 departure passage is p. 83, not 82. The Venice sale passage is entirely p. 83, not 82-83. The *Fortunatum* passage is p. 86, not 85; p. 85 ends "the test gave three Loth fine Gold." The final tincture passage straddles the break, pp. 87-88. The editor's "wrapped in mystery" sentence is p. 95, not 94, and stands under the running head "TRISMOSIN'S ALCHEMICAL PROCESS", not in an introduction and not in the Explanatory Notes, which begin overleaf.

The 1598 German original was not collated. The scan exists (https://archive.org/details/aureumvellusoder01tris) and the imprint was read for the printer's name, but no line of the German was checked against Kohn, an amateur translator of 1920 rather than a critical editor.

## 2. Layer one: the wanderings as the text tells them

**The miner Flocker.** As a young man the narrator meets a miner named Flocker who works alchemy in secret. Flocker fixes lead with a special sulphur, combines twenty loth of the prepared lead with one mark of pure silver, and half the result is gold; he dies without disclosing the artifice **[A as text: Kohn 1920, p. 82]**. **Correction applied:** the afterlife pass compressed this into "lead into gold". The text has lead *and* silver, and a partial transmutation. No place is named and none is inferable.

**The departure, 1473, and the road to Milan.** Eighteen months of travel, 200 florins of his own money, nothing found but particular processes of no great importance; then Laibach (Ljubljana), then Milan, where he comes to a monastery, hears lectures, and serves about a year **[A as text: Kohn 1920, p. 83]**. 1473 is the only year the narrative gives, and every later chronology hangs on it. The monastery is not named, and both pins are city-level placeholders.

**The counterfeit silver.** Itinerant in Italy, he falls in with an Italian tradesman and a German-speaking Jewish merchant passing English tin off as fine silver, and serves them fourteen weeks tending the fire. In Venice the merchant sells forty pounds of it to a Turkish merchant. Trismosin secretly takes six loth to a Latin-speaking goldsmith, who sends him to an assayer on Saint Mark's Place; on the cupel the metal fails and flies away in the fire **[A as text: Kohn 1920, pp. 83-84]**. **Fabrication removed:** the pass placed the sale "near the Rialto trading district" and pinned it there. The Rialto appears nowhere in this episode; the only location named is "Saint Marks' Place", and the pin is moved there.

**The college and the hospital.** Abandoning the trade for fear of prosecution, he asks the Rector of a Venice college for two daily meals and is sent on to a hospital for destitute foreigners, where the food is sumptuous **[A as text: Kohn 1920, p. 84]**. Neither institution is named. Some accounts identify the hospital with the German foundations near the Fondaco dei Tedeschi; the text does not, and that identification is **[R]**.

**Ponteleone and Tauler.** A returning assay-assistant recruits him to the laboratory of a Venetian nobleman at a mansion called Ponteleone, about six Italian miles outside Venice, whose chief chemist is a German named Tauler; wages, two crowns a week and board **[A as text: Kohn 1920, pp. 84-85]**. **Contradiction with Wikipedia, flagged:** that article compresses the sequence to "came to a college in Venice where he met a German alchemist named Tauler", fusing two separate episodes. The college and hospital come first and have nothing to do with Tauler, who appears later and outside the city. Tauler as the German who taught the fixing of mercury is confirmed; his placement in Venice proper is not. "Ponteleone" matches no surviving toponym; the pin is a terraferma placeholder, not a located site.

**The cinnabar, the name, the oath, the Sarlamethon.** Given a doctored cinnabar ore as a test, he fixes the mercury in two days and gets three loth of fine gold from nine; the nobleman calls him his Fortunatum, gives him twenty-nine crowns, and puts him on oath not to reveal the art. The same patron spends some 30,000 crowns on alchemical books and, separately, 6,000 for a Greek manuscript called *Sarlamethon*, whose tincture process Trismosin finishes in fifteen weeks, tinging three metals into fine gold **[A as text: Kohn 1920, pp. 85-86]**. **Correction applied:** the geography pass wrote the 6,000 as included in the 30,000. The text presents them as additive, roughly 36,000 in total. The *Sarlamethon* is unattested in anything else reachable; searched and not found.

**The drowning.** On the annual Marriage of the Sea the nobleman's pleasure ship is caught in a hurricane and he drowns with other Venetian lords; the laboratory is closed, the workers paid off, Tauler kept on **[A as text: Kohn 1920, p. 87]**. No Venetian civic record of such a storm was checked; the account stands on the autobiography alone. **Coordinate correction:** the pass pinned the ceremony mid-basin in the Bacino di San Marco, which is neither the departure point nor the ceremony site. The Bucintoro left from the Molo before the Doge's Palace and the ring was cast off San Nicolò al Lido. The file uses both pins.

**The Egyptian books, and the end.** He leaves Venice for "a still better place for my purpose", where Cabalistic and Magical books in the Egyptian language are entrusted to his care; he has them translated into Greek and then Latin, and there finds the Treasure of the Egyptians. He perfects the tincture, finds a red "as no scarlet can compare with", one part tinging 1,500 parts silver into gold, and the narrative closes with a verse signed with his name **[A as text: Kohn 1920, pp. 87-88]**. **Correction applied:** Wikipedia's summary and the chronology pass both had him travelling onward *to acquire* the books. The construction is passive; the books come to him, and no destination is named. Later commentary sometimes glosses this as Egypt, which the text does not say. The file pins only the point of departure. Everything after this in the traditional biography comes from other hands.

## 3. The legend: Constantinople, 1520, and Paracelsus

Tradition holds that Trismosin met Paracelsus in Constantinople in 1520 and instructed him in alchemy **[A that the tradition exists: en.wikipedia, "Salomon Trismosin", citing Patai 1994]**.

Two corrections to how the pool framed this, both flagged rather than resolved.

1. The pool asserted a "scholarly consensus" that the meeting is unverified. No source consulted establishes one. English Wikipedia asserts the meeting affirmatively and cites Patai for it. The atlas may doubt the claim; it must not describe its own doubt as the state of the field.
2. The pool said Paracelsus's article "names no meeting with Trismosin at all" and implied Constantinople is absent from it. Constantinople *is* there, among the destinations of the 1517-1524 travels **[A: en.wikipedia, "Paracelsus"]**. What is absent is Trismosin. The date is not anachronistic; the meeting is unattested outside Trismosin-adjacent literature.

The strongest position in the reachable literature goes further than doubt. Skinner, Prinke, Hedesan and Godwin (2019) treat Trismosin as a construct of Paracelsus's followers, which dissolves the meeting rather than questioning it **[R: known through the volume's description and Prinke's chapter title; not read]**. Within the corpus the rival-teacher problem is already on the board at Würzburg; see §8.

## 4. The Poysel error, corrected in three places

English Wikipedia states that Stephen Skinner identifies Trismosin with Ulrich Poysel, a teacher of Paracelsus. Three of the four gathering lenses carried it forward. It is wrong twice over.

- Skinner did not propose the identification. He co-authored *Splendor Solis: The World's Most Famous Alchemical Manuscript* (Watkins, 2019), in which Rafal T. Prinke's chapter treats "the reasons for the misattribution of Splendor Solis to Poysel and Trismosin" **[A: publisher's description of the contents]**. Wikipedia's sentence inverts its own citation.
- Poysel is not a documented teacher of Paracelsus. German Wikipedia dates Ulrich Poyssel's putative authorship to c. 1440, more than fifty years before Paracelsus was born on 10 November 1493 **[A: de.wikipedia]**. A teacher-pupil relation is chronologically impossible.
- Skinner is called "historian of esotericism" in the pool. He is an Australian author and publisher of esoteric works with a 2014 PhD in Classics from Newcastle **[A: en.wikipedia]**.

No Ulrich Poysel biographical page could be found in this pass. That gap stands.

## 5. Layer two: the Splendor Solis manuscripts

**Berlin, Kupferstichkabinett, MS 78 D 3.** The earliest known illuminated copy **[A: en.wikipedia, "Splendor Solis"]**. **Contradiction flagged, not resolved:** English Wikipedia gives Central German, 1532-1535; German Wikipedia's census gives "Nuremberg 1531", dates the oldest version 1531-1532, and calls the dialect Middle Low German. Year and dialect are both contested; the file carries 1531 with the disagreement in `date_confidence`.

**A false contradiction removed.** The geography pass flagged 1532-1535 against "circa 1582" as an unresolved conflict for the writer. It is not one. Those are two different manuscripts, both real: Berlin MS 78 D 3, and London, British Library, Harley MS 3469, made in Germany around 1582 **[A: en.wikipedia; British Library catalogue]**. Harley 3469 is the copy whose 22 plates are reproduced everywhere.

**Kassel, Paris, Nuremberg.** Universitätsbibliothek Kassel holds two copies, Ms. chem. 11 and Ms. chem. 21, its historic manuscripts kept at the Murhardsche Bibliothek on Brüder-Grimm-Platz **[A: de.wikipedia census]**; the pass's "not verified" caveat is dropped and the pin moved about 500 m southwest. Paris and Nuremberg copies are listed **[A: en.wikipedia]**, with about twenty known worldwide, but neither library nor shelfmark was confirmed. Those two pins, BnF Richelieu and the Germanisches Nationalmuseum, are plausible repositories and nothing more.

**The images.** 22 allegorical illuminations on vellum with gold-heightened borders in the manner of a book of hours, drawing on imagery associated with Dürer, Holbein, and Cranach **[A: en.wikipedia]**. **Correction applied:** the afterlife pass wrote that the 22 images are "arranged around seven flasks". The seven planetary flasks are a subset, a sequence inside the fourth treatise.

**Augsburg.** English Wikipedia states the original *Splendor Solis*, of seven chapters, appeared at Augsburg **[A only as an assertion of that article; the sentence has no citation]**. German scholarship contradicts it: a c. 1440 Middle Low German origin attributed to Poyssel, no Augsburg, seven *treatises* rather than chapters. The pool tagged this [A] and attached "circa 1530s", a decade appearing in no source. The file keeps the stop, downgrades it, and states both readings.

## 6. Layer two: the printings

- **Rorschach am Bodensee, 1598.** *Aureum vellus, oder, Guldin Schatz und Kunstkammer*, printed by Leonhard Straub **[A: the 1598 imprint, https://archive.org/details/aureumvellusoder01tris]**. **Correction applied:** English Wikipedia calls the *Aureum Vellus* a Latin collection and the pool repeated it twice. The book is German; only the title is Latin. **Contradiction flagged:** German Wikipedia gives 1599. The work appeared in parts across 1598-1599, so 1598 for the first is defensible and is what the file carries.
- **Paris, 1612.** *La Toyson d'or, ou la fleur des thresors*, translated by a figure identified only as "L. I." **[A: alchemywebsite.com; the 1612 imprint, https://archive.org/details/latoysondorovlaf00tris]**. **Correction:** the geography pass said no place is given for this translation. Paris is given.
- **England, 17th century.** *The Golden Fleece*, translated from the French by William Backhouse, surviving as Ashmole MS 1395 **[A: en.wikipedia]**. No translation date was found. The Ashmole manuscripts passed to the Bodleian when the Victorians separated Ashmole's library from the museum collection **[A: en.wikipedia, "Elias Ashmole"]**; the pass tagged that custody [R] as an inference from shelfmark convention, which understates it, and the file carries it as attested. One overstatement cut: the Ashmole collection is one named Bodleian collection among many.
- The *Aureum Vellus* helped popularise concepts from the earlier *Rosarium Philosophorum* **[A: en.wikipedia]**.

## 7. The afterlife of the name

**The 150 years, and the 1690s sighting.** J.K. reports from the preface of *Elucidarius Chymicus* (1617) that Trismosin lived by virtue of his medicine to one hundred and fifty, and that Schmieder believed his name was Pfeifer and the man a Saxon **[A that J.K. reports it: Kohn 1920, p. 95]**; the 1617 book was not fetched, so the claim is unverified at the primary layer. Franz Hartmann later claimed Trismosin was seen alive by a French traveller at the end of the seventeenth century, and that his real name was Pfeiffer **[R: via en.wikipedia; he cites nothing for either]**. **Contradiction, left standing as the tradition's own:** an adult traveller in 1473 seen alive in the 1690s is over two hundred years old, which the sources offer as evidence of the elixir rather than as a difficulty. Note the two spellings, and that Hartmann is late enough to have read Schmieder.

**J. Peter Södergård** judged the biographical tales to lend the texts an "aura of historicity" and little else **[A: via en.wikipedia]**. **The 20th-century revival:** the pool flagged the modern editions as unconfirmable, but three are nameable, Kohn 1920, the M. Moleiro facsimile of Harley 3469 in 987 numbered copies, and Watkins 2019 **[A: publisher and archive records]**. The hedge is dropped.

**No tomb.** Unlike Paracelsus at Salzburg and Trithemius at Würzburg, there is no grave, monument, or memorial anywhere **[R: absence across all reachable sources]**. The file makes this a stop rather than a silence.

## 9. Gaps stated as gaps

- Birth, death, burial. None. Not a lacuna in the record; an absence of record.
- The Flocker episode, the 1473 departure point, the Milan monastery, the Venice college, the hospital for foreigners. All unnamed in the text.
- "Ponteleone". Unmatched to any surviving toponym near Venice.
- The *Sarlamethon*. Unattested outside Kohn's text.
- The "still better place" of the Egyptian books. Unnamed; the file assigns it no independent pin.
- Ulrich Poysel. No biographical source found.
- Library and shelfmark for the Paris and Nuremberg *Splendor Solis* copies.
- The date of Backhouse's English translation.
- Any Venetian civic record of the nobleman's drowning.
- Any Paracelsian source, in Paracelsus's own hand, naming Trismosin.
