# Diogenes of Sinope: research report
*Compiled 2026-08-15 for `diogenes_of_sinope.journey.json`. Subject: Diogenes the Cynic, ho Kyon, "the Dog," c. 412/404 to 323 BCE. Sinope, Athens, Corinth, Crete.*

Legend: **[A]** = attested, source named in the entry · **[R]** = tradition, reconstruction, inference, or later legend · Contradictions are flagged and left standing, never adjudicated.

**The structural problem, stated once.** Everything here rests on one sink: Book 6 of **Diogenes Laertius**, *Lives and Opinions of Eminent Philosophers*, compiled in the third century CE, some six hundred years after the man died. **[A: standard consensus on the transmission]** Laertius is an anthologist: he quotes lost writers (Diocles, Eubulides, Menippus, Demetrius of Magnesia, Satyrus), prints incompatible versions side by side, and adjudicates almost nothing. The other load-bearing sources are later still, all around the second century CE: Plutarch, Dio Chrysostom, Lucian. **No contemporary of Diogenes records a single event of his life.** Every date is therefore soft, and each hedge sits in `date_confidence`, never in the prose.

## 1. Sinope: the banker's house, c. 412–355 BCE

**Birth.** Sinope, a Milesian colony on the south shore of the Black Sea, son of **Hicesias**, a *trapezites* (banker or money-changer) associated with the city's mint or holding office over its coinage. **[A: DL 6.20; Suda s.v. Diogenes]** Birth date disputed, c. 412 to c. 404 BCE **[R]**; the upbringing in letters, verse, athletics and horsemanship is inferred from the family's standing **[R]**.

**The coinage.** Debased or defaced coin was in fact issued at Sinope. **[A: numismatic evidence, via Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy]** The metal is **silver** (the eagle-on-dolphin drachms), not bronze; the issues date **c. 350–340 BCE**, with later dies carrying the magistrate name **ΙΚΕΣΙΟ / Hikesio**. **[A: numismatic dating, via Wikipedia "Diogenes," section "Sinope"]**

**⚠ How far the numismatics reach.** IEP: "the adulteration of Sinopean coinage is one event about which there is certainty." Wikipedia's survey: "modern scholarship remains divided" on whether the episode is historical or philosophical allegory. The coins corroborate that *a* scandal happened; they do not name a culprit. **Contradiction flagged; the file tags the claim [R].**

**⚠ The dating does not close.** Traditional exile c. 362–355 BCE against a numismatic anchor of c. 350–340 BCE, a decade or more later. Both cannot stand as written. The mint stop is pinned to the traditional bracket (c. 362 BCE) so the segment runs in narrative order; the numismatic anchor and the conflict are written into `date_confidence` on the mint and departure stops. **No reconciliation offered.**

**Who did it.** Three incompatible versions, none preferred by Laertius **[A: DL 6.20–21, citing Diocles and Eubulides]**: Hicesias alone debased the coin and died in prison; father and son together; the son acted and the father paid. Diogenes reportedly confessed his own guilt in a lost treatise, the ***Pordalos***. **[A as report: DL via IEP; text not extant, so unverifiable]**

**The oracle.** Apollo tells him *parakharattein to politikon nomisma*, to change the **political** currency. **[A: DL 6.20–21]** The qualifier *politikon* is what generates the ambiguity and must be kept. The Greek names the alternative shrine as **Delos**, Apollo's birthplace (τὴν Ἀπόλλωνος πατρίδα; Hicks: "to Delphi or to the Delian oracle"), **not** a shrine at Sinope. **[A: Greek text]** Modern readers take the oracle scene for a rationalizing legend built backward out of the pun. **[R]** *Nomisma* is coin and custom at once, and the Cynic tradition reads the whole biography out of that word. **[A: DL 6.71; Dio Chrysostom, Orations 8.4]**

**The departure.** Banished by decree, fleeing prosecution, or leaving voluntarily: the sources disagree, Laertius does not settle it, and "the exact date of Diogenes's departure from Sinope is uncertain." **[A that the dispute exists: DL 6.21, 6.49; Wikipedia survey]**

**Gap.** Nothing at all is known between the birth and the scandal: no teacher, no marriage, no office, no travel, roughly four decades absent.

## 2. Athens: the jar, c. 355–340 BCE

**Arrival.** No source narrates the crossing; the arrival is inferred from where the Athenian anecdotes begin, and modern datings run from the 360s to the mid-340s. **[R: DL 6.21–22, inferred route]**

**Antisthenes.** Tradition attaches him to Antisthenes, who drives him off with a stick and is told that no stick is hard enough to keep him away while there is anything worth hearing. **[A as anecdote: DL 6.21]**

**⚠ The master-pupil relation may be impossible.** Antisthenes is reported dead c. 366 BCE; Diogenes' arrival is placed anywhere from the 360s to c. 345. IEP: "this account has been met with suspicion, especially given the likely dates of Diogenes' arrival in Athens and Antisthenes' death." Some scholars think they never met. **[R]** DL 6.21 also gives **no location**; Cynosarges is merely where Antisthenes taught (DL 6.13), so the siting is inference on a scene that may not have happened. The pin is kept as a labelled placeholder.

**The mouse.** A mouse with no home, no fear of the dark and no craving settles his manner of life. **[A: DL 6.22]** DL gives no place and sets it *before* the jar. Placeholder pin.

**The pithos.** He lives in a large ceramic storage jar near the **Metroon**, sanctuary of the Mother of the Gods and repository of the state archive. **[A: DL 6.23, citing Diogenes' own letters; Lucian, Sale of Creeds 7]** "Tub" is a translation artifact, not a rival tradition. A boy breaks the jar and the Athenians supply another. **[A: DL 6.43]**

**The lamp.** He walks the Agora in daylight with a lit *lychnos*, an oil lamp, saying *anthropon zeto*, "I am looking for a man." **[A: DL 6.41]** The "**honest** man" of popular tradition is a later gloss with no warrant in the Greek, and the instrument is a lamp, not a lantern.

**The shameless acts.** Public masturbation in the marketplace, with the wish that hunger could be banished as easily by rubbing the belly. **[A: DL 6.46, 6.69]** Hicks at 6.69: "It was his habit to do everything in public, the works of Demeter and of Aphrodite alike." That exact wording is what the file carries.

**Other Athenian material.** Begging from statues to practise being refused **[A: DL 6.49]**; the runaway slave **Manes**, unpursued **[A: DL 6.55]**; the epithet ***kyon***, taken as a badge and giving *kynikos* to the school **[A: DL 6.33, 6.60–61]**; *kosmopolites*, the earliest recorded use in that sense **[A: DL 6.63]**.

**Plato.** Direct, documented rivalry, correctly cited: the **trampled carpets at Plato's own house**, while Plato entertained friends of Dionysius, not at the Academy ("thus I trample on the pride of Plato" / "with pride of your own, Diogenes") **[A: DL 6.26]**; "**a Socrates gone mad**" **[A: DL 6.54]**; the **plucked fowl** against "featherless biped," with the school's amendment "having broad nails" **[A: DL 6.40]**. The site of Plato's house is unknown, so the journey file uses the Academy coordinates and says in `date_confidence` that they stand in.

## 3. Festivals and armies, c. 340–330 BCE

**Seasonal pattern.** Winters at Athens, summers at Corinth. **[A: Dio Chrysostom, Oration 6.1–2, *Diogenes, or On Tyranny*]** Dio is the source; the pool had credited a Wikipedia summary of testimonia.

**Chaeronea.** Captured among Philip II's prisoners; asked who he is, answers "a spy upon your insatiableness"; Philip laughs and releases him. **[A as anecdote: DL 6.43]** DL does not date it, and the attachment to the battle of 338 is the later reader's. **[R]**

**Olympia.** The herald proclaims **Dioxippus** victor over men; Diogenes retorts that Dioxippus beats slaves while he beats men. **[A: DL 6.43]** DL gives **no Olympiad number** and does not say he "attended"; the 336 BCE date comes from Dioxippus of Athens' victory (Ol. 111, uncontested *akoniti* pankration), not from Laertius. The games fell at the full moon of the Elean sacred month, late summer, so the pool's "July 336" is dropped.

**Isthmia.** Remembered working the festival crowds on athletic vanity, nobility and kingship. **[R: Dio Chrysostom, Orations 8 and 9, composed 400+ years later in Diogenes' voice]** How much is Cynic oral tradition and how much Dio's rhetoric cannot be got out of the text. **Gap.**

**Rolling the jar.** With the city frantically preparing, he rolls his pithos up and down so as not to be the only idler. **[A: Lucian, *How to Write History* §3]** The pool cited Lucian's *Demonax*, a different work about a different philosopher; and in Lucian the threat is **Philip**, which points to **338 BCE**, not 336 and not Alexander.

**Alexander at the Craneum.** Newly made hegemon, Alexander finds him sunning himself and offers him anything; Diogenes asks him to stand out of his sunlight. Afterward: "Had I not been Alexander, I should have liked to be Diogenes." **[A as text: Plutarch, *Life of Alexander* 14, verified for the Isthmus assembly, "the suburb called the Cranium," "lying along in the sun," and the reply; also DL 6.32, 6.38, 6.60, 6.68]**

**⚠ The [A] tag on the event is unearned.** One source writing c. 430 years later; DL preserves rival versions; no contemporary of either man records the meeting. The pool called this "a documented interlock rather than a coincidental crossing" because Alexander's own journey file carries it, but **both sides of the exchange come from the one Plutarch passage**: one late source, not two independent records. The register keeps the scene; the whole hedge sits in `date_confidence`.

## 4. Piracy and sale, c. 330 BCE

**Capture.** Sailing toward **Aegina**, in one telling for a sea bath, taken by pirates under **Scirpalus**, or **Harpalus** depending on the manuscript. **[A: DL 6.74]**

**Sale.** Carried to **Crete** and exposed for sale; asked what he can do, answers "ruling men," points out a well-dressed Corinthian: "Sell me to this man; he needs a master." **[A: DL 6.74–75]** **Xeniades** buys him and is told he must be obeyed, since a physician or a steersman in slavery would still be obeyed. **[A: DL 6.29–30, 6.36, 6.74]**

**⚠ Three contradictions, all left standing.**
1. **Crete or Aegina.** DL 6.74 sends him to Crete for the sale; another strand keeps the sale at Aegina.
2. **The port is unnamed.** The pool offered two incompatible pins, 35.5138 / 24.018 (a Cretan harbor) and 35.2401 / 24.8093 (the island **centroid**, not a port). The file uses the first and says in `date_confidence` that it stands in for an unattested site.
3. **The episode may be fiction.** DL 6.74 sources it to **Menippus' *Sale of Diogenes***, a satire; Wikipedia's summary calls the story "probably apocryphal."

**The tutorship.** Charge of Xeniades' two sons: riding, archery, slinging, javelin, hunting, plain food and water, cropped hair, waiting on themselves, poetry and summaries of his teaching by heart. **[A: DL 6.30–31]** Freed or not, the sources disagree: DL has him grow old in the house and be buried by the sons, another strand frees him to the Craneum. **[A that both are transmitted: DL 6.31, 6.77–78] Not resolved.**

## 5. Corinth and death, c. 325–323 BCE

**The cup.** Seeing a child drink from its hands he throws his wooden cup away: "A child has beaten me in plainness of living." **[A: DL 6.37, trans. Hicks, verbatim confirmed]** The pool's flag on this quote as unverified is **lifted**; the same passage continues with the bowl thrown away after a boy eats lentils from hollowed bread. DL says "drinking out of his hands," not "cupped hands," and **names no place**: the Peirene siting is later tradition and painting **[R]**. The pool's lenses disagreed on whether the scene belongs to the Athens or the Corinth years; the file takes Corinth and says so in `date_confidence`.

**The writings.** A ***Republic***, tragedies, dialogues. **[A that the titles were transmitted: DL 6.80]** Nothing survives intact, authenticity was disputed in antiquity, and Satyrus gave the tragedies to **Philiscus of Aegina**, Diogenes' pupil. **[A: DL 6.73, 6.80]**

**Death.** At Corinth, c. 323 BCE, cause unadjudicated by Laertius **[A: DL 6.76–77]**: holding his breath; colic from a raw octopus; an infected dog bite; fever; old age, which modern scholarship favours (Roubineau, via secondary summary) **[R]**. Age at death **81** per Censorinus 15.2 against **about 90** in a tradition Laertius reports. **[A that both are transmitted] Left standing.**

**The synchronism.** Death on the same day as Alexander. **[A that the tradition exists: DL 6.79]** The attribution is to **Demetrius of Magnesia**, *On Men of the Same Name*, which the pool omitted, and Laertius reports it **flatly**, not skeptically; the skepticism is modern. **[R for the coincidence itself]**

**Burial.** He instructs that the body be thrown out unburied or into a ditch for the animals **[A: DL 6.79]**; a variant has him ask to be thrown into the **Ilissus** "that he might be useful to his brethren," a river at **Athens** though he dies at **Corinth**. **Incoherence noted, not resolved.** The Corinthians give him a public funeral and bury him **by the gate leading to the Isthmus**, with a pillar and a **dog of Parian marble** on it. **[A: DL 6.78]** The pool twice said "**western** gate"; the Isthmus and Craneion lie **east/northeast**, on the Kenchreai side. DL also specifies the Parian marble for the **dog**, not the pillar.

**⚠ Pausanias does not support the grave.** Pausanias 2.2.4 describes the Craneion (cypress grove, precinct of Bellerophon, temple of Aphrodite Melainis, tomb of Lais) and **does not mention Diogenes' grave at all**. The grave, pillar and dog rest solely on DL 6.78. Pausanias is retained only for the grove. Perseus and Theoi were unreachable during the verification pass, so this verdict rests on the standard content of 2.2.4 and should be re-checked directly.

## 6. Afterlife

**Bronze at Sinope.** His fellow-citizens raise bronze statues inscribed "Time makes even bronze grow old: but thy glory, Diogenes, all eternity will never destroy." **[A: DL 6.78]** The pool credited the epigram to **Philiscus of Aegina**; DL names **no author**, and Philiscus appears only as Diogenes' pupil and as Satyrus' candidate for the tragedies (6.73, 6.80). **Attribution dropped.**

**Sinop, 2006.** A statue by **Turan Bas**: Diogenes on a barrel, lamp in hand, dog at his feet; the barrel is the Latin mistranslation of the pithos. **[A: Sinop municipal monument records, via secondary summary]**

**Fiume, October 1920.** In D'Annunzio's occupied city, **Guido Keller** builds a tree-house, sleeps half-naked beside a trained eagle, walks the beach nude, and **names his way of living "the house of Diogenes."** **[A: `guido_keller.journey.json`, segments[4].stops[0]; the phrase is in that file's own text, direct naming, not inference]** A one-directional reception link, and it carries a stop.

## 7. Interlocks used and rejected

**Used.** **Alexander the Great**, met at the Craneum in 336 BCE; the pin is inherited byte-identical from `alexander.journey.json` (segments[0].stops[5]), **37.906 / 22.879**, and every Craneum stop reuses it rather than re-geocoding. **Plato**, documented rivalry across four passages. **Guido Keller**, who names Diogenes as his model.

**Rejected.** **Aristotle**: same city, same window, no meeting in any source or file, shared geography only. **Nietzsche**: his Leipzig prize essays are on **Diogenes Laertius**, the biographer, not the Cynic, and the lantern/madman resonance is stated in no file. **Hannibal** and **Gurdjieff**: both carry Crete pins (Gortyn 35.063 / 24.947; "the first stray bullet" 35.514 / 24.018, a near-twin of the coordinate used here by coincidence, not inheritance) with no tie to the sale. **The cynocephali cluster** (Ratramnus of Corbie, Ctesias, Saint Ahrakas): adjacent to *kyon* thematically, linked in no file. **Diogenes Laertius himself**: a different man of the same name, flagged rather than silently resolved, since a grep for "diogenes" across those files returns him throughout. The brief's canonical anchors (Kaaba, Temple Mount, Paris, Buenos Aires) have no connection here and none was manufactured.

**New pins, none inherited.** **Sinope**, **Aegina** and **Cynosarges** appear in no prior atlas file. The Sinope coordinates are a standard geocode, not a sourced ancient-city point: 42.0263 / 35.1552 for the ancient city, 42.0231 / 35.1531 for the modern waterfront statue.

**Coordinates corrected out of the pool.** Piraeus 37.942 / 23.6469 to **37.945 / 23.625**. Metroon 37.9755 / 23.7348 (Plaka, 1.1 km off) to **37.9755 / 23.7213**, used for every Agora stop. Isthmia 37.9219 / 22.9986 (800 m off) to **37.9159 / 22.9932**.

## 8. Open questions the record leaves open

1. A real currency crime, or an allegory read backward out of *parakharattein to nomisma*?
2. Why does the numismatic date (c. 350–340) fall a decade or more after the traditional exile (c. 362–355)?
3. Did Diogenes ever meet Antisthenes? Delphi or Delos? Crete or Aegina, and was there a sale at all?
4. Freed by Xeniades, or dead in his household?
5. Where is the grave? DL puts it by the Isthmus gate; no excavation has found it, and Pausanias, walking that road, does not mention it.
