# Moinuddin Chishti (Mu'in al-Din Chishti), c. 1143-1236: research report

*2026-08-09. Compiled for the Atlas of Journeys. Subject: Khwaja Mu'in al-Din Hasan Chishti, called Gharib Nawaz, benefactor of the poor. Born in Sistan, died at Ajmer in Rajasthan, where his tomb is one of the most visited pilgrimage sites in South Asia.*

**Legend.** **[A]** = attested in a named, reachable source. **[R]** = reconstruction, hagiographic tradition, or route inference. Contradictions are flagged and left standing; none is adjudicated here.

---

## The governing problem

There is no thirteenth-century record of this man: no chronicle entry, no letter, no administrative notice, no inscription made in his lifetime or the century after. Wikipedia, following P. M. Currie's *The Shrine and Cult of Mu'in al-Din Chishti of Ajmer* (OUP, 1989), states plainly that contemporary sources are silent on his ancestry and most of his biography, and that the now-standard hagiography derives from later compilations **[A]**. Currie's stated project is to separate the historical Mu'in al-Din from the legendary figure later chronicles built.

Everything below descends from two families of source, and the file turns on keeping them apart. **The encyclopedic tradition** (Wikipedia after Currie, Britannica) is thin, late and cautious, gives an early-thirteenth-century arrival in South Asia, and refuses most dates. **The devotional chronologies** (haqqfinder, chirageqalandariya, sufiwiki, and the Persian hagiographies behind them: Amir Khwurd's *Siyar al-Auliya*, 14th c.; Allah Diya Chishti's *Siyar al-Aqtab*, 1647) are dense, precise to the hijri year, and internally inconsistent. Where the two disagree, both are printed; the journey file carries the hedging in `date_confidence`, never in the campa prose.

**A tagging correction applied throughout.** The incoming pool carried several claims tagged [A] whose cited [A] source does not support the attached date. Three are downgraded: the Lahore meditation (Wikipedia gives no date), the arrival at Delhi and Ajmer (Wikipedia says early thirteenth century, and Iltutmish's reign begins 1211, not 1192), and the permanent settlement at Ajmer in 1192. An [A] tag on a date the [A] source does not give is a fabricated anchor.

## Phase 1: Sistan, the mill and the orchard (c. 1136/1143 - c. 1155)

**Birth.** Traditionally 1 February, in the village of Sanjar in Sistan, on what is now the Iran-Afghanistan border **[R]**. The year is contested: most modern encyclopedic sources give **1143** (536 AH) after Currie; some hagiographic chronologies give **530 AH / 1136** **[R, contradiction not resolved]**. Pin 31.0287, 61.5006, keyed to the modern Sistan district around Zabol; village-level precision is not available.

**Family.** Father Khwaja Ghiyath al-Din Hasan, mother Umm al-Wara, also called Bibi Mahe Noor; a household of local standing owning a mill and an orchard **[R, Wikipedia]**.

**The dervish.** At about nine he meets the wandering mystic **Ibrahim Qandozi**, who gives him a piece of bread **[R]**. A conversion topos, and the only account of his childhood that exists.

**The inheritance.** The father dies when the boy is fifteen or sixteen; he sells or gives away the mill and orchard and distributes the proceeds **[R]**. **Contradiction:** Wikipedia gives c. 1155, the devotional chronologies 544 AH / 1150. Not reconciled.

## Phase 2: Khorasan and the seminaries (c. 1156 - c. 1162)

**The migration.** Devotional sources have the family driven out of Sistan by raids on collapsing Seljuk authority **[R]**.

> **Correction applied.** The pool attributed these raids to "Tatars" under Sultan Ahmed Sanjar, wrong by a century. Ahmad Sanjar was defeated and captured in **1153 by Oghuz (Ghuzz) Turkmen**; the Mongol irruption into Khorasan is the 1220s.

**Bukhara and Samarkand.** He studies at seminaries in both **[A, Wikipedia, no dates]**. Popular tradition names his teachers **Maulana Husamuddin** and **Maulana Sharfuddin**, names appearing only in devotional chronologies and unverified against any academic source **[R]**.

**The tomb of Imam al-Bukhari.** He visits the grave of the hadith scholar during the Samarkand years **[R]**. *Coordinate corrected by ~35 km:* the pool gave 39.5766, 66.6835, open country southwest of Samarkand; the Memorial Complex of Imam al-Bukhari stands at **Hartang, 39.8150, 66.9445**, about 25 km north of the city.

**The tomb of al-Maturidi**, at Samarkand, 39.658, 66.975 **[R]**. Folded into the Samarkand stop in the journey file; the claim stands.

## Phase 3: Usman Harooni and the wandering years (c. 1163 - c. 1185)

**The master.** In the Nishapur district he meets **Khwaja Usman Harooni** and becomes his disciple **[R, Wikipedia and devotional sources]**.

**Contradiction, unresolved:** the discipleship is "roughly twenty years" in one strand and "two and a half years of intensive training" in another, both undated. The village of **Haroon** implied by the master's nisba is located by nobody; the journey file pins it at Nishapur and says so in `date_confidence`. That is an admission of ignorance, not a finding.

**The named encounters.** All hagiographic, none corroborated:

- **Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani** (d. 1166) at Baghdad, dated by one popular chronology to 551 AH / **1156** **[R]**. Wikipedia mentions the meeting without a date. The atlas already holds Jilani at Bab al-Azaj (33.3364, 44.4076); his own file flags his lineage claims as contested and contains no Chishti visit. This is a later order writing itself a receipt from an earlier one.
- **Najmuddin Kubra** (d. 1221), undated, location unspecified **[R]**.
- **Ohad Mahmud Al-Wahidi Ghaznavi**, in Syria, c. 555 AH / 1160 **[R, sufiwiki only]**. No meeting site is named; the pool used Damascus as a regional proxy. Dropped from the journey file rather than pinned on a guess.

**The itinerary.** Devotional route-lists give, outward, Tabriz, Astarabad, Bukhara, Kharqan, Samarkand, Merv (rendered "Memna") and Herat, and on a return leg Ghazni, Balkh, Astarabad and Rey; other lists add Kerman, Hamedan and Isfahan **[R, uncorroborated]**. "Memna" corresponds to no known toponym; identification with **Merv** is a guess, flagged in the stop's `date_confidence`.

> **Two coordinate corrections.** **Kharaqan, ~37 km off:** the pool gave 36.3, 55.0, suspiciously round and in empty desert; the Tomb of Abu al-Hassan Kharaqani is at **36.6338, 55.0693**, at Qaleh Now-e Kharaqan north of Shahrud. **Isfahan, label and pin disagreeing:** the stop was labelled "Jameh Mosque quarter" and pinned 32.6572, 51.6776, the bazaar area 1.6 km southwest; the mosque is at **32.6697, 51.6853**.

## Phase 4: The khirqa, the Hajj, and the command (c. 1185 - c. 1190)

**The parting.** At fifty-two he parts from Harooni in Baghdad; the master names him successor (sajjada nashin) and hands over relics said to have belonged to the Prophet **[R, sufiwiki]**.

**The Hajj.** Devotional chronology dates a pilgrimage to Mecca, with **Qutbuddin Bakhtiar Kaki**, to 583 AH / **1187** **[R]**. Canonical Kaaba pin inherited byte-identical: **21.4225, 39.8262**.

**The command.** At Medina, beside the Prophet's tomb or in a dream, he is instructed to go to Ajmer as the Prophet's representative **[R; Wikipedia references a dream leading him to India without a date]**. Nothing about it is documented, and everything after it follows from it.

## Phase 5: Into Hindustan (c. 1190 - c. 1211)

This is the most damaged stretch of the chronology, and the damage is worth stating precisely.

**Multan.** One chronology gives arrival on **10 Muharram 561 AH / 1165 CE**, with his cousin **Khwaja Syed Fakhr al-Din Gardezi Chishti** **[R]**. That sits twenty-six years before the same tradition's Ajmer settlement and twenty-two before its own date for the Hajj. No source repairs it.

**Lahore.** He meditates at the tomb-shrine of **Ali Hujwiri** (Data Ganj Bakhsh, d. c. 1072) before continuing east **[A for the event, Wikipedia; undated]**.

> **Correction: tag and date both wrong in the pool.** It carried this as [A] with the date 1192. Wikipedia mentions the meditation, attaches no date, and places his South Asian arrival in the early thirteenth century. Corrected to event [A], date undated.

**Delhi and Ajmer.**

> **Correction: internally contradictory in the pool.** It claimed arrival "contemporaneous with Iltutmish (r. 1211-1236)" under an [A] tag with the date field **1192**, nineteen years before that reign begins. Wikipedia says he reached Delhi *during* Iltutmish's reign. The 1192 belongs to the devotional [R] tradition, not to the [A] source.

**The two arrival traditions, not reconciled.** The devotional strand (sufiwiki, haqqfinder) gives Multan 1165, a first Ajmer arrival with forty followers camping above Ana Sagar in 587 AH / **1191**, and settlement in **1192** timed to Muhammad of Ghor's defeat of Prithviraj III at Tarain. The encyclopedic strand (Wikipedia after Currie) gives an early-thirteenth-century arrival in South Asia, no Ana Sagar episode, and Delhi during or after Iltutmish's reign, from 1211. The synchronism with Tarain is exactly the hagiographic elaboration Currie's project exists to strip out. Later chronicles go further and credit the saint with predicting or causing the conquest; that causal claim is **[R]** and is flagged as legend wherever it appears.

> **Coordinate note.** Ana Sagar lake centres on roughly **26.475, 74.625**, about 1.6 km west of the pool's pin. The Dargah pin, **26.4561, 74.6282**, is correct and is used for every Ajmer town stop.

## Phase 6: The Ajmer years (c. 1192/1211 - 1236)

**Marriages, in flat contradiction.**

**1194** (590 AH), to **Bibi Ummat-ullah**, with three children named Fakhruddin, Husamuddin and Bibi Hafiza Jamal **[R]**. **1209 or 1210**, to the daughter of **Saiyad Wajiuddin** **[A, Wikipedia citing Currie]**. **1223** (620 AH), a second marriage to **Bibi Asmat**, with a son Khwaja Ziauddin Abu Sa'id **[R]**. The 1194 and 1209 dates are not reconciled by any source that repeats either. Children counted across sources: three sons, **Abu Sa'id**, **Fakhr al-Din**, **Husam al-Din**, and one daughter, **Bibi Jamal** **[A]**.

**Comings and goings.** The devotional chronology lists a "third visit" to Ajmer in 602 AH / **1206** and a "fourth" in 610 AH / **1213**, and elsewhere sends him from Ajmer back to **Balkh** and toward Baghdad in 598 AH / **1200**, while simultaneously holding him permanently settled at Ajmer since 1192 **[R, internally inconsistent]**. The inconsistency is left visible in the journey file rather than smoothed.

**Delhi, 611 AH / 1214.** He stays at the khanqah of **Qutbuddin Bakhtiar Kaki** at Mehrauli and confers the khirqa on **Baba Fariduddin Ganjshakar** **[R]**. A second Delhi visit is listed for 621 AH / 1224 at the request of a local farmer, with no further detail **[R]**; omitted from the journey file for want of content.

**Taragarh.** A chillah, or forty-day retreat, on the hill above the town **[R]**. *Coordinate corrected by ~0.8 km:* Taragarh Fort is at **26.442154, 74.618288**; the pool's 26.4419, 74.6103 sits west of the hill.

**The successors [A]:** Qutbuddin Bakhtiar Kaki, d. **1235** at Delhi, predeceasing his master by a year; Fakhr al-Din, d. **1255** at Ajmer; Hamid al-Din Sufi Nagauri, d. **1274** at Nagaur.

**Death.** Standard modern date **15 March 1236** (6 Rajab 633 AH), at a traditionally cited age of 93, at Ajmer, buried on the site that becomes the Dargah **[A]**.

**Contradiction, six years wide.** One devotional chronology gives 6 Rajab **627 AH = 21 May 1230 CE**. Both traditions agree on the day of the Islamic month and on the town; only the Gregorian year and his age differ. *Phrasing corrected:* the pool rendered this as "21 May 627 AH (1230 CE)," conflating the Gregorian day with the hijri year. The conversion is sound; 627 AH began 20 November 1229.

## Phase 7: The shrine, 1236 to the present

Here the record becomes solid, and the paradox is exact: the least verifiable man in the corpus lies under one of its best documented buildings.

| Date | Event | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| 1332 | Muhammad bin Tughluq visits the tomb | [A, Wikipedia] |
| c. 1455 | Sultan **Mahmud** Khalji of Malwa expands the complex and raises the Buland Darwaza, c. 85 ft | [A] |
| 1532 | White marble dome over the tomb, date in gold lettering on the northern wall | [A] |
| 1562 | Akbar's first visit, coinciding with his marriage to Mariam-uz-Zamani | [A] |
| 1568 | Akbar's larger deg after Chittorgarh: seven-metal alloy, over six metres across, still cooked in | [A] |
| 1568, Jan 1570 | The two documented on-foot pilgrimages from Agra; the 1570 journey covers c. 370 km in sixteen stages | [A] |
| 1579, 1614 | Akbar rebuilds the sanctum; Jahangir donates the smaller deg | [A] |
| 1637 | Shah Jahan's marble Jami Masjid; Aurangzeb later adds the Sandali Masjid | [A] |
| 1911 | Nizam Gate, gift of Mir Osman Ali Khan of Hyderabad | [A] |
| 2024-11-27 | Ajmer civil court issues notice to the ASI on a Hindu Sena petition; Dargah Committee rejects it | [A] |

**Two attribution corrections applied.** The **Buland Darwaza donor**: the pool credited "Sultan Ghiyasuddin/Mahmud Khalji"; Wikipedia and the architectural sources credit **Mahmud Khalji of Malwa**, c. 1455, with Ghiyasuddin (r. 1469-1500) a minority variant, not an equal alternative. **Akbar's first visit**: the pool gave 1566; it was **1562**, coinciding with the Mariam-uz-Zamani marriage of 6 February. The pool also generalised the barefoot journeys into a pattern across all fourteen visits, where only 1568 and January 1570 are documented that way.

**Also [A]:** the annual Urs in Rajab, celebrated rather than mourned because the order holds he was reunited with God that day, drawing hundreds of thousands of pilgrims against roughly 20,000 daily visitors; **Jahanara Begum**'s devotional biography *Mu'nis al-Arwah*; A. R. Rahman's "Khwaja Mere Khwaja" for *Jodhaa Akbar* (2008).

**[R]:** the hereditary khadims and the tradition that a Brahmin family has supplied incense, sandalwood paste and ittar since the saint's own era; the post-1550 genealogy (*Siyar al-Arifin*) making him a descendant of Imam Musa al-Kazim, which modern scholarship calls historically untenable; the attribution to him of **Anis al-Arwah** (given by some editions to Usman Harooni instead) and **Dalil al-Arifin** (recorded elsewhere as a fourteenth-century disciple compilation).

## Quotations

All are hagiographic, from texts written between one and four centuries after the death, and are printed as the tradition gives them.

- "We have caught Rai Pithaura alive and have given him to the army of Islam." Amir Khwurd, *Siyaru'l-Auliya*, trans. in Currie (1989), pp. 29-30. Reported as his words on hearing of Prithviraj's capture. A second translation of the same utterance, from a different edition, reads "We have handed over Pithora alive to the army of Islam" (New Delhi 1985, pp. 111-12, quoted in S. R. Goel). The wordings disagree; neither has been checked against the Persian.
- "If God and His Prophet so will, it will not be long before I raze to the ground these idol temples." *Siyar al-Aqtab* (1647), in Currie. Attributed to him on seeing the temples around Ana Sagar, four centuries after his death.
- "The other miracle is that before his arrival the whole of Hindustan was submerged by unbelief and idol-worship..." *Siyaru'l-Auliya*, in Currie p. 30. The chronicler's praise-narrative, **not** the saint's words.
- "You are not truly of my religion if you depart in any way from my sunnat." *Siyar al-Aqtab*, in Currie. Framed as the Prophet's words to him in a vision, not his own.
- Three further *Siyar al-Aqtab* anecdotes, via Wikiquote citing Currie: the cry from inside the Prophet's tomb at Medina calling for Muinuddin; the unseen voice accepting each completed recitation of the Qur'an; and the opening of the anecdote licensing the second marriage, "Mu'in al-din had a second wife for the following reason: one night he saw the Holy Prophet in the flesh."
- **"Love all and hate none."** Ubiquitous on devotional and tourism sites, attributed to a final discourse. **[R]** and flagged: untraceable to *Anis al-Arwah*, *Siyar al-Auliya* or *Siyar al-Aqtab* in any translation fetched. Tradition, not attested text.

**Provenance caveat.** Every quotation above was seen with its citation on a fetched web page. Currie's book was not opened in this session; the page attributions are inherited, not confirmed.

## Corpus interlocks

Canonical pins inherited byte-identical: Jilani at Bab al-Azaj (33.3364, 44.4076), the tomb of Junayd (33.3344, 44.3747), the execution ground of al-Hallaj (33.3545, 44.3445), Nishapur (36.2064, 58.7958), the Kaaba (21.4225, 39.8262), Delhi (28.5244, 77.1855).

The Chishti silsila traces back through Junayd's sober school; Ibn Arabi prays at that grave in 1205, Baha ud-Din Walad's household passes in 1219, Attar stands at al-Hallaj's ground in 1179, and Abdelkader reaches the Jilani shrine in 1826, so the atlas already routes later Sufis through this cluster. Nishapur is Attar's lifelong home and Qushayri's city, and Qushayri's 1030-31 pilgrimage runs Nishapur, Rayy, Hamadan, Baghdad, Kufa, Medina, Mecca, the same string of stations the Chishti itinerary uses a century and a half later. The Kaaba pin is shared with Nizami's father (1189), Ibn Arabi (1202-1204) and Baha ud-Din Walad (spring 1220). Balkh is Ibrahim ibn Adham's city, abandoned by Baha ud-Din Walad's family in 1219. Ibn Battuta sits as qadi of Delhi in 1334, a resident witness to the order Moinuddin founded.

Slugs named in campa prose: `abd_al_qadir_al_jilani`, `junayd_al_baghdadi`, `hallaj`, `attar`, `abu_al_qasim_al_qushayri`, `baha_ud_din_walad`, `ibn_arabi`, `nizami`, `ibn_battuta`, `ibrahim_ibn_adham`.

## Gaps, stated as gaps

- **No contemporary source of any kind.** The one gap that is not a failure of searching.
- **Haroon**, Usman Harooni's home village: named by a nisba, located by nobody. The journey pin is Nishapur and is a placeholder.
- **"Memna"**: no known toponym. Merv is a guess.
- **The Syrian meeting** with al-Wahidi Ghaznavi: no site named anywhere; the pool's Damascus pin was a regional proxy and is dropped rather than carried as if located.
- **Currie's monograph**, and **Anis al-Arwah** and **Dalil al-Arifin**: none opened. Page attributions inherited; the authorship dispute stands open.
- **The length of the discipleship**: twenty years against two and a half, undated in every source.
