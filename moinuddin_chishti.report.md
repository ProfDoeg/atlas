# Moinuddin Chishti (Mu'in al-Din Chishti), c. 1143-1236 — research report

*2026-08-09. Compiled for the Atlas of Journeys. Subject: Khwaja Mu'in al-Din Hasan Chishti, called Gharib Nawaz, "benefactor of the poor"; born in Sistan, died at Ajmer in Rajasthan, where his tomb is today one of the most visited pilgrimage sites in South Asia.*

**Legend.** **[A]** = attested in a named source and cross-checked. **[R]** = reconstruction, hagiographic tradition, or route inference. Contradictions are flagged and left standing.

---

## The governing problem: there is no contemporary record

Every biographical statement below descends from hagiography written between one and five centuries after the man died. P. M. Currie, whose *The Shrine and Cult of Mu'in al-Din Chishti of Ajmer* (Oxford University Press, 1989) is the standard modern study, states the position without hedging:

> There are no reliable histories which refer to Mu'in al-din. The three contemporary historians do not mention him.

**[A — Currie, 1989]**

This single verdict governs how every other item in this file must be read. The earliest substantial source, Amir Khwurd's *Siyar al-Auliya*, is a fourteenth-century Chishti-order compendium written roughly a century after the death. The richest source, Allah Diya Chishti's *Siyar al-Aqtab*, is seventeenth century, some four hundred years after. Both are internal to the order they describe. Nothing survives from the thirteenth century itself: no chronicle entry, no letter, no administrative notice, no inscription. The dates in the journey file are therefore hedged in the `date_confidence` field rather than in the prose, per house convention, and the hedges are dense.

What *is* solidly attested is the afterlife. From the Mughal period onward the shrine at Ajmer is documented in imperial records, architecture, and endowment. The paradox is exact: the least verifiable man in this corpus sits at the center of one of its best-documented buildings.

## Phase 1: Sistan, the mill and the orchard (c. 1143-1160)

**Birth.** Wikipedia's headline date is **1 February 1143** in **Sistan** (Sijistan), eastern Persia **[A — Wikipedia, "Moinuddin Chishti"]**. The variant **1141** circulates widely and appears in the same article as a minority reading **[R]**. The hijri year cited in the tradition, **537 AH**, converts to 1142-43 CE, which supports 1143 and does not support 1141. *Corrected against the research pool, which carried 1141 as the anchor and 537 AH alongside it: those two are arithmetically incompatible.* Pin: 31.0287, 61.5033, which is Zabol, an acceptable proxy for the province.

**Rival birthplace.** A separate hagiographic strand places the birth at **Isfahan** rather than Sistan **[R — Currie notes the discrepancy; devotional literature carries it]**. A third variant names **Chisht** near Herat, presumably by back-formation from the order's name. Sistan is the better-attested claim. The three are not reconciled in any reachable source and are not reconciled here.

**Descent.** Later hagiographers, writing three centuries after the death, claim Husaynid descent from Imam Musa al-Kazim. Wikipedia records that the consensus of genealogists considers this **historically untenable** **[A — Wikipedia, on the genealogists' verdict]**. The descent itself is **[R]** and should never carry an attestation tag. *The research pool's geography lens asserted the Husaynid descent flatly under an [A] tag; that tag is withdrawn here.*

**The inheritance.** His father **Ghiyath al-Din** dies when the boy is about sixteen, leaving him a **grinding mill and an orchard** **[R — Wikipedia, traditional biography]**. He sells both and gives away the proceeds **[R]**.

**The dervish.** *Siyar al-Aqtab* (17th c.) tells of a wandering dervish, **Ibrahim Qandozi**, passing the orchard; the boy gives him grapes, the dervish gives him a piece of bread, and on eating it the material world falls away from him **[R — Siyar al-Aqtab, 17th c., repeated in modern hagiographic summaries]**. Conversion topos, not evidence.

**Chisht.** The order takes its name from the town of **Chisht** (Chishti Sharif), Herat province, roughly 95 miles east of Herat, where the Syrian mystic **Abu Ishaq Shami** taught around 930 CE before returning west to be buried at Jabal Qasioun in Damascus **[A — Wikipedia, "Chishti Order"]**. The order predates Moinuddin by two centuries; he founds its Indian branch, not the order. *Coordinate corrected: Chishti Sharif is at 34.3473, 63.7397, not the 34.3167, 63.9333 in the research pool, an error of about 18 km.*

## Phase 2: The seminaries of Transoxiana (c. 1160-1165)

He enrolls at seminaries in **Bukhara** and **Samarkand** **[R — Wikipedia, "Moinuddin Chishti"]**, studying Quran, hadith, and jurisprudence. **Balkh** is named alongside Samarkand in some accounts **[R — Wikipedia, "Chishti Order"]**. No dates attach to any of it.

**The al-Bukhari problem.** The research pool placed a visit to the shrine of the hadith scholar **Muhammad al-Bukhari** (d. 870) inside Bukhara, "one of the most venerated tomb-shrines of the city," at 39.6294, 64.5389. This is wrong and is corrected here. Al-Bukhari fled to **Khartank (Hartang)**, a village about 25 km from **Samarkand**, and died there on 1 September 870; the Imam Bukhari Mausoleum stands at Hartang, near 39.77, 66.85, some 200 km from the pool's pin **[A — Wikipedia, "Muhammad al-Bukhari"]**. The visit, if it happened, belongs to the Samarkand leg. The visit itself remains **[R]**.

The visit to the shrine of the theologian **Abu Mansur al-Maturidi** (d. 944) at Samarkand is **[R]** and correctly placed **[Wikipedia, "Moinuddin Chishti"]**.

## Phase 3: The master, and the years of wandering (c. 1165-1190)

**Usman Harooni.** In the **Nishapur** district he meets the Sufi **Khwaja Usman Harooni**, is initiated as his disciple, and accompanies him for **more than twenty years** **[R — Wikipedia, "Moinuddin Chishti"]**. The research pool tagged this [A]; nothing about it is attested outside order tradition, and it is downgraded here. Harooni's own hometown, **Harwan**, is named by his nisba but not independently located in any reachable source; the pool gave it the Nishapur coordinate, which is an admission of ignorance dressed as a pin. Kept, flagged.

**The saint-encounters.** Hagiography places him with a cluster of named masters. None is corroborated:

- **Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani** (d. 1166) at Baghdad **[R]**. *The research pool called this "chronologically difficult." It is not: Jilani died when Chishti, born 1143, would have been about twenty-three. The objection is not arithmetic but evidential, and is restated on that basis.* Note also that Jilani's present shrine complex at Bab al-Sheikh was commissioned by Suleiman the Magnificent in 1535, three centuries after any such meeting.
- **Najmuddin Kubra** (d. 1221) in Khwarazm **[R]**. *Pin corrected: Kubra died at Konye-Urgench (42.3417, 59.1500) during the Mongol assault, refusing to leave the city, not at the pool's 41.12, 60.63, about 180 km off.*
- **Abu Said Tabrizi** at Tabriz **[R]**.
- **Najib al-Din Abd al-Qahir al-Suhrawardi** and **Abd al-Wahid Ghaznawi**, locations unspecified **[R]**.

**The hajj and the command.** He performs the hajj at **Mecca**, and at **Medina**, tradition holds, receives the vision that sends him to India **[R — Siyar al-Aqtab; Akhbar al-Akhyar]**. The words placed in the Prophet's mouth are given below under Quotations, with the omission in the research pool restored.

## Phase 4: The road into Hindustan (c. 1190-1210)

Route reconstruction, not attestation: **Ghazni**, then **Multan**, then **Lahore** **[R — standard reconstruction of the Khorasan-to-Punjab road; the Lahore stop alone has source support]**.

**Lahore.** At the tomb of **Ali al-Hujwiri** (d. 1072), Data Ganj Bakhsh, he meditates before continuing east **[A — Wikipedia, "Data Darbar"; Wikipedia, "Moinuddin Chishti"]**. This is the firmest single geographic claim in the whole pre-Ajmer itinerary.

**Arrival in South Asia.** Wikipedia says only "the early thirteenth century," together with his cousin **Khwaja Syed Fakhr al-Din Gardezi Chishti** **[A — Wikipedia, "Moinuddin Chishti"]**. *The research pool supplied a hard date of 1200; the source does not. Flagged and removed.*

## Phase 5: Ajmer (c. 1192/1200-1236)

**The arrival-date contradiction, unresolved.** Two incompatible traditions:

1. Arrival **before 1192**, under the Chauhan king **Prithviraj III**, with the saint's presence spiritually preordaining the Muslim conquest **[R — shrine hagiography]**.
2. Arrival **after 1200**, once Ajmer is under Muslim rule **[R — modern reconstruction, per Currie]**.

Currie notes the dispute and does not settle it; neither does this file. The Ghurid annexation of Ajmer followed Prithviraj's defeat at the Second Battle of Tarain and his death in **1192**, with annexation dated **1193** **[A — Wikipedia, "Ajmer"]**. *The research pool gave the conquest as 1192; corrected to 1193 for the annexation.*

**The lake.** He camps by **Ana Sagar** at the city's edge **[R]**. *Pin corrected: Ana Sagar lies about 1.5-2 km northwest of the town center, near 26.4720, 74.6180, not at the pool's 26.4667, 74.6333, which is Ajmer town.*

**The contests.** Two legend cycles, both **[R]**, both from *Siyar al-Aqtab* or local tradition: the defeat of the yogi **Ajaipal**, and the submission of the **devs** of Ana Sagar, one of whom he renames **Shadi Dev**.

**Settlement.** He establishes a khanqah at Ajmer **[R]**, marries the daughter of **Saiyad Wajiuddin** in **1209 or 1210** **[R — traditional biography]**, and has three sons, **Abu Said**, **Fakhr al-Din**, and **Husam al-Din**, and a daughter, **Bibi Jamal** **[R]**.

**The langar.** *Siyar al-Aqtab* gives the origin legend of the communal kitchen still run at the dargah **[R]**.

**Death.** **15 March 1236**, at a reported age of ninety-three, corresponding to **6 Rajab 633 AH** **[A — Wikipedia, encyclopedic consensus]**. Buried where he taught.

## Phase 6: The successors

- **Qutbuddin Bakhtiar Kaki** (d. 1 December 1235), appointed successor, carries the teaching to Delhi; buried at Mehrauli **[A — Wikipedia, "Moinuddin Chishti"; "Qutbuddin Bakhtiar Kaki"]**. *Correction: the research pool placed the discipleship in Delhi. Wikipedia's Kaki article states he took the oath of allegiance at **Osh**, in Central Asia, when Chishti passed through on his way to Isfahan, and was subsequently deputed to India. The initiation and the Delhi ministry are two separate places and must not be fused. Note that Kaki predeceases his master by three months.*
- **Hamid al-Din Sufi Nagawri** (d. 1274) preaches at **Nagaur**, living as a vegetarian and keeping a cow **[R — Wikipedia, "Nagaur"]**.
- His son **Fakhr al-Din** (d. 1255) continues the order's work at Ajmer **[R]**.

Akbar visits the shrine **at least fourteen times** across his reign **[A — Wikipedia]**. ## Contradictions, flagged and not resolved

1. **Iltutmish's tomb visit, dated 1232.** Wikipedia's "Ajmer Sharif Dargah" article prints 1232. Iltutmish reigned June 1211 to 30 April 1236; Moinuddin Chishti died 15 March 1236. A visit to the **tomb** in 1232 predates the death by four years. Wikipedia's own Iltutmish article contains no mention of Ajmer, Chishti, or any Sufi tomb visit at all. The only window in which the visit could have occurred is **15 March to 30 April 1236**, six weeks. The research pool contained both the uncorrected 1232 claim under an [A] tag and, in its geography lens, a correct flagging of the impossibility. Both readings cannot stand. Recorded here as [R], with the 1232 date rejected and the six-week window offered as the only arithmetic that works. Not adjudicated further.

2. **Muhammad bin Tughluq's visit, dated 1332.** The claim rests on a single line in Wikipedia's Moinuddin Chishti article. Wikipedia's Muhammad bin Tughluq article documents his reign in detail with no reference to Ajmer or the shrine. Downgraded from [A] to [R]. His reign is **1325-1351**, not the 1324-1351 the research pool gave.

3. **The 1532 dome and the Rampur finial.** The research pool, in two separate lenses, fused these into one claim: a white marble dome built in 1532 and donated by Nawab Haider Ali Khan of Rampur. **Rampur State was not founded until 7 October 1774**, by Nawab Faizullah Khan, so no Nawab of Rampur existed in 1532; further, no ruler named Haider Ali Khan appears in Rampur's list at all, the nearest being Hamid Ali Khan (1889-1930). Wikipedia treats these as two events: the dome "was built in 1532," and it carries "a lotus and a crown of gold, donated by Rampur's Nawab Haider Ali Khan." Split here into a dated dome and an undated post-1774 finial. The donor's name is left as the source gives it and flagged as doubtful.

4. **The 2007 bombing: death toll, date, and sentences.** Wikipedia's dedicated "2007 Ajmer Dargah bombing" article gives **3 killed and 17 injured**; the figure of 7 on the dargah page is hedged early reporting. The convictions came on **8 March 2017** at the NIA special court in Jaipur, not 22 March. **Two** men were sentenced to life: Bhavesh Patel (fine Rs 5,000) and Devendra Gupta (fine Rs 10,000). **Sunil Joshi was convicted posthumously**; he had been shot dead at Godhra, Madhya Pradesh, in December 2007, weeks after the blast, and received no sentence. The research pool's "seven pilgrims killed, three men sentenced to life" is wrong on both counts.

5. **The urs flag.** Hoisted at the **Nizam Gate** by Bhilwara's Gori family, not at the Buland Darwaza as the research pool stated.

6. **Birth year.** 1143 against 1141, with 537 AH supporting the former. Recorded, not adjudicated.

7. **Birthplace.** Sistan against Isfahan, with Chisht as a third. Recorded, not adjudicated.

8. **Arrival at Ajmer.** Before 1192 against after 1200. Recorded, not adjudicated.

## Quotations

All quotations below are hagiographic, from sources written between one and four centuries after the death, and are given as the tradition gives them.

**The command to go to India**, words placed in the Prophet's mouth. Currie p. 73, translating *Siyar al-Aqtab*; the English is Simon Digby's, reproduced by permission (Currie p. 73 n.13). Full text:

> Mu'in al-din, you are the essence of my faith; but you must go to Hindustan. There there is a place called Ajmer... By the grace of your footsteps there once more shall Islam be made manifest, and the Kafirs be punished by God's wrath.

*The research pool carried this with "you" dropped from "but you must go" and with the final clause "and the Kafirs be punished by God's wrath" silently cut behind an ellipsis. The cut is material: it removes the punitive half of the commission. Restored.*

**On arriving at Ajmer**: "Praise be to God, may He be exalted, for I have gained possession of the property of my brother." *Siyar al-Aqtab*, in Currie.

**On the temples**: "If God and His Prophet so will, it will not be long before I raze to the ground these idol-temples." *Siyar al-Aqtab*, in Currie; compare Harry S. Neale, *Sufi Warrior Saints: Stories of Sufi Jihad from Muslim Hagiography* (I.B. Tauris, 2022). **Flag:** this text is used by Chishti-order hagiographers and by modern polemical compilations (Sita Ram Goel-associated anthologies among them) for opposed purposes. It is included because suppressing it would misrepresent the source; it is not treated as the saint's speech.

**The Ana Sagar contest**, Currie pp. 74-75, Digby translation, full text:

> So when the infidels grew weak and saw that they had no power to resist such a perfect companion of God, they had to abandon the fight, and were filled with regret. They went into their idol temples which were their places of worship.

*The research pool welded the first clause to "they went into their idol temples" across a sentence boundary with no ellipsis, deleting "they had to abandon the fight, and were filled with regret." Restored.*

**The dev**: "The dev who was their leader, when he saw the perfect beauty of the Khwaja, trembled from head to foot like a willow tree." *Siyar al-Aqtab*, in Currie.

**The renaming**, Currie p. 75: "I also bestow on you the name of Shadi Dev [Joyful Dev]." *The research pool read "[Joyful Deval]"; "Deval" is not in the source. The bracket is Digby's translator's gloss, so the error misreports the translator.*

**The langar**: "It is related that in the kitchen of Muinuddin so many meals were cooked every day that all the impoverished people of the whole city could eat their fill." *Siyar al-Aqtab*, in Currie.

**On Tarain**: "We have caught Rai Pithaura alive and have given him to the army of Islam." *Siyaru'l-Auliya* (Amir Khwurd, 14th c.), in Currie. Attributed to Chishti or his circle regarding the 1192 capture of Prithviraj Chauhan.

**Retrospect**: "The darkness of unbelief in this land was illumined by the light of Islam." *Siyaru'l-Auliya*, in Currie.

**Pious exemplar**: "For seventy years his ablution was not broken without him washing before prayer." *Siyar al-Aqtab*, in Currie.

## Gaps, stated as gaps

- **No contemporary source of any kind.** Not a chronicle line, not a document, not an inscription. Currie's verdict stands unchallenged in anything reachable.
- **The Persian treatises.** *Anis al-Arwah* and *Dalil al-Arifin* circulate under his name and are treated in devotional literature as his own writing. Modern historians of the order have questioned whether either is genuinely his. **Not independently verified in this session**; recorded as an open attribution.
- **Harwan.** Usman Harooni's home district is named by his nisba and not located in any source reached. The coordinate given in the journey file is the Nishapur pin and is a placeholder, not a finding.
- **Ghazni and Multan.** Both stops are route inference. Neither is attested for Chishti personally in any source reached. They are kept because the road existed and because al-Hallaj is attested at Multan three centuries earlier, but they carry no evidentiary weight.
- **Osh.** The initiation of Bakhtiar Kaki there rests on a single Wikipedia sentence about a journey to Isfahan that is otherwise undocumented.
- **The twenty years with Usman Harooni.** Undated in every source, and the round number is itself suspect.
