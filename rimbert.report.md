# Rimbert of Hamburg-Bremen (c. 830 - 11 June 888)

*Research report, 2026-08-15. Second archbishop of Hamburg-Bremen, disciple and successor of Ansgar, author of the Vita Anskarii, correspondent of Ratramnus of Corbie on the cynocephali, commander at Norditi in 884.*

**Legend.** **[A]** = attested, source named. **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition, hagiography, or modern inference. Contradictions are flagged and left standing; nothing is adjudicated by preference.

**Method note.** The spine of this report is the 1921 Charles H. Robinson translation of Rimbert's own *Vita Anskarii* (archive.org scan `anskarapostle00robiuoft`), read against the Fordham transcription of the same translation. Every page and chapter number below was checked against the scan's running headers, and several numbers in circulation are wrong (§7). Everything about Rimbert's own life, as opposed to Ansgar's, rests on thinner material: the anonymous *Vita Rimberti*, **not read in Latin**, reaching this report only through Robinson's introduction and the Ökumenisches Heiligenlexikon; Adam of Bremen's *Gesta*, likewise **not read directly**; and two Wikipedias that disagree on the most basic questions about him.

---

## 1. Origin: the one fact everyone states differently

The year c. 830 is conventional and unattested in any document **[R]**. The birthplace is a live split, not a gap:

- **English Wikipedia, verbatim:** "While his place of birth is uncertain it is widely accepted by historians that Rimbert was Danish." **[A as a report of the position]**
- **German Wikipedia, verbatim:** "Rimbert, der aus dem Adel Flanderns stammte, ist wahrscheinlich bei Torhout geboren." **[A as a report of the position]**
- **Ökumenisches Heiligenlexikon** agrees with the German position: born c. 830 in Flanders. **[A]**

Working notes resolved the split silently, and in one place invented a compromise ("the Danish/Frisian border region") that no source states, while setting the coordinates at Torhout in Flanders. Both moves are rejected. The dispute goes into the journey file as a `date_confidence` hedge, and the pin sits at Torhout only because Torhout is the one named place either tradition offers.

A third piece of evidence sits between the positions (§3): the *Vita Anskarii* at ch. XXXIII describes a priest named Rimbert "whose ancestors were of Danish extraction". If that man is the author, the English position has a near-primary source. Robinson's own footnote says he may not be.

## 2. Turholt and the household of Ansgar

**[A]** The manor of **Turholt (Torhout, Flanders)** was granted to Ansgar by **Louis the Pious**, c. 831, as the northern mission's endowment; **Charles the Bald withdrew it** in the 840s. Working notes had Charles the Bald as the grantor, inverting the story: he took it away, in the same decade the Danish fleet burned Hamburg (845), so the mission lost its money and its cathedral within a few years of each other. **[A]** Rimbert was trained as a monk at Turholt (both Wikipedias agree).

**[R]** The *Vita Rimberti* has him showing the marks of holiness from infancy and refusing children's games for prayer; the Heiligenlexikon warns that it contains "viel Legendäres". It was written **c. 900** by a monk of **Corvey abbey at Höxter**, correcting a range circulated as "c. 865-909" whose lower bound is impossible, the work postdating its subject's death in 888.

**[R]** He joined Ansgar's household at Bremen, is called Ansgar's *alumnus*, rode the circuit through Nordalbingia, and was at the deathbed. The years are undocumented; c. 850 onward is inference from the shape of the career.

## 3. The priest named Rimbert (Vita Anskarii ch. XXXIII)

**[A, verbatim, tr. Robinson (1921), p. 104]:**

> he ordained for this work a priest named Rimbert, whose ancestors were of Danish extraction. When he had sent him in Christ's name to those parts he was kindly received there by the king and the people, and by the help of the Lord he celebrated without restraint the divine mysteries in their midst.

**Correction to working notes:** this is **ch. XXXIII, p. 104**, not ch. XXXII, p. 105. Both digitizations agree and the running headers bracket the passage.

**[A] Robinson's footnote, verbatim:** "It is not clear whether this Rimbert is to be identified with the author of the Life of Anskar. According to Adam Brem., Rimbert could only have been in deacon's orders at this time."

This is the most consequential uncertainty in the file. If the identification holds, Rimbert was a priest working inside Denmark before 865 and of Danish descent; if not, the Danish-origin position loses its only near-primary support.

**A second contradiction sits on top of it.** Robinson's editorial note at p. 126 has him **in deacon's orders in 865**, at his election. The MGH title of Ratramnus's reply calls him **presbyter** at the time of the cynocephali exchange, which the same evidence dates **before** 865. He cannot be a presbyter before 865 and a deacon in 865. One is wrong; the reachable sources cannot say which.

## 4. The cynocephali correspondence

**[A]** Rimbert wrote to **Ratramnus, monk of Corbie**, asking whether the cynocephali, the dog-headed people reported at the edge of the mission field, possessed rational souls and could therefore be baptized. **[A]** Ratramnus answered with the *Epistola de Cynocephalis*, arguing from reported behaviour (villages, agriculture, herds, clothing, law, modesty) that they reason, that what reasons is human and descends from Adam, and so is a proper object of mission. **[A]** Rimbert's own letter **does not survive**; its content is known only because the reply restates it.

**Dating [A, by inference from the documents].** Working notes gave 865 and "c. 860s". Both are too late: the MGH title is *Epist. de cynocephalis ad Rimbertum **presbyterum** scripta*, addressing a priest and not an archbishop, and English Wikipedia gives "Ratramnus (died c. 868)". The defensible window is **the late 850s to 865**, and the place should not be called Bremen-the-archiepiscopal-see, only Bremen where Rimbert lived in Ansgar's household.

**[R]** The patristic authority behind the answer is **Augustine**, *City of God* XVI, on the monstrous races: no bodily strangeness puts a descendant of Adam outside the human family. This is stated in the atlas's own San Cristóbal file, not in a source consulted directly, and is carried as **[R]**.

**Not reachable.** No English translation of the *Epistola de Cynocephalis* was located; the Latin is *PL* 121:1153-56, not retrieved. **No direct quotation from either side of this correspondence appears anywhere in the journey file**, because none could be verified.

## 5. The succession of 865

**[A]** Ansgar died at Bremen on **3 February 865**; Rimbert was elected unanimously, travelled to the court of **Louis the German** for royal confirmation of his succession to the united see, then to **Archbishop Liudbert of Mainz**, who consecrated him. **[A, German Wikipedia]** He received the **pallium from Pope Nicholas I**.

**[A, Robinson's editorial note, p. 126, verbatim]:**

> As soon as the funeral rites were accomplished, Rimbert, the author of the Life of Anskar, who was then in deacon's orders, and who had been named apparently by Anskar prior to his death as his successor, was nominated as archbishop. This nomination was subsequently accepted by Pope Nicholas.

**Correction:** this is Robinson's own **editorial footnote**, not primary text, and it sits at the **foot of p. 126**, not p. 127, citing "Adam Brem. Hist. xxvii".

**Gaps.** No source names where Louis the German held court that year, so **Frankfurt** enters the journey file as his habitual seat, flagged in `date_confidence` as a placement; whether Rimbert crossed the Alps for the pallium or received it by envoy is likewise unstated and flagged in place. **[A]** He attended the **Synod of Worms**, 868 (German Wikipedia); nothing in the conciliar record is attributed to him personally.

## 6. The archbishop's field, 865-888

**[A]** Maintained Ansgar's **poorhouse at Bremen**; **no source attaches a year**, and the "868" in working notes is unsupported. **[A]** Founded a **monastery at Bücken** on the Weser (English Wikipedia: "founded a monastery at Buecken"); **no year in any source**, the circulating "870" is fabricated, and German Wikipedia omits Bücken entirely. Both given as undated within 865-888.

**[A, contested emphasis]** "Continued to preach to the Danes at **Hedeby**" (English Wikipedia), undated. **German Wikipedia does not mention Haithabu** and reads Rimbert as turning to administration and local defence instead. Two encyclopedias, two archbishops. Both carried.

**[R]** Continued oversight of **Ribe** (*Vita Anskarii* ch. XXXIV in Robinson, not ch. 24 as circulated; Schleswig is ch. XXXIII; the grantor is **Horik II**), **Schleswig**, and the other Danish churches. **[R] Aarhus**: a ninth-century church there falls in the field, but whether it was founded under Ansgar or under Rimbert is contested. **[R] Birka**: oversight continued after 865, and **whether Rimbert ever sailed to Björkö is not stated in any consulted source.**

**[R] Ransom of captives.** Robinson's introduction, p. 21, summarizing the untranslated *Vita Rimberti*, verbatim:

> In order to ransom Christians who had been captured by the Northmen he parted even with the gold and silver vessels of his church and with the horse which he kept for his own use.

A paraphrase of a Latin text not verified here, corroborated indirectly by the Bremen **Rembertistift**, which carries his name because of his medieval reputation for care of the poor and the sick.

**Rejected as invented.** Two sentences circulating as quotations could not be located in any source and are **not used**: "He would sooner walk than ride, that the price of a horse might redeem a captive" (attributed to the *Vita Rimberti*) and "He grieved that he had not been found worthy to suffer for Christ's sake" (attributed to the *Vita Anskarii*). The second states a real theme of the *Vita*, and appears in the journey file as prose, never in quotation marks.

## 7. The Vita Anskarii

**[A]** Written by Rimbert, addressed to the monks of Corbie, the house that trained Ansgar. **Dating contradiction, carried:** English Wikipedia, verbatim, "The Vita Anskarii was written sometime between the years 869 and 876"; German Wikipedia gives **c. 876**. Working notes gave **873**, a midpoint no source states; it is not used.

**[A, English Wikipedia]** The book's concurrent aims: legitimizing the contested union of Hamburg and Bremen, recruiting clergy for a dangerous field, and securing continued material support from West Frankish patrons.

**[A, Fordham introduction]** The text was lost after Adam of Bremen's day and **rediscovered by Philip Caesar in the middle of the seventeenth century**; his *Triapostolatus septentrionis* appeared in **1642**. The working notes' "c. 1600" is off by roughly forty years, and the loss runs closer to six centuries than five. The **oldest surviving manuscript is the Codex Stuttgardiensis G.32, tenth century**.

## 8. Viking pressure, refuge, and Norditi

**[R]** Renewed raiding forced Rimbert out of Bremen carrying Ansgar's relics and the church treasure; the source is Adam of Bremen at second hand, and **the Gesta was not read directly**. **[R] Stade** on the lower Elbe served as a secondary administrative point: general context, no document.

**Correction, load-bearing. Ramelsloh.** Working notes had it as an 880s fallback for Rimbert's canons, citing Adam of Bremen. German Wikipedia, "Ramelsloh": the place is first mentioned in **845**, when **Ansgar** reached it fleeing the Vikings and founded the chapter; it reappears around 983, under Archbishop Unwan. The 880s flight looks like **Ansgar's story doubled back onto his successor** and is presented in the journey file as exactly that. Coordinates corrected to **53.3419, 10.0253**.

**[A] Battle of Norditi, 884.** Rimbert personally commanded a Frisian force that defeated a Viking army and permanently drove Viking forces out of East Frisia (English and German Wikipedia agree). **Coordinate correction:** the circulating 53.5989 / 7.3453 lands inland roughly 10 km east of Norden and matches neither town nor battle site. German Wikipedia places the engagement in the former **Hilgenrieder Bucht**, municipality of **Hagermarsch, 53.6439 / 7.2936**, which the journey file uses; Norden itself is c. 53.595 / 7.205.

## 9. Last year, death, grave

**[A]** In **888** Rimbert obtained grants of **market, coinage and toll rights for Bremen** from **Arnulf of Carinthia**. **Title correction, inherited from both Wikipedias:** in 888 Arnulf was **King of East Francia**, elected November 887, and was not crowned emperor until **February 896**, eight years after both the grant and Rimbert's death. "Emperor Arnulf" is an anachronism and is not used.

**[A]** Died at **Bremen, 11 June 888**, after roughly twenty-three years as archbishop, and was succeeded by **Adalgar**. **[A, Fordham introduction]** The next archbishop of the line recorded dying on mission is **Unni, at Birka in 936**, forty-eight years later, read there as a lapse in missionary activity. Whether the gap is in the activity or in the record, the reachable evidence cannot say. **[R]**

**Grave.** Working notes stated flatly that no source locates the burial. That overstates it: the **Ökumenisches Heiligenlexikon** records a tradition, verbatim, "Rimbert wurde an der Außenmauer des Bremer Domes neben Willehad von Bremen bestattet" **[A as tradition]**. What remains true is that **no monument, inscription, or archaeologically confirmed burial** is documented, and the cathedral's own history pages do not list him among recorded archbishop burials **[R]**.

## 10. Cult

**Feast day, contradiction carried.** German Wikipedia gives **11 June**. English Wikipedia's body text states verbatim "His feast day is 4 February" while its **own infobox says 11 June**. The Heiligenlexikon reconciles them: **11 June** is the general Catholic commemoration, **4 February** a non-obligatory memorial in the dioceses of **Hamburg and Osnabrück**. Both dates are real; the journey file says so rather than choosing.

**[A]** Venerated as a saint, honored particularly for care of the poor and the sick; the **St.-Remberti-Stift** in Bremen, a foundation of c. 1300, carries his name, as do streets and churches in Bremen and Hamburg. A continuing local cult, not a fixed shrine. **[R, unverified]** A miracle list circulating in working notes (calming storms, exorcisms) **could not be located anywhere reachable** and is used nowhere in the journey file.

## 11. Contradictions left standing

1. **Danish or Flemish birth.** English Wikipedia against German Wikipedia and the Lexikon.
2. **Deacon or priest.** Robinson after Adam of Bremen has him a deacon in 865; the MGH title of Ratramnus's reply has him a presbyter before 865.
3. **Is the Rimbert of *Vita Anskarii* ch. XXXIII the author?** Robinson's footnote raises the doubt, then leaves it.
4. **Missionary or administrator.** English Wikipedia has him preaching at Hedeby; German Wikipedia never mentions the place and reads the episcopate as administrative.
5. **Vita Anskarii composed 869-876, or 876 flat.**
6. **Feast on 11 June or 4 February**, including inside a single Wikipedia article.
7. **The Aarhus church, founded under Ansgar or under Rimbert.** Contested.

## 12. Gaps stated as gaps

- **No year survives** for the poorhouse, the Bücken foundation, or the Hedeby preaching. The only dated acts of a twenty-three-year episcopate are Worms (868), Norditi (884), the Arnulf grants (888), and the death.
- **Rimbert's letter to Ratramnus is lost**; only the reply survives, and **no verbatim words of his own correspondence are verifiable in any reachable language.** Every quotation in the journey file comes from the *Vita Anskarii* or Robinson's apparatus.
- Unknown: whether he ever reached Birka; where Louis the German held court in 865; whether he went to Rome for the pallium; where exactly he lies.
