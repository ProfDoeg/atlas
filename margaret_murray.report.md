# Margaret Alice Murray (1863-1963): research report
*2026-08-09. Egyptologist, folklorist, first woman to hold an archaeology lecturing post in Britain; author of the witch-cult hypothesis. From the verified research pool; the lenses' own corrections are carried forward, not smoothed over.*

**Legend.** **[A]** = attested, source named · **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition, or inference · *contradictions are flagged where the lenses disagree and are not adjudicated here.*

A note on the source base first. Almost the whole biographical spine reaches us through two English Wikipedia articles, "Margaret Murray" and "Witch-cult hypothesis", plus a few site articles (Osireion, Għar Dalam, Borġ in-Nadur, the Spanish-language Trepucó). Her 1921 book was fetched in full and every Murray quotation below is verified against it. Everything else, including every critical quotation about her, was seen at second hand through Wikipedia's citations. That is a thin base for a hundred-year life, and it shows: the pool contradicts itself on four dates, and five coordinates in it were wrong by one to two kilometres. Corrections are stated where they occur.

---

## I. Two hemispheres, 1863-1893

**[A, Wikipedia "Margaret Murray"]** Margaret Alice Murray is born in Calcutta on 13 July 1863, to James Murray, a manager in the Serampore paper mills, and Margaret Carr, who came out to India as a missionary in 1857. An older sister, Mary, is in the household, with her paternal grandmother and great-grandmother.

*Fabrication flagged.* The geography lens as first drafted called her "fourth child", said two sisters died in infancy, gave her "a much older brother, John", and made her mother "a nurse and missionary worker". **None of that is in the source.** The John of her childhood is her **paternal uncle**, the vicar of Lambourn. Recorded here as errors caught in verification, not as claims.

**[A]** 1870: Margaret and Mary are sent to England and lodged with uncle John, a vicar, and his wife Harriet at Lambourn, Berkshire (51.5111, -1.5262, coordinate confirmed correct). *Flagged:* the pool said she "later calls these years unhappy in her autobiography". That is **unattested and runs against the source**, which credits uncle John with awakening her archaeological interest by taking her to see local monuments. Dropped.

**[A]** 1873-1875: her mother takes the girls to Bonn; both become fluent in German. 1875-1877: back in Calcutta. 1877: the family settles at Sydenham, London.

**[A] / contradiction flagged.** The Calcutta nursing years: the **chronology lens dates them to 1883**, the **geography lens to 1880-1887**. The source text reads "In 1880, they returned to Calcutta, where Margaret remained for the next seven years", nursing at the Calcutta General Hospital, run by the Anglican Sisterhood of Clewer, through a cholera outbreak. **The geography lens is right by three years.** The journey uses 1880 and records the disagreement in `date_confidence`.

**[A]** 1887: returns to England, social work at Rugby, Warwickshire. **[A]** 1891: living with her retired father at Bushey Heath, Hertfordshire; James Murray dies that year. **[A]** 1893: last journey to India, visiting her sister at Madras.

## II. Gower Street and the Egyptian seasons, 1894-1913

**[A]** January 1894: at thirty, with no prior academic training, Murray enrols at University College London to study Egyptology under Flinders Petrie. **[A]** 1895: publishes "The Descent of Property in the Early Periods of Egyptian History". **[A]** 1898: appointed junior lecturer, the first female lecturer in archaeology in the United Kingdom.

**[A, Wikipedia "Margaret Murray" and "Osireion"]** 1902: excavation season at Abydos under Petrie; she helps uncover the Osireion, published as *The Osireion at Abydos* (1904). **[A]** 1903: Saqqara, the Old Kingdom cemetery; *Saqqara Mastabas I* and *Elementary Egyptian Grammar* follow in 1905.

**[A]** From 1906 she lectures regularly at the Manchester Museum. **[A]** May 1908: she leads the public unwrapping of a mummy before an audience of over five hundred, the first such event led by a woman.

*Three corrections flagged.* (1) The pool called her "curator of Egyptology at the Manchester Museum"; **no such appointment appears in any source**, she travelled there to catalogue Petrie's material and to lecture. (2) The May 1908 event unwrapped **one** mummy, Khnum-nakht, not both of the Two Brothers. (3) *The Tomb of the Two Brothers* was published in **1910**, not 1908; 1908 is the unwrapping, not the book. **[A]** No curse legend attaches to the event in any source checked, despite the genre expectation; treat any such story as unattested.

**[A]** 1911: *Elementary Coptic (Sahidic) Grammar*. 1913: *Ancient Egyptian Legends*. 1914: Petrie founds the journal *Ancient Egypt*; Murray is its de facto editor.

---

## III. The turn to the witches, 1914-1924

**[R, Wikipedia "Margaret Murray"]** During the First World War she enrols as a volunteer nurse and, recuperating from illness, spends time at Glastonbury, where local folklore feeds her later interests. The causal weight usually put on Glastonbury is **[R]**: the source gives the stay and the interest, not that one produced the other.

**[R]** 1915: she begins branching from Egyptology into European witchcraft. **[A]** 1917 and 1920: two articles in *Folklore* laying out the hypothesis. **[A]** 1921: promoted to lecturer at UCL; Oxford University Press (Clarendon Press) publishes *The Witch-Cult in Western Europe: A Study in Anthropology*, refereed for the press by the folklorist Henry Balfour.

The thesis, in her own words **[A, primary text fetched]**: "Ritual Witchcraft, or, as I propose to call it, the Dianic cult, embraces the religious beliefs and ritual of the people known in late mediaeval times as 'Witches'." The full passages are carried in the journey file, verified against Project Gutenberg edition 20411.

*Two quote-locator errors corrected.* (1) "The evidence proves that underlying the Christian religion was a cult..." was cited to the **Preface**; it is in the **Introduction** (PG 20411, lines 325-329), after the Dianic-cult definition, and the circulating version cuts a clause and a sentence boundary with one ellipsis while claiming the ellipsis is internal to a single sentence. The journey quotes it whole. (2) "I am content merely to point out that it was a definite religion..." was cited as "closing of the Introduction"; it closes the Ritual Witchcraft paragraph about a fifth of the way in.

**[A]** 1922: senior lecturer. 1924: assistant professor of Egyptology at UCL. **[A]** 1927: honorary doctorate; joins the Folklore Society.

## IV. Malta, Menorca, and the field decade, 1921-1931

**[A]** 1921-1927: Murray leads excavation seasons on Malta, the longest sustained campaign of her career.

*The messiest area of the pool; the contradictions are left open.*

1. **Which sites.** The geography lens names **Santa Sofia [R]**, **Santa Maria tal-Bakkari [R]**, **Għar Dalam [A]**, **Borġ in-Nadur [A]**. The interlock lens instead asserts **Tarxien and Ħal Saflieni** "for the British School at Rome and the Museum". **Different sites, and neither lens cites a source that settles it.** The journey names no Maltese site the interlock lens alone claims.
2. **Borġ in-Nadur dates.** The geography lens gives a single 1923 season; Wikipedia's "Borġ in-Nadur" article gives "1922-1927: Margaret Murray investigated the main temple area". Unresolved; the journey file uses the span and hedges it.
3. **Coordinates corrected.** Borġ in-Nadur: draft 35.8283, 14.5219 is 0.7 km off, correct **35.8312, 14.5290**. Għar Dalam: draft 35.8494, 14.5286 is 1.4 km north of the cave, correct **35.8364, 14.5280**. Trepucó: draft 39.8814, 4.2447 is 1.9 km off, correct **39.8739, 4.2655** (es.wikipedia, which dates her clearing of the taula to 1931).
4. **Għar Dalam attribution.** The cave's own article credits Issel (1865) and Cooke (1892) and **does not mention Murray at all**. Her work there is attested only in accounts of the Maltese seasons, so the claim is stated modestly. **Sa Torreta de Tramuntana [R]** is dropped from the journey for want of any independent attestation.

**[A]** 1925: volunteer excavation of the Homestead Moat earthwork at Whomerle Wood near Stevenage. **[A]** 1929: writes the "Witchcraft" entry for the *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, presenting the hypothesis as settled consensus; **[A]** the entry stands until it is replaced in **1968**. **[A]** 1929: attends the British Association meeting in South Africa; **[R]** *which city she attended is not specified in any source consulted*; the pool's Cape Town placement was a convenience, so the stop is dropped from the journey rather than pinned on a guess. **[A]** 1930: *Egyptian Sculpture*; 1931: *Egyptian Temples* and, from Sampson Low, *The God of the Witches*. **[A]** 1932: *Maltese Folktales*, *Corpus of the Bronze Age Pottery of Malta*.

## V. The Soviet tour, the journal, the retirement, 1932-1935

**[A]** Early 1930s: Murray tours Soviet museums, Leningrad, Moscow, Kharkov, Kiev. **Gap:** no reachable source fixes the year, and nothing survives about what she saw or whom she met.

**[A]** 1933: on Petrie's retirement she takes over *Ancient Egypt*, renaming it *Ancient Egypt and the East*. **[A]** 1934: begins cataloguing the Egyptian antiquities of Girton College, Cambridge.

*Two corrections.* (1) The pool's "1934-1942" conflates two spans: **cataloguing 1934-1940**, **Cambridge lectures to 1942**. (2) Girton coordinate: draft 52.2189, 0.0866 sits 1.1 km south of the college; correct **52.2286, 0.0839**.

**[A]** 1935: retires from UCL, then spends time in Jerusalem aiding the Petries and joins the excavation at **Tall al-Ajjul** near Gaza. *Contradiction flagged:* the geography lens dated the Jerusalem stay "from 1933"; the source places it **after the 1935 retirement**. The journey uses 1935 and hedges.

**[A]** Late 1935: lecture tour through Oslo, Sweden, Finland, and Estonia. **Gap:** the itinerary is attested, the contents of the lectures are not. Stockholm is dropped from the journey for want of anything beyond the itinerary line; Oslo, Helsinki, and Tallinn are kept.

## VI. Late fieldwork and the war, 1937-1945

**[A]** March-April 1937: at seventy-three, Murray directs an excavation of cave dwellings at Petra. **[A]** 1939-1945: evacuated from London by the Blitz, based at Cambridge, teaching military personnel and continuing the Girton work.

**[A / R, thinly sourced]** 1945: Murray is briefly consulted on the unsolved "Bella in the Wych Elm" case at Hagley Wood, Worcestershire, **reportedly** invoking ritual murder. **The wording of what she actually said is preserved nowhere reachable**, and the source's "reportedly" is carried through rather than dropped. Hagley Wood coordinates (approximately 52.4262, -2.1247) are taken from the site; the pool gave none.

**[A]** 1949: *Ancient Egyptian Religious Poetry*, *The Splendour That Was Egypt*. **[A]** Post-1945 she lives in a bedsit on Endsleigh Street, Bloomsbury, teaching ancient Egyptian history twice weekly at the City Literary Institute into her nineties.

## VII. The long coda, 1953-1963

**[A]** 1953-1955: president of the Folklore Society, taking the chair at ninety after Allan Gomme's resignation. **[A]** 1954: *The Divine King in England*, extending the thesis to claim several English kings died as ritual sacrifices; it receives little serious academic attention. *Not asserted:* the specific royal figures often listed for this book are outside the verified pool and are not named in the journey. **[A]** 1957: publicly champions T. C. Lethbridge's disputed Wandlebury hill-figures. **[A]** 1960: donates her papers to the Folklore Society Archive. **[A]** 1961: *Folklore* devotes its autumn issue to a Festschrift for her ninety-eighth birthday.

**[A]** Early 1960s: moves into a North Finchley home run by retired nurses. **[A]** 1962-1963: eighteen months under twenty-four-hour nursing care at Welwyn, Hertfordshire. **[A]** 13 July 1963: her hundredth birthday; she publishes *The Genesis of Religion* and the autobiography *My First Hundred Years*, which is reviewed warmly. **[A]** 13 November 1963: dies at the Queen Victoria Memorial Hospital, Welwyn, aged 100.

*Coordinate corrected.* Draft 51.8112, -0.2094 falls south toward Welwyn Garden City, a different settlement. The hospital stood in **Welwyn village** (Fulling Mill Lane area); approximately **51.830, -0.213**.

*Contradiction between lenses.* The **geography lens states as a gap** that no crematorium is named in its source. The **afterlife lens** gives a funeral on **20 November 1963** and cremation at **Golders Green**, on Find a Grave memorial #133227825, which is user-compiled and weaker than the rest of this base. The journey carries that stop tagged **[R]** and keeps the gap behind it: **what became of the ashes is stated nowhere**. No grave or headstone is documented.

## VIII. Afterlife and reception

**[A]** A bronze bust by Stephen Rickard, commissioned by her former student Violet MacDermot, is held at the UCL Institute of Archaeology; UCL named a women's common room after her, having built it at her campaigning. **Gap:** neither memorial is dated in any reachable source.

**[A]** *The Witch-Cult in Western Europe* sold about 2,020 copies in its first thirty years, then became a bestseller in the 1950s and 1960s, which is what provoked scholars into refuting it in detail. **[A]** Ronald Hutton: she "treated her source material with reckless abandon". **[A]** By the 1970s the hypothesis faced definitive academic rejection, Cohn's *Europe's Inner Demons* (1975) being the standard demolition. **[A]** In 1996 Diane Purkiss argued the dismissal was itself shaped by an androcentric bias among the men who led it.

**Tag correction.** The chronology lens tagged the Wicca influence **[R]**. It should be **[A]**: the source states flatly that the Murrayite thesis "provided the blueprint for the contemporary Pagan religion of Wicca", and that Gardner claimed to have found the New Forest coven in 1939. The date 1954 belongs to *Witchcraft Today*, not to the influence. She is popularly styled the "Grandmother of Wicca" **[A]**.

**[A]** Kathleen Sheppard, *The Life of Margaret Alice Murray: A Woman's Work in Archaeology* (Lexington Books, 2013) is the first book-length biography. *Unconfirmed:* a reported 2022 Bloomsbury reissue could not be verified.

## Contradictions carried forward, not resolved

1. Calcutta nursing: 1883 (chronology lens) vs 1880-1887 (geography lens, matching the source text).
2. *The Tomb of the Two Brothers*: 1908 (chronology lens) vs 1910 (correct per source).
3. Malta sites: Għar Dalam and Borġ in-Nadur (geography lens) vs Tarxien and Ħal Saflieni (interlock lens). Not adjudicated.
4. Borġ in-Nadur: single 1923 season vs 1922-1927 span.
5. Jerusalem with the Petries: "from 1933" vs after the 1935 retirement.
6. Girton: cataloguing 1934-1940 vs lectures to 1942, conflated in the pool as one span.
7. Cremation: named as a gap by one lens, given as Golders Green, 20 November 1963, by another on a Find a Grave record.
8. Wicca influence tagged [R] by one lens and stated flatly by the source; corrected to [A].
9. Manchester: "curator of Egyptology" (unattested) vs cataloguer and lecturer (attested); one mummy unwrapped, not two.
