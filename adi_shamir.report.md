# Adi Shamir (b. 1952): research report

*2026-08-11. Subject living. Slug `adi_shamir`. QUEUE.md framing: "the S of RSA, co-invented the first practical public-key implementation alongside rivest and adleman", listed between Ron Rivest and Leonard Adleman.*

Legend: **[A]** = attested, source named · **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition, or inference. Contradictions are flagged in place and again in §7, never silently resolved.

**Framing note.** The brief asked for tomb and afterlife material. Shamir is alive and holds the Paul and Marlene Borman Professorial Chair of Applied Mathematics at Weizmann **[A: faculty page; research.com profile, 2026]**. There is no funerary site, so everything gathered under "afterlife" is living-honoree commemoration: award plaques, induction ceremonies, a patent number. A gap in the brief, not in the record; the journey's last stop says so rather than inventing a monument.

## 1. Tel Aviv, 1952 to 1976

- Born **6 July 1952, Tel Aviv** **[A: Wikipedia, 'Adi Shamir'; ACM Turing Award biography]**. No source reached names parents, street or hospital. The pin is the city; stated as a gap.
- **BSc mathematics, Tel Aviv University, 1973** **[A: same]**. Month and day not attested.
- **MSc computer science, Weizmann Institute, 1975** **[A: same]**.
- **Linear-time algorithm for 2-satisfiability, 1976** **[A: Wikipedia]**. Publication venue not identified in this pass: a gap.
- **PhD, Weizmann, "The Fixed Points Of Recursive Definitions", advisor Zohar Manna.** Year contradicted, see §7.1.

Nothing before 1977 concerns cryptography. Manna worked on program verification, and the thesis question, what a self-referring definition settles down to, is unrelated to everything Shamir became known for. Popular retellings imply a lifelong cryptographic vocation; the record does not support one.

## 2. Coventry and Cambridge, 1976 to 1980

- **Postdoctoral year, Warwick, host Mike Paterson** **[A: Warwick Dept. of Computer Science, 2023 honorary-degree announcement: "Prof. Shamir has been known in Warwick since 1976, when he spent a year as a post-doc with our own Prof. Mike Paterson", and "directly after Warwick he went to MIT"]**. The most useful correction the verification pass produced: it dates the year to 1976, names the host, and denies any gap before MIT, superseding an earlier pool entry that said "no publication anchors it" and dated it 1977.
- **MIT Laboratory for Computer Science, 545 Technology Square.** Start year contradicted, see §7.2.
- **The RSA construction, April 1977, Cambridge, Massachusetts** **[A]**. Rivest proposes, Adleman attacks, Shamir does some of each, by his own account **[A: National Inventors Hall of Fame; Splunk, 'RSA Algorithm in Cryptography']**. The seder night, the wine and the mathematics textbook are recorded from the participants' own account **[A: Wikipedia, 'RSA (cryptosystem)']**. What is legend is only the compression; see §7.6.
- **Gardner, "Mathematical Games", Scientific American, August 1977** **[A: column PDF]**, with the RSA-129 challenge and a $100 prize. The NSA pressing MIT to withhold the technical report **[A: Wikipedia, 'RSA cryptosystem']**. RSA-129 was factored in April 1994 by Atkins, Graff, Lenstra and Leyland on roughly 1,600 volunteer machines **[A]**, the clearest documented collapse of a legend attached to Shamir's work; it is not in the journey file, having no Shamir presence and no location.
- **"A Method for Obtaining Digital Signatures and Public-Key Cryptosystems", CACM 21(2), February 1978, pp. 120-126** **[A]**. Received 4 April 1977, revised 1 September 1977. The page range is confirmed independently by Shamir's own reference [5] in the 1979 paper. Both 1977 and 1978 circulate as "the date of the RSA paper": these are submission and publication, not a contradiction.

## 6. Quotations

Ten quotes are placed in the journey file.

**Verified by direct PDF extraction.** Four passages from **"How to Share a Secret", CACM 22(11), Nov 1979, p. 612**: the abstract, Liu's eleven-scientists puzzle, the "most secure key management scheme" passage, the "mutually suspicious individuals" line. Two from the **RSA paper, CACM 21(2), Feb 1978, p. 120**: the abstract's opening line and the "era of electronic mail" line.

**Verbatim but miscited in the pool, corrected here.**
- *"The reader is urged to find a way to 'break' the system..."*: verbatim in Section XI, Conclusions, but the pool cited **p. 132**, which does not exist; the paper runs pp. 120-126. Corrected to **p. 126**.
- *"...can break the reduced variant of DES with eight rounds in a few minutes on a personal computer."*: genuine, but this wording is the **Journal of Cryptology 4(1), 1991, p. 3** abstract, not the CRYPTO '90 extended abstract, which reads "up to eight rounds... on a PC". The journal citation is used.

**Reported speech, not a verified transcript**, flagged as such in `quote_source`.
- *"Cryptography is typically bypassed, not penetrated."*: third of the three laws, Turing lecture "Cryptography: State of the Science" (2002 award, lecture June 2003). Traced out of the aggregator swamp to Network World (4 May 2004) and Ian Grigg (26 May 2004). ACM hosts video; **no transcript retrievable**.
- *"Fully secure systems don't exist now and won't exist in the future."*: Threatpost's direct quotation from the RSA Conference 2015 panel. The circulating variant ("don't exist today and they won't exist in the future") is wrong and is corrected.

**Rejected.** *"Absolutely secure systems do not exist."*: found only in secondary blog summaries of the Turing lecture, and almost certainly the same remark as the two above restated. Left out of the journey file entirely.

Ten quotes across forty-five stops is about 22 percent, below the usual third. That is the honest ceiling: the verified pool is three papers and two pieces of reported speech, and Shamir's recorded words are almost entirely abstracts.

## 7. Contradictions, flagged and left standing

**7.1 PhD year, 1976 or 1977.** The Mathematics Genealogy Project (id 19271) lists "Ph.D. Weizmann Institute of Science 1977", dissertation "The Fixed Points Of Recursive Definitions", advisor Zohar Manna; current Wikipedia also gives 1977. An older pool entry asserting "Wikipedia gives 1976" is stale. 1976 appears as the year the thesis work and technical report are dated. **Not resolved**: the journey pins 1976 as the thesis year and states in date_confidence that the degree is very likely 1977.

**7.2 MIT start, 1977 or 1978.** Wikipedia and Warwick's "directly after Warwick he went to MIT" point to 1977; one Britannica-derived account says 1978. If the postdoc ran a full year from 1976 and RSA was built at MIT in April 1977, the Warwick year cannot have been much more than seven or eight months. **Not resolved.** The tension is written into the date_confidence of both stops.

**7.3 TWINKLE venue: CHES 1999 Worcester, not Eurocrypt '99 Prague.** Shamir's Eurocrypt '99 Prague paper that May was "Cryptanalysis of Skipjack", a different result. "Factoring Large Numbers with the TWINKLE Device" appeared at **CHES 1999, Worcester, Massachusetts, August 1999, LNCS 1717**; what is documented for May is that the paper circulated publicly around 5 May (Cryptome). The journey uses Worcester. The $5,000-per-unit figure and the never-built characterization stand **[A: Wikipedia, 'TWINKLE']**.

**7.4 TWIRL venue: CRYPTO 2003 Santa Barbara, not Eurocrypt 2003 Warsaw.** The pool gave place Warsaw and date May 2003 while its own source line read "Crypto 2003 proceedings". TWIRL is Shamir and Tromer, CRYPTO 2003, LNCS 2729, pp. 1-26, Santa Barbara, August 2003. Headline figure is a $10K device sieving 512-bit RSA in under ten minutes; the "low tens of millions" applies to the 1024-bit case.

**7.5 Turing citation wording.** ACM's official 2002 citation reads *"For their ingenious contribution for making public-key cryptography useful in practice"*; a second lens quoted *"for their seminal contributions to the theory and practical application of public-key cryptography"*. ACM's wording is followed and the journey paraphrases rather than quoting it.

**7.6 The seder night, [A] or [R].** One lens tagged it [A] (geography), another [R] (afterlife). Wikipedia's RSA article records it from the participants' own account. Resolution: **the night is [A], the compression is [R]**. The seder, the wine and the sleepless writing-up are attested; "a single eureka produced RSA" is legend, since months of proposal-and-attack made that night possible.

**7.7 AES-256, excluded.** The pool credited Shamir with the first related-key break of the **full** AES-256 at Asiacrypt 2009, conflating two papers. The full-AES-256 break is Biryukov and Khovratovich **alone**, Asiacrypt 2009; **Shamir was not an author**. The paper actually cited, "Key Recovery Attacks of Practical Complexity on AES-256 Variants with up to 10 Rounds" (Biryukov, Dunkelman, Keller, Khovratovich, Shamir), is **Eurocrypt 2010**, LNCS 6110, and attacks round-reduced variants. **No AES stop appears in the journey file.**

**7.8 Coordinates and placement, none adjudicated.** *545 Technology Square*: the canonical pin, in phil_zimmermann.journey.json and cited by nick_szabo.journey.json, is **42.3626, -71.0913**; an independent check places the building nearer **42.3634, -71.0899**, and the geography lens proposed 42.3656, -71.0951, near Vassar Street. The journey **inherits the canonical pin byte-identical** and records the discrepancy. *Weizmann*: three lenses gave 31.9046, 34.8093; 31.9070, 34.8127; 31.8969, 34.8186. The journey uses the first throughout. *"How to Share a Secret"*: dated November 1979 against a 1980 appointment, so it may have been written from Cambridge. The journey pins Rehovot and flags the choice.

## 8. Interlocks with existing atlas travelers

Named in campa where paths genuinely cross:

- **einstein**: the Weizmann campus is named for Chaim Weizmann, who crossed America with Einstein in 1921 raising money for a Hebrew university and became Israel's first president, dying in 1952, the year Shamir was born. From einstein.journey.json's 1921 stop. Note **[R]**: the link runs through the institution's name, not a meeting.
- **diffie** and **hellman**: "New Directions in Cryptography", November 1976, defines the problem RSA answers **[A: both files carry the stop]**. Named at the MIT arrival and at the Turing Award stop, where the thirteen-year gap between the awards is stated.
- **ralph_merkle**: twice. The knapsack broken in Chicago in 1982 **[A: ralph_merkle.journey.json quotes the break directly]**, and the 1996 Kanellakis Award, whose pin (40.7519, -73.9686) is inherited byte-identical and whose campa states that Shamir shares the prize with the man whose cipher he broke.
- **phil_zimmermann** and **hal_finney**: the 545 Technology Square pin, the 2000 public-domain release, the Moscone Center pin (37.7842, -122.4016) used for the 2015 panel and the 2019 absence, and PGP 2.0's RSA layer **[A]**. - **gauss**: modular arithmetic as laid out in the *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae*, 1801, sits underneath RSA; the link is stated in hal_finney.journey.json and named in the CACM 1978 campa.
- **nick_szabo**: not named in campa. The link runs through Chaum's blind signature being "built on the function Rivest, Shamir and Adleman published from 545 Technology Square in 1978", a lineage claim about the algorithm rather than a crossing of persons. Cited in the 545 stop's sources instead.

**Structural flag, not resolvable here.** phil_zimmermann and nick_szabo cite **`rivest_shamir_adleman.journey.json`**, naming four of its stops: "Cambridge, the couch and the mathematics textbook", "545 Technology Square, the method in Communications of the ACM", "San Francisco, the first RSA Conference", "Redwood City, RSA Data Security". **That file does not exist on disk.** The earlier intent was evidently one combined trio journey, while QUEUE.md lists three rows.
