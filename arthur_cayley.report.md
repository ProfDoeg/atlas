# Arthur Cayley (1821-1895): research report
*Compiled 2026-08-23 for `arthur_cayley.journey.json`. Barrister of Lincoln's Inn, first Sadleirian Professor of Pure Mathematics at Cambridge, author of about 967 papers.*

**Legend.** **[A]** attested, source named. **[R]** reconstruction, tradition, or a claim the verifier could not re-fetch. Contradictions are printed, not resolved. Unreachable sources are named with the reason.

**Standing caution.** The pool arrived in two passes; the second overturned many coordinates and several attributions, and this file follows the second. Some first-pass attributions were simply wrong, the Wilberforce Chambers article having been credited for four things it does not contain.

**Coordinates.** Ten pins from the first pass were wrong and are replaced, each correction restated in the relevant stop's `date_confidence`: Blackheath, Trinity College, Lincoln's Inn, 2 Stone Buildings, Somerset House, Lewisham, Girton, Newnham, Trinity Chapel and Mill Road Cemetery, with errors from 250 m to 2.1 km. At Trinity Chapel (now **52.2076, 0.1163**) and Mill Road Cemetery (now **52.2005, 0.1366**) the pool's two lenses disagreed with each other and both were wrong. Trinity College is **52.2070, 0.1146**, the canonical value in `newton.journey.json`, inherited byte-identical.

## 1. St Petersburg and the return, 1821-1835

- Born Richmond, Surrey, 16 August 1821, son of the merchant Henry Cayley and Maria Antonia Doughty. The family was based in St Petersburg, where Henry traded; Arthur was born on a visit to England and spent roughly his first seven years in Russia, among Russian, English and French. **[A: MacTutor]** Neither the Richmond house nor the Petersburg address is named anywhere consulted. **[gap]**
- The 1895 American commemorative literature (Macfarlane, Halsted, Peirce) built a legend that his mother was Russian, explaining his appearance and genius in racialized terms. She was English. **[R: the corrective reaches the pool through Jemma Lorenat via Crilly, whose article was login-walled]**
- Return to England, settling at 29 York Terrace, Regent's Park; Henry Cayley became a director of the London Assurance Corporation. **[A: MacTutor]** **Contradiction, unresolved:** MacTutor gives 1828, while Wikipedia, the 1911 Britannica ("left business in 1829"), Salmon (1883) and Macfarlane give 1829. Stop dated 1828-01-01, disagreement in `date_confidence`.
- **Correction.** The first pass conflated the return with Blackheath, which is the private school, not the household's address. Now separate stops. **[A: MacTutor; Britannica]** The school's name is nowhere given. **[gap]**
- Entered King's College School 1835 at fourteen, below normal entrance age; the school occupied the basement of King's College London on the Strand, 1829 to 1897. **[A: MacTutor; Wikipedia]** A chemistry prize there, asserted by the first pass and sourced to the Wikipedia article on Cayley, is not in that article and is **dropped**. **[gap]**

## 2. Trinity, 1838-1845

- Entered Trinity College, Cambridge, 1838, aged seventeen; first-year tutor George Peacock, later coached by William Hopkins. First paper as an undergraduate, 1841, in the Cambridge Mathematical Journal under Duncan Farquharson Gregory. Senior Wrangler and first Smith's Prize, 1842. Fellow of Trinity 1842, about four further years at Cambridge, some 28 papers in that journal. "On a Theory of Determinants", 1843. **[A: MacTutor throughout]**
- Summer 1842, a student reading party at Aberfeldy led with Edmund Venables; Francis Galton among the students and later the source for the recollection of Cayley's frail physique. **[A: MacTutor]** Venables's joking letter to Stokes about Cayley refusing to reenact Maskelyne's Schiehallion experiment belongs to this trip; retrospective anecdote, so no Schiehallion stop.
- Correspondence with George Boole begins 1844 **[A: MacTutor]**; a letter expresses a wish to visit Boole in Lincoln and see the cathedral **[R: no source confirms the visit]**. The stop records an intention and says so. Two 1845 papers on linear transformations and algebraic forms lay the ground of invariant theory. **[A: MacTutor]**

## 3. The continental journey, 1844

Travelled with Venables through the Swiss Alps and Italy. **[R: the verifier could not re-fetch this; the pool attributes it to MacTutor and Crilly]** Travel, architecture, painting and watercolour were lifelong pleasures. **[A: MacTutor]** **No source names a town or route**, so Chamonix is a placeholder for the standard British Alpine gateway of the period, and the Italian leg is described inside that stop rather than pinned. **[gap]**

## 4. Lincoln's Inn and the years at the Bar, 1846-1863

- Admitted to Lincoln's Inn 20 April 1846; pupil of the conveyancer Jonathan Henry Christie at 2 Stone Buildings, drafting settlements for the Marquis of Bute and the Earl of Shrewsbury's Staffordshire estates. Called to the Bar 3 May 1849; about fourteen years of practice, with between two and three hundred mathematical papers published in 1849 to 1863. **[A: Britannica; E. Campbell, Wilberforce Chambers, which confirms both figures]**
- **Quotation discipline.** Two quotations were silently mangled by the first pass and are restored. The Christie pupillage anecdote (M. G. Davidson, via Vaizey, *The Institute*, 1895-1907) had lost two sentences, leaving "Christie did so" without antecedent. The T. C. Wright recollection had been cut mid-sentence at "the honours", where Wright wrote "the honours he had won at Cambridge"; it is attributed to Forsyth, vol. VIII, p. xiv, with the Wilberforce reprint as the accessible copy. **[A, both]**
- Dublin, June 1848, during his pupillage, to hear William Rowan Hamilton lecture on quaternions; the two became friendly, but Cayley never adopted quaternions as a universal language. **[A: Wilberforce Chambers for the date; Wikipedia's Hamilton article for the venue]** The first pass tagged this [R] for want of a date; the hedge is withdrawn. George Salmon's presence there could not be confirmed and he is not staged. **[gap]**
- Fellow of the Royal Society, elected 3 June 1852; the Society held the east wing of Somerset House from 1780. Royal Medal 1859, folded into the same stop. **[A: Royal Society, Making Science; MacTutor]**
- 1854, "On the theory of groups, as depending on the symbolic equation theta^n = 1", now read as a founding document of abstract group theory. 1855, a paper sketching matrix multiplication, which he called compounding, in French in a German periodical. **[A: MacTutor]**
- 1858, "A Memoir on the Theory of Matrices", *Philosophical Transactions*: matrices as algebraic objects, the characteristic-equation result proved for 2x2 and illustrated for 3x3, the general principle stated but not proved. **[A: MacTutor; Britannica]** The name Cayley-Hamilton and the general proof are later.
- Unsuccessful candidate for the chair of natural philosophy at Marischal College, Aberdeen, 1856. **[A for the candidacy, MacTutor; R for the physical stop, no source saying whether he travelled or wrote]** Interest in the Glasgow chair of astronomy, 1859, likewise unsuccessful **[R]**, named inside the Aberdeen campa rather than pinned.

## 5. The Sadleirian chair, 1863-1868

- Elected the first Sadleirian Professor of Pure Mathematics, 1863, giving up a lucrative practice for a much smaller salary. **[A: Britannica; MacTutor]** The month is nowhere given; stop dated 1863-01-01. **[gap]**
- Married Susan Moline, daughter of a London and County Bank manager, at Lewisham, 8 September 1863. **[A: MacTutor; Mill Road Cemetery project]** The parish church is not named. **[gap]**
- **Contradiction, unresolved.** The inaugural lecture, "Analytical geometry: Introductory lecture". MacTutor gives 8 November 1863; Crilly's study of the unpublished text gives 3 November. The verifier fetched MacTutor and preferred it; the Springer article was login-walled. Stop dated 1863-11-08, conflict printed. The lecture room is not specified. **[gap]**
- Purchased Garden House, at the bottom of Little St Mary's Lane by Coe Fen, 1864; the home for three decades, kept by Susan and their daughter Mary after his death. **[A: Mill Road Cemetery project]** President of the London Mathematical Society 1868-1870; the Society, founded 1865, met initially at University College London, though the venue during his presidency is not itemized. **[A: LMS timeline; gap on the venue]**
- **The famous aphorism is not his.** That law exists "to say a thing in the greatest number of words, and of mathematics to say it in the fewest" is a story Cayley repudiated, per Forsyth's footnote on p. x of vol. VIII (corrected from the pool's "p. x-xi"). The file quotes the debunking, never the aphorism. **[A]**

## 6. Cambridge institutions, 1872-1880

- President of the Royal Astronomical Society 1872-1874, at Burlington House. **[A: RAS list, not re-fetched]** Honorary Fellow of Trinity 1872 **[A: Britannica]**; ordinary Fellow 1875 **[R: not re-verified]**.
- Gave direct help in teaching in the early days of Girton College. **[A: Wikipedia]** Girton opened at Benslow House, Hitchin, in October 1869 and moved to the Girton site in October 1873, so the earliest teaching may have happened in Hertfordshire. Stop dated 1873-10-01 and saying so.
- **Correction of substance.** The first pass placed Charlotte Angas Scott at Newnham. She was at Girton: entered 1876, sat the Tripos 1880, then researched algebraic geometry under Cayley's supervision, attending his lectures 1880-1884 on Modern Algebra, Abelian Functions, Theory of Numbers, Substitutions and Seminvariants. Moved to the Girton stop. **[A: MacTutor, Scott]** Cayley's own chairmanship of the Newnham council, undated in the sources, keeps its own stop with 1875-01-01 as an ordering placeholder. **[A: Wikipedia; gap on the dates]**
- 1874, a subscription commissioned a portrait by Lowes Cato Dickinson; a second by William Longmaid followed in 1884. Both are in Trinity's collection. **[A: Wikipedia]** The first pass credited MacTutor for the portrait and for Maxwell's contribution; MacTutor mentions neither, and Wikipedia calls Maxwell's contribution an address, not a poem. The verse is real, reprinted by Macfarlane and Forsyth, so the file quotes it while the campa does not name the genre.
- 1876, *An Elementary Treatise on Elliptic Functions*, Cambridge University Press. **[A: MacTutor; Britannica]**
- 1879, "On the Colourings of Maps", in the *Proceedings of the Royal Geographical Society*, an unexpected venue worth naming. Cayley publicized the four-colour problem, credited the conjecture to De Morgan, and did not solve it. **[A: Wikipedia, Four color theorem, MacTutor mentioning the problem nowhere, so the first pass's attribution to it is replaced]**

## 7. Baltimore and Southport, 1882-1884

- Lectured at Johns Hopkins, Baltimore, January to May 1882, on Abelian and theta functions, joining the American research milieu J. J. Sylvester had built there since 1876. **[A: MacTutor for dates and subject; Britannica for the invitation]** The first pass credited the Wilberforce article, which says nothing about Johns Hopkins, and dated the Homewood move to 1916; it was 1902. The pin is an estimate, Wikipedia giving no coordinates for the downtown campus. **[gap]**
- Copley Medal, 1882 **[A: Britannica; Royal Society catalogue]**, though some secondary sources print 1881; the disagreement is printed, not argued.
- President of the British Association at Southport, 1883; the presidential address treated Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometry and criticized Hamilton's algebra as the science of pure time. **[A: Britannica; Wikipedia; Macfarlane for the text]** The month is nowhere given, so the stop is dated 1883-01-01, and no hall is named. **[gap]**
- First De Morgan Medal, 1884. **[A: MacTutor; Britannica; LMS timeline]** No venue given; the file uses the Society's historic UCL home and says so. **[gap]**

## 8. The last years, 1889-1895

- 1889, Cambridge University Press began *The Collected Mathematical Papers*: thirteen quarto volumes to 1897, about 967 papers. Cayley edited seven; Forsyth, his successor in the chair, completed VIII to XIII. **[A: Britannica; Wikipedia; archive.org copies inspected]**
- 1889, "A theorem on trees", *Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics* 23, 376-378, later associated with n^(n-2); Borchardt had reached the same enumeration by another method in 1860. **[A for the priority note; R for the bibliography]**
- 1890, Chapter VI of the new edition of Tait's *An Elementary Treatise on Quaternions* **[R: pool account, not re-fetched]**, folded into the 1894 stop. 1894, "Coordinates versus Quaternions", *Proceedings of the Royal Society of Edinburgh*, source of the full-moon and pocket-map passage. **[A: verified twice by raw fetch against Macfarlane]**
- Health deteriorated over the final three years, with attacks leaving him bedbound for weeks; no secure modern diagnosis exists, the century's "internal malady" being descriptive, not clinical. **[gap]** Died at Garden House about six in the evening, 26 January 1895, after a severe attack on 8 January and a collapse from 21 January. **[A: Mill Road Cemetery project; MacTutor; Britannica]**
- **Contradiction, unresolved.** The funeral date: the geography lens gives "c.30 January-1 February 1895", the afterlife lens 2 February, which is also the burial date. The Mill Road Cemetery project confirms the chapel service, about 300 mourners, the Master of Trinity, the Bishop of Durham and German and American representatives, but no separate date could be extracted. The file dates both 1895-02-02 and prints the conflict.
- Buried at Mill Road Cemetery, 2 February 1895, east of the east path in the south of the parish of St Mary the Great; the headstone inscription is recorded there. The stone disappeared sometime in the 1980s, a broken stump remains, and a photograph surfaced in 2021 showing it still lying fallen at the site. **[A: Mill Road Cemetery project; burial and the missing stone confirmed independently by Wikipedia]**
- A memorial brass with a Latin inscription was placed in Trinity College Chapel, undated in the sources and so folded into the funeral stop. **[A: MacTutor; Mill Road Cemetery project]** **[gap]**

## 9. Afterward

- Lunar crater Cayley, 4 deg 00' N, 15 deg 06' E, formally named by the IAU in 1935, the name having circulated in lunar nomenclature earlier via William Radcliffe Birt and John Lee. **[A: Wikipedia, Cayley (crater)]** The stop's coordinates are selenographic, and it says so.
- The Cayley Formation, the smooth plains **east of** the crater (the first pass said "beside"), was the primary geological target of Apollo 16. The site was chosen on 3 June 1971 to test a volcanic-origin hypothesis; the returned samples disproved it, House Rock delivering the final blow. **[A: Wikipedia, Apollo 16]** Landing site 8.97301 S, 15.50019 E. The first pass called this "four centuries removed from him"; it is less than one.
- No period translation of Cayley's standalone works into another language was found; his corpus circulated in English, through offprints and citation by continental mathematicians. **[R: an absence in the sources consulted, not a confirmed negative]** **[gap]**

## Apparatus relations for the operator

Arthur Cayley is **not** listed in `EXCEPTIONS.md`. These pool relations are not rooted in his own life. Per the standing instruction they are recorded rather than deleted, with the direction stated.

1. **Werner Heisenberg, forward.** The pool holds that Cayley's 1858 matrix memoir supplied groundwork later drawn on by Heisenberg's 1925 matrix mechanics. **[R: retrospective historiography, not a documented transaction]** `heisenberg.journey.json` exists. Naming him here would put a 1925 artifact in an 1895 block, so no campa does; the link belongs on Heisenberg's own file, pointing back.
2. **Paul Dirac, forward.** The pool notes Dirac's operator formalism as a downstream user of the linear algebra Cayley established, and that no citation of Cayley by Dirac is stated. **[R]** Same direction, same ruling, same remedy.
3. **The lunar crater and Apollo 16, forward but different in kind.** Cayley's own name migrating onto mapped ground after his death: ordinary afterlife material, carried as stops.

Two relations were **kept** in campa, because Cayley stands inside them. **Charles Babbage, indirect, backward:** George Peacock, Cayley's first-year tutor, co-founded the Cambridge Analytical Society with Babbage in 1813, and Cayley arrived into the syllabus that campaign produced. The matriculation campa names Babbage as co-founder of the society whose work reached Cayley through his own tutor: influence, not shared geography. `charles_babbage.journey.json` **[A]** **Carl Friedrich Gauss, lineage:** Cayley's elliptic and Abelian function work descends from the field Gauss founded and left unpublished. No direct contact is claimed anywhere; the 1876 campa names Gauss as the origin of the line and asserts none. `gauss.journey.json` **[R]**

Not carried, shared geography being no reason to name another traveler: Newton at Trinity in 1661; Halley's 1684 Cambridge ride against Cayley's RAS presidency; Lincoln's Inn Fields as the site of Babbage's divided brain and Franklin's print-shop neighbourhood; St Petersburg as the birthplace of Anna Pavlova and Antoinette de Saint-Leger. Boole reaches the corpus through `claude_shannon.journey.json`; he is a real Cayley correspondent from 1844 and is named in the Lincoln campa in his own right, but Shannon is not.

**A corpus gap.** James Joseph Sylvester is the most load-bearing figure in Cayley's mathematical life: corresponding by 1847, co-architect of invariant theory, and the reason Cayley crossed the Atlantic in 1882. No journey file for him exists. He is named in two campas, the relation being direct, but is not counted as an interlock.
