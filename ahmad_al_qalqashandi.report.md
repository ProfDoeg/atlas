# Ahmad al-Qalqashandi (1355/56 - 1418): research report

*Shihab al-Din Abu al-Abbas Ahmad ibn Ali ibn Ahmad al-Fazari al-Shafi'i al-Qalqashandi. Mamluk chancery secretary, katib al-darj in the Diwan al-Insha at the Cairo Citadel; author of Subh al-a'sha fi Sina'at al-Insha, fourteen volumes, completed Shawwal 814 AH, whose cryptology section carried the Arabic cipher tradition of al-Kindi, Ibn Adlan and Ibn al-Durayhim to modern scholarship.*

Legend: **[A]** = attested, source named · **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition, or single uncorroborated source. Contradictions are flagged and left standing. Gaps are stated as gaps.

A note on the evidence base, made once and applying throughout: this subject has **no reachable scholarly monograph**. Everything below rests on tertiary encyclopedic sources (English Wikipedia, Arabic Wikipedia, Marefa) plus Muslim Heritage and New World Encyclopedia. Where they disagree, the disagreement is the finding. Where they agree, they may simply be copying each other; Arabic Wikipedia and Marefa share phrasing on the chancery dates.

---

## 1. Birth and family (1355/56)

- Born in the village of **Qalqashanda**, district of Qalyub, province of Qalyubiyya, eastern Nile Delta; the nisba is the village. **[A: Arabic Wikipedia; English Wikipedia; Marefa]**
- Birth year **756 AH**, given as 1355 by Arabic sources and "1355 or 1356" by English Wikipedia. **[A]** The Hijri year straddles two Christian years; arithmetic, not a source conflict.
- Descent claimed from the Arab tribe of **Fazara**, of the Ghatafan confederation; hence al-Fazari. **[A as a report: Arabic and English Wikipedia]** No genealogical document is cited, and no source says where the claim comes from.
- Nothing else about the household is recorded: no property, no father's occupation, no anecdote of childhood. **Gap.**

**Coordinate contradiction, flagged.** The village is in none of the mapping databases consulted (GeoNames: no match). Three figures circulate: 30.1833 / 31.2333, 30.1815 / 31.2060 (labelled "Qalyub town" but 2 km south of Qalyub), and Qalyub itself at 30.19972 / 31.20528 per English Wikipedia. The journey uses the first for the village, flagged approximate, and 30.1997 / 31.2053 for Qalyub. **[R throughout]**

## 2. Education (c. 1360s - 1380s)

- Studied in **Cairo and Alexandria**, specializing in Arabic letters, rhetoric (balagha), chancery composition (insha') and Shafi'i jurisprudence. **[R: Arabic Wikipedia only]** The Alexandria component rests on one source naming no institution and no teacher. The biographers' phrase, "the great shaykhs of the age," is a formula and not information.
- **No date is attested for any part of his schooling.** The span c. 1360s-1380s is inference from his birth year and his 791 AH chancery entry; education-stop dates are placement markers, hedged in `date_confidence`. **[R]**
- **Al-Azhar is not attested for him.** The obvious Shafi'i venue in Mamluk Cairo, entered as a labelled guess about a city. **[R: general knowledge, no source about al-Qalqashandi]**

### The Ibn al-Mulaqqin problem (contradiction, unresolved)

Arabic Wikipedia names the Shafi'i jurist and hadith scholar **Ibn al-Mulaqqin** (723-804 AH / 1323-1401 CE) among his teachers. **[R]** English Wikipedia's article on Ibn al-Mulaqqin lists eight notable students (among them Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani, al-Damiri and al-Maqrizi), and **al-Qalqashandi is not among them**. Neither list is exhaustive by construction, so the two are not strictly incompatible, but the teaching relation is uncorroborated from the other side and is tagged **[R]**, not [A].

Ibn al-Mulaqqin's own teaching posts *are* attested (English Wikipedia): Shafi'i fiqh at the mausoleum-madrasa of al-Salih Najm al-Din Ayyub; preaching at the mosque of al-Hakim; a spell on hadith at the Dar al-Hadith of the Kamiliyya. **[A for Ibn al-Mulaqqin, R for al-Qalqashandi's presence at any of them.]** The journey pins all three as possible sites of contact, each labelled possible.

**Coordinate corrections applied** (earlier figures were off by 200-300 m each): Salihiyya madrasa 30.049139 / 31.261333, not 30.0518 / 31.2606; al-Hakim Mosque 30.05444 / 31.26389, not 30.0567 / 31.2625. **[A: English Wikipedia]** The Kamiliyya's Dar al-Hadith stays approximate; its exact site is **not independently verified**. **Gap.**

## 3. The chancery under Barquq (791-801 AH / 1389-1399)

- Enters the **Diwan al-Insha**, the chancery of correspondence, in 791 AH under Sultan **al-Zahir Barquq**. **[A: Arabic Wikipedia; Marefa]**
- Post: **katib al-darj**, "Scribe of the Scroll," clerk of the Mamluk chancery. **[A: English Wikipedia]** Marefa, which carries the 791 AH date, gives **no title at all**; attributing "katib al-darj" to Marefa is a citation error to avoid.
- Service runs to roughly **801 AH**. **[A: two sources]** Arabic Wikipedia says "until approximately 801 AH"; Marefa says "to the end of the reign of al-Zahir Barquq, in the year 801 AH." The terminus is not arbitrary: **Barquq died 20 June 1399**, which falls in 801 AH. Initially tagged [R], upgraded to **[A]** on two sources plus that external anchor.
- The Diwan's exact building within the Citadel is **not specified** by any source consulted. **Gap.** The interlock pin 30.0293 / 31.2599 is used throughout, inherited byte-identical from `ibn_al_durayhim.journey.json` and `al_kamil.journey.json`.

### The two-year deputyship (contradiction, unresolved)

One Arabic account gives him about **two years as na'ib**, a deputy, at some unspecified point. **[R]** Two readings are in circulation and they are not the same office:

1. **na'ib al-hukm**, deputy judge, which fits his Shafi'i legal training and is the standard Arabic phrase;
2. **deputy governor**, which fits nothing else known about him and looks like a mistranslation of the Arabic.

Working notes cited **Marefa** for this post; Marefa's entry contains **no mention of it**, covering only birth, the 791-801 AH service, the composition, the works list and the death. The only source is Arabic Wikipedia. No court, no years. Tagged **[R]**; the journey coordinates are an acknowledged placeholder, not a building.

### Barquq's mausoleum (three corrections)

The Khanqah in the Northern Cemetery, pinned at **30.0491 / 31.2788** **[A: English Wikipedia]**, is commonly and wrongly called Barquq's own construction. **Faraj commissioned and built it**, the mausoleum chamber inscribed 1400-1401 and the complex finished 1410-1411, all after Barquq's 1399 death; Barquq had only ordered it and allocated 80,000 dinars. Faraj was **thirteen** at his accession, not ten. Earlier coordinates of 30.0454 / 31.2861 were about 900 m off.

## 4. The reign of Faraj and the making of Subh al-a'sha (1399-1412)

- Chancery service continues under **an-Nasir Faraj**, whose reign is twice interrupted and shadowed by Timur's campaigns in Syria. **[A: English Wikipedia 'An-Nasir Faraj'; Arabic Wikipedia]**
- Composition of **Subh al-a'sha fi Sina'at al-Insha** begins in **805 AH** (1402-3). **[A: Arabic Wikipedia; Marefa]**
- Completed **Shawwal 814 AH**, i.e. the first months of **1412**. **[A: Arabic Wikipedia for the Hijri month; English Wikipedia for 1412]**
- Fourteen volumes in modern editions; draws heavily on **al-Umari's** Masalik al-absar and on the Diwan's archive of state correspondence. **[A: English Wikipedia; Marefa]**
- Structure: a muqaddima plus **ten maqalat** on the secretary's qualifications and ethics, the geography of the Islamic lands, the forms of correspondence and decree, ranks and honorific titles, model documents, the classification of governorships, oaths and treaties and safe-conducts, royal letters, and the postal system down to the carrier pigeons. **[A: Arabic Wikipedia]**
- The geographical volumes run from al-Andalus to China, compiled from earlier geographers and diplomatic files. **No source consulted attests travel beyond Egypt**, a silence in the record rather than a demonstrated negative. **[R for the no-travel inference; A for the compiled character of the material]**
- Cited as a source on **al-Aqsa Mosque mosaics** in Jerusalem. **[A: New World Encyclopedia]** Whether he saw them is not stated; the journey pin is the mosque, flagged as not an attested journey.

**Unresolved: the dedicatee.** 1412 is also the year Faraj is deposed and killed. Whether the finished work was dedicated to Faraj, to the caliph al-Musta'in Billah (installed as a stopgap by the Syrian emirs), or to al-Mu'ayyad Shaykh (sultan later that year) is **not resolved in any source consulted**. **Gap.**

**Faraj's death, corrected.** Defeated at Baalbek, he fled into the **citadel of Damascus**, surrendered, and on **23 May 1412** was stabbed to death by a hired assassin **in his prison cell** (English Wikipedia). Earlier phrasing had him killed "near Damascus," which is wrong in kind: he died inside the city, in custody.

## 5. The cryptology section

The part of the book with a reception history outside Arabic administrative studies.

- The section catalogues **substitution and transposition** ciphers, gives **letter-frequency tables**, and lists letters that cannot co-occur in a single Arabic word. **[A: English Wikipedia, citing David Kahn, The Codebreakers]**
- **David Kahn** cited it as "the first published discussion of the substitution and transposition of ciphers" and "the first work in human history that described cryptology, because it described both cryptography and cryptanalysis." **[A: English Wikipedia, quoting Kahn]**
- **Caveat on "polyalphabetic," flagged.** The al-Qalqashandi article, after Kahn, calls it "the first description of a polyalphabetic cipher, in which each plaintext letter is assigned more than one substitution." 'History of cryptography' calls the same technique "later called **homophonic substitution**," which is not polyalphabetic substitution in the Vigenere sense. The flat label overstates the source; the journey describes the mechanism instead.
- The material comes from **Ibn al-Durayhim** (1312-1361). English Wikipedia: "Al-Qalqashandi **quoted** the text relevant to cryptology from the work of Ibn al-Durayhim." **[A]** Muslim Heritage names the treatise **Miftah al-Kunuz fi Idah al-Marmuz**, c. 1350. **[A as reported; the date is approximate]**
- The **ninety-character minimum** for reliable frequency analysis originates with **Ibn Adlan** (1187-1268), who taught at the Salihiyya mosque in Cairo and wrote for the Ayyubid al-Ashraf Musa. The encyclopedia names Ibn al-Durayhim as its source but **does not name Ibn Adlan**, whose rule travels forward inside the quoted material. **[A: ibn_adlan.journey.json; Wikipedia 'Ibn Adlan']**
- The chain behind all of it: **al-Kindi** (c. 801-873), Risala fi Istikhraj al-Mu'amma, the earliest known systematic description of frequency analysis. **[A: Wikipedia 'Al-Kindi', 'Frequency analysis']**

### Contradiction: does Ibn al-Durayhim's treatise survive?

Three statements, from three sources, that cannot all be right:

1. English Wikipedia, 'Al-Qalqashandi': the work "was once considered lost," implying survival or rediscovery. **[A as text]**
2. English Wikipedia, 'Ibn al-Durayhim': the cryptology book "was recently discovered, but has yet to be published," and **names no location**. **[A as text]**
3. English Wikipedia, 'History of cryptography': his "writings on cryptography **have been lost**." **[A as text]**

**Left unresolved.** Two further corrections belong here:

- **The Suleymaniye claim is struck.** No source consulted places Ibn al-Durayhim's manuscript in the Suleymaniye archive in Istanbul, which is the 1987 rediscovery site of **al-Kindi's** Risala, a different text by a different author. The conflation does not appear in the journey.
- **Extent of the borrowing.** Earlier working notes claimed the sources are silent on quotation versus paraphrase. They are not: English Wikipedia says **quoted**. What they do not do is **quantify** the extent, and the journey says so.
- **Ibn al-Durayhim's death.** 762 AH / 1361 per Arabic Wikipedia (birth 712 AH / 1312); English Wikipedia gives 1359-1362. **No source consulted names his place of death.** An earlier note offered "Mosul or Cairo (disputed)": there is no dispute, only silence, and Mosul is his birthplace by nisba. The journey pins the Cairo milieu and says so.
- **Eight cipher systems** is confirmed. **[A: English Wikipedia 'History of cryptography']** A "tableau-style substitution table" is **not** described in either the Ibn al-Durayhim article or 'History of cryptography'; that detail is unsupported and is not used.

## 6. Later works, retirement, death (c. 1412-1418)

Works beyond the encyclopedia, all **undated by every source consulted** (years in the journey are ordering only, flagged in `date_confidence`):

- **Ma'athir al-Inafa fi Ma'alim al-Khilafa**, on the caliphate. **[A: Arabic Wikipedia]**
- **Nihayat al-Arab fi Ma'rifat Ansab al-Arab**, Arab genealogy. **[A: Arabic Wikipedia; Marefa]**
- **Qala'id al-Juman fi al-Ta'rif bi-Qaba'il Arab al-Zaman**, tribes of his own time. **[A: Arabic Wikipedia; Marefa]**
- **Daw' al-Subh al-Musfir wa Ghunyat al-Mustafid wa'l-Musafir**, his own abridgement. **[A: Arabic Wikipedia; Marefa]**
- **Janna al-Dawh al-Muthmir**, a second abridgement. **[R: absent from the Arabic Wikipedia works list retrieved; uncorroborated.]**
- **al-Ghiyuth al-Hawami' fi al-Fiqh**, Shafi'i jurisprudence. **[A: Arabic Wikipedia]**

Retirement: said to have withdrawn from active office in his final years while keeping his standing at court. **[R: single Arabic source, itself a WebFetch summary of a page not independently verified.]**

**Death: Cairo, 16 July 1418 / 821 AH.** **[A: Arabic Wikipedia for the full date; Marefa and English Wikipedia for 1418.]**

**The age "contradiction" dissolved, not resolved.** English Wikipedia gives 62-63, Arabic Wikipedia about 65, and earlier notes called this an unresolved conflict between birth-year traditions. It is not. Both give **756 AH**: 821 minus 756 is **65 lunar years**, about **63 solar years**. A conversion artifact, with no disagreement about his birth to leave open.

**Burial: gap.** No tomb, mausoleum, mosque or cemetery is named for him in English Wikipedia, Arabic Wikipedia, or Marefa; all three stop at the date. Unusual for a Mamluk scholar of his standing, given how thoroughly the Qarafa cemeteries are documented.

## 7. Afterlife: printing, translation, reception

- **Bulaq Press, Cairo, 1323 AH / 1905**: first (partial) printed edition. **[A: Arabic Wikipedia 'Subh al-a'sha']**
- **Oxford, 1913**: zincograph printing, three of a planned seven volumes, never completed. **[A: same]**
- **Dar al-Kutub al-Misriyya, Cairo, 1331-1338 AH / 1913-1920**: first complete fourteen-volume edition. **[A: same]**
- **Dar al-Kutub al-Ilmiyya, Beirut, 1409 AH / 1987**, ed. Shams al-Din: the reprint most cited since. **[A: same]**
- **Translations**: partial German by **Ferdinand Wustenfeld** (1879) **[A]**; French renderings by **Henri Sauvaire** and by the Belgian Jesuit **Henri Lammens** **[A that both translated; which took what is contradicted]**. Arabic Wikipedia assigns Lammens the correspondence between kings and Sauvaire the general Arabic correspondence; working notes had these swapped. **Flagged; the journey states the disagreement rather than picking a side.**
- **Ibrahim A. Al-Kadi, "The Origins of Cryptology: The Arab Contributions," Cryptologia, April 1992**: credited with making the al-Kindi to al-Qalqashandi chain visible to Western cryptologists. **[A: English Wikipedia]**
- **David Kahn**, via Muslim Heritage: "Cryptology was born among the Arabs," a judgment reached through Subh al-a'sha. **[A at one remove; the Codebreakers text was not consulted directly.]**
- **IJMES 3:1 and 3:2 (1972)**: "Christian and Jewish Religious Dignitaries in Mamluk Egypt and Syria," parts I and II. **[A: English Wikipedia.]** Note it is **two articles**, not one study.
- **El-Toudy and Abdelhamid, 2017**: selections on "Seats of Government" and "Regulations of the Kingdom." **[A that they exist; form disputed.]** English Wikipedia cites this as *Al-Masaq: Journal of the Medieval Mediterranean* (2017), very likely a citation error: the work appears to be a 2017 Routledge monograph (ISBN 9781138962422) in the **Al-Masaq book series**, not a journal article. **Not confirmable: the Routledge page returned HTTP 403.** The journey says "under the Al-Masaq imprint," true either way.
- **No monument, mausoleum, street or institution** commemorating him could be confirmed. His afterlife is textual. **[R: gap across all sources consulted]**
