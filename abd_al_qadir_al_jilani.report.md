# Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani (c. 1077-1166): research report

*Prepared 2026-08-09 for the Atlas of Journeys. Subject 0 on QUEUE.md: the man whose tomb
has been a stop inside `abdelkader.journey.json` since the atlas began, never given a
journey of his own.*

**Legend.** **[A]** = attested, source named. **[R]** = reconstruction or tradition
reported as such. Where two reachable sources disagree, the disagreement is printed and
left standing.

---

## 0. What kind of subject this is

Al-Jilani is a twelfth-century Baghdad jurist and preacher around whose grave an order of
Sufis assembled itself over the following two centuries. Almost everything usable about him
comes either from modern reference summaries, which are thin, or from devotional hagiography
written 150 to 400 years after his death. Al-Shattanawfi's *Bahjat al-Asrar* (d. 1314) and
al-Tadifi's *Qala'id al-Jawahir* (d. 1556) are the texts that made his posthumous
reputation, and neither was reachable in translation.

A second correction runs through the whole file. Reference works routinely call him the
founder of the Qadiriyya. English Wikipedia is more careful and calls him only the *eponym*.
**The scholarly position, and the one adopted here, is that al-Jilani founded no tariqa in
his lifetime; the order was organised around his tomb and his lineage by descendants in the
centuries after him. [R]** Everywhere the research pool wrote "the order he founded" it has
been rewritten to "the order that took his name."

## 1. Gilan: birth and childhood (c. 1077 - 1095)

- Born **1 Ramadan 470 AH**, given in Common Era terms as **1077 or 1078**, in the village
  of **Na'if** in the district of **Rezvanshahr**, Gilan. **[A: Wikipedia, "Abdul Qadir
  Gilani"]**
- **Coordinate correction, load-bearing.** The pool carried the birthplace at two different
  points, 37.42 / 48.86 and 37.533 / 48.95, both described as Rezvanshahr. Neither is:
  Rezvanshahr sits at **37.548, 49.136** (Wikipedia, "Rezvanshahr, Gilan"), and the pooled
  points lie 16 and 30 km inland in the Talysh foothills. The journey file uses the correct
  pair. **[A]**
- **Birthplace dispute, not resolved.** A rival tradition places the birth at "Jil" in Iraq.
  Modern historians favour Gilan, which is what the nisba al-Jilani implies. The pool
  attributed the Iraqi variant to **Ibn Taghribirdi**; that could not be traced to him in
  anything reachable, so the dispute is kept and the named chronicler dropped. **[R]**
- Parents given as **Abu Salih** and **Umm al-Khayr Fatima**; the father's name Abu Salih
  Musa appears in the genealogy prefixed to *Futuh al-Ghaib*. The father dies while the boy
  is young; he is raised by his mother, and in some accounts a maternal grandfather of Sufi
  lineage. **[R]**
- **Hagiographic:** the infant refuses his mother's milk during the daylight hours of
  Ramadan. Standard in Qadiriyya devotional literature; no primary text naming it could be
  fetched. **[R]**

## 2. The road to Baghdad (1095)

- At about **eighteen** he leaves Gilan for Baghdad. **[A: Wikipedia, "Abdul Qadir
  Gilani", which dates the journey 1095]**
- **Hagiographic, and split from the above:** his mother sews forty gold dinars into his
  coat and makes him vow to speak the truth; the caravan is robbed; he tells the truth; the
  chief repents. The pool tagged the whole item **[A]** while conceding in its own text that
  the coin and the vow are "traditional narrative embroidery." Split here: **[A]** for the
  departure and date, **[R]** for the coins, vow, and robbery.
- **Name dropped.** The pool named the bandit chief **Ahmad Badawi**, which collides with
  Ahmad al-Badawi (c. 1199-1276), the Egyptian founder of the Badawiyya, born more than
  thirty years after al-Jilani died. In the standard tellings the chief is unnamed; naming
  him manufactures a false identification, and the name is removed.
- **The location is traditional, not attested.** Hamadan (34.7983, 48.5147, verified against
  Wikipedia) lies on the standard medieval road from Gilan to Iraq and is used as the
  conventional site, flagged as such. **[R]**

## 3. The schools of Baghdad (1095 to c. 1119)

All four teachers are attested in the same reference article. No pupillage carries a date,
and none carries a named building beyond the quarter.

- Hanbali fiqh under **Abu Sa'id al-Mubarak al-Mukharrimi**, at his school near the **Bab
  al-Azaj** gate. **[A: Wikipedia, "Abdul Qadir Gilani" and "Qadiriyya"]** *Correction:* the
  pool wrote "al-Mukharrimi (also transliterated al-Makhzoomi)." Two different nisbas, not
  two spellings of one: al-Mukharrimi from the Mukharrim quarter of Baghdad, al-Makhzumi
  from the Qurayshi clan of Banu Makhzum. Rendered "also given as al-Makhzumi."
- Hanbali theology under **Abu al-Wafa Ibn Aqil**, hadith under **Abu Muhammad Ja'far
  al-Sarraj**. **[A]**
- Sufi instruction under **Abu'l-Khayr Hammad ibn Muslim al-Dabbas**. **[A]** *Correction:*
  the pool glossed al-Dabbas as "a Baghdad baker." The nisba is from *dibs*, date syrup.
- **Hagiographic:** on arrival a Baghdad shaykh sends him a cup filled to the brim, meaning
  the city is already full of holy men; he lays a rose on the surface without spilling it.
  Wikipedia's "Qadiriyya" gives this as legendary founding narrative. **[R]**

## 4. The retreat, and a contradiction (c. 1100 to 1127)

- Tradition holds he spends roughly **twenty-five years** in ascetic retreat in the deserts
  and ruins of Iraq. **[R]** No place names survive in anything reachable. **Recorded as a
  gap, not filled:** the journey file pins the stop to Baghdad and says in `date_confidence`
  that no site is recorded.
- **Dated dream.** The twenty-first discourse of *Futuh al-Ghaib* is headed with a precise
  date: the night of **Sunday, 12 Dhu'l-Hijja, AH 516** (early 1123 CE), a dream of Iblis.
  **[A: trans. Muhtar Holland, Al-Baz Publishing]** The single dated event in the window.
- **Contradiction, flagged and not resolved.** Wikipedia's "Qadiriyya" article dates the
  succession to al-Mukharrimi's school to **1119**; its "Abdul Qadir Gilani" article dates
  the return to open preaching to **1127**. Either he held the school quietly for eight
  years, or one date is imprecise. Both are printed in the journey file as separate stops.

## 5. The public career (1127-1166)

- He begins preaching openly and the crowds outgrow the room. **[A for the 1127 date; R for
  the chair set up on open ground outside the gate]**
- He **inherits and enlarges his teacher's school** at Bab al-Azaj, later known as the
  **Madrasa al-Qadiriyya**. **[A for the inheritance and expansion; R for the name]** The
  pool tagged "he established the Madrasa al-Qadiriyya" as **[A]** because Wikipedia's
  sentence reads that way; the name is retrospective, and the entry is retagged.
- Teaching pattern: **hadith and tafsir in the mornings, spiritual discipline in the
  afternoons**, students from across the Islamic world. **[A]**
- Son **Abd al-Razzaq al-Jilani** born **1134**; disciple **Shaykh Qadib al-Ban**, later
  cited in Qadiriyya transmission chains. **[A]**
- **Numbers not verifiable:** four wives; forty-nine children, twenty-seven of them sons;
  hundreds of Jewish and Christian conversions after his sermons. None could be cross-checked
  against a primary genealogical or chronicle text. **[R]**
- His reputation is said to reach political figures including **Nur al-Din Zangi**, with no
  contact dated. **[R]**
- **Descent from the Prophet** through al-Hasan ibn Ali (hence sharif / sayyid) is traced by
  genealogists; modern scholars debate whether it is authentic or later construction.
  Printed as a debate. **[R]**

## 6. The books

Attributions are stable across reference listings. None was checked against a critical
edition, and the publication history of the printed Arabic editions is a gap.

- ***Al-Ghunya li-Talibi Tariq al-Haqq***, "Sufficient Provision for Seekers of the Path of
  Truth" (Holland's standard English title, verified on Open Library, 1995). **[A]** The
  pool rendered it "Treasure for Seekers," which is not a recognised rendering, and is
  dropped.
- ***Futuh al-Ghaib***, "Revelations of the Unseen," a collection of discourses. **[A]** Two
  English translations were read this session: Muhtar Holland (Al-Baz) and Maulana
  Aftab-ud-Din Ahmad (Sh. Muhammad Ashraf, Lahore).
- ***Al-Fath al-Rabbani***, "The Sublime Revelation," and *Al-Fuyudat al-Rabbaniya*. **[A as
  listings]**
- ***Kitab Sirr al-Asrar wa Mazhar al-Anwar***, "The Secret of Secrets." **[A]** *Title
  collision, flagged, not a claim about him:* the pseudo-Aristotelian *Secretum Secretorum*,
  an alleged letter from Aristotle to Alexander, also circulated in Arabic as *Sirr
  al-Asrar*. Two unrelated books, and no source consulted disambiguates them. **[R]**
- ***Jila' al-Khatir***, "Purification of the Heart." **Attribution unsettled. [R]**
  Wikipedia lists it under al-Jilani; its article on **Abd al-Razzaq Gilani** lists
  *Jala-ul-Khawatir (The Removal of Cares)* as the **son's** work. The usual reading is that
  these are the father's sermons compiled by the son; the journey file carries the caveat
  rather than choosing.

## 7. Sayings

- **"My foot is on the neck of every saint."** The claim of spiritual precedence tied to the
  title Ghawth al-A'zam. **[R: tradition, not a text he wrote]**
  - *Source upgraded.* The pool cited a Wikipedia footnote to a devotional press (Fatoohi,
    Adam Publishers, p. 24). A better chain exists: **Annemarie Schimmel, *Mystical
    Dimensions of Islam*, ch. 5, p. 247**, citing **Jami, *Nafahat al-uns*, p. 527**.
  - *Wording corrected:* singular **neck**, not "necks." Wikipedia has "necks."
  - *Verified negative:* the full texts of **both** *Futuh al-Ghaib* translations were
    searched for "foot is on", "neck of every", "necks of every". **Zero hits in either.**
    The saying is absent from the discourses and belongs to the Ghawth al-A'zam legend.
- **Al-Nawawi's praise** ("we have never known anyone more dignified than Baghdad's Shaykh
  Muhyi al-Din Abd al-Qadir al-Gilani, the Shaykh of Shafi'is and Hanbalis in Baghdad").
  **[R: attributed, provenance broken]** The words are real and not invented: verbatim in
  English Wikipedia, corroborated in substance by Arabic Wikipedia.
  - **The *Bustan al-'Arifin* attribution does not survive checking.** The complete English
    translation of al-Nawawi's *Bustan al-'Arifin* ("The Garden of the Gnostics", al-Faruq
    International; archive.org/details/garden_of_gnostics) was read through the closing
    colophon. **Al-Jilani is not mentioned in that text at all.**
  - Wikipedia's own footnote does **not** cite *Bustan al-'Arifin*; it cites a 2019 Arabic
    edition of *Futuh al-Ghayb* ed. Jamal al-Din Faleh al-Kilani, p. 8. The *Bustan*
    attribution is Wikipedia's unsourced prose, treated here as a later devotional
    attribution of uncertain provenance. **The quote is kept in this report and out of the
    journey file's `quote` fields**, where the caveat cannot travel with it.
- Discourse quotations in the journey file are verbatim from *Futuh al-Ghaib* in the two
  named translations. **[A as text]**

## 8. Death and burial (1166)

- Dies **11 Rabi' al-Thani 561 AH**, given in Common Era terms as **1166**. **[A]**
- **Month contradiction, flagged.** The pool's geography lens rendered the death as
  "1166-05." 11 Rabi' al-Thani 561 AH converts to roughly mid-February 1166, and no reachable
  source states a Common Era month. The journey file dates the stop **1166-01-01** and says
  in `date_confidence` that the AH date is attested and the CE month is not.
- **Age arithmetic corrected.** The pool wrote "aged about eighty-nine by the lunar
  calendar." 470 AH to 561 AH is **91 lunar years**; eighty-nine is the solar figure.
  Rendered as about ninety-one lunar, roughly eighty-eight or eighty-nine solar.
- Buried at his own school at **Bab al-Azaj**, which afterward takes the name **Bab
  al-Sheikh**. **[A]**
- **Coordinate reconciliation.** Three lenses gave three points for one grave. The mausoleum
  is at **33.3364, 44.4076** (Wikipedia, "Hadhrat al-Qadiriyya"); the afterlife lens carried
  33.3406, 44.3903, about 1.7 km off toward the Tigris. All are conformed to the first,
  which is byte-identical to the pin already in `abdelkader.journey.json`. **[A]**
- His son **Abd al-Razzaq** succeeds him as shaykh. **[A: Wikipedia, "Qadiriyya"]**
- **Downgraded.** The pool claimed **[A]** that Abd al-Razzaq "compiles and publishes a
  hagiography of his father" which established the reputation. The only support is one
  unreferenced sentence; the dedicated article on Abd al-Razzaq records no such work. The
  hagiographies that made the reputation are **al-Shattanawfi's *Bahjat al-Asrar*** (d. 1314)
  and **al-Tadifi's *Qala'id al-Jawahir*** (d. 1556), both well over a century after the
  son's death in 1207. Retagged **[R]**.

## 9. The shrine and the order after him

- **1258.** The Qadiriyya survives the Mongol sack of Baghdad as a functioning institution.
  **[A: Wikipedia, "Qadiriyya"]**
- **By the END of the fifteenth century** the order has distinct sub-branches in **Morocco,
  Spain, Turkey, India, Ethiopia, Somalia, and present-day Mali**. **[A, verified verbatim]**
  The pool wrote "by the fifteenth century"; corrected.
- **1508.** The shrine is destroyed after the Safavid capture of Baghdad under **Shah Ismail
  I**, whose own article names al-Jilani's tomb among the Sunni sites destroyed. **[A]**
- **1535.** **Suleiman the Magnificent has a dome built over the shrine, which still
  stands.** **[A]** Two corrections: the Ottoman capture of Baghdad was **1534** (the eyalet
  was established in 1535), so 1535 is the building work, not the conquest; and the
  mausoleum record specifies a **dome**, not a rebuilding of shrine and mosque.
- **Qadiri Harari suborder:** shrines in and around **Harar, Ethiopia** (9.3111, 42.1258)
  and at Borama, Somaliland. **[A]** Undated in anything reachable.
- **China.** **Khwaja Abdullah** enters China in **1674** and preaches until his death in
  **1689**; his student **Qi Jingyi Hilal al-Din** (1656-1719) roots Qadiri Sufism there and
  is buried at **Linxia**, which becomes the Chinese centre. **[A]** The pool compressed this
  so that Linxia's centrality appeared to come from Khwaja Abdullah rather than Qi Jingyi's
  tomb (the Da Gongbei shrine); and Linxia is at **35.6047, 103.2422**, about 3 km east of
  the pool's 35.6008, 103.2081.
- **Gyarvi Sharif**, "the Eleventh," his death anniversary of 11 Rabi' al-Thani, is observed
  annually worldwide. **[A]** No first-attested date for its origin exists: **a gap**.
- **Muhtar Holland's** translations through Al-Baz Publishing form the standard Anglophone
  corpus. **[R]** al-baz.com returned 404; *Sufficient Provision* is confirmed on Open
  Library.

## 10. The interlock: the boy from Mascara

This is why the subject sat at position 0 in QUEUE.md.

- **Emir Abdelkader** (1808-1883) is **named for al-Jilani**, a name already common in his
  family tree; his father Sidi Muhyi al-Din al-Hasani is **muqaddam of the Qadiriyya zawiya
  of Oued El-Hammam** near Mascara. On the pilgrimage begun in **1825** he travels to
  Damascus and Baghdad and venerates the graves of noted Muslims. **[A: Wikipedia,
  "Abdelkader El Djezairi"; abdelkader.journey.json]**
- **Geographic error corrected.** The pool's afterlife lens read the source as placing the
  graves of **both** Ibn Arabi and al-Jilani in Baghdad. The source reads: "travelling to
  Damascus and Baghdad, where he visited the graves of noted Muslims such as ibn Arabi and
  Abdul Qadir Gilani." Two cities, two tombs. **Ibn Arabi is buried in the Salihiyya quarter
  of Damascus.**
- **Date contradiction, flagged and not resolved.** The pool's afterlife lens dates the visit
  **1825**; its chronology and geography lenses date it **1826**, one to 1826-02. Wikipedia
  documents only the 1825 departure and gives no year for the Baghdad leg.
  `abdelkader.journey.json` carries 1826-02-01 as traditional. The journey file keeps
  **1826-01-01** for continuity, drops the month, and records the disagreement in
  `date_confidence`.
- **Existing atlas traces.** `junayd_al_baghdadi.journey.json` already carries a stop,
  "Baghdad, the Algerian at the Jilani shrine," cross-citing `abdelkader.journey.json`.
  Outside QUEUE.md and those two files, `census_real_persons_2026-08-02.md` has no
  independent mention of Jilani or the Qadiriyya. **[R: absence noted]**

## 11. Other atlas travellers on the same ground

Baghdad is the densest pin in the atlas. Most of these are chronological adjacency, not
contact, and are stated as such.

- **`hallaj`** (executed near Bab Khurasan, 922), **`junayd_al_baghdadi`** (d. 910, buried
  at Shunuziyya, and the chain the Qadiriyya runs back through), **`ibrahim_ibn_adham`** (a
  disputed Baghdad grave): the martyr-and-tomb landscape already in place. **[A]**
- **`ibn_arabi`**: twelve days in Baghdad in **1205**. The tombs the sources name him at are
  Junayd's and Hallaj's; they do not say he visited al-Jilani's. **[A for the visit; the
  omission is a gap]**
- **`baha_ud_din_walad`** and **`rumi`** (the old Baghdad graves, **1219**);
  **`ibn_battuta`** (the pontoon bridge and the tomb circuit, **1327**);
  **`abu_yazid_al_bistami`** and **`attar`** (the Kaaba circuit and the martyr-cult).
  **[A]**
- **`aristotle`**: translated into Arabic at the Bayt al-Hikma under al-Ma'mun two centuries
  before al-Jilani arrives; and the pseudo-Aristotelian *Sirr al-Asrar* collides with his
  book of that title. **[A for the translations; R for the collision]**
- **`ziryab`**, **`charlemagne`**, **`maimonides`**: all three reach Baghdad only as a source
  of learning or an embassy, not as a crossing. **[A as context]**
- **`bernard_clairvaux`** (1090-1153): a near-exact contemporary at the other end of the
  Mediterranean world. **No contact exists in any source; the pairing is inferred from dates
  alone. [R]** Kept in this report and out of the journey file.
- **`abdelkader`**: see section 10. The only real interlock.
