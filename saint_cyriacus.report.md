# Saint Cyriacus of Rome: research report

*2026-08-08. Cyriacus (Greek Kyriakos, Italian Ciriaco, German Cyriakus, French Cyriaque), deacon and martyr of Rome, feast 8 August, sixth name in the Franconian sequence of the Fourteen Holy Helpers. Queued at QUEUE.md line 160; his only existing atlas stop sits inside `catorce_santos_ayudantes.journey.json`.*

**Legend.** **[A]** = attested, source named. **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition, hagiographic legend. Contradictions are flagged and left standing. Coordinates are those used in `saint_cyriacus.journey.json`; departures from atlas coordinates are stated. "The pool" means the research pool as gathered, before verification.

---

## 0. What is actually known

The documented core is one sentence:

> "Of the Saint Cyriacus who, together with Saints Largus and Smaragdus and others (of whom Crescentianus, Memmia and Juliana are mentioned in the Roman Martyrology) is venerated on 8 August, all that is known with certainty, apart from their names and the fact of their martyrdom, is that they were buried at the seventh milestone of the Via Ostiensis on that date."
> **[A]** en.wikipedia, "Cyriacus", citing *Calendarium Romanum* (1969), p. 133.

Everything in sections 2 through 5 is legend built on that. The floor is: **names, martyrdom, a burial at a milestone, a date.** The pool's claim that the cult is "one of the best-attested among the Fourteen Holy Helpers" is contradicted by the source cited for it, and its "station church in Rome" does not exist (section 6).

---

## 1. Which Cyriacus

**[A]** At least 27 distinct saints named Cyriacus or Kyriakos exist in Christian tradition; seven are in the Roman Martyrology. This dossier is built by exclusion as much as by attestation. Kept out, each having contaminated at least one modern account of the deacon:

- **[A] Judas Cyriacus**, finder of the True Cross, patron of Ancona, died c. 360, feast 4 May (Catholic) / 28 October (Orthodox). The pool gave the Orthodox feast as 14 April, and called him a "fourth-century bishop associated with Jerusalem," reproducing the conflation it warns against; en.wikipedia distinguishes him from the legendary Bishop Judah Kyriakos of Jerusalem (died 133).
- **[A] Cyricus (Kyrikos) of Tarsus**, child martyr, the figure behind the Majdal Yaba inscription.
- **[A] Cyriacus the Anchorite**, Palestinian hermit, died 556, dedicatee of the ruin at Al-Fasayil.
- **[A] Quiriacus of Ostia**, died c. 235, feast 23 August, under whose name the Cyriacus dedication at Santa Maria in Via Lata is filed in that church's own article.
- **[R] Cyriacus of 8 February**, dead at Rome with nothing else surviving (catholicsaints.info).

**[A]** The Greek *kyriakos* means "belonging to the lord." en.wikipedia on the Roman tituli: "The 'tituli' were commonly named after their patron, often a lay patron in the early centuries: 'Cyriac' in Greek signifies simply 'patron.'" That supplies an alternative explanation for the Roman church of the name, and for parts of the legend possibly reverse-engineered from it.

## 2. Origins and the Baths, c. 280 to 303

**[R]** English tradition: "Cyriacus was a Roman nobleman who converted to Christianity as an adult," renounced his wealth, gave it to the poor. No parish, no year, no family, no parents; the birth pin is a **forced placeholder**, labelled so. **[R]** German tradition: a physician and exorcist trained at **Alexandria** (de.wikipedia). **Contradiction flagged.** The two origins are incompatible in kind: one gives him wealth to renounce, the other a technique to practise. No source reconciles them, and the exorcisms he is famous for fit the Alexandrian version better, which is reason to suspect either of being retrofitted.

**[R]** The diaconate: "um 300" under **Marcellinus** (de.wikipedia); by **Marcellus I**, pope only 308 to 309, i.e. *after* the usual year of death (it.wikipedia); or no diaconate at all, since en.wikipedia never calls him a deacon. The pool's date of 296 appears in no source. **[R]** "He spent the rest of his life ministering to the slaves who worked in the Baths of Diocletian" (en.wikipedia). Two embellishments are **not** in the sources and were struck: that he *baptized* them, and that they were *conscripts* rather than convicts. The date 298 likewise appears nowhere; the Baths were built 298 to 306.

**[A as text]** The prison-relief episode survives in the Orthodox synaxarion, in the OCA's modern English rendering: "A certain rich Christian, Thrason, sent food and clothing to the prisoners through the Christians Sisinius, Cyriacus, Smaragdus and Largus." Attested as *text* only, a modern rendering of a Greek account rather than the ancient passio. Three further verbatim passages from that page, all about companions in the same narrative, are carried into the journey as quotes.

## 3. The edict year, 303

**[A]** The first edict of the Great Persecution goes up at Nicomedia on 23 February 303: churches razed, scriptures surrendered, Christians stripped of rank and legal standing. Attested in `constantino_el_grande.journey.json`; Constantine, aged thirty, is at court that week. **[R]** The link from that edict to Cyriacus's death in Rome the same year is an **inference** from shared year and shared administration. No source states it.

## 4. The two exorcisms

**[R]** **Artemia** (Artemisia), daughter of Diocletian, exorcised and converted; her mother **Serena**, the emperor's wife, converts with her. Wikipedia flags "chronological impossibilities" in this strand without enumerating them. **[R]** Diocletian gives Cyriacus a house, in which he sets up a chapel (de.wikipedia). The pool attributed this to the Golden Legend and asserted the house stood on the site of the later church of San Ciriaco. Both corrected: the detail is de.wikipedia's, and de.wikipedia does **not** identify the house with the titulus. Against that identification stands the *kyriakos* etymology of section 1.

**[R]** **Jobia (Iobia)**, daughter of a Persian king, exorcised on a journey east; the royal household converts. No source names the city, so the journey pins **Ctesiphon (33.0959, 44.5806)** as a stated reconstruction pin.

**Hard contradiction.** The pool called the Persian king one who "cannot be securely dated," which understates it to misrepresentation. en.wikipedia names **Shapur I** (241 to 272) and says the episode "is impossible, because Shapur died thirty years before the reputed exorcism and because he had only one daughter named Adur-Anahid," then proposes **Narseh** (293 to 303) or **Hormizd II** (303 to 309) as substitutes: two patches to keep the story upright. The journey states it so.

## 5. Arrest, execution, grave

**[R]** Return from Persia dated **304**, execution dated **303**: impossible in sequence, and no teller appears to have noticed. The journey dates the return 0303-07-15 with the contradiction in `date_confidence`. **[R]** Re-arrest under **Maximian** (en.wikipedia), or **Galerius** (OCA); place unrecorded. **[A as text]** OCA: "He gave orders to drag Cyriacus behind his chariot stripped, bloodied, and in chains, to be shamed and ridiculed by the crowds." **[R]** Boiling oil before sentencing: de.wikipedia only.

**Manner of death, contradiction.** Latin tradition: **beheaded** on the Via Salaria with Largus and Smaragdus. OCA: "Saints Cyriacus, Smaragdus, Largus, and another prisoner, Crescentian, **died under torture**," while in the same paragraph Sisinius and Saturninus are beheaded. The two churches that keep him do not keep the same death.

**Year, contradiction.** 303 (en.wikipedia); **16 March 306** (it.wikipedia); and a de.wikipedia article whose lead says "† um 303" while its Personendaten block says "STERBEDATUM: um 309." The pool presented the 309 as a distinct German tradition from a disambiguation page: wrong on both counts. It is a metadata inconsistency inside one article, and the genuine rival date is **306**.

**Companions.** The Roman Martyrology names only **Crescentianus, Memmia and Juliana** alongside Largus and Smaragdus; the roster of roughly twenty comes from **Alban Butler**. The pool attached Cyriacides and Donata to the Martyrology; corrected.

**Execution coordinate.** The stop keeps the canonical Via Salaria pin **41.928, 12.509** for atlas consistency, although Porta Salaria stood at **41.9089, 12.4953** and a third pool value (41.9166, 12.4869) falls inside Villa Borghese, on no part of the road. No source gives the milestone.

**[A] The grave.** Burial at the **seventh milestone of the Via Ostiensis**, 8 August. The apparent conflict between an execution northeast of Rome and a burial southwest of it **is resolved by the sources themselves**, and an earlier draft was wrong to carry it as open: it.wikipedia has the bodies "trasferiti dalla via Salaria al settimo miglio della via Ostiense da papa Marcello I." A translation, not an original grave, so 8 August is a **burial date, not a death date**. Pool-internal coordinate clash: **41.8062, 12.3803** (roughly the 7.6th Roman mile, used) against **41.8218, 12.4308** (roughly the fifth).

## 6. The Roman titulus and Santa Maria in Via Lata

**[A]** A titulus **"Sanctus Ciriacus in Thermis Diocletiani"** is attested by **494**, Marcianus its cardinal priest under Gelasius I. Suppressed **1477** by Sixtus IV for Saints Ciro e Giulitta; restored **1493** by Alexander VI as S. Ciriaco; suppressed definitively **1587** by Sixtus V. **No church of the title survives in Rome.**

**[R]** Relics to **Santa Maria in Via Lata** (41.898154, 12.481300): en.wikipedia phrases it as a claim, "It is claimed his relics were moved to Santa Maria in Via Lata in Rome," with no date. The year 800 in the pool appears in no source and is carried in the journey as a stated placeholder; the tag was corrected from [A] to [R]. **[A]** In that church: "In the chapel to the left of the apse, is a Madonna with child and Saints Cyriac and Catherine by Giovanni Odazzi." **[A, flagged]** But the same article files its Cyriacus dedication under **Quiriacus of Ostia**: either the relics lie here under the wrong label or another saint's shrine has been absorbed, and this is not adjudicated.

**Dropped for want of a source:** a convent called "Santa Ciriaco" beside that church, unmentioned in its article, and the related claim that Gero modelled Gernrode on it.

## 7. The relics go north

**[A] 847, Neuhausen near Worms** (49.6444, 8.3524, corrected from the pool's 49.6519, 8.3742). Bishop Samuel of Worms fetches relics from Rome; the Cyriakusstift is built for them. Lorsch tradition, corroborated by de.wikipedia, "Cyriakusstift (Worms)", which also supplies the ending the pool recorded as unknown: the complex served as a lazaretto and was burned by French Revolutionary troops in the 1790s.

**[A] 963, Gernrode** (51.7228, 11.1467; the afterlife lens gave 51.7233, 11.1414). Convent founded **959**, church dedicated to Mary and Peter; "Gero returned from a trip to Rome with a valuable relic of Saint Cyriacus and the church was dedicated to this saint in **963**." The pool's "around 950" for the relic was struck: it would precede the foundation it re-dedicates.

**[A] 974, Altorf Abbey, Bas-Rhin** (48.5219, 7.5328, corrected from the pool's 48.5325, 7.6339, about 7.4 km too far east). Founded by Hugues IV of Nordgau and his wife Helwilde under a different dedication (fr.wikipedia). In the **mid-eleventh century** Pope **Leo IX** consecrates the altar and "offrit des reliques dont le bras de saint Cyriaque"; Cyriacus becomes principal patron and the abbey takes his name. The reliquary bust has a **twelfth-century wooden core**, plating from the **first half of the thirteenth**, restored 1883 to 1884; the pool dated it thirteenth-century flat.

**Non-contradiction, previously mis-flagged.** The pool read fr.wikipedia's **Leo IX** against de.wikipedia's **Leo X** as a 460-year discrepancy. They are two distinct gifts: Leo IX gives the arm, Leo X later distributes further portions into Alsace. Both plausibly true. The pool's claim that the abbey "took his name" at a translation dated 800 is wrong twice: Altorf was founded 974 and renamed some 250 years later.

## 8. The company of fourteen

**[A]** The devotion spreads in the **Rhineland during the Black Death**. Regensburg pin 49.0134, 12.1016, inherited byte-identical. Cyriacus's brief: **against temptation on the deathbed, diseases of the eye, and demonic possession.**

**[A]** The sequence closes as a fixed, ordered list by about 1400 to 1500 across Regensburg, Bamberg, Würzburg and Nuremberg, Cyriacus named in it. Nuremberg pin **49.4534, 11.0775**, inherited byte-identical from both `saint_acacius.journey.json` and `catorce_santos_ayudantes.journey.json`. *Note:* the interlock lens reported 49.4521, 11.0767; both files give 49.4534, 11.0775, and the files win.

**[R] 1446-07-28, Vierzehnheiligen** (50.1156, 11.0544, inherited byte-identical from `san_cristobal.journey.json`). Hermann Leicht sees fourteen children who ask for a chapel. The vision as recorded does not count them off by name; Cyriacus's presence is inferred from later company membership, following exactly the caveat that file applies to Christopher. **[A]** Modern reference works count him among the roughly half of the Fourteen taken to be historical rather than purely legendary.

## 10. The calendar

**[A] 8 August**, Catholic: a **Semidouble** in the Tridentine calendar, lowered to **Simple** in 1955, to a **Commemoration** in 1962. "They are not included in the 1970 reformed Calendar of the Roman Rite," surviving in the **Roman Martyrology** only. The pool's attribution to the "General Roman Calendar" was corrected. **[A] 7 June**, Eastern Orthodox; the pool's parenthetical "28 May, Julian" is wrong, since 7 June New Style is 25 May Old Style at the current offset, and is dropped. **[A] 15 July**, Church of the East.

**[R]** The 1969 reform drops Barbara, Catherine of Alexandria, Christopher and Margaret of Antioch; `catorce_santos_ayudantes.journey.json` states "ten of the fourteen are untouched." Cyriacus is one of the ten **by arithmetic**, not by name in any source.

## Contradictions carried, not adjudicated

All flagged in place above, none resolved. **Facts:** origin (Roman patrician vs. Alexandria-trained physician); ordaining pope (Marcellinus c. 300 vs. Marcellus I 308-309 vs. no diaconate at all); year of death (303 vs. 16 March 306 vs. "um 309" against "um 303" inside one de.wikipedia article); manner of death (beheading vs. death under torture, the latter inconsistent within its own paragraph); persecutor at the re-arrest (Maximian vs. Galerius); the Persian king (Shapur I, named and demonstrably impossible, with Narseh and Hormizd II offered as repairs); the return/death sequence (return 304, death 303); the identity of Santa Maria in Via Lata's Cyriacus (the deacon of 8 August, or Quiriacus of Ostia of 23 August); and Majdal Yaba, where en.wikipedia contradicts en.wikipedia over one inscription.

**Coordinates**, each detailed in place above: the seventh milestone (two values), Gernrode (two), Altorf (three), and the Via Salaria (three).

## Gaps, stated as gaps

- **No primary text was read.** The Golden Legend chapter "The Life of S. Ciriacus" (Caxton 1483, ed. F. S. Ellis) was confirmed present at sourcebooks.fordham.edu, but its text could not be extracted with the tools available this session. It remains the most important untapped source here. The medieval *Passio* was reached only through summaries, and the Roman Martyrology, *Calendarium Romanum* (1969) and Alban Butler only as summarized by en.wikipedia.
- **No archaeology.** No excavation or topographical report for the seventh milestone was located; the burial site is known only from calendar tradition. No source gives the milestone or the stretch of road for the execution.
- **No birth data at all.** Year, quarter, parents, all absent. The 280 pin is invented and labelled so.
- **The Cyriakusstift relics after the 1790s fire.** No source says what became of them.
- **Kaaba and Temple Mount.** The brief asked these hub pins be checked. No file in the corpus places Cyriacus, or any of the Fourteen, at either; the nearest Diocletianic-era Near East geography in the atlas is Nicomedia, Byzantium and Baalbek. **No stop was invented.**
- **Buenos Aires.** Also asked for, also absent. The only Fourteen-adjacent pin there is the barrio of San Cristóbal, which `san_cristobal.journey.json` itself calls "nominal: no relic, no legend, no crossing, only the name." **No stop was invented.**
