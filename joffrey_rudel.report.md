# Jaufre Rudel, prince of Blaye: research report

*Atlas of Journeys, working file. Subject slug: `joffrey_rudel`. Compiled 2026-08-07 from the verified research pool (Sonnet gather, Opus adversarial verification). Every claim below carries a tag. Contradictions are printed as contradictions.*

## On the name, and the method

The man is `Joffrey Rudel` in the atlas queue, `Jaufre Rudel` in English scholarship, `Jaufré Rudel` in French, `Giaufre Rudel` in Petrarch, `Geoffroi Rudel` in Mistral. The Occitan rubrics give **Jaufres Rudels de Blaia**. This report uses Jaufre Rudel.

**Legend.** **[A]** = attested, source named. **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition, conjecture, or a hedge printed as such in the source. The vida is treated throughout as a thirteenth-century literary artifact, never as testimony.

The pool rests almost entirely on tertiary encyclopedia articles in five languages plus what those articles quote of the primary Occitan material. Two consequences frame everything below. **No medieval document naming the poet was reached in this pass**: the 1106 Blaye charter, the disputed 1125 La Sauve-Majeure charter, the chansonniers BnF fr. 854 and fr. 12473, Marcabru in the Gaunt/Harvey/Paterson edition, and Jeanroy's *Les Chansons de Jaufre Rudel* (1915) are all cited at second hand. And **the biography is a reconstruction on top of a legend on top of six or seven poems**: the cansos survive, the vida was written roughly seventy years later to explain them, the modern life was written to explain the vida. Almost every narrative statement about Rudel is [R].

## 1. Blaye and the Rudel line, 1047 to 1127

**[R, fr.wikipedia "Jaufré Rudel"]** The troubadour's *arrière-grand-père*, a Jaufre called "Rudel," was a younger son of Geoffroi (Jaufre) "Taillefer," count of Angoulême, whose reign ran 1031 to 1047 and who died in 1047. The pool originally tagged this [A] and read 1047 as a year Geoffroi "lives"; both were wrong. 1047 is a death year, and a genealogical descent printed in a tertiary source is reconstruction, not attestation.

**[A, en.wikipedia "Jaufre Rudel", citing charter evidence]** A **Girard I Rudel** is titled *prince* of Blaye in a charter of **1106**. This is the single hardest documentary anchor in the whole dossier. The title itself is said to have been first borne by Girard's own father around **1090** **[A, same source]**.

**[R]** Girard is described as the troubadour's father by some accounts and as "probably" his father by others; at least one reading makes him grandfather. The sources do not settle it and this report does not either.

**[R, fr.wikipedia]** Jaufre Rudel is born at Blaye on the Gironde estuary, *vers 1110*; other figures in circulation are 1100 and 1113. **[R, en.wikipedia]** English reference works decline the decade entirely and give only "early to mid-twelfth century."

**[R, en.wikipedia "Wulgrin II of Angoulême"]** Suzerainty over Blaye is contested between the counts of Poitou and the counts of Angoulême. Wulgrin II of Angoulême (reigned 1120 to 1140, died 16 November 1140) "reclaimed this fortress from William X of Aquitaine in **1127**" and *probably* vested Jaufre with it. The pool's original 1130 date was wrong; 1127 is the anchor year. Note also the numbering trap: "William VIII of Poitou," which the pool used, is the Poitiers numbering for **William X of Aquitaine**, who succeeded in 1126. A reader taking "William VIII of Poitou" at face value lands in 1058 to 1086, eighty years off.

**[R, flagged as flimsy in the source itself]** A rival hypothesis makes Wulgrin II not the investing lord but Jaufre's father. Recorded, not adjudicated.

**[R, unverified]** The pool carried a **1125 charter of La Sauve-Majeure** naming "a Jaufre Rudel de Blaye" as witness. Verification could not corroborate it anywhere reachable; the charter consistently cited in the literature is the 1106 one naming Girard. Held as **unsourced** until a cartulary reference is produced. Abbey coordinates corrected to 44.7686, -0.3117.

## 2. The poet and the songs

**[R]** The pool placed Rudel at the court of **Guillaume IX of Aquitaine** alongside Marcabru and Cercamon "beginning in the 1120s." This is chronologically impossible and has been struck. William IX died 10 February 1126; Cercamon's floruit is c.1135 to 1145 and he served the courts of **William X**; Marcabru's crusade material dates from the 1140s. The defensible milieu is **William X's court in the 1130s and 1140s**, and even that is [R]: no charter and no poem places Rudel at any ducal court.

**[A, en. and fr.wikipedia]** **Six or seven cansos survive**; editors disagree on the count, and **four** carry musical notation. The corpus is described by its own editors as difficult to interpret.

**[A]** Among them, *Lanquan li jorn son lonc en may* and *Quan lo rius de la fontana* develop **amor de lonh**, love for a woman never seen.

Verbatim Occitan seen in the fetched articles, quoted here as transmitted, not as critically established text:

- "Jaufres Rudels de Blaia si fo mout gentils om, princes de Blaia" (vida, opening; fr.wikipedia). Italian Wikipedia gives the same line with *hom* for *om* and continues "Et enamoret se de la comtessa de Tripol..." Ordinary manuscript spelling variance; both attest the same formula.
- "Lanquan li jorn son lonc e May, / M'es belhs dous chans d'auzelh de lonh, / E quan mi suy partitz de lay, / Remembra'm d'un'amor de lonh." (it.wikipedia)
- "Quan lo rius de la fontana / S'esclarzis, si cum far sol" (fr.wikipedia)
- "D'quest amor suy cossiros, / Vellan e pueys somphnan dormen" (fr.wikipedia, from *Quan lo rossinhols el folhos*). **Textual flag:** *somphnan* looks like a transcription slip for *sompnhan* / *sompnan*, "dreaming." Not silently corrected here.

**[A, en.wikipedia]** *Lanquan li jorn son lonc en mai* is credited with influencing **Walther von der Vogelweide's** crusade song, the amor-de-lonh theme crossing into Middle High German lyric.

## 3. Hodierna, the woman the legend later named

The vida names the beloved only as *la comtessa de Tripol*, without a personal name. Everything in this section is about a real woman whom later readers attached to a story that does not name her.

**[A, en.wikipedia "Hodierna of Tripoli"]** Hodierna is the third daughter of Baldwin of Bourcq (later Baldwin II of Jerusalem) and Morphia of Melitene, born c.1115 to 1117. **Correction carried:** the pool placed her birth in the Kingdom of Jerusalem. Baldwin was count of **Edessa** from 1100 and became king only on 2 April 1118. Melisende, Alice and Hodierna were all born in the Edessan period; only Ioveta was born in the purple. Place: **County of Edessa**.

**[R]** Betrothal to Raymond II of Tripoli is "very possible" around **1122** (Kevin Lewis). The pool's 1127 is off by five years.

**[R]** Lewis presumes the **marriage delayed to c.1132**, Hodierna being too young earlier. The pool's 1130 is not supported. **[A]** The first hard attestation of her as countess is **December 1138**, at the court of Fulk and Melisende at Acre.

**[A]** She is **regent of Tripoli 1152 to 1155** for her minor son Raymond III.

**[R]** She dies **on 21 December**; the cited article states flatly that "the year and cause of her death are not recorded." Melisende died 11 September 1161, roughly three months before. c.1161 or c.1162 is a reconstruction, and the pool's [A] tag on "1162" was overstated. The interval to Melisende's death is about three months, not a year.

**[R, chronologically impossible as an identification]** Mistral's 1878 *Lou Tresor dóu Felibrige*, quoted in English translation on en.wikipedia, gives: "Mélisende, Mélissande or Mélissène, countess of Tripoli, daughter of Aimeri de Lusignan, loved by the troubadour Geoffroi Rudel." This substitutes Hodierna's **daughter** Melisende, born after Rudel's presumed death, for Hodierna herself. It is a nineteenth-century Provençal local tradition, not evidence about the 1140s, and it is what Rostand later dramatized. Mistral's own wording was not checked against the original; the accented form above is en.wikipedia's, and the pool's unaccented version was corrupt.

## 4. The crusade: one attestation, two irreconcilable routes

**[A, corrected date]** Bernard of Clairvaux preaches at **Vézelay on 31 March 1146**; Louis VII and Eleanor of Aquitaine take the cross there. The army departs Metz in **June 1147**. The pool folded both into "1147," a year late for the vow.

**[R, source line refuted]** The one roughly contemporary, non-legendary tie between a real Rudel and a crusade is **Marcabru's reference to a Jaufre Rudel "oltra mar."** The pool attributed this to *Pax in nomine Domini* and tagged it [A]. Wrong: that is Marcabru's **Reconquista** song, the one calling Spain a *lavador*, and its theatre is Iberia. Standard editions assign the Rudel tornada to **"Cortesamen vuoill comensar"** (PC 293.15), but no primary text was reachable to confirm it. The *fact* of the reference is repeated everywhere; the *poem* must be cited by number from Gaunt/Harvey/Paterson or not at all. Downgraded to **[R]**, title dropped.

**[R, conditional in the source]** French Wikipedia's embarkation account is written entirely in the conditional: *"Il se serait dans ce cas embarqué à Port-de-Bouc"*, landing *"le 13 avril 1148 à Saint-Jean-d'Acre."* The pool printed both as [A]. Both are hypotheses. Worse, the pool attached them to "the Aquitanian contingent accompanying Louis VII and Eleanor," which cannot be: Louis and Eleanor marched **overland** from Metz through Hungary and Constantinople, fought at Ephesus (24 December 1147) and Mount Cadmus (6 January 1148), took ship only at Attalia, and reached Antioch on 19 March 1148. The southern-French **sea** force is the separate Provençal contingent under Alphonse Jourdain of Toulouse, which waited until August. A man cannot be in both.

**[R]** Guillaume VI Taillefer, count of Angoulême 1140 to 1179, was alive in 1147, but **no reachable source places him on the Second Crusade**. The pool's "likely traveling with his cousin" is unsupported. Incidental, not evidence: Port-de-Bouc's first documentary attestation as *Portus de Boc* is 1147.

**[R, anachronism struck]** The pool called Limassol "the customary last major landfall" for western ships. In 1148 Cyprus was Byzantine and Limassol a minor town; its prosperity dates from Richard the Lionheart's conquest of 1191. The leg survives in the journey only as labelled hindsight. Coordinates corrected to 34.6747, 33.0442.

**[A, coordinates corrected]** The Mount Cadmus disaster is **6 January 1148**, near Laodicea on the Lycus. The pool's 37.7667, 29.6167 lies about 45 km east of the pass, in open plain. Use Honaz Dağı, approximately 37.68, 29.25.

**[R]** Death: English works give "in or after 1147"; German "um 1147"; French and Italian "vers 1148." **The pool's "one French tradition placing his death as late as vers 1181" could not be found in French Wikipedia or anywhere else reachable, and has been struck from both entries that carried it.** As printed it manufactured a disagreement the sources do not contain. The genuine spread is 1147 to 1148.

## 5. Tripoli, and what the vida says happened there

Everything in this section is **[R]**, from a single thirteenth-century literary source.

The vida: Rudel falls in love with the countess of Tripoli on the report of pilgrims returning from Antioch, without ever seeing her; he takes the cross to reach her; he falls mortally ill at sea; he is carried ashore at Tripoli taken for dead; the countess comes to his bed and he dies in her arms; she buries him honorably *en la maion del Temple* and takes the veil the same day.

The Spanish rendering of the death scene, as printed on es.wikipedia and corrected for the four accents the pool had stripped (the first of which changes the sense, *"Él enfermó"*, he fell ill, versus *"El enfermo"*, the sick man):

> "Él enfermó estando en la nave, y fue llevado a Trípoli, a un albergue y tenido por muerto. Se lo hicieron saber a la Condesa; y ella fue a él, hasta su lecho y lo tomó entre sus brazos."

The translator is uncredited on the page; cite it as the Spanish Wikipedia rendering, not as an authoritative translation.

**[R, explicit in en.wikipedia]** English-language scholarship flags the story as likely fictional, "derived from the enigmatic nature of Rudel's verse." **No contemporary chronicle of the Second Crusade corroborates the meeting, the deathbed, or the burial.** The Templars did hold property in Tripoli through the twelfth century, but no building, tomb, or inscription there has ever been linked to Rudel. The convent the countess is said to have entered is not named by any source: a gap in the text, not a detail suppressed.

**Topography, corrected.** The Citadel of Raymond de Saint-Gilles (Qal'at Sanjil) is at **34.4334, 35.8445**. The pool's 34.4372, 35.8494 sits about 600 m off the hill to the northeast, and it was repeated verbatim three times, so the error propagated. El Mina, the working harbor below the hill town where an arriving ship would actually make land, is 34.4486, 35.8128.

## 6. The vida and the afterlife

**[A/R]** The **vida** is anonymous in the manuscripts and generally attributed to **Uc de Saint Circ** (floruit 1217 to 1253), who compiled the vidas and razos late in life at the da Romano court in the Veneto. German Wikipedia dates his biographies to around 1225. The defensible bracket is **c.1225 to 1250**; the pool's flat "1250" is the far end of it.

**[A]** The poems and the vida survive only in Occitan chansonniers copied in Italy and southern France roughly a century after Rudel's death, notably **BnF fr. 854 and fr. 12473**. Nothing in his hand survives. **Location corrected:** these are held at the BnF **Richelieu** site, approximately 48.8677, 2.3378, not the François-Mitterrand site the pool pinned 4.5 km away. **[A, fr.wikipedia]** Chansonnier provençal K (fr. 12473) preserves a miniature of Rudel on horseback.

**[R, quotation refused]** Petrarch places Rudel among Love's captives in the *Triumphus Cupidinis*. The pool printed as Petrarch's verse the string "Rudel di Blaia, che navigò a vela per venir a morir al suo amore." That is prose, not terza rima, appears in no edition reached, and reads as a commentator's gloss. Standard editions give roughly "Giaufre Rudel ch'usò la vela e 'l remo / a cercar la sua morte," unchecked against a critical edition. **The quoted string is not printed as Petrarch in the journey file.** Arquà Petrarca corrected to 45.2700, 11.7183.

**[A]** Nineteenth-century Romantics retell or invoke the legend: **Uhland, Heine, Browning, Carducci, Swinburne** ("The Triumph of Time," "The Death of Rudel"). **[A]** **Edmond Rostand's** *La Princesse lointaine*, written for **Sarah Bernhardt**, premieres at the **Théâtre de la Renaissance, Paris, 5 April 1895**, heroine renamed Melisandre after Mistral; Bernhardt plays it in **London** the same year and **George Bernard Shaw** reviews it unfavorably. **[A]** Nino Berrini's *Rambaldo di Vaqueiras: I Monferrato* (1922) is influenced by Rostand. **[A]** Saariaho and Maalouf's opera ***L'Amour de loin*** premieres at the **Salzburg Festival, 2000**; Santa Fe, 2002.

**[A]** Editions: **Jeanroy (1915)**; bilingual Occitan-French by **Yves Leclair and Roy Rosenstein (2011)**.

**[A, fr.wikipedia]** Streets bear his name in Blaye, Bruges, Montpellier and Toulouse; a lycée in Blaye is named for him; in **2013** a micronation called **Helianthis** was founded in homage to the seigneurial principality of Blaye and to Rudel. **[A]** Francis Cabrel names him in the 2020 song "Rockstars du Moyen-Âge."

## Contradictions, printed and not resolved

1. **Death date.** en "in or after 1147" / de "um 1147" / fr and it "vers 1148." The further "vers 1181" the pool reported does not exist in the cited source and is struck.
2. **Filiation.** Girard I Rudel is Jaufre's father in some accounts, "probably" his father in others, grandfather in a third reading. A separate hypothesis, called flimsy by the source that reports it, makes Wulgrin II of Angoulême the father.
3. **Route east.** The vida requires a sea voyage and a shipboard illness. Louis VII's Aquitanian host went overland. Both are in circulation; nothing decides between them for Rudel personally, and they are not reconcilable.
4. **The countess.** Hodierna of Tripoli (the standard identification) versus her daughter Melisende (Mistral 1878, Rostand 1895). The daughter is chronologically impossible; the tradition is real and is the one that reached the modern stage.
5. **Number of cansos.** Six or seven, by editor. **Birth.** vers 1110 / 1100 / 1113 / "early to mid-twelfth century."
6. **The Marcabru poem.** The reference is universally repeated; the poem carrying it is not securely identified in any source reached.
7. **The 1125 La Sauve-Majeure charter.** Reported in the pool, corroborated nowhere. Held as unsourced.

## Gaps, stated as gaps

- **No grave, relic, or contemporary monument to Rudel exists at Blaye.** The present citadel is a Vauban fortress built five centuries after him. The town's memory of its prince rests on the legend alone.
- **No trace of the Templar burial at Tripoli**, and no independent record of it beyond the vida.
- **The countess's convent is unnamed** in every source.
- **Jerusalem is absent from the vida entirely**, which is a hole in the itinerary rather than evidence about it.
- **No document places Rudel at any court**, and **no contemporary chronicle mentions him at all** apart from the Marcabru tornada.
