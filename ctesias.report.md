# Ctesias of Cnidus, research report

*2026-08-27. Greek physician at the Achaemenid court of Artaxerxes II; author of the lost `Persica` (23 books) and `Indica` (1 book). Slug `ctesias`. Companion file `ctesias.journey.json`: 9 segments, 39 stops, 16 quotes.*

Legend: **[A]** = attested, source named · **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition, or modern conjecture.

Ctesias survives in no continuous text of his own. Everything reaches us through people who quoted, epitomised, or attacked him: Xenophon (a contemporary who marched against his employer), Diodorus, Plutarch, Photius, plus Strabo, the Suda, Lucian, Aristotle and Pliny in fragments. Every "Ctesias says" below really means "X says that Ctesias says."

---

## 1. Cnidus: birth, family, training (c.444/434 to c.415 BC)

**Birthplace [A].** Strabo 14.2.15, fetched, names Cnidus and the two works, *Assyrica* and *Persica*. That single line is the whole ancient statement of origin.

**Birth year [R], with a contradiction inside the research pool itself.** No ancient source gives a year, and three modern figures circulate. **444 to 434 BC** is livius.org's range, reckoned back from an assumed capture around 414 and labelled an estimate by its own source. **c.441 BC** is associated with T. S. Brown; the pool cited it to Lenfant's BMCR review 2012.04.27, and **that citation does not hold**, since the review never mentions 441 BC, a birth date, or a Brown chronology of the birth. It says only that the Tissaphernes captivity hypothesis "is attributed to Stronk 2004 (p. 14), although it was first made by Brown (1978, p. 4 and 7-10)." The 441 figure could not be traced to any fetchable source. **c.450 BC** appeared in the pool's chronology and contradicted its own next entry; dropped. The file carries `-0440-01-01` as an ordering device inside the livius range and asserts no birth year.

**Father's name [A, but doubled].** The Suda calls him **Ctesiarchus**; Lucian's preface calls him **Ctesiochus**. No ancient source reconciles the two, and neither does this file.

**Medical training [R].** Cnidus's fame for physicians called Asclepiads is attested (livius.org), and that Ctesias practised medicine is attested three ways (Strabo, Xenophon, Diodorus). That he trained in a *Cnidian school* rival to the *Coan school* of Hippocrates is standard modern reconstruction, in no ancient text fetched. No teacher named, no case book.

**Galen's testimonia [R, weaker than the pool presented them].** Two remarks reach us through Galen: a criticism of the Hippocratic technique for reducing a dislocated hip, and the note that hellebore, lethal in his father's generation, had become safer because physicians learned to prepare it. Both are relayed in the modern literature (Schmitt; Nichols 2011). Neither could be traced to a fetchable Galen text; the Iranica article returned HTTP 403 and livius.org mentions neither Galen nor hip dislocation.

**Gap.** Nothing is recorded about childhood, mother, siblings, languages, property, office, or travel before Persian service.

## 2. Into the King's service (c.415 or c.405 BC)

**Capture [A as bare fact, R in every particular].** Diodorus 2.32.4: Ctesias was "made prisoner and then retained by Artaxerxes because of his medical knowledge." He names no battle and no captor. (The pool said "no battle, date, or captor," which overstates the silence: Diodorus does situate him by Cyrus's expedition and does name Artaxerxes.)

**The Pissuthnes and Tissaphernes hypothesis [R].** That Ctesias crossed into Persian territory around 415/414 through Pissuthnes' revolt and was taken by Tissaphernes is **T. S. Brown's hypothesis of 1978**, defended in part by Stronk. Lenfant confirms the attribution to Brown but not the hypothesis. No primary grounding found.

**The chronology contradiction, flagged and not resolved.** The largest open question in the subject:

| | Long chronology | Short chronology |
|---|---|---|
| Arrival at court | c.415/414 BC | c.405/404 BC, at Artaxerxes II's accession |
| Basis | "Seventeen years" counted back from the Persica's last event | The reign of Artaxerxes II; "seventeen" exaggerated or corrupt |
| Held by | Brown, Stronk | Schmitt, and several handbook summaries |

The file pins the disputed capture at Sardis under the long chronology and the "seventeen years" stop at 405 under the short one, and each confidence field states that the two cannot both stand.

**Sardis pin [R].** No source names a place of capture. Sardis is the satrapal seat of Lydia and the point a prisoner from that coast would pass through; declared as inference in the stop.

## 3. The court: Susa, Babylon, possibly Ecbatana (c.405 to 401 BC)

**The office [A].** Strabo calls him physician to Artaxerxes without saying which; the "II" is a modern supplement. Plutarch, *Artaxerxes* 1 and 2, has him attending the king's wife, mother and children, and argues from that access that whatever farrago he wrote, he cannot have been ignorant of the name of the king he served. The access is the one thing no ancient writer disputes.

**The royal records claim [A as claim, untested as fact].** Diodorus 2.32.4: Ctesias says he investigated each king from royal records the Persians kept by law. (The pool's transcription read "a certain law of their kept"; corrected against the Loeb.) No Achaemenid archive of the kind has been recovered, and Diodorus had not seen one either.

**Ecbatana [R, the weakest stop in the file].** The pool sourced this circularly to "the dossier's chronological table" while conceding that no ancient text places Ctesias there. Kept because the mobility of the court is the material condition of the career, with the confidence field saying outright that it rests on the seasonal pattern and nothing else. It can be cut cleanly.

## 4. Cunaxa, 3 September 401 BC

**The battle [A].** About 70 km north of Babylon on the left bank of the Euphrates; the site has never been securely identified archaeologically. The pool's 33.3/43.95 is about 12 km off the standard placement, corrected to **33.325, 44.08**.

**The wound [A, the strongest evidence in the file].** Xenophon, *Anabasis* 1.8.26-27, verbatim: "This, according to the statement of Ctesias the surgeon, who further states that he himself healed the wound." Xenophon marched with the Ten Thousand, gives Ctesias no credit anywhere else, and had no motive to flatter him. It is the only episode of Ctesias's life attested by a hostile contemporary on the ground.

**The hill [A as Plutarch's report of Ctesias's claim; R as fact].** *Artaxerxes* **11**, not chs. 9-11 as the pool had it. That Ctesias was among the few companions on the hill is his own claim relayed by Plutarch, not Plutarch's statement, and the file says so. Plutarch closes the chapter by calling the account a blunt sword, long in killing Cyrus but killing him at last.

**Casualty figures [A], chapter corrected.** *Artaxerxes* **13**, not 11: the figure reported to Artaxerxes was nine thousand, and Ctesias thought the slain no fewer than twenty thousand. The same chapter sets Xenophon and Deinon against Ctesias and lets the three disagree.

**The rewards [A], re-sourced.** The pool credited modern secondary literature. It is Plutarch at *Artaxerxes* **14**: the king "gave generous rewards to Ctesias and others," specifying neither nature nor value. The only property the record names anywhere is the sword of storm-repelling Indian iron in the *Indica* [R], a marvel inside his own narrative rather than a documented holding.

## 5. Clearchus (401 BC)

All of it rests on Plutarch, *Artaxerxes* **18**, drawing on the *Persica*, with one independent confirmation from Photius.

- **The comb, the extra provisions, and the knife hidden in salt pork [A as claims].** Ctesias says he assisted Clearchus at Parysatis's urging and proposed the knife so the general could take his own death. Plutarch repeats it and vouches for nothing; whether the knife was sent, arrived, or was used is nowhere recorded.
- **The signet ring [A].** Engraved with dancing Caryatids, a token for Clearchus's people at Sparta. Whether it was ever delivered is not recorded anywhere; the file ends that stop on the gap.
- **The palm grove [A, corrected].** The pool had Parysatis secretly arranging the planting. Plutarch has no such thing: a blast of wind heaps earth over the body, dates fall on the mound, and a grove springs up. Parysatis removed. The pool also called the story unattested outside the Ctesian tradition, which is too weak the other way: **Photius, cod. 72 section 72.49, independently itemises "Clearchus's tomb with palm trees."**

**Plutarch's standing charge [A].** *Artaxerxes* 13 calls Ctesias prodigiously ambitious, partial to Sparta and to Clearchus, and always allowing considerable space in his narrative for himself, arguing that Xenophon would have named him at the post-Cunaxa parley had he been there, and names Phalinus instead. The file carries the charge unresolved.

## 6. Parysatis's reprisals (401 to 400 BC)

Chapters corrected; the pool ran them together into "around ch. 11-17." The Carian credited with the killing blow is *Artaxerxes* **14**; Mithridates at the banquet is **15**; Stateira, poisoned with "a little knife smeared with poison on one side," is **19**. All three are [A] as Plutarch's use of the *Persica* and [R] as to location, since Plutarch names no residence for any of them. The file hedges Stateira between Susa and Babylon as the sources do, chooses Susa, and says so.

## 7. The Indica (gathered at court; composed after 397 BC)

**The governing negative [A].** No source shows Ctesias travelling to India; Schmitt states it outright and Prusa agrees. The material comes from Persian and Indian informants at the court and from objects, animals and visitors arriving there.

**Consequence for the map, a method decision the operator should see.** The pool proposed seven Indian and inner-Asian pins (Indus, Cynocephali mountains, martikhora, horned ass, griffin mountains, Nineveh, Bactra). None are places Ctesias went. `EXCEPTIONS.md` forbids staging anyone at a place they never went, and `strabo.journey.json` sets the precedent by putting Strabo's Megasthenes, Amazons and Pytheas material at his own Amaseia pin. **All Indica and Assyrian material here therefore sits at the Susa court pin or at Cnidus, and no Indian coordinate appears in the file at all.** A deliberate reading of the rule, not an oversight.

Content carried (Cynocephali, martikhora, horned wild ass, griffins) is all [A] as text in Photius's epitome and [R] as to substance. One correction: the pool dropped part of the tribute figure. Section 40 reads 260 talents of amber, the same in purple dye, **and a thousand additional talents of amber**. Restored.

## 8. The western errand (399 to 397 BC)

**The Conon and Evagoras correspondence [A].** Photius, cod. 72 section 72.50: Conon's letter names Zeno, Polycritus or Ctesias as possible bearers, and Ctesias adds on his own account a request to be sent to the coast himself. Plutarch treats the discrepancy with Ctesias's own version, in which the king assigns the duty unprompted, as self-promotion. The same section lists the letter of Ctesias to Evagoras about reconciliation with Anaxagoras, prince of the Cyprians, Evagoras's agreement to pay tribute, and royal letters and gifts carried both ways.

**"Commander of the fleet" [dropped].** The pool's crawl claimed Pharnabazus appointed Ctesias commander of the fleet. The fetched Photius text gives naval command to **Conon**. Not carried in any form.

**Rhodes [contradiction, left open].** Section 72.51 records "proceedings against the Spartan ambassadors at Rhodes, and their acquittal." Some modern accounts turn this into a personal trial of Ctesias for pro-Persian service; the epitome's wording does not support that and no source fetched confirms it. The file states both readings and resolves neither.

**Cnidus and Sparta [A].** Section 72.50: "the visit of Ctesias to Cnidus, his native city, and to Sparta." **Structural correction:** the pool said this closed the narrative "before the work's remaining material on the earlier Assyrian and Median kings." Photius says the opposite. Books 1 to 6 treat Assyria before the Persian empire's foundation and Persian affairs begin only in book 7, so the Assyrian material precedes the Persian narrative. Clause deleted.

**Dascylium [dropped as a stop].** The pool's own text conceded that "no source places Ctesias's own body at Dascylium." Cut on the standing rule; the appointment it stood for is carried in the Salamis stop's prose.

## 9. Cnidus again, the books, the silence (397 BC onward)

**Return and composition [A, then R].** livius.org's fragment overview puts "Ctesias returns to Cnidus" last, at section 75. The *Persica* ends at Artaxerxes II's eighth regnal year, 398/397 BC, which is the standard argument that the work was written after the return.

**Twenty-three books [A, twice, and the pool's contradiction dissolved].** Photius gives 23; so does the Suda (Adler kappa 2521, fetched). The pool's claim that "the Suda tradition sometimes gives 24" is wrong about that entry and has been removed. Further: Strabo names the works as *Assyrica* and *Persica*, so he cannot be cited for the "23 books plus one Indica" framing. Re-sourced to Photius and the Suda.

**Against Herodotus [A].** Ctesias positioned the *Persica* against Herodotus, accusing him of lying. Ancient critics turned the charge back on him: Plutarch's "perfect farrago," Photius's reservations about the Indian material, Lucian's placement of "Ctesias the Cnidian, Herodotus, and many other" among the punished liars.

**The Assyrian books [A as content, R as history].** Ninus and Nineveh, Semiramis building Babylon and marching on India, the siege of Bactra. Diodorus takes the whole of it from him and cites him by name at 2.7.1. Out of these books comes the Greek succession-of-empires scheme. Modern Assyriology validates neither the dynasty nor the chronology.

**Battle of Cnidus, 394 BC [A as event, R as anything to do with him].** Conon broke Spartan naval power off Ctesias's own cape. livius.org suggests he "may have witnessed" it; conjecture, and the file leaves it open.

**Death [R].** Nothing attested: no year, place, manner, tomb, epitaph, cult, bust, coin or gem. livius.org offers an explicitly labelled guess of **374 to 364 BC**. The pool's chronology lens asserted that no later event is offered anywhere, contradicting its own afterlife lens. Corrected here and in the file, which carries the range and calls it arithmetic.

## Apparatus relations for the operator

Ctesias is **not** listed in `EXCEPTIONS.md`. The relations below are **not rooted in his own life** and, per the standing instruction, have not been silently deleted. **All run FORWARD**, later people engaging with a dead man's book, which `EXCEPTIONS.md` says is not licensable in general and never by an agent's own judgement; adding Ctesias to the list would license none of them, since it covers backward application only. All are kept as **sources and `suggested_refs` on his own stops**, none staged as a scene.

- **Aristotle** repeating the martikhora and horned ass at the Lyceum with reservations [R]: named in the martikhora campa, refs on that stop. The Lyceum visit is **Aristotle's** stop, on `aristotle.journey.json`.
- **Plutarch** at Chaeronea, **Photius** at Constantinople and **Lucian** at Samosata [A]: cited or quoted across the file as sources, never staged; each city is that writer's own pin. **Pliny** at Rome [R]: not carried; belongs on a Pliny file, which does not exist.
- **Ratramnus of Corbie's** *Epistola de Cynocephalis*, answering Rimbert [A]: not carried at all, since no citation chain runs back to Ctesias and the transmission goes as much through Pliny and Solinus. Already on `ratramnus_of_corbie.journey.json` and `rimbert.journey.json`. **Vincent of Beauvais and Marco Polo** relocating the dog-heads [A]: not carried.
- **Saint Christopher's** dog-headed iconography [R], not even derivative: not carried. The pool's own afterlife lens traces the Byzantine legend to confusion of *Cananeus* with *caninus* and assigns it to San Cristobal's file, which already carries the cynocephali material. **Oxyrhynchus and P.Oxy. 2330** [A as excavation, R as attribution]: not carried, the attribution disputed on dialect grounds.
- **The Lion of Knidos** [A]: kept as a `suggested_ref` on the Battle of Cnidus stop, flagged there and here as **emphatically not Ctesias's tomb**. Spotted by Richard Popplewell Pullan, not Newton as the pool had it; the Conon cenotaph theory is speculative, with "no artefact or inscription" to settle it.

**Recommendation:** nothing here needs Ctesias added to the list.

## Interlocks named in campa

Two, both on a direct textual relation rather than shared geography. **`cyrus`**: Ctesias wrote substantively about Cyrus the Great in the *Persica*, correcting Herodotus on his reign. **`aristotle`**: Aristotle read the *Indica* and repeated its zoology with reservations, tagged [R] in `date_confidence`.

**Deliberately not named**: `alexander`, `homer`, `benjamin_de_tudela`, `strabo`, `san_cristobal`, `rimbert`, `ratramnus_of_corbie`, where the connection is shared geography, a shared later tradition, or a one-line notice. **Herodotus** is named in the prose as Ctesias's declared rival, but `herodotus.journey.json` does not exist and he is not counted as an interlock.

**Gaps in the corpus.** No journey file exists for Xenophon, Artaxerxes II, Parysatis, Cyrus the Younger, Clearchus, Conon, or Evagoras I, every one of Ctesias's central attested relationships, so the Cunaxa and Clearchus segments stand without a sibling stop to interlock against.
