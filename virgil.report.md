# Virgil (Publius Vergilius Maro), 70 BC to 19 BC: research report

*Prepared 2026-08-02 for the Atlas of Journeys. Legend: **[A]** = attested, source named; **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition, or inference. Contradictions are flagged where found and left unresolved.*

## The evidentiary situation, stated first

Everything biographical about Virgil rests on one late text. The *Vita Vergilii* transmitted under the name of Aelius Donatus (fourth century CE) preserves what is generally taken to be a lost life by Suetonius, itself written a century and a half after the poet's death. Jerome's *Chronicon* corroborates the birth and death years and little else. There is no letter collection, no Virgilian *Ad Atticum*. Three contemporaries mention him in verse and are worth more than any anecdote: Horace (*Satires* 1.5, *Odes* 1.3), Propertius (*Elegies* 2.34), and Ovid, who says only that he saw him (*Tristia* 4.10).

The standard tag for a Virgil life-event is therefore **[R]**, not **[A]**, even when every handbook repeats it. The journey file marks the hedge in `date_confidence`, never in the campa prose.

## Birth and family, 70 BC

- Born **15 October 70 BC** at **Andes**, a village in the territory of Mantua, Cisalpine Gaul. **[A: Vita Donati; Jerome, *Chronicon*]**
- Father: a man of modest means, in the *Vita* at first the hired man of a certain **Magius**, an attendant on the magistrates, who **became Magius's son-in-law** through diligence, that is, married Magius's daughter. He increased the property by buying woodland and keeping bees. Mother: **Magia Polla**. **[A: Suetonius/Donatus, *Vita Vergilii* 1]**
  - *Correction logged.* The gathering draft had the father marrying Magius himself, a misreading of *gener*. Struck.
- Two omens, which the gathering draft conflated. The **mother dreams she gives birth to a laurel branch**, which on touching earth takes root and grows at once to full size. Separately, a **poplar branch** planted at the birthplace grows so fast that it equals poplars planted long before; it becomes known as Virgil's tree and is venerated by pregnant and newly delivered women. **[R: Vita Donati 2-3]**
- Siblings: **Silo**, who died a child (*impubes*); **Flaccus**, who died already grown (*iam adultum*) and whom Virgil is said to mourn as Daphnis; and a half-brother, **Valerius Proculus**, son of the mother's second marriage and later Virgil's co-heir. **[A: Vita Donati 14, 37]**
  - *Correction logged.* The draft claimed both brothers died before adulthood, one at seventeen. The *Vita* says the opposite of Flaccus, and no source gives an age of seventeen.

### Contradiction: where exactly is Andes

Three coordinates circulated in the pool for one village: 45.1667, 10.7833 (chronology lens, actually Mantua city); 45.1281, 10.8419 (geography lens, 2.4 km NE of Pietole); 45.1141, 10.8183 (it.wikipedia, the hamlet itself). The journey file uses **45.1141, 10.8183** at every Andes stop for internal consistency, which does not resolve the underlying problem: the identification of ancient Andes with modern **Pietole** (comune of Virgilio) is a medieval and early-modern pilgrimage tradition carried by Dante, Boccaccio, and Goethe, supported by a Celtic etymology (*and-*, the bends of the Mincio) and by a place called Pietole Vecchia. **It is not archaeologically confirmed.** **[R: it.wikipedia, *Pietole*]** No ancient source names the farm.

## Education, c. 65 to 48 BC

- Boyhood at **Cremona** until the toga virilis: *initia aetatis Cremonae egit usque ad virilem togam*. **[A: Vita Donati 6]**
- The draft claim that he began formal education at about age five appears in no ancient Life. Dropped as a modern inference. **[gap]**
- He assumes the **toga virilis at fifteen**, and tradition places this on the very day **Lucretius died**. **[R: Vita Donati 6; Jerome, *Chronicon*]** Modern scholars read this as a constructed literary succession rather than a coincidence anyone recorded at the time. The journey file keeps the claim and marks the hedge as `traditional`.
- Then **Milan**, then **Rome**, studying rhetoric and, per the *Vita*, medicine and above all mathematics (*medicinae quoque et maxime mathematicae*). **[R: Vita Donati 6-7]**
  - *Correction logged.* The draft listed **law** among his studies and tagged the entry **[A]**. Law does not belong: the single court case below is an episode, not a curriculum. The tag is overstated, since the curriculum rests solely on the late-antique *Vita*. Downgraded to **[R]**.
- He pleads **one case** in the courts and abandons oratory, his speech being slow and hesitant. **[R: Vita Donati]**
- He turns to **Epicurean philosophy under Siro**, near Naples, and the bay becomes his home for the rest of his life. **[A: Vita Donati; *Catalepton* in the Appendix Vergiliana; Philodemus papyri on Siro's circle]** The Appendix attributions are themselves contested, which weakens the corroboration.

### Contradiction: Naples or Herculaneum

Some modern scholars locate Siro's school at **Herculaneum**, arguing from the Epicurean library at the Villa of the Papyri. **No ancient text names Herculaneum in connection with Virgil.** Both are carried in the journey file, the Herculaneum stop marked `scholarly reconstruction`. Not adjudicated.

## The confiscations, 42 to 40 BC

- After **Philippi (42 BC)**, land around Cremona and Mantua is marked for veteran settlement. **[A: general Roman historical record]**
- The **family farm at Andes is confiscated**. **[R: Vita Donati; inferred from *Eclogues* 1 and 9]** The autobiographical reading of the *Eclogues* is debated by modern scholars and is the sole evidentiary basis for the episode's detail.
- Virgil is said to have **escaped an armed veteran by swimming the Mincio**. **[R: Vita Donati; scholia to *Eclogue* 9]** The named aggressor varies between manuscripts (Arrius, Clodius). No corroboration exists.
- **The farm is restored.** Here the sources openly disagree and are left unreconciled: *Vita Donati* credits **Octavian**; other accounts credit **Asinius Pollio**; Servius on *Eclogue* 9 and Donatus 19-21 also name **Cornelius Gallus** and **Alfenus Varus**; some modern scholars doubt the farm was ever fully returned. *Eclogue* 1's **deus nobis haec otia fecit** is generally read as Octavian, which is part of the argument rather than independent of it. **[A for the line; R for the identification]**

## The Eclogues and the circle of Maecenas, 42 to 37 BC

- The ten pastorals, modeled on Theocritus, composed roughly **42 to 39 BC**. **[A: ancient consensus; internal dating]** Publication is usually placed **39 to 38 BC**, exact date contested. **[R]**
- **Eclogue 4**, the Messianic Eclogue, dated by its reference to the consulship of **Asinius Pollio (40 BC)** and read from late antiquity onward as prophesying the birth of Christ. **[A: the text itself]**
- Entry into the circle of **Maecenas**, with Horace and Varius Rufus, by around 38 to 37 BC. **[A: ancient tradition; Horace's own poems]** That Virgil held property on the **Esquiline** is an inference from Horace, *Satires* 1.5 and 1.6, not a statement in any ancient text. **[R]**

## The journey to Brundisium, 37 BC

This is the one stretch of Virgil's life narrated by an eyewitness in the party. Horace, *Satires* 1.5, is the source for the whole segment, and the only reason the atlas can plot Virgil day by day anywhere.

- **Sinuessa**: Plotius, Varius, and Virgil meet the party. *Plotius et Varius Sinuessae Vergiliusque occurrunt* (1.5.40); *animae qualis neque candidiores terra tulit* (1.5.41-42). **[A]**
  - *Coordinate corrected.* The pool gave 41.2333, 13.8917, about 10 km north of the site. Wikipedia places ancient Sinuessa at 41.1414, 13.8528, in the comune of Sessa Aurunca. The journey file uses the corrected pin.
- **Caudium**: the party lodges at **Cocceius's well-stocked villa** (*villa... plenissima*) above the taverns, and the evening's entertainment is the flyting of the two buffoons, *Sarmenti scurrae pugnam Messique Cicirri* (1.5.51-70). **[A]**
  - *Correction logged, a large one.* The draft described a wretched inn, smoky green wood, and unripe pears. The villa is explicitly well-stocked; the green wood is at Trivicum, thirty lines later; the word for pears does not occur anywhere in *Satires* 1.5.
- **Beneventum**: the anxious host nearly burns his house down roasting thin thrushes (1.5.71-76). **[A]**
- **Trivicum**: a night of smoke from wet green branches, *udos cum foliis ramos urente camino* (1.5.79-85). **[A]**
  - *Coordinate corrected.* Pool gave 41.0464, 15.3011; Wikipedia places Trevico at 41.0486, 15.2336, about 6 km west.
- **Canusium**: gritty bread, no water worth the name, founded by valiant Diomedes (1.5.87-93). **[A]**
- **Rubi and Barium**: a long wet day's march, then the fishing town (1.5.94-97). **[A]**
- **Egnatia**: incense that catches on the temple threshold without flame, which Horace refuses to believe (1.5.97-101). **[A]**
- **Brundisium**: *Brundisium longae finis chartaeque viaeque est* (1.5.104). **[A]**

Capua, carried as a stop in the pool, is dropped from the journey file: Horace names it only in passing.

## The Georgics, 37 to 29 BC

- Four books on farming, dedicated to Maecenas, composed chiefly at **Naples**. **[A: Vita Donati; the poem's own dedication]**
- **Nola**: the town refuses to run its water to Virgil's nearby estate, and he strikes the city's name out of the poem, leaving *ora* at *Georgics* 2.224-225. **[R]**
  - *Source corrected.* The pool attributed this to Servius. The primary source is **Aulus Gellius, *Noctes Atticae* 6.20**, who reports the anecdote and is himself unsure whether it is true; Servius *ad Georg.* 2.225 is secondary. No manuscript preserves the alleged original reading, so the story is widely held apocryphal.
- **Atella, 29 BC**: on Octavian's return from Actium, Virgil reads the *Georgics* aloud to him over four successive days, Maecenas taking over when the poet's voice fails. **[A: Vita Donati 27]**

## The Aeneid, 29 to 19 BC

- Eleven years on the epic, chiefly at **Naples**, drafting in prose outline before versifying, with stretches in Campania and Sicily. **[A: Vita Donati 22-24]**
- **Cumae**: that Virgil visited the Sibyl's cave while writing Book 6 is inferred from the poem's topographic precision. **No ancient source states it.** **[R]**
- **Rome, the Palatine, c. 23 BC**: he reads books of the unfinished poem, including the sixth, to Augustus and Octavia; at the lines naming her dead son Marcellus she is said to faint, and to reward the poet per verse. **[R: Vita Donati 32]** Many scholars treat both the fainting and the sum as embellishment on a core historical reading.
- **Marcellus** died at **Baiae in 23 BC aged nineteen**, born 42 BC. **[A]** *Correction logged: the draft said twenty.*

## The last voyage and the death, 19 BC

- Sets out for **Greece and Asia**, meaning to spend three years revising away from Rome. **[A: Vita Donati 35]** Horace's *Odes* 1.3, the propempticon to the ship carrying Virgil, belongs to this departure or an earlier one; the ode's dating is unsettled and the journey file does not assert it.
- At **Athens** he meets Augustus returning from the East and abandons the eastern itinerary. **[R: Vita Donati 35]**
- At **Megara**, visiting the ruined town under a fierce sun, he falls ill. **[A: Vita Donati 35]**
- Dies at **Brundisium on 21 September 19 BC**, the *Aeneid* unrevised, in his fifty-first year. **[A: Vita Donati 35-36; Jerome, *Chronicon*]**
- On his deathbed he asks for the manuscript to be **burned**; Varius refuses; he calls for his own book-boxes and is not brought them. **[R: Vita Donati; Aulus Gellius]** The authenticity of the wish is disputed. It is a deathbed scene reported by nobody who was there.
- **Augustus overrides the request** and instructs **Lucius Varius Rufus** and **Plotius Tucca** to publish with minimal change and no additions, which is why the half-lines survive. **[A: Vita Donati]**
- Buried at **Naples**, beside the road to Puteoli at the second milestone. **[A for the burial; R for the structure now shown]** The tomb in the Parco Vergiliano a Piedigrotta is a Roman columbarium of uncertain original occupant; the identification rests on unbroken but unverifiable local tradition.
- The **epitaph**, *Mantua me genuit, Calabri rapuere, tenet nunc Parthenope: cecini pascua, rura, duces*, is transmitted only through the biographical tradition. **[R]** The Donatus Latin text could not be fetched in this session (repeated 404s), so the wording follows the standard transmitted form.

### Contradiction: when was the Aeneid published

The gathering draft stamped the publication **17 BC** with no support; no ancient source gives that year. Standard reference works date it **19 BC or shortly after**. The journey file stamps the stop at 19 BC and flags the range in `date_confidence`.

## The afterlife: Virgil after Virgil

- **Vergilius Vaticanus** (Rome, c. 400 CE) and **Vergilius Romanus** (c. 480-490 CE): two of the only three surviving illustrated manuscripts of classical literature are Virgil codices, both now in the Vatican Library. The third is the Ambrosian Iliad. **[A]**
- From the twelfth century Virgil is reimagined across Europe as a **magician and protector of Naples**: the bronze fly that drives real flies from the city, the brazen head, the egg holding up the castle. **[R]**
  - *Attribution corrected and tag downgraded.* The draft credited **John of Salisbury's *Policraticus* (c. 1159)** and tagged it **[A]**. The *Policraticus* contents summary shows no such passage, and Comparetti's *Vergil in the Middle Ages* treats the twelfth-century Naples legend under **Conrad of Querfurt**. The secure early witnesses are Conrad's letter (1194), **Alexander Neckam, *De naturis rerum* (c. 1190)**, and **Gervase of Tilbury, *Otia Imperialia* (1214)**. The journey file uses Conrad, 1194, tagged **[R]**.
- The Neapolitan civic cult at the tomb **predates and runs parallel to** Dante's literary use of Virgil rather than deriving from it. **[R: Spargo, *Virgil the Necromancer*, via secondary summary]**
- **Dante** makes Virgil a named character and seats his eternal home in Limbo's philosophic castle with Homer, Horace, Ovid, Lucan, and the philosophers. **[A: dante.journey.json, quoting *Inferno* I and IV]**
- **Petrarch's Virgil**: the frontispiece by **Simone Martini** shows Virgil under a tree with Aeneas, Servius, and a farmer. **[A]**
  - *Correction logged.* The draft dated it c. 1340s and implied Milan. Martini painted it **c. 1336 at Avignon**. Milan is only where the codex is now held, in the Biblioteca Ambrosiana. The journey file pins the Ambrosiana and states the Avignon origin in the campa.
- **Editio princeps, Rome 1469**, printed by **Conrad Sweynheym and Arnold Pannartz**. **[A]** The pool's cited URL returns 404 and is not used; the printers are named instead so the claim is checkable.
- **The Aldine italic Virgil, Venice 1501**. **[A]** Giulio Camillo lodges beside that press in 1510.
- **Dryden's *Works of Virgil*, London 1697**, the dominant English Virgil for over a century. **[A]**
- **Blake**, commissioned by Linnell in 1824, draws Dante and Virgil among the ice of Cocytus, working from a borrowed English translation since he read no Italian. **[A: william_blake.journey.json]**
- **Barbier's libretto for Thomas's *Françoise de Rimini***, Palais Garnier, 14 April 1882, opens with Dante and Virgil descending to the second circle. **[A: paul_barbier.journey.json]**
- **T. S. Eliot**, lecturing to the Virgil Society in 1944, names him the classic of all Europe. **[A]**
- **Mantua, 1397**: a statue of Virgil is said to have been torn down by a mob and later restored under humanist patronage. **[R, low confidence]** The search budget was exhausted before this could be checked. It is **not** in the journey file. An open lead.

## Honest gaps

- **No contemporary biography.** Every anecdote here except the Brundisium journey descends from one late-antique text of uncertain reliability.
- **No archaeology at Andes.** The birthplace is a devotional identification, not a dig.
- **The autobiographical reading of the Eclogues** is the sole detailed source for the confiscation and is itself contested.
- **Virgil's own voice about his life** survives nowhere. No letters. What the atlas calls his stops are mostly places the tradition put him.
- **Whether he visited Cumae** to write Book 6 is unknowable from the sources.
- **The Appendix Vergiliana** attributions, which underwrite the Siro connection, are disputed.
