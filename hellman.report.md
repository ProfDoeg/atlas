# Martin Hellman (b. 1945): research report

*Compiled 2026-08-03 for the Atlas of Journeys. Subject: Martin Edward Hellman, American electrical engineer, co-inventor of public key cryptography, later a nuclear risk analyst. A living subject: no death date, no tomb, so the "afterlife" material below covers monuments to the work rather than to a death.*

**Legend.** **[A]** attested, source named. **[R]** reconstruction, tradition, single-source self-report, or inference. Contradictions are flagged and left standing; unreachable sources are named with the reason.

**Two cautions.** Hellman is his own principal biographer: most of what circulates traces to his Stanford homepage (`ee.stanford.edu/~hellman`) and the Computer History Museum oral history of 11 March 2011 (X6076.2011), both candid, neither independent. Where a claim rests on one alone, this report says so. Second, the verified pool arrived carrying eleven flagged corrections against its own gathered material, including three fabricated or misplaced coordinates and four altered quotations, each stated below at the point where it bites.

## 1. The Bronx, 1945 to 1966

Born 2 October 1945 to a Jewish family in New York City. **[A: Wikipedia, "Martin Hellman"]**

*Contradiction.* The pool asserted the Bronx as birthplace, tagged **[A]**, with a coordinate. Wikipedia gives **New York City**, not the Bronx, which is attested only as where he grew up (CHM: "I grew up in the Bronx, okay?"). The journey keeps the Bronx pin, since the childhood demonstrably happened there, and records the downgrade in `date_confidence`. Birthplace within the city: **[R]**.

Bronx High School of Science, diploma 1963 **[A]**; the school has occupied its West 205th Street building since 3 March 1959 **[A]**. *Coordinate corrected:* the pool gave 40.8752, -73.8887; Wikipedia's figure for 75 W 205th St is **40.8783, -73.8908**, about 350 m off. *Honest gap:* no source reached names a teacher, class or incident from these four years.

NYU, B.E. electrical engineering, 1966 **[A]**. *Coordinate corrected, and it matters.* The pool pinned Washington Square. NYU's College of Engineering sat at the **University Heights campus in the Bronx** from 1894 until NYU sold the site in 1973 **[A: Wikipedia, "NYU Tandon"]**. A 1966 EE degree was earned at approximately **40.8571, -73.9120**. Hellman does not leave the Bronx for university. He stays in it.

## 2. Stanford, IBM, MIT, 1966 to 1971

M.S., Stanford, 1967 **[A]**. IBM Thomas J. Watson Research Center, Yorktown Heights, 1968 **[A]**, where **Horst Feistel** is in the same department running the cipher work that becomes DES: regular lunches, Feistel explaining ciphers **[A: CHM X6076.2011]**. Hellman names this as one of three events that turn him toward cryptography, the operative content being that IBM was spending money on it. Ph.D., Stanford, 1969, *Learning with Finite Memory*, advisor **Thomas M. Cover** **[A]**. Assistant professor, MIT, 1969 to 1971 **[A]**, where **Peter Elias** hands him a Shannon paper establishing cryptography as a branch of information theory: the third spark **[A: CHM]**.

*Single-source caution.* The Feistel lunches and the Elias handover are CHM-only, told by Hellman about himself thirty-five years later. Nothing contradicts them; nothing corroborates them.

## 3. Stanford faculty and the vacuum, 1971 to 1975

Joins the Stanford EE faculty 1971, twenty-five years to emeritus in 1996 **[A]**. Colleagues warn repeatedly that NSA already knows anything he could find and would classify it **[A as self-report, CHM; [R] as description of the field]**.

**Konheim referral, summer 1974.** Diffie, touring with Mary Fischer, visits the Watson lab; group director **Alan Konheim**, unable to share classified work, tells him to look up Hellman **[A: Wikipedia, "Whitfield Diffie"; diffie.journey.json]**. **First meeting, September 1974:** a scheduled half hour runs to eleven at night, and Hellman hires him the following spring as a part-time research programmer **[A: CHM; diffie.journey.json]**.

**Merkle.** **Ralph Merkle** at Berkeley reaches key distribution independently; a mutual friend brings the three together; Hellman calls him "another fool crazy enough to work in the area" **[A: CHM]**. Hellman then, in his own verb, "kidnaps" him to Stanford **[R: CHM only; the formal supervision is what is attested]**. Diffie enrols June 1975 under Hellman's sponsorship, never completes (unsatisfied physical examination), and stays as research assistant through 1978 **[A: diffie.journey.json]**.

## 4. DES and the key length, 1975 to 1977

*Contradiction flagged, not resolved.* The pool dated the public Diffie-Hellman criticism of the 56-bit key to 1975, tagged **[A]**. Three dates are in play: **March 1975 [R]**, the *Federal Register* publication of the proposed DES, from which Hellman's objections date; **1976 [A: Wikipedia, "Whitfield Diffie"]**, the recorded Stanford review with Dennis Branstad of NBS and NSA representatives; **June 1977 [A: Wikipedia, "DES"]**, "Exhaustive Cryptanalysis of the NBS Data Encryption Standard" in *Computer*, where that article dates the critique. The journey uses 1976, the only date with a documented event and named participants, and records the spread in `date_confidence`.

**Key shrink.** Declassified NSA history, quoted in Wikipedia's DES article: the agency "tried to convince IBM to reduce the length of the key from 64 to 48 bits. Ultimately they compromised on a 56-bit key." **[A]**

*Honest gap: Gaithersburg.* NBS held two open workshops on the dispute **[A]**. Whether Hellman was ever physically at the NBS facility is attested nowhere reached. The journey keeps a Gaithersburg pin as the institutional counterparty and states in the campa that no visit is attested, rather than staging one.

## 5. New Directions in Cryptography, November 1976

*IEEE Transactions on Information Theory* IT-22(6), Nov. 1976, pp. 644 to 654 **[A: PDF fetched directly from ee.stanford.edu/~hellman/publications/24.pdf]**. Three sentences are verified verbatim against that PDF, including the opening, "We stand today on the brink of a revolution in cryptography." The Jim Massey editorship detail is **[R: CHM only]**. Merkle's parallel result appears in *Communications of the ACM*, April 1978, submitted independently and earlier **[A]**.

*Coordinate corrected.* The pool pinned "IEEE editorial correspondence" at 40.7484, -73.9857, which is the **Empire State Building**, an arbitrary Manhattan pin. The journey uses IEEE's then-headquarters at 345 E 47th St, **40.7519, -73.9686**.

## 6. The threat of prosecution, 1977

**The Meyer letter.** NSA employee **Joseph A. Meyer** writes to the IEEE from his Maryland home address warning that publishing and exporting cryptography papers could carry five to ten years and heavy fines. Not an official agency communication **[A: CHM]**. *Two corrections:* the pool dated it "1976-1977"; the letter is **July 1977**. The pool also supplied a coordinate, 39.0836, -76.7734, in the Bowie/Crofton area, but **no published source gives Meyer's home address**. That pin is a fabrication and the journey does not use it: the stop sits at the canonical Fort George G. Meade coordinate, with the substitution disclosed in `date_confidence`. Wikipedia's "Crypto Wars" does not cover the incident at all.

**Stanford general counsel John Schwartz.** If export control is read broadly enough to cover the papers then the statute is unconstitutional, but that is opinion, not certainty; Stanford will defend through appeal but cannot pay a fine or serve a sentence **[R: CHM only]**.

**Cornell, October 1977.** At the IEEE International Symposium on Information Theory, Hellman reads two joint papers, one with Merkle and one with **Stephen Pohlig**, on the reasoning that a tenured professor survives prosecution and a student does not; each student stands at the podium beside him. No prosecution follows **[A: CHM]**.

**Inman.** NSA director **Bobby Inman** seeks to have cryptographic research declared born classified; he and Hellman come to know each other directly against agency advice; the review process converts from mandatory to voluntary **[A: CHM for the relationship; [R] for Hellman's causal role, his own account of his own influence]**. Inman reappears forty years later signing *Rethinking National Security*.

## 7. The patent, the knapsack, the dispersal

**US Patent 4,200,770, "Cryptographic Apparatus and Method."** *Correction:* the pool said "granted 1977." It was **filed 6 September 1977** and **granted 29 April 1980**; assignee Leland Stanford Junior University; inventors Diffie, Hellman, Merkle **[A: Google Patents US4200770A]**. Wikipedia's loose "1977" is the filing. The journey dates the stop to the filing and states the grant in the campa.

**Merkle-Hellman knapsack, 1978**, broken by **Adi Shamir in 1982 [A]**.

**The dispersal.** Merkle took his Stanford Ph.D. in 1979 and became manager of compiler development at **Elxsi** in **1980** per Wikipedia, moving to Xerox PARC in 1988; the CHM oral history implies 1979, and the discrepancy is left standing. Diffie did not go to "Bell Northern Research" as such: from 1978 to 1991 he was Manager of Secure Systems Research at **Northern Telecom, Mountain View** **[A]**, BNR being Northern Telecom's R&D arm, so the pool's gloss was half right.

**The RSA dispute.** RSA Data Security's founding paper credits Diffie, Hellman and Merkle with the invention; when Stanford seeks royalties, the company argues the patents are invalid. Hellman is angry, then later credits **Jim Bidzos** with building the industry. **[R: CHM only]**

## 8. Beyond War and the Soviet dialogue, 1982 to 1990

The weakest-sourced phase of the life, and the pool overstated it twice.

*Contradiction, founding date.* The pool gave 1982 in one entry and 1985 in another. Wikipedia dates the movement's establishment to **1984** in the Palo Alto area, its award programme to 1983 through 1990; 1982 belongs to the **Creative Initiative Foundation's** transition. Three dates, none adjudicated; the journey uses 1984 and records the spread.

*Contradiction, the editor credit.* Wikipedia names **Richard T. Roney** as editor of *Beyond War: A New Way of Thinking* and does not mention Hellman in connection with the movement's leadership at all. The principal-editor claim rests solely on Hellman's own biography. Downgraded **[A] to [R]**, both attributions set side by side in the campa.

**Spacebridge, 1984.** The Beyond War award goes to International Physicians for the Prevention of Nuclear War over a live Moscow to San Francisco satellite link, watched by more than 75,000 **[A]**. *Gap:* Hellman's presence in the hall is nowhere attested. ***Breakthrough: Emerging New Thinking*, 1987**, co-edited with **Anatoly Gromyko**; thirty-plus Soviet and Western scholars; simultaneous Russian and English publication; the sponsoring Soviet Academy of Sciences committee headed by **Evgeni Velikhov** **[A]**.

*Dropped from the journey.* The pool dated Hellman's work on ethnic tension at Stanford to **1990**; his own page says "the 1980s and 90s," with three awards from minority student organisations. The single-year anchor is invented precision, so the journey omits the stop rather than pin a fabricated year **[R]**.

*Undated op-eds.* "Secrecy is Obsolete" and "The Nazi Within" carry no dates. The latter addresses the **Bitburg** controversy, placing it at May 1985 **[R, inferred from subject and filename, and flagged in `date_confidence`]**; the text itself is verbatim-verified.

## Quotations: the corrections

1. **The Turing citation.** Two wrong versions circulated: "foundation for most regularly-used security protocols on the internet today" (an ACM *press release* phrase) and "for fundamental contributions to modern cryptography" (an ETHW paraphrase, put in quotation marks as though it were the citation). The real 2015 citation, per Wikipedia's Turing list, is "for inventing and promulgating both asymmetric public-key cryptography, including its application to digital signatures, and a practical cryptographic key-exchange method." ACM's own page returned **HTTP 403**; the gap is stated in the stop's `quote_source`.
2. **"Rethinking National Security."** The words are exact, the attribution is not. The page is a collective statement with roughly fifteen signatories, among them Richard Garwin, Rose Gottemoeller, John Hennessy, Bobby Inman, Jack Matlock and Leon Panetta; Hellman is organiser and copyright holder, not sole author. Re-tagged **[A] to [R]** as a Hellman quotation, retained with the collective attribution in both campa and `quote_source`.
3. **Two *Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists* fragments, both dropped from the journey.** "A risk analysis of nuclear deterrence could bring greater objectivity..." loses the opening condition "If requested by Congress and performed by the National Academies," turning an institutional proposal into a free-standing assertion; "It is essential to mitigate the risk before disaster strikes, not afterward" is a mid-sentence fragment with a capitalised "It," cut loose from the Deepwater Horizon comparison it hangs on.

The one *Bulletin* quotation retained is verified whole in `ee.stanford.edu/~hellman/publications/75.pdf`, 67(2), March 2011.
