# Johannes Trithemius (1462-1516): research report

*Abbot of Sponheim, then of the Schottenkloster St. Jakob at Würzburg. Chronicler, bibliophile, cryptographer, first man to write down the name of Faust. Compiled 2026-08-08 for the Atlas of Journeys.*

Legend: **[A]** = attested, source named. **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition, or inference. Contradictions between sources are flagged and left open; they are not adjudicated here.

A structural warning first: Trithemius is the rare subject whose own scholarship is partly forged, and the nineteenth century spent decades separating his archival work from his fabrications. Nothing here depends on the disputed chronicles, and where a claim rests on his own testimony about himself it is marked.

---

## 1. Trittenheim and the flight, 1462-1479

Born Johann Heidenberg (Johann von Heidenberg) on 1 February 1462 at Trittenheim on the Moselle, in the Electorate of Trier **[A: English Wikipedia, "Johannes Trithemius"]**. He takes the toponymic Trithemius later, from the village, in the humanist fashion.

His father dies while he is an infant **[A: en.wikipedia]**. His mother Elisabeth remarries seven years after that death, which computes to c. 1469 **[A for the interval: en.wikipedia; the year is arithmetic, not a dated document]**; the pool's "1460s" was too loose. The stepfather's hostility to the boy's schooling is confirmed by both the English and German articles **[A]**.

He teaches himself Greek, Latin, and Hebrew in secret against the stepfather's wishes **[A: en.wikipedia; but this rests on Trithemius's own later account, the only source for it, from a man with a documented habit of improving a story]**.

At about seventeen he leaves for an education, travelling through Trier, Cologne, the Netherlands, and Heidelberg **[A for the route: German Wikipedia]**, where he studies roughly 1479-1482 **[A: en.wikipedia; 1911 Britannica]**. The route is a bare list of names: no months, no Netherlandish town, no companions.

**Correction applied.** The gathering pass placed Heidelberg at the Alte Universität, a building erected 1712-1728, two centuries after Trithemius, and got its coordinates wrong besides. The journey file uses the corpus's canonical Heidelberg pin (see §9) and does not name the building.

## 2. Sponheim: the snowstorm and the abbacy, 1482-1483

Returning from Heidelberg toward home he is caught in a snowstorm, takes refuge at the Benedictine abbey of Sponheim near Bad Kreuznach, stays, and enters as a novice **[A: en.wikipedia; de.wikipedia]**. The most-repeated anecdote in his biography, and again from his own pen; tagged **[A]** because every reachable source carries it.

Elected abbot 29 July 1483, at twenty-one, roughly eighteen months after entering **[A: de.wikipedia for the date; en.wikipedia for the age]**.

**Internal contradiction in the pool, flagged.** One lens gave his age at election as 25, two others as 21. Born 1 February 1462 and elected 29 July 1483, he is 21; the 25 is an error and is not carried.

Given the abbatial blessing 9 November 1483 by Berthold von Oberg, suffragan bishop of Mainz **[A: de.wikipedia]**. No source says where the ceremony took place; placing it at Sponheim is **[R]**.

## 3. The library and the Sponheim books, 1483-1505

He rebuilds the library from roughly fifty volumes to more than two thousand, one of the largest private collections in Germany, drawing scholars from across Europe **[A: en.wikipedia; de.wikipedia, "Kloster Sponheim", which gives 48 books at his entry rather than "roughly fifty"]**. Works of the period reachable in sources:

- *Catalogus illustrium virorum Germaniae*, 1491, and *De scriptoribus ecclesiasticis*, 1494 **[A: 1911 Britannica via Wikisource]**
- *De laude scriptorum manualium*, written 1492, printed 1494 at Mainz by Peter von Friedberg **[A: en.wikipedia works list, "1492 (printed 1494)"]**. **Correction applied:** the pass said "published 1492", conflating composition with printing. The irony sharpens rather than dissolves.
- *Chronicon Sponheimense*, c. 1495-1509, whose composition runs past his departure **[A: en.wikipedia]**
- *Chronicon Hirsaugiense*, 1495-1503 **[A: en.wikipedia]**. **Correction applied:** the pass called this "the two-volume Chronicon Hirsaugiense", fusing two works. En.wikipedia lists it separately from *Annales Hirsaugienses* 1509-1514, and it is the *Annales* (St. Gallen, 1690) that is the two-volume expansion.
- *Steganographia*, c. 1499-1500 **[A: en.wikipedia]**

Bursfelde: he serves as featured speaker and chapter secretary at the annual general chapters of the Bursfelde Congregation of reformed Benedictine houses, 1492-1503 **[A for the role: en.wikipedia]**.

**Serious geographic caveat, flagged not resolved.** The pass pinned this at Bursfelde Abbey and got the coordinates wrong by ~6.6 km besides (correct: 51.5417 / 9.6247, de.wikipedia). The deeper problem: the same article states that of 68 general chapters held 1458-1530, only eleven convened at Bursfelde, the congregation preferring more central venues. He almost certainly did *not* spend those eleven years going there. The journey file keeps the corrected pin as the congregation's name-place and says so in the campa; the meeting towns are an unresolved gap.

## 4. Steganographia and the fall from Sponheim, 1499-1506

*Steganographia* is completed in manuscript c. 1499-1500 and circulates only among trusted correspondents in his lifetime **[A: en.wikipedia]**. The dedicatee is Philip, Count Palatine of the Rhine, and Trithemius signs as *Abbas Spanheimensis* **[A: Peterson's transcription, esotericarchives.com/tritheim/stegano.htm]**. Rumours about the unpublished book alarm contemporaries who suspect real conjuration, contributing to friction with superiors and patrons **[R: consensus reconstruction, en.wikipedia "Steganographia"; no contemporary document states the causal link in those words]**.

1504: with the Landshut War of Succession threatening the region, he moves the library, treasury, and archives to Kreuznach for safekeeping **[A: de.wikipedia, "Kloster Sponheim"]**.

Resignation after friction with his own monks over discipline and control of the library **[A for the event]**. **Contradiction, flagged not resolved:** en.wikipedia, "Sponheim Abbey" gives 1506; de.wikipedia gives 1505. The journey file dates the stop 1505 and carries the disagreement in `date_confidence`.

The pass tripped over this twice, placing both *Polygraphia* (1508) and *De septem secundeis* (1508) at Sponheim or in a "Sponheim/Würzburg transition period". There is no transition in 1508; he has been at Würzburg two years. **Corrected in all such placements.**

## 5. Würzburg: the abbey of the Scots, 1506-1516

Bishop Lorenz von Bibra appoints him abbot of the Schottenkloster, St James's Abbey at Würzburg, in 1506, and he holds the post until his death **[A: en. and de.wikipedia]**.

**Correction applied, affecting eight waypoints.** The pass pinned the Schottenkloster on the wrong bank of the Main and reused that pair for seven further Würzburg stops; a second lens had a third, equally wrong pair. St. Jakob stands at Schottenanger 15 in the Mainviertel, today the Don-Bosco-Kirche: **49.7957 / 9.9205** (de.wikipedia). See §9.

Summoned twice to Maximilian I's court, 1506-1507 **[A: en.wikipedia]**. **Gap:** no source names where; Innsbruck is a labelled placeholder for the emperor's most frequent residence.

*Liber octo quaestionum* opens by recalling that the emperor put the eight questions to him *in Castello Bopardiano*, at Boppard on the Rhine, *dudum*, some time ago **[A: esotericarchives.com/tritheim/8quest.htm]**. Boppard is where they were posed, not where the book was written.

Würzburg works of 1508: *Liber octo quaestionum*; *Antipalus maleficiorum* (dedicated to Joachim I of Brandenburg **[R: not re-verified]**); *De septem secundeis* (for Maximilian I, first printed Nuremberg 1522 by Johann Haselberg, printer Hieronymus Höltzel **[A: de.wikipedia]**); *Polygraphia* (written 1508, published 1518 **[A: en.wikipedia]**, against de.wikipedia's 1508-1515 in six volumes, **flagged**). It is the first published book on cryptography and introduces the *tabula recta*.

## 6. Faust, Paracelsus, Agrippa

**Faust [A, with three textual corrections].** In a letter to the astrologer Johannes Virdung of Hassfurt dated 20 August 1507, Trithemius denounces a wandering boaster as a charlatan. It is the earliest known written mention of the historical Faust and the root of the legend; the original is in the Vatican Library, described as the first, longest, and most consequential contemporary source on the man.

The epithet-string as the pass reported it was wrong in three ways and its provenance was false: English Wikipedia, cited as having reproduced it verbatim, truncates at "magus secundus etc." The full string comes from the German article, opens with *Magister*, reads *iunior* not *junior*, and gives *agromanticus*, of which *aeromanticus* is an editorial emendation: *Magister Georgius Sabellicus, Faustus iunior. fons necromanticorum, astrologus, magus secundus, chiromanticus, agromanticus, pyromanticus, in hydra arte secundus.*

Also **[A]**: the same letter reports that this Faust held a schoolmaster's post at Bad Kreuznach through the patronage of the knight Franz von Sickingen, and fled after an accusation of sodomy. Trithemius knew that town well, having carried his own library there three years earlier. The commonly appended phrases calling Faust "a vagabond, an empty talker and a cheat" were not located in any reachable full text and are left **[R]**.

**Paracelsus [R].** He is counted among the teachers of Paracelsus, traditionally at Würzburg c. 1508. Documentary evidence is thin; the claim rests on later tradition and on Paracelsus's own admiring references. The corpus already carries the pin, dated 1508-04-01 in `paracelsus.journey.json`. The journey file inherits it and keeps the [R] tag on the history.

**Rival tradition, flagged.** QUEUE.md line 152 lists "Salomon Trismosin, legendary alchemist, reputed teacher of Paracelsus" immediately after Trithemius. Two competing teacher-of-Paracelsus traditions, not adjudicated.

**Einsiedeln, debunked [R].** Later biographers have the young Paracelsus tutored at Einsiedeln by four bishops and by Trithemius. En.wikipedia, "Paracelsus" flags this as unsubstantiated: no record places Trithemius at Einsiedeln, none places Paracelsus at Sponheim or Würzburg before 1516. **Correction applied:** the pass pinned Einsiedeln ~12 km east of the abbey. Correct: 47.12667 / 8.751472.

**Agrippa [A].** In the winter of 1509-1510 Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa travels to St James's and holds several days of discussion with Trithemius on natural magic **[A: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Agrippa von Nettesheim"]**. **Correction applied:** the pass had him arrive "fresh from a forced departure from Dole", inverting the chronology; en.wikipedia has him forced out of Dole in 1510, after the visit. He came from Dole, where his lectures had drawn a heresy denunciation from the Franciscan prior Jean Catilinet. On 8 April 1510 he dedicates the first draft of *De occulta philosophia* to Trithemius, who praises it, urges deeper metaphysical grounding, and tells him to keep his occult studies secret **[A: SEP; en.wikipedia]**. In 1520 the corpus of Trithemius's magical manuscripts passes into his possession **[A: SEP, giving 1520 exactly, not "c. 1520"]**. No source says where.

**The necromancy legend [R].** Luther's *Tischreden* (1569) and Augustin Lercheimer's 1585 account have a magician understood to be Trithemius summoning Alexander the Great and Maximilian's dead wife Mary of Burgundy for the emperor, the apparition bearing a birthmark on the neck only the widower knew of. Later retellings move it to Charles V's court with Helen of Troy; Goethe inherits it, and it appears in the Grimms' *Deutsche Sagen* (the legend collection, **not** the *Kinder- und Hausmärchen*; the pass said "tales"). No location is given.

## 7. Death, tomb, memorials

Dies at Würzburg 13 December 1516, aged fifty-four, still abbot, and is buried in the church of St James's Abbey **[A: en. and de.wikipedia; 1911 Britannica]**. A tomb relief showing him in pontifical vestments with mitre and crosier is carved c. 1516 by Tilman Riemenschneider or his workshop **[A: de.wikipedia]**.

**Correction applied, and it matters.** The pass wrote that "his remains and monument were later moved to the Neumünster church" in 1825. No source says the body was translated; en.wikipedia says only "the tombstone was relocated". The grave did not move, the slab did. The same entry claimed the Neumünster was "refounded" in 1825; that foundation dates from around 1057 and its baroque church was built 1711-1716.

The Neumünster memorial is damaged in the 1945 bombing of Würzburg and later restored by the workshop of Theodor Spiegel **[A: en.wikipedia; restoration undated]**. The Schottenkloster church itself was destroyed in the war, rebuilt, and is now run by the Salesians as the Don-Bosco-Kirche.

## 8. The afterlife of the books

- Sponheim's library dispersed: to Heidelberg in 1601, to Rome in 1622-23 with the Bibliotheca Palatina **[A: de.wikipedia, "Kloster Sponheim"]**. The abbey closes in 1556, is briefly revived, and is suppressed by the French in 1802.
- Collected editions by Marquard Freher (1601) and Johannes Busaeus (1604-1624) **[A: de.wikipedia]**.
- *Steganographia* first printed at Frankfurt, 1606, with a key revealing the cryptographic nature of books one and two; on the Index Librorum Prohibitorum 1609-1900 **[A: en.wikipedia]**.
- A bust in the Ruhmeshalle at Munich, left side wall, inscribed ABT U. GELEHRTER **[A: de.wikipedia; bust undated, hall opened 1853, war-damaged, reopened 26 October 1972]**. Coordinates corrected to 48.1308 / 11.5457.
- A statue at Trittenheim, 1962 **[A for the year: de.wikipedia]**. **Gap:** no source gives its position.
- The third book of *Steganographia* is shown to be itself an enciphered treatise, overturning the reading of that volume as occult. **Contradiction inside the pool, flagged:** one lens dates this 1996 and credits Jim Reeds, another dates it 1998 and credits Reeds. Neither is right. Thomas Ernst published first, in 1996 ("Schwarzweisse Magie", *Daphnis*); Reeds published independently in 1998 ("Solved: The Ciphers in Book III of Trithemius's Steganographia", *Cryptologia*). The journey file dates the stop 1996, credits Ernst, and names Reeds in the same campa.

## 9. Coordinates: what was corrected, and the one pin left deliberately wrong

Nine coordinate errors in the gathering pass were corrected, each flagged above at its own entry. One further contamination: Trittenheim's parish church is St. Clemens, not St. Peter, which is Trier Cathedral and had bled across from the next waypoint.

One conflict is deliberately not corrected. Canonical corpus pins are to be inherited byte-identically, and both `paracelsus.journey.json` and `johann_faust.journey.json` pin Würzburg at **49.7913 / 9.9534**, wrong for the Schottenkloster by about 2.4 km. The journey file splits: the two stops that *are* the crossings with those files (the Virdung letter, the teaching of Paracelsus) carry the inherited pair so the threads join, and every other Würzburg stop carries the correct 49.7957 / 9.9205. If the corpus pin is fixed, move those two with it.

Canonical pins inherited byte-identically: Würzburg 49.7913 / 9.9534; Bad Kreuznach 49.8467 / 7.8667; Heidelberg 49.4102 / 8.7089; Cologne 50.9375 / 6.9603; Prague 50.0839 / 14.4213; Rome 41.8933 / 12.4828. Temple Mount and Kaaba are not implicated in any adjacent file and are not used.

## 10. Contradictions carried, not resolved

Five, each flagged where it occurs above and each carried into the journey file's `date_confidence` rather than adjudicated: the year of resignation from Sponheim (1505 de.wikipedia vs 1506 en.wikipedia); the composition of *Polygraphia* (1508 vs 1508-1515 in six volumes); the decipherment of Book III (Ernst 1996 vs Reeds 1998, two independent solutions habitually collapsed into one); the teacher of Paracelsus (Trithemius vs Salomon Trismosin, both queued); and the city of the 1518 *Polygraphia* imprint (Mainz vs Oppenheim vs Reichenau).

## 13. Style gate: arithmetic and ordering corrections

Applied to the journey file after the writing pass, none of them affecting a sourced claim.

- The Trittenheim birth campa said the statue came "four hundred and fifty years after his birth". The statue is 1962 and the birth 1462: five hundred years. Corrected.
- The Cologne campa put Agrippa's Würzburg visit "thirty years after" his birth in 1486. The visit is the winter of 1509-1510: twenty-three years. Corrected.
- The Heidelberg campa had Trithemius "eighteen months in the ground" at Luther's April 1518 disputation. He died 13 December 1516: sixteen months. Corrected.
- Sponheim to Bad Kreuznach is about ten kilometres, not "a dozen miles"; the campa now says a few miles. The Schottenkloster to the Neumünster is about eight hundred metres, so "a kilometre and a bit" became "less than a kilometre".
- Stop order in segment 3 broke year sequence: Bursfelde (1492) sat after Mainz (1494). The Bursfelde stop now stands between the bibliographies of 1491 and *De laude* of 1492. No dates were altered to achieve this.
