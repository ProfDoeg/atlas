# Johann Elert Bode (1747-1826): research report

*2026-08-07, for the Atlas of Journeys. Slug `johann_bode`. Companion: `johann_bode.journey.json`, 7 segments, 44 stops.*

Legend: **[A]** = attested, source named. **[R]** = reconstruction, inference or tradition. Where two reachable sources disagree, both readings are printed and neither is adopted. Inaccessible sources are named with the reason.

Bode has two addresses. He is born in Hamburg and works there unpaid and unschooled until he is twenty-five; he then goes to Berlin and stays fifty-four years, leaving it, so far as the record shows, once. The journey file has almost no travel in it. What moves is the sky. The afterlife segment supplies the geography the life withholds.

---

## 1. Hamburg, 1747-1772

**Birth.** Hamburg, 19 January 1747, eldest of nine children of a merchant family **[A: de.wikipedia]**. No street or house is preserved in anything reachable; the **Deutsche Biographie entry returned HTTP 404**. The pin (53.5503, 9.9937) marks the Altstadt generally, not a house. Stated as a gap.

**The eye.** A severe illness in youth permanently damaged his right eye **[A: en. and de.wikipedia]**. Neither source names the disease and **neither gives a year**. The stop is dated 1760-01-01, with `date_confidence` carrying the hedge: 1760 is an editorial midpoint of "his youth," not an attested date. It matters for the register, since every observation and engraving after it was made with one working eye.

**Busch.** Introduced at the Akademisches Gymnasium to the mathematician Johann Georg Busch, who opened his library and instruments; Bode's astronomy is self-taught off that shelf **[A: de.wikipedia]**. The building the Gymnasium occupied in the 1760s **could not be confirmed** (source page 404); the pin (53.5497, 9.995) approximates the Domplatz and Speersort area. **[A for the fact, R for the coordinate.]**

**First publication**, on the solar eclipse of 5 August 1766, at nineteen **[A: de.wikipedia]**.

**The Anleitung**, first edition 1768 **[A: en. and de.wikipedia]**. Two pool errors corrected. *Edition count:* "seven editions by 1801" has no support; en.wikipedia gives a 10th edition in 1844 and de.wikipedia says *mehr als zehn Auflagen*, so the journey says "more than ten editions, the last recorded in 1844." *The footnote:* see §6; the claim that the 1768 first edition carried the first published statement of the relation is **contested and is not asserted**.

**1769 and 1770.** Published on the transit of Venus of 3 June 1769; credited as independent co-discoverer of comet C/1769 P1 (Messier) **[A: de.wikipedia]**. Observed comet D/1770 L1 (Lexell) in 1770 **[A: de.wikipedia]**; the date within that year is not recorded.

## 2. Berlin, 1772-1786: Lambert's computer

**The summons.** Invited to Berlin by Johann Heinrich Lambert to compute ephemerides for the Prussian Academy of Sciences, 1772 **[A: en.wikipedia]**. Month not recorded.

**The tower.** The old Berlin Observatory: a tower on the roof of the royal Marstall in Dorotheenstadt, built 1700-1711 by Martin Grünberg on Gottfried Kirch's foundation, 27 m tall **[A: de.wikipedia "Berliner Sternwarte"; en.wikipedia "Berlin Observatory"]**.

> **Coordinate contradiction, flagged not resolved.** The geography material gives 52.5189, 13.3914 for the Alte Sternwarte; the afterlife material, citing en.wikipedia for the same building, gives 52.5177, 13.3889. About 100 m apart. The journey uses the first for the working-life stops and the second for the 1903 demolition stop, and declares the split in both `date_confidence` fields rather than silently picking one.

**The Jahrbuch.** First volume issued 1774, computed for the year 1776 **[A: en.wikipedia; de.wikipedia's wikitext reads *Bereits 1774 gab er für 1776 das erste Berliner Astronomische Jahrbuch heraus*]**.

> **A non-contradiction, dissolved.** The pool flagged an "1774 vs 1776" disagreement. There is none: 1774 is publication, 1776 coverage. One fact, two statements.
>
> **Two real ones, kept open.** Volume count: 51 (en.wikipedia) against 54 (ADB). End of series: 1957 (en.wikipedia's Bode article) against 1959 (en.wikipedia's Berlin Observatory and de.wikipedia's Berliner Sternwarte). Both spreads sit in the campa unadjudicated.
>
> **"Founded" is wrong, corrected.** The ADB (*ADB:Bode, Johann Elert*) says the series began under Lambert's direction from 1772 and that Bode became sole editor only after Lambert's death in **1777**. The journey follows the ADB and states in the campa that calling him its founder overstates the case.

**Marriage and children.** Married Johanna Christina Lange, Berlin, 14 July 1774 **[A: de.wikipedia]**; the church is not named anywhere reachable and the pin defaults to the observatory. Children, all **[A: de.wikipedia, years only]**: Anna Ernestine Christine (1775-1814), Johann Christian August Wilhelm (1777-1806), Theodor Heinrich August (1779-1804), Johanna Dorothea Karoline (1782). Johanna Christina died 1782, year only.

**M81 and M82** discovered 31 December 1774 from Berlin **[A: de.wikipedia]**.

**The sun as a dark body, 1776.** Bode published in *Beschäftigungen der Berlinischen Gesellschaft Naturforschender Freunde*, Bd. ii, p. 233, the view that the sun is a dark planetary body with land, water, mountains and valleys under a luminous atmosphere. **[A, independently verified by downloading Project Gutenberg ebook 28247, Agnes M. Clerke's *A Popular History of Astronomy During the Nineteenth Century*, 4th ed. (1902), and grepping it; Clerke's footnote 146 carries the German source and page.]** Quoted in the journey. Worth keeping because it is wrong: the register does not flatter its subjects.

**C/1779 A1**, discovered 6 January 1779 **[A: de.wikipedia]**. His only sole-credit comet.

**Second marriage.** Bode married Sophie Dorothea Lange; their son Karl Ferdinand Elert Bode was born 1784 and lived to 1857 **[A: de.wikipedia]**. **The marriage date is nowhere recorded.** The pool guessed "c.1783" and correctly tagged it **[R]**; the journey folds the marriage into the 1784 birth stop and says in `date_confidence` that the ordering is inference. Sophie died 1790, year only. He did not marry again.

## 3. Uranus, 1781-1784

**Discovery and pre-discovery.** Herschel reported the object in March 1781. Bode was directly involved in the follow-up, tracking down earlier unrecognised observations including Flamsteed's 1690 entry as the star **34 Tauri**, which allowed a better orbit **[A: en.wikipedia]**. The month Bode took it up is unrecorded; the journey defaults to April and says so.

> **Date error in the pool, corrected.** The chronology lens dated the naming proposal to **1781**. Wrong by a year: Bode proposed it in a treatise read on **12 March 1782**, as he states himself in the 1784 book, and as en.wikipedia's Uranus article has it. The pool's geography lens was right at 1782-03 and its chronology lens contradicted it.
>
> **Adoption is datable.** The last holdout, HM Nautical Almanac Office, dropped *Georgium Sidus* for Uranus only in **1850** **[A: en.wikipedia, "Uranus"]**. This became a stop.

**Klaproth, 1789**, named the newly isolated element uranium after Bode's planet **[A: en.wikipedia]**.

### The quotations, graded

Six were supplied; five are used. Their evidentiary quality is very uneven and the differences are the point.

1. **The Titius-Bode footnote** ("Let the distance from the Sun to Saturn be taken as 100..."). **[A, tertiary but cross-confirmed]**: Hoskin's translation in en.wikipedia's "Titius-Bode law," raw wikitext fetched directly; Wikipedia's second, variant rendering of the same footnote matches. Used at the 1772 stop. **The sun as a dark body**, verified at Clerke 1902 by full-text download, is likewise **[A]** and used.
2. **"A moving star that can be deemed a hitherto unknown planet-like object circulating beyond the orbit of Saturn."** **The weakest of the six, and the journey says so in the field the reader sees.** Confirmed: the sentence stands verbatim in en.wikipedia's "Uranus" wikitext, referenced to *Bode, Berliner Astronomisches Jahrbuch, p. 210, 1781, quoted in Miner, p. 11*. Not confirmed: the wording in Miner or in the Jahrbuch. **HathiTrust HTTP 403**, **Google Books API HTTP 429**, archive.org search-inside unavailable for both Miner scans. A phrase search found zero hits outside Wikipedia and its mirrors, consistent with an editor's own translation. Used with the chain and the failure printed in `quote_source`.
3. **The three passages from *Von dem neu entdeckten Planeten* (Berlin, 1784).** **[A, verified at the PRIMARY SOURCE]**, better than the pool claimed for them. Printed p. 88 of the Bayerische Staatsbibliothek scan (see §8 for the method) carries *Bereits in der am 12ten März 1782... vorgelesenen Abhandlung, habe ich den Namen des Vaters vom Saturn, nemlich Uranos... vorgeschlagen*, and immediately after it the mythology sentence used at the 1782 stop.

**Four corrections applied to the pool's source lines:**

- The English is **not** "as given in the Wikipedia article." Wikipedia's crowd translation is garbled ("Already in the pre-read at the local Natural History Society on 12th March 1782 treatise..."). The renderings used are the pipeline's own and better. `quote_source` now cites the 1784 print and the BSB scan, "translation ours."
- Wikipedia mistranslates *merkwürdigen* as "strange." In eighteenth-century German it is **remarkable, noteworthy**. Bode's point is that a name from a contemporary figure, Herschel's *Georgium Sidus* for George III, would jar against a mythological series.
- The body is the **Gesellschaft Naturforschender Freunde zu Berlin**. And Bode writes *Diodor von Cicilien*, i.e. **Diodorus Siculus, of Sicily**; Wikipedia's "Diodorus of Cilicia" is a mistranslation. The journey names him correctly.
- The Atlantes ellipsis spans **two printed pages**, 88 to 90, omitting *ein fleissiger und geschickter Himmelsforscher des Alterthums*. That is the most atlas-relevant clause in the passage, so it is carried in the campa prose and the ellipsis disclosed in `quote_source`.

## 4. Director, 1786-1826, and the grave

- Corresponding member, Imperial Academy of Sciences, St Petersburg, 1785; honorary member 1794 **[A: de. and en.wikipedia]**.
- Director of the Berlin Observatory and full member of the Prussian Academy, 1786 **[A: en. and de.wikipedia]**. Also director of the calculating institute descended from Kirch's 1700 foundation, later the Astronomisches Rechen-Institut, c. 1787-1825, which moved to Heidelberg after the Second World War **[A: en.wikipedia]**.
- Fellow of the Royal Society, April 1789, day not recorded **[A: en.wikipedia]**. He never went to England. Royal Swedish Academy 1794, Göttingen 1801, Royal Danish Academy December 1805, Accademia Nazionale delle Scienze 1810 **[all A: en. or de.wikipedia]**. The elections stop was cut for length and the honours fold into the Royal Society campa.
- **Seeberg, August 1798.** Attended the first European astronomers' congress near Gotha and met Jérôme Lalande **[A: de.wikipedia]**.
- **Uranographia, 1801**: twenty large copper plates, with the catalogue *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne* listing over 17,000 stars **[A: en.wikipedia "Uranographia"; de.wikipedia]**; described in later scholarship as the last masterwork of artistic celestial cartography **[A]**.
- **Retirement 1825**, Encke succeeding **[A: en. and de.wikipedia]**. **Death 23 November 1826, Berlin, aged 79** **[A: en.wikipedia, de.wikipedia, ADB]**.

> **Seeberg, four fixes.** (a) The pool's 50.9333/10.75 is about 1.5 km too far east and lands off the site; de.wikipedia's *Sternwarte Gotha* gives 50°56′02″N 10°43′42″E = **50.9337, 10.7282**, used. (b) The pool's note that the Seeberg article "did not corroborate the 1798 congress" is wrong: it reads *1798 kam es zum Ersten europäischen Astronomenkongress*, so the congress is attested independently of the Bode biography. (c) "His one documented journey outside the Hamburg-Berlin axis" is an inference from silence; the campa keeps the observation and adds that this is not the same as saying it was his only journey. (d) The interlock lens offered 50.9481, 10.7018 as a Seeberg pin sourced to `gauss.journey.json`. That file was grepped: no Gotha or Seeberg stop, no coordinate in that range. Nothing to inherit.

### The grave: an honest gap

**No source consulted names Bode's burial place.** en.wikipedia, de.wikipedia, the ADB (which gives the death date and no burial location) and the Commons category are all silent. **Findagrave memorial 56004181 returned HTTP 403.** The Sophienfriedhof article, listing notable burials of his era including Karsch, Zelter and Ranke, does not include him. Recorded as a gap; the death stop's coordinates default to the observatory and say so in `date_confidence`.

## 5. Afterlife, 1827-2013

- **Encke's Gedächtnisrede**, 1827 **[A: de.wikipedia; ADB]**.
- **Schinkel's new observatory**, built 1832-1835 for Encke; Enckeplatz and Enckestraße named 1927; **no comparable street bears Bode's name** **[A: en.wikipedia]**. *Coordinate corrected:* the pool's 52.51/13.39 is Mitte; the building stood in **Kreuzberg near the Hallesches Tor, 52.503, 13.393** (de.wikipedia), about 800 m south.
- **Neptune, 23 September 1846**, found by Galle and d'Arrest at that Kreuzberg observatory, far outside the law's predicted distance **[A: en.wikipedia]**. Same coordinate correction, from 52.52/13.405.
  > **Internal tension, both readings kept.** One pool entry says Neptune devastated the law's reputation; another says the law fit Uranus nearly exactly. Both are supported: Uranus's fit was good and Neptune's was not. "Devastating its scientific reputation" is the source editorialising and is not reproduced as fact.
- **Peirce, 1898**, citing Bode's law as a textbook case of fallacious reasoning; *Icarus* later stopped accepting refinement papers **[A: en.wikipedia]**. The Cambridge, Massachusetts pin is the lecture series' venue, **not** given by the cited source. **[R for the coordinate, flagged in the stop.]**
- **The Dorotheenstadt tower demolished 3 July 1903**; the Prussian State Library took the site in 1914 **[A: en.wikipedia]**.
- **Mary Adela Blagg, 1913**, refined the law with a progression ratio of 1.7275, unnoticed until A. E. Roy rediscovered it in 1953 **[A: en.wikipedia]**. **No source attaches a place, so it did not become a stop.** Recorded so the omission is deliberate.
- **Asteroid 998 Bodea**, found by Karl Reinmuth at Heidelberg, 6 August 1923, named on Bror Asplind's proposal; dark, presumed carbonaceous, 31-32 km, outer belt **[A: en.wikipedia]**.
- **The lunar crater Bode**, named by the IAU in 1935, 17.80 km across, 3.5 km deep, 6°42′N 2°24′W **[A: en.wikipedia]**. *Two fixes:* the pool's "near Mare Insularum" is wrong, the crater sits on raised ground between Mare Vaporum, Sinus Aestuum and Sinus Medii with Rimae Bode to its west; and the pool's tie of the 2021-2026 landing-site studies **specifically to China's lunar programme is unsupported by the cited article and has been dropped**. Having no terrestrial coordinate, the crater rides inside the Heidelberg campa.
- **Bovaird and Lineweaver, 2013**: a generalised Titius-Bode relation fits the spacing in 96% of a sample of exoplanet systems **[A: en.wikipedia]**. The Canberra pin is the authors' institution, **not** given by the cited source; flagged. **[R for the coordinate.]**

## 6. The central contradiction: when did Bode first print the law?

The one dispute the journey deliberately leaves open, because it is the dispute the sources are in.

- **Reading A.** en.wikipedia's "Johann Elert Bode" places an early statement of the relation in the *Anleitung* of **1768**, at Hamburg. The pool went further and called it "the first published statement," which is **not supportable**: Titius printed the relation in 1766 inside his German translation of Charles Bonnet **[A: en.wikipedia, "Titius-Bode law"]**.
- **Reading B.** en.wikipedia's "Titius-Bode law" states Bode published the relation in his **1772** compendium, the second edition of the *Anleitung*, and the Hoskin-translated quotation is cited to that edition, Berlin 1772.

**A geographical consequence the pool got wrong.** The pool filed the 1772 statement under **Hamburg**, noting "both list Bode as being in Hamburg at the relevant time." False: Bode moved to Berlin in 1772 at Lambert's invitation **[A: en.wikipedia]**. If the first Bode statement is 1772, the place is **Berlin**. The journey puts the rule stop in Berlin, dates it 1772-09-01 with September flagged as an editorial default, and states the disagreement inside `date_confidence`. The 1768 Hamburg stop mentions the same dispute from the other end and asserts nothing about the footnote.

**Attribution.** Bode promoted the formula at first **without crediting Titius**, adding it only in later editions **[A: en. and de.wikipedia]**. Stated flatly in the campa.

## 7. Interlocks with existing atlas travelers

Named in campa where the crossing is real. Pins marked inherited are byte-identical to the source file.

- **`gauss` [A].** Computes Ceres's orbit from Piazzi's three positions; von Zach recovers it 31 December 1801 in the gap Bode's series predicted. Gauss had derived the same rule independently as a schoolboy in 1792 and never published it. Brunswick pin 52.2681, 10.5266 inherited.
- **`humboldt` [A for the fact].** Talks Gauss into coming to Berlin for the 1828 Naturforscherversammlung, one year after Encke's memorial address, a few streets from Bode's tower. Berlin pin 52.517, 13.3888 inherited from `gauss.journey.json`. The proximity is the atlas's own juxtaposition.
- **`leonard_euler` [A].** Spends his last afternoon, 18 September 1783, discussing the orbit of Uranus with Lexell, the same Lexell who computed Bode's 1770 comet. Pin 59.9421, 30.2996 inherited; that is Euler's death address, not a Bode address, and the stop says so.
- **`newton` [R as a link, A for each half].** The Royal Society that elects Bode in 1789 had Newton for president; the Jahrbuch is Newton's mechanics turned into annual tables.
- **`edmond_halley` [R].** Bode's method at the 1781 stop, hunting old catalogues for the same body under another description, is Halley's with the comet. Parallel, not contact.
- **`kant` [R, labelled inside the prose].** The 1755 *Allgemeine Naturgeschichte und Theorie des Himmels* is the same era's lawlike cosmogony. **Whether Bode read Kant is stated nowhere**, and the campa says so.
- **`hans_christian_andersen` [A for Andersen's arrival].** Reaches Hamburg three days after leaving Copenhagen on 16 May 1831, five years after Bode's death, passing the birthplace whose house nobody can name. Hamburg pin 53.5511, 9.9937 as used in his file and in `hegel.journey.json`.

**Rejected.** `hegel`: Hamburg is a toponymic overlap centuries apart. `rene_hauy`: a grep of the file shows no Bode or Berlin-observatory mention. Olbers appears at the Bremen stop but has no journey file; the celestial-police credit is **[R]**, since the atlas files do not state Bode's role and it is given variously to von Zach, Olbers and Bode.
