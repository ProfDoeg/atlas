# Picatrix: a reading for Anthony

I use **[A]** for claims the atlas presents as attested, including reported textual evidence, and **[R]** for reconstruction, interpretation, disputed attribution, or an unresolved connection. These labels apply to the sentences that follow them until the next label; citations identify the supporting atlas file. Attestation here does not imply that I independently consulted the underlying manuscript or publication.

## 1. What the book is

**[A]** *Ghayat al-Hakim*, translated as *The Goal of the Sage* or *The Aim of the Sage*, is an Arabic compilation of astral and talismanic magic arranged in **four books**. Its Latin descendant is known as *Picatrix*. **[R]** The distinction between original and descendant matters: the dossier describes translation, abridgment, interpolation, and rearrangement, so the Latin text cannot simply stand for every feature of the Arabic. ([Qurtubi dossier](../dossiers/maslama_al_qurtubi.dossier.md))

**[A]** Its subjects combine cosmology with instructions for action: celestial causation, planetary images, talismans, lunar mansions, astrological timing, fumigations, prayers, invocations, and compounded substances. It relates planets and zodiacal signs to minerals, plants, animals, colors, perfumes, languages, and human activities. The journey describes twenty-eight lunar mansions and prayers addressed to the seven planets. These belong to a practical apparatus supported by an account of how celestial and terrestrial things correspond. ([Qurtubi dossier](../dossiers/maslama_al_qurtubi.dossier.md); [Qurtubi journey](../maslama_al_qurtubi.journey.json))

**[A]** The book belongs to a paired philosophical project. The preceding *Rutbat al-Hakim* treats alchemy; the *Ghaya* treats astral magic. The dossiers describe these as the two culminating results of philosophical education, with magic assigned the higher position. Logic, number, geometry, astronomy, and natural philosophy prepare the practitioner for these final disciplines. The intended sage therefore needs intellectual preparation as well as materials and procedures. ([Ahmad dossier](../dossiers/ahmad_al_majriti.dossier.md); [Qurtubi dossier](../dossiers/maslama_al_qurtubi.dossier.md))

**[A]** Its sources are heterogeneous: Hermetic writings, material associated with Harranian or Sabian planetary religion, Jābirian alchemy, the *Nabataean Agriculture*, and the *Epistles of the Brethren of Purity*. Plato, Aristotle, Hippocrates or Buqratis, al-Kindi, and other authorities appear through transmitted literature. **[R]** An ancient name in that literature does not establish authentic ancient authorship: the dossier emphasizes compilation, translation, and pseudepigraphy. ([Ahmad dossier](../dossiers/ahmad_al_majriti.dossier.md))

**[A]** The practical aims include troubling operations involving hostility, domination, separation, erotic coercion, sacrifice, poisons, and animal or bodily substances. The text also invokes God while prescribing addresses to planetary spiritual beings. **[R]** These contents do not establish that its compiler performed every operation or endorsed every borrowed doctrine. Its compilation of different materials leaves the relationship between prescription, belief, and personal practice unresolved. ([Ahmad dossier](../dossiers/ahmad_al_majriti.dossier.md))

**[R]** The supplied research supports this account of the book’s architecture and purposes, but does not provide a sufficiently complete synopsis to reconstruct each book or chapter separately. A more detailed sequence would require evidence beyond these files. ([Qurtubi dossier](../dossiers/maslama_al_qurtubi.dossier.md); [Ahmad dossier](../dossiers/ahmad_al_majriti.dossier.md))

## 2. The authorship problem

**[A]** Two historical Maslamas must be distinguished. **Maslama ibn Ahmad al-Majriti** was the astronomer and mathematician associated with Madrid and Córdoba, conventionally born around 950 and dead around 1007–08, with alternative death dates in the record. His documented work concerns astronomical tables, practical mathematics, and Ptolemy’s *Planisphaerium*. **Maslama ibn Qasim al-Qurtubi**, who died in 964, was a hadith transmitter and traveler associated with charms and magical operations. Both could carry Cordoban designations; “al-Qurtubi” alone does not distinguish them. ([Majriti dossier](../dossiers/maslama_al_majriti.dossier.md); [Qurtubi dossier](../dossiers/maslama_al_qurtubi.dossier.md))

**[R]** The strongest identification presented by the dossiers assigns the *Rutba* and *Ghaya* to **ibn Qasim**, following Maribel Fierro’s 1996 study. The argument combines chronology, biography, and textual evidence. The works’ internal dates fit his lifetime; their author’s reported travel and combination of religious learning with occult interests fit his profile; Ibn al-Faradi specifically associates him with charms. Later manuscript annotations also help explain how the two biographies became mixed. None supplies an autograph conclusively naming him. ([Qurtubi dossier](../dossiers/maslama_al_qurtubi.dossier.md))

**[A]** The commonly reported internal chronology places the *Rutba* in 339–342 AH/950–953 and the *Ghaya* in 343–348 AH/954–959. **[R]** On the astronomer’s conventional birth date, he would have been about nine when the *Ghaya* was completed. That is the chronological objection—not the erroneous claim, corrected in the report, that completion preceded his birth by fifty years. ([Ahmad dossier](../dossiers/ahmad_al_majriti.dossier.md); [Qurtubi report](../maslama_al_qurtubi.report.md))

**[R]** There is also an opposite chronological argument. The Ahmad report records Ritter’s proposal to raise the dates by roughly a century, making composition later than the astronomer’s death. Other summaries retain a mid-eleventh-century dating. The files additionally disagree about whether 343 AH marks completion of the *Rutba* or commencement of the *Ghaya*. These are competing readings, not parts of one settled chronology. ([Ahmad report](../ahmad_al_majriti.report.md); [Qurtubi report](../maslama_al_qurtubi.report.md))

**[A]** The reports identify the alchemist **al-Jildaki** as the earliest known explicit manuscript witness attaching the occult corpus to al-Majriti. **[R]** The journey’s Cairo stop at **1342-01-01 is an approximate marker**, not a dated act of attribution. Its explanation draws on disputed death dates: 1342–43 versus approximately 1361. Moreover, the journey specifically describes attribution of the *Rutba*, while report summaries speak more broadly. The Ahmad dossier also records an already fused “Maslama” corpus in Ibn Sab‘in by about 1269, complicating any claim that confusion began with al-Jildaki. ([Qurtubi journey](../maslama_al_qurtubi.journey.json); [Qurtubi report](../maslama_al_qurtubi.report.md); [Ahmad dossier](../dossiers/ahmad_al_majriti.dossier.md))

**[A]** **Ibn Khaldun** subsequently names Maslama ibn Ahmad al-Majriti as author of the *Ghaya*. The Ahmad report reproduces the attribution through a Spanish translation in de Callataÿ’s 2013 article. **[R]** His importance is that of an influential transmitter of the attribution, rather than its first known source. The journey’s **1377-01-01** stop represents the approximate dating of the *Muqaddima*’s composition, not an attested January day. ([Ahmad report](../ahmad_al_majriti.report.md); [Qurtubi journey](../maslama_al_qurtubi.journey.json))

**[A]** Fierro’s article appeared in *Studia Islamica* 84 in 1996, pages 87–112. The reports acknowledge that they did not obtain its full text. The dossiers incorporate subsequent manuscript and itinerary scholarship by de Callataÿ, Moureau, and others. **[R]** Their assessment is strong, broadly accepted attribution to ibn Qasim, with residual uncertainty concerning textual layers and the absence of a contemporary authorial signature. ([Qurtubi report](../maslama_al_qurtubi.report.md); [Qurtubi dossier](../dossiers/maslama_al_qurtubi.dossier.md))

**[R]** “**Ahmad al-Majriti**” is a further bibliographical problem. No independent medieval biography was established in the research. The economical explanation is that *ibn Ahmad*, the astronomer’s patronymic, became detached and was treated as a personal name. “Phantom” describes this unsupported bibliographical identity; it does not prove that nobody ever bore the name. ([Ahmad report](../ahmad_al_majriti.report.md))

## 3. The book’s journey

**[R]** The journey must be read as several kinds of movement: sources entering a compilation, translations entering new languages, manuscripts reaching repositories, and scholarship revising attribution. Its January 1 dates are approximate plotting markers throughout, even when the associated publication year is securely attested. They do not establish days of travel, copying, acquisition, or publication. ([Qurtubi journey](../maslama_al_qurtubi.journey.json))

**[R]** Kufa, Harran, Basra, and Baghdad initially mark source traditions. The report and journey deny evidence for ibn Qasim’s eastern travel, but the dossier supplies an extensive itinerary through named teachers and biographical notices. That disagreement should remain visible. Even accepting the dossier’s travel evidence does not tell us precisely where he acquired each source used in the *Ghaya*. ([Qurtubi report](../maslama_al_qurtubi.report.md); [Qurtubi dossier](../dossiers/maslama_al_qurtubi.dossier.md))

**[A]** The decisive linguistic passage came under **Alfonso X of Castile**. The Latin prologue supports a window of **1256–1258** for the Arabic-to-Castilian translation; Latin followed from Castilian. **[R]** This interval should not be divided into an independently documented 1256 commission and 1258 completion. Toledo and Seville are possible court settings, not established translation addresses. Their January 1 stops are approximate representations of the same uncertain interval. ([Ahmad report](../ahmad_al_majriti.report.md); [Qurtubi report](../maslama_al_qurtubi.report.md))

**[R]** Translator identities remain unsettled within the atlas. The Ahmad report names Yehuda ben Moshe as attested; its dossier calls his identification conjectural and also tentatively proposes Aegidius de Tebaldis for the Latin. The Qurtubi report rejects a secure identification. The complete Castilian text is lost; accounts differ over whether surviving Vatican material constitutes a fragment or related Alfonsine material. The intermediary stage therefore cannot be reconstructed completely here. ([Ahmad report](../ahmad_al_majriti.report.md); [Ahmad dossier](../dossiers/ahmad_al_majriti.dossier.md); [Qurtubi report](../maslama_al_qurtubi.report.md))

**[A]** For **Krakow**, the Ahmad dossier identifies Biblioteka Jagiellońska **MS 793 III**, dated **1458–59**, as a Latin witness. **[R]** This is more specific than the journey’s approximate **1400** marker, which explicitly leaves the manuscript undated. Its coordinates indicate the present repository, whose building dates from 1931–1939, not a medieval copying site. The files do not establish a continuous route bringing this particular copy there. ([Ahmad dossier](../dossiers/ahmad_al_majriti.dossier.md); [Qurtubi journey](../maslama_al_qurtubi.journey.json))

**[A]** **Florence** enters through Ficino’s *De vita libri tres*, published in **1489**, and through Pico’s reception of *Picatrix*. The Qurtubi dossier reports correspondence showing Ficino borrowed a manuscript and extracted useful material. **[R]** The reports are more cautious about direct dependence. The journey’s **1489-01-01** represents the publication year approximately; it does not date Ficino’s borrowing or Pico’s reading. ([Qurtubi dossier](../dossiers/maslama_al_qurtubi.dossier.md); [Qurtubi report](../maslama_al_qurtubi.report.md); [Qurtubi journey](../maslama_al_qurtubi.journey.json))

**[A]** The English transmission includes a Latin manuscript associated with **Simon Forman, Richard Napier, Elias Ashmole, and William Lilly**. **[R]** London’s approximate **1600** stop represents their Elizabethan and Stuart setting, not a transaction on a particular day. The reports describe a British Library manuscript; the journey points more generally toward Ashmole manuscripts at the Bodleian. Without a reconciled shelf mark, the atlas does not settle the exact manuscript identification. ([Majriti report](../maslama_al_majriti.report.md); [Qurtubi journey](../maslama_al_qurtubi.journey.json))

**[A]** At **the Escorial**, Zidan’s library was seized at sea in **1612** and incorporated in **1614**. **[R]** The approximate 1614 stop does not establish that a *Ghaya* manuscript arrived with it. Although the Ahmad dossier lists Escorial Arabic holdings, the specific Zidan provenance remains unproved. Repository presence and a particular acquisition history are separate claims. ([Qurtubi report](../maslama_al_qurtubi.report.md); [Ahmad dossier](../dossiers/ahmad_al_majriti.dossier.md))

**[R]** The modern recovery also contains conflicting dates: Printz’s identification of the Arabic source is placed around **1914** in the Ahmad dossier, around **1920** in reports, and approximately **1925** in the journey. Istanbul is asserted in one account and treated as unverified in another. These files support neither an exact discovery date nor an uncontested library location. ([Ahmad dossier](../dossiers/ahmad_al_majriti.dossier.md); [Ahmad report](../ahmad_al_majriti.report.md); [Qurtubi journey](../maslama_al_qurtubi.journey.json))

**[A]** **Ritter’s Arabic edition appeared in 1933**, as volume 12 of *Studien der Bibliothek Warburg*, with a Leipzig/Berlin Teubner imprint. Hamburg identifies the library and series; Ritter himself was resident in Istanbul. Ritter and Martin Plessner’s German translation followed in **1962**. **Pingree’s critical Latin edition appeared in London in 1986**, as *Studies of the Warburg Institute* 39; Brown marks his institutional association. **[R]** The corresponding January 1 stops are approximate year markers. ([Qurtubi report](../maslama_al_qurtubi.report.md); [Qurtubi journey](../maslama_al_qurtubi.journey.json))

**[A]** In **2019**, Dan Attrell and David Porreca published *Picatrix: A Medieval Treatise on Astral Magic*, translated from Pingree’s Latin edition. The report notes its catalogue attribution to Maslamah ibn Ahmad al-Majriti despite its citation of Fierro. **[R]** The Pennsylvania journey stop again supplies an approximate publication-year marker. The surviving catalogue name demonstrates persistence of an attribution, not renewed proof of authorship. ([Qurtubi report](../maslama_al_qurtubi.report.md); [Qurtubi journey](../maslama_al_qurtubi.journey.json))

## 4. Its afterlife

**[A]** **Agrippa** provides a substantive textual connection. His dossier places *Picatrix* material among the medieval and Arabic-Latin authorities contributing to his enlarged source base during his Italian years. The connections table records his use of its conjurational and astromagical tradition. The Qurtubi dossier also reports that he criticized its author for mixing natural magic with superstition. Reception here includes criticism as well as borrowing. ([Agrippa dossier](../dossiers/heinrich_cornelius_agrippa.dossier.md:75); [connections.csv](../connections.csv); [Qurtubi dossier](../dossiers/maslama_al_qurtubi.dossier.md))

**[R]** **Lovecraft supplies no established Picatrix connection in the requested evidence.** His report explicitly rejects the proposed Brown University association as shared institutional geography. Nothing in that passage establishes that he read the book or derived the *Necronomicon* from it. The attractive comparison cannot become a transmission claim. ([Lovecraft report](../h_p_lovecraft.report.md:257))

**[A]** The Picatrix rows in `connections.csv` name Pico, Agrippa, Trithemius, Paracelsus, Bruno, Campanella, Maimonides, Albertus Magnus, Yates, and Warburg, with mirrored rows repeating the relationships. **[R]** Those repetitions are not independent corroboration, and the common `wrote_about` classification conceals different evidentiary strengths. ([connections.csv](../connections.csv))

**[R]** Trithemius is a reported reader; Paracelsus a reported reader or inheritor whose direct borrowing remains uncertain. Bruno’s connection concerns images, cosmology, and magic, interpreted partly through Yates’s subsequently revised account. Campanella belongs to a later planetary and natural-magic tradition. Maimonides’ criticism concerns related astral literature without established use of this exact book; Albertus’s contextual connection lacks a specific citation. These should not become an uninterrupted chain of direct discipleship. ([connections.csv](../connections.csv); [Ahmad dossier](../dossiers/ahmad_al_majriti.dossier.md))

**[A]** Warburg sought *Picatrix* manuscripts, while Yates studied the work and incorporated it into her account of Renaissance Hermeticism. **[R]** Their connection is scholarly reception. Likewise, Plato and Aristotle enter the book’s source-world partly through pseudonymous texts, while Solomon and Zoroaster belong to legendary authority traditions. These are different relationships from identifiable textual borrowing. ([Ahmad dossier](../dossiers/ahmad_al_majriti.dossier.md); [Qurtubi dossier](../dossiers/maslama_al_qurtubi.dossier.md))

## 5. Open questions and gaps

**[R]** Authorship remains strongly argued rather than autograph-proven. The files cannot eliminate multiple redactions or later compilation, resolve every internal date, or show precisely when the astronomer’s name first became attached to each work. They also cannot demonstrate an independent “Ahmad al-Majriti.” ([Ahmad dossier](../dossiers/ahmad_al_majriti.dossier.md))

**[R]** Biography remains unevenly researched. The Qurtubi report’s claims that there is no birth date, teacher, student, or travel evidence cannot be repeated as the atlas’s final position: his dossier supplies reported or reconstructed information in those categories. Yet household details, income, burial place, and the circumstances of composition remain unknown in the supplied research. ([Qurtubi report](../maslama_al_qurtubi.report.md); [Qurtubi dossier](../dossiers/maslama_al_qurtubi.dossier.md))

**[R]** Transmission leaves identifiable gaps: the translators and their working location; the extent of surviving Castilian material; the exact English manuscript; the Escorial’s acquisition history; and Printz’s discovery date and location. The meaning and derivation of “Picatrix” also remain unsettled. Proposed explanations involving Buqratis or other linguistic transformations are hypotheses. ([Ahmad report](../ahmad_al_majriti.report.md); [Ahmad dossier](../dossiers/ahmad_al_majriti.dossier.md); [Qurtubi report](../maslama_al_qurtubi.report.md))

**[R]** Finally, these sources cannot determine which prescriptions the compiler personally practiced, how fully he believed his collected materials, or whether his esoteric interests implied formal sectarian membership. They establish neither a coordinated suppression of his identity nor a Lovecraft connection. Those limits belong inside the reading: they distinguish the history recoverable from this atlas from the further history it still asks someone to research. ([Qurtubi dossier](../dossiers/maslama_al_qurtubi.dossier.md); [Ahmad dossier](../dossiers/ahmad_al_majriti.dossier.md); [Lovecraft report](../h_p_lovecraft.report.md:257))
