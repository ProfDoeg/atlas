# Junayd al-Baghdadi, research report

*Abu al-Qasim al-Junayd ibn Muhammad al-Nahawandi al-Baghdadi, called al-Khazzaz and al-Qawariri. c. 215 AH / c. 830 CE to 297-298 AH / 910 CE. Baghdad, almost entirely.*

**Method.** Junayd's own writing survives (thirty-one treatises and letters, edited once, in 1962), but the life around it comes through hagiography compiled two to four centuries later: al-Sulami, al-Qushayri, Hujwiri, Attar. Where a saying could be traced to a fetched primary translation, that wording is used and the page given. The largest finding of this pass is that several of the most-repeated details about Junayd are wrong in circulation: the wrong city for the childhood episode, the wrong trade, an invented duration, an invented mosque, and a composite quotation welded out of two books.

**Legend.** **[A]** attested, source named. **[R]** reconstruction or tradition, including everything reaching us only through hagiography. Contradictions are flagged and left standing.

## 1. Origins and childhood (c. 830 to c. 840)

- Born in Baghdad c. 215 AH / c. 830 CE **[A: Arabic Wikipedia, 'al-Junayd al-Baghdadi'; English Wikipedia, 'Junayd of Baghdad']**. No day or month recorded; some sources place the birth as early as 210 AH.
- **The quarter is not attested.** An attribution of the birth household to Karkh reached this desk citing Encyclopaedia Iranica; **Iranica was not reachable (HTTP 403)**, neither Wikipedia article names a quarter, and the coordinate offered with the attribution (33.3315, 44.3721) does not match Karkh's own (33.3167, 44.40). **[R, weakly]** The journey file uses a generic Baghdad point.
- Family of Persian origin, traced to Nihawand, hence the nisba al-Nahawandi **[A: Arabic Wikipedia]**. No source places Junayd in the town or in contact with anyone there; genealogical only.
- The father trades in glassware (byname al-Qawariri); Junayd trades in silk (al-Khazzaz) **[A: Arabic Wikipedia]**. *See contradiction 2.*
- Orphaned young; raised by his maternal uncle Sari al-Saqati, scrap dealer turned ascetic, his first and principal master **[A: English Wikipedia; Wikipedia, 'Sari al-Saqati']**. The year of the father's death and the boy's age at it are given nowhere reachable. **Gap.**
- Sari prays that his nephew be given hadith before tasawwuf, not tasawwuf before hadith **[A: Wikipedia, 'Sari al-Saqati']**. This is the later doctrine in one sentence of family lore, and is exactly the anecdote a sober school preserves about its founder.
- At about seven, taken on hajj by Sari; before four hundred shaykhs he defines shukr, and they agree unanimously that no better definition exists **[R: Attar]**. Verified wording, fetched primary, Attar, *Muslim Saints and Mystics*, trans. Arberry, p. 269: "'Thankfulness,' said Jonaid, 'means that you should not disobey God by means of the favour which He has bestowed on you, nor make of His favour a source of disobedience.'" *See contradiction 1 on the venue.*
- A claim that the boy gave **forty distinct formulations** of shukr is **fabricated**. The sources record one definition, unanimously approved. The number appears to have migrated in from the unrelated legend of the first forty listeners.

## 2. Training in law and hadith (c. 838 to 857)

- **The teacher list will not survive a calendar.** **Abu Ubayd al-Qasim ibn Sallam** died 224 AH / 838 CE **in Mecca**, when Junayd was about eight and had never left Baghdad. **Bishr al-Hafi** died 841, when Junayd was about eleven, and Wikipedia's article on Bishr does not list Junayd among his transmitters. These are isnad attributions, lineage assembled backward, not attested study **[R]**. The research pool tagged the whole teacher list **[A]**; that tag is corrected here.
- Names that survive the calendar, and the men he actually studied hadith, law and the states under: Sari al-Saqati, al-Harith al-Muhasibi, al-Hasan ibn Arafa, Abu Thawr al-Kalbi **[A: Arabic and English Wikipedia]**.
- Studies under Ibrahim ibn Khalid, called Abu Thawr, and is permitted to issue fatwas in his teacher's own circle at about twenty, c. 850 **[A: Arabic Wikipedia; Wikipedia, 'Abu Thawr']**.
- Remembered as combining "the heart of the ascetic with the intellect of the jurist" **[A: Arabic Wikipedia]**. The phrase is genuine. **Naming hazard:** calling Abu Thawr's circle the "Thawri school" confuses it with the school of Sufyan al-Thawri (d. 161/778); English Wikipedia classes Junayd as Shafi'i. The journey file writes "Abu Thawr's circle".
- Al-Muhasibi, from whom Junayd takes muhasaba, self-examination, dies in Baghdad in 243 AH / 857 CE **[A: Wikipedia, 'Al-Muhasibi']**.

## 3. The shop, and the beginning of public teaching (c. 855 to c. 870)

- Keeps a shop, lives by trade, spends most of his time in prayer, refuses payment for instruction **[R: English Wikipedia and the biographical dictionaries]**. But **the "forty years" of private discipline before public teaching is unsupported**: English Wikipedia says only that "Junayd went back to Baghdad and took up selling glasses. However, he spent most of his time in prayer." No duration appears anywhere in it, and the span collides with the same pool's geography, which has him teaching publicly from the 850s. **Flagged, not resolved.**
- Refuses to preach until he receives what he takes as divine sanction in a dream of the Prophet **[R: Nicholson, *The Mystics of Islam*]**: "Sari al-Saqati frequently urged Junayd to speak in public, but Junayd was unwilling to consent until he received guidance from the Prophet in a dream."
- Of the first forty listeners, eighteen died on the spot and twenty-two fainted **[R: hagiographic tradition via English Wikipedia and Attar]**. Undated, uncorroborated, of a type that attaches to several masters.
- Becomes the leading figure of a distinct Baghdad school, later called sayyid al-ta'ifa, teaching sober law-abiding mysticism against ecstatic utterance; credited also as the first to expound tawhid as formal instruction inside organized Sufi teaching **[A: Arabic Wikipedia, which is the interested party]**. Priority cannot be tested against unrecorded rooms.

## 4. Doctrine and the sayings

Four sayings traced to fetched primary translations; one found to be fabricated in part.

- **On tasawwuf.** Kalabadhi, *The Doctrine of the Sufis (Kitab al-Ta'arruf)*, trans. Arberry (Cambridge, 1935), ch. I, p. 10, verified in the Internet Archive full text **[A]**: "It is the purification of the heart from associating with created beings, separation from natural characteristics, suppression of human qualities ... counselling all the community, being truly faithful to God, and following the Prophet according to the Law." Full text in the journey file.
- **The sentence welded to the front of that in circulation is not Junayd's and not Kalabadhi's.** "Sufism is that God makes thee die to thyself and become resurrected in Him" appears nowhere in that chapter; searches for "die to thyself", "live in Him" and "resurrect" return nothing. The nearest real text is Nicholson, *The Mystics of Islam*: "that God should make thee die to thyself and should make thee live in Him", printed **anonymously** among "a few specimens" of definitions. The composite is discarded. **[flagged fabrication]**
- **On the gnostic.** Kalabadhi, p. 139 **[A]**: "Junayd, being asked concerning the gnostic, said: 'The colour of the water is the colour of the vessel.'" The popular English ("the water takes on the color of the cup", via Michael Sells, 1996) changes the wording and shifts the subject from the knower to divine self-disclosure. Sells could not be opened; primary wording used.
- **On poverty**, and **to the poor**. Hujwiri, *Kashf al-Mahjub*, trans. Nicholson (1911), ch. II, p. 27 **[A]**: "When his heart is empty of phenomena he is poor." And: "O ye that are poor, ye are known through God, and are honoured for the sake of God: take heed how ye behave when ye are alone with Him." The version circulating with "honoured for God's sake" and "when alone with Him" is misquoted. Also verified in the same chapter, p. 23, his answer to Ibn Ata on wealth and poverty before the judgment, and in Nicholson's *Mystics of Islam* his refusal to sell Shibli the jewel of divine knowledge **[A as fetched texts; R as history, drawn from Attar and Jami]**.
- **On tawhid.** Abdel-Kader, *The Life, Personality and Writings of al-Junayd* (Luzac, 1962), p. 177, also at p. 75, via the Internet Archive **[A]**: "existence without individuality (shakhs) before God with no third person as intermediary between them ... completely obliterated both from himself and from God's call to him and his answer to God." Two corrections: the familiar English ("drowned in the sea of His unity, self-effaced") is a secondary paraphrase, not Abdel-Kader's translation; and this is not Junayd's definition of tawhid as such but the **second and highest** of his types of esoteric tawhid, the first preserving the declarant's individuality.
- Roughly thirty-one treatises and letters on tawhid, fana and baqa, and the conduct of seekers **[A: Arabic Wikipedia]**.

## 5. The network beyond Baghdad

Junayd appears never to have travelled except on pilgrimage. Every connection east and west of Baghdad is epistolary or textual.

- **Dhu'l-Nun al-Misri.** Reports link Junayd to his teaching; sources disagree whether this means a meeting or only second-hand transmission, and the disagreement is **left standing [R]**. The pool placed Dhu'l-Nun at **Fustat**; that is wrong. English Wikipedia: born Akhmim (796), died at **Giza** c. 859-862. His one documented Baghdad journey ran the other way, on a heresy charge in 829. **No source places Junayd in Egypt.**
- **Yahya ibn Mu'adh al-Razi**, named among his correspondents and routinely placed at Rayy, was not there: he left Rayy for Balkh and then Nishapur, where he died in 871. Any correspondence belongs to Khurasan **[R, retargeted]**. **Abu Uthman al-Hiri of Nishapur**: correspondence attested only in general tradition, not independently verified **[R]**.
- **Abu Yazid al-Bistami.** Junayd translates Bayazid's Persian sayings into Arabic although the two never met (Iranica states flatly that no record of a meeting exists); Sari al-Saqati links the circles. Hujwiri later names two rival schools from the pair, the drunken Tayfuriya against the sober Junaydiya. **[A: abu_yazid_al_bistami.journey.json]**

## 6. Disciples, the inquisition, and al-Hallaj

- **Ghulam Khalil's campaign** against the Baghdad Sufis, c. 264-266 AH / 877-880, brings several of Junayd's associates before the qadi; his standing as a licensed jurist is the reason usually given for his exemption. **[R: al-Sulami's Tabaqat tradition as summarized in Karamustafa, *Sufism: The Formative Period*; the Wikipedia page on Ghulam Khalil was not reachable.]**
- **Abu Bakr al-Shibli**, principal disciple **[A]**; the training story (salt-trading, begging where he had held office) is **[R]**, hagiographic and undated. **Ahmad al-Jariri**, leading student and transmitter **[A: Arabic Wikipedia]**.
- **Ruwaym ibn Ahmad** and **Abu Sa'id al-Kharraz**, same milieu, attested only in general tradition **[R]**. Ruwaym is **not** a link in the initiatic chains: English Wikipedia's 'Sufism' runs the Qadiriyya, Suhrawardiyya and Kubrawiya silsilas from Junayd to **Sari al-Saqati**. The pool's insertion of Ruwaym into the silsila is corrected out.
- **Al-Hallaj**, born c. 858 in Fars, took the Sufi habit from Amr al-Makki at Basra at about twenty and came to Baghdad to consult Junayd **[A: English Wikipedia]**. The **year is not in the article**; the pool's "878" is unsourced inference. The article places his departure at the crushing of the Zanj rebellion, 883; hallaj.journey.json dates the first door to June 876. **Range c. 876-883, open.** He later sets out for Mecca **against Junayd's explicit advice**, and **Amr ibn Uthman al-Makki** is reported to have said he would have killed him with his own hands **[A]**.
- **"Ana al-Haqq."** Sources conflict on whether it was said privately to Junayd or proclaimed publicly in a mosque; **not reconciled in the record, not reconciled here [R]**. The disownment and the prediction of death on a piece of wood are attested only in the Massignon-derived tradition **[R]**.
- Al-Hallaj was imprisoned some nine years and executed in Baghdad on **26 March 922**, twelve years after Junayd's death **[A]**. No source gives Junayd a role in that trial, because he was dead. Hagiography nonetheless brings him back to sign the death warrant "in the gown of the academy rather than the Sufi robe"; hallaj.journey.json flags this time-fold, and so does the journey file here.

## 7. Death and burial (910)

- Dies in Baghdad aged 79 or 80. Arabic Wikipedia: 297 or 298 AH. English Wikipedia: 910 CE, range 296-298 AH. Gibb Trust catalogue: "al-Junayd (d. 910)". **[A within a range]** The pool's "909" mismatches every source and its own other lenses; **corrected to 910**.
- **No source names a place of death** beyond the city. The pool's "dies at the Shuniziyya" overstates: both articles say he died in Baghdad and was **buried** there. A "Shuniziyya Mosque" used as venue for his teaching, writing and break with al-Hallaj appears to be invented; its coordinate (33.326, 44.373) is not used here.
- Final illness: continued praying, and when asked, replied that this was his wird **[R]**. Funeral prayer repeated seven times as mourners kept arriving **[R: uncorroborated]**.
- Buried in the **Shunuziyya cemetery** in Karkh beside Sari al-Saqati, who died in 867: forty-three years, so "more than four decades" is exact **[A: Wikipedia, 'Sari al-Saqati'; Arabic Wikipedia]**.
- **Coordinate correction.** The cemetery is today the **Sheikh Ma'ruf Cemetery**, "also known as Maqbarat Shuniziyyah", grown around the grave of Ma'ruf al-Karkhi (d. 815). Its infobox coordinate is **33.3344130, 44.3746859**; the figures circulating with this subject (33.326, 44.373 and 33.3241, 44.3661) are 1 to 1.4 km off. The journey file uses 33.3344, 44.3747, already the Baghdad pin in rumi.journey.json and ibn_arabi.journey.json.
- **Flag:** neither Wikipedia article on the cemetery lists Junayd or Sari al-Saqati among its notable burials. That link comes only from the two men's own articles.

## 8. Afterlife

- **Attar of Nishapur**, *Tadhkirat al-Awliya*, is the chief hagiographic channel and the source of most sayings in circulation **[A]**. **Date correction:** Attar is c. 1145 to c. 1221, killed in the Mongol sack of Nishapur, and the work was compiled across much of his life. "12th century" is a generation early; "late twelfth to early thirteenth, commonly c. 1220" is accurate.
- **Mi'raj forgery, 395 AH / 1005 CE.** An embellished ascent text circulating under the name of a certain Abu'l-Qasim is taken for al-Junayd's; the confusion is documented and tied to his kunya. **[A: abu_yazid_al_bistami.journey.json]**
- **Travellers of the corpus at the grave.** Ibn Arabi, Baghdad 1205, twelve days in "the city of al-Junayd and al-Hallaj before him". Rumi, about twelve, taken by Baha al-Din to pray at the tombs of Junayd and Ma'ruf al-Karkhi, c. October 1219. Ibn Battuta, 1327, recording al-Hallaj's execution and the scattering of his ashes. Abdelkader at the Jilani shrine, 1826, the Qadiriyya lineage descending through the sober Baghdad tradition **[R for that descent]**. **[A: ibn_arabi, rumi, ibn_battuta, abdelkader journey files]**
- **Abdel-Kader's edition**, Gibb Memorial Trust / Luzac, London, 1962: Arabic Rasa'il with English translation and biographical study **[A: Gibb Trust catalogue; HathiTrust and Internet Archive records]**; reissued in paperback by Edinburgh University Press for the Trust, 2014 **[A: gibbtrust.org]**. The Internet Archive item 'The Rasail Of Junayd', uploaded 4 November 2017, carries **no author, translator, publisher or publication year**, so "scans of Abdel-Kader's edition circulate" overstates what that page establishes **[A for existence; R for identity]**. The nur.nu repositories were not checked.
- **The shrine.** Tomb expanded into a mosque and shrine complex of roughly 2,500 square metres, renovated 1980, in Karkh **[A: Arabic Wikipedia]**. This **contradicts** the pool's claim that no monument distinct from the grave exists; the narrower true version is that no *state-sponsored secular* memorial or museum was found. **2023:** exhumations ordered across the Sheikh Ma'ruf cemetery for a road scheme; a fatwa permitted relocation, residents protested, and the authorities opted for a bridge over the cemetery instead, leaving affected families to bear the costs **[A: Wikipedia, 'Sheikh Ma'ruf Cemetery']**. The shrine's condition after this is undocumented.
- Named **sayyid al-ta'ifa** and placed at the head of later silsilas, always via Sari al-Saqati; Junayd himself names **Ali ibn Abi Talib** as the foundational shaykh of Sufism's principles **[A: English Wikipedia, 'Sufism']**. Devotional sites style him "1st Qutb" **[R: sufism.ir; only the title verified]**.

## Contradictions flagged, not adjudicated

1. **Mecca or Medina** for the shukr episode. Arberry's Attar: the Mosque of the Sanctuary. English Wikipedia: al-Masjid an-Nabawi. Both are carried in the journey file, as two stops.
2. **Silk or glass.** Glassware to the father and silk to Junayd (Arabic) against Junayd "selling glasses" (English).
3. **Date of the first meeting with al-Hallaj.** June 876 (hallaj.journey.json) against no year plus a departure in 883 (English Wikipedia).
4. **"Ana al-Haqq": private or public.** Unreconciled in the sources.
5. **Death year.** 296, 297 or 298 AH; 910 CE. Not narrowed by anything consulted.
6. **Shrine or bare grave.** A 2,500 square metre complex against a reported absence of any monument beyond the grave.
7. **Baghdad's coordinate.** The atlas carries three: 33.333 / 44.363 (hallaj), 33.3344 / 44.3747 (rumi, ibn_arabi), 33.3152 / 44.3661 (abu_yazid_al_bistami). All three are inherited byte-identical rather than averaged, each used where its own file used it: hallaj's for the al-Hallaj encounters, rumi's and ibn_arabi's for the cemetery and tomb, bistami's for generic Baghdad. Abdelkader's Jilani pin (33.3364 / 44.4076) is inherited for that stop. Mecca is the canonical Kaaba pin, 21.4225 / 39.8262.

## Gaps, stated as gaps

No exact birth date, only "c. 215 AH" with a floor as early as 210 AH. No birth quarter. No date for the father's death and no age for Junayd at it. No duration for the private years before public teaching. No place of death beyond "Baghdad", and no day or month. No independent attestation of the Ghulam Khalil episode's bearing on Junayd. No verification of the Abu Uthman al-Hiri correspondence beyond general tradition. No documented condition of the shrine after 2023.
