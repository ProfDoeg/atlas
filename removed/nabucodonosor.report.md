# Nabucodonosor II (Nebuchadnezzar II) of Babylon: research report

*2026-08-02. Atlas of Journeys. Nabu-kudurri-usur II, second king of the Neo-Babylonian dynasty, r. 605-562 BC. Slug: `nabucodonosor`.*

Legend: **[A]** = attested, source named · **[R]** = reconstruction or tradition. Contradictions are flagged and left standing. Gaps are stated as gaps.

## Method: what kind of evidence this reign has

Four classes carry nearly everything, and they do not overlap where it would help most.

1. **The Babylonian Chronicle series**, principally ABC 5 / BM (WA) 21946. Contemporary cuneiform, terse, campaign by campaign. **It runs out**: the surviving portion ends with the eleventh year, 594 BC, leaving a documentary hole of roughly 594-557 BC. The events this king is famous for fall inside it. **[A]**
2. **His own building inscriptions**, in quantity. Dense on brick and bitumen and gods, nearly silent on war: they mention no siege of Jerusalem, no siege of Tyre, and no garden. **[A]**
3. **The Hebrew Bible**, and in a wholly different evidentiary class, Daniel. For the destruction of Jerusalem the biblical account is the only narrative source; the Babylonian tablet for that year is not preserved.
4. **Berossus**, writing c. 290 BC, whose history is lost and survives only where Josephus quotes it. Third-hand at best.

Archaeology supplies the fifth line: Koldewey at Babylon, Stager at Ashkelon, Starkey at Lachish, the Weidner ration tablets.

## 1. Birth and the priesthood at Uruk (c. 642 BC?)

**[A]** Eldest son of Nabopolassar, the Chaldean who broke Assyria and founded the dynasty; so his own inscriptions. **[A]** Attested as *sanga*, high priest, of the Eanna temple of Ishtar at Uruk from 626/625 BC, in temple archive texts.

**Contradiction flagged, not resolved.** The pool carried two incompatible estimates: *c. 642 BC at Uruk*, derived from the Eanna priesthood, and *no earlier than c. 630 BC* in an unnamed Babylonian city, restated elsewhere as *c. 634 BC in the Southern Palace at Babylon* and derived by counting back from the reign length. The two entries contradict each other, the method claim does not survive checking, and a birth after 630 cannot be squared with a priesthood in 626. **The journey adopts c. 642 BC at Uruk, date_confidence inferred.** **[R]** in any case: no birth record survives.

## 2. Crown prince and field commander (c. 610-605 BC)

**[A]** Present at Nabopolassar's capture of Harran from the Assyrian rump under Ashur-uballit II, 610 BC; a contemporary letter puts him in the column. **[A]** Commands an army for several months in mountainous country, 607 BC (ABC 5). The Chronicle does not identify the mountains. **Gap:** the pool guessed "likely Zagros or Taurus foothills." Not carried; the stop is omitted rather than pinned to invented coordinates.

**[A] Carchemish, 605 BC.** He crosses the Euphrates and destroys Necho II's Egyptian force with its Assyrian allies. Jeremiah 46:2 dates a prophecy to the battle by name. **[A] Hamath:** the pursuit down the Orontes catches the remnant, and the Chronicle closes the season with the conquest of the whole of Hatti, the scribal term for Syria-Palestine.

**Contradiction flagged and corrected.** The pool dated Nabopolassar's death to 605-08-16 and the accession to 605-08-15: the father dying the day after the son took the throne. Impossible. The news reached the camp on 8 Abu, late July 605. **The journey places the death in July and the news at the frontier camp in late July. [A]**

**[R]** The dash home across the desert with a small escort, leaving war, captives and plunder to his officers, is Berossus via Josephus (*Against Apion* 1.19). No cuneiform corroboration; the camp is unnamed, so the Pelusium pin marks a theatre, not a findspot, and the campa says so.

## 3. Accession (605 BC)

**[A]** He sits on the throne 15 August 605 BC; the formal coronation is separate, 7 September; his first regnal year begins the following Nisan, spring 604.

**Error corrected.** The pool's geography lens collapsed these into one stop dated "1 Elul, 605 BC (7 September)." 1 Ululu fell in mid-August. The parenthetical was wrong by three weeks and contradicted the pool's own chronology, which had it right. The journey keeps the dates apart.

## 4. The Hatti-land campaigns, 605-594 BC

All **[A]** on ABC 5 unless marked.

- **604, Ashkelon.** Turned into a ruin heap, its king deported. Corroborated in the ground by the destruction layer at Tel Ashkelon (Stager); the reports were not consulted directly, and the corroboration comes from summary literature.
- **603.** A campaign with siege engines against a city the Chronicle does not name. **Gap.** Not pinned, not carried. **602.** Levantine circuit, tribute and booty.
- **601, the Egyptian frontier.** Both armies take heavy losses; Babylon withdraws. The only reverse the preserved Chronicle admits.
- **599, Kedar.** Raid on the Arab tribes of the northern steppe. **No site named.** Jeremiah 49:28-33 preserves an oracle usually read against it. The journey pins the stop at Dumat al-Jandal and says in the campa that the tablet names no place.
- **597, 2 Adar = 16 March, Jerusalem.** First siege; surrender; Jehoiachin deported; Zedekiah installed. The one event where a Babylonian tablet and a biblical chapter can be read side by side. They agree.
- **595.** A rebellion inside Babylon, put down; one clause, no detail. **Gap stated as gap:** the record documents armies leaving Babylon, not the politics of the city they leave. **594.** Last campaign in the surviving Chronicle.

**[A]** Jehoiakim, an Egyptian appointee, becomes Nebuchadnezzar's servant for three years and rebels (2 Kings 24:1). **No Babylonian text names Jerusalem in this window;** the submission is known only from the biblical account, and the journey says so.

**Error corrected.** The pool wrote that Jehoiachin was "eighteen years old and eighteen days on the throne." The reign length is wrong: 2 Kings 24:8 gives three months, 2 Chronicles 36:9 three months and ten days (9 December 598 to 15/16 March 597). "Eighteen" appears to have duplicated out of the age, which is retained per 2 Kings 24:8; 2 Chronicles 36:9's "eight years old" is generally treated as a copyist's error, noted, not adopted.

**Inference flagged.** The pool set Zedekiah's installation at Riblah citing 2 Kings 24:17 and Ezekiel 17:13-19. Neither names Riblah; the setting is inferred from the documented use of Riblah in 587 (2 Kings 25:6, 25:20-21). The journey keeps the stop and declares the inference in the campa.

**The documentary gap, 594-557 BC.** After 594 the destruction of Jerusalem, the years at Tyre, Gedaliah and the Egyptian campaign of 568 all fall into a hole in the contemporary cuneiform, and are reconstructed from the Hebrew Bible, from Josephus, from excavation, and from one broken tablet (BM 33041). The building inscriptions run right through these years and mention none of it.

## 5. The fall of Jerusalem (589-587 BC)

**[A] on 2 Kings 25 and Jeremiah 39, 52. No Babylonian Chronicle entry survives for any of it.**

- **589, tenth month, tenth day of Zedekiah's ninth year.** The siege begins (2 Kings 25:1). Roughly thirty months, interrupted once when Pharaoh Hophra marches; Jeremiah is arrested at the gate trying to leave during the interval (Jeremiah 37:5-11). Famine: Lamentations 4:10.
- **588/587, Lachish.** The ostraca, found in 1935 by **James Leslie Starkey** (the pool had "James Leon Starkey"). Letter IV reports watching for the fire signals of Lachish because Azekah can no longer be seen; Jeremiah 34:7 names the two as the last fortified cities standing. **The "gate guardroom" findspot in the pool is not corroborated and is dropped.**
- **587, summer, Riblah.** Zedekiah is taken near Jericho, watches his sons killed, is blinded, goes to Babylon in bronze fetters (2 Kings 25:4-7; Jeremiah 52:8-11). **587, month of Av:** Nebuzaradan burns the Temple, the palace and every great house, and the walls come down.

**Two contradictions flagged, both left standing in date_confidence.** *The day:* 2 Kings 25:8 gives the seventh of Av, Jeremiah 52:12 the tenth. *The year:* 587 and 586 both appear across the literature and the pool used both. Neither is adopted as settled.

**Gedaliah at Mizpah.** The pool asserted the murder came "roughly two months after his appointment," one reading (2 Kings 25:25) presented as settled. Wikipedia's 'Gedaliah' puts it at 582/581 BC and says outright that the dates are not clear from the Bible; that reckoning ties the murder to the third deportation of Jeremiah 52:30. The journey states the disagreement and adopts neither. **Coordinate corrected:** Tell en-Nasbeh is **31.8851 / 35.2164**, not the pool's 31.9022 / 35.1917, which lies about 3 km northwest in open ground. The identification of the tell as Mizpah is itself "possibly," and the campa flags it.

## 6. Tyre, the coast, Transjordan, Egypt (586-568 BC)

**[R] Tyre, thirteen years.** The figure rests on **Josephus alone** (*Against Apion* 1.21, drawing on Menander and Phoenician annals now lost) and is disputed. **A pool claim is withdrawn:** it said a variant manuscript tradition gives "three years and ten months," a discrepancy never resolved. No such variant is attested for Tyre in reachable sources. **The real inconsistency is internal to Josephus,** who elsewhere dates the siege's start to the seventh year, 598/597, which does not fit his own thirteen years ending 573. Flagged in date_confidence. **[A]** Ezekiel 29:17-20 concedes the siege earned no plunder worth the labour, every head rubbed bare and every shoulder worn, and promises Egypt as the wages instead.

**[R] Sidon.** The pool inferred submission from the *absence* of Sidon in any siege narrative. Argument from silence; the stop is dropped rather than pinned. **[R] Ammon and Moab, 582 BC:** Josephus, *Antiquities* 10.9.7, no Babylonian corroboration and no site named. Jeremiah 48-49 anticipates such a reckoning, but prophecy and later narrative history are different evidentiary classes. Not carried, for want of any locatable place.

**[A] Egypt, thirty-seventh year, 568/567 BC.** BM 33041, fragmentary; the break falls where the outcome would be. Migdol's own location is disputed; the pin is approximate and says so.

## 7. The building program

**[A]** on his own inscriptions, and on Koldewey for the fabric: Esagila, Etemenanki, the Ishtar Gate and Processional Way, the palaces, the Median Wall, Ezida at Borsippa, Ebabbar at Sippar, Eanna at Uruk, work at Kish, and the rock texts at Wadi Brisa and Nahr el-Kalb.

**Contradiction flagged: the date of the Ishtar Gate.** c. 575 BC (older conventional figure, in the pool's afterlife lens) versus c. 569 BC (current Wikipedia). The journey uses 569 and states both in date_confidence.

**Attribution corrected: the ziggurat of Ur.** The pool credited Nebuchadnezzar with adding a stage and facing to the ziggurat of Nanna, citing Woolley. That restoration was **Nabonidus's**: of Woolley's three layers the lowest is Ur-Nammu's and the two upper are Nabonidus's. Nebuchadnezzar did build at Ur (temenos wall, temple work), but not that. **The Ur stop is removed rather than silently reassigned.**

**Unverified, entered as such.** Two inscribed cylinders from the ziggurat mound at Kish, published in *Iraq*. Not reachable, not checked. The stop is kept and the campa says so.

**Coordinates corrected**, all four silently wrong in the pool. Esagila: **32.5339 / 44.4214**, not 32.5368 / 44.4211, which put it ~300 m north of Etemenanki when it lies south. Sippar (Tell Abu Habbah): **33.0588 / 44.2522**, not 33.0447 / 44.2919, ~4 km southeast of the mound. Kish (Tell al-Uhaymir): **32.5403 / 44.6047**, not 32.5439 / 44.6415, ~3.5 km too far east. Wadi Brisa: **34.352 / 36.384**, not 34.1667 / 36.4167, ~20 km too far south; the pool's own afterlife lens had it right.

**[R] The Hanging Gardens.** Berossus via Josephus: high walks on stone pillars, a pensile paradise to please a queen raised in Media. Against it: his own inscriptions never mention a garden; no confirmed structure at Babylon; Diodorus credits the terraces to an unnamed Syrian king for a concubine; Dalley argues they stood at Nineveh and were Sennacherib's. **Not resolved.** Carried as tradition, at the Babil mound, with the objections in the campa.

**[R] Amytis.** Berossus names her daughter of Cyaxares; the identification is reconstructed, not given, and one reading makes her Astyages's sister. **The pool's marriage date of 610 BC is attested nowhere and is dropped.** No contemporary Babylonian record names any wife of Nebuchadnezzar. Gap.

## 8. Daniel: three episodes, one verdict

**[A]** that the texts exist and say what they say. **[R]** as history. Daniel 2, the statue with the head of gold and feet of iron and clay; Daniel 3, the golden image on the plain of Dura and the furnace; Daniel 4, the seven years of madness. The final form of Daniel is dated by most scholars to c. 167-164 BC, four centuries after the reign. **No Babylonian administrative or chronicle text records any interruption of the rule**, and the madness is absent from Berossus, who had the temple records in front of him.

**Likeliest transmission, flagged as reconstruction:** the *Prayer of Nabonidus* (4Q242, Qumran) tells nearly the same story of **Nabonidus**, afflicted seven years at Teima and healed, and Nabonidus's long absence from Babylon is documented. The legend probably began with him and migrated to the more famous name. The fragment does not prove the transfer; it puts the older version in the ground.

## 9. Death and succession (562-560 BC)

**[A]** Dies at Babylon 7 October 562 BC, after forty-three years, at roughly eighty. **[A]** Amel-Marduk, the Evil-Merodach of 2 Kings 25:27, succeeds and in his first year releases Jehoiachin from prison. **[A, Berossus]** He is murdered c. 560, ending the direct line within two years. Twenty-three years later Cyrus takes Babylon.

**[A, negative]** No royal tomb has ever been identified, and no Neo-Babylonian royal necropolis has been located anywhere. Koldewey dug the city for eighteen years and found palaces, gates, temples and ration tablets, and no king's grave. Negative evidence, not proof no tomb existed.

## 10. Afterlife

**[A, corrected] The East India House Inscription.** Ten columns of archaising cuneiform listing the building works. Two corrections: it is **limestone, not "a large clay tablet,"** and it entered the **British Museum in 1938** (reg. 1938,0520.1), after the East India House collections were dispersed, not in the nineteenth century. Provenance otherwise right: found before 1803 by Sir Harford Jones Brydges, Resident in Baghdad. **[R]** Lithographed under Rawlinson's direction.

**[A, corrected] The Ishtar Gate in Berlin.** Excavated in fragments 1902-1914 (the pool's 1899-1917 is the Babylon expedition overall), reassembled in the Pergamon Museum, which opened in 1930. Only the smaller frontal gate was rebuilt; the larger rear section is in storage. **The pool's "estimated 80 percent modern replacement brick" is unsupported and is dropped.**

**[A] Modern editions.** Langdon 1912 is the German VAB 4; his English *Building Inscriptions of the Neo-Babylonian Empire, Part 1* is a separate Paris publication of 1905, and the pool conflated them. RINBE at Munich continues the corpus open-access.

**[A] Babylon, 1980s.** The Iraqi reconstruction stamped new brick courses naming Saddam Hussein as builder in the era of Nebuchadnezzar; archaeologists objected that it destroyed stratigraphy. **[A]** In classical Islamic historiography, notably al-Tabari, he is Bukht-Nasr, the tyrant who destroys Jerusalem and exiles its people.

## 11. The quotations: one confirmed, one fabricated source

**Confirmed verbatim.** The East India House passage matches Wikipedia's article character for character, including the spelling "Nabopolasser." **But the pool's source line is wrong twice over:** Wikipedia attaches no reference to that translation block, so it is anonymous and cannot be traced to a named edition; and the only Langdon item on the page is the German VAB 4 of 1912, not an English "Building Inscriptions of the Neo-Babylonian Empire (1912)." The journey cites an anonymous rendering and claims no translator.

**Fabricated source, caught.** The pool's second chronicle quotation was attributed to `jhalsey.com/jerusalembook/chronicles.html`: 404, no Wayback snapshot, and the domain is an unrelated personal portfolio. The wording traces to Wikipedia's 'Siege of Jerusalem (597 BC)', whose footnote reads "No 24 WA21946, The Babylonian Chronicles, The British Museum". The citation string was lifted from that footnote and reattached to a site that does not exist. **A substantive error rode along:** the pool glossed Hatti-land as "(Turkey/Syria)"; Wikipedia reads "(Syria/Palestine)." The journey uses Wikipedia's wording and re-sources it.

## 12. Corpus interlocks

**[A]** The queue and census both frame him as "Chaldean king of Babylon, destroyer of Solomon's Temple." Ten corpus documents name him. Named in campa: **solomon, cyrus, alexander, kircher, gurdjieff**.

- **solomon**: the Temple journey builds the house this one burns. Shared pin, byte-identical: **31.778 / 35.2354**. That file ends at Solomon's death, not the Temple's fall, so the connection is the corpus's own framing rather than a textual handoff. **[R]** on the linkage, **[A]** on the geography.
- **cyrus**: Belshazzar profanes "the very gold cups... that Nebuchadnezzar carried off from Jerusalem"; a campa there names him outright as the looter. **[A]**
- **alexander**: enters Babylon through the Ishtar Gate and sacrifices to Bel-Marduk as the Chaldean priests direct. The fabric is Nebuchadnezzar's, though that file does not name him as its builder. **[R]** on the attribution, **[A]** on the history.
- **kircher**: *Turris Babel*, the Tower out of Nimrod's Babylon; the Etemenanki identification is an inference not stated in that file. **[R] gurdjieff**: the Sarmoung Brotherhood founded in Babylon, "unbroken since Babylon"; the city is named there, Nebuchadnezzar is not.
- **Negative result, recorded:** Paris and Buenos Aires pins exist across the atlas but no textual link to Nebuchadnezzar was found. An absence, not a link.
