# Abd al-Rahman al-Ghafiqi (d. 732): research report

*Compiled 2026-08-09 for the Atlas of Journeys. Subject: Abu Sa'id 'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Abd Allah ibn Bishr ibn al-Sarim al-Ghafiqi, twice governor (wali) of al-Andalus, commander of the Umayyad army destroyed between Tours and Poitiers.*

**Legend.** **[A]** = attested, source named. **[R]** = reconstruction, inference, or later tradition. Contradictions are flagged where they occur and left standing. Gaps are stated as gaps.

**The evidence base.** One near-contemporary Latin witness (the Mozarabic Chronicle of 754, also called Isidore of Beja's Chronicle), a thin Arabic tradition compiled centuries later, and a large modern literature mostly about what the battle of 732 is supposed to mean. Almost every date reaches us through a tertiary encyclopedia, and where two encyclopedias disagree, nothing consulted reconciles them. Several claims in circulation turn out, on tracing, to be Wikipedia's own prose quoted back as a chronicle.

---

## 1. Name, tribe, birth

- Full name **Abu Sa'id 'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Abd Allah ibn Bishr ibn al-Sarim al-Ghafiqi**, of the tribe of Ghafiq, a division of the 'Akk. **[A, Arabic Wikipedia, 'عبد الرحمن الغافقي'; tribe corroborated at English Wikipedia, 'Abdul Rahman Al Ghafiqi']**
- **Birthplace: contradiction, unresolved.** Arabic Wikipedia gives the **Hijaz** and dates the birth only to "the seventh century." English Wikipedia's infobox gives **Tihamah, Arabia**, and calls Ghafiq "the Arab Tihamite tribe of Ghafiq." Different regions, roughly 800 km apart. **[A on both attributions; the fact is contested]** The journey pins the Tihama and states the Hijaz tradition in the campa; neither is preferred.
- **No birth year survives.** "Late seventh century" is inferred from his seniority in 721. **[R]**
- **Everything before 721 is a gap.** No office, campaign, teacher, marriage, or arrival in the west is dated anywhere. The Kairouan, Seville, and strait-crossing stations below rest on a single reported acquaintance and are [R] accordingly.

## 2. Ifriqiya and the crossing (undated, all [R])

- Later biography places him in the milieu of **Kairouan**, capital of Ifriqiya, known to **Musa ibn Nusayr** and to Musa's son **'Abd al-Aziz**. **[R, English and Arabic Wikipedia; no date in either]** He enters **al-Andalus** during or shortly after 'Abd al-Aziz's governorate, conventionally c. 714-716, with no post, rank, or year named. **[R]**
- The crossing at **Ceuta**, on Tariq ibn Ziyad's 711 route, is **recorded nowhere**. It is read backwards from his presence in the peninsula in 721 at officer rank. **[R]**
- **Correction to a common framing.** The capital was **not** moved from Seville to Cordoba by al-Samh in 719. Al-Hurr ibn 'Abd al-Rahman al-Thaqafi, governor from 716, "relocated the Andalusian administrative capital from Seville to Córdoba" soon after taking office. **[A, English Wikipedia, 'Al-Hurr ibn Abd al-Rahman al-Thaqafi']** Al-Samh, from March 719, inherited Cordoba as the seat.

## 3. Toulouse, 721, and the first governorship

- He fights at the **Battle of Toulouse (9 June 721)**, where the wali **al-Samh ibn Malik al-Khawlani** is killed and the force broken by **Duke Odo of Aquitaine**. **[A, English Wikipedia, 'Abdul Rahman Al Ghafiqi' and 'Battle of Toulouse (721)'; Arabic Wikipedia]** First appearance by name in any source.
- **Narbonne**, taken in the campaign begun in 719, is the Umayyad position north of the Pyrenees. That the Toulouse army *mustered there* is **not** in the sources, which say only that the campaign captured Narbonne and that al-Samh assembled "an army of Arabs and Berbers from Umayyad territories." **[R, downgraded from an earlier [A]]**
- The army in the field then **elects him governor**. **[A, Arabic Wikipedia; 'List of Umayyad governors of al-Andalus']**
- **First term length: contradiction.** Arabic Wikipedia gives Dhu al-Hijjah 102 to Safar 103 AH = **6 June to 1 September 721**; the governors list gives **July 721** only, with 'Anbasa from August; the English biography infobox gives **721-722**. Two of three support a term of weeks. **[A on the AH bounds; not resolved]**
- **Nothing is recorded of what he did in office.** Gap. He is succeeded by **'Anbasa ibn Suhaym al-Kalbi**, appointed from Ifriqiya. **[A]**

## 4. The nine blank years (721-730)

- 'Anbasa governs from **August 721** until his **death campaigning in southern Gaul, January 726**. **[A, governors list]**
- **Six** governors follow before al-Ghafiqi's return, not five, all sent from Ifriqiya **[A, same source]**: 'Udhra ibn 'Abd Allah al-Fihri (Jan-Mar 726); Yahya ibn Salama al-Kalbi (Mar 726-Jun 728); Hudhayfa ibn al-Ahwas al-Ashja'i (Jun-Dec 728); 'Uthman ibn Abi Nis'a al-Khath'ami (Dec 728-Apr 729); al-Haytham ibn 'Ubayd al-Kilabi (Apr 729-Feb 730); Muhammad ibn 'Abd Allah al-Ashja'i (Feb-Mar 730).
- **Al-Ghafiqi is absent from the record for the whole period.** The journey places him on the eastern march toward Zaragoza, inferred from his later competence in the Catalan passes and from nothing else. No chronicle puts him anywhere between 721 and 730. **[R]**

## 5. The second governorship (730 or 731)

- Appointed wali a second time; the governors list dates the term from **March 730**. **[A]**
- **Appointer: two errors in circulation.**
  - **'Ubayd Allah ibn al-Habhab** is **impossible**: he held Kairouan only **734 to 741**. **[A, English Wikipedia, ''Ubayd Allah ibn al-Habhab' and 'Ifriqiya']** The governor of Ifriqiya in 730 was **'Ubayda ibn 'Abd al-Rahman al-Sulami** (727-732). Near-homophones, conflated.
  - **Caliph Hisham ibn 'Abd al-Malik** as personal appointer is **unsupported**: neither biography says it, and the Arabic names the governor of Ifriqiya. **[R at best; the journey treats Damascus as the authority he governs under, not the signing hand]**
- **Hijri conversion error.** Arabic Wikipedia dates the term from **Safar 113 AH**, sometimes rendered 730 CE. But 1 Muharram 113 AH = 19 March 731, so **Safar 113 AH = 18 April to 16 May 731**: the Arabic date does not corroborate March 730, it points a year later. **[A on the arithmetic; divergence left open]**
- He works to reconcile the **Mudari** and **Yamani** factions. **[R, Arabic Wikipedia only]**

## 6. Munuza and the Cerdanya, 730-731

- **'Uthman ibn Naissa**, Latin **Munuza**, Berber deputy of the Cerdanya, makes peace with **Odo** in 730 and marries Odo's daughter **Lampegia**; raids into Aquitaine pause. c. 731 he rebels, attempts an independent Pyrenean lordship, and kills **Nambaudus, bishop of Urgell**. **[A, English Wikipedia, 'Uthman ibn Naissa']**
- Al-Ghafiqi destroys the revolt. Wording differs: the biography says he "killed" Munuza, the Munuza article says "defeated in battle and executed." **[A; minor contradiction noted]** The traditional site **Llivia** is flagged uncertain in the source itself. **[R]**
- He then accuses **Odo** of backing the revolt, given in the sources as the **pretext** for 732. **[R]**

## 7. The muster and the crossing, 731-732

- The **call for recruits from Yemen and the Levant** is flagged **[dubious]** in the English Wikipedia article itself and must stay **[R]**. Nothing may be built on it, including estimates of the army's size.
- The **Pamplona muster** is **not attested**. English Wikipedia, 'Battle of the River Garonne', says only that the force "crossed the Pyrenees at their west end, through Navarre." **[R]**
- **Troop numbers: two scales, not to be blended.** Modern estimates for 732 run roughly **15,000 to 30,000 per side** (Hanson and Schoenfeld: 20,000-30,000 on logistical grounds); older sources claim **60,000 to 400,000**. **[A, 'Battle of Tours']** The **70,000-80,000** figure is separate, from **Matthew Bennett, The Hutchinson Dictionary of Ancient and Medieval Warfare (1998)**, via 'Battle of the River Garonne', where such chronicle figures are noted to "have been questioned." **[A as attribution; figure disputed]** A single range of "20,000 to 80,000" matches no source and is withdrawn.
- The **Roncevaux crossing** is inferred from the customary Pamplona-Aquitaine road. No source names the pass. **[R]**

## 8. Aquitaine overrun, 732

- Raiding through **Vasconia**, then the advance on Bordeaux; the city is **taken and sacked** and Odo beaten outside it. **[A, English Wikipedia, 'Odo the Great' and 'Battle of the River Garonne'; Bayonne is named nowhere and is a route inference, [R]]**
- **Correction on Saint-Seurin.** The basilica does **not** stand from this period: nave and choir 11th-13th c., south portal 13th, chapels 14th-15th. A 5th-century oratory on the site had gone by the 9th century; only 4th-century palaeochristian necropolis remains in the crypt predate the raid. The site is that old, the building is not. **[A, French Wikipedia, 'Basilique Saint-Seurin de Bordeaux']**
- **Battle of the River Garonne.** English Wikipedia locates it at 45°02'29"N, 0°36'24"W = **45.0414, -0.6067**, the Bec d'Ambes at the Garonne-Dordogne confluence, hence the article's "or possibly at the Dordogne." **[A]** An earlier draft pinned it in central Bordeaux, 23 km too far south.
- **Correction to a much-quoted phrase.** The usual "God alone knows the number of the slain" is a misrendering. Fordham's Isidore of Beja reads: *"He gave battle to Duke Eudes (of Aquitaine) beyond the Garonne and the Dordogne, and put him to flight, so utterly [was he beaten] that God alone knew the number of the slain and wounded."* Tense "knew," phrase "the slain and wounded," and a named source rather than "unspecified Western chroniclers." **[A]**
- **"Al-Burdil" is misapplied.** Al-Burdhil / Burdil is the Arabic name for **the city of Bordeaux**, not for a battle on the Garonne. **[A]**
- **Single-source, uncorroborated.** Arabic Wikipedia has the campaign reaching **Arles, Lyon (ليون), and Besançon (بيزنسون)**; corroborated in no English-language source, and Besançon lies far outside any attested line of advance. **[R]** An earlier draft misread ليون as "Leon" in northern Spain; the Arabic reads Lyon.

## 9. The road between Poitiers and Tours

- Odo appeals to **Charles Martel**. **[R, standard narrative background, 'Battle of Tours'; not checked against a primary source]**
- **Angoulême** is a route inference from geography, not attested. **[R]**
- **Saint-Hilaire-le-Grand, Poitiers**: general devastation is attested; the burning of that church is **later tradition**, absent from the near-contemporary chronicles. **[R]**
- The **shrine of Saint Martin at Tours** is the campaign's named objective. **[A]** Isidore of Beja: *"Whereupon Abderrahman set in pursuit of Eudes; he destroyed palaces, burned churches, and imagined he could pillage the basilica of St. Martin of Tours."* **[A, Fordham]**
- **The Clain-Vienne confluence is at 46.7819, 0.5411**, Cenon-sur-Vienne, south of Châtellerault. **[A, French Wikipedia, 'Clain' and 'Cenon-sur-Vienne']** An earlier draft pinned 46.66, 0.55, some 13.5 km too far south, which put the confluence upstream of its own battlefield.

## 10. The battle: place and date, both contested

**The central contradiction of the dossier. Not resolved.**

**Place.** English Wikipedia has the armies meet "between the cities of Poitiers and Tours... where the rivers Clain and Vienne converge," on high wooded ground of Martel's choosing, after **seven days of skirmishing**. **[A as attribution]** French Wikipedia, 'Bataille de Poitiers (732)': *"Le lieu de la bataille n'est pas connu, et pas moins de trente-huit sites ont été avancés."* **[A]** French Wikipedia, 'Vouneuil-sur-Vienne', hedges that *il est possible que* the battle was fought on that commune and says the Franks repelled a razzia led by Abd el Rahman there; **it does not say he was killed there**, though an earlier draft asserted it did. **[A]** The **Moussais** identification is therefore **[R]**: one reconstruction among thirty-eight, and the one with an interpretive centre on it.

**Date.** Four positions: English Wikipedia, 'Battle of Tours', **10 October 732**; French Wikipedia, 'Vouneuil-sur-Vienne', **25 October 732**; Arabic Wikipedia, 27 Sha'ban 114 AH, rendered there as **21 October 732**; French Wikipedia, 'Bataille de Poitiers (732)', **25 October 732 or 17 October 733**.

**Correction that reshapes the disagreement.** Arabic Wikipedia's conversion is wrong: 1 Muharram 114 AH = 7 March 732, so **27 Sha'ban 114 AH = 26 October 732**, not 21 October. Corrected, the Arabic date falls within a day of the French 25 October and the two converge; the outlier is the traditional English **10 October**. French Wikipedia further leaves the **year** open, offering 17 October 733. The journey pins 10 October 732 only because that is how the event is indexed across the Anglophone atlas neighbourhood, and carries the full four-way disagreement in `date_confidence` on every stop on the field.

## 11. The death

- Killed in the battle while rallying his men against the Frankish counterattack. **[A]** Chronicle of 754: *"The people of Austrasia... killed the king, Abd ar-Rahman, when they found him, striking him on the chest."* **[A]** The title *king* is a Latin error; he was a wali.
- The Arabic tradition instead has him mortally wounded by an arrow while reordering the ranks. The two accounts are reconciled nowhere. **[A that both exist]**
- Chronicle of St Denis: *"he slew in that battle 300,000 men, likewise their king by name Abderrahman."* **[A as quotation; the figure is fantasy, the title again wrong]**
- **No source names his grave.** French regional sources say plainly that *le lieu exact de sa sépulture est inconnu*. **[R for the sourcing route, a web-search summary; the gap itself is real]**

## 12. Afterlife, legend, reception

- The battlefield is **Balat al-Shuhada**, the pavement or path of the martyrs, in the Arabic tradition. **[A]** But the sentence "Abd ar-Rahman and many of his men found martyrdom on the balat ash-Shuhada'i" is **not** Ibn Idhari's, as commonly claimed: it is Wikipedia's own prose in 'Battle of Tours', whose footnote cites Ibn Idhari al-Marrakushi, *al-Bayan al-Mughrib*, alongside Antonio Santosuosso. No translated edition containing it was located. Usable as unquoted fact, never as a quotation. **[A on the correction]** **Henry Coppée** adds that the honorific was applied to Toulouse and to other fields where Muslim armies were beaten: a recurring formula, not a site-specific marker. **[A, cited in 'Battle of Tours']**
- **A quotation rescued.** "That army went through all places like a desolating storm" is real and traceable, though not to Wikipedia (where it is tagged [citation needed]). It is **Edward Shepherd Creasy, The Fifteen Decisive Battles of the World, ch. VII**, whose footnote credits **José Antonio Conde, Historia de la Dominación de los Árabes en España (1820)**. Full wording: *"And that army went through all places like a desolating storm. Prosperity made those warriors insatiable."* Cite as Creasy after Conde, a 19th-century translation at two removes, never as a chronicle. **[A with that attribution]**
- **Rival death tradition.** **Haut-Quercy** holds he was not killed at Poitiers but withdrew toward Narbonne and was killed in **733 at Louchapt in the Lot**, under the Sangou cliff. **[R, French Wikipedia, 'Bataille de Poitiers (732)'; no coordinate for the Sangou site locatable, source base unidentified]** The neighbouring town of **Martel** claims Charles Martel as founder, undocumented before the 12th century (earliest reachable mention c. 1142). **[R]**
- **Burial legend.** At Moussais, *la Fosse au roi*, the King's Ditch. **[R, French heritage tradition via web search; no archaeology or document found]**
- **Commemoration and art.** The hamlet was renamed **Moussais-la-Bataille** (date not given) and the site drew more than **40,000 visitors in 2010**, ninth most-visited in the Vienne. Théodore Gechter's bronze of the combat, 1833, entered the Louvre; Charles de Steuben's painting followed in 1837. **[A, French Wikipedia, 'Vouneuil-sur-Vienne'; 'Battle of Tours']**
- **He wrote nothing.** No treatise, poem, letter, or dictated work is attributed to him anywhere consulted; the standing brief item on editions and translations does not apply.
- **Historiographic asymmetry.** Bernard Lewis notes that Arab historians "present it as a minor skirmish," and that contemporary chroniclers ranked the 718 siege of Constantinople higher. **[A, 'Battle of Tours']** The English Wikipedia biography **has no section** on tomb, monuments, legends, or rediscovery; the burial material is entirely French-language and folk. **[A]**

## 13. Atlas interlocks

**dihya** at Kairouan and Damascus (the same advance broke her in the Aures and sent her head east). **ziryab**, **maimonides**, **ibn_arabi** at Cordoba, his seat in 721 and again 730-732. **clovis** at Toulouse and Bordeaux. **charlemagne** at Pamplona and Roncevaux, both re-entered in 778. **charles_martel** across the whole 732 geography, and at Narbonne again for his failed 737 siege. **joan_of_arc** at Tours and at Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois, where Martel is said to have buried his sword as a votive for this victory and where Jeanne sent for a sword that was dug up.

Not inheritable: the **Kaaba** (21.4225, 39.8262), since no stop places him in the Hijaz as an adult; **Temple Mount** and **Buenos Aires**, unconnected. ### Named but not reached
- **Ibn Idhari al-Marrakushi, al-Bayan al-Mughrib** (13th c.): no translated edition located; the sentence attributed to it appears to be Wikipedia's prose. Quotation withdrawn.
- **José Antonio Conde, Historia de la Dominación de los Árabes en España** (1820): known only through Creasy's footnote, so the chain from Arabic chronicle to Conde to Creasy is unverified at its first link.
- **Antonio Santosuosso**, **Matthew Bennett** (Hutchinson Dictionary, 1998, for the 70,000-80,000 figure), **Bernard Lewis** ("minor skirmish"), **Henry Coppée** (the martyrdom formula): all known only through Wikipedia citations, none consulted directly.
- **The Haut-Quercy / Louchapt tradition**, **the missing-grave statements**, and **the Fosse au roi legend**: reached only as French Wikipedia summary or as web-search summaries, never as named publications.
- No academic monograph treats al-Ghafiqi as a subject in his own right; the modern literature handles him as a component of the Battle of Tours question. No Arabic primary chronicle (Ibn Idhari, Ibn al-Athir, al-Maqqari) was reached in translation, so every Arabic-tradition claim here arrives through Arabic Wikipedia or an English footnote and none has been read at source. Nothing consulted resolves the birthplace, the date, the site, or the year of appointment: four open contradictions, all left open.
