# William of Conches (Guillelmus de Conchis): research report

*Slug: `william_of_conches`. Norman grammarian and natural philosopher of the School of Chartres; commentator on Plato's Timaeus in Calcidius's Latin; author of the Philosophia mundi and the Dragmaticon; denounced in 1141; tutor to the sons of Geoffrey Plantagenet, Count of Anjou.*

**Legend.** **[A]** = attested, source named inline. **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition, or inference. Where sources contradict, both are printed and neither is preferred.

**Headline warning.** Almost nothing about this man's life is documented in the twelfth-century sense: no charter naming him, no letter to or from him reached here, no obit, no necrology entry, no episcopal or conciliar act. What exists is a large, securely attributed body of commentaries; one contemporary witness who sat in his lecture room (John of Salisbury); one hostile letter about him (William of Saint-Thierry, 1141); and later traditions about his birth, death and grave that do not agree. Every date below is a range or a placeholder, and the journey file's `date_confidence` fields carry that hedge rather than the prose.

---

## 1. Birth and origins (c. 1080 / c. 1085-1090)

- Born in Normandy in the Ouche country near Évreux **[A: English and French Wikipedia]**.
- **Date contradiction, not resolved.** English Wikipedia gives **c. 1085-1090**; French Wikipedia gives **"né autour de 1080"**. A decade apart, nothing to arbitrate. A third variant, "c. 1090", circulated in the gather and was **removed**: neither cited source gives it.
- **Place contradiction, not resolved.** English Wikipedia: "generally taken to have been Conches though possibly nearby **Tilleul**." French Wikipedia does not raise Tilleul for the birth at all. Tilleul is **Saint-Martin-du-Tilleul** (Eure), formerly Tilleul-Follenfant, thirty miles west near Bernay **[A]**.
- The toponym is the only name any source supplies. No family name, no father, no mother, no kin of any degree. **Gap.**
- Early schooling **[R]**, barely: Conches lies in the diocese of Évreux, so any pre-Chartres education happened inside that bishopric by administrative necessity. The gather's claim that Évreux cathedral was "the region's principal school of consequence" was **cut**: no consulted source says it, and the French article on the cathedral has nothing on a twelfth-century school.

## 2. Chartres and the studentship under Bernard (c. 1115-1124)

- Studied under **Bernard of Chartres** **[A: English Wikipedia; Metalogicon I.5]**.
- **Date corrected.** The gather anchored the studentship at 1110. Bernard is documented at the school only **by 1115** and was chancellor until **1124** **[A: 'Bernard of Chartres']**, so it falls inside c. 1115-1124 and no source narrows it. The journey file uses 1116 as an in-range placeholder and says so.
- **Blois** enters because Bernard taught there too, the sources placing William "under Bernard in Chartres and Blois" **[A for the phrase; R for his presence at Blois in any given year]**.
- Begins teaching **c. 1115-1120**, "based primarily in Paris and Chartres" **[A]**. Which years belong to which city is nowhere stated. **Gap.**

## 3. The works of the teaching years (c. 1120s-1130s)

Securely attributed **[A: English Wikipedia works list; editions of Jeauneau, Nauta, Dutton, Maurach, Ronca]**: **Glosae super Priscianum** (two authorial editions); **Philosophia mundi** (four books, later six: physics, astronomy, geography, meteorology, medicine); **Glosae super Platonem** (on Calcidius's partial Latin Timaeus); **Glosae super Boetium**; glosses on **Macrobius**, *Dream of Scipio*, and on **Juvenal**, *Satires*; the **Dragmaticon** (see §6).

- **Philosophia dated c. 1125-1130 [A]. Place of composition [R] only.** Two gather entries were tagged [A] for a Chartres siting while their own notes conceded it was inferred (the Priscian glosses and the Philosophia); both **retagged [R]**.
- Sources drawn on: Plato via **Calcidius**; **Macrobius**; and the Latin translations of Arabic medical material by **Constantine the African**, an early and notable use of that corpus by a Latin Christian philosopher **[A]**.
- Method: **integumentum**, reading scandalous or contradictory pagan narrative as an allegorical wrapper over orthodox truth **[A: English Wikipedia; Elford in Dronke]**.

### Quotations: what had to be corrected

The most error-prone part of the pool. Four candidate quotations were misquoted; three were not quotations at all.

- **Corrected, verified [A]:** `Haec elementa nunquam videntur, sed ratione divisionis intelliguntur.` (I.21; PL 172, col. 48ff.) The gather opened "Quae elementa numquam videntur"; **"Quae" for "Haec" is wrong**, fixed. `nunquam`/`numquam` is a harmless variant. Fourteen independent confirmations via Open Library search-inside (de Wulf, Ueberweg, Baumgartner, Eisler among them).
- **Corrected [A]:** `Si ergo illis digna velimus imponere nomina, particulas praedictas dicamus elementa, ista quatuor quae videntur elementata.` The gather dropped **`quatuor`**; PL 172 has it. Restored, unused: the stop it belongs to already carries I.21.
- **Verified [A]:** `Elementum ergo, ut ait Constantinus in Pantegni, est simpla et minima pars alicuius corporis, simpla ad qualitatem, minima ad quantitatem.` (I.22.)
- **Corrected [A]:** `Sed quoniam ipsi nesciunt vires naturae, ut ignorantiae suae omnes socios habeant, nolunt eos aliquid inquirere, sed ut rusticos nos credere nec rationem quaerere.` The "peasant faith" passage. **The gather's Latin was invented**: "Ignari vim naturae et sui expertes..." returns **zero hits anywhere**, and only `nolunt eos aliquid inquirere` survived. The authentic text is confirmed in fifteen-plus sources. **Locus corrected** from "Praefatio" to **I.23 (PL 172, col. 56)**.
- **Corrected and demoted:** the English "they do not want us to **look into** anything; they want us to believe like peasants and not to ask the reason behind things." The gather had "inquire into"; the circulating forms have "look into", and **no named translator or critical edition stands behind it.** Not used as a `quote`.
- **Corrected [A]:** `Willelmus de Conchis, gramaticus post Bernardum Carnotensem opulentissimus.` (Metalogicon **I.5**, not I.24.) The gather's inserted commas broke an unbroken predicate ("the most richly gifted grammarian after Bernard of Chartres") and garbled the gloss.
- **Three items rejected as manufactured quotations.** French Wikipedia puts only short fragments in quotation marks: « chercher la raison », « on ne retire rien à Dieu », « par sa seule volonté », « instrument de l'opération divine », plus the standalone line « Dieu gouverne le monde par l'intermédiaire de l'ordre naturel ». The gather wrapped the surrounding **editor's prose** into the quotations, producing sentences that exist in no source. Only the standalone line is used as a `quote`, its `quote_source` noting the underlying Latin and translator are unidentified; the other fragments appear in campa prose **unquoted**.
- **Rejected outright:** "pure elements are defined by their intrinsic temperature and moistness." A paraphrase of a paraphrase, appearing **nowhere** in the article it was sourced to; that article says "earth was cold and dry, water cold and moist, air hot and moist, and fire hot and dry." Unused.

## 4. John of Salisbury, 1137-1138

- **John of Salisbury studied under William at Chartres, 1137-1138 [A]**, recording his judgement decades later in the Metalogicon.
- **Correction, important.** The gather asserted John recorded "his own sharp criticism of William for abandoning strict grammatical teaching to chase natural philosophy." **This is not in the cited source and inverts the Metalogicon.** English Wikipedia records only praise, and in I.5 John's censure falls on the *turba imperitorum*, the Cornificians, who drove William off Bernard's method. Clause **deleted**; the journey file states the direction of blame correctly.

## 5. The accusation of 1141, and the withdrawal

- **William of Saint-Thierry** writes from **Signy Abbey** in the Ardennes a letter known in later tradition as **De erroribus Guillelmi de Conchis**, in **1141** **[A: 'William of Saint-Thierry']**, charging that the identification of the Platonic world-soul with the Holy Spirit is modalist.
- **Addressee corrected.** The gather listed **Bernard of Clairvaux and Geoffrey of Lèves**. English Wikipedia has the letter "addressed to Bernard in 1141", **Bernard alone**. Geoffrey of Lèves, bishop of Chartres (1116-1149), is not attested as an addressee; the joint addressing belongs to the earlier *Disputatio adversus Petrum Abaelardum* tradition. **Geoffrey dropped.**
- **The letter's own Latin could not be reached.** Its contents are known only through secondary paraphrase: the most consequential gap here, since this accusation ended his career.
- **What Bernard did about it is unknown.** No condemnation of William is recorded anywhere consulted: no council, no sentence, no demanded retraction, no episcopal act. The gather's "under the weight of the condemnation" was **corrected to "the accusation"**; it contradicted the pool's own note that he was "not formally prosecuted."
- The **Council of Sens (1141)** condemned **Abelard**. **No source places William at Sens** and the journey file does not put him there; Sens appears as the documented climate of the year, [R] for any tie to William personally.
- **He withdraws from public teaching after 1141 [A]**; the year is not given, and 1142 in the journey file is an explicit placeholder.
- **[R] The undocumented compromise.** That he reached some accommodation, not prosecuted but revising cautiously and leaving the schools, is inference from the *absence* of a condemnation record.

## 6. The Plantagenet household (c. 1143-1149)

- Enters the service of **Geoffrey Plantagenet, Count of Anjou**, as tutor to **Henry Plantagenet**, future Henry II, **c. 1143-1144 [A]**; tutors **Henry's brothers Geoffrey and William, 1146-1149 [A]**.
- **Place is unattested throughout.** English Wikipedia gives **no location** for the tutorship. "Anjou", "Angers", "Le Mans", "Normandy" and "Rouen" were each tagged [A] somewhere in the gather and have all been **retagged [R]**. Angers is the count's principal seat; Le Mans is Henry's birthplace (1133); Geoffrey entered Rouen 14 January 1144 and took the ducal title that summer. Those three facts are attested; William's presence at any of them is not.
- **Anachronism removed.** The gather sited this at the **Château d'Angers**; that castle was built **1230-1242** under Louis IX and was not Geoffrey's court **[A: 'Château d'Angers']**. Coordinate corrected to 47.4701, -0.5599.
- **Dragmaticon philosophiae**: dialogue reworking of the Philosophia, roughly doubling it and dropping only three to five per cent, with further Greek and Arabic material **[A]**. **Date corrected**: English Wikipedia gives "sometime between **1144 and 1149**"; the gather's single year 1147 was **fabricated precision**, and its "completed by 1149" treated a range's outer bound as attested. **Dedication corrected**: to Geoffrey as **Count of Anjou**; the gather's "now styled Duke of Normandy" is removed.

## 7. Death: three traditions, unreconciled

| Tradition | Date | Place | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| English | 1154 or shortly afterward | **Paris** | English Wikipedia **[A as a report of the tradition]** |
| English (alternative, same sentence) | 1154 or shortly afterward | **environs of Évreux** | English Wikipedia **[A as above]** |
| French | **c. 1150** or a little after | **Conches-en-Ouche** | French Wikipedia **[A as above]** |

The gather attributed "circa 1150-1154" to French Wikipedia. **That is a misquote.** The French lead reads *"né autour de 1080, mort vers 1150 ou peu après (dates approximatives) à Conches-en-Ouche en Normandie."* The French account differs from the English not by four years but by a **place of death the English tradition never mentions**. All three appear as separate stops, each labelled as one tradition among three. Not adjudicated. The stops run in date order within the segment, so the French c. 1150 siting at Conches-en-Ouche stands first and the English 1154 pair (Paris, then the Évreux alternative from the same sentence) follows; the ordering is chronological bookkeeping and carries no preference.

## 8. The grave, and the 1857 slab

- **[R]** Originally interred at **St Germanus's Church in Tilleul** (English Wikipedia). That church, *l'église Saint-Germain au lieu-dit le Tilleul*, is listed by French Wikipedia among the commune's **destroyed** buildings, on the **1850 cadastre** and no longer standing **[A]**. The stop marks a site with nothing on it.
- **Discovery attribution corrected.** The gather twice credited **Antoine Charma** with *finding* the stone. French Wikipedia says it was discovered by **Auguste Le Prevost**, and that Charma "se rendit sur les lieux mêmes où une pierre tombale avait été découverte... par son confrère, Auguste Le Prevost." **Charma examined and published the 1857 notice; he did not find it.** His verdict, quoted verbatim in the journey file, keeps the conditional the English tradition flattens: *"il s'agirait effectivement de la sépulture où repose Guillaume de Conches"*.
- **Remains versus slab.** English Wikipedia says the **funeral slab with his effigy** was moved to Sainte-Foy at Conches-en-Ouche; the gather turned this into a **translation of his remains**. **No source claims his body was moved.** French Wikipedia confirms only a twelfth-century *pierre tombale* in Sainte-Foy. The journey file says slab, not body.
- **Framing contradiction, not resolved.** English Wikipedia states the relocation as fact; French Wikipedia presents the Tilleul identification as a nineteenth-century scholarly conclusion and narrates no move. Both printed.

## 9. Afterlife: six centuries under other men's names

- The Philosophia mundi circulates **misattributed to Bede, William of Hirsau, and Honorius of Autun**, and is printed in PL 172 among the works of Honorius **[A]**. **Hugh of St Victor was removed** from this list: English Wikipedia names exactly three.
- Correct attribution re-established in **Rémi-Casimir Oudin's Commentarius de scriptoribus ecclesiae antiquis, 1722 [A]**. **Correction:** 1722 is a **publication year, not an act by a living scholar**. Oudin died in September 1717; the book appeared posthumously at Leipzig/Frankfurt **[A: 'Casimir Oudin']**. **Charles Jourdain (1838)** and **Jean-Barthélemy Hauréau (1858)** finish the restitution **[A]**.
- **Dutton's census: 68 manuscripts of the original edition, 16 of the six-book revision [A]**.
- **Critical editions.** The gather said they "proliferate from 1974 onward." **Corrected:** the list runs from **Ottaviano (1935)** and **Parent (1938)** through **Jeauneau (1957, 1965)**, **Maurach (1974, 1980)**, and **Ronca (1997)**, whose CCCM Dragmaticon with the English *A Dialogue on Natural Philosophy* is the standard modern edition.

## 11. Interlocks

Two travelers are named in a campa, each on a real relation, not shared geography:

- **Plato** (`plato.journey.json`): **direct, one-directional [A]**. The Glosae super Platonem is a line-by-line commentary on the Timaeus in Calcidius's Latin. A commentator's relation to the author he spends his life glossing is real, though the reverse could never be true.
- **Bernard of Clairvaux** (`bernard_clairvaux.journey.json`): **[A for the addressing; R for anything Bernard did]**. William of Saint-Thierry's 1141 letter about William of Conches was written **to Bernard**, asking him to move as he had against Abelard.

**Rejected as name-drops:** `saint_denis` (shared Paris geography only); `william_the_conqueror` (the ducal line reaches Henry II, William's pupil: a relation between the Conqueror and Henry, not with William); `giovanni_pico_della_mirandola` and `giordano_bruno` (thematic lineage only; neither file names William, neither man cites him).

**Meta-finding [A, corpus-wide grep of `working/journeys/*.journey.json`]:** no existing file names "William of Conches", "Bernard of Chartres", "Thierry of Chartres", "John of Salisbury", "Abelard" or "Heloise". Every interlock was established fresh from William's side; the Chartres and Paris coordinates are the only things inherited.

**A stop belongs to whoever was there.** Le Prevost's 1857 discovery and Charma's examination are *their* travel, not William's, and are not staged as a scene. They appear as a source and a `suggested_ref` on William's own grave stop at Tilleul, with Charma's sentence as that stop's `quote`.
