# George Washington (1732-1799) research report
*Compiled 2026-08-09. Slug `george_washington`.*

**Legend.** `[A: source]` = attested, source named. `[R]` = reconstruction, tradition, or a modern retrospective judgement a secondary source presents as fact. Contradictions are flagged, not resolved. Unreachable sources are named with the reason.

**The problem, inverted.** Washington is the first traveler in the atlas whose life is already a national scripture. For most subjects the difficulty is finding anything; here everything is findable, and much of it is Weems, Irving, or a schoolbook wearing a footnote. This pass spent its effort demoting claims to [R], splicing quotations back to their real texts, and moving pins. Eleven coordinates were wrong; one quotation was a fabrication with an authentic head.

**Calendar.** Born 11 February 1731 Old Style; 22 February 1732 is the Gregorian equivalent after Britain's 1752 change. That hedge sits in the journey's `date_confidence`, never in prose.

---

## 1. Tidewater origins, 1732-1752

- Born at the Pope's Creek tobacco plantation, Westmoreland County, 22 February 1732, first of Augustine Washington and Mary Ball's six children; family to Little Hunting Creek on the Potomac 1735, the tract Lawrence later renames Mount Vernon, and to Ferry Farm 1738. `[A: Mount Vernon Ladies' Association; Freeman vol. 1; Wikipedia GW]`
- Augustine dies 1743; George, eleven, inherits Ferry Farm and ten enslaved people. `[A: Wikipedia, GW and slavery]` The first fact of his life as a slaveholder, preceding everything but his birth.
- Shenandoah surveying 1748; licence from William & Mary and the oath as surveyor of Culpeper County 20 July 1749 `[A: Wikipedia GW]`. Barbados with the tubercular Lawrence, 2 November to 22 December 1751, contracting smallpox and coming away immune and lightly scarred, his only journey outside continental North America `[A: Barbados diary, ed. Toner, 1892]`.
- Initiated Entered Apprentice at Fredericksburg 4 November 1752 `[A: Lodge No. 4 records]`; Lawrence dies that year and George leases then inherits Mount Vernon `[A: Wikipedia GW]`. Cherry tree and thrown dollar: `[R: Weems, 1806]`.

## 2. The Ohio Country, 1753-1758

- October 1753: Dinwiddie sends him to demand French withdrawal. Logstown 24 November, councils with Tanaghrisson (the Half-King); Fort Le Boeuf 11 December, delivers the ultimatum to Jacques Legardeur de Saint-Pierre and gets a written refusal, published as a journal reprinted in London. `[A: Journal to the French Commandant on the Ohio, 1754]` The name Conotocarius, town-taker, is inherited from an earlier Washington, and when it was first said to his face is unfixed. `[R]`
- 28 May 1754, Jumonville Glen: his militia and Tanaghrisson's warriors ambush Joseph Coulon de Jumonville's party. `[A: Anderson, Crucible of War, 2000]` **The sequence of the killings is disputed between the English and French accounts and is not adjudicated here.** Whether Jumonville fell in the volley or was killed afterward by Tanaghrisson is `[R]` either way.
- 3 July 1754 Fort Necessity, the only surrender of his career `[A: NPS]`. 9 July 1755 Braddock's Field, volunteer aide with no command, two horses shot under him, four holes through his coat `[A: Washington to his mother, 18 July 1755, Papers]`; colonel of the Virginia Regiment again in August `[A: Wikipedia GW]`.
- 1758: Forbes Expedition, brevet brigadier, enters the Forks of the Ohio 25 November to find Fort Duquesne burned and abandoned, then resigns. Fort Cumberland is his staging base here and under Braddock. `[A: Anderson; Washington's Diaries]`

## 3. Planter, burgess, slaveholder, 1758-1774

- Burgess from 1758 for Frederick, later Fairfax `[A: Journals of the House of Burgesses]`. Marries Martha Dandridge Custis 6 January 1759, gaining control of a dower estate including eighty-four enslaved people `[A: Wikipedia GW; GW and slavery]`.
- 1766 out of tobacco into wheat with milling and hemp; 1770 William Crawford allots him 23,200 acres of bounty land; 1773 Patsy Custis dies and part of her inheritance settles his debts. `[A: Wikipedia GW]`
- 1774: drafts the Fairfax Resolves with George Mason, condemning the Atlantic slave trade, and goes as delegate to the First Continental Congress at Carpenters' Hall. `[A: Wikipedia GW; JCC 1774]`
- May 1774, Raleigh Tavern: dissolved burgesses reconvene to organise a boycott. `[A for the event]` **Citation failure flagged:** the pass cited "Journals of the House of Burgesses, Supplement 1770-1772" for a May 1774 meeting. Wrong volume. The event is attested elsewhere, but no citation I could stand behind was reachable, so the stop was dropped.

## 4. Command, 1775-1776

- 19 April 1775 Lexington and Concord; 15 June, Pennsylvania State House, Congress creates the Continental Army and elects him commander-in-chief unanimously, largely for the political value of a Virginian heading a New England war, and he declines a salary the next day. `[A: JCC 15 June 1775; Wikipedia GW]`
- Siege of Boston from the Vassall House, Cambridge, July 1775 to April 1776 `[A: NPS]`; Dorchester Heights fortified overnight 4 March 1776 with Knox's Ticonderoga guns, the British evacuating on the 17th `[A: McCullough, 1776]`.
- 27 August 1776, Long Island: severe defeat, the army saved by the night evacuation across the East River in fog on the 29th. `[A: McCullough]`
- **Correction carried forward.** The pass attached "roughly 5,400 troops" to the August retreat to Manhattan. Wikipedia's own sentence puts 5,400 in November and December 1776, after Fort Washington fell and the army retreated through New Jersey; in August it still numbered roughly nine to ten thousand. The figure is used at its correct date in the journey and nowhere else. `[A: Wikipedia GW]`

## 5. The crossing, the winters, the alliance, 1776-1781

- 25 December 1776 McConkey's Ferry, roughly 2,400 men across the ice-choked Delaware at night; Trenton next morning, 896 Hessian prisoners; Princeton 3 January 1777, ending in an artillery duel against troops in Nassau Hall. `[A: Fischer, Washington's Crossing, 2004]` Leutze's 1851 canvas is evidence of 1851. `[R as to the scene]`
- 11 September 1777 Brandywine: Lafayette wounded through the calf, staying on the field until Washington orders a surgeon to him. Winter 1777-1778 Valley Forge: supply collapse, von Steuben's drilling, the Isaac Potts house; the Conway Cabal offers Lafayette the command and he refuses. `[A: NPS Valley Forge; marques_de_lafayette.journey.json]`
- 28 June 1778 Monmouth: meets Charles Lee's unauthorised retreat and reforms the line. `[A: Papers, Rev. War Ser. vol. 15]` **Correction:** the documented exchange is a question about the reason, the disorder and the confusion, quoted verbatim in the journey. The profanity, including Charles Scott's "swore till the leaves shook on the trees," is later tradition `[R]`, tagged as such rather than folded into the [A] claim as the pass had it.
- 19 October 1781 Yorktown, Cornwallis surrenders. `[A: Papers vol. 33]` **Correction, and it reverses the anecdote.** The pool had Washington accepting the sword from O'Hara. He did not: O'Hara offered it first to Rochambeau, who declined and pointed to Washington, who also declined and gestured to Benjamin Lincoln, who took it. `[A: Wikipedia, Siege of Yorktown]` The deferral is the whole content of the story. "The World Turned Upside Down" stays `[R]`; Washington had denied the British the honours of war.

## 6. Laying it down, 1783

- 15 March 1783: confronts the Newburgh Conspiracy and puts on spectacles before his officers, with the remark about having grown gray and almost blind in his country's service. `[A: address to the officers, 15 March 1783; quoted verbatim in the journey]`
- **Place correction.** The pass put this at Hasbrouck House, Newburgh. Hasbrouck House was his residence April 1782 to August 1783, the longest-held headquarters of the war `[A]`, but the address was given at the New Building, or Temple, of the New Windsor Cantonment several miles southwest. The journey gives the address its own node at about 41.4867 / -74.0503 and names Hasbrouck House in `suggested_refs`.
- 4 December 1783 Fraunces Tavern, farewell in the Long Room `[A: Tallmadge, Memoir, 1858]`; 23 December, Annapolis, resigns his commission to the Confederation Congress `[A: JCC 23 December 1783]`.

## 7. The interval, 1784-1788

- 1784: Lafayette sends a Masonic apron embroidered by his wife Adrienne, delivered by John Rutledge Jr. `[R: Mount Vernon provenance; the two Lafayette journeys]` **Contradiction flagged, not resolved:** those journeys record that the apron later deposited at the Grand Lodge of Pennsylvania carries the traditional attribution to Adrienne and her daughters, and that the Grand Lodge's own museum record, dated to the 1829 transfer, later withdrew it. Two apron traditions, one gift, a museum that changed its mind. The journey states the gift and states the withdrawal.
- Great Falls canal works as president of the Potowmack Company, 1784-1785 `[A: Diaries]`, folded into the Mount Vernon Conference campa with no stop of its own; Virginia and Maryland commissioners settle Potomac navigation at Mount Vernon 25 to 28 March 1785 `[A: MVLA]`.
- October 1785: Houdon takes a life mask and body measurements for the statue commissioned in 1784, the marble reaching Richmond in 1796 `[A: Wikipedia]`. Castiglioni visits 25 to 29 December, recorded in Washington's own diary `[A: Travels in Virginia in Revolutionary Times, ed. Morrison, 1922]`; he guesses the age at fifty-seven when it is fifty-three, and that flag rides in `quote_source`.
- May to September 1787: presides over the Constitutional Convention. `[A: Farrand, Records of the Federal Convention]`

## 8. The presidency, 1789-1797

- 30 April 1789 Federal Hall, unanimous Electoral College, first oath. `[A: Wikipedia GW]` "So help me God" is `[R]`: the earliest printed accounts omit it and the phrase traces to Irving, 1859.
- December 1790: the Seneca chiefs Cornplanter, Half-Town and Great-Tree address the President. **The worst error in the gathering pass: a fabricated quotation with an authentic head.** The words "may see with your own eyes their faults; but hear our voice" do not appear in the document; "own eyes" and "faults" are both absent from it. The verified opening, quoted in full at the Market Street stop, begins "Father: The voice of the Seneca Nations speaks to you, the great counsellor..." The Town Destroyer passage a few lines later is genuine: "When your army entered the country of the Six Nations, we called you the town-destroyer..." The pass also garbled the signatory as "Complanter". `[A for the corrected text as printed at sullivanclinton.com; see §10 for the access problem and the re-verification owed]`
- 15 April 1791, Jones Point, the district's south corner stone. **Two claims fail.** He did not oversee the placement: the Alexandria Masonic lodge conducted the ceremony at a point Andrew Ellicott had determined, and his own role was the proclamation of 30 March 1791 initiating the survey. Nor did he choose the district to bear his name; the three commissioners named the federal city after him in September 1791. `[A: Wikipedia, Boundary markers of the original District of Columbia]`
- November 1790 to March 1797, President's House, Market Street: nine enslaved people from Mount Vernon rotated in and out of Pennsylvania to evade the state's gradual abolition law. `[A: Lawler, PMHB, 2002]`
- **Correction.** The pass had Oney Judge and Hercules Posey both fleeing the Philadelphia house. Only Judge did, 21 May 1796. Hercules escaped from **Mount Vernon** on 22 February 1797, having been left there after Christmas 1796. Mary V. Thompson established this; the older "Philadelphia, March 1797" account traces to Stephen Decatur Jr. (1933) and is disproven by a farm report of that week recording Hercules "absconded 4 [days ago]". `[A: Wikipedia, Hercules Posey]`
- October 1794: leads federalised militia against the Whiskey Rebellion from the Fort Cumberland muster, the only time a sitting President has commanded troops in the field. `[A: Papers, Pres. Ser. vol. 17]`
- 19 September 1796: the Farewell Address in Claypoole's American Daily Advertiser, drafted largely by Hamilton over a June 1792 Madison outline; Congress reads it aloud on his birthday, a House practice from February 1862, formalised in the Senate by 1899, dropped by the House in 1984. `[A: Wikipedia, Farewell Address]` **Correction:** not "roughly ten weeks before that year's presidential election": the 1796 election ran 4 November to 7 December, so publication came about six and a half weeks before polling opened. The presidency ends 4 March 1797. `[A: Wikipedia GW]`

## 9. Death, tomb, and the making of the canon

- 14 December 1799: dies at Mount Vernon after a sudden throat illness, aged sixty-seven, interred in the old family vault on the 18th, four days later, not three as the pass had it. `[A: Tobias Lear's account]` **Correction:** "acute epiglottitis" is a modern retrospective diagnosis and is contested, with quinsy or peritonsillar abscess and the effect of the bleedings among competing hypotheses. Lear describes symptoms, not a diagnosis. Cause of death is `[R]` and tagged so.
- **The manumission correction, the weightiest here.** The will did not free 317 people. 317 was the total enslaved population of Mount Vernon in 1799: 124 owned by Washington, 153 Custis dower slaves over whom he had no legal power, 40 rented. The will freed William Lee outright and provided for eventual manumission of the 124 only. Martha signed the deed in December 1800, effective 1 January 1801, not "the following year" read as 1800. `[A: Wikipedia, GW and slavery]`
- 1824: Lafayette is left alone in the vault, comes out weeping, carries away a ring cut from Washington's hair. `[A: marques_de_lafayette.journey.json]` The pool fixes only the year, so the journey defaults month and day and says so.
- 1830 an intruder fails to steal the skull; 1831 the tomb the will specified is built and the remains moved; 7 October 1837 the lead casket goes into a marble sarcophagus by John Struthers, Martha's to the left. `[A: Wikipedia, Mount Vernon]`
- **The Capitol crypt.** A tomb beneath the rotunda was in William Thornton's original design; the crypt was built under Bulfinch in the 1820s and never used. **Correction:** the flat 1827 completion date is unsupported as stated and the causal account is not single-stranded. Wikipedia attributes the non-use to disputes over design and cost, to the will, to Martha's assent to Mount Vernon burial, and to the 1832 refusal by John Augustine Washington. The crypt survives as the ground-floor thoroughfare, holding statuary and the compass marking the origin of the city's quadrants. `[A: Wikipedia, Washington's Tomb, US Capitol]`
- Weems publishes his Life in 1800 and adds the cherry tree in a later edition, uncorroborated `[A that Weems published it: Wikipedia, Parson Weems; R as to the anecdote]`, fixed in schooling by the McGuffey Reader. Lodge (1889) calls the Weems anecdotes false on their face; Mosher (1923) argues for the cherry tree's truth. **Recorded, not adjudicated.**

## 10. Gaps and unverified matter

1. **The Cornplanter speech.** Founders Online was unreachable (blank responses and 404s) and WebFetch returns 403 on sullivanclinton.com, from which the corrected text came by other means. The corrected sentence is quoted in the journey with the flag on `quote_source`, and still needs re-verification against American State Papers, Indian Affairs, vol. 1. An open obligation, not a solved problem.
2. **Thin quotation coverage.** The pool yielded nine verbatim quotations for a sixty-seven-year life, six from Lafayette's memoir or Castiglioni's travels, both in translation, one of them fabricated. Eight are used across forty-four stops, below the atlas's usual density, and Washington's own voice is largely absent: the Papers were reached only through secondary citation. A gap in the pass, not in the archive.
3. **Freemasonry after 1752.** The initiation record is firm; the later Masonic career, the 1793 Capitol cornerstone ceremony and the apron provenance were not independently researched. Freeman's volumes were likewise never consulted directly, only at one remove.
4. **The dower slaves after 1801.** The 153 Custis dower people were covered by neither manumission, and their dispersal among Custis heirs was not traced. The largest substantive hole here.
5. **Modern archaeology at the tomb.** No source found either confirming or ruling out any twentieth or twenty-first century exhumation. `[R: the negative is a gap, not a finding]`
6. **Attested but given no stop**, for count reasons; all still stand: Kip's Bay `[R]`, Cherry Street and the levee protocol, Morristown 1779-1780, Wethersfield 1781, the Great Falls canal works, Jones Point 1791, Brumidi's Apotheosis and the Washington Monument.

## 11. Coordinate discipline

Eleven pins moved. **Mount Vernon mansion:** the pass used 38.7292 / -77.0864 in four places, about 2.4 km north of the mansion near Fort Hunt. The atlas already carries Mount Vernon at **38.7075 / -77.0861** in the two Lafayette journeys, and this journey inherits that pin byte-identically at all seven Mount Vernon stops. **McConkey's Ferry:** 40.2971 / -74.8579 lands on the New Jersey bank and inverts the point of the crossing; corrected to 40.2975 / -74.8764. **Jumonville Glen:** 39.8494 / -79.6064 sits 4.7 km southeast, nearer Fort Necessity than the glen; corrected to 39.8794 / -79.6456, keeping the sites distinct. Also inherited byte-identically: Monmouth 40.2662 / -74.2932, Valley Forge 40.1012 / -75.4515, Brandywine 39.8734 / -75.5949, Yorktown 37.2384 / -76.5074, the State House 39.9489 / -75.15. Six further pins moved 0.85 to 3.8 km; the Williamsburg figure was the city, not the Capitol.

## 12. Interlocks with the existing atlas

Eight travelers are named in campa, each with its source journey in the stop's `sources`: **`marques_de_lafayette`**, six pins and the densest interlock so far (Brandywine, Valley Forge and the Conway Cabal, Monmouth, Yorktown, the Bastille key, the 1824 hour alone in the vault); **`madame_de_lafayette`** (the apron and its withdrawn attribution, and George Washington Lafayette sheltered at Mount Vernon during the Terror); **`louis_xvi`** (Yorktown as the battle his treasury bought); **`miranda`** (the capital in 1783, the Havana silver of 1780); **`hiram_abiff`** (the Fredericksburg lodge, and Mackey treating Hiram as legend); **`symmes`** (Cincinnati, named for the order Washington headed); **`tschiffely`** and **`alvear`** (the capital that never received his body). Paris and Buenos Aires anchor those journeys and are declined here: Washington never crossed the Atlantic. `[R]`
