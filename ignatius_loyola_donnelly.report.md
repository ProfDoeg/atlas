# Ignatius Loyola Donnelly (1831-1901): research report

*Compiled 2026-08-22 for `ignatius_loyola_donnelly.journey.json`. Congressman, lieutenant governor, newspaper editor, novelist, Populist platform-writer, and the man who put Atlantis back into circulation in English.*

**Legend.** **[A]** attested, source named inline. **[R]** reconstruction, tradition, or inference, including anything asserted without a document behind it. **[C]** a contradiction between sources, flagged and *not* resolved.

The dossier rests on a thin base: English Wikipedia, MNopedia, Donnelly's own books on Project Gutenberg, and two platform transcriptions. No archival material was consulted; that is the largest gap in the file. Where a correction overturned an earlier claim, the overturned version is printed too, so the error is not re-imported from an old note. Eleven coordinates were wrong in an earlier pass, each fix now restated in its own stop's `date_confidence`; two of them, Nininger and Calvary, were disagreements between research passes rather than between sources.

## 1. Philadelphia: birth, schooling, the bar (1831-1856)

- Born **3 November 1831**, Philadelphia **[A, Wikipedia, "Ignatius L. Donnelly"]**, in **Moyamensing**, an Irish immigrant district south of South Street **[A, MNopedia]**. District level only; no street address found.
- Father **Philip Carroll Donnelly** of **Fintona, County Tyrone** **[A, Wikipedia]**, a **physician** **[A, MNopedia]**; mother **Catherine Gavin Donnelly**, who **kept a pawn shop** **[A, Wikipedia]**. *Correction:* an earlier draft said "physician and druggist"; "druggist" is unsourced and dropped.
- **Central High School** under **John S. Hart** **[A, Wikipedia]**; attendance years nowhere found, so the stop's 1845 date is an ordering device **[R]**. The school left Juniper Street for Broad and Green in **September 1854**, after his time **[A, Wikipedia, "Central High School (Philadelphia)"]**.
- **The Mourner's Vision**, poem, **1850** **[A]**. No publisher, print run, or reception found. Gap.
- Clerked for **Benjamin Brewster**, later U.S. Attorney General **[R, asserted without a document]**.
- **[C] Bar admission.** Wikipedia: **1852**. MNopedia: **1853**. Unresolved; the stop uses 1852 and states the conflict. *Correction:* an earlier note framed this as "Wikipedia 1855 vs MNopedia 1853." Wikipedia nowhere says 1855; that is the marriage-and-move year.
- Married **Katherine McCaffrey**, 1855, three children; resigned a legal clerkship the same year to speak for the **Democrats**, and moved to Minnesota Territory in **1857** "amid rumors of a financial scandal in Philadelphia" **[A as reported by Wikipedia; the scandal itself [R]**, no particulars, charge, or outcome anywhere checked**]**.

## 2. Nininger City (1857-1859)

- **[C] Founder credit.** Wikipedia's Donnelly article credits him with co-founding the utopian community; Wikipedia, "Nininger, Minnesota" says the town "was founded and named by **John Nininger**, brother in-law of Governor Alexander Ramsey," incorporated winter 1857-58, and MNopedia has Donnelly "partnered with John Nininger to promote" it. **The file writes him as partner and promoter from 1857, not co-founder**, on the strength of the town article naming the incorporator, and records the conflict in `date_confidence`.
- *Chronology check that killed a claim:* an earlier note had him writing Nininger promotional literature in **1856**, impossible on his 1857 arrival **[A]**. The stop moves to 1857 and says why.
- Population approached **1,000 by spring 1858**; the **Panic of 1857** collapsed the venture and left him heavily indebted **[A, Wikipedia]**.
- Ran for the legislature and lost, **1857** and again **1858** **[A, Wikipedia]**. No districts, opponents, or margins in any source. Gap.
- Remained at Nininger for life in a large farmhouse among fields, called **the Sage of Nininger**; two plaques survive, for Good Templars Hall and the residence **[A, MNopedia]**.
- Post office **opened 1856, discontinued 1889 after 33 years** **[A, Wikipedia]**. *Arithmetic flag:* the usual gloss "opened not long after the 1857 founding" cannot stand with a 33-year run ending in 1889, which puts the opening before he arrived. Gloss dropped.
- The house survived to at least **1939**, a 1930s state-park effort failed, and it was later demolished **[A, Wikipedia]**. Posthumous, so report only.

## 3. Office: Wabasha Hill and Washington (1860-1869)

- **Lieutenant Governor, 1860-1863** **[A, Wikipedia]**; day of taking office not given, defaulted. **U.S. Representative**, Minnesota's 2nd district, seated **4 March 1863**, through the 40th Congress to **3 March 1869** **[A, Wikipedia]**.
- Radical Republican backing the **Freedmen's Bureau** and **women's suffrage** **[A, Wikipedia]**. No roll calls, dates, or speeches given; the 1866 stop is an ordering device **[R]**. The quarrel with the Minnesota Republican machine and the **Ramsey** faction that ended his career is **[R]**: repeated in general accounts, documented in nothing checked here.
- **The capitol he actually sat in** is the **first** (1854-1881, Wabasha Hill) and then the **second** (1883-1905, same site); the coordinates compilations supply are the **1905** building, opened four years after he died.
- **Library of Congress stop dropped.** An earlier reconstruction pinned him at the **Jefferson Building, opened 1897**; in his years the Library sat in the **Capitol's west front**, so the reading is folded into the 1863 Capitol stop and flagged **[R]** there.

## 4. The Anti-Monopolist and the farmer revolt (1874-1879)

- Founded the **Anti-Monopolist** at **St. Paul, 1874** **[A, MNopedia]**. *Correction:* an earlier pass tagged this **[R]**, "not independently verified"; MNopedia states it, so the flag is withdrawn. Its editorial content as described in the campa is **[R]**: no issues were read.
- **Minnesota Senate, 20th district, 6 January 1874 to 6 January 1879** **[A, Wikipedia]**; an earlier note ended the term in 1878.
- **[C] The crowd of ten thousand, 1877.** Wikipedia states verbatim: "In 1877, Donnelly spoke at a meeting of 10,000 people where he read his preamble to the People's Party conference platform." **Anachronistic:** the party did not exist until 1891-92 and the famous preamble is the **Omaha Platform** preamble of 1892, his 1877 vehicle being the Anti-Monopoly and Greenback agitation. The stop keeps the crowd and the preamble-reading, **drops the party name**, and is **[R]**. Location within Minnesota is not given; St. Paul is a declared placeholder.

## 5. Atlantis and Ragnarok (1880-1883)

- **Atlantis: The Antediluvian World**, Harper and Brothers, **1882**, a commercial success **[A, Wikipedia]**; the Harper buildings stood on the west side of **Franklin Square** **[A, Wikipedia, "Franklin Square (Manhattan)"]**.
- **Composition at Nininger is [R]**: no source says where he wrote it; inferred from continuous residence **[A, MNopedia]**.
- Five quotations, all **[A]**, verbatim from **Project Gutenberg ebook 4032** and checked against it: the Ch. I opening, Propositions 2 and 12, the Ch. II opening, and the closing "Further investigations and discoveries will, I trust, confirm the correctness of the conclusions at which I have arrived." *Correction:* an earlier note cited "Plato has preserved for us the history of Atlantis" to Chapter I; it opens **Chapter II**.
- **Available quotation deliberately unused:** "...they will aid us to rehabilitate the fathers of our civilization..." is verbatim, but an earlier note wrongly called it a direct continuation of the preceding quoted sentence; an intervening clause sits between them, so it is usable only with ellipses at both ends.
- **Ragnarok: The Age of Fire and Gravel**, **1883**, published by **D. Appleton and Company, New York** **[A, Wikipedia; Open Library]**. *Correction:* the geography pass gave Harper and Brothers. Wrong. **"Ragnarok also sold well" is [R]**, not asserted: that article carries no sales figures.
- **Kircher's map** in **Mundus Subterraneus (1665)** is an early modern attempt to site Atlantis **[A, via `kircher.journey.json`]**; that **Donnelly used it** is **[R]** on sources checked, and the stop carries the hedge.
- *Reception, attached to his own publication stop, not staged as anyone else's scene:* **Blavatsky** and the Theosophical Society "avidly supported" the book and absorbed the drowned continent **[A, Wikipedia, citing Jason Colavito]**, her **Secret Doctrine (1888)** citing Donnelly directly (**[A]** externally, **[R]** in-file, since `blavatsky.journey.json` does not name him); **Le Plongeon** sited his Mu in the Atlantic in **1884** following Donnelly **[A, his own journey file]**, within Donnelly's lifetime; **Steiner** is also named as a supporter **[A]**, unused for want of more than the name.

## 6. The Baconian years (1887-1888, 1899)

- **The Shakespeare Myth**, 1887; **Minnesota House, 25th district, Independent, 1887-1889** **[A, Wikipedia]**.
- **The Great Cryptogram**, **R. S. Peale and Company, Chicago, 1888** **[A, Open Library; Wikipedia]**. *Correction:* an earlier pass tagged the publisher **[R]**; upgraded. Peale's street address was not found, so the pin is city-level.
- Travelled to England to arrange English publication **by Sampson Low** **[A, Wikipedia]**. *Correction:* an earlier pass called this line unstable across fetches; it is in the article, flag withdrawn. **The location was wrong:** the firm was at **St. Dunstan's House, Fetter Lane from 1887**, and only later at Paternoster Row **[A, Wikipedia, "Sampson Low"]**.
- **Oxford and Cambridge Unions [A]**, after a contradiction resolved *against* an earlier pass. Wikipedia states verbatim that he spoke "at the Oxford (and Cambridge) Union in which his thesis 'Resolved, that the works of William Shakespeare were composed by Francis Bacon' was put to an unsuccessful vote." An earlier pass over the same article could not find the word "Oxford" and downgraded both stops to tradition: **the downgrade was the error, not the claim.** **No source gives the dates**; the May 1888 dates order the pair and say so.
- Lectured across **England, Scotland, and Ireland** **[A, MNopedia]**. No itinerary, venues, or dates found; London is a base pin and the stop admits it.
- **Stratford-upon-Avon is [R] and weak.** No source names a visit to Henley Street; inferred from the tour alone, marked as tradition, and written so the prose does not assert he went.
- **The Cipher in the Plays, and on the Tombstone**, 1899 **[A]**.
- *Downstream, not staged:* the Cryptogram set off a wave of Baconian cipher-hunting, **Owen** and **Gallup** extending the method, and **the Friedmans' The Shakespearean Ciphers Examined (1957)** finding no such ciphers in the text **[A, Wikipedia, "Baconian theory"]**. Both posthumous; a `suggested_ref` on his Chicago stop only.

## 7. Populism (1890-1900)

- **Caesar's Column**, as **Edmund Boisgilbert, M.D.**, **F. J. Schulte and Co., Chicago, 1890**; about **60,000** copies at once and **250,000** in all; set in the New York of **1988** under a plutocratic oligarchy **[A]**.
- **Doctor Huguet** 1891, **The Golden Bottle** 1892 **[A]**; plot summaries from the novels, place of composition **[R]**.
- **Minnesota Senate, 24th district, 5 January 1891 to 6 January 1895** **[A]**.
- **St. Louis Industrial Conference, February 1892**: Donnelly gives the closing speech and the delegates resolve to form the People's Party **[A, Wikipedia, "People's Party (United States)"]**. The figure of **twenty-two** organizations is **[R]** here. The quoted sentence, "We meet in the midst of a nation brought to the verge of moral, political, and material ruin," is attached by Wikipedia to this speech and **also opens the Omaha preamble**; both facts sit in the `quote_source`.
- **Omaha Platform preamble, 4 July 1892** **[A, Wikipedia]**. **[C] Wording:** authoritative texts read "the spirit of the grand general and **chief** who established our independence," with "the plain people" in quotation marks, per the **American Presidency Project** (following Porter and Johnson, *National Party Platforms*) and **Wikisource**; the "**chieftain**" variant appears only in Wikipedia's "Omaha Platform" article, and the file follows the two transcriptions. *Correction:* an earlier note cited the quotation to Wikipedia's Donnelly article, which does not contain it.
- **[C] Gubernatorial run, year corrected.** He ran in **1892**, not 1900: **39,862 votes, 15.58 percent**, against Knute Nelson's **109,220**, on 8 November 1892 **[A, Wikipedia, "1892 Minnesota gubernatorial election"]**; the 1900 returns list only Van Sant and Lind. **MNopedia also claims an 1888 run; the 1888 returns list only Merriam, Wilson, and Harrison**, so 1888 is unsupported and absent from the file.
- **The Bryan Campaign for the American People's Money**, 1896; **Minnesota House, 24th district, 4 January 1897 to 1 January 1899** **[A, Wikipedia]**.
- Married his secretary **Marian Hanson**, **1898** **[A, Wikipedia]**. Place and date within the year not attested, so the Nininger pin is **[R]**; her age is nowhere stated and is not asserted.
- Nominated for **Vice President** with **Wharton Barker** at the anti-fusion Populist convention, **Cincinnati, May 1900**; the ticket lost **[A, Wikipedia, "Wharton Barker"]**. Vote share not quoted from a source; the file says only "a fraction of a percent."

## 8. Death and afterlife

- Died at **Minneapolis, 1 January 1901**, aged sixty-nine **[A, Wikipedia; MNopedia]**. The tradition that he **collapsed after a New Year's toast** is **[R]**, called unattested inside the campa itself.
- Buried at **Calvary Cemetery**, 753 Front Avenue, **St. Paul** **[A, Wikipedia]**. **Honest gap:** Wikipedia's roster of notable Calvary burials **does not list Donnelly** though his biography page names the cemetery, **no headstone description was found**, and a FindAGrave lookup returned a blocked page. The stop describes no stone.
- **Papers** held at the **Minnesota Historical Society** **[A, MNHS/MNopedia]**; the quoted **1812-1973** span **could not be verified** and is omitted.
- *Posthumous influence, `suggested_refs` and report only, never stops:* **Churchward's** Mu; **Tolkien's** Númenor; **Velikovsky's** catastrophism; **Colavito** calling Atlantis "the platonic ideal of fringe history" **[A, Wikipedia]**.

## Stop ownership: what was refused

Three candidate stops were rejected on the rule that a stop belongs to whoever was actually there. **Le Plongeon naming Mu in New York (1884)** and **Spence writing The Problem of Atlantis in Edinburgh (1924)** cite Donnelly but belong on their own files, where they already sit. **Blavatsky writing the Secret Doctrine at Würzburg and Holland Park** is her travel and her file; Donnelly's file carries the Theosophical reception of *his own book* at *his own* publication stop. **The MNHS reading room** received his papers after his death, so it is folded into the Calvary stop.

The **canonical Paris pin** (48.8566, 2.3522) supplied by the interlock lens as a Francis Bacon hub was **not used**: his documented 1888 travel is England, Scotland, and Ireland, and no source puts him in Paris. Staging a visit to inherit a pin would have been an invention.

A real one-directional relation, but the book postdates Donnelly's death by twenty-three years, so a pointer from his file would be a new hash in an old block. **Not written**; it lives correctly in Spence's file.

3. **Serrano's Antarctic Hyperborea, via the Pytheas and Blavatsky files: FORWARD, and weak.** The chain runs Donnelly to Blavatsky to Serrano, wholly posthumous, and Serrano is nowhere recorded reading Donnelly. **Not written**, and it would fail even under a backward grant, being no relation of his own life.
4. **Velikovsky, Tolkien, the Friedmans: FORWARD.** All engaged his work after his death. `suggested_refs` and report matter only.
5. **Kircher: BACKWARD, and legal with no exception needed.** Kircher died in 1680 and the map is a 1665 artifact used by Donnelly himself: an old hash in a new block. It is a stop, hedged on evidence, not chronology.
