# Plato: report

**Sources.** The spine is Plato's own Seventh Letter (the only extended autobiographical document to survive from a classical Greek philosopher, and the primary source for all three Syracusan voyages, Dion, and Archytas's rescue), Diogenes Laertius's *Life of Plato* (Book III of the *Lives of Eminent Philosophers* — birth, naming, Socrates, the Aegina slave-market, the Academy, the death), and Plutarch's *Life of Dion* (the fullest narrative of the Sicilian court, the dust-covered palace floor, and Dion's assassination by Callippus). The doctrinal stops quote the dialogues directly: *Apology* and *Phaedo* for Socrates's trial and death, *Timaeus* and *Critias* for Sais and Atlantis, *Republic* VII and X for the cave and the myth of Er, *Phaedrus* for the chariot of the soul. Secondary orientation from the Loeb *Epistles*, MacTutor, and the Aeon essay on Plato's Italian voyages.

**Judgment calls.** (1) The Egyptian leg (Heliopolis, Sais) is the shakiest attested fact in Plato's biography — some ancient sources doubt he went at all — so both stops are marked "traditional." I placed Plato's visit to Sais as a *confirmation* of a story his own family (descended from Solon) already carried, rather than conflating Plato with Solon himself, who heard the tale from the priest Sonchis two centuries earlier. (2) The cave, myth of Er, and chariot are dated as Academy "telling" events in the 380s-370s, since the instruction asked that they be narrated as events, not merely cited as texts — these dates are frankly inferred from standard compositional chronology (Republic before Phaedrus) and should be read as symbolic placements, not attested days. (3) The Critias's ending is not artistic license — it genuinely breaks off mid-sentence in Zeus's speech; I used the manuscript's actual last words as the closing quote. (4) "Aristotle" and "Dante" per the curator's edge list are not yet independent files in the atlas (checked index.json — 130 journeys, no aristotle/socrates/pythagoras/archytas entries exist yet), so those connections are narrative-only for now, ready to bind when those journeys are written.

**Time-folds / gaps.** The three Syracuse trips (388-387, 367-365, 361-360 BC) are compressed from what were each roughly two-year sojourns; only the hinge events (the insult to Dionysius I, Dion's exile, Archytas's galley) get stops. The 33 years between the Academy's founding (387 BC) and Plato's death (347 BC) are thin on record outside the Sicilian episodes and the late dialogues — this is honest to the sources, which go dark on Plato's day-to-day teaching life entirely.

**Five richest episodes.** (1) The exchange with Dionysius I — "You talk like an old dotard." / "And you like a tyrant." — is one of the only verbatim exchanges to survive from Plato's life outside the dialogues. (2) The Aegina slave-market, where the same wit that nearly got him killed at Syracuse gets him acquitted on a capital charge by a juror's joke. (3) Archytas's thirty-oared galley arriving to pull Plato out of Ortygia — a Pythagorean engineer-statesman literally rowing to rescue Western philosophy. (4) The Critias stopping mid-sentence inside Zeus's judgment speech — the only classical text I know of whose ending *is* its content. (5) Dion's murder and Plato's cold fury at Callippus, his own former student — the philosopher-king experiment collapsing into a philosophy-school assassination.

**Connections to the atlas.** Direct edges: Zoroaster (both founders of a school whose fire and cave imagery converge — Zarathustra's fire-altar and Plato's cave are already siblings in the corpus); Symmes (the hollow-earth prophet inherits Atlantis and the myth of Er's underground geography directly from this file — his journey should eventually cite Timaeus/Critias as its deepest root); Hypatia (her file already runs Alexandria-to-neoplatonism; Plato's Academy is the trunk her line grows from). Aristotle, Archytas, Socrates, Pythagoras, and Dante are named edges awaiting their own files — Socrates's death here (399 BC) is the natural gate for a future Socrates journey, and Archytas's rescue is rich enough to deserve his own entry as engineer-statesman-Pythagorean. The Atlantis material also opens a direct line forward to any future Critias/Solon dataset, should the atlas want to trace the story back one more generation.
