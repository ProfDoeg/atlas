# Saint Eustace (Placidus, Eustathios): research report
*2026-08-08. Slug `saint_eustace`. Legendary dates c. 98-118 CE; cult attested from the 5th-8th centuries. Martyred in every version with his wife (Theopista, in Caxton Theospis, elsewhere Tatiana) and their sons Agapius and Theopistus.*

**Legend.** **[A]** = attested, source named in place. **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition, or inference, including everything resting on the Passio alone. Because the reference literature classifies this saint as fictitious, the [A]/[R] line here runs between **what a document says** and **what happened**, not between true and false: a sentence of the Golden Legend is [A] as text and [R] as history.

The structural fact: **the earliest surviving text postdates the events it narrates by at least three centuries and probably five, and the earliest hard evidence of the cult postdates them by six.** Sections 1-4 are literature; section 5 is where evidence begins. The narrative is told in `saint_eustace.journey.json`; what follows is the evidence audit, the corrections applied to the research pool, and the gaps.

---

## 1. The name and the office

**[R, Golden Legend]** A Roman of high rank named **Placidus** serves under **Trajan** (98-117) as commander of the emperor's armies. **[A, textual]** The Latin tradition gives his rank as ***magister militum***, the Greek Passio as ***stratelates***.

**[A, correction applied]** *Magister militum is an anachronism.* That office was created under **Constantine** in the fourth century and did not exist under Trajan. The Latin title is a later hagiographic gloss, not a Trajanic rank; the pool asserted it without caveat. (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Eustace; de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eustachius, "Feldherr unter Trajan".)

**[A, negative]** No second-century Roman source, inscription, historian, or administrative record names a general Placidus or Eustathius. This absence is the central basis for the scholarly verdict, not a gap in this session's searching.

**[R, church tradition]** His house is said to have stood where Sant'Eustachio stands, marked by a plane tree from which the church took the byname *in platana*. This works backward from the building and is not an ancient attestation.

## 2. The vision on Monte Guadagnolo

**[A, text]** The Caxton-Ellis Temple Classics wording, verified from the djvu text at archive.org (`TheGoldenLegendV6`), vol. VI, pp. 84-93, supplies all direct speech in the journey. Fifteen quotations were checked; nine are used.

**[A, place, corrected]** The traditional vision site is the **Santuario della Mentorella**, Monte Guadagnolo, Capranica Prenestina, 1,018 m, attested from the sixth century and Subiaco property until the fifteenth. **Its coordinates are 41.9214 / 12.9364.** The pool carried 41.8964 / 12.9107 in three entries: empty hillside 3.4 km southwest, pointing at nothing. A fourth pinned the vision at the town of Tivoli, 41.9633 / 12.7975, some 12 km west. All corrected. (it.wikipedia.org; OpenStreetMap/Nominatim.)

**[R]** No baptismal site is named in any version; the journey uses the traditional house site as an acknowledged placeholder.

**[A, literary]** The second vision states the Job pattern explicitly and in advance; the text makes the comparison itself. Philologists take Job as one of two acknowledged borrowings, the other being Greco-Roman romance convention.

## 3. The stripping and the reunion

**[R, Golden Legend]** Plague and robbers strip the household; the family flees Rome for Egypt; the shipmaster seizes Theopista for the unpaid fare; at an unnamed river a lion takes one son and a wolf the other, and herdsmen rescue both unseen by their father, who believes them dead. Eustace works fifteen years as a field-watchman until two officers sent by Trajan, **Antiochus and Acacius**, know him by an old war scar. Restored to command, he recovers his sons, overheard telling each other the story of the river, and then Theopista, who keeps a poor inn on the line of march.

**[R, supplied geography]** *The legend names no port of departure, no captain, no Egyptian harbour, no river, and no village.* Ostia (41.7555 / 12.2915), Alexandria's ancient harbour (31.2139 / 29.885) and an illustrative Delta point (30.8 / 31.0) are supplied, each flagged as supplied in the stop's `date_confidence` rather than passed off as attested.

**[A, textual, corrected]** The village of the fifteen years is **Badioson** (Βαδιοσών). The pool gave "Badissus/Baddas", which are Latin garblings (Badissus, Badesia) and secondary. English Wikipedia: "The village of Badioson where Eustace retires after the loss of his family is unknown." **A gap, not an omission.** No coordinate is possible; the journey reuses the Delta placeholder and says so.

**[R, flagged as observation, not citation]** Recognition-by-scar is the device that returns Odysseus to Ithaca; no source here makes that comparison. What *is* sourced is the classification of the plot as a pre-Christian "Man Tried by Fate" folktale adapted to a martyr frame.

**[A, corrected: the strongest internal evidence in the dossier]** The Passio **names** the river of the victory: the **Hydaspes**, where Alexander defeated Porus in 326 BCE. En.wikipedia: "the river Hydaspes where Eustace fights his victorious battle with the 'barbarians' alludes to Alexander the Great and bears no relation to any historical conflict fought by emperor Trajan." Two pool entries err here in opposite directions: the geography lens said flatly that "no campaign, enemy, or location is specified", the crossing note attached the Hydaspes to the river where the sons are lost. Neither holds. The crossing river is unnamed; the battle river is named and taken from Alexander. The journey inherits 32.933 / 73.733 byte-identical from `alexander.journey.json`.

## 4. The martyrdom

**[A]** Trajan dies about **9 August 117** at **Selinus in Cilicia** (modern Gazipasa, Turkey, roughly 36.27 N / 32.30 E). Hadrian is in Syria at the accession and does not enter Rome until **9 July 118**.

**[A, correction applied]** The pool placed Trajan's death at Rome, coordinate 41.8841 / 12.488, near the Circus Maximus, corresponding to nothing in the claim. **Fabricated location, withdrawn**; the 117 date and the succession are correct. The pool also called 118 "the first year of Hadrian's reign"; it is the year after his accession. Reworded.

**[R, Golden Legend]** Hadrian orders sacrifice in thanksgiving for the victory; the family refuses, is thrown to a lion that lies down and will not touch them, and is then shut inside a heated bronze bull. Three days later the bodies are found unburnt and buried by Christians at an unnamed place.

**[Contradiction, recorded not resolved]** *Where in Rome.* Sources variously say the Forum area, near the Capitol, or an amphitheatre; no adjudication is available and none is attempted. The journey uses the Forum for the bull, the Capitoline for the refusal, and the Colosseum for the lion, each with a supplied-venue caveat.

**[Contradiction, recorded not resolved]** *The year.* 118 is commonly given and confirmed as the tradition by en. and de.wikipedia ("um 118"), but the Caxton text itself dates the martyrdom to "about the year 120, calends of November", and some martyrologies give later dates. Both are carried.

## 5. Where the evidence actually begins

- **[A]** The oldest surviving Greek passion, **BHG 641**, is dated to **the 5th to 7th centuries**; the pool's floor of "no earlier than the 6th-7th century" is a century too late. The number **BHL 2760** given in the pool for the Latin recension **could not be verified in any source reachable this session** and is dropped.
- **[A]** Latin veneration is attested only from the **8th century**. The pool's "no cult before the 7th century" is a century conservative on the Greek side and a century optimistic on the Latin. Recast: nothing before the 5th at the earliest, nothing in the Latin West before the 8th.
- **[A]** A Roman church of Eustace is named in the pontificate of **Gregory II (731-741)**: the first hard evidence of the cult, already used as such by `catorce_santos_ayudantes`.
- **[A]** The church appears as a **diaconia in 795** under Leo III. Its high altar rests on a red porphyry urn said to hold the family's relics; **the translation date and the authenticity of the contents are not established**, and the Italian source does not claim otherwise.
- **[A]** Rebuilt completely 1195-1196 under Celestine III, then rebuilt Baroque across the 17th-18th centuries after repeated Tiber flooding, principal campaign 1701-1703. The facade carries a stag's head with a cross between the antlers, at once the conversion emblem and the arms of the Maffei, who paid.
- **[A]** Historians of hagiography classify Eustace as a **fictitious saint**; English Wikipedia uses the term. It follows from the absence of second-century evidence plus the identified literary borrowings.

## 6. The relics, and the Luxeuil problem

**[A, correction applied; the discrepancy resolves]** The pool held two conflicting entries: a translation of relics to Saint-Denis "in the reign of Charlemagne or shortly after" (c. 800), and a flag that the Saint-Denis basilica article mentions no Eustace at all. Resolution: **no Carolingian translation is attested.** The source is **Abbot Suger** (1122-1151), who "mentions the first relics of Eustace in Europe, at an altar in the royal Basilica of St Denis", sometimes dated 1140. The c. 800 date is withdrawn.

**[A, and not resolved]** English Wikipedia adds that **"the Eustace venerated at Saint-Denis may have been Eustace of Luxeuil"** (d. 611), a different saint. This is carried forward unresolved into the Paris stop: Saint-Eustache at Les Halles is renamed in 1303 on receiving relics from Saint-Denis, inheriting the identity problem with the bones.

## 7. Iconography and transmission

- **[A]** **Chartres, baie 43**, north nave aisle, glazed **1205-1215**: a full life cycle. **Three corrections.** (1) Not the north transept rose: baie 121, the north rose, is c. 1230, given by Louis IX and Blanche of Castile, and holds no Eustace cycle. (2) The date is 1205-1215, not 1225. (3) The "furriers" donor attribution is unsupported; the drapers are the usual attribution, and the donor is left unspecified rather than guessed. (fr.wikipedia.org, "Vitraux de la cathedrale de Chartres".)
- **[A]** **Jacobus de Voragine**, later archbishop of Genoa, includes the Life in the ***Legenda Aurea***, c. 1260, fixing the version the West reads thereafter; Caxton prints it in English in 1483.
- **[A]** **Pisanello**, *The Vision of Saint Eustace*, c. 1438-1442, National Gallery London **NG1436**; patron unconfirmed (Gonzaga, Visconti, Leonello and Borso d'Este all proposed). **[A] Durer**, engraving *Eustachius* (Bartsch 57), c. 1501, Nuremberg, his largest.
- **[A, correction applied, five centuries]** The stag passes to **Saint Hubert of Liege**. The pool dated the transfer to c. 900. It is **fifteenth century**: the story "was only attributed to Saint Hubert in the 15th century" and "has been appropriated from the legend of Saint Eustace"; the earliest Hubert hagiographies, 759-930, contain no vision. (en.wikipedia.org, "Hubertus".) The journey uses 1450 as a labelled mid-century bracket.
- **[A]** Medieval versions circulate detached from the saintly frame as secular romance. **Correction**: the exemplars named for the English and Iberian branch are ***Sir Isumbras*** and the Spanish ***Libro del Caballero Zifar***, plus a 13th-century Old French version in 2,052 rhyming octosyllables. *Guillaume d'Angleterre*, which the pool used as the type-name, is a related Old French romance and not that.

## 8. The Fourteen Holy Helpers

All pins taken byte-identical from journeys already researched.

- **[A]** **c. 1320, Regensburg**, Dominikanerkirche St. Blasius: the oldest surviving group fresco, showing **ten** helpers around Christopher, not fourteen. Whether Eustace is among the ten is **an open gap**; the source does not name them.
- **[A]** **c. 1400, Franconia**: the list closes and repeats without variation across Regensburg, Bamberg, Wurzburg and Nuremberg: Acacius, Barbara, Blaise, Catherine, Christopher, Cyriacus, Denis, Erasmus, **Eustace**, George, Giles, Margaret, Pantaleon, Vitus. `saint_acacius`, `saint_blaise`, `saint_cyriacus` and `saint_denis` state it verbatim and each already carries the Nuremberg pin: Eustace is named by position in four journeys before he has one of his own.
- **[A]** **1445-1446, Vierzehnheiligen**: the shepherd apparitions, fourteen children in red and white asking for a chapel. Eustace is counted in from the Franconian list, not named by the children. `saint_barbara`, `san_vito`, `giles` and `saint_acacius` hold this coordinate already; Eustace is the fifth.
- **[A]** **1498, Heilsbronn Minster**: the fourteen assembled in one altar, Eustace present by set membership; the descriptions reached do not identify figures individually. **[A] 1743-1772, Vierzehnheiligen**: Neumann's basilica, planned around an immovable altar on the apparition spot. **[A] 1780, Obernburg am Main**: Vambach's Nothelferaltar, "the one object in the corpus that three separate journeys inherited without changing a digit" (Barbara, Catherine, Vitus). Eustace makes four.
- **Dropped from the journey deliberately:** the **1503 Lindenhardt** winged altar (attribution contested between Grunewald and Hans von Kulmbach), cut for length. Recorded so the omission is visible.

## 9. Modern calendar: a contradiction inside the pool

The pool contains two incompatible statements about 1969. (1) Its chronology lens states that the reform of the General Roman Calendar **removed Eustace's feast** for the unreliability of the Acts. (2) Its interlock lens, quoting `catorce_santos_ayudantes.journey.json`, states that 1969 dropped **four** of the fourteen (Barbara, Catherine of Alexandria, Christopher, Margaret of Antioch) and that **ten, including Eustace, were untouched**.

Both cannot be right. **The atlas follows the arithmetic of the existing Fourteen Helpers file**, since that file is already canon and changing it silently would break four other journeys, but the disagreement is flagged in the stop's `date_confidence` rather than buried. **A reviewer should resolve it against the printed Calendarium Romanum (1969) before approval.** No copy was reachable this session.

**[A]** The Eastern churches commemorate Eustathios, Theopiste, Agapios and Theopistos on **20 September** with no historical-critical revision. The feast date is shared East and West and is not in dispute.

## 10. Toponymy: what the name actually reaches

**[A]** **Sint Eustatius**, Leeward Antilles: settled by the Dutch in 1636, later the Golden Rock, now a Dutch special municipality, the name in continuous official legal use. No source found records the act of naming; the 1636 date in the journey is the settlement year, labelled as such.

**[A, correction applied, two errors]** **Saint-Eustache, Quebec.** (1) Founded **1770**, incorporated **1835**, not 1780 as the pool had it. (2) **There is no Roman Catholic Diocese of Saint-Eustache**; the town falls under the Diocese of Saint-Jerome-Mont-Laurier. The claim is withdrawn and the entry rewritten as town and parish toponymy.

**[R, unverified]** The pool reports Eustace as patron of hunters, firefighters, and **the city of Madrid**. The Madrid patronage is asserted by en.wikipedia without date or source and **was not verified this session**; kept out of the journey rather than pinned.

## 12. Gaps, stated as gaps

1. **Badioson is unlocated.** Not "somewhere in Egypt". Unknown.
2. **The crossing river, the port of departure, the Egyptian landfall, the reunion village, the inn and the burial place are all unnamed.** Five supplied coordinates carry that fact in their `date_confidence`.
3. **BHL 2760 is unverified** and dropped. **The Madrid patronage** is unverified.
4. **The ten helpers in the 1320 Regensburg fresco are unnamed**, so Eustace's presence at that stage is unknown.
5. **The authenticity and translation date of the Sant'Eustachio relics** are not established, nor is **whether the Saint-Denis Eustace is Eustace of Luxeuil**.
6. **Whether the 1969 reform touched Eustace's feast** is contradicted inside the pool and is not resolved.
