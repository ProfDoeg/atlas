# Mongol Yam postal-relay system: Network Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `mongol_yam_postal_relay_system_network_dossier.md`  
**Period covered:** c. 1206–1400, with earlier precedents and later institutional afterlives  
**Geographic scope:** Mongolia, northern and southern China, Manchuria, Tibet, eastern Turkestan, Transoxiana, Iran, Iraq, the Caucasus, the Pontic–Caspian steppe, Rus’, and adjoining tributary or diplomatic corridors

## Evidentiary conventions

This dossier uses the following labels:

- **Documented:** directly attested by contemporary or near-contemporary texts, surviving objects, administrative compilations, or archaeology.
- **Probable:** supported by multiple sources but not directly documented at every place or date.
- **Inference:** a scholarly reconstruction from incomplete evidence.
- **Disputed:** significant sources or scholars disagree.
- **Unverified:** repeated in modern literature without adequate primary support.
- **Legend/romance:** a later story or simplification that should not be treated as documentary fact.

The “Yam” was never a modern public postal service with universal access, fixed tariffs, address-based delivery, and regular mailbags. It was primarily a state transport-and-communications entitlement: authorized travelers obtained horses, guides, vehicles, provisions, and lodging from a chain of obligated stations and households. It carried documents, but also people, baggage, intelligence, taxes, tribute, court supplies, diplomatic gifts, merchants, clerics, and occasionally prisoners.

## 1. Identification and Definition

### 1.1 What the network was

The Mongol Yam was a family of interconnected relay-post institutions created or consolidated by the Chinggisid empire. Its defining components were:

1. authorized travelers or dispatches;
2. credentials conferring access;
3. recognized routes;
4. stations maintaining remounts and other transport;
5. station personnel and guides;
6. obligated households supplying animals, fodder, provisions, lodging, labor, carts, boats, or cash;
7. officials who inspected credentials, apportioned burdens, and punished refusal or abuse.

Its central functions were:

- transmitting imperial commands;
- returning military and provincial intelligence;
- moving envoys, administrators, inspectors, and tax agents;
- coordinating armies and courts;
- connecting mobile royal encampments with cities and provincial administrations;
- sustaining diplomatic travel;
- transporting selected goods and court supplies;
- enabling some privileged commercial and religious travel.

The contemporary Chinese description in the *Yuan shi* says its purpose was to “communicate frontier conditions and promulgate orders.” That formulation accurately identifies its formal military-administrative core, but the surviving record shows broader practical use. The *Yuan shi*’s institutional account is preserved in its “Military Treatise,” chapter 101, under **站赤** (*zhanchi*).

The frequently used English phrase **“Mongol postal system”** is convenient but incomplete. “Imperial relay-transport network” better captures the movement of riders, envoys, animals, baggage, and provisions in addition to messages.

### 1.3 Chronological boundaries

Three formation dates appear in scholarship:

- **Under Chinggis Khan, before 1227:** **probable.** Changchun/Qiu Chuji’s journey of 1220–24 and Yelü Chucai’s documented use of post horses in 1227 indicate that organized relay transport existed before Ögedei’s famous decree.
- **1218:** Didier Gazagnadou associates establishment with a quriltai held in the context of western expansion. This is a **disputed reconstruction**, not a universally accepted foundation date.
- **1234, or the final years of Ögedei’s reign:** the conventional formal-foundation date. The *Secret History of the Mongols*, §279, credits Ögedei with ordering standardized stations, personnel, remounts, provisions, and connecting arrangements with Chagatai and Batu. David Morgan and Adam Silverstein argue that this was better understood as a reorganization or regularization of an existing practice.
- **1240:** some interpretations date the decree to the quriltai or narrative setting associated with the *Secret History*’s account. This depends partly on the contested dating and composition of that text.

Accordingly, the most defensible periodization is:

- **c. 1206–27:** emerging conquest and court communications;
- **c. 1230s–1259:** standardized and increasingly empire-wide Yam;
- **1260s onward:** several politically separate but institutionally related Chinggisid relay systems;
- **to 1368 in Yuan China; to 1335 and beyond in post-Ilkhanid Iran; through the later fourteenth century in Central Asia and the Jochid world.**

There was no single empire-wide closure date. The unified political network fragmented after Möngke’s death in 1259 and the wars of the 1260s, but regional Yams survived, were reformed, and generated successor institutions. The year **1368**, the fall of the Yuan capital Dadu to the Ming, is a defensible eastern boundary, not the extinction of Yam-derived posting across Eurasia.

### 1.4 What counts as part of the network

Included here are:

- official horse, cart, boat, foot, dog-sledge, and mixed relay stations;
- supporting station households;
- the credential and inspection system;
- roads and stages regularly served by those institutions;
- mobile khanal courts where routes terminated temporarily;
- the Yuan urgent-document relay insofar as it complemented station transport;
- regional Chinggisid successors derived from the imperial institution.

Not automatically included are:

- every caravan road within Mongol territory;
- the movement of whole armies, which drew on much broader pastoral and requisition systems;
- merchant caravans lacking official authorization;
- maritime shipping merely because it occurred under Mongol rule;
- every later Russian or Mongolian station called *yam* or *örtöö*.

## 2. Formation

### 2.1 Predecessors

The Yam had no single uncontested ancestor.

**Chinese precedent—documented:** Qin, Han, Tang, Song, Liao, Jin, and Western Xia states maintained official roads, stations, credentials, and relay horses. Mongol administrators inherited functioning or recoverable institutions in conquered north China. Safe-conduct tablets also had precedents under Liao, Jin, and Western Xia.

**Steppe precedent—probable:** nomadic rulers long used mounted messengers, guides, remount requisition, hospitality obligations, and chains of encampments. These arrangements were flexible and could follow moving courts rather than fixed cities.

**Islamic precedent—documented but its degree of influence disputed:** the Sasanian and caliphal **barīd** combined communications and intelligence. Khwarazmian and other Central Asian governments possessed courier arrangements before Mongol conquest. Silverstein considers the Mongol Yam within this longer postal history, while arguing that it became a distinct Chinggisid institution.

The most plausible reconstruction is institutional combination: mobile steppe communications were regularized with Uighur, Khitan, Chinese, Turkic, and Persian bureaucratic knowledge. A claim that the Mongols merely copied one Chinese model fails to explain the system’s steppe terminology, moving termini, and western adaptations.

### 2.2 The problem Ögedei addressed

The *Secret History* states that messengers had previously ridden directly among the population, requisitioning animals and provisions in an uncontrolled fashion. This exhausted both herds and subjects. Ögedei’s program was therefore simultaneously:

- a communications reform;
- a protection of the taxable and pastoral base;
- a centralization of requisition;
- an attempt to separate authorized imperial movement from private predation.

In §279, Ögedei orders post stations established at intervals, with personnel and horses assigned to them. The commonly translated prescription is **twenty post horses**, with keepers, sheep for provisions, mares for kumiss, carts, and oxen. Textual translation and manuscript problems require caution about treating every listed number as an empire-wide audited standard.

Ögedei consulted or coordinated with Chagatai; Batu was to connect the western lines. This shows that the Yam was conceived as an inter-*ulus* undertaking, not simply a road radiating from Karakorum.

### 2.3 Builders and founding institutions

- **Chinggis Khan:** established the imperial command structure and probably used organized relays; a fully standardized system under him is **not established**.
- **Ögedei Qa’an, r. 1229–41:** principal documented organizer and regularizer.
- **Chagatai:** associated in the *Secret History* with extending connections from his domain.
- **Batu:** associated with the western continuation into the Jochid lands.
- **Uighur and Khitan administrators:** their institutional influence is **probable**, but no surviving “founding committee” or comprehensive charter names all designers.
- **Local communities:** did most of the material building and provisioning, commonly under fiscal or labor compulsion.

No unitary foundation charter survives. The constitutional basis was khanal command, subsequently elaborated through decrees, household registers, administrative statutes, and provincial regulations.

### 2.4 Political and economic conditions

The system was enabled by:

- the territorial conquests of 1206–60;
- large horse herds and a culture of long-distance mounted mobility;
- appropriation of existing roads, bridges, ferries, cities, and tax systems;
- a multilingual chancery;
- portable credentials and seals;
- the household-registration principle, which assigned permanent hereditary or quasi-hereditary service burdens;
- military enforcement of compliance.

It also answered a structural difficulty: a ruler at Karakorum or a mobile *ordo* could not govern northern China, Iran, the Volga, and Central Asia through unassisted single-horse journeys.

## 3. Constituent Nodes

A complete gazetteer cannot be reconstructed. Yuan records provide aggregate provincial figures and numerous station names, but evidence is much thinner for the western khanates. Many stations were tented or built from perishable material. A named city on an itinerary was not necessarily itself a formally constituted Yam station.

### 3.1 Imperial and regional hubs

| Node | Dates and role | Evidence and qualifications |
|---|---|---|
| **Karakorum/Qaraqorum**, Orkhon Valley, Mongolia | Developed as Ögedei’s capital in the 1230s; central convergence point through the mid-thirteenth century. | **Securely attested** by Persian and Latin travelers and archaeology. It was connected to supply and communications networks despite lying in a region without an older dense urban system. After Kublai shifted the principal capital east, it remained strategically important but lost primacy. |
| **Dadu/Khanbaliq**, modern Beijing | Principal Yuan capital from Kublai’s reign; dominant eastern hub c. 1260s–1368. | **Securely attested.** Marco Polo describes roads radiating from Khanbaliq, though his totals are problematic. |

### 3.2 Yuan station categories

The *Yuan shi* describes:

- horse stations;
- ox stations;
- cart stations;
- donkey stations;
- boat or water stations;
- foot stations;
- palanquin stations;
- northeastern dog-and-sledge stations;
- mixed stations adapted to local geography.

This variety is important: “Pony Express” imagery obscures the boats of the Yangzi basin, carts of northern China, foot couriers, and dog transport in Liaodong.

The provincial count traditionally extracted from the *Yuan shi* is approximately:

| Jurisdiction | Stations reported |
|---|---:|
| Central Secretariat region | roughly 198 when land, water, and ox categories are combined in one common reading |
| Henan | 196 land and water |
| Liaoyang | 105 land, with dog stations separately discussed |
| Jiangzhe | 262 horse, palanquin, foot, and water |
| Jiangxi | 154 horse and water |
| Huguang | 173 land and water |
| Shaanxi | 81 |
| Sichuan | 132 land and water |
| Yunnan | 78 |
| Gansu | 6 in the abbreviated surviving summary |
| **Recorded total** | about **1,400** |

**Reliability:** moderate for the administrative snapshot or compilation behind the *Yuan shi*; low if treated as an exact simultaneous count for the entire Yuan realm. The total omits or incompletely represents Mongolia, Tibet, some frontier circuits, temporary stations, and chronological changes. It is not an empire-wide count extending to Iran or the Golden Horde.

### 3.3 Physical form and survival

Stations might include:

- stables or corrals;
- guest quarters;
- kitchens and stores;
- fodder and grain storage;
- wells;
- carts, harness, saddles, and boats;
- clerical space;
- surrounding service households;
- tents in pastoral regions rather than monumental buildings.

Marco Polo calls station lodgings “palaces,” sometimes furnished richly enough for high-ranking visitors. His description plausibly reflects major Yuan stations, but not every station across Eurasia.

Archaeological identification is difficult because:

- routes shifted with pasture, water, war, and the ruler’s location;
- timber, felt, mud brick, and tents leave limited traces;
- later dynasties reused sites;
- caravanserais, garrisons, villages, and relay stations can resemble one another;
- many proposed “Yam stations” are identified through later toponyms rather than excavation.

Karakorum is the best-studied central hub. Excavations demonstrate an artificial imperial city embedded in long-range supply and communication networks, but they do not yield a complete route map.

## 4. Connections

### 4.1 The network was not a single road

The expression “the Yam route” is misleading. The system was a branching and changing mesh of post roads. Later maps often draw a continuous Lisbon-to-Beijing or Venice-to-China line and label it either “Silk Road” or “Yam.” Neither was a single surveyed trunk line.

Major corridors included:

1. **Karakorum–north China:** through the Gobi and frontier settlements to Zhongdu/Dadu.
2. **Dadu–Shangdu:** serving the seasonal imperial movement.
3. **Dadu–Manchuria/Liaodong:** military and administrative roads, including cold-weather dog stations.
4. **Dadu–former Southern Song provinces:** expanded after conquest, using roads, waterways, boats, palanquins, and foot relays.
5. **Dadu–Gansu–Hexi Corridor–eastern Turkestan:** connecting the Yuan center with the Gobi margins, Ganzhou, Suzhou, Dunhuang-region roads, Hami, Turfan, and Beshbalik.
6. **Mongolia/eastern Turkestan–Ili–Transoxiana:** through Chagataid territory toward Almaliq, the Chu and Talas regions, Samarkand, and Bukhara.
7. **Transoxiana–Iran:** via Khurasan to Ilkhanid centers including Tabriz.
8. **Lower Volga–Central Asia:** connecting the Jochid realm with Khwarazm and the steppe.
9. **Rus’–lower Volga:** used by princes, tribute personnel, envoys, and clergy traveling to the khan’s court.
10. **Iran–Anatolia–Caucasus:** Ilkhanid military, fiscal, and diplomatic corridors.
11. **China–Tibet:** stations established through the Gansu/Qinghai approaches and within central Tibet.
12. **Provincial-to-port connections:** Dadu and inland provincial offices to Hangzhou, Quanzhou, Guangzhou, and other maritime outlets.

After 1260 these were not always politically interoperable. War between the Jochids and Ilkhans, conflict between Kublai and Kaidu, and rival Chagataid factions could block or redirect travelers.

### 4.2 What traveled

**Documented or strongly supported:**

- imperial edicts and sealed administrative papers;
- military intelligence and alarms;
- census, tax, and provincial reports;
- envoys and diplomatic correspondence;
- officials, inspectors, translators, and scribes;
- papal, royal, and ecclesiastical envoys;
- Buddhist, Christian, Daoist, and Muslim religious travelers when authorized;
- tribute and diplomatic gifts;
- baggage and provisions of official parties;
- tax collectors and financial agents;
- privileged merchants, especially some *ortoq* partners;
- artisans, specialists, doctors, and court personnel;
- prisoners or detained travelers under escort.

**Qualified:** The Yam facilitated commerce, but it was not principally a freight network for ordinary bulk trade. Caravans normally supplied their own animals and paid their own costs unless their members possessed official privilege. Bulk grain, military supplies, and tribute might travel along the same corridors without being relayed station-to-station like an urgent dispatch.

### 4.3 Distance and spacing

Sources give no universal spacing.

- Ögedei’s ideal system is usually reconstructed around stages suited to fresh horses and local water.
- Marco Polo gives **25–30 miles** between major Yuan horse posts, with **35–40 miles** in sparsely settled districts.
- Ghazan’s Ilkhanid reform prescribed stations **three farsakhs** apart. Because a *farsakh* varied, conversion to a precise modern distance is unsafe; approximately 15–20 kilometers in this context is often proposed.
- Yuan urgent-delivery posts were reportedly placed at intervals of **10, 15, or 25 li**, depending on location and regulation. Yuan *li* were not identical to modern kilometers.

Distances therefore depended on terrain, road category, settlement, animal capacity, and administrative period.

### 4.4 Speed

Reliable speed must be separated into traveler speed, single-courier speed, and message-relay speed.

- **Carpini’s mission:** left Kiev in early February 1246, reached Batu around April, and arrived near Güyük’s court on 22 July. This is extraordinary long-distance official travel but not proof that routine letters crossed Eurasia in the same time.
- **Rubruck:** his 1253–55 journey shows rapid compelled riding on exhausted mounts at some stages, but also delays at courts and political bottlenecks.
- **Ghazan’s ordinance:** a courier was supposed to cover **30 farsakhs in 24 hours**, or twice that distance through relay handoff. A report from Khurasan theoretically could reach Tabriz in four days. The report of Ghazan’s own death took about **twelve days** to reach Khurasan, demonstrating the difference between norm and performance.
- **Yuan urgent-delivery service:** later histories report an ideal of **400 li per day** for urgent documents. This is an administrative target, not a demonstrated annual average.
- Claims of **200–300 kilometers every day across the whole empire**, or of any message crossing “the empire in two weeks,” are **unverified generalizations** unless tied to a specific route and relay class.

### 4.5 Frequency, cost, and risk

No empire-wide timetable or annual letter count survives. Traffic fluctuated with campaigns, censuses, court migrations, tax seasons, diplomatic missions, and emergencies.

Direct monetary postage was generally not paid at each station by an authorized imperial messenger. The cost was displaced onto:

- station households;
- provincial tax assignments;
- state granaries or treasuries;
- compulsory labor;
- earmarked lands;
- animal levies;
- special loans or relief funds.

Principal risks included:

- horse mortality and exhaustion;
- desert water shortages;
- severe cold;
- flood, snow, and river hazards;
- warfare between khanates;
- banditry and impersonation;
- corrupt credential use;
- detours to feed private entourages;
- station-household flight;
- disease;
- translation failures;
- inability to locate a moving court.

## 5. Agents and Operators

### 5.1 Sovereigns and reformers

**Chinggis Khan:** created the territorial and command framework; probably employed organized post horses, but the fully standardized Yam cannot securely be attributed to a single 1206 enactment.

**Ögedei:** the principal institutional founder in the surviving Mongol narrative. His reform provided dedicated stations, horses, keepers, provisions, and inter-*ulus* coordination.

**Möngke, r. 1251–59:** attempted fiscal and administrative recentralization. Sources associate his reign with restrictions on misuse of credentials and use of imperial resources by merchants and princes.

**Kublai, r. 1260–94:** integrated steppe and Chinese station traditions, expanded coverage into conquered southern China and frontier regions, and established specialized central administration. In 1270 the Yuan created the **Office of the Controller-General of All Postal Stations** (諸站都統領使司); in 1276 it became the **Bureau/Commission of General Communication** (通政院). Administrative jurisdiction later oscillated between this organ and the Ministry of War.

**Ghazan, r. 1295–1304:** undertook the best-described western reform. He restricted credentials, prohibited princes and officials from freely creating entitled envoys, regularized stations, assigned responsibility to senior amirs, and shifted authorized expenses toward central budgeting.

### 5.2 Administrators

Yuan offices and roles included:

- central Secretariat and Ministry of War personnel;
- the Bureau of General Communication;
- provincial governments;
- station superintendents and station chiefs;
- **驛令** (*yiling*), station magistrates or directors;
- **提領** (*tiling*), supervisors;
- **脫脫禾孫** (*toqu’un/tuotuo hesun* in varied reconstructions), inspectors at junctions or checkpoints who examined credentials and traffic;
- clerks responsible for documents, seals, receipts, and allocations;
- local officials charged with filling vacancies among station households.

Western terminology is less uniform. Ilkhanid stations were placed under senior amirs, while couriers and envoys appear as *peyks*, *īlčīs*, or related terms depending on source and function.

### 5.3 Laboring operators

- riders and relay couriers;
- runners in the urgent-document service;
- grooms;
- horse, ox, mule, dog, and sheep keepers;
- cart drivers;
- boatmen;
- ferrymen;
- guides;
- cooks and lodging attendants;
- fodder and grain handlers;
- repair workers;
- translators and escorts.

Most remain unnamed in narrative sources. The documentary visibility of khans and foreign travelers therefore creates a misleading top-heavy picture.

### 5.4 Station households

The Yuan registered **站戶** (*zhanhu*, station households) separately for relay service. Their obligations could include:

- maintaining assigned animals;
- replacing dead animals;
- providing drivers or grooms;
- supplying carts, boats, harness, and equipment;
- feeding authorized travelers and their attendants;
- supplying lamp oil, firewood, fodder, drink, and lodging.

Some station households received tax or corvée exemptions and sometimes relief. One rule exempted up to four *qing* of their land from certain grain taxes. Such privileges did not necessarily offset costs.

A cited 1282 regulation assigned one proper post horse among groups of two, three, or five households and one cart among thirteen households. This should be read as a particular Yuan allocation rule, not an eternal Mongol-wide ratio.

Administrative records document impoverishment, flight, and forced replenishment of the station registers. One *Yuan dianzhang* passage describes famine and station-household exhaustion so severe that people pawned or sold relatives into others’ service. This is evidence of coercive extraction, debt, and family separation within the system—not a rhetorical flourish that can safely be ignored.

### 5.5 Users

Authorized users included:

- khans and royal households;
- princes and princesses;
- military commanders;
- official envoys;
- provincial administrators;
- tax and census agents;
- translators;
- foreign ambassadors;
- selected clergy;
- privileged *ortoq* merchants.

Marco Polo’s use is probable if the central claims of his Yuan service are accepted, but individual journeys attributed to him remain debated. Carpini and Rubruck are securely documented external travelers who received Mongol escorts and transport, though Rubruck did not always possess the status or support he desired.

### 5.6 Abusers, parasites, and enemies

Documented abuse came from:

- princes issuing or using unauthorized orders;
- officials inflating entourages;
- merchants presenting themselves as official envoys;
- forged or improperly issued tablets;
- station officials supplying horses without valid authorization;
- officials refusing service despite valid documents;
- couriers taking side roads to impose fresh requisitions;
- robbers impersonating official messengers;
- escorts demanding excess food, drink, animals, or gifts.

Rashid al-Din reports that in pre-reform Ilkhanid Iran false or abusive couriers plundered communities and sometimes hanged people. Ghazan’s measures directly targeted these practices.

## 6. Operation and Mechanism

### 6.1 Authorization

A traveler did not gain access merely by claiming imperial business. Access normally required one or more of:

- a written decree or sealed order;
- a **鋪馬聖旨**, an imperial authorization for post horses;
- a tablet of authority, conventionally called **paiza**, **gerege**, or Chinese **牌子/牌符**;
- a gold-lettered or silver-lettered round tablet for graded urgency;
- accompanying officers or interpreters who could validate the holder.

The *Yuan shi* ranks gold-lettered round credentials above silver ones in military urgency. Surviving paizas confirm that such tablets were physical objects, not literary inventions. A Yuan iron tablet in the Metropolitan Museum of Art bears a silver-inlaid inscription in ’Phags-pa script.

Credentials were symbols of delegated sovereign power. Their inscriptions warned subjects to obey and threatened offenders, but exact wording and privilege varied by issue. A paiza was not a modern passport guaranteeing border entry everywhere; it was an entitlement instrument whose validity depended on the issuer, script, seal, political relationship, and station personnel.

### 6.2 At a station

A typical official journey probably proceeded as follows:

1. The traveler or escort presented the order or tablet.
2. A station official checked entitlement, party size, urgency, and route.
3. The station registered or acknowledged the transfer.
4. Fresh mounts, guide, cart, boat, or other transport were assigned.
5. The official party received its authorized ration and, if stopping, lodging.
6. Spent animals remained for recovery; the party departed with station assets.
7. At the next stage, the process repeated.
8. Receipts or records helped prevent permanent loss of animals and identify the responsible station.

Practice diverged from rule. Language differences made interpreters important. Some station officials could recognize the authority of a tablet without reading its inscription. Excess entourages, side trips, or privately carried merchandise created disputes over entitlement.

### 6.3 Two delivery modes

The evidence supports at least two mechanisms:

- **Through-rider:** an envoy or courier retained the document and changed horses at each station.
- **True relay:** the dispatch was handed to a fresh runner or rider, enabling continuous day-and-night movement.

Marco Polo describes foot runners stationed at short intervals, wearing belts with bells so the next runner would be ready. A clerk prepared a receipt at transfer. The outline is consistent with the Yuan **urgent-delivery stations**, though his precise “three-mile” interval and universal description should not be generalized without Chinese corroboration.

### 6.4 Worked case: the papal mission of John of Pian del Carpine, 1245–47

This is the best documented end-to-end use of Mongol-supported movement across the western and central empire.

**Origin and payload:** Pope Innocent IV dispatched Carpini from Lyon in April 1245. He carried papal letters to the Mongol ruler, including *Cum non solum*. The mission sought information and demanded an end to Mongol attacks while urging conversion. The letters therefore carried diplomacy, religion, intelligence-gathering, and an implicit political warning.

**Approach to the network:** Carpini crossed central Europe and reached Kiev. On 4 February 1246 he departed Kiev using horses and an escort obtained through Mongol-controlled authorities. Gifts and negotiation were necessary to secure transport.

**Lower Dnieper and steppe:** His party moved through devastated territories and successively encountered Mongol officials. Progress depended on guides, escorts, and replacement animals rather than an independently navigated commercial itinerary.

**Batu’s court:** Around early April he reached Batu on or near the lower Volga. Batu functioned as a political checkpoint: foreign correspondence to the Great Khan could not simply pass without examination and forwarding.

**Across the Jochid and Central Asian zones:** The mission crossed the Ural/Yaik, steppe regions north of the Caspian and Aral basins, and territories associated with the Qangli, Muslim Central Asia, the former Qara Khitai realm, and the Dzungarian approaches. The travelers rode intensely and reported physical hardship.

**Imperial court:** They reached Güyük’s encampment near Karakorum on 22 July 1246 and witnessed his enthronement. The endpoint was a mobile imperial assembly rather than a permanent postal headquarters.

**Reply:** Güyük’s court generated a response in Persian, carrying the khan’s seal. It rejected papal assumptions and demanded submission. Thus the return “payload” was a document produced by a multilingual imperial chancery, politically opposite in meaning to the letter delivered.

**Return:** The mission departed in November 1246, retraced much of the route in winter, reached Batu again in May 1247, and ultimately returned to papal territory.

**What the case demonstrates:**

- journeys crossed several princely jurisdictions;
- high-level authorization and escorts mattered more than a single universal ticket;
- the system moved a human envoy and baggage, not merely a letter;
- political examination at intermediate courts was part of transmission;
- a mobile court could be the terminal node;
- logistical speed coexisted with long diplomatic delays;
- relay infrastructure did not eliminate hardship;
- messages could be translated, resealed, and politically transformed at the destination.

**Limit:** Carpini does not provide a complete list of formal station names or accounts. The case proves supported official transit, not that every overnight stop was a standardized permanent Yam station.

### 6.5 Security and policing

Security rested on:

- severe penalties for disobedience;
- documentary credentials and seals;
- inspectors at junctions;
- escort and guide chains;
- registration of station households and animals;
- restrictions on private use;
- investigation of missing resources;
- punishment both for unauthorized supply and for refusing legitimate service.

These mechanisms also enabled surveillance. Provincial information and intelligence moved toward the court, while inspectors monitored travelers. Calling this “espionage” is appropriate where covert intelligence was involved, but not every courier or traveler was a spy.

### 6.6 Finances

No consolidated imperial Yam budget survives.

**Early and steppe model:** designated households and decimal military-administrative units furnished animals and provisions.

**Yuan model:** registered station households bore much routine expense, supplemented by exemptions, grain stores, relief, provincial assignments, and occasional state funds. Northwestern stations could receive earmarked loan capital. Service therefore combined taxation in kind, corvée, household capital, and treasury intervention.

**Ilkhanid model before Ghazan:** local officials raised Yam expenses irregularly, creating arbitrary assessments.

**Ghazan’s reform:** central funds were supposed to cover official postal expenses. Restricting credential issuance was financially essential: the state could not budget a service whose users and entourages multiplied without authorization.

The recurring cycle was:

1. political demand expanded;
2. credential holders multiplied;
3. local obligations exceeded station resources;
4. animals died and households fled or borrowed;
5. officials reassigned households or issued relief;
6. rulers announced tighter controls;
7. elite evasion gradually returned.

## 7. Change over the Lifespan

### Phase I: Conquest communications, c. 1206–27

Mounted messaging, requisitioned remounts, guides, and inherited north-Chinese posts operated under Chinggis Khan. The evidence establishes organized communications but not a uniform Eurasian grid.

The 1218 Otrar incident illustrates the blurred category of merchant-envoy. A caravan and envoys sent by Chinggis Khan were accused of espionage by the Khwarazmian governor. The merchants were killed and goods seized, contributing to the Mongol invasion. Whether individual merchants were spies remains **unresolved**; the episode demonstrates that commerce, diplomacy, and intelligence were inseparable in frontier perceptions.

### Phase II: Ögedei’s standardization, c. 1229–41

This was the foundational administrative phase:

- fixed stages were prescribed;
- assigned horses and personnel replaced indiscriminate requisition in theory;
- station supplies were specified;
- links among the Great Khan’s, Chagatai’s, and Batu’s territories were coordinated;
- Karakorum became the central imperial hub.

The date 1234 is widely repeated but should be identified as a traditional dating of reform, not the first appearance of every Yam practice.

### Phase III: Empire-wide operation, 1240s–1259

Carpini, Rubruck, Juvaini, and early administrative records show the network in active use from Rus’ and the lower Volga to Mongolia and north China.

Möngke’s reign brought broader fiscal survey and efforts to suppress private exploitation. The network enabled universal-imperial claims, but its operation still depended on regional princes and mobile courts.

### Phase IV: Political fracture and regionalization, 1260s–1294

Möngke’s death in 1259 produced the Toluid civil war between Kublai and Ariq Böke. Other conflicts included:

- Berke–Hülegü war;
- Kublai–Kaidu conflict;
- rivalries within the Chagataid realm;
- intermittent Jochid–Ilkhanid hostility.

The Yam ceased to be reliably unified across the entire Chinggisid world. Regional systems nonetheless expanded.

In Yuan territory:

- 1270: creation of the central postal-station controller’s office;
- 1276: reorganization as the Bureau of General Communication;
- post routes were extended through conquered Southern Song lands;
- Tibet and Yunnan were incorporated through specialized regional transport.

### Phase V: Peak elaboration and corrective reform, c. 1290s–1320s

Marco Polo’s description reflects the perceived magnificence of late Kublai-era stations. His figures vary among manuscripts:

- more than **10,000** station buildings or establishments;
- **200,000** horses in some versions;
- **300,000** in others;
- as many as **400 horses** at major stations.

These figures are **textually documented claims but statistically unreliable**. Ten thousand stations each holding 400 horses would require four million horses, exposing internal inconsistency. The most useful conclusion is qualitative: observers saw the Yuan relay establishment as exceptionally large and well supplied along principal roads.

In Iran, Ghazan’s reforms after 1295:

- reduced entitled envoys;
- reserved credential issuance to the khan;
- prescribed stations at three-farsakh intervals;
- required fifteen sound horses per station;
- assigned two special couriers;
- placed responsibility on senior amirs;
- regularized central payment;
- distinguished urgent sealed reports.

The reforms resemble Yuan practice. Kim Hodong argues that they were modeled more on the Qa’an’s Chinggisid system than on a simple revival of the Islamic *barīd*.

### Phase VI: Strain, contraction, and divergent survival, c. 1320s–1400

In Yuan China, sources repeatedly complain of:

- station impoverishment;
- runaway households;
- excessive clerical and religious traffic;
- unauthorized credentials;
- corrupt requisitions;
- institutional transfers caused by weak supervision.

Yuan critics particularly complained about the number of Tibetan Buddhist clergy traveling to Dadu and using post horses. The existence of these complaints is **documented**; claims that Tibetan monks alone “caused the system to collapse” are polemical exaggeration. Royal, military, bureaucratic, mercantile, and fiscal overuse was broader.

In Iran, the Ilkhanate disintegrated after Abu Sa‘id’s death in 1335. Ghazan’s centralized system consequently lost its single treasury and sovereign authority, but relay and *ulāq* obligations continued under successor dynasties.

In Central Asia, Chagataid fragmentation and warfare made routes unstable. Timur later employed courier and relay institutions derived from the same Inner Asian administrative repertoire.

In the Jochid world, the Yam survived the fourteenth century and influenced Muscovite posting and transport obligations. It cannot be treated as extinct in 1368.

## 8. End or Transformation

### 8.1 Why the unified system ended

**Proximate political causes:**

- Möngke’s death in 1259;
- civil war between Kublai and Ariq Böke;
- wars among Chinggisid houses;
- independent fiscal and administrative centers;
- collapse of the Ilkhanate in 1335;
- Yuan expulsion from Dadu in 1368;
- later fourteenth-century conflict in the Golden Horde and Chagataid lands.

**Structural causes:**

- high maintenance costs;
- vulnerability of animal-based transport to pasture failure, famine, and epidemic;
- elite overuse of credentials;
- hereditary burdens causing household insolvency and flight;
- difficulty policing thousands of local transactions;
- mismatch between mobile courts and fixed routes;
- political fragmentation of what had been an inter-*ulus* entitlement;
- dependence on coercive requisition.

### 8.2 Transformation by region

**Ming China:** The Ming inherited roads, stations, couriers, household obligations, and much administrative knowledge from Yuan and earlier Chinese systems. It reorganized these within Ming institutions. This was both succession and ideological repudiation, not simple abolition.

**Mongolia:** *Örtöö* and *ulaa* transport obligations survived for centuries in changing forms under post-imperial Mongol polities and Qing rule.

**Iran:** Ilkhanid Yam practices continued into Timurid and later Iranian courier systems; *chapar* became the better-known later term.

**Rus’ and Muscovy:** *Yam*, *yamskaya gonba*, and *yamshchik* became embedded in Russian posting. The degree of direct borrowing is debated because Rus’ had earlier routes and obligations, but Mongol-Turkic terminology and Jochid administrative influence are difficult to deny.

**Central Asia:** Chagataid and Timurid polities maintained relays and requisitioned transport without an unbroken central archive demonstrating exact institutional continuity at every stage.

**Golden Horde successor states:** posting, envoy, tribute, and *ulāq* practices continued through successor khanates.

### 8.3 Assets, personnel, and records

Stations’ animals and supplies were consumable rather than permanent capital. On regime change:

- animals could be seized, reassigned, sold, or lost;
- service households remained taxable under a new category;
- routes and ferries were reused;
- station buildings became inns, local offices, settlements, or ruins;
- clerks and transport specialists could enter successor administrations;
- tablets lost validity when their issuing sovereign’s authority disappeared.

Much documentation was lost because:

- the imperial chancery moved with courts;
- paper records decayed;
- wars destroyed administrative centers;
- regional archives were dispersed;
- the surviving *Yuan shi* compressed earlier documents;
- Persian chronicles preserve reform rhetoric rather than daily station accounts;
- Jochid and Chagataid internal archives survive poorly.

## 9. Contested and Legendary Aspects

### 9.1 “Genghis Khan invented the world’s first postal service”

**Legendary simplification.** Earlier Chinese, Persian, Roman, Byzantine, Islamic, and steppe states operated relay communications. Chinggisid innovation lay in the scale, cross-cultural synthesis, and integration of an enormous conquered territory—not invention of posting itself.

### 9.2 “The Yam was founded in exactly 1234”

**Disputed.** This is a convenient date for Ögedei’s standardization. Evidence for functioning post horses before 1234 means it cannot safely be called the absolute beginning.

### 9.3 “A message crossed the entire empire in two weeks”

**Unverified as a general claim.** Very high speeds were possible for short urgent relays. No securely documented dispatch is shown crossing the maximum thirteenth-century imperial span on a fixed fourteen-day schedule.

### 9.4 “Every station had 400 horses”

**False as a universal statement.** Marco Polo associates hundreds of horses with major stations, but local norms varied greatly. Ögedei’s ideal prescription speaks of twenty horses; Ghazan prescribed fifteen; Yuan provincial stations differed with traffic and terrain.

### 9.5 “There were exactly 10,000 stations and 200,000 or 300,000 horses”

**Documented as Marco Polo manuscript claims; unreliable as audited totals.** Versions disagree, and the figures are internally inconsistent if 400 horses are assigned to every one of 10,000 stations. The *Yuan shi*’s approximately 1,400 recorded stations in the Yuan provinces belongs to a different scope and accounting framework.

### 9.6 “The Yam made the Silk Road safe”

**Romanticized.** Mongol political integration reduced some barriers and protected authorized movement during certain periods. Yet travelers still faced war, official extortion, harsh terrain, robbery, and political detention. The “Silk Road” itself is a nineteenth-century collective label, not the formal name of a single Mongol highway.

### 9.7 “A maiden carrying gold could cross the empire unharmed”

Variants of this peace-and-security motif occur in imperial praise traditions across cultures. It conveys a claim about sovereign order rather than a verifiable crime statistic. Evidence of robber impersonation, official violence, and station abuse prevents literal acceptance.

### 9.8 “Marco Polo administered or personally inspected the whole network”

**Unverified.** Marco Polo’s book gives a famous description and attributes official journeys to him. The degree and title of his Yuan service are debated; no Yuan record securely appoints him director of the Yam. His account is evidence for how the system was represented and probably observed, not proof of his command over it.

### 9.9 “Paizas were universal passports”

**Misleading.** They were graded authority tablets. Some conferred transport, provisioning, or protection privileges; others signified office or command. Their effect depended on issuer, inscription, seal, jurisdiction, and political conditions.

### 9.10 “The Yam spread the Black Death from China to Europe”

**Unresolved and overly broad.** Palaeogenetic evidence places the fourteenth-century pandemic’s major diversification near the Tian Shan in the late 1330s. Commercial, military, and transport routes through Chagataid and Jochid lands probably participated in westward movement, but no evidence identifies the official Yam as the specific carrier. Monica Green and other scholars emphasize Mongol-era Eurasian connectivity; newer reconstructions also stress political disruption, local ecology, rodents, caravans, and maritime transmission. “The Yam caused the Black Death” is not established.

### 9.11 “Mongols catapulted plague corpses into Caffa and began Europe’s epidemic”

Gabriele de’ Mussi’s account describes corpse-hurling during the 1346 siege. Some scholars consider biological transmission through that event plausible; others note that de’ Mussi was not an eyewitness at Caffa and that plague could have entered by infected rodents, fleas, steppe routes, or multiple maritime paths. The Yam is not directly implicated.

### 9.12 “The system remained unchanged until the twentieth century”

**False if literal.** Mongolian, Russian, Central Asian, and Iranian successor posts preserved names and operational principles, but their legal authorities, station spacing, funding, technology, and political geography repeatedly changed.

### 9.13 National ownership narratives

Modern Mongolian narratives understandably emphasize an indigenous imperial achievement; Chinese narratives often emphasize descent from earlier Chinese **驛傳**; Russian discussions debate Tatar-Mongol borrowing; Iranian narratives compare Yam with the *barīd* and later *chapar*. Each contains part of the institutional history. Exclusive national invention claims are unsupported because the system was an imperial synthesis operated by many subject populations.

## 10. Historiography and Representations

### 10.1 Primary-source base

**The *Secret History of the Mongols***  
Section 279 is foundational for Ögedei’s reform. It is close to the imperial tradition but survives through a later Chinese transcription and glossing tradition. Its date, layers, and precise wording remain debated.

**Ata-Malik Juvaini, *Tārīkh-i Jahāngushāy***  
A Persian administrator writing in the 1250s, later governor of Baghdad. He supplies an elite insider’s account of Chinggisid governance. His history praises patrons and is not an administrative register.

**Rashid al-Din, *Jāmiʿ al-Tawārīkh* and the history of Ghazan**  
Essential for Ilkhanid abuses and Ghazan’s reform. Rashid served the regime and presented reform as royal restoration of order; his detailed provisions are invaluable but partly normative.

**The *Yuan shi***  
Compiled under the Ming from Yuan documents. Chapters on stations preserve administrative terminology, decrees, institutional changes, and provincial totals. Rapid compilation, chronological layering, and lost source texts limit precision.

**The *Yuan dianzhang***  
A collection of Yuan statutes and administrative precedents. Particularly valuable for disputes, household impoverishment, credential abuse, and corrective orders.

**Marco Polo**  
Indispensable for station imagery, relays, accommodation, and outsider perception. Manuscript variation—especially 200,000 versus 300,000 horses—requires explicit caution.

**John of Pian del Carpine and William of Rubruck**  
Provide dated itineraries and operational observations. Both were Franciscan observers with religious and diplomatic purposes. Their hardships correct idealized institutional descriptions.

**Chinese travelers including Changchun/Qiu Chuji and Yelü Chucai**  
Important for evidence that relay travel preceded Ögedei’s formal reform.

**Paizas and seals**  
Material evidence confirms credentials in Mongolian and imperial scripts. Context is sometimes lost through antiquarian collecting, so provenance varies.

### 10.2 Major modern debates

**Formation:** David Morgan and Adam Silverstein favor pre-1234 operation followed by Ögedei’s regularization. Didier Gazagnadou gives greater weight to a 1218 formation context. Márton Vér synthesizes Chinese, Persian, and travel evidence for an existing Chinggis-era system.

**Institutional diffusion:** Was Yam fundamentally Chinese, steppe, or Islamic? Current comparative scholarship generally treats it as hybrid. Claims of one-way copying are difficult to sustain.

**Unity after 1260:** Older narratives speak of one Yam under a continuing “Pax Mongolica.” More recent work stresses separate Yuan, Chagataid, Ilkhanid, and Jochid systems, with intermittent diplomatic and commercial interoperability.

**Commerce:** Popular accounts make the Yam a public service for all merchants. Documentary work shows selective privilege, especially for *ortoq* partners, alongside repeated attempts to exclude unauthorized merchants.

**Ghazan’s reform:** Kim Hodong argues convincingly that it resembles the Qa’an/Yuan system and should not be reduced to restoration of the Islamic *barīd*.

**Quantification:** Marco Polo’s spectacular numbers remain widely repeated despite contradictions. Yuan official totals are firmer but geographically incomplete.

### 10.3 Archives and languages

Relevant materials survive in:

- Classical/Middle Mongolian, often through Chinese transcription;
- literary Chinese;
- Persian;
- Arabic;
- Latin;
- Old French and Franco-Italian manuscript traditions;
- Armenian;
- Georgian;
- Syriac;
- Tibetan;
- Turkic languages;
- Rus’ chronicles and later Russian administrative vocabulary.

Important holdings and research traditions are distributed among archives, libraries, museums, and archaeological institutes in China, Mongolia, Iran, Turkey, Russia, Central Asia, Europe, Korea, Japan, and the United States.

### 10.4 Archaeology

Archaeology has most effectively clarified:

- Karakorum’s built environment and imported supplies;
- the relationship between political hubs and road networks;
- frontier settlements in the Gobi and Hexi Corridor;
- bridge, road, caravanserai, and urban landscapes;
- the materiality of paizas.

It has not produced a complete excavated chain of stations from Dadu to Sarai. Archaeologists warn against labeling every roadside ruin a postal station without inscriptions, datable material, or documentary correlation.

### 10.5 Popular representations

The Yam appears indirectly or explicitly in:

- Marco Polo documentaries and dramatizations;
- Genghis Khan biographies;
- “Silk Road” museum exhibitions;
- Mongol Empire strategy games;
- historical maps presenting the empire as a communications web;
- modern Mongolian postal commemorations;
- travel literature comparing it with the Pony Express;
- online animations claiming 200-mile daily speeds.

Typical divergences from evidence include:

- depicting a single continuously open highway;
- making the service available to ordinary private correspondents;
- attributing invention entirely to Chinggis Khan;
- giving every station identical architecture;
- presenting paizas as modern passports;
- ignoring boats, carts, runners, service households, and coerced provisioning;
- treating the four khanates as one centrally administered state after 1260;
- omitting fraud, local impoverishment, and political checkpoints.

No major surviving medieval epic is devoted exclusively to the Yam. Its modern fame comes largely from brief passages in the *Secret History* and Marco Polo expanded into a symbol of “Pax Mongolica.”

## 12. Sources and Assessment

### 12.1 Principal documentary findings

1. **Ögedei’s reform:** securely present in *The Secret History of the Mongols*, but exact dating and whether it was creation or reorganization are disputed.
2. **Pre-Ögedei operation:** supported by Changchun and Yelü Chucai; therefore “founded from nothing in 1234” is untenable.
3. **Operational reality:** Carpini and Rubruck demonstrate guided, horse-changing, politically supervised travel, with severe physical hardship.
4. **Yuan scale:** approximately 1,400 stations is a legitimate official-history figure for listed jurisdictions, not a complete empire-wide census.
5. **Marco Polo totals:** authentic textual testimony but inconsistent and unsuitable as exact statistics.
6. **Coerced finance:** Yuan statutes document household obligation, impoverishment, flight, and family pawning or sale.
7. **Credential abuse:** documented in Chinese and Persian sources; repeated prohibitions prove both regulation and persistent evasion.
8. **Ilkhanid reform:** Rashid al-Din provides detailed regulations; Kim Hodong’s comparison makes Yuan/Chinggisid influence probable.
9. **Post-1260 unity:** institutional resemblance continued, but unified central governance did not.
10. **End:** no single extinction date exists; 1335 and 1368 mark regional political transformations.
11. **Commerce and religion:** privileged merchants and clergy used the stations, but neither group enjoyed unrestricted universal access.
12. **Plague:** Eurasian connectivity is relevant, but specific transmission by official Yam relays is unproven.
