# Transatlantic telegraph cable: Network Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier treats the transatlantic telegraph not as one artifact but as a changing communications system: submarine cables, shore ends, landing stations, terrestrial feeder lines, relay offices, instruments, cable ships, manufacturing works, corporate agreements, government concessions, operators, customers, and legal rules connecting Europe and North America.

The bounded period is approximately 1850–1900. The opening date includes the immediately necessary predecessor technology—the successful armored Channel cable of 1851 and Frederic Newton Gisborne’s Newfoundland telegraph—and the closing date marks transformation from an experimental Anglo-American route into a redundant, multinational Atlantic network. It did not “end” in 1900: telegraph cables continued into the twentieth century, were joined and partially displaced by radiotelegraphy after 1901, and were eventually superseded by transatlantic telephone and digital systems.

Evidence is labeled as follows:

- **Documented:** supported by contemporary records, official documents, physical remains, or mutually corroborating sources.
- **Reported contemporary figure:** a number published at the time, not necessarily independently audited.
- **Scholarly inference:** a later interpretation derived from documented evidence.
- **Disputed:** sources materially disagree.
- **Allegation:** asserted by an interested party without decisive proof.
- **Legend/romantic tradition:** later narrative not adequately supported by contemporary evidence.
- **Unresolved:** the surviving evidence does not establish a conclusion.

Distances in nineteenth-century accounts may mean statute miles, nautical miles, or “knots” of cable. Figures are retained in their source’s units where conversion would imply false precision.

## 1. Identification and Definition

### 1.1 What the network was

The transatlantic telegraph-cable network was a privately financed but state-assisted system for transmitting coded electrical impulses between European and North American telegraph networks through insulated copper conductors laid on or buried beneath the North Atlantic seabed.

It transported information rather than physical goods or people:

- private and commercial telegrams;
- commodity and securities prices;
- bank and credit instructions;
- insurance and shipping intelligence;
- newspaper dispatches;
- diplomatic and military orders;
- government messages;
- personal notices;
- meteorological and maritime reports;
- coded or cipher traffic.

Money and credit did not physically cross the cable. Orders capable of transferring ownership, releasing funds, accepting bills, buying securities, or directing shipments did. The distinction matters: the cable was an information infrastructure whose messages altered the disposition of material assets elsewhere.

The system’s principal components were:

1. submarine deep-sea cables;
2. heavily armored shore ends;
3. cable huts and landing stations;
4. terrestrial and short submarine feeder lines;
5. urban telegraph offices;
6. transmitting, receiving, testing, and recording instruments;
7. cable factories and gutta-percha supply chains;
8. cable-laying and repair ships;
9. private corporations and traffic-sharing agreements;
10. national and colonial landing concessions;
11. international cable-protection law.

### 1.2 Names

No single official name governed the whole network.

Contemporary English expressions included:

- **Atlantic Telegraph**
- **Atlantic cable**
- **Atlantic telegraph cable**
- **Transatlantic telegraph**
- **Trans-Atlantic Telegraph**
- **ocean telegraph**
- **submarine telegraph**
- **Anglo-American cable**
- **ocean cable**

Corporate names often functioned as practical network names:

- **New York, Newfoundland and London Telegraph Company**
- **Atlantic Telegraph Company**
- **Anglo-American Telegraph Company**
- **Direct United States Cable Company**
- **Commercial Cable Company**
- **French Atlantic Telegraph Company**
- **German Atlantic Telegraph Company**

French usage included:

- **câble télégraphique transatlantique**
- **câble transatlantique**
- **télégraphe transatlantique**
- **télégraphe sous-marin**
- **Société du Câble Transatlantique Français**
- **Compagnie française du télégraphe de Paris à New-York**

German usage included:

- **transatlantisches Telegraphenkabel**
- **Atlantikkabel**
- **unterseeisches Telegraphenkabel**
- **Deutsche Atlantische Telegraphengesellschaft**

Dutch legal terminology in the 1884 cable-protection convention was **onderzeese telegraafkabels**. The authentic diplomatic text was French: *Convention pour la protection des câbles sous-marins*. The Dutch government’s treaty database preserves both French and Dutch texts.

“First transatlantic cable” is ambiguous:

- The **1858 cable** was the first completed electrical cable across the Atlantic and briefly carried messages.
- The **1866 cable** was the first durable commercial connection.
- The recovered and completed **1865 cable**, opened in September 1866, gave the system its first redundancy.

Accordingly, describing 1866 simply as “the first cable” is inaccurate unless “first permanent” or “first commercially durable” is specified.

### 1.3 Boundaries: what counts as part of the network

Included here are the direct North Atlantic cables and indispensable feeders joining them to urban networks: Ireland–Newfoundland; France–Newfoundland or Massachusetts; Ireland–Nova Scotia–United States; and, at the period’s end, Germany–Azores–United States.

Also included are:

- Newfoundland’s overland line;
- the Cape Ray–Aspy Bay/Cabot Strait connection;
- terrestrial connections to St John’s, Halifax, Boston, New York, London, Paris, and Washington;
- cable stations and company urban offices;
- manufacturing and repair infrastructure.

Not every nineteenth-century Atlantic cable belongs to this narrower system. Caribbean, South American, Mediterranean, African, and imperial “All Red Line” routes intersected with it but are treated as adjacent systems unless they carried North Atlantic traffic or affected its governance.

## 2. Formation

### 2.1 Systems preceding it

Before electrical connection, letters, price lists, diplomatic dispatches, and newspapers crossed the Atlantic by sailing vessel and steamship. By the 1840s Cunard steamers offered relatively regular passages, but news still required roughly ten days under favorable conditions and longer under adverse weather. Telegraph networks had already compressed communication within Britain, continental Europe, Canada, and the United States. The Atlantic remained the principal discontinuity between them.

Submarine telegraphy became credible through short crossings. An unarmored Dover–Cape Gris-Nez conductor laid in 1850 failed within hours. A protected cable laid in 1851, using an insulated core surrounded by hemp and iron wires, established the structural pattern subsequently used for deep-sea cables. A contemporary technical retrospective in *Nature* identified the armored 1851 cable as the practical type from which later cables developed.

### 2.2 Newfoundland as bridge and news-gathering platform

In 1851 the Newfoundland legislature incorporated Gisborne’s Newfoundland Electric Telegraph Company to build lines from St John’s toward Trepassey and Carbonear. St John’s–Carbonear operated by March 1852. Gisborne initially intended to intercept news from arriving vessels and transmit it ahead of the ships, not necessarily to span the entire Atlantic.

The project failed financially. In 1854 Gisborne approached Cyrus West Field, a New York paper merchant who had retired from active business. Field and associates expanded the scheme into a complete Atlantic crossing.

The commonly repeated story that Field instantly conceived the Atlantic cable after glancing at a globe is **romanticized biography**. Documentary evidence securely establishes Gisborne’s proposal, Field’s consultation with Samuel Morse and hydrographer Matthew Fontaine Maury, and the subsequent corporate organization; it cannot establish the precise dramatic instant in which Field formed the larger idea.

### 2.3 The “Cable Cabinet”

Field recruited a syndicate conventionally called the **Cable Cabinet**:

- Peter Cooper, industrialist and first company president;
- Moses Taylor, merchant and banker;
- Marshall Owen Roberts, shipowner and financier;
- Chandler White, merchant;
- David Dudley Field, lawyer and Cyrus Field’s brother;
- Cyrus W. Field, promoter and principal transatlantic negotiator.

Samuel F. B. Morse supplied scientific prestige and advice but was not the system’s sole inventor or engineer. The network was a collective product involving promoters, naval hydrographers, electricians, manufacturers, mariners, operators, and governments.

### 2.4 Newfoundland charter and monopoly

On 15 April 1854 Newfoundland enacted the charter of the **New York, Newfoundland and London Telegraph Company**. Section 14 granted the company, for fifty years, the exclusive right to construct and operate most telegraphs on Newfoundland and to land foreign telegraphic cables within colonial jurisdiction. The privilege was conditional on completing specified works. The company also received rights associated with land grants; a British parliamentary debate described the concession as including selection of fifty square miles.

The company’s capital was reported as $1.5 million. The Newfoundland government retained a pre-emption right exercisable after twenty years but declined to exercise it in 1874. Newfoundland newspapers later attacked the monopoly, high rates, and proposals to extend company control. These criticisms are documented political positions, not proof that every claimed abuse occurred.

### 2.5 Completing the Newfoundland route

A first attempt to lay the Gulf of St Lawrence cable in 1855 failed when the cable was lost from the barque *Sarah L. Bryant*. A successful cable crossed Cabot Strait in 1856 between Cape Ray, Newfoundland, and Aspy Bay, Cape Breton. Coupled with Newfoundland’s difficult overland line and existing North American telegraphs, it created a partial Europe-facing communications chain awaiting only the ocean segment.

Construction across Newfoundland involved clearing and maintaining lines through sparsely settled country and severe weather. Sources document financial hardship and physical difficulty; the evidence reviewed here does not establish a system of enslaved or formally forced labor. Local wage labor, ship crews, and company employees performed the documented work.

### 2.6 Atlantic Telegraph Company

Field traveled to Britain, where he allied with John Watkins Brett, Charles Tilston Bright, and John Pender. The **Atlantic Telegraph Company** was organized in London in October–November 1856. Sources differ between:

- **20 October 1856:** organizational meeting or effective formation;
- **6 November 1856:** issuance of the prospectus and commonly cited formal founding date.

The first prospectus advertised £350,000 nominal capital in 350 shares of £1,000. It invoked the Newfoundland landing monopoly and government support. A later company statement reported £462,860 raised in 1856–57, reflecting additional capital beyond the initial prospectus.

### 2.7 State assistance

The enterprise was private but was never independent of states.

The British government agreed, subject to conditions, to:

- assist with naval surveying;
- consider supplying ships for cable laying;
- give government traffic priority;
- pay a £14,000 annual subsidy while the cable worked, reducible to £10,000 once net profit supported a six-percent dividend, for a stated twenty-five-year period;
- pay additional sums if government messages at normal tariffs exceeded the subsidy.

The United States supplied USS *Niagara*; Britain supplied HMS *Agamemnon* and naval assistance. Government help reduced private capital requirements and conferred legitimacy. It also embedded priority access for state messages.

### 3.2 Manufacturing nodes

The network also depended upon industrial sites that maps of routes usually omit:

- **Glass, Elliot & Company**, Greenwich;
- **R. S. Newall & Company**, Birkenhead;
- the **Gutta Percha Company**;
- later the **Telegraph Construction and Maintenance Company** (“Telcon”);
- **Siemens Brothers**, including Woolwich works;
- iron-wire, hemp, copper, and cable-machinery suppliers.

Manufacturing involved drawing copper, twisting the conductor, applying multiple gutta-percha layers with Chatterton’s compound, bedding the insulated core in fibers, and winding iron armor around it. Contracts increasingly imposed electrical resistance and quality tests.

### 3.3 Mobile nodes: ships

Principal cable vessels included:

- USS *Niagara*;
- HMS *Agamemnon*;
- HMS *Leopard* and support vessels;
- *Great Eastern*;
- *Albany*, *Medway*, and *Terrible*;
- CS *Faraday*;
- CS *Minia*;
- CS *Mackay-Bennett*.

*Great Eastern* was critical because its capacity allowed it to carry an entire Atlantic cable. *Faraday*, purpose-built for Siemens, laid approximately 50,000 nautical miles during its long career according to the compiled vessel record. *Mackay-Bennett*, launched in 1884, was a roughly 2,000-gross-ton repair vessel with cable machinery, electric lighting, and a specialized electrical staff.

### 3.4 Nodes often omitted

Urban offices were operational rather than symbolic termini. Customers generally did not travel to Heart’s Content or Valentia. They handed messages to a local telegraph office or commercial bureau, which forwarded traffic through interconnected company lines.

News agencies and exchanges were therefore functional nodes:

- Reuters in London;
- Havas in Paris;
- Wolff in Berlin;
- Associated Press offices;
- London and New York stock exchanges;
- banks, commodity exchanges, insurers, and newspaper offices.

## 4. Connections

### 4.1 Foundational connection chain, 1856–58

A practical New York–London message used multiple edges:

1. New York telegraph office to northeastern U.S. and Canadian lines;
2. connections through Nova Scotia/Cape Breton;
3. Aspy Bay to Cape Ray by submarine cable;
4. Cape Ray across Newfoundland by landline;
5. Newfoundland cable station to Ireland across the Atlantic;
6. Valentia through Irish and British land networks to London.

Thus, “the Atlantic cable” was never operationally only the deep-sea segment.

### 4.2 Principal deep-sea connections

#### Valentia–Trinity Bay, 1858

Approximately 3,000 statute miles of cable were manufactured because laying required slack and because the 1858 expedition divided the cable between two vessels. The ocean route itself was much shorter. *Agamemnon* and *Niagara* met in mid-Atlantic, spliced their halves, and steamed in opposite directions.

Signals first passed across the completed cable in August 1858. Public traffic was extremely slow. The system ceased functioning in September or October 1858; the exact date depends upon whether “failure” means the last intelligible signal, abandonment of commercial operation, or formal cessation.

#### Valentia–Heart’s Content, 1866

*Great Eastern* left Valentia on 13 July and reached Heart’s Content on 27 July. Contemporary expedition material gave the point-to-point distance as 1,663 miles, while popular descriptions often round the route to 1,800 miles. The difference reflects measurement conventions and cable slack.

The 1866 line was durable. In August–September the expedition recovered the broken 1865 cable from deep water, spliced on new cable, and completed a second functioning Atlantic circuit. A celebratory painting fixes 8 September 1866 as the completion-and-testing date of the paired 1865/66 system.

#### French routes, 1869 and 1879–80

The Société du Câble Transatlantique Français opened a route from Brest through Saint-Pierre to Massachusetts in 1869. It was presented as an alternative less dependent on British landing territory, but corporate consolidation later brought the system into Anglo-American influence.

The Compagnie française du télégraphe de Paris à New-York—the “Pouyer-Quertier” or P-Q company—laid a renewed Brest–Saint-Pierre–Cape Cod system in 1879, with a Brest/Déolen–Porthcurno connection in 1880 to collect London traffic.

#### Direct United States route, 1874–75

Siemens and CS *Faraday* constructed a route connecting Rye Beach, New Hampshire; Tor Bay, Nova Scotia; and Ballinskelligs, Ireland. It was intended to compete with the Anglo-American combination. A price war followed. John Pender acquired influence, litigation ensued, and the Direct company ultimately entered the traffic pool.

#### Commercial Cable routes, 1884 onward

John William Mackay and James Gordon Bennett Jr. organized the Commercial Cable Company to challenge the pool. Its structure joined:

- Waterville, Ireland;
- Dover Bay/Canso, Nova Scotia;
- Rockport, Massachusetts;
- Duxbury and Far Rockaway/New York;
- Weston-super-Mare and Le Havre through feeder cables.

The company’s New York cable ran underground from Coney Island through Brooklyn and across the East River Bridge to Wall Street. This direct approach reduced dependence on third-party long-distance landlines.

#### German Atlantic connection, 1900

Felten & Guilleaume organized the Deutsche Atlantische Telegraphengesellschaft in 1899. The Germany–Azores section opened on 26 May 1900 and the complete Germany–Azores–New York connection on 28 August 1900. This is a suitable terminal marker for the dossier: by then the network had become multinational and multiply redundant.

### 4.3 Direction, frequency, speed, and cost

Traffic was bidirectional but not necessarily balanced. Europe-to-America news, political intelligence, and market information coexisted with American commodity, securities, shipping, and private traffic flowing eastward.

Documented performance changed radically:

- The 1858 cable initially operated at tens of dots per minute under difficult conditions.
- Victoria’s 99-word congratulatory message reportedly required about sixteen and a half hours to transmit and verify.
- The durable 1866 system was capable of several words per minute; figures near eight words per minute are often reported but depended on instruments, cable condition, coding, and operator practice.
- Duplex working later permitted simultaneous opposite-direction operation. Claims that ordinary terrestrial quadruplex performance immediately applied without qualification to every ocean cable should be treated cautiously.

Reported tariff development:

| Date | Reported Atlantic rate | Reliability |
|---|---:|---|
| 1866 | $5 per word | Widely reproduced from company/industry histories; exact endpoint and surcharges must be checked for individual messages |
| 1867 | $2.50 per word | Same limitation |
| 1869 | $1 per word | Same limitation |
| 1874 | London–Washington, 4s 3d per word | Documented in U.S. diplomatic correspondence; route-specific |
| 1875 | about $0.50 per word | Widely reproduced; route and exchange-rate qualifications apply |
| 1875–90 | Australasian-to-Europe traffic cited at 9s 4d per word | Not a pure North Atlantic rate; demonstrates combined-network pricing |
| 1890s | reductions toward 4s–4s 9d on long imperial routes | Not uniform across all Atlantic endpoints |

A U.S. diplomatic report in 1874 noted that cable companies allowed messages of fewer than ten words at a per-word rate, unlike Western Union’s domestic ten-word minimum. Two-to-five-word cablegrams were therefore common.

## 5. Agents and Operators

### 5.1 Promoters and financiers

**Cyrus W. Field (1819–1892)** coordinated American finance, British investment, government support, publicity, and repeated expeditions. His importance is documented, but “Field invented the cable” is a biographical simplification.

**Peter Cooper (1791–1883)** served as the American syndicate’s senior financier and first president.

**Moses Taylor (1806–1882)** contributed banking and mercantile capital.

**Marshall Owen Roberts (1814–1880)** supplied shipping and investment expertise.

**John Pender (1816–1896)** became the dominant British cable financier. Through holdings, mergers, acquisitions, and “joint purse” arrangements he exercised substantial influence over transatlantic rates. Critics called him the “Cable King.” The existence of his influence is documented; claims that he personally dictated every tariff are too strong.

**John William Mackay (1831–1902)** used mining wealth to finance the Commercial Cable challenge.

**James Gordon Bennett Jr. (1841–1918)**, proprietor of the *New York Herald*, had both a commercial interest in cheaper news transmission and a strategic interest in an independent system.

### 5.2 Engineers and scientists

**Charles Tilston Bright (1832–1888)** was engineer-in-chief of the Atlantic Telegraph Company and supervised early expeditions.

**William Thomson (1824–1907), later Lord Kelvin**, analyzed signal retardation in long cables, developed the mirror galvanometer, advised on paying-out forces, participated in expeditions, and later developed the siphon recorder. His role is securely documented.

**Edward Orange Wildman Whitehouse (1816–1890)**, the company’s electrician in 1858, favored high-voltage induction coils and disputed Thomson’s theoretical conclusions. Later narratives often make him the sole destroyer of the 1858 cable. The evidence supports a more qualified conclusion: excessive voltages probably worsened damaged or defective insulation, but poor manufacture, inadequate testing, handling damage, and design limitations were also causal. The 1861 committee did not reduce the failure to one individual.

**Wildman Whitehouse versus Thomson** became a foundational professional dispute about mathematical theory, empirical engineering, signal strength, and responsibility. Some later retellings sharpen the conflict into a morality play beyond what the documentary record warrants.

**Matthew Fontaine Maury (1806–1873)** interpreted Atlantic soundings as indicating a relatively level “telegraphic plateau.” The name helped promotion, though the seabed was not literally a smooth plateau.

### 5.3 Mariners and technical crews

Named commanders and officers included Captain William Hudson of *Niagara*, Captain George Preedy of *Agamemnon*, Captain James Anderson of *Great Eastern*, and later cable-ship captains and electricians such as James Graves, James Blacklock, Captain Lugar, and H. Kingsford.

A typical expedition required:

- navigating officers;
- engine-room crews;
- seamen;
- cable hands stationed around tanks and paying-out machinery;
- electricians continuously testing continuity and insulation;
- mechanics;
- splicers;
- signalmen;
- shore parties;
- surveyors.

James Graves became superintendent at Valentia on 25 June 1865 at £350 per year and transferred to the Anglo-American company on 27 July 1866. He later participated in repairs of the 1866 and French cables. This is a rare documented salary for a named station official.

### 5.4 Station operators

Operators translated written telegrams into electrical signals, read mirror or recorder movements, copied text, repeated unclear groups, logged time and routing, calculated charges, and forwarded messages over another circuit.

The Commercial company’s Rockport station in 1886 reportedly employed a superintendent, assistant superintendent, and six operators. Larger nodes also required clerks, messengers, instrument technicians, guards, cooks, and domestic staff. Heart’s Content developed a distinct company-employed community with housing and marked occupational hierarchy.

### 5.5 Users

Commercial users predominated. A contemporary statement cited in later scholarship estimated that more than 90 percent of submarine-cable messages remained commercial around 1901. Principal customers were:

- banks and securities houses;
- commodity merchants;
- insurers;
- shipping companies;
- governments;
- news agencies and newspapers;
- wealthy private individuals.

The rate structure excluded most ordinary people from routine conversational use. Short, compressed personal cablegrams were possible, especially for death, illness, travel, and urgent family business.

### 5.6 States, regulators, and intelligence users

Britain and the United States claimed priority for government messages under subsidy arrangements. Colonial governments issued landing concessions. Telegraph administrations controlled onward connections and could delay, censor, or refuse messages.

Evidence for systematic peacetime reading of every Atlantic message in this period is lacking. Operators necessarily encountered plaintext, and governments possessed legal or practical powers over traffic. Cipher systems were used by governments and businesses. During war, cable censorship and cutting became explicit tools.

### 5.7 Labor and extraction

The Atlantic system depended upon copper mining, iron production, hemp and jute, coal, shipbuilding, and gutta-percha extraction.

Gutta-percha came from trees native to Southeast Asia. Indigenous knowledge and labor were central to locating and collecting it. Much extraction killed the trees rather than tapping them sustainably, producing documented ecological depletion. The Atlantic cables cannot be separated materially from colonial commodity networks, even though much extraction occurred far outside the Atlantic.

No reviewed source establishes that the specific 1858 or 1866 expedition used enslaved labor aboard the principal cable ships. The broader material economy operated through unequal colonial land, labor, and trading regimes. Claims of a single uniform labor system across all supplier regions would exceed the evidence.

## 6. Operation and Mechanism

### 6.1 Cable construction

The 1858 conductor consisted of seven copper wires twisted into a strand. It was insulated with gutta-percha, wrapped with fibrous material, and armored with iron wires. Shore ends received heavier protection against anchors, rocks, abrasion, and wave action.

Manufacture proceeded approximately as follows:

1. test and draw copper wire;
2. twist wires into a conductor;
3. pass the core through heated gutta-percha;
4. use dies to form uniform insulation;
5. apply Chatterton’s compound between insulating layers;
6. add serving material;
7. helically wind armor wires around the core;
8. immerse and electrically test finished cable;
9. coil it into large water-filled shipboard tanks.

By 1876 contracts could require insulation resistance on the order of 300 million ohms per nautical mile at 75°F. This was an industry benchmark cited in a contemporary U.S. naval engineering account, not necessarily the specification of the 1858 cable.

### 6.2 Why long cables were electrically difficult

A submarine cable behaves as a distributed resistor and capacitor. The conductive seawater outside the insulation and copper conductor inside formed a long capacitive system. A pulse therefore spread and decayed rather than arriving as a sharp terrestrial-telegraph impulse. Closely spaced signals blurred together.

Thomson’s answer was sensitive reception at low voltage:

- a tiny mirror attached to a magnet reflected a light beam;
- current deflected the mirror;
- an operator interpreted movements of the light spot;
- later the siphon recorder converted variations into a continuous ink trace.

Whitehouse attempted to operate electromagnetic relays using much stronger voltages. The resulting dispute concerned whether signal weakness should be solved by increasing power or by increasing detector sensitivity and controlling pulse timing.

### 6.3 Laying a cable

A cable ship steamed along a surveyed route while cable passed from tanks through guide wheels and paying-out machinery over the stern. Operators controlled braking tension. Too little cable could suspend it across seabed depressions and snap it; too much slack could exhaust the supply.

Electrical staff continuously tested the cable back toward shore. A sudden change indicated a fault, fracture, or loss of insulation. Navigators recorded position, water depth, cable length paid out, speed, and strain.

The 1865 expedition account estimated approximately 600,000 revolutions of the paying-out apparatus between Valentia and Heart’s Content, each representing a little more than £1 of project cost. This was a contemporary illustrative calculation, not an audited final cost.

### 6.4 Repair

Repair crews:

1. estimated the fault’s distance by electrical tests;
2. navigated to the calculated position;
3. dragged grapnels across the seabed;
4. caught and raised the cable, sometimes from more than 2,000 fathoms;
5. buoyed one end;
6. recovered and tested the other;
7. cut away defective cable;
8. spliced in new cable;
9. paid the repaired section back into the sea.

The successful recovery of the 1865 cable in 1866 demonstrated that deep-ocean repair was possible, although it remained slow, weather-dependent, and costly.

### 6.5 Message procedure

A commercial transaction generally followed this chain:

1. **Composition:** Sender wrote a message, often compressed or encoded to reduce word charges.
2. **Acceptance:** Clerk counted chargeable words, identified destination and routing, accepted payment or charged an account.
3. **Domestic transmission:** Message traveled to the cable company’s gateway office.
4. **Queueing:** Government priority, service messages, and commercial precedence affected order.
5. **Cable encoding:** Operator sent letters or code groups through controlled electrical reversals or impulses.
6. **Reception:** The far-end operator read mirror movements or siphon-recorder traces.
7. **Verification:** Uncertain words or groups could be repeated.
8. **Relay:** A clerk transferred the message to another cable or land circuit.
9. **Delivery:** Destination office sent a messenger or delivered directly to a subscribing institution.
10. **Commercial action:** Bank, exchange, newspaper, shipper, or government acted on the information.

Codebooks converted phrases into short artificial words. A single paid word might mean “buy 5,000 bushels if price below…” or another complex instruction. Companies regulated permissible code forms because unintelligible strings complicated charging and correction.

### 6.6 Worked example: Victoria–Buchanan, 16 August 1858

The Library of Congress preserves a contemporary print reproducing the congratulatory exchange between Queen Victoria and President James Buchanan.

The documentary chain is:

1. The British government prepared Victoria’s message at Osborne on the Isle of Wight.
2. It entered the British domestic telegraph system.
3. It was forwarded through London and Ireland to Valentia.
4. Valentia operators transmitted it through the 1858 Atlantic cable to Trinity Bay.
5. The message entered the Newfoundland and North American telegraph chain.
6. It reached Buchanan in Washington.
7. Buchanan’s reply traveled through the corresponding reverse sequence.

Victoria’s message was about 99 words. It reportedly took sixteen and a half hours to transmit and verify because the circuit’s signals were weak, distorted, and repeatedly interrupted. The electrical disturbance itself crossed the ocean far faster; the delay lay in manual sending, receiving, repetition, and correction.

Government traffic had priority. The exchange was ceremonial and promotional as well as diplomatic. It demonstrated genuine intercontinental signaling but did not prove that the cable was ready for reliable high-volume commerce.

A more practically consequential 1858 message ordered British troop movements connected with the response to the Indian Rebellion. The cable reportedly allowed regiments still in Canada to be retained or redirected, avoiding an unnecessary Atlantic movement and saving the British government a substantial sum. The fact of the order is documented in cable histories; precise savings vary between retellings and should be treated as a contemporary estimate rather than an audited figure.

## 7. Change over the Lifespan

### 7.1 1851–56: regional bridge-building

The initial phase consisted of Channel-cable precedent, Newfoundland terrestrial construction, corporate monopoly, and the 1856 Cabot Strait cable. At this point the “network” was an unfinished chain designed partly to beat steamships carrying European news.

### 7.2 1857–58: experimental Atlantic connection

The 1857 attempt began at Valentia but failed when cable broke after roughly 300–400 miles had been paid out; exact figures differ with the measure used.

The 1858 expedition suffered severe weather and repeated failed starts before completing the line in early August. Celebrations occurred across Britain and North America. The cable carried several hundred messages—Smithsonian archival description gives **723**—before failure. Counts differ because sources may include service signals, formal messages, or paid public traffic.

The cable’s brief life was nevertheless a proof of physical possibility.

### 7.3 1859–64: investigation and redesign

The failure damaged investor confidence. The British government and Atlantic Telegraph Company convened the 1861 Joint Committee on submarine cables. Its extensive evidence covered materials, electrical theory, factory testing, paying-out machinery, armor, shallow-water protection, and causes of failure.

The committee’s principal practical conclusion was not that ocean telegraphy was impossible, but that better design, quality control, testing, and handling could make it reliable.

The American Civil War impeded fundraising and redirected U.S. political attention. Meanwhile:

- copper quality improved;
- galvanometer practice matured;
- gutta-percha manufacture improved;
- armor and paying-out machinery were redesigned;
- *Great Eastern* became available after commercial failure as a passenger liner.

### 7.4 1865–66: durable system

The 1865 cable was much heavier and more carefully tested. *Great Eastern* reached roughly two-thirds of the route before the cable broke. Repeated grappling attempts recovered it several times but failed to complete the repair before the expedition ended.

A new financing vehicle, the **Anglo-American Telegraph Company**, helped fund the next attempt. The 1866 expedition successfully laid a fresh cable and then recovered and completed the 1865 line. The Smithsonian corporate guide states that Anglo-American received £125,000 annually from Atlantic Telegraph revenues and £25,000 from New York, Newfoundland and London company revenues under operating arrangements; the guide characterizes this as a 25-percent return.

### 7.5 1867–73: commercial consolidation

During three reported months in 1867, the cable carried 2,772 commercial messages, with average revenue near $2,500 per day. These are published historical figures rather than surviving message-ledger totals reproduced here.

Price information accelerated arbitrage. Modern economic research reports that the New York–London differential on U.S. Treasury bonds fell about 70 percent in the four months after the 1866 connection. This is a quantified scholarly finding, not proof that the cable alone determined every price change.

Additional cables increased capacity:

- French Atlantic cable, 1869;
- Anglo-American/Valentia–Heart’s Content additions, 1873 and 1874;
- corporate amalgamation of the New York, Newfoundland and London interests with Anglo-American.

The exact corporate chronology is inconsistently summarized. Smithsonian records describe takeover followed by amalgamation in 1873; some secondary accounts place elements of merger or asset consolidation in 1869–70. The difference reflects distinct legal acts, operational control, and final corporate amalgamation rather than necessarily contradictory events.

### 7.6 1874–83: competition and pooling

The Direct United States company challenged Anglo-American. Competition pushed rates downward, but Pender acquired shares and promoted a “friendly alliance.” Litigation in **Pender v Lushington** concerned shareholder voting rights and became an important British company-law case. By 1877 Direct had entered the joint-purse arrangement.

Jay Gould’s American Telegraph and Cable Company entered in 1881. A U.S. congressional report described a familiar sequence:

1. a challenger entered;
2. rates fell to one shilling per word;
3. the challenger’s cable failed;
4. it replaced the cable;
5. an alliance restored the rate to two shillings, or 2.50 francs, per word.

This was an official contemporary allegation of anticompetitive behavior backed by tariff history. Whether “monopoly” is the proper legal term varies by jurisdiction; economically, a small coordinated group controlled most capacity.

### 7.7 1884–94: Commercial Cable and redundancy

Mackay and Bennett constructed independent cables and connected them with Mackay’s Postal Telegraph system. Their entry again lowered rates and provided alternative routing.

Redundancy did not eliminate interruption. An 1899 industry argument reported:

- two of seven Atlantic cables broken simultaneously in 1887 and 1888;
- three broken in 1889;
- four broken in 1894;
- interruptions in most years;
- the three Commercial cables interrupted 78 times from 1884 to the report date.

Because these figures appeared in advocacy for an alternative route, they should be treated as **documented contemporary claims requiring ledger verification**. They nevertheless show that failures were routine rather than exceptional.

The same source claimed the 1869 Anglo-American cable suffered twenty interruptions during a twenty-four-year life, remained unavailable for approximately five and a half cumulative years, and cost more than £300,000 to repair.

### 7.8 1895–1900: multinational mature network

By the late 1890s, Britain, France, the United States, and Germany all had corporate or national interests in Atlantic cables. Landing sites extended from Ireland and Britain to France, Germany, the Azores, Newfoundland, Nova Scotia, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, and New York.

The German system’s completion in August 1900 represented strategic diversification away from exclusive reliance on British-controlled routes. It also marks the transformation of “the Atlantic cable” from a single celebrated line into infrastructure whose normal state was plurality.

## 8. End or Transformation

### 8.1 No single end date

The network did not end within the bounded period. Four defensible dates describe different transformations:

- **September 1858:** end of the first, experimental cable.
- **1866:** end of the experimental phase and beginning of permanent service.
- **circa 1900:** end of the pioneering Anglo-American phase; emergence of mature multinational redundancy.
- **1901 onward:** beginning of competition from long-distance wireless.
- **1956:** TAT-1 transatlantic telephone cable inaugurated a higher-capacity voice era.
- **1960s:** many nineteenth-century telegraph circuits and stations finally closed; Heart’s Content ceased cable operations in 1965.

### 8.2 Structural causes of transformation

The system changed because of:

- rising message demand;
- repeated cable faults;
- need for redundant circuits;
- rate competition;
- corporate pooling and consolidation;
- improved copper, insulation, and instruments;
- national-security concern about foreign-owned routes;
- competition from radiotelegraphy;
- later telephone and digital technologies.

### 8.3 Assets and successor organizations

Corporate lines were repeatedly leased, merged, or absorbed. Anglo-American cables eventually came under Western Union operation through a ninety-nine-year lease beginning in 1912, although Western Union terminated it in 1963 and paid compensation. Remaining ownership was reorganized through Transatlantic Cables Limited.

Commercial Cable became part of the Mackay corporate group and eventually participated in later telecommunications consolidations. British cable interests contributed to the lineage of Imperial and International Communications and Cable & Wireless.

### 8.4 Physical afterlife

Surviving or documented remains include:

- the Valentia cable-station landscape;
- Heart’s Content’s 1876 brick station, equipment, staff houses, and cable shore section;
- French cable buildings on Cape Cod;
- former stations at Waterville, Canso, Rockport, and other landings;
- cable samples in the Smithsonian and other museums;
- corporate archives, charts, instruments, and expedition art;
- cable segments still lying on the Atlantic seabed.

Heart’s Content and Valentia form a proposed transnational World Heritage ensemble. The nomination evidence identifies Valentia’s 1868 building as the earliest surviving permanently constructed transoceanic cable station and Heart’s Content as an exceptionally intact counterpart.

## 9. Failures, Disasters, Fraud, Litigation, Security, and Exploitation

### 9.1 Mechanical failures

Documented failures included:

- loss of the 1855 Gulf cable;
- breakage during the 1857 expedition;
- repeated breaks during 1858 attempts;
- progressive failure of the completed 1858 cable;
- loss of the 1865 cable in mid-ocean;
- recurrent later breaks caused by anchors, fishing gear, abrasion, geological conditions, defective joints, and storms near shore.

Deep water protected cables from some human interference but made repair difficult. Shallow shore approaches were the most frequently armored and often the most vulnerable.

### 9.2 The 1858 failure controversy

Three causal accounts circulated:

1. **Whitehouse destroyed the cable with excessive voltage.** Strong later tradition; partly supported by operating records.
2. **The cable was already defective because of manufacturing and handling failures.** Supported by testimony concerning poor insulation, storage, and quality control.
3. **The design was fundamentally too light and electrically marginal.** Supported by comparison with the 1865–66 cable.

The most defensible conclusion is multicausal. Excessive electrical stress likely accelerated failure in a cable already compromised by design, manufacture, and expedition damage. Assigning exclusive blame to Whitehouse is not established.

### 9.3 Sabotage aboard *Great Eastern*

During the 1865 expedition, faults were found that some participants believed had been deliberately introduced—a wire or metallic object forced through the insulation. Contemporary suspicion focused on sabotage by an unidentified person.

**Status: unresolved allegation.** Contemporary reports establish that suspicious defects were discovered. They do not identify a perpetrator or conclusively prove malicious intent. Later narratives that speak of a known saboteur exceed the evidence.

### 9.4 Monopoly and price manipulation

The Newfoundland fifty-year concession was a legal monopoly. The joint-purse companies coordinated revenues and rates. Parliamentary, diplomatic, and newspaper sources complained that new entrants were subjected to price wars and then absorbed into the pool.

The pattern is documented. Terms such as “predatory pricing” are later economic classifications unless used by contemporary sources. No reviewed evidence supports claims of physical “monopoly violence” by the Atlantic companies against rivals; control operated through concessions, ownership, interconnection, tariffs, acquisitions, and litigation.

### 9.5 Pender v Lushington

When Pender sought control of Direct United States, disputed shareholder votes were rejected by the company chairman, Lord Lushington. Pender litigated successfully. The case is significant both to the cable network and to company law because it treated voting rights attached to shares as enforceable property rights.

### 9.6 Cable cutting and censorship

The 1884 Paris Convention made willful or culpably negligent damage to a legally established submarine cable outside territorial waters punishable, while preserving an exception for actions necessary to save ship or life after precautions.

The convention did not abolish wartime cable cutting. In the Spanish-American War of 1898, U.S. forces cut cables serving Cuba and the Philippines. British authorities in Barbados restricted messages concerning belligerent warship movements. U.S. officials considered cable-office censorship to block Spanish government traffic while preserving commercial service.

These wartime events affected the wider Atlantic-connected network more than the core Ireland–Newfoundland cables, but they established the strategic principle: cables were both neutral commercial property and military communications targets, depending on belligerent interpretation.

### 9.7 Fraud and false telegrams

Telegraphic markets were vulnerable to fabricated or premature dispatches. False information could move prices before correction arrived. The 1873 global financial crisis and the 1874 “false Argentine telegrams” became subjects of later scholarship on telegraphic speculation.

No evidence reviewed here establishes that the Atlantic cable companies systematically fabricated market news. The vulnerability lay in authentication, agency competition, privileged access, and the speed with which a short message could trigger financial action.

### 9.8 Ecological extraction

Gutta-percha demand encouraged destructive harvesting in Southeast Asia. Trees were often felled to collect relatively small quantities of latex. This was not an incidental externality: without gutta-percha’s electrical insulation, the early deep-sea network was not practicable.

The precise quantity of gutta-percha attributable solely to North Atlantic cables is not established in the reviewed sources. Claims giving a single tree count for the 1858 or 1866 cable require verification against manufacturing accounts.

## 10. States, Rival Networks, and Consequences

### 10.1 States

Britain possessed several advantages:

- cable-manufacturing capacity;
- merchant and naval shipping;
- London finance;
- an extensive domestic and imperial telegraph system;
- access to Irish Atlantic landings;
- control of important cable companies.

The United States supplied capital, customers, naval aid, and immense domestic traffic but initially depended heavily on British-manufactured ocean cable and British or Canadian landing routes.

France and Germany developed direct routes partly to reduce strategic dependence. Newfoundland used landing rights as a colonial asset but surrendered much practical control through the long monopoly concession.

### 10.2 News agencies

The cable increased the strategic importance of Reuters, Havas, Wolff, and American press associations. Agency agreements allocated territories and exchanged news. The 1870 Reuters–Havas–Wolff “Ring Combination” was a news cartel distinct from, though dependent upon, the cable-company traffic pools.

It is inaccurate to claim that cables alone created news agencies: Havas, Wolff, and Reuters predated the permanent Atlantic connection. The cables changed their speed, reach, costs, and bargaining power.

### 10.3 Finance and trade

The system permitted near-contemporaneous comparison of:

- government bonds;
- exchange rates;
- cotton and grain prices;
- freight and insurance rates;
- commodity inventories;
- shipping arrivals.

Its principal economic effect was reduction of information latency and uncertainty, not instantaneous physical settlement. Transactions still depended on banks, exchanges, paper records, ships, and later confirmation.

### 10.4 Networks displaced and successor systems

The cable did not eliminate mail steamers. Ships continued to carry:

- lengthy documents;
- newspapers;
- contracts;
- physical securities;
- people and goods;
- cable-repair crews and materials.

Instead, the cable displaced the ship’s monopoly on urgent information. Later wireless offered communication without a vulnerable continuous conductor. Telephone cables displaced telegraph-only traffic, and fiber-optic cables restored submarine cable dominance for high-capacity global communications.

## 11. Contested and Legendary Aspects

### 11.1 “The cable ended war”

**Legend/self-promotional claim:** Instant communication would bind nations together and make war less likely.

Victoria’s 1858 message expressed hope that the cable would strengthen Anglo-American friendship. Promoters frequently associated telegraphy with peace and universal brotherhood.

**Evidence:** The cable improved diplomacy’s speed but also transmitted military orders, enabled censorship, and became a target in war. The pacification claim was aspiration and promotion, not an established outcome.

### 11.2 “One man connected the continents”

**Romanticized tradition:** Cyrus Field alone conceived and built the cable.

**Evidence against:** Gisborne, Maury, Morse, Bright, Thomson, Brett, Pender, manufacturers, governments, naval crews, operators, and investors performed indispensable roles. Field’s coordination remained exceptional, but singular authorship is untenable.

### 11.3 The “telegraphic plateau”

**Promotional scientific image:** A smooth, providential seabed existed specifically suited to carry the cable.

**Documented basis:** Naval soundings indicated a broad deep-ocean region without the extreme mountain relief some feared.

**Qualification:** Soundings were sparse. “Plateau” simplified uneven terrain, and providential language belonged to contemporary religious and promotional rhetoric.

### 11.4 Whitehouse as villain, Thomson as solitary savior

**Later dramatic narrative:** An empiricist charlatan burned out the cable while a mathematical genius had already supplied the correct solution.

**Documentary assessment:** Whitehouse’s high voltages were damaging and Thomson’s sensitive instruments were superior. But company governance, manufacturing defects, inadequate testing, and expedition damage also mattered. The villain–hero binary obscures institutional failure.

### 11.5 The unidentified saboteur

**Allegation:** A saboteur aboard *Great Eastern* deliberately pierced the 1865 cable.

**Evidence:** Suspicious faults were reported.

**Against certainty:** No offender was identified and accidental manufacturing contamination remains possible. Statements that a saboteur was caught, confessed, or was known are unsupported in the reviewed evidence.

### 11.6 Tiffany cable jewelry

After the 1858 success, sections of surplus cable were sold as souvenirs, famously by Tiffany & Co. Some pieces were made into watch fobs and commemorative objects.

**Documented core:** Authentic surplus cable was marketed.

**Collector problem:** Many later objects claimed to contain “the first Atlantic cable” lack provenance. Appearance alone cannot establish authenticity because several early cables used similar construction.

### 11.7 The cable as instantaneous communication

**Popular shorthand:** Europe and America communicated “instantly.”

**Qualification:** Electrical propagation was rapid, but complete telegram delivery involved queues, manual operation, relays, correction, and physical delivery. In 1858 a major message took many hours; in later decades minutes could still mean end-to-end processing rather than pure transmission.

### 11.8 “First Internet”

**Modern analogy:** The telegraph was a Victorian Internet.

**Usefulness:** Both systems formed networks, compressed time, supported codes, commerce, fraud, news, and social communication.

**Limits:** Telegraph capacity was tiny; access was expensive and mediated by clerks; messages were not normally end-user-to-end-user; switching, storage, addressing, ownership, and surveillance differed substantially. It is analogy, not historical identity.

### 11.9 Cable treasure stories

Pieces of abandoned copper cable and rumors of recoverable precious materials encouraged occasional salvage stories. The cable carried electrical signals, not treasure or bullion.

**Status:** No credible evidence supports a hidden transatlantic “cable treasure.” Valuable copper and iron existed in the cable itself, but deep-ocean recovery during the nineteenth century generally cost more than scrap value unless undertaken for repair.

## 12. Historiography and Representations

### 12.1 Contemporary documentary record

Important primary-source categories include:

- Newfoundland incorporation acts and legislative debates;
- Atlantic Telegraph Company prospectuses and shareholder papers;
- British subsidy agreements;
- U.S. congressional and diplomatic records;
- expedition journals;
- ship logs;
- cable-manufacturing records;
- the 1861 Joint Committee report and testimony;
- operator logs and tariff books;
- newspapers;
- corporate minute books and correspondence;
- surviving telegrams;
- charts and soundings;
- paintings and technical illustrations.

The 1861 report is unusually valuable because it preserves conflicting expert testimony rather than a single retrospective narrative.

### 12.2 Archives

Principal repositories include:

- Smithsonian National Museum of American History, Cyrus W. Field Papers;
- Smithsonian Anglo-American Telegraph Company Records;
- BT Archives;
- Library of Congress;
- The National Archives, United Kingdom;
- Newfoundland and Labrador archives and the Memorial University Digital Archives Initiative;
- Irish national and local heritage collections;
- French postal, telegraph, naval, corporate, and departmental archives;
- German company and technical archives;
- museums at Heart’s Content, Valentia, Porthcurno, and Cape Cod.

The Smithsonian Field collection contains correspondence, photographs, resolutions, maps, charts, and printed publications. The Anglo-American records document corporate operation and later succession.

### 12.3 Major interpretive traditions

Early histories emphasized heroic invention, national celebration, providence, and Field’s perseverance. Engineering histories shifted attention toward signal theory, cable construction, naval surveying, and professionalization.

Later scholarship studies:

- corporate monopoly and cartel behavior;
- empire and strategic communications;
- information costs and market integration;
- global news agencies;
- environmental extraction of gutta-percha;
- labor and cable-station communities;
- international law;
- media myths and technological spectacle.

### 12.4 Archival asymmetries

The best-preserved documentation disproportionately represents directors, engineers, governments, and companies. Ordinary operators, messengers, shore laborers, Southeast Asian collectors, domestic workers, and local communities appear less consistently. Absence from corporate archives should not be interpreted as absence from the system.

Exact traffic statistics are particularly difficult because:

- companies defined “message,” “word,” and “paid traffic” differently;
- service messages might be excluded;
- records survive unevenly;
- public reports could serve promotional or regulatory purposes;
- pooled companies sometimes reported aggregate rather than route-specific results.

### 12.5 Significant popular representations

- W. H. Russell, *The Atlantic Telegraph* (1865): illustrated contemporary expedition account; invaluable but closely associated with company promotion.
- Henry M. Field, *History of the Atlantic Telegraph*: family-centered history emphasizing Cyrus Field.
- Charles Bright, *The Story of the Atlantic Cable*: engineering-family account with extensive documentary material.
- Bern Dibner, *The Atlantic Cable* (1959): influential technological history.
- Arthur C. Clarke, *Voice Across the Sea* (1958): readable history, strong on engineering drama.
- Tom Standage, *The Victorian Internet* (1998): influential analogy-driven popular synthesis.
- PBS, *The Great Transatlantic Cable*: popular documentary treatment emphasizing Field, technical failure, and eventual success.
- Robert Charles Dudley’s 1865–66 paintings: commissioned visual record blending documentation and celebration.
- Tiffany cable souvenirs: material culture that helped turn infrastructure into patriotic relic.

Popular accounts often:

- call 1866 simply the first cable;
- reduce failure to Whitehouse’s voltage;
- omit Newfoundland’s monopoly;
- treat Heart’s Content and the 1858 Trinity Bay landing as the same terminus;
- ignore feeder networks and clerical labor;
- understate French, German, and American competition after 1866.

## 14. Findings and Unresolved Questions

The reconstructed system supports several firm conclusions:

1. There was no single transatlantic-cable network owner. A succession of competing and cooperating private corporations operated under national and colonial concessions.
2. The 1858 cable was real and operational, not merely an unsuccessful laying attempt.
3. Permanent service began in 1866, with redundancy achieved when the 1865 cable was recovered.
4. The network’s essential geography extended far beyond its two famous beaches.
5. Government subsidies and naval assistance were indispensable to its formation.
6. Commercial traffic, especially finance, trade, shipping, and news, dominated ordinary use.
7. Monopoly concessions and traffic pools were structural features, not incidental scandals.
8. Failure and repair were normal operational conditions.
9. The system depended materially upon colonial extraction of gutta-percha and globally dispersed industrial labor.
10. By 1900 it had transformed into a redundant multinational infrastructure rather than ended.

Important unresolved or incompletely established questions include:

- the exact count of distinct paid messages carried in 1858;
- the precise final intelligible transmission date of the 1858 cable;
- responsibility for suspicious 1865 cable faults;
- route-by-route annual traffic totals before standardized reporting;
- the proportion of messages read, copied, or retained by governments;
- complete workforce numbers and demographic composition at each station;
- exact quantities and sources of gutta-percha used in individual Atlantic cables;
- the full extent of informal interconnection discrimination against rival companies;
- the survival and precise seabed condition of every nineteenth-century cable section.
