# Jesuit reductions of Paraguay: Network Dossier

**Requested filename:** `jesuit_reductions_of_paraguay_network_dossier.md`  
**Bounded period:** 1609–1767, with local implementation of expulsion in 1768 and later institutional afterlives noted where necessary.

## Evidentiary key

- **Documented:** supported by contemporary records, material evidence, or convergent modern scholarship.
- **Reported:** appears in a contemporary source but may reflect its author’s interests or incomplete knowledge.
- **Scholarly inference:** a modern reconstruction from indirect or incomplete evidence.
- **Disputed:** credible sources offer incompatible interpretations or figures.
- **Allegation/propaganda:** a claim circulated polemically without adequate independent proof.
- **Tradition/legend:** commemorative or popular narrative not demonstrable as stated.
- **Unresolved:** surviving evidence does not permit a firm conclusion.

“Jesuit” and “Guaraní” are used descriptively. The network was neither exclusively clerical nor ethnically homogeneous: Guaraní communities supplied nearly all its inhabitants, labor, transport crews, soldiers, artisans, elected officers, and local knowledge.

## 1. Identification and Definition

### 1.1 What the network was

The Jesuit reductions of Paraguay were a connected system of Indigenous towns established within the Spanish monarchy and administered jointly—but very unequally—by Guaraní communities, their hereditary leaders and municipal officers, and members of the Society of Jesus. The mature core consisted of **thirty Guaraní mission towns** distributed across territory now divided among Paraguay, Argentina, and Brazil. UNESCO and national heritage institutions consequently treat them as a transnational cultural system rather than isolated monuments. [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/document/101965/); [IPHAN](https://www.gov.br/iphan/pt-br/superintendencias/rio-grande-do-sul/patrimonio-mundial)

Each town was simultaneously:

- a Catholic parish and center of conversion;
- a legally recognized *pueblo de indios*, within the Spanish “Republic of Indians”;
- a municipality with an Indigenous *cabildo*;
- a concentration and redistribution point for food, textiles, tools, livestock, and labor;
- a military-militia unit;
- a node in provincial Jesuit government and correspondence;
- a transport station linked by rivers, roads, paths, ferries, ranches, ports, and messenger relays;
- a workshop and cultural-production center;
- a fiscal unit owing tribute or equivalent services to the Crown.

The network carried yerba mate, cattle, hides, tallow, cotton, textiles, tobacco, timber, boats, metals, books, medicines, religious images, imported fabrics, wine and wax; it also moved tribute, credit, military service, labor drafts, missionaries, royal orders, annual reports, linguistic knowledge, music, bells, printed matter, captives, fugitives, epidemic pathogens, and Catholic doctrine.

### 1.2 Names

Documented or conventional names include:

- **Spanish:** *reducciones jesuíticas del Paraguay*; *misiones jesuíticas del Paraguay*; *misiones guaraníes*; *misiones jesuítico-guaraníes*; *los Treinta Pueblos*; *pueblos de Misiones*; *doctrinas de guaraníes*.
- **Portuguese:** *reduções jesuíticas do Paraguai*; *missões jesuíticas guaranis*; *Sete Povos das Missões* for the seven eastern-Uruguay towns.
- **Latin:** variants of *reductiones Paraquariae* or descriptions of peoples *ad Ecclesiam et vitam civilem reducti* occur in Jesuit writing; there was no single official Latin network title.
- **Italian:** *riduzioni gesuitiche del Paraguay*; *Repubblica guaranica* in later historiography.
- **French:** *missions* or *réductions jésuites du Paraguay/chez les Guaranis*.
- **English:** Jesuit reductions, Paraguay missions, Guaraní missions.
- **Guaraní:** no surviving evidence establishes a single pre-expulsion Guaraní name for the entire thirty-town network. Towns were commonly *táva*—settlements or towns—in later Guaraní usage. Applying a modern standardized Guaraní collective title retroactively would be conjectural.

“**Jesuit Republic of Paraguay**,” “**Guaraní Republic**,” “**Christian Commonwealth**,” and “**communist/theocratic state**” are historiographical or polemical labels, not the system’s constitutional name. It was legally within the Spanish monarchy, ecclesiastical dioceses, and the Society of Jesus, not a sovereign state.

### 1.3 “Paraguay” and the limits of the subject

The **Jesuit Province of Paraguay**, authorized in 1604, was far larger than the thirty Guaraní towns. It encompassed Jesuit colleges, residences, farms, and missions across parts of modern Argentina, Paraguay, Uruguay, Bolivia, Brazil, and historically Chile. The Guaraní reductions were one important subsystem within this province; Córdoba was the provincial administrative center. [Cambridge overview](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/latin-american-perspectives-on-science-and-religion/jesuit-science-in-the-missions-of-paraguay-and-rio-de-la-plata/7AC304BCC940906407B4EFFA198EEFFA)

This dossier treats as “inside” the network:

1. the mature thirty towns;
2. their abandoned or transferred predecessors in Guairá, Itatín, and Tapé;
3. dependent chapels, *estancias*, *vaquerías*, plantations, yerba forests, ports, ferries, and posts;
4. Jesuit procurator networks at Asunción, Santa Fe, Buenos Aires, Córdoba, and—when relevant—Europe;
5. Guaraní militia deployments and transport services that connected the towns to the monarchy.

It excludes the Chiquitos, Moxos, Chaco, and other missions as constituent towns, although they belonged to related Jesuit frontier systems.

### 1.4 Boundary dates

**1609** is conventional because Diego de Torres Bollo’s mission program produced San Ignacio Guazú, traditionally dated to December 1609, and the first Guairá foundations immediately thereafter. One recent reconstruction identifies **29 December 1609** as the founding of San Ignacio in Guairá, whereas Paraguayan tradition assigns the first foundation to San Ignacio Guazú. The discrepancy reflects different frontier initiatives and unstable early nomenclature rather than a single uncontested inaugural act. [Wilde, “Jesuits as Petitioners”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/americas/article/jesuits-as-petitioners-antonio-ruiz-de-montoya-and-the-issue-of-indigenous-slavery-in-the-early-seventeenthcentury-south-atlantic/6643B9550C999C3D2E5C17A6919F5CDD)

**1767** is the legal endpoint: Charles III’s Pragmatic Sanction of **2 April 1767** ordered Jesuit expulsion and seizure of temporal property. [Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/item/2018751329/) The order was implemented in the Río de la Plata missions principally in **1768**. The towns, populations, cabildos, churches, herds, and commercial routes continued under Crown-appointed secular administrators and non-Jesuit clergy. Thus:

- end of Jesuit legal control: 1767–68;
- end of the towns as functioning communities: gradual and geographically variable;
- destruction of much of the western mission zone: especially the wars of 1811–28;
- survival of former mission communities in Paraguay: into the nineteenth century.

## 2. Formation

### 2.1 Predecessors

Before 1609, Guaraní political and economic life was organized through dispersed settlements, extended-family houses, shifting cultivation, river travel, exchange, warfare, captive-taking, shamanic authority, and alliances under numerous *mburuvicha* or caciques. “Semi-nomadic” is too crude: mobility and resettlement coexisted with horticulture and territorial attachment.

Spanish colonization introduced Asunción, encomienda labor, tribute demands, Christian parishes, ranching, Atlantic goods, epidemic disease, and violent competition for Indigenous labor. Franciscan missionaries—notably Luis de Bolaños and Alonso de San Buenaventura—created earlier reductions from the late sixteenth century. Jesuit organization therefore adapted an existing Spanish-American instrument rather than inventing the reduction.

### 2.2 Institutional authorization

The reduction belonged to the monarchy’s broader policy of concentrating Indigenous populations into governable Christian towns. Royal patronage gave the Crown extensive authority over mission organization; bishops retained sacramental and visitation jurisdiction; governors, audiencias, and the Council of the Indies regulated tribute, labor, defense, and clerical disputes.

Governor **Hernando Arias de Saavedra (Hernandarias)** encouraged Jesuit expansion. **Diego de Torres Bollo**, first provincial of the independent Jesuit Province of Paraguay, supplied organizational leadership. The **Ordinances of Francisco de Alfaro (1611–12)** curtailed some abuses of Indigenous service and became an important legal reference, though enforcement remained incomplete. Jesuit missions offered an institutional barrier against direct encomendero access but did not remove Guaraní residents from colonial tribute, compulsory service, or royal jurisdiction. [Wilde](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/americas/article/jesuits-as-petitioners/6643B9550C999C3D2E5C17A6919F5CDD)

### 2.3 Why Guaraní communities entered

No single explanation is sufficient.

**Documented motives and conditions** included:

- attraction to iron tools, livestock, medicines, food reserves, ritual goods, and Jesuit alliances;
- protection against encomienda labor and Paulista slave raids;
- epidemic and military disruption of older communities;
- Indigenous leaders’ use of missions to reconstruct followers and authority;
- missionary persuasion and accommodation through Guaraní language;
- movement under direct or indirect coercion.

**Scholarly inference:** Guaraní communities treated mission affiliation as an adaptive political strategy, not simply passive submission. This interpretation is supported by recurrent negotiation, flight, selective participation, armed resistance, and the persistence of cacique-centered organization.

**Documented coercion:** missionaries and Christian Guaraní sometimes entered non-mission territory to recruit inhabitants; hostile Indigenous people could be captured and raised in missions. Pedro Lozano’s knowledge of the Aché, for example, partly came from approximately thirty Aché children or youths taken by Guaraní parties connected with a reduction. [Cambridge study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/empire-colonialism-and-the-human-sciences/modest-witnesses-of-violence/688FB91F151AA5C830A9D08FFE8C1A8F)

### 2.4 Founders and early operators

Important named builders included:

- **Diego de Torres Bollo:** provincial and organizer.
- **Marciel de Lorenzana** and **Francisco de San Martín:** associated with early Paraná missions.
- **José Cataldino** and **Simón Maceta:** early Guairá missionaries.
- **Roque González de Santa Cruz:** Paraguayan-born Jesuit, founder or organizer of Itapúa, Concepción, Yapeyú, and other southern missions; killed in 1628.
- **Juan del Castillo** and **Alonso Rodríguez:** killed with González during anti-mission resistance in 1628.
- **Antonio Ruiz de Montoya:** superior, chronicler, organizer of the Guairá evacuation, and petitioner against Indigenous enslavement.
- **Indigenous founders:** numerous named and unnamed caciques provided land, followers, guides, food, boats, and political consent. Jesuit narratives often compressed this indispensable agency into stories centered on priests.

## 3.2 Lost and predecessor nodes

The mature list conceals earlier geographic expansion.

### Guairá

Between approximately 1610 and 1628 Jesuits established about thirteen missions in the region bounded broadly by the Paraná, Paranapanema, Tibagi, and Piquiri rivers, now chiefly Paraná state, Brazil. Jesuit reports claimed more than **40,000** residents or converts; this is a contemporary institutional claim, not a complete independent census. Paulista expeditions destroyed most settlements and enslaved large numbers. [Wilde](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/americas/article/jesuits-as-petitioners/6643B9550C999C3D2E5C17A6919F5CDD)

Known names include Loreto, San Ignacio Miní, San Javier, San José, Encarnación, San Pablo, Santo Tomé, Jesús María, San Miguel, San Antonio, and others whose precise sites or continuous identities are disputed. Several mature towns preserved the corporate identity and patron saint of destroyed Guairá communities after migration.

### Itatín

Missions north and east of Asunción occupied portions of present Paraguay and Mato Grosso do Sul. Paulista attacks, Mbayá pressure, epidemics, and relocation broke this branch. Some refugee populations fed later towns, including Santiago and Santa María de Fe. Exact continuity between successive names is often difficult to demonstrate.

### First Tapé/Uruguay expansion

Roque González and colleagues established missions east and west of the Uruguay during the 1620s–30s. Raids destroyed or displaced the eastern settlements. A second eastward expansion beginning with São Borja in 1682 produced the permanent Seven Peoples.

## 3.3 Infrastructure below town level

A reduction was not merely its plaza.

Documented components included:

- church, sacristy, bell tower, cemetery, Jesuit residence, school and workshops;
- a central square used for processions, drill, punishment, festivals, and markets or distributions;
- long rows of Indigenous housing;
- *cotiguazú*, generally housing widows, unmarried women, or women considered vulnerable, but also functioning as an institution of surveillance;
- hospital or infirmary and, during epidemics, temporary isolation facilities;
- council house, storehouses, armory, prison, and guest lodging;
- household plots (*abambaé*) and communal fields (*tupambaé*);
- yerba plantations or distant wild-yerba gathering grounds;
- cattle and horse ranches, sheep flocks, dairies, slaughter places, and hide-processing sites;
- ports, canoes, ferries, carts, corrals, bridges, roads, and messenger paths;
- dependent chapels, ranch posts, guard posts, and seasonal camps.

The built center was therefore the visible control and redistribution hub of a much larger productive territory. Pablo Ibáñez’s archival and architectural research stresses that plantations, ranches, and peripheral establishments are necessary to understand the system. [Territory and urbanism dissertation](https://digibug.ugr.es/handle/10481/47121)

## 4. Connections

## 4.1 River routes

The **Paraná and Uruguay rivers** were the principal long-distance arteries. Canoes, rafts, and locally built boats carried bulk goods, passengers, militia, and messages. Tributaries connected individual towns to the main rivers. Downstream movement of yerba, hides, and textiles was generally easier than upstream movement of imports; upstream traffic required paddling, poling, towing, portage, or terrestrial alternatives.

Important river connections included:

- Paraná towns ↔ Itapúa/Encarnación ↔ Asunción;
- Candelaria/Loreto/San Ignacio ↔ downstream Paraná ↔ Corrientes and Santa Fe;
- Uruguay towns ↔ Yapeyú ↔ lower Uruguay and Río de la Plata;
- mission system ↔ Santa Fe and Buenos Aires procurator offices;
- Seven Peoples ↔ western Uruguay towns by crossings vulnerable during floods or war.

Evidence consists of Jesuit correspondence, procurator accounts, inventories of boats, official inspections, shipping obligations, maps, and archaeology. The record is too uneven for a reliable system-wide timetable.

## 4.2 Overland network

Roads and paths connected adjacent towns, fields, yerbales, ranches, and ports. Ox carts and pack animals moved heavy or inland cargo; mounted messengers and Indigenous runners moved orders and news. Some routes followed earlier Indigenous paths.

No single continuously engineered “Jesuit road” joined all thirty towns. Modern tourist routes sometimes turn multiple historically changing paths into one stable cartographic corridor. The secure historical proposition is a mesh of local and regional links, not a standardized trunk highway.

## 4.3 Administrative correspondence

A normal chain was:

**mission priest or cabildo → Superior of Missions, often at Candelaria → provincial in Córdoba → procurators/consultors → Jesuit general in Rome**.

Provincials compiled **Cartas Anuas**, regular narrative reports sent to the superior general. Surviving collections record foundations, conversions, conflicts, epidemics, martyrdoms, natural history, personnel, and finances, but they are selective institutional documents intended for governance and edification. [Digital Cartas Anuas collection](https://repositorio.bpm.parquedelconocimiento.com/handle/bpm/238?locale-attribute=en)

Royal commands ran through Madrid, the Council of the Indies, the viceroy or Audiencia, governors, mission superiors, priests, and Indigenous officers. Bishops maintained a partly independent visitation and sacramental network.

## 4.4 Commercial connections

Exports traveled chiefly toward Asunción, Santa Fe, Buenos Aires, and inland markets, including Córdoba, Upper Peru/Potosí, and Chile through merchant relays. Imports originated in regional Spanish-American production and Atlantic commerce.

A 1659 obligation recorded **79 arrobas of yerba at two pesos per arroba** in payment for imported cloth. A 1675 obligation exchanged **217 arrobas and 18 pounds of yerba** for luxury textiles, gold lace, silk stockings, and leather. These are documented individual transactions, not typical system-wide price series. [Hernández, “Productos importados”](https://www.scielo.br/j/rbh/a/R7QjrfYYCLMmvMzKTpc4KLR/?lang=es)

Goods moving outward included:

- yerba mate;
- cotton textiles and thread;
- tobacco;
- hides, tallow, cattle, and horses;
- timber, boats, and manufactured objects;
- wax and agricultural surplus.

Goods moving inward included:

- iron and steel;
- tools and weapons;
- salt in deficient areas;
- European or regional cloth;
- wine for Mass;
- wax, paper, books, pigments, medicines, and ornaments.

## 4.5 Military connections

The mission militia connected towns horizontally and bound them to the Crown. Units gathered under Indigenous commanders, transported their own provisions, and served against slave raiders, Portuguese forces, rebellious settlers, and frontier Indigenous enemies. Mission troops also supported Spanish campaigns well outside their towns.

The militia’s mobility depended on horses, boats, communal stores, messenger alarms, weapons inventories, and inter-town obligations. Bells functioned as both liturgical instruments and audible signals regulating time and summoning inhabitants. [“Bells in the Jesuit reducciones”](https://www.sciencedirect.com/org/science/article/pii/S221413241600056X)

## 5. Agents and Operators

## 5.1 Guaraní population and authorities

Guaraní residents were not simply “users”; collectively they were the network’s material operators.

Roles included:

- hereditary caciques and their followers;
- *corregidores*, *alcaldes*, *regidores*, sheriffs, clerks, and other cabildo officers;
- sacristans, catechists, musicians, choristers, teachers, and literate secretaries;
- farmers, herders, hunters, yerba gatherers, boat crews, cart drivers, builders, carpenters, smiths, sculptors, painters, printers, and weavers;
- militia commanders, firearm specialists, scouts, guards, and mounted soldiers;
- women who farmed, spun, wove, prepared food, sustained households, cared for the sick, and participated in confraternities and ritual production;
- children educated and assigned age- and gender-specific work;
- captives, refugees, fugitives, and newcomers integrated under varying degrees of compulsion.

Cabildos formally resembled Spanish municipalities. Officers were Indigenous, but Jesuit priests influenced nominations, elections, discipline, accounts, and judicial outcomes. Caciques retained corporate status and followers inside this municipal structure.

Guaraní literacy was selective. Some elite men wrote in Guaraní and Spanish, maintained accounts, copied music, printed books, and corresponded with royal officers. It is therefore inaccurate to describe all policy as simply emanating from two European priests.

## 5.2 Jesuit personnel

A mature town usually had only **two or three Jesuits**, typically:

- a priest responsible for doctrine, worship, discipline, and external relations;
- a companion responsible for temporal affairs or pastoral assistance;
- occasionally a lay brother or specialist.

For scale, one scholarly summary counts **249 Jesuits in the entire province in 1692, of whom 73 were in the missions**. Because “province” included colleges and other missions, this is compatible with approximately two clerics per Guaraní town. [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/latin-american-perspectives-on-science-and-religion/jesuit-science-in-the-missions-of-paraguay-and-rio-de-la-plata/7AC304BCC940906407B4EFFA198EEFFA)

Specialists included:

- **Antonio Sepp:** music, metallurgy, architecture, and descriptive writing.
- **Domenico Zipoli:** composer whose music circulated through the provincial mission system, although he worked mainly in Córdoba.
- **Buenaventura Suárez:** astronomy and instrument-making at San Cosme y Damián.
- **José Cardiel:** missionary, cartographer, traveler, and later defender of the reductions.
- **Florian Paucke** and **Martin Dobrizhoffer:** worked primarily in related non-Guaraní missions but left important comparative descriptions.
- **Nicolás Yapuguay:** Guaraní author associated with printed sermons and linguistic-religious production.
- **Domingo de Torres:** former soldier and Jesuit brother involved in firearms training at Mbororé.

## 5.3 External administrators and regulators

- Spanish governors of Paraguay and Buenos Aires;
- the Audiencias of Charcas and Buenos Aires;
- the viceroy of Peru and, from 1776, Río de la Plata;
- bishops of Asunción and Buenos Aires;
- royal treasury officials and tax collectors;
- merchants, carriers, creditors, and competing yerba producers;
- Portuguese governors, soldiers, settlers, and diplomats;
- Paulista *bandeirantes* and their Indigenous auxiliaries;
- encomenderos and Asunción municipal elites;
- the Jesuit provincial, consultors, procurators, and superior general.

## 5.4 Enslavement and coerced labor

Paulista expeditions captured and enslaved Indigenous people on a large scale. Jesuit records reported thousands captured or killed and towns burned; exact totals are uncertain because institutional reports were polemical and Paulista records incomplete. The destruction itself, forced marches, slave markets, and demographic effects are securely documented.

Within reductions, communal work was compulsory and disciplined. Residents did not generally receive regular wages for assigned communal labor, although evidence exists for bonuses, private possessions, and individual commerce. [Oxford Research Encyclopedia](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61800/chapter-abstract/546345395)

The wider Jesuit Province also owned and used enslaved Africans and Afro-descendants in colleges and estates. That labor was not the standard legal basis of the thirty Guaraní towns, but those towns exchanged goods through an institutional economy partly supported by enslaved labor elsewhere. Scholarship based on the *Cartas Anuas* rejects older denials of an African and enslaved presence in the province. [Tardieu](https://estudiosamericanos.revistas.csic.es/index.php/estudiosamericanos/article/view/71)

## 6. Operation and Mechanism

## 6.1 Land, labor, and distribution

Mission production conventionally divided into:

- **abambaé:** household or family-use plots and possessions;
- **tupambaé:** communal production described as belonging to God or the community.

The distinction was real but not absolute. Families consumed privately grown crops and owned some movable property. Communal fields, herds, and workshops provisioned clergy, churches, festivals, widows, orphans, the sick, militia, tribute payments, seed reserves, and periods of scarcity.

Work was allocated by bells, officers, priests, kin groups, age, gender, and skill. The communal system provided insurance against harvest failure but also allowed administrators to extract labor and control surplus. Its productivity cannot be measured simply by gross exports because much production was consumed internally.

## 6.2 A typical yerba-mate transfer

A reconstructed transaction proceeded approximately as follows:

1. **Authorization and labor assignment:** priest, cabildo, and production officers determined the required expedition or plantation harvest.
2. **Movement to source:** Guaraní workers traveled to cultivated groves or distant wild yerbales with provisions and tools.
3. **Harvest and processing:** leaves were cut, heated or scorched, dried, crushed, and packed.
4. **Return and storage:** loads moved by carriers, pack animals, canoe, or cart to mission storehouses.
5. **Accounting:** the town recorded the communal product, though surviving accounts are incomplete.
6. **Consolidation:** yerba from one or several missions was shipped toward a Jesuit procurator, particularly in Santa Fe or Buenos Aires.
7. **Sale or barter:** the procurator dealt with merchants, settled debts, paid taxes or costs, and purchased imported goods.
8. **Return shipment:** cloth, metal, wine, tools, paper, wax, or credit balances returned through the same procurement network.
9. **Redistribution:** mission administrators allocated imports among church, workshop, communal stores, officers, and households.

Risks included spoiled cargo, river accidents, theft, contraband charges, price fluctuations, merchant insolvency, corrupt or opaque accounting, labor flight, epidemic interruption, and official embargo.

### A documented transaction

In 1659, an obligation valued **79 arrobas of yerba at two pesos per arroba**, total nominal value **158 pesos**, against nineteen varas of *estameña* and eight of cambric. The source establishes commodity-credit equivalence but not necessarily immediate physical exchange at one place. The likely end-to-end chain was mission or Paraguayan production → regional intermediary or procurator → imported cloth stock → written obligation → delivery or account settlement. The precise originating reduction and transport time cannot be established from the published example. [Hernández](https://www.scielo.br/j/rbh/a/R7QjrfYYCLMmvMzKTpc4KLR/?lang=es)

## 6.3 Procurator controversy

Jesuit procurators centralized commerce. A critical letter from the Jesuit superior general complained that procurators bought beyond provincial needs, resold goods at substantial markups, and failed to render adequate accounts. It cited:

- recurring sales of **500 or 1,000 arrobas** of yerba and tobacco;
- a purchase of **20,000 pesos of wax** when only about 3,000 pesos’ worth was allegedly needed;
- **5,000 varas** of cloth bought on credit and resold;
- some missions allegedly owed **4,000–5,000 pesos** without receiving their balances.

These are documented internal allegations, valuable because they were not produced by an external enemy. They demonstrate a recognized accounting problem, but do not by themselves prove that all differences became private clerical profit. Possible destinations included operating capital, hidden institutional reserves, cross-subsidies, losses, and unauthorized gain. [Hernández](https://www.scielo.br/j/rbh/a/R7QjrfYYCLMmvMzKTpc4KLR/?lang=es)

## 6.4 Governance and discipline

The daily rhythm included dawn prayers, Mass, communal assignments, household production, workshop instruction, catechism, meals, evening worship, festivals, and militia drill. Bells made schedules audible over the town.

Discipline could include confinement, public penance, corporal punishment, removal from office, control over marriage, and pursuit of fugitives. Surviving Jesuit descriptions often portray punishment as paternal moderation; hostile observers portray despotism. That punishment and intensive surveillance existed is documented; comparative severity cannot be reliably reduced to either polemical version.

Women’s sexuality, residence, widowhood, and marriage were particularly regulated. The *cotiguazú* could provide collective support while also restricting movement.

## 6.5 Education, language, printing, and information

Missionaries learned and standardized varieties of Guaraní for catechesis. Grammar, dictionaries, confession manuals, sermons, songs, and translated doctrine circulated among towns. The choice of Guaraní allowed communication across the network and reinforced a mission-wide written register, while displacing or marginalizing some local linguistic variation and non-Christian ritual vocabularies.

A mission printing press began operating about **1700**, first associated with Loreto and later moved among Santa María la Mayor and San Francisco Javier. It produced Guaraní-language and bilingual religious works, including Nicolás Yapuguay’s *Sermones y ejemplos* (1727). The equipment was locally fashioned to a considerable degree; surviving imprints securely establish production, though claims that every component was mission-made are difficult to verify. [Josefina Plá collection](https://www.portalguarani.com/detalles_museos_otras_obras.php?PqwoiflUYTeslk=MTA4Nw%3D%3D)

Music moved through manuscripts, trained ensembles, instrument workshops, traveling specialists, and festivals. Musical accomplishment was both worship and political display.

## 6.6 Defense: Mbororé as a worked mobilization

After Guairá’s destruction, Ruiz de Montoya traveled through the imperial petitioning network seeking firearms and protection. A royal order of **21 May 1640** directed the viceroy to consider licensing and supplying weapons. Jesuit and Guaraní forces were already arming before final authorization; the petition partly regularized existing practice.

At Mbororé in March 1641:

1. scouts and mission correspondence warned of the expedition;
2. towns concentrated provisions, boats, weapons, and several thousand Guaraní;
3. Indigenous commanders operated under cacique **Don Nicolás Ñeengirú**;
4. Jesuit brother Domingo de Torres supplied military expertise;
5. rivercraft were fortified and the Uruguay–Mbororé confluence used as a defensive environment;
6. Guaraní forces defeated the Paulista column and Indigenous auxiliaries;
7. subsequent royal policy institutionalized the militia.

One reconstruction gives **3,000 Guaraní** against **400 Paulistas and 2,500 Tupí auxiliaries**; another gives approximately **2,000 Guaraní**. These are source-dependent campaign estimates, not modern muster rolls. The secure conclusion is a large, coordinated, firearm-using mission force and a decisive Paulista defeat. [Wilde PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/6643B9550C999C3D2E5C17A6919F5CDD/S000316152200150Xa.pdf/jesuits_as_petitioners_antonio_ruiz_de_montoya_and_the_issue_of_indigenous_slavery_in_the_early_seventeenthcentury_south_atlantic.pdf); [Redden](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/gdc/gdcebookspublic/20/19/66/79/96/2019667996/2019667996.pdf)

A quantitative reconstruction reports **609 firearms** distributed among missions in 1647 and another **150** supplied under a royal decree of 20 September 1649. A 1716 inspection at Santa Rosa recorded twenty-three shotguns plus ammunition and Indigenous weapons. These numbers describe inspected stocks, not the maximum number secretly or temporarily present.

## 6.7 Population and demographic mechanism

The thirty missions reached approximately **141,182–141,242 inhabitants in 1732**, depending transcription and aggregation. This is among the most secure system-wide figures because it derives from mission enumerations, but censuses may omit absentees or categorize people inconsistently. [Jackson bibliography](https://jesuitonlinebibliography.bc.edu/catalog/10833); [Cambridge summary](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/latin-american-perspectives-on-science-and-religion/jesuit-science-in-the-missions-of-paraguay-and-rio-de-la-plata/7AC304BCC940906407B4EFFA198EEFFA)

Population recovered between epidemics through high fertility and community recruitment, unlike many smaller frontier missions. Concentrated settlement and frequent inter-town movement also facilitated smallpox, measles, and other infections. Jesuits attempted quarantine and temporary hospitals, but effectiveness varied.

A 1764–65 smallpox outbreak reportedly killed more than **12,000** across the network. This is a scholarly reconstruction from mission vital data. Epidemic intervals and mortality differed by town, making a single average hazardous. Robert Jackson specifically criticizes demographic models that suppress regional variation.

## 7. Change over the Lifespan

## 7.1 1609–1628: experimental expansion

Jesuits established multiple mission fronts in Paraná, Guairá, Itatín, and Uruguay/Tapé. Settlements were fragile, frequently moved, and depended on particular cacique alliances. Priests promoted Guaraní-language Christianity, nucleated residence, monogamous marriage, and agricultural regularity.

Opposition came from encomenderos, shamans and political leaders who rejected Jesuit demands, communities that departed, and Paulista slave hunters. Roque González, Juan del Castillo, and Alonso Rodríguez were killed in 1628 during Indigenous resistance led in mission sources by Ñezú. Jesuit martyrological accounts document the deaths but frame the opponents through a theological lens.

## 7.2 1628–1641: destruction, exodus, and militarization

Paulista expeditions broke the Guairá system. Ruiz de Montoya led survivors by river and overland toward safer territory. The often-repeated figure of approximately **12,000 survivors traveling roughly 500 kilometers** is a plausible synthesis, but counts varied during movement because of death, desertion, separation, and later regrouping. [Overview](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/americas/article/jesuits-as-petitioners/6643B9550C999C3D2E5C17A6919F5CDD)

The exodus transformed the network:

- eastern outposts contracted;
- refugee towns became large composite communities;
- river crossings became strategic;
- defense shifted from avoidance to organized militia;
- imperial lobbying connected local survival to Madrid, Lima, Charcas, and Rome.

Mbororé in 1641 checked large-scale Paulista attacks on the consolidated core, though it did not end enslavement or border violence.

## 7.3 1641–1682: consolidation

The towns regularized cabildos, communal production, militia service, parish routines, and provincial oversight. Stone and timber churches replaced more temporary structures. Yerba and livestock commerce linked the towns more tightly to colonial markets.

Mission exemption from ordinary encomienda service was counterbalanced by tribute and military obligations. Crown authorities increasingly used Guaraní troops as a regional defense reserve.

## 7.4 1682–1732: eastern expansion and peak

The second occupation east of the Uruguay created the Seven Peoples. Large ranch territories and cattle reserves expanded. Yapeyú developed an especially extensive dependent estate and post system reaching toward the Banda Oriental.

Monumental churches, sculpture, foundries, clocks, instruments, manuscript copying, and printing appeared at selected nodes. Such specialization depended on inter-town exchange: no town independently reproduced the whole productive system.

The population peak in 1732—about 141,000—should not be mistaken for uninterrupted prosperity. Local epidemics, crop failures, raids, and labor demands continued.

## 7.5 1733–1749: demographic crisis and recovery

War-related mobilization, drought or anomalous weather, harvest failure, famine, and epidemics produced severe losses. At one mission examined by Jackson, population declined from **6,515 in 1731 to 2,044 at the end of 1737**; this is a local case, not the thirty-town total. Epidemics moved along the network’s transport connections. [Jackson](https://www.sciencedirect.com/org/science/article/pii/S2214132423000055)

Recovery followed in some towns through births, return migration, and redistribution, but the overall population did not regain the 1732 maximum before expulsion.

## 7.6 1750–1756: treaty crisis and Guaraní War

The **Treaty of Madrid, 13 January 1750**, attempted a comprehensive Spanish-Portuguese boundary settlement. Spain was to receive Colonia del Sacramento; Portugal was to receive territory containing the Seven Peoples. Guaraní inhabitants were ordered to evacuate west of the Uruguay, taking movable property but abandoning town sites and much productive infrastructure. [Treaty text](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/Tratado_de_Madrid_entre_Espa%C3%B1a_y_Portugal_%281750%29)

Resistance arose from attachment to land, distrust of Portuguese slave-taking, the inadequacy of replacement lands and transport, the scale of herds and buildings that could not be moved, and Guaraní claims of prior royal service. Jesuits negotiated, delayed, obeyed selectively, or sympathized with resistance; evidence does not support a simple proposition that the order centrally planned the rebellion.

Named Guaraní leaders included:

- **Sepé/José Tiarajú**, an official of São Miguel, killed 7 February 1756;
- **Nicolás Ñeengirú/Nanguirú**, an Indigenous commander, killed at Caiboaté;
- numerous cabildo officers and caciques whose positions varied.

Combined Spanish and Portuguese forces defeated the resistance at **Caiboaté/Caibaté on 10 February 1756**. Contemporary and later figures often report approximately **1,500 Guaraní dead**, but exact casualty counting is uncertain and sometimes polemical. European losses were far smaller.

The treaty was annulled by the **Treaty of El Pardo in 1761**, and the Seven Peoples returned nominally to Spain. Physical destruction, dispersal, confiscation, and political suspicion were not thereby reversed.

## 7.7 1757–1767: reconstruction under suspicion

Mission communities returned or regrouped. Royal officials investigated Jesuit conduct and inventories. European polemics represented the reductions as a secret state controlling vast riches and armies.

The Seven Peoples crisis did not alone cause the worldwide anti-Jesuit measures, but it supplied evidence and imagery for ministers already seeking stronger royal control. Portugal expelled the Jesuits in 1759; France suppressed them in the 1760s; Spain followed in 1767.

## 8. End or Transformation

## 8.1 Proximate termination

Charles III’s Pragmatic Sanction ordered:

- expulsion of Jesuits from Spanish dominions;
- seizure of their temporal property;
- prohibition on unauthorized public dispute over the royal motives.

The king invoked grave causes reserved in the royal mind rather than publishing a judicial demonstration of mission-specific guilt. It is therefore inaccurate to state that a proved Jesuit conspiracy in Paraguay legally caused the expulsion. [1767 printed sanction](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3APragm%C3%A1tica_sancion_de_su_magestad_en_fuerza_de_ley_para_el_estra%C3%B1amiento_de_estos_Reynos_%C3%A1_los_Regulares_de_la_Compania%2C_ocupacion_de_sus_Temporalidades_%28IA_A10915002%29.pdf)

Governor **Francisco de Paula Bucareli y Ursúa** organized implementation in the Río de la Plata. Jesuits were arrested, inventories taken, and priests transported through Buenos Aires toward exile. Removal from remote towns extended into 1768.

## 8.2 Structural causes

Convergent causes included:

- Bourbon centralization and distrust of corporate autonomy;
- conflict over ecclesiastical property and royal sovereignty;
- Portuguese and Spanish border diplomacy;
- merchant and settler hostility to mission privileges;
- accusations of opaque finance and commercial monopoly;
- the political effect of the Guaraní War;
- wider European anti-Jesuit policy;
- the monarchy’s desire to control Indigenous labor, territory, herds, and trade more directly.

No surviving evidence demonstrates a single secret master plan.

## 8.3 Transfer of assets

The Crown inherited or seized:

- churches and religious objects;
- storehouses and workshops;
- communal fields and herds;
- boats, carts, tools, and weapons;
- accounts, correspondence, censuses, and property inventories.

Franciscans, Dominicans, Mercedarians, or secular clergy assumed pastoral functions, while Crown officials controlled temporal administration. The new system separated spiritual and economic authority more sharply and inserted salaried administrators.

This was not an instantaneous economic collapse. Julia Sarreal argues that post-expulsion decline resulted from interacting demographic, administrative, and market changes, while some Guaraní elites gained greater direct access to property and commerce. By about 1800 the former mission population had fallen to roughly one-third of its 1732 peak, but variation among towns was large. [Economic-history review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-economic-history/article/guarani-and-their-missions-a-socioeconomic-history-by-j-s-sarreal-julia-stanford-ca-stanford-university-press-2014-pp-xiii-335-6500-cloth/9DE161D10001BBFEE73BBB7903FCF229)

## 8.4 Long afterlife

Former mission towns and militia entered the struggles among Paraguay, Corrientes, Buenos Aires, Portugal/Brazil, Artigas’s federation, and Andrés Guacurarí. Campaigns in 1817–28 burned or emptied numerous communities.

Material remains survived unevenly:

- monumental ruins at San Ignacio Miní, Loreto, Santa Ana, Santa María la Mayor, São Miguel, Jesús, and Trinidad;
- active towns at Encarnación, San Ignacio Guazú, Santiago, Santa Rosa, Santo Tomé, Yapeyú, São Borja, São Luiz Gonzaga, and Santo Ângelo;
- sculpture, bells, manuscripts, maps, printed books, instruments, and liturgical furnishings in museums, churches, and archives;
- land-use boundaries, place-names, road alignments, orchards, and archaeological deposits;
- Guaraní Catholic confraternities, musical traditions, surnames, and political memories, though direct continuity must be established locally rather than assumed.

Seven principal sites are now inscribed in two UNESCO World Heritage properties. [Paraguay UNESCO property](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/648)

## 9. Contested and Legendary Aspects

## 9.1 The independent “Jesuit state”

**Story:** the Jesuits created an autonomous or sovereign empire concealed inside Spanish America.

**Documented basis:** centralized clerical correspondence, territorial concentration, extensive communal property, armed militia, linguistic separation, and restricted settler access gave the system unusual corporate coherence.

**Evidence against the strong version:** towns paid tribute or substituted royal service; governors, bishops, audiencias, and royal inspectors asserted jurisdiction; militia fought for the Crown; Jesuits petitioned royal officials for privileges; Charles III could remove them without a war against an independent state.

**Finding:** “state” may function as a sociological analogy but is false as a statement of recognized sovereignty.

## 9.2 Socialist or communist utopia

**Story:** common property eliminated money, inequality, and exploitation.

**Documented basis:** communal land and herds, organized redistribution, support for dependents, and limited direct wage labor.

**Contrary evidence:** household property existed; caciques and officers enjoyed status distinctions; punishment and compulsory labor existed; external trade used prices, credit, and money; skilled workers could receive bonuses; Jesuit procurators controlled surplus and information.

**Finding:** “communism” is an anachronistic analogy. The economy combined household possession, corporate property, tribute, compulsory labor, redistribution, and market exchange.

## 9.3 Total Indigenous enslavement by Jesuits

**Story:** reductions were simply slave plantations under another name.

**Documented basis:** residence and labor were regulated; communal labor was compulsory; punishment, forced recruitment, and capture occurred; residents were incorporated into colonial extraction and military service.

**Evidence against legal equivalence:** mission Guaraní were Crown vassals and tributaries, not normally saleable chattel; communities maintained cabildos, families, property, and legal petitions; missions served as refuges from Paulista enslavement and some encomienda demands.

**Finding:** “slavery” inaccurately describes the ordinary legal status, while “voluntary utopia” suppresses documented coercion.

## 9.4 Hidden Jesuit treasure

**Story:** expelled Jesuits buried gold, silver, or sacred treasure beneath churches, tunnels, and roads.

**Origins and circulation:** confiscation rumors, incomplete inventories, later ruin-hunting, and nineteenth- and twentieth-century treasure literature.

**Evidence:** missions possessed silver liturgical objects and commercial stocks; administrators had opportunities to conceal movable property. No evidence establishes a coordinated buried treasury. Much wealth consisted of cattle, crops, buildings, tools, labor claims, and receivables—not portable bullion.

**Finding:** isolated concealment is possible; a network-wide treasure is legend.

## 9.5 Tunnels linking reductions

Subterranean drains, foundations, crypts, quarries, and storage spaces exist at particular sites. Claims of tunnels connecting towns separated by tens or hundreds of kilometers lack archaeological support. The legend converts real local underground features into an impossible regional transport network.

## 9.6 Nicolás I, King of Paraguay

**Story:** a Jesuit-controlled monarch, “Nicolás I,” ruled Paraguay or the Guaraní empire.

A French polemic, *Histoire de Nicolas I, Roy du Paraguai et Empereur des Mamalus*, circulated in several European languages during the 1750s. It drew on the real name of Guaraní commander Nicolás Ñeengirú and on wartime rumor. Later testimony identified a Nicolás Ñeengirú as an ordinary mission figure rather than a king. [Modern review of editions](https://www.hoy.com.py/especiales/2024/10/20/mito-leyenda-o-burla-nicolas-i-el-rey-jesuita-del-paraguay)

**Finding:** propaganda, not evidence of monarchy. Its historical importance lies in how European publics imagined Jesuit power.

## 9.7 “This land has owners”

The phrase associated with Sepé Tiarajú—Portuguese *Esta terra tem dono!*—expresses the well-documented substance of Guaraní land claims. Its exact wording and attribution in the form now commemorated are not securely established from a contemporaneous verbatim record. It belongs to political memory and regional identity.

## 9.8 The Mission as depicted in film

Roland Joffé’s *The Mission* (1986) combines historical elements from Mbororé, the Seven Peoples evacuation, Jesuit debates, and the Guaraní War. Its waterfall mission, compressed chronology, character arcs, and climactic massacre are dramatic constructions. The historical Seven Peoples were established plains-and-river towns with large built landscapes, not a single newly converted jungle community.

## 10. Historiography and Representations

## 10.1 Primary-source traditions

Principal source families include:

- **Cartas Anuas:** internally generated provincial reports;
- local censuses, baptismal, marriage, and burial registers;
- mission account books and inventories;
- procurator correspondence and merchant obligations;
- royal decrees, treaty records, inspections, and judicial investigations;
- Jesuit chronicles by Ruiz de Montoya, Pedro Lozano, José Cardiel, Antonio Sepp, and others;
- anti-Jesuit pamphlets and Portuguese/Spanish military reports;
- Guaraní-language letters and cabildo petitions;
- post-expulsion inventories and administrative reports;
- maps, architectural plans, archaeology, sculpture, and surviving books.

Biases differ. Jesuit relations emphasized conversion, martyrdom, order, and providence; settler critics emphasized illicit wealth and clerical tyranny; royal inventories sought administrable assets; Guaraní petitions adopted imperial legal formulas strategically.

## 10.2 Archives and losses

Major holdings are dispersed among:

- Archivo General de Indias, Seville;
- Archivo General de la Nación Argentina, Buenos Aires;
- Archivo Nacional de Asunción;
- Jesuit Roman Archives;
- Brazilian national and regional archives;
- provincial and diocesan archives in Córdoba, Corrientes, Misiones, Buenos Aires, and Paraguay;
- Biblioteca Nacional de España and European rare-book collections;
- mission-site museums and archaeological repositories.

Losses resulted from hurried expulsion, administrative dispersal, humidity, insects, fires, nineteenth-century warfare, looting, art-market removal, and weak provenance recording. Consequently, system-wide finance cannot be reconstructed as a continuous consolidated balance sheet.

## 10.3 Major debates

### Indigenous agency

Older histories alternated between heroic Jesuit civilizers and clerical despots. Recent scholarship reconstructs Guaraní entry, flight, officeholding, writing, militia leadership, production, and land claims as central.

### Demography

Massimo Livi-Bacci and Ernesto Maeder emphasized aggregate demographic patterns; Robert H. Jackson argues that aggregation obscures large regional differences and underuses tribute censuses. The 1732 maximum is broadly accepted; mechanisms and local mortality rates remain debated. [Jackson bibliographic summary](https://jesuitonlinebibliography.bc.edu/catalog/10833)

### Economic efficiency

Older apologetic literature treated collective organization as exceptionally efficient. Julia Sarreal argues that prosperity depended on subsidies and privileges as well as labor and scale, and that Guaraní private initiative was greater than Jesuit descriptions admitted. [Oxford](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61800/chapter-abstract/546345395)

### Jesuit responsibility for the Guaraní War

Portuguese and Bourbon polemics blamed Jesuit conspiracy. Modern work distinguishes local clerical sympathy, obstruction or negotiation from proof of a centrally ordered uprising. Guaraní cabildos articulated their own territorial and royal-service claims.

### Cultural hybridity

“Jesuit baroque” and “Guaraní baroque” describe genuinely collaborative production but can hide asymmetric power. Designs, saints, and liturgical functions were Catholic; execution, materials, motifs, and workshop transmission depended heavily on Indigenous makers.

## 12. Sources: Assessment and Research Conclusions

### 12.1 What can be established securely

The documentary and material record securely establishes:

1. a transregional network of thirty mature Guaraní towns;
2. earlier, larger but unstable fronts in Guairá, Itatín, and Tapé;
3. common Jesuit command and correspondence structures;
4. Indigenous cabildos and cacique organization;
5. communal and household production operating together;
6. extensive riverine and terrestrial movement;
7. large-scale yerba, livestock, textile, and hide production;
8. commercial procurement through Asunción, Santa Fe, Buenos Aires, and Córdoba;
9. compulsory communal labor and discipline;
10. refuge from, and warfare against, Paulista enslavement;
11. militia service for the Spanish Crown;
12. selective Indigenous literacy, publishing, music, science, and skilled manufacture;
13. recurrent epidemics amplified by settlement concentration and mobility;
14. the 1750 territorial crisis and autonomous Guaraní resistance;
15. expulsion by a general act of Bourbon policy rather than dissolution of the towns themselves.

### 12.2 What cannot be established precisely

The following remain unresolved or quantifiable only episodically:

- the exact population captured in Paulista raids;
- total annual commercial output for the whole 1609–1767 period;
- consolidated institutional profits;
- the proportion of labor volunteered, negotiated, evaded, or compelled;
- system-wide freight costs and average travel times;
- the degree of unrecorded private Guaraní trade;
- exact casualty totals at Mbororé and Caiboaté;
- responsibility of particular Jesuits for organizing resistance in 1753–56;
- the complete location of every early mission site and road;
- the amount of property hidden or removed before confiscation.

### 12.3 Overall reconstruction

The reductions were a network for concentrating and governing people, but concentration also made collective defense, food storage, specialist production, literacy, and demographic recovery possible. The same river-and-road system that moved yerba and orders moved armies and pathogens. The same militia that protected communities from slave raiders supplied compulsory service to the Crown and ultimately resisted the Crown’s territorial bargain. The same communal stores that supported widows and famine relief accumulated surplus under opaque clerical accounting. The same Guaraní-language regime that enabled continentally notable publishing and administration served conversion and surveillance.

The network ended as a Jesuit corporation in 1767–68. It did not disappear then. Its towns, land claims, cabildos, herds, militia traditions, religious art, and transport geography were redistributed among Indigenous communities, Crown officials, replacement clergy, merchants, armies, and emerging states. Its later fragmentation was a second history, produced by administrative reform, epidemic mortality, migration, commercial restructuring, and nineteenth-century warfare.
