# Comet Line: Network Dossier

## Scope, terminology, and evidentiary conventions

This dossier treats the Comet Line not simply as an organization but as a changing transport, shelter, document, intelligence, and finance system. Its principal function was to collect Allied servicemen—especially aircrew evading capture—in occupied Belgium and northern France; shelter, authenticate, disguise, and guide them through France; take them clandestinely into Spain; and deliver them to British diplomatic and intelligence personnel for repatriation through Gibraltar. In 1944 the same surviving personnel and connections were partly transformed into Operation or Mission Marathon, which concealed airmen in forest camps pending liberation.

The stated period, 1941–1944, is defensible but conceals several possible boundaries:

- **Spring–June 1941:** Andrée de Jongh, Arnold Deppé, Jacques Donny, Henri Debliqui, and associates began assembling a Belgian escape organization.
- **July 1941:** its first attempted southbound convoy left Belgium; the expedition failed as an end-to-end evacuation.
- **August–September 1941:** de Jongh reached Spain, established contact with Michael Creswell of the British embassy/MI9 apparatus, and secured British financial cooperation. This created the durable Belgium–Spain–Gibraltar system.
- **January 1943:** de Jongh was arrested. A memorial convention says that the earlier *Ligne Dédée* was thereafter called *Comète*, but contemporary usage was not necessarily uniform.
- **4 June 1944:** usually identified as the last conventional Comet passage toward Spain.
- **August–September 1944:** Marathon camps and surviving branches were liberated; Elvire De Greef accompanied four airmen from liberated Biarritz to Britain on 28 September. This is a plausible operational endpoint.
- **Postwar:** Comète became a recognized Belgian wartime service, veterans’ kinship, commemorative community, archival corpus, and memorial route.

### Evidentiary labels

- **Documented:** supported by contemporary records, named archival files, the surviving passage notebooks, individual evasion reports, or mutually corroborating testimony.
- **Well supported:** repeated in specialist reconstructions tied to identifiable records, although the underlying document has not been inspected here.
- **Plausible inference:** consistent with the system and evidence but not expressly recorded.
- **Disputed:** materially incompatible versions survive.
- **Unverified:** repeated claim whose underlying evidence is presently unclear.
- **Memorial figure:** a total compiled by a veterans’ or remembrance association; potentially valuable but not equivalent to a closed contemporary statistical return.
- **Legend/romanticization:** a later simplification or symbolic narrative, whether or not based on a factual core.

## 1. Identification and Definition

### Names

The network’s principal names were:

- **French:** *Ligne Dédée*, *Ligne Andrée*, *Ligne Comète*, *réseau Comète*, and sometimes *service Comète*.
- **English:** *Comet Line*, *Comète Line*, *Dédée Line*, and *escape-and-evasion line*.
- **Spanish:** *Red Comète* or *línea Comète*.
- **Dutch:** *Comète-lijn* and, in translations, *Komeetlijn*. The French proper name normally remained in use in bilingual Belgian records.
- **Operational language:** members commonly spoke of a *ligne* or *filière*, emphasizing a chain of relays rather than a mass political movement.

The remembrance association states that the organization first operated as *Ligne Dédée* and adopted *Comète* after Andrée de Jongh’s January 1943 arrest for security reasons. This is a significant internal tradition, but it should not be turned into an exact universal renaming date without examining surviving contemporary correspondence: wartime organizations often accumulated overlapping names, pseudonyms, and retrospective service titles. [Ligne Comète Line–Remembrance](https://www.cometeline.org/index.html)

The network was not:

- a single marked trail;
- a centrally staffed corridor in which every helper knew the whole route;
- a branch wholly owned or commanded by MI9;
- identical with all Belgian assistance to airmen;
- identical with the Pat O’Leary Line, Possum, Shelburne, Bourgogne, Dutch-Paris, or other escape systems;
- primarily an armed sabotage organization.

It was a compartmented, volunteer-operated relay network whose successive leadership groups tried to preserve a through connection. MI9 financed, advised, authenticated, and received its traffic, but Comète retained a substantial degree of operational autonomy. The Comète remembrance organization describes the parties as cooperating while remaining independent from 1941 to 1944. [Ligne Comète Line–Remembrance](https://www.cometeline.org/)

### Media carried

Comète moved or transmitted:

1. **People:** RAF, RCAF, RAAF, RNZAF, Polish, American, and other Allied aircrew; escaped servicemen and prisoners; Belgian and French volunteers or “burned” resistance personnel; couriers and agents.
2. **Documents:** false identity cards, work papers, ration documents, photographs, railway tickets, hotel or police papers, and British debriefing material.
3. **Money and credit:** Belgian and French currency for food, rent, tickets, guides, documents, bribes, and emergency costs; reimbursement credits calculated partly by successfully delivered evaders.
4. **Information:** downed-aircraft news, safe-house addresses, passwords, convoy schedules, police conditions, arrests, intelligence reports, warnings, and instructions from Madrid or London.
5. **Material:** civilian clothes, blue workmen’s clothing, shoes or rope-soled *espadrilles*, walking sticks, food, ration coupons, medical assistance, maps, photographs, and later tents and camp supplies.
6. **Trust:** personal introductions, family ties, church, professional, social, and prewar commercial relationships substituted for a formal membership bureaucracy.

The three small surviving Comète passage notebooks are particularly important. According to the association maintaining the reconstructed rolls, they were begun after de Jongh’s agreement with Creswell and recorded successful Pyrenean passages as evidence for British reimbursement. Their existence makes the 288-person south-route list substantially firmer than broader claims about everybody assisted somewhere in the network. [Comète evader register](https://www.cometeline.org/cometaviateurpasse.html) [Transcribed list of 288 military evaders](https://airforceescape.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/List-of-Comet-evaders-1941-1944.pdf)

### Legal and political standing

Comète had no lawful charter under German occupation, Vichy law, or Francoist Spanish border law. Its actions involved harboring enemy servicemen, falsifying documents, currency and ration violations, illegal frontier crossing, and resistance to German authority. German military and security courts could and did impose death sentences.

Its positive institutional standing was initially informal:

- Belgian patriotic initiative in occupied territory;
- recognition and funding through Michael Creswell and the British diplomatic/MI9 structure in Spain;
- later coordination with IS9/MI9 and the Belgian State Security in London;
- postwar recognition of qualifying participants as resistance or intelligence-and-action agents through Belgian administrative procedures.

Recognition as a postwar agent and actual wartime participation are not identical categories. The remembrance register distinguishes people with official files from “satellites” known to have assisted but lacking formal recognition. [Helper-register methodology](https://www.cometeline.org/LISTE-HELPERS-INTRO.htm)

### Geographic extent

At maximum conventional extent, the operating chain linked:

- aircraft-loss sites and first helpers in Belgium, the Netherlands, and northern France;
- Brussels, Liège, and provincial feeder cells;
- crossings near the Belgian–French frontier;
- Lille, Valenciennes, and railway connections to Paris;
- Paris safe houses and interchange points;
- Bordeaux, Dax, Bayonne, Anglet, Biarritz, Ciboure, Saint-Jean-de-Luz, Urrugne, Sutar, Larressore, Espelette, Itxassou, Bidarray, and other southern relays;
- Pyrenean or Bidasoa crossings;
- Oiartzun, Rentería, San Sebastián, and Bilbao;
- British diplomatic transport through Madrid to Gibraltar;
- thence by sea or air to Britain.

The line was therefore better understood as branching collection webs feeding a comparatively narrow trunk than as one uniform route. Its southern passages also changed after arrests and intensified controls. The often-mapped Brussels–Paris–Bayonne–Spain “line” is a valid schematic, but no single itinerary describes all journeys. [Classic Saint-Jean-de-Luz passage reconstruction](https://www.evasioncomete.be/TxtPassClassic.html) [Basque-country survey](https://aunamendi.eusko-ikaskuntza.eus/es/la-red-comete-en-el-pais-vasco/ar-154381/)

## 2. Formation

### Conditions of emergence

The Belgian capitulation of 28 May 1940 left British and other Allied soldiers stranded, prisoners moving eastward, and later an increasing number of aircrew coming down during operations over Belgium and northern France. Local civilians initially provided food, civilian clothing, concealment, or short-distance guidance. These acts did not automatically constitute Comète: the network emerged when helpers began joining local assistance into a repeatable end-to-end evacuation mechanism.

Several conditions made that possible:

- dense Belgian and French rail systems;
- urban anonymity in Brussels and Paris;
- prewar Belgian migration and business connections in southwestern France;
- experienced smuggling communities along the Franco-Spanish border;
- neutral Spain’s diplomatic presence and the British embassy/consular network;
- MI9’s demand for the return of trained aircrew;
- false-document skills and black-market access;
- family and friendship networks capable of absorbing arrests.

### Founders

**Andrée de Jongh**, born in 1916 and trained as a commercial artist and nurse, was the principal founder, organizer, convoy leader, and negotiator with the British. Her aliases included **Dédée** and, in British-related communication, **Postman**.

**Arnold Deppé**, who knew southwestern France from prewar residence or employment, supplied essential geographic and personal connections.

**Baron Jacques Donny** served as an early treasurer and financier.

**Henri Debliqui** and other early Brussels helpers participated in collecting and sheltering fugitives.

**Frédéric de Jongh**, Andrée’s father, subsequently directed or coordinated the Paris sector and, after his daughter’s arrest, held general leadership responsibility until his own arrest.

The system was not created in a single constitutive meeting. “Founded in June 1941” is a convenient retrospective date; the more precise reconstruction is a sequence: organization in spring or early summer, initial convoys in summer, and durable British-supported operation after de Jongh reached Spain.

### The failed first attempt

The first southbound experiment involved Belgian volunteers or servicemen whom the founders hoped to send toward Britain. Participants reached southern France or Spain, but Spanish authorities detained many; some were held at Miranda de Ebro, and the attempt did not yet create a secure handoff. The failure demonstrated that merely crossing the frontier was insufficient. A successful line required diplomatic reception, proof of identity, money, and a protected onward channel.

Sources disagree on the exact composition and counting of the earliest attempts. The remembrance reconstruction separates **15 persons in the first and second passages** from the later main register. That classification should be retained rather than silently adding all early travelers to the 288 authenticated military passages. [Comète evader register](https://www.cometeline.org/cometaviateurpasse.html)

### Agreement with Michael Creswell

In August or September 1941, de Jongh made her way into Spain and approached the British. Michael Creswell—British diplomat and MI9 representative, using names including **Timothy** and **Monday**—initially had reason to suspect provocation: an unknown young Belgian woman was claiming control of a cross-occupation escape system.

After verification, the British agreed to receive aircrew and reimburse expenses. RAF aircrew were to receive priority because trained airmen could be returned to operations. The agreement was oral and operational, not a public charter or British annexation of Comète. Thereafter:

1. Comète delivered authenticated fugitives.
2. British representatives took custody in Spain.
3. Creswell supplied funds and instructions.
4. Passage records supported accounting and verification.
5. De Jongh or another returning courier carried money and messages north.

The remembrance tradition dates the decisive agreement to **September 1941 in Bilbao**; other summaries loosely say August. September is the better documented organizational threshold, but the discrepancy may represent first contact versus settled arrangement rather than a substantive contradiction. [Passage-notebook account](https://www.cometeline.org/cometaviateurpasse.html) [Comète institutional history](https://www.cometeline.org/)

### Predecessors and analogues

The closest Belgian moral precedent was the First World War **Edith Cavell network**, which assisted Allied soldiers through occupied Belgium and the Netherlands. There is no evidence that Comète was its direct institutional continuation, but participants grew up within a Belgian memory culture that treated civilian escape assistance as patriotic resistance.

During the Second World War, Comète overlapped geographically and functionally with other lines, especially Pat O’Leary, Bourgogne, Possum, Shelburne, and Dutch-Paris. Transfers between organizations occurred when routes broke or particular cells had better access. Later retrospective maps risk making these organizations appear as parallel railway companies with fixed territories; their actual boundaries were permeable, personal, and crisis-driven.

## 3. Constituent Nodes

A “node” might be one apartment used once, a recurring safe house, a railway station, a family group, an entire city sector, a mountain farm, a consulate, or a forest camp. Addresses changed after arrests, so the following is a structural survey rather than an exhaustive safe-house directory.

### A. Collection zones

#### Brussels

**Status:** principal northern command, finance, documentation, shelter, and sorting center, 1941–early 1944.

Airmen collected in provincial districts were moved into Brussels apartments and houses. There they were concealed, questioned, photographed, clothed, assigned false identities, and grouped for movement south. Major organizers at different periods included Andrée de Jongh, Jacques Donny, Jean Greindl, Antoine d’Ursel, Yvon Michiels, Jules Dricot, Gaston Matthys, Micheline Dumon, Aline Dumon, and their associated cells.

Relevant German institutions included:

- Saint-Gilles Prison;
- German military courts;
- Geheime Feldpolizei premises;
- Luftwaffe police facilities;
- the Gestapo/Sipo-SD installation at 453 Avenue Louise.

The remembrance site identifies Rue Traversière 6 as a GFP headquarters, Avenue Charles Legrelle as a Luftwaffe-police site, and the Palace Hotel at Place Rogier as a requisitioned military-court venue. These specific site attributions derive from memorial research and should be checked against occupation directories for address-level legal precision. [Comète places inventory](https://www.cometeline.org/cometLieux.htm)

#### Liège, Limburg, Hainaut, Brabant, and other Belgian feeder areas

**Status:** decentralized aircrew-recovery, shelter, and courier zones.

Aircraft came down across a wide territory. Farmers, clergy, doctors, railway workers, police sympathizers, students, householders, and members of other resistance groups moved survivors toward Brussels or directly into a southbound link. These peripheral helpers often knew only the preceding and next contact.

The system’s scale cannot be inferred merely by counting formally enrolled agents. One airman could require successive aid from the finder, medical helper, clothes provider, photographer, forger, ration supplier, safe-house family, local courier, railway guide, frontier guide, Paris host, southern courier, and Basque guide.

### B. Belgian–French border belt

#### Hertain, Tournai, Rumes, Erquennes, Beaumont, Valenciennes, Lille

**Status:** variable crossing points and rail-transfer nodes.

The Belgian–French frontier was crossed on foot, by local tram, bicycle, or with guides who knew fields, tracks, and patrol patterns. **Henriette Hanotte**, alias **Monique**, is credited by the Comète association with guiding more than 100 airmen across this zone; one memorial account gives more than 135. That total is plausible and tied to reconstructed cases, but it remains a retrospective individual count.

**Charles Morelle** at Valenciennes and associated households formed an early relay. From Lille or Valenciennes, parties could take trains to Paris. [Comète border commemoration account](https://www.cometeline.org/cometnews.htm)

### C. Paris sector

**Status:** indispensable interchange and replenishment hub, 1941–1944; repeatedly destroyed and rebuilt.

Paris provided:

- railway arrival and departure points;
- temporary accommodation;
- identity-document production;
- clothing and food;
- guides for the next train;
- connections with French helpers and other networks;
- leadership contact between Belgian and southern sectors.

Key operators included **Frédéric de Jongh**, **Robert and Germaine Aylé**, **Georges and Virginia d’Albert-Lake**, **Jacques Le Grelle**, **Jean de Blommaert**, and numerous householders and couriers.

Paris was also a surveillance chokepoint. Stations, ticket checks, identity controls, curfews, hotel registers, and infiltrators made the city both useful and dangerous. Arrest of a courier who knew multiple addresses could expose several cells.

### D. Southwest France

#### Bordeaux and Dax

**Status:** rail relays and occasional staging points.

Convoys did not always go directly from Paris to Bayonne. Bordeaux and Dax supplied railway changes, waiting points, and alternative contacts. Route selection varied with arrests and policing.

#### Bayonne

**Status:** southern urban interchange.

The **Dassié** and **Lapeyre** families and associated helpers provided reception, lodging, transport, and links to Anglet and the coast. Arrests in March 1943 severely damaged this node.

#### Anglet

**Status:** southern command and financial center.

The household of **Elvire De Greef**, alias **Tante Go**, and **Fernand De Greef** became one of Comète’s most durable hubs. Their children **Freddy** and **Janine/Jeanine** assisted; Janine, about sixteen when she began major courier work, accompanied airmen on long-distance train journeys.

Anglet was not merely a safe house. Elvire De Greef managed money, schedules, southern personnel, relations with Creswell, and recovery after arrests. The passage notebooks were ultimately concealed in her custody according to the postwar reconstruction. [Comète evader register](https://www.cometeline.org/cometaviateurpasse.html)

#### Biarritz, Ciboure, Saint-Jean-de-Luz

**Status:** coastal relays before the classical Urrugne–Bidasoa passage.

**Kattalin Aguirre** was a central Basque helper in the Saint-Jean-de-Luz/Ciboure area. Parties received food, rested, changed clothing, and waited for the mountain guide or favorable conditions.

### E. Frontier farms and trails

#### Urrugne and Bidegain Berri

**Status:** final French safe-house belt on the classical route.

The farm **Bidegain Berri**, occupied by **Frantxia Usandizaga/Haltzuet**, became famous because de Jongh and three airmen were arrested there on 15 January 1943. Other Urrugne farms had served similar relay functions. Later commemoration has concentrated memory on the arrest site, making it appear more singular than the historical relay system actually was.

The walk from Saint-Jean-de-Luz or Ciboure to an Urrugne farm could take about two hours. Evaders received food, rope-soled shoes, sticks, and working clothes before the harder stage. [Saint-Jean-de-Luz passage reconstruction](https://www.evasioncomete.be/TxtPassClassic.html)

#### Bidasoa, Endarlatsa/Endarlaza, San Miguel, Pagogaña, Erlaitz, Oiartzun

**Status:** frontier and first Spanish mountain nodes.

The classical passage crossed or followed difficult hills and the Bidasoa frontier. One reconstructed itinerary crossed near the former San Miguel railway station, climbed toward Pagogaña and Erlaitz, and descended toward Oiartzun. Routes varied with water level, patrols, weather, and guide preference. [Auñamendi Basque survey](https://aunamendi.eusko-ikaskuntza.eus/es/la-red-comete-en-el-pais-vasco/ar-154381/)

#### Alternative inland routes

After the January 1943 arrests, Comète used or reconnoitered inland passages through:

- **Bidarray**;
- **Itxassou**;
- **Larressore**;
- **Espelette**;
- **Souraïde**;
- associated farms and restaurants, including the **Larre restaurant at Sutar**.

Guides included **Martin Orhatégaray**, **Pierre Etchégoyen**, **Pierre and Baptiste Aguerre**, and **Jean Elizondo**. Some routes were used only once or a few times. Therefore a modern “Comet trail” normally represents one documented variant, not the network’s entire frontier geography. [Alternative-passage reconstruction](https://www.cometeline.org/PassagesNouveaux.html)

### F. Spanish diplomatic chain

#### Oiartzun and San Sebastián

**Status:** reception, hiding, and transfer.

Spanish Basque helpers sheltered parties after the crossing and notified British contacts. British diplomatic vehicles or intermediaries collected authenticated airmen. Spanish police or the Guardia Civil remained a danger: unauthorized entrants could be arrested, held, or sent to Miranda de Ebro.

#### Bilbao

**Status:** original British-contact and accounting node.

De Jongh met Creswell here; the British consular connection authenticated deliveries, transferred money and messages, and arranged onward movement.

#### Madrid and Gibraltar

**Status:** diplomatic control and repatriation.

Aircrew were debriefed and moved under British protection from Spain to Gibraltar, then transported to Britain. Some evaders went through Portugal, but the standard Comète narrative proceeds through Gibraltar. Once in Allied custody, they ceased to be Comète’s operational responsibility.

### G. Marathon nodes, 1944

#### Fréteval Forest, Bellande/Villebout, Cloyes-sur-le-Loir

Two concealed camps collectively code-named **Sherwood** were organized near Fréteval. **Jean de Blommaert**, **Lucien Boussa**, Philippe and Virginia d’Albert-Lake, local guides, farmers, bakers, and merchants supported them. The first designated airmen traveled from Paris toward Châteaudun on 20 May 1944 and then walked or were guided toward Cloyes.

The best-supported total is approximately **152–156 airmen sheltered at Fréteval**. The difference probably reflects roster boundaries rather than a fundamentally different event. [Operation Marathon overview](https://www.evasioncomete.be/TxtBilan2.html)

#### Belgian Ardennes

Six frequently named camp areas were:

- Beffe;
- Villance;
- Acremont;
- Porcheresse;
- Bellevaux;
- Bohan.

They were intended as small, dispersed camps, often conceived for about twenty to twenty-five men each. **Albert Ancia**, **Gaston Matthys**, local organizers, and supply personnel established them. The balance reconstruction gives **93 evaders** in the Belgian camps; other summaries give 109 repatriated or approximately 150 in all non-Fréteval locations, depending on which projects and holding areas are counted.

The Beffe camp, housed partly in a former school-colony facility, reportedly received about thirty men. Inadequate leadership, insufficient supplies and money, and German searches forced evacuation toward Villance; four airmen were captured. These details demonstrate that Marathon was not uniformly secure or well supplied. [Comète fourth-period organization](https://www.cometeline.org/cometorg5-2.htm)

## 4. Connections

### Northern collection edges

A downed aircraft produced no automatic connection to Comète. Survivors first had to evade German searches and make contact with a trustworthy civilian. The connection often ran:

`crash site → farmer or local household → local resistance contact → provincial safe house → courier → Brussels`

What traveled north-to-south was principally the airman. What traveled in both directions included warnings, identity details, money, photographs, ration coupons, and instructions. Aircrew sometimes passed through several unrelated helpers before a Comète agent accepted them.

### Brussels–frontier edge

Couriers moved individuals or small groups by tram, bicycle, local train, and foot. The objective was to cross the Belgian–French frontier without using conspicuous official control points. Guides returned north after handing the party to a French relay.

Frequency cannot be reduced to a timetable. Convoys depended on aircraft losses, available guides, false papers, safe houses, railway conditions, arrests, and the southern sector’s capacity. A through-line could be operational while a specific feeder branch was inactive.

### Frontier–Paris edge

From Lille or Valenciennes, parties usually traveled by rail to Paris under civilian identities. Couriers instructed evaders how to behave, what names to use, and often forbade conversation in English. The airmen were passed to a Paris contact at a station, café, church, street corner, or safe house.

The most dangerous moments included:

- ticket purchase;
- identity checks;
- speaking or reacting incorrectly;
- railway delays or bomb damage;
- a guide being recognized;
- arrival to find that a contact had been arrested;
- an airman carrying military objects or incorrect papers.

### Paris–southwest edge

Paris-to-Bayonne was the trunk’s longest regular rail movement. Depending on controls, parties traveled through Bordeaux and Dax. The route became increasingly difficult after Germany occupied the former unoccupied zone in November 1942 and as Allied bombing damaged rail communications in 1944.

Journey times varied from roughly a day of rail movement to several days or weeks if parties had to wait in safe houses. No universal Brussels-to-Gibraltar duration can be given. Individual evasion reports show that the complete interval from aircraft loss to Allied custody could range from days to months.

### Pyrenean edge

The final clandestine stage generally proceeded at night in small groups. It included:

1. assembly in the coastal or inland Basque district;
2. change into suitable clothing;
3. removal or concealment of incriminating papers;
4. approach to an Urrugne or inland farm;
5. several hours of steep walking;
6. crossing the Bidasoa or a mountain frontier;
7. descent to a Spanish reception point;
8. concealment until British collection.

Risks included floodwater, hypothermia, exhaustion, falls, dogs, German and French patrols, Spanish guards, informers, and guides losing contact. **Antoine d’Ursel**, alias **Jacques Cartier**, drowned in the Bidasoa on 24 December 1943 during an attempted crossing—a documented demonstration that the landscape itself was lethal.

### Spain–Gibraltar–Britain edge

Once British personnel accepted a party, Comète received credit for successful delivery. Airmen were transported diplomatically to Madrid and Gibraltar and then returned to Britain. British debriefing generated **escape-and-evasion reports**, now concentrated in the UK National Archives’ WO 208 series. [UK National Archives, WO 208/3324 example](https://discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk/details/r/D7319011) [National Archives guide to helper records](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/indexing-helpers-of-allied-pows/)

### Reverse traffic

The line was not purely southbound. Returning organizers and couriers carried:

- money from Creswell;
- British instructions;
- authentication information;
- warnings;
- intelligence mail;
- news of successful arrival.

De Jongh’s British nickname **Postman**, and the reported usage of “letters” for mail and “parcels” for evaders, show how communications and human transport were deliberately blended in cover vocabulary. This terminology is well attested in secondary and commemorative accounts but should not be assumed to have been universal throughout the network.

## 5. Agents and Operators

### Central leadership succession

#### Andrée de Jongh (“Dédée,” “Postman”)

Founder, system designer, recruiter, British liaison, financial courier, and repeated international guide. She was arrested at Urrugne on 15 January 1943, imprisoned at Bayonne and Fresnes, interrogated repeatedly, and deported under Nacht und Nebel procedures to Ravensbrück and later Mauthausen. She survived.

Claims that she personally conducted **118 airmen to freedom** appear in her U.S. Medal of Freedom citation. A tighter reconstruction says approximately 118 people passed through the line before her arrest and that she personally accompanied about 78 airmen on roughly 33 successful crossings. The citation is authoritative evidence of Allied recognition but not necessarily a statistically exact passenger ledger. [De Jongh citation and biography](https://www.cometeline.org/cometorgnameDeJonghAndree.htm)

#### Arnold Deppé

Cofounder and southwest-France specialist. His arrest following the early 1941 journeys removed an important organizer and forced de Jongh to proceed more cautiously. Sources vary over whether his decisive arrest is dated August or December 1941; this may reflect different detention episodes or imprecise retelling and remains to be checked against his individual Belgian file.

#### Jacques Donny

Early treasurer and financial organizer. Arrested at home on 1 December 1942 following the late-1942 penetrations; condemned to death on 10 October 1943 and executed at Stuttgart on 24 February 1944. These dates are well supported in specialist chronologies.

#### Frédéric de Jongh (“Paul”)

Andrée’s father and Paris organizer. After her arrest he acted as overall head while southern crossings were managed by Nothomb. He was arrested with Robert and Germaine Aylé in Paris in June 1943, sentenced by a German military court, and shot at Mont-Valérien on 28 March 1944.

#### Jean Greindl (“Nemo”)

Rebuilt and directed the Brussels sector. Arrested on 6 February 1943, reportedly at the Swedish Canteen on Rue Ducale. He died in Saint-Gilles Prison during the Allied air attack of 7 September 1943. His death illustrates a recurrent ambiguity in fatality totals: network members died by execution, maltreatment, disease, camp conditions, accidental border hazards, and Allied bombing.

#### Jean-François Nothomb (“Franco”)

Principal international guide and later overall controller after the 1943 leadership losses. On 24 January 1943 he crossed into Spain to warn Creswell of de Jongh’s arrest. He reopened southern movement in spring 1943 and used alternative passages. Arrested in Paris in January 1944 after penetration by Jacques Desoubrie, he survived imprisonment and deportation.

#### Antoine d’Ursel (“Jacques Cartier”)

Led the Belgian sector during 1943 and helped finance movement. He drowned crossing the Bidasoa on 24 December 1943.

#### Jacques Le Grelle (“Jérôme”)

Sent from Britain to direct or reinforce the Paris sector in mid-1943. Arrested on 17 January 1944 and survived imprisonment.

#### Micheline Dumon (“Michou,” “Lily”)

A young courier and organizer who restored broken northern and southern links, identified or warned against Desoubrie, and escaped to Britain. She was central to the system’s capacity to regenerate after repeated arrests.

#### Aline Dumon

Safe-house operator and courier; involved in the 1944 reconstruction and movement toward Spain when the renewed Paris project was compromised.

#### Yvon Michiels, Jules Dricot, Gaston Matthys

Important organizers of later Belgian structures. Matthys was central to the Belgian Marathon sector. Dricot was arrested and died in German captivity.

#### Jean de Blommaert

Belgian officer and clandestine agent parachuted back into France in April 1944. He helped reorganize sheltering and established the Fréteval camps.

### Southern command and guides

#### Elvire De Greef (“Tante Go”)

Southern organizer, accountant, British liaison, and logistician. Her importance was managerial rather than merely domestic: she maintained the Anglet hub through multiple northern collapses and participated in 1944 discussions with MI9.

#### Fernand, Freddy, and Janine De Greef

The family furnished housing, transport, courier work, and protection. Janine’s documented long-distance guiding at approximately sixteen years of age exemplifies the use of adolescents, who could attract less suspicion but faced the same imprisonment and execution risks as adults.

#### Florentino Goikoetxea

Basque smuggler, mountain guide, and refugee or opponent of Francoist authority. His route knowledge made him the most celebrated frontier specialist. He was wounded and captured in 1944 but rescued or recovered through action involving the De Greef group before liberation.

Calling him simply a “smuggler” should not be read as an allegation of exploitation: cross-border smuggling was a practical body of knowledge on which many wartime escape systems depended. Whether individual guides accepted payment varied; Comète’s southern operations did incur guide and provisioning expenses.

#### Kattalin Aguirre

Major Saint-Jean-de-Luz/Ciboure helper, lodging provider, and contact with Basque guides.

#### Frantxia Usandizaga/Haltzuet and Juan Manuel Larburu

Operators at Bidegain Berri. Arrested with de Jongh and three airmen in January 1943. Later accounts state that de Jongh survived whereas Usandizaga and Larburu died following deportation.

### Paris and French personnel

Robert and Germaine Aylé, the Dassié and Lapeyre families, Charles Morelle, Philippe and Virginia d’Albert-Lake, Georges Merleau-Ponty, and numerous others maintained apartments, documents, tickets, meals, clothing, and station transfers. A complete prosopography runs to hundreds or thousands of names and cannot safely be reconstructed from celebrated biographies alone.

### British institutions and officers

#### MI9 / IS9

The British organization responsible for escape-and-evasion support supplied money, policy, authentication, communications, training, and reception. Wartime nomenclature varies: MI9 was reorganized, and continental operations may appear under IS9 designations.

#### Michael Creswell (“Timothy,” “Monday”)

The diplomatic and intelligence hinge in Spain. He authenticated de Jongh’s proposal, received successful parties, reimbursed expenses, and passed directions north.

#### Airey Neave

Escaped British officer who later worked in MI9. In 1944 he helped conceive and administer the shift from immediate evacuation to forest concealment and personally reached the Fréteval camp with advancing Allied forces in August.

### German and collaborationist counter-network

#### German institutions

Comète was investigated and attacked by overlapping German organizations:

- Abwehr counterintelligence and its field offices;
- Geheime Feldpolizei;
- Luftwaffe police and air-force security organs concerned with missing aircrew;
- Sicherheitspolizei and SD/Gestapo;
- German military courts;
- prison and deportation administrations.

Attribution of an arrest simply to “the Gestapo” in memoirs may obscure which agency actually conducted surveillance, arrest, interrogation, or trial.

#### Prosper Dezitter (“Aal,” “Captain Jackson,” and other aliases)

A Belgian collaborator and professional infiltrator who used English, Canadian connections, stolen or invented identities, weapons offers, false safe houses, and supposed London contacts to gain trust. His organization targeted multiple resistance and escape networks, including Comète.

Belgium WWII, a project associated with CegeSoma/State Archives expertise, attributes **at least 700 arrests** to the Dezitter group. Other publications claim 1,000 or 1,500; those larger figures should be treated as disputed unless the victim set and counting rules are supplied. [Belgium WWII biography of Dezitter](https://www.belgiumwwii.be/belgique-en-guerre/personnes/dezitter-prosper.html)

#### Florentine Giralt/Dings

Dezitter’s partner and collaborator, using gendered expectations and social contacts to gain confidence and introduce targets.

#### Jacques Desoubrie

French or Belgian-born German agent operating under names including **Jean Masson**, **Pierre Boulain/Poulain**, and others. He offered guide services, penetrated Comète’s transport connections, and helped cause the 1943–44 arrests in Paris and Brussels. Claims that he caused the arrest of exactly **150 Comète airmen**, **250 helpers**, or **more than 1,000 persons across networks** circulate; all require careful definition. His documented role in the January 1944 collapse is secure, but many numerical attributions are retrospective aggregate estimates.

Desoubrie was captured after the war, tried in France, convicted, and executed in 1949. Dezitter was likewise convicted in Belgium and executed in 1948.

#### Victor Demets and other penetrators

Memorial histories attribute the early exposure of Arnold Deppé to Victor Demets. Because aliases and postwar denunciation cases are complex, this specific causal claim should be checked against Deppé’s and Demets’s judicial dossiers rather than treated as established solely by repetition.

## 6. Operation and Mechanism

### Intake

An airman reaching a Comète-associated household was not automatically trusted. Helpers sought:

- name, rank, service, and unit;
- aircraft type and crash circumstances;
- knowledge unlikely to be possessed by an infiltrator;
- identification tags or papers;
- corroboration from the first helper;
- later, authentication questions or descriptions supplied through Allied channels.

Screening was necessarily imperfect. German agents could possess documents taken from captured airmen and could learn service terminology. The network’s inability to conduct secure centralized vetting was a structural vulnerability, not simply individual carelessness.

### Shelter

Safe houses furnished beds or concealed sleeping places, washing, food, first aid, and changes of clothing. Household labor fell heavily on women, who queued for food, altered clothes, managed neighbors’ suspicions, and maintained normal appearances. Children and adolescents carried messages or acted as lookouts. The presence of an English-speaking stranger endangered the entire household.

### Documents

False papers required:

1. a suitable assumed identity;
2. a photograph;
3. blank or altered identity forms;
4. stamps, signatures, and occupational details;
5. a cover story consistent with accent and behavior.

Airmen might be portrayed as deaf-mute, ill, Flemish, French-speaking Belgian, or manual workers. The exact technique varied. Documentation did not eliminate risk: a convincing card could fail if the holder could not answer an ordinary question.

### Convoy discipline

Parties were kept small. Couriers might sit separately on trains so that one arrest did not immediately incriminate everyone. Evaders were instructed not to speak, stare at military personnel, or travel with conspicuous Allied possessions.

Compartmentation limited each helper’s knowledge but was incomplete. Leaders, guides, document specialists, and couriers necessarily knew multiple contacts. Repeated use of the same station, apartment, or guide created pattern risk.

### Finance

Early operations drew on founders, sympathetic households, and private donors, including Jacques Donny. Following the Creswell agreement, British funds became the system’s principal institutional support.

Expenditures included:

- rail and local-transport fares;
- safe-house food and rent;
- clothing and shoes;
- photographs and forged documents;
- ration coupons or black-market provisions;
- guide payments and travel expenses;
- emergency relocation;
- medical care;
- later, forest-camp food, tents, and equipment.

The passage notebooks served as delivery and reimbursement evidence. This was not a commercial fare charged to airmen. Helpers generally served without wages, but the network had unavoidable cash costs and sometimes paid specialists or reimbursed households.

No complete audited wartime account has been identified in the accessible sources. Exact claims for “cost per airman” should therefore be treated cautiously unless tied to a dated MI9 statement or De Greef account. Inflation, black-market prices, currency conversion, losses through arrest, and the distinction between advances and reimbursement make a single unit cost misleading.

### Communications

Comète used:

- personal couriers;
- verbal messages;
- aliases and war names;
- coded descriptions of people or mail;
- prearranged meetings;
- British diplomatic communications from Spain to London;
- BBC warnings in the broader counter-infiltration environment;
- written passage lists kept in exceptionally compact form.

Couriers were often preferred to radio because most cells lacked radio sets and transmissions could be located. Couriers were slower but could carry money and evaluate local conditions.

### Worked journey: the first authenticated aircrew crossing, November 1941

The surviving list identifies three Polish airmen—**Jerzy Tomicki, Edward Ives, and Antoni Kowalski**—as the first aircrew taken across the Pyrenees by the newly British-supported system on **10–11 November 1941**. Tomicki was associated with Polish 305 Squadron and Wellington W5593, lost on 5 August 1941. [List of Comet evaders, entries beginning in 1941](https://airforceescape.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/List-of-Comet-evaders-1941-1944.pdf)

A cautious end-to-end reconstruction is:

1. **Aircraft loss and evasion:** the men survived operations over occupied Europe and avoided German custody.
2. **Belgian assistance:** local helpers hid and passed them into the emerging Brussels organization.
3. **Brussels preparation:** Comète arranged food, civilian appearance, and movement papers.
4. **Belgian–French crossing:** guides took them through the frontier belt outside normal controls.
5. **Rail to Paris:** they traveled under cover and entered the Paris relay.
6. **Paris to the Basque coast:** couriers moved them south by train through the occupied/Vichy administrative divide then in force.
7. **Final French shelter:** southern helpers placed them near Saint-Jean-de-Luz/Urrugne.
8. **Pyrenean crossing:** de Jongh and Basque assistance led them into Spain during the night of 10–11 November.
9. **British handoff:** Creswell’s system accepted them, removing the danger that they would remain unaffiliated detainees.
10. **Repatriation:** British authorities moved them toward Gibraltar and Britain.
11. **Accounting:** their names entered the passage notebook, forming proof of successful delivery.

The date and identities are documented by the reconstructed register. The exact safe houses and train times for every segment require correlation with each man’s WO 208 evasion report and contemporary Comète papers. It would be overstatement to assign the later standardized Urrugne itinerary to this first group without that comparison.

### Worked failure: the January 1943 Urrugne arrest

A second case shows how the mechanism could break.

On 13–14 January 1943, de Jongh left Paris with three RAF airmen—identified in later reconstructions as **Stan Hope, George Ross, and Bill Greaves**—and traveled toward the Basque frontier. Frédéric de Jongh, who had considered leaving, remained at Anglet because of dangerous weather. The party reached Bidegain Berri in Urrugne, but the Bidasoa was in flood. Goikoetxea judged an immediate crossing unsafe and slept elsewhere.

On the morning of **15 January**, police or soldiers raided the farm and arrested de Jongh, the three airmen, Frantxia Usandizaga, and Juan Manuel Larburu. Sources differ in calling the arresting force ten German soldiers, German-directed police, or French gendarmes. The specific agency is therefore **disputed**. A farm worker, often named **Donato**, is widely alleged to have informed, but “probably betrayed” is the strongest responsible formulation unless his interrogation or trial record is produced. [Specialist escape-line account](https://ww2escapelines.co.uk/escape-lines/belgium-france/comete/) [Belgium WWII account of de Jongh’s arrest](https://www.belgiumwwii.be/de/belgien-im-krieg/artikel/1943-01-15-verhaftung-von-andree-de-jongh.html)

The failure propagated through the system:

- the southern leader disappeared;
- safe houses became suspect;
- British contacts had to be warned;
- Nothomb crossed on 24 January without evaders to notify Creswell;
- alternative inland passages were accelerated;
- northern leadership had to decide which addresses were compromised;
- the surviving line paused, then resumed.

This illustrates why Comète must be understood as an adaptive network rather than as a trail.

## 7. Change over the Lifespan

### Phase I: Improvisation and proof of concept, spring–September 1941

Local Belgian assistance was converted into a proposed through-line. Early movements exposed two deficiencies: incomplete border security and no guaranteed British reception in Spain. Deppé’s arrest removed a founder, while de Jongh’s successful approach to Creswell supplied the missing diplomatic and financial terminal.

### Phase II: Dédée system, autumn 1941–November 1942

The network regularized:

- Brussels collection;
- Belgian–French frontier guides;
- Paris sector under Frédéric de Jongh and French associates;
- De Greef’s Anglet center;
- the Saint-Jean-de-Luz/Urrugne–Bidasoa passage;
- Creswell’s reception and reimbursement.

The number of airmen increased as the Allied air offensive grew. The line remained personal and decentralized but acquired repeatable procedure and a verified passenger ledger.

### Phase III: First large penetrations, November 1942–February 1943

Two supposed American airmen or German agents entered the line in late 1942, leading to arrests in Belgium and the southwest. Approximately 100 Brussels arrests are sometimes given; this is a rounded retrospective figure and should not be treated as an exact operation return.

The political geography also deteriorated. After the Allied landings in North Africa, Germany occupied the former Vichy unoccupied zone in November 1942. The line could no longer rely on crossing from one occupation regime into a nominally unoccupied French zone.

Donny was arrested in December. De Jongh was captured in January. Greindl was arrested in February. Conventional passages stopped temporarily.

### Phase IV: Reconstruction under Franco and d’Ursel, March–June 1943

Nothomb restarted the southern connection. Alternative routes through Bidarray, Larressore, Espelette, Itxassou, and Souraïde reduced reliance on the compromised coastal crossing.

In Belgium, Greindl’s successors rebuilt intake and transport. Antoine d’Ursel took a leading role; Frédéric de Jongh continued from Paris. The network’s regenerative strength came from compartmentation and personal redundancy, but rebuilding also meant recruiting people who had not been tested over long periods.

### Phase V: Renewed expansion and renewed penetration, June 1943–January 1944

Frédéric de Jongh, Robert Aylé, and others were arrested in June 1943. Jacques Le Grelle was inserted to strengthen Paris, while Nothomb remained senior operational leader. Allied bombing produced increasing numbers of evaders, and late-1943 activity was intense.

Jacques Desoubrie penetrated guide and liaison channels. Micheline Dumon became suspicious after seeing him connected to the already notorious Dezitter environment. Warnings did not immediately eliminate the threat; aliases and fragmented communications let him re-enter or reach adjacent groups.

D’Ursel drowned in December. Le Grelle and Nothomb were arrested in January 1944. Paris and Brussels connections were again largely broken.

### Phase VI: Final southbound movements and Marathon transformation, February–September 1944

Surviving Comète personnel tried to restore Paris–Pyrenees evacuation. Meanwhile, Allied bombing of French railways, German checks, and the approaching invasion made long-distance train evacuation increasingly costly and slow.

MI9/IS9 and Belgian State Security shifted policy. Rather than send every airman to Spain, helpers would concentrate them in concealed camps and await advancing Allied armies. In April 1944 Jean de Blommaert and Albert Ancia were parachuted into France. On 15 April they met Yvon Michiels and José Grimar in Paris to coordinate the new arrangements; the precise institutional boundary between “Comète” and “Marathon” subsequently became blurred.

The last conventional Comète use of the Spanish route is dated **4 June 1944** in specialist chronologies. Marathon camps operated until liberation in August and September. [Comète organizational periodization](https://www.cometeline.org/cometstructure.htm) [Marathon organizational account](https://www.cometeline.org/cometorgmara1.htm)

## 8. End or Transformation

### Proximate causes

The classic trans-Pyrenean line ceased because:

- the Allied invasion made liberation foreseeable;
- railway bombing and military movement disrupted long-distance travel;
- German security pressure and repeated arrests destroyed key northern and Paris cells;
- trained guides and safe houses had been exposed;
- forest concealment offered a shorter route to eventual recovery than Spain;
- MI9 deliberately adopted Marathon.

The end was therefore not one final German suppression and not simply Allied victory. It was a policy-driven transformation of a damaged transport network into a distributed concealment network.

### Structural causes

Comète’s strengths also caused its vulnerabilities:

- personal trust enabled rapid action but admitted convincing infiltrators;
- repeat routes produced expertise but created recognizable patterns;
- compartmentation protected peripheral helpers but central couriers accumulated dangerous address knowledge;
- family households made cheap, resilient shelter possible but exposed entire families;
- a narrow Pyrenean trunk created an unavoidable chokepoint;
- British reimbursement sustained the line but made successful border delivery strategically central;
- reliance on railways made the network vulnerable to checks and, in 1944, Allied interdiction.

### Liberation of the camps

Fréteval’s approximately 152–156 airmen were reached in mid-August 1944; Airey Neave arrived with Allied personnel on or about 13–14 August. Belgian Ardennes camps were liberated as Allied forces advanced in early September. Some camps were evacuated or reorganized ahead of German searches.

One compiled balance credits Marathon with **156 persons at Fréteval and 93 in Belgian camps**. Other accounts give **152 in France and 109 returned from Belgium**. These totals should not be combined: they probably count different statuses—received, continuously sheltered, liberated, or repatriated. [Comète balance sheet](https://www.evasioncomete.be/TxtBilan2.html)

### Final act

A chronology identifying the last Comète operation gives **28 September 1944**, when Elvire De Greef accompanied four Allied airmen from Biarritz by air to Britain. This was no longer clandestine passage through occupied territory; it is best treated as liquidation or completion of responsibility rather than the last classic escape-line journey.

### Personnel and records

Survivors returned from prisons and camps at different times. Many were in poor health. Some key witnesses had been executed or died in deportation, leaving postwar reconstructors dependent on surviving passenger notebooks, British reports, family papers, judicial proceedings, and retrospective testimony.

The three passage notebooks entered Belgian state protection. Individual recognition files, resistance-service records, State Security papers, and private collections are now distributed across Belgian State Archives/CegeSoma, the UK National Archives, French archives, military repositories, family collections, and veterans’ associations. CegeSoma notes that private and organizational collections are principally consulted at the General State Archives 2, while State Security, audiovisual, photographic, and wartime-press holdings have separate access arrangements. [CegeSoma collections guide](https://www.cegesoma.be/fr/consulter-nos-collections)

### Postwar successor forms

Comète did not survive as a clandestine transport service. It persisted as:

- a legally recognized wartime service category;
- **Comète Kinship**, linking helpers, evaders, and families;
- present-day **Ligne Comète Line–Remembrance**;
- annual commemorative walks in the Basque country;
- memorial plaques, monuments, preserved houses, and named streets;
- archival and genealogical reconstruction;
- a template in public memory for civilian aid to downed aircrew.

## 9. Scale, Cost, Loss, and Reliability of Figures

### Evaders and other assisted persons

| Figure | What it appears to count | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| 288 | Named Allied military personnel successfully passed into Spain through the southern line | **Best-supported core total.** Based on the passage notebooks and individual reconstruction. |
| 84 | Civilians, agents, volunteers, or other persons using the same channel | **Memorial compilation.** Category boundaries require case-level checking. |
| 176 | Persons captured during attempted evasion | **Memorial compilation.** Should not be added to successful passages as “saved.” |
| 394 | Airmen concealed until liberation, principally through Marathon-related activity | **Broad memorial compilation.** May include camps and dispersed shelters beyond strict Comète command. |
| 776 | Lives “saved” by Comète | **Common retrospective figure.** Counting method varies. |
| 700–800 | Total airmen or persons helped | **Rounded commemorative estimate.** Often conflates returned evaders with concealed men. |
| 957 | Total of the remembrance association’s four reconstructed sections | **Research database total**, not a single wartime operational statistic. |
| “More than 400” | Airmen returned, in de Jongh’s U.S. citation | **Honor citation figure**, useful evidence of contemporary Allied assessment but inconsistent with the later 288-name Pyrenean ledger unless it includes wider assistance. |

The safest quantified statement is:

> Comète’s surviving core record identifies **288 Allied military evaders successfully passed through Spain**. The larger system assisted additional civilians, captured would-be evaders, airmen transferred to other organizations, and several hundred men concealed until liberation. Depending on categories, postwar totals range from roughly **700–800** to a current memorial database of **957 assisted persons**.

### Helpers

Claims range from roughly 800 arrested participants to “more than 3,000” people who provided some assistance. Both might be compatible if one distinguishes recognized agents from occasional household or logistical helpers.

A closed membership total is impossible because:

- Comète was compartmented;
- informal assistance might occur once;
- records were destroyed or never created;
- postwar recognition rules excluded some genuine helpers;
- people worked simultaneously for several services;
- “member,” “agent,” “helper,” and “satellite” were not interchangeable.

### Deaths

Frequently encountered totals include:

- **more than 150** arrested participants who died;
- **approximately 200** or **more than 200** deaths;
- **216 Comète members** commemorated as having died for the Allied cause.

The 216 figure is a memorial roll, not necessarily a uniform causal category. It may include people executed, killed in action, dying in prisons or camps, dying from detention consequences, drowning during operations, or killed by bombing. [Comète memorial places page](https://www.cometeline.org/cometLieux.htm)

### Distances

The full route is frequently described as about **1,000 miles** or 1,600 kilometers. That is a useful order-of-magnitude description from Belgium to Spain, not a measured invariant itinerary. A Brussels–Paris–Basque–Madrid–Gibraltar journey was longer, while an airman collected in northern France began farther south.

### Finance

No reliable total wartime budget is available in the sources inspected. Known financial facts are:

- Jacques Donny served as early treasurer.
- Private Belgian funds supported the first organization.
- Creswell supplied British funding after successful contact.
- passage notebooks supported reimbursement by delivered RAF airman;
- De Greef managed southern finances;
- later branches sometimes ran short of cash;
- Marathon required bulk food and supply purchasing.

Any claim of a single fare or total “value” should be marked unestablished until the MI9 finance files and Belgian liquidation accounts are examined.

## 10. Failures, Penetrations, Arrests, and Internal Problems

### Early failure in Spain

The founders initially underestimated Spanish detention risk and the need for diplomatic reception. Some travelers were arrested or sent to Miranda de Ebro. This was an operational failure that directly produced the Creswell agreement.

### False airmen

German counterintelligence exploited the network’s central humanitarian obligation: airmen were supposed to be helped quickly. Agents posing as American or British aircrew could enter at the point where refusal might condemn a genuine survivor. Captured uniforms, identity tags, service knowledge, and genuine stories obtained under interrogation strengthened deception.

### Information concentration

Although Comète was compartmented, long-distance couriers and organizers needed several addresses. When arrested or surveilled, they could unintentionally lead police from one node to another. The danger did not require confession; physical surveillance of a trusted courier could be enough.

### Torture and coercion

Arrested helpers were subjected to solitary confinement, threats, beatings, repeated interrogation, military trial, Nacht und Nebel disappearance, imprisonment, and concentration-camp deportation. Sources do not support a sanitized account in which everyone successfully resisted interrogation. Information could be extracted through coercion, documents could be found during searches, and prisoners could be followed after controlled release.

### Collaboration and paid penetration

Dezitter, Giralt, Desoubrie, and other agents exploited resistance networks for German payment, privilege, protection, ideology, or self-interest. Their operations included false shelter, identity-paper collection, staged British connections, and delivery of victims into German or collaborationist custody. These were not ordinary “security mistakes” but an adversarial network designed to imitate the genuine one.

### Governance limitations

Comète had no secure centralized personnel registry during operations, no modern background-check system, no encrypted real-time alert network, and limited ability to distribute a suspect’s photograph rapidly. Alias changes and partial warnings allowed an infiltrator rejected by one cell to approach another.

### Misidentification and postwar attribution

The clandestine environment also created the possibility of incorrect accusations. Postwar testimony was affected by trauma, dead witnesses, aliases, institutional rivalry, and the desire to assign a complex collapse to a single traitor. Accordingly, specific arrest chains should be based on German files, surveillance reports, judicial records, and multiple witness statements rather than on memoir alone.

### No documented profiteering scandal internal to Comète

Some funds undoubtedly moved through black markets, and guides or suppliers could be paid, but the accessible evidence does not establish a major Comète leadership embezzlement or fare-extraction scandal. Absence of accessible evidence is not proof that no local fraud occurred.

## 11. Contested and Legendary Aspects

### “One thousand miles of freedom”

**Story:** a single continuous heroic path led from Brussels to Spain.

**Factual core:** a connected relay system did span that distance.

**Distortion:** routes branched, shifted, and sometimes transferred people to other organizations. Most helpers operated one small segment. Modern trail maps privilege the survivable and commemorated Basque crossing over destroyed urban nodes.

### The solitary girl who created and ran everything

**Story:** Andrée de Jongh alone invented, commanded, and personally guided the entire line.

**Factual core:** she was the indispensable founder, negotiator, system designer, and example.

**Distortion:** Deppé supplied southwestern knowledge; Donny supplied finance; Frédéric de Jongh and the Aylés ran Paris; the De Greefs made the southern hub viable; Basque guides controlled the frontier; hundreds of households operated the collection and shelter web. The “solitary heroine” narrative can erase the network whose effectiveness was her achievement.

### “The Germans did not believe her because she was a small young woman”

**Story:** after arrest, German investigators dismissed de Jongh’s truthful claim to be the leader.

**Assessment:** repeated in memoir and biographical literature and plausible given the record of prolonged interrogation and her survival. The stronger romantic form—that disbelief alone saved her—cannot be proved. German evidentiary uncertainty, ongoing investigation, prison policy, and bureaucratic decisions may all have mattered.

### The Donato betrayal

**Story:** a named neighboring farm worker betrayed de Jongh at Bidegain Berri.

**Assessment:** probable or traditional, but the arresting force and informer chain are inconsistently described. The accusation should remain **well-supported allegation**, not absolute fact, until contemporary investigation records are cited.

### Exactly 33 or 34 crossings

De Jongh’s January 1943 journey is called her **34th crossing**, while other summaries credit approximately 33 successful crossings. These can coexist if the arrest journey is counted as the 34th attempt rather than a completed crossing. Claims that she personally crossed the Pyrenees 34 times successfully are misleading.

### Exactly 118 airmen personally escorted

The U.S. citation says de Jongh “personally conducted 118 Allied flyers to freedom.” Passage reconstructions more often assign her personal convoy responsibility for about 78 among roughly 118 moved by the line before her arrest. The citation’s number should be quoted as an honor citation, not silently substituted for the passenger-by-passenger reconstruction.

### “Comète saved 800 airmen”

This is the best-known headline but not one uniform statistic. The 288 southbound authenticated military passages, Marathon camps, airmen hidden until liberation, people transferred elsewhere, civilians, and failed evaders are frequently conflated. “Approximately 800 persons assisted in a broad sense” is more defensible than “800 airmen taken to Spain.”

### A perfectly secret Marathon

Some summaries say Marathon “was never discovered.” Fréteval was successfully preserved as a whole, but German searches affected Belgian camps; Beffe was evacuated and four airmen were reportedly captured. “The principal scheme was not comprehensively penetrated” is safer than “no camp or participant was detected.”

### Purely altruistic versus paid guides

Commemorative accounts often emphasize unpaid sacrifice. That accurately characterizes many households, but an escape network still required money, black-market purchases, reimbursement, and sometimes paid guide work. Payment does not by itself negate resistance motivation. Conversely, describing every service as voluntary would be unhistorical.

### Treasure and conspiracy narratives

No substantial Comète treasure legend comparable to hidden resistance gold has been located. The relevant “treasure” tradition concerns hidden notebooks, lists, and archives. Claims of secret fortunes should be considered **unverified** unless supported by liquidation accounts or judicial files.

The most persistent conspiracy narrative is that a single German “master spy” caused virtually every collapse. Dezitter and Desoubrie caused real and extensive harm, but arrests also arose from surveillance, seized documents, coercive interrogation, reused addresses, accidental recognition, border informers, and overlapping penetrations. The single-traitor story simplifies a multi-agency counter-network.

## 12. Historiography and Representations

### Primary and near-primary sources

#### Passage notebooks

The three compact notebooks listing successful passages are the central internal quantitative source. Their strength is contemporary or near-contemporary entry tied to reimbursement. Their limitations are categorical: they record successful southern deliveries, not every person sheltered, transferred, arrested, or hidden during Marathon.

#### WO 208 escape-and-evasion reports

British debriefing reports provide individual itineraries, dates, helper descriptions, aliases, and travel conditions. They must be read critically: evaders often knew only aliases, misunderstood geography, or were deliberately denied helper identities.

#### MI9/IS9 records

British policy, finance, personnel, intelligence, and Marathon records are principally in UK National Archives holdings. Relevant series require file-level research rather than reliance on catalogue snippets.

#### Belgian recognition and State Security files

These contain membership claims, affidavits, service dates, postwar verification, and sometimes internal reports. Their strengths are case-level evidence and cross-examination. Their limitations include bureaucratic eligibility rules and retrospective memory.

#### German and collaboration trials

German operational papers, Belgian military-auditor files, and French proceedings against collaborators are essential for reconstructing infiltration. Postwar prosecution records may nevertheless frame events around legal charges rather than network analysis.

#### Oral histories and memoirs

Survivor testimony supplies domestic, emotional, and procedural information absent from official files. It must be correlated with dates and passenger records. Many memoirs were written after the line had acquired a heroic public narrative.

### Major works

- **Cécile Jouan [Suzanne de Jongh], _Comète_ (1947).** Earliest book-length internal narrative, written by Andrée’s sister in collaboration with her. Indispensable but familial and commemorative.
- **Airey Neave, _Little Cyclone_ (1954); French translation _Petit Cyclone_ (1956).** British MI9 interpretation centered on de Jongh.
- **Rémy, works on Réseau Comète (1960s).** Influential narrative history, rich in testimony but written before broad archival opening.
- **Airey Neave, _Saturday at MI9_ and later editions.** Important for the British institutional view and Marathon.
- **Donald L. Miller and later air-war histories.** Supply the strategic context but usually compress the ground system.
- **Alasdair MacDermott, “Comète, a World War II Belgian Evasion Line.”** More recent synthetic study associated with Belgian intelligence-and-action commemoration.
- **Marie-Pierre d’Udekem d’Acoz, _Andrée De Jongh: une vie de résistante_ (2016).** Modern biography using Belgian material.
- **Edward Stourton, _Cruel Crossing_ (2013).** Broader Pyrenean escape context, including Comète.
- **Juan Carlos Jiménez de Aberasturi Corta, studies of Red Comète in the Basque Country.** Important corrective to Belgian- and British-centered narratives.
- **Peter Eisner, _The Freedom Line_ (2004).** Accessible popular narrative; sometimes criticized for dramatization and simplification.
- **Megan Koreman, _The Escape Line_ (2018).** Comparative and social treatment of escape-line work and communities.

### Archival problems

A definitive history is impeded by:

- deliberate destruction of addresses and lists;
- death of central witnesses;
- records scattered across five or more national systems;
- aliases with variant spelling;
- inconsistent definitions of membership;
- postwar service rivalries;
- privacy restrictions on personal dossiers;
- memorial websites correcting earlier versions without formal edition histories;
- statistics copied from citation to citation without their original counting rule.

The CegeSoma/State Archives transfer of wartime-victim and resistance files has improved central access, but not all relevant private or foreign material is digitized. [CegeSoma archival overview](https://www.cegesoma.be/fr/consulter-nos-collections)

### Film, fiction, exhibitions, and memorial culture

Significant representations include:

- **_Little Cyclone/Petit Cyclone_**, biographical narrative derived from MI9 memory.
- **Michel Mees, _Archive_ / documentary treatment of Comète** (RTBF/FR3, 1992; cataloguing details vary).
- **_The Last Passage / Le dernier passage_** by Lurre Telleria and Enara Goikoetxea (2010), focusing on Basque frontier memory.
- **Comète exhibitions and annual commemorative crossings** in Belgium and the Basque country.
- **Kristin Hannah, _The Nightingale_ (2015).** Fiction drawing on Pyrenean escape-line motifs; it is not a documentary representation of Comète.
- Museums and memorial routes at Urrugne, Ciboure, Anglet, Brussels, and Fréteval.

Popular representations tend to:

- center one female guide;
- compress multiple rail and safe-house stages;
- depict a fixed mountain route;
- minimize finance and administrative work;
- portray infiltrators as individually omnipotent;
- merge Comète with other Pyrenean lines;
- give a single rounded “800 saved” total.

## 13. Chronology

### 1940

- **May–June:** Belgium and France are defeated. Stranded Allied troops and escaping prisoners receive spontaneous civilian help.
- **Late 1940:** informal Belgian shelter and movement chains develop. These are predecessors, not yet securely identifiable as Comète.

### 1941

- **Spring–June:** Andrée de Jongh, Arnold Deppé, Jacques Donny, and associates organize a repeatable line.
- **Summer:** first southbound attempts demonstrate the danger of Spanish detention and the absence of guaranteed reception.
- **August–September:** de Jongh reaches Spain and establishes contact with Michael Creswell.
- **September:** conventional date for the British–Comète operational agreement.
- **10–11 November:** first three recorded Allied airmen—Tomicki, Ives, and Kowalski—cross successfully through the Comète mechanism.
- **Late 1941:** arrests, including Deppé’s, force restructuring.

### 1942

- **Early–midyear:** Brussels, Paris, Anglet, and the classical Urrugne route become a regular system.
- **Easter period:** standardized blue workmen’s clothing is reported in use for Pyrenean parties.
- **November:** Germany occupies southern France after the Allied North African landings; Comète loses the relative advantage of the former unoccupied zone.
- **Late November:** false airmen or infiltrators trigger extensive arrests.
- **1 December:** Jacques Donny is arrested.

### 1943

- **15 January:** de Jongh, three airmen, Frantxia Usandizaga, and Juan Manuel Larburu are arrested at Bidegain Berri.
- **24 January:** Nothomb crosses into Spain without evaders to warn Creswell.
- **6 February:** Jean Greindl is arrested in Brussels.
- **February:** no normal Comète passenger passage is recorded.
- **March–May:** Nothomb restores the southern connection; inland passages are developed.
- **March:** arrests hit Bayonne-area families.
- **June:** Frédéric de Jongh, Robert Aylé, and associates are arrested; later executed.
- **Late June:** Jacques Le Grelle takes responsibility in Paris while Nothomb remains overall operational leader.
- **Summer–autumn:** increased Allied air operations produce heavy demand; the line again moves convoys.
- **7 September:** Greindl dies in Saint-Gilles Prison during an Allied bombing raid.
- **10 October:** German court condemns Donny to death.
- **24 December:** Antoine d’Ursel drowns in the Bidasoa.
- **Throughout late 1943:** Desoubrie penetrates liaison and transport connections.

### 1944

- **17–18 January:** Le Grelle, Nothomb, and multiple agents are arrested; Paris and Brussels structures are gravely damaged.
- **24 February:** Jacques Donny is executed at Stuttgart.
- **28 March:** Frédéric de Jongh, Robert Aylé, and Aimable Fouquerel are shot at Mont-Valérien.
- **10–11 April:** Jean de Blommaert and Albert Ancia are parachuted near Saint-Ambroix/Issoudun.
- **15 April:** they meet surviving organizers in Paris to integrate Comète personnel with Marathon.
- **20 May:** first documented party for Fréteval travels from Paris toward Châteaudun.
- **4 June:** conventional date of the last Comète use of the southbound escape route.
- **6 June:** Allied Normandy landings confirm the logic of static concealment.
- **May–August:** Fréteval/Sherwood and Belgian Ardennes camps operate.
- **13–14 August:** Neave and Allied forces reach the Fréteval airmen.
- **Late August–early September:** remaining French and Belgian camp systems are liberated or evacuated.
- **2 September:** prisoners from Saint-Gilles are placed on a deportation train, demonstrating that liberation did not end danger for captured members.
- **7 September:** approximately twenty airmen in one late Belgian camp are reported liberated; other camps emerge over following days.
- **28 September:** Elvire De Greef accompanies four airmen from Biarritz to Britain, often treated as Comète’s final operation.

## 14. Findings and Unresolved Questions

### Findings that can be stated with high confidence

1. Comète was an autonomous Belgian-origin escape-and-evasion system cooperating closely with MI9, not simply an MI9 field section.
2. Its decisive institutional formation occurred when de Jongh connected a Belgian–French relay to British reception in Spain in 1941.
3. The system transported people, money, documents, intelligence, supplies, and authentication—not airmen alone.
4. Brussels, Paris, Anglet, the Basque frontier, and British Spain formed its essential trunk.
5. Its southern ledger securely identifies 288 successful Allied military passages.
6. Repeated arrest waves did not produce one continuous decline; surviving members rebuilt the network several times.
7. Infiltration was a major cause of destruction, but not the sole cause.
8. Comète’s classic transport function ended through a combination of police destruction, transport disruption, Allied strategy, and conversion into Marathon.
9. Marathon belongs to Comète’s institutional afterlife and final operational phase but was not simply the old route under a new name.
10. Rounded claims of 700, 776, 800, or 957 describe different universes and should never be cited without a category definition.

### Questions requiring further archival work

1. What was the precise date and content of de Jongh’s first written or recorded agreement with Creswell?
2. Which early travelers belong to Comète proper rather than its experimental predecessor?
3. Can the financial accounts establish advances, reimbursements, guide payments, losses, and cost per completed journey?
4. Which German agency conducted the Bidegain Berri arrest, and what documentary evidence identifies the informer?
5. What exact arrest chain links each Desoubrie or Dezitter contact to particular Comète victims?
6. How many helpers worked once, how many were recognized agents, and how many operated simultaneously for other services?
7. Which of the 216 commemorated dead can be assigned to execution, deportation, operational accident, bombing, or later consequences?
8. What is the correct boundary between Comète, Marathon, and parallel MI9 missions in the 394-person “hidden until liberation” category?
9. Were any funds stolen or diverted during collapses, or do postwar files document only unavoidable operational loss?
10. How did Spanish local police, Guardia Civil, provincial authorities, and British diplomats negotiate individual releases and onward movement?

## 16. Source Assessment

The strongest quantitative evidence is the named 288-person successful-passage register because it derives from contemporary notebooks and can be cross-checked against service and evasion files. The strongest institutional evidence lies in the British WO 208 material and Belgian State Security and recognition dossiers.

The extensive Comète remembrance websites are unusually valuable because they preserve names, aliases, route reconstructions, organization charts, photographs, and references to individual files. They are nevertheless living memorial compilations: the sites acknowledge omissions and errors, and some pages conflate documentation with commemoration. Their claims have accordingly been identified here as documentary reconstructions, memorial totals, or traditions rather than treated as uniform primary evidence.

Wikipedia-derived results were used only as discovery aids and were not retained as independent authority where a specialist, archival, or institutional source was available. Exact claims that could not be traced beyond circular popular repetition have been marked disputed or unverified.
