# Silk Road: Network Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

**Subject.** “Silk Road” denotes a shifting system of interlocking overland roads, caravan circuits, steppe corridors, river crossings, mountain passes, maritime lanes, markets, tribute channels, diplomatic routes, postal relays, religious institutions, and merchant diasporas connecting eastern Asia, Inner Asia, South Asia, Iran, the Middle East, the Mediterranean, and—through secondary networks—northern and eastern Europe and eastern Africa.

It was never one road, one organization, or one continuously operating institution. No authority owned or chartered the whole. Most goods and messages moved through successive regional transfers rather than in a single journey from China to Rome. Archaeology does not support a permanently coherent transcontinental highway; it reveals intermittent, overlapping regional systems whose connectivity varied with political control, security, climate, and demand. [Modern Asian Studies](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/modern-asian-studies/article/silk-road-orientations-in-xinjiang-archaeology-and-shifting-implications-for-eurasian-studies/B20A70F9423180F005250433BB488C61)

**Bounded period adopted here:** approximately **130 BCE–1500 CE**, with antecedents and survivals noted separately.

- **Formation boundary, 139/138–126 BCE:** Zhang Qian’s first Han mission opened sustained Chinese court knowledge of Ferghana, Kangju, Bactria, and neighboring regions. His mission did not create Eurasian trade, but it helped turn older jade, horse, steppe, Iranian, and South Asian exchanges into a state-supported chain reaching from Han China into Central Asia.
- **Alternative beginnings:** Bronze Age exchanges; the Achaemenid Royal Road; Alexander’s conquests; Greco-Bactrian and Mauryan connections; or Han campaigns of 121–101 BCE. No single “foundation date” can be established.
- **End boundary, approximately 1450–1500:** the fragmentation of Mongol successor states, Timur’s wars, changing Central Asian political geography, Ming restrictions, Ottoman control of eastern Mediterranean approaches, and expanding Indian Ocean and Cape-route shipping transformed the balance of Eurasian commerce.
- **Alternative endings:** 751, 755–763, c. 1000, 1368, 1453, 1498, the seventeenth-century contraction of Tarim routes, or no ending at all. Regional caravan trade continued into the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. What ended was not Asian overland commerce but the medieval configuration retrospectively called the Silk Road.

**Evidence labels:**

- **Documented:** directly supported by contemporary texts, inscriptions, excavated documents, or securely provenanced artifacts.
- **Inference:** a reconstruction consistent with evidence but not directly stated by a contemporary source.
- **Disputed:** substantially different scholarly interpretations remain.
- **Traditional/legendary:** transmitted narrative without adequate contemporary confirmation.
- **Unknown:** surviving evidence cannot establish the answer.

## 1. Identification and Definition

### 1.1 Names

There was **no ancient or medieval official name for the complete network**. Consequently, none of the following is an ancient corporate title.

The modern term derives from German **Seidenstraße/Seidenstraßen**, “silk road(s),” introduced by Ferdinand von Richthofen in 1877 while discussing connections between China and Central Asia. Richthofen used the expression narrowly and infrequently; twentieth-century geographers and explorers enlarged it into the familiar trans-Eurasian concept. [Richthofen study](https://richthofen.eu/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/Silk-Road_2015-10-08.pdf)

Historically attested names usually designated particular components:

- Chinese **Xiyu** (西域), “Western Regions,” meant territories west of the Chinese core, especially the Tarim Basin and beyond.
- **Hexi/Hosi Corridor** designated the Gansu passage.
- Sanskrit **Uttarāpatha**, “Northern Road,” referred to northern South Asian circuits; it was not an exact synonym for the Silk Road.
- Persian and Arabic geographers described individual roads and stages rather than a single Eurasian system.
- Mongol **jam** and Turkic **yam** designated the imperial relay-post system, not ordinary commerce as a whole.
- **Rāhdānite** identified a debated group or category of Jewish long-distance merchants, not a road.
- **Ortoq/ortaq** designated commercial partnerships, especially under Turkic and Mongol patronage.

No ancient Sogdian umbrella name equivalent to “Silk Road” is securely known.

### 1.2 What belonged to the network

Included here are:

1. The Chinese capitals and northern frontier corridors.
2. The Hexi Corridor, Dunhuang, and Tarim Basin oasis chains.
3. Routes through the Tian Shan, Pamirs, Ferghana, Sogdiana, Bactria, and Khwarazm.
4. Iranian roads through Merv, Nishapur, Rayy, Hamadan, and Mesopotamia.
5. Southward branches through the Karakoram, Kashmir, Gandhara, and the Indus–Gangetic systems.
6. Steppe routes north of the Caspian and Black seas.
7. Mediterranean and Black Sea termini connected to Central Asian exchange.
8. Maritime systems of the South China Sea, Bay of Bengal, Indian Ocean, Persian Gulf, Arabian Sea, and Red Sea when functioning as complementary or competing Eurasian links.

Excluded as a literal claim is the familiar single red line from Xi’an to Rome. It is a modern cartographic simplification.

### 1.3 What the system carried

**Material cargo:** silk yarn and textiles; horses; jade; gold and silver; copper and iron; glassware; ivory; coral; pearls; lapis lazuli; turquoise; amber; furs; musk; slaves; weapons; armor; paper; books; ceramics; lacquer; cotton and wool textiles; carpets; dyes; spices; incense; medicines; sal ammoniac; sugar; wine; dried fruit; seeds; livestock; and artistic objects.

**Institutional and informational traffic:** ambassadors, tribute, diplomatic gifts, intelligence, military orders, tax records, contracts, letters, passports, religious texts, calendars, astronomical knowledge, stories, musical styles, dances, visual motifs, craft techniques, languages, and legal formulas.

**People:** merchants, factors, moneylenders, rulers, hostages, soldiers, refugees, artisans, physicians, entertainers, diplomats, pilgrims, monks, missionaries, translators, brides, enslaved persons, and deported or resettled populations.

**Biological traffic:** crops, animals, parasites, and pathogens. Ancient DNA from Kara-Djigach and Burana in present-day Kyrgyzstan identifies *Yersinia pestis* in people buried during the documented 1338–1339 “pestilence”; the strain lies near the ancestral diversification associated with the second plague pandemic. This supports a Central Eurasian source but does not prove one simple caravan itinerary for the Black Death. [Nature research article](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04800-3)

## 2. Formation

### 2.1 Predecessor systems

The Silk Road was assembled from older structures rather than founded ex nihilo:

- Prehistoric exchange moved jade from the Khotan region eastward and steppe goods across Inner Eurasia.
- Achaemenid imperial roads connected Anatolia, Mesopotamia, Iran, Bactria, and Sogdiana.
- Hellenistic successor kingdoms connected the Mediterranean-facing world with Iran, Bactria, and northwestern India.
- Mauryan roads and ports linked the Gangetic plain, the Deccan, and Indian Ocean.
- Nomadic pastoral corridors joined Mongolia, the Altai, Kazakhstan, and the Black Sea zone.
- Arabian Sea and Red Sea shipping already connected Egypt, Arabia, India, and East Africa.

### 2.2 Han intervention

In 139 or 138 BCE, Emperor Wu sent **Zhang Qian** to seek an alliance with the Yuezhi against the Xiongnu. Captured and held for years, Zhang eventually reached Dayuan/Ferghana, Kangju, the Yuezhi, and Daxia/Bactria. Of an original party exceeding one hundred, the *Shiji* says that only Zhang and Tangyi Fu returned after thirteen years. [Chinese Text Project, *Shiji* 123](https://ctext.org/shiji/da-wan-lie-zhuan/ens)

Zhang reported:

- agricultural and urban societies in Ferghana;
- prized “blood-sweating” horses;
- silver coinage and long-distance merchants farther west;
- Chinese products already reaching Bactria indirectly through southwestern trade.

**Documented consequence:** Han policy shifted toward embassies, military colonies, frontier roads, and campaigns intended to outflank the Xiongnu and obtain horses and alliances.

**Disputed interpretation:** older scholarship called Zhang the “founder” or “opener” of the Silk Road. Current scholarship generally treats his missions as a major reorganization and acceleration of pre-existing networks.

Han forces took the Gansu corridor from Xiongnu power, established commanderies and agricultural garrisons, and fought Ferghana in 104–101 BCE. Silk served simultaneously as diplomatic gift, military expenditure, taxable commodity, and exchange medium. The first system was therefore as much geopolitical and tributary as commercial.

### 2.3 No charter, owner, or central treasury

There was no Silk Road charter, headquarters, membership roll, or common law. Funding was distributed among:

- imperial treasuries maintaining frontier garrisons, canals, roads, post stations, walls, and embassies;
- oasis kings and municipal authorities;
- temples, monasteries, churches, and later Islamic charitable endowments;
- merchant families and partnerships;
- pastoral rulers who provided—or sold—safe conduct;
- villages compelled to provision armies, officials, and relay stations;
- private carriers and caravan owners.

Its legal standing changed at every frontier. A traveler might be an ambassador in one jurisdiction, a merchant in the next, a taxable alien elsewhere, and a suspected spy or smuggler farther on.

## 3. Constituent Nodes

This is a selective but system-wide inventory. Dates are periods of major network importance, not necessarily settlement dates.

### 3.1 Eastern capitals and corridors

| Node | Approximate role and period | Evidence/status |
|---|---|---|
| Chang’an, modern Xi’an | Han and Tang capital; court demand, tribute reception, western-market population, military administration; especially 2nd c. BCE–9th c. CE | Securely attested archaeology and dynastic records |
| Luoyang | Eastern Han and later capital; terminus for embassies, religious translation, foreign communities | Secure |
| Hexi Corridor | Narrow agricultural and military corridor between Tibetan Plateau and Gobi; road, wall, fort, farm, and beacon system | Secure; canals and fortifications survive |
| Wuwei, Zhangye, Jiuquan | Commandery towns and supply points in Gansu | Secure |
| Dunhuang | Junction before the desert; garrison, market, document center, monastery complex, and gateway to northern/southern Tarim routes | Exceptionally documented |
| Mogao Caves | Buddhist devotional, artistic, manuscript, and donor node; not merely a caravan warehouse | Secure; c. 4th–14th c. |
| Yumen and Yang passes | Controlled exits from Chinese territory | Secure, though exact use shifted |

UNESCO’s Chang’an–Tian Shan property comprises thirty-three sites including capitals, settlements, passes, paths, posthouses, beacon towers, walls, tombs, and religious complexes. Its surviving Hexi waterworks include approximately 90 km of canals around Suoyang. [UNESCO World Heritage dossier](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1442/)

### 3.2 Tarim Basin oasis chains

**Northern chain:** Hami–Turfan/Gaochang–Bezeklik–Kucha–Aksu–Kashgar.

**Southern chain:** Miran/Loulan–Niya–Keriya–Khotan–Yarkand–Kashgar.

The Taklamakan made oasis water, fodder, and political protection indispensable. Routes moved when rivers shifted or settlements lost water.

- **Turfan/Gaochang/Qocho:** fertile depression, irrigated partly through wells and underground channels; contracts, tax documents, cemetery goods, Buddhist and Manichaean sites.
- **Kucha:** music, Buddhist scholarship, cave temples, and a strategic northern-road position.
- **Loulan/Kroraina and Niya:** abandoned desert settlements whose dry conditions preserved wood, textiles, and administrative documents in Chinese and Kharoṣṭhī.
- **Khotan:** jade source, Buddhist kingdom, silk and textile producer, junction toward Kashmir and the Pamirs.
- **Yarkand:** southern-basin market and route toward the Karakoram.
- **Kashgar:** western convergence point toward Ferghana, the Pamirs, and Bactria.

The tidy “north road/south road” diagram is broadly useful but hides numerous seasonal, local, and now-lost tracks.

### 3.3 Central Asian core

- **Ferghana/Dayuan:** horses, agriculture, and access between the Tarim and Sogdiana.
- **Samarkand/Afrasiab:** Sogdian political and commercial center; its seventh-century “Ambassadors” paintings show a court presenting itself within a diplomatic world.
- **Bukhara and Varakhsha:** oasis economy, court, craft, and later Islamic commercial nodes.
- **Panjikent:** Sogdian urban settlement with merchant houses and exceptional wall paintings.
- **Tashkent/Chach:** junction between steppe, Ferghana, and Sogdiana.
- **Suyab/Ak-Beshim, Navekat/Krasnaya Rechka, Balasagun/Burana:** Chuy Valley centers serving Turkic, Sogdian, Buddhist, Christian, Manichaean, and later Islamic populations.
- **Merv:** oasis metropolis linking Central Asia, Iran, Afghanistan, and Mesopotamia.
- **Balkh/Bactra:** Bactrian, Buddhist, Iranian, and Islamic hub connecting the Oxus system with India.
- **Termez:** Amu Darya crossing and Buddhist/Islamic center.
- **Khwarazm/Gurganj:** lower Oxus market tied to steppe, Volga, Iran, and Transoxiana.
- **Talas:** route junction and site of the 751 battle; its long-term economic significance should not be reduced to that battle.

Sogdiana’s political extent changed; by the seventh century, Xuanzang could use the name broadly for Iranian-speaking regions between the Amu Darya and Issyk Kul, while later Muslim usage narrowed it toward the Samarkand–Bukhara countryside. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/sogdiana-iii-history-and-archaeology/)

### 3.4 Iranian, Mesopotamian, and Mediterranean nodes

- **Nishapur, Tus, Rayy, Hamadan:** stages across Khurasan and the Iranian plateau.
- **Ctesiphon and later Baghdad:** imperial capitals, consumption centers, financial and scholarly markets, and junctions toward Syria and the Persian Gulf.
- **Tabriz and Sultaniyya:** especially important under the Ilkhans.
- **Palmyra:** Roman-period caravan city connecting Syria with Mesopotamia; important but not the exclusive western terminus.
- **Antioch, Aleppo, Damascus:** Levantine markets joining inland roads to Mediterranean shipping.
- **Trebizond and Constantinople:** Black Sea and Byzantine outlets.
- **Tana/Azov, Caffa, Sudak, Soldaia:** medieval Black Sea interfaces with steppe routes.
- **Venice and Genoa:** secondary western commercial systems connected through Black Sea, Byzantine, Mamluk, and Ilkhanid markets—not direct owners of an Asian Silk Road.

### 3.5 South Asian and maritime nodes

- **Taxila, Purushapura/Peshawar, Kapisa/Begram, Bamiyan:** Gandharan and Kushan connections between India, Bactria, and Central Asia.
- **Kashmir and the Karakoram valleys:** difficult pilgrim and merchant branches marked by inscriptions and passes.
- **Mathura and the Gangetic plain:** production, political, and Buddhist circuits.
- **Bharuch/Barygaza, Muziris region, Arikamedu and other Indian ports:** Indian Ocean interchange.
- **Siraf, Hormuz, Kish, Basra:** Persian Gulf interfaces.
- **Aden and Red Sea ports including Berenike:** western Indian Ocean articulation.
- **Guangzhou, Quanzhou, Yangzhou:** Chinese maritime nodes with foreign merchant communities.
- **Southeast Asian straits and ports:** relay zone for China–Indian Ocean commerce.

The maritime system was not a late invention. The first-century *Periplus* and Pliny describe organized monsoon commerce between Roman Egypt, Arabia, and India. Pliny gives approximately forty days from Arabia to Muziris using the monsoon and notes armed protection against pirates. [Pliny, *Natural History* 6](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Pliny_the_Elder/6%2A.html)

## 4. Connections

### 4.1 Eastern land corridors

A state-supported traveler from Chang’an moved through commandery towns and farms of the Hexi Corridor to Dunhuang. Beyond the passes, traffic divided around the Tarim Basin. Chinese states maintained roads, posts, walls, canals, soldiers, and beacon systems when they controlled the region; oasis states controlled water, markets, tolls, and accommodations.

Traffic included silk payments to garrisons, grain, horses, official dispatches, tribute embassies, monks, contracts, and private goods. Frequency and volume cannot be reconstructed consistently.

### 4.2 Tarim–Sogdian connections

From Kashgar, routes crossed toward:

- Ferghana by the Irkeshtam/Torugart zones;
- the Alai and Pamirs;
- Tashkent and the Syr Darya;
- Samarkand and Bukhara;
- Balkh through mountain and Oxus crossings.

Sogdian merchant colonies formed a chain from their homeland into Gansu and northern China. Their language became a major mercantile and administrative medium. The network depended on kinship, community heads, interpreters, written correspondence, and residence abroad more than on merchants making repeated Rome-to-China journeys.

### 4.3 Iranian and western connections

The principal Iranian axis ran from Merv through Nishapur, Rayy, and Hamadan to Mesopotamia, with branches to the Caucasus, Anatolia, Persian Gulf, and Levant. Parthian and Sasanian governments sought revenue and strategic advantage from intermediary trade. Menander Protector’s account of the Sogdian Maniakh’s embassy in the 560s describes an effort to bypass Sasanian obstruction and sell silk through Turkic–Byzantine diplomacy. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/blog/who-were-sogdians)

### 4.4 Steppe connections

Routes across Mongolia, the Altai, Kazakhstan, the Volga basin, and the Black Sea moved horses, furs, slaves, silver, weapons, and diplomatic traffic. These were often faster in political communication than desert caravan routes but depended on pastoral alliances and seasonal grazing.

Substantial quantities of Islamic silver dirhams reached northern and eastern Europe through steppe and river networks. Their presence demonstrates connected exchange chains, not necessarily travel by Muslim merchants to every findspot.

### 4.5 Maritime connections

Ships exploited monsoon winds rather than hugging the coast continuously. Cargo could pass from Chinese ports through Southeast Asia and India, then through:

- the Persian Gulf to Iraq and Iran; or
- the Red Sea to Nile transport and Mediterranean ports.

Maritime carriage generally had a much greater bulk capacity than pack transport. Consequently, pepper, ceramics, cotton textiles, and other bulky commodities often traveled primarily by sea, whereas high-value, low-weight cargo was better suited to difficult overland carriage.

### 4.6 Speed, cost, and risk

No network-wide tariff or timetable existed.

Documented or reasonably grounded examples include:

- Chinese records and itineraries often measure roads in stages or days rather than standardized mileage.
- Pliny gives twelve days from Coptos to Berenike and approximately forty days from the Arabian coast to Muziris, but his account concerns the Roman–Indian maritime branch.
- Rabban Bar Ṣawma’s narrative places roughly two months between Ningxia and Khotan in the late thirteenth century. [Rabban Bar Ṣawma narrative](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/eastasia/13bar-sauma-nestorianpilgrim.asp)
- Well-served medieval regions might place caravanserais roughly 30–40 km apart—approximately a day’s journey—but this UNESCO generalization should not be projected uniformly backward into antiquity. [UNESCO overview](https://www.unesco.org/en/silk-roads/about-silk-roads)
- Étienne de la Vaissière estimates from cross-checked evidence that the price of silk approximately doubled between Dunhuang and Samarkand in the first half of the eighth century. The calculation is valuable but is not a universal freight rate. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/sogdian-trade/)

Risks included thirst, cold, avalanche, sandstorm, river loss, animal mortality, banditry, piracy, warfare, arbitrary tolls, confiscation, debt, fraud, enslavement, and epidemic disease.

## 5. Agents and Operators

### 5.1 States and rulers

Relevant governing powers included, at different times:

- Han, Northern dynasties, Sui, Tang, Tibetan, Song, Liao, Western Xia, Jin, Yuan, and Ming regimes;
- Xiongnu, Yuezhi/Kushan, Wusun, Kangju, Hephthalite, Turkic, Türgesh, Karluk, Uyghur, Karakhanid, Kara-Khitan, and Mongol powers;
- Achaemenid predecessors, Seleucids, Greco-Bactrians, Parthians, Sasanians, Samanids, Ghaznavids, Seljuks, Khwarazmshahs, Ilkhans, Timurids;
- Roman, Byzantine, Arab caliphal, Mamluk, Georgian, Armenian, Rus’, Venetian, and Genoese authorities;
- Indian and Southeast Asian states controlling intersecting roads and ports.

They maintained only portions of the system. Their interests included horses, taxation, intelligence, tribute, luxury consumption, military supply, ideological prestige, and control of frontier populations.

### 5.2 Key named figures

- **Zhang Qian** (died 114 BCE): Han envoy whose reports reorganized Chinese knowledge of Central Asia.
- **Emperor Wu of Han** (r. 141–87 BCE): financed expansion, embassies, and Ferghana campaigns.
- **Ban Chao** (32–102 CE): general and administrator who restored Han influence in the Western Regions.
- **Kanishka** (probable second century CE): Kushan ruler associated with an empire spanning major Indian–Central Asian junctions; his exact chronology remains disputed.
- **Faxian** (travel c. 399–414): Chinese Buddhist pilgrim to India who documented routes and monasteries.
- **Xuanzang** (602–664; journey 629–645): pilgrim and scholar whose itinerary is a major source for Central and South Asian geography.
- **Maniakh** (sixth century): Sogdian merchant-diplomat who promoted direct Turkic–Byzantine silk relations.
- **Nanai-vandak** (early fourth century): Sogdian merchant whose surviving letter reported upheaval in China.
- **An Lushan** (c. 703–757): frontier commander of Sogdian and Turkic background whose rebellion shattered Tang military and fiscal structures.
- **Ibn Faḍlān** (journey 921–922): Abbasid envoy whose account documents merchant–pastoral interaction toward the Volga.
- **Yelü Chucai** (1190–1244): adviser who advocated systematic taxation within the Mongol Empire.
- **Chinggis Khan** (c. 1162–1227): conquest destroyed cities and populations but created the imperial framework later supporting long-distance movement.
- **Ögedei** (r. 1229–1241): systematized the Mongol relay network.
- **Giovanni da Pian del Carpine** (journey 1245–1247) and **William of Rubruck** (1253–1255): papal and royal envoys.
- **Rabban Bar Ṣawma** (c. 1220s/30s–1294) and **Yahballaha III/Markos** (1245–1317): East-Syriac pilgrims; Bar Ṣawma later served as Ilkhanid envoy to Europe.
- **Marco Polo** (1254–1324): Venetian traveler whose account is important but textually layered and disputed in details.
- **Ibn Battuta** (1304–1368/69): traveler whose Central and eastern Asian sections contain unresolved itinerary problems.
- **Timur** (1336–1405): conquest concentrated wealth and artisans in Samarkand while devastating many connected regions.

### 5.3 Merchant communities

**Sogdians** were the most visible overland merchant diaspora between approximately the fourth and eighth centuries. They maintained settlements in the Tarim Basin and China; served as merchants, translators, entertainers, diplomats, officials, and religious intermediaries; and carried silk, musk, metals, slaves, and other goods. Their dominance should not be projected unchanged into the Han period or after the tenth century. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/sogdian-trade/)

Other important operators included Bactrians, Indians, Persians, Armenians, Syriac Christians, Arabs, Khwarazmians, Turks, Uyghurs, Tibetans, Chinese, Jews, Rus’, Italians, and Southeast Asian maritime merchants.

The **Rādhānites**, described by ninth-century geographer Ibn Khordadbeh as multilingual Jewish merchants using several transcontinental routes, are documented textually. Whether they constituted a cohesive organization or a descriptive category is disputed.

### 5.4 Coercive labor and slavery

Enslaved people were cargo, labor, domestic property, diplomatic gifts, and military manpower. A Dunhuang contract dated **991** records the sale of a twenty-eight-year-old woman, Xiansheng, for **five bolts of silk**, witnessed by a Buddhist monk and nun. This is direct evidence that slavery was integrated into oasis markets and denominated in textile money. [British Museum exhibition guide](https://www.britishmuseum.org/exhibitions/silk-roads/silk-roads-large-print-guide)

States also imposed corvée labor for walls, canals, garrisons, transport animals, and relay stations. Under Mongol rule, local inhabitants were compelled to maintain the **jam/yam** and provision authorized travelers. Abuse of requisitions and tax farming by politically connected **ortoq** merchants is documented. [Encyclopaedia Iranica, Mongols](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/mongols/)

## 6. Operation and Mechanism

### 6.1 A typical commercial transfer

A plausible composite mechanism, with each element documented regionally, was:

1. A producer or court delivered silk, grain, metalware, horses, or another commodity to a local market, tax office, monastery, or merchant.
2. A resident merchant purchased, received, or advanced goods using coin, weighed silver, silk bolts, barter, or credit.
3. The merchant assembled animals and hired drivers, interpreters, guards, and brokers; large “caravans” were not universal, and safe regions might be crossed in small convoys.
4. At political borders the party presented a letter, passport, diplomatic status, or local guarantor and paid duties, gifts, protection money, or bribes.
5. At an oasis, cargo was sold, repacked, pledged, taxed, or transferred to another merchant familiar with the next jurisdiction.
6. News about prices, wars, debts, and partners moved by letter or oral report.
7. Final goods might be consumed thousands of kilometers from origin despite no participant having crossed the entire distance.

### 6.2 Instruments

- Silk bolts served as commodity, diplomatic gift, military pay, tax receipt, and unit of value in parts of Inner Asia.
- Chinese bronze cash, Sasanian drachms, Byzantine solidi, Islamic dirhams and dinars, local imitations, bullion, and barter coexisted.
- Written sale, loan, marriage, apprenticeship, and slave contracts survive from desert sites.
- Islamic merchants used partnerships and credit arrangements; letters of credit and instruments ancestral to checks are reported for Central Asian commercial communities, though terminology and continuity with later bills of exchange require caution. [Encyclopaedia Iranica, Central Asian economy](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/central-asia-x/)
- Mongol **paiza/gerege** tablets authorized requisition and access to relay services. Their abuse forced repeated regulation.
- Caravanserais, **ribat**, monasteries, fortified compounds, and private hosts supplied storage, worship, information, fodder, and lodging.
- Beacons and relay posts were principally state communication systems; commerce benefited indirectly and through privileged access.

### 6.3 Worked case: Sogdian Ancient Letter II

This is among the clearest surviving demonstrations of commercial information moving through the network.

**Origin and date.** Nanai-vandak wrote in Sogdian from the Dunhuang region in **313–314 CE** to his “noble lord” Varzakk in Samarkand.

**Contents.** He reported catastrophic events in China, including famine and the destruction or collapse of major centers, and discussed the condition of dispersed Sogdian communities and commercial affairs. Some identifications of Chinese place-names remain debated.

**Physical mechanism.**

1. News from eastern China reached the Sogdian community at Dunhuang through travelers and resident correspondents.
2. Nanai-vandak compiled political intelligence and business information on paper in Sogdian.
3. The letter was wrapped in brown silk and hemp.
4. It was addressed westward toward Samarkand, approximately several thousand kilometers away.
5. It never arrived. It was recovered from a Han-period watchtower site west of Dunhuang by Aurel Stein’s expedition.
6. Its failure is as informative as successful delivery: communication depended on a carrier, safe passage, and a chain that could break during political collapse.

The manuscript is 42 × 24.3 cm and is catalogued by the British Library as Or.8212/95. [International Dunhuang Programme](https://idp.bl.uk/collection/A141F9841B0B4BE1BDDA4FE6E2265948/)

### 6.4 Governance, policing, and fraud

No central police existed. Protection combined garrisons, escorts, walls, watchtowers, tribal agreements, urban guards, and payments to local rulers. Protection and predation were often performed by the same authorities.

Common abuses included:

- envoys conducting private trade under diplomatic immunity;
- merchants presenting themselves as tribute bearers to gain court access;
- tax farmers extracting beyond assessed amounts;
- counterfeit or imitated coin;
- forged credentials and abuse of relay tablets;
- confiscation during war;
- rulers monopolizing silk or horses;
- banditry and piracy;
- enslavement of captives and debtors.

The surviving record rarely permits conviction-level reconstruction of individual fraud cases. Administrative texts nevertheless show litigation, witnesses, sureties, penalties, and disputes over debt and property.

### 6.5 Scale

Network-wide tonnage, value, caravan count, and personnel totals are **unknown**. Claims of vast continuous streams are unsupported.

Quantifiable fragments include:

- Zhang Qian’s original mission: more than 100 people; two returned after thirteen years, according to the *Shiji*.
- Tang expenditure westward: de la Vaissière, citing Éric Trombert, reports that over 10 percent of fiscal receipts at times went west, largely as silk and hemp for armies. This is a reconstructed fiscal proportion, not private trade volume.
- Dunhuang Library Cave: modern estimates vary around 50,000–70,000 manuscripts and objects because “item,” “fragment,” and “bundle” are counted differently. [British Museum collection history](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/china/exploring-silk-roads)
- Pliny alleges that India, the Seres, and Arabia drained **100 million sesterces annually** from the Roman Empire. Elsewhere he gives 50 million for India. These are elite Roman literary estimates, probably polemical and not audited trade accounts. [Pliny, *Natural History* 12](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Pliny_the_Elder/12%2A.html)
- Valerie Hansen argues from surviving documents that much private Tarim trade was small-scale and local, while state expenditures and diplomatic exchange were disproportionately important. Critics accept the scarcity of direct China–Rome contact but dispute how surviving documents should be extrapolated to total traffic. [Cambridge review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-chinese-history/article/china-and-beyond-in-the-mediaeval-period-cultural-crossings-and-interregional-connections-editors-by-dorothy-c-wong-and-gustav-heldt-new-delhi-manohar-publishers-in-association-with-cambria-press-2014-444-pp-9999-6299-cloth-the-silk-road-a-new-history-by-valerie-hansen-oxford-oxford-university-press-2012-320-pp-isbn-9780195159318-3495-20-cloth-2195-1299-paper/8C15AC7D44A4620240F2E591EEF38B24)

## 7. Change over the Lifespan

### Phase I: Prehistory to c. 130 BCE—substrates

Jade, metals, horses, crops, technologies, and artistic forms already crossed Eurasia. Achaemenid, Hellenistic, Indian, Chinese, and steppe systems remained politically separate but partially connected.

### Phase II: c. 130 BCE–220 CE—Han, Parthian, and Kushan articulation

Han expansion secured the Hexi Corridor and intermittently the Tarim. Parthian Iran, Kushan territories, Indian markets, and Roman demand formed connected but segmented systems. Silk reached the Mediterranean, while Roman glass and coin reached Asia. Direct Roman–Chinese merchant contact is not securely demonstrated.

The Kushan sphere linked Bactria, Gandhara, and north India, assisting movements of Buddhist institutions and imagery toward Central Asia.

### Phase III: 220–589—fragmentation and diasporas

Chinese political division did not terminate exchange. The Sogdian Ancient Letters show merchant communities across northern China by 313–314. Kushan fragmentation, Sasanian consolidation, Hephthalite power, and oasis-state autonomy repeatedly rerouted commerce.

Buddhist translation communities expanded. Faxian’s journey demonstrates functioning pilgrim circuits but also severe travel hazards.

### Phase IV: 589–755—Sui–Tang and Turkic–Sogdian high connectivity

Tang military power, Turkic political frameworks, oasis agriculture, and Sogdian diasporas generated the best-documented early-medieval phase. Western goods, music, wine culture, religious communities, and foreigners became visible in Tang cities. [Metropolitan Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/ko/essays/internationalism-in-the-tang-dynasty-618-906)

The Tang conquest of Gaochang in 640 and expansion into the Tarim placed substantial infrastructure under imperial management. Sogdian merchants mediated high-value trade; state silk payments were economically central.

### Phase V: 751–1000—rebellion, Tibetan and Uyghur power, Islamic Central Asia

The **Battle of Talas (751)** did not single-handedly end the Silk Road or necessarily transmit papermaking through captured artisans as later popular accounts claim. Tang retreat was more directly accelerated by the **An Lushan Rebellion (755–763)** and internal fiscal-military crisis.

Tibet controlled Dunhuang from the late eighth to mid-ninth century. Uyghur power and Sogdian expertise supported other circuits. Islamic conquests transformed Central Asian language, law, coinage, religion, and commercial orientation. Sogdian communities gradually assimilated; Persian and Turkic languages became more prominent.

Dunhuang remained active into the tenth and early eleventh centuries. Its documents record trade, religion, credit, labor, and slavery. [UNESCO Dunhuang](https://en.unesco.org/silkroad/content/dunhuang)

### Phase VI: c. 900–1200—Islamic, steppe, and maritime reconfiguration

Samanid silver, Khwarazmian merchants, Turkic partnerships, and Islamic urban institutions connected Central Asia with Iran, the Volga, and the steppe. Caravanserai construction intensified from approximately the tenth century. Pious endowments funded some roads, bridges, hostels, and water facilities.

Song-era maritime commerce and powerful Indian Ocean merchant communities expanded. Overland and maritime systems were complementary and competitive.

### Phase VII: 1206–1368—Mongol imperial integration

Mongol conquest caused massacres, deportations, destruction of irrigation and settlements, and immense extraction. It also brought much of Eurasia under related ruling houses, standardized diplomatic protocols, expanded relay communications, and protected privileged merchants and envoys.

The so-called **Pax Mongolica** was neither universal nor continuously peaceful. Civil wars between Mongol khanates, local rebellion, predatory taxation, and abuse of relay privileges persisted.

Ortoq partnerships joined merchants with Mongol princes, princesses, and officials. Political capital reduced commercial risk for insiders while facilitating monopolies, tax farming, and coercive lending. [Encyclopaedia Iranica, Ilkhanid economy](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/economy-5-part2/)

This period enabled unusually long documented journeys by Franciscans, Armenian and Syriac clergy, Italian merchants, Muslim travelers, and Mongol envoys.

### Phase VIII: 1330s–1500—pandemic, fragmentation, and maritime ascendancy

The second plague pandemic emerged from Central Eurasian ecological and exchange systems; plague deaths at Issyk Kul are documented in 1338–1339. The precise human, rodent, and commercial path west remains unresolved.

The collapse of Yuan rule, Ilkhanid fragmentation, Golden Horde conflicts, and Timurid warfare disrupted established protections. Timur rebuilt Samarkand through wealth and forcibly relocated artisans while his campaigns destroyed other urban and agricultural systems.

In the fifteenth century, centralized Ottoman, Safavid-precursor, Central Asian, and Ming political environments changed access and taxation. Indian Ocean shipping, and after 1498 direct Portuguese Cape navigation, altered the relative economics of intercontinental trade. Land routes survived, but the classical/medieval network became one component of a different global system.

## 8. End or Transformation

There was no single closure.

### Proximate disruptions

- Mongol successor-state warfare.
- Black Death mortality and commercial contraction.
- Timurid conquest and urban destruction.
- Political division of Central Asia.
- Ming frontier policy and fluctuations in tribute trade.
- Ottoman–European conflict and altered Mediterranean brokerage.
- Portuguese entry into the Indian Ocean after Vasco da Gama’s voyage of 1497–1499.

### Structural causes

- Maritime shipping could move much larger cargoes at lower unit cost.
- Political protection became regionally fragmented.
- Demand shifted toward bulk spices, cotton textiles, ceramics, and other goods suited to sea transport.
- Older merchant communities assimilated or redirected their operations.
- Ports accumulated capital and institutional advantages.

### What survived

Caravan commerce continued between China, Central Asia, Russia, India, and Iran. The seventeenth-century Yarkand–Turfan corridor nevertheless became unsafe enough that a 1633 report described a critical northern Tarim section as closed to secure traffic. [UNESCO paper on route closure](https://en.unesco.org/silkroad/sites/default/files/knowledge-bank-article/inner%20asian%20muslim%20merchants%20at%20the%20closure%20of%20the%20silk%20routes%20in%20the%20seventeenth%20century.pdf)

Assets were not liquidated because no network corporation owned them. Roads returned to local use; caravanserais became inns, forts, warehouses, shrines, ruins, or settlements; postal systems were absorbed by successor governments; merchant families changed language, religion, citizenship, and market orientation.

## 9. Contested and Legendary Aspects

### 9.1 “A single road from China to Rome”

**Story:** caravans left Xi’an and traveled continuously to Rome.

**Assessment:** modern romantic simplification. Goods could traverse that distance through serial exchange, but direct end-to-end journeys are rarely documented. Archaeological evidence favors local and regional relay systems.

### 9.2 Zhang Qian “founded” the Silk Road

**Story:** one explorer opened an unknown route in 138 BCE.

**Evidence for:** his mission transformed Han geographic knowledge and preceded sustained western expansion.

**Evidence against:** Central Asian and interregional exchange long predated him; Chinese goods were already reaching Bactria indirectly.

**Status:** commemorative tradition built around a real turning point.

### 9.3 Roman legions in China

**Story:** Roman prisoners captured after Carrhae settled at Liqian in Gansu, and modern villagers are their descendants.

**Origin:** modern attempts to connect ambiguous Chinese reports, the place-name Liqian, unusual battle formations, and local appearance.

**Assessment:** no Roman military inscription, securely Roman burial assemblage, or ancient DNA evidence establishes the claim. It remains speculative and has been amplified by tourism and media.

### 9.4 The “blood-sweating heavenly horses”

**Story:** Ferghana horses literally sweated blood and were of divine ancestry.

**Evidence:** Han texts clearly attest the reputation and strategic value of Dayuan horses.

**Interpretation:** possible blood-feeding parasites, coat effects, ritual language, or court propaganda. Biological explanation remains unproven.

### 9.5 Silk stolen to Byzantium in hollow staffs

Procopius reports that monks brought silkworm eggs from “Serinda” to the Byzantine Empire under Justinian, traditionally dated around 552. Sericulture did reach Byzantium in the sixth century, but identities, itinerary, and precise date are uncertain. The hollow-staff episode is plausible narrative detail, not independently verified.

### 9.6 Talas and the invention of Islamic paper

**Story:** Chinese prisoners captured at Talas in 751 taught papermaking to the Islamic world.

**Assessment:** papermaking moved westward, and Samarkand became important, but evidence for a single-event transfer by Talas prisoners is late and disputed. Paper was already circulating outside China. The battle should not be treated as a proven technological switch.

### 9.7 Marco Polo never went to China

**Allegation:** omissions such as the Great Wall, tea, footbinding, or Chinese script prove fabrication.

**Evidence against:** his account contains administrative, monetary, geographic, and court details difficult to derive solely from contemporary European knowledge; expected topics depend on later assumptions about what a traveler “should” report.

**Unresolved:** the text was mediated by Rustichello and survives in divergent manuscripts. Some episodes may be borrowed, misunderstood, or embellished. Total fabrication is not the scholarly consensus.

### 9.8 Prester John

A Christian sovereign imagined somewhere beyond the Islamic world entered Latin literature through the twelfth-century *Letter of Prester John*. Europeans successively identified him with rulers in India, Central Asia, Mongol regions, and Ethiopia. Real East-Syriac Christian communities and Christian members of Inner Asian ruling families supplied material for the legend, but the universal monarch and his fabulous kingdom were literary and diplomatic constructions.

### 9.9 “Pax Mongolica”

**Traditional picture:** Mongol unification made Eurasia safe for all travelers.

**Documented core:** related khanates, relay posts, credentials, diplomatic conventions, and elite sponsorship enabled exceptional mobility.

**Qualification:** conquest, taxation, requisition, civil war, enslavement, and selective privilege were constitutive, not incidental. Safety was differentiated by status and political moment.

### 9.10 Silk Road as peaceful intercultural dialogue

This modern heritage narrative reflects genuine exchange, multilingualism, religious movement, and artistic borrowing. It becomes inaccurate when it omits armies, tribute extraction, forced migration, slave markets, epidemic movement, monopoly, and state violence. “Exchange” could be voluntary, coerced, accidental, or imposed.

### 9.11 Lost cities and treasure

Desert-buried cities, hidden Buddhist libraries, and caravan treasure became central to nineteenth- and twentieth-century exploration literature. Abandoned settlements such as Niya and Loulan and the actual discovery of Mogao Cave 17 supplied a factual substrate. Claims of intact, undiscovered treasure caravans are generally folklore unless tied to excavated contexts.

## 10. Historiography and Representations

### 10.1 Construction of the concept

Richthofen’s 1877 **Seidenstraße(n)** was subsequently popularized by Sven Hedin, Albert Herrmann, Aurel Stein, and other geographers and explorers. The concept expanded from particular ancient China–Central Asia corridors into a civilizational metaphor covering much of Eurasia.

Recent scholarship increasingly uses **Silk Roads** in the plural and questions:

- whether “road” imposes false linearity;
- whether silk is overprivileged relative to horses, textiles, metals, slaves, and state finance;
- whether Mediterranean–Chinese linkage is exaggerated;
- whether maritime routes should be included;
- whether the label obscures locally specific histories.

### 10.2 Major documentary discoveries

**Dunhuang Cave 17.** Discovered around 1900 by Wang Yuanlu; sealed probably in the early eleventh century. It contained tens of thousands of manuscripts, fragments, paintings, and textiles in Chinese, Tibetan, Sanskrit, Khotanese, Sogdian, Uyghur, and other languages.

Aurel Stein reached Dunhuang in 1907. His expedition was financed 60 percent by the Government of India and 40 percent by the British Museum. He paid Wang four silver ingots for fifty bundles of Chinese and five bundles of Tibetan manuscripts plus paintings, textiles, and artifacts. Contemporary legality, informed consent, and ethics remain contested; the resulting dispersal is documented, not legendary. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/china/exploring-silk-roads)

Other major corpora include:

- Niya and Loulan administrative documents;
- Turfan contracts and religious manuscripts;
- Sogdian Ancient Letters;
- Mount Mugh documents from the last phase of independent Sogdiana;
- Bactrian documents;
- Jewish commercial documents from Afghanistan;
- Chinese dynastic histories;
- Arabic and Persian geographies;
- Syriac Christian narratives;
- Armenian histories;
- European missionary and merchant accounts;
- inscriptions in the Karakoram and Central Asian cemeteries.

The International Dunhuang Programme reunites dispersed materials digitally. Japan’s Digital Silk Road Project provides multilingual archives, maps, photographs, and digitized rare books; its Toyo Bunko archive describes 245 books and more than 72,000 digitized pages. [Digital Silk Road Project](https://dsr.nii.ac.jp/index.html.en)

### 10.3 Archives and languages

Material is dispersed among institutions in China, India, the United Kingdom, France, Germany, Russia, Japan, Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, Kyrgyzstan, Kazakhstan, Iran, Afghanistan, Turkey, Italy, and elsewhere.

Principal languages and scripts include Chinese, Tibetan, Sogdian, Bactrian, Khotanese, Sanskrit, Prakrit, Kharoṣṭhī, Brāhmī, Middle Persian, Parthian, Arabic, Persian, Syriac, Armenian, Greek, Latin, Hebrew, Old Turkic, Uyghur, Mongolian, and later European vernaculars.

Losses include perishable commercial records, destroyed cities and archives, undocumented excavation, looting, wartime destruction, colonial dispersal, and manuscripts reused in bindings or religious deposits. Survival in arid Central Asia creates a geographic bias: preserved desert paperwork should not automatically be treated as representative of wetter regions.

### 10.5 Popular representations and divergences

- **Marco Polo traditions and screen adaptations:** commonly turn a mediated commercial-diplomatic account into an individual heroic exploration narrative.
- **Henry Yule’s editions of Marco Polo:** foundational but shaped by nineteenth-century imperial geography.
- **Aurel Stein and Sven Hedin travel literature:** combines valuable observation with the extraction-oriented assumptions of its age.
- **UNESCO Silk Roads programs and exhibitions:** correctly emphasize plural routes and cultural exchange but public presentation sometimes smooths political violence and economic discontinuity.
- **Peter Frankopan, *The Silk Roads*:** a broad Eurasian reinterpretation; its “Silk Roads” are much wider chronologically and geographically than the ancient-medieval network reconstructed here.
- **British Museum, *Silk Roads* exhibition (2024–2025):** emphasizes multiple centers, coerced movement, local objects, and the period c. 500–1000 rather than one East–West line.
- **Video games and strategy games:** usually represent the system as fixed trade lanes producing automatic wealth, whereas historical connectivity depended on brokerage, water, political privileges, and repeated transshipment.
- **Modern “New Silk Road” and Belt and Road rhetoric:** uses the historical name to legitimate contemporary infrastructure and diplomacy. This is an afterlife of the metaphor, not institutional continuity.

## 12. Conclusions Established by the Evidence

1. The Silk Road was a **network of networks**, not a road or organization.
2. Eurasian exchange long predated Zhang Qian; Han intervention produced a major political and logistical reorganization.
3. Most movement was **relay movement** through local markets, diasporas, courts, monasteries, and transport specialists.
4. States were often the largest identifiable purchasers, payers, and infrastructure providers.
5. Sogdian merchants were central during the fourth–eighth centuries but did not control every route or period.
6. Maritime exchange was ancient and eventually carried much more bulk cargo than overland pack transport.
7. Silk functioned as commodity, status object, diplomatic instrument, tax medium, and money-like unit, but it was never the network’s sole cargo.
8. Religious transmission depended on translators, donors, monasteries, merchant communities, and political patronage; it was not an automatic byproduct of trade.
9. Coercion—slavery, forced provisioning, deportation, tribute, monopoly, and military violence—was embedded in the system.
10. Quantitative claims about total trade volume are generally unrecoverable. Local prices, contracts, fiscal allocations, manuscript counts, itineraries, and archaeological distributions are more reliable than grand totals.
11. The Mongol era increased elite and official mobility while also producing conquest, forced labor, and extractive privilege.
12. No single event ended the Silk Road. Between the fourteenth and sixteenth centuries its medieval configuration fragmented and was absorbed into new Central Asian, imperial, and oceanic systems.
13. The unified “Silk Road” is partly a nineteenth- and twentieth-century historiographical construction, but it names real historical connections when used cautiously and plurally.
