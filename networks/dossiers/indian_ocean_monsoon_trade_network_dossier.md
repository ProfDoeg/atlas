# Indian Ocean monsoon trade network: Network Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `indian_ocean_monsoon_trade_network_network_dossier.md`

**Subject:** The medieval maritime-commercial system linking the Swahili coast, Arabia and the Red Sea/Persian Gulf, India and Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia, and southern China.

**Working chronological bounds:** approximately **700–1511 CE**, with necessary treatment of antecedents before 700 and continuities after 1511.

**Evidence vocabulary used below**

- **Documented:** supported directly by contemporary texts, inscriptions, documents, coins, excavated contexts, or securely identified material remains.
- **Strong inference:** the evidence does not record the event or institution directly, but several independent data sets make the interpretation likely.
- **Plausible inference:** consistent with the evidence but not uniquely demonstrated.
- **Reported:** stated by a contemporary or near-contemporary source whose testimony requires qualification.
- **Disputed:** serious scholars or primary sources offer incompatible interpretations.
- **Tradition/legend:** a remembered or literary account not independently established.
- **Unresolved:** present evidence does not permit a confident conclusion.

## 1. Identification and Definition

### 1.1 What the network was

The “Indian Ocean monsoon trade network” was not an incorporated body, a single route, or an institution possessing a charter. It was a changing **ecology of seasonal navigation, ports, merchant communities, production zones, legal jurisdictions, religious institutions, credit relationships, and information channels**. Its physical engine was the predictable seasonal reversal of winds over the Arabian Sea and adjoining waters. Its institutional engine was a succession of local arrangements—customhouses, port officials, merchant brokers, rulers’ guarantees, partnerships, kinship groups, religious courts, warehouses, and ship-owning ventures—rather than centralized government.

In the Arabian Sea, southwesterly winds generally blow from approximately June through September or October and northeasterly winds from approximately November through April or May. The system was predictable enough to schedule ocean crossings but remained exposed to cyclones, reefs, currents, delayed wind changes, disease, piracy, war, and shipwreck. “Predictable” therefore does not mean mechanically regular or safe. [Cambridge, “Networks and social cohesion in ancient Indian Ocean trade”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-global-history/article/networks-and-social-cohesion-in-ancient-indian-ocean-trade-geography-ethnicity-religion/D32B1FF1DCA90833C02C80E7B37B166E)

The network was **polycentric and segmented**. Most cargoes, travelers, and information passed through several hands and ships rather than moving directly from East Africa to China. Important exchange zones included:

1. the Red Sea–Aden circuit;
2. the Persian Gulf–Hormuz/Siraf circuit;
3. East Africa–South Arabia–western India;
4. Gujarat–Malabar–Sri Lanka;
5. the Bay of Bengal;
6. the Straits of Malacca and Indonesian seas;
7. the South China Sea–Guangzhou/Quanzhou circuit.

The famous map-line running continuously from Kilwa to Quanzhou is therefore a **modern schematic representation**, not a fixed itinerary. Ports and routes changed with wind, political conditions, cargo, vessel type, access to fresh water, and the location of markets.

### 1.2 Names

There was no single medieval proper name for the whole network.

| Language or tradition | Attested or commonly used expression | Evidentiary status and meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Modern English | Indian Ocean trade network; monsoon trade system; maritime Silk Roads; western Indian Ocean trade | Modern analytical names, not medieval corporate titles |
| Arabic | **al-Baḥr al-Hindī** (البحر الهندي), “Indian Sea”; **Baḥr al-Hind** (بحر الهند); routes or science of **ʿilm al-baḥr** | Historical geographical and nautical vocabulary, but not the name of one organization |
| Arabic geographical usage | **Bilād al-Zanj** (بلاد الزنج), lands of the Zanj; **al-Sawāḥil** (السواحل), the coasts | Regional terms; “Swahili” ultimately relates to Arabic *sāḥil/sawāḥil*, but Kiswahili itself is a Bantu language |
| Kiswahili | **bahari ya Hindi**, Indian Ocean; **pwani**, coast; *Waswahili*, coastal people | Modern or continuing vocabulary; no demonstrated medieval Kiswahili title for the entire network |
| Persian | **Daryā-yi Hind** (دریای هند), Indian Sea | Geographical expression, not a chartered network |
| Sanskrit and Indic traditions | **samudra**, sea/ocean; named ports and merchant communities rather than a system-wide title | No single securely attested pan-oceanic institutional name |
| Chinese | **海道** (*haidao*, sea routes); **海舶** (*haibo*, seagoing ships); modern **海上丝绸之路/海上絲綢之路** (*Haishang Sichou zhi Lu*, Maritime Silk Road) | The last is a modern historiographic and heritage term. It should not be projected backward as a medieval self-designation |
| Portuguese | **Carreira da Índia**, India Run; **Estado da Índia** | Names for the Portuguese imperial system that entered and partially restructured the older network, not names for the pre-Portuguese whole |

Quanzhou was called **Zaytūn/Zaiton** in Arabic and western accounts. The Chinese state called its maritime-trade offices **市舶司** (*shibosi*). The Quanzhou office was established in 1087 and remained until its transfer to Fuzhou in 1472. [Quanzhou municipal account of the Maritime Trade Office](https://quanzhou.gov.cn/lyb/lytj/rmjdtj/202202/t20220224_2699981.htm)

### 1.3 Period boundaries

#### Beginning: why circa 700?

This is an analytical boundary, not a founding date.

- **Antecedents are much older.** Maritime links joined Mesopotamia, Arabia, the Indus region, India, Sri Lanka, East Africa, and the Mediterranean in antiquity. Late-antique and first-millennium exchange therefore cannot be treated as a blank prehistory.
- **Circa 600–800:** East African sites such as Unguja Ukuu, Fukuchani, Tumbe, Manda, and Shanga show increasingly durable coastal settlements and imported objects. Unguja Ukuu is securely dated broadly from the seventh to mid-eleventh centuries and contained Middle Eastern, South Asian, and Chinese ceramics alongside imported beads. [PNAS archaeobotanical study and site table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4914162/)
- **Seventh–ninth centuries:** Muslim and Persian Gulf merchants became increasingly important after the Islamic conquests; direct Arabia–China contacts are documented from at least the eighth century. [UNESCO overview](https://www.unesco.org/en/silk-roads/about-silk-roads)
- **Ninth century:** excavated mosques at Shanga and related sites indicate local Islamic practice, although individual dates and construction phases remain debated. [Cambridge, Horton on Shanga](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquaries-journal/article/abs/early-muslim-trading-settlements-on-the-east-african-coast-new-evidence-from-shanga/4380B8CD8CC7F45EB77B98FD48C5E766)

Thus circa 700 marks a recognizable medieval reorganization: Islamic commercial institutions in western sectors, expanding Persian Gulf and Red Sea navigation, South and Southeast Asian intermediary ports, and emerging Swahili coastal communities.

#### End: why 1511?

No single event ended the network. Three defensible endings are:

- **1498:** Vasco da Gama’s fleet reached Calicut, opening a direct armed Cape–India connection.
- **1505–1510:** Portuguese forts and naval stations at Sofala, Kilwa, Mozambique, Cochin, and Goa began imposing a new military-fiscal layer.
- **1511:** Afonso de Albuquerque captured Malacca, after earlier Portuguese expansion toward Hormuz and Goa. This placed several major chokepoints or commercial centers under Portuguese attack or control.

The dossier uses **1511** as the end of the bounded medieval biography because the old system was thereafter compelled to operate inside a new, enduring arena of Cape-route shipping, cannon-armed fleets, royal monopolies, fortified bases, and Portuguese *cartaz* licensing. The network did **not disappear**. Gujarati, Arab, Persian, Tamil, Malay, Javanese, Swahili, Chinese, and other traders continued, diverted routes, obtained passes, resisted, collaborated, or smuggled. Consequently “transformation” is more accurate than “collapse.”

### 1.4 What counts as inside

Included are:

- sea routes governed substantially by monsoon timing;
- coastal and island ports from southeastern Africa to southern China;
- inland feeder systems carrying gold, ivory, textiles, horses, grain, and other goods to ports;
- Red Sea and Persian Gulf outlets to Cairo, the Mediterranean, Iraq, and Iran;
- merchant correspondence, credit, partnership, brokerage, customs, insurance-like risk division, and religious adjudication;
- pilgrimage and diplomatic movement using the same shipping and ports;
- coerced movement, including slavery.

Excluded as independent systems, though treated where connected, are:

- the trans-Saharan network;
- inland Silk Road routes;
- purely local fisheries and cabotage without demonstrable participation in wider exchange;
- the Portuguese Cape system after it became a distinct imperial network.

## 2. Formation

### 2.1 No single founder

**Documented:** no ruler, merchant, community, or technological inventor “founded” the medieval Indian Ocean network. It accumulated through countless decisions by sailors, port communities, producers, rulers, brokers, and migrants.

The older claim that a Greek sailor named Hippalus “discovered” the monsoon route is not a tenable foundation story for the medieval network. Sailors in the Indian Ocean had used seasonal winds long before Greco-Roman authors described them. At most, surviving Mediterranean texts record when such knowledge entered a particular documentary tradition.

### 2.2 Preconditions

#### Environmental regularity

The monsoon made long open-water passages schedulable. A merchant could leave South Arabia or East Africa during one seasonal regime, reside abroad while buying or selling, and return on the reversed wind. A round trip between southern China and the Arab world could require roughly two years because vessels waited for multiple seasonal windows; a Guangzhou government history summarizes the traditional observation that voyages to nearer foreign countries might return in a year but voyages involving the “Dashi,” the Arab-Islamic world, took two. [Guangzhou municipal history](https://www.gz.gov.cn/zlgz/whgz/content/post_9296313.html)

#### Maritime knowledge

Navigation combined:

- inherited route knowledge;
- observation of stars and the altitude of celestial bodies;
- wind and current calendars;
- sounding and recognition of water color;
- landmarks, islands, birds, clouds, and smells;
- memorized bearings and measured runs;
- later, magnetic compasses and written rutters.

Aḥmad ibn Mājid’s fifteenth-century *Kitāb al-fawāʾid fī uṣūl ʿilm al-baḥr wa-l-qawāʿid* systematized winds, stellar navigation, routes, hazards, and port approaches. The Library of Congress preserves a manuscript presentation of this nautical tradition. The recurrent claim that Ibn Mājid personally piloted Vasco da Gama from Malindi is **disputed and probably a later identification**; da Gama certainly employed an experienced Indian Ocean pilot, but the pilot’s identity is not securely established as Ibn Mājid. [Library of Congress manuscript](https://www.loc.gov/resource/amed.2008401696/?st=gallery), [technical survey of Arab navigation sources](https://rutter-project.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/R_TN_Arab_Navigation-v51-1-1.pdf)

#### Shipbuilding

Western Indian Ocean vessels commonly used timber planking secured partly or wholly through stitching or lashing, with coconut coir playing an important role. Ibn Battuta reported that Indian and Yemeni ships were sewn because flexibility reduced catastrophic breakage on reefs. His explanation reflects a traveler’s interpretation, not an engineering experiment, but sewn-plank traditions are independently established. [South Indian selections from Ibn Battuta](https://akshara.ink/books/foreign-notices-south-india/25-1333-45-ad-ibn-battta/)

Chinese and Southeast Asian shipbuilding followed different traditions, including large compartmented hulls. Ships, crews, pilots, and cargoes did not map neatly onto ethnic labels: a “Chinese,” “Kollam,” or “Barībatan” ship could denote construction, home port, intended route, ownership, or customary designation rather than the identity of everyone aboard.

#### Port institutions

Ports provided:

- anchorages and pilots;
- water and provisions;
- labor for loading, unloading, repair, and portage;
- warehouses and customs houses;
- brokers and interpreters;
- weighing and assay;
- lodging and worship;
- courts and notaries;
- protection, convoy, or armed coercion.

The network could therefore function without a central sovereign. Rulers financed port works or protection when advantageous and extracted customs, compulsory purchases, gifts, anchorage dues, and tribute.

#### Social infrastructure

Repeated transactions depended on reputation, correspondence, family ties, religious affiliation, diaspora residence, and trusted agents. Religion could supply courts, common calendars, oath forms, almsgiving obligations, literacy, and lodging, but commerce was not divided into sealed Muslim, Jewish, Hindu, Buddhist, or Christian networks. Surviving Geniza letters show cooperation across religious lines and use of Muslim captains, Indian agents, Jewish merchant partnerships, and local rulers’ officials.

### 2.3 Predecessor systems absorbed

The medieval system inherited:

- Red Sea traffic between Egypt, the Horn of Africa, Arabia, and India;
- Persian Gulf traffic between Mesopotamia, Iran, India, and Southeast Asia;
- Roman and late-antique demand for aromatics, gems, ivory, silk, and pepper;
- South Indian and Sri Lankan port systems;
- Austronesian maritime mobility across island Southeast Asia and into Madagascar;
- Buddhist pilgrimage and monastic connections between South and East Asia;
- pre-Islamic Arabian and Persian merchant settlement.

It did not simply displace these systems. It recombined them after the Islamic conquests, Tang–Song commercial growth, the formation of Swahili towns, and the shifting political fortunes of ports such as Siraf, Aden, Hormuz, Guangzhou, and Quanzhou.

### 2.4 Formation on the Swahili coast

**Documented:** early Swahili settlements were African communities integrated with farming, fishing, craft production, and coastal movement. Kiswahili is a Bantu language. Archaeology no longer supports an account in which Persian or Arab colonists created coastal civilization on an empty or culturally inert shore. [Oxford Handbook summary](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/28041/chapter-abstract/211957817)

**Also documented:** immigrants from Southwest Asia did arrive, marry locally, and contribute to coastal communities. Ancient DNA from medieval burials indicates predominantly African ancestry with a significant Southwest Asian component; before about 1500 that Asian component is mainly Persian-related and disproportionately male. The sampling is limited to particular sites and mostly elite or urban burial contexts, so it cannot be generalized mechanically to the whole coast. [Nature ancient-DNA study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-05754-w)

The best-supported reconstruction is therefore **local African development plus sustained incorporation of overseas people, Islam, objects, and practices**, not either absolute isolation or wholesale foreign colonization.

## 3. Constituent Nodes

The dates below indicate participation in the medieval network, not necessarily initial settlement.

### 3.1 Swahili coast and southeastern Africa

#### Mogadishu and the Benadir ports

- **Role:** northern East African manufacturing and export zone; connection to Arabia and western India.
- **Goods:** textiles or cloth reported in later medieval descriptions, ivory, animal products, enslaved people, and provisions.
- **Evidence:** Arabic geography, traveler reports, ceramics, architecture, coins, and later Portuguese descriptions.
- **Status:** securely attested major port; exact trade volumes unresolved.

#### Barawa and Marka

- **Role:** intermediate Benadir ports linking Somali hinterlands and the wider ocean.
- **Evidence:** texts, settlement remains, and later navigation.
- **Status:** securely attested; their relative rank changed over time.

#### Shanga, Manda, and the Lamu archipelago

- **Dates:** approximately eighth–fourteenth/fifteenth centuries in varying phases.
- **Role:** early urbanizing and Islamic nodes; northern Swahili transshipment and craft centers.
- **Evidence:** mosques, houses, burials, imported ceramics, glass, beads, coins, and local pottery.
- **Scale:** Manda covered roughly 20–25 hectares at one stage; around 1100, imported ceramics constituted approximately 10 percent of domestic ceramic assemblages in the cited excavation synthesis. This is an archaeological frequency, not proof that imports formed 10 percent of all consumption or trade value. [“When Did the Swahili Become Maritime?”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4368416/)

#### Mombasa and Malindi

- **Role:** anchorages and commercial towns between the Lamu zone and Zanzibar; later diplomatic and strategic centers.
- **Fifteenth-century significance:** Chinese expeditionary contacts and, in 1498, Malindi’s cooperation with da Gama’s fleet.
- **Status:** securely attested; Malindi’s later reputation as the origin of China’s first giraffe is oversimplified because the 1414 giraffe was presented by Bengal, probably after reaching Bengal through Indian Ocean exchange.

#### Pemba: Tumbe and later towns

- **Tumbe:** approximately 600–950; substantial early settlement with unusually dense imported goods.
- **Role:** northern coastal redistribution, craft production, fishing, and agriculture.
- **Status:** securely attested archaeologically.

#### Zanzibar: Unguja Ukuu and Fukuchani

- **Unguja Ukuu:** seventh to mid-eleventh centuries; large pre-urban port with Middle Eastern, South Asian, and Chinese ceramics and imported beads.
- **Fukuchani:** sixth century onward, with evidence of settlement, craft activity, and imports.
- **Interpretation:** different bead distributions indicate that adjacent East African regions could belong to partially distinct commercial circuits rather than one uniform coastal market. [Zanzibar glass-bead study record](https://iris.unive.it/handle/10278/3694658)

#### Kilwa Kisiwani

- **Dates:** occupied from the early medieval period; major entrepôt especially from the thirteenth through fifteenth centuries.
- **Role:** principal intermediary for gold moving from southeastern African production zones through Sofala; also handled ivory, timber, animal products, ceramics, beads, textiles, precious metals, and other goods.
- **Infrastructure:** coral-stone mosques, elite houses, palace and commercial structures, harbor approaches, wells, causeways, and a minting tradition.
- **Political form:** a sultanate centered on a city-state, not a territorially uniform empire in the modern sense.
- **Survival:** extensive ruins; UNESCO World Heritage property with Songo Mnara. UNESCO summarizes its thirteenth–sixteenth-century commerce in gold, silver, pearls, perfumes, Arabian wares, Persian earthenware, and Chinese porcelain. [UNESCO Kilwa and Songo Mnara](https://whc.unesco.org/ar/list/144)

Claims that Kilwa monopolized all East African or southeastern African commerce should be treated cautiously. It was exceptionally important but coexisted with rival and complementary ports.

#### Mafia, Songo Mnara, and neighboring ports

- **Role:** satellite and rival nodes in the Kilwa region.
- **Evidence:** coral architecture, imported ceramics, coins, and maritime landscape study.
- **Status:** securely attested, but political subordination to Kilwa varied.

#### Comoros and northern Madagascar

- **Role:** stepping stones, provisioning zones, settlement regions, and links between East Africa, Madagascar, and the wider ocean.
- **Goods and transfers:** food plants, ceramics, beads, people, livestock, and craft traditions.
- **Evidence:** archaeology and archaeobotany demonstrate African and Austronesian connections.
- **Status:** securely within overlapping oceanic circuits; not every island was continuously part of the same political or commercial chain.

#### Chibuene and Sofala

- **Chibuene:** approximately 700–1000 as a southern entry point for imported beads and connection to the interior.
- **Sofala:** later principal outlet associated with gold from the Zimbabwe plateau.
- **Connection inland:** via rivers, overland carriers, regional markets, and political authorities rather than a single road.
- **Status:** securely attested; exact allocation of gold among ports cannot be reconstructed annually.

### 3.2 Arabia, Red Sea, and Persian Gulf

#### Aden

Aden was the western Indian Ocean’s best-documented medieval brokerage and customs center.

- **Peak documentary period:** especially eleventh–thirteenth centuries.
- **Institutions:** customs house (*al-furḍa*), governors, merchant representatives, shipowners, captains, porters, warehouses, brokers, Jewish courts, and correspondence channels.
- **Connections:** Egypt, Jeddah, South Arabia, East Africa, Gujarat, Konkan, Malabar, Sri Lanka, and occasionally farther east.
- **Evidence:** hundreds of Cairo Geniza letters, accounts, and legal instruments. Princeton’s continuing census reports **668 documents and counting**, dated mainly circa 1080–1240; Goitein initially identified more than 300. The expanding count reflects joins and reclassification, not 668 surviving complete letters. [Princeton Geniza Lab](https://genizalab.princeton.edu/projects/indian-ocean-documents-cairo-geniza)

#### Jeddah, ʿAydhab, and Red Sea ports

- **Jeddah:** principal maritime gateway for Mecca and the hajj.
- **ʿAydhab:** Egyptian–Red Sea transit point linked by desert routes to the Nile.
- **Role:** pilgrims, merchants, correspondence, spices, aromatics, and high-value cargo.
- **Constraints:** reefs, difficult winds, water supply, desert transfers, and taxation.

#### Fustat/Cairo and Alexandria

These were not Indian Ocean ports but were crucial terminal and financial nodes. Goods moved from Red Sea ports across desert or riverine connections to the Nile, then to Fustat and Alexandria. The Ben Ezra Synagogue Geniza in Fustat preserved letters that had traveled from India and Aden.

#### Mecca

Mecca was simultaneously a pilgrimage destination, market, information exchange, and legal-religious center. Pilgrim shipping carried merchandise, and merchants performed pilgrimage; the categories cannot be cleanly separated.

#### Siraf

- **Peak:** approximately ninth–tenth centuries.
- **Role:** Persian Gulf entrepôt serving Basra, Iran, India, and China.
- **Decline:** earthquakes, political shifts, environmental and channel changes, and the rise of successor ports; there was no instantaneous abandonment.
- **Status:** securely attested through archaeology and Arabic accounts.

#### Kish and Hormuz

- **Kish:** important after Siraf’s decline.
- **Old Hormuz and island Hormuz:** major thirteenth–fifteenth-century interchange between Iran, Arabia, India, and routes farther east.
- **Reported in 1442:** ʿAbd al-Razzāq described Hormuz as frequented by sailors from China-facing and Southeast Asian regions, Bengal, Sri Lanka, the Maldives, and India. His wording demonstrates perceived reach, not a ship register. [Study of Melaka in Arabic, Persian, and Turkish sources](https://doi.org/10.1080/13639811.2024.2373582)

#### Dhofar, Shihr, Qalhat, Muscat, and Socotra

These provided aromatics, water, transshipment, pilots, and staging. Socotra sat near routes between Aden, the Horn, East Africa, and India. Political control and relative importance shifted among South Arabian and Omani powers.

### 3.3 Western and southern India

#### Khambhat/Cambay and Gujarat ports

- **Role:** major manufacturing and redistribution zone.
- **Exports:** cotton textiles, indigo, beads, manufactured objects, foodstuffs, and re-exported cargo.
- **Imports:** horses, metals, aromatics, precious goods, and East African products.
- **Network position:** linked the Persian Gulf, Red Sea, East Africa, Indian coasts, and Southeast Asia.
- **Institutions:** merchant corporations and communities, brokers, shipowners, customs officials, and rulers’ port administrations.
- **Status:** securely major; surviving quantitative evidence is fragmentary.

#### Bharuch, Ghogha, Diu, and related ports

These acted as specialized or feeder ports within Gujarat’s shifting estuarine geography. Silting and changing channels were material determinants of port fortunes.

#### Konkan and Kanara ports: Chaul, Goa, Dabhol, Mangalore, Bhatkal, Honnavar

These connected hinterland products to ocean shipping.

**Mangalore** is exceptionally visible because Abraham Ben Yijū, a Jewish merchant originally from the western Islamic world, lived there in the 1130s–1140s, operated a brass workshop or associated manufacturing venture, and exchanged letters and cargo with Aden. His papers reveal one merchant’s network, not the total structure of Mangalore’s commerce.

#### Malabar: Kannur, Pantalayini Kollam, Calicut, Cochin, Kollam/Quilon

- **Pepper:** the defining export, though not the only one.
- **Imports:** horses, metals, precious goods, Chinese wares, Southeast Asian aromatics, and textiles from elsewhere in India.
- **Calicut:** principal late-medieval open entrepôt under the Samutiri/Zamorin. Muslim merchant communities and port officers were central to commerce while the ruler supplied political protection and extracted revenue.
- **Kollam:** major earlier port with links to the Red Sea, Persian Gulf, Sri Lanka, and China.
- **Cochin:** increased in importance in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries and allied with the Portuguese against Calicut.

#### Sri Lanka: Mantai and later ports

Mantai was a major early-medieval transshipment center positioned between the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea. Excavated materials include craft debris and imports from multiple regions. Its role changed as shorelines, kingdoms, and trade patterns shifted. Sri Lanka also supplied gems, cinnamon, elephants, and other products.

### 3.4 Bay of Bengal and Southeast Asia

#### Coromandel ports and merchant organizations

Tamil inscriptions document merchant associations such as the **Ainnurruvar/Ayyavole Five Hundred**, **Manigramam**, and **Anjuvannam**. Their exact organization varied by time and place; they were not multinational corporations in the modern sense. They endowed temples, negotiated privileges, organized protection, and participated in overland and maritime trade.

#### Bengal: Satgaon and Chittagong

- **Exports:** textiles, rice, sugar, and other products.
- **Role:** Bay of Bengal termini and links to inland river systems.
- **Status:** securely attested in later medieval travel and diplomatic sources.

#### Srivijaya/Palembang and the Strait system

From approximately the seventh century, Srivijayan power benefited from traffic through or around the Straits of Malacca. Claims of complete control are too strong: ships could use alternative passages, while authority fluctuated. A UNESCO study notes both navigational hazards near Aceh and evidence for coercion or piracy associated with the strait routes. [UNESCO paper on Indonesian trade contacts](https://es.unesco.org/silkroad/sites/default/files/knowledge-bank-article/trade_contacts_with_the_indonesian_archipelago.pdf)

#### Kedah, Aceh/Lambri, Java, and island ports

These were provisioning, collecting, and redistribution zones for forest products, spices, aromatics, metals, ceramics, and food. Merchant residence and political allegiance changed frequently.

#### Malacca

- **Formation:** around 1400, although exact founding and the date and circumstances of the ruler’s conversion to Islam are disputed.
- **Role:** fifteenth-century transshipment center connecting the Bay of Bengal, Indonesian archipelago, South China Sea, and western merchants.
- **Institutions:** ruler, *shahbandars* or harbor masters associated with different merchant communities, warehouses, markets, pilots, and multilingual brokerage.
- **End of medieval phase:** Portuguese conquest in 1511. The official Melaka state history records a Portuguese arrival in 1509 and Albuquerque’s conquest in August 1511, but its account of Parameswara’s conversion in 1414 reflects one state tradition among disputed chronologies. [Melaka state history](https://melaka.gov.my/kerajaan/info-negeri/sejarah.html)

### 3.5 China

#### Guangzhou/Canton

- **Role:** major Tang and Song maritime port.
- **Institutions:** foreign quarter or **蕃坊** (*fanfang*), foreign headman, markets, warehouses, mosque traditions, and maritime-trade supervision.
- **Violence:** in 878–879 the rebel Huang Chao’s forces attacked Guangzhou; later Arabic reports give very large numbers of foreign dead. The event is credible, but reported totals such as 120,000 or more are **not reliable census figures**.
- **Travel time:** western merchants commonly had to remain through seasonal cycles; later local histories describe a two-year Arab–China turnaround. [Guangzhou government history](https://www.gz.gov.cn/zlgz/whgz/content/post_9296313.html)

Claims that the Guangzhou *shibosi* was created precisely in 714 or that 100,000 foreign merchants lived there should be treated as **reported or traditional figures**, not secure administrative statistics. A Guangzhou historical office repeats the claim that the trading-season customs treasury received 50,000 dinars daily, but this derives from transmitted literary evidence and is not supported by surviving daily accounts. [Guangzhou historical-office discussion](https://www.gzsqw.org.cn/c/sdfzpc/smxz/39974.jhtml)

#### Quanzhou/Zayton

- **Peak:** tenth–fourteenth centuries, especially Southern Song and Yuan.
- **Maritime Trade Office:** established in 1087.
- **Infrastructure:** docks, bridges, pagodas serving as landmarks, warehouses, roads and canals, ceramic kilns, iron production, the Qingjing Mosque, Islamic tombs, Hindu religious sculpture, and other foreign-community remains.
- **Hinterland integration:** ceramics and metals moved from production sites to river and maritime transport.
- **Status:** securely one of the largest nodes, but superlatives such as “largest port in the world” are source-dependent and not quantifiable from surviving tonnage records. [UNESCO Quanzhou property](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1561/)

Zhao Rukuo, a maritime-trade official, compiled the **Zhufan zhi** (諸蕃志, 1225), describing foreign countries and commodities using both documentary material and information from merchants. It is indispensable but mixes direct commercial intelligence, earlier books, hearsay, and marvels. [Chinese Text Project](https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?if=en&res=520299)

#### Other Chinese nodes

Ningbo, Yangzhou, and later ports participated in domestic and overseas circulation. Their importance varied with dynasty, policy, river systems, warfare, and the location of courts and production zones.

## 4. Connections

### 4.1 East African interior to the Swahili coast

Gold, ivory, iron, animal products, timber, food, and enslaved persons reached coastal settlements through layered exchanges. Producers, local chiefs, caravan organizers, porters, market intermediaries, and coastal merchants all participated. No evidence supports an image of foreign merchants routinely traveling unimpeded from the ocean to every production zone.

The gold connection joined Zimbabwe-plateau polities and intermediary markets to Chibuene, Sofala, Kilwa, and other ports. Kilwa’s rise was associated with its position in this system, but the idea of an exclusive and continuously enforced monopoly is likely an exaggeration.

### 4.2 East Africa–South Arabia/Aden

**Cargo east or north:** ivory, gold, tortoiseshell, rock crystal, timber, animal products, ambergris, and enslaved people.

**Cargo south:** cloth, beads, ceramics, metals, glass, foodstuffs, and religious or written materials.

**Movement:** seasonal sailings with coastal stops; direct open-water passages were possible but not universal.

**Evidence:** Arabic geographies, archaeology, Geniza references, later navigational writing, and imported objects.

A circa-1104 Geniza court note concerns oriental goods carried by two merchants killed at the East African port identified in the edition as Qāḍib. The document records not an abstract “route” but a legal aftermath involving death, cargo rights, and testimony. [Princeton edition of T-S 12.627](https://commons.princeton.edu/media/geniza/5B.1.3%20India%20Book-%20Trade%20docs%20197-290/T-S%2012.627%20%28PGPID%209232%29.pdf)

### 4.3 Aden–western India

This is the best-documented connection.

- Ships were often named by destination: a “Kūlamī” ship for Kollam, a “Barībatanī” ship for Valapattanam.
- Merchants divided goods among vessels to spread risk.
- Captains collected freight, while porters, brokers, guards, and customs officials took fees.
- Letters and accounts traveled opportunistically with trusted merchants.
- Round trips often required residence abroad for a season or longer.
- Pepper, textiles, iron, brass and copper goods, indigo, lac, spices, aromatics, and coin or bullion moved through the connection.

### 4.4 Persian Gulf–Gujarat/Malabar

Siraf, Kish, Qalhat, and Hormuz connected Iranian and Iraqi markets with India. Horses were an important west-to-east cargo because many Indian rulers demanded imported warhorses. Textiles, spices, precious goods, food, and metals moved both ways. High-value cargoes could support long-distance shipment, but ships also carried bulky staples on regional legs.

### 4.5 India–Sri Lanka–Southeast Asia

Ships crossed the Bay of Bengal according to season, often via Sri Lanka, Coromandel ports, Bengal, Kedah, northern Sumatra, or the Nicobar and Andaman vicinity. The UNESCO study gives a typical pattern: northeast winds from late October through January or February carried ships from Java and Sumatra toward Sri Lanka and South India; southwest winds from late May through August carried ships eastward. Exact departure dates varied annually and locally. [UNESCO Indonesian trade study](https://es.unesco.org/silkroad/sites/default/files/knowledge-bank-article/trade_contacts_with_the_indonesian_archipelago.pdf)

### 4.6 Southeast Asia–China

Traffic concentrated through the South China Sea into Guangzhou and Quanzhou. Cargo included spices, aromatic woods, resins, medicines, ivory, rhinoceros horn, pearls, gems, metals, cottons, silk, and ceramics.

Chinese ceramics are highly visible archaeologically because they survive burial and breakage. Their visibility should not be mistaken for dominance by value or volume. Textiles, foodstuffs, wood, and people usually survive poorly.

### 4.7 China–western ocean: direct and relay models

Early Arabic accounts describe voyages connecting the Persian Gulf with China. By later centuries, long-distance commerce was increasingly segmented among western Asian, Indian, Southeast Asian, and Chinese shipping. Both models coexisted.

Aden-to-China was therefore sometimes an imagined commercial horizon rather than a routine voyage by the same merchant. Geniza merchants overwhelmingly operated in the western Indian Ocean; East Asian goods often reached them through Indian or Southeast Asian relays.

### 4.8 Pilgrimage, diplomacy, and information

The same routes carried:

- pilgrims to Mecca and Buddhist or Hindu sacred sites;
- envoys and royal gifts;
- Qurʾans, legal opinions, devotional texts, and scholarly letters;
- news of prices, wars, rulers, shipwrecks, and epidemics;
- navigational instructions;
- genealogies and prestige claims;
- diplomatic demands and tribute.

The Ming expeditions of 1405–1433 used established port knowledge and commercial infrastructure but were state fleets with diplomatic and military objectives. They did not create the routes they followed.

### 4.9 Travel time and frequency

No comprehensive timetable survives.

- A western Arabia–India crossing could take several weeks under favorable conditions.
- Delays awaiting wind could last months.
- China–Arabia round trips could consume approximately two years.
- “Annual trade” often meant one seasonally structured opportunity, not daily scheduled service.
- Cabotage might be frequent within a regional sailing season.
- Ships sometimes sailed in loose company for security, but permanent convoys were not universal.

Figures should be understood as ranges reconstructed from itineraries, not guaranteed passage times.

## 5. Agents and Operators

### 5.1 Sailors and pilots

Professional knowledge resided in pilots, captains (*rubban/nākhudhā* and cognate titles), helmsmen, sail handlers, shipwrights, caulkers, rope makers, divers, and fishermen familiar with local approaches. Elite travel writers preserve only fragments of their knowledge.

Aḥmad ibn Mājid is the best-known named navigator because his writings survive. His fame should not obscure generations of unnamed African, Arab, Persian, Gujarati, Malabari, Tamil, Malay, Javanese, and Chinese pilots.

### 5.2 Merchants and partnerships

Merchants might:

- own a whole ship;
- own shares in a vessel;
- rent cargo space;
- commission an agent;
- travel with goods;
- remain abroad as a resident factor;
- combine trade with manufacture, diplomacy, pilgrimage, or scholarship.

Partnership arrangements were flexible. Islamic *qirāḍ/muḍāraba*-type investment, Jewish partnership and agency instruments, family capital, advances, debt, and entrusted consignments all appear. A single commercial vocabulary did not govern the whole ocean.

### 5.3 Aden’s Jewish “India traders”

Named figures include:

- **Joseph Lebdī**, a prominent eleventh–twelfth-century India trader;
- **Maḍmūn ibn Ḥasan ibn Bundār**, merchant leader or *nāgid* in Aden and correspondent of Ben Yijū;
- **Abraham Ben Yijū**, resident merchant and manufacturer at Mangalore;
- **Ḥalfon ben Netanʾel**, merchant-scholar traveling among Egypt, Aden, India, and other locations;
- **Khalaf ibn Isḥāq**, Aden-based merchant and agent;
- **Saʿīd ibn Marḥab**, associated with the legal investigation of a wreck and also known as a Yemeni Jewish poet;
- **Abū Ghālib al-Rubbān**, a captain named in commercial correspondence;
- **ʿAlī ibn Manṣūr al-Fawfalī** and a shipowner rendered PDYR/Fidyār in scholarship.

These individuals belonged to wider, multi-confessional port societies. Their documentary visibility results from Geniza preservation, not necessarily demographic or commercial dominance.

### 5.4 Bomma and coerced agency

Ben Yijū owned an Indian enslaved man usually called **Bomma** in modern scholarship. Bomma traveled to Aden on business and acted as an agent; a letter reports that he became drunk and demanded money at Maḍmūn’s office. The episode demonstrates that an enslaved person could exercise commercial agency while remaining legally owned. It must not be romanticized as free partnership. [Cambridge Genizah Fragments discussion](https://wwwe.lib.cam.ac.uk/GF/22/)

Ben Yijū’s household and papers also reveal sexual and domestic relations shaped by slavery. Exact identities and family relationships have been reconstructed from fragmentary documents and remain partly disputed.

### 5.5 Swahili rulers and merchant elites

Named rulers in the Kilwa Chronicle include **ʿAlī ibn al-Ḥasan**, represented as a Shirazi founder. Coin evidence supports an early ruler of that name or title, but the chronicle’s migration narrative is not independently established in its full form.

Swahili political economies included:

- sultans or town rulers;
- councils and elite lineages;
- mosque authorities;
- merchants and brokers;
- craft producers;
- farmers and fishers;
- caravan and port labor;
- enslaved people.

The claim that foreign merchants “controlled” the coast is too simple. Local rulers and merchants controlled access, production, settlement, and legal protection, while overseas communities supplied ships, markets, capital, and information.

### 5.6 Indian port officers and rulers

At Calicut the Samutiri/Zamorin protected an open commercial market and derived revenue from trade. Muslim merchant leaders and a *shahbandar*-type port administration mediated foreign shipping. The city’s competitive advantage depended partly on a reputation that merchants could trade under protection regardless of origin.

Merchant communities included Mappilas of Malabar, Gujaratis, Tamils, Arabs, Persians, Jews, Christians, Malays, and Chinese. These were internally diverse categories, not corporations with unitary policies.

### 5.7 Chinese officials and foreign communities

Chinese maritime commerce operated under alternating encouragement and restriction.

- *Shibosi* officials registered ships, inspected cargo, collected tax or compulsory purchase shares, and regulated prohibited goods.
- Foreign quarters had recognized headmen and religious institutions.
- Officials sometimes recruited prominent foreign merchants to attract more shipping.
- Smuggling and unauthorized coastal trade persisted whenever official restrictions made licensed trade costly or impossible.

Zhao Rukuo’s position in maritime-trade administration gave him access to commercial intelligence, but he did not personally visit all the places described in the *Zhufan zhi*.

### 5.8 Diplomats and travelers

- **Ibn Battuta** traveled through East Africa, Arabia, India, the Maldives, Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia, and perhaps China in the fourteenth century. His Indian and Maldivian experiences contain direct observation; details of his China journey are disputed and may combine observation, reports, and literary geography. [French scholarly edition introduction](https://classiques.uqam.ca/classiques/ibn_battuta/voyages_tome_III/voyages_tome_III_intro.html)
- **ʿAbd al-Razzāq Samarqandī** traveled as a Timurid envoy through Hormuz to Calicut and Vijayanagara in 1442–1444. [Journey summary and translated extracts](https://naniwadekar.com/text/deccan/abdur-razzaq-1443.html)
- **Zheng He**, **Ma Huan**, and other Ming personnel traveled with state expeditions. Often-repeated fleet totals—over 300 ships and 27,000 or 37,000 personnel—depend on how different expeditionary and support vessels are counted and should not be treated as uniformly attested for every voyage.

### 5.9 Taxers, protectors, predators, and rivals

The same actor could be protector in one source and predator in another.

- Port rulers taxed ships and guaranteed markets.
- Naval forces suppressed some raiders while extracting compulsory payments.
- Pirates could sell protection or receive official employment.
- Merchants smuggled goods around customs.
- Officials could confiscate prohibited or undeclared cargo.
- Rival ports recruited foreign merchants and attacked competitors.
- Portuguese fleets later treated unlicensed navigation as grounds for seizure while describing the system as protection and monopoly enforcement.

## 6. Operation and Mechanism

### 6.1 Preparing a voyage

A merchant first selected a market and seasonal window. Capital could be personal, pooled, advanced by investors, or obtained through credit. The merchant then:

1. bought or commissioned goods;
2. packed them in bales, baskets, jars, boxes, or sacks;
3. marked ownership;
4. obtained cargo space from a shipowner or captain;
5. appointed a traveling agent or correspondent at destination;
6. drafted letters and instructions;
7. divided consignments among vessels when possible;
8. paid or recorded freight, brokerage, porterage, storage, and customs;
9. arranged witnesses or legal documentation.

Measurement was difficult because units differed. The **bahār**, for example, varied by commodity and port. Figures in bahārs cannot safely be converted into modern kilograms without specifying the local standard.

### 6.2 Arrival and customs

At a regulated port, a vessel might wait at an outer anchorage for pilotage or permission. Officials or brokers boarded, manifests or oral declarations were presented, and cargo was unloaded for weighing and inspection.

At Aden, the *furḍa* functioned as customs house and controlled space. A circa-1135 letter from Maḍmūn to Ben Yijū records copper and pepper along with customs, freight, porters, and safekeeping charges. This is direct evidence that the delivered price consisted of many small institutional costs, not merely purchase price plus ocean freight. [Geniza letter JRL B 4089](https://geniza.princeton.edu/en/documents/5233/)

At Song–Yuan Chinese ports, maritime-trade offices registered shipping, levied tax, conducted official purchasing, controlled selected commodities, and supervised foreign merchants. The precise rates varied by reign, port, and commodity.

### 6.3 Brokerage and sale

Foreign merchants required local knowledge: quality grades, currency exchange, weights, demand, creditworthiness, legal forums, and political patronage. Brokers could aggregate small purchases, find buyers, translate, arrange storage, and represent absent partners.

Goods were not necessarily sold immediately. A merchant might hold pepper if the price fell, sell textiles gradually, barter, or forward goods to another port.

### 6.4 Money, bullion, and credit

The network did not possess a universal currency.

Media included:

- gold dinars and local gold coinages;
- silver coins and bullion;
- copper coin;
- cowries, especially in particular regional markets;
- weighed metal;
- commodity credit;
- book accounts;
- debts and setoffs;
- entrusted consignments.

Chinese copper cash, Islamic dinars, Indian coinages, and local Swahili coins circulated in different spheres. Kilwa’s coins expressed local authority and facilitated exchange, but their presence outside Kilwa does not automatically prove political rule.

Credit reduced the need to transport coin for every transaction but did not create a single ocean-wide clearinghouse. Bills resembling later exchange instruments existed in some regions, while much remittance depended on correspondent balances and trusted carriers.

### 6.5 Information system

Letters carried:

- commodity prices;
- instructions to buy or sell;
- warnings about dishonest agents;
- news of ships and wrecks;
- debt collection;
- family matters;
- requests for legal opinions;
- political news;
- recommendations and reputational judgments.

Letters often existed in multiple copies. Maḍmūn’s circa-1135 letter survives in fragments of four or five copies, showing deliberate duplication against loss. [JRL B 4089](https://geniza.princeton.edu/en/documents/5233/)

Information moved only as fast as people and ships. A rumor of wreck might require corroboration over one or more seasons.

### 6.6 Security and risk

Risks included:

- reef or storm wreck;
- fire;
- spoiled cargo;
- water shortage;
- epidemic;
- piracy or private seizure;
- war and blockade;
- customs confiscation;
- theft by crew, porter, agent, or official;
- debtor default;
- sudden price falls;
- death abroad;
- disputed inheritance.

Risk was managed through diversification, reputation, partners at several ports, written instructions, witnesses, litigation, and cargo division. There is no evidence for a uniform marine-insurance system across the medieval ocean.

### 6.7 Worked example I: Khalaf ibn Isḥāq’s account for Ḥalfon, circa 1134

This is the closest surviving example of an annual account sent from Aden to a partner in India.

**Parties**

- **Khalaf ibn Isḥāq:** agent or commercial partner in Aden.
- **Ḥalfon ben Netanʾel:** recipient in Bharuch, Gujarat.

**Document**

- Cairo Geniza shelfmark **T-S J1.53**.
- Written in Judaeo-Arabic.
- Sent with a covering letter.
- Described by the Princeton Geniza Project as the only surviving account sent by an Aden merchant to a partner in India summarizing an entire year.

**Transaction**

1. Ḥalfon entrusted capital or goods to Khalaf or maintained an account with him.
2. Khalaf conducted sales and purchases in Aden over a year.
3. He recorded commodity proceeds and outstanding inventory.
4. The surviving summary totals **1,469 dinars** in the transactions described.
5. Pepper accounted for **828 dinars**.
6. Textiles accounted for **578.21 dinars**.
7. A small amount involved musk.
8. The account did not represent all business because some pepper remained unsold, apparently after a price decline.
9. The account and letter were carried from Aden toward Bharuch.
10. They later entered Ḥalfon’s papers and ultimately the Fustat Geniza.

The numbers are unusually reliable as document readings but their economic interpretation has limits. They do not reveal annual port volume, the purchasing power of every dinar, total invested capital, or all unsold goods. [Princeton Geniza account T-S J1.53](https://geniza.princeton.edu/en/documents/9063/)

### 6.8 Worked example II: the Kūlamī shipwreck and legal communication

A letter written after a vessel bound for Kollam disappeared reconstructs the network’s disaster-response mechanism.

1. A ship known as the **Kūlamī**, because it sailed for Kollam, departed with cargo belonging largely to one merchant but with goods from many Jewish Aden merchants.
2. It sailed in association with a **Barībatanī** ship bound for Valapattanam.
3. The second vessel supplied the principal report that the Kūlamī ship had been wrecked.
4. During the remainder of that year and the next, travelers arrived in Aden from India, the Zanj coast, Berbera’s hinterland, Ethiopia, and South Arabia.
5. Their reports were compared; all available testimony supported the conclusion that the ship was lost.
6. The Aden Jewish court considered whether the evidence was sufficient to release Sitt al-Ahl from the status of *agunah*, a woman unable to remarry because her husband’s death was not legally proved.
7. The court referred the difficult ruling to higher authorities in Egypt.
8. The letter therefore carried maritime intelligence, evidentiary summaries, and a legal question from Aden to the Mediterranean-connected Jewish scholarly world.

This case demonstrates the integration of shipping, rumor verification, multi-port travelers, family law, and correspondence. [Geniza letter PER H 161](https://geniza.princeton.edu/en/documents/5445/)

## 7. What the Network Carried

### 7.1 From East and southeastern Africa

Securely or plausibly documented exports include:

- gold;
- ivory;
- mangrove poles and other timber;
- tortoiseshell;
- rock crystal;
- ambergris;
- animal skins and exotic animals;
- iron or iron products in some circuits;
- enslaved persons.

“Slaves” was not a single commodity category. Captives and enslaved persons became soldiers, sailors, porters, agricultural laborers, domestic workers, concubines, artisans, and commercial agents. Surviving records rarely permit reliable totals for the medieval period.

### 7.2 From Arabia and the Persian Gulf

- frankincense and other aromatics;
- dates and foodstuffs;
- horses;
- pearls;
- metals and metalware;
- glass and ceramics;
- coin or bullion;
- re-exported Mediterranean and West Asian goods.

### 7.3 From India and Sri Lanka

- pepper;
- cotton textiles;
- indigo and dyes;
- iron and steel;
- rice and grain;
- sugar;
- coconut products;
- areca and betel;
- spices and medicines;
- brass and copper manufactures;
- beads;
- gems and pearls;
- cinnamon from Sri Lanka;
- elephants and ivory products;
- skilled labor and mercantile services.

Indian textiles were structurally important because they could be exchanged in Africa and Southeast Asia for local goods. Their archaeological signature is weak compared with ceramics.

### 7.4 From Southeast Asia

- cloves, nutmeg, and mace;
- camphor;
- sandalwood and aromatic woods;
- resins;
- tin;
- gold;
- spices and medicines;
- forest products;
- rice and provisions;
- ceramics and manufactured goods;
- enslaved persons.

### 7.5 From China

- silk;
- ceramics and porcelain;
- copper cash and metalware;
- paper;
- lacquerware;
- manufactured objects.

Chinese porcelain was never the sole or necessarily principal commodity. Its durability makes it disproportionately visible in excavation.

### 7.6 Intangible transfers

- Islam, including law, ritual, mosque forms, pilgrimage, and scholarship;
- Buddhism and Hindu religious forms in eastern sectors;
- Christianity and Jewish learning;
- Arabic as a language of trade, law, and scholarship in western sectors;
- Persian, Indic, Malay, and Chinese commercial vocabulary;
- shipbuilding and navigational techniques;
- crops and foodways;
- artistic forms and architecture;
- political titles and genealogical models;
- disease organisms, although specific transmission episodes are often unresolved.

## 8. Governance, Law, and Finance

### 8.1 Ownership

Nobody owned the network. Its components were owned or controlled by:

- rulers and states controlling ports;
- municipalities or town elites;
- religious foundations;
- shipowners and shareholder groups;
- merchants owning warehouses or workshops;
- rural producers and political authorities controlling supply zones;
- laborers and craftspeople owning tools or small boats;
- enslavers owning human beings under applicable legal regimes.

### 8.2 Political standing

The network existed under many jurisdictions:

- Fatimid, Ayyubid, and Rasulid authority in Egypt/Yemen;
- Abbasid and successor regimes around Iraq and Iran;
- Omani and Hormuzi rulers;
- Swahili city-states;
- Indian kingdoms and sultanates;
- Sri Lankan kingdoms;
- Srivijayan and successor states;
- Malacca;
- Tang, Song, Yuan, and Ming government.

A merchant leaving Aden for India changed legal regimes several times. Enforcement depended on location, community, ruler, and the ability to summon witnesses or patrons.

### 8.3 Religious law

Muslim merchants used Islamic courts and contracts; Jewish merchants used Jewish courts while operating in Islamic jurisdictions; Hindu and other merchant institutions used local assemblies, temples, inscriptions, and royal grants. These systems overlapped. A merchant might use a religious court for family or partnership law and a ruler’s port court for customs or violence.

### 8.4 Revenue

Port rulers financed protection and infrastructure from:

- customs;
- anchorage and market fees;
- compulsory royal purchase;
- gifts and tribute;
- brokerage or weighing charges;
- monopolies in selected commodities;
- fines and confiscations;
- taxes on resident communities.

Rates are poorly standardized in surviving evidence. Claims about enormous daily customs income at Guangzhou or total port revenue at Calicut are literary estimates unless attached to surviving fiscal accounts.

### 8.5 Costs

A shipment’s costs might include:

- purchase price;
- packaging;
- porterage;
- warehousing;
- brokerage;
- weighing;
- customs;
- ruler’s gifts;
- captain’s freight;
- agent commission;
- interest-like or profit-sharing return;
- losses from spoilage or price movement;
- protection or ransom.

The Geniza letters document this layered cost structure but cannot supply a universal percentage.

## 9. Failure, Fraud, Violence, and Suppression

### 9.1 Shipwreck and disappearance

Shipwreck was frequent enough to structure law and investment. The Kūlamī case shows that determining death could require a year or more of cumulative testimony. Cargo recovery depended on location, local custom, salvage capacity, and political power.

### 9.2 Fraud and breach of trust

Letters complain of:

- agents who failed to account;
- relatives whose reputations harmed business;
- delayed debt payment;
- inaccurate weights;
- customs avoidance;
- concealed cargo;
- captains or carriers who deviated from instructions.

Such accusations are evidence that disputes occurred, not proof that every accused person was guilty.

### 9.3 Robbery and killing

The circa-1104 Geniza deposition about merchants killed at an East African port shows that overseas homicide could generate litigation in Fustat. Long-distance recovery depended on witnesses and surviving partners rather than an ocean-wide police force.

### 9.4 Piracy

“Pirate” was often a political label. Raiders might be autonomous, protected by a ruler, or serving as naval auxiliaries. Chokepoints around the Straits, western Indian coasts, and Red Sea approaches were vulnerable. Coastal rulers could both suppress and profit from predation.

### 9.5 Slavery and forced labor

The network transported and employed enslaved people. East Africans were taken to Arabia, Persia, India, and other regions; people were also enslaved within India, Southeast Asia, and the Islamic world. Some enslaved agents traveled and traded, but commercial responsibility did not erase coercive legal status.

Portage, ship labor, domestic service, military service, and production could involve enslaved, bonded, dependent, or conscripted labor. Medieval sources rarely distinguish these categories consistently, so aggregate claims must remain cautious.

### 9.6 State violence in China

Foreign merchants were exposed to dynastic rebellion and collective violence. The Guangzhou massacre associated with Huang Chao in 878–879 is documented in transmitted Arabic reports, but the enormous victim totals are unverified. Later restrictions on maritime trade encouraged smuggling and displaced commerce among ports.

### 9.7 Ming expeditions

The Zheng He voyages combined diplomacy, trade, tribute exchange, intelligence, and coercion. They intervened militarily in Southeast Asian politics and attacked or arrested opponents. Describing them solely as peaceful friendship voyages is a modern state-centered simplification; describing them solely as colonial conquest is equally inadequate.

### 9.8 Portuguese naval violence

The Portuguese brought ships designed to combine ocean endurance with heavy artillery, supported by a monarchy seeking monopoly revenue and crusading advantage.

In 1502 Vasco da Gama seized and burned a Muslim passenger or pilgrim vessel conventionally called the **Miri/Merī**. A near-contemporary account reports approximately **380 men plus women and children**. Exact totals differ among sources, but the deliberate seizure, plunder, and burning are well attested. [University of Kent study citing the eyewitness *Calcoen*](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/id/document/3223875)

Portuguese forces bombarded ports, mutilated captives, seized ships, and attempted to block Red Sea commerce. Local rulers and merchants also used force, including attacks on Portuguese factories, fleets, and allies.

### 9.9 The *cartaz*

The *cartaz* was a Portuguese naval pass imposed on Asian shipping. It specified vessel, route, cargo, and sometimes prohibited destinations or commodities. Ships without acceptable papers risked seizure or destruction.

This was not simply anti-piracy protection. It was simultaneously:

- licensing;
- surveillance;
- customs and monopoly enforcement;
- coercive route control;
- a revenue device;
- a means of distinguishing allies from targets.

Enforcement was uneven. Merchants purchased passes, forged or evaded them, used non-Portuguese routes, sailed under protected rulers, or resisted with Ottoman, Gujarati, Malabari, and other support. [Om Prakash overview](https://aryabhattacollege.ac.in/samplepaper/637199480586038426IWE%20Unit%204%20Trading%20World%20of%20the%20Indian%20Ocea%20Om%20Prakash%20Ch%20Overview.pdf)

## 10. Change Over the Lifespan

### Phase I: circa 600–850 — coastal consolidation and Islamic-era reorientation

- African coastal villages expanded exchange and maritime specialization.
- Muslim and Persian merchants entered older routes.
- Guangzhou served as a major eastern terminal.
- Siraf and Gulf ports grew.
- Early mosques appeared on the Swahili coast.
- Srivijaya benefited from strait traffic.

The boundary between late antiquity and the medieval network is gradual.

### Phase II: circa 850–1000 — intensified long-distance linkage

- Siraf–India–China traffic became visible in Arabic writing.
- Imported ceramics and beads increased at several Swahili sites.
- Kilwa and other towns emerged or expanded.
- Islam acquired deeper local roots.
- Guangzhou suffered the Huang Chao attack.
- Siraf later declined, creating opportunities for Kish, Hormuz, Aden, and other ports.

### Phase III: circa 1000–1250 — documentary and urban expansion

- Aden became a central Red Sea–India intermediary.
- The Cairo Geniza corpus documents traders from circa 1080–1240.
- Manda, Shanga, Kilwa, Mogadishu, and other Swahili towns developed urban and stone-built institutions.
- Song China expanded maritime administration.
- Quanzhou’s *shibosi* opened in 1087.
- Gujarat, Malabar, Sri Lanka, and Tamil merchant organizations connected western and eastern sectors.
- Zhao Rukuo compiled the *Zhufan zhi* in 1225.

This period supplies unusually rich evidence, but that richness is partly archival accident.

### Phase IV: circa 1250–1350 — Yuan integration and changing hubs

- Yuan political unification supported extensive Chinese maritime commerce.
- Quanzhou reached exceptional prominence.
- Hormuz rose as the principal Gulf outlet.
- Kilwa strengthened its position in southeastern African gold exchange.
- Mongol wars and political instability disrupted some routes while opening others.
- Ibn Battuta’s journeys recorded a world of Muslim judges, merchants, ships, and port rulers from East Africa to China.

### Phase V: circa 1350–1400 — plague, political disruption, and reorganization

The Black Death affected port societies and commercial demand, but its exact Indian Ocean chronology and demographic impact are unevenly documented. It did not terminate trade.

In China, the transition from Yuan to Ming and maritime restrictions altered legitimate shipping. In the western ocean, merchant traffic continued through Hormuz, Aden, Gujarat, and Malabar.

### Phase VI: circa 1400–1433 — Ming expeditionary overlay

Seven major Zheng He expeditions connected China with Southeast Asia, India, Sri Lanka, Hormuz, Arabia, and East Africa. The fleets used established monsoon lanes and ports while adding state transport, tribute, intelligence, and naval intervention.

Reported fleet size varies. The Metropolitan Museum repeats a maximum of more than 300 ships and 37,000 sailors; such figures should be treated as reconstructions or totals associated with particular enumerations, not a fixed size for every expedition. [Metropolitan Museum giraffe record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/51649)

### Phase VII: circa 1433–1498 — merchant-led late-medieval peak

After Ming withdrawal from large expeditions:

- Malacca grew into a leading exchange center.
- Gujarati and Tamil merchants remained active eastward.
- Calicut became the principal Malabar entrepôt.
- Hormuz and Aden connected western markets.
- Kilwa and Sofala remained prominent in the African gold system.
- ʿAbd al-Razzāq and Ibn Mājid documented ports and navigation.

The network did not require Chinese state fleets to survive.

### Phase VIII: 1498–1511 — armed Cape-route intrusion

- **20 May 1498:** da Gama reached Calicut after obtaining an Indian Ocean pilot at Malindi. [Portuguese Navy history](https://www.marinha.pt/pt/a-marinha/historia/servir-portugal)
- **1500:** Cabral’s factory at Calicut was attacked and destroyed after escalating commercial conflict.
- **1502:** da Gama returned with a punitive fleet; the Miri massacre and bombardment intensified war.
- **1503:** the Portuguese consolidated alliance and fortification at Cochin.
- **1505:** forts or factories were established at Sofala and Kilwa; Francisco de Almeida became viceroy.
- **1507:** Portuguese operations targeted Hormuz; Mozambique Island became a base.
- **1508:** an Egyptian–Gujarati force defeated a Portuguese squadron at Chaul.
- **1509:** Portuguese victory at Diu weakened the coalition.
- **1510:** Albuquerque seized Goa.
- **1511:** Albuquerque conquered Malacca.

A Library of Congress chronological compilation records the sequence of Kilwa, Mombasa, Hormuz, Chaul, Goa, and Malacca campaigns. [Library of Congress chronology](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/gdc/gdclccn/a2/20/00/97/2/a22000972/a22000972.pdf)

## 11. End or Transformation

### 11.1 Proximate causes

The medieval configuration changed because Portugal:

- connected Atlantic and Indian Ocean shipping around the Cape;
- sent recurrent crown-financed armadas;
- deployed heavy shipboard artillery;
- fortified selected ports;
- claimed a monopoly in spices and strategic routes;
- imposed *cartazes*;
- attempted to divert commerce from the Red Sea toward Lisbon;
- exploited rivalries among Calicut, Cochin, Kannur, Gujarat, Hormuz, and other powers.

### 11.2 Structural causes

The old system had no unified navy or government because it did not previously require one. Its polycentric flexibility was a commercial strength but made coordinated resistance difficult. Port rivalries gave the Portuguese allies and bases.

Conversely, Portuguese strength was limited by distance, manpower, ship loss, corruption, local dependence, and the vastness of the ocean. They could seize chokepoints more readily than control every coastal voyage.

### 11.3 What did not end

- Monsoon navigation continued.
- Calicut, Gujarat, the Red Sea, and Southeast Asian routes continued outside or around Portuguese control.
- Asian merchants remained numerically and operationally indispensable.
- Existing brokers, pilots, shipwrights, porters, and financiers were absorbed into Portuguese ports.
- Ottoman, Gujarati, Acehnese, Malabari, and other powers contested Portuguese claims.
- Swahili communities continued trade, sometimes under Portuguese tribute and sometimes beyond effective control.

Thus 1511 marks a **change in the rules of violence and fiscal power**, not the disappearance of the Indian Ocean trading world.

### 11.4 Assets and personnel

Portuguese officials occupied or built:

- forts;
- factories;
- customs stations;
- arsenals;
- royal warehouses;
- naval patrol bases.

They also used existing anchorages, merchant districts, supply markets, pilots, interpreters, and local political alliances. Mozambique’s government history dates forts at Sofala to 1505 and Mozambique Island to 1507, followed by inland factories at Sena and Quelimane in later decades. [Government of Mozambique](https://portaldogoverno.gov.mz/penetracao-colonial/)

### 11.5 Successor systems

Successors and overlays included:

- the Portuguese *Estado da Índia*;
- Ottoman Red Sea defense and commerce;
- Safavid and Hormuzi Gulf systems;
- Mughal Gujarat and Surat;
- Aceh and Johor after the fall of Malacca;
- Omani maritime power;
- Dutch and English chartered-company systems;
- nineteenth-century Omani-Zanzibari commerce;
- steamship and telegraph networks.

These systems inherited monsoon knowledge, ports, diaspora communities, credit practices, and commodity geographies from the medieval network.

### 11.6 Surviving remains

Physical remains include:

- Kilwa Kisiwani and Songo Mnara;
- Swahili mosques, tombs, wells, houses, and harbor landscapes;
- Shanga, Manda, Unguja Ukuu, Tumbe, and related archaeological sites;
- Siraf and Hormuz remains;
- Aden’s port landscape, though heavily transformed;
- Indian port archaeology and inscriptions;
- Quanzhou’s docks, bridges, pagodas, mosque, tombs, production sites, and *shibosi* site;
- Guangzhou’s mosque and foreign-quarter traditions;
- shipwreck cargoes across Southeast Asian waters;
- manuscripts and documentary fragments in Cairo, Cambridge, Manchester, Oxford, Princeton-linked databases, London, Lisbon, Istanbul, China, India, and other collections.

## 12. Contested and Legendary Aspects

### 12.1 The Shirazi foundation tradition

**Story:** Persian princes or brothers from Shiraz sailed to East Africa, founded Kilwa and other towns, and established ruling dynasties. One form says ʿAlī ibn al-Ḥasan purchased Kilwa from an African ruler by surrounding the island with cloth; the seller later discovered that tides made it an island.

**Earliest recoverable form:** the tradition survives in the Arabic Kilwa Chronicle and a sixteenth-century Portuguese version associated with João de Barros. The sole surviving Arabic manuscript, British Library Or. 2666, was copied in 1877 from an older tradition, complicating textual reconstruction.

**Evidence for:**

- persistent local genealogical memories;
- Persian-related male ancestry in sampled medieval coastal burials;
- Persian Gulf objects and connections;
- coin evidence for a ruler called ʿAlī ibn al-Ḥasan.

**Evidence against a literal mass-colonization account:**

- Swahili language and settlement foundations are African;
- coastal sites precede the proposed migration;
- archaeology shows gradual transformation;
- the chronicle contains political genealogy and etiological story;
- no evidence establishes seven coordinated foundation ships or the cloth-purchase episode.

**Assessment:** Persian-related migration and elite intermarriage are documented; the chronicle’s precise voyage and dynastic sequence remain tradition or contested reconstruction. Ancient DNA supports part of the social memory without validating the complete legend. [Nature study](https://reich.hms.harvard.edu/sites/reich.hms.harvard.edu/files/inline-files/s41586-023-05754-w.pdf), [Iranica discussion](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/east-africa-i-economic-political-and-cultural-relations-through-1900/)

### 12.2 “Arab colonization created the Swahili”

This colonial-era model treated stone architecture, Islam, and imported goods as proof that civilization arrived from abroad.

**Assessment:** contradicted as a total explanation by settlement archaeology, linguistics, food remains, local pottery, and social continuity. Overseas migrants and influences were real, but they joined African societies. Colonial scholarship’s selective emphasis on mosques and imports distorted the balance of evidence. [Archaeology Magazine historiographic overview](https://archaeology.org/issues/january-february-2014/features/swahili-coast-towns/)

### 12.3 Sinbad as historical documentation

The *Sinbad* tales place merchants amid recognizable Indian Ocean motifs: Basra, islands, shipwrecks, monsters, precious goods, and repeated commercial voyages.

**Assessment:** literary folklore shaped by real maritime experience, not a recoverable merchant biography or itinerary. Individual episodes cannot be cited as documentary proof of routes or fauna.

### 12.4 The giraffe as a “tribute from Malindi”

A giraffe reached the Yongle emperor in 1414 from Bengal and was interpreted as a **qilin**, an auspicious creature. Bengal probably obtained it through western Indian Ocean connections. Later Ming contacts with East Africa supplied additional exotic animals.

**Assessment:** the animal’s East African origin is plausible or likely, but “Malindi directly sent China its first giraffe” compresses a multistage transfer. [Metropolitan Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/51649)

### 12.5 Zheng He’s “treasure ships”

Stories assign the largest ships lengths exceeding 120 meters and portray fleets of uniform gigantic vessels.

**Evidence:** Ming texts provide dimensions or categories, archaeological remains document major shipbuilding, and fleets were unquestionably large.

**Dispute:** translation of units, vessel categories, hydrodynamic feasibility, and whether dimensions refer to hull, deck, or ceremonial descriptions. The largest popular reconstructions are not archaeological measurements of an intact vessel.

### 12.6 Ibn Mājid as da Gama’s pilot

**Story:** the famous Arab navigator personally guided da Gama from Malindi to India.

**Evidence for:** later association, Ibn Mājid’s expertise, and da Gama’s use of a local pilot.

**Evidence against:** near-contemporary Portuguese sources do not securely name Ibn Mājid, and chronology and later Arabic material do not establish the identification.

**Assessment:** da Gama used Indian Ocean expertise; identification with Ibn Mājid is unverified and generally rejected or treated skeptically.

### 12.7 China “discovered” or colonized East Africa

Chinese ceramics and Zheng He’s voyages have generated claims of permanent Chinese colonies or Chinese discovery of Africa.

**Assessment:** Chinese goods and state fleets reached East Africa, and some people may have remained abroad, voluntarily or otherwise. No evidence demonstrates large permanent medieval Chinese colonies on the Swahili coast. “Discovery” is inappropriate for inhabited regions already linked to extensive networks.

### 12.8 Pre-Columbian African–American voyages

The Indian Ocean system is sometimes invoked to argue that Swahili, Malian, Arab, Indian, or Chinese sailors routinely crossed the Atlantic to the Americas.

**Assessment:** Indian Ocean navigational capacity does not itself prove Atlantic contact. No securely contextualized medieval American archaeological assemblage presently establishes a Swahili–Indian Ocean trade route to the Americas.

### 12.9 Portuguese “discovery of the sea route to India”

This traditional European phrasing means the first documented direct Portuguese voyage from Atlantic Europe around the Cape to India.

**Assessment:** Portugal discovered the route for itself. It did not discover India, East Africa, the monsoon, or the Malindi–Calicut connection. Da Gama’s final crossing depended on local navigational knowledge.

### 12.10 A harmonious pre-European ocean

One romantic counter-myth depicts pre-1498 commerce as peaceful free trade destroyed by Europeans.

**Assessment:** pre-Portuguese commerce usually lacked Portugal’s particular system of permanent armed monopoly patrols, but it included warfare, piracy, confiscation, slavery, coercive port policy, political tribute, and commercial rivalry. The contrast must be made institutionally, not as peace versus violence.

## 13. Historiography and Representations

### 13.1 Primary-source traditions

#### Arabic and Persian geography and travel

Major sources include:

- al-Masʿūdī;
- al-Idrīsī;
- Ibn al-Mujāwir;
- Yāqūt;
- Ibn Saʿīd;
- Ibn Battuta;
- ʿAbd al-Razzāq Samarqandī;
- Aḥmad ibn Mājid;
- Sulaymān al-Mahrī.

Their strengths are routes, ethnonyms, commodities, ports, winds, courts, and anecdotes. Their weaknesses include copying, hearsay, literary marvels, uncertain place identification, and elite perspective.

#### Chinese sources

- dynastic histories;
- port regulations and gazetteers;
- the *Zhufan zhi*;
- Wang Dayuan’s *Daoyi zhilüe*;
- Ma Huan’s *Yingyai shenglan*;
- Fei Xin and Gong Zhen;
- inscriptions and tombstones;
- ceramics and shipwrecks.

Chinese texts often classify foreign polities through tributary language that should not automatically be interpreted as effective Chinese sovereignty.

#### Geniza documents

The Cairo Geniza provides the most detailed merchant-level archive for the eleventh–thirteenth-century western Indian Ocean: letters, accounts, legal deeds, lists, and drafts. Its biases are substantial:

- Jewish preservation context;
- concentration in Egypt–Aden–India links;
- accidental survival;
- overrepresentation of literate merchants;
- fragmentary physical condition;
- many undated documents.

It nevertheless records operational details unmatched by chronicles.

#### Portuguese sources

From 1500, Portuguese documentation becomes dense: royal letters, armada instructions, chronicles, factory accounts, ship logs, legal claims, and correspondence. These sources provide dates and military detail but use imperial categories and often minimize Asian agency or justify violence.

### 13.2 Archaeology

Archaeology transformed interpretation by demonstrating:

- African foundations of Swahili settlements;
- variation among coastal communities;
- local craft and food production;
- gradual Islamization;
- imported ceramics and beads from multiple regions;
- coin circulation;
- integrated port landscapes rather than isolated stone monuments.

Imported-object counts must be contextualized. A concentration of Persian ceramics does not prove Persian political rule; Chinese ceramics do not identify the ethnicity of the carrier.

### 13.3 Major scholarly debates

#### Unity versus multiple circuits

K. N. Chaudhuri and later “Indian Ocean world” scholarship emphasized systemic unity. Critics and refinements stress regional circuits, ecological zones, and broken relays. The strongest model combines both: an interlinked macro-system composed of semi-autonomous networks.

#### Trade diaspora and trust

Philip Curtin’s trade-diaspora model encouraged study of resident foreign communities. Geniza scholarship and later work show that trust crossed religious boundaries and could rely on reputation, brokerage, and courts rather than ethnicity alone.

#### Swahili origins

Older Persian/Arab colonization theories were displaced by African-centered archaeological and linguistic work. Recent ancient DNA has complicated, not reversed, that conclusion by demonstrating significant Persian-related male ancestry in sampled communities.

#### European intrusion

Earlier histories portrayed 1498 as an abrupt world-historical break and Portuguese command as nearly total. Revisionist scholarship emphasizes Asian resilience and limited Portuguese capacity. A balanced reconstruction recognizes a genuine escalation in maritime coercion without mistaking claimed monopoly for complete control.

#### Quantification

The medieval ocean lacks continuous shipping registers or customs series. Researchers infer scale from:

- documentary accounts;
- coin finds;
- ceramics and beads;
- shipwreck cargoes;
- port size;
- architectural investment;
- literary descriptions.

No defensible total annual tonnage or value exists for the whole network.

### 13.4 Archives and losses

Major repositories include:

- Cambridge University Library’s Taylor–Schechter Genizah Collection;
- Bodleian Library;
- John Rylands Library;
- Jewish Theological Seminary and other Geniza collections;
- British Library Arabic and Persian manuscripts;
- Chinese national, provincial, municipal, and temple collections;
- Portuguese **Arquivo Nacional da Torre do Tombo** and related repositories;
- archaeological authorities and museums in Kenya, Tanzania, Mozambique, Oman, Yemen, India, Sri Lanka, Indonesia, Malaysia, and China.

Losses resulted from humidity, insects, war, redevelopment, looting, shoreline change, acidic soils, manuscript recycling, and the perishable nature of cloth, wood, rope, and paper.

### 13.5 Significant modern works

A working scholarly bibliography should include:

- K. N. Chaudhuri, *Trade and Civilisation in the Indian Ocean*.
- Janet Abu-Lughod, *Before European Hegemony*.
- S. D. Goitein and Mordechai A. Friedman, *India Traders of the Middle Ages*.
- Roxani Eleni Margariti, *Aden and the Indian Ocean Trade*.
- Sebastian R. Prange, *Monsoon Islam*.
- Edward A. Alpers, *The Indian Ocean in World History*.
- Michael N. Pearson, *The Indian Ocean*.
- Philippe Beaujard, *The Worlds of the Indian Ocean*.
- Abdul Sheriff, *Dhow Cultures of the Indian Ocean*.
- Mark Horton and John Middleton, *The Swahili*.
- Mark Horton, *Shanga*.
- Neville Chittick, *Kilwa* and *Manda*—foundational excavations whose cultural interpretations require later correction.
- John Carswell’s work on Mantai and maritime ceramics.
- Gerald R. Tibbetts, *Arab Navigation in the Indian Ocean before the Coming of the Portuguese*.
- Roderich Ptak, work on the China Seas and maritime Asia.

- Ashin Das Gupta, work on Indian merchants and ports.
- M. N. Pearson, Luís Filipe Thomaz, Geneviève Bouchon, and others on early Portuguese expansion.

### 13.6 Popular representations

#### Amitav Ghosh, *In an Antique Land*

Ghosh combines anthropology, memoir, and reconstruction of Ben Yijū and Bomma. It is unusually attentive to the Geniza evidence but uses literary framing and explicitly fills documentary silences.

#### *The Adventures of Sinbad*

Film, television, animation, and games transform a composite folklore cycle into a heroic biography. They preserve the imaginative geography of ocean commerce but are not historical reconstructions.

#### Maritime Silk Road exhibitions

Museums and UNESCO heritage projects illuminate ports and material culture. Some exhibitions adopt a continuous, harmonious “Silk Road” narrative shaped by modern diplomacy. Their archaeological data remain useful when separated from present-day policy language.

#### Zheng He films and television

Popular portrayals frequently magnify ship sizes, suppress military interventions, or present voyages as prototypes of modern geopolitical policy.

#### Vasco da Gama commemorations

Portuguese national narratives historically celebrated discovery and navigation while reducing the visibility of pilots, Asian merchants, massacre, blockade, and compulsory licensing. Postcolonial portrayals sometimes reverse the simplification by treating immediate Portuguese supremacy as complete.

## 15. Conclusions Established at Different Confidence Levels

### Securely established

- Medieval Indian Ocean commerce was a decentralized, monsoon-conditioned network rather than one route or organization.
- East Africa, Arabia, India, Southeast Asia, and China were connected through overlapping voyages and relay markets.
- Indigenous African communities created the foundation of Swahili society.
- Overseas settlers, including Persian-related men, joined and intermarried with those communities.
- Ports possessed customs, brokerage, legal, religious, and physical infrastructure.
- Goods, people, credit, letters, pilgrimage, technology, beliefs, and coercion moved through the same channels.
- Geniza documents directly demonstrate detailed eleventh–thirteenth-century Aden–India transactions.
- Portuguese entry introduced a durable system of armed royal monopoly, passes, forts, and Cape-route integration.
- The network survived 1511 in transformed form.

### Strongly inferred

- Most end-to-end Africa–China exchange occurred through several relays rather than single voyages.
- Textile trade was economically more important than its limited archaeological survival suggests.
- Marriage and kinship were important in stabilizing resident merchant communities.
- Political competition among ports helped both expand commerce and enable Portuguese intervention.

### Disputed

- Exact foundation dates for Kilwa and Malacca.
- Literal historical content of the Shirazi chronicles.
- Whether Ibn Battuta personally visited every Chinese place described.
- Maximum dimensions of Zheng He’s treasure ships.
- Ibn Mājid’s alleged role as da Gama’s pilot.
- The degree of Kilwa’s monopoly over Sofala gold.
- The extent to which the Portuguese immediately reduced Red Sea or Asian commerce.

### Not presently establishable

- Total annual tonnage or value of the whole network.
- Total medieval slave traffic across the Indian Ocean.
- Exact numbers of foreigners in Tang Guangzhou.
- A complete list of ships, sailors, or merchant houses.
- A single formation or termination date recognized by participants.
- A contemporary proper name for the entire system.

### Quantitative caution

The figures used in this dossier fall into three categories:

1. **document totals**, such as 668 Geniza items and counting;
2. **transaction figures**, such as the 1,469-dinar account;
3. **archaeological measurements**, such as Manda’s estimated hectares or ceramic frequencies.

None can be scaled directly into total Indian Ocean trade volume. Literary population, casualty, fleet, and customs figures are explicitly treated as reported estimates.
