# Potosí-to-Buenos Aires silver smuggling route: Network Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `potosi_to_buenos_aires_silver_smuggling_route_network_dossier.md`

## Scope, method, and evidentiary key

This dossier reconstructs the corridor connecting Potosí and the southern Atlantic through Buenos Aires, approximately **1580–1778**, with necessary attention to antecedents after 1545 and transformations through 1810.

The corridor was not a chartered organization with a stable membership, headquarters, or contemporary proper name. It was an overlapping system of:

- legally recognized roads and towns;
- licensed and unlicensed Atlantic shipping;
- illicit import and silver-export transactions;
- merchant credit and consignment;
- kinship, patronage, and official-protection networks;
- mule, cart, and pack-animal transport;
- forced movement of enslaved Africans;
- royal taxation, customs, confiscation, and prosecution;
- postal, official, and private correspondence.

The terms **“Potosí–Buenos Aires route,” “circuit,” “axis,” or “contraband network”** are principally analytical descriptions used by historians. They should not be mistaken for a formal colonial institution.

Evidence is marked as follows:

- **Documented:** directly supported by contemporary records or a close edition of them.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** a modern inference synthesizing incomplete records.
- **Official allegation:** a statement in inspections, prosecutions, confiscation proceedings, or royal correspondence; not automatically proved.
- **Estimate:** a reconstruction whose denominator or surviving documentation is incomplete.
- **Traditional/legendary:** a later story that cannot presently be demonstrated from contemporary evidence.
- **Unresolved:** the available evidence does not establish a firm answer.

## 1. Identification and Definition

### 1.1 What the network was

The network was the **Atlantic-facing commercial articulation of the Potosí mining economy**, running principally:

**Potosí → Tupiza → Humahuaca/Jujuy → Salta → San Miguel de Tucumán → Santiago del Estero → Córdoba → Buenos Aires → Río de la Plata → Brazil, Angola, Portugal, the Netherlands, France, Britain, or Spain.**

Silver generally moved south and east. Enslaved Africans, European and Brazilian manufactures, ironware, textiles, wine, sugar, paper, weapons, luxury goods, and commercial information generally moved north and west. Mules, hides, food, regional textiles, carts, and labor circulated in both the long-distance corridor and its regional branches.

The corridor competed with—but did not entirely displace—the authorized western route:

**Potosí → Oruro/La Paz or Arica → Callao/Lima → Panama → Portobelo → Caribbean convoy → Seville/Cádiz.**

“Evading the Lima monopoly” is broadly accurate but requires qualification. Lima did not possess a single statutory monopoly over all internal trade. Its merchants, consulado, wholesalers, and political allies benefited from the fleet-and-fair system, the Pacific connection, and rules intended to prevent Buenos Aires from drawing Upper Peruvian bullion and customers toward the Atlantic. The southern corridor evaded or circumvented a larger **Seville–Portobelo–Lima commercial order**, rather than merely one municipal prohibition.

### 1.2 Contemporary and historiographical names

No securely documented, universally accepted contemporary proper name for the illicit network has been found.

Attested or defensible descriptions include:

| Language | Term | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Spanish | *ruta Potosí–Buenos Aires* | Modern historiographical shorthand |
| Spanish | *circuito mercantil Potosí–Buenos Aires* | Modern analytical term |
| Spanish | *comercio por el puerto de Buenos Aires* | Contemporary generic description |
| Spanish | *comercio ilícito*, *trato ilícito*, *contrabando* | Contemporary/legal categories, varying by record |
| Spanish | *excesos y desórdenes del puerto de Buenos Aires* | Language associated with official investigation |
| Spanish | *arribadas forzosas* / *arribadas maliciosas* | Legal maritime pretext / alleged fraudulent practice |
| Spanish | *Camino Real del Perú* or *Camino Real a Potosí* | Road, not specifically the smuggling system |
| Portuguese | *contrabando da prata*, *comércio ilícito do Rio da Prata* | Descriptive scholarly terms |
| Portuguese | *rota Potosí–Buenos Aires* | Modern term |
| French | *route de Buenos Aires à Potosí* | Descriptive term in travel/historical literature |
| English | Potosí–Buenos Aires route; Buenos Aires contraband trade | Modern descriptive terms |

The word *contrabando* can obscure the spectrum between concealed commerce, tolerated irregularity, improperly licensed exchange, fraudulent confiscation, and legal trade accompanied by illegal side cargo. Historians including Zacarías Moutoukias consequently study illicit commerce together with colonial government rather than as a wholly separate underground economy.

### 1.3 Chronological bounds

**Primary dossier bounds: 1580–1778.**

- **1580** is chosen because Juan de Garay’s refoundation of Buenos Aires created the lasting port-town and administrative node through which the corridor operated.
- **1585–1586** supplies some of the earliest surviving evidence of shipping between Brazil and Buenos Aires.
- **1594** is an alternative beginning: the Crown’s prohibition made the conflict between the southern route and the authorized commercial system explicit.
- **1600–1615** is another defensible operational beginning because shipping, slave imports, silver payments, and the “confederado” political-commercial coalition became conspicuous.
- **1776–1778** is the principal transformation rather than an absolute termination. The creation of the Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata in 1776, Spanish seizure of Colonia del Sacramento in 1777, free inland circulation under Viceroy Cevallos, and the 1778 *Reglamento y aranceles reales para el comercio libre* transferred much of the old route into a legal Atlantic structure.
- **1810–1825** is the terminal period for the integrated colonial system: revolution, war, and the separation of Upper Peru from the Río de la Plata disrupted the corridor and divided it among successor states.

Thus the illegal route did not “end” on a single date. It was progressively **legalized, fiscalized, militarized, and then broken apart politically**.

### 1.4 What counts as part of the network

Included are:

- bullion, coin, and silverware moving toward the Río de la Plata;
- imported merchandise whose sale generated silver remittances;
- enslaved Africans transported from Buenos Aires toward Córdoba, Charcas, Potosí, Chile, and neighboring markets;
- merchant credit, correspondence, agents, procurators, factors, carriers, and officials enabling those movements;
- legal shipments and royal remittances where they shared the corridor and infrastructure;
- Colonia del Sacramento and informal Río de la Plata landing sites;
- enforcement bodies whose conduct shaped the network.

Excluded except as contextual branches are:

- all Potosí commerce indiscriminately;
- the Lima–Callao–Panama circuit except where it competed with the Atlantic route;
- post-independence trade treated as if it were unchanged colonial contraband;
- modern National Route 9 as though its exact alignment reproduced every colonial path.

## 2. Formation

### 2.1 Mining and metropolitan background

Silver was discovered at Cerro Rico in **1545**. Potosí subsequently became a major mining, minting, administrative, and consumption center. Its demand for food, draft animals, textiles, labor, mercury, tools, and imported manufactures stimulated interregional specialization.

The Toledo reforms of the 1570s expanded production through mercury amalgamation and the compulsory Indigenous labor draft known as the **mita**. The silver economy rested on coercive extraction: mitayos and other Indigenous workers mined and processed ore; free and coerced carriers moved supplies; enslaved Africans were employed particularly in domestic, artisanal, mint, and processing environments, although simplistic claims that no Africans worked near silver production are contradicted by documentary evidence.

The authorized imperial route directed Andean bullion and trade toward Lima, Callao, Panama, Portobelo, and the Caribbean convoy system. That arrangement was long, expensive, and vulnerable to interrupted sailings and war. Buenos Aires offered a geographically more direct Atlantic outlet, although its shallow approaches, sandbanks, lack of a deep-water harbor, and exposure to foreign shipping created other costs.

### 2.2 Urban and road antecedents

The north–south land corridor was not created by smugglers. It incorporated:

- pre-Hispanic Andean roads and exchange zones;
- the Inca road system;
- Spanish conquest routes;
- roads joining early colonial foundations such as Santiago del Estero, Córdoba, Salta, Jujuy, and Potosí;
- ranches, Indigenous communities, estancias, inns, river crossings, and later postal stages.

Important foundations included:

- Potosí, 1545;
- Santiago del Estero, 1553;
- Córdoba, 1573;
- Salta, 1582;
- Buenos Aires, permanently refounded in 1580;
- San Salvador de Jujuy, 1593.

These towns gave merchants courts, notaries, warehouses, churches, credit contacts, animal markets, and compulsory or purchasable labor. The road was therefore simultaneously royal infrastructure and the substrate of illicit commerce.

### 2.3 The immediate commercial stimulus

Portuguese and Portuguese-connected merchants in Brazil, Angola, Lisbon, and Atlantic entrepôts could supply enslaved Africans and European goods. Buenos Aires residents lacked a dependable authorized supply and possessed cattle, hides, foodstuffs, and access to the silver markets of the interior.

The resulting exchange had a strong monetary imbalance: Buenos Aires’ local exports often could not pay for its imports. Silver from the Peruvian interior closed the balance. A teaching synthesis based on earlier scholarship estimates that local exports covered only about **16 percent** of early imports and infers that the remaining **84 percent** was settled in coined, bar, unrefined, or worked silver. This is a **model-based inference, not an audited balance sheet**, and should not be treated as a precisely measured contraband share ([Cuadernos Docentes](https://iighi.conicet.gov.ar/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2020/04/Libro-Cuadernos-Docentes-N%C2%BA-10-Definitivo-compressed.pdf)).

### 2.4 Prohibition and licensed exceptions

A royal order of **28 January 1594** sought to close Buenos Aires to unauthorized movement of merchandise, bullion, and passengers. Reports accused Governor Fernando de Zárate and others of sending ships to Guinea and Angola and importing enslaved Africans without proper authorization. These were official allegations investigated by agents including Antonio Gutiérrez de Ulloa and Sancho de Figueroa; individual culpability requires examination of the respective proceedings ([Biblioteca Nacional historical publication](https://www.bn.gov.ar/micrositios/admin_assets/issues/files/320d4fe1de596ad9b8b215430dd757f6.pdf)).

A royal cédula of **1602** permitted limited exports from Buenos Aires—reported as 2,000 fanegas of flour, 500 quintales of dried meat, and 500 arrobas of tallow—to Portuguese possessions, while expressly attempting to prevent the privilege from becoming a conduit for Peruvian silver or interior commerce. A January 1606 order further restricted the inclusion of Córdoba and other interior produce ([nineteenth-century documentary discussion](https://catalogobibliotecas.buenosaires.gob.ar/media/PDF/40-00938_D.pdf)).

These permissions did not create the contraband corridor. They provided lawful hulls, documents, officials, and commercial occasions around which undeclared business could be organized.

### 2.5 No identifiable sole founder

**Unresolved/non-applicable:** there was no sole founder. The system emerged from the interaction of:

- Potosí mine owners, refiners, merchants, and consumers;
- Portuguese Atlantic traders;
- Buenos Aires vecinos and officeholders;
- interior wholesalers and carriers;
- enslavers and slave-trade contractors;
- royal officials who prohibited, licensed, taxed, seized, resold, or participated in commerce.

Juan de Vergara and Diego de Vega were important organizers of one early coalition, not founders of the entire corridor.

## 3. Constituent Nodes

### 3.1 Major hubs

#### Potosí

**Role:** source of silver; minting and assay center; enormous consumer market; northern terminus of the dossier’s core corridor.

Silver circulated as:

- registered coin;
- bars and assayed bullion;
- *piñas* or partially refined metal;
- worked silver;
- unregistered or under-registered metal;
- proceeds of merchandise and slave sales.

Potosí’s notarial records document sales, obligations, powers of attorney, freight, partnership, and forced human movement. A particularly important surviving corpus is four volumes of public deeds from **1635–1636**, containing 1,724 documents, including transactions involving **441 enslaved people** brought from African regions through Buenos Aires to Potosí. The figures come from the custodian’s documentary nomination and are unusually concrete, although they describe a corpus, not total traffic ([UNESCO nomination](https://media.unesco.org/sites/default/files/webform/mtd001/262_2025_bolivia.pdf)).

The original sixteenth-century mint and the later Casa de Moneda were institutions of legal production and fiscal control, but minted coin could subsequently enter both lawful and illicit circulation.

#### Buenos Aires

**Role:** Atlantic interface, administrative capital of the Río de la Plata governorship, receiving market, redistribution center, place of confiscation auctions, and point of silver export.

Buenos Aires lacked a modern enclosed harbor. Large ships commonly anchored offshore, while smaller craft, carts, boats, and laborers transferred cargo. This created numerous opportunities for under-declaration and diversion.

The urban node included:

- the governor and his household;
- royal treasury officers;
- cabildo members;
- notaries and courts;
- warehouses and private houses;
- religious buildings used as social or physical refuges;
- riverside landings;
- rural estancias supplying carts, hides, cattle, horses, and concealment.

Buenos Aires was not merely “outside” government. Its commerce often operated through government. Officeholding created information, seizure authority, access to licenses, command over guards, and influence over public auctions.

#### Córdoba

**Role:** principal interior articulation point; cart-building and livestock center; credit and redistribution market; junction toward Potosí, Chile, Santa Fe, and Buenos Aires.

Córdoba supplied mules and other livestock to the Andean economy. It also housed merchants, religious institutions, estates, workshops, and storage facilities.

An internal or “dry” customs barrier was ordered in the early 1620s. Sources variously date its establishment or effective operation to **1622** and **1625**. It was supposed to impose a **50 percent assessment** on specified Buenos Aires merchandise moving north and to prevent unauthorized bullion movement. The distinction between the authorizing decision, installation, and effective collection likely explains part of the dating discrepancy.

Two reported bypass patterns were:

- a route from Santa Fe through wetlands north of Mar Chiquita toward Santiago del Estero;
- a diversion leaving the main road near the Río Tercero/Villa María region and rejoining farther north.

These bypass descriptions are plausible and locally reported, but their exact seventeenth-century alignments are not fully reconstructed archaeologically ([Córdoba training history](https://www.policiacordoba.gov.ar/ingreso/assets/files/ETAC-MATERIALESTUDIOCOMPLETO2022.pdf)).

### 3.2 Northern transfer hubs

#### Santiago del Estero

A political and ecclesiastical center and an early node in the Tucumán road system. It connected regional textile and agricultural production with Córdoba, Tucumán, Salta, and Charcas. Its precise importance fluctuated as other towns grew.

#### San Miguel de Tucumán

A market and production node supplying textiles, food, livestock, and services. Routes did not always pass through identical urban alignments; seasonal conditions, river crossings, warfare, and commercial avoidance created alternatives.

#### Salta

A major staging, livestock, and mercantile center between Tucumán and Jujuy. Mules bred farther south could be held and fattened in the north before entering high-altitude markets. Merchants, muleteers, and estate owners linked Salta to both Potosí and Buenos Aires.

#### San Salvador de Jujuy

A chokepoint at the entrance to the Quebrada de Humahuaca. It was an important transition between cart and pack-animal transport. Carriage conditions northward favored mules and donkeys over the heavy ox-drawn carts used on the plains.

#### Quebrada de Humahuaca, Tilcara, Humahuaca, and the Puna approaches

These were not a single customs station but a chain of settlements, Indigenous communities, grazing areas, river valleys, and highland paths. They carried goods, animals, official mail, soldiers, and people under coercion. Warfare involving unconquered or resisting Indigenous communities periodically endangered or redirected the road.

#### Tupiza and surrounding valleys

A southern Charcas staging area linking the high plateau and Potosí with Jujuy. It provided fodder, rest, local brokerage, and route alternatives.

### 3.3 Atlantic and riverine nodes

#### Colonia del Sacramento

Founded by forces under Manuel Lobo in **January 1680** opposite Buenos Aires. Spanish forces expelled the Portuguese in July; it was restored in 1683, occupied until 1705, restored again in 1716, briefly captured in 1762, returned in 1763, and permanently taken by Spain in 1777.

Colonia became:

- a Portuguese fortified settlement;
- a marketplace for foreign manufactures and captives;
- an outlet for hides;
- a receiving point for silver;
- one component of a larger port complex rather than an isolated “smugglers’ island.”

Fernando Jumar argues that the relevant unit was the **Río de la Plata port complex**: Buenos Aires, Colonia, Montevideo after its foundation, and an indeterminate number of informal landing places. The surviving evidence does not support treating all trade at Colonia as illicit or all illicit trade as routed through Colonia ([Jumar, Colonia](https://www.memoria.fahce.unlp.edu.ar/libros/pm.7/pm.7.pdf)).

#### Montevideo

Founded and settled under Spanish authority in the 1720s. It became a fortified harbor, naval and customs node, and competitor/complement to Buenos Aires. Its better harbor altered shipping geography but did not automatically eliminate informal landing.

#### Informal landing sites

The Buenos Aires and Banda Oriental shores contained coves, beaches, islands, creeks, and estancias usable for unrecorded transfer. Individual locations changed, and the phrase “clandestine ports” should not imply permanent installations in every case.

#### Santa Fe, Paraná, and Paraguay branches

Santa Fe connected the Paraná–Paraguay river system with Córdoba and Buenos Aires. Yerba mate, tobacco, timber, textiles, livestock, and people entered the wider exchange network. Paraguay was a branch and supplier, not part of the linear Potosí–Buenos Aires core in every transaction.

### 3.4 Overseas nodes

#### Brazilian ports

Rio de Janeiro, Santos, Bahia, Pernambuco, and other Brazilian ports supplied shipping connections, credit, enslaved Africans, sugar, textiles, and re-exported European goods. Which port predominated varied by period and merchant association.

#### Angola and West/Central Africa

Luanda and other African embarkation regions were origins of people forcibly transported into the system. Documentary descriptions of “nation” or origin were often broad, commercial, imposed, or inaccurate. They cannot always be mapped cleanly onto modern ethnic identities.

#### Lisbon, Amsterdam, French ports, and British Atlantic bases

These furnished capital, merchandise, shipping, insurance, information, and markets for silver. In the eighteenth century Jamaica and British asiento infrastructure became important. Direct proof of a particular silver parcel’s ultimate destination is frequently absent.

### 3.5 Surviving physical and documentary remains

Physical remains include:

- Cerro Rico and Potosí’s mining landscape;
- the colonial urban fabric and Casa de Moneda collections;
- sections and successor alignments of the Camino Real;
- churches, cabildos, bridges, estancias, and archaeological road traces;
- Colonia del Sacramento’s fortified historic quarter;
- historic cores of Córdoba, Salta, Jujuy, and Buenos Aires.

Surviving documentation is dispersed among the Archivo General de Indias, Archivo General de la Nación Argentina, Archivo y Biblioteca Nacionales de Bolivia, Casa Nacional de Moneda, provincial and municipal archives, notarial protocols, ecclesiastical archives, Portuguese repositories, and European company or diplomatic collections.

## 4. Connections

### 4.1 Potosí–Jujuy

**Medium:** pack trains, individual riders, pedestrians, official messengers, coerced travelers.

**Cargo southward:** coin, bullion, worked silver, vicuña wool, Andean goods, letters, purchasing orders.

**Cargo northward:** enslaved people, European cloth, Brazilian products, iron, tools, regional food, livestock, correspondence.

**Risk:** altitude, cold, animal loss, robbery, Indigenous conflict, inspection, insolvency, and seizure.

Exact frequency cannot be established for illicit movements. Legal freight contracts, notarial obligations, travel accounts, customs papers, and seizures show repeated traffic, but surviving counts are not a complete census.

### 4.2 Jujuy–Salta–Tucumán–Santiago–Córdoba

This was a linked sequence rather than a sealed road. Goods could be:

- resold at intermediate markets;
- divided among carriers;
- repacked from mules to carts;
- nominally assigned to local purchasers;
- financed by new obligations;
- concealed within legal loads;
- redirected toward Chile or Paraguay.

Mules were both transport equipment and valuable merchandise. Southern breeding, Córdoba markets, northern fattening grounds, and Andean demand formed a system adjacent to the silver corridor.

### 4.3 Córdoba–Buenos Aires

The plains permitted ox-cart transport of bulky merchandise. Trips depended on weather, river conditions, draft animals, grazing, and the availability of escorts and labor. No single universal travel time is defensible. Contemporary itineraries imply journeys measured in **weeks to months**, while a complete Buenos Aires–Potosí commercial movement could take several months once rests, sales, repacking, and negotiations are included.

A later measurement in Moutoukias gives approximately **411 leagues for Potosí–Lima** in comparison with the southern route, but colonial leagues varied and should not be converted into a spurious exact kilometer figure.

### 4.4 Buenos Aires–Colonia

After Colonia became firmly established, small craft, boats, local sailors, and shore-based intermediaries carried people and goods across the estuary. Jumar’s analysis of confiscations between 1693 and 1777 warns that seizures measure enforcement encounters, not total contraband. The system after about 1720 was sufficiently structured to constitute a multiport complex, but its real volume remains unknowable from confiscations alone ([Jumar, confiscations](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S1405-22532008000100002&script=sci_arttext)).

### 4.5 Río de la Plata–Brazil–Europe

Silver could travel from Buenos Aires or Colonia to Rio de Janeiro and Lisbon, or enter Dutch, French, and British trading circuits. One exceptional documented comparison illustrates the scale hidden by purely Spanish shipping records: Jumar reports **6.4 million pesos belonging to the Royal Treasury** carried to Spain through the Colonia–Rio de Janeiro–Lisbon route in the Portuguese fleets of **1747 and 1749**. This was not necessarily private smuggling; it demonstrates that even official money could use nonstandard imperial paths ([Jumar, figures](https://www.memoria.fahce.unlp.edu.ar/trab_eventos/ev.711/ev.711.pdf)).

### 4.6 Credit and correspondence edges

Physical silver did not accompany every purchase. Merchants used:

- consignments;
- notarized debts and bonds;
- powers of attorney;
- partnership agreements;
- advances to carriers or factors;
- sales on credit;
- reciprocal accounts;
- remittances through third parties;
- kin and household agents.

Moutoukias’s central reconstruction is that personal networks were not a decorative supplement to “the market.” They allocated credit, information, office access, protection, and enforcement. The same patronage ties could organize legitimate administration and illicit commerce ([Moutoukias, “Réseaux personnels”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ahess_0395-2649_1992_num_47_4_279084)).

## 5. Agents and Operators

### 5.1 Producers and coerced labor

At the northern end stood:

- mine owners and lessees;
- refiners and mill owners;
- mint personnel;
- merchants and bankers;
- Indigenous mitayos;
- mingas and other wage or quasi-wage workers;
- carriers and ore processors;
- enslaved Africans and their descendants;
- women conducting retail, household production, credit, and property transactions.

The mita was a coercive state-supported labor draft. It formed part of the productive foundation from which silver entered commercial circulation. The route cannot be reconstructed honestly as only a merchant achievement.

### 5.2 Merchants, factors, and carriers

The corridor depended on many small and medium operators as well as conspicuous financiers:

- Portuguese and Portuguese-descended merchants;
- Spanish-American vecinos;
- Potosí and Charcas wholesalers;
- Buenos Aires importers;
- factors residing at distant markets;
- muleteers or *arrieros*;
- cart owners and *carreteros*;
- sailors, pilots, boatmen, and port laborers;
- interpreters and guides;
- notaries and clerks.

Indigenous, African, Afro-descended, mestizo, and mixed-status laborers were indispensable, but elite-generated sources often record them incompletely or only when conflict reached a court.

### 5.3 The early “confederados”

The term **confederados** is associated with an early seventeenth-century political-commercial coalition opposed to Hernandarias and linked to contraband.

#### Diego de Vega

A merchant from Madeira, reconstructed as a central organizer of an intercontinental association connecting Lisbon, Bahia, Buenos Aires, Potosí, and Chile. Credit and consignment gave him a broad field of debtors and dependents. Calling him “the first banker of Buenos Aires” is a later interpretive label, useful only if “banker” is understood functionally rather than as a chartered occupation ([study of Vega](https://bdigital.uncuyo.edu.ar/objetos_digitales/8102/rev.-haya-3y4-27-61.pdf)).

#### Juan de Vergara

A Sevillian officeholder, notary associated with the Holy Office, cabildo figure, and ally of Vega. His public offices and influence exemplify the overlap between municipal government, ecclesiastical privilege, credit, and contraband. Claims that he “owned” the cabildo are rhetorical shorthand; documentary evidence supports dominance and influence, not legal ownership.

A recent synthesis of research on Atlantic networks describes Vega and Vergara as connecting slave and goods imports through Buenos Aires with redistribution to Potosí and Chile ([Redes y relaciones Europa-América](https://www.memoria.fahce.unlp.edu.ar/libros/pm.5487/pm.5487.pdf)).

### 5.4 Governors and enforcement

#### Hernando Arias de Saavedra, “Hernandarias”

Hernandarias repeatedly denounced and prosecuted illicit commerce and became the political opponent of the confederados. He should not be converted into an uncomplicated anti-corruption legend: he was also a colonial governor and participant in the wider regime of conquest and coerced labor. On the narrower question, the records distinguish his enforcement efforts from the commercial participation alleged against several successors.

#### Diego de Góngora

First governor of the separated Río de la Plata governorship from 1618. Official proceedings accused him of arriving with illicit cargo, protecting the confederados, and permitting large unauthorized slave imports. Later summaries report that his residence proceeding found him culpable in extensive irregularities, but spectacular totals such as “5,000 enslaved people” or “300,000 ducats” require consultation of the original case before being accepted literally.

#### Jacinto de Láriz

Governor from 1646 to 1653. He was investigated and punished for contraband and other abuses; property was confiscated and penalties reportedly included a fine, banishment, and perpetual deprivation of office. His career demonstrates that prosecution could reach a governor, though usually after illicit commerce had already operated for years.

#### Other officials

Royal treasury officers, customs collectors, judges, military commanders, cabildo members, and ecclesiastical officeholders could:

- police contraband;
- issue or interpret permissions;
- initiate confiscation;
- buy confiscated goods;
- leak information;
- protect merchants;
- participate through relatives or intermediaries.

“Corruption” describes many acts in modern language, but analysis must retain the period distinction among prohibited self-dealing, tolerated gifts, patronage, jurisdictional dispute, and prosecuted fraud.

### 5.5 Religious institutions

Convents, colleges, brotherhoods, and clerics participated in the region’s property, credit, and transport economy. Allegations of Jesuit or other clerical contraband recur, but membership in a religious order does not prove participation in a particular illicit shipment.

A documented example concerning the enslaved couple Antonio Sosa and his wife reports that, after being moved from Colonia to Buenos Aires, they were concealed in the Dominican convent before the Charcas merchant Juan Castillo transported them toward Potosí. The account demonstrates the possible use of religious premises within a trafficking operation; it does not establish institutional Dominican sponsorship of the entire network ([Estudios Bolivianos](https://biblioteca-repositorio.clacso.edu.ar/bitstream/CLACSO/3507/1/Estudios_Bolivianos_1.pdf)).

### 5.6 Foreign companies

Successive Portuguese, French, and British slave-trading concessions created licensed presences that could shelter undeclared commerce.

The British South Sea Company received the 1713 asiento, conventionally summarized as permission to supply **4,800 enslaved Africans annually for thirty years**, with a nominal total of 144,000 and an authorized annual merchandise ship. Those were contractual ceilings or obligations, not proof of deliveries. The company used factors and shipping at Buenos Aires; accusations of contraband accompanied its operations. War in 1739 ended the effective British asiento system ([UCL thesis](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1349550/); [comparative slave-trade study](https://doi.org/10.1590/tem-1980-542x2017v230207)).

## 6. Operation and Mechanism

### 6.1 A generalized transaction

A plausible, recurrent transaction worked as follows:

1. A Portuguese, Spanish, French, Dutch, or British-connected merchant assembled textiles, ironware, sugar, spirits, slaves, or other goods in an Atlantic port.
2. The ship reached the Río de la Plata with a license, slave-trade concession, registration, military purpose, or asserted emergency.
3. In an **arribada maliciosa**, a captain allegedly fabricated or exaggerated damage, shortage, navigational distress, or accidental arrival.
4. Officials inspected the ship. Cargo might be declared, under-declared, landed clandestinely, or formally confiscated.
5. Confiscation did not necessarily defeat the organizers. If allied bidders controlled the auction, they could buy the cargo legally at favorable prices.
6. Merchandise was stored in Buenos Aires houses or rural sites, sold locally, or consigned northward.
7. Heavy loads moved by cart toward Córdoba. There they might be declared, divided, relabeled, taxed, or diverted around the dry customs barrier.
8. Farther north, loads were shifted increasingly to pack animals through Salta, Jujuy, Humahuaca, Tupiza, and Potosí.
9. A Potosí factor sold the merchandise or enslaved people for coin, bullion, or credit.
10. Silver moved south in smaller, more valuable loads, was netted against debts, entrusted to agents, concealed in legal consignments, or remitted by an alternative merchant chain.
11. At Buenos Aires or Colonia it paid foreign suppliers and left aboard Atlantic shipping.

This is a **synthetic mechanism**, supported by recurring documentary practices but not a claim that every shipment followed all eleven steps.

### 6.2 Security and concealment

Methods included:

- false or manipulated manifests;
- undeclared side cargo;
- night landing;
- dispersal among small boats;
- rural beaches and estancias;
- bribery or patronage;
- fraudulent emergency entry;
- collusive denunciation followed by auction;
- nominal ownership by a licensed or locally privileged person;
- mixing silver with legal royal or private remittances;
- alternative roads around inspection points;
- division into small parcels;
- advance warning from officials or local residents.

No verified general evidence supports a standardized cipher, secret badge, or permanent “smugglers’ guild.” Private letters may have used guarded language, but a system-wide code is **unestablished**.

### 6.3 Financing

Capital came from merchant partnerships, Atlantic suppliers, advances, retained sales proceeds, and credit. Operators often did not own the goods they moved. A factor sold on behalf of a distant principal and remitted proceeds after deducting commission, freight, duties, losses, and local expenses.

Financial risks included:

- confiscation;
- counterfeit or debased coin;
- borrower default;
- carrier flight;
- mortality among enslaved captives;
- animal loss;
- war;
- shipwreck;
- price changes between Buenos Aires and Potosí;
- prosecution of a protecting official.

### 6.4 Worked case I: Acarete du Biscay, 1657–1660

Acarete du Biscay, probably a Frenchman of Basque origin, entered Spanish America while presenting himself as a Spanish subject because foreigners were normally barred. In **1658** he traveled overland from Buenos Aires toward Potosí.

His itinerary records the practical sequence of the route:

**Buenos Aires → pampas and Saladillo region → Córdoba → Santiago/Tucumán zone → Salta → Jujuy → Humahuaca approaches → Potosí.**

He observed cattle abundance, cart and mule traffic, urban markets, Indigenous conflict, and the movement of animals toward Peru. He reportedly conducted trade in Córdoba and Potosí. His subsequent report was directed toward French strategic and commercial interests and must therefore be read both as travel testimony and intelligence.

What the case securely demonstrates:

- a foreign merchant could penetrate the nominally closed system;
- the Buenos Aires–Potosí journey was operational by the mid-seventeenth century;
- trade and reconnaissance were inseparable;
- the route was composed of ordinary colonial roads and towns, not a hidden continuous trail.

What it does **not** securely establish:

- total annual contraband;
- that every observation was personally verified;
- an exact representative journey time;
- the quantity and form of every silver remittance Acarete obtained.

The Library of Congress preserves a digitized English edition of his account ([Acarete, Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/resource/rbc0001.2019gen17198/?st=gallery)); a modern Spanish edition is catalogued by Google Books ([Spanish edition](https://books.google.com/books/about/Viajes_al_R%C3%ADo_de_la_Plata_y_a_Potos%C3%AD_1.html?hl=es&id=A05Xv9TPwuQC)).

### 6.5 Worked case II: enslaved people between Buenos Aires and Potosí, 1635–1636

The Potosí public deeds provide a collective end-to-end documentary case:

1. People were captured or purchased within African and Atlantic slave-trading systems.
2. They were shipped from regions described in the deeds as Angola, Congo, Guinea, Ghana, Cape Verde, Biafra, Luanda, and others.
3. They entered the South American corridor through Buenos Aires.
4. Merchants and carriers forcibly moved them approximately 2,000 kilometers inland toward Potosí.
5. Potosí notaries recorded sales, age, condition, imposed names, prices, maternity, origin labels, and purchasers.
6. Recorded destinations were frequently domestic service, though the documents also bear on artisanal, mint, and mining-related environments.

The corpus contains transactions involving **441 people** within 1,724 deeds from 1635–1636. It is the best bounded quantitative example located for this dossier, but it is neither a complete count of arrivals nor proof that every individual followed an identical route ([UNESCO nomination](https://media.unesco.org/sites/default/files/webform/mtd001/262_2025_bolivia.pdf)).

## 7. Scale

### 7.1 Early shipping, 1585–1615

A reconstruction attributed to Moutoukias gives the following movements between Buenos Aires and Brazilian coasts:

| Quinquennium | Arrivals | Departures | Recorded/import-value reconstruction | Export-value reconstruction | Enslaved people recorded |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1585–1590 | 10 | 1 | 17,661 | 10,576 | 3 |
| 1591–1595 | 29 | 1 | 192,961 | 19 | 228 |
| 1596–1600 | 27 | 33 | 16,063 | 30,735 | 49 |
| 1601–1605 | 41 | 52 | 130,404 | 63,444 | not supplied |
| 1606–1610 | 57 | 37 | 306,641 | 61,020 | 859 |
| 1611–1615 | 80 | 45 | 635,000 | 54,329 | 3,815 |

**Reliability:** moderate for documenting a trend, lower as a measure of complete traffic. Surviving proceedings disproportionately record detected or litigated voyages; monetary columns depend on valuation conventions; the table’s transcription should be checked against Moutoukias’s original book ([source reproducing the table](https://ojs.rosario-conicet.gov.ar/index.php/prohistoria/article/download/1162/1285?inline=1)).

### 7.2 Seized enslaved people at Colonia

A study of confiscations reports at least **877 enslaved Africans seized by Spanish authorities between 1740 and 1777**, with illegal traffic particularly visible in that period. It also reports illustrative market prices:

- up to 300 pesos for a young legally purchased captive in Buenos Aires;
- 100–120 pesos at Colonia in clandestine trade;
- resale at 180–200 pesos in Buenos Aires.

These are reported contemporary comparative prices, not invariant tariffs. Age, health, sex, skill, war, supply, credit terms, and legality altered valuations. Confiscations provide a minimum count of detected cases, not total traffic ([Portuguese-language study](https://www.scielo.br/j/tem/a/z3MCkNYKKzR6Kk5TZQ7fskm/)).

### 7.3 Legal Atlantic exports, 1715–1778

Fernando Jumar’s reconstruction from ship registers gives more than **58 million pesos of eight reales** in legal exports toward Spain between 1715 and 1778. A later synthesis divides this approximately as:

| Category | Pesos of eight reales | Share |
|---|---:|---:|
| Silver and gold | 50,514,373.618 | 87.02% |
| Hides | 5,742,933.750 | 9.89% |
| Vicuña wool | 798,620.625 | 1.38% |
| Other | 994,467.235 | 1.71% |
| **Total** | **58,050,395.228** | **100%** |

The table includes about 44.5 million pesos in silver and a little over 6 million in gold. It measures **legal registered exports**, not smuggling. Its relevance is that the same Atlantic orientation formerly associated with illicit silver became increasingly legal. It also proves that precious metals dominated the recorded export value.

Jumar explicitly cautions that registers reveal the Spanish legal circuit, not total Río de la Plata commerce. Portuguese, British, French, and clandestine movements prevent a complete balance ([Jumar, figures](https://www.memoria.fahce.unlp.edu.ar/trab_eventos/ev.711/ev.711.pdf)).

### 7.4 Why no trustworthy total for smuggled silver exists

A total is unrecoverable because:

- successful contraband was designed to leave no customs record;
- seizures measure failures, not all movement;
- officials sometimes registered only part of a cargo;
- coined silver was fungible and did not retain an origin label;
- Potosí silver mixed with silver from other sources;
- legal, illicit, and royal remittances could share carriers;
- litigation records exaggerate disputed commerce relative to ordinary traffic;
- archives are incomplete.

Claims that a fixed percentage—70, 80, or 90 percent—of all Buenos Aires commerce was illicit should therefore be treated as **estimates or rhetorical generalizations unless tied to a specified period and method**.

## 8. Change over the Lifespan

### Phase I: antecedents and emergence, 1545–1594

Potosí’s mining expansion generated continental demand. Spanish foundations stabilized a southward road. Buenos Aires’ permanent refoundation in 1580 created an Atlantic outlet. Initial shipping to and from Brazil demonstrated the commercial possibility.

### Phase II: prohibition and institutionalized evasion, 1594–1618

The 1594 prohibition made the southern outlet formally illicit for much of the trade. Limited licenses after 1602 enabled subsistence and exports but also created cover for additional cargo. Portuguese traders, Buenos Aires residents, and interior correspondents formed durable ties.

### Phase III: confederados, inspections, and the dry customs barrier, 1618–1645

The separation of the Río de la Plata governorship and Góngora’s arrival coincided with intense political conflict. Vergara, Vega, cabildo allies, officials, and creditors constituted a powerful coalition.

The Córdoba dry customs barrier of the 1620s attempted to protect fiscal revenue and the Lima-oriented market. It raised the incentive to evade Córdoba or disguise the destination of goods. Moutoukias treats **1580–1645** as the formative era of the Potosí–Buenos Aires route.

### Phase IV: mature corridor and foreign observation, 1645–1680

The route persisted through licensed ships, allegedly malicious arrivals, official participation, and long-distance merchant credit. Acarete’s journey supplies a rare direct description. Prosecutions such as that of Láriz show enforcement without systemic eradication.

### Phase V: Colonia and the multipolar estuary, 1680–1715

Portuguese foundation of Colonia inserted a fortified foreign node opposite Buenos Aires. Its first occupation may not have produced the dramatic immediate commercial transformation sometimes assumed: Jumar finds the qualitative evidence insufficient to show a huge initial increase, particularly because legal Spanish, English, French, and fraudulent-arrival traffic already supplied Buenos Aires.

### Phase VI: Colonia, the British asiento, and the port complex, 1715–1739

The South Sea Company opened an authorized factor presence while Colonia returned to Portuguese control. Legal slave trading and illegal merchandise exchange overlapped. Buenos Aires, Colonia, informal landings, and later Montevideo formed a connected port complex.

### Phase VII: war, confiscation, and commercial expansion, 1739–1762

The War of Jenkins’ Ear terminated the effective British asiento. Portuguese and local networks continued. Confiscation records show intense illicit trafficking in enslaved people. Spanish registered commerce also expanded unevenly.

### Phase VIII: late Bourbon reorganization, 1762–1778

Colonia changed hands temporarily in 1762–1763. Spanish authorities strengthened Montevideo, naval defense, fiscal administration, and authorized shipping.

In 1776 the Crown established the Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata, grouping Buenos Aires with Potosí, Charcas, Paraguay, Tucumán, and other regions. Cevallos’s expedition permanently captured Colonia in 1777. The 1778 commercial regulations authorized wider direct trade among designated Spanish and American ports.

This did not abolish smuggling. It changed its object: traders could now move many goods legally through Buenos Aires while still evading duties, prohibitions, wartime controls, slave-trade rules, or monopolies.

## 9. End or Transformation

### 9.1 Proximate institutional changes

The old system was transformed by four linked measures:

1. creation of the Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata in 1776;
2. conquest of Portuguese Colonia in 1777;
3. authorization of freer inland circulation;
4. the 1778 free-trade regulation.

The most important result was not modern “free trade,” but a broader **licensed trade within the Spanish imperial system**. Customs and royal taxation remained. Foreign commerce was still restricted.

### 9.2 Structural causes

The Crown’s reorientation reflected:

- Buenos Aires’ strategic importance against Portugal and Britain;
- the established commercial pull of the Atlantic;
- declining practicality of forcing all southern commerce through Lima;
- growth of Spanish registered shipping;
- the need to fund defense and administration;
- the fiscal advantage of legalizing and taxing traffic already occurring.

### 9.3 What happened to the network

- Merchants shifted portions of their business from concealed to registered forms.
- Existing roads, carriers, warehouses, credit relationships, and urban brokers remained useful.
- Buenos Aires became the viceregal capital and principal fiscal-military center.
- Potosí silver increasingly supported Buenos Aires’ treasury and defense as well as overseas commerce.
- Colonia ceased to be a Portuguese enclave, but contraband migrated to other shorelines, flags, paperwork, and customs frauds.
- The corridor became an official imperial trunk rather than disappearing.

### 9.4 Revolutionary disruption

After 1810, control of the Upper Peruvian silver region became a military and political question. Campaigns, requisitions, road insecurity, and new borders damaged the former integrated system. Bolivian independence in 1825 placed Potosí outside the United Provinces/Río de la Plata successor polity. Trade continued, but it was now transnational and reorganized.

## 10. Failures, Frauds, Suppressions, and Scandals

### 10.1 Fraudulent arrivals

The *arribada forzosa* was a lawful maritime category. A damaged or distressed vessel could seek safety. An *arribada maliciosa* was the alleged abuse of that rule to introduce goods. The same voyage might be described as necessity by its captain and fraud by a prosecutor.

### 10.2 Collusive confiscation

Merchants might arrange for their own cargo to be denounced, confiscated, and auctioned. Political allies then suppressed outside bidding or supplied credit so the organizers reacquired it at a discount with an appearance of legality.

This mechanism is well attested as a pattern. Proof for any named sale requires the individual auction and prosecution record.

### 10.3 Officeholding and self-dealing

Governors, treasury officials, judges, and cabildo members were repeatedly accused of:

- accepting bribes;
- trading through nominees;
- granting improper landing permission;
- concealing cargo;
- manipulating auctions;
- obstructing investigations;
- intimidating witnesses.

Residence proceedings generated extensive documentation, but they were politically adversarial. Charges must be distinguished from verdicts and verdicts from actual recovery of penalties.

### 10.4 Counterfeit and debased silver

The seventeenth-century Potosí mint scandal involved underweight or debased coin and prosecution of mint personnel and associates. Such coin circulated through interregional commerce and could affect the Buenos Aires route. It was not created by the route, and evidence does not justify treating every southern silver remittance as fraudulent coin.

### 10.5 Robbery and loss

Roadside robbery, runaway draft animals, river flooding, disease, shipwreck, warfare, and captive resistance imposed recurrent losses. The sources preserve exceptional disputes better than routine successful journeys, preventing reliable loss rates.

### 10.6 Enforcement paradox

Suppression itself financed parts of the system:

- confiscated goods were resold;
- officials received shares or fees;
- guards and informants were paid from seizures;
- privileged buyers acquired goods at auction;
- temporary prohibitions raised price differentials and therefore potential profits.

The policing network was consequently an adversary, a transaction cost, and sometimes a business partner.

## 11. Contested and Legendary Aspects

### 11.1 “A secret silver road”

**Legend/romantic construction:** a single concealed trail supposedly carried Potosí treasure directly to Buenos Aires.

**Finding:** most traffic used known roads, towns, and transfer points. Secrecy lay in ownership, paperwork, cargo composition, diversion around inspectors, and protection—not necessarily in a continuously hidden physical road.

### 11.2 The modern highway as the exact colonial road

The Camino Real broadly anticipated the Buenos Aires–Córdoba–Tucumán–Salta–Jujuy axis followed by modern transport. Exact identity with National Route 9 is **false or unproved**. Colonial routes shifted with settlements, river crossings, land tenure, weather, and avoidance strategies.

### 11.3 “All Potosí silver went out through Lima”

**Incorrect as an absolute statement.** The legal imperial ideal privileged the western route, but documentary evidence establishes southern silver movement from the late sixteenth century onward.

### 11.4 “All Buenos Aires commerce was contraband”

**Incorrect.** Buenos Aires handled licensed local exports, slave-trade contracts, royal supply, military shipping, navíos de registro, confiscated goods, and later regular Spanish commerce. Legal and illegal activity were interdependent.

### 11.5 “Colonia created the route”

**Incorrect.** The Potosí–Buenos Aires articulation was already mature before 1680. Colonia expanded and reorganized an existing estuarine system.

### 11.6 “Hernandarias destroyed the smugglers”

**Traditional heroic simplification.** His campaigns disrupted individuals and generated prosecutions, but the structural corridor survived him.

### 11.7 “The Jesuits ran the entire system”

**Unverified generalization.** Jesuit estates, colleges, procurators, livestock, credit, and regional transport made the order economically important. There were allegations and cases involving clerics or religious property. No evidence located establishes centralized Jesuit command of the Potosí–Buenos Aires contraband network.

### 11.8 Buried silver and lost caravans

Local traditions along the Camino Real frequently describe buried coin, hidden bullion, tunnels, abandoned caravan treasure, or wealth concealed during expulsions and wars.

These traditions may preserve memories of real bullion movement and insecurity, but no comprehensive contemporary record establishes a single great “Potosí–Buenos Aires treasure.” Each claimed cache requires separate archaeological provenance. Uncontextualized coins or metal objects do not by themselves prove a smuggling depot.

### 11.9 Nationalist interpretations

Argentine narratives have sometimes made contraband an early expression of resistance to Spanish or Peruvian domination. Portuguese/Brazilian narratives have sometimes presented Colonia primarily as commercial enterprise or frontier expansion. Spanish imperial narratives emphasized foreign penetration, fiscal loss, and disobedience.

All contain elements grounded in evidence, but the network’s participants were not operating toward nineteenth-century national independence. Their immediate aims included profit, supply, patronage, office, survival, and imperial competition.

## 12. Historiography and Representations

### 12.1 Major historiographical approaches

#### Older institutional history

Older works emphasized royal decrees, governors, customs institutions, and a binary opposition between Spanish monopoly and crime. They preserve valuable document citations but often accepted official categories too literally.

#### Internal-market interpretation

Carlos Sempat Assadourian reconstructed a **colonial economic space** organized around mining demand. Córdoba, Tucumán, Paraguay, Chile, and other regions specialized partly in response to Potosí. This approach explains why the route cannot be reduced to foreign ships and bullion: it was embedded in regional production ([Assadourian](https://codexvirtual.com/hbancaria/wp-content/plugins/documentos/files/estudios_historicos10.pdf)).

#### Contraband and colonial control

Zacarías Moutoukias’s *Contrabando y control colonial en el siglo XVII* remains central. He reconstructs the Atlantic/interregional system, merchant coalitions, shipping, confiscations, and the coexistence of illicit business with imperial government. His periodization of the early Potosí–Buenos Aires route around **1580–1645** is particularly influential ([bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Contrabando_y_control_colonial_en_el_sig.html?id=nZYTAAAAYAAJ)).

#### Personal networks

Moutoukias’s later work treats kinship, patronage, household, and administrative relationships as mechanisms allocating resources and authority. This displaced interpretations in which corruption was merely an external pathology imposed on otherwise impersonal institutions.

#### Port-complex interpretation

Fernando Jumar argues that eighteenth-century commerce must be studied through the **Río de la Plata port complex**, including Buenos Aires, Colonia, Montevideo, and informal landings. His quantitative work separates legal Spanish records from the unknowable total and warns against explaining change solely through the 1778 reform ([Jumar thesis record](https://www.memoria.fahce.unlp.edu.ar/library?a=d&c=tesis&d=Jte364)).

#### Slavery and forced migration

Recent research uses notarial sales, baptisms, wills, court cases, confiscations, and Atlantic shipping data to reconstruct the forced movement of Africans and Afro-descendants. The 1635–1636 Potosí deed corpus connects the Atlantic and Andean portions unusually clearly.

### 12.2 Principal debates

1. **How dominant was contraband?**  
   Everyone agrees it was substantial; no complete series establishes a stable percentage.

2. **Was Buenos Aires peripheral or already global?**  
   Politically and demographically small did not mean commercially isolated. Its networks connected Africa, Brazil, Europe, Potosí, Chile, Paraguay, and the Atlantic.

3. **Did Colonia cause eighteenth-century expansion?**  
   It was important, but legal Spanish shipping and older informal networks prevent a monocausal answer.

4. **Did 1778 create the Atlantic orientation?**  
   No. It legalized and accelerated a much older orientation.

5. **Was the system anti-imperial?**  
   Not in a simple sense. Royal officials and treasury money used it; participants sought favors from the Crown; illicit commerce could sustain imperial settlements and defense while violating imperial rules.

6. **Can confiscations measure total traffic?**  
   Only as minima and indicators of enforcement. Changes in confiscations may reflect policing rather than changes in actual commerce.

### 12.3 Archives and source biases

Key source types are:

- royal cédulas and orders;
- correspondence of governors, viceroys, audiencias, and the Council of the Indies;
- ship registers and manifests;
- royal treasury accounts;
- customs books;
- confiscation cases;
- residence proceedings;
- notarial protocols;
- slave sales and powers of attorney;
- wills and estate inventories;
- cabildo minutes;
- ecclesiastical registers;
- merchants’ letters;
- travel narratives;
- Portuguese administrative and mercantile records.

Biases include:

- illicit movements deliberately escaping registration;
- official rhetoric magnifying threats to obtain troops or authority;
- merchants minimizing culpability;
- enslaved and Indigenous people appearing through imposed legal categories;
- fragmentary survival;
- modern national archives dividing what was once one commercial space.

### 12.4 Archaeology and heritage

Archaeology is particularly valuable for road traces, river crossings, ceramics, imported goods, military sites, Colonia’s urban fabric, and Potosí’s industrial landscape. Archaeology rarely proves the legal status of an object’s movement. A foreign ceramic at an inland site establishes exchange more readily than “contraband.”

### 12.5 Popular representations

Important representations include:

- Acarete’s travel account, both firsthand report and foreign commercial intelligence;
- *El lazarillo de ciegos caminantes* by Alonso Carrió de la Vandera, published under the name Concolorcorvo, describing the later postal and road world;
- national histories depicting Buenos Aires as naturally destined for Atlantic freedom;
- heritage presentations of the Camino Real;
- Colonia del Sacramento museum and UNESCO interpretations;
- treasure stories and novels centered on Cerro Rico, silver caravans, pirates, Jesuits, or buried coin.

Popular versions often diverge from the record by giving the route a fixed map, centralized leadership, uninterrupted secrecy, or a nationalist political purpose.

## 14. Findings and unresolved questions

### Established with high confidence

- A durable commercial corridor connected Potosí with Buenos Aires by the late sixteenth century.
- Silver was indispensable to settling the Río de la Plata’s Atlantic trade imbalance.
- The same corridor carried foreign merchandise and enslaved Africans inland.
- Portuguese Atlantic connections were important before Colonia’s foundation.
- Colonial officials, merchants, and municipal institutions frequently overlapped.
- The Córdoba dry customs barrier failed to eliminate northbound merchandise or southbound silver.
- Colonia reorganized an existing network rather than creating it.
- The 1776–1778 changes transformed much of the corridor from prohibited trade into registered imperial commerce.
- Coercive Indigenous labor and African enslavement were constituent, not incidental, parts of the system.

### Plausible but incompletely quantifiable

- Illicit trade exceeded recorded legal traffic during substantial early periods.
- Most imported-value deficits were paid with Upper Peruvian silver.
- Personal protection and collusive enforcement lowered the risks of particular merchant groups.
- Route alternatives expanded or contracted in response to customs enforcement and war.
- A significant portion of silver leaving Colonia eventually reached Brazil and European markets.

### Not established

- A single formal name, charter, headquarters, or permanent membership.
- A continuous secret trail distinct from the Camino Real.
- A trustworthy total of smuggled silver.
- A stable annual traffic figure across the whole period.
- Central direction by the Jesuits, Portuguese Crown, Buenos Aires cabildo, or any single merchant family.
- One precise date on which contraband began or ended.
- A comprehensive identification of enslaved and Indigenous carriers whose labor sustained the network.

## 15. Sources and annotated bibliography

### Primary and near-primary sources

- **Acarete du Biscay, *An Account of a Voyage Up the River de la Plata, and Thence Over Land to Peru*.** Direct mid-seventeenth-century itinerary; valuable but shaped by foreign commercial and strategic interests.
- **Potosí public deeds, 1635–1636.** Notarial corpus now held by the Casa Nacional de Moneda; unusually detailed evidence of trafficking, forced migration, prices, identities, and merchants.
- **Royal cédulas and administrative correspondence.** Essential for prohibitions and licenses but express official intentions more reliably than actual compliance.
- **Cabildo and treasury records.** Reveal officeholding, auctions, revenue, supplies, disputes, and enforcement.
- **Ship registers.** Strong evidence for declared cargo and legal trade; structurally incomplete for contraband.
- **Confiscation proceedings.** Strong evidence that a detected transaction occurred; weak denominator for total traffic.

### Major scholarship

- **Zacarías Moutoukias, *Contrabando y control colonial en el siglo XVII: Buenos Aires, el Atlántico y el espacio peruano*.** Foundational systemic reconstruction.
- **Zacarías Moutoukias, “Réseaux personnels et autorité coloniale.”** Analysis of personal relations as economic and administrative infrastructure.
- **Carlos Sempat Assadourian, *El sistema de la economía colonial*.** Fundamental account of Potosí-centered regional articulation and internal markets.
- **Fernando Jumar, *Le commerce atlantique au Río de la Plata, 1680–1778*.** Major quantitative and port-complex study.
- **Fernando Jumar, “Algunas cifras del comercio ultramarino del Río de la Plata en el siglo XVIII.”** Registered export totals and methodological cautions.
- **Fernando Jumar, “El comercio intrarregional en el complejo portuario rioplatense.”** Uses confiscations while explaining their limitations.
- **Paulo César Possamai and related Portuguese-language scholarship on Colônia do Sacramento.** Important for the Portuguese frontier, urban, military, slave-trade, and contraband dimensions.
- **Studies of the South Sea Company asiento.** Necessary for distinguishing contractual privileges, actual slave deliveries, and accompanying illicit merchandise.
