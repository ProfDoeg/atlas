# Cusco ceque system: Network Dossier

**Requested filename:** `cusco_ceque_system_network_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier treats the Cusco ceque system as a changing network of shrines, social obligations, ritual journeys, waterways, roads, memories, and political claims—not simply as a geometric diagram.

The evidentiary labels used below are:

- **Documented:** explicitly stated in a colonial text, securely identified document, or archaeological observation.
- **Archaeologically supported:** material evidence plausibly matches the documentary description.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** an interpretation built from incomplete documentary or archaeological evidence.
- **Disputed:** significant specialists offer incompatible reconstructions.
- **Traditional/mythohistorical:** reported as Inca tradition but not independently datable as an event.
- **Legend or modern representation:** post-Inca storytelling or visualization not established as historical fact.
- **Unknown:** the surviving evidence does not permit a conclusion.

A necessary correction governs the entire dossier: “41 sightlines and 328 huacas” is one influential reconstruction, not a securely established physical inventory. The principal surviving account enumerates 328 huacas in its main list, four more in an appendix, and either 41, 42, or—depending on how one reads the divisions—nominally 41 directions represented by 42 physical branches. Other early reports give approximately 340, 350, 360, “more than 400,” or 450 shrines. Nor were all ceques demonstrably straight astronomical sightlines.

## 1. Identification and Definition

### 1.1 Names

The network had no surviving contemporary title equivalent to a modern corporate or administrative name. Relevant designations include:

- **Quechua:** *siq’i*, conventionally written *ceque*, *ceqque*, *seque*, *zeque* or *zeq’e* in Spanish sources. González Holguín’s early-seventeenth-century Quechua vocabulary glosses *ceqque* as a line, boundary, or demarcation. Modern Southern Quechua spellings include **siq’i**.
- **Quechua:** *wak’a*, Spanish colonial **guaca/huaca**: a sacred being, object, structure, natural feature, place, or localized power. “Shrine” is useful but narrower than the indigenous category.
- **Spanish scholarly names:** *sistema de ceques del Cuzco/Cusco*, *sistema de ceques y huacas*, *relación de las huacas*, and *relación de los adoratorios*.
- **English:** Cusco or Cuzco ceque system; system of ceques and huacas; ritual landscape of Cusco.
- **Center:** **Qurikancha/Qorikancha/Coricancha**, usually interpreted as “golden enclosure”; Spanish **Templo del Sol**.
- **Imperial geography:** **Tawantinsuyu/Tahuantinsuyo**, “the four parts together”; Chinchaysuyu, Antisuyu, Qullasuyu/Collasuyu, and Kuntisuyu/Cuntisuyu.

These are overlapping analytical labels. The colonial texts speak more concretely of ceques, roads, huacas, adoratorios, families, attendants, sacrifices, and the four parts than of a single institution called “the ceque system.”

### 1.2 What the network was

**Documented:** Bernabé Cobo’s *Historia del Nuevo Mundo* says that lines called ceques went outward from the Temple of the Sun, corresponding to the four royal roads from Cusco. Huacas were set “in order” upon them like stations of sacred places. Particular families or social divisions supplied attendants who cared for the huacas and performed prescribed sacrifices at prescribed times. Cobo’s chapters XIII–XVI of Book XIII are the fullest surviving inventory. [Bauer’s reproduction and discussion of Cobo](https://dokumen.pub/the-sacred-landscape-of-the-inca-the-cusco-ceque-system-9780292759541.html)

As a network, it connected:

- the Coricancha and other urban ceremonial spaces;
- royal roads and lesser paths;
- springs, fountains, canals, rivers, ravines, fields, hills, passes, caves, quarries, standing stones, carved rocks, buildings, palaces, tombs, and temples;
- royal descent groups or **panacas**, other **ayllus**, priests, shrine attendants, rulers, initiates, carriers, travelers, cultivators, and subject populations;
- water access, agricultural protection, political rank, territorial possession, royal memory, origin traditions, calendrical rites, astronomical observations in some cases, and communication with sacred powers.

It was therefore simultaneously:

1. a sacred topography;
2. an allocation of ritual labor;
3. a classification of social groups;
4. a repertoire of processional routes;
5. a register of dynastic and mythohistorical memory;
6. in some areas, an ordering framework for water and land;
7. possibly, in limited cases, an observational calendar.

It was **not** principally a freight, postal, credit, or commercial network. Goods traveled through it as offerings—llamas, guinea pigs, chicha, maize, coca, textiles, shells, gold, silver, carved wood and, in high-status rites, human beings—but there is no evidence of tariffs, fares, commercial accounts, money transfers, or traffic volumes comparable to a market network.

### 1.3 The 41/42-line and 328-huaca problem

Cobo reports nine ceques each in Chinchaysuyu, Antisuyu, and Collasuyu, and fourteen in Cuntisuyu:

- 9 + 9 + 9 + 14 = **41 named ceques**.
- Cobo says Cuntisuyu’s eighth ceque had two differently ranked portions, *collana* and *cayao*.
- Brian Bauer’s ground reconstruction treats those portions as two distinct outward branches. On that reading Cuntisuyu had fifteen physical lines, making **42** in all.
- Zuidema’s classic structural reconstruction retained **41 directions**.
- The safe formulation is therefore **41 recorded ceque entries or directions, plausibly 42 physical branches**. Calling all of them “sightlines” overstates the evidence.

The shrine totals are equally unstable:

| Source or reconstruction | Reported total | Evidentiary problem |
|---|---:|---|
| Cobo’s principal ordered list | 328 | A copied and abbreviated colonial inventory |
| Cobo’s added, unordered items | +4 | Explicitly omitted from the principal sequence |
| Cobo’s broader statement | at least 350 | Unclear whether all were ceque-system huacas |
| Polo de Ondegardo, one report | about 340 | Textual history and scope uncertain |
| José de Acosta’s report of Polo | about 360 | Second-hand |
| Polo, other passages | “more than 400” | May include all public shrines near Cusco, not only ordered ceque nodes |
| Martín de Murúa | 450 | Scope and derivation unclear |
| Albornoz versus Cobo | at least thirteen Chinchaysuyu names absent from Cobo | Shows that Cobo’s list was not exhaustive |

Cerrón-Palomino counts 332 in Cobo including the added items and stresses that Cobo’s nomenclature contains transcription and editorial corruption. [Cerrón-Palomino, “La onomástica de los ceques”](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/lexis/article/download/8392/8712/33088)

### 1.4 Bounded period

A defensible bounded biography is:

- **Long formation:** local sacred-place systems almost certainly preceded the empire; archaeological work places regional political consolidation in approximately **CE 1000–1400**.
- **Imperial articulation:** probably during the fifteenth century, especially the period conventionally assigned to Pachacuti Inca Yupanqui and his successors.
- **Fully operating imperial form:** certainly by the eve of the Spanish invasion in **1532–1533**.
- **Institutional dismantling:** begun with the occupation and Christian appropriation of Cusco in the 1530s; increasingly systematic by Polo de Ondegardo’s investigations in **1558–1560**, parish creation in **1559**, and anti-idolatry campaigns of the later sixteenth century.
- **Persistence and transformation:** clandestine worship remained documented in 1571; natural huacas, place names, irrigation relations, pilgrimage practices, and ritual analogues survived long after the unified state allocation system had ceased.

There is no known founding charter, decree, or exactly datable inauguration. Nor is there one day on which the system ended.

### 1.5 What counts as “inside”

A node belongs securely inside when it occurs in Cobo’s ordered list or in an independent early list demonstrably connected to Cusco’s ceque organization. Probable sites identified solely by modern toponymy or alignment are less secure.

The four imperial roads and Qhapaq Ñan intersected the system, but the entire imperial road network was not a ceque system. Cobo described an ordered sacred landscape around Cusco, not 41 physical highways extending to every imperial frontier.

## 2. Formation

### 2.1 Predecessors

**Plausible scholarly inference:** reverence for mountains, springs, caves, unusual stones, ancestors, origin places, and waterways was older and geographically broader than the Inca Empire. Polo reported ceque-like arrangements in more than one hundred Andean towns, and comparable radial ritual organizations have been proposed elsewhere. This makes invention from nothing by one ruler improbable.

Archaeological survey indicates that a centralized polity developed in the Cusco Valley between approximately CE 1000 and 1400 before rapid imperial expansion after 1400. That provides a political setting in which local shrine territories could be consolidated into a capital-wide order. [Bauer and Covey, “Processes of State Formation in the Inca Heartland”](https://anthrosource.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1525/aa.2002.104.3.846)

**Unknown:** no securely dated pre-imperial list proves that a 41-direction structure already existed during the Killke period. Killke material at some later huacas establishes earlier use of places, not necessarily the complete later system.

### 2.2 Pachacuti and imperial reorganization

Colonial traditions credit Pachacuti Inca Yupanqui with rebuilding Cusco, reorganizing its population and institutions, expanding the Coricancha, and establishing or relocating multiple huacas. One Cobo entry says Pachacuti encountered a falcon-shaped stone in a quarry and ordered it installed as **Cugiguaman** on a ceque. At least nineteen shrine descriptions associate him with particular sites or mythohistorical events.

John Rowe consequently argued that the organized system was largely a creation of Pachacuti and was modified under Huayna Capac. Other scholars see a longer, cumulative process. Bauer’s preferred model is flexible: as the capital expanded, rulers and kin groups incorporated, promoted, moved, reinterpreted, or abandoned nodes.

The conventional reign of Pachacuti, **1438–1471**, derives from Rowe’s historical chronology. Archaeological chronologies do not securely validate those exact years, and radiocarbon-based accounts can place processes associated with early expansion somewhat earlier. Therefore:

- **Traditional/chronicle claim:** Pachacuti was the great organizer.
- **Scholarly inference:** major fifteenth-century centralization is highly plausible.
- **Unestablished:** a single foundation date or complete top-down design.

### 2.3 Conditions enabling formation

The network depended on:

- Cusco’s growth as a regional and then imperial capital;
- the Coricancha’s establishment as a central state cult complex;
- incorporation of older local groups and their places into an Inca hierarchy;
- panaca formation around deceased rulers;
- labor and surplus sufficient to maintain temples, attendants, herds, fields, festivals, and sacrifices;
- roads linking the capital to the four suyus;
- irrigation regimes in which springs and canal heads were economically and ritually critical;
- administrative memory, probably including oral specialists and quipu records.

### 2.4 Charter, ownership, and standing

No written indigenous charter survives.

Ownership was distributed rather than corporate:

- Some buildings, estates, and objects belonged to rulers or their panacas.
- Some huacas had dedicated fields, herds, attendants, and ritual specialists.
- Particular ayllus had inherited or assigned maintenance duties.
- The ruler and state cult controlled major nodes and empire-wide ceremonies.
- Communities retained claims to local land and water expressed through their relationship to shrines.

Polo and Albornoz specifically instructed investigators to determine which huacas possessed fields, gold, livestock, attendants, or other property. This demonstrates that shrines could be economic institutions, although accounts for the Cusco system have not survived in quantities sufficient to reconstruct a system-wide budget.

## 3. Constituent Nodes

### 3.1 System architecture

The central hub was the **Coricancha**, at the intersection of the four conceptual quarters. Its functions included:

- principal temple of the Sun;
- ceremonial center;
- residence or workplace of priests and attendants;
- repository for cult images and offerings;
- geometric or conceptual origin of the ceque ordering;
- point from which some ceremonies moved outward.

Haucaypata, the principal plaza, was another major ritual assembly space but not the formal origin in Cobo’s description.

The network divided the valley as follows:

| Suyu | Position | Recorded ceques | Main-list huacas | Notes |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| Chinchaysuyu | northwest; Hanan/Upper Cusco | 9 | 85 | Cobo enumerates it counterclockwise |
| Antisuyu | northeast; Hanan/Upper Cusco | 9 | 78 | Routes toward the eastern highlands and montaña |
| Collasuyu | southeast; Hurin/Lower Cusco | 9 | 85 | Includes routes toward Huanacauri |
| Cuntisuyu | southwest; Hurin/Lower Cusco | 14 named, plausibly 15 branches | at least 80 | Its eighth entry appears divided |
| **Total** |  | **41 entries/42 branches** | **328** | Four additional unordered shrines follow |

The ceques were grouped in triads ranked **collana**, **payan**, and **cayao**. The words express relative precedence, but their exact semantic and social applications varied. A simplistic translation as “first/second/third” can conceal their contextual meanings.

### 3.2 Physical types of huaca

Bauer’s classification of Cobo’s 328 main-list nodes is approximate because descriptions are incomplete:

| Node type | Approximate number | Approximate share |
|---|---:|---:|
| Springs or water sources | 96 | 29% |
| Standing or significant stones | 95 | 29% |
| Hills and mountain passes | 32 | 10% |
| Royal palaces and temples | 28 | 9% |
| Fields and level places | 28 | 9% |
| Tombs | 10 | 3% |
| Ravines | 7 | 2% |
| Caves | 3 | 1% |
| Quarries | 3 | 1% |
| Stone seats | 3 | 1% |
| Sunset markers or tower groups | 3 | 1% |
| Trees | 2 | 1% |
| Roads | 2 | 1% |

The categories overlap and should not be treated as a perfect statistical census. Nevertheless, they demonstrate that the system was overwhelmingly grounded in water and stone, not in monumental temples alone.

### 3.3 Major and representative securely recorded nodes

#### Coricancha

**Securely attested and surviving in transformed form.** Its fine masonry remains within and beneath the Dominican complex of Santo Domingo. A ceremonial center may have predated Pachacuti, but the monumental form is conventionally associated with his reign. The Dominicans received or occupied the precinct after conquest; a monastery and church were built over Inca fabric during the sixteenth century. [Peruvian tourism inventory](https://consultasenlinea.mincetur.gob.pe/fichaInventario/index.aspx?cod_Ficha=1790)

#### Huanacauri

A mountain southeast of Cusco and one of the two most important sacred places in Cobo’s presentation, the other being the Coricancha. It was embedded in Inca origin traditions and elite initiation. Material dating to Killke and Inca periods has been found around associated places, supporting long use but not every recorded story.

#### Guaracince, Ch. 2:1

A level area in Chuquipampa within the Sun Temple complex. Cobo says it was considered the place from which earthquakes originated. Offerings reportedly included children during earthquakes, sacrificed camelids, burned clothing, and buried gold and silver. The statement is documentary evidence for the colonial report, not an archaeologically verified count of individual sacrifices.

#### Michosamaro, Ch. 1:1

A place near the Totocache slope. In tradition, a companion of Manco Capac was turned to stone there. Offerings included gold, clothing, marine shell, and other materials to obtain good rain.

#### Pilcopuquio, Ch. 1:3

A fountain and source of an irrigation ditch. Its inclusion displays the overlap of cult, water management, and royal memory.

#### Cirocaya, Ch. 1:4

A cave believed to release hail. Collective sacrifice sought to prevent crop damage.

#### Tambomachay and associated Antisuyu springs

Cobo’s Antisuyu first ceque includes **Tipcpuquiu**, a “boiling” spring; **Tambomachay**, described as an Inca lodging used while hunting; **Quinoapuquiu**, a double spring; and **Quiscourco**, a terminal round boundary stone. Tambomachay’s monumental fountains survive, but matching every recorded spring to existing hydraulic features remains partly reconstructive.

#### Vilcacona, An. 2:1

A ceremonial gathering node where, at a certain time, portable huacas and idols from Cusco were reportedly assembled, sacrificed to together, and returned to their places. This was therefore a temporary aggregation hub for sacred objects.

#### Curovilca, An. 2:4

A quarry. Sacrifices sought both continued stone supply and the stability of structures made from it. It connected extraction, construction, and ritual risk management.

#### Chuquimarca, An. 3:4

A temple of the Sun on Mantocalla hill, where the Sun was said to descend and sleep. Cobo reports child sacrifice among its offerings.

#### Chuquicancha, An. 6:3

A hill regarded as a house of the Sun. Molina’s account associates it with sacrifices for the Sun, Thunder, and Moon and prayers for the ruler’s health, prosperity, and victory.

#### Sacsahuaman and adjacent nodes

The fortress/ceremonial complex was venerated as a whole in connection with huacas such as a finely worked stone seat. Cobo’s list does not reduce Sacsahuaman to one node; several surrounding rocks, fountains, and places belonged to different ritual sequences.

#### Huanacauri–Matagua complex

Molina describes noble initiates traveling with their lineages and prepared llamas, sleeping at Matagua below Huanacauri, fasting, and ascending at sunrise. The modern Matao locality and Killke/Inca remains have been associated with Matagua, but exact identification remains debated.

#### Sunset pillars or *sucancas*

Three entries are described as groups of pillars or towers marking significant sunsets. These supply the strongest direct documentary evidence that at least some huacas served astronomical observation. Many were dismantled or disappeared, making exact azimuthal reconstruction difficult.

### 3.4 Secure, probable, and unknown locations

Bauer’s 1990–1995 field and archival project found plausible sites or approximate positions for **more than half** of Cobo’s huacas. This is not equivalent to identifying every shrine with archaeological certainty.

- Several ceques can be outlined from multiple matching places.
- Cu. 3, Cu. 11, and Cu. 12 could not be plotted confidently.
- Some Collasuyu lines appear irregular.
- Duplicate or similar place names create false-match risks.
- Colonial property descriptions often locate huacas relative to owners’ houses, estates, mills, or parishes that themselves moved or disappeared.
- Natural nodes survive physically but may have lost their names.
- Portable stones and cult objects could be moved, hidden, seized, buried, or destroyed.

The complete Cobo/Rowe inventory and Bauer-Beyersdorff onomastic appendix remain the practical node gazetteer; reproducing 332 entries without their textual qualifications would create false precision.

### 3.5 Extent

The shortest reconstructed ceques were under approximately five kilometers and contained only three shrines. Co. 4 and Co. 5 exceeded twenty kilometers. The longest documentary sequence extended at least forty-five kilometers toward Quiachilli near the Huillque pass and then continued to several unidentified nodes. Average membership was about eight huacas per physical line.

Accordingly, “Cusco ceque system” means the city and its surrounding valley and approaches, not merely the urban core. Some routes overlapped neighboring shrine landscapes, including Anta.

## 4. Connections

### 4.1 What an edge was

A ceque was not necessarily a constructed road. It was an ordered relationship among places. Its physical expression could include:

- a traversed ritual path;
- a chain of landmarks;
- a road or road segment;
- a canal or sequence of springs;
- a sector boundary;
- a direction toward a horizon feature;
- a mnemonic ordering preserved by specialists;
- several of these simultaneously.

The edge existed because people knew that one huaca followed another within a named sequence and because responsible groups periodically enacted that order.

### 4.2 Direction

Cobo lists nodes generally from the center outward. Descriptions of leftover offerings being deposited at the last shrine imply sequential outward service. However, ceremonies could also return toward Cusco, cross ceque sectors, or follow imperial roads. The written order should not be mistaken for a universal one-way traffic rule.

### 4.3 Straightness and sightlines

Zuidema and Anthony Aveni proposed that multiple ceques were horizon sightlines and components of an astronomical-calendar system. Cristóbal de Molina’s description of capacocha parties traveling directly across ravines and mountains rather than staying on royal roads lends some support to straight ritual trajectories.

Bauer’s fieldwork materially revised the model:

- some ceques or segments may have been straight;
- three tower sets had an explicitly observational role;
- many identified huacas do not fall on perfect rays;
- the practical lines often zigzag within wedge-shaped territories;
- the sites appear to define the ceque more often than an abstract ray selected the sites;
- adjacent ceques generally seem not to have crossed, although the evidence contains anomalies.

Thus **“41 sightlines” is disputed and probably incorrect as a general description**. A more accurate formulation is 41 recorded ritual directions, represented by perhaps 42 branches, of which a limited number had demonstrated or plausible astronomical functions. Fieldwork has called parts of Zuidema’s physical and calendrical reconstruction into question. [Bauer, Oxford Handbook summary](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34666/chapter-abstract/295369568)

### 4.4 Water connections

Jeanette Sherbondy argued that the ceque system encoded relationships among springs, canals, irrigation districts, ayllu territories, and political rank. Her key observation was that the social organization responsible for canal maintenance could coincide with ayllu organization and shrine responsibility. [Sherbondy, irrigation geography](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/240980961_Les_reseaux_d%27irrigation_dans_la_geographie_politique_de_Cuzco) [Sherbondy, “Los ceque”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/264118569_Los_ceque_Codigo_de_canales_en_el_Cusco_incaico)

The high proportion of springs supports a strong water dimension. What remains disputed is whether every ceque was primarily a canal code or territorial boundary. Bauer concluded that some huacas clearly served as land markers, while generalized use of all ceques as legal boundaries remains speculative.

### 4.5 Roads and long-distance ritual connections

Four high-level directions corresponded conceptually to the four royal roads. Individual huacas included bridges, passes, road sections, stopping places, and travelers’ offering points. The ceque system therefore interfaced with the Qhapaq Ñan without being identical to it.

Capacocha created longer chains connecting Cusco with provincial sacred centers. Parties carried offerings outward, often taking unusually direct routes, and communities received or forwarded them. Such journeys made imperial ritual authority visible across regional landscapes.

### 4.6 What traveled

Documented or reported movement included:

- priests, attendants, noble youths, rulers, lineages, runners, travelers, and sacrificial parties;
- live llamas, guinea pigs, and in some ceremonies children;
- chicha, maize, coca, textiles, feathers, marine shell, gold, silver, carved firewood, wooden maize ears, ash, blood, and food;
- portable huacas and cult images;
- prayers, songs, ritual instructions, claims to rank, origin narratives, and memory;
- ceremonial pollution or illness symbolically expelled from Cusco;
- political messages of rule, victory, prosperity, and territorial possession.

No reliable annual volume, tonnage, monetary value, or traffic frequency survives.

### 4.7 Speed, cost, and risk

No standard timetable, fare, or travel-speed schedule is documented. Terrain ranged from urban precincts to passes more than forty-five kilometers away. Risks included altitude, ravines, weather, hail, river crossings, crop failure, political conflict, and later prosecution for “idolatry.”

Costs were borne in labor and material offerings. Major huacas received expensive textiles, camelids, precious metals, and occasionally humans; minor sites might receive only coca or powdered shell. The phrase “ordinary sacrifice” conceals quantities now unknown.

## 5. Agents and Operators

### 5.1 The Inca ruler and court

The Sapa Inca stood at the political and ritual apex. Rulers:

- founded or monumentalized temples;
- designated or relocated certain huacas;
- assigned sacrifices;
- used shrines for coronation, warfare, hunting, commemoration, and dynastic legitimation;
- maintained estates and cults after death through their panacas.

Pachacuti is especially prominent, but Cobo’s list also connects shrines to Manco Capac, Sinchi Roca, Mayta Capac, Viracocha Inca, Amaru Topa, Topa Inca Yupanqui, Huayna Capac, queens including Mama Anaguarque and Mama Ocllo, and the Spanish-sponsored ruler Paullu Inca.

### 5.2 Panacas

Cusco’s royal descent groups maintained cults associated with deceased rulers and held responsibilities for selected ceques. The conservative reconstruction assigns one ceque to each of ten panacas. Panacas traditionally associated with the first five rulers held duties in Hurin Cusco; those of the next five in Hanan Cusco.

The evidence does not allow every ceque to be assigned securely. Cobo names responsible groups inconsistently, and later structural models fill documentary gaps.

### 5.3 Nonroyal ayllus

Ten nonroyal ayllus were integrated into Cusco’s social order. Zuidema and others reconstructed paired relationships between royal and nonroyal groups. Bauer judged this plausible but more speculative than the panaca assignments. Even if accepted, the custodians of nearly half the lines remain unknown.

Named groups in Cobo include Goacaytaqui, Vicaquirao, Usca Mayta, and others. Their responsibilities comprised supplying attendants, maintaining places, and presenting offerings.

### 5.4 Priests and huacacamayocs

**Huacacamayoc/wak’a kamayuq** designated a custodian or specialist charged with a shrine. Priests and attendants knew:

- the shrine’s name and origin account;
- what offering it required;
- the appropriate occasion;
- prayers and ritual procedures;
- the responsible social group;
- associated property or endowments.

Such expertise made them targets of colonial investigation and punishment.

### 5.5 Laborers, carriers, and sacrificed persons

The network depended on agricultural and pastoral production, textile labor, construction, canal maintenance, transport, cooking, brewing, and animal husbandry. Surviving sources privilege elite ritual and rarely identify individual laborers.

State and community obligations were not necessarily voluntary. Inca labor taxation and assigned service supplied labor and goods. Capacocha involved children selected, transferred, and killed under imperial religious authority. Some sources idealize their selection and treatment; that rhetoric does not alter the coercive fact that the state organized their deaths.

The evidence does not support calling all attendants “slaves.” Andean dependent categories, retainers, *yana*, and labor obligations do not map cleanly onto Atlantic chattel slavery.

### 5.6 Travelers and cultivators

Travelers made offerings for safe passage, especially at passes and road shrines. Farmers depended on water-source huacas and rites against hail, drought, frost, earthquake, or crop destruction. These users were operational participants even when they did not govern the system.

### 5.7 Spanish investigators and destroyers

#### Juan Polo de Ondegardo

Corregidor of Cusco from December 1558 to December 1560 and again in 1571–1572. He investigated Inca institutions, consulted indigenous records and specialists, and produced a chart or account of Cusco’s ceques and shrines. Whether he authored the lost source copied by Cobo is disputed:

- Bauer considers Polo the likeliest author and associates the record with 1559.
- Rowe earlier concluded that discrepancies prevented secure attribution.
- The word *carta* might mean map, chart, document, or letter.

Polo’s work combined administrative curiosity with a program for identifying and suppressing indigenous religion.

#### Cristóbal de Molina “el Cuzqueño”

A priest and skilled Quechua speaker, author of the *Relación de las fábulas y ritos de los Incas*, probably composed around 1573–1575 at Bishop Sebastián de Lartaún’s request. Molina says he interviewed elderly people and former participants familiar with the reigns of Huayna Capac, Huascar, and Manco Inca. His account is indispensable for festival routes and procedures, but his separate huaca list is lost. [CSIC digital edition](https://simurg.csic.es/view/990000117390204201) [Modern scholarly edition description](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/10.7560/723832.4.pdf)

#### Cristóbal de Albornoz

An ecclesiastical visitor and extirpator active from 1568 until his death in 1583. His *Instrucción para descubrir todas las guacas del Pirú y sus camayos y haziendas*, probably composed around 1582, gives an independent Chinchaysuyu shrine list and procedures for finding shrine personnel and property. He claimed to have found and ordered the destruction of more than two thousand huacas in the Chanca and Aymara areas. [Duviols edition listing](https://es.scribd.com/doc/130803915/Duviols-Instruccion-Para-Descubrir-Todas-Las-Huacas)

#### Bernabé Cobo

A Jesuit who completed *Historia del Nuevo Mundo* in 1653. He preserved the fullest list but wrote roughly a century after conquest and copied an earlier, now-lost manuscript. His avowed purpose was polemical: to demonstrate what he considered indigenous credulity. He abbreviated unevenly, introduced or transmitted spelling errors, and did not consistently name sources.

#### José de Acosta, Francisco de Toledo, Juan de Matienzo, Martín de Murúa

These colonial administrators and writers transmitted shrine counts, advocated investigation, or supported Christian administrative reorganization. Toledo’s reductions and ecclesiastical parish structures helped detach communities from older landscapes and ritual authorities.

## 6. Operation and Mechanism

### 6.1 Routine maintenance

A likely routine cycle, combining documented statements and cautious reconstruction, was:

1. A calendar, environmental event, life-cycle rite, or political ceremony triggered an obligation.
2. The responsible panaca or ayllu selected priests, attendants, animals, food, drink, textiles, shell, or other offerings.
3. Participants moved from Cusco or a local settlement toward the assigned huacas.
4. Sites were served in recognized order, commonly center to periphery.
5. Prayers were spoken; chicha was poured or sprinkled; blood might be applied; objects were burned, buried, deposited, or thrown into water.
6. At some terminal shrines, the remainder from earlier offerings was deposited.
7. The rite renewed relationships among sacred power, social group, resource, territory, and ruler.

The evidence does not establish that all 328 nodes were serviced annually or in one continuous circuit.

### 6.2 Ritual instruments

Known instruments and media included:

- spoken Quechua prayers and songs;
- sacrificial knives and fire;
- vessels for chicha;
- coca bags;
- finely ground *mullu* or other marine shell;
- textiles and miniature garments;
- camelids and guinea pigs;
- gold and silver objects or deposits;
- portable idols;
- possibly quipus for record and scheduling.

Polo reportedly obtained information from imperial quipu specialists. No surviving quipu can presently be matched securely to the complete ceque inventory. The popular image of the ceques as a giant landscape quipu is an illuminating analogy, not a documented Inca designation.

### 6.3 Differential offerings

Offerings were proportional to a huaca’s standing and function:

- At **Cachicalla**, passers-by reportedly offered coca taken from the mouth.
- At **Curovilca**, sacrifice sought continuing quarry output and structurally sound buildings.
- At springs, sacrifice sought continued water.
- At rain, hail, earthquake, sleep, health, and death-related sites, offerings addressed a named danger.
- **Guaracince** reportedly received children, llamas, clothing, gold, and silver during earthquakes.
- **Mantocalla** reportedly received wooden figures and wooden maize ears, burned camelids, and some children.
- Some major huacas received children through capacocha.

Colonial descriptions use “llamas” and “sheep” inconsistently in translation; Spanish *carneros de la tierra* means Andean camelids, not necessarily Old World sheep.

### 6.4 Worked example: Warachikoy journey to Huanacauri

Molina provides one of the best end-to-end ritual movements through the sacred landscape.

1. During **Capac Raymi**, conventionally corresponding approximately to December, elite boys prepared for **Warachikoy**, their formal recognition as adult men.
2. Each initiate prepared a llama for sacrifice.
3. The youths traveled with members of their lineages from Cusco toward Huanacauri.
4. They spent the night at **Matagua**, below the mountain.
5. On the following, tenth festival day, they fasted.
6. At sunrise they ascended toward the huaca Huanacauri.
7. The sacrificial llamas had been left at the foot of the hill at Matagua.
8. Ceremonies at Huanacauri formed part of a longer sequence of trials, dress, insignia, kin participation, and visits to other sacred places.
9. The completed journey changed the youths’ social status and publicly reproduced lineage and imperial hierarchy.

**Reliability:** the procedure is documented by Molina within about four decades of the invasion and based partly on elderly informants. The surviving manuscript is colonial and retrospective; exact day correspondences and route identifications remain imperfect. Bauer connects modern Matao and nearby Killke/Inca remains with Matagua but explicitly treats the identification as a proposal.

### 6.5 Worked example: expulsion of affliction during Citua

During **Citua/Coya Raymi**, approximately September:

1. Cusco was ritually cleared of outsiders for parts of the ceremony.
2. Groups assembled in the central plaza.
3. Runners carried or drove affliction outward along routes assigned to the four quarters.
4. Relay groups beyond the capital continued the movement.
5. On the Chinchaysuyu route, runners passed toward Anta and Tilca.
6. Sacrificial or purificatory material was ultimately deposited in a major river, including the Apurimac on that route.

This operation moved neither ordinary freight nor a literal pathogen. It enacted the transfer of danger from the imperial center to flowing water and simultaneously displayed the ordered relation between Cusco and surrounding populations.

### 6.6 Capacocha as an empire-spanning transfer

Capacocha was an irregular high-level state ceremony, often associated with a ruler’s accession, death, crisis, victory, or other major event.

- Children and valuable offerings were assembled or dedicated under state authority.
- Priests and carriers moved them from or through Cusco to designated provincial huacas.
- Molina says some parties traveled directly across the landscape instead of following royal roads.
- Receiving groups forwarded or escorted the offerings.
- The final deposit, sometimes at a remote mountain shrine, could include killing and burial.

A documented colonial land dispute cited by María Rostworowski shows carriers invoking capacocha to extend a territorial claim: they shouted that where their capacocha arrived, their lands arrived. This is evidence that ritual movement could be strategically manipulated to support property claims; it is not proof that every capacocha redrew boundaries.

### 6.7 Governance and discipline

Compliance was embedded in kin obligation, religious authority, and state hierarchy rather than a separate ceque bureaucracy. Sanctions under Inca rule are poorly documented specifically for missed ceque service. Colonial authorities later criminalized the same practices, interrogated custodians, seized objects and property, imposed penance or punishment, and ordered destruction.

### 6.8 Financing

No system-wide accounts survive. Documented inputs were:

- labor from responsible kin groups;
- produce and animals from community or state resources;
- shrine lands and herds;
- royal and panaca wealth;
- precious and imported materials, especially marine shell;
- specialist service.

The frequent offering of valuable textiles, metal, camelids, and labor shows substantial expenditure. Polo described “a great amount of property” being consumed in sacrifices, but no reliable annual monetary equivalent can be calculated.

## 7. Change over the Lifespan

### Period I: Local sacred landscapes before approximately 1400

Natural sacred places, ancestor cults, water sources, and origin places existed before imperial formation. Killke-period material at some later shrine localities supports continuity of use. The number and ordering of early lines are unknown.

### Period II: Regional consolidation, approximately 1000–1400

Archaeological models describe progressive centralization in the Cusco region. Local communities were incorporated through conflict, marriage, alliance, resettlement, and administrative consolidation. A larger shared sacred geography could have emerged during this process, but direct evidence for a complete ceque grid is absent.

### Period III: Imperial elaboration, fifteenth century to early sixteenth century

The Coricancha became the principal center; Cusco was organized as the meeting place of four suyus; royal panacas and incorporated ayllus received ranked positions and ritual duties. Older nodes were retained while royal houses, battle sites, quarries, roads, fountains, and dynastic memories were added.

Pachacuti receives the largest share of traditional credit. Topa Inca and Huayna Capac probably inherited and modified the system. A proposed Huayna Capac reorganization remains a scholarly reconstruction rather than a dated decree.

### Period IV: Late imperial operation and civil war, approximately 1500–1532

By the reigns of Huayna Capac, Huascar, and Atahualpa, the network linked the capital’s festivals, dynastic groups, water sources, and state cult. The Huascar–Atahualpa civil war likely disrupted personnel and authority, but sources do not provide a ceque-by-ceque account of damage.

### Period V: Invasion and political fracture, 1532–1558

Atahualpa was captured in 1532 and killed in 1533. Spanish forces occupied Cusco in November 1533. Gold and silver were removed from major religious institutions, and buildings and estates were allocated to conquerors and religious orders.

The Coricancha became the Dominican precinct. Many urban nodes became Spanish house lots, farms, mills, streets, churches, or parishes. Cobo’s list preserves these transitions by locating huacas relative to colonists such as Juan de Salas, Diego Cayo, Juan de Figueroa, or Garcilaso.

Cult did not end immediately. Paullu Inca appears in shrine traditions, and Polo wrote in 1571 that people continued venerating huacas when Spaniards could not see them.

### Period VI: Documentation for suppression, 1558–1572

Polo’s first corregidor term produced investigations of Cusco’s institutions. The first five indigenous parish churches were established in 1559. The lost source behind Cobo probably dates after 1559 because it mentions the parish of the Martyrs; a proposed upper bound is 1572, although that boundary is not firm.

This period paradoxically generated the record by which the system is now known. Indigenous specialists disclosed or were compelled to disclose names, sequences, rites, custodians, and properties; officials recorded them to facilitate Christian governance and destruction.

### Period VII: Extirpation and reduction, 1568–1580s

Albornoz’s campaigns, Toledo’s reductions, parish supervision, confiscation, and attacks on ritual specialists fragmented the operational network. Portable huacas could be burned, broken, seized, or hidden. Natural features could not be physically abolished, so authorities placed crosses or churches nearby, renamed places, monitored offerings, and punished custodians.

The Taqui Onqoy movement of the late 1560s proclaimed, according to hostile colonial sources, that huacas would defeat the Spaniards and their God. It shows that huaca-centered religious authority could become a language of anti-colonial mobilization. Its organizational connection to Cusco’s precise ceque assignments is unproven.

### Period VIII: Residual and transformed landscape, seventeenth century onward

By Cobo’s 1653 compilation, the centrally governed system no longer operated in its imperial form. Yet:

- many springs, hills, rocks, canals, roads, and place names survived;
- people continued local offerings and pilgrimage;
- land and water disputes preserved old names;
- Christian feast, cross, mountain, and irrigation rites could occupy overlapping landscapes;
- colonial manuscripts preserved a partial schematic memory;
- modern scholarship and heritage management reconstructed the network as an archaeological-cultural system.

## 8. End or Transformation

### 8.1 Proximate causes

The proximate causes were:

- defeat and removal of autonomous Inca imperial authority;
- seizure of the capital and its ceremonial wealth;
- conversion of the Coricancha into a Dominican institution;
- dispersal, death, conversion, concealment, or prosecution of priests and custodians;
- destruction or removal of portable sacred objects;
- construction over urban huacas;
- establishment of parishes and Christian ritual jurisdictions;
- Toledo’s population reductions;
- systematic anti-idolatry visitation.

### 8.2 Structural causes

The system depended on a political center capable of:

- ranking panacas and ayllus;
- mobilizing labor and surplus;
- sustaining the Coricancha cult;
- validating calendrical festivals;
- coordinating long-distance sacrifice;
- maintaining dynastic estates and ancestral cults.

Spanish colonial institutions dismantled or redirected all of these supports. Thus the network’s collapse was not only iconoclasm; it was also the loss of its governing, fiscal, demographic, and social operating environment.

### 8.3 Destruction and survival

Albornoz advocated consulting elders, caciques, parish boys, constables, and Inca plans to locate huacas even when communities said they had disappeared. Investigators were to identify priests and shrine property and determine who had destroyed the object. This was a systematic intelligence and confiscation operation.

Material outcomes varied:

- gold and silver were especially vulnerable to seizure and melting;
- buildings were reused as foundations or quarries;
- stones were broken, moved, buried, or Christianized;
- shrine lands and herds entered colonial tenure;
- springs, mountains, ravines, and horizons survived physically;
- traditions were concealed, reinterpreted, or detached from their former ceque order.

### 8.4 Date of “end”

Possible boundary dates serve different definitions:

- **1533:** end of uncontested imperial control of Cusco.
- **1530s:** appropriation of the Coricancha and major state-cult assets.
- **1559:** documentation and parish reorganization show active institutional suppression.
- **1572:** execution of Túpac Amaru and Toledo’s consolidation; useful for the end of the independent Neo-Inca state, but not a direct ceque-system termination.
- **1580s:** mature extirpation literature and continuing clandestine cult.
- **1653:** Cobo records the system as a past order while natural and local practices remain.

The most defensible conclusion is that the centrally integrated network ceased between the 1530s and later sixteenth century, while its nodes and localized practices underwent open-ended transformation.

### 8.5 Successors

No single institution replaced it. Functions were divided among:

- Catholic parishes, churches, crosses, confraternities, processions, and feast calendars;
- Spanish municipal and property jurisdictions;
- colonial irrigation and landholding arrangements;
- surviving ayllu institutions;
- clandestine or syncretic mountain, spring, ancestor, and earth rituals;
- modern archaeological, tourism, and heritage networks.

The Coricancha–Santo Domingo complex is the clearest architectural palimpsest of replacement without total erasure.

## 9. Failures, Manipulation, Violence, and Investigation

### 9.1 Administrative incompleteness

The record itself exposes operational instability:

- inconsistent shrine totals;
- uncertain responsible groups;
- duplicated or omitted nodes;
- Cobo’s four unordered additions;
- conflicting positions and names;
- huacas located in apparently incorrect suyus;
- lines impossible to plot confidently;
- mobile sacred objects.

These may reflect copying errors, late-sixteenth-century memory loss, genuine historical reorganization, strategic concealment, or the system’s original flexibility.

### 9.2 Territorial manipulation

The capacocha boundary case shows a ritual party deliberately extending a ceremonial endpoint and proclaiming the newly reached place as its land. Whether colonial testimony precisely preserves pre-conquest legal practice is uncertain, but it demonstrates that ritual infrastructure could be exploited politically.

### 9.3 Extraction and coercion

The system concentrated labor, animals, food, textiles, imported shell, and precious metals into noncommercial religious consumption. State and lineage authorities could compel production and attendance.

Human sacrifice, especially of children in capacocha and at certain major huacas, is repeatedly documented in colonial texts and supported more broadly by archaeological discoveries of high-altitude Inca sacrifices. Exact numbers for the Cusco ceque system are unavailable. Colonial authors’ polemical interest may color their language but does not justify excluding the practice.

### 9.4 Suppression and surveillance

Colonial officials used indigenous specialists’ knowledge against the network. Investigations asked for:

- names and locations;
- custodians and priests;
- ritual calendars;
- offerings;
- shrine wealth and fields;
- evidence of continued worship;
- community leaders concealing practices.

This combined ecclesiastical prosecution, intelligence gathering, property transfer, and cultural destruction.

### 9.5 Looting and archaeological loss

Pre-colonial offerings and colonial rumor made shrines targets for treasure seeking. Bauer observes that extensive looting leaves little prospect of recovering intact deposits from many lowland Cusco nodes. Urban expansion, agriculture, road building, quarrying, tourism, and undocumented collecting have caused further loss.

### 9.6 Fraud, robbery, lawsuits, and scandals

No surviving evidence supports a distinct corpus of “ceque-system fraud” comparable to commercial fraud. Relevant conflicts instead include:

- contested land and water claims invoking ritual markers;
- concealment of huacas from visitors;
- seizure of shrine property;
- unauthorized excavation and looting;
- potentially interested testimony by communities or officials.

Individual colonial lawsuits and land files are crucial for identifying sites, but a comprehensive case register has not been published.

## 10. Contested and Legendary Aspects

### 10.1 Manco Capac’s emergence and petrified companions

**Traditional/mythohistorical claim:** Manco Capac and companions emerged from Pacaritambo; beings or companions became stones at places later worshipped.

The stories are documented as sixteenth- and seventeenth-century traditions and were embedded in actual shrine practice. They cannot be treated as literal descriptions of datable migration events without independent evidence. Their historical importance lies in legitimating group origins and claims to places.

### 10.2 Pururauca stone warriors

**Legend documented in colonial tradition:** during Pachacuti’s battle against the Chanca, stones became warriors and helped save Cusco, afterward becoming sacred stones. Several ceque nodes were identified as Pururaucas.

Evidence supports worship of particular stones and the circulation of the narrative. It does not establish supernatural transformation or permit a confident reconstruction of the battle from the ceque list.

### 10.3 Pachacuti as sole designer

**Romanticized and disputed:** modern accounts often depict Pachacuti drawing a finished radial plan in one act. Colonial traditions do associate him with many nodes and with Cusco’s reorganization, but archaeology and the heterogeneous shrine inventory favor cumulative development.

### 10.4 Exactly 328 huacas as a lunar calendar

Zuidema interpreted 328 as significant: 41 × 8 and approximately twelve sidereal lunar revolutions. He integrated the ceque order with calendrical duties and social classification. [Zuidema’s Spanish edition](https://repositorio.pucp.edu.pe/items/4a0cd05f-895f-4696-946a-f82f859c7a13)

Objections include:

- Cobo’s actual total is not simply 328 when additions are counted;
- early writers gave several other totals;
- ceques did not all contain eight nodes;
- the inventory was incomplete;
- many physical routes are not straight;
- only a few huacas have explicit astronomical descriptions.

The hypothesis remains influential as a structural interpretation but is not an established decoding of an exact observatory-calendar machine.

### 10.5 The “giant quipu” image

Modern authors often compare ceques to cords and huacas to knots. This analogy captures ordered radiations from a center and may fit Andean habits of classification. No securely translated Inca statement calls the landscape a giant quipu. It should be labeled a modern explanatory metaphor.

### 10.6 Nazca-line ancestry

Comparisons with the Nazca Lines encouraged the assumption that ceques were straight ritual rays. Bauer notes major differences: Nazca has many line centers and hundreds of visible ground lines, while Cusco’s ceques were ordered through shrines and generally left no continuous constructed line. Direct historical descent is unproved.

### 10.7 Universal empire-wide replication

Polo asserted that similar ceque arrangements could be elicited in more than one hundred towns. Archaeologists have proposed radial systems at provincial centers, including sites in Chile and Argentina. Some are plausible, but geometry alone cannot establish Inca ritual function. Cusco’s exact hierarchy should not automatically be imposed on every radial archaeological pattern. [Comparative study at El Shincal](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0718-68942020000200165&script=sci_arttext)

### 10.8 Survival intact into the present

**Unverified in its strong form:** no evidence shows that all 41/42 ceques continued as a secret, continuously administered system. There are meaningful continuities in place names, water practice, ayllu identity, mountain worship, pilgrimage, and radial ritual formations elsewhere. Continuity of elements is not continuity of the complete imperial institution.

### 10.9 Treasure narratives

Because chroniclers described buried gold and silver at huacas and the Coricancha’s precious-metal decoration, the landscape attracted treasure narratives and looting. Specific claims of vast intact caches hidden along ceques generally lack documentary provenance or controlled archaeological confirmation.

## 11. Historiography and Representations

### 11.1 Principal documentary archive

#### The lost original ceque manuscript

The source copied by Cobo has not been found. Internal references suggest composition after 1559. Rowe proposed 1559–1572; Bauer favors Polo de Ondegardo and approximately 1559. The authorship remains disputed because textual discrepancies led Rowe to leave it open.

A manuscript reportedly once in Peru’s Biblioteca Nacional may have been lost in the fire of 10 May 1943, but its identification with the original ceque record is unverified.

#### Cobo, 1653

Cobo supplies the only nearly comprehensive ordered list. Strengths:

- 328 principal entries;
- ordering by suyu and ceque;
- names, forms, offerings, and custodial groups;
- preservation of a much earlier record.

Limitations:

- second-hand and abbreviated;
- colonial Christian polemic;
- declining descriptive detail after early entries;
- scribal and editorial corruption;
- uncertain relationship between 328 listed and all actual shrines.

#### Polo de Ondegardo, 1561 and 1571

Polo’s reports confirm charts or accounts of Cusco’s ceques, more than 400 sacrificial places within roughly a league and a half, and continued hidden worship. His administrative access makes him crucial; his role in suppression shapes the circumstances of disclosure.

#### Molina, approximately 1573–1575

Molina gives unusually detailed ceremonial procedures, Quechua prayers, Warachikoy, Citua, Inti Raymi, Capac Raymi, and capacocha. His testimony rests on interviews conducted only decades after conquest. The original manuscript history and Christian framing still require caution.

#### Albornoz, approximately 1582

Albornoz independently lists Chinchaysuyu huacas and describes investigative methods. Only a minority of his names correspond straightforwardly to Cobo’s spellings, demonstrating either change, different scope, transcription problems, or independent knowledge.

### 11.2 Modern scholarship

#### Paul Kirchhoff

His 1949 article helped establish the ceque organization as a scholarly problem.

#### Manuel Chávez Ballón

The Cusco archaeologist undertook early unpublished or partly published attempts to locate ceques and huacas on the ground.

#### R. Tom Zuidema

Zuidema’s 1964 *The Ceque System of Cuzco* made the system central to Andean anthropology. He reconstructed relationships among:

- moieties and suyus;
- panacas and ayllus;
- triadic rank;
- marriage and kinship;
- political precedence;
- ritual calendar;
- astronomy.

His work is foundational but highly structural, and he later revised parts of it. [Brill edition](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004612402/9789004612402_webready_content_text.pdf)

#### John H. Rowe

Rowe critically studied the source text, developed a numbering system still widely used, translated Cobo’s list into English, questioned straight-line assumptions, and analyzed panaca responsibilities and historical layers.

#### Jeanette Sherbondy

Sherbondy connected the ceques to irrigation, spring worship, landholding, and ayllu organization. Her work demonstrated that the network had material-economic functions beyond abstract cosmology.

#### Anthony Aveni and archaeoastronomy

Aveni collaborated in developing horizon and calendrical models. The strongest astronomical cases concern explicitly recorded pillars and solar observations; extending astronomical purpose to every ceque remains disputed.

#### Brian S. Bauer

Bauer’s 1990–1995 fieldwork combined walking survey, local interviews, archaeological observation, toponymy, and research in ten archives. His 1998 synthesis identified probable locations for more than half the nodes and demonstrated that many ceques were not perfect straight lines. He recast the system as a historically flexible expression of social and territorial relations. [University of Texas Press](https://utpress.utexas.edu/9780292792043/) [JSTOR book record](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7560/708655)

#### Rodolfo Cerrón-Palomino and Margot Beyersdorff

Their linguistic work exposes the difficulties of colonial spellings, Quechua and Aymara etymologies, folk reinterpretation, and names whose meanings are irrecoverable. Linguistic reconstruction can suggest a shrine’s character but cannot alone fix its location.

### 11.3 Current archaeological state

The evidence is uneven:

- monumental sites such as Coricancha, Tambomachay, Sacsahuaman, and some carved rocks survive conspicuously;
- many springs and hills remain but lack secure identification;
- urban huacas lie under colonial and modern Cusco;
- small stones and structures were easily destroyed or moved;
- archaeological surface material can establish occupation but not automatically the exact ritual name;
- oral toponymy is valuable but mutable.

### 11.4 Archives

Bauer’s research used archives in Cusco, Lima, and Seville, including:

- ecclesiastical and parish records;
- cabildo books;
- land and water litigation;
- censos and estate documentation;
- corregimiento cases;
- national-library and viceregal papers;
- early printed chronicles and manuscript copies.

Materials were written predominantly in Spanish, with Quechua names, formulae, prayers, and concepts; linguistic interpretation sometimes also requires Aymara.

### 11.5 Popular representation

The ceque system appears chiefly in:

- museum and heritage interpretation of Coricancha and Inca Cusco;
- tourist maps depicting perfectly straight rays;
- documentaries and illustrated histories of Inca astronomy;
- New Age and esoteric claims about “energy lines”;
- popular comparisons to ley lines, chakras, or a giant quipu;
- archaeological tourism at Tambomachay, Sacsahuaman, Q’enqo, and Huanacauri;
- modern Inti Raymi performances.

Common departures from evidence include presenting all lines as visible roads, all as astronomically aligned, exactly 328 as an uncontested calendrical total, or the entire system as a single design executed by Pachacuti.

No major film, novel, or game is known to offer a rigorously documented, ceque-centered reconstruction comparable to the scholarly literature. Representations are generally subordinate to broader narratives of Inca religion or “mystical Cusco.”

## 14. Conclusions

The Cusco ceque system was a capital-centered but territorially distributed institution whose fundamental unit was not an engineered line but an ordered relationship: a social group responsible for approaching particular sacred places with prescribed offerings and memories.

Its durable infrastructure consisted largely of the landscape itself—water sources, rocks, hills, passes, fields, caves, and ravines—supplemented by temples, palaces, roads, canals, fountains, pillars, tombs, seats, and portable cult objects. Its active infrastructure was human: panacas, ayllus, priests, attendants, farmers, carriers, travelers, initiates, rulers, and sacrificed persons.

It carried material offerings, ritual specialists, political precedence, territorial claims, water obligations, dynastic memory, prayers, calendrical knowledge, and assertions of imperial order. It consumed significant labor and wealth but was not a commercial network. Its operation was coercive in some contexts, most starkly in state-organized child sacrifice and compulsory labor, while also sustaining community relations to water, crops, ancestors, and place.

The system probably developed cumulatively from pre-imperial sacred landscapes and was substantially reorganized during fifteenth-century state formation. Its configuration changed as rulers, groups, estates, canals, and territorial claims changed. This flexibility better fits the evidence than a permanent, perfectly geometric master plan.

Spanish conquest destroyed its central government, seized its wealth, converted its hub, attacked its specialists, and repurposed its lands and buildings. Colonial officials recorded it precisely because they intended to dismantle it. The integrated institution disappeared during the sixteenth century, but many physical nodes, names, water relations, and ritual practices survived in altered settings.

The familiar formula “41 sightlines from the Coricancha connecting 328 huacas” should therefore be retained only with qualifications:

- 41 is the number of recorded entries; 42 may better represent physical branches.
- 328 is the main-list total, not an exhaustive historical census.
- Ceques were ritual directions or ordered pathways, not universally straight sightlines.
- Some alignments were astronomical; astronomy does not explain the entire network.
- The surviving list represents one late snapshot filtered through indigenous disclosure, colonial investigation, lost manuscripts, abbreviation, and later copying.
