# Grand Trunk Road: Network Dossier

**Proposed filename:** `grand_trunk_road_network_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

The expression **Grand Trunk Road** is anachronistic for most of the period covered here. No securely documented ancient or medieval institution continuously bore that English name, possessed a single charter, followed one immutable alignment, or operated under one administration from antiquity onward. The historical object reconstructed below is therefore a **succession of partly overlapping northern South Asian corridors**:

1. the ancient **Uttarāpatha** zone and routes;
2. the Mauryan “royal road” between the Indus region and Pāṭaliputra;
3. post-Mauryan commercial and pilgrimage corridors through Gandhāra, Punjab, the Yamuna–Ganges basin, and Bengal;
4. Delhi Sultanate imperial roads and courier relays;
5. the roads and sarais reorganized by Sher Shah Suri;
6. the Mughal imperial-road, sarai, milestone, revenue, and intelligence system;
7. their early-modern fragmentation and eventual colonial reconstruction as the Grand Trunk Road.

The dossier’s principal boundary is approximately **the sixth century BCE to 1707 CE**. The earlier boundary marks the emergence of historically visible northern urban, commercial, political, and Buddhist circuits. The later boundary—the death of Aurangzeb—is conventional rather than terminal: Mughal road infrastructure survived, while regional states, the Sikh misls and empire, Afghan powers, the East India Company, and later British India successively appropriated and rebuilt portions. Colonial reconstruction is treated as the network’s afterlife and transformation, not as part of the medieval system proper.

Evidence is marked as follows:

- **Documented:** supported directly by a contemporary or near-contemporary text, inscription, or securely identified material remain.
- **Probable inference:** a scholarly reconstruction consistent with the evidence but not explicitly documented.
- **Disputed:** substantial disagreement exists about interpretation, dating, identification, or extent.
- **Traditional/legendary:** transmitted in literary, religious, nationalist, local, or popular memory without sufficient contemporary proof.
- **Unestablished:** often repeated but not demonstrable from presently identified evidence.

## 1. Identification and Definition

### 1.1 What the network was

The Grand Trunk Road’s ancient-medieval predecessors were not merely a paved highway. They formed a **transport-and-communications ecology** consisting of:

- beaten roads, cart tracks, urban streets, passes, and riverbank routes;
- ferries and river crossings;
- bridges and causeways where political authority and local conditions permitted;
- market towns, customs points, fortified cities, capitals, and ports;
- monasteries, temples, mosques, shrines, serais, wells, tanks, gardens, and shade trees;
- royal and commercial couriers;
- horses, ox carts, pack cattle, camels, elephants, boats, and human porters;
- merchant caravans, money changers, brokers, caravan leaders, guards, craftsmen, mendicants, pilgrims, diplomats, armies, revenue officials, spies, slaves, prisoners, and refugees;
- letters, intelligence reports, imperial orders, coin, bullion, credit instruments, news, religious texts, relics, diplomatic gifts, and oral information.

The roads were physically discontinuous. River transport often substituted for road transport in the Ganges basin, while the alignment shifted with floods, political capitals, ford conditions, security, and the rise or abandonment of cities. The “network” is consequently better understood as **a durable east–west corridor with changing trunk alignments and feeder routes**, not as a single engineered road surviving intact for more than two millennia.

### 1.2 Names

| **Sarak-i-Aʿẓam / Sadak-e-Azam** / سڑک اعظم | Persianized Hindustani/Urdu | “Great Road” | Associated retrospectively and sometimes historically with Sher Shah’s road |

### 1.3 What counts as part of the network

Included are routes that at some period formed the main east–west corridor linking several of the following:

**Balkh/Kāpiśī–Kabul–Peshawar/Pushkalavati–Taxila–Lahore/Sakala–Jalandhar–Sirhind–Thanesar–Delhi/Indraprastha–Mathura/Agra–Kannauj–Prayaga/Allahabad–Varanasi–Pāṭaliputra/Patna–Rajmahal/Teliagarhi–Gaur/Pandua–Sonargaon**, with alternative eastern continuations toward **Tāmralipti**.

Excluded as a single continuous “GT Road” are:

- every route called Uttarāpatha in ancient literature;
- the whole Silk Road;
- all Mughal imperial roads;
- the entire Dakṣiṇāpatha;
- river navigation considered independently of its interchange with the land corridor;
- modern alignments merely near ancient sites without evidence of historical continuity.

### 1.4 Geographic extent

**Ancient core:** The strongest classical evidence concerns a royal road from the **Indus to Pāṭaliputra**. Strabo, drawing upon Eratosthenes and Megasthenes, reports a measured “royal road” of **10,000 stadia** between the Indus side of India and Palibothra/Pāṭaliputra. The precise stadium and itinerary are uncertain; conversion to a modern distance is therefore approximate. Nineteenth-century calculations gave about 1,149 British miles, but this depends on the chosen stadium and route reconstruction. [Strabo, *Geography* XV](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0239%3Abook%3D15)

**Probable wider ancient network:** Literature and archaeology support continuing connections westward through Gandhāra toward Bactria and eastward through the lower Ganges basin to Tāmralipti. They do not prove a single centrally maintained paved highway from Balkh to the Bay of Bengal.

**Sher Shah/Mughal configuration:** A near-contemporary Persian chronicle attributes to Sher Shah a road with sarais from his fort in Punjab—normally identified with **Rohtas near Jhelum**—to **Sonargaon in Bengal**, supplemented by roads from Agra to Burhanpur, Agra to Jodhpur/Chittor, and Lahore to Multan. The famous “Chittagong to Kabul” formulation is broader than the most explicit evidence and often combines later Mughal extensions with Sher Shah’s work.

**UNESCO nomination:** India’s tentative-list serial property uses the linked names “Uttarapath, Badshahi Sadak, Sadak-e-Azam, Grand Trunk Road,” but this is a modern heritage framework, not proof that all four constituted one historically continuous institution. [UNESCO tentative listing](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6056/)

## 2. Formation

### 2.1 Pre-Mauryan conditions

Between approximately the sixth and fourth centuries BCE, the Indo-Gangetic plain saw expanding agriculture, iron use, monetized exchange, fortified towns, territorial states, craft production, and itinerant religious communities. These processes created heavy demand for connections among:

- Gandhāran cities and the northwestern passes;
- the Punjab river system;
- Kuru-Pañcāla and the upper Ganges–Yamuna basin;
- Kosala, Vatsa, Kāśī, Vaiśālī, and Magadha;
- lower-Ganges outlets.

**Documented:** Buddhist texts describe merchants, caravan travel, river crossings, itinerant monks, rulers, cities, and northern regions called Uttarāpatha.

**Disputed:** Compiling these scattered passages into one fixed “Uttarāpatha highway” is a modern reconstruction. In different sources Uttarāpatha can mean a direction, route, macro-region, or collection of northern polities.

**Unestablished:** Assertions that the Grand Trunk Road was “built” in a specific prehistoric year, or that it has been an unchanged highway for 2,500 years, are unsupported.

### 2.2 Achaemenid and northwestern antecedents

Parts of Gandhāra and the Indus region entered the Achaemenid imperial sphere by the late sixth or early fifth century BCE. Achaemenid administration employed imperial roads, tribute systems, stations, and messengers elsewhere.

**Plausible inference:** Mauryan rulers may have inherited or learned from northwestern imperial practices.

**Unestablished:** The frequent statement that Chandragupta Maurya deliberately copied the Persian Royal Road is not securely stated in a Mauryan source. Similar administrative needs and the incorporation of previously connected territories are sufficient explanations without assuming direct imitation.

### 2.3 Mauryan consolidation

Chandragupta Maurya established the Mauryan Empire in the late fourth century BCE and received Seleucid territories west of the Indus following his settlement with Seleucus I, conventionally dated about 305–303 BCE.

Megasthenes, Seleucid ambassador at Chandragupta’s court, described administrative bodies concerned with cities, military matters, and roads. His work survives only in later Greek quotations.

**Documented through later transmission:** Greek geographical tradition knew a measured royal road from the Indus to Pāṭaliputra and a register of stages.

**Probable:** Mauryan administration regularized an older corridor serving the imperial capital, Taxila and northwestern provinces.

**Unestablished:** Chandragupta cannot presently be identified as the road’s unique “founder.” No surviving foundation edict records the construction of the whole highway.

### 2.4 Aśoka’s road works

Aśoka’s Major Rock Edict II and Major Pillar Edict VII record public works along roads: wells, trees, watering arrangements, and facilities for humans and animals. Pillar Edict VII states that banyan trees and mango groves were planted, and wells and watering places established. Translations differ over whether a term denotes rest houses or flights of steps to wells. One translation gives intervals of **eight krośas/kos**, while popular retellings sometimes mistranslate this as half a kos. [Pillar Edict VII translations](https://puratattva.in/the-mauryas-pe-vii/)

These works had both practical and ideological functions. Aśoka presented them as instruments of **dhamma**, royal welfare, and care for humans and animals.

**Important limitation:** The edict speaks of roads generally. It does not say all improvements were confined to the Indus–Pāṭaliputra road, nor does it document uniform construction across the entire later GT alignment.

### 2.5 Institutional character

There was no surviving road charter comparable to a medieval European corporation’s charter. Standing derived from:

- royal sovereignty over roads and ferries;
- local obligations concerning policing and maintenance;
- taxes, tolls, customs, and market dues;
- grants supporting religious establishments and water works;
- imperial appointment of road, intelligence, and revenue officials;
- the coercive capacity of armies and provincial governors.

Ownership was layered. States claimed authority, but local communities, landholders, religious endowments, merchants, and patrons built or maintained individual wells, bridges, sarais, tanks, and shrines.

## 3. Constituent Nodes

### 3.1 Major northwestern nodes

#### Balkh/Bactra

**Role:** Western gateway joining South Asian routes to Iranian and Central Asian systems; major source or transit region for horses, bullion, textiles, luxury goods, diplomats, soldiers, and Buddhist travelers.

**Status:** Secure as a long-distance node; its treatment as a formal terminus of one ancient Indian road is reconstructed rather than documented by a single charter.

#### Kabul/Kāpiśī

**Role:** Junction for routes through the Hindu Kush and toward Gandhāra.

**Risks:** Mountain weather, narrow passes, political fragmentation, brigandage, and the cost of fodder and pack transport.

#### Purushapura/Peshawar and Pushkalavati/Charsadda

**Role:** Gandhāran urban and political centers controlling approaches to the Khyber and Kabul valleys. They linked South Asian exchange with Central Asia.

#### Taxila/Takṣaśilā

**Role:** The best-attested northwestern hinge between routes from Bactria, Kashmir, the Indus, Punjab, and the Ganges plain.

Three principal archaeological urban sites—Bhir Mound, Sirkap, and Sirsukh—represent changing centers rather than one static city. Taxila’s route position underpinned its political, commercial, educational, and religious importance. [S. Sarkar, “Taxila—An Alternative Urbanisation Between the Silk Road and the Uttarapatha”](https://insa.ndl.gov.in/items/4eb46b50-c84d-4e0b-9c10-e3a0f34b3664/full)

**Chronology:** Important from at least the first millennium BCE; successively connected with Achaemenid, Macedonian, Mauryan, Indo-Greek, Śaka, Parthian, Kushan, and later regimes.

**Survival:** Extensive urban, monastic, stupa, and fortification remains.

### 3.2 Punjab nodes

#### Rohtas near Jhelum

Sher Shah began Rohtas Fort after defeating Humayun, principally to command the Gakhar country and secure the northwestern approach.

**Role:** Political-military anchor and the western endpoint named in the road program described by ʿAbbās Khān Sarwānī.

#### Lahore

**Role:** Mughal provincial capital, manufacturing and commercial center, court stop, mint and military hub; departure point for Kashmir, Multan, Kabul, and Delhi–Agra routes.

Travelers recorded markets supplied with Central Asian fruits and goods. Jahangir’s memoir notes the abundance of grape varieties in Lahore’s bazaars. [*Tuzuk-i-Jahangiri* digital edition](https://famineanddearth.exeter.ac.uk/displayhtml.html?id=fp_00180_en_tuzukijahangiri_vol01)

#### Shahdara and the Akbari Sarai

The sarai near Jahangir’s tomb at Shahdara is commonly called Akbari Sarai, but its present form may date substantially to the reigns of Islam Shah or Shah Jahan. Attribution remains disputed.

#### Gujranwala, Eminabad, Wazirabad, and Gujrat

These were intermediate market, craft, administrative, ferry, and provisioning nodes on different Lahore–Rohtas/Attock alignments. Local road positions shifted with rivers and political conditions.

#### Dakhni Sarai

A surviving fortified Punjab sarai, probably seventeenth-century. An Archaeological Survey report described enclosing walls, monumental tiled gateways, arcaded rooms, corner structures, a mosque, and possible hammam foundations. [Archaeological Survey annual report](https://ignca.gov.in/Asi_data/21765.pdf)

#### Sirhind

**Role:** Major strategic and commercial station between Punjab and Delhi; provincial administration, military passage, markets, gardens, religious institutions, and sarais.

#### Ambala, Thanesar/Kurukshetra, Karnal, Panipat and Sonipat

This chain connected Punjab with Delhi. Surviving kos minars and sarai remains securely attest the Mughal imperial alignment, although not necessarily an identical Mauryan one.

Panipat’s open plain repeatedly became a battlefield—in 1526, 1556 and 1761—because armies moving between the northwest and Delhi converged on this corridor. The road enabled invasion and imperial response alike.

### 3.3 Delhi–Yamuna nodes

#### Delhi/Indraprastha

Delhi became the decisive political and administrative hub under the Delhi Sultanate and Mughals. Multiple successive cities—Qila Rai Pithora, Siri, Tughluqabad, Jahanpanah, Firozabad, Dinpanah/Shergarh, and Shahjahanabad—shifted the road’s local approaches.

**Functions:**

- court and central chancery;
- principal market and redistribution center;
- intelligence clearinghouse;
- army assembly point;
- minting and revenue administration;
- junction toward Ajmer, Gujarat, the Deccan, Lahore, Multan, Bengal, and the Gangetic cities.

#### Palam and Badarpur

Approaches to Delhi with surviving or recorded sarais, gates, and milestones. Their remains illustrate how an imperial road was materially anchored outside city walls.

#### Mathura

Ancient religious and commercial center on the Yamuna, linking the northern corridor to western and central India. Buddhist, Jain, Vaiṣṇava, and other institutions attracted pilgrims and patronage. Under the Mughals it lay on the Delhi–Agra route.

#### Agra

Imperial capital under Akbar and Jahangir for substantial periods. The Sher Shah/Mughal system was not simply one northern line: Agra was the pivot of roads toward Delhi, Lahore, Allahabad, Ajmer, Malwa, and the Deccan.

### 3.4 Middle Ganges nodes

#### Kannauj/Kanyakubja

A major early-medieval political and commercial center. Xuanzang visited during Harsha’s reign in the seventh century. Its prominence redirected regional traffic even where it did not sit on every reconstructed ancient alignment.

#### Prayāga/Allahabad

Confluence of the Ganges and Yamuna; pilgrimage center, ferry/boat-transfer node, military position, and—after Akbar’s fort—major Mughal administrative and strategic station.

#### Kauśāmbī

Ancient fortified city and river-commercial center southwest of Prayāga. Its significance declined, demonstrating why the network’s nodes and alignments cannot be projected unchanged across centuries.

#### Varanasi/Kāśī/Banaras

Major river port, manufacturing center, pilgrimage destination, and scholarly-religious hub. Textiles, ritual goods, money, pilgrims, mendicants, manuscripts, and information converged here.

#### Chunar

Fortified Ganges chokepoint controlling river and road movement east of Varanasi. It figured prominently in struggles involving Sher Shah and the Mughals.

#### Sasaram

Sher Shah’s home base and mausoleum site. It lay in the Bihar section of the corridor and symbolically anchored Sur power.

#### Pāṭaliputra/Patna

Mauryan capital and a terminus of the royal road explicitly known to Greek geographical tradition. Later Patna remained a major commercial and administrative center, connected with river transport and commodities including textiles, saltpetre, opium in later periods, grain, and other regional products.

### 3.5 Eastern chokepoints and termini

#### Teliagarhi/Sikrigali and Rajmahal

A narrow corridor between the Rajmahal Hills and Ganges system formed the effective land gateway between Bihar and Bengal.

**Documented early-modern function:** customs and military control. Sources on Sher Shah’s fiscal policy state that goods entering from Bengal were assessed near the Bengal–Bihar boundary, while western imports were assessed near the Indus, with intermediate transit imposts restricted.

**Caution:** The ideal of only two customs points should not be mistaken for proof that unauthorized local exactions disappeared.

#### Gaur/Lakhnauti and Pandua

Successive Bengal capitals and major political, minting, commercial, and religious nodes. Changes in river courses, disease environments, and political capitals altered their road importance.

#### Sonargaon

Commercial and administrative center east of the main Ganges distributaries, connected with the textile-producing districts and Bengal river system. Sarwānī’s account names it as the eastern endpoint of Sher Shah’s great road.

#### Tāmralipti/Tamluk

Ancient port traditionally presented as the far eastern terminus of Uttarāpatha. Archaeology and literary evidence establish it as an important eastern maritime and riverine node, but the proposition that one continuously engineered road ran directly from Taxila to Tāmralipti is a modern synthesis. [Study of the Rājagṛha–Tāmralipti route](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.5353839)

### 3.6 Infrastructure nodes

#### Sarais

A sarai could contain:

- enclosing walls and gates;
- cells or arcades for travelers;
- animal tethering and storage space;
- wells, tanks, or stepwells;
- kitchens or grain supplies;
- mosque or prayer area;
- quarters for custodians, guards, postal staff, and sometimes separate Hindu and Muslim service staff;
- small markets;
- stables and relay animals.

Not every sarai had all these elements. Some were state foundations; others were built by nobles, merchants, imperial women, religious patrons, or local elites.

#### Kos minars

Kos minars were solid masonry distance markers, normally brick and lime-plastered, set along Mughal highways. A kos was not universally fixed; estimates around 3.2 kilometres are convenient but cannot be imposed uniformly. The size of Akbar’s or Jahangir’s measured kos may differ by period and locality.

The common claim that Sher Shah invented kos minars is disputed. The clearest surviving textual order is Jahangir’s. In 1619 he ordered milestones along a route under Bāqir Khan’s authority. Haryana’s official heritage account attributes the surviving series to Jahangir and reports **49 in Haryana**. [Haryana Tourism](https://haryanatourism.gov.in/places_of_interest/kos-minar/)

A 2013 Comptroller and Auditor General report recorded **110 protected kos minars** in five Archaeological Survey circles: 63 Chandigarh, 8 Jaipur, 15 Agra, 23 Lucknow and 1 Delhi. It also found that the ASI had not researched and analyzed them systematically as a category and documented conservation failures. This is a reliable count of protected monuments in those administrative records, not of all surviving or originally constructed minars. [CAG performance audit](https://cag.gov.in/webroot/uploads/download_audit_report/2013/Union_Performance_Ministry_Cultures_Monuments_Antiquities_18_2013_chapter_7.pdf)

## 4. Connections

### 4.1 Indus–Taxila–Pāṭaliputra

Strabo’s surviving account, ultimately dependent on Mauryan-age information, gives the strongest early evidence for a measured trunk road. The itinerary likely crossed Punjab rivers and the upper Gangetic plain, but identifications of individual stages remain debated.

**What moved:**

- administrators and royal orders;
- tax or tribute;
- soldiers, weapons, horses, and elephants;
- merchants and merchandise;
- diplomats;
- monks and religious texts;
- news and intelligence.

**Frequency:** Not quantifiable.

**Speed:** No reliable Mauryan end-to-end travel time survives. Messenger relays would have been much faster than caravans, but figures are unavailable.

**Cost:** No road-wide toll schedule survives.

### 4.2 Northwestern international connection

From Taxila and Peshawar, routes passed through the Khyber or other corridors to Kabul, Bactria, Iran, and Central Asia.

**Eastbound commodities included:** horses, precious metals, bullion, wine and fruit in some periods, glassware, luxury manufactures, and captives or slaves.

**Westbound commodities included:** textiles, spices obtained through redistribution, ivory, gems, aromatics, medicinal substances, manufactured goods, and religious texts.

Commodity lists vary greatly by century. “Silk Road” imagery must not be allowed to erase large-scale regional traffic in grain, salt, animals, cloth, utensils, and ordinary people.

### 4.3 Punjab–Delhi

This connection was both commercial and military. It conveyed almost every major invasion or counter-campaign between the northwest and Delhi. The same infrastructure that protected merchants also allowed rulers to concentrate troops, extract supplies, and monitor provinces.

**Risks:**

- river crossings and seasonal floods;
- political war;
- requisition by armies;
- brigandage;
- corrupt or unauthorized imposts;
- loss of animals;
- coerced provisioning.

### 4.4 Delhi–Agra–Allahabad–Patna

Under the Mughals, Agra and later Delhi operated as imperial pivots. Allahabad Fort secured the Ganges–Yamuna junction, while Patna integrated land travel with the navigable Ganges.

**Directionality:** High-value goods, imperial orders, troops, and revenue moved both ways. Bulk goods were often more cheaply carried by river than by road.

### 4.5 Bihar–Bengal

The Teliagarhi/Sikrigali bottleneck gave states a practical place to inspect, tax, block, or protect traffic. East of it, river channels were as important as land roads.

**Sher Shah’s fiscal design:** Later accounts attribute to him an effort to assess long-distance merchandise at frontier points rather than repeatedly at every locality. If implemented, this reduced transaction costs while concentrating revenue and surveillance.

**Reliability:** The policy is reported in laudatory administrative history. The degree of uniform enforcement cannot be measured.

### 4.6 Pilgrimage and religious transmission

The corridor connected or fed routes to:

- Taxila and Gandhāran Buddhist establishments;
- Mathura;
- Kurukshetra;
- Buddhist sites of the middle Ganges basin;
- Varanasi;
- Bodh Gaya, Nālandā, Rājagṛha, Vaiśālī and other sites via branches;
- Islamic shrines and mosques;
- later Sikh centers in Punjab.

Xuanzang’s seventh-century itinerary shows an interconnected religious geography including Kapisa, Taxila, Jalandhar, Mathura, Kannauj, Prayāga, Varanasi, Pāṭaliputra, Nālandā and Tāmralipti. It does not prove that he followed one highway throughout. [Xuanzang route study](https://frogbear.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/2.5.1_xuanzang_eng_oa-v2.pdf)

### 4.7 Cartographic warning

Maps drawing a single line from Balkh or Kabul to Tāmralipti often combine evidence separated by centuries. Famous nodes were not necessarily simultaneous members of one continuously administered system. A rigorous historical map requires date layers, alternative alignments, river courses, and confidence grades.

## 5. Agents and Operators

### 5.1 Mauryan rulers and officials

#### Chandragupta Maurya

**Role:** Unified much of the corridor under one state and established Pāṭaliputra as the capital of an empire extending into the northwest.

**Documented:** Greek-derived accounts associate his reign with Megasthenes’ embassy and the measured royal road.

**Disputed:** Whether he “built” the road or merely reorganized established routes.

#### Aśoka

**Role:** Ordered road-related welfare works and used inscriptions as a geographically distributed medium of royal communication.

His roads carried not only travelers but an imperial ethical-political program: dhamma edicts, instructions to officials, and the movement of dhamma-mahāmātras and other officers.

#### Agronomoi and related officials

Greek accounts describe officers measuring land and supervising matters including roads and distance markers.

**Caution:** Exact equivalence between Greek administrative categories and Mauryan offices is debated because Megasthenes survives through later excerpts.

### 5.2 Merchants, guilds, and financiers

Ancient and medieval traders operated individually and through kin, caste, locality, guild, and partnership relations. Caravan trade required:

- capital or merchandise advanced by principals;
- pack animals and drivers;
- brokers and market information;
- guards or political protection;
- arrangements for currency exchange;
- credit and remittance;
- storage, lodging, and dispute settlement.

Indian credit instruments later called **hundis** enabled value to be transferred without carrying the whole sum in specie. Their forms and terminology varied, and no evidence supports one centrally administered GT Road banking system. Sarrafs or money changers assessed coins, exchanged currencies, financed trade, and transmitted value through correspondent relations.

### 5.3 Religious institutions

Buddhist monasteries near routes provided lodging, information, ritual legitimacy, storage, patronage networks, and markets for donated goods. The relationship was reciprocal: merchants funded monasteries, while monasteries stabilized movement and connected communities.

Hindu and Jain institutions likewise benefited from and served pilgrims and merchants. In the Islamic period, mosques, khānqāhs, dargahs and madrasas became additional road institutions. Sikh gurdwaras emerged at important Punjab nodes in the early-modern period.

It would be inaccurate to depict the road as the property of any one religious community.

### 5.4 Delhi Sultanate operators

#### Sultans and central officials

Delhi sultans needed rapid intelligence from distant provinces. The barīd combined post, surveillance, and reporting. News writers, spies, provincial officers, mounted couriers, and foot runners formed overlapping chains.

#### Ibn Battuta’s couriers

Ibn Battuta, in India during Muhammad bin Tughluq’s reign, described two posts:

- **ulāq/ulūq:** mounted relay horses at intervals given in translation as four miles;
- **dāwa:** foot relays with three stations per mile.

A runner carried the letter in one hand and a rod or whip fitted with bells in the other. The bell warned the next relay, who prepared and took the dispatch onward without stopping. [Arabic text and translation context](https://echos.beniculturali.unipd.it/events/2195)

Ibn Battuta says intelligence could reach Delhi far faster than ordinary travel. One commonly reproduced comparison gives roughly **five days for news from Sind while a traveler required fifty days**. This should be read as testimony to relay speed, not as a universal timetable.

Couriers were low-status laborers exposed to heat, storms, animals, physical exhaustion, theft, and political punishment. Relay speed depended on many individuals running short segments at maximum effort.

### 5.5 Sher Shah Suri

Sher Shah, originally Farid Khan, defeated Humayun and ruled northern India as sovereign from 1540 to 1545.

**Network role:**

- repaired or reorganized principal roads;
- constructed or endowed sarais;
- placed postal horses and personnel;
- concentrated customs collection;
- imposed collective local responsibility for road crime;
- issued standardized silver rupiya coinage, facilitating exchange across his dominions;
- used roads to move armies and integrate Bengal, Bihar, Delhi, Punjab, and adjoining regions.

The fullest account is ʿAbbās Khān Sarwānī’s *Tārīkh-i Shēr Shāhī*, composed around 1580 under Akbar, approximately a generation after Sher Shah’s death. Sarwānī used Afghan informants and family connections but wrote under Mughal patronage. His statistics and praise require corroboration.

The chronicle attributes **1,700 sarais** to Sher Shah across his realm. This is a famous but uncertain figure. It cannot mean 1,700 structures on the Sonargaon–Rohtas road alone at two-kos spacing; it probably aggregates foundations on several roads or is rhetorical inflation.

### 5.6 Sarai staffs

Sarwānī describes personnel and provisions at Sher Shah’s sarais, including:

- custodians or shahnas;
- Brahmans for Hindu travelers;
- Muslim attendants or imams/muezzins in appropriate establishments;
- water carriers;
- cooks or suppliers;
- horses and riders for the post;
- guards.

**Documented claim in the chronicle:** separate arrangements were made for Hindu and Muslim travelers.

**Unresolved:** How standardized this was across the empire, how consistently costs were met, and whether poorer travelers actually obtained the promised services.

### 5.7 Village headmen and zamindars

Sher Shah’s system reportedly held local **muqaddams** and landholders responsible for robberies or murders on roads within their jurisdiction. Collective liability encouraged policing but could entail coercion, fines, hostage-taking, and punishment of communities unable or unwilling to identify offenders.

### 5.8 Mughal rulers

#### Akbar

Akbar absorbed Sur institutions into a larger and more durable system. His administration linked:

- imperial roads;
- news writers and spies;
- sarais;
- provincial commands;
- revenue offices;
- standardized currency and measures;
- military movement.

Abu’l-Fazl’s *Āʾīn-i Akbarī* is indispensable but normative and court-centered. Its administrative descriptions are not proof that every regulation worked uniformly.

#### Jahangir

Jahangir issued orders for milestones and infrastructure, and his memoir documents the imperial court’s movement along road networks. The better-documented chronology of kos-minar construction under Jahangir complicates the popular claim that all such markers were Sher Shah’s work.

#### Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb

Under Shah Jahan, monumental sarais, bridges, gardens, urban gates, and road architecture continued. Aurangzeb’s prolonged campaigns strained finances and redirected military traffic. The system continued but became vulnerable to warfare, regional rebellion, and fiscal pressure.

### 5.9 Noble and private patrons

Named nobles and patrons attached their reputations to sarais, wells, bridges, gardens and markets. Amanat Khan’s sarai near Amritsar and Wazir Khan-associated architecture in Punjab illustrate road infrastructure as piety, commerce, political display, and memorialization.

### 5.10 Coercive and illicit users

The network carried or facilitated:

- enslaved people and captives;
- prisoners under guard;
- armies living by requisition;
- tax collectors and confiscated revenue;
- spies and informers;
- bandits and protection rackets;
- fugitives, rebels, and smugglers;
- state campaigns of suppression.

No comprehensive traffic statistics distinguish voluntary from coerced movement. The road’s security institutions and its violence were inseparable: patrols protected some travelers while enabling extraction from others.

## 6. Operation and Mechanism

### 6.1 Ordinary journey

A merchant caravan’s journey typically required:

1. assembling goods and capital;
2. choosing a season and route from market intelligence;
3. hiring carriers, drivers, animals, and guards;
4. obtaining letters, bills, or recommendations;
5. paying city, market, ferry, frontier, or customs dues where applicable;
6. traveling between water and lodging points;
7. arranging fodder and grain;
8. presenting goods for inspection or valuation;
9. exchanging or assaying currency;
10. selling, consigning, storing, or forwarding goods at destination.

Travel speed depended on animal type, cargo, monsoon conditions, river crossings, road surface, and security. A heavily loaded ox caravan might cover only a modest daily distance; a horseman could go farther; a relay dispatch could move continuously.

### 6.2 Sarai procedure

At a fortified sarai, travelers entered through a controlled gate. Animals and bales occupied the courtyard or designated spaces; travelers used cells or arcades. Water came from wells or tanks. Supplies could be provided by the institution, purchased in an attached bazaar, or obtained from nearby villages.

Gates could be closed at night. Such closure improved defense but also gave custodians power to inspect, exclude, delay, or extort travelers.

### 6.3 Postal mechanism: a worked end-to-end transfer

The best-documented operational example is Ibn Battuta’s fourteenth-century foot post.

**Stage 1: Dispatch.** A letter or intelligence report left a town in a runner’s raised hand. The runner also carried a bell-fitted rod.

**Stage 2: Audible signaling.** Running at full effort, he shook the bells. Men waiting at the next small station heard him before arrival.

**Stage 3: Handoff.** A prepared runner took the letter immediately. The first runner stopped; the second continued toward the next station.

**Stage 4: Repetition.** With several stations per mile, the dispatch passed through many hands. Each runner covered only a fraction of the total distance.

**Stage 5: Reception and action.** At Delhi, the report entered the sultan’s information system, permitting orders, punishment, appointment, military action, or fiscal intervention.

**Payloads:** letters, news, official reports, and small high-value objects. Ibn Battuta states that the system could transmit intelligence faster than mounted post and could bring valued fruits for the sultan.

**Operational implication:** Its speed came not from one exceptional traveler but from parallel readiness, dense stations, signaling, and the transfer of exhaustion from one body to another.

### 6.4 Sher Shah’s relay and sarai system

Sarwānī says Sher Shah established sarais every **two kos** and kept two horses at each for news transmission. If a kos is approximated at 3.2 kilometres, two kos would be about 6.4 kilometres; other reconstructions yield longer intervals because the kos varied. The often repeated “eight kilometres” is an approximation, not a precise universal measurement.

If the network truly included thousands of relay animals and staff, maintenance required:

- fodder and grazing;
- replacement horses;
- salaries or revenue assignments;
- buildings and well repair;
- record keeping;
- supervision against misuse.

The exact annual budget is unknown.

### 6.5 Customs transaction

A merchant approaching a recognized frontier customs point would declare or expose goods, whose type, quantity, origin, and value could be assessed. Duties might be paid in coin or accounted against the consignment. Seals, receipts, or documentary proof could protect against repeated lawful assessment, although the surviving evidence does not allow one universal procedure to be reconstructed.

Sher Shah’s reputed restriction of duties to the Indus side for western goods and Sikrigali/Teliagarhi for Bengal goods served three purposes:

- concentrated revenue;
- reduced cumulative transit dues;
- enabled surveillance of high-volume chokepoints.

It also strengthened the state’s ability to blockade trade or seize goods.

### 6.6 Finance

No road-wide balance sheet survives. Financing mechanisms included:

- treasury expenditure;
- provincial revenue;
- land-revenue assignments;
- charitable or religious endowments;
- noble and merchant patronage;
- local labor and material obligations;
- market income;
- customs and transit taxation.

Claims that all travelers lodged and ate free throughout Sher Shah’s network derive largely from laudatory chronicles. Some sarais may have supplied fixed rations or services; others supported commercial vendors.

### 6.7 Security

Security depended on:

- forts and city garrisons;
- patrols and guards;
- responsibility imposed on village headmen;
- night closure of sarais;
- intelligence networks;
- convoy travel;
- armed servants and caravan guards;
- punishment and exemplary violence.

Robbery did not vanish. Political collapse, army movements, famine, and local revolt greatly increased risk.

### 6.8 Maintenance

Road surfaces were mostly earth, compacted soil, gravel, or local materials, not continuously stone-paved. Maintenance included:

- clearing vegetation;
- filling ruts;
- stabilizing approaches to rivers;
- repairing bridges, culverts, wells, gates, and sarai roofs;
- replanting trees;
- whitewashing markers;
- replacing ferry craft.

Local labor may have been paid, customary, tax-offset, corvée, or coerced. Existing sources rarely quantify the proportions.

## 7. Change over the Lifespan

### Period I: Early historic formation, c. 600–321 BCE

The northern route emerged from interactions among older paths, river corridors, mahājanapada capitals, markets, and religious itineraries.

**Secure:** long-distance mobility and northern regional exchange.

**Not secure:** one state-engineered continuous highway called the Grand Trunk Road.

### Period II: Mauryan imperial integration, c. 321–185 BCE

The Mauryan state joined Pāṭaliputra to Taxila and the northwest. A measured royal road and stage register entered Greek geographical knowledge. Aśoka added wells, trees, watering facilities, inscriptions, and official touring.

This is the earliest period in which a long east–west road can be described confidently as an integrated imperial system.

### Period III: Indo-Greek, Śaka, Kushan and Gupta-era networks, c. 185 BCE–550 CE

Political control fragmented and recombined, but Taxila, Mathura, Pāṭaliputra, ports, monasteries, and caravan connections remained important at different times.

Under the Kushans, the northwestern and Mathura zones were deeply connected with Central Asian exchange and Buddhist movement. Coin finds, inscriptions, art styles, monastic patronage, and urban archaeology attest connectivity, though not uniform road administration.

### Period IV: Early-medieval plural corridors, c. 550–1200

Urban hierarchies changed. Taxila declined; Kannauj rose; Pāṭaliputra lost its former imperial primacy; Bengal capitals and ports shifted. Xuanzang’s route demonstrates a still-connected religious and political landscape, but much travel used branches and river journeys.

The period is poorly served by any attempt to draw one fixed “GT Road.” It is better described as a family of northern corridors controlled by successive regional states.

### Period V: Delhi Sultanate integration, c. 1206–1526

Delhi’s emergence as an imperial capital reoriented the network. Routes to Lahore, Multan, Sind, Bengal, Awadh, the Ganges basin, and the Deccan were equipped with postal relays and intelligence stations.

Alauddin Khalji is often credited with expanding horse and foot posts, especially for military-market surveillance. Muhammad bin Tughluq’s system is directly described by Ibn Battuta.

**Institutional shift:** The corridor became an instrument of centralized intelligence as much as trade.

### Period VI: Sur reorganization, 1540–1555

Sher Shah’s reign produced the network’s most famous precolonial reorganization:

- road repair and alignment;
- dense sarai construction;
- postal horses;
- trees and wells;
- frontier customs reform;
- collective responsibility for road crime;
- monetary standardization;
- military and fiscal integration.

Sher Shah died in 1545 during the siege of Kalinjar, before the long-term consequences of the program could be tested under his personal rule. Islam Shah maintained parts of the Sur structure.

### Period VII: Mughal consolidation, 1555–1707

Akbar incorporated Sur road practices into a larger empire. Agra, Delhi, Lahore, Allahabad, Patna and Bengal were linked through administrative reporting, revenue remittance, military logistics, sarais, and markets.

Jahangir and Shah Jahan added milestones and monumental infrastructure. The surviving kos-minar and sarai landscape is substantially Mughal, even where popular tradition labels everything “Sher Shahi.”

By Aurangzeb’s reign, the system connected an enormous imperial territory, but the long Deccan wars and regional conflicts strained fiscal and military capacity.

## 8. End or Transformation

### 8.1 No single medieval termination

The road did not close in one year. Instead, several transformations occurred:

- ancient capitals declined and alignments shifted;
- imperial maintenance weakened when central authority contracted;
- river courses altered crossings and eastern routes;
- sarais lost endowments or were converted to forts, markets, villages, cantonments, warehouses, or residences;
- new powers appropriated the same corridor;
- postal systems changed patrons without necessarily losing personnel or techniques.

### 8.2 Post-Aurangzeb fragmentation

After 1707, Mughal succession struggles, Maratha expansion, Afghan invasions, Sikh power, regional nawabs, zamindari autonomy, and fiscal crisis reduced uniform imperial control.

Road traffic continued. Merchants and armies adapted by:

- purchasing protection;
- using convoys;
- shifting routes;
- negotiating multiple customs jurisdictions;
- favoring river transport where safer;
- relying more heavily on local political patrons.

### 8.3 Sikh and Afghan transformations

In Punjab, repeated warfare damaged some infrastructure while strategic nodes remained indispensable. Under Ranjit Singh’s Sikh Empire, sections between the Indus, Lahore, Amritsar and the Sutlej served a new state. Sarais could become cantonments or administrative compounds.

### 8.4 Colonial Grand Trunk Road

The East India Company inherited, surveyed, straightened and rebuilt sections of the older routes. The nineteenth-century colonial road—especially under governors-general including Lord William Bentinck and through engineering administration associated with figures such as Robert Napier—was a different system:

- engineered gradients and surfaces;
- standardized bridges and drainage;
- formal toll and public-works administration;
- dak bungalows;
- military cantonments;
- later telegraph and railway competition.

The name **Grand Trunk Road** belongs most securely to this colonial transformation. The British presented their road as both restoration and modern creation. That narrative minimized precolonial infrastructure while also converting heterogeneous earlier routes into one cartographic object.

### 8.5 What happened to the infrastructure

- Sarais became settlements, police posts, military camps, markets, ruins, protected monuments, or quarries for building material.
- Kos minars survived unevenly in fields, railway margins, settlements and highway verges.
- Wells and tanks silted, were rebuilt, or disappeared beneath urban expansion.
- Tree avenues were repeatedly replanted and felled.
- Forts remained military or administrative centers.
- Modern highways and railways sometimes followed the corridor but often bypassed older sarais.
- The India–Pakistan border divided what had been a major Lahore–Amritsar–Delhi connection.
- The Afghanistan–Pakistan frontier and later conflicts transformed the western approaches.

### 8.6 Physical remains today

Securely protected remains include kos minars, sarai gateways, bridges and fortifications in Punjab, Haryana, Delhi and Uttar Pradesh. An official Indian list includes Mughal sarais and kos minars at Amanat Khan, Fatehabad, Cheema Kalan, Dakhni, Nakodar, Uppal and other Punjab sites. [Government list of protected Punjab monuments](https://www.pib.gov.in/newsite/erelcontent.aspx?lang=2&reg=48&relid=27426)

Modern protection is incomplete. The CAG audit documented encroachment, deficient research, and conservation problems among protected kos minars.

## 9. Failures, Robbery, Coercion and Crisis

### 9.1 Banditry and official extraction

Road robbery appears throughout South Asian historical literature. Merchants faced both illicit bandits and official exactions. The distinction could blur where armed landholders, soldiers, or officials demanded unauthorized payments.

Sher Shah’s collective-liability rules are evidence that robbery was a persistent structural problem, not proof he eliminated it.

### 9.2 Warfare

The network was repeatedly disrupted by:

- invasions through the northwest;
- contests for Delhi;
- Afghan–Mughal warfare;
- rebellions in Bengal and Bihar;
- Punjab conflicts;
- Mughal succession wars;
- Maratha raids and Afghan campaigns after the bounded period.

Roads intensified destruction by channeling armies toward food, animals, water, markets and defensible nodes. Villages near major roads were especially exposed to requisition.

### 9.3 Forced movement

The network conveyed prisoners, hostages, deportees, enslaved captives, conscripted laborers, soldiers and camp followers. Early-modern South Asian armies were moving cities whose logistical demand could devastate roadside communities.

Direct route-wide slave-traffic totals do not survive. It would be misleading either to deny coerced movement or to invent quantitative estimates.

### 9.4 Forced labor and corvée

Road, bridge, fortification and sarai construction required large labor forces. Sources often celebrate patrons rather than record labor arrangements.

**Probable:** mixtures of hired work, artisan service, village obligations, convict labor, military labor and coercive requisition occurred.

**Unestablished:** the proportion attributable to each category for Sher Shah’s program.

### 9.5 Postal abuse and surveillance

Couriers and news writers protected governance but also supported:

- espionage;
- denunciation;
- monitoring of governors and nobles;
- rapid punitive orders;
- extraction of intelligence from local populations.

Court sources portray rapid reporting as good administration; persons under surveillance could experience it as coercive control.

### 9.6 Environmental failures

Monsoon flooding, shifting rivers, extreme heat, dust, drought, animal disease, and silted wells interrupted movement. Bengal’s mutable waterways could erase or relocate an alignment. Punjab rivers required ferries or seasonal crossings and periodically destroyed approaches.

### 9.7 No known road-wide lawsuit or investigation

No surviving ancient or medieval equivalent of a comprehensive public inquiry into the entire network has been identified. Disputes would have appeared in local courts, revenue proceedings, petitions, or chronicles, but the record is fragmentary. Modern conservation audits provide evidence about the afterlife, not the medieval operation.

## 10. Contested and Legendary Aspects

### 10.1 “The oldest and longest road in Asia”

**Story:** The GT Road is often called Asia’s oldest continuously operating road.

**Assessment:** It is one of South Asia’s most durable corridors, but “oldest” and “continuous” are not demonstrable. Many ancient roads elsewhere have comparable or earlier claims. The physical alignment, administrative system, endpoints and names changed repeatedly.

### 10.2 Chandragupta built the whole road

**Story:** Chandragupta Maurya constructed a highway from Bengal to Afghanistan in the third or fourth century BCE.

**Evidence for:** A Mauryan-age measured royal road joined the Indus region to Pāṭaliputra.

**Evidence against:** No contemporary source credits Chandragupta with constructing a Balkh-to-Tāmralipti highway. Western and eastern extensions are later reconstruction.

**Verdict:** Partly grounded, substantially overstated.

### 10.3 Direct imitation of the Persian Royal Road

**Story:** Chandragupta copied the Achaemenid Royal Road.

**Evidence for:** Achaemenid administration had ruled the northwest, and Persian imperial practices could have influenced successor states.

**Against:** No direct textual account of borrowing.

**Verdict:** Plausible comparative hypothesis, not documented fact.

### 10.4 Aśoka built rest houses every half-kos

**Story:** Aśoka placed rest houses and wells at half-kos intervals.

**Problem:** Translations of Pillar Edict VII differ. A prominent reading gives wells at **eight krośas**, and a disputed term may mean stairways rather than rest houses.

**Verdict:** Roadside welfare works are documented; the popular interval and precise facility are frequently mistranslated.

### 10.5 Sher Shah built the Grand Trunk Road from Kabul to Chittagong

**Story:** Sher Shah single-handedly built a 4,800-kilometre Kabul–Chittagong highway.

**Evidence for:** Sarwānī records major road and sarai programs, including a road from a Punjab fort to Sonargaon.

**Problems:**

- Sher Shah ruled only five years as emperor;
- the roads had predecessors;
- Sonargaon is not Chittagong;
- Rohtas/Jhelum is not Kabul;
- Mughal additions and later colonial reconstruction are often folded into his achievement;
- the 4,800-kilometre figure varies and is not securely derived from the primary account.

**Verdict:** Sher Shah’s major reorganization is historical; the standard geographical formula is romanticized expansion.

### 10.6 Sher Shah erected all kos minars

**Story:** Sher Shah invented and built the surviving kos minars.

**Evidence against:** Jahangir’s order for milestone construction is better documented, and surviving structures are often Mughal.

**Verdict:** Disputed attribution. Sher Shah may have used measurements or markers, but assigning every surviving minar to him is untenable.

### 10.7 Exactly 1,700 sarais

**Story:** Sher Shah built precisely 1,700 inns.

**Source:** *Tārīkh-i Shēr Shāhī*.

**Problems:** The figure is difficult to reconcile with the stated road spacing unless it encompasses several roads and other establishments. Few can be individually identified or excavated.

**Verdict:** A near-contemporary reported total, useful as evidence for perceived scale but not a verified inventory.

### 10.8 The road as an exclusively benevolent public work

Court chronicles and modern nationalist retellings emphasize shade, water, free lodging, safety and unity.

The same infrastructure served armies, taxation, intelligence, punitive expeditions and political centralization. This is not evidence that welfare claims were false; it demonstrates that imperial infrastructure was multifunctional.

### 10.9 Rudyard Kipling’s “river of life”

*Kipling’s Kim* turned the colonial GT Road into a moving panorama of India—pilgrims, soldiers, traders, officials and mendicants. This representation shaped global memory of the road as timeless and socially comprehensive.

It is literary evidence for the colonial imagination, not a neutral description of ancient or medieval operation.

### 10.10 National partition narratives

Modern Indian, Pakistani and Bangladeshi narratives sometimes claim the road as a national civilizational artery while projecting present borders backward. Historically, the network’s defining characteristic was precisely that it crossed the territories later divided among Afghanistan, Pakistan, India and Bangladesh.

## 11. Historiography and Representations

### 11.1 Primary-source traditions

#### Greek and Roman geographical texts

Megasthenes’ lost *Indika*, preserved through Strabo, Arrian, Diodorus and Pliny, provides early testimony about the Mauryan realm and measured royal road. Transmission through later authors introduces textual and interpretive problems. [McCrindle compilation](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/94/Ancient_India_As_Described_By_Megasthenes_And_Arrian_%28IA_AncientIndiaAsDescribedByMegasthenesAndArrian%29.pdf)

#### Aśokan inscriptions

These are contemporary royal documents in Prakrit and other languages/scripts. They securely establish road welfare works but not a complete itinerary.

#### Sanskrit, Pali and Prakrit literature

Grammatical, political, epic, Buddhist, Jain and narrative texts preserve terms and journeys. Their chronology, redaction and genre require care.

#### Chinese Buddhist travel records

Faxian and Xuanzang provide itineraries, distances, religious geography and urban descriptions. They record pilgrims’ routes rather than a highway administration manual.

#### Arabic and Persian sources

Ibn Battuta provides the clearest operational description of the Sultanate post. Persian chronicles, administrative compendia and imperial memoirs describe Sur and Mughal roads, sarais, milestones, news reporting and fiscal rules.

#### European travelers

Tavernier, Bernier and others describe roads, cities, markets, armies, sarais, distances and dangers. Their perspective was selective and often shaped by comparisons with Europe. Bernier’s *Travels in the Mogul Empire* survives in several editions. [Library of Congress record](https://www.loc.gov/item/05007866/)

### 11.2 Central historiographical debates

1. **Continuity versus recurrent reconstruction:** Was this one road surviving from antiquity, or a sequence of routes shaped by similar geography?
2. **Meaning of Uttarāpatha:** A physical road, region, direction, political-cultural zone, or all of these according to context?
3. **Mauryan centralization:** How much did the Mauryan state directly administer roads rather than rely on provincial and local institutions?
4. **Sher Shah’s originality:** Founder, restorer, or exceptionally energetic reorganizer?
5. **Scale of sarai construction:** Literal totals versus courtly rhetoric.
6. **Kos-minar attribution and measurement:** Sher Shah, Akbar, Jahangir, or several phases; and which historical kos?
7. **Land versus river transport:** Modern maps privilege roads, while bulk transport in the Ganges basin often depended on waterways.
8. **Public welfare versus imperial control:** These were simultaneous functions, not mutually exclusive interpretations.
9. **Colonial “improvement”:** British accounts often claimed to recover a decayed ancient highway while obscuring both precolonial sophistication and the colonial military-fiscal purpose of reconstruction.

### 11.3 Archive condition

Relevant evidence is dispersed across:

- inscriptions in museums and archaeological sites;
- manuscripts of Persian chronicles and administrative works;
- Arabic manuscripts and translations of Ibn Battuta;
- Chinese Buddhist canonical collections;
- Archaeological Survey of India reports;
- Pakistan’s archaeology and heritage records;
- Bangladesh archaeological and district records;
- British Library India Office Records;
- National Archives of India;
- provincial archives;
- museum collections;
- published Persian lithographs and colonial translations;
- local land records and oral traditions.

Losses include destroyed or altered sarais, missing local accounts, deteriorated manuscripts, undocumented demolition, changed rivers, road widening, railway construction, partition-era archival separation, and conflict damage.

### 11.4 Archaeology

Archaeological evidence is strongest at major cities, forts, sarais and surviving milestones. The earthen roadbed itself is difficult to date because later traffic repeatedly reworked it.

Secure inference requires association with:

- dated inscriptions;
- coins or ceramics in sealed contexts;
- architectural style;
- historical maps;
- measured sequences of milestones;
- excavated bridges or gateways.

Proximity to a modern highway alone is not proof of ancient continuity.

### 11.5 Popular representations

- **Rudyard Kipling, *Kim* (1901):** the most influential literary image of the GT Road as a “river of life”; colonial-period fiction, not ancient-medieval documentation.
- **Documentary photography:** nineteenth- and twentieth-century photographs made sarais, trees, milestones, carts and road populations part of imperial visual archives.
- **Partition literature and film:** the Lahore–Amritsar–Delhi corridor became a landscape of displacement and severance.
- **Modern travel writing:** often repeats the simplified sequence “Aśoka–Sher Shah–British” while compressing intervening centuries.
- **Heritage nominations and tourism:** promote linked ancient, Sur, Mughal and colonial identities; valuable for preservation but prone to asserting seamless continuity.
- **Film *Xuanzang* (2016):** dramatizes the pilgrim’s travels; useful as reception history, not route evidence.
- **Television histories of Sher Shah:** commonly portray him as sole builder of a Kabul-to-Bengal road and rarely discuss source problems.
- **Digital route maps and games:** frequently plot modern national highways as if they were ancient alignments.

## 14. Synthesis

The Grand Trunk Road’s deepest history is not the biography of one constructed object. It is the biography of a **geographical opportunity repeatedly converted into political infrastructure**.

The Indo-Gangetic plain offered a long east–west belt of agricultural wealth and navigable rivers. Its northwestern end opened toward Afghanistan, Iran and Central Asia; its eastern end opened through Bengal toward the Bay of Bengal. Major capitals arose on or near this belt because it concentrated people, food, tribute, markets and strategic movement. Once capitals existed, rulers built roads, ferries, posts and stations to hold them together. Those facilities attracted more commerce, pilgrimage and settlement, which made the corridor still more valuable.

The first securely recoverable imperial version was Mauryan: a measured royal road from the Indus region to Pāṭaliputra, supplemented under Aśoka by wells, trees, watering places and moral-administrative inscription. After the Mauryas, no permanent road corporation survived. Political fragmentation did not erase the corridor because merchants, religious institutions, cities and regional states continued to reproduce its useful connections.

The Delhi Sultanate made relay intelligence one of the network’s defining functions. Ibn Battuta’s bell-signaled runners reveal a system whose speed depended on dense stations and disciplined human labor. Sher Shah then imposed the most celebrated early-modern reorganization: sarais, posts, customs concentration, security liability, coinage, and an imperial road from Punjab to Bengal. His achievement was substantial but was neither the road’s beginning nor the complete Kabul-to-Chittagong system later legend attributes to him.

The Mughals expanded and monumentalized this infrastructure. Akbar integrated roads with provincial government, revenue and intelligence. Jahangir’s milestones made distance visible and governable. Sarais operated simultaneously as lodging houses, markets, post stations, checkpoints, religious foundations and symbols of sovereignty.

The network carried grain and cloth, but also command. It carried pilgrims and scriptures, but also spies and prisoners. It protected merchants and exposed villagers to armies. It enabled credit and cultural transmission while increasing the reach of taxation, requisition and punishment. None of these functions can be separated without distorting the system.

The medieval network did not end when one dynasty fell. Its administrative unity weakened after 1707, while its physical and commercial logic survived. The colonial Grand Trunk Road was therefore both successor and reinvention: it appropriated older alignments and institutions, subjected them to surveying and engineering, and retrospectively joined many centuries under one English name.
