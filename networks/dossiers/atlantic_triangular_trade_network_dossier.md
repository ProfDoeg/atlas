# Atlantic triangular trade (sugar, rum, slaves): Network Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `atlantic_triangular_trade_sugar_rum_slaves_network_dossier.md`

## Evidentiary conventions

- **Documented:** directly supported by contemporary records, statutes, correspondence, accounts, archaeological evidence, or a critically constructed voyage database.
- **Estimated:** reconstructed statistically from incomplete records; methodology and uncertainty matter.
- **Scholarly inference:** an interpretation supported by evidence but not directly stated in surviving records.
- **Disputed:** supported by competing interpretations or inconsistent evidence.
- **Legend/myth:** a later narrative that cannot be treated as a literal description of the historical system.
- **Unresolved:** surviving evidence does not permit a firm conclusion.

The word *slave* appears where it occurs in historical names, laws, or commercial categories. Otherwise, this dossier uses *enslaved person* to distinguish a human being from the legal-commercial status imposed upon that person.

## 1. Identification and Definition

### 1.1 What the network was

The “Atlantic triangular trade” was not a single chartered organization, centrally governed route, or uniform three-stage voyage. It is a retrospective name for interconnected maritime, commercial, financial, political, and coercive systems linking:

1. Europe and European colonies to Atlantic Africa;
2. African interiors and coasts to American plantation and mining regions;
3. the Americas to Europe, other American colonies, and—in the New England version—back toward Africa.

The best-known schematic was:

> European manufactured goods and specie → African captives → American plantation commodities → Europe.

A narrower North American schematic was:

> New England rum and merchandise → African captives → Caribbean plantations → molasses → New England distilleries.

Both circuits existed. Neither adequately describes the entire Atlantic economy. Many voyages were bilateral; ships changed itineraries; cargoes were transshipped; slave ships sometimes returned in ballast; Brazilian and Portuguese traders often operated direct South Atlantic circuits between Brazil and West-Central Africa; and the commodities sold in the Americas commonly generated bills of exchange rather than a literal return cargo. The Library of Congress itself distinguishes a European triangle from a North American variation. [Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/african-american-odyssey/slavery-the-peculiar-institution.html)

The most defensible analytical subject is therefore a **multipolar Atlantic system** composed of overlapping networks:

- the transatlantic trafficking of enslaved Africans;
- African inland capture, enslavement, transport, credit, and coastal commerce;
- plantation production of sugar, molasses, coffee, tobacco, indigo, rice, and later cotton;
- rum, cachaça, tafia, and aguardente manufacture and distribution;
- European and Asian textile, metalware, firearm, alcohol, cowrie, and luxury-goods supply chains;
- American provisioning trades in fish, flour, timber, livestock, horses, and barrel staves;
- insurance, bottomry, bills of exchange, partnership, mortgage, consignment, and commission networks;
- imperial customs, convoy, fortification, naval, judicial, and monopoly systems;
- resistance, maroonage, revolt, abolitionist correspondence, litigation, diplomatic suppression, and naval interception.

The SlaveVoyages project contains records of more than 36,000 transatlantic slaving voyages and more than 11,000 intra-American voyages. Its estimated universe runs from 1501 through 1866, outside the user’s nominal seventeenth-to-nineteenth-century limits at both ends. [SlaveVoyages](https://legacy.slavevoyages.org/) [SlaveVoyages estimates](https://legacy.slavevoyages.org/assessment/estimates?selected_tab=timeline)

### 1.2 Names and languages

No contemporaneous universal proper name existed.

| Language | Historically or currently used terms | Evidentiary note |
|---|---|---|
| English | *triangular trade*, *triangle trade*, *Atlantic slave trade*, *transatlantic slave trade*, *African trade*, *Guinea trade*, *Middle Passage* | “Guinea trade” could include gold, ivory, dyewoods, and other commerce. “Middle Passage” denotes the ocean crossing, not the whole network. |
| French | *commerce triangulaire*, *traite triangulaire*, *traite négrière*, *traite des Noirs*, *traite atlantique* | *Traite négrière* covers trafficking more accurately than the geometric expression. |
| Portuguese | *comércio triangular*, *tráfico atlântico de escravizados/escravos*, *tráfico negreiro*, *trato dos viventes* | Brazilian-Angolan commerce was frequently bilateral rather than triangular. |
| Spanish | *comercio triangular*, *trata atlántica*, *trata negrera*, *comercio de esclavos*, *asiento de negros* | *Asiento* properly denotes a Spanish Crown contract or license, not the entire network. |
| Dutch | *Atlantische driehoekshandel*, *driehoekshandel*, *trans-Atlantische slavenhandel*, *slavenhandel* | Dutch official descriptions often center the West-Indische Compagnie rather than a single triangle. |
| German, historiographic | *Atlantischer Dreieckshandel*, *transatlantischer Sklavenhandel* | Important in modern schoolbook and “triangular-trade myth” debates. |
| Danish | *trekantshandel*, *den transatlantiske slavehandel* | Commonly applied to Denmark–Gold Coast–Danish West Indies circuits. |
| Swedish | *triangelhandel*, *transatlantisk slavhandel* | Sweden’s direct role was smaller but included Saint-Barthélemy and commercial participation. |
| Haitian Creole | *komès triyangilè*, *komès esklav* | Principally modern terminology. |
| West African languages | No single demonstrable indigenous term for the whole Atlantic geometry | Individual societies possessed their own terms for war captives, pawns, dependants, merchants, markets, Europeans, and enslavement. Translating all of these as one uniform “slave trade” can erase legal distinctions. |

The earliest currently reported use of the conceptual “triangle” has been attributed to George Henry Moore’s 1866 *Notes on the History of Slavery in Massachusetts*, followed by later elaborations based partly on Rhode Island voyage correspondence. This genealogy remains less thoroughly established than the underlying commerce and should be regarded as a **historiographic finding requiring further bibliographic verification**, not as proof that eighteenth-century merchants never imagined triangular circuits.

### 1.3 Period boundaries

Three different chronologies must be distinguished:

- **Atlantic slave trafficking as a whole:** conventionally c.1501–1866. SlaveVoyages uses those endpoints for its estimates.
- **Mature sugar-slavery Atlantic network:** approximately the 1640s–1807/08, beginning with the plantation transformation of Barbados and the expansion of Dutch, English, French, and Portuguese imperial circuits.
- **Suppression and transformation:** 1807/08–1866, during which British and United States prohibitions coexisted with large illegal or foreign-flag trades to Brazil and Cuba.

The user’s “17th–19th c.” boundary is therefore useful for the network’s mature expansion, crisis, suppression, and afterlife. It is not its absolute beginning: Portuguese Atlantic slave trafficking and Iberian sugar colonies preceded 1600. Nor did it end uniformly in 1807, 1833, 1848, 1850, 1865, or 1888. Those dates concern different trades, jurisdictions, and forms of slavery.

### 1.4 Inclusion and exclusion

Included are transactions and infrastructures materially necessary to move captive people, plantation commodities, equipment, funds, or information through the Atlantic system.

Not automatically included are:

- every Atlantic voyage;
- every form of African slavery;
- the trans-Saharan, Red Sea, and Indian Ocean slave trades except where connected commercially to Atlantic supply;
- all plantation production;
- purely domestic slave trades, except as continuations or redistribution channels;
- the indentured-labor systems that succeeded emancipation, except as institutional afterlives.

## 2. Formation

### 2.1 Predecessors

The system drew upon several earlier institutions:

- Mediterranean sugar cultivation and coerced labor;
- Portuguese island plantations in Madeira and São Tomé;
- Iberian maritime expansion and Atlantic island colonization;
- African systems of enslavement, pawnship, dependency, commerce, warfare, and caravan transport;
- Portuguese feitorias, royal licenses, and merchant contracting;
- Spanish American silver and plantation economies;
- the Spanish *asiento*, which contracted foreign merchants to deliver specified numbers of enslaved Africans;
- European chartered companies combining monopoly, fortification, diplomacy, and private capital.

The English voyages associated with John Hawkins in the 1560s are often presented as the first English “triangles.” They were precedents, not the formation of the mature multinational system. Recent scholarship describes “triangular trade” as convenient shorthand and notes that Portuguese and Spanish merchants had established comparable circuits earlier. [English Historical Review](https://academic.oup.com/ehr/article/139/598-599/680/7640954)

### 2.2 Sugar’s structural role

The central transformation was the rapid development of capital-intensive sugar plantations. Sugar required:

- land clearance;
- planting, cutting, and rapid transport of cane;
- mills and rollers;
- boiling houses, furnaces, copper kettles, curing houses, and warehouses;
- skilled and unskilled labor;
- large initial capital;
- continuous supplies of food, timber, livestock, ironwork, containers, and replacement labor;
- merchant access to distant consumer markets.

Molasses, skimmings, and syrup were by-products or lower-value fractions of sugar manufacture. Distillation converted them into durable alcoholic commodities. Rum and related spirits entered naval provisioning, plantation rations, tavern and household markets, Indigenous commerce, and African coastal exchange. It is nevertheless **false as a universal proposition** that rum purchased most Atlantic captives. Textile, metal, firearm, cowrie, alcohol, and currency mixes varied greatly by African region and time.

### 2.3 Political and institutional conditions

The system grew because European states created protected colonial markets and supplied naval force, charters, customs rules, admiralty courts, forts, and diplomatic backing.

In the English/British system:

- the Navigation Act of 1660 restricted colonial carriage to English or colonial vessels and enumerated commodities;
- subsequent acts made England a metropolitan staple for important colonial goods;
- the Company of Royal Adventurers preceded the Royal African Company;
- Charles II chartered the Royal African Company in 1672;
- the company claimed a vast West African trading monopoly and maintained coastal forts, agents, ships, soldiers, and accounts;
- Parliament opened English African commerce to wider participation in 1698, weakening monopoly control without ending the company immediately.

The surviving Royal African Company material is dispersed among the British National Archives and repositories including the Bodleian Library. [UK National Archives catalogue](https://discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk/details/c/F161609) The Navigation Laws made British imperial shipping and the colonial staple system matters of state policy. [UK Parliament](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/tradeindustry/importexport/overview/navigationlaws/)

Other imperial frameworks included:

- the Dutch West-Indische Compagnie (WIC), chartered in 1621;
- French monopoly companies, the *Exclusif* colonial trading regime, and the 1685 ordinance later called the *Code noir*;
- Portuguese royal licensing and Brazilian merchant houses tied to Angola and the Bight of Benin;
- Danish companies and royal administration on the Gold Coast and in the Danish West Indies;
- Spanish *asientos*, licensed ports, customs officials, and contraband markets.

The WIC carried firearms, textiles, metal goods, pottery, powder, and iron to Africa; acquired gold, ivory, and enslaved persons; redistributed captives through Curaçao and other American markets; and carried plantation commodities toward Europe. Dutch archival holdings include the Old and Second WIC, the Society of Suriname, the Society of Berbice, and records of Dutch possessions on the Guinea coast. [Nationaal Archief](https://nationaalarchief.nl/onderzoeken/zoekhulpen/slavernijverleden) [Nationaal Archief overview](https://www.nationaalarchief.nl/beleven/nieuws/excuses-slavernijverleden)

### 2.4 African formation was not passive

European traders generally could not penetrate and command African interiors at will. They relied on African rulers, commercial brokers, military entrepreneurs, caravan organizers, canoe operators, linguists, creditors, and coastal merchants.

Captives entered the export network through:

- warfare and state expansion;
- raids and kidnapping;
- judicial enslavement;
- tribute;
- debt enforcement and pawnship conversion;
- sale by political or household authorities;
- famine and social dislocation;
- resale through multiple markets.

The relative importance of these channels differed sharply. Claims that Europeans personally captured all exported Africans are false; claims that Europeans merely bought people in an autonomous African market and bore no causal responsibility are equally misleading. **Documented structure:** European and American demand, imported goods, shipping capacity, and credit interacted with African political and commercial agency. Causation in particular wars remains case-specific.

No finite list can include the nearly one thousand places appearing in SlaveVoyages as construction, registration, clearance, embarkation, or disembarkation locations. The following are the principal node classes and representative hubs. SlaveVoyages groups those places into broad and specific regions precisely because port names and jurisdictions changed. ### 3.2 African embarkation regions and hubs

| Region/node | Function | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|---|
| Senegambia: Saint-Louis, Gorée, Gambia River, Albreda, James/Kunta Kinteh Island | River access, forts, provisioning, captives, gum, gold, hides | Securely documented; importance of individual memorial sites is sometimes exaggerated |
| Sierra Leone and Windward Coast | Roadsteads, rivers, shipboard trading, rice and provisions, captives | Secure; decentralized commerce often left thinner company records |

| Bight of Benin: Ouidah/Whydah, Lagos, Porto-Novo, Badagry | Major captive-export zone; brokers linked coast to Dahomey and interior markets | Secure; local route and “Door of No Return” narratives require site-specific criticism |

The forts were not uniformly giant “slave warehouses.” Some held captives temporarily; others mainly housed European staff, goods, arms, or administration, while most captives remained outside until embarkation. Their popular memorial function should not be substituted for port-by-port operational evidence.

Ghana’s forts originated between 1482 and 1786 as fortified trading establishments and were repeatedly transferred among European powers. Their role in captive transit intensified in the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. [UNESCO, Spanish](https://www.unesco.org/es/articles/las-rutas-de-las-personas-esclavizadas-los-22-primeros-lugares-se-incorporan-la-nueva-red-de-lugares)

Kunta Kinteh Island and associated Gambia sites span trading and suppression phases: the Six-Gun Battery (1816) and Fort Bullen (1826) were intended to obstruct illegal trafficking after the British prohibition. [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/761/)

Bridgetown’s Carlisle Bay functioned as a first port of call and a hub for sugar and enslaved-person traffic; its garrison projected British military power across the eastern Caribbean. [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1376)

Port Royal possessed a deep harbor and six defensive forts. Its 1692 earthquake preserved submerged urban archaeology documenting a hub whose artifacts came from Europe and Asia as well as the Atlantic colonies. [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1595/)

Portobelo, founded on 20 March 1597 to replace Nombre de Dios, connected Caribbean fleets and fairs to the trans-isthmian route. Its fortifications illustrate the armed infrastructure protecting Spanish imperial commerce. [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/135/)

### 3.4 Inland nodes

The iconic triangle suppresses thousands of indispensable inland places:

- African courts and capitals that regulated trade;
- weekly markets and fairs;
- caravan stations and river landings;
- barracoons and merchant compounds;
- plantations, mills, boiling houses, distilleries, and provision grounds;
- American auction houses and inspection yards;
- warehouses, ropewalks, cooperages, shipyards, refineries, and countinghouses;
- European textile districts, gun makers, brassworks, glass-bead producers, and copper manufacturers;
- banks, insurers, notaries, and admiralty courts.

These were not ancillary. Without them the ocean legs could not operate.

## 4. Connections

### 4.1 Europe or North America to Africa: the outward passage

Ships departed with assortments selected for particular African markets. Cargo might include:

- Indian and European cotton textiles;
- woolens and linens;
- iron and copper bars;
- brass pans and manillas;
- firearms, powder, and shot;
- knives, tools, beads, ceramics, hats, and clothing;
- tobacco;
- brandy, rum, gin, wine, or other spirits;
- cowrie shells;
- specie;
- provisions and replacement rigging.

Cargo was not normally exchanged in a single barter. Captains and supercargoes negotiated with multiple merchants over weeks or months, extended credit, paid customs and ceremonial gifts, and purchased captives in small groups. Prices were expressed in local “bars,” ounces, bundles, or standardized trade packages rather than only sterling coin.

The ship’s outward cargo also financed:

- port duties and royal customs;
- broker commissions;
- canoe transport;
- food for captives and crew;
- fort fees;
- interpreters and local labor;
- presents required to open negotiations;
- debt from earlier voyages.

### 4.2 Inland Africa to the coast

Captives could pass through several owners and languages before reaching an Atlantic vessel. They traveled on foot, by canoe, or in caravans. Some were confined in inland markets, merchant compounds, coastal barracoons, or forts. Others remained aboard a vessel while it cruised between purchase points.

**Unresolved at aggregate scale:** documentation is generally strongest at embarkation and weakest for the individual’s origin and path to the coast. Ethnonyms recorded by purchasers might indicate language, market, claimed origin, perceived identity, or only a broad commercial label.

### 4.3 Africa to the Americas: the Middle Passage

A ship might remain on the African coast for months before obtaining its intended complement. After departure:

- captives were counted and entered in commercial records;
- men, women, and children were often separated;
- many were confined below deck in restrictive spaces;
- shackling practices varied by sex, age, perceived risk, and voyage stage;
- crews rationed food and water and brought groups on deck;
- disease, dehydration, violence, suicide, and resistance caused deaths;
- captives exchanged information, formed relationships, preserved religious practices, and organized revolts;
- captains altered course in response to weather, markets, disease, war, or intelligence.

The “Middle Passage” could last several weeks or much longer. A UNESCO account for Liverpool describes a common 50–60-day transatlantic phase, but that is an indicative range rather than a universal duration. Coastal acquisition time made the total slaving voyage much longer. [UNESCO Liverpool nomination](https://whc.unesco.org/uploads/nominations/1150.pdf)

SlaveVoyages estimates approximately **12.52 million people embarked in Africa and 10.7 million disembarked**, mainly in the Americas. The difference is approximately 1.8 million deaths at sea, although the database totals do not measure all deaths during capture, inland movement, coastal confinement, or after landing. These are **statistical estimates**, not exact counts: documented voyage records are supplemented with imputations by carrier, year, route, and known voyage averages. [SlaveVoyages methodology](https://legacy.slavevoyages.org/blog/methodology-trans-atlantic) [Estimation paper](https://www.slavevoyages.org/documents/download/2010estimates-method.pdf)

The database methodology gives approximate averages of 304 embarked and 265 disembarked per inferred voyage. These cross-century averages conceal large differences by vessel, period, and route.

### 4.4 Sale and redistribution

On arrival, merchants or factors:

1. notified consignees and officials;
2. registered and inspected the ship where required;
3. quarantined or “refreshed” survivors in some ports;
4. advertised or privately arranged sales;
5. sold people individually, in groups, or through a rapid selection procedure;
6. accepted cash, produce, bills, promissory notes, or credit;
7. deducted commissions, duties, medical costs, and port charges;
8. remitted the net proceeds to owners and investors.

Purchasers moved enslaved people onward by coastal vessel, riverboat, wagon, or forced march. SlaveVoyages’ intra-American database documents over 11,000 maritime redistribution voyages, but this excludes most overland transfers. [Intra-American database methodology](https://legacy.slavevoyages.org/blog/methodology-intra-american)

### 4.5 Plantation to Atlantic market

Sugar’s chain ran:

> cane field → mill → juice clarification → boiling and concentration → crystallization → curing → muscovado sugar and molasses → hogshead or cask → warehouse → customs inspection → ship → refinery or consumer market.

Labor included planting, weeding, cutting, carting, milling, boiling, coopering, livestock care, carpentry, masonry, clerical work, and domestic service. The mill-and-boiling sequence imposed severe time pressure because harvested cane deteriorated rapidly.

Molasses traveled to:

- local plantation or urban distilleries;
- New England;
- other colonies by licensed or smuggled commerce;
- fisheries, naval, military, and household consumers.

New England distillers produced rum for local consumption, fisheries, maritime provisioning, Indigenous trade, the Caribbean, and Africa. Rum used in slave trading was important in particular North American circuits but represented only part of total production. A Rhode Island Historical Society treatment estimates that roughly one-tenth of Rhode Island rum went into the African slave trade and that local consumption absorbed a much larger share; the figure is interpretive and depends on incomplete production and export records. [Rhode Island Historical Society](https://www.rihs.org/assetts/files/publications/2002_Fall.pdf)

### 4.6 Information and correspondence

Information moved with:

- sailing instructions;
- duplicate and triplicate letters;
- account currents and invoices;
- bills of lading;
- price lists and market reports;
- captains’ logs and journals;
- newspapers and shipping lists;
- insurance policies and protests;
- plantation correspondence;
- intelligence about wars, embargoes, privateers, disease, revolts, and crop failures;
- African merchant and ruler messages carried orally or in writing;
- abolitionist pamphlets, petitions, ship diagrams, testimony, and newspapers.

Letters could outrun, accompany, or arrive after cargo. Merchants sent duplicates by different ships because capture, shipwreck, or delay was expected. Credit decisions therefore rested on stale and uneven information.

### 4.7 Credit and remittance

A successful circuit did not require a ship to load sugar after selling captives. Proceeds might instead become:

- a bill drawn on a London or colonial house;
- a balance in a factor’s account;
- a claim secured on plantation output;
- a return cargo bought by another vessel;
- local reinvestment;
- payment to creditors;
- compensation to partners;
- insurance recovery after a covered loss.

This is one reason literal triangle maps are inadequate. The **ship route, commodity route, credit route, and ownership route might have different geometries**.

### 4.8 Risk

Principal risks included:

- shipwreck and grounding;
- hurricanes and calms;
- war and privateering;
- piracy and theft;
- epidemic disease;
- spoiled food or water;
- desertion and mutiny;
- captive revolt;
- fraudulent accounting;
- market collapse;
- embargo, confiscation, and customs seizure;
- debtor default;
- mortality high enough to destroy expected returns;
- suppression after prohibition.

Marine insurance distributed some risks among underwriters. Whether captive mortality fell within a policy could generate litigation, as the *Zong* case demonstrates.

## 5. Agents and Operators

### 5.1 European and American institutions

- Monarchies and legislatures issued charters, statutes, treaties, and prohibitions.
- Chartered companies raised capital and operated forts, garrisons, ships, and agencies.
- Private partnerships financed individual voyages by divided shares.
- Merchant houses supplied goods and consigned human and commodity cargoes.
- Plantation owners mortgaged land, crops, mills, and enslaved people.
- Insurers priced maritime and wartime risks.
- Customs services collected duties and prosecuted smuggling.
- Admiralty and common-law courts decided seizures, insurance, debt, mutiny, and maritime claims.
- Navies convoyed commerce and later intercepted illegal traders.
- Churches and missionary bodies could own enslaved people, receive slave-derived income, defend or criticize the system, and circulate competing doctrines.

### 5.2 African political and commercial actors

Named rulers, states, and merchant formations varied across four centuries. Major actors included:

- rulers and officials of Dahomey;
- Oyo political and commercial authorities;
- Asante and Fante polities;
- Gold Coast caboceers and brokers;
- Efik merchant houses at Old Calabar;
- Bonny and New Calabar trading houses;
- Kongo and Ndongo authorities;
- Luso-African and Afro-Portuguese merchants;
- signares in Senegambia;
- caravan leaders, canoe operators, interpreters, creditors, and market women.

African actors were not a single collective. Some profited from captive export; some attempted to regulate or resist it; some were themselves captured; many political communities alternated among alliance, commercial dependence, warfare, and victimization.

### 5.3 Shipboard personnel

A slaving vessel commonly required more crew than an ordinary merchantman of comparable tonnage because it had to trade at multiple points, guard a large captive population, and manage disease and resistance. Roles included:

- master or captain;
- supercargo;
- first and second mates;
- surgeon;
- boatswain;
- carpenter;
- cooper;
- gunner;
- cook;
- sailmaker;
- ordinary and able seamen;
- boys and apprentices;
- interpreters or linguists;
- African pilots and canoe crews.

Captains possessed broad operational authority but remained bound by owners’ instructions, maritime law, insurance terms, and commercial accounting. Mortality among crews was high, particularly during extended African coastal residence.

### 5.4 Enslaved people as network actors

Enslaved people were the network’s forcibly transported subjects, its principal monetized “cargo” on slave voyages, and the labor force supporting much American commodity production. They also acted within and against the system through:

- negotiation and communication;
- preservation and adaptation of language, belief, foodways, and technical knowledge;
- care for other captives;
- shipboard insurrection;
- flight and maroonage;
- sabotage and work resistance;
- plantation conspiracy and revolt;
- military service;
- litigation and petition;
- purchasing or obtaining freedom;
- testimony to abolitionists, courts, and officials.

The People of the Atlantic Slave Trade databases preserve details for tens of thousands of named individuals, many derived from captured nineteenth-century vessels. Such names are invaluable but heavily skewed toward the suppression era and particular archival events. [People of the Atlantic Slave Trade](https://legacy.slavevoyages.org/past/database/african-origins)

## 6. Operation and Mechanism

### 6.1 Voyage formation

A merchant or partnership proceeded approximately as follows:

1. **Capitalization:** investors subscribed shares or supplied goods on credit.
2. **Vessel acquisition:** owners bought, chartered, or built a ship.
3. **Registration and clearance:** documents identified owner, master, tonnage, destination, and cargo, subject to evasion or falsification.
4. **Cargo assembly:** the supercargo’s assortment was tailored to anticipated African demand.
5. **Insurance:** hull, cargo, freight, and sometimes captive lives were insured under specified perils.
6. **Instructions:** owners prescribed trading regions, acceptable prices, destinations, commission rates, and remittance methods.
7. **Recruitment:** advance wages and coercive labor practices helped secure crews for an unusually dangerous voyage.
8. **African coast:** duties, gifts, negotiations, credit, and repeated small purchases filled the ship.
9. **Middle Passage:** provisions, confinement, discipline, medical practice, resistance, and mortality determined saleable arrivals.
10. **American sale:** agents sold survivors and collected cash, produce, or paper claims.
11. **Remittance or return cargo:** the ship might return with goods, sail in ballast, engage in another trade, or be sold.
12. **Final accounting:** owners deducted wages, commissions, insurance, port charges, leakage, bad debt, and losses before distributing profit—or recording a deficit.

### 6.2 Instruments

Typical instruments included:

- charter parties;
- bills of lading;
- manifests;
- invoices;
- insurance policies;
- bottomry and respondentia bonds;
- bills of exchange;
- promissory notes;
- powers of attorney;
- plantation mortgages;
- captain and supercargo instructions;
- factor accounts;
- muster rolls;
- customs bonds;
- registers and passes;
- notarial protests;
- sale accounts;
- prize-court decrees.

Not all transactions were literate or denominated in European money. Oral obligation, kinship, hostage-taking, pawnship, political guarantees, ceremonial exchange, and locally standardized commodity units were also essential.

### 6.3 Worked example: the brig *Sally*, 1764–1765

The *Sally* offers an unusually well-documented Rhode Island case because Brown University has digitized correspondence and accounts. [Voyage of the Sally](https://cds.library.brown.edu/projects/sally/story.html) [Brown digital collection](https://library.brown.edu/cds/slaveryandjustice/browse.php?class=date&date=1764&verb=retrieve)

#### Capital and owners

The Providence merchant brothers Nicholas, Joseph, John, and Moses Brown organized the voyage in 1764. The venture was entangled with the family’s wider commercial and industrial plans, including their iron interests and support for the College of Rhode Island.

#### Cargo

The vessel carried more than **17,274 gallons of New England rum**, the commodity for which Rhode Island slaving vessels were called “rum men” on the coast. It also carried other trading goods and provisions. The gallon figure is **documented in surviving Brown records**, not a system-wide estimate. [Brown digital collection](https://library.brown.edu/cds/slaveryandjustice/browse.php?class=date&date=1764&verb=retrieve)

#### Instructions and coastwise trade

Captain Esek Hopkins was instructed to purchase captives and protect their health because survival had direct commercial consequences. Like other vessels, the *Sally* did not exchange its entire cargo at one market. It traded along the African coast while correspondence and accounts recorded acquisition and mortality.

#### Embarkation and revolt

The vessel acquired 196 Africans according to Brown’s reconstruction. Captives attempted an uprising. Hopkins and the crew suppressed it; several captives were killed, wounded, or subsequently died. This was not exceptional in kind: shipboard resistance recurred throughout the trade, although surviving documentation is uneven and usually written by suppressors.

#### Middle Passage and mortality

Only 87 of the 196 captives survived to be sold—a mortality rate above 50 percent. The voyage was therefore a human catastrophe and a commercial failure. The figures are **documented for this voyage** and must not be generalized as the average mortality of the entire trade.

#### Sale and settlement

Survivors were sold in the Caribbean. Accounts then translated human sales into balances, commissions, and remittances. The owners lost money despite having insured the voyage. The family nevertheless continued to participate in slave-produced commodity commerce, ship supply, and distilling. Moses Brown later broke with slave trading and became an abolitionist; John Brown defended the commerce.

#### Why the example matters

The *Sally* demonstrates that:

- a recognizable rum–Africa–Caribbean circuit existed;
- the trade depended on detailed written instructions, credit, and insurance;
- captive resistance directly changed voyage outcomes;
- high mortality could eliminate profit;
- failure on one slave voyage did not sever a merchant house from the wider plantation economy;
- the triangle’s final leg could be a financial settlement rather than a hold full of molasses.

### 6.4 Worked legal failure: *Gregson v Gilbert* and the *Zong*

In 1781, crew members of the British slave ship *Zong* threw captive Africans overboard. The owners later sought insurance compensation, treating those killed as insured property lost through maritime necessity. The subsequent litigation was a civil insurance dispute, not a murder trial.

Royal Museums Greenwich holds a volume containing a court transcript, Granville Sharp’s letter of 2 July 1783 to the Admiralty, and an account of the killings. It identifies 132 deaths in the massacre; totals differ in the wider literature because different episodes and evidentiary reconstructions are counted. [Royal Museums Greenwich](https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/archive/rmgc-object-500960)

The ship’s log is not known to survive, and the first trial lacks a complete independent report. Therefore:

- **Documented:** people were deliberately thrown overboard; the owners made an insurance claim; King’s Bench ordered or considered a retrial on the insurance issues.
- **Disputed:** precise total killed and sequence of decisions.
- **Unsupported:** claims that the litigation itself convicted perpetrators of murder.
- **Systemic significance:** insurance doctrine, commodification, deficient navigation, water calculations, and evidentiary control converged in one case.

## 7. Change over the Lifespan

### Period I: Iberian precedents and Dutch disruption, before 1600–1640s

Portuguese traders established plantation and slave-trading connections among Atlantic islands, Africa, and Brazil. Spanish America relied on licensed and contraband deliveries under *asiento* arrangements. Dutch military-commercial expansion disrupted Iberian monopolies, occupied northeastern Brazil temporarily, and helped circulate capital, shipping expertise, credit, and sugar knowledge.

This was an Atlantic slave system, but not yet the mature British-French-Dutch Caribbean plantation complex usually depicted as “the triangle.”

### Period II: Sugar revolution and chartered expansion, c.1640s–1698

Barbados’s sugar transformation and English acquisition of Jamaica in 1655 expanded demand for forced labor and supplies. French Martinique and Guadeloupe developed parallel systems. Dutch merchants served multiple empires.

English Navigation Acts and the Royal African Company sought to bind shipping, African commerce, colonial demand, and English markets into a protected imperial system. The French 1685 ordinance regulated slavery, Catholic observance, punishment, sale, family status, and manumission in the Antilles. It was first published under the later collective title *Code noir* in the eighteenth century. [BnF](https://www.bnf.fr/fr/mediatheque/code-noir)

### Period III: Competitive private expansion, 1698–1740s

The erosion of the Royal African Company monopoly permitted greater British private participation. Bristol became prominent; Liverpool’s role grew. Newport merchants expanded their provisioning, rum, and African trades.

Imperial regulation also stimulated smuggling. The 1733 Molasses Act imposed duties on foreign colonial rum, molasses, and sugar entering British colonies—nine pence per gallon on foreign rum, six pence on molasses, and five shillings per hundredweight on sugar or paneles. Enforcement was incomplete, and French Caribbean molasses remained attractive to mainland distillers. [Molasses Act text](https://www.historycentral.com/documents/MOLASSES.html)

### Period IV: High-volume Atlantic system, c.1740s–1791

This was the classic period of Liverpool, Nantes, Bordeaux, Newport, Saint-Domingue, Jamaica, Bahia, Rio, Luanda, the Bights of Benin and Biafra, and West-Central African expansion.

Characteristics included:

- wider private investment;
- increasingly specialized vessels and outfitting;
- large plantation-capital and merchant-credit systems;
- imperial wars and privateering;
- expanded newspaper and insurance markets;
- refined systems of consignment and remittance;
- high captive embarkation volumes;
- major slave revolts, maroon wars, and growing abolitionist organization.

The British Dolben Act of 1788 regulated aspects of ship capacity. Parliamentary investigations collected plans, testimony, and quantitative evidence. Abolitionists converted commercial documentation into public campaigning material.

### Period V: Revolutionary fracture, 1791–1808

The Haitian Revolution began in 1791 and destroyed Saint-Domingue’s plantation-slavery regime. France abolished slavery in its colonies on 4 February 1794, restored it under Napoleon in 1802 where French power could enforce restoration, and permanently abolished it in 1848. [BnF Grégoire collection](https://www.bnf.fr/fr/fonds-abbe-gregoire-sur-lesclavage)

The revolution:

- removed or transformed the largest French plantation complex;
- displaced planters, merchants, skilled workers, and enslaved or free refugees;
- stimulated sugar and coffee expansion in Cuba, Brazil, and elsewhere;
- altered insurance and military risks;
- demonstrated that enslaved insurgents could destroy a major colonial regime;
- reshaped abolitionist and proslavery argument.

Britain prohibited participation in the transatlantic slave trade in 1807; the United States’ federal prohibition took effect in 1808. These measures ended neither slavery nor Atlantic trafficking.

### Period VI: Illegal expansion and multinational suppression, 1808–1850

After British and American prohibition:

- Portuguese/Brazilian and Spanish/Cuban trades expanded or persisted;
- merchants used neutral or fraudulent flags and papers;
- ships carried concealed equipment or fitted out after departure;
- captives were landed at remote locations;
- mixed-commission courts adjudicated captured vessels under treaties;
- the Royal Navy patrolled African, Atlantic, Brazilian, and Caribbean waters;
- freed or “liberated African” populations were settled or assigned under contested regimes in Sierra Leone, the Caribbean, Brazil, and elsewhere.

British sources report that the West Africa Squadron and associated operations seized nearly 1,400 slave ships and freed approximately 160,000 people between the early suppression voyages and 1870. These are **retrospective official estimates** and should not be mistaken for all illegal traffic prevented or all people re-enslaved after liberation. [UK Parliament debate](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld200607/ldhansrd/text/70607-0010.htm)

### Period VII: Contraction and reconfiguration, 1850–1866

Brazil’s 1850 Eusébio de Queirós law and stronger enforcement sharply curtailed the transatlantic trade to Brazil. Scholarship cited by the Library of Congress associates the decision not only with British pressure but also with fear of resistance, social instability, and epidemics linked to intensive late-1840s imports. [Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/item/hlas-bi97009382/)

Cuba remained a major illegal destination into the 1860s. SlaveVoyages uses 1866 as the final year of its estimated transatlantic series.

The network did not vanish. It transformed into:

- domestic slave trades;
- mortgage and commodity systems resting on already enslaved populations;
- post-emancipation indentured migration from India, China, Madeira, and elsewhere;
- free but coercively regulated plantation labor;
- continuing sugar, rum, finance, and shipping networks without lawful Atlantic slave imports.

## 8. End or Transformation

### 8.1 No single ending

| Date | Event | What ended—and what did not |
|---|---|---|
| 1772 | *Somerset* decision | Restricted forcible removal from England under the facts before the court; did not abolish colonial slavery |
| 1791–1804 | Haitian Revolution and Haitian independence | Destroyed slavery and French colonial sovereignty in Saint-Domingue; not the wider Atlantic system |
| 1794 | First French abolition | Abolished slavery in French colonies in law; implementation varied and Napoleon restored slavery in 1802 |
| 1807 | British Slave Trade Act | Prohibited British slave trading; did not emancipate enslaved people |
| 1808 | United States import prohibition effective | Prohibited foreign importation; domestic trade and illegal importation continued |
| 1815 | French commitment against trade after Napoleon | Suppression remained uneven |
| 1833/34 | British Slavery Abolition Act and implementation | Emancipation in most British colonies, followed by apprenticeship; compensation went to owners |
| 1848 | Permanent French colonial emancipation | Ended legal slavery in French colonies |
| 1848 | Danish West Indian emancipation | Ended slavery after revolt and official proclamation |
| 1850 | Brazil’s suppression law | Rapidly reduced Atlantic imports; slavery and internal trade continued |
| 1863 | Dutch emancipation | Ended legal slavery in Dutch colonies, with state-supervised labor transition in Suriname |
| 1865 | United States Thirteenth Amendment | Abolished slavery except as punishment for crime |
| 1866 | Last year in SlaveVoyages estimate series | Conventional end of transatlantic trafficking, not a universal institutional endpoint |
| 1873–86 | Puerto Rican and Cuban emancipation processes | Ended Spanish Caribbean slavery in stages |
| 1888 | Brazilian *Lei Áurea* | Ended legal slavery in Brazil |

Britain’s abolition bill received royal assent on 25 March 1807. Formal freedom for approximately 750,000 enslaved people in the British West Indies began on 1 August 1834, although compulsory “apprenticeship” delayed full freedom. [UK Parliament](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/legislativescrutiny/parliament-and-empire/parliament-and-the-american-colonies-before-1765/the-west-indian-colonies-and-emancipation/)

### 8.2 Proximate causes

Documented proximate forces included:

- statutory prohibition;
- revolts by enslaved people;
- Haitian military victory;
- abolitionist petitions and lobbying;
- consumer campaigns;
- parliamentary and revolutionary politics;
- treaty pressure;
- naval seizure;
- mixed-commission adjudication;
- Brazilian and Cuban domestic enforcement changes;
- changes in imperial sovereignty.

### 8.3 Structural causes

Competing scholarly explanations emphasize different combinations of:

- resistance by enslaved people;
- religious and humanitarian mobilization;
- metropolitan political culture;
- imperial rivalry and strategy;
- changing economic interests;
- industrial capitalism;
- sugar-market restructuring;
- declining or continuing plantation profitability;
- fiscal and administrative capacity;
- fear of revolution and demographic imbalance.

The older “declining economic interest caused abolition” thesis and the counterclaim that slave systems remained profitable are not mutually decisive at the level of individual jurisdictions. Profitability, political influence, war, and ideology changed at different rates. No monocausal account commands universal agreement.

### 8.4 Assets and personnel after abolition

- Slave ships shifted to lawful commerce, were sold, condemned, broken up, or seized.
- Forts became administrative stations, military posts, prisons, commercial factories, or ruins.
- Merchant capital moved into banking, industry, railways, real estate, plantations, and other shipping.
- Plantation owners received compensation in the British system; formerly enslaved people did not.
- Former slave-trade officers entered naval, colonial, banking, or commercial careers.
- Plantation production continued under apprenticeship, wage labor, tenancy, convict labor, and indenture.
- Ports, docks, warehouses, refineries, banks, family fortunes, place names, and collections retained material ties to the former network.

## 9. Failures, Fraud, Robbery, Disaster, Suppression, and Litigation

### 9.1 Commercial failure

A completed voyage could lose money because of:

- excessive acquisition time;
- crew or captive mortality;
- vessel loss;
- war capture;
- declining American prices;
- defective cargo selection;
- leakage or spoilage of rum and provisions;
- fraudulent agents;
- unpaid planter debts;
- unfavorable exchange rates;
- insurance exclusions.

The *Sally* is a documented example of catastrophic mortality and financial loss. It disproves any notion that every leg automatically yielded profit.

### 9.2 Smuggling and customs evasion

The mercantilist structure routinely generated illicit commerce:

- New England vessels bought French and Dutch molasses;
- Caribbean islands exchanged goods across imperial borders;
- Spanish American merchants obtained captives and manufactures through Curaçao, Jamaica, and other foreign depots;
- manifests undervalued cargo or concealed origins;
- bribes and political patronage obstructed enforcement.

Smuggling was not external to the network; it was one of its regular operating modes.

### 9.3 Privateering, piracy, and war

Atlantic wars redirected ships, raised insurance costs, created convoy systems, and converted merchantmen into prizes. Captured vessels and captive people could be condemned and sold by prize courts. European forts in Africa changed hands repeatedly. The distinction between state warfare, privateering, and piracy depended partly on commissions and the court recognizing them.

### 9.4 Revolt

Resistance occurred:

- before capture;
- during inland transport;
- in coastal confinement;
- aboard ships;
- during sale;
- on plantations;
- in maroon communities;
- through mass revolution.

Shipboard revolts could seize a vessel, force navigational changes, or end in mass killing. European records commonly minimize participants’ political objectives and name few of them.

### 9.5 Natural and maritime disaster

Hurricanes destroyed ships, ports, plantations, crops, and warehouses. The Port Royal earthquake of 1692 submerged much of the town and killed residents across legal and social categories. Archaeology now preserves part of the node that written commerce had created. [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1595/)

UNESCO-associated underwater research reports that perhaps nearly one thousand slave ships wrecked between Africa and the Americas, while only a small fraction have been located and studied. This is an **archaeological estimate with substantial uncertainty**. Senegalese surveys identified 24 underwater sites near Gorée and investigated wrecks including HMS *Sénégal*. [UNESCO Courier](https://courier.unesco.org/en/articles/deep-dive-transatlantic-slave-trade)

### 9.6 Suppression fraud

After prohibition, traders manipulated:

- national flags;
- ship ownership;
- destination papers;
- cargo descriptions;
- vessel fittings;
- coastal landing locations;
- crew testimony.

United States diplomatic records document vessels involved in Brazilian and African trafficking, British-Portuguese mixed commissions, and recurring use of American ships or the American flag. [US National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/research/african-americans/slavery-records-civil.html) [US National Archives, “Hydra”](https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/2000/winter/publications/prologue/2000/winter/hydra-slave-trade-documentation-1)

## 10. Contested and Legendary Aspects

### 10.1 The perfectly closed triangle

**Story:** Every slave ship sailed from Europe or New England to Africa, sold captives in the Caribbean, loaded sugar or molasses, and returned to its original port, earning profit on every leg.

**Assessment:** **Myth when asserted universally; documented for some voyages.** Bilateral routes, ballast returns, ship sales, inter-American redistribution, Brazilian-Angolan circuits, and financial remittance were common. One cited study of 195 eighteenth-century Dutch slave ships found that only 69 returned with substantial colonial cargo; 65 returned in ballast and 52 with little freight. That result should not automatically be projected onto other carriers, but it establishes that a slave voyage need not complete the textbook commodity triangle.

### 10.2 “Rum bought the slaves”

**Story:** New England rum was the principal commodity exchanged for all Atlantic captives.

**Assessment:** **Documented regionally, false globally.** Rum was the standard cargo on many Rhode Island voyages, including the *Sally*. Across the Atlantic system, however, textiles—many originating in Asia—metals, cowries, firearms, other alcohol, tobacco, and specie were also fundamental. In some African markets rum was marginal.

### 10.3 Profit on every leg

**Story:** Each leg produced a separate, guaranteed profit.

**Assessment:** **Commercial simplification.** Outward cargo could be sold at a margin; captives could yield another; a return cargo could yield a third. But voyage accounts also show losses, bad debt, unsold goods, mortality, ballast returns, insurance claims, and capital tied up for years.

### 10.4 The network as exclusively European

**Story:** Europeans entered African interiors, captured people, and directly managed the entire supply chain.

**Assessment:** **False as a general description.** European traders usually relied on African merchants and political authorities. Conversely, portraying Europeans as passive customers understates how Atlantic demand, imported weapons and goods, credit, forts, ships, and colonial labor demand altered political incentives and enlarged export capacity.

### 10.5 Gorée as the single or greatest “Door of No Return”

**Story:** Most or an enormous proportion of all Atlantic captives passed through the Maison des Esclaves on Gorée.

**Assessment:** **Memorial tradition inconsistent with aggregate voyage geography.** Gorée was a genuine Atlantic node, but its memorial architecture has been assigned a scale not supported by current voyage estimates. The building’s symbolic importance and the island’s documented trading history are real; the universal gateway claim is not.

### 10.6 Fort dungeons as the universal holding system

**Story:** Nearly all captives waited for embarkation in European castle dungeons.

**Assessment:** **Overgeneralization.** Fort confinement is documented at particular places. Many captives remained in African-controlled barracoons, towns, canoes, shore camps, or shipboard confinement. Some forts’ slave-trade function changed over time.

### 10.7 The “tenfold” price multiplication tale

**Story:** A person bought in Africa for goods worth a few pounds invariably sold for ten times that amount, after which sugar multiplied the profit again.

**Assessment:** **Didactic legend.** African trade goods carried transport and credit costs; prices varied by market, age, sex, health, and time; mortality and expenses were substantial; American sales frequently occurred on credit. Gross sale price is not net profit.

### 10.8 Treasure legends

Coastal forts, wrecks, and merchant houses attract stories of buried gold, coins, contraband, or unrecovered cargo. Some slave ships carried specie and valuable commodities, and wreck salvage occurred. No general evidentiary basis supports a hidden “triangular-trade treasure” tradition as a coherent historical phenomenon. Individual claims require archaeological provenance.

### 10.9 Conspiracy narratives

Claims that one ethnic, religious, banking, or secret group uniquely designed or controlled the Atlantic slave trade are **unsupported**. The documented network involved competing states, Christian denominations, African and European polities, chartered companies, private houses, colonial assemblies, smugglers, insurers, and consumers. Disproportionate participation by a particular port or merchant community at a specific time does not establish transhistorical secret control.

### 10.10 Romanticized merchant self-image

Merchants described themselves as adventurers, civilizers, patriots, providers of commerce, or bearers of Christianity. Abolitionist evidence and merchants’ own private accounts document confinement, mortality, commodification, punishment, and sale. The benevolent-trader narrative is therefore best classified as **self-justification**, although individual operators differed in practice and belief.

### 10.11 “Abolition ended the network overnight”

**Story:** The British Act of 1807 terminated the Atlantic system.

**Assessment:** **False.** It reorganized the system into prohibited and permitted sectors. Large illegal trades continued to Brazil and Cuba; British naval and diplomatic suppression became another Atlantic network; slavery and plantation commodity production continued.

## 11. Historiography and Representations

### 11.1 Major historiographic problems

#### Geometry versus network

Older textbooks favored a memorable triangle. Recent work stresses:

- multiple triangles;
- bilateral South Atlantic commerce;
- intercolonial shipping;
- Asian textiles and global commodity chains;
- financial circuits independent of ship routes;
- inland African networks;
- intra-American redistribution.

“Triangle” remains useful only when the exact circuit is specified.

#### Scale

Philip D. Curtin’s *The Atlantic Slave Trade: A Census* transformed quantitative study by lowering older speculative totals and insisting on route-based evidence. Subsequent work by David Eltis, David Richardson, Herbert Klein, Paul Lovejoy, Joseph Inikori, and the SlaveVoyages collaboration expanded and revised the dataset.

SlaveVoyages’ totals are estimates built from surviving records and statistical imputations. The 2019 expanded version contained 36,108 voyage entries and 274 variables; duplicate or misclassified voyages have been removed as research advances. [SlaveVoyages downloads](https://legacy.slavevoyages.org/blog/downloads-trans-atlantic)

#### Capitalism and industrialization

Debate continues over the degree to which slave trading and plantation profits financed British, French, Dutch, Portuguese, Brazilian, and North American industrialization. Strong claims include:

- direct profits supplied industrial capital;
- plantation markets stimulated shipping and manufacturing;
- Atlantic commodities enlarged taxation and consumption;
- slavery’s contribution was important but smaller than aggregate domestic capital formation;
- regional and sectoral effects were much larger than national averages.

The answer depends on whether one measures direct slave-voyage profit, plantation profit, merchant intermediation, customs revenue, downstream manufacturing, consumer demand, or institutional development.

#### African agency and demographic effects

Historians debate:

- the relationship between Atlantic demand and African warfare;
- long-term population loss;
- gender imbalance;
- political centralization and fragmentation;
- whether slave exports retarded development;
- the comparative role of firearms;
- how African commercial institutions adapted.

The archive is structurally uneven because many surviving sources were produced by European traders. African oral traditions, archaeology, linguistics, and local records are indispensable but do not eliminate uncertainty.

#### Mortality

Earlier narratives relied on spectacular cases or incomplete ship logs. Voyage-level databases permit route- and period-specific estimates. Still absent are complete counts of deaths:

- during capture;
- on inland marches;
- in coastal confinement;
- among crews;
- immediately after landing;
- during plantation “seasoning.”

### 11.2 Archives

Major repositories and record classes include:

- UK National Archives: Colonial Office, Admiralty, Treasury, customs, prize, and company records;
- Parliamentary Archives and historic debates;
- British Library and Bodleian Library;
- Bristol and Liverpool municipal and mercantile archives;
- Archives nationales and Archives nationales d’outre-mer, France;
- BnF and Gallica printed collections;
- departmental archives at Nantes, Bordeaux, La Rochelle, and other French ports;
- Nationaal Archief, Netherlands: WIC, Suriname, Berbice, Guinea Coast;
- Arquivo Nacional and Biblioteca Nacional, Brazil;
- Arquivo Histórico Ultramarino and Torre do Tombo, Portugal;
- Archivo General de Indias, Seville;
- Danish National Archives;
- Ghanaian, Senegalese, Beninese, Nigerian, Sierra Leonean, Angolan, and Mozambican national and regional collections;
- Caribbean national archives;
- United States National Archives, Library of Congress, Rhode Island Historical Society, and Brown University;
- Lloyd’s marine and insurance collections;
- notarial, probate, plantation, church, newspaper, and family archives.

Known losses include ship logs discarded after settlement, private papers destroyed or dispersed, archives lost through war, fire, humidity, insects, political upheaval, and deliberate concealment. Records of African inland movement and enslaved people’s own voices are especially incomplete.

### 11.3 Archaeology and material evidence

Important remains include:

- Ghanaian coastal castles and forts;
- Kunta Kinteh Island and Gambia fortifications;
- Ouidah-related sites;
- Gorée maritime landscape;
- Luanda and Benguela sites;
- Valongo Wharf in Rio de Janeiro;
- plantation mills, boiling houses, provision grounds, and cemeteries;
- Bridgetown and Port Royal;
- shipwrecks such as the *São José Paquete de Africa*;
- bottles, beads, firearms, shackles, ceramics, cowries, coins, and ballast;
- human remains analyzed with archaeological, isotopic, and genetic methods.

Archaeology can correct text-centered assumptions, but attributing a structure or artifact specifically to slave trafficking requires secure dating and context.

## 14. Findings

1. **The historical system was real; the single triangle was not its universal operating form.**
2. **Sugar was the principal plantation engine of the classic Caribbean network, but coffee, tobacco, rice, indigo, cotton, mining, and domestic service also generated demand.**
3. **Rum was important in Rhode Island and several African markets, but it was neither the sole nor universally dominant captive-purchase commodity.**
4. **The ship, commodity, credit, information, and ownership networks did not share identical routes.**
5. **African inland networks and political authorities were indispensable, while European and American demand, capital, shipping, and coercive capacity helped determine the system’s scale.**
6. **The network was legally plural: chartered monopoly, imperial regulation, licensed contract, private commerce, contraband, and illegal trafficking coexisted.**
7. **Resistance was an operational force throughout the system, not merely an external moral response at its end.**
8. **No single abolition date ended the network. It fragmented into illegal transatlantic traffic, domestic slave trades, suppression institutions, and post-emancipation labor systems.**
9. **Quantitative reconstruction is unusually advanced but remains incomplete and continuously revisable.**
10. **The most familiar public image—a neat sugar-rum-slave circuit earning profit at every corner—is best classified as a pedagogical compression constructed from real but nonrepresentative routes.**

### Source-critical note

Online access favors British, United States, French, Dutch, and UNESCO collections. Portuguese, Brazilian, Spanish, African, Danish, and Caribbean manuscript archives remain essential to a fully balanced reconstruction, but many are only partly digitized or searchable. Search-engine discoverability is not a measure of historical importance. Numerical claims above are therefore restricted to figures traceable to an identified database, institutional history, archive, or case study; where a comprehensive number cannot be established, none has been invented.
