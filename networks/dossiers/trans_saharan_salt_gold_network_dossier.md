# Trans-Saharan salt-for-gold trade: Network Dossier

## Scope note and evidentiary key

The “Trans-Saharan salt-for-gold trade” was not a chartered corporation, single road, or formally constituted organization. It is a modern analytical name for overlapping caravan, river, market, credit, political, religious, and information networks connecting Saharan salt deposits and Mediterranean markets with the gold-producing and gold-trading regions of the western Sudan.

This dossier uses a principal bounded period of **c. 700–1600 CE**:

- **c. 700** marks the emergence of securely documented, increasingly regular commerce between Islamic North Africa and the western Sudan. Earlier exchange is possible and in some cases probable, but its routes, scale, and commodities remain disputed.
- **1591–c. 1600** marks the Moroccan conquest of Songhay and fragmentation of the political system that had governed many central routes. It was a transformation, not the extinction of trans-Saharan commerce: salt caravans, slave trading, pilgrimage, scholarship, and regional exchange continued into the colonial and postcolonial periods.

Evidence labels used below:

- **Documented:** directly supported by contemporary or near-contemporary text, archaeology, or numismatics.
- **Probable inference:** the evidence supports the reconstruction but does not demonstrate it directly.
- **Disputed:** substantial scholarly disagreement exists.
- **Tradition/legend:** transmitted narrative without adequate contemporary corroboration.
- **Unresolved:** the available evidence does not permit a secure conclusion.

Arabic authors used racial-geographical terminology customary in their period, particularly **Bilād al-Sūdān**, literally “lands of the Blacks.” It denoted a broad belt south of the Sahara, not the modern Republic of Sudan. Historical quotations and titles retain such terminology where necessary; it is not adopted as the dossier’s analytical vocabulary.

## 1. Identification and Definition

### 1.1 Names

No medieval source gives the entire system a proper institutional name equivalent to “the Trans-Saharan salt-for-gold trade.”

Relevant expressions include:

- **English:** trans-Saharan trade; trans-Saharan caravan trade; western trans-Saharan trade; salt-and-gold trade.
- **French:** *commerce transsaharien*; *commerce de l’or et du sel*; *routes transsahariennes*.
- **Portuguese:** *comércio transaariano* or *comércio transsaariano*; *comércio do ouro*.
- **Spanish:** *comercio transahariano*; *rutas transaharianas*.
- **German:** *Transsaharahandel*; *Salzkarawanen*.
- **Arabic:** التجارة عبر الصحراء (*al-tijāra ʿabr al-ṣaḥrāʾ*, a modern descriptive expression); medieval writers more often described particular roads, merchants, kingdoms, and the **Bilād al-Sūdān** rather than naming a single system.
- **Tamasheq/Tuareg and regional usage:** *azalay/azalaï* and *taghlamt/taglem* refer to salt-caravan institutions in particular regions. Their indiscriminate application to the whole medieval system is anachronistic. The French word *azalaï* appears in colonial reporting in the early twentieth century; specialists dispute the common assumption that this French usage is simply an old Tuareg name for every Saharan caravan. [Encyclopédie berbère](https://journals.openedition.org/encyclopedieberbere/pdf/192)

### 1.2 What the network was

It was a layered system comprising:

1. Gold-producing districts in the upper Senegal, upper Niger, and forest–savanna transition.
2. African merchant communities—especially groups described collectively as Wangara or Juula/Dyula—moving gold, kola, cloth, and information through regional markets.
3. Sahelian kingdoms and empires that taxed, protected, redirected, or monopolized portions of commerce.
4. Desert-edge entrepôts where savanna, Saharan, and North African merchants transacted.
5. Camel routes structured by wells, pastures, guides, salt mines, and politically negotiated protection.
6. North African markets, mints, workshops, and credit communities.
7. Mediterranean and Islamic monetary systems that generated demand for West African gold.
8. Islamic institutions—mosques, judges, jurists, schools, pilgrimage routes, written contracts, Arabic literacy, and merchant diasporas—that reduced some transaction costs while never eliminating fraud or violence.

“Salt for gold” is therefore a useful shorthand but a misleading literal description. It suggests two principals meeting at one border and swapping equal loads. In practice there were multiple sales, advances, taxes, conversions of currency, river transfers, and intermediate merchants. Salt and gold often traveled in opposite directions, but they were accompanied by copper, textiles, beads, dates, horses, books, paper, cowries, ivory, kola nuts, leather, ostrich feathers, captives, free migrants, pilgrims, scholars, letters, and political intelligence.

### 1.3 Media and infrastructure

The network operated through:

- Dromedary-camel caravans.
- Donkeys and human porters south of the desert.
- Canoes and boats on the Niger and Senegal river systems.
- Wells, cisterns, oases, pasture zones, and cached water.
- Rock-salt mines and mining settlements.
- Periodic markets and permanent towns.
- Gold dust, nuggets, unstamped gold, dinars, mithqāl weights, cowries, copper, salt pieces, and cloth as monetary instruments.
- Oral guarantees, kinship, ethnicity, reputation, partnership contracts, written letters, Islamic commercial law, and judicial enforcement.
- Armed escorts, payments to pastoral and nomadic groups, and state garrisons.

### 1.4 Geographic extent

At its medieval maximum the western and central network extended:

- North to Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya, Egypt, al-Andalus, and Mediterranean markets.
- Across the western Sahara through Sijilmasa, the Drāʿa and Tāfīlālt, Wādān, Awdaghost, Taghaza, Walata, Tuwat, Ghadames, Ghat, Tadmekka, Agadez, Kawar, Bilma, and Zawila.
- South to the Senegal and Niger basins, Ghana/Wagadu, Mali, Songhay, Takrur, Gao, Timbuktu, Djenné, and Hausa regions.
- Indirectly into Bambuk, Bure, Galam, Sirba, Lobi, and later Akan gold zones.
- Eastward through pilgrimage and scholarly routes to Cairo, the Hijaz, and the wider Islamic world.

The present dossier concentrates on western and west-central routes whose gold and salt traffic served Ghana, Mali, and Songhay. The Kanem–Bornu–Fezzan corridor was connected but was not primarily part of the same salt-for-gold axis.

### 1.5 Boundaries of inclusion

Included are:

- Production, aggregation, transport, sale, taxation, and monetary conversion of salt and gold.
- Goods and people carried by the same infrastructure.
- Religious, documentary, and political systems that enabled or exploited it.
- Atlantic commerce where it competed or interconnected with the overland system.

Excluded except as background are:

- Roman-period Saharan exchanges that cannot securely be connected to the medieval system.
- Indian Ocean gold networks with no demonstrated western-Sudanese connection.
- Modern tourist representations of caravan routes.
- Nineteenth-century caravan finance except as evidence of institutional afterlife.

## 2. Formation

### 2.1 Predecessors

**Documented:** North Africa and the Sahara possessed exchange networks before the Islamic conquests. The Garamantes, oasis societies, pastoral groups, and Mediterranean settlements moved people and commodities across desert margins.

**Disputed:** whether substantial quantities of West African gold crossed the Sahara in Roman or Byzantine times. David Phillipson has suggested that Carthage’s unusual “globular” Byzantine solidi may reflect an emerging trans-Saharan gold supply. He explicitly presents the connection as a hypothesis; archaeological sourcing of the gold remains inconclusive. [Phillipson](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquaries-journal/article/transsaharan-gold-trade-and-byzantine-coinage/1465467520EE42288DCD1CAC7BBFA192)

The camel was indispensable to the later network, but the old textbook formula—“camel introduced, trade began”—is too deterministic. Camel transport, oasis settlement, political security, merchant capital, gold demand, and the growth of Islamic markets operated together.

### 2.2 Islamic-period acceleration

From the late seventh and especially the eighth century, several conditions aligned:

- Muslim conquest integrated North Africa into a monetary world with strong demand for gold.
- Towns such as Sijilmasa became desert-edge exchange and minting centers.
- Ibadi and other Muslim merchants developed communities linking North African oases to western-Sudanese markets.
- Arabic literacy provided correspondence, accounts, contracts, religious credentials, and a common language for some long-distance dealings.
- Political authorities could tax concentrated caravan traffic.
- Dromedaries enabled high-value cargo and provisions to cross waterless stages.

Sijilmasa, founded in **757 CE** according to the conventional chronology, became the most important northern terminus of the western route from approximately the ninth through fourteenth centuries. Excavations and coin finds support its role as a mint and commercial city, although the precise volume of its trans-Saharan traffic is unknown. A hoard found at Aqaba included 29 Sijilmasa-minted coins among 32 gold coins, demonstrating the wide circulation of its dinars but not proving where the metal in each coin originated. [Messier](https://www.middleeastmedievalists.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/UW-5-1-1993.pdf)

### 2.3 Early merchant institutions

Ibadi merchants from North African centers such as Tahert, Wargla, and Sijilmasa appear prominently in early connections. Claims that they “monopolized” all trans-Saharan commerce should be treated cautiously: the textual record privileges literate Muslim networks and may obscure non-Ibadi, Jewish, Berber, Soninke, and other participants. [Van Riel](https://fount.aucegypt.edu/etds/876/)

A fatwa by the Tunisian jurist al-Qābisī, who died in 1012, discusses **qirāḍ** investment partnerships for trade to the Bilād al-Sūdān. He accepted some documentary practice but worried that enforcement would be unreliable outside Islamic jurisdiction. This is direct evidence that merchants financed Sudan-bound trade through investment contracts and written instruments by the late tenth or early eleventh century; it does not establish that bills of exchange universally replaced cash. [Lydon](https://doi.org/10.4000/etudesafricaines.27569)

### 2.4 Formation of Ghana’s commercial position

Arabic authors from the eighth and ninth centuries knew Ghana as a powerful “land of gold.” By al-Bakri’s compilation of **1067–68**, Ghana stood between Saharan termini and southern gold networks. The relationship between commerce and state formation is probable but cannot be reduced to a single founding event. Ghana did not necessarily own the goldfields. Its advantage lay in controlling or taxing aggregation routes and markets while keeping northern merchants at a distance from producing zones.

The identification of medieval Ghana’s capital with **Koumbi Saleh** remains widely used but is not conclusively proven. Archaeology establishes a large medieval settlement there; the match to al-Bakri’s paired royal and Muslim towns is plausible rather than certain.

## 3. Constituent Nodes

### 3.1 Northern hubs

#### Sijilmasa, Morocco

- **Role:** principal northern head of the western route; market, oasis, mint, redistribution center.
- **Involvement:** founded conventionally in 757; commercial prominence from the ninth century; episodic political disruption and physical contraction after the fourteenth century.
- **Connections:** north to Fez and Mediterranean networks; south through the Tāfīlālt, Taghaza or western Mauritanian corridors, and Awdaghost/Ghana.
- **Evidence:** chronicles, geographies, gold coins, settlement archaeology and irrigation remains.
- **Survival:** extensive ruins near modern Rissani.
- **Qualification:** being a major mint does not prove that every Sijilmasa dinar used West African gold.

#### Tahert, Wargla/Sedrata, and Tlemcen

These Algerian centers supported merchant and religious networks, particularly Ibadi communities. Sedrata’s archaeology demonstrates oasis urbanism and far-reaching exchange. Their relative importance changed with dynastic politics and route security.

#### Tuwat/Gurara

A chain of oases in present Algeria that became increasingly important in the later medieval period. It connected the central Niger bend and Taghaza with North Africa. Control of Tuwat and its approaches mattered to Moroccan policy in the sixteenth century.

#### Ghadames, Ghat, and Zawila

Eastern Saharan nodes linking the Fezzan to the central Sudan. They carried captives, textiles, horses, dates, and other goods. They belonged to the wider trans-Saharan network but were less directly integrated with Bambuk–Bure gold than Sijilmasa’s western routes.

### 3.2 Saharan mines and stations

#### Awdaghost / Tegdaoust, Mauritania

- **Status:** securely attested in Arabic texts; Tegdaoust is the probable archaeological identification.
- **Role:** southern desert-edge market, provisioning center, and intermediary between Sijilmasa and Ghana.
- **Peak:** ninth–eleventh centuries.
- **Population:** Muslim merchants and locally rooted inhabitants; al-Bakri describes wealth, gardens, markets, and houses.
- **Political history:** associated at different times with Sanhaja groups and Ghanaian power; an Almoravid attack or seizure in 1054–55 is reported.
- **Unresolved:** whether this event materially “destroyed Ghana” is not supported. Awdaghost and Ghana must not be conflated.

#### Tadmekka / Essouk, Mali

- **Role:** early Islamic trading town on routes between North Africa and Gao.
- **Evidence:** excavation, imported glass and copper, local production, inscriptions, and remains interpreted as molds used to process very pure gold.
- **Chronology:** major occupation from roughly the eighth to fourteenth centuries, with changing intensity.
- **Importance:** archaeology provides rare material evidence for gold processing at a Saharan/Sahelian entrepôt rather than merely a literary report. [Nixon dissertation](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1444469/) [Essouk metallurgical study](https://brill.com/display/book/edcoll/9789004348998/B9789004348998_037.pdf)

#### Taghaza, northern Mali

- **Role:** salt-mining settlement and caravan junction.
- **Documented in 1352:** Ibn Battuta found buildings and a mosque made with salt slabs and camel skins, brackish water, imported food, and miners or laborers present at the site.
- **Labor:** his account says enslaved people of the Massufa worked the salt. This is direct evidence of coerced labor in the network.
- **Political history:** contested between Songhay and Morocco; abandoned or displaced after Moroccan pressure in the late sixteenth century.
- **Survival:** archaeological remains and mine workings, though dating individual structures is difficult.

#### Taoudenni

Taoudenni ultimately replaced Taghaza as the dominant salt source feeding Timbuktu. Its main prominence belongs to the post-medieval continuation of the system; the date and exact mechanism of the shift from Taghaza remain imperfectly established.

#### Ijil, Mauritania

A major western Saharan salt source associated with routes toward Wādān and the Senegal valley. It is sometimes neglected in narratives centered on Taghaza.

#### Bilma and Fachi, Niger

Salt and natron centers serving Agadez, Hausa markets, and the Lake Chad zone. Their caravans formed a connected eastern branch, later called *taghlamt* in regional usage.

### 3.3 Sahelian termini and markets

#### Ghana / Wagadu and Koumbi Saleh

- **Role:** royal center and market complex near the southern end of western routes.
- **Al-Bakri’s description:** two settlements approximately six miles apart—one predominantly Muslim with mosques and scholars, the other containing the king’s palace and sacred grove.
- **Taxation:** one gold dinar reportedly levied on each donkey-load of salt entering, two dinars when it left, with other rates for copper and merchandise.
- **Reliability:** al-Bakri wrote in al-Andalus and did not visit Ghana; his compilation used earlier merchants’ and geographers’ reports. The tax schedule is valuable but not independently audited.
- **Gold regime:** al-Bakri says the king reserved nuggets and allowed dust to circulate. Whether this was a permanent monopoly, a convention, or a literary simplification is unresolved.

#### Takrur

Located in the Senegal valley, Takrur became an early Muslim-ruled polity and an important western terminus. Its name was sometimes generalized by later writers to cover a larger West African region, complicating geographic identification.

#### Walata / Iwalatan

- **Role:** Mali’s northern reception point for caravans from Taghaza and Sijilmasa.
- **Documented operations:** merchants received advance letters from caravans, sent water parties into the desert, and arranged lodging.
- **Peak:** especially thirteenth–fourteenth centuries; later overshadowed by Timbuktu but never simply vanished.

#### Timbuktu

- **Foundation:** conventionally around the eleventh or early twelfth century; foundation traditions concerning a woman named Buktu are late and not securely verifiable.
- **Role:** interface between camel traffic and Niger river transport; market for salt, gold, grain, books, captives, and manufactured goods.
- **Peak:** fourteenth–sixteenth centuries under Mali and Songhay.
- **Institutions:** mosques, scholarly households, judges, schools, manuscript workshops, merchant lodgings, and markets.
- **Book trade:** al-Hasan al-Wazzan, or Leo Africanus, reported in the early sixteenth century that books imported from North Africa yielded especially high profits. His published *Description of Africa* was composed after captivity and conversion in Rome; its wording and editorial transmission require caution. [University of Cape Town](https://tombouctoumanuscripts.uct.ac.za/overview/timbuktus-history)

#### Gao and Gao-Saney

- **Role:** royal and commercial centers on the Niger, linked to Tadmekka and central Saharan routes.
- **Archaeology:** Gao-Saney shows imported glass, copper, and other materials from the first millennium CE. These finds securely demonstrate long-distance connections but do not by themselves identify each route or prove that gold paid for every import.
- **Peak:** Gao became the political core of Songhay and remained a major market through the sixteenth century. [ICESCO](https://iwh.icesco.org/gao-saney-site/)

#### Djenné and Djenné-jeno

Djenné connected Niger-bend caravan termini to the Inland Niger Delta and southern production zones. Djenné-jeno’s indigenous urban development began before intensive Islamic trade, disproving older claims that all West African urbanism was imported across the Sahara. The later Muslim town of Djenné became an important commercial and scholarly center.

### 3.4 Producing and aggregation zones

#### Bambuk

An auriferous region between the Senegal and Falémé rivers. Medieval sources knew gold from a western producing zone, but exact mine locations, output, and political control are poorly documented. Bambuk’s identification is secure at the regional level, not for every medieval working.

#### Bure

Upper Niger goldfield, increasingly important to Mali. The state could influence routes and tribute without necessarily managing individual diggings.

#### Galam and Sirba

Gold-bearing and trading regions mentioned in later geographic reconstructions. Their precise contribution in each medieval century remains uncertain.

#### Akan fields

Akan gold became increasingly important in the later medieval and early modern period. Portuguese coastal trade after the 1470s supplemented rather than instantaneously eliminated northern routes. [Smith and Boscariol](https://openurl.ebsco.com/contentitem/doi%3A10.1353/jwh.2022.0038?id=ebsco%3Adoi%3A10.1353%2Fjwh.2022.0038&sid=ebsco%3Aplink%3Acrawler)

## 4. Connections

### 4.1 Sijilmasa–Awdaghost–Ghana

This was the classic western corridor in ninth–eleventh-century Arabic geography. It was not necessarily a single visible road. Tracks shifted with water, pasturage, security, season, and political relations.

Traffic southward included salt, copper, cloth, beads, dates, manufactured goods, and possibly cowries. Northward traffic included gold, captives, ivory, hides, and ostrich products.

Reported travel times vary because “days” are itinerary units, not surveyed distances. Al-Bakri’s and related route descriptions suggest journeys lasting approximately two months between major termini, but no universal schedule existed.

### 4.2 Sijilmasa–Taghaza–Walata–Mali

Ibn Battuta’s 1352 journey provides the best operational narrative:

1. A caravan assembled at Sijilmasa.
2. It traveled approximately 25 days to Taghaza.
3. It rested there, acquired salt and water, and prepared for a ten-day waterless stage.
4. At Tasarahla, travelers repaired and covered waterskins.
5. A hired Massufa courier, the **takshīf**, went ahead carrying letters.
6. Walata merchants or friends came four days into the desert with water.
7. At Walata the caravan entered Mali’s commercial and political jurisdiction.

Ibn Battuta reports that one salt load sold for **8–10 mithqāls** of gold at Walata, **20–30** in the Mali capital, and sometimes **40**. This is a contemporary traveler’s price observation, not a continuous market series. Load size, salt quality, season, and the exact dinar/mithqāl equivalence are not specified. [Ibn Battuta translation](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/1354-ibnbattuta.asp)

### 4.3 Tadmekka–Gao

This central corridor connected Saharan merchants to the Niger bend. Archaeological imports and gold-processing remains at Tadmekka, combined with early Arabic descriptions, make the connection secure. Its precise traffic frequency and toll regime are unknown.

### 4.4 Taghaza–Timbuktu–Djenné

Salt was carried south to Timbuktu, then redistributed by river and overland toward the Inland Niger Delta and farther south. Grain and other provisions moved north because the mining settlements could not feed themselves. Gold and other high-value products moved toward Timbuktu and the desert.

### 4.5 Niger river connections

The Niger was not merely a terminus. Canoes transferred bulky salt and grain more cheaply than overland transport and linked Timbuktu, Gao, and Djenné with numerous smaller settlements. Ibn Battuta himself traveled by water during part of his visit.

### 4.6 Goldfields to imperial markets

Gold moved through several hands:

- Miners or mining communities recovered dust, grains, and nuggets.
- Local traders exchanged food, cloth, salt, tools, or other goods for gold.
- Wangara/Juula merchants aggregated it and moved it toward savanna markets.
- State agents taxed traffic or demanded tribute.
- Saharan and North African merchants acquired gold at desert-edge or Sahelian markets.
- North African dealers delivered it to mints, jewelers, courts, or Mediterranean exporters.

The frequent claim that Muslim traders were deliberately prohibited from seeing the mines is plausible and appears in Arabic narratives, but it should not be converted into a universal, perfectly enforced embargo.

### 4.7 Pilgrimage and intellectual connections

West African pilgrims followed commercial infrastructure toward North Africa, Cairo, and Mecca. They carried gold, letters, gifts, servants, and captives; they returned with books, legal ideas, teachers, architectural knowledge, and political contacts.

Mansa Musa’s pilgrimage in **1324–25** was an exceptional royal expedition, not a normal caravan. Al-Umari, writing from interviews in Cairo rather than as an eyewitness to Mali, records that Musa distributed and spent enough gold to affect Cairo’s gold market. The direction of the effect—gold’s value declining relative to silver—is credible. Exact entourage and gold totals differ radically among sources and cannot be treated as audited statistics. The original Arabic work is available in [Arabic Collections Online](https://sites.dlib.nyu.edu/viewer/books/columbia_aco004015/1).

### 4.8 Route maps as reconstructions

Modern maps often draw clean lines between cities. These are historiographic abstractions assembled from itinerary texts, environmental constraints, archaeology, and later routes. They do not represent paved roads, permanently surveyed alignments, or continuous state control. Raymond Mauny’s influential route maps remain useful syntheses but may project better-documented later alignments backward.

## 5. Agents and Operators

### 5.1 Producers

Gold was commonly extracted from alluvial deposits by washing and from relatively shallow workings. Mining labor included free households, dependents, occupational communities, and probably enslaved labor, but the balance cannot be quantified for the medieval period. Accounts that assign all mining to a single ethnic group are oversimplifications.

Salt at Taghaza was cut into slabs. Ibn Battuta explicitly associates its extraction with enslaved Massufa laborers. Food had to be imported, making miners vulnerable to supply interruption.

### 5.2 Merchant communities

#### Wangara / Juula

“Wangara” appears in Arabic and African traditions as a collective designation for Mande-linked Muslim gold merchants and diasporas. It should not be assumed to name one centralized guild. Juula communities later established commercial and religious settlements across the savanna.

Their advantages included:

- Knowledge of gold-producing and consuming markets.
- Multilingual capability.
- Kinship and host relationships.
- Islamic literacy and legal conventions.
- Capacity to move between Muslim and non-Muslim political environments.

#### Sanhaja and Massufa

Saharan Berber-speaking groups supplied guides, camels, escorts, labor, messengers, and political control over desert stages. The Massufa are especially visible in Ibn Battuta’s account at Taghaza, Walata, and on the desert crossing.

#### North African merchant-financiers

Merchants in Sijilmasa and other towns assembled investment capital, bought animals and cargo, employed agents, and converted gold into coin or other goods. Their ventures could use **qirāḍ**, in which an investor supplied capital and an agent traded with it for an agreed profit share.

#### Jewish merchants

Jewish merchants participated in North African and Saharan commerce, but the medieval evidence for their precise share in the western salt–gold routes is limited. Later Jewish merchant communities in Tuwat and Moroccan cities are better documented. Claims of a hidden Jewish monopoly are unsupported.

### 5.3 States and rulers

#### Ghana rulers

They taxed trade, received tribute, maintained courts, and controlled access to the royal center. Al-Bakri’s claim that the king monopolized nuggets would have limited private accumulation of large pieces while leaving dust marketable.

#### Mali rulers

Mali integrated gold-producing approaches, Sahelian markets, and portions of desert traffic. Mansa Musa used the hajj to make Mali legible to the wider Islamic world. Mali’s control was not uniform territorial administration in the modern sense; tributary relations, provincial governors, merchant communities, and negotiated allegiance overlapped.

#### Songhay rulers

Sonni Ali and the Askia dynasty controlled the Niger bend, Timbuktu, Gao, and major traffic corridors. Askia Muhammad’s pilgrimage and consultation with North African scholars strengthened Islamic claims to political legitimacy. Songhay derived revenue from markets, river traffic, agriculture, tribute, and captives as well as gold and salt.

#### Moroccan Saadians

The Saadian state claimed Taghaza as a taxable mine and sought revenue from Sudanese commerce. A surviving diplomatic letter asserts that mine revenue belonged to the Muslim treasury and proposes a tax of one mithqāl per camel. This was a political-legal claim, not proof of uncontested possession. [Hunwick, translated documents](https://modules.oc4d.org/BookBridge/books/Ghana%20History/Timbuktu-and-the-Songhay-Empire-Al-Sa-Dis-Ta-Rikh-Al-Sudan-John-O-Hunwick-2003-Brill-Academic-Publishers.pdf)

### 5.4 Guides, couriers, hosts, and carriers

Desert guides possessed specialized knowledge of stars, landforms, wells, wind-altered dunes, and seasonal pasture. Ibn Battuta remarked that his guide, though visually impaired, knew the route best.

The **takshīf** courier carried advance letters from a caravan to Walata. His failure could leave merchants without a water rendezvous. This joined information transmission directly to physical survival.

Hosts and commercial correspondents arranged lodging, storage, introductions, and market access. These functions are more securely documented than formal “caravanserai chains” of the kind known in some eastern Islamic regions.

### 5.5 Scholars and judges

Qadis, jurists, imams, teachers, scribes, and witnesses made contracts, authenticated transfers, resolved disputes, and connected merchant communities. Islamic law supplied a portable vocabulary of partnership, debt, agency, inheritance, and testimony, but actual enforcement depended on local power and reputation.

### 5.6 Enslaved people

Enslavement was integral, not incidental:

- Captives were exported north across the Sahara.
- Enslaved people worked salt deposits and domestic establishments.
- Women and children were traded as well as men.
- Ibn Battuta returned north with a caravan associated with a trader carrying approximately **600 enslaved women**. This is a report about one convoy, not an annual volume.
- Rulers gave enslaved people as gifts and incorporated them into courts and armies.
- Raiding, warfare, punishment, tribute, and purchase supplied captives.

No defensible total exists for medieval trans-Saharan slave exports. Later figures cannot safely be projected backward.

### 5.7 Named figures

- **Al-Yaʿqubi** (writing 872–73): early geographical evidence for Saharan routes and Sudanese kingdoms.
- **Al-Masʿudi** (tenth century): described Ghana and gold flows.
- **Al-Bakri** (d. 1094): compiler of the most detailed eleventh-century account; never visited West Africa.
- **Mansa Musa** (r. conventionally c. 1312–37): Mali ruler whose 1324–25 pilgrimage advertised the empire’s gold resources.
- **Al-Umari** (1301–49): Mamluk administrator and geographer who interviewed people familiar with Musa’s visit.
- **Ibn Battuta** (1304–c. 1368/69): traveled from Morocco through Taghaza and Walata to Mali in 1352–53.
- **Sonni Ali** (r. 1464–92): expanded Songhay power, took Timbuktu and Djenné, and controlled river and caravan nodes.
- **Askia Muhammad I** (r. 1493–1528): reorganized Songhay government and cultivated Islamic connections.
- **Ahmad al-Mansur** (r. Morocco 1578–1603): authorized the invasion of Songhay.
- **Judar Pasha:** commander of the Moroccan expedition that crossed the Sahara and defeated Songhay forces in 1591.
- **Al-Saʿdi** (1594–1655/56): author of *Taʾrīkh al-Sūdān*, an essential but later source for Songhay and the conquest.
- **Al-Hasan al-Wazzan/Leo Africanus** (c. 1494–after 1550): traveler and author whose descriptions shaped European images of Timbuktu.

## 6. Operation and Mechanism

### 6.1 Financing a caravan venture

A probable, source-supported sequence was:

1. Investors pooled coin, bullion, or merchandise.
2. A managing merchant received capital under partnership or agency terms.
3. Textiles, copper, beads, paper, dates, and other goods were bought in northern markets.
4. Camels, handlers, guides, waterskins, provisions, and protection were hired.
5. A caravan waited for a safe season and enough companions to distribute risk.
6. Cargo was sold or exchanged at intermediate markets; no merchant necessarily traveled from Mediterranean port to gold mine.
7. Proceeds were returned in gold, captives, ivory, leather, or other goods.
8. Profit was divided, debts settled, and gold sold to mint suppliers or jewelers.

Documented legal texts show written partnership and remittance practices, but medieval account books from the western routes survive only exceptionally. A universal transaction formula cannot be established.

### 6.2 Seasonality and provisioning

Caravans balanced:

- Heat and cold.
- Availability of grazing.
- Condition of wells.
- Risk of rain or sandstorm.
- Agricultural market cycles.
- Political security.

Camels carried commercial cargo plus water, fodder, food, tents, and equipment. Published “standard camel load” numbers vary by breed, terrain, and distance; applying one fixed kilogram value to medieval loads would create false precision.

### 6.3 Salt production and packing

At Taghaza, rock salt was cut into rectangular slabs suitable for camel transport and later fragmentation. Some salt was consumed; some served as a medium of exchange. A “load” was not a universally standardized monetary unit.

The statement that salt was literally “worth its weight in gold” everywhere is **false as a general rule**. Ibn Battuta’s price gradient demonstrates increasing value inland, but even his highest quotation does not establish weight-for-weight equivalence.

### 6.4 Gold measurement and conversion

Gold circulated as:

- Dust.
- Grains and small nuggets.
- Larger nuggets subject, according to al-Bakri, to royal reservation.
- Unstamped gold pieces.
- Minted dinars after reaching northern monetary systems.

At Tadmekka, al-Bakri said “bald” dinars were pure, unstamped gold. Archaeological molds have been interpreted as evidence for processing gold into standardized blanks. This interpretation is strong but not beyond dispute.

The **mithqāl** was a weight and monetary accounting unit, but regional standards varied. Converting medieval statements to modern kilograms or dollars without an explicitly identified standard is unreliable.

### 6.5 Taxes and political payments

Revenue took several forms:

- Import and export duties.
- Market fees.
- Transit tolls.
- Tribute.
- Protection payments.
- Royal monopolies or pre-emption rights.
- Taxes per animal or load.
- Gifts to officials and rulers.

Al-Bakri’s Ghana rates are the most famous schedule: one dinar on an incoming donkey-load of salt and two on an outgoing load. The unusual outgoing levy may indicate redistribution, differentiated jurisdiction, or textual ambiguity. It cannot be extrapolated to all periods.

### 6.6 Security

Security was produced through a mixture of:

- Large-group travel.
- Armed guards.
- Guides and negotiated passage.
- State patrols near core zones.
- Payments to desert groups.
- Merchant reputation and local hosts.

Threats included robbery, extortion, war, animal loss, thirst, getting separated, spoiled provisions, market-price changes, and political confiscation. Ibn Battuta personally recorded dead and missing travelers near a water source.

### 6.7 Communications

The takshīf system demonstrates an operational communications chain:

`caravan traveler → written letter → hired Massufa courier → Walata correspondent → outbound water party and lodging arrangements`

Information moving through the network included prices, road conditions, political changes, deaths, creditworthiness, religious rulings, caravan departure dates, and intelligence about gold availability.

### 6.8 Worked case: Ibn Battuta’s 1352 salt-road journey

This is the best documented single passage.

#### Assembly

Ibn Battuta joined a caravan departing Sijilmasa at the beginning of Muharram 753 AH, corresponding to February 1352. Its leader was Abu Muhammad Yandakan al-Massufi.

#### Sijilmasa to Taghaza

The party crossed approximately 25 days to Taghaza. The caravan’s survival depended on collective travel, known wells, guides, and carried water.

#### Taghaza

Ibn Battuta stayed ten days. He reported:

- No trees.
- Salt-slab buildings roofed with camel hides.
- Brackish water.
- Flies and lice.
- Food imported from the Maghrib and Sudan.
- Enslaved Massufa laborers.
- Sudanese traders collecting salt.
- Large business expressed in “hundredweights” of gold dust.

“Hundredweights” is the English translator’s rendering of a qualitative magnitude, not a recoverable audited tonnage.

#### Waterless crossing

Travelers loaded water for a ten-night stage. Ibn Battuta described a traveler found dead only about a mile from water, showing that route knowledge and group cohesion mattered as much as nominal distance.

#### Tasarahla

The caravan rested three days, repaired waterskins, filled them, and sewed sackcloth covers around them to reduce damage from wind.

#### Dispatch of the takshīf

A Massufa courier carried travelers’ letters to Walata. Merchants without personal friends could write to a reputable commercial host. Recipients came four nights outward with water.

#### Walata market

Salt loads sold for 8–10 mithqāls. Farther inside Mali, Ibn Battuta says the price rose to 20–30 and occasionally 40.

#### Evidentiary limits

The narrative was dictated after Ibn Battuta’s travels and shaped into literary form by Ibn Juzayy. Supernatural explanations involving demons belong to the narrator’s worldview; the underlying operational observations—wind-erased tracks, courier risk, water rendezvous, and death by disorientation—are independently plausible. [English text](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/1354-ibnbattuta.asp) [French text](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Voyages_%28Ibn_Battuta%2C_Sanguinetti%29/Le_voyage_au_Soudan)

## 7. What the Network Carried

### 7.1 Northbound

- Gold dust, grains, nuggets, and processed blanks.
- Enslaved women, men, and children.
- Ivory.
- Ostrich feathers and possibly eggs.
- Hides and leather goods.
- Kola nuts.
- Ebony and other woods.
- Pepper and grains of paradise in later commerce.
- Agricultural products over shorter segments.
- Political intelligence and information about markets.
- Pilgrims and students.

### 7.2 Southbound

- Rock salt.
- Copper and copper alloys.
- Textiles and clothing.
- Horses.
- Dates and other oasis products.
- Glass beads and vessels.
- Cowries ultimately originating in the Indian Ocean.
- Silver and coins, though many coins were valued as bullion.
- Paper, books, ink, and writing materials.
- Ceramics and luxury goods.
- Religious specialists, artisans, teachers, and migrants.

### 7.3 Horizontal and internal traffic

The system also moved goods between West African regions:

- Grain toward desert and urban consumers.
- Salt along the Niger.
- Fish and livestock products.
- Locally woven cloth.
- Iron tools and weapons.
- Kola from forest margins toward Sahelian markets.
- Captives between African states and markets.

### 7.4 Belief and knowledge

Islam spread partly through merchant settlement, intermarriage, schools, courts, pilgrimage, and royal patronage. It was not transmitted by commerce alone, nor was conversion everywhere immediate or coerced. Muslim commercial quarters could coexist with courts and rural populations following other religious systems.

Books and scholars became commercial cargo and travelers. Timbuktu manuscript culture preserves legal, devotional, scientific, literary, commercial, and historical works. Estimates of hundreds of thousands of surviving manuscripts concern much later accumulated collections, not medieval annual production. [Hamburg Centre for the Study of Manuscript Cultures](https://www.csmc.uni-hamburg.de/timbuktu/manuscripts.html)

## 9. Finances, Fraud, Violence, and Failure

### 9.1 Commercial risk

Merchants faced:

- Loss of animals or cargo.
- Death of partners.
- Unenforceable debts.
- Disputed weights and gold purity.
- Theft and robbery.
- Arbitrary tolls.
- Warfare and route closure.
- Price movements between departure and sale.
- Failure of couriers or water parties.

Islamic legal writings about Sudan-bound partnerships show concern over documentary validity and enforcement across jurisdictions. This is evidence of sophisticated finance and of its limits.

### 9.2 Adulteration and measurement fraud

Gold dust could be mixed with copper, sand, or powdered material. Clear descriptions are more abundant in early modern coastal accounts than in medieval Saharan records, so the practice’s medieval scale is unresolved. Repeated testing, touchstones, weights, trusted brokers, and reputation were likely controls.

Salt slabs could differ in quality and weight. No centralized cross-network inspectorate is documented.

### 9.3 Robbery and extraction

Desert groups appear in sources as guides, protectors, toll collectors, and raiders. These were not mutually exclusive occupations. States likewise combined protection with extraction: a tax might finance security, enrich a court, or both.

### 9.4 War and enslavement

Political expansion could capture trade nodes and people simultaneously. Enslaved persons were merchandise, laborers, gifts, soldiers, and domestic workers. The network rewarded states capable of converting warfare into taxable captives.

### 9.5 The Almoravid problem

A widespread narrative says the Almoravids conquered and destroyed Ghana in **1076**.

- **Documented:** Almoravid-associated forces took Awdaghost in 1054–55.
- **Later tradition:** chronicles and modern histories developed a conquest narrative involving Ghana.
- **Disputed:** contemporary evidence does not securely demonstrate a single Almoravid conquest of Ghana’s capital in 1076.
- **Conclusion:** Ghana’s decline should not be assigned to one unverified conquest. Political fragmentation, shifting routes, competing states, and ecological or economic change are alternative or complementary explanations.

### 9.6 Moroccan–Songhay conflict

In 1590 Ahmad al-Mansur sent an expedition under Judar Pasha across the Sahara. Firearms, political divisions within Songhay, and tactical circumstances contributed to Songhay’s defeat at Tondibi in 1591.

The date is disputed:

- *Taʾrīkh al-Sūdān* places the battle on 12 April.
- Other Moroccan, Spanish, and Sudanese materials place it in March.

The invasion captured Gao, Timbuktu, and Djenné but did not deliver effortless control of goldfields. Disease, distance, resistance, and logistics limited Moroccan authority. Scholarship emphasizing “muskets defeated spears” is incomplete; the campaign depended on Songhay’s internal crisis and a long, costly supply line. [Journal of African History](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-african-history/article/abs/archers-musketeers-and-mosquitoes-the-moroccan-invasion-of-the-sudan-and-the-songhay-resistance-15911612/7BE695BFE53648CF3324ED70F40512D6)

## 10. Change over the Lifespan

### Phase I: antecedents and uncertain beginnings, before c. 700

Oasis and desert-margin exchanges existed. Gold may have crossed the Sahara, but scale and continuity are unresolved.

### Phase II: Islamic commercial formation, c. 700–900

Sijilmasa and other North African centers grew. Muslim and especially Ibadi merchants established connections with Sudanese kingdoms. Arabic geographic knowledge became more precise.

### Phase III: Ghana–Awdaghost system, c. 900–1100

Ghana became the best-known gold power. Awdaghost flourished as an intermediary. Al-Bakri documented taxes, markets, merchant quarters, and royal control. Almoravid expansion reorganized northern and Saharan politics.

### Phase IV: transition and Mali’s rise, c. 1100–1300

Ghana fragmented or contracted; Sosso and then Mali gained power. Bure and other upper-Niger gold networks became more important. Walata and routes through Taghaza expanded.

### Phase V: Mali’s international high period, c. 1300–1450

Mansa Musa’s pilgrimage publicized Mali. Ibn Battuta documented the operational system. Timbuktu developed as a caravan–river hub. Mali’s authority reached major Sahelian and Saharan nodes but fluctuated.

### Phase VI: Songhay and Atlantic supplementation, c. 1450–1591

Songhay consolidated Gao, Timbuktu, Djenné, and salt routes. Portuguese traders reached West African coasts in the 1470s and founded São Jorge da Mina in 1482. Coastal commerce provided Akan and other producers with an additional outlet.

The Atlantic did not immediately “replace” the Sahara. It supplemented, competed with, and altered inland routes. Some gold continued north; some moved south to Portuguese markets. [Akan–Portuguese study](https://openurl.ebsco.com/contentitem/doi%3A10.1353/jwh.2022.0038?id=ebsco%3Adoi%3A10.1353%2Fjwh.2022.0038&sid=ebsco%3Aplink%3Acrawler)

### Phase VII: conquest and decentralization, 1591–c. 1600

Moroccan forces broke Songhay’s imperial center. Arma military elites established themselves in Niger-bend towns. Political fragmentation raised risks and altered taxation. Trade persisted through more decentralized institutions.

### Alternative periodizations

- A **numismatic periodization** emphasizes Islamic gold demand from the eighth century and minting cycles.
- An **imperial periodization** divides the story into Ghana, Mali, and Songhay.
- An **archaeological periodization** stresses multiple regional trajectories and warns that imperial labels obscure earlier urban centers.
- An **Atlantic periodization** makes the fifteenth century the decisive transition.
- A **long-duration Saharan interpretation** regards 1591 as only one reorganization in a system surviving into the nineteenth century.

## 11. End or Transformation

### 11.1 No single end date

The network did not end when Ghana declined, when Portuguese ships arrived, or when Morocco conquered Songhay.

The bounded medieval configuration ended through cumulative transformation:

- Atlantic outlets drew increasing quantities of gold and other goods toward the coast.
- New World bullion changed the wider monetary environment.
- Songhay’s imperial tax and security structure fragmented after 1591.
- Moroccan occupation redirected resources but could not centrally govern the whole network.
- Taghaza lost ground to Taoudenni.
- Slaves, salt, textiles, books, livestock, and pilgrimage remained important even as gold’s relative position changed.

A study of trans-Saharan adaptation emphasizes accommodation to Atlantic commerce rather than immediate displacement. [Lydon and colleagues, French-language article](https://www.scielo.br/j/vh/a/VC5Sn6PyFLGCvwHF5PNCyqb/?lang=fr)

### 11.2 Successor systems

Successors included:

- Taoudenni–Timbuktu salt caravans.
- Tuwat and Ghadames merchant networks.
- Juula commercial diasporas.
- Hausa and Bornu caravan systems.
- Atlantic gold and slave routes.
- Nineteenth-century Saharan credit networks.
- Colonial truck roads following or bypassing caravan alignments.

### 11.3 Fate of assets and personnel

- Camels, wells, guides, and merchant families remained in use.
- Timbuktu and Djenné continued as markets and scholarly centers.
- Arma elites occupied former Songhay centers.
- Gold production shifted among regions rather than ceasing.
- Some salt mines were abandoned; others expanded.
- Written archives remained dispersed in family libraries, mosques, North African collections, European repositories, and uncatalogued private holdings.

### 11.4 Physical and institutional remains

Surviving evidence includes:

- Ruins at Sijilmasa, Tegdaoust, Koumbi Saleh, Essouk-Tadmekka, Gao-Saney, and Taghaza.
- Mosques and urban fabric in Timbuktu, Gao, Djenné, and Walata.
- Salt extraction at Taoudenni and Bilma.
- Manuscripts held by Timbuktu families and institutions.
- Coins minted at Sijilmasa and other North African mints.
- Oral histories of Wagadu, Mali, Songhay, merchant families, and caravan peoples.

## 12. Contested and Legendary Aspects

### 12.1 The “silent trade”

**Story:** merchants placed goods at an agreed site and withdrew; unseen gold producers left gold beside them; exchanges proceeded by repeated withdrawal without speech or face-to-face contact.

**History of the claim:** versions appear in medieval descriptions of West African gold commerce and were repeated widely in European and school histories.

**Possible factual core:**

- Producers or intermediaries may have restricted outsiders’ access.
- Transactions between language groups could use brokers, gestures, or ritualized distance.
- Secrecy protected control over gold sources.

**Against literal universal acceptance:**

- Sustained gold commerce required negotiation of quality, quantities, credit, transport, and dispute.
- Arabic writers often transmitted marvel literature about remote lands.
- No evidence shows that the entire medieval gold trade operated silently.

Modern scholarship treats “silent trade” as a historical claim requiring contextual analysis, not a transparent description. [“Silent Trade: Myth and Historical Evidence”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/history-in-africa/article/abs/silent-trade-myth-and-historical-evidence/1C4DAF0E56226C6DDE644158C7A89487)

### 12.2 Ghana’s sacred gold monopoly

Al-Bakri’s account that the king reserved nuggets may preserve an actual fiscal rule. Later retellings turn it into a perfectly enforced monopoly that prevented inflation. No administrative records verify its coverage or duration.

### 12.3 The snake Bida

In Soninke Wagadu tradition, prosperity depended on sacrifices to a serpent named Bida. The killing of the serpent brought drought and the kingdom’s decline.

- **Classification:** oral tradition/legend with major historical importance.
- **Possible interpretations:** memory of ecological stress, political rupture, changing sacrifice, or conflict over authority.
- **Not established:** that a historical serpent cult caused Ghana’s decline or that the story identifies a datable event.

### 12.4 The Almoravid conquest of Ghana in 1076

This remains one of the most repeated claims in African history textbooks. The precise conquest lacks secure contemporary corroboration. It may combine the documented seizure of Awdaghost, later traditions about Islamization, and older historiographic assumptions.

### 12.5 Mansa Musa as the richest person ever

- **Documented:** Musa controlled access to a major gold-producing and trading empire and transported conspicuous quantities of gold.
- **Probable:** his spending affected gold’s exchange value in Cairo for a period.
- **Unverifiable:** a net worth in modern dollars.
- **Legendary inflation:** enormous entourage figures and statements that he permanently “destroyed Egypt’s economy.”
- **Historical effect:** his pilgrimage placed Mali prominently in Arabic geography and European cartography, including the 1375 Catalan Atlas.

### 12.6 Timbuktu as a city made entirely of gold

European imagination converted reports of gold commerce into visions of a golden metropolis. Timbuktu’s buildings were principally earthen. Its wealth lay in exchange, scholarship, land, livestock, people, books, and political office—not streets or palaces made of bullion.

### 12.7 Salt worth its weight in gold

This is a modern aphorism. Salt could become extremely valuable far from its source, and Ibn Battuta documented a steep spatial price increase. No evidence supports a stable, network-wide one-to-one weight exchange.

### 12.8 “The Portuguese ended the caravan trade”

Portuguese coastal trade diverted and supplemented gold flows but did not terminate Saharan commerce. Salt, slaves, books, textiles, livestock, pilgrimage, and regional trade continued for centuries.

### 12.9 Cartographic certainty

Maps depicting permanent roads and firm imperial borders exaggerate precision. Routes were corridors; political authority was layered; many identifications remain disputed.

## 13. Historiography and Representations

### 13.1 Primary textual corpus

The foundational Arabic sources include:

- Al-Fazari.
- Al-Yaʿqubi.
- Ibn al-Faqih.
- Al-Masʿudi.
- Al-Muhallabi.
- Al-Bakri.
- Al-Idrisi.
- Ibn Saʿid.
- Al-Umari.
- Ibn Battuta.
- Ibn Khaldun.
- Al-Hasan al-Wazzan/Leo Africanus.
- Al-Saʿdi’s *Taʾrīkh al-Sūdān*.
- The composite and textually problematic *Taʾrīkh al-Fattāsh*.

The standard English collection is Nehemia Levtzion and J. F. P. Hopkins, eds. and trans., *Corpus of Early Arabic Sources for West African History*. Its existence and contents are catalogued by the [Smithsonian](https://www.si.edu/object/corpus-early-arabic-sources-west-african-history-translated-jfp-hopkins-edited-and-annotated-n%3Asiris_sil_689895).

### 13.2 Source limitations

- Many writers never visited the regions described.
- Authors copied earlier texts, sometimes preserving obsolete information.
- Place names shifted or were duplicated.
- Distances were expressed in travel days.
- Court-centered accounts underrepresent laborers, women, rural producers, and non-Muslim merchants.
- Gold and salt rarely survive archaeologically in easily identifiable form.
- Written business archives are sparse for the medieval period.
- Later chronicles contain valuable traditions but cannot automatically be projected backward.
- The published *Taʾrīkh al-Fattāsh* combines layers, including a nineteenth-century redaction; treating it as an undifferentiated seventeenth-century witness is unsafe.

### 13.3 Archaeological turn

Earlier scholarship depended heavily on Arabic texts and imperial narratives. Archaeology at Tegdaoust, Koumbi Saleh, Djenné-jeno, Essouk-Tadmekka, Gao, and Sijilmasa has changed the field by demonstrating:

- Indigenous urbanism predating some state narratives.
- Imported glass and copper.
- Local craft production.
- Gold processing at Tadmekka.
- Complex settlement sequences not reducible to a single conquest.
- Difficulty of identifying trade commodities from durable remains alone.

A comparative archaeological agenda now emphasizes Sijilmasa, Sedrata, Koumbi Saleh, Awdaghost, Tadmekka, and Gao. [Collège de France](https://www.college-de-france.fr/en/agenda/symposium/history-of-the-sahara-from-650-ce-to-the-present/markets-and-materials-in-the-saharan-middle-ages-7th-15th-an-archaeological-perspective)

### 13.4 Major scholarly debates

1. **Pre-Islamic antiquity:** Was substantial gold already crossing the Sahara?
2. **Trade and state formation:** Did commerce create Ghana, or did political aggregation make commerce scalable?
3. **Almoravid conquest:** invasion, religious influence, commercial reorientation, or later historiographic construction?
4. **Control of goldfields:** direct imperial administration versus indirect access through autonomous producers.
5. **Silent trade:** literal institution, episodic practice, brokered encounter, or marvel motif?
6. **Gold volumes:** large macroeconomic importance versus inability to quantify tonnage.
7. **Islamization:** merchant-led gradual change versus royal and scholarly programs.
8. **Atlantic transition:** displacement, supplementation, or regional recombination.
9. **Meaning of “Wangara”:** ethnicity, occupation, diaspora, outsider label, or several of these.
10. **Koumbi Saleh:** Ghana’s capital or a major site not yet conclusively matched to the texts.

### 13.5 Archives and collections

Relevant materials survive in:

- Timbuktu family libraries and the Ahmed Baba Institute.
- Moroccan manuscript libraries and royal archives.
- Algerian, Tunisian, Mauritanian, Malian, Senegalese, and Nigerien repositories.
- Bibliothèque nationale de France.
- Arabic Collections Online.
- Portuguese royal and maritime archives.
- Spanish diplomatic archives concerning Morocco.
- Coin cabinets and archaeological collections in Africa, Europe, and North America.

The surviving Timbuktu corpus is enormous but mostly later than the early medieval formation of the network. Cataloguing remains incomplete. Manuscripts were damaged or dispersed through warfare, colonial seizure, private sale, environmental decay, and the 2012 occupation of northern Mali.

### 13.6 Popular representations

#### Histories and exhibitions

- Metropolitan Museum of Art, “The Trans-Saharan Gold Trade (7th–14th Century).”
- British Museum and related Mansa Musa exhibitions.
- “Caravans of Gold, Fragments in Time,” an exhibition emphasizing West Africa’s role in medieval global exchange.
- UNESCO *General History of Africa*.
- Boston University’s “Gold Road” educational project.

#### Literature and travel writing

- Modern biographies and children’s books about Mansa Musa frequently assign unsupported net-worth figures.
- Travel writing on Timbuktu often reproduces the “lost golden city” trope.
- Accounts of modern Taoudenni caravans illuminate environmental logistics but cannot be used uncritically as medieval ethnography.

#### Games and digital culture

Strategy games such as *Civilization*, *Crusader Kings*, and *Europa Universalis* represent Ghana, Mali, Songhay, gold provinces, and Saharan routes. They typically convert fluid corridors and negotiated jurisdictions into fixed borders and stable commodity nodes.

#### Film and television

Documentaries about Mansa Musa and Timbuktu commonly combine sound primary-source material with dramatic caravan imagery. Frequent divergences include exact wealth rankings, enormous unqualified caravan totals, and claims of complete imperial ownership of gold mines.

## 15. Conclusions

The medieval salt-for-gold trade was a network without a single owner, capital, charter, road, or lifetime. Its durable unit was not an empire but an adaptable chain of producers, merchant diasporas, water points, animals, markets, river craft, correspondence, credit, and negotiated political protection.

Its most secure history begins with the growing integration of North Africa and the western Sudan from the eighth century. Ghana, Mali, and Songhay successively captured revenue and political advantage from important portions of it, but none owned the system as a whole or necessarily administered the goldfields directly. Saharan communities supplied indispensable expertise and infrastructure. Muslim merchants and institutions expanded written finance, law, literacy, pilgrimage, and education. Non-Muslim producers and traders remained essential and are poorly represented in surviving texts.

Salt and gold were central but never exclusive. The same routes transported captives, books, horses, copper, cowries, cloth, kola, scholars, couriers, religious doctrines, and intelligence. Coerced labor at salt mines and the northbound traffic in enslaved people were structural parts of the system.

The network’s celebrated statistics are mostly less secure than its mechanisms. Ibn Battuta’s travel stages and price observations, al-Bakri’s reported duties, gold coins, imported glass, settlement archaeology, and Tadmekka’s metallurgical remains support detailed reconstruction. Annual tonnage, total profits, aggregate slave numbers, and modern valuations of Mansa Musa cannot be established.

Its medieval imperial form transformed after Portuguese coastal expansion and the Moroccan conquest of Songhay, but Saharan trade survived. Taoudenni salt, merchant families, Islamic scholarship, manuscript libraries, pilgrimage routes, and caravan practices constitute its institutional afterlife.
