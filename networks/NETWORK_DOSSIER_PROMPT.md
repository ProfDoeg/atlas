# NETWORK_DOSSIER_PROMPT.md

Standing prompt for commissioning a network research dossier — prose Markdown,
stage one of the pipeline (dossier first; a structured .network.json comes
later as a separate step, same shape as person dossier -> journey.json).
Sibling to `working/journeys/dossiers/PROMPT_TEMPLATE.md`. Drafted by
Fable 5.1, 2026-09-06.

---

[NETWORK NAME] ([PERIOD])
[ONE-LINE DESCRIPTION]

Please conduct a comprehensive, source-driven research dossier on this historical network. I want the fullest reconstructable history of the network as a *system*, from its formation to its end or transformation, across the bounded period given above. Reconstruct it in detail: what it was, what it moved or carried or connected (goods, money, credit, letters, information, people, pilgrims, orders, signals, belief), the real places and institutions and pieces of infrastructure that constituted it, the real connections between them, and the real people who built, ran, used, exploited, policed, and destroyed it. Treat the network itself as the subject of the biography.

Include, wherever available: official and alternative names for the network in every relevant language; precise dates and disputed dates for its formation, major reorganizations, and end; its geographic extent and how that extent changed; its legal, political, religious, or commercial charter and standing; who owned it, who governed it, who paid for it; how it actually worked at the operational level — the mechanism of a single transaction, journey, message, or transfer traced end to end; its scale wherever quantifiable (volumes, values, tonnage, letter counts, personnel, distances, travel times, costs), with the source and reliability of every figure; its relationship to states, churches, rivals, and the networks it replaced or was replaced by; its finances; its failures, frauds, robberies, disasters, suppressions, lawsuits, investigations, and scandals; and its afterlife. Include the good and the bad without judgment. Do not sanitize inconvenient material — coercion, slavery, forced labor, monopoly violence, espionage, and extraction are part of many networks' actual operation and must be stated plainly where documented.

For every factual claim, hold the same evidentiary discipline throughout: distinguish documented fact, plausible scholarly inference, allegation, disputed claim, unverified claim, propaganda, romanticized tradition, legend, myth, and unresolved question. Do not dismiss a claim merely because it has been used as propaganda or romance — investigate independently. Where sources disagree (on dates, routes, volumes, membership, causes of collapse), give the competing versions with names, dates, and sources. Mark anything that cannot be established explicitly.

Organize the dossier with sections along the following lines, adapting as the network's actual character requires:

1. **Identification and Definition.** What the network was; names and alternative names in all relevant languages; the medium or media it operated in (roads, ships, letters, bills of exchange, telegraph wire, kinship, ritual); its bounded period and why those boundary dates are chosen, including any scholarly dispute about when it "really" began or ended; what counts as inside the network and what does not.

2. **Formation.** How and why it came into being; predecessor systems it absorbed, imitated, or displaced; founders and founding institutions; charters, treaties, capitulations, or improvised beginnings; the political, economic, religious, and technological conditions that made it possible.

3. **Constituent Nodes.** A thorough survey of the network's real nodes — hubs, termini, waystations, chokepoints, entrepôts, branch houses, relay stations, missions, cells, offices, ports, passes — each with its location, its role in the system, its dates of involvement (founding, peak, decline within the network), what is physically or documentarily known of it, and what survives. Distinguish major hubs from minor stations, and say which nodes are securely attested, which are inferred, and which are traditional or conjectural.

4. **Connections.** The real edges between nodes, in whatever the network's actual medium was — routes, sea lanes, correspondence, credit and remittance relationships, kinship and marriage ties, chains of command, cable and wire. For each significant connection: what traveled along it, in which directions, how often, how fast, at what cost and risk, and the evidence for it (itineraries, waybills, surviving letters, account books, archaeology, oral tradition). Where a famous "route" is actually a later cartographic or historiographic construction, say so.

5. **Agents and Operators.** The people and institutions who ran the network and the people who used it: founders, directors, factors, agents, couriers, carriers, pilots, muleteers, conductors, correspondents, engineers, clerks, guards, and the enslaved or conscripted where applicable; also its regulators, taxers, protectors, parasites, smugglers, and enemies. Give the fullest available detail on key named figures — their roles, dates, and fates — while keeping the network, not any one biography, at the center.

6. **Operation and Mechanism.** How the system worked day to day: procedures, instruments (letters of credit, quipus, tokens, ciphers, timetables, protocols), governance and discipline, financing, security, maintenance, and communication about the network within the network. Trace at least one well-documented concrete case end to end — a single shipment, transfer, letter, journey, or transaction — as a worked example, with its sources.

7. **Change over the Lifespan.** A periodized account of the network's evolution: growth, reorganizations, technological or institutional shifts, geographic expansion and contraction, competition, crises and recoveries, peak, and decline. Make the periodization explicit and note where scholars periodize it differently.

8. **End or Transformation.** How and why it ended, was suppressed, was superseded, or transformed into something else; the proximate events and the structural causes, distinguished; what happened to its assets, personnel, infrastructure, and records; successor systems; physical and institutional remains today.

9. **Contested and Legendary Aspects.** Myth is as important as fact. Include the network's treasure legends, conspiracy narratives, survival myths, romanticized and nationalist retellings, hostile mythologies, and self-mythologies — and for each significant one, explain what the story says, when and where it first appears, who circulated it and why, what evidence exists for and against it, and how it has shaped the network's reputation. Label all such material clearly as legend, tradition, or disputed claim, and keep it separate from the documented record while giving it full and serious treatment.

10. **Historiography and Representations.** How the network has been studied and imagined: the major scholarly works and debates, the state of the archives (what survives, where, in what languages, what was lost or destroyed), key archaeological or documentary discoveries, and a substantial bibliography/filmography of significant popular treatments — histories, novels, films, games, exhibitions — noting where popular versions diverge from the evidence.

11. **Chronology.** A compact year-by-year (or period-by-period, where sources only allow that) timeline of the network's history from formation to end.

12. **Sources.**

Search broadly and deeply; prefer primary and archival sources — charters, account books, letters, itineraries, colonial and ecclesiastical records, court proceedings, engineering reports, archaeology — over encyclopedia summaries. Search in every language relevant to the network's geography and administration, not only English. Look for obscure, contradictory, and forgotten material. Write without editorial overlay — no moralizing, no ideological framing, no telling the reader what to think. Favor documentary presentation. Do not compress for elegance; detail over brevity.

End with one deduplicated list of every URL actually used (full https:// URLs, one per line, no shortened links, no duplicates). Output as a downloadable .md file named [slug]_network_dossier.md, where [slug] is the network's name in lowercase with underscores.
