# Dihya (al-Kahina) — from the Aurès to the Well of the Queen

**33 stops in 8 segments; c. 645-703 CE (her war) + a coda to 2026; calendar julian (per atlas convention); register national mythology; 3/33 quoted.**

## Sources
The primary "canon" here is not a single text but a chain of Arabic chronicles written 100-700 years after her death — Waqidi (d.822) via Ibn al-Athir, Ibn Abd al-Hakam (d.871, earliest substantial account), al-Bakri and al-Maliki (11th c.), and above all Ibn Khaldun's *Kitab al-'Ibar* (14th c.), the source of her name, her genealogy, and the Well of al-Kahina. I read these at second hand, through Norman Roth's "The Kahina: Legendary Material..." (1982), H.T. Norris's *The Berbers in Arabic Literature* (1982), Brett & Fentress's *The Berbers* (1997), and — most useful of all — Jessica Keuter's 2019 UCLA thesis *The Contested Legend of al-Kâhina: Prophetess or Propaganda?* (escholarship.org), which synthesizes and cross-checks the whole historiographic debate, including Hendrickx (2013), Maddy-Weitzman (2011), and Beider (2016). Modern-day material (the Baghaï statue, its 2016 burning, the Tizi Ouzou mural) comes from Cynthia Becker's photo-essay and French-language Algerian press (lematindz.net et al.). Wikipedia articles on Kusaila, Uqba ibn Nafi, Hassan ibn al-Nu'man, and the Battle of El Jem/Tabarka supplied the external, better-attested chronology of the Arab conquest that her own legend is embedded in.

## Judgment calls
- **Two chronological traditions for the final battle exist and disagree by geography, not just date**: one line (Wikipedia's Battle of El Jem/Tabarka) has her make her last stand at the amphitheater in Tunisia's Sahel, then flee back into the Aurès to die at Bir al-Kahina; another (Roth, quoting Abun-Nasr) has the second and final battle fought near **Gabès**, with her death following at a well "in the Auras." I followed the Gabès-then-Aurès line as more geographically coherent (El Jem is a very long way east of her home mountain to retreat *from*), and folded the El Jem tradition into the report rather than the map.
- **Bir al-Kahina's location is itself disputed** — Brett & Fentress place it "to the west of the Aures," while a still-standing well called Bir el-Kahen, cataloged by the Jewish heritage archive Diarna, sits near Bir el Ater in Tébessa Province, southeast of the range. I used the Diarna coordinates because the name has survived into the present at a fixed, visitable point — the strongest kind of evidence this legend offers — and noted the alternate reading here rather than picking a third, invented spot.
- **Her religion is treated as the tradition itself treats it: unresolved.** Ibn Khaldun's Jewish-Berber (Judeo-Berber) reading, the Christian reading (Talbi's genealogy, the Aurès's Donatist history), and the indigenous-polytheist reading are all live in the scholarship; I gave one campa to holding all three rather than adjudicating, which is itself the honest position — Keuter's thesis concludes the same way.
- **Her sons' names and paternity are contested** (Ibn Khaldun vs. Ibn Idhari vs. Norris give different names and even different counts), so I kept the ambiguity explicit in the text ("some tellings say... by a different account") rather than picking one roster.
- **Ibn Khaldun's claim that she lived 127 years and ruled 35** is flagged by every modern scholar as mythic excess (it makes her exactly Moses's age at death) — I did not build the birth date from that arithmetic; "c. 645" is an editorial placeholder consistent with her being a grown war-leader by the 680s-690s, marked `traditional`.
- **The scorched-earth policy's authorship is genuinely contested** by scholars (Roth suspects it was invented later to explain agricultural decline, or transferred from the Arabs' own documented tactics) — I narrated it as canon, per the register, but let the contest surface inside the stop's own text rather than hiding it.
- **The coda (711-2026)** follows the same device the atlas already uses for Lautaro (the 1812 Logia coda): the "years" field covers only her own lifetime; the last five stops carry her name's afterlife forward without claiming it as her biography.

## Gaps and time-folds
- Nothing about her before the mid-680s is attested at all; the first three stops are pure register-work, filling silence the way Neruda fills Lautaro's boyhood.
- The exact chronology of "how many battles" with Hassan (one, two, or three, depending on the chronicler) is irreconcilable; I chose the two-battle reading (Meskiana/Wadi Nini, then Gabès) that most modern secondary sources converge on.
- Day-level dates for almost everything are invented scaffolding over year-level (at best) attestation — honestly reflected in the confidence mix: 3 attested (all in the post-death coda, where real calendars resume), 12 inferred, 18 traditional.

## The five richest episodes
1. **The adoption of Khalid ibn Yazid** — an Arab prisoner made a Berber son by the Berber rite of milk-kinship, kept five years at her right hand, then sent back to the enemy with her own sons on the eve of her death.
2. **The dream of the head** — her own prophesied end, narrated and fulfilled to the letter three stops later at Damascus, the same device Joan's "tomorrow blood will flow" or Lautaro's Guacolda-dream perform elsewhere in the atlas.
3. **Bir al-Kahina** — a legend's one fixed, still-named, still-visited point in an otherwise placeless story.
4. **The statue at Baghaï** — raised in 2003 on the ground she once burned, torched by arsonists in 2016, and mended within days by the town's own young people: the myth still being fought over in real time, in the current news cycle rather than the chronicle.
5. **The 12,000 at the Straits** — her own people's spears opening Spain eight years after her death, under a general some traditions make her own kinsman — the bitterest and richest irony the legend offers.

## Connections in the atlas
Dihya is this atlas's other **Lautaro** — a resistance leader against an expanding empire whose only canon is the invader's own centuries-later chronicle, and who gets a legacy-coda rather than a clean death-stop (matching Lautaro's 1812 Logia beat with her own 1854 Fatma n'Soumer beat, drawn from the *same mountains*). She is also a structural sibling to **Joan of Arc**: both are illiterate rural prophetesses who lead armies on a voice's authority, both are captured through betrayal from within their own side, both are beheaded/burned as trophies of the winning empire, and both had their legend fought over by French nationalism a few centuries later — Hannoum and Maddy-Weitzman document the "Kahina-as-Joan-of-Arc" comparison explicitly in the colonial French sources themselves. Her adopted son Khalid, and her sons who cross into Spain, connect forward to **Tariq ibn Ziyad** (already legendary at Gibraltar) if that dataset is ever built. And her prophetic "dream of the head" belongs with Joan's foreseen wound and Lautaro's Guacolda-dream as the atlas's recurring device: the canon lets its heroes narrate their own martyrdom in advance.
