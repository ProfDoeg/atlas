# “Le Sacre de Napoleon” (David's painting): Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier tracks the monumental canvas painted under Jacques-Louis David’s direction in Paris in 1805–1807, now in the Musée du Louvre under inventory number **INV 3699**. It distinguishes that original from:

1. David’s second, full-size version, begun in Paris in 1808 and completed in Brussels in 1822, now at Versailles (**MV 7156**);
2. preparatory drawings, portrait studies, engravings, and reduced copies;
3. the historical coronation ceremony represented in the painting.

The work is “sacred” only in the sense that it depicts a religious consecration and deploys Christian ritual to legitimate sovereignty. It is not itself documented as a relic, cult object, or object of supernatural power. Its legendary aspect lies instead in its transformation of a contested political ceremony into an authoritative national image.

Evidence labels used below:

- **Documented:** supported by contemporary records or institutional object documentation.
- **Physical evidence:** visible inscription, technical examination, or material observation.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** an interpretation based on surviving records or images.
- **Imperial representation:** an officially sponsored claim embedded in the painting.
- **Tradition/anecdote:** repeated in biographical literature but not independently secured here.
- **Unresolved:** the available published evidence does not establish the matter.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Principal object

**Current official title:** *Le Couronnement de l’empereur Napoléon Ier et de l’impératrice Joséphine dans la cathédrale Notre-Dame de Paris, le 2 décembre 1804*.

**Common titles:**

- *Le Sacre de Napoléon*
- *Le Sacre de l’empereur Napoléon Ier*
- *Le Couronnement*
- English: *The Coronation of Napoleon* or *The Consecration of the Emperor Napoleon I and the Coronation of the Empress Joséphine*
- German: *Die Krönung Napoleons*
- Italian: *L’incoronazione di Napoleone*
- Spanish: *La consagración de Napoleón* or *La coronación de Napoleón*

The short French title is potentially misleading. *Sacre* derives from *sacrer*, “to consecrate,” and ordinarily denotes a monarch’s religious consecration, especially by anointing. David’s finished composition does not show Napoleon’s anointing or his act of crowning himself. It shows Napoleon preparing to place a crown on Joséphine. The Louvre therefore catalogues it under the fuller, more descriptive title while retaining *Le Sacre de l’empereur Napoléon Ier* as an alternative title. [Musée du Louvre collection record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010065720)

### Classification and catalogue identity

- **Class:** monumental history painting; dynastic state image; collective portrait; official commemoration.
- **Artist:** Jacques-Louis David (1748–1825).
- **Recorded collaborators:** Georges Rouget, principally on figures, and Ignazio Eugenio Degotti, principally on architecture and perspective.
- **Date:** 1806–1807 inscribed; begun 21 December 1805, substantially finished by mid-November 1807, with alterations in January 1808.
- **Medium:** oil on canvas.
- **Dimensions:** 6.21 m high × 9.79 m wide.
- **Current inventory:** INV 3699.
- **Earlier inventory:** MR 1437.
- **Department:** Département des Peintures, Musée du Louvre.
- **Current location:** Louvre, Denon wing, level 1, room 702, according to the current catalogue. [Musée du Louvre collection record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010065720)

The Louvre describes it as its second-largest painting after Veronese’s *Wedding Feast at Cana*. A printed description issued for its first exhibition in 1808 called it thirty feet wide and nineteen feet high and claimed more than two hundred life-size figures. The Louvre’s recent public interpretation counts **191 represented persons**. The discrepancy reflects different historical counting practices rather than evidence that figures were removed. [Louvre presentation](https://www.louvre.fr/louvreplus/video-au-louvre-le-sacre-de-napoleon)

### Inscriptions

**Lower right:**

> L. David. f.ebat.

This is a Latinizing artist’s formula, approximately “L. David was making [this].”

**Lower left:**

> 1806 & 1807.

The inscription records the main years of execution, although documentary evidence places the first work on the canvas in December 1805 and final revisions in January 1808. [Musée du Louvre collection record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010065720)

### Composition and depicted objects

The setting is the choir of Notre-Dame de Paris as transformed for the ceremony of 2 December 1804. Napoleon stands on the altar steps in a white satin tunic and crimson velvet mantle ornamented with golden bees. Holding Joséphine’s crown in both hands, he prepares to lower it onto her head. Joséphine kneels on a violet velvet cushion. Her train is carried by Adélaïde de La Rochefoucauld and Émilie de La Valette.

Behind Napoleon sits Pope Pius VII, his right hand raised in blessing. Cardinal Giovanni Battista Caprara and Cardinal Giovanni Braschi-Onesti are among the clergy. Imperial dignitaries hold insignia: Charles-François Lebrun carries an eagle-topped staff; Jean-Jacques-Régis de Cambacérès holds the hand of justice; Louis-Alexandre Berthier carries the orb; Talleyrand appears in his office as grand chamberlain. Members of the Bonaparte and Beauharnais families, court officers, diplomats, clergy, and spectators occupy the surrounding tiers.

At the compositional center above Joséphine sits Napoleon’s mother, Letizia Bonaparte. **Documented discrepancy:** she did not attend the ceremony. Her painted presence converts absence and family conflict into an image of dynastic unanimity. Other spatial arrangements were likewise compressed or rearranged. The Louvre stresses that the apparent documentary fidelity of faces and costumes conceals a deliberate reconstruction: David assembled participants on one side to make the action legible and included absent persons on imperial instructions. [Louvre presentation](https://www.louvre.fr/louvreplus/video-au-louvre-le-sacre-de-napoleon)

### Material construction and condition

The published catalogue identifies oil on canvas but does not provide, in its online record, a complete support analysis: number and orientation of canvas pieces, fiber identification, original priming, stretcher chronology, pigment analysis, lining history, or a condition map. Those details should not be invented from the work’s dimensions.

The canvas is currently presented in a monumental gilded frame. A gilt frame made by **Potrelle** was supplied on 2 December 1807 for **1,110 francs**. Whether every component of the presently visible framing system is that original 1807 fabric has not been established in the public sources consulted. The painting’s enormous size necessarily makes its support, tension, transport, and installation technically consequential, but a complete published conservation dossier was not located in open-access form.

No mutilation, theft, loss of a section, or deliberate iconoclastic attack on the Louvre canvas is documented in the sources consulted. Its repeated rolling, transport, storage, evacuation, rehanging, and climatic exposure are nevertheless essential parts of its material biography.

### Related version: Versailles, MV 7156

David received permission to make two replicas. The only completed full-size second version was begun in 1808, taken into exile, and finished at Brussels in 1822. It is oil on canvas, approximately 6.10 × 9.71 m, and is now displayed in the Salle du Sacre at Versailles as **MV 7156**.

It is not a forgery or a later museum facsimile. It is an authorized second version produced by David and assistants. Small differences distinguish it from the Louvre original; the most frequently noted is the pink rather than white dress of one of Napoleon’s sisters. Its long exhibition history has caused it to be casually called “the original at Versailles,” but the 1805–1807 commission canvas is the Louvre work. [Versailles collection record](https://collections.chateauversailles.fr/?permid=permobj_efb1c634-b95f-402f-8c6f-12848982844c)

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Commission

**Documented:** Napoleon commissioned David in September 1804, approximately three months before the coronation festivities, to commemorate four events:

1. the coronation;
2. the army’s oath following the distribution of the eagles;
3. Napoleon’s enthronement in Notre-Dame;
4. the imperial couple’s arrival at the Hôtel de Ville.

No price or final architectural destination was fixed at the outset. Of the four projected monumental canvases, David completed only the *Coronation* and the *Distribution of the Eagles*. The Louvre’s record accordingly corrects simplified accounts saying that the painting was from the outset definitively commissioned for the Tuileries Guards’ Room. That room was later considered, but the 1804 commission itself had no settled destination. [Musée du Louvre collection record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010065720)

David was appointed *Premier peintre de l’Empereur*. The commission made him both witness and image-maker of the new dynasty. He attended the ceremony and used sittings, portrait studies, costume records, architectural observation, and ceremonial documentation to construct the painting.

### Workshop and execution

Work on the final canvas began on **21 December 1805** in a studio installed in the disused chapel of the former Collège de Cluny, near the Place de la Sorbonne. The dimensions required a building-scale workspace rather than a conventional painter’s room.

The Louvre records the division of assistance as:

- **David:** design, direction, principal authorship, and substantial execution;
- **Georges Rouget:** assistance with figures;
- **Ignazio Eugenio Degotti:** assistance with architectural setting and perspective.

The painting was substantially completed by mid-November 1807. Minor but significant alterations were made in January 1808. Its signed dates, “1806 & 1807,” refer to the central execution period rather than the entire production process.

### Payment

David valued the work at **100,000 francs** in June 1806. The imperial household paid **65,000 francs**, in three installments:

- 25,000 francs in June 1805;
- 15,000 francs in April 1806;
- 25,000 francs in August 1807.

The first payment predates the recorded start of work on the full canvas and therefore relates to the commission and preparatory labor. The difference between David’s valuation and the amount paid records a real financial negotiation, not an uncertain modern estimate. [Musée du Louvre collection record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010065720)

### Change of subject during manufacture

Several preparatory compositions show Napoleon raising a crown toward his own head. David initially intended to represent the self-coronation, an event that actually occurred. The final canvas instead depicts the coronation of Joséphine.

Sylvain Laveissière attributes the decisive suggestion to François Gérard, who reportedly warned that the self-crowning pose would appear arrogant or provocative. David revised the central action after it had already progressed substantially. Napoleon later interpreted the alteration as an “intrigue” between Joséphine and David but approved its chivalric effect. This explanatory dialogue survives through later David literature and should be treated as a **scholarly reconstruction grounded partly in workshop recollection**, not as verbatim stenography of a securely preserved meeting. [Fondation Napoléon analysis](https://fondationnapoleon.org/chef-d-oeuvre/le-sacre-ou-le-couronnement/)

The change had structural consequences: Joséphine became the devotional and emotional center, while Napoleon retained agency as the source of the crown. The painting could thus commemorate his own sacre and self-coronation without directly showing the gesture most likely to be read as defiance of papal authority.

### Other alterations

- **Letizia Bonaparte:** added prominently despite her absence from the actual ceremony, reportedly at Napoleon’s insistence.
- **Pius VII:** David considered or painted a more passive papal pose. Napoleon required the pope to be shown actively blessing, producing the raised hand visible in the completed work.
- **Architecture and assembly:** persons were relocated and Notre-Dame’s choir was optically reorganized to make a coherent theater of power.
- **Napoleon’s first pose:** the original self-coronation conception was painted out in favor of the coronation of Joséphine.

A painted compositional study was later detected beneath David’s 1808 double portrait of Pius VII and Cardinal Caprara in the Philadelphia Museum of Art. Radiography and infrared examination of that separate panel disclosed a stage of the *Sacre* design and corroborated the evolution visible in drawings. It is evidence about David’s design process, not a technical examination of the Louvre canvas itself. [Fondation Napoléon preparatory-study account](https://www.napoleon.org/histoire-des-2-empires/tableaux/le-sacre-ou-le-couronnement-dessin-preparatoire-david/)

### Earliest public attestation

The completed canvas was transported to the Musée Napoléon at the beginning of February 1808. It opened to public viewing in the Salon Carré on **7 February 1808**. A descriptive guide, *Description du tableau exposé au musée Napoléon, représentant le couronnement de Leurs Majestés impériales et royales, peint par M. David*, was published by Labarre in two editions that year.

The description explains the deliberately composite instant: Napoleon has already placed two crowns successively on his own head, removed the second, and now raises it over Joséphine. This contemporary text is unusually valuable because it records how the image was officially explained at its first showing. The Louvre reproduces a substantial extract in its catalogue. [Musée du Louvre collection record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010065720)

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Ownership and custody require distinction. The imperial household paid for the work, but the canvas repeatedly returned to David’s studio and did not enter permanent museum custody immediately.

### 1804–1808: commission and workshop custody

- **September 1804:** commissioned by Napoleon as one of four commemorative canvases.
- **21 December 1805–mid-November 1807:** executed in the former Collège de Cluny chapel.
- **January 1808:** final alterations.
- **Early February 1808:** moved to the Musée Napoléon.

### 1808: first public exhibition

- **7 February–21 March 1808:** exhibited independently in the Salon Carré.
- **14 October 1808–early January 1809:** exhibited in the Salon of 1808 as catalogue no. 144.
- Contemporary spectators could use printed descriptions to identify the vast assembly of figures.

### 1809–1811: oscillation between studio and museum

- **1809:** returned to David’s Cluny studio.
- **25 August–October 1810:** returned to the Musée Napoléon for the exhibition connected with the decennial prizes.
- **April 1811:** considered for installation in the Guards’ Room at the Tuileries Palace; the plan was abandoned.
- **Spring 1811:** returned to David’s studio, where it could be seen by visitors on request.

The political difficulty of installing a giant image centered on Joséphine increased after Napoleon divorced her and married Marie-Louise of Austria in 1810. This is a strong contextual explanation for the abandoned palace installation, but it should not replace the documentary record with a single presumed motive.

### 1815–1819: Restoration and transfer into royal museum custody

David went into exile in Brussels after the Bourbon restoration. The painting remained in Paris. At the end of 1819 it was removed from the Cluny premises and placed in the stores of the Musée royal des Arts at the Louvre.

Between 1816 and 1824 it was entered in the royal museum inventory as **MR 1437**, valued at **80,000 francs**. That registration confirmed it as Crown or royal-museum property rather than part of David’s freely disposable exile estate. No sale, inheritance, confiscatory auction, or restitution claim involving the original is recorded.

### 1836–1889: Versailles

Around 1836 the painting was moved to Versailles for Louis-Philippe’s new historical galleries. It was installed in the Salle du Sacre and became publicly visible when the Musée de l’Histoire de France opened on **10 June 1837**.

Between 1852 and 1860, Louvre curator Frédéric Villot assigned the modern inventory number **INV 3699**.

During the Franco-Prussian War and Paris Commune crisis, the canvas was evacuated from Versailles to the Louvre for safety on **14 September 1870**. It remained there until December 1871, then returned to Versailles. The source documents an evacuation, not enemy seizure or theft.

### 1889–1939: transfer back to Paris

In 1889 the painting was removed from Versailles and shown in the **Exposition centennale de l’art français**, part of the Paris Exposition Universelle, open 5 May–31 October.

Beginning in August 1890 it was installed at the Louvre in the Salle des Sept-Cheminées. The original did not thereafter return to permanent display at Versailles; the palace ultimately substituted David’s second version.

### 1939–1946: wartime evacuation

The painting left public display in September 1939. The Louvre’s record gives the following sequence of refuges:

1. **Château de Chèreperrine**, Origny-le-Roux, Orne;
2. **Château de Sourches**, Saint-Symphorien, Sarthe;
3. **Château de Montal**, Saint-Jean-Lespinasse, Lot.

It returned from Montal to the Louvre on **7 February 1946**. No wartime seizure, Nazi transfer, loss, or restitution proceeding is recorded for it.

### 1949–present: Louvre display history

- **October 1949–1969:** Salle Daru.
- **1969–1994:** Salle Mollien.
- **Since 1994:** Salle Daru.
- **Current catalogue location:** room 702, Denon wing, level 1.

The complete official chain is provided in the Louvre’s current catalogue record. [Musée du Louvre collection record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010065720)

### Chain-of-custody assessment

The original’s chain is unusually continuous. There is no documented disappearance, theft, private-market transfer, colonial seizure, contested inheritance, or provenance gap. The principal ambiguity concerns legal possession between imperial payment and final museum delivery: the canvas was state-sponsored and paid for, but it remained in David’s working custody for long periods until 1819.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is the Louvre canvas the commissioned original?

**Yes, to the standard of documentary and physical art-historical identification.** Its signatures and dates, exhibition history, imperial payment record, movements, royal inventory MR 1437, and later Louvre inventory INV 3699 form a continuous documentary chain. No rival object has a credible claim to being the 1805–1807 commission canvas.

### The Versailles “replica”

The Versailles canvas is an authentic work from David’s enterprise but is not the first canvas. It was authorized, begun in 1808, completed under David in 1822, and subsequently exhibited commercially. Calling it a “copy” is accurate in sequence but can obscure its authorial status; calling it another “original” obscures the historically prior Louvre commission. “Authorized second version” or “full-size replica by David and his workshop” is more precise.

Its documented itinerary includes:

- begun in Paris in 1808;
- taken unfinished by David into exile;
- completed after ten months’ work in a room supplied by the Brussels municipal administration, 1822;
- unsuccessful effort to exhibit it in Brussels, May 1822;
- transported by Eugène David to London;
- exhibited at the Society of Painters in Oil and Watercolours, Pall Mall, November 1822–February 1823;
- exhibited by the Academy of Fine Arts at the New York Institution, Chambers Street, January–April 1826;
- Westminster Hall, Philadelphia, autumn 1826;
- Boston, April–September 1827;
- New York again, September–December 1827;
- back in Paris by 1833;
- entered Versailles in 1947;
- inaugurated in the Salle du Sacre on 14 June 1948.

The easily accessible Versailles record truncates the later private-provenance portion in search presentation, so the precise sequence of owners between the American exhibitions, the return to Paris, and state acquisition in 1947 is not fully reconstructed here. That is a gap in this dossier concerning **MV 7156**, not concerning the Louvre original. [Versailles collection record](https://collections.chateauversailles.fr/?permid=permobj_efb1c634-b95f-402f-8c6f-12848982844c); [Versailles historical presentation](https://www.chateauversailles.fr/napoleon-bonaparte)

### Attribution within the original

No serious dispute denies David’s authorship. The institutional attribution explicitly acknowledges Rouget and Degotti as participants. Because the canvas was a state-scale workshop production, authorship should not be reduced to the fiction that every passage was personally painted by David. Conversely, the documented use of assistants does not reduce it to “studio of David”: conception, control, major execution, signature, and contemporary reception all identify it as David’s work.

### Physical alterations versus substitution

The central action, Letizia’s presence, and the pope’s gesture were altered during production. These are documented revisions to the same canvas, not evidence of later substitution. The underlying self-coronation figure has art-historical importance, but the public source base consulted does not provide a complete radiographic map of the Louvre canvas.

### Forgeries and false claims

No forged full-size rival claiming to be the imperial commission is documented in the sources consulted. Numerous painted copies, prints, photographic reproductions, commercial posters, and digital images exist; resemblance alone does not confer identity. The commonest identity error is the innocent conflation of the Louvre original and the Versailles second version.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Makers and administrators

- **Jacques-Louis David (1748–1825):** principal artist; commissioned 1804; directed execution in 1805–1807; signed the canvas.
- **Georges Rouget (1783–1869):** documented collaborator on figures.
- **Ignazio Eugenio Degotti (1758–1824):** documented collaborator on architecture and perspective.
- **Potrelle:** supplied the gilt frame in 1807 for 1,110 francs.
- **Dominique-Vivant Denon:** director of the Musée Napoléon and central administrator of Napoleonic art policy; associated with the commission’s museum presentation.
- **Frédéric Villot:** Louvre curator who assigned INV 3699 between 1852 and 1860.
- **Louis-Philippe:** ordered the painting’s installation within the Versailles historical museum in the 1830s.

### Commissioner and principal subjects

- **Napoleon I:** commissioner, represented emperor, and active supervisor of the image. He required or approved changes that increased dynastic unanimity and papal participation.
- **Joséphine de Beauharnais:** represented at the principal narrative instant. Her kneeling coronation displaced Napoleon’s self-coronation as the finished subject.
- **Pope Pius VII:** represented blessing the action; his attendance furnished religious consecration while the composition kept Napoleon in control of the crown.
- **Letizia Ramolino Bonaparte:** painted in a place of honor despite her documented absence.
- **Joseph and Louis Bonaparte:** members of the represented imperial family.
- **Caroline Murat, Pauline Borghese, Élisa Baciocchi, Hortense de Beauharnais:** represented members of the dynastic court.
- **Eugène de Beauharnais:** represented in military dress near the clergy.
- **Joachim Murat:** represented as a high imperial dignitary.
- **Cambacérès, Lebrun, Berthier, Talleyrand, Caulaincourt, Bernadotte:** represented as holders or servants of state offices and regalia.

### Artistic network

- **François Gérard:** credited in the principal modern scholarly account with recommending the switch from self-coronation to Joséphine’s coronation.
- **Joseph-Marie Vien:** David’s former teacher, included in the assembly as an artistic homage.
- **David himself:** appears in the spectator gallery, drawing or recording the event. His self-inclusion asserts eyewitness and authorial authority.
- **Louis-Léopold Boilly:** painted the public viewing of the work at the Louvre in 1810. His picture documents the *Sacre* as a political and social spectacle rather than entering its custody chain. [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/438099)

### Institutions and state networks

- Imperial Household and its Intendance générale;
- Musée Napoléon/Musée du Louvre;
- Académie and Salon exhibition system;
- Musées royaux under the Bourbon restoration;
- Musée de l’Histoire de France at Versailles;
- the wartime evacuation network of French national collections.

### Divine and sacred associations

The painting contains Christian liturgical personnel, an altar, cross, clerical vestments, blessing, anointing context, and papal presence. It does not depict an apparition or claim divine manufacture. The implied divine association is institutional: the Catholic Church’s rites are presented as conferring sacred sanction upon the imperial office. God is not anthropomorphically represented as an acting figure.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### What the painting records

The historical ceremony combined:

1. anointing;
2. imperial crowning;
3. Joséphine’s crowning;
4. constitutional oath.

Napoleon had already become Emperor of the French through the sénatus-consulte of 18 May 1804. The Notre-Dame ceremony did not create his civil office. It added religious and historical legitimation, combining Roman-imperial, Carolingian, French-monarchical, Catholic, and post-Revolutionary constitutional forms. [Fondation Napoléon coronation dossier](https://www.napoleon.org/histoire-des-2-empires/dossiers-thematiques/le-sacre-de-napoleon-ier-le-2-decembre-1804-a-notre-dame/)

### What the painting was for

The canvas was not used in the coronation itself; it was commissioned beforehand but executed afterward as commemoration. Its functions were:

- monumental record;
- dynastic group portrait;
- imperial propaganda;
- political education;
- public spectacle;
- legitimation of the Bonaparte succession;
- representation of harmony between emperor, family, state, army, and Church.

Its visual argument is carefully balanced. Napoleon controls the crown, but Pius VII blesses. Joséphine kneels, but her coronation creates a dynasty-centered domestic focus. Revolutionary and imperial officeholders stand amid forms borrowed from older monarchy. The absent Letizia’s inclusion converts a divided family into an image of consent.

### Actual ceremonial and public uses

The object did not become a reusable component of coronation ritual. Its documented “ceremonial” life consists chiefly of state exhibition:

- independent imperial presentation in February–March 1808;
- Salon presentation in 1808;
- decennial-prize exhibition in 1810;
- historical-national display at Versailles from 1837;
- retrospective display at the 1889 Exposition Universelle;
- permanent canonical display at the Louvre after 1890.

Visitors’ removal of hats in Boilly’s 1810 painting may represent deference to the imperial image, ordinary indoor behavior, or improved visibility. The Met interprets the scene as evidence of the work’s highly political public reception; it is not evidence of a prescribed cultic ritual. [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/438099)

### Reproductive and emblematic function

David sold engraving rights connected with the composition in 1821 for a reported 15,000 francs. Prints allowed the image to circulate beyond the painting’s physical location. Reproduction detached the composition from its original scale and made it an emblem of the Napoleonic age.

The title *Le Sacre de Napoléon*, rather than the more literal “Coronation of Joséphine,” ensured that Napoleon remained the semantic center even though Joséphine’s coronation is the visible action.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### “Napoleon snatched the crown from the pope”

**Popular modern narrative:** Napoleon abruptly seized the crown from Pius VII and defiantly crowned himself.

**Assessment:** Napoleon did crown himself, but the act was planned within a negotiated ceremonial program; it was not an improvised wresting of the crown from the pope’s hands. David’s early design showed the self-coronation, while the finished painting shifted to Joséphine’s coronation. The “snatching” formulation is a dramatic later simplification.

### “David painted exactly what he saw”

**Imperial representation, not documentary fact.** David supplied individualized portraits and precise costumes, but the final image combines successive moments, rearranges the space, includes absentees, and reflects interventions after the event. The contemporary 1808 guide openly acknowledged that the painting united actions that occurred successively.

### Letizia’s balcony presence

**Documented pictorial fiction:** Napoleon’s mother appears at the center but did not attend. Her presence is not a later legend accidentally attached to the painting; it is deliberate imperial image-making built into the object.

### “The pope is merely a helpless spectator”

David’s design passed through a more passive papal conception, but the completed painting shows Pius VII blessing. Napoleon reportedly insisted that the pope should not appear to have come “to do nothing.” The finished gesture therefore advertises papal participation while maintaining the emperor’s dominance.

### “The Versailles painting is a fake”

**False.** It is a documented, authorized second version by David and his workshop, completed during his exile. It is later than the Louvre canvas but not counterfeit.

### “The painting was always in the Louvre”

**False.** It moved repeatedly among David’s Cluny studio, the Musée Napoléon, museum storage, Versailles, wartime refuges, and different Louvre galleries.

### “The canvas shows Napoleon’s coronation”

Only indirectly. Napoleon has already crowned himself in the narrative supplied by the 1808 description. The visible instant is his coronation of Joséphine. The conventional title compresses the whole ceremony into the depicted episode.

## 8. Current Status and Location

The original is in the French national collection and is catalogued by the Louvre as:

> INV 3699; former number MR 1437.

As of the Louvre’s current record, it is publicly visible in **room 702, Denon wing, level 1, Musée du Louvre, Paris**. Gallery access remains subject to museum opening conditions and operational closures. [Musée du Louvre collection record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010065720)

The painting is not recorded as stolen, missing, restituted, deaccessioned, privately owned, or subject to a current repatriation claim. Its passage from imperial to royal and then national-museum administration reflects French regime change rather than an international ownership transfer.

No present authenticity controversy affects INV 3699. No currently active legal custody dispute was found.

The second version, MV 7156, remains in the **Salle du Sacre, Château de Versailles**. That room takes its modern name from the composition and also displays David’s *Distribution of the Eagles* and other Napoleonic works. [Salle du Sacre, Versailles](https://www.chateauversailles.fr/decouvrir/domaine/chateau/salle-sacre)

### Conservation status

The original is sufficiently stable for monumental permanent display. Open institutional material reviewed for this dossier does not disclose a full recent conservation report. In particular, the following remain unestablished here:

- exact date and scope of the last structural treatment;
- lining or relining history;
- complete pigment and binder analyses;
- present stretcher date;
- quantified paint loss or retouching;
- whether the surviving Potrelle frame is entirely original or incorporates replacements.

These are unresolved documentary questions, not evidence of poor condition or undisclosed substitution.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Contemporary reception

The first public display was itself an orchestrated event. The printed identification guide encouraged viewers to read the work as a vast collective portrait. Boilly’s *The Public Viewing David’s “Coronation” at the Louvre* (1810) shows a mixed public gathered before it, some consulting a guidebook. The Met describes these displays as political spectacles endorsing Napoleon’s claim to power. [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/438099)

David’s son and biographer Jules-Louis David later devoted an entire chapter to the painting in *Le peintre Louis David: souvenirs et documents inédits* (1880), preserving documents but also helping establish a familial heroic narrative around its making. [Digitized 1880 volume](https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/david1880bd1/0421)

### Nineteenth-century reframing

Under Louis-Philippe, the painting ceased to function solely as Bonapartist state propaganda and became part of a reconciliatory national museum “dedicated to all the glories of France.” That institutional reframing helped canonize Napoleonic history without restoring the Napoleonic regime.

The 1889 centennial exhibition placed it within a lineage of French national art. Its permanent transfer to the Louvre in 1890 further shifted its primary identity from dynastic monument to canonical masterpiece.

### Replicas and reproductions

The second full-size canvas had an independent commercial life in London and the United States. Its exhibition circuit turned a French imperial image into a transatlantic spectacle. Prints, engravings, textbook illustrations, postcards, posters, puzzles, and digital reproductions have since made the composition far more familiar than either enormous physical canvas.

The reproduction history creates recurrent confusion:

- cropping often suppresses the architectural and spectator zones;
- captions frequently say Napoleon is being crowned by the pope;
- viewers at Versailles often assume they are seeing the Louvre canvas;
- photographic color differences are sometimes misread as evidence of radically different paintings.

### Film and digital interpretation

The painting has become a standard visual shorthand for Napoleon in documentaries, historical publications, museum media, and screen histories. The 2004 documentary *Napoléon-David: le sacre de l’image* used the painting to examine how David converted political ceremony into historical memory. [Centre national du cinéma catalogue](https://imagesdelaculture.cnc.fr/-/napoleon-david-le-sacre-de-l-image)

The Louvre now presents the work through digital video and high-resolution detail, emphasizing the 191 figures and the tension between portrait fidelity and reconstructed event. Versailles likewise uses high-resolution digitization to interpret its second version.

### Political afterlife

The image has served mutually incompatible readings:

- celebration of imperial sovereignty;
- evidence of Napoleon subordinating the Church;
- proof of reconciliation between revolutionary France and Catholic ritual;
- critique of authoritarian propaganda;
- national-historical memory detached from present Bonapartism.

These are receptions of the image. They do not change the original canvas’s custody or make it a ritual relic.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Principal documentary foundation

The most important evidence classes are:

- imperial commission and payment records;
- David’s report of 10 June 1806 concerning the projected series;
- the 1808 printed *Description du tableau*;
- Salon catalogues;
- royal and Louvre inventories MR 1437 and INV 3699;
- movement and evacuation records;
- preparatory drawings and portrait studies;
- David-family correspondence and memoir material;
- the two surviving full-size canvases.

The Louvre’s modern online record is exceptionally detailed on payments and movements and should take precedence over unsourced summaries.

### Major scholarly questions

#### 1. What is the correct subject?

The conventional title centers Napoleon’s sacre; the visible action is Joséphine’s coronation. Scholars treat that mismatch as fundamental to the painting’s political operation rather than a cataloguing error.

#### 2. Why did David abandon the self-coronation?

The Gérard explanation is persuasive and widely accepted: Joséphine’s coronation reduced the appearance of arrogance and gave Napoleon a chivalric role. Imperial intervention and compositional necessity probably also mattered. No single surviving order fully accounts for every stage.

#### 3. How documentary is the work?

Portrait likenesses, dress, insignia, and ceremonial furnishings were intensively researched. The temporal, spatial, and attendance record was nonetheless manipulated. The useful distinction is not “truth versus falsehood” but **documented details arranged into an official fiction**.

#### 4. Who painted which passages?

David’s authorship is secure, but Rouget and Degotti’s participation is explicit. A brushstroke-by-brushstroke allocation has not been established in the open sources reviewed.

#### 5. What is the status of the Versailles version?

Earlier writing sometimes treated replicas as automatically inferior. Current institutional treatment recognizes MV 7156 as an independent, authentic David-workshop version with its own history. Its differences are evidence of renewed execution, not necessarily errors.

#### 6. Did Napoleon commission the original specifically for the Tuileries?

The Louvre’s detailed record says the September 1804 commission named no precise destination. The Tuileries Guards’ Room was proposed later, in 1811. Sources describing it as the original fixed destination compress later planning into the initial commission.

### Major modern publications and exhibitions

- Sylvain Laveissière and collaborators, *Le Sacre de Napoléon peint par David*, Louvre/5 Continents, 2004.
- Jean Tulard, *Le Sacre de l’empereur Napoléon: histoire et légende*, Fayard/RMN, 2004.
- Antoine Schnapper’s catalogue raisonné work on David remains important for attribution and corpus placement.
- The Louvre’s 2004 exhibition gathered preparatory studies and ceremonial material around the canvas.
- Technical imaging of the Philadelphia double portrait revealed a painted *Sacre* composition underneath, materially advancing understanding of the design sequence. [Fondation Napoléon preparatory-study account](https://www.napoleon.org/histoire-des-2-empires/tableaux/le-sacre-ou-le-couronnement-dessin-preparatoire-david/)

### Limits of the accessible record

A truly exhaustive material dossier would require consultation of:

- Louvre conservation files;
- Centre de recherche et de restauration des musées de France imaging and treatment records;
- Archives nationales files of the Maison de l’Empereur;
- complete Salon criticism from 1808 and 1810;
- transport and wartime convoy records;
- Versailles acquisition papers for MV 7156;
- the full 2004 Louvre exhibition catalogue.

Those records were not all available in openly searchable full text. Consequently, unsupported claims about pigments, lining, cleaning, tears, structural interventions, and stretcher replacement have been excluded.

# “Le Sacre de Napoleon” (David's painting): Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier tracks the monumental canvas painted under Jacques-Louis David’s direction in Paris in 1805–1807, now in the Musée du Louvre under inventory number **INV 3699**. It distinguishes that original from:

1. David’s second, full-size version, begun in Paris in 1808 and completed in Brussels in 1822, now at Versailles (**MV 7156**);
2. preparatory drawings, portrait studies, engravings, and reduced copies;
3. the historical coronation ceremony represented in the painting.

The work is “sacred” only in the sense that it depicts a religious consecration and deploys Christian ritual to legitimate sovereignty. It is not itself documented as a relic, cult object, or object of supernatural power. Its legendary aspect lies instead in its transformation of a contested political ceremony into an authoritative national image.

Evidence labels used below:

- **Documented:** supported by contemporary records or institutional object documentation.
- **Physical evidence:** visible inscription, technical examination, or material observation.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** an interpretation based on surviving records or images.
- **Imperial representation:** an officially sponsored claim embedded in the painting.
- **Tradition/anecdote:** repeated in biographical literature but not independently secured here.
- **Unresolved:** the available published evidence does not establish the matter.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Principal object

**Current official title:** *Le Couronnement de l’empereur Napoléon Ier et de l’impératrice Joséphine dans la cathédrale Notre-Dame de Paris, le 2 décembre 1804*.

**Common titles:**

- *Le Sacre de Napoléon*
- *Le Sacre de l’empereur Napoléon Ier*
- *Le Couronnement*
- English: *The Coronation of Napoleon* or *The Consecration of the Emperor Napoleon I and the Coronation of the Empress Joséphine*
- German: *Die Krönung Napoleons*
- Italian: *L’incoronazione di Napoleone*
- Spanish: *La consagración de Napoleón* or *La coronación de Napoleón*

The short French title is potentially misleading. *Sacre* derives from *sacrer*, “to consecrate,” and ordinarily denotes a monarch’s religious consecration, especially by anointing. David’s finished composition does not show Napoleon’s anointing or his act of crowning himself. It shows Napoleon preparing to place a crown on Joséphine. The Louvre therefore catalogues it under the fuller, more descriptive title while retaining *Le Sacre de l’empereur Napoléon Ier* as an alternative title. [Musée du Louvre collection record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010065720)

### Classification and catalogue identity

- **Class:** monumental history painting; dynastic state image; collective portrait; official commemoration.
- **Artist:** Jacques-Louis David (1748–1825).
- **Recorded collaborators:** Georges Rouget, principally on figures, and Ignazio Eugenio Degotti, principally on architecture and perspective.
- **Date:** 1806–1807 inscribed; begun 21 December 1805, substantially finished by mid-November 1807, with alterations in January 1808.
- **Medium:** oil on canvas.
- **Dimensions:** 6.21 m high × 9.79 m wide.
- **Current inventory:** INV 3699.
- **Earlier inventory:** MR 1437.
- **Department:** Département des Peintures, Musée du Louvre.
- **Current location:** Louvre, Denon wing, level 1, room 702, according to the current catalogue. [Musée du Louvre collection record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010065720)

The Louvre describes it as its second-largest painting after Veronese’s *Wedding Feast at Cana*. A printed description issued for its first exhibition in 1808 called it thirty feet wide and nineteen feet high and claimed more than two hundred life-size figures. The Louvre’s recent public interpretation counts **191 represented persons**. The discrepancy reflects different historical counting practices rather than evidence that figures were removed. [Louvre presentation](https://www.louvre.fr/louvreplus/video-au-louvre-le-sacre-de-napoleon)

### Inscriptions

**Lower right:**

> L. David. f.ebat.

This is a Latinizing artist’s formula, approximately “L. David was making [this].”

**Lower left:**

> 1806 & 1807.

The inscription records the main years of execution, although documentary evidence places the first work on the canvas in December 1805 and final revisions in January 1808. [Musée du Louvre collection record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010065720)

### Composition and depicted objects

The setting is the choir of Notre-Dame de Paris as transformed for the ceremony of 2 December 1804. Napoleon stands on the altar steps in a white satin tunic and crimson velvet mantle ornamented with golden bees. Holding Joséphine’s crown in both hands, he prepares to lower it onto her head. Joséphine kneels on a violet velvet cushion. Her train is carried by Adélaïde de La Rochefoucauld and Émilie de La Valette.

Behind Napoleon sits Pope Pius VII, his right hand raised in blessing. Cardinal Giovanni Battista Caprara and Cardinal Giovanni Braschi-Onesti are among the clergy. Imperial dignitaries hold insignia: Charles-François Lebrun carries an eagle-topped staff; Jean-Jacques-Régis de Cambacérès holds the hand of justice; Louis-Alexandre Berthier carries the orb; Talleyrand appears in his office as grand chamberlain. Members of the Bonaparte and Beauharnais families, court officers, diplomats, clergy, and spectators occupy the surrounding tiers.

At the compositional center above Joséphine sits Napoleon’s mother, Letizia Bonaparte. **Documented discrepancy:** she did not attend the ceremony. Her painted presence converts absence and family conflict into an image of dynastic unanimity. Other spatial arrangements were likewise compressed or rearranged. The Louvre stresses that the apparent documentary fidelity of faces and costumes conceals a deliberate reconstruction: David assembled participants on one side to make the action legible and included absent persons on imperial instructions. [Louvre presentation](https://www.louvre.fr/louvreplus/video-au-louvre-le-sacre-de-napoleon)

### Material construction and condition

The published catalogue identifies oil on canvas but does not provide, in its online record, a complete support analysis: number and orientation of canvas pieces, fiber identification, original priming, stretcher chronology, pigment analysis, lining history, or a condition map. Those details should not be invented from the work’s dimensions.

The canvas is currently presented in a monumental gilded frame. A gilt frame made by **Potrelle** was supplied on 2 December 1807 for **1,110 francs**. Whether every component of the presently visible framing system is that original 1807 fabric has not been established in the public sources consulted. The painting’s enormous size necessarily makes its support, tension, transport, and installation technically consequential, but a complete published conservation dossier was not located in open-access form.

No mutilation, theft, loss of a section, or deliberate iconoclastic attack on the Louvre canvas is documented in the sources consulted. Its repeated rolling, transport, storage, evacuation, rehanging, and climatic exposure are nevertheless essential parts of its material biography.

### Related version: Versailles, MV 7156

David received permission to make two replicas. The only completed full-size second version was begun in 1808, taken into exile, and finished at Brussels in 1822. It is oil on canvas, approximately 6.10 × 9.71 m, and is now displayed in the Salle du Sacre at Versailles as **MV 7156**.

It is not a forgery or a later museum facsimile. It is an authorized second version produced by David and assistants. Small differences distinguish it from the Louvre original; the most frequently noted is the pink rather than white dress of one of Napoleon’s sisters. Its long exhibition history has caused it to be casually called “the original at Versailles,” but the 1805–1807 commission canvas is the Louvre work. [Versailles collection record](https://collections.chateauversailles.fr/?permid=permobj_efb1c634-b95f-402f-8c6f-12848982844c)

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Commission

**Documented:** Napoleon commissioned David in September 1804, approximately three months before the coronation festivities, to commemorate four events:

1. the coronation;
2. the army’s oath following the distribution of the eagles;
3. Napoleon’s enthronement in Notre-Dame;
4. the imperial couple’s arrival at the Hôtel de Ville.

No price or final architectural destination was fixed at the outset. Of the four projected monumental canvases, David completed only the *Coronation* and the *Distribution of the Eagles*. The Louvre’s record accordingly corrects simplified accounts saying that the painting was from the outset definitively commissioned for the Tuileries Guards’ Room. That room was later considered, but the 1804 commission itself had no settled destination. [Musée du Louvre collection record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010065720)

David was appointed *Premier peintre de l’Empereur*. The commission made him both witness and image-maker of the new dynasty. He attended the ceremony and used sittings, portrait studies, costume records, architectural observation, and ceremonial documentation to construct the painting.

### Workshop and execution

Work on the final canvas began on **21 December 1805** in a studio installed in the disused chapel of the former Collège de Cluny, near the Place de la Sorbonne. The dimensions required a building-scale workspace rather than a conventional painter’s room.

The Louvre records the division of assistance as:

- **David:** design, direction, principal authorship, and substantial execution;
- **Georges Rouget:** assistance with figures;
- **Ignazio Eugenio Degotti:** assistance with architectural setting and perspective.

The painting was substantially completed by mid-November 1807. Minor but significant alterations were made in January 1808. Its signed dates, “1806 & 1807,” refer to the central execution period rather than the entire production process.

### Payment

David valued the work at **100,000 francs** in June 1806. The imperial household paid **65,000 francs**, in three installments:

- 25,000 francs in June 1805;
- 15,000 francs in April 1806;
- 25,000 francs in August 1807.

The first payment predates the recorded start of work on the full canvas and therefore relates to the commission and preparatory labor. The difference between David’s valuation and the amount paid records a real financial negotiation, not an uncertain modern estimate. [Musée du Louvre collection record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010065720)

### Change of subject during manufacture

Several preparatory compositions show Napoleon raising a crown toward his own head. David initially intended to represent the self-coronation, an event that actually occurred. The final canvas instead depicts the coronation of Joséphine.

Sylvain Laveissière attributes the decisive suggestion to François Gérard, who reportedly warned that the self-crowning pose would appear arrogant or provocative. David revised the central action after it had already progressed substantially. Napoleon later interpreted the alteration as an “intrigue” between Joséphine and David but approved its chivalric effect. This explanatory dialogue survives through later David literature and should be treated as a **scholarly reconstruction grounded partly in workshop recollection**, not as verbatim stenography of a securely preserved meeting. [Fondation Napoléon analysis](https://fondationnapoleon.org/chef-d-oeuvre/le-sacre-ou-le-couronnement/)

The change had structural consequences: Joséphine became the devotional and emotional center, while Napoleon retained agency as the source of the crown. The painting could thus commemorate his own sacre and self-coronation without directly showing the gesture most likely to be read as defiance of papal authority.

### Other alterations

- **Letizia Bonaparte:** added prominently despite her absence from the actual ceremony, reportedly at Napoleon’s insistence.
- **Pius VII:** David considered or painted a more passive papal pose. Napoleon required the pope to be shown actively blessing, producing the raised hand visible in the completed work.
- **Architecture and assembly:** persons were relocated and Notre-Dame’s choir was optically reorganized to make a coherent theater of power.
- **Napoleon’s first pose:** the original self-coronation conception was painted out in favor of the coronation of Joséphine.

A painted compositional study was later detected beneath David’s 1808 double portrait of Pius VII and Cardinal Caprara in the Philadelphia Museum of Art. Radiography and infrared examination of that separate panel disclosed a stage of the *Sacre* design and corroborated the evolution visible in drawings. It is evidence about David’s design process, not a technical examination of the Louvre canvas itself. [Fondation Napoléon preparatory-study account](https://www.napoleon.org/histoire-des-2-empires/tableaux/le-sacre-ou-le-couronnement-dessin-preparatoire-david/)

### Earliest public attestation

The completed canvas was transported to the Musée Napoléon at the beginning of February 1808. It opened to public viewing in the Salon Carré on **7 February 1808**. A descriptive guide, *Description du tableau exposé au musée Napoléon, représentant le couronnement de Leurs Majestés impériales et royales, peint par M. David*, was published by Labarre in two editions that year.

The description explains the deliberately composite instant: Napoleon has already placed two crowns successively on his own head, removed the second, and now raises it over Joséphine. This contemporary text is unusually valuable because it records how the image was officially explained at its first showing. The Louvre reproduces a substantial extract in its catalogue. [Musée du Louvre collection record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010065720)

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Ownership and custody require distinction. The imperial household paid for the work, but the canvas repeatedly returned to David’s studio and did not enter permanent museum custody immediately.

### 1804–1808: commission and workshop custody

- **September 1804:** commissioned by Napoleon as one of four commemorative canvases.
- **21 December 1805–mid-November 1807:** executed in the former Collège de Cluny chapel.
- **January 1808:** final alterations.
- **Early February 1808:** moved to the Musée Napoléon.

### 1808: first public exhibition

- **7 February–21 March 1808:** exhibited independently in the Salon Carré.
- **14 October 1808–early January 1809:** exhibited in the Salon of 1808 as catalogue no. 144.
- Contemporary spectators could use printed descriptions to identify the vast assembly of figures.

### 1809–1811: oscillation between studio and museum

- **1809:** returned to David’s Cluny studio.
- **25 August–October 1810:** returned to the Musée Napoléon for the exhibition connected with the decennial prizes.
- **April 1811:** considered for installation in the Guards’ Room at the Tuileries Palace; the plan was abandoned.
- **Spring 1811:** returned to David’s studio, where it could be seen by visitors on request.

The political difficulty of installing a giant image centered on Joséphine increased after Napoleon divorced her and married Marie-Louise of Austria in 1810. This is a strong contextual explanation for the abandoned palace installation, but it should not replace the documentary record with a single presumed motive.

### 1815–1819: Restoration and transfer into royal museum custody

David went into exile in Brussels after the Bourbon restoration. The painting remained in Paris. At the end of 1819 it was removed from the Cluny premises and placed in the stores of the Musée royal des Arts at the Louvre.

Between 1816 and 1824 it was entered in the royal museum inventory as **MR 1437**, valued at **80,000 francs**. That registration confirmed it as Crown or royal-museum property rather than part of David’s freely disposable exile estate. No sale, inheritance, confiscatory auction, or restitution claim involving the original is recorded.

### 1836–1889: Versailles

Around 1836 the painting was moved to Versailles for Louis-Philippe’s new historical galleries. It was installed in the Salle du Sacre and became publicly visible when the Musée de l’Histoire de France opened on **10 June 1837**.

Between 1852 and 1860, Louvre curator Frédéric Villot assigned the modern inventory number **INV 3699**.

During the Franco-Prussian War and Paris Commune crisis, the canvas was evacuated from Versailles to the Louvre for safety on **14 September 1870**. It remained there until December 1871, then returned to Versailles. The source documents an evacuation, not enemy seizure or theft.

### 1889–1939: transfer back to Paris

In 1889 the painting was removed from Versailles and shown in the **Exposition centennale de l’art français**, part of the Paris Exposition Universelle, open 5 May–31 October.

Beginning in August 1890 it was installed at the Louvre in the Salle des Sept-Cheminées. The original did not thereafter return to permanent display at Versailles; the palace ultimately substituted David’s second version.

### 1939–1946: wartime evacuation

The painting left public display in September 1939. The Louvre’s record gives the following sequence of refuges:

1. **Château de Chèreperrine**, Origny-le-Roux, Orne;
2. **Château de Sourches**, Saint-Symphorien, Sarthe;
3. **Château de Montal**, Saint-Jean-Lespinasse, Lot.

It returned from Montal to the Louvre on **7 February 1946**. No wartime seizure, Nazi transfer, loss, or restitution proceeding is recorded for it.

### 1949–present: Louvre display history

- **October 1949–1969:** Salle Daru.
- **1969–1994:** Salle Mollien.
- **Since 1994:** Salle Daru.
- **Current catalogue location:** room 702, Denon wing, level 1.

The complete official chain is provided in the Louvre’s current catalogue record. [Musée du Louvre collection record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010065720)

### Chain-of-custody assessment

The original’s chain is unusually continuous. There is no documented disappearance, theft, private-market transfer, colonial seizure, contested inheritance, or provenance gap. The principal ambiguity concerns legal possession between imperial payment and final museum delivery: the canvas was state-sponsored and paid for, but it remained in David’s working custody for long periods until 1819.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is the Louvre canvas the commissioned original?

**Yes, to the standard of documentary and physical art-historical identification.** Its signatures and dates, exhibition history, imperial payment record, movements, royal inventory MR 1437, and later Louvre inventory INV 3699 form a continuous documentary chain. No rival object has a credible claim to being the 1805–1807 commission canvas.

### The Versailles “replica”

The Versailles canvas is an authentic work from David’s enterprise but is not the first canvas. It was authorized, begun in 1808, completed under David in 1822, and subsequently exhibited commercially. Calling it a “copy” is accurate in sequence but can obscure its authorial status; calling it another “original” obscures the historically prior Louvre commission. “Authorized second version” or “full-size replica by David and his workshop” is more precise.

Its documented itinerary includes:

- begun in Paris in 1808;
- taken unfinished by David into exile;
- completed after ten months’ work in a room supplied by the Brussels municipal administration, 1822;
- unsuccessful effort to exhibit it in Brussels, May 1822;
- transported by Eugène David to London;
- exhibited at the Society of Painters in Oil and Watercolours, Pall Mall, November 1822–February 1823;
- exhibited by the Academy of Fine Arts at the New York Institution, Chambers Street, January–April 1826;
- Westminster Hall, Philadelphia, autumn 1826;
- Boston, April–September 1827;
- New York again, September–December 1827;
- back in Paris by 1833;
- entered Versailles in 1947;
- inaugurated in the Salle du Sacre on 14 June 1948.

The easily accessible Versailles record truncates the later private-provenance portion in search presentation, so the precise sequence of owners between the American exhibitions, the return to Paris, and state acquisition in 1947 is not fully reconstructed here. That is a gap in this dossier concerning **MV 7156**, not concerning the Louvre original. [Versailles collection record](https://collections.chateauversailles.fr/?permid=permobj_efb1c634-b95f-402f-8c6f-12848982844c); [Versailles historical presentation](https://www.chateauversailles.fr/napoleon-bonaparte)

### Attribution within the original

No serious dispute denies David’s authorship. The institutional attribution explicitly acknowledges Rouget and Degotti as participants. Because the canvas was a state-scale workshop production, authorship should not be reduced to the fiction that every passage was personally painted by David. Conversely, the documented use of assistants does not reduce it to “studio of David”: conception, control, major execution, signature, and contemporary reception all identify it as David’s work.

### Physical alterations versus substitution

The central action, Letizia’s presence, and the pope’s gesture were altered during production. These are documented revisions to the same canvas, not evidence of later substitution. The underlying self-coronation figure has art-historical importance, but the public source base consulted does not provide a complete radiographic map of the Louvre canvas.

### Forgeries and false claims

No forged full-size rival claiming to be the imperial commission is documented in the sources consulted. Numerous painted copies, prints, photographic reproductions, commercial posters, and digital images exist; resemblance alone does not confer identity. The commonest identity error is the innocent conflation of the Louvre original and the Versailles second version.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Makers and administrators

- **Jacques-Louis David (1748–1825):** principal artist; commissioned 1804; directed execution in 1805–1807; signed the canvas.
- **Georges Rouget (1783–1869):** documented collaborator on figures.
- **Ignazio Eugenio Degotti (1758–1824):** documented collaborator on architecture and perspective.
- **Potrelle:** supplied the gilt frame in 1807 for 1,110 francs.
- **Dominique-Vivant Denon:** director of the Musée Napoléon and central administrator of Napoleonic art policy; associated with the commission’s museum presentation.
- **Frédéric Villot:** Louvre curator who assigned INV 3699 between 1852 and 1860.
- **Louis-Philippe:** ordered the painting’s installation within the Versailles historical museum in the 1830s.

### Commissioner and principal subjects

- **Napoleon I:** commissioner, represented emperor, and active supervisor of the image. He required or approved changes that increased dynastic unanimity and papal participation.
- **Joséphine de Beauharnais:** represented at the principal narrative instant. Her kneeling coronation displaced Napoleon’s self-coronation as the finished subject.
- **Pope Pius VII:** represented blessing the action; his attendance furnished religious consecration while the composition kept Napoleon in control of the crown.
- **Letizia Ramolino Bonaparte:** painted in a place of honor despite her documented absence.
- **Joseph and Louis Bonaparte:** members of the represented imperial family.
- **Caroline Murat, Pauline Borghese, Élisa Baciocchi, Hortense de Beauharnais:** represented members of the dynastic court.
- **Eugène de Beauharnais:** represented in military dress near the clergy.
- **Joachim Murat:** represented as a high imperial dignitary.
- **Cambacérès, Lebrun, Berthier, Talleyrand, Caulaincourt, Bernadotte:** represented as holders or servants of state offices and regalia.

### Artistic network

- **François Gérard:** credited in the principal modern scholarly account with recommending the switch from self-coronation to Joséphine’s coronation.
- **Joseph-Marie Vien:** David’s former teacher, included in the assembly as an artistic homage.
- **David himself:** appears in the spectator gallery, drawing or recording the event. His self-inclusion asserts eyewitness and authorial authority.
- **Louis-Léopold Boilly:** painted the public viewing of the work at the Louvre in 1810. His picture documents the *Sacre* as a political and social spectacle rather than entering its custody chain. [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/438099)

### Institutions and state networks

- Imperial Household and its Intendance générale;
- Musée Napoléon/Musée du Louvre;
- Académie and Salon exhibition system;
- Musées royaux under the Bourbon restoration;
- Musée de l’Histoire de France at Versailles;
- the wartime evacuation network of French national collections.

### Divine and sacred associations

The painting contains Christian liturgical personnel, an altar, cross, clerical vestments, blessing, anointing context, and papal presence. It does not depict an apparition or claim divine manufacture. The implied divine association is institutional: the Catholic Church’s rites are presented as conferring sacred sanction upon the imperial office. God is not anthropomorphically represented as an acting figure.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### What the painting records

The historical ceremony combined:

1. anointing;
2. imperial crowning;
3. Joséphine’s crowning;
4. constitutional oath.

Napoleon had already become Emperor of the French through the sénatus-consulte of 18 May 1804. The Notre-Dame ceremony did not create his civil office. It added religious and historical legitimation, combining Roman-imperial, Carolingian, French-monarchical, Catholic, and post-Revolutionary constitutional forms. [Fondation Napoléon coronation dossier](https://www.napoleon.org/histoire-des-2-empires/dossiers-thematiques/le-sacre-de-napoleon-ier-le-2-decembre-1804-a-notre-dame/)

### What the painting was for

The canvas was not used in the coronation itself; it was commissioned beforehand but executed afterward as commemoration. Its functions were:

- monumental record;
- dynastic group portrait;
- imperial propaganda;
- political education;
- public spectacle;
- legitimation of the Bonaparte succession;
- representation of harmony between emperor, family, state, army, and Church.

Its visual argument is carefully balanced. Napoleon controls the crown, but Pius VII blesses. Joséphine kneels, but her coronation creates a dynasty-centered domestic focus. Revolutionary and imperial officeholders stand amid forms borrowed from older monarchy. The absent Letizia’s inclusion converts a divided family into an image of consent.

### Actual ceremonial and public uses

The object did not become a reusable component of coronation ritual. Its documented “ceremonial” life consists chiefly of state exhibition:

- independent imperial presentation in February–March 1808;
- Salon presentation in 1808;
- decennial-prize exhibition in 1810;
- historical-national display at Versailles from 1837;
- retrospective display at the 1889 Exposition Universelle;
- permanent canonical display at the Louvre after 1890.

Visitors’ removal of hats in Boilly’s 1810 painting may represent deference to the imperial image, ordinary indoor behavior, or improved visibility. The Met interprets the scene as evidence of the work’s highly political public reception; it is not evidence of a prescribed cultic ritual. [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/438099)

### Reproductive and emblematic function

David sold engraving rights connected with the composition in 1821 for a reported 15,000 francs. Prints allowed the image to circulate beyond the painting’s physical location. Reproduction detached the composition from its original scale and made it an emblem of the Napoleonic age.

The title *Le Sacre de Napoléon*, rather than the more literal “Coronation of Joséphine,” ensured that Napoleon remained the semantic center even though Joséphine’s coronation is the visible action.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### “Napoleon snatched the crown from the pope”

**Popular modern narrative:** Napoleon abruptly seized the crown from Pius VII and defiantly crowned himself.

**Assessment:** Napoleon did crown himself, but the act was planned within a negotiated ceremonial program; it was not an improvised wresting of the crown from the pope’s hands. David’s early design showed the self-coronation, while the finished painting shifted to Joséphine’s coronation. The “snatching” formulation is a dramatic later simplification.

### “David painted exactly what he saw”

**Imperial representation, not documentary fact.** David supplied individualized portraits and precise costumes, but the final image combines successive moments, rearranges the space, includes absentees, and reflects interventions after the event. The contemporary 1808 guide openly acknowledged that the painting united actions that occurred successively.

### Letizia’s balcony presence

**Documented pictorial fiction:** Napoleon’s mother appears at the center but did not attend. Her presence is not a later legend accidentally attached to the painting; it is deliberate imperial image-making built into the object.

### “The pope is merely a helpless spectator”

David’s design passed through a more passive papal conception, but the completed painting shows Pius VII blessing. Napoleon reportedly insisted that the pope should not appear to have come “to do nothing.” The finished gesture therefore advertises papal participation while maintaining the emperor’s dominance.

### “The Versailles painting is a fake”

**False.** It is a documented, authorized second version by David and his workshop, completed during his exile. It is later than the Louvre canvas but not counterfeit.

### “The painting was always in the Louvre”

**False.** It moved repeatedly among David’s Cluny studio, the Musée Napoléon, museum storage, Versailles, wartime refuges, and different Louvre galleries.

### “The canvas shows Napoleon’s coronation”

Only indirectly. Napoleon has already crowned himself in the narrative supplied by the 1808 description. The visible instant is his coronation of Joséphine. The conventional title compresses the whole ceremony into the depicted episode.

## 8. Current Status and Location

The original is in the French national collection and is catalogued by the Louvre as:

> INV 3699; former number MR 1437.

As of the Louvre’s current record, it is publicly visible in **room 702, Denon wing, level 1, Musée du Louvre, Paris**. Gallery access remains subject to museum opening conditions and operational closures. [Musée du Louvre collection record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010065720)

The painting is not recorded as stolen, missing, restituted, deaccessioned, privately owned, or subject to a current repatriation claim. Its passage from imperial to royal and then national-museum administration reflects French regime change rather than an international ownership transfer.

No present authenticity controversy affects INV 3699. No currently active legal custody dispute was found.

The second version, MV 7156, remains in the **Salle du Sacre, Château de Versailles**. That room takes its modern name from the composition and also displays David’s *Distribution of the Eagles* and other Napoleonic works. [Salle du Sacre, Versailles](https://www.chateauversailles.fr/decouvrir/domaine/chateau/salle-sacre)

### Conservation status

The original is sufficiently stable for monumental permanent display. Open institutional material reviewed for this dossier does not disclose a full recent conservation report. In particular, the following remain unestablished here:

- exact date and scope of the last structural treatment;
- lining or relining history;
- complete pigment and binder analyses;
- present stretcher date;
- quantified paint loss or retouching;
- whether the surviving Potrelle frame is entirely original or incorporates replacements.

These are unresolved documentary questions, not evidence of poor condition or undisclosed substitution.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Contemporary reception

The first public display was itself an orchestrated event. The printed identification guide encouraged viewers to read the work as a vast collective portrait. Boilly’s *The Public Viewing David’s “Coronation” at the Louvre* (1810) shows a mixed public gathered before it, some consulting a guidebook. The Met describes these displays as political spectacles endorsing Napoleon’s claim to power. [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/438099)

David’s son and biographer Jules-Louis David later devoted an entire chapter to the painting in *Le peintre Louis David: souvenirs et documents inédits* (1880), preserving documents but also helping establish a familial heroic narrative around its making. [Digitized 1880 volume](https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/david1880bd1/0421)

### Nineteenth-century reframing

Under Louis-Philippe, the painting ceased to function solely as Bonapartist state propaganda and became part of a reconciliatory national museum “dedicated to all the glories of France.” That institutional reframing helped canonize Napoleonic history without restoring the Napoleonic regime.

The 1889 centennial exhibition placed it within a lineage of French national art. Its permanent transfer to the Louvre in 1890 further shifted its primary identity from dynastic monument to canonical masterpiece.

### Replicas and reproductions

The second full-size canvas had an independent commercial life in London and the United States. Its exhibition circuit turned a French imperial image into a transatlantic spectacle. Prints, engravings, textbook illustrations, postcards, posters, puzzles, and digital reproductions have since made the composition far more familiar than either enormous physical canvas.

The reproduction history creates recurrent confusion:

- cropping often suppresses the architectural and spectator zones;
- captions frequently say Napoleon is being crowned by the pope;
- viewers at Versailles often assume they are seeing the Louvre canvas;
- photographic color differences are sometimes misread as evidence of radically different paintings.

### Film and digital interpretation

The painting has become a standard visual shorthand for Napoleon in documentaries, historical publications, museum media, and screen histories. The 2004 documentary *Napoléon-David: le sacre de l’image* used the painting to examine how David converted political ceremony into historical memory. [Centre national du cinéma catalogue](https://imagesdelaculture.cnc.fr/-/napoleon-david-le-sacre-de-l-image)

The Louvre now presents the work through digital video and high-resolution detail, emphasizing the 191 figures and the tension between portrait fidelity and reconstructed event. Versailles likewise uses high-resolution digitization to interpret its second version.

### Political afterlife

The image has served mutually incompatible readings:

- celebration of imperial sovereignty;
- evidence of Napoleon subordinating the Church;
- proof of reconciliation between revolutionary France and Catholic ritual;
- critique of authoritarian propaganda;
- national-historical memory detached from present Bonapartism.

These are receptions of the image. They do not change the original canvas’s custody or make it a ritual relic.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Principal documentary foundation

The most important evidence classes are:

- imperial commission and payment records;
- David’s report of 10 June 1806 concerning the projected series;
- the 1808 printed *Description du tableau*;
- Salon catalogues;
- royal and Louvre inventories MR 1437 and INV 3699;
- movement and evacuation records;
- preparatory drawings and portrait studies;
- David-family correspondence and memoir material;
- the two surviving full-size canvases.

The Louvre’s modern online record is exceptionally detailed on payments and movements and should take precedence over unsourced summaries.

### Major scholarly questions

#### 1. What is the correct subject?

The conventional title centers Napoleon’s sacre; the visible action is Joséphine’s coronation. Scholars treat that mismatch as fundamental to the painting’s political operation rather than a cataloguing error.

#### 2. Why did David abandon the self-coronation?

The Gérard explanation is persuasive and widely accepted: Joséphine’s coronation reduced the appearance of arrogance and gave Napoleon a chivalric role. Imperial intervention and compositional necessity probably also mattered. No single surviving order fully accounts for every stage.

#### 3. How documentary is the work?

Portrait likenesses, dress, insignia, and ceremonial furnishings were intensively researched. The temporal, spatial, and attendance record was nonetheless manipulated. The useful distinction is not “truth versus falsehood” but **documented details arranged into an official fiction**.

#### 4. Who painted which passages?

David’s authorship is secure, but Rouget and Degotti’s participation is explicit. A brushstroke-by-brushstroke allocation has not been established in the open sources reviewed.

#### 5. What is the status of the Versailles version?

Earlier writing sometimes treated replicas as automatically inferior. Current institutional treatment recognizes MV 7156 as an independent, authentic David-workshop version with its own history. Its differences are evidence of renewed execution, not necessarily errors.

#### 6. Did Napoleon commission the original specifically for the Tuileries?

The Louvre’s detailed record says the September 1804 commission named no precise destination. The Tuileries Guards’ Room was proposed later, in 1811. Sources describing it as the original fixed destination compress later planning into the initial commission.

### Major modern publications and exhibitions

- Sylvain Laveissière and collaborators, *Le Sacre de Napoléon peint par David*, Louvre/5 Continents, 2004.
- Jean Tulard, *Le Sacre de l’empereur Napoléon: histoire et légende*, Fayard/RMN, 2004.
- Antoine Schnapper’s catalogue raisonné work on David remains important for attribution and corpus placement.
- The Louvre’s 2004 exhibition gathered preparatory studies and ceremonial material around the canvas.
- Technical imaging of the Philadelphia double portrait revealed a painted *Sacre* composition underneath, materially advancing understanding of the design sequence. [Fondation Napoléon preparatory-study account](https://www.napoleon.org/histoire-des-2-empires/tableaux/le-sacre-ou-le-couronnement-dessin-preparatoire-david/)

### Limits of the accessible record

A truly exhaustive material dossier would require consultation of:

- Louvre conservation files;
- Centre de recherche et de restauration des musées de France imaging and treatment records;
- Archives nationales files of the Maison de l’Empereur;
- complete Salon criticism from 1808 and 1810;
- transport and wartime convoy records;
- Versailles acquisition papers for MV 7156;
- the full 2004 Louvre exhibition catalogue.

Those records were not all available in openly searchable full text. Consequently, unsupported claims about pigments, lining, cleaning, tears, structural interventions, and stretcher replacement have been excluded.
