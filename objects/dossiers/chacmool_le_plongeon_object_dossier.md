# The Chacmool statue (Le Plongeon excavation): Object Dossier

**File name:** `the_chacmool_statue_le_plongeon_excavation_object_dossier.md`  
**Research current to:** 6 September 2026

## Evidentiary key

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary records, photographs, museum documentation, or mutually corroborating archival sources.
- **Scientific or material finding:** based on direct examination or laboratory analysis.
- **Scholarly inference:** a modern interpretation grounded in archaeological context or comparison.
- **Contemporary claim:** a statement made by a participant but not independently demonstrated.
- **Tradition or legend:** a reported story whose circulation is historically documentable but whose narrated event is not.
- **Discredited interpretation:** a proposal rejected by subsequent archaeology, epigraphy, chronology, or material evidence.
- **Unresolved:** evidence presently does not permit a secure conclusion.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Object tracked

This dossier tracks the single anthropomorphic stone sculpture excavated by Augustus Le Plongeon, Alice Dixon Le Plongeon, and Maya laborers at Chichén Itzá in 1875; removed to Pisté; taken into Mexican government custody in 1877; and now catalogued by Mexico’s Museo Nacional de Antropología as **05.0-01426**. It is not the later chacmool at the entrance of Chichén Itzá’s Temple of the Warriors, nor any of the approximately fifteen or more other reclining figures subsequently recorded at the site. The institutional catalogue and surviving excavation photographs make the identification of the displayed Mexico City sculpture with the Le Plongeon find unusually secure.

The statue is historically important for two distinct reasons:

1. It is a genuine pre-Hispanic sculpture from Chichén Itzá, probably carved during the Terminal Classic or Early Postclassic transformation of the city.
2. The name that Le Plongeon invented or assigned to this particular figure—*Chaacmol*, regularized by Stephen Salisbury Jr. as *Chac-Mool*—became the archaeological type-name for comparable reclining sculptures throughout Mesoamerica.

### Names and terminology

Recorded forms include:

- **Chaacmol** — Augustus Le Plongeon’s preferred spelling.
- **Chac-Mool** — Stephen Salisbury Jr.’s 1877 regularization, adopted because he considered it the more usual Yucatecan spelling.
- **Chac Mool / Chacmool / chacmool** — later museum and archaeological forms.
- **Estatua llamada Chac-Mool o Rey Tigre** — “statue called Chac-Mool or Tiger King,” in Jesús Sánchez’s 1877 study.
- **Rey Chacmool / Rey Tigre** — names used during the statue’s ceremonial reception in Yucatán.
- **Prince Coh / Prince Co / Prince Cay / Chac Mool** — identities assigned in the Le Plongeons’ developing pseudo-historical narrative. Their spelling varies across publications.

Le Plongeon explained *chaac* as “storm” or “irresistible power” and *mool* as the paw of a carnivorous animal, producing a sense such as “powerful paw” or “leopard.” Other nineteenth-century writers glossed the compound as “tiger,” “Tiger King,” or “puma.” Modern summaries often give “red jaguar,” “great jaguar paw,” “red paw,” or “paw swift as thunder.” These are not interchangeable translations.

The instability is substantive. Yucatec *chak/chaak* may mean “red,” “great,” or, depending on spelling and linguistic context, relate to storm or the rain deity Chaak; *mo’ol/mool* has been read as paw or claw. The archaeological term therefore preserves a nineteenth-century antiquarian label, not an ancient name securely read on the statue. No accepted decipherment identifies the sculpture itself as “Chacmool,” and no evidence establishes that ancient inhabitants of Chichén Itzá called this class of object by that term. Mary Ellen Miller accordingly treated *chacmool* as conventional modern terminology rather than an identification of the being represented.

### Class and form

The sculpture is a monumental, three-dimensional, reclining male figure carved with:

- the back and buttocks supported on a long rectangular base;
- knees raised and legs flexed;
- upper torso lifted;
- elbows or forearms bearing part of the body’s weight;
- head rotated sharply to one side, facing across the body rather than upward;
- hands meeting over the abdomen;
- a circular or shallow vessel/receptacle resting on or formed above the belly;
- headdress, ear ornaments, chest ornament, wrist ornaments, leg bands, and sandals.

This combination—reclining body, bent legs, turned head, and abdominal receptacle—defines the broader chacmool class. It should not be confused with a sacrificial victim lying supine on a *techcatl*, although some chacmools could themselves have served as offering tables or, in later Mexica contexts, sacrificial stones.

### Material, manufacture, and surface

**Documented/materially observed:**

- The figure and base are limestone.
- The statue was carved from local or probably local limestone.
- Le Plongeon described the figure as carved from one block of “beautifully white and homogeneous limestone.”
- Contemporary Yucatecan reporting called the stone “almost as hard as granite,” a description of perceived hardness, not a petrographic identification.
- Grecco Pacheco and Sellen report that the surface was polished and painted and that small remnants of original pigment remain visible.
- As of their 2019 study, no detailed published physical-and-chemical analysis of the limestone or pigments had been performed.

The sculpture therefore has no published quarry attribution, petrographic fingerprint, pigment inventory, or residue analysis securely identified in the sources used here. Claims that its abdominal receptacle contained blood, a heart, ashes, or royal remains originated with Le Plongeon and have not been chemically demonstrated.

### Dimensions and weight

The most detailed early measurements occur in Juan Peón Contreras’s 1877 notice:

- base thickness: **9 Spanish inches**;
- base length: **5 feet 3½ inches**;
- base width: **2 feet 10 inches**;
- reported total weight: approximately **3,500 pounds**.

A *Periódico Oficial* report translated by Salisbury said the statue measured “a little more than nine feet in length.” That figure conflicts sharply with Peón Contreras’s base length and with the apparent scale in photographs. The discrepancy may reflect mistranslation, measurement of statue plus transport apparatus, journalistic error, or confusion among Spanish, English, and local units. It should not be silently converted into a modern metric dimension. The Museo Nacional de Antropología’s public catalogue page supplies the catalogue number but does not publish a full modern measurement set.

The reported 3,500-pound weight—about 1.59 metric tonnes—also remains a nineteenth-century estimate rather than a documented modern weighing.

### Iconography

Le Plongeon described:

- a segmented band headdress formed from joined octagonal elements;
- feather bracelets and garters;
- sandals tied around feet and legs;
- a chest ornament suspended from a cord tied behind the neck;
- square lateral tablets in place of conventional ear ornaments;
- filed or serrated teeth.

He claimed the “tablets” contained hieroglyphs naming the represented person. Modern Maya epigraphy has not accepted that reading. Later scholarship instead notes Central Mexican/Tlaloc-related imagery on the ear ornaments and treats the costume within the military-sacrificial iconography of Chichén Itzá.

There is no securely translated dedicatory inscription on the object. No named sculptor, patron, ruler, workshop, or completion date is preserved.

### Abdominal receptacle and reported contents

The belly supports or incorporates a shallow circular receptacle. Le Plongeon reported fragments of two flint knives, greenstone beads, and dark organic-looking material in it, which he interpreted as the cremated heart of his imagined Prince Coh. Other projectile points and objects were recovered beneath or near the statue.

The documented facts are that portable lithic and shell materials formed part of the excavated deposit and that at least some were removed and dispersed. The interpretation of the residue as a human heart or blood is unverified. No surviving residue sample has been tied by an auditable chain of custody to a scientific analysis.

### Damage and present condition

Excavation photographs show the figure substantially complete when raised. It has not undergone the decapitation or gross fragmentation affecting many Mesoamerican sculptures. The surviving polychromy is slight. The object experienced demanding transport by improvised wooden apparatus and wagon in 1875 and again in 1877, but the consulted publications do not identify a major documented break, reconstructed limb, or modern replacement.

No comprehensive public conservation report was located. Assertions about adhesives, old fills, cleaning campaigns, or consolidation would therefore be speculative.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Archaeological origin

The statue came from the **Platform of the Eagles and Jaguars**—also historically called a “mausoleum” in Le Plongeon’s writings—on Chichén Itzá’s Great Terrace in Yucatán. The structure lies near the Great Ball Court, Tzompantli, Temple of the Jaguars, Platform of Venus, and El Castillo.

The platform is a square, radial structure with four stairways, feathered-serpent balustrades, and relief panels showing eagles and jaguars associated with human hearts and reclining or armed figures. Its present appearance incorporates twentieth-century restoration, principally interventions begun in 1931 and a fuller restoration led by Jorge Ruffier Acosta in 1951.

The excavation deposit reportedly included:

- the limestone chacmool;
- a reclining limestone jaguar, originally found headless;
- a separate limestone human head that Le Plongeon placed upon the jaguar;
- carved limestone façade panels;
- feathered-serpent stair elements;
- small limestone objects;
- shells and beads;
- jadeite or greenstone objects;
- flint, chalcedony, quartz, and greenstone projectile points or knives;
- ceramic plates;
- limestone vessels or “urns.”

Because Le Plongeon’s excavation preceded modern stratigraphic recording, the exact relationships among these objects cannot be reconstructed to current archaeological standards. His photographs, Alice’s diary, plans, letters, and later architectural work nevertheless establish that the chacmool was recovered from within or beneath the platform’s collapsed fabric, not purchased without context.

### Date

No radiometric or direct scientific date has been published for the sculpture. Dating is stylistic and contextual.

**Scholarly inference:** the object belongs to Chichén Itzá’s Terminal Classic/Early Postclassic monumental phase, conventionally around the ninth through eleventh centuries CE, with broader popular sources sometimes assigning the platform to approximately 900–1200 CE. A narrow year or reign cannot be justified.

This date invalidates Le Plongeon’s claims that the sculpture was 5,000 or 12,000 years old, that it commemorated events around the beginning of the Christian era, or that it recorded Maya founders of Egyptian civilization.

### Maker, quarry, and commission

- **Maker:** unknown.
- **Workshop:** unknown; probably a Chichén Itzá sculptural workshop.
- **Quarry:** unproven; local limestone is probable.
- **Commissioner:** unknown.
- **Named subject:** unknown.
- **Original indigenous name:** unknown.
- **Cost or labor account:** none preserved.

The highly integrated placement of comparable chacmools at entrances and in vestibules at Chichén Itzá supports the inference that the class formed part of planned ceremonial architecture. Whether this example was installed in its platform at initial construction, deposited as a dedicatory offering, ritually retired from another building, or concealed during alteration is unresolved.

### Earliest modern attestation

Alice Dixon Le Plongeon’s diary provides the closest contemporaneous record. The Museo Nacional de Antropología summarizes her entry as stating that Augustus discovered the statue on **Sunday, 1 November 1875**. Grecco Pacheco and Sellen place the beginning of its physical itinerary to Pisté on **20 December 1875**. Augustus later dated its concealment near Pisté to **5 January 1876**. These dates describe successive discovery, extraction, transport, and concealment, not necessarily contradictory versions of one event.

Le Plongeon’s memorial to President Sebastián Lerdo de Tejada was published in Yucatán’s official journal on **19 and 21 April 1876**. Salisbury presented an English-language documentary compilation to the American Antiquarian Society in 1877, reproducing letters, descriptions, official notices, and photographs.

### The Le Plongeon origin story

**Discredited nineteenth-century interpretation:** Le Plongeon claimed that murals and signs in the Temple of the Jaguars told a dynastic tragedy involving Queen Móo; her husband or lover, Prince Coh; and Coh’s brothers Huuncay and Aac. In his story Aac murdered Coh, after which Móo commemorated him in monuments. Coh was posthumously known as Chaacmol; the excavated reclining statue was his portrait; the nearby jaguar with an added human head was an “American sphinx”; and vessels in the deposit contained Coh’s blood, ashes, or heart.

The story grew across Le Plongeon’s correspondence, *Sacred Mysteries among the Mayas and the Quiches* (1886), *Queen Móo and the Egyptian Sphinx* (1896), and later writings. It was not an indigenous tradition recorded before the excavation. Its supposed names and narrative were products of the Le Plongeons’ erroneous decipherments and diffusionist belief that Maya civilization had helped create ancient Egypt and other Old World civilizations.

The story is historically important because it:

- gave the statue its enduring name;
- motivated Le Plongeon’s search and interpretation;
- supported his claim of ownership and hoped-for exhibition;
- linked the sculpture to Atlantis-and-Egypt speculation;
- shaped early journalism and later occult/popular treatments.

It is not a reliable account of manufacture or ancient identity.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Before 1875: archaeological deposition

The statue’s ancient path from workshop to installation and eventual burial is undocumented. Its recovery within the Platform of the Eagles and Jaguars indicates ancient deposition at Chichén Itzá. Whether that deposition was constructional, dedicatory, concealing, or secondary remains unresolved.

No evidence supports a medieval, colonial, or modern owner before excavation. It was not documented in the sixteenth-century chronicles under the name “Chacmool.”

### November–December 1875: excavation

**Documented:** Augustus and Alice Le Plongeon directed the work; Maya laborers performed much of the excavation and lifting. Government troops initially afforded protection because Chichén Itzá lay near the contested frontier of the Caste War.

Le Plongeon reported that workers excavated through a dangerous mass of rubble. He gave a depth of seven metres for the first vessel and approximately eight or eight and a half metres for the statue. These extraordinary depths derive from his account and are difficult to reconcile with the known architecture without the original section and unit conventions. They should be treated as contemporary claims, not verified modern stratigraphic measurements.

The lifting operation used timber, vines or bark ropes, a capstan-like device, and trestling. Photographs document successive stages and show Alice, Augustus, workers, the sculpture, and its improvised transport.

### December 1875–January 1876: Pisté and concealment

Grecco Pacheco and Sellen date the movement toward Pisté to **20 December 1875**. Peón Contreras said the transport required fifteen days. Augustus wrote that on **5 January 1876** he placed the statue about a quarter-mile from Pisté, roughly fifty yards from the road toward Dzitás, in scrub. He and Alice wrapped it in oilcloth, built a thatched roof over it, enclosed it with brush, and disguised the entrance.

Le Plongeon considered discovery and expenditure sufficient to give him proprietary rights. Mexican authorities regarded archaeological objects as national property and relied on the 1827 customs prohibition against export of “Mexican monuments and antiquities.” The concealment was thus not an innocent museum deposit: it was part of an unresolved attempt to control and potentially export the sculpture.

### 1876: attempted Philadelphia exhibition

Le Plongeon asked President Sebastián Lerdo de Tejada for permission to send the statue, associated reliefs, plans, tracings, and photographs to the United States Centennial Exhibition in Philadelphia. He argued that the assemblage would prove America was the cradle of world civilization and complained that Mexican law prevented him from realizing the material return on his excavation.

The export request was not granted. The statue never reached Philadelphia. Some portable associated finds and photographs were nevertheless sent north; they arrived too late for the exhibition, were acquired by Stephen Salisbury Jr., and entered American collections. That dispersal concerns the excavation assemblage but not the statue itself.

### February 1877: Mexican seizure/recovery

On **1 February 1877**, Juan Peón Contreras, director of the Museo Yucateco, left Mérida with an armed escort to retrieve the hidden object under authority of Governor Protasio Guerra. A road had to be opened through difficult terrain. A new wagon was built, and Peón Contreras reported that more than 150 Indigenous workers took turns hauling the monument.

From Le Plongeon’s position this was confiscation without consent; from the state’s position it was recovery of national archaeological property that the excavator had concealed while seeking export. Both descriptions belong in the record. It was not a voluntary gift by Le Plongeon.

### February–March 1877: Izamal and Mérida

The expedition reached Izamal on **26 February 1877**, where speeches and poems welcomed the “King Chac-Mool.” The statue entered Mérida on **1 March 1877** accompanied by a military band, officials, learned societies, schoolchildren, and crowds. Contemporary announcements invoked “Glory to Mexico,” “Glory to Science,” “Glory to Le Plongeon,” and “Glory to Yucatán.”

It entered the Museo Yucateco on its rustic wagon. Peón Contreras proposed a gilded inscription naming Le Plongeon as discoverer, Protasio Guerra as governor, and himself as the museum director who brought the sculpture in. The proposed permanent presentation was never completed.

This procession transformed the object from contested excavation find into a symbol of Yucatecan antiquity, civic learning, and regional prestige amid continuing conflict between white and Indigenous communities during the Caste War.

### April–May 1877: removal from Yucatán

Interim governor Agustín del Río authorized transfer to the Museo Nacional in Mexico City. The national war steamer **Libertad** arrived and departed so quickly that the proposed plaster copy for the Museo Yucateco could not be made. Peón Contreras’s note dated **6 April 1877** recorded Yucatán’s intention to ask the president for a cast as compensation.

Salisbury wrote that the ship carried the statue away in April; Grecco Pacheco and Sellen date its final transfer to the capital to **May 1877**. The most defensible reconstruction is departure from Yucatán in April and arrival or institutional incorporation in Mexico City by May. The steamer’s route included Veracruz before overland movement to the capital, but the exact unloading and railway itinerary was not established in the consulted sources.

The transfer represented both national preservation and central appropriation from a regional museum. It was not an international colonial removal, since the statue remained in Mexico, but it was a coercive central-government transfer contested by the excavator and regretted by Yucatecan officials.

### Le Plongeon’s claim and diplomatic appeal

While absent on Cozumel and Isla Mujeres, Le Plongeon learned that the object had been taken. On **1 May 1877**, from Cozumel, he addressed the United States minister in Mexico, John W. Foster, seeking redress and compensation as a United States citizen. He characterized the Yucatecan and federal actions as seizure and appropriation.

No evidence in the consulted record shows that the United States secured restitution, compensation, or recognition of Le Plongeon’s title. The statue remained Mexican national property. Later summaries report that Foster’s investigation found no enforceable obligation against Mexico and potential legal difficulty in Le Plongeon’s attempted export, but a complete diplomatic case file was not located here; that conclusion should remain tied to secondary historiography rather than treated as a quoted formal judgment.

### 1877–1964: Museo Nacional, Calle de Moneda

The sculpture entered the Museo Nacional’s archaeological collections in the former mint building on Calle de Moneda, Mexico City. It was displayed among monumental antiquities and later in the **Salón de Monolitos**, inaugurated in 1887. Historical museum photographs document it in the monolith gallery and in early twentieth-century displays.

Its juxtaposition with the Sun Stone and other monumental sculptures placed a Maya object from Yucatán within a national narrative centered in the capital. No deaccession, foreign loan, theft, or wartime evacuation of this particular sculpture has been established.

### 1964–present: Museo Nacional de Antropología

When the modern Museo Nacional de Antropología opened at Chapultepec in **1964**, the statue moved with the national archaeological collection. It is assigned to the Maya cultures display and catalogued as **05.0-01426**. Grecco Pacheco and Sellen reported in 2019 that it remained on public exhibition there; INAH’s museum page identifies it as the Chichén Itzá sculpture from the Platform of the Eagles and Jaguars.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is the present museum object the excavated statue?

**Conclusion: yes, with high confidence.** The continuity is supported by:

- a distinctive and substantially complete form;
- Le Plongeon’s serial excavation and transport photographs;
- contemporary published descriptions;
- official records of retrieval and transfer;
- historical photographs inside the Museo Nacional;
- the present INAH catalogue and provenance;
- no documented destruction or substitution.

There is no serious rival object claiming to be Le Plongeon’s original find.

### Is it an authentic pre-Hispanic sculpture?

The object’s excavation context, documented in 1875 photographs, establishes that it is not a nineteenth-century sculpture planted for discovery. Its material, weathering, style, and contextual relationship to Chichén Itzá’s architecture are consistent with pre-Hispanic manufacture.

No modern radiometric test can directly date limestone carving, and no published quarry or pigment analysis is known. Authentication consequently rests on archaeological context, contemporaneous visual documentation, and stylistic comparison rather than a single laboratory test.

### Was Le Plongeon’s context reliable?

Partly. The photographs, diary, and associated finds preserve real archaeological information. His excavation lacked modern stratigraphy, and his narratives repeatedly transformed observation into conjecture. The reported depth, reconstruction of the platform, addition of a separately found human head to the reclining jaguar, and interpretation of vessels as canopic urns demonstrate active manipulation of context and image.

Thus:

- **The sculpture is authentic.**
- **The excavation event is documented.**
- **Le Plongeon’s identification of its owner and tomb is not authentic ancient history.**
- **His illustrated architectural reconstructions cannot be treated as neutral site records.**

### Was it a portrait of “King Chac-Mool” or Prince Coh?

No ancient text securely identifies the figure as such. Le Plongeon claimed that ear ornaments and nearby murals provided names and biography; modern epigraphy does not confirm his reading. “Prince Coh,” Queen Móo, Huuncay, Aac, and the murder narrative are nineteenth-century constructions.

### Is it the first chacmool ever found?

It was the first sculpture to which the name *chacmool* was applied and the discovery that established the modern category. It was not necessarily the first object of the form ever noticed in modern times. Eduardo Matos Moctezuma points to Antonio de León y Gama’s earlier report, in the second edition of his work on the Sun Stone and Coatlicue, of a comparable sculpture found at Mexico City’s Mayorazgo de Guerrero. The priority claim must therefore be stated narrowly: Le Plongeon’s was the naming discovery, not unquestionably the first modern observation of the type.

### Was it an altar, offering table, deity, guardian, victim, or sacrificial stone?

This remains interpretive:

- Le Plongeon called it a portrait, not an idol.
- Jesús Sánchez and other early Mexican scholars compared it with newly recognized reclining sculptures.
- Vessel-bearing examples support use as recipients for offerings.
- Chichén Itzá placement at vestibules and entrances supports a mediating or threshold function.
- Costume and Tlaloc imagery connect the form to war, captives, rain, fertility, and sacrifice.
- Later Mexica examples sometimes bear *cuauhxicalli*-like vessels, and the *Crónica Mexicana* describes what López Luján and Urcid interpret as a chacmool used as a *techcatl* for sacrifice in 1487.

The late Mexica evidence cannot automatically specify the use of this earlier Maya example. “Sacrificial altar” is plausible as a class-level description but not proven for this individual statue.

### Ownership dispute

Le Plongeon argued labor, discovery, and expense made the statue his property and sought export permission. Mexican authorities invoked national ownership and the 1827 export prohibition. The Mexican state prevailed in possession and subsequent law strongly treats archaeological monuments as national property.

The object was therefore genuinely disputed, but not of uncertain present title in the museum’s institutional account. No active private claim, restitution proceeding, or Yucatán–federal lawsuit concerning it was located.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Ancient subjects and beings

- **Unknown Chichén Itzá sculptors, c. ninth–eleventh centuries:** carved the limestone figure. No names survive.
- **Unknown Chichén Itzá patrons and ritual personnel:** commissioned, installed, used, or deposited it.
- **Tlaloc:** Central Mexican rain and storm deity whose diagnostic features have been identified on the statue’s ear ornaments and on the costume of related figures. The connection is iconographic, not a deciphered name.
- **Chaak:** Maya rain/storm deity sometimes confused with the statue because of the modern name. No secure evidence establishes that the figure is Chaak.
- **Captive or warrior imagery:** Miller linked the chacmool posture and costume to Classic Maya depictions of captives, war, and sacrifice. This is a scholarly model, not a personal identification.

### Excavators and workers

- **Augustus Le Plongeon (1825/26–1908):** directed excavation, named the sculpture, engineered its lifting, concealed it, asserted ownership, sought export, photographed and published it, and created its pseudo-history.
- **Alice Dixon Le Plongeon (1851–1910):** co-excavator, photographer, recorder, conservator of mural copies, diarist, and active intellectual collaborator. Describing her merely as Augustus’s assistant obscures her documented labor and authorship.
- **Unnamed Maya workers:** excavated and raised the statue and hauled it to Pisté. Their names are absent from the published record, but their physical participation is visible in photographs and stated in contemporary accounts.
- **Government soldiers:** initially guarded the expedition in the Caste War frontier region.

### Custodians and officials

- **Sebastián Lerdo de Tejada:** president to whom Le Plongeon submitted the 1876 export memorial.
- **Porfirio Díaz’s federal government:** inherited the matter after the political change and rejected or did not authorize export.
- **Protasio Guerra:** governor under whose order Yucatecan authorities recovered/confiscated the object.
- **Juan Peón Contreras:** director of the Museo Yucateco; led the armed retrieval expedition, arranged transport and civic reception, and documented the object’s dimensions and proposed display.
- **Agustín del Río:** interim governor who permitted transfer from Mérida to the national museum.
- **John W. Foster:** United States minister in Mexico to whom Le Plongeon appealed.
- **Stephen Salisbury Jr.:** president of the American Antiquarian Society, patron and publisher; changed *Chaacmol* to *Chac-Mool* in print and acquired associated portable finds and photographs.
- **Museo Yucateco:** brief custodian in March–April 1877 and intended recipient of a plaster copy.
- **Museo Nacional:** federal custodian from 1877 to 1964.
- **Instituto Nacional de Antropología e Historia:** present governmental custodian through the Museo Nacional de Antropología.
- **Museo Nacional de Antropología:** current holding and displaying institution; catalogue 05.0-01426.

### Scholars

- **Jesús Sánchez:** published the first extended Mexican comparative study, *Estudio acerca de la estatua llamada Chac-Mool o Rey Tigre*, in 1877, helping detach the type from Le Plongeon’s single biography.
- **Teobert Maler:** documented Chichén Itzá and criticized or corrected aspects of Le Plongeon’s architectural interpretation.
- **Alfred Maudslay:** mapped and documented the site and identified the mound associated with the find.
- **Earl Morris and Carnegie Institution investigators:** excavated further chacmools at Chichén Itzá, proving the object belonged to a recurring class.
- **Jorge Ruffier Acosta:** led the 1951 restoration of the Platform of the Eagles and Jaguars.
- **Mary Ellen Miller:** produced the influential 1985 re-examination emphasizing Maya captive imagery, architectural context, Tlaloc costume, and sacrificial meaning.
- **Leonardo López Luján and Javier Urcid:** examined chacmools in sacrificial and Mexica textual contexts.
- **Lawrence Desmond, Phyllis Messenger, and later Claire Desmond:** reconstructed the Le Plongeons’ work through photographs, correspondence, and Alice’s diary.
- **Daniel Grecco Pacheco and Adam T. Sellen:** reconstructed the itineraries of the platform’s dispersed artifacts and summarized the statue’s custody and analytical gaps.
- **Daniel Juárez Cossío:** MNA curator who prepared the museum’s 2014 object feature.

### Legendary persons created by the Le Plongeons

- **Queen Móo:** alleged queen and widow/lover; a modern pseudo-historical figure constructed from Le Plongeon’s mural readings.
- **Prince Coh/Chaacmol:** alleged murdered ruler represented by the statue.
- **Aac:** alleged murderous brother.
- **Huuncay:** alleged elder brother.

These belong to the modern reception history of the object, not the securely reconstructed history of ancient Chichén Itzá.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Possible ancient function

The belly receptacle is the clearest functional feature. It was suited to hold offerings—food, incense, blood, hearts, liquids, valuables, or other substances—but no residue analysis identifies what this individual receptacle received.

Five Chichén Itzá chacmools were reportedly found in architectural positions at entrances to vestibules or antechambers, usually on central axes. This pattern supports a threshold role: the reclining figure may have received gifts before they passed symbolically to a deity or into a restricted interior.

Miller argued that the figures’ costume and posture connect them with captured warriors and Tlaloc-related sacrificial imagery. In that model, the chacmool is not necessarily a god but a transformed or impersonating human intermediary who presents offerings.

### Human sacrifice

The strongest textual association belongs to a later Mexica setting. Hernando Alvarado Tezozómoc’s *Crónica Mexicana* (1598) describes sacrificial activity during the 1487 dedication of the enlarged Templo Mayor. López Luján and Urcid interpret the relevant stone as a chacmool used as a *techcatl*, a stone on which captives were killed.

That passage demonstrates a possible late function for the object class. It does not show that the Le Plongeon statue itself received a human body or heart. Le Plongeon’s claim that its receptacle contained Coh’s cremated heart is unsupported.

### Associations with water and fertility

Tlaloc-related imagery links some chacmools with rain, water, mountains, agricultural fertility, and tribute. In later Central Mexican settings, the abdominal vessel could receive offerings directed to rain and earth powers. At Chichén Itzá, these associations overlap with martial imagery rather than excluding it.

### Civic uses after excavation

The statue acquired documented modern ceremonial roles:

- triumphal reception at Izamal, 26 February 1877;
- procession into Mérida, 1 March 1877;
- accompaniment by military music, officials, associations, students, and crowds;
- speeches and poems making it an emblem of Yucatecan antiquity and science;
- subsequent installation in a national museum narrative.

These were not survivals of ancient Maya ritual. They were nineteenth-century civic ceremonies using an archaeological object to produce regional and national identity.

### Access and handling

No ancient touch or access rules survive. Since 1877, movement and handling have been controlled by Mexican museum authorities. Present public access is visual, under museum conditions; touching is not permitted under ordinary museum practice.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The Queen Móo–Prince Coh tragedy

**Classification: modern invention presented as recovered ancient history.**

In its developed form:

1. Chichén Itzá was ruled by a royal family including Queen Móo and brothers Coh, Aac, and Huuncay.
2. Móo loved or married Coh.
3. Aac, driven by political and erotic rivalry, murdered Coh with three spear thrusts.
4. Móo built a memorial or mausoleum for him.
5. Coh received the posthumous name Chaacmol.
6. The reclining statue represented his human form.
7. The nearby feline sculpture, fitted by Le Plongeon with a separately found human head, represented him as an American sphinx.
8. Vessels and residue preserved his heart, blood, or ashes.
9. Móo later carried Maya civilization or its mysteries toward Egypt; related versions entangled the story with Atlantis.

Its earliest stages appear in Le Plongeon’s 1875–1877 interpretations and correspondence. It expanded in his books of 1886, 1896, and 1900 and in Alice’s lectures and articles. No pre-1875 Maya text or oral account is known to contain this narrative.

The legend served Le Plongeon’s diffusionist thesis, personalized his discovery, distinguished his work from conventional collecting, and made the statue appear comparable to Egyptian royal monuments. Its factual claims fail against Maya chronology, epigraphy, and archaeology.

### The speaking statue: “Conex! Conex!”

**Classification: contemporary reported transport legend.**

Peón Contreras wrote that Indigenous haulers said the statue spoke at night: **“Conex! Conex!”**, translated in the publication as “Let us go! Let us go!” The account appears in an official celebratory notice, where it heightens the drama of the journey.

The source proves that the story circulated by 1877. It does not establish that all workers believed it, nor that the words were independently recorded from named speakers. The report’s description of the laborers’ belief as “fanatical superstition” is the official writer’s framing.

### The 12,000-year burial and Old World migrations

**Classification: discredited pseudoarchaeology.**

Le Plongeon variously placed the burial thousands or tens of thousands of years before his own time and treated Maya civilization as ancestor or teacher of Egypt, Greece, Mesopotamia, and India. These dates conflict with Chichén Itzá’s archaeological chronology by many millennia.

The continued repetition of “12,000 years,” “Atlantis,” Egyptian royalty, or canopic burial in occult literature is modern reception, not ancient Maya tradition.

### The heart in the bowl

**Classification: unverified excavation claim transformed into legend.**

Le Plongeon interpreted dark material in or near the abdominal receptacle as the cremated heart of Prince Coh. No forensic chain of custody, histological examination, ancient DNA test, or chemical residue analysis confirms human cardiac tissue or blood. Associated flint and greenstone objects are real; the biographical interpretation is not established.

### Curse narratives

Unlike some famous excavated objects, the Le Plongeon chacmool has no securely documented early curse threatening its discoverer, transporter, or museum. Later fictional chacmools—notably Carlos Fuentes’s story—animate the type and make possession dangerous. Such fiction has influenced public expectations but is not an early legend of this specific artifact.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Location

**Museo Nacional de Antropología**  
Paseo de la Reforma and Calzada Gandhi, Chapultepec, Mexico City, Mexico  
Maya cultures collection/display  
**Catalogue number: 05.0-01426**

The museum’s 2014 “Pieza del Mes” identifies the statue, excavation, confiscation, and incorporation into the Museo Nacional. Grecco Pacheco and Sellen reported that it remained exhibited in the modern MNA after its 1964 transfer.

### Custody and legal status

The object is controlled by the Mexican federal state through INAH and the Museo Nacional de Antropología. Under contemporary Mexican heritage law, pre-Hispanic archaeological monuments are national property and ordinarily inalienable and imprescriptible.

No current foreign ownership claim, auction claim, or private title dispute was found. Le Plongeon’s claim ended without recovery. Yucatecan officials’ 1877 request for a plaster copy reflected regional grievance but is not known to have become a present legal restitution proceeding.

### Access

The object has been publicly displayed. Gallery availability can change temporarily for conservation, exhibition rotation, installation, or museum operations; the catalogue page does not provide live daily gallery status. Researchers seeking close examination, pigment sampling, or conservation files would require INAH authorization.

### Condition and analytical status

Published observations report:

- substantially complete limestone figure;
- surviving traces of paint;
- no published comprehensive physical-and-chemical characterization as of 2019;
- no published direct dating analysis;
- no published residue analysis of the bowl;
- no published quarry-sourcing study.

The most important current research deficit is therefore not authenticity but archaeometry and conservation publication.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Archaeological naming legacy

The object’s greatest modern effect is lexical. A name coined for one supposed ruler became the standard term for a widespread sculptural class. Comparable figures are now called chacmools at Chichén Itzá, Tula, Tenochtitlan, Tlatelolco, Cempoala, Tlaxcala, Michoacán, and elsewhere, despite regional, chronological, and iconographic differences.

The generic name can obscure variation. Some examples hold explicit vessels; some bear divine attributes; some are human, warrior-like, aged, or aquatic; and their architectural positions differ.

### José Martí

José Martí encountered the public controversy surrounding the find and left notes and a reclining self-caricature connected with the Chac Mool form. Later Martí scholarship treats the object as part of his emerging engagement with Indigenous American antiquity and anti-colonial cultural identity. This is a documented intellectual engagement, not custody.

### Henry Moore

Henry Moore repeatedly acknowledged the impact of reclining pre-Columbian sculpture, particularly the chacmool form, on his reclining figures. The turned head, raised torso, bent legs, and horizontal mass were transformed into modernist sculpture rather than copied archaeologically. Grecco Pacheco and Sellen single out Moore’s mid-twentieth-century *Reclining Figure* works as part of the artifact type’s modern itinerary.

The influence concerns the class and its museum images, not necessarily proof that Moore studied only catalogue 05.0-01426 rather than several examples.

### Carlos Fuentes

Carlos Fuentes’s short story **“Chac Mool”**, first published in the 1950s and collected in *Los días enmascarados* (1954), portrays a collector who acquires a stone Chac Mool that becomes animate and dominates him. The story materially reshaped the popular figure into an uncanny rain-associated being. That animate, vengeful creature is literary invention, not ancient testimony about Le Plongeon’s statue.

### Sebastián

Mexican sculptor Enrique Carbajal, known as **Sebastian**, developed numerous geometric works inspired by the chacmool posture. The Museo Nacional de Antropología mounted **“Chac mool. Sebastian”** from December 2023 to March 2024, bringing 26 works into dialogue with ancient Mesoamerican sculpture. The exhibition demonstrates continued use of the form as a national and transhistorical artistic motif.

### Replicas and casts

A plaster cast was proposed for Mérida in 1877 but could not be made before the steamer *Libertad* removed the original. Whether Mexico later supplied the promised copy was not established in the consulted sources.

Photographs, engravings, miniatures, museum reproductions, souvenirs, jewelry, and modern sculptures have proliferated. They often merge the Le Plongeon example with other Chichén Itzá chacmools, particularly the readily photographed figure at the Temple of the Warriors. Images labeled simply “the Chac Mool of Chichén Itzá” must therefore be checked against the original excavation photographs.

### National and regional symbolism

The object has successively served:

- Le Plongeon’s claim for American civilizational primacy;
- Yucatecan regional pride;
- Mexican federal claims to archaeological patrimony;
- national museum narratives;
- modern art’s search for non-European formal sources;
- literary treatments of collecting, cultural possession, and the return of the sacred;
- tourism and commercial imagery associated with Maya Mexico.

These uses are documented afterlives; they do not disclose the sculpture’s ancient name.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### First documentary corpus, 1875–1877

The essential early evidence comprises:

- Alice Dixon Le Plongeon’s diary;
- excavation and transport photographs;
- Augustus’s memorial to President Lerdo de Tejada;
- correspondence with Salisbury and Foster;
- the *Periódico Oficial* of Yucatán;
- Peón Contreras’s official notice;
- the Izamal/Mérida celebratory pamphlet;
- Salisbury’s 1877 American Antiquarian Society publication;
- Sánchez’s 1877 comparative study.

These sources are close to the events but partisan. Augustus argued for ownership and validation; Peón Contreras justified government recovery; Salisbury advocated for his associate; public ceremonies amplified regional nationalism.

### From unique “king” to archaeological type

The discovery of other reclining sculptures rapidly weakened Le Plongeon’s portrait theory. Sánchez compared the Chichén Itzá object with related Mexican figures. Subsequent discoveries at Tula, Chichén Itzá, Cempoala, Michoacán, and the Valley of Mexico established a geographically broad type.

This transition is historiographically crucial: *Chacmool* ceased to mean an alleged individual and became a formal archaeological category.

### Chichén Itzá–Tula problem

Chacmools became central to debate over connections between Chichén Itzá and Tula:

- older “Toltec invasion” models treated the form as Central Mexican importation;
- Maya-centered models stressed local antecedents, especially captive imagery;
- interaction models emphasize movement of people, objects, styles, religious practices, and political forms without requiring a simple one-way conquest.

The Le Plongeon example cannot independently settle directionality. Its limestone, Maya-site context, Tlaloc-related details, and military iconography embody precisely the cultural intermixture under debate.

### Miller’s 1985 reassessment

Mary Ellen Miller’s “A Re-examination of the Mesoamerican Chacmool” remains pivotal. She emphasized:

- formal consistency with Classic Maya captive poses;
- placement in architectural thresholds;
- abdominal vessels as offering receptacles;
- Tlaloc costume and war-sacrifice associations;
- possible Maya origins for the form without accepting Le Plongeon’s theories.

Her account replaced the simplistic question “Which god is it?” with contextual questions about bodily status, architecture, captivity, mediation, and offering.

### Sacrifice debate

López Luján and Urcid’s work on the Míxquic chacmool and the *Crónica Mexicana* strengthens the case that at least some later examples served directly in human sacrifice. The question remains whether every chacmool shared that function. Regional diversity argues against assuming a single use.

### Object-itinerary research

Grecco Pacheco and Sellen’s 2019 article is the most directly relevant modern object biography. It reconstructs how the platform assemblage separated:

- the chacmool went to Mexico City;
- the reclining jaguar entered the Palacio Cantón in Mérida;
- relief and architectural pieces were moved or reinstalled;
- portable finds entered the American Museum of Natural History and Peabody Museum;
- several vessels and small objects disappeared.

Their work also establishes the absence, as of publication, of detailed physical-and-chemical study of the statue.

### Archives and unpublished evidence

Important holdings include:

- Augustus and Alice Dixon Le Plongeon papers and photographs;
- American Antiquarian Society material associated with Salisbury;
- Getty Research Institute photographic holdings;
- Museo Nacional de Antropología historical and photographic archives;
- Biblioteca Carlos Menéndez copies of Yucatecan newspapers;
- INAH excavation, restoration, catalogue, and conservation files;
- Alice’s diaries as edited and published by Claire Desmond.

Potentially decisive information—modern measurements, conservation interventions, pigment observations, or accession history—may remain in internal museum records rather than public catalogue entries.

### Discredited theories that continue to circulate

The following should not be repeated as established archaeology:

- the statue is 12,000 years old;
- it portrays a historically verified King Chac-Mool;
- accepted hieroglyphic inscriptions name the figure;
- it is the tomb portrait of Prince Coh;
- its bowl was scientifically shown to contain Coh’s heart or blood;
- Queen Móo carried Maya civilization to Egypt;
- the sculpture proves Atlantis;
- all chacmools represent one deity or had one invariant function;
- Le Plongeon discovered the statue alone.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and near-primary sources

**Stephen Salisbury Jr., *The Mayas, the Sources of Their History: Dr. Le Plongeon in Yucatan, His Account of Discoveries* (1877).** The most accessible contemporary compilation. It reproduces or translates Le Plongeon’s memorial, correspondence, Peón Contreras’s report, press notices, excavation descriptions, and photographs. It is indispensable but sympathetic to Le Plongeon and occasionally uncertain about image captions and locality.

**Augustus Le Plongeon, memorial to President Sebastián Lerdo de Tejada, published 19 and 21 April 1876.** Preserved through Salisbury. Primary statement of Le Plongeon’s claim, intended Philadelphia exhibition, excavation narrative, and opposition to Mexican export restrictions.

**Juan Peón Contreras, official notice and letter to Agustín del Río (1877).** Preserved in Salisbury and archival citation. Primary account of retrieval, dimensions, weight, procession, museum plans, federal transfer, and the failed plaster cast.

**Yucatán, *Periódico Oficial*, 2 and 5 March 1877.** Contemporary accounts of the Mérida reception. Consulted through quotations and archival references in later scholarship.

**Jesús Sánchez, “Estudio acerca de la estatua llamada Chac-Mool o Rey Tigre” (1877).** Early Mexican museum study moving toward comparison with similar sculptures.

**Alice Dixon Le Plongeon’s diary.** Used through MNA’s dated summary and Claire Desmond’s edited diary as cited by Grecco Pacheco and Sellen. Primary record of excavation and associated finds.

### Museum and governmental sources

**Museo Nacional de Antropología, “Chac Mool,” Pieza del Mes, July 2014.** Authoritative public catalogue identification, number 05.0-01426, discovery date, custody summary, and present institutional assignment.

**Museo Nacional de Antropología, “Las colecciones en la calle Moneda.”** Institutional history and evidence for display of the Chichén Itzá and Tlaxcala chacmools in the old Museo Nacional.

**INAH/Secretaría de Cultura, 2023 Sebastian exhibition notice.** Documents present museum reception and summarizes archaeological and art-historical interpretations.

### Modern archaeological and historical scholarship

**Daniel Grecco Pacheco and Adam T. Sellen, “La historia de los artefactos itinerantes de la Plataforma de las Águilas y los Jaguares de Chichén Itzá, México,” *Estudios de Cultura Maya* 53 (2019), 11–44.** The central modern source for this dossier. It traces the statue and associated assemblage through archives and museums, distinguishes Le Plongeon’s constructions from evidence, and identifies analytical gaps.

**Mary Ellen Miller, “A Re-examination of the Mesoamerican Chacmool,” *The Art Bulletin* 67.1 (1985), 7–17.** Major formal, iconographic, architectural, and chronological reassessment.

**Leonardo López Luján and Javier Urcid, “El chacmool de Míxquic y el sacrificio humano.”** Important for sacrificial interpretation and Tezozómoc’s 1487 account; it should not be used to assign the same function automatically to every example.

**Adam T. Sellen and Lynneth S. Lowe, “Las antiguas colecciones arqueológicas de Yucatán en el Museo Americano de Historia Natural,” *Estudios de Cultura Maya* 33 (2009), 53–71.** Reconstructs dispersal of Le Plongeon-associated portable material into American collections.

**Lawrence G. Desmond and Phyllis M. Messenger, *A Dream of Maya: Augustus and Alice Le Plongeon in Nineteenth-Century Yucatan* (1988).** Foundational biographical and photographic reconstruction, cited through institutional and scholarly sources.

### Source limitations

No public source located in this research supplied all of the following for catalogue 05.0-01426:

- a modern metric measurement sheet;
- a verified contemporary weight;
- a petrographic analysis;
- quarry sourcing;
- pigment identification;
- residue analysis;
- a detailed conservation history;
- a complete accession-register transcription;
- a published transport manifest from Veracruz to Mexico City;
- a full diplomatic disposition of Le Plongeon’s compensation demand.

Those matters must therefore remain open rather than being filled from measurements or analyses of other chacmools.
