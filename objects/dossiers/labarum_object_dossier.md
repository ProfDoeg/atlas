# The labarum/Chi-Rho military standard: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary finding

The “labarum of Constantine” cannot presently be identified with any surviving artifact. No museum, church, treasury, or archaeological collection possesses a standard with a demonstrable chain of custody back to Constantine I. Its dimensions, weight, workshop, makers, date of manufacture, and fate are unrecorded.

Moreover, the evidence does not establish that every ancient reference concerns one continuously preserved object. Three related things must be distinguished:

1. **The sign reportedly placed on soldiers’ shields before the Battle of the Milvian Bridge in 312.** Lactantius describes this but does not describe a banner.
2. **The exceptionally rich imperial standard seen and described by Eusebius decades later.** This is the strongest literary evidence for an actual Constantinian labarum.
3. **Copies and successor standards.** Eusebius says Constantine ordered similar standards for all his armies; coins and monuments subsequently employ several different Chi-Rho and cross-bearing designs.

Accordingly, this dossier tracks the putative original—the sumptuous standard Eusebius says he personally saw—while treating battlefield predecessors, authorized copies, later imperial labara, palace relics, and modern reconstructions as separate objects unless continuity is independently demonstrated.

Evidentiary labels used below are:

- **Documented contemporary evidence:** surviving object, inscription, coin, or near-contemporary testimony.
- **Ancient reported testimony:** an ancient author’s assertion not independently verifiable.
- **Scholarly inference:** a reconstruction from incomplete evidence.
- **Late-antique or Byzantine tradition:** a later account of earlier events.
- **Institutional or devotional tradition:** a claim maintained in religious practice.
- **Modern reconstruction or invention:** a modern representation supplying details absent from early evidence.
- **Unresolved:** evidence does not permit a determination.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Object class

The labarum was an imperial Roman military standard of the general *vexillum* type: a long vertical pole crossed by a horizontal bar, from which a textile banner hung. In Constantine’s version, according to Eusebius, the structure was transformed into a conspicuously Christian—or eventually Christianized—imperial insignium incorporating a Christogram, precious materials, and portraits of the ruling dynasty.

It served simultaneously as:

- a military rallying standard;
- a portable image of imperial authority;
- a dynastic emblem;
- an asserted protective or victory-bearing object;
- a model copied for other formations;
- and, in later tradition, a relic of Constantine’s conversion and divinely sanctioned victory.

Calling the Chi-Rho itself “the labarum” is common modern usage but historically imprecise. The **Chi-Rho is a sign**; the **labarum is a standard that could carry or be surmounted by that sign**. Late Roman standards also existed with crosses, imperial portraits, stars, and other devices.

### Names and terminology

#### *Labarum*

- Latin: **labarum**, plural **labara**.
- Greek: **λάβαρον**, also transmitted in variant forms such as **λάβουρον**, **λάουρον**, and **λάβωρον**.
- Eusebius writes that the Romans “now call” the object *labaron*: **ὅπερ νῦν οἱ Ῥωμαῖοι λάβαρον καλοῦσιν**.
- English: *labarum*; sometimes “Constantine’s standard,” “standard of the Cross,” or “Chi-Rho standard.”
- French: *labarum*.
- German: *Labarum*.
- Italian and Spanish: *labaro*; Portuguese: *lábaro*.
- Modern Greek: **λάβαρο**, now also a generic word for a banner or ceremonial standard.

The etymology remains disputed. Proposed derivations from Latin *labor*, Greek words, Celtic or Basque roots, and a form related to *laureatum/laureata imago* have all appeared. Henri Grégoire proposed a relationship with the laurel-crowned imperial image borne on standards, but this has not produced consensus. Recent philological discussion generally treats Greek **λάβαρον** as a Hellenized form of Latin *labarum*, while acknowledging that the Latin word’s deeper origin is unknown. Claims that its derivation is securely Celtic, Basque, Germanic, or Mesopotamian should therefore be labeled hypotheses, not established etymology.

#### Chi-Rho

Greek: **ΧΡ**, normally superimposed as **☧**.

The name derives from:

- chi, **Χ**, the first letter of **Χριστός** (*Christos*, “Christ”);
- rho, **Ρ**, the second letter.

It is therefore a christogram, an abbreviation of Christ’s title/name. Its forms vary: upright or slanted rho; six- or eight-rayed arrangements; loop opening right or occasionally rendered differently; sometimes flanked by **Α** and **Ω**.

The Chi-Rho must not automatically be read as a depiction of the Crucifixion. In its primary graphic construction it abbreviates *Christos*. Ancient and late-antique writers nevertheless increasingly assimilated the Christogram, the cross-shaped structure of the standard, and the Cross itself.

#### “In this sign conquer”

The formulas later attached to the standard include:

- Greek: **τούτῳ νίκα** (*toutō nika*), “By this, conquer.”
- Latin: **in hoc signo vinces**, “In this sign you will conquer.”
- Latin numismatic variant: **HOC SIGNO VICTOR ERIS**, “By this sign you will be victorious.”

Eusebius places a short Greek message with the celestial sign in Constantine’s vision, not as an inscription on the textile banner. The widespread claim that **IN HOC SIGNO VINCES** or **ΤΟΥΤῼ ΝΙΚΑ** was physically embroidered on Constantine’s original banner is a later reconstruction, not something stated in Eusebius’s physical description.

### Eusebius’s physical description

The principal description occurs in *Life of Constantine* 1.30–31, written after Constantine’s death in 337, probably in the late 330s. Eusebius expressly says that he had personally seen the manufactured object.

According to his account:

- A **long spear or pole** formed the upright.
- It was **overlaid or sheathed with gold**.
- A **transverse bar** crossed it, producing a cross-like construction.
- At its summit was a **gold wreath set with precious stones**.
- Inside the wreath appeared the sign of the Savior’s name: chi and rho combined.
- A **square royal textile** hung from the crossbar.
- The cloth was richly embroidered, interwoven with gold, and covered with brilliant precious stones.
- On the upright, below the cross-shaped trophy and immediately above the textile, appeared **golden half-length portraits of Constantine and his children**.
- Eusebius’s wording implies a sumptuous, rigidly mounted imperial vexillum rather than a modern free-flying flag.

The surviving English translation may create ambiguity about the portraits’ exact number and position. Constantine had several sons, and scholars have consequently questioned whether the form Eusebius saw could have existed in 312. The dynastic portraits are one reason many date the fully developed standard to the 320s or 330s.

### Materials

**Ancient reported testimony:**

- structural pole or spear, probably wood or metal-cored, externally gold-covered;
- horizontal crosspiece;
- gold wreath;
- gemstones;
- purple or royal textile;
- gold thread or gold interlacing;
- additional gems attached to the textile;
- golden portrait medallions or busts.

No fragment survives for metallurgical, textile, pigment, gemological, or radiocarbon analysis. Consequently:

- the species of wood is unknown;
- the purity or alloy of the gold is unknown;
- “purple” cannot be assigned to Tyrian purple by scientific testing;
- the gemstones are not identified;
- construction joints, textile weave, embroidery method, and mounting system are unknown;
- no material provenance—from mine, loom, dyeworks, or treasury—is recoverable.

### Dimensions and weight

No ancient source gives measurements or weight. Reconstructions based on ordinary Roman *vexilla*, reliefs, or practical carrying requirements are comparative estimates, not measurements of Constantine’s standard.

Eusebius says selected guards carried it in turn on their shoulders. That implies substantial size or value but does not prove that it was exceptionally heavy: rotation could have been ceremonial or protective.

### Inscriptions

Eusebius records no inscription on the physical standard. Its meaningful signs were graphic:

- the Chi-Rho within the summit wreath;
- the portraits of Constantine and his sons;
- the cross-shaped pole and transverse bar.

A later ivory consular diptych made for Probus, about 406, depicts Honorius holding a Chi-Rho-topped standard bearing **IN NOMINE XPI VINCAS SEMPER**—“In the name of Christ, may you always conquer.” That inscription belongs to the depicted Theodosian-period standard, not demonstrably to Constantine’s object.

### Damage, repairs, and alterations

No repair, restoration, or physical alteration of the putative original is securely documented.

Eusebius reports one battle in which missiles lodged in the narrow area of the standard’s pole while its bearer remained unhurt. If historical, the event could have damaged the shaft. Eusebius does not say the standard was repaired, nor does he identify the battle. The story’s purpose is openly miraculous.

Later coins move the Christogram from the summit to the banner and sometimes substitute a cross. These may reflect actual changes in successor standards, different copies, or merely artistic conventions. They cannot be used to reconstruct alterations to one continuous object.

### What survives

No accepted physical component survives.

What does survive is an evidentiary dossier consisting of:

- Lactantius’s early account of a shield-sign;
- Eusebius’s purported eyewitness description;
- Constantinian and later coins;
- late Roman reliefs, ivories, mosaics, and manuscript images;
- late-antique historical and devotional retellings;
- Byzantine ceremonial references that may—or may not—refer to the original;
- modern ecclesiastical banners and replicas.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Roman military antecedents

The labarum’s basic form was not created from nothing. Roman armies already used:

- *vexilla*, textiles suspended from crossbars;
- eagles and other pole standards;
- *imagines*, standards carrying imperial portraits;
- *dracones*, windsock-like dragon standards;
- sacred standards that received honors and military oaths.

Roman military standards were both tactical instruments and holy embodiments of corporate identity. Constantine’s innovation, if reconstructed conservatively, lay in combining a traditional imperial and military support structure with a new celestial or Christian emblem and dynastic portraits.

There is no reliable evidence that every earlier imperial *vexillum* was itself called a *labarum*. Statements that Constantine merely replaced an eagle painted on a standard derive mainly from later antiquarian summaries, not from a surviving commission or contemporary technical description.

### Earliest literary attestation: Lactantius

The earliest extant narrative is Lactantius, *De mortibus persecutorum* 44.5–6, written in Latin within years of the Battle of the Milvian Bridge, conventionally dated around 313–316, though some scholars date completion later.

Lactantius says Constantine was warned in a dream to mark God’s heavenly sign on his soldiers’ shields before battle. His crucial text reads:

> *Commonitus est in quiete Constantinus, ut caeleste signum dei notaret in scutis atque ita proelium committeret. Facit ut iussus est et transversa X littera, summo capite circumflexo, Christum in scutis notat.*

A restrained translation is:

> Constantine was instructed in sleep to mark the heavenly sign of God on the shields and so enter battle. He did as commanded and, by means of a transverse letter X with its upper part bent around, marked Christ on the shields.

The precise sign remains disputed. Proposed readings include:

- a Chi-Rho;
- a staurogram or tau-rho;
- a cross-like monogram;
- an X with a curved stroke;
- or, according to a recent textual argument, a simpler cross whose more elaborate graphic description may be an intrusive gloss.

Most importantly, Lactantius:

- describes a dream, not a daylight apparition;
- places the sign on shields;
- does not use the word *labarum*;
- does not describe gold, gems, a banner, wreath, or portraits;
- does not report **in hoc signo vinces**.

Thus the earliest source attests a military sign associated with Christ, but not the elaborate object Eusebius later describes.

### The 310 pagan vision

A Latin panegyric delivered in 310 reports Constantine seeing Apollo accompanied by Victory, offering wreaths that foretold long rule. Scholars have variously understood this as:

- a separate pagan vision;
- a politically adaptable precursor to the Christian story;
- evidence that solar and Christian interpretations overlapped;
- or rhetoric unrelated to the later labarum.

This source prevents a simple assertion that every element of the later vision story was fixed in 312. It also helps explain scholarly proposals that Constantine’s new emblem was initially celestial or solar and only acquired an unambiguously Christian public interpretation over time.

### The Battle of the Milvian Bridge

On 28 October 312 Constantine defeated Maxentius outside Rome. The battle is secure history. That Constantine’s army used some unusual sign is supported most directly by Lactantius. The manufacture and presence there of Eusebius’s gem-encrusted labarum are not independently demonstrated.

The Arch of Constantine, dedicated in 315, attributes victory to divine prompting—*instinctu divinitatis*—but does not identify the Christian God or depict the Eusebian labarum. Constantine’s contemporary coinage continued for years to display Sol Invictus. These facts do not disprove a Christian commitment, but they show that official representation remained deliberately broad or mixed.

### Eusebius’s origin story

In *Life of Constantine* 1.28–31, Eusebius reports that Constantine told him—confirming the account with an oath—that around midday he and his army saw above the sun a cross-shaped trophy of light and words meaning “By this, conquer.” That night Christ appeared in a dream and instructed him to make a likeness of the sign as protection against enemies.

At dawn Constantine summoned craftsmen skilled in gold and precious stones, sat among them, explained the design, and ordered its fabrication. Eusebius then introduces the object he says he personally saw.

This is the first full manufacture story. It supplies:

- commissioner: Constantine;
- intended user: Constantine and his army;
- makers: unnamed goldworkers or jewelers;
- materials: gold, precious stones, rich cloth;
- design source: the reported celestial apparition;
- purpose: protection and victory;
- later copies: similar standards for all armies.

It supplies no:

- exact date of fabrication;
- city, palace, workshop, or mint;
- named artisan;
- contract, cost, weight, or dimensions;
- inventory entry;
- independently corroborated handover.

### When was the elaborate standard actually made?

Competing reconstructions include:

1. **312 manufacture:** Traditional reading of Eusebius. The standard was made immediately after the vision and preceded or followed Constantine’s victory over Maxentius.
2. **Between 312 and 324:** The original sign may have begun in a simpler form and developed into the richly jeweled standard used against Licinius.
3. **About 324 or later:** The fully Christianized labarum may belong to Constantine’s eastern victory, sole rule, and construction of Constantinople.
4. **Later dynastic remodeling:** A simpler object could have been augmented with portraits as Constantine’s sons became Caesars.
5. **Literary compression:** Eusebius may have retrojected the later object he saw into the 312 narrative.

The portrait detail is particularly important. If the standard bore recognizable portraits of multiple imperial children, its described form is difficult to place at the beginning of the Milvian campaign. The first unmistakable official numismatic representation of a Chi-Rho-topped labarum is generally dated to 327, although the Chi-Rho appears in other contexts earlier.

### Earliest material representations

#### Ticinum silver medallion, usually dated 315–319

Constantine wears a helmet bearing a small device widely interpreted as a Chi-Rho. It is evidence for the sign’s association with him, but:

- the reading of the tiny device has been debated;
- it is not an image of the labarum;
- it does not prove the Eusebian banner already existed in its final form.

#### Coinage, 319–324

Chi-Rho-like marks occur on coins as mintmarks or small devices. Some may reflect Christian intention; others may be workshop control marks. Their mere occurrence cannot always be treated as imperial theological declarations.

#### Constantinople bronze, RIC VII Constantinople 19, dated 327

This is the clearest Constantinian image of the standard. The reverse legend is **SPES PVBLICA**, “Hope of the Commonwealth.” It depicts:

- a labarum crowned by a Chi-Rho;
- a textile panel bearing three medallions;
- the shaft spearing or standing upon a serpent.

The three medallions correspond well to Eusebius’s dynastic portraits. The serpent has been read as the defeated Licinius and, in Christian interpretation, the serpent or dragon of evil. The coin demonstrates an official labarum design by 327; it does not prove the depicted standard was the exact artifact made in 312.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 312: reported military sign before Rome

**Ancient reported testimony:** Lactantius places the sign on soldiers’ shields immediately before the Milvian Bridge battle.

**Custody conclusion:** No discrete banner passes through anyone’s hands in this source. Treating Lactantius’s shield marks as the original labarum’s first field appearance conflates sign and object.

### 312–324: uncertain development

Eusebius retrospectively makes the standard a consequence of the vision and says Constantine continually used it. Historians cannot reconstruct its movements campaign by campaign.

Possible theaters include Constantine’s wars against:

- Maxentius in Italy, 312;
- Licinius in 316–317;
- Licinius again in 324, including operations at Adrianople, the Hellespont, and Chrysopolis.

Only the Licinian-war material in Eusebius provides an extended narrative about the standard’s battlefield use. The assertion that the sumptuous object was at every engagement is not independently documented.

### The special guard of fifty

In *Life of Constantine* 2.7–9, Eusebius says:

- Constantine selected fifty strong, brave, and pious members of his bodyguard;
- their sole duty was to surround, protect, and carry the standard;
- they bore it in turn on their shoulders;
- Constantine ordered it moved toward sectors where his forces were under pressure.

This is the closest thing to a recorded custodial institution. The men are unnamed. Modern scholars have tentatively connected them with the imperial guard and sometimes with the emerging *scholae palatinae*, but the organizational identification is an inference.

The standard was therefore represented not as private movable property entrusted to an individual heir but as controlled imperial regalia under a rotating palace or field guard.

### The unnamed bearer episode

Eusebius reports that during an unidentified battle:

1. the assigned bearer became afraid;
2. he handed the standard to another soldier and withdrew;
3. the first man was struck in the abdomen and killed;
4. the replacement remained unharmed;
5. missiles allegedly lodged in the standard’s shaft.

The episode presents transfer as an emergency battlefield handover rather than a legal change of possession. Neither bearer is named; the location and date are absent. It cannot be independently checked.

### Multiplication into copies

Eusebius states that Constantine commanded similar standards to be carried at the head of all his armies. This is decisive for questions of identity: already within Constantine’s reign, “the labarum” denoted both an exemplary imperial object and a class of copies.

A plausible reconstruction is:

- a primary personal or palace standard carried near Constantine;
- official duplicates distributed to field armies or elite formations;
- shield and helmet applications of the related sign;
- later variations produced without concern for exact replication.

No inventory enumerating the copies survives. No individual specimen can be followed afterward.

### Constantine and Eusebius, probably in the 330s

Eusebius says he saw the manufactured representation. He also says Constantine personally related the vision and bearer story to him during private leisure.

Their meeting place is not specified. Scholars often infer that Eusebius saw the object at Constantinople, probably in the imperial palace, during the later part of Constantine’s reign. This is plausible but not documented as an object-location statement.

### 337: Constantine’s death and the broken chain

Constantine died near Nicomedia in 337. The sources do not say:

- whether the original labarum accompanied his remains;
- whether it passed to Constantius II, Constantine II, or Constans;
- whether it remained in the palace;
- whether it was divided, remodeled, or replaced;
- whether one son received the original while the others used copies.

The chain of custody therefore breaks in 337, if not earlier.

### 337–361: the Constantinian sons and rival appropriations

Coinage under Constantine’s sons makes the labarum and Chi-Rho more common. This shows the emblem’s dynastic and imperial continuation, but it does not identify the original artifact.

In 350 Vetranio’s coinage depicted the ruler holding two standards and used the legend **HOC SIGNO VICTOR ERIS**. Magnentius also used large Christograms, sometimes flanked by alpha and omega. Constantius II likewise employed Christian standards.

These issues indicate competition for Constantinian legitimacy. They do not prove that Vetranio, Magnentius, or Constantius physically possessed Constantine’s original.

### 361–363: Julian

Christian sources accuse Julian of reversing Constantine’s reform of military standards.

- Gregory of Nazianzus says Julian acted against the “great symbol” that led the army together with the Cross.
- Sozomen, writing in the fifth century, says Julian restored the leading Roman standard to its former form after Constantine had reshaped it into the symbol of the Cross.
- A martyr narrative about Bonosus and Maximilianus depicts Christian standard-bearers refusing to remove a Christian sign.

These are polemical or hagiographic sources. They establish a late-antique memory that Julian paganized military insignia, but they do not record his destruction or confiscation of Constantine’s original labarum.

Claims that Julian abolished “the labarum” and Jovian restored precisely the same artifact exceed the evidence. What was reversed and restored may have been the design system of imperial standards.

### From Jovian to the Theodosian dynasty

Later histories and modern summaries describe Jovian as restoring the banner of the Cross after Julian. Again, this is better understood as restoration of Christian insignia than a documented recovery of a unique object.

Under Valentinian I, Valens, Gratian, Theodosius I, Arcadius, and Honorius:

- crosses increasingly appear in standards;
- Chi-Rho devices continue on shields and imperial insignia;
- court literature identifies the Christian standard with Constantine’s victory;
- the labarum becomes a general emblem of the Christian Roman emperor.

The iconographic changes may represent new manufactured standards. There is no continuous material provenance tying them to Constantine’s workshop.

### Circa 400: cross and Christogram standards

Jerome wrote around 403 that military banners carried signs of the Cross and imperial purple and diadems were decorated with the saving gibbet.

Prudentius, speaking through a poetic Theodosius, described:

- crosses gleaming in jewels or fashioned in gold on long shafts;
- Christ’s mark in jeweled gold upon the purple labarum;
- the Cross on the crest;
- Constantine rendered invincible by such a standard.

This is evidence for the ideology and appearance of contemporary standards, not an inventory of the original.

The Probus consular diptych, about 406, depicts Honorius with a Chi-Rho-topped standard inscribed **IN NOMINE XPI VINCAS SEMPER**. The object shown belongs to imperial representation under Honorius; it is not a portrait sufficiently exact to authenticate Constantine’s artifact.

### The Column of Arcadius

The Column of Arcadius, inaugurated in Constantinople in 403, was later destroyed, but early-modern drawings preserve its relief program. They show standards bearing crosses and Chi-Rho-like devices, including some among captured trophies.

If the drawings are accurate, they demonstrate that by the early fifth century the labarum type had become a symbol of Christian empire beyond the personal presence of the emperor. They do not document the original standard’s custody.

### Alleged preservation in the Great Palace

Later Byzantine tradition and some modern scholarship state that Constantine’s labarum was preserved in the imperial palace at Constantinople as a revered relic, guarded and brought out on great occasions.

The evidentiary problem is identity. Byzantine ceremonial texts refer to:

- imperial military banners;
- revered crosses;
- “the great and very beautiful and highly venerated cross of the emperor Constantine”;
- palace objects associated with Constantine.

Karin Krause has proposed that the “Cross of Constantine” venerated in palace ceremonies and carried with military standards may have been the original labarum or its transformed remains. This is a scholarly identification, not an explicit statement in the ceremonial text.

A tenth-century ceremony for Constantine’s feast involved:

- commemoration of the celestial vision;
- veneration at Constantine-associated sites;
- imperial and patriarchal reverence before Constantine’s Cross;
- movement of the great cross to Hagia Sophia together with imperial standards.

These details show that a major palace object was attributed to Constantine. They do not prove that it retained Eusebius’s purple textile, portrait medallions, jeweled wreath, or original pole.

### Possible transformation

If the palace “Cross of Constantine” was related to the labarum, several scenarios are possible:

1. the entire standard survived and was ceremonially reclassified as a cross;
2. only its jeweled summit or cross-shaped frame survived;
3. the banner and portraits were lost or removed;
4. a replacement inherited the original’s identity;
5. the palace cross was a distinct Constantinian relic;
6. the identification arose through ceremonial memory rather than material continuity.

No surviving Byzantine inventory resolves these alternatives.

### 1204 and afterward

The Fourth Crusade captured and extensively looted Constantinople in 1204. Palace and ecclesiastical treasuries were dispersed, destroyed, or exported. No securely identified labarum appears in the known Latin lists of booty or surviving Western reliquaries.

It is tempting to make 1204 the object’s destruction or disappearance date, but that is unproved because:

- it is not certain the original survived until then;
- it is not certain the palace cross was the labarum;
- no source records crusaders taking or destroying it;
- the Great Palace was already less central as an imperial residence.

The final fate could have involved earlier decay, stripping of precious materials, reuse, accidental destruction, iconoclasm, replacement, loss during palace abandonment, the sack of 1204, or later conquest. None can be established.

### 1453

The Ottoman conquest of Constantinople is often invoked in popular accounts as the terminal event for missing Byzantine regalia. No contemporary source identifies Constantine’s labarum among objects destroyed, captured, or preserved in 1453. Making 1453 its documented destruction date is therefore unwarranted.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Did the object exist?

The strongest affirmative evidence is Eusebius’s statement that he personally saw a richly made standard. Even if his account of its supernatural origin is rejected, that sentence is evidence that a court object matching his description existed in the later Constantinian period.

Supporting material evidence includes the 327 coin showing a Chi-Rho-topped standard with dynastic medallions. The correspondence is substantial, though coins are stylized ideological images.

The remaining possibilities are:

- Eusebius accurately described an actual standard;
- he described a later court standard while placing its creation too early;
- he synthesized features of more than one standard;
- or parts of his account are literary idealization.

Complete fabrication is possible in the abstract but is not the strongest explanation for the convergence between purported eyewitness description and official imagery.

### Was it present at the Milvian Bridge?

**Traditional position:** Yes; it was manufactured following the vision and used as Constantine’s victorious standard.

**Critical position:** Lactantius records shield marks only, while unmistakable labarum imagery appears later. Eusebius may have projected a mature standard of the 320s–330s back into 312.

**Intermediate position:** Constantine used a simpler celestial or Christ-related sign in 312; the jeweled dynastic labarum developed from it.

The intermediate reconstruction best explains the textual and numismatic sequence but remains an inference.

### Chi-Rho, staurogram, solar sign, or cross?

Lactantius’s graphic description is difficult. Eusebius’s sign is a Chi-Rho inside a wreath, while his entire standard is cross-shaped. Later writers often call it simply the Cross.

Scholarly positions have included:

- explicitly Christian Chi-Rho from 312;
- staurogram;
- solar halo or astral phenomenon reinterpreted as Christian;
- religiously ambiguous “supreme deity” sign;
- a sign whose Christian meaning intensified during the wars with Licinius;
- rhetorical invention or textual modification by Christian authors.

No physical original exists to settle the design.

### Did Eusebius knowingly misdate it?

Some scholars have charged Eusebius with anachronism, propaganda, or retrospective Christianization. Others stress that:

- he claimed Constantine as his sworn source;
- he personally saw the object;
- Christians could have used a sign privately before it became prominent in coinage;
- official religious ambiguity does not exclude Constantine’s personal Christian interpretation.

Eusebius’s apologetic and imperial purposes require critical handling but do not permit automatic dismissal of every material detail.

### Was there one original?

Eusebius’s own text both singles out “this representation” and says similar standards were made for all armies. Consequently, “originality” could mean:

- the first object commissioned from the vision;
- Constantine’s personal standard;
- the most precious palace exemplar;
- any official copy made under his authority;
- or the evolving imperial type.

Ancient users may not have applied the modern museum concept of immutable object identity. Replacement of textiles, remounting of gems, or duplication would not necessarily have invalidated the standard’s authority.

### Was the Byzantine “Cross of Constantine” the labarum?

**Case for:**

- attributed to Constantine;
- exceptionally large, beautiful, and revered;
- held in the imperial ceremonial environment;
- associated with the vision;
- processed with military standards;
- the labarum itself was cross-shaped.

**Case against:**

- Byzantine sources call it a cross, not unambiguously the labarum;
- Constantine was associated with several cross relics and monuments;
- no description matches Eusebius’s cloth, portraits, and wreath;
- centuries of undocumented continuity intervene;
- the object may have been a later relic, replacement, or separate ceremonial cross.

Status: **unresolved and probably unresolvable without new textual evidence**.

### Scientific examination

None has been performed on a recognized original because no recognized original survives.

There are therefore no:

- radiocarbon results;
- textile-fiber analyses;
- gold-alloy or gemstone tests;
- dendrochronological studies;
- conservation reports;
- accession records;
- forensic continuity studies.

Scientific examination of surviving Constantinian coins can authenticate and characterize those coins, but cannot authenticate the lost standard.

### Rival claimants and forgeries

No major museum or church currently advances a securely catalogued object as Constantine’s original labarum. Numerous commercial and ecclesiastical replicas exist, but they are normally presented as reconstructions or symbolic banners.

Claims online that particular modern banners duplicate the original inscription, exact colors, or proportions are usually unsupported. Especially problematic is the recurrent placement of **IN HOC SIGNO VINCES** on the fabric as though Eusebius recorded it there.

No famous early-modern or modern forged “original labarum” has achieved the institutional status associated with major forged relics. The dominant authenticity problem is not a rival claimant but conflation:

- sign with standard;
- 312 shield marks with Eusebius’s later object;
- original with copies;
- palace cross with original labarum;
- modern reconstruction with ancient specification.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Constantine I

**Connection:** commissioner, reported visionary, owner/controller, military user, and principal source for Eusebius’s account.

**Dates:** reported creation from 312 onward; secure reign 306–337.

**Evidence:** Lactantius; Eusebius; coins and imperial imagery.

### Unnamed Constantinian craftsmen

**Connection:** Eusebius says Constantine summoned artisans skilled in gold and precious stones, described the sign, and ordered them to reproduce it.

**Date:** traditionally 312; possibly later.

**Evidence:** *Life of Constantine* 1.30.

Their names, guild, workshop, origin, payment, and subsequent custody are unknown.

### Constantine’s sons

Probably Crispus, Constantine II, Constantius II, and/or Constans, depending on the date and interpretation of the portrait group.

**Connection:** represented in golden busts or medallions on the standard; the portraits projected dynastic continuity.

**Evidence:** Eusebius’s description and the three medallions depicted on the 327 coin.

Which children were represented is not stated.

### Eusebius of Caesarea

**Connection:** author who says he saw the object and heard the vision and bearer stories from Constantine.

**Date:** observation probably in the later reign; composition after 337.

He was not a custodian. His engagement is testimonial and interpretive.

### Lactantius

**Connection:** earliest surviving author describing the pre-battle shield sign.

**Date:** early fourth century.

He does not attest the sumptuous standard.

### Maxentius

**Connection:** opponent at the Milvian Bridge. Later tradition makes the sign or labarum the instrument of his defeat.

**Date:** 312.

He never possessed the object in surviving accounts.

### Licinius

**Connection:** opponent during the period when Eusebius most fully depicts the standard’s battlefield activity. The serpent pierced by the labarum on the 327 coin has often been interpreted as Licinius or as diabolical evil assimilated to him.

**Dates:** wars ending in 324.

The identification of the serpent specifically with Licinius is interpretive.

### Fifty unnamed guards

**Connection:** rotating bearers and dedicated protectors of the standard.

**Evidence:** Eusebius, *Life of Constantine* 2.8.

Possible relationship to the imperial bodyguard or *scholae palatinae* is scholarly inference.

### The unnamed fleeing and replacement bearers

**Connection:** battlefield transfer in Eusebius’s miracle story.

No names, unit, battle, or date survive.

### Constantius II, Vetranio, and Magnentius

**Connection:** deployed Chi-Rho and labarum imagery during succession conflict and civil war.

**Dates:** especially 350–353.

Their imagery competed for Constantinian legitimacy; possession of the original is not established.

### Julian

**Connection:** accused in Christian sources of removing Christian signs and restoring older standard forms.

**Dates:** 361–363.

No destruction of the original is documented.

### Jovian

**Connection:** traditionally credited with restoring Christian standards after Julian.

**Date:** 363–364.

The claim concerns official symbolism more securely than custody of one artifact.

### Gregory of Nazianzus

**Connection:** wrote polemically about Julian’s attack on the “great symbol” leading the army with the Cross.

**Date:** fourth century.

He is a witness to Christian memory and terminology, not a known keeper.

### Jerome

**Connection:** described crosses on contemporary military banners.

**Date:** about 403.

His testimony concerns the class of standards.

### Prudentius

**Connection:** poetically linked Theodosian cross standards to Constantine’s invincibility and described a jeweled Christ-sign on purple cloth.

**Date:** late fourth/early fifth century.

### Probus and Honorius

**Connection:** the consular diptych of Probus portrays Honorius holding an inscribed Chi-Rho standard.

**Date:** about 406.

This is a successor image, not the original.

### Imperial palace and guard institutions

The Great Palace at Constantinople is the most plausible later repository if the original survived. The palace guard and ceremonial departments would have controlled access and movement. Precise offices and inventories are not preserved for the original.

### Christ

**Connection in Christian interpretation:** the Chi-Rho encodes *Christos*; Eusebius says Christ appeared in the dream and ordered the sign copied.

**Evidentiary status:** ancient Christian testimony and doctrine, not independently verifiable material history.

### The Christian God

**Connection:** source of the “heavenly sign,” protection, and victory in Lactantius and Eusebius.

### Apollo and Sol

**Connection:** Constantine’s 310 vision involved Apollo and Victory; contemporary coins continued to honor Sol. Scholars debate whether solar imagery influenced the sign’s form or enabled religious ambiguity.

These deities are part of the interpretive environment, not reported owners of the labarum.

### The Cross

Not a person or deity, but increasingly treated as an active sacred power. Late-antique authors fused the labarum’s identity with the Cross of Christ and the emerging relic cult of the True Cross.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Tactical function

Like other military standards, the labarum could:

- indicate the commander’s position;
- organize movement;
- rally troops;
- transmit direction;
- provide a visible focus in battle.

Eusebius specifically says Constantine ordered it moved toward sections of the army under pressure.

### Protective function

Eusebius calls it a saving sign, trophy, and safeguard. Its asserted powers included:

- causing enemies to flee;
- giving soldiers renewed courage;
- protecting faithful bearers;
- attracting missiles into the shaft rather than the bearer;
- defeating hostile or demonic power.

These are reported beliefs and miracle claims, not empirically verified properties.

### Dynastic representation

The imperial portraits made the standard a portable presence of Constantine and his sons. Roman soldiers already honored imperial images and swore military oaths before standards. The labarum redirected that system through a Christian or Christianizing symbol.

Its three levels communicated:

1. heavenly sovereignty: Christogram;
2. victory: wreath and cross-shaped trophy;
3. earthly dynasty: imperial portraits.

### Legitimating function

After Constantine, possession or representation of a labarum meant more than Christian profession. It asserted succession to the victor chosen by heaven.

Vetranio’s **HOC SIGNO VICTOR ERIS** coinage and Magnentius’s monumental Christograms demonstrate that rival claimants could appropriate the sign. It was not confined to one theological faction or secure dynasty.

### Standard cult and Christian transformation

Roman standards had long been sacred military objects. Constantine’s innovation did not abolish the emotional and ritual structure of standard veneration; it changed its imagery and interpretation.

This creates a tension visible in the sources:

- Christian writers rejected pagan idols;
- yet Eusebius described the labarum in language resembling a sacred, protective military talisman;
- no prominent contemporary Christian polemic in the surviving record condemns Constantine’s treatment of it as idolatrous.

Modern scholars have compared its narrated battlefield function to the Ark of the Covenant: a divine sign transported to the critical point in battle and guarded by selected custodians.

### Oaths and honors

No surviving document records the exact oath formula sworn before Constantine’s labarum. Roman practice strongly supports standards and imperial images as oath-foci, but applying a precise ritual to this object is inference.

### Palace ritual

If the Byzantine “Cross of Constantine” was the labarum or its transformed core, it participated in:

- imperial feast-day procession;
- joint veneration by emperor and patriarch;
- movement from palace-associated space to Hagia Sophia;
- ceremonial association with current military standards.

This would mark a shift from battlefield talisman to palace relic. The identification remains disputed.

### Who could touch or carry it?

Eusebius’s account suggests restricted handling:

- fifty specially selected guards;
- carrying in rotation;
- vigilant protection;
- proximity to the emperor.

He does not state that others were legally or ritually forbidden to touch it. Later assertions of absolute access prohibitions are embellishments unless tied to specific Byzantine ceremonial rules.

### Symbolic diffusion

The standard’s component signs migrated far beyond the object:

- helmets and shields;
- coins and medallions;
- imperial textiles;
- sarcophagi;
- church mosaics;
- manuscripts and ivories;
- processional banners;
- liturgical ornaments;
- military insignia;
- civic, fraternal, and denominational logos.

By the fifth century, the cross could replace the Chi-Rho within a labarum without erasing its Constantinian meaning.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The celestial vision

#### Eusebian form

At midday Constantine and the army see a luminous cross-shaped trophy above the sun, accompanied by words meaning “By this, conquer.” Constantine is perplexed; at night Christ appears with the same sign and commands him to make its likeness. Craftsmen produce the standard. Constantine then seeks Christian instruction.

**Status:** ancient attested narrative, written decades after the event and attributed by Eusebius to Constantine’s sworn testimony.

#### Lactantian form

Constantine receives a dream instruction to place God’s heavenly sign on shields. There is no waking vision, no army as collective witness, no inscription, no craftsmen, and no labarum.

**Status:** earlier and simpler ancient narrative.

#### Rufinus’s form

Around 402 Rufinus adapted the story into a vision of a fiery cross, angels, **τούτῳ νίκα**, and transformation of military banners into the form of the Lord’s Cross.

**Status:** late-fourth/early-fifth-century elaboration. It reflects the period’s growing identification of the labarum with the Cross.

### The invulnerable bearer

The faithful replacement bearer survives a hail of missiles; the man who abandoned the standard dies. The shaft itself catches the weapons.

The story teaches that:

- sacred protection follows fidelity;
- cowardice and abandonment bring punishment;
- the standard’s power is conditional upon proper service.

**Status:** Eusebian miracle account, attributed to Constantine; battle, participants, and corroboration absent.

### Guaranteed victory

Eusebius says wherever the standard appeared, enemies fled and Constantine’s troops regained strength.

This is religious-imperial propaganda as well as miracle narrative. The practical effect of a revered rallying standard on morale is plausible, but universal supernatural efficacy is a faith claim.

### The serpent pierced by the labarum

The 327 **SPES PVBLICA** coin depicts the standard overcoming a serpent. Interpretations include:

- Licinius;
- defeated persecution;
- civil and military enemies;
- the dragon of Revelation;
- generalized cosmic evil.

Eusebius elsewhere describes a palace image of Constantine and his sons above a dragon or serpent thrust down by the saving sign. The iconography fuses political victory with biblical demonology. The coin does not label the serpent.

### “In hoc signo vinces” embroidered on the original

This is one of the most persistent later accretions. Eusebius attaches words to the celestial vision but omits them from his detailed description of the manufactured standard. Modern art and replicas routinely place the Latin sentence on the cloth.

**Status:** later artistic and devotional convention, not established physical fact.

### Immediate Christianization of the whole Roman army

Traditional summaries sometimes state that Constantine replaced all Roman eagles with the labarum immediately after 312.

The evidence does not support so complete or instantaneous a reform:

- pagan and traditional military imagery persisted;
- eagles continued in literary and numismatic evidence;
- Eusebius’s statements may concern selected imperial or army standards;
- broader transformation unfolded across the fourth century.

### Preservation for centuries in Constantinople

The tradition that the original remained under continuous guard in the Great Palace is plausible in outline but not demonstrable link by link. Later palace sources preserve the memory of Constantinian relics and a great Cross of Constantine. Modern writers have often compressed inference into certainty.

**Status:** Byzantine association plus modern scholarly hypothesis; continuous identity unproved.

### Destruction in 1204 or 1453

Neither date is supported by a source specifically naming the object. Both are reasonable contexts for loss but remain hypotheses.

### Constantine as inventor of the Chi-Rho

The letters Χ and Ρ existed, of course, and were used as abbreviations and graphic marks before Constantine. The extent of securely Christian Chi-Rho use before him remains debated. Constantine did not invent the letters or monogrammatic practice; his court’s use made this particular christogram an extraordinarily powerful imperial-Christian emblem.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Status

**Lost; possibly destroyed, dismantled, transformed, or replaced.**

No institution possesses an authenticated Constantinian original. There is no accession number, conservation file, legal owner, or current custodian.

### Last plausible location

If Eusebius saw the principal standard in the later reign, the most plausible location is Constantine’s imperial court, possibly the Great Palace at Constantinople. This is inference.

If the Byzantine “Cross of Constantine” was the standard’s surviving core, it remained within the Constantinopolitan palace ceremonial system into the middle Byzantine period. That identification is unresolved.

### Condition

Unknown. Organic textile would be highly vulnerable to:

- light;
- humidity;
- insects;
- repeated processional use;
- fire;
- deliberate stripping of jewels and gold;
- remounting.

Even if a metal summit survived for centuries, replacement of its textile and pole would be unsurprising. No source records such conservation.

### Legal status and disputes

There is no active repatriation or ownership proceeding because no recognized object exists.

The relevant modern dispute is historiographic: whether Byzantine references prove physical continuity and whether particular representations accurately preserve the design.

### Candidate or substitute objects

No serious physical claimant stands in for the original. Its most important proxies are:

- RIC VII Constantinople 19 coins in collections including the American Numismatic Society and other museums;
- the Ticinum medallion;
- later coins of Vetranio, Constantius II, Magnentius, Valens, Theodosius, and their successors;
- the Probus consular diptych at Aosta;
- drawings of the lost Column of Arcadius;
- mosaics such as the imperial panel at San Vitale, Ravenna, whose guards bear Chi-Rho shields;
- modern processional labara.

These are witnesses to the type and reception, not identity claimants.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Ecclesiastical banners

“Labarum” became a generic name for Christian processional banners in several traditions. Modern examples may carry:

- the Chi-Rho;
- Christ;
- the Virgin and Child;
- saints;
- crosses;
- **Α** and **Ω**;
- **IN HOC SIGNO VINCES**;
- parish or confraternity insignia.

Their structure often resembles a Roman vexillum, but colors, inscriptions, fringe, icons, and proportions generally reflect later liturgical art.

### Catholic and Orthodox reception

Constantine’s vision and labarum remain prominent in Catholic and Orthodox historical and devotional imagery. Orthodox liturgical commemoration of Constantine and Helena preserves the celestial Cross narrative. In both Eastern and Western Christianity the Chi-Rho functions independently of any military banner.

A banner at Sant’Apollinare in Classe, for example, demonstrates how the general labarum form could carry other Christian inscriptions: the depicted banner bears **ΑΓΙΟC ΑΓΙΟC ΑΓΙΟC**, “Holy, holy, holy,” rather than a Chi-Rho.

### Greek national reception

The Greek word **λάβαρο** became a broader term for sacred or national banners. Greek independence memory includes the “Holy Lavaro” of Agia Lavra, traditionally associated with the blessing of revolutionary arms in 1821.

That object and story belong to Greek national history; they are not a preserved Constantinian labarum. The shared name illustrates how Constantine’s standard became a category of sacred-political banner.

### Art

Major visual reinterpretations include:

- medieval manuscript illuminations of Constantine’s vision;
- Renaissance and Baroque cycles presenting a cross or inscribed banner;
- Raphael’s Vatican imagery of the vision and battle;
- Rubens’s Constantine tapestry designs;
- nineteenth-century historical paintings;
- church murals and icons.

Artists commonly supplied:

- a Latin inscription;
- a simple cross;
- a modern flag-shaped cloth;
- ranks of uniformly Christian soldiers;
- or an anachronistically complete version before the Milvian Bridge.

These images materially shaped popular memory but cannot be treated as evidence for the ancient object.

### Political and military mobilization

The phrase **in hoc signo vinces** has been used by:

- Christian military organizations;
- schools and universities;
- fraternities;
- political movements;
- heraldic bodies;
- commercial brands.

The Chi-Rho has likewise been mobilized across a wide ideological range, from ordinary church use to militant Christian nationalism. Such use invokes Constantine’s victory while often severing the sign from the complicated fourth-century evidence.

### Coins, replicas, and collecting

Modern replicas typically follow the 327 coin or a hybrid of that image and Eusebius’s text. Common features include:

- purple square cloth;
- gold fringe;
- three portrait medallions;
- a jeweled wreath;
- summit Chi-Rho;
- Latin victory motto.

Only the pole, crossbar, wreath, Christogram, jeweled royal cloth, and dynastic portraits have direct Eusebian support. Exact purple shade, fringe, portrait arrangement, and written motto are reconstructed.

### Literature and popular culture

The labarum appears in historical novels, documentaries, games, reenactment equipment, religious apologetics, and alternative histories. These treatments frequently collapse the dream, celestial vision, shield-sign, and mature palace standard into one moment on 27–28 October 312.

### Modern pseudo-historical claims

Recurring unsupported claims include:

- exact recovery of the banner’s proportions;
- certainty that its cloth bore the Latin motto;
- derivation from an ancient Basque *lauburu*;
- survival in a named modern church without provenance;
- use as the universal state flag of Byzantium in the modern sense;
- proof that Constantine’s vision was a particular comet, halo, planetary conjunction, or solar phenomenon.

Astronomical or meteorological interpretations may be hypotheses about the reported vision; none authenticates the object or establishes what Constantine saw.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Principal primary evidence

The indispensable texts are:

- Lactantius, *De mortibus persecutorum* 44;
- Eusebius, *Life of Constantine* 1.28–32, 1.37–40, 2.3–9, 3.3 and 4.20–21;
- Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 9.9;
- the Panegyric of 310;
- Gregory of Nazianzus, *Oration* 4.66;
- Sozomen, *Ecclesiastical History* 5.17;
- Jerome, *Letter* 107.2;
- Prudentius, *Contra Symmachum*;
- Rufinus’s continuation/translation of Eusebius;
- numismatic and monumental evidence.

### Source-critical problem

Lactantius is earlier but describes only shields. Eusebius is later and much fuller. His earlier *Ecclesiastical History* does not give the developed celestial-vision narrative found in the *Life*.

This generated several positions:

- Eusebius learned the account directly from Constantine only later;
- the story evolved at court;
- Eusebius reshaped memory for apologetic purposes;
- an editor interpolated or altered material;
- or differing genres explain the earlier silence.

There is no scholarly consensus reducing the accounts to a single undisputed chronology.

### Henri Grégoire and skepticism

Henri Grégoire argued forcefully against the traditional vision narrative and explored the word’s etymology and the possibility of later construction. His radical skepticism was highly influential but has not eliminated more moderate reconstructions.

### Andreas Alföldi and Constantinian iconography

Alföldi’s work emphasized coins, medallions, helmets, and imperial symbolism. The numismatic sequence remains central because it establishes what the state actually struck and circulated, even though it cannot directly reveal personal belief.

### Patrick Bruun

Bruun treated the later fourth-century expansion of the Christogram and labarum as part of a “struggle for the Constantinian heritage.” This explains why rival emperors and usurpers employed the sign without proving possession of an original relic.

### Peter Weiss and the solar-halo interpretation

Weiss connected Constantinian vision traditions with solar phenomena and the 310 panegyric. Related models propose a halo, parhelion, or cross-like solar display. These interpretations attempt to explain an experiential core but remain hypotheses dependent on a retrospective text.

### Modern work on graphic authority

Ildar Garipzanov and other scholars have approached Christograms as graphic signs of imperial authority rather than treating every occurrence as a transparent statement of personal conversion. This helps distinguish:

- local mintmarks;
- official central designs;
- apotropaic signs;
- dynastic insignia;
- later doctrinal symbolism.

### Joaquín Serrano del Pozo

Serrano del Pozo’s study of the Constantinian labarum and military standards stresses:

- Roman and non-Roman antecedents;
- ambiguity in the early sign;
- the labarum’s role as a charismatic object;
- its connection with the imperial bodyguard;
- reproduction for military forces;
- competition for Constantinian legitimacy;
- and the eventual dominance of the Cross over the Chi-Rho in standard imagery.

His later doctoral research develops the labarum as a Christianized apotropaic and charismatic object.

### Karin Krause and the palace cross hypothesis

Krause connects the original labarum with the “great and highly venerated Cross of Constantine” in Byzantine palace ceremonies. The proposal gives the object a possible medieval afterlife, but the identification depends on functional, spatial, and ceremonial correspondences rather than an explicit chain-of-custody statement.

### Unpublished and lost evidence

Major gaps include:

- the imperial workshop record;
- military inventories;
- palace treasury inventories from the relevant early centuries;
- the names and testimony of the bearers;
- a complete material description from another eyewitness;
- the fate of Constantinian palace treasures;
- physical remains of the standard;
- reliable records of replacement or repair.

### Discredited or overstated propositions still repeated

The following should not be presented as established:

- that Lactantius describes a labarum;
- that Eusebius proves the finished standard was at the Milvian Bridge;
- that Constantine immediately replaced every Roman eagle;
- that **IN HOC SIGNO VINCES** was embroidered on the original;
- that all Chi-Rho marks on early coins were centrally ordered Christian declarations;
- that Julian destroyed the original;
- that Jovian recovered the same artifact;
- that the palace Cross of Constantine certainly was the labarum;
- that the standard is documented as destroyed in 1204 or 1453;
- that an extant modern banner is the original.
