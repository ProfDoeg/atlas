# The Ishtar Gate: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

“The Ishtar Gate” presently denotes three related but non-identical things:

1. **The ancient architectural complex at Babylon**: a monumental, double gateway rebuilt in several stages under Nebuchadnezzar II in the sixth century BCE. Its foundations and lower walls remain in situ in Iraq.
2. **The Berlin museum reconstruction**: a reduced reconstruction of the façade of the ancient gate’s final glazed phase, assembled from excavated Babylonian fragments and extensive modern fabric and opened in 1930. It is not the complete ancient gate bodily transferred to Germany.
3. **Modern reconstructions at Babylon and elsewhere**: twentieth-century representations that reproduce the gate’s appearance but generally do not possess its material identity.

This dossier tracks the ancient monument first, then the archaeological fragments and reconstructions into which its fabric was dispersed. “Original” below means material made for the Neo-Babylonian building, not necessarily material still occupying its ancient position.

Evidence is labeled where necessary as **primary inscription**, **archaeological finding**, **scientific finding**, **documented modern history**, **scholarly reconstruction**, **institutional claim**, **political claim**, or **popular error**.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity, class, and names

The object was a **fortified ceremonial city gate**, not a freestanding triumphal arch. It pierced the northern defenses of Babylon’s eastern inner city and formed part of a double wall-and-gate system connected to the Processional Way.

Its ordinary Akkadian designation was the **Gate of Ištar**, conventionally normalized as *abullu ša Ištar* or represented in Neo-Babylonian sources by *Abul-Ištar*. Its ceremonial name was:

**Ištar-sākipat-tēbîša**  
“Ištar overthrows its assailant(s).”

The spelling and grammatical normalization vary slightly in modern publications—*sākipat tēbîša*, *sakipat-tebišu*, and similar forms occur—but the meaning is substantially agreed. The name belongs to the sacral topography in which Babylon’s gates bore divine names and protective ceremonial titles. The gate appears in the Babylonian topographical tradition conventionally called *Tintir = Babylon*. An open-access modern topographical synthesis locates it at approximately UTM 445780E, 3600820N, excavation grid Kasr 17–18u. [ORACC divine/topographical-name entry](https://build-oracc.museum.upenn.edu/btto/cbd/qpn/x000008500.html); [Pedersén, *Babylon: The Great City*](https://www.zaphon.de/WebRoot/Store28/Shops/83179382/MediaGallery/Pedersen-Babylon-Zaphon-2021-100dpi.pdf).

Relevant modern names include:

- English: **Ishtar Gate**
- German: **Ischtar-Tor**, increasingly **Ištar-Tor**
- French: **Porte d’Ishtar**
- Arabic: **بوابة عشتار**, *Bawwābat ʿIshtār*
- Turkish: **İştar Kapısı**

Ištar was the Akkadian form of the name of a major Mesopotamian goddess associated, in different contexts, with war, sexuality, political power, and the planet Venus. The gate’s animal program was not confined to her emblem.

### Architectural form

The excavated complex was a **double gate**:

- a smaller northern or outer gate;
- an intervening court;
- a larger southern or inner gate;
- flanking towers incorporated into the massive inner defensive walls.

The passage through the complete complex was approximately 48 metres long. Published excavation reconstructions give the northern gateway and towers a total width of about 28 metres and a depth near 11 metres. The southern gate chamber measured approximately 14.9 × 8.05 metres. The doorway was about 4.5 metres wide. These figures concern the excavated ancient plan; they should not be confused with the dimensions of the Berlin display.

No ancient wooden door leaves survive. Nebuchadnezzar’s inscriptions report doors of cedar and metal fittings or cladding. Their precise appearance, mass, and mechanical arrangement remain reconstructive.

One recent archaeological synthesis estimates that the entire gate complex contained approximately **63,000 cubic metres of masonry** and required some **5.7 million baked bricks**. This estimate concerns the enormous complete structure, including its buried and structural mass, not merely its ornamented façade. [Royal Inscriptions of Babylonia online corpus](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005473).

### Construction phases

Koldewey distinguished three principal Neo-Babylonian phases, associated with repeated raising of the Processional Way:

1. **First phase**: molded animal reliefs in unglazed brick.
2. **Intermediate phase**: a higher version necessitated by accumulated fills and the raising of the roadway, again with modeled animals, with some evidence of white coating or preparatory surface treatment.
3. **Final phase**: a monumental façade of molded, fired bricks with colored glaze, dominated by blue fields and rows of bulls and *mušḫuššu* creatures.

The earlier stages were not simply prototypes discarded elsewhere. As the street level rose, portions became buried and thereby survived in situ. The final elevation was largely lost to collapse and later brick removal; its recoverable glazed surface reached excavators mostly as displaced fragments.

Recent study of the middle street levels continues to refine the relative sequence. [German Archaeological Institute, “The Processional Street at the Ishtar Gate in Babylon”](https://publications.dainst.org/journals/zora/article/view/4471).

### Materials and fabrication

The gate’s structural materials included:

- sun-dried mud brick in portions of the mass;
- kiln-fired clay brick;
- bitumen mortar and waterproofing;
- molded fired-brick reliefs;
- colored silicate glaze;
- cedar for the lost doors, according to royal inscriptions;
- metal, probably including bronze or copper, for fittings and cladding described textually but no longer present.

The blue finish imitated the visual prestige of *uqnû*, “lapis lazuli” or a lapis-like deep-blue material. Popular descriptions sometimes say the gate was built of lapis lazuli; that is false. It was brickwork faced with blue-glazed ceramic.

Relief animals were planned across multiple specially molded bricks. Assembly marks and the regular disposition of components enabled figures to be built course by course. The colored glazes included blue backgrounds with yellow, white, brown, black, and other details. Because fragments underwent ancient collapse, salt contamination, excavation, desalination, cutting, filling, repainting, and remounting, the surfaces visible in Berlin are a composite of ancient glaze, modern repairs, and modern replacement bricks.

### Animal and ornamental program

The gate proper carried alternating horizontal registers of:

- **aurochs or wild bulls**, associated with the storm god Adad;
- **mušḫuššu**, horned, long-necked, scaled composite creatures with leonine forelegs, avian hind feet, and serpent-like bodies and tails, associated especially with Marduk and also Nabû.

The adjacent Processional Way displayed striding lions, the animal most closely associated with Ištar in this ensemble, and floral ornament. Lions visible in the museum’s connected display therefore belong primarily to the Processional Way, not to the gate façade proper.

Published estimates propose at least 575 bull and dragon images in the complete gate complex. That number is a reconstruction, not an ancient inventory. The Berlin workshops assembled 72 animal reliefs for the gate, Processional Way, and related Babylonian façades; this figure must not be presented as the ancient total. [Staatliche Museen zu Berlin, “From Fragment to Monument”](https://www.smb.museum/en/museums-institutions/vorderasiatisches-museum/exhibitions/detail/from-fragment-to-monument/).

### Inscriptions

The principal gate inscription is an Akkadian building inscription of Nebuchadnezzar II written in monumental cuneiform across glazed bricks. In substance it identifies the king, describes the earlier gates as having become too low as the streets were raised, reports excavation down to groundwater, and records rebuilding with bitumen, fired bricks, blue-glazed bricks, bulls, and fearsome dragons.

A concise translation of the central passage is:

> Both gate entrances had become too low because the street of Babylon had been raised. I excavated those gates, established their foundations at the level of the water with bitumen and baked brick, and had them skillfully built with baked bricks colored with shining blue glaze, upon which wild bulls and ferocious dragons were fashioned. I adorned them with luxurious splendor for all humankind to behold in wonder.

Wording differs among editions because the text survives in architectural fragments and parallel royal inscriptions. The phrase often reproduced online as “pure blue stone” should be understood as royal lapis imagery applied to glazed brick, not a geological description.

Royal inscriptions also associate the gate with Babylon’s walls **Imgur-Enlil** and **Nēmetti-Enlil**, whose spellings in older publications are often Imgur-Bel and Nimitti-Bel. [ORACC, Nebuchadnezzar II inscriptions](https://www.linearhistory.com/en/cuneiform/babylon7-q005492); [Koldewey in English translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/71398/pg71398-images.html).

### Berlin reconstruction

The Vorderasiatisches Museum catalogues the reconstructed final phase as:

- **Title**: *Ischtar-Tor (III. Baustufe)*
- **Object ID**: obj:1744146
- **Inventory coverage of incorporated original elements**: VA Bab 01408–VA Bab 01456 and VA Bab 07661
- **Material/technique**: ceramic, glazed
- **Museum dimensions**: height 14.75 m; width 26.41 m; thickness 4.38 m
- **Date assigned**: seventh–sixth century BCE
- **Institution**: Vorderasiatisches Museum, Staatliche Museen zu Berlin.

The absence of a single conventional accession number reflects the structure’s composite nature: it contains numerous separately tracked archaeological pieces within one museum installation. [Official collection record](https://search.smb.museum/object/obj-1744146).

The Berlin reconstruction represents only the smaller outer/northern portion and is reduced relative to the full ancient complex. Its architectural core, many plain bricks, joins, fills, and lost portions of animals are modern. It is best described as an **anastylosis-like museum reconstruction incorporating ancient façade fragments**, not as “the original gate” without qualification.

### Present condition

The in-situ lower phases at Babylon suffer from groundwater, soluble salts, rain, capillary moisture, erosion, visitor pressure, earlier excavations, incompatible modern repairs, and twentieth- and twenty-first-century site interventions. Conservation has included crack filling, drainage, moisture mitigation, caps or buffers on wall tops, monitoring, documentation, and selective repairs.

The Berlin fabric has recurring glaze instability and salt-related deterioration. Earlier treatments included soaking, paraffin consolidation, colored plaster fills, retouching, and later synthetic consolidants. Such treatments are themselves now part of the object’s conservation history.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Earlier gate

The Ishtar Gate did not originate as an entirely new institution under Nebuchadnezzar II. The gate’s name is attested before his reign, probably as early as the late Old Babylonian period, though the securely excavated monumental remains belong principally to the Neo-Babylonian rebuilding sequence. The earlier physical gate is inadequately preserved to reconstruct.

The topographical text *Tintir = Babylon* preserves the sacred name and place of the gate. Its textual history is older and more complicated than the sixth-century façade. Consequently:

- **earliest named gate** and
- **surviving/reconstructed glazed monument**

are not chronologically identical objects.

### Neo-Babylonian commission

**Documented by royal inscriptions and archaeology:** Nebuchadnezzar II, son of Nabopolassar and king of Babylon, ordered extensive rebuilding of Babylon’s walls, gates, palaces, temples, embankments, and ceremonial routes during his reign, conventionally dated 605/604–562 BCE.

The gate’s immediate practical problem, as the king’s text explains, was that successive raising of the Processional Way had left earlier entrances too low. Nebuchadnezzar’s builders excavated, founded replacement structures deeply near the groundwater, and raised new gate phases.

The familiar date “about 575 BCE” is a modern approximate date, not a year explicitly recorded in the gate inscription.

### Manufacture

No names of individual brick makers, mold carvers, glaze specialists, architects, or foremen have yet been securely connected to the building. The royal inscription attributes the project to the king in the conventional language of Mesopotamian royal construction.

Local Euphrates alluvial clays supplied the basic ceramic body. Artisans:

1. prepared clay bodies;
2. molded bricks and relief components;
3. dried and initially fired them;
4. applied glaze-forming compounds and pigments;
5. fired them again;
6. organized and numbered or marked components;
7. laid them with bitumen or other mortar into the façade.

The exact recipes and firing schedules remain subjects of technological study. “Made of blue stone” is ideological language; scientific and visual examination identifies glazed terracotta or ceramic.

### Absolute dating by archaeomagnetism

A 2024 *PLOS ONE* study sampled five **displaced** glazed and unglazed brick fragments assigned typologically to the three phases. The bricks retained thermoremanent records of the geomagnetic field at firing. The investigators concluded:

- bricks attributed to all three phases recorded closely similar field intensities;
- this supports relatively rapid construction without long chronological interruptions;
- comparison with the securely dated 586 BCE destruction field at Jerusalem places the firing after that event;
- the authors’ model crosses the regional curve around **569 BCE**, but this remains a probabilistic archaeomagnetic estimate, not an exact regnal year.

Limitations are important: the sampled fragments were not in situ, phase assignments depended on their decoration, and the regional comparison curve remains incomplete. The finding supports a later-Nebuchadnezzar date; it does not prove that the gate was funded by Jerusalem booty or built to commemorate Jerusalem’s destruction. [Di Chiara et al., “An archaeomagnetic study of the Ishtar Gate, Babylon”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10793895/); [Jerusalem reference study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7413505/).

### Purpose

The structure combined:

- defensive passage control;
- monumental royal display;
- a threshold between sectors of the city;
- sacral protection through divine naming and images;
- framing of the Processional Way;
- presentation of the king’s building achievement;
- participation in ceremonial movement between Marduk’s sanctuary, northern Babylon, and the extra-urban festival house.

There is no primary evidence that it was erected as a memorial specifically to the conquest of Jerusalem. That suggestion is a modern inference, weakened rather than confirmed by the archaeomagnetic work.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Sixth century BCE: construction and use

The gate was built into the fabric of Babylon and did not have a portable “owner” in the modern chattel sense. It fell under Babylonian royal and temple-state authority. Its inscription dedicated the king’s labor to Marduk, while its name assigned the threshold to Ištar.

After Cyrus II captured Babylon in 539 BCE, the city and gate entered Achaemenid imperial control. There is no evidence that Cyrus dismantled the gate. Babylon continued as an important city under Achaemenid and later rulers.

Alexander III entered Babylon in 331 BCE. The gate or its route likely remained meaningful within the city, but assertions that Alexander passed through the fully intact façade in precisely the form reconstructed in Berlin should be treated cautiously. Babylonian building maintenance, street levels, and urban occupation had changed.

Under Seleucid, Parthian, Sasanian, and early Islamic conditions, Babylon’s monumental core contracted and its fired bricks became a resource for later construction. No year can be assigned to the gate’s “destruction.” Its loss was gradual: abandonment, collapse, erosion, rising and changing groundwater, and systematic brick robbing reduced the superstructure.

### Early modern observation and pre-German collecting

European travelers and surveyors described Babylon’s ruins from the seventeenth through nineteenth centuries. Pierre-Joseph de Beauchamp transmitted late-eighteenth-century observations concerning Babylon and colored brick. Members of a French expedition in 1851–1854 collected glazed fragments at the Kasr, before their architectural source was securely identified.

These removals form part of the gate’s dispersal, but surviving publication does not permit every early fragment now in European collections to be linked to a documented find spot on the gate.

### 1897–1899: recognition and excavation project

Architect Robert Koldewey and Assyriologist Eduard Sachau examined Babylon in 1897 and recognized the importance of colored glazed fragments. The Deutsche Orient-Gesellschaft, founded in 1898, and the Royal Museums in Berlin developed the large excavation project in the context of German imperial competition with British and French archaeology.

Systematic excavation began on 26 March 1899 under an Ottoman permit. The excavators worked within Ottoman territory and under Ottoman antiquities administration. The project had scholarly aims, but acquisition of displayable material for Berlin was also an explicit institutional objective. [Staatliche Museen provenance exhibition](https://www.smb.museum/museen-einrichtungen/vorderasiatisches-museum/ausstellungen/detail/vom-fragment-zum-monument/).

### 1902: excavation of the gate

Targeted exposure of the Ishtar Gate took place primarily from March or July through November/December 1902, depending on whether preliminary or concentrated work is counted. A foundation deposit and building inscriptions confirmed the structure’s identification. The deeply buried earlier relief phases survived much better than the final glazed elevation.

The excavation left the lower walls exposed. This increased knowledge but also altered the monument’s hydrological and structural environment, beginning a modern conservation problem that remains unresolved.

### 1903 partage with Istanbul

The Ottoman antiquities administration and Berlin museums divided excavation finds. Berlin’s present account states that gate fragments reached it through two recognized distributions:

- a **1903 division with Istanbul**;
- a **1926 division with Baghdad**.

The first allocation occurred under Ottoman sovereignty. The exact legal meaning of arrangements described in later German accounts as permission to transport fragments for “proper treatment and assembly” remains part of the provenance debate. Ottoman law generally asserted state ownership over antiquities, while permits and negotiated partage could allocate excavated material.

Approximately 399 crates—often said to contain around 250 fragments each—were transported by sea from Basra to Hamburg and onward by inland waterways to Berlin. Exact counts in popular retellings should be treated as reconstructed from shipping and institutional records, not as a complete object inventory.

### 1903–1914: treatment and preliminary assembly

Friedrich Rathgen, head of the Berlin museums’ chemical laboratory, found that the fragments contained damaging soluble salts. They were soaked in large water tanks in a desalination process reportedly lasting about eighteen months.

Sorting and reconstruction trials began by 1906. Walter Andrae prepared drawings and supervised the assembly of animal panels. Early methods included:

- trimming some ancient fragments to fit;
- painting across breaks;
- paraffin or other consolidation;
- colored plaster filling;
- joining small fragments into larger relief panels.

Andrae later objected to excessive reshaping and pushed for closer correspondence between assembled panels and the archaeological evidence.

### 1914–1917: war and cessation

The First World War disrupted transport and fieldwork. Excavation continued intermittently until 1917, after which unshipped finds and records remained entangled in the transition from Ottoman rule to British occupation and the creation of the Iraqi state.

### 1926–1927: Iraqi division and second shipment

Negotiations occurred between the Berlin museums and the Iraqi Antiquities Administration or Iraq Museum. Berlin argued that joining the newly allocated fragments to those already in Germany was necessary for reconstruction. A second partage was concluded in 1926.

About 400 further crates of glazed fragments reached Hamburg in March 1927 and then Berlin. The museum’s current institutional account says the Babylon materials have been in Berlin since 1927 “as a result of agreements on the division of the finds.” It reports that roughly 30,000 of approximately 77,500 objects uncovered by the excavation are in the Vorderasiatisches Museum. [Babylon Collection project](https://www.smb.museum/en/museums-institutions/vorderasiatisches-museum/collection-research/research/babylon-collection/).

The legal fact of documented partage and the political characterization of the transfer are separate questions. German museum sources call it an authorized division; Iraqi officials and writers have repeatedly characterized the removal as colonial seizure or theft. The surviving documentary record needs a transaction-by-transaction legal edition before either phrase can substitute for analysis.

### 1927–1930: final assembly in Berlin

The second shipment was desalinated and sorted. Teams numbering as many as thirty workers assembled 72 animal reliefs over approximately two years. Ancient fragments were integrated with modern replacement bricks, fills, mortar, paint, and structural support.

The museum could not accommodate a full-size reconstruction of the double gateway. It therefore erected a reduced representation of the smaller outer gate, connected visually to reconstructed stretches of the Processional Way and other Babylonian façades.

The installation opened with the Pergamonmuseum in 1930. From that date onward, the Berlin reconstruction became a new composite object with its own continuous history.

### 1938 onward: conservation at Babylon

Iraqi antiquities authorities began documented crack filling at the in-situ gate by 1938. Subsequent campaigns addressed water, salts, erosion, wall caps, drainage, and stabilization. Documentation is uneven, and some early repairs are difficult to distinguish from ancient fabric.

### Second World War and divided Berlin

The Pergamonmuseum and Berlin collections experienced wartime closure, protective measures, bomb damage, and postwar division. Available sources consulted here do not establish that the Ishtar Gate was dismantled, evacuated to the Soviet Union, or materially destroyed. It remained in the Pergamonmuseum, which lay in East Berlin. The building and collection came under East German museum administration and, after reunification, the Stiftung Preußischer Kulturbesitz.

Claims that “the original gate was hidden in a vault during the war” are unsupported in the reviewed sources.

### 1950s–1980s: Iraqi replicas and reconstruction programs

A modern, smaller representation was built near Babylon in approximately 1958–1959, associated with a museum or visitor entrance. It was never a continuation of the ancient fabric.

From the late 1970s through the 1980s, the Iraqi government under Saddam Hussein undertook extensive reconstructions at Babylon. New walls and palace structures were placed over or beside ancient remains, sometimes with modern bricks stamped in Saddam’s name in deliberate imitation of Nebuchadnezzar’s royal building practice.

At the Ishtar Gate itself, conservation and reconstruction were complicated by exposed originals, raised modern masonry, drainage changes, and loss of archaeological legibility. The blue gate commonly photographed by tourists in Iraq is a modern evocation, not the façade removed from the ancient monument and later returned.

### 2002 and 2009: Iraqi return demands

In 2002 Iraqi antiquities officials publicly sought return of the Berlin gate material. A further request was reported in 2009. The demands formed part of broader Iraqi claims for antiquities removed during foreign excavation.

No restitution, judicial judgment, or binding bilateral settlement followed. Germany and the museum retained custody. Public accounts do not establish that Iraq filed a completed court action or UNESCO Intergovernmental Committee case specifically resulting in formal adjudication of the gate. [Contemporary report of the 2002 demand](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2002/may/04/iraq.babylon).

### 2003–2004: military occupation and damage

After the 2003 invasion of Iraq, United States forces established Camp Alpha at Babylon; command later passed to a Polish-led contingent. Heavy vehicles, trenches, gravel emplacement, military construction, earth moving, and use of archaeological sediments caused extensive damage and contamination across the site.

At the in-situ Ishtar Gate, John Curtis’s British Museum assessment recorded fresh damage to nine *mušḫuššu* reliefs that had not been visible in his June 2003 photographs. Vehicle vibration and direct contact were considered probable causes. Military activity also affected the Processional Way.

The damage is documented, though allocation of every individual abrasion to a particular national contingent or vehicle is not always possible. [U.S. Department of State-hosted damage report](https://eca.state.gov/files/bureau/babylondamagereport.pdf); [Curtis’s account of archaeology and military activity](https://student-journals.ucl.ac.uk/pia/article/id/288/print/).

### 2009–2019: heritage management and World Heritage listing

Iraqi authorities, international specialists, UNESCO, and the World Monuments Fund developed documentation, conservation priorities, and a site-management plan. The Ishtar Gate was classified among Babylon’s highest conservation priorities.

Babylon was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2019. The listed property includes the in-situ first or lower phase of the Ishtar Gate. UNESCO status did not transfer ownership of the Berlin fragments and does not determine the restitution issue. [UNESCO management and documentation material](https://whc.unesco.org/document/168481); [UNESCO discussion of original and reconstructed fabric](https://whc.unesco.org/document/170049).

### 2020–2023: provenance exhibition and closure

The Vorderasiatisches Museum mounted *From Fragment to Monument: The Ishtar Gate in Berlin* in 2020–2021. It explicitly addressed excavation, German imperial ambition, acquisition, fragmentation, desalination, reconstruction, and the role of modern material.

The entire Pergamonmuseum closed in October 2023 for major renovation. The reconstructed gate remains physically in the museum but is not publicly accessible.

### 2024–present

The archaeomagnetic study was published in 2024. As of September 2026:

- the Berlin reconstruction remains under control of the Staatliche Museen zu Berlin/Stiftung Preußischer Kulturbesitz;
- it is closed to visitors;
- announced partial reopening of the Pergamonmuseum in June 2027 will not include the wing containing the Ishtar Gate;
- full reopening is presently projected for the 2030s, commonly 2037;
- the ancient foundations remain at Babylon under Iraqi state heritage authority within the World Heritage property.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is the Berlin gate “the original”?

**No, if “original” means the complete ancient gate in its ancient structural continuity.** The Berlin monument is a twentieth-century reconstruction incorporating genuine sixth-century-BCE fragments.

**Yes only in a restricted material sense:** substantial visible elements—especially portions of animal reliefs, ornament, and inscription—are original excavated Babylonian glazed brick.

The following did not move to Berlin as a unified structure:

- the entire double gate;
- most of its enormous masonry core;
- its foundations;
- the lower unglazed relief phases;
- its courtyard;
- the full southern gate;
- the original cedar doors and metal fittings;
- all of its glazed façade.

### Is the Babylon gate “a fake”?

The ancient foundations and lower phases at Babylon are genuine archaeological fabric. Modern additions, repairs, and reconstructions surround or overlie parts of the remains. The separate blue replica used as a modern entrance is a replica. Calling the entire Babylonian presentation “fake” erases surviving ancient material; calling it the intact original is equally misleading.

### Reconstruction accuracy

The Berlin design rests on excavation plans, surviving brick sequences, assembly marks, preserved relief fragments, royal inscriptions, and comparative architectural reasoning. Its animal panels are not arbitrary inventions.

Uncertainties include:

- original height of upper portions;
- crenellations and roofline;
- complete color distribution;
- the form and ornament of doors;
- placement of every displaced fragment;
- extent of original white, yellow, and blue treatments in different phases;
- relationship between surviving fragments and the full double-gate elevation.

The museum’s scale reduction and selective presentation were deliberate. It is a scientifically informed reconstruction adapted to a gallery, not a one-to-one rebuild.

### Scientific examination

The 2024 archaeomagnetic investigation supports common firing dates for samples attributed to all three phases and a later part of Nebuchadnezzar’s reign, around or after 586 BCE, with a modeled central estimate near 569 BCE.

It does **not** independently authenticate every brick mounted in Berlin. Authentication of individual components rests on excavation provenance, inventory marks, ceramic and glaze characteristics, archival records, and reconstruction history.

No published radiocarbon date, DNA test, or single comprehensive chemical fingerprint of the whole museum installation was identified. DNA is not relevant to its ceramic identity.

### Legal authenticity and provenance

The museum relies on documented partage in 1903 and 1926. Iraqi demands emphasize the colonial power imbalance, the territorial origin of the fragments, and their status as an integral Iraqi monument.

The terms “legal acquisition,” “colonial seizure,” and “theft” are therefore being used at different analytical levels:

- **Documented fact:** formal divisions and shipments occurred through Ottoman and Iraqi antiquities administrations.
- **Documented historical context:** German imperial prestige and museum acquisition were explicit drivers.
- **Iraqi political claim:** the fragments constitute removed national heritage that should be returned.
- **Unresolved legal question:** no cited final court or arbitral ruling has invalidated or conclusively affirmed every component’s transfer under all applicable laws.

### Misidentifications and modern false claims

The following circulating statements are inaccurate or unproven:

- “The gate was made from lapis lazuli”: false; it is glazed brick.
- “The whole original gate is in Berlin”: false.
- “Nothing original remains in Babylon”: false.
- “The blue tourist gate at Babylon is Nebuchadnezzar’s intact gate”: false.
- “The Ishtar Gate itself was one of the canonical Seven Wonders”: imprecise. Ancient lists included the Walls of Babylon in some versions; modern sources often substitute the gate for the walls.
- “The gate was built in exactly 575 BCE”: excessive precision.
- “It was built to celebrate the destruction of Jerusalem”: unverified modern inference.
- “The Berlin gate has been scientifically proven to consist entirely of sixth-century bricks”: false; its modern fabric is acknowledged.

| **Helen Gries** | 2020s | Curator and author of a major study of the gate’s transformation from fragments into museum monument. |

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Processional use

The gate stood on the ceremonial route called, in one formulation, **Ay-ibūr-šabû**, commonly translated “May the enemy not pass,” and in another the Street of the Ishtar Gate. The route linked the northern sector and festival landscape with Babylon’s sacred center.

During the spring New Year festival, the **Akītu**, divine statues and participants moved between Marduk’s Esagila precinct and an extra-urban Akītu house. Reconstructed itineraries place the procession northward along the Processional Way and through the Ishtar Gate, with a river stage in some accounts. The French Ministry of Culture’s Babylon project identifies the eighth day as a major use of the route. [French archaeological portal](https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/babylone/fr/la-porte-dishtar-et-la-voie-processionnelle).

The precise itinerary and day varied over Babylon’s long festival history. It is safer to say that the gate framed documented ceremonial movement than that one immutable ritual script existed.

### Protective function

The ceremonial name “Ištar overthrows its assailants” gave the entrance a verbal defensive agency. Bulls and dragons were not merely decoration: they materialized the powers of Adad and Marduk and confronted persons approaching the threshold.

No contemporary text says that the animals literally animated at night or killed trespassers. Such claims belong to later fantasy.

### Royal legitimation

Nebuchadnezzar’s inscription makes the monument an assertion that:

- the gods chose and instructed him;
- he restored Babylon’s sacred and defensive order;
- he mastered water, earth, fire, bitumen, and rare-looking color;
- his construction would inspire wonder among humanity.

The gate thus legitimized kingship by converting royal building labor into visible divine service.

### Access and control

As an urban gateway, it regulated passage rather than functioning as an inaccessible inner-temple relic. Military, administrative, commercial, and ceremonial controls would have changed according to circumstance. No surviving gate-specific regulation comprehensively states who could pass, touch its reliefs, or operate its doors.

### Modern symbolic function

The gate now symbolizes:

- ancient Mesopotamian technical sophistication;
- Babylon and Iraq’s pre-Islamic cultural inheritance;
- Berlin’s archaeological collections;
- imperial-era archaeology and the ethics of partage;
- cultural displacement and restitution;
- the vulnerability of heritage during war.

Its image appears in tourism, educational materials, Iraqi institutional architecture, embassy façades, stamps, souvenirs, and visual branding.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### “One of the Seven Wonders”

**Later conflation:** Some ancient wonder lists included the Walls of Babylon. Modern retellings frequently substitute the visually famous Ishtar Gate for those walls or claim that the gate itself was displaced from the list by the Lighthouse of Alexandria.

The gate was part of Babylon’s defensive wall system, so the association is not wholly arbitrary. No secure classical canonical list, however, names the Berlin-style “Ishtar Gate” as a discrete wonder in the familiar modern wording.

### The dragon as a remembered living animal

The *mušḫuššu* was once commonly called the *sirrush*, following an obsolete or superseded reading. Early twentieth-century writers sometimes speculated that its apparently consistent anatomy might preserve a memory of a real reptile or dinosaur-like animal.

There is no zoological evidence for this. Its anatomy belongs to the deliberate combinatory logic of Mesopotamian composite beings. Connections with the dragon in the Greek additions to Daniel are interpretive comparisons, not proof that the Ishtar Gate portrayed a living captive dragon.

### Biblical Babylon

The Hebrew Bible and later Christian tradition transformed Babylon into a symbol of conquest, exile, arrogance, idolatry, luxury, and apocalypse. Although Nebuchadnezzar’s gate belongs to the historical city involved in the Judean deportations, biblical texts do not provide a physical description of the Ishtar Gate.

Modern displays frequently use the gate as a visual shorthand for:

- the Babylonian Captivity;
- the Book of Daniel;
- Bel and the Dragon;
- the Tower of Babel;
- the “Whore of Babylon.”

These are histories of reception, not primary attestations of the gate’s architecture.

### The Jerusalem-booty story

A modern suggestion holds that wealth taken from Jerusalem in 586 BCE financed the gate. Archaeomagnetic evidence makes construction after 586 plausible, but no surviving account identifies Jerusalem’s temple goods as the gate’s budget. Chronological possibility is not evidence of earmarked financing.

### “Stolen whole by Germany”

This is a politically powerful compression of a real removal. Germany did not lift an intact gate from its foundations. Excavators collected large numbers of displaced glazed fragments, received further material through partage, transported it in crates, and built a partial museum reconstruction with modern supplements.

The simplified story nevertheless reflects documented asymmetries: foreign excavation under imperial conditions, an explicit acquisition agenda, fragmentation of an integral monument, and concentration of celebrated material in a European capital.

### Saddam as a new Nebuchadnezzar

The Baʿathist state cultivated an analogy between Saddam Hussein and Nebuchadnezzar II. Modern bricks bearing Saddam’s name, palace reconstructions, festivals, and political imagery made Babylon a stage for Iraqi nationalism and presidential legitimacy.

This was a modern political program, not an inherited unbroken Babylonian ritual tradition. It materially altered the site and shaped later arguments over reconstruction and authenticity.

### Secret substitution and storage stories

Internet accounts sometimes claim that:

- the Berlin display is wholly fake while originals lie in storage;
- Germany secretly replaced ancient bricks after the war;
- the gate was taken by Soviet forces and later returned;
- the “real gate” is entirely underground.

No evidence found in the excavation, museum, conservation, or provenance sources supports these formulations. The actual position is less binary: ancient fragments remain mounted in an acknowledged modern reconstruction; other fragments are in museum storage and other collections; lower phases remain in Iraq.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Ancient monument at Babylon

The surviving ancient gate is located in the archaeological site of Babylon near Hillah, Babil Governorate, Iraq. The foundations and lower relief phases occupy their excavated position. They form part of the UNESCO World Heritage property **Babylon**, controlled and managed by the Iraqi state through its heritage authorities.

Condition concerns include:

- soluble salts;
- capillary rise and groundwater;
- rain erosion;
- loss of protective upper fabric;
- cracking and surface loss;
- past incompatible repairs;
- vibration and impact damage;
- the consequences of excavation below surrounding levels;
- modern construction and visitor management.

Public access is possible subject to Iraqi site conditions and management, but this is an archaeological ruin rather than an intact blue gateway.

### Berlin reconstruction

The glazed reconstruction stands in the south wing of the Pergamonmuseum, Museum Island, Berlin, in the Vorderasiatisches Museum collection.

Current legal controller: **Stiftung Preußischer Kulturbesitz/Staatliche Museen zu Berlin**.

Current access status, September 2026: **closed to the public** with the Pergamonmuseum’s renovation. Announced partial reopening in June 2027 does not include the Ishtar Gate wing; full museum reopening is projected for 2037. The object has not disappeared or been deaccessioned.

The museum installation’s catalogued dimensions and composite inventory are given above. The institution is improving environmental control, including control of ultraviolet exposure through renovation of the roof and gallery systems. [Pergamonmuseum profile](https://www.smb.museum/en/museums-institutions/pergamonmuseum/about-us/profile/).

### Dispersed fragments

Glazed bricks and panels associated with Babylon’s monumental façades are also held by museums including Istanbul’s archaeological collections and institutions in the United States. Not every isolated lion, bull, or dragon panel marketed or displayed as “from the Ishtar Gate” came from the gate proper; some belonged to the Processional Way or throne-room façades.

A complete global census requires reconciliation of:

- excavation field numbers;
- Ottoman and Iraqi division lists;
- shipping inventories;
- Berlin’s VA Bab registers;
- Istanbul inventories;
- transfers and exchanges to other museums.

The Berlin Babylon Project has been systematically correlating approximately 30,000 objects with surviving diaries, photographs, cards, packing lists, plans, and estate papers. The cataloguing is not equivalent to a published item-level provenance for every brick.

### Restitution status

Iraq has publicly requested return, notably in 2002 and 2009. Germany retains the reconstruction. The reviewed sources disclose no active judgment compelling return and no completed restitution agreement.

Because ancient and modern fabric are structurally interlocked, any future remedy would require choices beyond a simple handover:

- transfer of the complete museum composite;
- removal and transfer of selected ancient fragments;
- long-term loan;
- shared curation;
- digital and physical reconstruction in Iraq;
- transfer of documentation without physical deinstallation.

No such settlement has been announced.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Museum spectacle

The Berlin reconstruction changed the gate’s modern identity. In Babylon it was an archaeological ruin with buried lower phases and scattered fragments; in Berlin it became a towering, chromatically unified architectural image.

This reconstruction has strongly influenced textbooks, films, stage design, games, and digital reconstructions. Many show Berlin’s reduced outer gate as though it were the complete ancient double gate.

### Replicas

Notable replicas or evocations include:

- the modern gate at Babylon, built in the later 1950s and incorporated into subsequent visitor presentation;
- a reduced reconstruction traditionally associated with the Iraq or Babylon Museum;
- architectural façades in Iraqi diplomatic buildings;
- museum display panels assembled from dispersed ancient fragments;
- numerous temporary exhibition, theme-park, stage, and digital versions.

Replicas often add lions directly to the gate, simplify the double plan into one arch, make the entire building uniformly cobalt blue, and omit defensive depth.

### Art and literature

The gate became a visual emblem of imagined Babylon in:

- historical paintings and archaeological reconstruction art;
- biblical cinema;
- opera and theatrical settings for Babylonian subjects;
- comics and fantasy games;
- digital reconstructions of ancient cities.

Modern works routinely combine historically separate elements: the Ishtar Gate, Tower of Babel, Hanging Gardens, ziggurat Etemenanki, lion friezes, and motifs from Assyria or Persia.

### Iraqi nationalism

The monument has been used to assert a deep territorial history connecting modern Iraq with Mesopotamia. Its removal to Berlin functions in Iraqi public discourse as an example of foreign extraction. Its reconstruction at Babylon under the Baʿathist government served state spectacle and continuity claims.

These uses do not establish ethnic, political, or institutional continuity in themselves, but they are documented parts of the object’s modern history.

### Berlin and decolonial debate

German museum interpretation has shifted from celebrating technical reconstruction alone toward discussing:

- imperial archaeology;
- the objective of collecting for Berlin;
- partage systems;
- provenance archives;
- Iraqi perspectives;
- the distinction between fragments and monument;
- who has authority to narrate Babylon.

The 2020–2021 provenance exhibition and the 2023 poetry event *Gate: Between Babylon and Berlin* are examples of that shift. [“Gate: Between Babylon and Berlin”](https://www.smb.museum/fileadmin/website/Presse/Pressematerial/2023/08/VAM_230815_Gate_PR_EN.pdf).

### Commercial and popular distortion

Commercial images commonly:

- label the monument “lapis lazuli”;
- sell Assyrian lamassu imagery as Ishtar Gate imagery;
- describe *mušḫuššu* as dinosaurs;
- conflate the gate with the Hanging Gardens;
- reproduce the Berlin façade without acknowledging modern additions;
- call the Iraqi replica the recovered original.

These are modern marketing simplifications rather than results of archaeological research.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Foundational excavation publication

Robert Koldewey’s *Das Ischtar-Tor in Babylon nach den Ausgrabungen durch die Deutsche Orient-Gesellschaft* (1918) remains the foundational architectural report. Its plans, elevations, brick observations, inscriptions, and phase analysis are indispensable. It must also be read as a product of early twentieth-century excavation and museum priorities. [Digitized 1918 volume](https://commons.library.stonybrook.edu/iraqi-archaeology-digital-texts/108/).

Koldewey’s broader *The Excavations at Babylon* supplied an early English account and translations of royal building texts. His identification of brick architecture as recoverable monumental architecture transformed study of Mesopotamian cities.

### Walter Andrae and reconstruction

Andrae’s drawings and museum work converted excavated fragments into a coherent visual architecture. Later scholarship has examined the extent to which the Berlin installation is:

- a reconstruction of archaeological evidence;
- a modern museum design;
- an imperial display object;
- an early conservation experiment.

All four descriptions are applicable.

### Topography and inscriptions

Major advances came from critical editions of Babylonian topographical texts and royal inscriptions, especially work associated with Andrew George and modern open-access corpora such as ORACC/RIBo. These clarify the gate’s ceremonial name, relationships to city walls, and occurrences in Nebuchadnezzar’s building narratives.

### Technology and iconography

Research concerns:

- production sequences for molded glazed brick;
- glaze composition and firing;
- workshop organization;
- brick marks and assembly systems;
- meaning of the bull, lion, and *mušḫuššu*;
- whether animals functioned chiefly as divine symbols, apotropaic guardians, royal imagery, or a combination.

The strongest contextual view treats them as divine-symbolic and protective beings integrated with architecture and ritual movement, not as generic decoration.

### Dating controversy

Older literature commonly assigned the final gate to about 575 BCE on historical and architectural grounds. The 2024 archaeomagnetic work proposes a date later in Nebuchadnezzar’s reign, with an estimated center near 569 BCE, and argues that the phases were fired close together.

This is important but not a final exact date. More in-situ samples and a stronger southern Mesopotamian reference curve would improve precision.

### Provenance historiography

The largest documentary questions concern:

- terms of the Ottoman permit;
- content of the 1903 division;
- legal and administrative continuity between Ottoman and Iraqi authorities;
- negotiations preceding the 1926 partage;
- whether Iraq’s agreement was fully voluntary under British Mandate conditions;
- exactly which fragments belonged to which architectural context;
- subsequent exchanges and dispersals.

The museum preserves excavation diaries, photographs, packing lists, cards, architectural plans, accession books, and personal papers. Digitization has improved access, but a comprehensive critical edition of the gate’s entire transfer history remains desirable. [Official acquisition-log portal](https://www.smb.museum/en/museums-institutions/vorderasiatisches-museum/collection-research/acquisition-and-accession-logs/).

### Conservation debate

Two conservation philosophies repeatedly conflict:

- preserve exposed original mud and fired brick with minimal intervention;
- reconstruct or shelter enough architecture to protect it and make it intelligible.

At Babylon, modern superstructures may protect some surfaces while loading, obscuring, or changing the moisture behavior of ancient walls. In Berlin, early filling, cutting, painting, and consolidation made the reconstruction possible but reduced reversibility and blurred original/modern boundaries.

### Discredited or unsupported theories

The following remain popular despite inadequate evidence:

- biological reality of the *mušḫuššu*;
- construction of the gate from solid lapis;
- an exact 575 BCE completion date;
- a direct scriptural description of the gate;
- complete removal of the gate to Germany;
- total absence of ancient gate material in Iraq;
- proof that Jerusalem treasure paid for it;
- inclusion of the gate itself in every ancient list of Seven Wonders.

- Robert Koldewey, *Das Ischtar-Tor in Babylon nach den Ausgrabungen durch die Deutsche Orient-Gesellschaft* (1918).
- Koldewey’s translated excavation narrative.

### Principal modern scholarship and institutional resources

- Olof Pedersén, *Babylon: The Great City* (2021), an open-access topographical and architectural synthesis.

- Di Chiara et al., 2024 archaeomagnetic analysis.
- The Staatliche Museen zu Berlin Babylon Project, which correlates museum objects with excavation documentation.

### Evidentiary conclusion

The Ishtar Gate unquestionably existed as a large architectural complex at Babylon. Its identity is established by archaeological position, construction, royal inscriptions, foundation evidence, and ancient topographical texts.

No single present object is the whole gate:

- Iraq retains the archaeological place, foundations, lower structures, and some decorated ancient fabric.
- Berlin holds a monumental museum composite made from selected excavated original fragments and substantial modern reconstruction.
- Istanbul and other collections hold additional dispersed material.
- Modern replicas possess representational but not continuous material identity.

The gate’s life therefore did not end when Babylon declined. It changed from working threshold to ruin, from ruin to excavated architecture, from architecture to numbered fragments, and from fragments into rival but connected heritage objects: an exposed Iraqi archaeological monument, a German museum reconstruction, dispersed collection pieces, and a global image of Babylon.
