# The Crown of Charlemagne: Object Dossier

**Requested filename:** `the_crown_of_charlemagne_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary note

“The Crown of Charlemagne” is not the unambiguous name of one continuously surviving object. It has denoted at least four materially distinct things:

1. **Charlemagne’s historical imperial crown**, placed on or presented to him at Rome on 25 December 800. Contemporary and near-contemporary texts attest a valuable crown but do not identify any surviving artifact as that crown.
2. **Two closely related medieval French coronation crowns**, conventionally associated with Philip II Augustus and his queen, Ingeborg of Denmark. The larger king’s crown and, after its destruction, the smaller queen’s crown were successively called the *couronne de Charlemagne*. The former was dismantled or melted during the Catholic League’s control of Paris in 1590; the latter was destroyed at the Paris Mint in 1794.
3. **Napoleon I’s “Crown of Charlemagne,”** made in Paris in 1804 by Martin-Guillaume Biennais. It survives in the Louvre under inventory number **MS 91**.
4. **A Restoration-period ceremonial reproduction at Saint-Denis**, made in 1821–1824 by or under François-Joseph Bélanger, Jakob-Ignaz Hittorff, and the bronze-worker Pierre-François-Marie Feuchère. It survives in the treasury of the Basilica-Cathedral of Saint-Denis.

The Holy Roman Empire’s **Imperial Crown** (*Reichskrone*), now in Vienna, has also long been called “Charlemagne’s crown.” Current scholarship rejects its manufacture for Charlemagne: it is a distinct artifact, probably of the tenth to twelfth century. It is treated below as a major rival attribution, not as part of the French crown’s physical continuity.

Evidence is classified throughout as:

- **Documented:** supported by an inventory, act, official catalogue, contemporary narrative, or other direct record.
- **Scientific or technical finding:** derived from materials or technological examination.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** an inference from documentary, stylistic, or physical evidence.
- **Institutional or dynastic tradition:** an attribution maintained in ritual or official display without proof of physical continuity.
- **Legend:** a claim connecting an object to Charlemagne without contemporary evidence.
- **Unresolved:** the evidence permits no secure conclusion.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 The historical crown used in 800

The Latin sources for Charlemagne’s imperial coronation use ordinary crown vocabulary rather than a stable proper name. The *Liber Pontificalis* describes Pope Leo III placing a “most precious crown” on Charlemagne’s head: *corona pretiosissima*. Frankish annalistic accounts similarly record coronation but do not supply a technically useful description.

No contemporary text specifies:

- the crown’s dimensions or weight;
- whether it was a closed imperial crown, open circlet, Byzantine-style *stemma*, or another form;
- its gemstones, inscriptions, workshop, or previous owner;
- its disposition after the ceremony.

Consequently, every surviving crown identified as the one used in 800 rests on retrospective tradition.

### 1.2 The medieval French pair

The principal objects historically known in France as the *couronnes de Charlemagne* were two similar articulated gold crowns used for a king and queen. The early-sixteenth-century inventory summarized their relationship in Latin:

> *Corona qua Rex Francorum coronatur … Corona qua regina coronatur, predicte similis.*

Translation: “The crown with which the king of the Franks is crowned … The crown with which the queen is crowned, similar to the aforesaid.”

Danielle Gaborit-Chopin’s documentary reconstruction describes each as a broad circlet composed of **four convex rectangular gold plates**, called *couplières*, joined by hinges. Each plate was surmounted by a large fleur-de-lis. The repeated arrangement created four plate-and-fleuron units.

Across the circlet and four fleurs-de-lis were reportedly **48 major gemstones**:

- 16 balas rubies, now normally identified as red spinels;
- 16 emeralds;
- 16 sapphires.

On each fleur-de-lis, three red stones emphasized its lobes and an emerald marked its base. On each rectangular plate, sapphires occupied the four corners; a large balas ruby stood toward the upper center, accompanied below and to the sides by three emeralds. The visual rhythm was therefore systematically red, green, and blue.

The queen’s crown followed essentially the same design but was:

- smaller;
- lighter;
- set with less regular and generally smaller stones.

Whether both were made simultaneously as a pair is **unresolved**. The surviving texts establish similarity, not common manufacture.

#### The king’s bonnet or “tiare”

The king’s crown acquired a tall internal cap of crimson velvet, conventionally described as tiara-like. In the fourteenth century it was surmounted by a very large ruby or spinel, traditionally estimated at approximately 200 carats. Gold settings, pearls, and crossed attachments connected the summit ornament to the cap. An inscription reportedly recorded the royal donor.

This cap was not necessarily part of the crown’s original construction. Its association with King John II and his release from English captivity in 1360 is a documentary or near-documentary medieval attribution, but the exact history of each textile replacement is imperfectly recoverable. Henry II ordered a new velvet cap lined with satin in 1547. From the coronation of Louis XIII onward, the very tall cap was replaced or reduced to a comparatively simple bonnet. Hervé Pinoteau accordingly distinguished the durable hinged gold circlet from a succession of perishable textile interiors.

The complete medieval king’s crown, cap, stones, mountings, and suspension chains was reported as weighing close to four kilograms. This figure should not be read as the weight of the gold circlet alone.

Neither medieval crown survives intact. No fragment has an uncontested, documented provenance from either circlet.

### 1.3 The 1804 Louvre crown

The surviving Louvre object is officially catalogued as **“Couronne dite ‘couronne de Charlemagne’”**, inventory **MS 91**.

Its construction consists of:

- a circular band with an upper edge alternating palmettes and small globes;
- eight rising branches;
- a central globe;
- a surmounting cross;
- forty carved stones—cameos and intaglios—on the band and branches;
- a violet velvet cap embroidered in gold, supplied or renewed by Jacques-Évrard Bapst for Charles X’s coronation in 1825.

Dimensions recorded by the Louvre:

- diameter: **18.5 cm**;
- crown height: **25 cm**;
- case height: **26 cm**;
- case width: **28.4 cm**;
- case depth: **23.8 cm**.

The Louvre’s current database describes the metal as **gilded brass**; an older Louvre cartel called it **vermeil**, or silver-gilt. Published literature has also used “gilded copper.” Without a cited modern alloy analysis, the discrepancy must remain explicit. The present institutional record favors gilded brass, but the historical cataloguing history shows that the substrate has not always been described consistently.

The forty carved stones came from the former treasuries of Saint-Denis, Bourges, and Arpajon. Many were removed from a reliquary given to Saint-Denis in 1401 by Jean, duke of Berry—usually identified as the reliquary of the head of Saint Benedict. Thus, although the crown itself was made in 1804, some incorporated glyptic objects are ancient or medieval. Recent work by Philippe Malgouyres also identifies certain “antique” cameos collected by the duke of Berry as medieval or Gothic imitations of antiquity. The stones confer genuine antiquity of materials, not Carolingian identity.

An escutcheon on the object’s case is inscribed:

> **Couronne de / Charlemagne**

This is an identifying label, not a medieval inscription.

### 1.4 The Saint-Denis reproduction

The French Ministry of Culture catalogues a separate **“couronne du roi, dite de Charlemagne”** in the treasury of Saint-Denis as **Palissy PM93000283**. It was made between 1821 and 1824 and is attributed to Bélanger, Hittorff, and Feuchère.

The official record describes it as:

- gilded bronze;
- fitted with imitation cameos;
- a nineteenth-century reconstruction or ceremonial representation rather than a surviving medieval crown.

It belongs to a group of Restoration-era crowns for the king, queen, and dauphin and must not be confused with Louvre MS 91.

### 1.5 The Vienna Imperial Crown

The *Reichskrone* consists of eight hinged, round-topped gold plates:

- four taller plates bearing stones and pearls;
- four lower plates with cloisonné enamel images;
- a frontal cross;
- a single arch bearing a pearl inscription naming *Chuonradus*;
- later internal iron bands stabilizing the originally flexible structure.

The principal plates are between approximately 11.8 and 14.9 cm high. The enamel panels depict Christ and Old Testament royal figures. Empty settings and lower loops show that ornaments and pendant chains are missing.

It is not the same design as either the French medieval crowns or Napoleon’s eight-branched crown. The name “Charlemagne’s crown” belongs to its later cultic and imperial reception, not its demonstrated manufacture.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Charlemagne’s coronation crown

**Documented origin:** unknown. Pope Leo III crowned Charlemagne in Saint Peter’s Basilica in Rome on Christmas Day 800. The crown’s presence is textual fact; its manufacture and subsequent identity are not known.

Einhard’s *Vita Karoli Magni*, written after Charlemagne’s death, reports that Charlemagne would not have entered the church had he known Leo’s intention. That statement concerns the ceremony’s politics, not the crown’s provenance. Later representations habitually show Charlemagne wearing the crown familiar to the artist—fleur-de-lis, open medieval crown, papal tiara-like crown, or the Vienna *Reichskrone*. None is an eyewitness portrait.

### 2.2 Manufacture of the French crowns

The strongest documentary starting point is not Charlemagne but the Capetian period.

A royal act issued in 1261 by Louis IX records the deposit at Saint-Denis of crowns made for the coronation of kings and queens and associated with Philip II Augustus. It also mentions a smaller gold crown worn by the king during the coronation banquet. The two principal crowns were to be suspended over altars on solemn feast days.

The usual reconstruction is:

- Philip II commissioned or possessed the principal crowns in the late twelfth or early thirteenth century;
- he bequeathed them to Saint-Denis in 1223;
- Louis VIII recovered them, apparently compensating the abbey;
- Louis IX returned them permanently in 1260–1261.

However, Gaborit-Chopin emphasizes that no surviving document proves the exact manufacture date or proves that the two similar crowns were originally made as a single pair. Pinoteau proposed a date around 1180. Bernard Morel suggested that the abundance and size of the gems made production after Philip’s return from the Third Crusade more plausible. That is a materials-and-trade inference, not a documented commission.

A still more speculative theory makes the articulated plates older—sometimes Carolingian and specifically associated with Charles the Bald—and the fleurs-de-lis later Capetian additions. This hypothesis attempts to explain the alternate name “crown of Charles the Bald,” but no physical examination can now test it. The crowns’ destruction makes firm stylistic dating impossible.

### 2.3 Growth of the Charlemagne attribution

The 1261 act connects the crowns with Philip Augustus, not Charlemagne. The attribution to Charlemagne developed as other French coronation insignia were progressively placed under his patronage. Pinoteau dates the systematic naming of the group—sword, spurs, sceptre, hand of justice, and crown—from the late twelfth or thirteenth century, clearly established by 1271 and perhaps beginning by 1179.

The ideological purpose was dynastic continuity. Capetian kings were not Carolingians, but possession of “Charlemagne’s” insignia made the coronation appear to transmit an ancient, imperial, and Christian kingship through objects preserved by Saint-Denis.

### 2.4 Manufacture of the Napoleonic crown

After revolutionary destruction of the medieval crowns, Napoleon required a new imperial ceremonial system. Vivant Denon, director of the Musée central des Arts, selected antique and medieval cameos from national collections. Martin-Guillaume Biennais constructed the crown in Paris in 1804.

It was deliberately placed among the **Honneurs de Charlemagne**, while a separate group represented the **Honneurs de l’Empereur**. Calling the new object “Charlemagne’s crown” did not result from mistaken archaeological dating. It was a conscious program of political and ceremonial succession.

### 2.5 Manufacture of the Saint-Denis copy

The Saint-Denis crown was created in 1821–1824 during the Bourbon Restoration, before Charles X’s coronation. Its gilded bronze and false cameos made it suitable for historical display and royal mortuary or ceremonial use while avoiding the expense and security requirements of a gold and gemstone original.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 The crown of 800

- **25 December 800 — Saint Peter’s Basilica, Rome:** Pope Leo III crowned Charlemagne. The crown’s immediate keeper afterward is not recorded.
- **After 800:** the chain ends. There is no document connecting the Roman crown to Saint-Denis, Aachen, Nuremberg, Vienna, Monza, or another surviving treasury.

Any seamless chain from 800 to a present artifact is therefore legendary.

### 3.2 The medieval French crowns

#### Late twelfth or early thirteenth century: royal custody

The crowns were associated retrospectively with Philip II Augustus. Philip’s 1223 testamentary arrangements placed a king’s and queen’s crown at Saint-Denis.

#### 1223–1261: transfer, retrieval, and permanent deposit

- **1223:** Philip II’s bequest or deposit at Saint-Denis.
- **Soon after 1223:** Louis VIII recovered the crowns, apparently after a monetary arrangement with the monks.
- **9 October 1260 / May 1261:** Louis IX ordered their definitive return. The abbey became the continuing institutional guardian, while the monarchy retained ceremonial use.

This was not private ownership in the modern museum sense. Saint-Denis held the regalia under obligations of custody, liturgical display, and delivery for coronations.

#### Thirteenth through sixteenth centuries: use and movement

At each coronation requiring the crowns, Saint-Denis’s officers released and transported them, usually to Reims. The king’s crown was carried with the other regalia and placed on the altar before its ritual delivery.

A categorical claim that it crowned every monarch is unsafe. Documented exceptions include:

- sovereigns crowned when the treasury was inaccessible;
- Henry IV, crowned at Chartres in 1594 while Reims remained politically unavailable;
- coronations in which another crown was selected.

The crown’s use is nevertheless firmly attested as an established component of the French rite by the later Middle Ages.

#### 1340: crown-rich treasury and an identification problem

An act recording Philip VI’s borrowing from Saint-Denis reveals at least six crowns, including older pairs whose origins and ultimate fates are unclear. This abundance is crucial: a medieval reference to “the crown” cannot automatically be matched to the later Crown of Charlemagne. The treasury contained multiple royal, votive, funerary, personal, and coronation crowns.

#### Fourteenth to sixteenth centuries: alterations

- John II’s reign is associated with the addition of the tall crimson cap and great summit ruby.
- In 1547 Henry II ordered the textile interior renewed.
- Metal backing or reinforcement may have been applied to portions of one crown.
- Stones and settings were repaired or replaced over centuries; inventories describe an evolving ceremonial artifact, not an untouched twelfth-century object.

#### 1590: seizure by the Catholic League

During the succession crisis following Henry III’s death, the regalia were moved from Saint-Denis to the convent of Sainte-Croix-de-la-Bretonnerie in League-controlled Paris. Chapter records cited by later scholarship report that on 26 May 1590 the papal legate, the duke of Nemours, and other League representatives took Suger’s gold cross and the “oriental ruby” from the king’s crown. The crown itself may have been removed in June.

The larger king’s crown was dismantled and its precious metal realized to finance the League. “Melted by the duke of Mayenne” is the usual compressed account, but institutional participation and the staged removal of high-value components make the episode more complex than a single act by one person.

This was a political confiscation and destruction of treasury property.

#### After 1590: identity transferred to the queen’s crown

Because the original king’s crown had been destroyed, the smaller, nearly identical queen’s crown was reassigned to kings. Louis XIII was crowned with it in 1610. It then inherited the title “Crown of Charlemagne.”

The object used from Louis XIII through Louis XVI was therefore not physically the same crown used before 1590. The name and ritual identity migrated from one member of the pair to the other.

The queen’s own later coronations employed the crown associated with Jeanne d’Évreux rather than reclaiming the substituted crown.

#### 1610–1775: Bourbon use

The surviving medieval crown was transported to coronations, including those of Louis XIII, Louis XIV, Louis XV, and Louis XVI. At Louis XV’s coronation, Saint-Simon explicitly distinguished the “great crown of Charlemagne” from the sovereign’s newly made personal jewelled crown. The older crown performed the central ecclesiastical coronation; personal crowns could be worn afterward or at the banquet.

For Louis XVI’s coronation on 11 June 1775, contemporary descriptions record the Crown of Charlemagne as solid gold, set with rubies and sapphires, lined with a crimson satin cap embroidered in gold, and surmounted by a gold fleur-de-lis carrying thirty-six oriental pearls. Images show the archbishop of Reims placing it on the kneeling king. This was its last documented coronation use.

#### 1789–1794: national seizure and destruction

The abolition of the abbey and monarchy transformed the treasury into national property. Committees inventoried and divided its contents:

- objects judged useful to art, history, or instruction were reserved for museums or libraries;
- precious-metal objects not preserved were sent for dismantling and melting;
- stones were removed from their settings.

The destruction of the remaining Crown of Charlemagne began at the Paris Mint on **7 Floréal, Year II (26 April 1794)**. Dismantling continued over several days. The larger operation on treasury gold concluded by **27 Fructidor, Year II (13 September 1794)**, when approximately 450 marks of gold from the former treasury were delivered to the Mint.

The later destination of the crown’s individual gemstones is generally untraceable. Claims that a particular surviving emerald came from this crown are possible but not supported by an unbroken object-level chain of custody.

### 3.3 Napoleon’s crown

- **1804:** commissioned for Napoleon and made by Biennais in Paris from a new metal structure and reused carved stones selected by Denon.
- **2 December 1804:** present at Napoleon and Joséphine’s coronation in Notre-Dame de Paris. Accounts distinguish it from Napoleon’s gold laurel wreath. The crown represented the Honours of Charlemagne and was borne in the ceremonial procession. Reports that Napoleon briefly placed it on his own head coexist with ceremonial reconstructions describing it principally as carried and displayed; the exact sequence has been retold inconsistently.
- **1804–1816:** exhibited at Notre-Dame.
- **1816:** transferred to the Garde-Meuble.
- **1825:** restored by Jacques-Évrard Bapst for Charles X’s coronation; Bapst supplied the present violet velvet embroidered cap. Charles X was crowned with this constructed “Crown of Charlemagne,” giving it a second regime’s ritual legitimacy.
- **1848:** sheltered at the Ministry of Finance during the revolution.
- **1852:** deposited in the newly created Musée des Souverains at the Louvre.
- **After the Musée des Souverains:** retained in the Louvre’s national collections.
- **Present:** property of the French state, assigned to the Louvre’s Department of Decorative Arts.

### 3.4 The Saint-Denis reproduction

- **1821–1824:** made for the restored royal and funerary ceremonial ensemble.
- **Thereafter:** retained at Saint-Denis.
- **23 April 1981:** classified as a protected historic object.
- **Present:** French state property in the treasury of the Basilica-Cathedral of Saint-Denis.

### 3.5 Vienna’s Imperial Crown

The Imperial Crown’s early route is incompletely documented. The imperial regalia traveled with rulers and between strongholds. They later received a stable legal and civic deposit:

- **1424:** most imperial regalia were permanently entrusted to Nuremberg.
- **Coronations:** selected objects traveled from Nuremberg to Aachen and, particularly after 1562, Frankfurt.
- **1796:** moved from Nuremberg to protect them from French revolutionary forces, eventually reaching Vienna.
- **After 1806:** remained in the Habsburg treasury after the Holy Roman Empire’s dissolution.
- **1938:** transferred to Nuremberg under Nazi rule.
- **1945:** discovered concealed in Nuremberg and returned to Vienna after the war.
- **Present:** Imperial Treasury, Hofburg, Vienna.

This is the custody history of the *Reichskrone*, not of the French Crown of Charlemagne.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Was either French medieval crown Charlemagne’s?

There is no affirmative contemporary evidence. The documented Capetian provenance begins centuries after Charlemagne. The 1261 act attributes the crowns’ ceremonial purpose to the age of Philip Augustus. The name “Charlemagne” appears as part of a later program that assigned French regalia to a foundational emperor.

**Finding:** the literal Charlemagne provenance is unsupported. The objects were nevertheless authentic medieval French coronation crowns.

### 4.2 Was the pre-1590 crown the same object as the 1775 crown?

No. The larger king’s crown was destroyed in 1590. The smaller queen’s crown was then substituted and inherited the king’s crown’s name and function. Older summaries frequently collapse both into a single artifact “destroyed in 1794,” obscuring the 1590 substitution.

### 4.3 Were the crowns made for Philip Augustus?

This is plausible but not proven. The evidence supports their association with Philip’s royal property and Louis IX’s later deposit. It does not preserve a workshop account or commission. Proposed manufacture around 1180, after the Third Crusade, or partly in the Carolingian period remains scholarly reconstruction.

### 4.4 Is Louvre MS 91 a forgery?

It is not a forgery in the narrow sense of a secretly fabricated antiquity. It was knowingly made in 1804 for a political ritual. Calling it “Charlemagne’s crown” was an officially maintained ceremonial fiction or dynastic claim. Its catalogue now uses the distancing phrase *dite*—“so-called.”

Its cameos are older than the crown, but reused ancient material does not make the assemblage ancient. Nor does the presence of medieval pseudo-antique cameos turn Biennais into their forger: those stones had already circulated as antiques in medieval collecting contexts.

### 4.5 Metal disagreement

The Louvre’s records have variously called the crown brass-gilt, copper-gilt, and vermeil. Until a published elemental analysis resolves the substrate, the safest description is “a gilded base-metal or silver-gilt structure, currently catalogued by the Louvre as gilded brass.” This is a cataloguing/materials uncertainty, not an authenticity crisis.

### 4.6 Is the Vienna crown Charlemagne’s?

No known evidence supports manufacture before Charlemagne’s death in 814. Recent interdisciplinary study dates its components and alterations much later. Competing datings include:

- the later tenth century, sometimes associated with Otto I;
- the early eleventh century and Conrad II;
- the mid-twelfth century and Conrad III.

The arch names *Chuonradus*, but scholars dispute which Conrad is meant. Technical investigation found that the enamel inscriptions were not later additions, an important constraint on proposed dates. The frontal cross, arch, internal braces, and crown plates may belong to different construction phases.

The fourteenth-century veneration of the imperial regalia as relics of Charlemagne explains the traditional name. It does not establish ninth-century manufacture.

### 4.7 The Iron Crown of Lombardy and other candidates

Charlemagne was remembered as king of the Lombards, and later tradition associated him with Monza’s Iron Crown. Its own physical and ceremonial history is distinct. Evidence that Charlemagne actually wore the surviving object is lacking.

No candidate—French, Viennese, or Italian—has a continuous demonstrable chain to the crown used in Rome in 800.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

- **Charlemagne (742/748–814):** historical recipient of an imperial crown in Rome in 800; legendary former owner of later French and imperial regalia.
- **Pope Leo III:** placed the attested crown on Charlemagne at Saint Peter’s on 25 December 800.
- **Philip II Augustus:** probable owner or commissioner of the French medieval coronation crowns; connected through the 1223 bequest and 1261 act.
- **Ingeborg of Denmark:** traditionally associated with the paired queen’s crown and her coronation in 1193.
- **Louis VIII:** recovered the crowns from Saint-Denis after Philip’s death, apparently through compensation.
- **Louis IX:** permanently restored the crowns to Saint-Denis in 1260–1261 and prescribed their solemn display.
- **The abbots, treasurers, and monks of Saint-Denis:** custodians responsible for storage, ritual exhibition, inventories, and release for coronations.
- **John II:** associated with the crimson cap and summit ruby added to the king’s crown.
- **Henry II:** ordered the cap renewed in 1547.
- **The dukes of Mayenne and Nemours and Catholic League authorities:** involved in the 1590 seizure, dismantling, and monetization of the king’s crown.
- **Louis XIII, Louis XIV, Louis XV, and Louis XVI:** recipients of the substituted former queen’s crown at Bourbon coronations.
- **Archbishops of Reims:** principal ecclesiastical bearers and authenticators within the French coronation rite.
- **Vivant Denon:** designed or supervised the antiquarian program for Napoleon’s Honours of Charlemagne and selected reused cameos.
- **Martin-Guillaume Biennais:** manufactured the 1804 crown.
- **Napoleon I:** commissioner and ceremonial claimant to Charlemagne’s imperial succession.
- **Pope Pius VII:** participated in the 1804 consecration, though Napoleon controlled the crown’s ultimate handling.
- **Jacques-Évrard Bapst:** restored the crown and supplied its cap in 1825.
- **Charles X:** used the 1804 object at the final French royal coronation, giving a Napoleonic artifact a Bourbon ceremonial afterlife.
- **Bélanger, Hittorff, and Feuchère:** creators or designers associated with the 1821–1824 Saint-Denis reproduction.
- **French Convention, Commission temporaire des arts, and Paris Mint:** institutions that inventoried, selected, dismantled, and melted the surviving medieval crown.
- **Musée du Louvre:** present custodian of MS 91 and principal institutional authority distinguishing the artifact from the medieval crown.
- **Kunsthistorisches Museum and Imperial Treasury, Vienna:** present custodians and investigators of the rival *Reichskrone* tradition.
- **Christ, David, Solomon, and Hezekiah:** divine or biblical royal figures represented on Vienna’s crown, forming a theology of righteous kingship; they have no documented iconographic presence on the lost French circlets.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

The French crown’s primary function was not ordinary wear. It was a liturgical instrument used at the climax of the royal *sacre*.

The general ritual sequence was:

1. regalia brought from Saint-Denis and placed on or near the altar;
2. oath and anointing of the king;
3. delivery of spurs, sword, sceptre, and hand of justice;
4. blessing of the crown;
5. placement of the crown by the archbishop, assisted by peers;
6. enthronement and acclamation.

The crown’s sacred authority derived from several overlapping claims:

- placement on an altar before use;
- guardianship by Saint-Denis;
- incorporation into a rite of anointing;
- supposed descent from Charlemagne;
- repeated use across reigns;
- association with the biblical language of justice and eternal reward.

At Louis XVI’s coronation, the prescribed prayer connected the material crown to “glory and justice” and the future eternal crown. This did not make the artifact a sacramental object in the strict sense, but it made it more than royal jewelry.

The crown’s hinged construction facilitated transport and adjustment. Its great weight and tall cap also required assistance from peers and clergy. Its physical unwieldiness helped transform crowning into a collective act: the king was visibly supported by the political and ecclesiastical orders.

At Saint-Denis, crowns could be suspended above altars on major feasts. Thus they alternated between treasury objects, votive display, and active regalia.

After 1590, continuity of ritual mattered more than continuity of matter. The former queen’s crown could become “Charlemagne’s crown” because the office, name, custodial institution, and ceremonial act were transferred to it.

Napoleon used the Charlemagne title to claim a pre-Capetian and imperial genealogy. His ceremony combined Roman, Carolingian, French royal, revolutionary, and papal references. Charles X’s reuse of the Napoleonic crown in 1825 demonstrates the reverse appropriation: an artifact fabricated for the Empire was absorbed into restored Bourbon monarchy.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 Direct descent from the coronation of 800

**Tier: medieval or early-modern institutional tradition; historically unsupported.**

The fullest form claims that the crown preserved at Saint-Denis—or, in the German tradition, the crown preserved among the imperial regalia—was the crown placed on Charlemagne by Leo III. No continuous chain bridges Rome in 800 and either later treasury.

The story legitimized custodians and users. Saint-Denis could present the French king as heir to Charlemagne; Nuremberg and imperial custodians could present the elected Roman king as inheritor of the revived Western Empire.

### 7.2 The crown of Charles the Bald

**Tier: alternate medieval attribution; unresolved origin.**

French sources sometimes called the crown that of Charles the Bald rather than Charlemagne. This may preserve awareness that part of the object was Carolingian, or it may merely shift the legendary owner to a Carolingian ruler closely connected with Saint-Denis. Destruction prevents testing the hypothesis.

### 7.3 The paired crowns of Philip and Ingeborg

**Tier: scholarly reconstruction grounded in documentary association.**

The usual narrative makes the two similar crowns a deliberately manufactured royal pair. Gaborit-Chopin cautions that similarity alone does not prove simultaneous production; one could have been a later imitation of the other.

### 7.4 The great ruby

**Tier: documented object with elaborated royal legend.**

The summit stone was celebrated as an exceptionally large ruby. Pre-modern “ruby” terminology included spinel. Reports of approximately 200 carats may refer to an estimated rather than scientifically measured weight. Its removal in 1590 is better documented than its geological identity or ultimate fate.

### 7.5 Louis XVI: “It hurts me” or “It bothers me”

**Tier: anecdotal later tradition.**

Popular histories report Louis XVI complaining that the crown hurt or constrained him, followed by a prophetic interpretation that the Crown of France would trouble him more seriously. The episode is frequently repeated without a secure contemporary citation. It should not be treated as established speech.

### 7.6 Napoleon’s “ancient” crown

**Tier: official political invention, 1804.**

Napoleon’s crown was sometimes presented in ceremonial language that blurred the distinction between antiquity of some cameos and novelty of the metal structure. Modern cataloguing makes the 1804 manufacture explicit. The political effectiveness of the attribution is historical even though its literal Carolingian identity is false.

### 7.7 The Vienna relic tradition

**Tier: fourteenth-century sacralization of older imperial regalia.**

By the later Middle Ages the imperial insignia were venerated collectively as relics of Charlemagne. Their periodic public showing and their custody at Nuremberg developed around this sacred-imperial identity. Scientific and art-historical study has not confirmed a Charlemagne date for the crown.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Lost or destroyed objects

- **Crown used at Rome in 800:** fate unknown after the coronation; no authenticated fragment.
- **Larger medieval French king’s crown:** dismantled and melted under Catholic League authority in Paris in 1590.
- **Smaller medieval queen’s crown, later the king’s Crown of Charlemagne:** dismantled and melted at the Paris Mint beginning 26 April 1794. No surviving gemstone or fragment is securely documented as belonging to it.

### Louvre MS 91

- **Institution:** Musée du Louvre, Paris.
- **Department:** Decorative Arts, Middle Ages to modern period.
- **Inventory:** MS 91.
- **Ownership:** French state.
- **Current catalogue location:** Richelieu wing, Room 504, display case associated with the sceptre of Charles V.
- **Access:** ordinarily public when the gallery is open, subject to museum rotations, loans, and closures.
- **Condition:** extant, including Bapst’s 1825 cap and its historic case.
- **Catalogue status:** explicitly dated 1804 and called “so-called Crown of Charlemagne,” preventing institutional confusion with a Carolingian artifact.

### Saint-Denis copy

- **Institution:** Basilica-Cathedral of Saint-Denis treasury.
- **Catalogue:** Palissy PM93000283.
- **Ownership:** French state.
- **Legal status:** classified historic object since 23 April 1981.
- **Condition:** surviving gilded-bronze nineteenth-century ceremonial crown with imitation cameos.
- **Access:** treasury access is controlled by the monument’s opening and exhibition arrangements.

### Vienna Imperial Crown

- **Institution:** Imperial Treasury, Hofburg, Vienna.
- **Control:** Kunsthistorisches Museum Museumsverband.
- **Access:** normally on public display.
- **Current research:** the interdisciplinary **CROWN** project has used high-resolution imaging, microscopy, spectroscopy, gemological investigation, and technological study to distinguish construction phases, characterize gems and settings, and reassess dating. The project excludes a Charlemagne-era origin while leaving significant debate between Ottonian, Salian, and later datings.

No active legal restitution claim concerning the French Crown of Charlemagne can restore the destroyed medieval objects. Modern issues are instead ones of nomenclature, interpretation, heritage ownership, and the treatment of reused ecclesiastical treasury material in MS 91.

## 9. Modern Reception

The crown’s modern public image is composite. Illustrations frequently merge:

- the four-fleur-de-lis French medieval crown;
- Vienna’s eight-plate Imperial Crown;
- Napoleon’s eight-branched cameo crown;
- generic fantasy crowns.

This produces recurrent misidentification in books, tourism, commercial replicas, heraldic art, and online media.

### Visual records and reconstructions

The destroyed French crowns survive through:

- early inventories;
- Dom Michel Félibien’s engravings of the Saint-Denis treasury;
- Bernard de Montfaucon’s watercolor records;
- coronation images;
- modern drawn and three-dimensional reconstructions.

These are essential but not equivalent forms of evidence. An inventory records materials and number; an engraving may regularize shapes; a modern reconstruction fills gaps.

### Napoleonic art

The 1804 crown entered the political theater of the First Empire. Jacques-Louis David’s monumental *Sacre de Napoléon* centers on Joséphine’s crowning rather than Napoleon’s self-coronation and does not function as a neutral diagram of every object’s exact movement. Ingres’s *Napoleon I on His Imperial Throne* uses surviving and reconstructed regalia to present Napoleon as an atemporal sovereign.

The crown’s role in these images helped popularize the idea that Napoleon had physically taken Charlemagne’s own crown, although the object was newly fabricated.

### Bourbon reuse

Charles X’s 1825 use and the creation of substitute regalia for Saint-Denis recast revolutionary absence as restored material continuity. Replicas and reconstructions became instruments of monarchy in their own right.

### National and imperial symbolism

The name “Charlemagne” allowed different regimes to make incompatible claims:

- Capetian and Bourbon monarchy: continuity of Christian French kingship;
- Holy Roman emperors: continuity of Roman imperial office;
- Napoleon: revival and expansion of Western empire;
- nineteenth-century museums: a chronological history of national sovereignty;
- modern European reception: Charlemagne as a transnational “father of Europe.”

The crown accordingly operates less as one inherited object than as a transferable label of legitimacy.

### Contemporary museum interpretation

Modern institutional language generally distinguishes attribution from fact:

- the Louvre uses *couronne dite*;
- Saint-Denis’s Palissy record gives nineteenth-century dates;
- Vienna’s research program explicitly calls the Charlemagne attribution erroneous.

Popular retellings remain less disciplined and regularly caption the Vienna crown, Louvre crown, or a reconstruction simply as “Charlemagne’s crown.”

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

The object’s historiography is dominated by the loss of the medieval artifacts. Research must combine textual criticism, inventories, drawings, coronation records, and comparison with surviving metalwork.

### Principal documentary foundations

- Philip II’s testamentary arrangements of 1223.
- Louis IX’s 1260–1261 act of deposit.
- Saint-Denis chapter acts and treasury inventories.
- The detailed inventory of 1534.
- The extensive inventory of 1634.
- Félibien’s *Histoire de l’abbaye royale de Saint-Denys* and engraved plates.
- Montfaucon’s antiquarian drawings.
- Coronation books and eyewitness descriptions, especially for Louis XV and Louis XVI.
- Revolutionary inventories and proceedings of the Commission temporaire des arts.
- Louvre and Ministry of Culture catalogues for surviving nineteenth-century objects.

### Major modern studies

Hervé Pinoteau reconstructed the medieval crown’s appearance and history in *L’ancienne couronne française dite “de Charlemagne,” 1180?–1794* and summarized the distinction between the medieval and Napoleonic crowns in “Les deux couronnes françaises dites ‘de Charlemagne.’”

Danielle Gaborit-Chopin’s 1975 study remains fundamental because it emphasizes uncertainty: the crowns were similar, but neither their date nor their simultaneous manufacture is securely documented. Her work also embeds them within the much larger and often confusing population of crowns in the Saint-Denis treasury.

The Louvre’s 1987 *Regalia* exhibition and the 1991 *Trésor de Saint-Denis* exhibition integrated the crown into critical object catalogues rather than treating the Charlemagne attribution as literal provenance.

### Running debates

1. **Date:** circa 1180, post–Third Crusade, thirteenth century, or partly Carolingian.
2. **Pair or imitation:** simultaneous king-and-queen commission versus one crown copied from the other.
3. **Which crown survives in later descriptions:** king’s crown before 1590; queen’s crown under the inherited title afterward.
4. **The summit ruby:** exact mineral, weight, donor, removals, and fate.
5. **Surviving stones:** whether any emerald or other gem in a modern collection can be securely assigned to the crowns.
6. **Napoleonic metal:** brass-gilt, copper-gilt, or vermeil.
7. **Use in 1804:** precisely how long Napoleon wore or touched the Crown of Charlemagne and how contemporary ceremonial descriptions should be reconciled.
8. **Vienna dating:** Otto I, Conrad II, Conrad III, or a composite chronology.
9. **Authority to authenticate:** medieval abbey and monarchy, revolutionary commissions, nineteenth-century national museums, and contemporary conservation science have each exercised different forms of authority.

### Discredited or unsupported claims that continue to circulate

- that the French medieval crown is documented at Charlemagne’s coronation;
- that Vienna’s *Reichskrone* was made for Charlemagne;
- that Napoleon wore an original crown borrowed from Charlemagne’s treasury;
- that the single Crown of Charlemagne survived unchanged until 1794;
- that all incorporated cameos in the Louvre crown are both antique and Carolingian;
- that a modern illustrated reconstruction is an image of a surviving medieval object.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and early documentary evidence

- The *Liber Pontificalis* and Frankish annals provide the earliest accounts of the Roman coronation in 800. They attest a precious crown but provide no chain to any later artifact.
- Louis IX’s 1261 act, preserved through the Saint-Denis documentary tradition, is the central evidence for the crowns associated with Philip Augustus.
- The 1534 and 1634 treasury inventories furnish the detailed descriptions of structure, stones, and weight.
- Saint-Denis chapter acts, cited in the modern scholarship, document the League’s removal of the great ruby and crown in 1590.
- Saint-Simon’s account of Louis XV’s coronation distinguishes the old Crown of Charlemagne from the personal crown.
- Contemporary descriptions and imagery of Louis XVI’s 1775 coronation document the crown’s final use.
- Proceedings of the Commission temporaire des arts and Paris Mint records underpin the 1794 destruction chronology.

### Museum, heritage, and scientific catalogues

- Musée du Louvre, collection record for MS 91: maker, date, dimensions, current materials classification, provenance, display location, and bibliography.
- French Ministry of Culture, Palissy PM93000283: Saint-Denis copy, makers, dates, material, ownership, and protected status.
- Kunsthistorisches Museum/University of Vienna CROWN project: construction, materials, inscriptions, scientific methods, and current dating debate concerning the *Reichskrone*.
