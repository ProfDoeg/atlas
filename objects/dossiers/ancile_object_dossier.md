# The ancile (Numa's heaven-fallen shield): Object Dossier

**Proposed filename:** `ancile_object_dossier.md`  
**Status of dossier:** Current to 6 September 2026.

## Evidentiary synopsis

The *ancile* is not a surviving museum object. No shield now held by any museum, temple, state collection, or private owner has a documented provenance connecting it to the Roman cult described by ancient authors. Nor has an archaeological find been scientifically identified as one of Rome’s sacred *ancilia*.

What can be reconstructed is a layered object-history:

1. A Roman cult possessed—or represented itself as possessing—a set of twelve sacred shields, the *ancilia*, entrusted to the priestly Salii and carried in annual ceremonies. Literary, calendrical, epigraphic, iconographic, and numismatic evidence makes the historical existence of such a ritual set substantially more probable than the purely legendary details of its origin.
2. Within Roman tradition, one shield was the authentic *ancile*, a bronze shield sent from heaven to King Numa Pompilius; the other eleven were indistinguishable human copies.
3. The surviving sources cannot identify which shield was regarded as the original, establish that the set remained materially unchanged, or trace the shields beyond late antiquity.
4. The shields’ disappearance is undocumented. Statements that they were destroyed by Christians, hidden, buried, melted down, or removed from Rome are hypotheses or modern inventions unless supported by evidence not presently known.
5. Images on gems, reliefs, architectural terracottas, and coins are representations of the *ancilia*, not surviving portions of the shields themselves.

The dossier therefore tracks both:

- the **historical ritual ensemble of twelve shields**, whose exact material history is only intermittently visible; and
- the **singular heaven-fallen shield inside Roman narrative**, whose custody history is legendary.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Object class

The *ancile* was a sacred shield, cult implement, piece of priestly regalia, and state-protecting relic in Roman religion. The plural set, the *ancilia*, comprised twelve shields carried by the Salii Palatini, the priestly college associated principally with Mars Gradivus.

Ancient descriptions treat the shields as *sacra*—holy things in their own right—not merely costume or insignia. Late-antique commentary classifies the *ancilia* among the *pignora imperii*, supernatural “pledges” or “guarantors” upon which Roman dominion was believed to depend.

### 1.2 Names and grammatical forms

- **Latin singular:** *ancīle*, genitive *ancīlis*, neuter.
- **Latin plural:** *ancīlia*.
- **Greek descriptions:** Plutarch uses a transliteration or explanatory vocabulary connected with ἀγκύλος (*ankylos*, “curved”) and ἀγκών (*ankōn*, “elbow”).
- **English:** *ancile*; plural *ancilia*.
- **French:** *ancile*, plural *anciles*.
- **Italian:** *ancile*, plural *ancili* or Latinizing *ancilia*.
- **Spanish:** *ancil* or *ancile*; plural *anciles*.
- **Portuguese:** *ancile*; plural *ancis* in some reference works.
- **German:** *Ancile*; plural commonly *Ancilia*.

The singular name is now frequently used for Numa’s supposedly authentic celestial shield, while *ancilia* denotes the whole set. Ancient authors were not always so strict: Servius explicitly explains Virgil’s plural *ancilia* as a poetic plural for the single object that fell.

### 1.3 Ancient etymologies

The name’s etymology was already disputed in antiquity.

**Varro, *De lingua Latina* 7.43, first century BCE:**  
“*Ancilia dicta ab ambecisu, quod ea arma ab utraque parte ut Thracum incisa*”—the *ancilia* were named from being “cut on both sides,” like Thracian arms. Varro’s explanation derives the word from *amb-* (“on both sides”) and a form related to *caedere* (“cut”).

**Ovid, *Fasti* 3.375–378, composed around the beginning of the first century CE:**  
Numa calls the shield *ancile* because it is “cut back on every side” and presents no angle to the eye. Ovid’s poetic etymology emphasizes an all-curving, angleless contour.

**Plutarch, *Numa* 13, early second century CE:**  
Citing or discussing King Juba II, Plutarch reports several proposed Greek derivations:

- ἀγκύλος, curved;
- ἀγκών, elbow, because the shield was carried on the arm;
- ἄνωθεν, “from above,” because it descended from heaven;
- terms interpreted as cure, release from drought, or relief from calamity.

Plutarch treats these as speculative wordplay and remarks upon Juba’s eagerness to derive Roman terms from Greek. Modern etymology generally regards Varro’s connection with cutting on both sides as possible but not conclusively established. The existence of competing ancient explanations warns against translating the name as though its derivation were certain.

### 1.4 Number and identity

The canonical tradition gives **twelve shields**:

- one heaven-fallen original;
- eleven human-made replicas.

The number corresponds to the twelve Salii Palatini. The replicas’ function was not ordinary duplication for convenience. Their asserted perfect resemblance was an anti-theft and anti-identification device: a thief could not know which shield carried Rome’s supernatural guarantee.

The tradition thereby made the original deliberately unidentifiable. No source supplies an inscription, maker’s mark, damage pattern, placement, or secret priestly criterion by which it could be distinguished. Consequently, even within the legend, an unbroken chain of identity cannot be demonstrated after the copies were introduced.

### 1.5 Material

The clearest explicit material statement comes from Plutarch, who calls the falling shield a **bronze** or **brazen** shield. Dionysius describes the Salii with shields as part of bronze-heavy archaizing equipment, although his account does not give a laboratory-style description of the shield’s composition.

No surviving sample exists for metallurgical testing. Therefore:

- “bronze” is a literary description, not a scientific analysis;
- alloy composition is unknown;
- whether all twelve shields were solid bronze, bronze-faced wood, sheet bronze over an organic core, or differently constructed cannot be established;
- no dimensions, weight, thickness, fastening system, or manufacturing traces are recorded.

Modern illustrations that show a homogeneous bronze plate should not be mistaken for archaeological evidence.

### 1.6 Shape

The literary descriptions agree broadly that an *ancile* was unlike a conventional circular shield.

**Varro:** cut inward on both sides, comparable to a Thracian shield.

**Plutarch:** neither round nor a complete regular oval; the sides form curving indentations, while the ends curve and join above and below.

**Ovid:** cut around its perimeter and without visible angles.

This produces the familiar modern description “figure-eight shield,” but that phrase can mislead. Surviving ancient images exhibit at least two visual types:

- **Type A:** an elongated, single-piece shield with deeply indented sides;
- **Type B:** a double-lobed or paired-disc form, sometimes appearing to have an ovoid central plate.

Recent archaeological-iconographic research observes that Type A representations occur by the fourth century BCE and fit the literary “Thracian” description relatively well. Type B becomes particularly prominent from the late Republic and imperial period and resembles certain earlier Aegean and Italian double-lobed shield forms.

The divergence means that there was no single invariant representational template. It is possible that artists stylized the same cult objects differently; that the objects’ physical form changed; that different Salian colleges used different forms; or that “ancile” became an antiquarian visual category embracing more than one archaizing shield type.

### 1.7 Construction and fittings

Dionysius of Halicarnassus says attendants carried shields suspended from rods or poles, while Plutarch describes the Salii bearing and striking them. These accounts may describe different moments:

- attendants transporting the shields between ritual stations;
- priests taking them up for the armed dance.

Some images appear to show suspension by straps or a *lorum*. No securely identified original survives to determine:

- the position of handles;
- whether they had an arm-loop and handgrip;
- whether they were suspended through rings;
- whether the representations reproduce practical fittings or artistic convention.

### 1.8 Decoration and inscriptions

No ancient author records an inscription on an *ancile*. No surviving text describes painted scenes, embossed figures, heraldic devices, or a maker’s signature. Coins and reliefs generally render the shields schematically; apparent lines may represent borders, reinforcing ribs, lobes, or ornamental divisions.

The word **ANCILIA** appears on an Antoninus Pius coin type, but it labels the two shields depicted on the coin. It is not evidence that the physical shields themselves carried that inscription.

### 1.9 Dimensions, weight, condition

No reliable measurements survive. Terms rendered “small shield,” “buckler,” or “short shield” are relative literary classifications. Modern replicas have no evidentiary standing for ancient dimensions.

Current condition of the cult objects: **unknown; presumed lost or destroyed, but neither fate is documented**.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Traditional date

Roman tradition assigned the shield’s arrival to the reign of **Numa Pompilius**, conventionally the second king of Rome, whose traditional reign is 715–673 BCE. These dates are later Roman chronological constructions, not contemporary documentation.

Plutarch places the event in the eighth year of Numa’s reign. That precision belongs to the developed literary tradition and cannot be independently verified.

### 2.2 Earliest surviving textual evidence

The earliest surviving literary reference is probably a fragment of **Quintus Ennius’ *Annales***, composed in the early second century BCE and preserved by Varro:

> *Mensas constituit idemque ancilia primus.*

“He likewise first established the tables and the *ancilia*.”

The subject is Numa. The fragment establishes that by Ennius’ time Numa was credited with instituting the *ancilia*. It does **not**, in its surviving words:

- say that a shield fell from heaven;
- identify Jupiter, Mars, Egeria, or the Muses as donor;
- mention a plague;
- mention Mamurius;
- say that one original was hidden among eleven copies;
- describe the material or form.

Because Ennius survives only fragmentarily, absence from the extant line is not proof that his lost context lacked these details.

### 2.3 Republican evidence

**Varro, first century BCE**, quotes Ennius and explains the shields’ name by their bilateral indentations. His testimony establishes the term and antiquarian shape tradition but does not preserve a full descent narrative in this passage.

**Cicero**, in discussions of Roman religious signs, refers to the sacred equipment of Mars and its repository. His writings help document the institutional ritual world in which the shields were understood, not the historical manufacture of a surviving artifact.

**Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 1.20**, writing in the Augustan age, credits Numa with appointing twelve Salii to Mars Gradivus and gives them embroidered tunics and bronze breastplates; they were to carry the *caelestia arma* called *ancilia* through the city while singing hymns and performing a measured three-beat dance. Livy thus calls the shields “heavenly arms” but does not narrate their fall or Mamurius’ copies in the surviving chapter.

This distinction matters: the developed origin tale was not uniformly told even by authors who knew the cult.

### 2.4 The fully developed celestial origin

The fullest early surviving narratives are Augustan or imperial.

#### Ovid, *Fasti* 3.259–392

Ovid integrates the shield into a story about Numa’s dealings with Jupiter during a crisis. Egeria predicts that Jupiter will give a sure pledge of dominion. Numa announces the sign publicly; the sky opens and a shield descends gently before the assembled Romans. Numa sacrifices an unyoked heifer, takes up the gift, and realizes that sovereignty depends on it.

To prevent its identification and seizure, he orders multiple shields of the same shape. Mamurius executes the work and asks that his name be remembered in the Salii’s song.

Ovid supplies:

- Jupiter as giver;
- Egeria as intermediary;
- a public descent before the Roman people;
- the shield as *imperii pignus*, a pledge of rule;
- Mamurius as fabricator of the copies;
- incorporation of Mamurius’ name into ritual song.

#### Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities* 2.70–71

Dionysius places the shields within Numa’s organization of Roman religion and gives a detailed account of the Salii’s clothing, procession, music, attendants, and dance. His description is especially important for how the objects were handled ceremonially.

#### Plutarch, *Numa* 13

Plutarch situates the descent during a plague affecting Italy and Rome. A bronze shield falls into Numa’s hands. Numa says that Egeria and the Muses revealed that it came for the city’s salvation. Eleven identical shields must be made to frustrate a thief. After other artisans fail, Mamurius Veturius succeeds so completely that Numa cannot distinguish the original.

Plutarch further connects the episode with:

- consecration of the place of descent and neighboring meadows to the Muses;
- holy spring water used by the Vestals;
- cessation of the plague;
- creation of the Salian priesthood;
- multiple rival etymologies.

The plague-ending power is therefore explicit in Plutarch’s version, not safely projectable back into Ennius’ fragment.

### 2.5 Divine ownership

The object’s divine attribution varies subtly:

- Jupiter sends or causes the shield to descend in Ovid.
- The shields are called the “heavenly arms of Mars,” and their ritual custody belongs to Mars’s priests.
- Plutarch describes a celestial gift for Rome’s salvation without reducing its identity to a mundane weapon formerly wielded by a named god.
- Servius calls it a short, round shield that fell from heaven in Numa’s reign and was placed in Mars’s temple.

The best formulation is: **a shield sent from heaven, commonly understood as Jupiter’s pledge, ritually assimilated to the arms of Mars**.

### 2.6 Manufacture of the eleven copies

Mamurius Veturius is a legendary artisan, not a documented historical metalsmith. No workshop, contract, price, ore source, furnace, or archaeological product can be assigned to him.

Ovid says Mamurius asked not for money or property but for enduring glory in the Salian hymn. Plutarch reports an alternative interpretation: the sung words may not have been the personal name *Mamurius Veturius* but something like *veterem memoriam*, “ancient memory.” This raises the possibility that the craftsman’s biography developed from misunderstood archaic ritual language.

The story’s internal logic requires eleven copies of the same form, size, and appearance as the celestial object. It does not explain how terrestrial bronze could be physically identical to heavenly matter. That problem interested neither narrator because ritual indistinguishability, not materials science, is the story’s concern.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because no specimen survives, the chain must be divided into **legendary custody** and **historically attested institutional custody**.

### 3.1 Legendary chain

#### Heaven → Numa Pompilius

In Ovid and Plutarch, the shield descends from heaven during Numa’s reign. Ovid makes the event public; Plutarch says it came into Numa’s hands. The transfer mechanism is divine gift or pledge.

**Evidentiary tier:** developed Roman legend, attested no earlier in full than the late first century BCE or early imperial period.

#### Numa → Mamurius Veturius

Numa gives the shield as a model to Mamurius, who fabricates eleven indistinguishable copies.

The texts do not describe whether Mamurius retained physical possession during manufacture, worked in the royal residence, or was supervised. This is temporary legendary workshop custody, not ownership.

#### Mamurius → Numa/the Roman cult

Mamurius returns or completes the set. No price is paid in goods; the legendary compensation is commemoration in sacred song.

#### Numa → Salii Palatini

Numa institutes twelve Salii and entrusts the shields to their ritual care. The objects cease to be the king’s personal property and become Roman public-sacred property.

### 3.2 Institutional custody in Rome

#### Regia tradition

A strong body of later evidence associates the shields and Mars’s sacred spear or spears with the **Regia**, the old royal complex beside the Forum Romanum and later the official headquarters of the pontifical establishment.

Some ancient and older modern accounts instead emphasize:

- a shrine of Mars;
- the Salii’s Palatine curia;
- the *sacrarium Martis*;
- a repository on the Palatine.

These locations should not be flattened into one building. The terminology may reflect:

- separate storage, assembly, and ceremonial spaces;
- changes across centuries;
- inconsistent antiquarian reconstruction;
- confusion between the Regia’s Mars shrine and the Salii Palatini’s own headquarters.

The object’s exact shelf, room, chest, rack, or shrine is never securely described.

#### Annual movement through Rome

In March, the shields left storage and moved through the city. Dionysius says the Salii danced through the Forum, Capitol, and other public and private places. Stations or *mansiones* accommodated the procession and its ritual banquets; during a multi-day itinerary the shields could remain overnight away from their principal repository.

This created a recurring custody cycle:

1. repository opened or sacred objects “moved”;
2. shields issued to the Salii and attendants;
3. transport through city streets;
4. ritual handling, striking, and display;
5. overnight station custody where applicable;
6. eventual formal replacement in storage.

No surviving document names an individual priest who signed for a particular shield.

#### Military-calendar restrictions

Polybius reports that a Scipio’s membership in the Salii delayed his departure from Rome during campaigning connected with Antiochus III. The passage demonstrates that Salian obligations could materially impede military movement.

Suetonius reports that Otho departed against Vitellius while the *ancilia* had been moved but not yet put away, an act considered traditionally unlucky. This is not a transfer of ownership; it documents the shields’ calendar as a public constraint on political and military action.

#### Fires and rebuilding

The Regia was repeatedly destroyed or damaged in antiquity and rebuilt. Its archaeological phases include archaic construction and later Republican rebuilding. No surviving source gives a shield-by-shield account after any fire.

Therefore it cannot be established that:

- the same twelve physical shields survived every destruction;
- damaged shields were repaired;
- lost shields were replaced;
- the set was temporarily evacuated;
- one or more shields were remade without public acknowledgement.

The doctrine that one shield must remain the authentic celestial original would have created a strong institutional motive to assert continuity, but no ancient confession of replacement survives.

### 3.3 Imperial-era documentation

#### First century CE

Livy, Ovid, Dionysius, Plutarch, and later Suetonius describe the cult as an intelligible Roman institution. Their evidence supports continued imperial-age familiarity with the shields, although literary present tense does not invariably prove eyewitness observation.

In 69 CE, Otho’s allegedly inauspicious departure before the shields were stored presupposes that the ritual calendar remained publicly meaningful.

#### Second century CE

Antoninus Pius issued a bronze coin type showing two shields with the legend **ANCILIA** and **IMPERATOR II**. RIC III 736a is one catalogued variety. This is direct official iconographic evidence that the shields functioned as recognizable symbols in imperial political communication.

The coins are representations, not fragments or replicas used by the Salii.

#### Third and fourth centuries CE

The Salian institution persisted into the imperial period, though evidence becomes less continuous. A late inscription, conventionally cited as **CIL VI 2158**, records repair of the *mansiones Saliorum Palatinorum*, the stations of the Palatine Salii, which had long been neglected. The restoration was financed by high-ranking pagan religious officials associated with Vesta.

The damaged or restored stations establish concern for the Salian ritual infrastructure. They do not explicitly inventory twelve shields or certify that Numa’s objects survived unchanged.

Some scholarship associates a major repair with the fourth century, possibly the religious-political environment surrounding Constantius II’s visit to Rome in 357, but this dating and occasion require caution. A restored processional network does not by itself prove full restoration of every ancient rite.

### 3.4 Disappearance

No surviving ancient text records:

- the final Salian procession;
- formal suppression of the college;
- confiscation of the shields;
- transfer into imperial or ecclesiastical custody;
- burial or concealment;
- destruction by fire;
- melting for metal;
- Christian ritual destruction;
- survival into the medieval period.

The broader abolition and defunding of traditional Roman public cults in the late fourth century make cessation of ritual use historically plausible. But the exact relationship between anti-pagan legislation and the shields’ fate is unknown.

The chain of custody consequently ends in late antiquity with a **documentary break**, not a documented destruction.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Did a historical set exist?

The strongest case that historical ritual shields existed rests on cumulative evidence:

- early literary attribution in Ennius;
- Republican antiquarian description in Varro;
- detailed accounts of public carrying and dancing;
- calendar terminology involving movement and storage;
- political anecdotes whose logic depends on Salian obligations;
- iconography on gems, reliefs, architectural decoration, and coins;
- inscriptions concerning Salian stations.

This evidence is much stronger for a real cult ensemble than for a physical shield literally descending from the sky.

### 4.2 Was one shield believed to be the original?

Yes, in developed tradition. The entire purpose of the eleven copies was to conceal one authentic object.

Whether priests in historical Rome privately designated one shield as original cannot be established. Three possibilities remain open:

1. one shield was secretly distinguished;
2. the distinction was intentionally unrecoverable;
3. the “one original plus eleven copies” explanation was an aetiological legend imposed upon a pre-existing set of twelve ritual shields.

No source identifies an authentication procedure.

### 4.3 Was the set genuinely archaic?

Possible, but unproven.

Factors supporting antiquity include:

- the archaic language of the Salian hymn, reportedly difficult for later Romans;
- the priesthood’s deliberately archaizing dress and dance;
- central Italian and Aegean comparanda for bilobed shields;
- pre-imperial iconography;
- the cult’s integration into Rome’s old calendar.

Factors limiting certainty include:

- all surviving detailed narratives are many centuries later than Numa’s traditional lifetime;
- archaizing institutions could be restored, reshaped, or retrospectively attributed to kings;
- no shield is available for dating;
- visual forms vary;
- Roman authors routinely projected religious institutions back onto founding kings.

The reasonable scholarly position is that the cult was old, perhaps very old, but its precise date and the age of any individual shield are unknowable.

### 4.4 Meteoric origin

“Heaven-fallen” objects sometimes invite modern meteorite theories. No ancient source says the shield was made from meteoritic iron or describes a meteorite. Plutarch specifies bronze, while the copies are products of smithcraft.

No specimen has undergone:

- nickel-iron analysis;
- isotope analysis;
- metallography;
- radiocarbon dating of an organic core;
- thermoluminescence;
- residue analysis.

A meteoritic interpretation is therefore an unsupported modern speculation, not a scientific finding.

### 4.5 Continuity through fires and restorations

There is no evidence sufficient to decide whether the objects seen or imagined in the imperial period were:

- Numa-period survivals;
- early Republican replacements;
- post-fire reconstructions;
- partially ancient composites;
- ritual replicas periodically renewed.

Roman sacred objects could preserve identity through replacement of material, but no source describes that process for the *ancilia*. Institutional continuity and material continuity are not the same proposition.

### 4.6 Rival claimants

No credible present-day claimant is known. Museums possess ancient shields and artistic representations resembling *ancilia*, but none has a provenance to the Regia or Salii.

Objects marketed as an “ancile” today are normally:

- replicas;
- decorative antiquarian reconstructions;
- re-enactment equipment;
- modern fantasy objects;
- mislabeled ancient shield types.

There is consequently no active scientific authenticity dispute comparable to those surrounding rival relics. The principal controversy concerns whether the Roman cult objects were materially archaic and how their changing images relate to their actual form.

### 4.7 Authority to authenticate

In the legendary system, Numa initially receives and names the shield. Mamurius’ success paradoxically defeats ordinary visual authentication. Thereafter the Salii and Roman priestly establishment control the set through custody and ritual performance.

Modern museums and laboratories have no candidate object to test. Authentication has moved from priestly custody to philological and archaeological reconstruction, but the latter can authenticate only texts, images, and contexts—not the missing shield.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Numa Pompilius

**Connection:** legendary recipient, namer, first custodian, commissioner of copies, and founder of the Salii Palatini.  
**Date:** traditional seventh century BCE; earliest surviving association in Ennius, early second century BCE.  
**Tier:** early Roman literary tradition; no contemporary evidence.

### Mamurius Veturius

**Connection:** legendary metalsmith who made eleven indistinguishable copies.  
**Compensation:** commemoration in the Salian hymn.  
**Tier:** developed antiquarian and poetic legend. Historicity unestablished.

The name may be connected with archaic ritual language or an “ancient memory” rather than a biographical craftsman. Later scholarship has also explored associations between *Mamurius* and old forms of Mars’s name, but these are linguistic-religious hypotheses.

### Egeria

**Connection:** divine or nymphal adviser who reveals the shield’s meaning and necessary precautions to Numa. In Ovid she predicts Jupiter’s pledge; in Plutarch she and the Muses instruct Numa.  
**Tier:** legend.

### Jupiter

**Connection:** explicit celestial donor in Ovid; guarantor of Roman imperium.  
**Tier:** mythic-divine association.

### Mars Gradivus

**Connection:** deity whose “heavenly arms” the shields become; patron of the Salii; divine focus of their armed dance and hymn.  
**Tier:** historically attested Roman cult association, though the divine ownership narrative is religious tradition.

### The Muses

**Connection:** in Plutarch, co-revealers with Egeria; the place of descent and nearby meadows are consecrated to them.  
**Tier:** imperial literary tradition.

### Salii Palatini

**Connection:** twelve patrician priests who guarded, transported, displayed, danced with, and struck the shields.  
**Date:** institution attributed to Numa; historically attested in Republican and imperial Rome.  
**Tier:** documented cult body with legendary foundation.

### Salii Collini or Agonales

**Connection:** second Salian college, attributed to Tullus Hostilius and associated with the Quirinal. Their relationship to the famous Numan *ancilia* is less direct. Some modern scholarship uses the two colleges to explain divergent shield iconography.  
**Tier:** historical priestly institution; iconographic allocation disputed.

### Vestal Virgins and pontifical establishment

**Connection:** shared institutional environment of the Regia. Plutarch connects the descent-site spring with water for Vestal purification. Late repairs of Salian stations involve elite pagan priestly officials associated with Vesta.  
**Tier:** historical institutional association overlaid with legend.

### King Juba II of Mauretania

**Connection:** antiquarian authority cited by Plutarch for Greek etymologies and perhaps for interpretation of the hymn.  
**Date:** approximately 48 BCE–23 CE.  
**Tier:** documented scholarly engagement, not custody.

### Quintus Ennius

**Connection:** earliest surviving literary witness to Numa’s institution of the *ancilia*.  
**Date:** 239–169 BCE.  
**Tier:** literary witness; not an owner or eyewitness.

### Varro

**Connection:** preserves Ennius’ line and explains the shields’ name and shape.  
**Date:** 116–27 BCE.  
**Tier:** Republican antiquarian witness.

### Livy

**Connection:** describes Numa’s appointment of the Salii and their carrying of the “heavenly arms.”  
**Date:** late first century BCE.  
**Tier:** historical-literary engagement.

### Ovid

**Connection:** supplies the most elaborate surviving poetic narrative of Jupiter’s gift and Mamurius’ commission.  
**Date:** *Fasti* composed and revised around the turn of the era.  
**Tier:** literary mythography and Augustan religious interpretation.

### Dionysius of Halicarnassus

**Connection:** describes ritual handling, dress, routes, dance, and attendants.  
**Date:** late first century BCE.  
**Tier:** antiquarian-historical account.

### Plutarch

**Connection:** fullest surviving account of plague, descent, replicas, etymologies, and ritual dance.  
**Date:** early second century CE.  
**Tier:** later biographical and antiquarian tradition.

### Otho

**Connection:** not a custodian; according to Suetonius, he departed for war in 69 CE before the already moved shields had been ritually stored, violating a traditional restriction.  
**Tier:** reported historical engagement and omen narrative.

### Antoninus Pius

**Connection:** imperial coinage under his authority depicted and labeled the *ancilia*, mobilizing them as signs of Roman military and religious legitimacy.  
**Date:** second century CE.  
**Tier:** documented numismatic reception.

### Servius

**Connection:** late-antique commentator who explains the heavenly shield, copies, temple placement, and *pignora imperii*.  
**Date:** late fourth or early fifth century CE.  
**Tier:** antiquarian transmission, not custody.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Salian procession

The shields’ principal documented use was annual movement through Rome by the Salii. Livy describes the priests carrying the heavenly arms, singing hymns, and performing a solemn triple-beat dance.

Dionysius gives a fuller visual account:

- embroidered tunics;
- broad bronze girdles or breast coverings;
- scarlet-striped and purple-bordered *trabeae*;
- conical *apices*;
- weapons;
- music and song;
- dancing through the Forum, Capitol, and other places;
- attendants transporting shields suspended from poles.

Plutarch says the priests struck the shields with short daggers or rods while executing rapid, intricate armed movements.

The sources do not establish that every shield was continuously carried directly by a priest throughout the entire procession.

### 6.2 March cycle

The Roman religious calendar marked the period in which the *ancilia* were “moved” and later “stored”:

- *ancilia movere*: bringing out or setting in motion the shields;
- *ancilia condere*: formally replacing or hiding them.

Older reconstructions often make 1 March the opening and 24 March the closing of one continuous festival. More cautious scholarship notes that the surviving evidence is composite and may preserve several ceremonies and stations rather than an uninterrupted twenty-four-day procession.

Ovid’s placement connects the descent story with the Kalends of March and the beginning of Rome’s old military and calendrical year.

### 6.3 October rites

Some later reconstructions connect the shields with the October *Armilustrium* and the ritual closure of the military season. The evidence for an autumnal handling of the *ancilia* is less straightforward than for March, and accounts sometimes conflate purification of arms generally with a specific procession of Numa’s twelve shields.

The proposition that the shields were certainly paraded both in March and October should therefore be labeled **traditional reconstruction**, not uniformly documented fact.

### 6.4 Sound and impact

The shields were not merely displayed. They were struck in rhythm as part of an armed dance. Their material therefore had an acoustic function.

No source records:

- pitch;
- alloy tuned for resonance;
- damage caused by striking;
- replacement of dented surfaces;
- whether the short weapons struck shield faces, rims, or fittings.

### 6.5 Salian hymn

The *Carmen Saliare* was an archaic ritual song. Later Romans found its language difficult. Ovid’s story explains Mamurius’ name at the hymn’s end as payment for his craft.

Plutarch preserves an alternative: the words may have meant “ancient memory.” The difference is historiographically important. One reading treats the hymn as a maker’s memorial; the other suggests that the maker may have emerged from ritual words no longer understood.

### 6.6 Protection against plague and calamity

In Plutarch, the shield arrives during a pestilence and its arrival is associated with relief. The object thus functions as:

- apotropaic protection;
- proof of divine favor;
- a cure or cessation of collective disaster.

This power is explicit in the imperial narrative but not recoverable from the earliest Ennian fragment.

### 6.7 Guarantor of sovereignty

Ovid calls the gift a sure pledge of rule. Servius later includes the *ancilia* among seven *pignora imperii* sustaining Roman power.

The shield’s security and Rome’s security become homologous:

- preservation of the shield preserves dominion;
- theft would endanger the state;
- copies protect not merely property but imperial continuity.

This belief makes the copies part of the object’s power. Their indistinguishability is a ritual security system.

### 6.8 Constraints on war and public action

The period between moving and storing the shields could be treated as religiously unsuitable for departure on campaign. Suetonius’ Otho episode illustrates how observance or violation could be interpreted politically after an outcome.

Other late antiquarian reconstructions attach restrictions on battles, marriages, and particular priestly acts to the period. Each restriction derives from a patchwork of calendars and literary references; the whole package should not be imagined as a single surviving Salian rulebook.

### 6.9 Access and touch

The Salii and their attendants handled the objects publicly. There is no evidence that ordinary Romans could touch them, inspect them closely, or enter their repository.

No source describes a public identification test, private unveiling of the original, or penalty for touching a shield. Modern claims that only a king or chief priest could touch the original are unsupported unless tied to a particular late source.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The heavenly descent

**Story:** During Numa’s reign, heaven opens and a shield descends into Rome or into the king’s hands.

**Earliest full attestation:** Ovid and Plutarch, although Livy’s phrase “heavenly arms” shows that celestial origin was already familiar in the Augustan period.

**Variants:**

- Jupiter explicitly promises and sends it in Ovid.
- Plutarch frames it as deliverance during pestilence.
- Some summaries place it in Numa’s palace or Regia rather than an outdoor public gathering.
- Servius calls it short and round, whereas Plutarch denies that it was fully round.

**Historical assessment:** no contemporary evidence; mythic origin. The cult’s existence is not proof of celestial manufacture.

### 7.2 The prophetic guarantee

**Story:** Rome will retain power or supremacy as long as it preserves the shield.

The prophecy’s wording varies among epitomes and commentators. Ovid presents it as a *pignus imperii*. Servius reports a response that world dominion would be where the shield remained.

**Function:** the legend binds sacred custody to imperial destiny and turns priestly conservation into state security.

### 7.3 The eleven perfect copies

**Story:** Numa asks artisans to duplicate the shield. Others fail; Mamurius produces eleven perfect copies, defeating even Numa’s eye.

**Variant:** some abbreviated accounts simply say Numa ordered copies, omitting the competition among craftsmen.

**Function:** explains why there are twelve shields, why the original cannot be shown separately, and why an allegedly unique divine object appears as a standardized set.

### 7.4 Mamurius’ reward

**Story:** Offered any reward, Mamurius requests that his name be sung by the Salii.

**Variant:** *Mamurius Veturius* may conceal or pun on “ancient memory.” If so, the named craftsman could be secondary personification.

**Function:** incorporates human craftsmanship into sacred tradition without allowing the craftsman to own the divine gift.

### 7.5 The plague’s end

**Story:** The descent brings salvation during epidemic disease.

**Earliest clear surviving form:** Plutarch.

**Function:** makes the shield not only a military-sovereign pledge but a civic healing object.

### 7.6 Theft prevention without a recorded theft

The copies exist because a thief or enemy might remove the original. No ancient source supplies a documented successful or attempted theft of the Roman *ancile*. Stories that an enemy stole and later returned it are not part of the principal ancient record.

The imagined theft is preventive narrative. It may have been modeled in part on other tales of hidden or duplicated sovereignty talismans, especially the Trojan Palladium.

### 7.7 Mamuralia and the expelled old year

Modern and early-modern reconstructions often connect Mamurius with a March ritual in which an old man in skins was beaten and expelled, interpreting him as the old year or a scapegoat.

The evidence is difficult and late. Ovid does not present a “Mamuralia” under that formal name in his relevant account. Scholars have variously treated the episode as:

- expulsion of the old year;
- ritual scapegoat;
- smith mythology;
- a late antiquarian construction;
- an interpretation driven by resemblance between *Mamurius* and old names of Mars.

It should not be stated as established fact that the craftsman who made the shields was historically expelled or beaten.

### 7.8 Modern survival and hiding stories

No well-supported medieval or modern tradition identifies a hiding place. Claims that the shield survives beneath the Palatine, in a Vatican vault, in the Regia ruins, or in a secret pagan repository lack documentary provenance and should be classified as modern speculation or fiction unless independently sourced.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 The original shield

**Current status:** lost; physical existence as a heaven-fallen artifact unverified.

**Last known location:** cannot be stated more precisely than its late-antique institutional association with Rome’s Salian and pontifical cult infrastructure.

**Current owner or controller:** none known.

**Accession number:** none.

**Scientific examinations:** none.

**Photographs, casts, or measured drawings of the original:** none known.

### 8.2 The eleven copies

Their individual identities and fates are equally unknown. No catalogued shield has been securely linked to the set. It cannot be determined whether any survived separately after the cult ceased.

### 8.3 Archaeological sites

The remains of the **Regia** in the Roman Forum survive as an archaeological complex, but the site does not display Numa’s shield. Architectural and decorative finds associated with the Regia help reconstruct Roman religious imagery; they are not the missing relic.

The precise archaeological correlation between literary repositories, Salian curiae, and processional stations remains debated.

### 8.4 Representations

Material witnesses that survive include:

- engraved gems showing indented shields and transport;
- funerary or priestly reliefs associated with Salii;
- architectural terracotta or relief decoration from the Regia tradition;
- Republican and imperial coins;
- antiquarian drawings based on monuments.

These are evidence for representation and cultural memory, not chain-of-custody substitutes.

### 8.5 Legal and repatriation status

There is no known present ownership or restitution proceeding for the original *ancile*. Individual ancient coins, gems, and reliefs have their own museum or private provenances, but those are separate objects.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Antiquarian reconstruction

Renaissance and early-modern antiquarians inherited the literary descriptions and attempted to reconcile them with coins and reliefs. The resulting conventional image—a bronze figure-eight shield with deeply indented sides—became more visually stable in modern books than it had been in ancient art.

Nineteenth-century dictionaries of Greek and Roman antiquities reproduced engraved reconstructions and treated the combined narratives of Livy, Ovid, Dionysius, Plutarch, Festus, and Servius as though they described one fully recoverable ceremony. Those works remain widely copied online, often without their original qualifications.

### 9.2 Lexicographical survival

The word entered modern European antiquarian vocabulary. The *Dictionnaire de l’Académie française* defined the *ancile* as a sacred shield thought to have fallen from heaven and to guarantee the empire’s duration. Merriam-Webster records English use from 1579 and defines *ancile* as any of the twelve sacred Roman shields.

The semantic shift from one original to “any of twelve” reproduces the ancient identity problem in modern language.

### 9.3 Replicas

Modern replicas are made for:

- museums and teaching collections;
- Roman religious reconstruction;
- historical re-enactment;
- decorative sale;
- stage and screen;
- modern Roman polytheist practice.

Unless a replica is based on a specified relief, coin, or archaeological shield analogue, it represents an artist’s synthesis. No replica can claim measured descent from the lost cult objects.

### 9.4 Political and cultural symbolism

Ancient imperial coinage is the clearest documented political mobilization: pairing *ANCILIA* with Antoninus Pius’ imperial titles linked the emperor’s military authority with Rome’s ancient divine guarantees.

Modern nationalist treatments sometimes present the shield as a proto-national talisman of Roman destiny. This is a modern ideological adaptation of the ancient *pignus imperii* concept, not evidence that the object functioned like a modern national flag.

### 9.5 Literature and popular mythology

Modern retellings typically combine all ancient variants:

- plague;
- Egeria’s prophecy;
- Jupiter’s gift;
- bronze composition;
- Mamurius’ competition with other smiths;
- eleven indistinguishable replicas;
- Salii dancing throughout March;
- Rome’s survival depending on the original.

No single ancient source contains this exact composite in every detail.

### 9.6 Fantasy and gaming

“Ancile” is now used as a name for fictional force fields, shields, defensive technologies, and magical artifacts. Such uses retain the semantic core of supernatural protection while usually detaching the name from Numa, the Salii, and Roman ritual.

These are modern receptions, not continuations of the object’s custody.

### 9.7 Common modern inaccuracies

Frequently repeated but unestablished claims include:

- that the original was made of meteoric iron;
- that its exact dimensions are known;
- that all twelve surviving shields were destroyed by a named Christian emperor;
- that the Vatican possesses the original;
- that the shields were certainly Mycenaean imports;
- that “figure eight” was their single universally attested ancient form;
- that Mamurius was demonstrably a historical Etruscan smith;
- that one shield was visibly marked but only priests knew how to read the mark.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Source-critical problem

The event is traditionally placed in the seventh century BCE, but the earliest surviving words referring to Numa’s *ancilia* come from Ennius roughly five centuries later. Detailed narratives appear later still.

Research must therefore distinguish:

- the possible antiquity of the rite;
- the date when stories about it were recorded;
- the date of surviving physical representations;
- the traditional date attributed to Numa.

### 10.2 Historicity of Numa’s institutions

Ancient authors used Numa as the organizing founder of Roman religion. Attribution to him may preserve genuine institutional memory, retrospective systematization, or both. The statement “Numa founded the Salii and received the shield” is not equivalent to contemporary seventh-century documentation.

### 10.3 Shield typology

The major material debate concerns how literary descriptions correspond to iconography.

#### Type A hypothesis

A long shield with symmetrical side notches resembles Varro’s *ab utraque parte incisa* and Plutarch’s indented oval. It appears in relatively early imagery and may approximate the historically carried form.

#### Type B hypothesis

A double-lobed shield, sometimes resembling two joined oval or circular plates, becomes prominent in Augustan and imperial imagery and has analogues in much earlier Aegean and Italian material.

Possible explanations include:

- two representational conventions for one object;
- actual physical replacement or redesign;
- different shield forms for Palatine and Quirinal Salii;
- Augustan deliberate revival of archaic visual forms;
- artistic contamination from trophies and mythological shields.

No consensus permits reconstruction of the lost object to engineering accuracy.

### 10.4 Aegean or Mycenaean comparisons

Older scholarship frequently described the *ancile* as Mycenaean because bilobed or figure-eight shields are prominent in Bronze Age Aegean art. Formal resemblance is real, but direct historical transmission from Mycenaean Greece to Numa’s Rome is not demonstrated.

Central Italian material and iconography must also be considered. A resemblance across centuries does not establish that Rome’s cult preserved an imported Bronze Age shield.

### 10.5 Thracian comparison

Varro compares the bilateral cutting with Thracian arms. Plutarch’s description may also evoke the pelta. Whether this reflects the object’s actual origin, a Roman antiquarian typology, or vocabulary available for an unfamiliar archaic shape is disputed.

### 10.6 Mamurius as man, deity, or linguistic fossil

Interpretations include:

- historical-style master craftsman in legend;
- mythic smith;
- humanized aspect or name related to Mars;
- personification generated from obscure Salian words;
- scapegoat or old-year figure attached secondarily to the shield narrative.

The personal biography cannot be securely extracted from the ritual etymologies.

### 10.7 Augustan political use

Ovid’s narrative and Augustan-era representations belong to a program of renewed attention to old Roman priesthoods and sacred symbols. Scholars debate whether Augustan images reproduce genuinely archaic equipment or construct an archaizing visual past useful to the new regime.

The legend suited Augustan ideology particularly well:

- divinely promised imperium;
- Numa as peaceful religious founder;
- sacred continuity through restoration;
- an object whose preservation guarantees Rome;
- a skilled artisan rewarded by public memory.

### 10.8 *Pignora imperii*

Servius’ canonical list of seven guarantors is late. It should not automatically be projected unchanged into archaic or Republican Rome. Individual objects, including the *ancilia*, had older protective associations, but the standardized group of seven reflects late-antique antiquarian ordering.

### 10.9 State of primary evidence

The relevant evidence is dispersed among:

- fragments of Ennius;
- Varro’s linguistic works;
- Livy;
- Ovid’s *Fasti*;
- Dionysius’ *Roman Antiquities*;
- Plutarch’s *Numa*;
- Polybius;
- Suetonius;
- Festus and his epitomators;
- Servian commentary;
- Roman calendars;
- Salian inscriptions;
- coins, gems, funerary reliefs, and Regia decoration.

The *Carmen Saliare* survives only in fragments and quotations. No Salian archive or inventory listing twelve shields survives. No ancient conservation report survives. These losses prevent a true object-by-object chain.

## 12. Sources

### Primary literary sources

**Ennius, *Annales*, fragment preserved in Varro.** The earliest surviving words associate Numa with the institution of the *ancilia*. Because only a fragment survives, it cannot establish the original context’s full contents.

**Varro, *De lingua Latina* 7.43.** Preserves the Ennian fragment and the bilateral-cutting etymology.

**Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 1.20.** Describes Numa’s establishment of the Salii and their carrying of the heavenly arms.

**Ovid, *Fasti* 3.259–392.** Principal poetic account of Jupiter’s pledge, descent, naming, copying, and Mamurius’ reward.

**Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities* 2.70–71.** Detailed account of Salian organization, dress, procession, and handling of the shields.

**Polybius, *Histories* 21.13.** Evidence that Salian obligations affected the movement of a Roman commander.

**Plutarch, *Life of Numa* 13.** Principal account of plague, bronze composition, exact replicas, Salian dance, and competing etymologies.

**Suetonius, *Otho* 8.** Records the belief that departure before the moved shields had been stored was inauspicious.

**Servius, commentary on *Aeneid* 7.188 and 8.664.** Late-antique account of the shield’s descent, copies, location, and status among Rome’s guarantees of empire.

### Epigraphic, archaeological, and numismatic sources

**CIL VI 2158.** Inscription concerning restoration of stations of the Palatine Salii. It documents ritual infrastructure, not the physical survival of the original shield.

**Regia archaeological material.** Architectural phases and decorative imagery provide the physical context for Rome’s royal-pontifical cult center. No excavated object has been authenticated as an *ancile*.

**Antoninus Pius coinage, RIC III 736a and related types.** Two shields explicitly labeled *ANCILIA*, demonstrating official imperial iconography.

### Principal modern scholarship and reference works consulted

The recent *Journal of Roman Archaeology* study of early Roman priesthoods is especially useful for distinguishing the two iconographic shield types and relating literary, visual, and archaeological evidence.

Gianfranco Ferri’s research on the Salii, Salian movement through Rome, ritual stations, and the political use of Mamurius supplies a modern source-critical corrective to older synthetic accounts.

The nineteenth-century *Realencyclopädie* and *Dictionnaire des antiquités grecques et romaines* entries remain useful repositories of source references but reflect the assumptions of their period and must be checked against current archaeology.

### Overall assessment

The fullest defensible conclusion is that Rome historically possessed a ritual ensemble called the *ancilia*, probably shields or shield-shaped bronze cult objects carried by the Salii. The ensemble was ancient by the time surviving Republican literature described it, but its original date, manufacture, successive repairs, and material continuity cannot be established.

The singular “shield from heaven” belongs to Roman sacred history. Its narrative developed across surviving sources from Ennius’ bare attribution of the institution to Numa, through Varro’s antiquarian morphology and Livy’s “heavenly arms,” into the elaborate Augustan and imperial accounts of Jupiter’s gift, plague relief, Mamurius’ copies, and the guarantee of world rule.

The physical object and its legend converge in ancient ritual but separate under historical examination. The legend supplies a perfect chain—heaven, Numa, Mamurius, the Salii, Rome—while the documentary chain begins centuries after the alleged descent and ends without recording the shields’ fate.
