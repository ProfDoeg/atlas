# Globus cruciger: Object Dossier

**Research status:** 6 September 2026  
**Object class:** Christian imperial and royal insignia; iconographic attribute; heraldic emblem  
**Scope note:** *Globus cruciger* does not identify one continuously existing object. It names a form—the cross-surmounted globe—and, by extension, numerous separately manufactured regalia. Consequently no single chain of custody exists. This dossier reconstructs the history of the type, then distinguishes major surviving, lost, and replacement orbs. Claims about one exemplar must not be transferred to another.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Definition

A *globus cruciger* is a globe or approximately spherical body surmounted by a Christian cross. In a ruler’s hand it signifies earthly dominion subordinated to, authorized by, or exercised beneath Christ. In images of Christ it reverses the relationship: Christ holds the world itself. As tangible regalia it is conventionally carried with a sceptre during coronations and related ceremonies.

The underlying globe belongs to pre-Christian Mediterranean imperial imagery. Hellenistic and Roman rulers and deities used a sphere as an attribute of cosmic or universal authority; Roman Victory (*Victoria*, Greek *Nikē*) was frequently placed upon it. Christianity did not invent the globe but progressively Christianized its summit: first through Christograms and eventually through a cross. The Norwegian Royal Court accurately summarizes this transition as globe → globe with Victory → cross-surmounted globe from the fifth century onward. This is an institutional synthesis, not evidence that every surviving orb descends physically from one Roman prototype. [Norwegian Royal Court](https://www.kongehuset.no/monarkiet/kongelige-symboler/norges-regalier)

### 1.2 Names and etymology

- **Latin, modern scholarly usage:** *globus cruciger*, “cross-bearing globe.” *Globus* means ball or sphere; *cruciger* combines *crux/crucis*, “cross,” with *-ger*, “bearing,” from *gerere*. The alternative *globus crucifer* uses *-fer*, from *ferre*, “to carry.” The expression is descriptive rather than securely established as the ordinary ancient name of the insignia. Charles du Fresne du Cange used *cruciger* in his seventeenth-century numismatic writing, helping establish modern scholarly usage.
- **Latin documentary vocabulary:** *globus*, *orbis*, *orbis terrarum* (“circle/world of lands”), and medieval coronation descriptions such as *pomum aureum* (“golden apple”) occur. The multiplicity warns against assuming that every medieval “apple,” “globe,” or “sceptre” denotes a separate modern-style orb.
- **English:** *orb*, *royal orb*, *Sovereign’s Orb*, historically also *mound*. English *orb* derives through late Middle English from Latin *orbis*, “ring/circle”; “mound” reflects French *monde* and Latin *mundus*, “world.” [Oxford Learner’s Dictionaries](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/us/definition/english/orb)
- **German:** *Reichsapfel*, literally “imperial apple”; also *Herrscherapfel*.
- **French:** *orbe crucigère*, *globe crucifère*.
- **Italian:** *globo crucigero* or *globo crucifero*.
- **Spanish:** *orbe*, *orbe crucífero*.
- **Czech:** *královské jablko*, “royal apple.”
- **Polish:** *jabłko królewskie*, “royal apple.”
- **Danish:** *rigsæble*; **Norwegian:** *rikseple*; **Swedish:** *riksäpple*. All mean approximately “realm/state apple.”
- **Russian:** *держава* (*derzháva*), from a word associated with power, dominion, or the state. Russian terminology therefore emphasizes authority rather than shape.
- **Hungarian:** *országalma*, “country/realm apple.”

The “apple” terminology does not establish an origin in the Eden narrative. The round fruit-like form supplied an obvious vernacular name; the governing iconography is the world beneath the cross.

### 1.3 The form

No canonical material, dimensions, or construction exist. Examples range from small globes on coins and seals, through hollow or resin-filled goldwork, to gilded-silver nineteenth-century regalia. Common components are:

1. a spherical or compressed-spherical body;
2. an equatorial band;
3. a perpendicular half-band dividing the upper hemisphere;
4. a stem or mount;
5. a Latin, Greek, or jeweled cross.

The three visible zones conventionally acquired an explanation as Europe, Asia, and Africa separated by waters. That interpretation is firmly maintained by modern custodial institutions, including the Royal Collection, Danish Royal Collection, and Norwegian Royal Court. It should be described as a long-standing institutional-symbolic interpretation, not proof that every maker consciously constructed a geographical diagram. [Royal Collection Trust](https://www.rct.uk/collection/stories/the-crown-jewels/the-sovereigns-orb) [Royal Danish Collection](https://denkongeligesamling.dk/en/the-collection/objects/the-orb/) [Norwegian Royal Court](https://www.kongehuset.no/monarkiet/kongelige-symboler/norges-regalier)

### 1.4 Principal physical exemplars

#### A. Imperial Orb of the Holy Roman Empire

- **Current designation:** *Der Reichsapfel*.
- **Date and attribution:** approximately 1150–1210; stylistically late twelfth century; Cologne is proposed but uncertain.
- **Maker:** unknown.
- **Materials:** thin gold sheet, resinous internal filling, precious stones, pearls, and filigree.
- **Dimensions:** approximately 21 cm high, 9.5 cm wide and deep.
- **Catalogue:** Kunsthistorisches Museum, Vienna, Secular Treasury, **WS XIII 2**.
- **Construction:** the resin filling gives strength to the hammered hollow form. Four intersecting jeweled and filigreed bands cover the globe and pole; above is a cross with fleur-de-lis-like terminals.
- **Condition:** survives substantially as medieval goldwork, but it is one of at least three orbs formerly counted among the imperial regalia. Two others were lost during the 1796 evacuation. [KHM catalogue](https://www.khm.at/en/objectdb/detail/100444/) [KHM/Google Arts & Culture catalogue](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/imperial-orb-unknown/OAFGID48Ih8gfA?hl=en)

#### B. Sovereign’s Orb of the United Kingdom

- **Date:** 1661.
- **Goldsmith:** Sir Robert Viner/Vyner.
- **Catalogue:** Royal Collection, **RCIN 31718**.
- **Materials:** hollow gold globe; emeralds, rubies, sapphires, rose-cut diamonds, and pearls.
- **Dimensions and weight:** commonly catalogued at approximately 27.5 cm high, globe diameter about 16.5 cm, weight about 1.2 kg.
- **Decoration:** jeweled bands divide the globe into three zones. The cross bears rose-cut diamonds, a sapphire on one face, an emerald on the other, and pearls at the angles and arm terminals.
- **Identity:** an acknowledged Restoration replacement, not the pre-Commonwealth English orb. [Royal Collection Trust](https://www.rct.uk/collection/stories/the-crown-jewels/the-sovereigns-orb)

A second, smaller orb was made for Mary II’s joint coronation with William III in 1689. It belongs to the separate queen’s-regalia group and should not be confused with RCIN 31718.

#### C. Bohemian Royal Orb

- **Date:** first half of the sixteenth century, probably Ferdinand I’s reign; attribution to the Augsburg goldsmith Hans Haller in 1533–1534 is a scholarly hypothesis, not a signed certainty.
- **Materials:** 18-carat gold, sapphires, red spinels, pearls, and colored enamel.
- **Dimensions:** 22 cm high; 780 g.
- **Construction:** two flattened hemispheres joined by a decorative band and surmounted by a large cross. Six miniature sphinxes support the cross.
- **Decoration:** David’s anointing and David’s combat with Goliath appear on the upper hemisphere; Adam before the Creator, Adam conducted into Eden, and the warning to Adam and Eve appear below.
- **Inscriptions:** beneath the cross, **DOMINE IN VIRTUTE TUA LETABITUR REX ET SUPER SALUTARE TUAM EXULTABIT**, from Psalm 20/21: “O Lord, in thy strength the king shall rejoice, and in thy salvation shall he greatly exult.” Prague Castle’s recent catalogue also records the sense of a second inscription as “God rules the heavens and kings the earth.”
- **Identity:** it replaced an older orb associated with Charles IV’s regalia; it is not Charles IV’s original fourteenth-century orb. [Prague Castle](https://www.hrad.cz/en/prague-castle-for-visitors/other/the-bohemian-crown-jewels-10273) [2025 Prague Castle catalogue](https://www.hrad.cz/file/edee/2025/09/ckk_brozurka_a5_web-20250915-094812.pdf)

#### D. Austrian Imperial Orb

- **Date:** 1612–1617.
- **Maker:** Andreas Osenbruck, Prague.
- **Commission:** Emperor Matthias; designed to harmonize with Rudolf II’s private crown of 1602. Osenbruck also made the sceptre in 1615.
- **Materials:** gold, partial enamel, diamonds, rubies, sapphire, and pearls.
- **Height:** 26.9 cm.
- **Catalogue:** Kunsthistorisches Museum, Vienna, **SK/WS XIa 3**.
- **Identity caution:** this is the Habsburg house/Austrian imperial orb, not the medieval Holy Roman imperial orb WS XIII 2. [KHM Treasury guide](https://www.khm.at/fileadmin/content/KHM/Service/Tourismus/downloads/Schatzkammer_01.pdf)

#### E. Danish Orb

- **Date and occasion:** made for Frederik III’s coronation in 1648.
- **Function:** used in Danish coronations and anointings; last used as coronation regalia for Christian VIII’s anointing at Frederiksborg Castle Chapel in 1840.
- **Current ceremonial role:** displayed before a deceased monarch’s coffin during the *castrum doloris*.
- **Location:** Treasury beneath Rosenborg Castle, Copenhagen. [Royal Danish Collection](https://denkongeligesamling.dk/en/the-collection/objects/the-orb/)

#### F. Swedish royal orbs

Erik XIV’s 1561 regalia established a major surviving Swedish group, including crown, sceptre, key, and *riksäpple*. Swedish sources associate the orb with Erik’s coronation and with the Royal Treasury in Stockholm. It is notable in scholarship and popular catalogues for map-like decoration, sometimes described as the only surviving royal orb bearing a realistic contemporary world map. That superlative requires caution unless supported by a comparative catalogue.

A separate queen’s orb entered Swedish ceremonial practice. The Norwegian Royal Court dates Erik XIV’s introduction of a queen’s orb to 1568, a statement that probably refers to the queenly-regalia precedent rather than Erik’s own 1561 coronation set. [Royal Palaces of Sweden](https://www.kungligaslotten.se/artiklar-film-360/kungliga-slottet/2020-05-20-skattkammarens-historia.html)

#### G. Russian Imperial Orb

- **Russian name:** *Imperatorskaya derzhava*.
- **Date:** September 1762.
- **Maker:** court goldsmith Georg Friedrich Eckart.
- **Commission:** Catherine II’s coronation.
- **Materials reported in technical-historical literature:** approximately 465.11 g gold, 305.07 g silver, and 1,370 diamonds totaling about 221.34 carats.
- **Alteration:** the present appearance includes later modification, conventionally dated to about 1774.
- **Origin circumstance:** work began only on 7 September 1762, fifteen days before Catherine’s coronation on 22 September. Three older orbs were temporarily brought from the Armoury as contingencies. The Kremlin records that one “of Greek workmanship,” associated with Tsar Alexei Mikhailovich, was returned after the coronation with a gilded handle attached.
- **Reason for replacement:** the preceding orb used for Catherine I and Elizabeth had been stripped of stones and its gold reused; the loss was institutional dismantling, not a mysterious disappearance.
- **Location:** Diamond Fund/Kremlin complex, Moscow. [Moscow Kremlin Museums](https://www.kreml.ru/en-Us/exhibitions/virtual-exhibitions.koronatsii-v-moskovskom-kremle/koronatsiya-ekateriny-ii/)

#### H. Norwegian royal and queen’s orbs

- **King’s orb:** Adolf Zethelius, Stockholm, 1818; commissioned and paid for by Carl Johan for his Norwegian coronation. Gilded silver; gold rose-decorated bands; a miniature orb and chiseled Latin cross on top; 14 cm high, 10 cm diameter, 384 g.
- **Queen’s orb:** Erik Lundberg, Stockholm, for Désirée’s projected 1830 coronation. Gilded silver; amethyst bands; blue-enamel miniature orb, thirty small pearls, and a cross of eight pearls; 16 cm high, 10 cm diameter, 436 g.
- **Use:** the king’s orb participated in nineteenth-century coronations and the 1906 coronation of Haakon VII; the queen’s orb was first used when Queen Louise was crowned in 1860. [Norwegian Royal Court](https://www.kongehuset.no/monarkiet/kongelige-symboler/norges-regalier)

This list is representative, not an exhaustive census. Further orbs survive in the Prussian, Dutch, Romanian, Portuguese, and other royal collections; “globus cruciger” is also present as a small crown finial and in innumerable images, seals, coins, and sculptures.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Pre-Christian ancestry

The globe as a ruler’s attribute predates Christianity. Roman coins show emperors or personifications holding, receiving, or standing upon globes; the summit may bear Victory. The globe could express the *orbis terrarum*, cosmic order, imperial victory, or dominion. These images are iconographic ancestors, not *globi crucigeri* in the strict sense because they lack a cross.

Constantinian coinage and monuments introduced Christian signs into imperial victory language. A mid-fourth-century issue of the usurper Nepotianus places a Chi-Rho above a globe. This is a transitional Christianized globe, but a Christogram is not physically identical to the later plain cross.

### 2.2 Earliest cross-bearing depictions

The safest numismatic sequence is:

- **395–408:** Arcadius issues have been proposed as the earliest cross-bearing globe representations, but identification and dating remain contested.
- **Early fifth century:** Victory holding a cross-surmounted globe occurs in issues associated with Arcadius/Theodosius II.
- **By 423:** coinage of Theodosius II provides secure evidence. The Acropolis Museum identifies his reign as the first Byzantine coinage appearance. Numismatist Philip Grierson likewise placed the cross’s attachment to the globe in the reign of Theodosius II, while noting the earlier Chi-Rho form. [Acropolis Museum](https://www.theacropolismuseum.gr/en/node/1358) [American Numismatic Society Digital Library](https://numismatics.org/digitallibrary/ark:/53695/nnan99732)

The earliest evidence is therefore an **image**, not a surviving hand-held orb or an ancient text naming one *globus cruciger*. It shows that the type existed in official imperial representation; it does not prove that the engraver copied a specific treasury object.

### 2.3 Justinian’s monumental orb

Procopius, writing in the sixth century, described the colossal equestrian statue of Justinian in Constantinople’s Augustaeum. Justinian held a globe upon which stood a cross; Procopius explained that through the cross the emperor had obtained empire and victory in war. This is the earliest especially explicit literary interpretation of a materially represented cross-bearing globe. The statue was a monumental bronze, not portable coronation regalia. [Procopius, *Buildings* I](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/texts/Procopius/buildings/1B%2A.html)

The statue survived for centuries, lost parts, and was pulled down after the Ottoman conquest. Pierre Gilles saw surviving fragments in the sixteenth century; later accounts state that the bronze was melted, reportedly for cannon. What survives is textual and pictorial documentation, not the globe itself.

### 2.4 From image to portable regalia

Byzantine emperors regularly appear holding the type on coins, seals, ivories, and manuscripts, especially from Justinian I onward. Western rulers adopted or revived Roman-Byzantine insignial language. A crucial evidentiary distinction follows:

- images prove that a ruler wished to be represented with an orb;
- coronation ordines and inventories prove ceremonial or treasury objects;
- neither alone proves that a surviving orb is the one pictured.

Portable medieval orbs are poorly documented before the High Middle Ages. Non-precious burial orbs are attested from the eleventh century. The surviving Holy Roman imperial example dates stylistically to around 1200, centuries after the first iconography.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 History of the type

The type moved through representation before it becomes securely visible as surviving regalia:

1. **Hellenistic and Roman antiquity:** globe used by gods, personifications, and rulers.
2. **Fourth century:** globe combined with Christian monograms.
3. **Early fifth century:** plain cross securely replaces or supplements Victory/Christogram on imperial coinage.
4. **Sixth century onward:** normal Byzantine imperial attribute; monumental use documented for Justinian.
5. **Early medieval West:** transmitted through imperial images, seals, manuscripts, and ceremony.
6. **Eleventh–thirteenth centuries:** portable and funerary examples attested; surviving Holy Roman orb produced.
7. **Late Middle Ages–early modern period:** the orb becomes standard within many European coronation-regalia sets.
8. **Modern constitutional monarchies and republics:** some remain active regalia; others become museum objects or state-historical treasures.

### 3.2 Holy Roman imperial orb, WS XIII 2

**Manufacture, c.1150–1210:** Stylistic analysis favors Cologne. Possible intended rulers include Henry VI, Philip of Swabia, or Otto IV, but no commission document connects the object conclusively to any one of them. This is a documented gap at the beginning of its custody chain.

**Medieval itinerancy:** The imperial regalia traveled with rulers and custodians. Specific early movements of the orb cannot always be isolated from movements of the regalia as a group.

**1424:** Emperor Sigismund granted Nuremberg the privilege of keeping the imperial regalia “for all time.” They reached the city in March, reportedly disguised as an ordinary fish or herring shipment. Thereafter they were kept at the Heilig-Geist-Spital and removed for coronations and annual public display. [Nuremberg City Archives](https://www.nuernberg.de/internet/stadtarchiv/stadtgeschichte_daten.html) [Nuremberg Museums](https://museen.nuernberg.de/fembohaus/kalender-details/fuehrung-reichskleinodien-1479/)

**1796:** With French revolutionary forces approaching, Nuremberg’s council evacuated the regalia first toward Regensburg and then, through a secret operation, to Vienna. Two of the three orbs then belonging to the imperial-regalia complex disappeared during this evacuation. The surviving medieval orb is therefore not evidence that the set remained intact. [KHM/Google Arts & Culture](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/imperial-orb-unknown/OAFGID48Ih8gfA?hl=en)

**1800–1806:** The surviving regalia were deposited in Vienna. The Holy Roman Empire ended in 1806, but Vienna retained them. Nuremberg never received a new written transfer extinguishing its 1424 custodial privilege; later German and Austrian narratives consequently differ over whether Vienna’s retention was lawful custody, appropriation made possible by imperial collapse, or a settled historical fact.

**1938:** Following Nazi Germany’s annexation of Austria, Adolf Hitler ordered the imperial regalia transferred from Vienna to Nuremberg. The move was a political seizure under the Nazi regime and was deployed to link the “Third Reich” propagandistically with the medieval empire.

**1938–1945:** Initially displayed in the former St Catherine’s Church, the regalia were later concealed in the Historischer Kunstbunker beneath Nuremberg Castle against bombing and capture.

**1945:** United States forces recovered the cache.

**4–5 January 1946:** U.S. occupation authorities returned the regalia to Vienna. The Austrian National Bank documents the return and temporary custody arrangements. [Nuremberg Art Bunker](https://museen.nuernberg.de/kunstbunker/dauerausstellung/kunstwerke) [Austrian National Bank](https://www.oenb.at/Ueber-Uns/Bankhistorisches-Archiv/geschichte-erzaehlen/bilder-erzaehlen-geschichte/rueckholung-der-reichsinsignien.html)

**Since 1954:** displayed again in the Imperial Treasury in Vienna.

**Continuing legal-historical issue:** Nuremberg municipal history notes that Vienna obtained no written title superseding the “eternal” 1424 privilege. No active international restitution litigation was identified, but the custody history remains politically interpretable rather than juridically pristine. [City of Nuremberg](https://www.nuernberg.de/internet/stadtrecht/20_jahrhundert.html)

### 3.3 English/British chain

**Before 1382:** English coronation imagery and descriptions contain globe-like royal attributes, but terminology can overlap with sceptres.

**1382 and afterward:** the *Liber Regalis*, prepared for late medieval Westminster coronation practice, documents investiture with regalia. The surviving manuscript is Westminster Abbey MS 38; another fifteenth-century coronation text is British Library Add MS 38521. [Westminster Abbey](https://www.westminster-abbey.org/learning/christianity-in-10-objects/the-liber-regalis) [British Library catalogue](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/032-002057797)

**1649:** after Charles I’s execution and abolition of the monarchy, Parliament ordered the regalia delivered to trustees, “totally broken,” with gold and silver melted and jewels sold. The Society of Antiquaries holds the contemporary inventories and appraisals made on 13–15 August 1649. The pre-Commonwealth orb’s destruction belongs to the documented liquidation of royal property. [Society of Antiquaries, MSS/0108](https://collections.sal.org.uk/mss.0108) [transcribed historical inventory](https://readingroo.ms/7/8/7/9/78796/78796-h/78796-h.htm)

**1661:** Robert Viner made the present Sovereign’s Orb for Charles II’s coronation. Its continuity is ceremonial and institutional, not material.

**1669 onward:** the restored regalia were publicly exhibited in the Jewel House at the Tower of London, subject to removals for ceremonies and security.

**1689:** a separate orb was made for Mary II because she and William III were crowned as joint sovereigns.

**Subsequent coronations:** RCIN 31718 was repeatedly carried to Westminster Abbey and invested in the sovereign’s right hand, then placed on the altar before crowning.

**1952:** the orb was placed upon George VI’s coffin during his lying in state and funeral observances.

**1953:** used at Elizabeth II’s coronation.

**2022:** placed upon Elizabeth II’s coffin during lying in state and funeral rites; removed before interment.

**6 May 2023:** Archbishop Justin Welby presented it to Charles III during the Westminster Abbey coronation. The contemporary liturgy expressly identifies the orb as a seventeenth-century symbol of Christian sovereignty. [Church of England coronation liturgy](https://www.churchofengland.org/coronation/liturgy)

**Present custody:** Crown Jewels collection, Jewel House, Tower of London, controlled institutionally by the Crown and cared for through the Royal Collection/Historic Royal Palaces framework.

### 3.4 Bohemian orb

**1530s, probable:** commissioned during Ferdinand I’s reign. Bukovinská and Konečný proposed identification with goldwork supplied by Hans Haller of Augsburg in 1533–1534. Because no securely attributed comparative work by Haller survives, the identification remains inferential.

**Sixteenth–seventeenth centuries:** incorporated into the Bohemian crown-jewel ensemble, replacing Charles IV’s older orb. The regalia moved among St Vitus Cathedral, Karlštejn Castle, the Land Rolls office, Prague Old Town Hall, České Budějovice during threats, and Vienna under Habsburg custody. Prague Castle’s account often describes movements of the ensemble rather than documenting the orb separately. [Prague Castle](https://www.hrad.cz/en/prague-castle-for-visitors/other/the-bohemian-crown-jewels-10273)

**1791:** Leopold II returned the jewels from Vienna to Prague after representations from the Czech Estates. The seven-key custodial arrangement originated then: seven officeholders must convene to open the chamber and safe.

**1836:** last used in a Bohemian coronation, that of Ferdinand V.

**Twentieth century:** remained a focus of Czech state symbolism through Habsburg dissolution, the Czechoslovak Republic, Nazi occupation, communist rule, and the Czech Republic. During the German occupation the normal independent key-holding arrangement was disrupted when keys were surrendered to occupation authorities.

**Present:** secured in the Crown Chamber of St Vitus Cathedral at Prague Castle and displayed only exceptionally. Replicas are accessible in the Old Royal Palace.

### 3.5 Danish orb

**1648:** manufactured for Frederik III.

**Before 1661:** used in the elective-monarchical coronation order in which the king sat with sceptre and orb before anointing and crowning by members of the Council of the Realm.

**1661 onward:** absolutism altered Danish constitutional and coronation practice, but the object remained regalia.

**1840:** last coronation/anointing use, for Christian VIII at Frederiksborg.

**Since the end of coronations:** retained at Rosenborg and used mortuary-ceremonially during a monarch’s *castrum doloris*. [Royal Danish Collection](https://denkongeligesamling.dk/en/the-collection/objects/the-orb/)

### 3.6 Russian orb

**7–22 September 1762:** Eckart manufactured the new orb under extreme deadline pressure.

**22 September/3 October 1762:** used in Catherine II’s coronation in the Kremlin’s Dormition Cathedral.

**c.1774:** altered; later accounts associate the enriched form with an added great sapphire and diamond.

**1762–1917:** used in successive imperial coronations, culminating in Nicholas II’s in 1896.

**After 1917:** Bolshevik authorities inventoried and centralized the principal regalia. Many Romanov valuables were sold, but the chief coronation ensemble survived in state custody.

**Present:** retained in Moscow’s Kremlin/Diamond Fund collections. Claims that the present orb is a modern replica are unsupported by the institutional record; unlike some display crowns elsewhere, it is catalogued as the historic imperial object.

### 3.7 Norwegian orbs

**1818:** Zethelius produced the king’s orb in Stockholm for Carl Johan.

**1830:** Lundberg made the queen’s orb for Désirée, whose intended Norwegian coronation did not occur.

**1860:** queen’s orb first actually used, for Queen Louise.

**1906:** both sovereign regalia groups used at the coronation of Haakon VII and Maud—the last Norwegian coronation.

**Post-1908:** constitutional amendment ended coronations; subsequent monarchs received church blessings without being crowned. The orbs became museum and mortuary/state-ceremonial objects.

**Present:** normally exhibited in the west wing of the Archbishop’s Palace beside Nidaros Cathedral, Trondheim. They may be moved for royal funerary observance. [Nidaros Cathedral](https://www.nidarosdomen.no/en/attractions/riksregaliene)

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 There is no single “true orb”

The central identity error is categorical: the British, Holy Roman, Bohemian, Austrian, Russian, Danish, Swedish, and Norwegian orbs are not rival claimants to one relic. Each is an authentic object within a distinct regalia tradition. “Authenticity” means identity with its documented commission or historical ceremonial role, not identity with a unique archetype.

### 4.2 Earliest-image dispute

Some catalogues and secondary accounts place the first *globus cruciger* under Arcadius, others under Theodosius I, and institutional numismatic accounts place the first secure appearance under Theodosius II. The disagreement arises from coin dating and from whether a globe held by Victory, a Chi-Rho globe, and a plain-cross globe are treated as one type. The prudent finding is:

- Christianized globes occur by the mid-fourth century;
- possible cross-bearing forms occur around 395–408;
- secure, generally accepted coin evidence exists by Theodosius II’s reign, certainly by 423.

### 4.3 Holy Roman orb

Its medieval fabric is not seriously treated as a modern forgery, but its exact date, workshop, and commissioner are unresolved. “Cologne, around 1200” is stylistic attribution. Henry VI, Philip of Swabia, and Otto IV are competing historical contexts, not documented owners at manufacture. The loss of two other imperial orbs in 1796 also means that an earlier inventory reference to “an orb” cannot automatically be assigned to WS XIII 2.

### 4.4 British orb

The present object is indisputably seventeenth-century. It cannot be the medieval orb because Parliament documented the breaking and sale of the former regalia in 1649. Calling it “ancient” in ceremonial rhetoric describes the office or tradition, not the metalwork. Its recognized maker, date, catalogue number, and repeated ceremonial use give it unusually secure post-1661 identity.

### 4.5 Bohemian orb

The principal dispute is attribution. Prague Castle cautiously says first half of the sixteenth century, probably Ferdinand I. The more exact identification with Hans Haller’s 1533–1534 commission is plausible because documentary references fit the period and object class, but the absence of signed or securely comparative Haller goldwork prevents conclusive stylistic authentication.

The orb is also sometimes wrongly described as part of Charles IV’s original 1347 commission. The official catalogue explicitly states that it replaced the older Charles IV-era orb.

### 4.6 Russian orb

The 1762 orb is a documented replacement for dismantled predecessor material. Its later alteration complicates “original condition” but not basic identity. Older Russian orbs still exist in the Armoury, including one historically but incorrectly associated with Peter I; nomenclature and retrospective attribution have changed as cataloguing improved.

### 4.7 Scientific examination

Unlike organic relics, these objects are not readily resolved by radiocarbon or DNA testing. Authentication rests chiefly on:

- precious-metal and gem analysis;
- tool marks and construction;
- inventories and payment accounts;
- iconographic comparison;
- stylistic dating;
- documented custody.

No published comprehensive radiocarbon, DNA, or single laboratory campaign covering *globi crucigeri* as a class was identified. The major current scientific project on the Holy Roman regalia concerns the imperial crown, not the orb; its findings must not be silently transferred to WS XIII 2. [University of Vienna/KHM Crown Project](https://www.projekt-reichskrone.at/)

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Coronation investiture

The orb’s ritual force lies in temporary handling. It is presented, held, displayed, surrendered to the altar, or carried in procession. Possession is choreographed rather than equivalent to private ownership.

In the English rite the orb is placed in the sovereign’s right hand during investiture and then returned to the altar before crowning. The 2023 liturgy retained this sequence. The official interpretation is Christian sovereign power over the world under the cross. [Royal Collection Trust](https://www.rct.uk/collection/stories/the-crown-jewels/the-sovereigns-orb) [Church of England](https://www.churchofengland.org/coronation/liturgy)

In Denmark before absolutism, the king sat holding sceptre and orb before anointing and crowning by four councillors. This displayed a negotiated constitutional order as much as individual majesty. After coronations ceased, the orb migrated to royal death ritual.

In Norway, the crowned king and queen emerged wearing coronation mantles and holding orb and sceptre; this last occurred in 1906. [Norwegian Royal Court](https://www.kongehuset.no/monarkiet/kongelige-symboler/norges-regalier)

### 6.2 Legitimation

The cross does not merely decorate the world. It places rulership beneath Christian salvation history. In imperial usage it could articulate universal claims without demonstrating actual territorial control. The ruler’s small human hand encloses a miniature world, while the cross remains above both ruler and sphere.

In Christological art, especially *Christ in Majesty*, *Salvator Mundi*, and the Christ Child as ruler, the same form asserts divine rather than delegated sovereignty. When archangels or saints present an orb to a ruler, the image depicts transfer or authorization.

### 6.3 Funerary use

Orbs appear:

- as non-precious grave goods;
- on effigies and tomb sculpture;
- on royal coffins during lying in state;
- in *castrum doloris* arrangements.

These are separate operations. A burial replica is not necessarily a coronation orb; a treasury orb displayed upon a coffin may be removed before burial. The British orb was removed before Elizabeth II’s interment, while Danish practice places regalia before the coffin rather than disposing of them with it.

### 6.4 Access and handling

- **British:** public viewing behind high-security display; handling limited to authorized officers, clergy, conservators, and ceremonial bearers.
- **Bohemian:** seven officeholders jointly control access; public display only by presidential decision and only exceptionally.
- **Holy Roman/Austrian:** museum display under KHM authority.
- **Danish:** public treasury display; removed for prescribed mortuary ceremony.
- **Norwegian:** museum display with possible ceremonial relocation.
- **Russian:** state museum/Diamond Fund control with highly restricted handling.

No credible evidence was found for an inherent curse, miracle-working power, or required supernatural punishment associated with the object class.

### 6.5 Heraldic and architectural career

The cross-and-orb migrated from hand-held object to:

- crown finial or *monde*;
- sceptre terminal;
- royal and imperial arms;
- seals and coins;
- church-dome and civic-architecture finials;
- state emblems and decorations;
- printer’s marks and typographic symbols.

In heraldry a tiny orb above a crown can signify that the crown is “closed” and Christian-imperial, but heraldic meaning varies by jurisdiction.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 “The three bands prove medieval people believed in a flat earth”

**Classification:** modern misconception.

A spherical world held as an imperial attribute is incompatible with the claim that elite medieval culture universally imagined a flat earth. Institutional explanations treat the bands as three known continents, but this says nothing about denial of sphericity. The continents are regions on a globe, not three flat plates.

### 7.2 Eden’s apple

**Classification:** late associative interpretation, not documented origin myth.

Germanic and Slavic “realm apple” terminology encourages comparison with the fruit of Eden. Yet Genesis does not identify its fruit as an apple, and the pre-Christian globe already signified world dominion. The Bohemian orb does depict Adam and Eve, showing that a Renaissance commissioner could deliberately bring Genesis onto the spherical insignia; that particular decoration does not prove that all orbs derive from Eden.

### 7.3 Constantine invented the orb after seeing the cross

**Classification:** unsupported conflation.

Constantinian cross and labarum traditions helped Christianize imperial imagery, but the securely datable plain-cross globe appears later. No contemporary evidence says Constantine commissioned the first portable coronation orb.

### 7.4 The Holy Roman orb belonged to Charlemagne

**Classification:** institutional and national retrospective tradition, historically untenable for the surviving object.

Charlemagne was crowned emperor in 800; WS XIII 2 is stylistically dated about four centuries later. Carolingian use of globe imagery may be genuine, but it cannot establish Charlemagne’s physical ownership of the Vienna orb.

### 7.5 The British orb is medieval

**Classification:** ceremonial shorthand contradicted by documentation.

The office and ritual have medieval ancestry; RCIN 31718 was made in 1661. The older regalia were deliberately destroyed in 1649.

### 7.6 Universal possession

**Classification:** political symbolism, not legal fact.

Images imply that an emperor “holds the world,” but no orb conveyed title to the earth or automatically transferred sovereignty. It functioned as a material assertion whose effectiveness depended upon a recognized ritual, court, church, and political community.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Political imagery

The orb remains visible in royal coats of arms, crown designs, military badges, orders, coins, banknotes, and public monuments. Its political meaning now ranges from active Christian monarchy to historicized state heritage. Republics such as Austria and the Czech Republic preserve formerly monarchical orbs as public cultural property or nationally significant treasures without treating them as operative constitutional instruments.

The Nazi transfer of the imperial regalia in 1938 is the most consequential modern political mobilization of a physical orb. It attempted to manufacture continuity between the medieval empire and Hitler’s regime. Recovery and return to Vienna in 1946 repudiated Nazi custody but did not erase the older Nuremberg–Vienna title question.

### 9.2 Fine art and popular culture

The transparent sphere in Leonardo-associated *Salvator Mundi* has made the orb a modern focus of optical and attribution debate. Computational research found that the painting’s rendering could be optically compatible with a hollow glass sphere under conditions available around 1500. That study concerns a painted orb, not the material composition of coronation regalia. [Optical study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.03416)

Fantasy art, games, film, and royal pageantry frequently treat the orb as a magical object. Those powers are modern genre conventions, not attested historical beliefs about the regalia type.

### 9.3 Replicas

Replicas serve several distinct purposes:

- museum education, as in Prague’s Old Royal Palace and Nuremberg’s Fembo-Haus;
- protection of originals;
- commercial souvenir production;
- funerary or theatrical substitution;
- heraldic reconstruction.

A replica can accurately reproduce appearance without inheriting the original’s ceremonial custody or legal identity. Nuremberg’s replica of the imperial orb is not a rival claimant to WS XIII 2.

### 9.4 Alchemical symbol

A cross over a circle, conventionally rendered **♁**, was used in early modern alchemical and chemical notation for antimony and the *lupus metallorum*. Its resemblance and nomenclature connect it to the globus-cruciger form, but the sign is not proof that royal orbs were alchemical instruments.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

The subject crosses fields that often use different standards:

- **numismatics** establishes the earliest datable images;
- **art history** reconstructs iconographic transmission and workshop attribution;
- **liturgical history** distinguishes ceremonial objects and their investiture formulae;
- **goldsmiths’ studies** analyze construction and stylistic provenance;
- **archival research** establishes payments, inventories, destruction, and transfer;
- **museum cataloguing** assigns current identity and condition;
- **legal and political history** addresses state custody and restitution.

### Major evidentiary problems

1. **Image versus object:** a depicted orb may be an attribute rather than a portrait of an existing artifact.
2. **Group custody:** records often move “the regalia” collectively without itemized condition reports.
3. **Terminological instability:** *globus*, *pomum*, orb, mound, sceptre, and apple can overlap.
4. **Replacement:** a later object may inherit the predecessor’s ritual name.
5. **Retrospective attribution:** later treasuries associated objects with Charlemagne, Peter I, saints, or foundational rulers without contemporary evidence.
6. **National narratives:** Vienna and Nuremberg emphasize different lawful-custody moments; Czech accounts emphasize restoration from Vienna; post-imperial Russian catalogues redefine dynastic property as state patrimony.
7. **Uneven conservation publication:** detailed modern catalogues exist for some examples, while gem-by-gem scientific analyses and complete conservation histories remain unpublished or difficult to access.

### Important primary and near-primary sources

- Theodosius II coin series and standard numismatic catalogues.
- Procopius, *Buildings* I, on Justinian’s statue.
- Medieval coronation ordines, particularly the *Liber Regalis*.
- Sigismund’s 1424 Nuremberg custodial grant and municipal records.
- English parliamentary orders and the 1649 regalia inventories.
- Sixteenth-century Habsburg goldsmith accounts concerning Hans Haller.
- Kremlin records of Catherine II’s 1762 preparations.
- museum inventories of Vienna, London, Prague, Copenhagen, Stockholm, Moscow, and Trondheim.

### Discredited or unsupported propositions still circulating

- that WS XIII 2 was physically Charlemagne’s;
- that RCIN 31718 survived the English Commonwealth;
- that the orb originated as Eden’s apple;
- that its spherical form was anomalous in a supposedly universal medieval flat-earth cosmology;
- that every cross-over-circle sign is a royal orb;
- that all European orbs are copies of one lost relic.
