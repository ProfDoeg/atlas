# The Cyrus Cylinder: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary key

This dossier tracks the physical clay cylinder catalogued by the British Museum as **BM 90920 / 1880,0617.1941**, together with the detached Yale fragment **NBC 2504** where its identity and custody affect the reconstructed object. Contemporary tablet copies of portions of the inscription are witnesses to the text, not pieces of the cylinder.

Evidence is labeled where necessary:

- **Documented:** supported by excavation correspondence, museum records, contemporary publications, or securely identified objects.
- **Scientific/material finding:** based on physical examination or conservation records.
- **Scholarly inference:** a reasoned reconstruction not directly stated by contemporary evidence.
- **Institutional or political interpretation:** a later characterization promoted by a museum, government, international organization, or campaign.
- **Unresolved:** evidence does not permit a secure conclusion.

---

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity and names

The Cyrus Cylinder is a barrel-shaped, wheel-made and fired clay foundation cylinder bearing a Babylonian royal inscription issued in the name of **Cyrus II, “the Great,” king of Persia and conqueror of Babylon**. It was made at Babylon shortly after Cyrus captured the city in 539 BCE and was intended for burial in a building foundation rather than ordinary reading or ceremonial handling.

It has no surviving ancient title. Modern names describe either its issuer, form, or later interpretation:

- **English:** *Cyrus Cylinder*, *Cylinder of Cyrus*, formerly also *Edict of Cyrus* and *Charter of Cyrus*.
- **Persian:** استوانهٔ کوروش, *Ostovāne-ye Kurosh*, “Cylinder of Cyrus”; منشور کوروش, *Manshur-e Kurosh*, “Charter/Proclamation of Cyrus”; منشور حقوق بشر کوروش, *Manshur-e hoquq-e bashar-e Kurosh*, “Cyrus Charter of Human Rights.”
- **Arabic:** أسطوانة قورش, *Usṭuwānat Qūrush*.
- **French:** *Cylindre de Cyrus*.
- **German:** *Kyros-Zylinder*.
- **Akkadian royal name:** *Kuraš*, represented in the inscription as Cyrus’s name. The cylinder itself does not call its text a charter of rights.

“Cylinder” is archaeologically neutral. “Edict” and especially “human-rights charter” are modern interpretive titles. The latter became globally prominent through Pahlavi Iranian state commemoration and the presentation of a replica to the United Nations in 1971.

### Museum identity

- **Current principal object:** British Museum, Department of the Middle East.
- **Museum number:** 90920.
- **Registration number:** 1880,0617.1941.
- **Former number:** BM 12049.
- **Joined-object group:** G14030.
- **Associated loan number:** L1166.
- **Production place:** Babylon, in modern Iraq.
- **Findspot in the museum catalogue:** Amran, Babylon—Tell Amran ibn Ali, historically also written Omran or Telambran.
- **Date:** 539 BCE or shortly thereafter.

### Materials and manufacture

The British Museum identifies the material as fired clay and the technique as wheel-made. Its examination supplies a more complicated construction sequence:

1. A coarse clay core, containing unusually large grey stone inclusions, was formed first as a roughly conical mass.
2. Further clay was added to build out the barrel-shaped cylinder.
3. A finer clay slip was applied to the surface.
4. The cuneiform inscription was impressed into the still-plastic surface.
5. The completed cylinder was fired.

The coarse inclusions made the core structurally unstable and probably contributed to its breaking in antiquity. The museum suggests that the staged construction may indicate a standardized or mass-production procedure, although no second complete Cyrus cylinder is known.

### Dimensions

The British Museum records:

- Length: **21.9–22.8 cm**.
- Maximum diameter: **10 cm**.
- Diameter at end A: **7.8–8.2 cm**.
- Diameter at end B: **7.9 cm**.

No authoritative published weight was located. Claims giving a precise weight should therefore be treated as unverified unless accompanied by a museum measurement.

### Inscription

The text is written in **Akkadian**, specifically the learned Babylonian literary language of the period, in Babylonian cuneiform. It is arranged in 45 reconstructed lines. Lines 1–35 survive principally on the British Museum body; the detached Yale fragment supplies much of lines 36–45. The beginning and ending remain damaged.

The script and language are deliberately traditional and partly archaizing. The composition adopts Babylonian and Assyrian royal-inscription formulas, particularly models associated with Ashurbanipal. Cyrus’s Persian court is therefore speaking through a Babylonian scribal and theological idiom.

A line-by-line scholarly translation, based on Mordechai Cogan and Hanspeter Schaudig’s edition, is available in the linked text edition. Its contents may be summarized without inserting words absent from the clay:

- **Lines 1–10:** The unnamed Nabonidus is represented as an incompetent and impious ruler who disrupted cult, mishandled divine images, neglected Marduk, and imposed exhausting corvée.
- **Lines 11–19:** Marduk searches for a righteous ruler, calls Cyrus by name, delivers lands to him, accompanies him to Babylon, and admits him without battle. Nabonidus is handed over; the people and officials acclaim Cyrus.
- **Lines 20–22:** Cyrus gives his titulary and genealogy: king of the world, great king, mighty king, king of Babylon, king of Sumer and Akkad, king of the four quarters; son of Cambyses, grandson of Cyrus, descendant of Teispes, kings of Anshan.
- **Lines 22–30:** Cyrus says he entered Babylon peacefully, lived in the palace, protected the population, sought the welfare of Babylon and its sanctuaries, relieved burdens, and received tribute and submission.
- **Lines 30–35:** He claims to have returned divine images and gathered inhabitants back to their dwellings; the restored gods are asked to intercede with Bel, Nabu, and Marduk for Cyrus and his son Cambyses.
- **Lines 36–45:** The fragmentary conclusion concerns offerings and construction on Babylon’s fortifications and quay wall, including bitumen, brick, cedar, bronze, copper fittings, and Cyrus’s discovery of an earlier inscription of Ashurbanipal.

The sentence most often converted into a universal decree is lines 32–33. In context it concerns the return of divine statues and inhabitants to particular Mesopotamian sanctuaries and settlements. The text does **not** say that slavery was abolished, does not proclaim freedom of speech, does not name Ahuramazda, and does not formulate individual rights enforceable against a government.

### Damage, restoration, and present physical assembly

The cylinder now consists of numerous joined ancient fragments with modern fills. It was received in London already broken and incomplete. Missing portions include almost all of the opening lines and parts throughout the text.

The museum records that it was **refired on 28 August 1961**. Refiring archaeological clay was once used to harden unstable objects but would now require far more caution because it can alter the material and compromise some future analytical methods.

The object has documented treatment entries in 1962, 2007, 2009, 2010, 2013, 2015, 2020, and 2024. Public catalogue summaries do not disclose complete technical reports for every intervention. No published thermoluminescence analysis, clay-source study, residue analysis, or absolute scientific dating campaign was located. Its dating instead rests overwhelmingly on inscriptional and archaeological-historical evidence.

The original Yale fragment remains Yale property. Museum descriptions indicate that it was loaned and joined to the cylinder; at various exhibitions a cast or fitted representation has occupied its place. Consequently, “the Cyrus Cylinder” as displayed may be a composite of the British Museum’s ancient fragments, modern restorations, and either the ancient Yale piece or a substitute cast, depending on the exhibition.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Historical setting

Cyrus’s forces defeated the Babylonian army at Opis in 539 BCE. Sippar fell, and Babylon was occupied in October. The roughly contemporary Nabonidus Chronicle likewise represents the entry into Babylon as occurring without a major battle inside the city, although that does not validate every theological and political claim on the cylinder.

The inscription was made after the conquest, probably in 539–538 BCE, while Cyrus’s administration was consolidating legitimacy in Babylonia. The named authorial voice is Cyrus, but the physical maker and individual scribe are unknown.

**Scholarly inference:** Babylonian temple or court scribes, probably associated with the cultic-administrative environment of Marduk, composed the Akkadian text under royal authority. The attack on Nabonidus and praise of Marduk closely serve Babylonian priestly and Persian imperial interests.

### Purpose

The cylinder belongs to the long Mesopotamian tradition of royal foundation deposits. Such inscriptions recorded a king’s piety, construction, restoration, military success, and legitimate succession for gods and future rulers. They were buried in foundations or walls and were not statutes posted for continuous public consultation.

Line 43, referring to Cyrus’s discovery of an inscription of Ashurbanipal during building work, is self-referential evidence for that tradition: Cyrus presents himself as a proper restorer who finds, respects, and succeeds an earlier king’s deposit.

The British Museum describes the intended place as the foundations of Babylon’s city wall. Older accounts often called the findspot the site of Esagila, Marduk’s temple. The surviving excavation documentation is not precise enough to establish an exact architectural locus. “Foundation deposit in the Amran/Esagila area of Babylon, probably connected with Cyrus’s stated wall works” is the safest formulation.

### Clay provenance, workshop, and cost

The clay was almost certainly locally available Babylonian alluvial clay, but no published elemental or petrographic sourcing study was located. No quarry, clay pit, named workshop, scribe, contract, or price is documented. The surface, script, and genre are compatible with late Babylonian scribal production; they do not suggest manufacture in Persia.

### Earliest attestation

The artifact itself is the earliest surviving witness. Contemporary or near-contemporary historical texts such as the Nabonidus Chronicle illuminate the conquest but do not mention the cylinder as an object.

The two tablet fragments recognized by Wilfred Lambert and Irving Finkel in 2009–2010 as copies of part of the cylinder text show that at least some wording circulated beyond this particular foundation object. Their significance is real but limited: they establish textual copying, not necessarily empire-wide public promulgation, universal legal force, or mass distribution.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 539 BCE–1879 CE: buried at Babylon

**Documented/inferred from object type:** The cylinder was deposited during Cyrus’s building work at Babylon. It then remained in or near the ancient masonry until nineteenth-century excavation.

No ancient, medieval, or early-modern chain of handling is known. There is no evidence that Alexander, later Persian rulers, Jewish communities, Muslim rulers, medieval antiquarians, or European travelers saw or possessed it. Any seamless custody narrative connecting Cyrus directly to modern Iran is symbolic, not physical provenance.

### Ottoman permit and British Museum excavation

Hormuzd Rassam, born in Mosul and employed by the British Museum, directed excavations in Ottoman Mesopotamia for the museum. Austen Henry Layard, then British ambassador at Constantinople, helped secure an Ottoman **firman**. A more permissive permit was issued in 1878.

This was legally authorized excavation under the contemporary Ottoman regime. It was also part of nineteenth-century imperial archaeology in which a London institution financed excavation in Ottoman Iraq and exported large groups of antiquities. “Legally exported under an Ottoman firman” and “acquired within a colonial-imperial system” are compatible descriptions. The evidence found does not establish clandestine theft of BM 90920, but neither should the transaction be described as a gift from modern Iraq or Iran.

### Discovery, March 1879

An excavation inventory covers finds from **17–23 March 1879** at Telambran and Jumjumah and lists one “Cylinder unbroken.” Rassam himself was absent; his assistant and excavation overseer **Daud Toma** was directing the work. This explains later uncertainty about the exact locus and condition at the instant of discovery.

Rassam wrote to Samuel Birch on 20 November 1879 that the “Cylinder of Cyrus” had been found at Omran with approximately six hundred inscribed terracottas before he left Baghdad. That letter is the central provenance document cited by C. B. F. Walker and the British Museum.

The term “unbroken” in the field inventory conflicts with its broken condition on arrival. Possible explanations include:

1. It was initially lifted intact or substantially intact and broke during subsequent packing or transport.
2. The field description was cursory and overlooked fractures.
3. Pieces became detached while cases were opened, inspected, or repacked.
4. A piece was lost between excavation and museum inventory.

The surviving evidence does not identify when the Yale fragment separated or who removed it.

### Baghdad, Basra, and shipment to London

Cases from the excavation were opened at the British consulate, inspected or cleared in the presence of an Ottoman representative, and repacked. A merchant identified as **Baltazar** was entrusted to send the material by steamer through Basra.

The shipment reached the British Museum in **August 1879**. Theophilus G. Pinches inventoried it. His entry, presented to the trustees on 3 October, described a “portion of a fine cylinder containing an account of Cyrus’ taking of Babylon, his genealogy, &c.” This is direct evidence that the cylinder was already incomplete when formally examined in London.

### Announcement and publication

Henry Creswicke Rawlinson announced the find at the Royal Asiatic Society on **17 November 1879**. Rassam published notes in the Society’s journal in 1880; Pinches and Rawlinson rapidly copied, translated, and published the inscription. Early scholarship recognized it as a royal account of Cyrus’s conquest, genealogy, religious policy, and building work.

The British Museum formally registered it on **17 June 1880**, producing the accession number **1880,0617.1941**. Its acquisition year is recorded as 1880 even though it physically arrived in 1879.

### The missing Yale fragment

A detached clay fragment later catalogued as **NBC 2504** entered the collection assembled by the American clergyman and Yale alumnus James B. Nies. Its path from Iraq to Nies is undocumented. Yale sources acknowledge that how he acquired it remains unknown; dealer purchase has been suggested but is not securely reconstructed.

The fragment was published as an unidentified text in the Nies collection. Paul-Richard Berger recognized in 1970–1971 that it joined the Cyrus Cylinder and supplied lines 36–45. C. B. F. Walker announced the physical and textual identification in 1972; Berger published a fuller edition in 1975.

The match of clay, curvature, broken edges, and continuous text makes the identification secure. NBC 2504 is not a merely similar copy: it is a detached physical portion of the cylinder.

Yale retained title. The fragment was placed on long-term loan to the British Museum and physically integrated or represented in the restored display. It was apparently removed when the main cylinder went to Yale in 2024: the British Museum’s catalogue specifies that its 2024 Yale loan traveled “without Yale fragment Loan 1166.”

### Display and movement within the British Museum

The museum says the cylinder has been more or less continuously displayed since discovery. Documented locations include:

- At least 1900–1922: Babylonian Room, table-case G, no. 67.
- 1930s: Persian Room, wall-case 15.
- 1971–1972: *Royal Persia* exhibition at the British Museum after its first Iranian loan.
- 1975–1990: Iranian Room, case 9/6.
- 1994: Gallery 49.
- 1995–2005: Gallery 52.
- 2006–2007: Gallery 2, *The Changing Museum*.
- From 2007: Gallery 52, Ancient Iran, except when loaned.
- 2008–2009: British Museum exhibition *Babylon: Myth and Reality*.
- 2021: Victoria and Albert Museum, London, *Epic Iran*.
- 2024: Yale Peabody Museum.

### Loan to Iran, 1971

The Iranian government requested a temporary loan for the 2,500th-anniversary celebration of Iranian monarchy. Keeper R. D. Barnett reported the request to British Museum trustees on 9 July 1971.

The cylinder traveled at Iranian government expense from **7 to 22 October 1971** and was displayed from approximately **12–19 October** in the newly built Shahyad Monument in Tehran, now Azadi Tower. Barnett accompanied it. The museum’s report states that it returned safely.

It was selected as the central national symbol of the festivities and reproduced on publicity and a postage stamp. A much-reproduced image purporting to show the ancient original at Pasargadae instead shows a replica; the original was displayed in Tehran.

### Barcelona, 2006

The cylinder was lent to Barcelona’s Fundación “la Caixa” for *L’imperi oblidat* from **7 March to 11 June 2006**.

### Tehran, 2010–2011

The British Museum initially planned another Iranian loan but delayed it, citing the discovery and study of tablet fragments containing parallel text. Iranian officials publicly protested and threatened to suspend museum cooperation. The dispute combined scholarly scheduling, loan-risk concerns, and strained British-Iranian relations.

The cylinder eventually arrived in Tehran in September 2010 and was displayed at the National Museum of Iran from **12 September 2010 to 15 April 2011**, a substantially longer period than the initially announced four months. The cylinder returned to London afterward. Contemporary Persian-language reporting recorded its return after about seven months.

The loan included or was contextualized with the newly identified tablet fragments. British Museum trustees publicly described continued cultural exchange during difficult diplomatic relations as a reason to proceed.

### United States tour, 2013

The cylinder traveled through five American venues:

- Smithsonian’s Freer/Sackler galleries, Washington, D.C.: 15 March–26 April.
- Museum of Fine Arts, Houston: 3 May–14 June.
- Asian Art Museum, San Francisco: 21 June–2 August.
- J. Paul Getty Museum, Los Angeles: 9 August–20 September.
- Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York: 27 September–early December.

### India, 2013–2014

After the American tour, the cylinder was shown at the Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Vastu Sangrahalaya in Mumbai from December 2013 and subsequently in New Delhi, concluding in March 2014.

### Yale, 2024

The British Museum loaned the cylinder to the reopened Yale Peabody Museum for a special exhibition from **13 January to 30 June 2024**. The record states that the main object traveled without Yale’s own fragment. Conservation treatment is recorded in July 2024, plausibly following return, although the public summary does not give its purpose.

### Provenance gaps

The major gaps are:

- The exact foundation or wall context at Babylon.
- Whether it broke before excavation, immediately afterward, during consular inspection, in packing, or in transit.
- How and when NBC 2504 separated.
- The fragment’s custody between 1879 and acquisition by James B. Nies.
- Full unpublished details of every conservation intervention.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is BM 90920 ancient?

There is no serious scholarly dispute that the principal object is an authentic late-sixth-century BCE Babylonian foundation cylinder. Its secure nineteenth-century excavation history, script, language, literary forms, clay manufacture, named political context, and physical join with NBC 2504 mutually reinforce its authenticity.

No rival object credibly claims to be “the original Cyrus Cylinder.”

### Is NBC 2504 part of the same object?

Yes. Berger and Walker’s identification is accepted. The fragment restores continuous lines at the cylinder’s end and physically corresponds to the missing area. Its uncertain market provenance is a custody problem, not a serious identity problem.

### Are the Dailem fragments pieces of it?

No. Two British Museum tablet fragments identified around 2009–2010 contain portions of the same composition but are flat tablet copies, reportedly from Dailem near Babylon. They demonstrate textual reproduction. Calling them “missing fragments of the cylinder” is inaccurate.

### Dating

The standard date is shortly after the capture of Babylon in 539 BCE. This is based on:

- Cyrus’s Babylonian titles.
- The attack on the deposed Nabonidus.
- The reference to Cyrus’s son Cambyses.
- The building program following conquest.
- Paleography, language, and genre.

No competing scientific date has displaced this historical dating. The 1961 refiring may complicate thermoluminescence testing; no published modern TL result was located.

### “First declaration of human rights”

This is an interpretive authenticity dispute about the text’s identity, not the clay’s antiquity.

**Arguments used by advocates:**

- Cyrus represents his conquest as peaceful.
- He says he protected inhabitants, removed an unwanted labor burden, restored sanctuaries, returned divine images, and gathered displaced inhabitants back to dwellings.
- The biblical books of Ezra and Isaiah preserve a favorable Jewish memory of Cyrus’s authorization of temple restoration and return.
- These acts can be treated as early precedents for religious toleration, cultural diversity, and humane imperial rule.

**Main Assyriological objections:**

- The object is a foundation inscription, not a publicly enacted legal charter.
- Its beneficiaries are communities, cults, cities, gods, and royal subjects—not rights-bearing individuals.
- Restoration of temples and populations was a traditional Mesopotamian claim of legitimate kingship.
- Cyrus does not abolish slavery or coerced labor in general.
- Nothing in the text creates freedom of speech, equality before law, universal religious liberty, or legal remedies.
- The denunciation of Nabonidus and divine selection of Cyrus is partisan royal theology.
- The text records heavy tribute and rulers kissing Cyrus’s feet alongside claims of mercy.
- Similar formulas predate Cyrus; their presence is not proof of a new universal doctrine.

Amélie Kuhrt’s influential 1983 study situated the text within Mesopotamian royal convention. C. B. F. Walker called its essential character a Babylonian-Assyrian building inscription, not a general declaration of human rights or religious toleration. Modern specialists including Josef Wiesehöfer, Pierre Briant, Hanspeter Schaudig, Irving Finkel, and others differ in emphasis but generally reject a literal equation with a modern bill of rights.

### Expanded or false “translations”

Texts circulate online claiming that Cyrus:

- abolished slavery everywhere;
- guaranteed freedom of religion, speech, occupation, and movement;
- prohibited governors from oppression;
- promised that nobody would be punished for belief;
- invoked Ahuramazda;
- declared universal racial or national equality.

Those sentences are not translations of the surviving Akkadian. They are modern paraphrases, extrapolations, or fabrications. No single inventor of every expanded internet version has been securely established. The Pahlavi commemorative apparatus helped popularize an idealized reading, but not every false wording can be attributed personally to Mohammad Reza Shah or his government.

### Authority to authenticate

Authority has shifted:

- Babylonian scribes and royal officials authorized the ancient text.
- Rassam’s excavation established modern find provenance.
- Pinches and Rawlinson first identified and published it.
- British Museum curators control the main object’s cataloguing, conservation, display, and loans.
- Yale owns the detached fragment.
- Assyriologists establish readings through philology and joins.
- Iranian institutions, the United Nations, and UNESCO have conferred modern symbolic status but cannot alter the cuneiform content.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Ancient persons and gods

- **Cyrus II:** Named issuer and first-person speaker; conquest and restoration are represented as his actions, 539 BCE.
- **Cambyses I:** Cyrus’s father in the genealogy.
- **Cyrus I:** His grandfather in the genealogy.
- **Teispes:** Dynastic ancestor.
- **Cambyses II:** Cyrus’s son, included in prayers for royal welfare.
- **Nabonidus:** Deposed Babylonian king, presented anonymously at first and polemically as impious and oppressive.
- **Ashurbanipal:** Earlier Assyrian king whose foundation inscription Cyrus says he found during construction.
- **Marduk:** Babylon’s chief god and the composition’s principal legitimating actor; selects Cyrus and rejects Nabonidus.
- **Bel:** Title or aspect associated with Marduk; recipient of prayer.
- **Nabu:** God of writing and wisdom; asked to favor Cyrus and Cambyses.
- **The returned local gods:** Their images are restored to sanctuaries, and they are asked to pray for Cyrus.

No Zoroastrian deity is named. Ahuramazda’s frequent appearance in modern retellings is imported from other Achaemenid evidence.

### Modern custodians and interpreters

- **Hormuzd Rassam:** British Museum excavator who directed the Babylon project and reported the find.
- **Daud Toma:** Overseer apparently responsible for the excavation at the moment of discovery.
- **Samuel Birch:** Keeper of Oriental Antiquities and recipient of Rassam’s correspondence.
- **Baltazar:** Baghdad merchant entrusted with shipment.
- **Theophilus G. Pinches:** Museum scholar who inventoried the broken object and helped publish it.
- **Henry C. Rawlinson:** Announced the discovery and supervised early publication.
- **James B. Nies:** Collector in whose collection the detached fragment entered Yale.
- **P.-R. Berger:** Identified NBC 2504 as part of the cylinder.
- **C. B. F. Walker:** Published the join in 1972.
- **Wilfred G. Lambert and Irving Finkel:** Identified parallel tablet witnesses in the British Museum.
- **Mohammad Reza Shah Pahlavi:** Made the cylinder a central symbol of the 1971 monarchy celebrations.
- **Princess Ashraf Pahlavi:** Presented the UN with a replica on Iran’s behalf, 14 October 1971.
- **U Thant:** Accepted that replica and linked Cyrus’s policies to contemporary peace and human-rights concerns.
- **R. D. Barnett:** Accompanied and reported on the 1971 Iranian loan.
- **British Museum trustees:** Legal custodians controlling loans and conservation.
- **National Museum of Iran:** Host institution for the 2010–2011 loan.
- **Yale Babylonian Collection and Yale Peabody Museum:** Owner of NBC 2504 and 2024 host of the main cylinder.

### Institutional networks

The object moved through:

- Cyrus’s Babylonian royal and temple administration.
- The Ottoman antiquities-permit system.
- British diplomatic and museum networks in Mesopotamia.
- The British Museum’s Department of Oriental Antiquities/Western Asiatic Antiquities/Department of the Middle East.

- The United Nations diplomatic-gift system.
- International museum-loan networks in Iran, Spain, the United States, India, and Yale.
- UNESCO’s modern heritage and human-rights diplomacy.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Ancient function

The cylinder was not a cult image, weapon, royal regalia object, treaty tablet, or amulet. Its original ritual-political function was deposition in a foundation during royal construction. That act:

- informed the gods and future restorers of Cyrus’s works;
- established continuity with earlier legitimate kings;
- represented the conquest as divinely chosen;
- cast building and cult restoration as proof of kingship;
- placed a durable royal message inside Babylon’s sacred-urban fabric.

The deposition itself was ceremonial, but no detailed ritual for this particular object survives.

### Political legitimation

Possession of the physical cylinder did not confer ancient sovereignty. Its text legitimized Cyrus through Marduk, Babylonian genealogy of kingship, restoration, and peaceful entry. Modern possession and display have served different legitimating projects:

- The British Museum presents it as a major object of Babylonian and Achaemenid history and of global collecting.
- Pahlavi Iran presented it as proof of a 2,500-year tradition of enlightened Iranian monarchy.
- Iranian nationalists and diaspora groups use it as an emblem of pre-Islamic identity.
- The Islamic Republic has also exhibited it as Iranian heritage and a symbol of justice, despite the Pahlavi origins of its most prominent modern mobilization.
- The UN replica makes it an emblem of international peace and rights.
- UNESCO’s 2025 resolution reframed it cautiously as an early account embodying principles later associated with human rights and cultural diversity.

### Access and touching

In antiquity it was meant to be buried and inaccessible except during later rebuilding. Modern access is museum-regulated: the public may view it in a case but cannot handle it. Removal, travel, assembly, and conservation are restricted to authorized museum staff, couriers, registrars, and conservators. There is no ancient taboo or curse attached to touching it.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### Cyrus as divinely chosen liberator

**Early attested royal theology:** The cylinder says Marduk chose Cyrus, accompanied him, enabled his peaceful entry, and delivered Nabonidus to him. This is not later folklore; it is the ancient object’s central political narrative. It remains partisan evidence produced for the new regime.

### Universal liberation

**Modern elaboration:** The local restoration of gods and inhabitants became a story that Cyrus freed all enslaved peoples and granted universal religious liberty. The actual text is geographically and politically narrower. It may reflect real policies, but it does not formulate the later universal claim.

### Return of the Jews

The cylinder never names Judah, Jerusalem, Jews, or the Jerusalem temple. Biblical texts associate Cyrus with Jewish return and temple restoration, making comparison historically important. Nevertheless, the cylinder cannot be described as the physical decree to the Jews. The strongest defensible statement is that its claims are compatible with a broader imperial practice of restoring selected sanctuaries and displaced communities.

### “First charter of human rights”

**Twentieth-century political tradition:** This title was institutionalized during the 1971 celebrations and UN gift. It is not an ancient name and is not a consensus classification among historians of ancient Mesopotamia or human rights.

The UN’s gift database still calls its replica an ancient declaration of human rights and sometimes incorrectly describes the inscription as “ancient Persian.” The physical original is written in Akkadian Babylonian, not Old Persian.

In 2025 UNESCO adopted a resolution initially discussed under the title “Cyrus Cylinder: an Early Charter of Human Rights and Cultural Diversity.” The final language reportedly avoided the strongest claim and called it one of the earliest accounts embodying principles **later associated** with human rights. Reports that UNESCO formally authenticated it as “the first human-rights charter in world history” overstate the adopted wording.

### A cylinder meant for public proclamation

The parallel tablets prompted claims that the text was distributed throughout the empire. The discovery does support copying and circulation. It does not establish empire-wide publication, translation into imperial languages, display in every province, or legal promulgation. The cylinder itself remained a buried foundation object.

### Pasargadae display image

A photograph widely described as the original cylinder being honored at Pasargadae during the 1971 celebrations actually depicts a replica. The ancient object was temporarily displayed at Shahyad in Tehran. This is a documented modern misidentification, not evidence for another ancient claimant.

## 8. Current Status and Location

As of the latest available British Museum record, the principal cylinder has returned from its 2024 Yale loan and remains under the control of the **Trustees of the British Museum**, London. Its normal display is in the Ancient Iran gallery, **Room 52**, under museum number **90920**. Display can change for conservation or loans; visitors should consult the museum’s current gallery listing.

Its condition is stable enough for controlled international loans but remains incomplete, heavily joined, and dependent on modern conservation. A treatment entry dated 18 July 2024 is the most recent publicly listed intervention.

NBC 2504 remains the property of Yale’s Babylonian Collection. Its long-term physical relationship to the London display should be checked against current case labeling: the main object may show the original Yale fragment, a cast, or a reconstructed fill depending on loan arrangements.

### Legal status and claims

The British Museum treats BM 90920 as legally acquired through an Ottoman-authorized excavation and holds it under British statutory museum governance. Iran has repeatedly requested loans and has sometimes used the language of cultural return, but the cylinder was excavated in modern Iraq, not Iran. No completed legal restitution proceeding transferring ownership was located.

Modern Iraq has the strongest territorial connection to the findspot and place of manufacture. Iran has the strongest dynastic-national identification with Cyrus. Britain has current legal possession. Yale has title to the detached fragment. These are distinct forms of connection and should not be collapsed into a single ownership claim.

The cylinder has not been destroyed, lost, or supplanted by a rival claimant. Replicas in Iran, at the United Nations, in museum collections, and in commerce are explicitly modern copies.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century reception

Early publication occurred amid intense biblical archaeology. Scholars compared Cyrus’s Babylonian proclamation with Isaiah, Ezra, Daniel, and classical descriptions of Persian rule. This encouraged the cylinder to be treated as direct corroboration of biblical Cyrus, sometimes beyond what its wording permits.

The British Museum marketed casts in its “Biblical Archaeology” series. This framing helped establish its popular identity before the human-rights interpretation became dominant.

### Pahlavi transformation

In 1971 Mohammad Reza Shah celebrated 2,500 years of monarchy, calculating the anniversary from Cyrus’s rule. The cylinder became the festivities’ leading object-symbol despite its Babylonian language, Iraqi findspot, damaged condition, and relatively modest appearance.

It appeared on publicity and postage. Princess Ashraf presented a British Museum-made replica to U Thant on 14 October. The Metropolitan Museum of Art designed its case, accompanied by English and French interpretive texts. The UN catalogues it as gift **UNNY068G**, ceramic, approximately 3 × 8 × 3 inches, in the Conference Building.

U Thant’s contemporary statement was more measured than many later summaries: he called it evidence of an early attempt to establish peace and said Cyrus presented himself as a liberator who restored temples and subject populations. The UN’s present gift description makes broader claims about freedom, choice, resisting oppression, and human dignity that are interpretive rather than literal translations.

### Replicas

Known replica classes include:

- British Museum casts and commercial reproductions.
- Replicas made in Iran from a mold supplied by the British Museum.
- The 1971 UN replica.
- A pink plaster Iranian cast in a red velvet presentation box, catalogued by the British Museum as **C.209**.
- Educational, diplomatic, souvenir, and commemorative copies.
- Decorative objects including a British Museum-catalogued fountain-pen set bearing the cylinder image.

Replicas have sometimes been photographed or captioned as the original. None has an independent claim to antiquity.

### Post-1979 Iranian reception

The Islamic Republic did not eliminate the cylinder’s symbolic life. The 2010–2011 Tehran exhibition drew intense public attention and allowed the state to present an Achaemenid object through themes of justice, dignity, and Iranian civilizational continuity. Political disputes over delayed delivery demonstrated that symbolic custody could influence contemporary diplomacy.

Outside state institutions, the cylinder remains a major emblem for secular nationalists, monarchists, cultural heritage groups, and parts of the Iranian diaspora. Its meaning is contested: it can signify monarchy, pre-Islamic identity, religious tolerance, Iranian territorial civilization, or aspirations for present-day political rights.

### International museum tours

The 2013 American and Indian tours emphasized religious diversity, empire, and toleration. Their framing substantially expanded the cylinder’s public audience and often juxtaposed it with artifacts from Jewish, Babylonian, Persian, and other traditions.

### UNESCO, 2025

At UNESCO’s 43rd General Conference in Samarkand, an Iran- and Tajikistan-backed initiative on the cylinder was adopted without substantive debate. Sixteen states spoke on the item. The final phrasing placed it among early historical accounts embodying principles later associated with human rights, nondiscrimination, tolerance, diversity, and intercultural understanding.

This action constitutes a modern political and educational reception of the object. It is not a philological ruling that the Akkadian cylinder is a modern-style charter, nor a new scientific authentication of the clay.

### Popular culture and misinformation

The cylinder is reproduced in documentaries, schoolbooks, museum films, jewelry, posters, online graphics, nationalist videos, and human-rights literature. Its simplified popular image often combines three separate bodies of evidence:

1. What the Babylonian inscription actually says.
2. Biblical traditions concerning Cyrus and Judah.
3. Modern ideals of universal rights.

False translations thrive where these are fused. The most reliable corrective is a numbered scholarly translation preserving breaks and uncertainties.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### First phase: discovery and decipherment

Rawlinson, Pinches, and Rassam rapidly established that the object described Cyrus’s capture of Babylon. Rawlinson published a cuneiform copy in *The Cuneiform Inscriptions of Western Asia*; Rassam’s 1880 article and Pinches’s papers placed the find in contemporary Assyriology.

Initial work was constrained by the cylinder’s missing end and incomplete excavation records.

### Textual consolidation

F. H. Weissbach’s 1911 edition became an important early scholarly presentation. The anonymous publication of NBC 2504 did not initially solve the missing conclusion.

Berger’s recognition of the Yale join around 1970 transformed the text. Lines 36–45 established more clearly that the composition culminated in conventional building activity and an encounter with Ashurbanipal’s inscription. Walker’s 1972 notice and Berger’s 1975 edition integrated the fragment into scholarship.

### Genre and imperial policy

Amélie Kuhrt’s 1983 study remains central. It argued that interpretation must begin with Mesopotamian royal-inscription conventions rather than retrospective comparison with modern constitutional texts. Scholarship subsequently concentrated on:

- literary dependence on Assyrian and Babylonian models;
- the role of Marduk’s priesthood;
- whether Cyrus merely adopted local traditions or articulated a broader imperial policy;
- whether Nabonidus’s alleged impiety reflects fact, factional hostility, or both;
- relationships among the cylinder, Nabonidus Chronicle, Verse Account of Nabonidus, biblical texts, and local archival evidence;
- the nature and scale of population and cult-image restoration.

Hanspeter Schaudig produced a major modern edition of Nabonidus and Cyrus inscriptions. Irving Finkel’s 2013 edited volume combined a new translation, object history, archival research, conservation, reception, replicas, and the UN episode.

### Discovery of duplicate text

Lambert and Finkel’s identification of two tablet fragments in 2009–2010 changed one element of interpretation: the composition was copied onto forms other than the foundation cylinder. This weakened any assumption that the wording existed only on one buried object. It did not settle how widely, for whom, or with what legal force it circulated.

### Human-rights historiography

Three broad positions can be distinguished:

1. **Strong charter position:** it is the first declaration or charter of universal human rights.
2. **Precedent position:** it is not a modern charter, but Cyrus’s policies and rhetoric offer significant early precedents for religious toleration, return, and cultural accommodation.
3. **Genre-critical position:** it is conventional royal legitimation whose apparent humanitarianism must be read alongside conquest, tribute, hierarchy, coerced labor, and older Mesopotamian models.

The strong position is institutionally powerful but philologically weak. The second is defensible if expressed as comparison rather than translation. The third is closest to the dominant Assyriological classification, though “propaganda” should not be misunderstood as meaning that every stated action was invented.

### Archaeological and provenance research needs

Important research remains incomplete or inaccessible:

- Publication of a full modern conservation history, including adhesives, fills, refiring, and handling.
- Clay fabric and provenance analysis.
- Digitized three-dimensional imaging of every fragment and join.
- Complete publication of Ottoman permits and British Museum correspondence.
- Reconstruction of NBC 2504’s antiquities-market path.
- Full edition and contextual study of the Dailem tablet witnesses.
- A precise architectural reassessment of the findspot using Rassam’s records and later Babylon excavations.
- Comparative investigation of administrative archives to test specific restoration and labor claims.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and institutional records

The British Museum catalogue is the principal authority for registration, materials, dimensions, construction, conservation dates, loans, display history, associated names, and findspot. Its online entry incorporates archival summaries from trustee files, transfer books, excavation correspondence, and departmental inventories.

Rassam’s 1880 Royal Asiatic Society notice is the earliest published object report. Rawlinson and Pinches supplied the first cuneiform publication and decipherment. The 1971 UN press release documents precisely what U Thant said when accepting the replica and is preferable to later paraphrase.

UNESCO’s 2025 proceedings establish the organization’s actual wording and the political process more reliably than headlines declaring that UNESCO named the object the first human-rights charter.

### Text editions and major scholarship

- P.-R. Berger, “Der Kyrus-Zylinder mit dem Zusatzfragment BIN II Nr. 32,” *Zeitschrift für Assyriologie* 64 (1975), 192–234.
- Irving Finkel, ed., *The Cyrus Cylinder: The King of Persia’s Proclamation from Ancient Babylon* (2013; later editions under related subtitles).
- János Harmatta, “The Literary Patterns of the Babylonian Edict of Cyrus.”

### Assessment

The cylinder’s physical identity, antiquity, issuer, language, and general historical context are secure. Its exact original architectural placement and the early custody of the Yale fragment are not. Its classification as a Babylonian foundation inscription is strong. Its treatment as an important witness to Cyrus’s strategies of cult restoration and imperial legitimation is justified. Calling it a modern-style, universal, legally operative charter of human rights is a later political interpretation, not a literal description of the Akkadian object.
