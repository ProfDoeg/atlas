# The augur's lituus staff: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conclusion

“The augur’s lituus staff” does not designate one securely surviving archaeological artifact. It refers first to a class of curved ritual staffs used as insignia by Etruscan and Roman religious or political specialists, and second—more narrowly and famously—to a particular staff which late Republican Roman tradition identified as the **lituus of Romulus**.

These must be kept separate:

1. **The object type:** curved staffs represented in Etruscan art by the later seventh century BCE and repeatedly shown in Roman art, especially on coins and priestly reliefs.
2. **The traditional individual object:** a knotless, slightly curved wooden staff said to have been used by Romulus to delimit the heavens when taking auspices at Rome’s foundation; reportedly preserved in the Palatine headquarters or shrine of the Salii; reportedly recovered intact after that building burned.
3. **Modern objects called litui:** archaeological staffs, statuettes, reliefs, coins, antiquarian reconstructions, replicas, and unrelated curved trumpets. None has a documented identity as Romulus’s staff.

No museum currently publishes an accessioned object recognized as the original lituus of Romulus. No material specimen has a continuous provenance from archaic or Republican Rome. Its existence as a named object is securely documented only as a **Roman literary and institutional tradition first surviving in Cicero in the first century BCE**. Whether Cicero’s contemporaries still saw an actual staff in the Palatine collection, and whether such an object could have descended from an early Roman ritual implement, cannot now be established.

Evidence labels used below are:

- **Material evidence:** surviving archaeological or numismatic evidence.
- **Contemporary literary evidence:** an ancient text describing a practice or object of its author’s own period.
- **Retrospective ancient tradition:** an ancient writer’s report about a substantially earlier period.
- **Ancient skeptical testimony:** an ancient source expressly questioning a traditional claim.
- **Scholarly inference:** a modern reconstruction from texts or material evidence.
- **Legend:** a narrative whose supernatural or historical content cannot independently be verified.
- **Unresolved:** the available evidence does not decide the question.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Names

The Latin name is **lituus**, plural **litui**, conventionally pronounced approximately *LIT-u-us* in English and *LEE-too-oos* in reconstructed Classical Latin. Relevant expressions include:

- **lituus auguralis** — “augural lituus” or augur’s staff; mainly a modern classificatory expression.
- **lituus augurum** — “staff of the augurs.”
- **lituus Romuli** — “the lituus of Romulus,” Cicero’s designation for the claimed individual relic.
- **baculum sine nodo aduncum** — Livy’s “knotless, curved staff.”
- **incurvum et leviter a summo inflexum bacillum** — Cicero’s “a curved little staff, bent slightly at the top.”
- Greek discussions normally paraphrase the implement as a curved staff rather than preserving a securely attested indigenous Greek name for it.
- Italian: **lituo augurale**, **lituo di Romolo**.
- French: **lituus augural**, **bâton augural**.
- German: **Augurenstab**, **Krummstab**, or **Lituus**.

Latin *lituus* also names a long, curved military trumpet. Ancient and modern writers have consequently disagreed over whether the staff was named from the trumpet or the trumpet from the staff. Cicero explicitly presents the staff as named from its resemblance to the musical lituus. That statement is an ancient etymology, not a demonstrated linguistic derivation.

Nineteenth-century dictionaries commonly called *lituus* an Etruscan word meaning “crooked.” Modern linguistic work is more guarded: the word has often been classified as an Etruscan loan because it lacks a convincing Indo-European derivation and belongs to a cultural field associated with Etruria, but no securely demonstrated Etruscan source-form and derivation settle the question. “Etruscan” should therefore be labeled **probable or possible, not proved**. The Lewis and Short lexicon records the traditional Etruscan derivation; modern studies of proposed Etruscan loans warn that cultural attribution and absence of an Indo-European etymology are not, by themselves, linguistic proof.

### The general type

The augural lituus was a portable rod with a straight shaft and a curved or spirally bent upper end. Its silhouette ranges in surviving images from a simple shepherd’s crook to a tight inward spiral. The end was not normally rendered as a large hook intended to bear body weight. It functioned as an insignia and ritual pointing or delimiting instrument.

Livy’s description—**baculum sine nodo aduncum**, “a curved staff without a knot”—implies wood selected or prepared so that no natural knot interrupted it. Cicero’s diminutive **bacillum** suggests a relatively light staff rather than a large walking stick. No ancient source supplies a dependable length, diameter, weight, wood species, surface treatment, color, inscription, or metal fittings for Romulus’s supposed staff.

Modern publications sometimes describe litui generally as wooden or metal. Archaeological identification is difficult because:

- wood rarely survives;
- a detached bronze crook or terminal can resemble a furniture, chariot, or sceptre fitting;
- images do not reliably distinguish a ceremonial staff from a practical crook;
- the musical lituus can be confused with the staff in damaged or schematic images;
- the same curved form could signify political rank, ritual function, hunting, pastoral activity, or divine and royal authority.

### Cicero’s physical description of Romulus’s staff

In *De divinatione* 1.30, written in 45–44 BCE, Cicero’s speaker Quintus describes the named relic:

> *Qui quidem Romuli lituus, id est incurvum et leviter a summo inflexum bacillum…*

A close translation is: “That lituus of Romulus—that is, a curved little staff bent slightly at the top…”

He adds that it took its name from the similarly shaped trumpet and says that it had been deposited in the **curia Saliorum** on the Palatine.

This is the fullest surviving physical description of the claimed individual object. It gives:

- material: not expressly stated, although *bacillum* implies a small wooden rod;
- shape: curved, with a slight bend at the top;
- dimensions: not given;
- decoration: not given;
- inscription: none reported;
- maker’s mark: none reported;
- repairs or replacements: none reported;
- condition: reportedly found intact after a fire.

Livy’s knotless description occurs in the inauguration of Numa, not in a description of Romulus’s relic. It is evidence for what Augustan-period Romans thought the proper ritual instrument looked like, not direct evidence that Romulus’s staff was knotless.

### Surviving representations

Curved staffs conventionally called litui appear in Etruscan and Roman art:

- later seventh-century BCE Etruscan imagery includes spiral-ended staffs;
- sixth-century BCE architectural terracottas from central Italy show elite or ritual figures carrying curved staffs;
- bucchero relief decoration presents seated or standing elite figures with birds and curved staffs;
- tomb paintings and funerary reliefs show such staffs as insignia;
- a bronze figurine from the votive deposit beneath the Lapis Niger carries a curved staff;
- Republican and imperial coins use the lituus as a compact badge of augural office;
- portraits and reliefs associate it with magistrates, augurs, and emperors.

None of these representations is labeled “Romulus’s lituus.” They establish the antiquity and cultural reality of the object type, not the identity or survival of the legendary individual staff.

### What survives

The staff attributed to Romulus is **lost, destroyed, discarded, or never an authentic archaic object**. The evidence does not permit a choice among those possibilities. What survives is:

- Cicero’s description and custody story;
- Valerius Maximus’s shorter miracle report;
- Cicero’s own skeptical dismissal of the fire-survival story;
- later antiquarian repetition;
- numerous ancient images of the object type;
- archaeological comparanda, not the named relic itself.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Archaeological origin of the type

The curved staff considerably predates the surviving Roman literary descriptions. Comparable objects and insignia occur in the ancient Near East, Anatolia, and Italy. Claus Ambos and Ingrid Krauskopf trace a long Near Eastern history of curved staffs associated with gods, kings, shepherding, hunting, and authority. They argue that an eastern influence on Etruscan forms is more plausible than an entirely independent Italian development, particularly because strongly spiral-shaped litui occur in Etruria by the later seventh century BCE.

That remains a **typological and iconographic reconstruction**, not a provenance for Romulus’s staff. The use of “lituus” by modern Near Eastern archaeologists is itself retrospective: a Latin name is applied to Hittite or other curved staffs that had their own local terminology, including Hittite **GIŠkalmus**.

The earliest Italian material evidence currently cited in specialist literature consists primarily of representations, not securely identified complete wooden augural staffs. These representations demonstrate that curved staffs were already symbols of rank or ritual status in pre-Roman Italy. They do not establish whether Roman augural procedure was borrowed whole from Etruria, developed within shared central-Italian practice, or retrospectively classified as “Etruscan.”

### Traditional creation of the individual object

Roman tradition attached the staff to **Romulus**, conventionally Rome’s founder and first king. Cicero says Romulus used it to mark out the regions of the sky when founding the city:

> *Nempe eo Romulus regiones direxit tum cum urbem condidit.*

“Indeed, with it Romulus marked out the regions when he founded the city.”

The traditional date of Rome’s foundation, 753 BCE in the Varronian chronology, is not a manufacturing date supported by documentation. No source identifies:

- the maker;
- a workshop;
- the wood species;
- the source of the timber;
- a commission;
- a cost;
- a dedication inscription;
- a contemporary inventory.

The most that can be said is that late Republican Romans told a story in which Romulus possessed and used this particular implement at the foundation.

### Earliest surviving textual attestation

The earliest surviving explicit description of **Romulus’s individual lituus** is Cicero’s *De divinatione*, composed in 45–44 BCE. The date creates a gap of roughly seven centuries between the alleged use and its first surviving named description.

Cicero’s dialogue is especially important because it contains both belief and criticism:

- In Book 1, Quintus cites the lituus and its miraculous survival as evidence favoring divination.
- In Book 2.80, Marcus responds: *Omitte igitur lituum Romuli, quem in maximo incendio negas potuisse comburi … nihil debet esse in philosophia commenticiis fabellis loci*—in substance, “Leave aside Romulus’s lituus, which you say could not be burned in the great fire … invented tales have no place in philosophy.”

Thus the earliest surviving author to report the relic also preserves an explicit ancient classification of the story as a potentially **fabricated tale**. The skeptical passage does not prove that no staff existed in the Salians’ collection. It does show that miraculous survival and authentic identity were contestable within Roman elite discourse.

### Livy and the normative form

Livy, writing his early books probably in the late first century BCE, describes the inauguration of Numa Pompilius. The augur takes his seat facing south and holds in his right hand a **knotless, curved staff called the lituus**. He uses it to mark the celestial regions and establish the ritual field of observation.

Livy does not supply a custody history for Romulus’s staff in this passage. His testimony establishes the literary form and ritual function of an augural lituus as understood in the Augustan age.

### Valerius Maximus

Valerius Maximus, writing under Tiberius in the early first century CE, includes the relic among marvels:

> *Deusto sacrario Saliorum nihil in eo praeter lituum Romuli integrum repertum est.*

“When the shrine of the Salii had burned, nothing in it was found intact except the lituus of Romulus.”

Valerius calls the storage place a **sacrarium Saliorum**, whereas Cicero calls it a **curia Saliorum**. The difference may reflect varying names for a Saliar headquarters, assembly room, or sacred repository, or later uncertainty about the building’s precise institutional character.

### Virgil and Picus

Virgil’s *Aeneid* 7.187 depicts the ancient king Picus with the **Quirinal lituus** and a short sacred shield. This poetic image associates the staff with pre-Roman kingship, priesthood, and augural authority. It does not mention Romulus’s preserved relic. Servius’s late antique commentary glosses *lituus* simply as the curved staff of augurs.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because no surviving specimen can be identified, the following is a chain of **claims about custody**, not a verified physical provenance.

### 1. Romulus at Rome’s foundation

- **Claim:** Romulus held and used the staff to delimit celestial regions while founding Rome.
- **Date claimed:** traditionally 753 BCE.
- **Earliest surviving source:** Cicero, *De divinatione* 1.30, 45–44 BCE.
- **Evidence class:** retrospective ancient tradition.
- **Gap:** approximately seven centuries between alleged event and surviving account.
- **Physical transfer:** none described. The staff presumably remained a royal, priestly, or public sacred object, but the sources do not say how it moved from Romulus into later custody.

### 2. Deposit with the Palatine Salii

- **Claim:** The staff lay in the **curia Saliorum** on the Palatine.
- **Source:** Cicero, *De divinatione* 1.30.
- **Variant:** Valerius Maximus calls the building the **sacrarium Saliorum**.
- **Custodian:** by implication the **Salii Palatini**, the dancing priests associated with Mars and Rome’s archaic sacred shields.
- **Mechanism of transfer:** not recorded.
- **Date of deposit:** not recorded.
- **Inventory or inscription:** none survives.
- **Continuity problem:** nothing documents the staff between Romulus’s supposed lifetime and Cicero’s statement that it was deposited there.

The choice of the Salii as guardians is intelligible within Roman religious tradition. Their Palatine establishment preserved archaic royal and martial associations, most famously the **ancilia**, the sacred shields. Housing Romulus’s augural insignia there would connect the relic to the founder, Mars, priestly memory, and the permanence of the city.

### 3. Destruction of the building and recovery of the staff

- **Claim:** The Saliar building burned; everything else was consumed, but Romulus’s lituus was found intact.
- **Sources:** Cicero, *De divinatione* 1.30; Valerius Maximus 1.8.11.
- **Common modern reconstruction:** the fire is associated with the Gallic sack traditionally dated 390 or 387/386 BCE.
- **Important qualification:** Cicero’s immediate wording does not explicitly name the Gauls or date the fire. Modern scholars connect it with the Gallic destruction or with a fire remembered in the ritual calendar, but the identification is an inference.
- **Possible calendrical connection:** scholarship has associated the discovery with a notice in the *Fasti Praenestini* under 23 March, but the damaged calendrical and topographical evidence does not prove that the notice records the same event or the Gallic sack.
- **Condition claimed:** intact.
- **Conservation action:** none reported.
- **Witnesses:** none named.
- **Date of discovery:** unresolved.
- **Evidence class:** ancient miracle tradition, explicitly doubted by Cicero’s skeptical speaker.

This is the only reported “recovery” episode. There is no police, priestly, archival, or epigraphic record of the discovery.

### 4. Presumed continued Saliar custody

Cicero’s use of *cum situs esset*—“when it had been placed/lay”—identifies the traditional repository but does not clearly say that Cicero personally saw the object or that it remained there in 44 BCE. Valerius Maximus likewise reports the marvel but does not claim direct inspection.

Accordingly:

- **Possible:** an object presented as Romulus’s lituus remained in a Palatine sacred collection in the late Republic or early Empire.
- **Not established:** that Cicero or Valerius Maximus saw it.
- **Not established:** that it was catalogued or displayed.
- **Not established:** that its custodians claimed uninterrupted descent from the regal period.
- **Not established:** that the pre-fire and post-fire objects were physically identical.

### 5. Disappearance

No securely identified later text gives an observable transfer, theft, repair, imperial relocation, antiquarian discovery, Christian confiscation, or destruction of Romulus’s staff. Its chain ends in literary tradition.

Potential moments of loss include Rome’s recurrent fires, rebuilding of the Palatine, the suppression or attrition of traditional priesthoods in late antiquity, dispersal of temple collections, or simple decay of an organic object. None can be tied to this staff by evidence.

The chain therefore breaks no later than late antiquity and arguably already before Cicero: the literary record never becomes a modern-style documented provenance.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Was there a physical relic in Cicero’s Rome?

**Case that some object existed:** Cicero gives a precise repository and a concise physical description. Roman priestly bodies did preserve archaic-looking ritual objects. A staff exhibited or retained as Romulus’s could therefore have existed independently of whether it was genuinely eighth-century BCE.

**Case for literary or institutional legend:** the narrative has the structure of a relic miracle—general destruction, unique survival, recovery intact—and appears in an argument defending divination. Cicero’s skeptical speaker subsequently calls the lituus story one of the *commenticiae fabellae*, invented or fictitious tales.

**Assessment:** the existence of a staff claimed as Romulus’s in the late Republic is plausible but unverified. Its authenticity as Romulus’s personal object is unsupported.

### Could a wooden staff have survived from the eighth century BCE?

Long survival of wood is physically possible under carefully controlled conditions but ordinarily requires dry, waterlogged, frozen, sealed, mineralized, or continuously conserved environments. No such preservation history is supplied here. A wooden staff kept in active cult use and then exposed to a destructive fire would require exceptional circumstances to survive intact.

No scientific test was performed or is available:

- no radiocarbon date;
- no dendrochronology;
- no species identification;
- no residue analysis;
- no microscopic examination;
- no conservation report;
- no ancient or modern photograph;
- no measured drawing.

The absence of testing follows from the absence of an identified object.

### Replacement or substitution

Several historically plausible scenarios remain unresolved:

1. an old but post-Romulan staff was sincerely misidentified as the founder’s;
2. the Salii periodically renewed a perishable insignia while maintaining ritual identity;
3. an object was substituted after a fire and its continuity was institutional rather than material;
4. the “intact recovery” story developed around a relic already housed by the Salii;
5. the physical relic and the fire story were literary constructions;
6. an authentic early staff survived for some period but was later lost.

No source admits replacement, and no rival object is documented. These remain hypotheses, not events.

### Rival claimants

There are no well-supported modern rival claimants comparable to competing relics of a saint. Museums possess objects described typologically as litui or figures carrying them, but no major museum catalogue identifies one as **the lituus Romuli**.

### Authority to authenticate

In the traditional phase, implied authority lay with:

- the Salii as custodians;
- Roman antiquarian and priestly memory;
- authors such as Cicero and Valerius Maximus.

Cicero shows that literary repetition did not compel philosophical belief. In modern scholarship, authentication would belong to archaeological context, laboratory analysis, and documented provenance. Since the candidate object is absent, modern authority can assess only the tradition, not the artifact.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Romulus

- **Connection:** traditional first bearer and user; said to have marked out celestial regions with the staff at Rome’s foundation.
- **Claimed date:** eighth century BCE.
- **Source:** Cicero, *De divinatione* 1.30.
- **Status:** retrospective foundation tradition.

### Remus

- **Connection:** indirect. The competing auspices of Romulus and Remus form the wider foundation narrative, but no surviving early source places Romulus’s preserved staff in Remus’s hands.
- **Status:** narrative context, not custody.

### Numa Pompilius

- **Connection:** Livy’s inauguration scene supplies the most precise ancient description of ritual handling: the augur holds a knotless curved lituus and marks out the celestial field.
- **Date claimed:** traditionally late eighth or early seventh century BCE.
- **Source:** Livy 1.18.
- **Status:** retrospective exemplum; not a custody link to Romulus’s particular staff.

### The unnamed augur inaugurating Numa

- **Connection:** performs the recorded procedure with a lituus.
- **Action:** sits facing south, holds the staff in the right hand, surveys city and countryside, prays, and divides the heavens.
- **Status:** traditional ritual actor; bearer of a lituus, not necessarily Romulus’s.

### The Salii Palatini

- **Connection:** institutional custodians implied by the staff’s deposit in their Palatine curia or sacrarium.
- **Date:** alleged custody before the fire and possibly afterward.
- **Sources:** Cicero and Valerius Maximus.
- **Status:** traditional institutional provenance.

### Mars

- **Connection:** divine focus of the Salii and their martial ritual apparatus.
- **Status:** contextual divine association. No surviving source says Mars owned Romulus’s lituus.

### Jupiter

- **Connection:** augural ritual sought Jupiter’s assent or communicated will through signs in the marked celestial templum.
- **Status:** functional divine association, not ownership.

### Quintus Tullius Cicero

- **Connection:** dialogue-speaker who adduces the staff and its survival as evidence for divination.
- **Date:** 45–44 BCE.
- **Status:** literary witness to the tradition, not documented custodian.

### Marcus Tullius Cicero

- **Connection:** author and skeptical dialogue-speaker who rejects reliance on the lituus miracle.
- **Date:** 45–44 BCE.
- **Status:** earliest surviving critical examiner of the claim.

### Titus Livius

- **Connection:** describes the form and procedural use of the lituus in Numa’s inauguration.
- **Date:** late first century BCE.
- **Status:** literary antiquarian, not custodian.

### Virgil

- **Connection:** makes the lituus part of the insignia of King Picus.
- **Date:** *Aeneid*, late first century BCE.
- **Status:** poetic reception of the staff’s royal and augural symbolism.

### Valerius Maximus

- **Connection:** repeats the fire-survival story in a collection of wonders.
- **Date:** early first century CE.
- **Status:** later literary transmitter.

### Roman augurs and the College of Augurs

- **Connection:** users of the object type; the lituus became their most immediately recognizable insignia.
- **Status:** documented institutional association through texts, coins, and art. Individual litui cannot usually be traced from one augur to another.

### Roman magistrates and emperors

- **Connection:** the lituus appeared on coins and portraits to advertise augural office, religious authority, foundation, and legitimate rule. Augustus’s provincial coinage used it extensively.
- **Status:** emblematic association; images are not representations of a single transferable staff unless separately identified.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Delimiting the templum

The central ritual function was not to draw a permanent line physically across the sky. The augur used the staff as a directing instrument while verbally and conceptually establishing a **templum**, a bounded field within which signs would be observed and interpreted.

Livy 1.18 provides the classic procedure:

1. the augur seats himself with an oriented view;
2. he holds the lituus in his right hand;
3. he surveys city and countryside;
4. he invokes the gods;
5. he defines celestial regions;
6. he transfers or correlates that ordering with the terrestrial place;
7. he specifies the requested sign;
8. the sign confirms or refuses the proposed political act.

The implement’s power was procedural and institutional. Sources do not describe it as a magic wand emitting force independently of the augur, prayer, place, or divine response.

### Foundation of cities

Cicero links Romulus’s lituus to the founding of Rome. Later iconography, especially under Augustus, could exploit this association: the emperor as augur and founder or refounder of communities. The lituus therefore signified not only prediction but the establishment of legitimate relations among gods, territory, and political authority.

### Inauguration

Livy’s Numa episode places it in a royal inauguration. In historical Rome, augural validation was important to magistracies, assemblies, elections, public business, and consecrated space. The lituus represented the specialist competence that interpreted divine signs; possession alone did not automatically confer office.

### Insignia rather than continuously unique regalia

Ancient images show the lituus as a repeatable badge. Unlike a single crown or coronation stone, multiple augurs could possess or be represented with litui. Its political force lay in recognition of the type. The supposed staff of Romulus was exceptional because Roman memory transformed one member of that type into a founder relic.

### Numismatic career

The lituus became a concise symbol on Roman coins:

- it identifies an augural office or ancestral claim;
- it appears with other priestly implements, including sacrificial vessels;
- on Augustan provincial issues it could connect Augustus’s augurate with foundation, refoundation, or civic ordering;
- its reproduction did not claim that the literal relic was traveling among mints.

This distinction matters: an image of a lituus is an emblematic reference, not evidence for custody of Romulus’s staff.

### Funerary and elite use

Etruscan scenes place curved staffs with elite individuals, attendants, processions, contests, or birds. Scholars variously interpret them as priestly, augural, royal, magistratical, pastoral, or status attributes. Calling every depicted crook “an augur’s lituus” risks importing later Roman categories into earlier Etruscan imagery.

### Access and handling

No ancient source gives explicit access rules for Romulus’s relic. Its reported location in a Saliar sacred repository implies controlled institutional custody, but it is unknown:

- whether the public could see it;
- whether only Salii handled it;
- whether it was used after deposit;
- whether it was carried in processions;
- whether it was wrapped, boxed, or displayed;
- whether it was periodically renewed.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The founder’s staff

**Story:** Romulus, himself an augur, used the staff to divide the heavens into regions when founding Rome.

- **Earliest surviving explicit attestation:** Cicero, 45–44 BCE.
- **Type:** late Republican foundation tradition.
- **Historical core:** archaic Rome demonstrably attached political importance to auspices, and curved staffs were ancient Italian insignia. Romulus’s personal possession cannot be independently established.
- **Function of the story:** it projected late Republican augural authority back to the city’s founder and represented Rome itself as founded through valid communication with the gods.

### Miraculous survival in the fire

**Story:** the Palatine Saliar building burned completely; amid the ruins Romulus’s staff alone was found intact.

- **Earliest surviving attestation:** Cicero, *De divinatione* 1.30.
- **Later version:** Valerius Maximus 1.8.11 places it among *miracula*.
- **Variant terminology:** *curia Saliorum* in Cicero; *sacrarium Saliorum* in Valerius Maximus.
- **Traditional or scholarly dating:** often linked to the Gallic sack, conventionally 390 or 387/386 BCE; Cicero does not explicitly make that identification in the quoted sentence.
- **Evidence for factual core:** fires repeatedly damaged Rome; priestly collections existed on the Palatine.
- **Evidence against accepting the miracle literally:** no named witnesses or contemporary record; organic material’s claimed unique survival is extraordinary; Cicero’s skeptical speaker explicitly rejects the tale.
- **Political and religious function:** the survival dramatized Rome’s continuity through catastrophe. A founder’s instrument, preserved when its building perished, could stand for the durability of the city’s covenant with the gods.

### “Etruscan origin”

Roman augural practice was repeatedly associated with Etruscan religious expertise, and the lituus is often called Etruscan. Material parallels support close central-Italian and Etruscan connections. Nevertheless, a fully documented transmission—named Etruscan maker, date, recipient, and Roman adoption—is absent.

The strongest defensible formulation is:

- curved staffs were present in Etruscan elite and ritual imagery before the surviving Roman literary descriptions;
- Roman writers and later scholarship connected important divinatory institutions with Etruria;
- the precise origin of the word and the exact route by which the Roman implement and procedure developed remain disputed.

### Shepherd’s crook origin

The staff’s simple curve invited explanation as a stylized shepherd’s crook. Romulus’s identity as a pastoral founder made that association attractive. Curved staffs also served practical pastoral and hunting purposes, but formal ritual litui could become too short or tightly curled for practical use. The shepherd-crook explanation is a plausible morphological genealogy, not a documented manufacture narrative.

### Ancestry of the Christian crosier

Antiquarian and popular accounts sometimes present the episcopal crosier as a direct continuation of the augural lituus. The two share a curved pastoral silhouette, and Christian authors and artists knew Roman forms. But visual resemblance does not establish an unbroken chain of custody, manufacture, ritual meaning, or institutional descent. The crosier developed within Christian pastoral symbolism and liturgical history; “the lituus became the bishop’s crosier” is too categorical without a much more specific transmission history.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Romulus’s claimed lituus

- **Present location:** unknown.
- **Condition:** unknown.
- **Last secure form of evidence:** ancient literary report, not a modern sighting.
- **Museum accession:** none identified.
- **Published excavation context:** none.
- **Photograph, drawing from direct observation, or scientific record:** none.
- **Legal owner:** none identifiable.
- **Custodial institution:** the ancient Salii are the last claimed custodians.
- **Public access:** not applicable.
- **Repatriation or ownership dispute:** none current, because no recognized candidate object exists.

The responsible present-tense description is therefore: **lost or never materially identifiable; surviving only as a textual relic tradition and through representations of its object class**.

### Archaeological stand-ins

Museums hold Etruscan and Roman objects that illuminate the type:

- relief-decorated ceramics depicting persons with curved staffs;
- bronze figurines carrying crooks;
- tomb paintings and sarcophagi showing staff-bearing figures;
- Roman coins bearing the lituus emblem;
- stone reliefs of augurs, magistrates, or emperors.

These objects should be catalogued under their own accession histories. They are evidence for the lituus form and symbol, not fragments, copies with an ancient pedigree, or rival claimants to Romulus’s relic.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Antiquarian reconstruction

Early modern and nineteenth-century antiquarians reconstructed the lituus from Cicero, Livy, coins, reliefs, and Etruscan monuments. Reference works commonly illustrated a curved wand and repeated the Etruscan-origin theory. These reconstructions strongly shaped modern visual expectations even though the named relic had disappeared.

Diderot and d’Alembert’s *Encyclopédie* presented Romulus as instituting the lituus as the augurs’ badge and emphasized its constant appearance on medals. William Smith’s nineteenth-century *Dictionary of Greek and Roman Antiquities* consolidated the familiar definition from Cicero and Livy and reproduced comparative images.

### Museums and educational replicas

Modern museum labels, historical reenactment equipment, costume illustrations, and commercial replicas usually make the lituus a neat, polished crook. Such objects are reconstructions based on iconography. Their materials, dimensions, and finish should not be projected backward onto Romulus’s reported staff.

### Political and institutional symbolism

The ancient lituus remains useful in:

- reconstructions of Roman augural colleges;
- insignia and illustrations concerning Roman religion;
- scholarship on priesthood and imperial ideology;
- discussions of Augustus as augur and civic founder;
- modern visualizations of the foundation of Rome.

Its broad public image is now often entangled with the bishop’s crosier, the shepherd’s crook, and the musical lituus. These associations can obscure the ancient evidence.

### Literature, art, and popular media

Modern depictions of Romulus taking auspices frequently supply visual details absent from Cicero:

- a tall staff;
- polished hardwood;
- an elaborate spiral;
- metal or gilded ornament;
- a dramatic gesture toward circling birds.

These are artistic choices. No ancient text specifies such finish or decoration for the individual staff.

In games, historical fiction, and occult-oriented retellings, the lituus is sometimes treated as a divining wand endowed with intrinsic supernatural power. That is a modern simplification. Ancient ritual texts instead situate the staff within formal orientation, speech, observation, priestly authority, and divine signs.

### No demonstrated modern forgery of the named relic

No well-documented modern forger appears to have marketed an artifact accepted by a major institution as the authentic lituus of Romulus. Generic “Roman lituus” identifications can nevertheless be overconfident because metal crooks and terminals are difficult to distinguish from other fittings when archaeological context is poor.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Literary criticism

Cicero is both the indispensable source and the first surviving critic. Older antiquarian writing often quoted Book 1’s miracle without giving equal weight to Book 2’s rejection. Modern treatment must read the dialogue as a whole: Cicero staged Roman religious tradition and philosophical skepticism against one another.

A principal interpretive question is whether Book 1 preserves:

- a current Saliar display object;
- priestly archival lore;
- an antiquarian story circulating independently of a surviving object;
- or a conventional miracle tale.

The text does not resolve this.

### Topography of the Saliar repository

The **curia Saliorum** or **sacrarium Saliorum** is associated with the Palatine, but its precise location and architectural identity are disputed. Proposed locations near the southwest Palatine and identifications with buildings connected to Romulus depend upon later texts, fragmentary inscriptions, and topographical reconstruction.

Because the building itself is not securely identified, archaeology cannot presently match a destruction layer, storage room, or findspot to Cicero’s report.

### Date and identity of the fire

Modern writers often say the lituus survived the Gallic fire of 390 BCE. The association is plausible within Roman memory of the sack, but it is not explicit in Cicero’s sentence. Scholarship has also explored links with a calendrical notice associated with 23 March. The following distinctions should be maintained:

- **attested:** a Saliar building burned and the staff was said to have survived;
- **inferred:** the fire was the Gallic sack;
- **unresolved:** exact year, extent, find circumstances, and relationship to calendrical commemoration.

### Etruscan versus wider Near Eastern ancestry

Ambos and Krauskopf place the Etruscan lituus within a much older Near Eastern family of curved staffs. Their comparison includes:

- Near Eastern divine and royal staffs;
- Hittite *kalmus*;
- hunting and shepherding crooks;
- Italian spiral and simply curved staffs;
- differences between practical and ceremonial forms.

The argument supports cultural transmission or adaptation, but it does not reduce every curved staff to one unchanged object or meaning. Similar forms could be reinvented, borrowed, or reinterpreted.

### Identification in art

A central methodological dispute concerns when a curved object should be called a lituus. Contextual tests include:

- association with birds or augural action;
- priestly or elite costume;
- funerary procession;
- length and degree of curvature;
- the presence of a paired circular horn, suggesting that the object is the musical trumpet;
- whether the curve terminates in a bell opening;
- whether the implement is usable as a crook or throwing stick.

Older catalogues sometimes identified an “augur” wherever a curved staff appeared. Modern iconographic scholarship is generally more cautious.

### Material evidence and preservation bias

Most literary descriptions imply wood, but most recoverable candidates are bronze or appear only in stone, terracotta, ceramic, or coin images. The evidentiary record therefore exaggerates durable artistic representations and understates ordinary wooden implements. It is unsafe to infer that Roman litui were normally bronze simply because bronze examples or fittings survive better.

### Major limitations of the archive

No surviving evidence supplies:

- a Saliar inventory naming the staff;
- a Republican dedication;
- an excavation record;
- a transfer document;
- a restoration account;
- a measurement;
- a wood analysis;
- a drawing demonstrably made from direct inspection;
- a late antique notice of destruction or removal.

The dossier is consequently fullest in ritual meaning and reception, weakest in physical biography.

## 12. Sources

### Primary ancient texts

**Cicero, *De divinatione* 1.30 and 2.80.** The indispensable source for the alleged individual relic. Book 1 gives its form, Romulan use, Palatine repository, and survival; Book 2 supplies contemporary skepticism. Because the work is a dialogue rather than an inventory, statements assigned to its speakers must not automatically be treated as Cicero’s unqualified factual endorsement.

**Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 1.18.** Principal ancient description of the lituus as a knotless curved staff and of its procedural use in the inauguration of Numa.

**Virgil, *Aeneid* 7.187–188.** Associates the lituus with Picus and archaic royal-sacral authority.

**Valerius Maximus, *Facta et dicta memorabilia* 1.8.11.** Later report classifying the survival of Romulus’s staff among marvels.

**Servius, Commentary on *Aeneid* 7.187.** Late antique gloss defining the lituus as the curved staff of augurs.

**Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities*.** Important comparative evidence for Roman foundation, augural, regal, and priestly traditions, although not the clearest source for the custody of Romulus’s particular staff.

### Archaeological and iconographic scholarship

Claus Ambos and Ingrid Krauskopf, “The Curved Staff in the Ancient Near East as a Predecessor of the Etruscan Lituus,” provides the most useful comparative analysis of Near Eastern, Anatolian, Etruscan, and Roman curved staffs and of the danger of confusing ceremonial staffs with trumpets and practical crooks.

Nancy Thomson de Grummond’s work on Etruscan religion treats the curved staff as an attribute of Etruscan priests while emphasizing the importance of archaeological context.

The *Dictionnaire des antiquités grecques et romaines* and William Smith’s *Dictionary of Greek and Roman Antiquities* remain valuable historiographic compilations of texts, coins, reliefs, and antiquarian identifications. Their conclusions, particularly linguistic ones, must be checked against newer scholarship.

Modern work on Augustan provincial coinage shows how the lituus moved from a handled ritual implement into a widely reproducible sign of augurate, foundation, and political legitimacy.

### Final assessment

The history of “the augur’s lituus” divides at a visible seam. On one side stands a materially well-attested class of ancient curved staffs and images, rooted in a broad Mediterranean and Near Eastern language of ritual and authority. On the other stands an individual Roman founder relic whose biography is known only from sources written many centuries after its supposed creation.

The individual object’s reconstructable life is therefore:

- traditionally used by Romulus;
- transferred by an undocumented mechanism to the Palatine Salii;
- said to have survived a fire intact;
- invoked as proof of divine favor;
- challenged as an invented tale by Cicero’s skeptical speaker;
- subsequently repeated by Valerius Maximus;
- then lost from the record.

No evidence establishes its maker, material beyond the probable implication of wood, exact dimensions, continuous custody, survival into late antiquity, or identification with any present artifact. The legend is ancient and historically consequential; the relic’s authenticity and eventual fate remain unresolved.
