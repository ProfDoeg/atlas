# The Pelian ash spear (Chiron's gift): Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary finding

The Pelian ash spear is primarily a **mythic object preserved through literary tradition**, not an extant archaeological artifact. Its earliest surviving literary identity is the exceptional paternal spear wielded by Achilles in the *Iliad*: an enormous ash-wood weapon cut from Mount Pelion and given by Chiron to Peleus. Later traditions expanded its biography. The lost *Cypria* reportedly made Athena the shaft’s finisher and Hephaestus the maker or fitter of its metal head; the Telephus tradition gave the weapon the paradoxical power to heal the wound it had caused; the *Little Iliad* apparently described gold fittings and a forked point; and Quintus of Smyrna transferred it after Achilles’ death to Neoptolemus.

There was also a **physical claimant**. In the second century CE Pausanias reported that “the spear of Achilles” was dedicated in Athena’s sanctuary at Phaselis in Lycia. He specifically said that its point and butt-spike were bronze and presented it as material evidence for Bronze Age weaponry. Nothing establishes when this relic appeared, how it supposedly reached Phaselis, or whether its shaft survived. No securely identified part of it is now known.

Accordingly, three entities must remain distinct:

1. **The Iliadic spear**, an object within heroic narrative.
2. **The expanded literary spear**, accumulated from cyclic epic, tragedy, mythography, Roman poetry, and late antique epic.
3. **The Phaselis relic**, a real weapon or weapon parts displayed under Achilles’ name by the second century CE, without a documented chain linking it to any Bronze Age object or even to the literary tradition’s imagined weapon.

No archaeological, metallurgical, radiocarbon, dendrological, or provenance study has authenticated a surviving Pelian spear.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Object class and principal names

**Class:** heroic spear; heirloom weapon; divine or quasi-divine wedding gift; healing instrument in later tradition; eventually a sanctuary relic under an ancient authenticity claim.

The most precise early Greek designation is:

> **Πηλιάδα μελίην** (*Pēliada melíēn*)  
> “the Pelian ash [spear]” or “the ash spear from Pelion.”

Other forms include:

- **Πηλιάς μελίη** (*Pēliàs melíē*), “Pelian ash spear.”
- **ἔγχος πατρώϊον** (*enkhos patrōion*), “paternal/ancestral spear,” *Iliad* 19.387.
- **δόρυ μείλινον** (*dory meilinnon*), “ash-wood spear,” in Pseudo-Apollodorus’ account of Peleus’ wedding.
- **Chironis hasta**, “Chiron’s spear,” in Hyginus.
- **Pelias hasta**, **Peliaca hasta**, or **Peliaea cuspis**, Latin poetic expressions for “Pelian spear” or “Pelian point.”
- Modern English: **Pelian spear**, **Pelian ash spear**, **spear of Peleus**, **spear of Achilles**, and **Chiron’s spear**.

The English phrase “Chiron’s gift” is descriptively correct, but it is not an independently attested ancient proper name.

### 1.2 Etymology

**Πηλιάς** means “of or from Pelion,” the Thessalian mountain associated with Chiron. The epithet therefore describes geographical material origin, not ownership by Peleus; similarity between *Pēliás* (“Pelian”) and *Pēleus* is suggestive in poetry but etymologically distinct.

**μελίη**, the epic-Ionic form corresponding to Attic **μελία**, means an ash tree and, by metonymy, an ash-wood spear. Lexicographical tradition commonly identifies the tree as ash, sometimes specifically manna ash (*Fraxinus ornus*). The ancient word itself does not establish a modern botanical specimen identification for the legendary shaft.

### 1.3 Homeric description

The fullest early description occurs twice, with small verbal variation, at *Iliad* 16.140–144 and 19.387–391. At 16.140–144 Patroclus arms himself in Achilles’ equipment but leaves this spear behind:

> ἔγχος δ’ οὐχ ἕλετ’ οἶον ἀμύμονος Αἰακίδαο,  
> βριθὺ μέγα στιβαρόν· τὸ μὲν οὐ δύνατ’ ἄλλος Ἀχαιῶν  
> πάλλειν, ἀλλά μιν οἶος ἐπίστατο πῆλαι Ἀχιλλεύς,  
> Πηλιάδα μελίην, τὴν πατρὶ φίλῳ πόρε Χείρων  
> Πηλίου ἐκ κορυφῆς, φόνον ἔμμεναι ἡρώεσσιν.

A close translation is:

> He did not take the spear of the blameless son of Aeacus, heavy, great, and strong. No other Achaean could wield it; Achilles alone knew how. It was the Pelian ash that Chiron gave his dear father from Pelion’s summit, to be death for warriors.

The weapon is therefore:

- an **ash-wood shaft**;
- sourced from the **summit or heights of Pelion**;
- **heavy, great, and strong**;
- exceptionally difficult to brandish;
- kept in a **spear rack or spear case** when Achilles takes it up in *Iliad* 19;
- fitted with a visibly gleaming **bronze point**;
- capable of both throwing and thrusting;
- explicitly inherited from Achilles’ father.

No Homeric passage supplies its exact length, weight, shaft diameter, head shape, decorations, maker’s mark, or inscription. “Only Achilles could wield it” is a heroic exclusivity formula, not a measurable weight statement.

### 1.4 Later physical additions

The lost *Little Iliad*, as quoted in scholia, added:

> ἀμφὶ δὲ πόρκης  
> χρύσεος ἀστράπτει, καὶ ἐπ’ αὐτῷ δίκροος αἰχμή.

> Around it flashes a golden collar, and upon it is a forked point.

The **πόρκης** was probably a collar or ring associated with the spearhead’s attachment to the shaft. The passage supplies two details absent from Homer:

- a flashing **gold collar**;
- a **δίκροος αἰχμή**, a forked or double-pointed spearhead.

These details cannot safely be retrojected into the *Iliad*. The scholia say that Aeschylus and Sophocles also knew the double-pointed version, but the relevant works are fragmentary. Martin L. West notes that the fragment’s original narrative setting is conjectural and that the transmitted present tense may itself be problematic.

### 1.5 The Phaselis claimant

Pausanias, writing in the second century CE, reported a spear identified as Achilles’ in Athena’s sanctuary at Phaselis:

> “The point and butt-spike of the spear … are made of bronze. The truth of these statements I can vouch for.”

His wording establishes a displayed object with:

- a bronze **spearhead**;
- a bronze **butt-spike** or **sauroter**;
- an attribution to Achilles;
- sanctuary custody at Phaselis.

Pausanias does not give dimensions, weight, decoration, inscriptions, the condition of the wooden shaft, or any provenance for the dedication. His “vouching” concerns what he presents as observable material composition, not a demonstrated chain back to Achilles.

### 1.6 What does not survive

No presently catalogued museum object is accepted as:

- the Homeric Pelian spear;
- the spear seen by Pausanias;
- a scientifically authenticated fragment of either.

No ancient inscription identifying a surviving weapon as Achilles’ spear has been published. Modern illustrations, replicas, game models, and commercial reproductions are imaginative reconstructions.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Earliest securely recoverable textual form

The earliest attestation is the *Iliad*, conventionally placed in the later eighth or seventh century BCE, while recognizing that the poem emerged from a long oral tradition and that its exact date and process of textual stabilization remain debated.

The *Iliad* says only that:

1. the spear is an ash from Pelion;
2. Chiron gave it to Peleus;
3. it became Achilles’ paternal weapon;
4. only Achilles among the Achaeans could wield it.

It does **not** say:

- that the gift occurred at Peleus and Thetis’ wedding;
- that Athena polished it;
- that Hephaestus forged its head;
- that the spear was magically invincible;
- that it could heal;
- that Peleus formally bequeathed it;
- that Chiron gave it directly to Achilles.

The oldest complete surviving manuscript of the *Iliad* is the late tenth-century CE Venetus A, Marcianus Graecus Z. 454 (= 822), a vellum codex of 327 leaves. This late manuscript date does not make the poem medieval: the *Iliad* is quoted, performed, criticized, and represented throughout Classical and Hellenistic antiquity, and earlier papyri preserve portions of the text. It does, however, matter when distinguishing composition date from the date of a particular surviving witness.

### 2.2 The expanded manufacture tradition

A scholion on *Iliad* 16.140 attributes the following account to the lost *Cypria*:

> At the marriage of Peleus and Thetis, the gods assembled on Pelion for a feast and brought gifts to Peleus. Chiron cut a fine or stout ash and gave it for a spear; Athena polished it, and Hephaestus fitted it with a head.

This creates a distributed manufacture:

- **Pelion:** source of the tree.
- **Chiron:** selects and cuts the ash, and gives it to Peleus.
- **Athena:** finishes or polishes the shaft.
- **Hephaestus:** makes or attaches the metal head.
- **Peleus and Thetis’ wedding:** transfer occasion.

The *Cypria* was probably composed in the seventh or early sixth century BCE, though its authorship and dating are disputed. The relevant poem is lost; its spear-making account survives only through much later scholia. Consequently, “made by Chiron, Athena, and Hephaestus” is an ancient expansion, not a direct statement in a surviving archaic manuscript.

Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Library* 3.13.5, gives a shorter version: the gods celebrate the marriage on Pelion; Chiron gives Peleus an ash spear; Poseidon gives the horses Balius and Xanthus. Here Athena and Hephaestus are absent from the spear’s construction.

### 2.3 Wedding imagery and its limits

The wedding of Peleus and Thetis appears on major sixth-century BCE Attic vessels:

- the Sophilos dinos, about 580–570 BCE, British Museum 1971,1101.1;
- the François Vase, about 570 BCE, Archaeological Museum of Florence.

Both represent Chiron among the divine wedding procession. On the Sophilos dinos, Chiron appears as a hunter with quarry and a branch or pole. Neither vessel securely illustrates Athena polishing the Pelian spear or Hephaestus attaching its head. They are early evidence for the wedding tradition and Chiron’s presence, not certain portraits of the weapon’s manufacture.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because no continuous physical provenance exists, the chain must be divided into **narrative custody** and **documented relic custody**.

### 3.1 Narrative custody

#### A. Mount Pelion — Chiron

**Claim:** Chiron obtains or cuts an ash from Pelion.

**Evidence:** *Iliad* 16.143–144 and 19.390–391 establish Pelion and Chiron. The *Cypria* scholion explicitly says Chiron cut the ash.

**Tier:** early epic tradition; fuller manufacture is fragmentarily transmitted cyclic tradition.

#### B. Chiron to Peleus

**Mechanism:** gift.

**Occasion in Homer:** unspecified.

**Occasion in later tradition:** marriage of Peleus and Thetis on Pelion.

The *Iliad* calls it Achilles’ “father’s spear” and says Chiron gave it to Achilles’ father. Pseudo-Apollodorus and the *Cypria* place the gift amid the divine wedding gifts.

#### C. Peleus’ possession and use

The surviving archaic evidence does not narrate a specific exploit in which Peleus certainly uses the same spear. Later epic can assimilate Peleus’ exceptional lance to it. Valerius Flaccus’ *Argonautica* 1.402–404 describes Peleus aboard the *Argo* with a spear towering over other weapons as the relevant tree had towered over Pelian ashes. This is evocative identification, but it is Roman imperial epic rather than an independent custody document.

#### D. Peleus to Achilles

**Mechanism:** inheritance, paternal transfer, or outfitting; never narrated in detail by Homer.

The *Iliad* repeatedly calls the spear paternal. At 19.387 Achilles draws it from its rack after receiving his new divine armor. This establishes possession by Achilles but not the time or ceremony of transfer.

The scholion on *Iliad* 16.142 reports a non-Homeric explanation: Peleus learned how to use the spear from Chiron, then taught Achilles, while Achilles taught no one else. The scholiast dismisses this as fabrication. It may have developed to explain why Achilles alone could wield the weapon.

#### E. Phthia and Scyros

The spear’s presence in Phthia before the expedition is implied by its paternal origin. Some later versions of the discovery of Achilles on Scyros have the disguised youth reveal himself by instinctively taking up weapons displayed by Odysseus, but the displayed spear in that scene is not securely identified as the Pelian weapon. Claims that Chiron or Peleus stored the Pelian spear on Scyros for Achilles are later harmonizations.

#### F. Mysia/Teuthrania

In cyclic and mythographic tradition, Achilles wounds Telephus during the Achaeans’ mistaken landing in Mysia.

Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Epitome* 3.17–20, says:

1. Telephus is caught in a vine during the battle.
2. Achilles wounds him in the thigh with a spear.
3. The wound does not heal.
4. Apollo declares that the wounder must become the healer.
5. Telephus comes to Argos and asks Achilles’ aid.
6. Achilles heals him with rust scraped from the Pelian spear.
7. Telephus then guides the Greeks toward Troy.

Hyginus, *Fabula* 101, explicitly calls the weapon **Chironis hasta**, “Chiron’s spear.” In his version Odysseus interprets Apollo’s ambiguous oracle: it is not Achilles personally but the spear—the effective author of the wound—that must heal it. Scrapings from the spear are applied.

**Physical effect:** metal is scraped from the weapon, probably its corroded head. The tradition implies slight deliberate abrasion, not dismemberment or destruction.

**Tier:** non-Homeric mythic tradition. The *Cypria* included Telephus’ wounding and healing, but Proclus’ surviving summary does not specify rust; that detail is securely explicit in later mythography.

#### G. Argos/Aulis

Telephus’ healing is variously situated at Argos or within the renewed Achaean mobilization. The spear is temporarily used as a medical instrument. Custody does not pass to Telephus; Achilles remains its bearer.

#### H. The Troad and Achaean camp

At *Iliad* 16.140–144, the spear remains in Achilles’ quarters when Patroclus borrows Achilles’ armor, shield, helmet, sword, horses, and chariot. Patroclus takes two different spears “suited to his hands.”

This is a key identity test inside the narrative: Patroclus can imitate Achilles’ visible appearance but cannot take the singular paternal spear. The weapon therefore remains with Achilles and survives Patroclus’ defeat, Apollo’s disarming of him, and Hector’s seizure of Achilles’ first armor.

#### I. Achilles’ return to battle

At *Iliad* 19.387–391 Achilles draws the weapon from its rack and carries it with the new armor made by Hephaestus. Unlike the armor, the spear is not newly manufactured. It is the surviving element of his earlier equipment and his paternal inheritance.

#### J. Combat with Aeneas

At *Iliad* 20.273–291 Achilles throws the Pelian ash at Aeneas. It penetrates Aeneas’ shield through the bronze and hide layers and sticks in the ground beyond him. Poseidon rescues Aeneas and casts Achilles’ spear back down before Achilles’ feet.

The weapon:

- is thrown;
- penetrates a major shield;
- misses Aeneas;
- becomes embedded in the earth;
- is recovered through divine intervention.

#### K. Achilles’ slaughter beside the Scamander

After the Aeneas episode Achilles kills numerous Trojans with spear and sword. Not every occurrence is re-identified by epithet, but narrative continuity makes the paternal spear the weapon he is carrying unless another is specified.

#### L. Combat with Asteropaeus

At *Iliad* 21.161–199 Achilles throws the Pelian spear at the ambidextrous Asteropaeus and misses. It drives halfway into the riverbank. Asteropaeus tries three times to pull it free; on a fourth attempt he tries to bend or break it. Achilles kills him with his sword before he succeeds.

This is the weapon’s clearest narrated attempted seizure and destruction. It survives intact. Since Achilles possesses it again in Book 22, its recovery from the bank is narratively implicit even though not ceremonially described.

#### M. Death of Hector

At *Iliad* 22.131–133 Achilles approaches Hector brandishing the Pelian ash. He first throws and misses when Hector crouches; Athena secretly retrieves and returns the spear. Hector then throws his own weapon and receives no replacement because Athena, disguised as Deiphobus, has vanished. Achilles finally drives his spear through the vulnerable point where Hector’s neck meets his shoulders, avoiding the windpipe so Hector can still speak.

The Pelian spear is therefore the instrument of Hector’s death. Achilles withdraws it from the body before stripping Hector’s armor.

#### N. Penthesilea and Memnon

The lost *Aethiopis* narrated Achilles’ later victories over Penthesilea and Memnon, but its surviving summary does not identify his weapon. Quintus of Smyrna’s *Posthomerica*, probably third or fourth century CE, explicitly makes the Chironian or Pelian spear active in these post-*Iliad* battles:

- Achilles kills Penthesilea with a spear identified as Chiron’s workmanship.
- He uses his spear against Memnon.
- The weapon is associated with Achilles immediately before his own death.

These are late epic elaborations of events once treated in archaic cyclic epic; they cannot automatically reconstruct the wording of the lost *Aethiopis*.

#### O. Achilles’ death and recovery of his arms

After Achilles is killed by Paris and Apollo in the epic tradition, Greeks recover his body and equipment. Quintus describes the great spear laid beside the armor, still figuratively reeking of Hector’s blood.

The famous judgment between Ajax and Odysseus is conventionally called the contest for Achilles’ “arms.” Homer’s *Odyssey* emphasizes the armor; later cyclic reconstruction includes the broader panoply. Odysseus wins the contest, Ajax kills himself, and Odysseus temporarily controls Achilles’ equipment.

#### P. Odysseus to Neoptolemus

Proclus’ summary of the lost *Little Iliad* says that Odysseus brings Neoptolemus from Scyros and gives him his father’s arms. A fragment of the *Little Iliad* describing the gold collar and forked spearhead is often placed in this episode, but its exact original setting is uncertain.

This is the best-supported post-Achilles transfer:

- **From:** Odysseus, as winner or custodian of Achilles’ arms.
- **To:** Neoptolemus, Achilles’ son.
- **Mechanism:** return of paternal equipment.
- **Location:** Achaean camp before Troy.
- **Tier:** lost archaic epic reconstructed from Proclus and scholia.

#### Q. Neoptolemus’ use

Quintus’ *Posthomerica* 7–8 depicts Neoptolemus effortlessly carrying the massive Pelian shaft. In Book 8 he kills Eurypylus, son of Telephus, by driving the Pelian lance through his throat. Neoptolemus then identifies it as his father’s great spear.

The episode closes a deliberate generational circuit: the spear that wounded and healed Telephus kills Telephus’ son when carried by Achilles’ son.

#### R. After Troy

No dependable ancient narrative traces the Pelian spear from Neoptolemus’ departure from Troy to his kingdom in Epirus, his death at Delphi, or his descendants. Later accounts of Neoptolemus do not furnish an agreed final deposition for it.

The mythic chain therefore breaks after the Trojan sack or Neoptolemus’ return. Any direct transfer from Neoptolemus to Phaselis is unattested.

### 3.2 Documented relic custody

#### A. Unknown appearance at Phaselis

At some unknown date, a weapon identified as Achilles’ spear was dedicated or displayed in Athena’s sanctuary at Phaselis. Pausanias uses the perfect/passive sense “dedicated,” but gives no donor, date, dedicatory inscription, or origin story.

Possible contexts sometimes proposed include:

- a locally developed heroic relic cult;
- a claim reinforcing Phaselis’ association with Athena;
- an old bronze spear reidentified as heroic;
- a manufactured antiquity created for sanctuary prestige.

None can presently be demonstrated.

#### B. Pausanias’ observation, second century CE

Pausanias is the sole clear ancient eyewitness testimony presently available. He cites the spear while arguing that heroic-age weapons were bronze. His report proves that a recognized sanctuary object was shown as Achilles’ spear; it does not prove heroic-age identity.

#### C. Disappearance

There is no continuous record after Pausanias. Phaselis declined in late antiquity and the medieval period, and the relevant Athena sanctuary has not yielded an identifiable spear. The relic may have been:

- removed;
- looted;
- melted down;
- destroyed with the sanctuary;
- buried;
- dispersed into unidentified fragments;
- or lost from the record while surviving elsewhere.

No choice among these possibilities is presently evidenced.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Did the object physically exist?

The literary spear cannot be authenticated as a historical Bronze Age weapon. The Trojan War traditions may contain distant memories of Late Bronze Age warfare, but a named weapon’s survival through oral poetry is not a demonstrable artifact provenance.

The proper conclusion is:

- **Existence as a literary object:** certain.
- **Existence of some historical spear behind the tradition:** possible but unprovable.
- **Survival of that spear into historical antiquity:** unsupported.
- **Existence of a physical Phaselis claimant in Pausanias’ time:** strongly attested by his eyewitness report.
- **Identity of the Phaselis claimant with the mythic spear:** unverified and chronologically extraordinary.

### 4.2 Internal literary identity

The *Iliad* consistently distinguishes Achilles’ exceptional paternal spear from ordinary spears. Its repeated origin formula at Books 16 and 19, and the epithet “Pelian ash” in Books 20–22, support continuity within that poem.

Later literature alters the specification:

| Feature | Homeric *Iliad* | Later tradition |
|---|---|---|
| Shaft | Ash from Pelion | Chiron personally cuts it |
| Transfer | Chiron gives it to Peleus | Wedding gift |
| Shaft finishing | Not stated | Athena polishes it |
| Head manufacture | Bronze implied; maker unstated | Hephaestus fits or makes it |
| Head shape | Not stated | Forked/double point |
| Fittings | Not stated | Golden collar |
| Exclusive use | Achilles alone among Achaeans can wield it | Explained as secret technique |
| Supernatural power | Exceptional strength and penetration | Same weapon wounds and heals |
| Later heir | Not covered | Neoptolemus |

These are variants and additions, not one uniform ancient description.

### 4.3 The Phaselis claimant’s problems

No source supplies the necessary links:

- Who recovered the spear after Neoptolemus?
- Who transported it to Lycia?
- When was it dedicated?
- Why at Phaselis?
- Was its wooden shaft ancient?
- Did its form match the double-pointed tradition?
- Was there an inscription?
- Did sanctuary officials distinguish tradition from material assessment?

Pausanias does not claim scientific authentication. His test is typological: the fittings are bronze, consistent with his understanding of heroic-age arms. Bronze composition alone cannot identify a weapon or establish a second-millennium BCE date.

### 4.4 Scientific examination

No published modern analysis is known for the lost Phaselis weapon:

- no radiocarbon date for a shaft;
- no metallography;
- no lead-isotope study;
- no alloy analysis;
- no dendrological identification;
- no ancient DNA or residue study;
- no securely excavated context;
- no museum accession number.

The claim has therefore never passed a modern authenticity test because no securely identified specimen is available to test.

### 4.5 Rival claimants and forgeries

No major modern museum, church, treasury, or archaeological collection currently maintains a recognized rival “true Pelian spear.” Commercial replicas are reproductions, not provenance claimants. Online assertions that particular generic Mycenaean spearheads are Achilles’ weapon lack documented chains and should be treated as modern fantasy or misidentification.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Chiron

**Connection:** source-cutter, maker in some traditions, and donor to Peleus.

**Date of attestation:** *Iliad*, later eighth/seventh century BCE in conventional dating.

**Evidence tier:** earliest epic tradition.

### Peleus

**Connection:** first mortal recipient and Achilles’ father; the spear is repeatedly called paternal.

**Transfer:** gift from Chiron; wedding gift in later versions.

**Evidence tier:** earliest epic for receipt; cyclic and mythographic elaboration for occasion.

### Thetis

**Connection:** the gift is attached in later tradition to her marriage with Peleus. She does not make the spear. Her replacement armor for Achilles in *Iliad* 18–19 must not be confused with the older paternal spear.

### Athena

**Connection:** polishes the shaft in the *Cypria* tradition; retrieves Achilles’ missed throw during the duel with Hector; patron of the Phaselis sanctuary holding the later claimant.

These are three different relations: manufacture, battlefield intervention, and cult custody.

### Hephaestus

**Connection:** fits or fashions the spearhead in the *Cypria* fragment tradition. In Homer he makes Achilles’ replacement armor, but Homer does not say that he made the Pelian spearhead.

### Achilles

**Connection:** principal bearer; exceptional wielder; wounds Telephus, kills Hector, and in later epic kills Penthesilea and Memnon.

**Evidence tier:** Homeric for possession and Hector; later cyclic/mythographic or late epic for other episodes.

### Patroclus

**Connection:** explicitly cannot or does not take the spear while borrowing Achilles’ other equipment.

**Importance:** his non-possession defines the spear’s exclusive identity.

### Automedon

**Connection:** Achilles’ charioteer; travels with Achilles and the spear but never holds title to it.

### Telephus

**Connection:** wounded by the spear and later healed through material scraped from it.

**Evidence tier:** ancient but non-Homeric tradition.

### Odysseus

**Connection:** interprets Apollo’s healing oracle in Hyginus; wins custody of Achilles’ arms after his death; later transfers the paternal arms to Neoptolemus.

### Ajax son of Telamon

**Connection:** rival claimant to Achilles’ arms. He is not specifically documented as possessing the spear.

### Neoptolemus

**Connection:** final major mythic bearer; receives his father’s equipment and uses the Pelian spear, most explicitly against Eurypylus.

### Aeneas

**Connection:** target of a cast that penetrates his shield; saved by Poseidon.

### Poseidon

**Connection:** rescues Aeneas and restores the displaced spear to the battlefield before Achilles.

### Asteropaeus

**Connection:** attempts to extract and then break the spear when it is embedded in the Scamander’s bank; fails.

### Hector

**Connection:** killed by a thrust of the spear through the neck.

### Penthesilea and Memnon

**Connection:** post-*Iliad* opponents of Achilles. Their association with the particular spear is clearest in Quintus, not in surviving archaic texts.

### Eurypylus son of Telephus

**Connection:** killed by Neoptolemus with the weapon that had earlier wounded and healed his father.

### Apollo

**Connection:** supplies the ambiguous healing oracle in the Telephus tradition. In traditions of Achilles’ death Apollo helps Paris kill Achilles, but is not said to take custody of the spear.

### Athena’s sanctuary at Phaselis

**Connection:** only historically documented keeper of a physical object claimed as Achilles’ spear.

**Date:** attested by Pausanias in the second century CE.

### Homeric performers, rhapsodes, and Alexandrian scholars

**Connection:** transmitters and interpreters, not custodians of the physical weapon. Ancient editors and scholiasts preserved variant explanations of why only Achilles could wield it.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Wedding gift

In the expanded cyclic tradition, the spear is a prestigious divine wedding gift. It integrates a mortal groom into a network of gods and heroic beings and anticipates the military destiny of his son.

This function is later than, or at least not stated by, Homer. In the *Iliad* the gift occasion remains unspecified.

### 6.2 Dynastic heirloom

The spear moves through the male Aeacid line:

> Chiron → Peleus → Achilles → Neoptolemus.

Its custody marks legitimate heroic succession more clearly than ordinary battlefield acquisition. Patroclus can wear Achilles’ armor but cannot wield the paternal spear. Neoptolemus, by contrast, can carry it because he is Achilles’ son and heroic successor.

### 6.3 Instrument of heroic singularity

The weapon functions as an index of Achilles’ unmatched bodily power. Its weight and exclusivity distinguish him from every other Achaean. The restriction is not a recorded cult taboo; it is a poetic statement about identity and capacity.

### 6.4 Wound and cure

The Telephus episode makes the spear a **pharmakon**, both harmful and remedial. The healing is not a general property demonstrated on multiple persons. It operates through an oracle requiring the wounder—or the instrument that wounded—to heal.

Later proverbial thought often condensed this narrative into the principle that the one who wounded will heal. Assertions that the spear could universally heal any injury are modern exaggerations.

### 6.5 Sanctuary relic

At Phaselis the claimed spear functioned as:

- a heroic relic;
- a prestigious possession of Athena’s sanctuary;
- evidence, for Pausanias, about early military technology;
- potentially a local link to the Panhellenic Trojan tradition.

No surviving source describes a procession, oath, healing ritual, festival display, touching rule, priestly access restriction, or miracle performed with it at Phaselis.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 Chiron’s cutting of the ash

**Early core:** Chiron gives Peleus an ash from Pelion.

**Later elaboration:** Chiron selects and cuts the tree at Peleus’ wedding.

**Function:** connects the civilized centaur, the mountain, the Aeacid dynasty, and heroic warfare.

### 7.2 Athena and Hephaestus complete the weapon

**Tier:** ancient cyclic elaboration preserved in later scholia.

Athena polishes the shaft; Hephaestus supplies or attaches its head. This makes the spear a composite divine artifact, although its organic core remains Chiron’s mountain ash.

Modern retellings often flatten the account into “Hephaestus forged Achilles’ spear.” That is inaccurate: even the fuller ancient version divides manufacture among Chiron, Athena, and Hephaestus, and the original mortal recipient is Peleus.

### 7.3 Only Achilles can wield it

**Earliest attestation:** *Iliad* 16.140–144 and 19.387–391.

The poem says no other Achaean could wield it. It does not say the spear rejects other hands by magic.

**Later explanatory legend:** Chiron taught Peleus the technique, Peleus taught Achilles, and Achilles taught nobody. A Homeric scholiast labels this explanation fabricated. Neoptolemus’ later ability complicates literal claims that no one except Achilles could ever use it.

### 7.4 The spear heals Telephus

**Early narrative layer:** the *Cypria* included Telephus’ wounding and subsequent healing.

**Fully explicit forms:** Pseudo-Apollodorus and Hyginus.

Variants include:

- the healer is grammatically “the one who wounded”;
- Odysseus solves the oracle by identifying the spear as the wound’s agent;
- rust or filings from the spear are applied;
- some artistic treatments show Achilles medically attending Telephus without making the exact substance visually certain.

The story was prominent enough for ancient visual art. An Attic cup associated with the Sosias Painter depicts Achilles tending Patroclus’ wound and has been interpreted against the broader epic tradition of Achilles as healer; later Etruscan and Roman images more directly associated with Telephus depict the cure.

The healing legend does not establish historical pharmacology. Copper alloys can have antimicrobial effects under some conditions, but applying corroded bronze to a deep wound would not authenticate or medically explain the myth.

### 7.5 The forked spearhead

**Tier:** fragmentary cyclic tradition.

The *Little Iliad* described a double or forked point. Later tragedians reportedly borrowed this feature. Homer supplies no such shape, and the Phaselis claimant’s reported head is described only as bronze.

### 7.6 The attempted breaking by Asteropaeus

**Tier:** Homeric.

Asteropaeus cannot pull the spear from the riverbank and tries to bend or break it. The episode demonstrates resistance through narrative action, not an explicit statement of magical indestructibility.

### 7.7 Athena returns the missed spear

**Tier:** Homeric.

During the Hector duel Athena silently restores Achilles’ missed cast. The episode makes the spear effectively available for the fatal attack but does not give it an independent returning enchantment. Modern descriptions of the Pelian spear as automatically returning like a magical projectile confuse Athena’s one-time intervention with an inherent power.

### 7.8 Survival at Phaselis

**Tier:** ancient institutional attribution.

The Phaselis display asserted physical survival. The claim affected the spear’s historical reception because Pausanias treated it as empirical evidence. Yet no earlier source connects the weapon with Phaselis and no later chain documents its fate.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Mythic object

**Status:** legendary; no authenticated surviving specimen.

### Phaselis claimant

**Last securely reported location:** sanctuary of Athena on the acropolis of Phaselis, Lycia, in Pausanias’ second-century CE account.

**Modern site:** Phaselis Archaeological Site near Kemer, Antalya Province, Türkiye.

**Present condition:** lost or unidentified.

The principal sanctuary of Athena has not been identified with complete archaeological certainty. Proposed acropolis structures do not contain a published, authenticated spear corresponding to Pausanias’ description.

**Accession number:** none.

**Controlling institution for the ancient site:** the archaeological site falls under Turkish cultural-heritage administration. This does not imply custody of the lost spear.

**Display status:** no known display.

**Scientific work on spear:** none possible on a securely identified object.

**Ownership or repatriation dispute:** no active, documented restitution proceeding specifically concerning the Pelian spear is known, because no accepted surviving object has been located.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Translation and illustration

Modern translations of the *Iliad* have made “Pelian ash” one of Achilles’ most recognizable material attributes. Translators variously render it as:

- Pelian ash;
- ash spear from Pelion;
- Pelian spear;
- father’s spear;
- great ashwood lance.

Illustrators typically supply features not specified by Homer: leaf-shaped Mycenaean heads, Classical spearheads, engraved gold collars, leather grips, tassels, or supernatural glow.

### 9.2 Scholarship and literary interpretation

Modern critics frequently read the spear as:

- the remaining paternal component of Achilles’ equipment after Thetis supplies new armor;
- the object that prevents Patroclus from becoming a complete duplicate of Achilles;
- a marker of Aeacid succession;
- a transformed tree whose organic past remains legible in the poem;
- a weapon binding gift, inheritance, killing, and healing.

The weapon’s arboreal material has become especially important in ecological and material-culture readings. The phrase “Pelian ash” continually reminds the audience that the instrument of killing was once a living mountain tree.

### 9.3 Film and television

Screen adaptations of the Trojan War usually give Achilles a spear but seldom preserve the full object biography. Wolfgang Petersen’s *Troy* (2004), for example, makes spear fighting central to Achilles’ visual identity but removes the gods, Chironian manufacture, and the weapon’s healing tradition. Such weapons are adaptations, not claimed replicas.

### 9.4 Games

Modern games commonly detach the spear from its inherited history and turn it into collectible equipment or a skill:

- *Assassin’s Creed Odyssey* includes “Achilles’s Spear” as legendary equipment, but its placement and acquisition are gameplay inventions.
- *A Total War Saga: Troy* and related *Total War: Pharaoh—Dynasties* material visually emphasize Achilles’ massive ash spear as part of his battlefield identity.
- Modern strategy-game treatments have used “Pelian Spear” as the name of an Achilles ability.
- *Hades* gives Achilles a prominent spear-bearing visual identity and includes an “Aspect of Achilles” for the Eternal Spear, but the game’s weapon Varatha is not presented as a historically surviving Pelian relic.

These adaptations often turn the Homeric statement of exceptional handling into explicit magical statistics, returning attacks, area damage, or ownership restrictions.

### 9.5 Replicas and commerce

Replica makers sell products under names such as “Achilles spear” and “Pelian spear,” generally based on modern ideas of Mycenaean or fantasy weapons. Unless accompanied by transparent archaeological typology, these are decorative reconstructions. No standard, academically accepted full-scale reconstruction exists because the ancient texts provide no measurements.

### 9.6 Modern misinformation

Common modern inventions or distortions include:

- “Chiron gave the spear directly to Achilles.” Homer says he gave it to Peleus.
- “Hephaestus forged the entire spear.” That is a later simplification of a divided manufacture tradition.
- “It could heal any wound.” The ancient story concerns the specific wound it caused.
- “It always returned when thrown.” Athena retrieves it once.
- “No one but Achilles could ever touch it.” Homer says no other Achaean could wield it; later Neoptolemus does.
- “It has been excavated at Phaselis.” No such authenticated find is published.
- “The spear on display in a modern collection is the original.” No accepted claimant is known.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 The Homeric text

The foundational evidence is *Iliad* 16.140–144, 19.387–391, 20.273–291, 21.161–199, and 22.131–133, 273–330. Modern editions depend on medieval manuscripts, papyri, quotations, ancient editorial work, and scholia. The Venetus A is especially important because it preserves both the complete poem and ancient critical material.

### 10.2 Composition and inherited formula

Scholars disagree over the chronological relationship between the *Iliad*, broader oral Trojan traditions, and the written cyclic epics. The spear’s formula may preserve a traditional heroic-object biography older than the monumental *Iliad*, but that cannot be dated independently. The *Cypria* need not be the source of Homer’s allusion; both may draw upon wider traditions.

### 10.3 Meaning of Achilles’ exclusive ability

Two principal readings recur:

1. **Physical reading:** the spear is extraordinarily heavy and Achilles uniquely strong.
2. **Technical or initiatory reading:** “knew how to wield” implies specialized knowledge passed from Chiron through Peleus.

The ancient scholion favors ability rather than a secret technique and calls the instructional genealogy fictitious. Modern interpretation often allows both physical and symbolic force without asserting literal magic.

### 10.4 Weapon and tree

Material-oriented scholarship emphasizes the shaft’s prior existence as a Pelian tree. The weapon belongs to a broader Homeric pattern in which warriors fall like felled trees and timber becomes ships, spears, and funeral fuel. On this reading, the spear’s material history intensifies the poem’s linkage of heroic glory to environmental destruction and mortality.

### 10.5 The *Little Iliad* fragment

Debate concerns:

- whether its present tense should be emended;
- whether the description occurred during the arms contest or when Odysseus gave the weapons to Neoptolemus;
- what precisely **πόρκης** denotes;
- whether **δίκροος** means a bifurcated head, two functional points, or some unusual fitting;
- whether later tragedians inherited the detail directly from the *Little Iliad*.

West regards the transfer setting as probable but not certain and rejects elaborate reconstructions in which Achilles’ ghost teaches Neoptolemus a secret double-point technique.

### 10.6 Telephus’ cure

The early iconographic and literary development of the Telephus episode is complicated. Proclus’ summary confirms wounding and healing in the *Cypria* but not the precise rust remedy. Euripides’ lost *Telephus* of 438 BCE strongly shaped later versions, particularly Telephus’ arrival in rags and seizure of the infant Orestes. Pseudo-Apollodorus and Hyginus preserve later prose syntheses.

It is methodologically unsafe to assign every Hyginian detail to the archaic *Cypria*.

### 10.7 The Phaselis relic

Pausanias’ report has been used in scholarship on:

- heroic relics;
- the use of material objects as historical evidence in antiquity;
- Athena Polias’ cult at Phaselis;
- local appropriation of Panhellenic myth;
- ancient concepts of authenticity.

Modern archaeological work supports Athena’s importance at Phaselis through literary, epigraphic, and numismatic evidence. It has not authenticated the spear or conclusively located the exact shrine installation in which it stood.

Pausanias’ reasoning is itself historiographically important: he treats sanctuary relics as technological evidence. Modern archaeology cannot accept identification by name and metal alone, but his report records an ancient practice of object authentication based on cult custody, traditional labeling, and apparent material age.

### 10.8 State of the primary evidence

**Survives substantially:**

- the *Iliad*;
- Pseudo-Apollodorus’ *Epitome*;
- Hyginus’ *Fabulae*, through a damaged and difficult manuscript tradition;
- Pausanias;
- Ovid;
- Quintus of Smyrna;
- relevant ancient vessels and reliefs.

**Lost or fragmentary:**

- the *Cypria*;
- the *Aethiopis*;
- the *Little Iliad*;
- Euripides’ *Telephus*;
- relevant plays by Aeschylus and Sophocles;
- the Phaselis spear itself;
- any sanctuary inventory that might have documented its dedication.

The losses prevent a continuous reconstruction and make later summaries disproportionately influential.

## 12. Sources

### Primary literary sources

- **Homer, *Iliad* 16.140–144; 19.387–391; 20.273–291; 21.161–199; 22.131–330.** Earliest recoverable description, Achilles’ custody, attempted seizure, divine retrieval, and Hector’s death.
- **The *Cypria*, fragment preserved by the scholion on *Iliad* 16.140.** Wedding context and distributed manufacture by Chiron, Athena, and Hephaestus.
- **The *Little Iliad*, fragment 5 West.** Golden collar and forked head; probable but disputed association with the transfer to Neoptolemus.
- **Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Library* 3.13.5 and *Epitome* 3.17–20.** Wedding gift; Telephus wounded and healed with rust from the Pelian spear.
- **Hyginus, *Fabula* 101.** Chiron’s spear, Apollo’s oracle, Odysseus’ interpretation, and spear scrapings.
- **Pausanias, *Description of Greece* 3.3.8.** Eyewitness report of the Phaselis relic and its bronze point and butt-spike.
- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 12.64–145.** Roman poetic treatment of Achilles’ Pelian spear in the battle with Cycnus.
- **Valerius Flaccus, *Argonautica* 1.402–404.** Peleus’ outstanding spear aboard the *Argo*.
- **Quintus of Smyrna, *Posthomerica* 1–8.** Late antique continuation through Penthesilea, Memnon, Achilles’ death, recovery of the arms, transfer to Neoptolemus, and death of Eurypylus.

### Material and iconographic sources

- **Sophilos dinos**, British Museum 1971,1101.1, approximately 580–570 BCE. Wedding procession with Chiron; not a secure depiction of the completed spear.
- **François Vase**, approximately 570 BCE. Wedding of Peleus and Thetis with Chiron at the head of the procession.
- **Sosias Painter cup**, Berlin Antikensammlung. Achilles tending Patroclus’ wound; important to Achilles’ healing iconography but not a certain representation of Telephus or the Pelian spear’s rust.
- Later Etruscan and Roman representations associated with Telephus’ healing provide evidence for reception of the cure tradition, not for the spear’s historical existence.

### Principal modern scholarship and research resources

- Martin L. West, *Greek Epic Fragments* and *The Epic Cycle: A Commentary on the Lost Troy Epics*. Critical treatment of the cyclic fragments, their transmission, and the uncertain setting of the *Little Iliad* spear description.
- Homer Multitext Project. Manuscript documentation and digital edition of Venetus A.
- Center for Hellenic Studies research on Trojan-cycle vase painting and the Sosias cup.
- Phaselis archaeological and epigraphic research on Athena Polias and the proposed sanctuary on the acropolis.
- Modern material-literary studies of the spear as inherited object, exclusive heroic attribute, and transformed tree.

## Evidentiary conclusion

The fullest responsible reconstruction produces not one uninterrupted material object but a succession of related identities.

The earliest spear is an epic heirloom: Pelian ash, Chiron’s gift to Peleus, and the one part of Achilles’ inherited equipment that Patroclus cannot assume. In the *Iliad* it survives failed casts, penetration of Aeneas’ shield, embedding in the Scamander’s bank, Asteropaeus’ attempted destruction, and Athena’s retrieval before killing Hector. Cyclic tradition moves backward to elaborate manufacture and forward to give the spear gold fittings, a forked head, and transmission to Neoptolemus. The Telephus myth transforms removed corrosion into medicine. Late epic makes the weapon an explicitly dynastic instrument in the hands of Achilles’ son.

Separately, historical Phaselis displayed a bronze-fitted spear as the authentic weapon. Pausanias saw that claimant, but his testimony begins too late and supplies no provenance linking it to Neoptolemus, Troy, Peleus, or a Bronze Age workshop. Its disappearance prevents modern testing. Thus the Pelian spear is securely real as a continuously transmitted literary object, historically real as the identity assigned to a now-lost sanctuary relic, and unverified as a surviving weapon of any historical Achilles.
