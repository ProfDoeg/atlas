# The Book of Thoth (ancient Egyptian legend): Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `the_book_of_thoth_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary finding

“The Book of Thoth” is not a securely documented ancient artifact with a continuous physical provenance. The name now covers at least four historically distinct things:

1. **The legendary book in the First Tale of Setne Khaemwaset**, the principal object studied here: a unique divine manuscript written by Thoth, hidden beneath the Nile, stolen by Prince Naneferkaptah, buried with him, temporarily stolen again by Setne Khaemwaset, and finally returned to the tomb.
2. **Sacred writings attributed collectively to Thoth/Hermes**, including the Egyptian priestly books described by Clement of Alexandria around 200 CE.
3. **A real but fragmentarily preserved Egyptian composition conventionally titled by modern editors *The Ancient Egyptian Book of Thoth***: a master–disciple discourse copied in numerous Demotic and a few hieratic manuscripts between approximately the first century BCE and second century CE. No surviving manuscript identifies it as the magical volume in the Setne story.
4. **Modern occult “Books of Thoth,”** especially the alleged Egyptian origin of tarot proposed in 1781, Etteilla’s divinatory system, Aleister Crowley’s 1944 *Book of Thoth*, and Maurice Doreal’s 1939 *Emerald Tablets of Thoth the Atlantean*. These are documented modern receptions or inventions, not recovered copies of the legendary object.

The fundamental conclusion is therefore negative but precise: **no physical manuscript can presently be authenticated as the legendary Book of Thoth described in the Setne narrative**. What survives physically is the papyrus transmitting the story and, separately, a corpus of priestly instructional manuscripts that modern Egyptologists named the *Book of Thoth*.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Object class

In its earliest surviving narrative, the Book of Thoth is a **divine magical manuscript or scroll**. It is not described as a codex, stone tablet, deck of cards, set of metal plates, or illustrated funerary papyrus. Those forms belong to later reinterpretations.

The story normally called *Setne I*, *The First Tale of Setne Khaemwaset*, or *Setne Khaemwaset and Naneferkaptah* is preserved principally on Egyptian Museum, Cairo, **CG 30646**, also indexed as **TM 55857**. The manuscript is papyrus written in Demotic Egyptian. It originally comprised six numbered pages; pages 1–2 are missing, while pages 3–6 preserve most of the narrative. The surviving manuscript belongs paleographically to the Ptolemaic or early Greco-Roman period, although proposed dates vary. The Thesaurus Linguae Aegyptiae classifies it broadly as Hellenistic and records Thebes as its reported find region; Francis Ll. Griffith wrote in 1900 that it “is said to have been found in Thebes.” Its present location is the Egyptian Museum in Cairo. [Thesaurus Linguae Aegyptiae](https://thesaurus-linguae-aegyptiae.de/object/TMMEJ7JUWRGC7NMSZEHM5WTZSQ); [Griffith, *Stories of the High Priests of Memphis*](https://etana.org/sites/default/files/coretexts/15563.pdf)

### 1.2 Names and titles

There is no evidence that the legendary object bore a formal cover title equivalent to the modern English **Book of Thoth**.

Relevant names include:

- **Thoth**: Greek Θώθ, derived from Egyptian **Ḏḥwty**, commonly rendered Djehuty, the god associated with writing, reckoning, learned knowledge, ritual speech, and scribal practice.
- **Book of Thoth**: a modern descriptive title for the volume said in the story to have been written by Thoth himself.
- **Book of Magic**: an older Egyptological paraphrase used by Griffith and others.
- **The document/book belonging to Thoth**: the narrative repeatedly calls it simply “the book” or “my book/document” in Thoth’s reported complaint.
- **The Ancient Egyptian Book of Thoth**: the editorial title assigned by Richard Jasnow and Karl-Theodor Zauzich to a separate fragmentary master–disciple composition. It should not be silently identified with Naneferkaptah’s book.
- **Books of Hermes**: Greek-era writings credited to Hermes, the Greek deity identified with Thoth. Clement’s plural corpus is not a single Book of Thoth.
- **Livre de Thot / Book of Thoth tarot**: a late-eighteenth-century occult identification of tarot cards with a supposedly surviving Egyptian book.
- **The Book of Thoth: A Short Essay on the Tarot of the Egyptians**: Aleister Crowley’s twentieth-century title.

### 1.3 Legendary physical form

The Setne story gives few intrinsic details. It calls the object a book or document that can be:

- held in the hand;
- unrolled and read publicly;
- tied to a human body with royal linen;
- enclosed in a sequence of boxes;
- placed between or beside the dead in a tomb;
- copied onto a fresh sheet of papyrus.

These actions strongly imply a **papyrus roll**, but the material of Thoth’s original manuscript is never securely stated in the surviving portion. Naneferkaptah’s duplicate is explicitly made on “new papyrus.” It would therefore be an inference—not a textual fact—to say that the divine original was necessarily papyrus.

No dimensions, weight, illustrations, rubrication, binding, maker’s marks, or decoration are supplied. The surviving text specifies only that the book contains **two written spells. The first two pages of Cairo CG 30646 are lost, so an original introduction or fuller initial description may have disappeared.

### 1.4 Containers and protective apparatus

According to Ahwere’s embedded narrative, the book rested inside six nested containers:

1. an outer box of iron;
2. a box of copper;
3. a box of juniper wood;
4. a box of ivory and ebony;
5. a box of silver;
6. a box of gold.

The order is given from exterior to interior. Around the cache were six miles—or, in some translations, a *schoenus*—of serpents, scorpions, and other reptiles. An “eternal serpent” guarded the boxes directly. Naneferkaptah killed it twice, but it revived; on the third attempt he cut it in two and separated the halves with sand.

The repository lay beneath or within the Nile near Coptos, with twenty-one divine cubits of water over the fish seen there. The story does not identify a constructed chamber, named temple, or archaeologically locatable site. [Lichtheim translation](https://www.attalus.org/egypt/setne.html)

### 1.5 Text and powers

The book contains two spells:

- **First spell:** commands or enchants the sky, earth, underworld, mountains, and waters; enables the reader to understand the speech of birds, fish, reptiles, and animals; and permits sight of fish beneath deep water.
- **Second spell:** permits the reader to see the solar god Re/Pre appearing in the sky with his Ennead, as well as the moon and stars “in their forms.”

The spells therefore confer cosmic knowledge, command of natural realms, comprehension of nonhuman language, and direct divine or astronomical vision. The book is not described as granting simple omnipotence or immortality. It cannot save Naneferkaptah’s family from the divine judgment imposed for its theft.

### 1.6 Luminous behavior

When Setne takes the book from Naneferkaptah’s tomb, light goes before him and darkness follows behind; Ahwere laments that the tomb’s light has departed. When Setne restores the book, the tomb again appears filled with sunlight. The text thus makes the book or its presence a source of supernatural illumination. Whether it literally radiates light or symbolically restores divine power to the tomb is not fully determinable from the passage.

### 1.7 Surviving physical witnesses

No alleged original survives. The relevant real artifacts are:

- **Cairo CG 30646 / TM 55857:** principal Demotic manuscript of *Setne I*; papyrus; four surviving pages from an original six; Egyptian Museum, Cairo.
- **Cairo CG 30692:** fragment preserving material related to or varying the lost beginning of the story.
- **Other fragmentary Setne material:** evidence that the narrative circulated in variants, but not evidence for the magical book’s physical existence.
- **The Demotic/hieratic “Book of Thoth” corpus:** numerous separate papyrus manuscripts now dispersed among Copenhagen, Florence, Berlin, and other collections. These are physical manuscripts of a priestly dialogue, not authenticated remains of Naneferkaptah’s book.

The two categories must remain distinct: Cairo CG 30646 is **a manuscript about the legendary object**, while the Jasnow–Zauzich papyri are **real manuscripts bearing a modern scholarly title derived from Thoth’s role in their discourse**.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Legendary manufacture

In the Setne tale, a priest tells Naneferkaptah that the coveted book is one “that Thoth wrote with his own hand, when he came down following the other gods.” The divine author is thus also the maker. No human workshop, cost, commission, or production date is supplied.

The book initially belongs in Thoth’s “storehouse” or chamber. When Naneferkaptah steals it, Thoth complains before Re that the prince entered his storehouse, plundered it, seized the box containing his book, and killed its guardian. The narrative accordingly treats ownership as Thoth’s and acquisition by Naneferkaptah as theft, not as heroic discovery of ownerless treasure.

### 2.2 Earliest securely datable attestation

The earliest unambiguous surviving attestation of this particular magical book is **Cairo CG 30646**, copied in Demotic Egyptian during the Ptolemaic or early Greco-Roman period. Modern specialists often place the handwriting in the earlier Ptolemaic period; Griffith, writing before later paleographic refinements, favored the last century of Ptolemaic rule or first century CE. A surviving colophon dates the copy to “year 15, first month of winter,” but the ruler is not named, so the regnal year does not establish an absolute date. [Lichtheim](https://www.attalus.org/egypt/setne.html); [Griffith](https://etana.org/sites/default/files/coretexts/15563.pdf); [TLA](https://thesaurus-linguae-aegyptiae.de/object/TMMEJ7JUWRGC7NMSZEHM5WTZSQ)

The manuscript is many centuries later than the historical Khaemwaset, a son of Ramesses II who lived in the thirteenth century BCE. No Ramesside inscription names the legendary book or records the episode. The real prince’s documented antiquarian and priestly reputation probably helped make him a credible protagonist in later fiction, but that is literary reuse, not contemporary testimony. [UCL Digital Egypt](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/saqqara/khaemwaset.html)

### 2.3 What the earliest witness does not establish

Cairo CG 30646 does not establish:

- that Thoth was understood to have produced only one book;
- that an actual manuscript was ever recovered from a tomb;
- that historical Khaemwaset entered Naneferkaptah’s tomb;
- that Naneferkaptah, Ahwere, Merib, or Pharaoh Mernebptah were historical participants in the episode;
- that the book was made from papyrus rather than another writable material;
- that its contents extended beyond the two spells;
- that it was identical with later Hermetic writings, tarot, the Emerald Tablet, the Egyptian Book of the Dead, or the modern Demotic composition called the *Book of Thoth*.

### 2.4 Wider Egyptian background

The story grew in a culture in which divine authorship, temple libraries, “Houses of Life,” ritual books, and the attribution of writings to Thoth were intelligible institutions and conventions. Naneferkaptah is portrayed reading inscriptions in tombs, on royal monuments, and on stelae of House-of-Life scribes. His son Merib is entered in the House of Life’s register.

This background makes the legendary book culturally meaningful without proving that the unique magical volume existed. The House of Life was a real temple-associated intellectual and scribal institution; the Setne story turns restricted priestly knowledge into a narrative object whose illicit removal produces catastrophe.

### 2.5 The separate Demotic composition

Jasnow and Zauzich’s *Ancient Egyptian Book of Thoth* is a fragmentary dialogue between a “Master” and a disciple commonly translated as “The-one-who-loves-knowledge.” It discusses scribal craft, sacred geography, the underworld, ritual, animal knowledge, prophecy, and admission to restricted knowledge. The editors interpret it as a House-of-Life initiation or instructional text and as an Egyptian pendant to the Greek Hermetica.

The work is transmitted by multiple manuscripts, chiefly in Demotic but with hieratic evidence, dating roughly from the first century BCE through the second century CE. Its ancient title is not securely preserved. The name *Book of Thoth* is an editorial designation, justified by Thoth’s intellectual presence but not by a colophon identifying the composition with the Setne object. The 2005 edition was supplemented in 2021 with revised readings and eighty-nine newly identified fragments. [Harrassowitz 2021 edition](https://www.harrassowitz-verlag.de/The_Ancient_Egyptian_Book_of_Thoth_II/titel_6934.ahtml); [Bryn Mawr Classical Review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2006/2006.05.19/)

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because the object is legendary, the following chain records **custody inside the narrative**, not archaeological provenance.

### Link 1: Thoth’s authorship and possession

**Holder:** Thoth.  
**Place:** his divine chamber or storehouse; subsequently a protected location under the Nile near Coptos.  
**Mechanism:** divine manufacture and original ownership.  
**Evidence:** Ahwere’s account in *Setne I*.  
**Tier:** early attested legend.

The surviving portion does not explain whether Thoth himself placed the book beneath the river or whether the priest merely knew where Thoth’s property was hidden. It is uncertain whether the underwater place and the “storehouse” are identical.

### Link 2: Disclosure by an unnamed priest

An elderly priest encounters Naneferkaptah during a procession of Ptah and tells him of the book. In return for directions, the priest demands one hundred pieces of silver for his burial and two tax-exempt priestly stipends.

**Transfer:** information only; the priest never possesses the book.  
**Tier:** early attested legend.

### Link 3: Naneferkaptah’s expedition and theft

Naneferkaptah travels from Memphis to Coptos with Ahwere and Merib. After four days with the priests of Isis, he creates magically animated wax rowers and sailors, uses sand to divide the river, overcomes the reptiles and eternal serpent, opens the nested boxes, and removes the book.

**Holder:** Naneferkaptah.  
**Mechanism:** deliberate theft or plunder from Thoth.  
**Evidence:** Thoth’s complaint explicitly characterizes the act as entry into his storehouse, seizure of his box, and killing of his guardian.  
**Tier:** early attested legend.

### Link 4: Ahwere’s temporary handling

Naneferkaptah places the original in Ahwere’s hand. She recites both spells and experiences their powers.

**Holder/user:** Ahwere.  
**Mechanism:** temporary loan within the family.  
**Tier:** early attested legend.

The text does not say that she retains it afterward.

### Link 5: Naneferkaptah’s duplicate and ingestion

Naneferkaptah has a new sheet of papyrus brought, copies every word from the original, soaks the copy in beer, dissolves it in water, and drinks it. He thereby internalizes its knowledge.

This is the only stated act of copying. The duplicate is then physically destroyed by dissolution and ingestion. It has no subsequent custody chain.

**Original:** remains intact.  
**Copy:** manufactured, dissolved, consumed, and destroyed.  
**Tier:** early attested legend.

### Link 6: Divine judgment and the deaths

Thoth reports the theft to Re. A divine power is sent with the command that Naneferkaptah and his household not reach Memphis safely. Merib drowns first; Ahwere drowns at the same location; Naneferkaptah later binds the book to his body with a strip of royal linen and intentionally enters the Nile.

The book therefore physically accompanies Naneferkaptah during his death. It does not prevent the deaths and appears unable to override Re’s judgment.

### Link 7: Recovery of Naneferkaptah and the book

At Memphis, Naneferkaptah’s body is seen clinging magically to the rudders of Pharaoh’s vessel. When it is recovered, the book is still attached to his body.

**Place:** Nile, then Memphis.  
**Mechanism:** recovery with the drowned owner.  
**Tier:** early attested legend.

### Link 8: Burial with Naneferkaptah

Naneferkaptah is embalmed and buried in the Memphite necropolis. The book is interred with him. Ahwere and Merib remain physically buried at Coptos, although their spirits join him in the Memphite tomb through his magical skill.

**Holder/guardian:** dead Naneferkaptah.  
**Place:** an unidentified tomb in the Memphis necropolis.  
**Tier:** early attested legend.

The text gives no tomb number, architectural description, coordinates, or name of a recognizable cemetery sector. “Saqqara” is a common modern geographical gloss for the Memphite necropolis but is not an excavated identification.

### Link 9: Setne’s entry and contested wager

Setne Khaemwaset enters the tomb, sees the book, and demands it. Naneferkaptah proposes a game of fifty-two-point draughts. Naneferkaptah wins three games and magically drives Setne progressively into the ground. Setne’s foster brother Inaros/Anherru brings Ptah’s amulets and magical books from Pharaoh, allowing Setne to escape.

**Legal or ritual status:** contested possession; no consensual transfer is completed.  
**Tier:** early attested legend.

### Link 10: Setne’s seizure

Setne reaches out and takes the book. He reseals the tomb and carries the volume to the surface. Light precedes him and darkness follows.

**Mechanism:** theft or forcible seizure from a tomb after losing the agreed contest.  
**Holder:** Setne.  
**Tier:** early attested legend.

Pharaoh orders him to return it, warning that Naneferkaptah will otherwise force him to do so while carrying a forked stick and a burning brazier.

### Link 11: Setne’s use

Setne refuses. He spends his time unrolling the book and reading it aloud “to everyone.” The story does not report his successfully performing its two cosmic spells. His use is public and indiscriminate, contrasting with the book’s former restriction.

### Link 12: Naneferkaptah’s retaliation

Setne encounters Tabubu, a purported daughter of a priest of Bastet, and undergoes a humiliating vision or magical ordeal in which he signs away his property and consents to the murder of his children. He awakens naked; his actual children remain alive. Setne attributes the experience to Naneferkaptah.

The book does not physically move during this episode as preserved. The ordeal functions as coercion compelling restitution.

### Link 13: Return to the tomb

Setne returns the book while carrying the forked stick and burning brazier exactly as Naneferkaptah predicted. Light again fills the tomb.

**Mechanism:** compelled restitution.  
**Holder:** Naneferkaptah.  
**Place:** Memphite tomb.  
**Tier:** early attested legend.

### Link 14: Final state within the story

Setne subsequently retrieves the bodies of Ahwere and Merib from beneath the corner of a building at Coptos, restores the demolished building, and brings the bodies to Memphis. They are placed with Naneferkaptah, and the tomb is sealed.

The book is not explicitly mentioned in the final sentence, but the preceding restitution leaves it in the tomb. No ancient sequel describes another removal. Its legendary custody chain therefore terminates in a **sealed, unidentified Memphite tomb**. [Complete narrative](https://www.attalus.org/egypt/setne.html)

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Does the object exist?

There is no archaeological, museum, or documentary evidence that the unique supernatural Book of Thoth existed outside the story. No excavated papyrus contains the two spells in the form described. No manuscript has a documented chain from a Memphite royal tomb, through Naneferkaptah and Khaemwaset, to a modern collection.

The appropriate classification is therefore **legendary literary object**, not “lost artifact” in the ordinary archaeological sense.

### 4.2 Is Cairo CG 30646 the Book of Thoth?

No. It is the papyrus manuscript containing the tale. Its material text narrates possession of the book but is not the book possessed by the characters.

Confusing container and content produces a false provenance: Cairo CG 30646 reportedly came from Thebes and entered the Egyptian collection in the nineteenth century; the story’s object ends in a tomb near Memphis.

### 4.3 Is the Jasnow–Zauzich text the legendary book?

This is unproved. Similarities include:

- association with Thoth;
- restricted priestly and scribal knowledge;
- House-of-Life setting;
- animal, cosmic, underworld, geographical, and ritual knowledge;
- transmission in multiple Egyptian manuscripts.

Differences are decisive:

- it is a dialogue or examination, not a book of two spells;
- it survives in many manuscript copies, not as one guarded divine original;
- its modern title is editorial;
- no manuscript claims descent from the object stolen by Naneferkaptah;
- the surviving fragments do not reproduce the Setne book’s stated spells as a pair.

The strongest defensible formulation is that the real composition illuminates the **intellectual culture that made the legend possible**. It is not an authenticated material claimant.

### 4.4 Are Clement’s forty-two Hermetic books the same object?

No evidence supports identity. Around 200 CE, Clement of Alexandria describes a priestly procession carrying forty-two indispensable books attributed to Hermes: thirty-six philosophical or priestly books and six medical works. This is explicitly a plural library classified by subject, not one underwater magical manuscript containing two spells. It witnesses the broad attribution of Egyptian sacred learning to Thoth/Hermes, not the custody of Naneferkaptah’s object. [Clement, *Stromata* VI](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0210.htm); [Cambridge discussion](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811071/82530/excerpt/9781107182530_excerpt.pdf)

### 4.5 Is tarot the Book of Thoth?

Historically, no. Antoine Court de Gébelin and the Comte de Mellet advanced the Egyptian interpretation of tarot in volume VIII of *Monde primitif* in 1781. Mellet called it the “Book of Thoth” and proposed speculative Egyptian etymologies before Egyptian hieroglyphs had been deciphered. No ancient Egyptian tarot cards, translation chain, or manuscript-to-card transformation was produced.

The Morgan Library characterizes this as the late-eighteenth-century creation of a new occult identity for what had been a card game. It is a well-documented modern theory, not an ancient provenance. [Morgan Library](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/online/tarot/11); [1781 French text](https://www.tarock.info/gebelin.htm)

### 4.6 The Emerald Tablet and Doreal’s Emerald Tablets

Neither is the Setne object.

The singular **Emerald Tablet** is an alchemical text whose traceable textual history begins in medieval Arabic literature associated with the *Book of the Secret of Creation*. Its Hermetic attribution reflects the Hermes–Thoth synthesis, but its form and contents differ from the Setne book.

Maurice Doreal’s plural **Emerald Tablets of Thoth the Atlantean**, published by the Brotherhood of the White Temple in 1939, have no inspectable Egyptian or Atlantean originals, excavation record, source language, independent witnesses, or pre-Doreal chain of transmission. The documented object is Doreal’s twentieth-century publication. WorldCat records Doreal as the translator and the Brotherhood as publisher, but cataloguing a claimed translation does not authenticate its alleged source tablets. [WorldCat](https://search.worldcat.org/it/title/emerald-tablets-of-thoth-the-atlantean-a-literal-translation-of-one-of-the-most-ancient-and-secret-of-the-great-works-of-the-ancient-wisdom/oclc/8062038)

### 4.7 Scientific testing

No candidate Book of Thoth has undergone radiocarbon dating, ink analysis, fiber analysis, multispectral imaging, or forensic provenance testing under that claimed identity because no serious physical claimant exists.

The dating of Cairo CG 30646 and of the Demotic *Book of Thoth* fragments rests primarily on paleography, language, associated texts, and archaeological or collection context. These examinations date the witnesses, not the legendary object described in them.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Function inside the story

The book is an instrument of privileged knowledge and cosmic vision. Its use is performative: the reader recites its written spells, and the world responds. It provides:

- domination or enchantment of cosmic regions;
- comprehension of animal speech;
- vision through deep water;
- sight of Re, the Ennead, moon, and stars in their divine forms.

It also functions as a test of legitimate access. Naneferkaptah acquires knowledge without permission; Setne takes it despite warning and defeat. Both suffer. The narrative does not condemn knowledge itself—both heroes are praised as learned scribes—but dramatizes the consequences of violating divine and funerary ownership.

### 6.2 Restricted versus public knowledge

The book begins in a protected divine repository. An initiated priest knows its location but does not retrieve it. Naneferkaptah breaches the protections. Setne later reads it “to everyone,” transforming secret knowledge into public performance.

This movement from restricted archive to indiscriminate reading is part of the offense. The story likely reflects the social importance of temple control over ritual texts, although assigning a specific institutional polemic to the narrative remains scholarly inference.

### 6.3 Copying and ingestion

Naneferkaptah’s act of copying the text, dissolving it in beer, and drinking it gives the manuscript a bodily ritual function: written knowledge becomes internalized. Comparable practices of washing written formulas into liquid are known from magical traditions, but the story itself supplies the decisive evidence here.

The act preserves the **knowledge** while destroying the physical duplicate. It creates no surviving rival manuscript.

### 6.4 Funerary function

Once bound to Naneferkaptah’s corpse and placed in his tomb, the book becomes a grave possession and source of supernatural light. It supports the dead prince’s continued magical agency. Its removal darkens the tomb; its return restores light.

The book is not described as an ordinary Book of the Dead, although both belong to a wider tradition of efficacious writing used by the deceased.

### 6.5 Ceremonial context

The story frames major movements with ritual:

- Naneferkaptah learns of the book during a procession of Ptah.
- He is hosted by the priests of Isis at Coptos.
- After using the book, the family celebrates before Isis and Harpocrates.
- Setne later sacrifices an ox and goose and pours wine before the same deities before searching the Coptos necropolis.
- His return of the book involves the humiliating emblems of a forked stick and burning brazier.

There is no documented historical ceremony in which an actual Book of Thoth was displayed or used.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The theft beneath the Nile

**Tier:** earliest attested narrative core.

The book lies within progressively more precious containers, protected by an expanse of dangerous creatures and an immortal serpent. Naneferkaptah defeats the guardian, obtains the manuscript, and demonstrates both spells. This is the fullest ancient account of the object’s recovery.

The nested boxes express escalating sanctity and value. Attempts to read them as literal archaeological directions are unsupported.

### 7.2 The curse or divine prosecution

**Tier:** earliest attested narrative core.

The book does not carry an automatic curse in the later horror-fiction sense. Thoth discovers the theft, brings a legal complaint before Re, and receives judgment. The family’s deaths are therefore an act of divine adjudication, not necessarily a mechanical curse embedded in the object.

Modern summaries often flatten this distinction into “the Book of Thoth is cursed.”

### 7.3 The drowned book that returns to Memphis

**Tier:** earliest attested narrative core.

Naneferkaptah binds the book to himself and drowns, but his corpse is later found holding the ship’s rudders with the book still attached. The episode explains how an object stolen near Coptos can later be buried in the Memphite necropolis.

### 7.4 Setne and the tomb

**Tier:** earliest attested narrative core, although its beginning is partly reconstructed.

Setne finds the luminous manuscript between or beside the dead, loses a supernatural board-game contest for it, escapes through Ptah’s amulets, and seizes it anyway. This is the book’s second theft.

Because pages 1–2 of the main papyrus are missing, modern retellings that give a seamless account of Setne’s initial discovery incorporate reconstruction from context and related fragments.

### 7.5 Tabubu and compelled restitution

**Tier:** earliest attested narrative core.

The beautiful Tabubu draws Setne into an escalating bargain culminating in apparent filicide. He awakens naked; his children are alive. Naneferkaptah is implicated as the author of the illusion. The ordeal reverses Setne’s confidence and compels him to return the book.

Whether Tabubu is a real woman manipulated by magic, an apparition, or an entirely constructed vision is unresolved in the text.

### 7.6 The lost tomb

**Tier:** modern speculation built on an ancient narrative.

The story locates Naneferkaptah’s tomb in the Memphite necropolis but does not provide an archaeological locator. No recognized excavation has produced the named family together with the book. Claims that the volume remains recoverable beneath Saqqara are treasure-hunting extrapolations, not evidence-based archaeological hypotheses.

### 7.7 The forty-two books

**Tier:** ancient but separate tradition.

Clement’s forty-two Hermetic books attest the idea that Egyptian priestly knowledge belonged to Hermes/Thoth. Later writers often collapsed this library into a single primordial book. The collapse is interpretive; Clement describes a classified collection.

### 7.8 Tarot as the surviving Book of Thoth

**Tier:** documented modern invention, 1781.

Court de Gébelin and Mellet proposed that tarot images preserved Egyptian sacred knowledge through disguise and transmission. The proposal appeared before Champollion’s decipherment and rested on imaginative iconographic and linguistic speculation. It materially shaped subsequent occult tarot, even though its asserted ancient provenance failed historical scrutiny.

### 7.9 Emerald and Atlantean tablets

**Tier:** late modern accretion.

The medieval Emerald Tablet, the Setne book, and Doreal’s plural Atlantean tablets are separate textual constructions. Doreal’s work supplied Thoth with an Atlantean biography and a set of emerald records allegedly hidden beneath Egypt. No archaeological or manuscript evidence corroborates those claims.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Legendary object

**Status:** not extant as an authenticated physical artifact.  
**Last location in the earliest story:** sealed in Naneferkaptah’s unidentified tomb in the Memphite necropolis, together with Naneferkaptah, Ahwere, and Merib.  
**Present real-world location:** unknown because physical existence has not been established.

There is no accession number, legal owner, conservation record, public display, recognized shrine, or active restitution dispute for the legendary object.

### 8.2 Principal narrative manuscript

**Object:** Cairo CG 30646 / TM 55857.  
**Material:** papyrus.  
**Script/language:** Demotic Egyptian.  
**Current institution:** Egyptian Museum, Cairo.  
**Condition:** first two of six pages lost; remaining four substantially preserved, with wear and damage.  
**Access:** known principally through facsimile, editions, translations, and cataloguing.  
**Scientific analysis:** no published radiocarbon or materials testing was located; dating is paleographic and linguistic.

### 8.3 Separate Demotic *Book of Thoth* corpus

The manuscripts reconstructed by Jasnow and Zauzich are dispersed. Substantial groups are associated with the temple-library material from Tebtunis and other Egyptian temple contexts, including Dime/Soknopaiou Nesos and Elephantine. Papyrus Carlsberg inventory entries include P. Carlsberg 617, 618, 619, 673, 697, 698, 699, and 700, among others. [Papyrus Carlsberg inventory](https://pcarlsberg.ku.dk/publishedtexts/)

The Tebtunis temple deposit was discovered in 1931 by Carlo Anti and Gilbert Bagnani. Much of the excavated material is in Florence, while a large share of related material is in Copenhagen. The broader collection contains hundreds of Demotic, hieratic, hieroglyphic, and Greek manuscripts principally of the first and second centuries CE. [UCL Tebtunis library](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/writing/library/tebt.html); [Papyrus Carlsberg survey](https://pcarlsberg.ku.dk/survey/)

These fragments are genuine ancient Egyptian manuscripts, but their identity as the legendary object is unsupported.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Nineteenth-century recovery of the story

Hermann Brugsch’s 1865–1867 decipherment and publication revealed that Demotic preserved narrative literature, not merely administrative and legal documents. Émile Brugsch prepared a facsimile; later translations and studies by Gaston Maspero, Eugène Revillout, Johannes Hess, and Francis Ll. Griffith refined the reading. Griffith’s 1900 *Stories of the High Priests of Memphis* gave the tale a durable English scholarly form.

Lafcadio Hearn retold the narrative in *Stray Leaves from Strange Literature*, helping move the object from Egyptological publication into Anglophone weird and fantastic literature. [Hearn text](https://readingroo.ms/5/5/6/5/55650/55650-h/55650-h.htm)

### 9.2 Tarot

The most consequential modern transformation began in France:

- **1781:** Court de Gébelin’s *Monde primitif* argued for an ancient Egyptian origin of tarot.
- **1781:** the Comte de Mellet’s accompanying essay explicitly presented tarot as the **Livre de Thot** and offered divinatory interpretations.
- **1780s:** Etteilla systematized occult tarot and Egyptianized card meanings; his followers developed “Book of Thoth” teaching and divinatory institutions.
- **Nineteenth century:** Éliphas Lévi, Paul Christian, and later occultists elaborated the Egyptian temple-initiation fiction, including cards, paintings, or gold plates containing primordial wisdom.
- **1938–1943:** Frieda Harris painted the images of Crowley’s Thoth Tarot.
- **1944:** Crowley published *The Book of Thoth: A Short Essay on the Tarot of the Egyptians* in a limited edition. The documented 1944 copyright record describes it as “a short essay on the Tarot.” [Copyright record](https://100thmonkeypress.com/biblio/acrowley/articles/1944_xx_xx_thoth_copyright.pdf)

Crowley’s book and deck are original twentieth-century occult works. They do not claim a credible physical chain from Cairo CG 30646 or Naneferkaptah’s tomb.

### 9.3 Film

Universal’s *The Mummy* (1932) made a “Scroll of Thoth” central to its plot. The fictional scroll can restore life to the dead. Its production reportedly used imagery derived from an actual Book of the Dead papyrus, further blending distinct Egyptian textual categories in popular culture. The Oriental Institute’s study of the Book of the Dead notes this cinematic substitution. [ISAC, *Book of the Dead: Becoming God in Ancient Egypt*](https://oi-idb-static.uchicago.edu/multimedia/239131/oimp39.pdf)

The animated short *The Riddle of the Sphinx* (Vladimir Pekar, 1985) retold material involving the Book of Thoth and Naneferkaptah.

### 9.4 Weird fiction and the Necronomicon

In H. P. Lovecraft and E. Hoffmann Price’s “Through the Gates of the Silver Key,” an Egyptian “Book of Thoth” is associated with the background of occult knowledge later connected to the Necronomicon. This is literary adaptation, not a claim based on a new Egyptian witness. Lovecraft’s pseudo-bibliographic method helped establish the modern image of an ancient forbidden book whose reading brings dangerous knowledge.

### 9.5 Games and commercial culture

The name appears on commercial tarot editions, occult manuals, fantasy fiction, online games, and role-playing artifacts. In these settings the object is commonly represented as:

- a spellbook increasing magical power;
- a repository of all knowledge;
- an Egyptian grimoire;
- a resurrection manual;
- a tarot deck;
- a set of indestructible tablets.

Only the first of these resembles the ancient narrative, and even there the ancient powers are more specific than modern “all magic” formulations.

### 9.6 Modern Kemetic and esoteric reception

Modern practitioners sometimes treat the Jasnow–Zauzich composition as a recoverable priestly initiation text or devotional scripture. That is a modern religious use of genuine ancient textual fragments. It should be distinguished both from the Setne artifact and from claims that one intact primordial Book of Thoth has been rediscovered.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Early publication history

- **1865–1867:** Hermann Brugsch produced the foundational decipherment and discussion.
- **Late nineteenth century:** Maspero, Revillout, and Hess proposed corrections and interpretations.
- **1900:** Griffith published transcription, translation, commentary, manuscript description, and a study of the Setne cycle.
- **1908:** Wilhelm Spiegelberg published the papyrus in the Cairo catalogue series.
- **1980:** Miriam Lichtheim included a widely used translation in *Ancient Egyptian Literature*, volume III.
- **2006:** Sara Goldbrunner published *Der verblendete Gelehrte*.
- **2017:** Steve Vinson published *The Craft of a Good Scribe*, emphasizing history, narrative construction, and scribal ideology.

The TLA catalogue supplies an extensive bibliography and a continuously revised digital record. [TLA bibliography](https://thesaurus-linguae-aegyptiae.de/object/TMMEJ7JUWRGC7NMSZEHM5WTZSQ)

### 10.2 Date of Cairo CG 30646

Griffith placed the manuscript no earlier than the Ptolemaic period and provisionally around the last century BCE or first century CE. Later authorities commonly favor an earlier Ptolemaic date, sometimes the first half of the Ptolemaic period. The unnamed “year 15” in the colophon cannot independently decide the issue.

The distinction between **date of surviving copy** and **date of composition** remains essential. The tale may predate the manuscript, but no securely dated earlier complete witness establishes how much earlier.

### 10.3 Reconstruction of the missing opening

The principal manuscript begins on numbered page 3. The standard opening—Setne hearing of the book, locating the tomb, and confronting its occupants—is reconstructed from the surviving situation and related fragments. It is highly plausible but not preserved continuously in Cairo CG 30646.

Any quotation attributed to the missing opening must be labeled reconstruction rather than surviving verbatim text.

### 10.4 Historical memory of Khaemwaset

The historical prince Khaemwaset restored or investigated older monuments and held high priestly office at Memphis. Later authors transformed him into Setne, an exceptionally skilled but morally vulnerable magician and reader of old inscriptions. Scholars debate the degree to which specific historical actions generated particular narrative episodes. No evidence connects the historical prince to the actual theft of a supernatural manuscript.

### 10.5 Meaning of the tale

Major interpretations include:

- a warning against illicit access to divine knowledge;
- a story about proper scribal character and the limits of technical mastery;
- a defense of tomb ownership and funerary integrity;
- a reflection on House-of-Life authority;
- a narrative of restitution in which Setne learns through humiliation;
- a literary meditation on the antiquarian Khaemwaset tradition.

These readings are compatible in part. None turns the fictional custody chain into historical documentation.

### 10.6 The Demotic *Book of Thoth* debate

Jasnow and Zauzich announced the composition in the 1990s and published the editio princeps in 2005. Reviewers recognized the reconstruction as a major achievement while disputing individual joins, readings, translations, and the strength of the proposed connection to Greek Hermetica.

The 2021 revision incorporated alternative readings and eighty-nine newly identified fragments. Because the text is transmitted in many fragmentary witnesses, reconstruction remains provisional. The manuscripts are real; the unity, sequence, title, speakers, and ritual function of the reconstructed composition are scholarly questions.

### 10.7 Discredited or unsupported identifications

The following claims remain popular but lack adequate evidence:

- that tarot originated in pharaonic Egypt;
- that one intact Book of Thoth survived the burning of the Library of Alexandria;
- that the Egyptian Book of the Dead is the Book of Thoth;
- that the Emerald Tablet is Naneferkaptah’s manuscript;
- that Doreal translated physical Atlantean tablets;
- that an identifiable chamber beneath the Great Pyramid or Saqqara presently contains the book;
- that the Jasnow–Zauzich fragments reproduce the exact two spells of *Setne I*;
- that historical Khaemwaset’s possession of the book is recorded in Ramesside sources.

### Assessment of the evidence

The evidentiary hierarchy is unusually clear:

1. **Physical fact:** papyri transmitting *Setne I* and the separate Demotic master–disciple work exist.
2. **Textual fact:** *Setne I* describes a unique magical manuscript owned by Thoth and twice stolen.
3. **Plausible historical inference:** the legend reflects Egyptian temple libraries, restricted scribal learning, funerary texts, and the antiquarian reputation of Khaemwaset.
4. **Unresolved:** the precise composition date of *Setne I*, the complete lost opening, and the exact relation of the Jasnow–Zauzich work to Greek Hermetica.
5. **Unsupported identity claim:** any surviving papyrus, tarot deck, emerald tablet, or modern occult book is Naneferkaptah’s legendary manuscript.
6. **Current status:** the object’s existence outside literature cannot be established.
