# The angel's *schedula* delivered to Saint Giles: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conclusion

The “angel’s *schedula*” is the written object in the medieval legend commonly called the Mass of Saint Giles: while Giles celebrates Mass for a King Charles burdened by an unconfessed crime, an angel places a small written sheet on the altar. It identifies the crime, announces conditional forgiveness through Giles’s intercession, and, in the fullest early version, extends a promise of forgiveness to other repentant sinners who invoke the saint. Giles subsequently gives the sheet to the king.

Three analytically distinct objects have acquired this identity:

1. **The narrative *schedula*** in the Latin *Vita sancti Aegidii* and its numerous adaptations. This is securely attested as a literary object from about 891–915, not as a surviving early-medieval artifact.
2. **The alleged relic at Orléans**, reportedly preserved at the cathedral of Sainte-Croix until the religious violence of 1562. Its existence is reported in later scholarship, but no surviving inventory entry, physical description, image, or securely traced fragment was located in the accessible evidence surveyed here.
3. **Material representations of the legendary sheet**, especially the thirteenth-century Charlemagne window at Chartres and the painted sheet in the Master of Saint Giles’s *Mass of Saint Giles*, now National Gallery, London, NG4681. These are real artifacts depicting the legendary object; neither is the original *schedula* or a surviving fragment of it.

The primary conclusion is therefore:

> **The angelic original is a legendary object. A later physical claimant was reportedly kept at Orléans, but its antiquity and identity cannot now be tested and its very documentation remains incomplete. No authenticated *schedula*, fragment, reliquary, photograph, drawing, measurements, or scientific examination is presently known.**

The dossier accordingly follows both the legendary object’s textual “custody” and the much weaker historical trail of the alleged Orléans relic, while keeping the two separate.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Principal names

The earliest tradition does not give the object a unique proper name. It identifies it by an ordinary noun:

- **Latin:** *schedula*, accusative *schedulam* — a small sheet, slip, note, or written memorandum.
- **Variant Latin in liturgical witnesses:** *cartula* or *kartula* — a little charter, paper, or written sheet.
- **Medieval and modern French:** *cédule*, *feuille*, *billet*, *parchemin*; commonly *la cédule de saint Gilles* or *la cédule de Charlemagne*.
- **Middle English, in William Caxton’s translation of the *Golden Legend*:** “a schedule.”
- **German:** *Zettel*, *Sündenzettel*, *Brief*; the episode is sometimes described as the *Ägidiusmesse*.
- **English:** “the angel’s scroll,” “the angelic letter,” “the schedule of Saint Giles,” “Charlemagne’s confession,” or “the document of absolution.”

Latin *schedula* is a diminutive of *scheda/scida*, originally a strip or leaf of writing material. Its sense is therefore “little sheet” or “slip,” not the modern English sense of a timetable. The word by itself does not determine whether the support was papyrus, parchment, or paper. [Etymonline’s lexical history](https://www.etymonline.com/word/schedule) traces Late Latin *schedula* through Old French *cedule* to Middle English *sedule/cedule*.

The designation “Charlemagne’s confession” is potentially misleading. In the principal Latin narrative the sheet is not written by Charles and is not itself his sacramental confession. It is delivered from heaven, records the sin, and declares the terms of remission. Some later retellings blur these functions.

### 1.2 Class

The object belongs simultaneously to several medieval categories:

- a **miraculously delivered written message**;
- an **instrument of revelation**, disclosing what the king refuses to utter;
- an **instrument or notice of conditional remission**;
- in later reception, a purported **contact relic or heavenly autograph**;
- iconographically, a **scroll, sheet, letter, or phylactery**;
- liturgically, the central attribute of the **Mass of Saint Giles** episode.

It is not an indulgence in the later technical sense, nor is it presented as a papal charter, sealed diploma, sacramental formula, or material whose touch alone effects forgiveness.

### 1.3 Description in the earliest recoverable text

The Latin *Vita sancti Aegidii*, BHL 93–98b, dated by the IRHT’s FAMA database to approximately **891–915**, describes an angel placing a *schedula* on the altar during Mass. The sheet contains:

1. the king’s sin, written out in sequence or detail;
2. a statement that Giles’s prayers have obtained its remission;
3. conditions: the king must repent and cease committing it;
4. an appended general promise concerning sinners who invoke Giles and abandon their sin.

The episode is frequently summarized from the Latin as:

> On the following Sunday, while the holy man celebrated Mass and prayed for the king at the canon, the angel of the Lord appeared and placed on the altar a small sheet in which the king’s sin was set out in detail. It stated that through Giles’s prayers the sin was remitted, provided that the king repented and abstained from it thereafter.

Joseph Bédier reproduced and translated the substance of the passage, including the decisive noun *schedulam*, in his study of the Charlemagne legends. He also noted the concluding promise that any sinner who invoked Giles and ceased committing the offense could trust in divine remission. [Bédier’s discussion and quotation](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/0d/Les_l%C3%A9gendes_%C3%A9piques%3B_recherches_sur_la_formation_des_chansons_de_geste_%28IA_leslegendesepiqu03bedi%29.pdf).

No early source specifies:

- dimensions;
- weight;
- color;
- the animal source or preparation of any parchment;
- ink composition;
- handwriting or script;
- language;
- seal;
- folding pattern;
- container or reliquary;
- the angel’s name;
- what ultimately happened to the sheet after Giles gave it to Charles.

“Parchment” is a reasonable artistic convention but cannot be treated as a fact established by the earliest text.

### 1.4 Liturgical formulations

The legend entered the Office of Saint Giles. Cantus preserves, among others, the antiphon:

> *Beato Aegidio pro Carolo rege inter missam supplicante domino peccatum regis et venia peccati per cartulam delata est ab angelo.*

“While blessed Giles was supplicating the Lord during Mass for King Charles, the king’s sin and pardon for the sin were brought by an angel on a little sheet.”

A following antiphon reads:

> *Adjunctum erat in fine cartulae quod sancti Aegidii meritis quilibet peccator absolvatur a crimine si ulterius non vult admittere.*

“It was added at the end of the sheet that, through the merits of Saint Giles, any sinner should be absolved from the offense if he does not wish to commit it further.”

These chants preserve both the object’s delivery and the appended universal promise. [Cantus manuscript entry and transcription](https://cantusdatabase.org/source/123613/chants/?feast=2517).

A related liturgical series gives:

> *Orante sancto Aegidio super altare schedula ei posita est ab angelo Dei.*

“While Saint Giles was praying, a small sheet was placed for him upon the altar by an angel of God.”

It continues:

> *In ea unum regis peccatum apte legebat quod scienter reticuerat quando confitebatur.*

“On it he clearly read a sin of the king which [the king] had knowingly withheld when confessing.”

[Cantus Index, Office of Aegidius](https://cantusindex.uwaterloo.ca/feast/14090100?field_full_text_value=&page=1).

These are important variants. They make the object part of public liturgical memory and sharpen the offense from a sin the king “dared not confess” to one knowingly withheld in confession.

### 1.5 Description in the *Golden Legend*

Jacobus de Voragine’s thirteenth-century *Legenda aurea* closely abridges the older *Vita*. In Caxton’s English version:

> “the angel of our Lord appeared to him, and laid a schedule upon the altar where the sin of the king was written in by order”

The sheet also records the pardon, conditional upon repentance and abstention, and the promise to other repentant sinners. Giles then “delivered the schedule to the king,” after which the king confesses and asks pardon. [Caxton’s *Golden Legend*, “Saint Giles”](https://ccel.org/ccel/voragine/goldleg5/goldleg5.xiii.html).

The *Golden Legend* therefore supplies the last clear custody event within the principal narrative: **altar → Giles → King Charles**.

### 1.6 The painted object in National Gallery NG4681

The best-known physical representation appears in the Master of Saint Giles’s *Mass of Saint Giles*, painted around 1500. The angel holds a small pale written sheet inscribed:

> **EGIDI ME / RITO RE / MISSA SUNT / PECCATA / KAROLO**

Normalized:

> *Egidi merito remissa sunt peccata Karolo.*

“By the merit of Giles, sins have been remitted to Charles.”

The wording is a compressed visual label, not a transcription of the longer text described in the *Vita*. The painting shows only the declaration of remission; it does not visibly reproduce the sin, its conditions, or the general promise to other sinners.

The panel is oil on three boards of western European oak, 62.3 × 46.0 cm; painted surface 61.6 × 45.5 cm. The depicted *schedula* itself has no published measurements because it is an image within the painting. Scientific and conservation examinations concern the panel, not an actual relic. [National Gallery catalogue, identification and inscriptions](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/catalogues/campbell-2014/two-panels-from-an-altarpiece).

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Traditional origin

**Legendary claim:** the sheet originates in heaven or with God and is carried by “the angel of the Lord” to Giles during Mass. The text does not describe manufacture. It does not say an angel physically wrote it, although later writers and images naturally treated it as a heavenly document.

The message’s attributed authority is divine, but its ostensible documentary form is recognizably medieval: a written instrument publicly delivered to an altar and then shown to a ruler.

### 2.2 Earliest securely datable attestation

The earliest presently identifiable source is the anonymous Latin *Vita sancti Aegidii*, associated by scholarship with the abbey of Saint-Gilles in southern France. The IRHT dates the work **891–915**, identifies it under BHL 93–98b, and records approximately ninety manuscripts. [IRHT-CNRS FAMA record](https://fama.irht.cnrs.fr/en/oeuvre/272690).

This dating is materially more precise than the common description “a tenth-century life.” It means that the object first enters the surviving record as part of a hagiographic composition produced roughly a century after Charlemagne’s death and long after the period traditionally assigned to Giles.

A surviving witness formerly belonging to Saint-Père-en-Vallée at Chartres, municipal manuscript 57, fol. 206, is catalogued as a tenth–eleventh-century parchment manuscript containing BHL 93. It was damaged in the bombing of the Chartres library on 26 May 1944; 219 fragments survive. [Catalogue collectif de France, MS 57](https://ccfr.bnf.fr/portailccfr/ark%3A/16871/004D17010282).

Another digitized witness is BnF Latin 13779, fols. 8r–36v. [Biblissima manuscript record](https://iiif.biblissima.fr/collections/manifest/ce1118cc0a8ccd8372baba30ade12ebba9ac8304).

These manuscripts attest copies of the narrative, not the heavenly object.

### 2.3 Historical impossibility of the principal encounter

The traditional chronology of Giles places his death around 700 or in the early eighth century; Charlemagne was born around 742 and died in 814. The two cannot have participated in the narrated Mass.

Possible rationalizations include:

- the “Charles” was **Charles Martel**, Charlemagne’s grandfather;
- the hagiographer attached an older anonymous royal-sinner motif to Charlemagne;
- the story is a deliberate hagiographic construction without a historical royal encounter.

None establishes an actual sheet. The early *Vita* itself calls the ruler *Carolus*, and later medieval reception overwhelmingly understood him as Charlemagne. The National Gallery catalogue describes the *Vita* as chronologically inconsistent and reports the scholarly argument that it was written by a monk of Saint-Gilles early in the tenth century. [National Gallery catalogue, iconography](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/catalogues/campbell-2014/two-panels-from-an-altarpiece).

### 2.4 Probable institutional setting and purpose

The work’s association with Saint-Gilles makes an institutional motive plausible: the story magnifies Giles as an intercessor for sins too shameful to articulate and gives pilgrims a reason to seek his aid. That is a scholarly inference from the text’s provenance and effect, not proof that the episode was invented by a named monk for a documented campaign.

Bédier observed the promotional power of the appended promise: Giles becomes the privileged intercessor for sinners prevented by shame from full confession. The miracle also entered pilgrimage literature associated with the routes to Santiago.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because the object is legendary, its “chain of custody” divides into a **narrative chain**, a **textual chain**, and an **alleged relic chain**.

### 3.1 Narrative chain in the Latin *Vita*

1. **Heaven/divine source → angel.**  
   The sheet appears as a divinely authorized written message carried by an unnamed angel. No maker, writer, or point of production is described.

2. **Angel → altar.**  
   During the Sunday Mass, while Giles prays for Charles, the angel places the sheet on the altar. The wording makes this a deposition, not merely a vision.

3. **Altar → Giles.**  
   Giles reads or receives it. The text does not say who physically lifts it from the altar.

4. **Giles → King Charles.**  
   In the *Golden Legend*, Giles delivers the sheet to the king. Charles repents, confesses, and asks pardon.

5. **After Charles: no early chain.**  
   The principal narrative says nothing about storage, destruction, return to Giles, deposition in a church, or transmission to heirs. The earliest recoverable chain therefore ends with Charles.

No historically verifiable dates, locations, witnesses, or custodial records accompany these links.

### 3.2 Location of the legendary Mass

The early *Vita* does not name the church. It says that Giles had recently visited Sainte-Croix at Orléans, where he healed a demoniac, but that is not the same as saying the Mass occurred there.

Later traditions localized the event in several places:

- **Orléans/Sainte-Croix:** apparently the dominant relic tradition, encouraged by the preceding Orléans episode.
- **Saint-Denis:** Ivo, a monk of Saint-Denis, inserted the words that Giles prayed “in the same church of Saint Denis for the king and in his presence.” Ivo’s *Life of Saint Denis* was commissioned by Abbot Gilles de Pontoise, and a copy was presented to Philip V in 1317.
- **Avignon:** the later epic *Tristan de Nanteuil* places the Mass there.
- **Laon:** the Saint-Giles sheet became attached to local and epic geography, including traditions surrounding Charlemagne and Roland.
- **Saint-Gilles or Giles’s monastery:** some modern devotional retellings relocate the miracle there, but this is not stated by the earliest text.

The National Gallery catalogue stresses that Saint-Denis is supported by only one discovered textual tradition and that the Master of Saint Giles’s painted setting should not be treated as historical topography for the supposed miracle. [National Gallery catalogue](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/catalogues/campbell-2014/two-panels-from-an-altarpiece).

### 3.3 The alleged Orléans relic

The most important material claim is that the *schedula* itself was preserved at the cathedral of Sainte-Croix in Orléans.

Lorne Campbell’s National Gallery catalogue states:

> “the document itself was reported to have remained in the cathedral until the building was destroyed in 1562.”

This is an **institutional or antiquarian tradition**, not a demonstrated continuous provenance. The accessible catalogue does not publish:

- the first inventory in which the object appeared;
- its location within the cathedral treasury;
- a reliquary;
- an inscription or paleographic description;
- dimensions or material;
- any record of its arrival;
- an eyewitness account of its destruction.

The wording “was reported” is accordingly significant. [National Gallery catalogue, lines concerning Orléans](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/catalogues/campbell-2014/two-panels-from-an-altarpiece).

The churches and relics of Orléans were attacked after the city passed into Protestant control in April 1562. The general historical context—profanation of churches and destruction of relics—is documented, but no accessible contemporary destruction record found in this investigation names the *schedula* individually. [Musée protestant, First War of Religion](https://museeprotestant.org/notice/premiere-guerre-de-religion-1562-1563/).

The cathedral was plundered in 1562 and more severely ruined in renewed violence in 1567–68. It is therefore necessary to distinguish:

- **documented:** widespread iconoclasm and relic destruction at Orléans;
- **reported in modern scholarly cataloguing:** loss of the *schedula* in 1562;
- **not established here:** its individual destruction, survival, theft, dispersal, or substitution.

A nineteenth-century web transcription of a guide to Orléans lists “La cédule de l’empereur Charlemagne, apportée à saint Gilles par un ange” among cathedral curiosities. Because this appears in a retrospective catalogue-like passage and conflicts with the claimed 1562 loss, it may reproduce an older inventory or repeat antiquarian lore rather than record inspection of a surviving nineteenth-century object. It cannot establish survival without consulting the printed guide and its sources. [Transcribed guide passage](https://www.visites-p.net/gravure-ancienne-du-jour/orleans-19e.html).

### 3.4 Textual custody

The story’s demonstrable transmission is much fuller than that of any artifact:

- ca. 891–915: anonymous Latin *Vita sancti Aegidii*;
- tenth–fifteenth centuries: approximately ninety recorded Latin manuscripts;
- ca. 1170: Guillaume de Berneville’s 3,794-line Anglo-Norman *Vie de saint Gilles*;
- twelfth century: German *Kaiserchronik* adaptation;
- thirteenth century: liturgical offices, the *Legenda aurea*, the *Karlamagnús saga*, and visual cycles;
- fourteenth century: epics including *Tristan de Nanteuil*;
- ca. 1500: Master of Saint Giles panel;
- print culture: Caxton and later vernacular *Golden Legend* editions;
- modern scholarship, museum interpretation, parish teaching, tourism, and digital databases.

Guillaume’s poem survives principally in Florence, Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Conventi soppressi 99, fols. 111–145, with a fragment in British Library Harley MS 912, fols. 183v–184r. [ARLIMA record](https://www.arlima.net/eh/guillaume_de_berneville.html).

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Did the angelic sheet exist?

No evidence independent of the hagiographic story establishes its existence. There is no contemporary Carolingian reference, no early inventory, no royal archive entry, and no surviving artifact. The chronological impossibility of a meeting between the traditional Giles and Charlemagne strongly undermines a literal reconstruction of the event.

This does not prove that no later physical object was venerated under the name. Medieval churches frequently materialized narrative objects as relics, either through sincere identification, symbolic substitution, or fabricated provenance. For this case, however, the date and mechanism of materialization remain unknown.

### 4.2 Was the Orléans object the sheet in the *Vita*?

No verifiable evidence currently bridges the gap between:

- the tenth-century literary attestation, and
- the later report of an object at Sainte-Croix.

There is no documented custody from Charles to Orléans. Indeed, the *Vita* does not place the Mass at Orléans. The Orléans claim seems to arise from localization of a narrative whose preceding episode occurred there.

The alleged relic’s authenticity therefore cannot be ranked as merely “disputed but possible” on equal terms. It is better classified as:

> **a lost or unverified medieval/early-modern relic claimant whose identification rested on ecclesiastical tradition, with no presently recoverable material proof.**

### 4.3 Absence of scientific testing

No physical candidate is available for:

- radiocarbon dating;
- parchment species identification;
- ink analysis;
- multispectral imaging;
- handwriting comparison;
- DNA or proteomic examination;
- provenance research through surviving treasury labels;
- testing of a reliquary or mount.

No scientific result supports or disproves a particular artifact because no securely identified artifact survives.

### 4.4 Rival locations

Orléans and Saint-Denis are rival narrative localizations, not two extant rival sheets. Ivo’s Saint-Denis version may reflect the abbey’s program of connecting French royal sanctity to its church. Orléans’s claim drew strength from the *Vita*’s preceding Sainte-Croix episode and allegedly became embodied in a treasury object.

Neither localization belongs to the earliest form of the story.

### 4.5 The depicted sheet is not the relic

National Gallery NG4681 is sometimes the source from which modern audiences imagine the object’s appearance. Its painted inscription is late, abbreviated, and created for an image around 1500. The panel has been technically examined, but that examination authenticates and studies the painting, not the legendary sheet.

The same distinction applies to the Chartres glass, Aachen metalwork, wall paintings, manuscripts, and later stained-glass copies.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### God

**Connection:** ultimate authorizing source of the revelation and remission. The angel is “of the Lord,” and the pardon comes from the Lord through Giles’s prayers.  
**Date of association:** ca. 891–915 in the earliest recoverable *Vita*.  
**Tier:** hagiographic claim.

### The unnamed angel

**Connection:** courier who brings and deposits the sheet. The early *Vita* does not identify the angel.  
**Later identification:** some modern and scholarly retellings call the messenger Gabriel, but that name should not be projected into the earliest text without a specific manuscript witness.  
**Tier:** legendary divine actor.

### Saint Giles / Latin *Aegidius* / French *Gilles*

**Connection:** recipient, reader, intercessor, and transmitter of the sheet to Charles. The object establishes his special patronage over difficult confession and remission.  
**Tier:** relation within a tenth-century hagiographic narrative; the saint’s underlying historicity is separate from the miracle’s historicity.

### King Charles / Charlemagne

**Connection:** subject and eventual human recipient. His concealed crime and conditional pardon constitute the document’s content.  
**Identity:** later tradition firmly identifies him as Charlemagne. Chronology makes an encounter with the traditional Giles impossible. Charles Martel has been proposed as a rationalizing alternative, but the textual history is centered on Charlemagne.  
**Tier:** legendary association with a historical ruler.

### Guillaume de Berneville

**Connection:** author of the ca. 1170 Anglo-Norman verse adaptation that transmitted and elaborated the story. He is not a custodian of the object.  
**Tier:** documented literary engagement.

### The author of the *Kaiserchronik*

**Connection:** adapted the episode for a German imperial chronicle and made the ruler’s spiritual crisis part of the moral history of empire.  
**Tier:** documented literary transmission.

### Jacobus de Voragine

**Connection:** condensed the story in the thirteenth-century *Legenda aurea*, one of its most influential transmission vehicles.  
**Tier:** documented literary engagement.

### Ivo of Saint-Denis and Gilles de Pontoise

**Connection:** Ivo inserted Saint-Denis into the story in a work commissioned by Abbot Gilles de Pontoise. A copy was presented to King Philip V in 1317.  
**Tier:** documented textual relocation; not evidence that either possessed the *schedula*.

### Clergy of Sainte-Croix, Orléans

**Connection:** alleged institutional custodians of a relic identified as the sheet.  
**Date:** uncertain; said to continue until 1562.  
**Tier:** later institutional/antiquarian report; the initial acquisition and inventory history remain unestablished.

### Master of Saint Giles

**Connection:** ca. 1500 painter of the most influential surviving image of the sheet. The artist represented it as a small written document descending in an angel’s hands.  
**Tier:** documented artistic engagement.

### Giles’s cult network

The story circulated through:

- the abbey and pilgrimage center of Saint-Gilles-du-Gard;
- churches dedicated to Giles;
- liturgical scriptoria;
- the Santiago pilgrimage network;
- French royal and Charlemagne cults;
- German imperial historiography;
- the Dominican preaching and reading network of the *Legenda aurea*;
- cathedral visual programs at Chartres and Aachen.

The sheet functioned as a portable narrative node joining royal penance, saintly intercession, liturgy, pilgrimage, and written documentary authority.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Function within the legendary Mass

The *schedula* interrupts the ordinary boundary between secret sin and public written knowledge. Its sequence is:

1. Charles cannot or will not name the offense.
2. Giles intercedes during Mass.
3. Heaven converts the concealed matter into writing.
4. The writing appears on the altar, beside the Eucharistic action.
5. It announces remission conditioned on repentance and cessation.
6. Giles confronts or reassures Charles with it.
7. Charles repents and confesses.

The object is thus revelatory and admonitory. It does not allow the king to remain unrepentant merely because Giles has prayed for him.

### 6.2 Confession and absolution

Later devotion made Giles a patron of a good confession and of sinners troubled by shame. Some retellings overstate the episode by saying Giles could dispense with confession. The early narrative is subtler: the sheet reveals the concealed sin, requires repentance and reform, and is followed by the king’s confession.

The story nonetheless sits uneasily beside later sacramental doctrine if construed as automatic forgiveness without full confession. Medieval versions manage this tension by emphasizing contrition, cessation, Giles’s intercession, and Charles’s subsequent acknowledgment.

### 6.3 Written authority

The story employs the cultural force of a document:

- hidden oral knowledge becomes fixed text;
- divine judgment resembles a written legal record;
- pardon resembles a charter or rescript;
- placement on the altar authenticates the writing;
- an angel acts as courier;
- Giles acts as reader and authorized interpreter;
- delivery to Charles completes notification.

Its documentary appearance gives visible certainty to an otherwise invisible spiritual event.

### 6.4 Liturgical commemoration

The *schedula/cartula* became part of antiphons for Giles’s feast on 1 September. Liturgical repetition was an actual historical use of the object’s name and narrative, even if the original object was legendary. [Cantus Database](https://cantusdatabase.org/source/123613/chants/?feast=2517).

### 6.5 Political and dynastic function

In Charlemagne cycles, the sheet simultaneously accuses and rehabilitates the emperor. He has committed an enormous offense, but:

- he recognizes its gravity;
- seeks saintly aid;
- submits to ecclesiastical mediation;
- repents;
- receives divine pardon.

The story therefore supports neither simple royal glorification nor simple denunciation. It places even an emperor under divine and priestly judgment while preserving his sanctified public image.

At Chartres, the Mass scene concludes or crowns a window joining Charlemagne’s legendary eastern expedition, Spanish campaigns, Roland’s death, and royal piety. Modern interpretations connect this arrangement with the construction of Charlemagne as Christian ruler and quasi-saint. [Study of the Chartres cycle](https://www.medievalart.org.uk/PhD/4_Chartres_Charlemagne_Window.html).

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The unnamed sin

**Early attested form:** the *Vita*, Guillaume de Berneville, and the *Golden Legend* refuse to identify the crime. Its unspeakability is structurally essential: the sheet exists because Charles cannot speak.

### 7.2 Incest and the birth of Roland

**Later medieval elaboration:** Charles has intercourse with his sister Gisela/Gisèle; Roland is their child and is publicly treated as Charles’s nephew.

The earliest text explicitly identifying incest is generally identified as branch I of the thirteenth-century Old Norse *Karlamagnús saga*, compiled under or in the milieu of Hákon IV. It probably draws on lost Old French material. The Occitan *Ronsasvals* and later French and Italian epics also make Roland both nephew and son.

In fuller forms, the heavenly message may do more than pardon Charles: it states that the sister is pregnant, orders that the child be named Roland, predicts his future usefulness, and directs Charles to marry his sister to Milon, who will serve as Roland’s ostensible father. This content is not in the earliest *Vita*.

The legend’s relation to earlier images is debated. A fresco around 1200 at Le Loroux-Bottereau and the organization of the Chartres window have been interpreted as showing that the incest story circulated visually before the surviving Norse text. Such interpretations are plausible but not equivalent to an explicit inscription. [Joseph J. Duggan’s source review](https://scholar.lib.vt.edu/ejournals/ElAnt/V14N1/duggan.html); [Robertson on the Chartres interpretation](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/olifant/article/download/19012/25127/42045).

### 7.3 Necrophilia or post-mortem attachment

Later German-associated explanations connect Charles’s offense with abnormal attachment to a dead queen or woman, sometimes caused by a magic ring concealed on or under her tongue. Removal of the ring transfers or redirects the enchantment, and its disposal becomes attached to the legendary foundation or siting of Aachen.

This complex is not part of the early *Vita*’s account of the sheet. Modern summaries sometimes call the hidden sin “necrophilia,” but the precise relationship between individual German narratives, the magic-ring legend, and the Mass of Giles requires source-by-source treatment. It should not be offered as what the angel’s sheet “originally said.”

### 7.4 Sodomy

Some scholarship notes that traditions outside the dominant French incest narrative also interpreted the unnamed offense as sodomy. Again, this is a secondary identification of a deliberately blank narrative space, not a reading preserved in the earliest *schedula* text.

### 7.5 Miraculous erasure

In some later retellings the written marks of the sin vanish during Mass or as pardon is granted. This turns the object from a stable record into a performative image of cancellation: forgiveness is represented by literal erasure.

The early Latin account instead describes a sheet bearing both the offense and the declaration of remission. It does not clearly say that the letters disappear.

### 7.6 The surviving relic at Orléans

The claim that Sainte-Croix possessed the very sheet converts narrative into relic history. It may have encouraged local prestige and pilgrimage. Yet the seam remains visible:

- the early story does not locate the Mass at Orléans;
- the chain from Charles to the cathedral is missing;
- no material description survives in the sources examined;
- the object was reportedly lost during the sixteenth-century destruction.

The effects of the tradition—local identification, catalogue listing, and historical memory—are real even if the artifact’s authenticity is not demonstrable.

### 7.7 Charles Martel substitution

Because Giles and Charlemagne could not have met, some interpreters replace Charlemagne with Charles Martel. This may make the chronology less impossible, but it is a modern or learned rationalization unless supported by a particular medieval witness. It does not authenticate the sheet.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Legendary original

**Status:** not extant; no authenticated physical object is known.

**Location:** none.

**Condition:** unknowable.

**Ownership or legal control:** none applicable.

**Scientific access:** impossible in the absence of an object.

### 8.2 Alleged Orléans relic

**Last reported location:** treasury or ecclesiastical custody of Sainte-Croix Cathedral, Orléans.

**Reported fate:** destroyed or lost when the cathedral and its contents were attacked in 1562.

**Certainty:** the relic’s individual destruction is not independently established by the accessible evidence. It may have been destroyed, carried away, concealed, or never have existed as the old tradition claimed. No serious modern location hypothesis or rival extant claimant was found.

### 8.3 Surviving material stand-ins

#### The Chartres window

The Charlemagne window, bay 7 of Chartres Cathedral’s north ambulatory, contains a scene of the angel and sheet. The French Ministry of Culture dates the window traditionally to about **1225**, describes twenty-four panels, and records restorations by the Lorin workshop in 1921 and again in 1999. The window is property of the French state and was protected in the 1840 list. [French Ministry of Culture record IM28000516](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/palissy/IM28000516).

Panel 22 is commonly identified as the Mass of Saint Giles, with an angel handing the priest the written confession or pardon. [Chartres bay guide](https://www.medievalart.org.uk/Chartres/07_pages/Chartres_Bay07_key.html).

#### National Gallery NG4681

The Master of Saint Giles’s *Mass of Saint Giles* is in the National Gallery, London, accession **NG4681**. It is the most detailed surviving late-medieval visualization of the *schedula*. The panel entered the National Gallery in 1933 through the National Art-Collections Fund following the death of Lady Seaforth.

The painting is in generally excellent condition on the obverse, although the green curtain between the king and altar is seriously damaged. The reverse, showing Saint Peter in grisaille, is much less well preserved. It was treated in 1933, suffered four small accidental damages at the Gallery on 16 August 1933, and was cleaned on both sides in 1974–75.

Dendrochronology identified western European oak: dated rings in the boards extend into 1463. Examination has included microscopy, infrared reflectography, X-radiography, pigment sampling, gas chromatography, and GC–MS. Identified materials include chalk in animal glue, walnut-oil medium, azurite, verdigris, lead-tin yellow, lead white, vermilion, red lake derived from madder and kermes, and powdered glass. These findings concern the representation, not the legendary relic. [Full National Gallery technical catalogue](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/catalogues/campbell-2014/two-panels-from-an-altarpiece).

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 The Master of Saint Giles painting

NG4681 has largely displaced the absent relic in modern visual culture. Its angel, pale sheet, Latin inscription, kneeling emperor, and richly reconstructed Saint-Denis interior have supplied details the earliest text lacks.

The painting’s subject was not always recognized:

- 1752, 1756, and 1789: attributed to Dürer;
- 1756: Pierre-Jean Mariette misread the sheet’s inscription approximately as “God save Charles” and connected it with Charles the Bold;
- Richard Cosway described it as Thomas Aquinas saying Mass at Saint-Denis for Louis IX;
- other nineteenth-century cataloguers called it a Mass of Saint Gregory;
- W. H. James Weale correctly identified the Mass of Saint Giles in 1889;
- Hugo von Tschudi’s study of the related panels established the conventional name “Master of Saint Giles.”

The painted document itself was thus decisive in recovering the picture’s actual iconography, but only after centuries of misidentification.

### 9.2 Copies and derivatives

The National Gallery catalogue records:

- a colored copy made by John Carter when the picture was sold in 1789;
- a stained-glass copy by William Raphael Eginton, made before 1817 for Dudley House;
- Viollet-le-Duc’s 1859 architectural illustration of the altar without the figures;
- an altar, retable, and cross installed at All Saints, Brixworth, in or after 1896, probably based on Viollet-le-Duc’s illustration.

These are copies or architectural derivatives of NG4681, not relic claimants.

### 9.3 Chartres conservation and tourism

The Chartres scene remains publicly visible within the cathedral’s Charlemagne cycle. Modern guides often state that the sheet revealed incest and Roland’s birth, although the glass contains no surviving verbal statement to that effect. The explicit incest narrative comes from associated texts and iconographic interpretation.

### 9.4 Contemporary devotional use

Parishes and pilgrimage organizations continue to retell the miracle on Giles’s feast. The object remains a symbol of:

- sins difficult to confess;
- divine knowledge of concealed guilt;
- conditional mercy;
- the efficacy of saintly intercession;
- the necessity of repentance.

Modern pastoral retellings sometimes flatten medieval distinctions by saying that Giles simply “forgave Charlemagne” or that the paper was an unconditional absolution. Those are interpretations, not exact statements of the early source.

### 9.5 Popular embellishment

The identification of the crime as incest or necrophilia is now often presented without indicating its late and variant character. The name Gabriel is likewise frequently supplied to the angel. Both should be labeled later elaborations unless tied to a specified witness.

No consequential modern hoax, commercial fake, auction claimant, occult cult object, or repatriation dispute centered on an extant *schedula* was found.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Editions and textual study

The foundational printed source remains the Bollandist edition of the *Vita* in *Acta Sanctorum*, 1 September, pp. 299–304. The IRHT classifies the textual family under BHL 93–98b and records about ninety manuscripts.

Gaston Paris and Alphonse Bos’s 1881 edition of Guillaume de Berneville’s *Vie de saint Gilles* made the Anglo-Norman text and its Latin relationships available for modern study. Françoise Laurent published a modern bilingual edition in 2003.

### 10.2 Dating of the *Vita*

Older scholarship commonly described the work broadly as tenth or early eleventh century. The current FAMA entry’s 891–915 range supports composition around the turn of the tenth century. The National Gallery catalogue accepts the argument for an early-tenth-century monk of Saint-Gilles.

This is the date of the narrative’s recoverable composition, not of the supposed object.

### 10.3 The king’s identity

The central problem is chronological. Proposed positions include:

- the story always meant Charlemagne and ignored chronology;
- an older “King Charles” story concerned Charles Martel and was transferred to Charlemagne;
- Charles is a generic great ruler absorbed into Carolingian legend.

Medieval art and literature increasingly integrated the episode into Charlemagne’s biography, making later reception clearer than the story’s hypothetical prehistory.

### 10.4 Nature of the sin

The earliest text’s silence generated several traditions:

- incest with a sister and conception of Roland;
- necrophilic or post-mortem sexual attachment;
- sodomy;
- an unspecified enormous crime.

The incest tradition became especially useful for epic genealogy: Roland could be both the emperor’s son and nephew while remaining wholly Carolingian.

Joseph J. Duggan emphasizes that explicit identification appears in the thirteenth-century *Karlamagnús saga*, while earlier visual arrangements may presuppose the story. [Duggan](https://scholar.lib.vt.edu/ejournals/ElAnt/V14N1/duggan.html).

### 10.5 Chartres: text and image

The Charlemagne window’s sources and program remain debated. Clark Maines and later scholars have examined how the window combines several narrative complexes rather than simply illustrating one book. The juxtaposition of the Mass with Roland’s death may imply the incest genealogy, but the image itself does not explicitly state it.

The Ministry of Culture dates the window around 1225. Other specialists use ca. 1210–25. [French Ministry of Culture](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/palissy/IM28000516); [Chartres study](https://www.medievalart.org.uk/PhD/4_Chartres_Charlemagne_Window.html).

### 10.6 Saint-Denis versus Orléans

The early *Vita* names neither location for the Mass. The competing localizations arose later:

- Orléans through narrative proximity and the alleged relic;
- Saint-Denis through Ivo’s textual insertion and French royal imagery;
- further locations in epic adaptations.

The Master of Saint Giles’s choice of Saint-Denis cannot settle the historical question. The painting is filled with deliberate anachronisms, including monuments and objects made centuries after Charlemagne.

### 10.7 The Orléans relic problem

The alleged physical relic is the least adequately documented major issue. Future research should seek:

- medieval and early-modern inventories of Sainte-Croix;
- cathedral chapter accounts and visitation records;
- sixteenth-century descriptions of the treasury;
- Protestant and Catholic narratives of the 1562 sack;
- antiquarian histories of Orléans;
- the exact source behind Campbell’s statement;
- the printed source underlying the nineteenth-century online guide passage.

Until those are examined, both “the relic existed” and “it was destroyed in 1562” remain insufficiently resolved propositions.

### 10.8 Art-historical study of NG4681

The National Gallery’s 2014 catalogue by Lorne Campbell provides the fullest open-access object study of the painted representation. It integrates provenance, dendrochronology, infrared examination, paint analysis, conservation history, inscription study, iconography, and reconstruction of the dispersed altarpiece.

The catalogue rejects or treats skeptically several attempts to identify portraits and a specific royal commission. It also warns that the archaeological accuracy of the Saint-Denis interior should not be inflated into proof of the painting’s original location or patronage.

# The angel's *schedula* delivered to Saint Giles: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conclusion

The “angel’s *schedula*” is the written object in the medieval legend commonly called the Mass of Saint Giles: while Giles celebrates Mass for a King Charles burdened by an unconfessed crime, an angel places a small written sheet on the altar. It identifies the crime, announces conditional forgiveness through Giles’s intercession, and, in the fullest early version, extends a promise of forgiveness to other repentant sinners who invoke the saint. Giles subsequently gives the sheet to the king.

Three analytically distinct objects have acquired this identity:

1. **The narrative *schedula*** in the Latin *Vita sancti Aegidii* and its numerous adaptations. This is securely attested as a literary object from about 891–915, not as a surviving early-medieval artifact.
2. **The alleged relic at Orléans**, reportedly preserved at the cathedral of Sainte-Croix until the religious violence of 1562. Its existence is reported in later scholarship, but no surviving inventory entry, physical description, image, or securely traced fragment was located in the accessible evidence surveyed here.
3. **Material representations of the legendary sheet**, especially the thirteenth-century Charlemagne window at Chartres and the painted sheet in the Master of Saint Giles’s *Mass of Saint Giles*, now National Gallery, London, NG4681. These are real artifacts depicting the legendary object; neither is the original *schedula* or a surviving fragment of it.

The primary conclusion is therefore:

> **The angelic original is a legendary object. A later physical claimant was reportedly kept at Orléans, but its antiquity and identity cannot now be tested and its very documentation remains incomplete. No authenticated *schedula*, fragment, reliquary, photograph, drawing, measurements, or scientific examination is presently known.**

The dossier accordingly follows both the legendary object’s textual “custody” and the much weaker historical trail of the alleged Orléans relic, while keeping the two separate.

---

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Principal names

The earliest tradition does not give the object a unique proper name. It identifies it by an ordinary noun:

- **Latin:** *schedula*, accusative *schedulam* — a small sheet, slip, note, or written memorandum.
- **Variant Latin in liturgical witnesses:** *cartula* or *kartula* — a little charter, paper, or written sheet.
- **Medieval and modern French:** *cédule*, *feuille*, *billet*, *parchemin*; commonly *la cédule de saint Gilles* or *la cédule de Charlemagne*.
- **Middle English, in William Caxton’s translation of the *Golden Legend*:** “a schedule.”
- **German:** *Zettel*, *Sündenzettel*, *Brief*; the episode is sometimes described as the *Ägidiusmesse*.
- **English:** “the angel’s scroll,” “the angelic letter,” “the schedule of Saint Giles,” “Charlemagne’s confession,” or “the document of absolution.”

Latin *schedula* is a diminutive of *scheda/scida*, originally a strip or leaf of writing material. Its sense is therefore “little sheet” or “slip,” not the modern English sense of a timetable. The word by itself does not determine whether the support was papyrus, parchment, or paper. [Etymonline’s lexical history](https://www.etymonline.com/word/schedule) traces Late Latin *schedula* through Old French *cedule* to Middle English *sedule/cedule*.

The designation “Charlemagne’s confession” is potentially misleading. In the principal Latin narrative the sheet is not written by Charles and is not itself his sacramental confession. It is delivered from heaven, records the sin, and declares the terms of remission. Some later retellings blur these functions.

### 1.2 Class

The object belongs simultaneously to several medieval categories:

- a **miraculously delivered written message**;
- an **instrument of revelation**, disclosing what the king refuses to utter;
- an **instrument or notice of conditional remission**;
- in later reception, a purported **contact relic or heavenly autograph**;
- iconographically, a **scroll, sheet, letter, or phylactery**;
- liturgically, the central attribute of the **Mass of Saint Giles** episode.

It is not an indulgence in the later technical sense, nor is it presented as a papal charter, sealed diploma, sacramental formula, or material whose touch alone effects forgiveness.

### 1.3 Description in the earliest recoverable text

The Latin *Vita sancti Aegidii*, BHL 93–98b, dated by the IRHT’s FAMA database to approximately **891–915**, describes an angel placing a *schedula* on the altar during Mass. The sheet contains:

1. the king’s sin, written out in sequence or detail;
2. a statement that Giles’s prayers have obtained its remission;
3. conditions: the king must repent and cease committing it;
4. an appended general promise concerning sinners who invoke Giles and abandon their sin.

The episode is frequently summarized from the Latin as:

> On the following Sunday, while the holy man celebrated Mass and prayed for the king at the canon, the angel of the Lord appeared and placed on the altar a small sheet in which the king’s sin was set out in detail. It stated that through Giles’s prayers the sin was remitted, provided that the king repented and abstained from it thereafter.

Joseph Bédier reproduced and translated the substance of the passage, including the decisive noun *schedulam*, in his study of the Charlemagne legends. He also noted the concluding promise that any sinner who invoked Giles and ceased committing the offense could trust in divine remission. [Bédier’s discussion and quotation](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/0d/Les_l%C3%A9gendes_%C3%A9piques%3B_recherches_sur_la_formation_des_chansons_de_geste_%28IA_leslegendesepiqu03bedi%29.pdf).

No early source specifies:

- dimensions;
- weight;
- color;
- the animal source or preparation of any parchment;
- ink composition;
- handwriting or script;
- language;
- seal;
- folding pattern;
- container or reliquary;
- the angel’s name;
- what ultimately happened to the sheet after Giles gave it to Charles.

“Parchment” is a reasonable artistic convention but cannot be treated as a fact established by the earliest text.

### 1.4 Liturgical formulations

The legend entered the Office of Saint Giles. Cantus preserves, among others, the antiphon:

> *Beato Aegidio pro Carolo rege inter missam supplicante domino peccatum regis et venia peccati per cartulam delata est ab angelo.*

“While blessed Giles was supplicating the Lord during Mass for King Charles, the king’s sin and pardon for the sin were brought by an angel on a little sheet.”

A following antiphon reads:

> *Adjunctum erat in fine cartulae quod sancti Aegidii meritis quilibet peccator absolvatur a crimine si ulterius non vult admittere.*

“It was added at the end of the sheet that, through the merits of Saint Giles, any sinner should be absolved from the offense if he does not wish to commit it further.”

These chants preserve both the object’s delivery and the appended universal promise. [Cantus manuscript entry and transcription](https://cantusdatabase.org/source/123613/chants/?feast=2517).

A related liturgical series gives:

> *Orante sancto Aegidio super altare schedula ei posita est ab angelo Dei.*

“While Saint Giles was praying, a small sheet was placed for him upon the altar by an angel of God.”

It continues:

> *In ea unum regis peccatum apte legebat quod scienter reticuerat quando confitebatur.*

“On it he clearly read a sin of the king which [the king] had knowingly withheld when confessing.”

[Cantus Index, Office of Aegidius](https://cantusindex.uwaterloo.ca/feast/14090100?field_full_text_value=&page=1).

These are important variants. They make the object part of public liturgical memory and sharpen the offense from a sin the king “dared not confess” to one knowingly withheld in confession.

### 1.5 Description in the *Golden Legend*

Jacobus de Voragine’s thirteenth-century *Legenda aurea* closely abridges the older *Vita*. In Caxton’s English version:

> “the angel of our Lord appeared to him, and laid a schedule upon the altar where the sin of the king was written in by order”

The sheet also records the pardon, conditional upon repentance and abstention, and the promise to other repentant sinners. Giles then “delivered the schedule to the king,” after which the king confesses and asks pardon. [Caxton’s *Golden Legend*, “Saint Giles”](https://ccel.org/ccel/voragine/goldleg5/goldleg5.xiii.html).

The *Golden Legend* therefore supplies the last clear custody event within the principal narrative: **altar → Giles → King Charles**.

### 1.6 The painted object in National Gallery NG4681

The best-known physical representation appears in the Master of Saint Giles’s *Mass of Saint Giles*, painted around 1500. The angel holds a small pale written sheet inscribed:

> **EGIDI ME / RITO RE / MISSA SUNT / PECCATA / KAROLO**

Normalized:

> *Egidi merito remissa sunt peccata Karolo.*

“By the merit of Giles, sins have been remitted to Charles.”

The wording is a compressed visual label, not a transcription of the longer text described in the *Vita*. The painting shows only the declaration of remission; it does not visibly reproduce the sin, its conditions, or the general promise to other sinners.

The panel is oil on three boards of western European oak, 62.3 × 46.0 cm; painted surface 61.6 × 45.5 cm. The depicted *schedula* itself has no published measurements because it is an image within the painting. Scientific and conservation examinations concern the panel, not an actual relic. [National Gallery catalogue, identification and inscriptions](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/catalogues/campbell-2014/two-panels-from-an-altarpiece).

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Traditional origin

**Legendary claim:** the sheet originates in heaven or with God and is carried by “the angel of the Lord” to Giles during Mass. The text does not describe manufacture. It does not say an angel physically wrote it, although later writers and images naturally treated it as a heavenly document.

The message’s attributed authority is divine, but its ostensible documentary form is recognizably medieval: a written instrument publicly delivered to an altar and then shown to a ruler.

### 2.2 Earliest securely datable attestation

The earliest presently identifiable source is the anonymous Latin *Vita sancti Aegidii*, associated by scholarship with the abbey of Saint-Gilles in southern France. The IRHT dates the work **891–915**, identifies it under BHL 93–98b, and records approximately ninety manuscripts. [IRHT-CNRS FAMA record](https://fama.irht.cnrs.fr/en/oeuvre/272690).

This dating is materially more precise than the common description “a tenth-century life.” It means that the object first enters the surviving record as part of a hagiographic composition produced roughly a century after Charlemagne’s death and long after the period traditionally assigned to Giles.

A surviving witness formerly belonging to Saint-Père-en-Vallée at Chartres, municipal manuscript 57, fol. 206, is catalogued as a tenth–eleventh-century parchment manuscript containing BHL 93. It was damaged in the bombing of the Chartres library on 26 May 1944; 219 fragments survive. [Catalogue collectif de France, MS 57](https://ccfr.bnf.fr/portailccfr/ark%3A/16871/004D17010282).

Another digitized witness is BnF Latin 13779, fols. 8r–36v. [Biblissima manuscript record](https://iiif.biblissima.fr/collections/manifest/ce1118cc0a8ccd8372baba30ade12ebba9ac8304).

These manuscripts attest copies of the narrative, not the heavenly object.

### 2.3 Historical impossibility of the principal encounter

The traditional chronology of Giles places his death around 700 or in the early eighth century; Charlemagne was born around 742 and died in 814. The two cannot have participated in the narrated Mass.

Possible rationalizations include:

- the “Charles” was **Charles Martel**, Charlemagne’s grandfather;
- the hagiographer attached an older anonymous royal-sinner motif to Charlemagne;
- the story is a deliberate hagiographic construction without a historical royal encounter.

None establishes an actual sheet. The early *Vita* itself calls the ruler *Carolus*, and later medieval reception overwhelmingly understood him as Charlemagne. The National Gallery catalogue describes the *Vita* as chronologically inconsistent and reports the scholarly argument that it was written by a monk of Saint-Gilles early in the tenth century. [National Gallery catalogue, iconography](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/catalogues/campbell-2014/two-panels-from-an-altarpiece).

### 2.4 Probable institutional setting and purpose

The work’s association with Saint-Gilles makes an institutional motive plausible: the story magnifies Giles as an intercessor for sins too shameful to articulate and gives pilgrims a reason to seek his aid. That is a scholarly inference from the text’s provenance and effect, not proof that the episode was invented by a named monk for a documented campaign.

Bédier observed the promotional power of the appended promise: Giles becomes the privileged intercessor for sinners prevented by shame from full confession. The miracle also entered pilgrimage literature associated with the routes to Santiago.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because the object is legendary, its “chain of custody” divides into a **narrative chain**, a **textual chain**, and an **alleged relic chain**.

### 3.1 Narrative chain in the Latin *Vita*

1. **Heaven/divine source → angel.**  
   The sheet appears as a divinely authorized written message carried by an unnamed angel. No maker, writer, or point of production is described.

2. **Angel → altar.**  
   During the Sunday Mass, while Giles prays for Charles, the angel places the sheet on the altar. The wording makes this a deposition, not merely a vision.

3. **Altar → Giles.**  
   Giles reads or receives it. The text does not say who physically lifts it from the altar.

4. **Giles → King Charles.**  
   In the *Golden Legend*, Giles delivers the sheet to the king. Charles repents, confesses, and asks pardon.

5. **After Charles: no early chain.**  
   The principal narrative says nothing about storage, destruction, return to Giles, deposition in a church, or transmission to heirs. The earliest recoverable chain therefore ends with Charles.

No historically verifiable dates, locations, witnesses, or custodial records accompany these links.

### 3.2 Location of the legendary Mass

The early *Vita* does not name the church. It says that Giles had recently visited Sainte-Croix at Orléans, where he healed a demoniac, but that is not the same as saying the Mass occurred there.

Later traditions localized the event in several places:

- **Orléans/Sainte-Croix:** apparently the dominant relic tradition, encouraged by the preceding Orléans episode.
- **Saint-Denis:** Ivo, a monk of Saint-Denis, inserted the words that Giles prayed “in the same church of Saint Denis for the king and in his presence.” Ivo’s *Life of Saint Denis* was commissioned by Abbot Gilles de Pontoise, and a copy was presented to Philip V in 1317.
- **Avignon:** the later epic *Tristan de Nanteuil* places the Mass there.
- **Laon:** the Saint-Giles sheet became attached to local and epic geography, including traditions surrounding Charlemagne and Roland.
- **Saint-Gilles or Giles’s monastery:** some modern devotional retellings relocate the miracle there, but this is not stated by the earliest text.

The National Gallery catalogue stresses that Saint-Denis is supported by only one discovered textual tradition and that the Master of Saint Giles’s painted setting should not be treated as historical topography for the supposed miracle. [National Gallery catalogue](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/catalogues/campbell-2014/two-panels-from-an-altarpiece).

### 3.3 The alleged Orléans relic

The most important material claim is that the *schedula* itself was preserved at the cathedral of Sainte-Croix in Orléans.

Lorne Campbell’s National Gallery catalogue states:

> “the document itself was reported to have remained in the cathedral until the building was destroyed in 1562.”

This is an **institutional or antiquarian tradition**, not a demonstrated continuous provenance. The accessible catalogue does not publish:

- the first inventory in which the object appeared;
- its location within the cathedral treasury;
- a reliquary;
- an inscription or paleographic description;
- dimensions or material;
- any record of its arrival;
- an eyewitness account of its destruction.

The wording “was reported” is accordingly significant. [National Gallery catalogue, lines concerning Orléans](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/catalogues/campbell-2014/two-panels-from-an-altarpiece).

The churches and relics of Orléans were attacked after the city passed into Protestant control in April 1562. The general historical context—profanation of churches and destruction of relics—is documented, but no accessible contemporary destruction record found in this investigation names the *schedula* individually. [Musée protestant, First War of Religion](https://museeprotestant.org/notice/premiere-guerre-de-religion-1562-1563/).

The cathedral was plundered in 1562 and more severely ruined in renewed violence in 1567–68. It is therefore necessary to distinguish:

- **documented:** widespread iconoclasm and relic destruction at Orléans;
- **reported in modern scholarly cataloguing:** loss of the *schedula* in 1562;
- **not established here:** its individual destruction, survival, theft, dispersal, or substitution.

A nineteenth-century web transcription of a guide to Orléans lists “La cédule de l’empereur Charlemagne, apportée à saint Gilles par un ange” among cathedral curiosities. Because this appears in a retrospective catalogue-like passage and conflicts with the claimed 1562 loss, it may reproduce an older inventory or repeat antiquarian lore rather than record inspection of a surviving nineteenth-century object. It cannot establish survival without consulting the printed guide and its sources. [Transcribed guide passage](https://www.visites-p.net/gravure-ancienne-du-jour/orleans-19e.html).

### 3.4 Textual custody

The story’s demonstrable transmission is much fuller than that of any artifact:

- ca. 891–915: anonymous Latin *Vita sancti Aegidii*;
- tenth–fifteenth centuries: approximately ninety recorded Latin manuscripts;
- ca. 1170: Guillaume de Berneville’s 3,794-line Anglo-Norman *Vie de saint Gilles*;
- twelfth century: German *Kaiserchronik* adaptation;
- thirteenth century: liturgical offices, the *Legenda aurea*, the *Karlamagnús saga*, and visual cycles;
- fourteenth century: epics including *Tristan de Nanteuil*;
- ca. 1500: Master of Saint Giles panel;
- print culture: Caxton and later vernacular *Golden Legend* editions;
- modern scholarship, museum interpretation, parish teaching, tourism, and digital databases.

Guillaume’s poem survives principally in Florence, Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Conventi soppressi 99, fols. 111–145, with a fragment in British Library Harley MS 912, fols. 183v–184r. [ARLIMA record](https://www.arlima.net/eh/guillaume_de_berneville.html).

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Did the angelic sheet exist?

No evidence independent of the hagiographic story establishes its existence. There is no contemporary Carolingian reference, no early inventory, no royal archive entry, and no surviving artifact. The chronological impossibility of a meeting between the traditional Giles and Charlemagne strongly undermines a literal reconstruction of the event.

This does not prove that no later physical object was venerated under the name. Medieval churches frequently materialized narrative objects as relics, either through sincere identification, symbolic substitution, or fabricated provenance. For this case, however, the date and mechanism of materialization remain unknown.

### 4.2 Was the Orléans object the sheet in the *Vita*?

No verifiable evidence currently bridges the gap between:

- the tenth-century literary attestation, and
- the later report of an object at Sainte-Croix.

There is no documented custody from Charles to Orléans. Indeed, the *Vita* does not place the Mass at Orléans. The Orléans claim seems to arise from localization of a narrative whose preceding episode occurred there.

The alleged relic’s authenticity therefore cannot be ranked as merely “disputed but possible” on equal terms. It is better classified as:

> **a lost or unverified medieval/early-modern relic claimant whose identification rested on ecclesiastical tradition, with no presently recoverable material proof.**

### 4.3 Absence of scientific testing

No physical candidate is available for:

- radiocarbon dating;
- parchment species identification;
- ink analysis;
- multispectral imaging;
- handwriting comparison;
- DNA or proteomic examination;
- provenance research through surviving treasury labels;
- testing of a reliquary or mount.

No scientific result supports or disproves a particular artifact because no securely identified artifact survives.

### 4.4 Rival locations

Orléans and Saint-Denis are rival narrative localizations, not two extant rival sheets. Ivo’s Saint-Denis version may reflect the abbey’s program of connecting French royal sanctity to its church. Orléans’s claim drew strength from the *Vita*’s preceding Sainte-Croix episode and allegedly became embodied in a treasury object.

Neither localization belongs to the earliest form of the story.

### 4.5 The depicted sheet is not the relic

National Gallery NG4681 is sometimes the source from which modern audiences imagine the object’s appearance. Its painted inscription is late, abbreviated, and created for an image around 1500. The panel has been technically examined, but that examination authenticates and studies the painting, not the legendary sheet.

The same distinction applies to the Chartres glass, Aachen metalwork, wall paintings, manuscripts, and later stained-glass copies.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### God

**Connection:** ultimate authorizing source of the revelation and remission. The angel is “of the Lord,” and the pardon comes from the Lord through Giles’s prayers.  
**Date of association:** ca. 891–915 in the earliest recoverable *Vita*.  
**Tier:** hagiographic claim.

### The unnamed angel

**Connection:** courier who brings and deposits the sheet. The early *Vita* does not identify the angel.  
**Later identification:** some modern and scholarly retellings call the messenger Gabriel, but that name should not be projected into the earliest text without a specific manuscript witness.  
**Tier:** legendary divine actor.

### Saint Giles / Latin *Aegidius* / French *Gilles*

**Connection:** recipient, reader, intercessor, and transmitter of the sheet to Charles. The object establishes his special patronage over difficult confession and remission.  
**Tier:** relation within a tenth-century hagiographic narrative; the saint’s underlying historicity is separate from the miracle’s historicity.

### King Charles / Charlemagne

**Connection:** subject and eventual human recipient. His concealed crime and conditional pardon constitute the document’s content.  
**Identity:** later tradition firmly identifies him as Charlemagne. Chronology makes an encounter with the traditional Giles impossible. Charles Martel has been proposed as a rationalizing alternative, but the textual history is centered on Charlemagne.  
**Tier:** legendary association with a historical ruler.

### Guillaume de Berneville

**Connection:** author of the ca. 1170 Anglo-Norman verse adaptation that transmitted and elaborated the story. He is not a custodian of the object.  
**Tier:** documented literary engagement.

### The author of the *Kaiserchronik*

**Connection:** adapted the episode for a German imperial chronicle and made the ruler’s spiritual crisis part of the moral history of empire.  
**Tier:** documented literary transmission.

### Jacobus de Voragine

**Connection:** condensed the story in the thirteenth-century *Legenda aurea*, one of its most influential transmission vehicles.  
**Tier:** documented literary engagement.

### Ivo of Saint-Denis and Gilles de Pontoise

**Connection:** Ivo inserted Saint-Denis into the story in a work commissioned by Abbot Gilles de Pontoise. A copy was presented to King Philip V in 1317.  
**Tier:** documented textual relocation; not evidence that either possessed the *schedula*.

### Clergy of Sainte-Croix, Orléans

**Connection:** alleged institutional custodians of a relic identified as the sheet.  
**Date:** uncertain; said to continue until 1562.  
**Tier:** later institutional/antiquarian report; the initial acquisition and inventory history remain unestablished.

### Master of Saint Giles

**Connection:** ca. 1500 painter of the most influential surviving image of the sheet. The artist represented it as a small written document descending in an angel’s hands.  
**Tier:** documented artistic engagement.

### Giles’s cult network

The story circulated through:

- the abbey and pilgrimage center of Saint-Gilles-du-Gard;
- churches dedicated to Giles;
- liturgical scriptoria;
- the Santiago pilgrimage network;
- French royal and Charlemagne cults;
- German imperial historiography;
- the Dominican preaching and reading network of the *Legenda aurea*;
- cathedral visual programs at Chartres and Aachen.

The sheet functioned as a portable narrative node joining royal penance, saintly intercession, liturgy, pilgrimage, and written documentary authority.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Function within the legendary Mass

The *schedula* interrupts the ordinary boundary between secret sin and public written knowledge. Its sequence is:

1. Charles cannot or will not name the offense.
2. Giles intercedes during Mass.
3. Heaven converts the concealed matter into writing.
4. The writing appears on the altar, beside the Eucharistic action.
5. It announces remission conditioned on repentance and cessation.
6. Giles confronts or reassures Charles with it.
7. Charles repents and confesses.

The object is thus revelatory and admonitory. It does not allow the king to remain unrepentant merely because Giles has prayed for him.

### 6.2 Confession and absolution

Later devotion made Giles a patron of a good confession and of sinners troubled by shame. Some retellings overstate the episode by saying Giles could dispense with confession. The early narrative is subtler: the sheet reveals the concealed sin, requires repentance and reform, and is followed by the king’s confession.

The story nonetheless sits uneasily beside later sacramental doctrine if construed as automatic forgiveness without full confession. Medieval versions manage this tension by emphasizing contrition, cessation, Giles’s intercession, and Charles’s subsequent acknowledgment.

### 6.3 Written authority

The story employs the cultural force of a document:

- hidden oral knowledge becomes fixed text;
- divine judgment resembles a written legal record;
- pardon resembles a charter or rescript;
- placement on the altar authenticates the writing;
- an angel acts as courier;
- Giles acts as reader and authorized interpreter;
- delivery to Charles completes notification.

Its documentary appearance gives visible certainty to an otherwise invisible spiritual event.

### 6.4 Liturgical commemoration

The *schedula/cartula* became part of antiphons for Giles’s feast on 1 September. Liturgical repetition was an actual historical use of the object’s name and narrative, even if the original object was legendary. [Cantus Database](https://cantusdatabase.org/source/123613/chants/?feast=2517).

### 6.5 Political and dynastic function

In Charlemagne cycles, the sheet simultaneously accuses and rehabilitates the emperor. He has committed an enormous offense, but:

- he recognizes its gravity;
- seeks saintly aid;
- submits to ecclesiastical mediation;
- repents;
- receives divine pardon.

The story therefore supports neither simple royal glorification nor simple denunciation. It places even an emperor under divine and priestly judgment while preserving his sanctified public image.

At Chartres, the Mass scene concludes or crowns a window joining Charlemagne’s legendary eastern expedition, Spanish campaigns, Roland’s death, and royal piety. Modern interpretations connect this arrangement with the construction of Charlemagne as Christian ruler and quasi-saint. [Study of the Chartres cycle](https://www.medievalart.org.uk/PhD/4_Chartres_Charlemagne_Window.html).

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The unnamed sin

**Early attested form:** the *Vita*, Guillaume de Berneville, and the *Golden Legend* refuse to identify the crime. Its unspeakability is structurally essential: the sheet exists because Charles cannot speak.

### 7.2 Incest and the birth of Roland

**Later medieval elaboration:** Charles has intercourse with his sister Gisela/Gisèle; Roland is their child and is publicly treated as Charles’s nephew.

The earliest text explicitly identifying incest is generally identified as branch I of the thirteenth-century Old Norse *Karlamagnús saga*, compiled under or in the milieu of Hákon IV. It probably draws on lost Old French material. The Occitan *Ronsasvals* and later French and Italian epics also make Roland both nephew and son.

In fuller forms, the heavenly message may do more than pardon Charles: it states that the sister is pregnant, orders that the child be named Roland, predicts his future usefulness, and directs Charles to marry his sister to Milon, who will serve as Roland’s ostensible father. This content is not in the earliest *Vita*.

The legend’s relation to earlier images is debated. A fresco around 1200 at Le Loroux-Bottereau and the organization of the Chartres window have been interpreted as showing that the incest story circulated visually before the surviving Norse text. Such interpretations are plausible but not equivalent to an explicit inscription. [Joseph J. Duggan’s source review](https://scholar.lib.vt.edu/ejournals/ElAnt/V14N1/duggan.html); [Robertson on the Chartres interpretation](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/olifant/article/download/19012/25127/42045).

### 7.3 Necrophilia or post-mortem attachment

Later German-associated explanations connect Charles’s offense with abnormal attachment to a dead queen or woman, sometimes caused by a magic ring concealed on or under her tongue. Removal of the ring transfers or redirects the enchantment, and its disposal becomes attached to the legendary foundation or siting of Aachen.

This complex is not part of the early *Vita*’s account of the sheet. Modern summaries sometimes call the hidden sin “necrophilia,” but the precise relationship between individual German narratives, the magic-ring legend, and the Mass of Giles requires source-by-source treatment. It should not be offered as what the angel’s sheet “originally said.”

### 7.4 Sodomy

Some scholarship notes that traditions outside the dominant French incest narrative also interpreted the unnamed offense as sodomy. Again, this is a secondary identification of a deliberately blank narrative space, not a reading preserved in the earliest *schedula* text.

### 7.5 Miraculous erasure

In some later retellings the written marks of the sin vanish during Mass or as pardon is granted. This turns the object from a stable record into a performative image of cancellation: forgiveness is represented by literal erasure.

The early Latin account instead describes a sheet bearing both the offense and the declaration of remission. It does not clearly say that the letters disappear.

### 7.6 The surviving relic at Orléans

The claim that Sainte-Croix possessed the very sheet converts narrative into relic history. It may have encouraged local prestige and pilgrimage. Yet the seam remains visible:

- the early story does not locate the Mass at Orléans;
- the chain from Charles to the cathedral is missing;
- no material description survives in the sources examined;
- the object was reportedly lost during the sixteenth-century destruction.

The effects of the tradition—local identification, catalogue listing, and historical memory—are real even if the artifact’s authenticity is not demonstrable.

### 7.7 Charles Martel substitution

Because Giles and Charlemagne could not have met, some interpreters replace Charlemagne with Charles Martel. This may make the chronology less impossible, but it is a modern or learned rationalization unless supported by a particular medieval witness. It does not authenticate the sheet.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Legendary original

**Status:** not extant; no authenticated physical object is known.

**Location:** none.

**Condition:** unknowable.

**Ownership or legal control:** none applicable.

**Scientific access:** impossible in the absence of an object.

### 8.2 Alleged Orléans relic

**Last reported location:** treasury or ecclesiastical custody of Sainte-Croix Cathedral, Orléans.

**Reported fate:** destroyed or lost when the cathedral and its contents were attacked in 1562.

**Certainty:** the relic’s individual destruction is not independently established by the accessible evidence. It may have been destroyed, carried away, concealed, or never have existed as the old tradition claimed. No serious modern location hypothesis or rival extant claimant was found.

### 8.3 Surviving material stand-ins

#### The Chartres window

The Charlemagne window, bay 7 of Chartres Cathedral’s north ambulatory, contains a scene of the angel and sheet. The French Ministry of Culture dates the window traditionally to about **1225**, describes twenty-four panels, and records restorations by the Lorin workshop in 1921 and again in 1999. The window is property of the French state and was protected in the 1840 list. [French Ministry of Culture record IM28000516](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/palissy/IM28000516).

Panel 22 is commonly identified as the Mass of Saint Giles, with an angel handing the priest the written confession or pardon. [Chartres bay guide](https://www.medievalart.org.uk/Chartres/07_pages/Chartres_Bay07_key.html).

#### National Gallery NG4681

The Master of Saint Giles’s *Mass of Saint Giles* is in the National Gallery, London, accession **NG4681**. It is the most detailed surviving late-medieval visualization of the *schedula*. The panel entered the National Gallery in 1933 through the National Art-Collections Fund following the death of Lady Seaforth.

The painting is in generally excellent condition on the obverse, although the green curtain between the king and altar is seriously damaged. The reverse, showing Saint Peter in grisaille, is much less well preserved. It was treated in 1933, suffered four small accidental damages at the Gallery on 16 August 1933, and was cleaned on both sides in 1974–75.

Dendrochronology identified western European oak: dated rings in the boards extend into 1463. Examination has included microscopy, infrared reflectography, X-radiography, pigment sampling, gas chromatography, and GC–MS. Identified materials include chalk in animal glue, walnut-oil medium, azurite, verdigris, lead-tin yellow, lead white, vermilion, red lake derived from madder and kermes, and powdered glass. These findings concern the representation, not the legendary relic. [Full National Gallery technical catalogue](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/catalogues/campbell-2014/two-panels-from-an-altarpiece).

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 The Master of Saint Giles painting

NG4681 has largely displaced the absent relic in modern visual culture. Its angel, pale sheet, Latin inscription, kneeling emperor, and richly reconstructed Saint-Denis interior have supplied details the earliest text lacks.

The painting’s subject was not always recognized:

- 1752, 1756, and 1789: attributed to Dürer;
- 1756: Pierre-Jean Mariette misread the sheet’s inscription approximately as “God save Charles” and connected it with Charles the Bold;
- Richard Cosway described it as Thomas Aquinas saying Mass at Saint-Denis for Louis IX;
- other nineteenth-century cataloguers called it a Mass of Saint Gregory;
- W. H. James Weale correctly identified the Mass of Saint Giles in 1889;
- Hugo von Tschudi’s study of the related panels established the conventional name “Master of Saint Giles.”

The painted document itself was thus decisive in recovering the picture’s actual iconography, but only after centuries of misidentification.

### 9.2 Copies and derivatives

The National Gallery catalogue records:

- a colored copy made by John Carter when the picture was sold in 1789;
- a stained-glass copy by William Raphael Eginton, made before 1817 for Dudley House;
- Viollet-le-Duc’s 1859 architectural illustration of the altar without the figures;
- an altar, retable, and cross installed at All Saints, Brixworth, in or after 1896, probably based on Viollet-le-Duc’s illustration.

These are copies or architectural derivatives of NG4681, not relic claimants.

### 9.3 Chartres conservation and tourism

The Chartres scene remains publicly visible within the cathedral’s Charlemagne cycle. Modern guides often state that the sheet revealed incest and Roland’s birth, although the glass contains no surviving verbal statement to that effect. The explicit incest narrative comes from associated texts and iconographic interpretation.

### 9.4 Contemporary devotional use

Parishes and pilgrimage organizations continue to retell the miracle on Giles’s feast. The object remains a symbol of:

- sins difficult to confess;
- divine knowledge of concealed guilt;
- conditional mercy;
- the efficacy of saintly intercession;
- the necessity of repentance.

Modern pastoral retellings sometimes flatten medieval distinctions by saying that Giles simply “forgave Charlemagne” or that the paper was an unconditional absolution. Those are interpretations, not exact statements of the early source.

### 9.5 Popular embellishment

The identification of the crime as incest or necrophilia is now often presented without indicating its late and variant character. The name Gabriel is likewise frequently supplied to the angel. Both should be labeled later elaborations unless tied to a specified witness.

No consequential modern hoax, commercial fake, auction claimant, occult cult object, or repatriation dispute centered on an extant *schedula* was found.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Editions and textual study

The foundational printed source remains the Bollandist edition of the *Vita* in *Acta Sanctorum*, 1 September, pp. 299–304. The IRHT classifies the textual family under BHL 93–98b and records about ninety manuscripts.

Gaston Paris and Alphonse Bos’s 1881 edition of Guillaume de Berneville’s *Vie de saint Gilles* made the Anglo-Norman text and its Latin relationships available for modern study. Françoise Laurent published a modern bilingual edition in 2003.

### 10.2 Dating of the *Vita*

Older scholarship commonly described the work broadly as tenth or early eleventh century. The current FAMA entry’s 891–915 range supports composition around the turn of the tenth century. The National Gallery catalogue accepts the argument for an early-tenth-century monk of Saint-Gilles.

This is the date of the narrative’s recoverable composition, not of the supposed object.

### 10.3 The king’s identity

The central problem is chronological. Proposed positions include:

- the story always meant Charlemagne and ignored chronology;
- an older “King Charles” story concerned Charles Martel and was transferred to Charlemagne;
- Charles is a generic great ruler absorbed into Carolingian legend.

Medieval art and literature increasingly integrated the episode into Charlemagne’s biography, making later reception clearer than the story’s hypothetical prehistory.

### 10.4 Nature of the sin

The earliest text’s silence generated several traditions:

- incest with a sister and conception of Roland;
- necrophilic or post-mortem sexual attachment;
- sodomy;
- an unspecified enormous crime.

The incest tradition became especially useful for epic genealogy: Roland could be both the emperor’s son and nephew while remaining wholly Carolingian.

Joseph J. Duggan emphasizes that explicit identification appears in the thirteenth-century *Karlamagnús saga*, while earlier visual arrangements may presuppose the story. [Duggan](https://scholar.lib.vt.edu/ejournals/ElAnt/V14N1/duggan.html).

### 10.5 Chartres: text and image

The Charlemagne window’s sources and program remain debated. Clark Maines and later scholars have examined how the window combines several narrative complexes rather than simply illustrating one book. The juxtaposition of the Mass with Roland’s death may imply the incest genealogy, but the image itself does not explicitly state it.

The Ministry of Culture dates the window around 1225. Other specialists use ca. 1210–25. [French Ministry of Culture](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/palissy/IM28000516); [Chartres study](https://www.medievalart.org.uk/PhD/4_Chartres_Charlemagne_Window.html).

### 10.6 Saint-Denis versus Orléans

The early *Vita* names neither location for the Mass. The competing localizations arose later:

- Orléans through narrative proximity and the alleged relic;
- Saint-Denis through Ivo’s textual insertion and French royal imagery;
- further locations in epic adaptations.

The Master of Saint Giles’s choice of Saint-Denis cannot settle the historical question. The painting is filled with deliberate anachronisms, including monuments and objects made centuries after Charlemagne.

### 10.7 The Orléans relic problem

The alleged physical relic is the least adequately documented major issue. Future research should seek:

- medieval and early-modern inventories of Sainte-Croix;
- cathedral chapter accounts and visitation records;
- sixteenth-century descriptions of the treasury;
- Protestant and Catholic narratives of the 1562 sack;
- antiquarian histories of Orléans;
- the exact source behind Campbell’s statement;
- the printed source underlying the nineteenth-century online guide passage.

Until those are examined, both “the relic existed” and “it was destroyed in 1562” remain insufficiently resolved propositions.

### 10.8 Art-historical study of NG4681

The National Gallery’s 2014 catalogue by Lorne Campbell provides the fullest open-access object study of the painted representation. It integrates provenance, dendrochronology, infrared examination, paint analysis, conservation history, inscription study, iconography, and reconstruction of the dispersed altarpiece.

The catalogue rejects or treats skeptically several attempts to identify portraits and a specific royal commission. It also warns that the archaeological accuracy of the Saint-Denis interior should not be inflated into proof of the painting’s original location or patronage.

### Evidentiary cautions

- The National Gallery catalogue is the clearest accessible source for the reported Orléans relic, but the underlying medieval or early-modern inventory was not exposed in the online notes.
- General histories of the 1562 violence establish a credible destructive context, not the individual fate of this object.
- The nineteenth-century Orléans guide passage is ambiguous and cannot override the reported sixteenth-century loss.
- Modern museum and devotional pages frequently identify the sin or angel more specifically than the earliest sources do.
- No scientific literature on a physical *schedula* exists because no authenticated object is available for examination.
