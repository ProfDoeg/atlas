# The Roman civic crown (corona civica): Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `the_roman_civic_crown_corona_civica_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary conclusion

The Roman civic crown was not one unique piece of regalia with a continuous chain of custody. It was a repeatable class of Roman military decoration: a wreath made from the foliage of an acorn-bearing tree and awarded, in its developed Republican form, to a Roman citizen who saved another Roman citizen in battle. Individual wreaths were made for individual awards, normally from perishable vegetation. None has been securely identified among surviving archaeological material.

The dossier therefore tracks four related but materially distinct things:

1. the recurrent, physically manufactured military wreath;
2. particular crowns reported in literary sources, especially those awarded to named recipients;
3. the exceptional crown installed above Augustus’s house in 27 BCE and its successors in imperial ceremonial;
4. representations and symbolic descendants—coins, inscriptions, sculpture, architecture, heraldry, replicas, and modern emblems.

No evidence establishes that Augustus’s doorway crown, any Republican battlefield crown, and any crown worn in an imperial portrait were the same physical object. There is consequently no single continuous provenance. There are instead numerous short custody chains, usually ending when the organic wreath disappeared from the record.

### Evidentiary labels used

- **Contemporary or near-contemporary evidence:** inscriptions, coins, and authors writing close to the described institution or event.
- **Ancient retrospective report:** later Roman or Greek testimony about earlier practice.
- **Traditional/legendary history:** stories projected into Rome’s regal or early Republican period without contemporary documentation.
- **Modern scholarly inference:** reconstruction from texts, images, or comparative evidence.
- **Unresolved:** a proposition that available evidence cannot establish.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity and class

**Class:** military decoration; honorific wreath; ceremonial head ornament; subsequently an imperial emblem.

**Principal Latin name:** *corōna cīvica*, literally “civic/citizen crown” or “crown pertaining to citizens.”

- *corōna*: wreath, garland, or circular head ornament; Latin probably borrowed the word from Greek **κορώνη** (*korṓnē*), originally “crow,” then an object characterized by curvature. The exact semantic route from bird or curved object to wreath is reconstructed rather than documented in a surviving ancient etymological explanation.
- *cīvicus*: “of citizens” or “civic,” from *cīvis*, citizen.
- Elliptical Latin: *cīvica*, with *corōna* understood.
- Descriptive ancient names include *corona querna*, “oaken crown,” and expressions referring to oak or acorn-bearing foliage.
- Italian: *corona civica*, *corona di quercia*.
- French: *couronne civique*.
- German: *Bürgerkrone*, *Eichenkranz*, or untranslated *corona civica*.
- English: civic crown, civic wreath, oak crown, oak-leaf crown.

A frequent modern spelling, “corona querquensis,” is not the principal classical title and should not displace the securely attested *corona civica*. “Civic crown” must also be distinguished from the mural crown, grass or siege crown, naval crown, triumphal crown, and later heraldic crowns of civic corporations.

### Material

Pliny the Elder gives the fullest ancient botanical sequence:

1. originally *ilex* foliage;
2. subsequently *aesculus*, a tree sacred to Jupiter;
3. finally *quercus* or whichever local acorn-bearing tree was available, provided the symbolic “honour of the acorn” was preserved.

Pliny’s text is explicit that the material changed while the connection with the acorn-bearing oak family remained. Aulus Gellius similarly says the crown was made from *frons quernea*, oak foliage, and formerly from *ilex*.

These botanical terms are difficult to map perfectly onto modern taxonomy:

- *ilex* normally denotes the evergreen or holm oak, conventionally *Quercus ilex*;
- *quercus* is a general oak term and can refer to deciduous oaks;
- *aesculus* in Roman botanical usage is an oak, commonly associated by modern scholars with an Italian or Turkey-oak type. It is **not securely the modern horse chestnut**, *Aesculus hippocastanum*. Translations calling the ancient material “horse chestnut” confuse an ancient Latin tree-name with a later Linnaean genus.

Pliny expressly contrasts the wreath’s low material value with golden Roman decorations. The traditional military form was therefore living or recently cut vegetation, not a solid gold crown. Sculpted, painted, and numismatic examples sometimes render the wreath as an idealized, permanent ornament; these representations do not prove that ordinary award wreaths were metal.

### Construction

No ancient workshop specification survives. Literary language implies foliage gathered and assembled into a chaplet capable of being placed on the head. Visual evidence normally shows:

- two sprays of oak leaves curving upward;
- visible acorns;
- a tie, fillet, or ribbon at the back or bottom;
- an open or nearly closed front, depending on medium and period.

The crown could be worn on the head, carried or displayed, or fixed architecturally above a doorway. It was not necessarily one unbroken circular band. Coins frequently compress the wreath into a framing device around an inscription or shield.

No standard dimensions, weight, prescribed number of leaves, workshop, fastening method, or botanical collecting locality is recorded. Modern reconstructions that specify these details are conjectural.

### Inscriptions

A battlefield wreath made from vegetation could not conveniently bear a long permanent inscription, and no inscribed physical Republican wreath survives. The formula associated with the award appears principally in literature, architecture, and coin designs:

- **OB CIVEM SERVATVM** — “for a citizen saved”;
- **OB CIVES SERVATOS** — “for citizens saved”;
- Augustan coinage sometimes preserves the archaizing accusative plural **CIVIS** rather than classical *cives*;
- abbreviations include **O C S** and **S P Q R OB C S**;
- **S P Q R** means *Senatus Populusque Romanus*, “the Senate and Roman People.”

Seneca calls the crown *illa corona ob cives servatos*, “that crown for citizens saved.” Ovid says the reason for the crown above Augustus’s door was certified by writing: it indicated citizens saved through Augustus’s aid. This evidence supports the presence of an accompanying architectural or representational legend, not necessarily lettering physically woven into every military wreath.

### Surviving specimens and representations

No organic specimen has a secure archaeological identification, award record, or continuous provenance as a corona civica. Objects sometimes called “Roman civic crowns” in auction listings, costume catalogues, or unspecialized museum descriptions require independent examination; oak-leaf funerary wreaths, priestly crowns, festival garlands, and metal foliage ornaments are not automatically civic crowns.

Secure representations include:

- Roman coins showing an oak wreath, commonly containing **OB CIVIS/CIVES SERVATOS**;
- portraits of emperors wearing oak wreaths;
- carved architectural wreaths and reliefs;
- images pairing the wreath with Augustus’s laurel trees and *clipeus virtutis*, the shield of virtue.

Examples with published museum identities include:

- British Museum **R.6014**, an aureus dated to 27 BCE: eagle standing on an oak wreath with laurel branches; gold, 7.81 g; presently listed on display in Room 70.
- British Museum **1844,0425.432**, a silver denarius dated to 19 BCE: an oak wreath around an inscribed shield; 3.86 g.
- Metropolitan Museum of Art **99.35.6**, an aureus of 20–19 BCE: Augustus’s oak-wreathed head and the shield-and-laurel design; gold; on display in Gallery 166 at the time of the catalogue record.
- British Museum **BNK,R.14**, an aureus of Galba, 68–69 CE: oak-wreath reverse; gold, 7.32 g; not on display according to the catalogue.
- Roman Provincial Coinage I.777, a large copper-alloy issue of Hadrumetum, 7–6 BCE: **O C S** inside a wreath between laurel branches.
- RPC II.1478, a copper-alloy issue of 77/78 CE: **S P Q R OB C S** inside a wreath.

These coins are not surviving crowns; they are primary evidence for the wreath’s iconography, inscription, and political use.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Institutional origin

No founding decree, statute, or securely dated first award survives. Ancient writers believed the institution to be very old, but their claims must be divided into three layers.

### Legendary and annalistic origins

Pliny reports that Romulus crowned Hostus Hostilius with foliage because Hostus was first to break into Fidenae. Pliny calls this a *corona frondea*, a leafy crown, rather than unambiguously applying all the later technical requirements of the corona civica. The story belongs to Roman regal tradition and was written down many centuries after its supposed setting.

Plutarch reports that the young Gaius Marcius, later Coriolanus, received a civic crown after saving a Roman citizen during the campaign at Corioli. The event belongs to the legendary or heavily reconstructed early Republic. Plutarch wrote around the late first or early second century CE, roughly five centuries after the traditional date.

These stories demonstrate that imperial-period Romans projected the award deep into their national past. They do not document manufacture or custody in the sixth or fifth century BCE.

### Earliest surviving institutional description

The earliest securely datable extended description is in Polybius, *Histories* 6.39, written during the second century BCE. Polybius says that after a battle the commander publicly commended courage and gave awards. Men who protected and saved citizens or allies were honoured; the rescued men voluntarily presented crowns, or the military tribunes compelled them to do so. The rescued person thereafter revered the rescuer like a father.

Important limitations:

- Polybius describes the function and presentation but does not, in the surviving passage, securely specify oak.
- His category includes the saving of citizens **or allies**, whereas later legalistic descriptions restrict the civic crown to the saving of a Roman citizen.
- He does not provide dimensions, a manufacturing procedure, or a permanent central store of crowns.
- His account nevertheless places the underlying institution securely in the middle Republic.

A fragment of the second-century BCE comic poet Caecilius, preserved only through Gellius, mentions men coming with *ilignea corona*, a holm-oak crown. Because the original dramatic context is lost, it is valuable botanical evidence but cannot alone reconstruct the award ceremony.

### Development of formal rules

By the late Republic and early Empire, antiquarian accounts articulated stringent conditions:

1. the recipient must be a Roman citizen;
2. he must save a fellow Roman citizen in battle;
3. he must kill the enemy who threatened that citizen;
4. he must hold the position where the rescue occurred;
5. the rescued citizen himself must acknowledge the rescue;
6. third-party witnesses did not substitute for the rescued citizen’s acknowledgment.

Pliny says auxiliaries received no civic crown even for saving a king; nor did saving a commander increase the honour, because the founders intended the life of every citizen to have the highest value.

The rules were not entirely immutable. Gellius, citing the jurist Masurius Sabinus, records a case referred to Tiberius. A soldier saved a citizen and killed two enemies but had to abandon the position. Tiberius ruled that he nevertheless deserved the crown because the danger was so great that even brave soldiers could not hold the ground. This is evidence of imperial adjudication modifying or interpreting the older rule.

### Manufacture and cost

No ancient contract, public account, named maker, workshop, purchase price, or standard production center survives. Polybius’s account implies situational manufacture and presentation by the rescued man, supervised by military authority. Because the material was foliage, the financial cost was negligible. Pliny considered this deliberate: Rome assigned the saving of a citizen honour rather than a market price.

A recipient’s right to wear the crown permanently does not prove that the first perishable wreath was preserved permanently. Replacement wreaths must have been possible if the privilege was exercised for years, but no text prescribes their manufacture. Whether “to use it forever” meant retaining a legal insignial right, repeatedly making fresh wreaths, possessing a durable copy, or some combination remains unresolved.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### The normal Republican custody sequence

The fullest reconstructable standard chain was:

**vegetation at or near the army’s location → gathered and formed into a wreath by an unrecorded maker → presented by the rescued citizen, voluntarily or under pressure from the tribunes → possessed and worn by the rescuer → displayed in ceremonies and potentially in family commemoration → physical disappearance as vegetation decayed.**

Polybius makes the rescued citizen the principal donor. Later imperial practice shifted authority toward the emperor or his delegate. No central Roman armoury, temple treasury, or archive is known to have retained all civic crowns.

### Traditional early crowns

#### Hostus Hostilius

- **Supposed date:** regal period.
- **Donor:** Romulus.
- **Recipient:** Hostus Hostilius.
- **Reason:** first entry into Fidenae, according to Pliny.
- **Material:** foliage only; not securely identified as oak.
- **Custody after award:** wholly unrecorded.
- **Tier:** legendary/annalistic retrospective report.

#### Gaius Marcius Coriolanus

- **Supposed date:** early fifth century BCE.
- **Donor:** associated by Plutarch with the Roman commander and customary law.
- **Reason:** saving a citizen and conspicuous courage at Corioli.
- **Custody:** no physical after-history.
- **Tier:** ancient biographical tradition, not contemporary documentation.

### Republican crowns attributed to named soldiers

#### Lucius Siccius Dentatus

Valerius Maximus and Pliny report that Siccius Dentatus received fourteen civic crowns, in addition to numerous other decorations. His fifth-century BCE career was already a subject of exemplary Roman storytelling. The count is an ancient claim, not an inventory. No crown survives and no separate chain can be reconstructed for any of the fourteen.

#### Marcus Manlius Capitolinus

Livy and Pliny report six civic crowns, one for saving his commander Servilius. Again, no individual wreath is described after presentation. The story later served the tragic contrast between Manlius’s military services and his condemnation at Rome.

#### Publius Cornelius Scipio

Pliny reports that the young Scipio saved his father and refused to accept a civic crown from him. Pliny places the episode at the Trebia; other ancient accounts associate the rescue with the Ticinus. Because Scipio declined the decoration, there is no securely constituted crown to trace. This is a claimed non-transfer, not custody.

#### Julius Caesar’s Mytilene crown

Suetonius says Marcus Minucius Thermus awarded Julius Caesar a civic crown at the storming of Mytilene, conventionally dated about 81/80 BCE.

Reconstructable chain:

**unrecorded manufacture in the Roman army in Asia → award by or under Thermus’s authority → possession by Caesar.**

Nothing establishes what happened to the wreath afterward. It should not be confused with Caesar’s later privilege of wearing a laurel wreath or with crowns offered during his dictatorship. The civic crown and triumphal/laurel crown were distinct insignia.

### Proposed civic crown for Cicero

Gellius reports that the former censor Lucius Gellius Publicola proposed in the Senate that Cicero be awarded the civic crown for exposing and punishing Catiline’s conspiracy in 63 BCE. The proposed justification expanded battlefield rescue into salvation of the state.

The report establishes a proposal, not securely the manufacture and delivery of a particular wreath. No continuous ancient evidence shows a surviving “Cicero civic crown.” Later assertions that Cicero possessed such an object should therefore be qualified.

### Augustus’s doorway crown

This is the best-documented individual installation, though not a surviving artifact.

In 27 BCE the Senate granted Octavian the name Augustus, laurel decoration for his doorposts, a civic crown above his door, and a golden shield in the Curia Julia. Augustus himself records:

> *coronaque civica super ianuam meam fixa est*  
> “and a civic crown was fixed above my door.”

Cassius Dio says the laurel represented perpetual victory over enemies and the oak crown Augustus’s status as saviour of the citizens. Ovid repeatedly describes the oak crown and laurels at the Palatine entrance.

Reconstructable chain:

1. **Authorization:** vote of the Senate in January 27 BCE.
2. **Manufacture:** maker, precise material, dimensions, and cost unrecorded.
3. **Installation:** above the entrance of Augustus’s Palatine residence.
4. **Custody/control:** attached to the public presentation of Augustus’s residence, maintained under the authority of the princeps and state.
5. **Reproduction:** disseminated almost immediately in coins, poetry, relief, and provincial imperial imagery.
6. **Fate:** unrecorded.

Whether the installed crown was fresh vegetation periodically replaced, a durable architectural rendering, or both is unresolved. The verb *fixa est* indicates physical attachment but does not identify the substance. Numismatic images are schematic.

Augustus’s original house suffered fires and rebuilding, notably in 3 CE, and the Palatine imperial complex underwent repeated alteration. No source securely records the doorway crown’s survival through a particular fire, transfer to a treasury, destruction, or rediscovery. Claims assigning an extant wreath to this installation lack a documented chain.

### Imperial diffusion

Under the Julio-Claudian and later emperors the civic crown became an emblem of the princeps as collective saviour rather than merely a decoration for a single battlefield rescue.

Seneca told Nero that no ornament was more fitting to a ruler’s eminence than the crown “for citizens saved.” Coins of Caligula, Claudius, Nero, Galba, Vitellius, Vespasian, Trajan, and others reproduce the wreath and formula. These were separately struck coin issues, not pieces of Augustus’s crown.

Sculptural portraits show emperors wearing oak wreaths. Because portraits could be copied provincially and sometimes depart from official facial types, the presence of a corona civica may identify imperial status more securely than it identifies a particular emperor. Lee Ann Riccardi’s study of the so-called Kanellopoulos emperor is an example: the portrait’s wreath is imperial, but the sitter’s identity remains debated, with Trajan proposed on stylistic grounds.

By late antiquity the oak wreath was one among several inherited imperial head ornaments. Its disappearance as a regularly documented military award or exclusive imperial attribute was gradual. No abolition decree or final award is known.

### Provenance gaps

Every alleged original organic crown has a terminal gap. No chain carries any example from ancient award to modern collection. Surviving objects fall into three other categories:

- coins and inscriptions with continuous or partly documented modern museum provenance;
- ancient stone portraits and reliefs depicting crowns;
- modern reconstructions or replicas.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is there a surviving “true civic crown”?

No securely authenticated physical crown is known.

Authentication would require some combination of:

- controlled archaeological context;
- botanical or metallurgical identification;
- evidence that the object functioned as an award rather than a funerary or ritual wreath;
- an inscription or associated record identifying the recipient and award;
- a credible chain from excavation to collection.

No published candidate satisfies these conditions. Accordingly, statements that a museum “has the Roman civic crown” normally refer to a representation, an oak wreath of uncertain function, or a modern reconstruction.

### Organic crowns versus metal wreaths

Ancient graves have yielded gold and bronze wreaths imitating leaves. These may be funerary, priestly, athletic, royal, or ceremonial objects. Material resemblance does not establish the technical identity *corona civica*. Pliny and Gellius emphasize real foliage for the civic crown, while explicitly distinguishing golden crowns in other categories.

A gilded or jeweled wreath on an imperial portrait may represent an elaborated imperial crown, artistic translation, priestly insignia, or a hybrid of types. It should not be used without qualification to redefine the ordinary Republican award as a gold object.

### Botanical dispute

Modern translations have sometimes rendered *aesculus* as “horse chestnut.” This is botanically anachronistic or at least highly misleading. Ancient Roman writers place *aesculus* among acorn-bearing trees, whereas horse chestnut produces conkers rather than acorns and was not the relevant Italian oak. The secure conclusion is that several oak types could be used; exact Linnaean species often remains uncertain.

### The inscription problem

Popular accounts sometimes say every wreath “bore” the inscription **OB CIVEM SERVATVM**. The formula is securely associated with the award, especially on coins and the Augustan architectural emblem. Evidence is insufficient to establish that the words were physically attached to every field-made leaf crown.

### Augustus’s crown: individual object or constitutional emblem?

Two readings are not mutually exclusive:

- **Physical reading:** a particular crown was manufactured and fixed above the Palatine doorway.
- **Institutional reading:** the installed crown materialized a continuing honour, periodically renewable and reproducible.

The sources support both a physical installation and symbolic perpetuity, but not the survival of one unchanged botanical object.

### Republic-to-Empire continuity

Scholars agree on a major change in meaning but differ in emphasis. In Republican ideology the citizen saved another citizen; under Augustus the ruler claimed to have saved the citizen body as a whole. Some interpret this as appropriation of a traditional horizontal military honour by monarchy. Others emphasize continuity: Rome had already contemplated non-battlefield civic crowns for political saviours such as Cicero and Caesar. The surviving evidence supports transformation rather than simple replacement.

### Unverified popular claims

The following claims cannot presently be established:

- that civic-crown winners automatically entered the Senate after Sulla;
- that every recipient was legally compelled to wear the original crown at every public gathering;
- that Caesar constantly wore his Mytilene civic crown;
- that an extant metal oak wreath is Augustus’s doorway crown;
- that a single standardized crown passed from emperor to emperor;
- that the modern Italian emblem is a direct continuation of Augustus’s physical crown.

Pliny does attest the right of permanent use, preferential seating, public recognition, and exemptions; he does not document a surviving hereditary artifact.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Battlefield award procedure

Polybius describes a public military assembly after combat. The commander praised men who had distinguished themselves and distributed appropriate decorations. In the rescue category, the man saved presented the crown; if reluctant, the tribunes could compel him.

The ceremony did more than transfer an ornament. It publicly established:

- that the rescuer had performed the deed;
- that the rescued man owed his life to him;
- a lasting quasi-filial relationship between them;
- the rescuer’s reputation before the army and community.

Cicero remarks that soldiers were reluctant to acknowledge this debt, helping explain why the rescued man’s testimony mattered.

### Conditions and eligibility

The strict version recorded by Pliny and Masurius Sabinus demanded the saving of a Roman citizen, the killing of the assailant, and retention of the battlefield position. Pliny’s exclusion of auxiliaries shows that the crown privileged Roman citizenship rather than merely humane rescue.

The rescued commander’s high rank did not increase the award: in Pliny’s interpretation, the life of every citizen carried the maximum civic value.

### Privileges

Pliny records that a recipient:

- could wear the crown for life;
- was greeted by the audience rising when he entered public games, including the Senate;
- could sit in a place adjoining the senators;
- received exemption from public duties;
- extended exemption to his father and paternal grandfather.

The text does not say the crown itself was hereditary. The exemptions operated through the recipient’s paternal family, while the wreath remained his personal decoration.

### Paternal relationship

Polybius says the saved man must honour the rescuer as a father. The crown therefore created a socially meaningful relationship analogous to kinship. This helps explain its later compatibility with titles such as *pater patriae*, “father of the fatherland”: the ruler who saved all citizens became collectively paternal.

### Association with Jupiter

Plutarch offers several alternatives for oak:

- Arcadian acorn-eating ancestry;
- the ready availability of oak on campaign;
- oak’s sacredness to Jupiter, guardian of the city;
- the oak’s strength and noble fruit.

These are ancient explanations, not recoverable provisions of a founding law. In imperial imagery, the oak strengthened Augustus’s analogy to Jupiter without formally turning the crown into a divine object.

### Augustan constitutional symbolism

Augustus’s crown fundamentally enlarged the phrase “citizen saved.” It no longer commemorated one identifiable rescue. It represented his claim to have preserved the entire Roman citizen body by ending civil war and restoring public order.

The conjunction at his doorway was carefully structured:

- laurel trees: victory;
- civic oak crown: citizens saved;
- Palatine house: personal residence presented as a public locus of rule;
- golden shield in the Curia: *virtus*, *clementia*, *iustitia*, and *pietas*;
- title *Augustus*: sacred and constitutional elevation without a royal diadem.

Ovid’s exilic poetry complicates the benevolent message. His book sees the crown, learns that its owner “saved citizens,” and confronts the paradox that the poet himself has been excluded from imperial protection. The crown thus became a site where official clemency and experienced coercion could be compared.

### Imperial use

Seneca’s address to Nero makes clemency itself the deed that earns a civic crown. The ruler saves citizens by refraining from arbitrary killing. Pliny explicitly says that what had been a mark of soldiers’ courage had become an emblem of imperial clemency after civil war made not killing a citizen seem meritorious.

On coins, the wreath framed the ruler’s titulature or the phrase **OB CIVES SERVATOS**. Its replication converted a perishable personal decoration into a mass-produced, empire-wide political sign.

### Access and handling

No priesthood, hereditary keeper, taboo, or ritual prohibition on touching an ordinary civic crown is recorded. Its dignity came from the deed and status it certified. For Augustus’s doorway emblem, physical access would have been controlled by the imperial residence, but no specific handling protocol survives.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### Romulus as founder of crown-giving

**Tier:** ancient foundation legend.

Pliny’s Hostus Hostilius story places the origin of Roman leafy military crowns with Romulus. It makes the institution coeval with Rome’s founder and integrates later military honours into regal precedent. Because Pliny calls the object a leafy crown and connects it to first entry into a town, the exact later category may have been blurred with the mural crown.

### Coriolanus as ideal young recipient

**Tier:** ancient biographical legend or heavily reconstructed tradition.

Plutarch’s Coriolanus receives the civic crown while still young after refusing to abandon an older comrade. The episode establishes courage, loyalty, and citizenship before his later rupture with Rome. Its narrative function is biographical foreshadowing; it cannot be treated as a documented fifth-century award certificate.

### Siccius Dentatus, the “Roman Achilles”

**Tier:** Roman exemplum with potentially inflated totals.

Fourteen civic crowns made Siccius an embodiment of repeated citizen-saving. The extraordinary catalogue of wounds, battles, crowns, collars, armlets, and spears was transmitted as moralized military history. There is no surviving inventory or group of fourteen wreaths.

### Scipio’s refusal

**Tier:** ancient moral exemplum; location disputed.

Scipio’s refusal of a crown from his rescued father made the deed appear free of profit or self-advertisement. Pliny uses it in a passage praising the award’s nonmonetary nature. The disagreement over Trebia versus Ticinus illustrates the instability of the received narrative.

### Augustus saves everyone

**Tier:** official imperial claim with contemporary material attestation.

The proposition that Augustus had saved the citizen body is documented in contemporary state symbolism, not merely a later legend. Whether the political claim accurately characterizes the aftermath of civil war is a separate historical question. The factual core is that the Senate authorized the honour and Augustus publicly memorialized it.

### The crown as a survival relic

**Tier:** unsupported modern inference where asserted.

Because no ancient crown has a continuous provenance, stories identifying an extant wreath as “the crown of Augustus,” Caesar, or another recipient are not established. The authentic survivals are texts, inscriptions, coins, and sculpted images—not the documented original vegetation.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Status of the original object-class

The corona civica survives as an historically documented award type and iconographic form. No authenticated organic award crown is known to survive.

There is therefore:

- no accession number for “the” corona civica;
- no present owner of the unique original;
- no active restitution or repatriation claim concerning a securely identified crown;
- no published scientific examination—radiocarbon dating, botanical microscopy, residue analysis, or DNA analysis—of a generally accepted specimen;
- no extant shrine or treasury that maintains a plausible continuous physical identity.

### Augustus’s Palatine crown

**Status:** lost or destroyed at an unknown date.

**Last securely documented location:** fixed above the entrance to Augustus’s residence on the Palatine in Rome.

**Physical fate:** unrecorded. Fires, rebuilding, weathering, replacement, and the later transformation of the palace are all plausible mechanisms, but no source singles out the crown’s fate.

### Surviving proxies

The best-preserved evidence is dispersed internationally:

- coins in the British Museum, Metropolitan Museum of Art, Ashmolean Museum, Bibliothèque nationale de France, and other collections;
- marble and bronze imperial portraits in collections including Munich’s Glyptothek, the Metropolitan Museum, and the Paul and Alexandra Canellopoulos Museum in Athens;
- architectural and relief representations at Roman and provincial sites;
- the monumental copies of Augustus’s *Res Gestae*, especially the Latin and Greek text preserved at Ankara.

Each proxy has its own provenance. None inherits the physical identity of an awarded wreath.

### Legal status and access

Museum coins and sculptures are controlled under the legal regimes of their respective institutions. Published examples may be on display, in study collections, or in storage. Their ownership histories are histories of the representations, not of the ancient wreath depicted.

At the time of the cited museum records:

- British Museum R.6014 was on public display;
- British Museum BNK,R.14 was not on display;
- Metropolitan Museum 99.35.6 was on view;
- digital catalogues and RPC provide public access to high-resolution records and typological data.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Antiquarian reconstruction

Renaissance and early modern antiquarians recovered the corona civica primarily from literary and numismatic evidence. Drawn wreaths in dictionaries and emblem books helped standardize the familiar modern image: symmetrical oak sprays, acorns, and a tied ribbon.

William Smith’s nineteenth-century *Dictionary of Greek and Roman Antiquities* synthesized Polybius, Cicero, Livy, Pliny, Gellius, Dio, Ovid, Seneca, Suetonius, and coin evidence. Its entry remains influential online, though its botanical identifications and some institutional generalizations require modern qualification.

### Republican and civic symbolism

The phrase “civic crown” acquired broader meanings in post-classical Europe. Oak wreaths could represent civic merit, republican virtue, patriotic sacrifice, or national strength without reproducing the Roman eligibility rules.

In Italian revolutionary and republican imagery, Roman wreaths, oak, laurel, eagles, stars, and fasces were recombined. The flag of the Roman Republic of 1849 preserved by the Central Museum of the Risorgimento shows Garibaldi with laurel and oak imagery together with an eagle, fasces, and the Italian star. These are classical political symbols, but the oak element is not an authenticated continuation of a particular Roman crown.

### Emblem of the Italian Republic

Italy’s current emblem, adopted in 1948 and designed by Paolo Paschetto, contains:

- the Star of Italy;
- a steel cogwheel;
- an olive branch;
- an oak branch;
- the inscription **REPVBBLICA ITALIANA**.

The official presidential explanation says the oak embodies the strength and dignity of the Italian people, while the olive signifies peace. The emblem is wreath-like and participates in Italy’s long classical vocabulary, but official documentation does not identify it simply as a replica of Augustus’s corona civica. Calling it a direct survival would overstate continuity.

### Military medals and political imagery

Modern decorations often use oak leaves and wreaths, but identical vegetation does not prove direct derivation from the corona civica. Oak can independently signify endurance, valour, nation, or strength. A direct Roman genealogy should be claimed only when a founding statute, designer, or official commentary states it.

The corona civica has also appeared in modern authoritarian and nationalist classicism, especially where rulers were represented as restorers or saviours of the civic body. Such use revives the Augustan, rather than the strictly Republican battlefield, meaning. Individual examples require contextual attribution; an oak wreath alone is not diagnostic of Roman or Fascist allegiance.

### Literature, film, and popular culture

Modern historical novels, television productions, reenactments, games, and costume suppliers commonly give Roman officers metallic gold or bronze leaf circlets. This visually merges:

- civic oak;
- triumphal laurel;
- golden crowns;
- radiate crowns;
- Hellenistic diadems.

The merger is a modern simplification. Caesar’s frequently dramatized wreath is often a laurel associated with triumph and his perpetual-wearing privilege, not necessarily the civic crown received at Mytilene.

Commercial replicas are usually artificial leaves, cast metal, plastic, fabric, or painted resin. They are reconstructions without historical custody.

### Continuing scholarly and popular controversy

Current misunderstandings include:

- identifying every Roman leafy wreath as laurel;
- identifying every oak wreath as a corona civica;
- calling *aesculus* horse chestnut;
- describing the crown as Rome’s unequivocally “highest medal” without mentioning the grass crown;
- confusing Caesar’s civic and laurel crowns;
- treating Augustus’s political award as a conventional battlefield decoration;
- treating coin images as pictures of one surviving crown rather than standardized symbols.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Ancient evidence base

The principal textual witnesses are unevenly distributed:

- **Polybius, second century BCE:** earliest detailed description of the rescue-award relationship.
- **Cicero, first century BCE:** testimony to the social obligation and reluctance of rescued citizens.
- **Augustus, *Res Gestae* 34:** contemporary first-person record of the crown fixed above his house.
- **Ovid:** contemporary Augustan literary interpretation.
- **Seneca:** early imperial transformation into an emblem of clemency.
- **Pliny:** fullest account of material, hierarchy, rules, privileges, and exemplary recipients.
- **Plutarch and Suetonius:** biographies preserving individual award traditions.
- **Gellius:** antiquarian dossier citing Caecilius and Masurius Sabinus.
- **Cassius Dio:** later constitutional interpretation of the Augustan honours.

The lost works behind these authors include military records, annalists, legal-antiquarian texts, and family traditions. Gellius’s quotation of Masurius Sabinus is particularly important because the original *Memorabilia* does not survive independently.

### Numismatic evidence

Coinage provides contemporary or officially sanctioned evidence for imperial uses. Its strengths are:

- datable issues;
- legends such as **OB CIVIS SERVATOS**;
- distribution across Rome and provinces;
- repeatable iconography;
- known metals, weights, mints, and dies.

Its limits are equally important:

- the images are conventional;
- leaf species cannot always be identified botanically;
- a depicted wreath does not document the physical composition of Augustus’s doorway crown;
- coins describe an official claim, not an independently verified act of salvation.

Modern standard catalogues include *Roman Imperial Coinage*, British Museum catalogues, and *Roman Provincial Coinage*.

### Military-decoration scholarship

Valerie Maxfield’s *The Military Decorations of the Roman Army* remains a fundamental synthesis of Roman *dona militaria*. It situates the civic crown within changing systems of rank, command, and imperial award. The older image of an unchanging Republican award system has increasingly given way to attention to variation across period, rank, source genre, and political context.

### Augustan ideological scholarship

Modern studies, especially those influenced by Paul Zanker’s work on Augustan visual culture, treat the Palatine crown as part of a coordinated symbolic program. The important debate is not whether the crown existed—the *Res Gestae* establishes the installation—but how its traditional meaning was redirected:

- from saving one citizen to saving all citizens;
- from an award presented laterally by the rescued man to an honour conferred by Senate and People;
- from perishable battlefield wreath to permanent imperial sign;
- from individual memory to mass circulation.

### Imperial portrait identification

Oak crowns can help identify a figure as imperial, but not always which emperor. Provincial workshops copied, adapted, or misunderstood metropolitan portrait types. The Canellopoulos portrait studied by Riccardi demonstrates this problem: the crown is a meaningful attribute, while the sitter’s facial identity remains uncertain.

### Botanical historiography

Older translations frequently impose modern common names too confidently. Roman oak nomenclature does not map neatly onto present botanical taxonomy. The safest reconstruction is functional: the crown was made from the foliage of acorn-bearing trees, with holm oak, *aesculus*, and *quercus* named as variants.

### Unresolved research questions

- Did recipients preserve dried originals or commission fresh replacement wreaths?
- Was Augustus’s doorway crown botanical, metallic, painted, sculptural, or periodically renewed?
- How uniformly were the strict three conditions enforced before the Empire?
- Did Polybius describe one category combining citizen and allied rescues, or several awards compressed in his exposition?
- At what date did ordinary soldiers cease receiving the crown?
- How often do provincial sculpted oak wreaths represent the civic crown rather than priestly or local insignia?
- Can traces of pigments, metal attachments, or drilled foliage on imperial portraits distinguish types more securely?
- Do unpublished excavation archives contain organic wreath remains capable of botanical reassessment?

## 12. Sources

### Primary literary and epigraphic sources

**Polybius, *Histories* 6.39.** Earliest securely datable extended description of crowns for saving citizens or allies, presentation by the saved, tribunal compulsion, and the resulting paternal relationship.

**Cicero, *Pro Plancio* 72.** Republican evidence for the rescued soldier’s reluctance to confess dependence and the social consequences of the award.

**Augustus, *Res Gestae Divi Augusti* 34.** Contemporary first-person statement that a civic crown was fixed above his doorway by public authority.

**Ovid, *Fasti* 1.608–616; 4.953; *Tristia* 3.1.33–48.** Contemporary descriptions and interpretations of the Palatine crown.

**Seneca, *De Clementia* 1.26.5.** Connects the civic crown and **ob cives servatos** with imperial clemency.

**Pliny the Elder, *Natural History* 16.7–14.** Principal source for botanical materials, hierarchy, conditions, privileges, recipients, and imperial reinterpretation.

**Plutarch, *Life of Coriolanus* 3.** Early Republican award story and competing ancient explanations for oak.

**Suetonius, *Life of Julius Caesar* 2.** Caesar’s crown at Mytilene.

**Aulus Gellius, *Attic Nights* 5.6.** Materials, Caecilius fragment, Masurius Sabinus’s rules, Tiberius’s ruling, and the proposed crown for Cicero.

**Cassius Dio, *Roman History* 53.16.** Senate’s grant to Augustus and interpretation of laurel and oak.

**Valerius Maximus, *Memorable Deeds and Sayings* 3.2.24 and related passages.** Siccius Dentatus and Roman exemplary traditions.

### Material and catalogue sources

**British Museum collection records.** Object-level data for Augustan and Galban coins, including metal, date, weight, provenance, and display status.

**Metropolitan Museum of Art collection records.** Augustan aureus and imperial portrait documentation.

**Roman Provincial Coinage Online.** Coin legends, metals, mints, dates, types, specimens, and references.

**Official Italian state sources.** Legal and historical documentation for the 1948 emblem and its oak branch.

### Major modern scholarship and reference works

- Valerie A. Maxfield, *The Military Decorations of the Roman Army* (1981).
- Paul Zanker, *The Power of Images in the Age of Augustus*.
- Stefan Weinstock, *Divus Julius*.
- Lee Ann Riccardi, “Uncanonical Imperial Portraits in the Eastern Roman Provinces: The Case of the Kanellopoulos Emperor,” *Hesperia* 69 (2000).
- William Smith, “Corona,” *A Dictionary of Greek and Roman Antiquities* (1875).
- Modern botanical and iconographic work on oak, laurel, wreaths, and Roman imperial visual ideology.
