# The Chintamani Stone (Roerich): Object Dossier

> **File name:** `the_chintamani_stone_roerich_object_dossier.md`  
> **Research cutoff:** 6 September 2026  
> **Subject:** The small dark object received by Nicholas and Helena Roerich in Paris in October 1923, subsequently called “the Stone,” “Chintamani,” “Treasure of the World,” or “Gift of Orion”—not the generic wish-fulfilling jewel of Indian and Buddhist literature except where that older tradition was incorporated into the Roerichs’ interpretation.

## Evidentiary synopsis

The dossier concerns three things that must not be conflated:

1. **The traditional cintāmaṇi:** a fabulous wish-fulfilling jewel in Indian and Buddhist literature and iconography. It is a type or motif, not one securely traceable physical artifact.

2. **The Roerichs’ material object:** a small, dark, palm-sized object photographed in Paris in 1923 and reportedly kept in an older decorated casket. Its material existence in 1923 is supported by a contemporary photograph, Nicholas Roerich’s contemporary correspondence, Helena Roerich’s notebooks, and later portraits of the casket. No published laboratory examination, accession record, mass, dimensions, mineralogical analysis, or presently verifiable location has been found.

3. **The Roerichian legendary identity:** the claim that the 1923 object was a fragment of a celestial Stone kept by the Brotherhood of Shambhala, sent into the world during decisive historical periods and formerly held by Solomon, rulers of India and China, Timur, Akbar, and other sovereigns. This custody history first appears as a composite esoteric revelation written down by Helena Roerich in 1923 and published under Nicholas Roerich’s name in 1924. No independent evidence connects the Paris object to any ancient or medieval holder.

Accordingly, the strongest defensible conclusion is:

**A physical object and casket existed in Roerich custody in October 1923. Its sender, maker, material, date, pre-1923 provenance, post-Roerich custody, and present location have not been established publicly. Its identification as a meteorite, much less as the unique Chintamani of Shambhala or a fragment from Orion, remains unverified.**

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Object class and identity

The Roerich Chintamani belongs simultaneously—but at different evidentiary levels—to several classes:

- **Documented physical class:** a small mineral-looking or stone-like object kept as a talisman or relic.
- **Documented container:** an old decorated casket or shrine, itself wrapped in purple or purplish-red velvet and delivered inside an ordinary wooden shipping box.
- **Roerichian religious class:** a *teraphim*, covenant-object, bearer of a “ray,” and traveling fragment of a greater celestial Stone.
- **Comparative-symbolic class:** a modern material embodiment of the Indian and Tibetan wish-fulfilling jewel.
- **Popular modern class:** an alleged meteorite, extraterrestrial artifact, Philosopher’s Stone, Holy Grail analogue, or Shambhala power-object.

The dossier tracks the particular object delivered to the Roerich family in Paris on 6 October 1923. It does not assume that every image of a flaming jewel, every Tibetan “precious jewel,” or every legendary Chintamani refers to that individual object.

### 1.2 Names and linguistic forms

**Sanskrit:** चिन्तामणि, *cintāmaṇi*.

The compound comprises *cintā*—thought, reflection, concern, wish—and *maṇi*—jewel or precious stone. Contextually it means “wish-fulfilling jewel,” “thought-jewel,” or “jewel that supplies what is contemplated.” It is frequently translated too literally as “thinking stone.” Standard Sanskrit lexicography treats it as a fabulous jewel rather than a geologically defined substance. The Sanskrit form is considerably older than the Roerich movement, but it does not designate a single continuously identifiable stone. The [Monier-Williams digital dictionary](https://sanskrit-lexicon.github.io/MWS/) and the [Sanskrit Heritage lexical resources](https://sanskrit.uohyd.ac.in/SKT/) provide the philological framework.

**Tibetan:** ཡིད་བཞིན་ནོར་བུ, *yid bzhin nor bu*.

This means approximately “jewel [that acts] according to one’s wish.” The ordinary term *nor bu rin po che* means “precious jewel”; it is not inherently the name of one unique artifact. Nicholas Roerich generally printed “Norbu-rin-poche” or “Norbu-rinpoche,” treating the expression as a Tibetan counterpart of Chintamani. Heinrich August Jäschke’s 1881 dictionary, which Roerich cited in 1924, described a fabulous jewel whose possession procures inexhaustible riches and whose form resembles an oval fruit. The dictionary is accessible through the [University of Chicago’s Digital South Asia Library](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/dictionaries/jaschke/).

**Russian Roerichian names:**

- Камень, *Kamen’* — “the Stone”
- Камень Чинтамани, *Kamen’ Chintamani*
- Сокровище Мира, *Sokrovishche Mira* — “Treasure of the World”
- Дар Ориона, *Dar Oriona* — “Gift of Orion”
- Черный Камень, *Chernyi Kamen’* — “Black Stone”

**English Roerichian names:**

- the Stone
- the Black Stone
- Chintamani
- Treasure of the World
- Gift of Orion
- the Wandering Stone
- the sacred Stone

The earliest Roerich-family private records usually call it simply “the Stone.” The confident equation with the Hindu *cintāmaṇi*, Tibetan precious jewel, Grail stone, Solomon’s stone, and Shambhala relic is interpretive rather than philologically demonstrated.

### 1.3 Physical description of the 1923 object

The principal evidence is a black-and-white photograph preserved or reproduced from the Nicholas Roerich Museum archive. It shows a small object with a roughly heart-, seed-, fruit-, shell-, or tortoise-carapace-like profile. From the photograph it appears:

- approximately palm-sized;
- dark or gray-black;
- oblong;
- broadly bilaterally or axially symmetrical;
- flattened on the unseen or lower side;
- domed or humped above;
- edged by a relatively sharp lower perimeter;
- marked by an upper ridge or irregular crest.

Helena Roerich’s 1923 “Legend of the Stone” supplied several overlapping descriptions:

- “shaped like a human heart”;
- “the shape of a flat fruit or heart, oblong in form”;
- “length of my little finger”;
- “grayish luster like a dried fruit”;
- containing, in the legendary account, a “resplendent crystal.”

Those phrases occur within a visionary literary composition and cannot all be treated as measurements of the photographed object. Nevertheless, the photographed shape is compatible with the fruit- or heart-like language. A detailed independent discussion and reproduction appear in Ian Heron’s [material-evidence survey](https://roerichandchintamani.wordpress.com/2012/02/25/the-chintamani-of-the-roerichs-extraordinary-tales-of-an-extraterrestrial-talisman-2/).

No reliable published record gives:

- exact dimensions;
- weight;
- density;
- magnetic properties;
- surface composition;
- fracture characteristics;
- fusion crust;
- Widmanstätten structure;
- radionuclide profile;
- isotopic composition;
- petrographic section;
- catalogue or accession number.

The words “fragment of a meteorite,” common in later Roerich literature, therefore describe a traditional attribution, not a published scientific finding.

### 1.4 The casket and wrappings

Nicholas Roerich’s letter reporting receipt stated that the Stone was enclosed in a *kovcheg*—an ark, reliquary, or casket—and that the casket was wrapped in old purple velvet bearing a sign and the letter **M**. He reported a distinctive fragrance that became stronger over the course of a day. He also said the casket would remain unopened for three weeks and that the family would photograph it.

Surviving photographs show:

- an apparently old rectangular casket;
- decorated leather or another dark covering;
- shield-like, bird-like, human, or heraldic figures, depending on the observer;
- signs interpreted by the Roerichs as alchemical or Rosicrucian;
- a textile wrapping;
- an exterior pine shipping box addressed to “Monsieur et Madame N. Roerich,” care of Bankers Trust in Paris, reportedly marked “de la part de MM.”

The lettering “MM” was interpreted within the Roerich circle as “Master Morya.” That interpretation is not a secure identification of the sender.

The casket’s age, geographic origin, wood species, leather, pigments, inscriptions, repairs, and construction have not been reported through conservation analysis. “Medieval” is an institutional or devotional attribution, not an established scientific date.

### 1.5 Inscriptions

No authenticated inscription on the Stone itself has been published. Modern retellings sometimes claim that it bears Sanskrit, Tibetan, Shambhalic, or extraterrestrial characters. The available 1923 photograph is insufficient to establish writing.

The casket reportedly bears signs, figures, shields, and repeated “M” forms. No published epigraphic study establishes:

- the script;
- a reading;
- a date;
- a language;
- a known heraldic owner;
- a workshop or place of manufacture.

### 1.6 Representations

The object or its container appears, directly or symbolically, in works connected with the family:

- **Nicholas Roerich, *Treasure of the World—Chintamani***, usually dated 1924: a white horse descends carrying a flaming treasure. This is iconography, not a portrait of the small photographed object.
- **Svetoslav Roerich, portrait of Nicholas Roerich with the casket**, 1928: Nicholas is shown holding the reliquary.
- **Svetoslav Roerich, portrait of Helena Roerich**, 1937: the casket or its symbolism is again associated with the family.
- **Nicholas Roerich, *Chintamani***, 1935–1936: a later symbolic treatment.
- Additional paintings—including *Burning of Darkness*, *Command of Rigden Djapo*, *White Stone*, and Shambhala compositions—were later interpreted through the Stone narrative, though not all are literal depictions of the object.

The Nicholas Roerich Museum’s earlier catalogue lists *Treasure of the World—Chintamani* as tempera on canvas, 48 × 30 inches. The museum’s present collection catalogue records its paintings but does not publicly catalogue the physical Stone as an accessioned artifact. See the [museum catalogue](https://roerich.org/museum-paintings-catalogue.php) and its [1974 collection booklet](https://nrm.s3.amazonaws.com/website/archive/02_About_Nicholas_Roerich/1974%20-%20Nicholas%20Roerich.%20%28Booklet%29%20NRM.pdf).

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 The older religious motif

The Indian *cintāmaṇi* is not originally an extraterrestrial black meteorite with a traceable royal custody chain. It is a fabulous jewel or literary-religious category whose forms include:

- a jewel granting material wishes;
- one of the treasures of a universal monarch;
- a luminous jewel associated with nāgas;
- an emblem borne by bodhisattvas;
- a metaphor for the Buddha, Dharma, bodhicitta, compassion, a mantra, or awakened mind;
- an attribute of Cintāmaṇicakra Avalokiteśvara and other Buddhist figures.

The motif occurs widely in Sanskrit Buddhist and Hindu literature, but assigning one “first appearance” is difficult because many texts have composite dates and much older contents than their surviving manuscripts. The securely defensible statement is that wish-granting *maṇi* traditions were established in Indian Buddhist literature many centuries before the Roerichs; no early text located in this research supplies the Roerich Stone’s Orion–Shambhala–Solomon custody chain.

The [Digital Sanskrit Buddhist Canon](https://dsbcproject.org/) and the [GRETIL *Mahāvastu* text](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/1_sanskr/4_rellit/buddh/mhvastuu.htm) demonstrate the broad primary-text environment in which miraculous jewels occur. They do not authenticate the Paris object.

In Tibet the jewel became a pervasive emblem. The jewel may appear alone, in triple form, flaming, or on the back of the wind horse (*rlung rta*). A 1936 photograph from the Norbulingka stables records a painted horse bearing a flaming jewel ornament; the Pitt Rivers Museum identifies the emblem as Cintamani and notes its occurrence on wind-horse prayer flags. This provides independent evidence for the iconographic motif, but not for a unique stone traveling through history. See the [Pitt Rivers Museum Tibetan photographic collection](https://tibet.prm.ox.ac.uk/tibet/photo_BMR.86.1.48.1.html).

### 2.2 Earliest attestation of the specifically Roerichian Stone

The specifically Roerichian Stone emerges in 1923.

Helena Roerich’s notebook for March–June 1923 records a message concerning “the Ray of the Black Stone, which I will send to you.” This predates the Paris delivery and indicates that a promised object already occupied the family’s visionary communications. See [Notebook 15](https://materlatina.com/cadernos/en-notebook-15-26-03-192316-06-1923.html).

Her notebook for August–October 1923 contains:

- a vision on 25 August of a hand holding an elongated Black Stone;
- flames at both ends;
- a sensation of heat at her chest;
- instructions to carry the Stone in an ivory casket;
- instructions not to touch it when first opened;
- directions to photograph and close it;
- narrative fragments later assembled as the “Legend of the Stone”;
- an expectation, recorded on 16 September, that the Stone would arrive.

These are the earliest datable sources for the object’s anticipated Roerichian identity. They are private spiritual or mediumistic records—not independent reports from an ancient custodial institution. See [Helena Roerich, Notebook 17](https://materlatina.com/cadernos/en-notebook-17-16-08-192306-10-1923.html).

### 2.3 Physical appearance in the documentary record

The first securely dated physical event is **6 October 1923, 11:00 a.m., Paris**.

Nicholas Roerich wrote to Vladimir Shibayev that a package containing “the Stone” had arrived through Bankers Trust. The Roerichs were then staying at 1 rue de Messine, generally identified with the Lord Byron Hotel. Nicholas called the news a joyful but profound secret; he said only the New York circle and Shibayev were to know, warned against disclosure, described the casket and velvet, and promised a photograph.

This contemporary letter is the central provenance document. The Russian text is reproduced in [Nicholas Roerich’s collected letters](https://itexts.net/avtor-nikolay-konstantinovich-rerih/444425-pisma-tom-i-1896-1932-nikolay-rerih/read/page-8.html).

Some secondary accounts give 7 or 9 October. These appear to derive respectively from the date of the reporting letter or from later recollection. The contemporary sentence “Yesterday, 6 October” favors 6 October as the delivery date.

### 2.4 Unknown manufacture

No source establishes who:

- extracted, found, or fashioned the object;
- placed it in the casket;
- made the casket;
- wrapped it;
- deposited it with Bankers Trust;
- commissioned its delivery.

Its documented origin is therefore not a quarry, meteorite fall, workshop, excavation, or ancient treasury, but a **Paris package transmitted through a bank in October 1923**.

### 2.5 The claimed celestial origin

The Roerichian narrative states that the primary Stone came to Earth from Orion; the main body remained with the Brotherhood at Shambhala, while a fragment traveled among nations at decisive moments. Later writers added Sirius, extraterrestrial messengers, millions of years, geomagnetic mechanisms, and planetary recalibration.

Chronologically:

- **1923 private notebooks:** Black Stone, Orion, traveling fragment, Shambhala, historical rulers.
- **1924 publication:** “gift of Orion”; Stone moves between East and West and heralds a new era.
- **1930 *Shambhala*:** the “famous black stone,” fallen “from some other world,” identifies with Hindu Chintamani and Tibetan Norbu-rinpoche.
- **Later Roerichian and New Age literature:** increasingly literal extraterrestrial and technological interpretations.

No astronomical, meteoritical, or archaeological evidence demonstrates origin in Orion. Meteorites derive from Solar System bodies unless extraordinary evidence establishes otherwise; no such evidence has been published for this object.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 Traditional or legendary chain

The 1923–1924 “Legend of the Stone” is episodic and internally non-linear. Its claimed holders or stations include:

- a celestial origin associated with Orion;
- Shambhala or its Brotherhood;
- an unnamed ruler of India;
- Lanka and Ravana;
- a Chinese emperor;
- Solomon, to whom the spirit Uroil Zena supposedly delivered it;
- Ephraim, an otherwise unattested goldsmith ordered to cut a portion for Solomon’s ring;
- Timur or Tamerlane;
- Timur’s grandson;
- Mount Ararat;
- Novgorod and an unnamed knight;
- a “mountain of Pride”;
- Western religious authorities;
- a shrine brought from Rothenburg;
- an “unsuccessful union of western nations”;
- a future return eastward.

Nicholas Roerich’s 1930 *Shambhala* added or emphasized Solomon, Akbar, Timur, a Chinese emperor, and the tower of Rigden Jyepo. The complete contemporary English publication is available as [“Chintamani, the Treasure of the World,” *The Foreword*, December 1924](https://nrm.s3.amazonaws.com/website/archive/01_Nicholas_Roerich%27s_writings/Periodicals%20-%20articles%20of%20N.Roerich/N.%20Roerich%20-%20Legend%20%28The%20Foreword%2C%201924%2C%20Dec%29.pdf).

None of these legendary links supplies identifying physical details sufficient to connect an ancient object to the 1923 photographed piece. No inventory, royal chronicle, inscription, ring, excavation report, or contemporary witness corroborates the chain.

### 3.2 Documented chain

#### Before 6 October 1923: unknown

The Stone’s whereabouts and ownership are undocumented. The packaging reportedly said it was sent “on behalf of MM,” but that is not a legal sender’s identity.

A later oral account attributed to an unnamed informant of Nicholas Roerich Museum director Daniel Entin claimed that a member of an unnamed French secret society acted as intermediary and that the society released the object on instructions from another unnamed authority. Because neither society, informant, depositor, bank record, nor instruction has been produced, this remains unverified hearsay.

#### 6 October 1923: receipt in Paris

**Custodians:** Nicholas and Helena Roerich.  
**Mechanism:** package delivered through Bankers Trust.  
**Place:** Paris, apparently the Lord Byron Hotel/1 rue de Messine.  
**Evidence:** Nicholas Roerich’s near-contemporary letter; Helena Roerich’s notebooks; photographs of the Stone, casket, wrapping, and shipping box.

The family initially observed ritual precautions: delayed opening, limited disclosure, photography, avoidance of direct touch, and preparation of another inner container.

#### Late 1923: Paris to India

The Roerich family traveled by sea and arrived in Bombay on 2 December 1923. An International Centre of the Roerichs expedition history identifies the steamer as the *Macedonia*. The family subsequently traveled through Indian cultural centers and to Sikkim and Darjeeling. See the [ICR expedition hall](https://www.en.icr.su/museum/layout/expedition/) and the [National Gallery of Modern Art biography](https://www.ngmaindia.gov.in/ce_nicholas-roerich.asp).

It is highly probable that the casket traveled with them. The presence of the Stone itself is reported within family tradition but is not independently inventoried at Bombay customs or another checkpoint.

#### 1924–1925: India and expedition preparation

The family resided and traveled in Sikkim and northern India while planning the Central Asian Expedition. Nicholas painted *Treasure of the World—Chintamani*. Helena’s revealed text was published in English under Nicholas’s name in December 1924.

The object’s day-to-day storage is not documented publicly.

#### 1925–1928: Central Asian Expedition

Roerichian accounts state that the Stone accompanied the expedition. The documented expedition route ran through:

- Sikkim and northern India;
- Kashmir and Ladakh;
- the Karakoram;
- Khotan, Yarkand, Kashgar, Aksu, Kucha, Karashahr, Turfan, and Ürümqi;
- the Tian Shan and Lake Zaysan;
- Omsk and Moscow;
- Altai and Siberia;
- Buryatia and Ulaanbaatar;
- the Gobi, Gansu, Qinghai/Tsaidam, and Tibet;
- finally Sikkim and India.

An extensive route summary is supplied by the [Kazakhstan Roerich expedition project](https://www.roerich.kz/exp/), while expedition publications and photographs are catalogued by the [Nicholas Roerich Museum](https://www.roerich.org/roerich-museum-archive/expeditions.php).

The chain nevertheless has serious evidentiary weaknesses:

- No published customs declaration identifies the Stone.
- No expedition inventory available here lists it.
- No independent expedition member’s published scientific description establishes its continual presence.
- Statements that it crossed “all Asia” derive chiefly from family and follower tradition.
- It may have been concealed because of its religious or political significance within the group.

#### June 1926: Soviet Union and the Barchenko substitution theory

The expedition entered the Soviet Union, traveled to Moscow, and met officials. This generated a later theory involving Soviet occult researcher Aleksandr Barchenko:

1. the Roerichs brought the Stone into the USSR;
2. the original was transferred to Barchenko or installed at his laboratory;
3. a substitute meteorite fragment was given to the Roerichs;
4. the replacement continued with the expedition.

Variants link the operation to Gleb Bokii and Soviet security-service occult research.

No cited transfer receipt, laboratory inventory, police file, or surviving specimen proves this exchange. It is a speculative reconstruction from circumstantial associations and conflicting later stories. It cannot be inserted into the documented chain as fact.

#### 1926–1928: Mongolia and Tibet

The expedition spent months in Ulaanbaatar in 1926–1927 and traveled through Mongolia and Tibet. Some interpretations treat Mongolia as the symbolic destination of the Stone’s mission. Others say that the Stone was intended for the Panchen Lama, the Dalai Lama, Shambhala, the Altai, Soviet Russia, or a future federation of Asia.

The expedition was forcibly detained by Tibetan authorities north of Nagchu from October 1927 until March 1928. Roerich’s cable reporting the journey lists the itinerary and hardships but says nothing about the Stone. See the [contemporary expedition cable](https://roerichsmuseum.website.yandexcloud.net/DE/DE-034.pdf).

No verifiable handover in Mongolia, Tibet, or Shambhala is documented.

#### 1928: return to India

After the expedition, the casket was demonstrably—or at least pictorially—back with Nicholas Roerich. Svetoslav Roerich’s 1928 portrait shows Nicholas holding it. This is strong evidence that the casket was not permanently buried, surrendered, or left in Tibet.

It does not prove that the original 1923 object was still inside. A closed container cannot establish its contents.

#### 1928–1947: Naggar/Kullu

The Roerichs settled in Naggar in the Kullu Valley. The casket remained a central family symbol. Nicholas died in 1947. No public will, probate inventory, museum register, or succession instrument located in this research identifies the Stone.

The likeliest family succession, based on residence and later testimony, is:

- Nicholas and Helena Roerich jointly;
- Helena after Nicholas’s death;
- one or both sons, George and Svetoslav;
- eventually Svetoslav, who remained in India and managed substantial portions of the family heritage.

This is inference, not a complete documented legal chain.

#### 1947–1955: Helena Roerich

Helena moved to Kalimpong and died in 1955. Whether the Stone accompanied her or remained at Naggar is not publicly established.

#### 1955–1993: George and Svetoslav Roerich

George returned to the Soviet Union in 1957 and died in Moscow in 1960. No reliable evidence shows that he brought the Stone with him.

Svetoslav remained in India and died in 1993. Later Roerichian accounts commonly imply that he retained custody of the casket and relic, but no publicly available catalogue or dated custody declaration settles the matter.

#### After 1993: disputed and unknown

Several incompatible modern accounts circulate:

- the Stone remained in India at the Naggar estate;
- it passed to the International Centre of the Roerichs in Moscow;
- it was personally held by ICR director Lyudmila Shaposhnikova;
- it remained in a private family or initiatory custody;
- it had already been returned to Shambhala;
- the casket survives but is empty;
- the original was exchanged in Moscow in 1926;
- the relic was never a meteorite and its keepers avoid examination.

Ian Heron reported that modern photographs of the casket seemed to have originated in Russia and recorded a rumor connecting it with Shaposhnikova, while also noting contrary information attributed to a source of Daniel Entin. Heron ultimately could not establish present custody. See his [custody discussion](https://roerichandchintamani.wordpress.com/2012/03/02/208/).

As of the research cutoff, **the public chain ends with the Roerich family. The present custodian and location are unverified.**

### 3.3 The “cornerstone casket” confusion

The Stone’s casket has sometimes been said to have been deposited in the foundation of the Master Building/Roerich Museum in New York in 1929. Evidence reviewed by Heron indicates that the cornerstone deposit was a different casket brought from India by Sina Lichtmann and Frances Grant. The Stone’s distinctive casket returned to India and appears in post-expedition imagery.

The New York cornerstone claim is therefore best classified as a documented conflation between two containers.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Did a physical object exist?

Yes, with high confidence. A photograph, near-contemporary letters, packaging images, and repeated descriptions support the existence of a small object delivered to the Roerichs in 1923.

### 4.2 Was it stone?

Probably, but not scientifically established. The photograph resembles a small mineral object. It could conceivably have been metal, slag, ceramic, glass, a fossil, a worked mineral, or a composite. No test results are available.

### 4.3 Was it a meteorite?

Unproven.

Evidence offered in favor consists of:

- dark color;
- unusual profile;
- legendary celestial origin;
- statements by later Roerichians;
- speculative resemblance to meteorite fragments.

Missing evidence includes:

- iron-nickel assay;
- chondrules;
- fusion crust;
- regmaglypts;
- mineral phases diagnostic of meteorites;
- isotopic ratios;
- chain of custody from a documented fall or find.

The term “meteorite fragment” should therefore always be preceded by “claimed,” “said to be,” or equivalent language.

### 4.4 The Armanty/Mongon-Temee hypothesis

Ian Heron proposed that the object was a fragment of the giant Armanty iron meteorite, called Mongon-temee (“Silver Camel”) or Mongon-tosh (“Silver Saddle”), formerly situated in the Altai region and later moved to Ürümqi. The hypothesis rests on:

- the meteorite’s Central Asian setting;
- possible geographic symbolism for the Roerichs;
- the parent mass’s shape and local names;
- the possibility that a fragment could have been removed;
- perceived resemblance between the photographed talisman and meteoritic forms.

Heron’s summary states the identification positively, but the underlying argument is inferential. No trace-element comparison between the Roerich object and Armanty has been performed or published. No documented extraction of the fragment, transfer to Europe, or chain from Central Asia to Bankers Trust has been found. The hypothesis is ingenious but unverified. See [“The Roerichs’ Stone: A Summary of Research Findings”](https://roerichandchintamani.wordpress.com/2012/03/13/the-roerichs-stone-a-brief-summary-of-research-findings/).

### 4.5 Was it the ancient Indian *cintāmaṇi*?

This is not historically demonstrable. The Sanskrit word designates a fabulous jewel and symbolic category. There is no evidence for a single ancient stone preserved through all textual appearances.

### 4.6 Was it the Tibetan *nor bu rin po che*?

The expression means “precious jewel” and can denote sacred jewels generically. Roerich’s equivalence is comparative and theological, not an identification supported by Tibetan provenance documents.

### 4.7 Was it the Grail stone or *lapis exilis*?

Roerich connected the Stone with:

- the Grail;
- the *lapis exilis* of Wolfram von Eschenbach’s *Parzival*;
- the Philosopher’s Stone;
- Masonic symbolism;
- Solomon’s ring;
- meteorites and sacred black stones.

These are syncretic correspondences. Similarity of motif does not establish material identity. The Roerichian Stone’s alleged European medieval custody is first attested in the 1923 revelation, not in medieval inventories.

### 4.8 Was the “Legend of the Stone” ancient?

No evidence supports that description.

The published text presents itself as fragments of ancient traditions, but Helena Roerich’s dated notebooks show the material being composed or received during August–September 1923, shortly before the package arrived. Nicholas’s 1924 preface cites:

- Ferdinand Ossendowski’s *Beasts, Men and Gods* (1922);
- Jäschke’s Tibetan dictionary;
- contemporary astronomical reporting;
- comparative religious motifs.

The composite legend is therefore a documented twentieth-century esoteric synthesis drawing on older motifs. Individual components may be ancient; the joined custody narrative is not demonstrably so.

### 4.9 League of Nations claim

Later literature alleges that the Stone had been entrusted to the League of Nations and came to Roerich after the League failed to create peace.

Problems include:

- no League accession or transfer record is cited;
- the Stone reached Roerich in 1923;
- Roerich’s major League-related campaign for the cultural-protection pact belongs primarily to 1929–1931;
- contemporary correspondence says the package came through Bankers Trust, not from the League;
- the 1924 “Legend” refers cryptically to an unsuccessful Western union but does not supply documentary custody by the League.

The League story is a later accretion. Roerich did interact with League organs concerning his cultural-protection proposals, but that does not establish League possession of the Stone. Recent archival scholarship also finds that public claims about League support for the Roerich Pact exceeded the more cautious internal record. See [“Alternative Views of War and Peace Between the World Wars”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/07075332.2025.2546408).

### 4.10 Continuity problem

Even if the 1923 object survives, no public evidence establishes that any present claimant holds:

- the same Stone;
- the same casket;
- the casket with its original contents;
- an authorized replacement;
- a fragment of the same alleged parent body.

The distinction between container and contents is especially important. The casket appears in paintings and modern photographs; the Stone has rarely been openly shown.

### 4.11 Scientific examination

No peer-reviewed scientific examination of the Roerich object has been located. Claims that it contains a substance called “Moriy,” emits beneficial radiation, changes mass or color, or enhances psychic reception are religious or occult assertions.

The authority to authenticate it has consequently remained with:

- Helena and Nicholas Roerich, through revelation and recognition;
- members of their initiatory circle;
- family custodians;
- Roerich institutions and sympathetic writers.

It has never publicly shifted to a mineralogical laboratory, meteoritical society, archaeological authority, or museum conservation department.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Nicholas Roerich (1874–1947)

Recipient and custodian from 1923; authorial publisher of the 1924 legend; painter of Chintamani imagery; expedition leader. His writings transformed the private relic into a symbol of Shambhala and world renewal. **Evidence:** letters, paintings, books, expedition records.

### Helena Roerich (1879–1955)

Principal recorder and theological interpreter. Her notebooks contain the earliest Roerichian revelations, anticipated delivery, descriptions, handling instructions, powers, and legendary custody chain. **Evidence:** dated notebooks and correspondence.

### George/Yuri Roerich (1902–1960)

Orientalist and expedition member. He participated in the journey during which the object was said to have traveled but is not independently documented as its owner. **Evidence:** expedition association; later family tradition.

### Svetoslav Roerich (1904–1993)

Painter of portraits showing his father and mother with the casket; probable later family custodian or inheritor, though the legal transfer is undocumented. **Evidence:** portraits and family-estate history.

### Vladimir Anatolyevich Shibayev

Nicholas’s correspondent and trusted associate. He received the earliest detailed written disclosure of the delivery. His role was informational, not custodial. **Evidence:** Nicholas’s October 1923 letter.

### Sina Lichtmann/Fosdick

Member of the New York circle. She later described the casket’s painted shields, birds, and seated figure. She and Frances Grant brought a different casket from India for the Master Building cornerstone, creating later confusion. **Evidence:** memoir material and institutional histories.

### Frances Grant

Roerich associate and writer. In *Oriental Philosophy* (1936) she interpreted Chintamani as a sign of Maitreya’s approaching era. **Evidence:** published devotional interpretation.

### Master Morya / “M.M.”

The chief spiritual authority named in Helena’s notebooks. Instructions concerning the Stone were attributed to him. “MM” on the package was interpreted as his sign. His role belongs to the Roerichs’ religious account; no independently identifiable sender has been established.

### Rigden Jyepo

The prophesied ruler of Shambhala in Roerichian discourse. The main Stone supposedly remains in his tower, radiating influence over the world. **Evidence:** Roerich’s *Shambhala*; religious claim.

### Maitreya

The future Buddha. Roerichian writers treated the traveling Stone as a token of Maitreya’s advent or coming era. This linkage is a twentieth-century Shambhala interpretation built on older Buddhist expectation.

### Solomon

Claimed legendary recipient from the spirit Uroil Zena. He supposedly had a fragment cut for a ring. No biblical, ancient Jewish, or early Islamic source has been shown to contain this exact episode. **Tier:** 1923 Roerichian revelation.

### “Ephraim,” goldsmith of Judah

Supposedly cut the Stone for Solomon. No independent attestation located. **Tier:** Roerichian legend.

### Timur/Tamerlane

Claimed historical bearer whose power was enhanced by the Stone. No Timurid inventory or chronicle corroborates it. **Tier:** Roerichian legend.

### Akbar

Named by Nicholas Roerich as a possessor. No Mughal inventory, court chronicle, or jewel catalogue has been identified as evidence. **Tier:** Roerichian legend.

### Unnamed Chinese emperor

Said to have built a turquoise temple for the jewel. No dynasty, reign, temple, or Chinese text is specified. **Tier:** legend.

### Ravana

The Stone is said in the 1924 text to have lain hidden on Lanka because of Ravana’s treachery. This episode is not established as part of the Sanskrit *Rāmāyaṇa*. **Tier:** modern syncretic legend.

### Aleksandr Barchenko and Gleb Bokii

Connected by later researchers to occult and political networks in early Soviet Russia. A secret transfer or substitution of the Stone has been proposed but not documented. **Tier:** speculative reconstruction.

### Henry A. Wallace

The American politician wrote to Roerich in March 1933 of the “sacred, most precious casket,” the New Country, seven stars, three stars, and awaiting the Stone. His letter proves that Stone symbolism entered Roerich’s American political-esoteric network; it does not show that Wallace possessed or inspected it. The episode is discussed in Andrei Znamenski’s [study of Roerich and American messianic politics](https://ir.ung.edu/files/pdf?fileid=388a8ba9-b5b1-4386-a2b6-b5022d8391e8).

### Bankers Trust, Paris

Transmission institution through which the package reached the Roerichs. The bank is the only documented non-family link in the physical delivery chain. No underlying deposit record has been published.

### Nicholas Roerich Museum, New York

Holds archival photographs and correspondence relating to the Stone. It does not publicly claim current possession of the physical object. Its institutional description and collections are available on the [museum website](https://www.roerich.org/museum-about.php).

### International Centre of the Roerichs, Moscow

Displayed and published Roerich material and has been rumored to possess the casket or Stone. Its public materials do not provide a verifiable current accession entry for the Stone. See the [ICR website](https://www.en.icr.su/).

### International Roerich Memorial Trust, Naggar

Controls or administers significant parts of the family’s Indian memorial estate. Its public description lists museums, paintings, household material, and expedition collections but does not identify the Stone among displayed holdings. See the [IRMT institutional description](https://irmtkullu.com/about-us/structure-and-activities/).

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Handling protocol

The 1923 instructions prescribed:

- awaiting the Stone in a prepared spiritual state;
- initially leaving the casket unopened;
- not touching the Stone directly;
- photographing it;
- placing it in an ivory box;
- wrapping or shielding it;
- exposing it to cedar balsam rather than wine;
- permitting bearers to become accustomed to its supposed radiation.

These instructions are attested as Roerichian practice or intended practice. Their claimed physical necessity is not scientifically supported.

### 6.2 Divination

The legend gives the Stone an omen-system:

- darkening foretells clouds;
- heaviness foretells bloodshed;
- a star above it signals success;
- creaking signals an approaching enemy;
- a fiery dream signals world convulsion;
- tranquility authorizes courageous action;
- heat, vibration, cracks, weight, and color changes disclose future events.

No controlled observation or contemporaneous measurement has been published.

### 6.3 Psychic communication

Within Agni Yoga, the fragment carried a “ray” linking its custodian with the Brotherhood. It was said to enhance receptivity and communication. Later descriptions call it an energetic *teraphim*. These functions explain the secrecy, controlled access, and reluctance to submit it to ordinary handling.

### 6.4 Legitimation

Possession signified selection for a historical mission. In the legend, worthy rulers prosper while selfish holders lose it. For the Roerichs, receipt validated:

- their claimed relationship with the Masters;
- their Central Asian mission;
- their plans for spiritual-political renewal;
- their identification of culture as an instrument of world transformation;
- their expectation of a new era associated with Shambhala and Maitreya.

The Stone did not confer legally recognized sovereignty. Its power was charismatic and initiatory.

### 6.5 Expeditionary function

The object was reportedly carried as:

- a protective talisman;
- a sign that the expedition acted under Shambhala’s mandate;
- a possible object of delivery;
- a symbolic seed for a new polity or spiritual center.

The intended recipient is inconsistent across sources. Proposed destinations include Shambhala, the Panchen Lama, Soviet Russia, Mongolia, and a future spiritual federation.

### 6.6 Political symbolism

The Stone became entangled with:

- Roerich’s “Great Plan” for an Asian spiritual-political union;
- Soviet contacts;
- Mongolian and Tibetan prophecy;
- Henry Wallace and American New Deal esotericism;
- the Banner of Peace and Roerich Pact;
- the expectation of Maitreya.

The Stone’s documented political effect lies in its influence on rhetoric and correspondence, not in any independently demonstrated supernatural intervention.

### 6.7 Access rules

The earliest letter demanded secrecy. Only a narrow circle was to know. Direct touching was prohibited or discouraged. The casket, rather than the exposed Stone, became the public visual form. Restricted access helped preserve its sacred authority but also prevented independent authentication.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The celestial fall

**Story:** A great Stone arrived from Orion and founded or marked Shambhala. Its principal mass remains in the Brotherhood’s tower; fragments travel through the world.

**Earliest Roerichian attestation:** Helena Roerich’s 1923 notebooks and the 1924 published legend.

**Later accretions:** Sirius as an intermediary; extraterrestrial pilots; millions of years of history; technological functions.

**Evidence:** no physical or astronomical confirmation.

### 7.2 The wandering fragment

**Story:** A fragment is sent to nations during epochal change. Its presence brings power and prosperity; when a mission ends, the Stone returns eastward.

This is the narrative device that allows disparate sacred stones and famous rulers to be joined into one custody chain. It also makes absence unfalsifiable: the Stone “comes of itself” and cannot be retained by an unworthy possessor.

### 7.3 Solomon and the ring

**Story:** Uroil Zena delivered the Stone to Solomon. Solomon ordered Ephraim to cut off a portion and mount it in silver, engraving a flaming chalice. The act violated the integrity of divine substance.

**Attestation:** 1923–1924 Roerich text.

**Factual core:** ancient and medieval traditions assign Solomon magical rings, seals, control over spirits, and marvelous stones. No evidence connects those traditions to the Paris object.

### 7.4 Timur, Akbar, and rulers of Asia

**Story:** Timur, Akbar, an Indian ruler, and a Chinese emperor gained authority through the Stone. Its departure explained reversals of fortune.

**Function:** the legend interprets political power as temporary stewardship granted by a transhistorical spiritual authority.

**Evidence:** no contemporaneous royal source connects these rulers to a single Chintamani Stone.

### 7.5 Lanka and Ravana

The 1924 text says that the Stone lay on Lanka, hidden because of Ravana’s treachery. This grafts the object onto the *Rāmāyaṇa* world without supplying a textual citation. It is a modern elaboration.

### 7.6 Ararat and Novgorod

The fiery Stone is placed on Mount Ararat; an unnamed Novgorod knight dies over it because of unbelief. The freedom of Novgorod is interpreted as proof that the city once possessed it. These are etiological claims, not documentary history.

### 7.7 The Grail and *lapis exilis*

Roerich invoked the mysterious Grail stone of Wolfram’s *Parzival* and the alchemical wandering stone. The comparison helped universalize Chintamani as a single hidden object behind multiple civilizations. Comparative resemblance is real; identity is unproved.

### 7.8 The Rothenburg casket

A “Father Sulpicius” receives a vision directing that the heart-shaped Stone be placed in a shrine brought from Rothenburg, marked by four squares and the letter M. Later family interpretations linked this passage to their actual casket.

No medieval Father Sulpicius, Rothenburg inventory, or identifiable shrine has been established. The story is first attested immediately before the Paris package appeared.

### 7.9 The League of Nations

**Late story:** the fragment had been sent to assist the League; after the League failed, it passed to Roerich for return to Asia.

The allegation gained popularity through occult writers, particularly Andrew Tomas and later New Age authors. It lacks a League record and conflicts with the better-documented Bankers Trust delivery.

### 7.10 Nightly planetary ritual

Later Agni Yoga literature describes the main Stone resting underground on a cushion and marble foundation separated by a lithium disk. Members of the Brotherhood supposedly conduct nightly vigils that establish or restore planetary rhythm.

This highly technical imagery is a later esoteric elaboration, not part of early Indian *cintāmaṇi* tradition and not evidence about the Paris object.

### 7.11 Theft by Jesuits

A notebook passage states that “the Jesuits have stolen It in Paris.” Context and chronology are ambiguous: it may belong to the legendary historical chain rather than report a 1923 police incident. No theft report, named Jesuit, date, or recovery document has been found. It must not be narrated as a documented modern theft.

### 7.12 Return to Shambhala

One modern ending says that the Roerich expedition successfully returned the fragment to Shambhala. The survival of the casket in the 1928 portrait complicates but does not logically disprove that claim; the container might have returned empty. Conversely, the claim has no documented handover.

Another version says the Stone remained with the family because its mission had not ended. Its current disappearance permits both narratives to continue.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Status

**Unlocated or held in undisclosed private/institutional custody.**

No publicly accessible institution currently provides all of the following:

- an accession number;
- a present photograph with date and scale;
- a legal ownership statement;
- provenance documentation;
- a conservation report;
- permission for scientific examination.

### 8.2 Leading location hypotheses

#### India

The object may have remained among materials associated with Svetoslav Roerich, the Naggar estate, or the International Roerich Memorial Trust. The Trust’s public museum description does not list it.

#### Russia

The casket or object may have passed to the Soviet Roerich Foundation or International Centre of the Roerichs after Svetoslav’s death. Modern photographs of the casket circulated from Russian sources, encouraging this inference. No verifiable accession record was found.

#### Separate custody

The Stone may be privately held while one or more institutions possess only photographs or the casket. This possibility fits traditions of initiatory secrecy but remains unverified.

#### Lost, exchanged, or destroyed

No documented destruction is known. Substitution in Moscow, accidental loss, theft, or separation from the casket cannot be excluded because the chain is incomplete.

#### “Returned to Shambhala”

This is a religious claim, not a geographically verifiable location.

### 8.3 Condition

Unknown. The casket appears to have survived long enough to be photographed in the modern period, but its present condition and contents cannot be confirmed.

### 8.4 Legal control

Unknown. Potential stakeholders include Roerich family successors, Indian estate authorities, the International Roerich Memorial Trust, Russian Roerich institutions, or an unidentified private custodian. No public litigation specifically determining title to the Stone was located.

### 8.5 Access

Effectively unavailable. The public can consult reproductions, paintings, writings, and archival photographs. The physical object is not known to be on regular public display.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Roerich painting and publication

The Stone’s modern visual identity is primarily Nicholas Roerich’s creation. The white horse carrying a flaming jewel became more influential than the photographed dark fragment. His 1924 article and 1930 *Shambhala* established the basic English-language narrative. The complete 1930 text is available through the [Nicholas Roerich Museum](https://www.roerich.org/roerich-writings-shambhala.php).

### 9.2 Agni Yoga

The Stone functions in Agni Yoga as a material sign of the Hierarchy, the future age, psychic energy, and the bond between earthly agents and the Masters. Later followers expanded its energetic and cosmic characteristics.

### 9.3 Portraiture and relic imagery

Svetoslav Roerich’s portraits made the casket an emblem of Nicholas’s authority. A later viewer could recognize the object without ever seeing its contents. Google Arts & Culture’s ICR material on Svetoslav’s portraits refers to the 1928 portrait of Nicholas with a casket in the sequence of commissioned full-length images. See [Portrait of N. K. Roerich in a Tibetan Robe](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/portrait-of-n-k-roerich-in-a-tibetan-robe/QwE_T-Ixfwo6oA?hl=en).

### 9.4 Occult literature

Writers such as Andrew Tomas incorporated the Stone into narratives of:

- extraterrestrial contact;
- Shambhala as a physical hidden center;
- ancient advanced technology;
- secret societies;
- the Grail and Philosopher’s Stone;
- global political transformation.

These books substantially shaped popular belief but generally do not supply primary provenance documents.

### 9.5 New Age commodification

“Chintamani stones” are now sold commercially, often as:

- Moldavite;
- Libyan Desert Glass;
- tektites;
- meteorite fragments;
- “Saffordite” or other natural glass;
- spiritually “activated” crystals.

No standardized mineral species corresponds to “Chintamani.” Commercial use of the name does not create continuity with the Roerich object.

### 9.6 Literature, games, and popular culture

The best-known mass-cultural adaptation is *Uncharted 2: Among Thieves* (2009), in which the Cintamani Stone lies in Shambhala and grants superhuman power or longevity. The game combines Roerichian Shambhala imagery with adventure-fiction conventions and has strongly influenced contemporary searches for the term.

Modern UFO, hollow-earth, conspiracy, and esoteric media often treat the Roerich expedition as an attempt to locate a technological Shambhala. These accounts frequently:

- convert symbolic language into literal machinery;
- repeat the League of Nations story;
- claim Nazi pursuit without object-specific evidence;
- conflate Roerich’s first and later expeditions;
- present the Stone as scientifically confirmed meteorite material.

### 9.7 Political reception

Henry Wallace’s use of Roerichian language shows that the Stone’s symbolism entered politically consequential correspondence in the 1930s. It did not become an official object of the United States, League of Nations, Soviet Union, or Mongolian state.

The Roerich Pact and Banner of Peace are sometimes presented as institutional manifestations of the Stone’s mission. They are documented political-cultural projects, but their legal history does not authenticate the relic.

### 9.8 Replicas and images

Painted reproductions, postcards, casket images, and modern crystal substitutes function as devotional proxies. No officially documented exact replica with measured correspondence to the original is known.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Primary source base

The most important evidence consists of:

- Helena Roerich’s 1923 notebooks;
- Nicholas Roerich’s October 1923 correspondence;
- the 1923 photograph of the object;
- photographs of the casket, wrapping, and shipping box;
- the December 1924 English publication of the legend;
- Nicholas Roerich’s 1929–1930 travel and Shambhala writings;
- expedition records and photographs;
- Svetoslav Roerich’s portraits;
- correspondence within the American Roerich circle.

The primary record is unusually strong for receipt of a mysterious object, but exceptionally weak for everything before and after that receipt.

### 10.2 Devotional historiography

Roerich institutions and sympathetic writers generally accept:

- meteoritic nature;
- Orion origin as spiritual or literal truth;
- relationship to Shambhala;
- family custody;
- participation in the expedition;
- world-historical function.

Such sources preserve valuable documents but often do not clearly separate religious testimony from independently verified provenance.

### 10.3 Critical scholarship

Alexandre Andreyev, Andrei Znamenski, and scholars of modern esotericism place the Stone within:

- Theosophy and the Mahatma tradition;
- Russian Silver Age occultism;
- post-revolutionary political messianism;
- Soviet occult networks;
- Pan-Buddhist and Eurasian projects;
- the Roerichs’ construction of spiritual authority.

Andreyev’s *The Myth of the Masters Revived* reproduces and discusses the 1923 photograph and relates the Stone to the Roerichs’ occult lives. A bibliographic record is available through the [Russian State Library](https://search.rsl.ru/ru/record/01008055957). Znamenski’s *Red Shambhala* contextualizes Roerich’s Asian politics; its bibliographic entry is available through [Open Library](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL25016466M/Red_Shambhala).

### 10.4 Independent object-centered inquiry

Ian Heron’s extended online investigation is unusually focused on the physical object, its packaging, possible sender, casket, meteorite hypothesis, expeditionary purpose, and present whereabouts. Its strengths are close attention to photographs and contradictions. Its limitations are reliance on anonymous testimony, interpretive resemblance, and the absence of direct laboratory access.

### 10.5 Major unresolved questions

1. Who deposited the package with Bankers Trust?
2. Do the bank’s transaction or delivery records survive?
3. Who manufactured and decorated the casket?
4. Can its motifs be matched to a known European workshop or heraldic family?
5. What is the object’s composition?
6. Does the 1923 photograph show natural or worked morphology?
7. Did the object accompany every leg of the expedition?
8. Was it exchanged or deposited in Moscow, Mongolia, Tibet, or India?
9. What did Svetoslav inherit?
10. Where are the casket and contents now?
11. Are modern photographs of the casket independently dated and authenticated?
12. Would the present custodian permit non-destructive analysis?

### 10.6 Research required for resolution

A credible future examination should include:

- complete legal and custodial documentation;
- calibrated photography and 3-D scanning;
- X-ray radiography or micro-CT;
- portable XRF;
- Raman spectroscopy;
- magnetic susceptibility;
- density measurement;
- microscopy of unprepared surfaces;
- nickel, cobalt, gallium, germanium, iridium, and platinum-group-element analysis if meteoritic iron is suspected;
- stable-isotope testing if appropriate;
- dendrochronology or radiocarbon dating of casket wood where feasible;
- fiber and dye analysis of the wrappings;
- leather-species identification;
- pigment and binder analysis;
- multispectral imaging of the casket signs;
- comparison with Armanty material only after baseline characterization.

Until such work occurs, both authentication and debunking remain premature beyond the limited conclusion that a physical talisman was photographed in 1923.

- Archival scholarship on Roerich’s international political projects and the League of Nations.

### Source evaluation

The Roerich notebooks and letters are primary sources for what the Roerichs expected, received, believed, and did. They are not primary evidence for Solomon, Timur, Akbar, Orion, or Shambhala.

Roerich museums preserve indispensable materials, but institutional proximity to a living spiritual tradition requires careful separation between catalogue facts and doctrinal interpretation.

Heron’s research is valuable for assembling object photographs and contradictions but does not transform an untested visual hypothesis into a meteoritical identification.

Modern occult, UFO, commercial-crystal, and gaming sources document reception; they are not evidence for origin or provenance.
