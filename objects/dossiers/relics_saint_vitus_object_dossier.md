# Relics of St. Vitus: Object Dossier

**Proposed filename:** `relics_of_st_vitus_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary note

“Relics of St. Vitus” is not the name of one demonstrably continuous artifact. It denotes a dispersed and repeatedly reassembled relic complex: human bones or bone particles attributed to the martyr Vitus, together with the containers made for them and the institutional traditions that authenticated them. Medieval sources claimed complete or nearly complete bodies for more than one place. Prague eventually claimed an arm received in the tenth century and a larger body obtained at Pavia in 1355; Corvey claimed the body translated from Saint-Denis in 836; Pavia had claimed a body before Charles IV removed what he found there; numerous lesser churches claimed individual particles.

The dossier therefore tracks three principal branches:

1. the **Saint-Denis–Corvey assemblage**, first described in a near-contemporary ninth-century *Translatio sancti Viti*;
2. the **Prague arm relic**, associated retrospectively with Duke Wenceslas and King Henry I;
3. the **Pavia–Prague assemblage**, removed from San Marino at Pavia in January 1355.

The evidence does not establish that these branches came from one individual—or even that every surviving bone is human. No published radiocarbon, DNA, osteological, or proteomic examination specifically authenticating the principal Vitus relics in Prague or Corvey was located. Scientific studies of burials beneath the former Prague Rotunda of St. Vitus concern Přemyslid graves, not the imported relics of Vitus, and must not be confused with examination of the saint’s bones.

Throughout:

- **Documented** means supported by a contemporary or near-contemporary record, surviving inventory, physical object, or archival notice.
- **Traditional** means maintained by a church or later historical narrative but not independently demonstrated.
- **Hagiographic** identifies claims made by saints’ lives, miracle collections, or translation narratives.
- **Unresolved** means the available evidence does not permit identification or continuity to be established.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Names and classification

The relics are first-class bodily relics—principally bones, a purported skull or skull portion, an arm or forearm, a tooth, and smaller particles—attributed to the early Christian martyr Vitus.

Relevant names include:

- Latin: **reliquiae sancti Viti**, “relics of Saint Vitus”; **corpus sancti Viti**, “body of Saint Vitus”; **ossa sancti Viti**, “bones of Saint Vitus”; **caput sancti Viti**, “head of Saint Vitus”; **brachium/manus sancti Viti**, “arm/hand of Saint Vitus.”
- Czech: **ostatky svatého Víta**, **relikvie svatého Víta**; **rámě sv. Víta**, “arm of St. Vitus”; **hlava/lebka sv. Víta**, “head/skull of St. Vitus.”
- German: **Reliquien des heiligen Vitus/Veit**, **Vitusreliquien**, **Veitreliquien**.
- Italian: **reliquie di San Vito**, **corpo di San Vito**.
- French: **reliques de saint Vit/Guy**.
- South Slavic forms include **mošti svetog Vida/Vita** and related forms, although these generally belong to regional cults rather than the principal custody chains reconstructed here.

The Latin personal name *Vitus* was rendered *Vít* in Czech, *Veit* in German, *Vito* in Italian, and *Guy* in French. Its etymology is disputed. It has often been associated with Latin *vita*, “life,” but that is an interpretive association rather than a secure derivation.

### 1.2 The biological material

No complete modern osteological catalogue of all surviving Vitus-attributed material was found in an openly accessible institutional source. The principal historically described components are:

- a body or substantial skeletal assemblage taken from Saint-Denis to Corvey in 836;
- an arm or arm-bone relic traditionally given to Duke Wenceslas and kept at Prague;
- a body-like assemblage found in a Pavia altar in 1355, reported by one scholarly account as already “largely reduced to dust”;
- a head or skull relic at Prague, separately enclosed in a reliquary bust;
- an ulna or other long forearm bone recovered at Corvey after the Thirty Years’ War;
- a tooth given to Corvey by Würzburg in 1676;
- numerous particles distributed to dependent or friendly churches.

Terms such as *corpus* or “body” in medieval sources do not guarantee anatomical completeness. Medieval custodians could call a substantial assemblage, a principal relic, or even a shrine representing bodily presence a *corpus*.

### 1.3 Prague arm reliquary

The Prague arm relic is currently described institutionally and in modern catalogues as one of the oldest elements of the cathedral treasury. It has reportedly been housed since the fourteenth century in a silver arm-shaped reliquary. Such an anthropomorphic container is a “speaking reliquary”: its form identifies the bodily part it encloses.

The surviving public descriptions do not provide an agreed inventory number, complete measurements, weight, or a recent technical report. The often-repeated description—“silver arm,” sometimes “gilded silver arm”—should therefore be treated as a catalogue-level identification rather than a complete materials analysis.

It is not established that the bone now inside the arm reliquary is the same physical piece present when the earliest Prague inventories were made, still less that it arrived during Wenceslas’s lifetime.

### 1.4 Prague head reliquary or reliquary bust

A late Gothic reliquary bust identified as that of St. Vitus survives in the Prague cathedral treasury. A photographic catalogue records:

- date: before 1503;
- dimensions: approximately **63 × 42 cm**;
- materials: **silver, gilding, amethyst, chalcedony, chrysoprase, lapis lazuli, and other gemstones**;
- place: St. Vitus Cathedral, Prague Castle.

The bust represents the saint as a youthful male. It is a composite goldsmith’s object rather than an untouched fourteenth-century container. Its precise workshop and sequence of alteration require specialist catalogue consultation. The surviving form is generally assigned to the late fifteenth or very early sixteenth century. It should not be conflated with the fourteenth-century references to a “silver and gold head of St. Vitus”; it may replace, reuse, or continue the function of an earlier container.

The catalogue number **S2931N** belongs to Josef Sudek’s photographic negative, not to the cathedral object itself.

### 1.5 Prague altar, tomb, and lost shrine

Charles IV’s imported Pavia relics were incorporated into the liturgical architecture of the Gothic cathedral. Scholarship reconstructs a relationship among:

- the altar of St. Vitus;
- a tomb- or shrine-like reliquary;
- the choir and royal throne;
- the saint’s separately displayed head.

A medieval inventory phrase, *capsa de amatistinis lapidibus, in qua fuit beatus Vitus apportatus*, describes an amethyst-stone container in which the blessed Vitus was brought. This object is not securely identifiable among surviving pieces.

The shrine or “tomb adorned with images” removed from Prague in 1420 appears to have been a substantial reliquary structure. Whether it survived the Hussite period intact, was dismantled, or contributed material to later containers is unresolved.

### 1.6 Corvey medieval silver shrine

Professor Hedwig Röckelein’s reconstruction from inventories describes a large silver shrine traditionally commissioned by Abbot Warin soon after the 836 translation. It contained not only Vitus’s attributed bones but relics of approximately 150 other saints, Christ and Marian relics, blood attributed to St. Stephen, and remains attributed to St. Justinus.

The documented later shrine was therefore a collective reliquary, not an anatomically isolated container for Vitus. It stood in the *confessio* of the crypt beneath the choir. Its date as an object is uncertain: Corvey tradition associated it with Warin, but later rebuilding, refitting, or replacement cannot be excluded.

The shrine’s precious-metal casing was destroyed or carried off in the sack of Höxter in April 1634. No secure fragment of that metalwork has been identified.

### 1.7 Corvey surviving relics and replacement shrine

After 1634 Corvey recovered from local hiding places:

- an ulna or long forearm bone attributed to Vitus;
- several non-Vitus relics formerly kept with it.

Würzburg supplied a tooth attributed to Vitus in 1676 after Corvey requested restitution of material reportedly rescued from the destroyed shrine.

New painted wooden shrines for Vitus, Mercurius, and Justinus were commissioned during the 1660s. The Vitus shrine is now displayed in the Corvey museum. It is a post-destruction container, not the medieval silver shrine. A large reliquary cabinet made in 1699 and decorated with Vitus’s bust remains against the north wall of the former abbey church.

### 1.8 Other claimants and local relics

Relics bearing Vitus’s name are or have been claimed at Mazara del Vallo, San Vito dei Normanni, Positano, Santo Stefano del Sole, Herrieden, Mönchengladbach, Würzburg, and numerous other churches. One modern ecclesiastical source estimates roughly 150 communities possessing relics or particles.

These objects do not form a single demonstrable chain. Some may have received particles from Corvey; some arise from independent Italian traditions; others are *corpora sancta* from Roman catacombs later assigned Vitus’s name. A catacomb body called “San Vito” is not thereby evidence for anatomical continuity with the martyr represented in the *Martyrologium Hieronymianum*.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Earliest evidence for Vitus

Vitus’s name appears in the textual tradition of the *Martyrologium Hieronymianum*, a late antique martyrological compilation surviving in eighth-century manuscripts. This establishes an early cult but does not provide a securely historical biography, identify extant bones, or describe their exhumation.

By the pontificate of Gelasius I (492–496), a Roman place of worship associated with Vitus was known. Gregory the Great’s correspondence refers to a Sicilian monastery dedicated to Vitus. These notices demonstrate that the cult was established by the late fifth and sixth centuries, but they do not authenticate any presently surviving relic.

### 2.2 The Passion

The Latin *Passio sancti Viti*, conventionally indexed as BHL 8711 and related recensions, supplies the familiar narrative: Vitus is a Sicilian boy secretly converted by his nurse Crescentia and tutor Modestus; he resists his pagan father, travels to Lucania and Rome, cures or confronts an imperial household, survives tortures including the cauldron, and dies as a martyr.

The oldest surviving manuscripts are early medieval—the Bollandist manuscript tradition includes witnesses from the ninth century onward—while a Chartres manuscript of the tenth century contains the title *Vita beatissimi Viti martyris* and the incipit *Tempore quo Valerianus praeses sub Diocletiano…*. The narrative is not a contemporary martyrdom record. Modern hagiography treats Modestus and Crescentia as literary associates and the Passion as legendary.

The Passion does not establish where the historical Vitus, if there was one, was buried or how any authenticated grave was identified.

### 2.3 Hagiographic origin of the Saint-Denis relics

The Corvey translation tradition says that a devout person discovered Vitus’s relics at an unspecified site near Rome or in southern Italy, took them into Frankish territory, and deposited them on property connected with Fulrad, abbot of Saint-Denis. They were then transferred to Saint-Denis, conventionally dated to 756.

This is not documented by a surviving contemporary eighth-century acquisition instrument. The ninth-century Corvey author apparently relied on oral testimony, possibly information communicated by Hilduin of Saint-Denis. Thus:

- the presence of Vitus-attributed relics at Saint-Denis by the early ninth century is plausible;
- their discovery near Rome and arrival in 756 are hagiographic provenance;
- anatomical or scientific continuity with a late antique martyr is unproved.

### 2.4 Earliest firm object-level attestation: the 836 translation

The strongest early evidence is the *Translatio sancti Viti martyris*, composed at Corvey in the ninth century and describing events of 836. The author had access to institutional records, including a now-lost donation instrument, and recorded miracles said to have occurred en route and at Corvey through 837.

The transaction involved:

- Hilduin, abbot of Saint-Denis, formerly exiled at Corvey;
- Warin, abbot of Corvey;
- imperial and episcopal consent;
- removal or elevation of the relics at Saint-Denis;
- a journey beginning on 19 March 836;
- ceremonial arrival at Corvey shortly before the feast of Vitus, 15 June.

The text is near-contemporary and therefore strong evidence that a material assemblage identified as Vitus’s relics was physically transferred. It is not independent proof of the biological identity assigned to that assemblage.

### 2.5 The Prague origin tradition

The early Czech sources establish that Wenceslas built a church dedicated to Vitus in the first half of the tenth century. They do not securely say that Henry I gave him an arm relic from Corvey.

The detailed gift story appears only in the mid-thirteenth-century Wenceslas legend *Oriente iam sole*. In it, Wenceslas arrives late at Henry’s court; Henry, despite having forbidden anyone to rise for him, recognizes Wenceslas’s sanctity, seats him beside himself, and offers him a gift. Wenceslas requests relics of Vitus and builds the Prague church for them.

Historian František Graus emphasized:

- older Czech sources do not record where the Prague relic came from;
- the oldest Prague martyrology, surviving in a mid-twelfth-century copy, lacks a feast commemorating its translation;
- a translation entry appears only in a later, late-fourteenth-century recension;
- therefore the Henry–Wenceslas–Corvey chain is a late hagiographic construction, even if a tenth-century transfer remains possible.

The frequently repeated dates 925, 929, or 932 are reconstructions or traditional dates, not the date of a surviving donation charter.

### 2.6 The Pavia origin tradition

The church and monastery of San Marino in Pavia claimed a body of St. Vitus. The monastery is documented by 881 and was traditionally connected with the Lombard king Aistulf, who was said to have brought numerous relics from Rome in the eighth century. Later Italian tradition dates Vitus’s arrival to about 755.

No early inventory or translation record securely identifies how a body of Vitus reached San Marino. The claim was nevertheless established by 1355, when Charles IV acted on it.

This creates an irreconcilable medieval plurality: Saint-Denis/Corvey and Pavia both claimed a principal body. Neither chain supplies scientific evidence resolving the contradiction.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Before the eighth century: unknown

No demonstrable grave, excavation, or authenticated late antique chain survives. The Passions place Vitus’s activity and death variously in Sicily, Lucania, and Rome. The location and fate of any historical remains are unknown.

### c. 756: alleged transfer to Saint-Denis

According to the later Corvey tradition, Fulrad acquired or received Vitus’s body and brought it to Saint-Denis. The body was kept there for roughly eighty years. Evidence: ninth-century translation narrative relying partly on institutional memory and oral report. Status: **early medieval tradition, not contemporary eighth-century documentation**.

### 830–836: Hilduin’s promise

Hilduin of Saint-Denis had been exiled to Corvey in 830. Warin helped secure his restoration, and Hilduin promised relics in return. Consent was reportedly obtained from Emperor Louis the Pious, the bishop of Paris, and regional magnates. Status: **near-contemporary institutional narrative**.

### 19 March–June 836: Saint-Denis to Corvey

The relics were elevated and carried east. The *Translatio* narrates stops, public receptions, and miracles. The convoy arrived at Corvey two days before Vitus’s feast, conventionally 13 June, and the relics were formally deposited there. Status: **best-documented early physical transfer**, though identity remained devotional rather than forensic.

### Ninth–eleventh centuries: Corvey shrine and cult

Corvey made Vitus a principal patron. The relics were placed in a shrine, eventually in a crypt-confessio under the choir. They became central to Corvey’s identity and to Saxon Christian political memory.

Widukind of Corvey later presented the transfer as the moment when divine favor or “fortune” passed from the Franks to the Saxons. This is political theology rather than a custody record, but it documents the symbolic function of the relics.

Abbot Markward, beginning around 1090, revived their public cult and may have commissioned or renewed the shrine.

### Tenth century: possible Corvey-to-Prague partition

A bone of Vitus’s arm may have been given to Duke Wenceslas by Henry I. No contemporary transfer document survives. Because Corvey commonly distributed relic particles and because later Prague sources consistently possessed an arm relic, the transfer is possible; its donor, date, and Corvey origin remain unproved.

The relic was housed first in Wenceslas’s rotunda at Prague Castle. When the rotunda was replaced around 1060 by Spytihněv II’s Romanesque basilica, the relic would have passed into the new church’s treasury. This movement is an institutional inference, not a surviving handover record.

### 1147: mobility of the Corvey relics

King Conrad III confirmed a transaction involving Corvey property by placing a gold ring on the relics of Vitus brought to Frankfurt. This indicates that the relics or their shrine could travel and could serve as a juridical witness to grants. It also shows that “custody at Corvey” did not mean continuous physical immobility.

### Mid-thirteenth century: written formation of the Prague gift story

The *Oriente iam sole* legend supplied the first detailed Henry–Wenceslas transfer narrative. By the early fourteenth century the Dalimil Chronicle repeated the tradition. Documentation of the story thus begins roughly three centuries after the supposed event.

### 1344 onward: Gothic cathedral

When Prague became an archbishopric and construction of the Gothic cathedral began, the arm relic passed into the new cathedral’s cultic and treasury system. The church was conceived as coronation church, royal necropolis, and repository of the kingdom’s patrons.

### 10 January 1355: seizure or compelled surrender at Pavia

During his Italian expedition and after his coronation in Milan as king of Lombardy, Charles IV learned that San Marino at Pavia claimed the body of Vitus. He sent a delegation headed by the bishop of Pavia. Named participants included Bohuš, brother of Archbishop Arnošt of Pardubice; Petr of Šelenberk; Mikuláš of Landštejn; Václav of Švábenice; Přemysl of Honbice; and the Litoměřice canon Jan of Vartenberk.

On 10 January, in the presence of nobles and prelates, the altar was opened. A scholarly reconstruction says Charles proceeded despite strong local protest. The remains were found in poor condition, largely dust or fragmented material, and were removed.

The mechanism should not be euphemized. Charles possessed overwhelming royal authority in newly subjected Pavia. Sources may style the transaction an acquisition or authorized translation, but the reported protests make it at least a **coercive removal**, not a freely documented gift.

### 22 January 1355: Charles’s notice from Pisa

Charles wrote from Pisa to Archbishop Arnošt and the Prague chapter announcing that he had acquired Vitus’s remains at San Marino and would bring or send them to Prague. The letter was intended for public proclamation in Prague churches.

This is the earliest firm documentary anchor for the Pavia–Prague assemblage.

### 1355–1365: deposit in Prague

The relics arrived in Prague in 1355 and entered the cathedral treasury. They were arranged at or beneath an altar and within a tomb-like shrine in the Gothic choir. The altar of Vitus and the Virgin in the new choir was consecrated by Archbishop Jan Očko of Vlašim on 12 January 1365.

The relic’s positioning was integrated into the ceremonial topography of kingship: altar, saint’s body, throne, royal burials, and coronation route reinforced one another.

### Late fourteenth century: inventories and head relic

Cathedral inventories record the Vitus shrine and a “silver and gold head of St. Vitus.” The head was treated as a separately accessible relic. It is unclear whether it belonged to the Pavia deposit, the earlier Prague relic, or another acquisition.

### 1420: removal to Karlštejn

As the Hussite crisis endangered the cathedral, the chapter removed major relics and treasury objects to Karlštejn. An inventory mentions the “tomb of blessed Vitus adorned with images.” The movement is documented, but the exact contents and survival of the container are uncertain.

The cathedral was sacked in 1421. Its fixed furnishings and objects left behind suffered destruction. The evacuation preserved at least part of the Vitus complex, but the continuity of every bone and container across the crisis cannot be reconstructed.

### Late fifteenth–early sixteenth century: new bust

The surviving Prague reliquary bust was made before 1503, probably under Jagiellonian patronage. It either replaced an earlier head reliquary or refashioned its function. No evidence located establishes that all its gemstones or metal derive from the fourteenth-century “silver and gold head.”

### 1634: destruction of the Corvey shrine

Corvey had concealed some relics in anticipation of military violence, but the large Vitus shrine was moved behind Höxter’s walls. On 13 April 1634 Catholic League troops stormed the town. Contemporary estimates reported approximately 1,100 deaths.

Soldiers broke or carried away the silver shrine and other precious objects. Röckelein concludes that the looters were primarily interested in silver, gold, and gemstones rather than bones. One Catholic officer rescued some relics and gave them to Field Marshal Melchior von Hatzfeld. Hatzfeld took them to Cologne and then sent them to his brother Franz, prince-bishop of Würzburg.

Claims that the Corvey body was carried to Prague during the Thirty Years’ War are repeated in popular and ecclesiastical literature but are not supported by the more detailed archival reconstruction. The documented rescued material went to Cologne and Würzburg, while other bones remained in Corvey hiding places. Prague already possessed its Pavia assemblage and arm relic centuries earlier.

### 1662–1663: inventory and rediscovery of the Würzburg branch

A Corvey inventory dated 19 October 1662 records what remained. In 1663 the daughter of Corvey commissioner Stockhausen, serving as lady-in-waiting to Countess Alexandrine Marie von Velen, reported seeing Vitus relics in a Würzburg cathedral chapel during pilgrimage.

### 1660s–1676: replacement shrine and unsuccessful restitution

Corvey commissioned a new painted wooden shrine for Vitus in the 1660s. In 1676 the abbot requested return of the relics from Würzburg. Würzburg surrendered only a tooth attributed to Vitus. The rest of the material’s later identity and location are unresolved.

Corvey’s surviving Vitus material included an ulna or comparable forearm bone found in a wartime hiding place.

### 1699–1727: reconstructed Corvey treasury

Abbot Florenz von dem Velde commissioned two large reliquary cabinets in 1699, one decorated with Vitus’s bust. Reliquary holdings were replenished by gifts from Corvey priories, Benedictine and Cistercian houses, ecclesiastical allies, Rome, and institutions that no longer used their relics after Protestant reform.

The reconstructed treasury was not the original collection restored intact. It was a new assemblage combining survivors, returned particles, exchanged relics, and new acquisitions. In 1727 the new shrines were installed in the Benedict chapel east of the choir.

### 1802 onward: secularization and institutional succession

The Reichsabbey of Corvey was dissolved in the secularization of 1802. The church and surviving shrine remained locally, eventually under parish, diocesan, and museum care. The physical complex passed through the political regimes governing Corvey, while its sacred custody continued in the former abbey church.

### Twentieth century: Prague treasury reorganization

Antonín Podlaha and Eduard Šittler systematically catalogued the Prague cathedral treasury and published it in 1903. They helped reorganize the objects in the Hilbert Treasury in the cathedral’s north aisle.

In 1950, under Communist rule, the treasury ceased to function in its former public arrangement. From 1964 to 1990 approximately 140 major objects were displayed in the Chapel of the Holy Cross at Prague Castle. Following 1989, relations among the state, Prague Castle administration, archdiocese, and Metropolitan Chapter were renegotiated.

### 2011–2020: Prague Castle exhibition

A permanent exhibition of the St. Vitus Treasury opened in the Chapel of the Holy Cross in December 2011. It closed in March 2020; the chapel was cleared in 2021. Objects returned to the Hilbert Treasury or chapter/Prague Castle deposits.

### 2024: Dresden loan

The Vitus and Adalbert reliquary busts were among objects sent to Dresden for the exhibition *Fragmente der Erinnerung* at the Kunsthalle im Lipsiusbau, March–September 2024. This was described as the first major foreign presentation of parts of the Prague cathedral treasury.

### 2026: present disposition

The Prague arm relic, Vitus reliquary bust, and other Vitus-attributed material remain under the care of the Metropolitan Chapter of St. Vitus Cathedral, with storage and museum administration involving Prague Castle. The treasury is not presently maintained as the continuous public exhibition that existed from 2011 to 2020.

At Corvey, the post-1634 painted wooden Vitus shrine is displayed in the museum; the 1699 cabinet decorated with Vitus’s image remains in the former abbey church. The surviving bone relics remain ecclesiastical property or custody, though an itemized public digital catalogue is unavailable.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Was there a historical Vitus?

The cult is late antique, but the detailed biography is legendary. The name’s presence in the *Martyrologium Hieronymianum* is evidence for early commemoration, not proof of the Passion’s Sicilian noble family, Modestus and Crescentia, Roman exorcism, or cauldron torture.

### 4.2 Were the Saint-Denis remains Vitus?

The physical assemblage demonstrably existed by 836. Its asserted history before Saint-Denis lacks contemporary documentation. There is no identified original grave, inscription, seal, or surviving late antique authentication.

Strongest case for traditional identity:

- early and stable institutional attribution;
- near-contemporary ninth-century translation account;
- broad public recognition and liturgical continuity.

Strongest case against demonstrability:

- no secure late antique provenance;
- discovery narrative is vague;
- no scientific analysis;
- simultaneous competing bodies existed elsewhere.

Conclusion: **the 836 object is historically real; its attribution to Vitus is unverified.**

### 4.3 Was Prague’s arm from Corvey?

Supporting considerations:

- Corvey was the major Saxon center of Vitus’s cult;
- Henry I had strong Saxon and Corvey connections;
- Wenceslas built a Vitus church;
- Prague demonstrably possessed an arm relic by the later Middle Ages.

Problems:

- no contemporary charter or inventory;
- early Wenceslas legends do not identify a relic’s origin;
- the full story first appears in the thirteenth century;
- the early Prague martyrology lacks the translation feast.

Conclusion: **possible, institutionally traditional, but not documented as a tenth-century transfer.**

### 4.4 Corvey versus Pavia

By 1355 both places claimed Vitus’s body. They cannot both have possessed the complete remains of one person unless one or both claims used “body” loosely or relics had been divided.

Possible explanations include:

- both possessed unrelated remains given the same identity;
- one had the principal body and the other particles rhetorically described as a body;
- relic division was forgotten and competing institutional traditions developed;
- one claim was a medieval misidentification or fabrication.

No scientific work currently selects among these possibilities.

### 4.5 Did Charles IV obtain the “remaining” relics?

Modern summaries sometimes state that Charles obtained “the remaining relics” at Pavia, completing Prague’s possession. Charles’s own action treated the Pavia deposit as the saint’s body. It does not follow that Pavia held the remainder of the Saint-Denis/Corvey body. No documented chain connects Pavia’s deposit to Corvey’s.

### 4.6 Is the current Prague bust the medieval head reliquary?

A head reliquary was recorded in fourteenth-century inventories. The present bust dates before 1503. Functional continuity is strong; material continuity is not demonstrated. The present object may be:

- a new container for the same skull;
- a rebuilding incorporating older metal or stones;
- a replacement container into which some or all relics were transferred;
- a reliquary holding a different head fragment later assigned the same identity.

The sources currently available do not decide among them.

### 4.7 Did Corvey’s relics go to Prague in 1634?

This is a persistent popular claim. The detailed archival reconstruction identifies a different route: Höxter → Melchior von Hatzfeld → Cologne → Franz von Hatzfeld at Würzburg. Other Corvey material remained hidden locally.

No contemporary evidence cited by the claim demonstrates a 1634 transfer to Prague. The statement that Prague thereby obtained “the whole grave” should be classified as **later conflation or unsupported tradition**.

### 4.8 Scientific testing

No published scientific analysis of the main Prague or Corvey Vitus relics was located. In particular, no reliable source was found for:

- radiocarbon dating;
- species identification;
- sex or age estimation;
- DNA comparison among Prague, Corvey, Pavia, or Italian claimants;
- isotope analysis;
- documented examination of whether one individual or several are represented.

The 2018 DNA, isotope, and radiocarbon study of graves K1 and K2 beneath the former Rotunda of St. Vitus investigated medieval Prague burials and found conflicting evidence about their Přemyslid identities. It did **not** test Vitus’s imported relics.

### 4.9 Authority to authenticate

Authority shifted historically:

- late antique and early medieval local churches authenticated by cult, altar placement, labels, and episcopal acceptance;
- abbots Hilduin and Warin, together with emperor and bishop, authenticated the 836 transfer institutionally;
- Charles IV, the bishop of Pavia, witnesses, Archbishop Arnošt, and the Prague chapter authenticated the 1355 translation;
- post-Tridentine custodians relied increasingly on inventories, sealed packets, episcopal attestations, and canonical recognitions;
- modern museums catalogue reliquaries as works of art but generally do not claim laboratory authentication of their contents.

Ecclesiastical authenticity means authorization for veneration and continuity of tradition; it is not identical to scientific identification.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Altar relic

The foundational Christian function was consecratory and intercessory. Placing the bones near or beneath an altar made the saint liturgically present. At both Corvey and Prague, architectural space was reshaped around the claimed body.

### 6.2 Corvey feast and procession

On 15 June, Corvey celebrated Vitus’s feast with an *ostensio reliquiarum*. At the late-medieval height of the ritual:

- relics were displayed so worshippers could see or approach them;
- indulgences could be gained;
- donors were commemorated;
- twenty-one shrines and monstrances were carried in procession;
- the large silver Vitus shrine occupied a leading and prestigious position.

This practice is documented through inventories and liturgical reconstruction, though the exact route and order changed over time.

### 6.3 Miracles and healing

The ninth-century *Translatio* records miracles during the journey and in the year following arrival. These reports helped establish the newly transferred object as genuine: miraculous efficacy served as authentication.

Vitus later became an intercessor against epilepsy, convulsions, chorea (“St. Vitus’s dance”), rabies, animal diseases, lightning, storms, infertility, and eye and ear disorders. These powers accumulated over centuries and should not all be projected back into the 836 text.

### 6.4 Juridical function

The 1147 use of Vitus’s relics in confirming a property transaction exemplifies relics as legal witnesses. Touching or depositing a symbolic object on the shrine placed an agreement under saintly sanction.

### 6.5 Saxon political identity

Corvey’s possession made Vitus a patron of the abbey and, in monastic historiography, of Christian Saxony. Widukind’s claim that prosperity passed from the Franks to the Saxons with the body transformed the transfer into a sacred charter of Saxon ascendancy.

### 6.6 Bohemian dynastic function

In Prague the relics helped bind together:

- Wenceslas, the holy Přemyslid founder;
- the cathedral’s patron Vitus;
- Charles IV’s Luxembourg monarchy;
- the royal necropolis;
- coronation ritual;
- Prague’s claim to imperial and Roman-Christian status.

The alleged gift from Henry I retroactively represented Wenceslas as honored by the Saxon king and made the relic a token of legitimate Christian rulership.

### 6.7 Cathedral ceremonial topography

The imported Pavia remains were installed where their altar or shrine interacted with the royal throne. Scholarship interprets this as a deliberate fusion of saintly presence and enthronement. The object was not merely stored; it helped constitute the space in which Bohemian kingship was performed.

### 6.8 Visibility and access

Relics could alternate between concealment and display:

- kept in sealed shrines or treasuries ordinarily;
- exposed on feast days;
- carried in processions;
- taken on royal or legal journeys;
- evacuated in wartime;
- handled only by authorized clergy.

The precious container regulated access. Loss of the container in 1634 exposed the bones to dispersal and radically changed their institutional meaning.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 Discovery and journey from Italy to Francia

**Tier:** early medieval translation legend.

A devout traveler discovers the saint’s bones in Italy, carries them north, and deposits them near Saint-Denis. The lack of exact location or grave evidence is typical of translation legends whose purpose was to bridge an undocumented provenance gap.

Its historical effect was substantial: it supplied Saint-Denis and Corvey with a continuous sacred genealogy reaching back to the martyr’s death.

### 7.2 Miracles on the road to Corvey

**Tier:** near-contemporary hagiographic narrative.

The *Translatio* presents healings and providential events along the procession route. Miracles validate both the identity of the relics and their consent to relocation. The stories also map Vitus’s cult across the route and integrate local communities into Corvey’s sacred history.

### 7.3 Divine favor transferred from Franks to Saxons

**Tier:** tenth-century political interpretation.

Widukind’s narrative made the relic transfer a cause or sign of Saxon political success. Its purpose was not anatomical history but sacred political memory: Corvey’s possession testified that Christian imperial legitimacy had passed to Saxony.

### 7.4 Henry recognizes Wenceslas’s sanctity

**Tier:** mid-thirteenth-century hagiographic elaboration.

Henry orders courtiers not to honor the tardy Wenceslas, yet rises when the duke arrives, seats him beside himself, and offers him any gift. Wenceslas asks only for Vitus’s relics. The story demonstrates humility, sanctity, and divine recognition.

It also solves a later institutional question—why Prague had a Vitus church and arm relic—by supplying a prestigious donor. Older sources’ silence makes the episode historically unverified.

### 7.5 Prague possesses the complete Vitus body

**Tier:** late medieval institutional claim.

By combining the original arm with Charles’s Pavia acquisition, Prague could describe itself as possessing the saint’s full body or virtually complete remains. This was not anatomically established, and Corvey continued to claim surviving Vitus bones.

### 7.6 Corvey’s entire body went to Prague during the Thirty Years’ War

**Tier:** later conflation.

Popular accounts assert that Corvey’s remaining relics were stolen and taken to Prague, completing the body there. The archival reconstruction instead traces rescued relics to Cologne and Würzburg and records an ulna surviving locally. No secure Prague transfer is documented.

The legend probably arose because:

- Prague already claimed the complete body;
- Corvey’s main shrine disappeared;
- the earlier Prague–Corvey arm tradition offered an attractive bridge;
- later summaries collapsed distinct medieval and seventeenth-century events.

### 7.7 Vitus and the Slavic god Svetovid

**Tier:** modern comparative-religion theory, often overstated.

A widespread theory says the cult of *Sveti Vid* replaced that of the Slavic deity Svetovid/Svantevit, sometimes treating the names as equivalent. Linguistic resemblance and the history of Christianization encouraged the idea. Direct evidence that Prague selected Vitus to replace Svetovid is lacking, and the theory should not be used to explain the relic’s acquisition as fact.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Prague

The principal Prague relic complex remains associated with the Metropolitan Cathedral of Saints Vitus, Wenceslas and Adalbert at Prague Castle.

Likely present components include:

- arm bone in a silver arm reliquary;
- head or skull material in the late Gothic reliquary bust;
- smaller or dust-like remains associated with the altar or shrine tradition;
- documentary labels, inventories, and later containers.

Control lies principally with the Metropolitan Chapter, while Prague Castle institutions participate in conservation, storage, security, and exhibition. The larger cathedral-ownership litigation between church and state ended politically in 2010 without further pursuit, and the chapter’s ownership of the treasury is publicly stated.

The treasury’s 2011–2020 exhibition is closed. Objects are kept in the Hilbert Treasury and chapter/Prague Castle deposits; individual works may travel in approved exhibitions. Public access to the relic material itself is therefore restricted and intermittent.

### Corvey

At Corvey:

- the seventeenth-century painted wooden Vitus shrine is in the museum;
- the 1699 reliquary cabinet bearing Vitus’s image stands in the former abbey church;
- surviving relic material includes at least the traditionally identified forearm bone and tooth in ecclesiastical custody.

The medieval silver shrine is lost. Corvey remains an active Catholic church with regular worship and annual observance of Vitus’s feast.

### Pavia

San Marino no longer claims possession of the body removed in 1355, though the episode remains part of the church’s history. The physical altar context survives only as architecture and documentary memory; no authenticated remnant of the removed Vitus assemblage is identified there.

### Status of authenticity

Current ecclesiastical institutions treat the relics as objects of inherited veneration. No located source reports that their cult has been withdrawn. Conversely, no published scientific examination verifies that they derive from a third- or fourth-century Sicilian or Lucanian boy.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Museum object and photographic icon

The Prague bust has become the chief visual representative of the relic complex because the bone itself is normally hidden. Josef Sudek’s 1942–1945 photographs helped translate the object from liturgical treasure into an art-historical subject.

Modern catalogues emphasize Gothic and Jagiellonian goldsmithing, precious stones, and courtly representation. This museological identity coexists with continued sacred status.

### 9.2 Exhibitions

The St. Vitus Treasury exhibition at Prague Castle, open from 2011 to 2020, presented the relics as part of Czech cultural heritage. The 2024 Dresden exhibition placed the Vitus bust within a transregional narrative of fragments, memory, Bohemia, and Saxony.

These exhibitions partially reverse the relic’s medieval logic: viewers encounter the precious container aesthetically, while the invisible biological material receives less scrutiny.

### 9.3 Replicas and images

Copies, plaster casts, photographs, and published illustrations of the bust and arm reliquary circulate in museum, scholarly, ecclesiastical, and tourist contexts. At Corvey, images and sculptural representations of Vitus often stand in for the destroyed medieval shrine.

No modern replica has acquired a rival claim to biological authenticity.

### 9.4 Civic and national reception

St. Vitus Cathedral is routinely described as a spiritual symbol of the Czech state. This gives the relics a national afterlife, though the saint himself was not Czech and the principal documented body acquisition came from Italy.

Corvey similarly uses the 836 translation in presenting its Carolingian heritage and UNESCO-associated identity. The same saint therefore anchors both Czech and Saxon/Westphalian historical memory.

### 9.5 Medicine and popular culture

The term “St. Vitus’s dance” preserved the saint’s association with involuntary movement long after medicine differentiated chorea and other neurological conditions. The name has shaped modern public recognition of Vitus more strongly than knowledge of the relics themselves.

### 9.6 Tourism and pilgrimage economy

Prague and Corvey both market sites formed around the relic tradition. In Prague, cathedral access, treasury exhibitions, guidebooks, reproductions, and state ceremonial symbolism form a major tourist economy. At Corvey, the translation story supports museum interpretation, feast-day observance, and heritage tourism.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 The ninth-century *Translatio*

The indispensable primary source is the anonymous *Translatio sancti Viti martyris*. Its importance is twofold:

- it is evidence for the 836 object transfer;
- it is a crafted monastic narrative connecting relics, miracles, Saxon conversion, and Corvey’s institutional legitimacy.

Modern scholars caution that its author used both documentary and oral sources. The now-lost donation charter appears to have supplied the date and legal narrative, while the pre-Saint-Denis provenance depended on tradition.

### 10.2 Prague inventories

The Prague cathedral inventories of 1355 and later fourteenth-century dates are the principal sources for the relics’ containers, altar placement, and distinctions among body, head, arm, and other relics. They are preserved in the Prague cathedral chapter archive and were extensively published or summarized by Podlaha and Šittler.

The inventories document what custodians believed and labelled. They do not independently verify biological identity.

### 10.3 Podlaha and Šittler

Antonín Podlaha and Eduard Šittler’s 1903 *Chrámový poklad u sv. Víta v Praze / Der Domschatz in Prag* remains foundational. It brought together inventories, object descriptions, inscriptions, historical notices, and illustrations.

Its limitations are those of its period:

- devotional attribution was often accepted as catalogue identity;
- scientific testing was unavailable;
- later conservation and provenance questions were not yet developed;
- some medieval objects have since been re-dated or reinterpreted.

### 10.4 Graus and the Wenceslas transfer dispute

František Graus’s source criticism sharply separated the early foundation of the Vitus church from the late story of Henry’s gift. His observation that the provenance first appears in mid-thirteenth-century tradition remains central.

The debate is not whether Prague had a Vitus cult in the tenth century—it did—but whether the surviving arm relic can be traced to Henry I and Corvey. The evidence remains insufficient.

### 10.5 Charles IV’s Pavia acquisition

Modern scholarship uses Charles’s 22 January 1355 letter, Italian context, cathedral inventories, and chronicles to reconstruct the removal. Interpretations differ in tone:

- ecclesiastical accounts call it acquisition or translation;
- courtly narratives emphasize Charles’s piety;
- critical reconstructions note Pavia’s resistance and the coercive context.

The object-level fact—an altar was opened and material was taken—is stronger than the claim that it was authentically Vitus.

### 10.6 Architectural scholarship

Research on the cathedral choir emphasizes that relic placement, royal burial, liturgy, and coronation were planned as an integrated program. The shrine’s position was political and performative, not accidental storage.

### 10.7 Corvey archival reconstruction

Röckelein’s work, based on the 1662 inventory, the 1676 *Catalogus antiquus et novus*, and other Münster archival material, substantially corrects popular accounts of the 1634 loss. It distinguishes:

- material hidden and recovered locally;
- material rescued and sent to Würzburg;
- the destroyed precious-metal shrine;
- postwar replacement relics and containers.

This research undermines the unsupported claim that all Corvey relics went to Prague.

### 10.8 State of the scientific evidence

The chief unresolved scholarly need is a controlled, multi-institutional examination of all Vitus-attributed remains. A serious program would require:

1. complete osteological inventories;
2. non-destructive imaging;
3. species and anatomical identification;
4. radiocarbon dating where permitted;
5. ancient DNA and proteomic analysis;
6. comparison of Prague, Corvey, Würzburg, and major Italian specimens;
7. technical analysis of reliquaries, repairs, and transferred fittings;
8. publication of historic seals, labels, textile wrappings, and authentication documents.

Even such analysis could establish date, species, number of persons, and biological relationships; it could not identify a historical Vitus without an independent reference sample.

### 10.9 Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- that the Prague arm’s transfer in 925/929 is contemporary documented fact;
- that Pavia held the “remainder” of Corvey’s body;
- that Corvey’s complete relics were taken to Prague in 1634;
- that Prague now possesses a scientifically verified complete skeleton;
- that Vitus was selected specifically to replace the god Svetovid;
- that scientific testing of Prague Castle burials authenticated Vitus’s relics.
