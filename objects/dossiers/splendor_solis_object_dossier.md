# Splendor Solis: Object Dossier

**Object tracked.** “Splendor Solis” names both an anonymous alchemical work and a family of manuscript and printed witnesses, not one uniquely transmitted artifact. This dossier therefore distinguishes:

1. the textual and pictorial work *Splendor Solis*;
2. its oldest surviving illuminated witness, Berlin, Kupferstichkabinett, Cod. 78 D 3, dated 1531–1532 or approximately 1532–1535; and
3. the most famous and most complete luxury witness, London, British Library, Harley MS 3469, dated 1582.

Because popular references to “the Splendor Solis manuscript” usually reproduce Harley MS 3469, that physical volume is the principal object of the custody history. The Berlin codex is treated separately whenever questions of origin, archetype, or originality arise.

**Evidence labels used below:** **Documented** denotes a statement supported directly by an institutional record, inscription, dated manuscript, law, or contemporary notice. **Scientific finding** denotes instrumental materials or dating analysis. **Scholarly inference** denotes a reasoned reconstruction from palaeography, textual genealogy, or iconography. **Traditional attribution** denotes an attribution transmitted by later editions but not established by contemporary evidence. **Disputed** identifies competing scholarly conclusions. **Unverified** means the available sources do not establish the claim.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Identity, class, and names

*Splendor Solis* is an illustrated German alchemical treatise arranged as a preface and seven tractates concerning the philosophers’ stone, the generation and perfection of metals, and the cooperation of art with nature. It is neither a liturgical manuscript nor a relic. Its “sacred” character belongs to its rhetoric: God, creation, ancient sages, cosmic order, and concealed wisdom authorize the alchemical art. Its principal practical-symbolic objective is the production of the philosophers’ stone, understood as the agent capable of perfecting imperfect metals and, in some receptions, producing medicine or rejuvenation.

The title is Latin:

- **Splendor Solis** — “Splendour,” “Radiance,” or “Brilliance of the Sun.”
- Early German explanatory form: **Sonnen Glantz**, modernized **Sonnenglanz**, “sun-radiance” or “sun-glory.”
- A fuller manuscript title begins: **Das gegenwärtige Buch wird genannt Splendor Solis oder Sonnen Glantz, geteilt in sieben Traktat…**—“The present book is called *Splendor Solis*, or Sun-Radiance, divided into seven tractates….” The University of Pennsylvania’s manuscript preserves a closely corresponding title and describes the subject as the hidden stone of the ancient sages. [University of Pennsylvania, OPenn manuscript record](https://openn.library.upenn.edu/Data/0002/html/mscodex108.html)
- French printed title: **La Toyson d’or**, later spelled **La Toison d’or**, “The Golden Fleece.” This title encouraged the enduring but erroneous impression that the work belonged to the writings of “Salomon Trismosin.”
- Later English forms include **The Splendour of the Sun** and **The Sun’s Radiance**.

No evidence establishes that “Splendor Solis” was an ancient title. The surviving title belongs to the early-modern manuscript tradition.

### 1.2 Harley MS 3469: the principal physical object

The British Library catalogues the volume as **Harley MS 3469**, record identifier **040-001616146**, in the Western Manuscripts/Harley Collection. It is a German parchment codex dated **1582**, written in Gothic script, containing German text with Latin inscriptions. [British Library catalogue](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/040-001616146)

Its recorded physical characteristics are:

- parchment;
- leaf dimensions **320 × 220 mm**;
- written area approximately **190 × 150 mm**;
- 49 principal numbered folios, preceded by three numbered parchment flyleaves and accompanied by additional unfoliated parchment flyleaves;
- modern paper interleaves inserted before every miniature;
- Gothic script;
- 22 full-page miniatures in opaque colors and gold;
- full decorative borders;
- large calligraphic initials in gold and smaller capitals picked out in gold;
- modern British Museum/British Library binding: red leather with gold tooling, pink-silk endpapers, and gilt edges.

The present binding is therefore **not the manuscript’s documented sixteenth-century binding**. The catalogue does not specify the date of the institutional rebinding. The paper guards or interleaves are likewise later protective interventions. No published weight is given.

The 22 paintings, by present foliation, are:

1. **f. 2r, Arms of the Art** — a solar heraldic device representing alchemy.
2. **f. 4r, the philosopher with the flask** — a scholar holds a vessel containing golden liquid. Its scroll reads, with scribal irregularities, *Eamus quaesitum quatuor elementorum naturas*: “Let us go in search of the natures of the four elements.”
3. **f. 7r, the knight of the double fountain** — an armored figure stands over two basins or waters. The shield instructs the seeker to make one water from two, unite Sun and Moon, give it to the “true enemy” to drink, see him dead, and then make earth from water.
4. **f. 10r, Sun and Moon/the royal couple** — a queen and king embody female and male principles, commonly read as water and fire or volatile and fixed components. Their scrolls are conventionally transcribed as *Lac virginum* and *Coagula masculinum*, although readings vary.
5. **f. 13v, mining the ore** — miners extract silver and gold; Esther before Ahasuerus appears below.
6. **f. 15r, the philosophical tree or golden bough** — the image incorporates figures from the story of Aeneas and the golden branch.
7. **f. 16v, the young and old—or drowning—king** — death and renewal figure material transformation.
8. **f. 18r, the dark man in the swamp and the angel** — an angel offers a garment or cloth to a dark-skinned figure standing in mire.
9. **f. 19v, the winged hermaphrodite/rebis** — a two-headed composite figure represents conjunction and perfected union.
10. **f. 20v, dismemberment** — an armed man displays the severed head of a dismembered body.
11. **f. 21v, boiling the old man** — a figure is cooked in a cauldron, adapting the rejuvenation of Pelias associated with Medea in Ovid.
12. **f. 23r, Saturn and his children** — Saturn rides above terrestrial occupations; a child and dragon occupy the central flask.
13. **f. 24r, Jupiter and his children** — three differently colored birds appear in the flask.
14. **f. 25r, Mars and his children** — martial scenes surround a vessel containing a triple-headed bird.
15. **f. 26r, Sol and his children** — solar activities surround a vessel containing a dragon or lionlike composite.
16. **f. 27r, Mercury and his children** — Mercury’s chariot is drawn by cocks; below are arts, trades, and a queen in a flask.
17. **f. 28r, Venus and her children** — festive and amorous activity surrounds a peacock in a flask.
18. **f. 29r, Luna and her children** — hunting and fishing accompany a red king in the vessel.
19. **f. 30v, the black sun** — the darkened or setting sun signifies putrefaction and *nigredo*.
20. **f. 31v, children’s play** — a metaphor for the reputed simplicity of the perfected work.
21. **f. 32v, women’s work** — laundering or domestic work signifies washing, whitening, sublimation, or distillation.
22. **f. 33v, the red sun** — sunrise or solar radiance marks *rubedo* and completion.

The British Library’s descriptions and foliation are authoritative for the present codex. Some modern editions reorder Mercury and Venus because the received Harley sequence places them contrary to the usual planetary order; the 2019 study explicitly says the two are bound in the manuscript in the “wrong” order. That judgment concerns the presumed intended pictorial sequence, not a demonstrated modern rebinding accident. [Skinner, Prinke, Hedesan, and Godwin, 2019 study](https://www.rexresearch1.com/AlchemyArchives/SkinnerSplendorSolis.pdf)

### 1.3 Berlin Cod. 78 D 3: the oldest surviving illuminated witness

The Berlin codex is held by the Staatliche Museen zu Berlin, Kupferstichkabinett, as **Cod. 78 D 3**. Its leaves measure approximately **331 × 228 mm** and are painted in opaque color on parchment. The museum currently dates the individual miniatures to **1531–1532** and lists Jörg Breu the Elder as the present attribution, while preserving earlier attributions to the Nuremberg school, Georg Pencz, Hans Sebald Beham, Albrecht Glockendon, and Nikolaus Glockendon. [Staatliche Museen object record](https://search.smb.museum/object/obj-799339)

The Katalog der deutschsprachigen illustrierten Handschriften describes:

- 40 parchment leaves;
- one neat scribal hand;
- two columns of 20–28 lines;
- East Franconian written language;
- 19 surviving opaque-color miniatures;
- three lost miniatures;
- four miniatures cut out and subsequently mounted elsewhere in the volume;
- richly ornamented initials and abundant gold.

The missing images are the philosopher with the flask, the lunar planetary image, and the black sun. One image was placed on a modern leaf after the museum acquired the codex in 1903. [KdiH, Berlin Cod. 78 D 3](https://www.kdih.badw.de/datenbank/handschrift/2/3/1)

Thus the oldest illuminated witness is materially incomplete and has been rearranged. Harley MS 3469, though about fifty years later, preserves all 22 paintings and is the best-known complete luxury representative.

### 1.4 Other witnesses and copies

The manuscript tradition is plural. The principal illuminated witnesses produced by about 1600 are:

- Berlin, Kupferstichkabinett, Cod. 78 D 3;
- Berlin, Staatsbibliothek, Ms. germ. fol. 42;
- Kassel, Universitätsbibliothek, 2° Ms. chem. 21;
- London, British Library, Harley MS 3469;
- Nuremberg, Germanisches Nationalmuseum, Hs. 146766;
- Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, ms. allemand 113;
- Philadelphia, University of Pennsylvania, Oversize Ms. Codex 108, an unfinished luxury manuscript with spaces prepared for miniatures but no completed paintings.

Other textual witnesses survive at Copenhagen, Kassel, Leiden, Munich, Prague, Solothurn, and Wolfenbüttel. A later French manuscript is Paris, BnF, français 12297. A paper copy reportedly seen in a Bern private collection in 1937 is not securely located today.

The Philadelphia manuscript contains 41 parchment leaves, measures **306 × 200 mm** unbound, retains a sixteenth-century German vellum-over-pasteboards binding with remnants of green textile ties, and has blank pages prepared for images. Its boards are warped, the binding is separating, and the first quire is nearly detached. The University of Pennsylvania acquired it in 1952. Its complete digital photography supplies a useful control witness for the textual tradition. [OPenn](https://openn.library.upenn.edu/Data/0002/html/mscodex108.html)

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Earliest securely dated physical evidence

The earliest surviving illuminated manuscript is Berlin Cod. 78 D 3. Dates incorporated into its miniatures are read as **1531 and 1532**, although one older reading gave 1535. Its documented provenance begins only in nineteenth-century Paris; neither commissioner nor first owner is named in the manuscript.

The museum currently associates the work with Nuremberg, while Jörg Völlnagel’s stylistic study relocated its artistic conception to **Augsburg** and attributed the miniatures to **Jörg Breu the Elder**. The institutional and scholarly positions therefore do not fully coincide:

- **Documented:** the codex exists and contains early-sixteenth-century dates.
- **Institutional attribution:** Staatliche Museen currently lists Jörg Breu the Elder, while recording former attributions.
- **Scholarly inference:** Völlnagel argued for Augsburg and Breu.
- **Disputed:** Joachim Telle called the identification of this codex as the archetype “risky,” and Michael Roth emphasized that southern German and Austrian book painting remains too incompletely documented for a secure attribution.

No surviving contract, commission, workshop account, payment, or named patron has been found. Claims that Albrecht of Brandenburg or his brother Joachim I commissioned the manuscript are hypotheses based on artistic and courtly context, not documentary provenance. A further proposal assigned the work to Georg Pencz. None is established.

### 2.2 Was the text originally illustrated?

Two reconstructions compete.

**Völlnagel’s integrated-work hypothesis:** Berlin 78 D 3 was the original artistic conception; text and pictorial cycle were coordinated from the beginning and executed by a very small team, probably a scribe and painter.

**Text-first hypothesis:** the alchemical florilegium existed before the luxury cycle. Five surviving textual manuscripts have no references to illustrations, and some textual scholars place the composition in the later fifteenth century. On this model, a pre-existing compilation was furnished with a pictorial program by or before 1531.

Rafał Prinke considers the text-first model plausible because early non-illustrated branches exist and because the relatively crude Solothurn images may preserve a pictorial stage independent of the luxury manuscripts. Nevertheless, the precise lost ancestor cannot be recovered. The oldest securely datable *physical witness* remains the Berlin codex; a fifteenth-century textual origin is a scholarly reconstruction, not an extant artifact. [2019 study](https://www.rexresearch1.com/AlchemyArchives/SkinnerSplendorSolis.pdf)

### 2.3 Sources and manufacture of the pictorial program

The cycle was assembled from a broad store of late-medieval and Renaissance imagery:

- *Aurora consurgens* supplied or paralleled mining imagery and several symbols;
- *Sol und Luna* and the *Rosarium philosophorum* supplied conjunction, resurrection, and hermaphroditic forms;
- *Donum Dei* contributed the consistent use of symbolic figures inside flasks;
- the *Buch der heiligen Dreifaltigkeit* supplied related hermaphrodite and Christian-alchemical imagery;
- the visions attributed to Zosimos supplied the motif of cutting or dismemberment;
- Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* supplied the boiling and rejuvenation episode;
- Virgil’s *Aeneid* supplied the golden bough;
- Petrus Bonus’s *Pretiosa margarita novella* probably mediated classical quotations into an alchemical context;
- contemporary Planetenkinder prints, particularly a 1531 series associated with Georg Pencz, supplied groups of planetary occupations;
- prints by Burgkmair, Beham, Pencz, Aldegrever, and other German artists contributed individual figures or compositions;
- Netherlandish and Flemish books of hours informed the borders, landscapes, and jewel-like finish.

These borrowings do not make the manuscript a forgery. Early-modern workshop practice routinely adapted prints and manuscript models. The distinctive achievement was the coordination of inherited alchemical emblems, planetary astrology, courtly borders, biblical and classical scenes, and genre imagery.

### 2.4 Manufacture of Harley MS 3469

Harley MS 3469 was written and painted in Germany in **1582**, the date appearing on three folios. Its commissioner, scribe, painter, and workshop are unnamed.

Völlnagel concluded that it is a close descendant of the Nuremberg manuscript of 1545. Stanislas Klossowski de Rola attributed it to Gabriel Glockendon, but this is an attribution, not a documented signature. Its exact city of production remains unresolved; Augsburg and Nuremberg are the principal stylistic poles.

The lavish parchment, opaque pigments, gold, large initials, and complete sequence imply an expensive commission for a wealthy reader or collector. That inference is strong, but neither cost nor patron is recorded. No quarry, mine, animal source, parchment-production site, pigment analysis, or gold assay has been published in the sources examined.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 Harley MS 3469

**1582 — Germany.** The manuscript was produced, but its first owner and place of production are unknown. The chain of custody is blank for approximately the next century.

**2 September 1680 — Whitehall Palace, London: possible but unproven identification.** John Evelyn saw in Charles II’s library a German manuscript depicting the process of the philosophers’ great elixir in “incomparable miniature.” The description fits a *Splendor Solis*, but does not establish that it was Harley MS 3469. The modern British Library catalogue cautiously calls it “a manuscript comparable to Harley 3469.” Peter Kidd’s provenance study regarded identification with Harley 3469 as highly improbable. It must not be treated as a secure custody link.

**Before 1721 — “Mr Cyprianus,” “Mrs Priemer,” and an unnamed buyer: partly documented, identities disputed.** On a flyleaf Edward Harley wrote that the book had belonged to “the famous Mr Cyprianus”; that it was bought from Mrs Priemer, described as Cyprianus’s niece; and that an unnamed, later-erased donor gave it to Harley in 1721. The inscription is direct evidence for what Harley believed or had been told, but not independent verification of each earlier ownership link.

The British Library catalogue identifies Cyprianus as the Leipzig theologian Johann Cyprian (1642–1723). This identification has serious chronological difficulties: the manuscript was in Harley’s hands in 1721, before Cyprian died in 1723, and documentary work has not established the supposed niece or transfer.

Prinke proposed an alternative: “Cyprianus” might mean Archbishop William Laud, called *Cyprianus Anglicus* in Peter Heylin’s 1668 biography. Laud imported manuscripts from Germany and received books connected with the Earl of Arundel’s efforts to preserve Continental collections during the Thirty Years’ War. Laud had half-siblings and nieces, although no Mrs Priemer has been identified. This is an ingenious but unverified reconstruction. [2019 study](https://www.rexresearch1.com/AlchemyArchives/SkinnerSplendorSolis.pdf)

**1721 — Edward Harley.** This is the first secure dated private-custody link. Edward Harley, second Earl of Oxford and Mortimer, recorded receiving the volume as a gift. The donor’s name was subsequently erased. Whether the erasure was Harley’s or another person’s is not established.

**1741–1753 — Henrietta Harley and Margaret Cavendish Bentinck.** Edward Harley’s will placed his library with his widow Henrietta for life and then with their daughter Margaret, Duchess of Portland.

**1753 — sale to the British nation.** Henrietta and Margaret sold the Harley manuscripts to the nation for **£10,000** under the legislation establishing the British Museum. The sum covered the entire manuscript collection, not Harley MS 3469 individually. It became one of the British Museum’s foundation manuscripts. [British Library catalogue](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/040-001616146)

**1753–1973 — British Museum.** The codex remained in national custody in the Department of Manuscripts. By 1808 it had been formally catalogued as Harleian manuscript no. 3469. At an undocumented date it received its present institutional red-leather binding, silk endpapers, gilt edges, and protective interleaves.

**1973 — transfer to the British Library Board.** Section 3 of the British Library Act 1972 transferred the British Museum’s Department of Manuscripts holdings to the British Library Board on the appointed day. This was a statutory institutional transfer, not a private sale. [British Library Act 1972](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1972/54/pdfs/ukpga_19720054_en.pdf)

**1998–present — British Library, St Pancras.** The British Library opened at 96 Euston Road in 1998. Harley MS 3469 remains under the British Library Board’s control.

No theft, illicit export, wartime seizure, restitution claim, or contested title involving Harley MS 3469 appears in the institutional or scholarly sources examined.

### 3.2 Berlin Cod. 78 D 3

**1531–1532 or c. 1532–1535 — manufacture.** First owner unknown.

**Sixteenth century to late nineteenth century — provenance gap.** No documented chain currently bridges its manufacture and appearance in the Rodolphe Kann collection.

**By 1903 — Rodolphe Kann collection, Paris.** The codex belonged to the financier and art collector Rodolphe Kann.

**1903 — acquisition by the Berlin Kupferstichkabinett.** The museum acquired it from Kann’s collection. By or after this acquisition, one detached image was mounted on a modern folio. Three other images are lost and four surviving paintings had been cut out and reinserted. The dates and agents of the cutting are unestablished. [KdiH](https://www.kdih.badw.de/datenbank/handschrift/2/3/1)

**2005 — facsimile work and exhibition.** The Kupferstichkabinett completed its first full facsimile project and exhibited the manuscript from 24 June to 11 September 2005. Museum conservator-curator Michael Roth explained that digital photography and proofing required repeated access to the original, coordinated to reduce handling. [Staatliche Museen exhibition record](https://www.smb.museum/ausstellungen/detail/splendor-solis-oder-sonnenglanz/) [Roth on digitization](https://books.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/arthistoricum/catalog/view/179/235/76791)

**Present — Berlin.** It remains Kupferstichkabinett Cod. 78 D 3.

### 3.3 Other notable chains

- **Nuremberg Hs. 146766:** dated 1545; earlier provenance not established in the consulted record.
- **Paris, allemand 113:** dated 1577; probably associated with Emperor Rudolf II. It later passed into Johann Ferdinand von Schönfeld’s Museum der Technologie, founded in Vienna in 1799; the BnF acquired it in 1860. The claim that it was Rudolf’s “gold-making book” is supported by a chain through Schönfeld’s acquisition of part of Rudolf’s collection, but the exact date and mechanism of Rudolf’s acquisition remain uncertain. [KdiH Paris record](https://kdih.badw.de/datenbank/handschrift/2/3/6)
- **Kassel Ms. chem. 21:** dated 1588; purchased by a Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel in the first half of the eighteenth century; badly damaged in the Second World War; restored, but portions of miniatures remain missing.
- **Berlin Ms. germ. fol. 42:** documented in the Electoral Library by 1746.
- **Philadelphia Ms. Codex 108:** sixteenth century; former German library label survives; acquired by the University of Pennsylvania in 1952; earlier chain not established.
- **Bern/private paper copy:** sold in Paris in 1884, examined by Gustav Hartlaub in a Bern private collection in 1937, and not securely traced thereafter. It was probably copied after 1617 from the London manuscript or a close relative.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 There is no single “original” securely identified

All surviving codices are genuine early-modern witnesses, but “the original Splendor Solis” can mean three different things:

- the lost first version of the text;
- the lost or surviving prototype of the illustrated cycle;
- the most complete or artistically celebrated copy.

Berlin 78 D 3 is the earliest surviving illuminated witness and may be the archetype, but this is disputed. Harley 3469 is not the oldest copy; it is a later, exceptionally complete luxury descendant.

### 4.2 Authorship

**Salomon Trismosin — traditional attribution, historically unsupported.** The 1598 first volume of *Aureum vellus* presented Trismosin as the teacher of Paracelsus, but *Splendor Solis* appeared in a separate third volume in 1599 without any statement that Trismosin wrote it. The attribution arose when the 1612 French *La Toyson d’or* retained Trismosin’s name on a title page while publishing only an expanded translation of *Splendor Solis*. Julius Kohn repeated the attribution in 1920, and C. G. Jung and later writers made it standard in popular literature.

**Ulrich Poyssel/Poyselius — early attribution, unproven.** Prague and Leiden manuscripts assign the text to “Ulricus Poyssel canonicus.” A 1608 printed reference mentions “Udalrici Poysselii Splendorem Solis.” Prinke argues that a Poyssel may have owned or signed a copy that others reproduced, allowing ownership to become authorship. No contemporary biography establishes him as the author, and the theory that he was Paracelsus’s teacher belongs to later legend-building.

**Present evidentiary conclusion:** the compiler-author is anonymous.

### 4.3 Artist and commissioner

Attributions have moved among institutional and scholarly authorities:

- Nuremberg school;
- Nikolaus or Albrecht Glockendon and workshop;
- Hans Sebald Beham;
- Georg Pencz;
- Netherlandish mannerists;
- Jörg Breu the Elder and the Augsburg school.

The Berlin museum now lists Breu as the attributed painter, but also exposes the attribution history. No signature, contract, or payment record settles the question. Gabriel Glockendon’s supposed authorship of Harley 3469 is likewise unverified.

Proposed commissioners include Albrecht of Brandenburg and Joachim I Nestor. No documentary commission survives.

### 4.4 Scientific examination

No radiocarbon dating, multispectral survey, X-ray fluorescence pigment study, Raman analysis, parchment DNA analysis, peptide mass fingerprinting, or dendrochronological study of either Harley MS 3469 or Berlin 78 D 3 was identified in the sources used for this dossier.

Consequently:

- dates rest principally on written dates, palaeography, codicology, and stylistic comparison;
- geographic attribution rests on dialect and art-historical comparison;
- no published scientific result independently confirms the animal species of the parchment, sources of pigments or gold, or workshop.

Absence of a located publication does not prove that no conservation examination has ever occurred. It means that a publicly documented instrumental analysis could not be established here.

### 4.5 Alteration and continuity

Harley 3469 is materially continuous as a sixteenth-century text-and-image block, but its binding, flyleaf arrangement, paper interleaves, and possibly pictorial order reflect later custody. The donor’s name in Harley’s provenance note was erased.

Berlin 78 D 3 is incomplete: leaves and three paintings are lost; four paintings were excised and reattached; one is on a modern leaf. It remains an authentic early witness, but not in an untouched sixteenth-century state.

No documented modern forgery masquerading as Harley MS 3469 is known. Facsimiles are openly published reproductions. The commercial term “quasi-original” is marketing language, not an assertion that a facsimile is the historic codex.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

- **Anonymous compiler, late fifteenth or early sixteenth century:** assembled the seven-tractate florilegium from earlier alchemical authorities. Identity unresolved.
- **Anonymous scribe and painter(s), 1582:** made Harley MS 3469. No signatures survive.
- **Jörg Breu the Elder, c. 1475–1537:** present leading attribution for the Berlin paintings; documentary connection unproved.
- **Nikolaus and Albrecht Glockendon; Hans Sebald Beham; Georg Pencz:** successive candidates for the original pictorial program.
- **Gabriel Glockendon, c. 1515–c. 1595:** proposed painter of the London manuscript; unverified.
- **Albrecht of Brandenburg and Joachim I Nestor:** proposed commissioners of the archetype; disputed.
- **Paracelsus, 1493/94–1541:** not a documented owner or author. Later publishers constructed Trismosin and Poyssel as his teachers or associates to confer authority.
- **Salomon Trismosin:** a literary alchemical adept whose historical identity is unestablished; attached to *Splendor Solis* through the 1612 French edition.
- **Ulrich Poyssel/Poyselius:** named in one manuscript branch; possibly owner, copyist, or claimed author.
- **Leonhard and Georg Straub:** probably responsible respectively for printing and woodcutting the 1598–1599 Rorschach *Aureum vellus*.
- **Charles Sevestre and “L. I.”:** publisher and unidentified translator-commentator of the 1612 French edition.
- **Emperor Rudolf II:** probable owner of Paris allemand 113, not Harley 3469.
- **John Evelyn:** recorded seeing a comparable manuscript at Whitehall in 1680; no secure custody connection to Harley 3469.
- **Charles II:** the Whitehall manuscript was in his Royal Library, but its identity with Harley 3469 is disputed.
- **Johann Cyprian:** conventionally identified as the “Cyprianus” named in Harley’s note; chronological evidence counts against the identification.
- **William Laud:** alternative identification proposed because of the title *Cyprianus Anglicus*; unverified.
- **Mrs Priemer:** alleged intermediary owner and “niece” of Cyprianus; not independently identified.
- **Edward Harley:** first securely documented dated private possessor of Harley 3469, in 1721.
- **Henrietta Harley and Margaret Cavendish Bentinck:** inherited legal interests and joined in the 1753 national sale.
- **British Museum and British Library:** successive public custodians.
- **Julius Kohn:** translator of the 1920 English edition and major transmitter of the Trismosin attribution.
- **C. G. Jung:** used the images within analytical psychology, making them widely familiar outside manuscript and alchemical studies.
- **Gustav Friedrich Hartlaub:** inaugurated sustained modern art-historical attribution research in 1937.
- **Jörg Völlnagel:** argued for Augsburg, Breu, and an integrated artistic archetype.
- **Joachim Telle:** challenged both secure Breu attribution and identification of Berlin 78 D 3 as the archetype.
- **Rafał T. Prinke:** reconstructed textual transmission and dismantled the routine Trismosin attribution.
- **Peter Kidd:** critically investigated the Harley provenance and the Whitehall/Cyprianus identifications.
- **M. Moleiro and the British Library:** produced the 2010–2011 deluxe facsimile of Harley 3469.

The divine and mythological beings depicted or invoked include God as creator and guarantor of nature; Sol, Luna, Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn as planetary powers; angels as agents of purification; and classical or biblical figures including Aeneas, Esther, Ahasuerus, David, Bathsheba, Medea, and Pelias. These are iconographic and textual associations, not evidence that the manuscript functioned as a cult object.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

No coronation, liturgy, pilgrimage, oath, public procession, healing rite, or regular cultic use is documented. No evidence establishes a rule restricting the book to ordained, royal, male, or initiated hands.

Its primary historical functions were:

- **didactic-contemplative:** organizing natural philosophy and alchemical doctrine into a memorable sequence;
- **prestige:** displaying costly parchment, gold, calligraphy, and learned imagery;
- **esoteric:** presenting knowledge as deliberately concealed beneath quotations, emblems, and parables;
- **alchemical legitimation:** grounding the art in nature, God’s creation, antiquity, classical learning, and named sages;
- **collecting:** serving as an elite Kunstkammer or library object, especially in later princely and bibliophile contexts.

The text describes art as nature’s assistant, not as a power creating gold from nothing. It offers principles and allegories rather than a reproducible laboratory recipe. Claims that a reader can simply follow the images to manufacture gold are modern exaggerations.

The sequence’s main symbolic operations include generation, conjunction, death, putrefaction, washing, whitening, sublimation, multiplication, resurrection, and reddening. The planetary series coordinates metals, cosmic influences, human occupations, and operations inside the flask. The black and red suns frame the movement from decomposition to completion.

No curse, miraculous protection, sovereignty-conferring power, or prophecy is attested in the early manuscript tradition.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 “Written by Salomon Trismosin, teacher of Paracelsus”

**Tier: early-seventeenth-century publishing accretion, modern popular tradition.**

The 1599 printing did not call Trismosin the author. The 1612 French edition generated the association by retaining his name from the broader *Aureum vellus* apparatus. Kohn’s 1920 edition and Jungian literature normalized it. There is no secure evidence that Trismosin was a historical person, wrote the work, or taught Paracelsus.

### 7.2 “Written about 1440 by Ulrich Poyssel”

**Tier: scholarly reconstruction combined with unverified biographical legend.**

A fifteenth-century textual nucleus is possible. Manuscript attributions to Poyssel are real, but authorship, the date 1440, and a teacher-pupil relation with Paracelsus are not established. Some modern commercial accounts present this chain as fact; the documentary record does not.

### 7.3 “The Berlin manuscript is the original”

**Tier: influential scholarly hypothesis.**

It is the earliest surviving illuminated witness and may be the model for later luxury copies. Telle and others caution that an earlier textual or pictorial ancestor may have existed. “Oldest surviving” is established; “original” is not.

### 7.4 “The London manuscript belonged to Charles II”

**Tier: plausible identification formerly repeated as fact, now disputed.**

Evelyn’s diary proves that an illustrated German alchemical manuscript was in Whitehall in 1680. It does not name *Splendor Solis* or give sufficient codicological detail to identify Harley 3469. The British Library now says “comparable to” it, while Kidd rejected the identification as highly improbable.

### 7.5 Tarot and Kabbalistic correspondences

**Tier: modern occult reinterpretation.**

Because *Splendor Solis* has 22 images and the Tarot has 22 major trumps, Golden Dawn-associated writers proposed correspondences. The 2019 study argues that the match is numerical and superficial: beyond the seven planetary associations, image subjects and order do not correspond. Nothing in the sixteenth-century manuscripts identifies the cycle as Tarot or maps it onto the 22 paths of the Kabbalistic Tree of Life.

### 7.6 Psychological individuation

**Tier: twentieth-century Jungian reinterpretation.**

Jung and later analysts read alchemical conjunction, dismemberment, death, whitening, and rebirth as symbolic processes of psychic integration. This reception was historically consequential, but it is not an early statement of the manuscript’s purpose. Henderson and Sherwood’s *Transformation of the Psyche* is a major example.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Harley MS 3469

As of September 2026, Harley MS 3469 is catalogued in the British Library, 96 Euston Road, London NW1 2DB, under the control of the British Library Board. It is not described as being on permanent public display.

The catalogue currently states:

- restrictions apply;
- physical consultation must be arranged with British Library staff;
- manuscript and archive consultation occurs in London;
- the former digitized-manuscript link is listed, but digital images are currently unavailable through that catalogue;
- the object has the legal status “Not Public Record(s).”

It was listed in the British Library’s online *A History of Magic* presentation and lent or displayed in **Alchemy: The Great Art**, Berlin, 6 April–23 July 2017. [British Library catalogue](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/040-001616146)

No active ownership or repatriation dispute is recorded. Its title is vested in the British Library Board subject to the statutory regime established by the British Library Act. The Act generally makes disposal more restrictive than ordinary museum deaccessioning, although later legislation can authorize particular returns.

### Berlin Cod. 78 D 3

The oldest illuminated witness remains at the Kupferstichkabinett, Staatliche Museen zu Berlin, identification number **78 D 3**. Its individual pages are represented in the museum’s online collections. It is incomplete but conserved as a bound codex. No current restitution claim was found.

### Digital surrogates

A complete institutional digital surrogate is presently most readily available for Philadelphia Ms. Codex 108 through OPenn. The former British Library digital viewer for Harley 3469 is currently marked unavailable, although reproductions circulate through licensed facsimiles, publications, and image repositories.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Printed transmission

The first printed *Splendor Solis* appeared in the third part of *Aureum vellus* at Rorschach in **1599**, probably printed by Leonhard Straub and illustrated by woodblocks possibly cut by Georg Straub. A second 1599 issue and a pirated Leipzig edition of 1600 followed. The print cycle simplified architecture, landscapes, borders, and planetary scenes.

The **1612 Paris** *La Toyson d’or* freely translated and expanded the work, inserted separately printed and hand-colored images, and inadvertently or deliberately attached Trismosin’s name to it. A 1613 issue added the influential composite alchemical frontispiece.

A Hamburg edition followed in **1708**, with new engravings; it was reissued about 1718 under the title *Eröffnete Geheimnisse des Steins der Weisen*.

### 9.2 Twentieth-century revival

Julius Kohn’s **1920** English *Splendor Solis: Alchemical Treatises of Solomon Trismosin, Adept and Teacher of Paracelsus* reproduced the Harley pictures in black and white. Its title propagated two unsupported biographical claims: Trismosin’s authorship and his tutorship of Paracelsus. A public bibliographic record links digitized copies of that edition. [University of Pennsylvania Online Books Page](https://onlinebooks.library.upenn.edu/webbin/book/lookupid?key=olbp55305)

Jung’s use of alchemical images, followed by Jungian studies such as Henderson and Sherwood’s 2003 *Transformation of the Psyche*, shifted the manuscript’s public identity from a historical alchemical florilegium toward a map of psychological transformation.

Images from Harley 3469 have since circulated on book jackets, music releases, commercial labels, occult websites, fantasy publications, and self-help material. Such detached use often suppresses foliation, textual context, and the fact that the 22-image sequence is not an early Tarot deck.

### 9.3 Facsimiles

The Berlin Kupferstichkabinett’s first full facsimile project was completed in **2005**, accompanied by a scholarly commentary and exhibition. An edition of 998 numbered copies is recorded.

M. Moleiro published a deluxe Harley MS 3469 facsimile in **2010–2011**, limited to **987 numbered copies**, with a commentary by Völlnagel, Thomas Hofmeier, Peter Kidd, and Joscelyn Godwin. It reproduces the modern crimson binding, gold tooling, silk endpapers, and full color of the present codex. Commercial descriptions call it a “quasi-original”; this identifies a high-fidelity reproduction, not an artifact with sixteenth-century continuity. [Facsimile description](https://www.facsimiles.com/facsimiles/london-splendor-solis)

### 9.4 Exhibitions and public culture

- Berlin, **Splendor Solis oder Sonnenglanz**, 24 June–11 September 2005.
- Berlin, **Alchemy: The Great Art**, 6 April–23 July 2017.
- British Library online exhibition, **A History of Magic**, from 31 July 2017.
- The broader 2017–2018 *Harry Potter: A History of Magic* program placed historical alchemical objects before a very large popular audience, further integrating *Splendor Solis* imagery into contemporary fantasy culture.

No evidence establishes that the manuscript itself inspired J. K. Rowling’s fictional objects; its exhibition context is a documented modern engagement, not part of its early history.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

The historiography has moved through four main stages.

### Cataloguing and textual identification

The Harleian codex was catalogued in 1808. Robert Priebsch’s catalogue of German manuscripts in England supplied a more specialized description. Early historians of alchemy discussed the printed text without consistently attributing it to Trismosin.

### Iconographic attribution

Hartlaub’s 1937 study treated the illustrations as products of Dürer’s Nuremberg milieu, proposing Glockendon, Beham, or Pencz. René Alleau emphasized Beham and the Planetenkinder prints. Jacques van Lennep first suggested Netherlandish mannerists, then reassigned roles to Albrecht Glockendon, Beham, and Simon Bening. Barbara Daentler favored Albrecht Glockendon. Ulrich Merkl proposed Georg Pencz and Joachim I. Klossowski de Rola extended a Glockendon-family attribution across the manuscript group.

Völlnagel’s 2004 monograph was the most extensive codicological and art-historical synthesis. He argued for Augsburg, Jörg Breu the Elder, a coordinated text-image program, and Berlin 78 D 3 as the archetype. Telle and Roth remained cautious.

### Textual genealogy and authorship criticism

Prinke’s reconstruction emphasizes multiple branches:

- illuminated luxury codices;
- manuscripts with places marked for figures;
- manuscripts with no pictorial instructions;
- the Solothurn/*Aureum vellus* line;
- Poyssel-attributed copies;
- French and later occult recensions.

This genealogy shows why authorship cannot be inferred from a late title page and why the first text may have preceded the famous cycle.

### Provenance criticism

Peter Kidd’s work challenged the easy identification of Evelyn’s Whitehall manuscript with Harley 3469 and exposed the chronological weakness in identifying Harley’s “Cyprianus” with Johann Cyprian. The provenance before 1721 therefore remains unresolved.

### Conservation and scientific lacunae

Institutional catalogues document parchment, dimensions, binding, missing leaves, reinsertion, modern guards, and wartime damage to the Kassel witness. Published instrumental materials analysis remains a conspicuous gap. The most consequential technical intervention documented online is high-resolution digital facsimile production, undertaken partly to reduce future handling.

### Persistent discredited or unsupported claims

The following remain common online but should not be stated as fact:

- composition in 1440 by Ulrich Poyssel;
- Poyssel or Trismosin as Paracelsus’s established teacher;
- Trismosin’s authorship;
- secure commission by Albrecht of Brandenburg;
- secure painting by a named master;
- Harley 3469’s ownership by Charles II;
- the 22 pictures as an early Tarot system;
- Berlin 78 D 3 as unquestionably the unique original.

### Evidentiary limitations

The provenance of Harley MS 3469 is secure only from Edward Harley’s 1721 inscription onward. The Berlin codex’s provenance is secure only by its appearance in the Rodolphe Kann collection and 1903 museum acquisition. No commission document survives for either. The identities of the authors, scribes, painters, first patrons, “Cyprianus,” Mrs Priemer, and Harley’s erased donor remain unresolved. No published scientific materials analysis was established in the sources consulted.
