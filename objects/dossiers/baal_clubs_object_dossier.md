# Yagrush and Ayyamur, Baal-Hadad's clubs: Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `yagrush_and_ayyamur_baal_hadads_clubs_object_dossier.md`

## Executive finding

Yagrush and Ayyamur are two named supernatural weapons in the Ugaritic *Baal Cycle*. They are not surviving archaeological artifacts, and no ancient object is securely identified as either weapon. Their sole direct attestation is a passage written in alphabetic cuneiform on a Late Bronze Age clay tablet conventionally cited as **RS 3.367, column IV = CTA 2 IV = KTU/CAT 1.2 IV**, now represented by fragmentary tablets **AO 16640 and AO 16640 bis** in the Musée du Louvre.

In that passage the divine artisan Kothar-wa-Khasis makes or prepares two weapons, announces their names, and directs them against Yamm (“Sea”) and his parallel manifestation or title Nahar (“River”). Yagrush strikes Yamm’s upper torso but does not bring him down. Ayyamur strikes his head and forehead; Yamm collapses. The weapons move from Baal’s hand “like a bird of prey” and execute Kothar’s commands with a degree of apparent agency.

Almost everything commonly said about their appearance—metal heads, handles, lightning shapes, wind and thunder affinities, inscriptions, colors, or dimensions—is unattested ancient reconstruction or modern invention. Even the familiar classification “clubs” or “maces” is a defensible but not certain translation of Ugaritic **ṣmdm**, the dual of an incompletely understood weapon noun. The text supplies no material, dimensions, weight, ornament, storage place, later owner, destruction, or final disposition.

The frequently reproduced “Baal with Thunderbolt” stela, Louvre **AO 15775**, depicts a storm god raising a blunt weapon and holding a branching spear or lightning emblem. It establishes that such divine weapon imagery existed at Ugarit, but it contains no names or inscriptions identifying the depicted implement as Yagrush or Ayyamur. It is therefore comparative iconography, not a physical claimant.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Object class

**Evidentiary status: early attested mythic objects.**

Yagrush and Ayyamur are a pair of individually named divine weapons created for Baal in a combat narrative. They belong to the broad ancient Near Eastern class of personified or semi-autonomous weapons: implements that bear proper names, receive spoken commands, and act with more agency than an ordinary tool.

The Ugaritic passage calls them collectively:

- **ṣmdm**: grammatically dual, “two ṣmd-weapons.”
- Singular in the action clauses: **ṣmd**.

“Club” or “mace” is the prevailing practical translation. Alternatives in scholarship and translations include “weapons,” “bludgeons,” “smashers,” “axes,” and lightning-like missiles. Nothing excavated fixes the form. The verbs describe impact rather than cutting, which favors a blunt or smiting weapon, but does not by itself prove a conventional hand-club.

### 1.2 Names and scripts

The unvocalized Ugaritic spellings are:

- **𐎊𐎂𐎗𐎌 — ygrš**
- **𐎀𐎊𐎎𐎗 — ʾaymr**

The tablet’s alphabet normally records consonants rather than full vocalization. Consequently all vocalized forms are scholarly reconstructions.

Common modern forms include:

| Ugaritic spelling | Scholarly vocalizations and modern forms | Conventional sense |
|---|---|---|
| **ygrš** | Yagruš, Yagrušu, Yagarrish, Yagrush | “May he/it drive out,” “Driver,” “Chaser,” “Expeller” |
| **ʾaymr** | ʾAyamur, ʾAyamiri, Ayyamarri, Aymur | “May he/it expel,” “All-expeller,” “Expeller,” “Driver” |

Translations sometimes reverse “Driver” and “Chaser.” The safest procedure is to preserve the names and explain their wordplay:

- **ygrš** is transparently associated in its immediate context with the root **grš**, “drive out, expel.” The name is followed by the imperative **grš ym**, “drive out Yamm.”
- **ʾaymr** is followed by **mr ym**, likewise understood as “expel Yamm.” Its internal analysis is less secure. One influential explanation divides it into an emphatic or totalizing element **ʾay** plus a form from **mry/mr**, yielding something like “the one who expels anyone/everything.” Older proposals treated part of the name as a divine name or derived it from a different Semitic root; these have not won general acceptance.

The names are therefore functional or “speaking” names. Each states what the weapon is to do.

### 1.3 “Baal-Hadad”

The combat text normally calls the wielder **bʿl**, conventionally vocalized **Baʿlu**, “Lord,” and gives him titles such as “Rider on the Clouds.” Ugaritic sources elsewhere also use **Haddu/Hadad** for the storm god. “Baal-Hadad” is a convenient modern identification, but it should not be mistaken for the wording of every line in KTU 1.2 IV. The named weapons are explicitly placed in **Baal’s** hand in this passage.

### 1.4 Source-by-source physical description

#### KTU 1.2 IV, lines 11–17: Yagrush

Kothar makes or prepares two **ṣmdm** and proclaims their names. He tells Yagrush to drive Yamm from his throne and Nahar from the seat of his rule. The weapon is to leap, whirl, or swoop from Baal’s hand “like a raptor/eagle/falcon from his fingers” and strike Yamm’s shoulder, torso, back, or chest—the anatomical rendering varies because the parallel Ugaritic expressions are not entirely unambiguous.

The weapon does so. Yamm remains strong: his joints do not shake and his frame does not collapse.

#### KTU 1.2 IV, lines 18–26: Ayyamur

Kothar repeats the manufacture-and-naming formula. He commands Ayyamur to expel Yamm, move from Baal’s hand like a bird of prey, and strike Yamm’s **qdqd**, “head/crown/skull,” between or above the eyes of Judge Nahar.

Ayyamur obeys. Yamm sinks or collapses and falls to earth; his joints shake and his body or frame gives way.

### 1.5 What the text does not describe

No ancient source used here supplies:

- material;
- metal type;
- wooden shaft;
- dimensions;
- weight;
- color;
- ornament;
- maker’s mark;
- written inscription physically placed on either weapon;
- separate wind and lightning powers;
- handles shaped like serpents;
- storage in Baal’s palace;
- continuing ritual custody;
- survival after the battle;
- destruction or burial.

Kothar **speaks** their names; the tablet does not say he engraves those names on them. Modern images showing the names written on the clubs are creative reconstructions.

### 1.6 The physical witness: AO 16640 and AO 16640 bis

The legendary weapons do not survive, but the manuscript witness does.

The Louvre catalogues the relevant material as:

- **AO 16640**, excavation numbers **RS 3.367 / RS 3.346 / RS 3.367 + 3.346**;
- **AO 16640 bis**, associated with the same excavation numbers.

AO 16640 is a terracotta tablet fragment measuring approximately:

- height: **12.5 cm**;
- width: **9.9 cm**;
- thickness: **2.9 cm**.

AO 16640 bis measures approximately:

- height: **6 cm**;
- width: **6.9 cm**;
- thickness: **0.9 cm**.

The Louvre explicitly warns that the proposed physical join between the fragments is probably erroneous. This is an identity problem concerning the manuscript’s reconstruction, not evidence for two versions of the weapons.

The tablet is incomplete, written in Ugaritic alphabetic cuneiform, and dated institutionally to Late Bronze II, broadly **1400–1200 BCE**. The weapon episode itself belongs to RS 3.367, conventionally column IV.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Mythic manufacture

**Source:** KTU 1.2 IV 11–26.  
**Tier:** direct Late Bronze Age textual attestation.

The maker is **Kothar-wa-Khasis**—Ugaritic **kṯr w ḫss**, “Skillful-and-Wise,” the divine craftsman. The verb describing what he does to the two weapons, conventionally read with **nḥt**, has been translated “makes,” “fashions,” “casts,” “prepares,” or “strengthens.” “Forges” is idiomatic, given Kothar’s smith-like identity, but the passage does not state a metal, furnace, hammering procedure, or place of manufacture.

Kothar’s act has two linked components:

1. He prepares the pair of weapons.
2. He “proclaims,” “declares,” or “causes to be known” their names.

He then addresses each weapon directly. The commands anticipate the actions that the narrative immediately reports. This makes the naming function like commissioning or programming, although “magical consecration” and “incantation” are interpretive labels rather than explicit descriptions in the passage.

### 2.2 Narrative occasion

Yamm has claimed authority among the gods and demanded Baal’s submission. After a damaged and difficult section, Kothar assures Baal that he will strike his enemy and recover enduring kingship. The weapons are therefore made for a specific succession conflict: removal of Yamm from the throne and establishment or restoration of Baal’s rule.

Their names are not general labels such as “Thunder” or “Victory.” They are task-specific imperatives centered on expulsion from kingship.

### 2.3 Earliest physical attestation of the names

The oldest surviving evidence is the writing on RS 3.367. The Louvre dates the tablet broadly to the fourteenth–thirteenth centuries BCE. Current scholarship often places the surviving literary tablets associated with the scribe Ilimilku late in the thirteenth century BCE, partly because discoveries from the house of Urtenu prompted a reassessment of Ilimilku’s royal contemporaries. Older scholarship frequently dated him to the reign of Niqmaddu II in the fourteenth century BCE.

Accordingly:

- **Secure statement:** the names occur on a Late Bronze Age tablet from Ugarit.
- **Plausible current dating:** late thirteenth century BCE for Ilimilku’s literary activity.
- **Not established:** when the oral story was first composed.
- **Not established:** whether the weapon episode existed unchanged before this copy.
- **Not established:** whether an older written exemplar once existed.

The myth may preserve older Syrian storm-god combat traditions, but those traditions do not independently attest the names Yagrush and Ayyamur.

### 2.4 Archaeological discovery

RS 3.367 was found in **1931** on the acropolis of Ras Shamra, ancient Ugarit, in the building conventionally called the **House of the High Priest**, between the major temple precincts. Excavations were conducted by the French mission under Claude F.-A. Schaeffer.

Archival reconstruction is not perfectly simple. The old inventory reportedly described RS 3.367 only as a tablet fragment heavily encrusted with limestone, without measurements. Identification of the number with portions of KTU 1.2 also depends partly on Schaeffer’s excavation records. Scholarly studies have debated which pieces were found together and whether proposed joins are physically correct.

### 2.5 First publication and editorial history

Charles Virolleaud published the passage in the 1930s under the early designation **III AB A**. Successive reference systems renamed it:

- Virolleaud: **III AB A**;
- Cyrus Gordon’s corpus: **UT 68**;
- *Corpus des tablettes en cunéiformes alphabétiques*: **CTA 2 IV**;
- *Die keilalphabetischen Texte aus Ugarit*: **KTU 1.2 IV**;
- English-language usage: often **CAT 1.2 IV**.

The object names did not change on the tablet; their modern spellings changed because editions adopted different transliteration and vocalization conventions.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because the weapons are mythic, two chains must be kept separate: their **narrative custody** and the **archaeological custody of the tablet that attests them**.

### 3.1 Narrative chain of custody

#### Link 1: manufacture by Kothar-wa-Khasis

- **Holder/maker:** Kothar-wa-Khasis.
- **Mechanism:** divine manufacture or preparation.
- **Source:** KTU 1.2 IV 11 and 18.
- **Status:** directly attested in the narrative.

The text does not say who supplied materials, whether Kothar worked in a forge, or whether manufacture occurred at his customary mythic residence.

#### Link 2: deployment through Baal’s hand

- **Holder/wielder:** Baal.
- **Mechanism:** implied provision by Kothar; no formal gift formula survives.
- **Source:** KTU 1.2 IV 13–16, 20–24.
- **Status:** directly attested.

The weapons move from Baal’s hand or fingers. Whether Baal throws them, releases them, or merely serves as their point of departure is uncertain. Their flight is compared to a bird of prey.

#### Link 3: Yagrush’s unsuccessful strike

- **Action:** strikes Yamm’s upper torso.
- **Result:** Yamm does not fall.
- **Source:** KTU 1.2 IV 14–17.
- **Status:** directly attested; exact anatomy disputed.

Yagrush is not described as broken, lost, caught, or recovered after the strike.

#### Link 4: Ayyamur’s successful strike

- **Action:** strikes Yamm’s head/forehead.
- **Result:** Yamm falls and his bodily stability fails.
- **Source:** KTU 1.2 IV 21–26.
- **Status:** directly attested.

Ayyamur is not explicitly retrieved.

#### Link 5: disappearance from the surviving record

After Yamm’s fall, the text turns to Baal’s treatment of his opponent and Astarte’s intervention or rebuke. Neither weapon receives a securely identifiable later episode in the surviving *Baal Cycle*.

The narrative custody chain therefore ends during the combat. Claims that the clubs were thereafter stored in Baal’s palace, inherited by Melqart, buried, destroyed, or retained for future battles are unattested.

### 3.2 Custody of the textual witness

#### Late Bronze Age: House of the High Priest, Ugarit

The tablet was deposited, stored, or abandoned in the building now called the House of the High Priest. Whether it was a library copy, performance text, scribal product, school text, or official religious-literary archive remains debated.

#### Circa 1190 BCE: destruction and abandonment of Ugarit

Ugarit was destroyed near the end of the Late Bronze Age. The tablet remained in the ruins. No documented ancient, medieval, or early modern custody intervenes.

#### 1931: archaeological recovery

The French excavation mission recovered RS 3.367 from the acropolis. This was excavation, not a modern art-market acquisition.

#### Post-excavation division

Artifacts from the French excavations were divided under the then-operative partage system between Syrian collections and French institutions. The relevant tablet fragments entered the Louvre as **AO 16640/AO 16640 bis**. The Louvre records the acquisition mechanism as **partage après fouilles**.

This institutional history should not be collapsed into the fictional custody of the weapons: the Louvre owns tablet fragments bearing their story, not Yagrush and Ayyamur themselves.

#### Present custody

AO 16640 is held by the Musée du Louvre, Département des Antiquités orientales, French state property. The collection record updated in March 2026 lists it as visible in **Sully wing, level 0, room 301, case 12, “Literature and Mythology.”**

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Did the weapons physically exist?

There is no archaeological evidence that two historical weapons bearing these names existed. Their presence in an ancient text proves that the named weapons formed part of a Late Bronze Age narrative; it does not prove that physical cult weapons stood in a Ugaritic temple.

Possible models include:

- entirely literary supernatural weapons;
- mythic transformations of ordinary royal or divine maces;
- literary counterparts of temple weapons used in ritual;
- lightning weapons rendered metaphorically as clubs;
- personified weapons derived from wider ancient Near Eastern storytelling conventions.

None can presently be demonstrated as the exclusive explanation.

### 4.2 Is “club” authentic to the text?

Only provisionally. The Ugaritic noun **ṣmd** denotes the weapon but its precise typology is disputed. Reasons favoring a mace or club include:

- it strikes rather than pierces;
- Baal is widely depicted in the smiting pose with a raised blunt implement;
- storm gods commonly carry maces, axes, or stylized lightning weapons;
- the passage distinguishes the weapon term from clearer Ugaritic terms for swords and spears.

Reasons for caution include:

- iconographic weapons cannot be automatically equated with objects in a poem;
- impact verbs can be used with several weapon classes;
- a supernatural flying weapon need not correspond exactly to an archaeological type.

The dossier therefore uses “club/mace” as conventional shorthand, not a scientifically identified artifact class.

### 4.3 The “Baal with Thunderbolt” stela as a claimant

The Louvre stela **AO 15775 / RS 4.427**, discovered west of the Temple of Baal on 28 May 1932, shows a large striding deity, generally identified as Baal, with a raised club or mace and a long branching spear/lightning emblem. It is carved from oolitic limestone and measures 144 × 57.5 × 29 cm.

It is not Yagrush or Ayyamur, nor does it depict an identifiable pair:

- the stela has no inscription naming the handheld objects;
- only one raised blunt weapon is evident;
- the long branching object is normally interpreted as a spear and lightning/vegetal emblem;
- its date is catalogued broadly between the Middle and Late Bronze Ages;
- no source links it directly to KTU 1.2 IV.

It remains valuable evidence for Ugaritic conceptions of Baal as an armed smiting storm god. Identification of its raised weapon with one of the named clubs is possible only as an illustrative hypothesis.

### 4.4 Fragment and join dispute

The Louvre’s own notice says the proposed join between AO 16640 and AO 16640 bis is probably erroneous. Earlier editors associated RS 3.346 and RS 3.367 as parts of KTU 1.2, but the exact tablet reconstruction and column order have been reassessed.

This does not make the weapon names modern forgeries. Their signs are ancient and occur in the relevant text. The dispute concerns which fragments belonged to the same original tablet and how the surviving columns should be arranged.

### 4.5 Forgery and rival-claimant record

No ancient weapon, museum object, relic, or archaeological find is presently recognized by scholarship as a candidate “original Yagrush” or “original Ayyamur.” No documented forgery purporting to be either original has been identified in the sources consulted.

Modern replicas, fantasy weapons, ritual implements, and digital objects using the names are representations, not continuity claimants.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Function inside the narrative

The clubs accomplish a transfer of divine kingship. Their commands specify not merely that Yamm be wounded but that he be driven:

- from his throne;
- from the seat of his dominion.

Yagrush and Ayyamur therefore operate simultaneously as weapons and agents of political removal. Ayyamur’s successful blow makes possible the proclamation or recognition of Baal’s kingship.

### 6.2 Naming as activation

Kothar announces each weapon’s name and immediately addresses it. The weapon then performs the spoken command in almost formulaic repetition.

Scholars have variously described this as:

- performative naming;
- magical activation;
- weapon animation;
- “programming”;
- poetic personification;
- an incantatory command.

The first and last descriptions remain closest to the evidence. The text does not explicitly label the speech a spell, consecration, or ritual. Nor does it explain whether naming creates the weapons’ power or simply reveals it.

### 6.3 Autonomous motion

The repeated comparison to a bird of prey gives the weapons apparent self-motion. The verb **rtqṣ** can convey leaping, darting, dancing, whirling, or swooping. Thus translations range from “leaps from Baal’s hand” to “whirls in Baal’s hand.”

The distinction matters:

- “from” the hand suggests a projectile or returning divine weapon;
- “in” the hand suggests a rapidly wielded mace;
- “like a raptor from his fingers” strongly encourages the projectile reading.

The grammar and imagery support supernatural agency, but the text does not state that the clubs return to Baal after striking.

### 6.4 Cultic use at Ugarit

No ritual text has been identified that instructs priests to display, carry, strike with, or sacrifice before physical objects named Yagrush and Ayyamur.

The French Ugarit portal cautiously suggests that major myths may have been sung at royal banquets. If the *Baal Cycle* was performed there, the weapon episode had a ceremonial-literary life. That remains an inference from context, not a surviving performance rubric attached to KTU 1.2.

### 6.5 Symbolic domains

The clubs can reasonably be interpreted as symbols of:

- Baal’s martial power;
- storm violence;
- removal of chaotic or rival sovereignty;
- divine legitimation of kingship;
- the effectiveness of skilled craft joined to authoritative speech.

Assigning one club to wind and the other to lightning is a modern elaboration. The Ugaritic text does not divide meteorological powers between them.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The earliest and principal myth

**Tier: Late Bronze Age attested narrative.**

Yamm receives or claims kingship and demands Baal’s submission. The divine assembly is intimidated, but Baal resists. After damaged portions of the tablet, Kothar assures Baal of victory and prepares two named weapons.

Yagrush is commanded to drive Yamm and Nahar from their royal seats. It moves like a bird of prey and strikes Yamm’s upper body. Yamm remains firm.

Kothar then prepares and names Ayyamur. It receives nearly the same command but is directed against Yamm’s head. It strikes; Yamm collapses. Baal then continues the assault. The following verbs have been rendered “drags,” “scatters,” “dismembers,” “binds,” or “finishes” Yamm. Astarte intervenes or cries out, and the narrative announces Baal’s victory and kingship.

The clubs’ story is therefore short and asymmetrical: the first weapon fails to finish the task; the second succeeds.

### 7.2 Does Ayyamur kill Yamm?

The immediate passage can be translated as Baal destroying or finishing Yamm, but Yamm remains conceptually present elsewhere in the cycle and Ugaritic religion. Interpretations include:

- Yamm is killed in an older self-contained combat myth;
- he is defeated but not annihilated;
- the cycle incorporates incompatible narrative layers;
- his continuing presence represents the perennial threat of the sea;
- “destroy” in context means defeat or neutralize politically.

This uncertainty affects the weapons’ accomplishment. Ayyamur unquestionably brings Yamm down in the scene; whether it deals a fatal blow is unresolved.

### 7.3 Alleged biblical survivals

Scholars compare the episode with biblical passages in which a storm deity defeats Sea, River, Leviathan, or other aquatic opponents, including Psalms 74 and 89, Isaiah 27 and 51, Job 26, and Habakkuk 3. These comparisons illuminate a shared West Semitic combat tradition.

No biblical text, however, preserves the names Yagrush or Ayyamur. Claims that a named biblical weapon is literally one of the clubs are unsupported.

### 7.4 Comparative named weapons

Named and semi-autonomous divine weapons also occur in Mesopotamian and Anatolian traditions. Comparisons have been drawn with:

- Marduk’s weapons against Tiamat;
- Ninurta’s named weapons;
- the storm god’s weapons in the Hurrian-Hittite Kumarbi cycle;
- Zeus’s thunderbolt;
- Indra’s *vajra*.

These are typological parallels, not custody links. No ancient source says that Yagrush or Ayyamur passed into another god’s possession under a translated name.

### 7.5 Later equation with Melqart

**Tier: modern retelling and syncretic adaptation.**

Modern popular sources sometimes call the weapons those of Melqart, or treat Melqart as a later form of Baal-Hadad. Ancient Phoenician religion certainly permits comparisons among Baal, Hadad, Baal Ṣaphon, and Melqart, but the Ugaritic weapon episode names Baal as wielder. No ancient transfer of the clubs from Baal to Melqart is documented.

### 7.6 Modern occult elaboration

Recent occult literature has recast the weapons as ritual implements whose names were magically “charged” by Kothar. Such works sometimes:

- print modern renderings of their Ugaritic spellings;
- describe consecration procedures;
- assign elemental powers;
- identify one with wind and the other with lightning;
- change their forms or vocalizations.

These are modern esoteric constructions. They may creatively build on the text’s performative naming, but they are not evidence for Bronze Age practice.

**Status:** purely legendary/textual; no surviving physical object identified.

There is:

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Scholarship and museum presentation

Since the 1930s, translations have popularized the weapons as a matched pair of magical maces. Museum and educational materials frequently place the *Baal Cycle* passage beside images of AO 15775, encouraging a visual association between the poem and the smiting-god iconography. Responsible presentations preserve the distinction between illustration and identification.

### 9.2 Translation-created imagery

Modern translations materially shape the weapons’ appearance:

- “club” suggests a wooden shaft with a heavy head;
- “mace” suggests a metal war weapon;
- “hammer” evokes a thunder god;
- “axe” suggests a bladed storm weapon;
- “lightning weapon” turns them into meteorological projectiles;
- “swoops like an eagle” promotes the image of self-propelled missiles.

The Ugaritic text itself supports impact and rapid bird-like movement but does not choose among these modern visual forms.

### 9.3 *Final Fantasy XI*

The online game *Final Fantasy XI* uses **Yagrush** and **Aymur** as separate “Mythic Weapons.” Yagrush is associated with the White Mage job; Aymur with Beastmaster. This is name-borrowing, not a faithful reproduction of the Ugaritic pair’s form or custody. The official game’s Mythic Weapon system was publicized in 2008.

Fan reference material explicitly recognizes the names’ derivation from Baal’s weapons. Because the two names were split into separate game items with different job functions, the game created a modern afterlife independent of the ancient narrative.

### 9.4 *Campione!*

Jō Taketsuki’s Japanese light-novel series *Campione!*—published in twenty-one principal volumes between 2008 and 2017 and adapted for television in 2012—features **Yagrush and Aymur** in connection with Melqart. They are represented as magically self-guiding clubs or staffs.

This reception preserves the ancient weapons’ autonomous pursuit but changes:

- their wielder from Baal to Melqart;
- their role from a single combat against Yamm to reusable supernatural armaments;
- their powers into a modern fantasy system.

Subsequent fan fiction further assigns them explicit wind and lightning properties. Those details should not be projected back into Ugaritic religion.

### 9.5 Replicas and devotional objects

Modern craftspeople and revivalist practitioners sometimes make paired clubs, staffs, pendants, sigils, or altar tools under the names. No standardized form derives from an excavated original. Such objects are modern representations even when made for sincere religious use.

### 9.6 Internet-era corruption and stabilization

Online retellings commonly introduce several errors:

- presenting “Yagrush” and “Ayyamur” as fixed ancient vocalizations;
- calling both iron war-maces;
- saying their names were engraved on them;
- assigning wind to one and lightning to the other;
- treating the Louvre stela’s weapons as the named pair;
- making them Melqart’s ancient canonical weapons;
- claiming they were recovered archaeologically.

None of those propositions is established by KTU 1.2.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Early publication

Charles Virolleaud’s publication of the Ras Shamra poems introduced the pair to modern scholarship. Early translators worked before Ugaritic grammar and lexicography had stabilized, producing divergent spellings and identifications.

The text was initially treated in editions under labels such as **III AB A** and **UT 68**. Later concordances established its current identity as **KTU 1.2 IV**.

### 10.2 Standard editions

The principal modern reference traditions are:

- Andrée Herdner’s *Corpus des tablettes en cunéiformes alphabétiques* (**CTA**);
- Manfried Dietrich, Oswald Loretz, and Joaquín Sanmartín’s *Die keilalphabetischen Texte aus Ugarit* (**KTU**);
- Mark S. Smith’s *The Ugaritic Baal Cycle*, volume I;
- Simon B. Parker and collaborators’ *Ugaritic Narrative Poetry*;
- Dennis Pardee’s translation in *The Context of Scripture*;
- Nicolas Wyatt’s *Religious Texts from Ugarit*;
- Pierre Bordreuil and Dennis Pardee’s *A Manual of Ugaritic*.

### 10.3 Major debates

#### Meaning of ṣmd

The central object-level debate is whether **ṣmd** should be translated mace, club, axe, missile, or simply weapon. There is no excavated labeled example to settle it.

#### Meaning of the manufacture verb

The verb describing Kothar’s work has been connected with making, casting, preparing, bringing down, or strengthening. “Forges” remains a vivid conventional translation rather than a literal technical report.

#### Vocalization of the names

Ugaritic alphabetic writing does not preserve the full vowels. Yagrush, Yagarrish, Yagrušu, Ayyamur, Ayyamarri, and ʾAyamiri represent competing reconstructions, not different ancient objects.

#### Anatomical targets

Translations disagree over shoulder, back, torso, chest, or the space between the arms for Yagrush’s strike. Ayyamur’s target is more securely the head or crown, with a parallel phrase involving the eyes or forehead.

#### Weapon agency

The poem’s direct address and repeated fulfillment of commands suggest personification. Whether the weapons should be considered sentient beings, magically animated objects, poetic projectiles, or extensions of Baal’s force is interpretive.

#### One manufacture or two?

Both parallel sections say that Kothar prepares “two weapons” and proclaims “their names,” yet the narrative appears to introduce one weapon, let it strike, and then repeat the manufacture formula for the second. Explanations include:

- formulaic poetic repetition;
- two variant accounts combined;
- each stanza restating the pair before focusing on one member;
- Kothar preparing the second after the first fails;
- damage or displacement in the manuscript.

No consensus turns the pair into more than two weapons.

#### Dating Ilimilku

Older scholarship placed the scribe in the mid-fourteenth century BCE. Later evidence has favored a late-thirteenth-century setting. The tablet’s institutional date remains broad, while the exact historical setting of composition is debated.

#### Literary unity

The *Baal Cycle* may combine formerly independent traditions: Baal versus Yamm, the palace story, and Baal versus Mot. If so, the weapons may originally have belonged to a shorter kingship combat poem. That reconstruction is plausible but cannot be proven from a lost exemplar.

### 10.4 State of the primary evidence

The sole explicit evidence for the names is fragmentary but legible. There are no duplicate manuscripts of this weapon scene presently known. Consequently:

- textual emendations cannot be checked against a second ancient copy;
- vocalizations remain reconstructed;
- material description cannot be recovered;
- the end of each weapon’s career is undocumented;
- new joins or multispectral imaging might refine readings, but cannot be assumed to reveal absent narrative content.
