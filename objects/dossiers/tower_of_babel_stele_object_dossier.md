# The "Tower of Babel stele": Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

The object treated here is the small, rounded-top, inscribed stone monument formerly catalogued in the Schøyen Collection as **MS 2063** and now held by the **Iraq Museum, Baghdad**. Assyriologists increasingly call it the **Tower of Babylon Stele** or **Babylon Stele**. “Tower of Babel stele” is a modern popular title derived from the scholarly hypothesis that Babylon’s ziggurat **Etemenanki**, which the monument depicts and names, helped inspire Genesis 11:1–9. The stele does not narrate the biblical confusion of languages, does not use the Hebrew word *Babel*, and does not represent the biblical builders.

The evidence is unusually stratified:

- **Securely observable:** two joining stone fragments; dimensions, sculpture, Akkadian cuneiform text, damage, and present museum custody.
- **Strong scholarly conclusion:** manufacture under Nebuchadnezzar II, 604–562 BCE; the iconography and inscription are Neo-Babylonian.
- **Probable but incompletely documented:** discovery near Esagil at Babylon in the early 1990s and illicit removal from Iraq.
- **Documented modern custody:** acquisition of the fragments by Martin Schøyen’s collection in 1995 and 1999; Norwegian seizure in 2021; return to Schøyen in 2022; unconditional transfer to Iraq in December 2023.
- **Rejected modern provenance story:** excavation in 1917 by Robert Koldewey’s expedition and division into three pieces among excavators.
- **Disputed:** authenticity was questioned by Stephanie Dalley; Andrew George, Olof Pedersén, Jamie Novotny, and Frauke Weiershäuser accept the stele as ancient.
- **Speculative:** ancient removal to Susa by Xerxes, deliberate ancient erasure by a conqueror, and any direct connection between this particular monument and the composition of Genesis.

No ancient accession record, excavation register, commission document, workshop name, quarry source, recorded price, or continuous custody chain survives.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity and names

The artifact is a **Neo-Babylonian commemorative or display stele**, not a foundation cylinder, boundary stone, or surviving piece of the tower itself.

Names encountered in scholarship and display literature include:

- **Tower of Babel Stele** — the Schøyen Collection’s modern English title.
- **Tower of Babylon Stele** — preferred in Olof Pedersén’s archaeological publication.
- **Babylon Stele** — abbreviated Assyriological designation.
- **Schøyen MS 2063** — its former private-collection number.
- **Nebuchadnezzar II no. 1 / Nbk. 1** — its position in the 2024 *Royal Inscriptions of the Neo-Babylonian Empire* edition.
- **IM —, formerly MS 2063** — the 2024 scholarly catalogue’s provisional Iraq Museum designation; no published permanent Iraq Museum accession number was located.

The monument’s own short epigraph names the building:

> **É.TEMEN.AN.KI, ziqqurrat Bābili**  
> “Etemenanki, the ziggurat of Babylon.”

**Etemenanki**, Sumerian **É-temen-an-ki**, conventionally means “House, Foundation of Heaven and Earth.” Akkadian *ziqqurratu* denotes a stepped temple tower; *Bābili* is “of Babylon.”

The longer inscription also names the Borsippa ziggurat **Eurmeiminanki**, written **É-ur₄-me-imin-an-ki** and interpreted as “House Which Gathers the Seven Divine Powers of Heaven and Netherworld.” Its exact Sumerian analysis is conventionally translated rather than beyond dispute. The ORACC temple-name corpus records the spellings and identifies the structure as Nabû’s ziggurat at Borsippa. [ORACC temple-name entry](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/eisl/cbd/qpn-x-temple/x000001280.html)

“Babel” is the Hebrew biblical name **בָּבֶל, Bāḇel**. Genesis 11 makes it the occasion for a Hebrew wordplay with *bālal*, “to mix/confuse.” That biblical etymology is theological wordplay, not the historical Akkadian derivation of Babylon’s name.

### Material, form, and dimensions

The stele is:

- **47 cm high**
- **25.5 cm wide** in Pedersén’s measurement; the Schøyen catalogue rounded this to 25 cm
- **11 cm thick**
- composed of **two joining fragments**
- carved from a **black, fine-grained sedimentary stone**

It was long described loosely as basalt. After visual geological assessment, Nadhir Al-Ansari proposed **dark siltstone**, but this assessment was based on photographs and description rather than hands-on petrological examination. “Black stone” or “dark sedimentary rock” is therefore the safest description.

No published weight, quarry identification, petrographic thin-section study, elemental analysis, or isotopic sourcing was located.

The complete original outline appears recoverable despite extensive surface losses. The stele has a rounded or arched top and a preserved base sufficient to establish its upright orientation. Roughly twenty percent of the base remains. Much of the rear portion—especially the back of the lower two-thirds or the rear of the third stone layer—is lost.

Pedersén’s close inspection found that the stone consists essentially of three fine-grained dark layers, sometimes divided by thin white mineral material. The lamination has encouraged flaking and exfoliation. More flakes may detach without conservation. A near-vertical white seam runs through the monument between the king and ziggurat and continues into the inscription.

### Relief and architectural drawings

The upper portion carries low relief and incised architectural plans:

1. **At left:** a frontal elevation of a stepped ziggurat.
2. **Above it:** a plan of the sanctuary occupying the summit.
3. **On the left shoulder:** a second, similar but non-identical architectural plan.
4. **At right:** a much larger standing royal figure, identified from the inscription and context as Nebuchadnezzar II.

The elevation shows:

- a broad lowest terrace;
- a monumental frontal staircase arrangement;
- five further receding terraces;
- the summit sanctuary, producing the conventional total of seven levels when the temple-bearing summit is counted;
- different degrees of relief, with the lowest platform projecting more strongly than most upper levels.

The summit plan has two entrances and rooms arranged around a central court. Pedersén calculated that it is drawn at roughly 1.8:1 relative to the elevation below, rather than at the same scale. The southern entrance corresponds to the depicted staircase façade.

Andrew George proposed that the second shoulder plan might represent **Eurmeiminanki at Borsippa**, the other ziggurat discussed in the inscription. Pedersén regards this as preferable to treating it as a slightly different duplicate of Etemenanki, but the identification remains an inference.

The king wears a tall conical royal cap. He holds a staff in his left hand and extends his right hand toward the building. The object in that hand has been described as a rolled plan, a measuring device, or a foundation peg/nail. The stele itself does not label it. Pedersén’s 2023 examination determined that the staff is straight; an apparent bend in one earlier photograph and drawing resulted from misplaced loose fragments.

The upper-right corner may have been cut or sawn away. Lost divine symbols—possibly the crescent, solar disk, and star familiar from Mesopotamian monuments—have been conjectured, but nothing surviving establishes their presence. An alternative raised by Pedersén is that the ziggurat itself occupies the place of the divine referent.

The relief is highly important iconographically: if ancient, it is the only known contemporary elevation drawing of Etemenanki and the only surviving representation of Nebuchadnezzar II found in Mesopotamia. Other probable representations of the king are weathered rock reliefs in Lebanon. [Pedersén 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/iraq/article/tower-of-babylon-stele-found-in-babylon/A712F186B2FA1965854911081620467B)

### Inscription

The text is in **Neo-Babylonian monumental cuneiform**, in the **Akkadian language**, with Sumerian ceremonial building names. The Schøyen catalogue describes:

- a three-line epigraph near the ziggurat;
- three lower columns;
- approximately twenty-four lines per lower column.

Much is abraded or lost. The lower text is a variant of other royal building inscriptions of Nebuchadnezzar II concerning Etemenanki at Babylon and Eurmeiminanki at Borsippa.

The collection’s composite English translation reads in relevant part:

> “Etemenanki, the ziggurat of Babylon.”  
> “Nebuchadnezzar, king of Babylon, am I. In order to complete Etemenanki and Eurmeiminanki I mobilized all countries everywhere … from the Upper Sea to the Lower Sea, the distant nations, the teeming people of the world, kings of remote mountains and far-flung islands. The base I filled in to make a high terrace. I built their structures with bitumen and baked brick throughout. I completed it, raising its top to heaven, making it gleam bright as the sun.”

This is a readable composite, not a diplomatic line-by-line rendering of every preserved wedge. The authoritative editions are Andrew George’s 2011 study and Novotny and Weiershäuser’s 2024 RINBE edition. [George publication record](https://soas-repository.worktribe.com/output/451874) [RINBE project](https://www.en.ag.geschichte.uni-muenchen.de/research/rinbe/index.html)

The inscription’s claims are royal rhetoric. “All countries,” “Upper Sea to Lower Sea,” and “top to heaven” express universal kingship and monumental magnitude; they are not literal labor registers or measured construction reports.

### Damage, loss, and intervention

Documented condition includes:

- separation ancient or pre-acquisition into two main surviving pieces;
- missing surface flakes and much of the rear stone mass;
- heavy abrasion of the lower inscription;
- loss of the king’s upper face, parts of his left arm, shoes, staff, and other relief details;
- loss of the highest central part of the staircase;
- possible cutting or sawing at the upper-right corner;
- soil retained in cuneiform incisions;
- no complete professional cleaning as of Pedersén’s October 2023 inspection.

The lower inscription has areas reduced below the surrounding surface, leaving chiefly the deeper heads of wedges. George originally interpreted this as careful deliberate erasure, perhaps preparatory to replacing Nebuchadnezzar’s text. Pedersén’s examination left two possibilities open: **intentional ancient erasure** or **abrasion caused by later reuse**. No replacement inscription was added.

There is no evidence that either private owner or museum reconstructed missing inscriptional surfaces. Earlier drawings did, however, rearrange loose fragments incorrectly in the area of the royal staff.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Ancient manufacture

The inscription names Nebuchadnezzar II, king of Babylon, and commemorates his building of Etemenanki and Eurmeiminanki. Its creation therefore belongs within his reign, conventionally **604–562 BCE**. The exact year is unknown.

The monument was probably commissioned by the royal or temple administration for display or deposition in Babylon. No surviving text states who ordered it, which workshop cut it, where it originally stood, how much it cost, or from what quarry its stone came.

The physical sequence probably comprised:

1. quarrying and dressing imported stone—stone was comparatively scarce in alluvial Babylonia;
2. shaping a rounded-top slab;
3. laying out the pictorial and inscriptional registers;
4. cutting the relief at different depths;
5. incising the Akkadian royal text and building labels;
6. installing the stele in or near the Esagil–Etemenanki sacred complex.

The last step is inferred, not documented. Pedersén notes that the German excavations registered hundreds of inscribed stones at Babylon, including at least 366 objects from Nebuchadnezzar’s reign and about forty stelae or stele fragments. The present monument thus has a local archaeological class even though its precise installation context is lost.

### Original purpose

Its combined portrait, building elevation, plan, and inscription imply a commemorative function: it credited the king with completing the two great ziggurats and presented his construction as service to **Marduk** at Babylon and **Nabû** at Borsippa. Whether it was a public display monument, a temple treasury object, or a foundation/depositional object remains unresolved.

### Earliest physical attestation

Because the monument lacks an excavation number and its alleged discovery was not recorded by archaeologists, its first securely documentable modern existence is its entry into the Schøyen Collection:

- upper fragment reportedly acquired in **October 1995**;
- lower fragment reportedly acquired in **May 1999**.

The top fragment was publicly exhibited in Oslo in 1997, establishing physical existence by that date. The reunited object subsequently appeared on the Schøyen website and was formally published by Andrew George in 2011. [Schøyen catalogue](https://www.schoyencollection.com/history-collection-introduction/babylonian-history-collection/tower-babel-stele-ms-2063)

### Claimed archaeological origin

Pedersén published a newly recovered oral account in 2024:

- two fishermen from **Jumjuma**, south of ancient Babylon, found the two fragments in the early 1990s;
- they lay in the large open trench extending north from Walter Andrae’s 1900 excavation pit at Esagil on Tell Amran;
- the approximate location is about twenty metres north of Esagil, UTM zone 38N **445705E, 3599790N**;
- the pieces may have fallen from the trench side;
- the find level was later than Nebuchadnezzar, possibly Parthian;
- persons connected with Saddam Hussein’s security apparatus allegedly removed them from Iraq, declaring at the border that the stones were modern imitations.

This account is detailed and archaeologically plausible, but it is **retrospective oral testimony**, reported to Pedersén gradually between 2019 and 2022. There is no contemporary discovery photograph, field notebook, official Iraqi registration, customs record, or named finder in the published article. Its evidentiary status is therefore **probable reconstruction**, not controlled excavation provenance.

### Earlier false origin story

The Schøyen website originally claimed that Robert Koldewey’s German expedition found the stele in 1917 and that three fragments were divided among excavators and carried to different countries. Pedersén showed that this story is impossible: the expedition’s records and the extensive subsequent scholarship on Etemenanki know nothing of the monument. He judged the story probably invented by antiquities dealers. It must not be repeated as provenance. [Pedersén 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/iraq/article/tower-of-babylon-stele-found-in-babylon/A712F186B2FA1965854911081620467B)

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Sixth century BCE: royal and temple sphere

**604–562 BCE — probable manufacture in Babylon.**  
Nebuchadnezzar II is the speaking royal subject of the text. The object presumably entered the institutional custody of Esagil, Etemenanki, or the Babylonian crown. This is inference from content and likely find area; no ancient inventory names the stele.

### Later antiquity: displacement and damage

The stele’s later ancient history is unrecorded. Its deposition in a later, possibly Parthian, level suggests displacement long after Nebuchadnezzar’s reign.

Pedersén compares it with a Parthian-period concentration of more than 220 older precious objects and several *kudurru* monuments found in the same general part of Tell Amran. One plausible reconstruction is that older objects once kept at Esagil were collected, moved, discarded, or redeposited during the Parthian period.

George earlier proposed a different possibility. Because part of Nebuchadnezzar’s inscription appeared erased and because an Etemenanki cylinder was found at Susa, he suggested the stele might have been carried to Susa as booty, perhaps during Xerxes’ intervention at Babylon, and later left Iran through the antiquities market. He explicitly presented this as conjecture. The recovered Babylon find story now makes the Susa theory unnecessary, though not logically impossible if the monument traveled to Susa and back. There is no positive evidence for that itinerary.

The time and agency of the break into two fragments are unknown. Claims that it was originally broken into three excavator-owned pieces derive from the discredited 1917 provenance story.

### Early 1990s: reported discovery and removal

**Early 1990s — Tell Amran, Babylon.**  
Two local fishermen reportedly found both principal fragments in the old German trench near Esagil.

**Soon afterward — alleged illicit export.**  
Pedersén’s informants said people associated with Saddam’s security establishment transported the fragments out of Iraq while describing them as modern imitations. If accurate, this was smuggling of Iraqi archaeological property. The persons, route, border post, date, intermediaries, and consideration paid remain unpublished or unknown.

**Early-to-mid-1990s — international antiquities market.**  
The pieces entered the market separately. No complete, public chain from the finders to Schøyen has been produced. Dealer identities and sale prices were not found in the consulted sources.

### 1995–2021: Schøyen Collection

**October 1995 — acquisition of first fragment.**  
The Schøyen Collection reportedly acquired the upper fragment. The mechanism was presumably purchase, but the seller and price are unpublished.

**27 February–26 April 1997 — University Library, Oslo.**  
The rounded upper fragment was exhibited for the Bibliophile Society of Norway’s seventy-fifth anniversary.

**29 July–7 August 1998 — Faculty of Law Library, University of Oslo.**  
The upper fragment was displayed during the sixteenth congress of the International Organization for the Study of the Old Testament.

**May 1999 — acquisition of second fragment.**  
The collection reportedly obtained the lower fragment, allowing the principal pieces to be reunited.

**30 January–15 September 2003 — Kon-Tiki Museum, Oslo.**  
The top fragment was shown in the “Tigris” twenty-fifth-anniversary exhibition.

**2000s–2011 — study, photography, and online publication.**  
The object was made available to Andrew George. Its images circulated in biblical and ancient-history publications. George supplied the first full scholarly publication in 2011.

The collection described its location as Oslo and London at different times, but published sources do not give dated transport records between those cities. The safely documented custodian was Martin Schøyen’s private collection near Oslo.

### 2021 seizure and 2022 return

**24 August/3 September 2021 — Norwegian impoundment.**  
At Iraq’s request, Norway’s Ministry of Culture initiated action under section 23c of the Norwegian Cultural Heritage Act. Økokrim, the economic and environmental crime authority, seized the stele with numerous other cuneiform objects from Schøyen’s residence.

The Museum of Cultural History at the University of Oslo examined the impounded material and recommended return of many objects to Iraq, reasoning that Schøyen had not documented lawful export and that the balance of evidence indicated recent looting and illicit trade.

Schøyen disputed the allegations, maintained that the objects had been legally acquired, and challenged the process. [Schøyen’s 2021 statement](https://www.schoyencollection.com/news-items/response-to-unwarranted-iraqi-claims)

**September 2022 — evidence held insufficient for continued seizure.**  
Norwegian authorities returned the seized cuneiform objects, including MS 2063, to Schøyen. Pedersén summarizes the outcome as a Norwegian court finding that Iraq had not established the objects’ origin sufficiently. This was not an archaeological finding that the 1917 story was true, nor a positive demonstration of lawful ancient export; it was a legal failure of proof under the applicable Norwegian procedure. Schøyen characterized the return as vindication and the Iraqi allegations as unevidenced. [Schøyen’s 2022 statement](https://www.schoyencollection.com/news-items/seized-tablets-returned)

### 2023 transfer to Iraq

**September–October 2023 — renewed physical study.**  
Pedersén examined the original twice, once with Jamie Novotny and once with Rocío Da Riva. Three-dimensional photogrammetry documented its topography and condition.

**14 December 2023 — unconditional gift/return.**  
After receiving Pedersén’s reconstructed Babylon find history, Martin Schøyen transferred both fragments without conditions to representatives of the Republic of Iraq at the Iraqi Embassy in Oslo. Schøyen’s announcement framed the act as a gift to the Iraqi people; the RINBE catalogue calls it a formal return after the modern smuggling history became apparent. Both descriptions refer to the same transfer but emphasize different legal and moral characterizations. [Transfer announcement](https://www.schoyencollection.com/news-items/tower-of-babel-stele-gifted-to-the-people-of-iraq)

**Late 2023–2024 — Iraq Museum, Baghdad.**  
The stele entered the Iraq Museum. Pedersén anticipated professional conservation and display.

**By May 2025 — public museum display.**  
An Iraq Museum staff member reported selecting the stele as the first object for a new bilingual interpretive and digital-display initiative. Its installation included Arabic and English labels, a QR code linking to photographs, audio, documentary material, a full inscription translation, architectural analysis, and a three-dimensional reconstruction. This confirms museum display after the 2023 transfer. [British Museum International Training Programme report](https://www.bmitpglobalnetwork.org/2025/05/30/a-new-exhibition-at-the-iraq-museum-inspired-by-the-british-museums-international-training-programme/)

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### The question

Two issues must be separated:

1. **Is the stone and its inscription genuinely Neo-Babylonian?**
2. **Even if genuine, is “Tower of Babel stele” an accurate identity?**

The first concerns forgery. The second concerns interpretation: the stele undoubtedly labels Etemenanki, but identifying Etemenanki with the literary inspiration for Genesis’ tower is a historical hypothesis.

### Reasons doubt arose

Stephanie Dalley questioned the stele’s authenticity in two *Bibliotheca Orientalis* articles. The complete detailed arguments are not freely reproduced in the sources consulted, but the dispute centered on features of its provenance, script, wording, and iconography. General grounds for suspicion included:

- its appearance on the market without controlled excavation records;
- the false and changing 1917 discovery story;
- the object’s exceptional combination of royal portrait, named ziggurat, elevation, and plan;
- elements critics regarded as unusual or potentially dependent on modern reconstructions;
- limited early access to the original, leaving some scholars to judge photographs.

The object’s illicit-market context is a serious provenance defect but does not, by itself, prove forgery.

### Case for authenticity

Andrew George’s 2024 response tested Dalley’s objections against epigraphic and iconographic evidence and concluded that the monument is genuine. [George, “On the Authenticity…”](https://poj.peeters-leuven.be/content.php?id=3293875&journal_code=BIOR&url=article)

The affirmative case includes:

- the text is an internally coherent variant of Nebuchadnezzar II’s royal building inscriptions rather than a simple copy of one published text;
- its language, titulary, sign forms, and literary structure belong within the Neo-Babylonian royal corpus;
- the architectural relief and royal image fit Mesopotamian monumental conventions while preserving individual features not simply borrowed from a familiar modern picture;
- the surviving damage and stone lamination are physically integrated with the carved surfaces;
- dirt remains in the wedges and damaged pictorial areas;
- Pedersén’s close examination, with Novotny and Da Riva, identified errors in earlier photographs and drawings rather than evidence of modern carving;
- the recovered oral find history places both fragments in a credible archaeological context close to Esagil;
- the 2024 RINBE corpus accepts and edits it as Nebuchadnezzar II’s inscription no. 1.

Pedersén’s evidence strengthens authenticity but is not equivalent to a controlled excavation. Oral provenance can be mistaken or manipulated. Nor has a published laboratory program independently dated the carving.

### Scientific testing

No published radiocarbon test is applicable because the object is stone. No thermoluminescence test, microscopic tool-mark study, pigment analysis, residue dating, petrographic provenance analysis, surface-patina dating, or elemental study was located.

The principal technical work documented is:

- geological assessment from photographs by Nadhir Al-Ansari, suggesting siltstone;
- direct macroscopic study by Pedersén, Novotny, and Da Riva in 2023;
- high-resolution **3D photogrammetry** by Pedersén in October 2023.

The 3D model is publicly archived through Zenodo. [Zenodo record](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.10374395)

### Identity as the “Tower of Babel”

The inscription securely identifies the pictured structure as **Etemenanki, the ziggurat of Babylon**. It does not call it the Tower of Babel.

Many historians regard Etemenanki as the most plausible architectural and cultural background for Genesis 11 because:

- it was an enormous temple tower;
- it stood in Babylon, Hebrew Babel;
- its royal texts use the motif of raising its top toward heaven;
- Babylon was an imperial center containing populations and labor forces speaking many languages;
- Judean deportees lived in Babylonia during and after Nebuchadnezzar’s rebuilding program.

None of these points proves that the author of Genesis saw this stele or that one particular building episode generated the story. Andrew George has cautioned that the stele gives an idealized royal representation, not a photograph or proof that the ziggurat attained every planned dimension. [National Geographic discussion](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/tower-of-babel-genesis-bible-archaeology)

The precise identity statement should therefore be:

> The object is a Neo-Babylonian stele depicting and naming Etemenanki. It is popularly called the “Tower of Babel stele” because Etemenanki is widely considered a principal historical referent behind the biblical tradition. It is not archaeological proof that Genesis 11 records a literal event.

### Present balance of scholarship

The strongest recent specialist publications accept the artifact as genuine. Authenticity is not laboratory-proven, and its discovery history remains retrospectively reconstructed, but the available epigraphic, iconographic, physical, and contextual evidence favors a Neo-Babylonian origin. No identified modern forger or fabrication workshop has been demonstrated.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

No surviving ritual text prescribes ceremonies performed with this particular stele. No procession, oath, sacrifice, anointing, healing, curse, or access taboo involving the object is recorded.

Its probable ancient functions were:

### Royal commemoration

The stele made Nebuchadnezzar’s reconstruction of two sacred towers visible and permanent. The king’s enlarged figure, staff, extended hand, and first-person inscription present him as architect, organizer, conqueror, and pious servant of the gods.

### Temple dedication and legitimation

Building temples was a central duty of Mesopotamian kingship. By stating that Marduk placed the peoples of the world under Nebuchadnezzar’s authority and that those peoples labored on the ziggurats, the text converts imperial domination into divine service.

### Architectural record

The elevation and plans communicate ideal architectural form. Whether they are working drawings, retrospective plans, symbolically simplified images, or a mixture remains debated. Their small scale and monumental setting argue against use as ordinary construction-site blueprints.

### Sacred cosmology

The name Etemenanki—“House, Foundation of Heaven and Earth”—defines the tower as a vertical cosmic junction. The phrase about raising its top toward heaven is conventional royal language but also suits the ziggurat’s theological role.

### Modern symbolic function

Since publication, the stele has operated as:

- an emblem of Babylonian architectural achievement;
- a visual bridge between cuneiform culture and Genesis;
- evidence in popular arguments for or against a historical core behind the Tower of Babel story;
- an example in debates about private collecting, illicit excavation, academic publication of unprovenanced artifacts, seizure, and restitution;
- after 2023, an Iraqi national heritage object returned from a European private collection.

Schøyen’s claim that multilingual foreign labor generated the biblical “confusion of tongues” is a modern interpretive proposal. The inscription says that subjects from many lands were mobilized; it does not say that communication failed or that languages originated at the building site.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The biblical Babel association

**Early attested literary tradition:** Genesis 11:1–9 describes humanity as sharing one language, settling in Shinar, making baked bricks with bitumen, and building a city and tower “with its top in the heavens.” YHWH confuses the language and scatters the builders.

The stele’s inscription shares several motifs:

- Babylonian setting;
- tower reaching toward heaven;
- baked brick and bitumen;
- large-scale mobilization.

But it differs fundamentally:

- Nebuchadnezzar and Marduk replace Genesis’ anonymous human builders and YHWH;
- the royal text celebrates completion rather than divine interruption;
- it contains no language miracle;
- it also concerns Borsippa;
- it dates to the first millennium BCE, whereas Genesis presents its episode in primeval time.

The stele is therefore comparative evidence for the cultural environment of the biblical narrative, not a direct witness to the Genesis event.

### “The only contemporary picture of the Tower”

This modern formulation is partly factual and partly interpretive. It is apparently the only surviving Neo-Babylonian depiction explicitly labeled Etemenanki. Calling Etemenanki “the Tower of Babel” imports the modern historical identification. Calling the picture “contemporary” means contemporary with Nebuchadnezzar’s rebuilding, not with the primeval chronology of Genesis.

### The 1917 excavation legend

**Modern invention or dealer fiction:** Koldewey’s expedition allegedly found the monument in a hidden chamber in 1917, after which archaeologists divided its three pieces.

This story shaped internet descriptions and popular publications for years. It is contradicted by German excavation records and by the silence of specialists who reconstructed Etemenanki from those excavations. Pedersén judged it probably invented by dealers to supply a respectable pre-modern-market provenance.

### Xerxes and the Susa booty story

**Scholarly conjecture, 2011:** George considered whether a conquering ruler, perhaps Xerxes, erased the central text and removed the monument to a treasure house at Susa. The precedent was the Elamite removal of Mesopotamian monuments such as Hammurabi’s law stele, and an Etemenanki cylinder had indeed been found at Susa.

No inscription, excavation record, or ancient inventory ties MS 2063 to Xerxes or Susa. The reported Babylon discovery makes a direct Babylon-to-market route substantially more economical.

### Multilingual workforce as origin of Genesis

**Modern Schøyen interpretation:** Nebuchadnezzar’s mobilization of labor from across the empire produced practical linguistic confusion witnessed by Judean exiles, who later theologized it as Genesis 11.

The text supports imperial labor mobilization. It does not attest worksite confusion, Judean observation of the project, or direct composition of Genesis from this episode. The interpretation is possible cultural reconstruction, not documented history.

### Destruction, burial, and rediscovery

Schøyen’s 2023 transfer announcement described the stele as buried “next to the Tower itself in the Iraqi sands for 2,500 years.” That formulation compresses several uncertainties:

- the find level appears later than Nebuchadnezzar;
- the stone may have been moved from its original installation;
- its burial was not documented when it occurred;
- the modern find story is retrospective.

The physical damage does show a long post-use history, but not uninterrupted burial in one place.

## 8. Current Status and Location

As of the latest located institutional report, the stele is in the **Iraq Museum, Baghdad**, under the authority of the Republic of Iraq and its State Board of Antiquities and Heritage.

By May 2025 it had been made a centerpiece of a new interpretive display using:

- bilingual Arabic-English labeling;
- QR-linked extended material;
- photographs and audio;
- documentary video;
- a fuller translation of the inscription;
- analysis of the architectural drawings;
- a digital reconstruction of the tower.

A permanent Iraq Museum accession number has not been identified in public catalogues. The current RINBE designation is effectively **IM —, formerly MS 2063**.

Its current material condition remains fragile. Conservation needs identified in 2023 included professional cleaning, stabilization of exfoliating layers, and protection of the weak rear and lower surfaces. The consulted sources do not publish a completed conservation report.

Ownership and physical custody are no longer actively disputed: Schøyen transferred the monument without conditions. The 2021–2022 Norwegian proceeding ended before that transfer and did not compel restitution. The word **return** accurately describes restoration to the country of probable origin; **gift** accurately describes the voluntary legal mechanism asserted by the transferor.

The open-access three-dimensional record provides a digital surrogate for study and condition comparison. [3D archive](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.10374395)

## 9. Modern Reception

### Exhibitions and publication

The upper fragment’s 1997, 1998, and 2003 Oslo exhibitions introduced the monument before its full scholarly edition. After the fragments were reunited, photographs and Andrew George’s reconstruction became widely reproduced in Bible commentaries, histories of lost cities, architectural studies, museum media, documentaries, and internet articles.

George’s 2011 publication transformed it from an attractive market object into an edited royal inscription. Its conventional line drawing also influenced later reconstructions of Etemenanki.

### Digital and physical replicas

Pedersén produced open-access photogrammetry in 2023, enabling scholarly inspection, museum visualization, and three-dimensional replication. The Iraq Museum subsequently used the monument as the basis for a digital model of the tower.

Commercial resin reproductions are sold under the title “Tower of Babel Stele.” These are modern decorative replicas without independent historical identity. [Example of commercial reception](https://www.etsy.com/listing/614692370/tower-of-babel-stele-recreation)

### Documentary and popular imagery

The stele’s image has become a standard illustration for:

- Nebuchadnezzar II;
- Babylonian ziggurats;
- archaeological approaches to Genesis;
- the supposed physical appearance of the Tower of Babel.

This has often produced overstatement. Popular captions may say the stele was “found in 1917,” proves the biblical tower existed, or provides an exact architectural blueprint. The first is false, the second exceeds the evidence, and the third overlooks the image’s idealizing and schematic character.

### Religious reception

Some apologetic writers present the monument as confirmation of Genesis because it links a great Babylonian tower with brick, bitumen, universal labor, and sky-reaching rhetoric. Other writers regard Genesis as an Israelite polemic reversing Babylonian royal ideology: what Nebuchadnezzar depicts as divinely sanctioned imperial unity becomes in Genesis a project checked by YHWH. Both are interpretations of the relationship; the artifact itself contains no biblical citation.

### Heritage politics

The stele became part of a broader dispute over the Schøyen Collection’s Iraqi cuneiform holdings. Its seizure, court-ordered return to the collector, subsequent voluntary transfer, and new Baghdad display altered its public meaning:

> first a royal Babylonian monument; later an undocumented-market antiquity; then a celebrated private-collection “biblical” object; finally a returned Iraqi museum object and case study in the recovery of cultural heritage.

The 2025 Iraq Museum display deliberately recontextualized it through Iraqi custodianship, Arabic interpretation, and digital public access.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Before the stele

Modern reconstructions of Etemenanki formerly depended primarily on:

- German excavations at Babylon;
- Nebuchadnezzar’s and Nabopolassar’s other royal inscriptions;
- the Hellenistic “Esagila tablet,” Louvre AO 6555;
- Herodotus and other classical traditions.

The Hellenistic tablet supplies ideal dimensions but appears to omit a level or otherwise conflict with the stele’s proportions. Pedersén cautions against treating either source as a perfectly accurate survey.

### Andrew George, 2005–2006 and 2011

George’s earlier study of Babel archaeology and texts established the broader context. His 2011 chapter was the first full publication of MS 2063, with photographs, hand copies, transliteration, translation, comparison with parallel Nebuchadnezzar inscriptions, architectural analysis, and the tentative Susa hypothesis. [George 2011 record and files](https://soas-repository.worktribe.com/output/451874)

### Provenance criticism

The revelation that the reported 1917 find was untenable undermined confidence. Changes to the Schøyen website’s provenance language compounded the problem. Scholars of antiquities trafficking also criticized publication practices surrounding unprovenanced Iraqi objects and emphasized that scholarly validation can increase market value and obscure illicit histories.

The Norwegian Museum of Cultural History’s 2021–2022 assessment treated lack of export documentation and indications of recent looting as grounds for recommended return. The legal process did not ultimately sustain seizure.

### Dalley–George authenticity debate

Stephanie Dalley raised explicit doubts about authenticity. Andrew George’s 2024 article answered them individually and concluded that the monument is ancient. His conclusion rests chiefly on expert epigraphy and iconography rather than laboratory dating.

This exchange is important because it distinguishes two questions often conflated in public discussion:

- an object can be genuine but illegally excavated;
- a false provenance does not necessarily mean the object itself is false.

### Pedersén 2024

Pedersén changed the discussion by publishing:

- the reported early-1990s Babylon discovery;
- a precise approximate findspot;
- its relationship to the German trench and later archaeological levels;
- direct 2023 condition observations;
- corrections to earlier drawings;
- photogrammetric documentation;
- the 2023 transfer to Iraq.

His reconstruction is presently the fullest provenance account, but it depends on late oral reports rather than excavation documentation.

### Novotny and Weiershäuser 2024

The RINBE edition incorporated the monument into the authoritative corpus of Neo-Babylonian royal inscriptions as Nebuchadnezzar II no. 1. Its catalogue recognizes Babylon as provenance, records the former Schøyen number, states that the fragments were discovered in the 1990s and smuggled from Iraq, and records the December 2023 return. [Open-access RINBE front matter](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/118421/1/RINBE1-1_OA.pdf)

### Outstanding research questions

1. Can the finders or transporters be identified and their testimony independently recorded?
2. Do dealer, banking, shipping, customs, or collection files document the 1995 and 1999 transactions?
3. What was the exact stratigraphic origin within the Amran trench?
4. Where did the stone originate geologically?
5. Are the worn lower surfaces deliberate erasure or use-wear?
6. Was the second plan intended to depict Borsippa?
7. What occupied the missing upper-right corner?
8. Can microscopic tool-mark, patina, or residue analysis further test authenticity?
9. Has the Iraq Museum assigned a permanent accession number and completed conservation?
10. Was the object originally displayed in Esagil, deposited at Etemenanki, or transferred there in antiquity?

- Iraq Museum staff report on the 2025 exhibition.

### Principal archaeological and authenticity studies

- Olof Pedersén, “The Tower of Babylon Stele Found in Babylon,” *Iraq* 85, published online 20 February 2024, pp. 179–191. The central study of reported find circumstances, archaeological setting, physical inspection, provenance correction, and transfer.
- Andrew George, “On the Authenticity of the Tower of Babel Stele,” *Bibliotheca Orientalis* 81.1–2, 2024, pp. 7–19. Direct response to Stephanie Dalley and current affirmative authenticity argument.

- RINBE/ORACC data for Neo-Babylonian royal inscriptions and ceremonial temple names.

### Source criticism

The Schøyen catalogue remains valuable for its exhibit history and a convenient composite translation, but several claims require correction:

- its older 1917 discovery provenance was false;
- its “three fragments divided among excavators” story is unsupported;
- the Xerxes/Susa itinerary was always conjectural;
- its direct derivation of Genesis’ language story from Nebuchadnezzar’s workforce is speculative;
- an apparent statement that “Cyrus ca. 483 BC” invaded Babylon is an obvious name/date error, presumably intending Xerxes;
- it sometimes treats the biblical identification as established rather than interpretive.

Conversely, the Norwegian return of the object to Schøyen in 2022 did not authenticate the old provenance. It established only that the Iraqi claim did not satisfy the relevant legal standard for continued Norwegian seizure.

The dossier’s most defensible conclusion is that the stele is a genuine Neo-Babylonian royal monument with a severely defective modern provenance, probably found and illicitly exported from Babylon in the early 1990s, acquired in two stages by the Schøyen Collection, and voluntarily returned to Iraq in 2023. Its connection with the “Tower of Babel” is historically important but secondary: the object names and depicts Etemenanki, while “Tower of Babel” is the modern interpretive title imposed upon it.
