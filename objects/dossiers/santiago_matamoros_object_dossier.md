# Santiago Matamoros (cult image): Object Dossier

**Object tracked:** the large polychromed-wood processional group traditionally attributed to José Gambino, commissioned for or associated with the Compostela guild of jet workers (*gremio de los azabacheros / gremio dos acibecheiros*), long displayed in the north transept of Santiago de Compostela Cathedral, and since January 2026 exhibited in the Museo Diocesano de Santiago at San Martiño Pinario.

**Research date:** 6 September 2026.

**Evidentiary labels used below**

- **Documented:** supported by contemporary records, institutional catalogues, or direct modern reporting.
- **Scholarly conclusion:** interpretation accepted or argued in specialist research.
- **Institutional attribution/tradition:** identification maintained by the cathedral, museum, or church authorities without a published contract or conclusive technical analysis.
- **Reported:** stated in journalism or secondary literature but not independently documented in an accessible primary record.
- **Legend/tradition:** religious, literary, or national tradition rather than an established historical event.
- **Unresolved:** the accessible evidence is contradictory or incomplete.

---

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity

The object is an eighteenth-century Galician religious processional group representing James the Greater as an armed, mounted apparition overcoming Muslim combatants. In Spanish it is normally called:

- **Santiago Matamoros** — literally “James the Moor-killer.”
- **Santiago Caballero** — “James the Knight.”
- **Santiago ecuestre** — “Equestrian James.”
- **Paso procesional de Santiago Matamoros** — “Processional group of Santiago Matamoros.”

In Galician:

- **Santiago Matamouros**.
- **Santiago Cabaleiro**.
- **Paso procesional do gremio de azabacheiros/acibecheiros**.

The saint’s name derives from Latin *Sanctus Iacobus*, contracted in medieval Iberian usage through forms such as *Sant Iago* into *Santiago*. *Matamoros* is a Spanish compound of *matar*, “to kill,” and *moros*, historically “Moors,” a variable Christian Iberian designation for Muslims or inhabitants of Islamic territories. It is an iconographic and popular epithet, not a name found in the New Testament.

This dossier does **not** track:

1. the medieval stone “Clavijo tympanum” inside the cathedral;
2. the Santiago Matamoros figures in the cathedral baldachin or exterior architecture;
3. the marble Battle of Clavijo relief and crowning equestrian statue on the Pazo de Raxoi;
4. the eighteenth-century Matamoros oil painting now catalogued by the Museo Catedral;
5. other Spanish, Portuguese, American, or Philippine images with the same title.

Confusion among these objects is frequent. In particular, the Pazo de Raxoi sculpture is generally assigned to José Ferreiro, following a design associated with Gregorio Ferro and work begun jointly with Gambino; it is not the wooden processional group examined here.

### Physical composition

**Documented/institutionally published characteristics:**

- Type: free-standing, multi-figure processional sculpture or *paso*.
- Principal material: carved and polychromed wood.
- Date: described variously as the “middle years” or second half of the eighteenth century; the 1999 exhibition catalogue proposed approximately **1770?**
- Maker: attributed to or identified as José Gambino.
- Function: processional religious image, later a fixed devotional display and now a museum exhibit.
- Composition:
  - James mounted on a white horse;
  - the horse rears or rises energetically on its hindquarters;
  - James raises a sword in attack;
  - he wears a long tunic, mantle, pilgrim’s cape or *esclavina*, and broad-brimmed pilgrim’s hat;
  - scallop shells decorate the pilgrim attire;
  - crossed miniature staffs or *bordoncillos* appear on the hat;
  - beneath the horse are three complete fallen Muslim figures and the severed head of a fourth, according to direct press descriptions from 2009;
  - the defeated figures wear clothing and turbans conventionally used in early-modern European art to signify Muslims.

The rider is therefore a deliberate fusion of three identities: apostle, Compostela pilgrim, and mounted warrior. The sculpture does not depict an archaeologically plausible ninth-century soldier. Its clothing is the accumulated costume of the later cult.

The most precise published account of the lower group reports “three complete Saracens and the severed head of a fourth.” Later commentary sometimes simplifies this to “heads,” “several Moors,” or “four Muslims.” Those shortened descriptions should not be treated as separate physical inventories.

### Colour and construction

The horse is white, the colour prescribed by the Clavijo literary tradition. Published descriptions and photographs identify a red mantle and richly polychromed garments. The figures are arranged as a theatrical diagonal: the white horse rises above the compressed, violently contorted defeated bodies while the saint’s sword continues the upward movement.

The exact wood species, number and disposition of blocks, joinery, hollowing, ground layers, pigments, gilding, varnishes, metal fittings, total weight, and overall dimensions have not been found in an accessible conservation or catalogue record. No inscription, signature, maker’s mark, or dated commissioning text has been published in the sources reviewed.

This absence matters. The conventional attribution rests principally on style, historical association, and repeated catalogue identification—not, in the accessible evidence, on a signature or surviving contract.

### Condition, alterations, and accessories

No complete scientific condition report has been located. The known changes are functional and presentational:

- repeated carrying in processions exposed the group to movement, weather, handling, vibration, and candle smoke;
- cathedral tradition records that numerous candles blackened the white horse, eventually prompting replacement of open flames by electric lights;
- from approximately 2006 to 2020, large arrangements of fresh flowers were placed in front of the sculpture to conceal the defeated Muslim figures;
- the group was moved during the cathedral restoration campaign by 2020/early 2021;
- it was subsequently prepared for permanent museum display in the new Museo Diocesano, opened in January 2026.

There is no accessible published proof that the flowers were physically attached to the sculpture; reports describe them as a regularly renewed screen or jardinière placed before it. No published source establishes that the lower figures were carved away, painted over, or otherwise materially censored.

No record was found of radiography, dendrochronology, pigment analysis, X-ray fluorescence, CT scanning, wood identification, or a documented full conservation treatment of this particular group.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### The physical object

José Gambino was born at the Pazo do Faramello in 1719 and died in Santiago in 1775. He was a leading sculptor of late Baroque and Rococo Galicia and worked with his pupil and son-in-law José Ferreiro. His documented career establishes an appropriate chronological and stylistic setting for the group.

Modern authorities differ in precision:

- specialist cathedral scholarship by Ramón Yzquierdo Peiró calls it a polychromed wooden processional image carved by Gambino in the “middle years” of the eighteenth century for the jet-workers’ guild;
- María S. Ortega Romero catalogued it in 1999 as **José Gambino, 1770?**;
- other iconographic scholarship cautiously calls it “traditionally attributed” to Gambino and dates it to the middle of the century;
- a recent overview describes it as commissioned by the cathedral chapter in the second half of the century;
- other sources consistently say the commissioning body was the guild or confraternity of jet workers.

These are not identical claims. No accessible commission, payment, guild minute, or delivery receipt settles them. The safest reconstruction is:

> **Scholarly/institutional attribution:** made in Compostela in the middle or later eighteenth century, probably around 1770, in José Gambino’s artistic orbit and conventionally attributed to Gambino; commissioned for processional use by or in connection with the Compostela jet-workers’ confraternity. Whether the legal commissioner was the guild, a confraternity acting through it, or the cathedral chapter remains unresolved in the published evidence reviewed.

The claim that the object was “for the cathedral” is also ambiguous. It may describe intended ritual use, later placement, or present institutional association rather than original cathedral ownership.

### Material provenance and cost

The sculpture is wood, not jet. Its connection with the *azabacheros* is institutional and devotional, not a statement of raw material. No source reviewed identifies the tree species, timber source, workshop account, price, or polychromer.

The jet-working craft itself was established in Compostela by the later Middle Ages. Its artisans formed a guild and a confraternity under Saint Sebastian and operated near the cathedral’s north entrance, hence the name Acibechería/Azabachería. That history explains the guild’s proximity to cathedral ritual and its ability to sponsor a major processional image, but it is not evidence that jet was incorporated into this sculpture.

### Earliest secure attestation of this particular group

The accessible web sources do not expose its original eighteenth-century paperwork. The object is securely recognized in twentieth-century photographs, catalogues, exhibition planning, and later scholarship. Important early modern references include:

- **1929:** records for Galicia’s pavilion at the Ibero-American Exposition in Seville planned to include the “iconic Santiago Matamoros of José Gambino, from the Cathedral.” A provincial commission minute dated 29 May 1929 still reported that the contents, including the sculpture, had not arrived in Seville. The record therefore establishes intended transport and contemporary identification, but not conclusively that the original reached or was exhibited in Seville.
- **1930:** a copy was produced by the Compostela sculptor Liste, evidence that Gambino’s group was already treated as a canonical model.
- **1999:** it was catalogued in *Todos con Santiago. Patrimonio eclesiástico* as a processional image by Gambino, approximately 1770.

A complete search of the Archivo de la Catedral, guild books, confraternity accounts, notarial protocols, and the 1929 exhibition shipping records would be required to push the physical object’s documentary origin back to manufacture.

### The legend embodied by the object

The object visualizes the supposed Battle of Clavijo, conventionally dated to 844 and assigned to Ramiro I of Asturias against forces of ʿAbd al-Raḥmān II.

**Legend:** on the night before battle, James appeared to Ramiro, promised assistance, and declared that he would be seen on a white horse carrying a great white banner. In battle he encouraged the Christians and struck the Saracens. Victory ended the supposed tribute of one hundred maidens. Ramiro then granted the church of Santiago an annual levy of grain and wine and a warrior’s share of booty.

**Documentary finding:** the earliest surviving copy of the supposed royal privilege is not ninth-century. It is a twelfth-century parchment, Archivo de la Catedral de Santiago, **Carpeta 7, no. 1**, authenticated by the Compostela cardinal Pedro Marcio. Modern diplomatic scholarship regards it as a fabrication, probably made by Pedro Marcio between approximately 1158 and 1174 to expand the cathedral’s fiscal claims.

The text nevertheless became historically effective. Lucas of Tuy incorporated it into the *Chronicon mundi* in the thirteenth century, after which the Clavijo apparition became a model for later historiography.

The literary development was gradual:

1. The *Historia Silense* presents James as an intercessor in Ferdinand I’s capture of Coimbra in 1064.
2. The *Liber Sancti Iacobi* arms James and places him symbolically at the Christian host’s head.
3. The forged *Privilegio de los Votos* gives the white horse, banner, battlefield apparition, and direct attack.
4. Lucas of Tuy makes James explicitly animate Christians and strike Saracens.
5. Rodrigo Jiménez de Rada repeats the white horse and banner in *De rebus Hispaniae*.
6. Medieval and early-modern artists add trampling, bodies, severed heads, contemporary armour, swords, pilgrim dress, and individualized Muslim victims.

### Earliest relevant images

The object belongs to an iconographic genealogy, not to an uninterrupted material lineage:

- the cathedral’s so-called Clavijo tympanum, often dated around 1220 but subject to dating and restoration disputes, presents James as a mounted warrior;
- the **1326 Tumbo B miniature** in the cathedral archive is commonly identified as the earliest surviving representation that places defeated Muslims beneath the horse;
- later medieval and early-modern images increase the violence and racialized costume;
- Gambino’s sculpture is an eighteenth-century culmination of this evolution, not an object surviving from Clavijo or the Middle Ages.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Eighteenth century: manufacture and first keeper

**Probable, not fully documented:** the group was made in Compostela by José Gambino or his workshop, perhaps around 1770, for the jet-workers’ guild or confraternity and for processional use.

Its precise original chapel, guild house, storeroom, or altar is not established in the accessible sources. Recent cathedral scholarship calls it a guild commission. Some modern writing calls it cathedral property; later cathedral officials expressly denied that it was originally a cathedral object.

### Nineteenth century

No discrete nineteenth-century transfer has been established. The accessible record does not say where the group was normally stored or who legally owned it after the decline or dissolution of the old guild organization.

This is the largest gap in the custody chain. It should not be filled by assuming continuous cathedral possession.

### 1929: projected Seville exhibition

Provincial planning for Galicia’s pavilion at the 1929 Ibero-American Exposition named the Gambino Matamoros as a cathedral-supplied exhibit. On 29 May—the exposition’s inauguration date—records still described it among pieces not yet received in Seville.

Two interpretations remain possible:

- the original was shipped after that date and displayed;
- the intended loan failed, and its role was supplied through another representation or not realized.

The reviewed source does not prove either outcome. Consequently, physical travel to Seville is **unresolved**, not a confirmed custody event.

### 1930: reproduction by Liste

A sculpture or reproduction made by Liste in 1930 copied the Gambino composition. Published references do not show that the original changed custody. The episode demonstrates its status as a reproducible regional icon.

### Traditional annual processions

The group was carried in Compostela processions, reportedly on or around 25 July, feast of Saint James. Cathedral research links it with another processional group, Maximino Magariños’s Virgin of the Pillar, reflecting the legend that Mary encouraged James during his Spanish mission.

A 2004 retrospective report said the Matamoros continued to parade until roughly fifteen years earlier—approximately the late 1980s. Canon José Fernández Lago said that after returning from Jerusalem in 1980 he proposed that the image be removed from its prominent setting and cease to process. These statements place the loss of active processional function in the late twentieth century, but no final procession date has been located.

### The “1950s arrival” problem

In 2009 an unnamed cathedral source stated that the image was not cathedral property, had “arrived in the fifties for a procession,” and simply remained. In 2021 Fundación Catedral director Daniel Lorenzo similarly said that it was not an object originally belonging to the cathedral and had been installed provisionally in the 1950s.

This conflicts with:

- the 1929 description of the object as “from the Cathedral”;
- sources presenting it as a cathedral or chapter commission;
- accounts implying longstanding cathedral processional use.

Possible reconciliations include:

1. “from the Cathedral” in 1929 described temporary storage or institutional lending, not title;
2. the group was associated with cathedral ceremonies before receiving a permanent niche in the 1950s;
3. it left and returned;
4. “arrived in the fifties” refers specifically to the north-transept camarín, not to its first presence anywhere in the cathedral;
5. distinct legal ownership by the former guild survived while the cathedral held physical custody.

No accessible deed or inventory decides among these. The distinction between **legal ownership**, **ritual custody**, and **display location** must therefore remain open.

### Circa 1950s–2004: north-transept camarín

The group occupied a small gated niche or “camarín” in the north arm of the transept, close to the Acibechería entrance. The architectural recess was not designed for it. It had originated as part of an entrance to Lope de Mendoza’s Gothic chapel; later alterations left a shallow space incapable of accommodating an ordinary retable.

The display was prominent to visitors entering from the north. Devotees lit so many candles that soot reportedly darkened the white horse. Electrical illumination replaced at least some candle use for conservation reasons.

### 2004: announced removal

On 30 April–2 May 2004, press reports stated that the cathedral chapter had decided to replace the Matamoros with another Gambino sculpture depicting James as a pilgrim. Chapter spokesman José Fernández Lago said the purpose was to welcome people of every ethnicity and avoid causing offence. He denied that the decision responded to the 11 March Madrid train bombings.

Initial reports predicted transfer to the Cathedral Museum. Public reaction was intense. A later cathedral account claimed Archbishop Julián Barrio received approximately 700 letters, from religious and nonreligious correspondents, supporting retention.

The institutional record became contested almost immediately:

- newspapers reported a chapter decision;
- cathedral authorities later denied that a formal vote had occurred;
- later accounts state that the object was removed temporarily in 2004;
- another later description says the proposed replacement “never happened.”

The soundest conclusion is that a removal or relocation was announced and probably implemented briefly, but no permanent museum transfer occurred.

### 2006–2020: return with floral screen

By 2006 the group was again in the north-transept niche. A large arrangement of flowers concealed the three fallen bodies and severed head.

In 2009:

- a cathedral nun confirmed that the flowers were intended to hide the Muslim figures;
- the arrangement was replaced weekly;
- an unnamed canon said the order came from a person with authority;
- the precise decision-maker and any formal chapter act remained undisclosed.

The intervention altered the object’s public meaning without altering its surviving carved fabric: from most viewing angles, pilgrims saw a serene white equestrian saint rising from flowers, while the narrative violence was obscured.

Scholarly testimony confirms that the flower-screened presentation continued from 2006 through 2020.

### 2020/2021: removal to the Capilla de Alba

During the cathedral’s large restoration campaign, the group was removed from the north-transept niche and taken to the Capilla de Alba, a chapel near the Pórtico de la Gloria then closed during works.

In its former niche the cathedral installed:

- the funerary slab or memorial presentation of Bishop Teodomiro; and
- a gilded-silver reliquary showing James as pilgrim, associated with Archbishop Lope de Mendoza/Isorna’s collection.

Fundación Catedral director Daniel Lorenzo described the changes as part of a wider reorganization of cathedral imagery and said the final disposition of the Matamoros was then undecided. Press commentary interpreted the move as a permanent retreat from the image’s violence.

### 2021–2025: restricted or nonpublic custody

The best contemporary evidence places the group in cathedral-controlled custody, initially in the Capilla de Alba, with little or no ordinary public access. Later scholarship described the chapel as a more discreet location. No sale, restitution, theft, or deaccession is documented.

### January 2026–present: Museo Diocesano, San Martiño Pinario

The Archdiocese of Santiago and Fundación Catedral opened the Museo Diocesano de Santiago in the former monastery of San Martiño Pinario in January 2026.

The museum’s official Galician-language exhibition page, **IACOBUS**, explicitly lists:

> “Santiago Matamouros (Paso procesional do gremio de azabacheiros)”

and identifies the works as ranging from contemporary reinterpretations to José Gambino’s Santiago Matamoros.

Museum director Ramón Yzquierdo told contemporary media that the sculpture removed from the cathedral camarín had become part of the new museum’s permanent exhibition.

Thus the present physical chain is:

> jet-workers’ guild/confraternity or related commissioner → processional custody in Compostela → uncertain nineteenth- and early twentieth-century storage → cathedral-associated use and display → permanent or quasi-permanent north-transept placement from the 1950s → temporary 2004 displacement → returned, flower-screened, 2006–2020 → Capilla de Alba, approximately 2020/21–2025 → Museo Diocesano, San Martiño Pinario, January 2026–present.

Legal title remains unpublished. Physical control is now exercised within an archdiocesan museum administered through the Archdiocese/Fundación Catedral’s Compostela Sacra programme.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is the surviving sculpture the eighteenth-century guild image?

No rival claimant to the specific Compostela guild image has been found. The same recognizable multi-figure group appears in modern photographs and descriptions before and after the 2004 controversy. There is no evidence of a substitution, modern replica being passed off as the original, or destructive reconstruction.

Nevertheless, a rigorous identity statement must remain qualified:

- there is no published accession number;
- no signature or inscription is reported;
- no publicly accessible scientific examination establishes the wood’s age;
- no unbroken eighteenth-to-twentieth-century inventory sequence has been located;
- a 1930 copy by Liste exists or existed and could create confusion if documentation is imprecise.

The present museum object is institutionally accepted as the same Gambino processional group formerly displayed in the cathedral. There is no positive evidence against that identity.

### Attribution to José Gambino

Three formulations occur:

1. **Firm institutional attribution:** “work of José Gambino.”
2. **Catalogue attribution:** José Gambino, approximately 1770.
3. **Cautious scholarly wording:** “traditionally attributed to” Gambino.

The caution is appropriate because the accessible record supplies neither signature nor commission. Stylistic consistency with Gambino, its date, local provenance, relationship to the Pazo de Raxoi programme, and long institutional tradition make the attribution plausible and widely accepted. Direct workshop documentation has not been demonstrated here.

Participation by José Ferreiro or the broader workshop is possible in chronological terms but was not expressly documented for this group in the sources reviewed.

### Date

Proposals range from the middle of the eighteenth century to about 1770. Since Gambino died in 1775, all fall within his lifetime. “1770?” is a catalogue hypothesis, not a carved or documentary date.

### Ownership dispute

This is more serious than the authorship dispute:

- guild commission and continuing guild identity are repeatedly asserted;
- cathedral and journalistic sources often call it a cathedral image;
- a 2009 report says it was not cathedral property;
- a 2021 cathedral official says it was not originally a cathedral piece and was only provisionally installed in the 1950s;
- the 2026 museum labels it by guild function but exhibits it within an archdiocesan collection.

No living claimant, lawsuit, restitution petition, or title litigation has been identified. The uncertainty concerns historical ownership, not a publicly active legal dispute.

### Scientific authenticity testing

No published scientific campaign specific to the group was located. Claims that it is eighteenth-century and by Gambino therefore remain art-historical and institutional, not laboratory findings.

### Confusion with other works

The following misidentifications must be avoided:

- the Matamoros painting catalogued online by the Museo Catedral is oil on canvas, attributed to Domingo A. de Uzal, not this sculpture;
- the Raxoi façade and rooftop Matamoros are monumental stone works;
- the cathedral contains several additional representations of the warrior saint;
- numerous Galician parish sculptures deliberately copy Gambino’s composition;
- José Ferreiro made a related processional image for the Galician confraternity at San Ignacio in Buenos Aires;
- Liste made a copy in 1930.

Similarity demonstrates influence, not identity.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Processional function

The object was constructed as a *paso*: a group sufficiently mobile and theatrically composed to be carried through streets. Its vertical design allowed the white horse and elevated sword to remain visible above bearers and crowds.

It processed in Compostela into the late twentieth century. One tradition associates it with the feast of Saint James on 25 July and pairs it with a Virgin of the Pillar group. The pairing staged two legends:

1. Mary’s appearance to encourage James during his supposed mission in Hispania;
2. James’s later return as supernatural defender of Christian armies.

No complete published ceremonial rubric, bearer list, route, guild precedence, music programme, or procession account was located.

### Guild function

For the jet workers, the group served as:

- corporate cult image;
- public representation of the guild;
- participant in civic-cathedral ceremonial;
- emblem of proximity to the apostolic shrine;
- demonstration of collective wealth and status.

The guild’s association with pilgrimage commerce was especially apt: Gambino’s warrior wears the attributes—hat, scallop shells, cape, staffs—sold and reproduced by Compostela’s pilgrim trades.

### Devotional practice

The north-transept display attracted candles in sufficient numbers to blacken the horse. This is material evidence of repeated popular engagement, although no miracle register or ex-voto inventory specific to the statue has been located.

The current museum describes James’s iconography as spiritually active and multivalent—apostle, pilgrim, intercessor, and warrior—but museum display is no longer equivalent to an active processional cult.

### Political and fiscal symbolism

The group visually condensed the Clavijo legend underlying the Voto de Santiago. The forged privilege claimed annual grain and wine from lands across Hispania and a warrior’s share of booty. The levy survived in changing forms until abolition by the Cortes of Cádiz in 1812 and definitively in 1834.

The sculpture itself did not create or collect the tax; it made the ideological premise visible. Possession of the image did not confer sovereignty, but displaying and processing it advertised:

- apostolic protection of Christian monarchy;
- Santiago’s patronage of Spain;
- Compostela’s claim to national religious centrality;
- the cathedral’s historical entitlement to offerings;
- the guild’s participation in that sacred civic order.

### Access and control

In the cathedral the group was behind a grille but highly visible. From 2006 to 2020 its full narrative was deliberately restricted by flowers. During its Capilla de Alba residence it was largely inaccessible. Since January 2026 it is publicly accessible by paid museum admission during opening hours, subject to closures and rotation for conservation or research.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The Battle of Clavijo

**Tier: twelfth-century ecclesiastical fabrication incorporating earlier warrior-saint traditions; later medieval national legend.**

The fullest form says that Ramiro I rejected a humiliating Muslim demand for one hundred maidens, fifty noble and fifty common. Driven to Clavijo and apparently trapped, Ramiro received James in a dream. The apostle promised victory and said he would appear on a white horse with a white banner. The next day James entered the battle, encouraged the Christians, and struck their enemies. Ramiro rewarded Compostela with an annual levy and a share of conquest booty.

**Evidence against literal historicity:**

- no contemporary ninth-century record establishes the battle;
- the supposed royal instrument survives only in a twelfth-century copy;
- its diplomatic form and fiscal programme are anachronistic;
- Pedro Marcio’s authentication claims reliance on a lost earlier treasury text;
- modern specialists broadly regard the privilege as forged;
- scholars have related elements to the real Battle of Simancas in 939, Coimbra in 1064, or other conflicts.

**Historical effect:** irrespective of factual truth, the charter circulated, was used by Lucas of Tuy, generated fiscal litigation and payments, shaped royal patronage, and produced enduring imagery. The Gambino group is one material result of that effective legend.

### The white horse

**Tier: securely attested in the twelfth-century Privilegio and thirteenth-century chronicles.**

The privilege promises James *in albo equo*—on a white horse—bearing a great white banner. Later iconography retained the horse but often replaced the banner with a sword or combined both. Gambino’s white horse is therefore tied to the oldest surviving Clavijo text, while the particular fallen bodies and pilgrim costume are later artistic additions.

### The tribute of one hundred maidens

**Tier: legendary component of the forged privilege, elaborated in later historiography.**

The story supplied a moral cause for war and cast Christian victory as liberation from sexual tribute. It also made the annual ecclesiastical levy appear an exchange of gratitude for deliverance. No contemporary evidence confirms the supposed tribute.

### “Santiago y cierra, España”

**Tier: later military and literary tradition.**

The battle cry is linked retrospectively with James’s military patronage. The wording should not be projected automatically into the ninth-century episode. Its later circulation contributed to the statue’s intelligibility as a victory image.

### Continued miraculous power

The candles and devotional use demonstrate that worshippers treated the sculpture as a focus of intercession. No securely documented miracle uniquely caused by this particular wooden group was located. Assertions that the statue itself secured military victories or supernatural cures remain unverified.

### The 2004 “ban”

**Tier: modern media narrative built around a real but inconsistently recorded institutional decision.**

The simplified modern story says that the cathedral “banned” Santiago Matamoros after the Madrid bombings to appease Muslims. The evidence is more complicated:

- the proposal reportedly predated the attacks;
- the chapter spokesman denied a causal link;
- cathedral authorities later denied that the necessary formal vote occurred;
- the group returned or remained after temporary removal;
- the lower figures were subsequently hidden with flowers;
- no Muslim organization is documented as having demanded removal.

The episode is therefore neither wholly invented nor accurately represented by the shorthand “banned after 11-M.”

It appears in the museum section **IACOBUS**, officially identified in Galician as:

## 9. Modern Reception

### Canonical model and copies

Gambino’s group became a model for Galician equestrian James images. Scholarship identifies close dependence in:

- parish sculptures at Santiago do Castelo and Santiago do Burgo;
- a Matamoros purchased for Sigrás in 1855, though that object has its own distinct provenance;
- José Ferreiro’s processional sculpture for the Galician confraternity at San Ignacio in Buenos Aires;
- a copy by Liste in 1930;
- the wider Raxoi Battle of Clavijo ensemble, whose dynamic white horse, attacking saint, banner, and defeated Muslims share the late-Baroque visual language.

Copies are receptions of the object, not pieces of its custody chain.

### The 1929 Ibero-American Exposition

The proposed display in Galicia’s pavilion placed the sculpture within Primo de Rivera-era presentations of regional identity and Hispanism. Santiago Matamoros was used to link Galicia’s apostolic past with an imperial and transatlantic Spanish narrative. Records show the intended role clearly, although the original sculpture’s actual arrival remains uncertain.

### Twentieth-century nationalism

The warrior James was repeatedly mobilized in conservative, nationalist, and military discourse. A later account reports that during the Spanish Civil War the papal nuncio publicly associated James with Franco at Brunete; this claim concerns the broader icon, not an action performed with the Gambino sculpture.

The statue’s cathedral processions and prominent display nevertheless situated it in the same symbolic field: Catholic Spain, military patronage, Reconquista memory, and national unity.

### 2004–2020 controversy

The object became internationally known less through its artistry than through disputes over religious violence, Muslim representation, terrorism, multicultural pilgrimage, and “political correctness.”

The sequence materially transformed its reception:

- 2004: announced replacement by Santiago Peregrino;
- popular correspondence in defence of the image;
- cathedral denials and partial reversal;
- 2006–2020: floral concealment of the defeated Muslims;
- 2020/21: removal from the main visitor route;
- 2026: recontextualization as a museum object within a deliberately plural display of James’s changing identities.

The flower screen itself became an interpretive intervention: not restoration, destruction, or iconoclasm, but controlled visibility.

### Tourism and commerce

The image was prominent near one of the cathedral’s busiest entrances and was reproduced in photographs, postcards, guidebooks, online travel pages, and press coverage. Its fame now supports interpretation in the paid diocesan museum circuit. No reliable revenue figures specific to the object are published.

### Contemporary reinterpretation

The Museo Diocesano’s IACOBUS installation juxtaposes the historical Matamoros with contemporary work by David Planas and with alternative identities of James. The curatorial argument treats the apostle’s imagery as changing rather than presenting the warrior form as his sole or timeless identity.

No documented modern forgery marketed specifically as the original Gambino group has been found. Numerous commercial sculptures titled “Santiago Matamoros” exist, but they do not claim credible identity with this object.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Major object-specific scholarship

The key modern references identified are:

- María S. Ortega Romero, “Santiago Matamoros (paso procesional), José Gambino, 1770?”, in *Todos con Santiago. Patrimonio eclesiástico* (1999), pp. 204–205.
- Ramón Yzquierdo Peiró, “Misit me Dominus. Santiago el Mayor en las colecciones de la Catedral compostelana” (2017), which identifies the group as Gambino’s mid-eighteenth-century commission for the jet-workers’ guild.
- Francisco Singul’s study of the Battle of Clavijo as an illustrated emblem and the Raxoi programme, which treats Gambino’s guild procession as the immediate model for later works.
- broader iconographic studies by Serafín Moralejo, Antón Sicart Giménez, Javier Gómez Darriba, and others on the development from mounted saint to Matamoros.

### Principal debates

#### Date and maker

The attribution oscillates between certainty and caution. A published contract would greatly improve the case. Until then, “José Gambino, c. mid-eighteenth century or c. 1770?” is preferable to an exact date.

#### Commissioner and owner

Guild, confraternity, chapter, and cathedral are used inconsistently. The 1950s “temporary” installation claim conflicts with the 1929 cathedral provenance. The unresolved question is not merely where it stood but who possessed legal title at each stage.

#### Cathedral continuity

The object’s display history is often compressed into “always in the cathedral.” Contemporary cathedral testimony instead indicates that its familiar north-transept installation began in the 1950s. Earlier cathedral association is likely but not equivalent to continuous residence in that niche.

#### 2004 removal

Press, chapter statements, and retrospective accounts disagree over whether there was a formal vote, an actual withdrawal, a proposed transfer, or a short-lived removal. What is certain is the public announcement, controversy, continued/renewed display, and later flower screen.

#### Medieval iconographic origin

Older literature often treated the Clavijo battle and warrior image as direct ninth-century history. Modern work separates:

- eleventh-century intercessory traditions;
- twelfth-century fabricated charter;
- thirteenth-century chronicles and mounted images;
- the 1326 image with trampled enemies;
- early-modern elaborations;
- Gambino’s eighteenth-century synthesis.

The cathedral tympanum’s date and degree of later restoration remain debated; it should not be used uncritically as a precisely dated prototype.

### State of primary evidence

Known or indicated repositories include:

- Archivo de la Catedral de Santiago, especially the forged privilege at Carpeta 7, no. 1;
- cathedral chapter and museum records;
- guild or confraternity documentation, if surviving;
- A Coruña provincial records for the 1929 pavilion;
- photographs and newspapers documenting the twentieth- and twenty-first-century display;
- unpublished or inaccessible conservation and movement files.

Priority archival questions are:

1. Is there an original commission or payment to Gambino?
2. Did José Ferreiro or a polychromer share the work?
3. What guild inventory first names the group?
4. Where was it stored in the nineteenth century?
5. Was it shipped to Seville in 1929?
6. What precisely happened to it in 2004?
7. What internal movement and condition reports accompanied the 2020/21 and 2026 transfers?
8. Who presently holds legal title?
9. What are its verified dimensions, weight, wood species, and conservation history?

## 11. Chronology

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **1064** | Ferdinand I captures Coimbra. Later accounts develop James’s intercessory and military role; this is part of the legend’s prehistory, not the sculpture’s history. |
| **c. 1158–1174** | Pedro Marcio probably creates or authenticates the fabricated *Privilegio de los Votos*, placing James on a white horse at Clavijo. |
| **Early 13th century** | Lucas of Tuy incorporates the Clavijo narrative into the *Chronicon mundi*. |
| **c. 1220 or later, disputed** | Mounted James appears in the cathedral’s so-called Clavijo tympanum. |
| **1326** | Tumbo B miniature shows mounted James with defeated Muslims beneath the horse, commonly regarded as the earliest surviving complete Matamoros formulation. |
| **1719** | José Gambino is born at the Pazo do Faramello. |
| **Mid-18th century / c. 1770?** | The polychromed wooden processional group is made in Compostela, conventionally attributed to Gambino and commissioned for the jet-workers’ guild or confraternity. |
| **1775** | Gambino dies, establishing the latest possible date for an entirely autograph work. |
| **18th–20th centuries** | The group participates in Compostela processions; exact sequence and storage locations remain undocumented in accessible sources. |
| **1855** | A separate Matamoros image influenced by the Gambino model is purchased for Sigrás; this is evidence of reception, not transfer of the original. |
| **29 May 1929** | Galicia pavilion records identify the Gambino Matamoros “from the Cathedral” as an intended Seville exhibit, but state that it had not yet arrived. Actual shipment remains unresolved. |
| **1930** | Liste produces a copy after the Gambino sculpture. |
| **1950s** | According to later cathedral officials, the group arrives or is installed “provisionally” in the cathedral for a procession and remains in the north-transept niche. |
| **1980** | Canon José Fernández Lago later says he proposed ending its procession and removing it from the prominent setting after a Jerusalem visit. |
| **Late 1980s, approximate** | Public processional use reportedly ends. |
| **1999** | Catalogued as José Gambino, *Santiago Matamoros (paso procesional)*, c. 1770? |
| **30 April–2 May 2004** | The cathedral announces replacement by a Gambino Santiago Peregrino to avoid offending visitors; the relation to 11-M is officially denied. |
| **2004** | The group is reportedly removed temporarily; strong public reaction follows. Later authorities dispute whether a formal chapter vote occurred. |
| **2006** | The Matamoros is again displayed in the north transept. Flowers begin or are reported as screening the fallen Muslims. |
| **13 August 2009** | Detailed press investigation confirms that cathedral attendants replace the concealing flowers weekly and reports contested ownership and 1950s installation. |
| **2006–2020** | Scholarly testimony records continued flower-screened display. |
| **2020/early 2021** | During cathedral restoration the group is moved to the closed Capilla de Alba. Teodomiro’s memorial and a pilgrim-James reliquary replace it in the niche. |
| **2021–2025** | Held in restricted cathedral/archdiocesan custody; final location initially undecided. |
| **8 January 2026** | Museo Diocesano de Santiago opens at San Martiño Pinario. Ramón Yzquierdo confirms the Matamoros as part of its permanent exhibition. |
| **2026–present** | Publicly exhibited in the museum’s IACOBUS section as *Santiago Matamouros (Paso procesional do gremio de azabacheiros)*. |

### Evidentiary assessment

The post-1929 movements and the 2004–2026 display history are comparatively well documented. The weak portion is the physical object’s first century and a half: neither the original commission nor a continuous chain of inventories was available online. Accordingly, the attribution, exact manufacture date, first keeper, nineteenth-century location, and legal title should remain qualified.

The most important verified present-day development is easily missed by older guides: the sculpture is no longer hidden in the Capilla de Alba or displayed in its former cathedral niche. It entered the new Museo Diocesano’s public exhibition in January 2026.
