# Novalis's engagement ring for Sophie von Kuhn: Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `novaliss_engagement_ring_for_sophie_von_kuhn_object_dossier.md`

**Research status:** Current to 6 September 2026.

**Evidentiary key**

- **Documented:** supported by a contemporary document, museum record, or directly observable object.
- **Institutional account:** asserted by the present custodian or another heritage institution but not fully documented in the published material.
- **Scholarly inference:** reasoned reconstruction explicitly identified as such.
- **Family tradition:** provenance supplied by descendants without a presently published contemporary transfer document.
- **Reception history:** demonstrable later interpretation or use, not evidence for the original object’s identity.
- **Unresolved:** the available evidence does not establish the claim.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity

The object commonly called the **Novalisring**, **Novalis-Ring**, **Verlobungsring von Novalis**, or **Verlobungsring von Novalis und Sophie von Kühn** is a late-eighteenth-century portrait ring now held on renewable private loan by the municipal museum in Weißenfels, Saxony-Anhalt. It contains a miniature profile portrait identified as Christiane Wilhelmine Sophie von Kühn (1782–1797) and bears an inscription conventionally transcribed:

> **Sophia sey mein Schuz Geist**

Modern German: *Sophia sei mein Schutzgeist.*  
English: “Sophia, be my guardian spirit.”

The familiar description “Novalis’s engagement ring for Sophie von Kühn” is potentially misleading. The published scholarly reconstruction distinguishes at least two rings mentioned in 1795 correspondence:

1. a ring apparently intended for Sophie; and
2. Friedrich von Hardenberg’s own ring, possibly “the ring with the S.”

The surviving Weißenfels ring portrays Sophie and therefore makes better sense as a ring **worn by Hardenberg/Novalis**, not as the ring worn by Sophie. Ludwig Stockinger accordingly calls it the “‘Verlobungsring’ Friedrich von Hardenbergs,” while stressing that its identification with either ring mentioned in the letters remains inferential.

### Names and inscription

“Novalis” was Friedrich von Hardenberg’s literary name. The compound **Novalisring** is a later curatorial and popular designation, not an eighteenth-century title documented in the surviving correspondence.

The inscription uses historical spelling:

- **sey** for modern *sei*;
- **Schuz Geist**, written as two words and without the modern *t* in *Schutz*;
- **Sophia**, rather than Sophie.

“Maria/Sophia” does not appear to be an alternative personal name for Sophie von Kühn. Stockinger interprets **Sophia** as a deliberate semantic expansion of her name toward Greek **σοφία, sophía**, “wisdom,” and thus toward *philosophía*, “love of wisdom.” That is a literary-philosophical interpretation, not a demonstrated statement by the engraver or patron.

### Construction and appearance

Published photographs show:

- a narrow metal hoop;
- an oval, elevated bezel or portrait compartment;
- a small painted profile portrait of Sophie facing toward the viewer’s left;
- pale flesh tones against a light ground;
- brown or dark hair, gathered behind the head;
- a dark blue or blue-green garment;
- a narrow bright-metal border around the miniature;
- a protective transparent cover, apparently glass;
- engraving on the inner circumference of the hoop.

The miniature is a bust-length profile, not merely a silhouette. Stockinger considers its surviving color more faithful than that of a related, badly faded watercolor portrait.

The photographs suggest a yellow or warm-colored metal, and an isolated web caption calls it a gold ring. No accessible museum catalogue, conservation report, hallmark transcription, assay, or materials analysis was found that securely establishes whether the hoop and bezel are solid gold, a gold alloy, gilded metal, or another material. It should therefore be described as **yellow metal, traditionally or popularly identified as gold**, pending publication of technical examination.

Likewise, the following have not been found in a published object record:

- dimensions;
- weight;
- ring size;
- metal fineness;
- support and pigments of the miniature;
- glass composition;
- hallmark or maker’s mark;
- accession or inventory number;
- condition report;
- conservation history.

The museum calls the piece a loan, so an ordinary acquisition or accession number may not exist; an internal loan or deposit number probably does, but it has not been published.

### The disputed date

The inscription includes a date, but readings conflict:

- Heinz Ritter read it as **15 March 1795**.
- Hermann Liebmann published **19 March 1796**.
- The Weißenfels museum informed Stockinger that **19 March 1796** is unquestionably the correct reading on the physical object.
- Three exclamation marks reportedly follow the year and were said by Liebmann to have been scratched in later.

The date is central because:

- 15 March 1795 was remembered by Hardenberg as the day Sophie gave him assurance that she would be his;
- 19 March 1797 was Sophie’s death date;
- no comparable event is documented for 19 March 1796.

Stockinger therefore proposes, explicitly as conjecture, that the date may have been engraved after Sophie’s death, that “19 March” was intended to commemorate her death, and that “1796” was an error. The later exclamation marks might record a subsequent owner’s recognition of that inconsistency. No microscopy, handwriting comparison, or metallurgical sequencing of the engraving and scratches has been published.

### Related but distinct objects

The ring must not be conflated with three related portraits:

1. **Pearl-bordered miniature/brooch/medallion.** An oval miniature of Sophie, bordered by twenty-seven small pearls, said to have been commissioned for Novalis’s mother, Auguste Bernhardine von Hardenberg. It began as a brooch and was later adapted or worn as a pendant. The Hardenberg family placed it on permanent loan at the Novalis Museum, Schloss Oberwiederstedt, in 2012. Its portrait closely resembles that in the ring.

2. **Watercolored pencil portrait.** A surviving bust-length portrait of Sophie, published in the 2009 Oberwiederstedt exhibition catalogue. Albrecht Pohlmann’s discussion of portrait practice around 1800 makes it plausible that such a drawing supplied the model for miniature paintings, but this has not been technically proved.

3. **Caroline von Hardenberg’s copy.** A copy reportedly made by Novalis’s sister Caroline; the original is apparently lost, and a photograph survives in the Mansfeld Museum at Hettstedt.

The twenty-seven-pearl medallion has sometimes entered discussions about the missing ring intended for Sophie. Stockinger rejects an identification as intrinsically doubtful: a ring made for Sophie would more probably have shown Hardenberg, not Sophie herself.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Earliest documentary evidence for rings

The oldest relevant primary evidence consists of two letters written by Hardenberg’s brother Karl in 1795:

- **16 June 1795:** “Anbei schicke ich Dir Deinen Ring” — “Enclosed I send you your ring.”
- **21 August 1795:** “Du erhältst hierdurch den Ring mit dem S.” — “You receive hereby the ring with the S.”

These letters prove that rings circulated between the brothers. They do not describe a portrait, metal, maker, inscription, or full date, and they do not independently prove that either mentioned object is the ring now in Weißenfels.

The historical-critical edition’s commentary interprets the June object as the ring for Sophie and the August object as Novalis’s own engagement ring. Stockinger emphasizes that these editorial notes are hypotheses, despite their later treatment as settled fact.

### Date of manufacture

Two competing chronologies must be retained:

- **Traditional/institutional chronology:** the ring was commissioned in 1795 in connection with the private understanding of 15 March and the rings mentioned that June and August.
- **Alternative reconstruction:** the portrait setting, the present inscription, or both may date to late 1796 or after Sophie’s death in March 1797.

A primary letter from Sophie dated **16 December 1796** says that Hardenberg’s mother wanted her likeness and that “the painter” had not yet completed “the two pictures” but promised to send them soon. This is strong evidence for two miniature portraits being produced around the end of 1796. The corresponding objects may be the portrait in the ring and the portrait in the pearl-bordered brooch, but Sophie’s letter does not specify their eventual settings.

Consequently, the frequent municipal formulation that Hardenberg “had the ring made in 1795” is possible but not conclusively established for the portrait ring in its present form. One plausible sequence is:

1. engagement rings existed or were ordered in 1795;
2. two miniature portraits were painted in late 1796;
3. one portrait was incorporated into Hardenberg’s ring;
4. the inscription or at least its date may have been added or altered later.

No documentary commission, invoice, jeweler’s account, or workshop record has been published.

### Maker and painter

The jeweler and engraver are unidentified.

Gabriele Rommel tentatively attributes the miniature portraits to **Friedrich Wilhelm Senewaldt**, a Berlin portrait miniaturist known for his portrait of Immanuel Kant. Senewaldt was connected with the Hardenberg family and portrayed family members. The attribution is explicitly circumstantial—“could well” have been the recipient of the commission—and is unsupported by a signature, payment record, or technical comparison published in the consulted sources.

The material origin of the metal, glass, pigment, and portrait support is unknown.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 1795–1801: Hardenberg and the first documentary gap

**1795:** Two rings are mentioned in Karl von Hardenberg’s letters to Friedrich. Identification of the surviving ring with “the ring with the S” is plausible but unproved.

**Late 1796:** Sophie reports two unfinished portraits. One may be the ring miniature.

**19 March 1797:** Sophie dies at Grüningen.

**20 April 1797:** Hardenberg records showing “S[ophie]’s portrait” to the wife of the district administrator. He does not call it a ring, so this is evidence that he possessed and displayed a likeness, not proof that the object shown was the surviving ring.

The city of Weißenfels states that Hardenberg, distressed by the ring after Sophie’s death, gave it to his friend and former superior **Coelestin August Just** in 1797. No contemporary gift letter, inventory, diary entry, or will confirming this transfer has been published. Stockinger’s examination is more cautious: the path into the Just family is plausible but unknown.

Hardenberg died in 1801. No published probate inventory has been cited as recording the ring.

### The Just-to-Liebmann reconstruction

The published provenance first advanced by Heinrich/Hermann Liebmann depends on Hardenberg’s close relationship with the Just household:

- **Coelestin August Just** was the Tennstedt district administrator under whom Hardenberg worked.
- Just’s niece **Caroline Just** kept house for him and was a friend and confidante of Hardenberg.
- The Liebmann family tradition supposed that Hardenberg’s ring passed to Caroline.
- A related **Christiane Caroline Just** was said to have married Carl Friedrich Liebmann, thereby bringing the ring into the Liebmann family.

This reconstruction contains documented genealogical errors and unresolved transfers.

Heinrich Liebmann treated Caroline Just and Christiane Caroline Just as sisters. Stockinger shows that they were cousins: they were daughters of two different brothers of Coelestin Just. Caroline’s 1802 marriage to her cousin Carl August Just strengthened the family connection, but it does not document a transfer of the ring.

Stockinger also could not verify Liebmann’s statement that Christiane Caroline Just was his great-grandmother. Accessible genealogical information named **Erdmuthe Eleonore Jud**, rather than Christiane Caroline Just, as the wife of Carl Friedrich Liebmann. A prior marriage remains possible but unestablished.

Accordingly, the claimed links are:

1. **Hardenberg → Coelestin Just or Caroline Just:** institutional/family tradition; no published transfer document.
2. **Caroline Just → Christiane Caroline Just:** plausible family transmission; undocumented.
3. **Just family → Carl Friedrich Liebmann:** family reconstruction; genealogically problematic.
4. **Successive Liebmann descendants:** family inheritance; individual links have not been published.

The city’s statement that the history is traceable “without gaps” and that marriage transferred the ring into the Liebmann family in 1800 is inconsistent with Stockinger’s source criticism. The scholarly account expressly identifies gaps.

### 1916: first secure publication of the surviving object

The earliest secure public attestation of the **specific surviving Weißenfels object** found in this research is Hermann Liebmann’s article:

- **Hermann Liebmann, “Ein neues Bild der ersten Braut von Novalis,” Deutsche Literaturzeitung 37 (1916), pp. 222–224.**

Liebmann described and published the ring, reported the inscribed date as 19 March 1796, mentioned three subsequently scratched exclamation marks, and proposed its route through the Just and Liebmann families.

The object’s existence is secure from this point. Its identity before 1916 depends on the family provenance and its congruence with eighteenth-century letters and portrait evidence.

### 1925: deposit in Weißenfels

A receipt preserved in the Museum Weißenfels documents that on **29 June 1925** the ring was deposited as a loan by:

- **Karl Otto Heinrich Liebmann** (1874–1939), mathematician and Heidelberg professor; and
- his sister **Hedwig Vollers, née Liebmann** (1872–1941).

The receipt stipulated that the ring was to be returned to their lawful heirs at any time upon a joint declaration. This is the first published, discrete custody record with date, depositors, recipient, and legal condition.

The object was therefore not donated to the city. Ownership remained with the Liebmann family or its lawful successors; the municipal institution became bailee and custodian.

### 1933–1945: National Socialist period

Heinrich Liebmann, who had Jewish ancestry but was Protestant, was placed under political pressure and sought retirement in 1935. The sources consulted do not document whether this persecution affected the ring or its loan agreement.

No published record was found of:

- confiscation;
- forced sale;
- evacuation;
- wartime storage;
- physical damage;
- transfer to another depot;
- recovery immediately after the war.

The absence of such evidence is important because the later owners themselves reported that the ring’s wartime fate was unknown to them for many years.

### 1945–1990: Soviet occupation and the German Democratic Republic

The ring remained somewhere within the custody of the Weißenfels museum system, but its precise wartime and GDR-era locations have not been published. Judith Liebmann stated in 2022 that the family did not know what had happened to it during the Second World War or the DDR period.

No evidence was found for nationalization of title. The 2022 renewal of the private loan and the museum’s continued description of Judith Liebmann as heir support continuity of private ownership, although the entire legal file has not been published.

### 1990–2001: rediscovery of the custody relationship

After German reunification, Karl-Otto Liebmann was able to contact Weißenfels to inquire about the family property. In summer **2001**, Judith and Karl-Otto Liebmann visited the museum. According to Judith’s public recollection, the ring was found:

> wrapped in brown packing paper in a cupboard.

This is a descendant’s eyewitness account, subsequently published by the city. It does not establish how long it had been there, whether that cupboard was secure museum storage, or whether the object had been formally inventoried throughout the interval. No loss or theft is alleged. Rather, the episode documents neglect, storage, and a lapse in exhibition and family communication.

The couple asked that it be “freed” and displayed again.

### 2001–2012: re-exhibition

By a further Liebmann visit in **2012**, the ring was displayed in the museum’s Novalis room. The exact date of reinstallation, any conservation work, and the construction of its display mount have not been published.

### 2022: renewal of the loan

On **2 May 2022**, during the celebrations marking Novalis’s 250th birthday, **Professor Judith Liebmann**, identified by the city as the ring’s heir, traveled from the United States to Weißenfels. She:

- lectured on the ring and her family’s history;
- renewed its loan agreement;
- expressed the wish that it remain in Weißenfels “with Novalis.”

The renewal confirms continuing private ownership and municipal custody at that date.

### 2022–2026

The ring was exhibited in the Novalis presentation associated with Museum Weißenfels. A visitor photographed it on display on 11 January 2024, furnishing independent visual evidence that it was publicly displayed in Weißenfels at that time.

No later published transfer, withdrawal, sale, deaccession, theft, restitution claim, or change of ownership was found. Its latest reconstructable status is therefore **private property of Judith Liebmann or the Liebmann heirs, on loan to and displayed by Museum Weißenfels**.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### What is secure

The following propositions are well supported:

- A physical portrait ring exists.
- It has been publicly documented since 1916.
- It was deposited with the Weißenfels municipal museum in 1925.
- The museum has retained it under a renewable loan relationship.
- Its miniature is closely related to other surviving portraits identified as Sophie von Kühn.
- Its inscription contains “Sophia sey mein Schuz Geist” and an anomalous date.
- Eighteenth-century correspondence independently mentions rings associated with Hardenberg in 1795.
- A December 1796 letter independently mentions two miniature portraits of Sophie in production.

### What is probable but unproved

- The portrait represents Sophie von Kühn.
- The portrait miniature is one of the two pictures mentioned by Sophie in December 1796.
- Hardenberg wore the ring.
- The ring is the “ring with the S” sent by Karl in August 1795.
- It passed from Hardenberg into the Just family and thence into the Liebmann family.

These inferences reinforce one another but do not constitute an unbroken documentary chain.

### Principal difficulties

#### Two-ring problem

The historical-critical edition proposes two engagement rings: Sophie’s ring and Hardenberg’s. The present ring’s portrait of Sophie favors identification as Hardenberg’s ring, but the letters do not describe it sufficiently.

#### Date problem

The physical inscription reportedly reads 19 March 1796, while the commemoratively meaningful dates are 15 March 1795 and 19 March 1797. This could indicate:

- a simple engraving error;
- a later memorial inscription;
- alteration of an earlier ring;
- an erroneous modern reading;
- or a later object assembled from authentic portrait material.

No scientific or epigraphic examination has resolved the alternatives.

#### Provenance problem

The object’s chain between Hardenberg’s lifetime and its publication in 1916 is reconstructed from family relationships, some of which Liebmann stated incorrectly. Stockinger regards the route as plausible, not proven.

#### Authorship of the inscription

Richard Samuel’s commentary has been quoted as saying that Novalis himself entered the date and inscription. No handwriting comparison or primary testimony establishes that he personally engraved or scratched it. That attribution should be treated as editorial tradition.

#### Painter attribution

The Senewaldt attribution remains circumstantial.

### Scientific examination

No published evidence was found for:

- X-ray fluorescence or other metal analysis;
- pigment analysis;
- microscopy of inscription strokes;
- examination of tool marks;
- dendrochronology or radiocarbon dating, neither of which would normally be especially useful without an appropriate organic support;
- analysis comparing portrait support and pigments with the Oberwiederstedt medallion;
- handwriting or letter-form comparison;
- forensic examination of the three exclamation marks;
- a formal provenance-research report.

The ring’s authenticity is therefore based on convergence of documentary, genealogical, iconographic, and custodial evidence—not laboratory authentication.

### Rival claimant

No rival physical ring claiming to be **Novalis’s own Sophie portrait ring** was found. The pearl-bordered medallion is a related object, not a serious present rival, though it has complicated discussion of what became of the separate ring intended for Sophie.

### Assessment

The most defensible formulation is:

> The Weißenfels object is a genuine late-eighteenth- or early-nineteenth-century-style portrait ring, securely documented in the Liebmann family by 1916 and in municipal custody since 1925. Its identification as the ring worn by Friedrich von Hardenberg in connection with Sophie von Kühn is accepted in Novalis scholarship and is plausible, but it is not established by a continuous documentary provenance or scientific examination.

Calling it a forgery would exceed the evidence. Calling its custody “gapless” also exceeds the evidence.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Original function

The object’s likely original function was that of a **personal portrait and betrothal ring**: an intimate image worn on the body and associated with a marriage promise. No formal ring-exchange ceremony is documented.

The March 1795 understanding was private. A more official engagement followed in July 1796 after Hardenberg obtained paternal consent. It is therefore uncertain whether the present ring commemorated:

- the private promise of 15 March 1795;
- the official 1796 engagement;
- Sophie’s portrait commission in late 1796;
- or her death in March 1797.

### Memorial function

If Hardenberg retained and wore the ring after Sophie’s death, it functioned as a mourning jewel or contact relic. That use is probable but not directly recorded. Hardenberg’s 1797 journal mentions showing her portrait and documents prayer, grave visits, and the deliberate sacralization of her memory; it never explicitly mentions the ring.

Stockinger calls the surviving object a potential **Berührungsreliquie**, a contact relic, for later admirers—provided its possession by Hardenberg is accepted. This describes reception, not a formal ecclesiastical classification.

### Protective and inspirational function

“Be my guardian spirit” assigns the image a protective and mediating role. Hardenberg elsewhere called Sophie his “Genius,” and after her death described her image as a “better self” and “miraculous image” that might protect him from evil or impurity. These statements demonstrate his symbolic use of Sophie’s image, although none specifies that the ring itself possessed supernatural power.

The inscription also anticipates the line in the dedicatory sonnet to *Heinrich von Ofterdingen* in which the beloved is to become the poet’s muse and the quiet guardian spirit of his poetry. The ring consequently operates in scholarship as a compact material emblem of a wider poetic construction:

**Sophie → Sophia/Wisdom → genius or guardian spirit → muse → mediator between finite life and transcendence.**

### Ceremonial afterlife

Documented modern ceremonial uses include:

- presentation within the Novalis rooms in Weißenfels;
- public interpretation during Novalis’s 250th-birthday week in May 2022;
- ceremonial renewal of its loan by Judith Liebmann;
- incorporation of its image into the audiovisual installation **RE:ZITATION** in November 2022.

There is no evidence of formal religious veneration, healing claims, oath-taking, restricted ritual handling, or a legally recognized relic cult.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The “perfectly documented relic” narrative

**Late institutional formulation:** Hardenberg commissioned the ring in 1795, gave it to Coelestin Just after Sophie’s death in 1797, and marriage carried it into the Liebmann family in 1800.

This is the fullest narrative currently circulated by the city of Weißenfels. Its likely basis is Liebmann family research combined with the 1795 letters. Against it stands Stockinger’s finding that the transfer to Caroline or Coelestin Just is undocumented and that the alleged Just–Liebmann genealogy contains errors.

The narrative should therefore be labeled **institutional tradition with a probable factual core**, not a gapless documentary chain.

### The ring as Sophie’s ring

Popular captions sometimes describe it simply as “Sophie’s engagement ring.” The portrait makes this unlikely if “Sophie’s” means the ring she wore. Its more likely function was to allow Hardenberg to wear Sophie’s image. The separate ring intended for Sophie is lost or unidentified.

### The date as the secret engagement date

The commonly reproduced date, 15 March 1795, matches Hardenberg’s later account of receiving Sophie’s promise. The museum’s physical reading of 19 March 1796 contradicts this. The conventional reading appears partly driven by the biographical desire for the ring to record the private engagement.

### The date as memorial cipher

Stockinger’s proposal that “19 March 1796” is a posthumous memorial inscription with the death year mistakenly entered one year early is a plausible modern scholarly hypothesis. It is not an established hidden message.

### The three exclamation marks

Liebmann reported three marks added after the date. Stockinger suggests a later owner may have scratched them in astonishment at the wrong year. This is conjecture. Without microscopic sequencing, their date and intention are unknown.

### The sacred ring

Modern Novalis reception often treats Sophie as the poet’s saint, Beatrice, muse, or intermediary. The ring’s portrait and “guardian spirit” inscription make it unusually receptive to this interpretation. Its sacred character is therefore **literary and secular-reliquary**, not the result of canonization, ecclesiastical authentication, or recorded cult authorization.

### The cupboard rediscovery

The story of the heir finding the ring wrapped in brown paper in a museum cupboard in 2001 has the shape of a relic-recognition tale but rests on Judith Liebmann’s published personal recollection. Its factual core is credible; what remains unknown is the administrative history that placed it there.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Scholarly visualization

Hermann Liebmann’s 1916 article brought the ring into public Novalis scholarship. Thereafter it became a standard illustration in biographies and editions, including works by:

- Gerhard Schulz;
- Herbert Uerlings;
- Heinz Ritter;
- Hermann Kurzke.

Repetition converted a plausible identification into something often presented without qualification.

### Museum display and “secular relic” status

Since its 1925 deposit, the ring has served Weißenfels as a small but symbolically dense link to the poet’s physical life. Its attraction lies less in precious material than in a claimed direct bodily association: if authentic, it joined Hardenberg’s hand with Sophie’s image.

The ring’s period of unexhibited storage during or after the DDR era interrupted this function. Its renewed display after 2001 restored its role as an anchor object within the Novalis memorial landscape.

### Literature

Penelope Fitzgerald’s historical novel **The Blue Flower** (1995) materially shaped English-language public understanding of Hardenberg and Sophie, although the ring is not the sole center of the narrative. Stockinger uses Fitzgerald’s depiction to illuminate the process by which Sophie’s ordinary historical features were transformed into the poet’s ideal image.

The ring also appears in modern biographical, educational, and web accounts as the supposedly definitive surviving portrait of Sophie.

### Contemporary art and digital reproduction

The 2021–2022 project **Für Novalis: Schmuck** invited contemporary jewelers and artists to respond to Novalis. Works such as the collier **Sophie** used copper, textiles, and deliberately unreadable letter fragments to evoke the relationship’s fragmentary record. These are artistic responses, not replicas of the historical ring.

For the **RE:ZITATION** audiovisual event in Weißenfels on 27 November 2022, images of the Novalis ring were incorporated with Novalis and Heinrich Schütz materials. The physical object’s image thus became separable from the artifact and entered projection-based public commemoration.

### Photography and open digital circulation

A high-resolution visitor photograph made in January 2024 was released under CC0 through Wikimedia Commons. This has made the ring’s outward appearance widely reproducible, while not supplying technical metadata about the artifact itself.

### Replicas, forgeries, and commerce

No documented full-scale museum replica, commercial authorized copy, counterfeit, or forged Novalis ring was found. Modern jewelry sold under the unrelated product name “Novalis” should not be connected with this object.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Primary documentary base

The central primary evidence consists of:

- Karl von Hardenberg’s letters of 16 June and 21 August 1795;
- Sophie von Kühn’s letter of 16 December 1796 concerning two portraits;
- Hardenberg’s posthumous recollections of 15 March 1795;
- Hardenberg’s 1797 journal and letters concerning Sophie’s portrait and memorialization;
- Hermann Liebmann’s 1916 description and provenance account;
- the museum’s 29 June 1925 receipt;
- Judith Liebmann’s published recollections and 2022 loan renewal.

The Hardenberg and Sophie texts are published in the historical-critical edition:

**Novalis: Schriften. Die Werke Friedrich von Hardenbergs**, edited by Paul Kluckhohn, Richard Samuel, Heinz Ritter, Gerhard Schulz, Hans-Joachim Mähl, et al., Stuttgart, 1977–2006.

### Major object-specific studies

**Hermann Liebmann, 1916.** First known detailed publication of the surviving object. Valuable as evidence that the ring was then in the family, but its genealogy and date interpretation require correction.

**Kurt Pötsch/Pötzsch, “Der Ring eines Dichters.”** Published in the *Weißenfelser Heimatbote* 6 (1960), pp. 208–211, and apparently also in *Philobiblon* 6 (1962). This helped establish the object’s place in postwar reception. Full digital text was not located.

**Heinz Ritter, 1967 and 1986.** Advanced the 15 March 1795 reading and published related Sophie portraits. His works helped stabilize the conventional engagement-ring interpretation.

**Richard Samuel and the historical-critical editors.** Distinguished two rings and connected one with Sophie and one with Hardenberg. Their terse editorial notes have often been treated as documentary statements despite being reconstructions.

**Albrecht Pohlmann, 2009.** Placed the Sophie portraits within everyday miniature-portrait practice around 1800 and raised the possibility that the watercolor supplied a model for the miniatures.

**Gabriele Rommel, 2012/2025 publication context.** Proposed Senewaldt as the portraitist and documented the related Hardenberg-family brooch/medallion.

**Ludwig Stockinger, 2022.** The most rigorous publicly accessible reconsideration of authenticity, date, provenance, and cultural meaning. His study separates the museum tradition from what the documents actually prove.

### Active questions

1. What metal and portrait support were used?
2. Does the ring bear hallmarks or a maker’s mark?
3. Can the inscription’s date be independently read under magnification?
4. Were the words, date, and three punctuation marks made in one campaign?
5. Does the engraving resemble Hardenberg’s hand, or was it professionally cut?
6. Was the miniature painted by Senewaldt?
7. Is it one of the two pictures mentioned in December 1796?
8. Was an earlier 1795 ring refitted with a 1796 miniature?
9. Did Hardenberg give it to Coelestin Just, Caroline Just, or someone else?
10. What is the correct Just–Liebmann family pathway?
11. Where was the ring during the Second World War?
12. What museum records exist for the DDR period?
13. What conservation treatment preceded its renewed display?
14. What is its internal loan or inventory number?

### Necessary future work

A reliable object catalogue should combine:

- high-resolution recto, verso, profile, and hoop photography;
- dimensions and weight;
- non-destructive metal analysis;
- microscopic examination of the inscription and later scratches;
- pigment and support analysis where safe;
- comparison with the Oberwiederstedt medallion and watercolor;
- complete transcription of the 1925 loan receipt;
- museum registrar files from 1925 onward;
- wartime and postwar storage inventories;
- probate, marriage, and family papers from the Just and Liebmann families;
- any surviving correspondence of Heinrich and Hedwig Liebmann concerning the deposit.

Until such work appears, the ring remains a highly plausible historical relic with a securely documented modern life but an incompletely documented early biography.

### Evidentiary conclusion

The surviving ring is not legendary in the sense of being purely textual: it is a real, repeatedly photographed museum object with a secure custody record from 1925 and a secure published existence from 1916. Its identification with a ring actually owned and worn by Friedrich von Hardenberg is credible and accepted in the specialist tradition, but the present public evidence does not establish an unbroken chain from 1795 or 1796 to 1916. The uncertain date, absence of a commission or early inventory, defective family genealogy, unknown transfer through the Just family, and lack of published scientific examination must remain visible parts of the object’s history.
