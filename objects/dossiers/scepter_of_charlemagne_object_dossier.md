# The Scepter of Charlemagne: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conclusion

The object conventionally called the **Scepter of Charlemagne** is not a surviving Carolingian sceptre and has no documented physical connection to Charlemagne (c. 742–814). The securely identifiable artifact is a late-fourteenth-century French royal sceptre commissioned or substantially refashioned under **Charles V of France** and deposited in the treasury of the abbey of Saint-Denis on **7 May 1380**. Its summit represents Charlemagne and bears his name; this imagery, rather than genuine Carolingian provenance, produced the later title *sceptre de Charlemagne*. The Louvre now catalogues it more accurately as the **Sceptre de Charles V**, inventory **MS 83**.

The artifact is nevertheless not wholly unchanged. Its surviving upper portion is substantially medieval, but its crown, miniature sceptre, animal ornaments, stones, enamel, and shaft have been altered or replaced at different dates. In 1804 Martin-Guillaume Biennais lengthened it with material taken from the separate **1394 cantorial staff of Guillaume de Roquémont**, erasing most of that staff’s inscription in the process. The two objects have since been separated and are catalogued as **MS 83** and **MS 83 BIS**. Thus “the Scepter of Charlemagne” has three overlapping identities:

1. a genuine Valois royal sceptre of the later fourteenth century;
2. an ancien-régime object traditionally, but falsely, attributed to Charlemagne;
3. a composite Napoleonic ceremonial object assembled from two medieval artifacts and subsequently disassembled.

No published radiocarbon, metallographic, isotopic, or comparable laboratory campaign specifically devoted to MS 83 was located. Its dating rests primarily on inventories, representations, stylistic and technical examination, and the history of documented repairs.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Names

The principal names are:

- **French:** *Sceptre de Charles V* (“Scepter of Charles V”), the Louvre’s present title.
- **French traditional:** *Sceptre de Charlemagne* or *sceptre dit de Charlemagne* (“Scepter of Charlemagne” or “sceptre so-called of Charlemagne”).
- **English:** *Scepter/Sceptre of Charles V*; traditionally, *Scepter/Sceptre of Charlemagne*.
- **Latin inscriptional name:** *Karolus Magnus*, literally “Charles the Great,” whence French *Charlemagne*.

French *sceptre* derives through Latin *sceptrum* from Greek *skēptron*, a staff or ruler’s rod. The Academy’s historical dictionary preserves that derivation. The object’s Charlemagne name is iconographic and institutional, not a reliable statement of manufacture or ownership. [Académie française](https://www.dictionnaire-academie.fr/article/A9S0756)

### Present cataloguing and measurements

The Musée du Louvre registers the object as:

- **Title:** *Sceptre de Charles V*
- **Inventory number:** **MS 83**
- **Department:** Département des Objets d’art du Moyen Âge, de la Renaissance et des temps modernes
- **Classification:** sceptre; regalia; goldsmiths’ work
- **Date:** before 1380, possibly from 1364 onward
- **Place of manufacture:** Paris
- **Present dimensions:** height **60 cm**, width **7 cm**
- **Owner:** French state
- **Institution responsible:** Musée du Louvre.  
  [Louvre collections record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010113393)

These measurements describe the presently separated, relatively short object. They must not be confused with:

- an early recorded length of approximately **three feet nine inches**, with a reported weight around **2.2 kg**;
- the approximately six-foot or 1.8-metre configuration produced by lengthening the sceptre for early-modern and Napoleonic ceremony;
- an erroneous modern exhibition-brochure caption giving the object’s present height as **1.86 m**, evidently conflating a ceremonial long configuration with the museum object now measuring 60 cm.  
  [Louvre “Théâtre du pouvoir” brochure](https://www.insei.fr/sites/default/files/www/sites/default/files/medias/brochure-exposition_louvre.pdf)

### Structure

The surviving object consists of four principal superimposed elements held together by an internal rod:

1. a short shaft;
2. a spherical or bulbous historiated knop;
3. an open six-petalled fleur-de-lis corolla;
4. a seated statuette of Charlemagne.

The upper shaft is engraved with fleurs-de-lis and rosette-like knots. The Louvre identifies the shaft as **gilded silver (vermeil)**, while the principal upper components are gold. Recorded materials include:

- gold, formerly enamelled;
- gilded silver;
- a *rubis balais*, now identified mineralogically as spinel;
- shell pearls;
- coloured glass;
- formerly, other precious stones now lost or replaced.  
  [Louvre collections record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010113393)

The metalwork employs casting, engraving, chasing, repoussé, openwork, gilding, stone-setting, and originally enamel over three-dimensional gold. The formerly white-enamelled fleur-de-lis is cited as an early example of enamel applied *en ronde-bosse*. The enamel is now absent. [Panorama de l’art/RMN–Grand Palais](https://panoramadelart.com/analyse/sceptre-de-charles-v)

### Historiated knop

Three repoussé scenes on the knop concern Charlemagne and Saint James the Great. They derive not from Charlemagne’s documented life but from the medieval legendary complex associated with the **Pseudo-Turpin Chronicle**, transmitted as Book IV of the *Liber Sancti Jacobi/Codex Calixtinus*:

1. **Saint James appears to Charlemagne**, directing him toward Spain and Compostela.
2. **The miracle of the flowering lances:** warriors’ lances planted in the earth sprout or blossom, identifying those destined for martyrdom or death.
3. **Saint James rescues Charlemagne’s soul from a demon:** the emperor’s soul is represented as a small nude child while his body lies below.

The figures are identified through attributes: James’s halo, pilgrim’s hat, and mantle; Charlemagne’s crown, beard, sword, and royal bearing. The scenes turn the ruler into warrior, pilgrim, defender of Christianity, and beneficiary of apostolic intercession. [Panorama de l’art/RMN–Grand Palais](https://panoramadelart.com/analyse/sceptre-de-charles-v)

The medallions are bordered with pearls and settings. Precious stones formerly occupying several settings have been replaced with coloured glass. In 1804, imitation green stones replaced diamonds in three central settings and glass replaced three sapphires, according to the reconstruction summarized from the 1991 Saint-Denis treasury catalogue.

### Fleur-de-lis and statuette

The knop supports a three-dimensional six-petalled lily, originally enamelled white. Above it, Charlemagne sits frontally on a throne. He has:

- a bifurcated beard;
- prominent cheekbones;
- deep-set eyes;
- a narrow aquiline nose;
- an imperial crown;
- a globus cruciger in one hand;
- a small sceptre in the other.

The miniature sceptre’s original tip is lost; its present termination is a replacement. The throne originally incorporated two lions and two eagles. Only one animal ornament is securely medieval; Biennais supplied three replacement eagles in 1804, and one of those later disappeared. The crown worn by the statuette is itself a replacement made before 1624. A pearl formerly surmounted it; Biennais substituted a cross in 1804.

### Inscription

Openwork gold letters around the throne base read, in the Louvre’s transcription:

> **SANTUS [sic] KAROLUS MAGNUS ITALIA ROMA GALIA ET [?] ALIA**

A cautious translation is:

> “Saint Charles the Great—Italy, Rome, Gaul, and [?] other [lands].”

*SANTUS* is an erroneous or abbreviated form of *SANCTUS*. The ending is damaged or problematic. An inventory reading recorded from 1534, conventionally expanded as “*Italia, Roma, Galia et Germania*,” cannot be reconciled straightforwardly with the surviving letters. The Louvre therefore leaves part unresolved and notes two possibilities: the sixteenth-century reading was wrong, or the inscription was subsequently altered with unusual skill. No responsible reconstruction should silently restore *Germania*. [Louvre collections record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010113393)

### Current condition and losses

Documented or strongly reported changes include:

- loss of most original precious stones;
- replacement of stones with coloured glass;
- loss of the white enamel from the lily;
- replacement of the statuette’s crown before 1624;
- replacement of the miniature sceptre or its tip;
- replacement of a crowning pearl with a cross;
- replacement of several throne animals;
- partial reconstruction of the shaft;
- repeated interventions in **1722, 1775, 1804, and 1825**;
- temporary incorporation of parts of the Roquémont cantorial staff.

The present object is consequently an authentic medieval regalia component with extensive early-modern and nineteenth-century replacement material—not an untouched fourteenth-century survival.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Documentary origin

The physical object’s secure documentary horizon is the reign of Charles V. His general movable-property inventory, compiled in 1379–1380 under officials including Philippe de Savoisy, Gilles Malet, Jean de Vaudetar, Gabriel Fatinant, and Jean Crété, records a sceptre corresponding closely to the surviving object. Jules Labarte published the relevant entry as no. 3449, pp. 353–354 of his 1879 edition. [Digitized Labarte inventory](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/5f/Inventaire_du_mobilier_de_Charles_V%2C_roi_de_France_%28IA_inventairedumobi00laba%29.pdf)

The Louvre states that Charles V delivered the sceptre to the treasury of Saint-Denis on **7 May 1380**, in preparation for the coronation of his son, the future Charles VI. That transfer is the first secure anchor in the object’s uninterrupted institutional provenance. [Louvre collections record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010113393)

### Date and commission

The defensible date range is **1364–1380**, with **before 7 May 1380** certain. Two reconstructions remain possible:

- **Early manufacture, about 1364:** illuminations in Charles V’s Coronation Book show a related sceptre at or shortly after his 1364 coronation.
- **Later completion or refashioning, about 1370–1380:** the extant historiated summit may have been prepared for Charles VI rather than used by Charles V.

The **Coronation Book of Charles V**, commissioned in 1365 and now British Library **Cotton MS Tiberius B VIII/2**, contains images of the king holding a broadly similar sceptre. The manuscript proves that the visual type existed at Charles V’s court by the mid-1360s. It does not by itself prove that every depicted detail belongs to present MS 83. [British Library catalogue](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/040-001102218)

In the manuscript images, the statuette appears associated with openwork foliage rather than necessarily the exact surviving lily-and-historiated-knop arrangement. This has encouraged the hypothesis that Charles V altered an existing sceptre shortly before his death, replacing an earlier leafy support with the present Charlemagne cycle and enamelled lily. The Louvre cautiously dates the object “before 1380 (1364?).”

### Maker

The medieval maker is not documented securely. The Louvre includes **Hennequin du Vivier** with a question mark. Hennequin was a royal goldsmith active in the relevant court environment, and attribution is stylistically and circumstantially plausible, not documentary certainty. The craftsmanship is Parisian and closely related to court sculpture and precious-metal work of Charles V’s circle.

No contract, payment account, quarry or mine provenance, or reliably stated original cost has been located. Assertions that Hennequin certainly made the whole sceptre should therefore be labelled attribution, not fact.

### Political and devotional program

The choice of Charlemagne was intentional Valois dynastic imagery. Charles V and his heir Charles VI shared the name *Carolus* with the emperor. The new Valois line faced Plantagenet claims during the Hundred Years’ War and cultivated images of Carolingian descent, Christian empire, learned kingship, and divine protection. The sceptre placed a miniature Charlemagne literally above the French lily and in the hand of each crowned king.

This was not a neutral antiquarian commemoration. It was a material assertion that Valois government stood in continuity with an idealized Christian emperor. The Louvre describes the imagery as a manifesto of legitimacy. [Panorama de l’art/RMN–Grand Palais](https://panoramadelart.com/analyse/sceptre-de-charles-v)

### Traditional origin versus physical origin

| Claim | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|
| Charlemagne owned or used this sceptre | Ancien-régime tradition; contradicted by the object’s Gothic style and fourteenth-century documentation |
| Charles V commissioned or refashioned it | Strong documentary and institutional conclusion |
| It may have appeared in Charles V’s 1364 coronation | Possible, based on the Coronation Book; identity not conclusive |
| It was prepared in its documented form for Charles VI | Strong Louvre interpretation, supported by the 1380 inventory and transfer |
| Hennequin du Vivier made it | Plausible attribution, not securely documented |

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 1364–1380: royal possession

The object originated in the treasury or workshop environment of Charles V. Whether the present upper work was completed for Charles V’s own use in 1364, or subsequently for Charles VI, remains unresolved.

### 7 May 1380: transfer to Saint-Denis

Charles V delivered the sceptre with other coronation objects to the **abbey of Saint-Denis**, custodian of French regalia. This was not an ordinary personal gift in the modern sense: Saint-Denis held the objects institutionally for reuse in royal sacres. The sceptre remained royal ceremonial property embedded within an ecclesiastical treasury.

### 1380: coronation of Charles VI

Charles VI was crowned at Reims on 4 November 1380. Museum scholarship identifies this as the first secure coronation use of the present sceptre. Thereafter it travelled from Saint-Denis to coronations and was returned to the abbey’s treasury.

### Fifteenth and sixteenth centuries

The sceptre is generally reported as having served at the coronations of French kings after 1380, with two important exceptions:

- **Charles VII, 1429:** the sceptre’s use is not established. The war and political fragmentation disrupted normal custody and ceremonial arrangements.
- **Henry IV, 1594:** Saint-Denis and its treasury were inaccessible because Paris was controlled by the Catholic League; new regalia were made for the coronation at Chartres.

“Used at every coronation except these two” is a useful institutional summary, but individual physical handling should be asserted only where inventories, ceremonial accounts, or images attest it.

The 1534 Saint-Denis inventory described the object and supplied the now-questioned reading ending in *Germania*. By this time the attribution to “Saint Charlemagne” was institutionalized.

### Seventeenth century

By 1624 the statuette’s crown had already been replaced. During the Bourbon period the sceptre was treated as one of the **Honneurs de Charlemagne**, a larger group including the supposed crown, sword, spurs, and other insignia traditionally linked with the emperor.

A seventeenth-century description quoted by Frédéric Masson calls it the gold sceptre of Saint Charlemagne, describing the saint seated with lions and eagles, crown, sceptre, globe, white-enamelled lily, gold knop, and precious stones. [Masson, *Le Sacre et le couronnement de Napoléon*](https://www.mediterranee-antique.fr/Fichiers_PdF/MNO/Masson/Sacre.pdf)

Michel Félibien’s 1706 history and engravings of the Saint-Denis treasury became major witnesses for the regalia’s pre-Revolutionary appearance and arrangement. The sceptre was kept in one of the treasury armoires and left Saint-Denis for sacres under formal ecclesiastical custody. [Michel Félibien, 1706](https://books.google.com/books?hl=fr&id=b92MaD_LHGEC)

The sceptre was lengthened before or for Bourbon ceremonial use. Frédéric Masson states that until Louis XIV’s coronation its length was comparable to the short hand of justice, after which it was mounted on a plain gold shaft about six feet high. That long shaft later disappeared.

### 1722: Louis XV

The object was repaired for Louis XV’s coronation on 25 October 1722. It was carried or held in the ceremony and appears in coronation imagery, although portraits must be used cautiously: artists could substitute, invert, enlarge, or schematize regalia.

### 1775: Louis XVI

For Louis XVI’s coronation, the goldsmith Auguste and the furnisher Ravoisier examined and repaired the coronation objects, including this sceptre. A contemporary notice recorded that the crown, sceptre, hand of justice, sword, clasp, spurs, and ceremonial book were taken from Saint-Denis for the event. [Bachaumont, *Mémoires secrets*, 1775](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3ADe_Bachaumont_-_M%C3%A9moires_secrets%2C_T8%2C_Adamsohn.djvu/88)

### 1792–1795: revolutionary seizure

The Revolution dissolved the old custodial system and exposed the Saint-Denis treasury to confiscation, dispersal, and melting. Most medieval French coronation regalia were destroyed or stripped for materials. This sceptre survived because commissioners reserved selected objects for their historic or artistic interest.

The Louvre record says that it was transferred to the Museum on **5 December 1795**, while citing a procès-verbal dated **15 Frimaire Year II**. That Republican-calendar date corresponds to **5 December 1793**, not 1795. A Louvre exhibition brochure likewise says the deposit occurred in 1793. The museum’s record therefore contains an internal chronological inconsistency. The safest formulation is:

- revolutionary seizure from Saint-Denis;
- documentary museum handover on 15 Frimaire Year II/5 December 1793;
- later Louvre cataloguing gives 5 December 1795, apparently through conversion or transcription error.

The object was numbered **71** in the transfer documentation. The mechanism was confiscation by the revolutionary state, not voluntary donation.

### 1804: Napoleonic reconstruction

For Napoleon’s coronation, the surviving sceptre was withdrawn and repaired by the imperial goldsmith **Martin-Guillaume Biennais**. Work attributed to him includes:

- reconstructing part of the shaft;
- replacing the pearl above Charlemagne’s crown with a cross;
- supplying three eagles;
- replacing gems with imitation stones or glass;
- probably removing deteriorated white enamel from the lily;
- joining the sceptre to portions of Guillaume de Roquémont’s 1394 cantorial staff;
- reportedly charging **1,800 francs**.

The Roquémont staff was a distinct liturgical object of gilded silver. Its inscription named donor Guillaume de Roquémont, Prior Guillaume Barraut, and Abbot Guy de Monceau and dated it **1394**. Biennais filed away most of that inscription when adapting the staff as an extension. The Louvre now acknowledges this intervention explicitly. [Louvre record for MS 83 BIS](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark:/53355/cl010117028)

The surviving transcription includes:

> *D’argent fist faire ce baston … Car pour loyauté maintenir / Le doit chantre en sa main tenir … L’an M.CCC et quatre vingt / Et quatorze…*

In sense:

> “He had this staff made of silver … for maintaining proper order the cantor must hold it in his hand … in the year 1394.”

This inscription exposes a major nineteenth-century misunderstanding. Antiquaries once concluded that the entire “Charlemagne sceptre” was merely a cantor’s staff. The more precise modern conclusion is that the royal sceptre and cantorial staff were originally separate medieval objects temporarily made into a composite in 1804.

At Napoleon’s coronation in Notre-Dame on **2 December 1804**, Marshal **Catherine-Dominique de Pérignon** carried the reconstructed old sceptre among the *Honneurs de Charlemagne*. Napoleon himself used a distinct newly created set, the *Honneurs de l’Empereur*. Descriptions or paintings showing Napoleon personally holding “Charlemagne’s sceptre” must therefore be distinguished from the documented procession. [Masson](https://www.mediterranee-antique.fr/Fichiers_PdF/MNO/Masson/Sacre.pdf)

### 1804–1816: Notre-Dame de Paris

The Louvre records the sceptre as displayed in the treasury of Notre-Dame de Paris between 1804 and 1816. This was exhibition and ceremonial custody, not a transfer of state ownership.

### 1816–1825: state custody and restoration

After removal from Notre-Dame, the regalia returned to state storage. The sceptre was repaired again for the coronation of **Charles X**, held at Reims on 29 May 1825.

### 1825: last coronation use

Charles X’s sacre was the object’s last documented use in a French coronation. The ceremony revived ancien-régime forms while using a mixture of medieval survivals, nineteenth-century restorations, and new objects. The sceptre’s survival gave it unusual ceremonial authority because so much of the original treasury had been destroyed.

### 1848–1872

During the Revolution of 1848 the sceptre was sheltered at the **Ministry of Finance**. It returned to the Louvre in **1852** and was exhibited in the **Musée des Souverains** from 1852 until that museum’s closure in 1872. [Louvre collections record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010113393)

### Late nineteenth century to present

The sceptre passed into the Louvre’s permanent decorative-arts collections. It appeared in the Galerie d’Apollon and in major exhibitions, including:

- *Le trésor de Saint-Denis* (Louvre, 1991);
- *Paris 1400* (Louvre, 2004);
- *Le roi et son image* (Archives nationales, 2007);
- *Théâtre du pouvoir* (Louvre, 2017–2018);
- *Hyacinthe Rigaud ou le portrait Soleil* (Versailles, 2020–2021).

Its custody since the nineteenth century is French state ownership administered by the Louvre.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Was it Charlemagne’s?

No. The surviving object is approximately five and a half centuries later than Charlemagne. Its Gothic figure style, enamel technique, Valois fleurs-de-lis, Charles V inventory, and fourteenth-century visual context exclude Carolingian manufacture.

The name “Scepter of Charlemagne” is an **officially maintained ancien-régime attribution** that became a ceremonial truth: it mattered because institutions and kings treated the object as Charlemagne’s, not because the attribution was historically sound.

### Is MS 83 the sceptre described in 1380?

The evidence is strong but not absolute in every component. Points supporting continuity include:

- correspondence between the 1380 description and the surviving shaft, knop, stones, narrative scenes, lily, and Charlemagne statuette;
- continuous Saint-Denis institutional custody;
- later inventories and representations;
- documented rather than concealed modern repairs.

The main uncertainty concerns whether the sceptre illustrated in 1364–1365 is the same artifact in an earlier state or a closely related predecessor.

### Is it actually the Roquémont cantorial staff?

Not as a whole. Pierre Révoil and nineteenth-century writers discovered the cantorial inscription beneath the covering of the long shaft and concluded that the supposed sceptre was a choir staff. Later object-based study separated the histories:

- **MS 83:** royal sceptre summit and short shaft, before 1380;
- **MS 83 BIS:** surviving portions of Guillaume de Roquémont’s cantorial staff, 1394;
- **1804 composite:** MS 83 lengthened using part of MS 83 BIS and additional work by Biennais.

The Louvre now catalogues both separately. The cantorial staff presently measures **53 cm**; its twisted section is 24.2 cm, and its width between two knots is recorded as 33.5 cm. [Louvre MS 83 BIS](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark:/53355/cl010117028)

### Inscription dispute

The final geographical term in the throne inscription remains unresolved. “Germany” was read in 1534 and repeated for centuries. The surviving openwork letters do not permit that reading. Either:

- the sixteenth-century transcriber normalized or misunderstood the inscription;
- the base was altered afterward;
- letters were lost, rearranged, or replaced.

No published examination cited by the Louvre resolves the issue.

### Scientific examination

The Louvre identifies spinel rather than “ruby” and distinguishes shell pearl from other pearl types, indicating modern materials identification. Yet no dedicated published technical report was found giving:

- alloy percentages;
- solder chronology;
- X-radiography;
- enamel composition;
- gem-by-gem spectroscopy;
- provenance of the gold or silver;
- microscopic differentiation of medieval and nineteenth-century tooling.

The absence of a located publication is not proof that conservation laboratories have never examined the object. It means that no such full report can presently be cited here.

### Authority to authenticate

Authority moved through several regimes:

- Charles V’s household and treasury officials identified and inventoried it;
- the abbey of Saint-Denis maintained its sacred-dynastic identity;
- royal masters of ceremonies and goldsmiths certified it for use;
- revolutionary commissioners decided it was historically worth preserving;
- Napoleon’s administration reclassified it as an “Honor of Charlemagne” while materially remaking it;
- nineteenth-century antiquaries challenged the legend through inscriptions and style;
- the Louvre now separates traditional title, material history, and scholarly attribution.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

The sceptre’s practical purpose was coronation investiture. In the developed French rite, after anointing and vesting, the archbishop placed the sceptre in the king’s **right hand** and the rod or hand of justice in his left. The BnF summarizes the sceptre as the emblem of royal power and the hand of justice as the emblem of the ruler’s judicial function. [BnF Essentiels](https://essentiels.bnf.fr/fr/image-explorer/a35fa2f7-17f0-47e7-8b74-aa98282580f8-sacre-un-rite-passage)

The object operated on several symbolic levels:

- **Royal authority:** the staff materialized the king’s capacity to command.
- **Moral rectitude:** the “straight rod” traditionally signified correction of wrongdoing and just government.
- **Dynastic continuity:** each king received an object represented as having passed from Charlemagne through his predecessors.
- **Sacral kingship:** the figure was called *Sanctus Karolus* and placed within a rite of anointing and liturgy.
- **Imperial ambition:** Charlemagne’s orb and crown evoked universal Christian rule.
- **French sovereignty:** the emperor rises from a fleur-de-lis, visually incorporating Carolingian empire into Capetian-Valois France.
- **Apostolic sanction:** Saint James commands and finally saves Charlemagne in the knop’s narrative.

After the ceremony the object did not remain the king’s private possession. It returned to Saint-Denis, reinforcing the abbey’s function as permanent guardian of the monarchy’s material continuity.

No special healing power, curse, oath-taking practice, or rule that only the king could physically touch it is securely documented. Numerous abbots, monks, goldsmiths, commissioners, marshals, conservators, and museum staff necessarily handled it. Any claim of an absolute touch taboo would conflict with its documented custodial history.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### Legend 1: Charlemagne owned the sceptre

**Tier:** late medieval/early-modern institutional tradition; historically disproved as literal provenance.

By the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries Saint-Denis descriptions called it the sceptre of “Saint Charlemagne, king of France and emperor.” Its image, inscription, and presence among other pseudo-Carolingian regalia made the attribution persuasive within ceremonial culture.

The tradition served several interests:

- Saint-Denis gained prestige as guardian of the founding emperor’s relic-like regalia.
- French kings received an apparently ancient warrant of sovereignty.
- Valois and Bourbon rulers could claim continuity across dynastic ruptures.
- Napoleon could appropriate both monarchy and empire without admitting dependence on either alone.

The factual core is not ownership by Charlemagne but deliberate fourteenth-century construction of Carolingian memory.

### Legend 2: Charlemagne’s Spanish expedition and Compostela

**Tier:** twelfth-century legendary source reused in fourteenth-century court art.

The Pseudo-Turpin narrative claims that Saint James appeared to Charlemagne and ordered him to free the apostle’s Spanish shrine. The historical Charlemagne did campaign in Iberia in 778, but the fully developed Compostela mission, flowering lances, and supernatural battle over his soul are literary and devotional elaborations. Their presence on the sceptre records belief and political iconography, not documentary biography.

### Legend 3: The flowering lances

**Tier:** medieval miracle narrative.

Charlemagne’s warriors plant their lances; some bloom during the night, signifying divine selection. The sceptre translates this miracle into precious metal. Its function is theological: military death under the divinely commanded king becomes martyrdom rather than defeat.

### Legend 4: Saint James saves Charlemagne’s soul

**Tier:** medieval salvific legend.

At the emperor’s death a demon attempts to claim his soul. Saint James intervenes, and the soul is rescued. The image promises apostolic protection to a ruler who advances the saint’s cause. On a coronation sceptre, it also warns that kingship is judged beyond death.

### Legend 5: the whole sceptre was secretly a cantor’s staff

**Tier:** nineteenth-century antiquarian error based on a genuine discovery.

The concealed 1394 inscription proved that the long lower extension was a cantorial staff. Early interpreters generalized the finding to the whole sceptre. Modern cataloguing demonstrates a composite: the royal summit predates the cantorial staff, which was only joined to it in 1804.

### Legend 6: Napoleon personally wielded Charlemagne’s sceptre

**Tier:** persistent visual and popular conflation.

Napoleon’s portraits often place Carolingian-looking regalia in his hands. Ceremonial evidence distinguishes the old *Honneurs de Charlemagne*, carried by marshals, from Napoleon’s own newly made *Honneurs de l’Empereur*. Pérignon carried the old sceptre. Napoleon held the new imperial sceptre and hand of justice during portions of the rite. The image of direct physical succession from Charlemagne was propaganda more than literal choreography.

## 8. Current Status and Location

As of the Louvre’s current online catalogue:

- **Object:** *Sceptre de Charles V*
- **Inventory:** MS 83
- **Owner:** French state
- **Custodian:** Musée du Louvre, Department of Decorative Arts
- **Recorded present display status:** **not on display**
- **City:** Paris
- **Condition:** surviving medieval upper work with extensive replacements and lost enamel/stones.  
  [Louvre collections record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010113393)

The related cantorial-staff fragment:

- **Object:** *Bâton de Guillaume de Roquémont*
- **Inventory:** MS 83 BIS
- **Recorded display:** Richelieu wing, Level 1, Room 504, case associated with the Scepter of Charles V.  
  [Louvre MS 83 BIS](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark:/53355/cl010117028)

These two catalogue statements create an important practical distinction: the Roquémont fragment may be displayed while MS 83 itself is recorded as off view. Visitors should consult the Louvre immediately before travel because gallery rotations change.

No current ownership, restitution, or repatriation proceeding was located. The transfer to the museum originated in revolutionary confiscation from a French religious institution, but the present legal owner is the French state. Its status in a national collection normally makes deaccession tightly restricted under French public-domain law.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Royal and imperial portraiture

The sceptre’s image entered official portraiture even when the physical object was not present before the sitter. Its recognizable long shaft and Charlemagne finial appear or are evoked in images of French kings, including representations associated with Louis XIII and Louis XV.

Portraits are not straightforward records of handling. Hyacinthe Rigaud’s famous state image of Louis XIV, for example, uses the shorter sceptre made for Henry IV rather than MS 83 as the king’s support. Modern institutional commentary explicitly warns against identifying every painted royal staff as the Scepter of Charlemagne. [Château de Versailles](https://www.chateauversailles.fr/sites/default/files/presse/documents/ressource-pedagogique_commentaire-louis_xiv_par_hyacinthe_rigaud.pdf)

### Napoleonic imagery

Napoleon transformed the object from French royal regalia into an emblem of renewed western empire. The Scepter of Charles V and the hand of justice were crossed behind the imperial shield in heraldic compositions. The association continued under Napoleon III; a surviving seal description places the shield on an ermine mantle with the sceptre and hand of justice in saltire. [SIGILLA/IRHT-CNRS](https://sigilla.irht.cnrs.fr/A.php/241019)

Ingres’s *Napoleon I on His Imperial Throne* and related images helped establish a popular visual synthesis in which Napoleon appears as heir simultaneously to Rome, Charlemagne, and the French monarchy. That synthesis should not be mistaken for an exact inventory of the objects he personally held during the coronation.

### Charlemagne’s later iconography

The fourteenth-century sceptre influenced later images of Charlemagne himself. Jean du Tillet’s sixteenth-century *Recueil des rois de France* shows the emperor with sceptre and globe; the object may have supplied a model. Later representations routinely placed medieval or early-modern French regalia retroactively in the hands of the ninth-century emperor. [Utpictura18](https://utpictura18.univ-amu.fr/notice/20239-charlemagne-jean-tillet-recueil-rois-france-fol-36v)

The Rochet brothers’ nineteenth-century equestrian monument of Charlemagne outside Notre-Dame likewise uses anachronistic regalia, including a sceptre modeled on the object made for Charles V. The anachronism is programmatic: modern France visualizes Charlemagne through the objects later French monarchy claimed from him.

### Photography, reproductions, and exhibitions

Léon Vidal photographed the sceptre for Paul Dalloz’s *Trésor artistique de la France* in 1878. The Musée d’Orsay preserves this early photographic work under a title that still calls the object a ninth-century sceptre, illustrating how institutional titles can preserve obsolete attributions. [Musée d’Orsay](https://www.musee-orsay.fr/it/opere/sceptre-leffigie-de-charlemagne-ixeme-siecle-60520)

The object has since been reproduced in museum postcards, educational media, exhibition catalogues, commercial replicas, and histories of French regalia. Its public image commonly exaggerates three propositions:

- that it belonged to Charlemagne;
- that it survives wholly intact;
- that Napoleon personally used it as his own sceptre.

All three require correction.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Primary documentary base

The object is unusually well documented for medieval regalia because it appears across several evidence classes:

- Charles V’s 1379–1380 movable-property inventory;
- the 7 May 1380 Saint-Denis transfer;
- Charles V’s illustrated Coronation Book;
- the Saint-Denis inventories of 1534 and 1634;
- early-modern treasury guides and descriptions;
- Michel Félibien’s 1706 text and engravings;
- coronation accounts and repair records of 1722 and 1775;
- revolutionary confiscation records;
- Napoleonic ceremonial and goldsmith records;
- 1825 restoration records;
- nineteenth-century museum catalogues and antiquarian studies.

The Louvre cites the relevant revolutionary archival record as Archives nationales **20144787/4**, an extract from the procès-verbal of 15 Frimaire Year II.

### Major studies

Important publications listed in the Louvre bibliography include:

- Jules Labarte, *Inventaire du mobilier de Charles V, roi de France* (1879), pp. 353–354, no. 3449.
- Henry Barbet de Jouy, *Notice des antiquités… composant le musée des Souverains* (1868), pp. 69–74.
- Alfred Darcel and Émile Molinier, Louvre catalogue of enamels and goldsmiths’ work (1891), no. D 943.
- Blaise de Montesquiou-Fezensac and Danielle Gaborit-Chopin, *Le Trésor de Saint-Denis*, volumes I–III (1973–1977).
- *Regalia: Les instruments du Sacre des Rois de France; les “Honneurs de Charlemagne”* (Louvre exhibition catalogue, 1987).
- Daniel Alcouffe, ed., *Le trésor de Saint-Denis* (Louvre exhibition catalogue, 1991), no. 57.
- Elisabeth Taburet-Delahaye, ed., *Paris 1400* (2004), no. 6.
- Michele Tomasi, “Charlemagne et saint Jacques à la cour du roi de France: sur l’iconographie du sceptre de Charles V” (2017).

The Louvre’s full bibliography is the best consolidated guide to earlier inventory editions, museum catalogues, exhibition records, iconographic studies, and gemmological discussion. [Louvre collections record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010113393)

### Nineteenth-century controversy

An 1845 report, repeated in the *Gazette archéologique* around 1889–1890, describes Révoil’s discovery of the Gothic verse beneath velvet on the shaft. Because the verse said the cantor should hold the staff, some antiquaries declared the venerated sceptre an ecclesiastical baton. [*Gazette archéologique*](https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/gazette_archeologique1889/0095/text_ocr)

This was partly corrective and partly mistaken. It demolished belief in a wholly Carolingian object but conflated two components. Twentieth-century catalogue work, particularly the Saint-Denis corpus, reconstructed the 1804 joining of the royal sceptre and Roquémont staff.

### Current debates and unresolved questions

1. **1364 or later?** Did Charles V hold the present sceptre in 1364, or does the Coronation Book show a precursor?
2. **Single manufacture or refashioning?** Was the historiated Charlemagne summit conceived as one project, or added to an existing royal staff?
3. **Maker:** Is attribution to Hennequin du Vivier sustainable beyond circumstantial and stylistic evidence?
4. **Inscription:** What did the final geographical phrase originally say?
5. **Repair chronology:** Which individual stones, animals, structural pieces, and letter forms belong to each intervention?
6. **Original length:** How should the measurements in medieval inventories be reconciled with the present 60 cm and later long configurations?
7. **Scientific publication:** Can technical imaging and alloy/enamel analysis provide a component-by-component chronology?

Popular literature continues to repeat the discredited ninth-century attribution and sometimes identifies the entire object as a disguised cantorial staff. Neither position reflects the present catalogue evidence.

The Louvre catalogue is authoritative for present cataloguing, materials, dimensions, ownership, and institutional provenance, but its museum-transfer date conflicts with the Republican-calendar date it cites. Modern educational brochures sometimes conflate the long ceremonial form with the present 60 cm object. Wikipedia-derived summaries are useful for locating older bibliography but were not treated as primary authority where museum or archival records were available.
