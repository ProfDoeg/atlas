# "The Light of the World" (Holman Hunt): Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

“The Light of the World” names both a composition and three autograph oil paintings associated with William Holman Hunt:

1. **Keble College version**, Oxford: the first and principal version, painted approximately 1851–1853/54 and exhibited in 1854.
2. **Manchester version**, Manchester Art Gallery: a smaller autograph version, worked on approximately 1851–1856.
3. **St Paul’s version**, London: a monumental late replica, begun about 1899/1900 and completed in 1904 with substantial assistance from Edward Robert Hughes.

This dossier tracks the **Keble painting as the original object**, but reconstructs the other two wherever the name, image, custody history, or public legend would otherwise be ambiguous. They are separate physical objects, not successive restorations of one canvas.

Evidence is labeled where necessary:

- **Documented:** supported by contemporary records, catalogues, legal proceedings, or institutional documentation.
- **Artist’s testimony:** Hunt’s retrospective account, valuable but not automatically contemporary or impartial evidence.
- **Institutional tradition:** maintained by the current custodian but sometimes simplified or internally inconsistent.
- **Scholarly inference:** reconstruction by modern historians from incomplete evidence.
- **Unverified or disputed:** reported but not securely demonstrated by the consulted documentation.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Identity and names

**English title:** *The Light of the World*.

The title invokes Christ’s declaration in John 8:12, “I am the light of the world,” but the represented action comes principally from Revelation 3:20: Christ stands outside a closed door and knocks. The two biblical texts are therefore complementary rather than alternative titles.

Common translations include:

- French: *La Lumière du monde*
- Spanish: *La luz del mundo*
- Portuguese: *A Luz do Mundo*
- Italian: *La luce del mondo*
- Russian: *Светоч мира* or *Свет мира*, depending on translator

No documentary evidence found in this research suggests that Hunt assigned an authoritative non-English title.

### 1.2 Object class and composition

Each version is an allegorical Christian oil painting showing a nearly life-size or reduced figure of Christ standing at night before a long-closed wooden door. Christ raises his right hand to knock and holds an elaborate lantern in his left. He wears:

- a white robe;
- a richly ornamented priestly or liturgical garment;
- a red mantle or cloak;
- a jeweled breast ornament;
- a crown incorporating thorns;
- a luminous halo or aureole.

The door has no exterior handle. Its boards, ironwork, and threshold are overgrown by ivy, brambles, and weeds. Fallen fruit lies in the foreground. Trees and a star or dawn light appear beyond.

The absence of an exterior handle is not an accidental omission: in the established interpretation, the door represents the human soul or “obstinately shut mind,” which must be opened from within.

### 1.3 Keble College version

**Date:** usually catalogued 1851–1853 or 1851–1854. Keble calls it the original painted in 1853; the Royal Academy exhibited it in 1854.

**Maker:** William Holman Hunt (1827–1910).

**Medium/support:** oil on canvas. A specialist description records “oil on canvas over panel,” apparently indicating that the canvas was mounted or supported on panel; the date and precise conservation history of that mounting are not established in the accessible sources.

**Format:** tall, narrow composition with an arched top.

**Dimensions:** published measurements vary slightly:

- approximately 49⅜ × 23½ inches (125.4 × 59.7 cm);
- another record gives approximately 121.9 × 61 cm.

The discrepancy may reflect differing measuring conventions, image versus support, or inaccurate secondary transcription. No current Keble technical catalogue resolving it was located.

**Inscription:** the lower frame or display includes Revelation 3:20, in an English form substantially equivalent to:

> Behold I stand at the door and knock. If any man hear my voice and open the door I will come in to him and will sup with him and he with me.

The visible composition is signed at lower right, commonly transcribed “W. Holman Hunt.”

Some descriptions report the Latin words **“Me non praetermisso, Domine!”** in the gilded spandrels, translated approximately “Do not pass me by, Lord.” That reading is repeated in continental and popular sources but was not confirmed in a current technical catalogue from Keble; it should therefore be treated cautiously.

**Materials and technique:** no object-specific published pigment analysis was found. Hunt’s documented general practice included a brilliant white ground, fine sable-brush work, oil and varnish/resin mixtures, and repeated glazing intended to produce a jewel-like or translucent luminosity. Scientific study of other Hunt paintings and surviving palettes has identified lead white, vermilion, madder lakes, cadmium yellow, chromium and emerald greens, natural ultramarine, Prussian and cobalt blues, earth pigments, poppy-seed and linseed oils, and pine resin. These findings should **not** be converted into a pigment list for this painting without direct sampling.

### 1.4 Manchester version

**Date:** approximately 1851–1856; often described as a second, smaller autograph version.

**Maker:** William Holman Hunt.

**Medium:** oil on canvas.

**Dimensions:** 49.8 × 26.1 cm; framed dimensions approximately 77 × 54 cm.

**Accession number:** **1912.53**.

**Differences:** smaller scale; variations in Christ’s gaze and the disposition of the red cloak; details differ from both the Keble and St Paul’s paintings.

The Manchester version is not simply a modern workshop copy. It was painted by Hunt during or shortly after the original campaign and later served as a compositional point of reference for the monumental replica.

### 1.5 St Paul’s version

**Date:** begun about 1899 or 1900; completed 1904.

**Makers:** William Holman Hunt, with substantial assistance from Edward Robert Hughes (1851–1914).

**Institutional reference:** St Paul’s Cathedral **Ref. No. 8190**.

**Medium:** oil painting on a very large canvas in an elaborate gilded frame. Exact verified image dimensions were not published in the accessible cathedral record. Contemporary and later accounts describe the framed assembly as weighing just under one ton, but this figure requires confirmation from a technical or installation record.

**Frame:** Renaissance-inspired carved, modeled, gessoed, and gilded woodwork. St Paul’s attributes its making to **Hilda Hewlett** and an otherwise incompletely identified **Miss Smith**. Hewlett recalled the difficulty of translating Hunt’s wishes while his vision was failing and his sketches had become imprecise.

**Differences from the original:** the scale is monumental; Christ’s gaze, drapery, light effects, and halo were revised. In the late version the halo is generally described as a luminous ring rather than the more solid disc-like form of the earlier composition.

**Condition:** conserved and repaired in 2024, including treatment of both painting and frame, then relocated within St Paul’s.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Conception

Hunt later connected the picture with a period of religious uncertainty and with Revelation 3:20. He claimed that the verse supplied the organizing image and that he painted under what he regarded, despite his own unworthiness, as a “divine command,” rather than because the scene was merely a promising subject.

That is **artist’s retrospective testimony**, not proof of supernatural origin. It does establish how Hunt wanted the work’s genesis understood.

The title itself evokes John 8:12. The synthesis produced an image in which Christ is simultaneously:

- the light of salvation;
- the bearer of the lantern or word of conscience;
- the visitor seeking admission;
- king, priest, and suffering redeemer.

### 2.2 Place and manner of painting

Hunt painted the vegetation and nocturnal setting from nature, working at night in an orchard associated with Worcester Park Farm near Ewell, Surrey. Early biographical accounts describe a temporary hut and working hours extending from about 9 p.m. until 5 a.m., by moonlight and, when necessary, candlelight.

Other retellings place part of the work in or near the Oxford University Press garden, probably reflecting Hunt’s stays with Thomas and Martha Combe. The evidence supports more than one working location, but does not justify conflating them.

The figure and finishing work were completed in the studio; one early account states that the picture was finished in John Everett Millais’s studio.

Keble now states that Hunt could not perfect the dawn until he saw an appropriate sunrise at Bethlehem. This creates a chronological problem: the original was exhibited in London in 1854 while Hunt was already in the Holy Land, and John Ruskin wrote publicly about the finished painting on 5 May 1854. A later Holy Land observation therefore cannot straightforwardly explain the completion of the exhibited original. It may relate to subsequent adjustments, a later version, or institutional compression of Hunt’s broader search for authentic eastern light. The claim should not be repeated as settled chronology.

### 2.3 Models

Hunt’s account indicates that Christ’s head was composite rather than a portrait of one sitter. He referred to:

- a clay model or cast;
- his father;
- John Everett Millais;
- John Capper;
- covert observation of Thomas Carlyle;
- sculpted or painted “departed heroes”;
- Christina Rossetti, who reportedly sat after Hunt believed he had already secured the head’s masculine structure.

Popular claims naming one exclusive “model for Christ” therefore oversimplify a cumulative construction.

### 2.4 Earliest secure public attestation

The earliest unambiguous public documentary milestone is the **Royal Academy exhibition of 1854**, where *The Light of the World* appeared with Hunt’s *The Awakening Conscience*.

On 5 May 1854, while Hunt was abroad, John Ruskin published a letter in *The Times* defending the painting. Ruskin reported watching viewers dismiss it as absurd because Christ carried a lantern, then supplied a detailed symbolic reading. His letter is crucial contemporary evidence for the object’s appearance, title, exhibition, early reception, and rapidly developing interpretive apparatus.

### 2.5 Manufacture of the Manchester version

The small Manchester painting was worked on between approximately 1851 and 1856. The overlapping dates indicate that the first two versions were not separated by the half-century dividing them from the St Paul’s replica. Published sources do not disclose a secure original commission, first purchaser, or complete pre-1912 custody chain for the Manchester canvas. That gap should remain explicit.

### 2.6 Manufacture of the St Paul’s version

Work began around 1899/1900. Hunt’s motives, as reconstructed from his writings and later scholarship, included:

- dissatisfaction with the physical treatment and accessibility of the Keble original;
- a desire to recover or clarify details obscured by deterioration;
- a desire to create a public, monumental culmination of the image;
- the commercial and devotional power already demonstrated by reproductions.

Hunt’s eyesight was failing from glaucoma and age. Edward Robert Hughes, his trusted studio assistant, undertook substantial execution. The precise division of labor remains a connoisseurial and documentary question. The object was nevertheless issued and accepted under Hunt’s name, with Hughes’s participation increasingly acknowledged by scholarship and the cathedral.

Christie’s documentation for a Hughes watercolor relates it directly to this process and notes that Hughes continued altering aspects of the composition after 1904, including the halo. That evidence complicates any claim that the St Paul’s painting was entirely finished by Hunt alone at a single moment.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 Keble original

#### 1851–1854: Hunt’s custody

The painting remained in Hunt’s possession during manufacture. It was transported to and exhibited at the Royal Academy in London in 1854.

#### 1854: sale to Thomas Combe

**Documented:** Thomas Combe, superintendent or Printer to Oxford University Press and a patron sympathetic to the Pre-Raphaelites and Oxford Movement, acquired the painting for **400 guineas**. Ruskin recorded the price in his diary; the figure was repeated in Hunt’s early biography and the *Dictionary of National Biography*.

This was a major price in Hunt’s early career.

#### 1854–1872: Combe custody

The painting was held by Thomas Combe in Oxford. Hunt’s relationship with Thomas and Martha Combe gave him continued access to the work and to their patronage network.

#### 1872: offer or gift to Keble College

After Thomas Combe’s death, Martha Combe offered or gave the painting to Keble College. The institutional account dates the offer to 1872.

The transfer carried an expectation that it would be associated with the college chapel, but this did not produce immediate installation there. Architect William Butterfield and benefactor William Gibbs opposed or resisted placing it in Butterfield’s chapel scheme. The disagreement is documented in Keble’s history and demonstrates that acceptance by the college did not mean agreement about liturgical suitability.

#### 1872–1878: imperfectly documented interim

The exact rooms in which the picture was kept immediately after the gift are not securely reconstructed in the online sources consulted.

#### 1878–1890s: Keble Library

When the college library opened in 1878, the painting was installed there. It was reportedly hung above or near hot-water pipes.

Hunt later complained that heat and inadequate care had injured the painting. Sources repeat that he repaired or repainted damaged passages. The accessible record does not provide a dated conservation report identifying precisely which layers are Hunt’s later intervention, whether the canvas was lined or mounted at this stage, or how much original paint was affected.

Keble also charged members of the public to see the work. Hunt regarded the conditions and restricted or monetized access as contrary to his intentions. These grievances contributed to his decision to make the monumental replica.

#### 1892–1895: purpose-built side chapel

Martha Combe died in 1891. Accounts state that she left **£3,000** toward a side chapel for the painting. Architect J. T. Micklethwaite created the addition in the south transept area, involving relocation or elevation of the organ. Construction is generally dated 1892–1895.

The painting was moved from the library into the side chapel, where it remains. This ended the principal custody controversy, although not Hunt’s anxiety about the earlier damage.

#### Present

The original remains owned or controlled by Keble College, University of Oxford, in the side chapel of Keble Chapel. It is publicly viewable when the chapel is open; a wall control illuminates it. The side chapel is not wheelchair accessible.

No theft, wartime evacuation, deaccession, restitution claim, or contested title was identified in the consulted record.

### 3.2 Manchester version

#### 1851–1856: manufacture

Held by Hunt during its extended execution.

#### 1856–1912: provenance gap

The accessible Manchester and Art UK records consulted for this dossier do not provide the complete chain of private custody or the identity and mechanism by which the gallery acquired it. General sources say Manchester purchased it in 1912, but no price or vendor was located.

#### 1912–present: Manchester Art Gallery

Accessioned in 1912 as **1912.53**. It remains in Manchester’s public collection. An online record has listed it in Gallery 5, but gallery placements are changeable and should be verified before travel.

No authenticity challenge, theft, or major treatment record was located.

### 3.3 St Paul’s version

#### Circa 1899/1900–1904: Hunt studio

The canvas was constructed under Hunt’s direction, with Hughes’s assistance. The Manchester version was reportedly used as a compositional reference because Hunt lacked convenient access to the Keble original.

#### 1904: Fine Art Society exhibition and Booth purchase

After completion it was exhibited at the Fine Art Society in London. Charles Booth—shipowner, industrialist, social investigator, and philanthropist—purchased it from Hunt.

Accounts differ over whether Booth commissioned the work in 1903 or bought it after it had been independently initiated. The chronology of work beginning around 1899 weighs against treating Booth as the original commissioner of the entire project. “Purchaser and promoter” is more secure than “sole commissioning patron.”

A 1904 colonial newspaper report attributed to correspondence about the transaction stated that Booth would buy it, send it to Australia, the United States, and Canada, arrange affordable color reproductions, and ultimately leave it to the Tate. That projected destination was not fulfilled: Booth instead gave it to St Paul’s. The proposed American leg also did not become part of the best-documented itinerary.

#### 1904–1905: Britain and preparation for tour

The framed work was shown in London and reportedly Glasgow. Because of its size and weight, painting and frame traveled crated and could be separated and reassembled.

#### 1905: Canada

The painting sailed to Halifax in February 1905. It was displayed in the York Theatre Assembly Rooms and then moved westward through major Canadian cities, including:

- Halifax;
- Montreal;
- Toronto;
- Winnipeg;
- Vancouver.

Booth hired custodians to accompany it. Percy Fennell lectured on its symbolism and reportedly slept beside it in a hammock with a loaded revolver. Another attendant was tasked with removing the canvas from its frame within three minutes in case of fire. These colorful details derive from later narrative drawing on tour documentation; the underlying payroll or instructions were not independently inspected here.

#### Late 1905–April 1906: Australia

The painting crossed the Pacific and was shown in major Australian centers, including Adelaide, Broken Hill, Melbourne, and Sydney. Brisbane sought inclusion.

Contemporary reports document unusually large crowds and devotional behavior. Claims that 25,000 people per day attended in Sydney and that four-fifths of Australia’s population saw it circulate widely, but should be treated as promotional estimates unless reconciled with venue turnstiles and census figures.

It remained in Sydney through 8 April 1906.

#### April–May 1906: New Zealand

The painting arrived at Auckland on Easter Day, 15 April 1906, according to contemporary announcements and later itinerary reconstruction. Its keeper or tour manager was Mack Jost.

Documented venues or cities included:

- **Auckland**, April 1906;
- **Palmerston North**, 27–29 April;
- **Wellington Town Hall**, through 13 May;
- **Christchurch Art Gallery**, mid-May;
- **Dunedin**;
- **Invercargill**, where contemporary press estimated about 15,000 viewers.

Christchurch newspapers reported crowds of approximately one thousand waiting outside and temporary closure of doors because of congestion. The painting later appeared in the art gallery of the New Zealand International Exhibition at Christchurch in November 1906, indicating either a return or an extended Australasian itinerary rather than a simple uninterrupted eastward journey.

#### 1907: southern Africa

The painting toured southern Africa, including Cape Town and Johannesburg. Scholarship reports that in Johannesburg it was displayed behind the altar in an Anglican setting. A complete city-by-city itinerary was not recovered from accessible sources.

The journey functioned simultaneously as:

- a traveling exhibition;
- a Christian mission;
- an imperial cultural event;
- a publicity enterprise for authorized reproductions;
- a demonstration of Booth’s philanthropy.

Calling it a neutral “world tour” conceals its largely British imperial geography. Conversely, calling it solely propaganda would suppress the documented devotional agency of local viewers. Both structures operated together.

#### 1908: gift to St Paul’s Cathedral

Booth gave the painting to the Dean and Chapter of St Paul’s. It was formally received or dedicated during a service in **June 1908**. Psalm 119 was sung, including the verse “Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path.”

The transfer was a gift, not a national purchase or colonial seizure.

#### 1908–2024: St Paul’s custody

The painting hung in St Paul’s, at different times in the south aisle and, more recently, a north transept chapel. Its cathedral setting encouraged treatment as both a work of art and a devotional image.

No documented wartime destruction or theft was found. St Paul’s survived bombing during the Second World War, but no source consulted specifies whether this painting was evacuated, boxed, or otherwise protected. Its wartime location therefore remains unresolved here.

#### 2024: conservation and relocation

In July–October 2024, St Paul’s undertook conservation of the painting and elaborate frame. The work included conditioning, cleaning or stabilization, and repairs; the cathedral’s public material does not publish a full technical treatment report.

On 17 October 2024 it was unveiled in the **south nave aisle**, relocated from the north transept chapel. St Paul’s 2024 annual report records completion of the conservation and relocation.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Which is “the original”?

The **Keble College painting** is the first completed and exhibited version and therefore has the strongest documentary claim to “the original.”

The Manchester painting is an authentic Hunt version, not an unauthorized reproduction, but is smaller and was completed over an overlapping or slightly later period.

The St Paul’s painting is an authorized late replica by Hunt and Hughes. Cathedral publicity and gift-shop material sometimes calls it “the original,” but that is inaccurate if “original” means the first 1854 exhibited object. It may properly be called the cathedral’s original painting, Hunt’s final version, or the monumental autograph/studio replica.

### 4.2 Hunt versus Hughes

The St Paul’s work bears Hunt’s authorship and conception, but failing sight made Hughes’s assistance substantial. Nineteenth-century copyright and attribution law privileged the named artist’s hand and generally did not recognize studio co-authorship clearly. Modern scholarship is more explicit about Hughes.

The unresolved question is not whether Hughes participated—he did—but the extent of his execution in individual passages. No comprehensive technical imaging or comparative brushwork report was located.

### 4.3 Continuity of the Keble object

There is no evidence that the Keble painting was secretly replaced. Hunt’s complaints concern heat damage, poor hanging, and repair. If the canvas was mounted over panel or extensively retouched, those are alterations to the same historical object, not evidence of substitution.

No published radiocarbon dating, dendrochronology, pigment-authentication campaign, or forensic challenge has been reported. Such tests would have limited value for a securely documented nineteenth-century canvas unless a specific replacement allegation arose.

### 4.4 Institutional inaccuracies

Several recurrent claims require qualification:

- **“Painted after Hunt saw dawn at Bethlehem”:** chronologically problematic for the version already exhibited in 1854.
- **“Commissioned by Charles Booth”:** possible for later phases, but the large painting appears to have been begun before Booth’s documented purchase.
- **“Most traveled artwork in history”:** a promotional superlative, not demonstrated by comparative scholarship.
- **“Seen by four-fifths of Australia”:** a contemporary or later publicity estimate, not a verified census-based count.
- **St Paul’s version as “the original”:** incorrect relative to the Keble painting.

These do not make any canvas a forgery. They demonstrate how institutional and popular narratives compress a complicated three-object history.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Intended symbolic program

Ruskin’s 1854 interpretation distinguished two lights:

- the lantern’s red, searching light: conscience exposing sin;
- the softer light from Christ’s head and crown of thorns: peace and salvation.

The lantern light falls on the closed door, weeds, and fallen apple. Ruskin read the apple as hereditary or original guilt. The chain around Christ’s wrist suggests that the painful revelation of sin appears to constrain the very hand offering mercy.

Additional established meanings include:

- **closed, handleless door:** the human will or soul, openable only from within;
- **rust, nails, weeds, and ivy:** prolonged neglect and spiritual refusal;
- **fallen apples:** the Fall and sin; later interpretation also connects apple imagery with redemption;
- **autumn vegetation:** life approaching its end;
- **morning star/dawn:** a new spiritual beginning;
- **crown with thorns:** kingship united with sacrifice;
- **ornamented vestments/breastplate:** priesthood;
- **red cloak:** sovereign authority, sacrifice, or both.

Not every botanical species or jewel has a demonstrably documented meaning. Later devotional literature often expanded the symbolism beyond what Hunt or Ruskin securely attested.

### 6.2 Devotional use

The paintings were not created as miraculous icons or liturgical implements. Nevertheless, the image acquired para-liturgical functions:

- viewers prayed before it;
- sermons and lectures explicated it;
- reproductions entered churches, schools, nurseries, confirmation manuals, and private homes;
- St Paul’s received the monumental canvas in a religious service;
- Keble now offers meditation before the original;
- the cathedral places the late version in an active worship environment.

Its “powers” are metaphorical and devotional, not documented claims of healing, curse, or supernatural intervention.

### 6.3 Access and handling

No taboo forbids touching or seeing the painting on theological grounds. Ordinary museum and conservation restrictions apply.

During the imperial tour, security and fire planning governed access. The object’s large frame, crowds, and combustible canvas created practical risks. At Keble, architectural access is restricted by stairs and the chapel’s opening hours rather than ritual exclusion.

### 6.4 Legitimation and imperial function

Ownership did not confer crown or ecclesiastical office. Possession nevertheless conferred cultural and philanthropic prestige:

- Combe’s ownership connected Pre-Raphaelitism with Oxford religious patronage.
- Keble’s possession made the college a destination.
- Booth’s tour presented private wealth as public Christian benefaction.
- Colonial exhibitions used the painting as evidence that imperial cities could support serious art and shared Protestant culture.
- St Paul’s transformed a privately owned tour object into a quasi-national sacred artwork.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The handleless door

**Early attested interpretation:** The door lacks an exterior handle because it can be opened only from within.

This is not a medieval legend or later visual accident. It belongs to the picture’s nineteenth-century allegorical reception and was reinforced by Hunt’s own explanations.

### 7.2 The divinely commanded painting

**Artist’s devotional claim:** Hunt painted because he felt divinely commanded.

Historically, this documents the artist’s vocation and self-understanding. It cannot establish divine causation. Later religious retellings often turn a qualified statement of conscience into a literal commission from God.

### 7.3 The Bethlehem sunrise

**Institutional tradition with chronological difficulty:** Keble says Hunt needed the Bethlehem dawn to perfect the light. Since the first version was exhibited before his relevant Holy Land experience could have been incorporated in the ordinary manner, the story may preserve a memory relating to later revision or another version. It should not be used to date the original without further archival evidence.

### 7.4 “The face of Jesus”

During the 1905–1907 tour, some viewers reportedly reacted as if they had encountered Christ himself, praying or fainting. These responses are historical facts about reception, not evidence that the image reproduces Jesus’s physical appearance.

Hunt constructed the head from multiple British sitters, sculptural precedents, imagination, and observation. Stories identifying Christina Rossetti—or any one man—as the sole model are reductive.

### 7.5 The most traveled and most viewed painting

St Paul’s calls the late version “the most travelled art work in history,” and popular sources call it one of the world’s most viewed paintings. The journey was unquestionably extensive and attracted very large crowds. The superlative is, however, not supported by a comparative dataset and belongs to promotional tradition.

### 7.6 The Australian four-fifths

The claim that four-fifths of Australia’s population saw the painting is a famous tour statistic. It may reflect accumulated attendance claims, repeated visits, generous estimation, or press promotion. Until venue records are aggregated and compared with the contemporary census, it remains **unverified**.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Reproductive print and photography

William Henry Simmons produced the principal authorized engraving. An etched state is dated 1859; the finished mixed-method mezzotint was published by Ernest Gambart & Co. on 7 May 1860 and dedicated to Thomas Combe.

Hunt received **£200** for the engraving rights. The British Museum’s impression records:

- mixed-method mezzotint on chine collé;
- 760 × 545 mm;
- Revelation 3:20;
- Hunt as painter;
- Simmons as printmaker;
- Gambart as publisher;
- McQueen & Co. as printer.

Cheap unauthorized photographs followed. In **Gambart v Ball** (1863), the court held that reduced photographic copies could infringe the copyright in an engraving even when marketed to consumers unable to afford the full-size print. The painting itself had been sold before the Fine Arts Copyright Act 1862 and therefore did not obtain the new painting copyright retrospectively.

The image consequently occupies an important place in British copyright history: its dissemination was both devotional mass culture and a test case in controlling mechanically reproduced art.

### 9.2 Books, sermons, and music

Notable adaptations and engagements include:

- John Lucas Tupper’s poem “The Light of the World,” published in *The Crayon* in 1855;
- Richard Glover’s *The “Light of the World”; or, Holman Hunt’s Great Allegorical Picture Translated into Words*, with editions in the 1860s and 1871;
- William Henry Fisk’s related image *The Guest* (1867);
- Horatius Bonar’s devotional poetic engagement in the 1870s;
- Arthur Sullivan’s oratorio *The Light of the World* (1873);
- Edwin Arnold’s *The Light of the World; or, The Great Consummation*, illustrated in editions associated with Hunt in the 1890s;
- countless church cards, stained-glass adaptations, school images, confirmation books, calendars, postcards, and domestic prints.

Sullivan’s oratorio shares the biblical title and participates in the composition’s cultural field, but is not simply a musical transcription of Hunt’s painting.

### 9.3 Replica objects

The composition has been copied in:

- stained glass;
- engravings and photogravures;
- photographs and cartes-de-visite;
- lithographs;
- postcards;
- framed devotional prints;
- church banners;
- educational booklets;
- digital reproductions.

The National Trust catalogues a small 1960s Medici Society print at Tredegar House, demonstrating the image’s continued domestic-devotional circulation a century after the original.

Edward Robert Hughes’s related watercolor, sold at Christie’s in 2022, has independent object history and is important evidence for his role in the monumental version.

### 9.4 Colonial and postcolonial reception

The world tour’s geography reveals its imperial structure. The painting traveled with English-language lectures and biblical explanations through settler-colonial cities and southern Africa. Contemporary colonial newspapers framed its circulation as public uplift, imperial generosity, and evidence of cultural maturity.

Modern scholarship has asked how this apparently universal Christian image was mediated through British race, empire, commerce, and missionary culture. That analysis does not erase local religious responses; it situates the logistics and rhetoric that made them possible.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Primary documentary bases

The principal primary bodies of evidence are:

- Royal Academy exhibition documentation for 1854;
- John Ruskin’s letter to *The Times*, 5 May 1854;
- Hunt’s correspondence;
- Hunt’s *Pre-Raphaelitism and the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood* (1905);
- Hunt’s published defenses of his symbolism;
- Combe and Keble records;
- Gambart’s publication and copyright litigation;
- Charles Booth papers at Senate House Library;
- colonial newspaper reporting from 1904–1907;
- St Paul’s reception, inventory, and conservation records;
- Manchester accession documentation.

The Booth archive guide identifies correspondence concerning *The Light of the World*, Margaret Booth, and Holman Hunt. A complete reconstruction of the tour would require systematic consultation of those manuscript files alongside shipping, railway, insurance, and venue records.

### 10.2 Major scholarship

- **George P. Landow,** *Replete with Meaning: William Holman Hunt and Typological Symbolism* (1979): foundational study of Hunt’s symbolic method.
- **George P. Landow,** “Shadows Cast by *The Light of the World*: William Holman Hunt’s Religious Paintings, 1893–1905” (1982): connects the late replica with Hunt’s final religious production.
- **Jeremy Maas,** *Holman Hunt and the Light of the World* (1984): principal object-centered monograph, especially important for the world tour.
- **Judith Bronkhurst,** *William Holman Hunt: A Catalogue Raisonné* (2006): authoritative catalogue of Hunt’s works and documentary history.
- **Carol Jacobi and Katharine Lochnan, eds.,** *Holman Hunt and the Pre-Raphaelite Vision* (2008): artistic, material, and religious context.
- **Elena Cooper,** *Art and Modern Copyright* (2018), and related work: establishes the picture’s significance in reproduction and copyright history.
- **Kate Nichols,** “British Art and Empire: Holman Hunt’s *The Light of the World* Reflected in the Mirror of the Colonial Press” (2007): scrutinizes tour chronology and imperial reception.

### 10.3 Running debates

1. **Date of inception:** sources variously begin the original in 1849, 1850, or 1851. The safest catalogue range is 1851–1853/54 unless new dated correspondence establishes an earlier start.

2. **Place of painting:** Worcester Park/Ewell and Oxford traditions need not be mutually exclusive, but claims that the whole painting was made in only one setting are too simple.

3. **Bethlehem light:** difficult to reconcile with the original’s 1854 exhibition.

4. **Models:** composite head versus popular single-sitter identifications.

5. **Degree of Hughes’s authorship:** acknowledged but not fully apportioned.

6. **Tour numbers:** contemporary publicity, retrospective claims, and countable attendance must be distinguished.

7. **Damage at Keble:** Hunt’s complaints are documented, but the precise material consequences await publication of conservation records.

8. **The word “original”:** a persistent institutional and commercial source of confusion.

### 10.4 Scientific examination

No object-specific, peer-reviewed technical examination of any of the three paintings was located in the accessible sources. In particular, this dossier found no published:

- X-radiograph;
- infrared reflectogram;
- XRF pigment map;
- cross-section study;
- binder analysis;
- dendrochronology;
- complete modern condition report.

The absence of a publicly located report does not prove that institutions have never examined the paintings. St Paul’s conservation staff necessarily assessed the late version in 2024, but the technical documentation has not been published in the sources consulted.

### Evidentiary limits

The fullest unresolved areas are:

- the Manchester version’s custody before 1912;
- the exact material structure and complete restoration history of the Keble version;
- a verified city-by-city, date-by-date tour itinerary;
- tour insurance and shipping arrangements;
- reliable aggregate attendance figures;
- the precise division of labor between Hunt and Hughes;
- publication of the 2024 St Paul’s conservation report;
- wartime custody of the St Paul’s canvas.

No evidence found supports theft, forgery, secret substitution, disputed legal ownership, or supernatural efficacy for any of the three paintings.
