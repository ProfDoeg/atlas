# Etemenanki (Tower of Babel): Object Dossier

## Evidentiary note

Etemenanki was a real monumental ziggurat in Babylon. Its excavated foundations, Babylonian inscriptions, topographical texts, mathematical descriptions, and references in Greek and Roman authors establish its historical existence. Very little of its superstructure survives.

The “Tower of Babel” is the unnamed tower in Genesis 11:1–9. Identifying that literary tower specifically with Etemenanki is a plausible and widely accepted historical interpretation, but it is not demonstrated by the biblical text and should not be treated as an ancient proper name for the monument.

This dossier therefore tracks two related but non-identical subjects:

1. **The physical monument Etemenanki**, reconstructed from archaeology and Babylonian, Persian-period, Hellenistic, and classical testimony.
2. **The Tower of Babel tradition**, beginning with Genesis and subsequently attached to Etemenanki, other Mesopotamian ruins, and imaginary structures.

Evidence is labeled as follows:

- **Documented:** directly attested by contemporary or near-contemporary text, excavation, or administrative record.
- **Archaeological finding:** physical observation or stratigraphic interpretation.
- **Royal claim:** a ruler’s commemorative statement, valuable evidence but rhetorically interested.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** an inference from incomplete evidence.
- **Disputed:** supported by competing interpretations.
- **Biblical narrative / classical tradition / later legend:** evidence for reception rather than necessarily for historical events.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Identity and class

**Etemenanki** was the principal ziggurat, or staged temple tower, of the god Marduk at Babylon in central Mesopotamia, beside the low temple complex called Esagila. It occupied its own enormous enclosure north of Esagila and was one of the principal monumental and cosmological structures of the city.

It was not a freestanding observatory, royal palace, tomb, or ordinary temple. A ziggurat consisted of a massive raised core and receding stages supporting a summit sanctuary. Babylonian evidence connects Etemenanki with Marduk and the cultic topography of Babylon. Later Greek authors called the complex the sanctuary of **Zeus Belos** or **Belus**, equating Marduk under his title *Bēl*, “Lord,” with Zeus.

### 1.2 Names, scripts, and etymologies

The ceremonial Sumerian name is conventionally written:

**É.TEMEN.AN.KI**

Common transliterations include:

- **E-temen-an-ki**
- **Etemenanki**
- Less consistently, **Etemenankia**
- Older publications sometimes print **Etemenanki** or **E-temen-an-na-ki** according to differing sign readings.

The conventional translation is:

> **“House, foundation-platform of heaven and earth”**

Component analysis:

- **É**: house, temple
- **TEMEN**: foundation, foundation platform, foundation deposit or peg
- **AN**: heaven
- **KI**: earth

“House of the foundation of heaven and earth” is serviceable, although *temen* carries architectural and ritual meanings that “foundation” alone does not exhaust.

Royal inscriptions also call it the **ziqqurrat of Babylon**, Akkadian *ziqqurrat Bābili*. The Akkadian noun *ziqqurratu* derives from a verb meaning “to rise” or “be high.”

Other relevant names are:

- **Babylon**, Akkadian **Bābili/Bāb-ilim**, conventionally interpreted by Babylonian scribes as “Gate of the God.”
- Hebrew **בָּבֶל**, *Bābel*, the ordinary biblical name for Babylon.
- Genesis 11 associates *Babel* by wordplay with Hebrew **בלל**, *bālal*, “to mix/confuse.” This is a Hebrew literary etymology, not the historical derivation of the Akkadian city-name.
- Greek **Βαβυλών**, *Babylōn*.
- Greek descriptions include the temple or tomb of **Belos/Belus**.
- Arabic **بابل**, *Bābil*, for Babylon.

The biblical expression “Tower of Babel” is modern shorthand. Genesis refers to “a city and a tower” and subsequently names the city Babel; it never supplies the tower with a proper name.

### 1.3 Location and plan

The excavated site lies at Babylon near modern Hillah in Babil Governorate, Iraq. The tower occupied the area traditionally designated **Sahn**, a low, flat, seasonally wet depression. Its central coordinates fall within the protected archaeological property of Babylon, inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2019.

The German excavations identified a nearly square foundation:

- Koldewey’s published measurements are approximately **91.48 × 91.66 metres**.
- Later surveys and reconstructions differ by small amounts because different wall lines, erosion boundaries, and ancient units have been used.
- The correspondence to approximately **180 Babylonian cubits per side** is strong.

The precinct surrounding the tower was much larger. Textual reconstructions envisage a square enclosure entered through monumental gates, with subsidiary buildings and circulation spaces connecting it to Esagila and Babylon’s processional system.

### 1.4 Materials and construction

**Archaeological and textual evidence** indicates the characteristic Mesopotamian monumental system:

- A massive core of sun-dried mud brick.
- Courses or layers incorporating reed matting.
- A facing or structural mantle of fired brick.
- Bitumen mortar and waterproofing.
- Timber employed in doors, fittings, scaffolding, or structural elements.
- Monumental external stairways or ramps.

Nabopolassar’s foundation inscription says he set the foundations on the “breast of the netherworld,” made ceremonial tools of precious or exotic materials, imposed labor on the peoples under his rule, and used bitumen and baked brick. This is a royal, cosmological formulation but also confirms principal materials and organized labor.

Nebuchadnezzar II likewise states that he rebuilt with baked brick and bitumen and raised the summit toward heaven. His inscriptions describe cedar or other fine woods and metal fittings for temples, though not every material mentioned in the wider building program can be assigned securely to Etemenanki itself.

The surviving excavated remains are largely the robbed and waterlogged foundation zone. Ancient and later builders removed reusable baked bricks; erosion and the high water table reduced the mud-brick core. The conspicuous water-filled outline visible in modern photographs is not the intact tower but its excavated and robbed footprint.

### 1.5 Height and stages

The often-repeated dimensions—about **90 or 91 metres square and 90 or 91 metres high**—derive primarily from the late Babylonian **Esagila tablet**, Louvre **AO 6555**, copied at Uruk in 229 BCE from an older exemplar. The tablet gives mathematical dimensions for Esagila and seven levels of Etemenanki.

In approximate modern equivalents, its idealized scheme has been reconstructed as:

1. First stage: about 90 × 90 metres, approximately 33 metres high.
2. Second: about 78 × 78 metres, approximately 18 metres high.
3. Third: about 60 × 60 metres, approximately 6 metres high.
4. Fourth: about 51 × 51 metres, approximately 6 metres high.
5. Fifth: about 42 × 42 metres, approximately 6 metres high.
6. Sixth: about 33 × 33 metres, approximately 6 metres high.
7. Summit sanctuary: about 24 × 24 metres and approximately 15 metres high.

This produces an ideal total close to 90 metres.

These figures are **textually documented but architecturally disputed**. Andrew George and Hansjörg Schmid emphasized that the tablet may preserve an ideal scholarly or metrological description rather than a measured survey of a standing Neo-Babylonian building. Archaeology securely confirms the approximate base width but not the total height. Schmid’s reconstruction yielded a substantially lower tower—commonly summarized at roughly 60–70 metres—depending on the assumed slopes, stage heights, and staircase system.

The **Tower of Babylon stele**, formerly Schøyen Collection MS 2063 and now in the Iraq Museum, shows seven superimposed stages including the summit building. Its schematic elevation is the only known ancient image explicitly captioned as Etemenanki. It supports a seven-level conception but cannot by itself settle the exact height.

### 1.6 Stairways

Excavation exposed evidence for a monumental frontal approach. Reconstructions generally include:

- A broad central stairway projecting from one face.
- Two flanking stairways ascending toward the first or second stage.
- Further access by smaller stairs or ramps on upper levels.

Herodotus instead describes an ascent winding around the outside and counts eight towers, one placed on another. Whether he saw Etemenanki personally, reproduced Babylonian information imperfectly, or counted architectural components differently remains disputed. His description should not be merged uncritically with the excavated frontal-stair system.

### 1.7 Summit sanctuary

The Esagila tablet and later textual evidence indicate a summit temple. Herodotus describes, at the top:

- A large chamber.
- A richly furnished couch.
- A golden table.
- No cult statue.
- One Babylonian woman selected by the god as the only human permitted to sleep there.

He attributes the explanation to the “Chaldaeans,” meaning Babylonian temple specialists. He adds that they claimed the god visited the chamber, while explicitly saying he did not believe the claim. This is valuable evidence for a reported fifth-century BCE ritual tradition but is not an excavation report.

The Tower of Babylon stele includes plan-like diagrams interpreted as the summit sanctuary’s rooms. Because these drawings are schematic and damaged, precise room identifications remain partly conjectural.

### 1.8 Inscriptions belonging to the building program

No complete inscribed architectural skin remains in place. Evidence consists principally of portable or displaced objects:

- Foundation cylinders of Nabopolassar, including the example now catalogued as **CBS 9090** in the Penn Museum.
- Nebuchadnezzar II cylinders commemorating Etemenanki’s completion or restoration, including Metropolitan Museum of Art **86.11.370**.
- Stamped bricks bearing Nebuchadnezzar’s standard title and reference to his care for Esagila and Ezida.
- The black-stone Tower of Babylon stele, now in Baghdad.
- Copies and fragments found away from their original emplacement, including one reported from Susa.

These witnesses are evidence for the tower but are not necessarily fragments of its masonry. Foundation deposits could be buried in or beside a structure; later conquest and antiquities collecting displaced others.

### 1.9 Present physical condition

The ziggurat’s superstructure is destroyed. What remains at Babylon consists of:

- Foundation traces.
- Robbed wall lines and trenches.
- mud-brick remnants.
- Water-filled depressions.
- Archaeological plans, photographs, soil and brick observations.
- Nearby displaced rubble deposits that have been associated with ancient clearance.

No intact stage or summit sanctuary survives. Modern drawings, scale models, virtual reconstructions, and paintings are reconstructions, not preserved fabric.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Unknown first construction

The original construction date is not securely documented.

Babylon was an important city by the Old Babylonian period, especially under Hammurabi in the eighteenth century BCE. A temple tower may have existed then, and Hansjörg Schmid interpreted some archaeological phases as evidence for predecessors beneath the Neo-Babylonian structure. Nevertheless:

- No surviving Old Babylonian royal inscription securely states that Hammurabi built Etemenanki.
- The foundation area was repeatedly rebuilt and later extensively cleared.
- The first tower’s date, plan, and height cannot be reconstructed with confidence.

Statements that Hammurabi “built the Tower of Babel” are therefore **scholarly conjecture or popular compression**, not documented fact.

### 2.2 Literary attestations before the Assyrian rebuilding

The ceremonial name appears in first-millennium copies of Babylonian literary and topographical compositions, particularly:

- The Babylonian topographical series commonly called **Tintir = Babylon**.
- The **Erra Epic**.
- Liturgical and temple lists.
- Material associated with Babylonian creation theology.

Some compositions may originate in the late second millennium BCE, but surviving manuscripts are later. Consequently, their evidence distinguishes imperfectly between:

- The age of the literary composition.
- The date when its present wording was fixed.
- The date of the surviving tablet.
- The age of the physical monument named in it.

Andrew George consequently describes Etemenanki’s second-millennium history as nearly unrecoverable.

The first securely identifiable **royal-inscriptional use of the name** occurs in Esarhaddon’s rebuilding records in the seventh century BCE. A broader destructive reference in Sennacherib’s account probably includes the Babylonian temple towers but does not always name Etemenanki in the surviving formulation.

### 2.3 Sennacherib’s destruction, 689 BCE

In 689 BCE, after prolonged Babylonian resistance, the Assyrian king **Sennacherib** captured Babylon. His inscriptions claim that he:

- Destroyed the city.
- Demolished temples and temple towers.
- threw their brickwork into canals or the Euphrates.
- Carried away divine and human property.
- Flooded or erased portions of the urban landscape.

This is a **contemporary royal claim**, expressed in deliberately totalizing language. Babylon was severely damaged, but the rhetoric of making it vanish “like a meadow” should not be read as a literal archaeological inventory.

Etemenanki was probably among the damaged monumental structures. Nabopolassar later states that it had become weak and collapsed before his reign; Esarhaddon’s inscriptions explicitly describe rebuilding Babylon’s sacred structures.

### 2.4 Esarhaddon’s rebuilding

Sennacherib was assassinated in 681 BCE. His son **Esarhaddon** reversed his anti-Babylon policy and presented himself as Babylon’s restorer.

His inscriptions state that he rebuilt Esagila and Etemenanki. The work included:

- Ritual purification of the site.
- Re-establishment of ancient foundations.
- Brick manufacture.
- Rebuilding of sacred precincts.
- Restoration of divine services and urban population.

The texts claim that Babylon’s destruction had been divinely ordained but that Marduk reversed the written term of punishment. This theological explanation served Assyrian reconciliation with Babylonian priestly and civic interests.

### 2.5 Ashurbanipal and later seventh-century work

Esarhaddon’s son **Ashurbanipal** continued or completed work at Babylon. Bricks and inscriptions associate him with Esagila and Etemenanki. Political control of Babylon was shared uneasily with his brother **Šamaš-šuma-ukīn**, king of Babylon, whose revolt ended in 648 BCE.

The precise amount of tower fabric attributable to Esarhaddon, Ashurbanipal, and Babylonian authorities is disputed because Nabopolassar and Nebuchadnezzar subsequently undertook a comprehensive rebuilding.

### 2.6 Nabopolassar’s new foundation

After Assyrian power collapsed, **Nabopolassar**, founder of the Neo-Babylonian dynasty, began a major reconstruction. His Etemenanki inscription survives on at least two clay cylinders. One, Penn Museum **CBS 9090**, was found in the nineteenth century in a niche associated with Etemenanki.

The inscription says that:

- Etemenanki had weakened and collapsed.
- Marduk commanded the king to secure its foundations on the netherworld and make its top rival heaven.
- Nabopolassar fashioned ceremonial hoes, spades, and brick moulds from ivory, ebony, and *musukkannu* wood.
- He levied a vast workforce from his country.
- The king, his sons—including Nebuchadnezzar—and high officials ceremonially carried baskets.
- He laid the foundation with bitumen and fired brick.

The text is both a building record and a ritual performance of royal humility. The elite’s symbolic basket-carrying does not mean that the physical labor was voluntary; the inscription explicitly invokes levied manpower.

The exact starting year is uncertain. The surviving inscription is generally assigned to Nabopolassar’s reign, 626–605 BCE, and sometimes approximately to 625 BCE.

### 2.7 Nebuchadnezzar II’s completion

Nabopolassar died in 605 BCE. His son **Nebuchadnezzar II** continued the project throughout his long reign, 605–562 BCE.

In royal inscriptions Nebuchadnezzar claims that he:

- Completed Etemenanki.
- Raised its summit to heaven.
- Used fired brick and bitumen.
- Mobilized rulers and peoples from across his empire.
- Made the building shine like the sun.
- Completed Etemenanki together with the great ziggurat at Borsippa, E-ur-me-imin-anki.

The black-stone Tower of Babylon stele repeats this program and supplies a labeled elevation. Its wording belongs to royal universalism: the monument embodied Marduk’s world rule and Nebuchadnezzar’s capacity to mobilize a multilingual empire.

The Neo-Babylonian structure was therefore not manufactured at one moment. It was the result of:

- Reuse of an ancient sacred emplacement.
- Assyrian-period reconstruction.
- Nabopolassar’s refoundation.
- Nebuchadnezzar’s enlargement and completion.
- Later maintenance and eventual dismantling.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

For a fixed monument, “custody” means control of the city, temple administration, construction workforce, sacred access, and later archaeological and heritage authority. The land and surviving foundations never circulated as a portable object.

### Before 689 BCE: Babylonian temple custody

The ziggurat belonged institutionally to Marduk’s sanctuary complex. Its daily material administration would have involved Esagila’s priestly, scribal, labor, and property organizations under successive Babylonian and foreign rulers.

The early chain is incomplete. No continuous archive identifies every rebuilding authority or chief administrator.

### 689 BCE: Sennacherib’s conquest

- **Transfer mechanism:** military conquest and punitive destruction.
- **Controller:** Assyrian crown.
- **Action:** destruction of Babylon’s monumental and sacred fabric, probably including Etemenanki.
- **Evidence:** Sennacherib’s inscriptions; later rebuilding texts.
- **Qualification:** extent of actual demolition must be distinguished from royal claims of total annihilation.

### 681–669 BCE: Esarhaddon

- **Transfer mechanism:** dynastic succession and restoration policy.
- **Controller:** Esarhaddon as Assyrian and Babylonian sovereign.
- **Action:** rebuilding and ritually re-legitimizing the monument.
- **Institutional partner:** Babylonian temple personnel.
- **Evidence:** Esarhaddon’s royal inscriptions.

### 668–c. 627 BCE: Ashurbanipal and Babylonian sub-kings

- **Controller:** Assyrian imperial dynasty, with changing local Babylonian arrangements.
- **Action:** continuation or completion of sacred reconstruction.
- **Evidence:** royal inscriptions and stamped building material.
- **Gap:** the exact custody of tower works during Šamaš-šuma-ukīn’s revolt is not recoverable.

### 626–605 BCE: Nabopolassar

- **Transfer mechanism:** collapse of Assyrian authority and establishment of Neo-Babylonian rule.
- **Action:** comprehensive refoundation.
- **Labor:** levied subjects and royal dependants.
- **Evidence:** foundation cylinders.

### 605–562 BCE: Nebuchadnezzar II

- **Transfer mechanism:** hereditary succession.
- **Action:** completion and embellishment.
- **Custodians:** crown and Esagila organization acting in Marduk’s name.
- **Evidence:** cylinders, bricks, royal inscriptions, Tower of Babylon stele.
- **Political context:** peoples incorporated by Babylonian conquest were represented as contributors to the monument. This included populations deported from conquered territories, although no surviving work roster proves that Judean deportees personally built Etemenanki.

### 562–539 BCE: Later Neo-Babylonian kings

Amēl-Marduk, Neriglissar, Lābāši-Marduk, and Nabonidus successively controlled Babylon. Etemenanki presumably remained an operating cult monument, but surviving sources give much less information about major structural work.

### 539 BCE: Cyrus the Great

Cyrus captured Babylon. The Cyrus Cylinder presents the transition as Marduk’s choice and Cyrus as a restorer of sanctuaries. Etemenanki passed into Achaemenid imperial control while temple institutions continued to function.

Claims that Cyrus deliberately removed the tower’s stairways are not securely supported by contemporary documentation and should be treated as later reconstruction.

### 522–486 BCE: Darius I

Babylon revolted more than once against Darius. Persian reconquest affected the city, but no contemporary text establishes a specific destruction of Etemenanki by Darius.

### Xerxes, traditionally 484–482 BCE

Greek and later classical traditions connect Xerxes with:

- Suppression of a Babylonian revolt.
- Removal of a golden divine image.
- Destruction or damage to the sanctuary of Belus.

The historicity and scale of a “Xerxes destruction” remain disputed. Modern scholarship has rejected the once-common assumption that Xerxes simply abolished Babylonian religion. Babylonian documents show institutional continuity, while the tower may nevertheless have deteriorated or suffered targeted damage.

Arrian, writing centuries later, says Xerxes destroyed the temple that Alexander wished to rebuild. Strabo similarly attributes demolition of the “tomb of Belus” to Xerxes. These are **late classical testimonies**, not contemporary Persian records.

### Fifth century BCE: Herodotus’ reported monument

Herodotus describes the sanctuary as still existing “in my time,” with a square precinct and eight superimposed towers. Whether he visited Babylon is disputed. His account preserves plausible cultic details alongside measurements and Babylonian narratives that cannot all be verified.

If it describes Etemenanki, the tower or at least its cultic superstructure remained recognizable in the fifth century BCE. Alternatively, Herodotus may combine reports from different parts of the Bel/Marduk complex.

### Artaxerxes IV Arses, 338–336 BCE

A damaged Babylonian source may indicate royal interest in restoring Esagila and Etemenanki shortly before Alexander. Its interpretation is uncertain, and no completed reconstruction is documented.

### Alexander the Great, 331–323 BCE

Alexander entered Babylon in 331 BCE and ordered work on the sanctuary. Babylonian records and later authors indicate that large-scale rubble clearance preceded proposed reconstruction.

Accounts differ:

- Strabo says Alexander ordered the mound cleared and that 10,000 men worked for two months without completing the task.
- Arrian reports that Alexander intended to rebuild the temple on its old foundations.
- Diodorus says Babylonian “Chaldaeans” requested restoration.
- Babylonian chronicles and astronomical diaries provide more contemporary evidence for temple work and expenditure, although damaged passages complicate exact attribution.

Alexander did not complete a rebuilt Etemenanki. He died in Babylon in 323 BCE. The often-repeated statement that he “entirely demolished the Tower of Babel” should be qualified: he ordered removal of already ruined fabric in preparation for reconstruction. This clearance may have destroyed surviving upper remains, but it was not represented as punitive destruction.

### 323–281 BCE: Early Successors and Seleucus I

Clearance or temple work continued intermittently. Administrative texts record money, labor, or tithes associated with Esagila. Large quantities of ziggurat rubble were moved. Andrew George and Eleanor Robson associate a great dumped mound east of the city, later incorporated into the **Homera** area and Greek theatre landscape, with this clearance.

This is a plausible archaeological-historical reconstruction, not an individually traceable transfer of every brick.

### Antiochus I, principally 281–261 BCE

Antiochus I, first as crown prince and then king, cultivated Babylonian priestly institutions. The **Ruin of Esagila Chronicle** records work involving elephants and wagons and an accident or ritual offense during clearing. The damaged chronicle has produced divergent reconstructions; older accounts that Antiochus angrily ordered the destruction of the temple after stumbling are not supported by the improved reading.

The **Antiochus Cylinder** commemorates rebuilding at Esagila in Babylon and Ezida at Borsippa, beginning ceremonially on 27 March 268 BCE. It does not establish that Etemenanki itself was successfully reconstructed.

### 229 BCE: Esagila tablet copied at Uruk

A scribe named Anu-bēlšunu copied the mathematical tablet now Louvre AO 6555 at Uruk. By this date the text preserved an authoritative plan or learned description of Etemenanki. It does not prove the tower was then standing to the stated height.

This is a transfer of **textual knowledge**, not custody of the monument.

### Later Hellenistic, Parthian, and Sasanian periods

Esagila and Babylonian scholarship persisted for centuries, but Etemenanki was not rebuilt as a functioning monumental tower. Brick robbing, erosion, canal changes, sedimentation, and settlement transformed the site.

The monument’s identity eventually ceased to operate as a continuous cultic object. Later observers encountered mounds and ruins rather than a known intact Etemenanki.

### Medieval and early modern identifications

Jewish, Christian, and Islamic geographical traditions continued to locate Babel in Mesopotamia, but several different ruins competed for identification:

- Babylon itself.
- **Birs Nimrud**, the remains of the ziggurat of Borsippa.
- Aqar Quf/Dur-Kurigalzu.
- Other conspicuous brick mounds.

Birs Nimrud was especially persuasive because it retained a dramatic standing core. It was repeatedly called the Tower of Babel before cuneiform decipherment established that it was the ziggurat of Borsippa.

These were identity attributions, not physical transfers of Etemenanki.

### Nineteenth-century exploration

European travelers, antiquarians, local diggers, and early archaeologists surveyed and excavated Babylon’s mounds. Portable bricks, tablets, cylinders, and antiquities entered Ottoman, European, and American collections through excavation, purchase, diplomatic division, and the antiquities market.

The exact discovery histories of many objects referring to Etemenanki are incomplete. The site remained Ottoman territory until the First World War.

### 1899–1917: German Oriental Society excavation

The Deutsche Orient-Gesellschaft expedition under **Robert Koldewey** excavated Babylon. Work on the Etemenanki area was difficult because:

- The remains lay low rather than forming a high mound.
- The water table flooded trenches.
- Ancient brick robbing had removed much of the fabric.
- Alexander-period and later clearance had disturbed the superstructure.

In 1913 the excavators traced the approximately 91-metre square base and central core. Koldewey’s plans supplied the first modern archaeological identification of the actual ziggurat footprint.

The excavation did not remove an intact “Tower of Babel” to Berlin. Unlike the reconstructed Ishtar Gate, Etemenanki survives principally in situ as foundations.

### 1918–1957: Iraqi territory and antiquities administration

After the Ottoman period and British occupation, Babylon came under the antiquities regimes of the British Mandate and then the Kingdom of Iraq. Custody of the site passed to Iraqi state institutions.

### 1957–1972: renewed German-Iraqi research

German Archaeological Institute investigations resumed. In 1962 **Hansjörg Schmid** conducted targeted examination of Etemenanki’s core and foundation remains. His eventual monograph, *Der Tempelturm Etemenanki in Babylon* (1995), reassessed Koldewey’s records, the Esagila tablet, building phases, and possible reconstruction.

### Baʿathist restoration period

From the late 1970s and particularly during the 1980s, the Iraqi government reconstructed large portions of Babylon and used the site for national festivals and political representation. New walls were built over ancient fabric, often with modern bricks inscribed in Saddam Hussein’s name in conscious imitation of Nebuchadnezzar’s stamped bricks.

Etemenanki itself was not reconstructed to full height. The broader interventions nevertheless altered drainage, archaeological context, visitor circulation, and the presentation of the ancient city.

### 2003–2004: military occupation of Babylon

Following the invasion of Iraq, coalition forces established a military installation within the archaeological site. UNESCO and archaeological assessments documented damage at Babylon from earthmoving, trenches, vehicle traffic, gravel, sandbags filled with archaeological material, contamination, and disturbance.

Available reports discuss Babylon as a whole. Damage must not automatically be assigned to Etemenanki unless a report locates it there. The tower footprint remained part of the affected protected landscape, but it was not blown up or newly demolished in 2003.

### 2019–present: World Heritage property

Babylon was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2019. The Etemenanki remains form part of the Iraqi state-owned archaeological property.

Current control rests with Iraqi cultural-heritage authorities, principally the State Board of Antiquities and Heritage under the Ministry of Culture, Tourism and Antiquities, with UNESCO monitoring under the World Heritage Convention.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Did Etemenanki exist?

Yes. This is not reasonably disputed. Its existence is established by:

- In-situ foundations.
- Assyrian and Babylonian royal inscriptions.
- Babylonian topographical and mathematical texts.
- Foundation cylinders.
- Stamped bricks.
- The explicitly captioned Tower of Babylon stele.
- Classical descriptions that broadly correspond to Babylon’s Marduk complex.

### 4.2 Is Etemenanki the Tower of Babel?

**Unresolved as a question of direct textual identity.**

Arguments supporting influence or identification include:

- Genesis locates the city and tower in **Babel/Babylon**, in the land of Shinar.
- The building materials—kiln-fired brick and bitumen—are characteristic of southern Mesopotamia and explicitly attested at Etemenanki.
- The phrase “its top in the heavens” resembles Mesopotamian royal descriptions of high temples and Etemenanki inscriptions.
- Nebuchadnezzar’s rebuilding mobilized subjects from a multilingual empire.
- Judean deportees lived in Babylonia during or shortly after the Neo-Babylonian reconstruction.
- Etemenanki was Babylon’s most conspicuous sacred tower.

Limitations:

- Genesis never names Etemenanki or Marduk.
- The narrative does not describe stages, a summit shrine, dimensions, or Nebuchadnezzar.
- It does not say that the completed tower was physically destroyed.
- The date and composition history of Genesis 11 remain disputed.
- “A tower in Babylon” could draw on the broader ziggurat tradition rather than one building.
- The identification is a historical-literary inference, not an equivalence stated in either Babylonian or biblical sources.

The most defensible formulation is: **Etemenanki is the strongest known historical candidate for the architectural and ideological background of Genesis 11, but the biblical tower is a literary object and cannot be proven to be a description of Etemenanki.**

### 4.3 Was the tower 90 metres high?

The base was approximately 91 metres square. A height of approximately 90 metres is supported by AO 6555’s ideal scheme but not independently verified by surviving fabric.

The competing position, based on archaeological engineering and Schmid’s analysis, reconstructs a lower tower. The disagreement concerns whether the Esagila tablet records:

- Actual dimensions of Nebuchadnezzar’s completed building.
- A canonical scholarly plan.
- An ideal or symbolic geometrical design.
- A combination of remembered measurements and scribal systematization.

No surviving wall rises high enough to decide the issue.

### 4.4 Did Herodotus see it?

Disputed. Herodotus writes as if the sanctuary existed in his time but does not plainly say “I saw it.” His Babylonian account contains both accurate-seeming details and notorious exaggerations or inherited reports.

His eight “towers” may reflect:

- Seven stages plus the summit temple.
- A different counting convention.
- A garbled report.
- A structure not identical in form to the Neo-Babylonian design.

### 4.5 Did Xerxes destroy it?

Not securely established. The evidence is retrospective and inconsistent.

- Herodotus reports removal of a golden image but not the complete demolition of the tower.
- Strabo and Arrian, writing centuries later, attribute destruction of the temple or “tomb of Belus” to Xerxes.
- Babylonian documentary evidence demonstrates continued cultic and scholarly activity after Xerxes.
- The tower may have suffered damage, loss of fittings, interrupted maintenance, or gradual collapse without one total destructive episode.

“Destroyed by Xerxes in 482 BCE” is consequently too definite.

### 4.6 Did Alexander destroy it?

Alexander ordered removal of ruined material in order to rebuild. The clearance was real enough to appear in several traditions and Babylonian records, but the reconstruction was never completed.

Calling this an act of “destruction” obscures its purpose. It was dismantling or rubble clearance of a decayed monument for a restoration project, albeit one that ultimately erased surviving fabric without replacing it.

### 4.7 The Tower of Babylon stele

The black-stone stele formerly catalogued as **MS 2063** has an unusually important and controversial modern provenance.

Physical description:

- Black stone, probably basalt.
- Two joining fragments.
- Approximately 47 × 25.5 × 11 centimetres.
- Rounded top.
- Cuneiform text in three columns.
- Image of a standing king, almost certainly Nebuchadnezzar II.
- Labeled seven-level elevation of Etemenanki.
- Two plan drawings.
- Areas of the text deliberately erased in antiquity.

Earlier scholarship had to rely on an undocumented market provenance. Andrew George published it in 2011 as a Neo-Babylonian royal inscription. Questions surrounding private antiquities collections and undocumented Iraqi material properly limited confidence in its stated findspot.

Olof Pedersén subsequently reported that photographs and documentation identify it as found in the early 1990s in the old German excavation trench at Amran, about twenty metres north of Esagila, in a later—possibly Parthian—level. It then spent approximately twenty-eight years in the Schøyen Collection.

Following Norwegian legal proceedings involving a broader group of Iraqi antiquities, a court found that Iraq had not established its claim to the seized objects; they were returned to the collection. Martin Schøyen then transferred the Etemenanki stele to Iraq. It is now in the **Iraq Museum, Baghdad**.

Its late archaeological level does not make the stele a modern forgery. It indicates ancient displacement from its original setting. Its script, language, iconography, damage, and inscription support a Neo-Babylonian date. Its authenticated Babylon findspot substantially strengthens its evidentiary value.

### 4.8 Birs Nimrud as a rival Tower of Babel

The ziggurat at Borsippa, now Birs Nimrud, was repeatedly identified as the Tower of Babel by early travelers. Cuneiform evidence instead identifies it as **E-ur-me-imin-anki**, the ziggurat of Nabû’s temple Ezida.

It remains important because:

- Nebuchadnezzar rebuilt it in the same program as Etemenanki.
- Its standing ruin influenced modern visual reconstructions.
- Its local and European “Tower of Babel” identity endured after scholarly correction.

It is a rival **reception-site**, not the same monument.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Marduk

- **Connection:** principal god of Babylon; Etemenanki was his ziggurat.
- **Period:** at least first-millennium BCE attestations, probably earlier.
- **Evidence:** temple lists, royal inscriptions, topographical texts.
- **Function:** divine commissioner in royal rhetoric; cosmological owner and beneficiary.

### Nabû

- **Connection:** Marduk’s son and patron of writing; invoked by Nabopolassar and Nebuchadnezzar. His Borsippa ziggurat was rebuilt alongside Etemenanki.
- **Evidence:** royal inscriptions.

### Sennacherib

- **Connection:** conqueror and destroyer of Babylon in 689 BCE.
- **Evidence:** Assyrian royal inscriptions.
- **Qualification:** specific surviving references to Etemenanki vary in explicitness.

### Esarhaddon

- **Connection:** restorer of Babylon, Esagila, and Etemenanki beginning after 681 BCE.
- **Evidence:** royal building inscriptions.
- **Political function:** reconciliation of Assyrian kingship with Babylonian sacred authority.

### Ashurbanipal

- **Connection:** continued or completed rebuilding.
- **Evidence:** inscriptions and building material associated with the temple complex.

### Nabopolassar

- **Connection:** refounded the weakened ziggurat and deposited foundation cylinders.
- **Period:** 626–605 BCE.
- **Evidence:** contemporary cylinders.
- **Role:** commissioner and ritual participant.

### Nebuchadnezzar II

- **Connection:** completed or substantially completed the Neo-Babylonian monument.
- **Period:** 605–562 BCE.
- **Evidence:** cylinders, bricks, stele, royal inscriptions.
- **Role:** commissioner, imperial mobilizer, and principal historical figure in the tower’s modern image.

### Babylonian labor forces

- **Connection:** manufactured, transported, and laid millions of bricks.
- **Evidence:** royal claims of levied populations and the scale of the work.
- **Limitation:** personal names and exact workforce composition are largely unknown.

### Esagila priesthood and scribes

- **Connection:** cultic guardians, ritual experts, administrators, and preservers of topographical knowledge.
- **Period:** first millennium BCE into the Hellenistic era.
- **Evidence:** temple texts, chronicles, scholarly tablets, Herodotus’ “Chaldaeans.”
- **Qualification:** “Chaldaean” in Greek texts is not a precise institutional title for every Babylonian priest.

### Cyrus II

- **Connection:** acquired Babylon in 539 BCE; placed its cult institutions under Achaemenid sovereignty.
- **Evidence:** Babylonian chronicles and Cyrus’ royal presentation.

### Xerxes I

- **Connection:** alleged or traditionally reported destroyer of Bel’s temple.
- **Evidence:** later Greek and Roman authors.
- **Status:** disputed.

### Alexander III of Macedon

- **Connection:** ordered clearance and intended reconstruction.
- **Dates:** 331–323 BCE.
- **Evidence:** Babylonian records and later Greek histories.
- **Outcome:** work unfinished at his death.

### Seleucus I and Antiochus I

- **Connection:** continued patronage and clearance or rebuilding activity around Esagila.
- **Evidence:** Babylonian chronicles, administrative texts, Antiochus Cylinder.
- **Outcome:** no restored Etemenanki is documented.

### Anu-bēlšunu

- **Connection:** scribe who copied the Esagila tablet at Uruk.
- **Date:** 229 BCE.
- **Importance:** transmitted the most detailed ancient dimensions of Etemenanki.

### Robert Koldewey

- **Connection:** director of the German excavations that located and surveyed the tower’s foundation.
- **Period:** excavation of Babylon, 1899–1917; major Etemenanki work in 1913.
- **Evidence:** excavation publications and plans.

### Hansjörg Schmid

- **Connection:** conducted renewed investigation of the ziggurat core in 1962 and produced the major architectural reassessment published in 1995.
- **Importance:** challenged simple acceptance of the 90-metre-high reconstruction.

### Andrew R. George

- **Connection:** editor of Babylonian topographical texts and author of a major synthesis of the tower’s archaeology, history, and inscriptions.
- **Importance:** distinguished textual idealization from recoverable architectural history and published the Tower of Babylon stele inscription.

### Olof Pedersén

- **Connection:** reconstructed the stele’s Babylon findspot and documented its transfer from the Schøyen Collection to the Iraq Museum.
- **Publication:** 2024 issue of *Iraq*.

### Martin Schøyen and the Schøyen Collection

- **Connection:** private custodian of the Tower of Babylon stele for approximately twenty-eight years.
- **Transfer:** stele subsequently given to Iraq following contested proceedings concerning Iraqi antiquities.
- **Present status:** no longer the stele’s custodian.

### Iraqi State Board of Antiquities and Heritage

- **Connection:** legal and administrative custodian of Babylon and the surviving Etemenanki foundations.
- **Present role:** protection, research authorization, conservation, and visitor management.

### UNESCO and ICOMOS

- **Connection:** assessment and monitoring of Babylon as a World Heritage property.
- **Role:** advisory and treaty-based oversight; neither owns the monument.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Cosmic architecture

Etemenanki’s ceremonial name joined heaven and earth. The tower physically raised Marduk’s sanctuary above the alluvial plain and symbolically established Babylon as a cosmic center.

This symbolism should not be translated into the unsupported claim that ziggurats were literally designed as stairways for humans to climb to heaven. Their cultic meaning centered on divine presence, sacred elevation, and the ordering of city and cosmos.

### 6.2 Building ritual

Nabopolassar’s inscription records a formal royal building ceremony:

- Purification or recovery of the ancient foundation.
- Manufacture of ceremonial tools.
- Participation by king, princes, officials, and workers.
- Basket-carrying by the royal household.
- Foundation work in bitumen and brick.
- Prayer that later kings respect the inscription and monument.

These actions converted forced imperial labor into a public theology of shared service to Marduk.

### 6.3 Summit rite

Herodotus reports that a single local woman chosen by the god slept in the summit chamber beside a couch and golden table. He compares this with ritual traditions at Egyptian Thebes and Patara.

Evidentiary status:

- **Attested report:** Herodotus’ fifth-century text.
- **Claim attributed to Babylonian priests:** that the god visited the bed.
- **Not independently established:** exact selection procedure, frequency, or whether the practice continued throughout the tower’s history.

### 6.4 Akītu and processional setting

Babylon’s New Year festival centered on Marduk, Esagila, the Processional Way, and the Akītu temple outside the inner city. Etemenanki formed part of this sacred landscape and dominated its skyline.

Direct evidence that a particular annual procession ascended the ziggurat is limited. It is safer to say that the tower monumentalized the theology enacted by the festival than to insert it into every ritual stage without textual support.

### 6.5 Legitimation

Possession and restoration of Etemenanki conferred several kinds of legitimacy:

- Babylonian kingship as service to Marduk.
- Imperial stewardship of Babylon.
- Continuity with ancient foundations.
- Power to mobilize labor and resources.
- Reconciliation after conquest or civil war.

Esarhaddon, Nabopolassar, Nebuchadnezzar, Alexander, and Antiochus I each used proposed or actual temple restoration to negotiate authority in Babylon.

### 6.6 Access and taboo

The summit was restricted sacred space. Herodotus’ account implies that ordinary visitors could ascend part of the tower and rest on seats, while the summit chamber had limited ritual access.

No complete Babylonian regulation governing who could climb, touch, or enter Etemenanki survives. Modern claims that only kings or priests could ever ascend are plausible generalizations but not securely documented rules for all periods.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 Genesis 11:1–9

The earliest textual Tower of Babel narrative is Genesis 11:1–9, preserved in Hebrew manuscripts much later than its composition. Dating proposals range widely, from monarchic-period composition to exilic or post-exilic formation. Many scholars see its present context in the Pentateuch as the result of first-millennium BCE literary development.

The story says:

- The whole earth had one language and shared words.
- Migrants settled in a plain in the land of Shinar.
- They manufactured bricks and used bitumen for mortar.
- They proposed to build a city and a tower “with its top in the heavens.”
- Their purposes were to make a name and avoid being scattered.
- YHWH descended to see the city and tower.
- YHWH observed that their unity and common language enabled unlimited projects.
- Their language was confused so they could not understand one another.
- They were scattered over the earth.
- Construction of the city ceased.
- The city was called Babel because there YHWH “confused” the language of the earth.

Important negative evidence:

- Nimrod is not named in Genesis 11.
- No king commissions the tower.
- The builders are humanity collectively.
- The tower’s materials are specified, but not its measurements or stages.
- God does not explicitly knock it down.
- No summit shrine or idol appears.
- The narrative explains linguistic and geographical diversity through theological etiology.

### 7.2 Association with Etemenanki

The association probably grew because Etemenanki supplied a compelling historical background:

- Babylonian location.
- Brick and bitumen.
- Monumental height.
- Imperial labor.
- A name connecting heaven and earth.
- Rebuilding in the age of Judean exile.

The stele’s claim that Nebuchadnezzar mobilized “all countries everywhere” is strikingly relevant to the Babel tradition. It does not prove that multilingual confusion halted the works. Nebuchadnezzar’s inscription says the project was completed.

The Schøyen Collection’s earlier suggestion that Genesis directly records workplace confusion among Nebuchadnezzar’s multilingual laborers is a modern hypothesis, not an established historical explanation.

### 7.3 Nimrod

Genesis 10 describes Nimrod as a mighty figure whose kingdom began with Babel and other Mesopotamian cities. Later Jewish and Christian interpreters joined Genesis 10 and 11 and made Nimrod builder or tyrant of the tower.

This is a **later exegetical synthesis**. Genesis itself never states that Nimrod built it.

In subsequent traditions Nimrod became:

- A rebel against God.
- An idolatrous ruler.
- A giant or hunter.
- An astrologer.
- A persecutor of Abraham.
- A figure who intended to attack heaven.

These elaborations belong to Jewish, Christian, and Islamic reception, not Etemenanki’s documented custody chain.

### 7.4 Assault on heaven

Later traditions transformed “a tower with its top in the heavens” into a literal military assault on God. Builders shoot arrows upward; arrows return bloodied; some attempt to install an idol or wage war on heaven.

These details do not occur in Genesis 11. They are later moral and cosmological elaborations.

### 7.5 Physical destruction

Popular retellings often say God destroyed the tower by earthquake, fire, wind, or lightning. Genesis says that language was confused, humanity was scattered, and city-building ceased. It does not describe the tower’s collapse.

The image of divine physical destruction is therefore a later accretion, reinforced by the sight of ruined Mesopotamian ziggurats and by artistic convention.

### 7.6 The tower as origin of languages

Genesis presents language differentiation as divine action. Later chronological systems attempted to identify a fixed number of resulting languages—often seventy or seventy-two—by combining the story with genealogical lists.

Historical linguistics does not treat the story as an empirical account of language diversification. Its significance is theological, literary, and cultural.

### 7.7 The tower as tomb of Belus

Greek and Roman writers sometimes called Babylon’s tower or sanctuary the “tomb of Belus.” This reflects Hellenistic interpretation of Babylonian sacred architecture and possibly euhemerism, in which a god was treated as an ancient king or hero.

Babylonian sources do not define Etemenanki as Marduk’s tomb. The summit temple and the “tomb of Belus” should therefore be kept distinct as Babylonian and classical interpretive frames.

### 7.8 Xerxes as archetypal destroyer

Later classical histories made Xerxes responsible for demolishing the monument. This fitted broader Greek depictions of Xerxes as a violator of temples. Modern scholarship treats the claim cautiously because contemporary Babylonian evidence does not document the alleged total destruction.

### 7.9 Alexander’s impossible restoration

The enormous labor allegedly required to clear the ruin became a classical theme. Strabo’s 10,000 laborers working for two months dramatize the monument’s scale and the futility of restoration.

The factual core—Alexander ordered clearance and contemplated rebuilding—is credible. Exact numbers and motives are not independently confirmed.

### 7.10 Birs Nimrud legends

Birs Nimrud’s scorched-looking brick core generated stories that divine fire had blasted the Tower of Babel. Travelers interpreted vitrified or fused masonry as proof of biblical judgment.

Cuneiform identification showed that Birs Nimrud was the Borsippa ziggurat. The physical observations were real; the identification and causal narrative were mistaken.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Monument

**Location:** Archaeological Site of Babylon, Babil Governorate, Iraq, within the UNESCO World Heritage property.

**Condition:** Destroyed as an above-ground ziggurat. Foundations and robbed structural traces survive in situ. Much of the footprint is low, wet, and seasonally or permanently water-filled.

**Ownership and control:** Iraqi state archaeological property, administered by national and provincial heritage authorities.

**Access:** Babylon is open to visitors under Iraqi site management, although access to individual archaeological sectors may be restricted for conservation, security, or research. The Etemenanki footprint is not an intact climbable monument.

**Conservation concerns:**

- High groundwater and waterlogging.
- Salinization and erosion of mud brick.
- Poor drainage.
- Earlier excavation trenches.
- Modern construction and reconstruction elsewhere at Babylon.
- Visitor and infrastructure pressure.
- Legacy damage from military occupation.
- Need for a comprehensive conservation plan and consistent documentation.

UNESCO’s 2025 decision continued to request an augmented management and conservation framework, impact assessment procedures, risk planning, and prioritized emergency interventions.

### 8.2 Tower of Babylon stele

**Present location:** Iraq Museum, Baghdad.

**Former designation:** Schøyen Collection MS 2063.

**Status:** Returned to Iraq after approximately twenty-eight years in the Norwegian private collection.

**Importance:** Contemporary or near-contemporary Neo-Babylonian visual and textual evidence for Etemenanki’s appearance and royal rebuilding ideology.

A current Iraq Museum accession number was not located in the published sources consulted. It should not be assumed that the former private number MS 2063 remains its museum identifier.

### 8.3 Nabopolassar cylinder

One principal Etemenanki foundation cylinder is:

- **Institution:** University of Pennsylvania Museum of Archaeology and Anthropology.
- **Catalogue:** CBS 9090.
- **CDLI record:** P464300 / CDLI tablet record 780 in the current interface.
- **Find history:** reported from a niche in Etemenanki in the nineteenth century.
- **Material:** clay.
- **Content:** Nabopolassar’s foundation and rebuilding narrative.

### 8.4 Nebuchadnezzar cylinder

A major cylinder is:

- **Institution:** Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York.
- **Accession:** 86.11.370.
- **Date:** reign of Nebuchadnezzar II, 605–562 BCE.
- **Material:** clay.
- **Content:** reconstruction of Etemenanki.
- **Status:** portable documentary witness, not the monument itself.

### 8.5 Esagila tablet

- **Institution:** Musée du Louvre, Paris.
- **Accession:** AO 6555.
- **Date of copy:** 229 BCE.
- **Provenance:** Uruk.
- **Content:** mathematical dimensions of Esagila and Etemenanki.
- **Status:** later copy of an older scholarly text; not an architectural blueprint recovered from the ziggurat.

### 8.6 No current ownership or repatriation dispute over the foundations

The foundations remain in Iraq and are not subject to a competing foreign ownership claim. Disputes concern conservation, interpretation, excavation history, and portable antiquities rather than title to the fixed monument.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Pre-archaeological visual form

Before Etemenanki’s footprint and texts were understood, artists supplied forms absent from Genesis:

- Multi-level spirals.
- Classical arches and colonnades.
- Mountain-like ruins.
- Minaret-inspired cones.
- Gothic or Renaissance construction sites.

These images belong to the biblical tower’s reception, not reliable architectural reconstruction.

### 9.2 Pieter Bruegel the Elder

Bruegel produced at least two surviving *Tower of Babel* paintings in 1563:

- The large Vienna panel in the Kunsthistorisches Museum.
- The smaller Rotterdam panel in Museum Boijmans Van Beuningen.

His tower resembles the Roman Colosseum, with repetitive arches wrapped around an incomplete circular mass. It bears little architectural resemblance to a mud-brick Mesopotamian ziggurat.

Bruegel’s images profoundly shaped the modern visual archetype of Babel as:

- Gigantic.
- Circular or spiral.
- Simultaneously advanced and structurally doomed.
- A metaphor for imperial ambition, urban diversity, failed coordination, and political order.

Recent scholarship warns against reducing the paintings to a single sermon about pride. Their Antwerp context implicates multilingual commerce, civic cooperation, labor organization, urban development, sovereignty, and social fracture.

### 9.3 Gustave Doré

Doré’s nineteenth-century biblical illustrations popularized a vast, receding, conical Babel. The form owes more to romantic Orientalism and visible Middle Eastern monumental ruins than to Etemenanki’s excavated plan.

### 9.4 Archaeological reconstruction models

Koldewey and later Schmid generated square, staged reconstructions grounded in:

- The excavated base.
- AO 6555.
- comparative ziggurat architecture.
- evidence for frontal stairs.
- ancient images, particularly after publication of the stele.

Museum models often choose a precise seven-stage, 90-metre-high form. Such models visualize one interpretation and should not be mistaken for a fully known elevation.

### 9.5 Fritz Lang’s *Metropolis* (1927)

The film includes a Babel sequence in which planners and laborers fail to understand one another: “the mediator between head and hands must be the heart.” Babel becomes a myth of class division and communicative breakdown in industrial modernity.

The film’s monumental city and tower imagery helped transfer Babel from biblical illustration to science fiction and dystopian architecture.

### 9.6 Literature and language metaphors

Major modern uses include:

- Jorge Luis Borges, “The Library of Babel” (1941), transforming Babel into an infinite combinatorial library.
- “Babel” as a conventional name for translation software, multilingual corpora, programming systems, communications projects, and linguistic confusion.
- Architectural criticism using Babel as shorthand for the skyscraper, megaproject, or failed universalism.

Most such uses concern Genesis and post-biblical symbolism rather than Etemenanki itself.

### 9.7 Iraqi national reception

Modern Iraqi governments have used Babylon as a national and political symbol. Saddam Hussein’s rebuilding program placed modern inscribed bricks over ancient remains and presented the regime as heir to Nebuchadnezzar.

The interventions largely reconstructed palaces, walls, and visitor settings rather than Etemenanki. Nevertheless, the ziggurat’s fame contributed to Babylon’s use as a stage for festivals, sovereignty, and historical continuity.

### 9.8 Heritage tourism

Etemenanki’s identification as the “Tower of Babel” gives an otherwise visually modest, waterlogged foundation exceptional tourism value. Visitors frequently arrive expecting a standing tower and instead encounter an archaeological negative: the footprint left by dismantling, brick robbing, erosion, and excavation.

Commercial reconstructions commonly blend:

- The seven-stage Esagila-tablet model.
- Bruegel’s visual drama.
- biblical scenes of language confusion.
- Neo-Babylonian glazed decoration for which no direct evidence exists on Etemenanki’s upper stages.

### 9.9 Modern hoaxes and overclaims

Common unsupported claims include:

- Archaeologists found the intact Tower of Babel.
- A surviving tower at Birs Nimrud is Etemenanki.
- The Tower of Babylon stele proves every detail of Genesis.
- Judean captives are documented by name as tower builders.
- Alexander destroyed a complete standing tower.
- Xerxes’ destruction is securely documented in contemporary Persian records.
- Etemenanki was exactly 91 metres high.
- Its upper stages were certainly painted in seven planetary colors.
- It functioned primarily as an astronomical observatory.

Each goes beyond the available evidence.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Cuneiform decipherment and localization

Before cuneiform decipherment, biblical and classical texts dominated identification. Large ruins such as Birs Nimrud appeared more plausible than Etemenanki’s low, robbed site.

Cuneiform place-names and inscriptions separated:

- Babylon from Borsippa.
- Etemenanki from E-ur-me-imin-anki.
- Marduk’s Esagila complex from Nabû’s Ezida complex.

### 10.2 Koldewey’s excavation

Koldewey’s work established the physical footprint and its relationship to Babylon’s sacred topography. His publication remains primary evidence because parts of the site subsequently deteriorated or became inaccessible.

Limitations include:

- Excavation methods of the period.
- Difficult groundwater conditions.
- Incomplete stratigraphic resolution.
- Reliance on architecture already heavily robbed.
- Preliminary interpretations revised by later research.

### 10.3 The Esagila tablet

The publication and interpretation of AO 6555 transformed reconstruction by supplying a numerical seven-level scheme. Early reconstructions often treated it as a literal architectural survey.

Later analysis emphasized its mathematical character. Babylonian scholarly texts could preserve ideal proportions, canonical dimensions, symbolic numbers, or exercises framed as real architecture. The tablet remains indispensable, but its figures must be tested against archaeology rather than simply imposed upon it.

### 10.4 Schmid’s reconstruction

Schmid’s 1995 monograph is the principal modern architectural study. It:

- Re-examined Koldewey’s plans.
- Integrated the 1962 fieldwork.
- Proposed building phases.
- reassessed stair arrangements.
- Compared archaeological evidence with AO 6555.
- produced a lower and structurally argued reconstruction.

Its conclusions remain influential but not universally final, particularly because the missing elevation leaves several solutions possible.

### 10.5 Andrew George’s synthesis

George’s “The Tower of Babel: Archaeology, History and Cuneiform Texts” remains a key historical synthesis. It stresses:

- The near-total obscurity of the tower’s second-millennium history.
- The importance of distinguishing composition dates from manuscript dates.
- The Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian rebuilding phases.
- Documentary evidence for Alexander and Seleucid clearance.
- The mathematical and idealizing nature of some dimensional evidence.

His editions of *Babylonian Topographical Texts* and the Tower of Babylon stele are foundational.

### 10.6 The stele’s changing status

When the Tower of Babylon stele was published from a private collection, its archaeological provenance was unknown. Pedersén’s recovery of its reported find circumstances—Babylon, early 1990s, old Amran trench—changed the discussion materially.

Open issues include:

- Its original installation point.
- When and why portions of its inscription were erased.
- When it was displaced into a later archaeological level.
- The complete history between discovery and entry into the Schøyen Collection.
- Its formal Iraq Museum registration and display history.

### 10.7 Genesis dating and dependence

Scholars disagree on when Genesis 11 reached its present form and how directly it engages Babylonian monuments.

Positions include:

- An older Israelite tale later integrated into the Primeval History.
- An exilic response to Neo-Babylonian imperial ideology.
- A post-exilic critique of concentrated urban and imperial power.
- A broadly Mesopotamian building myth without dependence on a single ziggurat.

The physical Etemenanki cannot settle the literary dating. Conversely, Genesis cannot supply missing architectural details about Etemenanki.

### 10.8 Herodotus

Debate concerns:

- Whether he visited Babylon.
- Whether his stadium measurements are literal.
- Whether his eight towers equal seven stages plus a sanctuary.
- Whether the spiral ascent is accurate.
- Whether summit rites were contemporary practice or priestly report.
- Whether descriptions of upper and lower sanctuaries have been conflated.

His text is an ancient witness, but not a substitute for excavation.

### 10.9 Discredited or weakened theories

The following remain popular but are no longer strong scholarly positions:

- **Birs Nimrud is Etemenanki.**
- **Xerxes certainly annihilated Babylonian religion.**
- **Alexander encountered a completely standing tower.**
- **AO 6555 is a straightforward construction blueprint.**
- **Ziggurats were primarily astronomical observatories.**
- **Genesis is an eyewitness construction report.**
- **The Tower of Babylon stele depicts the biblical language miracle.**

## 12. Sources

### Primary and ancient sources

- **Genesis 11:1–9.** Hebrew narrative of the city and tower at Babel. The tower is unnamed and is not explicitly identified with Etemenanki.
- **Sennacherib’s royal inscriptions.** Contemporary Assyrian claims concerning the destruction of Babylon in 689 BCE.
- **Esarhaddon’s royal inscriptions.** Records of rebuilding Babylon, Esagila, and Etemenanki.
- **Nabopolassar 6 and related Etemenanki cylinders.** Contemporary foundation narrative, available through the Royal Inscriptions of Babylonia online corpus.
- **Nebuchadnezzar II’s Etemenanki inscriptions.** Royal completion and restoration claims.
- **Tower of Babylon stele.** Neo-Babylonian inscribed and figured stone, formerly MS 2063, now Iraq Museum.
- **Tintir = Babylon and related topographical texts.** Temple names and sacred topography.
- **Esagila tablet, Louvre AO 6555.** Uruk copy dated 229 BCE, preserving mathematical dimensions.
- **Babylonian chronicles and astronomical diaries.** Evidence for Alexander and Seleucid-period work.
- **Antiochus Cylinder.** Royal rebuilding program dated to 268 BCE.
- **Herodotus, *Histories* 1.181–183.** Description of the sanctuary and tower of Zeus Belos.
- **Diodorus Siculus, Books 2 and 17.** Classical traditions concerning Babylon and Alexander.
- **Strabo, *Geography* 16.1.5.** “Tomb of Belus,” Xerxes, and Alexander’s attempted clearance.
- **Arrian, *Anabasis of Alexander* 7.17.** Alexander’s proposed rebuilding and the ruined temple.
