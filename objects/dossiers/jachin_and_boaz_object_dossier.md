# Jachin and Boaz: Object Dossier

**Proposed filename:** `jachin_and_boaz_object_dossier.md`  
**Research current to:** 6 September 2026

## Evidentiary key

- **Textual attestation:** what an ancient source says, without presuming that every narrated event occurred.
- **Documented historical context:** independently supported persons, places, practices, or events.
- **Material/comparative evidence:** archaeological evidence for analogous objects or architecture, not for these pillars themselves.
- **Scholarly inference:** a reconstruction argued from texts or comparanda.
- **Religious tradition:** an interpretation received within a religious community.
- **Legend or later elaboration:** a story not present in the earliest sources.
- **Modern symbolic reception:** a later object, image, or practice borrowing the pillars’ names or form.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Identification

Jachin and Boaz are the proper names assigned in the Hebrew Bible to two monumental metal pillars erected at the entrance of the Jerusalem temple attributed to King Solomon. Their principal description occurs in 1 Kings 7:13–22, with inventory notices in 1 Kings 7:41–42 and parallel or retrospective descriptions in 2 Chronicles 3:15–17; 4:12–13; 2 Kings 25:13–17; Jeremiah 27:19–22; and Jeremiah 52:17–23. The biblical texts ultimately say that Babylonian forces broke up the pillars and carried their bronze to Babylon. No surviving artifact has been authenticated as either pillar or as a fragment of one.

The pair should therefore be classified as a **lost or destroyed monumental architectural-cultic furnishing known only through texts**, rather than as a purely mythological object. Their actual existence remains archaeologically unverified, but their destruction is narrated as part of an otherwise historically grounded Babylonian sack of Jerusalem.

### 1.2 Names, languages, and orientation

The Masoretic Hebrew names are:

- **יָכִין — Yāḵīn / Yakhin / Jachin / Jakin.**
- **בֹּעַז — Bōʿaz / Boaz.**

“Jachin” reflects conventional English biblical spelling; “Yakhin” more closely represents the Hebrew. Greek textual traditions variously transliterate the names, while Josephus’s Greek account appears in English translations as *Jachin* and *Booz* or *Boaz*. Latin Christian tradition commonly used *Jachin* and *Booz/Boaz*; German sources use *Jachin und Boas*; French, *Jakin et Boaz*.

In 1 Kings 7:21, the pillar on the **right** is named Jachin and that on the **left** Boaz. Because the temple is normally reconstructed as facing east, “right” when facing outward from the temple would be south and “left” north. Second Chronicles 3:17 makes that geographic interpretation explicit in many translations: Jachin on the south, Boaz on the north. Orientation in later Masonic diagrams is inconsistent because lodge orientation, the observer’s viewpoint, and different ritual traditions reverse the apparent left and right.

### 1.3 Etymology

**Jachin** is most naturally understood as a causative verbal form of the Semitic root כון, “to be firm, established,” hence “he establishes,” “he will establish,” or “may he establish.” The implicit subject may be Israel’s God, the king, or an unnamed agent. Jachin also occurs as a personal name elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible, so it need not have been coined for the pillar.

**Boaz** is more difficult. The traditional vocalization is identical to the personal name Boaz in Ruth. A common proposal divides it as *bō ʿōz* or revocalizes it as *bəʿōz*, “in him is strength” or “with strength.” The two names can then be read as a continuous formula: “He establishes [it] in strength.” Another reconstruction treats them as abbreviated monumental inscriptions or acclamations concerning the Davidic king: “YHWH will establish [the king’s throne]” and “in [YHWH’s] strength [the king rejoices].” The biblical text does not provide an interpretation.

Older theories identified the names with deities, divine epithets, royal ancestors, or corrupted Phoenician names. These remain hypotheses. There is no securely attested ancient inscription on the pillars themselves, and the statement that Solomon “called its name” Jachin or Boaz does not establish that either name was physically engraved on it. Modern images that place a large letter **J** or **B** on the shaft derive principally from Masonic and occult iconography, not from Kings.

### 1.4 Materials

The Hebrew term is **נְחֹשֶׁת, nəḥōšet**, which can denote copper or a copper alloy. Older English Bibles usually say “brass”; modern translations normally say “bronze.” No sample exists, so the alloy composition cannot be established scientifically. “Bronze” is a reasonable conventional translation but not a laboratory finding.

First Kings 7:45 describes Hiram’s temple furnishings collectively as polished, burnished, or smoothed bronze. Verse 47 says Solomon did not weigh the bronze because of its great quantity. These statements belong to the literary inventory and do not yield an independently measured mass.

### 1.5 Form and dimensions in the Hebrew textual tradition

The fullest Masoretic description gives each shaft:

- height: **18 cubits**;
- circumference: **12 cubits**;
- capital: **5 cubits** high in 1 Kings 7 and Jeremiah 52;
- hollow construction;
- wall thickness: **four fingers**, supplied explicitly in Jeremiah 52:21;
- decoration: cast-bronze capitals, lattice or network, chains, rows of pomegranates, and lily work.

Using plausible ancient cubits of roughly 44.5–52.5 cm produces approximate dimensions rather than secure conversions:

- shaft height: about **8.0–9.5 m**;
- circumference: about **5.3–6.3 m**;
- external diameter if circular: about **1.7–2.0 m**;
- capital height: about **2.2–2.6 m**;
- total shaft-plus-capital height: approximately **10.2–12.1 m**.

A four-finger wall is roughly 7–8 cm under common reconstructions. If all measurements described a simple uniform cylinder, each shaft alone could have required metal on the order of tens of metric tonnes. That calculation is illustrative only: the cubit, profile, taper, wall uniformity, casting method, and textual accuracy are uncertain.

### 1.6 Capitals and ornament

First Kings 7:16–20 describes two cast capitals, five cubits high, placed on the shafts. Their ornament involved:

- lattice or network;
- twisted chains;
- two rows of pomegranates;
- a lily or lotus-like upper form;
- rounded or “bowl-shaped” portions whose exact geometry is obscured by difficult Hebrew.

First Kings 7:20 gives 200 pomegranates associated with each capital; 7:42 totals 400 for the pair. Jeremiah 52:23 says there were ninety-six pomegranates “toward the four winds” or “on the sides,” with one hundred visible on the network. This may distinguish visible and total ornaments, preserve a different inventory tradition, or reflect textual damage. Second Chronicles 3:16 describes chains and one hundred pomegranates, but its compressed wording is not fully congruent with Kings.

“Lily work” does not mean that the entire capital was necessarily a naturalistic lily. Reconstructions have proposed lotus bowls, palmettes, inverted calyxes, or composite Phoenician-style capitals. No reconstruction commands consensus.

### 1.7 Major textual discrepancies

The descriptions survive in several partially inconsistent textual forms:

| Feature | 1 Kings 7, Masoretic tradition | Greek 1 Kings | 2 Chronicles 3 | 2 Kings 25 | Jeremiah 52 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Shaft height | 18 cubits each | commonly 18 | 35 cubits, wording disputed | 18 | 18 MT; some Greek evidence differs |
| Circumference | 12 cubits | some witnesses give 14 | not clearly preserved | 12 implied in parallels | 12 |
| Capital height | 5 cubits | textual variation | 5 | 3 | 5 |
| Wall thickness | not in MT at this point | four fingers in one Greek form | not stated | not stated | four fingers |
| Pomegranates | 200 per capital / 400 total | variant arrangement | 100 in compressed account | pomegranates mentioned | 96 plus 100 in a difficult notice |

Second Chronicles’ “thirty-five cubits” has been explained as:

1. the combined length of two approximately eighteen-cubit shafts, allowing for rounding;
2. a measurement taken while the shafts lay end to end;
3. an erroneous sum derived from numbers in Kings;
4. a scribal corruption;
5. evidence of a different architectural or textual tradition.

No explanation is demonstrable. The capital discrepancy between three cubits in 2 Kings 25:17 and five in Kings’ construction account and Jeremiah 52 could reflect a copying error, different points of measurement, alteration of the capitals during their working life, or distinct source traditions.

### 1.8 Construction and inscriptions

The texts describe the shafts and capitals as cast metal. Jeremiah’s statement that the shafts were hollow excludes solid cast cylinders if taken literally. Large ancient hollow castings could be produced in sections or around cores, but the texts do not say whether the shafts were cast as single pieces, assembled from drums or plates, or joined after casting.

No inscription is quoted, no maker’s mark is reported, and no ancient representation is securely identifiable as Jachin or Boaz. The names may have been ceremonial names rather than labels visible on the objects.

### 1.9 Structural or freestanding?

The wording says Hiram “set up” the pillars at or for the porch. Many scholars regard them as freestanding because:

- they appear in the inventory of bronze furnishings rather than in the structural building account;
- monumental freestanding entrance pillars are known from ancient Near Eastern imagery and architecture;
- their elaborate capitals and named identities suggest monuments as much as roof supports.

Others have reconstructed them as engaged or load-bearing porch columns. The Hebrew prepositions and the uncertain porch design do not settle the question. Tel Moẓa, ʿAin Dara, Tell Tayinat, and other temples provide broad architectural comparisons, not a blueprint or proof for Jerusalem.

### 1.10 Condition and surviving components

According to 2 Kings 25:13 and Jeremiah 52:17, Babylonian forces **broke the pillars into pieces** and transported their bronze to Babylon. There is no securely documented later reference to identifiable fragments, capitals, pomegranates, or recycled metal from them. Their present physical condition is therefore best classified as:

> **Destroyed and untraced; constituent metal presumably dispersed or recycled, with no authenticated survivor.**

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Traditional manufacture narrative

The biblical construction narrative attributes the project to Solomon and its bronze work to a Tyrian craftsman named Hiram or Huram. First Kings 7:13–14 calls him the son of a widowed Israelite woman from Naphtali and a Tyrian bronze worker. Second Chronicles 2:13–14 instead gives his maternal affiliation as Dan and expands his competence to metals, stone, wood, textiles, and engraving. These are variant literary portraits, not two independently documented biographies.

The sequence in Kings is:

1. Solomon sends for Hiram from Tyre.
2. Hiram casts the two shafts and their capitals.
3. He adds networks, chains, pomegranates, and lily work.
4. The pillars are erected at the temple porch.
5. Solomon names the right pillar Jachin and the left Boaz.
6. The pair enters the catalogue of bronze furnishings made for the temple.

First Kings 7:46 says the bronze furnishings were cast in the Jordan plain, in clay-rich ground between **Succoth** and **Zarethan**. Second Chronicles 4:17 gives the related form Zeredah/Zeredathah. If historical, this indicates a major foundry operation in the Jordan Valley and subsequent transport of very heavy components uphill to Jerusalem. The exact archaeological locations and workshops have not been established.

No contract, price, quarry or mine record, metallurgical sample, or excavated mold can be connected securely to the pillars. The origin of the copper and alloying metal is unknown.

### 2.2 Date claimed by the narrative

First Kings 6:1 places temple construction in Solomon’s fourth regnal year, conventionally located in the mid-tenth century BCE under traditional biblical chronologies. The temple is said to have taken seven years. The pillars would belong near the end of that construction campaign.

That date is **a date supplied by the biblical narrative**, not a scientifically measured date for the pillars. Archaeological debate about the scale and centralization of tenth-century Judah remains substantial, and the Temple Mount itself has not undergone a controlled modern excavation capable of recovering the building’s complete Iron Age plan.

### 2.3 Earliest datable attestation

The earliest surviving full attestation is textual, in the Book of Kings. The manuscripts themselves are much later—the principal complete Masoretic codices are medieval—but the work contains pre-exilic, exilic, and possibly post-exilic material. The common critical model places major Deuteronomistic composition or editing in the late seventh and sixth centuries BCE, with the fall of Jerusalem in 587/586 BCE furnishing the terminus for the book’s concluding form.

Accordingly:

- **Earliest securely datable extant evidence:** manuscript witnesses and ancient translations transmitted centuries after the events.
- **Probable date of the underlying pillar account:** unresolved; it may preserve a monarchic temple inventory, an exilic recollection, literary reconstruction, or a combination.
- **What the earliest account does say:** two named bronze pillars, Hiram’s manufacture, dimensions, capitals, ornament, and placement.
- **What it does not establish independently:** that Solomon commissioned them in the tenth century, that Hiram was a historical individual, or that the transmitted dimensions are mechanically accurate.

Recent literary scholarship has emphasized that 1 Kings 7:13–47 is a crafted “monumental inventory”: it may preserve technical memory while also functioning as a textual monument to the vanished temple.

### 2.4 Later textual development

Jeremiah 27, set during Zedekiah’s reign, lists “the pillars, the sea, the stands, and the rest of the vessels” as objects remaining after the deportation of 597 BCE and predicts their eventual removal to Babylon. Second Kings 25 and Jeremiah 52 then narrate that removal after Jerusalem’s final fall.

Chronicles, generally dated to the Persian or early Hellenistic period, retells the construction with compressed and sometimes divergent measurements. Josephus, writing in the late first century CE, largely harmonizes and embellishes the biblical description in *Jewish Antiquities* 8. He is a witness to reception of the biblical account, not an eyewitness to the First Temple pillars.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because no physical object survives, every link is textual. The following separates narrated custody from independently documented context.

### 3.1 Jordan Valley manufacture

**Claim:** Hiram’s bronze work was cast for Solomon in the Jordan plain between Succoth and Zarethan.

- **Source:** 1 Kings 7:13–47; parallel in 2 Chronicles 4.
- **Mechanism:** royal commission and manufacture.
- **Custodian:** Solomon’s administration, according to the narrative.
- **Evidentiary status:** biblical textual attestation; no connected foundry remains, molds, transport records, or metal samples.

### 3.2 Transport to Jerusalem and installation

**Claim:** The pillars were moved to Jerusalem and installed at the porch of YHWH’s temple; Solomon named them.

- **Source:** 1 Kings 7:21; 2 Chronicles 3:17.
- **Location:** the entrance or porch of the temple.
- **Mechanism:** installation as part of royal temple construction.
- **Status:** textual attestation.

The logistics are unstated. The route, transport apparatus, erection method, foundations, and installers are unknown.

### 3.3 Temple custody under the Judahite monarchy

From their installation until the Babylonian destruction, the texts treat the pillars as temple property under royal-priestly custody. There is no transaction transferring ownership to an individual.

Several royal removals of temple wealth are narrated:

- Shishak/Sheshonq’s seizure of temple and palace treasures under Rehoboam;
- Asa’s dispatch of silver and gold to Ben-hadad;
- Joash’s payment to Hazael;
- Ahaz’s alteration or removal of bronze furnishings;
- Hezekiah’s stripping of plated temple doors;
- the Babylonian deportation and removal of treasures in 597 BCE.

None of these passages explicitly says Jachin or Boaz was removed. Jeremiah 27:19–22 actually treats the pillars as still present after 597 BCE. Claims that an earlier invader took them and that later pillars were replacements lack textual or material support.

### 3.4 Possible royal ceremonial use

Second Kings 11:14 describes the boy-king Joash standing “by the pillar, according to custom” during his acclamation. Second Kings 23:3 similarly places Josiah “by the pillar” while making a covenant.

The noun may refer to one of the two named pillars, another temple column, or a designated royal platform or station. The passages do not say “Jachin” or “Boaz.” They are therefore evidence that a pillar-like location had royal-ceremonial significance, but they cannot securely place either king beside a particular named object.

### 3.5 The 597 BCE deportation and surviving furnishings

Jeremiah 27 distinguishes items already carried to Babylon with Jehoiachin from large bronze objects still in Jerusalem. Its list includes the pillars. If the passage preserves contemporary information, the objects survived the 597 deportation.

The prophecy says the remaining vessels would be taken to Babylon and remain there until divine visitation. Unlike Ezra’s later account of returned gold and silver temple vessels, no post-exilic source identifies the great pillars as restored. The destruction notices explain the difference: they were transported as broken raw material rather than intact ritual objects.

### 3.6 Destruction in 587/586 BCE

**Claim:** Chaldean/Babylonian troops broke up the bronze pillars and carried their metal to Babylon.

- **Sources:** 2 Kings 25:13–17; Jeremiah 52:17–23.
- **Commander in the surrounding narrative:** Nebuzaradan, captain of Nebuchadnezzar II’s guard.
- **Mechanism:** wartime seizure, deliberate dismemberment, and deportation as booty or recyclable metal.
- **Destination:** Babylon.
- **Status:** strong ancient textual attestation within accounts of a historically established Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem.

The wording is unambiguous about dismemberment. The act was not a ceremonial transfer or preservation in a collection: the shafts were rendered transportable as bronze. Their artistic identity ended even if their metal continued to exist.

### 3.7 Babylon and the end of the traceable chain

The sources do not identify:

- a Babylonian temple, palace, treasury, foundry, or workshop receiving the fragments;
- weights assigned to each pillar;
- an inventory entry naming Jachin or Boaz;
- later recasting;
- return under Cyrus;
- any surviving fragment.

The chain of custody therefore ends upon arrival of unspecified bronze in Babylon. Statements that the pillars were hidden beneath the Temple Mount, carried to Rome, preserved by secret orders, or recovered by crusaders are unsupported.

### 3.8 Second Temple discontinuity

The Second Temple was not the same building, and no secure early source says it possessed restored Jachin and Boaz. Ezekiel’s visionary temple has projecting elements translated in some versions as pillars or jambs, but it does not preserve the named pair as objects with an unbroken chain of custody.

Josephus’s descriptions of Herod’s temple include monumental entrance architecture, but not surviving Solomonic pillars under these names. Architectural echoes are not continuity of identity.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Did the pillars physically exist?

The strongest positive case is cumulative:

- several biblical passages describe the pair;
- the destruction accounts preserve technical details such as hollowness and wall thickness;
- Jeremiah 27 treats them as objects still present shortly before the destruction;
- freestanding or paired temple-entry pillars have ancient Near Eastern comparanda;
- monumental bronze casting was technically possible in the Iron Age.

The limiting evidence is equally important:

- no fragment, foundation, mold, inscription, or contemporaneous image has been identified;
- no independent Phoenician, Assyrian, Egyptian, or Babylonian record names them;
- the texts were edited and transmitted after the alleged tenth-century manufacture;
- the measurements are internally inconsistent;
- direct archaeological access to the Temple Mount is exceptionally limited.

The defensible conclusion is neither “certainly fictitious” nor “archaeologically proven.” They are **plausible historical furnishings preserved in literary sources but materially unattested**.

### 4.2 Is the Solomonic date authentic?

There is no direct dating procedure because no specimen survives. The date depends on accepting some historical core in Kings’ attribution of the temple to Solomon.

Comparative architecture demonstrates that the described plan is intelligible in an Iron Age Levantine and north-Syrian environment. The Iron Age temple at Tel Moẓa, begun in the late tenth or early ninth century BCE according to its excavators, has an east-facing long-room plan and entrance installations relevant to the debate. This supports architectural plausibility; it does not authenticate Solomon, Hiram, or the Jerusalem pillars.

### 4.3 Were they one original pair throughout?

No source reports replacement, repair, or recasting between installation and destruction. Yet discrepancies between construction and destruction accounts—especially capital heights—have generated proposals that the pillars were altered. This is possible but unproved. A scribal or source-history explanation is at least as plausible.

### 4.4 Were they structural?

Both positions remain open:

- **Freestanding interpretation:** supported by their classification among bronze furnishings and by entrance-pillar comparanda.
- **Structural interpretation:** supported by the natural architectural meaning of porch columns and some reconstructions of the façade.
- **Hybrid possibility:** they may have been visually independent monumental columns incorporated into the porch ensemble without being principal roof supports.

No surviving base or socket resolves the question.

### 4.5 Were they fire altars, cressets, incense stands, cosmic trees, or royal monuments?

All are scholarly interpretations, not textual identifications.

- William Robertson Smith and later writers proposed giant fire altars or cressets.
- Other theories identify them with sacred trees, cosmic pillars, mountains, phallic symbols, obelisks, divine presence, or threshold guardians.
- Political readings interpret their names as royal acclamations legitimating the Davidic dynasty.
- Some see their floral and fruit decoration as Edenic or creation symbolism.
- Others stress that paired columns visually mark the transition from public court to divine dwelling.

The strongest minimal interpretation is that they monumentalized the temple threshold. More specific ritual or cosmic functions remain disputed.

### 4.6 Rival claimants and alleged survivals

No museum or religious institution has an accessioned artifact broadly accepted by specialists as Jachin, Boaz, or a fragment of either. Later columns carrying the names are quotations, replicas, or symbolic successors.

There has been no radiocarbon analysis, metallography, lead-isotope analysis, thermoluminescence testing, or DNA testing of the original pillars because no authenticated specimen exists. Any modern object marketed as a literal fragment would require a documented excavation context, metallurgical study, epigraphy, and a chain through the Babylonian destruction; no such claimant currently meets those conditions.

### 4.7 Authority to authenticate

In antiquity, the narrative assigns authority through royal naming and temple installation. Later Jewish and Christian interpreters authenticated the story through canonical scripture. Modern historical inquiry instead requires textual criticism, archaeology, and materials analysis. Since there is no candidate artifact, no museum, laboratory, state, church, lodge, or rabbinical institution presently has a physical object that it can authenticate as the original.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Solomon

- **Connection:** commissioner, temple builder, recipient of Hiram’s work, installer or namer.
- **Date in traditional chronology:** tenth century BCE.
- **Source:** 1 Kings 6–7; 2 Chronicles 2–4.
- **Status:** biblical attribution; the precise historical scale of Solomon’s building program remains debated.

### Hiram/Huram, the bronze worker

- **Connection:** maker of the pillars and other bronze furnishings.
- **Origin:** Tyre.
- **Parentage:** son of a Naphtalite widow and Tyrian bronze worker in Kings; associated maternally with Dan in Chronicles.
- **Status:** textual figure; no independent workshop record.

He must not be confused with Hiram I, king of Tyre, though the shared name helped later legend merge royal, architectural, and craft traditions.

### Hiram, king of Tyre

- **Connection:** Solomon’s supplier and diplomatic counterpart in the wider temple-building story.
- **Direct connection to the pillars:** not explicitly their maker.
- **Status:** ancient royal figure also known through later Phoenician traditions quoted by Josephus, but the biblical synchronization is debated.

### YHWH, God of Israel

- **Connection:** divine owner of the temple and likely implied subject of interpretations such as “He establishes.”
- **Status:** named divine being central to the textual and ritual setting.
- **Caution:** Kings does not explicitly decode either pillar as an image of YHWH, and Israelite aniconism complicates claims that they represented the deity directly.

### The Jerusalem temple priesthood

- **Connection:** institutional keepers of the sanctuary in which the pillars stood.
- **Evidence:** inferred from temple administration and the destruction narratives.
- **Limitation:** no named priest is individually recorded maintaining or repairing them.

### Joash and Josiah

- **Connection:** each is described standing at “the pillar” in a royal covenant or enthronement setting.
- **Dates:** conventionally ninth and seventh centuries BCE.
- **Status:** possible but unproved interaction with one of the named pillars.

### Nebuchadnezzar II

- **Connection:** Babylonian king whose forces captured Jerusalem; ultimate royal beneficiary of the seized bronze.
- **Date:** 587/586 BCE destruction.
- **Status:** historically documented king; the pillars themselves are not named in surviving Babylonian records.

### Nebuzaradan

- **Connection:** commander overseeing the destruction and deportation described in Kings and Jeremiah.
- **Status:** textual agent in the pillars’ destruction context.

### Chaldean/Babylonian troops and metal workers

- **Connection:** broke up and transported the pillars.
- **Status:** collective agents named in the biblical destruction accounts.
- **Unknown:** who physically cut, crushed, loaded, weighed, transported, or recast the metal.

### Jeremiah and the Jeremiah tradition

- **Connection:** Jeremiah 27 uses the still-standing pillars as part of a prophecy about forthcoming deportation; Jeremiah 52 narrates their destruction.
- **Status:** literary-prophetic engagement, not custody.

### The Chronicler

- **Connection:** post-monarchic retelling and reinterpretation of their manufacture.
- **Date:** usually fourth or third century BCE, with wider proposals.
- **Effect:** preserved the names while transmitting a sharply different height.

### Josephus

- **Connection:** retold the construction in *Jewish Antiquities* 8, written in the 90s CE.
- **Status:** later historian and interpreter, not custodian or eyewitness.

### Freemasons and Masonic institutions

- **Connection:** adopted the temple, Hiram, and the pillars as central ritual architecture and vocabulary.
- **Secure attestation:** Samuel Prichard’s 1730 exposure *Masonry Dissected* already asks what two great pillars were seen in the porch and answers “Jachim and Boaz,” followed by biblical dimensions and ornaments.
- **Status:** modern symbolic network; no chain of physical descent from the Jerusalem objects.

### Arthur Edward Waite and Pamela Colman Smith

- **Connection:** Waite commissioned and explained, and Smith illustrated, the High Priestess seated between black **B** and white **J** pillars in the Rider–Waite–Smith Tarot.
- **Date:** deck issued 1909; *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot* appeared in 1910/1911 editions.
- **Status:** modern occult-artistic reception.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Function in the First Temple

The texts never specify a sacrifice, offering, lamp-lighting, libation, or prayer performed directly upon the pillars. They are not called altars, images, doorposts, or memorial stelae. Their certain textual functions are:

- marking and monumentalizing the porch;
- carrying named identities;
- displaying costly bronze craftsmanship;
- contributing to the visual program of networks, flowers, and pomegranates.

### 6.2 Threshold symbolism

The most restrained scholarly interpretation sees the pair as threshold monuments. Paired columns in ancient images can stand for a shrine façade or passage into sacred space. On this reading, Jachin and Boaz marked movement from the public court toward the temple interior and articulated the boundary between ordinary and divine space.

This interpretation fits the location but remains inferential. It does not establish later claims that initiates passed between them in a secret rite.

### 6.3 Royal legitimation

The names’ apparent vocabulary of establishment and strength permits a royal reading. The pillars may have embodied a prayer or proclamation that YHWH would establish the Davidic throne in strength. The “pillar” beside which Joash and Josiah stood may represent a formal royal station at the sanctuary entrance.

The political interpretation is plausible because the temple was a royal foundation and the Davidic covenant uses the language of establishing a house and throne. It is not explicitly stated in 1 Kings 7.

### 6.4 Creation, fertility, and sacred-tree readings

The capitals’ lilies, networks, and pomegranates have been interpreted as stylized vegetation. Together with the great bronze “Sea,” oxen, basins, cherubim, palms, and flowers elsewhere in the temple description, the pillars can be incorporated into a cosmic or Edenic reading of the sanctuary.

Such readings explain the decorative program coherently but should not be converted into a lost ritual manual. No source says worshippers venerated Jachin as one tree and Boaz as another.

### 6.5 Fire-pillar hypothesis

The proposal that the hollow shafts supported braziers or served as giant cressets draws on possible fire installations and on the symbolic pillars of cloud and fire in Exodus. It is ingenious but lacks explicit textual support and requires reconstructing features not mentioned in Kings.

### 6.6 Access and touch

The texts state no special touch taboo. Their porch location likely made the pillars visible from the court. Access to the temple precinct was nevertheless regulated by royal and priestly systems, and the king’s conventional station “by the pillar” suggests ceremonial ordering of space.

### 6.7 Political significance of loss

Their destruction had both material and symbolic force:

- Babylon acquired valuable bronze;
- the temple façade was stripped of monumental markers;
- Judah’s royal-sacral order was publicly dismantled;
- the later inventory preserved their measurements after the objects ceased to exist.

The detailed destruction notice converts wreckage into cultural memory. Their textual afterlife became a substitute for material survival.

### 6.8 Masonic ceremonial function

By 1730, an English Masonic catechism represented the initiate as approaching a symbolic Temple of Solomon and encountering Jachin and Boaz at its porch. Later lodge traditions placed actual or painted pillars in the room, sometimes bearing globes, pomegranates, or the initials J and B.

These are not replicas in an archaeological sense. They are ritual props and didactic symbols. Meanings vary by jurisdiction but commonly include:

- strength and establishment;
- the threshold into Masonic knowledge;
- the biblical Temple and the craft of building;
- dualities such as active/passive, celestial/terrestrial, light/dark, or male/female.

Many dualistic meanings belong to later esoteric systems and should not be projected back onto ancient Israel.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 “He will establish in strength”

**Tier:** ancient-name interpretation or scholarly reconstruction.

The names are frequently combined into a divine sentence: “He will establish [the temple or throne] in strength.” This is grammatically possible after revocalizing or interpreting Boaz, but no ancient source explicitly says the two were intended to form one sentence.

### 7.2 Pillars named for human ancestors

**Tier:** later interpretation.

Some Jewish and Christian commentators connected Boaz with David’s ancestor in Ruth, while others treated Jachin as a priestly or ancestral name. The biblical construction narrative does not identify either pillar with those people.

### 7.3 Jachin and Boaz as primordial or antediluvian pillars

**Tier:** conflated legend.

Medieval and early modern traditions knew stories of two antediluvian pillars on which the descendants of Seth or the sons of Lamech preserved the arts against destruction by fire and flood. In Masonic and occult retellings, these “two pillars” were sometimes visually or conceptually merged with Jachin and Boaz. The Genesis/antediluvian pillars and Solomon’s bronze pillars are separate traditions.

### 7.4 Hiram Abiff and the pillars

**Tier:** early modern Masonic legend.

Modern Freemasonry transformed the biblical bronze worker into **Hiram Abiff**, master architect and central figure in a drama of murder, fidelity, lost knowledge, and raising. The biblical Hiram is not murdered, is not called “Abiff” as a surname, and is not said to possess a secret word. The legend is a documented development of early modern Masonic ritual, securely visible in eighteenth-century exposures. It profoundly shaped modern understanding of Jachin and Boaz but supplies no historical evidence about the ancient objects.

### 7.5 Hidden or surviving pillars

**Tier:** unsupported modern speculation.

Popular occult and treasure narratives sometimes suggest that the pillars:

- survived intact beneath Jerusalem;
- concealed archives or sacred knowledge;
- were carried to Rome;
- were inherited by the Knights Templar;
- encode astronomical or Kabbalistic secrets;
- survive as the twin towers of particular churches.

The destruction texts explicitly say the bronze was broken up and removed. No documented provenance links any later columns to the original material.

### 7.6 Masculine and feminine pillars

**Tier:** modern esoteric elaboration.

Jachin and Boaz are often assigned opposite genders, colors, celestial bodies, or electrical and sexual polarities. These systems draw from Masonic, Hermetic, occult, and modern psychological symbolism. The Hebrew Bible gives neither pillar a gender, color, planet, zodiacal association, nor moral polarity.

### 7.7 Kabbalistic pillar identification

**Tier:** later synthesis.

Modern diagrams sometimes identify Jachin and Boaz with the right and left pillars of the Kabbalistic Tree of Life—mercy and severity—with a balancing middle pillar between them. The Tree’s fully developed diagrammatic tradition is medieval and early modern, not an Iron Age plan of Solomon’s porch. The synthesis is historically important in Western esotericism but not evidence for the pillars’ original function.

### 7.8 “While the pillars stand, the kingdom stands”

**Tier:** interpretive paraphrase, not a securely located ancient oracle.

The names easily invite a stability legend, and writers have treated them as guarantees of temple or dynasty. No primary text records a formal prophecy in those exact terms. Their eventual destruction could, however, be read retrospectively as the visible termination of royal and temple stability.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Physical status

Jachin and Boaz are **not extant**. The last narrated location of their constituent bronze is Babylon following the destruction of Jerusalem in 587/586 BCE. The metal’s subsequent destination is unknown.

No accession number exists. No authenticated fragment is publicly displayed, kept in a treasury, held by a private collector, or undergoing conservation. There is no legitimate present ownership or repatriation proceeding concerning the original pillars because no identifiable property survives.

### 8.2 Site

Their traditional site is the porch of the First Temple on the Jerusalem Temple Mount/Haram al-Sharif. The exact foundations of the First Temple porch have not been exposed in a controlled modern excavation. The site’s religious and political sensitivity sharply limits archaeological intervention.

### 8.3 Scientific examination

No scientific examination of an original component has occurred. Studies concern:

- philology and textual criticism;
- reconstruction from dimensions;
- comparative architecture and iconography;
- experimental or historical analysis of ancient bronze casting;
- excavation of analogous temples;
- the archaeology of Iron Age Jerusalem.

Claims that science has proved the pillars’ alloy, date, origin mine, exact weight, or survival are false unless clearly labeled as calculations or comparative hypotheses.

### 8.4 Present stand-ins

Real objects now carrying their identity include:

- Masonic lodge pillars, tracing boards, jewels, and educational models;
- synagogue columns and paired façade towers interpreted as allusions;
- Christian architectural pairs explicitly inscribed or traditionally identified as Jachin and Boaz;
- temple reconstruction models;
- tarot cards and occult diagrams;
- commercial sculptures and architectural ornaments.

These objects receive or quote the name; none has continuity of material identity.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Jewish architecture and ritual art

Paired columns flanking Torah arks became a powerful visual means of evoking the Jerusalem temple. Early modern German synagogue ark painting sometimes rendered engaged columns as Jachin and Boaz. Later synagogue façades used twin towers or pylons interpreted as temple-pillar references.

Such identifications require case-by-case evidence. Two columns in a synagogue are not automatically Jachin and Boaz: structural requirements and regional Christian architecture often explain double-naved interiors. The Old–New Synagogue in Prague, for example, has two central octagonal pillars sometimes interpreted symbolically, but architectural historians treat that reading cautiously.

The reconstructed Worms Synagogue and other German examples have generated stronger associations because of inscriptions or local interpretive traditions. Destruction, reconstruction, and restoration complicate any claim that a presently visible column is medieval fabric.

### 9.2 Christian architecture

Medieval and early modern churches invoked Solomon’s Temple as a model of sacred architecture. Paired entrance columns, twisted “Solomonic” columns, and named Jachin/Boaz shafts appear in ecclesiastical interpretation. Würzburg Cathedral’s Romanesque columns have been associated with the names and with the Temple façade.

These are typological descendants, not claimed bronze survivals. “Solomonic column” also commonly means a spiral shaft associated in Christian tradition with columns from the old St Peter’s in Rome; that form is not described for Jachin or Boaz.

### 9.3 Freemasonry

Freemasonry produced the pillars’ most influential modern afterlife. Early eighteenth-century lodge literature relocated the initiate symbolically into Solomon’s Temple. In *Masonry Dissected* (1730), Prichard’s catechism asks:

- what was seen after passing through the porch;
- the names of the two great pillars;
- their height and circumference;
- the ornament of their capitals.

The answers closely follow 1 Kings. A later exposure titled *Jachin and Boaz; or, An Authentic Key to the Door of Free-Masonry* first appeared in London in 1762 and was repeatedly reprinted, including numerous American editions between 1793 and 1827.

Over time, symbolic pillars became physical lodge furnishings. Their details vary:

- terrestrial and celestial globes may replace the biblical capitals;
- J and B may be printed on the shafts;
- their positions can reverse;
- pomegranates, lilies, networks, or spheres may be combined;
- the pair may frame the lodge entrance or appear only on a tracing board.

The globes are a modern Masonic elaboration; Kings does not place globes on the capitals.

### 9.4 Western occultism and ceremonial magic

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century occultists combined Masonic pillars with Kabbalah, alchemy, astrology, Egyptianizing imagery, and theories of polarity. Jachin and Boaz consequently came to stand for oppositions reconciled through initiation: mercy/severity, expansion/contraction, light/darkness, male/female, conscious/unconscious.

This synthetic symbolic system is historically modern. Its vocabulary should not be represented as a recovered Phoenician or Solomonic doctrine.

### 9.5 Tarot

Pamela Colman Smith’s High Priestess card for the Rider–Waite–Smith deck places the seated figure between a black pillar marked **B** and a white pillar marked **J**. Waite’s *Pictorial Key to the Tarot* explicitly calls them the pillars “J. and B. of the mystic Temple” and places a pomegranate-decorated veil behind the figure.

The card reversed the ordinary English ordering in visual space: B appears on the viewer’s left and J on the viewer’s right. It helped universalize the now-familiar black/white polarity, though the biblical text does not assign colors.

### 9.6 Literature and popular culture

The names have appeared in novels, esoteric manuals, fantasy, games, music, architectural fiction, and conspiracy literature, usually as shorthand for:

- a guarded threshold;
- secret knowledge;
- balanced opposites;
- a temple or initiatory chamber;
- Masonic influence.

These uses generally descend through Freemasonry or Tarot rather than through independent study of Kings.

### 9.7 Replicas and reconstructions

Modern models range from text-conscious archaeological renderings to imaginative pillars bearing globes, flames, spirals, giant letters, or black-and-white coloring. The last four features are not securely ancient. Reconstructions should state which textual witness controls their height, capital design, and pomegranate count.

No full-scale replica can claim exact fidelity because the profile, base, capital assembly, alloy, casting sequence, surface finish, and structural placement remain unresolved.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Early textual interpretation

Jewish and Christian commentators concentrated on the names, dimensions, symbolism, and relationship to the Davidic monarchy. Josephus transmitted a readable harmonized description but did not supply independent provenance.

Early modern antiquarians attempted graphical reconstructions of Solomon’s Temple, often making the pillars more architecturally regular than the Hebrew warrants. Their images strongly influenced later Bibles, churches, Masonic tracing boards, and popular reconstructions.

### 10.2 Comparative-religion phase

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century scholars compared Jachin and Boaz to:

- Egyptian obelisks;
- Phoenician temple columns;
- Mesopotamian sacred poles;
- Greek and Syrian monumental pillars;
- fire altars and cressets;
- cosmic mountains or sacred trees.

Some theories relied on very broad diffusionist assumptions and are no longer persuasive in their original form. The comparative method nevertheless established that monumental paired entrance pillars were not architecturally unimaginable in the ancient Near East.

### 10.3 Major twentieth-century debates

Recurring questions included:

1. freestanding or structural;
2. pillars, braziers, stelae, or sacred trees;
3. Phoenician import or local Israelite development;
4. divine, royal, ancestral, or sentence-like names;
5. whether the capitals resembled lilies, lotuses, bowls, or palmettes;
6. whether Kings preserves an authentic technical inventory;
7. how to reconcile Kings, Jeremiah, Chronicles, and the Septuagint.

William F. Albright, R. B. Y. Scott, W. Robertson Smith, and numerous later scholars proposed influential but competing models. Consensus never formed around a complete reconstruction.

### 10.4 Textual criticism

The Hebrew and Greek witnesses differ sufficiently that reconstruction cannot proceed from the Masoretic Text alone. Particularly important are:

- the Greek form of 1 Kings, which includes measurements absent from or different in the Masoretic account;
- the shorter Greek Jeremiah, generally understood to reflect an early Hebrew edition distinct from the later Masoretic form;
- the relationship between Jeremiah 52 and 2 Kings 25;
- Chronicles’ 35-cubit notice;
- variations in the numbers of chains and pomegranates.

Recent scholarship treats the six principal biblical descriptions as a developing textual tradition rather than assuming that every number came from one pristine architectural record.

### 10.5 Archaeological comparanda

The most important comparisons come from temples and cultic representations in Syria-Palestine and the broader Near East. Tel Moẓa is especially significant because it is near Jerusalem and belongs to the Iron Age. Its excavators date construction of the temple complex to the late tenth or early ninth century BCE and compare its long-room plan with northern Syrian temples and the biblical temple description.

The proper inference is limited: such finds show that the general architectural language of the biblical description belongs to a real regional tradition. They do not prove that the Jerusalem façade looked identical or that its columns carried the names Jachin and Boaz.

### 10.6 David Shapira and recent monographic treatment

Recent work has renewed close study of the pillars’ symbolism, textual layering, and fate. David Shapira has examined their meaning against ancient Near Eastern religious concepts. Daniel Prokop’s *The Pillars of the First Temple (1 Kgs 7,15–22)* systematically integrates epigraphic, textual, archaeological, and iconographic evidence and emphasizes the difficulty of obtaining a single secure reconstruction.

Current scholarship increasingly distinguishes:

- a possible historical Iron Age pair;
- the literary monument constructed by the biblical writers;
- the changing pillars of Jewish, Christian, Masonic, and occult memory.

### 10.7 Evidence still missing

A decisive study would require evidence not presently available:

- controlled excavation of the relevant First Temple porch context;
- inscribed fragments naming a pillar;
- foundry debris securely tied to Solomon’s Jerusalem project;
- Babylonian booty or metal inventories naming the objects;
- an authenticated bronze fragment with archaeological context and diagnostic composition;
- an early image securely labeled as the pair.

The Temple Mount’s archaeological constraints make recovery of such evidence unlikely in the immediate future.

### 10.8 Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

The following should not be presented as established history:

- that the pillars have been found;
- that they survive under the Temple Mount;
- that they were made of modern brass;
- that one was black and the other white;
- that they carried terrestrial and celestial globes;
- that initiates passed between them in Solomon’s secret school;
- that their measurements encode modern scientific constants;
- that they were columns of the Kabbalistic Tree of Life in the tenth century BCE;
- that Hiram Abiff was murdered beside them;
- that Masonic lodge pillars descend through a physical chain from the First Temple.
