# Dhul Khalasa: Object Dossier

**File name:** `dhul_khalasa_object_dossier.md`  
**Research current to:** 6 September 2026

## Scope and evidentiary note

“Dhul Khalasa” does not denote an extant, securely provenanced museum object. Medieval Islamic texts use the name variously for:

1. a carved or inscribed white stone;
2. the cult image or *nuṣub* associated with that stone;
3. the building that housed it;
4. the sanctuary or pilgrimage complex as a whole; and
5. at times, the locality in which the sanctuary stood.

The dossier therefore tracks three analytically distinct things:

- **the reported cult stone/image**;
- **its enclosing sanctuary or “house”;**
- **later ruins and sites identified with that sanctuary.**

No surviving stone, structure, fragment, photograph, drawing, excavation assemblage, museum accession, or scientific test establishes an uninterrupted material identity between any present object and the idol described in the literary sources. A Saudi reference work nevertheless identifies architectural ruins near Tabāla as remains of the sanctuary; a competing twentieth-century reconstruction placed it at Tharūq in Daws territory and said those ruins were demolished in 1925. These may concern different sites, different monuments carrying the same name, or incompatible identifications of one traditional sanctuary.

The principal evidence is retrospective. The most detailed physical description occurs in *Kitāb al-Aṣnām*, attributed to Hishām ibn Muḥammad ibn al-Kalbī (d. 206 AH/821–22 CE), about two centuries after the reported destruction. The destruction narrative is also preserved in hadith collections compiled in the ninth century, especially the *Ṣaḥīḥ*s of al-Bukhārī and Muslim. Verses transmitted as pre-Islamic poetry may preserve earlier memories, but their surviving written transmission is Islamic-era and must not be treated as contemporary inscriptional evidence.

No presently published pre-Islamic inscription has been securely identified as naming Dhū al-Khalaṣa. The Qurʾān does not name it.

### Evidentiary labels

- **Documented textual fact:** what an identifiable source actually says.
- **Traditional historical report:** an event reported retrospectively in Islamic literary tradition.
- **Plausible inference:** a reconstruction consistent with, but not proven by, the evidence.
- **Institutional identification:** a modern governmental, heritage, or scholarly institution’s identification of a site.
- **Unverified claim:** a claim for which no adequate primary or scientific documentation was located.
- **Legend or eschatological tradition:** a religiously meaningful account not independently testable as object history.
- **Unresolved:** the available evidence does not permit a choice among alternatives.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Names and referents

The standard Arabic form is:

- **ذُو الخَلَصَة** — *Dhū al-Khalaṣa*, commonly anglicized **Dhul Khalasa**, **Dhu al-Khalasa**, **Dhu’l-Khalasa**, or **Dhi al-Khalasa** after case inflection.
- Variant vocalizations recorded by medieval commentators include **Dhū al-Khuluṣa**, **Dhū al-Khuluṣah**, and a form with fatḥa on the first consonant and sukūn on the second: *al-Khalsa*. The unvocalized consonantal text, ذو الخلصة, does not decide among them.
- **الكعبة اليمانية** — *al-Kaʿba al-Yamāniyya*, “the Yemenite/Southern Kaʿba.”
- **الكعبة الشامية** — *al-Kaʿba al-Shāmiyya*, “the Syrian/Northern Kaʿba,” occurs in a hadith variant whose syntax and referent troubled later commentators. Some understood it as another designation of Dhul Khalasa; others thought the expression differentiated the Meccan Kaʿba from the southern sanctuary.
- **الطاغية** or **طاغية دوس** — *al-ṭāghiya*, “the false object of worship” or “the transgressing idol,” specifically “the ṭāghiya of Daws,” in hadith wording.
- **الولية** — *al-Waliyya*, a regional or later reported name applied to the site or cult object in some Arabic geographical literature.
- **العبلاء** — *al-ʿAblāʾ*, associated in later local writing with a white stone, a tree, or the site beside the supposed sanctuary.

The grammatical construction *dhū* means “possessor of” or “the one characterized by.” Consequently, “Dhul Khalasa” may originally have meant “possessor of al-Khalaṣa,” leaving open whether *al-Khalaṣa* was a quality, plant, place, cult group, sanctuary, or separate divine name.

Medieval lexicographical explanations connect *al-khalaṣa* with a fragrant climbing plant bearing red, bead-like fruit. Other interpretations derive the term from the Arabic root **خ ل ص**, carrying senses of purity, separation, deliverance, or being made exclusive. Popular translations such as “the one of salvation,” “the god of redemption,” or “the pure one” are interpretive and are not established ancient titles. Ibn al-Athīr objected to treating *Dhū al-Khalaṣa* straightforwardly as the idol’s proper name because *dhū* normally governs a common noun; this grammatical observation helped sustain the medieval disagreement over whether the name belonged to the stone or the building. [Ibn Ḥajar commentary preserved by Hadith Portal](https://hadithportal.com/index.php?bab_id=2202&book=33&h_id=4119&sharh=16&show=hadith&uid=0)

### 1.2 What kind of object was it?

The sources support at least four definitions:

| Definition | Source language | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|---|
| A carved/inscribed white *marwa* stone with a crown-like form or design | Ibn al-Kalbī | Detailed but retrospective literary description |
| A *nuṣub* or several *nuṣub* worshipped inside a building | Muslim’s Jarīr report | Retrospective hadith |
| A “house” belonging to Khathʿam and Bajīla, called a Kaʿba | al-Bukhārī and Muslim | Retrospective hadith |
| The wider shrine and pilgrimage destination | Poetry, Ibn al-Kalbī, geographers | Composite inference from later sources |

The safest description is therefore: **a regional southwest-Arabian cult sanctuary reportedly centered upon a worked white stone or stone cult-image, accompanied by divinatory equipment and enclosed in a building conventionally called a “Kaʿba.”**

Calling it simply a “god” is imprecise. Some modern reference works do so, but the early Islamic texts often distinguish the physical stone, the *nuṣub*, the house, and the community’s act of worship. The sources do not preserve a mythology, genealogy, personality, iconographic canon, or securely attested divine name independent of the object and sanctuary.

### 1.3 Reported material and appearance

Ibn al-Kalbī’s description, in Nabih Amin Faris’s translation, is:

> “a carved piece of white quartz with something in the form of a crown upon its head.”

The Arabic commonly printed behind this is approximately:

> **مَرْوَةٌ بَيْضَاءُ مَنْقُوشٌ عَلَيْهَا كَهَيْئَةِ التَّاج**

That is better rendered conservatively as **“a white stone, worked or incised upon it in the likeness of a crown.”** *Marwa* can denote a hard white stone, flint, quartz, or quartz-like stone; it is not a mineralogical laboratory identification. “Crown upon its head” is Faris’s somewhat anthropomorphic rendering. The Arabic does not prove that the stone had a human head.

Claims that it was a phallus “topped by a crown” are modern interpretive embellishments, not what Ibn al-Kalbī explicitly says. No early source gives a sexed anatomical form.

The same chapter calls it “the White Quartz Idol of Tabāla” in a translated line of poetry. [Ibn al-Kalbī/Faris, “Dhu-al-Khalasah”](https://www.bible.ca/islam/library/Al-Kalbi/khalasah.htm)

### 1.4 Unknown physical properties

No reliable source supplies:

- height, width, circumference, or weight;
- exact geological composition;
- quarry or extraction site;
- sculptor, donor, or workshop;
- date of manufacture;
- structural plan of the original sanctuary;
- foundation inscription;
- dedicatory text;
- maker’s mark;
- paint, gilding, clothing, face, limbs, or other anthropomorphic features;
- a contemporary image;
- the dimensions or inscriptions of the divination arrows;
- a securely identified surviving fragment.

Reports that necklaces, ostrich eggs, or sacrifices were hung upon “Dhul Khalasa” occur in later commentarial discussions and may conflate different objects or sanctuaries. They should not be treated as part of the earliest recoverable physical description.

### 1.5 Sanctuary architecture

The hadith narratives call the sanctuary a **bayt**, “house,” and a **Kaʿba**, a term that need not imply an exact architectural copy of the Meccan sanctuary. Ibn al-Kalbī refers to “the building which stood over Dhul Khalasa.”

A modern Saudi encyclopedic account identifies ruins on **ʿIrq al-Ṭāghūt**, west of upper Tabāla, as the sanctuary. It reports:

- stone walls surviving to about two metres;
- relatively dressed masonry;
- five adjacent rooms, each approximately 4 × 3 m;
- additional southern rooms;
- a western structure about 7 × 4 m;
- square rooms;
- traces interpreted as a surrounding circular sacred enclosure.

These measurements describe **the modern ruins attributed to the temple**, not a securely excavated plan of the seventh-century cult building. The page cites a regional archaeological encyclopedia but provides no stratigraphic report, radiocarbon result, ceramic chronology, inscription, or material link to the named cult. Its identification is institutional but not archaeometrically demonstrated. [Encyclopedia of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia](https://saudiency.kapl.org.sa/Loader.aspx?BookID=69&PID=15&TOCID=6&pageid=24)

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Manufacture

**Unresolved.** No ancient or medieval source states who made the stone, who commissioned it, when it was installed, or where its material was quarried. There is no recoverable fabrication history.

Later general Muslim narratives attribute the introduction or redistribution of Arabian idols to ʿAmr ibn Luḥayy. Some commentaries specifically say that he erected a Dhul Khalasa below Mecca. That is a traditional explanatory genealogy for Arabian idolatry, not a documented commission, and may refer to a different object of the same name.

### 2.2 Earliest potentially pre-Islamic witness: transmitted poetry

Ibn al-Kalbī preserves or cites verses associated with Dhul Khalasa.

#### The vengeance oracle

A man whose father had been killed reportedly sought an oracle at Dhul Khalasa. The divination arrows forbade vengeance, and he rebuked the object:

> “O Dhul Khalasa, had you been the wronged one,  
> your father the slain and buried one,  
> you would not have forbidden killing the enemy.”

Some transmitters attributed the episode to **Imruʾ al-Qays ibn Ḥujr al-Kindī**, conventionally a sixth-century poet; Ibn al-Kalbī reports the attribution as disputed. The verse could preserve a pre-Islamic cult memory, but its earliest extant documentation is much later. It is not a contemporary inscription.

#### The oath poem

Verses attributed to **Khidāsh ibn Zuhayr al-ʿĀmirī**, addressed to ʿAthʿath ibn Waḥshī al-Khathʿamī over a violated pact, invoke:

> “God and the White Stone of Tabāla”

alongside the oath of the Christian Lakhmid ruler **al-Nuʿmān III**. If authentic, this places the shrine in a sixth- or early seventh-century oath culture and shows that invocation of the stone could coexist rhetorically with invocation of God and a Christian ruler’s covenant. Again, the verse’s surviving witness is Islamic-era literary transmission. [Ibn al-Kalbī/Faris](https://www.bible.ca/islam/library/Al-Kalbi/khalasah.htm)

Modern research is divided over how confidently such poems may be used. Peter Webb has treated a poetic use of *muʿtamir* as evidence for a pilgrimage center at Dhul Khalasa, while G. R. Hawting emphasizes that the narratives about Arabian idols survive in Muslim works whose recoverable forms are considerably later and whose polemical structures require scrutiny. [Hawting, *The Idea of Idolatry and the Emergence of Islam*](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/The-Idea-of-Idolatry-by-G.-R.-Hawting.pdf)

### 2.3 Earliest securely datable written attestations

The earliest extant datable textual witnesses presently recoverable are Islamic-era:

- materials incorporated in early sīra and genealogical transmission during the eighth and ninth centuries;
- *Kitāb al-Ṭabaqāt al-kabīr* of Ibn Saʿd (d. 230 AH/845 CE);
- *Kitāb al-Aṣnām*, attributed to Ibn al-Kalbī (d. 206 AH/821–22 CE);
- al-Bukhārī (d. 256 AH/870 CE);
- Muslim ibn al-Ḥajjāj (d. 261 AH/875 CE).

Ibn Saʿd says Jarīr came to Medina in the year 10 AH, was asked what had become of Dhul Khalasa, and was sent against it. His transmitted material also preserves the standard 150-rider figure. [Ibn Saʿd, delegation of Bajīla](https://hadithportal.com/index.php?bab_id=130&book=802&chapter_id=1&e=206&f=0&show=bab)

Al-Bukhārī’s versions define the object principally as a **house**, while Muslim’s fuller version says it was a house in Yemen belonging to Khathʿam and Bajīla, containing worshipped *nuṣub*. [Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3823](https://sunnah.com/bukhari/63/48) [Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3020](https://sunnah.com/bukhari%3A3020) [Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2476](https://dorar.net/hadith/sharh/24808)

### 2.4 Epigraphic silence

No secure pre-Islamic inscription naming **Dhū al-Khalaṣa** was located. This absence does not prove that the sanctuary did not exist, but it means that its name, date, divine identity, and ritual profile are not independently controlled by contemporary epigraphy. Hawting observes generally that the literary catalogues of idols may preserve historical names and locations while simultaneously shaping them within later monotheist narratives about the defeat of idolatry. [Hawting](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/The-Idea-of-Idolatry-by-G.-R.-Hawting.pdf)

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

## 3.1 Pre-Islamic custody: traditional reconstruction

According to Ibn al-Kalbī:

- the object stood at **Tabāla**, between Mecca and Yemen, approximately seven nights’ journey from Mecca;
- its custodians were **Banū Umāma**, a branch of **Bāhila ibn Aʿṣur**;
- it was venerated by **Khathʿam**, **Bajīla**, the **Azd of al-Sarāt**, neighboring groups of **Hawāzin**, and Arabs resident around Tabāla;
- worshippers approached it with sacrifices.

This is the only relatively coherent custodian statement. It is retrospective, unsupported by a surviving temple archive or inscription, and differs from hadith formulations emphasizing Khathʿam, Bajīla, or Daws. [Ibn al-Kalbī/Faris](https://www.bible.ca/islam/library/Al-Kalbi/khalasah.htm)

The custody arrangement may have involved a specialist hereditary *sadāna*, but neither the legal form of custodianship nor an individual custodian’s name is preserved.

## 3.2 Oracular handling

The stone itself is not said to have circulated. People traveled to it. The principal movable ritual accessories were divination arrows or lots. In the vengeance story, a petitioner “shuffled” or drew the arrows and received a prohibition. Later Arabic summaries describe three arrows:

- **al-āmir**, “the commanding”;
- **al-nāhī**, “the forbidding”;
- **al-mutarabbiṣ**, “the waiting/undecided.”

The three-name set is a later literary description; no physical arrow is known to survive.

## 3.3 Destruction under Jarīr ibn ʿAbd Allāh

### Date

The common traditional chronology places the expedition in **10 AH/631–632 CE**, after Jarīr ibn ʿAbd Allāh al-Bajalī’s conversion and before Muhammad’s death. Al-Wāqidī reportedly placed Jarīr’s arrival in Ramadan 10 AH, but other reports imply an earlier conversion. The exact Gregorian month is therefore not secure.

### Commission

In the hadith account, Muhammad asked Jarīr:

> “Will you relieve me of Dhul Khalasa?”

Jarīr departed with **150 cavalrymen of Aḥmas**, a Bajīla group. Variant reports give 170 or 190; 150 is the dominant figure in al-Bukhārī and Muslim.

### Action against the object and building

The transmitted accounts say that Jarīr’s force:

- reached the sanctuary;
- fought defenders;
- broke or demolished it;
- burned it;
- reported that it had been left like a diseased or scabby camel.

Al-Bukhārī 3020 expressly says that Jarīr **dismantled and burned the house**. Another version says “we broke it and killed whoever we found there.” [Al-Bukhārī 3020](https://sunnah.com/bukhari%3A3020) [Al-Bukhārī 3823](https://sunnah.com/bukhari/63/48)

Muslim’s version says:

> “He came to it, burned it with fire, and broke it.”

It separately mentions a Yemeni diviner using arrows. Jarīr ordered him to break the arrows and profess Islam under threat of execution; the man complied. [Muslim 2476 text and commentary](https://dorar.net/hadith/sharh/24808)

### Resistance and casualties

Ibn al-Kalbī’s fuller but later narrative says Khathʿam and Bāhila defended the shrine. It gives:

- 100 men of Bāhila and its custodians killed;
- many Khathʿam killed;
- 200 men of Banū Quḥāfa ibn ʿĀmir ibn Khathʿam killed.

These figures do not appear in the concise canonical hadith versions and cannot be independently verified. Ibn al-Kalbī also transmits a lament by a Khathʿam woman describing Banū Umāma’s defeat while defending the shrine. [Ibn al-Kalbī/Faris](https://www.bible.ca/islam/library/Al-Kalbi/khalasah.htm)

### Messenger and confirmation

Jarīr sent **Abū Arṭāʾa**, identified in Ibn Saʿd’s tradition as Ḥusayn ibn Rabīʿa, to announce the destruction. Muhammad reportedly blessed the men and horses of Aḥmas five times. [Ibn Saʿd narrative](https://hadithportal.com/index.php?book=80002&h_id=376&show=hadith)

### What was destroyed?

The sources permit three reconstructions:

1. **Total destruction:** the building, cult object, and equipment were demolished and burned.
2. **Cult destruction with structural survival:** the object and ritual fittings were destroyed, but massive masonry remained.
3. **Partial dismantling followed by reuse:** the cult stone survived as building material and was incorporated into a mosque threshold.

The second and third are necessary to explain later reports but are not stated explicitly in the canonical hadith. “Burned” cannot literally consume a quartz or flint stone; it applies naturally to a roof, doors, wooden furnishings, cloth, or the wider building. “Broke” could apply to the stone and masonry.

## 3.4 Reuse as a mosque threshold

Ibn al-Kalbī’s text concludes:

> “At the present time Dhul Khalasa constitutes the threshold of the gate of the mosque at Tabala.”

Yāqūt al-Ḥamawī (d. 626 AH/1229 CE) later repeats that Dhul Khalasa was the threshold of the Tabāla mosque. This is the most concrete post-destruction custody claim: the former cult stone was allegedly **spoliated into Islamic architecture**.

Its evidentiary limits are substantial:

- no dimensions or identifying marks are supplied;
- no traveler’s drawing or inscription confirms it;
- no chain explains who installed it;
- “Dhul Khalasa” may refer to the stone or remains of the building;
- Yāqūt may be repeating Ibn al-Kalbī rather than documenting a thirteenth-century inspection.

A modern Saudi regional encyclopedia accordingly concludes that the cult object itself was destroyed in the seventh century and that the threshold notice is the only later report about the stone. [Saudi regional encyclopedia, “Dhul Khalasa”](https://www.ksaency.com/article/%D9%85%D8%A7%D9%87%D9%8A-%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%85%D9%88%D8%A7%D9%82%D8%B9-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%A3%D8%AB%D8%B1%D9%8A%D8%A9-%D9%81%D9%8A-%D9%85%D9%86%D8%B7%D9%82%D8%A9-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%A8%D8%A7%D8%AD%D8%A9-%D9%81%D9%8A-%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%85%D9%85%D9%84%D9%83%D8%A9-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%B9%D8%B1%D8%A8%D9%8A%D8%A9)

The supposed threshold has not been traced into a modern collection or securely identified in situ.

## 3.5 The twentieth-century Tharūq destruction report

A long editorial note in a modern edition of al-Azraqī’s *Akhbār Makka*, associated with editor Rushdī Ṣāliḥ Malḥas, advances a different location and afterlife:

- Dhul Khalasa stood at **Tharūq** in the mountains of Daws, not at Tabāla;
- substantial walls remained into the twentieth century;
- a tree called **al-ʿAblāʾ** stood beside them;
- after King ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz took the Hijaz in 1343 AH/1924–25, he appointed ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz ibn Ibrāhīm over Ṭāʾif;
- a campaign entered the Daws mountains in Rabīʿ II 1344 AH, approximately late 1925;
- the force burned the tree, demolished the walls, and threw the rubble into the valley;
- an unnamed participant allegedly said that fewer than forty men could not move one of the stones.

The surviving web transcription states that this erased the structure’s trace. [*Akhbār Makka* editorial material, pp. 382–83](https://www.masaha.org/book/view/4638/page/383)

This account must not be converted into a second documented destruction of the original idol:

- it concerns **walls and a tree**, not an identified surviving white cult stone;
- the eyewitness is unnamed;
- no official order, campaign diary, photograph, archaeological plan, or contemporary newspaper report was located;
- the identification of the ruins as Dhul Khalasa is asserted rather than scientifically demonstrated;
- the account conflicts geographically with Tabāla, approximately several days away in the editorial reconstruction;
- it may represent destruction of a locally venerated shrine subsequently equated with the hadith object.

Thus the 1925 event is best classified as a **twentieth-century historical claim about the demolition of attributed ruins**, not proof that the seventh-century idol survived or its cult was continuously restored.

## 3.6 Presently claimed Tabāla ruins

A Saudi government-sponsored encyclopedia identifies a surviving ruin complex at Tabāla, on ʿIrq al-Ṭāghūt, as remnants of Dhul Khalasa’s temple. If this attribution is correct, the site—not necessarily the cult stone—remains partly in place. [Encyclopedia of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia](https://saudiency.kapl.org.sa/Loader.aspx?BookID=69&PID=15&TOCID=6&pageid=24)

This creates an unresolved custody and identity problem:

- the Tharūq account says the attributed structure was eradicated in 1925;
- the Tabāla institutional account describes standing masonry;
- neither supplies decisive archaeological dating or an inscription naming the sanctuary.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Did Dhul Khalasa physically exist?

**Strongest case for a historical cult center:**

- several independent hadith recensions remember a named “house” destroyed by Jarīr;
- Ibn al-Kalbī gives a specific material description, location, custodial clan, worshipping constituencies, ritual uses, and poetry;
- transmitted poetry uses the name or “White Stone of Tabāla” in contexts of oath and divination;
- the location and associated tribes are geographically coherent;
- later geographical tradition remembers physical reuse of a stone.

**Reasons for caution:**

- the sources survive in works compiled roughly two centuries after the supposed pre-Islamic cult;
- there is no securely identified contemporary inscription;
- the Qurʾān does not mention it;
- literary accounts of idol destruction belong to a broader monotheist genre in which the defeat of idols demonstrates the triumph of true religion;
- contradictions concern location, custodians, worshippers, grammatical referent, and whether one or several objects bore the name;
- the physical description rests principally on one antiquarian tradition.

The appropriate conclusion is that **a historical sanctuary or cult object is plausible and perhaps probable, but its exact form and the unity of all traditions under one identity are not archaeologically demonstrated**.

### 4.2 Idol versus temple

This is the central identity dispute.

- Ibn al-Kalbī: Dhul Khalasa is a white worked stone, with a building above it.
- Al-Bukhārī: Dhul Khalasa is a *bayt*, a house.
- Muslim: it is a house containing worshipped *nuṣub*.
- Some commentators: *al-Khalaṣa* was the house and *Dhū al-Khalaṣa* the idol.
- Others: Dhul Khalasa was the house or sanctuary, not the idol.
- Still others: al-Khalaṣa was the name of its worshipping community.

Modern summaries that alternate freely among “god,” “idol,” and “temple” conceal this primary ambiguity.

### 4.3 One Dhul Khalasa or several?

Ibn Ḥajar al-ʿAsqalānī considered whether the **Khathʿam/Bajīla house** destroyed by Jarīr and the **Daws ṭāghiya** mentioned in the end-time hadith were different. His reasoning was geographical and genealogical: Daws and Khathʿam belonged to distinct tribal groupings and territories. Other commentators identified them as one regional sanctuary shared by several tribes. [Ibn Ḥajar commentary](https://hadithportal.com/index.php?bab_id=2202&book=33&h_id=4119&sharh=16&show=hadith&uid=0)

Three possibilities remain:

1. one major sanctuary served Khathʿam, Bajīla, Daws, and neighboring groups;
2. two cult objects or sanctuaries shared the name;
3. later transmitters merged local cults under a famous name.

No material evidence resolves the question.

### 4.4 Tabāla versus Tharūq

**Tabāla claim**

- explicitly named by Ibn al-Kalbī;
- repeated in the prophecy’s explanatory clause in Muslim;
- associated with the modern ruins identified by Saudi heritage literature;
- geographically situated about 60 km northwest of modern Bīsha. [Saudipedia, Ancient City of Tabāla](https://saudipedia.com/%D9%85%D8%AF%D9%8A%D9%86%D8%A9-%D8%AA%D8%A8%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%A9-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%A3%D8%AB%D8%B1%D9%8A%D8%A9)

**Tharūq claim**

- arises from traditions connecting the object to Daws;
- Tharūq is remembered as a major Daws settlement;
- twentieth-century local-historical writing places the sanctuary at a rocky rise near a branch valley called Shaʿb Dhī al-Khalaṣa;
- the Malḥas editorial note says walls there survived until 1925.

The Tabāla tradition has the clearer early textual anchor. The Tharūq identification may preserve a second site, an alternative localization of the Daws tradition, or a modern harmonization. Neither has been proven through a published excavation.

### 4.5 Authenticity of the supposed ruins

No published evidence located for this dossier records:

- controlled excavation of the alleged temple;
- diagnostic pre-Islamic cult deposits;
- architectural phasing;
- radiocarbon or luminescence dates;
- petrographic comparison with the described white stone;
- an inscription naming Dhul Khalasa;
- a stratified destruction layer datable to approximately 632;
- a securely documented mosque threshold matching Ibn al-Kalbī’s stone;
- legal accession or conservation records for fragments.

The phrase “remains of the temple” in modern Saudi institutional literature is therefore an **attribution**, not a demonstrated identity.

### 4.6 No authenticated modern claimant

No museum or shrine is known to display a stone catalogued as the original Dhul Khalasa. No accession number, authenticated fragment, cast, or laboratory-tested claimant was located. Images online labelled “Dhul Khalasa” commonly depict generic ruins, maps, modern illustrations, or unrelated Arabian stone objects.

No early text unambiguously identifies a separate divine being represented by the stone. Treating “Dhul Khalasa” as a fully personified deity risks converting the physical cult object’s name into a divine biography the evidence does not preserve.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Pilgrimage and circumambulation

Dhul Khalasa was reportedly a destination to which tribes **came**, made offerings, and sacrificed. The designation “Yemenite Kaʿba” indicates a regional sacred house and pilgrimage focus, not necessarily a political attempt to replace Mecca.

The end-time hadith imagines Daws women moving or circumambulating around Dhul Khalasa. Later commentators explicitly interpret the bodily movement as produced by vigorous or crowded circumambulation. [Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2906](https://sunnah.com/muslim/54/64)

The hadith is evidence that ninth-century tradition associated the object with circumambulation. It does not independently document the choreography of the pre-Islamic rite.

### 6.2 Sacrifice and gifts

Ibn al-Kalbī says worshipping groups **venerated it and came with sacrifices**. The account does not specify:

- animal species;
- slaughter procedure;
- recipient of meat or hides;
- festival date;
- priestly fee;
- whether blood contacted the stone;
- whether gifts were permanently stored.

### 6.3 Divination

The clearest functional episode involves **azlām**, divining arrows or lots. A petitioner asked whether to avenge his father; the result forbade action. The insulting response shows that the oracle’s decision could be rejected rhetorically even if the divinatory process was recognized.

The ritual probably involved an officiant, a set of labeled or functionally differentiated arrows, and a randomizing or drawing procedure. The precise mechanism is not recorded for this shrine.

### 6.4 Oath witness

The Khidāsh poem makes “God and the White Stone of Tabāla” witnesses to a covenant. This suggests that the stone’s sacred authority could guarantee agreements. It does not show that the object was owned, carried, or touched by the parties.

### 6.5 Sacral protection and tribal legitimacy

The resistance to Jarīr, if the narrative is historical, shows that custody had collective significance extending beyond possession of an inert statue. Banū Umāma’s prestige may have derived from guardianship, while regional tribes shared access and sacrificial obligations. No source says possession conferred kingship or hereditary sovereignty.

### 6.6 Access rules and taboos

No reliable source defines who could:

- enter the building;
- touch the stone;
- draw the arrows;
- officiate;
- see the object;
- receive offerings.

Women’s participation appears in the prophetic tradition but is not enough to reconstruct gendered access rules.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The failed vengeance oracle

**Tier:** early literary tradition, possibly containing a pre-Islamic poetic core.

A bereaved man consulted Dhul Khalasa before avenging his father. When the lot forbade vengeance, he mocked the oracle: had Dhul Khalasa’s own father been murdered, it would not counsel restraint. Some transmitters made the man Imruʾ al-Qays.

The story may preserve:

- real use of divinatory arrows;
- elite skepticism toward unfavorable oracles;
- the shrine’s reputation beyond a single tribe.

It may also have been preserved because the poet’s rebuke made the idol look powerless or morally obtuse to later Muslim readers.

### 7.2 The white stone as covenant witness

**Tier:** transmitted pre-Islamic poetry; authenticity not independently verifiable.

The poem attributed to Khidāsh invokes both God and the White Stone of Tabāla. Later readers could interpret this as evidence of polytheism, but the actual religious logic may have involved layered oath witnesses rather than a simple “one god versus many gods” scheme.

### 7.3 Muhammad’s prayer for Jarīr

**Tier:** canonical Islamic tradition.

Jarīr said he was insecure on horseback. Muhammad struck or placed his hand upon Jarīr’s chest and prayed that God make him firm, guiding, and rightly guided. Jarīr then said he never fell from a horse again. The miracle belongs to Jarīr’s biography, but in the object’s history it marks ritual preparation for the destroying expedition. [Al-Bukhārī 4355–57](https://sunnah.com/bukhari/64/381-383)

### 7.4 “Like a scabby camel”

**Tier:** early Islamic destruction rhetoric.

The messenger reported that the shrine had been left like a **scabby camel**. The simile probably evokes a blackened, mottled, stripped, or ruined surface after burning. It is not a technical condition report.

### 7.5 Return before the Last Hour

**Tier:** canonical eschatological tradition.

Abū Hurayra transmits Muhammad’s prophecy:

> “The Hour will not come until the women of Daws go round Dhul Khalasa.”

Muslim’s recension adds that it was an idol worshipped by Daws at Tabāla. [Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2906](https://sunnah.com/muslim/54/64)

Later commentators interpret this as a future relapse into idolatry. The prophecy profoundly influenced subsequent reception: reports of rural veneration near attributed ruins could be presented as its fulfillment, and modern polemical writing sometimes treats any alleged revival as proof of prophecy.

The text does **not** state that the original stone would remain materially intact. A rebuilt object, substituted stone, or renewed cult at the old location could satisfy later theological interpretations. Accordingly, the prophecy cannot serve as evidence for continuous physical survival.

### 7.6 Nineteenth- or twentieth-century “revival”

**Tier:** late, weakly documented claim.

Popular accounts sometimes claim that Dhul Khalasa was worshipped again until 1815 or the 1920s and then destroyed by Wahhabi or Saudi forces. The available source chain is inadequate:

- early hadith predicts future worship;
- later commentators discuss its meaning;
- twentieth-century local history describes walls and a sacred tree destroyed in 1925;
- popular summaries merge these elements into a continuous revival of the ancient cult.

No located nineteenth-century observer describes worship of an authenticated white idol, names priests, records liturgy, or documents continuity from antiquity. The claim that “the pagan cult survived until 1815” should therefore be marked **unverified and probably conflated with campaigns against later local shrine or tree veneration**.

### 7.7 Egyptian or “Amalekite” builders

**Tier:** modern speculation.

Some modern Arabic writing claims that the alleged walls were too massive for ancient Arabs and therefore must have been made by Egyptians, Amalekites, or Jurhum. This argument rests on outdated assumptions about Arabian architectural ability and provides no excavation or inscriptional evidence. It is not a documented origin tradition from antiquity.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 The cult stone

**Status:** lost, destroyed, or unrecognized.

The last specific custody claim is that the stone served as the threshold of a mosque at Tabāla. Its subsequent fate is unknown. It may have been:

- worn down in use;
- broken during rebuilding;
- removed as ordinary masonry;
- buried;
- reused again;
- confused with another stone;
- or never physically present in the mosque despite the literary report.

No authenticated fragment is publicly catalogued.

### 8.2 The original sanctuary

**Status:** disputed.

Two principal candidate locations exist:

1. **Tabāla**, in modern ʿAsīr Province, roughly 60 km northwest of Bīsha. A Saudi institutional encyclopedia describes standing remains attributed to the temple at ʿIrq al-Ṭāghūt. The attribution lacks published decisive dating.
2. **Tharūq**, in Daws/Zahrān territory, associated with a twentieth-century claim that attributed walls were destroyed in 1344 AH/1925 and rubble thrown into a valley.

The sites should not be silently merged. The modern Tabāla remains, if correctly reported, also show that the Tharūq destruction cannot have eliminated every monument subsequently called Dhul Khalasa.

### 8.3 Control and access

The reported sites lie within the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia and are subject to Saudi antiquities, land, and access regimes. No specific protected-site registration number, excavation permit, public gallery, ticketed access arrangement, or active ownership dispute was located.

### 8.4 Conservation and scientific examination

No published conservation program or scientific examination of the supposed cult stone was found because no accepted stone is known. No modern laboratory has authenticated an object as Dhul Khalasa.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Religious interpretation

Dhul Khalasa remains prominent in Islamic discussions of:

- the eradication of pre-Islamic cults;
- Jarīr ibn ʿAbd Allāh’s merits;
- prohibitions on divination;
- the legitimacy or illegitimacy of burning cult buildings;
- signs of the Last Hour;
- the possibility that idolatry will recur.

Modern sermons frequently conflate the seventh-century object with later sacred trees, tombs, or ruins. Such uses document the name’s reception, not the artifact’s material survival.

### 9.2 Heritage literature

Saudi reference works treat Tabāla as an important ancient city on the Yemeni pilgrimage and trade routes. One official-style encyclopedia accepts the ruin identification; another regional discussion insists that the idol ended with Jarīr and treats the Tharūq ruins as a late claim. This tension illustrates the object’s modern dual status: both a condemned cult image and a potential archaeological monument.

### 9.3 Internet imagery and replicas

No official replica or museum cast was located. Modern digital illustrations commonly supply architectural or anthropomorphic detail absent from the sources. In particular:

- humanoid statues;
- crowned heads;
- phallic pillars;
- elaborate temple façades;
- female-goddess imagery

should be labelled modern visualization unless tied to an identified scholarly reconstruction.

### 9.4 Political and polemical uses

The story is used in modern arguments about:

- Wahhabi and Saudi iconoclasm;
- the reliability of hadith prophecy;
- attempts to revive reconstructed Arabian polytheism;
- whether pre-Islamic Arabian religion has been accurately preserved by Muslim authors;
- destruction of archaeological heritage.

Claims that twentieth-century destruction “fulfilled” the prophecy belong to confessional interpretation. Conversely, claims that the whole sanctuary was invented by Muslim polemic go beyond the evidence. The extant sources permit skepticism about detail but do not demonstrate fabrication.

### 9.5 Tourism and commercialization

Tabāla appears in regional heritage descriptions, but Dhul Khalasa has not acquired the documented museum, pilgrimage-tourism, or replica economy associated with many surviving sacred objects. No established commercial trademark or official emblem based on the stone was located.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Ibn al-Kalbī and the manuscript tradition

Ibn al-Kalbī’s *Kitāb al-Aṣnām* became the foundational dossier of Arabian idols. Its modern textual history matters:

- Yāqūt extensively quoted it;
- Julius Wellhausen used Yāqūt’s excerpts in *Reste arabischen Heidentums* before a manuscript edition was widely available;
- Aḥmad Zakī Pasha acquired a manuscript in Damascus and published an edition in Cairo in 1914, with a more widely distributed edition in 1924;
- Nabih Amin Faris published the standard English translation in 1952;
- Rosa Klinke-Rosenberger produced a German edition and translation with extensive notes.

Hawting emphasizes that the surviving *Kitāb al-Aṣnām* appears accretive, with repetition, variants, and possible interpolation. He argues that it must be read as part of a monotheist literary tradition about idolatry, not as an unmediated ethnographic notebook. At the same time, he allows that names, tribes, and places may preserve historical realities. [Hawting](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/The-Idea-of-Idolatry-by-G.-R.-Hawting.pdf)

### 10.2 Hadith evidence

The Jarīr account appears under several headings in al-Bukhārī, including:

- merits of the Anṣār/Jarīr;
- jihad and burning houses;
- military expeditions.

These recensions agree on the central elements—commission, 150 Aḥmas horsemen, destruction, and blessing—but vary in whether Dhul Khalasa is a house, the Kaʿba of Yemen, or a place containing idols. Muslim’s version provides the clearest phrase “a house … in which were *nuṣub* that were worshipped.”

The repetition strengthens evidence that a stable narrative circulated, but it does not make the ninth-century compilations contemporary records of the 630s.

### 10.3 Medieval geographical debate

Medieval commentators and geographers preserved several incompatible statements:

- at Tabāla;
- in Yemen broadly understood;
- associated with Daws;
- associated with Khathʿam and Bajīla;
- below Mecca;
- transformed into a mosque threshold;
- possibly distinct Daws and Khathʿam objects.

Rather than errors that can all be harmonized, these may record the spread of a cult name, confusion between shrine and idol, or later attempts to locate a famous tradition.

### 10.4 Modern archaeological attribution

The Saudi Encyclopedia’s Tabāla identification gives the fullest published online architectural description but does not expose the evidence needed for authentication. The cited regional work by Misfar al-Khathʿamī should be treated as the next bibliographical lead. Essential unpublished or unlocated evidence would include:

- site survey coordinates;
- plans and photographs;
- pottery and small finds;
- building phases;
- environmental or destruction samples;
- records of local names;
- mosque architectural history;
- identification of the alleged threshold.

Until such material is published, “Temple of Dhul Khalasa” remains an attributed site-name.

### 10.5 Discredited or unsupported theories still circulating

The following should not be stated as established:

- that Dhul Khalasa was certainly a fully anthropomorphic goddess;
- that the image was demonstrably phallic;
- that “Dhul Khalasa” certainly means “god of redemption”;
- that its builders were Egyptian, Amalekite, or Jurhum colonists;
- that the original idol remained in continuous worship until 1815 or 1925;
- that the present Tabāla ruins have been scientifically authenticated;
- that the Tharūq walls were unquestionably the building attacked by Jarīr;
- that the shrine rivaled Mecca through a coordinated political program;
- that the stone presently survives in a known mosque, museum, or private collection.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and early literary sources

1. **Ibn al-Kalbī, Hishām ibn Muḥammad.** *Kitāb al-Aṣnām*. The Dhul Khalasa chapter supplies the physical description, Tabāla location, custodians, worshipping tribes, poems, sacrifices, destruction narrative, and threshold claim. The work survives in a later manuscript tradition and contains accretive material.

2. **Ibn Saʿd.** *Kitāb al-Ṭabaqāt al-kabīr*. Supplies the year-10 setting for Jarīr’s delegation and an expanded account of the commission, expedition, and messenger.

3. **Al-Bukhārī.** *Ṣaḥīḥ*, nos. 3020, 3823, 4355–57 and related recensions. Principal canonical witness for the “house,” 150 riders, destruction, burning, casualties, and blessing.

4. **Muslim ibn al-Ḥajjāj.** *Ṣaḥīḥ*, nos. 2476 and 2906. Supplies the house-with-*nuṣub* wording, destruction and diviner episode, and the Daws eschatological prophecy.

5. **Yāqūt al-Ḥamawī.** *Muʿjam al-Buldān*. Repeats geographical and mosque-threshold traditions, often drawing on Ibn al-Kalbī.

### Modern editions and studies

6. **Faris, Nabih Amin, trans.** *The Book of Idols*. Princeton, 1952. Useful English translation, but “white quartz” is a translation rather than mineralogical analysis.

7. **Hawting, G. R.** *The Idea of Idolatry and the Emergence of Islam: From Polemic to History*. Cambridge University Press, 1999. Essential source-critical treatment of the late literary construction and possible historical residues of Arabian-idol traditions.

8. **Encyclopedia of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, ʿAsīr volume.** Modern institutional identification and architectural description of ruins at Tabāla.

9. **Saudipedia, “Ancient City of Tabāla.”** Modern geographical and historical orientation.

10. **Modern edition of al-Azraqī, *Akhbār Makka*, editorial discussion at pp. 382–83.** Source of the Tharūq localization and 1925 demolition report. The passage is modern editorial material, not al-Azraqī’s ninth-century testimony.

### Final assessment

The fullest defensible reconstruction is of **a probably historical regional cult center remembered under the name Dhul Khalasa, centered on a worked white stone and a divinatory and sacrificial sanctuary somewhere in southwest Arabia, most strongly associated in the earliest detailed geography with Tabāla**. Its stone was reportedly broken in 10 AH/631–32, though a later source says it survived as a mosque threshold. No material chain connects that threshold to any extant object.

The building’s identity is less settled. Remains at Tabāla are institutionally identified with it, while a competing Daws tradition places ruins at Tharūq and says they were demolished in 1925. Neither claimant has been validated by a published inscription or controlled scientific excavation. Consequently:

- **existence of some historical shrine:** plausible to probable;
- **exact form of the cult image:** known only from retrospective literary description;
- **identity of idol, house, and deity:** unresolved;
- **precise location:** Tabāla is textually strongest, but disputed;
- **continuous survival after 632:** unproved;
- **revived pagan worship into modern times:** unverified;
- **present physical object:** none authenticated;
- **present architectural remains:** claimed at Tabāla, not conclusively identified.
