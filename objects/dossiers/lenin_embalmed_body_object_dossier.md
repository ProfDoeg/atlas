# Lenin's embalmed body: Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `lenins_embalmed_body_object_dossier.md`  
**Research status:** current to 6 September 2026

> **Evidentiary key**
>
> - **Documented:** supported by a contemporary record, archival catalogue, official act, photograph, or multiple independent accounts.
> - **Scientific finding:** reported by specialists or in technical literature; access to the body and its records is unusually restricted, so reproducibility is limited.
> - **Institutional claim:** stated by the Mausoleum laboratory, a successor institution, or a government body, but not independently verified.
> - **Scholarly inference:** an interpretation based on archival, ethnographic, architectural, or political evidence.
> - **Disputed / unverified:** conflicting testimony or a claim unsupported by accessible physical evidence.
> - **Propaganda / ritual formula:** a political claim whose historical significance does not depend on literal truth.
> - **Modern legend:** a post-1924 story—wax substitution, intended resurrection, occult machinery—for which no adequate documentary evidence is known.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Object and names

The object is the chemically preserved, repeatedly treated, partly reconstructed human cadaver of Vladimir Il′ich Ul′ianov, better known as Lenin, who died at Gorki near Moscow on 21 January 1924. It has been publicly exhibited, apart from closures and wartime evacuation, in a transparent sarcophagus inside Lenin’s Mausoleum on Red Square.

Relevant names include:

- **Russian:** `тело В. И. Ленина` (*telo V. I. Lenina*, “the body of V. I. Lenin”); `забальзамированное тело В. И. Ленина` (“the embalmed body of V. I. Lenin”); `останки В. И. Ленина` (“the remains of V. I. Lenin”).
- **Official 1924 wording:** documents variously call it the `тело` (“body”), `гроб с телом` (“coffin with the body”), and `гроб с останками` (“coffin with the remains”). On 25 January the Politburo approved a draft resolution preserving the “coffin with the remains of V. I. Lenin” in a crypt by the Kremlin Wall. The original is catalogued as RGASPI, fond 17, opis′ 163, delo 396, folios 57–58. [State Historical Museum digital catalogue](https://lenin.shm.ru/2267-2/)
- **Popular Russian:** `мумия Ленина` (“Lenin’s mummy”), common but technically imprecise. The preservation team generally describes its procedure as long-term embalming, conservation, or preservation of biological structures rather than natural or desiccating mummification.
- **Relic language:** `мощи Ильича` (“Ilyich’s relics”) appears chiefly in polemic, journalism, or comparison with Orthodox saints’ relics. It was not the preferred official Soviet designation.
- **Mausoleum:** `Мавзолей Владимира Ильича Ленина`, ordinarily `Мавзолей В. И. Ленина`; from 1953 until Stalin’s removal in 1961 the façade and institution were rededicated to Lenin and Stalin.

“Lenin” was Ulyanov’s revolutionary pseudonym, not a name bestowed on the cadaver. No securely established etymology of the pseudonym is required to identify the object, and competing explanations of that living person’s pen name do not affect the body’s provenance.

### 1.2 What physically survives

**Documented baseline.** The corpse is not anatomically complete. Alexei Abrikosov performed the autopsy on 22 January 1924, from 11:40 to 14:50 according to the published act. The cranial vault was opened; the brain and thoracic and abdominal viscera were removed; vessels were cut during the autopsy. The autopsy act names Abrikosov and those present and describes profound cerebral-arterial disease and fresh hemorrhage. [Published autopsy act](https://hai-nyzhnyk.in.ua/doc2/1924%2801%2922.lenin.php) [Contemporary publication catalogued by the State Historical Museum](https://lenin.shm.ru/2252-2/)

Consequently, the displayed object comprises principally:

- skeleton and remaining connective framework;
- skin and surviving subcutaneous and muscular tissues;
- hair, moustache, and beard;
- repeatedly impregnated biological tissues;
- closed eyelids over artificial ocular supports or prosthetic eyes;
- filling, supporting, sealing, and contour-restoration materials;
- clothing and a concealed preservative-bearing undergarment;
- no brain and no complete set of viscera.

Claims that “all organs” remain in the Mausoleum body are false. Some removed material entered separate institutional custody. In the 1941 evacuation inventory, the transported collection reportedly included the body, Lenin’s heart, brain preparations, a bullet formerly removed from him, and the laboratory’s manipulation journal. These separate specimens should not be confused with the displayed cadaver.

### 1.3 Materials

The best accessible technical synthesis identifies the long-term Soviet-Russian system as a dynamic replacement of tissue water with preservative compounds rather than simple drying. Constituents reported for Lenin’s treatment include:

- formaldehyde/formalin;
- ethanol;
- glycerol;
- potassium acetate;
- sodium acetate;
- water;
- thymol;
- quinine compounds;
- hydrochloric and acetic acids in particular stages;
- hydrogen peroxide and ammonia for correcting discoloration;
- gelatin, casein or animal glues for sealing;
- paraffin, white petrolatum, wax, and later restoration compounds for volume correction.

The 1924 main bath is reported as approximately 240 litres of glycerol, 110 kilograms of potassium acetate, and 150 litres of water, with quinine/hydrochloric-acid solution added. The body was first exposed to progressively stronger formaldehyde, alcohol, and glycerol treatments; numerous access incisions were made because the autopsy had interrupted the vascular system. Artificial eyes were inserted, the lids sutured, and the head and hands received repeated local treatment. [Journal of Plastination technical review](https://journal.plastination.org/articles/embalming-techniques-for-long-term-preservation-of-bodies/)

A modern form of the Soviet-Russian technique uses a hydrophobic, nonwoven, disposable overall sealed at the cuffs and neck and filled with roughly ten litres of preservative solution, beneath the visible clothes. It is reasonable, but not publicly demonstrated at every treatment, that Lenin’s current preservation uses this general system. The exact contemporary recipe and all repair records remain restricted.

### 1.4 Form, appearance, dress, and pose

The body lies supine, head slightly elevated, arms alongside the torso with forearms bent so that the hands remain visible. It is normally shown in a dark suit, white shirt, and tie. The face, scalp, hands, and part of the shirtfront are illuminated; much of the cadaver is concealed by clothing.

Publicly accessible sources do not supply an authoritative current weight or complete set of body dimensions. Lenin’s living stature is often given in biographies, but it should not be imported as an exact measurement of the treated cadaver. Conservation can change mass and tissue volume, and the body is positioned on a supporting pedestal.

The visible colour and contours are not spontaneous properties of preserved flesh. They are managed through moisture equilibrium, bleaching or colour correction, injected fillers, lighting, fixed pose, and optical calibration. Laboratory methods have included:

- relief mapping of head and hand contours;
- stereophotography;
- photoelectric colour measurement;
- microscopic sampling;
- analysis of fluid exchange;
- adjustment of soft-tissue volume with heated paraffin/petrolatum mixtures;
- repair of incisions with gelatin, silk, and formalin-treated cotton;
- controlled temperature, humidity, filtration, and illumination.

The sarcophagus and lighting are therefore parts of the object’s display apparatus, though not components of the corpse in the strict sense. Technical descriptions of related bodies preserved by the same laboratory show that fixed coordinates were used to reproduce pose and lighting accurately.

### 1.5 Damage, defects, and repair

The 26 March 1924 examination recorded an object already in serious decline:

- yellow-earthy discoloration of the head;
- darker regions around the orbits, nose, temples, and ears;
- drying and parchment-like changes;
- deformation around the eyes;
- changes to fingers and lips;
- depression and disruption at the cranial saw line;
- other post-mortem and autopsy defects.

The protocol is reproduced in the documentary appendix to Ilya Zbarsky’s memoir. [Zbarsky documentary volume](https://www.belousenko.com/books/memoirs/zbarsky_lenin.pdf)

Known or reported subsequent interventions include:

- replacement of collapsed ocular volume with prosthetic eyes;
- suturing of the eyelids;
- repeated treatment of the face and hands;
- concealed incisions across the abdomen, shoulders, forearms, thighs, calves, back, buttocks, palms, and fingers to facilitate diffusion;
- contour correction by injected or inserted artificial material;
- treatment of fungal or mould spots;
- repairs to localized skin damage;
- periodic removal of microscopic specimens;
- recurrent immersion and re-impregnation;
- replacement of garments and undergarments.

A frequently repeated report says gelatin experimentation damaged skin on a foot or ankle and that a patch was applied. The laboratory’s restricted documentation prevents an independent, full accounting of that repair. It should be treated as specialist testimony rather than an openly inspectable conservation record.

### 1.6 Condition as an object

The body is neither an untouched corpse nor a wax sculpture. “Original” tissue persists, but it has been chemically transformed, repeatedly sampled, and supplemented. Alexei Yurchak describes the conservation principle as preservation of **form and dynamic physical characteristics** while biological matter is continuously altered or replaced. That is a scholarly description of identity practice, not evidence that literally every molecule has been exchanged. [Yurchak, “Form versus Matter” record](https://openurl.ebsco.com/contentitem/gcd%3A111939005?id=ebsco%3Agcd%3A111939005&sid=ebsco%3Aplink%3Acrawler)

No dependable public percentage of “original Lenin” exists. Figures circulated in tabloids—often 10, 23, or 50 percent—do not derive from a published inventory, CT study, mass balance, or peer-reviewed measurement and should not be repeated as findings.

No museum accession number for the body has been published. The Mausoleum is not part of the Moscow Kremlin Museums’ collection; the museums explicitly say the Mausoleum falls outside their institutional composition. [Moscow Kremlin Museums FAQ](https://kreml.ru/ru/services/frequent-questions)

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Biological death and conversion into a preserved object

Lenin died at approximately 18:50 on 21 January 1924 in the estate’s Large House at Gorki. The cadaver as an administratively controlled political object begins immediately: officials arrived, a funeral commission was formed, photography and transport were ordered, and access was regulated.

The earliest secure visual and documentary attestations are therefore not later relic legends but:

- deathbed photographs at Gorki;
- the government death announcement of 22 January;
- the autopsy act of 22 January;
- funeral-commission records;
- photographs of the red coffin, procession, funeral train, Hall of Columns, and Red Square ceremony.

The Russian archival portal’s funeral exhibition identifies the government announcement as RGASPI, f. 16, op. 1, d. 2, l. 7; the emergency Central Committee plenum protocol as RGASPI, f. 17, op. 2, d. 110, ll. 1–2; and the funeral commission’s public-access notice as RGASPI, f. 16, op. 1, d. 16, ll. 1–2. [Russian archival exhibition](https://lenin.rusarchives.ru/pohorony-vi-lenina)

### 2.2 First embalming

On 22 January Abrikosov performed a conventional temporary embalming associated with autopsy and lying in state. The reported solution contained water, formalin, ethanol, zinc chloride, and glycerol. It was intended to retard decomposition for days, not preserve the body indefinitely. Cutting the great vessels, normal in autopsy, later made whole-body perfusion impracticable.

The severe outdoor cold and the cold Hall of Columns helped retard decomposition during the public farewell. This contingent preservation should not be transformed into a claim that the long-term experiment had been planned before death.

### 2.3 Decision to preserve

The decision emerged in stages.

1. **22–25 January:** authorities decided to retain the coffin in an accessible crypt. The State Historical Museum catalogues the Politburo’s 25 January approval of the Presidium resolution.
2. **Public justification:** Soviet newspapers printed resolutions and letters urging that the body be kept. Some were genuine expressions of mass mourning; the extent to which the campaign was centrally orchestrated remains debated.
3. **Initial horizon:** accessible evidence supports keeping the body for continued viewing, not yet a scientifically settled plan for indefinite preservation.
4. **February–March:** as deterioration became visible, commissions considered artificial refrigeration, freezing, and different chemical methods.
5. **March:** Vorobiev and Boris Zbarsky proposed an experimental long-term chemical treatment based partly on Nikolai Melnikov-Razvedenkov’s formalin–alcohol–glycerol–acetate technique.
6. **24–26 March:** the funeral commission became the Commission for Perpetuating Lenin’s Memory; Vorobiev’s conditions were accepted; the main procedure began 26 March.
7. **26 July:** the official commission judged the operation successful.
8. **1 August:** the preserved body was reopened to the general public.

RGASPI’s centenary exhibition catalogues the critical documentary sequence: a 3 March proposal involving Zbarsky; the executive troika’s 11 March decision to investigate Professor Shor’s method; the medical commission’s 12 March conclusion that no established method guaranteed long-term preservation; Vorobiev’s signed conditions of 25 March; the 2 June progress announcement; and the 26 July acceptance protocol. [RGASPI Mausoleum exhibition](https://rgaspi.kaisa.ru/mausoleum/object/938480923)

### 2.4 Makers and technique

The body has no single “maker.” Its present condition is cumulative.

- **Alexei I. Abrikosov:** autopsy and first short-term embalming, 22 January.
- **Vladimir P. Vorobiev:** principal anatomist of the experimental long-term procedure.
- **Boris I. Zbarsky:** biochemist, organizer, and later long-serving laboratory head.
- **P. I. Karuzin, A. L. Shabadash, A. N. Zhuravlev, Ya. G. Zamkovsky**, and other assistants: participants in the 1924 work.
- **Nikolai F. Melnikov-Razvedenkov:** intellectual predecessor whose 1898 method for anatomical preparations supplied an important technical basis.
- **Sergei Mardashev, Yuri Denisov-Nikolsky, Vladimir Bykov, Yuri Romakov**, and successive laboratory personnel: developers and custodians of the later system.

Vorobiev and Zbarsky did not create preservation ex nihilo. Their achievement was adaptation under emergency conditions to an autopsied whole body intended to retain lifelike surface form indefinitely.

### 2.5 Cost and material provenance

No reliable comprehensive cost ledger for the 1924 operation has been published online. Records document special procurement: chemicals, instruments, a rubber bath made at the Moscow “Kauchuk” factory, laboratory alterations, electrical supply, and working accommodations beside the Mausoleum. The provenance of individual formaldehyde, glycerol, acetate, textile, glass, and wax batches is not publicly reconstructed.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because a corpse is not an ordinary alienable commodity, “ownership” is legally and ethically awkward. The chain below tracks physical possession, administrative control, and custodial responsibility, not private title.

### 3.1 Gorki: 21–23 January 1924

- **21 January:** body in the Large House at Gorki.
- **22 January:** examined, autopsied, and temporarily embalmed there under state supervision.
- **23 January, about 09:30:** placed in a red coffin and carried from the house to the Gerasimovskaya railway platform.
- **Transport:** loaded into baggage car no. 1691; hauled to Moscow by locomotive U-127.
- **Arrival:** about 13:00 at Moscow’s Paveletsky—then commonly Saratovsky—station.
- **Custody:** funeral commission, medical personnel, party leadership, railway and security personnel.

The State Historical Museum holds a photographic album documenting Gorki, the procession, train, station, Hall of Columns, funeral, and Mausoleum construction. [State Historical Museum album catalogue](https://catalog.shm.ru/entity/ALBUM/700868471)

### 3.2 Hall of Columns: 23–27 January 1924

The coffin was carried from Paveletsky station to the Hall of Columns in the House of Trade Unions. Public viewing continued day and night. Contemporary accounts claimed hundreds of thousands and eventually about a million viewers; such crowd totals are estimates, not individually verifiable counts.

At midnight on 26 January public access ceased. On 27 January the coffin was borne in a state procession to Red Square.

### 3.3 First Mausoleum: 27 January–spring 1924

A temporary wooden crypt, designed at great speed by Alexei Shchusev, was built in frozen ground near the Kremlin Wall. The coffin was lowered into it on 27 January.

The enclosure was not initially an adequate long-term conservation environment. Visitor heat, humidity, and condensation became problems. It closed on 30 January for alterations and thereafter underwent repeated technical changes.

### 3.4 Experimental laboratory phase: March–July 1924

A working laboratory was installed in or beside the temporary structure. On 26 March the corpse was removed from normal display and subjected to the principal long-term procedure. During this phase it passed physically through:

- inspection table;
- rubber bath;
- separate or localized head and hand treatments;
- repeated chemical baths;
- wrapping and dressing;
- experimental display.

The Mausoleum briefly opened in June for Comintern delegates, providing a politically important preview before the procedure was formally declared successful.

### 3.5 Second wooden Mausoleum and Melnikov sarcophagus: 1924–1930

A larger wooden Mausoleum replaced the first structure. Konstantin Melnikov won the commission for a geometrically faceted glass sarcophagus. The body was installed within it and reopened to the public on 1 August.

This is an important distinction in object identity:

- the **cadaver** continued;
- the **first coffin/display environment** did not;
- the **sarcophagus** was a replaceable apparatus and was later replaced;
- the **building** was rebuilt twice.

### 3.6 Permanent stone Mausoleum: 1930–1941

The current granite-and-labradorite Mausoleum, designed by Shchusev and collaborators, was erected in 1929–30. The body and Melnikov display case were transferred into it.

A state commission examined the body in January 1934 and formally praised its condition. In 1939 the Politburo reorganized and institutionalized Zbarsky’s laboratory. A secret January 1939 memorandum to Stalin called for detailed documentation of the 1924 procedure, training younger specialists, laboratory expansion, sarcophagus improvement, and renewed treatment. [1939 secret memorandum](https://hrono.ru/dokum/193_dok/19390108beria.php) The 11 December 1939 Politburo resolution established the laboratory under Zbarsky. [Published Politburo resolution](https://istmat.org/node/67991)

### 3.7 Wartime evacuation to Tyumen: July 1941–March/April 1945

This was the body’s only documented long absence from Moscow.

- **26 June 1941:** Politburo decision to evacuate followed the German invasion.
- **3 July:** an evacuation act signed by Kremlin commandant Nikolai Spiridonov and Boris Zbarsky recorded removal under NKGB order no. 00255. The original is identified as Central Archive of the FSB, f. 17, op. 25, d. 10, l. 86. [Archival catalogue and act description](https://docs.historyrussia.org/ru/nodes/222857-akt-o-vyvoze-tela-v-i-lenina-v-tyumen-3-iyulya-1941-g)
- **Rail journey:** a special train carried the body, laboratory equipment, staff, and security eastward. Published accounts disagree whether arrival was 7 or 10 July; the discrepancy should remain explicit pending inspection of train logs.
- **Tyumen location:** the building of the Tyumen Agricultural Institute/Technical School at 2 Respubliki Street, conventionally identified as the temporary Mausoleum laboratory.
- **Cover:** the operation was secret. The building was secured, windows masked or bricked, and environmental control improvised.
- **Custody:** Zbarsky’s scientific group, Kremlin command personnel, and state-security guards.
- **Monitoring:** conditions were checked continuously. Contemporary wartime reports claimed the body improved or remained unchanged; these were internal institutional assessments, not independent examinations.
- **25 or 26 March 1945:** some accounts date the departure from Tyumen.
- **29 March:** order for re-evacuation.
- **April:** arrival and reinstatement in Moscow.

The Tyumen regional heritage account confirms the 1941 Politburo decision and April 1945 return. [Tyumen regional account](https://tumen.rcfh.ru/presscenter/novosti/evakuatsiya-tela-lenina-v-tyumen/)

The wartime transfer does not create a documentary gap sufficient to support substitution. There is a signed removal act, identified personnel, a guarded destination, medical reporting, and a return operation. Much operational detail nevertheless remained classified.

### 3.8 Return and new sarcophagus: 1945–1953

On return, the body went through examination and restorative treatment before public reopening. The Melnikov sarcophagus was replaced in 1945 by a new design associated with Shchusev and engineer/sculptor Boris Yakovlev; later modifications incorporated stronger and eventually bullet-resistant glass.

The body’s transfer from one sarcophagus to another was a normal custodial intervention, not replacement of the cadaver.

### 3.9 Shared chamber with Stalin: 1953–1961

After Stalin died in March 1953, the same laboratory embalmed his body. On 9 March it was placed beside Lenin’s. The institution became the Mausoleum of V. I. Lenin and I. V. Stalin.

This was not a transfer of Lenin’s body. It altered the room’s ritual configuration and political meaning. On the night of 31 October–1 November 1961, following the Twenty-Second Party Congress, Stalin’s body was removed and buried by the Kremlin Wall. Lenin’s remained in place; the façade was restored to his name alone.

### 3.10 Late Soviet custody: 1961–1991

The body remained under the Mausoleum laboratory and Kremlin commandant/security system. Major phases included:

- repeated bath treatments, commonly described as occurring at intervals of roughly eighteen months;
- daily surface and environmental checks;
- closures for treatment;
- development of microscopy, relief measurement, colourimetry, and non-invasive monitoring;
- laboratory relocation in 1976 to a better-equipped research facility at 2 Krasina Street while the display and service facilities under the Mausoleum continued to function;
- stronger glass and security after attacks.

The laboratory became a technical export network, preserving or advising on the bodies of Georgi Dimitrov, Klement Gottwald, Ho Chi Minh, Agostinho Neto, and others. Lenin’s body remained the reference object around which the expertise developed.

### 3.11 Post-Soviet custody: 1991–present

After the Soviet Union dissolved:

- the body was not transferred to Lenin’s relatives, the Communist Party, or a museum;
- the Russian state retained control of the Mausoleum and Red Square security;
- the scientific group was reorganized within or in association with the All-Russian Scientific Research Institute of Medicinal and Aromatic Plants, VILAR;
- public and private funding arrangements changed and were often opaque;
- the Federal Protective Service and its Kremlin Commandant’s Office continued to control access and security;
- periodic treatment continued.

A 2016 state procurement reportedly allocated nearly 14 million rubles to maintaining Lenin’s “lifelike appearance.” This demonstrated state expenditure despite recurring assertions that preservation was wholly privately funded. The Mausoleum building is a federal cultural-heritage object; the body’s distinct legal status is much less clearly codified in publicly accessible law.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Is the displayed body Lenin’s?

The strongest available conclusion is that there is a documented continuity from the cadaver at Gorki to the body presently maintained in the Mausoleum.

Evidence includes:

- contemporary deathbed photographs;
- named autopsy witnesses and a published autopsy act;
- photographed transport and lying in state;
- continuous commission records in early 1924;
- treatment protocols and named practitioners;
- long-running inspection records;
- a signed 1941 evacuation act;
- identifiable Tyumen custody;
- postwar return and laboratory continuity.

No rival physical claimant exists. No second body with an evidence-based claim to be Lenin has been produced.

### 4.2 Wax-effigy story

Rumours that a wax figure replaced the corpse arose very early and resurfaced after the Tyumen evacuation and again around the Soviet collapse.

**Evidence for:** the visible skin has a waxlike appearance; artificial material has been used for supports and localized contour repair; viewing is distant, dimly lit, and guarded; conservation records are restricted.

**Evidence against:** named medical continuity; documented biological sampling; flexible joints and treated tissues described by laboratory personnel; separate observers allowed close access; and no documented substitution event. Journalist Louis Fischer reported that Boris Zbarsky opened the sarcophagus and moved the head to demonstrate that it was not a wax statue.

The correct finding is therefore: **the body contains artificial materials but is not documented as an artificial effigy.** The inference “some wax or paraffin is present, therefore the whole is wax” is invalid.

### 4.3 “Double” or substitute-cadaver claim

Ilya Zbarsky, son of Boris and himself a former laboratory worker, was reported as recalling experiments with similar-looking bodies or substitute models. Former laboratory specialist Yuri Romakov denied that “doubles” were stored for rotational display. [Contemporary report on the dispute](https://fakty.ua/108637-v-laboratorii-pri-mavzolee-net-quot-dvojnikov-quot-vladimira-ilicha-lenina)

Anatomical research cadavers, casts, models, or visual doubles used in experimentation would not prove substitution. No chain of evidence identifies a supposed substitute, removal of Lenin’s remains, or a date when exchange occurred. The claim remains unverified.

### 4.4 Continuity through material replacement

The deeper authenticity problem is philosophical and curatorial: if tissues are chemically transformed and defects filled, in what sense is it the “same” body?

Three identities can be distinguished:

1. **Biographical identity:** remains originating from Vladimir Ulyanov’s cadaver.
2. **Material identity:** persistence of original tissue. Substantial material transformation has occurred, but its precise extent is unpublished.
3. **Formal/display identity:** continued maintenance of a recognizable, officially standardized Lenin.

The laboratory privileges the third while retaining enough of the first two to reject “replica” as an adequate description. Yurchak’s formulation—continuous change of matter in order to hold form stable—captures this institutional ontology.

### 4.5 Scientific examination and its limits

Reported examinations include histology, light and electron microscopy, chemical analysis, surface-relief mapping, stereophotography, colourimetry, tissue sampling, and preservative-fluid analysis. Technical literature reports recognizable microscopic structures alongside:

- karyolysis;
- reduced nuclear volume;
- loss or reduction of DNA, RNA, and histones;
- changes in epidermal and dermal structure;
- chemical replacement of tissue water;
- altered proteolytic-enzyme activity.

These findings concern preserved tissue and the general laboratory method. Raw datasets, complete photographs, recent CT imaging if any, tissue maps, and an independently reviewed present-day inventory of Lenin’s body have not been released.

No published DNA authentication comparing a sample from the Mausoleum body with a verified Ulyanov-family reference has been found. No radiocarbon test would meaningfully prove individual identity, and no such test is reported. The body’s identity rests primarily on custody, not modern genetic testing.

### 4.6 Authority to authenticate

Authority moved through:

- the doctors and funeral commission in January 1924;
- the Commission for Perpetuating Lenin’s Memory;
- government medical commissions;
- the Mausoleum laboratory and Soviet party-state;
- Kremlin security and Russian federal institutions;
- post-Soviet VILAR specialists.

This concentration of custody and authentication in the same state-linked system is an evidentiary limitation. It does not disprove continuity, but it restricts independent audit.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Institutions

### Medical and scientific actors

- **Alexei Ivanovich Abrikosov (1924):** performed autopsy and temporary embalming; his severing of major vessels constrained later treatment.
- **Vladimir Petrovich Vorobiev (1924–1937):** principal anatomist of long-term preservation; insisted on chemical immersion rather than freezing.
- **Boris Ilyich Zbarsky (1924–1952):** biochemist, organizer, laboratory head, wartime custodian in Tyumen.
- **Nikolai Melnikov-Razvedenkov:** predecessor whose late nineteenth-century anatomical-preservation chemistry was adapted.
- **P. I. Karuzin, A. L. Shabadash, A. N. Zhuravlev, Ya. G. Zamkovsky:** 1924 working team.
- **Sergei R. Mardashev:** postwar biochemical specialist and laboratory director; studied diffusion and equilibrium in preserved tissues.
- **Ilya B. Zbarsky:** Boris’s son, laboratory worker and later memoirist; an important but sometimes controversial witness.
- **Yuri M. Lopukhin:** physician and author of a major historical-medical study, *Illness, Death and Embalming of V. I. Lenin: Truth and Myths*.
- **Yuri Denisov-Nikolsky, Vladimir Bykov, Yuri Romakov, and later VILAR personnel:** successive custodians and method developers.
- **Alexei Yurchak:** anthropologist and historian of the body’s material and political ontology; an interpreter, not a custodian.

### Political and administrative actors

- **Felix Dzerzhinsky:** chaired the funeral/perpetuation commission and authorized resources and security.
- **Leonid Krasin:** supervised preservation questions and supported technical proposals, including refrigeration before the chemical method prevailed.
- **Joseph Stalin:** supported preservation politically and later made Lenin’s body central to dynastic legitimization; he did not personally embalm or possess it.
- **Mikhail Kalinin, Lev Kamenev, Grigory Zinoviev, Alexei Rykov, Nikolai Bukharin, and other leaders:** participants in funeral and policy decisions.
- **Nadezhda Krupskaya:** Lenin’s widow, who publicly opposed personality worship. Her 29 January appeal asked workers not to build monuments, palaces, or magnificent ceremonies in Lenin’s name. This is strong evidence of opposition to cultic commemoration, although its exact relationship to every technical preservation decision is sometimes overstated.
- **Leon Trotsky:** later recalled opposition to treating Lenin’s body like saints’ relics. His account is important but retrospective.
- **Nikolai Spiridonov:** Kremlin commandant and signatory of the 1941 evacuation act.
- **Federal Protective Service/Kremlin Commandant’s Office:** present security and access authority.

### Architectural and display actors

- **Alexei Shchusev:** architect of the first, second, and permanent Mausoleum schemes.
- **Konstantin Melnikov:** designer of the 1924 faceted glass sarcophagus.
- **Boris Yakovlev:** associated with the postwar sarcophagus.
- **A. L. Pasternak:** reportedly prepared watercolour records of nine skin areas before the long-term procedure, an early attempt to fix a colour baseline.

### Institutions and networks

- Commission for Organizing Lenin’s Funeral.
- Commission for Perpetuating the Memory of V. I. Lenin.
- Central Executive Committee of the USSR.
- Politburo and Central Committee.
- Kremlin Commandant’s Office, OGPU/NKVD/NKGB/KGB and successor security bodies.
- Mausoleum Laboratory.
- VILAR and its biomedical-conservation centre.
- Moscow and Kharkov medical/anatomical institutions.
- Institute of the Brain, custodian of the separately removed brain material.
- Communist parties and governments that imported the laboratory’s preservation system.
- Communist Party of the Russian Federation and affiliated memorial groups, which advocate keeping the body in place.

There is no divine being formally associated with the object. Comparisons with Orthodox saints, relics, incorruptibility, or a sacred sovereign body are analytical or polemical comparisons, not evidence that Soviet doctrine deified Lenin.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Lying in state and funeral

The body’s first function was funerary: to receive mass farewell in the Hall of Columns and at the 27 January ceremony. Simultaneous stoppages of work, sirens, artillery salutes, honour guards, banners, and speeches synchronized the cadaver with the new Soviet state.

The body was described as interred in a `склеп`—a crypt—even though it remained visible and unburied in the ordinary earth-burial sense.

### 6.2 Pilgrimage and civic initiation

Once reopened in August 1924, visiting became a Soviet civic ritual:

- citizens queued through Red Square;
- school groups and Young Pioneers attended;
- military personnel and foreign communist delegations visited;
- wreaths and flowers were presented;
- official guests were photographed outside;
- silence, uncovered heads, steady movement, and non-contact were enforced.

This was not a liturgy in Soviet legal terminology, but its regulated approach, guarded threshold, visible incorruptibility, and exemplary founder encouraged comparison with pilgrimage. The Soviet state rejected religious explanations and credited science.

### 6.3 Political legitimation

The body conferred no constitutional office. Its symbolic position nevertheless legitimized rulers who stood above it on the Mausoleum tribune. The spatial relationship implied succession:

- Lenin’s body below;
- current leadership above;
- mass procession before them.

Stalin’s placement beside Lenin in 1953 was an unusually literal claim to co-canonical status. Removing Stalin in 1961 equally used control of preserved bodies to revise legitimate succession.

### 6.4 Military and state ceremony

The Mausoleum served as reviewing stand for parades. Standards captured from Nazi Germany were cast before it in the 24 June 1945 Victory Parade, after Lenin’s body had returned from Tyumen. The body was concealed inside during many exterior events but remained the building’s legitimating centre.

Since the Soviet collapse, exterior state ritual has weakened. The former permanent honour guard, Post No. 1, left the Mausoleum in 1993 and was later established at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier. Contemporary parade staging often visually screens the Mausoleum.

### 6.5 Access rules and taboo

The body may not be touched by visitors. Photography and video are restricted; visitors are screened; large bags and weapons are prohibited; silence and respectful conduct are expected. The FSO’s 2021 rules expressly govern admission and conduct in the Mausoleum and Kremlin Wall Necropolis. [FSO visitor rules via ConsultantPlus](https://www.consultant.ru/document/cons_doc_LAW_381865/d22b29273083efc8b236e162758e4434b2057c94/)

The restricted laboratory creates a second, scientific taboo: only authorized specialists may undress, manipulate, sample, bathe, or repair the body.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 “The people spontaneously demanded eternal preservation”

**Official tradition:** factory resolutions and letters demanded that Lenin remain visible forever, and the state merely obeyed mass grief.

**Documented core:** public requests, newspaper letters, and organizational resolutions did exist. On 25 January *Rabochaia Moskva* printed appeals under a heading equivalent to “Lenin’s body must be preserved.”

**Qualification:** party bodies controlled press and memorial organization, and the technical project was decided within a small state commission. Historians disagree about how spontaneous the campaign was. “The people demanded it” functioned as political authorization and should not be accepted as a complete causal account.

### 7.2 Stalin secretly planned a religious cult before Lenin died

A retrospective story places a 1923 Politburo discussion before Lenin’s death, with Stalin presenting a proposal from provincial comrades to preserve the body, and Trotsky and others condemning it as Orthodox-style relic worship.

This is plausible and appears in memoir literature, but no surviving contemporary stenographic record conclusively verifies every quoted exchange. The strongest documentary chain begins after death. The story should be classified as **retrospective testimony with a plausible political core**, not a verbatim archival transcript.

### 7.3 The body was preserved for future resurrection

**Modern legend.** According to this theory, Bolsheviks or scientists expected future technology to revive Lenin.

No 1924 medical or commission document located here proposes biological revival. The autopsy removed brain and viscera, cut vessels, and chemically fixed tissues—actions incompatible with preserving a person for resuscitation. Mausoleum scientists have rejected the story. It may reflect later fusion of Soviet technological utopianism, Russian cosmism, cryonics, and science fiction.

### 7.4 Frozen, not embalmed

Early officials seriously considered refrigeration or freezing, and machinery was discussed or ordered. The method ultimately adopted was chemical embalming and immersion. Saying that Lenin is “kept frozen” is false. Environmental cooling is used, but the body is not a cryonic specimen.

### 7.5 Wax doll or rotating doubles

These rumours arose from the body’s smooth appearance, restricted viewing, wartime absence, and secrecy. They are unsupported by an identified substitution event or alternative remains. Artificial eyes, waxes, paraffins, glues, and fillers are present or have been used; that factual substrate helped generate the whole-doll legend.

### 7.6 The Mausoleum as occult ziggurat or energy machine

Post-Soviet esoteric literature and internet media describe the stepped Mausoleum as a Babylonian ziggurat, magical accumulator, sacrificial altar, Masonic structure, or device for controlling crowds.

The building does use stepped monumental forms and consciously archaic funerary associations. No credible design record shows occult machinery or ritual magic. Shchusev’s project belongs to modern monumental architecture, with possible broad echoes of ancient tomb forms, not a documented Mesopotamian cult programme.

### 7.7 Lenin’s incorruptibility proves sanctity or scientific omnipotence

Soviet presentation made the unchanged appearance proof of socialist science. Religious critics sometimes reverse the symbolism, calling it a parody of incorrupt saints.

The documented reality is active, expensive, recurrent intervention. The body’s appearance is not spontaneous incorruption. Its political effectiveness, however, depended on making the labour of preservation largely invisible.

### 7.8 Secret destruction in Tyumen

A late rumour says the original body decomposed, burned, disappeared, or was replaced during evacuation. Wartime secrecy makes the story narratively attractive. The signed evacuation act, named staff, wartime reports, and documented return weigh strongly against it. No contemporary evidence of loss has surfaced.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Location

As of the latest verifiable reports, the body remains in the mourning hall of Lenin’s Mausoleum, Red Square, Moscow, Russian Federation, beneath the transparent upper structure of the sarcophagus.

The Mausoleum stands before the Kremlin’s Senate Tower, within the Red Square/Kremlin ensemble inscribed on UNESCO’s World Heritage List in 1990. UNESCO calls it the Soviet Union’s prime example of symbolic monumental architecture. That inscription protects the architectural ensemble; it is not a separate international authentication of the cadaver. [UNESCO property record](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/545)

### 8.2 Custody and legal regime

- **Security and access:** Russian Federal Protective Service/Kremlin Commandant’s Office.
- **Scientific maintenance:** the successor Mausoleum specialist group associated with VILAR.
- **Building:** cultural-heritage property of federal significance and component of the Kremlin–Red Square World Heritage ensemble.
- **Body:** no published accession number or separate, comprehensive public statute has been identified. State possession and maintenance are clear in practice; the exact juridical classification of the remains is less transparent.

The official title of the federal heritage object has retained the historically anomalous wording “Mausoleum of Lenin Vladimir Ilyich and Stalin Joseph Vissarionovich” in some registers, reflecting its listing history rather than Stalin’s present physical presence.

### 8.3 Access

Admission has traditionally been free and limited to short daytime periods on selected days, subject to closures for state events, security, conservation, or Red Square restrictions. Exact hours change and should be verified immediately before a visit; the Kremlin Museums warn that the Mausoleum is not one of their institutions.

The public sees the body only while walking past it. No ordinary visitor examination, close photography, or lingering is permitted.

### 8.4 Conservation

Published specialist descriptions indicate:

- continual environmental monitoring;
- regular surface inspection;
- corrective local treatment as required;
- full or substantial re-impregnation about every eighteen months, although public schedules vary;
- replacement of clothing;
- inspection of colour, contour, flexibility, and tissue condition.

The Journal of Plastination reports that since 1992 preservation has been undertaken by VILAR specialists and identifies formaldehyde, ethanol, glycerol, sodium and potassium acetate, and thymol as core substances.

### 8.5 Burial controversy

There is no active judicial restitution case comparable to an ordinary museum-provenance dispute. The dispute is political and commemorative.

Positions include:

- **Communist Party and preservation groups:** the Mausoleum is an existing burial, historic monument, and World Heritage component; removal would be political desecration.
- **Russian Orthodox and conservative voices:** an exposed body should receive ordinary burial, often proposed beside Lenin’s mother in St Petersburg or in another family grave.
- **Liberal and anti-communist advocates:** removal would end an authoritarian state cult.
- **Pragmatic/state position:** postpone action because removal would divide society.

In late 2024 President Vladimir Putin said society might eventually reach the question of burial but that no divisive step should be taken in current circumstances. [Interfax report](https://www.interfax.ru/russia/999617) The Kremlin had previously stated that no removal was planned for the 2024 centenary. [TASS](https://tass.com/society/1381203)

No authoritative 2025–26 decision to remove or bury the body has been found.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Replicas and representations

Replicas occur in wax museums, exhibitions, film sets, cartoons, political installations, and internet imagery. These should not be confused with laboratory experimental models or alleged substitute cadavers.

The sarcophagus itself has existed in successive versions:

1. temporary coffin/crypt arrangement;
2. Melnikov’s faceted glass design;
3. postwar sarcophagus;
4. strengthened and technically modified later form.

The earlier Melnikov design has its own museum afterlife in drawings, models, and surviving components.

### 9.2 Political imagery

The body’s literal visibility reinforced a vastly larger field of Lenin images:

- portraits in schools and offices;
- busts and monumental statues;
- stamps, medals, posters, banknotes, books, and film;
- replicated Mausoleum tribunals and parade imagery;
- photographs of leaders standing over the body’s chamber.

Yurchak argues that the preserved body and canonized textual/visual Lenin developed as related political forms: both required continuous editorial maintenance while being presented as unchanged.

### 9.3 Literature, art, film, and popular culture

Important responses include:

- Vladimir Mayakovsky’s poem **“Vladimir Ilyich Lenin”** (1924), especially the formula “Lenin lived, Lenin lives, Lenin will live,” which became a quasi-liturgical political slogan.
- Soviet documentary funeral films and newsreels, which made the corpse’s transfer and mass mourning foundational national imagery.
- Mikhail Romm’s **Lenin in October** (1937) and the wider screen canon, which did not primarily depict the corpse but standardized the living face whose likeness the body’s conservation sought to maintain.
- Aleksandr Sokurov’s **Taurus** (2001), centred on Lenin’s bodily decline before death and working against the invulnerable canonical image.
- Alexei Yurchak’s “Lenin mushroom” analysis, which treats late-Soviet parody and the preserved body as connected transformations of canonical political form.
- *Spirou à Moscou* (1990), whose plot involves the threatened theft of Lenin’s body.
- recurring comedy, horror, alternate history, and videogame scenarios in which Lenin revives, is cloned, stolen, or mechanized.

These fictions materially shaped the modern resurrection and substitute-body legends, but none is evidence about the cadaver’s physical history.

### 9.4 Tourism and changing prominence

The Mausoleum shifted from mass civic pilgrimage toward a mixture of heritage tourism, political commemoration, curiosity, and contested memory. An Associated Press centenary report cited approximately 450,000 annual visitors and contrasted that figure with Soviet-era queues, while noting that modern parade structures often conceal the building. [Associated Press centenary report](https://apnews.com/article/de0e5ebc0fb12892b0ae0564f60baa78)

Visitor totals are institutionally reported estimates and fluctuate with closures and travel conditions.

### 9.5 Post-Soviet mobilization

In September 1991 rumours of imminent secret removal produced pickets and a “people’s guard” on Red Square. Communist organizations subsequently made preservation of the body a recurring political cause. Conversely, politicians have repeatedly proposed burial, removal, a referendum, or conversion of the building.

A 2021 Levada Center Moscow survey found opinion nearly evenly divided: 45 percent favoured removal and burial, 42 percent leaving the body in place. The sample was 505 Moscow adults, not a nationwide poll. [Levada Center](https://www.levada.ru/2021/05/25/moskva-zahoronenie-tela-lenina/)

### 9.6 Security incidents and attacks

The object’s symbolic status motivated repeated attempts to damage it.

- **19 March 1934 — documented attack:** Mitrofan Mikhailovich Nikitin entered with a Nagant revolver, fired two shots toward the body and missed, then killed himself. His protest letter condemned conditions in the countryside. An OGPU report survived in the Presidential Archive’s Stalin collection.
- **20 March 1959:** a visitor struck the sarcophagus glass with a hammer; the glass resisted.
- **14 July 1960:** Minibaev climbed the barrier and kicked through glass. Fragments reportedly damaged the body’s skin, requiring treatment. This is one of the few attacks known to have caused physical injury to the cadaver.
- **1963:** sources report an explosive incident in which the perpetrator was killed; details remain less secure than for 1973.
- **1 September 1973:** a suicide bomber detonated an explosive beside the sarcophagus, killing himself and a visiting couple, injuring children and guards. The strengthened sarcophagus protected the body. Later accounts identify the bomber as Nikolai Savrasov, but early investigation did not immediately establish his identity or motive.
- **Post-Soviet incidents:** protest shouting, attempted entry, paint or thrown objects, and alleged plans to steal or destroy the body; most did not reach it.

A contemporary archival discussion of the 1934 incident and later attacks reproduces the chain from the OGPU report. [Attack history and archival references](https://rusk.ru/st.php?idar=25459)

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Primary record

The strongest primary collections include:

- **RGASPI:** Central Committee, Politburo, funeral commission, perpetuation commission, correspondence, architectural and photographic files.
- **State Archive of the Russian Federation:** Central Executive Committee and Mausoleum administrative records.
- **Central Archive of the FSB:** security and 1941 evacuation records.
- **Presidential Archive of the Russian Federation:** high-level decisions and incident reports.
- **State Historical Museum:** photographs, funeral objects, digital documents, and the former Central Lenin Museum collections.
- **Mausoleum laboratory/VILAR records:** medical protocols, treatment journals, photographs, samples, measurements, and formulas; much remains inaccessible.
- **Institute of the Brain archives:** records of the separately removed brain.
- **Tyumen archives:** wartime accommodation, security, and recollection.

The RGASPI centenary exhibition materially improves documentary access by giving archival signatures for the March–July 1924 decisions.

### 10.2 Major works

- **Boris Zbarsky, *Mavzolei Lenina*** (wartime/postwar editions): insider presentation of the preservation project, shaped by Soviet publication constraints.
- **Ilya Zbarsky with Samuel Hutchinson, *Lenin’s Embalmers* / memoir editions:** family and laboratory testimony; invaluable but retrospective.
- **Yuri Lopukhin, *Bolezn′, smert′ i bal′zamirovanie V. I. Lenina: Pravda i mify* (1997):** detailed medical history by a specialist associated with the field.
- **Nina Tumarkin, *Lenin Lives! The Lenin Cult in Soviet Russia* (1983):** foundational study of political cult formation.
- **Benno Ennker, work on the Lenin cult:** emphasizes institutional and political construction.
- **Alexei Yurchak, “The Bodies of Lenin” and related work:** examines the relationship among biological material, canonized form, sovereignty, and scientific practice.
- **Modern embalming literature:** places the body in the Soviet-Russian long-term preservation school and supplies technical chemistry absent from most political histories.

### 10.3 Principal debates

#### Was indefinite preservation planned before death?

Retrospective memoirs say Stalin raised it in 1923; surviving post-death records are much firmer. The safest conclusion is that anticipatory discussion may have occurred, but indefinite chemical preservation became concrete only after death and visible deterioration.

#### Did popular demand cause the decision?

Popular demands were attested, but they operated inside a managed political-media system. Neither wholly spontaneous grief nor wholly fabricated propaganda explains the evidence alone.

#### Was the body intended as a religious relic?

The Soviet state explicitly attributed preservation to science and attacked religion. Structurally, however, historians find strong analogies with relic display, pilgrimage, incorruptibility, and sacred sovereignty. “Relic” is analytically illuminating but was not an admitted doctrinal classification.

#### How much original tissue remains?

Unknown. No public quantitative audit supports popular percentages.

#### Is it a corpse, mummy, sculpture, or installation?

Each captures one dimension:

- corpse: biological and legal origin;
- embalmed body: conservation procedure;
- mummy: popular long-term preserved-remains category;
- sculpture: continuous formal reconstruction;
- installation: dependence on sarcophagus, light, climate, guards, and visitor choreography.

The most precise description is **a chemically preserved and repeatedly reconstructed human cadaver maintained within a permanent technical-display system**.

#### Did preservation generate useful science?

The laboratory developed methods in tissue preservation, transplantation support, vascular perfusion, skin testing, microscopy, and solution analysis. Claims of benefit should be examined individually; the body undeniably sustained a long-lived research infrastructure, but not every later biomedical development can be causally credited to Lenin’s cadaver.

### 10.4 Source limitations

- medical and security secrecy;
- institutional self-authentication;
- publication under Soviet censorship;
- retrospective memoir embellishment;
- conflation of the body, brain, heart, bullet, clothing, sarcophagus, and Mausoleum;
- popular repetition of unsourced percentages and formulas;
- contradictory dates for the Tyumen journey;
- inability of independent researchers to inspect the current body or complete treatment log.

### Evidentiary conclusions

- **Existence:** certain; extensively photographed, documented, and continuously institutionally maintained.
- **Identity:** continuity with Lenin’s 1924 cadaver is strongly supported by provenance; no credible rival claimant.
- **Anatomical completeness:** false; brain and viscera were removed, and artificial supports and restoration materials are present.
- **Exact proportion of original tissue:** unestablished.
- **Whole-body wax replacement:** unsupported.
- **Wartime substitution:** unsupported.
- **Cryonic storage or intended resurrection:** contradicted by the autopsy and chemical fixation record.
- **Current exact formula and condition:** partly confidential and not independently auditable.
- **Present location:** Mausoleum, Red Square, Moscow.
- **Present dispute:** political dispute over continued display and burial, not a resolved legal transfer or restitution proceeding.
