# Penelope's shroud for Laertes: Object Dossier

## Evidentiary finding

Penelope’s shroud is a **legendary, textually attested textile**, not a surviving archaeological object. No ancient or modern institution possesses a physical cloth credibly claimed to be the shroud; there is no accession number, findspot, scientific examination, or continuous material provenance. Its reconstructable history therefore consists of:

1. the shroud’s narrated manufacture, repeated destruction, compelled completion, washing, and display within the *Odyssey*;
2. the transmission and interpretation of those passages;
3. ancient and modern visual representations that supply physical details absent from Homer;
4. the transformation of the textile into a literary, artistic, political, and linguistic symbol.

The dossier consequently distinguishes the **shroud within the story** from **real representations and reconstructions of it**.

---

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Object class

A large woven cloth intended—or represented as intended—for the future funeral of Laertes, father of Odysseus. Penelope uses its manufacture as the condition postponing her remarriage: she will choose a suitor only after completing the cloth. By day she weaves; by torchlight at night she secretly takes apart the day’s work. After three years the deception is exposed, and in the fourth year she is forced to finish it.

The *Odyssey* calls it both:

- **φᾶρος / phâros**: a large cloth, mantle, robe, winding sheet, or broad textile. The semantic range is wider than the English “shroud.” The LSJ entry defines its Homeric use principally as a large piece of cloth and records uses as clothing, sailcloth, and funerary covering. “[Shroud]” is therefore a contextual translation, not the object’s narrowly fixed Greek name. [LSJ, “φᾶρος”](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/dictionaries/entry/urn%3Acite2%3Ascaife-viewer%3Adictionaries.v1%3Alsj-n110368/)
- **ἱστός / histós**: loom, warp, web, or the textile upon a loom; in the episode commonly **μέγας ἱστός**, “great web” or “great loom-work.”

Later languages generally name it through Penelope rather than Laertes:

- Latin: **tela Penelopes**, “Penelope’s web/cloth”; Ovid has **pendula tela**, the “hanging web.”
- Italian: **la tela di Penelope**.
- French: **la toile de Pénélope**.
- Spanish: **la tela de Penélope**.
- German: **Penelopes Gewebe** or **Penelopes Webstuhl** when the loom is emphasized.
- English: **Penelope’s web**, **Penelope’s shroud**, or **Laertes’ shroud**.

“Web” in older English translations means woven fabric, not spiderweb.

### 1.2 Homeric physical description

The three internal accounts occur at *Odyssey* 2.93–110, 19.137–156, and 24.128–148. The first two describe the contemplated object but reveal remarkably little about its appearance.

Securely stated in Homer:

- It is a **φᾶρος**, a large cloth.
- It is woven on a **large loom/web**, μέγας ἱστός.
- It is intended for Laertes when death overtakes him.
- It is substantial enough that failure to provide it would expose Penelope to public reproach because Laertes had possessed great wealth.
- It is woven during daylight.
- It can be undone at night, with torches placed beside the loom.
- After exposure of the trick, Penelope completes it under compulsion.
- In Amphimedon’s Book 24 account, the completed cloth is washed and displayed and appears “like the sun or moon.”

Not stated in Homer:

- fiber;
- color;
- dimensions;
- weight;
- weave structure;
- number or type of loom weights;
- thread count;
- border construction;
- figured decoration;
- dyes;
- whether Penelope spun the yarn herself;
- whether assistants regularly helped weave it;
- whether it was meant to cover, wrap, dress, or be burned with Laertes;
- whether it ever reached Laertes;
- what happened to it after its display.

Claims that it was specifically linen, wool, white, embroidered, purple, or covered with mythological figures are reconstructions, not Homeric facts.

### 1.3 “Like the sun or moon”

Only Amphimedon’s retrospective account says that Penelope:

> washed the great web and showed it; it was like the sun or moon.

This can indicate brilliance, cleanliness, workmanship, visual splendor, or poetic elevation. It does not establish a literal solar or lunar design. The comparison also belongs to a dead suitor’s compressed and partly distorted narration, not to an omniscient technical description.

A major interpretive problem follows: the Book 24 account makes the completion and display of the cloth coincide closely with Odysseus’ return. That coincidence has encouraged readings of the shroud as simultaneously funerary, bridal, and narrative—an object whose completion terminates Penelope’s suspended status.

### 1.4 Likely construction technology

A vertical **warp-weighted loom** is a plausible historical model for an elite Greek household textile. Ancient images and archaeological loom weights document that technology. It also makes repeated unweaving mechanically possible: woven cloth could be rolled around a cloth beam, so observers would not necessarily see the entire textile at once. Susan T. Edmunds emphasizes that this helps explain how the deception could remain visually plausible. [Center for Hellenic Studies, “Picturing Homeric Weaving”](https://www-current.chs.harvard.edu/susan-t-edmunds-picturing-homeric-weaving/)

This is contextual inference, not a specification in the poem. Homer does not describe Penelope’s loom mechanism.

### 1.5 The Chiusi image

The most important ancient visualization is an Attic red-figure skyphos attributed to the anonymous **Penelope Painter**, conventionally dated about 460–430 BCE and found at La Badiola near Chiusi in Etruria. It is now in the Museo Archeologico Nazionale at Chiusi, inventory **62705**, formerly civic collection **C 1831**.

One side shows Penelope seated before a vertical loom while Telemachus stands nearby. A narrow, richly patterned textile hangs from the loom. The visible band includes winged and fantastic figures interpreted as pegasi, griffins, or related ornamental creatures. The other side represents Odysseus’ recognition during foot washing.

Important qualifications:

- The vase was made several centuries after the conventional date of the *Odyssey*.
- It is a representation of a textile, not the textile itself.
- The scene is not a literal illustration of a Homeric encounter: Telemachus does not visit Penelope at this loom in the transmitted *Odyssey*.
- The pictured textile is commonly identified as Laertes’ shroud, but no surviving inscription names the cloth.
- White-painted figure names once accompanied the characters but disappeared or became illegible, reportedly in the course of restoration.
- The iconography may reflect epic traditions, a lost monumental painting, theatrical material, or a combination of sources.

The museum interpretation dates the vessel around 460–450 BCE and calls it the earliest known representation of Penelope. [Wikimuseo/Museo Archeologico Nazionale di Chiusi](https://www.wikimuseo.it/w/en/museochiusi/959f12cc-b645-47eb-b2d8-967f44870ce2/p/2968b53b-bf61-44c8-bf0c-829231ec930f?tour=7794c9c3-3578-49b9-b940-2ac75bf58581)

Bottino gives the museum number, reconstructs the lost inscriptions, and surveys the disputes concerning the loom’s pattern and the image’s literary model. [Aldo Paolo Bottino, *The Phâros of Laertes: Weaving the Fabric of Epic*](https://chs.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/The-Pharos-of-Laertes-1.pdf)

### 1.6 Physical survival

No material fragment of the narrated shroud survives. There are:

- no ancient textile fragments securely associated with Penelope, Laertes, Odysseus, or an Ithacan royal household;
- no documented discovery of such a cloth;
- no relic tradition centered on a candidate shroud;
- no museum claimant;
- no scientific dating or fiber analysis;
- no legitimate chain connecting any archaeological textile to the Homeric object.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Traditional narrative origin

Within the *Odyssey*, Penelope devises the textile project after the suitors begin pressing her to remarry. In her own account she says a **daímōn**, an unnamed divine power or supernatural prompting, first put the idea into her mind. This does not mean that a god physically manufactures the shroud. Penelope sets up the loom in the halls and performs the weaving herself.

The purpose she publicly announces is funerary and reputational:

- Laertes is elderly but still alive.
- When death overtakes him, he should possess an appropriate burial cloth.
- Penelope fears reproach from Achaean women if a rich man’s corpse lacks a proper covering.
- Her explanation is socially credible precisely because textile production and preparation of the dead were strongly associated with women.

The private purpose is delay: completion will trigger the choice of a new husband.

### 2.2 Earliest textual attestation

The earliest securely recoverable attestation is the *Odyssey* itself, conventionally associated with Homer and generally dated to the late eighth or early seventh century BCE, though its formation within oral tradition and the point at which it became a stabilized written text remain disputed. Cambridge’s history of the Homeric text notes both the traditional late-eighth-century dating and growing support for a seventh-century date. [Cambridge, “History of the Text”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/homer-odyssey-books-xiii-and-xiv/history-of-the-text/084C45F1DE0E89B357E966F607D261E2)

The poem’s three repetitions are exceptional. Steven Lowenstam describes the shroud story as the only narrative repeated almost verbatim three times in Homer. [Steven Lowenstam, “The Shroud of Laertes and Penelope’s Guile”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/298877644_The_shroud_of_Laertes_and_Penelope%27s_guile_Homer_%27Odyssey%27)

The extant written witnesses are later than the presumed composition. The shroud cannot responsibly be dated by treating a modern printed Greek text as the original physical document. It emerged through oral-formulaic composition and textual transmission whose early stages are incompletely recoverable.

### 2.3 The three Homeric attestations

#### A. Antinous’ accusation: *Odyssey* 2.93–110

Antinous tells Telemachus that Penelope set up a great web and announced:

> “Young men, my suitors, since godlike Odysseus is dead, wait—though eager for marriage—until I finish this cloth.”

She identifies the cloth as a funeral covering for Laertes, lest the Achaean women reproach her. Antinous says that the suitors accepted the condition; she wove by day and undid the work by night. For three years the deception continued. In the fourth, **one of the women who knew the plan** informed the suitors, who caught Penelope undoing it. They compelled her to finish.

Evidentiary character: hostile testimony by a suitor, delivered publicly to assign blame to Penelope and justify the suitors’ continued occupation of Odysseus’ house.

The Greek text and commentary are available through Dickinson College Commentaries. [*Odyssey* 2.84–128](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/homer-odyssey/ii-84%E2%80%93128)

#### B. Penelope’s confession: *Odyssey* 19.137–156

Speaking to the disguised Odysseus, Penelope says the gods first put the stratagem in her mind. She repeats the public speech about Laertes and the danger of reproach. Her description of the working process is direct:

> “By day I kept weaving at the great web; by night I would unravel it after setting torches beside me.”

She says she concealed the trick for three years. In the fourth, the suitors came upon her **through the actions of her maids**, whom she condemns as shameless, and loudly rebuked her. She completed the web unwillingly and under compulsion.

Evidentiary character: first-person testimony by the maker, but still a crafted speech delivered to a stranger whose identity she does not officially know.

Greek and English texts are preserved on Perseus. [*Odyssey* 19.148–189](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D19%3Acard%3D148)

#### C. Amphimedon’s underworld account: *Odyssey* 24.128–148

The dead suitor Amphimedon recounts the ruse to Agamemnon. He again describes three years of weaving and undoing, discovery in the fourth year, and compelled completion. His version adds a terminal phase:

- Penelope completes the great web.
- She washes it.
- She displays it.
- It shines like the sun or moon.
- An evil spirit then brings Odysseus home.

Amphimedon also implies that Penelope and Odysseus coordinated the suitors’ destruction more directly than the preceding narrative warrants.

Evidentiary character: posthumous, partisan retrospective. The added display is nevertheless part of the received poem and supplies the only explicit description of the finished object’s appearance.

### 2.4 No named commissioner or workshop

There is no external commission, workshop account, payment, inventory, or maker’s mark. Penelope is both self-appointed commissioner and principal maker. The household supplies the loom, yarn, lighting, and probably ancillary labor, but the source does not itemize them.

### 2.5 Material inference

Wool would be historically plausible because wool was central to Greek domestic textile production and is often the default Homeric textile material. Linen is also possible within the broader ancient textile economy. Neither can be assigned to this shroud from the text.

“Shroud” should therefore not be expanded into “white linen shroud.” That familiar modern image is partly conditioned by later Mediterranean, Christian, and post-antique burial imagery.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because the object exists only within narrative, “custody” means control of its production and disposition in each source.

### 3.1 Yarn and loom: before narration

No source identifies where the raw fiber originated, who prepared it, who spun the yarn, or when the loom was warped. Penelope says she set up the great web in the royal halls. The object’s first recoverable location is consequently the **palace or household of Odysseus on Ithaca**, specifically its female domestic working space.

### 3.2 Penelope’s exclusive control

For approximately three years within the story, Penelope controls the textile’s physical state:

- Day: she adds woven fabric.
- Night: she reverses that addition.
- Result: the object exists in deliberately unstable form, neither wholly absent nor allowed to become complete.

It is misleading to say that she completely starts over every morning. Homer says she undoes the day’s weaving; the amount remaining at any point is not specified. The shroud may have grown, remained nearly static, or repeatedly returned to an earlier boundary.

### 3.3 Knowledge shared with household women

The ruse cannot remain purely private because one or more female servants know it. The sources vary:

- Antinous: a woman who knew the plan tells the suitors.
- Penelope: her maids enable the discovery.
- Amphimedon: female servants reveal or betray the act.

Later summaries often name **Melantho** as the informer. The transmitted Homeric passages do **not** name her in this episode. Assigning the betrayal specifically to Melantho is a later inference or elaboration based on her hostility to Penelope and relationship with Eurymachus elsewhere in the poem.

### 3.4 Seizure of practical control by the suitors

When the suitors catch Penelope unraveling the cloth at night, they do not formally take title to it. They do, however, terminate her control over its incomplete condition and force her to finish it. This is the crucial custody transition:

| Phase | Effective controller | Object’s state |
|---|---|---|
| Public project announced | Penelope | Planned funerary cloth |
| Three-year deception | Penelope | Woven by day, undone by night |
| Discovery | Suitors collectively | Unweaving interrupted |
| Compelled completion | Penelope under coercion | Finished cloth |
| Washing and display | Penelope | Finished, cleaned, publicly visible |
| After display | Unrecorded | Fate unknown |

### 3.5 Washing and display

In Book 24 Penelope washes and exhibits the finished fabric. Washing is a significant final act: it changes the object from loom-work into a presentable textile. It also ends the secrecy that defined its manufacture.

No recipient is named at the display. The cloth is shown to the suitors, or at least in their presence. There is no delivery to Laertes.

### 3.6 Failure of the announced transfer

The publicly declared chain was:

**Penelope → completed shroud → Laertes’ corpse/funeral**

That transfer never occurs in the poem. Laertes remains alive through the conclusion and is physically rejuvenated by Athena before the final confrontation. Thus the shroud’s stated destination is deferred beyond the narrative.

### 3.7 Final disappearance

After Book 24’s retrospective mention, the textile disappears from the story. No ancient source used here establishes:

- storage in a chest or treasury;
- use at Laertes’ eventual death;
- inheritance by Telemachus;
- destruction by burning;
- ritual dedication;
- theft;
- burial;
- survival into historical Ithaca.

The chain of custody ends immediately after display. Every subsequent physical fate is unknown.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Did the physical object exist?

There are three analytically distinct questions:

1. **Does a cloth exist in the narrative?** Yes. The poem repeatedly treats it as a material loom-work and, in Book 24, as completed and displayed.
2. **Did a historical Penelope manufacture a historical shroud?** Not established. No independent evidence verifies the historicity of the characters or episode.
3. **Does any surviving textile possess a plausible claim to be that shroud?** No.

The correct classification is therefore **mythic object with early literary attestation**, not “lost archaeological artifact.”

### 4.2 Is the Book 24 cloth the same object?

Within the received narrative, almost certainly: Amphimedon calls it the great web Penelope had woven and undone. Some scholars, however, have questioned whether the repeated shroud narrative sits comfortably within the poem’s compositional history.

The close of the *Odyssey*, from about 23.297 through Book 24, has been disputed since antiquity. Ancient Alexandrian scholars reportedly treated a point in Book 23 as the poem’s “end” or completion; modern Analysts have regarded portions of Book 24 as later addition, while Unitarians defend its structural integration. [Cambridge, “The Odyssey”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/new-surveys-in-the-classics/article/iii-the-odyssey1/A76FD52966DBD2DD97FA2B6553F8014F)

This is a dispute about textual composition, not evidence for a second shroud.

### 4.3 Which of the three accounts is authoritative?

No account is a neutral inventory:

- Antinous seeks to condemn Penelope.
- Penelope explains herself and shifts blame toward disloyal servants.
- Amphimedon constructs a version of events favorable to the slaughtered suitors.

Their substantial verbal agreement establishes the episode within the poem; their differences reveal focalization and retrospective reshaping.

Lowenstam argues that the repetition’s changing perspectives are central to the shroud’s significance rather than accidental duplication. The competing analytical tradition attempted to identify one version as original and the others as derivative. [Lowenstam](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/298877644_The_shroud_of_Laertes_and_Penelope%27s_guile_Homer_%27Odyssey%27)

### 4.4 Was it genuinely a funeral shroud?

Within the public explanation, yes: Penelope explicitly says it is for Laertes’ funeral. Within her hidden intention, it is also an instrument for postponing marriage. Scholars have accordingly described it as:

- a burial textile;
- a pseudo-funerary project;
- a temporary substitute for declaring Odysseus legally or socially dead;
- an anti-wedding textile;
- a paradoxical wedding garment whose completion precipitates reunion with Odysseus;
- a metapoetic image for making and unmaking narrative.

These interpretations are compatible with its literal narrated function but are not additional physical identities.

### 4.5 Whallon’s proposed identity with Odysseus’ robe

William Whallon suggested that the **φᾶρος** in which Odysseus appears after reassuming his identity at *Odyssey* 23.155 could be Penelope’s completed fabric. This produces an elegant transformation: a shroud designed for the father becomes a garment for the restored son/husband.

The proposal lacks an explicit narrative statement. The common noun *phâros* alone cannot establish identity. It remains a speculative literary interpretation, not a custody link.

### 4.6 Is the Chiusi textile an authentic image of the shroud?

The skyphos is an authentic fifth-century BCE artifact, but its depicted textile cannot authenticate the narrated object’s appearance. Its authority has changed over time:

- Archaeologists authenticate the vase as ancient Attic pottery.
- Connoisseurial attribution assigns it to the “Penelope Painter.”
- Iconographers identify the seated woman, loom, and youth as Penelope and Telemachus.
- Literary interpretation associates the woven band with Laertes’ shroud.

Only the first proposition concerns the vase’s material authenticity. The final proposition is an interpretation of its imagery.

### 4.7 No scientific examination of the shroud

No radiocarbon dating, microscopy, dye analysis, isotope study, DNA analysis, or conservation assessment has been conducted because no candidate textile exists. Scientific work on Greek funerary textiles and loom technology supplies context only.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Intended funerary function

Penelope announces that the textile is for Laertes’ funeral when “deadly fate” overtakes him. In Homeric funerals, cloth can dress, cover, or wrap the dead and participate in the body’s display and cremation. Hector’s body, for example, receives cloth coverings in the *Iliad*. Historical Greek evidence likewise connects textiles with the laying out of the corpse, funerary display, burial, and cremation.

Laertes’ shroud therefore belongs to an intelligible funerary economy, even though its exact intended placement cannot be reconstructed.

### 6.2 Protection of household honor

Penelope fears that an Achaean woman might say:

> He acquired much property, yet lay without a cloth.

The textile is thus reputational capital. It demonstrates:

- the wealth of Laertes’ household;
- Penelope’s competence as daughter-in-law and household manager;
- performance of elite feminine labor;
- fulfillment of obligations toward the dead.

The public credibility of the project depends on these expectations.

### 6.3 Marriage-regulating instrument

The shroud performs no magical act. Its power is contractual and rhetorical: the suitors accept completion as the trigger for remarriage. Its unfinished state suspends Penelope between marriage to Odysseus and a new marriage.

The project therefore regulates:

- access to Penelope;
- succession in Odysseus’ household;
- consumption of Telemachus’ patrimony;
- the timing of political and domestic transfer.

### 6.4 Temporal function

The woven object is a material clock that Penelope repeatedly reverses. Day advances the marriage timetable; night moves it backward. After discovery, time resumes its forward direction.

This temporal reading is especially strong because the shroud links three deaths:

- Laertes’ anticipated death;
- Odysseus’ presumed death;
- the suitors’ approaching deaths.

Completion does not lead to Laertes’ funeral. It is followed by Odysseus’ return and the suitors’ destruction.

### 6.5 Symbol of cunning

Antinous condemns the act as deceit while simultaneously crediting Penelope with exceptional intelligence. Her skill joins textile craft to **mêtis**, adaptive cunning—the quality most famously associated with Odysseus.

The cloth thus materializes spousal likeness: both husband and wife survive through concealment, delayed disclosure, and control of signs.

### 6.6 Metapoetic function

Ancient Greek and Latin repeatedly connect weaving with verbal composition: Latin *textus* and “text” derive from weaving vocabulary. Modern Homeric scholarship accordingly reads Penelope’s work as a figure for epic composition:

- weaving creates ordered pattern;
- unweaving interrupts or revises it;
- repetition preserves a narrative while altering its perspective;
- completion fixes a previously unstable form;
- display makes the work public.

Bottino develops the phâros as a figure for the “fabric” of epic itself. This is scholarly interpretation, not an explicitly stated Homeric ritual. [Bottino](https://chs.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/The-Pharos-of-Laertes-1.pdf)

### 6.7 No attested cult

There is no evidence that the shroud was:

- worshiped;
- carried in procession;
- dedicated in an Ithacan sanctuary;
- used for healing or divination;
- treated as a relic;
- subject to priestly access restrictions;
- believed to confer magical protection.

Comparisons with Athena’s peplos or other sacred textiles are interpretive analogies, not evidence of a cult of Laertes’ shroud.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The canonical ruse

**Tier: early attested myth, Homeric**

Penelope promises not to remarry until she completes Laertes’ funeral cloth. By day she weaves it; by night she undoes the work beside torches. The deception lasts three years. During the fourth year a servant or servants expose it. The suitors catch her and compel completion.

This is the stable core shared by all three Homeric tellings.

### 7.2 Divine inspiration

**Tier: Homeric variant confined to Penelope’s telling**

Penelope says a daímōn put the stratagem in her mind. The source does not name Athena. Because Athena patronizes weaving and cunning and is elsewhere said to have given Penelope skill, later readers often identify the inspiring divinity with Athena. That identification is plausible but unconfirmed.

### 7.3 The treacherous maid

**Tier: early unnamed agent; later named elaboration**

Homer provides an unnamed informer or a group of maids. Later retellings commonly make Melantho the individual traitor. Homer establishes Melantho as disloyal elsewhere but does not attach her name to discovery of the loom trick.

### 7.4 The cloth that never ends

**Tier: post-antique proverbial transformation**

The popular version often says that Penelope’s web was “never-ending.” Strictly, it did end: the trick lasted three years and the cloth was forcibly completed in the fourth. “Never-ending” describes its intended delaying function and later proverbial meaning, not its narrative fate.

### 7.5 The shroud as survival magic

**Tier: symbolic modern retelling**

Modern retellings sometimes imply that by refusing to complete the shroud Penelope keeps Laertes, Odysseus, or the household alive. Homer does not give the cloth magical life-preserving power. The association arises from structural symbolism: finishing funerary fabric signifies accepting death, while unweaving reverses that acceptance.

### 7.6 The shroud transformed into Odysseus’ robe

**Tier: modern scholarly hypothesis**

The suggestion that the finished phâros becomes Odysseus’ robe in Book 23 is unattested in the narrative. It is a literary identity hypothesis based primarily on shared terminology and thematic economy.

### 7.7 Folktale analogues

Scholars have compared the episode with folktales in which a woman delays an unwanted marriage or lover by imposing an indefinitely extendable task. Such parallels may preserve a widespread narrative type. They do not demonstrate that the Homeric story was copied from any one recoverable folktale.

### 7.8 The shroud as a wedding garment

**Tier: modern interpretive development**

Because completion immediately precedes the contest of the bow, Odysseus’ return, slaughter of the suitors, and reunion, the funeral cloth can be read as an inverted bridal textile. It marks movement from suspended widowhood back to marriage. This is symbolically compelling but is not a change of stated purpose within the story.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Legendary object

**Status:** no physical survival established.  
**Location:** none.  
**Custodian:** none.  
**Condition:** not applicable; its last narrated state is completed, washed, and displayed.  
**Legal status:** no ownership or restitution dispute.  
**Access:** through the textual tradition and representations.

No serious archaeological hypothesis currently locates the cloth. Any claim that it lies in an Ithacan tomb, palace deposit, or Mediterranean collection would require evidence not presently available.

### 8.2 Principal real object standing in for it

The most important material surrogate is:

**Attic red-figure skyphos attributed to the Penelope Painter**

- Date: approximately 460–430 BCE; museum presentation gives about 460–450 BCE.
- Manufacture: Attica.
- Findspot: La Badiola near Chiusi, Italy.
- Present institution: Museo Archeologico Nazionale, Chiusi.
- Inventory: 62705; formerly C 1831.
- Medium: painted ceramic.
- Relevance: earliest major surviving depiction of Penelope seated before a loom bearing a figured textile.
- Authenticity: ancient vase secure; identification of the textile as Laertes’ shroud highly plausible but interpretive.
- Lost feature: painted character names are no longer readable.
- Public access: museum display conditions should be checked directly before travel.

The University at Buffalo maintains a study image and catalogue entry. [University at Buffalo Digital Collections](https://digital.lib.buffalo.edu/items/show/35781)

A separate British Museum skyphos attributed to the Penelope Painter, registration **1867,0508.1118**, is useful for the painter’s corpus but does not possess the defining Penelope-and-loom scene. [British Museum collection record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/G_1867-0508-1118)

### 8.3 Modern experimental reconstruction

The Chiusi loom image has generated practical reconstruction work. Ellen Harlizius-Klück reconstructed the textile’s starting border in connection with the “Weaving Codes—Coding Weaves” project. Such work tests whether the pictured design can be produced on an ancient-style loom; it does not reproduce an archaeologically preserved shroud.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Proverb and political metaphor

The object’s name has detached from Laertes and become a widespread metaphor for work continually undone.

Treccani defines **la tela di Penelope** figuratively as a task or situation that is prolonged excessively or never reaches completion. [Treccani](https://www.treccani.it/vocabolario/tela1/)

The metaphor can be used in two different ways:

- neutral or pessimistic: an endless, repeatedly restarted undertaking;
- accusatory: a process in which one actor undoes progress made by another.

The proverb erases several original details: Laertes, the funerary purpose, coercive remarriage, the three-year limit, and the cloth’s eventual completion.

### 9.2 Ovid

In *Heroides* 1.9–10, probably composed in the late first century BCE, Ovid’s fictional Penelope says that the hanging web tires her “widowed hands” as she tries to deceive or pass the long night. The allusion condenses the entire Homeric strategy into two lines and shifts emphasis toward loneliness and elegiac suspension:

> *nec mihi quaerenti spatiosam fallere noctem / lassaret viduas pendula tela manus.*

Michael Hendry’s edition records the textual variants. [Ovid, *Heroides* 1](https://www.curculio.org/Ovid/h01.html)

Ovid does not name Laertes or describe a completed shroud in the passage.

### 9.3 Propertius

Propertius, *Elegies* 2.9.5–6, characterizes Penelope as undoing by nocturnal guile what she had woven by day. The formulation converts the shroud into a compact emblem of marital fidelity accomplished through deception. A modern commentary notes that Propertius deliberately condenses the Homeric formula and gives the faithful Penelope language suggestive of secret intrigue. [Durham commentary on Propertius 2.1–12](https://etheses.dur.ac.uk/14366/1/Donnelly_Peter_PhD_Thesis._A_Commentary_on_Select_Poems_of_Propertius_2.1-12_%28corrections_made%29.pdf?DDD3+=)

### 9.4 Renaissance and early-modern imagery

Illustrated manuscripts and printed editions of Ovid and Homer increasingly made the loom a primary identifier of Penelope. Artists supplied:

- horizontal or vertical looms;
- colored yarn;
- servants assisting or watching;
- suitors approaching the workroom;
- lamps emphasizing secrecy;
- ornamental borders not specified by ancient texts.

These are reception histories, not evidence for the original object’s form.

### 9.5 Angelica Kauffman

Kauffman painted *Penelope at Her Loom* in Rome in 1764. The painting presents an introspective, isolated Penelope, with the loom functioning as attribute and emotional architecture. Brighton & Hove Museums describes it as Kauffman’s first single-figure treatment of Penelope and an important early history painting. [Brighton & Hove Museums](https://brightonmuseums.org.uk/discovery/history-stories/penelope-goes-on-a-journey-of-her-own-angelica-kauffmans-masterpieces-in-brighton-hove-museums-collection/)

### 9.6 Joseph Wright of Derby

Wright’s *Penelope Unravelling Her Web by Lamp Light* relocates attention from public weaving to secret nighttime destruction. Artificial illumination becomes narratively essential: Homer explicitly mentions torches, while the modern painting turns that detail into the principal dramatic effect.

### 9.7 Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century painting

Victorian and Edwardian artists frequently turned the episode into an image of fidelity, domestic labor, patience, or resistance:

- John Roddam Spencer Stanhope, *Penelope*;
- John William Waterhouse, *Penelope and the Suitors* (1912);
- numerous academic paintings, prints, book illustrations, and decorative cycles.

Waterhouse and related artists often depict the textile as already extensive and ornate. Such scale and decoration are modern artistic choices.

### 9.8 Tapestry cycles

Early-modern and later tapestry cycles devoted to Ulysses sometimes represent Penelope at the loom as one episode within the hero’s homecoming. Hardwick Hall’s Ulysses tapestries include a loom scene identified as the weaving of Laertes’ shroud. [National Trust Collections](https://www.nationaltrustcollections.org.uk/object/1129448.8)

These works create a productive doubling: a tapestry depicts the manufacture of another textile.

### 9.9 Modern literature

The web has become a major feminist and metapoetic image. Important tendencies include:

- Penelope as an author who constructs and revises her own narrative;
- unweaving as resistance to patriarchal closure;
- the maids as suppressed collaborators or casualties;
- textile production as a model for memory and textuality.

Margaret Atwood’s *The Penelopiad* (2005) retells the homecoming through Penelope and the twelve hanged maids. The shroud belongs to a wider reconsideration of whose labor and testimony sustain the canonical story.

### 9.10 Commercial and experiential use

Modern artists and textile practitioners have created objects titled *Laertes’ Shroud* or *Penelope’s Web*, sometimes inviting participants to unravel them. These are acknowledged contemporary artworks, not relic claimants.

The name is also used for:

- textile collections;
- installations;
- theatrical productions;
- music;
- literary journals;
- software and research projects organized around iteration or reversible work.

Such usages commonly retain the idea of recursive labor while omitting the funerary object.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Analytical versus Unitarian readings

Earlier Homeric Analysts treated repetition and discrepancy as evidence of multiple compositional layers. Their questions included:

- Which of the three shroud accounts was earliest?
- Were the others interpolations or adaptations?
- Does Book 24 preserve a separate version?
- Did the tale originate as an independent folktale?

Unitarian and narratological approaches instead ask why the poem repeats the same episode through three interested speakers. On that reading, variation is characterization:

- Antinous blames Penelope.
- Penelope emphasizes divine prompting, coercion, and servants’ betrayal.
- Amphimedon reduces the suitors’ culpability and magnifies conspiracy against them.

### 10.2 Lowenstam’s object-centered interpretation

Lowenstam’s 2000 study redirected attention from generic “guile” to the particular object and process: Penelope is making a **shroud**, and the making is repeatedly reversed.

His principal thematic associations include:

- weaving as symbolically burying the absent Odysseus;
- unweaving as reviving or refusing to bury him;
- delaying Laertes’ symbolic death;
- extending the suitors’ lives until the process is interrupted;
- linking Penelope’s alternating movement with Odysseus’ circuitous return;
- making the cloth a structure for the poem’s own temporal design.

These are interpretations, not claims of ancient ritual doctrine.

### 10.3 The three years and fourth year

The formula “for three years, but when the fourth came” belongs to a broader epic pattern in which a condition persists through three units and breaks in the fourth. It should not be casually converted into a modern day-by-day production calendar.

The chronology also corrects the popular claim that Penelope wove the shroud throughout Odysseus’ entire twenty-year absence. The ruse occupies approximately three years and ends in the fourth, near the poem’s present action.

### 10.4 Feasibility of unraveling

Modern practical discussion has asked how Penelope could remove the weft without immediate detection. A warp-weighted loom with completed fabric rolled or positioned above the working edge offers a plausible mechanism. She may have removed recent weft rather than dismantling the entire cloth.

The poem’s silence about daily inspection, accumulated fabric, servants’ schedules, and loom-room access prevents exact reconstruction.

### 10.5 What did the pictured Chiusi textile represent?

Major positions include:

1. **Laertes’ shroud:** the standard interpretation, because Penelope, Telemachus, and the loom directly evoke the episode.
2. **A generic emblem of female weaving:** possible but unnecessarily weak given the mythic figures.
3. **A version derived from lost epic or cyclic traditions:** supported by divergences from the transmitted *Odyssey*.
4. **A scene influenced by Sophocles’ lost *Niptra* or another drama:** proposed because the reverse face’s recognition scene differs from Homer.
5. **A reflection of lost monumental painting:** supported by composition, shadow effects, and classical visual conventions.

No preserved source identifies the exact model used by the painter.

### 10.6 Was the cloth patterned?

Homer does not say. The Chiusi skyphos shows a patterned band. Older scholarship sometimes called its figures embroidery because of doubts about producing them on the pictured loom. Experimental and comparative textile research has made figured weaving more plausible, while also emphasizing how schematic vase images are.

The Munich Museum für Abgüsse notes both Hugo Blümner’s earlier embroidery interpretation and the rarity of embroidery among ancient textile finds. [Museum für Abgüsse Klassischer Bildwerke München](https://abgussmuseum.de/de/infoblaetter/penelopes-webstuhl)

### 10.7 Oriental textile imagery

Margaret Miller and subsequent scholars have connected the rich patterned band on the Chiusi vase with Greek perceptions of elaborate Near Eastern textiles. Bottino cautions that this tells us about fifth-century Athenian visualization, not necessarily the textile imagined by the archaic poet.

### 10.8 Legal-death interpretation

Recent scholarship has placed the shroud within uncertainty about Odysseus’ legal and social status. The suitors behave as though the absent ruler can be replaced; Penelope neither fully accepts widowhood nor restores ordinary marriage. The shroud materially manages that ambiguity. A recent study of Odysseus’ kingship cites the shroud passages in discussing remarriage and household authority. [“Legal Death and Odysseus’ Kingship”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/8D3E3431C4D4FE85E8529A8D6E3C52B6/S0009838823000873a.pdf/legal-death-and-odysseus-kingship.pdf)

### 10.9 State of the primary evidence

The indispensable evidence remains:

- the three Homeric passages;
- their manuscript and papyrus transmission as represented in modern critical editions;
- ancient Latin allusions, especially Propertius and Ovid;
- the Chiusi skyphos;
- related classical images of Penelope, looms, funerals, and textiles.

No archival inventory or archaeological report documents an actual Laertean shroud.
