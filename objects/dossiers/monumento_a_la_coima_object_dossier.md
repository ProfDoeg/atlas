# The "Monumento a la Coima": Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary finding

The object popularly called the **“Monumento a la Coima”** is not an independently erected monument and not a single freestanding statue. It is a **pair of colossal architectural sculptures permanently incorporated into the principal façade of the former Argentine Ministry of Public Works building in Buenos Aires**. One figure holds a small chest or box; the other turns its open palm behind its body while looking away. Those gestures generated the interpretation that the pair represents the giving and furtive receipt of a bribe.

The sculptures unquestionably exist. Their authorship by the Italian-born sculptor **Troiano Troiani** is now accepted in the Argentine national heritage catalogue and supported by period photographs preserved by the Centro de Documentación e Investigación de la Arquitectura Pública (CeDIAP). What is not documented is the popular story that architect and public official **José A. Hortal** secretly ordered the gestures as revenge against suppliers or officials who demanded bribes during construction.

The essential evidentiary distinction is therefore:

- **Documented physical object:** two monumental Art Deco architectural figures made for and installed on the Ministry of Public Works building during its 1934–1936 construction.
- **Documented attribution:** Troiano Troiani, supported by governmental heritage documentation and archival photographs.
- **Probable original iconographic programme:** monumental allegorical decoration appropriate to a public-works ministry; one later report says the figures were intended as Architecture and Engineering, but its claimed period source is not identified.
- **Legend:** Hortal altered or commissioned the gestures to depict a bribe as a clandestine protest.
- **Modern popular identity:** the pair, or sometimes only the open-handed figure, is called the *Monumento a la Coima*.
- **Unresolved question:** when that name and explanatory story first entered oral circulation. No contemporaneous 1930s source found in this research calls it a monument to bribery.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Object class and identity

**Class:** integral architectural sculpture; paired façade allegories; monumental public art.

**Tracked object:** the two original figures mounted at the outer ends of the monumental entrance composition of the former Ministerio de Obras Públicas de la Nación, today usually called the **Edificio MOP**.

**Popular name:** *Monumento a la Coima*.

**Variants:**

- *Monumento de la Coima*
- *Monumento al Soborno*
- *Monumento a la Corrupción*
- *la estatua de la coima*
- *las estatuas de la coima*

The singular popular title is unstable. Some accounts apply it to the open-palm figure alone; others treat the chest-bearer and open-handed figure together as a two-part tableau. The official heritage description does not use the bribery name, referring instead to **“dos hieráticas figuras”**—two hieratic figures—flanking the main portal. The Buenos Aires municipal tourist guide, by contrast, adopts the popular collective name while qualifying the interpretation with *“dicen que”* (“they say that”), an unusually clear institutional acknowledgment that the title is traditional rather than original. [Argentine heritage catalogue](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/ex-ministerio-de-obras-y-servicios-publicos); [Buenos Aires tourist guide](https://cdn2.buenosaires.gob.ar/turismo/TurismoEnBarrios_BarrioCascoHistoricoCircuito1.pdf)

### Name and etymology

In Argentine Spanish, **coima** means a bribe or payoff; *monumento a la coima* therefore means “monument to the bribe.” The name is ironic, not a documented commission title. No inscription on the sculptures identifies them by it.

The surviving documentary trail suggests three successive naming regimes:

1. **1930s official/architectural record:** decorative monumental figures by Troiani, without a bribery title in the sources located.
2. **Undated oral-professional tradition:** a story among architects, public employees, guides, and Buenos Aires raconteurs that the gestures encode a bribe.
3. **Twenty-first-century public name:** *Monumento a la Coima*, used by newspapers, tourist literature, websites, books, and social media.

A 2014 investigation by *La Nación* reported that neither Hortal’s supposed motive nor the title appeared in official files or in the public-works bulletin examined by specialists Cristina Beatriz Fernández and Carmen Magaz. [*La Nación*, 22 May 2014](https://www.lanacion.com.ar/buenos-aires/monumento-a-la-coima-edificio-evita-nid1690504/)

### Number, disposition, and appearance

The object consists of **two colossal, stylized human figures**, commonly described in modern reports as female. They stand symmetrically at the outer corners of the massive entrance zone facing Avenida 9 de Julio, at approximately second-floor level.

- **Chest-bearing figure:** holds a rectangular chest, coffer, box, or small safe in both hands.
- **Open-palm figure:** keeps an arm close to the body, turns the hand behind or beside the torso with the palm exposed, and directs the face or gaze elsewhere. The combination looks, to modern viewers, like a person discreetly waiting to receive money without openly acknowledging the transaction.

The forms are simplified, geometric, massive, and frontal, combining classical allegory with Art Deco stylization. The national heritage catalogue calls them *hieratic* and attributes both the figures and the portal’s decorative lamps to Troiani. They belong to the building’s monumental entrance rather than to its largely neutral rationalist tower shaft. [National Commission of Monuments catalogue](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/sites/default/files/guia_de_monumentos_caba_-_cnmlybh_parte_1.pdf)

### Material

A 2022 article in *La Nueva* says that a period publication described the figures as made of **“piedra Mar del Plata”** and originally intended to represent Engineering and Architecture. The article does not identify that publication by title, number, or page. The material attribution is consequently **plausible secondary testimony, not independently verified here from a named primary specification**. [*La Nueva*, 26 February 2022](https://www.lanueva.com/elmundo/nota/2022-2-26-6-30-47-el-monumento-a-la-coima-en-un-edificio-emblematico-y-apropiado-del-pais)

“Piedra Mar del Plata” is an Argentine building-stone designation rather than evidence that the figures were monolithic blocks. No accessible conservation report located in this research states whether they are solid carved stone, assembled blocks, cast artificial stone, or stone cladding over an armature. Popular articles calling them simply “stone” should not be treated as technical examinations.

### Dimensions and weight

No authoritative published dimensions or weight for either figure were found. Period and archival photographs showing Troiani beside separate sculptural heads and arms demonstrate that the works are substantially larger than life and were fabricated in large components before installation. *La Nación* reproduced such a CeDIAP photograph in 2023. [*La Nación*, 15 September 2023](https://www.lanacion.com.ar/lifestyle/monumento-a-la-coima-la-historia-de-la-escultura-que-sorprendio-a-tucker-carlson-en-la-entrevista-nid15092023/)

Claims giving exact measurements without a conservation survey should therefore be regarded as unsupported.

### Construction and attachment

The figures appear to have been produced in separate monumental components—at least heads and arms are visible as detached pieces in the CeDIAP photograph—and assembled into the façade composition. Their precise anchoring system has not been published in the accessible sources.

They are architectural fabric, not movable museum objects. Their “support” is the reinforced-concrete MOP building, whose structure and principal construction were undertaken by José Scarpinelli’s firm under engineer Marcelo Martínez de Hoz and architect Alberto Belgrano Blanco, with José Hortal supervising as director general of architecture. [Official heritage record](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/ex-ministerio-de-obras-y-servicios-publicos)

### Inscriptions and signatures

No inscription, title, date, or explanatory legend has been reported on the figures themselves. A modern historical account states that the figures are signed by Troiani, but the exact position, lettering, and condition of the signature are not supplied. [Moderna Buenos Aires](https://www.modernabuenosaires.org/noticias/la-nacion-la-historia-del-edificio-de-obras-publicas-un-gigante-que-cumple-80-anos)

Between or near the sculptural ensemble is the building’s monumental identifying composition, crowned by **“M.O.P.”**, with the names of President Agustín P. Justo, ministers, and officials—including Hortal—carved in the façade’s dolomitic stone. That governmental inscription belongs to the building and should not be mistaken for a title or caption for the sculptures.

### Damage, repair, and condition

By 2010 the building exterior exhibited serious deterioration: exposed and oxidized reinforcing steel, lost concrete cover, missing material, cracked and detached render, damaged balconies and lintels, and falling fragments that required pedestrian-protection scaffolding. The surviving technical specifications quoted in a contemporary architectural report describe the façade generally but do not provide a separate condition assessment of the two figures. [2010–2011 restoration documentation excerpt](https://barriocivicodemendoza.wordpress.com/2011/04/29/puesta-en-valor-del-edificio-del-ex-mop-bs-as/)

A façade-and-terrace restoration began in September 2010. Accessible sources do not specify whether the figures were cleaned, consolidated, repaired, partly recarved, or left untouched, nor do they identify replacement material. It is therefore unsafe to claim either complete material originality or substantial replacement.

As of September 2026, recent photographs and reports continue to show both figures in situ. No loss, removal, fracture, theft, or substitution of either member of the pair has been documented.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Administrative origin of the building

The sculpture’s origin cannot be separated from the creation of the Ministry of Public Works headquarters.

In 1932 José A. Hortal, then director of the national architectural service, proposed using a constrained federal parcel near Plaza Belgrano and Pasaje Aroma for a high-rise headquarters that would gather ministry offices then dispersed among approximately ten buildings. The tower form allowed the state to build on the limited site. [Official heritage record](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/ex-ministerio-de-obras-y-servicios-publicos)

The Argentine Congress sanctioned **Law 11,714 on 20 September 1933**, published in the *Boletín Oficial* on 29 September. It authorized the executive to devote the federal property to construction of the ministry building. This official dating corrects secondary accounts that say the law was approved or promulgated on 26 November. [Official record of Law 11,714](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/normativa/nacional/ley-11714-293717)

A detailed 1937 article in *Revista de Arquitectura* gave the project cost as **3,888,524.19 pesos moneda nacional**, divided into 2,956,280 pesos for the principal contract, 748,067.68 pesos for works outside that contract, and 185,167.89 pesos for contingencies. It also recorded the state’s financial justification: consolidation would replace approximately 240,000 pesos in annual rent. [*Revista de Arquitectura*, no. 199, July 1937](https://es.scribd.com/document/541267497/Revista-de-Arquitectura-Ano-XXIII-NA%C2%BA-199-Julio-1937)

Construction began on **15 November 1934**. The reinforced-concrete structure rose with exceptional speed; contemporary-derived accounts give 138 working days for the structural frame. The completed building was inaugurated in September 1936. It comprised two basements, ground floor, twenty-two upper floors, a roof, and an observation terrace; published heights vary between 93 and 96 metres depending on the measuring point. [*La Nación*, 2023](https://www.lanacion.com.ar/lifestyle/monumento-a-la-coima-la-historia-de-la-escultura-que-sorprendio-a-tucker-carlson-en-la-entrevista-nid15092023/)

### Commission and maker

The current Argentine national heritage catalogue explicitly identifies **Troiano Troiani** as sculptor of the entrance figures and Art Deco lamps. Troiani, born in Buja, Friuli, in 1885 and active in Argentina during the first half of the twentieth century, produced numerous architectural sculptures, medals, portraits, and public monuments. [Official heritage record](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/ex-ministerio-de-obras-y-servicios-publicos)

Period photographs preserved by CeDIAP show Troiani or workshop activity with the figures’ colossal heads and arms before assembly. These images materially strengthen the attribution. They also contradict versions that call Hortal the sculptor: Hortal was the commissioning or supervising architect in the legend, not the executing sculptor.

The exact contract, fee, workshop address, fabrication dates, assistants, stone supplier, and installation date have not been located. No accessible primary document found here says Hortal personally commissioned the figures, though his position made participation plausible.

### Intended subject

Three levels of evidence must be kept apart:

1. **Official heritage description:** two monumental or “hieratic” figures by Troiani; no bribery meaning stated.
2. **Later secondary claim:** a period publication supposedly called them allegories of **Engineering and Architecture** and described their material as Piedra Mar del Plata. The underlying publication remains unidentified.
3. **Popular legend:** Hortal replaced or subverted that programme by asking Troiani to make the pair enact a bribe.

A complication is that Engineering and Architecture were standard allegorical subjects elsewhere in the building. A recent academic study identifies Alfredo Guido panels devoted to those disciplines in the entrance hall. Thus later narrators may have transferred the names of documented interior allegories to Troiani’s exterior figures. Without the alleged period caption or original sculpture schedule, the exterior pair’s official subjects remain unresolved. [Academic study of the building’s art programme](https://repositorio.utdt.edu/bitstreams/22d53aea-a724-4cbc-ba2f-6d11672b6e2b/download)

### Earliest secure attestation

The earliest secure evidence located is the **completed building and photographic architectural record of 1936–1937**, especially the extensive illustrated article “El edificio para el Ministerio de Obras Públicas de la Nación” in *Revista de Arquitectura*, no. 199, July 1937. That article documents the building immediately after completion.

The photographs show that the figures were part of the original completed architectural composition. This rules out claims that they were a much later insertion. But the accessible OCR of the article does not call them *Monumento a la Coima* or explain a bribery allegory.

The earliest securely dated occurrence of the fully developed popular story found in open web sources is a **17 December 2013** post summarizing Daniel Balmaceda’s *Historias insólitas de la historia argentina*. By May 2014, *La Nación* called the story a legend more than seventy years old, but supplied no dated pre-2013 text. [Roberto Patxot, 2013](https://robertopatxot.wordpress.com/2013/12/17/un-monumento-a-la-coima/); [*La Nación*, 2014](https://www.lanacion.com.ar/buenos-aires/monumento-a-la-coima-edificio-evita-nid1690504/)

Accordingly:

- **Physical pair attested:** 1936–1937.
- **Attribution to Troiani:** supported by archival imagery and official heritage documentation.
- **Bribery interpretation:** date of origin unknown.
- **Claim that the interpretation began in 1936:** traditional, not established by a contemporary written source.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because the figures are permanently attached to a federal building, their custody is architectural rather than a sequence of sales and private ownership. No evidence was found that either sculpture ever left the building.

### 1934–1936: fabrication and installation

The Argentine national government commissioned the MOP building. The architectural project was produced within the Dirección General de Arquitectura under Hortal; Alberto Belgrano Blanco was the principal project architect; Marcelo Martínez de Hoz directed the engineering work; and José Scarpinelli’s company was principal contractor. Troiani produced the monumental entrance sculptures.

The fabrication location is undocumented in the sources located. Archival photographs of detached components indicate workshop production followed by assembly at the site. By the September 1936 inauguration, the pair was in place.

### 1936–present: continuous federal ownership

No sale, gift, inheritance, ecclesiastical transfer, export, private possession, museum accession, or deaccession is recorded. Title remained with the Argentine national state as the building passed among administrative occupants.

The pair remained physically anchored to the same façade even as the building’s institutional use changed. Modern sources variously describe occupation by the Ministry of Public Works, the Ministry of Health, the former Ministry of Social Development, and successor agencies. Those changes concern administrative use, not ownership of the sculptural ensemble.

### 1947: avenue reaches the building

The building did not originally stand in the centre of a completed Avenida 9 de Julio. The first section of the avenue opened in 1937 farther north; only in 1947 did demolition and extension bring the road around the MOP tower. This transformed the object’s visual setting. The figures, designed for a façade on a conventional parcel, became details on a solitary tower isolated amid the avenue. [*La Nación*, 2023](https://www.lanacion.com.ar/lifestyle/monumento-a-la-coima-la-historia-de-la-escultura-que-sorprendio-a-tucker-carlson-en-la-entrevista-nid15092023/)

The change matters to the object’s later folklore: millions passed the building, yet the relatively low, corner-mounted figures remained difficult to notice from street level. Their simultaneous visibility and obscurity helped produce their reputation as a “hidden” public message.

### 1955: attack on the building

On 16 June 1955 Argentine naval aircraft bombed and strafed central Buenos Aires in an attempted coup against President Juan Domingo Perón. Secondary architectural accounts state that the MOP building was among the targets attacked after aircraft refuelled. No source found here identifies specific impact damage to Troiani’s figures. Their survival is evident, but whether they sustained or received repairs after the attack is unestablished.

### Late twentieth century: neglect and demolition proposals

The building suffered prolonged deterioration. President Carlos Menem proposed demolition in 1995, reportedly considering it an obstruction to traffic. The proposal was not executed. Since the sculptures form part of the façade, destruction of the building would almost certainly have destroyed, dismantled, or displaced them.

No evidence was found that the figures themselves were separately inventoried or provisionally removed during this period.

### 2002: heritage protection

On 11 April 2002 Congress enacted **Law 25,582**, published on 8 May, declaring the entire former Ministry of Public Works building a **Monumento Histórico Nacional**. The statutory protected object is the building in the block bounded by Avenida Nueve de Julio, Avenida Belgrano, Moreno, and Lima. The law does not name the Troiani figures separately, but as integral façade sculpture they fall within the declared building. [Full text of Law 25,582](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/normativa/nacional/ley-25582-74096/texto)

This changed the practical authority over alteration: the sculptures remained federal property while the national heritage regime and the Comisión Nacional de Monumentos acquired protective standing.

### 2010–2011: restoration and new façade intervention

A major restoration of façades and terraces began in September 2010 under a contract reportedly signed in August. The intervention addressed dangerous façade deterioration. The available technical excerpt does not isolate work on the pair, so the extent of treatment to original fabric cannot be reconstructed.

In 2011 two enormous steel images of Eva Perón, designed by Alejandro Marmo, were installed on the building’s north and south upper façades. They did not replace or physically absorb Troiani’s figures. They did, however, alter the building’s public iconography: the modestly visible 1930s pair became part of a façade dominated by the much larger illuminated Evita portraits.

### 2022: renewed demolition proposal

Buenos Aires legislator Roberto García Moritán presented a proposal on 31 August 2022 to improve circulation by demolishing the MOP building. Contemporary reporting noted that national-historic-monument status was a major legal obstacle. No demolition occurred. [*La Nación*, 2023](https://www.lanacion.com.ar/lifestyle/monumento-a-la-coima-la-historia-de-la-escultura-que-sorprendio-a-tucker-carlson-en-la-entrevista-nid15092023/)

### 2024–2026: national-government review and continued survival

In late 2024 officials in President Javier Milei’s administration discussed the building’s condition and possible demolition, while also considering removal of the Evita installations under a policy against partisan imagery on state property. Cabinet chief Guillermo Francos said demolition was being analysed but did not announce a decision. [*La Nación*, 10 December 2024](https://www.lanacion.com.ar/politica/el-gobierno-evalua-quitar-el-mural-de-eva-peron-del-edificio-de-la-9-de-julio-amparandose-en-un-nid10122024/)

Reports and photographs published in 2025 and August–September 2026 still describe and show the Troiani pair in place. Thus no proposed demolition had been executed by the dossier date. [Baires Secreta, 4 September 2025](https://bairessecreta.com/monumento-coima-corrupcion-buenos-aires/); [Es Nota, 27 August 2026](https://www.esnotadiario.com/sociedad/el-monumento-a-la-coima--la-escultura-que-expone-el-gran-flagelo-argentino_a6a902b0396b74c9ffdd11e35)

### Chain-of-custody summary

| Period | Physical holder/location | Mechanism and evidence |
|---|---|---|
| c. 1934–1936 | Troiani/workshop, then MOP construction site | Commission and fabrication; exact contract and workshop not located |
| September 1936 onward | Argentine national state, MOP building | Installed as original architectural sculpture |
| Later twentieth century | Same building under changing federal ministries | Administrative reassignment; no physical transfer |
| 2002 onward | Same federal building, protected as a National Historic Monument | Law 25,582 |
| 2010–2011 | Same site during façade restoration | Conservation campaign; sculpture-specific interventions unpublished |
| September 2026 | Same original façade | Recent descriptions and photographs confirm continued presence |

There is no known gap in the pair’s **location**, although there are major gaps in the documentation of fabrication and conservation.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is the physical pair original?

The evidence strongly supports continuity from the building’s completion:

- the figures appear in the original completed architectural composition;
- period workshop/construction photography survives;
- they remained integral to the building;
- no removal, destruction, or replacement has been documented.

There is no rival claimant, museum copy, devotional replica, or alternative physical object bearing a competing identity.

### Is “Monumento a la Coima” the original identity?

No. The title is demonstrably popular and unofficial. The national heritage catalogue avoids it, while the city tourist guide records it as a received name.

The central identity dispute is iconographic, not physical:

- **Popular case:** the chest and backward palm form a visually coherent exchange; the averted look suggests concealment; the placement on a public-works ministry is pointed; oral tradition connects Hortal’s alleged frustration to the gestures.
- **Sceptical documentary case:** no located 1930s contract, plan, bulletin, dedication, artist’s statement, or press report calls the group a bribery allegory; researchers quoted in 2014 found no official documentation for Hortal’s motive; the story appears in accessible dated sources only much later.
- **Alternative case:** the figures were ordinary state allegories, possibly Architecture and Engineering, whose ambiguous gestures attracted a retrospective urban legend.

The present evidence supports the formulation: **authentic 1936 sculptures, authentic modern folklore, unproven original anti-corruption intention**.

### Attribution dispute

Older or derivative sources sometimes:

- call Hortal the creator;
- attribute the design to Alberto Belgrano Blanco;
- say Hortal personally altered the original scheme;
- state that Troiani’s participation is merely conjectural.

The current official heritage attribution and archival workshop photograph make Troiani’s execution the strongest-supported conclusion. Hortal and Belgrano Blanco belong to the commissioning architectural structure, not to the object’s carving unless new documents show otherwise.

### Scientific examination

No radiocarbon dating, petrographic analysis, isotopic stone-provenance study, pigment analysis, 3-D survey, structural scan, or published laboratory examination of the figures was found. Their date rests on architectural and photographic documentation, which is sufficient to place them at the building’s completion but does not resolve their exact material composition or repair history.

### Forgery or substitution

No evidence of forgery, replica substitution, clandestine replacement, or false fragments exists. Nor is there evidence that the gestures were recut after installation. Claims that the “real” figures were hidden or replaced appear absent from the documented tradition.

### Authority to authenticate

Authority has moved through several overlapping domains:

- **1930s:** the Dirección General de Arquitectura and Ministry of Public Works controlled commission and acceptance.
- **Professional architectural press:** documented the completed building.
- **CeDIAP:** preserves relevant public-architecture plans and photographs.
- **National Commission of Monuments:** recognizes the building and attributes the façade sculpture to Troiani.
- **Municipal tourism authorities:** recognize the popular name, but not its historical truth.
- **Journalists and historians:** transmit and interrogate the bribery legend without institutional power to authenticate it.

None. The pair has no documented cult, saintly, mythological, or supernatural referent. Its figures are secular architectural allegories.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Original function

The figures’ secure original function was to give monumental and artistic emphasis to the entrance of a national ministry. They softened and humanized the otherwise severe administrative tower and participated in a broader state art programme that included murals, frescoes, lamps, inscriptions, and interior allegories.

They were not designed as objects to be processed, touched, sworn upon, carried, crowned, or venerated.

### Ceremonial setting

The building became the backdrop to major political events, most notably the mass rally of 22 August 1951 associated with Eva Perón’s eventual renunciation of the vice-presidential candidacy. The sculptures were physically present above the site but are not documented as having played any role in the ceremony.

Likewise, the first Argentine television transmission on 17 October 1951 used an antenna on the building’s roof. That event belongs to the building’s history, not to a use of the figures.

### Symbolic transformation

The pair acquired a symbolic function only through reinterpretation. Once read as a bribe, it became:

- a visual shorthand for corruption in public contracting;
- an ironic commentary on the institution whose façade it occupies;
- a recurring illustration in articles about Argentine political corruption;
- a tourist curiosity;
- a rhetorical “national monument” invoked across partisan positions.

The symbolism is especially durable because it does not require the legend to be true. The gestures themselves permit the reading, and the building’s public-works history supplies the context.

### Access and visibility

The sculptures are visible from public space but mounted too high for ordinary physical access. They are best seen from the 9 de Julio/Metrobus area or from an oblique viewpoint across Moreno. No public-touch ritual or restricted-viewing rule exists. Close inspection would require access controlled by the building’s federal custodian.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The revenge-of-Hortal legend

**Full form:** During construction Hortal was repeatedly confronted with demands for money—variously attributed to suppliers, contractors, municipal officials, or national officials—either to speed up deliveries and permits or to prevent delays. Exasperated, he commissioned Troiani to place two unexpected figures on the façade. One holds the money or valuables in a box; the other discreetly extends a hand while looking away. Hortal thereby exposed the bribery surrounding public works without making an overt accusation.

**Status:** later urban legend; no located contemporary confirmation.

**Earliest accessible dated transmission located:** December 2013, in a post citing Daniel Balmaceda; extensively investigated in 2014 by *La Nación*.

**Variants:**

1. Hortal himself designed the figures.
2. Hortal commissioned Troiani to design them.
3. Troiani independently inserted the visual joke.
4. The figures were absent from the original plans.
5. Ordinary allegories of Architecture and Engineering were changed at the last moment.
6. The alleged bribe-seekers were suppliers.
7. The alleged bribe-seekers were officials who controlled permissions or progress.
8. The corruption either slowed the work or demanded payments to accelerate it.

These variants are mutually significant. The legend lacks a stable culprit, transaction, or documentary witness—characteristics typical of oral urban folklore.

### “Not in the plans”

Modern retellings often say the final figures did not appear in the original plans. The 2014 report carefully qualified this as “at least not in the format they finally had.” No comparative plan set was reproduced to establish the claim. It therefore remains an unverified component of the legend.

### “Architecture and Engineering were replaced”

This version says two conventional allegories were originally specified, but Hortal asked Troiani to turn them into a bribery scene. A 2022 report attributes the original programme to an unnamed period publication. Until that source is identified, the statement remains plausible but unverified.

Moreover, documented interior panels already represented Architecture and Engineering, raising the possibility that later memory conflated separate parts of the building’s artistic programme.

### “The only monument to bribery in the world”

Numerous news and tourism accounts call it the world’s only monument to a bribe. No global survey supports the claim, and comparable satirical monuments, artworks about corruption, or unofficially renamed objects may exist elsewhere. This is **promotional folklore**, not an established art-historical fact.

### “It proves corruption occurred during construction”

The gestures do not prove that any bribe was solicited or paid. No named official, payment record, court file, police report, contract dispute, or contemporary accusation tied to the sculpture has been produced. The legend may preserve workplace memory, or it may be retrospective satire. The underlying events remain unestablished.

### Legend’s documented effects

Whatever its origin, the story has materially affected the object:

- it supplied the pair’s dominant modern name;
- brought a previously overlooked architectural detail into guidebooks;
- made the sculptures relevant to reporting about public-works scandals;
- prompted repeated photographic documentation;
- gave preservation advocates and demolition commentators a distinct object to discuss;
- drew international attention in 2023 after Javier Milei mentioned or showed the story during Tucker Carlson’s visit/interview context.

These effects are historical even though the originating anecdote is unverified.

### Condition

Recent images confirm visual survival. A current sculpture-specific condition survey has not been published. The 2010 technical record established serious general façade deterioration, after which restoration was undertaken. The principal documented threat has been demolition of the building, proposed or discussed in 1995, 2022, and 2024. None proceeded. As of **6 September 2026**, the latest located reports still place the pair on the façade. No enacted demolition order or removal programme was found.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Popular-history revival

The object’s modern celebrity is largely a creation of the 2010s. Daniel Balmaceda’s popular-history treatment, the 2013 online summary, *La Nación*’s 2014 investigation, and Luis Gasulla’s 2017 book transformed a specialist or oral story into widely searchable cultural lore.

Infobae’s 2017 article explicitly connected the sculpture to contemporary controversies about political management of public works. [Infobae, 8 March 2017](https://www.infobae.com/politica/2017/03/08/la-historia-del-desconocido-monumento-a-la-coima-unico-en-el-mundo-y-en-plena-9-de-julio/)

### Tourism

The Buenos Aires government incorporated the name into official neighbourhood-tourism literature. Its guide tells visitors that the two statues are known as the *Monumento a la Coima* and “are said” to symbolize the payment of bribes in 1930s Argentina. This institutionalizes the folklore while preserving a verbal caveat. [Buenos Aires tourist guide](https://cdn2.buenosaires.gob.ar/turismo/TurismoEnBarrios_BarrioCascoHistoricoCircuito1.pdf)

Commercial and popular guidebooks reproduce the anecdote, often inviting pedestrians to find the supposedly “hidden” figures. Their concealment is optical, not literal.

### Political use

The pair has been invoked across ideological divisions:

- as proof or emblem of long-standing corruption in state contracting;
- as satire directed at Peronist administrations because the building later housed social-policy agencies and acquired the Evita portraits;
- as satire directed at the conservative government of the 1930s that commissioned the building;
- as an argument for preserving the tower’s layered political history;
- occasionally as something to save separately if the building were demolished.

These uses frequently collapse distinct periods. The Troiani sculptures belong to the Justo-era state of the 1930s; the Evita images date to 2011; modern corruption scandals do not establish the figures’ original meaning.

### International circulation

In 2023 the story received renewed coverage after American broadcaster Tucker Carlson’s encounter with Javier Milei. English- and Portuguese-language retellings then presented the object as an Argentine curiosity. A 2025 Brazilian article described it as public art about *propina*, showing how the local word *coima* was translated into another national vocabulary of bribery. [UOL, 16 March 2025](https://www.uol.com.br/splash/colunas/arte-fora-do-museu/2025/03/16/buenos-aires-nao-tem-so-denuncia-de-propina-tem-tambem-estatua-para-ela.htm)

### Replicas, casts, and commercial objects

No authorized replica, plaster cast, reduced bronze, souvenir edition, museum cast, or commercial reproduction of either figure was located. Photographs, videos, memes, and guidebook images are the principal reproductions.

No modern forged “original model,” alleged fragment, or false Troiani maquette has been reported.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Primary documentary base

The central primary or near-primary bodies of evidence are:

1. **Law 11,714 (1933):** legal authorization for the building.
2. **Ministry and Dirección General de Arquitectura records:** plans, contracts, bulletins, and construction photography; only a portion is readily accessible online.
3. ***Revista de Arquitectura*, no. 199 (July 1937):** extensive contemporary description and photographs of the completed building.
4. **CeDIAP holdings:** drawings, elevations, construction photographs, and photographs of the sculptural components.
5. **Building inscriptions and contractor marks:** physical evidence naming the state and project personnel.
6. **Restoration tender and technical specifications, 2010:** evidence of condition and intervention.
7. **Law 25,582 (2002):** present historic-monument protection.

### Architectural scholarship

Recent architectural scholarship treats the MOP building as a hybrid of rationalist engineering and monumental state architecture. Its plain tower shaft is contrasted with the highly ceremonial portal, Troiani figures, lamps, murals, frescoes, and official inscriptions. The sculptural pair therefore belongs to a deliberate artistic programme, not to an otherwise unornamented technical structure.

Cecilia Durán’s work on Argentine public architecture and specialist press situates the building within a larger 1925–1943 state programme in which art was used to embody national productivity, work, technology, territory, and institutional dignity. This context favors an original allegorical reading, although it does not preclude a satirical secondary gesture. [Cecilia Durán, “Moderna y monumental”](https://sedici.unlp.edu.ar/bitstream/handle/10915/74903/Documento_completo.pdf-PDFA.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1)

### The 2014 documentary challenge

The most important critical intervention is Valeria Vera’s 2014 *La Nación* report. Cristina Beatriz Fernández and Carmen Magaz stated that no precise documentation or official mention confirmed Hortal’s supposed authorship or motive, even in the public-works bulletin. Magaz regarded the idea as artistically possible, not proven. This distinction has often disappeared in later retellings, which convert possibility into fact.

### Unresolved archival questions

A definitive study would require:

- locating the sculpture commission, payment certificates, or contractor sub-files;
- identifying every relevant *Boletín de Obras Públicas* entry;
- examining original elevations to test whether the figures were late additions;
- identifying the unnamed period publication said to call them Architecture and Engineering;
- examining Troiani’s papers, account books, correspondence, photographs, or estate;
- studying Hortal’s correspondence and administrative files for complaints about suppliers or corruption;
- conducting a material and condition survey;
- establishing the earliest printed use of *Monumento a la Coima* through newspaper and oral-history archives.

### Discredited or unsupported simplifications

The following statements circulate widely but exceed the evidence:

- “Hortal sculpted the figures.”  
  **Incorrect attribution:** Troiani was the sculptor.

- “The object was officially commissioned as a monument to bribery.”  
  **Unsupported:** no such title or commission record has been found.

- “Officials definitely demanded bribes during construction.”  
  **Unverified allegation:** no identified transaction or accused official accompanies the story.

- “The sculptures were secretly added after completion.”  
  **Unsupported:** they were part of the building by its original completion; whether their final design departed from early plans remains unproved.

- “It is the only monument to bribery in the world.”  
  **Unverified promotional claim.**

- “It dates from 1934.”  
  **Possible fabrication starting point, not a secure installation date.** Construction began in 1934; the figures were certainly present by the 1936 completion.
