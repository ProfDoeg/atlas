# Excalibur and its scabbard (Morgan le Fay): Object Dossier

**Proposed filename:** `excalibur_and_its_scabbard_morgan_le_fay_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary finding

Excalibur is not a surviving archaeological artifact with a continuous physical provenance. It is a legendary sword whose identity developed across Welsh, Latin, French, and English texts. Its scabbard is a still later literary addition. No extant sword has a demonstrable chain of identity back to any historical Arthur, and no scientific examination can be assigned to “Excalibur itself.”

The fullest reconstructable history is therefore:

1. the textual history of Arthur’s named sword;
2. the changing custody chain within each literary version;
3. the history of actual medieval swords represented as Arthur’s;
4. the modern replicas, alleged discoveries, and visual reconstructions that inherit the name.

The parenthetical association with **Morgan le Fay** requires correction. In the influential Post-Vulgate–Malory tradition, Morgan does not forge or originally bestow Excalibur. Arthur entrusts the sword and scabbard to her; she substitutes counterfeits, passes the genuine pair to Accolon, later steals the recovered scabbard, and throws it into a lake. She is therefore thief, counterfeiter, temporary custodian, and destroyer or concealer of the scabbard—not its original maker or legitimate owner.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Object class and identity

**Class:** legendary royal sword and associated magical scabbard.

**Existence classification:** literary/mythic object. The medieval record establishes the existence of stories and manuscripts, not of an identifiable ancient sword.

**Primary identity problem:** “Excalibur” does not designate one fully consistent object throughout Arthurian literature. Depending on the text, it is:

- Arthur’s already-owned Welsh sword;
- an Avalonian sword mentioned without an acquisition story;
- a sword carried temporarily by Gawain;
- the sword Arthur draws from a stone or anvil;
- a second sword given by a Lady of the Lake after the stone-sword breaks;
- a sword whose cutting ability is supernatural or exemplary;
- one member of a sword-and-scabbard pair whose scabbard is more valuable than the blade.

The widespread modern formula “Excalibur is the sword in the stone” is correct for some medieval texts, especially the Vulgate *Merlin*, but not for the Post-Vulgate and Malory sequence in which the stone-sword and lake-sword are distinct. Malory confusingly calls both swords Excalibur, probably because he combined incompatible sources.

### Names, languages, and etymologies

- **Middle Welsh:** *Caledfwlch*, conventionally pronounced approximately /kalɛd-vulχ/. The usual analysis is *caled*, “hard,” plus *bwlch*, “breach, cleft, notch”: hence “Hard Cleft,” “Hard Breach,” or possibly “hard-cutting.”
- **Latin:** *Caliburnus*, Geoffrey of Monmouth’s form.
- **Old French:** numerous forms including *Caliborc*, *Calibourc*, *Escalibor*, *Escaliborc*, and related spellings.
- **Middle English:** *Excalibur*, *Excaliber*, *Excalibour*, and variants.
- **Middle Cornish:** *Calesvol* or a closely related form in *Beunans Ke*.
- **Breton:** modern and traditional comparanda include *Kaledvoulc’h*.

The leading etymological position derives Geoffrey’s *Caliburnus* and the French forms from Welsh *Caledfwlch*. A relationship with Irish **Caladbolg**, the name of a heroic sword, has repeatedly been proposed, but the direction and exact nature of borrowing remain disputed. Geoffrey’s spelling may also have been reshaped by Medieval Latin *calibs/chalybs*, “steel.” None of these etymologies supports the frequently repeated claim that “Excalibur” simply means “sword.”

The Vulgate *Merlin* supplies a medieval fictional etymology: *Escalibor* is said to be a Hebrew name meaning, in French, something like “cuts iron, steel, and wood.” This is part of the romance, not valid Hebrew philology. Chrétien de Troyes had already described the sword as cutting iron as though it were wood.

### Physical description by source

#### *Culhwch ac Olwen*

The Middle Welsh story names **Caledfwlch** among Arthur’s particularly guarded possessions. Arthur may grant Culhwch almost any request, but specifically withholds his sword, mantle, ship, spear, shield, knife, and wife. Later, Arthur’s warrior Llenlleawg the Irishman uses Caledfwlch to kill Diwrnach and assist in taking his cauldron.

The text supplies no dimensions, weight, material specification, maker, inscription, scabbard, lake origin, or jewel decoration. Any precise visual reconstruction goes beyond this evidence.

#### Geoffrey of Monmouth, *De gestis Britonum/Historia regum Britanniae*

Geoffrey calls it Arthur’s excellent or peerless sword, **Caliburnus**, and says it was made on the island of Avalon: *gladium Caliburnum optimum, in insula Avallonis fabricatum*. The text thus supplies:

- material: implicitly a forged metal sword, but no alloy;
- manufacture: “made/forged” on Avalon;
- quality: excellent or best;
- decoration, dimensions, inscriptions, scabbard: unreported.

Geoffrey places it among Arthur’s martial regalia with his shield Pridwen, helmet, and spear Ron. It is used in battle, including Arthur’s Continental campaign, but has no independent voice or ritual of choosing a king. A modern critical Latin text and translation is available in Michael Reeve and Neil Wright’s edition of Geoffrey’s work.

#### Wace and the French verse tradition

Wace’s *Roman de Brut* adapts Geoffrey into Anglo-Norman French and transmits the name through unstable spellings. The sword remains Avalonian and excellent. Manuscript variation explains why no single medieval spelling can be treated as canonical.

#### Chrétien de Troyes, *Erec et Enide* and *Perceval*

In *Erec et Enide*, composed about 1170, Arthur ceremonially lends his sword to Gawain so that Gawain can gird Erec with it during Erec’s wedding. This is an early instance of the object’s role in conferring chivalric status.

In the unfinished *Perceval ou le Conte du Graal*, probably composed in the 1180s, Gawain bears *Escalibor*, “the best sword there was,” which “cut iron as [it cut] wood.” Chrétien does not explain how Gawain acquired it. This is an important divergent custody tradition: Arthur’s sword can circulate among his knights without being permanently alienated.

#### Vulgate Cycle

The early-thirteenth-century Vulgate *Merlin* associates Excalibur with the royal sword-in-the-stone test. A sword fixed in an anvil or stone appears with an inscription declaring that whoever draws it is the rightful king. Arthur removes it, proving his divinely sanctioned kingship.

In this version, the sword later returned to the lake is the stone-sword. The narrative gives the blade an inscription and a sovereign-selecting function absent from Geoffrey and the early Welsh material.

#### Post-Vulgate *Suite du Merlin*

The Post-Vulgate cycle, composed in the thirteenth century after the Vulgate, separates the objects:

1. Arthur obtains a royal sword through the stone test.
2. That sword breaks in combat with Pellinore.
3. Merlin takes Arthur to a lake.
4. An arm holds aloft a sword.
5. The Lady of the Lake permits Arthur to receive it.
6. The sword is accompanied by a scabbard.
7. Merlin explains that the scabbard is more valuable than the blade because its wearer will not lose blood, however severely wounded.

This is the principal medieval source of the familiar “Lady of the Lake gives Excalibur” narrative and of Morgan’s theft.

#### Malory, *Le Morte Darthur*

Thomas Malory, writing in 1469–70, follows the Post-Vulgate lake episode but had already called the sword drawn from the stone “Excalibur.” His compilation consequently contains two acquisition stories under one name.

Malory’s lake-sword is held above the water by an arm “clothed in white samite.” Arthur rows to it and takes both sword and scabbard. When asked which he prefers, he chooses the sword; Merlin tells him that the scabbard is worth ten swords because its wearer will lose no blood.

No reliable medieval text gives the canonical gold-and-blue, runic, cross-shaped, or jewel-heavy appearance familiar from modern art. Malory says that Morgan commissions substitutes “like” the originals, implying that the real pair had recognizable forms, but does not provide measurements or technical construction details.

### The scabbard

The scabbard has no independent proper name in the principal texts. Its defining properties are:

- it belongs with the lake-given Excalibur;
- while worn, it prevents loss of blood;
- it does not necessarily prevent wounds;
- it can be removed, stolen, counterfeited, and discarded;
- once separated from Arthur, it is never securely recovered in Malory.

Its material, core construction, leather covering, suspension system, dimensions, color, decoration, and maker are unspecified. Modern images that make it gold, jeweled, inscribed, or heraldic are reconstructions or inventions.

### What survives

No blade, hilt, scabbard, fragment, casting mould, medieval inventory description, or reliquary can be securely identified as the object described in the romances. What survives physically is its documentary record:

- manuscript copies of *Culhwch ac Olwen*;
- more than two hundred manuscripts of Geoffrey’s history;
- manuscripts of French Arthurian romances;
- the Winchester manuscript of Malory;
- Caxton’s 1485 printed edition;
- medieval illustrations, including a French manuscript image of Giflet returning the sword to the lake.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Earliest attestation problem

Two different “earliest” dates must be distinguished:

- **probable composition date of a story**, reconstructed linguistically;
- **date of the oldest surviving manuscript that physically contains the name**.

### *Culhwch ac Olwen*

*Caledfwlch* occurs in *Culhwch ac Olwen*. The story was long dated to approximately 1100, but Simon Rodway’s linguistic reassessment argued for composition in the second half of the twelfth century. The dating remains debated.

The earliest surviving witness is the incomplete copy in the **White Book of Rhydderch**, Peniarth MS 4, copied in the mid-fourteenth century, probably at Strata Florida. A later fuller witness appears in the **Red Book of Hergest**, Jesus College MS 111, copied between 1382 and about 1400. The National Library of Wales identifies the White Book as the earliest compendium of Welsh prose and Peniarth MS 4 as the earliest surviving copy of *Culhwch and Olwen*. [National Library of Wales](https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/manuscripts/the-middle-ages/white-book-of-rhydderch)

Thus:

- **earliest recoverable literary stratum:** possibly late eleventh or twelfth century;
- **earliest surviving physical witness:** mid-fourteenth century;
- **what it says:** Arthur owns a sword called Caledfwlch and treats it as a possession not casually lent;
- **what it does not say:** Avalon, Lady of the Lake, sword in stone, Morgan, magical scabbard, kingship inscription, or final return to water.

### Geoffrey of Monmouth

Geoffrey completed an early form of his Latin history around 1136 and revised it thereafter. His *Caliburnus* is the earliest securely datable appearance of the object in a broadly transmitted authored work. Geoffrey identifies its place of manufacture as Avalon but supplies no smith, commission, cost, or manufacturing process. An early manuscript witness is British Library **Arundel MS 10**. [British Library catalogue](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/040-002039290)

The claim “forged in Avalon” is the earliest origin notice attached to the sword. It is a narrative statement, not a documented workshop provenance. Geoffrey does not say that Morgan forged it. His later *Vita Merlini* associates Morgan with Avalon and healing Arthur, which helped later audiences connect her with the sword’s island of origin, but that is not a manufacturing account.

### Growth of the origin story

- **c. 1136, Geoffrey:** made on Avalon; Arthur already possesses it.
- **mid-twelfth century, Wace:** Avalonian weapon transmitted into French.
- **late twelfth century, Chrétien:** extraordinary cutting sword; Gawain may carry it.
- **early thirteenth century, Vulgate *Merlin*:** royal sword in stone/anvil; inscription identifies the rightful king.
- **later thirteenth century, Post-Vulgate:** a first royal sword breaks; a separate Excalibur and scabbard come from the Lady of the Lake.
- **1469–70, Malory:** preserves both the stone and lake episodes but calls both swords Excalibur.
- **modern retellings:** often collapse the two into a single sword, make the Lady its maker, or give Morgan a manufacturing role not found in Malory.

### Was it forged by a supernatural smith?

No principal early source names a smith. Claims that Excalibur was forged by:

- Morgan le Fay;
- the Lady of the Lake;
- Merlin;
- Wayland/Weland;
- fairies generally;
- elves;
- a smith on another world;

are later reconstructions unless tied to a specified adaptation. Geoffrey only says it was manufactured on Avalon. Avalon’s supernatural character invites inference, but inference is not a named maker.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because the sources are mutually incompatible, a single seamless custody chain would be false. The chains follow.

### A. Welsh chain: *Culhwch ac Olwen*

1. **Arthur possesses Caledfwlch.** Its acquisition is unreported.
2. **Arthur refuses to pledge it to Culhwch** among a short list of reserved possessions.
3. **Llenlleawg the Irishman wields it** during the taking of Diwrnach’s cauldron and kills Diwrnach.
4. **Subsequent fate:** unreported.

Whether Arthur formally lends the sword or Llenlleawg simply uses it as a member of Arthur’s company is not narrated with modern property-law precision.

### B. Geoffrey’s Latin chain

1. **Manufacture:** Avalon.
2. **Custodian:** Arthur.
3. **Use:** Arthur carries it in campaigns in Britain and on the Continent.
4. **Final disposition:** Geoffrey does not report what happens to Caliburnus after Arthur is mortally wounded and taken to Avalon.

There is no scabbard biography and no Morgan theft.

### C. Chrétien’s divergent circulation

1. Arthur possesses the sword.
2. Arthur lends it to Gawain for Erec’s investiture or wedding ceremony.
3. In *Perceval*, Gawain himself carries Escalibor.
4. No explanation reconciles these episodes with Geoffrey or with later lake traditions.

### D. Vulgate chain

1. A sword appears fixed in stone or anvil as a providential kingship test.
2. Arthur draws it and acquires the crown.
3. Arthur retains and uses it.
4. At Arthur’s final battle, the mortally wounded king orders **Giflet/Girflet** to throw it into a lake.
5. Giflet initially conceals his failure and falsely reports a result.
6. On obeying, he sees a hand emerge, seize the weapon, brandish it, and disappear.
7. The sword’s supernatural receiver becomes its final custodian; its later whereabouts are not narrated.

A surviving medieval illustration in BnF Français 112 (3), fol. 229v, depicts Giflet throwing Escalibor into the lake. [Biblissima manuscript record](https://portail.biblissima.fr/en/ark%3A/43093/ifdata604618da030039d56a62730ce872656a42416857)

### E. Post-Vulgate and Malory chain: sword and scabbard

#### Acquisition

1. Arthur’s stone-sword breaks against Pellinore.
2. Merlin leads him to a lake.
3. A hand and arm rise holding a sword.
4. The Lady of the Lake authorizes Arthur to take it in return for a future gift or service.
5. Arthur receives Excalibur and its scabbard.
6. Merlin explains the scabbard’s anti-hemorrhagic power.

#### Entrustment to Morgan

7. Arthur entrusts the sword and scabbard to his sister **Morgan le Fay**, believing her loyal. The act is a bailment or guardianship rather than a transfer of title.
8. Morgan has a counterfeit sword and counterfeit scabbard made.
9. She retains or returns the false pair to Arthur.
10. She sends the genuine sword and scabbard to her lover **Accolon**, whom she intends to use against Arthur.

Malory’s text explicitly distinguishes “counterfeit, brittle and false” replacements from the genuine pair. [Malory, Project Gutenberg text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1251/1251-h/1251-h.htm)

#### Accolon’s custody and the duel

11. A dwarf acting for Morgan delivers Excalibur and the scabbard to Accolon before his enchanted duel with Arthur.
12. Arthur fights with the counterfeit sword. It fails under combat stress.
13. Accolon’s genuine Excalibur cuts as expected; the scabbard prevents him from losing blood.
14. **Nimue/the Damsel of the Lake**, recognizing the treachery, uses enchantment so that Excalibur falls from Accolon’s hand.
15. Arthur recovers Excalibur.
16. Arthur removes the scabbard from Accolon. Accolon’s accumulated wounds then bleed.
17. Accolon dies after confessing Morgan’s plot.
18. Arthur sends Accolon’s body to Morgan with the message that he has regained both sword and scabbard.

#### Morgan’s second theft and destruction of the scabbard

19. Morgan learns of the plot’s failure.
20. While Arthur sleeps, she attempts to steal Excalibur, but he grips it.
21. She takes the scabbard instead.
22. Arthur pursues her.
23. To prevent recovery, Morgan throws the scabbard into a deep lake.
24. It sinks; no hand receives it, and Malory provides no subsequent recovery.

This is the scabbard’s last securely narrated location in Malory. “Destroyed” is possible but not stated. The correct status is **lost or deliberately discarded in a lake within the legend**.

#### Final disposition of the sword

25. Arthur continues to possess Excalibur after losing the scabbard.
26. After the final battle at Salisbury/Camlann, the mortally wounded Arthur orders **Bedivere** to throw it into nearby water.
27. Bedivere twice hides it and falsely claims compliance.
28. Arthur tests the reports by asking what Bedivere saw.
29. On the third attempt, Bedivere throws Excalibur.
30. An arm rises, catches it, brandishes it three times, and disappears.
31. Arthur is taken away in a barge accompanied by women including Morgan.
32. No later return of the sword is reported.

Malory substitutes Bedivere for the French tradition’s Giflet. Tennyson and most modern English-language retellings follow Bedivere.

### F. The historical sword called Caliburn

A separate, physically possible object enters the record in 1191. Roger of Howden reports that Richard I gave King Tancred of Sicily “that excellent sword of Arthur” which the Britons called Caliburn. The Latin appears in two related redactions:

> *gladium optimum Arturti … quem Britones vocaverunt Caliburnum*

and

> *gladium illum optimum Arturti … quem Britones Caliburne vocant*

A source anthology translates the episode: Richard gave Tancred “that best of swords which the Britons call Caliburne.” [Crusade sourcebook containing the Howden passage](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/TA_Archer-CrusadeofRichard%20I-1912.pdf)

This establishes that:

- a material sword was presented;
- Richard’s court represented it as Arthur’s Caliburn;
- the gift operated in diplomacy between Richard and Tancred;
- a contemporary or near-contemporary chronicler recorded the identity claim.

It does **not** establish that the sword was ancient, that it came from Glastonbury, that it was Geoffrey’s imagined object, or that Richard himself believed the claim literally. The sword disappears from surviving documentation after its delivery to Tancred. Tancred died in 1194; the Hohenstaufen conquest of Sicily followed. No demonstrable successor object is known.

The theory that this was a sword associated with Geoffrey Plantagenet’s 1127/1128 knighting, possibly attributed to Wayland, is a scholarly reconstruction based on other dynastic sword traditions. It remains unproved.

### Gaps in custody

The literary sword’s custody has no documentary bridge into material history. The 1191 diplomatic sword has:

- no surviving technical description;
- no inventory number;
- no traced Sicilian treasury history;
- no known scabbard;
- no documented link to Morgan, Avalon, Glastonbury, or a lake;
- no securely identified modern descendant.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Does Excalibur exist?

No presently known artifact has an evidence-based claim to be the sword described in the medieval narratives. Because those narratives are internally divergent and set in an undemonstrated Arthurian past, authenticity cannot be tested against a stable original specification.

### The 1191 royal sword

The strongest evidence for a real object once publicly called Caliburn is Roger of Howden’s report. Its authenticity divides into two questions:

- **Was a sword given?** The contemporary diplomatic context makes this credible.
- **Was it Arthur’s?** No independent evidence supports that attribution.

The object may have been:

- an older royal or dynastic sword sincerely treated as a relic;
- a ceremonial sword furnished with an Arthurian attribution;
- a political fiction understood by giver and recipient;
- an ordinary prestigious weapon rhetorically elevated by the chronicler.

No surviving scientific examination is possible because the sword has not been identified.

### Glastonbury confusion

Arthur’s alleged grave was announced at Glastonbury Abbey around 1190–91. Later retellings sometimes connect Richard’s sword with artifacts allegedly recovered there. The chronology is close, but the sources do not establish that the Sicilian Caliburn came from the excavation. This proposed link must remain **unverified**.

### Sword in stone versus lake-sword

This is not a dispute between two physical claimants but an identity conflict created by textual compilation:

- **Vulgate:** the stone-sword is Excalibur.
- **Post-Vulgate:** the stone-sword breaks; lake Excalibur is a second weapon.
- **Malory:** both are called Excalibur without adequate reconciliation.
- **many modern retellings:** collapse them into one, or have the Lady repair and return the broken sword.

No version is the uniquely “original” complete biography. Each is an identifiable stage in the legend’s history.

### Counterfeits inside the legend

Morgan’s substitutes are the earliest important Excalibur forgeries within the narrative. Their purpose is operational deception:

- disarm Arthur;
- make him believe he possesses his normal sword and scabbard;
- give Accolon the effective originals;
- enable regicide and Morgan’s seizure of power.

The counterfeit blade fails because it is brittle and cannot cut as Excalibur does. The counterfeit scabbard lacks the original’s protective virtue. The exposure occurs through combat performance, not visual inspection.

### Modern claimants and misidentifications

Numerous modern attractions and news stories call swords found in rocks, lakes, or rivers “Excalibur.” Such labels normally indicate resemblance to the legend, not a provenance claim. Medieval or later swords embedded in stone—including the sword associated with San Galgano in Tuscany—belong to independent traditions. They are not candidates for Arthur’s sword merely because their setting resembles the sword-in-stone episode.

No museum accession catalogue currently recognizes a weapon as authentic Excalibur. No radiocarbon, metallographic, isotopic, residue, or DNA study has authenticated one.

### Authority to authenticate

Authority has shifted historically:

- **romance narrator and inscription:** identify the rightful sword and king;
- **royal court:** could assert that a dynastic weapon was Arthur’s, as in 1191;
- **monastic institution:** could support Arthurian relic claims;
- **antiquarian and philological scholarship:** compares manuscripts and textual descent;
- **museum archaeology and conservation science:** can date real candidates but cannot demonstrate identity without provenance;
- **popular media and tourism:** regularly bestow the name metaphorically, without scholarly standing.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Legitimation of sovereignty

In the Vulgate and Malory traditions, the stone-sword functions as a juridical and sacred test. An inscription announces that the man able to draw it is rightful king. Arthur’s successful extraction substitutes miraculous proof for disputed genealogy and baronial consent.

This function belongs specifically to the stone-sword version. Geoffrey’s Caliburn legitimates Arthur by excellence and conquest, not by choosing him.

### Chivalric investiture

Chrétien’s *Erec et Enide* gives the sword a ceremonial function when Arthur allows Gawain to use it in girding Erec. Contact with the royal weapon communicates honor and incorporation into an elite chivalric order.

### Martial function

Across the traditions the sword:

- cuts exceptionally well;
- penetrates armor or iron;
- marks Arthur’s royal battlefield presence;
- can be lent to a champion;
- gives Accolon a decisive advantage when Arthur holds the counterfeit.

Its destructive power is not consistently magical. Geoffrey calls it excellent; Chrétien makes it cut iron like wood; Malory demonstrates superiority through the failure of the imitation.

### Scabbard magic

The scabbard’s function is protective rather than offensive. Malory’s formula is that the wearer “shall lose no blood,” however badly wounded. It should not be inflated into complete invulnerability:

- wounds still exist;
- Accolon begins bleeding when deprived of it;
- it does not necessarily prevent pain, impact, capture, or removal;
- its loss prepares the conditions for Arthur’s eventual mortal wounding.

### Taboo and guarded access

In *Culhwch*, Arthur excludes Caledfwlch from the goods Culhwch may demand. In Malory, the catastrophe results from trusting the pair to Morgan. At Arthur’s death, Bedivere’s desire to preserve the valuable sword violates the king’s command; proper disposal requires surrendering material value to an otherworldly claim.

### Political function of the 1191 sword

Richard I’s gift to Tancred occurred during crusading diplomacy and negotiations over alliance and succession. A weapon represented as Arthur’s converted legendary British kingship into diplomatic capital. The gift also resonated with Richard’s designated heir, Arthur of Brittany. The chronicled attribution is therefore politically intelligible even if materially unverifiable.

### Symbolic afterlife

Excalibur became shorthand for:

- rightful sovereignty;
- Britain or England’s heroic antiquity;
- a weapon that recognizes legitimate authority;
- irresistible force;
- a power that must be surrendered rather than inherited;
- national recovery or the return of a once-and-future king.

No historical British coronation rite is documented as using Excalibur itself.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The Avalonian forge

**Tier:** early medieval literary tradition.

Geoffrey says Caliburn was made on Avalon. He does not explain the island’s metallurgy, identify a smith, or narrate delivery. Later storytellers filled those gaps because Avalon had become a place of supernatural women and Arthur’s healing.

### The sword in the stone

**Tier:** thirteenth-century romance elaboration.

A divinely significant sword appears in stone or anvil. Its inscription declares that whoever draws it is king. Arthur succeeds, sometimes repeatedly, while established nobles fail. The episode converts an object into an ordeal of legitimacy.

The stone is not part of the sword’s earliest Welsh or Latin history.

### Gift of the Lady of the Lake

**Tier:** Post-Vulgate elaboration, later canonical through Malory.

After Arthur’s first sword breaks, Merlin takes him to the water. A disembodied arm holds a sword above the surface. Arthur receives it from the Lady, together with the scabbard, in return for a future boon.

Modern retellings often identify the giver as Nimue or Viviane. Malory’s text contains multiple lake-associated women, and their identities should not be automatically collapsed.

### Morgan and Accolon

**Tier:** Post-Vulgate narrative, adapted by Malory.

Morgan exploits Arthur’s trust, commissions functionally useless replicas, and equips Accolon with the genuine objects. The plot makes authenticity physically consequential: the true sword cuts, the false one fails; the true scabbard preserves blood, the false one does not.

Morgan’s objective is Arthur’s death and, in Malory, advancement of Accolon and herself. This episode is the strongest basis for associating her name directly with the scabbard.

### Loss of the scabbard

**Tier:** Post-Vulgate–Malory tradition.

After Accolon’s defeat, Morgan cannot remove the recovered sword from the sleeping Arthur because he holds it tightly. She steals the scabbard and throws it into deep water during pursuit. The act makes Arthur permanently vulnerable.

The scabbard is not returned by the lake-hand at Arthur’s death. That scene concerns the sword alone.

### Return of Excalibur

**Tier:** Vulgate tradition, transformed by Malory.

The dying Arthur commands that the sword be thrown into a lake. Giflet in French tradition, Bedivere in Malory, initially refuses because the object appears too valuable to destroy. When it is finally thrown, an arm catches and brandishes it before submerging.

The episode can be read as restitution to an otherworldly owner, ritual disposal, or narrative closure. The text does not authorize a search for a recoverable weapon at a named modern lake.

### Arthur will return with the sword

**Tier:** broad later Arthurian expectation, not a consistent object-specific prophecy.

The “once and future king” tradition concerns Arthur’s possible survival or return. Some modern versions add that Excalibur waits with him or will reappear when Britain needs it. That precise weapon-centered formula is not established in the earliest sword texts.

### Alleged historical survival

**Tier:** historical attribution, authenticity unresolved.

Richard’s 1191 gift demonstrates that an actual sword could be installed in Arthurian pseudo-provenance. Its disappearance in Sicily subsequently encouraged speculation, but no evidence traces it to a treasury, grave, crusader cache, or modern collection.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Legendary sword

**Status:** no verified physical artifact; final legendary location beneath or beyond an unnamed body of water.

**Controller:** none in law.

**Accession number:** none.

**Condition:** not applicable.

### Legendary scabbard

**Status:** lost in a lake within the Morgan narrative.

**Current location:** unspecified and unrecoverable from the text.

**Condition:** unknown.

### 1191 Caliburn

**Status:** lost after delivery to Tancred.

**Last documented legendary-attribution custody:** Tancred of Sicily, 1191.

**Serious hypotheses:** it may have remained in the Sicilian royal treasury, been transferred after the Hohenstaufen conquest, lost its Arthurian identity, or been destroyed or reused. No hypothesis currently has an identified object attached to it.

### Documentary witnesses

- **White Book of Rhydderch, Peniarth MS 4:** National Library of Wales, Aberystwyth. [Institutional description](https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/manuscripts/the-middle-ages/white-book-of-rhydderch)
- **Red Book of Hergest, Jesus College MS 111:** Bodleian Libraries, Oxford.
- **Geoffrey manuscripts:** widely dispersed; Cambridge maintains a research catalogue, while British Library Arundel MS 10 is one identified witness. [Cambridge Geoffrey manuscript project](https://www.asnc.cam.ac.uk/research/monmouth/)
- **Malory, Winchester manuscript:** British Library Additional MS 59678, 473 plus eight folios, dated approximately 1471–83. It lacks its first and last quires and three internal leaves. [British Library catalogue](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/032-002249253)
- **Caxton edition:** first printed at Westminster, completed 31 July 1485. [ARLIMA bibliographical record](https://www.arlima.net/editions-anciennes/2529.html)
- **Illustrated return scene:** BnF Français 112 (3), fol. 229v, Paris.

The Winchester manuscript was purchased from Winchester College by the British Library on 26 March 1976. Physical consultation is controlled under the Library’s reader-access procedures.

### Current disputes

There is no active legal ownership or repatriation proceeding over Excalibur itself. Disputes concern:

- interpretation of the Welsh name;
- date of *Culhwch ac Olwen*;
- relationship of Caledfwlch to Irish Caladbolg;
- whether Malory knowingly conflated two swords;
- whether Richard’s Caliburn was sincere relic, dynastic regalia, or diplomatic fiction;
- the identity and number of the women called Lady of the Lake.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Literary revival

Alfred Tennyson’s **“Morte d’Arthur”** (1842), later incorporated into *Idylls of the King* as “The Passing of Arthur,” made Bedivere’s reluctant return of the sword central to Victorian Arthurianism. Tennyson’s cycle appeared in stages between 1859 and 1885. Its popularity helped turn the Malorian version into the default English narrative. The full text is preserved by the University of Rochester’s Camelot Project. [Tennyson text](https://d.lib.rochester.edu/camelot/text/tennyson-idylls-of-the-king.html)

Aubrey Beardsley’s illustrations for J. M. Dent’s 1893–94 Malory edition established an influential fin-de-siècle visual language of decorative medievalism. Neither Beardsley nor Tennyson was documenting an artifact.

T. H. White’s *The Sword in the Stone* (1938) and *The Once and Future King* (1958) reinforced the merger of Excalibur with the sovereignty test. Disney’s animated *The Sword in the Stone* (1963) popularized that synthesis internationally.

### Film

Important cinematic transformations include:

- **Robert Bresson, *Lancelot du Lac* (1974):** austere demythologization;
- **Monty Python and the Holy Grail* (1975):** attacks the political logic of sovereignty derived from a lake-distributed sword;
- **John Boorman, *Excalibur* (1981):** makes the weapon a visual and political axis of the entire legend, merges narrative strands, and strongly shaped late-twentieth-century public imagery;
- ***First Knight* (1995):** uses Arthurian regalia with reduced supernatural emphasis;
- ***King Arthur* (2004):** pursues pseudo-historical reconstruction;
- ***King Arthur: Legend of the Sword* (2017):** turns Excalibur into a spectacular superweapon;
- ***Cursed* (2020):** reassigns the “Sword of Power” and Lady-of-the-Lake material to Nimue.

Boorman’s film is especially responsible for modern assumptions that Excalibur is one sword successively drawn, broken, reforged or restored, and returned.

### Opera and music

Arthurian musical works often employ the sword indirectly through sovereignty and Grail symbolism. Wagner’s *Parsifal* does not feature Excalibur; attempts to identify its weapons with Arthur’s sword are comparative, not textual identity. The name has nevertheless become common for albums, bands, compositions, and stage spectacles.

### Comics, games, and fantasy

Excalibur appears as:

- a British superhero team name in Marvel Comics;
- a summonable or collectible weapon in role-playing games;
- the prototype “legendary sword” in fantasy taxonomies;
- a magical weapon in Japanese manga, anime, and games, sometimes divided into Caliburn and Excalibur as separate upgrade stages;
- an Arthurian weapon in *Fate/stay night* and related media, where it becomes an energy-projecting sacred armament.

These are documented adaptations, not survivals of medieval properties.

### Replicas and tourism

Commercial replicas frequently use:

- stainless-steel blades;
- cast zinc-alloy hilts;
- gold-colored fittings;
- faux gemstones;
- invented Latin, runic, or Celtic inscriptions;
- decorative scabbards.

Such features usually derive from a particular film or a generic medieval-fantasy style. They do not reconstruct a source description.

Tintagel’s Arthurian economy demonstrates the material power of the legend. Geoffrey had identified Tintagel as Arthur’s place of conception, and Richard, Earl of Cornwall, built a castle there in the 1230s partly because of that association. English Heritage now presents the archaeological site together with Arthurian interpretation and the modern sculpture *Gallos*. [English Heritage history](https://production.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/tintagel-castle/history-and-legend/)

King Arthur’s Great Halls in Tintagel, opened in the twentieth century, includes a replica anvil-and-sword installation dating from 1927. Historic England explicitly records the replica among the building’s decorative features. [Historic England listing](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1267387)

### Political and commercial metaphor

“Excalibur” has named military operations, weapon systems, vehicles, software, hotels, entertainment venues, and consumer products. The name generally signifies decisive power, Britishness, exclusivity, or authority conferred on a chosen user. These uses preserve the symbolic object while detaching it from the medieval custody narrative.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Major primary-source bases

The indispensable source families are:

1. *Culhwch ac Olwen*, edited from the White and Red Book witnesses;
2. Geoffrey of Monmouth’s *De gestis Britonum/Historia regum Britanniae*;
3. Wace’s *Roman de Brut*;
4. Chrétien de Troyes’s *Erec et Enide* and *Perceval*;
5. the Vulgate *Merlin* and *Mort le roi Artu*;
6. the Post-Vulgate *Suite du Merlin*;
7. Malory’s Winchester manuscript and Caxton print;
8. Roger of Howden’s *Gesta* and *Chronica* for the 1191 material sword.

### Dating *Culhwch*

Earlier scholarship frequently placed the tale around 1100. Simon Rodway argued in 2005, on linguistic grounds, for a date in the second half of the twelfth century. This affects whether Caledfwlch clearly predates Geoffrey’s Caliburnus as surviving composition. The manuscript itself is later than both proposed dates, so the priority question cannot be settled simply by codex dating.

### Etymology

The principal debate is among:

- direct derivation from Welsh *Caledfwlch*;
- a broader Celtic relationship including Irish *Caladbolg*;
- influence of Latin *chalybs/calibs* on Geoffrey’s form;
- hybrid transmission involving scribal reinterpretation.

The fanciful “Hebrew” explanation in the Vulgate is evidence for medieval name-making, not modern etymology.

### Composite identity

Modern scholarship treats the Arthurian corpus as a changing textual network rather than a single canon. “Contradictions” are often signs of source combination:

- Chrétien lets Gawain carry the sword;
- the Vulgate identifies it with the sword in the stone;
- the Post-Vulgate invents a separate lake-sword and scabbard;
- Malory combines both.

The question “Which account is the true Excalibur story?” therefore has no textually responsible single answer.

### Morgan’s role

Earlier mythographic scholarship sometimes treated Morgan as an otherworldly owner reclaiming Avalon’s property. The romance evidence supports a narrower claim: she is explicitly entrusted with the objects, replaces them, equips Accolon, and later disposes of the scabbard. Any theory that she was their original maker or the same individual as the granting Lady requires argument beyond Malory’s text.

### Comparative mythology

Scholars have compared the return of Excalibur with the Ossetian Nart hero Batradz, whose sword must be cast into water after his death. Similarities include the companion’s reluctance and a supernatural disturbance of the water. Possible historical relationship has been debated, but resemblance does not demonstrate borrowing or identify a historical artifact.

### The 1191 relic

Roger of Howden’s notice is central to the study of early Plantagenet Arthurianism. The chief interpretations are:

- genuine royal belief in an inherited Arthurian relic;
- deliberate dynastic theater;
- diplomatic exchange given legendary coloring;
- chronicler’s rhetorical construction.

The report is evidence of a medieval authenticity claim and of a physical transfer, but not of the claim’s truth.

### State of evidence

No conservation report or scientific publication concerns an authenticated Excalibur. The strongest material evidence belongs to manuscripts and printed books, which can be catalogued, dated, and examined. Claims about metallurgy, blade typology, or Romano-British manufacture necessarily reconstruct what a hypothetical historical Arthur might have used; they do not describe the literary object.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and manuscript-facing sources

- *Culhwch ac Olwen*, principally the White Book of Rhydderch and Red Book of Hergest witnesses; modern edition by Rachel Bromwich and D. Simon Evans.
- Geoffrey of Monmouth, *De gestis Britonum/Historia regum Britanniae*, especially the critical edition by Michael D. Reeve with translation by Neil Wright.
- Chrétien de Troyes, *Erec et Enide* and *Perceval ou le Conte du Graal*.
- *Lancelot-Grail/Vulgate Cycle*, especially the *Merlin* and *Mort le roi Artu*.
- Post-Vulgate *Suite du Merlin*, including the Huth manuscript tradition.
- Thomas Malory, *Le Morte Darthur*, Winchester manuscript and Caxton tradition.
- Roger of Howden, *Gesta Henrici II* and *Chronica*, 1191 notices.
- Alfred Tennyson, “Morte d’Arthur” and *Idylls of the King*.

### Source assessment

The National Library of Wales and British Library catalogue records establish the location, date, and physical identity of manuscripts. Critical editions establish textual readings. Roger of Howden is evidence for the 1191 representation and transfer of a sword, not proof of Arthurian authenticity. Later literary, tourism, and heritage sources document the reception of the object rather than its physical survival.
