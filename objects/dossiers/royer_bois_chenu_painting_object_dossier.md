# The Lionel Royer painting, Bois Chenu Basilica: Object Dossier

## Scope note and evidentiary key

The expression “the Lionel Royer painting, Bois Chenu Basilica” does not identify a single securely titled canvas. The French state catalogue identifies an **ensemble of eight monumental paintings**, collectively titled *Vie de Jeanne d’Arc*, by Lionel Royer. Six are marouflaged along the nave and two occupy the transept. This dossier therefore tracks the **eight-part ensemble** while enumerating each component. The frequently reproduced *Sacre de Charles VII à Reims* and *Bataille de Patay* are individual members of that ensemble, not alternative identities for the whole.

Evidence is labeled as follows:

- **Documented:** supported by an official heritage record, archival description, signed or dated inscription, or contemporary record.
- **Institutional account:** asserted by the basilica, diocese, heritage authority, or local interpretive literature but not independently demonstrated by surviving primary documentation available online.
- **Scholarly inference:** a reasoned reconstruction from style, preparatory work, building history, or related commissions.
- **Tradition:** religious, patriotic, or local belief concerning Jeanne d’Arc or the site.
- **Unverified:** repeated in secondary sources but not confirmed in the accessible commission, payment, transport, or conservation records.
- **Incorrect or misleading usage:** terminology that obscures the object’s physical character.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Official identity

**Official French title:** *Ensemble de 8 tableaux : Vie de Jeanne d’Arc*  
**Object type:** eight paintings forming a narrative cycle  
**Artist:** Lionel-Noël Royer, usually styled Lionel Royer (1852–1926)  
**Date:** 1911–1918 according to the signed dates recorded by the French Ministry of Culture  
**Official object record:** Palissy no. **PM88002005**  
**Location:** Basilica of Sainte-Jeanne-d’Arc, also called the Basilica of Bois-Chenu or Bois Chênu, Domrémy-la-Pucelle, Vosges, France  
**Legal status:** property of a diocesan association; classified as historic-monument objects on 19 August 2013, after inscription on 19 June 2006  
**Current placement:** walls of the nave and transept

These particulars come from the French Ministry of Culture’s Palissy record, the controlling public catalogue for the ensemble. That record was created in 2011 and most recently revised on 16 April 2026. It describes the works as oil paint on canvas, marouflaged to the walls, surrounded by fleur-de-lis ornament, signed, dated, and furnished with explanatory legends. It gives normalized dimensions of **670 × 320 cm**. Because the dimensions are entered once for the ensemble rather than separately for all eight components, they should be treated as the standard or approximate format of a monumental panel, not necessarily as eight independently verified identical measurements. No weight is published. [French Ministry of Culture, Palissy PM88002005](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/palissy/PM88002005)

### 1.2 Names and terminology

The following names occur:

- *Vie de Jeanne d’Arc* — the collective official title.
- *Ensemble de huit tableaux* — official object classification.
- *Peintures de Lionel Royer* or *toiles de Lionel Royer* — ordinary French descriptions.
- *Peintures murales* — common on early postcards and in popular descriptions.
- “Murals” or “frescoes” — common English-language captions.

“Fresco” is technically misleading. The official materials entry is **oil on canvas, marouflaged**: the paintings were executed on textile supports and then permanently adhered to an architectural surface. They are therefore movable paintings made architecturally integral, not pigments applied to wet plaster.

“Bois Chenu,” “Bois-Chenu,” and “Bois Chênu” are spelling variants attached to the place and basilica. They are not original titles inscribed by Royer. The building is also called:

- *Basilique Sainte-Jeanne-d’Arc*;
- *Basilique nationale Sainte-Jeanne-d’Arc*;
- *Basilique de Domrémy*;
- *Basilique du Bois-Chenu*.

The ensemble was produced before the church received the canonical title of minor basilica in 1939. Calling it the “Bois-Chenu Basilica cycle” is therefore a retrospective locational name.

### 1.3 The eight component paintings

The official record summarizes the cycle as eight scenes extending “from [Jeanne’s] first communion to the stake” but names only six subjects in its prose. A detailed local account associated with Bernard Mugnier’s history of the basilica supplies the complete eight-part sequence and placement. [Detailed basilica account](https://domremylapucelle.leclosdomremy.fr/basilique.htm)

#### Six canvases in the nave

1. **Jeanne at Bois-Chenu / Jeanne listening to the Voices**  
   Common titles include *Jeanne au Bois-Chenu*, *Jeanne écoutant les Voix*, and descriptions such as “the place where Jeanne hears her voices.”

   The scene incorporates the local landscape, a fountain, the so-called Beau May or Fairy Tree, dancing children, and the seigneurial figures Pierre de Bourlémont and Béatrix. The supernatural call of Jeanne is presented within a localized reconstruction of Domrémy tradition.

2. **Recognition of the Dauphin at Chinon**  
   Common titles include *Jeanne reconnaît le Dauphin à Chinon*, *La reconnaissance du roi à Chinon*, and *Jeanne à Chinon*.

   Jeanne identifies Charles among the courtiers despite his attempt, in the received story, to conceal himself in the crowd. In modern photographs a scroll bearing **DE PAR LE ROY DU CIEL** and a standard inscribed **JESUS / MARIA** are visible. The Dutch and English Corvinus descriptions independently record those inscriptions. [Corvinus visual account](https://corvinus.nl/2022/06/27/domremy-in-the-footsteps-of-joan-of-arc/)

3. **Entry into liberated Orléans**  
   Common titles include *Entrée de Jeanne à Orléans*, *Jeanne accueillie à Orléans*, and *Orléans libéré*.

   The local guide interprets the subject as the triumphant reception of Jeanne on 8 May 1429. This is a narrative condensation rather than a neutral documentary reconstruction.

4. **Battle or victory of Patay**  
   Common titles include *La Bataille de Patay*, *Jeanne d’Arc victorieuse à Patay*, and *Jeanne mène la bataille*.

   Jeanne rides with her standard amid a dense cavalry action. The work is widely reproduced in histories of Jeanne and the Hundred Years’ War and is sometimes incorrectly called a fresco. A reproduction is displayed in the town hall at Patay as part of a modern local historical presentation; it must not be confused with Royer’s original marouflaged canvas at Domrémy. [French Ministry of Culture, 2024 Patay program](https://www.culture.gouv.fr/Media/medias-creation-rapide/cvl_jep_loiret_2023.pdf)

5. **Coronation of Charles VII at Reims**  
   Common titles include *Le Sacre de Charles VII*, *Le Sacre de Reims*, and *Jeanne d’Arc au sacre de Charles VII*.

   Jeanne stands prominently with her standard amid the acclamation surrounding the coronation. The local interpretation associates it with the traditional statement that, because her standard “had been at the labor,” it was just that it should share the honor. The painting’s modern photographic reproductions have become one of the most familiar images of Jeanne at Reims.

6. **The stake at Rouen**  
   Common titles include *Le Bûcher de Rouen*, *Le Supplice de Rouen*, *La Mort de Jeanne*, and *Jeanne d’Arc au bûcher*.

   Jeanne appears at the stake, with English soldiers treated as impassive observers and disturbed spectators and judges behind them. A surviving preparatory drawing described at auction in 2021 bears the title *Le Sentencier, état final, Le bûcher de Rouen 1910–14, Basilique de Domrémy*. This establishes the survival of at least one paper study associated with the composition, although its private custody after the auction is not published. [2021 auction catalogue](https://docs.prod-indb.io/2021/10/01/121756_896446759_c01a5937c9aac585c67bf66bf9e7b904.pdf)

#### Two canvases in the transept

7. **First Communion of Jeanne at Domrémy**  
   Common titles include *Première Communion de Jeanne d’Arc* and *Première communion de Jeanne dans l’église de Domrémy*.

   The official photographic catalogue has a dedicated record, **MHR44_20228800342**, created in 2022. The local account calls the composition imaginary and notes that its church architecture is not a faithful reconstruction of the medieval parish church. Thus, even within basilica interpretation, it is presented as devotional historical painting rather than literal architectural documentation. [French Ministry of Culture photographic record](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/memoire/MHR44_20228800342)

8. **Last Communion of Jeanne at Rouen**  
   Common titles include *Dernière Communion de Jeanne d’Arc*, *La dernière communion dans la prison*, and *La dernière communion de Jeanne*.

   Jeanne receives the Eucharist amid supplicating clerics and mocking or indifferent guards; her heavenly Voices appear above or around the scene. The composition makes sacramental climax, hostility, and supernatural confirmation coexist in one pictorial field.

### 1.4 Construction and surface

The paintings are:

- textile canvases;
- painted in oil;
- adhered to masonry or prepared architectural surfaces by marouflage;
- surrounded by fleur-de-lis framing;
- accompanied by captions or quotations;
- individually signed and dated.

The government record transcribes the signatures and dates only collectively:

> LIONEL ROYER 1911 à 1913 / LIONEL ROYER 1916 / LIONEL ROYER 1918.

It does not map every date to an individual canvas. Any scene-by-scene dating more precise than that must therefore be treated cautiously unless established from direct inspection.

The same record reports inscriptions “concerning the iconography,” meaning the legends beneath the scenes. No accessible official transcription supplies the complete wording of all eight. Modern photographs show texts, but a definitive diplomatic transcription—including capitalization, punctuation, later repainting, and whether the lettering belongs to the original marouflage campaign—has not been published in the sources consulted. The commonly reported inscription beneath the Rouen scene, “Mes voix étaient de Dieu” followed by an invocation of Jesus, should be verified against the object before being quoted as a complete original text.

### 1.5 Condition, repairs, and losses

The 2026 Palissy entry does not record missing portions, replacement canvases, destructive alteration, theft, or an adverse condition finding. Recent official photographs indicate that the eight works remain in situ. That is evidence of survival, not a conservation diagnosis.

No accessible source found for this dossier supplies:

- canvas weave or fiber analysis;
- ground-layer or pigment analysis;
- infrared, ultraviolet, X-radiographic, or multispectral examination;
- adhesive identification;
- stretcher or reverse examination before marouflage;
- treatment reports;
- a mapped condition survey;
- evidence of lining, varnish removal, overpaint removal, consolidation, or remounting;
- separate measurements for every canvas.

Accordingly, claims that the paintings have “never been restored,” are “in original condition,” or are “perfectly preserved” cannot be made.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 The architectural setting before Royer

The paintings were conceived as part of a national-religious memorial rather than acquired for a pre-existing museum collection. The church’s first stone was laid on 3 November 1881 near the site where tradition located Jeanne’s encounter with her Voices. Paul Sédille designed the initial building. After his death in 1900, the Demay family—Georges and then Émile and René—continued the work. The nave existed before the later transept and eastern or western enlargement was completed. [French Ministry of Culture, Mérimée PA88000042](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/merimee/PA88000042)

The building campaign matters directly to the paintings:

- six canvases were designed for the nave’s lateral wall bays;
- two were ultimately placed in the transept;
- the changing architectural plan helps explain why the ensemble was made and installed in phases;
- the transept canvases could not have occupied their present positions until the enlarged church provided those surfaces.

The first church was dedicated to Saint Michael because Jeanne had not yet been beatified. Jeanne was beatified in 1909, canonized in 1920, proclaimed a secondary patron saint of France in 1922, and the completed church was consecrated to her in 1926. The cycle was thus created during the transition from a memorial church connected with her cause to a church formally dedicated to a canonized saint.

### 2.2 Immediate commission

**Documented:** Royer produced and signed the eight canvases between 1911 and 1918.

**Institutional/local account:** The six nave paintings were begun around 1910 and substantially executed between 1910 and 1913. Two further works followed during the First World War, consistent with the official dates of 1916 and 1918.

**Reported but not fully verified:** Modern interpretive material at Patay states that Louis-Maurice Boutet de Monvel had a project for six paintings on Jeanne’s life at Domrémy but, being too ill to continue, entrusted the work to Royer in 1910. This account is plausible: Boutet de Monvel was a major illustrator of Jeanne’s life and died in 1913. Nevertheless, the accessible source is a modern local interpretive program, not the commission contract or correspondence. The mechanism—formal assignment by a committee, personal handover by Boutet de Monvel, or later selection of Royer—remains to be established from archival documents. [Patay interpretive dossier](https://s663cc1bde8267f7a.jimcontent.com/download/version/1734781409/module/19729309625/name/JEP%2016-17%20septembre.pdf)

No accessible source gives the original contract date, price, payment schedule, donor allocation, precise commissioning body, studio address, supplier of canvas, paint manufacturer, marouflage contractor, or transport arrangements.

### 2.3 Royer’s earlier Johannic designs

The cycle did not arise without precedent. In 1893 Royer prepared ten watercolor designs, at one-tenth scale, for a competition to provide windows for Orléans Cathedral. He did not receive or execute that glass commission. In 1897 he gave the ten designs to the Société historique et archéologique du Maine. Later scholarship connects that series with Domrémy’s Johannic imagery.

This earlier body of work is important but must be kept distinct:

- the 1893 watercolors are preparatory or antecedent designs with their own custody history;
- the Domrémy objects are large oil paintings on canvas;
- subject overlap does not make the watercolors reduced replicas of all eight final canvases;
- Royer also designed stained-glass imagery associated with Domrémy, executed with the Lorin workshop, which is another medium and another object group.

The regional heritage inventory documents reproductions of three Royer window designs for Domrémy around 1899 and identifies Charles Lorin’s workshop involvement. [Regional heritage inventory](https://inventaire.patrimoines.laregion.fr/dossier/IM46120486)

### 2.4 Manufacture and installation

The most plausible working sequence is:

1. compositional studies on paper;
2. full-scale or enlarged transfer to canvas in Royer’s working space;
3. oil painting on portable textile supports;
4. transport in rolled or otherwise manageable form;
5. adhesion to the prepared basilica walls;
6. application or completion of the fleur-de-lis architectural framing and captions.

Only stages 3 and 5 are directly secured by the official material description. The rest is standard technical inference for paintings of this scale and should not be mistaken for a documented workshop diary.

The six nave canvases were likely installed after their completion in the early 1910s. A later devotional guide says the canvases were installed in **1913 and 1926**, apparently distinguishing the nave and transept campaigns. This is compatible with the building sequence but remains an institutional claim requiring confirmation from installation accounts. [Modern pilgrimage guide](https://pelerinagesdefrance.fr/IMG/pdf/domremy-1.pdf)

### 2.5 Wartime production

At least part of the cycle was painted during the First World War: the official record includes 1916 and 1918 signatures. Construction at the basilica was interrupted during the war. That interruption may explain the extended chronology, but no source found specifies:

- whether Royer continued in Paris or worked at Domrémy;
- whether finished canvases were stored before installation;
- whether wartime shortages altered materials;
- whether the 1916 and 1918 canvases entered the building immediately;
- whether Royer revised earlier panels after 1913.

These remain open provenance and conservation questions.

### 2.6 The Salon of 1913 problem

The Palissy historical note states that the work earned Royer “the first prize for history painting at the Salon of 1913.” This claim is problematic as worded. Royer had already obtained the second first Grand Prix de Rome in 1882 and had a long Salon career; “first prize of history painting” is not adequately identified by institution, catalogue entry, jury, or award title. No corroborating Salon catalogue or official prize list was located.

The statement should therefore be cited as an **official database assertion requiring verification**, not repeated as established fact. It may conflate exhibition, critical reception, an older academic distinction, or another award.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 Pre-object phase: conception and preparatory lineage

**1893:** Royer made ten one-tenth-scale watercolor designs for the Orléans Cathedral stained-glass competition.

**1897:** He donated those designs to the Société historique et archéologique du Maine. They constitute an antecedent iconographic archive, not the Bois-Chenu paintings themselves.

**Around 1899:** Royer’s designs were used for stained glass at Domrémy in collaboration with Charles Lorin. These windows demonstrate his prior connection to the basilica’s decorative program but are separate objects.

### 3.2 Creation and first custody

**1910–1913, reported:** Work on the six nave compositions.

**1911–1913, documented by signatures:** Dates associated with the early group.

**1913, probable/institutional:** Installation of the nave canvases in their intended wall bays.

From the moment of installation, the paintings were physically attached to the church fabric. Their custody was therefore inseparable from the basilica’s religious administration even though French heritage law subsequently classified them as movable objects.

The original commissioning and owning entity is not fully identified in the online record. The building project involved:

- the Diocese of Saint-Dié;
- Eudist fathers responsible for the sanctuary and pilgrimage organization;
- the Bois-Chenu committee;
- private and national donations.

The exact legal recipient named in Royer’s commission remains unestablished.

### 3.3 First World War phase

**1914–1918:** The building campaign was interrupted.

**1916 and 1918:** Additional Royer signatures documented on the ensemble.

No accessible record establishes where the later canvases were kept between completion and marouflage. If they were made away from Domrémy, the object chain has an undocumented studio-to-sanctuary link. If they were executed or finished in the building, that fact also remains undocumented.

### 3.4 Completion of the setting

**1920 onward:** The Bois-Chenu committee undertook the larger transept-and-apse campaign, effectively doubling the church’s area.

**1926:** The completed church was consecrated to Saint Joan of Arc on 28 August. Modern pilgrimage literature associates 1926 with installation of the later Royer canvases, presumably the two Communion subjects in the transept. This is compatible with their present setting but is not supported in the accessible sources by an installation invoice or dated photograph.

Royer died on 30 June 1926, shortly before the consecration. There is no evidence that ownership passed through his estate: the installed works belonged to or were held for the sanctuary rather than remaining artist’s property.

### 3.5 Later custody

**1939:** The church received the title of minor basilica. This altered the status of the building, not ownership or identity of the canvases.

**Second World War:** No evacuation, seizure, bomb damage, hiding, or removal of the Royer paintings was found in the consulted records. Silence should not be converted into a claim that no precautions were taken.

**1968:** The Eudists removed or stored substantial furnishings from the crypt, including paintings by Alphonse Monchablon and the statue of Notre-Dame des Armées. The local account does not say that the Royer cycle was moved. Its wall-marouflaged construction and upper-church location appear to have left it outside that rearrangement. [Basilica historical account](https://domremylapucelle.leclosdomremy.fr/basilique.htm)

### 3.6 Heritage protection and present legal custody

**19 June 2006:** The ensemble was inscribed as a historic-monument object, according to the later classification record.

**26 June 2007:** The Commission nationale des monuments historiques considered or approved the classification proposal, as referenced in the Palissy observations.

**1 December 2011:** The present Palissy object record was created.

**28 May 2013:** The basilica in its entirety was classified as a monument historique under its architectural record.

**19 August 2013:** The eight paintings were separately classified as historic-monument objects by order no. 054/2013.

This dual protection is significant. The canvases are materially joined to a protected building but retain an individual movable-object record. Their owner is given as an **association diocésaine**. The architectural record lists mixed ownership categories for portions of the broader site, including diocesan-association and departmental property. This does not mean the Department of Vosges owns the eight paintings; the object-specific record controls on that point.

Classification restricts disposal, removal, alteration, and restoration under French heritage law. No deaccession, sale, export, restitution claim, or competing proprietary claim is recorded.

### 3.7 Documented gaps

The chain is unusually stable but incompletely documented at its beginning:

- No accessible commission contract.
- No purchase price.
- No list of donors assigned to individual paintings.
- No studio inventory or delivery receipt.
- No transport or marouflage contractor record.
- No firm scene-by-scene installation dates.
- No published 1914–1918 storage record.
- No published restoration history.

After installation, there is no evidence of sale, inheritance, theft, wartime seizure, or physical relocation. The works appear to have remained at Bois-Chenu continuously, but the absence of removal records is not itself proof of uninterrupted presence.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Is the present ensemble by Lionel Royer?

The attribution is secure to the ordinary standard for modern monumental painting:

- the canvases are signed “LIONEL ROYER”;
- they bear dates consistent with Royer’s lifetime;
- they remain in the building for which they were made;
- they are recognized by the French state heritage inventory;
- preparatory material connected with the Rouen composition survives;
- their style and iconographic relationship to Royer’s earlier Jeanne designs are coherent.

No rival artist attribution, substitution claim, or forgery allegation was found.

### 4.2 Is it one painting or eight?

This is the central identity problem created by modern cataloguing and image captions. The official identity is an **ensemble of eight paintings**. References to “the Lionel Royer painting at Bois Chenu” may point to:

- *Bataille de Patay*;
- *Sacre de Charles VII*;
- *Jeanne at Bois-Chenu*;
- the complete cycle.

A valid identification must name the represented episode or cite the ensemble record. Without that, a singular title is underdetermined.

### 4.3 Six versus eight

Some publications speak of **six** paintings because they count only the nave sequence. The two Communion scenes are in the transept and belong to a later physical or conceptual phase. The official French classification nevertheless treats all eight as one ensemble.

The six/eight discrepancy is therefore not evidence that two paintings are missing or spurious. It reflects:

- placement in two architectural zones;
- different production dates;
- the original six-scene narrative versus its sacramental expansion;
- shorthand guidebook practice.

### 4.4 Paintings versus “frescoes”

Calling the works frescoes creates a false material identity. The present objects are oil-painted canvases marouflaged to walls. A photograph caption or textbook calling *Patay* a “fresco” does not establish another version of the work.

### 4.5 Originals, studies, windows, postcards, and replicas

Several related object classes must be distinguished:

1. **The eight original marouflaged oil paintings** at Domrémy.
2. **The 1893 watercolor designs** formerly entered in the Orléans window competition and donated in 1897 to the Société historique et archéologique du Maine.
3. **Stained-glass windows** made from Royer’s designs with the Lorin workshop.
4. **Preparatory drawings**, including the auctioned *Sentencier* study for the Rouen scene.
5. **Early postcards and photographic reproductions**, some labeled *Lionel Royer, pinxit*.
6. **Modern reproductions**, including the copy of *Jeanne d’Arc victorieuse à Patay* in Patay town hall.

None of the latter objects is the original wall-mounted painting.

### 4.6 Scientific examination

No published scientific authentication campaign was found. There is no need to invoke scientific testing where signatures, continuous location, contemporary context, and official cataloguing already provide strong attribution, but such work would still be valuable for technical chronology and conservation.

Unanswered technical questions include:

- whether all eight use the same canvas and ground;
- whether wartime panels differ materially from the 1911–1913 group;
- whether assistants participated;
- whether the inscriptions and decorative borders were executed by Royer, an architectural painter, or another contractor;
- whether the paintings were modified during installation;
- whether later overpaint affects Royer’s original surface.

### 4.7 Official record inconsistencies

The Palissy record itself contains compressed or questionable information:

- it lists the year field as “1911; 1918,” while the inscription field gives 1911–1913, 1916, and 1918;
- it names only six subjects in the historical summary though it classifies eight paintings;
- it attributes a “first prize” at the 1913 Salon without identifying the prize;
- its single dimensions entry does not explain whether every painting is identical in size.

These inconsistencies do not undermine authenticity, but they warn against treating catalogue prose as a full conservation dossier.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Lionel-Noël Royer

**Connection:** painter, designer, and signatory of the eight canvases, 1911–1918.  
**Evidence:** signatures and French state catalogue.  
**Context:** academic history painter; veteran of the Franco-Prussian War; designer of earlier Johannic imagery. A 2019 museum exhibition described the patriotic and religious dimensions of his history painting and dated his Domrémy decorative work principally to 1910–1913. [Musée de la Guerre de 1870 exhibition release](https://www.museedelaguerre1870.fr/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/CP-Lionel-Royer-lhistoire-mise-en-sc%C3%A8ne.pdf)

### Louis-Maurice Boutet de Monvel

**Connection:** reported original designer or intended artist for a six-painting project, said to have passed the work to Royer in 1910 because of illness.  
**Evidence tier:** modern local institutional account; commission documents not located.  
**Importance:** his well-known Jeanne imagery offers a plausible visual and programmatic background but should not be treated as joint authorship of Royer’s extant canvases without documentary evidence.

### Paul Sédille

**Connection:** architect of the original sanctuary; his wall and bay scheme provided the physical setting later occupied by the nave canvases.  
**Dates:** principal architect from 1881 until his death in 1900.  
**Additional association:** Royer had already contributed designs to the basilica’s stained-glass program during Sédille’s period.

### Georges, Émile, and René Demay

**Connection:** successor architects who continued and completed the basilica and later enlargement. Their decisions determined the transept setting of the two Communion canvases.

The Demay-Sédille archive, Vosges series **157 J**, contains plans, photographs, drawings, tracings, site notebooks, and correspondence for the basilica, dated broadly 1882–1988. It is the most promising surviving archive for resolving commission and installation questions. The fonds is privately derived, purchased by the archives, freely communicable, and supported by a summary inventory. [Description of the Sédille-Demay fonds](https://archives.meurthe-et-moselle.fr/sites/default/files/Contenu/expositions/Architectes/SedilleDemay.htm)

### Diocese of Saint-Dié

**Connection:** institutional sponsor and present ecclesiastical framework; the object record assigns ownership to a diocesan association.

### Eudist Fathers

**Connection:** administrators of construction and pilgrimage at the sanctuary.  
**Evidence:** local basilica history.  
**Custodial role:** institutional keepers rather than personal owners. Their documented 1968 alteration of crypt furnishings demonstrates active curatorial authority within the complex, although no intervention to the Royer cycle is recorded.

### Bois-Chenu committee

**Connection:** fundraising and building body, especially for the post-1920 enlargement.  
**Possible object role:** likely involved in commissioning, paying for, receiving, or installing the paintings; exact legal role unverified in accessible records.

### Société historique et archéologique du Maine

**Connection:** recipient in 1897 of Royer’s ten watercolor designs from the 1893 Orléans competition.  
**Importance:** custodian of the principal antecedent design series.

### Charles Lorin and the Lorin workshop

**Connection:** stained-glass maker associated with Royer’s Domrémy designs.  
**Caution:** Lorin’s documented role concerns windows, not manufacture of the oil paintings.

### François Janvier

**Connection:** named under “sources d’archives” in the Palissy record.  
**Role:** not explained by the online notice. He may have supplied documentation or research used for the classification file; more cannot securely be stated.

### Dominique Guéniot and Bernard Mugnier

**Connection:** authors or editors of the principal modern monographic history cited by the Ministry: *La Basilique Sainte-Jeanne-d’Arc de Domrémy-la-Pucelle* (Langres, 2001), especially pp. 89–95 and 403–415.  
**Role:** historians of the object, not members of its custody chain.

### Jeanne d’Arc

**Connection:** sole narrative protagonist of the ensemble and, after canonization, patron of the church. The paintings represent her childhood piety, divine mission, political recognition, military action, royal legitimation, sacramental life, and martyrdom.

### Charles VII

**Connection:** depicted at Chinon and Reims. His recognition and coronation are the political core of the cycle.

### Saint Michael the Archangel

**Connection:** one of Jeanne’s Voices and the original dedicatee of the church. He belongs to the supernatural iconography of Jeanne’s mission and to the wider decoration of the basilica.

### Saints Catherine and Margaret

**Connection:** Jeanne’s other principal Voices in Catholic tradition and in the basilica’s decorative program. They appear in or are implied by the visionary scenes.

### Jesus and the Virgin Mary

**Connection:** invoked through the **JESUS / MARIA** standard inscription and the Eucharistic paintings. The cycle interprets Jeanne’s action within Catholic sacramental and Christological frameworks.

### Pierre and Béatrix de Bourlémont

**Connection:** represented or identified in the Bois-Chenu scene by local interpretation. Their inclusion anchors the legendary-local landscape in named seigneurial history. Their pictorial presence does not establish that they witnessed Jeanne’s visions.

### English soldiers, clerics, judges, townspeople, and military followers

These collective figures structure the cycle’s moral and political contrasts. Individual identifications should not be made without a keyed study. The compositions combine historical personages, types, and imagined spectators.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Liturgical environment

The paintings are not autonomous museum pictures. They form a monumental devotional cycle inside an active Catholic church. Their functions include:

- visual instruction in Jeanne’s life;
- framing pilgrimage through a narrative sequence;
- connecting national history to sacramental theology;
- surrounding Mass, prayer, and feast observances;
- presenting Jeanne’s military and political actions as consequences of divine vocation;
- preparing the viewer for the sanctuary’s mosaics and high altar.

The two Communion paintings make Eucharistic practice the frame of Jeanne’s life. Her first communion marks formation; her final communion marks death, reconciliation, and martyrdom. Their transept location separates them from, while theologically enclosing, the six-part historical narrative.

### 6.2 Pilgrimage

The sanctuary developed as a pilgrimage site before the cycle was made. By installing paintings below the nave windows at monumental scale, the commissioners transformed wall surfaces into a continuous pilgrimage narrative. Modern diocesan material still instructs visitors to identify episodes in Royer’s canvases, showing that the cycle remains an active catechetical apparatus rather than merely historic decoration. [Diocese of Saint-Dié visitor activity](https://www.catholique88.fr/sites/default/files/diocesesaintdie_sapree_vadrouille_n2.pdf)

### 6.3 National symbolism

The cycle binds together:

- Lorraine as Jeanne’s birthplace;
- France as the kingdom she is represented as rescuing;
- Catholic sacramental legitimacy;
- the coronation of Charles VII;
- military victory over an occupying enemy;
- martyrdom at English hands.

Its production between 1911 and 1918 gave these themes immediate force during the years before and during the First World War. Royer’s own career and the 2019 Loigny exhibition locate his art within post-1870 patriotism and the culture of revanche. This does not prove that every scene was commissioned as wartime propaganda; it does show that contemporary viewers encountered a national-deliverance cycle while northeastern France again faced German invasion.

### 6.4 Legitimation

The Chinon and Reims scenes depict two stages of legitimate rule:

1. Jeanne recognizes the hidden or unacknowledged Dauphin.
2. The Dauphin becomes the consecrated king.

The standard, described as acting “by command of the King of Heaven,” makes heavenly authority validate earthly monarchy. The cycle therefore does more than illustrate biography: it constructs a chain from revelation to military success to sacramental kingship.

### 6.5 Sacred status of the paintings

The canvases are sacred art by location, subject, and use, but no evidence indicates that they are relics, miracle-working images, canonically crowned icons, or objects of a distinct cult. Pilgrims may pray before or among them, yet the primary cultic subject is Saint Joan and the principal ritual focus is the basilica’s altar and liturgy.

No special taboo concerning touching, viewing, veiling, or moving the canvases is published. Historic-monument law and conservation practice now provide the principal restrictions on physical intervention.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

The paintings themselves have not accumulated a separate miracle, curse, theft, survival, or substitution legend. Their legendary content lies chiefly in the episodes they visualize.

### 7.1 The Voices at Bois-Chenu

**Story represented:** Jeanne hears heavenly voices near the Bois-Chenu landscape, traditionally associated with Saint Michael, Saint Catherine, and Saint Margaret.

**Evidentiary status:** Jeanne testified at her trial that she experienced voices and apparitions; their divine identity belongs to her testimony and religious interpretation. The exact placement of a first revelation at the later basilica site is a localized tradition rather than an archaeologically verifiable fact.

**Pictorial elaboration:** Royer supplied visible heavenly beings, landscape, fountain, tree, dancers, and observers. These details should not all be read as elements given together in the earliest trial testimony.

### 7.2 The Fairy Tree and Beau May

The Domrémy traditions concerning an old tree, children’s dances, garlands, a spring, and stories about fairies were discussed during Jeanne’s trials. Later local memory connected these motifs to the Bois-Chenu site. Royer’s painting combines them into a unified historical tableau.

The image therefore preserves a nineteenth- and early twentieth-century synthesis of medieval testimony and local topography. It is evidence for how the story was visualized around 1911, not direct evidence of a fifteenth-century gathering.

### 7.3 Recognition of the disguised Dauphin

**Story represented:** Charles hides among his courtiers at Chinon, but Jeanne recognizes him without having met him.

**Status:** an established element of popular and devotional narrative, derived through medieval accounts but shaped by later retelling. Royer emphasizes miraculous intuition and the contrast between courtly deception and inspired certainty.

The local guide’s phrase “comédie grotesque” is modern interpretation, not an original title or inscription.

### 7.4 Jeanne as battlefield commander at Patay

Royer shows Jeanne charging amid combat with her standard. The historical Jeanne participated in the Loire campaign and Patay victory, but the painting is not a literal eyewitness record of her tactical conduct at the moment shown. It gives pictorial form to the national image of Jeanne as mounted warrior and divinely favored commander.

### 7.5 The standard at the coronation

The statement that Jeanne’s standard deserved honor because it had shared the labor belongs to the received trial tradition. Royer makes the standard visually central, allowing it to signify both military achievement and heavenly mandate.

### 7.6 The Communion scenes

No secure record fixes the appearance of Jeanne’s first communion at Domrémy. The basilica’s own interpretive account calls Royer’s architectural setting imaginary.

The final communion at Rouen has a stronger documentary basis in accounts of Jeanne’s last hours, but Royer adds a fully staged emotional and supernatural composition. Mocking guards, supplicating clerics, apparition of the Voices, and sacramental focus produce devotional meaning rather than a neutral reconstruction.

### 7.7 The martyr’s final declaration

The Rouen picture and its captioned context affirm that Jeanne’s Voices were from God. This is a theological conclusion of the cycle and a cornerstone of her cult. The painting was produced while her sanctity was being formally recognized: after beatification in 1909 and before or around canonization in 1920. Its claim is therefore historically situated within the successful canonization movement.

### 7.8 Modern accretions

Modern internet captions frequently:

- call the works frescoes;
- assign the whole cycle the date 1913;
- describe a single image as “the Lionel Royer painting”;
- reproduce *Patay* as if it were a medieval witness;
- present every architectural and costume detail as historically exact.

These are modern simplifications. The official date range is 1911–1918, the medium is oil on marouflaged canvas, and the cycle combines research, academic convention, devotional invention, and patriotic symbolism.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Present location

The eight original canvases remain in the **Basilique Sainte-Jeanne-d’Arc du Bois-Chenu**, 8 lieu-dit La Basilique, 88630 Domrémy-la-Pucelle, Vosges, France.

Their distribution remains:

- six along the walls of the nave;
- two in the transept.

The official tourism office advertises the monumental Royer paintings as part of the publicly visitable interior. As of its 2026 listing, admission to the basilica is free, with seasonal daily opening hours. Religious services or site operations may alter access. [Plaine des Vosges tourism listing](https://www.tourisme-plainedesvosges.fr/offres/basilique-sainte-jeanne-darc-domremy-la-pucelle-fr-4610079/)

### 8.2 Control and legal regime

**Owner recorded for the ensemble:** an association diocésaine.  
**Religious authority:** Diocese of Saint-Dié.  
**Heritage supervision:** French historic-monument authorities, including the regional conservation service and the Ministry of Culture.

Because the works are classified objects, conservation or displacement requires heritage oversight. They are not known to be for sale, on loan, or subject to a restitution action.

### 8.3 Condition and documentation

The Ministry’s Mémoire database created photographic records in 2022, including the first-Communion panel. Images linked from the ensemble record provide recent official visual documentation. These photographs establish presence but do not replace a condition report.

No public evidence was found of:

- imminent structural danger;
- active detachment from the walls;
- recent vandalism;
- wartime or accidental loss;
- a current restoration campaign;
- a conservation emergency.

Conversely, no public report certifies that the adhesive, canvas, paint layers, and varnish are stable.

### 8.4 Archival witnesses

The Sédille-Demay fonds includes an undated postcard of the “death of Jeanne” painting under **Archives départementales des Vosges, 157 J 214**. This provides an archival image witness to the Rouen panel. An interior view is catalogued as 157 J 208. [FranceArchives search record](https://francearchives.gouv.fr/fr/subject/222544?es_originators=Cabinet+d%27architectes+S%C3%A9dille-Demay&es_publisher=34309&indexentry=222544&page=2&restrict_to_single_etype=False&sort=-sortdate)

The date of postcard production is not supplied in the online notice. It cannot by itself establish the earliest installation date.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Postcards and photographic circulation

The cycle entered popular visual culture through postcard series. Surviving cards identify Royer with the Latin formula *pinxit* and reproduce individual subjects, especially:

- Bois-Chenu and the Voices;
- Chinon;
- Orléans;
- Patay;
- Reims;
- the stake at Rouen.

Wikimedia Commons presently groups dozens of images under “Murals of Basilique du Bois-Chenu,” including early postcard reproductions. These digital groupings are useful for reception history but inherit the technically inaccurate “murals” terminology. [Wikimedia Commons, Lionel Royer category](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category%3ALionel-No%C3%ABl_Royer)

### 9.2 Patay reproduction

Patay town hall displays a reproduction of Royer’s *Jeanne d’Arc victorieuse à Patay* in a civic historical circuit. Its local title differs from the Palissy-derived phrase *Jeanne mène la bataille*. This replica has acquired a secondary public function:

- it localizes Royer’s national religious image at the actual town associated with the battle;
- it participates in municipal historical education;
- it is displayed alongside later works and commemorative material.

It is not an alternate claimant to the original.

### 9.3 Books and historical illustration

The *Patay* and *Reims* compositions are repeatedly reproduced in books, websites, educational materials, and popular histories of the Hundred Years’ War. Detached from the basilica, they can appear to be generic historical illustrations. Their modern reception often suppresses:

- the eight-part cycle;
- the sacramental Communion panels;
- the inscriptions;
- the architecture;
- the 1909–1920 canonization context.

### 9.4 Television and tourism

In 2015 the basilica served as a filming location for the French television program *Secrets d’Histoire: Jeanne d’Arc, au nom de Dieu*, broadcast on France 2. The cycle formed part of the modern visual environment through which Jeanne’s story was presented. The paintings also remain central in tourism descriptions and diocesan activity sheets.

### 9.5 Political and religious use

The cycle has been usable by several overlapping constituencies:

- Catholic pilgrims;
- French national commemorators;
- regional heritage tourism;
- military and patriotic memory;
- municipal commemoration at Patay;
- popular medieval history.

No evidence was found of a current organized dispute over the cycle’s political meaning, demands for removal, or a formal interpretive controversy inside the basilica. Scholarly criticism, however, situates such imagery within the late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century union and conflict of Catholic, monarchical, republican, military, and nationalist appropriations of Jeanne.

### 9.6 Public-domain status and reproductions

Royer died in 1926. His paintings are out of copyright under ordinary French term calculations, subject to rights in particular photographs and institutional reproduction policies. Modern photographs may carry their own neighboring or contractual restrictions. An image’s presence on Wikimedia or a postcard marketplace does not establish unrestricted rights in that particular reproduction.

### 9.7 Forgeries and misattributions

No forged full-scale “Royer Bois-Chenu painting” was identified. The principal market risk is looser:

- studies bearing posthumous studio stamps;
- copies or reproductions offered without clear labeling;
- postcards described as paintings;
- unrelated Jeanne images attributed to Royer because his Domrémy cycle is famous.

The 2021 auctioned *Sentencier* study was described as carrying Royer’s estate or studio stamp rather than an original full signature. That does not make it a forgery, but provenance and attribution should be evaluated independently for any work on paper.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Principal object catalogue

The key modern record is Palissy **PM88002005**. Its strengths are:

- official object identity;
- material and technique;
- dimensions;
- signatures and dates;
- location;
- ownership category;
- protection history;
- bibliography.

Its limits are:

- no component-by-component measurements;
- no complete inscription transcription;
- no mapped condition report;
- no commission or price;
- no treatment history;
- incomplete subject list;
- ambiguous Salon-prize statement;
- no mapping of individual dates to scenes.

### 10.2 Architectural scholarship

Hubert Collin and Christian Corvisier’s “Domrémy-la-Pucelle – Basilique du Bois-Chenu,” published in the 2008 *Congrès archéologique de France* volume for Nancy and southern Lorraine, is the principal architectural study cited in general records. It treats the paintings within the building’s construction and decorative program rather than as autonomous easel works.

The Ministry’s Mérimée record is important because it dates the building campaigns to 1881, 1926, 1934, and 1946 and identifies Royer among the creators of the protected monument.

### 10.3 Basillica monograph

The Ministry cites Dominique Guéniot and Bernard Mugnier, *La Basilique Sainte-Jeanne-d’Arc de Domrémy-la-Pucelle* (Langres, 2001), pp. 89–95 and 403–415. This is likely the fullest printed source for the commission and iconography. The detailed online basilica account explicitly credits Bernard Mugnier and provides the complete eight-scene enumeration.

Because the book’s full text was not accessible online, any unpublished contract, donor list, installation narrative, or restoration detail it may contain cannot be incorporated here without direct consultation.

### 10.4 Royer scholarship

The 1998 issue of the *Revue historique et archéologique du Maine* included material under the title “Peindre l’Histoire – Lionel Royer” and Chantal Bouchon’s study of Jeanne d’Arc windows and the participation of artists from Maine in the Orléans competition. This scholarship traces Royer’s Johannic designs back to the 1893 competition and emphasizes the relationship between historical reconstruction, allegory, religious imagery, and national meaning.

Jean-François Luneau’s study of Félix Gaudin and stained glass is relevant to Royer’s glass designs and the late nineteenth-century decorative network, but the glass must not be conflated with the oil cycle.

### 10.5 National memory scholarship

A scholarly book preview reproduces *Joan of Arc’s Victory at Patay* and labels it a “fresco” dated 1913. The reproduction demonstrates the image’s importance in modern national-memory studies, while its terminology exemplifies how secondary scholarship can obscure the actual support. [Oxford University Press preview](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780191651939_A23595411/preview-9780191651939_A23595411.pdf)

### 10.6 Archives and unpublished evidence

The most important archival repository is:

**Archives départementales des Vosges, fonds Sédille-Demay, 157 J**

Reported contents:

- 283 plans;
- approximately 0.50 linear metres of papers;
- photographs;
- drawings and tracings;
- site notebooks;
- correspondence;
- dates 1882–1988;
- freely communicable;
- summary finding aid.

Research priorities within this fonds should include:

- correspondence with Royer;
- correspondence with Boutet de Monvel;
- committee minutes;
- invoices and donor records;
- wall-preparation and marouflage specifications;
- installation photographs;
- First World War storage;
- later maintenance.

The Palissy notice states “Fiche CAOA manquante à la MPP” and directs researchers to the Conservation des antiquités et objets d’art. This is a material historiographical limitation: the expected local object file was missing from the Médiathèque du patrimoine et de la photographie when the notice was compiled.

### 10.7 Unresolved debates and research questions

1. Who formally commissioned Royer?
2. What documentary basis supports the claimed handover from Boutet de Monvel?
3. Which paintings bear 1911, 1912, 1913, 1916, and 1918 respectively?
4. When were the two transept canvases physically installed?
5. Were the canvases painted in Royer’s Paris-area studio or partly in situ?
6. Who performed the marouflage?
7. Who designed and executed the fleur-de-lis frames and captions?
8. What was the price, and which donors paid it?
9. Did assistants enlarge or paint from Royer’s designs?
10. What alterations occurred after installation?
11. Is the alleged 1913 Salon prize a catalogue error or shorthand for a documented distinction?
12. Are all ten 1893 watercolors still together, and how closely does each correspond to the eight final works?
13. What is the present location of the auctioned *Sentencier* study and other preparatory drawings?

No authenticity “war” exists comparable to those surrounding medieval relics. The scholarly problem is instead reconstructing production phases and separating eight originals from their large family of studies, windows, postcards, photographs, and replicas.

### Source limitations

The online evidence is sufficient to establish the ensemble’s identity, material, artist, date range, present location, ownership category, protection, and general iconography. It is not sufficient to reconstruct:

- the original contract;
- full payment history;
- donors;
- workshop personnel;
- transport;
- exact installation dates;
- technical analysis;
- restoration interventions;
- complete inscriptions.

Those questions require direct work in the Archives départementales des Vosges, diocesan archives, Conservation des antiquités et objets d’art files, Médiathèque du patrimoine records, the Société historique et archéologique du Maine collections, and possibly Royer family or studio papers.
