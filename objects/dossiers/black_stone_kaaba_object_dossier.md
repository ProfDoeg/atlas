# The Black Stone of the Kaaba: Object Dossier

**Object:** The Black Stone of the Kaaba  
**Arabic:** الْحَجَرُ الْأَسْوَدُ  
**Transliteration:** *al-Ḥajar al-Aswad*  
**Alternative designation:** الرُّكْن الأَسْوَد, *al-Rukn al-Aswad*, “the Black Corner”; in early ritual reports simply الرُّكْن, *al-rukn*, “the Corner”  
**Object class:** Sacred architectural relic; cultically significant cornerstone; pilgrimage marker  
**Present location:** Eastern corner of the Kaaba, Masjid al-Haram, Mecca, Saudi Arabia  
**Current custodian:** The Saudi state through the authority responsible for the Grand Mosque and the Prophet’s Mosque  
**Current physical form:** Several small dark fragments embedded in a reddish-brown binding matrix and enclosed by a replaceable oval frame of silver  
**Scientific status:** Material composition undetermined. No publicly documented laboratory sampling, petrography, elemental analysis, isotopic analysis, or absolute dating has been performed on an authenticated fragment still under the Kaaba’s custodianship.

## Evidentiary conventions

This dossier distinguishes the following categories:

- **Documented or directly observed:** Supported by eyewitness description, contemporary or near-contemporary chronicle, official custodial record, or photography.
- **Textually attested tradition:** A claim preserved in identifiable Islamic literary sources but not independently demonstrable as physical history.
- **Scientific finding:** A conclusion based on direct analytical examination. Very little evidence concerning the Black Stone qualifies.
- **Visual or comparative inference:** A geological proposal based on photographs, descriptions, or comparison rather than a sampled authenticated fragment.
- **Disputed report:** Reported in historical literature but contradicted by other sources or lacking contemporary corroboration.
- **Legend or pious tradition:** Theological or sacred history not susceptible to ordinary archaeological verification.
- **Modern claim:** A recent attribution, reconstruction, or popular story whose historical chain is incomplete.

Dates are given in both AH and CE where useful. Conversion between lunar and solar calendars can produce a one-year discrepancy in secondary literature.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Name

The standard Arabic name, **الْحَجَرُ الْأَسْوَدُ** (*al-Ḥajar al-Aswad*), means literally “the Black Stone”:

- **ḥajar**: stone or rock;
- **al-aswad**: the black.

Classical Arabic usage can shorten the name to **al-Ḥajar**, “the Stone,” when the referent is understood. *Lisān al-ʿArab* explicitly notes that “the Black Stone” is the stone of the Sacred House and may simply be called *al-ḥajar*. It is also called **al-Rukn**, “the Corner,” because the stone and the eastern corner containing it became metonymically interchangeable in ritual language. Canonical hadith therefore often refers to touching or pointing toward “the Corner” without repeating “Black Stone.”

The compound **al-Rukn al-Aswad**, “the Black Corner,” names the corner by its relic. The popular expression **al-Ḥajar al-Asʿad**, “the Most Fortunate/Blessed Stone,” is a rhyming devotional alteration of *al-Aswad*. It is not the principal early name, and some modern jurists reject it as an unwarranted embellishment.

Turkish **Hacerülesved**, Persian and Urdu **Ḥajar-e Aswad**, Malay/Indonesian **Hajar Aswad**, and European “Black Stone,” *Pierre noire*, *Schwarzer Stein*, and *Piedra Negra* all derive from the Arabic.

### 1.2 What physically exists

The object visible today is not an intact freestanding rock. It is a **composite installation** consisting of:

1. exposed dark fragments traditionally identified as surviving pieces of the historic Black Stone;
2. a reddish-brown or black-brown binding compound holding those pieces together;
3. material extending into or behind the Kaaba’s masonry;
4. an oval silver protective frame fixed to the corner.

Saudi institutional descriptions say that approximately **fifteen pieces** may remain within the complete installation, while **eight small pieces of varying size** are visible at the surface. The largest exposed piece is conventionally described as about the size of a date. The visible setting is approximately 30 cm across when the silver frame is included. The stone is installed about 1.5 m above the present *maṭāf*, the paved circumambulation level. The claim that additional, largely white material remains hidden inside the wall derives from medieval testimony rather than modern imaging or excavation.

There is no published accession number. It is a living component of the Kaaba, not a museum object.

### 1.3 Surface, color, and texture

The visible fragments are black to deep reddish-brown, with smoother, reflective high points produced by prolonged handling, kissing, polishing, cleaning, and application of perfume. Modern high-resolution photographs show irregular dark inclusions surrounded by reddish-brown matrix.

Johann Ludwig Burckhardt’s 1814 eyewitness description remains unusually valuable. He reported:

- an irregular oval about seven inches across;
- an undulating surface;
- approximately a dozen smaller pieces joined with a small amount of cement;
- a deep reddish-brown color approaching black;
- whitish and yellowish particles;
- a supporting border resembling pitch mixed with gravel;
- a silver band and silver nails.

Burckhardt thought it looked like lava, but expressly recognized that ritual wear made visual identification difficult. His description documents appearance, not mineralogy.

The Spanish traveler Domingo Badía y Leblich, traveling as ʿAlī Bey al-ʿAbbāsī in 1807, described a blackish matrix with small colored particles and a large surrounding silver plate. Richard Burton touched and kissed the stone during his 1853 pilgrimage but did not obtain a sample.

A report attributed to Austrian diplomat Anton von Laurin concerns a fragment allegedly removed during the Egyptian-Ottoman recovery of the Hijaz in or around 1817. Laurin described:

- a pitch-black exterior;
- a silver-gray, fine-grained interior;
- small bottle-green inclusions.

The fragment’s precise removal, subsequent custody, and present location are not securely documented in the accessible record. Its identification as genuine therefore cannot support a conclusive mineralogical diagnosis.

### 1.4 Dimensions over time

Reported dimensions vary dramatically because writers measured different things: the exposed surface, the frame, the matrix, or the presumed concealed body.

- A medieval description gives a length around one cubit.
- Muḥammad ibn Nāfiʿ al-Khuzāʿī reportedly examined it when returned by the Qarmatians and described it as approximately one cubit long, black only at its head and otherwise white.
- Ibn ʿAllān transmitted a description of roughly half a cubit long, one-third cubit wide, and four *qīrāṭs* thick.
- ʿAlī Bey placed it 42 inches above the pavement and described the framed installation.
- Burckhardt measured the exposed oval at roughly seven inches, about 18 cm.
- Modern descriptions commonly give an exposed area around 16 × 20 cm or an oval frame approximately 30 cm in diameter.

These numbers cannot be assembled into a simple story of steady shrinkage. Rearrangement of fragments, altered mortar, replacement frames, different cubit standards, and confusion between exposed and concealed portions all intervene.

### 1.5 Binding materials and frame

Medieval repair accounts mention mixtures of powdered stone or recovered splinters with **musk** and **lakk**, a word which may denote lacquer, resin, or red coloring matter. Modern Saudi descriptions characterize the surface matrix as a compound containing wax, amber, and musk, though no public conservation analysis gives exact proportions.

The frame is pure or high-grade silver according to custodial sources. It is protection, reinforcement, and visual boundary, not part of the stone proper. The present frame has no published inscription visible to pilgrims.

The earliest well-defined silver reinforcement is traditionally attributed to **ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Zubayr** after the fire of 64 AH/683 CE. Later rulers repeatedly repaired or replaced silverwork:

- Hārūn al-Rashīd ordered reinforcement around 188 AH/804 CE;
- successive Abbasid, Mamluk, and Ottoman custodians renewed it as required;
- Ottoman Sultan Mehmed V Reşad reportedly supplied a frame around 1331 AH/1913 CE;
- Saudi rulers repaired or replaced the frame in the twentieth century;
- an official Saudi account records complete replacement under King Khālid in 1979 and again under King Fahd in 2001;
- the frame received documented maintenance in September 2021.

An older frame preserved at Topkapı Palace is a removed fitting, not the stone itself.

### 1.6 Damage and survival

The present object is visibly fragmented. The principal reported causes are:

- cracking during the Kaaba fire of 64 AH/683 CE;
- forcible extraction by the Qarmatians in 317 AH/930 CE;
- subsequent transport and reinstallation;
- deliberate blows in 413 AH/1022–23 CE;
- other reported assaults in 363 AH/973–74 and 990 AH/1582;
- ordinary mechanical stress, repairs, repeated touching, and surface treatment.

It cannot be established how many fragments were lost in each episode. Nor can the present ratio of ancient stone to later binding compound be measured from published information.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Geological origin: unknown

No quarry, workshop, maker, commission, or geological source is documented. The stone’s material has never been securely identified.

Proposals include:

- basalt or another volcanic rock;
- granite;
- agate or chalcedony;
- a stony meteorite;
- meteoritic or terrestrial impact glass;
- another siliceous stone;
- a composite of more than one material after repeated breakage and repair.

None is established scientifically.

### 2.2 Earliest secure textual horizon

The Qur’an repeatedly mentions the Sacred House, Abraham and Ishmael’s connection with it, pilgrimage, and circumambulation. Qur’an 2:125–129 associates Abraham and Ishmael with purifying and raising the foundations of “the House”; Qur’an 22:26–29 likewise associates Abraham with the sanctuary and commands circumambulation. **The Qur’an does not name, describe, or narrate the origin of the Black Stone.**

The earliest surviving detailed narratives are preserved in Islamic literary compilations written down generations after Muhammad:

- Ibn Isḥāq’s *Sīrat rasūl Allāh* (d. 150 AH/767 CE), surviving chiefly through Ibn Hishām’s early ninth-century recension;
- al-Azraqī’s *Akhbār Makka* in the ninth century;
- the major Sunni hadith collections of the ninth century;
- later Meccan chronicles, geographical works, pilgrimage accounts, and universal histories.

These works may preserve earlier oral and written material, but their manuscript dates are later than the events they describe.

The physical stone must have been installed by the time that ritual reports concerning Muhammad and early Muslims took form. The traditional account places it in the pre-Islamic Kaaba before about 605 CE. There is, however, no surviving pre-Islamic inscription, external chronicle, securely contemporary poem, or archaeological report identifying it by name. One early twentieth-century scholarly study noted the silence of supposedly contemporary poetry and questioned whether it had yet acquired its later preeminence. That is an argument from silence, not proof of absence.

### 2.3 The Quraysh rebuilding and Muhammad’s arbitration

**Textually attested early Islamic tradition:** Around 605 CE, when Muhammad was about thirty-five, Quraysh rebuilt the Kaaba after fire, flood, or structural deterioration. When the walls reached the level of the Black Stone, the clans disputed who would receive the honor of replacing it. They agreed to accept the next entrant as arbitrator. Muhammad entered; he placed the stone on a cloak, had representatives of the clans lift it together, and then installed it with his own hands.

The narrative appears in the Ibn Isḥāq–Ibn Hishām tradition and al-Azraqī. It gives the earliest narratively specific “custody event” in the stone’s biography: temporary removal during construction, collective lifting, and replacement by Muhammad.

The event cannot be independently verified from a contemporary non-Islamic source. Its importance within the sīra is also literary: it depicts the pre-prophetic Muhammad as *al-Amīn*, “the trustworthy,” preventing bloodshed and reconciling Quraysh.

### 2.4 Traditional Abrahamic origin

Islamic tradition develops a much older sacred history:

1. God or an angel sent the stone from Paradise.
2. It was originally white—whiter than milk or snow.
3. Human sin, especially the touch or sins of the descendants of Adam, blackened it.
4. In some versions it accompanied Adam and marked the first sanctuary.
5. During Noah’s Flood it was concealed or preserved on Mount Abū Qubays near Mecca.
6. When Abraham and Ishmael built or rebuilt the Kaaba, Gabriel brought it, or the mountain disclosed it.
7. Abraham placed it in the eastern corner as a marker for circumambulation.

The widely transmitted hadith in *Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī* states: “The Black Stone descended from Paradise, and it was whiter than milk; then the sins of the children of Adam blackened it.” Al-Tirmidhī classified the report *ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ*. Other reports call the Stone and the Station of Abraham two rubies or sapphires of Paradise whose light God extinguished.

These are theological attestations, not geological observations. “Ruby” (*yāqūta*) in such texts marks paradisal substance and luminosity; it cannot be translated directly into a modern mineral species.

### 2.5 Growth of the origin narrative

The layers should be kept distinct:

- **Qur’anic layer:** Abraham, Ishmael, the House, and circumambulation; no Black Stone.
- **Hadith layer:** Muhammad touched or kissed the Corner; the Stone came from Paradise and was originally white.
- **Meccan chronicle and *qiṣaṣ* layer:** Adamic sanctuary, concealment on Abū Qubays, Gabriel’s intervention, recovery during Abrahamic construction.
- **Later devotional layer:** the Stone received humanity’s primordial covenant; it is God’s “right hand on earth”; it will testify with eyes and a tongue.
- **Modern popular layer:** “from heaven” is frequently recast as “a meteorite.” That equivalence is not made by the early texts and is a modern naturalizing interpretation.

### 2.6 Baetyl hypothesis

Modern historians have sometimes classified the Black Stone as a **baetyl**, an aniconic sacred stone comparable to cult stones elsewhere in Arabia and the eastern Mediterranean. This is a comparative category, not an ancient Meccan label securely applied to it. Sacred stones are well attested in the broader region, and later Muslim sources describe other Arabian shrines with red or white stones. That context makes pre-Islamic veneration plausible, but it does not establish:

- the Stone’s geological nature;
- a particular deity to whom it belonged;
- a line of descent from Nabataean, Roman, Phrygian, or Anatolian stones;
- identity with Elagabalus’s stone, Cybele’s stone, or any other famous baetyl.

Internet narratives carrying the Stone from Çatalhöyük through several ancient civilizations to Rome and then Mecca have no documented provenance and are modern inventions or conflations.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 Legendary custody before documented history

The traditional chain is:

**Paradise → Adam or the first earthly sanctuary → concealment at Abū Qubays during the Flood → Gabriel/Abraham and Ishmael → Kaaba.**

Every link before late antiquity belongs to sacred tradition. No excavated context, inscription, or datable contemporary document bridges this chain.

Another traditional narrative says that when Jurhum lost control of Mecca, ʿAmr ibn al-Ḥārith ibn Muḍāḍ buried the Stone and other Kaaba valuables in Zamzam. A Khuzāʿī woman observed the burial and enabled her people to recover it. This story belongs to the traditional pre-Islamic custody cycle preserved by later Meccan historians; it is not independently documented.

### 3.2 Quraysh custody and the rebuilding, approximately 605 CE

By the traditional account the Stone was already installed in the Kaaba under Quraysh custodianship. During reconstruction it was removed and then returned through Muhammad’s cloak arbitration. Mechanism:

- temporary architectural removal;
- shared ceremonial lifting by Quraysh clans;
- personal reinstallation by Muhammad.

This is the first detailed narrative in which named human hands move the object.

### 3.3 Muhammad and the early Muslim community, 610s–632

After the Muslim conquest of Mecca in 8 AH/630 CE, the Kaaba was cleared of idols. The Stone remained in place and was incorporated into Islamic pilgrimage practice.

Canonical hadith reports say that Muhammad:

- kissed the Stone;
- touched the Corner;
- while mounted, touched it with a crooked staff and kissed the staff;
- in another version, pointed toward it while circumambulating.

The evidence concerns ritual conduct, not ownership. Custody remained attached to the Kaaba and its Meccan guardians.

The Stone’s status was deliberately differentiated from an independently powerful idol. In the famous report preserved in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī*, Caliph ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb addressed it: “I know that you are a stone and can neither harm nor benefit. Had I not seen the Messenger of God kiss you, I would not have kissed you.” This report became the classical boundary-setting text: contact is imitation of prophetic practice, not belief in autonomous stone-power.

### 3.4 Fire, fragmentation, and Ibn al-Zubayr’s silver binding, 64–65 AH/683–685 CE

During the Second Fitna, Umayyad forces commanded by al-Ḥuṣayn ibn Numayr besieged ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Zubayr in Mecca. Fire damaged the Kaaba in 64 AH/683 CE. Later sources state that heat cracked the Black Stone into three pieces.

Ibn al-Zubayr rebuilt the Kaaba. He is credited with:

- keeping the broken pieces together;
- returning them to the reconstructed corner;
- first binding or encircling the Stone with silver.

The precise construction of his reinforcement is unknown. Nevertheless, the visible fragmentation and the long tradition of silver supports make a major early repair highly plausible.

After Ibn al-Zubayr’s defeat in 73 AH/692 CE, al-Ḥajjāj ibn Yūsuf altered the Kaaba’s structure on the order of Caliph ʿAbd al-Malik. The Stone remained in its ritual corner; the sources do not describe another removal at that rebuilding.

### 3.5 Abbasid reinforcement, 188 AH/804 CE

By al-Azraqī’s time the silver had thinned or loosened. Caliph Hārūn al-Rashīd reportedly ordered work around 188 AH/804 CE:

- masonry above and below the Stone was opened or drilled;
- molten silver was introduced to secure the installation;
- the frame or ligature was strengthened.

This operation illustrates why “the object” is not simply the visible rock: its continuing identity depended on successive custodial matrices, metal supports, and surrounding masonry.

### 3.6 Qarmatian seizure, 317 AH/930 CE

The most securely attested displacement occurred during the Qarmatian sack of Mecca in the pilgrimage season of 317 AH/930 CE. Forces led by **Abū Ṭāhir Sulaymān al-Jannābī** attacked pilgrims and inhabitants, plundered the sanctuary, and removed the Black Stone.

The core facts are supported by multiple medieval historians and accepted in modern scholarship:

- violent Qarmatian entry into Mecca;
- pillage of the Kaaba;
- forcible extraction of the Stone;
- transport to Qarmatian territory in eastern Arabia;
- absence from Mecca for about twenty-two years;
- return in 339 AH/951 CE.

Accounts add that a man struck the Stone with a heavy implement while challenging the sanctuary’s miraculous protection. The extent to which this blow broke it, as opposed to exploiting earlier fractures, cannot be established.

### 3.7 Where the Qarmatians kept it

Sources variously locate it at:

- **al-Aḥsāʾ/al-Lahsa**, the Qarmatian capital region;
- **Hajar**, a geographical name used for eastern Arabia;
- **al-Qaṭīf**;
- a place called **al-Juʿba**;
- a Qarmatian “Mosque of Dissent,” where it was allegedly displayed to redirect pilgrimage.

The general movement from Mecca to Qarmatian eastern Arabia is secure. Its exact building-level location is not. Later Sunni sources are polemical and sometimes conflate eastern Arabian place-names.

The Stone’s removal was a political and religious act. Farhad Daftary interprets it cautiously as intended to symbolize the end of Islam’s outward legal era in Qarmatian eschatology. Modern claims that it was routinely used as a latrine are not securely present in the best-known early narratives and should not be repeated as documented fact.

### 3.8 Negotiations and disputed ransom

Abbasid and Fatimid authorities demanded its return. Medieval accounts disagree about money:

- Ibn al-Athīr says the commander Bajkam offered **50,000 dinars**, which the Qarmatians refused.
- The Qarmatians are reported to have answered, “We took it by command, and we return it by command.”
- Some later sources say it was ransomed or purchased.
- Nāṣir-i Khusraw’s eleventh-century *Safarnāma* says it was ultimately bought back.
- Other narratives insist that it was returned without payment, whether through ideological instruction, political negotiation, Fatimid pressure, or changing Qarmatian policy.

No surviving receipt or contemporary financial account resolves the issue.

### 3.9 Return through Kufa, 339 AH/951 CE

The best-attested return sequence is:

1. Qarmatian agents brought the Stone to Kufa.
2. It was hung or displayed on the seventh column of Kufa’s congregational mosque.
3. A declaration said that it had been taken and returned by command.
4. It was carried to Mecca.
5. It arrived in Dhū al-Qaʿda 339 AH, conventionally corresponding to 951 CE.
6. It was restored to the Kaaba.

Some sources instead associate the installation with the Day of Sacrifice and give 950 or 952 CE. The difference partly reflects conversion and narrative chronology.

The agent is named variously as **Sanbar/Sunbur/Shanbar ibn al-Ḥasan**, sometimes described as a Qarmatian official or intermediary. Later sources attach Caliph al-Muṭīʿ, an ʿAlid intermediary, or a Fatimid command to the settlement.

### 3.10 Identity challenge at Kufa and Mecca

A medieval geographical report preserves a Qarmatian taunt: how could the recipients know that the returned stone was the original? In response, later traditions supplied tests:

- it floated in water;
- it did not become hot in fire;
- a custodian recognized marks placed upon it;
- a learned man distinguished it from substitute black stones.

These tests are legendary or apologetic unless supported by independent contemporary testimony. No surviving protocol records a controlled examination. The historical continuity claim rests chiefly on communal recognition and the public return process, not scientific authentication.

### 3.11 The 363 AH/973–74 assault

Later Meccan chronicles report that a man “from the land of the Romans,” sometimes identified as Christian, struck the Stone with a pick or mallet after being paid to destroy it. A Yemeni pilgrim reportedly killed him before a second effective blow.

This is an attested medieval report but lacks a named perpetrator and contemporary corroboration. His motive, sponsor, and religious identity should be treated as allegations transmitted in confessional historical literature.

### 3.12 The 413 AH/1022–23 assault and repair

This incident is reported in substantially greater detail. After Friday prayer during the pilgrimage season, a man said to have come from Egypt approached as if to touch the Stone. Armed with a sword and mace, he struck it three times, reportedly declaring that neither Muhammad nor ʿAlī could stop him and asking how long the Stone would be worshipped.

The crowd killed, dismembered, and burned him; suspected companions were also killed. The blows:

- damaged the center of the exposed face;
- caused three fingernail-sized splinters to fall;
- exposed a brownish-yellow, granular interior likened to poppy seeds;
- produced cracks to either side.

The Banū Shayba, hereditary guardians of the Kaaba, gathered the fragments, mixed them with musk and *lakk*, filled the damaged places, and allowed the repair to set. The incident is preserved in Ibn al-Jawzī and later Meccan histories.

Some sources connect the attacker to Fatimid Caliph al-Ḥākim bi-Amr Allāh. That allegation is not independently proven. Al-Ḥākim had died in 411 AH/1021 CE, so direct responsibility requires either chronological adjustment or a surviving faction acting in his name.

### 3.13 The reported 990 AH/1582 assault

Ibn ʿAllān and later accounts say that a non-Arab Iraqi struck the Stone with a club or mace and was killed by an official called Jāwush. Details are sparse. The event belongs in the transmitted chronology but cannot be reconstructed closely.

### 3.14 Ottoman repairs and alleged exported fragments

Ottoman rulers, as imperial patrons of the Two Holy Mosques, maintained the frame and surrounding masonry. Turkish tradition identifies small dark inserts at several sites as fragments detached during repairs and sent to Istanbul:

- four small stones in the Sokollu Mehmed Pasha Mosque at Kadırga—near the entrance, mihrab, minbar doorway, and minbar canopy;
- a stone above the entrance of Sultan Süleyman’s tomb;
- a stone associated with Edirne’s Old Mosque.

Their institutional tradition links them to sixteenth-century work conducted under Süleyman I and to buildings designed by Sinan. However:

- no complete transfer dossier from Mecca to Istanbul has been published;
- no authenticated comparison sample exists;
- the Kadırga, Süleymaniye, and Edirne stones may not all have the same composition;
- a 2023 visual/mineralogical discussion found the Edirne stone texturally unlike both the Kaaba and Istanbul examples.

They must therefore be catalogued as **traditional claimant fragments**, not authenticated detached pieces.

### 3.15 The 1626 flood and 1627 reconstruction

A major flood in 1039 AH/1626 CE caused three walls of the Kaaba to collapse. The Ottoman-sponsored rebuilding of 1627 reconstructed the building. Sources do not report loss of the Stone; the eastern corner was stabilized and the relic retained or returned to its place.

This episode underscores a distinction: most of the present Kaaba superstructure is not the fabric standing in Muhammad’s lifetime, but the Stone’s custodial identity is claimed to have survived successive rebuildings.

### 3.16 The 1674 contamination allegation

Burckhardt, writing in the nineteenth century, repeated a report that in 1674 someone smeared the Stone with excrement, so that those kissing it soiled their beards. Suspicion allegedly fell on Persians.

This is not a contemporary eyewitness account by Burckhardt. The perpetrator and motive are unknown; ethnic attribution is polemical. The report should be labeled **later allegation**, not proven Persian action. The alleged contaminant would have been cleaned away and did not constitute a custody transfer.

### 3.17 Wahhabi-Saudi and Egyptian-Ottoman conflict, early nineteenth century

Wahhabi-Saudi forces controlled Mecca from 1803 until the Egyptian-Ottoman reconquest. Some later French scholarship claimed that during their occupation the Kaaba was plundered and the Stone trodden upon. The exact physical treatment is poorly documented and requires caution.

In or around 1817, during repairs under Muḥammad ʿAlī Pasha’s administration, a fragment was reportedly removed and examined by Anton von Laurin. Its fate is unknown. If genuine, this represents a poorly documented detachment, not an acknowledged permanent transfer of custody.

### 3.18 Theft of a fragment, 1351 AH/1932 CE

In May 1932 a pilgrim, identified variously in modern reports as Persian or Afghan and named in a contemporary Hijaz newspaper account as **ʿAbd al-Sattār ibn ʿAbd al-Ghaffār al-Afghānī**, removed:

- a piece of the Black Stone;
- a piece of the Kaaba’s covering;
- silver pieces from sanctuary fittings or stairs.

Guards arrested him. Saudi accounts say the recovered fragment was personally restored by King ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz. The reported date of reinstallation is 28 Rabīʿ I 1351 AH, corresponding in Saudi retrospective reporting to 30 or 31 August 1932.

Later summaries say the thief was executed. The precise trial record and execution documentation have not been located in the sources used here.

### 3.19 Saudi-era repairs

Published custodial chronology includes:

- repairs to the older frame under King ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz;
- a replacement silver frame under King Saʿūd, commonly dated 1375 AH/1956 CE;
- replacement under King Khālid in 1979;
- replacement under King Fahd in 2001;
- surrounding marble renewed during the comprehensive 1996 Kaaba restoration;
- periodic cleaning, perfuming, inspection, and frame maintenance;
- documented frame maintenance on 7 September 2021.

The exact conservation products and engineering drawings are not public.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Is the present installation the same object known in early Islam?

The strongest case for continuity is historical rather than scientific:

- medieval sources consistently locate a sacred stone in the same ritual corner;
- its removal in 930 and return in 951 were public, politically consequential events;
- later attacks and repairs were openly recorded rather than concealed;
- visible fragmentation accords with that repair history;
- no source documents an admitted wholesale replacement.

The limits are equally important:

- no secure sample can link the present fragments chemically to an early fragment;
- no chain-of-custody instrument survives from 930–951;
- medieval recipients themselves faced a substitution question;
- some material was lost, powdered, reattached, or absorbed into repair compound;
- the proportions of original stone and later matrix are unknown.

The most supportable conclusion is: **the present installation possesses a long, continuous custodial identity as the Black Stone, but material continuity of every visible fragment cannot be independently proven.**

### 4.2 Qarmatian substitution hypothesis

The possibility that the Qarmatians returned another stone has been raised since the medieval period. Evidence for substitution is weak:

- the challenge appears as a taunt or skeptical question rather than an admission;
- there is no competing object with a documented Qarmatian provenance;
- Meccan authorities accepted and installed the returned object;
- later reports say people familiar with it recognized it.

The floating, fire, and secret-mark tests are apologetic traditions, not scientific evidence. Thus substitution is possible in the abstract but undocumented.

### 4.3 Meteorite hypothesis

The popular claim that the Stone is a meteorite is not an established fact.

Paul Partsch reportedly classified it as meteoritic in 1857; M. A. R. Khan argued for a meteoritic origin in 1938; the Prior–Hey catalogue listed “Kaaba” as a possible meteorite. These judgments were based on reports rather than a demonstrably authenticated laboratory specimen.

Problems include:

- absence of a sampled authenticated fragment;
- no published nickel, iron, chondrule, oxygen-isotope, or fusion-crust analysis;
- reported hardness and siliceous appearance;
- the traditional report that the Stone floated, if factual, would be inconsistent with ordinary stony and iron meteorites.

The Meteoritical Bulletin records the historical literature but does not thereby certify the Stone as an approved meteorite.

### 4.4 Agate hypothesis

Robert S. Dietz and John McHone argued in 1974 that the Stone was probably agate, citing alleged diffusion banding, hardness, and physical descriptions. Their article’s title—“Kaaba Stone: Not a Meteorite, Probably an Agate”—is more definite than the evidence permits.

They did not conduct laboratory analysis on the installed Stone. “Agate” remains a comparative visual hypothesis.

### 4.5 Wabar impact-glass hypothesis

Elsebeth Thomsen proposed in 1980 that the Stone might be impactite glass from the Wabar craters in the Rubʿ al-Khālī. This could accommodate dark glassy material and, potentially, low-density fragments.

Objections include:

- Wabar lies roughly 1,100 km from Mecca;
- the date of the Wabar impact is now generally thought much more recent than the millennia assumed by early proposals;
- no compositional match exists;
- photographs show apparently crystalline or granular components rather than homogeneous impact glass;
- the required transport history is undocumented.

The Wabar identification is speculative and presently weak.

### 4.6 Basalt, lava, and granite

Burckhardt thought the surface resembled lava. Later travelers suggested basalt or granite. A claim that T. E. Lawrence secretly examined it with a magnifying glass and identified granite has circulated in recent geological literature, but its documentary basis is uncertain and should not be accepted without the cited primary travel record.

Photographic texture alone cannot reliably distinguish weathered basalt, altered igneous rock, siliceous stone, glass, or a repair composite.

### 4.7 The 2023 image-based study

K. Kocak’s 2023 paper used the Saudi high-resolution image to estimate visible grain sizes and compare claimed Turkish fragments. It identified:

- dark gravel-like areas approximately 9.7–27 mm;
- small dark prismatic forms;
- whitish elongated crystals tentatively likened to plagioclase;
- reddish-brown matrix;
- an apparently granular rather than uniformly glassy texture.

The author concluded that volcanic and impact-glass interpretations were disfavored and stressed that no chemistry or isotopic work exists. These observations are **image analysis**, not direct petrography. Lighting, polish, perfume, matrix, digital processing, and the uncertain identity of Turkish comparanda sharply limit the result.

### 4.8 Turkish claimant fragments

The Sokollu Mosque fragments have a strong local and Ottoman devotional attribution, but no laboratory comparison can prove them part of the Kaaba stone. The Edirne claimant was described as tonalitic in the 2023 study and visually unlike the Kaaba fragments. That result challenges at least the assumption that all Turkish claimants are pieces of one rock.

They should not be used as proxies for chemical analysis of the Meccan installation.

### 4.9 Authority to authenticate

Authentication authority has moved through several forms:

- hereditary Meccan guardians, especially the Banū Shayba;
- caliphal and sultanic patrons who financed restoration;
- jurists who determined permissible handling;
- Ottoman imperial custodianship;
- Saudi royal and administrative custodianship;
- modern geologists commenting from descriptions and images.

No independent museum or laboratory has custody. In practice, the Meccan religious-custodial chain, not scientific certification, defines the object’s operative identity.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Marker of circumambulation

The Stone’s most fundamental physical function is to mark the start and completion of each circuit of **ṭawāf**, the counterclockwise circumambulation of the Kaaba. A pilgrim aligns with it at the beginning of each of seven circuits.

Its position is therefore operational, not merely decorative. Modern authorities have installed a visual marker or light opposite the Stone to help pilgrims on crowded or elevated levels identify the correct line.

### 6.2 Touching and kissing

The hierarchy of practice in Sunni jurisprudence is broadly:

1. kiss the Stone if safely possible;
2. otherwise touch it with the hand and kiss the hand;
3. otherwise touch it with an implement and kiss the implement, following a reported prophetic precedent;
4. otherwise point toward it and say *Allāhu akbar*.

Canonical reports document Muhammad touching the corner with a curved staff while mounted. The flexibility is important: bodily contact is recommended but not required for a valid pilgrimage, and harming others to reach it is contrary to the governing ritual ethic.

### 6.3 Theological boundary

ʿUmar’s declaration that the Stone cannot independently harm or benefit is central to Islamic interpretation. Veneration is framed as obedience and imitation of Muhammad, not worship of matter. Muslim theologians therefore distinguish:

- respect for a divinely designated sign;
- belief that the Stone has autonomous agency.

Claims that Muslims “worship the Stone” reproduce an old polemical category rather than the tradition’s own ritual theology.

### 6.4 Eschatological witness

Hadith literature says that on the Day of Resurrection the Stone will have eyes or a tongue and testify for those who touched it “rightfully” or sincerely. This is a belief about God granting it witness, not ordinary present-day animation.

A related tradition calls it God’s “right hand on earth,” through which worshippers symbolically pledge allegiance. Scholars often interpret this metaphorically, as a covenantal sign. The expression should not be mistaken for a universally literal doctrine.

### 6.5 Expiation and blessing

Some reports state that touching the Black Stone and Yemeni Corner removes sins. Pilgrims also associate contact with blessing. Yet the strongest canonical corrective remains ʿUmar’s statement: the material does not independently dispense benefit or injury.

### 6.6 Perfuming and cleaning

Modern custodial reports say the Stone is perfumed with high-quality *ʿūd* oil several times daily. Cleaning and inspection are performed by specialist teams. These acts are both conservation and ritual honor.

### 6.7 Political symbolism

Possession did not formally create a caliph, but control over the Stone could demonstrate power over pilgrimage:

- Quraysh clans nearly fought over the honor of placing it.
- Ibn al-Zubayr’s repair accompanied his rival caliphate in Mecca.
- The Qarmatian removal exposed Abbasid inability to protect the sanctuary and challenged pilgrimage order.
- Ottoman maintenance expressed the sultan’s role as patron of the Two Holy Mosques.
- Saudi conservation and imaging form part of present custodial legitimacy.

The object operates less as transferable regalia than as a fixed focus whose control validates the custodian.

### 6.8 Access and taboo

The Stone can ordinarily be seen from the *maṭāf*. Physical approach depends on crowd conditions and security management. Access to Mecca and the sanctuary is restricted to Muslims under Saudi law and practice. Touching is public but managed; removal, damage, or unauthorized sampling is forbidden.

During the COVID-19 period, barriers and capacity restrictions prevented or sharply limited direct contact. Later relaxation restored broader access. The 2021 “Virtual Black Stone” initiative was educational and preparatory, not a juristic replacement for physical pilgrimage.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 Descent from Paradise

**Early attested Islamic tradition:** The Stone descended from Paradise. The Tirmidhī version says it was originally whiter than milk.

The story establishes the object as not merely locally sacred but continuous with the paradisal order. It also explains why its natural terrestrial source is absent from tradition.

### 7.2 Blackened by human sins

**Hadith tradition:** Human sins turned the white Stone black.

Variants specify:

- sins of the children of Adam;
- contact with polytheists;
- the burden of those who touched it;
- earthly diminution of paradisal light.

Later moralists use the story analogically: if sin can blacken stone, it can more readily blacken the human heart. No physical inference about carbonization, oxidation, or meteorite crust follows from the narrative.

### 7.3 Adam, the covenant, and the first sanctuary

**Later elaboration:** God placed the primordial covenant—humanity’s acknowledgment of divine lordship—inside or upon the Stone. Adam used it at the first sanctuary. Its role as witness at Judgment derives from this covenantal function.

The Qur’an’s primordial covenant passage does not name the Stone. The link is exegetical and devotional.

### 7.4 Preservation during Noah’s Flood

**Later sacred-history tradition:** The Stone was concealed in Abū Qubays during the Flood. When Abraham rebuilt the House, the mountain called out or Gabriel retrieved it.

Variants shift agency among:

- Gabriel;
- Abū Qubays itself;
- Ishmael searching for a cornerstone;
- a heavenly *sakīna* guiding the construction.

### 7.5 Floating and fireproof identity tests

**Post-Qarmatian authentication legend:** The true Stone alone floated when placed in water or survived fire without becoming hot. A related story says a Meccan scholar recognized secret marks while Qarmatian substitutes sank.

No controlled contemporary account survives. The legend answers a real historical anxiety—whether the returned object was genuine—and its apologetic purpose is transparent.

### 7.6 Camels injured during removal

Later chronicles say forty camels died or were injured carrying the Stone away, while one young camel returned it unharmed. This is a miracle narrative accentuating the illegitimacy of removal and legitimacy of restoration. It is not a reliable measure of the Stone’s weight.

### 7.7 The Qarmatian latrine story

A widespread modern claim says the Qarmatians used the Stone as a toilet. The better-attested medieval core is removal, eastern Arabian detention, political-religious challenge, and return. The latrine detail is not securely established in the principal early accounts and should be labeled **late polemical or modern amplification** unless an earlier primary citation is produced.

### 7.8 Secret substitution and missing fragments

Modern conspiracy narratives claim that:

- the Qarmatians returned a fake;
- the original remains in Bahrain or al-Aḥsāʾ;
- Ottoman rulers stole substantial portions;
- Britain, Iran, or another government possesses the original;
- the visible pieces are entirely artificial.

No rival object has a documented provenance sufficient to support these claims. The reasonable unresolved question is narrower: how much original material survives in the repaired installation?

### 7.9 Meteorite as modernized sacred narrative

Calling the Stone a meteorite often functions as a modern reconciliation of “descended from heaven” with natural science. The hadith says Paradise, not interplanetary space. The meteorite identification first became prominent through modern European mineralogical speculation; it is not the scientific confirmation of an ancient statement.

## 8. Current Status and Location

As of **6 September 2026**, the Black Stone remains installed in the eastern corner of the Kaaba in the Masjid al-Haram, Mecca.

Its current status is:

- in active daily ritual use;
- publicly visible to authorized mosque visitors;
- physically accessible when crowd and security arrangements permit;
- regularly cleaned and perfumed;
- protected by a silver frame;
- supervised by the Saudi authority responsible for the Two Holy Mosques;
- not catalogued as a museum accession;
- not available for destructive sampling.

A Saudi Press Agency report of 7 March 2026 describes continuing regular maintenance, protective silver framing, and advanced cleaning methods. There is no publicly known ownership or repatriation lawsuit. The operative legal and custodial position treats it as an inseparable sacred component of the Kaaba under Saudi administration.

### Condition

The exposed surface remains fragmented but stable within its matrix. Saudi sources report eight visible pieces. The frame was renewed in 2001 and maintained in 2021. No published condition report identifies active cracking, mineral decay, corrosion beneath the frame, or percentage material loss.

### Imaging

In 2021 the Grand Mosque authority created an exceptionally detailed focus-stacked panorama:

- 1,050 photographs;
- about seven hours of photography;
- approximately fifty hours or a week of processing;
- a reported composite resolution of 49,000 megapixels;
- laser scanning or digital-model work associated with the project.

The image is a major documentation event. It is not a chemical scan and cannot determine provenance or mineral species.

### Access

Physical kissing is subject to crowd control. A guard and rope system have been used to regulate approach. Pointing from a distance remains ritually accepted. The stone is not removed for exhibition or loan.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Photography and mass circulation

For most of history, knowledge of the surface depended on pilgrims’ testimony. Modern telephotography and the 2021 focus-stacked image transformed public access to its visual detail. The official photographs revealed the separation between dark fragments and reddish matrix more clearly than most earlier images.

Online circulation also produced confusion: many viewers assumed the whole brown matrix was the relic, while others counted visible dark areas as independent “stones.” The number depends on whether fissures and matrix boundaries are treated as divisions.

### 9.2 Virtual Black Stone

On 13 December 2021, Shaykh ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Sudays inaugurated the **Virtual Black Stone Initiative**, developed with the Grand Mosque exhibitions authority and Umm al-Qura University’s Hajj and Umrah research institute. It proposed VR or multisensory preparation allowing remote users to see or simulate approaching the relic.

The project generated misleading headlines about a “metaverse Hajj.” The official initiative was interpretive and educational; it did not declare virtual contact equivalent to physical *ṭawāf* or pilgrimage.

### 9.3 Architectural replicas

Replicas of the Kaaba appear in Hajj training centers, museum exhibitions, educational displays, miniatures, and commercial devotional art. Their “Black Stones” are representational markers, not rival relics. Some full-scale replicas have drawn controversy when mistaken for alternative pilgrimage sites.

### 9.4 Ottoman claimant fragments as secondary relics

The stones in the Sokollu Mehmed Pasha Mosque and related Turkish sites have developed their own devotional lives. Visitors touch them, and guides sometimes suggest that pilgrims unable to reach the Meccan Stone may touch these fragments. This is local devotional practice, not an authenticated extension of the formal Hajj rite.

Their fame also serves Turkish-Ottoman heritage narratives emphasizing imperial care for Mecca. The religious importance of the claims is real even though material identity remains unproven.

### 9.5 Commerce and souvenirs

Images of the Stone appear on posters, prayer guides, jewelry, pilgrimage manuals, educational models, and digital media. Commercial objects advertised as containing “Black Stone dust” or fragments have no credible provenance and should be presumed unauthenticated. Legitimate custodians do not operate a trade in the Stone.

### 9.6 Polemical reception

Christian missionary, secularist, and anti-Islamic literature has frequently described the Stone as:

- an idol;
- a surviving pagan deity;
- a meteorite worshipped by Muslims;
- a sexual or fertility symbol;
- a Qarmatian substitute.

Muslim apologetic literature responds with ʿUmar’s hadith and the distinction between worship and prophetic imitation. Both sides sometimes overstate geology: critics call it definitively meteoritic, while apologetic treatments sometimes present floating, internal whiteness, or extraterrestrial origin as scientifically demonstrated.

### 9.7 Literature and visual art

The Stone appears in:

- medieval pilgrimage narratives;
- European Orientalist travel writing;
- illustrated Hajj manuscripts and maps;
- photographs and films of the pilgrimage;
- historical novels concerning the Qarmatians;
- modern documentaries about the Kaaba.

Unlike portable regalia, it has no extensive fictional career independent of the sanctuary. Modern representation usually uses it as a visual synecdoche for Hajj, Mecca, or the unity of the Muslim community.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Principal primary-source families

The documentary history depends on several bodies of evidence:

1. **Qur’an:** establishes the House, Abrahamic association, and circumambulation, but not the Stone by name.
2. **Hadith:** records Muhammad’s and early Muslims’ ritual conduct and sacred-origin sayings.
3. **Sīra:** especially Ibn Isḥāq through Ibn Hishām, for the Quraysh reconstruction.
4. **Meccan chronicles:** al-Azraqī, al-Fākihī, al-Fāsī, Ibn Fahd, and successors.
5. **Universal chronicles:** al-Ṭabarī, Miskawayh, Ibn al-Jawzī, Ibn al-Athīr, al-Dhahabī, Ibn Kathīr, and others.
6. **Geographical and travel literature:** al-Iṣṭakhrī, Yāqūt, Nāṣir-i Khusraw, Ibn Jubayr.
7. **European pilgrim-travelers:** Joseph Pitts, ʿAlī Bey, Burckhardt, Burton, and later observers.
8. **Ottoman repair records:** archival orders and correspondence, only partially published.
9. **Saudi institutional documentation:** twentieth- and twenty-first-century restoration, maintenance, photography, and digital initiatives.
10. **Geological literature:** speculative because direct sampling is absent.

### 10.2 Source problems

The evidence is unusually difficult because:

- the site is continuously sacred and cannot be excavated like an archaeological ruin;
- destructive analysis is religiously and institutionally unacceptable;
- early histories were written after the events;
- anti-Qarmatian sources are hostile;
- measurements refer to different physical components;
- repair compounds obscure original surfaces;
- official conservation details are limited;
- alleged detached fragments lack full chains of custody.

### 10.3 Nineteenth-century mineralogical debate

European observers brought geological vocabulary but rarely laboratory access. Visual resemblance generated incompatible classifications: lava, basalt, granite, agate, and meteorite. These identifications reveal the limitations of unaided surface inspection more than the Stone’s actual composition.

### 10.4 Dietz–McHone and Thomsen

The principal modern debate is:

- **Dietz and McHone, 1974:** probably agate, not meteorite;
- **Thomsen, 1980:** possibly Wabar impact glass;
- subsequent literature: neither can be demonstrated without sampling.

The changing date assigned to the Wabar impact substantially weakened the Wabar theory.

### 10.5 Image analysis versus laboratory science

The 2021 high-resolution photographs permit mapping of visible fragments and texture. The 2023 Kocak paper extracted grain measurements and compared Turkish stones. It did not establish composition.

A rigorous program, if ever authorized, would require:

- non-contact hyperspectral and Raman imaging;
- portable X-ray fluorescence, with recognition of surface contamination limits;
- micro-CT or ultrasonic mapping through the matrix;
- analysis of documented detached repair debris, if any exists;
- comparison of all Turkish claimant fragments;
- full archival provenance before destructive sampling;
- separate characterization of stone, adhesive, perfume residue, and corrosion products.

No such comprehensive published project presently exists.

### 10.6 Discredited or unsupported theories

The following should not be stated as fact:

- the Stone has been scientifically proven to be a meteorite;
- it came from Wabar;
- it is definitively agate;
- it has been radiocarbon dated;
- NASA tested it;
- it is a remnant of a Hindu *liṅga*;
- it traveled from Çatalhöyük or Rome to Mecca;
- the Qarmatians admitted returning a substitute;
- all Turkish “fragments” are genuine;
- touching it has measurable medical effects;
- the present Stone is entirely original or entirely replacement material.

Radiocarbon dating would in any case ordinarily be inappropriate for a mineral rock unless datable organic material associated with a secure ancient context were available.

### 10.7 State of the question

The responsible scholarly position is:

- A physical, fragmented sacred-stone installation unquestionably exists.
- Its ritual history is securely attested from early Islamic literature onward.
- Its pre-Islamic presence is strongly asserted by Islamic tradition but lacks external contemporary documentation.
- Its removal in 930 and return in 951 are historical.
- Its exact geology is unknown.
- Its custodial identity is continuous, but absolute material identity cannot be demonstrated.
- Major gaps remain in published Ottoman and Saudi conservation documentation.
