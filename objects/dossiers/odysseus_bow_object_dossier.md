# Odysseus's bow: Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `odysseuss_bow_object_dossier.md`  
**Status at 6 September 2026:** legendary/literary object; no surviving artifact or securely documented physical claimant.

## Evidentiary key

- **Primary literary evidence:** what an ancient text actually says.
- **Ancient interpretation:** scholia, commentary, or ancient visual reception.
- **Archaeological comparison:** evidence for real bows or axes, not evidence that this particular bow existed.
- **Scholarly inference:** a reconstruction compatible with some evidence but not directly stated.
- **Later tradition/retelling:** post-Homeric elaboration or adaptation.
- **Unverified modern claim:** an assertion circulating without adequate ancient or archaeological support.

The central distinction is decisive: the *Odyssey* gives the bow a detailed life inside heroic narrative, but no independent document, excavated artifact, cult inventory, dedicatory inscription, or ancient relic tradition demonstrates that the narrated weapon existed materially.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity

Odysseus’s bow is the personal bow kept in Odysseus’s palace on Ithaca and used in Books 21–22 of the *Odyssey* to complete Penelope’s contest and kill her suitors. Within the poem it is also an inherited weapon of Eurytus and Iphitus:

> Eurytus → his son Iphitus → Odysseus.

It is not the bow of Heracles. That separate weapon, inherited by Philoctetes in the best-known tradition, has often been confused with it because Heracles kills Iphitus and is connected with Eurytus’s family. Homer never says Heracles possessed the Ithacan bow.

### Names

The weapon has no unique ancient proper name comparable to *Excalibur*. Relevant designations are descriptive:

- Ancient Greek **τόξον** (*tóxon*), “bow,” also capable collectively of meaning bow-and-arrows or archery.
- **τόξον Ὀδυσσῆος**, “the bow of Odysseus,” a natural descriptive expression rather than a formal title.
- **τόξον παλίντονον** (*tóxon palíntonon*), *Odyssey* 21.11 and 21.59. The adjective can mean “bent back,” “back-stretched,” or “under countervailing tension.” It does not by itself identify a precise modern bow category.
- **μέγα τόξον**, “great bow,” used during the stringing scene.
- English **Odysseus’s bow**; Latinized English **the bow of Ulysses**. Modern European equivalents include French *l’arc d’Ulysse*, Italian *l’arco di Ulisse/Odisseo*, German *der Bogen des Odysseus*, and Modern Greek *το τόξο του Οδυσσέα*.

The common description “the bow of Eurytus” accurately describes its earlier Homeric ownership but can create confusion with other bows attached to the Eurytus–Heracles cycle.

### What Homer physically specifies

The fullest description occurs in *Odyssey* 21:

- It is **παλίντονον**, “back-bent/back-stretched” (21.11, 59).
- It is repeatedly called **well-polished**.
- It is kept with a **quiver** containing numerous “groan-bringing” arrows (21.11–12).
- It hangs from a **peg** inside or together with a bright bow-case, **γorytos** (*gōrytós*), in a remote storeroom (21.53–54).
- Odysseus turns it over and examines it to see whether **worms or grubs have eaten the horn**, *κέρα ἶπες ἔδοιεν* (21.393–395). Horn was therefore present, or at least imagined as a vulnerable component.
- Odysseus strings it while seated and then plucks the string. It gives a clear, high sound compared with a swallow’s cry (21.404–411).
- The stringing is compared to a singer fitting a newly twisted **sheep-gut string** to a lyre peg. This identifies the simile’s lyre string; it does **not conclusively state** that the bowstring itself was sheep gut.
- It is powerful enough, in the narrative, to send an arrow through the aligned targets and to penetrate human bodies.

### What Homer does not specify

No source supplies:

- dimensions;
- mass;
- draw weight or draw length;
- wood species;
- animal species furnishing the horn;
- glue or sinew backing;
- maker or workshop;
- date of manufacture;
- inscriptions, maker’s marks, metal fittings, paint, gilding, or inlay;
- exact bowstring material;
- exact shape in modern technical terms;
- exact construction of the arrows;
- whether the arrows used against the suitors retained the poison Odysseus once sought.

“Gray iron” in the contest belongs to the axes or iron targets, not to the bow.

### Composite-bow question

**Plausible but disputed scholarly inference:** Henry Balfour and later writers read the horn, reversed curvature, bow-case, difficulty of stringing, and the suitors’ use of heat and fat as evidence for a reflex composite bow made from a wooden core, horn, sinew, and animal glue. A composite bow is particularly vulnerable to damp and delamination; this has been invoked to explain why Odysseus left it at home rather than carry it by sea to Troy.

That reconstruction is possible, but not certain. Scholars have objected that:

- *palíntonos* is not a technical blueprint;
- the poem mentions horn but not a wooden core, sinew backing, or glue;
- horn could belong to a joined or horn-reinforced construction;
- real Mycenaean and early Greek bow evidence does not permit a unique identification;
- interpreting every poetic detail as mechanically exact commits what has been called a documentary fallacy.

The defensible description is therefore: **a powerful, horn-containing, back-bent bow, possibly composite or joined; exact construction unresolved**.

### Condition within the narrative

The bow has remained stored for approximately the duration of the Trojan War and Odysseus’s wandering—traditionally twenty years. Odysseus explicitly inspects it for insect damage. No damage is found or at least none prevents operation. The suitors warm and grease it, but the poem records no resulting alteration. It functions immediately when Odysseus strings it.

No source describes its condition after the battle.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Earliest securely reconstructable attestation

The earliest surviving detailed attestation is the Greek *Odyssey*, especially Books 19, 21, and 22. The monumental poem is commonly dated to the late eighth or seventh century BCE, though composition, textualization, and stabilization were processes rather than a single datable publication event. Martin L. West argued for the last third of the seventh century BCE; late-eighth- or early-seventh-century dates remain widely defended. The transmitted text rests on a long oral-formulaic tradition and much later manuscripts and papyri.

No securely dated pre-Homeric image or inscription identifies this particular bow.

### What the earliest evidence says

*Odyssey* 21.13–41 gives the object’s retrospective provenance:

1. Eurytus formerly carried the bow.
2. At his death he left it in his house to his son Iphitus.
3. Iphitus met the young Odysseus at Messene, in the house of Ortilochus.
4. Iphitus gave Odysseus the bow.
5. Odysseus reciprocated with a sword and strong spear, inaugurating a guest-friendship.
6. Heracles killed Iphitus before the two new friends could share one another’s tables.
7. Odysseus used or carried the bow within his homeland but did not take it to Troy.
8. It remained in his palace as a memorial of his friend.

The poem does not describe manufacture. It identifies neither a divine donor nor a craftsman.

### Eurytus and Apollo

In *Odyssey* 8.215–228, Odysseus recalls Eurytus as an exceptional archer who recklessly challenged the gods; Apollo killed him in anger. This supplies the poem’s explanation of Eurytus’s death and helps account for Iphitus’s inheritance.

Later genealogical retellings frequently say that Apollo gave Eurytus his bow or taught him archery. That proposition is often repeated online as though it were stated in Book 21. It is not. The Homeric evidence establishes Eurytus’s possession and death at Apollo’s hands, not the bow’s divine manufacture or gift by Apollo.

### Historical background versus narrated origin

The *Odyssey*’s heroic action is conventionally placed after a Trojan War imagined generations before the poem’s composition. Its material culture combines inherited Bronze Age memories, conditions familiar to early Iron Age singers, and poetic invention. Consequently:

- “Mycenaean bow,”
- “Scythian bow,”
- “Hittite bow,” and
- “Apollo’s bow”

are not established object identifications.

A real horn composite bow could have been known in the eastern Mediterranean before the poem’s textual stabilization. That technological possibility does not establish that a historical Eurytus, Iphitus, or Odysseus owned the narrated specimen.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

This section separates the weapon’s **internal narrative chain** from any **external physical history**.

### External/documentary chain

There is none. No identifiable material bow has a provenance leading back to ancient Ithaca, Eurytus, Iphitus, or Odysseus. No museum accession record begins such a chain. The documentation starts and ends with texts and representations.

### Internal Homeric chain

#### 1. Eurytus — first named bearer

**Source:** *Odyssey* 21.31–33; compare 8.223–228.  
**Evidence tier:** primary epic narrative.

Eurytus “was accustomed to bear” the bow. Its acquisition by Eurytus is unstated. Apollo kills Eurytus after his presumptuous challenge in archery.

**Place:** Eurytus’s “lofty house”; the poem does not identify which disputed Oechalia is intended in the bow passage.

#### 2. Eurytus to Iphitus — inheritance

**Source:** *Odyssey* 21.31–33.  
**Mechanism:** paternal bequest or inheritance at Eurytus’s death.  
**Evidence tier:** primary epic narrative.

The bow remains in Eurytus’s house until inherited by Iphitus.

#### 3. Iphitus transports it during his search

**Source:** *Odyssey* 21.22–37.  
**Evidence tier:** primary epic narrative.

Iphitus is searching for twelve lost mares and their mule foals. He brings the bow with him and meets Odysseus in Messene, at Ortilochus’s house.

#### 4. Iphitus to Odysseus — reciprocal gift

**Source:** *Odyssey* 21.13–37.  
**Place:** meeting associated with Messene; Book 21 also calls Iphitus the guest-friend whom Odysseus encountered in Lacedaemon, producing a geographical formulation commentators have discussed.  
**Mechanism:** gift exchange. Iphitus gives the bow; Odysseus gives a sword and spear.  
**Meaning:** establishment of *xeinía*, hereditary guest-friendship.

This is not a sale, theft, conquest, or wager. Heracles’s later killing of Iphitus is explicitly condemned as a violation of the guest-table and divine order, but Heracles does not take this bow: it has already passed to Odysseus.

#### 5. Odysseus’s use in Ithaca

**Source:** *Odyssey* 21.38–41.  
**Evidence tier:** primary epic narrative.

Odysseus carries or uses it in his own land. Penelope later recalls that he formerly set up twelve axes and shot through them (*Odyssey* 19.570–581), making the eventual contest a repetition of a household feat.

#### 6. Storage during the Trojan expedition and wanderings

**Source:** *Odyssey* 21.38–41, 44–56.  
**Location:** an innermost or remote treasury/storeroom of the Ithacan palace.  
**Custodial control:** Odysseus’s household; practically, Penelope has access through the storeroom key.  
**Evidence tier:** primary epic narrative.

Odysseus deliberately does not carry the bow aboard the black ships to Troy. The poem’s stated reason is memorial attachment: it lies at home as a keepsake of a dear guest-friend. Protection from marine damp is a modern mechanical inference, not Homer’s expressed reason.

The storeroom also contains bronze, gold, worked iron, and clothing chests. The bow hangs on a peg with its case and quiver.

#### 7. Penelope removes it from storage

**Source:** *Odyssey* 21.1–79.  
**Mechanism:** lawful household access, not transfer of ownership.  
**Evidence tier:** primary epic narrative.

Athena places the idea of the contest in Penelope’s mind. Penelope takes a curved bronze key with an ivory handle, opens the storeroom, takes the bow and case down, sits with them on her knees, and weeps. She then carries the bow and quiver into the hall.

The poem simultaneously calls the contest an athletic prize and “the beginning of slaughter.”

#### 8. Penelope entrusts it for the contest

**Source:** *Odyssey* 21.68–101.  
**Handlers:** Penelope and Eumaeus.  
**Evidence tier:** primary epic narrative.

Penelope announces that she will follow the man who can most easily string the bow and shoot through all twelve axes. Eumaeus receives or handles it and weeps; Philoetius likewise mourns at the sight. Antinous rebukes them.

#### 9. Telemachus handles the bow

**Source:** *Odyssey* 21.101–139.  
**Evidence tier:** primary epic narrative.

Telemachus digs a trench or prepares the alignment, sets up twelve axes, and attempts three times to string the bow. On the fourth attempt he appears close to succeeding, but Odysseus gives him a restraining signal and he stops. This is important evidence against the later simplification that the weapon is magically stringable only by Odysseus.

#### 10. Leodes and other suitors attempt it

**Source:** *Odyssey* 21.144–187.  
**Evidence tier:** primary epic narrative.

Leodes, a ritual specialist or diviner among the suitors, is the first suitor explicitly described attempting it. He fails and predicts that the bow will cause grief and pain. Other suitors follow and fail.

#### 11. Melanthius supplies heat and fat

**Source:** *Odyssey* 21.175–185.  
**Mechanism:** attempted conditioning.  
**Evidence tier:** primary epic narrative.

At Antinous’s direction, Melanthius brings fire and a large piece of fat. The suitors warm and grease the bow to make it easier to bend. They still fail.

Whether this is sensible maintenance of a composite bow, a misunderstanding by unskilled users, or a poetic device remains disputed.

#### 12. Eurymachus attempts it

**Source:** *Odyssey* 21.245–268.  
**Evidence tier:** primary epic narrative.

Eurymachus warms the bow and strains at it but cannot string it. His distress is less about losing Penelope than about the shame of proving inferior to Odysseus.

Antinous proposes postponement and sacrifice to Apollo, the archer god. He does not complete an attempt before Odysseus receives the bow.

#### 13. Eumaeus delivers it to the disguised Odysseus

**Source:** *Odyssey* 21.359–387.  
**Evidence tier:** primary epic narrative.

At Telemachus’s order, Eumaeus carries the bow across the hall despite the suitors’ threats. Penelope has withdrawn upstairs. Eurycleia secures the women’s quarters; Philoetius closes the courtyard gate. The physical delivery is part of the household coup.

#### 14. Odysseus inspects, strings, and sounds it

**Source:** *Odyssey* 21.390–415.  
**Evidence tier:** primary epic narrative.

Odysseus rotates the bow, checking whether grubs have eaten the horn. The suitors misread the inspection as connoisseurship by a beggar who may own or intend to copy such bows. He strings it easily, compared with an expert singer fitting a lyre string, and plucks it. Its string sings like a swallow. Zeus thunders as a favorable sign.

This is an authentication scene inside the poem: recognition occurs through embodied skill and the object’s performance rather than inscription or testimony.

#### 15. The shot through the axes

**Source:** *Odyssey* 21.416–434.  
**Evidence tier:** primary epic narrative.

Without rising from his seat, Odysseus takes an arrow lying ready, aims, and sends it through the entire alignment without missing. He tells Telemachus the guest has not disgraced him. Telemachus takes up his weapons and stands beside him.

#### 16. Use in the killing of the suitors

**Source:** *Odyssey* 22.1 onward.  
**Evidence tier:** primary epic narrative.

Odysseus moves to the great threshold, pours the arrows at his feet, and declares the first contest finished. Invoking Apollo, he chooses a new target:

- Antinous is killed by an arrow through the throat.
- Eurymachus is shot in the chest/liver while charging with a sword.
- Amphinomus is killed by Telemachus’s spear, not the bow.
- Odysseus continues shooting until the accessible arrows are exhausted; the battle then proceeds with spears and close combat.
- The poem summarizes that many remaining suitors have already fallen beneath Odysseus’s arrows.

The bow’s tactical value depends on controlled space: Odysseus occupies the threshold while the hall has been secured and the suitors’ weapons removed. It is consequently both weapon and instrument of planned enclosure.

#### 17. End of traceable custody

After Book 22 the bow drops from the narrative record. Books 23–24 do not assign it a new keeper, destroy it, dedicate it, bury it, or pass it to Telemachus. Its continued presence in Odysseus’s household is a reasonable inference, not an attested subsequent custody link.

### Break in the chain

The chain terminates completely after the slaughter. No surviving sequel tradition provides a secure later biography for this particular weapon. Accounts of Odysseus’s later travels or death focus on other implements—notably Telegonus’s stingray-tipped spear—and do not establish the bow’s fate.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Did the object physically exist?

**Finding:** unestablished.

Evidence favoring a possible real-world substrate:

- horn-containing and reflexed bows were technologically possible in the ancient eastern Mediterranean;
- the poem includes mechanically suggestive details: horn, case, curvature, inspection, heating, greasing, and a distinctive stringing procedure;
- bows were perishable objects rarely preserved in Greece.

Evidence against identifying a historical specimen:

- every custody link belongs to heroic narrative;
- no inscription, inventory, archaeological context, or independently dated record identifies it;
- neither Odysseus nor the named transfers are historically documented participants in an object provenance;
- precise technical readings of poetic adjectives are disputed;
- ancient vase paintings postdate the poem’s likely composition and are receptions of the story, not portraits of an authenticated object.

The appropriate classification is **literary/legendary weapon with possible observation of real archery technology behind its description**.

### Is any extant bow claimed as the original?

No credible museum, archaeological service, shrine, or scholarly publication identifies a surviving bow as Odysseus’s. There is:

- no accession number;
- no relic shrine;
- no recognized Ithacan candidate;
- no radiocarbon date;
- no materials analysis;
- no DNA or species identification;
- no conservation history;
- no chain of provenance from antiquity.

Commercial replicas and screen props are modern representations and do not claim evidentiary identity.

### Odysseus’s bow versus Heracles’s bow

This is the most persistent identity confusion.

- **Odysseus’s bow:** Eurytus → Iphitus → Odysseus in *Odyssey* 21.
- **Heracles’s bow:** passed to Poeas or Philoctetes in later traditions and essential to the capture of Troy.

Heracles kills Iphitus, but Homer says Iphitus had already given the Eurytid bow to Odysseus. Collapsing the two weapons produces an internally unnecessary duplicate ownership and is not supported by Book 21.

### Was the bow magical?

Homer does not call it enchanted, divinely forged, sentient, unbreakable, or restricted by a supernatural ownership spell. Its exceptional character rests on:

- provenance from a renowned archer;
- emotional value as a guest-gift;
- unusual power;
- the strength and expertise required to string it;
- its responsiveness to its rightful user;
- divine signs surrounding its use.

Athena motivates Penelope’s decision and Zeus thunders after the string sounds, but neither god magically strings the weapon. Telemachus nearly succeeds. “Only its true owner can string it” is therefore a later simplifying formula, not the poem’s literal rule.

### Were the arrows poisoned?

*Odyssey* 1.255–266 says Odysseus sought a man-killing drug from Ilus of Ephyra for his bronze-tipped arrows. Ilus refused from fear of the gods; Anchialus of Taphos supplied it because of friendship.

It is possible that this anticipates Odysseus’s later archery. Nevertheless:

- Book 21 does not say the stored arrows are poisoned.
- Book 22 explains deaths through penetrative wounds.
- The poison-seeking episode need not refer to the same batch of arrows.
- Ancient scholia reportedly connected the poison with the suitors’ rapid deaths, but modern scholarship has rejected that as unnecessary to the narrative physiology.

The arrows used in the hall **cannot securely be labeled poisoned**.

### What did the arrow pass through?

The Greek repeatedly speaks of shooting through twelve **axes**, with the exact physical arrangement underdescribed. Major reconstructions include:

1. through aligned haft-sockets in axeheads;
2. through rings or loops on axes;
3. through openings in double-axe heads;
4. past or between aligned axe blades;
5. an intentionally marvelous or mechanically imprecise poetic feat.

Jean Bérard argued that ancient commentators saw no difficulty and reconstructed an intelligible apparatus. The Ashmolean Museum uses perforated Bronze Age double axes to illustrate one possible solution. Critics respond that known openings may be too small for a fletched arrow to traverse twelve separated targets and that the poem calls the targets iron, whereas many comparative double axes are bronze.

No archaeological consensus identifies a single apparatus as “the” Homeric arrangement.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Guest-gift and memorial

The bow’s first explicit function in Odysseus’s life is commemorative. It materializes the new *xeinía* between him and Iphitus. Because Heracles kills Iphitus before reciprocal table fellowship occurs, the weapon becomes the surviving token of a friendship violently interrupted.

### Household treasure

Its location among gold, bronze, worked iron, and stored textiles marks it as a *keimḗlion*: a valuable laid away, carrying biography and status beyond immediate utility.

### Marriage contest

Penelope makes possession and competent operation of the bow the qualification for replacing Odysseus. The test has two stages:

1. string the bow;
2. shoot through twelve aligned axes.

This is not documented as an Ithacan historical marriage ritual. Within the poem, it is modeled on Odysseus’s prior household feat and serves as an impossible or nearly impossible criterion.

### Instrument of recognition

The bow does not provide recognition through a visible label. It verifies identity through a sequence:

- intimate inspection;
- knowledge of its construction;
- effortless stringing;
- characteristic musical sound;
- exact shot;
- successful combat.

The suitors recognize superiority before they fully understand identity. The object thus reveals Odysseus performatively.

### Sovereignty and household restoration

The man capable of operating the absent king’s weapon proves capable of reclaiming his place. The bow simultaneously decides:

- who may claim Penelope;
- who commands the hall;
- who controls violence inside the palace;
- who is master of the household’s stored property.

Its transfer from Penelope through Eumaeus to Odysseus materially realigns the household around its former ruler.

### Apollo, sacrifice, and divine legitimation

Antinous proposes postponing the contest and sacrificing goats to Apollo, famed for the bow. Odysseus invokes Apollo before shooting Antinous. Zeus answers the sounding string with thunder. These are religious framings of action, but the bow is never itself worshipped, dedicated, or called sacred.

### Musical symbolism

The stringing simile assimilates archer to singer. Odysseus handles the bow as an expert singer handles the lyre, and the weapon produces a swallow-like note. The scene links control of lethal force with technical and musical mastery; the epic’s hero momentarily resembles the epic performer.

### Access rules

No formal taboo is stated. Practical access is nevertheless tightly controlled:

- the bow is kept in a locked inner room;
- Penelope holds or can obtain the key;
- servants transport it under command;
- the suitors debate who may touch it;
- doors are locked as it reaches Odysseus.

The control is household and strategic rather than priestly.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The Eurytus–Iphitus–Odysseus lineage

**Tier:** earliest attested narrative.

Eurytus bears the bow; Iphitus inherits it; Iphitus gives it to Odysseus during a meeting generated by two property disputes. This lineage supplies heroic age, inherited excellence, and a guest-friendship biography.

### Heracles and the murdered donor

**Tier:** earliest attested narrative.

Iphitus searches for his mares and encounters Heracles. Heracles kills him despite receiving him at his table and retains the mares. The bow survives the murder only because Iphitus has already transferred it. The object therefore becomes, without supernatural agency, the memorial that escapes its donor’s killer.

### Eurytus challenges Apollo

**Tier:** Homeric background myth.

Eurytus’s archery is so exceptional that he contests divine superiority; Apollo kills him. Later accounts enlarge Eurytus’s archery contest and his daughter Iole’s marriage, but they do not give a reliable new material history of Odysseus’s particular bow.

### The unstringable bow

**Tier:** Homeric narrative, later simplified.

In the *Odyssey*, several men fail; Telemachus nearly succeeds; Odysseus succeeds easily. Later retellings often convert comparative difficulty into an absolute spell: “only Odysseus,” “only the rightful king,” or “only the true owner” can string it. Those formulas sharpen the recognition motif but are not Homer’s explicit mechanics.

### Divine gift from Apollo

**Tier:** later or conflated tradition.

Modern myth summaries sometimes extend Apollo’s connection with Eurytus into a claim that Apollo made or gave this exact bow. The *Odyssey* does not say so. Any such statement requires a separately cited later source and should not be retrojected into Homer.

### The bow as a disguised key

**Tier:** modern critical interpretation.

The weapon is often described metaphorically as the “key” to Odysseus’s identity or kingship. This is a persuasive reading of its function, not an ancient name or supernatural property.

### Poisoned massacre

**Tier:** ancient commentary and modern debate.

Because Book 1 mentions Odysseus acquiring arrow poison, some interpreters connect it to Book 22. The connection is possible at the level of narrative foreshadowing but materially unproved. The slaughter scene does not mark the arrows as coated, and the wounds described are sufficient to kill without poison.

### The “impossible” axe shot

**Tier:** Homeric feat with disputed reconstruction.

The feat has attracted experimental archery, archaeological comparison, geometric reconstruction, and popular demonstrations. None can authenticate the original arrangement because the text is sparse and no associated equipment survives. Claims that the arrow punctured twelve solid iron blades are generally based on visual or verbal exaggeration, not the clearest reading of the Greek.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Original object

**Status:** no physical existence independently established; present material location unknown and probably inapplicable.

There is no evidence that:

- an original survived antiquity;
- ancient or medieval custodians claimed to possess it;
- it was excavated;
- it was destroyed in a documented event;
- fragments, casts, or measurements survive.

“Lost” should therefore be used cautiously. A historically documented artifact can be lost; this bow is first of all **unverified as a historical artifact**.

### Textual location

Its surviving identity resides chiefly in the manuscript and print tradition of the *Odyssey*, especially:

- 1.255–266: poison sought for Odysseus’s arrows;
- 8.215–228: Odysseus’s archery and Eurytus’s death;
- 19.570–581: Penelope announces the contest;
- 21.1–434: provenance, removal, attempts, stringing, and axe shot;
- 22.1 onward: use against the suitors.

The Greek text itself passed through oral performance, rhapsodic transmission, Hellenistic editorial scholarship, papyri, medieval manuscripts, printed editions, and modern critical editions. That is the document’s provenance, not the weapon’s physical provenance.

### Real objects presently standing in for it

Museums preserve ancient representations, not the bow:

- **Berlin, Antikensammlung, F 2588:** Attic red-figure skyphos attributed to the Penelope Painter, about 440 BCE, showing Odysseus shooting at the suitors.
- **Metropolitan Museum of Art, 1990.11.1:** Attic red-figure oinochoe attributed to the Disney Painter, about 450–440 BCE; its unkempt archer is plausibly identified as Odysseus attacking the suitors.
- **Philadelphia Museum of Art, 2012-130-1:** N. C. Wyeth, *The Trial of the Bow* (1929), a modern reconstruction of the decisive moment.
- Numerous modern theatrical bows, replicas, and film props visualize the object without claiming authenticity.

No current legal, ownership, or repatriation dispute over an “original bow of Odysseus” is documented.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Renaissance painting and print

Pintoricchio’s **Penelope with the Suitors**, approximately 1509, now National Gallery NG911, shows the bow and quiver hanging within Penelope’s domestic interior. The painting translates the stored weapon into Renaissance household imagery.

Francesco Primaticcio represented the contest and slaughter in the Galerie d’Ulysse at Fontainebleau, approximately 1555–1559. The gallery was destroyed in 1738–1739, but copies and engravings preserve portions of its imagery. The represented bow is therefore part of a lost artwork’s secondary survival, not a record of an ancient object.

### N. C. Wyeth

Wyeth painted *The Trial of the Bow* in 1929 for George Herbert Palmer’s edition of the *Odyssey*. The painting emphasizes a monumental, rigid bow and the tunnel-like alignment of axeheads. Long thought missing, it was found in the Philadelphia offices of GlaxoSmithKline and entered the Philadelphia Museum of Art as accession 2012-130-1. Its own recovery history is sometimes playfully compared with Odysseus’s homecoming.

### Film and television

Modern adaptations must choose a physical design where Homer leaves uncertainty. Their bows vary between self bows, recurved bows, and heavily reflexed composite-looking props.

Notable treatments include:

- *Ulysses* (1954), directed by Mario Camerini;
- the television miniseries *The Odyssey* (1997), directed by Andrei Konchalovsky;
- *The Return* (2024), directed by Uberto Pasolini, which strips away much divine machinery and makes the old bow central to an aging veteran’s bodily recovery and violent return;
- Christopher Nolan’s *The Odyssey* (2026), which retained the contest while giving the string’s pluck a marked role in the film’s sound design and score.

These props possess their own production histories but are not replicas in an archaeological sense.

### Music

Jorge Rivera-Herrans’s concept album **EPIC: The Musical** culminated in the *Ithaca Saga* in 2024. “The Challenge” turns Penelope’s announcement about stringing her husband’s bow into a recurring musical and dramatic test. Online discussion surrounding the work has helped popularize claims that the bow is a rare “palintonos recurve,” that the suitors hold it backward, or that only an owner of Odysseus’s bloodline can string it. Those propositions should be separated from the Homeric text.

### Games and commercial imagery

Games inspired by Greek mythology commonly give “Odysseus” or “Ulysses” named bows magical statistics, poison effects, or legendary rarity. *Assassin’s Creed Odyssey* includes legendary bows and poison-arrow mechanics, but its “Fanged Bow” is not presented by the ancient source as Odysseus’s historical weapon. Such designs often merge three separate Homeric elements:

- Odysseus’s bow;
- his search for arrow poison;
- the supernatural weapon conventions of role-playing games.

### Popular reconstruction and misinformation

Recurring modern claims include:

- the bow was exactly seven feet long;
- it required two men to string;
- it was Scythian;
- it was made by Apollo;
- only a descendant of Hermes could use it;
- the suitors failed solely because they held it backward;
- the shot bored through twelve solid iron blades.

None is established by Homer. Some are imaginative reconstructions; others are demonstrably stronger than the evidence.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Textual interpretation

The object’s study begins with ancient Homeric scholarship. Scholia and, later, Eustathius of Thessalonica commented on the contest, the string’s sound, and the target arrangement. Modern editions inherit a text shaped by Hellenistic criticism and manuscript transmission.

The principal textual uncertainties do not erase the chain of custody, which is unusually explicit, but they affect physical reconstruction:

- scope of *palíntonos*;
- meaning of the verbs for bending/stringing;
- whether “horns” implies a composite bow;
- form and spacing of the axes;
- whether the stringing simile supplies actual material information.

### Henry Balfour and technological diagnosis

Henry Balfour’s 1921 study, “The Archer’s Bow in the Homeric Poems,” became foundational for the composite-bow interpretation. The argument correlates the poem with Near Eastern reflex composite bows:

- horn construction;
- reverse resting curvature;
- specialized stringing;
- bow-case storage;
- vulnerability to insects and damp;
- use of heat and fat.

The reconstruction has been influential but criticized for treating poetry as a technical specification and for selecting archaeological comparanda that may not match either the imagined heroic period or the poem’s composition date.

### William McLeod and archery in Homer

William E. McLeod’s work on the bow in Greece placed Homeric descriptions within broader archaeological and iconographic evidence. Later scholarship generally accepts that Greek poets knew multiple bow forms but remains cautious about assigning a unique ethnographic type to Odysseus’s weapon.

### The axe contest

The target problem has produced an unusually long literature:

- early archaeological diagrams;
- experiments with aligned sockets or rings;
- comparison with Bronze Age double axes;
- Jean Bérard’s 1955 reconstruction;
- arguments that no known aperture permits the clean trajectory described;
- readings emphasizing poetic impossibility rather than reconstructable sport.

The Ashmolean’s public interpretation favors perforated double axes as an intelligible Bronze Age survival. Other specialists note that the poem explicitly calls the axes iron and that neither metallurgy nor hole size neatly resolves the problem.

### Narrative and symbolic studies

Recent interpretation treats the bow as a “significant object” whose biography gathers together:

- broken and restored guest-friendship;
- household memory;
- Penelope’s agency;
- Telemachus’s maturation;
- the distinction between rightful use and consumption by the suitors;
- singer-like expertise;
- recognition and restored sovereignty.

Richard Ready’s “Why Odysseus Strings His Bow” stresses the prized object’s circulation and the significance of its being stored as a treasure. Studies of para-narratives identify the Book 1 poison story as prospective framing for the revenge.

### Oral tradition and historical inference

Because the *Odyssey* is the product of oral-formulaic composition and later textual stabilization, a detail may preserve:

- old material culture;
- an early Iron Age practice;
- inherited formula;
- narrative fantasy;
- or a recombination of all four.

It is therefore unsound to assign the bow automatically to a single archaeological century.

### State of the evidence

Available evidence:

- Greek text of the *Odyssey*;
- ancient scholia and Byzantine commentary;
- later ancient mythographical references to Eurytus, Iphitus, and Odysseus;
- ancient representations of the slaughter;
- comparative bows, bow imagery, and axeheads;
- modern experimental and mechanical reconstructions.

Missing evidence:

- the object;
- an ancient dedicatory or ownership inscription;
- an independent inventory;
- a find context;
- measurements or material samples;
- an ancient representation explicitly labeled as the inherited bow during its transfer;
- any post-*Odyssey* physical custody record.
