# The Hanging Gardens of Babylon: Object Dossier

## Dossier scope and evidentiary finding

**Object class:** legendary or lost monumental garden complex; traditionally one of the Seven Wonders of the ancient world.

**Central finding:** no physical structure can presently be authenticated as the Hanging Gardens of Babylon. No surviving Neo-Babylonian inscription names such a monument, no excavated remains at Babylon have been securely identified with it, and no ancient witness whose account survives can be shown to have seen it. The dossier therefore follows three related but non-identical objects:

1. **The literary Hanging Garden:** the terraced, mechanically irrigated royal garden described in Greek and Latin texts.
2. **The Babylon candidate:** a hypothetical garden attached to Nebuchadnezzar II’s palace in Babylon, conventionally dated to the sixth century BCE.
3. **The Nineveh candidate:** Sennacherib’s archaeologically and epigraphically attested palace garden and waterworks at Nineveh, built in the early seventh century BCE and proposed by Stephanie Dalley as the physical monument behind the classical descriptions.

These identities must not be collapsed. A magnificent garden certainly existed at Nineveh. Royal gardens existed in Babylonia. What has not been demonstrated is that either was the monument later called the Hanging Gardens of Babylon.

**Evidence labels used below:**

- **Documented:** directly supported by an inscription, excavated context, manuscript, or institutional record.
- **Ancient literary report:** stated by an ancient writer but not independently verified.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** an inference from several bodies of evidence.
- **Traditional attribution:** a culturally persistent claim whose original evidence is incomplete.
- **Disputed:** actively contested by specialists.
- **Legend or modern image:** narrative or visualization unsupported as a literal record of the monument.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Identity and names

The familiar English name is **the Hanging Gardens of Babylon**. “Hanging” does not mean plants dangling in baskets. It represents Greek language describing a garden raised or suspended above ordinary ground:

- **Greek:** ὁ κρεμαστὸς κῆπος, *ho kremastòs kêpos*, “the suspended/elevated garden.” The adjective *kremastós* can mean hanging, overhanging, suspended, or raised.
- **Greek poetic expression:** κάπων αἰώρημα, *kápōn aiṓrēma*, approximately “the suspension/elevation of gardens,” in the epigram transmitted under Antipater’s name.
- **Latin:** *horti pensiles*, “suspended” or “overhanging gardens,” the conventional Latin formulation associated with Roman transmission.
- **German:** *Die Hängenden Gärten von Babylon* or *die Hängenden Gärten der Semiramis*.
- **French:** *les Jardins suspendus de Babylone*.
- **Arabic:** حدائق بابل المعلقة, *Ḥadāʾiq Bābil al-Muʿallaqa*, “the suspended gardens of Babylon.”
- **Russian:** Висячие сады Семирамиды, *Visjačie sady Semiramidy*, “the Hanging Gardens of Semiramis.”

No demonstrated Akkadian name for the monument survives. This absence is consequential: Babylonian topographical and royal building texts name many temples, walls, palaces, streets, gates, and waterworks, but none can securely be translated “Hanging Garden.”

“Babylon” derives through Greek Βαβυλών, *Babylṓn*, from Akkadian *Bāb-ilī*, conventionally “Gate of the God(s),” also written logographically KÁ.DINGIR.RA. Dalley has emphasized evidence that “Babylon” or analogous sacred-city terminology could be applied beyond the southern capital, potentially assisting the Nineveh identification. Critics answer that this does not demonstrate that Greek writers actually transferred this particular monument from Nineveh to Babylon.

The alternate title **Gardens of Semiramis** is a later attributional tradition. The historical Assyrian queen Sammu-rāmat, active in the ninth century BCE, became the “Semiramis” of Greek legend, to whom numerous Asian buildings were assigned. Diodorus explicitly says the Hanging Garden was *not* built by Semiramis, despite placing it inside a long account dominated by her supposed works.

### 1.2 No extant monument

There is no surviving garden, ruin, fragment, inscription, foundation deposit, maker’s mark, or museum accession number that can be catalogued as the authenticated Hanging Gardens. Consequently:

- dimensions are literary, not measured;
- materials are reported or reconstructed, not sampled from the monument;
- no radiocarbon, dendrochronological, botanical, residue, mortar, or materials analysis has ever been performed on an authenticated component;
- no inscription from the claimed object exists;
- no documented fragment was removed to a museum;
- no scientific continuity test can connect a modern ruin with the classical monument.

### 1.3 Physical descriptions in the ancient sources

#### Berossus as transmitted by Josephus, first century CE, claiming a source of about 290 BCE

Josephus quotes or paraphrases the lost *Babyloniaca* of the Babylonian priest Berossus. Nebuchadnezzar is said to have built a new palace in fifteen days, erected high stone terraces within it, planted them with trees, and made them resemble mountains for a wife raised in Media who desired the landscape of her homeland. Josephus’ German text of *Against Apion* 1.141 renders the essential statement as high stone terraces planted with every kind of tree, producing the appearance of natural mountains.

This is the only major transmission that both names **Nebuchadnezzar** and embeds the garden in his palace-building program. It is not, however, a manuscript of Berossus. The chain is Berossus → later epitomists or excerptors, notably Alexander Polyhistor → Josephus. Whether the garden sentence belonged to Berossus exactly as transmitted is disputed because the preceding building catalogue closely follows a known Nebuchadnezzar inscription while the Hanging Garden clause has no equivalent in that inscription.

#### Diodorus Siculus, *Library of History* 2.10, mid-first century BCE

Diodorus provides the most detailed architectural description. He says:

- the garden stood “beside the acropolis”;
- it was built not by Semiramis but by a later “Syrian king” for a Persian concubine homesick for mountain meadows;
- the plan was square, four plethra on each side, roughly 120 metres per side if an Attic plethron of about 30 metres is assumed;
- its approach rose like a hillside;
- successive parts stood tier above tier, resembling a theatre;
- galleries beneath carried the planted surface;
- the highest gallery rose fifty cubits, approximately 22–26 metres depending on the cubit used;
- supporting walls were twenty-two feet thick, with ten-foot passages between them;
- the roofs used stone beams, sixteen feet long and four feet wide;
- above them lay reeds in bitumen, two courses of baked brick set in cement, a lead moisture barrier, and deep soil;
- large ornamental trees grew in that soil;
- projecting galleries received daylight and included royal rooms;
- hidden machines raised abundant water from the river.

The specification is technically coherent as a description of waterproofed terraces, but it remains a literary report composed centuries after the alleged construction. Some measurements or materials may derive from descriptions of other Near Eastern monumental buildings.

#### Strabo, *Geography* 16.1.5, composed around the turn of the Common Era

Strabo likewise gives a square of four plethra per side. It comprised vaulted terraces rising above one another on cube-like hollow supports filled with earth so that large trees could grow. The pillars, vaults, and terraces were made of baked brick and asphalt. Stairs led upward, accompanied by continuously operated water-lifting machines drawing from the Euphrates.

Strabo and Diodorus agree on the square dimensions, terracing, vaults, deep soil, large trees, and mechanical irrigation, suggesting a shared earlier tradition. Their structural details disagree in part: Diodorus emphasizes stone beams, reeds, brick, bitumen, and lead; Strabo describes baked-brick and asphalt vaults and pillars.

The traditional translation “Archimedes screws” is too definite. Strabo calls them water-raising devices; manuscript interpretation permits screw-like machinery, but he neither attributes them to Archimedes nor supplies a technical drawing.

#### Quintus Curtius Rufus, *History of Alexander* 5.1.32–35, probably first century CE

Curtius locates the gardens on or near Babylon’s citadel. They rose as high as the walls and resembled woods overhanging their native mountains. Rock-built columns supported squared-stone platforms carrying deep soil and irrigation water. He reports trees with trunks eight cubits thick and heights of fifty feet, claims the structure still survived in his own narrative present, and attributes it to a “Syrian king” acting for a wife who missed wooded country.

Curtius’ enormous trunk dimension should not be converted uncritically into botanical data. It may refer to circumference, reproduce exaggeration, or be textually corrupt.

#### Pseudo-Philo, *On the Seven Wonders*, surviving in a medieval manuscript

The work long attributed to the Hellenistic engineer Philo of Byzantium is now frequently treated as a late-antique rhetorical composition, although it may preserve older material. Its manuscript basis includes Heidelberg, Palatinus graecus 398, and a dependent thirteenth-century copy.

Pseudo-Philo describes:

- plants apparently suspended in air;
- stone columns on a common foundation;
- closely spaced palm-wood beams;
- a deep mass of earth;
- broad-leaved trees and numerous flowers;
- land ploughed above the heads of people walking between columns;
- elevated reservoirs;
- water forced upward through twisting or spiral mechanical arrangements;
- continuous root moisture and perennial greenery.

Unlike Strabo, Diodorus, Curtius, and Josephus, the surviving garden chapter does **not name Babylon**. The same treatise separately attributes Babylon’s walls to Semiramis. That separation has become important in Dalley’s argument that the garden’s original location could have been elsewhere.

### 1.4 Composite description and its limits

The classical composite is a large, square or broadly quadrangular, terraced park built over inhabited or accessible vaulted substructures. Its principal materials were said to be stone, baked brick, bitumen, reeds or palm wood, lead, deep soil, and water. Large trees, flowers, grasses, and possibly fruit trees made an artificial mountain. Human-powered machines continuously elevated river water.

No source provides all these features simultaneously. The familiar modern image—a steep wedding-cake pyramid covered in trailing vines, waterfalls, domes, and exotic women—is chiefly post-antique reconstruction.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Traditional Babylonian construction

The standard tradition assigns the monument to **Nebuchadnezzar II**, king of Babylon from 605 to 562 BCE, usually for **Amytis**, conventionally described as a Median princess who missed her mountainous homeland.

The evidence is weaker than the familiarity of this story suggests:

- Nebuchadnezzar’s kingship and massive rebuilding of Babylon are documented.
- His palaces, fortifications, temples, streets, brick inscriptions, and other works are archaeologically and epigraphically attested.
- No surviving contemporary text states that he built the Hanging Gardens.
- The queen’s name is not supplied in Josephus’ garden sentence.
- Amytis’ identity and marriage are reconstructed from later historical traditions; she is not securely documented as Nebuchadnezzar’s queen in surviving contemporary Babylonian royal records.
- Diodorus calls the woman a Persian concubine; Curtius a wife; Berossus/Josephus a wife raised in Media.
- Diodorus and Curtius call the builder a Syrian king, an elastic classical term that can encompass Assyrian or Mesopotamian rulers.

Thus “Nebuchadnezzar built the Gardens for Amytis” is a **traditional synthesis**, not one primary source’s complete statement.

### 2.2 Earliest reconstructable textual attestation

The earliest potentially datable attestation is the lost work of **Berossus**, normally placed around 290 BCE. It survives only through writers centuries later. Berossus was a Babylonian priest writing in Greek under Seleucid rule and may have consulted cuneiform records. This gives his testimony potential weight, but not immunity from interpolation, adaptation, or literary borrowing.

A lost account by **Cleitarchus**, an early Hellenistic historian of Alexander, may also underlie Diodorus and Curtius. Cleitarchus wrote after Alexander’s campaigns but was not necessarily an eyewitness. Some scholars regard his Alexander history as the major source from which the elaborate garden developed.

The earliest surviving, directly inspectable reference may be the epigram transmitted under **Antipater**, probably of the second or first century BCE. It lists the *kápōn aiṓrēma*, “suspension of gardens,” among world marvels but does not itself state their location. It therefore proves that an elevated-garden wonder circulated in Hellenistic literature, not that the structure stood at Babylon.

### 2.3 Significant silence

Herodotus, writing in the fifth century BCE and offering an extended description of Babylon, does not mention the gardens. This silence is not proof of nonexistence: Herodotus’ knowledge of Babylon is itself disputed, and he omitted or misunderstood other features. It nevertheless matters because his text is earlier than every extant garden account.

The absence is more striking in:

- Nebuchadnezzar II’s extensive building inscriptions;
- Babylonian topographical texts, including the tradition represented by *TINTIR = Babylon*;
- excavated administrative and architectural records from the palace;
- securely identified Babylonian visual representations.

The French Ministry of Culture’s archaeological presentation states plainly that Babylonian archaeological and textual sources provide no proof that the gardens existed at Babylon.

### 2.4 The Nineveh construction

Sennacherib, king of Assyria from 704 to 681 BCE, transformed Nineveh into his capital. This program is documented by his own inscriptions and archaeology. It included:

- a vast royal palace;
- orchards and botanical collections;
- a park designed to reproduce varied landscapes;
- canals drawing water from distant uplands;
- aqueducts, including the monumental stone aqueduct at Jerwan;
- installations intended to provide Nineveh with a reliable flow of water;
- technological claims involving cast bronze and water lifting.

Sennacherib described a garden near his palace as resembling the Amanus mountain region and planted it with fruit trees, aromatics, and vegetation from different lands. This is contemporary documentation of a monumental irrigated royal landscape, but no surviving inscription calls it the “Hanging Garden.”

Dalley’s reconstruction identifies this complex as the wonder later misplaced at Babylon. Its strongest supports are:

- the exact existence at Nineveh of a monumental royal garden and extraordinary waterworks;
- mountain-imitation language comparable to the classical homesickness narrative;
- Assyrian palace reliefs showing elevated or hillside gardens, channels, and aqueducts;
- the possibility that Pseudo-Philo’s spiral water mechanism reflects Sennacherib’s technological language;
- classical confusion between Assyria and Babylonia;
- Pseudo-Philo’s failure to state the garden’s location.

Its principal weaknesses are:

- no ancient text explicitly says “the Hanging Garden of Babylon was at Nineveh”;
- the relevant garden reliefs may depict gardens under Ashurbanipal rather than provide a portrait of Sennacherib’s exact complex;
- Sennacherib’s technological text is difficult and does not indisputably describe an Archimedean screw;
- the Greek accounts explicitly connect their garden with the Euphrates, Babylon, or Babylon’s acropolis;
- a transmission mechanism relocating Nineveh’s monument has been proposed but not documented;
- Nineveh’s gardens and Babylon’s legendary garden could have been distinct.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because the purported object was immovable architecture, it did not pass through private hands like a relic or jewel. “Custody” here means construction authority, royal use, political control of its site, abandonment, excavation, restoration, and the later attachment of its identity to candidate ruins.

### 3.1 Traditional Babylon chain

#### c. 605–562 BCE: Nebuchadnezzar II

**Traditional attribution:** construction inside or beside a royal palace at Babylon. Josephus’ Berossus fragment assigns high planted terraces to Nebuchadnezzar. No contemporary Babylonian confirmation survives.

**Purported purpose:** to reproduce Median or Persian mountain scenery for the king’s wife or concubine.

**Purported materials and labor:** monumental masonry, waterproofed terraces, soil, trees, and a water-lifting installation. No contract, workforce list, cost statement, foundation deposit, or botanical inventory has been identified with the project.

#### After 562 BCE: Neo-Babylonian successors

No source records maintenance, transfer, alteration, or royal use. If the monument existed, it would have passed with the palace successively to Amel-Marduk, Neriglissar, Labashi-Marduk, and Nabonidus. This is political succession, not an individually documented custody chain.

#### 539 BCE: Achaemenid conquest

Cyrus II took Babylon. No source records seizure, destruction, or repair of the garden. The city and palaces continued under Persian rule. Diodorus reports Persian kings carrying away golden temple objects, but this passage does not say they dismantled the garden.

#### 331–323 BCE: Alexander and the Macedonian court

Alexander entered Babylon in 331 BCE and died there in 323 BCE. Later writers placed episodes of his final illness in royal or garden environments, but the surviving reliable evidence does not establish that Alexander visited the Hanging Gardens. Julian Reade has explored whether Alexander-period observation and storytelling helped transmit the tradition. That remains a reconstruction, not a custody document.

#### Seleucid and Parthian periods

Babylon declined gradually rather than in a single documented catastrophe. There is no dated record of the garden’s destruction. Statements that it was destroyed by an earthquake in a particular century, especially the second century BCE, circulate widely but lack a secure ancient source tied to the object.

Strabo’s sentence about a monument being “in ruins” and demolished by Xerxes refers in context to the tomb or temple of Belus, not securely to the Hanging Garden. It should not be used as a destruction certificate for the garden.

#### First century BCE–first century CE

Diodorus writes of Babylon’s broader decay while describing the garden in the past tense. Curtius’ rhetoric can be read as claiming the garden survived in his day, although his work dramatizes Alexander’s fourth-century-BCE world and the statement is not an archaeological inspection report. The sources do not establish a terminal date.

#### Late antiquity to the nineteenth century

The monument disappears as a physically visitable structure. Its chain becomes literary: manuscripts, epitomes, geographical works, wonder lists, and illustrated reconstructions transmit the name. No medieval traveler documents custody of recognizable ruins.

### 3.2 Babylon candidate ruins: modern custody

#### 1899–1917: German excavations

The Deutsche Orient-Gesellschaft and the Royal Museums of Berlin excavated Babylon under Robert Koldewey. Work in the Kasr or palace area began around 1900.

Koldewey identified a group of vaulted rooms in the northeastern part of Nebuchadnezzar’s Southern Palace as the substructure of the Hanging Gardens. His case emphasized:

- unusually massive walls;
- evidence of hewn stone, uncommon in Babylonian architecture;
- vaulting capable of bearing weight;
- a distinctive installation interpreted as a well with three adjoining shafts;
- comparison with classical descriptions of stone supports and water raising.

The excavated footprint was much smaller than Diodorus’ and Strabo’s four-plethron square. Its distance from the Euphrates and its integration into the palace service quarter also caused difficulty.

Subsequent interpretation of hundreds of tablets found in the area favors an administrative, storage, or ration-distribution function. The vaulted building remains real; its identity as the Gardens is widely discounted.

#### 1930s–1970s: Iraqi antiquities administration

The Babylon site came under Iraqi antiquities authority. Two palace arches were reconstructed in 1938. From the late 1950s, Iraqi authorities re-excavated and restored portions of Babylon for research and public presentation.

Beginning in 1963, the northeastern Southern Palace area identified by Koldewey with the Gardens was **partially reconstructed**. Those modern walls are not surviving garden architecture and should not be presented as an authenticated reconstruction.

#### 1980s: Revival of Babylon project

Saddam Hussein’s government undertook extensive reconstruction directly over ancient foundations. The Southern Palace was heavily rebuilt, often rapidly and with modern materials. Surviving documentation is incomplete; relevant archives were looted or destroyed after the regime fell in 2003.

These works changed the physical context in which Koldewey’s candidate is seen. They did not authenticate it. Rather, they introduced modern fabric above ancient remains and complicated later archaeological interpretation.

#### 2003–present

Military occupation, traffic, erosion, groundwater, salinization, poorly controlled construction, and earlier rebuilding damaged Babylon’s archaeological landscape. The precise Hanging Garden candidate was not stolen or removed, but its surrounding site suffered.

Babylon was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2019. Iraqi Antiquities and Heritage Law No. 55 of 2002 governs the property. UNESCO’s listing recognizes Babylon’s archaeological and symbolic importance while acknowledging continuing debate over the location and even existence of the Hanging Gardens.

### 3.3 Nineveh candidate chain

#### c. 700 BCE: Sennacherib

**Documented:** Sennacherib commissioned the palace, garden, canals, and aqueduct system at Nineveh. The Jerwan aqueduct and royal inscriptions belong to this program.

#### 681–612 BCE: Assyrian successors

Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal inherited Nineveh’s royal landscape. Ashurbanipal’s North Palace reliefs depict irrigated orchards, gardens, and aqueducts. One British Museum panel, registration **1856,0909.36 (BM 124939)**, is gypsum, about 208.3 × 129.54 × 18 cm, and shows a hillside landscape watered by an aqueduct probably associated with Sennacherib’s network. Its companion is BM 124940.

These panels are evidence for Assyrian royal garden imagery, not fragments of the garden itself.

#### 612 BCE: fall of Nineveh

Nineveh was conquered by a Median-Babylonian coalition. Palaces were burned and royal power ended. The degree and timing of destruction across the waterworks and garden landscape are not fully known. A living garden requiring intensive maintenance probably declined rapidly after the political and hydraulic system failed, but that is a practical inference.

#### Nineteenth century: excavation and dispersal

Austen Henry Layard and successors excavated Nineveh. Palace reliefs were removed to the British Museum and other collections under nineteenth-century Ottoman-era excavation and division practices. Some relevant relief imagery survives only in drawings; its exact architectural context and relation to Sennacherib’s garden remain disputed.

#### Twentieth–twenty-first centuries

The garden landscape itself has not been excavated as a single, bounded monument corresponding to classical dimensions. Sennacherib’s canal network and the Jerwan aqueduct remain in northern Iraq. The palace mounds of Nineveh have suffered urban encroachment, conflict, looting, erosion, and destruction associated with ISIS occupation of Mosul. The portable reliefs in London remain in British Museum custody.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Was there a Babylonian Hanging Garden?

#### Strongest evidence in favor

- Berossus was a Babylonian priest relatively close in time to the Neo-Babylonian dynasty.
- Josephus’ transmission explicitly links Nebuchadnezzar to high, tree-planted palace terraces.
- Diodorus, Strabo, and Curtius preserve mutually overlapping architectural traditions.
- Babylonian and Assyrian engineers demonstrably possessed the masonry, waterproofing, canal, and lifting technologies necessary for large irrigated gardens.
- Royal gardens were normal instruments of Mesopotamian kingship.
- Babylon’s river course shifted, and unexcavated or destroyed riverside areas could conceal or have erased a garden.
- Absence from surviving royal inscriptions is important but not conclusive.

#### Strongest evidence against

- No contemporary Babylonian inscription names it.
- No securely identified archaeological remains correspond to it.
- Herodotus does not mention it.
- The major accounts are late and interdependent.
- Their stereotyped “eastern despot,” Persian woman, mountain homesickness, and invisible machinery motifs may be Hellenistic literary construction.
- Measurements in the classical sources exceed or conflict with excavated palace candidates.
- Berossus’ transmitted clause may have been supplemented during epitomization.

**Assessment:** existence at Babylon remains unproved. It is too strong to call the garden demonstrably fictitious, but equally unwarranted to describe it as an excavated monument.

### 4.2 Koldewey’s vaulted building

#### Case for

Massive walls, vaults, stone remains, and a three-shaft well seemed to match Diodorus’ load-bearing substructure and water machinery. Its palace location matched Berossus.

#### Case against

The building is too small; it lies awkwardly far from the ancient river; its chambers and tablet archive indicate administrative or storage use; the well interpretation is not exclusive; and no planted surface, root pits, irrigation channels, waterproof garden layer, pollen assemblage, or dedicatory inscription confirms horticulture.

**Assessment:** real Neo-Babylonian palace architecture, but not authenticated as the Hanging Gardens.

### 4.3 The western or riverside Babylon hypotheses

Julian Reade and others have considered locations nearer the Euphrates, including the Western Outwork and sectors beside the Northern Palace. These better fit the sources’ dependence on river water and monumental scale. Much of the relevant ancient riverfront is altered, inaccessible, eroded, or insufficiently excavated.

**Assessment:** topographically plausible but archaeologically unverified.

### 4.4 The Nineveh hypothesis

Dalley first developed the identification in articles in the 1990s and later in *The Mystery of the Hanging Garden of Babylon* (2013).

#### Case for

Sennacherib’s garden and hydraulic works are contemporary and documented; Nineveh’s reliefs depict elevated irrigated landscapes; the mountain-replica language matches the classical garden’s defining image; Assyrian technical innovation offers a candidate for the water screw; and Greco-Roman authors frequently blurred Assyrian and Babylonian geography.

#### Case against

Reinhold Bichler, Robert Rollinger, and other critics emphasize that similarity does not establish identity. The sources that specify place put the garden at Babylon and beside the Euphrates. No transmission text records the supposed relocation of the name. The bronze mechanism’s translation is contested, and the relief evidence is not a labeled portrait of “the Hanging Garden.” Nineveh may simply possess a different, better-documented royal garden.

**Assessment:** the strongest physical analogue and a serious candidate, not a solved identification.

### 4.5 Pure-literary or composite hypothesis

On this interpretation, Cleitarchus or another early Hellenistic writer combined:

- knowledge of Mesopotamian palace gardens;
- Babylon’s famous walls and palaces;
- Assyrian garden traditions;
- Persian paradeisoi;
- Greek engineering marvels;
- the literary motif of a homesick royal woman.

Later writers inherited and systematized the resulting wonder. Jona Lendering further notes that Josephus’ Berossus passage may have combined authentic Babylonian building material with a Cleitarchan garden story.

**Assessment:** explains the absence of a physical match, but cannot prove that no unusual Babylonian garden existed.

### 4.6 Authority to authenticate

Authenticating authority has shifted:

- **Ancient kings and scribes:** construction inscriptions and royal ideology.
- **Hellenistic and Roman authors:** literary authority and the canon of wonders.
- **Early modern artists and antiquarians:** visual authority through reconstruction.
- **Nineteenth-century excavators:** archaeological identification, exemplified by Koldewey.
- **Modern Assyriologists and archaeologists:** philology, stratigraphy, topography, and comparative hydraulic study.
- **Iraqi heritage authorities and UNESCO:** legal custody and site management, not authentication of the individual wonder.
- **Museums:** authentication of relevant reliefs and inscriptions, not of the legendary identity.

No current institution officially possesses “the original Hanging Gardens.”

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Garden as royal microcosm

No surviving source describes a liturgy, sacrifice, coronation, oath, taboo, priesthood, or public procession performed in the Hanging Gardens. It should not be classified as a temple.

Its reported function was royal:

- pleasure and visual delight;
- accommodation within or beside palace architecture;
- demonstration of dominion over water and fertility;
- artificial reproduction of mountain ecology in an alluvial plain;
- provision of shaded royal rooms;
- display of wealth, engineering, and imported vegetation.

For Mesopotamian kings, the ability to gather plants, animals, waters, and foreign landscapes into a controlled park expressed universal sovereignty. Sennacherib’s inscriptions make this political-natural symbolism explicit at Nineveh. Applying it to a Babylonian Hanging Garden is a plausible comparative inference.

### 6.2 Dynastic affection

The homesick-wife story converts imperial landscape engineering into an act of conjugal devotion. Its earliest secure form belongs to Hellenistic or later literary transmission. No contemporary marriage inscription, queen’s text, or court record confirms it.

The story performed explanatory work: it answered why a flat, hot city possessed an artificial mountain and why its plants supposedly evoked Media or Persia.

### 6.3 Wonder status

The garden’s most consequential ceremonial function was textual rather than liturgical: it became a *theama*, “sight,” or *thauma*, “wonder,” in Greek culture. Inclusion in lists of seven created a canon of elite travel, education, and geographical imagination.

Pseudo-Philo explicitly frames writing as a substitute for travel. The literary Garden could therefore be “seen” by readers after the physical structure—if it existed—had vanished.

### 6.4 Political symbolism

- Classical writers made Babylon a stage for superhuman royal wealth.
- Semiramis’ attachment turned the Garden into proof of legendary female sovereignty.
- Nebuchadnezzar’s attachment joined it to imperial Babylon and, in later Western reception, to biblical images of magnificence and despotism.
- Saddam Hussein’s revival of Babylon used Nebuchadnezzar and the ancient city as Iraqi nationalist symbols. The Hanging Gardens benefited from that symbolic revival, although no genuine Garden was reconstructed.
- Modern Iraqi tourism presents the Gardens as part of Babylon’s world-historical identity while archaeological materials acknowledge that their location is unresolved.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The homesick Median queen

**Story:** Nebuchadnezzar’s wife, raised among the green mountains of Media, suffered in flat Babylonia. The king built an artificial mountain planted with trees to console her.

**Earliest recoverable source:** Berossus as quoted by Josephus, around three centuries after the supposed construction.

**Variants:**

- wife raised in Media;
- Persian concubine;
- unnamed Persian wife;
- later named Amytis;
- builder described as Nebuchadnezzar, a Syrian king, or Assyrian ruler.

**Assessment:** early-attested Hellenistic narrative, not contemporary biography.

### 7.2 Semiramis as builder

**Story:** Semiramis created the Hanging Gardens as part of her enormous Asian building program.

**Development:** Greek tradition assigned walls, canals, roads, cities, and monuments to Semiramis. The historical Sammu-rāmat became a composite conqueror and builder.

**Contrary ancient testimony:** Diodorus specifically says the Garden was not hers but belonged to a later ruler.

**Assessment:** ancient attributional legend. The continuing name “Gardens of Semiramis” records the legend’s success, not proof of authorship.

### 7.3 The invisible water machine

Diodorus says water was raised abundantly while the mechanism remained unseen from outside. Pseudo-Philo describes twisting or spiral mechanical action. Strabo places continuously operated lifting devices beside the stairs.

Modern retellings often turn these passages into a precise Archimedes screw. That is an interpretive modernization. Dalley argues that Sennacherib’s cast-bronze technology supplies the missing prototype; critics dispute the translation and identification.

### 7.4 Destruction by earthquake

Numerous popular accounts give the Garden a neat death date, commonly after the first century CE or through earthquakes in the second century BCE. No source located for this dossier documents such an event specifically affecting the Gardens.

**Assessment:** unverified modern harmonization. The responsible statement is that the monument’s destruction or abandonment is unrecorded.

### 7.5 Alexander saw or died near the Garden

Alexander unquestionably died in Babylon. Later Alexander histories describe the city’s wonders and sometimes garden settings. No surviving contemporary record demonstrates that he inspected, occupied, or died in the Hanging Gardens.

**Assessment:** plausible literary association, not documented custody.

### 7.6 The “found by Koldewey” story

Early twentieth-century and popular presentations often reported that Koldewey discovered the Hanging Gardens. What he discovered was a vaulted palace building; “Hanging Gardens” was his interpretation. Subsequent archaeology has not validated the label.

**Assessment:** documented discovery plus disputed identification.

### 7.7 “Dalley solved the mystery”

Media reports frequently present the Nineveh hypothesis as a definitive solution. Dalley assembled a substantial cumulative case, but specialists have not reached universal agreement.

**Assessment:** serious modern scholarly theory, popularly overstated as proof.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 The named object

**Status as of September 2026:** lost, legendary, or composite; no authenticated physical location.

No legal owner or keeper possesses the Hanging Gardens themselves. No accessioned fragment is recognized as part of them. There is no public display of original Garden material.

### 8.2 Babylon stand-in

**Location:** Babylon Archaeological Site, Babil Governorate, Iraq, near Hillah.

**Candidate:** the northeastern vaulted building of the Southern Palace traditionally identified by Koldewey.

**Condition:** ancient foundations and wall fragments coexist with twentieth-century Iraqi reconstruction. The area attributed to the Gardens was partially reconstructed beginning in the 1960s. It is not an accurate surviving garden superstructure.

**Control:** Republic of Iraq through its antiquities authorities, under national heritage law and the UNESCO World Heritage framework.

**Access:** the Babylon site is generally a heritage and visitor destination, subject to Iraqi administrative and security conditions. UNESCO reports continuing conservation problems involving water, salts, erosion, incompatible reconstruction, infrastructure, and past military use.

### 8.3 Nineveh stand-in

**Location:** ancient Nineveh, within modern Mosul, Nineveh Governorate, Iraq; hydraulic works extend into the surrounding region, including Jerwan.

**What survives:**

- archaeological traces of canals and aqueducts;
- the Jerwan stone aqueduct;
- palace mounds and architectural remains;
- royal inscriptions;
- excavated reliefs and drawings depicting gardens.

**Portable evidence:** British Museum relief **1856,0909.36 / BM 124939**, paired with BM 124940, depicts an irrigated Assyrian garden landscape. This is sometimes presented as visual evidence for the “real Hanging Garden,” but the museum describes it as an Ashurbanipal palace relief showing a landscape watered by an aqueduct probably built by Sennacherib.

### 8.4 Present disputes

There is no current restitution proceeding specifically for “the Hanging Gardens,” because no component has been authenticated. Broader issues do exist:

- Iraqi control and conservation of Babylon and Nineveh;
- colonial-era removal and present museum custody of Assyrian reliefs;
- damage from war and occupation;
- the legitimacy and reversibility of twentieth-century reconstructions;
- public presentation of an uncertain monument as historical fact;
- whether UNESCO and tourism narratives should foreground Babylonian tradition or the Nineveh hypothesis.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Early modern image-making

The Garden’s standard visual form is largely an early modern creation.

Maarten van Heemskerck designed a representation around 1570. Philips Galle engraved it in 1572 as part of a series of eight wonders. The image combines Babylon’s walls, Semiramis on horseback, towers, and a terraced garden in a Renaissance architectural idiom.

The Louvre holds Heemskerck’s preparatory drawing, **RF 36730 Recto**. The British Museum holds a Galle engraving, **1875,0508.46**, measuring approximately 212 × 267 mm. Neither preserves ancient visual evidence. Together they materially shaped the later convention that Semiramis, Babylon’s walls, and the Gardens belonged to one scenic ensemble.

Athanasius Kircher and later architectural antiquarians produced increasingly elaborate reconstructions using contemporary European arches, gardens, fountains, and Baroque spatial effects.

### 9.2 Nineteenth-century reconstruction

Color lithographs and garden histories turned the object into an orientalized palace mountain thick with palms and hanging vines. H. Jäger’s *Gartenkunst und Gärten sonst und jetzt* (1888) reproduced one influential reconstruction.

These images supplied features absent from early texts:

- waterfalls pouring visibly down façades;
- densely cascading vines;
- domes, minarets, or Persianate pavilions;
- precise stair pyramids;
- public promenades crowded with courtiers;
- tropical vegetation chosen for spectacle rather than historical ecology.

### 9.3 Archaeological visualization

Koldewey’s excavations replaced fantasy with architectural plans and a reconstruction anchored to his vaulted-building hypothesis. Although the identification lost favor, its drawings continued to circulate in textbooks and museum displays, giving a disputed candidate the appearance of archaeological certainty.

Modern reconstructions of Dalley’s Nineveh theory instead emphasize:

- an elevated garden above palace colonnades;
- water arriving through Sennacherib’s canal network;
- a bronze lifting device;
- wooded Assyrian hills rather than a ziggurat-like structure in flat Babylon.

### 9.4 Literature, film, games, and commercial culture

The Gardens recur as shorthand for vanished splendor and artificial paradise. Particularly influential visual environments include:

- nineteenth-century illustrated histories of the Seven Wonders;
- D. W. Griffith’s *Intolerance* (1916), whose monumental Babylon set helped fix an enormous, theatrical public image of Babylon even when individual structures were freely reconstructed;
- historical novels, biblical epics, documentaries, and educational films;
- strategy games such as the *Civilization* series, where the Hanging Gardens function as a buildable world wonder conferring population, health, food, or growth benefits;
- board games, puzzles, model kits, commercial garden designs, hotel names, and residential projects.

The gaming convention transforms a disputed monument into a standardized technological upgrade. This is modern symbolic reception, not evidence of its ancient function.

### 9.5 Iraqi national reception

The twentieth-century reconstruction of Babylon presented the city as a foundational Iraqi and Mesopotamian achievement. Saddam Hussein compared his building program with Nebuchadnezzar’s and placed inscribed modern bricks into reconstructed walls. The political project affected the archaeological site surrounding the putative Garden but did not reproduce a securely known Hanging Garden.

### 9.6 Scholarship in mass media

Dalley’s work generated documentaries and headlines announcing that the Garden had been “found” at Nineveh. The publicity successfully corrected the assumption that Babylon was the only relevant site, but often suppressed the distinction between:

- proof that Sennacherib had a major garden;
- proof that later classical authors knew it;
- proof that it was the same object they called the Hanging Garden.

Only the first is secure.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Ancient textual tradition

The source history is unusually fragile:

1. Berossus’ original Greek work is lost.
2. Cleitarchus’ Alexander history is lost.
3. Other possible early sources, including Onesicritus, survive incompletely or through later authors.
4. Josephus transmits Berossus through intermediary excerpting.
5. Diodorus and Curtius probably share earlier material.
6. Pseudo-Philo survives in a medieval manuscript and has disputed authorship and date.
7. The sources may therefore look more numerous and independent than they are.

The surviving corpus establishes a persistent ancient story, not multiple independent eyewitness confirmations.

### 10.2 Koldewey and the archaeological phase

Koldewey’s *Das wiedererstehende Babylon* and *The Excavations at Babylon* brought stratigraphic and architectural evidence into the discussion. His identification dominated much twentieth-century popular writing because it joined an excavated object to the famous name.

The later reading of the room complex as administrative or storage space significantly weakened that case. The episode illustrates an authenticity hazard: excavation discovered a real building, while literary expectation assigned it a legendary identity.

### 10.3 Finkel and source criticism

Irving Finkel’s synthesis of the ancient evidence emphasized how difficult it is to reconcile the classical accounts with Babylonian archaeology. Study of Nebuchadnezzar’s inscriptions sharpened the negative evidence: a king otherwise eager to record monumental construction does not clearly claim this wonder.

### 10.4 Dalley’s Nineveh thesis

Dalley’s 1993 and 1994 articles, subsequent studies, and 2013 monograph argued that cuneiform, iconographic, hydraulic, and classical sources converge on Nineveh.

Her work changed the debate permanently by showing that the question is not simply “real or fictional at Babylon.” A third possibility—a real Assyrian monument mislocated in transmission—must now be addressed.

### 10.5 Criticism and unresolved problems

Bichler and Rollinger questioned whether the proposed correspondences amount to identity. Reade explored alternative pathways involving Alexander and Babylon’s topography without treating the problem as fully solved. Timothy Potts stressed that a garden at Babylon would not be culturally anomalous and that absence of proof should not become proof of absence.

Major unresolved questions include:

- Did Josephus preserve Berossus’ authentic garden sentence?
- Did Berossus use a lost Babylonian record, a Greek story, or both?
- Was Cleitarchus the principal inventor or transmitter?
- Does Sennacherib’s inscription describe a water screw, another lifting mechanism, or something unrelated?
- What exact monument appears in the lost and surviving Nineveh garden reliefs?
- Could the Euphrates have erased the most plausible Babylonian riverside site?
- Were the classical authors describing one monument or a composite?
- When did the garden become attached to the canonical Seven Wonders?
- Was “Babylon” ever used in precisely the way required to rename Nineveh’s garden?

### 10.6 Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- **“Archaeologists found the Gardens at Babylon.”** They found a disputed vaulted building.
- **“The Gardens were destroyed by an earthquake in 226 BCE.”** No secure object-specific source establishes this.
- **“Herodotus described them.”** He did not.
- **“Diodorus says Semiramis built them.”** He explicitly denies this.
- **“Ancient texts call the machinery an Archimedes screw.”** The identification is interpretive.
- **“Amytis is securely documented as commissioner or beneficiary.”** She is not.
- **“Dalley proved beyond dispute that they were at Nineveh.”** Her thesis is influential but contested.
- **“No Babylonian text mentions gardens at all.”** Too broad: Babylonia had gardens and garden terminology. The accurate statement is that no extant text securely names the canonical Hanging Gardens.

### 10.7 State of the primary evidence

**Extant:**

- Greek and Latin literary accounts in later manuscript traditions;
- Josephus’ transmission of Berossus;
- Neo-Babylonian building inscriptions and topographical texts that do not securely name the Garden;
- Sennacherib’s royal inscriptions;
- Jerwan and other hydraulic remains;
- Nineveh reliefs;
- Koldewey’s excavation records and published plans;
- Iraqi, UNESCO, and conservation documentation for Babylon.

**Lost or incomplete:**

- Berossus’ original *Babyloniaca*;
- Cleitarchus’ Alexander history;
- relevant works of Alexander’s companions;
- parts of Pseudo-Philo;
- architectural superstructures, planting, and machinery of either candidate garden;
- some Nineveh reliefs known through drawings;
- portions of twentieth-century Iraqi reconstruction archives destroyed or looted after 2003.
