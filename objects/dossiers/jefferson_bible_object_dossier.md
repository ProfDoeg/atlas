# The Jefferson Bible: Object Dossier

**Requested filename:** `the_jefferson_bible_object_dossier.md`  
**Research current to:** 6 September 2026

## Evidentiary key

- **Documented fact:** supported by a surviving object, contemporary manuscript, account, institutional accession record, or official publication.
- **Scientific/conservation finding:** derived from physical examination or instrumental analysis of the surviving volume.
- **Scholarly inference:** a reconstruction advanced from incomplete evidence.
- **Institutional tradition:** a statement maintained by the Smithsonian or another custodian but not independently demonstrated in the cited public record.
- **Later claim or reception:** an interpretation imposed after Jefferson’s lifetime.
- **Unresolved:** the surviving evidence does not permit a firm conclusion.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity

The object conventionally called **the Jefferson Bible** is a handmade multilingual Gospel harmony or scrapbook assembled by Thomas Jefferson at Monticello in approximately 1819–1820. Its title, written by Jefferson on its manuscript title page, is:

> **THE LIFE AND MORALS OF JESUS OF NAZARETH / EXTRACTED TEXTUALLY FROM THE GOSPELS / IN GREEK, LATIN, FRENCH & ENGLISH**

It is neither a complete Bible nor a new translation. It contains excerpts cut from six printed New Testaments—two copies apiece of three editions—and pasted into a new sequence. The extracts are drawn only from the canonical Gospels of Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John. Jefferson synchronized corresponding passages in Greek, Latin, French, and English and rearranged them into a continuous life of Jesus emphasizing conduct, parables, and moral teaching.

The popular title **“Jefferson’s Bible”** or **“the Jefferson Bible”** was not Jefferson’s own title. Cyrus Adler and the United States National Museum publicized that designation after the Smithsonian acquired and exhibited the volume in 1895. The name subsequently expanded in ordinary usage to include printed editions, translations, adaptations, and sometimes Jefferson’s separate, lost 1804 compilation. The Smithsonian’s official object record classifies the surviving volume under the identifiers “Religion” and “Bible” and assigns it object number **NMAH 1414507**. [Smithsonian object record](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_1414507)

### The two Jefferson compilations

Two distinct objects must not be conflated:

1. **The Philosophy of Jesus of Nazareth**, compiled at Washington in 1804. Jefferson described this as a small English-only arrangement of Gospel extracts, reportedly about forty-six pages. No original or complete copy is known to survive. Its exact physical disposition after Jefferson’s lifetime is unestablished.

2. **The Life and Morals of Jesus of Nazareth**, assembled about 1819–1820 and bound shortly afterward. This is the surviving Smithsonian artifact tracked in the remainder of this dossier.

Jefferson himself linked the projects intellectually, but the second was not merely the first volume rebound: it was newly constructed with four parallel languages, more narrative material, maps, a manuscript table, and a new binding.

### Format and dimensions

The surviving text proper comprises **forty-three folded folios**. Smithsonian descriptions alternately call it an **84-page** work or an **86-page book**, depending on whether the title and preliminary leaves are included in the count. This is a counting difference, not evidence that leaves have vanished.

Jefferson probably bought “royal”-size blank paper, approximately 19 × 24 inches, divided each sheet into six pieces measuring about 9½ × 8 inches, and folded those pieces to make leaves approximately **8 inches high by 4¾ inches wide**. Published shorthand dimensions of about 8.3 × 5 inches refer to the closed bound volume rather than every irregular handmade leaf. [Smithsonian conservation account](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/blogs/smithsonian-books/2024/04/11/how-the-smithsonian-conserved-jeffersons-bible/)

### Materials

Scientific and microscopic examination conducted for the 2010–2011 conservation campaign identified an unusually heterogeneous composite object:

- twelve categories or types of paper, including Jefferson’s blank support paper, papers from the six source books, map paper, end leaves, index paper, marbled paper, and the binding’s compensation stubs;
- seven printed inks if the maps are included, or six Gospel-source printing inks in the museum’s narrower count;
- at least three formulations of Jefferson’s manuscript iron-gall ink, with four manuscript-ink categories recorded in the broader survey;
- animal glue and starch paste beneath the clippings;
- linen sewing thread;
- two-colored silk endbands;
- boards and paper spine linings;
- marbled pastedowns;
- red goatskin, conventionally described as red morocco leather;
- gold tooling on the cover and spine.

The apparent discrepancies in published counts arise from classification: “six printing inks” counts the six Gospel volumes, while “seven” includes the maps; “four manuscript inks” is a survey category, while “at least three recipes” refers to distinguishable iron-gall formulations. [Conservation overview](https://americanhistory.si.edu/JeffersonBible/conservation/) [Microscopic survey account](https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/stories/peek-inside-conservation-jefferson-bible)

### Source editions

Jefferson used two copies of each of the following:

- **Greek and Latin:** Johann Leusden’s Greek New Testament with facing Latin, published by Wingrave in London in 1794. The Latin ultimately belongs to the textual tradition used in the Plantin Polyglot.
- **French:** an Ostervald New Testament, Paris, 1802.
- **English:** a King James New Testament published by Jacob Johnson in Philadelphia in 1804.

Two copies were necessary because a passage printed on one side of a leaf could not always be cut out without sacrificing text wanted from the reverse. The mutilated English source volumes survived separately and helped Cyrus Adler recognize and locate the finished book. [Smithsonian source-book and conservation history](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/blogs/smithsonian-books/2024/04/11/how-the-smithsonian-conserved-jeffersons-bible/)

### Page design and construction

Jefferson ruled a vertical center line on the front and back of each support sheet. Tiny pencil hatching marked the intended upper margin. He pasted extracts into two columns on each page so that an open spread ordinarily presents:

- Greek and Latin on the left;
- French and English on the right.

He supplied page numbers, a table of Gospel citations, marginal notes, corrections, and the title in his own hand. His clipping was extremely close to the printed text. Physical examination nevertheless found small repairs and adjustments made during construction:

- On page 5, in Luke 6:12, Jefferson corrected the English word “out” to “up.”
- On page 40 he supplied an omitted verse by hand in the margin.
- On page 64 he excised the word “as” from Matthew 24:38.
- On page 22 he replaced text accidentally sliced from the edge of a clipping; darkened adhesive beneath the repair now reveals the otherwise precise correction.
- Unused clippings and skeletal page borders remained inside the mutilated source books.

The first narrative extracts begin with Luke’s account of the Roman enrollment and Jesus’s birth. The work ends with Jesus’s burial, drawing finally on John 19. It does not continue to the Resurrection. Jefferson nevertheless retained material that modern readers may call supernatural—including angels in the birth narrative and several references to future judgment—so the common description “the Bible with every supernatural element removed” is an overstatement. His exclusions followed his own judgments, not a consistently stated modern naturalist rule.

### Maps and preliminaries

The binding contains two folded printed maps, one of Palestine and one of Asia Minor. The 1904 description says they were removed from another book and retained their original printed page references, “page 1” and “page 414.” Their precise parent publication has not been securely identified in the public Smithsonian documentation reviewed here.

The sequence documented in Cyrus Adler’s 1904 introduction was:

1. upper cover;
2. two manuscript leaves containing Jefferson’s table of texts on the first two and a half pages;
3. blank matter and flyleaves;
4. manuscript title page;
5. folded maps of Palestine and Asia Minor;
6. the ruled and pasted Gospel sequence;
7. lower cover.

### Binding

The finished gatherings were bound by Richmond bookbinder **Frederick August Mayo**, rather than by Jefferson’s aging former binder Joseph Milligan. The red morocco covering bears gilt tooling. Mayo compensated for the added thickness of the pasted clippings by attaching alternating one- and two-layer folded paper stubs, about one centimeter wide, at the centerfolds.

A watermark showed that the stub paper was manufactured by P. A. Mesier between 1817 and 1822, evidence consistent with an early-1820s binding. The binder originally partially sewed groups of three thin folios and reinforced the spine heavily with adhesive. That economical construction eventually became a principal agent of damage.

No identified contemporary bill expressly naming *The Life and Morals* has been found. Mayo’s surviving 1822 account includes charges for binding “2 Vols Biblia” in extra calf, but those volumes cannot be securely equated with the red-morocco Jefferson Bible. It is evidence of Mayo’s work for Jefferson, not a conclusive invoice for this object. [Mayo account, c. 1 September 1822](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Jefferson/03-18-02-0546-0002)

### Damage and present physical state

Before modern treatment, the adhesives had stiffened the pages while the binding resisted opening. Approximately **98 percent** of the primary pages had cracked or torn where they met the compensation stubs. The paper was brittle, page movement was restricted, and continued exhibition or research handling risked further loss.

Among the small inclusions documented during conservation was a single curly reddish hair protruding from under a clipping on page 7. Other hairs and loose matter were recovered from between pages and retained. No published DNA result identifies the hair with Jefferson or any other person. Calling it “Jefferson’s hair” would therefore exceed the evidence.

Following conservation, the volume retains Jefferson’s paper, clippings, handwriting, maps, original leather cover, and original silk endbands. Removed original stubs and incidental materials were individually labeled and preserved as part of the artifact’s record. Modern Japanese tissue, reversible conservation adhesives, new sewing, spine linings, and an anoxic enclosure are additions made openly in 2011.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Intellectual antecedents

Jefferson’s project developed over decades rather than from a single commission. There was no patron and no commercial publisher. His relevant intellectual context included Enlightenment criticism, Gospel harmonies, moral philosophy, and the writings of Joseph Priestley, Conyers Middleton, Lord Bolingbroke, and others. None of those antecedents makes the finished object a copy of an earlier work; its multilingual cut-and-paste format and particular selection are Jefferson’s.

In a letter to Joseph Priestley of 27 January 1800, Jefferson proposed a comparative treatment of ancient moral philosophy and Jesus. The letter is the earliest clear documentary precursor to the project, but no physical volume yet existed. [Jefferson to Priestley, 27 January 1800](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/mss/mtj/mtj1/022/022_0048_0049.pdf)

On 21 April 1803 he sent Benjamin Rush a “Syllabus of an Estimate of the Merit of the Doctrines of Jesus, compared with those of others.” This was a conceptual outline, not the surviving book.

### The lost 1804 object

Jefferson later told William Short that he had attempted the extraction “too hastily” twelve or fifteen years before 1819, during “2 or 3 nights only at Washington” after finishing the day’s official papers. That retrospective description points to 1804, while he was president. [Jefferson to William Short, 31 October 1819](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Jefferson/03-15-02-0141-0001)

In 1816 he described the result to Charles Thomson:

> “a wee little book … which I call the Philosophy of Jesus,” made by “cutting the texts out of the book, and arranging them on the pages of a blank book.”

He then said that, if he had time, he would add the Greek, Latin, and French texts beside it. This proves that the volume he described in January 1816 was still English-only and therefore not the surviving four-language artifact. [Jefferson to Charles Thomson, 9 January 1816](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Jefferson/03-09-02-0216)

No confirmed leaf of *The Philosophy of Jesus* survives. Published reconstructions rely chiefly upon Jefferson’s table or later testimony and must not be treated as facsimiles of the lost physical volume.

### Creation of the surviving object

The four-language *Life and Morals* was planned by 1816 and executed approximately 1819–1820. Smithsonian examination places its assembly at Monticello. Jefferson was seventy-seven in 1820.

The production sequence is reconstructable from tool marks, materials, and handwriting:

1. Jefferson acquired duplicate copies of three New Testament editions.
2. He prepared blank folded supports and ruled their centers.
3. He chose and cut parallel Gospel passages from all six source volumes.
4. He aligned them in a synthesized chronology.
5. He pasted them in Greek, Latin, French, and English columns.
6. He numbered pages, wrote the title and table, and made textual corrections.
7. Maps and preliminary leaves were assembled with the Gospel folios.
8. Frederick Mayo bound the material in red morocco.

There is no documented quarry, mine, or single paper mill provenance for all components. The P. A. Mesier watermark dates only the removed stubs, while the printed title pages identify the cities and dates of the principal textual source volumes. Instrumental analysis has characterized the component materials but has not produced independent absolute dating comparable to radiocarbon analysis.

### Purpose

Jefferson repeatedly characterized the undertaking as private. To Thomson he called it evidence that he was a disciple of Jesus’s doctrines. To Short he described separating moral “gold” from biographical “dross.” The physical work was therefore both a reading tool and the material enactment of his theory that authentic teachings could be distinguished from later additions.

In April 1820 Jefferson explained that the “Syllabus” represented Jesus’s doctrines, “not all of mine,” and that he read them with “a mixture of approbation and dissent.” This cautions against identifying every retained passage as Jefferson’s personal creed. [Jefferson to William Short, 13 April 1820](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Jefferson/03-15-02-0505)

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 1819–1826: Monticello and the Richmond bindery

**Documented/inferred chain:** Jefferson constructed the folios at Monticello. They necessarily traveled to Mayo’s bindery in Richmond for binding and returned to Jefferson, although no surviving shipment receipt expressly names this volume.

Jefferson’s correspondence documents his regular use of Mayo. On 28 December 1820 he asked whether Mayo was still accepting work and discussed sending more than one hundred volumes for binding. In August 1821, a box of Jefferson’s books was carried from Monticello through the household of Governor Thomas Mann Randolph; a receipt says it was delivered by **Philip Evans**, an enslaved Black man belonging to Randolph. That shipment is not demonstrably the Jefferson Bible, but it documents the human and transport network through which Jefferson’s bindings moved. [Jefferson to Mayo, 28 December 1820](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Jefferson/03-16-02-0409) [Jefferson to Mayo, 12 August 1821](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Jefferson/03-17-02-0354)

Once bound, *Life and Morals* remained at Monticello or in Jefferson’s immediate custody until his death on 4 July 1826. Claims that he read it every night come from later family recollection and should be labeled **family tradition**, not a surviving daily reading log.

### 1826–1836: Martha Jefferson Randolph

The Smithsonian’s received provenance says Jefferson’s eldest daughter, **Martha “Patsy” Jefferson Randolph**, inherited the volume. Jefferson did not identify it by title in the publicly accessible will evidence reviewed for this dossier, so the exact legal mechanism is not as well documented as the later family possession. It may have passed among personal papers and books rather than under a specific testamentary clause.

After Monticello’s dispersal, the object was no longer kept at its place of manufacture. Precisely where Martha stored it at every point—Tufton, Edgehill, Washington, Boston, or another family residence—has not been established from an object-specific transport record.

### 1836–1892: the Randolph family

Later accounts place the volume with Jefferson’s grandson **Thomas Jefferson Randolph** and subsequently with Randolph descendants. The publicly available chain is less granular than many summaries imply. It is safe to say that the object remained in the Jefferson-Randolph family; it is not safe to assign every intermediate transfer a precise date without an inheritance inventory.

By the later nineteenth century it was associated with **Sarah Nicholas Randolph**, Jefferson’s great-granddaughter and author of *The Domestic Life of Thomas Jefferson*. Her death in 1892 preceded its passage to her sister **Carolina Ramsey Randolph**. Smithsonian accounts explicitly state that Carolina obtained it from Sarah.

This period—roughly sixty-six years—was not a disappearance in the sense of theft or unknown ownership. It was private family custody with very limited public knowledge. Nevertheless, exact residence, handling history, and successive legal title within the family remain incompletely published.

### 1886: source books rediscovered

While cataloguing a private Hebrew library in Baltimore in 1886, Cyrus Adler found two mutilated English New Testaments. An attached note said Jefferson had cut them up to make *The Life and Morals of Jesus*. Adler sought the finished compilation but did not then locate it.

These were source objects, not the finished Jefferson Bible. Their survival supplied unusually strong material corroboration of the fabrication process: the voids and leftover clippings could be compared with the mounted English passages.

### 1895: purchase by the Smithsonian

As Smithsonian librarian and curator of religion, Adler renewed the search. In 1895 he located *Life and Morals* with Carolina Ramsey Randolph and acquired it for the United States National Museum for **$400**. Contemporary and institutional records characterize the transaction as a purchase, not a gift or confiscation. [Smithsonian history of the acquisition](https://americanhistory.si.edu/jeffersonbible/history/page-5.cfm)

The acquisition transferred the object from private family ownership to a federally administered national collection. It also transformed an obscure family manuscript into a public national artifact.

### 1895: Atlanta exhibition

Soon after acquisition, the volume traveled from Washington to Atlanta for the **Cotton States and International Exposition**, open 18 September–31 December 1895. Adler’s exhibition catalogue described “Thomas Jefferson’s Bible, consisting of texts from the Evangelists, historically arranged,” and recorded its title, annotations, concordance, and Jefferson handwriting. [Adler’s exhibition report](https://repository.si.edu/bitstream/handle/10088/29838/Adler_1896_943-1023.pdf) [Smithsonian exposition records](https://siarchives.si.edu/collections/siris_arc_216677)

This is the earliest firmly documented public exhibition of the artifact and the setting in which “Jefferson’s Bible” became its durable public name. It then returned to the National Museum in Washington.

### 1902–1904: congressional reproduction

Representative **John Fletcher Lacey of Iowa** encountered the object and promoted its publication. On 10 May 1902 Congress authorized 9,000 copies—3,000 for the Senate and 6,000 for the House—by photolithographic process, with an introduction by Adler.

For reproduction, every page was photographed in black and white. The original was not printed from movable type or dismembered. The resulting edition reproduced the page design and handwriting, though it could not reproduce the colors and material textures accurately.

The Government Printing Office ultimately produced **9,642 copies** at a cost of **$21,258.60**. Congressional debate included support for the work as an ethical condensation and objections that government publication promoted irreligion or a mutilated Christianity. Lacey reportedly attempted to rescind the order after arranging a possible commercial publication, but production was already substantially complete. [Congressional Record, 10 May 1902](https://www.congress.gov/57/crecb/1902/05/10/GPO-CRECB-1902-pt5-v35-22.pdf) [Official GPO history](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/gdc/gdcebookspublic/20/23/69/55/10/2023695510/2023695510.pdf)

### 1904–1957: congressional presentation tradition

The government edition was distributed to members of Congress. A practice developed of presenting copies to incoming members until the supply was exhausted in **1957**. This was a tradition attached to the facsimiles, not a ritual use of the original artifact. The original remained in National Museum custody.

One copy of the 1904 edition was later reportedly placed in the cornerstone of the Jefferson Memorial. Again, that act involved a reproduction, not the unique manuscript.

### Twentieth-century museum custody

The original remained under Smithsonian control in Washington. It was periodically exhibited, photographed, researched, and handled. No theft, wartime evacuation, loss, deaccession, private sale, or adverse ownership claim has been identified.

Repeated opening, ordinary aging, the original glue, and the binding structure progressively damaged it. Eventually it became too fragile for ordinary exhibition and scholarly handling. This was gradual mechanical and chemical deterioration, not a single destructive event.

### 2009–2011: examination, disbinding, and conservation

In 2009–2010 a Smithsonian team determined that the binding structure was actively damaging the folios. A Collections Care and Preservation Fund award of **$155,500** supported conservation of the Bible and two source books. [Smithsonian collections report](https://www.si.edu/content/governance/pdf/Collections_09-2011.pdf)

The treatment proceeded as follows:

1. Conservators created written, photographic, and microscopic documentation.
2. A custom survey recorded about 200 observations per page—more than 20,000 data points.
3. Minute samples, approximately 20–200 microns, were analyzed.
4. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy identified animal glue and starch paste.
5. Micro-X-ray fluorescence detected and mapped inorganic elements relevant to deterioration.
6. Solubility and microchemical tests assessed the manuscript inks and whether aqueous treatment was safe.
7. Conservators faced the leather with removable Japanese kozo tissue.
8. The original cover was removed intact by separating the paper spine linings.
9. The original silk endbands were detached and retained.
10. Adhesive and spine linings were softened and removed.
11. The damaging compensation stubs were removed with poultices and microspatulas, individually labeled, and preserved.
12. Dirt was removed with soft brushes after microscopic testing.
13. Tears and cracks were repaired with Japanese tissue and reversible adhesive.
14. Every folio was photographed with a 50-megapixel Hasselblad camera and Zeiss macro lens by Hugh Talman.
15. Each of the forty-three folios was sewn individually rather than in the binder’s original three-folio partial-sewing pattern.
16. Japanese paper spine linings replaced the direct application of heavy adhesive to Jefferson’s pages.
17. The original endbands were resewn into their original positions.
18. The original red leather cover and marbled papers were reattached with reversible materials.
19. A blue internal paper marker was inserted to guide any future conservator needing to separate the binding safely.
20. The volume was placed in a custom oxygen-free or anoxic storage enclosure.

The treatment did not restore an imagined pristine appearance, replace Jefferson’s clippings, or conceal the intervention. Its goal was stabilization, reversibility, and renewed access. [Smithsonian step-by-step treatment record](https://americanhistory.si.edu/JeffersonBible/conservation/step-by-step.cfm)

### 2011–2012: exhibition after conservation

The conserved book went on view in the Albert H. Small Documents Gallery at the National Museum of American History on 11 November 2011. The exhibition included the original volume, two mutilated English source books, and a 1904 government edition. It closed in 2012; Smithsonian pages give 28 May and 15 July in different descriptions, apparently reflecting an extension or differing page updates. [Smithsonian press release](https://www.si.edu/newsdesk/releases/national-museum-american-history-displays-thomas-jefferson-s-book-life-and-morals-jesus-naz) [Exhibition record](https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/exhibitions/jefferson-bible)

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is the Smithsonian volume Jefferson’s original?

The attribution is exceptionally strong. Evidence includes:

- continuous family provenance from Jefferson’s descendants, though not every intrafamily transfer is documented separately;
- Jefferson’s handwriting in the title, table, pagination, notes, and corrections;
- consistency between the object and Jefferson’s descriptions of a multilingual compilation;
- physical correspondence between mounted passages and surviving mutilated source books;
- early nineteenth-century papers, printing, adhesives, ink, and binding;
- a binding attributable to Frederick Mayo, a documented Jefferson bookbinder;
- public acquisition and description in 1895, before a commercial market for replicas had developed;
- no rival claimant and no evidence of substitution.

No modern scientific test alone “proved” authorship. Rather, provenance, handwriting, source-volume correspondence, construction evidence, and materials agree. No serious scholarly authenticity dispute concerning the Smithsonian object itself was found.

### Continuity versus restoration

The volume is materially the same composite artifact acquired in 1895, but it is not untouched:

- its original cover and endbands survive;
- its original pages and clippings survive;
- original binding stubs were removed and separately preserved;
- old direct spine adhesion was replaced;
- all folios were resewn in a structurally stronger pattern;
- Japanese tissues and reversible adhesives now support damaged areas;
- new spine linings and an enclosure were added.

Calling it “unaltered” would therefore be inaccurate. Calling it a replica after conservation would be equally inaccurate. It is an openly conserved original whose binding mechanics were substantially modified to protect its constituent leaves.

### The lost 1804 volume

The principal identity confusion is between the surviving book and *The Philosophy of Jesus*. The latter is a separately attested, English-only object, now lost. A publication offering “Jefferson’s original 1804 Bible” cannot be a physical facsimile unless an unknown original resurfaces. Most such editions are textual reconstructions or reprints of the later work under a loose title.

### “Six Bibles,” “four Bibles,” or three editions?

All formulations can mislead without explanation:

- Jefferson used **six physical printed volumes**;
- they represented **three bibliographical editions**;
- they furnished **four languages**, because Greek and Latin appeared together.

### The razor

Jefferson clearly cut the printed sheets with a very sharp implement, and “razor” has become the standard description. No securely proven cutting tool is accessioned with the volume. References to “Jefferson’s razor” should therefore be understood as a reasonable functional description, not identification of a surviving personally associated razor.

### Hair attribution

The hairs found beneath or between leaves are authentic inclusions in the historic object. No cited forensic test identifies their human source, age, sex, ancestry, or relationship to Jefferson. The possibility that one entered during Jefferson’s assembly is plausible but unresolved.

### Textual authenticity

The title says the contents were “extracted textually,” and most extracts reproduce printed source wording. Yet the work is not a neutral transcription. Jefferson:

- selected among four Gospel narratives;
- changed their sequence;
- juxtaposed sentences never adjacent in the source books;
- corrected words;
- omitted clauses;
- occasionally repaired or altered individual fragments.

Thus it is authentically Jefferson’s compilation but not an authentic early text of Jesus in the modern critical-historical sense. Jefferson offered no manuscript stemma, linguistic criteria, or source-critical proof that each retained saying originated with the historical Jesus.

### No legendary or miraculous identity claim

Unlike a saint’s relic, the volume carries no old claim of divine manufacture, miraculous survival, or supernatural power. Its “sacred” status is cultural, religious, political, and museological. Describing it as a national relic is a modern metaphor and institutional reception, not a claim that it possesses liturgical authenticity.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Jefferson’s private use

No formal liturgy is attached to the original. Jefferson constructed it for private moral and comparative reading. Family tradition says he read from it nightly before retiring. The statement is plausible and widely repeated, but the frequency cannot be independently verified from a reading diary.

The object was not known to have been used:

- for presidential oath-taking;
- in church worship;
- in communion;
- for legal swearing;
- in a Masonic ceremony;
- to evangelize Indigenous peoples;
- as an official United States religious text.

The last point is especially important because Jefferson did produce or support other religious and educational projects. Claims that this particular volume was a missionary Bible for Native Americans conflate it with different plans and documents.

### A private exercise made public

Its original function was analytic: comparison across languages, rearrangement of chronology, and extraction of teachings. Its later functions were created by institutions:

- **Museum specimen:** Adler displayed it as an example of American religious history.
- **National relic:** proximity to Jefferson’s own hand made it an object of civic reverence.
- **Legislative gift:** the 1904 facsimile became a presentation book for members of Congress.
- **Religious-liberty emblem:** Adler and later commentators interpreted the object as evidence of freedom of conscience.
- **Polemic:** Christians, Unitarians, humanists, secularists, and Christian nationalists cited it for incompatible accounts of the founding era.

Possessing the object never conferred constitutional authority, office, ordination, or sovereignty. Its political force lay in reproduction and interpretation rather than ceremonial control of the original.

### Access rules

During Jefferson’s lifetime access was intentionally narrow. He feared the use of his religious opinions in public controversy and disclosed the project only to selected correspondents.

Under Smithsonian custody, restrictions are conservational rather than priestly. The unique original cannot be freely handled. Physical access is controlled by the National Museum of American History, while high-resolution digital images and facsimiles provide public access to its contents.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### “Jefferson rewrote the Bible”

**Tier: modern shorthand with a factual core.**

Jefferson did physically alter printed New Testaments and construct a new Gospel narrative. He did not rewrite the whole Bible, create a replacement canon for the nation, or issue a translation. “Edited excerpts from the four Gospels” is more exact.

### “He cut out everything supernatural”

**Tier: later simplification.**

He omitted the Resurrection and many miracle narratives, rejected doctrines such as the Trinity and Incarnation, and excluded passages he regarded as contrary to reason. But the surviving book retains the angelic birth announcement, references to God, judgment, and other statements modern readers may regard as supernatural. His boundary was personal and uneven.

### “He made it secretly while president”

**Tier: conflation of two objects.**

The 1804 *Philosophy of Jesus* was made in Washington while Jefferson was president and was guarded as a private work. The surviving multilingual *Life and Morals* was made after his presidency, principally in 1819–1820 at Monticello.

### “It was a secret Bible no one knew about until 1895”

**Tier: exaggerated reception story.**

The finished book remained within Jefferson’s family, and few outsiders knew it. Jefferson had nevertheless described the earlier project and his intended multilingual expansion in correspondence. Adler’s 1895 acquisition made the physical volume broadly public; it did not uncover an object literally unknown to every living person.

### “Jefferson read it every night”

**Tier: family tradition.**

This rests on later testimony. It may preserve genuine household memory, but the absolute formulation is not independently documented.

### “Congress made it America’s official Bible”

**Tier: false modern inference.**

Congress authorized a facsimile edition and distributed it to legislators. It did not establish the text as scripture, prescribe its use, or make it an official national Bible.

### “Every member of Congress received one from 1904 onward”

**Tier: overstatement.**

Copies were distributed according to a congressional presentation practice until the stock was exhausted in 1957. The practice was not permanent, and the original volume never circulated among members.

### “The Smithsonian received it as a patriotic gift”

**Tier: inaccurate institutional sentiment.**

Carolina Randolph sold it for $400. Later writing sometimes describes her motive as entrusting a family treasure to the nation, but the legal mechanism was purchase.

### “The hairs are Jefferson’s”

**Tier: unverified claim.**

Hair was found under and between pages. No published DNA or comparative forensic analysis identifies it. The object’s context permits the possibility but not the conclusion.

### “The book proves Jefferson was an atheist, deist, Unitarian, or orthodox Christian”

**Tier: competing ideological appropriations.**

The artifact proves what Jefferson selected for this project, while his letters explain much of his reasoning. It does not by itself settle every label applied to his changing religious thought. Jefferson called himself a Christian in the sense of being a disciple of Jesus’s ethics, rejected central orthodox doctrines, and told Short that even the retained teachings were “not all of mine.”

### “Its wording recovers the historical Jesus”

**Tier: Jefferson’s claim, not a demonstrated scientific finding.**

Jefferson believed literary and moral quality allowed him to distinguish Jesus’s teachings from later fabrication. Modern historical-Jesus scholarship uses different linguistic, textual, and comparative methods and does not treat Jefferson’s selection as a critical edition of an ancient source.

## 8. Current Status and Location

The original is owned and controlled by the **Smithsonian Institution** and held in the **National Museum of American History, Washington, D.C.**, in the Political History/Presidential History and Religion collections under object number **NMAH 1414507**.

The dedicated 2011–2012 exhibition is closed. No current Smithsonian page reviewed in September 2026 establishes that the original is on continuous public display. Its status should therefore be reported as **held in the museum collection, with physical display dependent on exhibition and conservation decisions**, not “permanently on view.”

The conserved volume is housed in a custom anoxic enclosure intended to slow oxidation, acidic hydrolysis, paper darkening, brittleness, and fiber breakage. Handling is restricted. Researchers must proceed through museum collection-access procedures.

Public access exists through:

- the Smithsonian’s interactive digital document;
- complete post-conservation color photography;
- the 2011 Smithsonian Books facsimile;
- the 1904 government photolithographic edition and later digital reproductions.

There is no known restitution, repatriation, ownership, or rival-custody dispute. The 1895 purchase predates the museum’s modern departmental structure but remains the basis of title. No claimant has alleged that it was stolen, looted, exported illegally, or sold without authority.

The two mutilated English source volumes also remain significant Smithsonian-associated artifacts. They are not constituent leaves of the bound volume and should be catalogued separately when discussing present physical holdings.

## 9. Modern Reception

### The 1904 federal edition

The 1904 photolithographic edition was the decisive event in the work’s public afterlife. Until then, the artifact’s cultural force depended largely on exhibition and description. Photographic reproduction turned a unique private scrapbook into a text capable of political and religious circulation.

The publication provoked contemporary dispute. Supporters portrayed it as a compact ethical account of Jesus and evidence of Jefferson’s religious seriousness. Opponents called federal publication an attack on Christianity or objected to Adler, a Jewish scholar, introducing a work about Jesus. Adler ultimately requested that his name not be foregrounded on the title page amid sectarian controversy.

Because the edition was monochrome, many later readers knew the object as a flat black-and-white document rather than as a red leather book composed of differently colored papers and inks.

### Commercial publishing

Commercial publishers repeatedly detached the English text from the four-column object. These editions vary materially:

- some reproduce the 1904 photographic pages;
- some print only Jefferson’s English sequence;
- some modernize punctuation or typography;
- some add introductions casting Jefferson as an orthodox believer;
- others frame him as a Unitarian, deist, rationalist, secularist, or precursor of modern historical-Jesus research;
- some inadvertently restore material Jefferson omitted.

Consequently, a book sold as *The Jefferson Bible* may reproduce the artifact, transcribe one linguistic column, reconstruct the lost 1804 work, or merely adapt Jefferson’s principle.

### Liberal religious reception

Unitarian and Unitarian Universalist publishers found the work compatible with an emphasis on Jesus’s ethics and skepticism toward Trinitarian dogma. Beacon Press’s editions, including a later edition introduced by Forrest Church, helped keep it in print. [Beacon Press edition](https://www.beacon.org/The-Jefferson-Bible-P451.aspx)

### Secular and humanist reception

Humanists have treated Jefferson’s cutting as an emblem of reason applied to scripture. In 2013 the American Humanist Association’s Humanist Press issued *A Jefferson Bible for the Twenty-First Century*. It extended selective “gold and dross” treatment to the Hebrew Bible, Qur’an, Bhagavad Gita, Buddhist texts, and Book of Mormon and distributed copies to political officials. This was a new anthology inspired by Jefferson, not an edition of his physical artifact. [American Humanist Association announcement](https://americanhumanist.org/news/2013-01-scriptures-go-under-the-knife-in-a-jefferson-bible-f/)

### Conservative Christian and nationalist reception

Some conservative writers cite the work to prove Jefferson’s attachment to Jesus and Christianity. That claim has a documentary basis insofar as Jefferson praised Jesus and called himself a Christian in a defined sense. Presentations that omit his rejection of Jesus’s divinity, miracles, Resurrection, atonement, Trinity, and biblical inerrancy materially distort the object’s selection and his letters.

Other Christian critics have used the same omissions as evidence that Jefferson subordinated revelation to private reason. The artifact therefore supports rival mobilizations because it combines devotion to Jesus’s moral teaching with radical rejection of orthodox doctrine.

### Smithsonian facsimile and digital surrogate

In November 2011 Smithsonian Books published the first complete high-quality color facsimile from Hugh Talman’s post-treatment photographs, accompanied by a separate historical and conservation volume. A limited facsimile issue of 500 copies was also produced. The Smithsonian Libraries record identifies copies 482 and 484 in its holdings. [Smithsonian Libraries catalogue](https://www.si.edu/object/jefferson-bible%3Asiris_sil_996185)

The digital surrogate allows the pages to be turned virtually and has become the most accessible approximation of consulting the object. It also shifts use away from the fragile original.

### Film and popular culture

The conservation campaign was filmed for the Smithsonian production *Jefferson’s Secret Bible*. The artifact is mentioned in Dan Brown’s novel *The Lost Symbol* (2009), which brought it into a popular milieu of Washington secrets and founding-era symbolism. The novel did not supply reliable provenance evidence.

The phrase “Jefferson Bible” now functions generically in journalism to mean an ideologically selective abridgment. That metaphor often ignores the material specificity of Jefferson’s four-language artifact.

### Replica objects

Replicas fall into several classes:

- 1904 congressional photolithographs;
- later photographic facsimiles;
- English-only reset editions;
- color facsimiles reproducing stains, cut edges, handwriting, and maps;
- digital page images;
- adaptations using Jefferson’s name but not reproducing his selection.

None is a rival claimant to the original. Some 1904 copies have independent provenance through members of Congress and have become collectible political artifacts in their own right.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Primary evidence

The object itself is the central primary source. Other essential evidence includes:

- Jefferson’s letters to Priestley, Rush, Adams, Thomson, and Short;
- his 1803 syllabus;
- the mutilated source New Testaments;
- Frederick Mayo’s correspondence and accounts;
- Jefferson and Randolph family papers;
- the Smithsonian’s 1895 acquisition and exposition records;
- Adler’s 1895–1898 exhibition descriptions;
- the 1902 Congressional Record;
- the 1904 photolithographic edition and Adler introduction;
- National Museum catalogue and exhibition records;
- the 2010–2011 conservation survey, images, samples, and treatment records.

### Major research problems

#### Dating

“1819,” “1820,” and “1819–1820” all appear in responsible publications. Jefferson’s October 1819 letter still speaks of the project as contemplated or previously attempted; Smithsonian evidence says the surviving volume was made by 1820. The safest formulation is **assembled in 1819–1820 and bound around 1820–1822**.

#### Relation between the two compilations

Older accounts often treated the 1804 and 1820 undertakings as stages of one book. Jefferson’s 1816 letter proves a material distinction: the existing “wee little book” lacked the Greek, Latin, and French columns he wished to add. The surviving volume is therefore a second fabrication, though it developed from the same program.

#### Selection criteria

Jefferson’s own rhetoric describes separating genuine moral teaching from impossibility and priestly corruption. Scholars debate whether the actual selection consistently follows that program. The retained birth narrative, eschatological language, and other difficult passages show that “all miracles removed” is not an adequate textual description.

#### Gospel-harmony traditions

The sequence belongs broadly to the long tradition of Gospel harmonies. Charles Thomson’s *Synopsis of the Four Evangelists* provided a contemporary comparison, but the precise extent to which Thomson determined Jefferson’s final ordering remains a scholarly question. Material cut marks alone cannot reveal every intellectual source.

#### Binding date and invoice

Mayo’s craftsmanship and the Mesier watermark support the institutional attribution and early-1820s date. The absence of a bill expressly naming *Life and Morals* leaves the exact delivery and price unresolved.

#### Family provenance

The broad line Jefferson–Randolph family–Carolina Randolph–Smithsonian is secure. Object-specific wills, inventories, and dated transfer receipts do not publicly document every intermediate hand or residence. Histories should preserve that distinction instead of manufacturing an unbroken year-by-year chain.

#### Conservation and authenticity

The 2011 intervention improved structural access but altered the binding’s functioning. Conservation ethics favored retention of original covers and endbands, reversible repairs, preservation of removed stubs, and full documentation. Researchers examining sewing or opening behavior must distinguish Mayo’s original construction from the 2011 structure.

### Important modern studies

The Smithsonian’s 2011 facsimile and companion volume by Harry R. Rubenstein, Barbara Clark Smith, and Janice Stagnitto Ellis supply the fullest institutionally authorized material account.

Peter Manseau’s *The Jefferson Bible: A Biography* (Princeton University Press, 2020) shifts attention from Jefferson’s theology to the object’s reception: Adler’s acquisition, the Atlanta display, congressional printing, commercial editions, religious appropriation, and conservation. It is the leading book-length object biography. [Publisher’s introduction and index](https://pup-assets.imgix.net/onix/images/9780691209692/9780691209692.pdf?fm=pdf)

The American Institute for Conservation’s *Book and Paper Group Annual* published technical discussion of the source books and treatment, including the hypothesis that some page splits were deliberately made to facilitate clipping. That remains a conservation inference rather than a fact recorded by Jefferson. [AIC Book and Paper Group Annual 31](https://cool.culturalheritage.org/coolaic/sg/bpg/annual/v31/bpga31-ebook.pdf)

### Discredited or misleading popular theories

- The surviving object was made in the White House in 1804: **false; this conflates it with the lost precursor.**
- Jefferson wrote a complete new Bible: **false.**
- Every supernatural reference was removed: **false as an absolute statement.**
- Congress adopted it as official scripture: **false.**
- The Smithsonian owns only a later copy: **unsupported and contradicted by provenance and material evidence.**
- Hair found in the book has been proven to be Jefferson’s: **unverified.**
- The volume was designed for conversion of Native Americans: **unsupported for this object.**
- Jefferson published it during his lifetime: **false.**
