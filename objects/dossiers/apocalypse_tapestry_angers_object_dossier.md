# The Apocalypse Tapestry of Angers: Object Dossier

**Object:** The Apocalypse Tapestry of Angers  
**French:** *Tenture de l’Apocalypse*, *Tapisserie de l’Apocalypse*, *Apocalypse d’Angers*, *Tenture de Louis d’Anjou*  
**Date:** Commissioned after 1373; documented in production in 1377–1379; conventionally completed by about 1380–1382  
**Present location:** Domaine national du château d’Angers, Angers, Maine-et-Loire, France  
**Legal owner:** French state  
**Official heritage record:** Monument historique object PM49000182; classified 6 June 1902  
**UNESCO status:** Inscribed in the International Memory of the World Register in 2023  
**Physical status:** Real, substantially surviving medieval textile; dismembered and partly lost after 1782; reconstructed and restored from the nineteenth century onward; approximately 100–104 metres of the original ensemble survives, depending on the method of measurement and inclusion of fragments  
**Assessment date:** 6 September 2026

## Evidentiary key

- **Documented:** supported by contemporary accounts, inventories, chapter records, legal instruments, photographs, or official custody records.
- **Scientific or technical finding:** based on physical examination, conservation documentation, or laboratory comparison.
- **Strong scholarly reconstruction:** inference accepted in recent specialist literature but not stated verbatim in a contemporary record.
- **Disputed:** competing scholarly reconstructions remain.
- **Institutional interpretation:** an interpretation presented by the Château d’Angers, the French Ministry of Culture, UNESCO, or another custodian.
- **Tradition or anecdote:** reported retrospectively without adequate contemporary documentation.
- **Unestablished:** a claim that available evidence does not presently permit the dossier to verify.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Identity and names

The object is a monumental narrative wall hanging composed not of one continuously woven cloth but of a coordinated series of large tapestry pieces. It illustrates the Revelation, or Apocalypse, attributed in Christian tradition to John of Patmos. It was commissioned by Louis I, duke of Anjou, and executed in late-fourteenth-century Parisian tapestry production.

The principal French name, *Tenture de l’Apocalypse*, is the most precise. *Tenture* derives from *tendre*, “to stretch” or “hang,” and denotes an ensemble of textiles intended to be hung as architectural furnishing. *Tapisserie de l’Apocalypse* is equally established but may misleadingly suggest one indivisible carpet-like object. Medieval accounts called its components *tapiz*, *tappis*, or *draps de tapisserie à l’istoire de l’Apocalice*: tapestry cloths bearing the story of the Apocalypse. A 1380 royal-library note called the projected ensemble Louis’s *beau tapis*, “fine” or “beautiful tapestry.”

“Apocalypse” comes through Latin *apocalypsis* from Greek ἀποκάλυψις, *apokálypsis*, “uncovering” or “revelation.” The modern association of “apocalypse” primarily with catastrophic destruction describes only part of the textile’s program: its narrative culminates in divine victory and the New Jerusalem.

No medieval unitary title woven into the textile survives. The present title is descriptive and continuous with the language of the ducal accounts.

### 1.2 Object class

The tapestry is simultaneously:

- a monumental movable textile furnishing;
- a sequential pictorial cycle;
- a princely prestige object;
- a Christian scriptural visualization;
- a portable architectural environment for court and ceremonial display;
- in UNESCO’s present classification, a form of documentary heritage or “woven manuscript.”

It is not a liturgical vestment, relic, or permanently consecrated furnishing. Its prolonged custody and festival display in Angers Cathedral gave it a religious institutional life, but no reliable source establishes that it was treated as a miracle-working relic or cult object.

### 1.3 Original configuration

The best-supported recent reconstruction gives **six great pieces**, each organized around:

- one full-height enthroned or seated “Reader” beneath an architectural canopy; and
- fourteen narrative scenes, arranged in two horizontal registers of seven.

That scheme yields **six Readers plus eighty-four narrative scenes**. The French Monument historique record describes six pieces and eighty-four *tableaux*. Earlier scholarship frequently reconstructed seven pieces or counted ninety scenes; modern secondary sources continue to repeat “ninety.” The discrepancy reflects, among other things, differing treatment of the six introductory Readers, missing panels, nineteenth-century assembly, and an additional piece given to the cathedral by Anne of France in 1490. The six-piece reconstruction is discussed separately below.

The official heritage record gives the original ensemble as more than 140 metres long and approximately six metres high. Recent institutional descriptions usually place its original surface near **800–840 square metres**. Each great piece would have been approximately 23–24 metres long.

The presently displayed material measures approximately **100–104 metres long and 4.3–4.5 metres high**. The reduction in height results particularly from loss of the upper sky bands, lower flowery ground bands, borders, and explanatory inscriptions. Approximately **500 square metres** now survive in displayable form. The sixth original division is the most incomplete.

### 1.4 Materials

The surviving medieval structure is predominantly wool:

- undyed or naturally colored wool warp;
- colored wool weft;
- approximately twenty-five distinguishable color nuances in the present technical account;
- originally some metal-wrapped or metal-plated threads, including gold-bearing details, of which traces survive.

Institutional descriptions now emphasize wool and gold-plated threads. Older popular descriptions sometimes add extensive silk and silver. The 1961 examination found areas with gold thread, but the scale and precise composition of all metallic and non-wool components require more narrowly published analytical documentation before expansive claims about silk and silver should be treated as established.

The principal historic color ranges include:

- blue, conventionally associated with woad or pastel;
- red, associated with madder;
- yellow, associated with weld;
- ivory and natural wool tones;
- orange and green produced through dyeing or color combination.

Claims assigning every color to a specific plant should be treated cautiously unless linked to a published analytical result. The official 2025 exhibition account states that the yarns employed principally vegetable red, blue, and yellow colorants, with some mineral or other color sources.

### 1.5 Weaving technique

The tapestry was woven on a loom in tapestry weave, conventionally described as *tapisserie de basse lisse* or low-warp work. The weft entirely creates the visible image over the structural warp.

A defining feature is its so-called **“without a reverse”** construction, *sans envers*: thread ends and joins were concealed within the weave so that the image remains legible on both faces. The reverse is not literally identical in every technical respect, but it is unusually finished and pictorially coherent. Because it was protected from centuries of direct illumination, the reverse retains much stronger color and is a crucial record of the original appearance.

The 1961 examination, conducted after removal of later backing, found:

- exceptionally clean and vivid imagery on the reverse;
- extensive use of tapestry-weaving techniques;
- no use of one technique described in the published summary as *liures*;
- surviving metallic-thread areas;
- remnants of explanatory inscriptions;
- comparatively limited nineteenth-century reweaving over much of the surviving medieval fabric.

The textile’s reversibility supports the possibility that it was sometimes hung where both sides could be seen, perhaps as a freestanding enclosure or movable partition. That proposed use is plausible but not proven for every recorded display.

### 1.6 Color, composition, and imagery

The great pieces alternate dominant red and blue grounds. Figures are approximately human scale. The original lower edge presented a flowered earth; the upper edge continued into a populated celestial band with angels and other figures. The result was an environment in which the terrestrial foreground visually met the spectator’s space and the heavens rose above it.

Each division was introduced by a monumental Reader. The remaining scenes follow Revelation broadly from left to right and from the upper to lower register, although the exact original hanging sequence had to be reconstructed after dismemberment.

Subjects include:

- John on Patmos and the seven churches;
- Christ with the sword;
- God enthroned;
- John’s tears and the adoration of the elders;
- the Lamb and the opening of the seals;
- the Four Horsemen;
- the souls of the martyrs;
- earthquakes and cosmic portents;
- angels with trumpets;
- fire, Wormwood, darkened celestial bodies, and locusts;
- the angels at the Euphrates;
- the measuring of the Temple;
- the Two Witnesses;
- the Woman Clothed with the Sun;
- Michael and the dragon;
- the beasts from sea and earth;
- the number of the Beast;
- harvest and vintage;
- bowls of wrath;
- the Whore of Babylon;
- Babylon’s fall;
- the Last Judgment;
- the New Jerusalem.

The images are not merely transcriptions of the biblical text. Architecture, armor, clothing, furniture, musical instruments, ships, agriculture, heraldry, and urban forms belong to the visual world of the late fourteenth century.

### 1.7 Inscriptions and heraldry

Originally, explanatory texts in Gothic script ran beneath scenes. Most were removed or lost, particularly through cutting and nineteenth-century rearrangement. The Monument historique record expressly notes that a text beneath each scene once served as its identifying legend.

Heraldic and emblematic features include:

- the arms of Louis I of Anjou, France ancient—blue sown with gold fleurs-de-lis—differenced by a red border;
- the double-barred cross associated with Anjou;
- repeated letters or monograms interpreted in relation to Louis and his dynasty.

Earlier authors interpreted certain repeated “Y” forms as a reference to Yolande of Aragon and therefore as evidence that part of the ensemble was later. Later heraldic study rejected that chronology as unnecessary or implausible. The absence of the Jerusalem cross associated with Louis’s later Neapolitan claims has also been used to argue that the design substantially predates his active Sicilian kingship.

There is no surviving maker’s signature in the modern sense.

### 1.8 Damage, loss, and restoration

The textile has suffered:

- fading of its illuminated face;
- cutting into numerous independent panels and strips;
- loss of many complete scenes;
- loss of much of the upper and lower borders;
- loss or removal of explanatory captions;
- damp exposure;
- use as coverings, packing material, stable furnishing, floor covering, or building repair material;
- nineteenth-century washing, stitching, backing, reordering, reweaving, and completion;
- later stress from hanging and imperfect environmental control.

Published specialist summaries identify scenes 9, 29, 36, 41, and 75 as materially completed in late-nineteenth-century restoration. Some reconstructed panels consist of medieval fragments joined by *renchaînage*, a tapestry-weave joining method, now difficult to see from the front.

The present display therefore contains:

1. extensive original medieval fabric;
2. original fragments reassembled into reconstructed scenes;
3. nineteenth-century joins, supports, and reweaving;
4. later conservation supports;
5. separate fragments not incorporated into the principal hanging.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Commissioner and purpose

**Documented:** Louis I of Anjou, second son of King John II of France and brother of Charles V, commissioned the ensemble. Louis already possessed an exceptional tapestry collection: an inventory of 1364 listed seventy-six tapestries, many historiated and of substantial dimensions.

The commission expressed wealth, mobility, courtly magnificence, scriptural learning, and dynastic ambition. Recent interpretation also connects its heraldry and imagery to Louis’s political position during the Hundred Years’ War and to his ambitions in Naples, Sicily, and Jerusalem. These political readings are plausible but should not be mistaken for a surviving statement of intent by Louis.

### 2.2 Terminus post quem: Charles V’s Apocalypse manuscript

A 1373 inventory of Charles V’s library recorded an illustrated French Apocalypse. During a 1380 inspection, a note stated that the king had given or lent it to the duke of Anjou “to have his beautiful tapestry made.”

This manuscript has been identified with **Bibliothèque nationale de France, Français 403**, an Anglo-French illustrated Apocalypse produced at Salisbury in the thirteenth century. It contains seventy-eight half-page Apocalypse paintings, in addition to twelve images from the life of John.

**Strong scholarly conclusion:** because the manuscript was still in the royal inventory in 1373, design of the tapestry in its documented form probably did not begin earlier than 1373.

**Qualification:** Français 403 supplied a compositional and iconographic repertory but was not simply enlarged scene by scene. Jean de Bruges transformed its manuscript forms for monumental textile, and other related Anglo-French Apocalypse manuscripts may also preserve common prototypes.

### 2.3 Jean de Bruges’s designs

In January 1378, new style, a ducal account recorded a payment of **50 francs** to Hennequin de Bruges, painter to the king, toward what was or would become due for *pourtraitures et patrons* made for the Apocalypse tapestries.

Hennequin de Bruges is also known as:

- Jean de Bruges;
- Jean Bondol;
- Jean de Bondolf;
- Jan Bondol.

The distinction between *pourtraitures* and *patrons* suggests small-scale designs and full-scale working patterns, although scholars differ about how literally the payment language should be read. Fabienne Joubert has argued that the production may have involved several design levels and perhaps another hand, particularly in later sections where motifs recur and stylistic handling changes.

### 2.4 Nicolas Bataille’s role and the weaving shop

On 7 April 1377, new style, Louis paid **1,000 francs** to Nicolas Bataille toward the making of two Apocalypse tapestry cloths already made for the duke.

In June 1379, Bataille received **300 francs** toward a contract of **3,000 francs** for three further Apocalypse tapestries to be delivered and accepted by Christmas 1379.

These records establish Bataille’s central contractual role but do not necessarily mean that he personally wove the entire ensemble. Modern scholarship often identifies him more accurately as a Parisian tapestry merchant, entrepreneur, and coordinator.

The actual weaving is frequently assigned to the Paris workshop of **Robert Poisson**, also rendered **Poinçon** in later accounts. The attribution is a scholarly reconstruction based on documentary and workshop connections, not a surviving signature or complete production contract. Poisson is documented in connection with a different Apocalypse commission for Philip the Bold of Burgundy. Official and scholarly sources vary in describing him as weaver, workshop master, model maker, or probable manufacturer.

### 2.5 Place and date of production

Paris is the most probable production centre. The documented work spans at least 1377–1379. Institutions commonly date the completed ensemble:

- 1373/1375–1380/1382;
- 1377–1382;
- or, more cautiously, circa 1375–1382.

“Seven years” is a modern synthesis rather than a surviving record of uninterrupted production. The accounts explicitly document five tapestry cloths; the relationship of those five contracted cloths to the six reconstructed great pieces is not completely transparent. They may represent stages or subdivisions in production rather than the full final count.

### 2.6 Cost

The surviving accounts record at least:

- 1,000 francs toward two pieces;
- 50 francs toward Jean de Bruges’s designs and patterns;
- 300 francs paid on a 3,000-franc contract for three pieces.

These are installments, not a complete final account. The full material and labor cost is not securely recoverable from the cited records. Modern attempts to translate the amount into contemporary purchasing power are too method-dependent to present as fact.

### 2.7 Materials provenance

No surviving document securely identifies:

- the sheep breeds or geographic source of the wool;
- a particular dye workshop;
- the mines or metalworkers supplying the metallic threads;
- the exact loom locations within Paris.

Claims that the wool was specifically Flemish or that particular dyestuffs came from named regions are plausible within medieval trade patterns but remain unestablished for this object without direct analytical or archival proof.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

## 3.1 1370s–1380s: production and ducal possession

**Documented:** production was under way for Louis I of Anjou by 1377. Payments continued through 1379. The object entered the movable property of the Angevin ducal house.

Louis died in 1384 during his Italian campaign. No evidence examined for this dossier documents the tapestry traveling to Italy with him.

### 3.2 1400: Arles

The first specifically recorded ceremonial use generally cited is the wedding of Louis II of Anjou and Yolande of Aragon at Arles in 1400. The tapestry was reportedly hung in the courtyard of the archiepiscopal palace.

This episode demonstrates that the ensemble was transportable over a considerable distance and could turn a court or ecclesiastical courtyard into a temporary pictorial architecture.

### 3.3 1404: Angers Cathedral display

Angers institutional history records an installation in Saint-Maurice Cathedral in 1404, the principal local building large enough to accommodate a major display. This was a use, not a transfer of ownership: the tapestry remained ducal property.

### 3.4 Angevin dynastic custody

The textile passed within the Angevin princely house:

- from Louis I to his successor Louis II;
- through the line of Louis II and Yolande of Aragon;
- eventually to their son René of Anjou.

The exact physical residence of every piece at every moment is not documented. It should not be assumed that the ensemble remained permanently at the Château d’Angers. As a princely tapestry it could travel between residences and ceremonial sites.

A later reconstruction says Yolande retained or received it and at her death in 1442 passed it to René. That account is plausible as dynastic custody, but the precise legal mechanism and whether every component traveled together have not been independently established here.

### 3.5 Baugé and René’s bequest

By the later fifteenth century the tapestry was at René’s residence at Baugé. René’s testament of **22 July 1474** bequeathed the Apocalypse hanging to Angers Cathedral.

The transfer was not immediately completed. Political conflict with Louis XI affected René’s control of Anjou, and the textile remained at Baugé. After René’s death in 1480, a royal letter of **17 July 1480** authorized or implemented its delivery to the bishop and cathedral at Angers. Chapter records then describe six pieces.

This is the decisive documented change of title:

**Angevin ducal house → chapter and church of Saint-Maurice, Angers**, by testamentary bequest executed in 1480.

### 3.6 1490: Anne of France’s additional tapestry

On **29 May 1490**, Anne of France, duchess of Bourbon, gave another Apocalypse tapestry piece to the chapter. A 1505 inventory distinguished:

- six pieces given by King René;
- a seventh given by the duchess of Bourbon.

This seventh item was long treated as an original seventh division of Louis I’s ensemble. Donald King and Fabienne Joubert argued that it was a distinct but related Apocalypse tapestry, possibly one inherited through another Valois line. Its presence in the same cathedral collection helped create the later seven-piece reconstruction.

### 3.7 Cathedral storage and use, 1480–1760s

The chapter stored the tapestry rolled in chests and hung it on major occasions, including:

- Easter;
- Pentecost;
- All Saints;
- Christmas;
- the feast of Saint Maurice;
- solemn royal or episcopal entries.

The storage-and-display cycle probably contributed both to preservation and wear. Darkness in storage protected colors; repeated rolling, handling, suspension, and exposure stressed the textile.

The tapestry was displayed in the nave, transept, or choir according to architectural and ceremonial requirements. In 1623, four pieces were reportedly in the nave, while the rood screen interfered with sightlines to pieces in the choir.

A cathedral inventory or valuation of 1533 reportedly assigned the tapestry a value of 200,000 livres. That number is important evidence of perceived worth but should not be treated as a realizable market price without the original valuation context.

### 3.8 1767–1782: withdrawal and attempted sale

By the eighteenth century the tapestry’s medieval style had lost favor. In **1767**, the chapter removed at least part of it because the hangings were said to prejudice or impair the voices of the cathedral choir.

Installation of new choir woodwork from 1778 to 1783 further displaced the textile. Julien Péan de La Tuilerie’s 1778 description recorded a large surviving extent, although converting his measurements into modern dimensions is not straightforward.

On **5 April 1782**, the chapter offered its tapestries for sale. No satisfactory buyer was found for the Apocalypse ensemble. This failure prevented a complete commercial transfer but did not preserve the object intact.

### 3.9 Revolution: confiscation, dispersal, and destructive reuse

During the French Revolution, ecclesiastical property was nationalized. The Apocalypse tapestry was removed into a public art or property depot at the former Abbey of Saint-Serge.

Its later condition proves large-scale cutting and dispersion. Retrospective reports state that pieces were used as:

- frost covers for orange trees;
- horse blankets or stable insulation;
- floor mats;
- packing cloth;
- curtain linings;
- coverings over damaged walls;
- material in or near convent and cathedral service areas.

The exact evidentiary status differs by episode. The Saint-Serge depot and dismemberment are documented broadly; several individual uses derive from reports collected decades afterward, including recollections by cathedral personnel. They are credible descriptions of the textile’s degradation but not all are supported by contemporaneous invoices or orders.

It is misleading to say simply that the tapestry was “stolen.” Its church ownership was extinguished or transformed by revolutionary confiscation, followed by administrative neglect, cutting, alienation, and private appropriation. Some fragments undoubtedly left public or ecclesiastical custody, but the identities of individual takers are largely unknown.

### 3.10 1803–1825: partial restitution and renewed neglect

Bishop Charles Montault-Désilles reportedly obtained restitution of surviving pieces in **1803**. Another recollection gives **1806** for recovery. The difference probably reflects separate administrative and physical stages.

At that time the remains were reportedly divided into about fifteen large assemblages containing fifty-eight scenes, joined in groups without regard to narrative sequence. They were repaired sufficiently for renewed display in the cathedral transept.

In **1825**, cathedral refurnishing led to another removal. The fragments were stored at the episcopal palace or former seminary, in conditions later described as damp and damaging.

A once-common account said Bishop Guillaume Angebault bought the tapestry from the state Domain administration in 1843 for 300 francs and gave it to the cathedral. Archival work by Antoine Ruais found that the Apocalypse pieces do not appear among the episcopal furnishings Angebault purchased. The more likely reconstruction is that church property already deposited at the bishop’s residence was mistakenly treated as the bishop’s personal property and then administratively returned to the cathedral.

### 3.11 1846–1848: Louis-François Joubert’s intervention

Louis-François Joubert became custodian of the cathedral treasury in 1846. In **1848** he secured episcopal recognition that the tapestry should return to cathedral control and began systematic examination.

His inventory recorded approximately fifty-five pieces then in hand. Removal of later linings exposed seven additional scenes or substantial portions concealed within the assemblages. Joubert compared the textiles with the biblical narrative and surviving Apocalypse imagery and began to undo arbitrary groupings.

The celebrated “rediscovery of 1848” should therefore be understood as an act of recognition, classification, and rescue. The tapestry had not vanished completely; fragments had remained in ecclesiastical or episcopal storage, but their order, significance, and identity as a major medieval ensemble had largely been lost.

### 3.12 1849–1863: first major restoration campaign

Under Joubert’s direction, the remains underwent:

- unpicking of inappropriate assemblies;
- narrative reordering;
- sewing and joining;
- washing;
- backing;
- *renchaînage* of adjoining fragments;
- limited reweaving where gaps prevented direct joining.

Joubert trained or supervised local women weavers for the work and obtained material and institutional funding. The cathedral fabric council began modest support in 1852; the state increased assistance in 1854.

This restoration preserved a textile otherwise at serious risk. It also transformed the object. Modern conservation distinguishes Joubert’s reconstruction from untouched medieval fabric: his decisions fixed a sequence, joined fragments, and sometimes continued backgrounds, contours, or figures beyond what survived.

### 3.13 Documentation and public exhibition

Beginning in 1853, Léon de Joannis prepared line engravings of the tapestry. Publication in the 1860s provided an important visual record of its nineteenth-century state.

The ensemble or selected panels appeared at major exhibitions. Sources consulted for this dossier support participation in the **1867 Paris Universal Exposition** and the display of two panels at an Union centrale des beaux-arts exhibition in **1876**. A source giving “1864 Universal Exhibition” appears to conflate the publication or another exhibition with the 1867 Exposition Universelle.

In **1868**, Joubert located additional panels and substantial fragments in a private house. Their earlier route out of institutional custody is unrecorded.

### 3.14 1863–1900: continued and more interventionist restoration

After Joubert’s retirement, Canon Alexandre Machefer succeeded him as custodian. Joubert, Machefer, and historian Louis de Farcy participated in a conservation commission. Funding increased after 1868.

Later nineteenth-century work was more interventionist. Missing portions and, in several cases, large compositional areas were woven anew. Surviving technical scholarship specifically identifies scenes numbered 9, 29, 36, 41, and 75 as substantially completed in this period.

By **1870**, the reconstructed ensemble had formally returned to display in the cathedral.

### 3.15 1902–1905: state protection and ownership

The tapestry was classified as a French Monument historique object on **6 June 1902**.

Under the 1905 separation of Church and State and the subsequent inventories of ecclesiastical goods, the tapestry entered French public ownership while remaining associated with or assigned to cathedral use. The legal history should not be simplified into a voluntary cathedral gift: state ownership followed the statutory settlement of pre-existing church property.

### 3.16 1910–1953: episcopal-palace museum

In **1910**, the Beaux-Arts administration obtained use of the former episcopal palace as a tapestry museum. Most of the Angers textile collection, including the Apocalypse, could be displayed there more continuously than in the cathedral.

Emmanuel-Louis Mas photographed the tapestry in Angers in **1943**, providing evidence that it remained accessible to official heritage photography during the occupation. The sources consulted do not establish a documented wartime evacuation, concealment, seizure attempt, or transport. Claims about its precise protective arrangements during 1939–1945 should therefore be marked **unestablished** until archival movement files are examined.

In 1937 or 1938—local sources differ by one year—the artist Jean Lurçat encountered the tapestry in Angers. The discrepancy may reflect an initial visit and a later decisive viewing.

### 3.17 1953–1954: return to the Château d’Angers

The French army returned the castle to the Monuments historiques administration in 1947. In 1952 the state decided to build a dedicated tapestry gallery.

Architect Bernard Vitry designed an L-shaped gallery on the south and east sides of the seigneurial courtyard. Construction occurred in **1953–1954**, and the Apocalypse tapestry was installed there in **1954**. This was a physical return to the ducal castle associated with its early history, but not necessarily to a documented original room.

### 3.18 1961 examination

In **1961**, conservators removed the backing, cleaned the textile, photographed and studied its reverse, and assessed the extent of restoration. This campaign materially changed understanding of the work:

- the reverse preserved much stronger medieval color;
- the finishing confirmed the exceptional *sans envers* technique;
- metallic-thread remains and fragments of inscriptions were found;
- much surviving imagery proved less heavily reworked than observers had feared.

### 3.19 1981–1982 conservation

The official Monument historique record documents:

- removal from display;
- dust removal;
- replacement of the backing or lining.

This was a preventive-conservation intervention rather than an attempt to restore missing imagery.

### 3.20 1990s gallery reconstruction

After about forty years, the Vitry gallery no longer provided adequate environmental conditions. Fading and concerns over air quality, lighting, humidity, and hanging systems led to closure and major redevelopment.

The refurbished gallery reopened in **1996**, with:

- reduced and controlled illumination;
- improved ventilation and humidity management;
- revised mounting;
- cleaner air;
- a dark architectural setting designed to reduce further light damage.

The gallery’s present penumbra is therefore a conservation measure, not an attempt to recreate medieval lighting.

### 3.21 1924–2025: the Ratton-Ladrière fragments

A group of approximately thirty small textile fragments was acquired in **1924** by Paris dealer Charles Ratton from the German dealer Otto Wegener. How Wegener obtained them is unknown.

During an inventory in the COVID-19 lockdown of **2020**, the Galerie Charles Ratton & Guy Ladrière rediscovered the fragments in storage. Stylistic resemblance suggested a connection to the Angers tapestry.

At the request of the Pays de la Loire regional cultural authority:

- textile conservator Montaine Bongrand examined them;
- Mohamed Dallel, head of the textile division at the Laboratoire de recherche des monuments historiques, carried out comparative technical work.

**Technical finding:** the analyses confirmed that the fragments belonged to the Apocalypse Tapestry. They form part of a decorative fleuron frieze associated with the monumental Reader introducing the fourth great piece; the colors, dimensions, patterns, and technique match.

**Custody reconstruction:** documentary and visual evidence suggests, but does not prove, that they were recovered in the 1860s and set aside because they could not be incorporated into the contemporary reconstruction. Their movement from Angers into the art market remains undocumented.

Guy and Sandrine Ladrière donated them to the French state in **2021**. They were restored at the state’s direction and reunited administratively with the tapestry. One was exhibited at Angers in *L’Apocalypse, et après…* through 1 June 2025.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Is the displayed textile Louis I’s medieval commission?

Yes, to a high degree of documentary and material certainty. The identification rests on a converging body of evidence:

- ducal payment accounts;
- the royal-library note concerning the manuscript model;
- Angevin and cathedral custody records;
- iconographic and heraldic content;
- medieval weaving technique;
- early modern inventories;
- nineteenth-century documentation made during recovery;
- physical continuity among the fragments.

There is no serious rival physical object claiming to be Louis I’s Apocalypse tapestry.

The correct qualification is that the present display is **the surviving, reconstructed remainder of the original ensemble**, not the complete untouched object as it existed circa 1382.

### 4.2 Six or seven original pieces?

**Earlier reconstruction:** seven great pieces, partly because seven is prominent in Revelation and because cathedral inventories listed seven Apocalypse textiles after Anne of France’s 1490 donation.

**Current stronger reconstruction:** Louis I’s ensemble contained six pieces. Each held fourteen narrative scenes and one introductory Reader. Cathedral records associated six with René’s bequest and identified a seventh as Anne’s separate donation.

Donald King’s reconstruction, accepted and developed by Fabienne Joubert and later French scholarship, explains the documentary evidence without requiring an incomplete later continuation for Yolande of Aragon.

The question is historiographically important because nineteenth- and twentieth-century cataloguing physically and intellectually arranged the fragments under the seven-piece assumption.

### 4.3 Eighty-four or ninety scenes?

The answer depends on terminology:

- six pieces × fourteen narrative panels = eighty-four;
- six full-height Readers are additional large compositions;
- if Readers are counted with the narrative units, the total becomes ninety pictorial fields.

Some publications use “ninety scenes”; the French heritage record uses eighty-four *tableaux* plus the introductory figures. Statements that only sixty-nine, seventy-one, or seventy-eight survive likewise reflect different counting systems:

- complete scenes only;
- complete scenes plus fragments;
- inventory units as presently mounted;
- narrative scenes versus Readers.

The official Palissy record presently identifies sixty-nine complete panels and nine fragments as inventory numbers 1–78. These figures should not be forced into apparent agreement with every popular count.

### 4.4 Designer versus maker

**Documented:** Jean de Bruges was paid for designs and patterns; Nicolas Bataille received the principal tapestry payments.

**Probable:** Bataille coordinated production as merchant-contractor; Robert Poisson/Poinçon’s workshop undertook at least much of the weaving.

**Unestablished:** the names and number of individual weavers, dyers, spinners, and assistants.

Descriptions of Bataille as the sole “artist” or personal weaver overstate the evidence. Descriptions of Poisson as certainly responsible for every surviving panel also exceed the surviving contracts.

### 4.5 Relationship to BnF Français 403

The manuscript’s documented loan or transfer for making the tapestry establishes a direct project connection. It does not demonstrate mechanical copying.

Scholars have variously emphasized:

- Français 403;
- BnF Latin 14410, from Saint-Victor;
- manuscripts at Cambrai, Metz, Namur, and elsewhere;
- a broader Anglo-French Apocalypse family;
- Jean de Bruges’s independent transformation of manuscript prototypes.

The most defensible conclusion is that Français 403 was an important and documented resource, while the tapestry’s iconography reflects a larger manuscript tradition and substantial creative adaptation.

### 4.6 Nineteenth-century additions

The restoration history is not a forgery scandal: the cathedral did not secretly replace the object and market the result as entirely untouched. Nevertheless, earlier display and publication did not always distinguish medieval weaving from nineteenth-century completion as rigorously as current conservation ethics would require.

Later examination identified extensive original fabric and also major reconstructed areas. Scenes 9, 29, 36, 41, and 75 require particular caution. Any iconographic argument based on those restored details must establish whether the relevant form is medieval or nineteenth-century.

### 4.7 Ratton fragments

The fragments rediscovered in 2020 are authenticated by comparative technical examination, not merely by dealer tradition. Their identity as parts of the tapestry is secure according to the responsible French laboratory and cultural authority.

Their provenance between probable nineteenth-century recovery and Otto Wegener’s ownership is unresolved. Authentication of fabric does not authenticate every proposed step in its market history.

### 4.8 Scientific dating

No published radiocarbon campaign specifically establishing the date of the principal tapestry was identified in the sources used here. Its dating does not depend principally on radiocarbon analysis because contemporary financial records securely anchor manufacture.

Likewise, no DNA analysis of wool, isotopic sourcing of fibers, or comprehensive publicly accessible molecular-dye survey was identified. Assertions that such tests have precisely located the sheep, dye plants, or workshops should not be accepted without a conservation or laboratory publication.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Princely ceremonial furnishing

The object’s earliest function was probably princely rather than narrowly liturgical. Its scale, cost, heraldry, reversibility, and portability made it suitable for:

- marriages;
- solemn entries;
- diplomatic receptions;
- feasts;
- outdoor or courtyard enclosures;
- temporary halls;
- dynastic ceremonies.

Its first widely cited documented deployment, at the Arles wedding in 1400, exemplifies this use.

The hypothesis that its six pieces stood on wooden structures surrounding spectators like a tournament enclosure is plausible but not proven by a surviving installation plan.

### 6.2 Cathedral use

After 1480, the tapestry became a cathedral treasure. It was hung for major feasts and solemn occasions rather than remaining continuously exposed.

That use had several functions:

- visualization of Revelation;
- transformation of cathedral architecture;
- proclamation of the cathedral’s association with René and the Angevin dynasty;
- display of institutional wealth;
- commemoration of its donor;
- ceremonial reception of rulers and prelates.

No complete medieval or early modern hanging manual survives in the sources consulted. Precise sequences, attachment systems, prayers, or processional procedures cannot be reconstructed.

### 6.3 Order of the True Cross

Modern Château interpretation proposes that the tapestry may have decorated ceremonies of Louis’s chivalric Order of the True Cross because double-barred crosses appear on angelic banners. This is an **institutional hypothesis**, not a documented occasion of use. The emblems certainly relate the heavenly narrative to Angevin political symbolism, but the specific order-related function remains unproven.

### 6.4 Political and dynastic meaning

The tapestry’s scale was itself a political fact. It converted costly material, labor, royal artistic service, and scripture into a mobile statement of rank.

Its imagery could support several contemporary readings:

- divine order overcoming chaos;
- the righteous prince aligned with heavenly justice;
- criticism or demonization of military enemies;
- hope amid war, plague, famine, and schism;
- Angevin claims to sacral and dynastic legitimacy.

Some figures wear armor or occupy cities resembling the late-fourteenth-century world. Scholars and modern interpreters have found allusions to English enemies in the Hundred Years’ War. Such correspondences are persuasive in places but should not turn every demonic figure into a securely identified political portrait.

### 6.5 Liturgical status and alleged powers

No evidence located establishes:

- relic status;
- miracle-working power;
- protective efficacy against fire, plague, or siege;
- a curse on those who damage it;
- prescribed touching or veneration;
- pilgrimage indulgences attached specifically to it.

Its sacred significance derived from subject, setting, and ecclesiastical custody, not from a documented claim that the material textile possessed supernatural agency.

### 6.6 Access rules

Historically, handling was controlled by princely servants and later cathedral personnel, but no medieval taboo restricting sight or touch has been identified.

Current access is regulated by ordinary monument security and conservation:

- public viewing is permitted;
- the gallery is kept dim;
- flash photography is prohibited;
- direct touching is forbidden;
- handling and examination require heritage authority.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

The tapestry has accumulated surprisingly little autonomous folklore compared with relics, regalia, or weapons. Its “legends” are principally modern simplifications of real events.

### 7.1 “The lost tapestry rediscovered in 1848”

**Modern compressed narrative:** Joubert found a vanished masterpiece in 1848.

**Documented core:** he recognized, classified, and rescued dispersed fragments that had remained in ecclesiastical or episcopal storage and elsewhere.

**Correction:** the object was not recovered intact from a single hiding place. “Rediscovery” describes the recovery of identity and significance as much as physical location.

### 7.2 Horse blankets and orange-tree covers

**Retrospective but plausible tradition:** tapestry pieces were used for horses, orange trees, floor coverings, packing, curtains, and wall repair.

**Evidence:** these accounts were recorded during nineteenth-century recovery and are consistent with the cutting, staining, wear, and dispersion of the surviving textile.

**Qualification:** the dossier cannot assign a contemporary revolutionary document to every individual use. They should be reported as credible episodes of destructive reuse, not embroidered into stories about named vandals.

### 7.3 “Looted during the Revolution”

This phrase mixes several processes:

- legal revolutionary confiscation;
- storage in a public depot;
- official neglect;
- unauthorized cutting or alienation;
- private reuse.

“Looted” is accurate for some dispersal in a broad sense but too simple as a chain-of-custody description.

### 7.4 “Seven because Revelation is the book of seven”

The seven-piece scheme acquired symbolic attractiveness because Revelation is structured by sevens: churches, seals, trumpets, bowls, and other sequences.

The documentary distinction between René’s six pieces and Anne of France’s additional piece undermines the claim that Louis necessarily ordered seven great tapestries. The numerological scheme is a historiographic accretion, not established original intent.

### 7.5 “A medieval comic strip”

Calling the ensemble the world’s largest medieval “comic strip” or *bande dessinée* is a modern pedagogical metaphor. It accurately conveys sequential image-text narrative but is not a medieval classification. It can obscure the textile’s architectural scale, ceremonial mobility, heraldic program, and originally integrated captions.

### 7.6 “Prophecy of the modern ecological or nuclear crisis”

Modern audiences frequently read the poisoned waters, falling stars, war, famine, and burning cities as predictions of nuclear war or ecological collapse. These are modern receptions, not evidence that the medieval makers foresaw modern events.

The analogy has nevertheless produced real cultural effects: exhibitions, artistic commissions, environmental interpretation, and especially Jean Lurçat’s modern tapestry response.

### 7.7 No documented curse or miracle cycle

No credible early or later source located for this dossier supplies a curse, survival prophecy, miraculous rescue, or supernatural punishment attached specifically to the textile. Claims of that sort should be considered modern invention unless a dated source is produced.

## 8. Current Status and Location

As of **6 September 2026**, the tapestry is on permanent public display in the dedicated gallery of the **Domaine national du château d’Angers**, 2 promenade du Bout-du-Monde, Angers, France.

The official event and visitor pages list the exhibition as open throughout the year on the monument’s calendar. The tapestry gallery closes fifteen minutes before the monument. Access is included with admission; the route is accessible to wheelchair users and visitors with strollers. The gallery offers guided visits, an audio guide, tactile interpretation, Braille and large-print material, and an introductory film.

### Present physical environment

The textile is:

- mounted vertically in a long, controlled gallery;
- kept under low illumination;
- protected by a prohibition on flash;
- housed in an air-conditioned space reported by the monument as maintained near 19°C during heat events;
- monitored within a preventive-conservation system developed during the 1990s reconstruction.

### Ownership and control

- **Owner:** French state.
- **Heritage authority:** Ministry of Culture, including the relevant regional heritage services.
- **Site manager:** Centre des monuments nationaux.
- **Legal protection:** classified Monument historique object since 1902.
- **International recognition:** UNESCO Memory of the World, 2023.

No active repatriation, church-state ownership lawsuit, or rival title claim was identified. The former cathedral association remains historically important, but the state’s present ownership is officially recorded.

### Inventory and catalogue identity

The collective Monument historique identifier is **PM49000182**. The official record describes the components through inventory numbers 1–78, representing complete scenes and fragments. This is a heritage-protection identifier rather than necessarily a museum accession number in the conventional single-object format.

### Recovered fragments

The Ratton-Ladrière fragments were donated to the state, restored, and reunited with the object’s institutional corpus. At least one was publicly shown through 1 June 2025. Available sources do not establish that the fragments have been permanently rewoven into the main display; they should be understood as separately mounted associated originals.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Nineteenth-century rediscovery and Gothic revival

Joubert’s reconstruction, Joannis’s engravings, exhibition in Paris, and Louis de Farcy’s publications transformed the tapestry from neglected furnishing into a canonical medieval artwork.

This reception was not passive scholarship. It physically altered the object:

- fragments were selected and ordered;
- discontinuities were closed;
- lost imagery was recreated;
- cathedral display converted the remains into an apparently continuous pictorial cycle.

The nineteenth century thus both saved the tapestry and created the form in which later audiences learned to see it.

### 9.2 Jean Lurçat and *Le Chant du Monde*

Jean Lurçat’s encounter with the Apocalypse Tapestry in 1937 or 1938 became a foundational episode in modern French tapestry history. He admired:

- the limited but forceful palette;
- monumental, legible forms;
- integration of image and weaving;
- capacity to address collective catastrophe.

His *Le Chant du Monde*, woven at Aubusson from 1957 until his death in 1966, deliberately answers the medieval Apocalypse with a twentieth-century cycle. Its early sections confront atomic war and mass destruction; later sections turn toward renewal. The surviving ten tapestries measure about eighty metres and are displayed at the Musée Jean-Lurçat et de la Tapisserie contemporaine in Angers.

The association has materially shaped Angers’s cultural identity: the city presents the medieval Apocalypse and Lurçat’s modern response as paired monumental cycles.

### 9.3 UNESCO Memory of the World

France submitted the tapestry for UNESCO Memory of the World recognition in 2021. UNESCO inscribed it in 2023 as the oldest and largest surviving narrative tapestry and as an irreplaceable documentary source.

This designation treats the textile not only as fine art but as a document:

- of medieval Apocalypse interpretation;
- of Anglo-French manuscript transmission;
- of Valois and Angevin political culture;
- of tapestry manufacture;
- of ceremonial use;
- of preservation, damage, and reconstruction.

### 9.4 Replicas and reproduction

The tapestry has been reproduced through:

- nineteenth-century engravings;
- photographic campaigns;
- official books and foldouts;
- educational facsimiles;
- postcards, posters, textiles, and souvenirs;
- digital scene-by-scene interpretation;
- immersive films;
- comic-book and family-education formats.

No full-scale woven replica with an independent rival identity was identified. Commercial reproductions are representations, not competing claimants.

### 9.5 Contemporary exhibitions and art

The 2024–2025 exhibition *L’Apocalypse, et après…* placed the medieval work in dialogue with later apocalyptic art and presented one of the recovered fragments.

Current programming includes:

- tours on gesture, warfare, disaster, color, and weaving;
- artist performances responding to missing scenes;
- family interpretation;
- the “Festival de l’Apocalypse”;
- contemporary installations and music in the gallery.

These programs continue a long history in which the object changes meaning through each new architectural and social setting.

### 9.6 Tourism and civic identity

The tapestry is one of Angers’s defining cultural attractions. It links:

- Château d’Angers tourism;
- cathedral history;
- the Jean Lurçat museum;
- textile education;
- UNESCO branding;
- regional heritage publications;
- commercial imagery.

Its presentation as a “monster” or “sacred monster” of heritage is modern promotional language. Its significance does not depend on that rhetoric, but such language demonstrates how the object has become an emblem of Angers.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Primary documentary base

The strongest evidence consists of:

- Louis I’s financial accounts, including Archives nationales, KK 242, folio 66v and related entries;
- Charles V’s royal-library inventories of 1373 and 1380;
- BnF Français 403;
- René of Anjou’s testament of 22 July 1474;
- Louis XI’s letter of 17 July 1480;
- Angers cathedral chapter registers and inventories, particularly those distinguishing René’s six pieces from Anne of France’s additional gift;
- eighteenth-century chapter deliberations concerning removal and sale;
- Joubert’s reports and inventories preserved in episcopal or cathedral archives;
- nineteenth-century engravings and photographs;
- the Monument historique protection file;
- twentieth-century conservation photographs;
- laboratory and conservation records for the newly recovered fragments.

### 10.2 Foundational scholarship

Important stages include:

- Léon de Joannis’s nineteenth-century visual publication;
- Louis de Farcy’s *Histoire et description des tapisseries de la Cathédrale d’Angers*;
- Jules Guiffrey’s recovery and publication of the Nicolas Bataille account entries;
- Léopold Delisle and Paul Meyer’s study of French Apocalypse manuscripts;
- René Planchenault’s catalogues and reconstruction work;
- Donald King’s reassessment of the number of original pieces;
- Fabienne Joubert’s studies of manufacture, design process, and early historiated tapestry;
- Antoine Ruais’s archival work on cathedral custody and restoration;
- Christian de Mérindol’s heraldic analysis;
- the *Cahiers de l’Inventaire* catalogue;
- Jacques Cailleteau and Francis Muel’s modern synthesis.

### 10.3 Changing attribution

Early scholars commonly described Nicolas Bataille simply as the tapestry’s weaver. Later work distinguishes:

- Jean de Bruges as designer;
- Bataille as merchant, contractor, or production coordinator;
- Poisson/Poinçon as probable workshop master;
- anonymous weavers as the actual makers.

This is not merely semantic. Court tapestry production was collaborative, and modern “single artist” labels distort the surviving payment structure.

### 10.4 Source-manuscript debate

Older scholarship sought one direct model, alternately favoring BnF Français 403 or Latin 14410. The documentary loan of Français 403 gives it privileged status, but comparison reveals departures and affiliations with a broader Anglo-French family.

Current understanding emphasizes a design process:

**manuscript tradition → small designs → full-scale patterns → translation by weavers**, with invention and adaptation at each stage.

### 10.5 Reconstruction debate

The principal structural dispute concerned:

- six versus seven pieces;
- eighty-four versus ninety scenes;
- the position of fragmentary images;
- whether later parts were designed for Yolande of Aragon;
- how the Readers and caption bands functioned.

The six-piece reconstruction now fits the cathedral evidence best, but popular sources and older catalogues still repeat the seven-piece model.

### 10.6 Conservation ethics

Joubert’s restoration must be judged in its historical context. Without it, much more could have disappeared. Under modern standards, however, washing, extensive joining, reweaving, and completion complicate authenticity.

Current scholarship asks not “is it authentic?” in the abstract, but:

- which fibers and woven forms are medieval;
- which joins belong to Joubert;
- which images were recreated later;
- which borders and captions have vanished;
- how modern mounting changes the reconstructed sequence.

### 10.7 Unresolved questions

The following remain incompletely established:

1. The exact year the commission began.
2. The complete final cost.
3. The identities and number of individual weavers.
4. Whether Robert Poisson wove all or only part of it.
5. The original source of its wool and every dye.
6. Its complete itinerary between Louis I’s death and the securely recorded later Angevin uses.
7. Its exact hanging arrangements in ducal ceremonies.
8. The degree to which particular contemporary enemies are encoded in monsters and riders.
9. The precise circumstances in which each fragment was dispersed after 1782.
10. The chain by which the Ratton fragments reached Otto Wegener.
11. The precise wartime handling of the tapestry during 1939–1945.
12. Whether a comprehensive modern fiber, metal-thread, and dye-analysis report exists but remains unpublished or inaccessible.
