# Joan of Arc's Banner: Object Dossier

**Proposed filename:** `joan_of_arcs_banner_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary key

This dossier concerns the large military standard made for Joan of Arc in 1429, while distinguishing it from the smaller ensign or pennon commissioned with it. Medieval witnesses and later writers frequently confused the two.

Evidence is labeled as follows:

- **Documented:** established by a contemporary administrative record or direct testimony.
- **Contemporary report:** written during Joan’s lifetime but not necessarily by an eyewitness.
- **Retrospective testimony:** given later by a participant or witness.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** an inference from incomplete or conflicting evidence.
- **Institutional tradition:** maintained by a museum, church, municipality, or commemorative body.
- **Legend or late accretion:** unsupported as an event in the physical object’s history.
- **Unresolved:** the evidence does not permit a firm conclusion.

The central finding is straightforward: a physical large standard demonstrably existed in 1429–1430, but it is now lost. No textile currently preserved in a museum or church has a documented chain of custody from Joan’s standard. Its material, appearance, movements, and disappearance must be reconstructed from accounts, chronicles, trial testimony, and later images—not from an extant artifact.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity and names

The object is generally called:

- **French:** *l’étendard de Jeanne d’Arc*, *l’étendard de la Pucelle*, occasionally *la bannière de Jeanne d’Arc*.
- **Middle French forms:** *estandart*, *estaindard*, *banière*, *enseigne*.
- **Latin trial terminology:** *vexillum*.
- **English:** Joan of Arc’s banner or standard.

Strictly, *étendard*, *bannière*, *pennon*, and *enseigne* were not interchangeable technical categories. Joan herself was asked whether she had an “*estandart ou banière*,” indicating that even contemporary judicial vocabulary was not perfectly stable. The royal payment account is more useful: it distinguishes “*ung grant estandart et ung petit*”—one large standard and one small one. Later narrative sources identify the smaller object more specifically as a *pennon*.

This dossier tracks the **large standard**. The smaller pennon is included where necessary because descriptions of its Annunciation imagery, shield, dove, and inscription have repeatedly been transferred to reconstructions of the large standard.

### What survives

**No authenticated fabric, fringe, pole, finial, painted fragment, or other physical component of the 1429 large standard is known to survive.** It consequently has:

- no museum accession number;
- no measurable present dimensions or weight;
- no published conservation record;
- no radiocarbon, fiber, pigment, or dye analysis;
- no continuous post-1430 provenance.

All exact modern images are reconstructions.

### Materials

Joan described the standard at Rouen as white, “of white cloth or *boucassin*,” with silk fringe. The French wording transmitted in the trial tradition is approximately:

> “de toile blanche ou boucassin … et les franges étaient de soie.”

*Boucassin* denotes a fine textile, usually understood as cotton or a cotton-like cloth, although medieval usage was not entirely uniform. Joan’s uncertainty between ordinary white cloth and *boucassin* cautions against identifying the fiber more precisely. The royal account states that painter Hauves Poulnoir supplied both the painting and the materials but does not itemize them.

Later chronicles call the ground white satin. That is not as strong as Joan’s testimony and may reflect literary substitution or confusion between the large standard and another ensign.

The decoration was painted rather than securely documented as embroidered. Philippe Contamine emphasizes that the account pays Poulnoir as a painter for having “painted and supplied materials.” A painted thin cloth normally could not carry unrelated designs on opposite faces, because the medium would penetrate or remain visible through the fabric. This fact is important when assessing elaborate modern two-sided reconstructions.

### Form and dimensions

No dimensions survive. The probable form was that of a long, tapering military standard, likely terminating in divided tails. Its size cannot responsibly be converted into exact modern measurements.

The long two-tailed form reproduced in many modern reconstructions is plausible by comparison with fifteenth-century standards but is **not directly measured from Joan’s object**. Modern rectangular procession banners are not replicas of its construction.

### Securely or strongly attested decoration

The following features have the best evidence:

1. **White ground.**  
   Attested by Joan, the 9 July 1429 report preserved by Antonio Morosini, Dunois, and later chronicles.

2. **A divine figure holding the world.**  
   Joan said “Our Lord” or God was represented holding the world. Pasquerel remembered Christ seated in judgment upon the clouds. A contemporary Italian report describes the Lord blessing with one hand and holding the world with the other.

3. **Two angels.**  
   Joan repeatedly confirmed two angels, painted in the conventional manner seen in churches. She declined to identify them as literal portraits of the visionary figures who appeared to her.

4. **One or more fleurs-de-lis.**  
   Sources disagree about whether each angel carried a lily, whether one presented a single lily for divine blessing, and whether lilies were scattered across the field or concentrated on the tails.

5. **The names “Jhesus Maria.”**  
   Joan recalled that the names were written on it, though her phrasing—“as it seems to her”—was guarded. “Jhesus” is a Middle French spelling of Jesus; *Maria* is the Latin form of Mary.

6. **Silk fringe.**  
   Stated by Joan.

### Major descriptive variants

| Source | Date/status | Description |
|---|---:|---|
| Clément de Fauquembergue | 10 May 1429; contemporary, not eyewitness | Sketches Joan with a two-tailed ensign marked “JHS.” |
| Anonymous *Bourgeois de Paris* | 1429; contemporary, not eyewitness | Says only “Jhesus” was written on her standard. |
| Albi report | May 1429; contemporary hearsay | Places Our Lady on the ensign; probably describes or confuses the pennon. |
| Letter reported by Antonio Morosini | 9 July 1429 | White standard; the Lord “in manner of a Trinity,” holding the world and blessing; an angel on either side presenting fleurs-de-lis. |
| Joan at Rouen | 27 February and March 1431; direct testimony | White cloth or *boucassin*; field associated with lilies; world and two angels; “Jhesus Maria”; silk fringe; God holding the world. |
| Jean Pasquerel | 1456; participant recalling 1429 | Christ seated in judgment on clouds; an angel holding a fleur-de-lis that Christ blessed. |
| Jean de Dunois | 1456; participant recalling 1429 | White standard; Our Lord associated with a fleur-de-lis. |
| *Chronique de Tournai* | compiled 1455 | Christ seated over a rainbow, showing his wounds; angels beside him with lilies. |
| *Journal du siège d’Orléans* | compiled c.1467 | Large white standard with two angels holding lilies; separate pennon with the Annunciation. |
| *Mystère du siège d’Orléans* | final form c.1470 | White field, shining sun or mandorla, Majesty, two angels, lilies, and “Ave Maria”; poetically shaped and textually derivative. |

These descriptions are not perfectly reconcilable. In particular:

- “God the Father holding the world,” “Christ in judgment,” and a Trinitarian figure may be divergent readings of related iconography.
- One angel with a lily and another with a sword fits contemporary Last Judgment imagery better than two lily-bearing angels, but the sword is a **scholarly reconstruction**, not an uncontested textual fact.
- A reverse showing an Annunciation, a French shield, or a dove probably results from combining the large standard with the smaller pennon.
- No source securely proves that Charles VII, Charlemagne, or Saint Louis appeared on the large standard. Such figures belong to later interpretive reconstructions.

The City of Orléans’s historical exhibition concluded that an exact recreation is impossible because no eyewitness drawing survives, descriptions conflict, and no other work by Poulnoir has been identified.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Documentary manufacture

The object’s existence is secured by a royal financial record: the thirteenth and final account of **Hémon Raguier**, treasurer of wars for Charles VII. It records a payment of **25 livres tournois** to:

> “Hauves Poulnoir, paintre, demourant à Tours, pour avoir paint et baillié estoffes pour ung grant estandart et ung petit pour la Pucelle.”

Translation: “Hauves Poulnoir, painter residing at Tours, for having painted and supplied materials for one large standard and one small one for the Maid.”

This is the object’s strongest origin document. It establishes:

- patronage by or through the royal military administration;
- manufacture by Hauves Poulnoir;
- Tours as the maker’s residence and overwhelmingly likely place of work;
- a paired commission, large and small;
- painting plus textile supply;
- a combined payment of 25 livres tournois.

It does **not** establish:

- an exact completion date;
- separate prices for the two ensigns;
- dimensions;
- precise fiber;
- pigment composition;
- whether assistants participated;
- a complete iconographic program.

Joan was in Tours between approximately 5 and 21 April 1429 while being equipped with armor and military insignia. Pasquerel later testified that he was in Tours when the standard was painted. The most defensible manufacturing window is therefore **April 1429**, before her departure toward Blois and Orléans.

A later La Rochelle narrative incorrectly or loosely placed its manufacture at Poitiers. The royal account and Pasquerel favor Tours.

### Intended function

The commission equipped Joan as a recognized military leader or charismatic war captain. A large standard marked the presence of a commander, served as a rallying point, and carried political-religious identification. Its white field and divine imagery presented Joan’s campaign as undertaken under the “King of Heaven”; the fleurs-de-lis connected that mission to the Valois king.

### Traditional or visionary origin

At her trial Joan stated that the voices of **Saint Catherine and Saint Margaret** told her:

> “Take the standard on behalf of the King of Heaven.”

She said that the entire program—God, angels, and colors—was made at divine command. This is a first-person claim about revelation, not evidence that the physical textile had a supernatural material origin. The seam between the two lineages is clear:

- **Documented physical origin:** a paid commission executed by a named painter in Tours.
- **Joan’s religious account:** the decision and imagery were commanded through her voices.

Later descriptions sometimes make the standard itself heaven-sent or miraculously efficacious. No contemporary record says that the cloth descended from heaven.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### April 1429: workshop and delivery

**Documented.** Hauves Poulnoir made and delivered the standard and smaller ensign, receiving payment through Treasurer Hémon Raguier. Initial title was effectively attached to Joan’s royal equipment, although medieval administrative ownership does not map neatly onto modern personal-property categories.

### Blois

Before the relief expedition entered Orléans, Joan and the army assembled at Blois. The *Chronique de la Pucelle* says that the standard and her armor were blessed in the church of **Saint-Sauveur at Blois**. This account was compiled decades later but may preserve contemporary information.

The standard accompanied a procession in which clergy sang hymns and the force moved under a consciously penitential and religious program.

### Entry into Orléans, 29 April 1429

The large standard entered Orléans with Joan. The small ensign was reportedly accidentally burned during the tumult surrounding her entrance; this claim is preserved in Orléans institutional scholarship, but its precise primary basis is less secure than the large standard’s payment record. Whether the small one was replaced is unknown.

### Fighting around Orléans, 4–8 May 1429

The large standard functioned as a battlefield locator and rallying device. It was present in the attacks on the English positions, including the fighting at the Tourelles.

Jean d’Aulon’s 1456 testimony supplies the most object-specific episode. During the assault on 7 May, the person carrying Joan’s standard wished to follow troops crossing the ditch and handed it to a man attached to the lord of Villars, known as **Le Basque**. D’Aulon directed or encouraged Le Basque to move the standard forward. Joan seized or pulled at the end of the standard, apparently trying to recover it; its motion was interpreted by troops as a signal to renew the assault. The standard therefore acted materially—not merely symbolically—in the battle’s final phase.

Claims that the cloth itself miraculously caused the victory are interpretations. The testimony supports a rallying effect.

### Loire campaign and march to Reims

The standard accompanied Joan through the Loire operations and the royal expedition toward Reims. It is associated in contemporary and near-contemporary narratives with:

- Jargeau;
- Meung-sur-Loire;
- Beaugency;
- Patay;
- the approach to Troyes;
- the march through Châlons;
- Reims.

Not every source independently specifies the same standard at every location; some references may use *banner* generically.

### Coronation at Reims, 17 July 1429

The standard was carried into **Reims Cathedral** for the coronation of Charles VII. At Rouen Joan was asked why her standard received more prominence than those of other captains. She replied:

> “It had been in the hardship; it was right that it should be in the honor.”

She denied making it flutter or revolve around the king’s head. The object’s presence is well attested, but many paintings showing Joan standing immediately beside the altar are later compositions rather than documentary depictions.

### Paris and Saint-Denis, September 1429

The standard accompanied Joan during the unsuccessful attack on Paris, where she was wounded. It should not be confused with the armor and captured sword she later offered at Saint-Denis. Her trial testimony specifically identifies those arms as an offering; it does not clearly say she deposited the standard there.

### Operations of late 1429 and early 1430

The standard evidently remained in Joan’s service through operations including Saint-Pierre-le-Moûtier and La Charité-sur-Loire. Named custody at every stage is not documented. Joan sometimes carried it personally; at other times an unnamed standard-bearer or members of her military household carried it.

### Compiègne, 23 May 1430: disappearance

Joan was captured during a sortie outside Compiègne by Burgundian forces. The standard disappears from reliable documentation at approximately this point.

No surviving Burgundian inventory, trophy notice, English judicial exhibit, ransom record, church donation, or treasury entry securely identifies the standard afterward. Pierre Cauchon’s court questioned Joan from memory; the physical object was not produced in evidence.

Possible fates include:

1. **Captured with other equipment and destroyed, divided, or discarded.**
2. **Recovered by a French follower during the retreat.**
3. **Left elsewhere before the sortie.**
4. **Taken as unrecorded spoil and subsequently lost.**

The frequent statement that it “remained in Burgundian hands” is plausible but not demonstrated by a custody record. Contamine notes the striking absence of any Burgundian claim to so conspicuous a trophy. The precise last bearer and disposition are unresolved.

### After 1430

The documented physical chain ends. There is no reliable evidence that:

- it was sent to Rouen;
- it was burned with Joan in 1431;
- it was preserved secretly by her family;
- it entered a royal, ecclesiastical, or municipal treasury;
- any existing textile is a fragment of it.

Its later “custody history” is therefore the history of descriptions, images, reconstructions, and ceremonial substitutes—not continued custody of the original fabric.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### The original object

The contemporary payment record, eyewitness-associated testimony, and repeated 1429 references make the original standard’s existence certain beyond reasonable historical dispute.

Its present survival is unsupported. Since no candidate textile possesses a chain beginning before or around 1430, scientific testing cannot presently answer whether any surviving object is the original.

### The supposed Chantilly standard

Nineteenth-century writers sometimes connected a damaged old ensign at Chantilly with Joan, reading fragmentary lettering as part of *In hoc signo vinces*. This identification never acquired a satisfactory provenance connecting it to Joan and is absent from current authoritative treatment as her authentic standard.

It should be classified as a **discarded antiquarian attribution**, not a rival with a documented claim.

### The Azeglio tapestry

The so-called Azeglio tapestry is an important historical image, not Joan’s banner. Discovered or purchased in Switzerland by the Marquis d’Azeglio in the nineteenth century, it depicts an armored Joan-like figure carrying an ensign. It strongly influenced later reconstructions.

Current French cataloguing dates the tapestry to approximately **1490**, decades after Joan’s death. The related work associated with the Musée Condé has museum notice **Joconde 00000106182**; the Orléans example is catalogued as **A.6921** and identified as a late-fifteenth-century German tapestry. Its imagery cannot function as an eyewitness record.

Nineteenth-century enthusiasm sometimes treated it as unusually exact. Modern scholarship treats it as later reception evidence.

### Allegedly medieval miniatures

Several highly detailed banner images appeared on the art market after publication of the trial sources by Jules Quicherat in 1841–1849. Their convenient agreement with newly accessible texts creates serious authenticity problems. The French Archives nationales now describes one purported Joan miniature as probably made in the **late nineteenth century in imitation of the fifteenth century**.

Such images must not be reverse-engineered into evidence for the lost textile.

### Modern reconstructions

No reconstruction is “the authentic banner.” Important examples include:

- **1855, Orléans:** white silk procession banner made for festivities accompanying the inauguration of Denis Foyatier’s statue; one-sided and based primarily on Joan and Pasquerel.
- **1876:** another reconstruction on the same general model, deposited at Les Invalides.
- **1894, Notre-Dame de Paris:** attempted to combine witness descriptions; placed the smaller pennon’s imagery on the reverse.
- **1909, Orléans:** influenced by the Azeglio tapestry and the Notre-Dame reconstruction.
- **1909, Henri de Barenton:** combined imagery belonging to both standard and pennon on one face; not accepted by later critical scholarship.
- **1920 and 1929:** banners produced for canonization and quincentenary observances.
- **1931, Reims Cathedral:** large English-made reconstruction, influenced especially by the La Rochelle account.
- **1936, Orléans:** ceremonial standard whose design continues to structure the city’s Johannine observances.

Their authenticity is commemorative and institutional, not material continuity with Poulnoir’s work.

### Scientific examination

There has been no scientific examination of the original because no securely attributable material survives. Claims that “science proved” a particular reconstruction correct are therefore false. At most, art-historical comparison can identify plausible cloth forms, lettering, pigments, or iconographic conventions.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Blessing

The *Chronique de la Pucelle* says Joan had the standard and armor blessed at Saint-Sauveur in Blois. This is an attested late-medieval report of ritual treatment, not evidence that the cloth was formally consecrated as a relic.

### Battlefield function

Its practical functions were to:

- show Joan’s position;
- rally troops;
- transmit movement in confused combat;
- embody command;
- distinguish her company.

Joan said she preferred carrying it during attacks so that she would avoid killing anyone. Her statement, “I never killed anyone,” belongs to sworn trial testimony. The standard thus allowed her to occupy a forward military role without making the sword her primary sign.

### Claimed protective or victorious power

Rouen’s interrogators asked whether Joan expected victory through the standard and whether another bearer would enjoy the same good fortune. She answered that victory belonged to God, not to the cloth or herself.

The prosecution alleged superstition: soldiers supposedly made pennons modeled on her “fortunate” standard in expectation of victory. Joan did not confirm that the object possessed autonomous magical power. The banner-related accusations were not among the final twelve articles on which the prosecution concentrated.

### Coronation legitimacy

At Reims it linked three claims:

1. divine authority, through the King of Heaven;
2. dynastic legitimacy, through the fleurs-de-lis;
3. achieved military deliverance, through its campaign history.

Its place in the cathedral made it an actor in the public presentation of Charles VII’s legitimacy, although it was not itself part of the traditional French coronation regalia.

### Orléans ceremonial substitute

In present Orléans observances, a reconstructed standard symbolizes Joan’s civic and religious presence. The mayor ceremonially hands it to the bishop. For the 2026 festivities, the Diocese announced its transfer by Mayor Serge Grouard to Bishop Jacques Blaquart.

This ritual concerns a twentieth-century ceremonial representative, not the surviving 1429 textile. The French inventory of intangible cultural heritage records the standard as a central component of the Johannine festivals.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### “The banner won battles”

**Early attested belief.** Soldiers and observers associated it with Joan’s successes, and Rouen’s prosecution said warriors copied it as “fortunate.” The strongest factual core is psychological and tactical: troops rallied around it. No source permits demonstration of supernatural agency.

### “It was dictated by heaven”

**Joan’s own religious claim.** She said Saints Catherine and Margaret ordered her to take it on behalf of the King of Heaven and dictated the general imagery. The material object was nevertheless manufactured by Poulnoir under a paid royal commission.

### “It was an exact vision of Joan’s angels”

**Rejected or qualified by Joan.** Asked whether the painted angels resembled those she saw, she said they were painted as angels were represented in churches. She refused further identification. Modern portraits treating the picture as an exact visual transcript of her visions exceed the testimony.

### “It carried Saint Michael with a sword and Gabriel with a lily”

**Plausible scholarly reconstruction, not explicit fact.** This arrangement fits Last Judgment imagery and later efforts to reconcile the sources. Joan expressly declined to identify the two figures as Michael and Gabriel.

### “Its reverse bore the Virgin, a dove, or the arms of France”

**Likely conflation.** Annunciation imagery is strongly connected to the smaller pennon. A La Rochelle text describes a blue shield, white dove, and scroll reading “De par le Roy du Ciel,” but is chronologically and descriptively problematic. Modern two-sided standards often attach this program to the large standard without adequate material justification.

### “The original is preserved at Orléans, Reims, Paris, or Chantilly”

**Unsupported.** These institutions possess or have possessed reconstructions, associated artworks, or allegedly related ensigns. None has a continuous provenance from 1429.

### “It was burned with Joan”

**Late supposition.** No execution record places it at Rouen, and the court questioned Joan without producing it. Its disappearance around Compiègne is the final evidentiary horizon.

### “Joan waved it over Charles VII’s head”

**Judicial allegation denied by Joan.** At Rouen she denied making it turn or flutter around the king during the coronation. Later artistic scenes sometimes visualize the allegation as fact.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Original large standard

**Status:** lost, probably destroyed at an unknown date after May 1430.

**Last approximately documented location:** in Joan’s military sphere around Compiègne before or at her capture on 23 May 1430.

**Current location:** unknown.

**Condition:** unknowable; no authenticated fragment is available.

**Ownership or legal control:** none presently identifiable.

**Access:** impossible.

There is no current restitution case because no extant object has been legally or scientifically identified as the original.

### Principal stand-ins

- **Orléans ceremonial standard:** a twentieth-century reconstruction used in the annual Johannine festivals; civic and diocesan ceremonial control alternates symbolically during the observances.
- **Reims Cathedral reconstruction:** large standard offered in 1931.
- **Azeglio tapestry, Orléans:** late-fifteenth-century representation, Hôtel Cabu–Musée d’histoire et d’archéologie, accession A.6921.
- **Related Azeglio-associated tapestry/reproduction, Chantilly:** Musée Condé, Joconde notice 00000106182.
- **Fauquembergue drawing:** Archives nationales, Parlement de Paris register X/1a/1481, fol. 12r; earliest contemporary drawn association of Joan with a standard, but imaginary rather than eyewitness.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Nineteenth-century reconstruction

Publication of Joan’s trial records by Jules Quicherat in 1841–1849 transformed the banner from a loosely remembered attribute into an object of antiquarian reconstruction. The timing matters: highly detailed “medieval” images appearing after those publications may derive from printed sources.

The 1855 Orléans banner established a white procession-banner type. Later reconstructions increasingly combined:

- Joan’s trial description;
- Pasquerel’s Last Judgment;
- the pennon’s Annunciation;
- the Azeglio tapestry;
- modern heraldic expectations.

### Art and public imagery

The standard became Joan’s most durable visual attribute in:

- nineteenth-century history painting;
- church statuary and stained glass;
- opera and theater;
- cinema;
- schoolbooks;
- patriotic posters;
- comics and games.

These works often simplify it into a white flag bearing fleurs-de-lis, a cross, “Jhesus Maria,” or an image of Christ. Joan is also frequently shown with the French tricolor, an anachronism.

### Religious mobilization

Beatification in 1909 and canonization in 1920 encouraged church banners and devotional reproductions. The original military object was reframed as a quasi-relic or emblem of sainthood, although no original textile was available for veneration.

### Civic and national symbolism

Orléans made the reconstructed banner a central ceremonial object. Its transfer between mayor and bishop expresses the festivals’ combined civic, military, and religious character.

The hymn **“À l’Étendard”**, used in Orléans since the end of the nineteenth century, helped popularize the phrase “standard of deliverance.” The phrase is commemorative, not an authenticated medieval title.

Joan and her banner have also been mobilized by monarchist, republican, Catholic, nationalist, and far-right movements. These appropriations concern the banner’s symbolic image; they do not contribute to its physical provenance.

### Commercial use

Modern reproductions appear as:

- flags and wall hangings;
- church banners;
- festival souvenirs;
- stickers;
- miniatures;
- heraldic products;
- game and film props.

Most reproduce the 1909/1936 Orléans family of designs rather than an independently reconstructed medieval object.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Primary evidence

The principal evidence consists of:

1. Hémon Raguier’s royal war account.
2. Joan’s 1431 condemnation-trial interrogations.
3. Contemporary reports by Fauquembergue, the *Bourgeois de Paris*, Morosini’s correspondents, the Albi clerk, and related chroniclers.
4. Testimony by Pasquerel, Dunois, and d’Aulon in the 1455–1456 nullification proceedings.
5. Later fifteenth-century chronicles and the *Mystère du siège d’Orléans*.
6. Early images, none securely by an eyewitness.

### Nineteenth-century scholarship

Eugène de Certain’s 1859 study exemplifies the first critical phase. It was prompted partly by the Azeglio tapestry and attempted to compare texts with newly recovered images. That scholarship was valuable but remained vulnerable to romantic nationalism and overconfidence in late visual sources.

### Twentieth-century reconstruction debates

Colonel de Liocourt’s 1974 study assembled extensive evidence on fifteenth-century standards and became influential even though later scholars rejected many conclusions.

Debate has centered on:

- whether the deity was God the Father or Christ;
- whether the composition represented the Last Judgment;
- whether both angels carried lilies;
- whether one carried a sword;
- whether the field itself was semé-de-lis;
- whether blue tails bore the royal arms;
- whether front and reverse differed;
- whether Annunciation, dove, and shield imagery belonged to the pennon;
- the number and type of ensigns Joan possessed.

### Philippe Contamine’s critical intervention

Contamine’s study, published in *Francia* and summarized in the *Bulletin de la Société nationale des Antiquaires de France*, is the most important modern corrective. Its principal conclusions are:

- two ensigns must be distinguished;
- testimony is incomplete and contradictory;
- several witnesses were not eyewitnesses;
- painted-cloth construction limits the possibility of unrelated obverse and reverse designs;
- the large standard probably featured an apocalyptic or judgment image close to the divine figure described by Pasquerel;
- Annunciation and dove imagery most likely belong to the pennon;
- no exact reconstruction is possible;
- the standard disappeared around Compiègne.

The Orléans exhibition adopted this cautious approach while creating an explicitly hypothetical reconstruction.

### Outstanding questions

Unresolved issues include:

- the standard’s exact dimensions and outline;
- the fiber meant by *boucassin* in this commission;
- pigment and binder;
- precise arrangement of words and figures;
- whether the field or only tails carried fleurs-de-lis;
- the identity and attributes of each angel;
- whether a replacement pennon was made after the reported fire;
- who bore the standard at Compiègne;
- whether it was captured, recovered, divided, or destroyed;
- whether any unidentified fragment survives without provenance.

## 11. Chronology

| Date | Object history | Evidentiary status |

## 12. Sources

### Primary and near-primary sources

- The thirteenth account of Hémon Raguier, treasurer of wars, survives through archival transcription and scholarly quotation. Its payment to Hauves Poulnoir is the controlling manufacture record.
- The condemnation proceedings survive in French minutes and an official Latin redaction. Joan’s descriptions on 27 February, 10 March, and 17 March 1431 are the closest evidence to the object’s appearance and asserted meaning.
- The nullification proceedings preserve testimony from Pasquerel, Dunois, and d’Aulon.
- Fauquembergue’s sketch survives in the Parlement de Paris register, Archives nationales X/1a/1481, fol. 12r.
- The Morosini chronicle preserves the detailed 9 July 1429 report.
- The *Journal du siège d’Orléans*, *Chronique de la Pucelle*, *Chronique de Tournai*, and *Mystère du siège d’Orléans* preserve increasingly retrospective variants.

### Critical and institutional sources

- Philippe Contamine, “Remarques critiques sur les étendards de Jeanne d’Arc,” *Francia* 34/1 (2007), pp. 187–200; summarized in the *Bulletin de la Société nationale des Antiquaires de France*.
- Eugène de Certain, “Note sur l’étendard de Jeanne d’Arc,” *Bibliothèque de l’École des chartes* 20 (1859), pp. 355–368.
- Maison de Jeanne d’Arc/Centre Jeanne d’Arc, Orléans, historical exhibition on the standard and its reconstructions.
- Archives nationales exhibition, “1429 – Jeanne d’Arc, le premier portrait.”
- French Ministry of Culture, national museum and intangible-heritage records.
- Diocese of Orléans, current festival program.
