# Paracelsus's sword "Azoth": Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `paracelsuss_sword_azoth_object_dossier.md`  
**Research current to:** 6 September 2026

## Executive finding

The evidence supports the existence of a sword habitually carried by Paracelsus (Theophrastus von Hohenheim, 1493/94–1541). It does **not** establish that this sword was itself named *Azoth*, that *AZOTH* was engraved on its historical pommel, that its hilt contained a philosopher’s stone, universal medicine, demon, or familiar spirit, or that the sword survived Paracelsus’s death.

The evidence develops in distinct stages:

1. **Documented during Paracelsus’s lifetime:** a sword with a conspicuously large spherical pommel appears in his 1540 portrait. Johannes Oporinus, who had lived with him, independently remembered Paracelsus carrying a sword and behaving violently with it while drunk.
2. **Posthumous visual reinterpretation:** an anonymous print traditionally attributed to Balthasar Jenichen—of uncertain date, probably in the 1560s—and securely dated Cologne woodcuts of 1567 add *AZOTH* to the pommel.
3. **Late-sixteenth-century legend:** Andreas Libavius and other writers identify *azoth* as Paracelsus’s universal medicine and associate it with his sword hilt.
4. **Seventeenth-century synthesis:** Melchior Adam reports that some people believed the hilt contained the potent medicine or philosopher’s stone Paracelsus called *Azoth*. Other hostile traditions converted the hidden substance into a familiar spirit or devil.
5. **Modern reification:** occult biographies and popular culture increasingly turned “Azoth” from the alleged contents or inscription of a pommel into the proper name of a supernatural sword.

Accordingly, “Paracelsus’s sword Azoth” is best classified as a **lost or unidentified sixteenth-century sword surrounded by a posthumously constructed legendary identity**. No authenticated physical claimant, museum accession, scientific examination, or continuous chain of custody is known.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Object class and evidentiary identity

**Documented object:** a personal sword carried by Paracelsus and represented in a lifetime portrait.

**Legendary object:** “Azoth,” a magical or alchemical sword whose pommel supposedly contained a universal medicine, philosopher’s stone, mercury preparation, laudanum pills, or familiar spirit.

These identities must not be collapsed. The lifetime evidence establishes the sword but not the name. The first surviving representations connecting *AZOTH* with its pommel are posthumous.

### 1.2 Names

- **Paracelsus’s sword**: modern descriptive designation.
- **German:** *Schwert des Paracelsus*, *Paracelsusschwert*; early narrative sources simply use *Schwert* or its Latin equivalents.
- **Latin:** *gladius* or *ensis*; *capulus gladii* means the hilt or handle.
- **Azoth / AZOTH:** originally a substance-name, subsequently an inscription, and only much later the sword’s proper name.
- **“Trusty Azoth”:** a literary personification popularized in nineteenth-century English writing, not a sixteenth-century designation.
- **Sword of Paracelsus / Azoth Sword:** modern fantasy terminology.

### 1.3 Etymology of *azoth*

The historically defensible etymology derives medieval Latin *azoc/azot(h)* from Arabic **الزئبق**, *al-zāʾūq/al-zāwūq*, “mercury” or “quicksilver,” possibly through Syriac and ultimately Iranian linguistic ancestry. Medieval Latin *azoc* is attested before Paracelsus; the spelling with final *-t* may reflect manuscript confusion between *c* and *t*, while *azoth* is an embellished early-modern form. The term therefore was neither coined by Paracelsus nor originally the name of a weapon. [American Heritage Dictionary](https://www.ahdictionary.com/word/search.html?q=azoth); [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/azoth)

Later alchemical and occult writers proposed a symbolic construction from the first and last letters of Latin, Greek, and Hebrew alphabets—A, Z, Ω, and ת (*tav*)—making *Azoth* signify beginning and end. That is a learned esoteric interpretation, not the accepted historical etymology.

### 1.4 Reconstructable physical appearance

The best evidence is the portrait convention derived from the engraving generally attributed to Augustin Hirschvogel and dated 1540. Paracelsus grips a large, approximately spherical pommel. Only the upper part of the weapon is visible. The image does not securely reveal:

- blade length or profile;
- steel composition;
- cross-section or edge geometry;
- guard construction;
- grip covering;
- maker or workshop;
- weight;
- scabbard;
- maker’s marks;
- decoration elsewhere on the sword.

The scale and portrait composition encouraged later writers to call it unusually large or a two-handed or executioner’s sword. The portrait alone cannot establish such a typology.

The contemporary pommel is shown as a plain round object. It bears **neither the word *AZOTH* nor a chemical sign**. Julius Friedrich Aberle’s systematic nineteenth-century study emphasized that neither the 1540 engraving, its 1552 copy, nor a related Nuremberg oil portrait displays the name. [Aberle, *Grabdenkmal, Schädel und Abbildungen*](https://www.zobodat.at/pdf/MGSL_28_0269-0355.pdf)

### 1.5 The posthumous *AZOTH* pommel

The securely dated 1567 frontispiece to *Philosophiae magnae* shows Paracelsus with both hands around a large, vessel-shaped pommel inscribed:

> **AZOTH**

The image reverses and elaborates the lifetime portrait. It adds Paracelsus’s attributed arms, emblematic figures derived from his prognostications, and moral-religious inscriptions. The frame includes:

- *ALTERIVS NON SIT, QVI SVVS ESSE POTEST* — “Let no one belong to another who can belong to himself.”
- *OMNE DONVM PERFECTVM À DEO, IMPERF. À DIABO.* — “Every perfect gift is from God; the imperfect from the Devil.”
- *LAVS DEO, PAX VIVIS, REQVIES ÆTERNA SEPVLTIS* — “Praise to God, peace to the living, eternal rest to the buried.”

The Science History Institute holds and digitizes an impression of this woodcut. It describes the print, not an extant sword, and assigns no physical properties to the represented weapon beyond what the picture supplies. [Science History Institute](https://digital.sciencehistory.org/works/f1881m50j)

### 1.6 Possible Jenichen predecessor

Aberle described an anonymous etched sheet attributed stylistically to Balthasar Jenichen. It copied the 1540 portrait, added *Azoth* to the pommel, and may have preceded the 1567 woodcut. Aberle thought it could be earlier than 1565; later cataloguing has generally been more cautious. A related Jenichen-attributed Paracelsus portrait in Munich is dated broadly to 1576–1600. [Deutsche Digitale Bibliothek](https://www.deutsche-digitale-bibliothek.de/item/ZT62PH5T5EU5MZ3B7KVKKUFF5ORCLEYW?lang=en)

Thus:

- **Earliest securely dated *AZOTH* image:** Cologne, 1567.
- **Possible earlier image:** anonymous Jenichen-attributed print, probably 1560s, exact date unresolved.
- **Lifetime *AZOTH* inscription:** not demonstrated.

### 1.7 Condition and surviving components

No authenticated sword, hilt, blade, scabbard, fragment, or detached pommel is presently known. Nothing has been subjected to metallurgical, radiographic, stylistic, or residue analysis.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Manufacture

No contract, armourer’s account, purchase record, gift instrument, or workshop attribution survives. The following are unknown:

- date and place of manufacture;
- smith, cutler, or hilt-maker;
- original owner;
- steel or other material provenance;
- cost;
- whether the pommel was hollow;
- whether the sword was functional, ceremonial, or primarily a personal emblem.

### 2.2 Alleged executioner provenance

Johannes Oporinus’s recollection, preserved and reused by Paracelsus’s opponents and biographers, states that Paracelsus claimed to have received the sword from a torturer or executioner. The phrasing is hearsay within eyewitness testimony: Oporinus witnessed the sword but reports Paracelsus’s assertion about its origin.

The claim is plausible in the limited sense that Paracelsus, as a surgeon and medical practitioner, cultivated knowledge obtained outside university medicine. It is not a documented transfer. No executioner is named, and neither place nor date is supplied.

### 2.3 Earliest secure evidence for the physical sword

The 1540 portrait is the earliest secure visual attestation used here. It shows Paracelsus at approximately forty-seven, gripping the spherical pommel. A surviving British Museum impression associated with the portrait tradition confirms that Hirschvogel-derived images circulated in early Paracelsian publications, although not every version shows the sword. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/image/1613672328)

Oporinus’s account provides an independent textual attestation. He recalled that Paracelsus slept in his clothes with the sword beside him and sometimes rose at night, drew it, and struck or threw it against the wall. Karl Sudhoff reproduced the account in his critical biographical work. [Sudhoff, *Paracelsus, ein deutsches Lebensbild*](https://www.theatrum-paracelsicum.com/Texte/Sudhoff/Paracelsus%2C_ein_deutsches_Lebensbild_%281936%29)

### 2.4 Earliest secure evidence for the name *AZOTH* on the weapon

The first securely dated connection is the 1567 Cologne woodcut, twenty-six years after Paracelsus’s death. The volume was printed by Gerhart Vierendunck for the heirs of Arnold Birckmann. Its bibliographic record describes the pommel inscription explicitly. [Bibliographia Paracelsica BP085](https://www.theatrum-paracelsicum.com/Bibliographia_Paracelsica/BP085)

The engraving does not prove that the historical sword bore the word. It proves that by 1567 an editor, publisher, or image-maker considered *Azoth* an effective emblem for the posthumous construction of Paracelsus.

### 2.5 The estate inventory and silver sphere

Michael Toxites printed Paracelsus’s testament and an associated inventory in 1574; the original testament is not preserved. The inventory includes:

> “Ein geschrauffte sylberine Kugel, an einem sylberin khettl, wigt acht lot und ein quintt.”

That is, a screw-opening silver sphere on a silver chain, weighing eight lots and one quint.

Aberle proposed that this could have helped generate the story about medicine in a screw-opening pommel. The inventory wording, however, describes an object on a chain, not a sword fitting. It does not call the sphere *Azoth* or say that it contained medicine. Treating it as the historical pommel is therefore conjectural.

The sword itself has not been identified in the surviving inventory evidence. Salzburg’s municipal history confirms that Toxites’s 1574 printing, rather than an original testament, is the transmitted source. [City of Salzburg](https://www.stadt-salzburg.at/archiv/die-chronik-der-stadt-salzburg/paracelsus)

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Before 1540 — alleged acquisition

**Traditional claim:** Paracelsus acquired the sword as a gift from a torturer or executioner.

**Evidence:** Paracelsus’s reported statement, transmitted by Oporinus.

**Status:** unverified. Donor, place, date, and transfer circumstances are absent.

### 1540 — in Paracelsus’s possession

The lifetime portrait shows him physically associated with a sword. Oporinus’s testimony indicates that this was not merely a portrait prop: Paracelsus habitually carried a sword while Oporinus lived with him.

**Status:** documented association, though the precise date at which Paracelsus obtained it remains unknown.

### Unspecified period before 1541 — domestic use

Oporinus says Paracelsus:

- kept the sword beside him when sleeping;
- drew it during nocturnal, drunken episodes;
- struck or threw it against the floor or wall;
- frightened Oporinus, who feared being injured.

This is the sole concrete surviving account of something done **with** the physical weapon. It comes from a hostile witness and circulated through anti-Paracelsian controversy, but its mundane description of a constantly carried sword agrees with the portrait evidence.

### September 1541 — last possible custody by Paracelsus

Paracelsus dictated his will in Salzburg on 21 September and died on 24 September 1541. No reliable deathbed account records the sword at his side, its bequest, or its transfer.

### After 24 September 1541 — chain breaks

The sword’s physical chain of custody ends completely. No executor, heir, purchaser, creditor, friend, armoury, church, or civic authority is documented as receiving it. No sale price or probate transfer is known.

Possible but unproven fates include:

- sale with ordinary effects for the benefit of Salzburg’s poor;
- retention by an executor or associate;
- dismantling or reuse of the blade and fittings;
- loss;
- destruction;
- prior disposal by Paracelsus;
- survival under an unidentified description in a private or institutional collection.

None is sufficiently evidenced to be preferred.

### 1560s onward — custody becomes representational

After the physical trail vanishes, “Azoth” persists through pictures and texts:

1. an anonymous Jenichen-attributed print adds *AZOTH*;
2. the 1567 *Philosophiae magnae* frontispiece disseminates the motif;
3. later prints copy or reinterpret it;
4. biographers convert the depicted label into a story about a substance inside the hilt;
5. modern fiction converts the hilt story into a named supernatural weapon.

This is a chain of **iconographic and literary transmission**, not physical custody.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Did the sword exist?

Probably yes. The combination of a lifetime portrait and Oporinus’s independent account makes possession of a real sword considerably better supported than most elements of the legend.

### 4.2 Was the real sword called *Azoth*?

Not established. No lifetime text has been found calling the sword *Azoth*. Early accounts treat *azoth* as a substance or medicine, not necessarily as the weapon’s name.

### 4.3 Was *AZOTH* engraved on the real pommel?

No surviving evidence establishes this. The 1540 image shows no inscription. The word first appears in posthumous iconography. The simplest evidence-based description is that *AZOTH* was an emblematic addition to the portrait tradition.

### 4.4 Did the hilt contain a medicine?

Possible in the abstract—hollow or compartmented fittings existed—but unproved for this sword. The evidence consists of:

- the posthumous inscription;
- late-sixteenth- and seventeenth-century reports;
- Aberle’s proposed connection with the screw-opening silver sphere on a chain.

No hilt or residue survives.

### 4.5 Did it contain laudanum?

This is a later specification of the medicine legend. No contemporary inventory calls the supposed contents laudanum, and no chemical sample exists. The proposition is unverified.

### 4.6 Did it contain the philosopher’s stone?

Melchior Adam wrote in 1620 that **some people thought** the substance kept in the sword handle, called *Azoth*, was a most powerful medicine or the philosopher’s stone. His wording records an already circulating belief rather than certifying an object he had examined. [Theatrum Paracelsicum: Melchior Adam](https://www.theatrum-paracelsicum.com/Biographies/Melchior_Adam)

### 4.7 Did it contain a demon or familiar spirit?

This belongs to hostile anti-Paracelsian legend. Thomas Erastus and later polemical authors depicted Paracelsian medicine as magical or demonic. The sword’s concealed power was consequently recast as a *spiritus familiaris*. This reflects confessional and medical polemic, not an inspected physical feature. Erastus’s four-part *Disputationum de medicina nova Philippi Paracelsi* is accessible in a contemporary digitization. [Utrecht University Library](https://dbc.library.uu.nl/handle/20.500.14918/436967)

### 4.8 Is the screw-opening silver sphere the pommel?

Aberle’s identification is a plausible explanation for the legend’s formation but not a demonstrated identification. The attached silver chain weighs against reading the inventory entry as an ordinary mounted pommel. It could have been a container, pendant, pomander, or other personal object.

### 4.9 Are there rival claimant swords?

No institutionally catalogued claimant was located. Internet images, replicas, role-playing props, or weapons labelled “Azoth” lack a documented provenance to Paracelsus. None should be treated as a candidate original without an independently verifiable pre-modern chain.

### 4.10 Scientific examination

No examination of the sword has occurred because no candidate artifact has been identified. Investigations of Paracelsus’s remains—including reported elevated mercury—bear on his medical practices but do not authenticate the sword or its supposed contents. A recent scholarly discussion links the mercury finding to the medicine tradition, but that is contextual evidence only. [Wels, *Daphnis* 53 (2025)](https://brill.com/view/journals/daph/53/2-3/article-p430_7.pdf)

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Documented function

The sword’s documented function was personal carriage. There is no evidence that Paracelsus employed it in:

- combat;
- an execution;
- a duel;
- healing ritual;
- initiation;
- oath-taking;
- alchemical manufacture;
- court ceremony;
- liturgy.

Oporinus’s account describes threatening or uncontrolled domestic handling, not ritual practice.

### 6.2 Portrait function

In the lifetime portrait, the large pommel supplies a strong visual attribute. It presents Paracelsus as an independent, mobile practitioner rather than a sedentary university physician. Its exact intended symbolism is unknown.

In 1567 the publishers transformed it into an alchemical emblem. The *AZOTH* pommel signified concentrated Paracelsian knowledge: medicine, mercury, transformation, beginning and end, or a concealed *arcanum*. Heide Klinkhammer interprets its vessel-like form as consciously alchemical and as part of a program presenting Paracelsus as a German or second Hermes. [Klinkhammer, “Hermes Trismegistos … Paracelsus”](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9783110781908-006/pdf)

### 6.3 Supposed healing function

The legend assigns the hilt one or more of these functions:

- portable store for a universal medicine;
- container for mercury-based *azoth*;
- holder of laudanum pills;
- repository of the philosopher’s stone;
- talismanic source of medical efficacy.

Diderot and d’Alembert’s *Encyclopédie* stated in 1751 that Paracelsus’s *azoth* was said to be a preparation of gold, silver, and mercury and that he reportedly carried some in his sword pommel. The article’s “it is said” formulation shows that it was repeating tradition, not reporting an artifact. [*Encyclopédie*, “Azoth”](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/L%E2%80%99Encyclop%C3%A9die/1re_%C3%A9dition/AZOTH)

### 6.4 Taboos and access

No historical access rule is recorded. Modern warnings that the sword is dangerous, demonic, or usable only by an adept belong to fictional and occult reception.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The executioner’s sword

**Story:** Paracelsus received a great sword from an executioner.

**Earliest basis:** Oporinus’s report of what Paracelsus said.

**Possible function:** It associated Paracelsus with illicit or socially marginal practical knowledge—executioners sometimes possessed anatomical, surgical, and medicinal expertise—but opponents could also use the association to make him appear disreputable.

**Verdict:** early attested allegation, not documented provenance.

### 7.2 The universal medicine in the pommel

**Story:** Paracelsus carried his sovereign medicine in the sword’s hollow pommel.

**Development:** posthumous *AZOTH* imagery appears by 1567; Libavius links Paracelsian *azoth* to the universal cure by 1595; Adam records the hilt-container belief in 1620.

**Variants:** gold-silver-mercury amalgam, philosophical mercury, laudanum, healing pills, elixir of life.

**Verdict:** late-sixteenth-century legend built around a real sword, an alchemical substance-name, and possibly a separate screw-opening silver container.

### 7.3 The philosopher’s stone

**Story:** the pommel contained the philosopher’s stone, giving Paracelsus wealth, cures, or transmutational power.

**Earliest clear formulation used here:** Adam’s 1620 report that “some” identified the hilt’s contents with the stone.

**Verdict:** early-seventeenth-century elaboration.

### 7.4 The familiar spirit or devil

**Story:** Paracelsus trapped, carried, or consulted a demon in the hilt.

**Context:** anti-Paracelsian authors accused him of magic and illicit spiritual assistance. A mysterious enclosed *arcanum* was easily transformed into a familiar spirit.

**Verdict:** hostile polemical legend. No physical or eyewitness evidence supports it.

### 7.5 The magically empowered sword

**Story:** the weapon itself performed magic, commanded elements, healed, transmuted, or released destructive power.

**Development:** this is principally a modern synthesis. Earlier sources place power in a substance or spirit **inside** the pommel. Modern retellings move that power into the entire sword and make “Azoth” its proper name.

**Verdict:** modern occult and fantasy reification.

### 7.6 *Liber Azoth* as evidence for the sword

The work *Liber Azoth sive de ligno et linea vitae* was first printed in 1591 and is pseudo-Paracelsian. It cannot serve as Paracelsus’s eyewitness explanation of his sword. Its presence in later collected works encouraged readers to treat *Azoth* as a personal Paracelsian doctrine. The Herzog August Bibliothek’s alchemy thesaurus expressly identifies it as pseudo-Paracelsian. [Herzog August Bibliothek](https://alchemie.hab.de/thesaurus/)

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Physical sword

**Status:** lost, unidentified, or no longer extant.

**Location:** unknown.

**Owner or custodian:** unknown.

**Legal status:** no known title, restitution, or repatriation dispute.

**Access:** impossible because no authenticated artifact has been located.

**Scientific work:** none on the sword.

### Surviving representations

The most important securely documented surrogate is the 1567 *Philosophiae magnae* portrait. Digitized impressions or reproductions are held or presented by:

- the Science History Institute, Philadelphia;
- libraries holding copies of the 1567 book;
- public-domain image repositories;
- print collections preserving later derivatives.

These institutions hold the **image**, not Paracelsus’s weapon.

### Serious present hypothesis

The strongest reconstruction is:

- Paracelsus owned and carried a real sword;
- its later fate is unrecorded;
- the *AZOTH* inscription originated in posthumous portraiture;
- the medicine-in-pommel story was subsequently inferred, elaborated, or rationalized from that image and from traditions about Paracelsus’s portable remedies.

There is presently no evidentiary basis for a treasure hunt or for identifying an unattributed sixteenth-century sword solely from an *AZOTH* inscription.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Occult biography

Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century occult biographies frequently treated the legend more literally than critical historians did. The phrase “trusty Azoth” personified the weapon and encouraged readers to interpret *Azoth* as its proper name rather than its alleged contents.

Arthur Edward Waite reviewed the tradition while recognizing that the stories developed around the portrait and that the word’s meaning was uncertain. [Waite, *Lives of Alchemystical Philosophers*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/68687/68687-h/68687-h.htm)

### 9.2 “Rosicrucian portrait”

The 1567 image is routinely called the “Rosicrucian portrait.” That label is retrospective: the organized Rosicrucian manifesto controversy belongs to the early seventeenth century. The portrait certainly supplied imagery congenial to later Paracelsian and Rosicrucian interpretation, but calling it Rosicrucian must not imply documented membership by Paracelsus in a later fraternity.

### 9.3 Fate franchise

The Japanese *Fate* franchise made “Azoth Sword” a class of magical implement and gave Paracelsus a supernatural “Sword of Paracelsus.” In this fictional reconstruction:

- the blade consists of highly purified philosopher’s stones;
- it amplifies magic;
- it manipulates five elements;
- it can emit destructive pseudo-ether.

These properties are modern fiction, not historical tradition. The franchise also uses an “Azoth Sword” in the Tohsaka–Kotomine storyline, where it operates as a ceremonial magical dagger and plot object. A translated franchise glossary explicitly says it was inspired by the sword associated with Paracelsus. [TMdict](https://www.tmdict.com/legacy/en/a.azoth-sword.html)

### 9.4 Video games and fantasy

The names “Azoth,” “Sword of Paracelsus,” and “Paracelsus” recur in games and fantasy works, usually as alchemical weapons, catalysts, philosopher’s-stone blades, or sentient arms. These works draw on the modern composite legend rather than independently preserving early evidence.

### 9.5 Online pseudo-history

Contemporary websites often state as fact that:

- Paracelsus forged the sword;
- named it *Azoth*;
- made its blade from philosopher’s stone;
- trapped a demon in it;
- used it to cast elemental magic.

None of these propositions is established by sixteenth-century documentation. Their resemblance to modern game lore provides a useful warning that fictional specifications are being recycled as biography.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Primary evidence base

The evidence is unusually narrow:

1. the Hirschvogel-attributed lifetime portrait tradition;
2. Oporinus’s recollection;
3. Paracelsus’s printed testament and inventory as transmitted by Toxites;
4. the Jenichen-attributed sheet;
5. the 1567 Cologne *Philosophiae magnae* frontispiece and accompanying verses;
6. Libavius’s polemical discussion;
7. Adam’s 1620 biography;
8. later encyclopedic and occult retellings.

### 10.2 Julius Friedrich Aberle

Aberle’s 1880s–90s work remains fundamental because he compared portraits chronologically and consulted the printed estate inventory. His key findings were:

- authentic early portraits lack *AZOTH*;
- a Jenichen-attributed print may precede the 1567 woodcut;
- the 1567 portrait securely connects the word with the pommel;
- the screw-opening silver sphere might explain the container legend.

His final point is an inference, not a demonstrated identification.

### 10.3 Karl Sudhoff

Sudhoff systematized Paracelsian bibliography and reproduced early testimony, including Oporinus. His work helped separate authentic writings from the extensive pseudo-Paracelsian corpus. That distinction is essential because *Liber Azoth* is posthumous and pseudonymous.

### 10.4 Current scholarship

Recent scholarship treats the sword chiefly as part of Paracelsus’s posthumous iconography and the construction of “Paracelsus as Hermes.” It emphasizes that the *AZOTH* portrait is an editorial and emblematic product rather than a neutral documentary likeness.

### 10.5 Unresolved questions

- What was the exact date and maker of the Jenichen-attributed print?
- Did it precede the 1567 woodcut, and was it the latter’s direct model?
- Did a now-lost oral tradition about medicine in the hilt already exist before the image?
- Was the inventory’s silver sphere connected with medicinal storage?
- What happened to the physical sword after September 1541?
- Did Paracelsus ever use *azoth* for a particular preparation, rather than as a variable alchemical term?
- Was the real sword an ordinary sidearm, unusually large weapon, executioner’s sword, or something else?

None can presently be answered conclusively.

| 17 January 1567 | Preface to the Cologne *Philosophiae magnae* edition is dated. |
| 1567 | The securely dated woodcut frontispiece shows *AZOTH* on the pommel and embeds the sword in a program of Paracelsian-Hermetic symbolism. |
| 1572–74 | Erastus’s anti-Paracelsian polemics help develop the interpretation of Paracelsus’s powers as magical or demonic. |
| 1574 | Michael Toxites prints Paracelsus’s testament and inventory. |
| 1591 | Pseudo-Paracelsian *Liber Azoth sive de ligno et linea vitae* appears in print; later readers often mistake it for Paracelsus’s own testimony. |
| 1595 | Libavius explicitly connects Paracelsian *azoth* with universal medicine and the sword-hilt tradition. |
| 1620 | Melchior Adam records the belief that the hilt contained the potent medicine or philosopher’s stone called *Azoth*. |
| 1751 | The French *Encyclopédie* repeats that Paracelsus reportedly carried a gold-silver-mercury preparation in his sword pommel. |
| 1880s–91 | Aberle critically compares the portraits and inventory, identifying the posthumous development of the *AZOTH* motif. |
| Late nineteenth–early twentieth century | Occult biographies popularize “Azoth” as the sword’s personal name and emphasize supernatural interpretations. |
| Twentieth century | Sudhoff and later Paracelsus scholarship distinguish authentic, pseudo-Paracelsian, iconographic, and legendary evidence. |
| Twenty-first century | Fantasy franchises and online retellings depict Azoth as a philosopher’s-stone or elemental magic sword. No physical claimant emerges. |
| 6 September 2026 | No authenticated sword or component is publicly catalogued; its fate remains unknown. |

## 12. Sources and Evidentiary Assessment

### Principal primary and near-primary sources

- **Hirschvogel-attributed portrait, 1540:** strongest visual evidence for the real sword; no *AZOTH* inscription.
- **Johannes Oporinus’s recollection:** eyewitness evidence for possession and handling; hostile in tone and transmitted through later controversy.
- **Paracelsus testament and inventory, printed by Michael Toxites in 1574:** useful but posthumously printed; original testament lost.
- **Anonymous Jenichen-attributed print:** potentially the first *AZOTH* pommel, but its dating and attribution are unresolved.
- ***Philosophiae magnae*, Cologne, 1567:** earliest securely dated *AZOTH* image.
- **Thomas Erastus, 1570s:** primary evidence for hostile anti-Paracelsian interpretation, not for a demon’s actual existence.
- **Andreas Libavius, 1595:** early witness to the medicine-and-hilt tradition.
- **Melchior Adam, 1620:** reports what “some” believed; late secondary testimony rather than inspection.

### Major critical and institutional sources

- Julius Friedrich Aberle’s portrait and monument study;
- Karl Sudhoff’s Paracelsian bibliography and critical biography;
- *Bibliographia Paracelsica* and Theatrum Paracelsicum;
- Science History Institute’s record and high-resolution image;
- Herzog August Bibliothek’s alchemical terminology project;
- recent scholarship by Heide Klinkhammer and Volkhard Wels.

### Overall confidence assessment

| Proposition | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Paracelsus possessed and carried a sword | **Well supported** |
| The portrait weapon and Oporinus’s sword were the same object | **Plausible inference, not provable** |
| An executioner gave it to him | **Early reported claim, unverified** |
| The historical pommel was spherical and large | **Supported by portrait evidence** |
| The historical sword bore *AZOTH* | **Not established; absent from lifetime portrait** |
| The hilt was hollow | **Unverified** |
| It held medicine or pills | **Posthumous tradition** |
| It held mercury, gold, and silver | **Eighteenth-century specification of the tradition** |
| It held the philosopher’s stone | **Early-seventeenth-century legend** |
| It held a familiar spirit or devil | **Hostile polemical legend** |
| The entire weapon was named “Azoth” | **Predominantly modern interpretation** |
| The sword survives | **No evidence** |
| A present museum or collector owns the authentic sword | **No known claimant** |
