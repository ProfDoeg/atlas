# San Guinefort (venerated greyhound cult): Object Dossier

**Proposed filename:** `san_guinefort_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary note

“San Guinefort” is not a securely identified portable relic with an accession number or an unbroken chain of custody. The name designates several things that must be kept separate:

1. a greyhound said to have been unjustly killed after saving an infant;
2. the dog’s reputed body or bones, reportedly deposited in a well and later exhumed and burned;
3. the reputed grave-shrine: well, stone cairn, memorial trees, and surrounding grove;
4. the broader healing site now called the **Bois de Saint-Guignefort** at Sandrans in the Dombes, Ain, France;
5. the ritual deposits left there over centuries;
6. the textual and visual object created by successive retellings.

Only the fourth and fifth categories are archaeologically demonstrable. The dog, its killing, and the identification of any bones as its remains rest entirely on a thirteenth-century report of local oral tradition. No surviving canine relic has been authenticated, no canine bones from the shrine have undergone scientific analysis, and no modern object can be shown to be the dog’s body or a fragment of it.

This dossier therefore tracks an **object-complex**: a reputed canine relic that was reportedly destroyed, a physical cult site that survives, excavated ritual deposits, and the legend through which the absent relic retained an identity.

### Evidentiary labels

- **Documented:** supported directly by a contemporary text, archaeological report, catalogue, or other primary record.
- **Reported contemporary testimony:** something Étienne de Bourbon says he heard or did, but which cannot be independently corroborated.
- **Archaeological finding:** material recovered and described in an excavation report.
- **Scholarly inference:** an interpretation offered by a named scholar.
- **Institutional or local tradition:** maintained by a religious, civic, or local body but not independently demonstrated.
- **Legend:** an asserted event within the narrative tradition.
- **Modern retelling or invention:** a post-medieval representation that adds people, dates, motives, or material details absent from the medieval evidence.
- **Unresolved:** the surviving evidence does not decide the issue.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Principal identity

The earliest secure textual witness calls the being:

> **sanctum Guinefortem** — “Saint Guinefort”

and then identifies it as:

> **canis quidam leporarius** — “a certain coursing dog/greyhound.”

The section is conventionally titled **De adoratione Guinefortis canis**, “On the worship of Guinefort the dog.” These forms survive in Étienne de Bourbon’s Latin *Tractatus de diversis materiis praedicabilibus*, written between approximately 1250 and 1256. The Institut de recherche et d’histoire des textes catalogues the work under **Stephanus de Borbone** and dates its composition to 1250–1256; about twenty manuscript witnesses were known to the FAMA project in 2019, although the authorial original is lost ([Bibale/IRHT](https://bibale.irht.cnrs.fr/39653)).

Later forms include:

- **French:** *saint Guinefort*, *Guinefort*, *saint Guignefort*.
- **Franco-Provençal/local form:** commonly represented as *Saint-Guignefort* in the place-name *Bois de Saint-Guignefort*.
- **Latin:** *sanctus Guinefortus*; in the accusative, *sanctum Guinefortem*.
- **English:** Saint Guinefort, the Holy Greyhound, the Greyhound Saint.
- **Spanish:** San Guinefort.
- **Italian:** San Guinefort or San Guiniforte, although Italian churches bearing the latter name generally refer to a human martyr, not demonstrably to the Dombes dog.

The animal’s sex is not stated through a grammatical proper name alone, but Étienne’s narrative treats the greyhound as male in conventional translation. The dog’s breed designation is only **leporarius**, meaning a hare-hunting or coursing hound. Modern illustrations that give Guinefort a particular coat colour, conformation, collar, or heraldic appearance are not evidence of the medieval animal’s appearance.

### 1.2 The name and its disputed origin

No securely attested pre-cult name for the dog survives. It is possible that *Guinefort* was remembered as an animal’s name, but it is equally possible that an existing human saint’s name became attached to an anonymous dog story.

Jean-Claude Schmitt proposed that devotion to a human martyr Guinefort, associated especially with Pavia and transmitted through ecclesiastical networks, may have reached the Dombes before being assimilated to the local dog. A contemporary scholarly review summarizes his suggested route through Cluniac connections and a possible 1158 circuit involving relics of Saint Taurin. The same review calls the hypothesis a cumulative but not conclusive inference ([Persée review of Schmitt](https://www.persee.fr/doc/mar_0758-4431_1980_num_8_1_1092_t1_0175_0000_3)).

Jacques Dubois argued more directly for substitution or conflation between an obscure human martyr and the dog-martyr. He emphasized that Étienne at first assumed Guinefort was a human saint and discovered only after inquiry that local worshippers meant a dog ([Dubois, 1980](https://www.persee.fr/doc/jds_0021-8103_1980_num_1_1_1408)).

Other popular etymologies—derivation from Greek *kynephoros*, “dog-bearer,” or from Celtic-looking elements meaning “white/pure stream”—are speculative and should not be treated as established. The name’s resemblance to historical human saints named Guinefort/Guinifort is real; the direction of transmission is unresolved.

### 1.3 The legendary dog and reputed relic

Étienne supplies no dimensions, colour, age, pedigree, collar, owner’s name, or anatomical description. The physical description recoverable from his account is limited to:

- a **leporarius**, a greyhound or coursing hound;
- large and strong enough to fight and kill a “very large serpent”;
- badly bitten during the fight;
- its mouth and head covered in the serpent’s blood;
- killed by the lord with a sword;
- its body thrown into a well before the castle gate;
- covered with a great heap of stones;
- memorial trees planted nearby.

The narrative therefore describes a body deposited without a reliquary, coffin, inscription, shrine building, or recognized ecclesiastical translation. Later references to a “coffin,” formal tomb, chapel, statue, or canonically constituted shrine are embellishments unless tied to a separate, later cult site.

### 1.4 The physical cult site

The site identified by twentieth-century research is the **Bois de Saint-Guinefort/Guignefort**, in the commune of Sandrans, approximately two kilometres southeast of Châtillon-sur-Chalaronne. Archaeologist Jean-Michel Poisson described it as a north–south spur approximately **200 × 75 metres**, with a maximum elevation of **256 metres**. Its visible features included a transverse ditch, a mound, and a platform ([Poisson, 1980](https://www.persee.fr/doc/arcme_0153-9337_1980_num_10_1_1379_t1_0433_0000_2)).

Excavation found:

- a ditch cut into the natural substrate;
- levelling fill used to form or regularize the platform;
- shallow postholes apparently defining the corner of a timber building;
- at least one deeper posthole possibly belonging to a defensive structure;
- evidence of a sparse and probably short-lived eleventh–twelfth-century occupation built principally in wood and wattle-and-daub;
- a later circular pit about **one metre in diameter**;
- in and around that pit, a very large number of complete and fragmentary children’s leather shoes;
- approximately sixty coins dated from **1851 to 1919**.

These are the only securely reported cult-associated objects. The finds demonstrate repeated nineteenth- and early twentieth-century ritual deposition at the medieval occupation site. They do not identify the dog, its well, or its bones ([Poisson, 1981](https://www.persee.fr/doc/arcme_0153-9337_1981_num_11_1_1076_t1_0308_0000_2)).

The municipal planning record continues to inventory the location as archaeological site **01 393 0001, Bois de St.-Guignefort**, characterized by postholes and a ditch and assigned broadly to the central Middle Ages ([Sandrans planning report](https://sandrans.fr/wp-content/uploads/sites/618/2021/09/01393_rapport_1_20180313.pdf)).

### 1.5 What survives

**Survives:**

- the wooded place-name and landscape;
- archaeological traces of medieval occupation;
- the excavated nineteenth–twentieth-century deposit of shoes and coins, although the accessible sources consulted do not give a present repository or accession numbers;
- manuscript copies of Étienne’s *Tractatus*;
- later publications, photographs, signs, artistic representations, and local commemorative material.

**Reportedly destroyed:**

- the reputed dog’s bones;
- the thirteenth-century grove cut and burned by order of Étienne;
- potentially the particular well or stone cairn described in the story, neither of which has been securely identified archaeologically.

**Unverified or lost:**

- the original animal;
- its sword-killed skeleton;
- the serpent;
- the sword;
- the cradle;
- any original memorial stones or trees;
- the edict reportedly promulgated against renewed use of the site.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 No demonstrable manufacture

As a biological being, the legendary dog was not “manufactured.” No record identifies its breeder, birth, acquisition, or training. As a relic, the body was supposedly created by the violent death and subsequent memorial deposition described in oral tradition.

The shrine was likewise not described as a commissioned architectural work. According to the legend, the household:

1. threw the dog into a well before the gate;
2. covered it with a heap of stones;
3. planted trees beside it “in memory of the event.”

The shrine therefore began, in the story, as a private memorial assemblage rather than a church altar or purpose-built reliquary.

### 2.2 Earliest securely datable textual attestation

The earliest secure witness is Étienne de Bourbon’s *Tractatus de diversis materiis praedicabilibus*, composed in Latin in approximately **1250–1256**. The work is a large Dominican preaching collection organized around the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit. The Guinefort passage occurs in a treatment of superstitions considered insulting to God.

The critical institutional record dates the work to 1250–1256 and notes a complicated manuscript tradition: several early witnesses to the first textual state, about fifteen incomplete copies, and later abridgements and excerpt collections ([Bibale/IRHT](https://bibale.irht.cnrs.fr/39653)). The authorial manuscript is not known to survive.

The passage begins:

> *Sic faciebant nuper in diocesi Lugdunensi, ubi, cum ego predicarem contra sortilegia et confessiones audirem, multe mulieres confitebantur portasse se pueros suos apud sanctum Guinefortem. Et cum crederem esse sanctum aliquem, inquisivi, et audivi ad ultimum quod esset canis quidam leporarius…*

A close translation is:

> “This happened recently in the diocese of Lyon, where, while I was preaching against divinations and hearing confessions, many women confessed that they had carried their children to Saint Guinefort. Since I believed him to be some saint, I inquired, and finally heard that he was a certain greyhound…”

The Latin and the beginning of the complete passage are reproduced in the Cambridge excerpt from Schmitt’s study ([Cambridge University Press excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805211/08805/excerpt/9780521108805_excerpt.pdf)). A complete English translation is available through Fordham’s Internet Medieval Sourcebook ([Fordham translation](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/guinefort.asp)).

### 2.3 What the earliest text establishes

Étienne’s text establishes with reasonable confidence that, before the completion of his work:

- women in the diocese of Lyon spoke in confession of taking children to a being called Saint Guinefort;
- Étienne understood the being, after inquiry, to be a greyhound;
- the cult involved a particular rural place, offerings, clothing deposits, trees, and immersion in the Chalaronne;
- Étienne says he visited the site, preached there, destroyed bones and trees, and obtained a secular prohibition.

It does **not** independently establish:

- that a dog actually saved an infant;
- that the dog was named Guinefort in life;
- that the bones exhumed were canine;
- that they belonged to the animal in the story;
- when the supposed killing occurred;
- when the cult began;
- whether the castle’s destruction occurred as narrated;
- whether Étienne’s edict survives;
- whether the community’s story had already borrowed a widely circulating faithful-dog tale.

### 2.4 Place-name uncertainty

Translations and secondary accounts variously render the nearby religious settlement as **Villeneuve**, **Neuville**, or **Neuville-les-Dames**. Étienne situates it near a settlement of enclosed nuns, on the land of the lord of Villars-en-Dombes. Modern scholarship generally identifies the cult site with Sandrans, not with a surviving castle at Neuville itself.

This identification was materially strengthened when excavators found an abandoned eleventh–twelfth-century defended occupation precisely beneath the later pilgrimage deposits. That correspondence supports the location of the cult but does not prove the dog narrative.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because the relic was a body in a landscape rather than a traded object, “custody” means physical control over the body, the shrine, access to the site, and the authority to define its identity.

### 3.1 Legendary household custody

**Date:** undated; placed before Étienne’s visit.

**Claim:** A lord, his wife, their infant, and a nurse occupied a castle on the Villars estate. Their greyhound remained with the infant when the humans left.

**Mechanism:** domestic possession.

**Evidence:** only the oral account embedded in Étienne’s exemplum.

No member of the household is named. Modern retellings that name the lord, wife, nurse, healer, or child are fictional unless separately sourced.

### 3.2 Creation of the reputed relic

**Legendary sequence:**

1. A large serpent approached the unattended child.
2. The dog fought and killed it, overturning the cradle.
3. The nurse and parents saw blood and mistakenly believed the dog had killed the child.
4. The lord killed the dog with his sword.
5. The child was found alive and the serpent dead.
6. The household, grieving its error, threw the dog’s body into a well before the gate.
7. Stones were heaped over it and trees planted nearby.

The lord’s act converted a domestic animal into a memorialized body. The story does not say that the family declared it holy, invited pilgrimage, or appointed a keeper.

### 3.3 Communal appropriation

**Reported development:** Local peasants heard of the dog’s “noble deed” and unjust death, visited the site, honoured it “as a martyr,” and sought aid in illness and other needs.

**Custodial change:** The body and memorial passed in practice from a private household’s landscape to an unofficial communal shrine. No deed, donation, parish transfer, or ecclesiastical recognition is recorded.

**Physical additions and uses:**

- salt and unspecified offerings;
- infants’ swaddling clothes hung on thorn bushes;
- nails driven into trees;
- passage of naked infants through an opening between two trunks;
- candles attached to a tree;
- use of the nearby river Chalaronne.

The shrine was therefore continuously remade through deposits, piercing, tying, burning, and handling rather than conserved as a fixed monument.

### 3.4 Custody by ritual specialists

Étienne says mothers first consulted an **old woman** in a fortified place one league away. She instructed them and led them to the site. She functioned as guide, ritual technician, and practical custodian, but Étienne does not give her name or say that she owned the land.

This distinction matters: she is a documented participant in Étienne’s account, not the owner of the dog or a demonstrable hereditary priestess.

### 3.5 Étienne de Bourbon’s seizure and destruction

**Date:** before 1261; probably around the middle of the thirteenth century.

Étienne reports:

> “We had the dead dog disinterred, and the sacred wood cut down and burned, along with the remains of the dog.”

He further says he caused the territorial lords to issue an edict threatening seizure and sale of the property of anyone who thereafter gathered there for the same purpose ([Fordham translation](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/guinefort.asp)).

This is the closest thing to a documented seizure:

- the local community lost practical access under threat;
- secular lords supplied coercive authority;
- the bones were removed from the reputed grave;
- the trees and bones were burned;
- the dog-relic ceased, if the report is accurate, to exist as a recoverable body.

No record says the ashes were retained, buried, translated, or dispersed. There is no surviving reliquary and no subsequent claimant to a physical fragment.

The text’s plural “we” leaves the workmen unnamed. Étienne directed or caused the action; it need not mean he personally wielded the spade or axe.

### 3.6 The first major break

After Étienne’s suppression, the custody chain breaks. No continuous series of medieval or early-modern visitation records has been found. Episcopal visitation records cited in later scholarship apparently do not give a continuous account of the dog cult. Assertions that worship proceeded without interruption for “seven hundred years” are therefore stronger than the evidence permits.

What can be said is:

- the place-name and ritual complex survived or were revived;
- later testimony and archaeology demonstrate nineteenth- and early twentieth-century cult activity;
- the absence of intervening documentation prevents an unbroken chain from being proved.

### 3.7 Nineteenth-century rediscovery and documentation

The medieval text first appeared in print in abbreviated form in Quétif and Echard’s *Scriptores Ordinis Praedicatorum* in **1719**. It was reproduced or summarized through nineteenth-century demonological and folkloric literature before Albert Lecoy de La Marche published a fuller Latin text in **1877**, pp. 325–328. Jacques Dubois provides the publication history ([Dubois, 1980](https://www.persee.fr/doc/jds_0021-8103_1980_num_1_1_1408)).

In **1879**, Augustin Vayssière conducted a local inquiry and published *Saint Guinefort. Origine et histoire du culte rendu à ce prétendu saint dans la paroisse de Romans (Ain)*. Informants reportedly continued to identify Guinefort as a dog. By this period, the legend had changed: in some testimony the lord’s castle had become a woodsman’s cabin. Ritual evidence included large quantities of children’s clothing and knotted branches. Lester K. Little’s review of Schmitt summarizes the nineteenth-century controversy ([New York Review of Books](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1981/04/30/the-greyhound-saint/)).

In **1886**, Abbé Delaigne defended a human-saint interpretation against Vayssière’s dog identification. This was not a dispute over two rival skeletons but over the identity projected onto one cult and place.

### 3.8 Material custody in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries

Coins and children’s shoes were deposited in or around a circular pit. The coin sequence, **1851–1919**, provides a material terminus for at least the final archaeological phase of pilgrimage. The shoes demonstrate that children or their representatives deposited worn personal objects. They do not prove that every visitor believed Guinefort was literally a dog.

Oral testimony collected by Schmitt in the 1970s remembered an elderly woman operating into the 1930s who begged, lit candles in church for others, and undertook pilgrimages on behalf of people unable to make them. She was understood locally as the last practical keeper or intermediary of Guinefort. Her name is not securely supplied by the accessible sources consulted ([summary of Schmitt’s fieldwork](https://www.dogsinhistory.org/stories-dogs-in-history/saint-guinefort-greyhound-medieval-dog-france)).

Reports of a last curative visit in the 1940s depend on retrospective oral history. They should not be converted into a precise termination date.

### 3.9 Archaeological intervention

Poisson’s excavations in 1979–1980 shifted part of the object-complex from vernacular custody into archaeological custody. Excavation was not neutral preservation: it removed shoes, coins, and soil from the ritual context for recording and study.

The accessible preliminary reports do not identify:

- a museum accession;
- the current storage institution;
- a conservation report for the leather;
- a complete catalogue of coins;
- any radiocarbon, dendrochronological, zoological, or biomolecular testing.

Those are documented gaps rather than evidence that the finds were lost.

### 3.10 Present control

The wooded location remains within the commune of Sandrans and is listed in municipal planning documentation as an archaeological site. The available official municipal description identifies the Chalaronne as the commune’s principal watercourse and treats tourism as locally significant, but does not describe a living sanctioned cult or a museum reliquary ([Sandrans municipal presentation](https://sandrans.fr/je-decouvre-sandrans/presentation/)).

No current ownership litigation, ecclesiastical claim, repatriation demand, or rival physical relic claimant was found in the consulted sources.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Did the greyhound exist?

**For existence:**

- Étienne says multiple women independently confessed participation in the cult.
- He says he investigated and visited the site.
- The archaeological site corresponds strikingly to his description of an abandoned defended settlement.
- The dog’s body was said to have been exhumed in his presence.

**Against certainty:**

- all information about the dog comes through a didactic text written to condemn “superstition”;
- the narrative belongs to a widely diffused faithful-hound story family;
- no named witness to the killing appears;
- no independent contemporary record survives;
- no animal remains are available for examination;
- even if Étienne exhumed bones, their species, date, and identity were not recorded scientifically.

**Assessment:** A thirteenth-century cult of a being understood by devotees or by Étienne as a dog is well attested. The historicity of the particular dog and rescue episode is unresolved.

### 4.2 Were the destroyed bones the dog’s?

Étienne’s wording treats the excavated remains as those of the dog. No osteological inspection is described. Possible alternatives include:

- genuine canine remains associated with an earlier memorial;
- bones of another animal;
- human remains;
- an assemblage planted or retrospectively identified as Guinefort;
- no recoverable remains, with the account compressed for rhetorical purposes.

No source permits adjudication.

### 4.3 Is the Bois de Saint-Guignefort the medieval shrine?

The case is substantial but circumstantial.

**Supporting evidence:**

- persistent place-name;
- location within the Dombes near the places named by Étienne;
- spur, ditch, mound, and platform;
- eleventh–twelfth-century light-built defended occupation abandoned before or around the time of Étienne;
- direct archaeological evidence for later child-healing pilgrimage.

**Limitations:**

- no inscribed marker naming Guinefort was excavated;
- no identified medieval well or canine burial was reported;
- the nineteenth-century pit represents a later phase;
- precise medieval cadastral continuity is not fully demonstrated in the preliminary reports.

**Assessment:** The Sandrans site is the strongest and generally accepted candidate. It is not authenticated by a named relic or inscription.

### 4.4 Dog versus human saint

At least two human Guinefort traditions circulated in western Europe, including a martyr connected with Pavia and other regional saints bearing similar names. Churches and chapels named for Guinefort should not automatically be assigned to the dog cult.

The dispute has three main models:

1. **Dog first:** a real or legendary dog was locally honoured and given its own name.
2. **Human saint first:** devotion to a human Guinefort reached the Dombes, where his name and healing function became attached to a faithful-dog story.
3. **Cultic fusion:** an existing healing site, a human saint’s name, a dog-and-serpent tale, and rites concerning sick or “changed” children gradually converged.

Schmitt favored a historically layered fusion. Dubois emphasized substitution of the human martyr for, or by, the dog-martyr. No inscription or early liturgical calendar from Sandrans resolves the sequence.

### 4.5 Canonical status

Guinefort the greyhound was never canonized by the Roman Church. Étienne’s action was suppression, not examination for canonization. “Saint” here records vernacular cult title, not an official juridical status.

Rebecca Rist interprets the suppression as simultaneously:

- rejection of an unauthorized cult;
- rejection of divine honours to an animal or other creature;
- opposition to magical or demonic ritual;
- intervention against rites that endangered children.

Her study emphasizes that the episode concerns control of sanctity and pastoral safety as much as abstract doctrine ([Rist, 2019](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/81816/)).

### 4.6 Scientific testing

No radiocarbon dating, DNA study, isotopic examination, or zoological authentication of “Guinefort” has been reported. Such testing is probably impossible unless previously unrecognized bones from the excavation or another securely provenanced context survive.

The scientific evidence applies to the **site**, not the saint:

- stratigraphic remains of medieval occupation;
- a nineteenth–twentieth-century ritual pit;
- coins providing dates;
- children’s footwear identifying the ritual population or intended beneficiaries.

### 4.7 Modern false specificity

Several circulating modern accounts should be treated cautiously:

- a precise date such as **1246** for Étienne’s suppression is not supplied by the medieval passage;
- a coffin is not mentioned; the body was placed in a well under stones;
- a formal church or chapel over the dog is not mentioned;
- a canonical feast of 22 August for the dog is not demonstrated merely because a human Guinefort was commemorated then;
- images routinely captioned as medieval portraits of Guinefort are generally later paintings of dogs or modern illustrations;
- invented dialogues placing the twentieth-century historian Marc Bloch beside Étienne are explicitly modern literary constructions, not medieval evidence.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Étienne de Bourbon / Stephanus de Borbone

**Date:** active in the episode before 1261; text composed c. 1250–1256.  
**Connection:** Dominican preacher and inquisitorial agent who recorded the cult, investigated it, preached against it, ordered destruction of the reputed relic and grove, and obtained a secular edict.  
**Evidence:** first-person statements in the *Tractatus*.

### The unnamed lord of the castle

**Date:** legendary, before Étienne’s visit.  
**Connection:** reputed owner of the dog; killed it with a sword; helped create the memorial deposition.  
**Evidence:** legend reported by Étienne.

### The unnamed lady, infant, and nurse

**Date:** legendary.  
**Connection:** family at the centre of the rescue and mistaken killing.  
**Evidence:** legend reported by Étienne.

### The unnamed old woman

**Date:** thirteenth century according to Étienne.  
**Connection:** taught and led the child-healing ritual.  
**Evidence:** Étienne’s report; no independent identification.

### Women and mothers of the Dombes

**Date:** before Étienne’s intervention and in later phases.  
**Connection:** principal ritual users; carried children, made offerings, transferred infants between trees, left clothing, burned candles, and immersed children in the Chalaronne.  
**Evidence:** Étienne’s confessional report, later ethnography, archaeological footwear deposits.

### Lords of the land

**Date:** thirteenth century.  
**Connection:** issued, according to Étienne, an edict threatening confiscation and sale of the possessions of future participants.  
**Evidence:** Étienne’s account; no separate charter identified.

### Dominican Order

**Date:** thirteenth century onward.  
**Connection:** institutional network through which Étienne’s condemnation and exemplum circulated in manuscripts and preaching.  
**Evidence:** manuscript tradition and authorial affiliation.

### Fauns of the wood of Rimite

**Date:** attested in Étienne’s text.  
**Connection:** beings invoked during a child-exchange ritual. Mothers reportedly asked them to accept the sick child thought to belong to them and return the family’s healthy child.  
**Status:** beings within reported vernacular belief, described by Étienne as demons; not independent historical actors.

### The Devil

**Date:** Étienne’s interpretation.  
**Connection:** theological agent whom Étienne blamed for deceiving worshippers; one woman reportedly interpreted a wolf as the Devil in animal form.  
**Status:** authorial religious explanation, not an archaeologically testable component.

### Augustin Vayssière

**Date:** 1879.  
**Connection:** archivist and folklorist who investigated the cult locally and published informant testimony identifying Guinefort as a dog.  
**Evidence:** his 1879 study and later scholarly discussion.

### Abbé Delaigne

**Date:** 1886.  
**Connection:** clerical polemicist who argued for a human Saint Guinefort against the canine interpretation.  
**Evidence:** summarized in later historiography.

### Jean-Claude Schmitt

**Date:** fieldwork in the 1970s; book first published 1979, enlarged edition 2004.  
**Connection:** historian who reunited the medieval text, comparative folklore, field testimony, archaeology, ritual analysis, and social history. His work established the modern scholarly framework.  
**Evidence:** *Le saint lévrier* and its reviews ([WorldCat record](https://search.worldcat.org/fr/title/Le-saint-levrier-%3A-Guinefort-guerisseur-d%27enfants-depuis-le-XIIIe-siecle/oclc/5796157)).

### Jean-Michel Poisson

**Date:** 1979–1980 excavations; reports 1980–1981.  
**Connection:** archaeologist responsible for investigation of the Sandrans site and discovery of the late ritual pit, shoes, and coins.  
**Evidence:** excavation notices in *Archéologie médiévale*.

### Jacques Dubois

**Date:** 1980.  
**Connection:** historian of sainthood who examined the dog/human-saint substitution and publication history.  
**Evidence:** “Saint Guinefort Vénéré des Dombes.”

### Rebecca Rist

**Date:** 2019.  
**Connection:** historian who reconsidered the episode within papal, inquisitorial, pastoral, and doctrinal control of sanctity.  
**Evidence:** peer-reviewed article in *Reinardus*.

### Association Saint-Guignefort

**Date:** active by the late twentieth and twenty-first centuries.  
**Connection:** Châtillon-sur-Chalaronne local-history association named after the cult; in 2005 published *Saint Guignefort: légende, archéologie, histoire*, containing Étienne’s legend, literary parallels, archaeological material, and historical commentary.  
**Evidence:** Bibliothèque nationale de France catalogue, FRBNF 41194383 ([BnF catalogue](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb41194383z)).

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Thirteenth-century healing and exchange rite

Étienne provides the fullest early procedural record:

1. A woman with a sick or weak child consulted an old woman one league away.
2. The old woman instructed and led her to the site.
3. Salt and unspecified other things were offered.
4. The child’s swaddling clothes were hung on surrounding thorn bushes.
5. Nails were driven into the trees.
6. The naked child was passed or thrown nine times between two tree trunks, from mother to ritual specialist.
7. The participants invoked the fauns of the wood of Rimite.
8. They asked the fauns to take the weak child believed to belong to them and return the family’s own child healthy, fat, and alive.
9. The infant was placed naked on cradle straw at the foot of a tree.
10. Two short candles were fixed to the tree near the child’s head and lit.
11. The adults withdrew beyond sight and hearing until the candles burned down.
12. If the child remained alive, it was immersed nine times in the fast-flowing Chalaronne.

The number nine structures both passage and immersion. The rite resembles a trial, exchange, and rebirth rather than simple petitionary prayer.

### 6.2 Changelings

The ritual’s spoken logic presupposed that a weak or sick child might not be the parents’ true child but one substituted by woodland beings. The ceremony attempted a reverse exchange.

This is a belief reported by a hostile clerical source. It should not automatically be generalized to all women, all visits, or every later phase of the cult.

### 6.3 Danger and death

Étienne says witnesses told him that candles burned and killed several infants. He also recounts a woman who saw a wolf approaching a child and intervened. He calls participating women murderous or “infanticidal.”

The hazards are physically credible, but the reported deaths remain uncorroborated. His rhetoric served the moral purpose of the exemplum. The appropriate conclusion is that the described procedure exposed infants to fire, cold, animals, and dangerous immersion—not that a quantified death toll is known.

### 6.4 Later ritual forms

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century evidence indicates changes:

- children’s shoes were deposited in a pit;
- coins were offered;
- children’s clothing was left;
- branches were knotted, reportedly to bind fever or release impaired limbs;
- representatives could undertake pilgrimage for absent families;
- church candles could be lit by an intermediary.

The later rite therefore shifted from direct exposure and immersion toward votive deposition, sympathetic binding/unbinding, and delegated pilgrimage.

### 6.5 Symbolic function

Guinefort embodied:

- protection of infants;
- innocence punished through hasty judgment;
- the faithful domestic animal;
- the unjustly killed martyr;
- non-clerical access to healing;
- women’s ritual expertise;
- contested authority to recognize sanctity.

For Étienne, the shrine symbolized misdirected worship and dangerous superstition. For later scholars and artists it became an emblem of conflict between official religion and vernacular practice, although that binary can oversimplify the changing local evidence.

### 6.6 Access and taboo

No evidence indicates a formal priesthood, admission fee, relic-display schedule, or restriction by sex. Women and children dominate the narrative, but Étienne says people sought help for illnesses and “other needs,” so the cult may not have been exclusively pediatric.

After suppression, attendance for the prohibited purposes theoretically exposed a participant’s property to confiscation and sale. Whether this penalty was ever enforced is unknown.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The faithful greyhound

**Tier:** earliest attested local legend, recorded c. 1250–1256.

The core story is the familiar tragic pattern of a faithful animal falsely accused:

- guardian animal;
- vulnerable child;
- animal adversary;
- misleading blood and disorder;
- impulsive killing;
- discovery of the rescued child;
- remorse and memorialization.

The story is not unique to the Dombes. Analogues appear in the *Seven Sages of Rome* or Sindbad narrative cycle and in later traditions such as the Welsh Gelert story. Comparative scholarship classifies it within the broad “faithful hound” complex. The existence of parallels does not prove that Étienne copied the story, but it prevents treating every narrative element as local historical testimony.

### 7.2 Destruction of the castle

Étienne says the castle was subsequently destroyed “by divine will” and the land became deserted. This serves several narrative functions:

- divine judgment;
- explanation for why a domestic memorial stood in an abandoned wood;
- removal of the original household from the custody chain;
- creation of a liminal space appropriate to fauns and illicit rites.

Archaeology found an apparently ephemeral eleventh–twelfth-century occupation abandoned before the later cult deposits, lending the abandonment motif a possible factual setting. It does not demonstrate divine causation or connect the abandonment with the dog’s death.

### 7.3 The dog as martyr and healer

The peasants reportedly interpreted the dog’s innocent death as martyrdom. Strictly speaking, the dog did not die for Christian confession, and Étienne rejected the attribution of sacred honour. Yet the narrative supplies a martyr-like structure:

- innocence;
- voluntary or faithful defence of another;
- unjust death;
- memorial grave;
- posthumous aid.

The healing power is not located in a described fluid, touch-relic, or incorrupt body. It arises from place, story, memorial trees, exchange ritual, and invocation.

### 7.4 The wolf as devil

**Tier:** contemporary reported visionary interpretation.

One participant told Étienne that a wolf approached the exposed child and that it was, or might have been, the Devil in wolf form. Étienne reports her interpretation rather than an independently witnessed transformation.

### 7.5 Human-saint substitution

**Tier:** scholarly reconstruction of cult development.

The theory that a human martyr Guinefort was transformed into or fused with a dog saint explains the name and possible feast associations. It remains an inference. No source records a formal moment when villagers replaced one with the other.

### 7.6 Continuous seven-century survival

**Tier:** popular compression of discontinuous evidence.

Modern summaries often say the cult survived continuously from the thirteenth century to the 1930s or 1940s. The beginning and late phase are documented, but the intervening record is sparse. It is safer to describe:

- medieval attestation;
- suppression;
- later reappearance or survival;
- nineteenth-century ethnographic documentation;
- material evidence dated 1851–1919;
- twentieth-century memories.

Continuity is plausible but not documentarily complete.

### 7.7 Modern fictional additions

The 1987 Franco-Swiss film *Le Moine et la Sorcière* (*Sorceress*), directed by Suzanne Schiffman and written with Paméla Berger, builds an original dramatic plot around Étienne’s recorded encounter. Its named healer, personal conflicts, and detailed village drama are artistic constructions, not recovered biography. The film was released in France on 23 September 1987 ([AlloCiné](https://www.allocine.fr/film/fichefilm_gen_cfilm=2689.html)).

Modern online dialogues that make Marc Bloch Étienne’s novice, date the event precisely to 1246, or describe Guinefort’s coffin are creative works. They should not be folded back into the medieval dossier.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Reputed relic

**Status:** destroyed, if Étienne’s account is accurate.

No authenticated fragment, ash deposit, reliquary, skeleton, or replacement relic is known. The remains reportedly exhumed in the thirteenth century were burned with the grove. Their prior identity was never scientifically established.

### 8.2 Cult site

**Location:** Bois de Saint-Guinefort/Guignefort, commune of Sandrans, Ain, Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes, France; approximately two kilometres southeast of Châtillon-sur-Chalaronne.

**Status:** surviving landscape and registered archaeological locality, not a museum-held object. A modern sign and continuing place-name mark the association. The available municipal site inventory records postholes and a ditch under archaeological identifier 01 393 0001.

**Access:** The consulted official sources do not provide a guaranteed public opening schedule, legal trail right, or permission to enter particular parcels. The surrounding commune promotes walking and tourism, but that should not be taken as permission to excavate, deposit offerings, light fires, or enter private woodland.

### 8.3 Archaeological finds

**Known finds:** children’s leather shoes, fragments, and approximately sixty coins dated 1851–1919.

**Repository:** not established from the accessible excavation notices.

**Condition:** not published in the sources consulted.

**Accession numbers:** none located.

**Scientific work:** stratigraphic and typological archaeological examination is reported; no published DNA, radiocarbon, residue, or leather-species analysis was located.

### 8.4 Ritual status

There is no evidence of present official Roman Catholic veneration, a canonical feast, an operating chapel, or a recognized relic. Local historical memory remains active through the place-name, scholarship, signage, exhibitions, publications, and the Association Saint-Guignefort.

No current custody or repatriation dispute was found.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Print scholarship and public rediscovery

The cult’s modern public life began with the recovery of Étienne’s text:

- **1719:** abbreviated early printed notice in Quétif and Echard.
- **1825/1846:** adaptations in demonological and theological dictionaries.
- **1877:** Lecoy de La Marche’s influential Latin publication.
- **1879:** Vayssière’s field inquiry.
- **1886:** Delaigne’s clerical response.
- **1979:** Schmitt’s *Le saint lévrier*.
- **1983:** English translation, *The Holy Greyhound*.
- **2004:** enlarged French edition.
- **2005:** local collective volume *Saint Guignefort: légende, archéologie, histoire*.

The BnF records the 2005 volume as a 63-page illustrated publication of the Association Saint-Guignefort, including extracts from Étienne and the *Roman des sept sages* ([BnF catalogue](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb41194383z)).

### 9.2 Film

*Le Moine et la Sorcière* made Guinefort part of a broader drama about healing, gender, heresy, and clerical authority. It materially shaped the international public image of the cult by supplying faces, dialogue, architecture, and motives absent from Étienne.

Rebecca Rist notes that the film helped generate or accompany further cultural offshoots, including television treatment, an installation by Venezuelan artist José Antonio Hernández-Diez, and the local association ([Rist article](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/81816/8/REVISED%20REVISED%20ARTICLE%20Saint%20Guinefort.pdf)).

### 9.3 Visual representations

No medieval image securely representing the Dombes dog is known. Many internet articles use unrelated paintings of greyhounds, sometimes captioned as though they depict Guinefort. These are illustrative substitutions.

Modern devotional-style pictures, icons, medals, tattoos, pet memorials, and commercial prints represent a new material reception. Unless tied to the Sandrans ritual or documented ecclesiastical use, they are not continuations of an authenticated medieval cult object.

### 9.4 Animal-sainthood discourse

Guinefort is now frequently called “the only dog saint.” That formulation is rhetorically effective but historically imprecise:

- he was never canonized;
- other animals have been treated as holy, miraculous, commemorative, or quasi-saintly;
- other dog-associated cults and canine miracle stories exist;
- the phrase converts a contested local attribution into canonical status.

### 9.5 Heritage identity

The Association Saint-Guignefort adopted the name for local historical work extending well beyond the dog legend. This transformed a suppressed cult-name into a civic and scholarly heritage emblem. The association’s work includes local archives, publications, exhibitions, and historical commemoration ([Patrimoines de l’Ain](https://patrimoines.ain.fr/n/saint-guignefort-a-chatillon-sur-chalaronne/n:1412)).

### 9.6 Tourism and commercial reception

The story circulates widely through travel writing, dog-history websites, podcasts, blogs, occult compilations, and social media. Common distortions include:

- presenting the cult as officially Catholic;
- giving the dog a formal canonization;
- turning the cairn into an elaborate chapel;
- claiming uninterrupted documentation through every century;
- treating unrelated statues of human saints as images of the dog;
- dating the rescue or suppression to a precise year without evidence.

These retellings are part of Guinefort’s modern object-life because they direct visitors and generate replicas, but they are not primary evidence.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Étienne as witness and polemicist

The central historiographical problem is that the earliest source is both unusually direct and deeply interested. Étienne claims firsthand investigation, site visitation, preaching, and suppression. But he wrote an exemplum designed to demonstrate the danger of superstition.

His account separates several voices imperfectly:

- his own observation;
- women’s confessions;
- local oral history;
- unnamed witnesses to deaths;
- one participant’s wolf story;
- his theological interpretation.

A responsible reconstruction must not treat every clause as equally eyewitness.

### 10.2 Textual history

Étienne’s original manuscript is lost. The *Tractatus* survives in multiple manuscripts of different completeness and in later abridgements. Its scholarly recovery proceeded through:

- Quétif and Echard, 1719;
- Lecoy de La Marche, 1877;
- Schmitt, 1979 and later editions;
- modern critical work on the *Tractatus* in the *Corpus Christianorum* series.

The IRHT record lists approximately twenty manuscript witnesses known through FAMA as of 2019 and explains the appearance of abridgements and unordered excerpt collections from the fourteenth century onward ([Bibale/IRHT](https://bibale.irht.cnrs.fr/39653)).

### 10.3 Folklorist approach

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century scholars often compared the dog-and-serpent story with international faithful-animal legends. This established that the narrative was migratory, but it sometimes detached the tale from the material child-healing rite and local social setting.

The relevant narrative family includes the dog-and-serpent episode of the *Seven Sages* tradition. Similarity demonstrates transmission possibilities, not that the Dombes worshippers knowingly adapted a written romance.

### 10.4 Schmitt’s synthesis

Schmitt’s 1979 study changed the field by combining:

- source criticism;
- oral history;
- field investigation;
- archaeology;
- comparative ritual;
- saint-name distribution;
- social and economic history;
- symbolism of dogs, trees, summer feast dates, and Sirius.

His famous longue-durée reconstruction is powerful but not universally accepted in every link. Reviewers questioned whether nineteenth-century evidence proved uninterrupted survival and whether the Pavia-to-Dombes transmission could be demonstrated rather than inferred. The later review literature nevertheless treats the book as foundational ([review in *Archives de sciences sociales des religions*](https://journals.openedition.org/assr/pdf/2848)).

### 10.5 Archaeology’s contribution

Poisson’s excavations independently confirmed two key components:

1. an abandoned medieval occupation suitable for Étienne’s ruined-castle setting;
2. a late healing-pilgrimage deposit involving children.

Archaeology did **not** confirm:

- the dog’s grave;
- a medieval well;
- the serpent;
- the household;
- the sword killing;
- continuous use from the thirteenth through nineteenth centuries.

### 10.6 Saint or dog?

Dubois framed the problem as a history of substitution. Vayssière’s informants reportedly said the saint was a dog; Delaigne argued for a human saint. Schmitt’s more diachronic answer was effectively that the identity changed: dog, saint, and composite cult were not mutually exclusive at every period.

The best present formulation is:

- Étienne understood the thirteenth-century cult as directed to a dog;
- the name may have come from a human saint;
- later worshippers need not all have agreed on the being’s zoological identity;
- some churches bearing the same name belonged to human-saint traditions;
- the Sandrans site is not evidence that every European Saint Guinefort was canine.

### 10.7 Suppression and “the Inquisition”

Popular accounts often say “the Inquisition burned Guinefort.” Étienne was a Dominican active in inquisitorial preaching and investigation, but his narrative describes a local pastoral and coercive intervention, not a formal animal heresy trial. No dog was tried posthumously, no sentence against an animal is preserved, and no papal bull naming Guinefort is known.

Rist situates the action within the broader institutional consolidation of sanctity and inquisitorial authority while emphasizing the immediate danger posed by the rites ([Rist, 2019](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/81816/)).

### 10.8 Unpublished or inaccessible evidence

Important research gaps include:

- complete publication of excavation records and find catalogues;
- present repository of the shoes and coins;
- full numismatic list;
- conservation history of organic materials;
- land records for the wood;
- the exact oral-history recordings or transcripts used by Schmitt;
- systematic review of episcopal visitation records;
- any surviving copy or reference to Étienne’s secular edict;
- archaeozoological review of any unclassified bones from the site.

### Overall conclusion

The fullest defensible reconstruction is not the biography of a surviving sacred dog-body. It is the history of an unstable sacred assemblage.

A community in the thirteenth-century Dombes visited an abandoned settlement and addressed a being called Guinefort, whom Étienne de Bourbon understood to be a greyhound. Its reputed body lay in a stone-covered well amid memorial trees. Women used the place in rites meant to heal or exchange sick children. Étienne seized the shrine, caused the bones and grove to be burned, and obtained a coercive prohibition. Documentation then becomes intermittent. By the nineteenth century, dog-identifying legend and healing practices were again or still present. Archaeology later demonstrated a medieval defended occupation and a final cult phase materially represented by children’s shoes and coins dated 1851–1919.

No surviving bone can be linked to Guinefort. No scientific test verifies the animal. The enduring “object” is therefore the place and its repeatedly reconstructed identity: first domestic memorial in legend, then vernacular shrine, condemned relic, votive landscape, archaeological site, scholarly case study, civic heritage name, and modern emblem of contested sanctity.
