# The Nobel medals of von Laue and Franck, dissolved in acid: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier tracks two related but distinct objects:

1. the Nobel Prize in Physics medal awarded to Max von Laue for 1914; and  
2. the Nobel Prize in Physics medal awarded jointly—one individual medal to each laureate—to James Franck for 1925.

Both were deposited at Niels Bohr’s Institute for Theoretical Physics in Copenhagen. On 9 April 1940, the day Germany occupied Denmark, George de Hevesy dissolved them in aqua regia. Their original relief surfaces, inscriptions, patina, tool marks, and individual physical identities thereby ceased to exist. Gold recovered after the war was delivered to the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences in January 1950. Replacement medals were subsequently struck.

The phrase “the dissolved Nobel medals” therefore denotes several successive material states:

- two individually engraved, struck gold medals;
- gold-bearing acidic solution, probably accompanied by insoluble or precipitated alloy constituents;
- recovered and refined gold;
- two newly struck replacement medals;
- and, in modern memory, an emblematic story attached both to those replacements and to an otherwise unpreserved laboratory container.

The central evidentiary problem is continuity. The replacement medals reproduce the institutional identity of the awards, but they are not physically continuous with the originals as manufactured objects. Moreover, no surviving assay or mint record located for this dossier proves that each replacement contains the gold of its corresponding original. One scholarly reconstruction argues that the recovered material may instead have entered the mint’s general refining stream.

The best contemporary or near-primary documentation consists of:

- Hevesy’s autobiographical account, first published in 1958 and reprinted in 1962;
- correspondence among Max von Laue, Niels Bohr, George de Hevesy, and representatives of the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences;
- the Academy’s receipt for the recovered gold;
- the Nobel Foundation board proceedings of 28 February 1952;
- the surviving replacement medal and papers of James Franck at the University of Chicago;
- and a dossier in the Niels Bohr Archive.

A particularly useful critical study is Stephan Schwarz’s “The Case of the Bottled Nobel Medals,” based on correspondence and records in the Niels Bohr Archive. It distinguishes archival evidence from later simplifications and identifies several unresolved questions.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Object class and names

The objects were award medals: specifically, gold Nobel Prize medals for physics issued under Alfred Nobel’s testament and awarded by the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences.

Common names include:

- **Max von Laue’s Nobel Prize medal**;
- **James Franck’s Nobel Prize medal**;
- German: **Nobelmedaille**, plural **Nobelmedaillen**;
- Danish: **Nobelmedalje**, plural **Nobelmedaljer**;
- Swedish: **Nobelmedalj**, plural **Nobelmedaljer**;
- the “dissolved Nobel medals”;
- the “bottled Nobel medals,” a modern descriptive title;
- Danish popular formulation: **Nobelpriserne på flaske**, “the Nobel Prizes in bottles.”

Strictly, it was the medals, not the Nobel Prizes themselves, that were dissolved. The monetary prizes, diplomas, and legal status of the awards were separate. “Melted” is also chemically inaccurate: the medals were dissolved, not liquefied by heat.

### 1.2 Original medals

The physics and chemistry Nobel medals share the same design. It was created by the Swedish sculptor and medal engraver Johan Erik Lindberg, usually called Erik Lindberg, and finalized in 1902. The Nobel Foundation states that, until 1980, the Swedish Nobel medals were made of 23-carat gold, weighed approximately 200 grams, and measured 66 millimetres in diameter. Precise weights could vary. Contemporary numismatic descriptions classify the early type as very high-fineness gold, approximately 0.958–0.992 depending on the source and specimen; without an assay record for these two medals, “23-carat gold” is the safest institutional specification.

The originals were almost certainly manufactured by the Swedish Royal Mint, Kungliga Myntverket. Nobel medals were produced there from 1902 through 2010. Their individual workshop or mint-production records have not been located in the public sources examined.

#### Obverse

The common obverse carried:

- a left-facing relief portrait of Alfred Nobel;
- **ALFR. NOBEL**;
- **NAT. MDCCCXXXIII**—*natus 1833*, “born 1833”;
- **OB. MDCCCXCVI**—*obiit 1896*, “died 1896”;
- Lindberg’s signature and the date 1902.

#### Reverse

The reverse presented an allegory of natural science:

- Nature, conventionally represented as a goddess resembling Isis, emerges from clouds and holds a cornucopia;
- the Genius of Science raises the veil from Nature’s face;
- the fields are marked **NATURA** and **SCIENTIA**;
- the awarding body is abbreviated **REG. ACAD. SCIENT. SUEC.**, *Regia Academia Scientiarum Suecica*, the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences;
- a tablet or cartouche below the figures bore the laureate’s engraved name.

Around the reverse appeared:

> **INVENTAS VITAM IUVAT EXCOLUISSE PER ARTES**

Literally, “It is beneficial to have improved life through discovered arts.” It adapts Virgil, *Aeneid* 6.663: *inventas aut qui vitam excoluere per artes*.

The engraved personal name was crucial in April 1940. Hevesy emphasized that discovery of a medal carrying Laue’s name would connect the gold directly to him.

### 1.3 Individual inscriptions

Published sources establish that each Swedish Nobel medal bore its laureate’s name on the reverse tablet. The original medals therefore would have been engraved for:

- **MAX VON LAUE**, or a mint-era equivalent;
- **JAMES FRANCK**, or a mint-era equivalent.

The exact punctuation, capitalization, line division, mint marks, edge marks, and weights of the destroyed originals have not been recovered from a published catalogue or photograph specific to those specimens. Generic photographs of Nobel medals should not be mistaken for photographs of these originals.

### 1.4 Dissolved state

Aqua regia—Latin “royal water,” German **Königswasser**, Danish **kongevand**—is normally a freshly prepared mixture of concentrated hydrochloric and nitric acids, conventionally in an approximate 3:1 volume ratio. Nitric acid oxidizes metallic gold, while chloride ions stabilize the oxidized gold as soluble chloroaurate complexes. The result is commonly a yellow, orange, or yellow-brown solution containing tetrachloroaurate(III), often represented as chloroauric acid in strongly acidic solution.

The Royal Danish Academy’s modern account calls the resulting liquid pale yellow. That color is chemically plausible but is not independently documented in a contemporary laboratory record.

Because the medals were alloyed rather than chemically pure gold:

- copper could dissolve;
- silver would tend to form sparingly soluble silver chloride;
- other minor constituents could precipitate or remain as residue;
- complete dissolution of intact 200-gram medals would have been slow.

Schwarz reports an archival hypothesis that the medals may first have been machine-milled into shavings in the institute workshop. He expressly presents this as an inference; no surviving work order or eyewitness statement proves it.

No published evidence establishes:

- the volume or exact recipe of acid;
- whether there was one vessel or multiple vessels;
- whether both medals entered a single bath;
- the vessel’s label;
- whether the alloy residues were retained;
- or the exact time required.

The common image of a single ordinary beaker quietly standing on a shelf is a later visualization, not securely documented fact.

### 1.5 Recovered metal and replacement medals

The original medallions were not reassembled. Metallic gold was recovered from the solution after the war and sent to Stockholm. New medals were struck from Nobel dies and engraved anew.

The Franck replacement is catalogued by the University of Chicago as:

- “Nobel Prize medal, 1925”;
- “Recast 1951”;
- gold;
- approximately 6½ centimetres in diameter;
- held with the James Franck Papers, Box 22.

The catalogue’s diameter is consistent with the standard 66-millimetre Nobel medal. “Recast” is institutional shorthand. Technically, Nobel medals are struck, not simply cast, and the replacement was a newly manufactured medal.

No published scientific assay of the Franck replacement has been identified. No published measurement establishes what fraction, if any, of its atoms came from Franck’s original medal rather than Laue’s medal, another dissolved medal, added refining gold, or mint stock.

The Laue replacement was manufactured and delivered, but its present location is unestablished. A 2023 scholarly article states that all trace of it has been lost. No accessible museum catalogue or archive accession record located here contradicts that statement.

### 1.6 What survives

Surviving or documented:

- Franck’s replacement medal at the University of Chicago;
- the James Franck Nobel diploma;
- correspondence and administrative papers concerning the dissolution and replacement;
- photographs of Franck’s original 1926 award ceremony;
- Laue’s surviving Nobel diploma or its archival successor—the diploma was transferred from the Niels Bohr Archive to the Archive of the Max Planck Society in June 2000;
- documentary references to the recovered gold and replacement medals.

Destroyed as individual artifacts:

- both original gold medals;
- their original engraved surfaces;
- their individual manufacturing traces and patina;
- any recoverable physical distinction between “Laue’s gold” and “Franck’s gold,” if the metals were combined.

Unverified or apparently lost:

- the wartime vessel or vessels;
- any original solution;
- Laue’s replacement medal;
- assay records linking recovered gold to either replacement.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Design origin

Alfred Nobel’s will required the prize institutions to award a gold medal bearing the testator’s image and an appropriate inscription. In 1901 the Swedish institutions commissioned Erik Lindberg, then studying medal engraving in Paris, to design the Swedish medals for physics, chemistry, physiology or medicine, and literature.

The reverses were not approved in time for the first ceremony. Temporary medals were issued in 1901; Lindberg’s final designs were ready in 1902. His physics-and-chemistry design remained the standard type from which the Laue and Franck medals were struck.

### 2.2 Max von Laue medal

The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded the 1914 Nobel Prize in Physics to Max von Laue:

> “for his discovery of the diffraction of X-rays by crystals.”

The decision was announced on 11 November 1915. World War I disrupted the normal award cycle and ceremony. Laue delivered his Nobel lecture in Stockholm on 3 June 1920. The exact day on which the physical medal was placed in his hands is not made explicit in the online Nobel records examined, but its manufacture and delivery must be placed between the Academy’s award decision and his eventual possession of it before its transfer to Copenhagen.

The medal was made for Laue, and his engraved name converted a standard institutional type into an individualized artifact.

### 2.3 James Franck medal

The Academy awarded the 1925 Nobel Prize in Physics jointly to James Franck and Gustav Hertz:

> “for their discovery of the laws governing the impact of an electron upon an atom.”

The prize was reserved in 1925 and awarded in 1926. Franck and Hertz received their prizes in Stockholm on 10 December 1926. Franck’s banquet speech and photographs preserved in his papers attest his presence. His individual gold medal was separately engraved for him.

### 2.4 Material provenance

The original source of the gold—the mine, refiner, supplier, or refining batch—is not published. The Swedish Royal Mint’s production was documented institutionally, but no public lot-specific records were found for either medal.

The recovered gold’s later refining history is likewise incomplete. A receipt establishes delivery to the Academy, but the available documentation does not prove segregation through refining and reminting.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 Laue medal: award to Copenhagen

After award and delivery, Laue personally possessed his medal. By 1940 it was at Niels Bohr’s institute in Copenhagen.

Hevesy wrote that Laue had sent it there “for safe-keeping.” A 1947 Laue letter confirms the underlying custody relationship: Laue wrote that he had committed to Bohr’s charge not only the Nobel medal but also his gold Planck Medal, Ladenburg Medal, small Italian Matteucci Medal, and Nobel diploma.

The exact date and carrier of the Nobel medal’s transfer are undocumented in the public sources. Schwarz suggests that a visitor from Copenhagen may have carried it out of Germany after the Ossietzky controversy, but labels this conjectural. It should not be repeated as established smuggling history.

### 3.2 Franck medal: 1926–1934

Franck received his medal in Stockholm on 10 December 1926 and retained it through his Göttingen years.

The University of Chicago finding aid says that in 1934, while a guest at Bohr’s institute, Franck placed it in an office safe there and apparently forgot to reclaim it when he returned to the United States. This is the most specific institutional account of its arrival. “Forgot” is the archivist’s inference, not a statement established by a contemporary note.

Franck had resigned his Göttingen post in 1933 in protest against the Nazi state’s treatment of German Jews. By 1940 he lived in the United States and was no longer situated in Germany. This matters because later retellings sometimes imply that both men personally faced the same immediate legal exposure. They did not.

### 3.3 Custody at Bohr’s institute

The medals were kept in the Institute for Theoretical Physics, Blegdamsvej, Copenhagen—now the Niels Bohr Institute.

Sources disagree on whether they were stored together in “the safe,” “an office safe,” or more generally at the institute. Hevesy specifically described Bohr as worrying about Laue’s medal on the morning of the occupation. He then stated that he dissolved Laue’s “and also James Franck’s” medal.

The finding aid for the Franck Papers contains a significant error: it says Franck’s medal was converted to powder “along with Bohr’s Nobel medal.” Bohr’s own medal was not present. Archival records show that Bohr and August Krogh had donated their medals to a Finnish-relief auction held on 12 March 1940; an anonymous buyer subsequently donated them to the Museum of National History at Frederiksborg Castle. Hevesy’s account and the Niels Bohr Archive identify the dissolved pair as Laue and Franck.

### 3.4 9 April 1940: dissolution

Germany invaded Denmark on 9 April 1940. Hevesy later wrote that, upon arriving at the laboratory, he found Bohr concerned about Laue’s medal. He proposed burial, but Bohr objected that it might be unearthed. Hevesy therefore decided to dissolve it.

His account continues:

> “While the invading forces marched in the streets of Copenhagen, I was busy dissolving Laue’s and also James Franck’s medals.”

This is the core first-person testimony. It was published eighteen years after the event, not written contemporaneously. Nonetheless it is reinforced by subsequent correspondence and the documented transfer of recovered gold.

The exact participation of Bohr is uncertain. He was the custodian and objected to burial; Hevesy claimed the decision and chemical work. Statements that “Bohr dissolved the medals” compress these different roles.

### 3.5 1940–1943: concealed chemical material

After dissolution, the gold-bearing material remained at the institute. Popular accounts say it stood openly on a laboratory shelf. Hevesy’s published account does not specify the shelf or vessel.

The medals’ solid bodies no longer existed. Custody had shifted from two identifiable medallions to unmarked chemical material in an occupied scientific institute.

### 3.6 December 1943–February 1944: German occupation of the institute

A major chronological correction is necessary. German forces did not conduct the decisive search of Bohr’s institute on 9 April 1940. The institute’s German occupation began on 6 December 1943, after the collapse of Danish governmental cooperation and Bohr’s escape.

Hevesy fled Denmark for Sweden in October 1943. He later wrote that the German occupiers searched the institute, particularly the vault where the medals had been stored, without discovering the gold.

German investigators were primarily interested in nuclear-physics research and confiscated the cyclotron logbook. The dissolved metal escaped detection.

### 3.7 1945 and after: recovery

The Royal Danish Academy’s modern retelling says Hevesy returned in 1945 and found the bottle where he had left it. That account depends on later tradition and does not cite a contemporary recovery report.

Schwarz found no record of the precise recovery process. He reports a source stating that the work was done by “Bohr’s third son.” Niels and Margrethe Bohr’s third son was Ernest Bohr, but the identification and exact laboratory role require caution because the underlying source was not reproduced as a formal technical report.

A standard recovery procedure would have involved evaporation or chemical precipitation of gold, followed by heating and refining. Schwarz notes that cupellation with lead could have removed impurities. These are reconstructions of probable technique, not documented steps in this specific operation.

### 3.8 1947: Laue’s caution

On 21 January 1947 Laue wrote from Zurich to Bohr. Otto Stern had told him of Bohr’s efforts concerning the Nobel medal. Laue thanked Bohr but urgently asked that neither the medal be sent to Germany nor related correspondence be directed to his German address.

The reason had changed. After Germany’s defeat, German property abroad was subject to Allied and Danish controls. The material that had been concealed from Nazi-era restrictions now risked classification under postwar confiscation measures.

Laue’s letter also sought restoration or replacement of his other medals and Nobel diploma. Thus the dissolution episode may have affected more than the two Nobel medals. Archival notes mention “two plates totaling 200 g, and several pieces totaling 249 g,” plus Laue’s 54-gram Italian medal. The Planck Medal proved to be gold-plated silver with some palladium. It is unresolved which non-Nobel medals entered the acid and what survived.

### 3.9 January 1950: transfer of recovered gold

The strongest custody document after dissolution is an Academy receipt.

By 20 January 1950 a package addressed to Arne Westgren, permanent secretary of the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, was collected from the Danish Embassy in Stockholm. Academy caretaker J. Eklund signed a receipt describing it as allegedly containing gold from the Nobel medals of Max von Laue and Niels Bohr.

That description was wrong. On 24 January 1950 Bohr wrote to Westgren correcting “Niels Bohr” to James Franck. Copies survive in the Niels Bohr Archive.

The route was therefore:

- Bohr’s institute or Danish custodians;
- Danish diplomatic custody in Stockholm;
- Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences.

The amount of gold, its assay, and packaging are not stated in the published account.

### 3.10 Manufacture and presentation of replacements

Schwarz states that replacement medals were supplied to:

- von Laue in connection with the Nobel Foundation’s fiftieth anniversary in 1950;
- Franck through the Swedish consular presence in Chicago.

The Nobel Foundation proceedings of 28 February 1952 record that Franck received his recoined medal at the University of Chicago on 31 January 1952.

The University of Chicago finding aid calls the medal “Recast 1951” and describes “the presentation of the recast medal in 1951.” This conflicts with the Foundation minutes’ exact ceremony date. The most likely reconciliation is manufacture or documentary dating in 1951, followed by formal presentation on 31 January 1952.

The finding aid preserves a recollection communicated orally by Victor Weisskopf in 1974. Franck supposedly said at the presentation:

> “I am so delighted about this, as I know now that half of my medal is Niels Bohr’s.”

That remark is historically problematic. Bohr’s medal was not dissolved. It is best treated as a late oral recollection, possibly a confusion of Bohr’s custodial role with ownership of the gold.

### 3.11 Later custody

Franck’s replacement medal passed with his papers and memorabilia into the University of Chicago Library’s Special Collections Research Center. The finding aid inventories it in Box 22.

Laue retained or received his replacement after 1950. No continuous post-delivery custody chain has been found. A recent scholarly article describes it as lost without trace.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Were the medals real?

Yes. Their existence is supported by:

- the Nobel Foundation’s award records;
- the standard production and presentation of individual medals;
- Franck’s 1926 ceremony photographs and archival medal entry;
- Laue’s and Hevesy’s correspondence;
- Bohr’s 1950 correction concerning the recovered gold;
- the Academy’s receipt;
- and the surviving Franck replacement.

There is no serious scholarly claim that the original medals were fictitious.

### 4.2 Are the replacements the same objects?

Under material-culture criteria, no—not as fabricated objects. Dissolution destroyed the originals’ form, surfaces, engravings, and individual histories as medals. The replacements are institutionally authorized successors or replicas.

A philosophical claim that the “same gold” preserves identity is not fully supportable because:

- the gold may have been mixed;
- refining removes and redistributes material;
- additional gold may have been required;
- no published chain-of-batch record establishes segregation;
- and Schwarz suggests the recovered gold may have been sold or reprocessed while replacements were struck to current standards.

Therefore:

- **award identity:** continuous;
- **institutional authenticity:** strong;
- **physical artifact identity:** broken;
- **atom-for-atom continuity:** unproved.

### 4.3 Was the recovered gold used in the replacements?

Frequently asserted, not demonstrated by an accessible assay or mint ledger.

Hevesy wrote that “the gold was recovered” and that the Nobel Foundation presented new medals. Those clauses do not explicitly say the new medals were made from precisely that gold. Later institutional and popular sources often merge them into a single claim.

Schwarz’s cautious conclusion is that the replacements were probably made to current specifications and that the Copenhagen gold may have been sold or reprocessed. This remains unresolved unless Royal Swedish Academy or mint production records establish otherwise.

### 4.4 Were Bohr’s medals dissolved?

No. This is a documented false variant. Bohr’s and August Krogh’s Nobel medals had been sold at the Finnish-relief auction on 12 March 1940 and transferred to Frederiksborg. The University of Chicago finding aid’s statement that Franck’s medal was dissolved with Bohr’s is inconsistent with the Niels Bohr Archive, Hevesy’s first-person account, and the Academy correspondence.

### 4.5 Was the act legally necessary?

The risk was real but has often been overstated.

Documented context:

- Nazi Germany tightly controlled the export of gold and foreign currency;
- contemporary German guidance said gold and precious metals required authorization for foreign transfer;
- serious exchange offenses could bring imprisonment and, after the 1936 law against economic sabotage, potentially capital punishment in aggravated cases;
- Hitler’s 1937 decree forbade German nationals from accepting future Nobel Prizes after the award to Carl von Ossietzky.

However:

- Laue’s medal predated the Nobel ban;
- Franck was in the United States and no longer in the same legal position as Laue;
- no German confiscation order specifically directed at these medals has been found;
- occupied Denmark initially retained civil administration;
- the institute was not occupied and searched until December 1943;
- and non-destructive hiding or diplomatic deposit was possible.

Hevesy’s phrase “almost a capital offence” should be understood as his retrospective characterization, not a quotation from a statute specifically governing Nobel medals.

### 4.6 “Converted to powder”

The University of Chicago finding aid says Hevesy “converted” the medals “to powder.” This may conflate:

- preliminary milling into filings;
- postwar precipitation of metallic gold powder;
- or the dissolution process generally.

Hevesy said he dissolved the medals. No direct evidence shows that powder, rather than solution, was the principal wartime storage state.

### 4.7 The vessel

No authenticated bottle, flask, or beaker has been located or catalogued. Claims that “the beaker still exists” are unsupported by the sources examined. Images used in popular accounts are usually illustrative laboratory glassware.

### 4.8 Franck medal authentication

The University of Chicago’s custody and finding aid give the Franck replacement a secure institutional provenance. It is not a private-market claimant or unauthorized duplicate. No scientific examination has been published, but there is no identified dispute over its status as the replacement officially issued to Franck.

### 4.9 Laue medal status

The Laue replacement’s issuance is documented. Its present identity cannot be physically tested because its current location is unknown. No rival object has been identified as a claimant.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Original function

The medals were instruments of Nobel ceremonial recognition. They materially embodied decisions of the Royal Swedish Academy and individualized those decisions through engraved names.

Franck’s original medal was presented in the Nobel festivities of 10 December 1926. Laue’s award was delayed by wartime conditions; he gave his Nobel lecture on 3 June 1920.

They possessed no formal magical or religious function. Their “sacred” character is modern and civic: they signify international scientific authority, discovery, scholarly achievement, and the institutional memory of Alfred Nobel.

### 6.2 Concealment function

After transfer to Copenhagen, the objects acquired a secondary function as endangered portable wealth and evidence. Their gold made them subject to economic controls; their inscriptions made them traceable to owners.

Dissolution separated these functions:

- the inscriptions and recognizable award form were destroyed;
- the gold value was preserved;
- the symbolic award identity was retained only through documents and institutional recognition.

### 6.3 Replacement ceremony

Franck’s replacement was formally presented at the University of Chicago on 31 January 1952. The ceremony transformed a newly struck object into the authorized successor of the destroyed medal.

Laue’s replacement appears to have been connected with the Nobel Foundation’s fiftieth-anniversary observances in 1950, although the exact ceremony and date need further archival confirmation.

### 6.4 Political symbolism

The objects now symbolize:

- protection of colleagues under dictatorship;
- scientific solidarity across borders;
- chemistry used for concealment rather than destruction for its own sake;
- and the persistence of institutional honor despite material destruction.

These meanings are later historical receptions. There is no evidence that Hevesy staged the act as a public political performance in 1940; secrecy was essential to its purpose.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 “Hidden in plain sight on a shelf”

**Status:** plausible later elaboration.

Many accounts say the bottle stood openly on a laboratory shelf for the duration of the war. Hevesy’s published first-person account says the medals were dissolved and survived the occupation but does not describe an ordinary shelf, dust, or an intentionally conspicuous position.

### 7.2 “The Nazis immediately searched the institute”

**Status:** false compression.

The occupation of Denmark began on 9 April 1940. The German occupation and detailed search of Bohr’s institute occurred in December 1943. Combining the invasion and search into one scene heightens drama but distorts chronology.

### 7.3 “Hevesy dissolved Bohr’s medal”

**Status:** archival and popular error.

Bohr’s medal had already been auctioned for Finnish relief. The dissolved medals belonged to Laue and Franck.

### 7.4 “The Nazis would have executed the owners”

**Status:** unverified extrapolation.

Laue faced potential danger from identification of unauthorized German gold abroad. Exchange offenses could be severe, and Nazi punishment was arbitrary. But no source proves that discovery would automatically have resulted in execution. Hevesy wrote “almost a capital offence” and later told Laue that he would probably have gone to prison.

### 7.5 “Both owners smuggled their medals to Denmark”

**Status:** partly unsupported.

Franck personally left his medal in Copenhagen in 1934. Laue entrusted his to Bohr, probably through an intermediary. No identified customs record or contemporaneous testimony establishes a joint smuggling operation.

### 7.6 “The medals were remade from their own gold”

**Status:** institutional tradition, materially unproved.

Recovered gold was delivered to the Academy, and replacement medals were issued. The missing link is evidence that the mint segregated and used that particular gold. Popular retellings treat this as certain; the surviving published documentation does not.

### 7.7 “Each laureate got his own gold back”

**Status:** chemically and documentarily unproved.

If the medals were dissolved or refined together, individual atoms could not practically be reassigned. Franck’s alleged remark about having “half” of Bohr’s medal demonstrates how quickly a story of mixed substance emerged, although it mistakenly names Bohr.

### 7.8 “The bottle survived as a relic”

**Status:** unverified modern claim.

No accession number, laboratory label, photograph with secure provenance, or museum catalogue for such a vessel has been found.

### 7.9 “The entire war passed with the solution untouched”

**Status:** broadly plausible but imprecise.

The material remained undiscovered through the institute’s 1943–44 occupation. Its exact custodial condition, whether moved, inspected, relabeled, divided, or chemically altered over time, is undocumented.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Original medals

**Status:** destroyed as medals on 9 April 1940.

No solid fragment securely catalogued as part of either original Nobel medal is known. Their design survives generically in the Nobel dies, other medals, photographs of the standard type, and the replacement medal.

### 8.2 Recovered original gold

**Status:** fate after delivery to Stockholm unresolved.

The gold was received by the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences in January 1950. It may have been refined into the replacements, mixed into mint stock, sold, or otherwise reprocessed. No separately identified residue or bullion sample is known.

### 8.3 Franck replacement

**Location:** Special Collections Research Center, University of Chicago Library, Chicago, Illinois, United States.

**Catalogue:** James Franck Papers, Box 22; “Nobel Prize medal, 1925. Recast 1951. Gold. 6 and ½ cm. in diameter.”

The finding aid does not state that it is permanently on public display. Access is therefore archival and ordinarily subject to the library’s reading-room, security, and handling rules. Its legal ownership appears to be institutional, derived from transfer of Franck’s papers and memorabilia.

### 8.4 Laue replacement

**Location:** unknown.

A recent scholarly source says the replacement has been lost without trace. The Archive of the Max Planck Society and the Deutsches Museum hold important Laue materials, but no catalogue located for this dossier lists the replacement Nobel medal as part of their holdings.

### 8.5 Vessel and solution

**Location:** unknown or destroyed.

No authenticated vessel is presently catalogued. No surviving solution is known.

### 8.6 Disputes

There is no current restitution or repatriation dispute identified for the Franck replacement. The major live issue is historical and material: whether it contains recovered original gold.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Scientific folklore

The story has become a standard chemistry anecdote. It appears in:

- chemistry teaching;
- accounts of aqua regia;
- histories of nuclear medicine and radiochemistry;
- Nobel institutional publicity;
- biographies of Bohr and Hevesy;
- histories of scientific resistance under National Socialism;
- numismatic writing.

Its popularity rests on a compact transformation narrative: gold becomes invisible, survives occupation, and becomes a medal again.

### 9.2 Institutional reception

The Nobel Foundation and Niels Bohr Archive have used the episode to explain both Nobel-medal design and scientific solidarity. The Royal Danish Academy published a Danish retelling under the idea of Nobel Prizes “poured into bottles.”

The Max Planck Society presents the story as an especially secure wartime hiding place for two medals entrusted to the Copenhagen institute.

### 9.3 Visual culture

Modern illustrations usually show:

- a gold Nobel medal;
- Hevesy or Bohr;
- a beaker or laboratory bottle containing yellow liquid;
- German soldiers or Nazi imagery.

Unless tied to archival photography, these are reconstructions. No photograph of the actual dissolution has been identified.

### 9.4 Replica and replacement culture

Franck’s extant medal is itself an official replacement that acquired an independent biography. It is not a commercial replica: it was authorized by the prize institutions and presented ceremonially to the laureate.

Laue’s replacement similarly carried official identity, although it is now lost.

### 9.5 Distortion through repetition

Recurring modern errors include:

- “melted” rather than dissolved;
- “Bohr’s and Franck’s medals” rather than Laue’s and Franck’s;
- immediate Nazi search in April 1940;
- certainty that the same gold was reminted;
- certainty that one intact bottle remained untouched;
- automatic death penalty;
- and presentation of the replacement medals in 1952 without distinguishing Laue’s earlier replacement history.

These errors do not invalidate the central event; they show how a documented act accumulated narrative compression.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Hevesy’s narrative

The foundational published narrative is Hevesy’s autobiographical “A Scientific Career,” published in *Perspectives in Biology and Medicine* in 1958 and reprinted in *Adventures in Radioisotope Research* in 1962.

Strengths:

- written by the person who performed the chemical operation;
- identifies both medals;
- supplies the burial discussion and motive;
- states that gold was recovered and new medals presented.

Limitations:

- written eighteen years later;
- shaped as autobiography;
- offers no laboratory details;
- telescopes the wartime history;
- characterizes legal risk rather than citing legislation.

### 10.2 Correspondence

Laue’s 21 January 1947 letter to Bohr is independent evidence that Bohr held the Nobel medal and other Laue awards. It also records postwar sensitivity about returning German property.

Hevesy’s 6 January 1957 letter to Laue closely repeats the later published story and adds that German personnel searched the vault and that Laue probably would have been imprisoned.

Bohr’s 24 January 1950 letter to Westgren corrects the Academy’s receipt and conclusively identifies the second medal as Franck’s rather than Bohr’s.

### 10.3 Institutional records

The Academy receipt documents the transfer of recovered metal. The Nobel Foundation proceedings document Franck’s replacement ceremony on 31 January 1952.

The University of Chicago finding aid supplies vital object-level evidence but also demonstrates the hazards of secondary archival description. Its statement that Bohr’s medal was dissolved is wrong, while its catalogue of the extant Franck replacement is authoritative for present custody.

### 10.4 Stephan Schwarz’s revisionist analysis

Schwarz’s study is the most detailed published critical reconstruction located. It contributes:

- the 1947 and 1957 correspondence;
- the Academy receipt and Bohr correction;
- the distinction between the April 1940 invasion and December 1943 institute occupation;
- the uncertain recovery process;
- the possibility of preliminary milling;
- doubts about whether the recovered gold entered the replacements;
- and the postwar legal complications governing German property.

Schwarz regards Hevesy’s decision as possibly panicked and points out less destructive alternatives. That judgment is interpretation, not documentary fact. His factual corrections are nevertheless important.

### 10.5 Chemical reconstruction

No contemporary laboratory notebook has been identified. Later writers infer conventional aqua-regia chemistry:

1. preparation of hydrochloric and nitric acids;
2. possible mechanical reduction of the medals;
3. oxidative dissolution of gold;
4. wartime storage as chloroaurate solution;
5. postwar precipitation or evaporation;
6. heating and refining;
7. minting of replacements.

Only steps 3, 4 in general, 5 in general, and 7 are historically attested. The precise reagents, quantities, operators, and refining sequence remain unknown.

### 10.6 Unpublished or incompletely exploited evidence

Potentially important repositories include:

- the Niels Bohr Archive’s medals dossier;
- George Hevesy Scientific Correspondence;
- Bohr General and Private Correspondence;
- Royal Swedish Academy administrative and mint records;
- Nobel Foundation proceedings;
- the Meitner Collection at Churchill Archives Centre;
- the James Franck Papers;
- Laue’s papers in German repositories.

A complete object biography would benefit from publication of:

- the full 1950 package receipt;
- Bohr’s correction;
- any accompanying assay;
- mint orders and invoices;
- replacement-medal engraving records;
- correspondence arranging both ceremonies;
- and any inventory of Laue’s estate mentioning the replacement.

### 10.7 State of the question

Secure:

- two genuine Nobel medals existed;
- both were at Bohr’s institute;
- Hevesy dissolved them on 9 April 1940;
- the dissolved gold escaped German discovery;
- gold was recovered and delivered to the Academy;
- official replacements were issued;
- Franck’s replacement survives at Chicago.

Probable:

- the original medals were standard 23-carat, approximately 200-gram, 66-millimetre Swedish Nobel medals;
- recovered gold underwent conventional refining;
- the original metals were mixed at some stage.

Possible but unproved:

- pre-dissolution milling;
- storage in a single bottle on an open shelf;
- recovery by Ernest Bohr;
- incorporation of the same recovered gold in the replacements.

False or contradicted:

- dissolution of Niels Bohr’s medal;
- an immediate April 1940 German search of the institute.

Unresolved:

- exact arrival date of Laue’s medal in Copenhagen;
- exact vessel, volume, and chemical procedure;
- custody between recovery and diplomatic delivery;
- assay and mint batch;
- present location of Laue’s replacement;
- survival of any associated residue or glassware.

| **30 January 1937** | Hitler announces a ban on Germans accepting future Nobel Prizes after the Ossietzky award. | Documented context; not a confiscation order for existing medals. |
| **12 March 1940** | Bohr’s and August Krogh’s medals are auctioned for Finnish relief, proving Bohr’s medal was not among those later dissolved. | Documented in Niels Bohr Archive. |
| **9 April 1940** | Germany invades Denmark. Hevesy dissolves Laue’s and Franck’s medals in aqua regia after Bohr rejects burial. | First-person retrospective account, corroborated by later records. |
| **1940–1943** | Gold-bearing material remains at the institute. | General fact documented; exact vessel and location uncertain. |
| **October 1943** | Hevesy escapes to Sweden. | Documented biographical context. |
| **6 December 1943–3 February 1944** | German personnel occupy and search the institute; gold is not found. | Documented; corrects later April 1940 compression. |
| **1945 or shortly afterward** | Gold-bearing material is recovered after liberation. | Recovery documented in general; exact date and method uncertain. |
| **21 January 1947** | Laue asks Bohr not to send the medal or write about it to his German address; discusses restoration of other medals and diploma. | Surviving letter. |
| **By 20 January 1950** | Package of recovered gold reaches the Danish Embassy in Stockholm and is collected by the Royal Swedish Academy. | Academy receipt. |
| **24 January 1950** | Bohr corrects receipt: gold relates to Laue’s and Franck’s medals, not Laue’s and Bohr’s. | Surviving letter copy. |

| **1951** | Franck replacement is struck or catalogued as “Recast 1951.” | University of Chicago catalogue. |
| **31 January 1952** | Franck receives replacement at a University of Chicago ceremony. | Nobel Foundation proceedings of 28 February 1952. |
| **1958** | Hevesy publishes “A Scientific Career,” the principal first-person narrative. | Documented publication. |
| **1962** | Story reprinted in *Adventures in Radioisotope Research*, vol. 1, p. 27. | Documented publication. |

## 12. Sources

### Primary and near-primary sources

- George de Hevesy, “A Scientific Career,” *Perspectives in Biology and Medicine* 1:4 (1958), later reprinted in *Adventures in Radioisotope Research*, vol. 1 (Pergamon, 1962), p. 27. The quoted passage is reproduced by the Niels Bohr Archive.
- Max von Laue to Niels Bohr, 21 January 1947, quoted and translated by Stephan Schwarz from Niels Bohr Archive material.
- George de Hevesy to Max von Laue, 6 January 1957, copy in the Meitner Collection, Churchill Archives Centre, reproduced by Schwarz.
- Royal Swedish Academy receipt for recovered gold, January 1950, described from copies in the Niels Bohr Archive.
- Niels Bohr to Arne Westgren, 24 January 1950, correcting the medal attribution.
- Nobel Foundation proceedings, 28 February 1952, recording Franck’s presentation on 31 January.
- Nobel Foundation official award pages for Laue and Franck.
- James Franck Papers, University of Chicago Library, especially Box 22 and the collection’s historical note.

### Archival and institutional sources

- Niels Bohr Archive, George Hevesy Scientific Correspondence and collections overview.
- University of Chicago Library, Guide to the James Franck Papers.
- Nobel Foundation, medal design, manufacture, and wartime history.
- Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, laureate record for Max von Laue.
- Royal Danish Academy of Sciences and Letters, Danish account of the medals.
- Max Planck Society, institutional history and reception.
- German Federal Archives, records concerning the Ossietzky Nobel Prize controversy.
- Deutsches Historisches Museum, chronology of the 1937 Nobel ban.

### Critical scholarship

- Stephan Schwarz, “The Case of the Bottled Nobel Medals,” *Gamma* 150. The most detailed source-critical study located.
- Mariusz Jaskólski, “Mit ‘oryginału’ / The Myth of the ‘Original,’” *Nauka* 1 (2023), which notes that Laue’s replacement medal is now untraced.
- Hilde Levi, *George de Hevesy: Life and Work* (Copenhagen: Rhodos, 1985), cited by Schwarz.
- Stefan Rozental, *Niels Bohr: Memoirs of a Working Relationship*.
- J. D. Cockcroft, “Georg de Hevesy,” *Biographical Memoirs of Fellows of the Royal Society* 13 (1967), substantially based on Hevesy’s autobiographical material.

### Source assessment

The Niels Bohr Archive materials and Nobel Foundation proceedings establish the essential chain. The University of Chicago finding aid is indispensable for Franck’s medal but unreliable where it substitutes Bohr’s medal for Laue’s or Franck’s partner medal. Modern popular accounts are useful principally as evidence of reception, not as independent proof.
