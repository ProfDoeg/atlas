# The pileus of Libertas: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conclusion

**Identity finding:** no surviving source identifies a unique, continuously transmitted object called “the pileus of Libertas,” comparable to a particular crown, sword, or relic. The phrase is best understood as an **object-type and iconographic attribute**: the *pileus*, a close-fitting ancient felt cap associated with free status and manumission, when carried by or placed beside the Roman personification Libertas.

Accordingly, this dossier tracks four related but non-identical things:

1. ordinary Greek and Roman *piloi/pilei*, most of which were perishable clothing;
2. the cap as an attribute of Libertas in Roman art and coinage;
3. particular surviving representations, especially the EID MAR coins of Marcus Junius Brutus;
4. the post-antique “liberty cap,” often reshaped into or confused with the forward-drooping Phrygian cap.

There is **no demonstrable chain of custody for an original divine cap**, no ancient testimony that a particular cap was kept in a temple as Libertas’s regalia, and no museum accession that can credibly be identified as “the” pileus of the goddess. Its reconstructable life is therefore the transmission of a design, ritual association, and political name through many independently manufactured objects.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Classification

- **Ancient material class:** brimless cap, normally interpreted as felted wool.
- **Ritual and social class:** sign of free or newly conferred status.
- **Divine and allegorical class:** attribute of Libertas, the Roman personification or divine power of liberty.
- **Political class:** republican and anti-tyrannical emblem.
- **Numismatic class:** a repeated device on struck coins rather than a removable component of them.
- **Post-antique class:** liberty cap, bonnet rouge, or bonnet phrygien; these later forms should not automatically be treated as physically identical to the Roman pileus.

### Names

- Latin: *pīleus* or *pilleus*; plural *pilei*. The spelling varies in ancient and later texts.
- Greek: πῖλος, *pîlos*, a felt cap or felt object.
- Latin descriptive expressions: *pileus libertatis*, “cap of liberty,” is principally a descriptive scholarly and post-antique formulation; *ad pileum vocare*, literally “to call [slaves] to the pileus,” meant to summon them with a promise of freedom.
- English: pileus, liberty cap, cap of liberty.
- French: *bonnet de la Liberté*, *bonnet rouge*, and, increasingly, *bonnet phrygien*.
- Italian: *pileo*, *berretto della libertà*.
- German: *Freiheitsmütze*; later *phrygische Mütze*.

The root is connected with Greek πῖλος, meaning felt and then a felt cap. A standard antiquarian description defines it as “any piece of felt,” especially a skullcap of felt. Ancient terminology did not establish the rigid modern taxonomy by which every rounded cap is a pileus and every forward-drooping cap is “Phrygian.” Those categories overlap in post-antique reception but refer to distinguishable ancient iconographic traditions. [Smith, “Pileus”](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/secondary/SMIGRA%2A/Pileus.html)

### Reconstructed ancient appearance

The Roman liberty pileus was normally represented as:

- soft and brimless;
- close-fitting or slightly conical;
- rounded, pointed, or compressed depending on artist and medium;
- probably made from felted wool when used as actual clothing;
- usually undecorated in its liberty symbolism;
- not necessarily red;
- generally lacking the long forward-falling point characteristic of the conventional modern Phrygian cap.

The surviving evidence does not provide a canonical color. Literary references to a white cap or white wool are sometimes invoked. Livy describes emancipated volunteer soldiers dining *pileati aut lana alba velatis capitibus*—wearing pilei or with their heads wrapped in white wool—after being promised freedom. That passage supports the connection between white head-covering and emancipated status, not the existence of a unique white cap owned by Libertas. [Latin text of Livy](https://www.e-rara.ch/download/pdf/28680647.pdf)

### How Libertas uses it

In Roman imperial coin iconography Libertas commonly **holds the pileus in one hand**. She does not consistently wear it. Her other attributes include:

- a long rod or staff, often interpreted as the *vindicta* or *festuca* associated with formal manumission;
- a sceptre;
- a cornucopia;
- occasionally other attributes reflecting the issue’s political message.

A British Museum aureus of Vitellius, minted in Spain or perhaps Tarraco in AD 69, shows Libertas holding a pileus and long sceptre. It is gold, weighs 7.340 g, and is registered as R.6591. [British Museum R.6591](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_R-6591)

A denarius of Antoninus Pius, minted at Rome in AD 139 and found in the Pyrford hoard, shows Libertas holding the pileus in her right hand and a vertical rod in her left. It is silver, weighs 3.05 g, and is registered as 1957,0207.5. [British Museum 1957,0207.5](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_1957-0207-5)

A radiate of Quintillus, minted at Siscia in AD 270, substitutes a cornucopia for the rod or sceptre. The British Museum specimen is copper alloy, 21 mm in diameter, weighs 3.53 g, and bears registration number 1929,0716.3. [British Museum 1929,0716.3](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_1929-0716-3)

### Inscriptions

The cap itself normally bears no inscription. Meaning is supplied by adjoining legends:

- **LIBERTAS** — “Liberty.”
- **LIBERTAS AVGVSTA** — “Augustan/imperial liberty.”
- **LIBERTAS PVBLICA** — “Public liberty.”
- **LIBERTAS P[OPVLI] R[OMANI] RESTITVTA** — “The liberty of the Roman people restored.”
- **EID MAR** — *Eidibus Martiis*, “on the Ides of March,” beneath a pileus between two daggers on Brutus’s coinage.

### What survives

What survives is a distributed corpus:

- ancient coins in gold, silver, and copper alloys;
- engraved gems, reliefs, and other representations;
- textual descriptions;
- post-antique medals, prints, seals, flags, sculptures, clothing, coins, and civic emblems;
- a few genuinely surviving revolutionary caps, which are not Roman objects.

No ancient cap has a documented provenance identifying it as a cap used in a specific manumission, carried after Caesar’s assassination, or belonging to a temple image of Libertas.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### The physical cap-type

Felt caps long predate Roman Libertas. Greek πῖλος designated both felt and objects made from it. Caps of broadly similar construction appear in Greek, Italic, Anatolian, and eastern iconography. The *pilos* also belonged to the visual identity of the Dioscuri, craftsmen, travelers, and other figures. Consequently, the cap’s material origin cannot be assigned to a single commission or workshop.

A felt pileus would have been produced by matting and compressing animal fibers—normally wool—rather than weaving them into ordinary cloth. It could then be shaped, trimmed, and possibly sewn or blocked. No ancient source gives specifications for a “Libertas model.”

### Earliest textual evidence for the liberty association

The earliest evidence is not a story of a goddess acquiring a cap but Roman language and practice associating the pileus with release from slavery.

Livy, writing in the Augustan age about events of 214 BC, uses *servi ad pileum vocati* for slaves summoned with the prospect of freedom. In his account of Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus’s volunteer slave troops at Beneventum, freed soldiers feast wearing pilei or white wool. Livy further says that Gracchus commissioned a painting of the event for the Temple of Libertas on the Aventine. This is exceptionally important: it connects emancipated people, pileate heads, commemoration in an actual Temple of Libertas, and an image said to have been commissioned after the event. The painting does not survive, and Livy’s account was written roughly two centuries later. [Latin text of Livy](https://www.e-rara.ch/download/pdf/28680647.pdf)

Plautus, Persius, Diodorus, and later lexicographical and antiquarian sources also associate pileus-wearing with free or freed status. Persius’s Fifth Satire links formal manumission language with liberty while satirizing the difference between legal and moral freedom. These texts establish a semantic field, not a uniform ceremony conducted identically in every century. [Persius, Satire V](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/pdf/eph340b2289846.pdf)

### Earliest visual evidence attached to Libertas

A traditional numismatic chronology places an early personification of Libertas holding a pileus on a denarius of M. Porcius Laeca in 119 BC. The female figure drives a quadriga and is crowned by Victory. Interpretations vary because Roman Republican coin figures may be identified through familial, political, and accompanying symbols rather than explicit labels. The Italian *Enciclopedia dell’Arte Antica* treats this as the earliest monetary representation of Libertas and notes that the name itself first appears on later Republican issues. [Treccani, “Libertas”](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/libertas_%28Enciclopedia-dell%27-Arte-Antica%29/)

By 54 BC Marcus Junius Brutus issued a denarius with the head of Libertas and an ancestral scene of L. Junius Brutus accompanied by lictors. The British Museum’s Eriswell-hoard specimen is silver, weighs 3.66 g, and is registered as 1973,0822.281. The type invokes the Brutus family’s asserted connection to the expulsion of Rome’s kings. [British Museum 1973,0822.281](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_1973-0822-281)

The decisive autonomous image of the cap came in 43–42 BC: a pileus centered between two daggers above **EID MAR** on coinage issued for Marcus Junius Brutus by L. Plaetorius Cestianus. This was not the goddess’s physical cap pressed into service; it was a deliberately minted sign equating Caesar’s assassination with liberation.

### Manufacture of the EID MAR representations

The EID MAR issues were die-struck in silver and, very rarely, gold. Their mint traveled with Brutus’s forces in the eastern Mediterranean, conventionally placed in Macedonia or northern Greece shortly before the battles of Philippi in 42 BC. The cap, daggers, letters, and portrait were engraved in dies; each coin is a separately struck physical carrier of the emblem.

The British Museum summarizes the issue as Brutus’s celebration of Caesar’s assassination: the cap between daggers and the inscription identify the Ides of March as an act presented by its perpetrators as liberation. [British Museum introduction](https://www.britishmuseum.org/exhibitions/nero-man-behind-myth/introduction-to-ancient-rome)

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Why no singular custody chain exists

No source says that Libertas possessed a unique divine pileus that passed from keeper to keeper. Nor is there a documented repository containing such an object. The defensible chain is therefore one of **ritual use, representation, copying, collection, and reinterpretation**.

### Object-family chain

#### Before the late Republic

Felt caps circulated as ordinary and specialized headgear around the Mediterranean. The association with free status developed within Roman social practice or language by at least the period represented in Livy’s account of 214 BC. No individual cap can be followed.

#### 214 BC, event as narrated by Livy

Enslaved volunteer soldiers serving under Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus were promised freedom. After victory, Livy depicts them publicly dining with pilei or white wool on their heads. Gracchus allegedly had the scene painted in the Aventine Temple of Libertas. The painting’s later custody and destruction are unknown. This is a **literary report of a lost commemorative image**, not a surviving artifact.

#### Late second to first centuries BC

Roman moneyers transferred the cap from perishable clothing to reproducible political imagery. Dies and coins multiplied the sign far beyond any ritual cap. No central institution controlled authenticity: recognition rested on established visual convention, the moneyer’s authority, and the mint.

#### 15 March 44 BC

Appian, writing in Greek in the second century AD, says that after Caesar’s murder the conspirators moved through Rome displaying a cap on a spear as a sign of freedom. This is the only major literary statement commonly cited for an actual cap carried in that episode. It was written long after the event and is not corroborated in the same detail by every narrative source. The bearer is not securely identified, and the cap’s origin and fate are unrecorded. [Appian, *Civil Wars*, Book II](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0232%3Abook%3D2)

#### 43–42 BC

Brutus’s moving military mint converted the claimed scene into the EID MAR reverse. The coins passed through army pay, trade, private retention, loss, burial, recovery, antiquities commerce, and museum acquisition. Individual denarii now have different provenances; they are not parts of one regalia object.

#### AD 41–54 onward

Imperial mints appropriated Libertas. A copper-alloy as of Claudius shows her holding the cap under the legend **LIBERTAS AVGVSTA**. The London Museum specimen, FCS87[826]<60>, is 25 mm, 8.51 g, and not currently recorded as on display. [London Museum](https://www.londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/v/object-342017/coin-as/)

Galba and the civil-war mints of AD 68–69 revived explicitly anti-tyrannical imagery. A Spanish silver denarius now in the British Museum shows the bust of Libertas and, on its reverse, a pileus between two daggers—an intentional copy of Brutus’s EID MAR composition. It was later owned by Count John Francis William de Salis and donated to the British Museum in 1860; registration R.10085. [British Museum R.10085](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_R-10085)

From the Flavians through the third century, imperial issues repeatedly represented Libertas holding pileus, sceptre, rod, or cornucopia. Their message could be freedom restored by an emperor, public liberty, imperial beneficence, or release from a predecessor’s government. The formerly anti-autocratic sign thus entered autocratic state coinage.

#### Medieval and Renaissance reception

Direct institutional continuity is not established. Ancient coins, literary texts, reliefs, and antiquarian collections provided multiple channels of rediscovery. Renaissance emblem books and personification manuals reassembled the cap, staff, spear, and female figure. Copying was conceptual rather than a transfer of one object.

#### Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries

European and Atlantic political imagery adapted the pileus as the “cap of liberty.” Artists increasingly gave it the longer, forward-drooping form associated with Phrygian or other eastern figures in classical art. The distinction between the Roman freedman’s pileus and the Phrygian cap became blurred.

The cap-on-pole motif entered British and American revolutionary visual culture. Abel Buell’s 1784 map of the United States includes Liberty holding a pole supporting a liberty cap. The Library of Congress describes it as symbolizing freedom from enslaving authority. [Library of Congress](https://loc.gov/exhibits/mapping-a-new-nation/special-presentation/closerlook.html)

#### French Revolution

The emblem became an item of actual dress, the *bonnet rouge*, as well as a symbol on seals, medals, weapons, ceramics, banners, and liberty trees. It was imposed on Louis XVI during the journée of 20 June 1792. These caps were newly manufactured revolutionary garments, not ancient Roman relics.

A surviving French cap dated about 1790 in the Metropolitan Museum is made of wool, cotton, and linen; accession 2015.568. Its band is embroidered with a cap on a pike and fasces. It was acquired through the Alfred Z. Solomon–Janet A. Sloane Endowment Fund in 2015. This is a documented historical liberty cap, but not the pileus of Libertas. [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/689887)

Another late-eighteenth-century French cap, Landesmuseum inventory FD 96, is made of wool, silk, and ramie thread and measures 23 × 28 cm. Its unusual reused material suggests economical artisanal manufacture; the museum notes that very few revolutionary examples survive. [LEO-BW/Landesmuseum](https://www.leo-bw.de/en/detail/-/Detail/details/DOKUMENT/blm_museumsobjekte/8F7892C1482573783B4D8DA8922DC4CF/Phrygische%2BM%C3%BCtze%2Bals%2BSymbol%2Bder%2BFranz%C3%B6sischen%2BRevolution)

The National Convention’s 1792 seal prescription represented France as an antique-clad woman holding a pike surmounted by the Phrygian or liberty cap. [French National Assembly](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/dyn/12/questions/QANR5L12QE49797)

#### United States in the nineteenth century

The cap remained in federal coinage and allegory. The U.S. Mint identifies liberty caps and poles among the classical devices used on early national coins. [U.S. Mint](https://www.usmint.gov/news/inside-the-mint/evolution-of-liberty-on-coins)

Its emancipation meaning also made it politically contentious. Thomas Crawford’s original design for the U.S. Capitol’s *Statue of Freedom* included a liberty cap. Secretary of War Jefferson Davis objected that the cap signified emancipated slaves and was inappropriate for Americans supposedly “born free.” Crawford replaced it with a crested helmet. The objection did not deny the cap’s meaning; it depended upon that meaning. [U.S. Senate](https://www.senate.gov/about/historic-buildings-spaces/capitol/lady-freedom-among-us.htm)

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Was there ever one “pileus of Libertas”?

No primary evidence establishes one. The singular title is a modern nominalization of a recurring attribute. Treating every depiction as a portrait of one physical cap would confuse iconography with provenance.

### Was the pileus actually conferred in formal manumission?

Ancient evidence firmly connects it with freedom and freed status, but modern summaries often collapse several separate acts into a single standardized ceremony. In *manumissio vindicta*, the rod, declaration, magistrate, and legal procedure are better documented than a mandatory presentation of a cap at every ceremony. The pileus may signify the resulting status or public celebration rather than being a universally required legal instrument. Claims that every freed slave ceremonially received the same prescribed cap should therefore be marked **traditional generalization**, not universally documented procedure.

### Pileus versus Phrygian cap

This is the principal identity dispute.

- The Roman liberty pileus was generally a close cap of felt.
- The Phrygian cap in ancient art was associated with Trojans, Phrygians, Amazons, Mithras, and eastern peoples.
- Early-modern artists combined the Roman liberty meaning with the more visually distinctive forward-falling eastern cap.
- Revolutionary France institutionalized the hybrid as the *bonnet phrygien*.

The modern French state itself calls Marianne’s cap Phrygian and repeats the freed-slave explanation. That is an authoritative account of **current national symbolism**, not proof that the Roman pileus had precisely the modern form. [Élysée](https://www.elysee.fr/la-presidence/marianne)

### EID MAR authenticity disputes

The EID MAR series is both the clearest ancient representation of the autonomous liberty pileus and a major field of forgery and provenance controversy.

The British Museum possesses a gold piece registered G3,RIG.11 that imitates the EID MAR type. The museum catalogues it flatly as a **modern forgery**. It came from the collections of George III and George IV and entered the museum in 1825. It is displayed in Room 1 as a classified forgery rather than an authentic aureus. [British Museum forgery](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_G3-RIG-11)

One authentic or widely accepted EID MAR aureus, pierced in antiquity or later for suspension, was exhibited on long-term loan at the British Museum from 2010 to 2021. Michael Crawford had questioned its authenticity based on earlier evidence, but subsequent scholarship and direct examination produced broader acceptance. It was offered by Numismatica Ars Classica in 2022; its published history notes both the dispute and the British Museum loan. [NAC auction record](https://www.numisbids.com/sale/5689/lot/474)

A third gold example appeared on the market and was auctioned by Roma Numismatics in October 2020 for £3.24 million including fees, reported as about US$4.19 million. Numismatic Guaranty Company authenticated and graded it Mint State, ★, strike 5/5, surface 3/5, “Fine Style.” Authentication of manufacture did not validate its ownership history. [NGC/CoinNews report](https://www.coinnews.net/2020/11/06/ngc-ancients-certified-eid-mar-aureus-of-brutus-sells-for-4-188-million/)

The auction provenance was subsequently exposed as false. Authorities alleged that the coin had been recently and illicitly excavated; dealer Richard Beale admitted falsifying provenance documentation in connection with its sale. The purchaser surrendered it, and it was repatriated to Greece in 2023. Thus:

- **material authenticity:** accepted by the grading service and most reporting;
- **published provenance:** fabricated;
- **legal custody:** sale undone through surrender and repatriation;
- **present institutional identity:** Greek state cultural property, not an artifact with the pre-20th-century pedigree asserted at auction.

This case demonstrates why physical authenticity and lawful provenance must be tested separately.

### Authority to authenticate

Authority has shifted among:

- ancient issuing mints and political authorities;
- antiquarian collectors;
- catalogue compilers and die specialists;
- museums;
- commercial grading firms;
- courts, prosecutors, and cultural-property authorities.

None can authenticate a coin as the literal cap carried in 44 BC. They can authenticate metal, manufacture, dies, typology, find history, and lawful title.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

- **Libertas:** Roman divine personification whose name supplies the cap’s dominant political meaning. The cap is normally held, not necessarily worn.
- **Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus:** consul whose emancipated volunteer troops appear pileate in Livy’s narrative of 214 BC; reportedly commissioned a painting in the Aventine Temple of Libertas.
- **Marcus Junius Brutus:** used Libertas on his 54 BC ancestral coinage and the autonomous pileus on EID MAR coins in 43–42 BC.
- **L. Plaetorius Cestianus:** moneyer named on the EID MAR denarii; operationally connected to the manufacture of the best-known cap image.
- **Julius Caesar:** never a custodian of the object; his assassination supplied the event the EID MAR cap interpreted as liberation.
- **Caesar’s assassins/Liberatores:** Appian attributes to them the public display of a cap on a spear after the assassination.
- **Claudius, Galba, Vitellius, Antoninus Pius, Caracalla, Quintillus, and other emperors:** issuers under whom imperial mints reused Libertas-with-pileus types.
- **Roman mint personnel:** die engravers, moneyers, metal workers, and striking crews who materially reproduced the cap.
- **Freedmen and freedwomen:** the social group whose status made the pileus intelligible as a liberty sign; no single individual can be identified as the archetypal wearer.
- **Abbé Henri Grégoire and the French National Convention:** associated with the 1792 republican seal that placed a liberty cap on a pike.
- **Louis XVI:** wore a revolutionary liberty cap under crowd pressure on 20 June 1792; this was an actual revolutionary garment, not ancient regalia.
- **Marianne:** post-revolutionary French national personification who wears the hybridized Phrygian liberty cap.
- **Thomas Crawford:** included a liberty cap in an early design for the U.S. Capitol figure.
- **Jefferson Davis:** caused the cap to be removed from that design because of its freed-slave meaning.
- **Richard Beale and Roma Numismatics:** associated with the 2020 sale of an authentic EID MAR aureus using fabricated provenance.
- **British Museum, Metropolitan Museum, London Museum, Bibliothèque nationale de France, American Numismatic Society, and other collections:** custodians of particular representations or later caps, never of a universally recognized original.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Manumission

The pileus represented transition from slavery to freedom. It could be worn by emancipated persons, appear in public celebrations, or stand metonymically for freedom in the phrase *ad pileum vocare*. The rod carried by Libertas refers more specifically to the legal declaration and touch associated with *manumissio vindicta*.

### Saturnalia

Pileus-wearing was also associated with Saturnalian suspension or inversion of ordinary hierarchy. This reinforces its connection with temporary freedom and social reversal, although Saturnalian usage and formal manumission were not identical institutions.

### Military recruitment

Roman commanders could “call slaves to the pileus”: offer freedom in exchange for military service. Here the cap functioned as promised status and recruiting rhetoric.

### Anti-tyrannical display

On EID MAR coinage, the cap between daggers asserts:

1. a tyrant was killed;
2. liberty resulted;
3. the killers were liberators;
4. recipients of the coin should accept that interpretation.

This is propaganda by the assassins’ faction, not neutral evidence that Roman society unanimously regarded the killing as liberation.

### Imperial legitimation

Imperial issues transformed the sign. A reigning emperor could claim to grant, restore, or protect *libertas*. The pileus therefore did not retain an exclusively republican or revolutionary meaning. Its use by imperial mints demonstrates the Roman distinction between liberty as a legally and politically structured condition and modern claims of unrestricted individual freedom.

### Cap on a pole

Appian’s post-assassination cap-on-spear narrative and the EID MAR coin provided a potent compositional model. In early-modern art the accompanying implement could be called a spear, pike, staff, rod, or liberty pole. These are not always the same object:

- the *vindicta* belongs to manumission;
- a spear can display the cap;
- a civic liberty pole is a later political monument;
- revolutionary pikes were actual weapons as well as emblem-bearers.

### Access and taboo

No evidence establishes priestly restrictions on who could touch a unique cap of Libertas. Ordinary caps were wearable objects. Coins circulated widely. Modern state versions are regulated through seal, mint, heraldic, or institutional design rather than sacred access rules.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### “The assassins carried the cap through Rome”

**Tier: ancient, late-attested narrative.** Appian reports a cap displayed on a spear after Caesar’s death. The episode is plausible within Roman symbolism but is not contemporary eyewitness testimony. No source identifies the cap’s maker, prior owner, bearer, later keeper, or fate. Later images often turn this reported cap into the exact model on Brutus’s coins, but that identity is inferential.

### “Every freed Roman slave received a pileus”

**Tier: ancient association, later systematization.** The cap undoubtedly signified freedom, but popular accounts often describe a universal, uniform presentation ceremony more precisely than the evidence allows. Roman manumission varied by legal form, place, and period.

### “The Roman liberty cap was the Phrygian cap”

**Tier: early-modern conflation, now institutional tradition.** Ancient images distinguish the close pileus from forward-drooping eastern caps more often than modern summaries admit. Renaissance and revolutionary artists fused them. The fusion is historically consequential and now real as a national symbol, but it is not evidence of exact ancient formal identity.

### “The liberty cap was always red”

**Tier: modern projection.** Red is fundamental to the French *bonnet rouge* and many later revolutionary depictions. Roman literary and numismatic evidence does not establish red as the fixed color of Libertas’s pileus. Livy’s white wool points in another direction for one emancipation context.

### “A genuine EID MAR coin is the actual regalia”

**Tier: category mistake.** The coin is a physical ancient artifact bearing a representation. Its engraved cap is no more the goddess’s detachable possession than the portrait of Brutus is Brutus’s body.

### Secret survival and unique-original claims

No serious archaeological or textual evidence supports the survival of the specific cap allegedly carried in Rome on 15 March 44 BC. Any modern cap marketed as that object would require an extraordinary custody record and scientific evidence; no recognized claimant possesses such a record.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### The putative original

**Status:** unverified as a singular historical object; no known location; no accession number; no documented fragments.

If Appian’s cap-on-spear episode preserves an actual event, that particular cap’s fate is completely unknown after 44 BC.

### Principal physical stand-ins

- **British Museum modern EID MAR aureus forgery:** G3,RIG.11; displayed as a forgery in Room 1 according to the collection record.
- **British Museum civil-war denarius copying EID MAR:** R.10085; not on display.
- **British Museum Vitellius aureus with Libertas holding pileus:** R.6591; not on display.
- **British Museum Antoninus Pius denarius:** 1957,0207.5; not on display.
- **British Museum Quintillus radiate:** 1929,0716.3; not on display.
- **London Museum Claudian as:** FCS87[826]<60>; not listed as on display.
- **Metropolitan Museum revolutionary cap:** 2015.568; not on view.
- **Landesmuseum revolutionary cap:** FD 96; institutional collection status recorded online.
- **Repatriated 2020-sale EID MAR aureus:** surrendered and returned to Greece in 2023; public reporting does not supply a permanent gallery or final Greek accession number.

### Legal and ownership questions

There is no ownership dispute over a singular pileus of Libertas. Disputes concern individual coins and antiquities:

- title under cultural-property law;
- illicit excavation and export;
- fabricated collection histories;
- private versus public ownership;
- museum acquisition ethics;
- authenticity of particular specimens.

## 9. Modern Reception

### France

The hybrid Phrygian liberty cap became one of the most durable signs of the French Republic. It appears on:

- Marianne busts;
- state seals;
- coins and banknotes;
- postage stamps;
- official graphics;
- municipal sculpture;
- revolutionary and republican art.

Its political temperature varied. The National Assembly records that Adolphe Thiers prohibited the revolutionary cap on municipal Marianne imagery in 1871 as seditious; it reappeared after 1879. No single official Marianne bust has ever been legally mandatory. [French National Assembly](https://www2.assemblee-nationale.fr/decouvrir-l-assemblee/patrimoine/la-bibliotheque/les-bustes-de-marianne/presentation)

In 2022 Paris 2024 unveiled the Olympic and Paralympic “Phryges,” anthropomorphic mascots explicitly shaped as small Phrygian caps. This turned the ancient/revolutionary emblem into mass-produced toys, costumes, animations, and commercial merchandise. [Paris 2024](https://press.paris2024.org/news/paris-2024-unveil-its-mascots-ac38-7578a.html)

### United States

The cap and pole appeared in Revolutionary imagery and early federal coinage. The U.S. Mint continues to interpret the device through Roman manumission. Its rejection from the Capitol’s *Statue of Freedom* exposed the contradiction between American liberty imagery and the persistence of slavery.

### Latin America and the Atlantic world

Liberty caps occur in the arms, seals, flags, and coinages of several American republics. These are descendants of the Atlantic revolutionary emblem, usually mediated by French, North American, Spanish liberal, and neoclassical design rather than direct copying of one Roman artifact.

### Art and literature

Among the most influential images is Eugène Delacroix’s *Liberty Leading the People* (1830), in which Liberty wears the red forward-drooping cap. The painting helped naturalize the cap as something Liberty herself wears, unlike many Roman coins where Libertas presents it in her hand.

Revolutionary prints showing Louis XVI in the cap fixed another use: the emblem could be imposed upon a ruler as a public test or humiliation.

### Replicas, forgeries, and commercial copies

Replica EID MAR coins, fantasy pieces, souvenir caps, Marianne statuettes, political costumes, and Olympic merchandise constitute a vast secondary object-family. Replicas become misleading when artificially aged or supplied with false provenance. The British Museum’s royal-collection forgery and the fabricated provenance attached to the 2020 aureus illustrate separate offenses:

- counterfeit manufacture;
- false ownership history attached to a genuine ancient object.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Major evidence classes

1. Literary texts: Livy, Plautus, Persius, Appian, Diodorus, and later commentators.
2. Republican coinage: catalogued through Michael Crawford’s *Roman Republican Coinage* and the online CRRO framework.
3. Imperial coinage: catalogued through *Roman Imperial Coinage*, museum corpora, and RIC Online.
4. Temple history and lost painting traditions.
5. Renaissance emblematic literature.
6. revolutionary decrees, seals, prints, garments, and political journalism;
7. museum accession files and commercial auction catalogues;
8. die studies, metallurgical examination, and law-enforcement provenance research.

CRRO explicitly presents itself as an online implementation of Crawford’s typology, supplemented by museum collections and die-link research. Its records remain typological tools, not proof that every specimen has a lawful modern provenance. [CRRO](https://numismatics.org/crro/)

### Running debates

- Whether the 119 BC coin figure should be identified unequivocally as Libertas.
- How routinely actual freed persons wore pilei.
- Whether the pileus belonged inside the formal legal ritual or marked its result.
- How the *vindicta*, sceptre, spear, and liberty pole became conflated.
- When and why European artists substituted the Phrygian form.
- Authenticity and die relationships among the three reported EID MAR aurei.
- Whether low survival of EID MAR coins reflects official withdrawal, melting, normal attrition, or some combination.
- The legal consequences of unverifiable or fabricated antiquities provenance.

### Discredited or misleading claims

- There was one hereditary cap belonging to the goddess.
- A currently displayed cap has an unbroken custody chain from Brutus.
- All Roman liberty caps were red.
- Pileus and Phrygian cap are formally interchangeable in every ancient context.
- Commercial authentication of a coin establishes legal ownership.
- Museum display by itself proves authenticity.

## 11. Chronology

| Date | Object history |
|---|---|
| Before the Roman Republic | Felt caps of the *pilos/pileus* family exist in Mediterranean material and visual culture; no liberty-specific unique object is documented. |
| 214 BC event; narrated by Livy in the late first century BC | Enslaved volunteer soldiers are called to freedom and later dine pileate or wrapped in white wool; Gracchus reportedly commissions a painting for the Aventine Temple of Libertas. |
| 119 BC, traditional numismatic dating | Denarius of M. Porcius Laeca conventionally identified as an early representation of Libertas holding a pileus. |
| 54 BC | Marcus Junius Brutus issues denarii bearing Libertas and his claimed tyrannicide ancestor L. Junius Brutus. |
| 15 March 44 BC | Caesar is assassinated. Appian later reports that the conspirators displayed a cap on a spear as a sign of liberty. |
| 43–42 BC | Brutus and L. Plaetorius Cestianus issue EID MAR denarii and exceptionally rare aurei: pileus between two daggers. |
| 42 BC | Brutus and Cassius are defeated at Philippi; the cap’s imagery survives on dispersed coinage. |
| AD 41–54 | Claudian bronze coins show **LIBERTAS AVGVSTA** holding a pileus. |
| AD 68–69 | Civil-war mints revive the cap-between-daggers design and “liberty restored” language; Galba and Vitellius issues employ Libertas. |
| AD 112–114 | Trajan’s restoration coinage reissues Brutus’s 54 BC Libertas type; a British Museum specimen was formerly in the Blacas collection. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_1867-0101-1659) |
| AD 139 and later second century | Antoninus Pius and successors employ Libertas holding pileus and rod/sceptre. |
| AD 270 | Quintillus issue from Siscia shows Libertas with pileus and cornucopia. |
| Renaissance–seventeenth century | Antiquarian, emblematic, and allegorical art revives the cap as an attribute of Liberty; eastern and Roman cap forms begin to merge. |
| Eighteenth century | Cap-on-pole imagery becomes prominent in British and American liberty iconography. |
| 1784 | Abel Buell’s U.S. map depicts Liberty with cap and pole. |
| 1789–1790 | Liberty caps appear in French revolutionary imagery and actual political dress. |
| 20 June 1792 | A revolutionary crowd places a red liberty cap on Louis XVI. |
| 22 September 1792 | The French Convention prescribes a republican seal showing antique-clad Liberty with a cap-topped pike. |
| 1790s | Bonnet rouge imagery spreads through clothing, medals, seals, weapons, ceramics, and festivals. |
| 1825 | George IV’s collection supplies the British Museum with what is later classified as a modern forged EID MAR aureus. |
| 1830 | Delacroix paints Liberty wearing the red cap in *Liberty Leading the People*. |
| 1850s | Thomas Crawford’s proposed liberty cap for the U.S. Capitol statue is rejected after Jefferson Davis’s objection; a crested helmet replaces it. |
| 1860 | Count de Salis donates a genuine AD 68 Spanish denarius copying the cap-between-daggers design to the British Museum. |
| 1871 | Thiers suppresses the bonnet as a seditious attribute on French municipal Marianne imagery. |
| 1879 onward | The cap returns and proliferates with Third Republic Marianne imagery. |
| 2010–2021 | A pierced EID MAR aureus is displayed on long-term loan at the British Museum. |
| 2015 | The Metropolitan Museum acquires its ca. 1790 French revolutionary cap, accession 2015.568. |
| October 2020 | Roma Numismatics sells a newly surfaced EID MAR aureus for a record price using a subsequently exposed false provenance. |
| 2022 | The pierced aureus formerly loaned to the British Museum is offered at auction; Paris 2024 introduces the Phryges. |
| 2023 | The purchaser surrenders the 2020-sale aureus; it is repatriated to Greece following the provenance investigation. |
| 2024 | Olympic and Paralympic Phryges give the liberty-cap form worldwide commercial visibility. |
| 2026 | The cap remains an official and popular attribute of Marianne; no unique ancient “pileus of Libertas” has been identified. |

## 12. Sources

### Primary and ancient evidence

- Livy, *Ab Urbe Condita*, especially Books 23–24: emancipated volunteer soldiers, pilei or white wool, and the commemorative painting in the Temple of Libertas.

- Persius, *Satires* 5: legal and philosophical freedom and the manumission formula.

- Roman Imperial Coinage Online.

- William Smith, *Dictionary of Greek and Roman Antiquities*, “Pileus.”
- Museum collection and conservation records.
- Auction catalogues used only where they document an individual specimen’s asserted or disputed history.

### Evidentiary assessment

The strongest evidence concerns the **symbol’s visual use on surviving coins**. The literary evidence securely establishes a Roman association between pileus and liberty but is less capable of reconstructing uniform ritual procedure. The weakest claim is the premise that one physical divine cap existed and passed through a recoverable custody chain. No presently available primary, archaeological, or museum evidence supports it.
