# The Mahmal (ceremonial pilgrimage palanquin): Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `the_mahmal_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary note

“The Mahmal” was not one immutable artifact with a single continuous chain of custody. It was a recurring class of ceremonial palanquin and, by extension, the camel, procession, and state pilgrimage caravan centered upon it. Several governments maintained separate mahmals; their wooden frames, textiles, banners, and metal fittings were repaired, replaced, recombined, and reused. Surviving objects called *mahmal* are therefore individual covers or reconstructed ensembles from this larger institution, not demonstrably the same physical object first recorded in the thirteenth century.

This dossier principally tracks:

1. the Egyptian or Cairo mahmal as an annually deployed ceremonial institution;
2. its individual surviving material remains;
3. the parallel Damascus/Syrian mahmal where its history bears directly on the Egyptian institution;
4. other regional mahmals only where they clarify the object-type or competing claims to ceremonial precedence.

No scientific dating, fiber analysis, metallurgical study, or continuous accession-level provenance has been published online for most surviving examples. Where museum catalogues assign dates, those are normally based upon inscriptions, named rulers, style, and documentary context rather than laboratory analysis.

Evidence labels used below are:

- **Documented:** supported by an object, inscription, contemporary account, institutional catalogue, or administrative record.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** a conclusion drawn by modern historians from primary evidence.
- **Institutional tradition:** asserted by a museum or government cultural body without a complete documentary demonstration.
- **Traditional/legendary:** a historically important claim whose factual basis is absent or contradicted by earlier evidence.
- **Disputed/unresolved:** sources materially disagree or the chain cannot be reconstructed.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Names and terminology

The standard Arabic form is **المَحْمِل**, usually transliterated *al-maḥmil* in Classical Arabic and pronounced *maḥmal* in Egyptian usage. It comes from the Arabic root **ḥ-m-l**, “to carry” or “bear”; the noun can denote something carried, a conveyance, litter, mounting, or load-bearing apparatus. European sources variously spell it *mahmal*, *mahmel*, *mahmil*, *maḥmal*, *maḥmil*, and formerly *mahmil chérif*. Ottoman Turkish museum usage includes **mehmel**.

Relevant extended names include:

- **المحمل المصري** (*al-maḥmal al-miṣrī*), the Egyptian mahmal;
- **المحمل الشريف** (*al-maḥmal al-sharīf*), “the Noble Mahmal”;
- **المحمل النبوي الشريف**, “the Noble Prophetic Mahmal,” a modern Egyptian museum designation;
- **المحمل الشامي** (*al-maḥmal al-shāmī*), the Syrian or Damascus mahmal;
- **جمل المحمل** (*jamal al-maḥmal*), the mahmal camel;
- **كسوة المحمل** (*kiswat al-maḥmal*) or **ستر المحمل** (*sitr al-maḥmal*), its cover;
- **زفّة المحمل**, the mahmal procession;
- **دَوَران المحمل**, the ceremonial circuit or “turning” of the mahmal through Cairo.

The term was elastic. It could mean:

1. the bare wooden litter;
2. the litter with its textile cover and metal finials;
3. the camel bearing it;
4. the entire Egyptian pilgrimage caravan;
5. colloquially, the expedition carrying the Kaaba textiles and imperial subsidies.

That linguistic expansion has produced a durable misconception: the mahmal itself did **not normally carry the annual *kiswa*** of the Kaaba. The kiswa and associated textiles traveled in separate boxes and on separate animals within the same caravan. The mahmal functioned above all as a visible emblem of the dispatching ruler.

### Basic form

The mature Egyptian mahmal was an unoccupied, approximately square wooden frame with vertical lower sides and a high pyramidal roof. A central upper post terminated in a large finial; four smaller finials stood at the corners. A tailored tent-like cover fitted over the frame. The ensemble was secured to a specially selected camel.

The Cairo Governorate’s institutional description gives:

- a square wooden framework;
- pyramidal top;
- red or green brocade cover;
- vegetal ornament and embroidered inscription bands;
- gold-colored embroidery;
- fringed lower edge;
- four silver or silver-gilt corner ornaments;
- in some accounts, two small Qur’ans in gilded-silver cases inside.

The last point is **disputed**. Medieval and modern observers variously described the mahmal as empty, as containing a Qur’an, or as accommodating Qur’ans in small cases. The object’s political meaning depended substantially on its being an empty throne-like sign of an absent ruler, but actual contents may have varied by period. No rule applicable to every Egyptian mahmal has been established.

### Covers and operational components

By the nineteenth century the Egyptian mahmal had at least two operational covers:

- an elaborate official cover used in ceremonial appearances, principal towns, and formal entries into Mecca and Medina;
- a simpler “daily” cover, normally green, used during travel and, by the nineteenth or early twentieth century, inside the Meccan sanctuary.

The official cover was not necessarily made anew each year. It was returned to Cairo, repaired at the textile workshop, stored, and reused. This makes “the annual mahmal” an annually assembled object whose components had different ages.

A complete ensemble could include:

- wooden frame;
- fitted pyramidal *sitr*;
- central and corner supports;
- five metal finials;
- five small finial banners;
- two larger double-sided state banners or *bayraq*;
- camel breastcloth, headgear, saddle coverings, ropes, and tack;
- portable Qur’an cases in some descriptions.

A surviving nineteenth-century camel bib in the Khalili Collection, **TXT 541**, is red silk, relief-embroidered in silver and silver-gilt wire over cotton padding, with tassels and silver sequins. Its inscription quotes Qur’an 16:7: “And they carry your loads to a land that you could not reach except with great trouble to yourselves…” [Khalili Collection catalogue](https://www.khalilicollections.org/collections/hajj-and-the-arts-of-pilgrimage/bib-for-the-camel-of-the-mahmal-txt-541/).

### Principal surviving examples

#### A. Qānṣūh al-Ghūrī cover, Topkapı Palace, Istanbul

**Documented object.** The oldest presently identified surviving mahmal cover is associated by inscription with the Mamluk sultan al-Ashraf Qānṣūh al-Ghūrī, who ruled Egypt in 1501–1516.

- Institution: Topkapı Palace Museum, Istanbul.
- Historical catalogue location: Turkish embroidery section.
- Number reported by Jacques Jomier: **263, “Mehmel.”**
- Date: early sixteenth century, before the Ottoman conquest of Egypt.
- Surviving component: embroidered cover, formerly displayed on a wooden support.
- Display history: photographed and displayed around 1940–1950; Jomier reported it subsequently in storage.
- Decoration: applied-cloth Arabic inscription bands and medallions, including the ruler’s titulature and Mamluk blazon.

Jomier published the object and inscriptions in 1972. His article remains the basic object-specific study; it does not establish exactly when or how the cover reached Istanbul. Transfer after the Ottoman conquest is plausible but **not documented by a published seizure, treasury-entry, or transport record** in the sources consulted. [Jomier, “Le maḥmal du sultan Qānṣūh al-Ghūrī”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/anisl_0570-1716_1972_num_11_1_955).

#### B. Damascus mahmal cover, Khalili Collection TXT 289

**Documented object.**

- Place of manufacture: Istanbul.
- Date/name: AH 1067/AD 1656–57, in the name of Ottoman Sultan Mehmed IV.
- Materials: green silk; silver and silver-gilt wire embroidery over cotton-thread padding.
- Finials: gilded copper alloy, catalogued as later additions.
- Dimensions assembled: 355 × 190 × 174 cm.
- Identity: cover for the Damascus mahmal, not the Cairo mahmal.
- Present custody: Khalili Collection, accession **TXT 289**.

Because the finials are later and the cover is mounted as an ensemble, the present display configuration is not an untouched seventeenth-century object. [Khalili catalogue](https://khalilicollections.org/collections/hajj-and-the-arts-of-pilgrimage/khalili-collection-hajj-and-the-arts-of-pilgrimage-a-complete-cover-for-a-damascus-mahmal-txt289/).

#### C. Egyptian mahmal, Khalili Collection TXT 469

**Documented composite, repaired object.**

- First use: during the reign of Mahmud II, 1808–1839.
- Renovation and reuse: under Khedive Ismāʿīl, 1867–1879.
- Materials: black silk with colored silk appliqué; silver and silver-gilt wire over cotton padding; five silver finials.
- Dimensions assembled: approximately 350 × 135 × 100 cm.
- Inscriptions: two roof faces name Mahmud II; other faces record renovation/reuse by Ismāʿīl.
- Present custody: Khalili Collection, **TXT 469**.

This is direct material evidence that covers were treated as renewable state equipment rather than discarded annually. [Khalili catalogue](https://www.khalilicollections.org/collections/hajj-and-the-arts-of-pilgrimage/txt-469/).

#### D. Egyptian mahmal, Khalili Collection TXT 442

**Documented composite.**

- Date: 1867–1876.
- Place: Cairo.
- Cover and banners: red silk with green and gold-colored silk appliqué; silver and silver-gilt wire over cotton padding.
- Finials: copper alloy.
- Frame: catalogued as contemporary.
- Dimensions assembled: approximately 400 × 130 × 102 cm.
- Components: cover, wooden frame, five finials, five finial flags, and two large state banners.
- Alteration: the large central finial bears the name and *tughra* of Mahmud II, who died decades before the cover’s manufacture; it was demonstrably reused.
- Present custody: Khalili Collection, **TXT 442**.

This is especially important evidence against treating a named mahmal as a unitary original. Its cover, frame, flags, and central finial have distinct histories. [Khalili catalogue](https://khalilicollections.org/collections/hajj-and-the-arts-of-pilgrimage/khalili-collection-hajj-and-the-arts-of-pilgrimage-mahmal-txt442/).

#### E. Egyptian mahmal, Khalili Collection TXT 492

**Documented object, catalogue dating problem.**

- Place: Cairo.
- Commissioner named in the inscription/catalogue: Sultan Abdülmecid I.
- Presenter: Egyptian governor Muḥammad Saʿīd Pasha.
- Materials: black silk with red and brick-colored appliqué; silver and silver-gilt wire over cotton padding.
- Dimensions assembled: approximately 250 × 140 × 106 cm.
- Components: cover, finial banners, and side banners with Ottoman *tughra*.
- Decoration: stylized palms on the base.
- Present custody: Khalili Collection, **TXT 492**.

The online record provides no single converted date. Abdülmecid’s reign and Saʿīd’s governorship constrain the commission to the mid-nineteenth century. [Khalili catalogue](https://www.khalilicollections.org/collections/hajj-and-the-arts-of-pilgrimage/txt-492/).

#### F. Egyptian mahmal, Khalili Collection TXT 529

**Documented reinscribed/reused object.**

- Dates embroidered: AH 1316 and 1318/AD 1898–99 and 1900–01.
- Place: Cairo.
- Materials: black silk with red and brick-colored appliqué; silver and silver-gilt wire over cotton padding; five gilded-copper finials.
- Dimensions assembled: approximately 325 × 144 × 107 cm.
- Inscriptions: one roof side records a commission; the opposite side records renewal two years later.
- Present custody: Khalili Collection, **TXT 529**.

The online catalogue calls the commissioner “Sultan Abdulmajid I” while assigning the object to 1898–1901 and associating it with ʿAbbās Ḥilmī II. Since Abdülmecid I died in 1861, that catalogue wording is internally inconsistent and probably represents a naming or metadata error. The dates and inscriptions require direct rechecking against the object and printed catalogue. [Khalili catalogue](https://www.khalilicollections.org/collections/hajj-and-the-arts-of-pilgrimage/txt-529/).

#### G. King Fuʾād mahmal, Egyptian Geographical Society

**Institutional tradition; physically reported.**

The Ethnographic Museum of the Egyptian Geographical Society in Cairo holds an “original model” presented by King Fuʾād I. It is described as heavy dark-purple silk embroidered in raised gold and silver thread, including the Throne Verse, with vegetal and geometric decoration. Doris Behrens-Abouseif identified the mahmal of Fuʾād as displayed there in the 1990s. The museum’s public-access details are current, but no online accession number, measurements, full inscription transcription, or conservation report was located. [Egyptian cultural-institution record](https://torath.gov.eg/providers/EGS); [museum report](https://www.albayan.ae/culture/2000-07-16-1.1037803).

#### H. King Farouk-era mahmal, Suez National Museum

**Institutional attribution.**

The museum’s official guide states that its Mahmal Hall displays “the last Mahmal made in the reign of King Farouk,” together with Kaaba textile fragments and related calligraphic textiles. The guide does not publish an accession number, precise date, dimensions, or full provenance. It should not automatically be identified with the Fuʾād specimen or with the mahmal involved in the 1926 confrontation. [Suez National Museum guide](https://egymonuments.gov.eg/media/8979/museum-of-suez-en.pdf).

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Secure historical beginning

The best-supported origin of the Cairo mahmal institution is the reign of Sultan al-Ẓāhir Baybars. Medieval chronicles associate the first dispatch with **659 AH**, conventionally converted in this context to **1261 or 1266**, depending on the event and chronological calculation being cited. The sources do not preserve a manufacturing contract or the first frame itself.

Doris Behrens-Abouseif’s critical study distinguishes the documented Baybars association from the later story of Shajar al-Durr. She identifies the first Egyptian-caravan association under Baybars and shows that the queen’s supposed role belongs to a much later explanatory legend. [Behrens-Abouseif, “The Mahmal Legend”](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/records/7neyt-x0w42).

Baybars’s innovation was not the sending of the Kaaba’s covering. Muslim rulers had dispatched *kiswa*, money, and supplies long before the mahmal. The innovation was the highly visible, unoccupied, ruler-representing litter at the head of the caravan. It accompanied Mamluk claims to protect the Holy Cities after the Abbasid caliphate’s fall in Baghdad and after Baybars installed an Abbasid caliphal line in Cairo.

### What the earliest evidence does and does not establish

It establishes:

- a mahmal associated with the Egyptian caravan under Baybars;
- an empty or symbolically occupied palanquin;
- a political and ceremonial function connected with Mamluk leadership of the pilgrimage.

It does **not** establish:

- a surviving physical first mahmal;
- an exact workshop;
- dimensions, weight, or complete materials;
- that every later Cairo mahmal copied one first object without alteration;
- that Shajar al-Durr created or personally rode in it;
- that the kiswa was physically placed inside it.

### The Shajar al-Durr origin legend

A widespread modern account says that Shajar al-Durr traveled to Mecca in 1247 or 1250 in a magnificent camel litter and that the subsequently empty litter commemorated her pilgrimage. This is **legend, not contemporary documentation**.

The story arose from later attempts to explain why an empty, royal-looking litter accompanied the caravan. Behrens-Abouseif found that contemporary and near-contemporary sources record pilgrimages by other Mamluk-court women but do not establish Shajar al-Durr’s supposed foundational journey. The Mahmal’s actual earliest historical association is with Baybars after her death. The legend nevertheless served several functions: it supplied a royal “first rider,” explained the emptiness, and tied the ceremony to the celebrated female founder of the Mamluk regime.

### Manufacture and repair in Cairo

Later Egyptian covers were produced or maintained through the state textile establishment commonly called **Dār al-Kiswa**, eventually located at al-Khurunfush in Cairo. The online evidence does not justify assigning every surviving cover to that building: Ottoman commissions could originate in Istanbul, and fittings migrated between ensembles.

The Khalili examples demonstrate the manufacturing vocabulary:

- woven silk ground;
- colored silk appliqué;
- padded relief embroidery;
- silver and silver-gilt wire;
- tailored pyramidal and rectangular panels;
- metal finials and embroidered flags;
- ruler’s name and *tughra* as dating and authorization devices.

After return from the Hijaz, the cover went back to the workshop for inspection, repair, alteration, and storage. Reinscription under a new ruler was possible, as TXT 469 and TXT 529 show.

No reliable documented cost for a complete mahmal has been found in the accessible sources. Figures given for whole pilgrimage caravans, the *surra*, tribal payments, or the kiswa must not be misreported as the cost of the mahmal itself.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 1260s–1517: Mamluk state custody

Under Baybars and his successors, the Cairo government maintained the mahmal as state ceremonial property. It passed annually through a temporary chain of operational custody:

1. sultan and central administration;
2. treasury or textile establishment;
3. officials arranging the Cairo ceremony;
4. *amīr al-ḥajj*, commander of the caravan;
5. mahmal camel handlers and military escort;
6. ceremonial placement in Mecca, ʿArafāt, Minā, and Medina;
7. return expedition;
8. workshop or treasury storage in Cairo.

This was custody, not private ownership. The caravan commander did not acquire title to it.

Ibn Battuta’s fourteenth-century account provides early eyewitness evidence for the Cairo procession: in Rajab the mahmal was taken through the city amid mounted troops and public spectacle, stimulating prospective pilgrims to prepare. [ECHOS presentation of the Ibn Battuta passage](https://echos.beniculturali.unipd.it/events/1982).

Some Mamluk rulers curtailed secondary processions. Qāytbāy suppressed the Rajab circuit, and Qānṣūh al-Ghūrī briefly revived and again restricted it. Such actions changed the ceremony, not necessarily the annual dispatch itself.

### Early sixteenth-century material break

The al-Ghūrī cover is the first surviving physical witness. After the Ottoman defeat of the Mamluks in 1516–1517, it eventually entered the Topkapı collections. The precise transfer is **unresolved**. It may have been taken as imperial booty, transferred with other Mamluk treasury property, or sent later. Calling it documented “loot of 1517” would exceed the evidence presently located, although conquest is the historical context of its relocation.

### 1517–early nineteenth century: Ottoman system

After conquering Egypt, the Ottoman sultan inherited the Cairo mahmal and pilgrimage-administration apparatus. A second major imperial mahmal left Damascus, gathering pilgrims from Anatolia and Syria. Both represented the Ottoman sovereign, but Cairo and Damascus retained distinct administrative and regional identities.

Other mahmals appeared from Yemen and, at different periods, Iraq, the Maghrib, Darfur, Hyderabad, and Arabian polities. Not all were permanent institutions, and their presence could become a contest over rank. Illustrations sometimes show the Egyptian, Syrian, and Yemeni mahmals together.

The Cairo mahmal’s route generally ran from Cairo across Sinai and down the northwestern Arabian corridor. It was vulnerable to water shortage, tribal tolls, robbery, military conflict, and epidemic. Payments to route tribes were part of the caravan’s protection system; these payments traveled with the expedition but were not contents of the litter.

The Saudi-Wahhabi occupation of the Holy Cities in the early nineteenth century disrupted the ceremony. Wahhabi doctrine rejected veneration of the mahmal and objected to music and other practices treated as innovations. Egyptian-Ottoman reconquest restored the old state pilgrimage apparatus.

### Nineteenth-century transport transformation

From **1861**, the mahmal was moved from Cairo by railway to Suez and then by steamship to Jeddah rather than completing the old overland desert route. The ritual departure through Cairo remained important even after transportation ceased to be caravan-based in the literal sense.

Surviving covers show repeated reuse:

- Mahmud II’s older fittings could be joined to a cover of the 1867–1870s;
- a Mahmud II cover was reinscribed or renovated under Ismāʿīl;
- the same official cover could make multiple round trips;
- simpler travel covers took most routine wear.

A mahmal’s custody chain consequently resembled that of regimental colors or theatrical regalia: central storage, annual issue, travel, return, repair, and reissue.

### 1908–1914: Damascus and the railway

The Damascus caravan began using the Hejaz Railway after it reached Medina in 1908. The railway carried pilgrims and imperial ceremonial equipment to Medina, though the remaining journey to Mecca still required other transport. The Syrian mahmal tradition ended with the First World War and Ottoman collapse; a Khalili catalogue and modern scholarship place its disappearance around 1914.

### 1923 interruption

Egyptian-Hijazi relations were already strained under Sharif Hussein’s Hashemite government. A 1923 dispute caused the Egyptian mahmal to turn back without completing its expected ceremonies. This episode anticipates 1926 and demonstrates that “annual continuity” was never absolute.

### The 1926 Mahmal Incident

On the first days of the pilgrimage in June 1926, the Egyptian mahmal procession, accompanied by an armed Egyptian guard and military music, encountered Najdi *Ikhwān* pilgrims or fighters who regarded the ceremony and music as forbidden innovations. Stones were reportedly thrown; Egyptian troops fired.

The exact date and casualties vary:

- reports place the confrontation on 18, 20, or 22 June, reflecting calendar conversion and reporting dates;
- Egyptian-oriented accounts commonly report 25 Najdis killed and an Egyptian officer and three soldiers wounded;
- Saudi-oriented accounts report five killed;
- accounts differ over whether the Najdis were armed and over which side initiated lethal violence.

King ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz intervened personally or through his forces to stop the confrontation. The mahmal was removed from public view and did not resume its ceremonial course.

The competing official narratives were published almost immediately. A Hijazi-Najdi communiqué reached Cairo on 23 June and appeared in *al-Ahram* on 24 June, while Egyptian reports relied on telegrams from the *amīr al-ḥajj*, Maḥmūd ʿAzmī Pasha. The conflict therefore became an international propaganda contest as well as a physical assault on the procession. [Contemporary-report retrospective citing the 1926 communiqués](https://www.youm7.com/story/2018/6/23/%D8%B3%D8%B9%D9%8A%D8%AF-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%B4%D8%AD%D8%A7%D8%AA-%D9%8A%D9%83%D8%AA%D8%A8-%D8%B0%D8%A7%D8%AA-%D9%8A%D9%88%D9%85-23-%D9%8A%D9%88%D9%86%D9%8A%D9%88-1926-%C2%AB%D9%88%D9%83%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%A9-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%AD%D8%AC%D8%A7%D8%B2/3844093).

The incident ended the mahmal’s physical travel to Mecca. Egypt withheld official pilgrimage participation, payments, and the mahmal for years. Diplomatic relations were normalized by the 1936 Egyptian-Saudi treaty. Egypt later resumed sending Kaaba textiles, but that was not a restoration of the old armed mahmal procession.

### 1927–1952: procession without pilgrimage

After 1926 the object’s ceremonial life divided:

- in Saudi Arabia, the mahmal was excluded as an objectionable innovation and competing symbol of sovereignty;
- in Cairo, mahmal processions continued as public and state spectacles even though the litter no longer completed its ancient journey.

King Fuʾād’s name became attached to one surviving Cairo example. Later King Farouk-era mahmals or replicas continued to represent the tradition domestically. The last royal-era Cairo parade is generally dated to **1952**, the year of the Egyptian revolution.

### 1952–present: museum dispersal

The abolition of the monarchy removed the ruler whom the empty litter represented. Surviving mahmals and components entered museums, institutional collections, and eventually the art market.

Known present repositories include:

- Topkapı Palace Museum: al-Ghūrī cover, no. 263 in Jomier’s documentation;
- Egyptian Geographical Society Ethnographic Museum: Fuʾād-associated mahmal;
- Suez National Museum: Farouk-era mahmal;
- Khalili Collection: seven Egyptian and Syrian mahmal covers, including TXT 289, 442, 469, 492, and 529;
- Metropolitan Museum of Art: a composite seventeenth-/nineteenth-century camel shaffron associated with a mahmal caravan, accession **2020.300a–d**, not the mahmal itself. [Met catalogue](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/948931).

The pre-accession ownership histories of the privately held Khalili mahmals are not supplied in the online object records. Thus the chain between state/workshop custody and modern collection custody remains incomplete.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### There is no single continuously authentic “Mahmal”

The essential authenticity finding is categorical: no evidence supports the survival of one mahmal continuously from Baybars to the twentieth century. “The Egyptian Mahmal” was an office-bearing ceremonial assemblage. Authenticity attaches at several levels:

- authentic historical cover;
- authentic fitting reused on a later cover;
- period wooden frame;
- modern display frame;
- authentic object from a particular reign;
- later replica representing the institution.

### The al-Ghūrī claimant

The Topkapı cover is the strongest candidate for the **earliest surviving physical mahmal component**, because it carries al-Ghūrī’s inscriptions and Mamluk heraldic vocabulary. It is not the Baybars mahmal. Its exact path into the Ottoman treasury remains undocumented in the accessible record.

### Reuse as authentic practice

TXT 469, TXT 442, and TXT 529 show that replacement and alteration were normal:

- TXT 469 combines a Mahmud II commission with an Ismāʿīl-era renovation;
- TXT 442 has a reused Mahmud II central finial on a later cover;
- TXT 529 records a renewal two years after its first dated inscription.

Such objects are not “forgeries.” Their composite state is evidence of historical maintenance.

### Museum reconstruction

When a surviving textile is mounted on a wooden structure, the frame may be original, contemporary, later, or modern. TXT 442’s frame is catalogued as contemporary; TXT 289’s metal finials are explicitly later. Topkapı displayed the al-Ghūrī textile on a frame, but the published account does not prove that frame was Mamluk.

### Catalogue inconsistency in TXT 529

The Khalili online record’s attribution of an 1898–1901 object to Abdülmecid I is chronologically impossible as written. Possible explanations include:

- erroneous online ruler-name transcription;
- reuse of an older *tughra* or design;
- confusion between Abdülmecid and Abdülhamid II;
- incorrect interpretation of the renewal inscription.

This is an unresolved catalogue problem, not evidence that the textile itself is forged.

### Qur’an versus emptiness

Claims that the mahmal “contained the Qur’an” are neither universally demonstrable nor necessarily incompatible with its political emptiness: a small Qur’an case could be placed within an otherwise riderless litter. Sources must be dated individually. The statement that the mahmal always carried the kiswa inside it is false.

### Scientific examination

No published radiocarbon dating, dye chromatography, metal assay, fiber microscopy, or dendrochronology was located for the principal mahmal covers. Dating presently rests on:

- embroidered names and regnal titles;
- *tughra* forms;
- recorded renewal dates;
- textile and decorative style;
- collection and documentary context.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Cairo circuits

The mahmal appeared in major annual processions. The Rajab circuit announced the approaching pilgrimage season and prompted recruitment and preparation. The later Shawwāl ceremony accompanied formal transfer to the *amīr al-ḥajj* and departure.

Crowds decorated shops and streets. Mounted Mamluks or later troops displayed horsemanship and lance exercises; bands, drummers, Sufi groups, officials, artisans, camel handlers, and pilgrims joined the procession. The ruler or representative ceremonially inspected or delivered the pilgrimage textiles.

### Route ritual

At halts, the mahmal marked the commander’s camp and the state’s presence. Its precedence among rival mahmals was politically charged. At Mecca, ʿArafāt, Minā, and Medina it occupied conspicuous positions. Khalili documentation describes the formal cover being used when passing principal cities and on entries into Mecca and Medina.

### Ruler’s substitute

The mahmal was an empty royal body. It allowed a sultan who did not perform the pilgrimage to appear symbolically along the road and at the sanctuaries. It asserted:

- protection of pilgrims;
- financing of the Holy Cities;
- entitlement to appoint or command the caravan;
- relationship with the Meccan sharif;
- status within the wider Muslim political order.

### Blessing and popular devotion

People touched, kissed, or rubbed themselves against the mahmal or associated Qur’an case, particularly on its return, seeking *baraka*. Its arrival home after visiting the sanctuaries endowed it with devotional prestige.

This was popular religious practice, not a prescribed rite of the hajj. Reformist and Wahhabi critics classified it as *bidʿa*, an unauthorized innovation. That disagreement was not abstract: it led to physical exclusion and violence.

### The mahmal and the kiswa

The mahmal’s strong visual association with the Kaaba covering made it a metonym for Egypt’s kiswa service. Nevertheless:

- the mahmal was not the kiswa;
- the kiswa was not ordinarily draped over the mahmal;
- the kiswa traveled in separate chests;
- old Kaaba textiles returned or were distributed through distinct administrative procedures.

### Access and handling

The camel was specially selected for strength and appearance and was sometimes ceremonially exempted from ordinary labor afterward. Skilled handlers controlled it. State officials regulated the object’s issue, procession, security, and storage. The public could approach it during processions, but its operational handling remained specialized.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### Shajar al-Durr as first rider

**Tier: later origin legend.**

The fullest common version says Shajar al-Durr went to Mecca in a splendid litter; afterward an empty replica was annually sent to commemorate her pilgrimage. Some retellings date this to 1247, before she became sultana.

Against it:

- no contemporary source documents the foundational journey;
- the first historical mahmal association belongs to Baybars;
- Shajar al-Durr died before the documented institution began.

Its endurance reflects the explanatory power of a memorable royal woman occupying the otherwise empty structure.

### The mahmal carried the kiswa

**Tier: modern simplification.**

Popular accounts frequently say the mahmal “carried the Kaaba covering.” In institutional terms the procession and caravan transported the kiswa; physically, separate camels and chests bore it. The imprecision comes from using *mahmal* for the entire expedition.

### The mahmal was always empty

**Tier: broadly attested but not universally provable.**

It carried no royal passenger and was therefore ceremonially empty. Some sources, however, place one or two Qur’ans inside. The safest formulation is “passengerless,” not necessarily “containing absolutely nothing.”

### A single seven-hundred-year-old object

**Tier: modern rhetorical compression.**

Statements that “the mahmal” survived for seven centuries describe the institution, not a single frame and textile. Surviving examples record replacement, repair, reinscription, and recombination.

### Sacred object versus political banner

These are not mutually exclusive descriptions. The mahmal acquired popular blessing through proximity to pilgrimage and sacred textiles, while simultaneously operating as a sovereign emblem. Reformist hostility focused precisely on the devotional force accumulated by a state symbol.

## 8. Current Status and Location

There is no mahmal currently traveling as part of the official hajj.

### Confirmed or institutionally reported locations

- **Topkapı Palace Museum, Istanbul:** Qānṣūh al-Ghūrī cover, historically catalogued no. 263. Current display status is not confirmed; Jomier reported it in storage after mid-century display.
- **Khalili Collection:** seven Egyptian and Syrian covers. Its online catalogue documents TXT 289, TXT 442, TXT 469, TXT 492, and TXT 529 in detail. The collection is privately held and exhibited through loans and digital access rather than a permanent public museum. [Collection overview](https://khalilicollections.org/all-collections/hajj-and-the-arts-of-pilgrimage/).
- **Egyptian Geographical Society, Cairo:** King Fuʾād-associated mahmal in the Ethnographic Museum. The institution reports public visiting hours, though access can be administratively restricted.
- **Suez National Museum:** Farouk-era mahmal in the Mahmal Hall.
- **Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York:** camel shaffron associated with mahmal processions; not currently on view according to the catalogue.
- **Johns Hopkins University Special Collections:** a photographic postcard of the Damascus mahmal, useful as documentary evidence rather than a surviving mahmal. [Collection record](https://aspace.library.jhu.edu/repositories/3/resources/1812).

No present repatriation case specifically concerning a mahmal was located. The absence of a public claim should not be mistaken for a complete lawful-provenance record, especially for privately collected examples whose movement out of Egyptian, Syrian, Ottoman, or successor-state custody is unpublished.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Photography, prints, and postcards

From the nineteenth century onward the mahmal became one of the most reproduced images of Cairo and the hajj. European engravings presented it as an exotic urban spectacle; Egyptian lithographs and photographs represented state service to Islam.

The Khalili Collection preserves:

- “The Egyptian Mahmal on its Way to Mecca,” **ARC.pt 76**;
- “The Blessing of the Mahmal,” **ARC.pt 101**;
- “The Return of the Mahmal from Mecca to Cairo,” **ARC.pt 123**;
- late nineteenth-century photograph of the Damascus mahmal, **ARC.pp 166**;
- photographs of Egyptian processions and the mahmal.

The images are important evidence but must be dated independently: generic prints were recopied, recolored, and sold long after the scenes they purported to show.

### Museumization

After the ceremony ceased, Egyptian institutions recast the mahmal as:

- evidence of Egypt’s historical service to the Holy Cities;
- textile and embroidery heritage;
- royal and state ceremonial art;
- a symbol of older Cairo popular culture.

Museum labels sometimes preserve older conflations between mahmal, caravan, and kiswa. Their claims must be checked against individual object inscriptions.

### Political memory

In Egyptian nationalist memory, the mahmal can symbolize Cairo’s former religious prestige. In Saudi state memory, 1926 marks the displacement of foreign armed ceremonial authority and the establishment of Saudi control over the kiswa and pilgrimage order. Each memory selects a different feature of the same object: service and continuity on one side, contested sovereignty and condemned innovation on the other.

### Commercial and festival replicas

Replica mahmals appear in museum displays, heritage events, television reconstructions, tourist imagery, and Ramadan or pilgrimage-themed decoration. Unless accompanied by accession data, such structures should be assumed to be replicas. No recognized modern replica has inherited the ritual authority of the historical mahmal.

### Scholarship and exhibition

The 2012 British Museum exhibition *Hajj: Journey to the Heart of Islam* and the Khalili Hajj collection helped shift modern attention from the mahmal merely as picturesque folklore to its material construction and political agency. Europeana’s account similarly emphasizes workshop production, public blessing, and rivalry among caravan centers. [Europeana](https://www.europeana.eu/en/stories/hajj-and-the-remarkable-story-of-the-mahmal).

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Jacques Jomier

Jomier’s 1953 *Le Maḥmal et la caravane égyptienne des pèlerins de La Mecque (XIIIe–XXe siècles)* remains the foundational monograph. It assembled Arabic chronicles, administrative history, eyewitness descriptions, photographs, and route information over nearly seven centuries. The study is extensive but predates modern material-culture methods and much museum digitization. [IFAO catalogue](https://www.ifao.egnet.net/publications/catalogue/?coll=qal&nb=10&nv=0&page=352&total=4193).

His 1972 article documented the al-Ghūrī textile at Topkapı, including its inscription program and museum status. It established that physical mahmal material survives from the final Mamluk period.

### Doris Behrens-Abouseif

Her 1997 article is the key critique of the Shajar al-Durr legend. It reconstructs the pilgrimage activity of Mamluk court women and shows how later historical memory grafted a female royal origin story onto an institution first securely associated with Baybars.

### Richard McGregor and material religion

Later work treats the mahmal as a devotional object rather than only a political institution. This approach explains how an officially empty state emblem became something people touched for blessing and why reformist rejection became so intense.

### Political history of 1926

Modern studies place the incident within:

- the post-Ottoman struggle for the Hijaz;
- King Fuʾād’s interest in wider Islamic leadership after abolition of the Ottoman caliphate;
- Ibn Saʿūd’s conquest and consolidation;
- incompatible armed pilgrimage jurisdictions;
- Wahhabi opposition to music and object-centered devotion;
- competition over the kiswa and Holy Cities’ subsidies.

The principal unresolved factual question is the exact casualty count. Egyptian, Saudi, diplomatic, and press accounts were partisan. A definitive reconstruction requires parallel use of Egyptian Foreign Ministry and Interior Ministry files, Saudi records, British diplomatic correspondence, and the 1926 Arabic press.

### Archival gaps

The following evidence remains insufficiently published online:

- annual Dār al-Kiswa workshop accounts specifically identifying mahmal construction costs;
- repair registers linking surviving textiles to particular journeys;
- treasury entry for the al-Ghūrī cover at Topkapı;
- original acquisition records for the Khalili mahmals;
- accession and conservation files for the Fuʾād and Farouk examples;
- technical textile studies;
- exact documentation of the final transfer from state ceremonial equipment to museums.

### Discredited or weak theories that remain popular

- **Shajar al-Durr invented the mahmal:** unsupported legend.
- **The same mahmal traveled for seven centuries:** incompatible with surviving evidence of replacement and recombination.
- **It was the vehicle carrying the kiswa:** generally a confusion between the litter and caravan.
- **1926 was solely a theological dispute:** incomplete; sovereignty, diplomatic recognition, armed jurisdiction, and control of pilgrimage resources were central.
- **The mahmal continued traveling until Egypt stopped making the kiswa in 1962:** false conflation. The mahmal ceased entering the Hijaz after 1926; Cairo display processions continued to about 1952; Egyptian kiswa supply followed a separate chronology.
