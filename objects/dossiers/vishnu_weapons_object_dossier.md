# Vishnu's five named weapons (Sudarshana Chakra, Panchajanya, Kaumodaki, Sharnga, Nandaka): Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary key

This dossier treats the *pañcāyudha*—Viṣṇu’s five weapons—as a related legendary ensemble:

1. **Sudarśana Cakra** — discus or wheel  
2. **Pāñcajanya** — conch trumpet  
3. **Kaumodakī** — mace or club  
4. **Śārṅga** — bow  
5. **Nandaka** — sword  

They do not constitute a surviving physical regalia set. No museum, temple treasury, archaeological excavation, scientific examination, or securely provenanced historical inventory possesses five artifacts demonstrably identical with the weapons narrated in Sanskrit literature. Their recoverable “lives” therefore consist principally of:

- their changing descriptions and custody in Sanskrit texts;
- their manifestation as independent divine beings or *āyudhapuruṣas* (“weapon-persons”);
- material representations on coins, stone reliefs, bronzes, paintings, temple icons, seals, and ritual branding tools;
- their continuing ritual, literary, political, and popular reception.

The five did not enter the record simultaneously. The wheel, conch, and mace are considerably more prominent in early Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa and Viṣṇu iconography than the bow and sword. The canonical four attributes of the common four-armed Viṣṇu image are normally conch, discus, mace, and lotus—not all five weapons. “Viṣṇu’s five weapons” is therefore a literary and liturgical classification, not necessarily the inventory represented in an ordinary Viṣṇu icon.

Evidence is labeled as follows:

- **Primary textual evidence:** what a Sanskrit work’s received text says. Its narrative is evidence for the history of the tradition, not proof that the narrated material event occurred.
- **Material evidence:** an extant coin, sculpture, painting, inscription, or ritual implement.
- **Scholarly inference:** an interpretation based on philology, iconography, archaeology, or historical comparison.
- **Religious tradition:** a claim maintained within a living community or commentary.
- **Late or uncertain tradition:** a story whose antiquity, textual source, or relationship to the earlier record cannot be securely established.
- **Unresolved:** the available evidence does not permit a determination.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 The ensemble: *pañcāyudha*

Sanskrit **पञ्चायुध**, *pañcāyudha*, means “five weapons” (*pañca*, five; *āyudha*, weapon or martial implement). In Vaiṣṇava usage it can denote the five named implements carried, commanded, or embodied by Viṣṇu-Nārāyaṇa or Kṛṣṇa.

They are frequently listed in a fixed devotional formula: conch, discus, mace, bow, and sword. A familiar closing verse of the *Viṣṇusahasranāma* calls Viṣṇu *śaṅkhabhṛn nandakī cakrī śārṅgadhanvā gadādharaḥ*—bearer of the conch, Nandaka, the discus, the Śārṅga bow, and the mace—and ends with *sarvapraharaṇāyudhaḥ*, “he whose weapons are every instrument of attack.”

The five are not merely equipment in later theology. They can speak, appear, obey summons, receive hymns, be worshipped, incarnate, and act independently. Consequently, “object” and “divine being” are overlapping categories.

### 1.2 Sudarśana Cakra

**Scripts and forms**

- Sanskrit: **सुदर्शन चक्र**, *Sudarśana Cakra*
- Common modern forms: Sudarshan Chakra, Sudarsana Chakra
- Tamil: **சுதர்சன சக்கரம்**, *Cutarcaṉa Cakkaram*; the personified form is also **சக்கரத்தாழ்வார்**, *Cakkarattāḻvār/Chakrathalvar*
- Generic designation: **चक्र**, *cakra*, “wheel, circle, discus”
- Personification: *Cakrapuruṣa*, “Wheel-person”; *Sudarśanapuruṣa*

**Name.** *Sudarśana* combines *su-* (“good, excellent, auspicious”) and *darśana* (“seeing, sight, appearance, vision”). It can mean “beautiful to behold,” “auspicious vision,” or “good sight.” In theological interpretation it also becomes Viṣṇu’s effective or all-penetrating vision. *Cakra* has a much wider semantic range: wheel, circle, cycle, sovereignty, military formation, ritual diagram, and missile.

**Textual form.** The sources describe it as a thrown, returning, radiant wheel. The *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* gives it a thousand spokes, tremendous effulgence, extraordinary speed, and autonomous agency. In the *Mahābhārata* it appears when Kṛṣṇa thinks of it, cuts off Śiśupāla’s head, and kills Daityas and Dānavas.

The most unusually concrete description comes at the destruction of the Yādavas in the *Mausala Parvan*: Kṛṣṇa’s discus, “given by Agni,” is described in the Ganguli translation as **made of iron, with a nave of the hardest adamant**, after which it ascends into the sky before Kṛṣṇa’s death. This is a description within epic narrative, not a metallurgical examination of an extant object.

**Iconographic form.** Early images may show a comparatively plain wheel or thick discus, sometimes in profile and sometimes frontally. Later art supplies flames, serration, a central boss, spokes, concentric bands, and geometric diagrams. As *Cakrapuruṣa*, it may be:

- a male attendant standing beside Viṣṇu;
- a human figure with a wheel behind or above the head;
- a figure holding the wheel;
- a fierce, multi-armed deity within a flaming wheel;
- in southern temples, a two-sided icon with Sudarśana on the front and Yoga-Narasimha on the reverse.

A fifth–sixth-century stone *Cakrapuruṣa* in the Los Angeles County Museum of Art demonstrates the Gupta and post-Gupta anthropomorphic tradition. LACMA describes a male figure with the discus rising behind his head and identifies these personified weapons as partial manifestations of Viṣṇu’s energy.

**Dimensions and weight.** No primary narrative supplies a consistent diameter or weight. Claims assigning modern engineering dimensions, revolutions per minute, nuclear properties, mercury mechanisms, or scientifically measured cutting power are modern speculation unless tied to a particular replica.

### 1.3 Pāñcajanya

**Scripts and forms**

- Sanskrit: **पाञ्चजन्य**, *Pāñcajanya*
- Common form: Panchajanya
- Generic designation: **शङ्ख**, *śaṅkha*, conch shell
- Personification: *Śaṅkhapuruṣa* or *Pāñcajanyapuruṣa*
- Tamil generic: **சங்கு**, *caṅku*

**Name.** Grammatically *pāñcajanya* can mean “of, belonging to, or descended from Pañcajana/Pañcajanya.” In the Kṛṣṇa cycle it is conventionally connected with the marine being Pañcajana. Because *pañca* also means “five,” later allegorical readings associate it with the five elements or senses. Those symbolic derivations should not be confused with the narrative etymology from Pañcajana.

**Physical class.** It is a conch trumpet, a natural marine shell modified or used as a wind instrument. Historical ritual conches are commonly cut at the apex and blown through the opening. One older iconographic survey identifies the Viṣṇu conch with a large marine gastropod traditionally called *Murex tritonis*; modern taxonomy and individual depictions should not be assumed to identify a precise species.

**Textual description.** It is loud, “divine,” and terrifying in battle. *Bhagavadgītā* 1.15 states simply:

> पाञ्चजन्यं हृषीकेशो देवदत्तं धनंजयः ।  
> *pāñcajanyaṃ hṛṣīkeśo devadattaṃ dhanañjayaḥ*  
> “Hṛṣīkeśa [Kṛṣṇa] blew Pāñcajanya, and Dhanañjaya [Arjuna] Devadatta.”

The verse names and uses it but does not describe its manufacture, handedness, dimensions, color, or decoration. During the Kurukṣetra narrative its sound functions as a command, omen, announcement of presence, and psychological weapon. In *Drona Parvan*, Yudhiṣṭhira hears its “loud and terrible blasts,” said to fill the three worlds with fear.

**Iconographic form.** In sculpture it ranges from a naturalistic shell spiral to a heavily stylized white or pale attribute. As *Śaṅkhapuruṣa*, it may be a male attendant holding a conch or standing beneath a conch-shaped emblem.

No surviving shell has a defensible continuous provenance from the epic narratives.

### 1.4 Kaumodakī

**Scripts and forms**

- Sanskrit: **कौमोदकी**, *Kaumodakī*
- Common variants: Kaumodaki, Koumodaki; modern misspellings include Komodaki or Gomodaki
- Generic designation: **गदा**, *gadā*, mace or club
- Personification: *Gadādevī*, “Mace-goddess,” usually grammatically and iconographically female

**Name.** The etymology is uncertain. Traditional explanations derive it from a name or epithet *Kumodaka*, or interpret it through delight or exhilaration, but no consensus historical derivation is established. The name should not be silently translated as though its etymology were certain.

**Epic description.** In the *Mahābhārata*’s Khāṇḍava episode, Varuṇa gives Kṛṣṇa a mace named Kaumodakī. It can slay every Daitya and, when hurled, makes a sound like thunder. This is the clearest custody transaction in the five weapons’ epic biographies.

The weapon may be carried or thrown. Iconographically it is commonly a long-shafted, top-heavy club. Kushan- and Gupta-period representations tend to show a relatively simple heavy head; later examples become segmented, fluted, lotus-like, globular, or richly ornamented.

**Personification.** Because *gadā* is grammatically feminine, the anthropomorphic mace is normally Gadādevī, an ornamented woman. She may hold a fly whisk or the mace itself, or stand with the mace emblem behind her. At Deogarh and in related Gupta art she belongs to the court of embodied divine weapons.

**Materials and measurements.** No stable primary description identifies historical timber, metal, alloy, length, or weight. Modern replicas in brass, bronze, steel, wood, resin, or precious metal are representations.

### 1.5 Śārṅga

**Scripts and forms**

- Sanskrit: **शार्ङ्ग**, *Śārṅga*
- Common forms: Sharnga, Sharanga, Saranga
- Full expression: *Śārṅgadhanus*, “the Śārṅga bow”
- Epithet of the bearer: *Śārṅgadhanvan*, “he whose bow is Śārṅga”

**Name and construction.** Sanskrit lexicography connects *śārṅga* with *śṛṅga*, “horn”; the adjective can mean “made of horn” or “horny.” This permits, but does not prove, interpretation as a composite or horn bow. The traditional Dhanurvedic category of horn bows cannot be used to reconstruct the divine bow’s exact construction.

**Physical description.** The literature presents it as a celestial, formidable bow, but supplies no single stable set of dimensions, materials, string, draw weight, inscriptions, or decoration. Later art generally renders it according to the bow technology familiar to the artist: a long curved bow in sculpture and manuscript painting, sometimes richly ornamented.

Śārṅga appears less consistently than conch, discus, and mace in the standard four-armed image. It occurs in multi-armed forms and in rare complete arrays of personified weapons.

### 1.6 Nandaka

**Scripts and forms**

- Sanskrit: **नन्दक**, *Nandaka*
- Also: *Nandakī*, “bearer of Nandaka,” as an epithet of Viṣṇu
- Generic designations: **खड्ग**, *khaḍga*, sword; **असि**, *asi*, sword
- Tamil form: **நந்தகம்**, *Nantakam*

**Name.** From the root *nand*, “rejoice, delight,” Nandaka is conventionally interpreted as “the delighter” or “that which gives joy.”

**Agni Purāṇa description.** Its fullest origin legend describes a blue-hued sword with a jeweled hilt in the setting of Brahmā’s sacrifice and the attack of the demon Loha. Textual summaries differ on whether the sword arises from the sacrifice, belongs initially to Loha and is seized by Viṣṇu, or is produced for Viṣṇu. The received chapter must therefore be cited directly rather than harmonized with later retellings.

**Symbolic anatomy.** The *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* equates the pure sword with knowledge (*jñāna* or *vidyā*) and its sheath with ignorance (*avidyā*). This yields the enduring “sword of knowledge” interpretation: it destroys error rather than merely bodies.

**Iconography.** Nandaka is rare in ordinary four-armed Viṣṇu images but appears in expanded forms and as a male *Khaḍgapuruṣa*. At the Gupta temple at Deogarh, a sword-bearing youth in the reclining Viṣṇu panel has been identified as Nandaka. A further weapon-person appears in the Madhu-Kaiṭabha composition at Mahabalipuram.

No historical sword has a continuous or testable claim to be Nandaka itself.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Methodological caution about “earliest”

The Sanskrit epics and Purāṇas are layered works transmitted orally and through repeatedly copied manuscripts. A story’s location in an “ancient” text does not produce an exact year of composition. The *Mahābhārata* was built over centuries, with much of it commonly placed between approximately the third century BCE and third or fourth century CE. The Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute’s Critical Edition was assembled between 1919 and 1966 by comparing manuscript recensions; older English translations often reflect the longer vulgate.

The Purāṇas present still greater dating difficulties. The *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* has been assigned dates ranging across much of the first millennium CE, while scholars commonly place the final form of the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* in the later first millennium. Consequently, this dossier gives textual strata or broad scholarly ranges, not fictitious precise dates.

### 2.2 Precursors: divine wheel and Vāsudeva’s attributes

The Vedas contain wheels, weapons, conches, bows, swords, and Viṣṇu, but this does not mean that all five later personal names are Vedic. General references to a wheel or weapon should not be retroactively renamed Sudarśana.

The earliest material evidence relevant to the mature tradition may be the controversial bilingual coins of the Indo-Greek king Agathocles, approximately 190–180 BCE. One figure has been identified as Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa holding a wheel and conch, paired with Saṃkarṣaṇa-Balarāma holding plough and pestle or club. The identification is influential but not unanimous: Osmund Bopearachchi and others have warned that individual objects in the figure’s hands may have been misread. Even if the Vāsudeva identification is accepted, the coin proves the paired attributes, not necessarily that the wheel and conch were already called Sudarśana and Pāñcajanya.

Kushan-period images from Mathura provide firmer evidence for the developing four-armed Vāsudeva-Viṣṇu type with mace and wheel. The conch becomes increasingly regular; the sword and bow remain less prominent.

### 2.3 Earliest secure named textual cluster

The *Mahābhārata* supplies early explicit attestations:

- **Pāñcajanya** is named as Kṛṣṇa’s conch and is blown at Kurukṣetra.
- **Kaumodakī** is named and transferred by Varuṇa to Kṛṣṇa in the Khāṇḍava episode.
- **Śārṅga** appears as the bow of Viṣṇu or Kṛṣṇa and produces the epithet *Śārṅgadhanvan*.
- **Sudarśana** or “the discus” appears as Kṛṣṇa’s summoned decapitating missile, though passages vary in use of the personal name versus generic *cakra*.
- **Nandaka/Nandakī** occurs in the wider epic and early post-epic Vaiṣṇava repertoire but is less narratively prominent.

A dramatic intermediate witness is Bhāsa’s *Dūtavākya*, difficult to date precisely but generally placed in the early centuries BCE or CE. In it, Kṛṣṇa summons his weapons and they appear as persons. This is significant evidence that personification was a literary idea before or alongside its Gupta sculptural efflorescence.

### 2.4 Competing origin stories

#### Sudarśana

At least three major origin lineages coexist:

1. **Agni/Varuṇa transaction — epic tradition.** In the Khāṇḍava narrative, Agni arranges the weapons needed by Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna. Varuṇa supplies Kaumodakī, while the text treats the discus as provided for Kṛṣṇa’s use. The *Mausala Parvan* later explicitly calls the discus “given by Agni.”

2. **Viśvakarman and the Sun — Purāṇic tradition.** Saṃjñā cannot endure the brilliance of her husband Sūrya. Her father Viśvakarman pares down or diminishes the Sun’s excess radiance and fashions divine weapons from it, including Viṣṇu’s discus. Lists of the products vary across retellings; the trident and other divine objects are often included. This makes Sudarśana condensed solar substance rather than forged earthly metal.

3. **Śiva’s gift — Śaiva and later Purāṇic tradition.** Viṣṇu worships Śiva with a thousand lotuses. When one is missing, he offers one of his lotus-like eyes; Śiva, satisfied, grants the discus. The *Vāmana Purāṇa* preserves another Śiva–Viṣṇu weapon story involving the testing or division of a great discus. These accounts give Śiva theological priority and should not be collapsed into the Viśvakarman account.

The narratives are sectarian alternatives, not different documented stages in the manufacture of one physical object.

#### Pāñcajanya

The principal Kṛṣṇa narrative is preserved in the *Harivaṃśa* and *Bhāgavata Purāṇa*. As a fee for their teacher Sāndīpani, Kṛṣṇa and Balarāma seek his dead or missing son. The Ocean says a marine demon named Pañcajana took him. Kṛṣṇa kills Pañcajana and takes the conch associated with him. The boy is not found inside it in the *Bhāgavata* version; Kṛṣṇa carries the conch to Yama’s realm, blows it, and restores the teacher’s son. The conch becomes known as Pāñcajanya.

Variants disagree about Pañcajana’s anatomy:

- a demon who inhabits a shell;
- a demon in the form of a conch;
- a marine monster whose shell or bodily casing becomes the conch;
- later popular retellings in which the conch is fashioned from the demon’s bones.

Only the first three are close to the primary textual ambiguity. “Made from his bones” should be labeled a later interpretation unless a particular source is supplied.

A second, widespread but textually variable tradition lists the conch among products of the churning of the ocean. It should not be substituted for the Pañcajana story.

#### Kaumodakī

The *Mahābhārata* gives the clearest origin-of-possession: Varuṇa hands the mace to Kṛṣṇa for the burning of the Khāṇḍava forest. It does not narrate a workshop or raw material.

Later summaries claim that Viśvakarman made the mace from the bones of a demon named Gada. The circulation of this story is documented in modern devotional literature, but its precise early textual foundation is considerably less secure than the Varuṇa episode.

#### Śārṅga

In one *Rāmāyaṇa* lineage, Viśvakarman makes two mighty bows so that the power of Śiva and Viṣṇu can be tested: Śiva’s bow and Viṣṇu’s bow. Viṣṇu proves superior; the bows then pass into terrestrial royal custody. The bow of Viṣṇu ultimately reaches Paraśurāma, who confronts Rāma with it after the breaking of Śiva’s bow. The identification of this unnamed or generically designated “bow of Viṣṇu” with Śārṅga is traditional but must be handled carefully where the verse does not itself give the name.

Other epic passages treat Śārṅga simply as Viṣṇu’s celestial bow, without a manufacture story. Later accounts assign it to Brahmā or Viśvakarman.

#### Nandaka

The *Agni Purāṇa* gives Nandaka an elaborate sacrificial origin. Brahmā performs a sacrifice on Mount Meru or beside the celestial Gaṅgā. The hundred-armed demon Loha interrupts it. Viṣṇu appears, wields or seizes Nandaka, and dismembers Loha. The fragments of the demon become terrestrial iron or the material of earthly weapons. The story is an aetiology of iron as well as of the sword.

Because online summaries differ over who initially possessed the sword, the cautious reconstruction is:

- sacrifice and demonic interruption;
- manifestation or acquisition of Nandaka by Viṣṇu;
- Loha’s killing and dismemberment;
- transformation of his fragments into iron.

The sword’s blue color and jeweled hilt belong to this developed Purāṇic description, not to the earliest general references to Viṣṇu as *Nandakī*.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

This is a narrative chain, not a physical archaeological provenance.

### 3.1 Sudarśana: custody by textual version

| Stage | Custodian/place | Mechanism | Evidence and status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primordial | Viṣṇu | Intrinsic divine attribute | General Vaiṣṇava theology; no transfer required |
| Solar-forging version | Sūrya → Viśvakarman → Viṣṇu | Excess solar radiance removed, fashioned, bestowed | Purāṇic tradition |
| Śaiva version | Śiva → Viṣṇu | Reward for worship and eye-offering | Śaiva/Purāṇic tradition |
| Khāṇḍava | Agni/Varuṇa’s divine arsenal → Kṛṣṇa | Supplied so Kṛṣṇa can aid Agni | *Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parvan |
| Khāṇḍava battlefield | Kṛṣṇa | Used to kill Daityas, Dānavas, and forest defenders | Epic narrative |
| Rājasūya at Indraprastha | Kṛṣṇa | Summoned mentally; used to decapitate Śiśupāla | *Mahābhārata*, Sabhā Parvan |
| Ambarīṣa episode | Viṣṇu sends it to protect Ambarīṣa | Autonomous pursuit of Durvāsas; calmed by Ambarīṣa’s prayer | *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 9.4–5 |
| Before destruction of Dvārakā | Kṛṣṇa/Yādava territory | Disappears upward into the firmament | *Mahābhārata*, Mausala Parvan |
| Transcendent restoration | Viṣṇu | Later theology treats it as eternally present | Religious interpretation |

The *Mausala Parvan* is the closest thing to a “loss report”: the weapon visibly leaves the Yādavas before Kṛṣṇa’s death. It is not found, inherited, buried, or accessioned. The text resolves the custody chain by removing it from the human world.

Later Purāṇic narratives are not necessarily situated between these epic links. They often reset the object in cyclical sacred time.

### 3.2 Pāñcajanya

| Stage | Custodian/place | Mechanism | Evidence and status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Marine realm at Prabhāsa | Pañcajana or the Ocean | Demon inhabits or embodies a shell | *Harivaṃśa*; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* |
| Ocean → Kṛṣṇa | Kṛṣṇa kills Pañcajana and takes the conch | Martial seizure | Primary narrative tradition |
| Yama’s realm | Kṛṣṇa | Blown to summon or announce Kṛṣṇa during recovery of Sāndīpani’s son | *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 10.45 |
| Dvārakā and campaigns | Kṛṣṇa | Personal battle trumpet | Epic and Purāṇic narrative |
| Kurukṣetra | Kṛṣṇa as Arjuna’s charioteer | Blown before battle and during combat | *Bhagavadgītā* 1.15; *Mahābhārata* |
| After Kṛṣṇa | Unstated | No secure narrative transfer or surviving shell | Documented gap |

Unlike Sudarśana, Pāñcajanya has no comparably explicit departure scene in the cited *Mausala Parvan* passages. Its post-Kṛṣṇa fate is unrecorded. Assertions that it remains hidden in a particular ocean, temple, cave, or celestial treasury are religious or folkloric claims unless independently sourced.

### 3.3 Kaumodakī

| Stage | Custodian/place | Mechanism | Evidence and status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Varuṇa’s treasury | Varuṇa | Prior custody unexplained | *Mahābhārata* |
| Khāṇḍava | Varuṇa → Kṛṣṇa, at Agni’s request | Gift or issue for battle | *Mahābhārata* Ādi Parvan |
| Khāṇḍava battlefield | Kṛṣṇa | Available against divine and demonic opposition | Epic narrative |
| Kurukṣetra period | Kṛṣṇa’s chariot arsenal | Daruka instructed to load Kaumodakī with discus, bow, arrows, and other weapons | *Mahābhārata* Drona Parvan |
| Later fate | Unstated | No physical recovery or subsequent human inheritance | Documented gap |

The mace’s custody is unusually explicit at acquisition but not at disappearance.

### 3.4 Śārṅga

The bow has several partially incompatible chains.

**Epic divine-bow chain**

1. It belongs to Viṣṇu.
2. Kṛṣṇa bears it as Viṣṇu’s manifestation.
3. In *Drona Parvan*, Kṛṣṇa orders his charioteer to prepare his bow and other weapons.
4. The later fate is unstated.

**Rāmāyaṇa dual-bow chain**

1. Viśvakarman makes two divine bows for Śiva and Viṣṇu.
2. Following their confrontation, the bows enter separate terrestrial lines.
3. Śiva’s bow reaches the royal house of Mithilā.
4. Viṣṇu’s bow passes through divine or sage custodians and reaches Paraśurāma.
5. Paraśurāma presents or challenges Rāma with it.
6. Rāma strings it and demonstrates his supremacy.
7. The subsequent chain varies.

The seam is crucial: identifying every “bow of Viṣṇu” in the *Rāmāyaṇa* as the identical Śārṅga of Kṛṣṇa may be traditional harmonization rather than something stated consistently by the earliest recoverable text.

### 3.5 Nandaka

| Stage | Custodian/place | Mechanism | Evidence and status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sacrificial arena at Meru | Produced, revealed, or held during Brahmā’s sacrifice | Mythic manifestation; versions differ | *Agni Purāṇa* |
| Loha conflict | Viṣṇu and/or Loha | Viṣṇu acquires and uses the sword | Purāṇic narrative |
| Divine custody | Viṣṇu | Becomes permanent named sword and personified servant | Vaiṣṇava literature |
| Iconic retinue | Appears among weapon-persons | Summoned as a being or portrayed as a youth with sword | Drama and sculpture |
| Later fate | Eternal with Viṣṇu; no terrestrial artifact | Theological continuity, not physical provenance | Religious tradition |

### 3.6 The ensemble as incarnating beings

Śrīvaiṣṇava traditions associate divine attendants and Āḻvārs with Viṣṇu’s attributes. Lists are not wholly uniform. Pāñcajanya, Sudarśana, Nandaka, Kaumodakī, and Śārṅga may be treated as partial incarnations serving the earthly work of Viṣṇu. Such claims concern theological identity, not physical transfer of a weapon into a human body.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Do the five weapons physically exist?

**Documented finding:** no historical artifact has a demonstrated chain of custody identifying it as any one of the five divine originals.

There are countless consecrated manifestations:

- temple discs and conches;
- *cakrattāḻvār* icons;
- processional weapons;
- *śaṅkha-cakra* branding stamps;
- miniature emblems installed in icons;
- replica swords, bows, and maces;
- archaeological sculptures of the *āyudhapuruṣas*.

Within ritual systems, a consecrated icon may be treated as the real presence of Sudarśana or another weapon. That is a theological identity created by consecration, not a museum-style claim that the icon is the same worked object Kṛṣṇa carried at Khāṇḍava.

### 4.2 Competing Sudarśana origins

The Viśvakarman, Agni/Varuṇa, and Śiva accounts cannot all be read as a single literal fabrication history without adding harmonizing assumptions. Their differences are historically meaningful:

- the solar-forging account relates the discus to radiance and cosmic energy;
- the Agni account integrates it with Kṛṣṇa’s epic arming;
- the Śiva account places its authorization within Śaiva supremacy and interdivine gift exchange.

No laboratory test can adjudicate among them because no candidate original has been submitted.

### 4.3 Is Pāñcajanya a shell, a demon’s body, or a manufactured instrument?

The primary narratives’ grammar and translation permit several readings of Pañcajana as:

- a demon dwelling in a shell;
- a demon having the form of a shell;
- a marine being whose shell is taken.

Later art almost invariably regularizes the object into a recognizable conch. Statements that it is literally made from a demon’s bones are not uniformly supported by the earliest passages.

### 4.4 Is the Viṣṇu bow in the *Rāmāyaṇa* Śārṅga?

This remains a textual-identity problem. Tradition often says yes. A critical account must note that names, divine-bow stories, and custody sequences differ. “Viṣṇu’s bow,” “the celestial bow,” and “Śārṅga” are not automatically interchangeable in every recension.

### 4.5 The Agathocles coin dispute

Coins attributed to Agathocles of Bactria are frequently presented as the earliest images of Balarāma and Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa, with the latter holding conch and wheel. The identification rests on the paired iconography and South Asian legends, but specialists have disputed whether the apparent conch might be another object and whether every feature has been correctly read.

Even on the favorable interpretation, the coin supplies no labels *Sudarśana* or *Pāñcajanya*. It documents an iconographic pairing, not the names or custody stories.

### 4.6 The “five” versus the canonical four attributes

A four-armed Viṣṇu commonly carries:

- conch,
- discus,
- mace,
- lotus.

This does not mean the bow and sword were lost or replaced in a physical incident. It reflects iconographic selection. In multi-armed forms, textual enumerations, hymns, and embodied retinues, Śārṅga and Nandaka return.

### 4.7 Scientific testing and forgery

There is no reported radiocarbon, metallographic, isotopic, residue, or forensic study of a candidate “original” weapon because no serious candidate original is institutionally recognized.

Modern objects marketed as “real Sudarshan Chakra,” “original Panchajanya,” or archaeologically recovered divine weapons require ordinary provenance scrutiny. Without excavation context, accession history, material analysis, and independent publication, such claims are unverified. Computer-generated images and fabricated news stories are not material evidence.

The astronomical phenomenon known as “Sudarshan Chakra” in devotional comparison, weapon-shaped cloud photographs, and claims that advanced machinery has reproduced the divine discus are modern analogy, not authentication.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Viṣṇu/Nārāyaṇa

Primary divine owner of all five. The weapons embody his destructive, protective, cognitive, royal, and cosmic powers. In Vaiṣṇava theology they remain inseparable from him even when acting independently.

### Kṛṣṇa/Vāsudeva

Principal epic bearer. Named connections include:

- acquisition of Kaumodakī and the discus in the Khāṇḍava episode;
- killing Pañcajana and taking Pāñcajanya;
- blowing Pāñcajanya at Kurukṣetra;
- killing Śiśupāla with the discus;
- carrying or preparing Śārṅga, Kaumodakī, and the discus;
- disappearance of the discus before his death.

### Agni

Instigator of the Khāṇḍava arming. He needs Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna to repel Indra and arranges divine weapons. The episode makes him a donor or procurement agent rather than an enduring owner.

### Varuṇa

Keeper of the divine treasury from which Kaumodakī is transferred to Kṛṣṇa. He also supplies Arjuna’s Gāṇḍīva, quivers, and chariot. His connection is one of the most discrete custody links in the corpus.

### Viśvakarman

Celestial maker in later origin traditions:

- forms Sudarśana from solar radiance;
- makes the two bows of Śiva and Viṣṇu in a *Rāmāyaṇa* lineage;
- is credited in some later traditions with Kaumodakī or other divine arms.

The attributions are version-dependent.

### Śiva

Donor or authorizer of Sudarśana in Śaiva Purāṇic narratives. In the lotus-eye story, the gift follows Viṣṇu’s devotion. Elsewhere the two gods test divine weapons. Śiva is not uniformly represented as the discus’s maker.

### Sūrya, Saṃjñā, and Viśvakarman’s family

Their domestic crisis generates the solar-origin account. Saṃjñā’s inability to endure Sūrya’s heat motivates Viśvakarman’s reduction of his brilliance; the removed substance becomes divine weapon material.

### Pañcajana

The marine demon or shell-being killed by Kṛṣṇa. His name becomes attached to the conch. He is a prior possessor, inhabitant, embodiment, or source material depending on the version.

### Sāndīpani and his son

Sāndīpani’s request for his son’s return sends Kṛṣṇa into the sea and then to Yama. The teacher is not the conch’s owner in the primary narrative; he is the cause of the recovery expedition.

### Yama

Hears Pāñcajanya in his realm and receives Kṛṣṇa during the recovery of Sāndīpani’s son. The conch functions as a trans-cosmic summons.

### Arjuna

Kṛṣṇa’s ally at Khāṇḍava and chariot passenger at Kurukṣetra. Pāñcajanya’s sound frames his instruction in the *Bhagavadgītā*. The weapons are not transferred to him.

### Daruka

Kṛṣṇa’s charioteer. In *Drona Parvan*, Kṛṣṇa instructs him to prepare the chariot with Kaumodakī, discus, bow, arrows, and other equipment, and to respond to Pāñcajanya’s blast. Daruka is a documented narrative keeper and transporter, not owner.

### Śiśupāla

Killed by Sudarśana at Yudhiṣṭhira’s Rājasūya. The episode establishes the discus as both execution weapon and proof of Kṛṣṇa’s superhuman status.

### Ambarīṣa and Durvāsas

In the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa*, Sudarśana protects King Ambarīṣa, destroys a fiery being created by Durvāsas, and pursues the sage through cosmic regions. Neither Brahmā nor Śiva can stop it. Viṣṇu directs Durvāsas back to Ambarīṣa, whose prayer finally pacifies the wheel. The episode gives a devotee temporary effective control over the weapon through devotion and intercession.

### Brahmā and Loha

Brahmā’s sacrifice provides the setting for Nandaka’s manifestation. Loha’s body, cut apart by the sword, becomes iron in the aetiological legend.

### Bhāsa

Early Sanskrit dramatist whose *Dūtavākya* turns the weapons into speaking or visible dramatic persons. His association is literary, not custodial.

### Kālidāsa

In *Raghuvaṃśa* 10.60, Viṣṇu’s weapon forms appear as small or dwarf-like figures. The verse became important to art historians interpreting Gupta *āyudhapuruṣas*.

### Vedānta Deśika (1268–1369)

Śrīvaiṣṇava theologian and poet who composed hymns to Sudarśana and arrays of divine weapons. His *Sudarśanāṣṭaka* and *Ṣoḍaśāyudha Stotra* helped define the fierce, protective, multi-weapon Sudarśana of South Indian devotion. A tradition says he composed Sudarśana hymns during an epidemic near Tirupputkuzhi; the healing is a religious tradition, not independently documented epidemiology.

### Gupta rulers, especially Candragupta II

Gupta royal imagery appropriated the wheel-person. The rare *Cakravikrama* coin type has been interpreted as showing a king receiving objects or power from Cakrapuruṣa. This linked divine weapon, universal sovereignty, and royal legitimacy. The exact identification of the figure was debated before numismatic and iconographic comparisons favored Cakrapuruṣa.

### Temple institutions and Śrīvaiṣṇava lineages

South Indian Viṣṇu temples preserve Sudarśana as an independently worshipped deity, often with Narasiṃha on the reverse. Śrīvaiṣṇava teachers also administer the heated conch-and-disc marks of initiation. These institutions possess consecrated embodiments and emblems, not an archaeologically continuous epic original.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Martial functions

**Sudarśana** cuts, burns, pursues, destroys armies, and defeats other weapons. Its capacity to return or reappear in Kṛṣṇa’s hand makes it a supernatural missile rather than an ordinary historical war-quoit.

**Pāñcajanya** signals commencement, presence, confidence, victory, and alarm. At Kurukṣetra it belongs to a named orchestra of conches. Its blast is described as physically and psychologically overwhelming.

**Kaumodakī** is the close-combat and thrown force weapon. Its thunderous sound links physical power with storm imagery.

**Śārṅga** represents distance warfare, royal martial competence, and inexhaustible divine archery.

**Nandaka** cuts both enemies and ignorance. Later exegesis makes knowledge itself a weapon.

### 6.2 Cosmological symbolism

The *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* interprets Viṣṇu’s attributes as manifestations of cosmic principles. Common correspondences include:

- Kaumodakī — intellect, power, or primordial knowledge;
- Pāñcajanya — the elemental or sense-producing aspect of ego;
- Sudarśana — mind, cosmic ordering, time, or divine vision;
- Nandaka — knowledge, with ignorance as its sheath;
- Śārṅga — the sense-related or action-producing principle in some interpretive systems.

Lists differ between texts and commentaries. A single modern chart should not be presented as universal ancient doctrine.

### 6.3 Sudarśana as a deity

The *Ahirbudhnya Saṃhitā*, an important Pāñcarātra text, develops Sudarśana into a central cosmological and ritual power. It links the wheel with mantras, royal protection, military success, and universal sovereignty. A king worshipping the divine sovereign within the Sudarśana diagram seeks *cakravartin* status.

This is not merely a metaphorical weapon cult. Sudarśana receives:

- installation;
- mantra recitation;
- fire offerings;
- ablution;
- independent shrines;
- processions;
- petitions for protection, health, removal of enemies, and restoration of order.

### 6.4 *Śaṅkha-cakra-lāñchana* and initiation

In the Śrīvaiṣṇava *pañcasaṃskāra* initiation, heated metal stamps representing Viṣṇu’s conch and discus are applied to the shoulders. Practice, gender rules, temperature, permanence, and contemporary observance vary by lineage.

A nineteenth–twentieth-century brass branding stamp in Singapore’s national collection documents the material culture of the rite. Its museum description states that heated conch and discus stamps were applied to male initiates, with gentler or non-permanent application reported for women and children. This is evidence for a historical ritual tool, not Pāñcajanya itself.

### 6.5 Temple processions and water rites

Consecrated Sudarśana icons continue to be carried in festivals and bathed. At Tirumala and other southern temples, *Cakrasnāna* places the processional Sudarśana emblem in sacred water at the culmination of major festivals. Devotees enter or touch the water in conjunction with the rite. The ritual object is a temple-owned embodiment under priestly custody.

### 6.6 Protective hymns

The *Pañcāyudha Stotra* invokes each of the five weapons for protection. Vedānta Deśika’s hymns expand Sudarśana’s arsenal to sixteen weapons. In these texts the weapons are addressed directly, not merely mentioned as Viṣṇu’s possessions.

Reported benefits—protection from fear, disease, enemies, poison, or adverse supernatural influences—are claims of the hymn traditions. They are not clinical findings.

### 6.7 Royal and political symbolism

The wheel had a political life wider than Sudarśana:

- *cakravartin*, “wheel-turning universal ruler”;
- royal wheel standards;
- Gupta coinage and Vaiṣṇava kingship;
- the wheel as cosmic and territorial order.

Not every royal wheel is Sudarśana. Identification requires context, accompanying figures, inscriptions, or an established iconographic type.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Weapons

### 7.1 Sudarśana and Śiśupāla — early epic attestation

At Yudhiṣṭhira’s Rājasūya, Kṛṣṇa receives the first honor. Śiśupāla objects and abuses him. Kṛṣṇa explains that he promised Śiśupāla’s mother to forgive one hundred offenses. When the limit is exceeded, Kṛṣṇa thinks of the discus; it appears in his hand and cuts off Śiśupāla’s head. A radiant energy emerges from the dead king and enters Kṛṣṇa.

**Function of the story:** legitimates Kṛṣṇa’s preeminence in a contested royal assembly and makes the wheel an instrument of both punishment and liberation.

**Tier:** early epic narrative, though exact verse history remains subject to the layered transmission of the *Mahābhārata*.

### 7.2 Sudarśana, Ambarīṣa, and Durvāsas — Purāṇic theological elaboration

Durvāsas becomes angry because Ambarīṣa has ritually broken a fast before feeding him. He produces a fiery attacker. Sudarśana destroys it and pursues him. Durvāsas flees to Brahmā, Śiva, and Viṣṇu; none will override the protection accorded to the devotee. Ambarīṣa’s prayer finally calms the weapon.

The king’s hymn identifies Sudarśana with fire, Sun, Moon, water, earth, sky, air, senses, sacrifice, truth, and dharma; calls it thousand-spoked, swift as mind, and destroyer of weapons; and addresses it as a conscious lord.

**Function:** subordinates ascetic anger and even divine hierarchy to the bond between Viṣṇu and his devotee.

**Tier:** later Purāṇic theological narrative.

### 7.3 The solar forge

Saṃjñā cannot withstand Sūrya’s heat. Viśvakarman reduces the Sun’s excess splendor and fashions divine arms from it, including Sudarśana.

**Variants:** the number and identity of objects made from the solar fragments differ. Modern retellings frequently produce a standardized list that may not match a particular Purāṇa.

**Function:** explains the discus’s radiance, fire, speed, and solar appearance.

**Tier:** Purāṇic origin tradition.

### 7.4 Viṣṇu’s lotus-eye sacrifice

Viṣṇu offers Śiva one thousand lotuses. Śiva conceals one to test him. Finding the count short and being lotus-eyed, Viṣṇu offers his own eye. Śiva restores or rewards him and grants Sudarśana.

**Function:** Śaiva–Vaiṣṇava theological reciprocity, frequently used to demonstrate either Viṣṇu’s devotion or Śiva’s generosity.

**Tier:** later Purāṇic and sectarian tradition. It is not part of the Khāṇḍava acquisition account.

### 7.5 Pāñcajanya and the teacher’s son

Kṛṣṇa descends into the sea at Prabhāsa, kills Pañcajana, and takes the conch. Because the missing boy is not inside the being in the *Bhāgavata* version, Kṛṣṇa goes to Saṃyamanī, Yama’s city, and blows the conch. Yama returns Sāndīpani’s son.

**Function:** the shell is trophy, passport, summons, and proof that Kṛṣṇa can cross the boundaries of sea and death.

**Tier:** *Harivaṃśa* and Purāṇic Kṛṣṇa narrative.

### 7.6 Nandaka and Loha

Loha interrupts Brahmā’s sacrifice. Viṣṇu defeats him with Nandaka. The fragments of Loha become iron and the sources of mundane weapons.

**Function:** connects sacred sacrifice, cosmic sword, demon body, and terrestrial metallurgy. The demon’s name *Loha* itself means metal or iron, making the story an overt aetiology.

**Tier:** Purāṇic elaboration.

### 7.7 Śārṅga and the divine bow contest

Viśvakarman constructs bows for Śiva and Viṣṇu. The gods’ superiority is tested, and Viṣṇu prevails. The bows then enter royal and sage custody, eventually structuring Rāma’s encounters with Sītā’s bow test and Paraśurāma.

**Identity caution:** the bow called “Viṣṇu’s bow” is not named Śārṅga in every passage or recension.

**Tier:** epic divine-bow tradition with later harmonization.

### 7.8 Sudarśana and Satī’s body

Later Śākta pilgrimage traditions say Viṣṇu used Sudarśana to divide Satī’s corpse while grief-stricken Śiva carried it, producing the *Śakti Pīṭhas* wherever pieces fell. The number and location of the pīṭhas vary substantially—four, eighteen, fifty-one, fifty-two, or 108 in different systems.

This story is not the uniform earliest account of the pīṭhas. Regional texts and temples map the weapon’s act onto competing sacred geographies.

**Function:** explains the distribution of Śākta shrines and integrates Viṣṇu’s weapon into a Śiva–Satī narrative.

**Tier:** later Purāṇic and regional pilgrimage elaboration.

### 7.9 Unverified modern claims

The following should not be presented as ancient facts without a precise primary citation:

- Sudarśana was a technologically engineered aircraft or guided nuclear weapon.
- It rotated at a measured modern speed.
- It was constructed with mercury, uranium, lasers, or electrical machinery.
- NASA photographed or recovered it.
- a specific modern conch is Pāñcajanya;
- an excavated sword or bow has been scientifically authenticated as Nandaka or Śārṅga;
- all five weapons have fixed dimensions encoded in a single ancient manual.

Such claims are modern speculation, miscaptioned imagery, or devotional extrapolation unless supported by evidence not presently available.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 The legendary originals

**Status:** unverified as historical artifacts; no known physical location.

Within living Vaiṣṇava theology, they remain eternally present with Viṣṇu, manifest in his icons, and available through mantra and worship. Within the epic narrative, Sudarśana ascends into the firmament before Kṛṣṇa’s death. The other four lack an equally clear terminal custody report.

There is:

- no accession number for an original;
- no current human legal owner;
- no documented repatriation claim;
- no laboratory dossier;
- no continuous chain from an epic custodian to a modern institution.

### 8.2 Material embodiments and representations

Representative—not exhaustive—surviving objects include:

- **LACMA, Los Angeles:** *Vishnu’s Personified Discus (Chakra Purusha)*, fifth–sixth century, stone; a Gupta/post-Gupta manifestation of Sudarśana.
- **Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York:** four-armed Viṣṇu sculptures and a tenth–eleventh-century Punjab stela with conch, discus, mace, and personified attendants.
- **National Museum of Asian Art, Washington:** twelfth-century black-schist *Vishnu with Consorts*, accession F1927.7; the museum notes that provenance research is underway. It is currently not on view.
- **British Museum, London:** damaged Viṣṇu sculptures and a circa-1830 Tiruchirappalli painting showing the processional icons of Viṣṇu and his consorts, museum number 1992,0410,0.1.18.
- **Deogarh, Uttar Pradesh:** the sixth-century Dashavatara temple’s reclining Viṣṇu panel, including an important gathering of personified weapons. The panel remains an architectural sculpture, though individual identifications and condition require current site documentation.
- **Government Museum Chennai and related Indian collections:** Chola and later images of Cakrapuruṣa and Gadādevī.
- **South Indian temples:** numerous consecrated Sudarśana–Narasimha icons and processional discs under temple control.
- **Asian Civilisations Museum/National Heritage Board collection, Singapore:** nineteenth–twentieth-century brass branding tools documenting *śaṅkha-cakra* initiation.

These objects have their own separate provenances. None should be called “the original Sudarshana” or “the original Kaumodaki.”

### 8.3 Access and conservation

Access varies:

- museum objects may be displayed, stored, or under provenance research;
- archaeological reliefs are generally publicly accessible subject to site regulation;
- temple icons may be visible only during prescribed worship;
- processional and festival icons may be handled only by authorized priests;
- ritual branding implements may remain monastic property or museum artifacts.

No single institution controls the identity of all five. Authority is distributed among textual scholars, museums, archaeological agencies, temples, hereditary priests, monastic teachers, and devotional communities.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 National and political imagery

The *cakra* became a powerful modern Indian emblem, most prominently the twenty-four-spoked Aśoka Cakra on the national flag. That wheel is historically identified with the Mauryan/Aśokan dharma-wheel, not with Sudarśana. Popular devotional discourse sometimes conflates or compares them, but they are distinct iconographic genealogies.

Political language frequently calls a military system, policy, or leader “Sudarshan Chakra” to evoke speed, precision, protection, and irresistible force. Such metaphor does not establish continuity with the mythic weapon.

### 9.2 Print, film, television, comics, and games

Calendar art and mass printing standardized Sudarśana as a thin, serrated, flaming golden disc balanced on Kṛṣṇa’s finger. Early sculpture often shows a thicker wheel held or supported differently. The now-familiar fingertip-spinning pose is therefore only one historical visualization.

Twentieth-century mythological cinema, the televised *Mahabharat* and *Ramayan*, Amar Chitra Katha comics, animation, devotional serials, and digital visual effects established several modern conventions:

- Sudarśana as a glowing, self-guided energy disc;
- Pāñcajanya as a large milk-white right-turning shell;
- Kaumodakī as an enormous gold mace;
- Śārṅga as an ornate longbow;
- Nandaka as a jeweled blue or gold sword.

These visual details often combine Purāṇic adjectives with modern fantasy design.

In role-playing games, comics, anime-influenced art, and online fiction, “Sudarshan Chakra” is frequently reclassified as a boomerang, buzz saw, energy weapon, or magical projectile. This materially shapes contemporary expectations but is not evidence for ancient construction.

### 9.3 Replicas and commerce

Replicas are sold as:

- brass or copper discs;
- miniature conches;
- ritual mace, sword, and bow sets;
- jewelry and pendants;
- vehicle emblems;
- temple souvenirs;
- weapons for festival tableaux and actors;
- digitally fabricated cosplay objects.

Commercial listings sometimes call a replica “original” in the sense of authentic devotional merchandise. That wording must not be mistaken for archaeological authenticity.

### 9.4 Contemporary devotion

Sudarśana homa, Sudarśana mantra recitation, *Pañcāyudha Stotra* recitation, *Cakrasnāna*, and conch-disc initiation remain practiced. Their living status means that the weapons are not merely obsolete literary motifs.

Pāñcajanya also names institutions, periodicals, organizations, products, and public campaigns. These borrow the conch’s associations with proclamation and mobilization.

### 9.5 Public controversies

Current popular disputes concern:

- whether the weapons encode lost advanced technology;
- whether their stories are literal history, sacred narrative, symbolism, or all three;
- sectarian priority in Sudarśana’s origin;
- whether early coins show Vāsudeva’s conch and wheel;
- which modern visual design is “scripturally accurate”;
- whether ritual branding should be understood as initiation, bodily discipline, heritage, or harm.

No consensus encompasses religious, historical, and archaeological approaches.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Philological reconstruction

The main obstacle is textual stratification. The names and episodes are dispersed through:

- the *Mahābhārata* and its *Bhagavadgītā*;
- the *Harivaṃśa*;
- the *Rāmāyaṇa*;
- the *Viṣṇu*, *Bhāgavata*, *Agni*, *Vāmana*, *Śiva*, *Skanda*, and other Purāṇas;
- Pāñcarātra Saṃhitās;
- classical drama and poetry;
- medieval stotras and iconographic manuals.

The BORI Critical Edition is indispensable for determining which *Mahābhārata* passages are supported across manuscript traditions. Ganguli’s public-domain translation remains useful but represents a nineteenth-century edition and vocabulary; “adamant,” for example, is a translator’s rendering, not a mineralogical identification.

### 10.2 The decisive art-historical study

C. Sivaramamurti’s “The Weapons of Viṣṇu” remains foundational. It classifies *āyudhapuruṣas* by form:

1. dwarf weapon-persons;
2. persons with the weapon rising behind or over the head;
3. persons carrying their weapon;
4. further composite and regional arrangements.

It links literary personification to Gupta, Deogarh, Rajgir, Mathura, Mahabalipuram, Bengal, and Chola material. Its date and terminology require supplementation by newer archaeology, but the typology remains influential.

W. E. Begley’s *Viṣṇu’s Flaming Wheel: The Iconography of the Sudarśana-Cakra* concentrated on the transformation from attribute to autonomous deity and remains central to Sudarśana studies.

### 10.3 Coinage and kingship

The *Cakravikrama* coins of Candragupta II generated debate over whether the reverse figure was Viṣṇu, a goddess, or Cakrapuruṣa. Comparative iconography led scholars including V. S. Agrawala and Sivaramamurti toward Cakrapuruṣa. The interpretation makes the coin a compact image of divine weapon-power granted to the king.

The Agathocles coins raise a different issue: the identification of the South Asian figures and their attributes. They may document conch-and-wheel Vāsudeva imagery by approximately 180 BCE, but the reading of individual objects and the relationship to later Viṣṇu iconography remain debated.

### 10.4 The transition from object to person

Literature personifies weapons before the surviving Gupta sculptural corpus. Bhāsa and Kālidāsa supply textual analogues; fourth–sixth-century art makes the weapon court visible. The direction of influence is unlikely to be simple. Ritual invocation, royal ideology, drama, and sculpture probably participated in the same broad development.

### 10.5 Sudarśana’s Tantric and Pāñcarātra expansion

The *Ahirbudhnya Saṃhitā* transforms Sudarśana from one weapon into a comprehensive power of action, protection, mantra, and sovereignty. Otto Schrader’s study of Pāñcarātra literature was an early major scholarly entry point. Later work examines how the two-armed weapon-person developed into the many-armed southern Sudarśana deity.

The date of the *Ahirbudhnya Saṃhitā* and the chronology of individual iconographic prescriptions remain debated. Textual prescription cannot automatically date a sculpture; nor can the survival date of a sculpture alone date the doctrine.

### 10.6 Discredited or weak theories

Weak methodologies include:

- treating every wheel in Indian art as Sudarśana;
- identifying generic ancient conches as Pāñcajanya;
- taking a late Purāṇic fabrication story as the earliest record;
- assigning exact modern materials from allegorical statements;
- citing a devotional retelling without identifying its primary source;
- harmonizing contradictory Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava origins into one historical manufacture;
- claiming archaeological discovery from an unsourced social-media image;
- dating an epic episode to the supposed calendar year of the Kurukṣetra war rather than to the text’s compositional history.

### 10.7 State of the evidence

Substantial evidence remains difficult to access:

- many Sanskrit works lack complete modern critical editions and translations;
- temple bronzes and processional implements may lack published catalogues;
- colonial-era collecting histories remain incomplete;
- museum records often identify only “discus,” “conch,” or “mace,” not the personal weapon;
- iconographic identification can depend on damaged attributes;
- regional vernacular retellings have not been exhaustively indexed.

A full five-weapon stemma across all Sanskrit and regional recensions remains a desideratum.
