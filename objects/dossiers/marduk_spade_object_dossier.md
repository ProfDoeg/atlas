# The spade/marru of Marduk: Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `the_spade_marru_of_marduk_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary conclusion

The “spade of Marduk” is not one securely identified, continuously surviving artifact. It is an ancient Mesopotamian divine emblem instantiated in many different things: a possibly three-dimensional cultic implement invoked in Old Babylonian legal proceedings; carved or engraved images on cylinder seals, stamp seals, kudurrus and stelae; monumental Assyrian reliefs; and at least one related bronze implement dedicated to Marduk’s son Nabû at Chogha Zanbil.

Accordingly, there is no defensible single chain of custody from ancient Babylon to a modern museum. The object’s recoverable “life” consists of:

1. an incompletely documented cult object or class of objects called the divine spade of Marduk;
2. a stable iconographic type transmitted through hundreds of individually made representations;
3. surviving archaeological embodiments whose separate provenances can be reconstructed;
4. later interpretations, including a politically charged Assyrian reshaping of the emblem and modern confusion with a spear, weapon, playing-card spade, or “rocket.”

This dossier tracks the emblematic object-class and identifies representative physical embodiments without treating any representation as “the original.”

### Evidentiary labels

- **Primary/documented:** ancient inscription, excavated object, excavation record, or museum accession record.
- **Scientific/technical:** material or manufacturing determination based on examination.
- **Scholarly inference:** interpretation accepted or proposed by modern specialists.
- **Disputed:** two or more plausible identifications remain.
- **Tradition/myth:** ancient theological narrative rather than recoverable object history.
- **Modern invention:** a recent claim unsupported by ancient evidence.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity and class

The object is a **divine emblem and probable cult implement** belonging principally to Marduk, city-god of Babylon and eventually head of the Babylonian pantheon. In modern Assyriological literature it is conventionally called:

- **English:** spade, shovel, or hoe of Marduk;
- **Akkadian:** *marru*, “spade/hoe,” conventionally normalized with a doubled *r*;
- **Sumerian/logographic writing:** **GIŠ.MAR**, with GIŠ functioning as the wooden-object determinative;
- **general category:** a divine symbol or emblem, for which Akkadian *šurinnu* is sometimes used.

The specialized iconographic study by Nils C. Ritter defines it as a long-handled implement with a triangular blade and identifies its primary divine referent as Marduk. The term *marru* is an ordinary implement-name, not an otherwise known proper name unique to a legendary relic. The phrase “Marduk’s marru” therefore means “Marduk’s spade,” not necessarily “the artifact named Marru.” [Ritter, “Spade,” *Iconography of Deities and Demons*](https://www.religionswissenschaft.uzh.ch/idd/prepublications/e_idd_spade.pdf)

### Ancient written identifications

The strongest iconographic control is a Kassite-period kudurru on which the triangular blade itself bears **𒀭𒀫𒌓, dAMAR.UTU**, the standard logographic spelling of Marduk’s name. That inscription establishes that at least this emblem was intentionally identified as Marduk’s, rather than merely resembling an agricultural implement. Ritter catalogues the piece as Louvre **SB 3224**, limestone, from Susa, dated to the reign of Meli-Šipak, approximately 1188–1174 BCE. [Ritter, pp. 1, 3](https://www.religionswissenschaft.uzh.ch/idd/prepublications/e_idd_spade.pdf)

Marduk’s divine name also occurs syllabically as *ma-ru-tu-uk*, *mar-duk*, and related forms, as well as logographically as dAMAR.UD/UTU. The etymology of **Marduk** is disputed; the traditional reading of AMAR.UTU as “calf of the sun-god” may be learned interpretation rather than the historical origin of the name. Biblical and later forms include Hebrew *Mĕrōdāk/Merodach* and Greek *Mardochaios*. [ORACC, “Marduk”](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/listofdeities/marduk/index.html)

### Iconographic construction

Four principal forms have been distinguished:

1. **Basic type.** A vertical shaft terminating in a triangular, tapered, rounded, solid, framed, or hollow blade. It may stand on the ground, a baseline, a pedestal, a temple-like shrine, or a reclining *mušḫuššu* dragon.

2. **Crossbeam type.** A shaft with a horizontal bar immediately below the triangular head. The bar may be narrow, swollen, or doubled. It is especially common on first-millennium seals.

3. **Globe-and-pike type.** A very slender shaft with a central globe and a pointed upper end, usually shown with Nabû’s stylus on a pedestal before a worshipper.

4. **Tasselled type.** An elongated, often chevron-patterned shaft with paired ribbons, fringes, beads, or tassels below the head. A Neo-Assyrian seal indicates that the shaft could be represented as completely wrapped in cloth, the tassels apparently being the exposed ends of that wrapping. The meaning is unresolved; Ursula Seidl proposed a connection with Akkadian *riksu*, “bond,” “band,” or “ritual arrangement.” [Ritter, “Spade”](https://www.religionswissenschaft.uzh.ch/idd/prepublications/e_idd_spade.pdf)

The emblem is normally upright. That orientation helps distinguish it from Nabû’s wedge or stylus, although damaged or schematic examples remain ambiguous.

### Materials

No single material defines the emblem because most surviving examples are images:

- low-relief carving on limestone, marble, diorite, basalt, dolerite, alabaster, or unidentified dark stone;
- intaglio on hematite, chalcedony, carnelian, jasper, agate, lapis lazuli, sardonyx, serpentine, rock crystal, garnet, limestone, and earthenware seals;
- clay relief or appliqué on an incense-burner fragment;
- possibly metal in full-size ritual implements.

Ritter’s catalogue includes approximately seventy-eight examples, more than half without recorded archaeological provenance. The assemblage therefore documents the emblem securely but cannot yield one continuous physical biography. [Ritter, pp. 3–5](https://www.religionswissenschaft.uzh.ch/idd/prepublications/e_idd_spade.pdf)

### Representative physical embodiments

#### Louvre SB 3224

- limestone kudurru;
- approximately 18 × 12 cm;
- found at Susa;
- made in Babylonia under Meli-Šipak, approximately 1188–1174 BCE;
- blade inscribed dAMAR.UTU, “Marduk”;
- present institutional attribution: Musée du Louvre.

This is the most important surviving object for authenticating the emblem’s identity.

#### Metropolitan Museum of Art 1985.45

- upper fragment of a polished limestone kudurru;
- Babylonia, Second Dynasty of Isin, approximately 1156–1025 BCE;
- 16.5 × 10 × 18.4 cm;
- shows a *mušḫuššu* facing a temple façade or altar bearing Marduk’s spade;
- fragmentary, with only a small part of the inscription surviving;
- acquired through the Rogers Fund in 1985;
- not presently on view. [Metropolitan Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/327048)

#### British Museum 89361 / N.1204

- large white-veined lapis-lazuli cylinder seal;
- Middle Assyrian, approximately 1400–1300 BCE;
- length 2.81 inches;
- Marduk’s triangular, herringbone-decorated spade stands on a reclining *mušḫuššu* and striped rectangular altar;
- paired tassels are indicated, though one survives only in traces;
- an original cuneiform inscription was erased and crude guilloche ornament added over its ends;
- excavated by Austen Henry Layard;
- not on display. [British Museum collection record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_N-1204)

The museum’s dating is earlier than the principal securely controlled corpus of tasselled examples. Its cultural attribution and iconographic interpretation should therefore be retained with the catalogue’s own qualifications.

#### British Museum 89810

- banded grey-and-brown agate cylinder seal;
- approximately 729–700 BCE in Ritter’s catalogue;
- Marduk’s tasselled spade, with chevron-decorated handle, and Nabû’s stylus stand on altars beneath a winged solar disk;
- worshipper and armed divine figures occupy the central scene. [British Museum collection record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1873-0723-1)

#### Morgan Seal 692

- bluish chalcedony cylinder seal;
- southern Mesopotamia, approximately 810–790 BCE;
- 28 × 13 mm;
- worshipper before Marduk’s spade and Nabû’s stylus on a platform;
- an inscription was erased and replaced by a star and linear marks;
- acquired by J. Pierpont Morgan between 1885 and 1908. [Morgan Library & Museum](https://www.themorgan.org/seals-and-tablets/84316)

#### British Museum 1983,0101.305

- dyed sardonyx cylinder seal;
- probably southern Mesopotamia, approximately 700–539 BCE;
- height 2.9 cm, diameter 1.6 cm;
- two worshippers or priests: one before the moon-god Sîn, the other before Marduk’s spade mounted on a *mušḫuššu*-altar. [Institute for the Study of the Ancient World exhibition catalogue](https://isaw.nyu.edu/exhibitions/ishtar-gate/objects/cylinder-seal-sin-spade)

### Related but not identical: the Chogha Zanbil implement

A real metal object discovered at Chogha Zanbil is often compared with Marduk’s emblem. Roland de Mecquenem’s 1938 excavation report describes a copper or bronze object 54.6 cm long: one end pointed, the other a broad spear- or spade-like blade, with the shaft formed as a serpent holding the blade in its mouth. A damaged cuneiform inscription and careful workmanship identified it as votive; de Mecquenem thought it more likely a sceptre than a weapon. The excavators associated it with Nabû, not Marduk. [De Mecquenem excavation report](https://www.mom.fr/mecquenem/rapport/afficher/id/48/annee/1938)

This is evidence that three-dimensional divine “spades” existed, but it is **not the spade of Marduk**. Nabû could occasionally share the emblem, and the two gods’ symbols were closely paired.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Earliest textual attestation

The emblem is first attested in **Old Babylonian legal documents**, before its securely datable monumental representations. One legal case, conventionally associated with Kutalla near Ur, sends litigants or witnesses to swear before a group of divine emblems, including:

- the *šurinnu* of Nanna;
- the divine bird of Ninnar;
- the divine spade of Marduk;
- a divine stone weapon.

The reference implies a localized physical cult object capable of serving as the focus of an oath. The document does not describe its dimensions, material, maker, or installation. It consequently establishes the existence of a cultic referent but does not allow that object to be matched with any excavated implement. [Discussion of the Old Babylonian legal evidence](https://escholarship.org/content/qt3vc480pw/qt3vc480pw.pdf)

### Earliest iconographic attestations

Ritter dates the earliest depictions to Old Babylonian cylinder seals of the eighteenth–seventeenth centuries BCE, specifically the reign of Samsu-iluna, 1749–1712 BCE. The earliest examples appear with royal or divine presentation scenes. Their identification is not uniformly secure because:

- similar forms can represent incense burners or stands;
- the Old Babylonian spade may accompany the god Amurru or his crook-and-gazelle emblem;
- Marduk’s later pairing with the *mušḫuššu* had not yet become standard.

The chronological claim “Marduk’s symbol already in the Old Babylonian period” is well supported textually; identifying every contemporary triangular object in glyptic art as his emblem is not. [Ritter](https://www.religionswissenschaft.uzh.ch/idd/prepublications/e_idd_spade.pdf); [ORACC](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/listofdeities/marduk/index.html)

### Manufacture

No workshop archive, commission, price, timber source, or metal analysis survives for the Old Babylonian cultic object. A practical Mesopotamian *marru* would presumably combine a wooden handle and a working head, but transferring that reconstruction to the divine emblem is hazardous. The iconographic blade may be solid, framed, hollow, decorated, wrapped, or transformed into an almost spear-like form.

The Chogha Zanbil example demonstrates sophisticated metal manufacture. Modern technical discussion describes it as a spade-shaped implement assembled through casting-on, with the serpent-head joint linking shaft and blade and possibly combining prefabricated modules or different copper alloys. [*The Elamite World*](https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/PLACES/IndOc-Gulf/ElamHistory-AlvarezMon2018.pdf)

### Meaning of the original form

The ancient sources do not explain why the spade represented Marduk. Proposed explanations include:

- **agricultural deity:** the implement signifies cultivation and fertility;
- **canal-digger:** it represents the excavation and maintenance of irrigation canals, hence control over life-giving water;
- **cosmic builder:** in first-millennium theology, the earth-working tool could signify Marduk’s construction and organization of the cosmos;
- **name-pun:** association between *marru* and Marduk’s name has occasionally been proposed, but no secure ancient explanation demonstrates such a pun.

Toshiko Oshima argues that the canal-digging interpretation fits texts presenting Marduk as provider of water from rivers, canals, springs, floods, and rain. The emblem’s meaning nevertheless remains a scholarly reconstruction, not an explicitly preserved ancient definition. [Oshima, “Marduk, the Canal Digger”](https://janes.scholasticahq.com/api/v1/articles/2451-marduk-the-canal-digger.pdf)

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because this is an emblematic object-class, the appropriate chain is a sequence of attestations and separately documented embodiments.

### Old Babylonian period, eighteenth–seventeenth centuries BCE

**Primary/documented:** a divine spade of Marduk stood among other cult symbols used in legal oath-taking. Its location is associated with Kutalla and the gate or cultic jurisdiction of Marduk. Its manufacture and later fate are unknown.

**Iconographic:** cylinder seals produced under and after Samsu-iluna introduce the upright triangular form. Surviving specimens often entered modern collections without excavated provenance.

### Kassite and Middle Babylonian period, approximately sixteenth–twelfth centuries BCE

The emblem became regular on kudurrus or entitlement *narûs*. These monuments recorded royal grants, purchases, inheritance, and confirmation of landholding. They were probably deposited in temples rather than erected as field boundary stones. The divine symbols placed the legal act under supernatural protection and visually embodied the gods named in curse formulae. [University of Mainz kudurru overview](https://www.ao.altertumswissenschaften.uni-mainz.de/bringing-the-past-to-light/kudurru/)

The spade was combined increasingly with:

- Marduk’s *mušḫuššu*;
- a pedestal or shrine façade;
- Nabû’s wedge/stylus;
- astral symbols of Sîn, Šamaš, and Ištar;
- Ea’s goat-fish and ram-headed staff;
- other divine emblems enforcing the transaction.

#### Elamite removal to Susa

A major group of Babylonian monuments was carried east to Susa as war booty during Elamite campaigns in Babylonia in the late twelfth century BCE. This is an ancient conquest transfer, not an ordinary exchange. The transferred group included kudurrus bearing Marduk’s emblem.

A representative documented example is Louvre **SB 26**, made in Babylonia under Marduk-apla-iddina I, 1171–1159 BCE. The Louvre records that it was taken from Babylonia to Susa as war booty in the twelfth century BCE. French excavators under Jacques de Morgan found it at Susa in 1899–1902; it entered the French national collection through the official division of excavation finds and is now held by the Louvre, on deposit at the Institut du monde arabe in Paris. The limestone monument is 47 × 20 × 17 cm, hammered and cleaned, and bears eighteen divine symbols. The museum currently calls Marduk’s pointed emblem a “lance,” illustrating the continuing nomenclatural ambiguity. [Louvre SB 26](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010174456)

That is the custody history of **SB 26**, not of all Marduk-spades.

### Middle Assyrian and Neo-Assyrian transmission

Marduk’s emblem entered Assyrian glyptic and monumental art. It appears on seals owned or dedicated by individuals, on stelae erected by Assyrian officials and kings, and on rock reliefs. Assyrian use appropriated the authority of Babylonian theology while often subordinating Marduk to Aššur.

A now-lost relief from the Central Palace at Nimrud reportedly showed Assyrian soldiers removing anthropomorphic divine statues during Tiglath-pileser III’s conquest of Babylonia in 731 BCE. One bearded cult figure held a spade-like implement. If the implement was a *marru*, the figure may have been Marduk, but its head broadened in an unusual direction and the identification remains disputed. The relief’s loss makes modern drawings and publications its only documentary survival. [IDD, “Marduk”](https://www.religionswissenschaft.uzh.ch/idd/prepublications/e_idd_marduk.pdf)

### Sennacherib’s intervention, 689 BCE and afterward

Sennacherib destroyed Babylon in 689 BCE and removed cult property from Esagila. This episode affected the emblem’s representational history even though no text securely states that a particular physical spade was seized.

In Assyrian royal art from Sennacherib onward, Marduk’s pointed spade was reshaped into an ovoid or cone-like implement resembling the purification cone held by protective genies. Eckart Frahm interprets this as part of Sennacherib’s theological program: Marduk’s role was restricted to that of Asalluhi, god of incantation and purification, while Aššur absorbed Marduk’s cosmic and royal functions. [Frahm, discussion and Appendix 2](https://oi.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/ois8.pdf)

This is a strong contextual interpretation, not an ancient caption saying “the spade has been changed into a cone.” Its evidence is the coordinated change in iconographic form, the political context of Babylon’s destruction, and Assyrian theological compositions hostile to Marduk.

### Neo-Babylonian restoration

After the fall of the Sargonid empire, Babylonian images restored a visibly spade-like head. Some late examples have slightly toothed edges, which Frahm reads as a deliberate visual rejection of the Assyrian cone interpretation. Whether every artisan or viewer understood this as conscious counter-propaganda cannot be established.

During the sixth century BCE, stamp seals showing a worshipper before Marduk’s spade and Nabû’s stylus became mass-produced. This greatly expanded the emblem’s circulation beyond palace and temple monuments.

### Achaemenid period

The image became less frequent after the Persian conquest of Babylon in 539 BCE but did not disappear. Seal impressions in the fifth-century Murašû archive at Nippur and examples from Persepolis retain worshippers facing pedestals surmounted by the spade and other divine symbols. The emblem had spread from Babylonia into Iran, Syria, Jordan, and Palestine/Israel. [Ritter](https://www.religionswissenschaft.uzh.ch/idd/prepublications/e_idd_spade.pdf)

### Disappearance

There is no documented destruction date for “the” spade because there was no single continuous object. The iconographic series thins during and after Achaemenid rule. Late Babylonian religion continued into Seleucid and Parthian times, but presently published evidence does not demonstrate an unbroken later cult of a physical *marru*.

### Modern rediscovery and collecting

- **1840s–1870s:** seals and kudurrus entered European collections through antiquarian purchase and British excavations. Documentation was often limited.
- **1873:** British Museum seal 89810 was registered after acquisition from excavated or collected material.
- **1899–1902:** French excavators recovered Babylonian kudurrus at Susa. Division of finds transferred a share to France.
- **1938:** de Mecquenem excavated the bronze Nabû implement at Chogha Zanbil; seventeen crates went to Tehran and, according to the report, eight crates from the season were sent to the Louvre under the excavation-sharing regime. The presently accessible documentation does not securely state which share received the Nabû implement.
- **1885–1908:** J. Pierpont Morgan acquired Seal 692; its earlier archaeological custody is undocumented.
- **1985:** the Metropolitan Museum acquired kudurru fragment 1985.45 through the Rogers Fund. Its published online record gives no earlier find or ownership history.

These gaps are important: a correct museum accession history is not necessarily an archaeological provenance.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Was there one original spade?

**Unresolved, but unlikely on present evidence.** Old Babylonian legal references support at least one physical cult emblem. The wide geographic and chronological corpus shows that the form was reproducible. No surviving object has a documented chain connecting it to the oath-object at Kutalla or to Marduk’s principal temple Esagila.

### Which images truly represent Marduk’s spade?

Secure cases combine one or more of the following:

- the name dAMAR.UTU written on the blade;
- placement upon Marduk’s *mušḫuššu*;
- pairing with Nabû’s stylus;
- occurrence within a known series of labeled divine symbols;
- correspondence between carved emblem and the deity invoked in an accompanying text.

Ambiguous cases include:

- Old Babylonian objects accompanied by Amurru’s crook;
- triangular incense burners or offering stands;
- “spear-head” or “lance” forms on damaged kudurrus;
- an unusual implement held by an anthropomorphic god;
- spades occasionally assigned to Nabû.

### Spade, spear, hoe, or standard?

Museum catalogues variously call the emblem a spade, spear-head, lance, standard, or unidentified pointed object. “Spade” is supported by the ancient term *marru* and the inscribed Kassite example. Yet the iconographic design is ceremonial, not a mechanically exact drawing of an ordinary tool. Describing every pointed version as a spear is therefore no safer than treating every version as a literal working shovel.

### The Nabû problem

Nabû’s normal emblem is the wedge-shaped stylus, but literary and visual evidence occasionally assigns a spade to him. The Chogha Zanbil metal implement was dedicated to Nabû and resembles Marduk’s emblem closely. That overlap does not invalidate Marduk’s identification; it reflects the close father-son theological relationship and transmission of attributes.

### The Sennacherib “cone”

Frahm’s reconstruction sees deliberate Assyrian reinterpretation after 689 BCE. The sequence is persuasive but not beyond dispute because no preserved decree orders the redesign. The safe formulation is:

- **documented:** late Neo-Assyrian forms become markedly ovoid and resemble purification cones;
- **scholarly inference:** this was a controlled theological diminishment of Marduk;
- **further inference:** later Babylonian toothed blades deliberately repudiated the Assyrian interpretation.

### Scientific examinations

No published radiocarbon, thermoluminescence, residue, DNA, or metallurgical study authenticates one candidate as Marduk’s original cult implement. Such testing is largely inapplicable to stone reliefs whose dating depends on inscription, archaeological context, and style.

Material descriptions in museum catalogues are technical identifications, but the available online records do not report quarry-source analysis for the kudurrus or alloy composition for the Chogha Zanbil object.

### Forgeries and modern misidentifications

No documented ancient-style forgery has established itself as the “true spade of Marduk.” More common are modern unsupported identifications:

- a “rocket” or spacecraft;
- a magical weapon used against Tiamat;
- an emblem derived from the modern playing-card suit;
- a universal occult sigil.

The “rocket” interpretation is a modern pseudoarchaeological invention. Ancient texts and securely identified images call or depict an implement comparable to a spade or hoe; no ancient source describes it as a vehicle, engine, or technological apparatus.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Divine associations

- **Marduk:** principal owner and referent, securely established by Old Babylonian legal terminology and the dAMAR.UTU inscription on SB 3224.
- **Nabû:** Marduk’s son; his stylus is the spade’s most frequent companion from the ninth century BCE onward. Nabû could occasionally receive a spade himself.
- **Mušḫuššu:** serpent-dragon serving as Marduk’s animal and pedestal. The emblem may stand on its back or on a shrine before it.
- **Asalluhi:** god of incantation assimilated to Marduk. In the proposed Sennacherib reform, Marduk’s cone-like emblem expressed a restricted identity as purification god.
- **Ea/Enki:** Marduk’s theological father; Ea’s goat-fish and ram-headed staff frequently accompany the spade on kudurrus.
- **Aššur:** Assyrian national god who absorbed aspects of Marduk’s cosmic authority, animal, costume, and mythology under Sargonid royal theology.
- **Amurru/Martu:** his crook appears with some early spades, creating identification problems.
- **Sîn, Šamaš, Ištar, Adad, Nusku, Gula, Nergal, Zababa, Išḫara:** members of the divine-symbol assemblies on kudurrus and seals.

### Royal and institutional associations

- **Samsu-iluna, 1749–1712 BCE:** earliest securely dated iconographic horizon.
- **Meli-Šipak, approximately 1188–1174 BCE:** ruler under whom several important inscribed or richly symbolic kudurrus were made.
- **Marduk-apla-iddina I, approximately 1171–1159 BCE:** confirmed grants represented on surviving Susa kudurrus.
- **Šutruk-Naḫḫunte and later Elamite conquerors:** associated with the removal of Babylonian monuments to Susa; attribution of an individual monument to a particular campaign must be made object by object.
- **Tiglath-pileser III:** his 731 BCE Babylonian campaign supplied the context for the lost Nimrud relief of deported divine images.
- **Sennacherib:** destruction of Babylon in 689 BCE and probable theological reinterpretation of the emblem.
- **Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal:** restored Babylonian buildings and cult equipment while maintaining complex Assyrian claims over Marduk’s authority.
- **Jacques de Morgan:** directed the French mission that excavated many Susa kudurrus in 1899–1902.
- **Roland de Mecquenem:** excavated and documented the Chogha Zanbil Nabû implement in 1938.
- **Austen Henry Layard:** excavator or collector associated with British Museum 89361.
- **J. Pierpont Morgan:** modern collector of Morgan Seal 692.
- **Louvre, British Museum, Metropolitan Museum, Morgan Library, National Museum of Iran:** present custodial institutions for separate material embodiments or closely related objects.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Oath-taking

The oldest explicit function is juridical. Litigants swore before the divine spade alongside other cult emblems. The emblem was thus treated as a localized presence or witness of Marduk. Breaking the oath invited divine sanction.

Whether the object itself was touched, carried, unveiled, or merely approached is not recorded in the cited legal case.

### Protection of grants and titles

On kudurrus the spade formed part of a visual divine assembly protecting land grants and judicial decisions. It did not physically mark only a geographic boundary; it ratified the written transaction. The gods’ symbols and accompanying curses threatened anyone who:

- erased the inscription;
- damaged or removed the monument;
- altered boundaries;
- reopened a settled claim;
- substituted another name;
- denied the grant to the beneficiary’s descendants.

The image therefore acted in Marduk’s name as a guarantee of legal permanence.

### Worship on seals

First-millennium seals commonly depict a worshipper raising one or both hands before the spade and Nabû’s stylus. The scene may express personal devotion, institutional affiliation, or divine authentication of the transaction sealed by the object.

The seal itself was then rolled or stamped onto clay, multiplying the emblem through administrative practice. This is the principal mechanism by which the spade traveled widely: not as one relic, but as a repeated authoritative image.

### Royal legitimation

Marduk conferred Babylonian kingship. Although the New Year ritual centered on taking Marduk’s hand and his anthropomorphic cult statue rather than demonstrably on the spade, the emblem condensed his authority on documents and royal monuments. Its presence could signify that a royal grant stood under the patronage of Babylon’s supreme god.

### Irrigation, fertility, and creation

The canal-digger interpretation joins practical and cosmic functions. A *marru* opens watercourses and works soil; Marduk supplies the water needed for human settlement; the cosmic Marduk constructs an ordered world. The emblem could therefore signify the transformation of undifferentiated land into productive, legally organized territory. This remains an interpretive synthesis, not an ancient explanatory gloss. [Oshima](https://janes.scholasticahq.com/api/v1/articles/2451-marduk-the-canal-digger.pdf)

### Tassels and binding

The tasselled emblem may visualize *riksu*: binding, agreement, or ritual arrangement. If correct, the cloth-wrapped spade simultaneously represents deity and binding oath. The proposal is plausible in legal-symbolic contexts but unproven.

### Access and handling

The existence of full-size cult symbols is documented, but no preserved rule states who alone could touch Marduk’s spade. Its placement among oath-emblems suggests controlled temple or civic access. Images on seals, however, made the sign available to officials and private owners far beyond Esagila.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### No ancient forging myth

No known Mesopotamian narrative explains who forged Marduk’s spade, from what supernatural material, or how he acquired it. This sharply distinguishes it from mythic weapons such as Ninurta’s Šarur or Marduk’s storm weapons in *Enūma eliš*.

Claims that Ea forged the spade for Marduk, that it fell from heaven, or that it was the weapon used to split Tiamat are unsupported unless a specific late source is produced.

### Marduk and Tiamat

In *Enūma eliš*, Marduk fights Tiamat with winds, bow, arrow, net, mace, storm, and related weapons. The poem does not make the spade his decisive weapon. Later images can place a spade near a victorious god and serpent, allowing the emblem to identify Marduk, but identification is not the same as a narrative claim that he fought with the spade.

### The canal-digger narrative

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Marduk may originally have been patron of canal excavation, with the spade representing this function. Later creation theology elevated local hydraulic work into cosmic construction.

This is not a preserved mythic episode with a beginning and ending. It is a reconstruction from vocabulary, hymnic descriptions of water provision, and the emblem.

### Assyrian demotion and Babylonian recovery

The proposed transformation from pointed spade to purification cone after 689 BCE forms an iconographic political narrative:

1. Sennacherib destroys Babylon and appropriates Marduk’s functions for Aššur.
2. Assyrian imagery blunts the spade into a purification cone.
3. Marduk is restricted to the Asalluhi role.
4. Post-Assyrian Babylonian artisans restore and even serrate the blade.

The first and second points are materially observable; the precise ideological intent of the shape change and the “defiance” of later artisans are scholarly interpretations. [Frahm](https://oi.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/ois8.pdf)

### Modern “ancient astronaut” legend

Modern websites sometimes label the emblem a rocket, launch vehicle, or advanced weapon. The claim depends on visual resemblance and is contradicted by:

- the ancient designation *marru*;
- the dAMAR.UTU inscription identifying ownership, not technology;
- its appearance among other divine emblems;
- Old Babylonian legal use;
- the existence of functional and ceremonial spade-like implements.

This is a modern invention, not a variant ancient tradition.

## 8. Current Status and Location

There is no unique surviving “Spade of Marduk” whose present location can be stated. The original Old Babylonian oath-object is **lost or unidentified**.

Important surviving embodiments include:

- **Louvre SB 3224:** inscribed Kassite representation, Musée du Louvre, Paris; detailed current gallery status not established by the accessible record.
- **Louvre SB 26:** Louvre property, on deposit at the Institut du monde arabe, Paris; limestone kudurru from Susa, anciently taken from Babylonia as war booty. [Louvre](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010174456)
- **Louvre SB 22:** Meli-Šipak kudurru with pointed spade and dragon; Louvre, Paris. [Louvre record](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark:/53355/cl010174452)
- **Metropolitan Museum 1985.45:** New York; not on view. [Metropolitan Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/327048)
- **British Museum 89361:** London; not on display.
- **British Museum 89810:** London.
- **British Museum 1983,0101.305:** London; exhibited on loan at the Institute for the Study of the Ancient World in the documented exhibition.
- **Morgan Seal 692:** Morgan Library & Museum, New York.
- **Chogha Zanbil Nabû spade/sceptre:** excavated in Iran in 1938; the accessible online records reviewed here do not establish its present inventory number or display location securely enough to state one.

No current legal proceeding specifically seeks restitution of “the spade of Marduk.” Broader questions attach to individual objects:

- Babylonian monuments seized by Elam in antiquity;
- finds divided between Iran and France under twentieth-century excavation law;
- nineteenth-century Mesopotamian objects with incomplete export histories;
- unprovenanced seals acquired on the art market.

Those histories must not be collapsed into one ownership dispute.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Museum and scholarly image

The emblem is now one of the standard visual identifiers of Marduk, commonly reproduced in dictionaries, museum labels, teaching material, and digital reconstructions. Its public image usually derives from Kassite kudurrus or elaborate Neo-Assyrian seals, not from the earliest Old Babylonian examples.

### Replicas and modern impressions

Museums routinely make modern impressions from ancient cylinder seals. These are openly labeled study or display products rather than ancient objects. The modern impression exhibited beside British Museum 1983,0101.305 helps viewers read the otherwise reversed intaglio design. [ISAW](https://isaw.nyu.edu/exhibitions/ishtar-gate/objects/cylinder-seal-sin-spade)

Commercial pendants, tattoos, and devotional objects reproduce stylized versions. They are modern replicas of an iconographic form; none has an ancient custody claim.

### Contemporary religious use

Modern Mesopotamian reconstructionists and individual pagans use the spade as Marduk’s devotional sign. This restores an ancient association but not necessarily ancient ritual practice. Wearing a spade pendant, placing it on a modern altar, or treating it as a personal sigil should be classified as modern religious reception.

### Confusion with the playing-card spade

The ancient emblem is not historically derived from, nor the source of, the modern playing-card suit. Their common English label reflects loose resemblance.

### Popular culture

Modern fictional franchises occasionally use “Marduk” symbols of their own design. For example, a symbol circulated as the “Marduk symbol” from *Stargate* is a franchise graphic, not an archaeological transcription. Such designs have sometimes been detached from their fictional context and recirculated as ancient sigils.

### Pseudoarchaeology

The “spade-rocket” interpretation is among the most consequential modern distortions. It removes the emblem from the documented worlds of irrigation, oath-taking, temples, seals, and kudurrus and reclassifies it as technological hardware. No philological, archaeological, or material evidence supports that reading.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Early cataloguing

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century cataloguers often called kudurrus “boundary stones” and described individual emblems morphologically as spear-heads, lances, wedges, standards, or weapons. These catalogues remain valuable for discovery and collection history but preserve outdated functional assumptions.

Leonard W. King’s *Babylonian Boundary-Stones and Memorial-Tablets in the British Museum* assembled texts, line drawings, and object histories. Subsequent scholarship showed that many *narûs* were temple deposits or legal memorials rather than markers planted at the edge of a field.

### Iconographic classification

Ursula Seidl’s *Die babylonischen Kudurru-Reliefs* established the fundamental corpus and typology of divine symbols. Tallay Ornan’s *The Triumph of the Symbol* placed the rise of emblematic representation in the broader history of Mesopotamian strategies for depicting divinity.

Ritter’s 2009 “Spade” lemma provides the clearest compact catalogue: four phenotypes, chronological and geographic distribution, associations, object types, and bibliography. Its principal conclusions are:

- earliest textual evidence is Old Babylonian;
- earliest images belong to the eighteenth–seventeenth centuries BCE;
- Kassite kudurrus standardize the form;
- Assyrian adoption becomes visible by the late ninth century;
- Neo-Babylonian stamp seals mass-produce it;
- Achaemenid examples become rarer;
- the overall type remains remarkably stable. [Ritter](https://www.religionswissenschaft.uzh.ch/idd/prepublications/e_idd_spade.pdf)

### Agricultural deity versus canal-digger

Black and Green connected the spade with an agricultural origin for Marduk. Oshima refines or contests that interpretation by emphasizing canal digging and Marduk’s textual association with supplying water. The two interpretations overlap but are not identical: irrigation is agricultural infrastructure, yet the canal-digger model gives Marduk a more specifically hydraulic and civic function.

The debate remains unresolved because the emblem predates surviving explanatory theology.

### Marduk versus Amurru in early glyptic

Old Babylonian examples do not consistently pair the spade with Marduk’s later dragon. Some occur with Amurru or his crook. There is no known text establishing a dAMAR.UTU = dMartu syncretism that would solve the problem. Early glyptic identifications should therefore be treated as probable or possible, not automatic.

### Spade versus incense burner

Schematic seal cutting can make a triangular implement resemble an incense stand. Matthews, Seidl, Ritter, and museum cataloguers differ on individual examples. Context—pedestal, dragon, stylus, worshipper, and inscription—is more probative than outline alone.

### Assyrian ideological redesign

Frahm’s interpretation of the cone-shaped Sargonid emblem is among the most important recent proposals. It integrates iconography with:

- Babylon’s destruction in 689 BCE;
- Assyrian redaction of Babylonian theology;
- the “Marduk Ordeal” tradition;
- Aššur’s adoption of Marduk’s cosmic attributes;
- later Babylonian restoration of a blade-like form.

It should be cited as a sophisticated historical interpretation, not reported as a directly recorded act of iconographic censorship.

### State of the evidence

The evidence is uneven:

- numerous images survive;
- many seals lack archaeological provenance;
- the earliest cult object is known only from text;
- no Esagila inventory presently identifies a surviving spade by dimensions and material;
- some reliefs are lost;
- museum terminology is inconsistent;
- scientific material studies remain limited or unpublished;
- the original excavation and division records for some Susa and Chogha Zanbil finds are incomplete or difficult to correlate with modern accession numbers.
