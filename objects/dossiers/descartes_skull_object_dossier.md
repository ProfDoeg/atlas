# Descartes's skull: Object Dossier

**Proposed filename:** `descartes_skull_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary finding

“Descartes’s skull” presently denotes at least two different human remains:

1. **The Paris cranium**, a nearly complete adult human cranium without mandible or teeth, covered with ownership and provenance inscriptions and catalogued by the Muséum national d’Histoire naturelle as **MNHN-HA-19220**. The Musée de l’Homme continues to identify and exhibit it as the skull of René Descartes.
2. **The Lund fragment**, a left parietal bone catalogued by Lund University Historical Museum as **LUHM 508**, inscribed internally *Cartesi – döskalla 1691. No 6.* It entered the university collection by 1780.

The Paris cranium indisputably exists and can be followed securely from the collection of Anders Sparrman in the early nineteenth century to its present museum custody. Its identity as Descartes’s skull is not scientifically established. A 2020 source-critical study by Andreas Manhag and Per Karsten concluded that most of its purported eighteenth-century owner inscriptions are false and called the Paris attribution a forgery. French investigators, particularly Philippe Charlier and collaborators, defend the attribution on anthropological and craniofacial grounds. Neither candidate has been authenticated by DNA, and neither has a demonstrably continuous chain back to Descartes’s 1666 exhumation.

The Lund fragment has an earlier documented museum provenance, but its identification is likewise unproved. Its advocates regard it as probably one portion of a skull deliberately separated along its sutures. The extant Paris cranium cannot be the parent skull of LUHM 508 because it retains its own left parietal bone.

The correct archival conclusion is therefore:

> **Documented fact:** Paris and Lund preserve two incompatible human remains attributed to Descartes.  
> **Unresolved identity:** No published genetic or other individualizing test has proved that either came from René Descartes.  
> **Strong source-critical challenge:** The traditional pre-1797 custody chain inscribed on the Paris cranium conflicts with eighteenth-century inventories and publications.  
> **Institutional attribution:** The Musée de l’Homme nevertheless catalogues and displays the Paris object as Descartes’s skull.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Names and object class

The principal names are:

- **French:** *crâne de Descartes*, *crâne de René Descartes*, formerly also *tête de Descartes*.
- **Swedish:** *Descartes skalle*, *Cartesii skalle*, *Cartesi döskalla* or modern *Descartes dödskalle*.
- **Latin:** *calvaria Cartesii*—“the skull-cap/cranium of Cartesius.”
- **English:** “Descartes’s skull” or “the skull of René Descartes.”

*Cartesius* is the conventional Latin form of Descartes. Swedish *skalle* means skull; *dödskalle* literally means “dead person’s skull.” The Lund spelling *döskalla* is archaic and has been used as evidence that its inscription is old, although paleographic age alone cannot identify the person from whom the bone came.

The objects belong simultaneously to several classes:

- human skeletal remains;
- alleged personal relics;
- learned or “secular” relics of genius;
- anatomical and anthropological specimens;
- inscribed collection objects;
- museum exhibits.

Neither was originally manufactured as an artifact. Their artifactual lives began through exhumation, separation, inscription, collecting, cataloguing, and display.

### 1.2 The Paris cranium: MNHN-HA-19220

The Muséum catalogues the specimen as *Homo sapiens*, bone, with the dimensions:

- height: **14 cm**;
- length: **16 cm**;
- width/depth: **18.05 cm**;
- material: human bone;
- institutional number: **MNHN-HA-19220**.

The catalogue gives “France” as origin, an institutional field that should not be mistaken for a scientifically demonstrated birthplace of the individual. Descartes himself was French, while the alleged recovery history begins in Stockholm. The official record and current display information are published by the [Musée de l’Homme](https://www.museedelhomme.fr/fr/crane-de-descartes).

The cranium is described in the specialist literature as:

- approximately complete;
- yellowish to brownish;
- lacking the lower jaw;
- lacking its teeth;
- retaining the left parietal bone;
- extensively written upon in Swedish, Latin, and personal names.

No reliable published weight was located.

#### Principal provenance inscription

The Swedish inscription has been transcribed as:

> *Cartesii skalle, tillvaratagen af Is. Planström, då liket åhr 1666 skulle föras till Frankrike, och sedan här i Stockholm förvarad.*

Translation:

> “The skull of Cartesius, taken into custody by Is. Planström when the body was to be transported to France in 1666, and thereafter preserved here in Stockholm.”

Older French transcriptions sometimes render the last clause more loosely as “since that time hidden in Sweden.” The modern scholarly transcription says “preserved here in Stockholm.” The inscription is the assertion upon which the theft story largely rests; it is not an independent contemporary record of the 1666 exhumation.

#### Latin verse

The cranium bears a quatrain associated with the Swedish scholar Sven Hof:

> *Parvula Cartesii fuit hæc calvaria Magni,*  
> *Gravius reliquias Gallia busto tegit.*  
> *Sed laus ingenii toto diffunditur orbe,*  
> *Mirisque in coelis mens pia semper ovat.*

A close translation is:

> “This little skull was that of the great Cartesius;  
> Gaul covers his remaining relics in a weightier tomb.  
> But the praise of his genius is spread through the whole world,  
> and his pious mind triumphs forever in the wondrous heavens.”

The verse turns the skull explicitly into a learned relic. Its presence does not prove identity: Hof’s verse and story were in print by 1750 and could be copied.

#### Names written on the Paris cranium

Transcriptions made by Sparrman in 1808, Carl Palmstedt in March 1821, and Berzelius in 1844 record some combination of:

- *Is. Planström*;
- *J. Hægerflycht*;
- *Arckenholtz*;
- *Anders Anton von Stierneman 1751*;
- *Ol. Celsius*;
- *J. Åhgren 1797*.

Later summaries sometimes add Sparrman and Arngren/Ahrengren as owners, but those names belong to its externally documented early-nineteenth-century history rather than necessarily to the original six-name inscription.

Manhag and Karsten argue that four names—Hägerflycht, Arckenholtz, Stiernman, and Celsius—are demonstrably false as statements of successive ownership of this intact cranium. Their conclusion is based on conflicts with the 1780 Lund inventory and Arckenholtz’s own published statement that he possessed only a **piece** of a skull.

### 1.3 The Lund fragment: LUHM 508

LUHM 508 is:

- a whitish-grey fragment of adult human **left parietal bone**;
- approximately **140 × 132 mm**;
- separated along or near cranial sutures;
- inscribed on the internal surface:

> *Cartesi – döskalla 1691. No 6.*

Translation:

> “Descartes’s skull, 1691, No. 6.”

The meaning of “No. 6” is unresolved. Earlier scholars interpreted it as a collection number. Manhag and Karsten propose that it marked the sixth of several fragments produced by “skull blasting”: filling a cranium with dry peas or grain and adding water so that expansion forces the bones apart at the sutures. That reconstruction is plausible but unproved; no contemporary record says that Descartes’s skull was treated this way.

The fragment was associated in Lund with:

- a handwritten note containing Hof’s Latin verse and bibliographical references;
- a second note concerning comparison of the handwriting with Johan Arckenholtz’s manuscripts;
- a tall wooden cup, **LUHM 3150**, approximately 302 mm high, with a carved skull on its lid and an internal diameter of about 150 mm.

The fragment fits the cup. The cup’s old number “No 37” does not match known Museum Stobæanum or Historical Museum inventories. Manhag and Karsten suggest that the vessel was a purpose-made or repurposed reliquary and that “37” may derive from Anders Anton von Stiernman’s collection. Both propositions remain inferences.

### 1.4 Other alleged pieces

Eighteenth-century texts document claims concerning at least three attributed remains:

1. a skull or portion seen by Sven Hof in the possession of Olofsson Bång before Hof’s 1750 publication;
2. a piece acquired by Johan Arckenholtz in 1754;
3. another piece then in the collection of the Hägerflycht family.

It is not established whether any of these three was LUHM 508. Their subsequent physical fates, except for the possible Lund connection, are unknown.

A finger bone was deliberately removed from the remains in 1666 for the French ambassador Hugues de Terlon. This is not part of either skull candidate, but its documented removal matters because it proves that Descartes’s body was treated as a source of personal relics. Its present whereabouts are unknown.

## 2. Origin, Separation, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Biological origin

If either candidate is genuine, it formed naturally during Descartes’s lifetime. No source determines the geographical origin of the bone itself. The identification depends on later funerary and collecting history, not manufacture.

René Descartes died in Stockholm on 11 February 1650. Pierre Chanut arranged the burial. Descartes’s coffin was placed in or beneath a large wooden funerary monument at the cemetery of the orphanage associated with Klara parish in Stockholm. Later evidence differs over whether the coffin rested above ground inside the elevated monument or in a shallow grave beneath it. The monument was described and engraved by Jöran Andersson Nordberg in 1727.

### 2.2 The documented 1666 exhumation

Friends in France sought the return of Descartes’s remains after reports that the Stockholm monument was deteriorating. Hugues de Terlon, French ambassador in Sweden, organized the exhumation.

Adrien Baillet’s 1691 biography, based partly on Terlon’s correspondence and a manuscript relation, says:

- the grave was opened publicly on **1 May 1666**;
- Terlon, his household, incoming ambassador Simon Arnauld de Pomponne, clergy, and others attended;
- the body had substantially decomposed;
- the bones were arranged over the remaining ashes in a new, shorter metal container;
- Terlon formally requested and received a bone of the hand;
- protocols were placed in the new coffin;
- it was sealed and enclosed within strong iron bars;
- it was packed as diplomatic baggage and guarded before departure.

The convoy left Stockholm in June, remained for approximately three months at Copenhagen, and departed overland on 2 October 1666 through Jutland, Lower Germany, Holland, Flanders, and Picardy. Customs officials stopped it at Péronne before it reached Paris in early 1667. The remains were deposited first at Saint-Paul and ceremonially entombed at Sainte-Geneviève-du-Mont on 24 June 1667.

Baillet does **not** report a missing skull. Jean-Baptiste-Joseph Delambre emphasized this omission in 1821: the account speaks generically of bones and ashes, and the precautions make an unnoticed removal of the entire skull difficult, though not impossible. The 1666 protocols themselves have not supplied a surviving explicit inventory of every bone.

### 2.3 Earliest attestation of a stolen skull

The earliest located published story that a Stockholm guard removed the skull is not from 1666. It appears in **Sven Hof’s notice in *Lars Salvius Lärda Tidningar*, no. 27 (1750)**. Hof reported seeing a supposed Descartes skull with his friend Olofsson Bång and associated its acquisition with an indebted city-guard officer.

In 1751 Johan Arckenholtz repeated the story in his memoirs concerning Queen Christina. In the fourth volume, published in 1760, he added that during his 1754 Swedish journey he acquired **a portion** of the purported skull, while another portion was in the cabinet of the late Hägerflycht.

Thus:

- the removal by Planström is an **eighteenth-century reported tradition**, not a presently known contemporaneous 1666 record;
- the earliest publications describe attributed skull material in more than one piece;
- the intact Paris object is securely visible in the record only much later.

### 2.4 The year 1691 on LUHM 508

If the Lund inscription is contemporary with the date it gives, the fragment existed as an attributed Descartes relic by 1691. No independent 1691 inventory has yet been produced. Manhag and Karsten consider the spelling consistent with the period and the obscure date less likely to have been selected by a later forger.

They suggest 1691 may be the year an entire skull was separated into numbered pieces. That is explicitly a scholarly reconstruction, not documented fact.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

## 3.1 Descartes’s actual remains, 1650–1819

### 1650: Stockholm burial

Descartes’s body was placed at Klara cemetery in a coffin and substantial wooden monument arranged by Pierre Chanut. This is the last point at which skull and body can safely be presumed to have been together.

### 1 May 1666: exhumation

Terlon’s party removed the remains. One hand bone was formally given to Terlon. No contemporary evidence presently establishes that Isaac Planström removed the skull.

### 1666–1667: transport to Paris

The sealed package moved from Stockholm to Copenhagen, then overland through northern Europe to Paris. This well-documented custody route applies to the remains placed in the coffin, but it cannot establish whether the skull was among them.

### 1667–1790s: Sainte-Geneviève

The transported remains were interred in a copper coffin at Sainte-Geneviève. The burial reportedly included identifying protocols.

### Revolutionary exhumation

During the 1790s Alexandre Lenoir recovered what he believed were Descartes’s remains while saving monuments from revolutionary destruction. He found only poor skeletal remnants, including portions of long bones and a small piece thought to be frontal bone. He reportedly used part of an alleged cranial fragment to make rings.

The coffin Lenoir opened was wooden and lacked the protocol expected in the copper coffin deposited in 1667. Manhag and Karsten therefore argue that he opened the wrong burial and that Descartes’s transported remains may remain beneath the former church site, partly under Rue Clovis. This is a reasoned but disputed reconstruction.

### 1795–1819: Musée des Monuments français and Saint-Germain-des-Prés

Lenoir placed the recovered bones in an antique sarcophagus in the garden of the Musée des Monuments français in 1795. The remains later moved through post-Revolutionary custody and were reinterred at Saint-Germain-des-Prés in 1819. During preparations for that reburial, the absence of a skull attracted learned attention.

The French National Convention had decreed in October 1793 that Descartes deserved the honors of the Panthéon and that his body and Pajou’s statue should be transferred there. The order was never executed.

## 3.2 Traditional custody chain of the Paris cranium

The traditional chain, principally reconstructed from the skull’s inscriptions and Berzelius’s letter, is:

1. **Isaac Planström**—allegedly removed it during the 1666 exhumation.
2. Following Planström’s financial difficulties or death, it allegedly passed through creditors, sometimes identified in popular accounts as a brewer and the brewer’s son.
3. **Anders Anton von Stiernman**, allegedly owner in 1751.
4. **Olof Celsius the Younger**, allegedly next owner.
5. **Johan or J. Hägerflycht**.
6. **Johan Arckenholtz**.
7. **Johan Fischerström**.
8. **J. Åhgren/Ahlgren**, whose name and date 1797 appear on the cranium.
9. **Anders Sparrman**, who received it from Ahlgren.
10. **Anders Andersson Ahrengren**, purchaser at the sale of Sparrman’s collection.
11. **Jöns Jacob Berzelius**, who repurchased it from Ahrengren.
12. **Georges Cuvier / Académie des sciences / Muséum national d’Histoire naturelle**.
13. **Musée de l’Homme**, where it remains.

Only the later portion is securely documented. The Stiernman–Celsius–Hägerflycht–Arckenholtz sequence is contradicted by older records concerning fragments.

## 3.3 Securely reconstructable custody of the Paris cranium

### Before or by 1797: J. Åhgren

“J. Åhgren 1797” is the earliest inscription Manhag and Karsten regard as probably authentic on the object itself. The person’s precise acquisition mechanism remains unestablished.

### Before 1808–1820: Anders Sparrman

Sparrman reported that the cranium had belonged to Johan Fischerström, then Ahlgren, who gave it to him. In 1808 Nils Henric Sjöborg and Bengt J:son Bergqvist published this information in a Lund dissertation. This is the first firm published description tying the approximately complete Paris candidate to a named physical collection.

Sparrman retained it until his death on 9 August 1820. His daughter later recalled that he showed it to King Gustav IV Adolf, an engagement rather than a custody transfer.

### 1821: auction and Ahrengren

After Sparrman’s death, his collection was auctioned. A Stockholm newspaper, *Argus*, reported on 14 March 1821 that the supposed Descartes skull had sold for about 17 or 18 *riksdaler banco*. The exact sale price recorded by Berzelius was **18 riksdaler 36 skilling banco**.

The buyer was **Anders Andersson Ahrengren (1770–1841)**, a Stockholm gambling-house proprietor. Berzelius later disparaged him as a celebrated gaming-house owner; the social description should not be mistaken for evidence against the transaction.

### 1821: Berzelius

Berzelius had heard in Paris in 1819 that Descartes’s reinterred remains lacked a skull. Back in Sweden, he learned of the Sparrman sale. He persuaded Ahrengren that the object should return to France and bought it for the same price Ahrengren had paid.

On **6 April 1821**, Berzelius wrote from Stockholm to Georges Cuvier, offering the cranium and explaining its alleged chain. The letter was published in full in the 1872 *Journal de zoologie* and preserved through the Académie’s documentation.

### April–May 1821: Académie des sciences

Cuvier presented the cranium to the Académie des sciences on **30 April 1821**. Berzelius requested that it be placed with the bodily remains. Instead, the Academy examined the claim.

Alexandre-Maurice Blanc de Lanautte assisted with historical provenance research. Cuvier and Claude-Louis Berthollet considered comparison with portraits favorable. Delambre prepared a skeptical memorandum dated 8 May for the minutes of 14 May, stressing that Baillet’s account gave no evidence that the skull had disappeared in 1666.

Cuvier reported on 19 May that its form corresponded closely with known portraits. That was an ocular and artistic comparison, not a modern identification.

### Muséum custody

The request to reunite it with the remains at Saint-Germain-des-Prés was not carried out. Cuvier placed it in the Muséum’s anatomical collections at the Jardin des Plantes. It later passed administratively into the anthropological collections and ultimately the Musée de l’Homme.

Published museum material occasionally gives erroneous dates—for example, one institutional press document said Cuvier received it in 1861. The primary correspondence and Académie record establish **1821**.

The Musée de l’Homme now inventories it as MNHN-HA-19220 and includes it in the permanent Galerie de l’Homme.

## 3.4 Custody of the Lund fragment

### Before 1765: Anders Anton von Stiernman

The 1780 museum inventory says the donor had inherited the fragment from her father, Anders Anton von Stiernman. That makes Stiernman its earliest named owner in a contemporary institutional provenance record. How he acquired it is unknown.

### 1765–1779/80: Andreetta Katarina Celsius

Stiernman died in 1765. His daughter Andreetta Katarina married Olof Celsius in 1766. The inventory expressly identifies **her**, not her husband, as inheritor and donor.

### 1779 or 1780: Museum Stobæanum, Lund

Olof Celsius took office in Lund in April 1779. The 1780 inventory records:

> “A piece of Descartes’s skull, with a Latin verse *Parvula Cartesii* etc., as well as directions to those authors who mention this antiquity. Given by the Bishop’s wife, born von Stiernman, who had inherited it from her father the Deputy Director.”

The wording securely proves that an object identified as a piece of Descartes’s skull was in the Lund collection by 1780. It does not independently prove the biological attribution.

### Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

For about forty years the fragment was treated as an important university relic. After France accepted the intact cranium in 1821, the fragment became embarrassing: both could not belong to the same skull. Scholars including Esaias Tegnér, Carl Magnus Fürst, and museum director Otto Rydbeck classified or exhibited it as a curiosity or forgery.

### Present

LUHM 508 remains in Lund University Historical Museum. Research beginning with comparison studies in 1983 and culminating in Manhag and Karsten’s 2020 chapter rehabilitated it as a serious claimant. Lund’s public presentation now describes the recent archival case in its favor, while acknowledging that no DNA result has settled the identity.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 The case historically made for Paris

The Paris attribution has rested on four principal arguments:

1. **The skull’s Swedish inscription** says Planström took it in 1666.
2. **The inscribed names** appeared to constitute a successive chain of learned Swedish owners.
3. **Its sex and adult age** are compatible with Descartes.
4. **Its cranial proportions and reconstructed face** have been judged compatible with contemporary portraits.

Berzelius accepted the inscriptions. Cuvier favored the portrait comparison. An Académie investigation in 1821 judged authenticity sufficiently probable for institutional acceptance. Ahlström and colleagues in 1983 could not anatomically decide between Paris and Lund but leaned toward Paris because they trusted its inscriptions. The Musée de l’Homme and Charlier’s team continue to accept it.

None of these arguments uniquely identifies an individual. Compatibility of sex, age, population affinity, and portrait proportions excludes some candidates but cannot prove that the bone is Descartes’s.

### 4.2 Delambre’s 1821 objections

Delambre observed that Baillet’s detailed narrative:

- reports no missing skull;
- describes ceremonial witnesses and formal protocols;
- describes a sealed metal container reinforced with iron;
- records the ambassador’s formal request for one hand bone, suggesting that removal of a more important relic would not casually have been permitted.

Delambre did not prove that theft was impossible. He demonstrated that the inscription’s narrative did not accord smoothly with the earliest detailed source.

### 4.3 The Lund contradiction

The Paris object retains its left parietal bone. LUHM 508 is itself a left parietal fragment. They therefore cannot both be portions of one skull.

The 1780 Lund inventory predates the Paris object’s securely documented appearance in Sparrman’s collection. It also shows that:

- Stiernman’s daughter inherited and donated a **piece**;
- Olof Celsius was not the recorded owner;
- Arckenholtz wrote that he acquired a **piece** in 1754;
- another **piece** belonged to Hägerflycht.

Consequently, names that appeared to certify the Paris cranium instead reproduce the known history of attributed fragments.

### 4.4 Manhag and Karsten’s forgery argument

Their 2020 findings were:

- four of the six ostensible early owner inscriptions on the Paris cranium are incompatible with surviving sources;
- the Planström story, Hof’s poem, and the names Hägerflycht and Arckenholtz were publicly available in eighteenth-century publications;
- Stiernman and Celsius were widely known as successive custodians of a famous library, which could have encouraged a forger to assume that a skull relic passed in the same way;
- the false inscriptions were probably produced after Olof and Andreetta Celsius moved to Lund in 1779 but before Fischerström’s death in 1796;
- “J. Åhgren 1797” is probably the only genuine old owner inscription in the group.

They conclude flatly that the Paris inscriptions are forgeries and that the cranium is not Descartes’s. The proposed forger is unknown. The likely motive would have been enhancement of a skull’s monetary, social, or scholarly value.

This conclusion is strongly argued but has not generated universal agreement or a genetic disproof. The museum has not withdrawn its identification.

### 4.5 Scientific examinations

#### Carl Magnus Fürst, 1908

Fürst argued that open sutures on the Lund fragment indicated someone younger than Descartes, who died at fifty-three. Later research rejected suture openness as a sufficiently reliable individual age test; elderly persons can retain open sutures.

#### Ahlström and colleagues, 1983

This comparative investigation reassessed both remains. It found that anatomy alone could not identify either candidate. It rejected Fürst’s age exclusion of Lund but favored Paris largely because the authors accepted the apparent inscribed provenance.

#### Craniofacial and forensic work on Paris

Philippe Charlier and collaborators report that the Paris skull is compatible with Descartes in:

- male sex;
- estimated age at death;
- geographical/population origin;
- craniofacial correspondence with contemporary portraits.

The exact evidentiary force is disputed because portraits are artistic representations and the compatibility criteria are not individualizing.

#### CT scanning and virtual endocast, 2017

Charlier, Philippe Froesch, N. Benmoussa, Alain Froment, Russell Shorto, Isabelle Huynh-Charlier, and colleagues CT-scanned the Paris cranium and produced a three-dimensional virtual endocast. Their study, “The brain of René Descartes (1650): A neuro-anatomical analysis,” appeared in the *Journal of the Neurological Sciences*, volume 378, pages 12–18.

The study:

- reconstructed the approximate endocranial volume and external brain surface;
- assessed metric and nonmetric features;
- described an unusually large ethmoidal sinus osteoma, a benign bony growth;
- treated the specimen’s attribution as accepted rather than testing its provenance.

The scan is scientifically informative about the human whose cranium is in Paris. It does not authenticate that person as Descartes.

### 4.6 DNA

No published DNA comparison has authenticated either Paris or Lund.

Potential reference samples are problematic:

- Descartes left no known direct descendants;
- the genealogical relationship and burial identity of collateral relatives would need demonstration;
- the bones at Saint-Germain-des-Prés may themselves be misidentified;
- Terlon’s documented finger relic is lost;
- destructive sampling of museum-held human remains raises conservation and ethical issues.

DNA matching Paris or Lund to bones at Saint-Germain-des-Prés would establish common biological identity only if the church bones were independently shown to be Descartes’s. The disputed revolutionary exhumation prevents that assumption.

### 4.7 Present balance of evidence

- **Paris object as historical museum specimen:** certain.
- **Paris chain from Sparrman to the Musée de l’Homme:** well documented.
- **Paris chain before Fischerström/Ahlgren:** seriously compromised.
- **Planström’s theft:** eighteenth-century tradition, possible but not contemporaneously demonstrated.
- **Lund fragment in Stiernman/Celsius custody and at Lund by 1780:** documented.
- **Lund inscription genuinely made in 1691:** plausible, not conclusively demonstrated.
- **Lund fragment as Descartes:** possible and supported by archival coherence, not scientifically proved.
- **Paris cranium as Descartes:** institutionally maintained and anatomically compatible, but challenged by major provenance contradictions.
- **Identity of Descartes’s true skull:** unresolved.

- **Pierre Chanut:** arranged Descartes’s Stockholm burial and original monument in 1650.
- **Hugues de Terlon:** organized the 1666 exhumation and transport; received a hand bone as a relic. His procedures are central evidence against an easy clandestine skull theft.
- **Simon Arnauld de Pomponne:** witnessed the exhumation as incoming French ambassador.
- **Isaac Planström:** Stockholm guard officer named by later tradition and the Paris inscription as thief or remover. No located contemporaneous 1666 document confirms the act.
- **Olofsson Bång:** possessed a purported skull or fragment seen by Sven Hof before 1750.
- **Sven Hof:** published the first located theft narrative and composed or transmitted the Latin relic poem.
- **Johan Arckenholtz:** acquired an attributed fragment in 1754 and documented a second fragment in Hägerflycht’s collection.
- **Hägerflycht family:** possessed an attributed portion according to Arckenholtz.
- **Anders Anton von Stiernman:** documented through the 1780 Lund inventory as the prior owner of the fragment inherited by his daughter.
- **Andreetta Katarina Celsius:** inherited the Lund fragment and donated it to the university by 1780.
- **Olof Celsius the Younger:** Andreetta’s husband; wrongly made an owner in the Paris inscription’s alleged succession.
- **Johan Fischerström:** reported by Sparrman as an earlier owner of the Paris cranium.
- **J. Åhgren/Ahlgren:** reportedly received it after Fischerström and gave it to Sparrman; “J. Åhgren 1797” is the inscription most likely to be genuine.
- **Anders Sparrman:** documented possessor by 1808; the Paris object was auctioned after his death.
- **Anders Andersson Ahrengren:** bought it at the Sparrman auction and resold it to Berzelius at cost.
- **Jöns Jacob Berzelius:** recovered it from Ahrengren, constructed its French restitution as the rectification of a Swedish wrong, and donated it in 1821.
- **Georges Cuvier:** received and presented the object, favored its authenticity, and placed it in the Muséum.
- **Jean-Baptiste-Joseph Delambre:** produced the principal contemporary skeptical assessment.
- **Alexandre-Maurice Blanc de Lanautte:** conducted historical research for the Académie’s authentication inquiry.
- **Alexandre Lenoir:** exhumed and curated the supposed bodily remains during the Revolution; his discovery of the wrong type of coffin casts doubt on the church remains.
- **Carl Magnus Fürst:** anatomically rejected the Lund fragment in 1908 on age grounds later considered unreliable.
- **Carl Gustaf Ahlström and collaborators:** compared Paris and Lund in 1983.
- **Philippe Charlier and collaborators:** conducted forensic, craniofacial, and CT-based studies supporting Paris.
- **Andreas Manhag and Per Karsten:** reconstructed the fragment histories and challenged Paris in 2020.
- **Académie des sciences:** first French institutional authenticator in 1821.
- **Muséum national d’Histoire naturelle / Musée de l’Homme:** legal and curatorial custodian of MNHN-HA-19220.
- **Museum Stobæanum / Lund University Historical Museum:** custodian of LUHM 508 and its associated vessel and papers.
- **French and Swedish diplomatic networks:** enabled the body’s original transport and later framed the skull’s return as national restitution.
- **Republican France:** attempted to mobilize Descartes’s remains as national remains through the unexecuted Panthéon decree.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

The skull has not served in a formal Christian liturgy and is not an officially canonized saint’s relic. Its documented uses are instead those of a **secular or scientific relic**.

### Funerary function

If removed in 1666, its first separate status resulted from interference with a reburial. The Paris cranium was offered back to France so that it might be reunited with the remains at Saint-Germain-des-Prés. That reunion was never performed.

### Collecting and display

In eighteenth-century Sweden, attributed pieces functioned as cabinet curiosities and objects of scholarly prestige. Possessing part of the philosopher’s head linked the collector materially to intellectual genius. The Lund fragment’s fitted cup resembles a reliquary and would have regulated storage and presentation.

Sparrman reportedly showed the intact candidate to Gustav IV Adolf. This was a private demonstration of possession and authenticity, not a transfer.

### National restitution

Berzelius treated his acquisition as corrective diplomacy. He said he wished to remedy what a Swede had done more than 160 years earlier. The object thereby became evidence simultaneously of Swedish misconduct and Swedish scientific honor.

### Scientific function

At the Muséum it became:

- an anatomical specimen;
- an object for portrait-based identification;
- part of collections associated with phrenology and the study of famous heads;
- a CT-scanned substrate for virtual reconstruction of an endocranial cast;
- a museum device for discussing relations between body, mind, mechanism, and human identity.

### Political-symbolic proposals

The skull has repeatedly been proposed for ceremonial transfer:

- the unexecuted revolutionary project concerned Descartes’s remains generally;
- in the 1990s and again around 2009, proposals associated with François Fillon sought transfer to La Flèche, where Descartes had studied;
- in 2010–11 French deputies proposed its solemn transfer to the Panthéon.

None displaced it from the Musée de l’Homme. Questions about authenticity and the propriety of moving or burying a museum-held human remain complicated the projects.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 “The captain stole the seat of genius”

**Tier:** eighteenth-century attested tradition; later institutional narrative.

The standard story says Isaac Planström, captain or officer of the Stockholm city guard supervising the 1666 exhumation, removed the skull and substituted another. He kept the true skull as the finest relic of the philosopher.

The first located publication dates from 1750, eighty-four years after the event. Arckenholtz repeated and elaborated it. The Paris inscription materializes the story on the bone and has often been treated by the museum as proof. In evidentiary terms, however, the inscription repeats a story rather than corroborating it.

### 7.2 Debt, creditors, and a brewer

**Tier:** later elaboration from the Planström tradition.

Popular chains say indebted Planström lost the skull to a creditor, commonly described as a Stockholm brewer, after which it passed to the brewer’s son and successive collectors. The general connection between Planström and debt occurs in eighteenth-century accounts. The continuous brewer-to-son custody sequence is not securely demonstrated by the sources examined here.

### 7.3 Skull blasting and numbered relics

**Tier:** modern scholarly hypothesis.

Manhag and Karsten propose that a genuine stolen cranium was filled with peas or grain and split along its sutures in 1691, creating numbered saleable relics. LUHM 508 would be “No. 6.”

Supporting circumstances are the fragment’s separation at sutures, its number, the existence of multiple eighteenth-century attributed fragments, and the commercial culture of learned relics. Against it stands the absence of a contemporary description of the procedure or a surviving set of numbered pieces.

### 7.4 Genius inhering in the skull

**Tier:** early-modern and nineteenth-century symbolic interpretation.

Terlon’s request for a hand bone was justified because the hand had produced Descartes’s immortal writings. The skull was even more readily imagined as the physical container of philosophical genius. Hof’s poem separated the smallness of the cranium from the worldwide reach of the mind.

Later phrenological and biometric attention translated this relic belief into purported science: the skull’s form was inspected for the bodily correlates of intellect. Modern neuroscience no longer accepts simple cranial shape as a measure of philosophical ability. The 2017 virtual endocast study offered anatomical description, not proof that intellectual achievement can be read from skull morphology.

### 7.5 The wrong body beneath Paris

**Tier:** modern source-critical reconstruction.

Because Lenoir reportedly opened a wooden coffin without the protocol deposited in the copper coffin in 1667, Manhag and Karsten contend that he recovered another person’s remains. In their reconstruction, Descartes’s transported body may remain under Rue Clovis.

The discrepancy is substantial. The exact survival and location of the 1667 coffin have not been demonstrated archaeologically. The claim remains unresolved.

### 7.6 “France possesses the thinker’s head”

**Tier:** national and institutional tradition.

Berzelius’s gift allowed France to claim that Descartes’s skull had returned home. Its separation from the body paradoxically strengthened its independent symbolic usefulness: it could be exhibited as the head associated with Cartesian reason. Lund’s recent counterclaim reverses that geography, asserting that a likely genuine piece remained in Sweden while France received a constructed relic.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Paris

**Object:** alleged cranium of René Descartes  
**Institution:** Musée de l’Homme, Muséum national d’Histoire naturelle  
**City:** Paris, France  
**Inventory:** **MNHN-HA-19220**  
**Material:** human bone  
**Status:** institutionally identified as Descartes’s skull and included in the permanent Galerie de l’Homme  
**Access:** public museum display, subject to gallery rotations and institutional conservation requirements  
**Condition:** nearly complete cranium; no mandible; teeth absent; inscribed surface; CT-scanned

The museum’s current object page repeats the Planström-theft narrative as fact and does not prominently incorporate the 2020 Lund challenge. A current gallery plan identifies the skull among the permanent-display highlights. There is no located active legal ownership or restitution proceeding. Proposed transfers to La Flèche or the Panthéon were political initiatives, not completed changes of title.

### Lund

**Object:** alleged fragment of René Descartes’s skull  
**Institution:** Lund University Historical Museum  
**City:** Lund, Sweden  
**Inventory:** **LUHM 508**  
**Associated object:** wooden cup/reliquary **LUHM 3150**  
**Material:** human left parietal bone  
**Status:** retained in the university collection and publicly discussed as a probably authentic candidate by museum researchers  
**Condition:** large, whitish-grey fragment, inscribed internally; no published DNA authentication

### The bodily remains

Bones traditionally identified as Descartes’s remain at Saint-Germain-des-Prés, Paris. Their identity is compromised by the coffin discrepancy. The documented 1666 hand/finger relic is lost. Other alleged skull fragments recorded in eighteenth-century collections have not been securely located.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Museum representation

The Paris skull acquired its modern public identity through scientific display rather than pilgrimage. Its placement in the Musée de l’Homme makes it part of a narrative about humanity, cognition, bodily diversity, and the history of scientific attempts to read character from anatomy.

The museum’s own presentation links the skull to Cartesian dualism and mechanism: Descartes’s body has become an exhibit used to discuss the thinker who separated thinking substance from extended substance.

### Replicas and casts

Berzelius requested a plaster cast after donating the cranium; surviving correspondence shows that Cuvier offered one, but the 2020 study says Berzelius’s request was never fulfilled.

A plaster bust incorporating a cast of the cranium is catalogued by the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris as *Buste de Descartes avec montage incorporé d’un moulage de son crâne*, showing the skull’s use in artistic-anatomical pedagogy.

Commercial “museum quality” replicas modeled on the Paris cranium are now sold in resin or resin-gypsum. Their existence extends the disputed object’s morphology independently of its authenticity: copies reproduce the Paris candidate, not an independently verified skull of Descartes.

### Literature

Important modern treatments include:

- Russell Shorto, *Descartes’ Bones: A Skeletal History of the Conflict Between Faith and Reason* (2008), which made the travels of the remains into a history of modern European ideas;
- Philippe Comar, *Mémoires de mon crâne: René Descartes* (1997), a literary retelling voiced through or centered upon the wandering skull;
- medical and popular articles on the alleged ethmoidal osteoma and virtual brain reconstruction;
- extensive press coverage after the 2020 Lund study, often framing the issue as a France–Sweden contest.

### Political mobilization

The skull has been invoked as:

- a relic of French rationalism;
- a possible object for national burial in the Panthéon;
- a heritage object associated with Descartes’s education at La Flèche;
- evidence in competing French and Swedish cultural claims.

These uses do not depend on biological authenticity. The proposals themselves are part of the object’s history.

### Ethics of display

The object participates in wider debate about displaying identifiable human remains. The Musée de l’Homme has publicly reconsidered the status and presentation of human bodies and holds a very large osteological collection. Descartes’s skull differs from remains acquired through colonial violence, but questions of consent, posthumous dignity, museum possession, and burial remain applicable.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Primary evidentiary base

The principal early sources are:

- Terlon’s 1665–66 correspondence concerning exhumation and transport;
- the exhumation protocols cited by Baillet, though their present archival status requires further investigation;
- Adrien Baillet, *La Vie de Monsieur Des-Cartes* (1691);
- Jöran Andersson Nordberg’s description and engraving of the Stockholm monument (1727);
- Sven Hof’s 1750 notice in *Lärda Tidningar*;
- Johan Arckenholtz’s memoirs, especially the 1751 and 1760 volumes;
- Lund’s 1780 Museum Stobæanum inventory;
- Sjöborg and Bergqvist’s 1808 dissertation;
- *Argus*, 14 March 1821;
- Berzelius’s letter to Cuvier, 6 April 1821;
- Académie des sciences minutes and Delambre’s memorandum, April–May 1821;
- Palmstedt’s 1866 historical publication.

### Nineteenth-century authentication

The 1821 inquiry privileged:

- inscriptions;
- reputation of named Swedish scholars;
- visual comparison with portraits;
- patriotic restoration.

Delambre’s skepticism remained a minority countercurrent. Once the Muséum accepted the object, institutional continuity gave the attribution additional authority.

### Twentieth-century anatomy

Fürst’s 1908 study excluded Lund on cranial-suture grounds. Later anthropology discredited that inference. The 1983 comparative study reopened the anatomical question but continued to give decisive weight to the Paris inscriptions.

This reveals the central historiographic problem: anatomy was interpreted through an assumed provenance, while the provenance was accepted partly because the anatomy was compatible.

### Twenty-first-century divergence

French work has concentrated on the Paris object’s morphology, facial correspondence, pathology, and virtual neuroanatomy. Swedish work has concentrated on inventories, inscriptions, ownership contradictions, and the material culture of collections.

The methods answer different questions:

- CT scanning can describe the Paris individual’s cranium;
- archival work can test whether the inscribed chain is credible;
- neither alone can establish biological identity.

The most consequential recent work is Manhag and Karsten’s chapter, “The true skull of Descartes?—A source critical study,” in *Collecting Curiosities* (2020). It did not genetically disprove Paris; it attacked the historical foundation on which prior anatomical confidence rested.

### Outstanding research needs

1. Full paleographic and chemical examination of every inscription on MNHN-HA-19220.
2. Pigment or tool-mark chronology to determine whether inscriptions were made together.
3. Direct radiocarbon dating, if ethically and curatorially acceptable.
4. Ancient-DNA comparison between Paris, Lund, and independently authenticated relatives.
5. Archival recovery of the 1666 protocols and complete diplomatic correspondence.
6. Archaeological or geophysical investigation of the former Sainte-Geneviève burial area, if legally feasible.
7. Re-examination of Saint-Germain-des-Prés remains and their containers.
8. Identification of Olofsson Bång’s skull, the Hägerflycht fragment, Arckenholtz’s fragment, and Terlon’s hand bone.
9. Complete study of the Lund cup, its wood, date, inscription, and Stiernman collection number.
10. A publicly accessible high-resolution transcription and imaging set for both candidates.
