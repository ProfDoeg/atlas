# The Kiswah (Kaaba's covering): Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

“The Kiswah” is not one materially continuous relic. It is a succession of coverings made for the Kaaba, normally replaced at intervals and, in recent centuries, annually. Individual historic kiswahs survive only as complete subsidiary curtains, large panels, fragments, reused textiles, photographs, workshop patterns, or pieces dispersed as official gifts and souvenirs. The current covering is the kiswah installed on 1 Muḥarram 1448 AH, 16 June 2026.

This dossier therefore tracks:

1. the continuing institution and object-type called the Kiswah;
2. individually identifiable coverings where dates, inscriptions, patrons, or catalogues permit;
3. the subsidiary textiles conventionally treated as parts of the kiswah ensemble; and
4. traditions about its pre-Islamic origin, without presuming that those traditions document a surviving textile.

Evidence is labeled as follows:

- **Documented:** supported by a contemporary inscription, dated object, government record, museum catalogue, or reasonably contemporary account.
- **Scientific finding:** obtained by material or conservation analysis.
- **Historical report:** preserved in a named historical source, sometimes written substantially after the event.
- **Scholarly inference:** a modern reconstruction from primary evidence.
- **Institutional or pious tradition:** maintained by a religious or governmental institution but not independently contemporary with the alleged event.
- **Disputed or unresolved:** incompatible accounts or insufficient evidence.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Identity and object class

The Kiswah is an architectural and ritual textile covering the exterior walls of the Kaaba in the Masjid al-Ḥarām, Mecca. In modern use it comprises four large black-silk wall coverings, a cotton lining, an embroidered belt, subsidiary panels and medallions, and a separate door curtain.

The Kaaba itself is not the kiswah. Nor should the following associated textiles automatically be identified as portions of the exterior covering:

- curtains and hangings for the Kaaba’s interior;
- the curtain of the internal **Bāb al-Tawbah**, or Door of Repentance;
- coverings of Maqām Ibrāhīm;
- textiles for the Prophet’s Mosque in Medina;
- the embroidered covering of the Egyptian or Syrian **maḥmal**, the ceremonial palanquin accompanying a pilgrimage caravan.

Museum and auction descriptions have sometimes used “kiswah” loosely for any textile made for Mecca or Medina. Identification must be tested by inscription, dimensions, form, patron, workshop record, and provenance.

### 1.2 Names

- Arabic: **كِسْوَة الكعبة**, *kiswat al-Kaʿbah*, “the clothing/covering of the Kaaba.”
- Also vocalized **كُسْوَة**, *kuswah*.
- Arabic plural: **كُسًا** or **كِسْوات / كُسْوات**.
- English spellings: *Kiswah, Kiswa, Kiswah of the Kaaba, Kaaba covering*.
- Turkish: **Kâbe örtüsü**, “Kaaba covering”; Ottoman sources also retain Arabic *kisve*.
- Persian and Urdu commonly use **کسوه** or expressions meaning the Kaaba’s garment.
- South Asian English commonly calls it **Ghilāf-e-Kaʿba**, from Persian/Urdu *ghilāf*, cover or case.

The Arabic root **k-s-w** means to clothe or provide clothing. *Lisān al-ʿArab* defines *al-kiswah/al-kuswah* as clothing, “one of the garments,” and explains *kasawtu fulānan* as clothing someone in a garment. The name thus treats the building metaphorically as a body that is dressed. [Arabic Academy](https://www.arabicacademy.gov.eg/ar/%D9%85%D8%AD%D8%B1%D9%83-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%A8%D8%AD%D8%AB/%D9%83%D9%8E%D8%B3%D8%A7?exact_search=true); [*Lisān al-ʿArab* entry](https://wiki.dorar-aliraq.net/lisan-alarab/%D9%83%D8%B3%D8%A7)

Specialized parts include:

- **ḥizām** (حزام), the gold-embroidered belt around the upper portion;
- **sitārah** (ستارة), curtain;
- **burquʿ/burqaʿ** (برقع), literally veil, used for the exterior door curtain;
- **burdah** (بردة), mantle, another historical name for the door curtain;
- **ṣamadīyah**, a square or medallion panel bearing Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ;
- **qanādīl**, lamp-shaped embroidered panels;
- **rannāt** or ropes and loops securing the covering;
- **izār**, a lower wrap or border, also the word used for the temporary white protection placed around the exposed lower walls during the annual raising of the black covering.

### 1.3 The 1448 AH/2026 exterior kiswah

According to the Saudi Press Agency, the covering installed on 16 June 2026 was made at the King Abdulaziz Complex for the Holy Kaaba Kiswah in Umm al-Jūd, Mecca, over approximately eleven months by about 150 Saudi craftspeople. It consists of 47 rolls or panels of black silk and weighs approximately 1,410 kilograms. [SPA installation report](https://www.spa.gov.sa/en/N2613391)

The published material totals are:

- 825 kg natural silk;
- 400 kg raw cotton for the lining;
- 60 kg pure silver used in embroidery;
- 120 kg silver wire plated with gold;
- 24-karat gold plating;
- 47 rolls or panels for the exterior covering.

These figures explain most, but not necessarily every kilogram of, the reported total. They should not be silently conflated with earlier figures—850, 980, or 1,415 kg—which describe different annual coverings or different accounting conventions. [SPA preparation report](https://www.spa.gov.sa/ar/N2613265?type=news&uuid=N2613265)

The silk is woven in black with repeated, comparatively unobtrusive devotional formulae visible through changes in weave. Prominent inscriptions and ornaments are built in padded relief and couched with silver or gold-plated silver wire. The modern ensemble has four principal wall sections and the door curtain. Published official descriptions of the closely related 1446–47 AH configurations enumerate:

- sixteen belt panels;
- seven panels beneath the belt;
- lamp-shaped inscriptions below it;
- four *ṣamadīyah* panels bearing Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ;
- ornaments around the waterspout, Black Stone, and Yemeni Corner;
- the door curtain;
- approximately sixty gilded fastening rings at the lower marble *shādhrawān*.

[Saudi official description of the 1446 replacement](https://www.uqn.gov.sa/details?p=25253); [official component summary](https://pep.gov.sa/en/news/483)

### 1.4 Dimensions

Official reports do not always give a single coherent set of finished dimensions, and dimensions have changed with construction and workshop practice. Frequently published modern measurements include:

- approximately 14 m height for each exterior drop;
- individual woven panels or rolls about 98 cm wide;
- belt approximately 47 m in total circuit and about 95 cm high;
- modern exterior door curtain approximately 6.35 m by 3.33 m in the 1447 AH report.

The 2025 covering weighed about 1,415 kg and used 825 kg silk, 410 kg cotton, 120 kg gold-plated silver thread, and 60 kg silver. Its door curtain measured 6.35 × 3.33 m. The minor difference from the 2026 totals demonstrates that “the standard modern kiswah” is not a perfectly invariant object. [SPA 1447 report](https://www.spa.gov.sa/fr/N2346999)

### 1.5 Construction

The present manufacturing sequence comprises:

1. water desalination or purification;
2. washing and preparing the natural silk;
3. dyeing;
4. plain and jacquard weaving;
5. printing or transferring full-scale inscription guides;
6. padded hand embroidery and couching of metal-wrapped threads;
7. sewing the panels to the cotton lining;
8. assembly, inspection, transport, and installation.

Modern weaving combines machine production with hand embroidery. Historical Egyptian manufacture similarly combined woven grounds, appliqué, card or cotton padding, and silver or silver-gilt wire couched over the raised forms.

### 1.6 Inscriptions

The exact distribution has varied by year and period. Modern coverings use Qurʾanic passages concerning the Kaaba, pilgrimage, God’s unity, and divine sovereignty, together with invocations and the patronal inscription.

Commonly documented texts include:

- Qurʾan 2:125: “And [remember] when We made the House a place of return for mankind and a place of security…”
- Qurʾan 2:144, on turning toward the sacred direction of prayer;
- Qurʾan 3:96–97: “Indeed, the first House established for mankind was that at Bakkah…” and the duty of pilgrimage;
- Qurʾan 22:26–27, concerning Abraham, purification of the House, and proclamation of pilgrimage;
- Qurʾan 112, Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ, on the four square *ṣamadīyah* panels;
- invocations including **يا حي يا قيوم** (*Yā Ḥayyu yā Qayyūm*, “O Ever-Living, O Self-Sustaining”), **يا رحمن يا رحيم**, and **الله أكبر**.

The 2026 Saudi announcement states that the new covering contains thirty Qurʾanic verses. The 2025 announcement counted sixty-eight verses, apparently using a different method of counting repeated or separate inscriptions. The discrepancy is documentary, not necessarily evidence of a radical redesign.

The door curtain has historically carried a denser program. A 1909 Ottoman example includes Qurʾan 27:30, “It is from Solomon, and it is: In the name of God, the Merciful, the Compassionate”; Qurʾan 17:80; Sūrat Quraysh; sovereign titles; and devotional formulae. [Sotheby’s 1909 burquʿ catalogue](https://www.sothebys.com/en/auctions/ecatalogue/2006/arts-of-the-islamic-world-l06220/lot.20.html)

### 1.7 Documented historic examples

Because most exterior coverings were cut up, complete door and interior curtains are disproportionately represented among survivors.

- **Khalili TXT 530:** fragment of a nineteenth-century door curtain, 59 × 47.5 cm, bearing Qurʾan 27:30 in mirrored *thuluth*. [Catalogue](https://khalilicollections.org/collections/hajj-and-the-arts-of-pilgrimage/fragment-from-a-sitarah-for-the-door-of-the-kabah-txt-530/)

These objects are related members of the textile succession. None is “the original Kiswah.”

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 What can be established

The practice of covering the Kaaba is pre-Islamic, but no securely identified pre-Islamic covering survives. Knowledge of the earliest coverings comes from Islamic-era literary accounts compiled centuries after some of the events they describe.

The Qurʾan refers repeatedly to the Kaaba or Sacred House but does not narrate the origin of its textile covering. The earliest extensive surviving dossier is in **al-Azraqī’s ninth-century *Akhbār Makkah***, supplemented by Ibn Isḥāq’s prophetic biography as preserved and edited by Ibn Hishām, al-Fākihī, and later Meccan historians. These sources preserve earlier chains of oral and written transmission, but their narratives are not contemporary inscriptions or textile records.

### 2.2 The Tubbaʿ origin tradition

**Institutional and literary tradition:** The first complete covering was supplied by the Himyarite ruler generally identified as Abū Karib Asʿad, called **Tubbaʿ**, conventionally placed in the early fifth century CE. In the developed story, he approached Mecca, received counsel from learned men, honored the sanctuary, saw in a dream that he should cover it, and successively tried palm-fiber cloth, striped Yemeni fabric, and fine cloth.

Al-Azraqī is often cited for the statement that Tubbaʿ was the first to cover the House completely. Ibn Hishām’s recension of Ibn Isḥāq also transmits the account. The texts are early evidence that ninth-century Muslims believed in a pre-Islamic Himyarite origin; they are not contemporary proof that a specific fifth-century textile was made or installed.

Other reports name:

- Adnan, an ancestor in Muhammad’s genealogy;
- Ismāʿīl;
- Qusayy ibn Kilāb;
- Khālid ibn Jaʿfar, said to have presented a silk-patterned cloth;
- Abū Rabīʿah ibn al-Mughīrah, a wealthy Quraysh merchant who supposedly alternated with the whole tribe in paying for the covering.

The multiplicity of “first coverer” claims results partly from differing definitions: first covering of any kind, first complete covering, first silk covering, first ruler to supply one, or first annual system. No candidate can be treated as the documented maker of a surviving object.

### 2.3 Quraysh and layered coverings

Al-Azraqī’s tradition says Quraysh assessed its clans for the cost of the covering. Gifts of textiles could be placed over earlier textiles rather than replacing them. This produced a layered accumulation of hides, carpets, striped cloths, and fine fabrics. The account is plausible as a description of votive accumulation but survives in later historiography.

### 2.4 Muhammad and the first Islamic covering

**Historical report:** At the conquest of Mecca in 630, Muhammad initially left the existing covering in place. A commonly repeated report says it was accidentally burned when a woman fumigated or perfumed the sanctuary and a spark reached the cloth. Muhammad then clothed the Kaaba in Yemeni textiles. Al-Māwardī states that he covered it with Yemeni cloth; al-Azraqī preserves related traditions. [Egyptian Ministry of Endowments survey](https://awkafonline.gov.eg/events-and-seasons-articles/241/10445/%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%85%D8%AD%D9%85%D9%84-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%B4%D8%B1%D9%8A%D9%81-%D9%88%D9%83%D8%B3%D9%88%D8%A9-%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%83%D8%B9%D8%A8%D8%A9-%D8%B9%D8%A8%D8%B1-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%B9%D8%B5%D9%88%D8%B1)

No fragment is known, and the narrative was recorded later. It nevertheless constitutes the traditional beginning of the Islamic kiswah succession.

### 2.5 The early caliphate

Historical reports attribute Egyptian **qabāṭī**, fine white linen associated with Coptic Egyptian production, to ʿUmar and ʿUthmān. Muʿāwiyah reportedly supplied two coverings annually—fine linen and silk brocade—and established periodic stripping away of the previous layers. ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Zubayr supplied coverings after rebuilding the fire-damaged Kaaba in the 680s.

The fabrics, colors, and replacement dates vary between sources. No securely catalogued Rashidun or Umayyad covering survives.

### 2.6 Abbasid system and the establishment of black

In 160 AH/776–77, Caliph al-Mahdī found multiple old coverings burdening the building. Meccan attendants reportedly warned that their weight endangered the walls. He ordered the accumulated textiles removed, perfumed the walls, and installed a replacement. This episode is important because it marks the conceptual change from cumulative offerings to a regularly stripped and renewed architectural covering.

Al-Maʾmūn was said to cover the Kaaba three times yearly:

- red silk brocade around the pilgrimage season;
- Egyptian *qabāṭī* at the beginning of Rajab;
- white brocade around Ramadan or the festival.

Black became dominant under the Abbasids, whose dynastic color was black. Later tradition often credits al-Nāṣir, who ruled from 1180 to 1225, with fixing black as the enduring exterior color after experiments with green. The evidence supports a gradual development rather than a single unambiguous invention.

### 2.7 Material origins

Known or reported sources shifted with political control and textile supply:

- Yemeni striped and woven fabrics in pre-Islamic and early Islamic reports;
- Egyptian linen and later silk;
- Iraqi brocade under caliphal patronage;
- Yemeni supplies during periods when Baghdad’s authority weakened;
- Cairo workshops under Fatimid, Ayyubid, Mamluk, Ottoman, khedival, and royal Egyptian patronage;
- Istanbul production for some internal textiles;
- Meccan manufacture under the Saudi state.

The modern raw silk is imported before processing in Mecca; the precise country of origin for the silk used in each current covering is not consistently disclosed in official reports. Claims that every component is wholly “Saudi-made” normally refer to processing, weaving, design, embroidery, and assembly, not necessarily cultivation of the silk or mining of the metals.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 Pre-Islamic custody: traditional chain

The traditional custody sequence runs from Himyarite or ancestral patrons to Quraysh. Because coverings were consumable and often layered, this is not a chain of custody for one object. It is a sequence of asserted patronage.

The Kaaba’s custodial family, the Banū Shaybah, descended in Islamic tradition from ʿUthmān ibn Ṭalḥah, retained responsibility for the building’s key and participated in receiving and installing textiles. Their custody should not be conflated with personal ownership of every covering.

### 3.2 Muhammad and the early caliphs, 630–660s

The inherited pre-Islamic covering remained briefly after the conquest, was reportedly destroyed accidentally by fire, and was replaced with Yemeni cloth. Abū Bakr is not consistently credited with a distinct replacement. ʿUmar and ʿUthmān commissioned Egyptian linen coverings.

The textile chain is wholly broken: no individual cloth from this phase is known.

### 3.3 Umayyad and Ibn al-Zubayr periods

Muʿāwiyah regularized multiple annual coverings and removal of obsolete cloth. During the siege of Mecca in 683, the Kaaba and its textile furnishings burned. Sources dispute whether the fire began from the besiegers’ projectiles or from a flame within Ibn al-Zubayr’s encampment; both versions were politically useful. A historical compilation preserves the competing accounts and describes the building as wearing carpets below and striped cloth above when the fire spread. [Account of the 683 fire](https://dorar.net/history/event/295)

Ibn al-Zubayr rebuilt the Kaaba and covered it. A second Umayyad siege in 692 led to another reconstruction under al-Ḥajjāj. No covering can be demonstrated to have survived either episode.

### 3.4 Abbasid custody

Abbasid caliphs supplied textiles through imperial and Egyptian production systems. Their names and dates increasingly appeared on the textiles, making coverings instruments of political presence in Mecca.

The destruction of Baghdad in 1258 ended the Abbasid court’s practical control of the supply. This did not destroy one continuous “Abbasid kiswah”; it ended an institutional commissioning chain.

### 3.5 Fatimid, Yemeni, Iraqi, Ayyubid, and rival coverings

When central authority fragmented, rulers in Egypt, Yemen, Iraq, and elsewhere competed to send coverings. Installing a ruler’s textile, especially when it bore his name, publicly asserted his claim to protect or dominate the sanctuary.

Some rival gifts were accepted as internal coverings or passed through Cairo under conditions preserving the Egyptian ruler’s precedence. Other attempted offerings were refused. A 1444 Timurid example discussed below demonstrates that an apparently devotional gift could become a diplomatic contest over custody and sovereignty.

### 3.6 Mamluk institutionalization

Baybars is associated with the formal Mamluk pilgrimage caravan and kiswah display in 1266. The Egyptian sultan’s control of the covering became a public assertion of authority over the Hijaz. The maḥmal accompanied the caravan but was not itself the container of the entire covering in the simple sense often claimed; the textiles and supplies traveled with a larger convoy.

In 750 AH/1349–50, Sultan al-Ṣāliḥ Ismāʿīl ibn al-Nāṣir Muḥammad endowed the revenue of the Egyptian villages of **Baysūs** and **Sandabīs** for an annual Kaaba covering and periodic textiles for Medina. When the endowment’s revenues became insufficient, later rulers supplemented them from the treasury and added property.

Mamluk authorities attempted to monopolize the honor. Al-Maqrīzī reports that Sultan Qalāwūn obtained a promise from the Meccan sharif Abū Numayy not to accept a covering from elsewhere. [Scholarly study of political competition](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/16/3/358)

In 848 AH/1444, Timurid ruler Shāh Rukh was allowed to send an interior covering, but only through Egypt and with the Egyptian caravan. This compromise permitted the devotional gift while denying an independent Timurid ceremonial entry. [IFAO study](https://www.ifao.egnet.net/anisl/38/20)

### 3.7 Ottoman transfer, 1517 onward

After Selim I conquered Egypt in 1517 and Ottoman supremacy was recognized in the Hijaz, responsibility passed to the Ottoman sultans. Manufacture remained substantially Egyptian, preserving Cairo’s workshops, artisans, endowments, and caravan infrastructure under Ottoman sovereignty.

Under Süleyman I, some interior textiles began to be made in Istanbul while exterior textiles continued to come principally from Egypt. Later Ottoman arrangements moved particular internal furnishings or their fabrics between Istanbul and Cairo. General statements that “all kiswah manufacture moved to Istanbul” are too broad: surviving Cairo-made exterior curtains and workshop records show continuing Egyptian production.

Removed textiles followed several paths:

- return to Istanbul and entry into the imperial treasury;
- preservation in the Chamber of Sacred Relics at Topkapı;
- presentation to dignitaries;
- use as wall hangings;
- placement over royal or elite tombs;
- division and distribution in Mecca.

Thus Istanbul, Cairo, Mecca, Ottoman mausolea, mosques, and private collections became secondary custodial sites.

### 3.8 War and interruption

French occupation of Egypt in 1798 interrupted the normal Cairo system. A replacement was prepared in the courtyard of the Sultan Ahmed Mosque in Istanbul. This was an emergency assertion that Ottoman sovereign responsibility continued despite the loss of Cairo. [Study of the Sultanahmet covering](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/lale/article/1623943)

In 1806, during the first Saudi state’s control of Mecca, Imām Saʿūd ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz supplied a covering described as red; black was subsequently restored. The episode shows that the modern black appearance was established but not absolutely invariant.

### 3.9 Dar al-Kiswah at Khurunfish

Muḥammad ʿAlī established or reorganized the **Warshat/Dār al-Kiswah** at Khurunfish in Cairo in 1817. It housed weaving, dyeing, pattern transfer, sewing, embroidery, and metal-working operations. European machinery and imported materials were incorporated alongside inherited manual techniques.

A late nineteenth-century observer, ʿAlī Mubārak, found that the larger workshop complex had declined and was making principally the kiswah, but it later recovered. The dated 1897–98 Met curtain demonstrates both Cairo manufacture and participation in global commodity networks.

### 3.10 The 1926 maḥmal confrontation

After ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz captured Mecca in 1924–25, Egyptian and Saudi understandings of the maḥmal ceremony collided. Najdi forces and pilgrims objected to music and aspects of the ceremonial procession as unlawful innovations. On 18 June 1926, Najdis confronted or attacked the Egyptian escort; the Egyptian guard opened fire. Accounts differ on the exact sequence, casualties, and whether stones or weapons were used first.

The confrontation disrupted diplomatic relations. It should be called a violent political-religious conflict, not merely a picturesque “end of tradition.” The covering itself was not necessarily destroyed, but the route and authority under which future coverings would enter Mecca became untenable.

### 3.11 First Saudi workshop, 1927–1937

King ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz ordered a Meccan kiswah workshop at Ajyād in 1346 AH/1927. Indian master weavers and other specialists helped establish production. An official 1349 AH covering bore an inscription stating that it had been made in Mecca by command of King ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz, King of the Hijaz, Najd, and dependencies. [Saudi Official Gazette retrospective](https://www.uqn.gov.sa/details?p=19216)

The Meccan workshop made coverings for roughly a decade. Following the 1936 Egyptian–Saudi settlement, Egyptian production resumed in 1937 or 1938; sources differ by one or two years depending on whether treaty, manufacture, dispatch, or installation is counted.

### 3.12 Egyptian resumption and final rupture

Cairo again supplied the exterior covering until 1962. The maḥmal parade itself had already been curtailed or abolished, but textile manufacture continued.

In 1962 an Egyptian ship arrived at Jeddah with pilgrims and the kiswah ensemble. Saudi authorities did not accept the covering; accounts variously say it was refused at Jeddah, returned, or withdrawn by Egypt amid the severe political rupture associated with the North Yemen conflict and Egyptian–Saudi rivalry. The exact initiative is described differently in Egyptian and Saudi narratives. It is safest to record that the Egyptian covering was not installed and that Saudi production became exclusive from that year. [Ahram account](https://english.ahram.org.eg/UI/Front/Inner.aspx?NewsContentID=375060); [comparative historical study](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/16/3/358)

### 3.13 Modern Saudi manufacture

A temporary or renewed factory at Jarwal produced the 1381 AH/1962 covering. Its inscription named King Saʿūd as donor. In 1397 AH/1977, a purpose-built factory opened at Umm al-Jūd on the old Mecca–Jeddah road, combining mechanized weaving with hand embroidery. [General Authority history](https://alharamain.gov.sa/public/?page=kaaba-kiswah-ar); [SPA factory report](https://www.spa.gov.sa/488218)

In 2018 the factory was renamed the **King Abdulaziz Complex for the Holy Kaaba Kiswah**. Administrative control now lies with the General Authority for the Care of the Affairs of the Grand Mosque and the Prophet’s Mosque.

### 3.14 Annual removal and dispersal

The old covering is removed during installation of the new. It does not ordinarily pass intact into a public museum accession. Officials detach reusable or especially valuable embroidered elements, and the retired fabric is divided. Portions may be:

- retained by Saudi institutions;
- presented to foreign governments or international organizations;
- given to mosques, museums, exhibitions, or dignitaries;
- incorporated into framed gifts;
- enter private collections and, eventually, the auction market.

This dispersal is deliberate and anciently rationalized through a report attributed to ʿĀʾishah: old coverings could be sold and the proceeds spent on the poor and travelers. The practice prevents a single comprehensive chain of custody after removal.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 There is no materially continuous original

The principal authenticity error is the phrase “the original Kiswah.” The institution is continuous in a qualified historical sense; its matter is not. The current covering was manufactured in 2025–26 and installed in June 2026. It is not the cloth associated with Tubbaʿ, Muhammad, an Abbasid caliph, or an Ottoman sultan.

Continuity is ritual, functional, locational, and institutional—not textile identity.

### 4.2 Criteria for authenticating a fragment

A credible attribution may rest on:

- a sovereign’s name or *ṭughrā*;
- a Hijri date;
- a patronal formula;
- verses and layouts matching a known workshop pattern;
- dimensions and seam placement;
- documented official gift;
- photographs of presentation;
- provenance to Meccan custodians, an imperial treasury, or a recognized recipient;
- fiber, dye, and metal-thread analysis;
- comparison with full-scale workshop stencils.

An inscription alone is not conclusive: modern replicas can reproduce it. Conversely, absence of a date does not prove a fragment false because many repeating ground and border panels were undated.

### 4.3 Confusion among related sacred textiles

Auction and private-market objects are frequently described as “from the Kiswah” when they may instead come from:

- a Medina tomb or mosque curtain;
- a maḥmal covering;
- a covering for Maqām Ibrāhīm;
- an interior Kaaba curtain;
- a modern commemorative panel made at or in the style of the workshop but never installed.

The British Museum’s 2016,6030.2, for example, is a black silk and precious-metal-thread curtain for the Prophet’s Mosque at Medina, not an exterior Kaaba covering. Its correct catalogue entry illustrates why black silk and gold embroidery are insufficient identifiers. [British Museum catalogue](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_2016-6030-2)

### 4.4 Scientific examination of the 1897–98 Bāb al-Tawbah curtain

The most substantial published technical examination concerns the Met’s interior curtain, not the present exterior kiswah. Conservation revealed a paper slip sewn between the black silk and cotton lining, marked “year 315,” establishing 1315 AH/1897–98.

Analysis included:

- microscopy of fibers and weave;
- X-radiography;
- metal-wire examination;
- scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive spectroscopy;
- crystallographic examination of wire microstructure;
- high-performance liquid chromatography with photodiode-array detection of dyes.

Findings included:

- high-purity silver wire, with gilding on selected wires;
- manufacture compatible with late nineteenth-century wire-drawing practice;
- indigo in the black ground, not securely distinguishable as natural or synthetic;
- early synthetic organic dyes in colored appliqués;
- tannin treatment of silk, probably for weighting and handling;
- no detected tin weighting of the sampled silk;
- fading of unstable synthetic pink and green dyes;
- corrosion-related deterioration near silver elements;
- a trademark on cotton lining connecting the Cairo workshop with an industrial bleach mill in northwestern England.

The evidence supports, rather than undermines, the inscribed date and Cairo attribution. It also disproves any simplistic notion that a sacred Ottoman textile necessarily used only local or preindustrial materials. [Metropolitan Museum Journal technical study](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/Metropolitan_Museum_Journal_v_52_2017.pdf)

### 4.5 Provenance gap in the Met curtain

The curtain’s inscription names Sultan Abdülhamid II and Khedive ʿAbbās Ḥilmī II, and the internal date was discovered during conservation. Its later custody before acquisition by Maan Z. Madina is less fully published. The museum records Madina’s 2009 gift but does not publish an uninterrupted chain from removal in Mecca to his possession. Authenticity of manufacture is strongly supported; complete modern provenance is not publicly reconstructed.

### 4.6 Uncertain Bāb al-Tawbah identifications

The Met study notes that some auctioned curtains labeled as Bāb al-Tawbah hangings are smaller and differ iconographically from securely comparable examples. Their intended architectural position remains unresolved. This is a genuine classification question rather than proof of forgery.

### 4.7 Replicas and “workshop-made” pieces

Modern commemorative panels may be produced using comparable techniques and materials. A piece can therefore be:

1. installed on the Kaaba;
2. produced as a spare;
3. produced at the same complex but never installed;
4. a later authorized replica;
5. an unofficial commercial imitation.

Only the first is literally a removed portion of an installed kiswah. Sales descriptions often obscure these distinctions. No publicly accessible universal serial registry for distributed fragments has been identified.

### 4.8 Scientific limits

No published radiocarbon program has established the age of the kiswah institution or authenticated a supposed pre-Islamic fragment. Radiocarbon dating would in any case date organic fiber, not prove installation on the Kaaba. For recent pieces, inscriptions and documentary provenance are more discriminating than radiocarbon.

No DNA analysis relevant to authenticity has been reported. Claims that a fragment is “from the Prophet’s Kiswah” or an early caliphal covering remain unverified unless supported by exceptional archaeological provenance, of which none is presently published.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Divine and prophetic associations

- **God/Allah:** The Kaaba is designated the Sacred House and direction of prayer; inscriptions frame the covering as service to God, not as clothing for an embodied deity.
- **Abraham/Ibrāhīm and Ishmael/Ismāʿīl:** Qurʾanic narratives link them to raising or purifying the House. Modern kiswah inscriptions quote passages concerning Abraham and pilgrimage. This is scriptural association, not evidence that either made a textile.
- **Muhammad:** Historical tradition credits him with retaining the inherited covering briefly and then supplying Yemeni cloth after it burned. Reports associated with his family also shaped disposal of old coverings.
- **ʿĀʾishah:** A report attributed to her supports selling old coverings and giving proceeds to the poor and travelers.

### Custodians and makers

- **Banū Shaybah / Āl al-Shaybī:** hereditary key-holding and custodial family; receive or participate in receiving the new covering and maintain access to the building.
- **Quraysh clans:** traditionally pooled the cost before Islam.
- **Coptic Egyptian weavers:** associated with *qabāṭī* linen supplied under early caliphs; individual makers are unnamed.
- **Dār al-Kiswah craftspeople:** Cairo’s specialist weavers, dyers, embroiderers, calligraphers, and metal-thread workers.
- **King Abdulaziz Complex craftspeople:** modern Saudi workforce responsible for preparation, weaving, embroidery, assembly, maintenance, and replacement.

### Named patrons and political holders of commissioning authority

- **Tubbaʿ Abū Karib Asʿad:** traditional first complete coverer; fifth-century ruler, attested only in later literary accounts.
- **Muhammad:** traditional first Islamic patron.
- **ʿUmar and ʿUthmān:** patrons of Egyptian linen coverings.
- **Muʿāwiyah:** credited with two annual coverings and systematic removal.
- **ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Zubayr:** rebuilt and re-covered the Kaaba after the 683 fire.
- **al-Mahdī:** ordered accumulated layers removed in 776–77.
- **al-Maʾmūn:** associated with three annual changes.
- **al-Nāṣir:** associated with making black the lasting exterior color.
- **Baybars:** developed the Mamluk caravan and public claim to the covering.
- **Qalāwūn:** asserted Egyptian exclusivity.
- **al-Ṣāliḥ Ismāʿīl:** endowed villages for recurring manufacture in 1349–50.
- **Shāh Rukh:** permitted to send an interior covering through Egyptian channels in 1444.
- **Selim I:** inherited the system after conquest of Egypt in 1517.
- **Süleyman I and later Ottoman sultans:** commissioned exterior and interior sanctuary textiles.
- **Sultan Abdülhamid II:** named on the Met 1897–98 curtain.
- **Khedive ʿAbbās Ḥilmī II:** supervised its Egyptian manufacture and appears in its inscription.
- **Muḥammad ʿAlī:** founded or reorganized the Khurunfish workshop in 1817.
- **King Ḥusayn of the Hejaz:** gave kiswah pieces to Queen Mary in 1918.
- **King ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz:** founded the first Saudi workshop in 1927.
- **King Saʿūd:** patron named on the 1381 AH/1962 Meccan covering.
- **Kings Fayṣal, Khālid, Fahd, ʿAbd Allāh, and Salmān:** successive Saudi patrons of factory expansion and modernization.

### Calligraphers

- **ʿAbd Allāh Zuhdī:** designed inscriptions for nineteenth-century Kaaba and Medina textiles; his stencils continued in use after his death in 1879.
- **ʿAbd al-Raḥīm Amīn:** twentieth-century/early twenty-first-century calligrapher whose signature appears on a 1424 AH/2003–04 Saudi door-curtain design. [Documented example](https://artofhajj.com/en/collections/p066--sitarah-for-the-door-of-the-kabah-embroidered-on-kiswah-fabric-presented-by-king-fahd)

### Institutional networks

- Meccan custodians and the administration of the Masjid al-Ḥarām;
- caliphal treasuries and textile workshops;
- Egyptian *awqāf* administration;
- Mamluk and Ottoman pilgrimage-caravan commands;
- Ottoman imperial treasury and Topkapı’s Sacred Relics collection;
- Egyptian Ministry of Endowments;
- Saudi General Authority for the Care of the Affairs of the Two Holy Mosques;
- King Abdulaziz Complex;
- museums, diplomatic gift collections, auction houses, and private collectors receiving retired sections.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Dressing the sanctuary

The kiswah marks the Kaaba as a protected, honored sacred structure. The act of clothing a building draws on courtly and devotional practices of presenting costly textiles, but its Islamic meaning is tied to reverence for the sanctuary and service to pilgrims.

The cloth does not itself define the validity of pilgrimage. The Kaaba remains the qibla and site of circumambulation when the textile is lifted or being replaced.

### 6.2 Annual replacement

The replacement date has varied:

- early reports describe Ramadan, Rajab, ʿĀshūrāʾ, and pilgrimage-season changes;
- later practice centered on the Hajj, commonly 8 or 9 Dhū al-Ḥijjah;
- from 2022 Saudi authorities transferred the annual installation to 1 Muḥarram.

The covering installed in 1447 AH was replaced between midnight and 6:40 a.m. on 1 Muḥarram, demonstrating the highly organized modern operation. [SPA 1447 completion report](https://spa.gov.sa/N2347889)

Installation proceeds wall by wall from the roof. The new panels are raised, fixed at the top, lowered over the old covering, aligned and sewn together; the old textile is removed beneath them. Corners, openings, belt panels, ornaments, door curtain, and lower fastenings are then adjusted.

### 6.3 Raising during Hajj

Before the Hajj season, workers raise the lower portion about three meters and cover the exposed transition with a white cotton band approximately 2.5 m wide and 54 m around. An official report describes thirty-six specialists and ten lifting devices.

The stated reason is preservation: large crowds touch, pull, cut, or soil the textile. Saudi authorities explicitly reject beliefs that cutting or taking the cloth provides independent blessing and describe such cutting as tampering. [SPA report on raising the kiswah](https://spa.gov.sa/N2109024); [earlier official explanation](https://www.spa.gov.sa/w1918203)

Popular language sometimes calls the exposed white band the “iḥrām of the Kaaba.” That is metaphor, not evidence that the building performs the pilgrim’s legal state of *iḥrām*.

### 6.4 Perfuming, maintenance, and repair

The Kaaba and its textile are perfumed. Modern technical teams inspect the covering throughout the year, mend tears, reinforce fastenings, and repair damage from wind, rain, heat, crowd contact, and abrasion.

The covering is therefore altered during its installed life. A removed modern kiswah may contain repairs and replaced seams; “original condition” should not be assumed.

### 6.5 Political legitimation

Control over the kiswah historically signaled:

- practical access to Mecca;
- security of the pilgrimage route;
- wealth sufficient to commission costly cloth;
- recognition of a ruler’s name at Islam’s principal sanctuary;
- a claim to guardianship of the holy cities.

The Mamluk refusal of rival coverings, Ottoman continuation after 1517, emergency manufacture during the French occupation, Saudi creation of a workshop after 1926, and final exclusion of the Egyptian covering in 1962 were political acts conducted through a ritual textile.

### 6.6 Barakah and relic status

Removed sections have been treated as carrying **barakah**, blessing derived from contact with the sanctuary. They have been touched, kept, displayed, placed on tombs, framed, and given diplomatically.

This veneration does not produce a uniform legal or theological doctrine. Saudi official explanations discourage cutting or touching the installed covering for blessing, while governments continue presenting authenticated retired portions as honored gifts. The two practices distinguish unauthorized physical appropriation from controlled custodial distribution.

### 6.7 Access and taboos

- Pilgrims can see and, when barriers permit, touch lower areas, though touching the kiswah is not a prescribed component of Hajj or ʿumrah.
- Cutting or tearing it is forbidden.
- Entry into the Kaaba and access to internal curtains are restricted to custodians, officials, invited dignitaries, and workers.
- Handling during manufacture and installation is controlled institutionally.
- Removed pieces are not intrinsically available for public purchase from the official complex merely because commercial sellers claim them.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 Tubbaʿ’s dream

**Early Islamic literary tradition:** Tubbaʿ dreams repeatedly that he should cover the House. He improves the materials until it is fully clothed and instructs successors to preserve the practice.

Variants differ on:

- the ruler’s exact identity;
- whether Jewish learned men converted or advised him;
- the sequence of palm fiber, Yemeni striped cloth, and fine fabric;
- whether he was the first ever or merely the first to use a complete textile.

The story linked the Kaaba to an honorable pre-Islamic monotheistic past and incorporated Himyarite royal memory into Islamic sacred geography.

### 7.2 Ismāʿīl or Adnan as first coverer

**Later genealogical and pious traditions:** Ismāʿīl or Adnan is credited with the first covering. These accounts place the practice deeper in Abrahamic or Arab ancestry. They are not supported by a surviving early inscription or textile.

### 7.3 The accidental burning under Muhammad

**Historical tradition:** A woman fumigating the covering accidentally sets it alight, after which Muhammad supplies a new Yemeni covering. The episode explains both destruction of the inherited cloth and the beginning of an Islamic replacement. Its documentary basis is later historical transmission, not a contemporary eyewitness manuscript.

### 7.4 The weight that threatened the Kaaba

**Strong early historical report:** By al-Mahdī’s pilgrimage, old coverings had accumulated until their weight was feared to endanger the walls. The caliph orders them removed.

Even if embellished, the story explains a major material transition: offerings ceased to be indefinitely superimposed, allowing each new textile to replace rather than merely cover its predecessors.

### 7.5 Black as timeless sacred color

**Modern misconception:** The Kaaba has not always worn black. Sources report white, red, green, striped, and multicolored coverings. Black became dominant through Abbasid and later practice. Treating the modern color scheme as unchanged from Abraham or Muhammad is a late projection.

### 7.6 “The maḥmal carried the kiswah”

The maḥmal is often represented as a camel litter containing the covering. In developed Mamluk and Ottoman ceremonies it was principally an emblematic, often empty palanquin, while the kiswah and supplies traveled in the caravan’s baggage. It accompanied and symbolized the gift; it was not invariably its literal container.

### 7.7 Miraculous or automatic protection

Claims that the kiswah itself protects the Kaaba from all physical harm are devotional interpretations, not descriptions borne out by material history. Coverings have burned, torn, faded, been cut, soiled, and replaced. The documented sacred response has been maintenance and renewal.

### 7.8 “The Kaaba goes into iḥrām”

This is a modern popular metaphor for raising the black covering and exposing the white protective band. Official reports explain the operation in conservation and crowd-management terms.

### 7.9 Fragment legends

Private fragments often acquire family stories: an ancestor received one after pilgrimage, an Ottoman official brought it back, or it came from a particular sovereign’s covering. Such claims may be true, but without an inscription, documented presentation, or continuous provenance they remain unverified. Contact with Mecca cannot be inferred from appearance alone.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Installed covering

As of 6 September 2026, the installed exterior textile is the 1448 AH kiswah placed on the Kaaba on 16 June 2026. It was manufactured at the King Abdulaziz Complex at Umm al-Jūd and remains under the control of the General Authority for the Care of the Affairs of the Grand Mosque and the Prophet’s Mosque.

It is in active ritual and architectural use, visible to pilgrims but not a museum object. Physical access is managed within the security and operational regime of the Grand Mosque.

### 8.2 Condition

No official report located for this dossier records major damage to the 1448 AH textile after installation. It is subject to scheduled inspection and repair. It will normally remain until the beginning of 1449 AH unless emergency replacement becomes necessary.

### 8.3 Legal status

The installed covering is under Saudi state and sanctuary administration. Removed fragments may enter distinct legal categories depending on date, registration, state ownership, and mode of gift.

Saudi heritage law restricts sale or export of registered movable antiquities and requires documentation for protected heritage objects. It does not follow that every recent officially gifted fragment is inalienable, but sellers must still establish lawful title and provenance. [Saudi antiquities and museums law](https://laws.boe.gov.sa/BoeLaws/Laws/LawDetails/a7d4493b-7c03-4c2d-b3c6-a9a700f275cd/1?csrt=7497416880301807365)

### 8.4 Retired and dispersed coverings

There is no single current repository for all retired coverings. Significant holdings include:

- Topkapı Palace Museum, Istanbul;
- King Abdulaziz Complex and Saudi exhibitions;
- Egyptian textile and Islamic collections;
- Khalili Collections;
- Metropolitan Museum of Art;
- Royal Collection;
- United Nations Headquarters;
- mosques, royal collections, diplomatic collections, museums, and private holdings worldwide.

The Khalili Hajj collection includes exterior and interior kiswah elements, belts, external and internal door curtains, Maqām Ibrāhīm coverings, maḥmals, full-size workshop designs, and modern Saudi pieces. [Khalili collection overview](https://khalilicollections.org/all-collections/hajj-and-the-arts-of-pilgrimage/)

### 8.5 Custody disputes

No active international repatriation proceeding over the installed kiswah was identified. Questions surrounding dispersed pieces generally concern provenance, classification, authenticity, or lawful sale rather than a unified demand that all retired textiles be returned.

The larger historical dispute—Egyptian, Ottoman, Hashemite, or Saudi authority to commission the covering—was resolved in practice, not by adjudication: Saudi Arabia has controlled manufacture and installation since 1962.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Diplomatic gifts

Retired kiswah pieces have become instruments of state diplomacy. They communicate guardianship of the holy places and provide recipients with a material connection to Mecca.

Documented examples include:

- King Ḥusayn’s 1918 gift to Queen Mary;
- the Saudi door curtain presented to the United Nations, associated with a 1980s presentation;
- gifts to heads of state, mosques, museums, and exhibitions.

The UN’s display labels the object as a former door covering, though the public catalogue does not provide a detailed annual installation history or laboratory report.

### 9.2 Museum transformation

Once removed, catalogued, and displayed behind glass, a textile moves from active architectural use to relic, diplomatic object, textile specimen, and work of Islamic art. Museum practice often emphasizes:

- calligraphy;
- metal-thread embroidery;
- patronal inscriptions;
- workshop technique;
- political sovereignty;
- pilgrimage memory.

The change of setting can obscure its former scale and exposed condition. A framed fragment is not representative of the moving, weathered, continuously handled complete covering.

### 9.3 Auction market

Complete door curtains and large panels have appeared at Sotheby’s, Christie’s, and Bonhams. Auction catalogues are valuable for inscriptions and dimensions but are interested-party records. Their provenance statements require independent checking.

The high value of authenticated fragments encourages:

- overbroad use of “Kiswah”;
- attribution of modern workshop pieces as installed cloth;
- vague family provenances;
- replicas marketed without clear labeling;
- division of larger panels into more saleable framed pieces.

No comprehensive published corpus of forged kiswah fragments exists, so absence from a forgery list is not authentication.

### 9.4 Replicas, souvenirs, and commercial imagery

Black textiles with gold Qurʾanic calligraphy are reproduced as:

- wall hangings;
- prayer-space decorations;
- miniature Kaaba models;
- printed pilgrimage souvenirs;
- jewelry and key rings;
- mosque curtains;
- educational replicas.

Commercial use has made the black-and-gold appearance globally recognizable, but it also encourages the historically false impression that the visual form is primordial and unchanged.

### 9.5 Photography, film, and broadcast

Nineteenth-century travel accounts described the covering; twentieth-century photography documented Egyptian caravans, workshop production, and installation. Saudi television and internet broadcasting have made the annual replacement a global media ceremony.

Images of workers lifting panels or the temporary white band regularly generate inaccurate online claims that the Kaaba has “changed color,” lost its covering, or entered a literal state of iḥrām.

### 9.6 National narratives

Egyptian presentations emphasize centuries of Egyptian textile skill and the Khurunfish workshop. Saudi presentations emphasize the 1927 foundation, the 1962 transition, national craftsmen, modern technology, and service to the Two Holy Mosques. Ottoman scholarship emphasizes imperial custodianship and the Sacred Relics treasury.

Each narrative preserves genuine evidence but tends to foreground its own state’s period of control. The object’s history is transregional: Yemeni, Iraqi, Egyptian, Meccan, Ottoman, Indian, European-industrial, and modern Saudi contributions all appear in the record.

### 9.7 Contemporary workshop tourism

The King Abdulaziz Complex is a controlled visitor attraction where approved visitors can see aspects of weaving and embroidery. Its public presentation combines working factory, heritage demonstration, and state narrative. [Visit Saudi](https://www.visitsaudi.com/en/destinations/makkah/attractions/the-kiswa-factory-of-the-holy-kaaba-in-makkah)

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Primary textual foundations

The principal early sources include:

- al-Azraqī, *Akhbār Makkah*;
- Ibn Isḥāq/Ibn Hishām, *Sīrat Rasūl Allāh*;
- al-Fākihī, *Akhbār Makkah*;
- al-Ṭabarī;
- al-Māwardī;
- Nāṣir-i Khusraw’s *Safarnāmah*;
- al-Maqrīzī;
- al-Qalqashandī;
- Ibn Taghrībirdī;
- al-Jazīrī;
- Quṭb al-Dīn al-Nahrawālī;
- Ottoman surre registers, treasury inventories, and Egyptian endowment records;
- ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Jabartī for late Ottoman/Egyptian practice;
- Richard Burton for a detailed nineteenth-century outsider’s description.

These sources differ in genre and temporal distance. Later Meccan compilations frequently repeat al-Azraqī while adding traditions current in their own periods.

### 10.2 Earliest-attestation problem

Scholars agree that covering preceded Islam but cannot date the first covering securely. The Tubbaʿ story is an early attested tradition about an earlier event, not contemporary fifth-century documentation.

A rigorous formulation is:

> By the ninth century, Muslim historians transmitted a developed account that a Himyarite Tubbaʿ first covered the Kaaba completely; the practice itself is independently understood to have existed before Islam, but the first textile and first patron cannot presently be identified archaeologically.

### 10.3 The color question

Popular histories often announce a single ruler who made the kiswah black forever. The sources instead show:

- multiple annual coverings of different colors;
- black associated with Abbasid representation;
- later green and white episodes;
- black’s eventual stabilization;
- exceptional red or white coverings afterward.

The debate is therefore about the date of standardization, not whether non-black coverings existed.

### 10.4 Cairo versus Istanbul

The Ottoman production system was distributed. Sovereign authorization lay in Istanbul; exterior production was ordinarily Egyptian; some internal textiles and components were produced in Istanbul; designs and materials could circulate between both centers. Statements assigning “the Ottoman kiswah” exclusively to either city flatten the evidence.

### 10.5 Mamluk monopoly and soft power

Recent scholarship treats the covering as an instrument of sovereignty or “soft power.” The strongest evidence is concrete:

- ruler’s names embroidered into the textile;
- refusals or conditional acceptance of rival offerings;
- endowments;
- ceremonial dispatch;
- armed escorts;
- the 1926 confrontation;
- the 1962 refusal.

The language of “soft power” is modern analytical terminology. Historical actors spoke in terms of service, honor, sovereignty, caliphate, guardianship, and control of the pilgrimage.

### 10.6 Disposal and relic-making

The normal cutting and redistribution of exterior coverings explain their fragmentary survival. Interior curtains survive more often intact because they were less exposed and replaced less frequently. The Met study suggests Bāb al-Tawbah curtains were changed as needed, perhaps every few years, rather than only at each accession or every year.

### 10.7 Conservation studies

The 2017 Met study is especially important because it integrated:

- inscriptional evidence;
- a newly discovered internal date slip;
- weave and stitching analysis;
- precious-metal examination;
- dye chemistry;
- industrial trademarks;
- economic history.

It changed the object from a generalized “Ottoman sacred curtain” into a precisely dated artifact of Cairo’s religious craft and global industrial trade.

Comparable published scientific work on the present annual exterior coverings remains limited. Official laboratory testing focuses on tensile strength, dye performance, weather resistance, and production quality, but detailed datasets are not routinely published in peer-reviewed form.

### 10.8 Archival gaps

Important gaps include:

- no surviving pre-Islamic covering;
- no known securely identified Prophetic, Rashidun, or Umayyad fragment;
- incomplete annual lists of coverings before the modern period;
- uneven publication of Ottoman surre and treasury archives;
- incomplete provenance between removal and later private ownership;
- no comprehensive international census of fragments;
- inconsistent official weight and component figures across years;
- limited public access to modern production specifications and distribution registers.

### 10.9 Discredited or misleading popular propositions

The following should not be repeated as established fact:

- that the present cloth is the same object first installed by Abraham or Tubbaʿ;
- that the kiswah has always been black;
- that the maḥmal necessarily contained the covering;
- that every black-and-gold sacred textile is from the Kaaba;
- that manufacture was uninterrupted in one Egyptian workshop from ʿUmar to 1962;
- that every “factory-made” panel was installed;
- that touching or cutting the installed covering is an obligatory pilgrimage rite;
- that radiocarbon or DNA testing has authenticated an ancient “original Kiswah.”
