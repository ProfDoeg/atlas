# The Fort-de-France Josephine statue: Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `the_fort_de_france_josephine_statue_object_dossier.md`

## Evidentiary note

The monument was a securely documented nineteenth-century public work, not a legendary or sacred object in the strict sense. Its commission, construction, transport, installation, municipal ownership, mutilation, legal protection, displacement, and destruction are documented by governmental archives, heritage records, contemporary newspapers, photographs, and later scholarship.

The principal evidentiary distinctions used below are:

- **Documented:** established by an official record, contemporary account, photograph, or multiple mutually consistent sources.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** a historian’s or research project’s reading of documented evidence.
- **Reported but unverified:** testimony or journalism not independently corroborated in the available record.
- **Legend/tradition:** a story attached to the site or object without adequate contemporary evidence.
- **Unknown:** the available public record does not permit a firm conclusion.

A recurring complication is terminology. The French heritage record calls the property *inscrit au titre des monuments historiques*—“registered” or “listed” as a historic monument—not *classé*, the higher level of French protection. Several secondary accounts inaccurately call it “classified.” The protected property comprised both “the statue and its socle.”

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity and names

The object tracked here was the monumental marble figure erected in 1859 on the Savane at Fort-de-France, Martinique, together with its architectural pedestal and associated fittings.

Documented or commonly used names include:

- **Statue de l’Impératrice Joséphine**
- **Statue de l’impératrice Joséphine, située sur la Savane** — official title in the French Mérimée heritage database
- **Monument à l’impératrice Joséphine**
- **Statue de Joséphine de Beauharnais**
- **Statue de Joséphine**
- In English: **Statue of the Empress Joséphine** or **Fort-de-France Joséphine Monument**

“Joséphine” was the name by which Marie-Josèphe-Rose Tascher de La Pagerie, later Joséphine de Beauharnais and empress of the French, became publicly known. In the monument’s inscription she was identified principally through her imperial title and Martinican birth.

The official heritage identifier is **Mérimée PA00105984**. The French Ministry of Culture lists the work as a *monument*, dated 1859, by **Vital-Dubray**, situated on municipal public land and owned by the commune of Fort-de-France. The statue and its base were entered in the supplementary historic-monument inventory by order of 31 December 1992.

### The figure

The standing figure was carved from **white Carrara marble**. A twentieth-century description gives the statue itself as approximately two metres high, whereas the University of Exeter’s *Gravées dans le marbre / Cast in Stone* catalogue records **2.70 m**. The complete monument—including pedestal and granite substructure—rose approximately **five metres above ground**. The discrepancy probably reflects imprecise historical measurement or differing definitions of the carved figure; no measured conservation survey has been located.

Joséphine was represented standing in imperial costume derived from her dress at the coronation of 1804:

- a long Empire-style gown;
- the imperial mantle fastened at the shoulders;
- a diadem;
- earrings;
- bare arms, although the historical coronation costume had long decorated sleeves;
- her right hand placed beneath her left breast, close to her heart, in one description;
- her left arm supported on a short architectural element or column and associated with a medallion bearing the profile of Napoleon I.

A later heritage case study says she faced the Bay of Fort-de-France and, symbolically, the Trois-Îlets area where she was born. Some secondary descriptions call the item in her right hand an Antillean flower. Historical descriptions instead emphasize the hand-on-heart pose. This difference may derive from confusion between the Fort-de-France sculpture and Dubray’s related later versions or from damage obscuring the hand. It should not be treated as resolved without examination of high-resolution early photographs.

The *Moniteur de la Martinique* stated that Dubray used two portrait sources:

1. a bust by Antonio Canova said to have been made from life and removed from the Tuileries for his use; and
2. François-Joseph Bosio’s bust, which Joséphine had reportedly given to her daughter Hortense because of its likeness.

The resulting face was praised in the official contemporary description as combining grace with majesty. That face was physically lost when the statue was decapitated in September 1991.

### Pedestal and setting

The figure stood on an architectural **marble pedestal**, described as having cornices and Corinthian pilasters. That pedestal rested on a broad square **granite mass or platform**. The ensemble originally included:

- a high ornamental iron grille;
- corner candelabra;
- planted royal palms and aligned avenues designed to strengthen the monument’s visual centrality;
- bronze plaques or panels on the four sides.

The principal bronze relief represented the **coronation of Joséphine at Notre-Dame de Paris**. Other faces recorded significant dates:

- her birth: **23 June 1763**;
- her marriage to Napoleon Bonaparte: **9 March 1796**;
- the coronation: **1804**;
- the monument’s erection.

A recorded dedicatory inscription read:

> L’AN MDCCCLVIII  
> NAPOLÉON III RÉGNANT  
> LES HABITANTS DE LA MARTINIQUE  
> ONT ÉLEVÉ CE MONUMENT  
> À L’IMPÉRATRICE JOSÉPHINE  
> NÉE DANS CETTE ÎLE

Translation:

> In the year 1858, Napoleon III reigning, the inhabitants of Martinique erected this monument to the Empress Joséphine, born on this island.

Sources reproduce the inscription with minor omissions or layout differences. Although it gives 1858, the completed monument was inaugurated in August 1859. The year probably records the intended or substantive completion of fabrication rather than the public unveiling.

### Material choice

Carrara marble was not an incidental choice. Minutes of the Paris commission dated **6 January 1855** record a preference for white marble’s “splendour” and reservations about bronze because its dark colour might cause confusion “in view of local customs” or conditions. The *Cast in Stone* researchers interpret these euphemisms as racialized: promoters did not want the white Creole empress represented in a dark material among a predominantly Black and mixed-race post-emancipation population. That is a **scholarly interpretation**, but it rests on explicit archival statements about colour and “local” objections.

Napoleon III supplied or paid for the marble, and Dubray reportedly selected it personally in Italy after attending the inauguration of his statue of General Abbatucci in Corsica.

### Damage, alteration, and losses

The documented physical sequence is:

- weathering and ordinary outdoor exposure from 1859 onward;
- relocation within the Savane in 1974;
- loss of the head during the night of 21–22 September 1991;
- repeated applications of red paint after the decapitation;
- occasional clothing or wrapping, including black cloth in 1998 and a yellow vest in 2018;
- whipping during a performance in 2012;
- fire set around or against it on 16–17 July 2020;
- attacks on the pedestal with hammers on 26 July 2020;
- toppling with ropes;
- fragmentation of the marble body after its fall and further blows;
- burning of debris;
- removal of surviving fragments to an undisclosed storage location;
- retention of part of the pedestal in situ, later converted into a planter.

The original head has never been publicly recovered. Public sources do not establish whether the bronze coronation relief and all inscription plaques survived, were removed earlier, were stolen during the demolition, or remain among municipal fragments. Likewise, no published conservation inventory identifies every surviving piece.

### Present survival

The intact monument no longer exists. Its known or reported survivals are:

- marble fragments held by or for the municipality at an undisclosed location, reported in 2021;
- fragments carried away by participants as trophies on 26 July 2020;
- an unidentified remainder of the pedestal or its foundation on the Savane;
- photographs, postcards, engravings, news footage, and archival plans;
- the legal and documentary record;
- related but **not identical** sculptures by Dubray, especially the 1865 Joséphine from Bois-Préau.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### First proposal

The Ministry of Culture’s official record says that an initial proposal for a Joséphine monument dates to **1837**. The accessible record provides no complete contract or realized design from that initiative. It should be regarded as the earliest documented proposal, not the origin of the surviving 1859 marble.

The effective campaign began under the Second Empire. In **1852**, the municipal councils of Saint-Pierre and Fort-de-France voted to raise additional funds for a monument. Other Martinican communes subsequently approved additional taxation or “centimes additionnels.”

The claim, repeated in some summaries, that Napoleon III personally initiated and simply donated the statue is misleading. The surviving administrative history indicates a Martinican initiative supported by the colonial government and imperial court. The people described in the dedication as “the inhabitants of Martinique” were represented politically and financially through colonial municipal and general councils. Scholars emphasize that the organizing leadership was dominated by the white Creole planter elite—the *habitants-sucriers*—rather than by the population at large.

### Financing

By late 1854 the projected cost was approximately **45,000 francs**, assembled from:

- **25,000 francs** voted by the colonial General Council;
- **12,000 francs** given by Napoleon III;
- subscriptions collected in Martinique and metropolitan France;
- supplementary local taxation voted by Martinican communes.

The Archives nationales d’outre-mer catalogue identifies a substantial documentary series:

- **MAR 30/262:** correspondence between the minister of the Navy and the governor, 1852–1858;
- **MAR 30/263:** commission minutes, 1855–1857;
- **MAR 30/264:** correspondence among the Colonial Directorate, Ministry of State, Imperial Household, and commission, 1853–1856;
- **MAR 30/265:** correspondence with commission members, Vital-Dubray, and architect Manguin, 1854–1857;
- **MAR 30/266:** Le Pelletier de Saint-Rémy’s reports and accounting papers, 1854–1856;
- **MAR 30/267:** subscription accounts, 1854–1858;
- **MAR 30/268:** correspondence with Count de Nieuwerkerke, 1854–1856;
- **MAR 30/269:** supply contracts and accounts, 1856–1858;
- **MAR 30/270:** shipment of the monument;
- **MAR 94/814:** the General Council’s 25,000-franc credit and related correspondence.

These files are the principal primary archive for a full reconstruction of contracts, payments, fabrication, and shipping.

### Governing bodies

Two bodies supervised the project:

- a colonial committee in Martinique, operating under the governor and supervising subscriptions, site preparation, and local work;
- a Paris commission established in late 1854 to supervise the statue and pedestal.

The Paris commission was chaired by **Count Tascher de La Pagerie**, Joséphine’s relative and an official in Empress Eugénie’s household. Other participants included Senator Dariste, Le Pelletier de Saint-Rémy, the vicomte de Fougainville, Count de Nieuwerkerke, and senior officials.

### Design and makers

The figurative sculpture was entrusted to **Gabriel-Vital Dubray**, usually styled **Vital-Dubray** (1813–1892). The pedestal was designed by a government architect whose name appears variously as **Manguin**, **Mauguin**, or “Pierre Manguin” in secondary records. Archival catalogue MAR 30/265 uses **Manguin**. Théodore Baude also names marble workers or sculptors **Dupuy and Parfonry** as participants.

A plaster design was displayed at the **Paris Exposition universelle of 1855**, commonly dated to 15 May. That plaster was a preparatory or exhibition version, not the installed marble. Its later location is not established in the sources consulted.

The first stone of the Fort-de-France structure was ceremonially laid on **12 July 1856**. Governor Louis-Henri de Gueydon processed to the Savane with colonial civil and military authorities; Bishop Étienne Le Herpeur blessed the stone; the ceremony included speeches, a twenty-one-gun salute, dinner, and reception. This is documented in *La France d’Outre-Mer* of 15 July 1856 and reconstructed by Laurence Brown.

### Site choice

The Ministry favored the Savane, while colonial councillors initially preferred the square by the Government Palace. In 1855 Governor Gueydon endorsed the Savane. In 1856 he proposed a position aligned with urban streets rather than the exact mathematical center of the park. New tree avenues were planned to make the location appear central and monumental.

The choice put the figure between the colonial city and Fort Saint-Louis, facing the bay. This was an intentionally conspicuous setting, not merely a convenient vacant plot.

### Fabrication and transport

The statue and pedestal components were produced in France and Italy-linked supply networks, using Carrara marble selected by Dubray. A letter of **28 July 1858** from Le Pelletier de Saint-Rémy says that all monument components were shipped from **Le Havre** aboard the merchant vessel **Le Roi d’Yvetot**, operated by the Louédin firm.

The exact quarry, carving workshop address, shipping date, arrival date, and unloading arrangements have not been established from the accessible digitized sources. MAR 30/269–270 should contain the best evidence.

### Completion and inauguration

Dates differ:

- the official Mérimée notice says **28 August 1859**;
- Baude and several travel accounts say the statue was erected or inaugurated on **29 August 1859**;
- the *Cast in Stone* project describes festivities on **29–31 August**;
- an unreliable tertiary entry gives 26 August.

The best reconstruction is that an inauguration program began on or about **28–29 August**, with principal ceremonies on 29 August and festivities extending through 31 August. Without the full official program, silently selecting a single day would conceal the source conflict.

The inauguration combined:

- military and religious ceremony;
- unveiling of the statue;
- official speeches;
- an agricultural exhibition and competition;
- elite banquets;
- balls, fireworks, popular games, and dances;
- delegations from Guadeloupe, Saint Lucia, Barbados, Grenada, Saint Thomas, Saint Croix, Britain, and Venezuela.

Governor Maussion de Candé estimated that approximately 10,000 additional visitors entered Fort-de-France.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 1837–1852: unrealized proposal

**Custodian:** none; project only.  
**Status:** the Ministry of Culture records a proposal in 1837, but no physical object from it is known.

### 1852–1854: colonial fundraising initiative

**Responsible bodies:** municipal councils of Saint-Pierre and Fort-de-France, other communes, General Council, and colonial governors Vaillant and Gueydon.

The project passed institutionally through colonial political and administrative bodies. Funds were raised by public taxation, colonial appropriation, subscriptions, and the emperor’s gift.

### 1854–1858: commission and manufacture

**Supervisors:** Martinican colonial committee and Paris commission.  
**Maker:** Vital-Dubray, assisted in the monument’s architectural and marble work by Manguin, Dupuy, and Parfonry.  
**Physical locations:** design work in France; marble sourced in Italy; components prepared for Atlantic transport.

The Paris commission functioned as the immediate commissioning authority. No evidence found indicates private ownership by Napoleon III, the Tascher family, or Dubray after completion.

### 1858–1859: Atlantic transfer

**Mechanism:** commissioned public work shipped for installation.  
**Route:** Le Havre to Martinique aboard *Le Roi d’Yvetot*.  
**Recipient:** colonial authorities in Martinique.

The shipment was not a colonial seizure, private gift, or later museum transfer. It was delivery of a monument already funded for public erection.

### 1859–1974: central Savane

**Location:** near the center of the Savane, Fort-de-France.  
**Custodial regime:** colonial and later municipal public monument; ultimately municipal property.

The monument appears continuously in travel writing, postcards, stereographs, and photographs. A Library of Congress stereograph documents it in its early setting. No wartime evacuation or concealment is reported.

The ornamental grille originally restricted physical access. Count Tascher had anticipated “acts of individual malice” in 1855 and proposed guarding the work. The grille’s exact removal date is not established.

### 1974: municipal relocation

During redesign of the Savane under Mayor **Aimé Césaire**, the monument and the war memorial were moved. Joséphine was removed from the park’s commanding center and installed at the **northwestern margin**, parallel to rue de la Liberté, near the edge of the park.

A performance kiosk replaced it near the center. The municipality did not destroy or dispose of the monument; it altered its spatial hierarchy. The *Cast in Stone* authors interpret the relocation as part of Césaire’s “cannibalization” of colonial urban symbolism: the imperial figure was displaced and a space for public cultural use substituted.

### Night of 21–22 September 1991: decapitation

The statue was decapitated and stained or splashed with red paint. *France-Antilles Martinique* reported the event on 24 September.

The head disappeared and has never been publicly recovered. The available contemporary record did not identify the perpetrators. A later oral-history reconstruction by writer and researcher **Zaka Toto** attributes the act to people associated with **Yich Telga** (“Children of Telga”), linked to the Alliance révolutionnaire caraïbe. According to this testimony, the date intentionally marked the anniversary of the September 1870 southern Martinique insurrection, and the red material represented the blood of enslaved ancestors.

That attribution remains **reported oral testimony**, not a judicially established finding. Some accounts call the red substance blood; contemporary and later physical descriptions more safely identify it as red paint unless forensic evidence emerges.

Claims that the city ordered a replacement head circulate in secondary sources, but no municipal contract, invoice, photograph, sculptor’s name, or present location for such a replacement has been found. The reported replacement head should therefore be treated as **unverified**.

### 31 December 1992: heritage registration

The French state registered “the statue of the Empress Joséphine and its socle” as a historic monument. The owner was recorded as the **commune**.

The timing matters: the damaged, headless monument—not an intact hypothetical original state—was the physical property existing when protection was granted. *Cast in Stone* says it was registered *en l’état*, in its existing state.

The act did not transfer ownership to the national government. It imposed a public heritage regime on municipal property.

### 1998: black shroud

For the 150th anniversary of abolition, the headless figure was wrapped or draped in black. This was a symbolic intervention, not a restoration. It foregrounded the monument’s association with slavery and the disputed public memory of Joséphine.

### November 2012: Sarah Trouche performance

Artist **Sarah Trouche**, nude and covered in red paint, whipped the statue for approximately twenty minutes during the Pool Art Fair. She demanded that Joséphine “tell us the truth,” linking the work to the Kalinago, slavery, and French colonial power. This was a documented artistic use of the object, not a custody transfer.

### December 2018: yellow vest

The monument was dressed in a *gilet jaune* during the Yellow Vest protests. The identity of the person or group responsible is not established in the consulted sources.

### 16–17 July 2020: fire attack

During unrest following the detention of activists supporting Kéziah Nuissier, burning material was placed against or around the Joséphine and Belain d’Esnambuc monuments. Contemporary video and local reporting show the fire. Marble is not combustible, but heat can crack, spall, discolor, and structurally weaken it; no published conservation assessment has been found.

### July 2020 ultimatum

Activists associated with **Rouge Vert Noir** publicly demanded that Mayor Didier Laguerre remove the monument, threatening to destroy it if the city did not act by 26 July.

The mayor and municipal memory commission were already considering official removal. A proposal concerning the monument was expected before the council on 28 July. The activist destruction preceded that meeting. The city did not authorize the demolition.

### 26 July 2020: toppling and fragmentation

The event was filmed and disseminated through social media and local news:

1. participants struck and opened the pedestal with heavy tools;
2. ropes were attached to the figure;
3. the headless statue was pulled down;
4. the fall shattered or severely fractured the marble;
5. people jumped on and struck the remains;
6. some participants carried away fragments;
7. debris was burned;
8. nearby, the monument to Pierre Belain d’Esnambuc was also pulled down.

A parliamentary question described the statue as attacked with sledgehammers, pulled down by ropes, and placed on a fire. The Fondation Napoléon characterized it as irreparably destroyed. “Irreparably” is a conservation judgment that cannot be independently verified without a complete fragment inventory, but the intact nineteenth-century work unquestionably ceased to exist.

The city declined to file a complaint. The public prosecutor nevertheless opened an investigation because the destroyed works were publicly owned protected monuments.

### After July 2020: removal to secret storage

Municipal or public services gathered at least some fragments. In July 2021 RCI reported that the remains of the destroyed monuments were stored at an undisclosed location. Officials discussed possibly bringing them together in a museum or interpretive memory space. No published accession number or formal museum deposit has been found.

Because participants also removed pieces, the post-destruction custody chain is divided:

- **municipally retained fragments:** location publicly withheld;
- **privately removed fragments:** identities, number, and locations unknown;
- **head removed in 1991:** missing;
- **remaining in-situ fabric:** part of the pedestal/foundation.

### December 2020–2021: pedestal as planter

Rouge Vert Noir activists converted the remaining pedestal structure into a permaculture planter intended for public planting and harvesting. It was reportedly neglected, attracted pests, and was set on fire in June 2021, reportedly by an unhoused person. The attribution and motivation are not documented by a judicial finding.

### 2022–2025: prosecution

In May 2022, five activists were placed under formal investigation in cases involving the 2020 statue destructions. Eleven defendants eventually appeared before the Fort-de-France correctional court in November 2025 concerning four monuments.

On **17 November 2025**, according to RCI’s report of the judgment:

- defendants prosecuted concerning the Joséphine and d’Esnambuc destructions were acquitted;
- the court characterized the conduct as part of political militant action;
- in the combined proceedings, most defendants were acquitted and two received findings of guilt with waivers of punishment on other counts.

This judicial outcome did not restore ownership of fragments taken away, settle the head’s location, or remove the surviving property from municipal control.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Was it the original commissioned monument?

Yes, in the ordinary documentary sense. Its continuity from 1858–1859 delivery to the Savane is supported by:

- commission and accounting files;
- a named sculptor and architect;
- shipping correspondence;
- inauguration reporting;
- continuous photography and travel descriptions;
- municipal and heritage records.

No evidence suggests that the entire Fort-de-France statue was secretly replaced before 1991 or 2020.

### Material and version complications

Dubray made closely related Joséphine statues. The Fort-de-France work must not be confused with:

- the **1865 marble statue associated with Bois-Préau**, displayed in Paris on avenue Joséphine/avenue Marceau, presented at the 1867 Exposition universelle, later entering the national museum collection in 1932;
- the **resin-and-marble-powder copy** installed outdoors at Bois-Préau in September 2021 after the 1865 original was removed for conservation;
- the 1855 plaster model or exhibition design, whose survival is unestablished.

The Bois-Préau original was conserved in 2021 after biological growth, pollution, cracking, and loss of surface detail were found. It is a related autograph version, not the recovered Fort-de-France statue. Its survival does not make the destroyed Martinican work a mere expendable copy.

A reference in the 1859 *Moniteur* to “another version” with an unfortunate black vein in its marble demonstrates that multiple versions or blocks were being compared. It does not show that the Fort-de-France monument was replaced.

### Portrait authenticity

Dubray never met Joséphine, who died in 1814. The work’s facial authenticity was therefore mediated through earlier portrait busts by Canova and Bosio. Contemporary promoters asserted that Bosio’s bust was especially faithful. That is an inherited likeness claim, not a scientific identification.

### The missing head

No claimant head has been authenticated. Rumors about its hiding place have circulated since 1991, but no publicly documented discovery, laboratory examination, marble match, tool-mark analysis, or chain of custody exists.

### Reported replacement head

Secondary sources say the city commissioned a new head but did not attach it. In the absence of primary documentation, the following remain unknown:

- whether it was actually commissioned or completed;
- who made it;
- whether it was marble, plaster, or another material;
- whether it copied the surviving Bois-Préau head;
- where it is now.

### Scientific examination

No published petrographic, isotopic, tool-mark, pigment, structural, or forensic analysis of the Fort-de-France marble has been located. No comprehensive conservation report after the 1991 decapitation, July 2020 fire, or destruction is publicly cited.

Thus:

- Carrara origin is historically documented but not, in the sources found, independently confirmed by modern analysis;
- the red coatings have not been publicly characterized;
- the number and condition of stored fragments are unknown;
- the feasibility of anastylosis or partial reconstruction has not been publicly assessed.

### Authority to authenticate

Authority shifted among:

- the commissioning committees and imperial arts administration during manufacture;
- colonial authorities at installation;
- the municipality as owner;
- the Ministry of Culture and historic-monument administration after 1992;
- courts and investigators dealing with destruction;
- potentially a future museum or conservation laboratory if fragments are catalogued.

No later activist, heritage association, or Napoleonic foundation acquired legal power to determine the work’s ownership or authenticity.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Makers and designers

- **Gabriel-Vital Dubray / Vital-Dubray (1813–1892):** commissioned sculptor; designed and carved the figure, selected the marble, and determined its pose and orientation. Documented, 1850s.
- **Manguin/Mauguin:** government architect responsible for the pedestal. The spelling varies; ANOM uses Manguin. Documented, 1854–1857.
- **Dupuy and Parfonry:** named by Baude as sculptor-marble workers. Their precise contractual roles require confirmation in MAR 30/269.
- **Antonio Canova and François-Joseph Bosio:** makers of portrait busts used as models. They did not work directly on the monument.

### Commissioners and administrators

- **Municipal councils of Saint-Pierre and Fort-de-France:** early institutional promoters and funders, from 1852.
- **Martinique General Council:** voted a 25,000-franc credit.
- **Governors Vaillant, Gueydon, and Maussion de Candé:** respectively supported, sited, and inaugurated the project during its progress.
- **Count Tascher de La Pagerie:** chaired the Paris commission and raised concerns about possible damage.
- **Le Pelletier de Saint-Rémy:** commission delegate whose correspondence documents shipment and administration.
- **Count de Nieuwerkerke:** director-general of imperial museums and commission member.
- **Napoleon III:** contributed 12,000 francs and the marble; the monument honored his grandmother and helped connect the Second Empire to Martinique.
- **Bishop Étienne Le Herpeur:** blessed the foundation stone in 1856.

### Owner and custodians

- **Commune of Fort-de-France:** documented legal owner in the Mérimée record.
- **French Ministry of Culture / historic-monument services:** regulatory authority after registration in 1992.
- **Unidentified municipal storage service:** reported keeper of surviving fragments after 2020.

### Political and artistic interlocutors

- **Aimé Césaire:** as mayor, oversaw the 1974 redesign that moved the statue to the Savane’s margin. His connection is municipal and spatial, not physical ownership.
- **Guy Méthalie:** wrote and performed “Yo déplacé Joséphine” after the relocation, making the movement of the object part of popular song.
- **Djo Dézormo:** independentist singer whose 1978 “Joséphine” rejected the monument and predicted its removal into the sea.
- **Édouard Glissant:** used the headless empress in *Tout-monde* (1993), transforming the damaged monument into literary imagery.
- **Sarah Trouche:** used it in a 2012 performance involving whipping and red body paint.
- **Zaka Toto:** gathered later testimony about the 1991 decapitation.
- **Valérie-Ann Edmond-Mariette and Adélaïde Marine Gougeon:** researchers responsible for the detailed *Cast in Stone* case study.
- **Rouge Vert Noir:** activist collective associated with the 2020 ultimatum, demolition, and later planter.
- **Didier Laguerre:** mayor at the time of destruction; announced that the city was already considering formal removal and declined to file a municipal complaint.
- **French public prosecutor and investigating judiciary:** pursued the destruction case despite the city’s position.

### Subject represented

- **Joséphine de Beauharnais:** represented subject, not an owner or user of the object; she died forty-five years before its inauguration.
- **Napoleon I:** represented secondarily in the hand-held medallion and coronation relief.
- **Napoleon III:** named in the dedication and represented institutionally by the monument’s imperial program.

### Divine or sacred associations

The monument was not a cult image and had no documented divine owner, guardian, relic status, or miracle tradition. Bishop Le Herpeur’s blessing of the foundation was a standard Catholic component of a public ceremony; it did not establish the statue as a sacred object.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Imperial and colonial legitimation

The monument’s principal original function was commemorative and political. It connected:

- Joséphine’s Martinican birth;
- white Creole prominence;
- Napoleon I’s imperial memory;
- Napoleon III’s Second Empire;
- the colonial territory’s asserted loyalty to France.

The coronation relief and imperial costume elevated a locally born planter-class woman into a dynastic bridge between Martinique and metropolitan sovereignty.

Laurence Brown argues that the statue helped articulate a post-1848 settlement in which the white Creole elite represented itself as indispensable to colonial prosperity and imperial identity after emancipation. The agricultural exhibition accompanying the inauguration displayed the colony’s productive wealth as part of the same program.

### Ceremonial use

The best-documented ceremonial use was the 1859 inauguration:

- military procession;
- blessing;
- gun salute;
- speeches and unveiling;
- diplomatic and intercolonial attendance;
- agricultural exhibition;
- banquets, dances, games, and fireworks.

There is no evidence that the monument subsequently became the site of a fixed annual imperial liturgy, oath, pilgrimage, or offering practice.

### Restricted access

The original high grille both framed and protected the monument. Its sponsors anticipated malicious damage and contemplated a guard. The monument was nevertheless installed in an open civic park, not a restricted treasury or sanctuary.

### Counter-rituals

Later actions developed their own ceremonial language:

- red paint evoked blood;
- decapitation reversed imperial portrait authority;
- the 1998 black shroud linked the figure to mourning and slavery;
- the 2012 whipping performance staged interrogation and punishment;
- the 2018 yellow vest appropriated it for contemporary protest;
- the 2020 toppling used an ultimatum, collective ropes, blows, public acclamation, fragment-taking, and fire;
- the planter attempted to turn an imperial pedestal into a site of shared food production.

These were political performances involving the material object, whether or not participants described them as ritual.

### Political force of possession and placement

Possession did not confer formal sovereignty, but **placement** conferred narrative priority. At the Savane’s center, the monument presented Joséphine and the imperial connection as central to Martinican public history. Moving it to the edge in 1974 reduced that authority. Keeping it headless after 1991 made its contestation part of the official streetscape. Destroying it in 2020 terminated its role as an honorific figure while creating a new contested object: fragments and an empty or repurposed base.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The cannonball legend

A persistent story says the statue stood on the exact spot where, in 1790, a cannonball fell at the young Joséphine’s feet without harming her.

**Tier:** local legend or later commemorative tradition.

The documented siting debates of 1854–1856 concerned urban alignment, visual effect, the Savane versus the Government square, and the relation of the site to streets and bay. These records undermine the idea that promoters simply identified and marked a securely remembered 1790 impact site. No contemporary 1790 record confirming the episode was located.

The legend helped fuse physical site, providential survival, and imperial destiny: the woman allegedly spared at that place would later become empress and return symbolically in marble.

### Joséphine as author of slavery’s restoration

A central public narrative holds that Joséphine persuaded Napoleon to restore slavery in 1802.

**Tier:** long-standing political and popular attribution; historically disputed.

No documentary evidence cited in the monument scholarship establishes that Joséphine caused Napoleon’s decision. Historians instead place the policy within Bonaparte’s colonial, military, economic, and diplomatic program. Moreover, Martinique had remained under British occupation when France abolished slavery in 1794; slavery there was maintained during British control and continued after the island returned to France. The 1802 measures operated differently across colonies.

This does not sever the monument from slavery. Joséphine was born into a slaveholding planter family, remained connected to slave property, and the 1850s monument was promoted by post-emancipation white Creole elites. The distinction is between:

- **documented structural and familial association with slavery**, and
- **unproven personal responsibility for the specific 1802 decision**.

The causal story mattered materially even where its narrow historical claim remained unproven: it motivated paint attacks, decapitation, performances, removal demands, and destruction.

### The lost head

Since 1991, the head’s fate has generated stories ranging from concealment by independence activists to disposal in the sea or retention as a trophy.

**Tier:** unresolved rumor complex.

No claimant has produced the head publicly. No police inventory or authenticated photograph after removal has surfaced in the consulted material. Zaka Toto’s interviews provide a possible account of perpetrators but do not establish the head’s destination.

### Decapitation as the guillotine Joséphine escaped

Modern tourism and journalistic accounts sometimes say the monument’s beheading echoed the guillotine Joséphine narrowly escaped during the Terror.

**Tier:** modern symbolic interpretation.

The parallel is retrospective. No evidence shows that the 1991 actors chose decapitation principally to invoke Joséphine’s imprisonment under the Terror. Their reported symbolism concerned colonial rule, slavery, and the 1870 insurrection.

### Immediate resistance at inauguration

An 1885 passage in Alfred Parépou’s *Atipa*, later translated by Michel Lohier, says that on the day of inauguration a Black man smeared excrement on the statue and received a three-month sentence.

**Tier:** early literary testimony, not independently confirmed here.

This is significant because it challenges the assertion that the statue stood uncontested for more than a century. *Atipa* appeared twenty-six years after the inauguration and is a literary work in Guianese Creole, not a court register. The incident should be investigated in 1859 Martinican judicial and newspaper records before being accepted as documented fact.

### The monument as “gift of Napoleon III”

**Tier:** simplification or modern institutional legend.

Napoleon III donated money and reportedly the marble, but municipal and colonial bodies initiated, taxed, funded, and administered the monument. Calling the whole work his gift erases the more complex commission.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Status as of September 2026

The monument is **destroyed as an intact public artwork**. It has not stood on the Savane since 26 July 2020.

The latest precise public custody report located states that surviving pieces were kept in a **secret or undisclosed storage location** by the authorities in 2021. No later public catalogue, accession number, conservation report, reconstructed display, or exact repository has been found.

Accordingly:

- **Marble body:** fragmented; some pieces publicly reported in official storage, others taken by participants.
- **Original head:** missing since September 1991.
- **Pedestal:** substantially damaged; a remnant remained on site and was converted into a planter.
- **Bronze reliefs and plaques:** present before destruction; current disposition not publicly established.
- **Grille and candelabra:** no current inventory found.
- **Ownership:** the municipality was the registered owner. No lawful deaccession or ownership transfer has been identified.
- **Heritage record:** Mérimée PA00105984 remains publicly accessible and still records the 1992 registration. The notice was updated in April 2026 but does not narrate the 2020 destruction.
- **Access:** no publicly advertised access to stored fragments.
- **Public site:** the former emplacement no longer presents the standing statue.

### Legal regime

At destruction, the statue and base were protected municipal property. French historic-monument protection did not physically prevent demolition. The national heritage database continues to describe the property in the present tense, illustrating the lag between legal-documentary identity and material condition.

No source located records:

- a formal removal from the historic-monument register;
- a state-authorized demolition;
- a completed compensation or restitution order;
- municipal authorization for private holders to retain fragments;
- a conservation decision declaring reconstruction impossible.

### Criminal proceedings

The prosecution culminated in November 2025. Those prosecuted in relation to Joséphine and d’Esnambuc were reported acquitted. This resolved the criminal charges against those defendants but not the heritage, ownership, or conservation status of surviving material.

### Future display proposals

After the destruction, officials discussed collecting the removed monuments in a museum or memory center where their histories could be explained. The Fort-de-France memory commission continued examining contested names and monuments. No completed museum installation for the Joséphine fragments has been identified.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Early visual circulation

The monument became a recurring image in:

- stereographs;
- postcards;
- travel books;
- illustrated histories of Martinique;
- tourist literature.

A Library of Congress stereograph preserves an early view of the statue in the Savane. Such images extended the monument’s reach beyond its physical site and helped make it an emblem of Fort-de-France.

### Popular music

The 1974 relocation quickly entered popular culture.

- **Guy Méthalie, “Yo déplacé Joséphine”** (successful during Carnival 1976): treated the displacement through humor, rumor, sexuality, and the population’s relation to the Savane.
- **Djo Dézormo, “Joséphine”** (1978): denounced her as a racist *békée* and enslaver and predicted that the statue would one day be thrown into the ocean. The song was reportedly censored and initially had limited circulation.

These songs demonstrate that the physical movement of the monument, not merely Joséphine’s biography, generated cultural production.

### Literature

Édouard Glissant’s **Tout-monde** (1993) incorporated the headless empress into a wider literary meditation on Martinican history, memory, and fragmentation. Kylie Sago has studied the relation between Dubray’s Joséphine statues, Glissant’s writing, and the contested imperial monument.

### Performance art

Sarah Trouche’s 2012 action made the existing damage part of the work. Rather than reproducing the intact imperial image, she engaged the already headless, paint-marked object as a material witness in contemporary debates.

### Protest object and informal dressing

The figure became a surface for recurrent public interventions:

- red paint;
- slogans around the base;
- black mourning cloth;
- a yellow vest;
- fire;
- ropes and public toppling.

Its unrepaired decapitation became its most recognizable modern visual form. From 1991 to 2020, the object displayed both the original imperial commission and the history of resistance to it.

### Replicas and related versions

The later Bois-Préau Joséphine by Dubray complicates public reception. It preserves a complete version of the sculptor’s iconography, but it is not the Fort-de-France original. In 2021 the Bois-Préau original itself was replaced outdoors by a resin and marble-powder reproduction.

No authorized full-scale replica of the destroyed Fort-de-France monument has been identified. The photographs, postcards, and related Bois-Préau sculpture are therefore the principal visual surrogates.

### Decolonization and “déchoukaj”

The 2020 destruction was described by activists as **déchoukaj** or **déchoukage**, from a Creole term for uprooting. In this usage it means more than administrative removal: it frames the physical extraction of a monument as uprooting colonial authority.

The act occurred amid:

- worldwide monument disputes after the killing of George Floyd;
- Martinican struggles over colonial public memory;
- protests concerning police conduct;
- mobilization over chlordecone contamination;
- earlier destruction of Victor Schœlcher monuments;
- demands for greater recognition of enslaved people’s self-emancipation and Black Martinican leaders.

The Joséphine monument therefore shifted from an imperial honorific object to a central exhibit—while still standing—in a conflict over who controls public historical narration.

### Tourism

Before 2020, travel guides commonly presented the headless statue as a landmark. Some repeated simplified or erroneous claims:

- that Napoleon III alone commissioned or gave it;
- that Joséphine directly restored slavery;
- that the monument was inaugurated in 1856;
- that the cannonball story was documented fact.

These claims became part of tourism’s informal interpretive layer even when unsupported.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Primary documentation

The richest surviving archival corpus is at the **Archives nationales d’outre-mer**, especially MAR 30/262–270 and MAR 94/814. It contains:

- ministerial and gubernatorial correspondence;
- commission minutes;
- subscription accounts;
- contracts with makers and suppliers;
- accounting records;
- shipment records.

Other primary or near-contemporary sources include:

- *La France d’Outre-Mer*, 15 July 1856, on the foundation ceremony;
- *Moniteur de la Martinique*, including 10 January 1856 and 13 October 1859;
- inauguration speeches and Governor Maussion de Candé’s reporting;
- early photographs and stereographs;
- *France-Antilles Martinique*, 24 September 1991;
- municipal communications and audiovisual records from July 2020;
- criminal investigation and court reporting, 2022–2025.

The full historic-monument protection dossier and the 1992 order are available for consultation at the Médiathèque du patrimoine et de la photographie in Charenton-le-Pont. The order itself is also available through French geospatial heritage documentation.

### Major scholarly contributions

**Maurice Nicolas, “La Statue de l’Impératrice Joséphine à Fort-de-France” (1997)** reproduced or drew attention to the 1859 *Moniteur* description, preserving important information about costume and portrait models.

**Laurence Brown, “Créole Bonapartism and Post-Emancipation Society: Martinique’s Monument to the Empress Joséphine” (2006)** reconstructed the monument’s political origin, financing, ceremonies, and relation to post-emancipation white Creole power. This remains the foundational scholarly object history.

**Richard Burton, “Trois statues…” (1991)** offered a semiotic reading of Joséphine, the colonizer, and the liberator in Martinique’s monumental landscape.

**Anny Dominique Curtius (2013)** examined painted bodies and decapitated statues as an interpretive history of Fort-de-France.

**Kylie Sago (2019)** compared Dubray’s Joséphine statues and analyzed the headless empress through Glissant’s *Tout-monde* and the geography of French imperial commemoration.

**Valérie-Ann Edmond-Mariette and Adélaïde Marine Gougeon**, for *Gravées dans le marbre / Cast in Stone*, assembled the most useful public event-by-event digital object biography, integrating archival research, photographs, oral testimony, performance, and the 2020 destruction.

**Audrey Célestine, Valérie-Ann Edmond-Mariette, and Zaka Toto (2023)** treated the Martinican statue *déchoukaj* within comparative de-commemoration studies.

### Principal debates

#### Who initiated the monument?

Older popular accounts center Napoleon III. Archival research shows that Martinican municipal and colonial elites initiated and financed much of it, although Napoleon III was an important donor and dynastic beneficiary.

#### What did white marble signify?

One reading treats it as conventional neoclassical prestige and climatic/material choice. The commission’s language about bronze’s “dark colour,” possible confusion, and “local customs” supports a stronger interpretation of racial coding. Scholars disagree less about the words than about how explicitly their racial logic should be stated.

#### Was the monument universally accepted until the late twentieth century?

No secure basis exists for universal acceptance. The possible inauguration-day defacement recorded in *Atipa*, early protective anxieties, later political songs, and eventual attacks indicate a more complicated history. On the other hand, evidence of sustained organized protest before the 1970s remains limited.

#### Did Joséphine cause slavery’s restoration?

The causal accusation is central to the monument’s reception but lacks documentary proof. Scholarship generally distinguishes her slaveholding milieu from direct authorship of the 1802 policy.

#### Was leaving it headless preservation or neglect?

Competing readings include:

- failure to conserve a protected artwork;
- practical recognition that a replacement head would be removed again;
- deliberate maintenance of the statue as evidence of contested memory;
- municipal indifference;
- a Césairian practice of absorbing and neutralizing colonial symbols.

No published municipal resolution definitively establishes one intention for the entire 1991–2020 period.

#### Was the 2020 act destruction, removal, or transformation?

Legally and materially it was destruction of protected public property. Activists and sympathetic scholars also describe it as *déchoukaj*, political action, or transformation of public memory. These descriptions address different questions and are not mutually exclusive: the marble was physically destroyed, and the destruction acquired political and cultural meaning.

### Gaps in the record

Important unresolved research questions include:

- the content and fate of the 1837 proposal;
- exact contracts and workshop locations;
- precise block/quarry provenance;
- complete costs and final accounts;
- exact arrival and installation dates;
- definitive inauguration date and program;
- fate of the 1855 plaster;
- removal dates for grille and candelabra;
- identity of the 1991 perpetrators;
- location of the original head;
- documentary basis for the alleged replacement head;
- conservation treatment, if any, after 1991;
- fate of every bronze plaque;
- inventory and condition of post-2020 fragments;
- whether private fragments have been recovered;
- current status of the 1992 historic-monument registration.

### Source cautions

The e-monumen entry contains an obvious erroneous statement that the monument was erected in **1893**, contradicted by its own 1859 date and by the complete contemporary record. It also categorizes the object under bronze because of its reliefs even though the main statue and pedestal were marble. It is useful for images and object indexing but unreliable on those points.

Several tourism and tertiary pages give **1856** as the inauguration year, confusing the foundation ceremony with completion. Some sources state **26**, **28**, or **29 August 1859**. The evidence supports late-August festivities, with 29 August the most frequently substantiated principal inauguration day.

The official Mérimée record says the head was “destroyed” in 1991, while contemporary and later accounts say it was removed and disappeared. Until the head is recovered or its destruction is documented, “missing after decapitation” is the most exact formulation.
