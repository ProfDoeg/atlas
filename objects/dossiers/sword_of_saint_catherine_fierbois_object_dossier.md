# Fierbois provenance detail of the Sword of Saint Catherine: Object Dossier

**Proposed filename:** `fierbois_provenance_detail_of_the_sword_of_saint_catherine_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary conclusion

This dossier concerns the sword that Jeanne d’Arc testified she had caused to be fetched in 1429 from the church of Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois in Touraine. It is variously called the **épée de Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois**, **épée de Fierbois**, **épée de sainte Catherine**, **épée de Jeanne d’Arc**, and, only in later tradition, the **épée de Charles Martel**.

The most defensible reconstruction is:

1. A material sword almost certainly existed. Jeanne described it under hostile interrogation in 1431, and several fifteenth-century narrative sources independently knew the episode.
2. It was an already-used iron or steel sword, rusty when recovered, bearing five crosses on or near the part of the blade closest to the hilt.
3. It was retrieved from the church of Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois after Jeanne wrote to the clergy, probably between late March and April 1429. A Tours arms merchant or armourer served as intermediary.
4. The precise findspot varies in the sources: in the earth near or behind the altar, among votive swords, in a chest with old iron, or on a knight’s tomb. Jeanne herself admitted uncertainty over whether it was before or behind the altar, while insisting it lay only shallowly in the ground.
5. The clergy at Fierbois and people at Tours supplied expensive scabbards; Jeanne ordered a practical leather scabbard.
6. Jeanne said she carried the sword continually until the withdrawal from Saint-Denis after the failed assault on Paris in September 1429.
7. She did not have it when captured at Compiègne on 23 May 1430. She then carried a sword taken from a Burgundian.
8. Later testimony and chronicles allege that the Fierbois sword was broken when Jeanne struck a camp follower or prostitute with it. The location, date, circumstances, repair, and even whether the broken weapon was certainly the Fierbois sword are not consistently reported.
9. No documented custody chain continues beyond Jeanne’s company. No surviving object has a secure provenance from Fierbois or from Jeanne.
10. The attribution to Charles Martel is an unattested retrospective legend. It is absent from Jeanne’s testimony and the reliable core of the earliest accounts.
11. The composite weapon in the Musée des Beaux-Arts de Dijon, inventory **CA 1489**, is not supported as the Fierbois sword. Museum examination identifies it as a historically altered composite object whose documented provenance begins only with its purchase in 1826.

Throughout this dossier, the following labels are used:

- **Documented:** directly supported by a contemporary administrative, judicial, or material record.
- **Early testimony:** a statement made by a participant or near-contemporary, but not necessarily independently verified.
- **Early narrative:** a fifteenth-century chronicle or narrative account.
- **Scholarly inference:** a reconstruction proposed from the surviving evidence.
- **Institutional tradition:** a claim maintained by a church, municipality, museum, or heritage body.
- **Later legend:** a claim not present in the earliest evidence and first visible substantially later.
- **Unresolved:** the available evidence cannot establish the claim.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity and names

The object’s only secure historical identity is relational: it was the sword obtained for Jeanne d’Arc from the church or chapel of Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois in 1429.

Relevant names include:

- **French:** *l’épée de Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois*; *l’épée de Fierbois*; *l’épée de sainte Catherine*; *l’épée de Jeanne d’Arc*.
- **Middle French formulations:** *une espée qui estoit à Saincte-Catherine du Fierbois*; *l’espée trouvée à Sainte-Catherine de Fierbois*.
- **Latin trial language:** *gladius* or *ensis* associated with *ecclesia Sanctae Catharinae de Fierbois*.
- **English:** Sword of Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois; Sword of Saint Catherine; Sword of Fierbois; Joan of Arc’s sword.
- **Later legendary title:** Sword of Charles Martel.

“Saint Catherine” is Catherine of Alexandria, one of the saints whom Jeanne identified with her visionary counsel. *Fierbois* was the settlement and pilgrimage sanctuary in Touraine. The name does not mean that Catherine was alleged to have forged or personally wielded the weapon. It means that the sword came from her sanctuary and acquired sacral significance through that association.

### Secure physical characteristics

From Jeanne’s interrogation of 27 February 1431:

- It was a **sword**, not a dagger, falchion, ceremonial baton, or banner ornament.
- It was found **rusted**.
- It bore **five crosses**.
- It was recovered from the ground or a place perceived as being in or under the earth near the altar.
- It could be cleaned by rubbing: the clergy rubbed it and the rust came away readily.
- Jeanne said that it had not lain there long, or at least accepted the implication that its condition did not indicate remote antiquity.
- It was sufficiently serviceable to be worn through military campaigning.
- No contemporary source supplies its total length, blade length, weight, fuller pattern, pommel type, grip material, guard form, edge geometry, inscriptions beyond the crosses, or maker’s mark.

The trial wording places the crosses “upon” the sword. Some later narratives specify the blade near the hilt. Jean Chartier’s French chronicle says that it had five crosses “in the blade, fairly near the handle.” The precise arrangement—linear, quincunx, Jerusalem-cross-like, punched, engraved, or inlaid—is unknown. No reliable early image of the sword survives.

### Material

No source records a metallurgical examination, and no surviving candidate has a secure chain to the Fierbois weapon. It was presumably made principally of **ferrous metal**, probably forged steel or iron-and-steel construction appropriate to a late-medieval sword. This is a typological inference, not a scientific finding about the lost object.

Claims that the five crosses were gold inlays, that the sword was Carolingian, or that it bore a Jerusalem cross are not established by the trial evidence.

### Scabbards

Jeanne gave unusually specific evidence concerning three scabbards:

1. A **crimson or red velvet** scabbard.
2. A **cloth-of-gold** scabbard.
3. A scabbard of **good, strong leather**, ordered by Jeanne herself.

The first two were supplied through the clergy or supporters at Fierbois and Tours. Whether they were complete scabbards with wooden cores and metal fittings or rich textile coverings over such cores is not specified. None survives with an established provenance.

### Damage, repair, and loss

A later body of evidence says that Jeanne broke an important sword while striking a woman or women associated with the army. The versions do not agree about:

- whether the incident occurred at Saint-Denis, Château-Thierry, Auxerre, Gien, or after Patay;
- whether one woman or several were struck;
- whether the weapon broke across the woman’s back or during repeated blows;
- whether the king had it repaired;
- whether the broken sword was certainly the Fierbois weapon.

The sword and every securely associated scabbard are now lost.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Manufacture

**Unresolved.** No contract, payment account, workshop attribution, maker’s name, or scientific examination survives. The sword was already present at Fierbois when Jeanne requested it.

The condition described in 1431—rusted but easily cleaned and apparently functional—fits an old deposited weapon better than a deeply buried artifact of many centuries. It does not permit close dating.

### Fierbois before the sword’s recovery

Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois was already a pilgrimage sanctuary before Jeanne’s arrival. A collection of miracles associated with the shrine begins in 1375 and records a developed cult of Catherine. Military equipment given as ex-votos would be unsurprising at a shrine frequented by soldiers and travelers, although the sweeping modern assertion that “generations of soldiers” had left swords there must be distinguished from individual fifteenth-century descriptions of several weapons.

The present church is not the exact building interior from which the sword was retrieved. The earlier chapel was damaged or destroyed by fire in 1440; the present late-Gothic church was subsequently erected on or near its site. Consequently, modern illustrations showing Jeanne inside the present building are retrospective reconstructions.

### Jeanne’s visit

Jeanne acknowledged in 1431 that she had visited Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois on her journey to Chinon and heard three Masses there in one day. This matters because some later retellings say that she knew of the sword despite never having been to Fierbois. Her denial concerned having previously seen the sword or knowing the man sent to fetch it, not having visited the sanctuary.

### Earliest recoverable attestation

The sword’s retrieval occurred in 1429, but the letter Jeanne said she dictated to the clergy does not survive. The earliest exceptionally detailed surviving account is her testimony at Rouen on **27 February 1431**, incorporated both in the interrogations and in her answer to Article XIX.

In substance, Jeanne said:

- while at Tours or Chinon, she sent for a sword in the church of Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois;
- it was found immediately, rusty, in the earth near the altar;
- it bore five crosses;
- she knew of it through her “Voices”;
- she did not know or had never seen the man who fetched it;
- she wrote to the clergy asking whether they were willing for her to have it;
- the clergy sent it;
- it was not deep in the earth;
- she was uncertain whether the precise position was before or behind the altar;
- the clergy rubbed it and the rust came away readily;
- an arms merchant or armourer from Tours fetched it;
- two rich scabbards were provided;
- she ordered a strong leather one;
- she never blessed it or had it blessed;
- she valued it because it came from Saint Catherine’s church;
- she did not know what the five crosses signified.

The complete English translation of the relevant trial passage is available in the [Fordham Medieval Sourcebook](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/basis/joanofarc-trial.asp), while Quicherat’s nineteenth-century edition can be consulted in its [digitized first volume](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Livre%3AProc%C3%A8s_de_condamnation_et_de_r%C3%A9habilitation-1.djvu).

### The prosecution’s alternative account

Article XIX of Jean d’Estivet’s accusation alleged that Jeanne, “consulting demons and using divination,” had secretly placed or caused the sword to be placed in the church so that its supposed revelation would deceive princes, clergy, nobles, and common people.

This is a **contemporary allegation**, not proof of fraud. The prosecution supplied no demonstrated witness to concealment, no identified accomplice, and no physical evidence. Jeanne denied the allegation and referred to her earlier account.

Its value for object history is nevertheless substantial: it proves that by 1431 the sword episode was considered politically and theologically important enough to require an adversarial explanation. Control over the interpretation of the object—relic-like providential sign or staged instrument of deception—was already contested.

### Traditional sacred origin

Jeanne attributed her knowledge to her Voices, identified elsewhere with Saint Michael, Saint Catherine, and Saint Margaret. She did not say:

- that Saint Catherine had owned the sword;
- that the sword descended from heaven;
- that it possessed magical powers;
- that its crosses represented the Five Wounds;
- that Charles Martel had left it;
- that it was intrinsically invincible.

Her stated affection rested on provenance: it came from Catherine’s church.

### Charles Martel attribution

The popular story says Charles Martel deposited the sword after defeating an Umayyad army near Tours or Poitiers in 732 and that it awaited a later divinely chosen savior of France.

This is a **later legend**. It is absent from Jeanne’s testimony and cannot be reconciled comfortably with the physical description of a sword whose rust was easily removed and which was understood not to have been buried deeply or for an immense period. No Carolingian documentation links Charles Martel to the object or establishes that he founded the Fierbois shrine.

The legend retrospectively joins two military deliverance narratives:

- Charles Martel’s victory over a foreign army;
- Jeanne’s campaign to relieve Orléans and secure Charles VII’s coronation.

Its political utility is evident, but its material provenance is unsupported.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Before 1429: unidentified depositor and unknown custody

The chain begins with a gap.

A sword was deposited, concealed, stored, or lost within the earlier church at Fierbois. Its maker, date, original owner, and mechanism of entry are unknown.

Competing early explanations include:

- a votive offering among other weapons;
- old iron stored in a chest;
- a sword placed near or on a knight’s tomb;
- an object shallowly buried near the altar.

No source securely identifies its depositor. The Charles Martel link belongs to later tradition.

### February 1429: Jeanne at Fierbois

On the journey from Vaucouleurs to Chinon, Jeanne stopped at Fierbois and heard Mass three times. She did not say that she removed or inspected the sword during this visit.

**Custody:** clergy or keepers of the Fierbois sanctuary.

### Late March–April 1429: request and recovery

After examination at Poitiers and while at Chinon or Tours, Jeanne dictated a letter to the Fierbois clergy. The original is lost; its content is known only through her 1431 testimony.

The clergy searched for the sword. Exactly who performed the physical excavation or search is not named. They found a rusty sword marked with five crosses and cleaned it.

**Transfer mechanism:** ecclesiastical consent and gift or delivery in response to Jeanne’s written request.

**Intermediary:** an unnamed Tours arms merchant or armourer.

The [reconstruction of the lost letter](https://archive.joan-of-arc.org/joanofarc_letter_March-April_1429.html) properly treats the trial testimony as evidence for its existence, not as the original text.

### 1429: Tours

The sword passed to Jeanne at or through Tours. Local supporters and the Fierbois clergy supplied rich scabbards. Jeanne ordered the utilitarian leather scabbard.

This is the first point at which Jeanne’s personal custody is established.

### April–September 1429: military custody

Jeanne testified that she carried it continually until the French withdrawal from Saint-Denis following the assault on Paris. Likely stages in its physical itinerary therefore include:

- Tours;
- Blois;
- the Orléans campaign;
- the Loire operations;
- the route to Reims;
- Reims at the time of Charles VII’s coronation;
- the subsequent advance toward Paris;
- Saint-Denis.

Not every place can be tied to a separate object-specific record. “Carried continually” is Jeanne’s general testimony and should not be converted into an independently documented sighting at every engagement.

Jeanne also testified that she preferred her standard to her sword and bore the standard in battle so as not to kill anyone. No reliable source records a person killed by her with the Fierbois sword.

### September 1429: endpoint of secure custody

Jeanne said that she carried the Fierbois weapon continually **until she left Saint-Denis**. At Saint-Denis she offered a complete suit of white armour and a sword captured before Paris as a votive gift. Her wording distinguishes that deposited sword from the Fierbois weapon.

Accordingly, the sword historically recorded at the abbey of Saint-Denis cannot automatically be identified as the Fierbois sword.

### Late 1429–1430: broken or entrusted?

The post-Saint-Denis chain is irrecoverable.

Two principal reconstructions compete:

#### Broken-sword tradition

Jean Chartier and later testimony say Jeanne broke a sword while striking a camp follower or prostitute in enforcement of military discipline. Jean II, duke of Alençon, recalled the incident during the nullification proceedings. Later versions identify the broken sword as the Fierbois weapon and say Charles VII was displeased.

The testimony is important but was given more than twenty years after the alleged event. Narrative sources disagree over location and timing. The claim is therefore **credible early testimony, not an uncontested custody record**.

Some versions add that a royal craftsman received the pieces for repair but could not restore the weapon. This detail is not consistently attested.

#### Custody with Jeanne’s brothers or household

At Rouen, Jeanne said that when captured she no longer had the Fierbois sword. Discussion of her property suggests that her brothers or household controlled various horses, equipment, and possessions. It is possible that the sword, intact or broken, remained with them. No receipt, inventory, inheritance record, or later transfer confirms that possibility.

### 23 May 1430: capture at Compiègne

Jeanne explicitly stated that she did **not** have the Fierbois sword when captured. She carried a weapon taken from a Burgundian.

Therefore:

- the Burgundians did not demonstrably seize the Fierbois sword at Compiègne;
- the English did not demonstrably acquire it through Jeanne’s transfer to their custody;
- later claims that it became English war booty have no secure evidentiary basis.

### After 1430

No continuous documentary chain exists. The object disappears from verifiable history.

Claims that it was:

- buried at Lagny;
- walled into a pillar;
- hidden beneath an abbey;
- preserved at Saint-Denis;
- carried away by the English;
- retained by Jeanne’s brothers;
- destroyed in the French Revolution;
- preserved in a private château;
- incorporated into another weapon

are unsupported, weakly supported, or refer to different swords associated with Jeanne.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Did the 1429 sword exist?

The affirmative case is strong:

- Jeanne described the sword in detailed, materially mundane terms under hostile interrogation.
- Her accusers did not deny the existence of a recovered sword; they alleged that she had planted it.
- Fifteenth-century chroniclers knew of the episode.
- The acquisition involved multiple persons—the Fierbois clergy, an intermediary from Tours, and people who supplied scabbards.

The surviving evidence cannot prove supernatural revelation, but it makes the existence of a physical sword substantially more likely than literary invention.

### Was the retrieval staged?

The prosecution alleged deliberate concealment. No corroborating evidence survives. The possibility cannot be disproved absolutely because neither the original letter nor testimony from the searchers survives, but it remains an unproved hostile allegation.

Naturalistic scholarly explanations have included:

- Jeanne saw or heard about the sword during her earlier visit;
- she learned of a group of votive weapons and selected one through a detailed message;
- local clergy chose a suitable marked sword after receiving her request;
- the later narrative compressed an ordinary search into an immediate discovery.

These are possible reconstructions, not documented findings.

### Was it Charles Martel’s sword?

No credible provenance supports this. The attribution is absent from the foundational evidence and lacks a chain across approximately seven centuries. It is properly classified as a later patriotic and religious legend.

### Did the rust vanish miraculously?

Jeanne said the clergy rubbed the sword and the rust came away easily. Later retellings convert this into spontaneous or miraculous cleansing. The early statement describes manual cleaning; it does not explicitly claim a miracle.

### Was it magical or blessed?

Jeanne denied blessing it or having it blessed. She denied magical practices. The prosecution alleged that she treated the sword, ring, and banners as luck-bearing enchanted objects. That allegation was part of the heresy case and cannot be accepted as a neutral description.

### Was the broken sword the Fierbois sword?

Possibly, but not conclusively. The duke of Alençon’s memory and Jean Chartier’s narrative connect the breaking to a significant sword, while Jeanne’s own “until Saint-Denis” endpoint is compatible with losing it through breakage around that stage. Contradictions in place and chronology prevent certainty.

### Dijon claimant: inventory CA 1489

The best-known surviving candidate is the **Épée dite de Jeanne d’Arc**, Musée des Beaux-Arts de Dijon, inventory **CA 1489**.

Its documented facts are:

- steel;
- length 95 cm;
- width 16.3 cm;
- purchased by the city or museum in 1826 from Joseph Bertholomey;
- no documented pre-1826 provenance;
- inscriptions include “Charles septiesme,” “Vaucouleurs,” and “1419”;
- it incorporates French and Orléans arms and an image derived from the sixteenth-century monument to Jeanne at Orléans;
- it bears a gold-damascened mark associated with the Toledo armourer Lupus Aguado;
- examination found it to be composite;
- the blade consists of two welded parts, considered fifteenth-century;
- its guard, wired grip, and pommel are stylistically and functionally incoherent;
- the mounting is later;
- the “1419” date is historically anomalous for Jeanne’s career.

Fabrice Cognot’s 2010 assessment found the blade excessively long for the one-handed mounting, the S-shaped guard nonfunctional, and the pommel foreign to normal medieval typologies. The museum concludes that the object is more plausibly a later commemorative or League-period construction than a weapon used by Jeanne. Its official record, updated in February 2026, preserves the cautious title “said to be” Jeanne’s sword rather than authenticating it as such. See the [French national museum catalogue](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/joconde/01370033300).

This weapon is historically valuable as an artifact of the post-medieval cult of Jeanne. It is not the documented Fierbois sword.

### Saint-Denis sword

Jeanne deposited at Saint-Denis a sword “won before Paris” together with armour. Seventeenth-century descriptions of weapons or fragments at Saint-Denis have sometimes been connected to her. Her own testimony distinguishes the offered sword from the Fierbois weapon. Later confusion between the two has produced a false continuity.

### Other reputed swords

Weapons at various museums, churches, châteaux, and private collections have been attributed to Jeanne, including objects said to be:

- the Vaucouleurs sword;
- a royal gift;
- the Orléans sword;
- the sword used at Compiègne;
- a fragment or guard of the Saint-Denis sword.

None has a demonstrated chain from the Fierbois recovery. “Joan of Arc’s sword” is therefore a category of competing commemorative attributions, not a single securely surviving artifact.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Votive setting

Before Jeanne acquired it, the sword belonged to the material environment of a pilgrimage church. If it was one of several donated weapons, its original function at Fierbois was likely ex-voto deposition: arms surrendered, dedicated, or displayed in thanksgiving or supplication.

That reconstruction is plausible but not demonstrable for this individual sword because its depositor is unknown.

### Authorization of Jeanne’s mission

The recovery functioned as a public sign that apparently corroborated Jeanne’s claimed supernatural knowledge. This explains why the condemnation tribunal treated it as evidence requiring either a divine, demonic, or fraudulent explanation.

The object’s force arose less from unusual craftsmanship than from the conjunction of:

- predicted location;
- distinctive five-cross mark;
- recovery from a saint’s sanctuary;
- Jeanne’s identification with Saint Catherine;
- the political crisis of 1429.

### Military function

Jeanne wore the sword through the campaigns of 1429. Sources commonly depict it as a fighting weapon, but Jeanne said she personally preferred her standard and carried it to avoid killing. The sword therefore performed several overlapping functions:

- sidearm;
- visible token of military command;
- sign of saintly authorization;
- disciplinary instrument;
- portable association with Fierbois.

### Discipline

Early witnesses describe Jeanne driving prostitutes or camp followers from the army and striking them with the flat of a sword. The reported breaking belongs to this disciplinary context rather than to combat.

This episode subsequently became moralized in opposing directions:

- admirers treated it as evidence of Jeanne’s insistence on a morally purified army;
- critics read it as violence against vulnerable women;
- providential narratives treated the breakage as a portent of declining fortune.

The sources establish neither supernatural punishment nor a causal relationship between the break and Jeanne’s later capture.

### Coronation symbolism

The sword was probably present during the Reims campaign if Jeanne’s statement that she wore it continually until Saint-Denis is taken literally. Her banner, however, received the explicit ceremonial honor at Charles VII’s coronation. Later art frequently elevates the sword into the central coronation relic; that emphasis exceeds the contemporary evidence.

### Five crosses

Jeanne explicitly said she did not know their purpose. Later interpretations include:

- the Five Wounds of Christ;
- the Jerusalem cross;
- a crusading or military-order mark;
- repeated consecration marks;
- a maker’s or owner’s device.

None can be confirmed. The popular equation of the five crosses with the five fleurs-de-lis on the sword in Jeanne’s granted arms confuses two different iconographic descriptions.

### Not a formal relic

No surviving fifteenth-century record shows that the sword was canonically authenticated, placed in a reliquary, or authorized for veneration. It acquired relic-like significance through narrative and association, but Jeanne herself rejected the suggestion that she had it blessed or used it magically.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The revealed sword

**Tier: early attested religious claim.**

Jeanne said her Voices revealed the sword’s location. The claim is directly attested in her own testimony. Whether the information was supernatural is beyond documentary verification.

Later retellings sharpened the episode by removing Jeanne’s uncertainty, saying she specified an exact place and that the sword appeared immediately.

### Miraculously vanishing rust

**Tier: later elaboration from early evidence.**

The source says clergy rubbed the sword and the rust came away without difficulty. Later accounts say the rust vanished upon discovery, upon Jeanne’s touch, or without human action. Those forms are embellishments.

### Charles Martel’s sword

**Tier: later patriotic legend.**

In the full legend, Charles Martel used the sword in the battle of 732, founded or endowed the Fierbois shrine, and deposited the weapon for a future deliverer of France. Jeanne’s supernatural counselors then directed her to it.

No eighth-century source or early Joan source establishes this. The legend supplies the sword with an ancient national pedigree and turns its reappearance into typological repetition: France delivered once from foreign invasion and delivered again.

The claim continues in municipal, religious, tourism, and popular heritage presentations, generally with “according to tradition” language. Its survival as institutional tradition does not create archaeological provenance.

### A Templar or Hospitaller weapon

**Tier: modern speculation.**

The five crosses have encouraged association with the Knights Templar, Hospitallers, crusaders, or a Jerusalem-cross emblem. There is no surviving blade to inspect, and the medieval witnesses did not identify the marks in this way. These theories are conjectural.

### A prophecy hidden beneath the altar

**Tier: late accretion.**

Modern retellings often imply a standing prophecy that a chosen maiden would recover the sword. No such prophecy appears in Jeanne’s testimony.

### The sword that could not be repaired

**Tier: early narrative with providential elaboration.**

Jean Chartier’s account reportedly says that after the sword broke, a royal craftsman could not restore it—evidence, in his narrative logic, that it came by miracle and was lost through misuse. The episode transforms mechanical failure into moral omen.

Whether a repair was actually attempted cannot be independently established.

### Burial at Lagny

**Tier: regional modern legend.**

A local tradition places the sword or its pieces at Lagny-sur-Marne, buried beneath the abbey church, concealed in a subterranean passage, or walled into a pillar or chapel. No medieval inventory, deposition record, excavation report, or continuous local tradition establishes this. It is a proposed solution to a documentary disappearance, not a recovered provenance.

### Survival at Saint-Denis

**Tier: conflation.**

Jeanne gave armour and a captured sword to Saint-Denis. Later writers merged this offering with the Fierbois sword. Her own interrogation distinguishes the two objects.

### Revolutionary destruction

**Tier: unverified retrospective explanation.**

Because many ecclesiastical arms and relics were destroyed or dispersed during the Revolution, disappearance in that period is frequently proposed. This presupposes an undocumented survival from 1430 to 1789 and therefore cannot repair the provenance gap.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Historical object

**Status:** lost; possibly broken or dispersed by 1429–1430.

**Last secure custodian:** Jeanne d’Arc or members of her military household.

**Last secure general location:** Saint-Denis/Paris campaign, September 1429, based on Jeanne’s statement that she wore it until leaving Saint-Denis.

**Last possible later location:** somewhere within Jeanne’s household network, if references to her brothers holding her effects included the sword.

There is:

- no accession number;
- no verified fragment;
- no scientific analysis;
- no photograph, cast, or measured drawing made from the original;
- no secure early modern inventory;
- no active legal title or repatriation proceeding concerning an authenticated object.

### Site associated with recovery

The church of Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois remains the principal commemorative site, but the present church postdates the recovery. Heritage presentations and a public statue preserve the local association. A monument inscription records the traditional date **23 April 1429** for Jeanne’s sending for the sword, but that precision is commemorative rather than established by the trial transcript. The French heritage record for the monument is available through [POP/Palissy](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/palissy/IM37002205).

### Dijon sword

**Location:** Musée des Beaux-Arts, Dijon.

**Accession:** CA 1489.

**Ownership:** property of the municipality of Dijon.

**Status:** extant composite historical weapon; not authenticated as the Fierbois sword.

Its museum catalogue provides the fullest public technical description and provenance. It should be displayed and discussed as a claimant or commemorative artifact, not as the recovered Fierbois weapon.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Nineteenth-century revival

The sword became prominent during the nineteenth-century revival of Jeanne as a Catholic, monarchical, republican, and national symbol.

William Etty’s monumental painting, *Jeanne d’Arc trouve l’épée dans la chapelle de Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois* (1842–1846), supplied a visual environment absent from the early sources. The painting is now in the Musée des Beaux-Arts d’Orléans, inventory 2024.24.1. Its museum record documents its exhibition at the Royal Academy in 1847 and later ownership history. See the [Musées d’Orléans catalogue](https://museesorleans.fr/oeuvres/musee%3AMUS_BIEN%3A729382/).

A separate nineteenth-century painting presented the weapon explicitly as Charles Martel’s sword and relocated the scene into a fantastical forest setting, demonstrating how rapidly artistic treatment could naturalize a late legend. The work is catalogued by the [Louvre](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark%3A/53355/cl010056076).

### Nationalist function

After the Franco-Prussian War, local clerical and patriotic discourse increasingly joined Fierbois, Charles Martel, Jeanne, and the recovery of lost French territory. An Orléans museum catalogue notes that the Charles Martel association was particularly affirmed by local prelates after 1870–1871. The sword thus moved from a sign within Jeanne’s mission to a reusable emblem of national deliverance.

### Beatification and canonization era

Jeanne’s beatification in 1909 and canonization in 1920 created strong demand for authenticating associated objects. The Dijon sword was advanced as a possible Joan relic in 1911, although earlier catalogues had called it merely a sword “from the time of Charles VII.” This is a documented example of devotional and national circumstances altering an object’s attribution.

### Replicas

Modern commercial swords labeled “Jeanne d’Arc,” “Sword of Saint Catherine,” or “Fierbois sword” necessarily reconstruct the missing form. Common features—crosses arranged on the blade, fleur-de-lis pommels, elaborate inscriptions, cruciform guards, or white-and-gold scabbards—usually derive from modern iconography rather than an archaeological original.

Such replicas should not be treated as facsimiles. At best they are historically informed conjectures; frequently they are fantasy designs.

### Heraldry

The arms associated with Jeanne’s family show a sword supporting a crown between fleurs-de-lis. Modern reception often identifies that heraldic sword with the Fierbois weapon. The historical descriptions of the physical sword specify five crosses, whereas the heraldic composition has different symbolic elements. The identity is interpretive, not an exact depiction.

### Film, literature, and games

Modern narratives routinely make the sword:

- Charles Martel’s weapon;
- a supernatural relic;
- proof that Jeanne was chosen;
- an indestructible or magically empowered blade;
- a weapon lost at her capture.

These features exceed the documentary core. Their importance lies in shaping public expectations: viewers frequently approach claimant swords expecting a visibly ancient Carolingian weapon with elaborate cross symbolism, although the trial describes only a rusty sword with five crosses.

### Contemporary local culture

Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois uses the sword episode in public history, festivals, tourism, monumental inscriptions, stained glass, and local associations. These are genuine elements of the object’s memorial afterlife, although they do not establish the original sword’s survival.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Primary record

The essential source is the condemnation trial of 1431, particularly:

- interrogation of 27 February;
- question of 17 March concerning the crosses;
- Article XIX and Jeanne’s reply on 27 March;
- related questions about magical use, blessing, and the sword carried at capture.

The proceedings survive through a complex documentary tradition rather than a verbatim modern transcript. The authentic formal Latin record was prepared from minutes; the French responses were translated and normalized. Quicherat’s 1841–1849 publication remained foundational, though later critical editions by Pierre Tisset and Yvonne Lanhers improved the textual basis.

A useful account of the formation of the trial record is given in Quicherat’s [notice on the condemnation proceedings](https://jeanne.wiki/quicherat-proces/tome5/notice-condamnation).

### Nullification evidence

The 1450–1456 proceedings provide retrospective witness testimony. Jean II, duke of Alençon, and other companions remembered Jeanne’s behavior in the field. These witnesses are valuable but spoke roughly two decades after the events, and their accounts sometimes combine observation, camp memory, and developed interpretation.

The third volume of Quicherat’s edition, containing nullification material, is available as a [digitized PDF](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/23/Proc%C3%A8s_de_condamnation_et_de_r%C3%A9habilitation_de_Jeanne_d%27Arc%2C_dite_La_Pucelle-_Proc%C3%A8s_de_r%C3%A9habilitation_%28IA_ProcesDeCondamnationV3%29.pdf).

### Chronicles

Principal narrative traditions include:

- Jean Chartier’s *Chronique de Charles VII*;
- the *Chronique de la Pucelle*;
- the *Journal du siège d’Orléans*;
- the Parisian bourgeois chronicle;
- Italian diplomatic and narrative reports;
- regional chronicles.

These sources corroborate the sword’s fame but vary in location, handling, and interpretation. Jean Chartier’s manuscript tradition can be traced through [BnF Français 5051](https://portail.biblissima.fr/fr/ark%3A/43093/mdatab13e733b5f05870643ab7f4ea7473cc55c9ed3d1), while a modern study cautions that the relationship between Chartier’s Latin and French chronicles is textually complicated: [Bibliothèque de l’École des chartes](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bec_0373-6237_1926_num_87_1_448752).

### Principal debates

#### Exact findspot

- Jeanne: shallow earth near the altar, probably behind it, but she was unsure.
- Other accounts: among several swords, in a chest with old iron, at a particular place in the church, or on a knight’s tomb.

The divergence may reflect different stages: storage among weapons, discovery near the altar, and later narrative decoration. It cannot be harmonized with confidence.

#### How Jeanne knew

Interpretations divide among:

- supernatural revelation;
- information learned during her earlier visit;
- knowledge of the shrine’s votive objects;
- cooperation by the clergy;
- deliberate staging.

Only the claim of revelation and the accusation of staging are directly documented. Neither metaphysical conclusion can be historically demonstrated.

#### Age and type

The absent object prevents typological or metallurgical analysis. Scholarly descriptions that call it Carolingian, crusader, fourteenth-century, or a standard fifteenth-century arming sword go beyond the evidence.

#### Breaking

Early evidence makes the general incident plausible, but uncertainty persists over weapon identity, date, place, degree of damage, and repair. It should not be represented as a completely documented destruction.

#### Survival

No serious provenance bridges the gap after 1430. All surviving candidates fail the continuity test.

### Discredited but persistent theories

The following circulate widely without adequate support:

- the physical sword has been scientifically dated to the eighth century;
- the Dijon sword is the Fierbois sword;
- the weapon was definitely destroyed during the Revolution;
- the five crosses prove Templar ownership;
- the sword was Excalibur-like or intrinsically magical;
- the rust vanished at Jeanne’s touch;
- the Burgundians captured the Fierbois sword at Compiègne;
- a medieval prophecy explicitly foretold its recovery by a maiden.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and near-primary evidence

The central documentary source is the 1431 condemnation trial. Quicherat’s edition remains readily accessible and contains the Latin formal record and associated materials. The nullification testimony is essential for the breaking episode but must be treated as retrospective evidence.

Jean Chartier’s chronicle, the *Chronique de la Pucelle*, and the *Journal du siège d’Orléans* are near-contemporary narrative witnesses. They are not interchangeable: their differences over the sword’s location and fate must be preserved rather than harmonized.

The supposed 1429 letter to the Fierbois clergy is lost. Any modern “text” of that letter is a reconstruction from Jeanne’s 1431 description.

### Material and museum evidence

The French national Joconde record for Dijon CA 1489 is the authoritative public catalogue for the principal claimant. It supplies dimensions, inscriptions, acquisition history, examination results, exhibition history, and bibliography.

The Musées d’Orléans catalogue documents William Etty’s painting and its modern provenance. The Louvre and Salon catalogues document nineteenth-century artistic transformation of the sword into Charles Martel’s weapon.

### Source criticism

Several modern websites reproduce reliable primary-source passages but add unverified commentary. Their transcriptions have been used only where checked against the structure of the trial record or established editions. Municipal and ecclesiastical pages are valuable evidence for current institutional tradition, not proof of medieval provenance.

Popular claims about Charles Martel, miraculous rust removal, burial at Lagny, Templar ownership, and surviving swords have been included as reception history only.
