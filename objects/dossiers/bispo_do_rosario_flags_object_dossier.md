# Arthur Bispo do Rosario's embroidered flags: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

“Arthur Bispo do Rosario’s embroidered flags” is not the published title of one uniquely identifiable object. It is an English collective description for a group of textile works that Brazilian catalogues classify as **estandartes**—banners or standards, rather than national or maritime flags. Each surviving banner is a separate physical object with its own catalogue number, imagery, inscriptions, dimensions, condition, and exhibition history.

This dossier therefore tracks the **corpus of embroidered estandartes**, with particular attention to the five individually documented examples presently visible in the Museu Bispo do Rosario Arte Contemporânea’s online catalogue:

1. *Eu preciso destas palavras escrita / Desenhos Geométricos*, MBRAC.ABR.0001.
2. *Navios de guerra*, MBRAC.ABR.0004.
3. *Avenida Rosangela Maria*, MBRAC.ABR.0005.
4. *Colônia Juliano Moreira / Reconheceram o filho de Deus*, MBRAC.ABR.0006.
5. *Dicionário de nomes letra A I*, MBRAC.ABR.0007.

The same catalogue also classifies *África*, MBRAC.AB.0506, as an *estandarte*, although it differs materially from the predominantly embroidered sheet banners. Conservation research identifies at least one further banner, *Vós Habitantes do Planeta Terra, Eu Apresento Suas Nações / A História Universal*. Contemporary records state that **fifteen estandartes** were exhibited in 1982, but the accessible exhibition documentation does not securely enumerate all fifteen. Consequently, no claim is made here that the six named objects constitute the complete corpus.

Evidence labels used below:

- **Documented:** supported by institutional records, catalogues, contemporary photographs, or archival documentation.
- **Material finding:** based on direct physical examination or conservation documentation.
- **Artist’s testimony:** a statement recorded from Bispo himself; reliable evidence for his conception of the works, not necessarily for supernatural events.
- **Scholarly inference:** a reasoned interpretation not conclusively demonstrated.
- **Institutional or curatorial attribution:** a title, category, date, or meaning assigned after the objects entered museum discourse.
- **Unresolved:** not established by presently accessible evidence.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Object class and terminology

The authoritative digital catalogue defines an **estandarte** as a work, usually textile, ornamented with embroidered words, symbols, and names. The Portuguese word derives from the military and ceremonial “standard”: a normally portable emblem suspended from or fastened to a staff. “Banner” is the closest general English translation. “Flag” is usable descriptively, but it may imply a conventional vexillological object that these works are not.

Bispo did not ordinarily sign, date, or give stable art-market titles to his productions. The present titles commonly derive from prominent inscriptions or subjects and were systematized by Frederico Morais, Gerardo Vilaseca, and later museum cataloguers. Titles containing a slash generally identify the different subjects or inscriptions on the two faces. The museum’s current use of *designação: estandarte* is therefore a posthumous classificatory act, albeit one grounded in the objects’ construction and in their documented capacity to be raised on staffs.

“Arthur Bispo do Rosario” is the spelling adopted by the current museum and catalogue raisonné. The formerly widespread “Rosário,” with an acute accent, remains common in publications. The museum explains that baptismal and biographical research favored the unaccented documentary spelling.

### 1.2 General construction

The conventional embroidered banners are large, rectangular, two-faced textiles assembled from discarded or previously used hospital sheets. Conservation researcher Vanessa Magalhães Pinto, after in-person examination, described their standard construction as:

- fragments of worn sheet fabric joined by hand sewing;
- generally two parallel textile layers, permitting both faces to be worked;
- embroidery, writing, drawing, and occasional ink applied over the assembled ground;
- a cylindrical wooden support along the upper edge;
- narrow fabric, plastic, synthetic-leather, or cord loops attaching the textile to that support;
- in some examples, blue threadwork around the middle of the supporting rod, resembling the technique later catalogued as ORFA, *Objetos Recobertos por Fios Azuis* (“Objects Covered with Blue Threads”).

The sheets were already aged and damaged before incorporation. Their present yellow, beige, ochre, or unevenly white colors reflect different manufacturing histories, repeated institutional use, oxidation, light, dirt, humidity, and mechanical wear. Some pieces contained tears, loosened weave, stains, and losses when Bispo acquired them. He joined or overlaid patches and sometimes embroidered directly across seams. Material examination suggests that he normally constructed the textile field first and embroidered it afterward, since stitches cross the joins between fragments.

The visible seams are not merely incidental repairs. In *Colônia Juliano Moreira*, four differently toned vertical sections organize the imagery into columns. Frederico Morais interpreted the tonal variations as creating depth; conservation analysis found that they also structure the written and pictorial fields.

The embroidery incorporates text as image. Letters vary in size and density; lists, names, abbreviations, numbers, geographical entries, anatomical terms, naval signals, and narrative passages coexist with figurative drawings. The objects have no known maker’s marks, conventional signatures, or securely inscribed completion dates. No published weights were located.

### 1.3 Individually catalogued banners

#### A. *Eu preciso destas palavras escrita / Desenhos Geométricos*

- **Accession:** MBRAC.ABR.0001.
- **Class:** *estandarte*.
- **Themes:** asylum life; inventory.
- **Materials:** fabric, sewing thread, cotton thread, wood, felt-tip ink, and other ink.
- **Techniques:** sewing, covering, embroidery, and writing.
- **Overall catalogue dimensions:** 124 × 208 × 4 cm.
- **Textile alone:** approximately 120 × 190 cm.
- **With support, in the detailed description:** approximately 126 × 208 cm.
- **Support:** round wooden rod, 4 cm thick and 208 cm long; its center is covered by embroidered blue-thread work. A soutache trim and seven small synthetic-leather strips plus three cords connect textile and rod.

The front combines a representation of a carnival *clóvis*, Sugarloaf Mountain and Morro da Urca joined by the cable car, long alphabetical lists beginning with A, a diagrammatic male body, and an extended first-person account dated “22 DEZEMBRO 1938 – MEIA NOITE.” The central male figure bears “CLOVES” on his chest and is surrounded by anatomical terminology. Beneath him appears the phrase from which the title was derived: “EU PRECISO DESTAS PALAVRAS · ESCRITA”—approximately, “I need these words written.”

The autobiographical panel says that seven angels in special cloud-like forms accompanied Bispo and left him behind the walled house at Rua São Clemente 301, Botafogo. It then traces a route through Rio and describes his intended religious presentation. Its spelling, punctuation, word division, and syntax are Bispo’s own and should not be silently normalized.

The reverse contains a row of buildings or “palaces” labeled with countries, a list and map of Brazil, numerous geometric figures, and a rural scene. Because it maps places, words, bodies, states, and personal revelation on a single surface, it is one of the clearest material expressions of the inventory project.

#### B. *Navios de guerra*

- **Accession:** MBRAC.ABR.0004.
- **Class:** *estandarte*.
- **Materials:** fabric, thread, cotton thread, wood, and ballpoint ink.
- **Techniques:** sewing, covering, embroidery, and writing.
- **Dimensions:** 141 × 132 × 1 cm.
- **Condition details:** made from aged white sheet fragments; two lighter textile patches are sewn and embroidered into the reverse.

Its densely embroidered text inventories warships, Brazilian states, naval personnel, signal codes, fleet divisions, maneuvers, torpedoes, anti-aircraft guns, ports, hours, and orders. Named vessels or naval terms include *Humaitá*, *Brasília*, *Santos*, *Rio de Janeiro*, *Bahia*, *Niterói*, *Pernambuco*, *Ceará*, *Santa Catarina*, and *Mato Grosso*. It incorporates a version of a signal alphabet and such commands as extinguishing lights, preparing to sail, hoisting signals, and entering or leaving port.

The work materially connects Bispo’s later inventory with his documented service in the Brazilian Navy, where he was trained as a signalman. The banner is not a surviving naval signal flag, however; it is a later mnemonic and classificatory construction using naval language.

#### C. *Avenida Rosangela Maria*

- **Accession:** MBRAC.ABR.0005.
- **Class:** *estandarte*.
- **Themes:** asylum life; inventory.
- **Materials:** fabric, thread, cotton thread, wood, ink, and graphite.
- **Techniques:** sewing, covering, embroidery, and writing.
- **Dimensions:** 129 × 177 × 1 cm.

The title names Rosangela Maria Grilo Magalhães, a psychology trainee who visited Bispo at the Colônia in the early 1980s and whose name appears repeatedly across his production. The museum’s current record does not provide a publicly searchable full transcription comparable to that for MBRAC.ABR.0001. Its exact date remains unestablished.

#### D. *Colônia Juliano Moreira / Reconheceram o filho de Deus*

- **Accession:** MBRAC.ABR.0006.
- **Class:** *estandarte*.
- **Themes:** asylum life; inventory.
- **Materials:** fabric, thread, cotton thread, and wood.
- **Techniques:** sewing, covering, embroidery, and writing.
- **Dimensions:** 136 × 164 × 4 cm.

The banner consists of several pieces of aged white cotton sheet, embroidered across both faces and suspended from wood. At the upper edge, soutache is attached through six small synthetic-leather strips and two cords. The center of the wooden rod carries blue-thread work, itself covered by sewn plastic.

One face shows plans or diagrammatic views of the Colônia Juliano Moreira: pavilions, an electric network, figures in blue institutional uniforms, a wall and entrance, a church, buses, cars, a motor scooter, livestock, and people. Other bands contain writing and repeated circular, triangular, linear, and alphabetic signs.

The reverse begins “RECONHECERAM FILHO DO HOM[EM] 24 DE DEZEMBRO – 1938” (“They recognized the Son of Man, 24 December 1938”) and lists named persons associated with the psychiatric hospital and the Ulisses Viana unit. This inscription is the source of the second half of the modern title.

Material examination distinguished several chronological layers:

- sheet pieces and seams incorporated by Bispo;
- original repairs likely made by Bispo to stabilize already damaged cloth;
- embroidery crossing those joins;
- a textile adhesion over a hole and an edge completion interpreted as later conservation interventions;
- tulle placed over attachment areas, absent in earlier photographs and therefore demonstrably post-production;
- two wooden slats added over the cylindrical rod after Bispo’s lifetime or at least after the early photographic record;
- the cylindrical rod itself, judged original.

A 1985 photograph by Walter Firmo shows Bispo connecting a cylindrical support to a bamboo pole and raising a banner. This and other photographs confirm that the rods were functional suspension elements, not merely modern museum hanging devices.

#### E. *Dicionário de nomes letra A I*

- **Accession:** MBRAC.ABR.0007.
- **Class:** *estandarte*.
- **Materials:** fabric, thread, cotton thread, wood, and plant fiber.
- **Techniques:** sewing, covering, embroidery, and writing.
- **Dimensions:** 138 × 231 × 4 cm.

Its modern title—“Dictionary of Names, Letter A I”—describes its lexicographic or nominative function. The catalogue’s biographical research notes that it preserves names of some men associated with Bispo’s naval-apprentice years. The object belongs to the broader practice through which he recorded persons as well as things.

#### F. *África*

- **Accession as printed:** MBRAC.AB.0506.
- **Class:** *estandarte*.
- **Theme:** inventory.
- **Materials:** paper, wood, fabric, metal, lime-based paint or whitewash, graphite, colored pencil, and cotton thread.
- **Techniques:** assembly, carpentry, drawing, painting, and writing.
- **Dimensions:** 117 × 81 × 5 cm.

Unlike the sheet-based embroidered standards, *África* is materially closer to a mounted mixed-media panel. Its inclusion demonstrates that *estandarte* is a curatorial class based partly on form and function rather than a uniform medium.

#### G. *Vós Habitantes do Planeta Terra, Eu Apresento Suas Nações / A História Universal*

This work is documented in conservation research but its current accession number and full catalogue description were not recovered from the accessible public interface. Direct examination found embroidery crossing joins, overlapped textile reinforcements on the reverse, and dense stitched zones supported by extra layers of fabric. The title translates approximately as “You inhabitants of planet Earth, I present your nations / Universal History.”

### 1.4 What survives and what has been altered

The banners survive as discrete museum objects, but their original environment does not. Bispo continually rearranged hundreds of objects within the connected cells of the Ulisses Viana Pavilion. After his death the ensemble was dismantled and removed for security. The position of each banner relative to other works, raw materials, his bed, architectural surfaces, and accumulated objects was not fully documented before removal.

Accordingly, two levels of loss must be distinguished:

1. **The individual banners largely survive**, with age, artist-made patches, and later conservation additions.
2. **The larger spatial work or environment is irrecoverable.** Later exhibitions can display the pieces but cannot reproduce the changing arrangement maintained by Bispo.

No published destructive fiber analysis, dye analysis, radiocarbon dating, or scientific authentication campaign was found. “Cotton” and other material identifications in the catalogue appear to be visual, documentary, or conservation identifications rather than results of a published laboratory program.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Documentary origin of the physical objects

The individual banners are catalogued as **undated**. Bispo rarely dated his work, and a date embroidered inside a narrative—for example 22 or 24 December 1938—is not necessarily a manufacture date. It records the event narrated.

The strongest material and documentary reconstruction places most of the surviving production in the Colônia Juliano Moreira, especially during Bispo’s residence in the connected former punishment cells of the Ulisses Viana Pavilion after his return in 1964. Conservation research describes the principal period of production as approximately **1964–1989**, while acknowledging earlier creative activity and intermittent institutional residence.

The manufacturing location is therefore securely the Colônia for the examined standards, but the year or sequence of each one remains unresolved. *Avenida Rosangela Maria* probably postdates Bispo’s encounters with Rosangela Magalhães around 1981; that is a plausible terminus derived from subject matter, not an inscribed or scientifically determined date.

### 2.2 Materials’ provenance

The banner grounds came from dirty, discarded, damaged, or used sheets collected inside the psychiatric institution. Some had probably been used by patients before Bispo incorporated them. The thread had several possible sources:

- unravelled blue institutional uniforms;
- unravelled sheets or other textiles;
- thread supplied or purchased through patients, employees, visitors, and families;
- miscellaneous sewing materials available within the Colônia.

The popular formula that all of Bispo’s thread came from unravelled uniforms is too absolute. The museum describes that source as a theory for the characteristic blue thread, while conservation and oral evidence show a broader supply network.

Bispo also received money or materials in exchange for helping other patients. Families and employees reportedly brought or purchased requested supplies. There is no surviving commission, purchase invoice, or documented cost for an individual banner.

### 2.3 Method of manufacture

Material evidence supports the following sequence:

1. Select already-used sheet pieces with varying color, weave, and condition.
2. Join fragments by hand to create two rectangular faces.
3. Patch losses or reinforce weakened areas, sometimes adding extra cloth behind dense embroidery.
4. Embroider words, lists, figures, maps, or symbols over the assembled ground, including across seams.
5. Add written or drawn marks in graphite, ballpoint, felt-tip pen, or paint where required.
6. Sew the two worked faces around their edges.
7. Attach the upper edge to a cylindrical wooden rod with loops or strips.
8. Cover or embroider parts of the rod; where desired, connect it to a longer pole so the banner could be raised.

No evidence of machine sewing by Bispo was identified in the cited technical study.

### 2.4 Earliest attestations

Several evidentiary moments must be separated:

- **1943 photographic claim:** a later Americas Society catalogue captions an image of Bispo wearing the *Manto da Apresentação* as published in *O Cruzeiro* in 1943. This attests textile production early in his institutional life, but it does not securely identify one of the present banner objects.
- **18 May 1980:** Samuel Wainer Filho’s *Fantástico* television report recorded Bispo and his work at the Colônia. Frederico Morais stated that he first learned of Bispo from this report.
- **1982:** Hugo Denizart filmed Bispo, his environment, and works for *O prisioneiro da passagem*. This is primary audiovisual evidence of the ensemble and Bispo’s own explanation of his mission.
- **25 July 1982:** fifteen banners entered *À margem da vida* at MAM Rio. This is the first securely documented public museum display of the banner corpus outside the Colônia.
- **1985:** Walter Firmo photographed Bispo handling and raising a banner at the Colônia.
- **13 April 1989:** *O Globo* published “Os estandartes da ‘lucidez’ de Bispo do Rosário,” reporting moisture damage and attributing to Bispo a request for proper storage.

### 2.5 Spiritual origin, distinguished from physical manufacture

**Artist’s testimony:** Bispo described a revelation at midnight on 22 December 1938 in which seven angels accompanied or deposited him at the house at Rua São Clemente 301. By 24 December he had reached the Monastery of São Bento and announced himself as the one who had come to judge the living and the dead. Police took him to the Hospital Nacional de Alienados; he was subsequently transferred to the Colônia Juliano Moreira.

He later explained that voices directed his work and that his mission was to represent or reconstruct everything existing on Earth so that it could be presented at the final judgment. The banners were components of that mission: records, inventories, maps, and presentations rather than, in his own terminology, autonomous artworks.

This is the religious genealogy of the objects. It is documented that Bispo recounted and embroidered it. Whether the supernatural encounter occurred is not an art-historical or material claim susceptible to verification.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 Creation to 1982: Bispo’s custody inside the Colônia

During manufacture, Bispo exercised unusually strong practical control over the ensemble despite his legal and institutional status as a psychiatric patient. By the 1980s he occupied a group of connected former isolation cells in the Ulisses Viana Pavilion. He possessed the key, restricted entry, and reportedly admitted visitors according to whether they correctly identified the color of his aura.

The banners and other productions were both made and stored there. Finished objects and potential raw material were not separated according to museum categories. Hugo Denizart remembered that the arrangement changed between visits; banners and other objects formed a continually revised environment.

The objects were exposed to dirt, dust, humidity, roof leaks, insects, handling, and deterioration. Bispo nonetheless undertook repairs and expressed preservation concerns. In Fernando Gabeira’s 1985 film he discussed cleaning dust with a toothbrush and making protective covers. The April 1989 newspaper report attributed to him the complaint that leaks were wetting the works and the request for a large place where people could see them before they were destroyed.

### 3.2 The 1982 loan to MAM Rio

Fifteen banners left the Colônia for *À margem da vida*, opened at the Museu de Arte Moderna do Rio de Janeiro on 25 July 1982 under Frederico Morais. Maria Amélia Lopes Mattei and Hugo Denizart helped facilitate the inclusion. This was a temporary exhibition movement, not a sale or permanent alienation.

One surviving catalogue illustration reproduced a detail of *Colônia Juliano Moreira / Reconheceram o filho de Deus*, establishing that MBRAC.ABR.0006 was among or was directly associated with the 1982 selection. Accessible sources do not name the other fourteen with equal certainty.

After the exhibition the banners returned to Bispo’s environment. He subsequently refused Morais’s proposal for a larger solo exhibition at MAM Rio, even after being offered a room in which he could live beside the works. Bispo said that the objects were records and that he could not separate from them. *À margem da vida* remained his only documented art exhibition during his lifetime.

### 3.3 1989: death, emergency removal, and institutional custody

Bispo died at the Colônia on 5 July 1989. The director, Izabel do Carmo Torres da Silva, had already convened discussions about the collection’s future because of his age, illness, and the works’ vulnerability.

Soon after his death, a group resolved to create the Associação de Amigos dos Artistas da Colônia Juliano Moreira. Its successive presidents included Denise Correia, Frederico Morais, and Gerardo Vilaseca. Its initial objectives were inventory, photography, cleaning, restoration, exhibitions, publications, and the identification of suitable storage.

The first protective act was to remove the objects from the Ulisses Viana cells to a room beside the Colônia’s administrative directorate. Morais justified the move as protection from dismantling or appropriation by patients and employees who might reuse practical components. This was preventative action against feared loss; the cited record does not document a specific banner theft or name an offender.

The removal:

- secured the individual objects;
- ended Bispo’s personal custody;
- destroyed or obscured the changing spatial arrangement;
- made it difficult to distinguish completed works from stored materials;
- introduced uncertainty about loose or unfixed components.

The former Museu Nise da Silveira at the Colônia thereafter housed the ensemble. No deed of sale, private inheritance, or transfer to a commercial collector appears in the consulted record. The works remained in public institutional custody on the Colônia grounds.

### 3.4 1989–1991 exhibition circuit

A major posthumous exhibition, *Registros de minha passagem pela Terra*, opened at the Escola de Artes Visuais do Parque Lage in Rio on 18 October 1989 according to the present museum catalogue; a later conservation thesis gives 18 November, illustrating a one-month discrepancy in secondary literature. The museum catalogue is preferred for the day, while the conflict should remain visible.

About 500 works were displayed. The exhibition then traveled to:

- Museu de Arte Contemporânea da Universidade de São Paulo, opening 8 March 1990;
- Museu de Arte do Rio Grande do Sul, Porto Alegre, 16 June 1990;
- Museu de Arte de Belo Horizonte, July 1990;
- Centro de Criatividade de Curitiba, 12 September 1990.

Individual online records establish that *Eu preciso destas palavras escrita* traveled with this circuit. Accessible records do not prove that every banner accompanied every venue.

In April 1991, approximately 430 works went to Kulturhuset in Stockholm as part of *Viva Brasil Viva*, in what became Bispo’s first international exhibition. *Eu preciso destas palavras escrita* is individually recorded there.

### 3.5 Cataloguing, cleaning, and naming in 1991

The Stockholm project prompted systematic documentation and cleaning. Frederico Morais and Gerardo Vilaseca catalogued **802 works** and devised practical categories and titles. Claudia Nunes carried out conservation work, including the documented washing of the *Manto da Apresentação*. The consulted source does not say that a banner was washed, so that intervention must not be generalized.

This was a decisive identity event. Objects that Bispo had regarded as elements of a single mission were assigned discrete titles, catalogue identities, classes, and exhibition functions. Later catalogue numbers such as MBRAC.ABR.0001–0007 embody this museological transformation.

### 3.6 Heritage protection, 1992–1994

Paulo José Pardal sought state heritage listing through INEPAC. Provisional approval came in November 1992 and unanimous definitive approval on 26 April 1994. The listing covered the ensemble of 802 identified works, including the banners. It did not transfer them to a new private owner; it imposed preservation controls on the collection.

### 3.7 1993–2003 movements

Known movements of the collection or named banners include:

- MAM Rio, *Arthur Bispo do Rosário: o inventário do universo*, 19 January 1993;
- Sala Athos Bulcão, Teatro Nacional, Brasília, 27 May 1993;
- Pavilhão Ulisses Viana, Colônia Juliano Moreira, *Arthur Bispo na Colônia*, June 1993;
- Instituto Brasil–Estados Unidos, Rio, 1994;
- 46th Venice Biennale, June 1995;
- Museu Nise da Silveira, *Eu preciso destas palavras. Escrita*, December 1996;
- Caixa venues in Brasília and Rio, 1998–99;
- *Brasil + 500: Mostra do Redescobrimento*, São Paulo, 2000;
- Guggenheim Museum, New York, *Brazil: Body and Soul*, 2001;
- Galerie nationale du Jeu de Paume, Paris, a Bispo solo exhibition in 2003.

The catalogue for MBRAC.ABR.0001 individually confirms its presence at Venice, the Guggenheim, and Jeu de Paume. General statements that “Bispo’s work” traveled cannot be converted into proof that every extant banner traveled.

### 3.8 1995 conservation and the Venice loan

Fundação Bienal archival material includes a May 1995 report documenting conservation and restoration before works were shipped to Italy for the 46th Venice Biennale. The foundation sponsored the treatment. The publicly summarized report does not enumerate every treated banner or describe each intervention.

A later direct examination of *Colônia Juliano Moreira* identified non-original tulle, an adhered textile repair, an edge completion, and added wooden slats. The tulle was absent from earlier photographs and is securely a post-production conservation addition. The exact campaign in which each addition was made remains unestablished; it should not automatically be assigned to May 1995.

### 3.9 Museum succession, 2000–present

The former museum unit at the Colônia was renamed Museu Bispo do Rosario in 2000 and Museu Bispo do Rosario Arte Contemporânea in 2002. These were institutional name changes, not sales or breaks in custody. The collection remained at the former Colônia, now within the municipal health complex.

In 2017 the museum began **Projeto Inventário do Mundo**, involving renewed cataloguing, cleaning, storage reorganization, and conservation. The entire collection underwent an anoxic treatment intended to eliminate termite infestation, and the storage areas were renovated. A conservation laboratory was established in 2020.

IPHAN’s Consultative Council unanimously approved federal listing on 19 September 2018. Definitive protection took effect through inscription in 2022 and was publicly announced in April–May 2023:

- listing no. 1842-T-18;
- administrative proceeding no. 01450.001794/2018-21;
- *Livro do Tombo das Belas Artes*, volume 2, folio 94;
- inscription no. 750.

IPHAN describes the protected collection as **805 pieces**, whereas the 1991 and INEPAC inventory comprised 802. The difference reflects subsequent inventory practice or the definition of units, not three documented acquisitions. The accessible notice does not reconcile the discrepancy object by object.

### 3.10 Custody gaps and negative findings

No sale, inheritance, private collecting history, restitution demand, colonial seizure, wartime evacuation, theft, disappearance, or recovery of one of the identified banners was established.

The main provenance gaps are instead internal:

- individual manufacture dates are unknown;
- the complete membership of the 1982 group of fifteen remains unidentified in accessible sources;
- many later exhibition lists name the overall collection but not the particular banners loaned;
- dates and authorship of some physical conservation additions remain uncertain;
- the 1989 dismantling erased original spatial relationships;
- the shift from 802 to 805 catalogue units is not publicly itemized.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Are the museum banners the objects Bispo made?

There is strong continuity evidence:

- they remained in the cells under Bispo’s control;
- they were photographed and filmed there during his lifetime;
- selected banners traveled to MAM Rio in 1982 and returned;
- the ensemble was secured immediately after his death;
- it was catalogued as a body in 1991;
- it has remained in the Colônia museum’s custody;
- material examination identifies recurring construction patterns consistent across the corpus.

No rival claimant, alternative “true banner,” documented forgery, or substituted replica was found.

### 4.2 The central identity dispute: object versus environment

The difficult authenticity question is not conventional forgery but **what constitutes the work**. Bispo did not organize his production as the museum now does. He continually rearranged banners, textiles, found objects, raw materials, garments, and constructions in his cells. The posthumous division into 802 or 805 objects necessarily imposes boundaries he did not consistently state.

Two defensible identities coexist:

1. Each banner is a discrete, materially coherent textile object.
2. The individual object was once part of a larger, mobile, cumulative environment whose arrangement and conceptual unity have been lost.

The museum presentation preserves the first but cannot restore the second.

### 4.3 Titles and dates

Most titles are institutional attributions derived from inscriptions. They should not be presented as titles formally declared by Bispo. Likewise, “n.d.” is not a casual omission: manufacture dates cannot normally be inferred from dates mentioned in the embroidery.

*Eu preciso destas palavras escrita* records 22 December 1938, but the object was likely made later. *Uma obra tão importante que levou 1986 anos para ser escrita*, another textile work reproduced in the Americas Society catalogue, contains “1986” as part of its statement; such internal dates require interpretation rather than automatic acceptance as completion dates.

### 4.4 Conservation additions and continuity

Later treatments have not made the banners inauthentic, but they have created composite physical histories. In *Colônia Juliano Moreira*:

- the cylindrical rod is considered original;
- extra slats are post-production;
- tulle over joins is a conservation addition;
- at least one adhered repair differs from Bispo’s sewn patches;
- other patches and reinforcements are likely original artist interventions.

These distinctions were made through comparison with historical photographs, construction technique, and physical examination—not chemical dating. The conservator cautions that repair, wear, found-object history, and artistic construction can be visually ambiguous. Removing an apparently crude patch could therefore erase Bispo’s own intervention.

### 4.5 Scientific examination

No radiocarbon test, fiber microscopy report, dye chromatography, DNA analysis, dendrochronology, or forensic handwriting study was found. The most substantial technical investigation located was Pinto’s 2017 in-situ visual and constructional analysis with photographic documentation. It established recurring seams, supports, knots, patches, inscriptions, and stitch structures and identified some later interventions.

The absence of laboratory testing is not a serious obstacle to attribution because custody and photographic continuity are comparatively strong. It does limit precision about fiber species, manufacture of component cloth, pigment composition, and intervention chronology.

### 4.6 Authority to authenticate

Authority shifted over time:

- **Before 1989:** Bispo controlled inclusion in his material universe and decided what could leave.
- **1989–91:** the Colônia administration and Association of Friends secured and selected the objects.
- **1991 onward:** Morais, Vilaseca, conservators, and museum personnel established discrete titles and categories.
- **1992–94:** INEPAC gave the collection state heritage recognition.
- **2018–23:** IPHAN conferred federal recognition and legal protection.
- **Present:** the Museu Bispo do Rosario Arte Contemporânea maintains the catalogue raisonné and controls conservation, documentation, and loans.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Arthur Bispo do Rosario

Maker, assembler, embroiderer, writer, custodian, and ceremonial user, principally 1964–1989 for the extant corpus. He called the objects records and parts of a divinely mandated representation of the world, not “artworks” in the ordinary professional sense.

### Patients of the Colônia Juliano Moreira

Their worn sheets and uniforms supplied material. Some gave Bispo objects or received his assistance. They were also represented by name or image in the banners. Specific material contributions cannot generally be assigned to named patients.

### Colônia employees and patients’ families

They provided, sold, or exchanged materials and, after Bispo’s death, were perceived as potential reusers of functional components. That prospective risk must not be turned into an allegation that any named person stole a banner.

### Rosangela Maria Grilo Magalhães

Psychology trainee who visited Bispo periodically around 1981. Her name entered numerous works, including *Avenida Rosangela Maria*. She did not own or commission the banner.

### Hugo Denizart

### God, Christ, angels, and the dead

In Bispo’s own religious system, God was the eventual recipient or judge of the reconstructed world; Bispo identified himself in Christic or messianic terms; seven angels attended the 1938 revelation; and the mission concerned judgment of the living and dead. These beings belong to the objects’ documented symbolic system. Their supernatural agency is a belief claim, not a verified provenance link.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Inventory for judgment

The banners catalogue the world: nations, Brazilian states and municipalities, human anatomy, acquaintances, hospital residents, ships, naval hierarchies, institutional architecture, roads, religious revelation, and linguistic fragments. Bispo described his overall task as representing all existing material for presentation at judgment.

Their function was therefore simultaneously:

- mnemonic;
- classificatory;
- testimonial;
- cartographic;
- nominative;
- prophetic or eschatological;
- ceremonial.

### 6.2 Raising and display

Historical photographs show Bispo raising at least one banner on a bamboo extension. This is direct evidence that the upper wooden supports enabled handling like ceremonial standards. Photographs also show banners raised inside the cell environment.

No documentary evidence establishes a fixed liturgical calendar, public procession, formal congregation, or repeated rite in which all the banners were carried. Claims that they were used in conventional religious processions would exceed the evidence.

### 6.3 Possible cultural precedents

Scholars have compared the banners with standards that open northeastern Brazilian *reisado* or Epiphany processions and with the embroidered ceremonial culture of Japaratuba. Such connections are plausible in form and regional memory, but there is no documentary proof that a particular childhood banner served as a direct model.

Naval flags and signaling are firmer biographical precedents for *Navios de guerra*: Bispo served as a signalman and filled the textile with signal codes, fleet groupings, and commands. Even here, the object is not a copied service flag but a reconstructed maritime memory.

### 6.4 Access restrictions and aura

Bispo kept the key to his cells and subjected visitors to the question “What is the color of my aura?” Only those who gave an acceptable answer entered. This was an attested access rule governing the objects’ original environment.

The rule had practical and symbolic effects:

- it protected materials in an institution where loss was possible;
- it preserved Bispo’s control over his work;
- it separated the cell complex from the surrounding hospital;
- it contributed to later descriptions of the space as a temple or sacred environment.

The precise “correct” color was not shown to be universal; it appears to have depended on Bispo’s judgment in each encounter.

### 6.5 Political and institutional function after 1989

Posthumously the banners acquired roles Bispo did not authorize in conventional terms:

- evidence for the creative agency of psychiatric patients;
- symbols of Brazil’s anti-asylum and psychiatric-reform histories;
- canonical works of Brazilian contemporary art;
- representations of Black and Afro-Brazilian cultural experience;
- state- and federally protected cultural patrimony;
- emblems for the museum bearing his name.

These functions are historically consequential even though they differ from Bispo’s stated eschatological purpose.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The seven angels

**Tier: artist’s recorded account, later institutional retelling.**

Bispo said that seven angels appeared at midnight on 22 December 1938 and accompanied or deposited him at the Botafogo house. The fullest early material account presently available is not an external witness statement but the embroidery on *Eu preciso destas palavras escrita*. The story later became the standard institutional origin narrative for his entire production.

What is established:

- Bispo embroidered and orally recounted the event.
- He left the Leone household, moved through Rio, appeared at São Bento, and entered police and psychiatric custody.
- The revelation became foundational to his work.

What is not established:

- independent observation of the angels;
- that any surviving banner was physically begun that night;
- a continuous production chronology from December 1938 onward.

### 7.2 “Recognition of the Son of Man”

**Tier: embroidered claim.**

*Colônia Juliano Moreira / Reconheceram o filho de Deus* states that named people recognized the “Son of Man” on 24 December 1938. The listed hospital and Ulisses Viana names may memorialize later institutional associates rather than literal witnesses present on that date. The surface collapses autobiographical time; it should not be read as a contemporaneous signed affidavit.

### 7.3 Reconstructing everything on Earth

**Tier: repeatedly recorded artist’s doctrine.**

Bispo held that he had to represent the world’s existing materials for divine presentation. Later critics frequently compare the ensemble to Noah’s Ark. The ark analogy is interpretive: Bispo built many ships and sought comprehensive preservation, but no evidence shows that he formally titled the corpus “Noah’s Ark.”

### 7.4 “Threads stolen from uniforms”

**Tier: partly documented, popularly exaggerated.**

Bispo did obtain thread by unraveling institutional uniforms. Popular accounts sometimes convert this into the claim that every blue stitch came from uniforms he stole. The sources establish reuse and institutional acquisition, not that all thread had one origin or that the materials were necessarily stolen. Some were found, exchanged, bought, or given.

### 7.5 Prophecy of international travel

A 1982 catalogue text recalled a psychologist saying Bispo was “delirious” in claiming that his material would go to Paris; Denizart responded that the material indeed would go to Paris. The works later reached Paris, most prominently at Jeu de Paume in 2003. This coincidence has the structure of a fulfilled prophecy in later retellings, but the contemporary remark arose amid real curatorial plans and advocacy. It is not evidence of supernatural foreknowledge.

### 7.6 The isolated, wholly uninfluenced genius

**Tier: modern critical myth under revision.**

Bispo was not formally trained in an art academy and no evidence links his work to occupational-therapy instruction by Nise da Silveira. Nevertheless, he was not culturally empty or socially detached. His work contains naval training, boxing, newspapers, Catholic language, carnival figures, institutional architecture, northeastern textile practice, commerce, personal relations, and the labor of other Colônia residents. Presenting him as a creation outside all culture erases the networks materially embedded in the banners.

## 8. Current Status and Location

The identified banners belong to or are safeguarded as part of the **Coleção Museu Bispo do Rosario Arte Contemporânea**, at:

Museu Bispo do Rosario Arte Contemporânea  
Complexo Instituto Municipal de Assistência à Saúde Juliano Moreira  
Estrada Rodrigues Caldas 3400  
Taquara/Jacarepaguá, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil.

The museum reports public hours Tuesday through Saturday, 9:00–17:00, but that does not mean every banner is continuously displayed. The collection circulates through temporary exhibitions and otherwise occupies controlled storage. No source consulted confirms which banner is on gallery display on 6 September 2026; the precise display status must therefore be recorded as **unverified**.

The collection is protected under:

- definitive INEPAC state listing, 26 April 1994;
- federal IPHAN listing approved in 2018 and definitively inscribed in 2022;
- IPHAN inscription no. 750 in the *Livro do Tombo das Belas Artes*.

Federal listing restricts destruction, mutilation, unauthorized alteration, and unregulated removal under Brazil’s heritage regime. The Museu Bispo do Rosario controls conservation and loans; IPHAN exercises heritage oversight.

Current physical concerns include:

- naturally aged and mechanically weakened sheet fibers;
- earlier moisture damage;
- yellowing and staining;
- potential detachment at joins and supports;
- ambiguity between intentional construction, original repair, wear, and later treatment;
- risks from repeated travel and suspension;
- the altered plastic and mixed materials around some rods.

The museum’s 2017 project cleaned, catalogued, treated infestation through an anoxic environment, and upgraded storage. No current repatriation, title, or rival-ownership litigation concerning the banners was located.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Transformation into modern art

The decisive reception event was *À margem da vida* in 1982. MAM Rio displayed the banners beside work by prisoners, institutionalized children, older asylum residents, and psychiatric patients. The exhibition framed social marginality as its organizing condition. Bispo did not become a professional art-world participant: he refused the later solo proposal and continued to deny that his records were art.

After his death the objects were rapidly reframed as Brazilian contemporary art:

- the approximately 500-work retrospective of 1989–90;
- the 430-work Stockholm presentation of 1991;
- MAM Rio in 1993;
- Venice in 1995;
- *Brazil: Body and Soul* at the Guggenheim in 2001;
- Jeu de Paume in 2003;
- the 30th São Paulo Biennial in 2012;
- the 55th Venice Biennale in 2013;
- *The Keeper* at New Museum in 2016;
- *Histórias Afro-Atlânticas* at MASP in 2018;
- the first United States solo exhibition, *All Existing Materials on Earth*, at Americas Society in 2023.

*Eu preciso destas palavras escrita* is documented at many of these venues and became one of the most frequently reproduced banners.

### 9.2 “Outsider art,” *art brut*, and contemporary art

Bispo has repeatedly been classified as an outsider or *art brut* artist. That label explains his exclusion from conventional training and institutions but can obscure his cultural knowledge and the institutional conditions of production. Other curators place him directly in contemporary art, comparing his inventories, found objects, seriality, and language with Marcel Duchamp, conceptual art, accumulation, and installation.

The strongest evidence cautions against claiming direct artistic influence from Duchamp: the category “objetos duchampianos” was invented by cataloguers after Bispo’s death. Similarity does not demonstrate that Bispo studied Duchamp.

### 9.3 Race, institutional violence, and anti-asylum memory

Recent exhibitions increasingly identify Bispo as an Afro-Brazilian artist and locate the banners within histories of race, forced confinement, colonial classification, and Black Atlantic movement. Earlier criticism often universalized his “madness” or religious vision while underplaying race and the material economy of the asylum.

The present museum’s curatorial writing explicitly treats this change as an ethical issue: aesthetic recognition should not erase psychiatric violence, racialization, the labor of other institutionalized people, or the fact that Bispo’s sheets and uniforms came from a coercive institution.

### 9.4 Film, photography, performance, and literature

Major works shaping public understanding include:

- Hugo Denizart, *O prisioneiro da passagem* (1982), the essential filmed interview and cell-environment record;
- Fernando Gabeira, *O Bispo* (mid-1980s), documenting Bispo’s handling and conservation concerns;
- Walter Firmo’s 1985 photographic essay;
- Helena Rocha and Miguel Przewodowski, *O Bispo do Rosário* (1993);
- Geraldo Motta and Gisella Mello, *O senhor do labirinto* (released around 2010);
- Luciana Hidalgo, *Arthur Bispo do Rosário: O senhor do labirinto*;
- Arrigo Barnabé, *Missa In Memoriam Arthur Bispo do Rosario* (created for *Ordenação e vertigem* in 2003);
- Jadiel Alves, *A Cor do Revés*, a dance work inspired by Bispo’s life and production.

These works popularized the messianic biography and often made the *Manto da Apresentação* the single emblem of the oeuvre. The banners’ own histories can consequently be overshadowed by the mantle.

### 9.5 Carnival and ceremonial afterlife

Bispo and the imagery of banners, robes, ships, and inventory entered samba-school culture. Schools including Renascer de Jacarepaguá, Império Serrano, and Acadêmicos de Niterói have produced Bispo-related parade themes. These are modern receptions, not continuations of a documented rite led by Bispo.

### 9.6 Replicas, forgeries, and market status

Photographic reproductions, educational banners, costumes inspired by the mantle, and exhibition graphics circulate widely. No authenticated full-scale replica with an independent custody history was identified, and no documented forged Bispo banner was located.

The museum corpus has not followed a conventional auction-market provenance. Its legal protection and institutional custody sharply distinguish it from privately circulating modern artworks. No reliable auction record for one of the identified estandartes was found.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Foundational documentation

The principal primary and near-primary sources are:

- Bispo’s embroidered inscriptions;
- hospital and psychiatric records;
- the 1980 *Fantástico* report;
- Denizart’s 1982 film and exhibition catalogue;
- Walter Firmo’s 1985 photographs;
- Gabeira’s film;
- the April 1989 *O Globo* report;
- the 1989 *Registros de minha passagem pela Terra* catalogue;
- the 1991 inventory;
- INEPAC and IPHAN listing records;
- museum accession files, photographs, and exhibition histories.

### 10.2 Major interpretive phases

**Psychiatric/institutional phase:** The works first existed within an asylum and were legible to outsiders as patient handiwork or symptomatic production.

**Art-critical phase:** Morais and colleagues reframed the objects as a major artistic corpus, compared them to contemporary art, assigned categories, and organized national and international exhibitions.

**Preservation phase:** Cataloguing and cleaning in 1991, conservation before Venice in 1995, and later technical study shifted attention toward material continuity.

**Ethical, racial, and decolonial phase:** Recent museum work foregrounds Bispo’s Black identity, institutional coercion, diasporic and northeastern cultural references, and the danger of turning his biography into a romantic myth of madness.

### 10.3 Key disputes

#### When were the banners made?

Unresolved individually. The broad period 1964–89 is likely for much of the corpus; subject matter can provide approximate termini, but internal narrative dates do not prove manufacture dates.

#### Did Bispo make “art”?

Bispo rejected the label and called the works records serving a mission. Museums regard them as art. This is not a binary authenticity test: an object may enter art history without having been created under the professional identity “artist.”

#### Are the banners autonomous works?

Museological practice says yes. Installation-oriented scholarship argues that their original meaning also depended on the entire cell environment and its continual rearrangement. Both claims describe different historical states.

#### Do the banners derive from *reisado*, naval flags, or asylum materials?

All are relevant, but at different evidentiary levels. Naval knowledge and institutional materials are documented. Direct derivation from childhood *reisado* standards is a plausible cultural inference.

#### Was Bispo influenced by Duchamp?

Formal comparison is longstanding; direct documentary influence is unproven. “Duchampian objects” is a posthumous curatorial classification.

#### 802, 805, or more than 1,000 works?

- 802: 1991 inventory and INEPAC listing.
- 805: current IPHAN-protected collection.
- “More than 1,000”: looser exhibition and journalistic counting, sometimes treating subcomponents separately.

These figures concern cataloguing units, not necessarily physical growth or disappearance of the collection.

#### Did the museum save or dismember the work?

The emergency removal protected vulnerable objects from reuse and environmental damage. It simultaneously dismantled a changing total environment and lost spatial information. Preservation and dismemberment occurred through the same act.

### 10.4 Conservation historiography

Pinto’s 2017 research materially revised simplistic assumptions about the banners. It showed that:

- discoloration may predate artistic use;
- apparently crude patches may be deliberate artist repairs;
- seams can organize imagery;
- embroidery crossing seams helps reconstruct working order;
- later museum stabilizations are identifiable through photographic comparison;
- supports and attachment devices have artistic and performative significance;
- conservation must preserve both “damage” and reused material history when those features belong to the work.

No comprehensive published object-by-object conservation dossier for every banner was located. Museum accession and treatment files may contain further information not yet publicly digitized.

## 12. Sources

### Primary, institutional, and official sources

The most authoritative object-level source is the Museu Bispo do Rosario’s digital catalogue raisonné. It provides accession numbers, materials, measurements, transcriptions, technical photographs, exhibition histories, and current institutional terminology.

The IPHAN notice and Diário Oficial listing establish the legal status of the 805-piece collection. The 89th Consultative Council record supplies the institutional reasoning and 2018 approval history.

The Museu Bispo do Rosario’s museum history and chronology document the succession from Museu Nise da Silveira to the present institution, conservation projects, anoxic pest treatment, exhibitions, and collection location.

The Fundação Bienal’s archival dossier confirms the 1995 conservation campaign and Venice shipment.

### Technical and scholarly sources

Vanessa Magalhães Pinto’s 2017 UFMG dissertation is the principal publicly accessible material study. Its findings rest on in-situ examination and photographic documentation and are especially valuable for separating artist repairs from later interventions.

The 2023 Americas Society catalogue provides an English-language exhibition history, object records, translations, and bibliography. Its framing as the first US solo presentation is corroborated by the institution’s announcement.

Kaira M. Cabañas’s study of Bispo’s contemporaneity examines the curatorial transition from asylum production to contemporary art and the consequences of the 1982 exhibition.

The recent catalogue raisonné’s bibliography consolidates dissertations, exhibition catalogues, press coverage, and critical literature, but many early accession and treatment files remain accessible only through the museum archive rather than full public digitization.

### Research limitations

A genuinely exhaustive chain for every banner would require consultation of:

- the 1982 MAM Rio loan list;
- the 1989 cell-removal inventory and photographs;
- the complete 1991 object inventory;
- INEPAC’s full listing file;
- the May 1995 conservation report and treatment sheets;
- object-specific outgoing-loan forms;
- condition reports before and after each international exhibition;
- the museum’s accession and conservation databases;
- IPHAN proceeding 01450.001794/2018-21 in full.

Those materials were referenced but not all were available in complete searchable form. Consequently, this dossier distinguishes known collection-level movement from individually proven banner travel.
