# The Sword of Saint Catherine (Joan of Arc's sword): Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `the_sword_of_saint_catherine_joan_of_arcs_sword_object_dossier.md`

## Evidentiary note

The object discussed here is not an extant authenticated relic. A real sword is strongly attested in contemporary and near-contemporary records: in 1429 Joan of Arc obtained an old, rusted sword from the church of Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois; in 1431 she described it under interrogation. Its manufacture, maker, precise form, and fate after 1429 are unknown.

Three things must be kept distinct:

1. **The historical Fierbois sword:** the sword Joan said was found at Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois and which she carried in 1429.
2. **Later legendary identities:** especially the claim that it had belonged to Charles Martel after the battle conventionally dated to 732.
3. **Later candidate or substitute objects:** notably an elaborate composite sword in the Musée des Beaux-Arts de Dijon and a sword formerly attributed to Joan in the treasury of Saint-Denis. Neither has a documented provenance connecting it to the Fierbois sword.

The principal first-person evidence survives in the record of Joan’s 1431 trial. The interrogations took place in French, were registered by notaries, and were subsequently transmitted principally through authenticated Latin copies. BnF Latin 5965 is a fifteenth-century authenticated exemplar collated against the original register and subscribed by three apostolic notaries; its digital facsimile is available through Gallica. Fragments of the French minute also survive through the d’Urfé manuscript tradition. Pierre Champion’s 1920–21 critical edition printed the Latin text with French translation and notes. [Biblissima/BnF manuscript record](https://portail.biblissima.fr/en/ark%3A/43093/mdata6bfa0798b14bd659ef6deab9a36e27bc320c5b94), [Champion critical notice](https://jeanne.wiki/champion-condamnation/tome1/notice), [BnF catalogue record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark%3A/12148/cb35346490r).

Evidence labels used below are:

- **Documented:** stated in contemporary administrative, judicial, or eyewitness evidence.
- **Contemporary allegation:** asserted by Joan’s prosecutors but not independently proved.
- **Retrospective testimony:** recollection given in the 1450–56 investigations, often more than twenty-five years later.
- **Scholarly inference:** reconstruction from the evidence, not an attested fact.
- **Institutional or local tradition:** a claim maintained by a church, museum, locality, or custodian without a continuous documentary chain.
- **Legend or modern invention:** a story not found in the early evidence and demonstrably appearing substantially later.
- **Unresolved:** the surviving evidence does not decide the question.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity and names

The most accurate designation is **the sword from Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois carried by Joan of Arc**.

Recorded and conventional names include:

- Middle French: **l’espée de Sainte-Catherine de Fierbois** or **l’espée trouvée à Sainte-Catherine de Fierbois**.
- Modern French: **l’épée de Fierbois**, **l’épée de Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois**, **l’épée de Jeanne d’Arc**.
- English: **the Fierbois sword**, **Joan of Arc’s sword**, and **the Sword of Saint Catherine**.
- Legendary designation: **l’épée de Charles Martel**—a late attribution, not part of Joan’s recorded description.

“Sword of Saint Catherine” can mislead: no early source says the sword belonged personally to Catherine of Alexandria, was used in her martyrdom, or contained her relics. It was named through its place of discovery, the church dedicated to her at Fierbois.

The place-name appears in late-medieval forms such as *Fierbois* and *Fierboys*. The frequently repeated derivation from Latin *ferus boscus* or *ferus locus*, “wild wood,” is a traditional antiquarian etymology, not a securely documented medieval derivation. The sanctuary’s miracle collection locates “Madame sainte Katherine appelée Fierbois” in the parish of Sainte-Maure, diocese of Tours. The relevant manuscript, BnF Français 1045, was compiled between approximately 1470 and 1483 from accounts dated 1375–1470. [IRHT/JONAS manuscript record](https://jonas.irht.cnrs.fr/consulter/oeuvre/detail_oeuvre.php?oeuvre=10357).

### What the primary evidence says it looked like

Joan’s testimony on 27 February 1431 supplies nearly the entire reliable physical description:

- It was an **épée**, a sword, rather than a dagger or ceremonial object.
- It was found **in the earth**, not deeply buried.
- It was **rusted**.
- It bore **five crosses**.
- Clergy at Fierbois rubbed or cleaned it; Joan said the rust then came away “without effort.”
- It received three scabbards:
  - one of **crimson/red velvet**;
  - one of **cloth of gold**;
  - one of **strong leather**, commissioned by Joan herself.

The critical point is that the trial does not record:

- overall length;
- blade length or width;
- weight;
- number of edges;
- fuller or blade profile;
- crossguard and pommel forms;
- grip material;
- maker’s mark;
- metallurgical composition;
- inscriptions other than the five cross signs;
- precise arrangement or size of those crosses.

The sword was presumably iron or steel because it rusted, but no surviving sample exists for metallurgical confirmation. “Steel,” “tempered blade,” or a specific fifteenth-century typology should therefore be treated as inference, not observation.

The earliest testimony merely places five crosses “upon” the sword. Some later narratives locate them on the blade near the hilt; a late-medieval dramatic text relocates them to the *croisée*, which can mean the cross or guard. Assertions that they formed a Jerusalem cross, represented the Five Wounds, or were arranged quincuncially are later interpretations. Joan herself, asked on 17 March 1431 what they signified, answered that she did not know.

### Scabbards and fittings

The sumptuous velvet and cloth-of-gold scabbards were gifts associated with the clergy of Fierbois and people at Tours. The surviving record is ambiguous about precisely which community supplied which luxury scabbard, but Joan contrasted both with the strong leather scabbard she had made for practical service.

No scabbard has a documented survival. No belt, suspension furniture, guard, pommel, or detached blade fragment can now be securely assigned to the Fierbois sword.

### Present condition

**Historical sword:** lost; condition and survival unknown.

**Dijon candidate/substitute:** extant, but its museum does not authenticate it as Joan’s sword. The Musée des Beaux-Arts de Dijon catalogues it as *Épée dite de Jeanne d’Arc; Épée du temps de Charles VII*, inventory **CA 1489**. It is steel, 95 cm long and 16.3 cm wide across the guard. The blade bears royal and Orléans arms, the words **CHARLE SEPTIESME** and **VAUCOULEUR**, and the date **1419** repeated five times. It also incorporates the gold-damascened name of the Toledo swordsmith Lupus Aguado and a representation derived from the sixteenth-century Orléans monument to Joan.

Museum study concludes that the Dijon sword is composite: two fifteenth-century blade portions were welded together into an excessive total length, while its S-shaped guard, wire grip, and pommel are incoherent and partly nonfunctional. Its pommel does not fit medieval sword typologies and is more reminiscent of later Scottish weapons. It is therefore not a viable physical match for the plainly described working sword from Fierbois. [French Ministry of Culture, Joconde notice](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/joconde/01370033300).

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Manufacture

No contract, payment, named smith, workshop, mine, or date of manufacture is known. Because the sword was already rusted when recovered in 1429, it had been deposited or neglected before Joan received it. The degree of corrosion cannot date it.

The strongest contextual explanation is that it was one of the arms deposited at Fierbois as an ex-voto. The sanctuary served soldiers, prisoners, and travelers during the Hundred Years’ War. Its miracle book records gifts made in fulfillment of vows. In June 1428, for example, Jean du Chastel reportedly came to the shrine wearing armor taken from an English opponent after a judicial combat and gave that harness to the chapel “in perpetual memory.” This is the earliest surviving explicit notice of deposited arms at the sanctuary. Françoise Michaud-Fréjaville therefore regards a military votive context as historically plausible, while expressly declining to identify Joan’s sword with any particular offering. [Michaud-Fréjaville, “Sainte Catherine, Jeanne d’Arc et le saut de Beaurevoir”](https://journals.openedition.org/crm/386).

### The event of discovery

Joan passed through Fierbois before reaching Chinon in early March 1429 and said that she heard three masses there. Later, while at Tours or Chinon, she dictated a letter to the clergy asking that she be given a sword whose location and distinguishing crosses she said had been revealed by her “voices.”

Her trial account states that:

1. the sword lay in or under the earth, near the altar;
2. she thought it was behind the altar but admitted uncertainty whether it was in front or behind;
3. it was not deeply buried;
4. she had never seen the man sent to retrieve it;
5. a Tours arms merchant or armorer fetched it;
6. the clergy cleaned and sent it to her.

The dictated letter itself is lost. Its former existence is attested only through Joan’s testimony and later narrative references. The complete sequence can be read in Champion’s translation of the 27 February session. [Champion, 27 February 1431](https://jeanne.wiki/champion-condamnation/tome2/proces1).

### Variant early accounts

Early narrative traditions do not agree on the sword’s exact resting place:

- **Joan’s 1431 testimony:** shallowly in the earth near the altar, probably behind it.
- **Journal du siège d’Orléans / Chronique de la Pucelle tradition:** found among several swords given to the church and identified by its five crosses.
- **Guerneri Berni’s Italian account:** associated it with a knight’s tomb.
- **Jean Chartier:** placed it only at a “certain place” in the church.

These variants suggest that the discovery became a circulating wonder-story almost immediately. They do not establish deliberate fraud, nor do they establish supernatural revelation. [Comparative source notes](https://archive.joan-of-arc.org/joanofarc_letter_March-April_1429.html).

### Earliest secure attestation

The object itself appears to have entered Joan’s custody in March or April 1429. The earliest surviving fully checkable first-person account is her sworn interrogation of **27 February 1431**. Chronicles preserving versions of the story were written or compiled later, even where they drew on information circulating in 1429.

### The Charles Martel origin legend

No record contemporary with Charles Martel, no early medieval chronicle, and no pre-Joan inventory connects his sword with Fierbois. Joan did not call her sword Charles Martel’s. Neither the 1431 trial record nor Jean Chartier makes that identification.

Current scholarship places the explicit literary linkage very late. William Blanc and Christophe Naudin identify Jean Chapelain’s epic *La Pucelle, ou la France délivrée* (1656) as probably the first text explicitly to connect Joan’s sword with Charles Martel. Oral or local anticipation cannot be disproved, but it is undocumented. [Blanc and Naudin, *Charles Martel et la bataille de Poitiers* excerpt](https://atelmed.hypotheses.org/files/2015/11/martel_intro_chap_1.pdf).

The developed legend says that, after defeating an invading Muslim army in 732, Charles Martel pursued survivors into the Touraine woods, founded or restored a chapel of Saint Catherine, and deposited his sword there in thanksgiving. Seven centuries later, Providence directed Joan to the same weapon so that a second deliverer of France could wield it. This is a national-Christian typology joining two military heroes; it is not a documented chain of custody.

A further chronological problem is that the cult of Catherine of Alexandria in western Europe, and this specific sanctuary, are not documented at Fierbois in Martel’s lifetime. The sanctuary’s secure documentary horizon begins in the later Middle Ages, especially with a healing dated 1375 in its miracle collection.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Before 1429: unknown

The sword’s maker, original owner, manufacture date, and route to Fierbois are unrecorded.

Plausible possibilities include:

- an ex-voto deposited by a soldier or former prisoner;
- an obsolete weapon stored with other offerings;
- an object deliberately hidden during warfare;
- an ordinary church possession later interpreted as wondrous.

None can be selected conclusively.

### March–April 1429: church of Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois

The first reconstructable custody point is the church at Fierbois. Joan’s language indicates that the clergy controlled disposal of the object: she requested their consent, and they sent it to her. Thus the mechanism was effectively an ecclesiastical gift or authorized transfer, not theft or unilateral seizure.

The person who physically found it is unnamed. The man who carried or fetched it is described as a Tours arms merchant/armorer; Joan said she had never previously seen him.

### 1429: Joan’s custody

Joan testified that she carried the Fierbois sword continually from receiving it until her departure from Saint-Denis after the unsuccessful attack on Paris in September 1429. This places it, at minimum, in her campaigning equipment during:

- the march to Orléans;
- the relief of Orléans;
- the Loire campaign;
- the march to Reims;
- Charles VII’s coronation campaign;
- the advance toward Paris.

That does not prove she personally drew it in each battle. Her own explanation was that she preferred to carry her banner in assaults so as not to kill anyone, and that she had killed no one.

### Use against camp followers

Several witnesses remembered Joan driving prostitutes away from the army, sometimes with a drawn sword. Louis de Coutes and chroniclers preserved versions of such episodes at different places.

The Duke of Alençon testified retrospectively in 1456 that she once struck a prostitute with a sword so forcefully that the weapon broke, and that Charles VII was displeased, reportedly saying she should have taken a stick rather than risk a sword. The episode is frequently identified as the destruction of the Fierbois sword.

That identification is **disputed**:

- the testimony came more than twenty-five years later;
- accounts differ over place and date;
- Joan’s 1431 statement that she retained the Fierbois sword until leaving Saint-Denis can fit a breakage near Saint-Denis, but not versions placing the event at Gien or Auxerre earlier in 1429;
- her references to having the sword at Lagny complicate some chronologies;
- “broke” could mean blade, guard, or another sword altogether;
- no fragment is recorded.

The broken-on-a-prostitute episode is therefore retrospective evidence of possible damage, not a secure destruction record.

### Saint-Denis, September 1429

After being wounded in the failed assault on Paris, Joan deposited armor and a sword at Saint-Denis as a customary military ex-voto. At her trial she explicitly distinguished this offered sword from the Fierbois sword: the offering was an armor set and a sword “won before Paris.”

This distinction is essential. Later Saint-Denis tradition attributed a sword in the treasury to Joan, but that object—if it genuinely came from her offering—need not have been the Fierbois sword.

### After Saint-Denis

Joan said the Fierbois sword was no longer with her when she was captured at Compiègne on 23 May 1430. She carried another sword, taken from a Burgundian. Asked where the Fierbois sword was, she declined to say. She also stated generally that her brothers guarded her goods, including swords, horses, and other property.

Possible final documented transition:

- Joan → one or both brothers or household retainers, after September 1429.

This is not specific enough to establish which brother received the sword, where, or for how long.

### Capture at Compiègne

The weapon captured with Joan was **not**, by her testimony, the Fierbois sword. Consequently, Burgundian or English custody of Joan after May 1430 does not establish custody of this object.

### Saint-Denis treasury tradition

A sword later shown at Saint-Denis was said by monks to have belonged to Joan. Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century inventory descriptions also associated it with Charles VII. One reported form described a sword with two golden angels on the pommel, one with the sun and the other with the Virgin. Jacques Doublet wrote in 1625 that “the sword alone remained,” with a buff-leather belt whose rings, fittings, and buckles were gold, which he said he had often seen.

This object’s identity is unresolved:

- it may have been Joan’s documented votive sword captured before Paris;
- it may have been a royal sword later reassigned to Joan;
- it may have been a composite treasury attribution;
- it has no demonstrated connection to the five-cross Fierbois sword.

A visitor’s account still listed “the sword of Joan of Arc” in the treasury in the eighteenth century. Thereafter its chain disappears amid the dispersal and destruction of the Saint-Denis treasury during the Revolution. No surviving sword has been securely matched to it. The treasury’s history is documented through medieval and early-modern inventories, including the critical edition of the 1634 inventory. [Review of *Le trésor de Saint-Denis: inventaire de 1634*](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ccmed_0007-9731_1975_num_18_70_2003), [early-modern visitor description](https://curiositas.org/cabinet/curios629).

### Post-medieval gap

From the fifteenth century onward, there is no continuous record of the Fierbois sword itself. Any chain running from Joan to Saint-Denis, Lagny, a private family, the Revolution, or a modern museum necessarily contains an undocumented substitution or gap.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Did a physical Fierbois sword exist?

The strongest affirmative evidence is Joan’s detailed 1431 testimony, including mundane features—the clergy’s permission, an unnamed armorer, rust removal, and three scabbards—and the story’s independent circulation in fifteenth-century narrative sources.

This establishes beyond reasonable historical doubt that an object presented and used as the Fierbois sword existed. It does not establish:

- supernatural foreknowledge;
- ancient manufacture;
- ownership by Charles Martel;
- miraculous removal of corrosion;
- survival after 1429.

### Prosecutorial fraud allegation

Article 19 of the 1431 accusation alleged that Joan had consulted demons about the sword or had hidden it herself so that a staged discovery would persuade princes, clergy, and common people to believe her revelations. Joan denied the allegation and referred back to her account.

This was a **contemporary accusation**, not a proved finding based on witnesses to concealment. No evidence identifies an accomplice or shows Joan handling the sword before its discovery. [1431 accusation and testimony](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/basis/joanofarc-trial.asp).

### Miraculous rust removal

Joan said the clergy rubbed the sword and that its rust came away immediately and without effort. The wording permits at least two readings:

- devotional reading: extraordinary or miraculous cleansing;
- ordinary reading: loose surface rust was readily removed through cleaning.

No sample, conservation record, or independent technical description survives. The claim cannot be tested.

### Was the sword broken?

The Duke of Alençon’s later testimony is evidence that a remembered sword-breaking episode circulated among Joan’s companions. It does not securely identify the broken weapon.

Positions include:

- **Traditional identification:** Joan broke the Fierbois sword on a prostitute, ending its physical career.
- **Continuity objection:** some accounts place the break before dates on which Joan seems still to describe or carry the Fierbois weapon.
- **Alternative-object hypothesis:** she broke another sword.
- **Repair hypothesis:** the weapon was damaged and repaired.
- **Narrative-symbolic hypothesis:** later witnesses or writers connected an actual disciplinary incident to the famous sword because its breakage made a moralized ending.

No physical evidence decides among them.

### The Dijon sword

The Dijon object entered the municipal museum by purchase in **1826** from Joseph Bertholomey. It was cautiously catalogued in 1831 as a sword from the time of Charles VII. In the atmosphere surrounding Joan’s 1909 beatification, Étienne Metman proposed that it might have belonged to her. Subsequent study rejected that implication.

Evidence against identity with the Fierbois sword includes:

- no provenance before the nineteenth century connecting it to Joan;
- inscriptions referring to Vaucouleurs, Charles VII, Orléans, and 1419 that are absent from every early description;
- iconography copied from a sixteenth-century monument;
- a composite, partly nonfunctional construction;
- anachronistic or typologically incoherent hilt components;
- no match for the simple five-cross description.

Modern museum interpretation sees it more plausibly as a late-sixteenth-century object associated with Joan’s cult among Catholic Leaguers, assembled around older blade material. It is displayed and catalogued as an **épée dite de Jeanne d’Arc**—a sword “said to be” hers—not as an authenticated relic. [Joconde notice](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/joconde/01370033300).

### Other claimants

Claims have periodically attached Joan’s identity to swords in private collections, at Wandonne, Lagny, Saint-Denis, and elsewhere. None has a published continuous provenance, matching early description, and scientific examination sufficient to establish identity.

No candidate has undergone a publicly documented program combining:

- metallography;
- radiography;
- alloy and slag-inclusion study;
- dendrochronology or radiocarbon dating of organic hilt material;
- residue study;
- comparison with fifteenth-century votive weapons;
- continuous archival provenance research.

For the original, such testing is impossible until a credible candidate is found.

### Who could authenticate it?

Authority has shifted:

- **1429:** the clergy of Fierbois authenticated the discovered object pragmatically by finding a sword with the predicted signs.
- **1431:** the trial court treated its meaning as a theological and evidentiary issue.
- **1450–56:** witnesses authenticated memories of Joan’s use, not a surviving artifact.
- **Early modern period:** Saint-Denis monks and treasury cataloguers assigned identities through institutional tradition.
- **Modern period:** museums, arms historians, conservators, and laboratories require material and provenance evidence.

No church, state, museum, or laboratory currently recognizes an extant sword as the authenticated Fierbois weapon.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Votive context

Fierbois was a pilgrimage shrine especially frequented by soldiers, captives, fugitives, and their families during the Hundred Years’ War. Its recorded practices centered on vows, pilgrimage after deliverance, testimony before shrine custodians, and deposition of objects associated with danger or liberation. The sword’s likely prior status as a votive offering is plausible in this setting but not explicitly documented.

### Military function

The object was a wearable sword and therefore potentially functional. Joan’s use, however, was as much emblematic as martial:

- it linked her campaign to a revered female military saint;
- its discovery operated publicly as a “sign” validating her claims;
- its recovery preceded the relief of Orléans and coronation campaign;
- it marked her as an armed commander while her banner remained her preferred battlefield object.

Joan denied killing anyone and said she carried the banner during assaults to avoid doing so. That does not establish that the sword was blunt, unsharpened, or purely ceremonial.

### Discipline rather than combat

Reports of Joan chasing or striking prostitutes show swords being used for coercion and camp discipline. Even if the famous breakage involved a different sword, later tradition made the Fierbois weapon an instrument of moral policing.

### Blessing and magical fortune

At trial Joan said:

- she had **not** had the sword blessed;
- she did not know that it had previously been blessed;
- she had not placed it on an altar to make it more fortunate;
- she loved it because it came from Saint Catherine’s church.

The prosecution nevertheless alleged that the sword, banner, and ring were objects of superstition or magical manipulation. Her answers draw a distinction between devotional association and talismanic power.

### Coronation and legitimacy

No source establishes that the sword was used in Charles VII’s anointing or substituted for the coronation sword. Joan probably had it among her equipment during the Reims campaign, but the coronation’s liturgical sword was a separate regalian object.

Symbolically, however, the found sword supported the same program: divine favor, liberation of Orléans, and the lawful king’s consecration at Reims.

### Heraldic afterlife

Arms granted to Joan’s family show a sword upright, flanked by fleurs-de-lis and surmounted by a crown. Although often identified in later commentary as the Fierbois sword, the heraldic charge has no five visible crosses and is not a technical portrait of the object. It expresses royal service and military deliverance.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The divinely revealed sword

**Tier:** early attested claim.

Joan said her voices told her the sword’s location and identifying marks. The successful search was widely treated as evidence of revelation.

The historical core is strong: a sword was found in response to her instructions. What cannot be historically established is the source of her knowledge. Possibilities proposed over time include revelation, knowledge acquired during her earlier visit, information from shrine personnel or companions, coincidence among several votive swords, and fraud. Only the last was formally alleged at the trial, and it was not proved.

### Miraculous de-rusting

**Tier:** early attested report, later miracle elaboration.

Joan’s wording says that after the clergy rubbed it, the rust came away immediately and without effort. Later retellings commonly suppress the rubbing and say the rust fell away at Joan’s touch. The latter formulation is a substantive embellishment not present in her testimony.

### Charles Martel’s sword

**Tier:** late accretion, probably seventeenth-century literary invention or formalization.

Full legend:

1. Charles Martel defeated Muslim forces near Tours/Poitiers in 732.
2. He pursued them into the wild woods around Fierbois.
3. In thanksgiving he founded or restored a chapel to Catherine.
4. He deposited his victorious sword behind the altar.
5. The weapon remained hidden for approximately seven centuries.
6. Joan, another providential savior of France, recovered it and defeated the English.

The legend served several later interests:

- joining Joan to an older national savior;
- casting conflict with England through a crusading analogy;
- advertising Fierbois as an ancient national shrine;
- supporting nineteenth-century church restoration and pilgrimage fundraising;
- supplying visual artists with an archaeological miracle scene.

The early documents do not support the chain. Jean Chapelain’s 1656 poem is the earliest identified explicit literary linkage. The story was presented as established history in nineteenth-century restoration appeals—for example, an 1856 subscription notice declared that Martel deposited the sword and Joan recovered it seven centuries later. [1856 restoration appeal](https://archives.ville-saumur.fr/_depot_amsaumur/_depot_arko/fonds/echo_saumurois/pdf/1856/FRAC049328_ECHSAU_1856_04_26.pdf).

### The Five Wounds or Jerusalem-cross explanation

**Tier:** later symbolic interpretation.

The five crosses have been interpreted as:

- Christ’s Five Wounds;
- a Jerusalem cross;
- a crusader or military-order sign;
- five separate devotional marks;
- later confusion with the five fleurs-de-lis or sword of the d’Arc family arms.

No early witness describes their arrangement, and Joan denied knowing their meaning. These interpretations cannot authenticate a candidate sword.

### The sword broke because Joan’s mission had ended

**Tier:** later providential interpretation of retrospective testimony.

In moralized biographies, the blade breaks upon contact with a prostitute, or because Joan improperly used a sacred weapon, and the accident predicts the failure at Paris or her capture. The reported breakage may have a factual core; its interpretation as a divine withdrawal is literary and theological.

### Hidden at Lagny

**Tier:** local modern legend.

Lagny traditions propose that Joan left the damaged sword with the abbey of Saint-Pierre and that it was buried in a subterranean passage or sealed in a pillar of the chapel of Notre-Dame-des-Ardents. No medieval inventory or excavation report confirms this. The story attempts to bridge the documentary gap after September 1429 by giving the lost object a local resting place.

### Secret survival at Saint-Denis

**Tier:** early-modern institutional tradition with an uncertain object.

Saint-Denis demonstrably displayed a sword attributed to Joan. The unsupported step is identifying it with the Fierbois sword rather than her separately documented votive sword, a royal sword, or a later attribution. Its subsequent disappearance encouraged claims that it survived the Revolution in private or institutional custody; none has been established.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Historical object

**Status:** lost.

**Last secure location:** in Joan’s custody through the Saint-Denis stage of the 1429 campaign.

**Last secure documentary appearance:** Joan’s descriptions and refusals during the 1431 trial.

**Possible immediate custodian after Joan:** her brothers or household, but this is not tied to a place or dated transfer.

**Condition:** unknown; it may have been broken, repaired, hidden, recycled, deposited, or destroyed.

**Legal owner:** impossible to determine. The clergy of Fierbois voluntarily transferred it to Joan; there is no later title document, inheritance record, confiscation, or accession.

### Fierbois today

The church of Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois remains the site associated with the discovery, but the medieval chapel known to Joan was substantially replaced by the later church. No authenticated sword or fragment is preserved there. The site interprets and commemorates the tradition rather than housing the original object.

### Dijon sword

- **Institution:** Musée des Beaux-Arts de Dijon.
- **Inventory:** CA 1489.
- **Owner:** commune of Dijon.
- **Acquisition:** purchase, 1826.
- **Status:** extant museum object; not authenticated as Joan’s sword.
- **Interpretation:** composite commemorative or cult object incorporating older blade material.
- **Access:** subject to the museum’s current display rotation; the national catalogue records its custody, not a guarantee of permanent exhibition. [Joconde notice](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/joconde/01370033300).

### Saint-Denis sword

**Status:** lost or unidentified.

The sword shown in the early-modern treasury cannot presently be traced to a surviving accessioned object. The French Revolution provides the broad context for the treasury’s dispersal, but there is no item-specific record proving whether this particular sword was melted, stolen, destroyed, transferred, or miscatalogued.

### Repatriation and ownership disputes

No active legal restitution or repatriation proceeding concerning the authentic Fierbois sword can exist without an identified object. Current controversies concern attribution and cultural memory rather than title to a demonstrably surviving relic.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Nineteenth-century historicism

The sword became a major visual shorthand for Joan’s divine vocation during the nineteenth-century revival of her cult.

William Etty’s monumental painting **_Jeanne d’Arc trouve l’épée dans la chapelle de Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois_** (1846; oil on canvas, approximately 300 × 200 cm) treats the discovery as sacred revelation. It formed part of Etty’s Joan triptych, conceived around religion, loyalty, and patriotism and exhibited at the Royal Academy in 1847. After the triptych’s dispersal, the central panel reappeared and was acquired by the Musée des Beaux-Arts d’Orléans in 1982. [Musées d’Orléans program and collection account](https://museesorleans.fr/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/PRG-MUSEES-MARS-AOUT-25-web.pdf).

The Charles Martel identity also became useful in fundraising for restoration of the Fierbois church. The 1856 subscription appeal presented the complete seven-century continuity as fact, demonstrating how legend could materially affect the building associated with the lost sword.

### Beatification and canonization era

Joan’s beatification in 1909 and canonization in 1920 intensified searches for material relics. In this context, the Dijon composite sword was promoted as a possible personal weapon. Museum and arms-historical study later reduced the attribution from possibility to commemorative tradition.

### Replicas

Modern replicas generally choose one of three designs:

1. a plain late-medieval arming sword with five small crosses on the blade;
2. a sword bearing a Jerusalem-cross arrangement;
3. copies inspired by the elaborate Dijon object.

Because the original’s dimensions and hilt are unknown, no commercial or ceremonial replica is an archaeological facsimile. Claims of “exact reproduction” are marketing, not scholarship.

### Literature and drama

The sword participates in two distinct narrative traditions:

- trial-based drama, emphasizing Joan’s testimony about revelation and the interrogators’ suspicion of sorcery;
- romantic-national epic, identifying it with Charles Martel and treating it as a French equivalent of Excalibur, Joyeuse, or Durendal.

Jean Chapelain’s *La Pucelle* (1656) was particularly consequential because it appears to have fixed the Martel linkage in literary culture. Later popular biographies repeated it without identifying its late source.

### Film, television, comics, and games

Screen and illustrated retellings frequently show Joan discovering a pristine, shining sword herself. This differs from the primary account, in which unnamed churchmen conducted the search and cleaned the rusted weapon before it was sent to her.

Fantasy treatments often give the weapon a proper name, supernatural combat powers, a cruciform or flame-like blade, or relic status. None of these details derives from the 1431 description. The historical sword’s recognized “powers” consisted in the meaning assigned to its discovery, not any attested capacity to make its bearer invulnerable or victorious.

### Political and religious mobilization

The sword’s image has been adaptable:

- Catholic usage emphasizes Saint Catherine, revelation, chastity, and providential mission.
- Republican and nationalist imagery uses the armed maiden as territorial liberator.
- Royalist imagery joins the sword to Charles VII’s crown and fleurs-de-lis.
- Anti-English and, later, anti-German propaganda detached the symbol from its exact fifteenth-century context.
- Far-right and integral-nationalist appropriations have sometimes emphasized the invented Charles Martel genealogy.

These are receptions of an image. No political body possesses the physical Fierbois sword.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Primary-source scholarship

The modern documentary foundation was laid by Jules Quicherat’s five-volume **_Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc_** (1841–49), which gathered trial records and chronicles. Pierre Champion’s 1920–21 edition improved the treatment of the Latin record and surviving French minute. Later editions by Pierre Duparc provided the nullification proceedings.

The textual record has limitations:

- the original daily French register is lost;
- interrogation material survives largely through Latin redaction;
- Joan’s dictated letter to the Fierbois clergy is lost;
- no 1429 church inventory or receipt identifies the sword;
- the names of finder and armorer are absent;
- retrospective witnesses disagreed over the breaking episode.

### Sanctuary scholarship

The **_Livre des miracles de Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois_**, edited by Yves Chauvin, is crucial for reconstructing the shrine’s military and votive culture. Its single principal witness, BnF Français 1045, was compiled around 1470–83 and records 237 miracles dated between 1375 and 1470. It does not furnish a continuous early medieval history of the site or identify Joan’s sword as Martel’s. [Review of Chauvin’s edition](https://www.persee.fr/doc/assr_0335-5985_1977_num_43_2_2120_t1_0233_0000_2).

Françoise Michaud-Fréjaville’s study emphasizes the shrine’s concrete function for soldiers and prisoners and documents the pre-Joan deposition of captured armor. This makes a votive sword historically intelligible without invoking either fraud or Carolingian survival.

### Major debates

#### Was Joan’s knowledge miraculous?

History can establish the prediction and discovery but not adjudicate a supernatural cause. Naturalistic explanations remain possible but unproved.

#### Was the object buried?

Joan said it was in earth and shallowly placed. Other sources place it among votive swords or on a tomb. The disagreement may reflect:

- different oral informants;
- a weapon stored among offerings in or near the floor;
- mistranslation or compression;
- later narrative embellishment.

#### Was it ancient?

“Rusty” is not a date. It could have been decades old, recently neglected, or genuinely much older. No typological description survives.

#### Did it belong to Charles Martel?

The claim fails as documentary provenance. Its first identifiable explicit literary expression is approximately nine centuries after Martel and more than two centuries after Joan.

#### Did Joan break it?

Possible but unproved. The late testimony is real; the identity of the damaged sword is uncertain.

#### Was the Saint-Denis sword the same object?

There is no continuous chain and Joan explicitly distinguished the sword she offered at Saint-Denis from the Fierbois sword. Identity is therefore unlikely on the presently known evidence, though not physically disprovable because both objects are lost.

#### Is the Dijon sword authentic?

It is an authentic historical composite object, but not authentically Joan’s Fierbois sword. Its old blade portions do not rescue an incoherent provenance and later hilt.

### Discredited but persistent theories

- that “five crosses” unquestionably means a Jerusalem cross;
- that the sword was certainly forged for Charles Martel;
- that it was Godefroy de Bouillon’s weapon;
- that it passed through Du Guesclin and Louis d’Orléans before burial;
- that Joan drew it personally from a stone or altar;
- that rust vanished when she touched it;
- that the sword captured at Compiègne was the Fierbois sword;
- that the Dijon sword is the original;
- that a sword displayed at Saint-Denis proves continuous survival of the Fierbois weapon.

None is supported by a complete documentary chain.

## 11. Chronology

| Date | Object history |
|---|---|
| **732** | **Legend only:** Charles Martel wins the battle of Tours/Poitiers and supposedly deposits his sword at Fierbois. No contemporary or medieval evidence links his weapon to the site. |
| **1375** | Earliest miracle dated in the surviving Fierbois collection; establishes the shrine’s later-medieval activity, not the sword. |
| **June 1428** | Jean du Chastel reportedly deposits captured armor at Fierbois, providing direct evidence for military ex-votos before Joan. |
| **Early March 1429** | Joan passes through Fierbois, hears three masses, and proceeds toward Chinon. |
| **March–April 1429** | From Tours or Chinon she dictates a request for a sword near the altar. Clergy find a shallowly placed, rusted sword marked by five crosses, clean it, and send it through a Tours armorer. |
| **Spring 1429** | Two luxury scabbards—red velvet and cloth of gold—are supplied; Joan commissions a strong leather scabbard. |
| **April–May 1429** | Sword accompanies Joan’s military household during the relief of Orléans. Actual lethal use is not documented. |
| **June–July 1429** | Carried during the Loire and Reims campaigns according to Joan’s statement that she bore it continually. |
| **July–September 1429** | Episodes of Joan driving prostitutes from the army later become associated with the sword. Accounts conflict about when and where a weapon broke. |
| **8 September 1429** | Joan is wounded during the attack on Paris. |
| **September 1429** | At Saint-Denis she offers armor and a different sword captured before Paris. She later says she carried the Fierbois sword until departing Saint-Denis. |
| **Late 1429–1430** | Chain breaks. Joan later carried a Burgundian sword. The Fierbois sword may have gone to her brothers or household. |
| **23 May 1430** | Joan is captured at Compiègne without the Fierbois sword. |
| **27 February 1431** | She gives the fullest physical and provenance description during her Rouen trial and refuses to identify its current location. |
| **17 March 1431** | Asked the meaning of the five crosses, she says she does not know. |
| **27 March 1431** | Prosecution alleges demonic consultation or staged concealment; Joan denies it. |
| **1456** | Duke of Alençon retrospectively testifies that Joan broke a sword striking a prostitute; identification with Fierbois remains uncertain. |
| **c. 1470–1483** | Surviving Fierbois miracle manuscript compiled; documents sanctuary culture but not a Martel provenance. |
| **16th century** | Saint-Denis inventories and monastic tradition identify a treasury sword with Charles VII and/or Joan. Connection to Fierbois undocumented. |
| **1625** | Jacques Doublet says he repeatedly saw a sword and richly fitted belt attributed to Joan at Saint-Denis. |
| **1656** | Chapelain’s *La Pucelle* probably provides the first explicit major literary identification with Charles Martel. |
| **18th century** | A sword attributed to Joan continues to be shown at Saint-Denis; its identity and later fate are unknown. |
| **1792–1793 and after** | Saint-Denis treasury dispersed and partly destroyed during the Revolution. No item-specific fate is documented for the attributed Joan sword. |
| **1826** | Dijon purchases the composite sword, later inventory CA 1489, from Joseph Bertholomey. |
| **1831** | Dijon object cautiously published as a sword “of the time of Charles VII.” |
| **1846–1847** | William Etty paints and exhibits the monumental discovery scene, consolidating the sword’s romantic visual form. |
| **1856** | Fierbois restoration appeal circulates the Charles Martel chain as national and ecclesiastical history. |
| **1909–1911** | Beatification-era interest prompts Metman to propose the Dijon sword as possibly Joan’s. |
| **1930s–1950s** | Arms historians increasingly interpret the Dijon object as commemorative and composite. |
| **1982** | Etty’s discovery painting is acquired by the Musée des Beaux-Arts d’Orléans. |
| **2010–2011** | Fabrice Cognot and Sophie Jugie’s examination reinforces that the Dijon sword’s hilt is incoherent and nonfunctional and its blade composite. |
| **23 February 2026** | French national museum record updated: Dijon sword remains catalogued only as *dite de Jeanne d’Arc*. |
| **Present** | The historical Fierbois sword remains lost; no extant claimant has an adequate provenance or scientific authentication. |

### Assessment

The documentary core is narrow but solid: in 1429 an old rusted sword marked with five crosses was retrieved from the Fierbois church at Joan’s request and transferred to her; she carried it during much of that year and no longer possessed it at her capture. Everything before its presence at Fierbois and everything after its disappearance from Joan’s immediate equipment is either inference, retrospective testimony, institutional attribution, or legend. No present object can be shown to be the same sword.
