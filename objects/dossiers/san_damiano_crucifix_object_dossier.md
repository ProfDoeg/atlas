# The San Damiano Crucifix: Object Dossier

**Research status:** 6 September 2026  
**Requested filename:** `san_damiano_crucifix_object_dossier.md`

## Evidentiary key

- **Documented:** supported by a contemporary or near-contemporary document, institutional record, conservation report, or direct observation of the artifact.
- **Scientific/technical finding:** reported result of physical examination or conservation treatment.
- **Scholarly inference:** an interpretation based on style, paleography, iconography, construction, or historical context.
- **Institutional tradition:** a claim maintained by the Poor Clares, Franciscan institutions, the Diocese of Assisi, or the Basilica of Santa Chiara.
- **Hagiographic tradition:** a claim transmitted in saints’ lives whose purpose is devotional as well as historical.
- **Pious tradition:** a belief or identification current in worship but not independently demonstrable.
- **Unresolved:** the available evidence does not permit a firm conclusion.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity and names

The object tracked here is the large, cross-shaped Romanesque panel painting presently venerated in the **Oratory or Chapel of the Crucifix adjoining the Chapel of San Giorgio in the Basilica of Santa Chiara, Assisi, Italy**. It is conventionally identified as the crucifix before which Francis of Assisi prayed at the rural church of San Damiano during the early phase of his conversion, traditionally dated to 1205 or 1206.

Its principal names are:

- Italian: **Crocifisso di San Damiano**, **Croce di San Damiano**, and, in devotional usage, **Crocifisso che parlò a san Francesco**.
- Latin descriptions in medieval and early-modern records include **crucifixus qui locutus fuit beato Francisco in Sancti Damiani ecclesia**, “the crucifix that spoke to blessed Francis in the church of San Damiano.”
- English: **San Damiano Crucifix**, **San Damiano Cross**, and sometimes **Cross of Saint Damian**.
- French: **Crucifix de Saint-Damien** or **Croix de Saint-Damien**.
- Spanish: **Crucifijo de San Damián**.
- German: **Kreuz von San Damiano** or **Damianokreuz**.

“San Damiano” identifies the church from which the painting came, dedicated to the physician-martyr Saint Damian, one of the paired saints Cosmas and Damian. It is not the name of the painter or of a separate saint associated with the artifact.

Although frequently called an “icon,” it is physically a Western Italian monumental **painted cross**—Italian *croce dipinta*—using an Italo-Byzantine or Romanesque pictorial vocabulary. “Icon cross” is a useful modern devotional description, but it is not a medieval catalogue classification.

The original must be distinguished from:

1. The modern copy suspended above the altar in the church of San Damiano.
2. Innumerable reduced devotional replicas, pendants, prints, and painted copies.
3. The seventeenth-century sculpted wooden crucifix by Fra Innocenzo da Petralia, formerly often called Innocenzo da Palermo, in a side chapel at San Damiano.
4. The later painted crucifix attributed to the Master of Santa Chiara over the high altar of the Basilica of Santa Chiara.
5. Giotto-workshop and later paintings of Francis praying before a crucifix at San Damiano. Those are representations of the episode, not the artifact itself.

### Dimensions and support

Published dimensions conflict:

- A widely repeated measurement is approximately **190 cm high × 120 cm wide × 12 cm thick**.
- The Italian Episcopal Conference’s youth-pastoral presentation gives **210 × 130 cm**.
- No publicly accessible modern condition report located for this dossier resolves whether the difference results from measuring only the medieval panel, including later framing or mount, or simple repetition of incompatible secondary measurements.

The most defensible description is therefore: a monumental cross approximately **1.9–2.1 metres high and 1.2–1.3 metres wide**.

Institutional descriptions state that the support is **walnut**, covered with a coarse linen cloth on which the image was painted. The usual technical description is tempera or water-based painting on cloth attached to a shaped wooden panel. Public sources do not supply a complete wood anatomy report, pigment analysis, binding-medium analysis, radiograph, dendrochronology, or radiocarbon result. “Walnut” and “linen” are well-established institutional descriptions, but the underlying technical reports are not fully published online.

No reliable weight has been published. No accession or inventory number comparable to a museum accession number was found; the object remains ecclesiastical property or custody rather than a museum accession.

### Form and composition

The cross has a tall central shaft, broad terminals at the arms, an upper tab or cimasa, and an expanded lower termination. A red border, decorated in places with shell-like or geometric motifs, encloses a predominantly dark red and black field.

The central Christ is a **Christus triumphans**, the living or victorious Christ:

- His body is upright rather than collapsed.
- His eyes are open.
- His head is erect or only slightly inclined.
- The wounds and flowing blood are visible, but death is not rendered as bodily defeat.
- He wears a pale or golden loincloth, elaborately modeled with linear highlights.
- The cruciform halo is ornamented.
- His disproportionately large scale establishes a hierarchy between Christ and the witnesses.

The image combines Crucifixion, Resurrection, and Ascension rather than presenting one instant of the Passion in a naturalistic way.

### Upper register

Immediately above Christ is a rectangular titulus. It gives the Johannine charge identifying Jesus as the Nazarene and king of the Jews, conventionally transcribed:

> **Iesus Nazarenus Rex Iudaeorum**  
> “Jesus the Nazarene, King of the Jews.”

The lettering is abbreviated and partially affected by age and restoration. Popular descriptions often normalize it into classical Latin or “INRI”; a proper diplomatic transcription requires the paleographic study by Vittorio Formentin published in Milvia Bollati’s 2016 volume.

Above the titulus, Christ appears a second time, shown ascending within a circular or mandorla-like compartment. He carries a cross-staff or banner and is received by angels. At the summit, the **right hand of God the Father** descends from a semicircle. This is not a second crucifixion but the completion of Christ’s passage to the Father.

### Principal witnesses

Five large figures stand beneath the crossbar.

On the viewer’s left, Christ’s right:

- **Mary, mother of Jesus**.
- **John the Evangelist**.

On the viewer’s right, Christ’s left:

- **Mary Magdalene**.
- **Mary, mother of James**, sometimes described as Mary of Clopas.
- **The centurion**.

The figures’ names were written below them. Four bear halos. The centurion does not. Several raise fingers toward Christ or place hands to their cheeks, gestures of testimony, speech, grief, or astonishment.

A small head, with other partial heads behind it, appears near the centurion. Modern interpretations include:

- the centurion’s son or servant, linked to the healing stories in Matthew 8 or John 4;
- the centurion’s household, representing “he and his household believed”;
- unbelieving spectators;
- the artist’s self-portrait.

None is established. The self-portrait explanation, though often repeated, lacks a maker’s inscription or other corroboration.

### Minor witnesses

At the lower left is the diminutive lance-bearer identified by inscription as **Longinus**, the traditional name of the soldier who pierced Christ’s side.

Opposite him is a small male figure generally called **Stephaton** or **Stephanatus**, the sponge-bearer. Unlike Longinus, his name is not securely displayed on the painting. The identification rests on his traditional pairing with Longinus and on interpretation of what he once held. The name “Stephaton” itself developed in medieval tradition from or in association with the Greek word for sponge; it is not supplied by the canonical Gospels.

Six angels occupy the ends and underside of the crossbar. Their gestures direct attention toward Christ.

### Lower register and losses

At the foot of the cross are six small, heavily damaged figures. Their upper portions survive; much of the lower terminal has suffered paint loss. Proposed identifications include Damian, Rufinus, Michael, John the Baptist, Peter, and Paul—saints associated with Assisi or nearby churches. This is an old but unproven reconstruction. The official Santa Chiara description appropriately calls them **six saints no longer identifiable**.

Blood from Christ’s feet descends toward this damaged company. The black or dark area beneath is variously interpreted as the tomb, the underworld, death, or simply a compositional ground. Assertions that a specific skull of Adam was originally present are not securely demonstrated for the surviving San Damiano panel.

Near Christ’s left calf is a small bird. Competing readings are:

- **rooster**, recalling Peter’s denial or proclaiming the dawn;
- **peacock**, a late-antique and medieval sign of immortality;
- a less determinate bird included in the decorative or narrative program.

The Poor Clares’ current explanation calls it a small cock, *ales diei nuntius*, “the winged herald of day.” The peacock interpretation also remains in circulation. Damage and repainting preclude certainty.

### Condition

The panel survives substantially intact, but the painted surface has:

- extensive abrasion and loss, especially at the bottom;
- cracking, lifting, and flaking associated with the movement of the wood and cloth ground;
- darkening and surface alteration;
- old restorations and retouching;
- areas whose present appearance was materially affected by the 1939 intervention.

The 1939 conservator found paint lifting in flakes. Contemporary custodians suspected that heat from electric bulbs inside a glazed niche and temperature changes from a thin, sun-warmed wall contributed to the damage. Later work removed the enclosing glass and altered the lighting and backing.

Crispino Valenziano and Felice Accrocca have warned that Rosalia Alliana/Alliano’s 1939 treatment altered some features. Consequently, iconographic arguments based on very small details must distinguish surviving medieval paint from restoration.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Physical origin

**Documented maker:** unknown.  
**Documented commissioner:** unknown.  
**Documented workshop:** unknown.  
**Recorded cost:** unknown.  
**Material provenance:** unknown.

Current institutional dating varies from approximately **1100** to the **first half or middle of the twelfth century**. The Poor Clares’ official description places it, “with all probability,” in the first half of the twelfth century. Milvia Bollati’s work favors the middle of that century. Some Franciscan educational literature says “about 1100,” while other discussions place it later in the twelfth century.

The dating is stylistic rather than scientific. No published radiocarbon or dendrochronological date has been located.

The painting belongs to the Umbrian tradition of monumental *Christus triumphans* crosses and has repeatedly been compared with the painted cross associated with Alberto at Spoleto, dated 1187. The Spoleto comparison establishes a regional visual context; it does not prove common authorship.

Older literature often spoke of a “Syrian” painter or of Syrian monks bringing the type to Umbria. More careful formulations describe **Eastern Mediterranean, Byzantine, or Syrian iconographic influence** within an Umbrian work. The painter’s ethnicity and monastic status are not documented.

### San Damiano church context

The church of San Damiano existed before Francis. Arnaldo Fortini reported a documentary mention in **1030**. A record of **1103** concerns local provision for books, ornaments, and bells; suggestions that the crucifix itself formed part of that campaign are conjectural. No known twelfth-century commission, payment, dedication, or inventory explicitly identifies the surviving cross.

It was probably made as an altar, chancel, or rood image for ecclesiastical display. Claims that it hung specifically above a “Blessed Sacrament altar” or that its position proves San Damiano’s parochial status are plausible reconstructions, not direct documentation.

### Earliest evidence for Francis’s repair of San Damiano

Thomas of Celano’s **First Life of Francis**, written in 1228–1229, says that Francis restored the ancient and ruined church of San Damiano. It does **not** narrate a painted crucifix moving its lips or speaking. This omission matters: the earliest securely dated Franciscan biography knows the repair but not, at least in its surviving text, the speaking-image episode.

Francis’s own surviving writings do not explicitly describe the encounter. A prayer beginning *Altissimu, gloriosu Deus*—“Most High, glorious God, enlighten the darkness of my heart”—is transmitted in manuscripts as a prayer said before the crucifix at San Damiano. Most manuscripts connect it with the location, but the prayer itself does not name or physically describe the cross.

### Earliest attestation of the speaking crucifix

The episode first enters the surviving biographical tradition in the **Legend of the Three Companions**, chapter 5, and Thomas of Celano’s **Remembrance of the Desire of a Soul**, commonly called the Second Life, chapter 6.

The *Three Companions* tradition is connected with material submitted to Minister General Crescentius of Jesi in 1246, but the composition and integrity of the surviving *Legenda trium sociorum* are disputed. Its account says, in substance, that Francis was prompted “in spirit” to enter San Damiano; while praying before an image of the Crucified, he heard:

> “Francis, do you not see that my house is being destroyed? Go, therefore, and repair it for me.”

The text adds that Francis experienced the speaker as Christ crucified. Depending on how one dates the surviving recension, this may be the earliest textual statement of the miracle.

Celano’s Second Life was produced in response to the Franciscan leadership’s call for additional memories, conventionally dated to 1246–1247. Its Latin calls the object a painted image and says that it moved its lips:

> *Imago Christi crucifixi, labiis picturae motis, loquitur…*  
> “The image of Christ crucified, moving the lips of the painting, speaks…”

Celano’s command reads:

> *Francisce, vade, repara domum meam, quae, ut cernis, tota destruitur.*  
> “Francis, go, repair my house, which, as you see, is being completely destroyed.”

This is a near-contemporary hagiographic attestation, written roughly forty years after the alleged experience, not an eyewitness deposition.

Bonaventure’s **Major Legend**, written in 1260–1263 and made the Order’s normative life of Francis in 1266, repeats and intensifies the episode. The voice speaks **three times** and the narrator explicitly interprets “my church” as ultimately referring to the universal Church, even though Francis initially understands the command materially.

### Earliest secure object-specific record at Santa Chiara

A fourteenth-century calendar in **Assisi, Biblioteca Comunale, Codex 344, fol. 63ra**, reportedly records that at Santa Chiara were honorably preserved relics together with:

> *crucifixo qui locutus fuit beato Francisco in Sancti Damiani ecclesia*  
> “the crucifix that spoke to blessed Francis in the church of San Damiano.”

This is the earliest presently identified documentary notice explicitly joining:

1. a physical crucifix at Santa Chiara,
2. its former location at San Damiano, and
3. its identity as the speaking crucifix.

It is later than the alleged event and later than the 1257 move. Thus the physical chain before the fourteenth-century record is strong institutional tradition but not supported at every step by contemporary inventories.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Before ca. 1205/1206

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The cross was made in Umbria in the twelfth century and installed at San Damiano. No commission or delivery record survives. Its exact position in the church is unknown.

### Ca. 1205/1206: Francis at San Damiano

**Hagiographic tradition:** Francis prayed before a painted Crucifixion image in the ruined church, heard Christ tell him to repair “my house,” and began restoring the building.

The date is reconstructed from the chronology of Francis’s conversion. Sources and modern institutions alternate between 1205 and 1206. The event is not recorded in Francis’s writings or Celano’s First Life; it is first narrated about four decades later.

The later fresco traditionally attributed to Giotto in the Upper Basilica of San Francesco represents the scene inside a collapsing church. Its cross is a schematic *Christus triumphans*, not a reliable portrait or condition record of the surviving panel.

### Ca. 1211/1212–1257: the community of Clare at San Damiano

Francis installed or helped establish Clare and her companions at San Damiano, where the Poor Ladies lived for approximately four decades. The cross therefore remained in a church used by Clare’s community. Tradition holds that Clare and her sisters prayed before it, but surviving early sources do not provide a dated register of individual ceremonies performed before the panel.

Custody during this period was institutional rather than personal: the church, its clerical arrangements, and the women’s community all participated in its care. Calling Clare its “owner” would be misleading.

### 1257: transfer from San Damiano to Santa Chiara/San Giorgio

**Institutional tradition, supported by later records:** When the Poor Ladies transferred from San Damiano to their new monastery beside the former church of San Giorgio, they took the cross with them.

This was not a sale or secular gift. It was the movement of a sacred furnishing or Franciscan memorial with the community that had kept it. No price is recorded.

Construction of the Basilica of Santa Chiara began in the 1250s. In 1263 the sisters obtained permission to dismantle or relocate the old San Giorgio chapel within the developing monastic complex because pilgrims and clergy entering the claustral area disturbed enclosure. A new chapel or internal church was created alongside the basilica’s south transept, communicating with the public church through a barred opening. The cross came to be displayed within this enclosed liturgical system.

### Fourteenth century

The calendar in Assisi Codex 344 explicitly locates the speaking crucifix with the relics at Santa Chiara. This is the first secure object-specific documentary anchor after the transfer.

Its placement served two audiences:

- the enclosed Poor Clares, for whom it was part of the choir and reliquary environment;
- pilgrims in the external church, who could see or venerate it through a grille.

### Fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries

Documents cited by Emanuele Zappasodi refer to the choir grille and to an altar before it. A record of July 1509 describes the area as:

> *ante cratem ferream que dicitur la grata del crocifixo*  
> “before the iron grille called the grille of the Crucifix.”

Another formula places the crucifix *intus dictum monasterium prope cratem*, “inside the said monastery near the grille.”

On **12 January 1488**, Assisi’s communal council appropriated money for a **new cabinet, or *armario*, to house the cross**. This is evidence that the image was not merely a private monastic furnishing: the city regarded its preservation as a civic and devotional concern.

### 1618

A printed pilgrim guide reported:

> “Inside the monastery, behind the reliquary, is shown the celebrated Crucifix that spoke to Saint Francis at San Damiano.”

The wording indicates restricted but managed visibility from outside the enclosure.

### 1743 and later eighteenth century

Architectural adjustments moved the sisters’ communion opening on the south side of the nave. The broader reliquary and grille arrangements were repeatedly reorganized. Available public records do not establish that the cross left the monastic complex during these works.

### 1813: Napoleonic suppression

During the Napoleonic suppression of the monastery, the *Memoriae breves: 1793–1813* record that the crucifix was brought out and exposed at the high altar for **ten days**, “like the Forty Hours,” with the costs met by alms.

The report is significant in two respects:

- the object survived the suppression in the monastery’s custody;
- its public exposure was exceptional enough to be recorded.

No evidence was found that it was confiscated, sold, stolen, or removed from Assisi.

### 1880

The cabinet funded in earlier centuries or one of its successors was replaced in **1880**. Ambrogio Mariani described the cross as kept in a cabinet in a small room of its own beside the nuns’ choir, visible through a grille beneath the relic cupboard.

### 1900

When the old nuns’ choir was opened to the public and became the Chapel of the Blessed Sacrament, the crucifix was placed in a grated recess against the chapel’s southern side. The community’s choir was moved farther into the monastic complex.

This was a major shift from a primarily cloistered object seen through a grille to an artifact increasingly arranged for public pilgrimage.

### 1923–1932

In 1923 Franciscan and Clarian relics were placed in a rotating cabinet in the crucifix recess.

In **1932**, the cross was enclosed in a glazed niche lighted internally by four electric bulbs. The installation improved visibility but created an unstable and possibly damaging microclimate.

### 7 January–15 May 1939: restoration

Rosalia Alliana—also reported as Rosalia Alliano—treated the crucifix under the Royal Superintendency for Monuments and Galleries of Umbria. A. Bertini Calosso issued the superintendent’s report.

The treatment responded to paint lifting in scales. After restoration:

- a new backing and air space were constructed;
- the protective front glass was removed;
- four internal bulbs were replaced with two external reflectors;
- the cross returned to substantially the same recess.

Later critics judged the intervention technically inadequate and considered some features altered. A complete operation-by-operation account is reproduced or discussed in Bollati’s 2016 volume, but the full report is not readily available online.

### 1953: procession through Assisi

The cross probably left Santa Chiara for the first time in centuries in a **1953 *peregrinatio*** through Assisi. The route passed from Santa Chiara through the Piazza del Comune and briefly reached the small square at San Damiano. It did not enter and remain inside its former church.

### 1957–1958: public installation

Sources differ by one year:

- Franciscan devotional accounts say that during Holy Week **1957** it was first placed for public veneration over a new altar in the Chapel of San Giorgio.
- The detailed Santa Chiara architectural history says that in **1958**, when two more bays of the San Giorgio chapel were opened to the public, it was set in a frame at the center of a large glass partition separating the Blessed Sacrament area.

These accounts may describe successive phases rather than a contradiction: ceremonial exposure in 1957 followed by the completed architectural installation in 1958.

### 1999–2001: chapel restoration and conservation

During restoration of the San Giorgio chapel:

- the large glass partition was removed to restore the architectural unity of the chapel;
- the entire surface of the crucifix was cleaned;
- the painted surface and support were reviewed and treated;
- the display arrangement was revised.

A dedicated report on the 2000-era conservation is published in the appendices to Bollati’s *Francesco e la croce di S. Damiano*. Public summaries do not disclose analytical results sufficient to claim scientific dating or a complete pigment identification.

### 15–19 June 2016: temporary return to San Damiano

For the Jubilee of Mercy, the Poor Clares and Friars Minor arranged a five-day return.

The cross was:

1. lowered from its position in the Chapel of San Giorgio;
2. placed in a protective wooden transport structure;
3. carried to San Damiano;
4. unpacked inside the church;
5. mounted high above the altar in what organizers considered an approximation of its medieval setting;
6. exposed for prayer and liturgical events;
7. returned afterward to Santa Chiara.

Organizers described this as its first prolonged return inside San Damiano in almost eight centuries. The 1953 procession was acknowledged as a partial precedent.

### 2016–present

The cross returned to its ordinary place in the Basilica of Santa Chiara. No later transfer, theft, ownership action, or competing custody claim was found.

### Custody gaps

The chain is not equally documented throughout:

- Manufacture to ca. 1205: no object-specific record.
- Ca. 1205 to 1257: identification rests on Franciscan hagiography and institutional continuity at San Damiano.
- 1257 transfer: strongly embedded institutional tradition, but the surviving explicit object-specific record is later.
- Fourteenth century onward: increasingly dense documentary evidence through calendars, municipal appropriations, pilgrimage guides, monastic records, architectural documents, photographs, and conservation reports.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is the surviving panel medieval?

Yes. Its construction, style, iconographic type, and long documented presence are consistent with a twelfth-century Umbrian painted cross. No serious modern scholarship located for this dossier treats it as a modern forgery.

### Is it the cross that stood at San Damiano?

The evidence is strong but not unbroken:

**For continuity**

- The cross is stylistically older than Francis.
- The Poor Clares moved from San Damiano to Santa Chiara in 1257.
- A fourteenth-century Assisi manuscript explicitly identifies the Santa Chiara cross as the one that spoke to Francis at San Damiano.
- Later civic and monastic records consistently preserve that identity.
- No rival physical object with an equally old local provenance exists.

**Limitations**

- No pre-Franciscan inventory identifies it.
- No contemporary account by Francis describes its appearance.
- Celano’s First Life does not recount the speaking crucifix.
- The first miracle narratives date from about forty years after the supposed event.
- The first surviving document securely identifying the Santa Chiara object is later still.
- A substitution between 1205 and the fourteenth century cannot be logically disproved, although there is no affirmative evidence that one occurred.

**Conclusion:** continuity is highly plausible and is the established institutional identification; it is not demonstrable by an uninterrupted set of contemporary object records.

### Did the image literally speak?

That is a religious claim, not a proposition susceptible to materials analysis. Historically demonstrable facts are:

- Francis restored San Damiano.
- By the mid-thirteenth century Franciscan sources said that a painted crucifix there had spoken to him.
- By the fourteenth century the Santa Chiara cross was identified as that image.
- The story materially affected the preservation, movement, display, and replication of the object.

### Date and attribution

Competing dates include ca. 1100, the first half of the twelfth century, the middle of the century, and a broader twelfth-century range. Attribution remains “anonymous Umbrian painter,” sometimes narrowed to an Assisi or Spoleto-area workshop.

Claims that the artist was a Syrian monk are unverified. Eastern or Syrian iconographic influence does not establish the maker’s birthplace.

No signature has been reported. The nearby small head is not secure evidence of a self-portrait.

### Scientific examination

No publicly documented radiocarbon dating, dendrochronology, X-ray fluorescence campaign, multispectral imaging program, or binding-medium analysis was found. Published conclusions remain substantially stylistic, technical, and paleographic.

This absence should not be converted into an assertion that no such examination has ever occurred. Conservation files may contain unpublished photography or sampling. What can be said is that no decisive scientific dating result has entered the accessible scholarly record.

### Restoration and original appearance

The 1939 restoration complicates claims about minute iconographic details. Later scholars have specifically criticized the quality of that intervention. The principal composition is unquestionably medieval, but some contours, colors, facial details, lettering, and small attributes visible today may include retouching.

The severe damage to the bottom prevents confident identification of the six saints. Likewise, interpretations of the bird and the figures by the centurion should be labeled hypotheses, not readings secured by inscriptions.

### Copies and confusion

The cross now at San Damiano is a **copy**, openly presented as such. The seventeenth-century sculpted crucifix by Fra Innocenzo is another, entirely separate artwork. No institutional attempt to pass either off as the medieval original was found.

No rival monastery or church maintains a serious competing claimant to the unique physical identity of the San Damiano original.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Medieval liturgical image

The object was made for a church, probably for conspicuous placement in or above the sanctuary or chancel. Its scale, cross shape, painted witnesses, titulus, and ascending Christ made it simultaneously:

- an altar or rood image;
- a visual exposition of the Passion;
- an image of the living and victorious Christ;
- an object before which prayer was performed.

Its original ritual position is not recorded.

### Francis’s prayer

The central ritual episode is Francis’s prayer before the cross. The prayer *Altissimu, gloriosu Deus* is associated by manuscript rubrics with prayer before the San Damiano crucifix:

> Most High, glorious God,  
> enlighten the darkness of my heart  
> and give me right faith, certain hope, and perfect charity,  
> sense and knowledge, Lord,  
> that I may carry out your holy and true command.

Whether Francis composed it at the precise moment of the reported locution cannot be independently established.

### Clare and the Poor Ladies

At San Damiano, the cross formed part of the devotional environment of Clare and her sisters. After 1257 it was installed inside their enclosed choir or near its grille. This placement allowed continued monastic prayer while also accommodating pilgrimage.

### Relic-like status

The panel does not contain a bodily relic of Francis and was not made from material believed to be part of the historical cross of Jesus. Its relic status is **associative**: it was preserved as a physical witness to Francis’s conversion and divine commission.

By the fourteenth century it was catalogued or mentioned together with sacred relics. In later arrangements it stood behind or beneath reliquary cabinets. Civic financing in 1488 indicates that its cult had become part of Assisi’s collective sacred patrimony.

### Exceptional public exposition

Documented ceremonial uses include:

- ten days’ high-altar exposition in 1813;
- the 1953 procession through Assisi;
- public installation in 1957–1958;
- the five-day return and liturgical program at San Damiano in 2016.

### Franciscan emblem

The cross now symbolizes the command to “repair” or “rebuild” the Church. This interpretation is rooted in Bonaventure’s ecclesiological reading but has become much broader in modern Franciscan usage. Replicas appear in Franciscan churches, schools, retreat houses, formation centers, and homes worldwide.

Possession of the original confers no legal office, sovereignty, or sacramental authority. Its legitimating function is spiritual and institutional: it binds the Poor Clares and Friars Minor to the founding narratives of Franciscan and Clarian life.

### Access rules

For centuries the cross remained physically inside the Poor Clares’ enclosure and was seen through a grille. Its custody embodied the tension between:

- monastic enclosure;
- liturgical use;
- pilgrimage demand;
- conservation and public visibility.

Since the mid-twentieth century it has been publicly visible in the basilica complex, though touching it is not permitted and movement requires coordination among the Poor Clares, Franciscan custodians, ecclesiastical authorities, and heritage professionals.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The speaking image

**Tier:** mid-thirteenth-century hagiographic tradition.

Francis, undergoing conversion, enters the dilapidated church of San Damiano and kneels before the Crucifixion image. Christ speaks from the painting and commands him to repair the house that is falling into ruin. Francis initially understands the words literally and gathers money and labor to rebuild San Damiano and other churches.

In Celano’s version, the painted lips move. Francis trembles and experiences an interior transformation. Bonaventure has the command repeated three times and explains that it prophetically referred to the universal Church.

**Possible historical core:** Francis indisputably repaired San Damiano and regarded the Crucified Christ as central to his vocation. The precise sensory form of the experience is known only through later hagiography.

### Literal church versus universal Church

**Tier:** later thirteenth-century theological elaboration.

The earliest form centers upon the ruined building. Bonaventure makes the double meaning explicit: Francis first repairs the material chapel, then fulfills the deeper command by renewing the Church as a community.

Modern retellings often place the universal interpretation directly into the original speech. That collapses two stages of the tradition.

### The artist as Syrian monk

**Tier:** modern or older scholarly-devotional speculation.

The work’s Eastern-looking conventions prompted claims that a Syrian monk painted it or that Syrian monks brought its exact type to Umbria. Eastern Mediterranean influence is credible; the artist’s Syrian identity is undocumented.

### The centurion’s household

**Tier:** modern interpretive tradition.

The heads behind the centurion are said to represent his healed son and family. Different writers link them to different Gospel narratives. The image is damaged or ambiguous, and no surviving inscription settles the question.

### The bird

**Tier:** unresolved iconographic tradition.

The bird has been called a rooster, herald of dawn and reminder of Peter’s denial, or a peacock, symbol of immortality. Neither identification should be presented as fact.

### The six saints below

**Tier:** later antiquarian reconstruction.

They have been identified as Damian, Rufinus, Michael, John the Baptist, Peter, and Paul. The lower panel’s losses make this impossible to verify.

### First return “after eight centuries”

**Tier:** ceremonial shorthand.

The 2016 event was widely advertised as the cross’s first return to San Damiano in almost eight centuries. More precisely, it was the first known prolonged reinstallation **inside** the church. The cross had approached San Damiano during the 1953 procession.

## 8. Current Status and Location

As of 6 September 2026, the original is held at:

**Basilica di Santa Chiara**  
Piazza Santa Chiara 1  
06081 Assisi, Perugia, Italy

It is displayed in the **Oratory/Chapel of the Crucifix connected with the Chapel of San Giorgio**, on the right side of the basilica complex.

The historic controlling community is the **Protomonastery of the Poor Clares of Santa Chiara**. The Basilica and its chaplaincy are served by Friars Minor. The object is also subject to Italian cultural-heritage law and ecclesiastical regulation; moving it is not an ordinary private-owner decision.

The original remains:

- publicly visible during basilica opening hours;
- actively venerated;
- part of a functioning sacred complex, not merely a museum exhibit;
- inaccessible to ordinary physical handling;
- without a publicly advertised museum accession number.

The official sanctuary currently lists opening generally as:

- morning: 06:30–12:00;
- afternoon: approximately 14:00–17:45 or 18:45 in daylight-saving time;
- continuous opening on specified weekends and feast days.

Hours may change for worship or conservation.

At San Damiano, a modern copy occupies the position associated with the medieval original. The original’s 2016 return was temporary.

No current ownership, restitution, repatriation, theft, or claimant dispute was found. No post-2001 scientific examination with published analytical results was located.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Replication

The San Damiano design is among the most widely replicated Franciscan images. Copies range from:

- full-size painted church crosses;
- hand-painted wooden panels;
- printed plaques;
- school and retreat-house furnishings;
- sterling-silver pendants;
- rosary and chaplet components;
- inexpensive tourist souvenirs sold in Assisi and internationally.

These objects reproduce the identity and story of the original but are not relics merely because they copy it. A copy acquires relic status only under some additional devotional theory, such as contact with the original, and such status should not be presumed.

A 2016 anonymous donation placed large replicas in three heavily used buildings at Saint Francis University in Pennsylvania. The university explicitly presented them as markers of a “Franciscan-hearted” institutional identity.

### Public image after 1957

Public access to the original in the late 1950s enabled closer photography, publication, mass reproduction, and iconographic commentary. The object’s present international visibility is therefore partly modern. Earlier pilgrims knew of it, but its enclosure and grille limited direct visual access.

Michael Scanlan’s 1983 devotional explanation became a major English-language source for the now-standard figure-by-figure reading. Many institutional webpages reproduce or adapt it, sometimes turning hypotheses into apparent certainties.

### Literature and scholarship

Important modern treatments include:

- Leone Bracaloni, *Storia di San Damiano in Assisi* (1926).
- Bracaloni, “Il prodigioso Crocifisso che parlò a S. Francesco” (1939).
- Kajetan Esser’s work on Francis’s prayer before the cross.
- Optatus van Asseldonk, “Il Crocifisso di San Damiano visto e vissuto da S. Francesco.”
- Michael Scanlan, *The San Damiano Cross: An Explanation* (1983).
- Michael Guinan, *The Franciscan Vision and the Gospel of John: The San Damiano Crucifix, Francis and John, Creation and John* (2006).
- Milvia Bollati, *Francesco e la croce di S. Damiano* (2016).
- Crispino Valenziano’s iconological treatment (2019).
- Bollati, “La croce di San Damiano” (2024).

### Visual arts

The episode was incorporated into the late-thirteenth-century Francis cycle in the Upper Basilica of San Francesco. That fresco profoundly shaped later images of the encounter: Francis kneels alone within a visibly ruined chapel while the cross addresses him.

Jacopo Ligozzi and other early-modern artists repeated the subject. Modern devotional art generally copies either the surviving panel or the Giotto-type scene.

### Controversy generated by a replica

In 2010 a three-metre San Damiano-inspired crucifix painted by Janet Jaime for Saint Charles Borromeo Catholic Church in Warr Acres, Oklahoma, drew complaints that the modeling of Christ’s abdomen appeared genital. The artist reportedly agreed to modify it. The controversy concerned a modern derivative, not the Assisi original, but demonstrates how the famous pattern can generate an independent object history.

### Commerce and pilgrimage

The cross’s silhouette and image support a substantial devotional-goods market. Commercial descriptions frequently repeat historically insecure claims:

- an exact date of 1100;
- a Syrian monastic author;
- exact identification of every minor figure;
- certainty that every color and border motif has a fixed theological meaning.

Such statements should be understood as modern catechetical systems unless tied to medieval texts or technical evidence.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Early documentation

The principal sources are dispersed among:

- Franciscan hagiographies;
- Assisi Biblioteca Comunale, Codex 344;
- the Protomonastery archive;
- Assisi communal deliberations;
- architectural and liturgical documents concerning the grille and choir;
- early pilgrim guides;
- nineteenth-century descriptions;
- records of the Umbrian superintendency;
- twentieth- and twenty-first-century conservation files.

Some material remains unpublished or is accessible only through later transcriptions.

### Hagiographical development

The critical progression is:

1. **Celano’s First Life:** Francis repairs San Damiano; no speaking image.
2. **Legend of the Three Companions:** the command from the Crucified appears.
3. **Celano’s Second Life:** the painted lips move; the episode is described as unprecedented.
4. **Bonaventure:** the voice speaks three times; the Church-wide meaning becomes explicit.
5. **Later Franciscan tradition:** the object becomes a relic-like witness to the Order’s mission.

This sequence does not disprove Francis’s experience. It demonstrates how its literary form developed.

### Dating debate

The cross has been dated:

- ca. 1100;
- early twelfth century;
- first half of the twelfth century;
- mid-twelfth century;
- broadly to the twelfth century.

The debate relies on comparisons with Umbrian painted crosses, especially works around Spoleto, and on paleography and iconography. Assertions of an eleventh-century date persist in popular material but are less consistent with the current Santa Chiara and Bollati dating.

### Stylistic origin

The work is frequently called Byzantine or Syrian. Current caution requires separating:

- **documented:** it is an Italian object long at Assisi;
- **stylistic finding:** it uses conventions with Eastern Christian and Mediterranean antecedents;
- **unverified:** the painter personally came from Syria;
- **historical context:** central Italy received artistic forms through Adriatic, Roman, monastic, and pilgrimage networks.

### Iconographic debate

Disputed features include:

- rooster versus peacock;
- identity of the figure opposite Longinus;
- identity of the heads by the centurion;
- identities of the six saints below;
- precise theological meaning of the shell-like border;
- extent to which the program depends specifically on John’s Gospel;
- whether later repainting has distorted key details.

The five principal witnesses, Longinus, titulus, angels, Ascension, and hand of the Father are comparatively secure.

### Conservation debate

The 1939 restoration is both indispensable and problematic. It saved unstable paint and improved the display environment, but later scholars believe it also altered the work. Modern readings that treat every visible line as twelfth-century evidence are therefore methodologically unsafe.

The 1999–2001 intervention was more conservation-oriented and accompanied by renewed study. Its documentation, together with the 1939 report, was made more accessible through Bollati’s 2016 publication.

### Catalogue status and research needs

The object lacks a conveniently accessible modern technical catalogue entry comparable to records in Italy’s national cultural-property database. Outstanding needs include:

- a diplomatic transcription and mapped image of every inscription;
- high-resolution visible, infrared, ultraviolet, and X-radiographic documentation;
- dendrochronological assessment if the support permits it;
- non-invasive pigment and gilding analysis;
- separation of original paint, old repaint, 1939 retouching, and later conservation;
- publication of the complete 1939 and 1999–2001 reports;
- reconciliation of the conflicting dimensions;
- a formal legal and inventory statement by the custodial institution.
