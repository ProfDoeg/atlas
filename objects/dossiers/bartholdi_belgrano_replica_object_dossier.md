# Bartholdi's small-scale Statue of Liberty replica, Belgrano: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier concerns the approximately 2.9-metre cast-iron figure of *La Liberté éclairant le monde* standing in the Barrancas de Belgrano, Buenos Aires—not the separate Bartholdian figure on the façade of Escuela Normal Superior No. 9 “Domingo Faustino Sarmiento” at Callao 450, and not the later Val d’Osne figure at Villa Aberastain, San Juan.

The Belgrano object is physically attested, carries the names of Auguste Bartholdi and the Val d’Osne foundry, corresponds to model no. 833 in the foundry’s catalogue, appears in a photograph dated January 1931, and remains municipal property in a protected public landscape. Its precise manufacturing, acquisition, and first-installation dates are nevertheless unresolved.

Three dates commonly attached to it must not be conflated:

- **1875:** based on a later local historian’s interpretation of former Belgrano justice of the peace José G. Saborido’s administrative memorandum, which mentions installing “the statue of Liberty” but does not describe or attribute that object.
- **3 October 1886:** repeatedly given in modern journalism, but demonstrably also the opening date associated with the Sarmiento school and its separate architectural statue; no located contemporary record securely connects that day with the Barrancas figure.
- **1884:** sometimes reported as a date cast on the object. The detailed 2008/2018 field inventory records the Bartholdi and foundry inscriptions but not “1884.” Its presence requires renewed examination.

Evidence labels used below are:

- **Documented:** supported by a primary record, physical inspection, official register, or contemporary image.
- **Institutional record/tradition:** stated by a responsible archive or government body, but without the underlying primary document reproduced.
- **Plausible inference:** consistent with the evidence but not independently documented.
- **Disputed/unverified:** repeated in sources but contradicted, incomplete, or unsupported.
- **Modern legend:** a popular claim produced by conflation or exaggeration.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity and names

The object is a freestanding, reduced cast-iron edition of Frédéric-Auguste Bartholdi’s monumental allegorical figure:

- **French formal title:** *La Liberté éclairant le monde*.
- **English:** *Liberty Enlightening the World*; commonly *Statue of Liberty*.
- **Spanish:** *La Libertad iluminando al mundo*, *Estatua de la Libertad*, *La Libertad porteña*, and *Estatua de la Libertad de Barrancas de Belgrano*.
- **Foundry catalogue designation:** *Liberté*, model no. **833**, Val d’Osne catalogue no. 2.
- **Local heritage designation:** “Estatua de la Libertad – Réplica.”

“Liberty” derives through French *liberté* and Spanish *libertad* from Latin *libertas*. The phrase “enlightening the world” refers simultaneously to the torch’s literal light and Enlightenment political ideas. The shorthand “replica” is serviceable but imprecise: this is not a later unauthorized imitation. It is an authorized commercial reduction of Bartholdi’s model, cast by the French Val d’Osne art foundry and bearing the sculptor’s name. It is therefore an original nineteenth-century cast of a reduced edition, not the “original Statue of Liberty” in the sense of the copper monument designed for New York.

### Object tracked

This dossier tracks only the Barrancas figure located near La Pampa and Avenida Virrey Vértiz/Arribeños in Belgrano. Other Argentine versions are separate objects with separate provenances:

1. The architectural figure on Escuela Normal No. 9 at Callao 450.
2. The modified Val d’Osne version at Villa Aberastain, San Juan, whose tablet bears Argentine devices and the dates 1810–1910.
3. Miscellaneous later copies and commercial imitations elsewhere in Argentina.

### Form and iconography

Field inspection conducted for the French metallurgical-heritage database describes the Belgrano work as a standing female figure in classical drapery. She:

- wears a radiate crown;
- advances or stands with one foot forward;
- raises a torch in her right hand;
- cradles a tablet in her left arm;
- has broken chains at her feet.

The tablet reportedly bears the American independence date in Roman numerals, corresponding to **IV JULY MDCCLXXVI** or a close rendering. The exact letter-by-letter inscription should be checked in situ because accessible field records summarize rather than transcribe it photographically. Broken shackles signify release from tyranny or enslavement; the tablet joins liberty to law; the radiate crown and torch make the figure an illuminator.

### Materials and technique

The sculpture is **cast iron** (*fonte de fer*, Spanish *hierro fundido*), not copper, bronze, or repoussé sheet metal. It was produced from a reusable industrial mould by the Val d’Osne foundry. This distinguishes it technically from the New York colossus, whose outer surface consists of hammered copper sheets carried on a metal armature.

Modern Buenos Aires reporting describes the iron as painted or patinated to resemble bronze. Historical and modern photographs show markedly different appearances, variously described as reddish, bronze-coloured, dark, or greenish. These are coating and weathering states; they do not establish changes in the underlying metal. The often-repeated phrase *hierro rojo* may describe an old red surface treatment rather than a scientifically identified iron type.

### Dimensions

The most defensible dimensions come from the Val d’Osne entry for model no. 833:

- **Height:** 2.90 m.
- **Base footprint:** approximately 0.74 × 0.74 m.
- **Scale:** conventionally described as about **1:16** of Bartholdi’s 46.05-metre New York figure.

Buenos Aires tourism material rounds the height to “nearly three metres.” The weight of this particular cast has not been located in a published inventory.

The stone or masonry pedestal increases its total elevation, but no reliable dimensions, material analysis, construction date, architect, or contractor for that pedestal were found.

### Inscriptions and marks

The metallurgical-heritage field inventory records:

- on one side of the plinth: **A. BARTHOLDI**
- on another:  
  **FONDU PAR LE**  
  **VAL D’OSNE**  
  **58 Bd VOLTAIRE PARIS**

Translation: “Cast by the Val d’Osne [foundry], 58 Boulevard Voltaire, Paris.”

The mark functions as both maker identification and commercial address. It is powerful evidence that the extant figure is a Val d’Osne cast made from a Bartholdi model.

Some derivative sources instead report “8 Rue Voltaire” and add the year “1884.” These details conflict with the field transcription of “58 Bd Voltaire,” which is also found on documented Val d’Osne examples elsewhere. Until a current high-resolution record of every face of the plinth is published, the “8 Rue Voltaire/1884” wording should be treated as **unverified**.

### Catalogue identity

The specialized inventory identifies the work with Val d’Osne catalogue no. 2, plate 624/625 C, model no. 833. A securely catalogued example of the same model at Poitiers is recorded as 2.90 × 0.74 × 0.74 m and carries the same foundry address. Different purchasers could customize the tablet or pedestal while retaining the same body mould.

The serial nature of the cast is essential to its identity. “By Bartholdi” means that Bartholdi created and authorized the sculptural model; it does not establish that he personally handled molten iron, finished the Belgrano casting, selected its colour, or supervised its Argentine installation.

### Pedestal, orientation, and setting

The figure stands on a stone or masonry pedestal on the sloping edge of the Barrancas de Belgrano. Modern descriptions place it near La Pampa and Avenida Virrey Vértiz, with some sources using La Pampa and Arribeños. Approximate published coordinates are **34°33′38.66″ S, 58°26′51.56″ W**.

Its elevated situation gives the torch and crown a skyline profile, although mature vegetation can conceal the statue from paths and surrounding streets. The present Barrancas landscape was redesigned by Carlos Thays in 1892. That landscape date does not itself prove when the statue entered the park.

### Damage, repairs, coatings, and condition

A Museo de Obras de Arte record reproduced by *La Nación* shows the statue displaced and broken following an attack or act of vandalism in **1986**. The French heritage inventory says it was “returned to its original place in 1986, after damage.” The exact components fractured, the repair materials, whether any pieces were newly cast, and whether the current pedestal is original have not been published.

In **2015**, the municipal Coordinación de Monumentos y Obras de Arte—MOA—restored artworks in the Barrancas, explicitly including this statue. The government’s announcement does not provide an object-level treatment report. Cleaning, corrosion removal, coating analysis, internal reinforcement, repainting, or replacement of elements therefore cannot be inferred individually.

No published metallographic, paint-stratigraphy, X-ray, ultrasonic, or structural analysis of the Belgrano cast was located. Its present structural condition beneath the coating remains undocumented in publicly accessible sources.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### The design lineage

Bartholdi began designing *La Liberté éclairant le monde* around 1870. The Musée Bartholdi regards a terracotta sketch dated 1870, identifiable partly through the broken chains, as the earliest known formulation. Preparatory terracotta and plaster models followed around 1872–1874. In 1875 the Franco-American Union approved and promoted the project, and bronzed terracotta “committee models” were sold to raise money.

The surviving “committee model” at the Metropolitan Museum of Art is dated 1875, measures 121.3 cm high, and already shows the torch-bearing figure and broken chains. The Musée Bartholdi dates its committee models broadly to 1875–1878.

The full-scale New York statue was constructed in France from 1875/1876 to 1884, presented to the United States on 4 July 1884, shipped in pieces in 1885, reassembled, and dedicated on 28 October 1886. The Belgrano cast therefore belongs to a broader family of authorized models and reductions produced during and after the colossal project.

### Creation of the Val d’Osne edition

The Belgrano figure corresponds to a commercial cast-iron edition issued by the Société du Val d’Osne. Its reusable mould reproduced Bartholdi’s composition at approximately 1:16 scale. The firm offered model no. 833 through its catalogue, allowing municipalities and commemorative committees to purchase a recognizable Bartholdian Liberty without commissioning a unique sculpture.

No foundry order ledger, invoice, bill of lading, customs entry, municipal payment voucher, or correspondence between Bartholdi, Val d’Osne, and an Argentine purchaser has been located in the accessible sources consulted. Consequently:

- the casting year is not securely established;
- the individual who placed the order is unknown;
- the quoted price, freight cost, and customs route are unknown;
- the casting shop and iron source within the wider Val d’Osne system are not documented;
- Bartholdi’s personal involvement in this particular sale is unknown.

### The 1875 claim

Alberto Octavio Córdoba’s *El barrio de Belgrano: hombres y cosas de su pasado histórico*, published by the Municipality of Buenos Aires in 1968, states that a statue of Liberty was installed while José G. Saborido served as Belgrano’s justice of the peace. Córdoba cited a memorandum Saborido presented on leaving office. A later researcher reproduces the relevant passage:

> “en la Casa Municipal y Comisaría se han introducido algunas mejoras compatibles con el estado precario de su caja, tales como la colocación de gas interior y la cañería para la iluminación exterior, la colocación de la estatua de la Libertad, el empedrado y los faroles en las entradas, las pinturas en el salón y de las rejas exteriores”

Saborido held office from 3 January to 11 April 1875. The passage therefore documents, through Córdoba’s transcription, an object called a “statue of Liberty” associated with improvements to the municipal house and police station.

It does **not** establish that this was the surviving Barrancas object:

- no sculptor is named;
- no foundry is named;
- no material or dimensions are given;
- no Barrancas location is stated;
- the syntactic context instead suggests the municipal building;
- Bartholdi’s final reduced form and its Val d’Osne catalogue edition had not yet been securely dated to early 1875.

The 1875 reference could concern another allegorical Liberty, an architectural ornament, or a now-lost predecessor. Identity with the present figure is possible only as an unsupported hypothesis.

There is also a jurisdictional problem. Belgrano was then a separate municipality in Buenos Aires Province and was incorporated into the federal capital only in 1887. Claims that the “Municipality of Buenos Aires” bought the statue in 1875 or 1886 require clarification: they may retrospectively describe the municipal owner, confuse Buenos Aires with the Municipality of Belgrano, or refer to a later acquisition.

### The 3 October 1886 claim

Numerous modern sources say the Barrancas figure was inaugurated on 3 October 1886, twenty-five days before New York’s dedication. The accessible evidence does not reveal a contemporary newspaper report, municipal resolution, invitation, photograph, or inscription proving this date.

The date has an identifiable competing association: 3 October 1886 is reported as the inauguration date of the institution later known as Escuela Normal Superior No. 9, whose building or architectural program has its own Bartholdian Liberty. Modern accounts have sometimes moved the school’s date onto the Barrancas object.

Accordingly, “3 October 1886” is an **institutional/popular tradition, not presently verified for this object**.

### Earliest secure attestation of the surviving object

The chain currently rests on three kinds of evidence:

1. **Physical evidence:** the extant casting’s Bartholdi and Val d’Osne marks and its correspondence with catalogue no. 833.
2. **Historical image:** an Archivo General de la Nación photograph dated January 1931, inventory no. 286901, showing the figure in the Barrancas.
3. **Later municipal/MOA documentation:** twentieth-century records describing municipal acquisition, damage, repair, and custody.

Thus, the present research can securely place the marked object in the Barrancas by **January 1931**. An earlier arrival in the late nineteenth or early twentieth century is highly plausible, but its exact date and original Buenos Aires location remain open.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Design and reproduction rights, circa 1870–1880s

- **Holder/maker:** Auguste Bartholdi created the sculptural composition.
- **Mechanism:** artistic design, enlargement, and authorized reproduction.
- **Evidence:** surviving dated models in the Musée Bartholdi and Metropolitan Museum; the Belgrano cast’s signature; Val d’Osne catalogue attribution.
- **Status:** documented design lineage.

### Manufacture by Val d’Osne, after the model’s development

- **Holder/maker:** Société du Val d’Osne, France.
- **Mechanism:** commercial cast-iron reproduction from model no. 833.
- **Evidence:** foundry inscription and catalogue match.
- **Date:** uncertain. It cannot safely be fixed to 1875, 1884, or 1886 from the presently available evidence.
- **Status:** manufacture documented; date unestablished.

### Export to Argentina

- **Sender:** presumably Val d’Osne or a commercial intermediary.
- **Recipient:** unknown; later tradition says a municipality.
- **Mechanism:** purchase and maritime export are probable but no invoice, shipping manifest, customs record, or acceptance resolution has been published.
- **Date:** before January 1931; likely considerably earlier.
- **Status:** inferred event with an undocumented transactional chain.

### Possible municipal-building predecessor, 1875

- **Keeper:** Municipality of Belgrano, if Córdoba’s account accurately reflects Saborido’s memorandum.
- **Place:** apparently the Casa Municipal y Comisaría.
- **Mechanism:** “placement” as a public improvement.
- **Identity:** not demonstrably the Barrancas cast.
- **Status:** documented mention of an unidentified statue; disputed connection to this object.

### Alleged installation, 3 October 1886

- **Keeper:** variously described as the Municipality of Belgrano or Municipality of Buenos Aires.
- **Place:** often said to be the Barrancas.
- **Mechanism:** alleged inauguration.
- **Evidence:** later repetition only; the date is entangled with the Sarmiento school.
- **Status:** unverified.

### Municipal transition, 1887 onward

Belgrano became part of the federal capital in 1887. If the object already belonged to the Municipality of Belgrano, its public ownership would presumably have passed into the capital’s municipal estate. If it arrived later, the federal-capital municipality may have purchased it directly. No transfer inventory or accession record has been identified.

### Barrancas redesign, 1892

Carlos Thays redesigned the Barrancas as a landscaped public park. The statue may have been installed, reinstalled, or integrated into the redesigned landscape at this time or later. This is a plausible contextual hypothesis, not a documented transfer.

### Photographic custody, January 1931

- **Keeper/owner:** public municipal authorities.
- **Place:** Barrancas de Belgrano.
- **Evidence:** AGN photographic inventory no. 286901.
- **Status:** earliest presently secure dated visual attestation.

The image shows that the statue’s outdoor public display predates the later MOA’s establishment.

### MOA documentary custody, from 1953

The Museo de Obras de Arte began operating in 1953. Its coordinator later explained that records for older works were reconstructed from books and archives. MOA’s file describes the Barrancas work as a copy of the New York statue standing on a stone pedestal. MOA became the municipal technical custodian responsible for its conservation, although no museum accession number has been published.

### Damage and displacement, 1986

- **Event:** vandalism or an “attack” broke the figure and displaced it from the pedestal.
- **Perpetrator:** unknown in published accounts.
- **Motive:** unknown.
- **Loss:** apparently temporary; the object was not stolen permanently.
- **Response:** municipal repair/restoration and return to its original site during 1986.
- **Replacement question:** no treatment report establishes whether every surviving visible component is original nineteenth-century iron.

This is the most consequential known physical interruption in the object’s history.

### Francis Beaumatin inquiry, 1990

Francis Beaumatin, identified as a member of a French “Club of Friends of the Statue of Liberty,” learned of the Buenos Aires version through a magazine and wrote to municipal cultural authorities requesting photographs and history. The response reportedly relied on Córdoba’s 1968 book and repeated the 1875 placement claim.

Beaumatin never possessed the statue. His role was historiographic: his inquiry helped formalize and circulate the early-date tradition.

### National metallurgical-heritage survey, 2008 and 2018

Sebastián Katz and Nani Arias’s French nineteenth-century metallurgical-art registration program examined and catalogued the object for Argentina’s national heritage administration. Their record identified its material, model, inscriptions, foundry, location, and 1986 return. It received database identifier **M662**, not necessarily a legal museum accession number.

### Municipal restoration, 2015

During the renovation of the Barrancas, MOA restored the Statue of Liberty together with other park artworks. The object remained in municipal custody and was returned to public display. No deaccession, private loan, sale, or change of ownership is recorded.

### Protected public-landscape status, 2022–present

Buenos Aires planning regulations list “Estatua de la Libertad – Réplica” among the protected monuments and singular elements of **APH 44 Barrancas de Belgrano**. Intervention requires consultation with the competent heritage authority. The work remains controlled by the City of Buenos Aires in situ.

### Gaps in the chain

The chain breaks in several material places:

- between Val d’Osne manufacture and Argentine arrival;
- between arrival and first documented placement;
- between the unidentified 1875 “statue of Liberty” and the marked Bartholdi cast;
- between any alleged 1886 inauguration and the 1931 photograph;
- in the technical details of the 1986 reconstruction;
- in the undocumented scope of the 2015 conservation.

No private owner, collector, thief, dealer, auction house, or foreign government can presently be inserted into those gaps on reliable evidence.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is it genuinely derived from Bartholdi?

**Yes, to the standard supported by cast sculpture.** The figure carries “A. BARTHOLDI,” bears Val d’Osne’s mark and address, and matches a documented foundry catalogue model attributed to Bartholdi. No serious rival attribution has been located.

This does not mean that Bartholdi personally cast or finished it. The correct attribution is approximately:

> Designed/modelled by Frédéric-Auguste Bartholdi; cast and commercially issued in iron by the Val d’Osne foundry.

### Is it “the original Statue of Liberty”?

**No.** The phrase is a modern journalistic exaggeration.

The New York monument is the specific colossal copper statue conceived for Bedloe’s Island, constructed in France between 1875/1876 and 1884, shipped in 1885, and dedicated in 1886. The Belgrano object is a reduced cast-iron edition. It may be an original Val d’Osne casting, but it is not the unique original of the New York monument.

### Is it older than the New York statue?

The answer depends on what “older” means:

- Bartholdi’s small preparatory models certainly predate the New York monument’s dedication.
- A reduction could have been cast before 28 October 1886.
- The Barrancas object’s supposed 3 October 1886 inauguration would make its public installation twenty-five days earlier than the New York dedication, but that date is unverified.
- The 1875 claim would make it extraordinarily early, but the underlying memorandum does not identify a Bartholdi or Val d’Osne figure.
- The New York statue’s construction was underway before either alleged Buenos Aires inauguration and was completed in Paris in 1884. Comparing only dedication dates does not make the reduction the design’s “original.”

### Could the 1875 statue be the present figure?

The strongest case **for** identity is:

- Córdoba connected Saborido’s 1875 “statue of Liberty” with the later local monument;
- Bartholdi had already produced recognizable models by 1870–1875;
- reduced committee models were being distributed or sold around 1875.

The strongest case **against** identity is:

- Saborido’s text lacks artist, material, size, and foundry;
- it apparently locates the object at the municipal/police building, not the Barrancas;
- the Belgrano figure corresponds to the more developed 2.9-metre Val d’Osne catalogue edition;
- no evidence connects that cast with Saborido;
- Belgrano’s separate municipal status conflicts with some later accounts of acquisition by Buenos Aires;
- a generic allegorical Liberty was a common nineteenth-century image.

The evidence presently favours treating the 1875 object as **unidentified**, not automatically as the surviving Bartholdi cast.

### Is the current figure wholly original after 1986?

Unresolved. Photographs show serious displacement and reported breakage. No accessible conservation report inventories original pieces, replacement castings, welds, internal armatures, or reconstructed details. The core identity appears continuous because the repaired work retained its form and marks and was returned in the same year, but component-level authenticity cannot be assessed.

### Scientific and technical testing

No published scientific examination of the Belgrano object was found:

- no metallography or alloy/compositional report;
- no casting-sand or foundry analysis;
- no radiography;
- no paint-layer chronology;
- no thermoluminescence or other dating method;
- no archival comparison of mould seams with authenticated casts;
- no structural engineering report.

The attribution rests on physical inscriptions, morphology, catalogue comparison, municipal records, and historical photography—not laboratory testing.

### Rival claimants and confused objects

The principal identity rival is not a forgery but the separate Liberty on the Sarmiento school. Its institutional inauguration date appears to have supplied the Barrancas object’s often-repeated date. The Villa Aberastain figure is another Val d’Osne edition but has a separately modified Argentine tablet and a twentieth-century provenance. Neither is the same object.

No documented counterfeit of the Belgrano statue has been identified.

### Authenticating authorities

Authority has shifted among:

- Bartholdi, as author of the model;
- Val d’Osne, as manufacturer and publisher of the cast edition;
- municipal cultural authorities and MOA, as owner and conservator;
- the Archivo General de la Nación, as custodian of historical photographic evidence;
- national/French metallurgical-heritage registrars, as catalogue comparators;
- city heritage-planning authorities, as legal protectors.

No single body has published a complete provenance dossier or scientific certificate of authenticity.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Intended function

The Val d’Osne reduction was a piece of serial civic sculpture. Its function in Buenos Aires appears to have been:

- ornamenting public space;
- personifying political liberty;
- participating in the nineteenth-century importation of French sculpture and urban furniture;
- linking Buenos Aires visually to European and transatlantic republican culture.

No original Argentine dedication text, named commemorative constituency, or surviving inauguration program has been found. It cannot therefore be assigned confidently to an Argentine independence anniversary, immigrant commemoration, abolitionist ceremony, or Franco-Argentine diplomatic event.

### Symbolic program

The figure’s program is inherited from Bartholdi’s American monument:

- **torch:** enlightenment, reason, or liberty illuminating the world;
- **radiate crown:** emitted light, often later described as seven rays for seas or continents;
- **tablet:** law and the American Declaration of Independence;
- **broken chain:** liberation from tyranny and, in the project’s post-Civil War context, association with emancipation.

The Metropolitan Museum explicitly relates the committee model to American abolition of slavery. That historical context belongs to the design lineage, though no evidence shows that Buenos Aires’s purchaser foregrounded abolition.

### Ceremonial use

The alleged 1875 and 1886 inaugurations are insufficiently documented to reconstruct ceremonies. No verified procession, oath, wreath-laying protocol, civic holiday, pilgrimage, or recurring observance focused on the Belgrano statue was found.

Its 1986 return and 2015 restoration may have included municipal presentation, but no object-specific ceremony is documented in the sources consulted.

### Political legitimation

Possession of the figure has not conferred office, sovereignty, or legal authority. Its political significance is emblematic rather than constitutional. The object participated in the visual self-presentation of Buenos Aires as a modern, internationally connected city furnished with French academic and industrial art.

### Access and taboos

The statue stands in an open public park and can be viewed without admission. No restriction on seeing or approaching it is recorded. Touching, climbing, marking, or damaging it would be governed by ordinary public-property and heritage rules, not religious taboo.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### “The original Statue of Liberty is in Argentina”

- **Classification:** modern journalistic invention/exaggeration.
- **Claim:** the Belgrano work is the true or first original, while New York has a later version.
- **Factual core:** both derive from Bartholdi’s design; the Buenos Aires cast may have been publicly installed before the New York dedication.
- **Contrary evidence:** it is an editioned 1:16 cast-iron reduction, while the New York statue is the intended colossal copper monument. Bartholdi’s design and models predate both dedications.
- **Effect:** the claim has generated tourism articles, social-media discussion, and renewed local attention.

### “It was inaugurated twenty-five days before New York”

- **Classification:** widespread modern tradition; unverified for this object.
- **Claim:** inauguration occurred on 3 October 1886 versus New York’s 28 October.
- **Problem:** 3 October is associated with the Sarmiento school and its separate statue. No contemporary Barrancas inauguration evidence was located.
- **Factual core:** a nineteenth-century Buenos Aires Liberty may indeed have preceded New York’s dedication, but the present chain cannot prove the exact date.

### “It was installed in 1875”

- **Classification:** scholarly/local-history inference subsequently institutionalized.
- **Earliest identified source:** Córdoba’s 1968 history, relying on Saborido’s 1875 memorandum.
- **Claim:** the surviving Bartholdi figure was installed while Saborido held office.
- **Problem:** the memorandum only says “the statue of Liberty” and suggests a municipal-building location.
- **Effect:** Buenos Aires tourism and popular accounts have converted a cautious identity problem into a confident date.

### “The statue was made from the same mould as the Paris replicas”

- **Classification:** plausible but imprecisely phrased.
- **Factual core:** the Belgrano object belongs to Val d’Osne’s serial model no. 833, so other iron casts were derived from the same production model or mould system.
- **Qualification:** Paris contains several different Bartholdian models and replicas in terracotta, plaster, bronze, and other materials. They were not all produced from one identical mould.

### “The figure is bronze”

- **Classification:** common visual misidentification.
- **Evidence against:** physical/categorical records identify cast iron; bronze-coloured coatings account for the appearance.
- **Status:** false as a statement of the substrate.

### “1884 is inscribed on its base”

- **Classification:** unresolved modern claim.
- **Support:** repeated by derivative replica lists.
- **Problem:** the specialized field transcription records only Bartholdi, Val d’Osne, and 58 Boulevard Voltaire.
- **Required test:** direct photographic documentation of all plinth faces after cleaning.

### “It was hidden or forgotten”

- **Classification:** modern rhetorical framing.
- **Factual core:** vegetation and the statue’s position make it inconspicuous, and many residents reportedly do not know it exists.
- **Qualification:** it has remained in a public municipal park, appears in official heritage guides, and has received conservation; it was not archivally “lost.”

No curse, miracle, prophecy, sacred relic tradition, secret substitution story, or treasure legend specific to this object was found.

## 8. Current Status and Location

As of the latest official and specialist records consulted, the object remains:

- **Location:** Barrancas de Belgrano, near La Pampa and Avenida Virrey Vértiz/Arribeños, Belgrano, Comuna 13, Buenos Aires, Argentina.
- **Approximate coordinates:** 34°33′38.66″ S, 58°26′51.56″ W.
- **Custodian/owner:** City of Buenos Aires.
- **Display:** permanently outdoors and publicly accessible.
- **Legal/planning status:** listed as a protected singular monument within APH 44 Barrancas de Belgrano; intervention must be referred to the competent authority.
- **Technical caretaker:** municipal MOA/Coordinación de Monumentos y Obras de Arte.
- **Known condition history:** damaged and displaced in 1986, repaired and returned; restored in the 2015 Barrancas works.
- **Accession/identifier:** no museum accession number found; specialist metallurgical database identifier M662; AGN historical photograph inventory no. 286901.
- **Current dispute:** no ownership or repatriation litigation was located. The principal dispute is historiographic—its date and identity—not legal title.
- **Access:** free outdoor viewing; visibility can be limited by vegetation.
- **Recent examination:** no scientific examination or object-specific post-2015 conservation report was located.

The listing in the city’s protected-elements schedule supplies strong evidence of continuing institutional recognition. It does not certify the traditional 1875 or 1886 dates.

## 9. Modern Reception

### From urban ornament to local curiosity

For much of its documented twentieth-century life the figure functioned as one among several artworks in the Barrancas. The 1931 AGN photograph now gives it a historical aura independent of its original commission.

The 1990 Beaumatin inquiry helped reframe it as an internationally noteworthy Bartholdi replica. Later newspaper and tourism coverage increasingly presented it through comparisons with New York.

### Municipal heritage and tourism

Buenos Aires’s tourism guide describes the nearly three-metre figure as a work by Bartholdi and acknowledges uncertainty, while repeating an 1874/1875 early-date tradition. The city’s heritage guide identifies it more conservatively as a cast-iron Val d’Osne replica.

The 2015 park renovation placed the statue within a broader heritage package that included fountains, busts, railings, the bandstand, paths, drainage, and vegetation. Its modern value is therefore simultaneously artistic and landscape-based.

### Photography and digital circulation

The AGN’s January 1931 image has been widely reproduced. Modern photographs emphasize the contrast between a globally familiar silhouette and a small, leafy Buenos Aires park.

Online articles regularly use headlines such as “the original Statue of Liberty is in Argentina.” These headlines materially shape reception but exceed the evidence. Social-media discussion has further detached the 1875 and 3 October 1886 dates from their uncertain documentary bases.

### Replicas with independent lives

The Belgrano cast belongs to a transnational family rather than serving as the ancestor of every later copy. Comparable Val d’Osne model no. 833 figures stand or stood at Poitiers, Saint-Étienne, Cambrin, Châteauneuf-la-Forêt, and other locations. Purchasers sometimes altered inscriptions to localize the image.

Argentina’s Villa Aberastain cast demonstrates this adaptability: its tablet was converted from the American date to Argentine national symbols and 1810–1910. It is not a copy made from the Belgrano object.

### Political and commercial reuse

The Barrancas figure is invoked as evidence of French influence on Buenos Aires and as a point of local pride. No appearance specific to it on Argentine currency, official heraldry, postage stamps, or national flags was located. Its image circulates principally in tourism, newspapers, neighborhood history, photography, and online trivia.

No documented modern forgery or commercial reproduction claiming to be this particular Belgrano cast was identified.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### The principal documentary traditions

The historiography consists of four partially disconnected bodies of evidence:

1. **Bartholdi scholarship and museum catalogues**, documenting the evolution from the 1870 sketch through preparatory and committee models.
2. **Val d’Osne catalogues and metallurgical-heritage research**, identifying the 2.90-metre iron edition as model no. 833.
3. **Buenos Aires local history**, especially Córdoba’s 1968 publication and its quotation of Saborido’s 1875 memorandum.
4. **Municipal/archival records**, including the 1931 AGN photograph, MOA’s reconstructed file, records of the 1986 damage, and the 2015 restoration.

The central scholarly task is to connect these bodies without assuming they describe one object at every stage.

### Córdoba and the 1875 date

Córdoba’s work is valuable because it preserves or transmits Saborido’s administrative language. Its weakness is identification: “the statue of Liberty” is treated as if it necessarily means the later famous Bartholdi form.

The primary memorandum should be relocated and examined in full. Its archival reference, attached reports, accounts, architectural plans, and neighboring entries could identify the supplier or location. Until then, Córdoba’s interpretation remains a hypothesis, not a provenance document for the marked Val d’Osne cast.

### The 1886-date problem

The 3 October claim is pervasive but poorly sourced. Its exact match with the Sarmiento school’s institutional inauguration warrants suspicion of conflation. A systematic search should examine:

- Belgrano municipal minutes and expenditure books for 1884–1887;
- Buenos Aires provincial official registers;
- customs records for French iron sculpture;
- shipping arrivals from Le Havre/Bordeaux/Marseille;
- newspapers around 3 October 1886;
- Val d’Osne order and export books;
- correspondence in the Musée Bartholdi and French diplomatic archives.

No such primary record was located in the accessible web corpus.

### Cataloguing discrepancies

The specialist field record gives:

- model 833;
- cast iron;
- approximately 1:16;
- “A. BARTHOLDI”;
- “FONDU PAR LE / VAL D’OSNE / 58 Bd VOLTAIRE PARIS.”

Derivative sources give plate 625 rather than 624, “8 Rue Voltaire,” and sometimes “1884.” These differences may result from catalogue pagination, transcription mistakes, or marks omitted in one survey. Only direct inspection and the original catalogue sheet can resolve them.

### Conservation evidence

The most important unpublished or inaccessible evidence may be MOA’s treatment files:

- photographs before and after the 1986 repair;
- fragment and replacement inventories;
- welding or pinning specifications;
- coating formulations;
- 2015 condition survey;
- pedestal interventions.

Without these, claims about complete material continuity remain untestable.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

The following should not be repeated as settled fact:

- that the Barrancas cast is “the original” Statue of Liberty;
- that 1875 certainly dates this particular object;
- that 3 October 1886 certainly marks its inauguration;
- that the Municipality of Buenos Aires purchased it while Belgrano was still an independent municipality;
- that “1884” is unquestionably cast into the base;
- that it is bronze;
- that one mould produced every Paris and Buenos Aires replica.

### State of knowledge

The most defensible synthesis is:

> The Barrancas figure is an authentic Val d’Osne cast-iron edition of Bartholdi’s model no. 833, probably imported as municipal ornamental sculpture. It was certainly in the Barrancas by January 1931 and probably arrived decades earlier. Its exact casting, purchase, arrival, and original-installation dates remain undocumented. The celebrated dates of 1875 and 3 October 1886 rest on two different chains of later inference, neither of which securely identifies the surviving cast.

## 12. Sources

### Primary, official, and institutional evidence

- **Archivo General de la Nación Argentina**, photographic inventory no. 286901, reproduced in AGN’s *Legado* and Wikimedia Commons. The image is dated January 1931 and is the earliest secure dated visual evidence located for the statue in the Barrancas.
- **Government of the City of Buenos Aires**, APH 44 protected-elements schedule. Lists “Estatua de la Libertad – Réplica” at La Pampa and Virrey Vértiz and subjects interventions to heritage review.
- **Government of the City of Buenos Aires**, report on the 2015 Barrancas restoration. Identifies MOA as restorer of the statue and other park artworks.
- **Government of the City of Buenos Aires**, Belgrano tourism circuit. Supplies location, approximate dimensions, Bartholdi attribution, and the city’s qualified repetition of an early-date theory.
- **Government of the City of Buenos Aires, heritage guide.** Identifies the object as an iron replica from the French Val d’Osne foundry.
- **Musée Bartholdi**, educational catalogue of the American rooms. Documents the 1870 sketch, preparatory models of c. 1872–1874, and committee models of c. 1875–1878.
- **Metropolitan Museum of Art**, object no. 1999.49. Provides a securely catalogued 1875 bronzed-terracotta committee model and the abolitionist/republican context.
- **U.S. National Park Service**, Statue of Liberty construction chronology and facts. Establishes the dates and material history of the New York monument used as the comparator.
- **E-monumen/ASPM metallurgical-heritage database**, record M662. Based on field research by Argentine specialists; identifies material, foundry, catalogue model, inscriptions, location, and the 1986 return.
- **E-monumen record for Poitiers model no. 833.** Supplies independent catalogue dimensions and a comparative cast with the same foundry address.
- **Buenos Aires public-library catalogue.** Confirms the bibliographic identity and municipal publication of Córdoba’s 1968 history.

### Secondary and journalistic evidence

- **Alberto Octavio Córdoba**, *El barrio de Belgrano: hombres y cosas de su pasado histórico*, Municipalidad de la Ciudad de Buenos Aires, 1968. The relevant page is reported to quote Saborido’s memorandum. The book itself was not fully accessible online in this research.
- **Pablo R. Bedrossian**, “La Estatua de la Libertad en Buenos Aires,” 2020. Reproduces the critical wording attributed to Córdoba/Saborido and correctly raises the municipal-jurisdiction and location problems.
- ***La Nación***, “De Nueva York a Belgrano: ‘La Libertad’ escondida en Buenos Aires,” 2019. Publishes information from MOA and AGN, including the 1986 damage and Beaumatin inquiry. Valuable for institutional testimony, but some technical statements—such as calling the New York skin “bronze”—are inaccurate.
- **Wikimedia Commons**, historical AGN photograph and category. Useful as a digital surrogate for the 1931 image and for modern-location photography, but not an independent authority for the disputed date.
- **Mi Belgrano**, 2015 coverage. Corroborates that the statue was included in the municipal restoration of the Barrancas.
- **Modern popular articles and replica lists.** Used only to map reception and identify recurring claims; not relied on to settle provenance.

### Research priorities

A fuller archival reconstruction requires:

1. locating Saborido’s original 1875 memorandum and its administrative file;
2. searching Belgrano municipal inventories and council minutes through 1887;
3. searching Buenos Aires municipal acquisitions and park inventories after 1887;
4. locating Val d’Osne sales ledgers, invoices, export lists, and mould records for model no. 833;
5. searching Argentine customs and port records for French cast-iron statuary;
6. reviewing Buenos Aires newspapers around 3 October 1886;
7. separating records for the Barrancas object from those for the Sarmiento school figure;
8. obtaining MOA’s complete 1986 and 2015 conservation files;
9. conducting and publishing an inscription survey, coating stratigraphy, metallographic characterization, radiography, and mould-seam comparison with securely dated no. 833 casts.
