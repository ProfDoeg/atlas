# The Coronation Stone of Moctezuma II: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary summary

The object tracked here is the rectangular carved basalt monument held by the Art Institute of Chicago under accession number **1990.21**, currently catalogued as the **Coronation Stone of Moctezuma Xocoyotzin**. It has also been published as the **Stone of the Five Suns**, **Coronation Stone of Motecuhzoma II**, and, in Spanish, **Piedra de los Cinco Soles**, **Piedra de la Coronación de Moctezuma II**, or **lápida conmemorativa de la entronización de Moctezuma Xocoyotzin**.

Three findings govern the dossier:

1. **The physical object exists and its identity since 1971 is secure.** A photograph and an analytical report place it at Stendahl Art Galleries in Los Angeles by 10 February 1971. It subsequently belonged to Seth G. Atwood’s Time Museum and, since 9 January 1990, to the Art Institute of Chicago.

2. **Its archaeological provenance is undocumented.** It was not excavated scientifically, no findspot is recorded, and no photograph, inventory, excavation report, ownership record, or export document predating 1971 has been published. “Tenochtitlan,” “the ritual center of Tenochtitlan,” and “Mexico City” are attributions or reported traditions, not documented findspots.

3. **Both its antiquity and its identification as a coronation monument require qualification.** Its imagery and carving have been accepted by several leading scholars and museums as late Mexica work, probably about 1503. Nevertheless, Mexica-sculpture specialist Emily Umberger questioned its authenticity in a 2019 personal communication reported in a 2023 peer-reviewed article. No published laboratory examination has resolved the question. “Coronation Stone” is a modern interpretive title: nothing establishes that a ruler stood or sat on it, or that it was physically employed in an enthronement ceremony.

The most defensible short description is therefore:

> A documented basalt monument of uncertain pre-1971 history, attributed on stylistic and iconographic grounds to late Mexica Tenochtitlan, whose carved dates have been interpreted—plausibly but not conclusively—as commemorating the accession of Motecuhzoma Xocoyotzin in the year 11 Reed, conventionally correlated with 1503.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Present identity

- **Institution:** Art Institute of Chicago.
- **Department:** Arts of the Americas.
- **Accession number:** **1990.21**.
- **Current catalogue title:** **Coronation Stone of Moctezuma Xocoyotzin**.
- **Former or alternative titles:** *Coronation Stone of Motecuhzoma II (Stone of the Five Suns)*; *Stone of the Five Suns*; Spanish *Piedra de los Cinco Soles* and *Piedra de la coronación de Moctezuma II*.
- **Classification:** sculpture; ceremonial object.
- **Attributed culture:** Mexica, conventionally called Aztec.
- **Attributed place:** probably Tenochtitlan, now Mexico City.
- **Attributed date:** circa 1503.
- **Material:** catalogued as basalt.
- **Technique:** relief carving and incision.
- **Current location:** Gallery 161, Art Institute of Chicago; the museum’s API marked it “on view” when updated in August 2026.
- **Credit line:** Major Acquisitions Fund.

These catalogue facts appear in the museum’s live collection record and API. The same record carefully documents the post-1971 provenance but states the Tenochtitlan origin more categorically than the surviving provenance evidence warrants.

### Names and their history

The monument has no known surviving Indigenous proper name. No pre-Conquest or colonial text has been shown to identify this particular block. All names now used are modern descriptive or interpretive titles.

**Stone of the Five Suns** describes the five calendrical signs conventionally read as the successive cosmogonic eras. This was the title used for catalogue no. 6 in H. B. Nicholson and Eloise Quiñones Keber’s 1983 National Gallery of Art catalogue, *Art of Aztec Mexico: Treasures of Tenochtitlan*. That catalogue interpreted two additional dates as probably referring to Motecuhzoma’s accession but retained explicit uncertainty about the object’s precise function.

**Coronation Stone of Moctezuma/Motecuhzoma II** turns that interpretation into a title. By the Art Institute’s 1992 exhibition *The Ancient Americas*, the piece was styled *Coronation Stone of Moctezuma II (“Stone of the Five Suns”)*. Later Art Institute guides retained “Stone of the Five Suns” parenthetically; the current title omits it.

“Coronation stone” can consequently mislead in two ways:

- It sounds like a documented Indigenous object class, although none has been established.
- It can imply an object used as a throne, seat, platform, or contact relic during coronation. No source documents such use.

**Motecuhzoma / Moctezuma / Moteuczoma.** Classical and colonial spellings vary because the name was recorded through different Nahuatl and Spanish orthographies. The University of Oregon/Wired Humanities Nahuatl Dictionary lists *Motecuhzoma, Moteuczoma, Moteczuma, Moteucçoma, Moctezuma,* and other forms. Frances Karttunen normalizes it as *Motēuczōma*. The constituent roots are associated with *tēuctli/tēcuhtli*, “lord,” and *zōmā*, “to become angry/frown”; translations include “he frowns like a lord,” “he becomes angry like a lord,” and Diego Durán’s *Señor enojado*. The appended *Xocoyotzin* distinguishes the younger ruler: “the Younger,” with the reverential suffix *-tzin*. “Moctezuma II” is a retrospective European numbering convention, not part of the object’s inscription.

### Dimensions

The Art Institute gives:

- **67.4 × 57.8 × 22.9 cm**
- **26½ × 22¾ × 9 inches**

The 1983 National Gallery catalogue gives 22.8 cm high, 67.3 cm long, and 57.7 cm wide—effectively the same measurements with different axis ordering and rounding.

Thomas L. Grigsby’s 2023 article gives **55.9 × 66 × 22.9 cm**. The reason for that discrepancy is not explained. The holding institution’s measurements should be preferred pending remeasurement.

No reliable published weight was located. Estimates derived only from nominal basalt density would be inappropriate because the block’s exact volume, cavities, losses, and stone composition have not been published.

### Form and carving

The piece is a thick rectangular block carved on all six faces:

#### Principal face

The principal broad face carries seven calendrical signs in relief.

At the four corners are:

- **4 Jaguar — *Nahui Ocelotl***, lower right.
- **4 Wind — *Nahui Ehecatl***, upper right.
- **4 Rain — *Nahui Quiahuitl***, upper left.
- **4 Water — *Nahui Atl***, lower left.

At the center is:

- **4 Movement — *Nahui Ollin***.

In the late Mexica cosmological scheme followed by Nicholson and later authors, the four corner dates name the catastrophic endings of four former worlds or “Suns,” while 4 Movement names the present era and its anticipated destruction by earthquake or violent movement. The arrangement is a quincunx: four peripheral eras around a central present.

Two further signs interrupt the cosmogonic group:

- At the upper center, **1 Cipactli**, variously translated “Crocodile,” “Caiman,” or more cautiously “Crocodilian/Earth Monster.” It appears in an unusual full-bodied form rather than as a simple day-sign head.
- At the lower center, **11 Acatl**, “11 Reed,” enclosed in a squared year cartouche.

These two glyphs are the basis of the coronation interpretation.

#### Four narrow sides

Each of the four edges reportedly carries a nearly repeated crouching **Tlaltecuhtli**, the personified earth, with an open mouth and flint-knife teeth. Each figure is flanked by paired **atl tlachinolli** signs.

*Atl tlachinolli*, literally “water, burned thing/field,” is a paired verbal-visual expression conventionally translated “water and fire” or “burned water.” In Mexica political and ritual imagery it connotes warfare, particularly sacred or total war. Richard Townsend paraphrased the sides as asserting an earth or territory gained and maintained through war.

The near-identical repetition of Tlaltecuhtli on every edge visually transforms the rectangular block into a bounded model of terrestrial space rather than simply a flat inscription panel.

#### Underside

The opposite broad face bears **1 Rabbit — *Ce Tochtli***. The 1983 catalogue identifies this as a calendrical name associated with Earth and with the world’s creation.

This glyph differs technically from the other carving: it is **incised**, whereas the principal face and sides are carved in relief. Nicholson expressly noted that it is made “in a style that differs from the relief carving of the other surfaces.” No published technical explanation has been found. Possibilities include a different working stage, hand, function, or an intentional distinction between the visible upper composition and a hidden downward-facing sign, but those remain hypotheses.

### Original orientation

Nicholson and Townsend reconstructed the stone as lying horizontally on a floor:

- The Five Suns face would have been uppermost and readable from above.
- The 1 Rabbit face would have contacted or faced the earth.
- Tlaltecuhtli would have occupied the four bounding sides.

That reconstruction is iconographically coherent, but it is not supported by an excavated setting. The 1983 catalogue emphasized that no example of the type had been found *in situ*.

### Surface, color, damage, and conservation

The published photographs show worn edges, abrasions, small surface losses, and uneven relief consistent with an object that has experienced weathering or rough handling. A proper condition map has not been published.

Mexica monuments were frequently painted, and Townsend suggested that the block may originally have been brightly colored. No pigment analysis, surviving pigment report, or chemical confirmation specific to this object was located. Original polychromy is therefore a contextual possibility, not an established property.

No published record was located for:

- Petrographic identification beyond the catalogue term “basalt.”
- Quarry-source analysis.
- Tool-mark microscopy.
- Residue or pigment analysis.
- Radiometric or archaeomagnetic dating.
- Surface-weathering analysis.
- Three-dimensional forensic comparison with excavated Mexica monuments.
- Documented restoration, recutting, consolidation, or replacement.
- A measured weight.
- A public conservation report.

The Art Institute marks the work as having advanced imaging, but no publicly accessible technical findings establishing date or authenticity were found.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Proposed ancient manufacture

**Institutional attribution:** unknown Mexica maker, probably Tenochtitlan, circa 1503.

**Evidence:** style, iconography, and the interpreted combination of the year 11 Reed and day 1 Cipactli.

Nicholson considered the carving “obviously very late” in Mexica stylistic terms. He compared the five era signs with those on:

- The Mexica Sun Stone.
- Other “five Suns” monuments in Mexico.
- A related rectangular stone at Yale’s Peabody Museum.
- Mexica sculptures in which cosmic dates, terrestrial imagery, and dynastic history were combined.

The principal-face relief is accomplished and iconographically literate. Nicholson called it a “decidedly superior specimen” distinguished by bold and vigorous high relief.

No maker, workshop, quarry, commission, price, transport episode, or dedication is recorded. No surviving Mexica or colonial account describes the carving of a small rectangular monument identifiable as this object.

### The coronation hypothesis

The reasoning developed in the 1983 catalogue is:

1. Five glyphs name the cosmic eras.
2. 11 Reed, because of the carving’s assumed late style, is read as the Mexica year corresponding to 1503.
3. In Alfonso Caso’s correlation, 1 Cipactli occurred once in that year, on the eighth day of the festival Huey Tecuilhuitl.
4. Colonial sources associate Motecuhzoma’s enthronement with the day 1 Cipactli.
5. The two historical dates were therefore probably added to a cosmic monument to commemorate the ruler’s accession.
6. Their placement presents the new reign as a renewal of time and situates Mexica imperial rule within the succession of created worlds.

This is a plausible iconographic argument, but each step after the reading of the glyphs contains an inference. The object does not include a plainly identifiable portrait or name glyph of Motecuhzoma, and it does not say alphabetically that it commemorates a coronation.

### Competing calendar conversions

The exact European date is not settled:

- Nicholson, following the Caso correlation, gave **15 July 1503, Old Style**.
- The present Art Institute text gives **15 July 1503**.
- Grigsby’s 2023 calculation gives **25 July 1503**.
- Fernando de Alva Ixtlilxóchitl’s colonial history says the oath occurred on **24 May 1503**, the ninth day of Toxcatl, on *ce cipactli* in the year *matlactli-om-ce acatl*—1 Cipactli in 11 Reed.
- Other documentary traditions place Motecuhzoma’s accession or initial installation in **1502**, following Ahuizotl’s death.

These are not necessarily descriptions of a single instantaneous ceremony. Mexica succession could include election, ritual seclusion, anointing or installation, a military campaign, sacrifice, tribute presentation, public recognition, and allied ceremonies. Nonetheless, the differing conversions mean that the frequently printed exact date “15 July 1503” should not be presented as mechanically certain.

### Documented earliest appearance of the physical object

The earliest secure attestation is **Los Angeles, February 1971**.

The Art Institute cites two pieces of evidence:

1. An unpublished typewritten analytical study prepared by **Hasso von Winning** and sent to Stendahl Art Galleries on **10 February 1971**, inscribed “HvW Feb. 10, 1971” and “to Stendahl 10-II-71.” The museum reports that this document is now in the West Lake Society archive in Rhode Island, with a copy in its curatorial file.
2. A color slide photographed by **H. B. Nicholson** at Stendahl Art Galleries, labelled “5 Suns Stone,” marked “S” for Stendahl, and bearing a February 1971 processing code. The original is in the H. B. Nicholson Slide Archive at California State University, Los Angeles, with a copy in the museum’s file.

The 1983 catalogue called the reported findspot **“Purportedly found in Mexico City”**, stated that the stone entered a Los Angeles private collection in the winter of 1971, and admitted: “No precise provenance data are available.” Its suggestion that the stone was probably found near central Mexico City was an inference from style, subject, and reported origin.

Thus, two separate chronologies must remain visible:

- **Attributed manufacture:** Tenochtitlan, about 1503.
- **Documented physical existence:** Los Angeles, 10 February 1971.

The interval between them—approximately 468 years—is completely undocumented.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Attributed pre-Conquest phase, circa 1503–1521

The Art Institute says the object was originally in Tenochtitlan’s ritual center. Townsend likewise placed the proposed commission in the sacred precinct.

**Status:** plausible institutional and scholarly reconstruction, not excavated provenance.

No evidence identifies a particular temple, room, courtyard, floor, cache, or palace. No contemporary description ties the object to Motecuhzoma’s coronation. No documented ritual use survives.

### Conquest and colonial period, 1521–1821

**Custody:** unknown.

Tenochtitlan was captured in 1521, and its ritual architecture was dismantled, buried, reused, or built over. That general history supplies possible mechanisms for the stone’s disappearance, but none can be attached specifically to this block.

It is not identified in:

- Conquest-period inventories.
- Recorded royal gifts to Spain.
- Colonial ecclesiastical confiscations.
- Published antiquarian collections.
- The documented discoveries of major Mexica monuments in Mexico City.
- Known nineteenth-century museum inventories.

There is no evidence that the stone was deliberately buried, reused in a building, hidden by Indigenous custodians, excavated during construction, or held continuously in a private family collection.

### Nineteenth and early twentieth centuries

**Custody:** unknown.

This silence is particularly significant because such monuments as the Sun Stone and Coatlicue were discovered publicly, described, drawn, and transferred into institutional custody. No equivalent discovery record exists here.

If the Chicago object was removed from Mexico after 1897, Mexican law normally required governmental authorization to export an archaeological movable object. The 1897 legislation treated archaeological monuments as protected national patrimony and prohibited export without legal permission; subsequent statutes strengthened those controls. No export permit or date of departure has been published.

This does **not**, by itself, prove illegal export. The object might have left earlier, might have had an authorization now untraced, or might not be ancient. Because the exit date and circumstances are unknown, the legal history cannot presently be reconstructed.

### Stendahl Art Galleries, by 10 February 1971–14 November 1972

**Location:** Los Angeles, California.

**Holder/dealer:** Stendahl Art Galleries.

**Mechanism of acquisition by Stendahl:** unknown.

**Evidence:** von Winning report, Nicholson slide, and museum curatorial documentation.

The surviving record does not disclose:

- Who offered or consigned the stone.
- Whether Stendahl owned it or acted as agent.
- A Mexican seller or excavator.
- Findspot.
- Export route.
- Customs documentation.
- Price paid by Stendahl.
- Previous owners.

The Getty Research Institute describes the wider Stendahl trade in pre-Hispanic antiquities as including objects that were often looted, forged, or assembled from fragments. That institutional characterization concerns the dealer’s market generally, not a finding about this monument. It is nevertheless relevant provenance context because the stone first appears in that market without an earlier chain.

### Seth G. Atwood and the Time Museum, 14 November 1972–9 January 1990

Stendahl sold the stone to **Seth G. Atwood (1917–2010)** on **14 November 1972**.

**Evidence:** a letter from Patricia Atwood dated 18 December 1989, cited by the Art Institute and copied in its curatorial file.

Atwood was an industrialist and founder of the Time Museum in Rockford, Illinois. The monument entered the scholarly record while associated with that museum.

#### 1983–1984 Washington loan

- **Venue:** National Gallery of Art, Washington, D.C., in association with Dumbarton Oaks.
- **Exhibition:** *Art of Aztec Mexico: Treasures of Tenochtitlan*.
- **Dates:** 28 September 1983–8 January 1984.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is the stone ancient?

The dominant museum and catalogue position is yes: it is an authentic late Mexica monument. Supporting considerations include:

- Coherent use of Mexica calendrical signs.
- Close stylistic and iconographic parallels with accepted Mexica state monuments.
- Sophisticated integration of form, hidden underside, sides, and principal face.
- The combination of Tlaltecuhtli, *atl tlachinolli*, cosmic-era dates, and an apparent historical year.
- Bold, technically accomplished relief accepted by Nicholson, Quiñones Keber, Townsend, Matos Moctezuma, Solís, López Luján, McEwan, and lending institutions.

The strongest published reservation is Grigsby’s 2023 footnote:

> “While Emily Umberger has questioned the authenticity of the monument (personal communication 2019), the information contained in its ideographs supports the thesis presented here that it is a date-specific monument to Motecuhzoma II’s coronation.”

That statement establishes doubt by a named specialist, but it does not disclose Umberger’s reasons, examination method, or final assessment. It is not a published demonstration of forgery.

Factors making investigation necessary include:

- No pre-1971 documentation.
- Emergence through a dealer whose market included both looted objects and forgeries.
- No documented archaeological context.
- An authentication or analytical report prepared for the dealer at the moment of emergence.
- No published scientific or technical authentication.
- The difference between the incised underside and relief-carved remaining faces.

Factors that do **not** by themselves prove forgery include the dealer provenance, iconographic neatness, and unusual object type. Conversely, iconographic coherence alone cannot prove antiquity: knowledgeable modern carving is possible.

**Present status:** unresolved in published evidence. The museum catalogues the stone without an authenticity qualification; one leading scholar’s doubt survives in a footnote reporting private communication.

### Is it Motecuhzoma’s coronation monument?

This is a separate question from antiquity.

The case for the title rests on the juxtaposition of 11 Reed and 1 Cipactli and colonial evidence associating the latter with royal enthronement. The iconographic composition plausibly makes a new ruler’s era continuous with cosmic creation.

The limitations are substantial:

- Motecuhzoma’s name is not unambiguously carved.
- The exact calendar conversion differs among scholars.
- Colonial records disagree about 1502 and 1503 stages of accession.
- No source says this block was commissioned for, displayed at, or used in the coronation.
- Nicholson described it as an “essentially religious monument” and said its precise function was uncertain.
- The modern title can collapse “commemorates a coronation” into “was a stone used in a coronation.”

A more cautious identity would be **“Stone of the Five Suns, possibly commemorating the accession of Motecuhzoma Xocoyotzin.”**

### Is it the same object described by an ancient or colonial source?

No ancient or colonial description has been securely matched to it. It should not be confused with:

- The monumental circular **Sun Stone** in Mexico City.
- The **Stone of Tizoc**.
- The **Stone of Motecuhzoma I**.
- The **Teocalli of Sacred Warfare**.
- The related rectangular Five Suns monument at Yale.
- The stone box often called **Motecuhzoma II’s box**.

There are iconographic affinities, but no rival claimant is seeking the same physical identity. The problem is not multiple extant “coronation stones”; it is the retrospective assignment of a specific historical function to an unprovenanced block.

### Scientific testing

No published scientific test capable of resolving antiquity was located. In particular, there is no known public report of:

- Quarry provenance or elemental fingerprinting.
- Microscopic tool-trace analysis distinguishing stone tools from modern metal or rotary tools.
- Analysis of deposits inside recesses.
- Comparison of weathering beneath carved and uncarved surfaces.
- Pigment or binder analysis.
- Luminescence or other direct dating.
- Experimental carving study.
- Archival reconciliation with Stendahl’s purchase and import records.

Basalt itself cannot be dated to the moment of carving by ordinary radiocarbon analysis. Authenticity would have to be approached through geological sourcing, carving technology, surface history, residues, and provenance.

### Authority to authenticate

Authority has shifted among:

- Dealers and dealer-associated experts in 1971.
- Private collectors and the Time Museum.
- Academic cataloguers and major exhibition committees.
- The Art Institute as owner and cataloguing institution.
- Independent specialists such as Umberger.
- Potential laboratory investigators.
- Mexican heritage authorities, were provenance or restitution proceedings initiated.

No single institutional label settles archaeological authenticity.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### What is documented

The object has been displayed in modern museums as a sculpture and ceremonial object. Its ancient ritual use is not directly documented.

### Proposed ancient placement

Its all-sided program suggests horizontal placement. The downward-facing 1 Rabbit sign would have addressed or contacted the earth, while Tlaltecuhtli repeated around the perimeter defined the block as terrestrial space. The upper quincunx organized cosmic time.

This reconstruction supports possible use as:

- A floor monument.
- A low altar or offering surface.
- A commemorative slab.
- A symbolic terrestrial platform.

No cavity, channel, binding fixture, or documented wear pattern has been published to demonstrate sacrifice, receipt of offerings, use as a seat, or repeated human contact.

### Political function

Townsend read the ensemble as a charter of imperial legitimacy:

- The cosmos begins with Earth.
- Four earlier ages end catastrophically.
- The present age occupies the center.
- The ruler’s year and day are inserted into cosmic time.
- Tlaltecuhtli and *atl tlachinolli* associate terrestrial possession with sacred warfare.
- Motecuhzoma is thereby presented as heir to the present world.

This interpretation fits a wider Mexica practice of embedding rulers, conquests, and dedications within cosmic history. It remains an interpretation of imagery, not a translated dedicatory sentence.

### Coronation practice versus object use

Colonial narratives describe Mexica accession rites involving:

- Election by elite officeholders.
- Ascent to the temple.
- Incensing the image of Huitzilopochtli.
- Anointing or blackening of the ruler’s body.
- Penitential seclusion, fasting, bloodletting, and ritual bathing.
- Bestowal of garments and royal insignia.
- Warfare and capture of sacrificial victims.
- Public sacrifice, dancing, feasting, and distribution of honors.

The existence of such rites does not demonstrate that this block participated in them. No colonial author mentions a Five Suns slab being stood upon, touched, carried, installed, or consecrated during Motecuhzoma’s accession.

### Believed powers, taboos, and access

No source attributes autonomous magical powers, healing, curse, prophecy, or protection to this particular object. Its imagery refers to cosmic destruction and renewal, but that is not equivalent to a legend that the physical stone controlled those forces.

No ancient rule governing who could touch or see it has been preserved. Modern museum access is visual; handling is institutionally restricted as for a major collection object.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

The stone has little independent folklore. Its “legendary” content is the history of the Five Suns encoded on its face and the modern narrative constructed around its coronation function.

### The Five Suns

In the scheme used to interpret the sculpture:

1. **4 Jaguar:** a former world destroyed by jaguars.
2. **4 Wind:** destroyed by catastrophic winds or hurricanes.
3. **4 Rain:** destroyed by fiery rain.
4. **4 Water:** destroyed by flood.
5. **4 Movement:** the present world, expected to end through earthquakes or violent movement.

Colonial Nahua and Spanish-language sources preserve variants in the order, presiding deities, inhabitants, durations, and methods of destruction. The sculpture’s quincunx should therefore be treated as one materially expressed Tenochtitlan tradition, assuming authenticity, rather than a complete and universally fixed “Aztec creation myth.”

### 1 Rabbit and the creation of Earth

The underside’s 1 Rabbit has been interpreted as the calendrical name or date of Earth’s creation and as associated with Tlaltecuhtli. Its concealed placement materially joins the monument to the earth beneath it.

This is an iconographic and mythological interpretation, not an inscription saying “the Earth was created on this day.”

### The ruler as heir to the Fifth Sun

The modern scholarly narrative presents Motecuhzoma’s reign as a new political age deliberately equated with cosmic renewal. That proposition begins as a cautious conclusion in the 1983 catalogue and becomes more assertive in later museum labels and popular accounts.

The stone has therefore generated a modern institutional legend of its own: that it is a securely identified, specially commissioned “coronation stone” marking an exact day, 15 July 1503. The underlying interpretation may be correct, but neither the function nor the European date is as firmly established as the modern title suggests.

### Alleged sacred-precinct origin

Statements that the object “was originally located within the ritual center of Tenochtitlan” are modern provenance reconstructions. The earliest catalogue said only “purportedly found in Mexico City” and “probably” near its center. Repetition has converted a reported or inferred origin into an apparently documented site.

- **Public access:** visible with museum admission, subject to gallery rotations and institutional restrictions.
- **Digital access:** collection images and alternative views are distributed through the Art Institute’s public API and IIIF system.

### Ownership and repatriation

No specific, active Mexican governmental, Indigenous-community, judicial, or diplomatic claim to this object was located in the sources searched.

That absence does not resolve title. If it is an ancient Mexica monument removed from Mexico after export restrictions took effect, its missing export history would be legally and ethically material. Mexico’s 1897 law restricted export of archaeological movable objects without authorization; later laws made archaeological monuments national property, inalienable and imprescriptible, and generally prohibited export.

Because no departure date, permit, seller, or find record is public, four possibilities remain open:

1. It left Mexico before effective restrictions.
2. It left later with authorization.
3. It left later without authorization.
4. It is not an excavated ancient Mexican object.

No published evidence presently selects conclusively among them.

### Recommended unresolved work

A defensible modern investigation would require:

- Full publication of the 1971 von Winning report.
- Examination of Stendahl acquisition, consignment, payment, and shipping files.
- Review of Mexican export-permit registers.
- Release of the Art Institute’s 1989–1990 acquisition-diligence file, subject to lawful confidentiality restrictions.
- Petrography and potential geological sourcing.
- Microscopic study of every carving technique, especially the underside.
- Residue, pigment, and accretion analysis.
- Comparative three-dimensional study against securely excavated Mexica sculpture.
- Direct publication of Umberger’s reasons for questioning authenticity.
- A current independent condition and authenticity report.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Museum canonization

The object’s public status changed rapidly:

- In 1971 it was a dealer-held “5 Suns Stone.”
- In 1983 it was a privately owned but nationally exhibited *Stone of the Five Suns* of uncertain function.
- By 1992 it was the *Coronation Stone of Moctezuma II*.
- By the 2002–2010 international exhibitions it had become a standard illustration of Motecuhzoma’s kingship.
- It is now an Art Institute “icon” and “essential” work.

Exhibition, publication, and museum ownership transformed a nearly unprovenanced object into a canonical image of Mexica political theology.

### Educational use

The stone is widely reproduced in school curricula, museum guides, university lectures, and popular histories. These treatments commonly state rather than qualify that:

- It was commissioned for Motecuhzoma.
- It commemorates his coronation.
- It comes from the sacred precinct.
- Its date is exactly 15 July 1503.

The educational simplification has obscured the incomplete provenance, calendar disagreement, and authenticity question.

### Replicas and digital circulation

Documented derivatives include:

- Museum and educational photographs.
- Annotated diagrams identifying the Five Suns.
- Commercial fine-art prints.
- A downloadable three-dimensional STL model marketed for 3D printing.
- Online reconstructions and illustrations.
- Poems and artworks explicitly titled “After the Coronation Stone of Motecuhzoma II.”

No official full-scale ritual replica or cast with its own custody history was located.

### Chicago cultural context

The museum’s current description links the central 4 Movement sign to comparable Mexica imagery in Chicago’s Pilsen neighborhood, including murals and pavement medallions. Independent reporting identifies the 18th Street station imagery and street medallions principally as adaptations of the much larger Sun Stone in Mexico City, not necessarily direct copies of the Chicago block. The formal overlap arises because both monuments employ 4 Movement and Five Suns imagery.

The stone’s residence in Chicago nevertheless participates in a broader Mexican and Mexican-American visual landscape. Community reporting has asked directly how an object presented as a sacred piece of Tenochtitlan came to be in a Chicago museum—an important reception question even in the absence of a formal claim.

### Confusion with the Sun Stone

Popular pages frequently conflate the Chicago block with Mexico’s circular, approximately 24-ton Sun Stone. Errors include:

- Giving the Chicago object the Sun Stone’s discovery history.
- Calling either monument the unique “Stone of the Five Suns.”
- Applying the Chicago coronation interpretation to the circular stone without qualification.
- Describing Pilsen replicas of the Sun Stone as replicas of the Chicago object.

The two works share calendrical imagery but are physically, historically, and institutionally distinct.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 1971: dealer-era examination

Hasso von Winning’s unpublished analytical report is the earliest known examination. Its full argument and observations have not been published. Nicholson’s contemporaneous slide supplies visual proof of the object’s existence at Stendahl but no earlier provenance.

### 1983: foundational catalogue

Nicholson and Quiñones Keber’s National Gallery catalogue remains the critical foundational record because it:

- Printed the earliest detailed physical description.
- Recorded the reported Mexico City origin as unverified.
- Admitted the lack of precise provenance.
- Read the seven principal-face signs.
- Proposed the 1503 coronation connection.
- Identified the four Tlaltecuhtli edge figures.
- Noted the underside’s divergent incised technique.
- Declared the precise function uncertain.

Later literature has often preserved the interpretation while dropping the qualifications.

### Townsend’s synthesis

Townsend expanded the object into a statement of imperial world-order. He reconstructed a counterclockwise reading from 4 Jaguar through the present 4 Movement era and paraphrased the whole monument as Motecuhzoma’s divinely sanctioned inheritance of the earth through sacred warfare.

An early edition or caption contained errors reading the lower sign as 2 Reed and the upper sign as 12 Alligator. Terry Stocker and Gordon Whittaker criticized these mistakes; later editions corrected the year to 11 Reed and the day to 1 Cipactli. Stocker further objected to “alligator” as zoologically inappropriate for Mesoamerica and preferred “caiman.” “Crocodilian monster” or the untranslated *Cipactli* avoids imposing an exact modern species on a mythic sign.

### Spanish and Mexican scholarship

Eduardo Matos Moctezuma and Felipe Solís treated the piece among Mexica solar monuments. The Spanish-language catalogue for *Moctezuma: Aztec Ruler* called it a *Piedra de la coronación* but phrased its function cautiously: the dates had been proposed as indicating the probable coronation date, and the monument may have commemorated it. The catalogue emphasized the combination of mythological and historical dates as a device of official legitimation.

### Hajovsky

Patrick Hajovsky’s *On the Lips of Others: Moteuczoma’s Fame in Aztec Monuments and Rituals* placed the monument within Motecuhzoma’s construction of fame and royal presence. His work is important for distinguishing physical royal imagery from later narratives about the ruler.

### Grigsby, 2023

Grigsby used the Chicago monument as evidence for a further proposal: that the great Sun Stone also commemorated Motecuhzoma’s accession. He calculated 1 Cipactli as 25 July 1503 and connected it to the trecena culminating in 13 Reed and to the mythic ignition of the Fifth Sun.

His paper is also the only located publication to disclose Umberger’s authenticity doubt. The paper does not resolve that doubt and, at points, describes the stone’s sacred-precinct origin more categorically than the provenance record permits.

### Present scholarly state

The following propositions have different evidentiary standing:

- **Secure:** a carved basalt block corresponding to accession 1990.21 exists; it has been continuously documented since February 1971; its custody since then is well established.
- **Strong visual reading:** the face contains signs conventionally read as 4 Jaguar, 4 Wind, 4 Rain, 4 Water, 4 Movement, 1 Cipactli, and 11 Reed.
- **Broad scholarly attribution:** late Mexica style, probably Tenochtitlan.
- **Plausible interpretation:** it commemorates Motecuhzoma’s accession.
- **Unresolved:** whether 11 Reed converts to the asserted exact date; whether every carving phase is ancient; whether the stone is wholly authentic.
- **Unsubstantiated as documented fact:** original installation in the Templo Mayor sacred precinct.
- **Not established:** actual physical use in a coronation.
- **Unknown:** quarry, maker, excavator, findspot, pre-1971 owners, Mexican exit date, export authorization, original paint, ancient damage, modern repairs, and purchase prices.
