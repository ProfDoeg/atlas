# The stylus/tablet emblem of Nabu: Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `the_stylus_tablet_emblem_of_nabu_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary finding

The “stylus/tablet emblem of Nabu” is not one uniquely identifiable, continuously preserved cult object. It is a **class of divine symbol** repeatedly represented on monuments, seals, sealings, rock reliefs, and probably cult furniture from late-second- and first-millennium BCE Mesopotamia. Some representations may reproduce an actual three-dimensional emblem once installed in a sanctuary or carried ceremonially, but no excavated object can presently be identified securely as *the* original cult emblem of Nabu.

Accordingly, this dossier tracks three related but distinct things:

1. **The iconographic type:** a wedge-shaped stylus, double rod, or grooved writing implement, sometimes mounted on a tablet, writing board, pedestal, or the back of a *mušḫuššu* dragon.
2. **Possible ancient three-dimensional cult emblems:** objects represented as standing on pedestals and receiving worship, but not themselves securely recovered or inventoried.
3. **The surviving representations:** especially the late-Kassite kudurrus Louvre Sb 25 and British Museum BM 90836, the disputed Tukulti-Ninurta I pedestal in Berlin, Sennacherib’s Bavian reliefs, and numerous Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian seals.

The evidence therefore does **not** sustain a conventional single-object chain of custody. It sustains a history of an emblematic form transmitted through multiple material witnesses.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Classification

The emblem is a **divine symbol, cult sign, and iconographic attribute** of the Mesopotamian god Nabû. Depending on its manifestation, it can be classified as:

- an imagined or actual cult emblem placed on a pedestal;
- a stylus or writing reed represented in relief;
- a stylus-and-tablet or stylus-and-writing-board ensemble;
- an emblem engraved on a boundary or entitlement monument;
- an image cut into a personal seal and reproduced in clay impressions;
- a divine standard mounted on a sacred animal;
- an attribute held by an anthropomorphic image of Nabû.

It is not the same thing as the mythological **Tablet of Destinies**, although both belong to the conceptual field of divine writing and determining destinies. Some scholars have proposed that particular “tablet” representations refer to oath documents or a Tablet of Destinies rather than Nabû’s ordinary writing apparatus. These identifications must be assessed object by object.

### 1.2 Names and terminology

The god’s name is conventionally normalized as **Nabû**. Relevant forms include:

- Akkadian: *Nabû*; earlier syllabic form *Nabium*, written, among other forms, `dNa-bi-um`.
- Common logograms: `dAG` and `dNÀ`; more elaborate learned spellings include `dNÀ.KÁM`, `dMUATI`, `dTU.TU`, and `dŠÀ.TÚ`.
- Biblical Hebrew: נְבוֹ, *Nəḇō* or Nebo.
- Greek and Latin transmission: Ναβοῦ and Nabû/Nebo in modern scholarship.

The name is usually connected with the Semitic root *nbʾ*, “to call, name, announce, designate.” Proposed senses include “announcer/herald” and “the named/designated one.” The etymology concerns the god, not the name of the emblem.

The principal Akkadian expression for the implement is:

- **qan ṭuppi**, literally “reed of the tablet,” conventionally translated “tablet stylus,” “writing reed,” or “stylus.”

Nabû may be called:

- **bēl qan ṭuppi**, “lord of the tablet stylus”;
- “holder of the tablet stylus” in royal dedicatory language;
- a god “learned in the scribal art.”

Modern descriptions such as “wedge of Nabu,” “stylus of Nabu,” “tablet and stylus,” “writing board and stylus,” and “Nabu standard” are analytical labels, not necessarily ancient proper names for one named relic.

The Akkadian term *šurinnu* can designate a divine emblem or standard, but applying “Nabû’s *šurinnu*” to every stylus representation is broader than the securely published object descriptions permit.

### 1.3 Basic forms

Alessandra Gilibert’s iconographic classification distinguishes three principal stylus forms:

1. **Double rod:** two close, parallel vertical lines, sometimes joined by transverse bars; some examples have a pointed, trapezoidal, or indented terminal.
2. **Wedge:** a single large cuneiform-like wedge, normally vertical, its narrow end downward; some examples have a central knob or bifurcated end.
3. **Combined or replicated forms:** two wedges, or a double rod and wedge appearing together.

The symbol can occur:

- alone;
- standing on a small stepped pedestal;
- resting on or rising from a rectangular tablet;
- associated with a hinged diptych or triptych writing board;
- paired with Marduk’s spade or *marru*;
- mounted with the spade on a crouching *mušḫuššu*;
- beside a worshipper;
- beneath astral signs such as the crescent, star, or Pleiades.

The double rod and wedge probably represent different writing implements or different graphic renderings of an implement, not necessarily different theological aspects of Nabû. A wedge-ended reed was suitable for impressing clay. The “double rod” may reproduce a stylus for a waxed board or an ink-writing implement. Its exact mechanics remain debated.

### 1.4 Materials

No surviving three-dimensional object is securely authenticated as Nabû’s original temple stylus or tablet. Consequently, statements that “the emblem was made of reed and clay,” “wood and wax,” or precious metal describe possible prototypes, not an extant cult object.

The surviving carriers are made from several materials:

- limestone kudurrus;
- gypsum or alabaster-like stone cult furniture;
- rock faces;
- chalcedony, carnelian, agate, hematite, and other seal stones;
- clay sealings impressed from such seals.

The depicted “tablet” can be a rectangular slab, a hinged wooden diptych or triptych, a closed board seen in profile, or an altar-like base. Some supposed tablets may instead be doors, architectural elements, oath documents, cult pedestals, or other objects.

### 1.5 Major surviving witnesses

#### A. Louvre Sb 25: the unfinished kudurru

- **Inventory:** Sb 25; excavation number AS 6067; Pézard and Pottier 24; Paulus OI 19; Seidl no. 40.
- **Date:** reign of Meli-Šipak, approximately 1186–1172 BCE.
- **Origin:** Babylonia.
- **Findspot:** Susa, excavated between 1900 and 1904.
- **Material:** limestone.
- **Dimensions:** 54 × 36 × 23.5 cm.
- **Technique:** relief carving.
- **Condition:** unfinished and anepigraphic; the space prepared for an inscription was never inscribed.
- **Present location:** Louvre, Richelieu wing, room 227.

The monument has three registers. Divine symbols occupy its upper zone; a procession of musicians, gods, and animals appears below; crenellated structures enclose the unused inscription field. A horned serpent surrounds the base.

Recent analysis identifies a **triptych writing board** among its symbols. Two rows of hinges distinguish it from a simple clay tablet. A grooved implement accompanies it. This combination has been assigned to Nabû because later textual and iconographic evidence identifies the stylus as his symbol.

Sb 25 is among the earliest relatively secure Babylonian witnesses for the emblem. Its lack of inscription, however, means the identification is comparative rather than supplied by a caption on the object itself.

#### B. British Museum BM 90836

- **Museum number:** BM 90836.
- **Registration:** 1890,1004.1.
- **Probable date:** twelfth century BCE, Kassite or immediately post-Kassite.
- **Material:** limestone.
- **Dimensions:** height 4.25 inches; width 11.25 inches; thickness 4 inches.
- **Condition:** badly damaged on the carved face; the back may have been rubbed down.
- **Present status:** British Museum, not on display.

The remaining reliefs are arranged in registers. The British Museum describes a symbol resembling a corded bundle and two rods standing upon a shrine beside a horned dragon. More recent scholarship interprets the relevant rectangular form as a **diptych writing board**, its central strip and two short marks representing a spine and hinges. The board is paired with a grooved stylus.

This object has an exceptional modern find history. It was reportedly recovered in the City of London during foundation work in Knightrider Street, together with broken seventeenth-century Dutch tiles. It was purchased from H. S. Barfoot in 1890. The circumstances strongly indicate that it reached London in antiquarian or commercial circulation before its rediscovery; they provide no archaeological provenance in Mesopotamia.

#### C. Berlin pedestal VA 8146 + VA 8277

- **Excavation number:** Ass. 19869.
- **Museum numbers:** VA 8146 + VA 8277.
- **Date:** reign of Tukulti-Ninurta I, approximately 1243–1207 BCE.
- **Findspot:** the Ishtar temple at Aššur.
- **Material:** described as greenish-grey gypsum stone or alabaster-like stone.
- **Height:** approximately 57.5 cm.
- **Present custodian:** Vorderasiatisches Museum, Staatliche Museen zu Berlin.

The king is shown twice—standing and kneeling—before a rectangular upright object and rod placed on a pedestal. Older and still-current museum interpretation calls these a tablet and stylus, hence symbols of Nabû.

The inscription complicates that identification. It calls the monument the cult pedestal of **Nusku**, “chief vizier of Ekur” and bearer of the just sceptre. Alternative interpretations identify the rod with Nusku’s sceptre, the rectangular form as a door or cult structure, or the whole as a representation of an oath document or Tablet of Destinies. The usual iconographic identification as Nabû’s tablet and stylus is therefore disputed.

Because the pedestal predates Sb 25, accepting it as Nabû’s emblem would make it the earliest monumental witness considered here. Because its inscription names Nusku, it cannot be treated as an uncontested earliest attestation.

#### D. Bavian rock reliefs

Sennacherib’s relief and inscription complex at Bavian/Khinnis in northern Iraq dates to 704–681 BCE. Its inscribed sequence names major gods—including Marduk and Nabû—in an order that can be compared with accompanying emblems. Scholars regard this as a decisive first-millennium key for matching Nabû with the stylus.

Unlike a museum object, the Bavian witness remains carved into its rock context. Weathering, damage, and the complex relationship among niches, reliefs, and repeated inscription panels require caution when using it as a one-to-one iconographic caption.

#### E. Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian seals

Hundreds of possible examples survive; no exhaustive corpus has been published. Representative catalogued objects include:

- British Museum 1883,0419.2: cylinder seal with crescent, star, Nabu’s wedge or stylus, Pleiades, and Marduk’s spade.
- British Museum 1846,0523.348: cylinder seal with worshipper and large stylus or wedge of Nabû.
- British Museum 1938,0108.118: cylinder seal with an exceptionally large stylus or wedge in the upper field.
- British Museum 1873,0723.1: cylinder seal showing the emblems of Nabû and Marduk standing on altars.
- Louvre AO 4366: Neo-Babylonian chalcedony stamp seal showing a priest before Marduk’s spade and Nabû’s stylus mounted on a horned dragon.
- Louvre AO 7700: conical white-chalcedony stamp seal with a worshipper before Marduk’s implement and Nabû’s stylus.

These objects are not pieces of one emblem. They are independent witnesses to its diffusion.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 What can be established

The iconography was developed in **Babylonia**, apparently during the late Kassite period. The earliest secure cluster is dated to the late twelfth century BCE:

- Louvre Sb 25, under Meli-Šipak;
- BM 90836, probably twelfth century BCE;
- additional late-Kassite kudurrus, including one dated to Marduk-apla-iddina I, bearing grooved-stylus forms.

Gilibert’s synthesis dates the first appearance of the stylus securely identifiable as Nabû’s symbol to Babylonian kudurrus of the twelfth through ninth centuries BCE. A twelfth-century kudurru caption provides the oldest indirect textual support; Sennacherib’s Bavian arrangement supplies a clearer deity-symbol correlation.

### 2.2 Earlier possibilities

Wedge-like implements occur in Old Babylonian art, sometimes carried by divine figures. These images demonstrate that writing implements could function as divine attributes before the late Kassite period. They do not always identify the deity as Nabû and therefore cannot automatically be inserted into the emblem’s secure history.

The Tukulti-Ninurta pedestal is earlier than the late-Kassite kudurrus but remains disputed. Its relief resembles later tablet-and-stylus compositions; its inscription explicitly invokes Nusku. It is evidence for a thirteenth-century BCE cultic complex involving king, pedestal, rod, and rectangular object—not incontrovertible proof that Nabû’s canonical emblem already existed in exactly that form.

### 2.3 Manufacture of the prototype

The actual prototype has not survived. Comparative evidence permits several reconstructions:

- a reed stylus used on clay;
- a grooved or double-ended stylus used with waxed wooden boards;
- a wooden diptych or triptych with hinges;
- a precious or ceremonial version of ordinary scribal tools mounted in a shrine.

The hinged boards shown on Sb 25 and BM 90836 closely resemble real writing boards depicted in later Assyrian art. Administrative texts attest wooden writing boards in late-second-millennium Mesopotamia, though terminology and proposed materials remain contested. The iconography therefore seems to monumentalize familiar scribal technology.

No workshop, commission, named maker, quarry, price, or materials account survives for an original Nabû emblem.

### 2.4 Development

The reconstructed sequence is:

- **Late second millennium BCE:** writing board and grooved stylus appear among divine emblems on Babylonian kudurrus.
- **Thirteenth–twelfth centuries BCE:** possible adoption or reinterpretation in Assyria; the Berlin pedestal remains ambiguous.
- **Ninth–seventh centuries BCE:** spread in Assyria; incorporation into monumental and seal imagery.
- **Late eighth–sixth centuries BCE:** rapid expansion of the emblem on worship seals.
- **Neo-Babylonian period:** the stylus and Marduk’s spade become a standardized paired motif, frequently mounted on pedestals or a *mušḫuššu*.
- **Achaemenid fifth century BCE:** the motif gradually disappears from the conventional Near Eastern glyptic repertoire.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 No recoverable custody chain for a single original

No source documents the making, dedication, storage, transport, theft, or recovery of one uniquely named stylus/tablet cult object. Ancient records describe temples and images of Nabû and identify him with scribal implements, but do not permit a statement such as “this stylus moved from Borsippa to Babylon and now survives in museum X.”

The custody history therefore divides into:

- a **traditional/cultic history** of Nabû’s divine writing apparatus;
- the **archaeological history** of separate monuments depicting it;
- the **modern collection histories** of those monuments.

### 3.2 Traditional and institutional settings

#### Borsippa

Nabû’s principal sanctuary was Ezida at Borsippa. Because the god was “lord of the tablet stylus,” a ceremonial writing implement or emblem may have formed part of its cult inventory. No surviving object has been linked securely to that inventory.

During the Babylonian New Year festival, Nabû’s image traveled from Borsippa to Babylon. Sources concern the god’s statue and ceremonial barque more clearly than an independently transported tablet-and-stylus emblem. Claims that “the stylus itself” made this journey remain an inference from imagery and cult context.

#### Babylon

Nabû participated in the theology of Marduk’s court and in the determination or recording of destinies. His stylus consequently represented divine authorization and written permanence. Kudurru images place it among the gods who sanction land grants and curses. This is symbolic attendance, not evidence that a portable physical stylus was carried to every legal transaction.

#### Assyria

Nabû’s cult entered Assyria by the Middle Assyrian period and became prominent at Aššur, Kalhu, and Nineveh. First-millennium seals and palace materials show that his symbol was used by royal officials, scribes, and private seal owners. The symbol’s “custody” was therefore decentralized: it circulated as an image through many hands.

### 3.3 Custody of principal physical witnesses

#### Louvre Sb 25

1. **Manufacture:** Babylonia under Meli-Šipak, 1186–1172 BCE.
2. **Ancient removal:** taken from Babylonia to Susa as war booty in the twelfth century BCE. The precise campaign and captor are not established by an inscription on this unfinished object.
3. **Deposition at Susa:** circumstances and date unknown.
4. **Excavation:** found at Susa between 1900 and 1904 by the French mission directed by Jacques de Morgan.
5. **Allocation:** transferred to France through the excavation division system.
6. **Modern custody:** French state, assigned to the Louvre’s Département des Antiquités orientales.
7. **Loans and exhibitions:** documented loans include the Metropolitan Museum exhibition on Susa in 1992; Berlin’s 2008 “Babylon” exhibition; and later exhibitions concerning music and fantastic animals.
8. **Present location:** Louvre, room 227.

The movement from Babylonia to Susa is ancient seizure or war booty. The later transfer from excavated Susa to Paris occurred under the French archaeological and antiquities regime then operating in Iran. The Louvre describes the mechanism as *partage après fouilles*.

#### BM 90836

1. **Manufacture:** probably Babylonia, twelfth century BCE.
2. **Ancient and early modern custody:** entirely unknown.
3. **Rediscovery:** found during warehouse foundation work in Knightrider Street, London, reportedly with broken Dutch tiles.
4. **Transfer:** purchased by the British Museum from H. S. Barfoot.
5. **Registration:** 4 October 1890.
6. **Present location:** British Museum stores; not displayed.

The approximately three-millennia gap between manufacture and London rediscovery cannot presently be bridged. Its collection history is documented; its archaeological provenance is not.

#### Berlin VA 8146 + 8277

1. **Manufacture/dedication:** made under Tukulti-Ninurta I for a cultic setting associated by its inscription with Nusku.
2. **Ancient location:** Ishtar temple at Aššur.
3. **Discovery:** excavated by the German Oriental Society expedition at Aššur, led by Walter Andrae; field number Ass. 19869.
4. **Transfer to Berlin:** entered the Vorderasiatisches Museum under the Ottoman-era excavation division system.
5. **Modern custody:** Staatliche Museen zu Berlin.
6. **Present status:** museum collection; the museum maintains a public catalogue entry.

No evidence located records theft, wartime loss, replacement, or restoration of this pedestal, though its composite inventory number shows that surviving fragments were joined or catalogued together.

#### Seals

Most museum seals have individual, often incomplete provenances. Many entered collections through the antiquities market without controlled excavation. Consequently:

- their stones and carvings can be physically genuine;
- their precise findspots may be unknown;
- inscriptions may have been added after the initial carving;
- stylistic date ranges can be broad;
- ownership before museum acquisition may be undocumented.

The British Museum’s and Louvre’s catalogue records must be consulted separately for each seal; they cannot be collapsed into a single custody chain.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Is there a “real” emblem?

There is no known claimant to a unique “true stylus of Nabû.” Museums display representations, not an object authenticated as the original stylus from Ezida.

The correct identity finding is therefore:

- **The emblematic type is ancient and securely attested.**
- **Numerous ancient depictions are physically extant.**
- **A unique original cult object is neither preserved nor demonstrably identified.**

### 4.2 Berlin pedestal dispute

The most important identification controversy concerns VA 8146 + 8277.

**Case for Nabû’s emblem:**

- The relief resembles later depictions of a rectangular writing tablet or board with stylus.
- A royal worshipper kneels before it as before a divine presence.
- Later Babylonian and Assyrian imagery securely associates similar implements with Nabû.
- The Berlin museum continues to describe tablet and stylus as symbols of Nabû, while acknowledging the inscriptional difficulty.

**Case against a straightforward Nabû identification:**

- The inscription calls the monument a pedestal of Nusku.
- Nusku’s ordinary iconography includes a lamp and can include rod-like objects or a sceptre.
- The upright rectangle has also been read as a door, architectural form, oath document, or Tablet of Destinies.
- Reading the scene through later Nabû iconography risks circular reasoning.

**Present state:** unresolved. It should be cited as a probable or possible antecedent, not as an uncontested Nabû emblem.

### 4.3 Tablet versus writing board

Older publications often called every rectangular object a clay tablet. Recent studies have identified hinges and panel divisions on Sb 25 and BM 90836, arguing that they show wooden diptychs or triptychs.

This reinterpretation matters because it changes the emblem from a generalized “clay tablet and stylus” into a more technologically varied ensemble that can represent:

- clay writing;
- wax-board writing;
- possibly ink writing;
- administrative documents in several media.

The proposed reading of some textual occurrences of *lēʾu*, “writing board,” has also been corrected. One late-Kassite kudurru passage formerly translated as “he sealed the writing board” is now read as referring to a land-survey document. The iconographic case for boards remains strong; that particular textual proof has failed.

### 4.4 Stylus versus spade

Nabû’s stylus is frequently paired with Marduk’s spade. Simplified or damaged examples can be confused. Relevant distinctions include:

- the stylus is normally a wedge or narrow double rod;
- Marduk’s implement often has a broader triangular or tasselled head;
- either can stand on a pedestal;
- both may be mounted together on a *mušḫuššu*.

Museum identifications are comparative and can change with cataloguing.

### 4.5 Scientific testing

No published radiocarbon, metallurgical, residue, DNA, or use-wear examination has authenticated an original cult stylus. Stone monuments are dated principally by:

- inscriptions;
- regnal chronology;
- excavation context;
- style;
- comparison with dated kudurrus and seals.

The stone itself cannot be radiocarbon-dated. Petrographic or quarry analysis does not appear in the cited museum records for the principal witnesses.

### 4.6 Seal authenticity and additions

The emblem became a mass-produced Neo-Babylonian glyptic motif. This raises several identity issues:

- unprovenanced seals may be difficult to date narrowly;
- owners’ West Semitic inscriptions were sometimes squeezed into spaces after the image had been cut;
- the inscription therefore need not identify the original owner or explain the iconography;
- modern replicas and forgeries can imitate the standard worship scene.

No specific modern forgery has become an institutionally recognized claimant to “the stylus of Nabû.” The broader antiquities-market risk is real but should not be converted into accusations against catalogued pieces without object-specific evidence.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Nabû

The emblem’s primary divine referent. Late-second- and first-millennium texts describe him as divine scribe, holder or lord of the tablet stylus, possessor of learned arts, and recorder or determiner of destinies.

### Marduk

Nabû’s father in first-millennium Babylonian theology. Marduk’s spade and Nabû’s stylus form the most common paired divine-symbol composition on later seals. Their pairing visualizes divine succession, family, and the linked religious authority of Babylon and Borsippa.

### Tašmētu

Nabû’s consort, especially in Assyria. She does not normally supply the stylus emblem but belongs to its temple and devotional network.

### Nisaba

Earlier Mesopotamian goddess of grain, accounting, and scribal knowledge. Stylus-and-tablet imagery cannot always be assigned automatically to Nabû in periods or contexts where Nisaba or other scribal deities may be relevant. The rise of Nabû did not erase her cult instantly.

### Nusku

The god explicitly named on the Tukulti-Ninurta pedestal. Nusku’s presence creates the principal early identity dispute.

### Mušḫuššu

The composite serpent-dragon serving Marduk and Nabû in later art. On seals, the stylus may stand on its back, either alone or with Marduk’s spade. The animal is a bearer or sacred associate, not part of the stylus itself.

### Meli-Šipak

Kassite king under whom Sb 25 and other early stylus/writing-board monuments were produced. No evidence identifies him as the emblem’s inventor.

### Marduk-apla-iddina I

Kassite successor under whom additional kudurrus carry grooved-stylus forms. One Louvre kudurru made in his reign confirms an earlier land grant and was later carried to Susa as booty.

### Tukulti-Ninurta I

Commissioner of the Berlin cult pedestal. His depicted acts of standing and kneeling before its central object are a documented royal engagement with the pedestal; whether that object represents Nabû is disputed.

### Sennacherib

Commissioner of the Bavian relief and inscription complex. The divine list and corresponding images supplied scholars with an important key for identifying Nabû’s emblem.

### Bēl-tarṣi-ilumma

Governor of Kalhu who dedicated statues to Nabû for Adad-nerari III and Sammu-ramat. His inscription calls Nabû the holder of the tablet stylus and documents the emblem’s theological meaning, though it does not place him in custody of a unique physical stylus.

### Jacques de Morgan and the French mission at Susa

Excavators responsible for the modern recovery of Sb 25 and many Babylonian monuments removed to Susa in antiquity. Their work transferred Sb 25 into the French state collection through excavation division.

### Walter Andrae and the German Aššur expedition

Excavators of the Berlin pedestal, responsible for its modern archaeological context and publication.

### Scribes, seal cutters, and seal owners

These constitute the emblem’s largest human network. Seal cutters reproduced it; owners carried it; scribes and administrators impressed related seals onto documents. Few cutters are named. Many seal owners are known only through short inscriptions, sometimes added secondarily.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Divine presence without anthropomorphic image

The emblem could stand for Nabû without depicting his body. A worshipper facing the stylus therefore confronted the god through a non-anthropomorphic sign. In some scenes the emblem receives worship alone; in others it is paired with Marduk’s spade.

This does not prove an aniconic theology in which Nabû lacked a statue. Anthropomorphic statues and images of Nabû also existed. Symbol and image were parallel modes of divine representation.

### 6.2 Legal authorization

On kudurrus, divine emblems appear above legal texts recording grants, exemptions, confirmations, and curses. Their functions include:

- witnessing the transaction;
- making the divine guarantors visibly present;
- threatening violators with divine punishment;
- projecting the grant into the future;
- placing written royal action within cosmic order.

Nabû’s stylus was especially apt because the grant’s force depended upon writing, copying, sealing, remembering, and preserving names. It was not merely decorative shorthand for literacy.

### 6.3 Personal worship and protection

On late Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian seals, a standing worshipper raises one or both hands before the stylus and spade. Because seals were worn or carried and applied to documents, the emblem could operate simultaneously as:

- a devotional image;
- an ownership device;
- an instrument of legal authentication;
- a protective sign;
- a statement of alignment with Marduk and Nabû.

The popularity of this image on mass-produced seals shows that it was not confined to temple priests.

### 6.4 Divine writing and destinies

Nabû’s theology expanded from scribal patronage to wisdom, divine decision, naming, and the writing of destinies. The stylus could consequently signify:

- recording divine decrees;
- assigning or confirming kingship;
- maintaining cosmic and political order;
- command over learned knowledge;
- the permanence of names and royal memory.

The emblem should not automatically be called “the Tablet of Destinies.” The relationship is conceptual; object identities vary.

### 6.5 Festival movement

A seal interpreted as showing the stylus within a crescent-ended boat has been connected with Nabû’s annual cult journey. This is a scholarly suggestion, not a captioned event. The documented New Year procession concerns Nabû’s cult image traveling between Borsippa and Babylon; independent transport of the stylus emblem remains unproved.

### 6.6 Access and handling

No surviving rule specifies who could touch a unique Nabû stylus emblem. General Mesopotamian temple practice placed divine images and equipment under priestly and royal control, but object-specific prohibitions cannot be reconstructed.

Seal versions were personally handled and widely circulated. Kudurru versions were carved public or institutional representations, not detachable sacred implements.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The divine scribe

**Tier: ancient theological tradition.**

Nabû is the gods’ learned scribe. His stylus manifests his capacity to write, preserve, calculate, and issue binding determinations. This is the emblem’s fundamental story, attested in royal dedications, hymnic epithets, and iconography.

The sources do not preserve a narrative in which a named craftsman forged the first stylus, nor do they specify a miraculous raw material from which it was created.

### 7.2 Writer of destinies

**Tier: ancient theological development.**

Nabû’s writing was connected with the destinies of gods, kings, cities, and people. At New Year, divine decrees and kingship were renewed within the Marduk-centered festival system. Later summaries often turn this into a concrete statement that Nabû carried one singular “Tablet of Destinies.” Ancient evidence supports his role in writing destinies more securely than it supports the identity of every stylus-and-board image as the mythic Tablet.

### 7.3 The heavenly book

**Tier: ancient conceptual association; later generalized retelling.**

The stylus can be understood as writing in a divine register: the gods’ records, heavenly writing, omens, or destiny. Modern popular accounts sometimes enlarge this into an all-knowing cosmic book. That formulation is interpretive and should not be quoted as the description of a surviving named object.

### 7.4 The Tukulti-Ninurta “tablet of destinies”

**Tier: modern scholarly hypothesis.**

One interpretation identifies the rectangular object on the Berlin pedestal with a Tablet of Destinies or an oath document worshipped under Nusku’s authority. Other scholars retain the Nabû tablet-and-stylus reading or identify Nusku’s sceptre. No consensus makes the object a secure physical survival of a mythical tablet.

### 7.5 Modern pseudo-archaeological elaborations

**Tier: modern invention or unsupported retelling.**

Online images of kudurrus and stylus emblems are sometimes described as technological devices, electrical apparatus, extraterrestrial symbols, or records of lost advanced science. No cited Assyriological, archaeological, museum, or scientific source supports those readings. They do not belong to the ancient documented tradition.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 The original cult emblem

**Status:** unidentified, lost, destroyed, or never a single unique object.

No museum, sanctuary, Iraqi authority, or scholarly catalogue currently identifies a surviving three-dimensional implement as the original stylus/tablet emblem of Nabû.

### 8.2 Principal representations

#### Louvre Sb 25

- French state property.
- Assigned to the Musée du Louvre.
- Displayed in the Richelieu wing, room 227 according to the current collection record.
- Condition: unfinished but substantially preserved.
- No current published restitution proceeding specific to Sb 25 was located.

#### British Museum BM 90836

- British Museum collection.
- Not on display.
- Condition: fragmentary and damaged.
- Original Mesopotamian findspot unknown.
- Its unusual London rediscovery history leaves a major provenance gap.

#### Berlin VA 8146 + 8277

- Vorderasiatisches Museum, Staatliche Museen zu Berlin.
- Excavated at Aššur.
- Preserved as joined fragments or associated inventory components.
- Interpretation remains contested between the museum’s Nabû-symbol description and scholarship emphasizing Nusku.

#### Bavian/Khinnis relief

- In situ in northern Iraq.
- Not a movable museum object.
- Subject to environmental deterioration and the broader conservation risks affecting Iraqi rock monuments.

#### Seals

Examples are dispersed among the British Museum, Louvre, Metropolitan Museum of Art, Iraq Museum, and other institutional and private collections. Hundreds of possible examples exist, many without controlled archaeological provenance.

### 8.3 Legal and ethical status

The surviving witnesses fall under different histories:

- ancient Babylonian objects removed to Susa as war booty;
- European excavations conducted under Ottoman or Iranian antiquities arrangements;
- museum purchases from dealers or owners;
- undocumented antiquities-market circulation;
- in-situ Iraqi cultural heritage.

There is no single modern legal dispute over “ownership of Nabû’s stylus,” because the emblem is not one identifiable chattel. Individual carriers may nevertheless raise questions of archaeological partage, colonial collection, undocumented export, or repatriation.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Archaeological illustration and museum display

The emblem became widely recognizable through nineteenth- and twentieth-century publications of kudurrus, Assyrian reliefs, and seals. Line drawings often simplified ambiguous forms into a clear “tablet and stylus,” sometimes creating more certainty than the worn originals justify.

Museum labels commonly use Nabû’s stylus as a visual key to identifying otherwise crowded divine-symbol scenes.

### 9.2 Dictionaries and educational art

Black and Green’s *Gods, Demons and Symbols of Ancient Mesopotamia* popularized the image of a wedge-shaped stylus and writing board. Educational websites now repeat the formula “Nabu’s symbol was the tablet and stylus,” usually without explaining that it describes an iconographic class rather than a unique preserved relic.

### 9.3 Graphic and religious reuse

Modern Mesopotamian revivalists and contemporary polytheists reproduce the wedge or stylus as:

- a devotional pendant;
- a digital icon;
- an altar emblem;
- a symbol of scribes, scholarship, and literacy.

These uses are modern reconstructions based on ancient iconography. No continuous documented chain links a present-day replica to an ancient temple emblem.

### 9.4 Public architecture

The modern bronze figure of “Nabu” installed with Tahmurath in the Library of Congress’s John Adams Building belongs to a modern allegorical program celebrating writing and transmitted knowledge. It is not a copy of a securely identified ancient statue or cult stylus. Such works helped convert Nabû into a generalized emblem of civilization, libraries, and literacy.

### 9.5 Games, fantasy, and occult material

Nabu/Nebo appears in fantasy fiction, comics, games, astrological compilations, and occult writing. The ancient stylus is frequently transformed into:

- a magical wand;
- a book of omniscience;
- a sigil;
- a weapon that rewrites fate.

These are modern receptions. Ancient evidence supports the associations with divine writing and destinies but not most modern narrative specifications.

### 9.6 Replica market and forgery risk

Commercial “Sumerian,” “Babylonian,” or “Nabu” seals and kudurru replicas often reproduce the stylus-and-spade worship scene. Sellers may omit that an item is modern or attach invented provenances. Authenticity must be determined from the individual object’s material, carving, inscription, surface, acquisition record, and scientific examination—not from the presence of a recognizable Nabû emblem.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Early documentation

Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century excavation reports and museum catalogues established the principal material corpus:

- French publications of the Susa excavations;
- British Museum catalogues of Babylonian boundary stones;
- German excavation reports from Aššur;
- catalogues of cylinder and stamp seals.

These works often used “boundary stone” for *kudurru*. Current scholarship stresses that many are entitlement monuments or documentary stones rather than field markers physically planted on boundaries.

### 10.2 Ursula Seidl

Seidl’s studies of Babylonian kudurru reliefs provided the essential typology and comparative framework for divine symbols. Her catalogue numbers remain standard. She argued for the development and formal variation of Nabû’s stylus, tablet, and board imagery.

### 10.3 Francesco Pomponio

Pomponio’s study of Nabû synthesized textual and iconographic evidence, including the symbol’s large seal corpus and regional distribution.

### 10.4 Alessandra Gilibert

Gilibert’s *Iconography of Deities and Demons* entry distinguishes double-rod, wedge, and combined phenotypes; dates their spread; and emphasizes the limits of the available corpus. She estimated that a comprehensive catalogue would probably exceed 300 items, mostly seals of unknown provenance.

### 10.5 Writing-board reassessment

Recent work by Cammarosano, Jendritzki, Streckfuss, and Zimmermann has reexamined the practical technology underlying the emblem. Hinges on Sb 25 and BM 90836 support identifying the rectangular forms as triptych and diptych writing boards. This materially grounds the divine symbol in contemporary administration.

At the same time, philological correction has removed one supposed textual reference to sealing a *lēʾu* writing board. The broader conclusion is therefore mixed:

- the iconography strongly supports hinged boards;
- some textual evidence previously enlisted in support has been reread;
- the precise writing surface—wax, wood, ink, or another preparation—cannot always be determined.

### 10.6 The Berlin pedestal debate

The pedestal demonstrates the methodological danger of identifying divine symbols by visual resemblance alone. Its inscription names Nusku, while the relief resembles later Nabû imagery. Major options include:

- Nabû’s tablet and stylus represented on an object dedicated to Nusku;
- Nusku’s sceptre and cult structure;
- an oath document under divine protection;
- a Tablet of Destinies;
- a door or architectural element.

The strongest conclusion is that the relief’s identity is not settled.

### 10.7 Gaps in the evidence

No complete corpus joins:

- all kudurru occurrences;
- monumental Assyrian examples;
- seals from controlled excavations;
- unprovenanced museum seals;
- inscribed West Semitic adaptations;
- possible actual cult furniture.

Many early excavation archives and museum acquisition records remain incompletely digitized. The principal modern catalogues are typological, regional, or collection-specific.

## 12. Sources

### Primary texts, corpora, catalogues, and museum records

1. **ORACC, Ancient Mesopotamian Gods and Goddesses, “Nabu.”** Concise scholarly synthesis with spellings, theology, cult history, iconography, and bibliography.

2. **ORACC/RINAP, Sennacherib 223, Bavian inscription.** Akkadian transliteration and English translation of the royal inscription associated with the Bavian monument.

3. **Louvre, Sb 25.** Current institutional record for the unfinished Meli-Šipak kudurru: dimensions, material, excavation history, legal custody, loans, bibliography, and display location.

4. **British Museum, BM 90836.** Current institutional record for the twelfth-century kudurru fragment, including its London find circumstances and acquisition.

5. **Staatliche Museen zu Berlin, VA 8146 + 8277.** Institutional identification of the Tukulti-Ninurta pedestal and acknowledgment that its inscription names Nusku.

6. **Louvre, AO 4366 and AO 7700.** Representative Neo-Babylonian stamp seals showing worshippers before Nabû’s stylus and Marduk’s emblem.

7. **British Museum seal records.** Representative cylinder seals documenting the wedge/stylus in Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian glyptic.

### Major scholarly studies

8. **Alessandra Gilibert, “Stylus,” Iconography of Deities and Demons.** Typology, chronology, geographical distribution, deity associations, and major bibliography.

9. **Lisa-Sophie Zimmermann, “Knocking on Wood: Writing Boards in the Kassite Administration.”** Detailed analysis of Sb 25 and BM 90836 as hinged writing boards and reassessment of textual evidence.

10. **Ursula Seidl, *Die babylonischen Kudurru-Reliefs: Symbole mesopotamischer Gottheiten*.** Fundamental catalogue and typology, cited through museum and later scholarly records.

11. **Susanne Paulus, *Die babylonischen Kudurru-Inschriften von der kassitischen bis zur frühneubabylonischen Zeit*.** Modern corpus and legal-historical treatment.

12. **Michael Herles, “Nachtrag zum Vogel Strauß unter besonderer Berücksichtigung des Kudurrus Sb 25.”** Detailed description and redrawing of Sb 25.

13. **British Museum, *Babylonian Boundary-Stones and Memorial Tablets*.** Early publication of the British Museum kudurru corpus.

### Evidentiary limitations

No published source consulted identifies:

- a surviving original three-dimensional stylus from Nabû’s Ezida temple;
- a continuous chain of custody for such an implement;
- a scientific dating program directed at an alleged original emblem;
- a current claimant presenting a physical object as “the one true stylus of Nabû”;
- a documented ancient name unique to one cult stylus;
- a secure maker, workshop, cost, or materials account for a cult prototype.

The dossier consequently identifies the emblem as a historically real and extensively documented **iconographic and cultic object-class**, not a single surviving relic.
