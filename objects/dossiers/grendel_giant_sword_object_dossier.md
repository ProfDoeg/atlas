# The giant-forged sword from Grendel's mere: Object Dossier

**Proposed filename:** `the_giant_forged_sword_from_grendels_mere_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary finding

The giant-forged sword from Grendel’s mere is not a surviving archaeological artifact and is not securely identifiable with any real weapon. It is an unnamed literary object known from a single surviving medieval witness of the Old English poem *Beowulf*: London, British Library, Cotton MS Vitellius A XV, principally lines 1557–1698. The physical sword exists only within the poem’s story-world. Its blade is destroyed within that story; its jeweled golden hilt survives long enough to pass from Beowulf to the Danish king Hrothgar, after which its fate is not reported.

The earliest securely datable material evidence is therefore not a sword but the manuscript containing its description, copied in England around AD 1000—commonly bounded paleographically to approximately 975–1025—by two scribes. The poem itself may be older, perhaps substantially older, but its composition date remains disputed. Consequently:

- **Documented fact:** a description of the sword survives in the medieval manuscript.
- **Literary fact:** within the poem, an ancient, superhumanly large sword is found, used twice, partially destroyed, and transferred to Hrothgar.
- **Plausible inference:** the poet imagined a recognizable early-medieval iron sword with an exceptionally elaborate hilt, transformed by legendary scale, antiquity, and authorship.
- **Unresolved:** whether the sword derives from an earlier oral tale, another lost text, a recognizable historical artifact type, or the poet’s own synthesis.
- **Unsupported claim:** that a physical “real sword of Beowulf” corresponding to this weapon survives or has been excavated.

The dossier tracks the **one unnamed sword in the mere episode**, not Hrunting, Nægling, Eanmund’s sword, the Sutton Hoo sword, or later weapons called “Golden Hilt.”

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Object class and identity

**Class:** legendary sword; narrative weapon; inscribed treasure or heirloom.

**Primary source:** *Beowulf*, lines approximately 1557–1698, uniquely preserved in Cotton MS Vitellius A XV.

**Canonical modern designation:** “the giant sword,” “giant-forged sword,” “giant-wrought sword,” “sword from Grendel’s mere,” or “the giant’s sword.” None is a proper name supplied by the poem.

### 1.2 Names, epithets, and disputed naming

The poem does not name this sword as it names **Hrunting** and **Nægling**. Instead it accumulates descriptions:

| Old English expression | Line(s) | Literal or close sense | Evidentiary note |
|---|---:|---|---|
| *sigeeadig bil* | 1557 | “victory-blessed blade/sword” | Descriptive phrase, not demonstrably a name. |
| *eald sweord eotenisc* | 1558 | “ancient giantish/ettin sword” | *Eotenisc* may mean made by, belonging to, or characteristic of *eotenas*. |
| *ecgum þyhtig* | 1558 | “powerful/strong in its edges” | Indicates cutting power, possibly multiple cutting edges by grammatical convention rather than multiple blades. |
| *wigena weorðmynd* | 1559 | “warriors’ honor/glory” | Marks prestige. |
| *wæpna cyst* | 1559 | “the choice/best of weapons” | Superlative valuation. |
| *god ond geatolic* | 1562 | “good and splendid/fitting/ornate” | *Geatolic* concerns excellence or adornment; translations vary. |
| *giganta geweorc* | 1562 | “work of giants” | Explicit legendary manufacture. |
| *hringmæl* | 1564 | “ring-marked,” “ring-patterned,” or possibly a substantive “ring-patterned sword” | Interpretation is debated; sometimes associated with patterned metalwork. |
| *wigbil* | 1607 | “war-blade” | Generic poetic designation. |
| *brodenmæl* | 1616 | “woven-patterned” or “pattern-welded blade” | Often connected to visible pattern-welding, though a precise archaeological reconstruction is not possible. |
| *gylden hilt* | 1677 | “golden hilt” | Descriptive phrase; occasionally modernized as “Golden Hilt.” |
| *enta ærgeweorc* | 1679 | “primeval/ancient work of giants” | *Enta geweorc* elsewhere describes monumental ruins; it connotes a lost superhuman past. |
| *wundorsmiþa geweorc* | 1681 | “work of wonder-smiths” | The manuscript’s phrase has generated debate over whether it means marvelous smiths or the marvelous work of smiths. |
| *ealde lafe* | 1688 | “ancient remnant/heirloom” | At this point only the hilt remains. |
| *irena cyst* | 1697 | “choicest of irons” | Confirms iron as the blade’s material in the poem’s conception. |
| *wreoþenhilt* | 1698 | “twisted/wreathed hilt” | May refer to construction, wire wrapping, interlace, or serpentine ornament. |
| *wyrmfah* | 1698 | “serpent-stained/serpent-patterned” | Indicates serpent or dragon-like ornament; exact technique is unspecified. |

**“Golden Hilt” as a name:** Some modern writers capitalize *gylden hilt* as though it were a proper name, and comparisons have been made with Old Norse *Gullinhjalti* (“Golden-Hilt”) in *Hrólfs saga kraka*. The Old English syntax does not require a proper name. “Golden Hilt” should therefore be labeled a **modern or scholarly naming convention**, not the weapon’s securely attested name.

### 1.3 Source description on discovery

The key introduction reads:

> *Geseah ða on searwum sigeeadig bil,*  
> *eald sweord eotenisc, ecgum þyhtig,*  
> *wigena weorðmynd; þæt wæs wæpna cyst,*  
> *buton hit wæs mare ðonne ænig mon oðer*  
> *to beadulace ætberan meahte,*  
> *god ond geatolic, giganta geweorc.*

A close translation is:

> Then he saw among the war-gear a victory-blessed blade, an ancient giantish sword, powerful in its edges, an honor to warriors. It was the choicest of weapons, except that it was larger than any other man could carry into the play of battle: good and splendid, the work of giants.

The Old English text can be checked in the manuscript-oriented transcription at [Beowulf on Steorarume](https://heorot.dk/beowulf-ms-text.html) and in the [McMaster Beowulf Project](https://www.humanities.mcmaster.ca/~beowulf/old/old23.html). A modern open translation is available through the [Old English Poetry Project](https://oldenglishpoetry.camden.rutgers.edu/beowulf/).

### 1.4 Size, mass, and proportions

No measurements or weight are given. The source states only that the sword is *māre*—greater or larger—than any other man could carry into battle. The comparison establishes:

1. superhuman scale;
2. Beowulf’s exceptional strength;
3. a reason the weapon could remain usable in a monster’s hall but inaccessible to ordinary men.

Modern illustrations commonly turn it into an enormous two-handed fantasy sword. The poem does not specify a two-handed grip, blade length, crossguard span, or weight. Beowulf seizes its hilt and swings it decisively, but the number of hands used is unstated.

### 1.5 Materials

The poem supplies three principal material indicators:

- **Iron:** *irena cyst*, “choicest of irons,” line 1697.
- **Gold:** *sciran goldes*, “bright/shining gold,” on the hilt’s plates or guards at line 1694.
- **Jeweling or treasure ornament:** the surviving hilt is *since fāge*, approximately “treasure-adorned,” “jewel-colored,” or “variegated with treasure,” line 1615.

The sword should not be described as made entirely of gold. The blade is iron; gold belongs to the hilt or its fittings.

No source identifies the ore, mine, timber, bone, horn, leather, adhesive, gemstones, or alloy. No physical sample exists for metallography, X-ray fluorescence, radiocarbon dating, or other analysis.

### 1.6 Construction and decoration

The blade is called *brodenmæl*, a compound connected with woven or interlaced patterning. It is frequently interpreted against early-medieval pattern-welded swords, whose differently worked iron or steel rods create visible linear, chevron, or serpentine patterns. That archaeological comparison is plausible but cannot establish the construction of a nonexistent or unlocated object.

The hilt is:

- gold-bearing;
- treasure- or jewel-adorned;
- *wreoþenhilt*, twisted or wreathed;
- *wyrmfāh*, serpent-patterned;
- marked on its *scennum*, an obscure plural noun variously rendered as plates, guards, surfaces, or portions of the hilt.

The precise referent of *scennum* is unresolved. Reconstructions that place a narrative panel on the pommel, guard, grip plate, or scabbard mount are interpretive.

### 1.7 The inscription

The relevant text is:

> *Hroðgar maðelode, hylt sceawode,*  
> *ealde lafe; on ðæm wæs or writen*  
> *fyrngewinnes, syðþan flod ofsloh,*  
> *gifen geotende, giganta cyn …*  
>   
> *Swa wæs on ðæm scennum sciran goldes*  
> *þurh runstafas rihte gemearcod,*  
> *geseted ond gesæd, hwam þæt sweord geworht,*  
> *irena cyst, ærest wære,*  
> *wreoþenhilt ond wyrmfah.*

A conservative translation:

> Hrothgar spoke; he examined the hilt, the ancient remnant. On it was written the beginning of ancient strife, when the Flood, the pouring ocean, struck down the race of giants … Thus on the surfaces of shining gold it was correctly marked in rune-letters, set down and declared, for whom that sword, choicest of irons, had first been made, with twisted hilt and serpent-patterning.

Two informational components appear:

1. an account associated with the ancient struggle and destruction of giants by a flood;
2. a statement identifying the person for whom the sword was first made.

The poem summarizes rather than reproduces the owner’s name or the inscription verbatim. That missing name cannot be recovered.

Whether the Flood passage itself is part of the inscription is disputed. Dennis Cronan argues that lines 1689b–1693 are probably narratorial explanation rather than inscriptional content; other readings treat the hilt as bearing a verbal text, a picture of the Flood, or a combination of image and runes. The scholarly problem is summarized in [Cronan’s “Hroðgar and the Gylden Hilt in Beowulf”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/traditio/article/abs/hrogar-and-the-gylden-hilt-in-beowulf/E902524A4339D18B6A972F3A0714CED4).

The word *runstafas* can mean runic characters, but “letters” or meaningful written characters is possible in some contexts. The text says the marks communicated for whom the sword was made; it does not give a surviving runic sequence that can be transliterated.

### 1.8 Damage, transformation, and condition

The sword undergoes complete destruction of its blade:

> *þa þæt sweord ongan / æfter heaþoswate hildegicelum, / wigbil wanian … þæt hit eal gemealt ise gelicost*  
>   
> “Then the sword began, after the battle-blood, to diminish into battle-icicles … until it all melted, most like ice.”

The poem further says:

> *Sweord ær gemealt, / forbarn brodenmæl; wæs þæt blod to þæs hat*  
>   
> “The sword had melted before; the patterned blade burned away—the blood was so hot.”

This is not ordinary battle breakage. The blade diminishes or liquefies like ice released by the divine ruler of seasons. The hilt alone remains intact enough to be carried, displayed, read, and presented.

**Final literary condition:** blade destroyed; ornamented hilt extant in Hrothgar’s hands; subsequent fate unrecorded.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Earliest securely datable attestation

The earliest physical witness is the *Beowulf* manuscript, Cotton MS Vitellius A XV. The British Library describes it as parchment, now mounted in nineteenth-century paper frames, with a binding measuring approximately 245 × 185 mm and parchment leaves approximately 202 × 120 mm. The manuscript was damaged in the 1731 Cotton Library fire. Its catalogue record documents blackening, cockling, and losses at page edges: [British Library catalogue](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/040-001102971).

The sword episode is copied in the first of the poem’s two scribal hands, before the change of scribe at line 1939. The narrative is therefore materially attested around AD 1000.

The composition date of *Beowulf* is separate from the manuscript date. Proposals range from the early eighth century to near the time of the surviving copy. Linguistic and metrical arguments have supported an early date, while manuscript-centered and historical arguments have supported later dates. The safest formulation is:

- the episode existed in written form by approximately AD 1000;
- an earlier date is probable to many scholars but not demonstrable to a universally accepted year;
- its pre-manuscript form and transmission cannot be reconstructed as a custody chain comparable to that of a physical artifact.

An overview of the continuing dating dispute is available through the [Oxford Research Archive](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A0062e122-3ae0-4e6a-931d-53064b2efc86).

### 2.2 Legendary manufacture inside the poem

The makers are never individually named. The object is attributed collectively to:

- *eotenas*—ettins or giants;
- *gigantas*—giants, a term capable of carrying biblical and learned Latin associations;
- *entas*—giants associated elsewhere in Old English poetry with ancient monumental works;
- *wundorsmiþas*—wonder-smiths or marvelous smiths.

The sword was made for an unnamed first recipient whose identity was written on its golden fittings. No workshop, cost, commission date, place of forging, or method of transfer is supplied.

### 2.3 Was it forged before the biblical Flood?

The juxtaposition of giant manufacture, a Flood narrative, and the sword’s survival encourages a pre-Flood reading: an antediluvian weapon made by giants or by smiths belonging to their world. That reconstruction is compelling but not stated as a formal chronology.

A difficulty follows: if the Flood killed the giants, how did the sword and its hilt survive? The poem does not answer. Possible scholarly readings include:

- the weapon is a surviving relic of the condemned world;
- its inscription commemorates the Flood but does not entail that the sword itself predates it;
- the Flood passage is narratorial rather than part of the inscription;
- the story combines biblical giants with Germanic *eotenas* without demanding a systematic genealogy;
- a giant lineage survived or reappeared in the form of Grendel’s family;
- the object deliberately embodies temporal contradiction—an artifact made by the defeated race that later becomes an instrument against its descendants.

These are interpretations, not additional narrative facts.

### 2.4 Biblical and Germanic components

The Flood account resembles the Christian tradition of Genesis 6–9, particularly the destruction of a violent antediluvian world populated by *gigantes* in Latin biblical tradition. *Beowulf* had already placed Grendel among Cain’s monstrous descendants and mentioned *eotenas*, *ylfe*, *orcneas*, and *gigantas* who fought against God.

At the same time, *eoten*, cognate with Old Norse *jǫtunn*, belongs to Germanic giant vocabulary. The sword therefore stands at an intersection:

- Germanic legendary monsters and giant-made treasures;
- Christian universal history;
- early-medieval knowledge of inscribed prestige weapons;
- poetic conventions that attributed ruins or ancient masterworks to giants.

The poem does not identify a named divine smith, dwarf, angel, or human artificer.

### 2.5 Possible material models

Archaeological swords from the Migration Period and early medieval northern Europe provide analogues for:

- iron or steel blades;
- pattern-welding;
- gold, garnet, or wire-decorated hilts;
- named owners or makers;
- runic inscriptions;
- serpentine or interlace ornament.

The Sutton Hoo sword is often used illustratively because of its patterned blade and exceptionally rich gold-and-garnet hilt. It is not a candidate for the sword in the poem. No provenance, inscription, find context, or scientific result connects it to Grendel’s mere or Beowulf.

The giant sword’s enormous scale, melting blade, antediluvian associations, and superhuman manufacture remain literary.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because the sword is legendary, its custody chain must be reconstructed exclusively from the poem. Each link below is a claim about the story-world, not physical history.

### 3.1 Manufacture by giants or wonder-smiths

**Date:** legendary deep past, before Beowulf’s lifetime; possibly imagined as antediluvian.

**Holder or responsible party:** unnamed giant makers or wonder-smiths.

**Evidence:** *giganta geweorc* at line 1562; *enta ærgeweorc* and *wundorsmiþa geweorc* at 1679–1681.

**Mechanism:** manufacture for an unnamed original recipient.

**Uncertainty:** Whether the makers and intended owner were giants, whether “wonder-smiths” describes giant smiths, and whether the weapon predates the Flood are not expressly resolved.

### 3.2 First intended owner

**Date:** legendary past.

**Holder:** unnamed.

**Evidence:** the hilt stated *hwam þæt sweord geworht … ærest wære*—for whom the sword had first been made.

**Mechanism:** commission, dedication, or original manufacture.

**Break in chain:** the poem withholds the owner’s name. Nothing establishes how long that person possessed it, whether it was inherited, lost, captured, buried, or deliberately deposited.

### 3.3 Unknown transmission into the underwater hall

**Date:** wholly unspecified.

**Location:** the submerged or water-enclosed hall at the bottom of Grendel’s mere, in the Danish region of the poem.

**Holder:** uncertain. Grendel’s mother is resident guardian of the hall; Grendel’s corpse is present; the weapon hangs or lies among *searwum*, war-gear or armor.

**Mechanism:** unknown.

Calling Grendel’s mother the sword’s “owner” is stronger than the evidence warrants. She possesses or controls the hall in which it is stored, but the poem does not say she commissioned, inherited, stole, or wielded it. Her possession is **contextual inference**.

### 3.4 Seizure by Beowulf

**Date:** during Beowulf’s Danish expedition, in the legendary sixth-century setting generally inferred for the heroic action.

**Transfer:** emergency taking from the hall’s armory or collection during combat.

**Evidence:** lines 1557–1564.

After Hrunting fails to cut Grendel’s mother, she throws Beowulf down and attempts to kill him with a knife or short sword. Beowulf sees the giant weapon among the war-gear, grips its hilt, draws or brandishes it, and strikes.

This is not described as a gift. In ordinary terms it is seizure of a weapon from an enemy-controlled space during battle.

### 3.5 Killing of Grendel’s mother

**Action:** Beowulf strikes her neck. The edge breaks through the “bone-rings,” penetrates the doomed body, and kills her.

**Evidence:** lines 1565–1569.

The poem implies that divine judgment enabled the victory: immediately before Beowulf sees the weapon, the narrator says holy God governed the outcome. Whether the sword itself has autonomous magical power is not stated.

### 3.6 Decapitation of Grendel’s corpse

**Action:** Beowulf carries the sword through the hall, finds the dead Grendel, and cuts off his head.

**Evidence:** lines 1570–1590.

The weapon therefore performs two distinct acts:

1. killing Grendel’s mother;
2. mutilating or taking a trophy from the already dead Grendel.

The second act produces the head later displayed at Heorot.

### 3.7 Destruction of the blade

**Date:** immediately after the decapitation.

**Agent:** the monsters’ hot, poisonous, or supernatural blood; the text is not entirely explicit whether the decisive blood is Grendel’s mother’s, Grendel’s, or both.

**Mechanism:** melting or burning away “like ice.”

**Evidence:** lines 1605–1617 and Beowulf’s retrospective report at 1665–1669.

The destruction occurs only after the sword has completed both tasks. The poem offers no recovery of blade fragments. “Battle-icicles” describes its waning form, but the text does not say solid fragments were collected.

### 3.8 Beowulf retains the hilt

Beowulf ignores the hall’s other treasures and takes only:

- Grendel’s head;
- the treasure-adorned hilt.

He swims upward with them. The head later requires four men to carry it on a spear or pole; the poem does not say that the hilt itself required multiple bearers.

### 3.9 Transport from the mere to Heorot

**Route:** underwater hall → surface of the mere → land route → Heorot.

**Bearers:** Beowulf carries the hilt out of the mere. The Geatish company accompanies him from the shore. The precise individual carrying the hilt on the overland journey is not separately named.

**Evidence:** lines 1612–1650.

### 3.10 Presentation to Hrothgar

Beowulf reports his victory and gives the hilt to Hrothgar:

> the golden hilt was placed in the hand of the old battle-leader, the ancient work of giants.

The transfer is trophy presentation and diplomatic gift. The object passes into the possession of *Denigea frean*, the lord of the Danes, described as the best of the kings between the seas who distributed treasure in Scandinavia.

The poem’s wording stresses a reversal: after the fall of the “devils” or hostile beings, their wonder-smiths’ work enters the Danish king’s possession.

### 3.11 Hrothgar’s examination

Hrothgar looks at the hilt: *hylt sceawode*. The narrator then supplies or mediates its inscriptions and decoration. Hrothgar does not quote the text, identify its original owner, or explicitly explain it. He proceeds to address Beowulf at length about fame, rulership, generosity, mortality, and the negative example of Heremod.

Whether Hrothgar can read the inscription is disputed:

- He may read it silently.
- He may recognize its memorial conventions without deciphering every word.
- The narrator, not Hrothgar, may be supplying information unavailable to the Danish king.
- The king may be contemplating an image rather than reading continuous writing.
- His subsequent speech may be prompted thematically by the hilt without directly interpreting it.

### 3.12 End of the chain

No later line states what Hrothgar does with the hilt. It is not recorded as:

- returned to Beowulf;
- placed in a treasury;
- inherited by Hrothulf or Hrothgar’s sons;
- buried;
- reforged;
- displayed;
- lost in the burning of Heorot;
- carried to Sweden, Geatland, or England.

The chain therefore ends in Hrothgar’s possession at Heorot. Any continuation is modern invention.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Does the physical object exist?

No physical candidate has an evidence-based claim to be this sword or its hilt. There is:

- no excavation report;
- no accession number;
- no medieval inventory of the hilt outside the poem;
- no independent inscription;
- no recorded find from a Danish mere;
- no chain connecting an extant sword to *Beowulf*.

The correct status is **purely legendary/literary object, materially attested only as text**.

### 4.2 Identity confusion with Hrunting

The giant sword is frequently confused with Hrunting because both occur in the same fight.

They are separate objects:

| Hrunting | Giant sword |
|---|---|
| Named weapon. | Unnamed. |
| Lent to Beowulf by Unferth. | Found in the underwater hall. |
| Celebrated heirloom with a prior battle reputation. | Ancient giant-made weapon with an inscribed golden hilt. |
| Fails to injure Grendel’s mother. | Kills her and decapitates Grendel. |
| Returned or offered back to Unferth after the expedition. | Hilt is given to Hrothgar. |
| Does not melt. | Blade melts; hilt survives. |

Statements that “Hrunting melted” or that Hrothgar received Hrunting’s hilt are false to the surviving poem.

### 4.3 Confusion with Nægling and Eanmund’s sword

Nægling is Beowulf’s sword in the dragon fight and breaks there. Wiglaf carries an *eald sweord eotenisc* associated with Eanmund, Weohstan, and Onela. The recurrence of “ancient giantish sword” as a formula does not make Wiglaf’s sword identical to the destroyed weapon from the mere.

The mere-sword’s blade no longer exists after line 1617, and its hilt belongs to Hrothgar. A later reappearance would require narrative explanation that the poem never supplies.

### 4.4 “Golden Hilt” and *Gullinhjalti*

A sword named *Gullinhjalti* appears in later Old Norse legendary tradition, notably *Hrólfs saga kraka*. Its name means “Golden-Hilt,” and Hrothgar’s Old Norse analogue Hrólfr kraki belongs to a related Danish legendary world. The resemblance is important comparative evidence.

It does not establish identity. The Old Norse saga is much later than the surviving *Beowulf* manuscript, and its weapon has a different narrative custody chain. Possible relations include:

- common legendary tradition;
- borrowing or indirect textual influence;
- independent use of a natural prestige-weapon name;
- modern scholarly association stronger than the medieval evidence permits.

### 4.5 Inscription disputes

Major questions include:

1. **Text or image?**  
   *Writen* and *runstafas* support writing, but the summarized Flood scene could be pictorial, textual, or mixed.

2. **What is on the *scennum*?**  
   The anatomy of the hilt term is uncertain.

3. **Which language?**  
   Runes are a script, not a language. Proposals have included a Germanic language and, much more speculatively, Hebrew. The poem gives no characters or quoted words.

4. **Does Hrothgar read it?**  
   The narration does not state that he reads it aloud.

5. **Is the Flood account inscribed?**  
   Cronan argues it is probably narratorial commentary; other scholars include it in the hilt’s inscription or imagery.

6. **Whose name was present?**  
   The poem says the hilt declared for whom the sword was made but omits the actual name. Attempts to supply Cain, a giant, Noah, Hrothgar, or another figure are conjectural.

### 4.6 Archaeological analogy versus claimant

The Sutton Hoo sword, the Seax of Beagnoth, and other inscribed or richly decorated early-medieval weapons show that the poem’s audience could imagine:

- runic marks on metal;
- gold hilt fittings;
- interlace and animal ornament;
- heirloom swords with social biographies.

They authenticate the **cultural plausibility of individual features**, not the identity or existence of this particular weapon.

### 4.7 Authority to authenticate

No medieval court, church, museum, or laboratory ever authenticated the object. Modern authority rests with:

- manuscript conservators and cataloguers, for the material witness;
- paleographers, for manuscript dating;
- editors and philologists, for the Old English text;
- literary and religious historians, for interpretation;
- archaeologists, for comparisons with real weapon forms.

None can turn the literary sword into a proven physical artifact without new independent evidence.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Giant or wonder-smith makers

**Date:** legendary past.  
**Connection:** collective manufacturers.  
**Evidence tier:** explicit literary attribution, identities unknown.

### Unnamed first recipient

**Date:** legendary past.  
**Connection:** person for whom the sword was originally made, according to the inscription.  
**Evidence tier:** explicit existence of a named-on-object recipient within the narrative; actual name absent.

### Grendel’s mother

**Date:** during the Danish episode.  
**Connection:** guardian or occupant of the hall containing the weapon; killed by it.  
**Evidence tier:** explicit narrative presence; legal ownership unestablished.

### Grendel

**Date:** immediately after his mother’s death.  
**Connection:** his corpse is decapitated with the sword; his blood may contribute to the blade’s melting.  
**Evidence tier:** explicit for decapitation; blood’s precise role is somewhat ambiguous.

### Beowulf

**Date:** Danish expedition.  
**Connection:** finder, seizer, wielder, carrier, and transferor. He uses the sword twice, retains the surviving hilt, removes it from the mere, and gives it to Hrothgar.  
**Evidence tier:** explicit literary custody.

### Hrothgar

**Date:** immediately after Beowulf’s return.  
**Connection:** recipient, examiner, and final attested custodian.  
**Evidence tier:** explicit literary custody.

### Unferth

**Connection:** no custody of this sword. He lends Hrunting, whose failure creates the crisis in which Beowulf finds the giant blade.  
**Evidence tier:** indirect narrative association.

### Geatish companions

**Connection:** accompany Beowulf from the mere; witness the trophy’s return. Four carry Grendel’s head, but they are not said to carry the hilt.  
**Evidence tier:** explicit collective participation, no individual custody.

### Danes and Heorot’s court

**Connection:** public audience for the presentation and Hrothgar’s examination.  
**Evidence tier:** explicit narrative setting.

### The giants/*eotenas*

**Connection:** attributed makers; the Flood narrative concerns the destruction of the giant race.  
**Evidence tier:** explicit, but the exact relationship between makers, drowned giants, and Grendel’s family is interpretive.

### Cain

**Connection:** Grendel belongs to Cain’s accursed lineage elsewhere in the poem. The sword is used against Cain’s descendants and bears or evokes a Flood account about hostile giants. Cain is not said to have owned or forged it.  
**Evidence tier:** explicit genealogy for Grendel; ownership claims would be conjectural.

### The eternal Lord/God

**Connection:** the Flood is presented as divine punishment. God governs Beowulf’s victory immediately before he finds the sword and releases winter’s bonds in the simile for its melting.  
**Evidence tier:** explicit narratorial theology; the sword is not explicitly called a consecrated divine artifact.

### Anonymous *Beowulf* poet

**Date:** before the surviving manuscript, exact date unknown.  
**Connection:** literary creator or transmitter of the object’s extant narrative.  
**Evidence tier:** necessary inference from the poem’s existence; identity unknown.

### Two manuscript scribes

**Date:** around AD 1000.  
**Connection:** transmitted the poem in Cotton MS Vitellius A XV. The sword episode falls in the first scribe’s portion.  
**Evidence tier:** paleographical finding.

### Laurence Nowell

**Date:** by 1563.  
**Connection:** earliest securely documented early-modern owner of the Nowell Codex, not of the fictional sword. His name and date occur in the manuscript.  
**Evidence tier:** documentary.

### Sir Robert Bruce Cotton and heirs

**Date:** seventeenth century to 1702.  
**Connection:** custodians of the manuscript containing the story.  
**Evidence tier:** documentary.

### Humfrey Wanley

**Date:** late seventeenth century; catalogue published 1705.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Practical narrative function

The sword resolves a combat after human weaponry fails. Hrunting cannot cut Grendel’s mother; the giant weapon can. It functions as:

- last-resort weapon;
- instrument of rescue;
- proof of Beowulf’s superhuman strength;
- instrument of vengeance for the Danes;
- means of producing Grendel’s head as a public trophy.

### 6.2 Trophy and diplomatic gift

Once the blade melts, the object changes class. It is no longer a functioning sword but a portable hilt, inscription-bearing antiquity, and trophy.

Beowulf’s presentation to Hrothgar performs several social functions:

- verifies the underwater victory;
- transfers enemy treasure to the king whose people were attacked;
- converts private martial success into public and royal property;
- affirms the bond between Beowulf and Hrothgar;
- allows Hrothgar to respond with instruction about good and bad kingship.

This is the closest the hilt comes to ceremonial use. The poem gives no recurring rite, enthronement use, oath, procession, shrine, or hereditary regalia function.

### 6.3 Judicial and providential function

The narrator frames the episode as divine judgment. The weapon made by giants becomes the means by which later monsters associated with Cain and divine enmity are destroyed. The inversion is significant: the hostile world’s own masterpiece executes judgment on its surviving representatives.

It remains uncertain whether the poet means:

- God directly provided the sword;
- providence guided Beowulf’s sight and hand;
- the weapon was inherently destined for this function;
- or the theological statement simply interprets Beowulf’s fortunate discovery.

### 6.4 Inscription as memorial object

The hilt stores multiple pasts:

- its unknown commissioning;
- ancient conflict;
- the Flood or destruction of giants;
- its own creation by giants;
- Beowulf’s killing of the monsters.

After the blade melts, the hilt becomes a relic-like record rather than an implement. It connects Hrothgar’s present to remote sacred history.

### 6.5 Access and taboo

No handling taboo is recorded. Beowulf can seize it; Hrothgar receives and examines it. There is no priesthood, ritual guardian, curse for touching, or prohibition against viewing the inscription.

Its inaccessibility before Beowulf comes from physical situation and scale rather than stated taboo: it is in a monster-haunted underwater hall and too large for ordinary men to bear.

### 6.6 Legitimation

Possession does not formally confer kingship. Nevertheless:

- Beowulf’s ability to wield it authenticates exceptional heroic status.
- His surrender of it to Hrothgar respects treasure-giving hierarchy.
- Hrothgar’s reception transforms it into royal treasure.
- Its imagery supports the king’s ensuing meditation on righteous rule.

Claims that it was a Danish coronation sword or sovereign regalia are modern inventions unsupported by the poem.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The giant-forging legend

**Tier:** earliest attested and integral to the poem.

The weapon is said repeatedly to be the work of giants and wonder-smiths. The poem does not narrate the forging scene. It does not name a forge, smith, patron, or intended war.

Later visual culture often supplies volcanic forges, gigantic anvils, magic fire, named smiths, or a two-handed design. These details are modern elaborations unless tied to a particular adaptation.

### 7.2 The antediluvian sword

**Tier:** strong scholarly reconstruction, not explicit narrative chronology.

The hilt’s ancient-strife and Flood material has encouraged the view that it is a pre-Flood artifact. In this reading, the hilt is a material survivor of the first corrupt world. Its survival makes it both witness and paradox: giants perish, but their craftsmanship remains.

The poem never says “this sword survived Noah’s Flood,” never names Noah, and never mentions an ark in this passage.

### 7.3 The Flood on the hilt

**Tier:** early attested description; form disputed.

The traditional reading says the hilt depicts or narrates the biblical Flood destroying the giant race. Important variants in scholarship are:

- verbal runic inscription;
- pictorial representation;
- image with runic caption;
- owner inscription plus separate narratorial Flood commentary;
- Germanic deluge resonance alongside biblical meaning.

A proposed parallel with the drowning of primordial giants in Ymir’s blood belongs to comparative interpretation. The poem’s references to the eternal Lord and punishment strongly favor a Christian biblical frame, though Germanic giant vocabulary remains active.

### 7.4 The miraculous melting

**Tier:** earliest attested and integral to the poem.

The blade melts after contact with the monsters’ blood, like ice when God loosens frost. Modern paraphrases sometimes say:

- it dissolves in Grendel’s “acid blood”;
- the mother’s blood alone destroys it;
- it melts because it has completed its destiny;
- God deliberately un-makes it.

Only the melting, heat, blood, ice simile, and theological comparison are explicit. “Acid” is a modern scientific metaphor, not the poem’s explanation.

The episode has been read as:

- purification;
- baptismal imagery;
- Harrowing-of-Hell imagery;
- destruction of pagan violence;
- transition from weapon to readable memorial;
- withdrawal of a supernatural weapon after its ordained task;
- failure of the violent heirloom economy;
- proof that no usable giant technology may circulate among humans.

These are scholarly interpretations, not separate medieval legends.

### 7.5 The self-destroying heirloom

**Tier:** modern critical formulation grounded in the text.

Unlike most elite objects in *Beowulf*, the sword cannot continue through inheritance or gift exchange. Only the hilt circulates. Recent material-culture criticism treats this as a deliberate refusal of the poem’s normal economy of weapons passing from lord to retainer and from father to son. The argument is developed in [“Anachronic Entanglements: Archaeological Traces and the Event in Beowulf”](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-03956-0_4).

### 7.6 Relationship to *Grettis saga*

**Tier:** later literary parallel; direction of transmission disputed.

In *Grettis saga*, Grettir enters a dangerous cave, confronts monstrous opponents, encounters an unusual giant-associated weapon, and emerges with proof of his deeds while observers assume him dead. Scholars have long debated whether:

- both works preserve a common international tale type;
- the Icelandic saga was influenced directly or indirectly by *Beowulf*;
- similarities arose through intersecting oral traditions;
- individual motifs have independent origins.

The parallel does not continue the same sword’s custody history. A recent book advances a specific direct-transmission argument from England to Iceland, but that reconstruction is not consensus: [*Beowulf and Grettis saga: From England to Iceland, 1016–1219*](https://boydellandbrewer.com/book/beowulf-and-grettis-saga-9781843847632/).

### 7.7 Relationship to *Gullinhjalti*

**Tier:** later Old Norse analogue.

The golden-hilted sword *Gullinhjalti* in *Hrólfs saga kraka* has been associated with *Beowulf*’s *gylden hilt*. The resemblance may preserve related Danish legendary material, but no medieval source says the weapons are identical.

### 7.8 Modern magical-weapon legend

**Tier:** modern retelling.

Popular summaries often call the sword “magical,” “enchanted,” or “the only weapon capable of killing Grendel’s mother.” The last idea reflects the plot but overstates the source: the poem shows that Hrunting fails and the giant blade succeeds, not that an explicit prophecy or invulnerability rule designated one unique weapon.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 The object itself

**Status:** legendary; no physical survival established.

Within the poem:

- the blade is destroyed in Grendel’s mere;
- the hilt is last seen in Hrothgar’s hands at Heorot;
- its subsequent fate is unknown.

There is no serious archaeological hypothesis locating the hilt today.

### 8.2 The unique material witness

The surviving manuscript is:

**London, British Library, Cotton MS Vitellius A XV**, specifically the Nowell Codex portion, folios 94–209. *Beowulf* begins at folio 132r. The volume is held by the British Library.

Its documented modern custody is:

1. Laurence Nowell by 1563;
2. Sir Robert Bruce Cotton;
3. Cotton’s son Sir Thomas Cotton;
4. Cotton’s grandson Sir John Cotton;
5. the British nation following the statutory gift;
6. British Museum from its foundation in 1753;
7. British Library from 1973.

The manuscript survived the Ashburnham House fire of October 1731 but suffered heat and edge damage. It was mounted in paper frames and rebound by the British Museum in 1845. Modern digital imaging, backlighting, and ultraviolet examination recover or clarify readings obscured by damage and framing. The [Electronic Beowulf critical edition](https://ebeowulf.uky.edu/criticaledition.html) identifies restorations and distinguishes manuscript text, early transcripts, and editorial conjecture.

The sword episode remains readable; its authenticity as part of the medieval manuscript is not in dispute.

### 8.3 Access

The manuscript is institutionally controlled and normally accessed under British Library collection rules rather than handled casually. Digital images are available through [Electronic Beowulf](https://ebeowulf.uky.edu/), which is based closely on the unique manuscript.

### 8.4 Candidate objects and replicas

No museum identifies a weapon as the authentic giant sword. Modern:

- stage props;
- film props;
- illustrations;
- decorative replicas;
- cosplay weapons;
- game models;
- teaching reconstructions

are representations, not claimants with medieval provenance.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Recovery into print

The object’s modern public life follows that of *Beowulf*:

- **1696:** Thomas Smith catalogued the Cotton manuscripts.
- **1705:** Humfrey Wanley printed a description and extracts from the poem.
- **1786–1787:** Thorkelin and his copyist James Matthews transcribed the manuscript.
- **1815:** Thorkelin published the first complete edition, *De Danorum rebus gestis secul. III & IV*, with a Latin translation.
- **Nineteenth century onward:** successive editions and vernacular translations made the giant sword a standard scene in the poem’s reception.
- **Late twentieth and twenty-first centuries:** digital facsimiles, searchable editions, films, graphic novels, games, and online retellings greatly expanded its visual forms.

Electronic Beowulf notes that the Thorkelin transcripts preserve hundreds of readings subsequently damaged or lost: [Thorkelin transcripts](https://www.uky.edu/~kiernan/Thorkelin/Th_1/).

### 9.2 Translation as redesign

Because the sword has no fixed visual depiction, translations materially shape its imagined form. Translators variously render:

- *eotenisc* as “giantish,” “made by giants,” “of the giants,” “of Jotun times,” or “ettinish”;
- *hringmæl* as ring-marked, ring-patterned, damascened, or ornamented;
- *brodenmæl* as woven-patterned, patterned, damascened, or pattern-welded;
- *scennum* as plates, guard, surfaces, or hilt;
- *runstafas* as runes, rune-staves, or letters;
- *wyrmfah* as serpent-patterned, snake-ornamented, dragon-decorated, or worm-stained.

These are not minor stylistic changes. They determine whether modern readers imagine a historical early-medieval weapon, a rune-covered magical sword, or a monumental fantasy blade.

The [Beowulf’s Afterlives Bibliographic Database](https://beowulf.dh.tamu.edu/) records more than a thousand translations, editions, representations, and adaptations from 1705 onward. It should be used to locate reception witnesses, not as evidence that every adaptation preserves the sword.

### 9.3 J. R. R. Tolkien

Tolkien’s scholarship helped reorient *Beowulf* criticism toward the poem as a unified work of art rather than merely a quarry for Germanic history. His posthumously published translation, *Beowulf: A Translation and Commentary* (2014), describes the weapon within his archaizing heroic vocabulary.

Comparisons between the giant sword and Tolkien’s own ancient named swords—especially weapons that glow, bear inscriptions, or return from lost ages—are influential but generally comparative. Tolkien did not simply reproduce the giant sword as a single Middle-earth object.

### 9.4 Seamus Heaney and modern classroom reception

Seamus Heaney’s 1999 translation became one of the most widely read modern versions. Its treatment of the mere episode helped fix the image of a massive, superbly made ancestral blade melting in monster blood for late twentieth- and early twenty-first-century readers.

School summaries often simplify the scene into a “magic sword” episode and sometimes incorrectly merge it with Hrunting. That conflation is a modern pedagogical error, not a medieval variant.

### 9.5 Gareth Hinds’s graphic novel

Gareth Hinds’s *Beowulf: A Graphic Novel*, issued in its major collected form in 2007, gives the episode an influential visual form. Candlewick describes it as a graphic retelling of the poem; bibliographic details appear on the [publisher’s page](https://www.candlewick.com/9780763630232/beowulf-a-graphic-novel/) and in [BABD record 384](https://beowulf.dh.tamu.edu/detail?id=384).

Any blade size, color, ornament, or hilt layout in Hinds’s work is an artistic reconstruction.

### 9.6 Robert Zemeckis’s *Beowulf* (2007)

The performance-capture film directed by Robert Zemeckis, written by Neil Gaiman and Roger Avary, radically revises the plot. Its screenplay retains a giant sword hanging in Grendel’s mother’s cavern, but the encounter and the relationship between Beowulf and the mother are transformed. The screenplay’s stage direction explicitly identifies “a giant’s sword” on the wall: [screenplay draft](https://faculty.winthrop.edu/kosterj/engl510/Readings/script_beowulf.pdf).

The film’s sword is an adaptation, not a continuity claimant. The film does not preserve the poem’s custody sequence in a documentary sense.

### 9.7 Other films and freer adaptations

The 1999 science-fantasy film *Beowulf* is a loose adaptation in which Grendel’s mother is defeated by different means; it does not preserve the giant sword’s Old English biography faithfully. Other films, novels, comics, television works, and games omit the weapon, merge it with Hrunting, turn it into an enduring magical sword, or supply it with a name.

These alterations demonstrate that the sword’s modern identity is unstable. The most common modern inventions are:

- a formal proper name;
- a prophecy;
- permanent indestructibility;
- ownership by Beowulf before entering the mere;
- return of the intact blade to the surface;
- later use against the dragon;
- identity with Hrunting, Nægling, Excalibur, or *Gullinhjalti*.

None occurs in the Old English text.

### 9.8 Replicas and merchandise

Commercial “Beowulf swords” are usually based on film concept art, generalized Viking swords, or modern fantasy aesthetics. Without explicit documentation, such products cannot be assumed to represent this particular weapon rather than Hrunting, Nægling, or a generic sword.

There is no authorized archaeological replica because there is no excavated original and no source dimensions from which to reconstruct one.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Textual foundation

Major textual resources include:

- the Cotton manuscript;
- the Matthews and Thorkelin transcripts;
- nineteenth-century collations and editions;
- Klaeber’s *Beowulf*;
- the fourth edition of *Klaeber’s Beowulf and the Fight at Finnsburg*, edited by R. D. Fulk, Robert E. Bjork, and John D. Niles;
- Kevin Kiernan’s *Electronic Beowulf*.

Electronic Beowulf is particularly important because it distinguishes:

- visible manuscript readings;
- readings preserved only by early transcripts;
- editorial emendations;
- conjectural restorations;
- evidence recoverable with backlighting and ultraviolet imaging.

### 10.2 The sword-hilt inscription

The inscription has generated several overlapping controversies.

#### Inscription versus narration

Traditional readings take the hilt as bearing the Flood story. Dennis Cronan argues that the account of the giants’ destruction is probably narratorial commentary, with the actual inscription chiefly identifying the original owner or commissioner. His argument depends partly on syntax, narrative voice, and the sequence introduced by *syðþan*. The issue remains open.

#### Writing versus image

Some scholars reconstruct a runic text; others allow an image plus inscription. Since the narrator summarizes meaning but supplies no letters, either medium—or a mixed program—can explain the passage.

#### Hrothgar’s literacy

Scholarship divides over whether Hrothgar:

- reads the hilt;
- interprets conventional memorial signs;
- sees but cannot decipher it;
- or is merely the focal observer while the narrator addresses the audience.

This matters because the poem’s Scandinavian characters inhabit a pre-Christian heroic past while the narrator and manuscript audience possess Christian historical knowledge.

#### Language of the inscription

The hilt bears *runstafas*, but the underlying language is not identified. Richard J. Schrader proposed Hebrew; this has not become a consensus view. A Germanic-language inscription is culturally more intuitive, but even that remains inference.

### 10.3 *Scennum*

The hapax or rare form *scennum* is central to physical reconstruction. Proposed senses include:

- plates;
- guard plates;
- hilt surfaces;
- parts of the pommel;
- metal fittings.

Because no parallel fixes the anatomy beyond dispute, illustrations that locate the entire Flood scene on one hilt component are speculative.

### 10.4 *Wreoþenhilt ond wyrmfah*

The phrase has been understood as:

- a structurally twisted or wire-wrapped hilt plus separate serpent ornament;
- two complementary descriptions of interlaced serpentine design;
- a hendiadys describing one integrated ornamental program.

Victoria Symons places the serpent ornament within a wider early-medieval network of runes, beasts, and decorated weapons in [“Wreoþenhilt ond wyrmfah”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/representing-beasts-in-early-medieval-england-and-scandinavia/wreoenhilt-ond-wyrmfah-confronting-serpents-in-beowulf-and-beyond/3FDDF2EE2136FA64AE978D7AA3CB02AD).

### 10.5 Who are the smiths?

The manuscript’s *wundorsmiþa geweorc* has been conventionally understood as “work of wonder-smiths.” Philological criticism has questioned whether the compound necessarily designates a special supernatural class of smiths. The preceding giant-work formulas nevertheless keep the manufacture firmly within a legendary, nonordinary past. The debate is discussed in [“Wonder-Smiths and Others”](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11061-016-9504-6).

### 10.6 Pattern-welding

*Hringmæl* and *brodenmæl* have often been connected to pattern-welded blades. Archaeology shows that early-medieval sword blades could display woven, ringed, or serpentine patterns produced through composite forging. The words remain poetic, however, and do not provide a metallurgical specification.

The cautious conclusion is:

- pattern-welding is a strong archaeological analogue;
- “the blade was scientifically shown to be pattern-welded” would be false;
- translations that state the technique as certainty go beyond the source.

### 10.7 Cause and meaning of melting

Martin Puhvel’s “The Melting of the Giant-Wrought Sword in *Beowulf*” and subsequent discussions compare folkloric and saga motifs. Interpretations include:

- corrosive or supernaturally hot blood;
- fulfillment of a magical object’s single purpose;
- purification or baptism;
- seasonal and solar imagery;
- Christian supersession of pagan material power;
- transformation from weapon into memorial text;
- narrative removal of an overpowered weapon;
- destruction of an object that cannot appropriately enter human gift exchange.

The literal narrative cause is hot battle-blood. The symbolic cause is disputed.

### 10.8 Materiality and unreadability

James Paz emphasizes the hilt as a difficult, partially unreadable thing: the blade is absent, the surviving fragment bears signs whose full content is withheld, and both Hrothgar and the modern reader confront mediated knowledge. The object resists complete reconstruction by design.

Recent material-literary criticism similarly treats the blade’s loss as a change in object category—from usable ancient weapon to archaeological fragment. This approach is valuable because it does not pretend the intact sword survives the episode.

### 10.9 Christian typology

The mere fight has been compared to:

- Christ’s descent into Hell;
- baptism;
- divine deliverance;
- Old Testament punishment;
- the defeat of Cain’s descendants;
- Christian reordering of Germanic heroic matter.

The sword participates in these readings through its providential discovery, Flood imagery, destruction of monsters, and ice-melting simile. None makes it a liturgical relic or historically venerated Christian object.

### 10.10 Comparative tradition

The principal comparative bodies are:

- *Grettis saga*;
- *Hrólfs saga kraka*;
- other Old Norse giant-fighting and cave-fighting narratives;
- Germanic traditions of extraordinary smiths and inherited swords;
- biblical and apocryphal giant traditions;
- archaeological corpora of runic and richly ornamented weapons.

Comparison can illuminate motifs. It cannot supply missing links in the sword’s individual provenance.

### 10.11 Discredited or unsupported popular theories

The following continue to circulate but lack adequate evidence:

- the Sutton Hoo sword is Beowulf’s giant sword;
- the poem records a recoverable historical sword now in a museum;
- the sword was called Hrunting;
- the blade survived and became Nægling;
- “Golden Hilt” is unquestionably its proper Old English name;
- the runes have been deciphered;
- the hilt names Beowulf;
- the sword was forged by Wayland;
- the blade was made of meteorite;
- the inscription describes Ragnarök;
- the weapon later became Excalibur.

No cited primary source establishes any of these propositions.

### Assessment of the evidentiary record

The textual evidence permits an unusually detailed biography for a legendary weapon: manufacture by anonymous giant smiths; dedication to an unnamed first owner; unexplained deposition in an underwater hall; seizure and two violent uses by Beowulf; supernatural destruction of the blade; recovery and transport of the hilt; royal transfer; and final examination by Hrothgar.

It does not permit recovery of the sword’s dimensions, maker, first owner, route into the mere, inscriptional wording, post-Hrothgar fate, or physical location. Those absences are not incidental archival gaps that modern reconstruction can confidently bridge. They are part of the object’s surviving literary form: an ancient artifact whose surface claims to preserve names and history while the poem withholds precisely those particulars.
