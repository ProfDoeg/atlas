# Marianne's Phrygian cap: Object Dossier

**Scope note.** “Marianne’s Phrygian cap” is not one uniquely identifiable cap with a continuous material biography. It is a reproducible emblematic object-type: the soft, usually red cap attached first to Liberty and later to Marianne, the female personification of the French Republic. No surviving textile cap has an authenticated claim to be *the* cap of Marianne, because Marianne is an allegory and French law has never prescribed a unique physical model. Accordingly, this dossier distinguishes:

1. ancient eastern “Phrygian” headgear;
2. the Roman *pileus* of manumission, from which the freedom symbolism principally derives;
3. real red caps worn and manipulated during the French Revolution;
4. the cap as an attribute carried on poles or worn by allegorical figures;
5. innumerable later sculptures, seals, coins, medals, stamps, logos, costumes, and replicas.

The cap’s reconstructable “life” is therefore a history of **forms, exemplars, representations, suppression, revival, and institutional control**, not a single chain of custody.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Classification and principal names

**Class:** headgear; revolutionary political insignia; iconographic attribute; republican and national symbol.

**French names**

- *bonnet phrygien*: “Phrygian cap,” literally a cap associated with ancient Phrygia in Anatolia.
- *bonnet rouge*: “red cap,” the term most commonly used during the Revolution for the wearable political object.
- *bonnet de la Liberté* or *bonnet de liberté*: “cap of Liberty.”
- *bonnet des affranchis*: “freedmen’s cap,” an interpretive designation tying it to Roman manumission.
- *bonnet jacobin*: “Jacobin cap,” especially in hostile or retrospective usage.
- *bonnet civique*: “civic cap,” encountered in revolutionary symbolic language.

The *Dictionnaire historique du vocabulaire français* records *bonnet phrygien* in a newspaper report of 10 March 1792, describing a red cap “whose point bends forward in the manner of the Phrygian horn.” This is important: contemporaries did not merely wear an undifferentiated red woollen cap and have later historians rename it. At least some observers explicitly called its bent-point form “Phrygian” in 1792. The same lexical record shows that *bonnet rouge* remained the politically charged everyday expression, capable of meaning either the garment or, metonymically, a revolutionary who wore it. [CNRTL historical vocabulary](https://www.cnrtl.fr/definition/bhvf/bonnet%20phrygien)

**Classical terms**

- Greek **πῖλος** (*pîlos*): a felt cap, usually conical or rounded.
- Latin **pileus** or **pilleus**: a felt cap associated, among other uses, with freed slaves and the Saturnalia.
- Greek **Φρυγία** (*Phrygía*) and Latin *Phrygia*: Phrygia, the Anatolian region from which the modern adjective derives.

The Roman *pileus* and the eastern cap now conventionally called “Phrygian” were not originally the same object. The former was ordinarily a close-fitting, rounded or conical felt cap; the latter was represented as a soft, high cap whose apex bends forward, sometimes with cheek or neck flaps. Eighteenth-century revolutionary iconography conflated their visual forms and meanings.

### 1.2 Physical form

No binding specification fixes Marianne’s cap. Its recurring characteristics are:

- a soft, close-fitting crown;
- a high apex curved or folded forward;
- usually red colouring;
- wool, knitted textile, felt, or an unspecified cloth when physically worn;
- often a blue-white-red cockade attached at the side or front;
- occasionally embroidery, patriotic inscriptions, or tricolour edging;
- in sculpture, plaster, terracotta, marble, bronze, cast metal, resin, or painted composition;
- in print, heraldry, seals, coins, and logos, a simplified curved triangular silhouette.

The Musée de l’Armée distinguishes the conical *pileus* from the bent Phrygian form and notes that revolutionary “liberty caps” assumed several forms. It describes actual popular caps as knitted wool and relates them to the headgear of poor people, artisans, sailors, and galley prisoners. [Musée de l’Armée teaching dossier](https://www.musee-armee.fr/fileadmin/user_upload/Documents/Support-Visite-Fiches-Objets/Fiches-Louis-XIV-Napo-Bonaparte/fo-hausse-col.pdf)

This evidence supports only a **general material reconstruction**. There was no national workshop, authorized pattern, regulated weight, or standardized set of dimensions. A cap worn by a sans-culotte was a textile garment; a cap surmounting a liberty pole could be textile, painted wood, carved stone, cast metal, or merely depicted.

### 1.3 Colour and attached devices

The emblem became conventionally red, but “red” was politically contested rather than physically inevitable. Louis-Sébastien Mercier later recalled that some accepted the cap while rejecting red as suggestive of blood. Revolutionary representations could make it white or tricolour; monochrome medals and coins left its imagined colour open. A study of revolutionary numismatic iconography finds the cap’s hue to have been a persistent political issue rather than a stable material property. [Gazette des archives study on Liberty and her cap](https://www.persee.fr/doc/gazar_0016-5522_1989_num_146_1_4157)

The tricolour cockade frequently accompanies the red cap but is not inherent to it. A cap can remain iconographically legible without a cockade, while a cockade could independently signify national adherence.

### 1.4 Inscriptions

There is no canonical inscription on the cap itself. Associated legends include:

- **LIBERTÉ**
- **RÉPUBLIQUE FRANÇAISE**
- **RÉPUBLIQUE UNE ET INDIVISIBLE**
- **LIBERTÉ, ÉGALITÉ, FRATERNITÉ**
- revolutionary unit, section, club, or festival names.

On the seal and its descendants, inscriptions belong to the surrounding seal field rather than to the cap. On coins and medals the cap is normally an uninscribed attribute.

### 1.5 Representative surviving objects

These are examples of the material family, not rival claimants to a unique original:

1. **Revolutionary embroidered Phrygian cap, Musée de l’Armée.** The museum has exhibited a textile “bonnet phrygien brodé du symbole révolutionnaire” during conservation demonstrations. The public notice gives neither an inventory number nor complete measurements, preventing a full provenance reconstruction from the accessible record. [Musée de l’Armée conservation notice](https://www.musee-armee.fr/fileadmin/user_upload/Documents/Communiques_Presse/MA_CP_JEP_2016.pdf)

2. **National Guard officer’s gorget, Musée de l’Armée, c. 1789–1792.** A sword at its centre carries a liberty cap amid royal and revolutionary devices. The coexistence of fleurs-de-lis, the motto “La Nation, la Loi, le Roi,” civic imagery, and the cap documents the emblem before the final fall of the monarchy. [Musée de l’Armée collection essay](https://collections.musee-armee.fr/un-hausse-col-dofficier-de-la-garde-nationale-revolution-francaise/)

3. **Copper uniform button, Musée Carnavalet, inventory B191.** Anonymous, late eighteenth century; diameter 2.7 cm, thickness 0.7 cm; decorated with a fasces, cap, and two cannon. Jacques Bacot donated it to the museum in 1929. This is a securely catalogued surviving bearer of the symbol, not an actual textile cap. [Paris Musées, B191](https://www.parismuseescollections.paris.fr/es/node/980077)

4. **Anonymous etching of Louis XVI and the liberty cap, Musée Carnavalet.** Made about 1792, it is titled *Louis XVI, roi des Français et d’un peuple libre/Bonnet de la Liberté, Présenté au Roi par le peuple Français, le 20 juin 1792*. It records the political identity attributed to the cap shortly after the event but is not the cap used at the Tuileries. [Paris Musées catalogue](https://www.parismuseescollections.paris.fr/fr/musee-carnavalet/oeuvres/louis-xvi-roi-des-francais-et-d-un-peuple-librebonnet-de-la-liberte)

5. **Marianne bust by Paul Aubert, Angers.** Twentieth-century moulded plaster, 65 × 39 × 24 cm, signed “P AUBERT ANGERS,” with an additional inscription on the back. Its mode of acquisition is unknown; it belongs to the commune of Angers and is assigned to the Musée des Beaux-Arts/Galerie David-d’Angers, having been placed in the city hall’s reception room. [POP/Joconde 000SC018501](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/joconde/000SC018501)

6. **Maurice Valentin Borrel medal, Louvre OAP 3103.** Nineteenth-century copper medal showing Marianne in a Phrygian cap with the legend **REPUBLIQUE FRANCAISE**; one of six identical Louvre examples, OAP 3099–3104. [Louvre collections](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark%3A/53355/cl010465324)

These survivals demonstrate multiple material realizations. None has priority as “Marianne’s original cap.”

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Ancient eastern headgear

What modern catalogues call a Phrygian cap appears in Greek and Roman visual conventions for eastern peoples, heroes, and divinities—notably Persians, Trojans, Amazons, Attis, and Mithras. Its bent crown signified exotic or eastern identity. It did **not** intrinsically mean political liberty.

A British Museum figure wears the cap with a short tunic, cloak, and bow. Although long identified as Attis, the museum cautions that the attributes are not exclusive to him. That warning illustrates the basic problem: the cap is an iconographic classifier, not proof of a unique identity. [British Museum object 1856,0701.1](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/H_1856-0701-1)

### 2.2 The Roman *pileus* and manumission

Roman imagery associates Libertas with a *pileus* and rod. A British Museum gold coin depicts Libertas holding both. [British Museum R.6700](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_R-6700)

The freedom meaning arose from Roman manumission and republican iconography, not from a demonstrated Phrygian practice. The celebrated EID MAR coin of Brutus placed a *pileus* between daggers after Caesar’s assassination. Early-modern antiquarians and artists inherited this symbolic vocabulary. During its later transmission the squat or conical *pileus* was increasingly rendered with the forward-falling crown of eastern headgear.

**Scholarly finding:** Marianne’s cap is thus a hybrid image: Phrygian in conventional shape, Roman in its principal political explanation.

### 2.3 Pre-republican French visual antecedents

A December 1789 print in the Musée Carnavalet collection depicts an allegorical woman, still within the constitutional-monarchical formula “Nation, Law and King,” holding a fasces or club crowned by a liberty cap. It demonstrates that the cap was already available as an allegorical attribute before the Republic and before Marianne’s name became joined to the female figure. [Paris Musées, *Représentation de la cocarde nationale*](https://www.parismuseescollections.paris.fr/fr/musee-carnavalet/oeuvres/representation-de-la-cocarde-nationale-decembre-1789-revolution-francaise)

Maurice Agulhon emphasized that three histories must not be collapsed:

- the female allegory of Liberty;
- the cap as Liberty’s attribute;
- the popular name “Marianne.”

The image of the capped woman precedes the securely attested joining of the name and image. Agulhon dated their general fusion only to the mid-nineteenth century. [Agulhon, “Marianne, réflexions sur une histoire”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ahrf_0003-4436_1992_num_289_1_1894)

### 2.4 Emergence of the wearable red cap, 1791–1792

Several origin stories competed almost immediately:

- it was the ordinary woollen cap of poor workers and peasants;
- it came with Mediterranean sailors or revolutionaries from the Midi;
- it derived from the red headgear of galley prisoners;
- it was popularized by the amnestied mutineers of the Swiss Châteauvieux regiment;
- it consciously copied the Roman freedman’s *pileus*;
- artists selected the visually more attractive Phrygian curve and supplied the ancient name after the popular red cap was already in use.

No single story accounts securely for every early use. Jules Michelet later rejected the claim that the symbol originated with the Châteauvieux soldiers, arguing from late-1791 and January–February 1792 political activity that adoption preceded their April triumph. That is an influential nineteenth-century reconstruction, not contemporary proof of one exclusive origin. [Michelet, *Histoire de la Révolution française*](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AMichelet_-_OC%2C_Histoire_de_la_R%C3%A9volution_fran%C3%A7aise%2C_t._3.djvu/398)

The strongest conclusion is plural: familiar working headgear, classical visual learning, existing Liberty imagery, and revolutionary political performance converged in 1791–1792.

### 2.5 Earliest explicit “Phrygian” wording

The historical vocabulary database cites the *Journal des théâtres*, 10 March 1792: during an interval, patriots appeared in red caps with points curved “à la manière du Corno Phrygien” and announced the cap as a rallying signal in public places. This is presently the clearest securely dated lexical attestation located for the revolutionary object under an explicitly Phrygian description. [CNRTL historical vocabulary](https://www.cnrtl.fr/definition/bhvf/bonnet%20phrygien)

### 2.6 Official adoption in republican seals

The chronology has often been compressed or inconsistently dated:

- On **22 September 1792**, the Convention ordered public acts dated from Year I of the Republic and adopted a national-seal design showing a woman associated with a fasces and a pike surmounted by the liberty cap.
- A decree commonly dated **25 September 1792** changed the seal of the Archives and administrative bodies.
- Later official summaries frequently attribute a standing antique-clad woman with a cap-topped pike to “the decree of 22 September.”

The apparent conflict partly reflects distinct but closely related deliberations and later conflation of national and archival seals. A nineteenth-century legislative collection indexes the abolition decree to 21–22 September and the decree changing the Archives’ and administrative seals to 25 September. [Collection complète des lois, index](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3ACollection_compl%C3%A8te_des_lois%2C_d%C3%A9crets%2C_ordonnances%2C_r%C3%A8glemens_et_avis_du_Conseil_d%27%C3%89tat%2C_tome_5%2C_1834.djvu/1)

A 2005 government answer describes the Convention’s decision as adopting Abbé Grégoire’s proposal for Liberty “in the features of a woman dressed in antique style, standing, holding in her right hand a pike surmounted by the Phrygian cap or cap of Liberty.” The same answer confirms that no later legislation imposes a unique Marianne model. [Assemblée nationale written answer](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/dyn/12/questions/QANR5L12QE49797)

**What the decree did:** institutionalized the cap-bearing female figure in republican state imagery.

**What it did not do:** create “Marianne,” prescribe a textile prototype, authorize one workshop, establish exact dimensions, or identify a unique cap as a state relic.

### 2.7 Origin of the name “Marianne”

The oldest identified use of *Marianno* for revolutionary France or the Republic is Guillaume Lavabre’s Occitan song *La Garisou de Marianno* (“Marianne’s Recovery”), composed at Puylaurens in late 1792, probably October or November. It imagines the revolutionary polity as a sick woman recovering after the fall of the monarchy and military victories.

Agulhon held this to be the first occurrence of the name for the Republic but stressed that name and capped image initially followed separate paths. The name remained especially southern and popular before joining the established female effigy more generally during the Second Empire. [Agulhon, 1992](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ahrf_0003-4436_1992_num_289_1_1894); [BnF educational dossier](https://multimedia-ext.bnf.fr/pdf/Revol2_Marianne.pdf)

The familiar tale that Paul Barras named the Republic after his hostess Marie-Anne is a later anecdote without comparable contemporary documentation. It should be classified as **legend**, not provenance.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because no single original exists, custody must be reconstructed at three levels: wearable caps, state-controlled designs, and individual surviving representations.

### Before 1789: dispersed prototypes

Eastern bent caps existed in ancient images and artifacts; Roman *pilei* existed as garments and symbols. Early-modern European artists, engravers, antiquarians, and publishers transmitted and confused these forms. There was no French national custodian.

### 1789–1791: cap on staff, not yet Marianne’s personal headgear

Liberty caps occur in French revolutionary allegory and ornament while France remained a monarchy. The cap could crown a club, fasces, sword, or pike rather than a woman’s head. Its “custody” lay with printmakers, patriotic societies, military units, festival organizers, and private wearers.

### 1791–spring 1792: wearable partisan sign

Red caps entered clubs, theatres, streets, military displays, and patriotic festivals. Manufacture was decentralized. Milliners, knitters, households, and wearers made or acquired them as ordinary garments converted into signs. No documented central issuing authority existed.

### 20 June 1792: the cap placed on Louis XVI

Armed demonstrators entered the Tuileries and presented or imposed a red liberty cap on Louis XVI. He wore it and drank to the health of the nation while refusing to withdraw his vetoes. The episode transformed the cap into a performative object: by putting it on the king, demonstrators temporarily subordinated royal embodiment to popular sovereignty.

The exact textile cap’s maker, previous owner, subsequent keeper, and fate are unrecorded. No surviving museum cap has a documented chain connecting it to Louis XVI.

Contemporary and later prints multiplied the act. The Musée Carnavalet warns that some familiar royalist compositions are later than the event; they document reception, not eyewitness detail. [Assemblée nationale account](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/dyn/histoire-et-patrimoine/revolution-francaise/declaration-de-guerre-manifestation-populaire-des-girondins); [Paris Musées commentary](https://www.parismuseescollections.paris.fr/ru/node/390949)

### September 1792: from partisan object to state attribute

After the monarchy’s abolition, the Convention transferred the cap into the official seal’s iconography. Custody of the sign now belonged simultaneously to state seal engravers and offices, while real caps remained in popular and militant circulation. This was an adoption of the **image**, not confiscation or nationalization of all physical caps.

### 1793–1794: peak diffusion and contested enforcement

Red caps were worn by sans-culottes, club members, officials, section leaders, and participants in civic festivals. Caps were donated collectively: on 3 December 1793 the Convention heard that an Orléans patriot had given one red cap to every pupil of Léonard Bourdon’s national institution. [Archives parlementaires](https://archives-parlementaires.persee.fr/prt/d8a3dbbf-1b14-4905-b6e7-a147cec9636e)

Women’s relationship to the cap exposed a conflict between revolutionary participation and official control. On 29 October 1793 women petitioned against activists attempting to force other women to wear it. The Convention’s debate occurred amid suppression of women’s political clubs. The record documents coercive social use of the garment; it does not establish a universal legal requirement that all women wear it. [Archives parlementaires, 8 brumaire an II](https://www.persee.fr/doc/arcpa_0000-0000_1911_num_78_1_41213_t1_0020_0000_6)

The object thus passed through no single chain but through repeated gift, purchase, local manufacture, collective adoption, compulsion, and theatrical use.

### Thermidor, Directory, Consulate, and Empire

After the fall of Robespierre, the red cap’s strong identification with militant Jacobinism made it vulnerable. Thermidorian culture ridiculed or attacked conspicuous Jacobin dress. The Directory retained classical republican imagery selectively, but artists could neutralize the cap by altering colour or moving it from human head to staff. Under the Consulate and Empire, imperial emblems displaced the radical cap in official state display.

There was no general destruction inventory. Individual textile caps, being fragile everyday objects and politically dangerous possessions, largely disappeared without recorded custody.

### Restoration and July Monarchy, 1814–1848

Royal regimes removed republican imagery from official contexts, but the cap survived in opposition prints, caricature, clandestine republican culture, historical works, and commemorative objects. In the 1830 Revolution, Eugène Delacroix’s *Liberty Leading the People* gave the cap a canonical artistic afterlife. The figure is properly Liberty, not a documented portrait of Marianne; subsequent popular culture frequently merges the two.

### Second Republic, 1848–1852

The Republic again required a female image but disputed whether she should be militant or orderly. The cap could denote a “social,” radical, or insurgent Republic; crowns, rays, stars, agricultural wreaths, and uncovered hair offered moderate alternatives.

A government competition for a republican figure produced divergent designs. Official patronage did not settle a single model. The government recommended a bust by Vital-Gabriel Dubray, but did not establish it as the legally exclusive Marianne. On 3 March 1849 authorities prohibited or discouraged emblems regarded as seditious, including radical versions of the cap and exposed breast. The cap consequently moved back toward opposition culture.

### Second Empire, 1852–1870

Official imperial symbols displaced Marianne. The name and capped female effigy nevertheless converged in republican clandestine culture. “Marianne” could name secret republican societies, while busts, medals, and images circulated privately. Here the symbolic chain survived outside state custody.

### 1870–1871: Republic, war, and Commune

After the fall of Napoleon III, republican images re-entered public buildings. During the Paris Commune, the cap was mobilized by radical republican and revolutionary culture. A short-lived newspaper titled *Le Bonnet Rouge* reportedly used cap-wearing vendors, demonstrating conversion of the garment into publication branding and street performance. [Association des Amies et Amis de la Commune](https://commune1871.org/la-commune-de-paris/histoire-de-la-commune/a-l-heure-du-bilan/1005-revolution-francaise-et-commune-de-paris-de-1871-entre-fascination-et-rupture)

### 1871–1879: conservative republican restraint

Adolphe Thiers regarded the cap as seditious and favored a pacified female Republic crowned with vegetation. Early Third Republic city-hall busts therefore often wear oak, olive, or wheat, sometimes with a star. The Assemblée nationale dates the cap’s official-looking reappearance to 1879. [Assemblée nationale, bust collection](https://www2.assemblee-nationale.fr/decouvrir-l-assemblee/patrimoine/la-bibliotheque/les-bustes-de-marianne/presentation)

This was not the physical removal of one national cap. It was administrative and political pressure on municipal commissioners, sculptors, and display choices.

### 1879–1914: municipal multiplication

With republican consolidation, capped busts proliferated in town halls, schools, courts, private republican homes, civic festivals, medals, and monuments. Production was commercial and decentralized. Sculptors prepared models; manufacturers issued plaster, terracotta, bronze, and composition copies; municipalities purchased locally.

No statute imposed one official bust. Municipalities retained the object, while manufacturers owned moulds and artists’ rights according to ordinary law. This explains differences in cap height, curvature, cockade, hair, breast covering, armour, inscriptions, and colour.

Agulhon’s research established that this multiplication, rather than the custody of a unique original, constituted Marianne’s material political power. [Review of *Marianne au pouvoir*](https://www.persee.fr/doc/roman_0048-8593_1990_num_20_68_6133)

### 1914–1940: normalization

The cap increasingly signified France as well as militant revolution. It appeared in wartime propaganda, veterans’ insignia, coins, medals, stamps, posters, monuments, and municipal interiors.

The 1915 Croix de guerre, designed by Paul-Albert Bartholomé, placed a capped Republic at its centre. Changes in the surrounding date—from 1914–1915 through 1914–1918—show how the cap’s image was mechanically reproduced across successive issues. [Musée de l’Armée](https://actualites.musee-armee.fr/vie-du-musee/les-invalides-dans-la-grande-guerre-episode-10-remise-de-decorations-dans-la-cour-dhonneur/)

### Vichy, 1940–1944

The French State rejected the Republic’s official visual language. Marianne busts were removed, stored, damaged, or replaced in public buildings; republican metal objects could also fall within wartime metal-recovery campaigns. Practices differed locally, and no comprehensive national custody list exists.

It would be inaccurate to claim that one order found and destroyed every capped Marianne. Many survived through municipal storage, neglect, private concealment, or because removal was uneven.

### Free France and Liberation, 1943–1946

The cap recovered a strong association with republican legitimacy. The *Marianne d’Alger*, designed by Louis Fernez, appeared in the context of the French Committee of National Liberation and liberated Corsica. Edmund Dulac’s 1945 design paired Marianne’s cap with the Cross of Lorraine; Pierre Gandon’s Marianne, selected under Charles de Gaulle, became a principal postwar definitive issue. [Assemblée nationale postal chronology](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/14/evenements/timbre-2013/tradition-republicaine.pdf)

The cap’s custody now extended through state printing and postal distribution: original drawings and dies had particular custodians, but millions of printed impressions entered private hands and ordinary circulation.

### Fifth Republic, 1958–present

Marianne busts continue in municipal buildings by custom, not general statutory command. A 2005 government answer explicitly stated that manufacturers may make and sell them and each municipality may choose its model. [Assemblée nationale written answer](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/dyn/12/questions/QANR5L12QE49797)

In 1999 the government created the “bloc Marianne,” combining a white profile with cap on a blue-and-red field, the words *République française*, and the republican motto. A 2020 redesign enlarged and humanized the profile and standardized state communications. The Service d’information du Gouvernement treats the block as a state identity mark. [French government brand history](https://www.info.gouv.fr/exposition-la-parole-de-letat-miroir-des-epoques); [current state-brand specification](https://www.info.gouv.fr/marque-de-letat)

The cap has therefore moved from cloth to an infinitely reproducible state-controlled digital asset.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Is there an original?

**No original “cap of Marianne” is documented.** Any claim that a particular museum textile is *the* national cap would require:

- a unique commissioning record;
- uninterrupted custody;
- evidence that the Republic or Marianne was conceived as owning that particular garment;
- correspondence between the object and the official seal or foundational representation.

No located institutional catalogue makes such a claim.

### 4.2 The central identity confusion: *pileus* versus Phrygian cap

Official and popular accounts often state that liberated Roman slaves wore “Phrygian caps.” This is an instructive historical simplification. Roman freedmen were associated with the *pileus*; the forward-folding eastern cap served other ethnic and divine iconographic functions. Revolutionary and pre-revolutionary artists fused the two.

The confusion was recognized early. Antiquarian Aubin-Louis Millin complained in 1806 that a Phrygian cap was inappropriate because Phrygians were stereotyped as enslaved, while A.-E. Gibelin made a related criticism in Year IV. [Numismatic iconography study](https://www.persee.fr/doc/gazar_0016-5522_1989_num_146_1_4157)

This does not make Marianne’s cap a “forgery.” It makes it a historically constructed emblem whose received name does not accurately identify the Roman manumission object.

### 4.3 Did the revolutionaries inherit it from galley prisoners?

The galley-prisoner and Châteauvieux explanations are traditions with partial contextual plausibility but no conclusive single-object chain. Red woollen caps were worn by multiple popular and Mediterranean groups. The symbol’s emergence cannot securely be assigned to one surviving cap brought from a galley, port, or regiment.

### 4.4 Did the Convention create Marianne in 1792?

The Convention created or adopted a female republican seal with a liberty-cap attribute. It did not name that woman Marianne. The popular name’s separate Occitan attestation and later merger with the image are well supported by Agulhon. Statements that “the Convention chose Marianne in a Phrygian cap” are convenient retrospective summaries, not precise descriptions of the 1792 act.

### 4.5 Is Delacroix’s woman Marianne?

*Liberty Leading the People* depicts an allegory of Liberty commemorating the July Revolution of 1830. Her red cap contributed decisively to the modern visual idea of Marianne, but the painting is not evidence that a historical Marianne led the barricades or that Delacroix copied a state-standard model. Calling her Marianne is a later interpretive fusion.

### 4.6 Celebrity “models”

Claims that Brigitte Bardot, Mireille Mathieu, Catherine Deneuve, Inès de la Fressange, Laetitia Casta, or Évelyne Thomas became the official Marianne commonly overstate private, mayoral, artistic, or publicity initiatives. France has no single legally prescribed Marianne face. The Élysée itself lists these faces as part of Marianne’s changing imagery, while the government’s legal answer confirms the absence of an imposed model. [Élysée](https://www.elysee.fr/la-presidence/marianne)

### 4.7 Scientific examination

No comprehensive laboratory program has tested “Marianne’s cap,” because no unique claimant exists. Individual textile examples may undergo fibre identification, dye analysis, conservation imaging, and dating, but the publicly accessible Musée de l’Armée notice located here does not publish results for its embroidered cap. Claims about the exact fibre, dye, maker, or revolutionary owner of that specimen therefore remain **unestablished in this dossier**.

### 4.8 Authority to authenticate

Authority is distributed:

- museums authenticate and catalogue particular artifacts;
- conservators identify materials and interventions;
- historians assess the documentary development of the emblem;
- the state defines current uses of its graphic identity;
- municipalities choose their busts;
- courts determine intellectual-property or misuse disputes involving particular works;
- no church, crown, or constitutional tribunal authenticates one “true” cap.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Classical and mythological associations

- **Libertas:** Roman personification of liberty; associated with the *pileus* and manumission rod. This is the cap’s principal political-symbolic ancestor.
- **Attis:** Phrygian companion of Cybele, conventionally represented in eastern clothing and bent cap. His cap supplies form, not the Roman freedom meaning.
- **Mithras:** Roman mystery-cult god represented in eastern dress and cap; an iconographic relative, not a documented source of French republican ritual.
- **Paris, Aeneas, Trojans, Persians, Amazons:** figures and peoples whose caps taught European artists to read the shape as “eastern” or “Phrygian.”

### Revolutionary associations

- **Guillaume Lavabre:** Occitan shoemaker-poet whose *La Garisou de Marianno* gives the earliest identified name “Marianne” to the revolutionary polity, late 1792. He did not manufacture a cap.
- **Abbé Henri Grégoire:** conventionnel associated with the report and official republican seal language that joined a female Liberty to the cap-topped pike.
- **Louis XVI:** made to wear a red liberty cap during the Tuileries demonstration of 20 June 1792. His contact is one of the few famous episodes involving an actual cap rather than a representation.
- **Châteauvieux mutineers:** traditionally credited with popularizing the red cap after their amnesty; the exclusivity of this origin is disputed.
- **Sans-culottes:** principal collective wearers and political owners of the cap’s radical street meaning.
- **Jacobin Club and Paris sections:** networks through which cap-wearing, debate, and political policing spread.
- **Women’s revolutionary societies and market women:** participants in the 1793 conflict over whether women could or should be compelled to wear the cap.
- **Léonard Bourdon and pupils:** beneficiaries of an Orléans patriot’s collective gift of red caps in December 1793.

### Nineteenth-century associations

- **Eugène Delacroix:** made the red cap central to the enduring image of barricade Liberty in 1830.
- **Vital-Gabriel Dubray:** sculptor whose republican bust received provisional-government recommendation after 1848.
- **Adolphe Thiers:** associated with conservative suppression of the cap in early Third Republic public imagery.
- **Communards:** reclaimed revolutionary signs, including the red cap, in 1871.
- **Marianne manufacturers and municipalities:** the commercial-administrative network that multiplied capped busts after 1879.
- **Maurice Valentin Borrel:** medalist responsible for capped Marianne designs, including Louvre examples.
- **Maurice Agulhon:** historian who reconstructed the separate and eventually converging careers of female allegory, cap, and name.

### Twentieth- and twenty-first-century associations

- **Paul-Albert Bartholomé:** designed the central capped Republic of the Croix de guerre.
- **Louis Fernez:** designer of the *Marianne d’Alger*.
- **Edmund Dulac:** designer of a Liberation-era Marianne combining Phrygian cap and Cross of Lorraine.
- **Pierre Gandon:** designer of the post-Liberation definitive Marianne chosen under Charles de Gaulle.
- **Service d’information du Gouvernement:** custodian of the present “bloc Marianne” identity system.
- **Agency W and Paris 2024 design team:** creators of the Olympic and Paralympic “Phryges,” anthropomorphic caps unveiled in 2022.
- **Tony Estanguet:** Paris 2024 president who presented the choice as an “ideal” rather than an animal mascot.
- **Joachim Roncin:** Paris 2024 head of design associated with translating the cap’s static silhouette into a moving character.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Manumission and liberty

The modern doctrine says the cap signifies a slave set free. Its immediate classical referent is the Roman *pileus*. In revolutionary use, the cap converted individual manumission into collective political emancipation: the French people represented themselves as freed from arbitrary rule.

### 6.2 Wearing as profession of political identity

A red cap could mark the wearer as a patriot, sans-culotte, Jacobin, radical republican, or adherent of popular sovereignty. It functioned as portable speech. Wearing it in a theatre, club, assembly, procession, or street made a political claim visible without words.

Its meaning depended on context:

- to supporters: liberty, equality, civic vigilance, fraternity;
- to moderates: popular patriotism but potentially excessive militancy;
- to opponents: Jacobinism, violence, irreligion, mob rule, or “the Terror.”

### 6.3 Capping persons

Placing the cap on another person was a political rite of incorporation, humiliation, protection, or compelled assent. Louis XVI’s capping on 20 June 1792 is the archetypal example. It did not legally free him or transfer sovereignty, but symbolically forced the king to wear the people’s sign.

### 6.4 Capping poles and weapons

The cap on a pike, lance, staff, sword, club, or fasces transformed the support into a liberty pole. It could head a procession, define a rallying point, crown a seal, or signal a liberated civic space. This form preserved the Roman emblematic logic more closely than wearing a Phrygian cap directly on Liberty’s head.

### 6.5 Funerals, festivals, and civic education

During the Revolution the cap appeared in festivals, temporary architecture, funeral processions, educational institutions, theatre, and section ceremonies. The gift of caps to Bourdon’s pupils made political clothing an educational instrument. Later Marianne busts in schools and town halls turned the cap into an everyday lesson in republican government.

### 6.6 Seal and documentary authority

On the republican seal the cap did not itself authenticate documents. The engraved seal matrix and lawful act of sealing did so. The cap visually announced that the authority represented was republican and grounded in liberty rather than monarchy.

### 6.7 Political temperature

The cap’s presence or absence became an index of regime and faction:

- capped, bare-breasted, armed Marianne: democratic, social, insurgent, vigilant;
- diademed, veiled, helmeted, or vegetation-crowned Republic: orderly, moderate, institutional, pacific.

These are tendencies, not invariant rules. Artists frequently mixed attributes.

### 6.8 No sacred power or access taboo

The cap has been treated with civic reverence, but no documented orthodox French tradition attributes miracles, curses, healing, or supernatural protection to one material specimen. No binding taboo reserves it to a priesthood, sex, class, or office. Its force is political and ceremonial.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 “The freed slave’s Phrygian cap”

**Status:** historically influential conflation.

The story says Roman masters placed a Phrygian cap on freed slaves, making the same garment an obvious revolutionary symbol. The factual core is Roman association of the *pileus* with manumission. The curved eastern cap was a separate ancient form. Modern official explanations often preserve the simplified version because it communicates liberation efficiently.

### 7.2 The Châteauvieux origin

**Status:** early historical tradition; exclusive causal claim disputed.

The story says the amnestied Châteauvieux mutineers returned wearing red galley or prison caps, and Paris adopted them. Their procession undoubtedly strengthened the cap’s visibility, but evidence of earlier usage makes them popularizers rather than demonstrable inventors.

### 7.3 The Marseillais and galley-prisoner origin

**Status:** plausible cultural transmission, unproved as a single origin.

The tale connects southern revolutionaries to Mediterranean sailors and Toulon or Marseille galley prisoners. Red knitted caps belonged to this wider material culture. No documented individual cap has been traced from a galley prisoner to Marianne imagery.

### 7.4 The Café Procope “first cap”

**Status:** local and popular legend.

Stories place the first revolutionary wearing of the cap at the Café Procope. No uniquely documented cap, wearer, date, maker, or custody chain substantiates a singular first event. The café’s genuine revolutionary associations made it a natural magnet for origin legend.

### 7.5 Barras names Marianne

**Status:** late anecdote/unverified claim.

In this story Barras asks his hostess, Madame Reubell, her name; “Marie-Anne” prompts him to declare it perfect for the Republic. The anecdote lacks the documentary priority of Lavabre’s 1792 song and should not displace it.

### 7.6 The cap as timeless national emblem

**Status:** retrospective national tradition.

Modern summaries sometimes project the capped Marianne continuously from 1789 to the present. In fact, monarchies and empires displaced her; republics alternately suppressed and restored the cap; moderate republicans often preferred wreaths or diadems. Its present familiarity is the outcome of repeated political conflict.

### 7.7 “Every town hall must have one”

**Status:** widely repeated but legally false as a general rule.

French governmental replies state that no statute or regulation prescribes a unique Marianne bust or obliges municipalities to choose one model. A 2025 Senate bill proposed making a publicly visible bust compulsory precisely because no such national obligation already existed. [French Senate bill no. 314](https://www.senat.fr/leg/ppl24-314.pdf)

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Status of the emblem

Marianne and her cap remain recognized republican symbols, but Article 2 of the Constitution designates the blue-white-red tricolour—not Marianne or the cap—as the national emblem. It also names the French language, *La Marseillaise*, the motto, and the Republic’s democratic principle. [Constitution, Article 2](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/loda/article_lc/LEGIARTI000006527453/2026-03-22)

Thus:

- **constitutional status:** not independently enumerated;
- **institutional status:** entrenched republican symbol and state identity mark;
- **ownership:** no ownership of the generic historical idea; particular artworks, trademarks, designs, matrices, moulds, and files have particular owners or rights-holders;
- **custody:** distributed among government offices, archives, museums, municipalities, private collections, and commercial producers.

### 8.2 Present “locations”

There is no single present location. Significant classes are:

- Marianne busts in French municipal buildings;
- revolutionary artifacts and printed representations in the Musée Carnavalet, Musée de l’Armée, Louvre, Bibliothèque nationale de France, Musée de la Révolution française, departmental museums, archives, and private collections;
- the state’s digital and printed “bloc Marianne” across ministries, prefectures, official websites, signage, forms, and communications;
- postage-stamp drawings, dies, proofs, and issues in postal collections and private philately;
- sculptural monuments in public squares;
- mass-produced Phryges and other commercial replicas worldwide.

### 8.3 Condition and conservation

Individual textile caps are vulnerable to fading, fibre breakage, insect damage, deformation, and loss of attached cockades or embroidery. Stone and plaster caps may chip; bronze versions acquire corrosion or patina; painted versions lose the red surface that conveys their meaning. Conservation belongs to the condition report for each artifact, not to the emblem collectively.

No evidence supports a general repatriation claim or international title dispute concerning “Marianne’s cap.” Particular artifacts may have incomplete acquisition histories, but no claimant identified here asserts ownership of a unique national original.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Stamps, coins, medals, and official print

The cap’s great modern expansion occurred through reproducible state media. It appeared on medals, military decorations, coins, and definitive postage stamps. Postal Mariannes are not one uninterrupted design: they form successive commissioned types whose differing caps register changing ideas of the Republic.

Postwar types include the *Marianne d’Alger*, Dulac, Gandon, and numerous later presidential selections. Some omit or stylize the cap; others make it the chief identifying feature. Since 1989, names such as *Marianne du Bicentenaire*, *Marianne du 14-Juillet*, *Marianne des Français*, *Marianne et l’Europe*, *Marianne et la jeunesse*, and *Marianne l’engagée* have framed each issue politically. [Overview of Marianne stamps](https://www.lemonde.fr/mondephilatelique/article/2024/05/29/la-marianne-tout-un-art-du-timbre-poste-grave_6236230_5470897.html)

### 9.2 The state graphic mark

The 1999 “bloc Marianne” converted the allegory into corporate-style identity architecture. In 2020 the state retained the block while enlarging the face, standardizing placement, and introducing the proprietary Marianne typeface. The current system specifies the block’s relationship to institutional names and the motto.

This is not a new physical cap. It is a controlled family of vector and typographic assets whose correct reproduction is governed by administrative brand standards. [State brand](https://www.info.gouv.fr/marque-de-letat)

### 9.3 Political appropriation and counter-image

Republicans, socialists, radicals, feminists, trade unionists, anti-colonial movements, caricaturists, and nationalist opponents have all reused the cap. Its meaning is relational:

- worn at demonstrations, it can reactivate popular sovereignty;
- used by the state, it denotes administrative legitimacy;
- exaggerated in hostile cartoons, it can imply disorder or ideological coercion;
- placed on new female bodies, it can claim inclusion in the Republic;
- removed or replaced, it can signal moderation, rejection of revolutionary memory, or an alternative account of France.

### 9.4 Art and literature

Major works shaping public perception include:

- Delacroix, *La Liberté guidant le peuple* (1830);
- Honoré Daumier’s political caricatures;
- municipal bust production after 1870;
- republican monuments such as the Place de la République ensemble in Paris;
- propaganda posters from both world wars;
- Marianne postage stamps and press cartoons.

These works have often caused Liberty, Republic, Nation, and Marianne to be treated as interchangeable. Historically they overlap but were not always identical.

### 9.5 Paris 2024: the Phryges

The Paris 2024 organizers unveiled the Olympic and Paralympic Phryges on 14 November 2022. Designed by the W agency with the organizing committee, they turn the cap into an anthropomorphic body. The tricolour cockade becomes an eye; its ribbons become eyelashes; the Games emblem appears on the chest. The Paralympic figure has a running blade.

The organizers explicitly selected an ideal rather than an animal and identified that ideal as liberty and progress. [Olympic Studies Centre mascot history](https://library.olympics.com/digitalCollection/DigitalCollectionAttachmentDownloadHandler.ashx?documentId=2955633&parentDocumentId=161811&skipCopyright=true&skipWatermark=true)

This was an authorized modern redesign, not a newly discovered ancient tradition. Licensed plush toys transformed the national symbol into a commodity. Paris 2024 reported plans for nearly 400,000 official plush mascots using recycled polyester, with part of production located in France. [Paris 2024 sustainability report](https://library.olympics.com/cnospa/digitalCollection/DigitalCollectionAttachmentDownloadHandler.ashx?documentId=3415773&parentDocumentId=3415772&skipCopyright=true&skipWatermark=true)

### 9.6 Commercial replicas

Commercial caps range from historical-reenactment clothing and Bastille Day costume pieces to busts, souvenirs, pins, toys, and political merchandise. Their material construction—synthetic felt, polyester plush, resin, plaster, or base metal—usually bears no relationship to eighteenth-century wool caps beyond colour and outline.

A licensed Paris 2024 pin, for example, is catalogued commercially as Chinese-made zamak. It is an authentic licensed Olympic product but not an authentic revolutionary artifact. [Olympics shop listing](https://shop.olympics.com/fr/paris-2024/paris-2024-olympics-mascot-joyful-pin-badge/t-1211990830%2Bp-6714034185731%2Bz-9-2989827744?_ref=p-OSLP%3Am-GRID%3Ai-r10c1%3Apo-31)

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Maurice Agulhon’s reconstruction

The foundational modern scholarship is Maurice Agulhon’s trilogy:

- *Marianne au combat: l’imagerie et la symbolique républicaines de 1789 à 1880*;
- *Marianne au pouvoir: l’imagerie et la symbolique républicaines de 1880 à 1914*;
- *Les Métamorphoses de Marianne: l’imagerie et la symbolique républicaines de 1914 à nos jours*.

Agulhon’s central methodological advance was to treat monuments, busts, names, festivals, and popular language as political evidence. He rejected a timeless, unitary Marianne and reconstructed the gradual conjunction of female allegory, cap, and name.

### 10.2 Principal debates

**Pileus or Phrygian cap?**  
The liberty symbolism belongs primarily to the Roman *pileus*, while the received visual form is eastern. The “error” itself became historically productive.

**Popular garment or learned iconography?**  
The cap could be both. Ordinary red woollen headgear made the sign wearable; classical imagery made it intelligible as liberty.

**Who introduced it?**  
Châteauvieux soldiers, southern revolutionaries, galley prisoners, theatre-going patriots, artists, antiquarians, and sans-culottes have all been credited. Evidence supports convergence more strongly than one inventor.

**When did it become Marianne’s?**  
The cap-bearing female allegory appears before the name. Lavabre’s *Marianno* dates from late 1792, but Agulhon argues that broad fusion of name and image occurred only around the middle of the nineteenth century.

**Official or popular?**  
Both, at different moments. The Convention used the cap in seals; governments later suppressed it; municipal and private actors restored it; the modern state employs it graphically without constitutionalizing Marianne.

**Radical or national?**  
Originally capable of marking militant popular politics, it was gradually normalized as an emblem of the Republic and France. Periodic political reuse restores its radical charge.

### 10.3 Evidentiary limitations

- Few worn textile caps have detailed published provenance.
- Newspaper descriptions and prints often cannot be tied to a surviving garment.
- Later memoirs and histories disagree about first adoption.
- Nineteenth-century prints of 1792 events may silently modernize costume.
- Town-hall bust records are dispersed through municipal deliberations, invoices, sculptors’ catalogues, foundry records, local inventories, and parish or prefectural disputes.
- Removal and survival under Vichy varied locally and remain incompletely inventoried.
- “Official Marianne” claims in journalism frequently ignore the absence of a compulsory national model.

### 10.4 Discredited or overstated propositions

The following should not be repeated as established fact:

- that the Phrygians invented a political liberty cap;
- that Roman freed slaves necessarily wore the forward-drooping cap now worn by Marianne;
- that one Châteauvieux prisoner’s cap became the national emblem;
- that Louis XVI’s 20 June cap survives in an identified collection;
- that the Convention officially named its female seal “Marianne”;
- that Delacroix painted a historical woman named Marianne;
- that France has one official Marianne bust or face;
- that every municipality is already legally required to display one;
- that a surviving museum cap can be authenticated as Marianne’s unique original.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and near-primary documentary sources

- Revolutionary parliamentary proceedings concerning compulsory cap-wearing and gifts of caps.
- Convention-era seal decrees as transmitted in legislative collections and institutional summaries.
- Contemporary and near-contemporary revolutionary prints catalogued by the Musée Carnavalet.
- Official government, parliamentary, constitutional, museum, and Olympic records.
- Surviving museum objects with published inventory numbers.

### Major scholarship and research aids

- Maurice Agulhon’s trilogy on Marianne.
- Agulhon’s methodological essay “Marianne, réflexions sur une histoire.”
- Study of Liberty’s cap in revolutionary coin and medal iconography.
- Museum studies distinguishing the Roman *pileus* from the bent Phrygian cap.
- Historical lexicography documenting the expressions *bonnet rouge* and *bonnet phrygien*.

### Source assessment

The documentary record is strongest for the cap’s **images, prescribed symbolic uses, and political reception**. It is weakest for the manufacture and individual provenance of wearable revolutionary caps. Prints prove that episodes and visual formulas circulated; unless an image is demonstrably contemporary and observational, it cannot by itself prove the exact construction of a cap or identify a surviving specimen.

No examined source supplies an accessioned artifact with an uninterrupted chain from a foundational event to the present. The historically defensible object is consequently the reproducible emblematic type, not a lost national relic.
