# The Codex Calixtinus: Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `the_codex_calixtinus_object_dossier.md`  
**Research current to:** 6 September 2026

> **Scope and identity rule.** This dossier follows the particular medieval manuscript preserved by the Cathedral of Santiago de Compostela under the shelfmark **Archivo-Biblioteca de la Catedral de Santiago de Compostela, CF-14**. That artifact must be distinguished from the *Liber Sancti Iacobi*—the composite work transmitted by CF-14 and other manuscripts—and from later manuscripts, editions, translations, and facsimiles commonly called “Codex Calixtinus.” Except where explicitly discussing those witnesses, “the Codex” below means CF-14.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Identity and names

The Codex Calixtinus is a twelfth-century illuminated parchment manuscript containing the earliest, richest, and most nearly complete surviving recension of the *Liber Sancti Iacobi*, a compilation devoted to the apostle James the Greater, his cult at Compostela, the liturgy of his feasts, reported miracles, the legendary translation of his body to Galicia, the Pseudo-Turpin history of Charlemagne in Spain, and the roads and institutions serving pilgrims to Santiago. It is simultaneously a liturgical book, hagiographic collection, legendary chronicle, musical source, institutional manifesto, and pilgrimage geography. It is not simply—or primarily—a portable “medieval travel guide”: the guide occupies only Book V.

Principal names include:

- **Latin:** *Codex Calixtinus*, “Calixtine Codex” or “Codex of Calixtus”; *Codex Compostellanus*, “Compostelan Codex”; *Liber Sancti Iacobi* or *Liber Sancti Jacobi*, “Book of Saint James”; *Iacobus* or *Jacobus*, a conventional scholarly shorthand derived from the saint’s Latin name.
- **Spanish:** *Códice Calixtino*, *Códice de Calixto*, *Libro de Santiago*.
- **Galician:** *Códice Calixtino*, *Libro de Santiago*.
- **French:** *Codex Calixtinus*, *Livre de Saint-Jacques*.
- **English:** *Codex Calixtinus*, *Book of Saint James*.
- **German:** *Jakobsbuch*.

“Calixtinus” does not identify a securely documented author or commissioner. It derives from the compilation’s attribution to **Pope Calixtus II**—Guido of Burgundy, pope in 1119–1124—whose supposed prefatory letter presents him as collector or author. Modern scholarship treats that attribution as **pseudepigraphic**. The codex is therefore “Calixtine” because it speaks under papal authority, not because Calixtus demonstrably wrote, owned, commissioned, or handled it.

A further distinction is essential:

- **Artifact:** Santiago CF-14, a particular assemblage of parchment leaves, inks, pigments, sewing, and later bindings.
- **Textual work:** the *Liber Sancti Iacobi*, whose contents circulated in different selections and arrangements.
- **Modern bibliographical usage:** “Codex Calixtinus” is sometimes applied loosely to the work, to other medieval witnesses, or to modern facsimiles. Those usages do not make the objects physically identical.

### 1.2 Present catalogue identity

- **Repository:** Archivo-Biblioteca Capitular, Cathedral of Santiago de Compostela, Galicia, Spain.
- **Shelfmark:** **CF-14**.
- **Date assigned by the Spanish Memory of the World register:** between **1138 and 1172**, while acknowledging continuing disagreement.
- **Probable production environment:** Compostelan cathedral scriptorium, questioned rather than conclusively established.
- **Extent:** **225 parchment folios**, written on both sides.
- **Average leaf dimensions:** approximately **295 × 214–215 mm**; one specialist music catalogue records approximately 294 × 224 mm.
- **Layout:** ordinarily one column of 34 lines.
- **Language:** overwhelmingly Latin. Vernacular words, place-names, and ethnographic vocabulary occur inside the Latin text; the manuscript should not thereby be catalogued as substantially Galician-language.
- **Script:** a formal twelfth-century book hand commonly described as Gothic or transitional protogothic book script, with additions by several hands.
- **Music notation:** principally Aquitanian neumes, commonly written *in campo aperto*—without a ruled staff—alongside later polyphonic material.
- **Current repository description:** the University Complutense manuscript project calls CF-14 the richest, most complete, and earliest surviving copy of the compilation. [UCM manuscript record](https://www.ucm.es/esleim2/calixtinus)

Published measurements are not wholly uniform because cataloguers have measured the leaf, written area, or present binding differently. A Google Arts & Culture record supplied by the Cathedral Foundation gives the bound object as approximately **31 × 28.4 × 6 cm**, while codicological catalogues give the parchment leaves at roughly 29.5 × 21.5 cm. These figures describe different physical components and are not necessarily contradictory. [Cathedral Foundation object record](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/illuminated-initial-of-the-codex-calixtinus-i-of-iacobus-compostelan-scriptorium/OQGbTGlWiEc_mQ?hl=en)

No reliable published weight was located. No published scientific identification of the animal species used for every parchment leaf, nor a published comprehensive pigment analysis or radiocarbon campaign on CF-14, was found in the sources consulted. It is therefore safer to say **parchment**, not to assert calfskin throughout.

### 1.3 Physical construction and campaigns of production

Codicological analysis does not support the picture of one author writing a finished book from beginning to end. At least four scribal hands have been distinguished, two closely related in appearance. Scholarship commonly designates the principal copyist **“Scriptor I”**, a descriptive label rather than a known historical identity.

The original compilation was enlarged in stages. Differences in hands, decoration, quire structure, foliation, and the contents of the final gatherings reveal at least two major production campaigns and later additions. Alison Stones found that the principal miniatures and historiated initials belong to sections produced during the first campaign, particularly Books I and IV, while systems of lesser initials correspond to different stages of copying. [Williams and Stones, *The Codex Calixtinus and the Shrine of St James*](https://elibrary.narr.digital/xibrary/10.24053-9783823350040)

The leaves retain medieval foliation in Roman numerals. Book IV also acquired a separate Arabic foliation after it was detached, material evidence of its period as a separate volume. [Musica Hispanica catalogue](https://musicahispanica.eu/source/22620)

### 1.4 Contents in the present manuscript

The standard modern division is:

| Physical location | Conventional division | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| ff. 1r–2v | Prefatory letter | Pseudonymous letter attributed to Calixtus II |
| ff. 2v–139r | Book I | Sermons, offices, Masses, lessons, hymns, chants, and liturgical material for Saint James |
| ff. 139v–155v | Book II | Twenty-two miracle narratives associated with Saint James |
| ff. 156r–162v | Book III | Narratives of the apostle’s martyrdom and legendary translation from Jerusalem to Galicia; related observances |
| ff. 163r–191r | Book IV | *Historia Karoli Magni et Rotholandi*, the Pseudo-Turpin chronicle |
| ff. 192r–213v | Book V | *Iter pro peregrinis ad Compostellam*, the so-called Pilgrim’s Guide |
| ff. 214r–219v | Appendix I | Primarily musical additions |
| f. 220 | Structural break/lacuna | The manuscript’s precise original structure here has been debated |
| ff. 221r–225r | Appendix II | Later additions, including polyphony and documents supporting the codex’s claimed transmission |

This foliation and arrangement follow the current physical object, after the reintegration of Book IV in 1966. [UCM manuscript record](https://www.ucm.es/esleim2/calixtinus)

Book I is by far the largest component. Book V is famous because of its descriptions of roads, shrines, rivers, peoples, hospitality, dangers, and the city and cathedral of Santiago, but it occupies roughly twenty-two of 225 folios. Calling the whole codex “the first travel guide” materially misidentifies the object.

### 1.5 Decoration

The manuscript contains:

- Large decorated and historiated initials.
- Smaller colored initials marking textual divisions.
- Figural and ornamental work in several colors.
- The celebrated initial **I** of *Iacobus*.
- Narrative imagery associated especially with Saint James and the Pseudo-Turpin material.
- Rubrication and visual hierarchies coordinating liturgical use.
- Musical notation integrated with the written text.

Descriptions of the codex as filled with “full-color miniatures” can be misleading if they suggest a lavish picture Bible with continuous illustration. Its illumination is important but selective. The decoration functions primarily to order, authorize, and monumentalize a complex institutional book.

The precise composition of the medieval inks and pigments has not been established in the accessible sources by a published modern instrumental survey. Statements identifying particular minerals or organic colorants should therefore not be treated as findings about CF-14 without a conservation report.

### 1.6 Music

Music appears principally in Book I and the appendices. The codex preserves monophonic liturgy for Saint James and a celebrated group of early polyphonic pieces. Its Aquitanian notation and the international stylistic affiliations of the repertory have been central to arguments about where the compilation was assembled.

A specialist database records **21 compositions**, including named and anonymous works. [DIAMM catalogue](https://www.diamm.ac.uk/sources/3970/) The attribution headings attach pieces to clerics and churchmen from places including Paris, Vézelay, Soissons, Bourges, Chartres, and elsewhere. Those headings are evidence for the compilers’ claims, not automatic proof that every named person composed the assigned piece.

The conductus **“Congaudeant catholici”** has often been called the earliest surviving three-voice composition. Its notation has generated disagreement: the disposition of the voices and possible copying problems mean that how it was intended to be performed is not beyond dispute.

Recent musical scholarship has complicated a simple French-versus-Spanish origin model. Comparison with earlier fragments suggests that the Calixtine office reworked an older Saint James repertory, probably to remove or moderate legendary elements and furnish Compostela with a more scripturally and patristically defensible liturgy. [Ruiz Torres, “New evidence concerning the origin of the monophonic chants”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/plainsong-and-medieval-music/article/abs/new-evidence-concerning-the-origin-of-the-monophonic-chants-in-the-codex-calixtinus/A801BC2801C76F47BFAE27A62307EC8B)

### 1.7 Binding, damage, losses, and alterations

The present binding is not the manuscript’s original twelfth-century binding. The object’s binding history includes at least these documented or strongly inferred interventions:

1. **Early assembly and expansion:** gatherings produced in different campaigns were joined into the composite codex.
2. **Loss of an initial blank:** codicological reconstruction suggests a blank leaf once preceded present f. 1.
3. **Separation of Book IV:** in 1609 or, according to another technical account, around 1619, the Pseudo-Turpin section was removed. The operation damaged or disturbed gatherings conventionally numbered 20, 21, and 24.
4. **Separate early-modern bindings:** Alonso Rodríguez León bound the reduced main codex and detached Book IV separately, reportedly in doeskin.
5. **Later red-velvet covering:** the main codex was at some point covered in red velvet, subsequently described as worn.
6. **1966 restoration:** Book IV was returned to its original sequence. The restoration required rebinding and structural alteration; projecting or oversized leaves were trimmed to make the block more uniform. This was irreversible loss of original parchment margins.
7. **2011–2012 theft:** the codex spent approximately a year wrapped in paper and plastic in a garage. Police and cathedral experts reported it recovered intact or in good condition, but an accessible, detailed post-recovery conservation report was not located.

The separation date appears as **1609** in much modern literature and **1619** in the specialist DIAMM description. Unless the original binding account by Rodríguez León is re-examined, the discrepancy should remain explicit. [DIAMM catalogue](https://www.diamm.ac.uk/sources/3970/)

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Secure and insecure chronological anchors

The codex is not dated by a straightforward scribal colophon saying that CF-14 was completed in a named year. Its chronology is reconstructed from internal textual dates, palaeography, codicology, and external copying.

Important anchors include:

- The compilation contains material dependent on events and persons of the early twelfth century.
- A miracle associated with **1139** supplies a frequently used *terminus post quem* for part of the collection.
- Internal evidence conventionally places the initial compilation no earlier than approximately **1135–1139**.
- In **1173**, Arnaldus or Arnaldo de Monte, a monk of Ripoll, recorded that he copied five books concerning the apostle from a book at Santiago.
- Later additions to CF-14 may extend toward approximately **1173** or slightly beyond.

The Spanish Memory of the World register therefore gives the broad range **1138–1172**, rather than a single date. [Spanish Ministry of Culture register](https://www.cultura.gob.es/en/cultura/archivos/difusion/registro-memoria-unesco/2017/codice-calixtino/liber-calixtinus.html)

A date such as “c. 1140” is a useful shorthand for the main production campaign, not a documented completion year for all 225 present folios.

### 2.2 Place of manufacture

Three broad models have competed:

1. **Compostelan manufacture:** CF-14 was produced in, or under the direction of, the Cathedral of Santiago’s scriptorium. Its institutional agenda, local knowledge, subsequent presence at Santiago, and aspects of its script and decoration support this.
2. **French or trans-Pyrenean compilation:** the texts and music show strong connections to Poitou, Cluny, Vézelay, Paris, and the pilgrimage roads of France. Some scholars have assigned an especially large role to the Poitevin cleric Aymeric Picaud.
3. **Composite model:** materials were written or collected in several regions and assembled or monumentalized at Compostela in more than one campaign. This best accommodates the book’s heterogeneous texts and international repertory without turning one named traveler into the author of everything.

The manuscript’s own explicit claims—that material was gathered in Rome, Jerusalem, France, Germany, Italy, and especially Cluny—belong to its rhetoric of universal authority. They cannot by themselves locate the workshop where CF-14’s parchment was ruled, written, painted, and bound.

The UCM catalogue responsibly gives “scriptorium of the Cathedral of Santiago de Compostela (?)” with a question mark. [UCM manuscript record](https://www.ucm.es/esleim2/calixtinus)

### 2.3 Makers and commissioners

No surviving contract, account, payment record, or unambiguous colophon names the commissioner, parchment maker, principal scribe, illuminator, or binder of the original codex.

Probable institutional context:

- The compilation promoted the apostolic status of the Compostelan see.
- It supplied a grand Roman-form liturgy for Saint James.
- It connected Santiago to papal, Cluniac, French, and Carolingian authority.
- It described and regulated the international pilgrimage network terminating at the cathedral.
- Its program accords with the ambitions of the Compostelan church during and after the episcopate of **Diego Gelmírez**, although direct documentary proof that Gelmírez commissioned CF-14 has not been found.

Any statement that Aymeric Picaud “wrote the Codex Calixtinus” is too strong. At most, he is a candidate for participation in compilation, delivery, or the composition of particular portions, especially Book V and some poetic material.

### 2.4 Earliest external attestation: Ripoll, 1173

The most important early documentary witness is now **Archivo de la Corona de Aragón, Ripoll 99**. Its letter says that **Arnaldus de Monte**, monk of Santa Maria de Ripoll, visited Santiago and copied a volume containing five books concerning the apostle. The manuscript explicitly dates this activity to **1173**. The relevant text survives at Ripoll 99, ff. 47v–48r. [Archive of the Crown of Aragon record](https://www.cultura.gob.es/archivos-aca/en/actividades/exposiciones-virtuales/expo-patronato/exposicion/archivo-como-museo/manuscritos.html)

This proves that a five-book *Liber Sancti Iacobi* was available at Santiago by 1173. It does **not conclusively prove** that the exemplar was CF-14 in exactly its present state.

Two reconstructions remain in circulation:

- **Traditional identification:** Arnaldo copied the Compostela Codex itself, making 1173 a firm *terminus ante quem* for CF-14’s principal body.
- **Alternative stemma:** Arnaldo copied another, perhaps lost, Compostelan exemplar; CF-14 and Ripoll 99 may descend from related but non-identical textual traditions.

Comparative musical work by Verena Förster Binz has argued that Ripoll 99 is neither simply copied from the present CF-14 nor necessarily copied from one lost archetype shared with it. The two may represent distinguishable traditions. The Archive of the Crown of Aragon accordingly describes this as an active century-old debate. [Archive discussion of Ripoll 99](https://www.cultura.gob.es/archivos-aca/it/actividades/documentos-para-la-historia-de-europa/calixtinus.html)

### 2.5 The Aymeric Picaud and Gerberga notice

An added bifolium near the end, now f. 221, contains a document purporting to show that **Aymeric Picaud**, a cleric associated with Parthenay-le-Vieux in Poitou, and his companion **Gerberga of Flanders** brought or donated the book to Santiago. An associated papal document attributes recognition of the gift to **Pope Innocent II**.

Evidentiary status:

- The writing exists materially in CF-14.
- It is a later addition to the initial body.
- Its papal authority is considered spurious or fabricated.
- It may preserve a memory of real carriers, but it may equally have been constructed to authenticate the book and its international-papal pedigree.
- It does not establish that Aymeric authored all five books.
- Claims that Gerberga secretly wrote the Codex are modern speculation without documentary or codicological proof.

The notice is therefore part of the object’s medieval authentication machinery, not a modern certificate of provenance.

### 2.6 Pseudepigraphy as manufacture of authority

The prefatory letter attributed to Calixtus II says, in effect, that the pope collected material concerning Saint James over fourteen years while traveling through many regions. The codex assigns sermons, liturgical pieces, and narratives to popes, bishops, and eminent clerics.

This framework gave a heterogeneous local compilation the appearance of papal and pan-European authorization. Modern scholarship distinguishes:

- **Material fact:** the attributions are written in the manuscript.
- **Historical judgment:** Calixtus II did not compose the compilation in its surviving form.
- **Open question:** particular items may preserve authentic earlier texts or associations, even where the universal authorial framework is fictive.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 Twelfth century: production and cathedral custody

**c. 1138–1172 — Probable production and assembly.**  
The principal body and later additions were copied in multiple campaigns, probably for the institutional purposes of the Compostelan church. No external transfer document records the cathedral acquiring CF-14.

**By 1173 — A five-book exemplar at Santiago.**  
Arnaldo de Monte’s Ripoll letter establishes that an extensive *Liber Sancti Iacobi* could be consulted and copied at the cathedral. Whether that exemplar was precisely CF-14 remains disputed.

**Later twelfth century — Additions and authentication.**  
The musical and documentary appendices were incorporated. The notice involving Aymeric Picaud and Gerberga created a traditional donation history. Because that history is embedded in a suspect documentary complex, it cannot be accepted as an ordinary deed of gift.

### 3.2 Medieval and early-modern use

Marginal annotations by Compostelan canons show that the manuscript was read and handled for centuries, reportedly at least into the sixteenth century. Its liturgical texts were resources for the cult of Saint James, but evidence that the entire bound codex was regularly carried to the choir or altar for ceremonies is limited. It increasingly functioned as a cathedral library and archival book.

Its texts circulated independently. Book IV, the Pseudo-Turpin chronicle, enjoyed an especially extensive separate transmission; selections from other books entered liturgical, hagiographic, and pilgrimage literature. Those copies are descendants or relatives in textual history, not transfers of CF-14 itself.

### 3.3 Separation of Book IV

**1609 or c. 1619 — Physical dismemberment.**  
The Pseudo-Turpin section, now ff. 163–191, was removed from the codex. The likely context was the chronicle’s loss of credibility and its politically awkward presentation of Charlemagne as conqueror or liberator of Spain. Juan de Mariana and other historians had attacked such French-centered narratives.

The motive is inferred rather than recorded in a surviving order. Proposed explanations include:

- an attempt to suppress or quarantine a text considered fabulous;
- discomfort with its pro-French political implications;
- rearrangement for independent consultation;
- less plausibly, theft.

The separated book remained in the Cathedral archive. It was not alienated to an outside owner. Alonso Rodríguez León rebound both resulting volumes. Separate foliation and physical damage record the operation. [DIAMM catalogue](https://www.diamm.ac.uk/sources/3970/)

### 3.4 Obscurity and nineteenth-century scholarly recovery

The codex ceased to command sustained attention and was effectively unknown to international scholarship, although it remained in cathedral custody.

**1879–1880 — Public scholarly notice.**  
Jesuit historian **Fidel Fita** and **Aureliano Fernández-Guerra** visited Santiago in 1879. Their 1880 *Recuerdos de un viaje a Santiago de Galicia* discussed the manuscript and reproduced or transcribed portions of its music.

**1882 — First complete publication of Book V.**  
Fita and French linguist **Julien Vinson** published what they called Book IV because the detached Pseudo-Turpin book was not then counted in the main codex. This was actually the present Book V, the Pilgrim’s Guide. The original edition survives digitally. [Google Books record of Fita and Vinson’s edition](https://books.google.com/books/about/Le_Codex_de_Saint_Jacques-de_Compostelle.html?id=SlAKAQAAMAAJ)

**1886 — Recovery of the detached Book IV.**  
Cathedral archivist and historian **Antonio López Ferreiro** identified the separate Pseudo-Turpin volume in the Cathedral archive as the missing fourth book. Fita publicized the finding. Accounts calling this the “rediscovery of the Codex” collapse two events: renewed study of the main manuscript in 1879–1882 and recognition of its detached Book IV in 1886. [Valladolid study discussing the 1886 recovery](https://uvadoc.uva.es/bitstream/handle/10324/4305/Disc.Apert.UVA1991-92.PDF?sequence=1)

No change of ownership occurred. Scholarship restored the object’s identity before conservation physically restored its unity.

### 3.5 Twentieth-century editions and restoration

**1930s–1944 — First full scholarly edition.**  
Walter Muir Whitehill prepared the first complete printed transcription directly from the manuscript. In 1944 the Instituto Padre Sarmiento de Estudios Gallegos issued:

1. Whitehill’s Latin transcription;
2. Dom Germán Prado’s photographic and modern transcription of the music;
3. studies and indexes, including Jesús Carro García on the miniatures.

This transformed a closely guarded cathedral manuscript into an internationally usable source. [Biblioteca Digital de Madrid bibliographic record](https://bibliotecavirtualmadrid.comunidad.madrid/bvmadrid_publicacion/es/consulta/registro.do?id=425678)

**1951 — Spanish translation.**  
Abelardo Moralejo, Casimiro Torres, and Julio Feo published a complete Spanish translation, subsequently reissued.

**1966 — Physical reunification and restoration.**  
The detached Pseudo-Turpin gatherings were placed back between Books III and V. The manuscript was rebound. During the work, oversized or projecting parchment leaves were trimmed. The intervention restored the medieval textual sequence but altered historical evidence accumulated in the separate volumes and removed material from some leaf edges. It would not satisfy every principle of contemporary minimal-intervention conservation.

**1993 — Full-color commercial facsimile.**  
Kaydeda Ediciones issued a numbered facsimile of CF-14. This became a major surrogate for libraries and exhibitions. A facsimile reproduces page images but is a modern printed object with its own paper, binding, and provenance.

**1998 — New critical transcription.**  
Klaus Herbers and Manuel Santos Noia published a revised Latin edition for the Xunta de Galicia, addressing shortcomings in Whitehill.

### 3.6 Theft, concealment, recovery, and return

#### Discovery of the loss

On **5 July 2011**, cathedral personnel reported CF-14 missing from the archive’s secure chamber. The eventual judicial findings placed the taking on **4 July 2011**.

The thief was **Manuel Fernández Castiñeiras**, a former electrician who had worked at the Cathedral for approximately twenty-five years and retained intimate knowledge of its rooms, habits, and keys. Sources vary in supplying “Manuel” or “José Manuel”; the Supreme Court materials anonymized defendants, while press accounts commonly used Manuel Fernández Castiñeiras.

#### Method

The Spanish Supreme Court’s reported findings establish that Fernández Castiñeiras:

- entered the cathedral and archive area;
- passed doors not proved to have been locked;
- reached the chamber containing the Codex;
- found the chamber door with its keys left in place;
- removed the manuscript;
- concealed it under his clothing.

This was not a sophisticated international art-market operation. It was an insider theft exploiting deficient key control and institutional trust. The proceedings also exposed long-running thefts of Cathedral cash and other books and documents. [Published text of Supreme Court judgment 747/2015](https://editorial.tirant.com/free_ebooks/E000020004448.pdf)

#### Concealment

Fernández Castiñeiras placed the manuscript in a garage or garage-store at **O Milladoiro**, near Santiago. It was wrapped in newspapers or other paper, enclosed in plastic bags, and hidden among boxes, building materials, or sacks. Later accounts say it was inside a rabbit-feed sack. The precise sequence of wrappings varies by report; their common documentary core is concealment in paper and plastic in his garage.

#### Investigation

The Policía Nacional’s Historical Heritage Brigade:

- conducted visual inspections;
- interviewed or took statements from almost fifty people connected with the case;
- examined properties associated with Fernández Castiñeiras and his family;
- recovered more than €1.2 million in cash during the initial searches;
- seized cathedral keys, internal correspondence, medieval or early books, religious objects, and eight modern copies or facsimiles of the Codex.

The money and other property were related to the broader criminal case but were not components of CF-14.

#### Recovery

On **4 July 2012**, almost exactly a year after the taking, police found the Codex in Fernández Castiñeiras’s O Milladoiro garage. The Spanish government’s announcement said it was held by police pending expert examination for damage. [Spanish government recovery announcement](https://www.lamoncloa.gob.es/lang/en/gobierno/news/Paginas/2012/20120704_CalixtineCodex.aspx)

Contemporaneous reports described the volume as apparently intact or in perfect condition. The archbishop and cathedral dean identified it as authentic. Its physical identity could be checked against its unique parchment, foliation, damage, decoration, binding, and extensive photographic record; there was no credible rival manuscript substituting for it.

#### Return

After police custody, authentication, and examination, the manuscript was ceremonially returned to the Cathedral on **6 July 2012**. Its ownership had never legally left the Cathedral chapter; police custody was evidentiary and protective.

#### Criminal proceedings

In February 2015, the Audiencia Provincial of A Coruña convicted Fernández Castiñeiras in connection with the Codex theft, continuing thefts, and money laundering. Spain’s Supreme Court, in judgment **747/2015 of 19 November 2015**, partly altered the legal characterization or penalty while sustaining the central findings.

The conviction concerned the theft of a real, specifically identifiable manuscript. No judicial finding supports suggestions that the Cathedral staged the loss or that the recovered volume was a facsimile.

### 3.7 Post-theft security and access

The theft led the Cathedral and other Spanish ecclesiastical repositories to review physical security, key custody, inventories, surveillance, and staff access. The exact present security arrangements are appropriately not public.

The original has appeared under controlled conditions. In June 2026 the Cathedral advertised reserved, small-group archive visits during which the original Codex, *Tumbo A*, and *Breviario de Miranda* could be viewed in the Sala López Ferreiro. That was a special program, not evidence of permanent unrestricted display. [Cathedral archive visits, June 2026](https://catedraldesantiago.es/dia-internacional-de-los-archivos-en-la-catedral/)

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Is CF-14 a genuine twelfth-century manuscript?

Yes, in the ordinary codicological sense. Its parchment, scripts, quire structures, musical notation, decoration, medieval additions, early copying history, and centuries of physical alteration are consistent with twelfth-century production and later use. No serious scholarship treats CF-14 as a modern forgery.

“Authenticity,” however, divides into separate questions:

| Question | Finding |
|---|---|
| Is CF-14 materially medieval? | Securely yes. |
| Is it the oldest surviving extensive witness of the *Liber Sancti Iacobi*? | Generally yes, subject to how individual fragments and related recensions are classified. |
| Is every part contemporary? | No; it was made and enlarged in stages. |
| Was it written by Calixtus II? | No credible evidence; attribution is pseudepigraphic. |
| Was it written entirely by Aymeric Picaud? | Unproved and unlikely. |
| Was it the exact exemplar copied by Arnaldo de Monte in 1173? | Disputed. |
| Does it transmit historically accurate accounts of James, Charlemagne, and Roland? | It mixes liturgy, institutional claims, legend, fiction, and historical recollection; each text requires separate criticism. |
| Is the post-2012 object the same manuscript stolen in 2011? | Securely identified; no serious substitution claim has evidentiary support. |

### 4.2 “Original” versus witness

Institutions and media frequently call CF-14 “the original Codex Calixtinus.” That is acceptable when distinguishing it from modern facsimiles, but textually imprecise.

The *Liber Sancti Iacobi* was compiled from earlier texts. Some of those survive elsewhere in earlier or independent forms. CF-14 is not the autograph of a single author. It is the earliest monumental manuscript witness to a composite work, produced through revision and addition.

### 4.3 Calixtus II

The attribution to Pope Calixtus II is an **official medieval fiction** embedded in the book. It served several functions:

- supplied papal authority;
- associated Compostela with a Burgundian pope connected to the Iberian royal dynasty;
- strengthened the legitimacy of the local liturgy;
- converted a heterogeneous dossier into an apparently unified work.

The attribution’s falsity does not make CF-14 a forged artifact. It makes portions of its claimed authorship pseudepigraphic.

### 4.4 Aymeric Picaud

The strongest case for Aymeric’s involvement rests on:

- his name in the added donation notice;
- the text’s detailed knowledge of western France and the pilgrimage roads;
- linguistic and cultural features compatible with a Poitevin or French cleric;
- the possibility that one mobile compiler coordinated dispersed material.

The case against identifying him as sole author includes:

- the codex’s multiple hands and compositional layers;
- the heterogeneous dates and genres of its texts;
- the dubious documentary setting in which his name occurs;
- the absence of an autograph signature or independent commission;
- strong Compostelan institutional shaping.

A careful formulation is: **Aymeric Picaud may have carried, compiled, or written some material associated with the *Liber Sancti Iacobi*, especially Book V, but he is not securely demonstrated to be author of CF-14 as a whole.**

### 4.5 Ripoll 99 and the lost-archetype problem

Arnaldo’s dated copy is the decisive continuity problem. If Ripoll 99 was copied directly from CF-14, then CF-14 was substantially complete at Santiago in 1173. If it was copied from a lost related manuscript, CF-14’s presence at Santiago is less directly documented for that year.

Comparative readings and musical variants prevent a simple assumption of direct copying. The prudent conclusion is:

- a five-book Compostelan *Liber* existed by 1173;
- CF-14 belongs to that immediate textual and institutional environment;
- exact exemplar-descendant identity remains unresolved.

### 4.6 Scientific examination

No accessible source located for this dossier documented:

- radiocarbon dating of CF-14;
- DNA or ZooMS species analysis of its parchment;
- comprehensive X-ray fluorescence or Raman identification of pigments;
- multispectral recovery of erased texts;
- dendrochronology of an original wooden binding;
- a publicly released forensic environmental report after the garage concealment.

Dating is therefore primarily palaeographic, codicological, textual, and comparative—not radiometric. Statements that “science proved it was written in 1140” are unsupported.

### 4.7 Recovery authentication

After the 2012 recovery, the archbishop, dean, police specialists, and manuscript experts accepted the volume as CF-14. Its singular material features made identification considerably stronger than merely comparing the title on a binding. No rival claimant exists, and no documented mismatch has been reported.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### 5.1 Sacred figures

- **James the Greater / Latin *Iacobus Maior*.** Central sacred subject. The codex furnishes his feasts with liturgy; recounts his miracles; narrates his martyrdom and legendary translation; makes him patron and miraculous defender of pilgrimage and Spain.
- **Christ.** Presented as the ultimate source of apostolic authority and miracles.
- **The Virgin Mary.** Appears within the liturgical and devotional framework and the codex’s Christian cosmology.
- **Angels.** Act in visions, translations, protections, and the Pseudo-Turpin narratives.
- **The Trinity.** Invoked in the book’s liturgy and theological program.

These are relationships within texts and cult, not demonstrable physical encounters with the manuscript.

### 5.2 Medieval ecclesiastical and political persons

- **Pope Calixtus II (d. 1124):** pseudonymous authorial authority; no proof he made or possessed CF-14.
- **Pope Innocent II (pontificate 1130–1143):** named in suspect documentation supporting the supposed donation.
- **Diego Gelmírez (bishop/archbishop of Santiago, d. 1140):** the codex’s program fits the ecclesiastical ambitions of his see; direct commission remains inference.
- **Aymeric Picaud:** possible contributor, compiler, or carrier; named in the added donation notice.
- **Gerberga of Flanders:** named as Aymeric’s companion in that notice; no proof she authored or copied the manuscript.
- **Arnaldo de Monte / Arnaldus de Monte:** Ripoll monk who copied a five-book Compostelan exemplar in 1173, providing the earliest firm external anchor.
- **Alberic of Ostia:** associated in the manuscript with a miracle notice dated 1139, important for dating.
- **Charlemagne:** literary protagonist of Book IV; no contact with the twelfth-century physical manuscript.
- **Roland:** literary protagonist in Book IV.
- **Archbishop Turpin:** fictive narrator or attributed author of Book IV; the surviving chronicle is not an authentic eyewitness report by the historical Turpin.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Liturgical function

Book I provides offices, sermons, readings, chants, Mass material, hymns, and feast observances for Saint James, particularly the July and December festivals. The codex helped present Compostela as possessing a liturgy worthy of an apostolic shrine.

The texts are evidence that the compilers intended or represented these rites for performance. They do not prove that every musical or homiletic item in CF-14 was performed exactly as written in the Cathedral. The scale and archival history of the volume suggest that it could also function as an authoritative exemplar from which practical service books were copied.

### 6.2 Pilgrimage

Book V maps four principal French routes converging toward the Pyrenees and the route across northern Iberia to Compostela. It discusses:

- stages and distances;
- hospices, bridges, churches, and relics;
- water quality and dangerous rivers;
- peoples encountered along the road;
- commercial deception and bad hospitality;
- the city of Santiago and its cathedral;
- conduct expected of pilgrims and shrine personnel.

Its ethnographic insults, praise, warnings, and institutional recommendations are the judgments of a particular clerical text, not neutral descriptions of entire peoples. Modern route promotion often extracts attractive passages while minimizing the guide’s hostility toward Navarrese, Basques, and other groups.

### 6.3 Miracles and protection

Book II presents Saint James as:

- freeing captives;
- rescuing travelers at sea;
- healing the sick;
- saving pilgrims from robbery, hanging, drowning, or demonic deception;
- punishing fraud or irreverence;
- accepting or defending sincere pilgrims.

These are medieval miracle claims. Their historical importance lies in how they represented the risks of pilgrimage, advertised the saint’s reach, and encouraged recourse to the Compostelan cult. They are not independently verified supernatural events.

### 6.4 Institutional legitimation

The manuscript advanced several linked claims:

- James preached or possessed apostolic authority connected with Spain.
- His body came miraculously to Galicia.
- Compostela was therefore an apostolic see.
- Popes and eminent churchmen approved its liturgy.
- Charlemagne opened or protected the route.
- The shrine belonged within the universal Christian geography of Rome and Jerusalem.

Possession of the manuscript did not legally confer sovereignty in the manner of regalia. Its power was documentary and symbolic: it assembled the arguments, rites, and stories by which Compostela represented itself as one of Latin Christendom’s principal pilgrimage centers.

### 6.5 Access and taboo

No medieval rule has been located stating that only a particular officeholder could touch or see CF-14. Access would nevertheless have been restricted by literacy, clerical status, library custody, and the value of parchment books.

Modern access is controlled for conservation and security. The 2011 theft demonstrated that customary trust and informal key practices had overridden effective restriction. Since recovery, viewing the original has generally required institutional permission or participation in a controlled exhibition or archive visit.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The pope-authored universal book

**Story:** Calixtus II spent fourteen years traveling through many lands and gathering materials concerning Saint James, then authorized the collection.

- **Earliest attestation:** the medieval prefatory apparatus of the *Liber* itself.
- **Status:** medieval pseudepigraphy.
- **Function:** papal authorization, textual unity, international prestige.
- **Factual core:** the compilation genuinely incorporates material from different regions and periods.
- **Against literal acceptance:** Calixtus died before some datable contents; the physical manuscript was copied by several later hands.

### 7.2 The apostle’s translation to Galicia

**Story:** after James was martyred in Jerusalem, disciples transported his body by sea to Galicia under divine guidance; miraculous events secured the burial place later revealed at Compostela.

- **Source within the codex:** Book III, drawing on earlier Jacobean translation traditions.
- **Status:** sacred legend and institutional tradition.
- **Material relationship:** CF-14 is not the relic and cannot authenticate the body; it is a major medieval witness to the story.
- **Historical effect:** the legend explains and legitimizes the shrine to which the codex directs worship and travel.

### 7.3 Charlemagne and the star road

**Story:** Saint James appeared to Charlemagne, showed him a road of stars leading toward Galicia, and commanded him to free the apostle’s land and road from Muslim rule. Charlemagne campaigned in Spain; Roland and other heroes died in the resulting wars.

- **Source:** Book IV, the Pseudo-Turpin.
- **Status:** medieval legendary historiography, not an eyewitness chronicle.
- **Factual core:** Charlemagne’s forces did campaign south of the Pyrenees in 778, and Roland’s death at Roncevaux entered epic tradition.
- **Legendary transformation:** the expedition becomes a divinely ordered proto-crusade establishing the Camino.
- **Institutional use:** the story links French pilgrims, Carolingian prestige, and Compostela.
- **Later consequence for the object:** the chronicle’s historical discredit and political awkwardness probably helped cause its physical removal from the codex in the seventeenth century.

### 7.4 Aymeric’s delivery

**Story:** Aymeric Picaud and Gerberga of Flanders brought the codex to Santiago and donated it with papal approval.

- **Earliest attestation:** the appended manuscript documents themselves.
- **Status:** medieval provenance claim in a suspect or forged documentary setting.
- **Possible factual core:** a French cleric and companion may have transported texts or participated in compilation.
- **Unsupported elaboration:** that Aymeric single-handedly authored all five books, or that Gerberga secretly authored them.

### 7.5 “The first travel guide”

This is a **modern popular reframing**, not the manuscript’s medieval title or complete function. Book V can reasonably be described as an exceptionally early systematic European pilgrimage guide, but the full codex is chiefly liturgical and hagiographic.

The label has shaped tourism, journalism, facsimile marketing, and Camino identity. It also leads to recurrent errors:

- treating the guide as the entire manuscript;
- calling Aymeric a modern-style travel writer;
- assuming pilgrims carried CF-14 along the road;
- converting polemical medieval ethnography into objective tourism reporting.

### 7.6 Theft myths and conspiracy narratives

The unusual theft generated claims that:

- an international collector ordered it;
- the manuscript was taken to suppress secrets;
- the recovered volume was a facsimile;
- cathedral authorities staged the affair.

The investigation and conviction instead support an insider theft by Fernández Castiñeiras, who concealed the original locally. No credible evidence has emerged for an occult secret, commissioned international theft, or substitution.

The Codex contains legends; it is not itself known to bear a curse, supernatural protection, or prophecy comparable to legends attached to some regalia and relics.

## 8. Current Status and Location

As of September 2026:

- **Object:** original Codex Calixtinus, CF-14.
- **Location:** Archivo-Biblioteca de la Catedral de Santiago de Compostela, Santiago de Compostela, Galicia, Spain.
- **Controller and owner:** Cathedral chapter under Spanish ecclesiastical ownership and the applicable Spanish and Galician cultural-heritage regimes.
- **Condition:** publicly described as good and intact after recovery, subject to the known earlier trimming, rebinding, wear, and structural interventions.
- **Access:** restricted and supervised. It is not an ordinary open-stack manuscript. Special archive visits and exhibitions occasionally permit viewing.
- **Custody dispute:** none located. No repatriation claimant or rival owner is presently asserting title.
- **Identity dispute:** none credible regarding the recovered artifact.
- **UNESCO status:** inscribed in 2017 on the **Memory of the World International Register** as *Codex Calixtinus or Compostellanus*, recognizing documentary significance rather than transferring ownership. [Spanish UNESCO-register page](https://www.cultura.gob.es/cultura/archivos/difusion/registro-memoria-unesco/2017/codice-calixtino/liber-calixtinus.html)
- **Recent documentation:** high-resolution photography of the original was undertaken for a new facsimile presented in June 2026. Cathedral staff and the publisher performed color adjustment in direct comparison with CF-14. [Cathedral announcement](https://catedraldesantiago.es/presentacion-de-la-nueva-edicion-facsimilar-del-codice-calixtino/)

The 2026 photography is a new documentary record of condition and color, not a scientific redating.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Editions and facsimiles

Important stages include:

- **1882:** Fita and Vinson’s Latin edition of Book V.
- **1931:** Peter Wagner’s study of the Jacobean chants.
- **1944:** Whitehill’s complete Latin transcription; Prado’s music volume; Carro García’s study of miniatures.
- **1951:** complete Spanish translation by Moralejo, Torres, and Feo.
- **1988:** Manuel C. Díaz y Díaz’s major codicological monograph.
- **1993:** numbered full-scale Kaydeda facsimile.
- **1998:** revised Latin transcription by Herbers and Santos Noia.
- **2026:** new Incipit Manuscript Ediciones facsimile, limited to 500 copies, based on high-resolution photography. Copy no. 1 was announced as a gift to Pope Leo XIV.

Facsimiles now exist in libraries and pilgrimage organizations worldwide. Each may be advertised as “the Codex Calixtinus,” but none shares CF-14’s medieval parchment or custody history. During the 2011 investigation, police recovered eight facsimiles or copies from properties connected with the thief; these were not competing originals.

### 9.2 Music performance

Modern early-music ensembles have reconstructed and recorded the manuscript’s chant and polyphony. Performance necessarily adds decisions absent or ambiguous in the notation:

- rhythm;
- voice alignment;
- treatment of unstaffed neumes;
- pronunciation;
- whether certain parts were alternatives or simultaneous voices;
- instrumental participation.

Recorded “music of the Codex Calixtinus” is therefore modern performance scholarship based on medieval notation, not an audible preservation of a twelfth-century occasion.

### 9.3 Camino revival and tourism

The twentieth- and twenty-first-century revival of the Camino made Book V the codex’s public face. Passages are reproduced in guidebooks, exhibitions, municipal interpretation, route literature, journalism, and pilgrim certificates. The codex provides historical prestige for the modern route economy, while the modern Camino supplies an audience and commercial market for translations and facsimiles.

Its influence is often retrospective: medieval pilgrims did not generally carry or consult CF-14. The book describes and ideologically orders a network; it was not a mass-produced handbook.

### 9.4 Political and cultural symbolism

The codex has been invoked as:

- evidence of medieval European mobility;
- a foundational document of the Camino;
- a symbol of Galicia’s place in European culture;
- a monument of Spanish documentary heritage;
- evidence of cultural contact among Iberian and trans-Pyrenean regions;
- a musical monument of medieval Europe.

These claims can coexist, but nationalist readings may selectively emphasize either its Compostelan production or its French and European connections. The artifact itself records a mixed institutional world rather than a modern national category.

### 9.5 Public image after the theft

The 2011–2012 theft moved CF-14 from specialist fame into international news. Its garage concealment became part of the object’s modern biography and exposed the vulnerability of church archives. Subsequent accounts, documentaries, and dramatizations often focus on the eccentricity of the thief and the cash found in his properties. That criminal narrative should not displace the manuscript’s much longer material and textual history.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Early antiquarian study

Nineteenth-century scholars approached the codex principally as:

- a source for pilgrimage geography;
- evidence for the medieval cult of James;
- a witness to the Charlemagne-Roland tradition;
- a musical curiosity.

Fita and Fernández-Guerra introduced it to a broad learned readership. López Ferreiro integrated it into his monumental history of the Compostelan church.

### 10.2 The Whitehill edition

Whitehill’s 1944 transcription was the first complete printed access to the Latin manuscript. Germán Prado’s music volume made its notation widely available. The edition remains foundational but is not a diplomatic facsimile and contains readings later corrected.

The scholarly review history notes that, before 1944, only selected offices, sermons, the translation narrative, hymns, musical passages, and Book V had been printed directly from CF-14. [Contemporary review of the Whitehill edition](https://publicacions.iec.cat/repository/pdf/00000149/00000008.pdf)

### 10.3 Díaz y Díaz and the material turn

Manuel C. Díaz y Díaz’s codicological study, especially *El Códice Calixtino de la Catedral de Santiago* (1988), changed the debate by emphasizing:

- multiple hands;
- successive production campaigns;
- alterations to gatherings;
- the relationship of contents to physical structure;
- a stronger Compostelan role in manufacture;
- caution toward unitary authorship by Aymeric.

His proposed chronology includes some materials earlier than the main copying campaign. Catalogue summaries occasionally compress this into confusing date strings such as “1138–1173; 1050–1060.” The earlier dates apply to textual or musical antecedents, not to the manufacture of the present bound parchment codex as a whole.

### 10.4 French, Compostelan, and composite-origin models

The old dispute is not simply “France or Spain.” It involves several levels:

- where individual texts originated;
- where the archetypal compilation was assembled;
- where CF-14 was copied;
- where its music came from;
- where its illuminators trained;
- who authorized its institutional program.

French linguistic and musical affinities do not prove manufacture in France. Compostelan institutional purpose does not prove every component was composed locally. The most durable modern model is transregional compilation followed by staged Compostelan production or intervention.

### 10.5 Textual criticism and newly identified witnesses

Study of manuscripts such as Ripoll 99, Salamanca MS 2631, Vatican, Madrid, British, and other fragmentary or abridged witnesses has shown that the *Liber* circulated in multiple recensions. A recent study of Douai 842 adds evidence for a shorter *Libellus sancti Iacobi* tradition and further undermines the assumption that every witness descends mechanically from present CF-14. [“El *Libellus sancti Iacobi* en Douai 842”](https://estudiosmedievales.revistas.csic.es/index.php/estudiosmedievales/article/view/1331)

The object history of CF-14 and the stemmatic history of its text therefore overlap but are not identical.

### 10.6 Major surviving related manuscripts

These are not fragments of CF-14:

- **ACA, Ripoll 99:** Arnaldo de Monte’s dated 1173 selection or copy.
- **Universidad de Salamanca, MS 2631:** fourteenth-century *Liber Sancti Iacobi* witness with distinct illumination and contents. [Salamanca catalogue](https://gredos.usal.es/handle/10366/128808?locale-attribute=es&show=full)
- Manuscripts or extracts in the Biblioteca Nacional de España, Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana, British Library, and other European collections.

The number of Pseudo-Turpin witnesses is much greater than the number of complete five-book *Liber* manuscripts because Book IV circulated independently.

### 10.7 Unresolved questions

The principal unresolved questions include:

1. Was CF-14 made wholly at Compostela?
2. How much of the compilation, if any, was coordinated by Aymeric Picaud?
3. Was CF-14 the exact book Arnaldo saw in 1173?
4. What was the precise sequence and date of its production campaigns?
5. Which musical attributions preserve genuine authorship?
6. What was the exact original quire structure near the appendices?
7. Was Book IV detached in 1609, 1619, or through a process spanning those dates?
8. Who ordered that separation, and was the motive doctrinal, historiographic, political, or practical?
9. What materials were removed in the 1966 trimming, and is a complete technical record preserved?
10. What unpublished conservation findings were made after the 2012 recovery?

### 10.8 Discredited or overstated claims still circulating

- **“Calixtus II wrote the Codex.”** Pseudepigraphic tradition, not established authorship.
- **“Aymeric Picaud wrote the entire Codex.”** Possible participant elevated into an unsupported sole author.
- **“It was written in 1140.”** Approximate shorthand presented as an exact date.
- **“It is the world’s first tourist guide.”** Modern promotional simplification of Book V.
- **“The codex was lost until 1886.”** It remained in the Cathedral; scholars renewed attention to the main volume before identifying detached Book IV.
- **“Book IV was stolen in 1609.”** Separation is documented materially; theft is not established.
- **“The 2012 manuscript might be a replica.”** Unsupported by the recovery, examination, and judicial record.
- **“UNESCO owns or administers it.”** False; inscription recognizes heritage significance without changing title.

The traditional papal authorship and donation apparatus are historically important medieval claims but not reliable modern provenance documents.
