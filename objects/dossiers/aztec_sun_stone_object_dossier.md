# The Sun Stone / Aztec Calendar Stone: Object Dossier

**Object tracked:** the single monumental Mexica carved disk recovered in Mexico City’s Plaza Mayor on 17 December 1790 and now displayed in the Sala Mexica of the Museo Nacional de Antropología, Mexico City.

**Filename:** `the_sun_stone_aztec_calendar_stone_object_dossier.md`

**Status of evidence labels used below:**

- **Documented:** supported by contemporary records, institutional custody records, photographs, or the surviving object.
- **Scientific/technical finding:** based on physical examination or geological/material identification.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** an argued reading of images, dates, function, or historical references.
- **Traditional identification:** repeated in institutional or popular accounts but not securely demonstrated.
- **Legend or modern accretion:** a narrative whose historical basis is absent, uncertain, or demonstrably late.
- **Unresolved:** evidence is presently insufficient to decide.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity and names

The object is a monumental, circular, relief-carved Mexica stone sculpture conventionally called the **Piedra del Sol** (“Sun Stone”), **Calendario Azteca** (“Aztec Calendar”), **Calendario Mexicano** (“Mexican Calendar”), **Aztec Calendar Stone**, and occasionally **Piedra de los Soles** (“Stone of the Suns”). “Aztec Calendar” became especially influential through eighteenth- and nineteenth-century antiquarian interpretations, beginning with Antonio de León y Gama’s attempt to read the object as a calendrical and astronomical instrument. It remains the most familiar commercial name, but it is potentially misleading: the monument contains calendrical signs and cosmic-period imagery, yet it is not a complete operational calendar in the modern sense.

No securely attested pre-Hispanic proper name for this particular surviving disk is known. Post-Conquest sources use overlapping terms for monumental ritual stones:

- **temalacatl** or **temalácatl**, generally interpreted as a large round “stone spindle” or disk used in gladiatorial sacrifice;
- **cuauhxicalli**, from Nahuatl *cuāuhtli* (“eagle”) and *xīcalli* (“gourd vessel” or bowl), conventionally “eagle vessel,” a receptacle for sacrificial blood or hearts;
- **cuauhxicalco**, a building or enclosure associated with such rites, sometimes confused in translations with *cuauhxicalli*;
- Spanish expressions including *piedra del Sol*, *piedra grande*, *tajón*, *rueda de piedra*, and “sacrificial stone.”

These terms do not always denote the same type of object, and colonial authors sometimes conflated buildings, containers, altars, and gladiatorial platforms. No colonial passage can be connected to the surviving Sun Stone merely because it mentions a “great stone” or “stone of the sun.” Yolotl González Torres’s comparison of the terminology emphasizes this persistent ambiguity ([Estudios de Cultura Náhuatl](https://nahuatl.historicas.unam.mx/index.php/ecn/article/view/78135)).

### Material

**Scientific/technical identification:** a dense volcanic rock normally described as **olivine basalt** or basaltic volcanic stone. Older mineralogical descriptions vary. Alexander von Humboldt called it a dark gray trap or porphyritic basaltic rock and noted elongated feldspar crystals, amphibole, and mica-like inclusions. Later Mexican catalogues identify olivine basalt.

Institutional accounts generally place the probable source in the volcanic terrain south of Mexico-Tenochtitlan—either the Pedregal de San Ángel/Xitle region or the Xochimilco area. This is a **probable regional attribution**, not an identified quarry demonstrated by a published geochemical match. An INAH exhibition catalogue accordingly says that the material “could have been obtained” in the Pedregal de San Ángel or Xochimilco ([INAH catalogue](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/images/exposiciones/catalogos/catalogo_1612.pdf)). Geological compatibility alone does not prove which lava flow supplied the block.

### Dimensions and weight

Published measurements vary slightly according to measuring method and rounding:

- diameter: approximately **3.55–3.60 m**;
- thickness: commonly reported around **1.0–1.22 m**;
- estimated weight: approximately **24–24.5 metric tonnes**.

An early Museo Nacional description gives a mean diameter of 3.55 m and an estimated 24,400 kg ([Anales del Museo Nacional](https://mna.inah.gob.mx/docs/anales/196.pdf)). Current INAH descriptions normally round the diameter to 3.6 m and weight to 24 tonnes ([INAH](https://inah.gob.mx/foto-del-dia/piedra-del-sol)).

The relief occupies one broad face. The circumference and rear are much less elaborately worked. Its mass, substantial thickness, and lack of a known architectural mounting make reconstruction of its original installation difficult.

### Structure of the carved face

The design is organized in concentric registers, although the conventional number of “rings” depends on whether narrow bands are counted independently.

1. **Central face and 4-Ollin frame.** A frontal face appears within the quadripartite sign *Nahui Ollin*, “4 Movement.” The figure has clawed hands holding human hearts and a projecting flint-knife-like tongue. It has traditionally been identified as the solar deity **Tonatiuh**. Alternative readings identify it as an earth-associated deity or monster, as **Tlaltecuhtli**, as a nocturnal solar/earth power sometimes called **Yohualtecuhtli**, as a composite solar-earth image, or—most recently and controversially—as a deified ruler, Motecuhzoma II, fused with Huitzilopochtli and the sun.

2. **Four previous suns or cosmic eras.** Four compartments surrounding the central face contain signs conventionally read as:
   - **4 Jaguar** (*Nahui Ocelotl*);
   - **4 Wind** (*Nahui Ehecatl*);
   - **4 Rain** (*Nahui Quiahuitl*);
   - **4 Water** (*Nahui Atl*).

   In colonial Nahua cosmogonic accounts, these name destroyed earlier worlds. The precise ordering and associated beings differ among textual traditions.

3. **Additional calendrical and directional elements.** Smaller signs and quincunx-like arrangements have been related to cardinal directions, world quarters, solar rays, and the spatial ordering of time.

4. **Twenty day signs.** A prominent band contains the twenty named signs of the 260-day *tonalpohualli*: Crocodile/Caiman (*Cipactli*), Wind (*Ehecatl*), House (*Calli*), Lizard (*Cuetzpalin*), Serpent (*Coatl*), Death (*Miquiztli*), Deer (*Mazatl*), Rabbit (*Tochtli*), Water (*Atl*), Dog (*Itzcuintli*), Monkey (*Ozomatli*), Grass (*Malinalli*), Reed (*Acatl*), Jaguar (*Ocelotl*), Eagle (*Cuauhtli*), Vulture (*Cozcacuauhtli*), Movement (*Ollin*), Flint (*Tecpatl*), Rain (*Quiahuitl*), and Flower (*Xochitl*). Their presence is the strongest reason for the “calendar stone” designation, but the sequence alone does not constitute a complete calendrical calculator.

5. **Bands of repeated elements.** Registers of dots, quincunxes, feather- or jade-like forms, solar rays, and other conventional motifs have received competing numerical, astronomical, sacrificial, and royal readings.

6. **Fire serpents.** Two enormous **xiuhcoatl** (“turquoise/fire serpent”) bodies frame the design. Their heads meet at the bottom, where human or divine faces emerge from their jaws. Their tails meet near the top.

7. **Date 13 Reed.** Between the upper tails is the year sign **13 Acatl**, “13 Reed.” It is not an alphabetic inscription and does not name a ruler in plain text. Competing interpretations treat it as:
   - the historical year **1479**, during Axayácatl’s reign;
   - the mythic year in which the present sun was created;
   - an ideologically chosen date capable of referring to both myth and political history;
   - a retrospective or commemorative date on a monument made under Motecuhzoma II.

The disk therefore carries no continuous written dedication, maker’s signature, quarry mark, or unequivocal statement of manufacture.

### Construction technique

The sculpture was produced by quarrying or selecting a massive volcanic block, roughly shaping it, transporting it, flattening the display face, and carving the relief by pecking, percussion, abrasion, and finishing with stone tools. Copper or harder mineral abrasives may have assisted finishing, but a tool-by-tool technical analysis of this specific monument has not been published in the accessible institutional literature used here.

The high regularity of the concentric design implies careful planning, measured layout, and coordinated work by specialist stone carvers. No named sculptor is attested.

### Orientation and intended posture

The modern display is vertical. Many scholars believe the object originally functioned horizontally or on a low platform, principally because:

- comparable *temalacatl* and *cuauhxicalli* monuments were horizontal;
- the imagery is radially organized;
- colonial accounts describe captives fighting or being sacrificed on round stones;
- the monument’s thickness and mass suit a platform more readily than an architectural wall relief.

This remains a **scholarly reconstruction**, not a documented observation of the stone in its pre-Conquest setting. The cavity or damaged central area has sometimes been described as a blood or heart receptacle, but it is not the deep central basin characteristic of some securely identified *cuauhxicalli*.

### Color

Mexica monumental sculpture was often painted. The surviving Sun Stone is now predominantly the natural gray-brown of weathered basalt. Early descriptions and images do not establish a complete original color scheme. Twentieth-century reconstructions have proposed combinations of red, ochre, blue, white, and black, but no comprehensive pigment analysis securely reconstructing the monument’s original polychromy was located.

A secondary institutional publication states that the Sun Stone, Coatlicue, and Stone of Tízoc were painted after installation in the 1964 museum ([Museo Soumaya publication](https://www.museosoumaya.org/wp-content/revistas/2021/2021-07Julio.pdf)). The precise materials, extent, date, and subsequent removal or weathering of that intervention require confirmation from MNA conservation files.

### Damage and condition

The monument survives substantially intact, but its carved face is worn and locally damaged. Visible losses include edge chipping, abrasions, impact pits, erosion of shallow details, and a conspicuous fracture or fissure extending into the upper area. Exposure outdoors against the cathedral for approximately ninety-four years subjected it to rain, temperature cycling, pollution, handling, and impact.

A frequently repeated story says occupying United States soldiers used it for target practice in 1847. Pits on the surface are sometimes presented as bullet scars. The occupation itself is historical, but no contemporary military report, police record, diary, or conservation study securely attributing particular damage to gunfire was located. The claim should therefore be labeled **popular tradition or unverified historical allegation**, not documented fact.

There is no evidence that the disk has been replaced, divided, substantially reconstructed, exported, sold, or privately owned since 1790.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Physical origin

The stone is a Late Postclassic Mexica work, stylistically and archaeologically attributable to imperial Mexico-Tenochtitlan before the Spanish conquest of 1521. The basalt probably came from the southern Basin of Mexico, perhaps the Pedregal de San Ángel/Xitle or Xochimilco volcanic zone. No secure quarry face, abandoned extraction scar, transport route, workshop account, payment record, or named artist has been identified.

Its place of discovery—within the former ceremonial and political center of Mexico-Tenochtitlan—strongly supports manufacture for the Mexica capital. It does not prove where within the sacred precinct it originally stood.

### Competing dates

#### Axayácatl and 1479

The traditional dating makes the year glyph **13 Reed** equal to **1479**, within the reign of Axayácatl (1469–1481). Diego Durán, writing in the later sixteenth century, says Axayácatl was occupied with a famous great stone carved with “months and years, days and weeks.” Hernando Alvarado Tezozómoc preserves a related account. Alfredo Chavero and many later writers joined these passages to the surviving disk.

This argument has three weaknesses:

1. Durán and Tezozómoc wrote after the conquest.
2. Several monumental ritual stones existed.
3. The descriptions are not sufficiently individualized to prove that their stone is the surviving Sun Stone.

Thus “completed in 1479 for Axayácatl” is an old, plausible identification, not a documented commission.

#### Motecuhzoma II, 1502–1520

Other scholars date the monument to the reign of Motecuhzoma II. Arguments include stylistic placement, proposed ruler imagery, readings of royal insignia, and comparison with monuments associated with the ruler. David Stuart interprets the central image as a deified portrait of Motecuhzoma II fused with Huitzilopochtli and solar power ([University of Texas announcement](https://art.utexas.edu/news/new-book-david-stuart-reconsiders-identity-calendar-stone-tenochtitlan)). A 2023 calendrical study similarly proposes a connection between the creation of the Fifth Sun and Motecuhzoma II’s accession ([Estudios de Cultura Náhuatl](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S0071-16752023000100047&script=sci_arttext)).

INAH publications are not fully uniform: one public account repeats 1479 under Axayácatl, while another exhibition text proposes manufacture around 1512 for Motecuhzoma II. This institutional variation accurately reflects unsettled scholarship rather than two physical objects.

#### The 1507 New Fire ceremony

Another interpretation connects the monument with the **New Fire ceremony of 1507**, the completion of a 52-year Calendar Round. This would give its cosmic registers and year sign a commemorative function under Motecuhzoma II. The hypothesis is compatible with a sixteenth-century date but remains interpretive rather than epigraphically demonstrated.

### Accounts of quarrying and failed transport

Juan de Torquemada’s *Monarquía Indiana* reports that Motecuhzoma II ordered a great stone brought from **Tenanitlan/Tenanitla**, near Coyoacán. When workers attempted to cross a bridge at Xoloco, the bridge failed and the stone fell. The stone spoke or resisted in some variants, warning that it would not reach its destination; many haulers died. Torquemada’s text can be consulted in the UNAM critical digital edition ([UNAM, *Monarquía Indiana*](https://historicas.unam.mx/publicaciones/publicadigital/monarquia/volumen/01/03Libro_Segundo/miv1144.pdf)).

This episode is sometimes used as the quarry-and-transport history of the Sun Stone. That identification is untenable as a continuous physical biography unless one supposes recovery of the fallen block or conflation with another commission. The safest conclusion is:

- the story demonstrates that colonial tradition remembered imperial quarrying and transport of huge ritual stones from south of Tenochtitlan;
- it may preserve practical knowledge about movement over causeways and bridges;
- it does **not** securely identify the surviving disk.

### Earliest certain attestation of this physical object

The earliest unambiguous documentation is its recovery on **17 December 1790**. Master builder **José Damián Ortiz de Castro** reported it to colonial authorities. León y Gama reproduced the report and described the find in his 1792 book. His account says the rear surface was encountered at about half a vara below the old plaza level, eighty varas west of the second door of the royal palace and thirty-seven north of the Portal de las Flores ([León y Gama, 1792](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/63/Descripci%C3%B3n_hist%C3%B3rica_y_cronol%C3%B3gica_de_las_dos_piedras_que_con_ocasi%C3%B3n_del_nuevo_empedrado_que_se_est%C3%A1_formando_en_la_plaza_principal_de_M%C3%A9xico_%28IA_descripcionhisto00leon%29.pdf)).

The physical disk, its 1792 engraved representation, nineteenth-century paintings and photographs, and museum transfers form a continuous identity chain from this discovery to the present.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Before 1521: inferred Mexica custody

**Secure general attribution:** the stone belonged to the monumental and ritual landscape of Mexico-Tenochtitlan.

**Unresolved particulars:** its commissioning ruler, original temple or courtyard, exact orientation, and ritual history.

Candidate locations include a solar temple, a *cuauhxicalco*, an open ceremonial precinct, or another platform in or near the Templo Mayor enclosure. Assertions that it was unquestionably the disk described under Axayácatl or the object transported under Motecuhzoma II exceed the evidence.

### 1521 to the mid-sixteenth century: conquest displacement

After the fall of Tenochtitlan, the ceremonial precinct was dismantled and colonial Mexico City built over it. Durán wrote that a great sacrificial stone remained near an acequia by the cathedral and that Archbishop **Alonso de Montúfar** ordered it buried so that memory of the sacrifice performed there would be lost. Montúfar was archbishop from 1551 until 1572.

The physical Sun Stone may be the object Durán saw, but the identification is not absolutely demonstrable. If correct, the sequence would be:

1. pre-Conquest ritual installation;
2. overthrow or displacement during or shortly after the conquest;
3. survival exposed near the colonial cathedral;
4. deliberate burial by order of Montúfar between 1551 and 1572.

This is the most plausible traditional bridge across the pre-1790 gap, but documentation becomes certain only in 1790.

### 17 December 1790: rediscovery

**Documented.** Workers lowering and repaving the Plaza Mayor under the urban reforms of Viceroy **Juan Vicente de Güemes Pacheco de Padilla**, second Count of Revillagigedo, encountered the monument. It lay carved-face down, since its uncarved posterior surface appeared first. León y Gama relied on Ortiz de Castro’s official report for the date and measurements.

The discovery occurred four months after the recovery of the Coatlicue statue. The two finds became foundational objects in the formation of Mexican archaeology and the future national collection ([MNA history](https://mna.inah.gob.mx/historia_detalle.php?id=4)).

### 1791: emplacement at the cathedral

Cathedral officials **José Uribe** and **Juan José Gamboa** requested the stone from the viceroy. It was granted on the condition that it be conserved and publicly exposed. It was installed vertically against the base or exterior wall of the cathedral’s western tower, facing public space.

León y Gama supported preservation rather than reburial. The exact mechanics of lifting the 24-ton disk are incompletely documented in accessible sources, though tackle, pulleys, timber supports, rollers, and concentrated labor would have been required.

The transfer did not constitute a modern private gift. It was an administrative placement by viceregal authority with the cathedral fabric acting as custodian and public display site.

### 1791–1885: public exterior monument

For roughly ninety-four years the Sun Stone remained outdoors at the cathedral. Its custody during this period was institutionally ambiguous by modern standards: cathedral authorities maintained the wall on which it was installed, while scholars and the state increasingly treated the stone as a public antiquity.

Key engagements included:

- **1791–1792:** measurement, drawing, and interpretation by León y Gama and associated draftsmen and engravers.
- **1803–1804:** examination by Alexander von Humboldt during his stay in New Spain.
- **1810 onward:** international circulation through Humboldt’s *Vues des Cordillères* ([Smithsonian Libraries](https://library.si.edu/digital-library/book/vuesdescordille1humb)).
- **nineteenth century:** repeated drawing, painting, photography, molding, and discussion by Mexican and foreign scholars and travelers.

It was not secluded like the Coatlicue. Its accessibility made it a civic landmark but also exposed it to weather, touching, vandalism, and accidental impacts.

### 1847: alleged target practice

During the United States occupation of Mexico City, the stone remained attached to the cathedral. Later accounts claim soldiers shot at it. The allegation is plausible in context but presently unverified. No transfer, theft, or attempt to remove the monument is documented, and it remained in place after the occupation.

### 1885: removal to the Museo Nacional

**Documented.** Under Museo Nacional director **Jesús Sánchez**, the stone was detached from the cathedral and moved in August 1885 to the former Casa de Moneda building on Calle de Moneda. The operation caused public discussion and some complaints about taking a familiar monument from an open civic setting into a museum.

It was installed as the principal object of the new **Galería de Monolitos**, formally inaugurated on 16 September 1887 by President Porfirio Díaz. Historical photographs document the disk in the museum courtyard or monolith gallery ([Library of Congress photograph](https://www.loc.gov/item/2016818798/); [MNA collection history](https://mna.inah.gob.mx/historia_detalle.php?id=11)).

The 1885 movement is the point at which direct cathedral custody ended and national museum custody became explicit.

### 1885–1964: Museo Nacional, Calle de Moneda

The Sun Stone remained in the Museo Nacional through several administrative reorganizations:

- Museo Nacional;
- Museo Nacional de Arqueología, Historia y Etnografía;
- later separations of historical, anthropological, and archaeological collections.

Its physical location remained the Calle de Moneda complex even when institutional names and ministries changed. It served as the visual and conceptual center of the monumental Mexica collection.

The object was photographed with political leaders, including Porfirio Díaz and Venustiano Carranza. These appearances were political and symbolic engagements, not transfers of title or personal possession.

No wartime evacuation, revolutionary concealment, theft, sale, or substitution is documented. Museum access was interrupted or reorganized during the Mexican Revolution and later remodeling, but the object stayed in state institutional custody.

### 27 June 1964: transfer to Chapultepec

**Documented.** The disk was moved from the Calle de Moneda museum to the newly constructed Museo Nacional de Antropología in Chapultepec. Contemporary reports describe a specially engineered platform and tractor-transporter designed to reduce vibration and support the mass. The final journey reportedly took little more than an hour, though preparation took much longer. Photographs show the monument passing the cathedral and moving through Mexico City ([Excélsior retrospective](https://www.excelsior.com.mx/comunidad/2014/07/25/972700); [INEHRM photograph](https://repositorio-inehrm.cultura.gob.mx/fototeca/el-presidente-adolfo-lopez-mateos-en-la-inauguracion-de-la-nueva-sede-del-museo-nacional-de-antropologia/)).

It was installed vertically at the axial end of the **Sala Mexica**, where it became the focal point of the gallery.

### 1964–present

The stone has remained in the Museo Nacional de Antropología. No loan, overseas exhibition, deaccession, restitution proceeding, or change of ownership has been identified. Replicas, casts, photographs, and digital models travel; the original does not appear to have left Mexico City since its manufacture.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is the displayed object the one found in 1790?

**Yes, beyond reasonable historical doubt.** The chain from Ortiz de Castro’s report and León y Gama’s engraved image through nineteenth-century views, photographs at the cathedral, photographs in the Calle de Moneda museum, and the documented 1964 transfer is continuous. Individual chips and weathering changes are compatible with that continuity.

There is no serious rival claimant, replacement theory, museum substitution allegation supported by evidence, or second monument claiming to be the 1790 find.

### Is it the stone described by Durán or Tezozómoc?

**Unresolved.** The surviving monument fits the broad category of large solar and sacrificial stones described in sixteenth-century histories. Yet the sources mention several commissions, and known monuments such as the Stone of Tízoc and Stone of Motecuhzoma I show that multiple great disks existed. Similarity of type is not identity.

### Is 13 Reed a manufacture date?

**Disputed.** The equation 13 Reed = 1479 is calendrically possible and matches Axayácatl’s reign. But year signs in Mexica art can commemorate mythic events, historical ceremonies, conquests, dedications, dynastic claims, or combinations of these. The sign is evidence; “completed in 1479” is an interpretation.

### Who is the central figure?

Major positions include:

- **Tonatiuh:** traditional and still common museum/popular identification; supported by the solar context, 4-Ollin frame, rays, hearts, and sacrificial knife tongue.
- **Tlaltecuhtli or an earth monster:** supported by clawed hands, hearts, and iconographic parallels to earth deities.
- **Yohualtecuhtli or nocturnal solar-earth aspect:** attempts to reconcile solar and terrestrial traits.
- **Composite cosmic being:** treats strict one-name identification as inappropriate for Mexica visual theology.
- **Motecuhzoma II–Huitzilopochtli–solar ruler:** David Stuart’s royal-political reading, in which the central image is explicitly tied to Motecuhzoma II.
- **Eclipsed or dying sun:** Susan Milbrath interprets the image and surrounding signs in relation to eclipse danger, cosmic destruction, and earthquake ([Florida Museum](https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/science/ominous-new-interpretation-of-aztec-sun-stone/)).

No surviving caption names the figure. Institutional labels calling it Tonatiuh should be read as a conventional interpretation, not a deciphered personal name.

### Was it really a calendar?

It contains the twenty day signs and multiple calendrical dates, so “calendar” is not wholly false. It does not display all combinations needed to run the 260-day and 365-day counts, nor does it operate mechanically. Current scholarship more often treats it as a **cosmological, temporal, sacrificial, and political monument using calendrical notation**.

León y Gama’s reading as a highly technical clock, sundial, or correction device belongs to the history of scholarship. It is not accepted as a demonstrated original function.

### Was it a temalacatl or cuauhxicalli?

**Disputed.**

- Horizontal posture and comparison with ritual disks support *temalacatl* use.
- Solar imagery and heart-bearing claws support a sacrificial offering function.
- The absence of conquest scenes around the side and of an unequivocal tethering fixture complicates comparison with the Stone of Tízoc.
- Its shallow or damaged center is unlike a large purpose-made bowl.

A combined ceremonial function is possible: cosmic image, dynastic monument, sacrificial platform, and recipient of offerings need not have been mutually exclusive.

### Scientific testing

The object’s authenticity has not depended on radiocarbon dating: basalt contains no datable organic carbon corresponding to carving. Stylistic, contextual, historical, and material evidence establishes pre-Hispanic Mexica manufacture.

No published program of radiometric surface dating, portable XRF mapping, residue analysis, microscopic tool-mark analysis, or quarry-matching geochemistry specific to the entire Sun Stone was located. Absence of such a publication should not be mistaken for proof that museum conservators have never examined it. Detailed internal condition and conservation reports may remain unpublished.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Mexica rulers

- **Axayácatl, r. 1469–1481:** traditional commissioner under the 1479 interpretation. The association rests on the 13 Reed sign and post-Conquest chronicle passages, not a surviving dedication.
- **Motecuhzoma II, r. 1502–1520:** proposed commissioner or represented ruler under later datings; associated with monumental stone transport narratives and New Fire commemoration.
- **Tlacaelel:** appears in colonial histories as a political-religious architect of Mexica imperial sacrificial institutions and commissioner or advocate of solar stones. His link to this disk is inferential.
- **Motecuhzoma I, r. 1440–1469:** associated in Durán with an earlier monumental sacrificial stone, probably not the surviving Sun Stone.

### Colonial clerics and officials

- **Alonso de Montúfar, archbishop of Mexico, 1551–1572:** said by Durán to have ordered burial of a sacrificial stone to suppress memory of indigenous sacrifice. Identification with this disk is plausible but unproven.
- **Juan Vicente de Güemes, Count of Revillagigedo, viceroy, 1789–1794:** ordered the urban works that exposed the monument and authorized its preservation.
- **José Damián Ortiz de Castro:** master builder whose report documents the 17 December 1790 discovery.
- **José Uribe and Juan José Gamboa:** cathedral officials who requested custody for public display.
- **Antonio de León y Gama, 1735–1802:** astronomer and antiquarian who measured, interpreted, published, and helped preserve the object. He did not own it.
- **Francisco de Agüera:** associated with the engraved image published in León y Gama’s work; the Getty identifies a 1792 representation under his name ([Getty Research Institute](https://www.getty.edu/research/exhibitions_events/exhibitions/obsidian_mirror/aztec_calendar_stone.html)).

### Nineteenth- and twentieth-century scholars and custodians

- **Alexander von Humboldt:** examined the stone at the cathedral and circulated its image and measurements internationally.
- **Alfredo Chavero:** advanced an influential nineteenth-century Axayácatl/1479 and calendrical interpretation ([Museo Nacional text](https://mna.inah.gob.mx/docs/anales/72.pdf)).
- **Jesús Sánchez:** Museo Nacional director who initiated its 1885 removal to the museum.
- **Porfirio Díaz:** inaugurated the Galería de Monolitos in 1887 and used the ancient monumental collection within a national-modernizing state display.
- **Eduardo Matos Moctezuma and Felipe Solís Olguín:** produced influential modern syntheses emphasizing the stone’s solar, calendrical, and sacrificial dimensions ([Secretaría de Cultura bibliographic record](https://sic.gob.mx/ficha.php?table=fondo_editorial_inah&table_id=4)).
- **Leonardo López Luján:** reconstructed the monument’s repeated physical movements and the history of its study.
- **Cecelia F. Klein, Emily Umberger, Khristaan Villela, Mary Ellen Miller, Susan Milbrath, David Stuart, and other Mesoamericanists:** contributed competing iconographic, political, chronological, and functional readings.

### Divine and cosmic beings

- **Tonatiuh:** conventional identification of the central solar face.
- **Tlaltecuhtli:** earth deity or earth monster proposed as an alternative or component of the central figure.
- **Huitzilopochtli:** Mexica patron deity, solar-war power, and possible referent in political readings.
- **Xiuhcoatl:** pair of fire serpents encircling the disk.
- **Xiuhtecuhtli:** fire and time deity, sometimes connected with the fire-serpent imagery and New Fire ceremony.
- **The four prior suns:** not merely calendar labels but personified cosmic eras and modes of destruction.
- **The present Fifth Sun, Nahui Ollin:** the current world-age, destined in colonial Nahua accounts to end through movement or earthquakes.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Sacrificial use

The principal reconstruction places the stone horizontally within a ritual precinct. It may have:

- supported gladiatorial sacrifice during **Tlacaxipehualiztli**, when a captive tethered to a stone fought armed opponents;
- received blood or hearts as nourishment for the sun;
- served as a platform for other sacrificial or dedicatory performances;
- materialized the cosmic order those rites were understood to maintain.

No eyewitness description explicitly says, “this surviving carved disk was used on this named day for this sacrifice.” The reconstruction joins iconography, colonial descriptions, and comparison with related monuments.

### Calendrical and temporal function

The twenty day signs, 4-Ollin, 13 Reed, previous suns, and fire serpents integrate several temporal scales:

- the 260-day divinatory count, *tonalpohualli*;
- the 365-day solar count, *xiuhpohualli*;
- 52-year Calendar Rounds;
- mythic world ages;
- royal historical time;
- the daily passage and sacrificial maintenance of the sun.

The monument therefore does not merely “tell time.” It represents time as cosmic history, ritual obligation, and political order.

### Political function

Mexica monumental sculpture joined cosmology to conquest. A ruler who commissioned such a disk could present imperial warfare and tribute as necessary to feed the sun and preserve the world. Under the Axayácatl reading, the stone monumentalized late-fifteenth-century imperial expansion. Under the Motecuhzoma II reading, it connected the ruler’s accession or 1507 New Fire ceremony with cosmic renewal.

The absence of a unanimously accepted royal portrait does not eliminate political function: installation, access, scale, quarry labor, and sacrificial use were themselves exercises of state power.

### Access and handling

Pre-Conquest access rules are not preserved for this particular stone. If it stood in the sacred precinct, ritual specialists, rulers, warriors, captives, and attendants would have had differentiated access. Modern visitors may view but not touch it. Movement, sampling, reproduction, and conservation are regulated by INAH.

### National symbolism

After independence, the disk shifted from suppressed “idol” or antiquity to national patrimony. It came to symbolize:

- the antiquity and scientific sophistication of Indigenous Mexico;
- continuity between pre-Hispanic civilization and the modern nation;
- Mexican sovereignty and cultural distinction;
- the authority of the national museum.

Its museum placement at the end of the Sala Mexica resembles an altar-like focal arrangement. This is modern museography, not a reconstruction of its ancient setting.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The Five Suns

**Early colonial-attested cosmogony; not an invention of the modern stone literature.** Nahua sources describe successive worlds destroyed by jaguars, wind, fiery rain, and water, followed by the present era 4 Movement. The Sun Stone visually condenses this cycle.

Variants disagree about sequence, durations, gods, inhabitants, and the transformations of survivors. Modern diagrams often harmonize these differences into a single fixed doctrine. The stone confirms the importance of the four earlier eras and the present 4-Ollin world, but it does not narrate every detail found in colonial texts.

### The speaking or resisting stone

**Colonial legend.** In the Motecuhzoma transport story, an enormous stone resists imperial command, speaks, predicts disaster, or declares that it cannot reach Tenochtitlan. It falls when a bridge breaks, killing laborers.

The narrative may encode:

- the engineering risk of moving huge volcanic blocks;
- criticism of royal overreach;
- retrospective portents of the conquest;
- the notion that sacred matter possessed will and agency.

It is not securely the biography of the surviving Sun Stone.

### Burial to suppress idolatry

Durán’s report that Montúfar ordered a sacrificial stone buried is historically plausible and consistent with colonial campaigns against Indigenous ritual. The popular retelling sometimes says the Sun Stone was buried “to save it from the Spaniards.” That reverses the stated motive. If Durán’s stone was this object, concealment preserved it physically, but the reported purpose was suppression of ritual memory.

### Prophecy of the Fifth Sun’s end

The central 4-Ollin sign was linked in colonial Nahua cosmology to the end of the present world through earthquakes or violent movement. Modern accounts often turn the entire monument into an explicit “apocalypse prophecy.” That is an exaggeration: the imagery situates the current era within a history of destroyed suns, but it does not give a modern calendar date for the world’s end.

### “Maya calendar” and 2012

**Modern error.** The Sun Stone is Mexica, not Maya, and is unrelated to the Maya Long Count rollover of December 2012. Its image was nevertheless widely used in 2012 apocalypse merchandise and media, often because it was more visually recognizable than Maya inscriptions. This is a documented modern misidentification, not an ancient prediction.

### Extraterrestrials, Atlantis, lost races, and occult ages

**Modern inventions or esoteric reinterpretations.** Twentieth- and twenty-first-century writers have claimed the stone encodes:

- extraterrestrial technology;
- Atlantis or Lemuria;
- planetary catastrophes unknown to archaeology;
- zodiacal ages;
- chakras, hidden energy systems, or racial epochs;
- an impossibly precise astronomical computer.

No pre-Hispanic or early colonial source supports these claims. Their historical importance lies in modern occult reception and commercialization, not in reconstructing the artifact’s manufacture or original use.

### Bullet scars

The story that United States troops shot at the disk in 1847 is a late and widely repeated historical claim. Until contemporary documentation or forensic impact analysis is produced, it should remain explicitly labeled **unverified**.

## 8. Current Status and Location

As of **6 September 2026**, the original Sun Stone is displayed vertically in the **Sala Mexica, Museo Nacional de Antropología**, Avenida Paseo de la Reforma and Calzada Gandhi, Bosque de Chapultepec, Mexico City.

INAH’s current visitor listing identifies the Piedra del Sol and Sala Mexica and gives regular public access Tuesday through Sunday ([Museo Nacional de Antropología visitor page](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/es/node/4237)). Access conditions and ticket prices can change; the object itself is normally in permanent display rather than a temporary exhibition.

### Custody and ownership

Under articles 27 and 28 of Mexico’s **Ley Federal sobre Monumentos y Zonas Arqueológicos, Artísticos e Históricos**, pre-Hispanic archaeological monuments are property of the Nation, inalienable and imprescriptible ([current statutory text](https://www.diputados.gob.mx/LeyesBiblio/pdf/LFMZAAH.pdf)). The federal government exercises custody through the Instituto Nacional de Antropología e Historia and the Museo Nacional de Antropología.

The museum does not “own” the stone as an alienable collection asset, and neither the cathedral nor descendants of any historical official hold title.

### Accession and catalogue information

The object is consistently catalogued institutionally as the Piedra del Sol in the MNA’s Mexica collection. A stable object-specific public accession number was not located in the accessible online catalogue material consulted. It should not be inferred from image identifiers or exhibition numbers. Confirmation requires the MNA’s internal archaeological inventory or formal catalogue record.

### Condition and conservation

The stone is structurally stable enough for permanent vertical display but bears accumulated ancient, colonial, and modern damage. Its great weight makes mounting, seismic protection, and emergency movement significant conservation issues. No current public record indicates an active structural emergency.

A comprehensive recent condition report, pigment report, or seismic-mount specification was not found. These are therefore **unestablished**, not absent.

### Repatriation and ownership disputes

No current international repatriation dispute concerns the original: it remains in the city and country where it was made and rediscovered. Broader debates concern the centralization of Indigenous heritage in Mexico City, national ownership doctrine, museum interpretation, and authority to reproduce Indigenous cultural imagery. No identified community or foreign state is presently litigating title to the disk.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Early publication and global image

León y Gama’s 1792 engraved plate converted a fixed monument into a reproducible scholarly image. Humboldt’s publications gave it a European and transatlantic audience. Nineteenth-century photography further detached the design from the original stone: it could now circulate through books, postcards, museum catalogues, schoolrooms, and exhibitions.

The Getty’s “Obsidian Mirror-Travels” project traces representations from the 1792 engraving through later museum photography ([Getty Research Institute](https://www.getty.edu/research/exhibitions_events/exhibitions/obsidian_mirror/aztec_calendar_stone.html)).

### Casts and replicas

Molding allowed full-scale or reduced copies to travel without the original. Documented examples include:

- casts of the Mexican national collection shown at nineteenth-century international exhibitions;
- a cast of the Calendar Stone among Mexico’s contributions to the Columbian Historical Exposition in Madrid, 1892–1893 ([United States commission report](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/ec/Report_of_the_United_States_Commission_to_the_Columbian_Historical_Exposition_at_Madrid_-_1892-93_-_with_special_papers_%28IA_b24883232%29.pdf));
- a cast exhibited by Eufemio Abadiano in New Orleans and Washington in the 1880s ([*Popular Science Monthly*, 1887](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Popular_Science_Monthly/Volume_31/May_1887/Mexican_Antiquities));
- later museum replicas, including a full-scale outdoor replica at the Florida Museum of Natural History and reduced plaster or commercial casts in many collections;
- a small plaster replica catalogued by Cambridge’s Museum of Archaeology and Anthropology ([Cambridge object record](https://collections.maa.cam.ac.uk/objects/548292/)).

Replicas are not rival claimants. Some old casts may preserve details subsequently weathered or damaged, but that potential requires direct comparative study.

### Money

The stone’s rings and central face have repeatedly appeared on Mexican coinage. Banco de México describes the current or recent ten-peso design as using the central circle representing Tonatiuh and the Five Suns ([Banco de México](https://www.anterior.banxico.org.mx/dyn/divulgacion/billetes-y-monedas/billetes-monedas-conmemorativ.html)). Different denominations have isolated different rings, turning the monument into a modular state emblem.

In 2007 Banco de México issued a one-kilogram silver collectors’ coin bearing the “Calendario Azteca o Piedra de los Soles” ([Banco de México release](https://www.banxico.org.mx/publicaciones-y-prensa/relacionados-con-monedas/%7B38993214-2735-FBCD-D557-3A5604646354%7D.pdf)).

### Literature and visual art

- **Octavio Paz, “Piedra de sol” (1957):** the 584-line poem takes its title and circular structure from cyclical time associated with the monument and the Venus synodic cycle. It is not a description of the artifact but materially strengthened “Piedra de Sol” as a metaphor for recurrence, erotic time, and Mexican historical consciousness.
- Mexican muralists, graphic designers, photographers, and textbook illustrators repeatedly used the disk as shorthand for the pre-Hispanic past.
- The pattern appears in public architecture, jewelry, clothing, tattoos, logos, tourist art, restaurant décor, and political graphics.

### National and political mobilization

In the Porfirian period, the stone’s transfer to a monumental gallery presented the Mexican state as heir and scientific custodian of ancient civilization. Revolutionary and post-revolutionary governments retained it as a central national symbol. Photographs of presidents before it joined political authority to archaeological antiquity.

The 1964 museum installation placed the stone on the main axis of the Sala Mexica, creating one of the most recognizable museum vistas in Latin America. Its display participates in a national narrative centered on Mexica Tenochtitlan; critics note that this can overshadow the diversity and continuing presence of Indigenous peoples.

### Commercial distortions

The most consequential distortions are:

- calling it “Maya”;
- presenting it as a literal wheel calendar;
- claiming that it predicts 2012;
- assigning every small motif one fixed astronomical numerical value;
- selling modern hybrid designs combining Maya glyphs, zodiac symbols, or invented motifs as authentic copies.

These objects are replicas or derivative designs, not forgeries unless represented as ancient originals.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### León y Gama and antiquarian archaeology

Antonio de León y Gama’s *Descripción histórica y cronológica de las dos piedras* (1792) is the foundational object study. It recorded discovery circumstances, measurements, an engraved image, and an extended calendrical interpretation. Its importance is documentary even where its conclusions are obsolete.

León y Gama argued against dismissing the monument as meaningless idolatry. At the same time, his effort to demonstrate exact calendrical science led him to over-systematize ambiguous motifs. The work survives as both primary evidence and interpretation ([digital text](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/Descripci%C3%B3n_hist%C3%B3rica_y_cronol%C3%B3gica_de_las_dos_piedras_que_con_ocasi%C3%B3n_del_nuevo_empedrado_que_se_est%C3%A1_formando_en_la_plaza_principal_de_M%C3%A9xico%2C_se_hallaron_en_ella_el_a%C3%B1o_de_1790)).

### Humboldt

Humboldt inspected the monument, discussed its material and weight, and reproduced it for an international readership in *Vues des Cordillères et monuments des peuples indigènes de l’Amérique* (1810–1813). His comparative framework mixed careful observation with broad civilizational analogy, characteristic of early nineteenth-century Americanist scholarship.

### Nineteenth-century national scholarship

Alfredo Chavero and Museo Nacional scholars increasingly treated the stone as a historical, artistic, and national document. Chavero strongly associated 13 Reed with 1479 and Axayácatl. His work also contained diffusionist comparisons no longer accepted, illustrating how the object was recruited into arguments about civilization, migration, and national antiquity.

The 1885 museum transfer changed study conditions: scholars obtained better controlled access, while the object ceased to be an unrestricted urban monument.

### Twentieth-century synthesis

Alfonso Caso’s identification of the central figure as Tonatiuh and systematic reading of the cosmic eras became canonical in textbooks. Eduardo Matos Moctezuma and Felipe Solís integrated archaeological evidence, comparative sculpture, ritual practice, and imperial ideology. The result is more nuanced than the popular “calendar,” but museum presentation continued to privilege a solar reading.

### Major continuing debates

1. **Date:** 1479/Axayácatl versus early sixteenth century/Motecuhzoma II.
2. **Central identity:** Tonatiuh, Tlaltecuhtli, Yohualtecuhtli, composite deity, eclipsed sun, or ruler-deity portrait.
3. **Function:** calendar, cosmogram, *temalacatl*, *cuauhxicalli*, dynastic monument, New Fire commemoration, or multifunctional ritual sculpture.
4. **Original position:** horizontal platform versus vertical or inclined display.
5. **Original site:** solar precinct, *cuauhxicalco*, Templo Mayor-related enclosure, or another central ceremonial location.
6. **13 Reed:** manufacturing date, dedication, mythic date, dynastic reference, or deliberately multivalent sign.
7. **Relationship to colonial chronicles:** whether any of the stones described by Durán, Tezozómoc, or Torquemada is the surviving disk.
8. **Polychromy:** which surfaces were painted and with which pigments.
9. **Quarry:** Pedregal de San Ángel/Xitle, Xochimilco, or another southern Basin source.
10. **Meaning of “calendar”:** useful shorthand versus a category that distorts the object’s ritual and political character.

### Discredited or unsupported ideas still circulating

- It is a Maya artifact.
- It predicted the end of the world in 2012.
- It is a mechanical or digital-equivalent astronomical computer.
- Every register is an exact modern astronomical table.
- The central figure has been conclusively and universally identified.
- Torquemada’s speaking, bridge-falling stone is certainly this disk.
- Its precise quarry and transport workforce are documented.
- All visible pits are proven bullet strikes from 1847.

### State of the primary evidence

The strongest primary documentation consists of:

- the surviving monument;
- Ortiz de Castro’s 1790 report as transcribed by León y Gama;
- León y Gama’s measurements, narrative, and engraved plate;
- cathedral and viceregal correspondence concerning display;
- sixteenth- and seventeenth-century chronicles describing possibly related stones;
- nineteenth-century drawings, paintings, photographs, museum catalogues, and transfer records;
- twentieth-century photographs and engineering documentation of the 1964 move.

Important lacunae include:

- no pre-Hispanic dedication text;
- no named sculptor or workshop;
- no confirmed quarry;
- no secure pre-1521 installation record;
- no uninterrupted documentation between conquest and alleged mid-sixteenth-century burial;
- incomplete public access to museum conservation and inventory files.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and near-primary sources

- Antonio de León y Gama, *Descripción histórica y cronológica de las dos piedras que con ocasión del nuevo empedrado que se está formando en la plaza principal de México, se hallaron en ella el año de 1790* (Mexico City, 1792). Essential for the find circumstances, early measurements, official report, first published image, and initial calendrical interpretation.
- Diego Durán, *Historia de las Indias de Nueva España e islas de Tierra Firme*, composed in the later sixteenth century. Important for descriptions of great sacrificial stones, Axayácatl’s monument, and Montúfar’s alleged burial order, but not an unambiguous object-specific custody record.
- Hernando Alvarado Tezozómoc, *Crónica Mexicana* and related historical tradition. Important for the Axayácatl commission narrative.
- Juan de Torquemada, *Monarquía Indiana* (first published 1615). Source for the Motecuhzoma II/Tenanitla transport and bridge-collapse story.
- Alexander von Humboldt, *Vues des Cordillères et monuments des peuples indigènes de l’Amérique* (1810–1813). Early eyewitness examination and international reproduction.
- Nineteenth-century Museo Nacional catalogues and *Anales del Museo Nacional*. Important for dimensions, material descriptions, gallery installation, and changing interpretations.
- Historical photographs held by the Library of Congress, Mexican archives, INAH, and other repositories documenting the cathedral and Calle de Moneda phases.

### Institutional and scholarly sources

- Museo Nacional de Antropología and INAH histories of the collection and galleries.
- Eduardo Matos Moctezuma and Felipe Solís Olguín, studies of the Calendar Stone and other solar monuments.
- Leonardo López Luján, “‘El adiós y triste queja del gran Calendario Azteca’: el incesante peregrinar de la Piedra del Sol,” on its physical movements and reception.
- Yolotl González Torres, “Entre cuauhxicallis y temalacates,” on colonial terminology and ritual functions.
- David Stuart, *King and Cosmos: An Interpretation of the Aztec Calendar Stone*.
- Modern studies by Susan Milbrath and others on eclipse imagery, chronology, cosmogenesis, and royal ideology.
- Current Mexican federal monument law for ownership and custody status.

### Evidentiary conclusion

The surviving disk has a secure material identity and continuous documented history from its 1790 recovery to the present. Its earlier biography is necessarily reconstructive. It is unquestionably a pre-Hispanic Mexica monument; it was almost certainly made for the ceremonial-political center of Tenochtitlan; and it deliberately joins solar movement, destroyed world ages, calendrical signs, sacrifice, and authority. Its exact commission, date, original emplacement, named central being, and specific ritual use remain disputed.

The object’s later life is unusually clear. Colonial authorities converted a buried ritual sculpture into a publicly exposed antiquity; scholars converted it into a printed calendar; the national museum converted it into archaeological patrimony; and the modern Mexican state and commercial culture converted its concentric imagery into a portable national emblem. These interpretations did not replace the physical stone, but they repeatedly changed what the stone was understood to be.
