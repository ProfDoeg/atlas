# Hermes's tortoise-shell lyre: Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `hermes_s_tortoise_shell_lyre_object_dossier.md`  
**Status:** legendary object; no surviving physical claimant is known.

## Evidentiary key

- **Primary mythic attestation:** an ancient literary or visual source describing the object within myth.
- **Material comparator:** an excavated instrument, fragment, model, or representation illustrating the historical type, but not the legendary original.
- **Scholarly inference:** a modern reconstruction or interpretation based on texts, images, archaeology, or experimental work.
- **Later variant:** an ancient or post-antique retelling that materially changes the object's construction, movements, or custody.
- **Modern reception:** an adaptation, replica, artwork, ritual object, or commercial product inspired by the tradition.
- **Unestablished:** a claim for which no adequate evidence has been identified.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity

Hermes's tortoise-shell lyre is the first lyre in the principal Greek myth of musical invention: a small, portable stringed instrument constructed by the newborn Hermes from a mountain tortoise and subsequently transferred to Apollo. Its most detailed early description occurs in the anonymous Greek hexameter poem conventionally called the fourth *Homeric Hymn*, the *Hymn to Hermes*. The hymn is commonly placed near the end of the sixth century BCE, although a date in the early fifth century remains possible; neither its author nor its original performance context is securely known. The object belongs to myth rather than to a documented archaeological provenance.

The dossier tracks the instrument made by Hermes in that hymn and the ancient variants explicitly presented as the same first lyre. It does not treat every lyre owned by Apollo, Orpheus, Amphion, or a Muse as automatically identical. Later sources sometimes merge those instruments into a single custody chain; others keep them separate.

### Names and terminology

- **Ancient Greek:** λύρα, *lýra*, “lyre.” Its deeper etymology is uncertain.
- **Ancient Greek:** χέλυς, *chélys*, literally “tortoise” or “turtle,” secondarily a tortoise-shell lyre. The semantic transfer reflects the soundbox's material. Ancient writers could consequently call the instrument itself “the tortoise.”
- **Ancient Greek:** χελώνη, *chelṓnē*, tortoise; this is the ordinary zoological term used in descriptions of the shell.
- **Ancient Greek:** φόρμιγξ, *phórminx*, an older or more elevated word for a lyre-family instrument. It is not necessarily a precise synonym for the tortoise-shell *chelys*.
- **Latin:** *lyra*; *testudo*, literally “tortoise,” used metonymically for a lyre.
- **English:** “Hermes's lyre,” “Hermes' lyre,” “tortoise-shell lyre,” “turtle-shell lyre,” “chelys-lyre,” and, after its transfer, “Apollo's lyre.”

The modern expression “Lyre of Hermes” often denotes replicas or instrument models rather than a single relic. It has no established ancient status as a unique proper name.

### Description in the *Homeric Hymn to Hermes*

The primary description is in *Hymn to Hermes* 24–61:

1. Hermes encounters a tortoise at the courtyard gate or threshold of the cave where Maia lives.
2. He carries it inside.
3. He removes its limbs and hollows the body or shell with a grey-iron implement.
4. He cuts stalks or lengths of reed to measure and fixes them through or across the shell.
5. He stretches oxhide over the shell.
6. He installs two horns as arms.
7. He fits a crosspiece or yoke across the arms.
8. He stretches **seven strings of sheep gut**.
9. He tests the strings with a *plēktron*, a plectrum or key/pick.
10. The finished instrument sounds immediately.

The Greek text contains several difficult or disputed readings in this construction passage, particularly at lines 41 and 47–48. Consequently, translations that confidently prescribe a completely reproducible workshop procedure exceed what the transmitted text can guarantee. The secure structural elements are the hollowed tortoise shell, hide covering, paired arms or horns, crossbar, seven gut strings, and plectrum. The full passage is available in the [Perseus Greek and English text](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0138%3Ahymn%3D4); the textual problems and modern editions are summarized in a study of the passage and its organology ([Pressto/Adam Mickiewicz University PDF](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/sppgl/article/download/11760/16389)).

### Physical characteristics that can and cannot be reconstructed

**Attested in the hymn:**

- Natural tortoise-shell resonator.
- Oxhide membrane or soundboard.
- Two horns serving as arms.
- A crosspiece/yoke.
- Seven sheep-gut strings.
- Reed elements whose exact structural role is textually uncertain.
- A plectrum.
- A hollow, portable body.
- Sufficient tonal range for accompanied singing.

**Not stated:**

- Exact species, sex, or dimensions of the tortoise.
- Overall height, width, depth, or weight.
- Length or pitch of the strings.
- Tuning system.
- Number or form of tuning pegs.
- Bridge dimensions.
- Decoration, color, inscriptions, maker's marks, precious-metal fittings, or carrying strap.
- Repair history or physical condition after its transfer to Apollo.
- Ultimate destruction or deposition.

The phrase “seven strings” should not be silently converted into the claim that the strings symbolized the Pleiades, planets, or seven-string cosmology. Such interpretations occur in later musical and philosophical traditions but are not supplied by the hymn's manufacture scene.

### Historical *chelys* construction

Greek *chelys* lyres generally had a convex shell or shell-shaped resonator closed with animal skin, paired arms, a yoke, strings of approximately equal length, a bridge, tailpiece, and often a suspension strap. Surviving representations show them played against the left side of the body: the player could strum or pluck with a plectrum and damp or touch strings with the other hand. Experimental and museum reconstructions commonly use horn or curved wooden arms and gut or modern substitute strings.

These comparators establish that the hymn describes a recognizable Greek instrument type. They do not establish that Hermes's individual lyre existed.

### Material comparators and representations

No archaeological instrument has a defensible claim to be Hermes's original. Relevant comparators include:

- **British Museum 1816,0610.501:** a Classical Greek lyre, probably fifth–fourth century BCE, restored from remains; its wooden structure incorporates a ceramic imitation of a tortoise-shell soundbox. It was said to come from Athens and was purchased from Thomas Bruce, seventh Earl of Elgin, in 1816. It is a historical instrument, not Hermes's lyre ([British Museum catalogue](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/G_1816-0610-501)).
- **British Museum 1873,0320.39:** a limestone model of a tortoise-shaped lyre box from the sanctuary of Apollo at Idalion, Cyprus. It confirms the recognizable religious and visual form, not the mythical object's survival ([British Museum catalogue](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/G_1873-0320-39)).
- **Acropolis Museum Ακρ. 6673:** a Pentelic-marble fragment, 0.18 × 0.32 m, from the Parthenon's east pediment, dated 437–432 BCE. It represents a tortoise-shell-shaped lyre held by Apollo; drilled holes accommodated metal components. This is a sculptural representation, not an actual musical instrument and not identified by the museum as the particular object made by Hermes ([Acropolis Museum](https://www.emfc.gr/en/parthenon-east-pediment-lyre)).
- **Acropolis Museum Ακρ. 1346:** a fragmentary Parian-marble statue of Hermes, dated 450–400 BCE. The seated god probably held a tortoise in his missing right hand, visually alluding to the invention. Again, it is not the lyre itself ([Acropolis Museum](https://www.theacropolismuseum.gr/en/statue-hermes)).
- **British Museum B263, 1863,0330.3:** an Attic black-figure amphora showing Apollo playing a *chelys* while Hermes watches. It witnesses the gods' linked iconography but does not necessarily portray the transfer episode ([British Museum catalogue](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/G_1863-0330-3)).

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Earliest securely reconstructable literary attestation

The earliest surviving detailed account is the *Homeric Hymn to Hermes*. The poem's exact date is disputed. Linguistic and compositional analysis has often placed it in the late sixth century BCE; some recent treatments allow the first half of the fifth century. An older commentary likewise judged it later than the other long Homeric hymns and emphasized that the seven-string lyre already belonged to an established cultural setting ([Allen and Sikes commentary](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.04.0029%3Atext%3Dcomm%3Apoem%3D4); [University of Michigan thesis and commentary](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/29de4ffb-ded2-486b-9513-ea88c23f1a07)).

The hymn says:

- Hermes is born at dawn.
- By midday he plays the lyre.
- That evening he steals Apollo's cattle.
- The tortoise appears at Maia's doorway near Mount Cyllene in Arcadia.
- Hermes calls it a welcome omen and remarks that alive it protects against harmful magic, while dead it will sing.
- He kills and converts it into the instrument.
- His first song recounts Zeus's union with Maia and his own conception, then Maia's household.
- He leaves the lyre in or beside his cradle while undertaking the cattle theft.

The hymn does not claim to be an eyewitness record, identify a human maker, supply a historical date, or assert that the instrument survived into the poet's lifetime.

### Geographic origin

**Hymnic version:** Maia's dwelling or birth-cave on Mount Cyllene in Arcadia. The tortoise is encountered at its threshold.

**Pausanias's localized tradition:** In the second century CE, Pausanias placed the discovery on nearby Mount Chelydorea, adjoining Cyllene, where Hermes was said to have found a tortoise and fashioned a lyre from its shell. The place-name itself invited connection with *chelys*, although Pausanias does not claim to have seen the instrument or a relic of it ([Pausanias 8.17.5](https://www.theoi.com/Text/Pausanias8B.html)).

**Apollodoran version:** The mythographic *Library*, conventionally attributed to Pseudo-Apollodorus and probably compiled in the early Roman imperial period, reverses the hymn's order. Hermes first steals Apollo's cattle, sacrifices two, then returns to Cyllene, finds a tortoise browsing before the cave, cleans its shell, and strings it with material from the sacrificed cattle. In this version, the cattle theft supplies the strings ([Pseudo-Apollodorus 3.10.2](https://cts.perseids.org/read/greekLit/tlg0548/tlg001/perseus-eng2/3.7.3-3.10.3)).

### Manufacture as mythic transformation

The hymn deliberately presents manufacture as the conversion of a silent or potentially protective animal into a speaking artifact. Hermes addresses the living tortoise, kills it, and gives its dead shell a voice. The instrument therefore embodies several linked inversions:

- living animal → dead shell;
- silent creature → singer;
- found natural form → constructed technology;
- domestic threshold object → medium of divine exchange;
- killing → musical creation;
- theft dispute → reciprocal settlement.

Marco Romani Mistretta interprets the episode as a major ancient representation of Hermes as craftsman and *technitēs*, emphasizing the god's quick perception of a natural body's latent technical possibilities ([“Hermes the Craftsman: The Invention of the Lyre”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/gaia_1287-3349_2017_num_20_1_1716)).

### Relationship to actual instrument history

The hymn is an aetiology for an already existing class of Greek instrument, not evidence that a single inventor historically created the lyre. Lyres existed in the eastern Mediterranean long before the likely composition of the hymn. Possible tortoise-shell instrument fragments from Late Bronze Age Phylakopi on Melos have been debated, and reconstructions have explored whether their drilled holes served lyre fittings. Such material may demonstrate the antiquity of shell lyres, but it cannot establish Hermes as a historical maker or identify one specimen as his object.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because this is a legendary object, the custody chain consists of story claims. Each source must be kept separate.

### Chain A: *Homeric Hymn to Hermes*

#### 1. The tortoise

Before manufacture, the tortoise is a free animal feeding near Maia's dwelling on Cyllene. It has no named owner.

#### 2. Hermes: acquisition and manufacture

Hermes takes the animal without a transfer from another owner, kills it, and fabricates the lyre. In mythical terms he is maker, first owner, first player, and first singer.

#### 3. Temporary storage at Maia's cave

After playing, Hermes places the hollow lyre in his sacred cradle. He then leaves to steal Apollo's cattle. The object remains in Maia's dwelling; the hymn does not assign Maia ownership or use.

#### 4. Hermes retrieves and plays it before Apollo

After Apollo discovers the theft and Zeus orders restitution, Hermes leads Apollo to the cattle. Hermes then plays the lyre and sings a theogonic song. Apollo is overcome by pleasure and desire for the instrument.

#### 5. Gift or exchange to Apollo

At *Hymn to Hermes* 434–496, Hermes offers Apollo the lyre. The terms function both as gift and negotiated exchange: Apollo receives the instrument and music, while Hermes gains reconciliation, friendship, honor, and control of cattle or pastoral wealth. Apollo promises that the lyre will bring delight at feasts, dances, and processions to one who understands how to question it, but will produce empty babble for an ignorant player.

Apollo takes the lyre in his left hand and tests it with the plectrum. This is the hymn's final explicit custody link for the physical instrument.

#### 6. Post-transfer fate

The hymn does not record:

- where Apollo stores it;
- whether it goes to Delphi, Delos, Olympus, or another sanctuary;
- whether Apollo later gives this same instrument to Orpheus or Amphion;
- whether it is repaired, restrung, destroyed, buried, or transformed into stars.

Any uninterrupted chain beyond Apollo is therefore a later construction.

### Chain B: Sophocles' *Ichneutae* or *Trackers*

Sophocles' fragmentary fifth-century BCE satyr play survives principally in Papyrus Oxyrhynchus IX 1174, first published in 1912. In the preserved action:

- Apollo has lost cattle.
- Silenus and the satyrs track them to the vicinity of Hermes's cave.
- They hear an unprecedented sound from inside.
- Cyllene, serving as Hermes's nurse, explains that the rapidly growing infant has made a sounding object from a dead animal.
- Through an extended riddle she identifies the body as that of a tortoise and the voice as the lyre.
- The satyrs infer theft after seeing or hearing about cattle hides.

The surviving papyrus breaks before the transaction with Apollo. Consequently, this version establishes custody by Hermes at the cave but does not preserve the transfer. It also places dramatic stress on the lyre's uncanny novelty: listeners initially cannot categorize its sound. Greek text and translation are available through the [Scaife/Perseus edition](https://scaife.perseus.org/library/urn%3Acts%3AgreekLit%3Atlg0011.tlg008.perseus-grc2/redirect/).

### Chain C: Pseudo-Apollodorus

Pseudo-Apollodorus gives a more explicitly transactional sequence:

1. Hermes steals Apollo's cattle.
2. He sacrifices two animals at Pylos.
3. Returning to Cyllene, he finds and cleans out a tortoise shell.
4. He uses cords made from the sacrificed cattle to string it.
5. He invents a plectrum.
6. Apollo locates him and obtains restitution of the cattle.
7. Apollo hears the lyre.
8. Apollo gives Hermes the cattle **in exchange for the lyre**.

This differs from the hymn both chronologically and materially. The hymn gives sheep-gut strings and makes the lyre before the theft; Pseudo-Apollodorus makes it afterward with material from Apollo's cattle. The difference cannot be harmonized without altering one source.

### Chain D: Philostratus

Philostratus the Elder's *Imagines* 1.26, written in the Roman imperial period, describes a painting of Hermes's birth and invention. It says that Hermes fashioned the lyre from two horns, a crossbar, and a tortoise shell, and “presented it first to Apollo and the Muses, then to Amphion of Thebes.” The sequence is textually awkward and may compress the history of lyre music rather than report literal successive custody of one artifact. It nevertheless creates a later transfer chain:

**Hermes → Apollo and the Muses → Amphion**

The source does not explain whether Apollo transfers it to Amphion, whether the Muses share ownership, or whether “it” means the individual lyre or the art of lyre playing ([Philostratus, *Imagines* 1.26](https://www.theoi.com/Text/PhilostratusElder1B.html)).

### Chain E: Hermes–Orpheus–Terpander tradition

A fragment transmitted under Nicomachus of Gerasa gives a substantially different lineage:

1. Hermes invents the seven-string lyre.
2. Hermes hands the musical art or lyre to Orpheus.
3. Orpheus transmits its use to successors including Thamyris and Linus.
4. After Orpheus is killed by Thracian women, his lyre is thrown into the sea.
5. The sea carries it to the shore near Antissa on Lesbos.
6. Fishermen find it.
7. They bring it to Terpander.
8. Terpander takes it to Egypt, carefully works on it, shows it to Egyptian priests, and represents himself as its first inventor.

Whether this fragment means the same physical instrument at every point is uncertain. Its narrative begins with transmission of musical knowledge but later treats Orpheus's lyre as a movable artifact. The account may conflate instrument, technique, and inventor genealogy. A French edition and translation preserves the chain ([Nicomachean fragments](https://www.musicologie.org/theses/nicomaque_02.html)); Mistretta discusses the same testimony ([Persée](https://www.persee.fr/doc/gaia_1287-3349_2017_num_20_1_1716)).

### Chain F: celestial transfer

Aratus, in the third-century BCE *Phaenomena* 268–274, calls the constellation Lyra the little “Tortoise.” Hermes pierced or strung the tortoise beside his cradle and subsequently “brought it into heaven.” Here Hermes, rather than Apollo, performs the catasterism. Aratus does not recount the cattle bargain in this passage ([Aratus, *Phaenomena*](https://www.theoi.com/Text/AratusPhaenomena.html)).

Pseudo-Eratosthenes and Hyginus preserve an alternative celestial history tied to Orpheus:

1. Mercury/Hermes makes the lyre from a tortoise.
2. Apollo receives it and teaches Orpheus, or it is given directly to Orpheus in compressed accounts.
3. Orpheus uses it during life.
4. After his death, Apollo and Zeus arrange its placement among the stars.

This should not be combined uncritically with the terrestrial story in which Orpheus's lyre washes ashore on Lesbos and reaches Terpander. Both are ancient variants of its post-Orphic fate. Hyginus's Latin account is preserved at *Astronomica* 2.7 ([text and translation](https://ogcma.byu.edu/OrpheusEurydiceANCIENT_Hyginus.htm)); the Pseudo-Eratosthenic evidence survives in epitomized and revised forms, not as an autograph work of Eratosthenes ([Catasterisms 24](https://livingpoets.awh.durham.ac.uk/w/index.php/Eratosthenes_Catasterisms_24)).

### Custody-chain summary

| Source | Chain | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|---|
| *Homeric Hymn to Hermes* | tortoise → Hermes/maker → cradle at Maia's cave → Apollo | Earliest detailed surviving literary chain |
| Sophocles, *Ichneutae* | dead tortoise → Hermes in cave | Early dramatic variant; ending lost |
| Pseudo-Apollodorus | tortoise and cattle material → Hermes → Apollo by exchange | Later mythographic variant |
| Philostratus | Hermes → Apollo and Muses → Amphion | Roman-period pictorial/literary compression |
| Nicomachean fragment | Hermes → Orpheus/successors → sea → Lesbos fishermen → Terpander → Egypt | Late theoretical/inventor genealogy; identity of artifact uncertain |
| Aratus | Hermes → heaven as Lyra | Hellenistic catasterism |
| Pseudo-Eratosthenes/Hyginus | Hermes/Mercury → Apollo/Orpheus → heaven | Alternative catasterism |

There is no documentary chain from any of these mythical episodes to a surviving museum object.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Did the individual object exist?

No surviving evidence establishes that it did. The sources belong to poetry, drama, mythography, astronomical mythology, and art description. They are evidence that Greeks and Romans told stories about a first tortoise-shell lyre, not physical documentation of that lyre.

The story is technically plausible in the limited sense that real Greek shell lyres were constructed from comparable materials. Technical plausibility does not authenticate a unique divine artifact.

### Is any surviving instrument the original?

No museum, excavation report, or scientific publication identified in this research makes a credible claim that a surviving lyre is Hermes's original. Classical instruments and fragments are centuries later than the mythical setting and are catalogued as human archaeological objects.

No accession number, find report, radiocarbon date, species analysis, DNA study, inscription, or continuous provenance has been published for an alleged original.

### Are Apollo's, Orpheus's, and Hermes's lyres identical?

Ancient sources disagree:

- The *Homeric Hymn* certainly makes Hermes's lyre become Apollo's.
- Hyginus and related catasterism traditions can make that instrument pass to Orpheus.
- Philostratus connects it instead or additionally with the Muses and Amphion.
- Other Greek traditions credit Orpheus himself, Amphion, Terpander, or other culture heroes with innovations in lyre music.
- Diodorus gives Hermes more than one incompatible invention context.

The responsible conclusion is not that a single artifact unquestionably passed through every famous lyrist's hands, but that later writers built competing genealogies of musical authority around the first lyre.

### Three strings or seven?

The *Homeric Hymn* unequivocally describes seven gut strings. Diodorus Siculus 1.16, in an Egyptian cultural-history framework, attributes to Hermes/Thoth a three-string lyre corresponding to the Egyptian year's three seasons and to high, low, and middle pitch. This is not securely the same narrative object. Elsewhere Diodorus says Hermes made a tortoise-shell lyre after Apollo's contest with Marsyas, a chronology incompatible with the infant-Hermes story ([Diodorus 5.75.3](https://www.theoi.com/Text/DiodorusSiculus5C.html)).

### Authenticating authorities

Authority over the object's identity has moved between:

- poets and performers, who generated the earliest surviving narratives;
- mythographers, who systematized divergent stories;
- astronomical writers, who identified it with a constellation;
- ancient artists and viewers, who recognized a tortoise or *chelys* as an attribute;
- philologists, who establish and emend the transmitted Greek and Latin texts;
- archaeologists and organologists, who compare the description with material lyres;
- museums, which catalogue actual instruments and representations.

None can authenticate a physical “original” in the absence of a claimant and provenance.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Hermes

**Connection:** finder of the tortoise; killer and transformer of the animal; maker, first owner, first performer, singer, negotiator, and transferor.  
**Date of attestation:** late Archaic or early Classical period in the *Homeric Hymn*.  
**Tier:** primary mythic attestation.

### The tortoise

**Connection:** source organism and resonating shell; addressed by Hermes as both living protective omen and prospective dead singer.  
**Tier:** primary mythic component rather than owner.

### Maia

**Connection:** Hermes's mother and occupant of the cave in which the lyre is made and briefly stored. She neither makes nor plays it in the hymn.  
**Tier:** primary mythic association; not a custody link.

### Zeus

**Connection:** adjudicates the cattle dispute and orders Hermes to return the herd, thereby creating the conditions for the lyre's presentation to Apollo. He does not physically handle it in the hymn. In later catasterism, Zeus approves its placement among the stars.  
**Tier:** primary mythic legal authority; later celestial authority.

### Apollo

**Connection:** listener, recipient, tester, and principal divine beneficiary. The lyre's music changes his response from anger to delight and formalizes his friendship with Hermes.  
**Tier:** primary mythic custodian after Hermes.

### Mnemosyne and the Muses

**Connection:** Hermes honors Mnemosyne first in his reconciliatory song because he belongs to her sphere; later sources associate the lyre jointly with Apollo and the Muses.  
**Tier:** early symbolic association; later custody claim in Philostratus.

### Cyllene/Kyllene

**Connection:** mountain and localized divine nymph. In Sophocles she acts as Hermes's nurse and explains the instrument to the satyrs.  
**Tier:** fifth-century dramatic association.

### Silenus and the satyrs

**Connection:** first bewildered auditors in *Ichneutae*; they hear but do not possess the instrument. Its sound contributes to their discovery of Hermes's location.  
**Tier:** fifth-century dramatic association.

### Orpheus

**Connection:** later pupil, recipient, or inheritor of the lyre tradition; in catasterism accounts his death precipitates its placement in heaven.  
**Tier:** later variant; physical identity with Hermes's lyre disputed.

### Amphion

**Connection:** Philostratus names the Theban wall-builder as a later recipient.  
**Tier:** Roman-period variant.

### Linus and Thamyris

**Connection:** recipients of musical knowledge from Orpheus in the Nicomachean genealogy.  
**Tier:** late theoretical tradition; possession of the artifact itself unestablished.

### Terpander

**Connection:** in the Nicomachean fragment, receives the sea-borne lyre from fishermen at Lesbos, alters or carefully works it, takes it to Egypt, and claims invention before Egyptian priests.  
**Tier:** later legendary attribution attached to a historical archaic musician.

### Lesbos fishermen

**Connection:** unnamed finders of the lyre after its post-Orphic sea journey.  
**Tier:** later legendary custody link.

### Egyptian priests

**Connection:** audience before whom Terpander allegedly presents the instrument and asserts priority. They do not clearly take custody.  
**Tier:** later legendary network.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Musical function

In the hymn the lyre accompanies song. Hermes's first performance recounts his divine parentage and household; the decisive second performance before Apollo invokes gods and cosmic order. The instrument is thus immediately associated with:

- hymnic praise;
- genealogical or theogonic song;
- improvisation;
- feasting;
- dance;
- procession;
- pleasure and social reconciliation.

Apollo describes it as responsive to knowledge: a skilled player who approaches it intelligently receives instruction or beautiful sound, while an ignorant user produces meaningless noise. This is not a curse but a claim about technical and inspired competence.

### Negotiating and diplomatic function

The lyre resolves a dispute that cattle restitution alone does not settle. Its exchange transforms Hermes and Apollo from antagonist and victim into reciprocal allies. The object consequently mediates between:

- theft and restitution;
- pastoral wealth and musical skill;
- a younger god seeking honors and an established Olympian controlling them;
- cunning intelligence and prophetic or musical authority.

It is not merely a bribe in the hymn's larger structure. It is a prestige technology whose novelty allows Hermes to negotiate a recognized sphere of divine action.

### Sacrificial and animal symbolism

The materials join several dead animals in one sounding body: tortoise shell, oxhide, sheep gut, and horn. In Pseudo-Apollodorus, Apollo's own slaughtered cattle furnish the strings, making the exchange more pointed: Apollo receives a beautiful instrument partially made from the animals Hermes stole and killed.

### Apollonian emblem

After the transfer, the lyre becomes one of Apollo's principal attributes. Images of Apollo with a *chelys* or kithara do not necessarily depict this unique lyre, but the Hermes narrative supplied an origin for Apollo's musical instrument and authority.

### Celestial function

The constellation Lyra turns a terrestrial artifact into a permanent heavenly sign. Aratus's name “Tortoise” preserves the material origin even after the object becomes a star figure. Later catasterism traditions use its elevation to commemorate Orpheus, Apollo, Hermes, or their combined musical lineage.

### Protective power

Before killing the tortoise, Hermes says that alive it can ward off harmful magic or act as protection. The hymn does not subsequently say that the completed lyre retains this apotropaic function. Claims that Hermes's lyre itself was a routinely used magical talisman are therefore unestablished.

### Cult and ceremony

Actual tortoise-shell lyres were used in Greek education, domestic music, festival performance, symposia, funerary imagery, and cult. No ancient source found here records the unique mythical lyre being carried in a historical procession, inventoried in a temple treasury, touched by priests, or displayed as a relic. There were therefore no documented access rules for the artifact.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The threshold encounter

**Tier:** earliest detailed surviving narrative.

Hermes encounters the tortoise as he crosses the threshold of Maia's dwelling. He laughs, treats the meeting as a favorable sign, and immediately sees uses unavailable to an ordinary observer. The episode characterizes invention as recognition: nature already provides a resonant cavity, but Hermes perceives and realizes its musical potential.

### “The dead creature speaks”

**Tier:** early dramatic elaboration.

In Sophocles' *Ichneutae*, Cyllene describes an impossible-seeming phenomenon: a dead creature has acquired a voice. Her riddle makes the audience reconstruct the object conceptually before it is named. The satyrs' fear and confusion mark the lyre's sound as unprecedented.

### The cattle bargain

**Tier:** early attested core with variant mechanics.

In the hymn, Hermes makes the lyre before stealing the cattle. After Zeus orders restitution, the instrument's sound captivates Apollo, and Hermes gives it to him as part of a negotiated settlement.

Pseudo-Apollodorus reverses the manufacturing order: the stolen cattle provide the string material, and Apollo explicitly gives Hermes the herd in exchange for the lyre.

### The Marsyas anachronism

**Tier:** later alternative origin.

Diodorus Siculus reports that Hermes made a tortoise-shell lyre after Apollo defeated and punished Marsyas, then tore the strings from his earlier instrument in remorse. This version solves a different narrative problem: it replaces or renews Apollo's music after the Marsyas episode. It cannot describe the same chronological sequence as the newborn Hermes's first-day invention without extensive harmonization.

### Gift to Amphion

**Tier:** Roman-period variant.

Philostratus says Hermes presented the instrument first to Apollo and the Muses and afterward to Amphion. This grafts the invention story onto Amphion's music-assisted construction of Thebes. Whether an imagined painting actually displayed each transfer or Philostratus supplied the interpretive genealogy is unresolved.

### Gift to Orpheus

**Tier:** Hellenistic and Roman-period elaboration.

In astronomical and musical genealogies, Apollo gives the lyre or its art to Orpheus. The transfer explains Orpheus's superhuman music and connects Hermes's invention, Apollo's patronage, and Orpheus's mortal mastery.

### Sea journey to Lesbos

**Tier:** late theoretical or antiquarian legend.

After Thracian women kill Orpheus, his instrument is cast into the sea, reaches Antissa, is found by fishermen, and passes to Terpander. This legend gives Lesbos and Terpander a material connection to primordial music. It also contains a critical edge: Terpander allegedly presents the inherited instrument in Egypt as his own invention.

### Catasterism

**Tier:** Hellenistic and Roman astronomical myth.

Two principal forms survive:

- Hermes himself places the “Tortoise” in heaven.
- Apollo and Zeus place Orpheus's lyre among the stars after his death.

The constellation is not physical evidence that the artifact survived; it is a mythic transformation or memorial identification.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### The legendary object

**Current status:** no physical survival established.  
**Current location:** not applicable or unknown within material history.

Possible outcomes within ancient stories include:

- continued possession by Apollo;
- transmission to the Muses, Amphion, or Orpheus;
- arrival on Lesbos and transfer to Terpander;
- removal to Egypt;
- celestial transformation into Lyra.

These are mutually overlapping or incompatible literary endings, not archaeological hypotheses about a lost artifact.

There is no known shrine, treasury, museum, private collection, or state authority currently claiming legal title to an authenticated original. There is no repatriation litigation or ownership controversy concerning such an original.

### Real objects standing in for it

The most important modern stand-ins are:

- archaeological *chelys* lyres or fragments;
- reconstructed working instruments;
- the Parthenon marble lyre fragment associated with Apollo;
- statues and vase paintings linking Hermes, tortoises, Apollo, and lyres;
- the constellation Lyra;
- modern instruments sold or exhibited under names such as “Lyre of Hermes.”

These represent the myth or the instrument class. None has the required identity continuity.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Philological recovery

The modern image of the object depends heavily on editions and translations of the *Homeric Hymn*. Hugh G. Evelyn-White's 1914 English translation has been particularly influential online. Phrases such as “first made the tortoise a singer” and the construction inventory have entered popular retellings through that translation.

The 1912 publication of P.Oxy. IX 1174 restored substantial portions of Sophocles' *Ichneutae*, revealing an ancient dramatic treatment previously known only fragmentarily. The papyrus made the strange first sound of the lyre and Cyllene's riddle available to modern theatre.

### Theatre, ballet, and opera

Albert Roussel composed *La Naissance de la lyre*, op. 24, in 1922–1923 to a libretto by Théodore Reinach based on Sophocles' *Ichneutae*. It premiered at the Paris Opéra on 1 July 1925, with choreography by Bronislava Nijinska and staging involving Pierre Chéreau and artists Raymond Legueult and Maurice Brianchon. It is a major modern transformation of the object's discovery and first hearing into staged musical drama. The Bibliothèque nationale de France preserves the production record ([BnF catalogue](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/affiner.do?afficheRegroup=false&critereRecherche=&index=NRC3&listeAffinages=FacSpe_-1%3BFacUnivers_musique%3BFacDat_1900.0%211999.9%3BFacDat_1920.0%211929.8&motRecherche=&nbResultParPage=100&numNotice=&triResultParPage=6&trouveDansFiltre=&typeNotice=)).

Tony Harrison's *The Trackers of Oxyrhynchus*, first performed in 1988, reworked Sophocles' papyrus discovery and satyr play. Its modern concern is as much the survival, ownership, and accessibility of classical culture as the cattle theft; the lyre's recovered voice becomes entangled with the recovered papyrus's voice.

### Reconstructions

Modern craftspeople and researchers have built playable *chelys* lyres from natural or artificial shells, leather membranes, horn or wood arms, gut or synthetic strings, and reconstructed bridges and yokes. These experiments test acoustics and ergonomics. They cannot reproduce an object whose dimensions and tuning were never recorded.

The Kotsanas Museum describes a reconstruction with a tortoise-shell resonator, ox- or goatskin membrane, horn or wooden arms, wooden yoke, strings, bridge, tailpiece, suspension strap, and plectrum ([Kotsanas Museum](https://collection.kotsanasmuseum.com/en/exhibits/106074/)). Smith College likewise presents an educational reconstruction of the historical *chelys* type ([Smith College](https://www.smith.edu/bigbad/historyscitech/museum/ancient_inventions/hsc12b.htm)).

### Visual art and museum symbolism

The tortoise, shell lyre, and infant Hermes remain mutually identifying motifs. Museums often explain tortoise-bearing Hermes sculptures through the invention story. Apollo's lyre in institutional decoration can more broadly signify music, poetry, education, rational order, or the Muses, even when Hermes is absent.

### Astronomy

“Lyra” remains the official Latin name of the northern constellation. Its brightest star, Vega, and modern astronomical study have no material connection with the legendary artifact, but the ancient catasterism survives in the name and iconographic convention.

### Contemporary religious and commercial use

Modern Hellenic polytheists sometimes place miniature lyres, tortoise images, or commercial “Lyres of Hermes” on devotional altars. These are contemporary ritual engagements and replicas, not continuous ancient relic cults. Commercial sellers frequently describe replicas as “Homeric” or “ancient Greek”; unless tied to a specific measured reconstruction, such labels indicate design inspiration rather than exact reproduction.

No documented modern forgery has achieved significant institutional recognition as the authentic divine original. Generic souvenirs presented as “museum copies” should not be mistaken for authenticated copies of a particular ancient artifact.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Dating the hymn

The *Hymn to Hermes* lacks an external composition date. Arguments rely on language, meter, intertextuality, narrative technique, and cultural references. Major positions range from the later sixth century BCE into the early fifth. Attempts to date it solely from the seven-string instrument are circular unless the independent chronology of Greek string development is established.

### The construction passage

Lines 41–51 have generated philological and organological debate because:

- the reading describing removal of the tortoise's body is uncertain;
- the placement and function of the cut reeds are difficult;
- manuscripts and editors differ over words describing the shell;
- translations may impose the form of known Classical instruments on obscure poetic instructions.

Modern reconstructions therefore combine text with vase imagery and surviving instrument remains. They demonstrate workable possibilities, not a unique solution.

### Myth versus technological history

Earlier scholarship sometimes treated divine-inventor traditions as garbled historical memory. Current evidentiary discipline treats Hermes's invention as an aetiological narrative operating within a culture that already possessed lyres. Archaeological shell lyres may illuminate the story's material vocabulary without verifying its plot.

### Order of lyre-making and cattle theft

This is the clearest narrative dispute:

- *Homeric Hymn*: lyre first; cattle theft second; sheep-gut strings.
- Pseudo-Apollodorus: cattle theft and sacrifice first; lyre second; cattle-derived cords.
- Sophocles: cattle hides near the cave allow satyrs to connect theft and instrument, but the fragmentary text does not preserve a complete workshop chronology.

The differences probably reflect active recomposition rather than damage to a single canonical account.

### One lyre or several?

Ancient musical historiography often organizes culture through inventor succession. Hermes, Apollo, Orpheus, Linus, Amphion, Terpander, and others can occupy overlapping positions. When a text says one figure “received the lyre,” it may refer to:

- the specific first artifact;
- a lyre of the same type;
- the art of playing;
- a tuning system;
- cultural authority over music.

Failure to distinguish those senses produces an artificially continuous provenance.

### Interpretation of death and voice

The tortoise's conversion into music has attracted structural, religious, material, and ethical readings. Strong textual observations include the repeated opposition between living silence and dead song and Hermes's immediate technical intelligence. Claims about shamanism, planetary harmony, or a universal prehistoric tortoise cult require independent evidence and should not be treated as the hymn's explicit meaning.

### State of the evidence

The principal evidence consists of:

- medieval manuscripts of the *Homeric Hymns*;
- P.Oxy. IX 1174 and smaller fragments of Sophocles' *Ichneutae*;
- manuscripts of Pseudo-Apollodorus, Pausanias, Diodorus, Aratus, Hyginus, Philostratus, and musical theorists;
- vase paintings, sculpture, reliefs, and instrument models;
- excavated lyre remains and shell fragments;
- modern experimental reconstructions.

No primary archival record, inventory, votive inscription, or excavated context documents the legendary object as a unique artifact.

## 12. Sources

### Principal primary sources

- Anonymous, *Homeric Hymn to Hermes* 17–67, 389–502. The foundational manufacture and transfer narrative.
- Sophocles, *Ichneutae*/*Trackers*, especially the Cyllene–satyr dialogue preserved in P.Oxy. IX 1174.
- Aratus, *Phaenomena* 268–274. Hermes and celestial “Tortoise.”
- Diodorus Siculus, *Library of History* 1.16 and 5.75.3. Three-string and post-Marsyas invention traditions.
- Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Library* 3.10.2. Manufacture after the cattle theft and explicit exchange.
- Pausanias, *Description of Greece* 8.17.5. Localization on Mount Chelydorea.
- Pseudo-Eratosthenes, *Catasterisms* 24. Orphic celestial tradition.
- Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.7. Mercury, Apollo, Orpheus, and catasterism.
- Philostratus the Elder, *Imagines* 1.26. Construction and transfer to Apollo, Muses, and Amphion.
- Nicomachean excerpts on the seven-string lyre, Orpheus, Lesbos, Terpander, and Egypt.

### Critical and scholarly resources

- Allen, Thomas W., and E. E. Sikes, *The Homeric Hymns*, commentary on Hymn 4.
- Evelyn-White, Hugh G., *Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns and Homerica*.
- Mistretta, Marco Romani, “Hermes the Craftsman: The Invention of the Lyre,” *Gaia* 20 (2017): 5–22.
- Modern textual study of the construction passage in “Aspects of Myth in the Homeric Hymn to Hermes.”
- Museum catalogues for archaeological comparators and ancient representations.

### Source assessment

The *Homeric Hymn* is the indispensable primary source because it describes both manufacture and first transfer. Pseudo-Apollodorus is the clearest alternative chronology. Sophocles is especially valuable for ancient reception of the instrument's new sound. Aratus, Pseudo-Eratosthenes, Hyginus, Nicomachus, and Philostratus document later efforts to extend the object's custody into astronomical, Orphic, Theban, Lesbian, and Egyptian histories.

Museum catalogues are authoritative for the physical comparators' dimensions, materials, dates, acquisition histories, and current locations. They provide no support for identifying any material specimen as the legendary original.
