# Joan of Arc's armor (harnois blanc): Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `joan_of_arcs_armor_harnois_blanc_object_dossier.md`

## Evidentiary finding

Joan of Arc unquestionably possessed and wore armor. A royal war-account records payment of **100 livres tournois** to an unnamed armorer for a complete harness made for her in 1429. Contemporary and near-contemporary witnesses subsequently describe her wearing full armor in combat. Joan herself testified in 1431 that she had deposited at Saint-Denis “a complete white harness suitable for a man-at-arms,” together with a captured sword, after being wounded at Paris.

No piece now preserved, however, has a demonstrated physical chain of custody from Joan. At least three historically distinct ensembles have been conflated under “Joan of Arc’s armor”:

1. **The Tours harness**, commissioned for Joan in spring 1429.
2. **The Saint-Denis ex-voto**, described by Joan as a complete white harness and deposited after the assault on Paris in September 1429. It may have been the Tours harness, but that identity is not proved.
3. **The harness worn at Compiègne**, seized when Joan was captured on 23 May 1430. It may again have been the Tours harness, a replacement, or captured equipment.

A “harness of the Maid” inventoried at Amboise in 1499 is the earliest known candidate for a surviving physical suit, but the attribution is unsupported by an unbroken provenance and appears in an inventory containing other legendary attributions. Later Paris museum armor once called Joan’s is Renaissance work and cannot be hers. Other claimed suits and modern replicas have no credible continuity with the armor documented in 1429–30.

Accordingly, Joan’s armor is best classified as a **documented but lost artifact, surrounded by later candidate objects, antiquarian attributions, reconstructions, and national-religious imagery**.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Names and terminology

The principal contemporary expressions are:

- **Middle French:** *harnois*, *harnoys*, *harnais* — equipment or military gear; in this context, a coordinated defensive armor ensemble.
- **Middle French:** *harnois complet* — complete harness.
- **Middle French:** *blanc harnois*, *harnois blanc* — “white harness.”
- **Latin trial record:** *album harnesium*, followed in some versions by the vernacular gloss *gallice un blanc harnois*.
- **Modern French:** *armure de Jeanne d’Arc*.
- **English:** “Joan of Arc’s armor,” “white harness,” or “white armour.”

“White” does not mean painted white. In fifteenth-century armor vocabulary, *harnois blanc* ordinarily designated visible, polished plate armor—“bright” or “white” metal—as distinguished from defenses principally covered in textile, such as some brigandines. The term therefore describes a technological and visual class, not a sacred color and not necessarily a uniformly bare-metal suit in the later sixteenth-century sense.

The surviving royal payment calls the object simply a complete harness. Joan’s 1431 examination supplies the most explicit contemporary identification: *un blanc harnois entier, tel qu’il le faut à un homme d’armes*—a complete white harness of the kind appropriate to a man-at-arms. The French minute and official Latin procès survive in several manuscript witnesses; the most reliable approach is to compare the vernacular minutes with the formal Latin recension rather than depend upon a single modern translation. The principal condemnation manuscript tradition includes BnF Latin 5965 and related volumes; Biblissima catalogs Latin 5965 as a manuscript in the Bibliothèque nationale de France’s Département des manuscrits. [Biblissima manuscript notice](https://portail.biblissima.fr/en/ark%3A/43093/mdata6bfa0798b14bd659ef6deab9a36e27bc320c5b94)

### 1.2 What can be said physically

No authenticated component survives, so the dimensions, weight, steel composition, workshop marks, articulation system, surface finish, and exact decorative scheme are unknown. Claims assigning it a measured height or weight are reconstructions, not observations of Joan’s object.

The documentary minimum is:

- it was a **complete harness**;
- it was made by an armorer, probably at Tours;
- it cost 100 livres tournois;
- at least one armor associated with Joan was a *blanc harnois entier*;
- she used more than one kind of head defense during her campaigns;
- armor worn at Compiègne was covered by a rich red or vermilion cloth-of-gold *huque*.

A comparable June 1429 notarial description of another complete harness—not Joan’s—enumerates cuirasses, complete leg harness, forearm defenses, upper-arm defenses, sallet, throat defense, and reinforcing plates. A French military-costume text of 1446 defines a normal *harnois blanc* as a closed cuirass, forearm and upper-arm defenses, leg harness, gauntlets, a visored sallet, and a small bevor covering the chin. These parallels give a period-appropriate functional model but cannot prove the precise contents of Joan’s suit. Adrien Harmand assembled these records and comparative images in his 1929 reconstruction study. [Harmand, armor chapter](https://jeanne.wiki/harmand-costumes-armure/armure)

A responsible reconstruction might include:

- an arming doublet or *gippon*, padded and fitted with points for suspending armor;
- a breast- and back-defense, probably a multi-part cuirass with a lower abdominal plate or *pansière*;
- laminated waist protection or *braconnière*;
- shoulder and upper-arm plates;
- vambraces and elbow defenses;
- gauntlets or mitten gauntlets;
- thigh defenses, poleyns, greaves, and sabatons;
- supplementary mail at armpits, elbows, groin, throat, or other plate gaps;
- one or more helmets.

This is a typological reconstruction, not a recovered inventory.

### 1.3 Helmets

Different sources associate Joan with different head defenses:

- **Sallet:** Jean d’Aulon’s rehabilitation testimony says that at Saint-Pierre-le-Moûtier she wore a *salade*. As her squire and the officer responsible for assisting her in arms, d’Aulon is a particularly strong witness.
- **Chapeline or war hat:** the duke of Alençon’s testimony concerning Jargeau has been interpreted as indicating a broad-brimmed assault helmet.
- **Headpiece with mail throat defense:** the disputed 1499 Amboise inventory describes an *abillement de teste* with a mail *gorgerey*, gilded border, and crimson-satin lining.

These need not describe one immutable suit. Helmets and auxiliary pieces could be changed according to tactical circumstance, damage, comfort, and availability.

The familiar image of Joan continuously wearing a fully enclosed, late-Gothic sallet or a sixteenth-century fluted suit is unsupported. Harmand specifically criticized later artists who equipped her with helmet forms several decades too late. [Harmand, armor chapter](https://jeanne.wiki/harmand-costumes-armure/armure)

### 1.4 Decoration and coverings

Nothing establishes that the Tours plates themselves bore lilies, crosses, engraved saints, or heraldic decoration. The price suggests a good-quality custom harness, but expense alone does not prove ornament.

At Compiègne, Burgundian chroniclers describe Joan over her armor in a rich **vermilion/red cloth-of-gold huque**. This garment was a textile covering, not part of the metal structure. Georges Chastellain and Jean Le Fèvre de Saint-Remy independently preserve versions of the striking image: Joan mounted, fully armed, with her standard, her armor covered by red-gold fabric. [Harmand, introductory source collection](https://jeanne.wiki/harmand-costumes-armure/introduction)

### 1.5 Damage

The armor was exposed to repeated campaigning. Joan was wounded:

- by an arrow at Orléans in May 1429;
- by a stone or projectile at Jargeau in June 1429, according to rehabilitation testimony;
- in the thigh during the assault on Paris on 8 September 1429;
- in other episodes reported less consistently.

The records do not identify a surviving penetration, repair, or damaged component. On 8 May 1429, the day after the assault on the Tourelles, Dunois remembered Joan wearing only a light mail coat; the *Chronique de la Pucelle* calls it a *jasseran*. That episode shows that “Joan’s armor” was not necessarily worn as a single complete ensemble every day. [Harmand, armor chapter](https://jeanne.wiki/harmand-costumes-armure/armure)

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Commission and payment

The earliest documentary anchor is a war-account associated with **Hémon Raguier**, royal treasurer of the wars. In the extracts published by Jules Quicherat, the crown paid an armorer **100 livres tournois** for a complete harness for “the Maid.” Nineteenth-century historians cite the account as: “au maistre armurier, pour ung harnois complet pour ladite Pucelle, cent livres tournois.” Gaston du Fresne de Beaucourt identifies the account and refers readers to Quicherat’s documentary collection. [Du Fresne de Beaucourt, *Histoire de Charles VII*, vol. II](https://www.mediterranee-antique.fr/Fichiers_PdF/DEF/Fresne_Beaucourt/Charles_7_2.pdf)

The payment is frequently dated to April 1429, during Joan’s stay at Tours before the relief expedition to Orléans. Louis de Coutes’s rehabilitation testimony supports Tours as the place where she was armed. The evidence establishes royal procurement more securely than it establishes the precise forge or address.

### 2.2 Maker

The account, as published, does not name the “master armorer.” Later Tours tradition names **Colas or Nicolas de Montbazon** and connects the manufacture with a site now associated with the house called “À la Pucelle Armée.” The surviving building is later than 1429; the plaque and house tradition therefore mark a commemorated location, not a preserved authenticated workshop.

**Assessment:** an armorer working at or through Tours is documented; Montbazon’s individual authorship is an antiquarian or local attribution that requires evidence beyond the generic payment entry.

### 2.3 Materials

The plates were almost certainly iron or steel, with leather straps, iron or brass buckles and rivets, textile linings, padding, and some mail. That statement is a strong inference from the object class, not a materials analysis. No authenticated fragment is available for metallography.

The 1499 Amboise entry, if related to Joan’s actual armor, adds mail, gilding, and crimson satin. Because the identity of that harness is disputed, those materials cannot safely be transferred to the Tours commission.

### 2.4 Cost and quality

Harmand compared the 100-livre payment with:

- 84 livres for the duke of Orléans’s armor in 1415;
- 55 livres for a complete custom harness in 1447;
- 82 livres 10 sous for a harness of Charles VII in 1447.

Such comparisons suggest a substantial, good-quality commission, though prices also reflected date, components, urgency, ornament, supply, and accounting practice. They do not prove that Joan’s armor was unusually heavy, gilded, Italian, or luxurious. [Harmand, armor chapter](https://jeanne.wiki/harmand-costumes-armure/armure)

### 2.5 Earliest visual evidence

No accepted life portrait shows the suit accurately enough to serve as a technical record. The well-known marginal drawing by Clément de Fauquembergue, made in 1429, depicts Joan symbolically despite the artist never having seen her; it is not an equipment portrait. Later manuscript illuminations, paintings, statues, and stained glass routinely modernized the armor according to the fashions or artistic expectations of their own periods.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because the surviving documents may describe more than one harness, the chain must be reconstructed in branches rather than forced into a single continuous line.

### 3.1 Spring 1429: royal procurement

- **Place:** probably Tours.
- **Commissioner/payer:** the royal administration of Charles VII.
- **Maker:** unnamed master armorer.
- **Recipient and user:** Joan of Arc.
- **Mechanism:** royal military provision.
- **Evidence:** Hémon Raguier’s account as preserved in later documentary extracts.
- **Status:** documented, though the original account’s administrative context should be checked in a critical edition.

Joan then carried or wore armor during the Loire campaign, the march to Reims, the coronation campaign, and subsequent operations.

### 3.2 April–May 1429: Orléans

Joan entered Orléans on 29 April. Testimony and chronicles repeatedly place her armed during the fighting. After being wounded at the Tourelles on 7 May, she appeared on 8 May in light mail rather than necessarily in the complete plate harness.

**Custody:** Joan and her household retained her military equipment. Jean d’Aulon, her squire, would have had functional responsibility for arming her, maintenance, horses, and campaign logistics, although no itemized armor inventory survives.

### 3.3 June–July 1429: Loire and coronation campaigns

The armor accompanied Joan through Jargeau, Meung-sur-Loire, Beaugency, Patay, the advance to Reims, and Charles VII’s coronation. Sources describe her armed but do not supply a serially traceable inventory. Different helmets appear in witness testimony, suggesting either interchangeable elements or multiple headpieces.

### 3.4 September 1429: Paris and Saint-Denis

Joan was wounded in the thigh during the unsuccessful assault at the Porte Saint-Honoré on 8 September 1429. At her 1431 trial, she stated that she offered at Saint-Denis:

> “a complete white harness, such as is suitable for a man-at-arms, with a sword which I had gained before Paris.”

She explained that she made the offering “out of devotion,” following the custom of men-at-arms when wounded, and because Saint-Denis was France’s battle cry. She denied the prosecution’s insinuation that the armor had been placed there to be adored as relics. The accusation and her answer can be compared in the online condemnation dossier. [Condemnation proceeding, articles and replies](https://www.ste-jeannedarc.fr/condamnation/ordinaire_public2.html)

This is the crucial physical transfer:

- **Donor:** Joan.
- **Recipient/keeper:** the abbey church of Saint-Denis.
- **Mechanism:** votive offering after a wound.
- **Objects:** complete white harness plus a captured sword.
- **Evidence:** Joan’s own judicial answer in 1431.
- **Status:** documented.

### 3.5 Was the Saint-Denis harness the Tours harness?

This is unresolved.

**Case for identity:** Joan had received a costly complete suit only months earlier; there is no equally explicit account for a second complete harness before September. It would have been natural to offer the armor in which she had been wounded.

**Case against certainty:** Joan’s wording does not explicitly say “the armor made for me at Tours.” The grammar and later summaries led some writers to suppose the ex-voto was captured equipment. Campaigning could produce replacements, repairs, booty, or multiple ensembles. Joan’s subsequent ability to appear fully armed proves that she either retained another suit, recovered the votive one, or obtained a replacement.

The source supports “a white harness offered by Joan,” not an unquestionable equation with the Tours commission.

### 3.6 1429–30: removal from Saint-Denis

After Charles VII’s forces withdrew, Anglo-Burgundian forces reoccupied Saint-Denis. Later authors say the armor was removed or carried off. Jacques Doublet, writing his history of the abbey in 1625/26, stated that when Saint-Denis was retaken, “the Maid’s arms were removed,” while the sword and its buff-leather belt remained; he claimed personally to have seen the sword and belt many times. Henri Wallon reproduced Doublet’s statement but warned that the supposed sword, like the later museum armor, might not have been authentic. [Wallon, note on the Saint-Denis arms](https://ste-jeannedarc.fr/histoire_wallon/wallon_V-1.html)

This episode has often been described as an English seizure or pillage. The broad military change of possession is historical, but surviving citations do not furnish a contemporary receipt naming individual soldiers, a destination, or subsequent inventory. The armor’s documentary chain therefore breaks at Saint-Denis.

### 3.7 Late 1429–May 1430: replacement or retained armor

Joan continued campaigning. Jean d’Aulon’s testimony places a sallet on her at Saint-Pierre-le-Moûtier. By May 1430 she again possessed a full harness.

Possible reconstructions are:

1. she had offered a secondary or captured suit at Saint-Denis and retained the Tours harness;
2. she retrieved the ex-voto before leaving the region;
3. she obtained a new or captured suit afterward;
4. “complete harness” in narrative sources allowed a mixed assemblage of retained and replaced components.

None can presently be proved.

### 3.8 23 May 1430: capture at Compiègne

Jean Le Fèvre de Saint-Remy describes Joan leaving Compiègne on a fine warhorse, “very well armed in full harness,” wearing over it a rich vermilion cloth-of-gold huque. Chastellain offers a closely related description. [Source extracts concerning Compiègne](https://www.jeannedomremy.fr/TextesDivers/Adrien%20-%20Harmand%20-%20Jeanne%20d%20Arc%20-%20Ses%20costumes%20son%20armure.pdf)

Joan was pulled or forced from her horse and captured by Burgundian forces. Her person and immediate equipment fell into enemy possession. At her trial she said that the sword she carried when captured was one taken from a Burgundian; it was not the Fierbois sword. The trial does not give a corresponding component-by-component description of the captured armor.

Immediate custody should therefore be expressed cautiously:

- the capturing military party took her and what she wore;
- Joan passed into the control of **Jean de Luxembourg** and Burgundian authority;
- the armor likely became captors’ property as war booty;
- no surviving receipt traces its later disposal.

The statement sometimes made that Philippe the Good personally took the armor and later returned it to the French crown is conjectural unless supported by an independent inventory or transfer record.

### 3.9 1499: the Amboise candidate

An inventory of the royal armory at the château of Amboise, dated 23 September 1499, lists:

> “the harness of the Maid, furnished with upper-arm defenses, a pair of mittens, and a headpiece in which there is a mail throat-defense, the border gilded, the interior furnished with crimson satin, lined with the same.”

This is the most important posthumous candidate. It implies that, sixty-nine years after Compiègne, a royal armory identified a particular harness as Joan’s. [Harmand’s transcription and discussion](https://jeanne.wiki/harmand-costumes-armure/armure)

Its difficulties are substantial:

- no surviving document connects the Compiègne captors with Amboise;
- no transfer date or donor is recorded;
- the item’s description is incomplete;
- the same armory inventory included objects attributed to Clovis and to the legendary Lancelot;
- late-medieval and early-modern treasuries regularly attached heroic names to older weapons.

Harmand believed the attribution might nevertheless be genuine and suggested that the Burgundians could have restored Joan’s armor after political reconciliation. He offered no documentary transfer establishing that scenario. It remains a plausible antiquarian hypothesis, not a provenance link.

### 3.10 Disappearance of the Amboise armor

The subsequent custody of the 1499 harness is not securely reconstructed. No surviving modern museum object has been demonstrated to be that entry. Its disappearance could reflect decay, dispersal, reuse, reattribution, transfer, loss during political disturbances, or scrapping. None of those fates is presently documented for the individual object.

### 3.11 Sixteenth-century Anet claim

Antiquarian literature reports an armor called Joan’s in the arms room of the château d’Anet in 1581. The attribution lacks a demonstrated connection to Tours, Saint-Denis, Compiègne, or the Amboise item. Even if the inventory wording is accurately reported, it establishes only that a suit at Anet bore Joan’s name by 1581.

### 3.12 Saint-Denis sword as a residual relic

Doublet’s seventeenth-century statement that the armor was gone but the sword and decorated buff belt remained created a separate relic tradition. The sword was not necessarily Joan’s habitual Fierbois sword: her own trial answer identifies the ex-voto sword as one gained before Paris.

Later visitors described a very large “sword of the Maid” in the Saint-Denis treasury. Its dimensions caused antiquarian skepticism. The object’s subsequent identification and fate are uncertain, and it should not be used to bridge the armor’s missing provenance. [Ayroles’s collection of the Doublet and Père Daniel traditions](https://jeanne.wiki/ayroles-vraie-jeanne/tome4/livre1)

### 3.13 Revolutionary and museum-era attributions

Armor later exhibited or cataloged in Paris as Joan’s did not possess a valid fifteenth-century provenance. George Francis Laking found that an armor in the early Musée d’Artillerie records had been attributed to “La Pucelle” for no established reason. Stylistic and technical examination identified it as much later, probably Renaissance and associated with an Italian/Milanese context rather than Tours in 1429. [Laking, *A Record of European Armour and Arms*, vol. III](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AA_record_of_European_armour_and_arms_through_seven_centuries_%28Volume_3%29.djvu/307)

The Musée d’Artillerie was later combined with the Musée historique de l’Armée to form the present Musée de l’Armée. That institutional succession does not make the museum’s old attribution a continuation of Joan’s custody chain.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Does authentic armor survive?

**Current evidentiary answer:** no armor has been authenticated as Joan’s, and no extant suit has a complete documentary provenance to her.

That conclusion does not deny the original object’s existence. Its existence is independently supported by the royal account, Joan’s testimony, witness testimony, and hostile and favorable chronicles.

### 4.2 The three-object problem

Much confusion results from assuming every record concerns one suit.

| Record | What it establishes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Royal account, 1429 | A complete harness was commissioned for Joan for 100 livres | Exact components, decoration, maker’s name, or later fate |
| Joan’s trial answer, 1431, describing September 1429 | She offered a complete white harness and captured sword at Saint-Denis | That the harness was necessarily the Tours suit |
| Compiègne chronicles, 1430 | She wore full armor when captured | That it was the Tours or Saint-Denis harness |
| Amboise inventory, 1499 | A royal armory labeled a harness as “the Maid’s” | Authenticity or continuity from 1430 |
| Later Musée d’Artillerie attribution | A museum once displayed/cataloged a suit under her name | Fifteenth-century date or ownership by Joan |

### 4.3 Amboise: strongest candidate, weak continuity

The 1499 entry is relatively early and institutionally significant. A royal collection could have preserved genuine equipment. Its details—mail throat defense, gilded edge, crimson satin—are compatible in a broad sense with elite fifteenth-century equipment.

Against this stand the sixty-nine-year documentary gap, the legendary objects in the same inventory, and the absence of a subsequent identifiable survivor. No published metallurgical examination can test an object that has not been located.

### 4.4 Musée d’Artillerie armor

The old Paris attribution fails on chronology and provenance. It represents the broader nineteenth-century museum practice of attaching royal or heroic names to visually impressive armor. Laking explicitly compared the practice with similar false attributions formerly made in the Tower of London. [Laking](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AA_record_of_European_armour_and_arms_through_seven_centuries_%28Volume_3%29.djvu/307)

This is not a case of scientific testing disproving Joan’s DNA or personal measurements. It is a connoisseurial and typological rejection: the armor belongs to a later manufacturing world and lacks a credible custody chain.

### 4.5 The 1996 commercial claim

In 1996 a Paris antiques dealer, Bernard de Souzy, promoted a suit as possibly Joan’s, reportedly after observing its small proportions. The Musée de l’Armée’s curator expressed skepticism. Small dimensions cannot identify a wearer, and no documentary chain connected the suit to Joan. [Contemporary report in *The Independent*](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/found-joan-of-arc-s-suit-of-armour-1337507.html)

The claim remains unverified and should not be presented as a discovery.

### 4.6 Sex, size, and armor identification

Armor cannot be assigned to Joan merely because it is narrow, short, or appears “female.” Fifteenth-century armor was tailored, but body size varies across sexes and ages; suits can also be composites, altered, restored, assembled from mismatched pieces, or made for youths. No uncontested measurement set for Joan exists.

### 4.7 Scientific examination

No authenticated candidate has undergone a published scientific program capable of confirming Joan’s ownership. Potential tests—metallography, slag-inclusion comparison, trace-element analysis, tool-mark study, textile and leather dating, dendrochronology of associated display supports, or biomolecular residue work—could establish date and workshop relationships but would still require documentary provenance to identify the wearer.

No credible DNA authentication of the armor exists.

### 4.8 Authority to authenticate

The power to name the armor shifted over time:

- **1429–30:** Joan, her household, the royal administration, and her captors had direct knowledge.
- **Saint-Denis:** the abbey controlled the votive object and its displayed identity.
- **Royal armory at Amboise:** inventory clerks institutionalized an attribution by 1499.
- **Early-modern treasuries:** clerics and antiquarians transmitted relic labels.
- **Nineteenth-century museums:** military curators and cataloguers inherited and revised heroic attributions.
- **Modern scholarship:** authentication depends upon documentary provenance, technical history, and scientific examination rather than institutional tradition alone.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Charles VII

Royal beneficiary of Joan’s campaign and ultimate source of the administration that paid for her harness. The surviving account makes the armor part of royal military provisioning, not a self-financed peasant acquisition.

### Hémon Raguier

Treasurer connected with the account recording 100 livres for the complete harness. His association is administrative; he is not documented as maker or physical keeper.

### Unnamed master armorer of Tours

Documented occupational maker or supplier. His personal identity is not securely recoverable from the published payment formula.

### Colas/Nicolas de Montbazon

Named in later Tours tradition as the armorer. The attribution is not established by the quoted royal account and should remain labeled traditional or hypothetical.

### Jean d’Aulon

Joan’s squire and close campaign attendant. His rehabilitation testimony is central for her use of a sallet and other equipment. His role makes him a likely day-to-day keeper and organizer of her arms, although no custody inventory survives.

### Jean, duke of Alençon

Campaign associate whose rehabilitation testimony contributes evidence about Joan’s armor, horsemanship, wounds, and head protection.

### Jean de Dunois

Witness to Joan’s conduct at Orléans and to her use of light mail on 8 May 1429.

### Jean Le Fèvre de Saint-Remy and Georges Chastellain

Burgundian chroniclers who described Joan fully armed at Compiègne. They are evidentiary witnesses or later narrators, not custodians of the suit.

### Jean de Luxembourg

The principal lord into whose power Joan passed after capture. Her worn equipment would normally have entered the captors’ control, but no itemized receipt assigns the armor personally to him.

### Philip the Good, duke of Burgundy

Political superior of the Burgundian forces and participant in the negotiations over Joan. Later theories make him an intermediary in the armor’s hypothetical return, but no documented restitution of the harness has been identified.

### Abbey of Saint-Denis

Recipient and guardian institution of the September 1429 ex-voto. It converted military equipment into a displayed devotional and dynastic object.

### Jacques Doublet

Monk and historian of Saint-Denis who reported in 1625/26 that the arms had been removed while a sword and decorated belt remained. His testimony is late but includes a claim of personal observation of the surviving sword.

### Jules Quicherat

Nineteenth-century editor whose five-volume publication of the condemnation and rehabilitation materials made the financial and judicial documents foundational to modern study.

### Adrien Harmand

Author of the most sustained early technical reconstruction, *Jeanne d’Arc: ses costumes, son armure* (1929). His component-by-component reconstruction remains influential but contains conjectural identifications, especially his confidence in the Amboise attribution.

### God, Saint Michael, Saint Catherine, and Saint Margaret

Joan attributed her mission to divine instruction and her “voices.” No source says that these beings forged or physically bestowed the Tours armor. The armor’s religious character came from the mission in which it was used and its deposition as an ex-voto, not from a documented supernatural manufacture.

### Saint Denis

The dedication of the receiving abbey and a patronal figure closely associated with the French monarchy. Joan said she chose Saint-Denis because it was the French battle cry—*Montjoie Saint Denis*—and because warriors customarily made offerings after wounds.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Military function

The harness protected Joan while mounted, directing troops, approaching fortifications, carrying her standard, and participating in assaults. Its practical function must not be displaced by later symbolism: it was working campaign equipment exposed to arrows, stones, lances, falls, rain, mud, and repeated transport.

Armor also made Joan visually legible as a military commander. Her clothing and armor placed her within the material culture of the man-at-arms and formed part of the gendered conduct scrutinized at Rouen.

### 6.2 Royal and command function

The royal payment materially authorized Joan’s position. Supplying a costly full harness, horses, attendants, and standards integrated her into Charles VII’s military apparatus. The armor was therefore an instrument of command status even if it was not formal regalia.

At Reims, armored appearances helped frame Joan as the divinely supported escort of Charles VII’s restoration. The armor itself was not used to crown or anoint him.

### 6.3 Votive offering

The Saint-Denis deposition is the clearest ritual act involving the object. Joan characterized it as:

- an act of devotion;
- a customary offering by a wounded combatant;
- connected to her wound before Paris;
- directed to Saint-Denis because of the saint’s association with France.

Her judges reframed the display as an attempt to make the populace venerate the armor “as relics.” Joan denied that purpose. Thus two contemporary interpretations survive:

- **Joan’s interpretation:** conventional warrior’s ex-voto.
- **Prosecutorial allegation:** self-exalting relic display.

The hostile allegation is evidence that the public display could be politically and devotionally charged; it is not proof that Joan ordered worship of the armor. [Trial text](https://www.ste-jeannedarc.fr/condamnation/ordinaire_public2.html)

### 6.4 Trophy function

If Anglo-Burgundian forces deliberately removed the Saint-Denis arms, their value reversed: what had been a French votive memorial became enemy booty or a suppressed symbol. Similarly, the armor taken at Compiègne was material proof that the celebrated Maid had been captured.

### 6.5 Relic formation

Joan was not canonized until 1920, but objects associated with her attracted relic-like treatment long before formal canonization. The Saint-Denis display, the Amboise label, early-modern treasury traditions, and museum attributions progressively detached “the armor of the Maid” from ordinary military equipment and made it a historical-sacral name capable of migrating between objects.

There is no evidence of an established liturgy in which authentic armor was ritually touched or processed after Joan’s canonization, because no authentic armor was available.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 “Heaven-sent white armor”

Later retellings sometimes present Joan’s shining armor as virtually miraculous. The primary record instead documents an ordinary fiscal mechanism: a paid armorer supplied it for 100 livres tournois. The mission may have been understood religiously; the manufacture was not recorded as supernatural.

### 7.2 Armor forged exactly to Joan’s female form

It was probably tailored, as quality armor normally was. But no surviving measurements or fitting record describes its accommodation to breasts, hips, height, or weight. Modern “female armor” imagery often projects later costume conventions onto the fifteenth century. A functional harness need not have announced the wearer’s sex externally.

### 7.3 Permanently shining silver armor

*Harnois blanc* means polished or uncovered plate, not silver armor. No source identifies silver as its material.

### 7.4 One armor throughout the entire career

This is possible but unproved. The Saint-Denis offering followed by renewed use of full armor creates a documentary problem. Historical reconstructions that confidently carry one unchanged suit from Tours to Compiègne and then Amboise conceal the gaps.

### 7.5 The Amboise return legend

Harmand proposed that the Burgundians may have returned Joan’s armor after later reconciliation, allowing it to enter the royal armory at Amboise. This bridges the provenance elegantly but lacks a transfer document. It is a scholarly conjecture, not an early-attested legend or documented restitution.

### 7.6 Survival in a museum

The recurring story that Joan’s armor stands in the Musée de l’Armée confuses commemorative displays, reconstructions, and formerly misattributed later armor with a genuine relic. The museum preserves important medieval and Renaissance arms, but its general collection history does not authenticate a Joan suit. [Musée de l’Armée medieval and Renaissance galleries](https://www.musee-armee.fr/votre-visite/les-espaces-du-musee/departement-ancien-armures-et-armes-anciennes-xiiie-xviie-siecle.html?ADMCMD_simTime=1741683600)

### 7.7 Armor as Joan’s principal weapon

Modern images often give the suit and sword primary importance. Contemporary evidence emphasizes her standard at least as strongly. Joan said she carried the standard to avoid killing anyone and that she had never killed a man. The armor functioned as protection and command equipment rather than proof of personal swordsmanship.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Original Tours harness

- **Status:** lost.
- **Last certain custodian:** Joan and her military household, unless it is identical with the Saint-Denis offering.
- **Present location:** unknown.
- **Condition:** unknown.
- **Accession number:** none.
- **Scientific examinations:** none possible on an authenticated survivor.

### 8.2 Saint-Denis white harness

- **Status:** lost after documented deposition.
- **Last certain location:** abbey church of Saint-Denis, September 1429.
- **Later report:** removed after the opposing forces regained Saint-Denis.
- **Present location:** unknown.
- **Identity with Tours harness:** unresolved.

### 8.3 Compiègne harness

- **Status:** captured and thereafter untraced.
- **Last certain location:** on Joan’s body during the sortie and capture at Compiègne, 23 May 1430.
- **Immediate control:** Burgundian captors.
- **Present location:** unknown.
- **Identity with Tours or Saint-Denis armor:** unresolved.

### 8.4 Amboise candidate

- **Status:** documented by attribution in 1499; now unidentified or lost.
- **Last certain location:** royal armory, château of Amboise.
- **Authenticity:** disputed and unproved.
- **Current candidate object:** none securely linked.

### 8.5 Museum and private claimants

No museum currently publishes an armor with an accepted accession identity as the authentic harness of Joan of Arc. Later suits called hers are better classified as:

- mistaken heroic attributions;
- reconstruction armor;
- theatrical or commemorative armor;
- privately promoted candidates without adequate provenance.

There is no active legal title or repatriation dispute over an authenticated original because no authenticated original is known.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Nineteenth-century national image

After the French Revolution and especially during the nineteenth-century revival of Joan as a national figure, armor became her dominant visual attribute. Romantic, academic, Catholic, republican, and nationalist representations differed politically but repeatedly used a polished plate harness to make her mission immediately recognizable.

Artists often used contemporary museum armor or later Gothic and Renaissance models. Consequently, public memory commonly dresses a woman of 1429 in armor styles dating from the later fifteenth or sixteenth century.

### 9.2 Sculpture and painting

Works by Emmanuel Frémiet, Paul Dubois, Jules-Eugène Lenepveu, Jean-Jacques Scherrer, Henri Chapu, and others helped standardize the armored Joan. The Musée d’Orsay catalogs Chapu’s *Jeanne d’Arc en armure*, while noting its present institutional location in the Louvre’s graphic-arts department. [Musée d’Orsay object notice](https://www.musee-orsay.fr/fr/oeuvres/jeanne-darc-en-armure-241276)

These are reception objects, not technical copies of the lost armor.

### 9.3 Harmand’s 1929 reconstruction

Adrien Harmand attempted a source-based reconstruction using documentary vocabulary, comparative armor, manuscript images, and a young female model. His work was a major advance over fantasy armor because it addressed the actual technology of 1420–30. Nevertheless, the reconstructed cuirass, articulation, mittens, helmet, and undergarments remain reasoned selections among period possibilities. The BnF’s Catalogue collectif de France records the work and a copy in the Musée de l’Armée library. [CCFr catalog record](https://ccfr.bnf.fr/portailccfr/ark%3A/16871/0018682235)

### 9.4 Film, theater, and popular culture

Stage and screen productions necessarily manufacture new suits. Influential examples include:

- Carl Theodor Dreyer’s *La Passion de Jeanne d’Arc* (1928), which largely centers imprisonment rather than a detailed campaign harness;
- Victor Fleming’s *Joan of Arc* (1948);
- Robert Bresson’s *Procès de Jeanne d’Arc* (1962);
- Jacques Rivette’s *Jeanne la Pucelle* (1994);
- Luc Besson’s *The Messenger: The Story of Joan of Arc* (1999).

Games, comics, animation, advertising, and fantasy art commonly transform the armor into a personalized costume, sometimes feminized, magical, anachronistically fluted, or decorated with lilies. These designs document Joan’s modern reception, not the lost artifact.

### 9.5 Festivals and replicas

Orléans, Reims, Compiègne, and other cities use replica armor in commemorations. Such replicas can themselves acquire continuity through annual ceremonial use, repair, and display. A theft reported at Compiègne in 2022 concerned pieces of a modern commemorative armor associated with the Compagnons de Jehanne—not surviving plates from 1430. [*Le Parisien*, report on the replica theft](https://www.leparisien.fr/oise-60/compiegne-mais-qui-a-vole-des-pieces-de-larmure-de-jeanne-darc-12-09-2022-4HPPJEFOQVCTVJTZRWK3BQRR6I.php)

The incident illustrates how a replica can become a locally significant object while remaining materially distinct from the historical harness.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Primary documentary base

The essential evidence consists of:

- royal financial accounts published in documentary collections;
- the 1431 condemnation proceedings;
- the 1450s rehabilitation/nullification investigations;
- French, Burgundian, and English chronicles;
- the 1499 Amboise armory inventory;
- early-modern Saint-Denis treasury history.

The condemnation proceedings survive in multiple manuscripts rather than a modern stenographic transcript. The vernacular French minutes and official Latin record differ in wording and status. The BnF manuscript tradition should be consulted directly for exact philology. Pierre Champion surveyed the manuscripts; BnF Latin 5970 is one authenticated rehabilitation manuscript, signed by the notaries, while Pierre Duparc’s later critical edition also used British Library Stowe 84. [Champion manuscript study](https://jeanne.wiki/champion-manuscrits-rehabilitation/texte) [BnF Latin 5970 description](https://jeanne.wiki/bnf-latin-5970)

### 10.2 Jules Quicherat

Quicherat’s five-volume *Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc* (1841–49) assembled trial texts, testimony, accounts, and chronicles. It remains indispensable, but later manuscript work and editorial standards require checking its readings against manuscripts and later critical editions.

### 10.3 Viollet-le-Duc

Viollet-le-Duc used Joan’s testimony in his history of French arms and armor, treating the Saint-Denis passage as evidence for the meaning of *harnois blanc*. His work helped move discussion toward technical vocabulary but shared the nineteenth century’s confidence in reconstructing lost ensembles from comparative material. [Viollet-le-Duc, “Armure”](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_raisonn%C3%A9_du_mobilier_fran%C3%A7ais_de_l%E2%80%99%C3%A9poque_carlovingienne_%C3%A0_la_Renaissance/Armure)

### 10.4 Adrien Harmand

Harmand’s 1929 study remains the fullest technical reconstruction. Its strengths include:

- careful differentiation of plate components;
- use of contemporary comparative documents;
- attention to under-armor clothing;
- recognition of several helmet types;
- explicit criticism of artistic anachronisms.

Its weaknesses include:

- occasional movement from typological probability to overly confident reconstruction;
- a willingness to accept the Amboise label;
- speculation about Burgundian restitution;
- assumptions about how Joan’s command role affected visor use.

### 10.5 Laking and the rejection of museum romance

Laking’s survey of European armor explicitly treated the old Musée d’Artillerie attribution as unsupported. This represents the transition from heroic museum naming toward comparative chronology and workshop attribution. [Laking](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AA_record_of_European_armour_and_arms_through_seven_centuries_%28Volume_3%29.djvu/307)

### 10.6 Major unresolved questions

1. Was the Saint-Denis ex-voto the Tours harness?
2. If it was, how was Joan re-equipped before Compiègne?
3. Did the Saint-Denis armor survive its removal, and where was it taken?
4. Was the 1499 Amboise harness genuinely hers?
5. Could the Amboise entry describe the Compiègne armor rather than the Saint-Denis armor?
6. When and why did the Amboise object disappear?
7. Does an unrecognized component survive under another attribution?
8. Can the original Hémon Raguier account reveal more about payment date, administrative authorization, or supplier than later excerpts preserve?

No present answer should collapse these separate questions into a single seamless biography.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and near-primary evidence

- The condemnation proceedings, especially Joan’s interrogation concerning the Saint-Denis offering, are the strongest direct evidence for the *blanc harnois*. The hostile articles must be distinguished from Joan’s replies.
- Hémon Raguier’s account is the principal documentary evidence for manufacture and cost.
- Rehabilitation testimony from Jean d’Aulon, Alençon, Dunois, and others supplies equipment and use details.
- Le Fèvre de Saint-Remy and Chastellain supply Burgundian descriptions of the Compiègne harness.
- The 1499 Amboise armory inventory is the earliest posthumous physical claimant.
- Doublet’s Saint-Denis history records the early-modern institutional memory of the removed armor and retained sword.

### Critical cautions

- Modernized online transcriptions are useful for access but should be checked against manuscript images and critical editions.
- Quicherat is foundational but not the final word on manuscript readings.
- Harmand is indispensable for technical reconstruction but not itself evidence that the reconstruction’s selected components belonged to Joan.
- Museum and treasury labels demonstrate attributed identity at a given date; they do not, without supporting provenance, demonstrate original ownership.
- No modern claimant should be accepted from dimensions, visual impression, devotional use, or institutional prestige alone.
