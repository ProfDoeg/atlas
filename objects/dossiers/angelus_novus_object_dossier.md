# Paul Klee's "Angelus Novus": Object Dossier

**Proposed filename:** `angelus_novus_object_dossier.md`  
**Research current to:** 6 September 2026

## Evidentiary key

- **Documented:** supported by a contemporary document, institutional catalogue, physical examination, or several mutually consistent sources.
- **Scientific/technical finding:** derived from conservation examination or instrumental analysis.
- **Scholarly inference:** an interpretation argued from evidence but not directly documented.
- **Reported recollection:** testimony given later by a participant or near-participant.
- **Disputed:** materially conflicting accounts survive.
- **Unverified:** repeated in secondary literature without an accessible primary record.
- **Reception history:** something done with the image or its name, not evidence of Klee’s original intention.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Identity

*Angelus Novus* is a unique work on paper made by Paul Klee in 1920. It belongs to the Israel Museum, Jerusalem, where it is catalogued as **B87.0994**. Klee entered it in his own oeuvre register as **1920, 32**; modern references therefore sometimes style it *Angelus Novus, 1920, 32*. The authoritative published medium and dimensions are **oil transfer and watercolor on paper, mounted on cardboard, 31.8 × 24.2 cm**. The museum credit identifies it as a gift of Fania and Gershom Scholem, Jerusalem, with John Herring, Marlene and Paul Herring, and Jo Carole and Ronald Lauder, New York ([Jewish Museum object and exhibition record](https://thejewishmuseum.org/exhibitions/paul-klee-other-possible-worlds/); [Staatliche Museen/Zentrum Paul Klee work list](https://www.smb.museum/fileadmin/website/Presse/Pressematerial/2008/10/11_Klee_Werkliste.pdf)).

It is variously called a drawing, watercolor, painting, oil-transfer drawing, monoprint, or transfer monotype. “Oil-transfer drawing with watercolor” is the least misleading technical description: it is a unique drawing transferred mechanically through an oil-coated intermediary, then colored, rather than a conventional editioned print.

### 1.2 Names

- **Latin:** *Angelus Novus*, literally “new angel.” *Angelus* means messenger or angel; *novus* means new, novel, fresh, or unprecedented.
- **German:** *Neuer Engel*, “New Angel,” Benjamin’s ordinary translation; also *Engel der Geschichte*, “Angel of History,” the name Benjamin gave not to Klee’s object as such but to the figure he imagined through it.
- **Hebrew:** אנגלוס נובוס, a transcription of the Latin; מלאך חדש (*mal’akh ḥadash*), “new angel,” is the semantic translation.
- **French:** *Ange nouveau*; **Italian:** *Angelo nuovo*; **Spanish/Portuguese:** *Ángel nuevo/Anjo novo*. These are reception-language translations, not titles written by Klee.
- **Common modern epithet:** “the Angel of History.” This derives from Walter Benjamin’s 1940 thesis IX and should not be silently substituted for Klee’s title.

There is another Klee work also entered in 1920 under the title *Angelus novus*, catalogue number **1920, 69**, a pencil drawing on letter paper, approximately 30 × 22 cm, now recorded in a private Swiss collection. It is not the Israel Museum object. The present dossier tracks **1920, 32** ([published list of Klee’s angel works](https://brill.com/previewpdf/book/9783846746684/B9783846746684-s017.xml)).

### 1.3 Appearance

The sheet presents a frontal, fantastic, approximately anthropomorphic figure against a pale, variegated ground. Its head is disproportionately large. Wide, asymmetrical eyes look outward; the mouth is open, displaying pointed or toothlike forms; curls or scrolls flank the head; the raised, fanlike appendages are read as wings. The legs are short and darkly articulated. Warm ochres, yellow-browns, reddish pinks, muted blue-green, and violet accents sit around and between the black transferred lines.

Benjamin’s famous description—staring eyes, open mouth, spread wings—is selective but recognizably grounded in the image. It is not a neutral inventory. The picture itself does not depict ruins, dead bodies, Paradise, a storm, or movement into the future. Those belong to Benjamin’s allegory.

Klee signed the work **“Klee”** near the lower right. Published references report its title and work number in the artist’s mounting or catalogue apparatus, but a complete recto-and-verso inscription transcription is not available in the open museum records consulted. No published weight was found.

### 1.4 Construction and technique

Klee developed his oil-transfer process around 1919. He covered one side of a sheet with oil paint or printer’s ink, placed it face-down on the intended support, positioned a preparatory drawing above it, and traced the design with a stylus or needle. Pressure transferred broken, granular lines to the final sheet; incidental rubbing could also produce smears or patches. Klee then added watercolor. The method preserved a relation to an original drawing while introducing pressure, interruption, texture, and chance ([MoMA technical account](https://www.moma.org/momaorg/shared/pdfs/docs/press_archives/5699/releases/MOMA_1978_0135_126.pdf); [MoMA, comparable oil-transfer work](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/37347); [British Museum discussion](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1990-0623-22)).

Klee commonly mounted works on paper to thin secondary cardboard. Museums conserving comparable Klee sheets have found discoloration, former mounting damage, cutting, and folding, but these general findings must not be transferred automatically to *Angelus Novus* ([Moderna Museet conservation report](https://www.modernamuseet.se/en/news/conservation-paul-klee/)).

### 1.5 The hidden interleaf

The physical object is more complex than the conventional formula “paper on cardboard” suggests. Beneath Klee’s image is an older printed sheet used as an interleaf or secondary element. A narrow portion of the engraving remains visible around the margins. In 2015 artist R. H. Quaytman identified it as an **1838 engraving of Martin Luther by Christian Friedrich Müller**, after a 1521 portrait associated with Lucas Cranach the Elder. The engraving bears the later traditional declaration attributed to Luther at Worms: “Here I stand, I can do no other; God help me.”

The sequence appears to be:

1. Klee’s oil-transfer-and-watercolor sheet;
2. the nineteenth-century Luther engraving, largely concealed but visible at its edges;
3. a cardboard mounting support.

Whether Klee chose the engraving for iconographic reasons or simply reused available printed paper remains disputed. Annie Bourneuf argues that the visible interleaving participates in the picture’s early-Weimar religious and political context. She does **not** claim that Benjamin demonstrably knew the identity of the hidden sitter. Nor is there evidence that Klee intended a future viewer to detach the sheets ([University of Chicago Press summary](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/B/bo146792297.html); [Oxford Academic abstract](https://academic.oup.com/chicago-scholarship-online/book/45487?itm_content=University_of_Chicago_Press_0&login=false)).

### 1.6 Condition

The museum has not published a full current condition report in the accessible sources. The sheet survives intact enough for display and lending. No documented restoration, cleaning, pigment replacement, or physical separation of its layers was found. Scholem wrote that Benjamin “cut” it from its frame in 1940, but the object shows no publicly reported evidence that its artist’s cardboard mount was cut; Bourneuf cautions that Scholem’s verb may not be literal. The known alterations are framing, unframing or removal from a frame, remounting or reframing at undocumented dates, and non-invasive technical investigation.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Creation

**Documented:** Klee made the work in 1920, almost certainly in Munich, where he lived and worked. Its number, 1920, 32, comes from the artist’s own systematic oeuvre register. The work predates Klee’s formal move to teach at the Bauhaus in 1921.

No commission, patron, contract, or documented manufacturing cost is known. It appears to have been an independently conceived studio work offered through Klee’s dealer Hans Goltz. The source and manufacturer of the paper, watercolor, oil medium, cardboard, and reused Luther engraving have not been established in published technical literature.

A preparatory sheet has not been securely identified in the sources consulted. Because the oil-transfer method normally employed a separate model drawing, one probably existed, but its survival and present location cannot be established.

### 2.2 First exhibition and sale stock

The earliest secure public attestation is the large Paul Klee exhibition at **Galerie Hans Goltz, Munich, May–June 1920**, where *Angelus Novus* was shown. Goltz represented Klee and held the picture when Benjamin purchased it in late May or early June 1921.

Sources sometimes say Benjamin bought the work “from Klee.” The immediate transaction, however, was through Goltz’s gallery. No evidence shows that Benjamin and Klee met; the Zentrum Paul Klee explicitly notes that they did not ([Zentrum Paul Klee interpretive guide](https://guide.zpk.org/fr/fokus-cover-star-klee)).

### 2.3 Original meaning

No surviving statement by Klee explains this particular angel. Any claim that he painted “the angel of history,” anticipated the Holocaust, depicted Benjamin, or made a specifically Jewish angel is reception history, not documented intention.

Bourneuf reconstructs a plausible context: post–First World War debates about religious art, confessional difference, revolutionary politics, and Jewish-Christian exchange. The concealed Luther engraving makes that context materially relevant. Her interpretation remains a scholarly argument, not a recovered declaration by Klee.

Klee made many angelic or quasi-angelic figures before and after 1920, including *Angelus descendens* (1918), *Feuer=Engel* (1919), the second *Angelus novus* drawing (1920, 69), and a large late group in the 1930s. The Israel Museum sheet is therefore neither his first angel nor a unique excursion into the subject.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

## 3.1 Klee and Galerie Hans Goltz, 1920–1921

- **1920:** Paul Klee, Munich—maker and first owner.
- **By May 1920:** Galerie Hans Goltz, Munich—dealer or sales custodian; included in Klee’s May–June exhibition.
- **Late May or early June 1921:** Walter Benjamin bought the work through Goltz for **1,000 paper marks**. The currency is sometimes inaccurately called Reichsmarks in later accounts; “marks” or “paper marks” is safer for a 1921 German transaction. The amount is repeatedly grounded in Benjamin-Scholem correspondence and later testimony ([Portuguese/English exhibition study](https://ccbbstg.blob.core.windows.net/site-prod/2021/07/PaulKlee.pdf)).

No surviving bill of sale has been located in the open sources consulted.

## 3.2 Temporary custody with Gershom Scholem, 1921

Benjamin initially left the picture with his friend Gershom Scholem. Scholem lived in Munich during part of this period and spent time contemplating the picture. For Benjamin’s twenty-ninth birthday, **15 July 1921**, Scholem wrote “Gruß vom Angelus” (“Greeting from the Angelus”), later used in part as the epigraph to thesis IX.

In **November 1921**, Scholem sent the picture to Benjamin in Berlin, after Benjamin had established a new residence. This is custody, not ownership: Benjamin remained the owner.

## 3.3 Benjamin’s Berlin residences, 1921–1933

The picture hung in Benjamin’s successive working and living spaces. It became:

- an image for private contemplation;
- the namesake of a projected journal, *Angelus Novus*, announced in 1922 but never issued;
- an element in Benjamin’s personal angelology and autobiographical writing;
- eventually the visual point of departure for thesis IX of “On the Concept of History.”

Benjamin’s affection for the object is well documented, but phrases such as “his most prized possession” are retrospective characterizations rather than an inventory ranking.

## 3.4 Separation during exile, 1933–1935

After the Nazi seizure of power, Benjamin left Germany in 1933. The best-documented reconstruction says that the work initially remained behind, rather than traveling with him immediately. Friends brought it to him in Paris in **1935**. Accounts saying he carried it when he fled Germany compress this two-stage movement.

The identities of the persons who transported it to Paris and its exact Berlin storage location have not been established in the accessible sources. This is a documented gap.

## 3.5 Paris and the disputed wartime route, 1935–1949

The picture was with Benjamin in Paris by 1935. When he fled the city before the German occupation in June 1940, it was separated from him for the last time. Two incompatible custody accounts survive.

### Account A: Bataille and the Bibliothèque nationale

Scholem’s influential narrative says Benjamin removed the work from its frame, put it with manuscripts in luggage, and entrusted the material to Georges Bataille, then working at the Bibliothèque nationale. Bataille concealed Benjamin’s papers there during the occupation. After the war, the picture and papers passed to Theodor W. Adorno and the Institute for Social Research.

This account is repeated by the Zentrum Paul Klee, the Institute for Advanced Study, and much of the Benjamin literature ([Zentrum Paul Klee](https://guide.zpk.org/fr/fokus-cover-star-klee); [Institute for Advanced Study](https://www.ias.edu/ideas/paul-klee%E2%80%99s-angelus-novus)).

### Account B: Dora Benjamin to the Adornos in New York

Rolf Tiedemann, editor of Benjamin and director of the Adorno Archive, reported that Theodor and Gretel Adorno repeatedly told him that they received the *Angelus* in **New York in 1941**, in a group of materials Benjamin had instructed his sister **Dora Benjamin** to send them. On this version Bataille was essential to the survival of other manuscripts but not necessarily to the picture.

The two accounts cannot presently be reconciled. Possible explanations include divided caches, conflation of the picture with manuscripts, or imprecise later memory. No accessible shipping receipt, Bibliothèque nationale deposit record, customs record, or contemporaneous Adorno inventory conclusively decides the matter. A detailed recent treatment preserves the conflict rather than adopting Scholem’s account automatically ([Bourneuf text](https://dokumen.pub/behind-the-angel-of-history-the-quotangelus-novusquot-and-its-interleaf-9780226816715.html)).

Benjamin died at Portbou on 26 September 1940. The picture did **not** accompany him over the Pyrenees and was not in the fatal suitcase whose contents have generated separate legends.

## 3.6 The Adornos and the Institute for Social Research

Whichever wartime route is correct, the picture came into the custody of **Theodor W. and Gretel Adorno** and the Institute for Social Research. If received in New York in 1941, it remained in American exile until the Adornos returned to Europe; if recovered through Bataille, the postwar transfer date is not securely published.

In **1949**, the Adornos returned to Frankfurt. The picture traveled with them or with the Institute’s recovered property. It reportedly hung first at the Institute for Social Research and later over the piano in the Adornos’ home. Jürgen Habermas and Max Horkheimer were among visitors who saw it there. These sightings establish custody, not ownership.

## 3.7 Benjamin’s will and the ownership dispute

Benjamin’s **1932 testament** expressed a wish that the picture go to Scholem. Yet Benjamin’s son, **Stefan Benjamin**, was his legal heir. A prolonged disagreement followed between Scholem, asserting Benjamin’s specific testamentary intention, and Stefan, holding the heir’s legal interest.

This is often misrepresented as Adorno simply “giving” the object to Scholem immediately after the war. It remained with the Adornos for decades. After Theodor Adorno’s death in 1969, Gretel Adorno participated in efforts to resolve its disposition.

A settlement between Scholem and Stefan Benjamin eventually allowed the picture to go to Jerusalem. The work was transported there in **1972**. Available summaries do not disclose the complete legal instrument, any payment, or whether title was transferred as inheritance, settlement, gift, or release of claim. The correct description is therefore: **transfer following a negotiated resolution between Benjamin’s heir and intended beneficiary**.

## 3.8 Gershom and Fania Scholem, Jerusalem, 1972–1980s

From 1972 the work hung in Gershom and Fania Scholem’s apartment in the Rehavia neighborhood of Jerusalem. It remained a privately held object but was accessible to visitors and scholars.

Gershom Scholem died in 1982. Sources disagree slightly on the museum-transfer year:

- some institutional and scholarly accounts say Fania Scholem gave it in **1987**;
- other sources say it has been in the Israel Museum since **1989**.

The museum accession number begins **B87**, strongly supporting an accession or acquisition date in 1987, although formal transfer, fundraising, cataloguing, or first display may have extended beyond that year. The published credit includes both Scholems and several American donors, indicating that the museum acquisition involved supplementary donor support rather than a simple unassisted bequest by Fania alone. No public source consulted explains the financial arrangement.

## 3.9 Israel Museum, late 1980s–present

The Israel Museum is the present owner and custodian. The work belongs to its Prints and Drawings collection. It has been displayed in Jerusalem and lent sparingly because it is a light-sensitive work on paper.

Documented or well-attested presentations include:

- Israel Museum collection displays after acquisition;
- *Remembering*, Israel Museum, 2012, where it formed a conceptual link between works by Joseph Beuys and Tadeusz Kantor ([Culture.pl report](https://culture.pl/pl/wydarzenie/wspolna-wystawa-kantora-i-beuysa-w-jerozolimie));
- *Die Engel von Klee*, Zentrum Paul Klee, Bern, 2012; the original was present only for a limited part of the exhibition, consistent with conservation limits ([Zentrum Paul Klee exhibition page](https://www.zpk.org/de/ausstellung/die-engel-von-klee));
- technical study connected with R. H. Quaytman’s *חקק, Chapter 29*, Tel Aviv Museum of Art, 2015;
- planned loan to *Paul Klee: Other Possible Worlds*, Jewish Museum, New York, 20 March–26 July 2026.

The 2026 loan did not reach New York at the exhibition’s opening. International transport disruption connected with the Iran war left the original in Jerusalem; the Jewish Museum installed an authorized exhibition copy in a recessed display space. The official exhibition page continued to list the Israel Museum original, while the gallery label disclosed that a copy was on view ([official copy notice](https://thejewishmuseum.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Paul-Klee_Complete-Exhib-Texts-FINAL.pdf); [contemporary report](https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2026/04/01/paul-klee-angelus-novus-missing-exhibition-jewish-museum-new-york-iran-war)). No reliable source establishes that the original arrived before the exhibition closed. As of September 2026, the safest conclusion is that it remained in Israel Museum custody.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Is the Israel Museum sheet Klee’s original?

**Yes, on the available evidence.** Its identity rests on:

- Klee’s signature;
- entry in Klee’s own work register as 1920, 32;
- exhibition through Klee’s dealer in 1920;
- purchase by Benjamin in 1921;
- extensive correspondence and testimony;
- physical consistency with Klee’s oil-transfer practice;
- inclusion in the Paul Klee catalogue raisonné;
- an unusually strong custody chain despite the 1940–postwar routing dispute.

No rival object claims to be Benjamin’s *Angelus Novus*. No serious attribution challenge or forgery allegation was found.

### 4.2 The second *Angelus Novus*

Klee’s catalogue number 1920, 69 is a separate pencil drawing with the same title. Its existence does not challenge the identity of 1920, 32. It shows that the title was not exclusively attached to one physical sheet in Klee’s studio practice.

### 4.3 “Original,” “monoprint,” and reproductions

Because the transfer method produced a unique receiving sheet, the Israel Museum object is the original. Later posters, photographic reproductions, facsimiles, exhibition copies, and digital files are not impressions from an edition made by Klee.

The Jewish Museum’s 2026 shop marketed a **five-color facsimile on Rives laid paper, edition of 500**, printed in Germany. Its dimensions, 12.6 × 15.75 inches, differ from the original; it is plainly commercial reproduction, not a Klee print ([Jewish Museum shop](https://shop.thejewishmuseum.org/paul-klee-print-angelus-novus-1920.html)).

### 4.4 Hidden-image claims

The Luther engraving is not a conjectural image perceived in stains. Its exposed margins correspond to a documented 1838 engraving, and Quaytman achieved a detailed visual match. The attribution is accepted in subsequent scholarship.

The interpretation is less certain:

- **Physical fact:** a Müller engraving after Cranach lies beneath the Klee sheet.
- **Strong identification:** the sitter is Martin Luther.
- **Plausible inference:** Klee deliberately left portions visible.
- **Unproved:** Klee intended the angel to supersede, answer, conceal, or transform Luther.
- **Unproved:** Benjamin identified Luther or knew what the entire underlying engraving showed.
- **Unsupported:** the angel is a coded portrait of Luther or Benjamin.

### 4.5 Authority to authenticate

Authority has moved from maker and dealer to documentary scholarship and museum conservation:

1. Klee’s register and signature;
2. Galerie Goltz’s exhibition and sale;
3. Benjamin’s and Scholem’s correspondence;
4. the Paul Klee Foundation/Zentrum Paul Klee catalogue raisonné;
5. Israel Museum curatorial and conservation examination.

No laboratory dating was needed to establish authorship, and no published radiocarbon, pigment-spectroscopic, fibre, or binder analysis was found.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

The work had no documented liturgical, magical, coronation, oath-taking, pilgrimage, or sacramental function. It is a secular modern artwork whose later treatment acquired quasi-reliquary qualities.

### 6.1 Benjamin’s contemplative use

Scholem called it a picture for meditation and a reminder of Benjamin’s spiritual vocation. Benjamin’s repeated moves with the small sheet and his desire to recover it in exile support a strong private attachment. This was intellectual and personal practice, not an institutional ritual.

### 6.2 Projected journal

Benjamin’s proposed journal *Angelus Novus* was conceived in 1921–22 and never published. Its announcement says a journal’s true vocation is to express or announce the spirit of its age. The image’s title therefore became a programmatic emblem for criticism able to confront discontinuity and seek philosophical universality. The announcement remained unpublished in Benjamin’s lifetime ([Benjamin, “Announcement of the Journal Angelus Novus”](https://cominsitu.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/walter-benjamin-selected-writings-volume-1-19131926-1.pdf); [centenary study](https://ojs.unito.it/index.php/COSMO/article/view/7809)).

### 6.3 “Angel of History”

In thesis IX of “On the Concept of History” (written in 1940), Benjamin imagined Klee’s figure as looking backward at one accumulating catastrophe while a storm from Paradise drives it irresistibly into the future. The storm is “what we call progress.”

The distinction is essential:

- Klee made *Angelus Novus*.
- Benjamin said the “angel of history must look like” Klee’s figure.
- Later culture commonly renamed Klee’s sheet “The Angel of History.”

Benjamin’s text transformed a privately owned modern drawing into a portable emblem of catastrophic history, failed repair, involuntary futurity, and criticism of progress. The German text is available in full here: [“Über den Begriff der Geschichte,” thesis IX](https://hor.de/get/zitate/benjamin_bg_an2.html).

### 6.4 Access and display

There is no religious taboo governing touch or sight. Access is controlled by ordinary museum law, conservation policy, copyright, and loan agreements. Because watercolor and paper are vulnerable to light, public display is intermittent. The rarity of exposure has contributed to the object’s aura, but conservation—not ritual prohibition—is the reason.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 “The picture foretold the Holocaust”

**Modern retrospective legend.** Klee made the picture in 1920; Benjamin wrote thesis IX before his death in 1940. The work’s later association with Nazi persecution and the Holocaust is historically understandable but not evidence that Klee encoded a prophecy. Benjamin’s “single catastrophe” is philosophically broader than one future event.

### 7.2 “Benjamin carried it in his last suitcase over the Pyrenees”

**False conflation.** The picture remained in Paris or had already been sent toward New York. It did not accompany Benjamin to Portbou. The legendary suitcase he carried across the border contained a manuscript or papers whose identity and fate remain disputed; it was not the Klee sheet.

### 7.3 “Bataille saved the angel from the Nazis”

**Early postwar custody tradition, disputed in detail.** Bataille did safeguard Benjamin manuscripts at the Bibliothèque nationale. Scholem extended this history to *Angelus Novus*. The Adorno-Tiedemann recollection offers a different route through Benjamin’s sister. Bataille’s role in saving the specific sheet is therefore plausible and canonical, but not conclusively proved.

### 7.4 “Benjamin’s will simply made Scholem owner”

**Overstatement.** Benjamin’s stated wish favored Scholem, but Stefan Benjamin was the legal heir. The work remained with the Adornos, and a settlement was needed before its 1972 transfer to Jerusalem.

### 7.5 “Klee hid Luther as an intentional secret message”

**Unresolved scholarly question.** Klee visibly mounted the sheet over an engraving whose margins remained exposed. That was an artist’s physical act. The proposition that he selected Luther to stage a confessional or political conflict is a sophisticated inference, especially in the context Bourneuf reconstructs, but no studio note confirms it.

### 7.6 “The figure is a Jewish or Kabbalistic angel”

**Reception claim, not established iconography.** Benjamin and Scholem brought Jewish mystical and Talmudic ideas to their readings. Klee was not Jewish, and no evidence proves that this work depicts a specific being from Kabbalah. Claims about Klee’s direct Kabbalistic intention should be treated cautiously.

### 7.7 “It is a painting by Benjamin”

**Common category confusion.** Klee made it; Benjamin owned and interpreted it. Benjamin’s reading became so dominant that the physical work is often treated as an illustration of a text written twenty years later.

## 8. Current Status and Location

**Owner and custodian:** The Israel Museum, Jerusalem.  
**Collection:** Prints and Drawings.  
**Accession number:** **B87.0994**.  
**Legal status:** museum property acquired through the credited gift/donor arrangement; no current restitution or title dispute was found.  
**Physical status:** extant; original sheet, interleaf, and mount remain together.  
**Public access:** intermittent rather than permanent, subject to works-on-paper conservation and loan schedules.

The museum’s older digital systems also associated it with internal item number **199799**, but B87.0994 is the accession identifier used in the 2026 official loan record.

For the New York exhibition of March–July 2026, the original was supposed to travel from Jerusalem. War-related flight and transport disruption prevented shipment at least through the documented opening period. An authorized copy occupied the intended gallery. No source consulted confirms later arrival. The object should therefore be located, as precisely as public evidence allows, **in Israel Museum custody in Jerusalem**, probably in controlled storage when not displayed.

The museum has not published the work’s current lux limits, storage enclosure, frame specification, insurance valuation, or exact storage room. Those details cannot be established.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Philosophical icon

Benjamin’s thesis IX made the image one of the twentieth century’s most reproduced philosophical emblems. It appears in scholarship on historical materialism, memory, trauma, fascism, revolution, modernity, colonialism, ecological catastrophe, and the ideology of progress. Such uses usually reproduce Benjamin’s angel rather than analyze Klee’s material object.

### 9.2 Visual art

- **HAP Grieshaber, *Engel der Geschichte*, 1964–1981:** a series of twenty-three publication issues named after Benjamin’s formulation.
- **Anselm Kiefer, *Der Engel der Geschichte: Mohn und Gedächtnis*, 1989:** lead books and related works engage the angel of history, ruin, memory, and postwar Germany; an example belongs to the Israel Museum ([Hebrew University study](https://ef.huji.ac.il/sites/default/files/europe/files/kobi_ben_meir_for_website2.pdf)).
- **R. H. Quaytman, *חקק, Chapter 29*, 2015:** paintings and publication developed from direct examination of the object and discovery of the interleaf. Quaytman’s intervention materially changed scholarship on the original ([Sequence Press](https://www.sequencepress.com/products/chapter-29)).
- Numerous political graphics and installations reproduce the spread-winged figure as an emblem of ruins accumulating under the name of progress. These are transformations of Benjamin’s allegory, not copies claiming authenticity.

### 9.3 Film, theatre, and music

- Wim Wenders’s *Der Himmel über Berlin* (*Wings of Desire*, 1987) is regularly linked to Benjamin’s angelic Berlin and the *Angelus* reception, though it does not adapt the object’s provenance literally.
- Laurie Anderson’s “The Dream Before,” on *Strange Angels* (1989), recasts the angel of history in a song set in Berlin.
- The Austrian company TheaterAngelusNovus, active from 1981 to 1988 under Josef Szeiler, adopted the name.
- Egill Ólafsson released an album and title track *Angelus Novus* in 2001.
- John Wolf Brennan’s composition *N-gl*, recorded by Ensemble Sortisatio in 2003, responded to the picture.
- Toshio Hosokawa incorporated Scholem’s “Gruß vom Angelus” in *Sternlose Nacht* (2010/12) and *Drei Engel-Lieder* (2014).

These examples document the image-name’s cultural mobility; they do not enter the custody history.

### 9.4 Reproduction and commerce

The image circulates through posters, books, postcards, apparel, digital files, and purported “limited editions.” Unless accompanied by an institutional facsimile statement, such objects are reproductions rather than prints authorized or signed by Klee.

The 2026 Jewish Museum facsimile is an unusually well-documented example: five-color printing, edition of 500, explicitly sold as a modern print. The museum’s temporary exhibition copy likewise acquired a short institutional life of its own when geopolitical conditions prevented the original’s shipment.

### 9.5 Political deployment

The “angel of history” is repeatedly invoked after wars, revolutions, terrorist attacks, forced migrations, and ecological disasters. Its adaptability rests on the structure of Benjamin’s text: retrospective vision, debris, frustrated repair, and coercive movement called progress. No single political movement controls the emblem.

The object itself has not appeared on a national flag, official coat of arms, circulation coin, or banknote in the sources consulted.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Principal documentary bases

The object’s history is reconstructed from:

- Klee’s handwritten oeuvre register;
- Galerie Goltz’s 1920 exhibition documentation;
- Walter Benjamin–Gershom Scholem correspondence;
- Scholem’s “Walter Benjamin and His Angel” and *Walter Benjamin: The Story of a Friendship*;
- Benjamin’s 1922 journal announcement, autobiographical fragments including “Agesilaus Santander,” and 1940 “On the Concept of History”;
- Benjamin’s 1932 testament and later reports about the estate settlement;
- Adorno-circle recollections transmitted by Rolf Tiedemann;
- the Paul Klee catalogue raisonné;
- Israel Museum catalogue and conservation records;
- Quaytman’s and Bourneuf’s investigation of the interleaf.

### 10.2 Catalogue raisonné

The Paul Klee Foundation’s nine-volume *Catalogue raisonné Paul Klee* is the basic authority for Klee’s corpus. Works from 1919–1922, including *Angelus Novus*, appear in volume 3. Klee’s own unusual numbering system—year followed by sequence number—accounts for “1920, 32.”

### 10.3 Scholem’s interpretive dominance

Scholem’s 1972 essay “Walter Benjamin und sein Engel” established a biography of Benjamin through the angel: acquisition, private identification, journal project, autobiographical angel, historical angel, survival, and testamentary destination. It is indispensable but not neutral. Scholem was at once witness, interpreter, claimant, and eventual recipient.

Consequently, his custody narrative must be weighed against Tiedemann’s report of the Adornos’ contrary account. Scholem’s role does not invalidate his testimony; it does require explicit source criticism.

### 10.4 Material turn

Earlier scholarship concentrated overwhelmingly on Benjamin’s text. Quaytman’s 2015 discovery redirected attention to the object’s layered construction. Technical investigation included:

- close visual examination of the exposed print margins;
- conventional infrared reflectography, which did not reveal the concealed image adequately;
- construction of a mock-up;
- infrared thermography trials at the University of Rome Tor Vergata;
- thermographic examination of the original, which yielded no significant decipherable result;
- manual and digital comparison by Quaytman, culminating in identification of Müller’s engraving.

Thus the decisive “scientific” methods failed; patient visual-documentary comparison succeeded. Michael Maggen of the Israel Museum publicly described this process ([University of Copenhagen seminar record](https://nors.ku.dk/cc/previousseminars/cc16/cc16programme/thursday-13-april-2016/maggen/)).

### 10.5 Annie Bourneuf’s reconstruction

Bourneuf’s *Behind the Angel of History: The “Angelus Novus” and Its Interleaf* (2022) is the fullest object-centered modern study. It connects the material superimposition of angel and Luther to debates over art, Protestantism, Catholicism, Judaism, religious socialism, and interreligious exchange after the First World War.

Its central interpretive risk is also its strength: it derives historical meaning from an underlying print without documentary proof of Klee’s motive or Benjamin’s recognition. Bourneuf generally signals those limits. Her argument supersedes older accounts only on the physical identity of the interleaf, not on every question of intention.

### 10.6 Unresolved research questions

1. What exact paper stocks, pigments, oil binder, and adhesive did Klee use?
2. Where did Klee obtain Müller’s 1838 Luther engraving?
3. Was it selected for its subject or reused opportunistically?
4. Did Benjamin ever see enough of it to identify Luther?
5. Who stored the sheet in Berlin from 1933 to 1935, and who carried it to Paris?
6. Did Bataille hide the picture, or did Dora Benjamin send it to New York in 1941?
7. What contemporary documents record its arrival in Adorno’s custody?
8. What were the exact legal and financial terms of the 1972 settlement?
9. Was the Israel Museum gift legally completed in 1987, 1989, or in stages?
10. What conservation treatment and reframing occurred before and after museum accession?
11. Did the original eventually travel to New York before the 2026 exhibition closed?

These are not trivial omissions. They identify where the object’s apparently familiar biography remains dependent on later narrative.

## 11. Chronology

| Date | Object history |
|---|---|
| **1521** | Lucas Cranach portrait tradition later used for Müller’s image of Luther. This is prehistory of the hidden support, not of Klee’s drawing. |
| **1838** | Christian Friedrich Müller produces the Luther engraving eventually used beneath Klee’s sheet. |
| **1919** | Klee develops or systematizes his oil-transfer drawing method. |
| **1920** | Klee makes and registers *Angelus Novus* as 1920, 32, probably in Munich. |
| **May–June 1920** | Exhibited at Galerie Hans Goltz’s large Klee exhibition in Munich. |
| **Late May/early June 1921** | Walter Benjamin buys it through Goltz for 1,000 marks. |
| **June–November 1921** | Benjamin leaves it temporarily with Gershom Scholem. |
| **15 July 1921** | Scholem writes “Gruß vom Angelus” for Benjamin’s birthday. |
| **November 1921** | Scholem sends the picture to Benjamin’s new Berlin residence. |
| **1922** | Benjamin drafts the announcement for a projected journal titled *Angelus Novus*; the journal never appears. |
| **1932** | Benjamin’s testament designates Scholem as intended recipient of the picture. |
| **1933** | Benjamin flees Nazi Germany; the picture initially remains behind. |
| **1935** | Friends bring the work to Benjamin in Paris. Their identities and route remain inadequately documented. |
| **June 1940** | Benjamin leaves Paris. Scholem says he entrusts the unframed picture and papers to Georges Bataille; the Adorno-Tiedemann account instead places a later shipment by Dora Benjamin. |
| **1940** | Benjamin writes thesis IX of “On the Concept of History,” transforming the figure into the “angel of history.” |
| **26 September 1940** | Benjamin dies at Portbou. The work is elsewhere, not in his border-crossing suitcase. |
| **1941** | **Disputed account:** the Adornos receive it in New York from Dora Benjamin with other materials. |
| **Post-1945** | **Alternative account:** Bataille releases Benjamin’s saved materials, which pass to Adorno/Institute custody. |
| **1949** | The Adornos return to Frankfurt; the work is thereafter documented at the Institute for Social Research and in their home. |
| **1969** | Theodor Adorno dies. Gretel Adorno continues dealing with the picture’s disposition. |
| **1972** | Following agreement between Gershom Scholem and legal heir Stefan Benjamin, the picture goes to Jerusalem. |
| **1972–1982** | Hangs in Gershom and Fania Scholem’s Rehavia apartment. |
| **1982** | Gershom Scholem dies. |
| **1987** | Probable Israel Museum accession date, supported by B87.0994 and accounts naming Fania Scholem’s gift. |
| **1989** | Some sources date the work’s arrival or establishment in the museum to this year; reason for discrepancy remains unresolved. |
| **2012** | Displayed in Jerusalem in connection with *Remembering* and lent for a limited period to *Die Engel von Klee* in Bern. |
| **c. 2013–2015** | R. H. Quaytman and Israel Museum conservators investigate the visible underlying print; infrared methods prove inconclusive. |
| **June 2015** | Quaytman identifies the hidden sheet as Müller’s 1838 engraving of Luther after Cranach. |
| **2015** | Quaytman presents *חקק, Chapter 29* at the Tel Aviv Museum of Art. |
| **2022** | Annie Bourneuf publishes the principal monograph on the object and its interleaf. |
| **20 March 2026** | *Paul Klee: Other Possible Worlds* opens at the Jewish Museum, New York, without the original because transport from Israel is disrupted; an authorized exhibition copy is shown. |
| **26 July 2026** | New York exhibition closes. No consulted source confirms that the original arrived. |
| **6 September 2026** | Best-supported current status: original extant in Israel Museum custody, Jerusalem. |
