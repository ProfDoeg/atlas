# Mama Huaco's bola and sling: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary finding

**Classification:** legendary weapons embedded in the Inca origin traditions of Pacaritambo and the Ayar siblings.

**Finding:** no surviving artifact, archaeological specimen, museum object, accession record, inventory entry, or scientifically examined weapon can presently be identified as “Mama Huaco’s bola” or “Mama Huaco’s sling.” The colonial texts do not establish that the two expressions designate a single object—or even that Mama Huaco personally possessed the sling sometimes attached to her in modern retellings.

The record instead contains three related but textually distinct things:

1. **Mama Huaco’s *ayllos*** in Juan Díez de Betanzos’s *Suma y narración de los incas* (completed in 1551): a bola-type weapon with which she kills an inhabitant of the coca-and-chili settlement near Cuzco.
2. **Mama Guaco’s *haybinto*** in Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa’s *Historia índica* (1572): explicitly glossed as “a stone tied to a rope, with which she fought.”
3. **The sinew slings carried out of Pacaritambo** in Betanzos: the male Ayar siblings emerge with richly worked bags containing “*hondas de niervos*.” The chronicle does not assign one of these slings specifically to Mama Huaco. Betanzos instead makes Ayar Cachi the prodigious slinger who opens ravines in hills.

Accordingly, “Mama Huaco’s bola and sling” is best treated as a **modern composite label** joining her securely attested bola-type weapon to slings belonging to the broader Ayar migration narrative. The physical weapons, if the traditions preserve memories of real ones, are lost beyond identification. Their reconstructable “life” is therefore the history of their successive descriptions, reinterpretations, conflations, and representations.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Names

The bearer’s name appears in colonial Spanish orthographies as:

- **Mama Guaco**
- **Mama Huaco**
- **Mama Vaco**
- **Mamaguaco**
- Modern normalized forms include **Mama Wako**, **Mama Waqu**, and occasionally **Mama Huaca**.

*Mama* is the familiar Quechua title or kin term “mother,” also used honorifically. The second element is unsettled. A frequently cited comparison comes from Ludovico Bertonio’s early-seventeenth-century Aymara vocabulary, in which *huaccu* was understood by later scholars as a “manly” or physically hardy woman. Luis Ramos Gómez uses that lexicographic comparison when discussing why colonial authors repeatedly characterize Mama Guaco as *varonil*. It remains an etymological proposal, not a demonstrated personal-name derivation. The visual and textual similarity between *Huaco* and Quechua *waka/huaca*, “sacred being/place/object,” has also encouraged reinterpretation, but the name must not automatically be translated “Mother Huaca.” The two words are not shown to be identical by the narrative sources. [Ramos Gómez’s study](https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/REAA/citationstylelanguage/get/chicago-author-date?publicationId=20651&submissionId=24878) and the [RAE historical treatment of *huaca*](https://temporalraeweb.rae.es/dhle/huaca) illustrate the separate linguistic questions.

The weapon vocabulary is equally important:

- **Ayllos / ayllos:** Betanzos’s name for the weapon. This is a weapon sense distinct from *ayllu*, “kin group, lineage, or social collectivity,” although colonial spelling makes the forms identical.
- **Haybinto:** Sarmiento’s form. He defines it internally as “a stone tied to a rope.”
- **Bola / boleadora:** modern Spanish functional classifications applied by editors and historians. These are useful glosses but are not Betanzos’s or Sarmiento’s exact generic terms.
- **Honda:** Spanish “sling.”
- **Huaraca / warak’a / waraqa:** Quechua terms commonly used for an Andean sling.
- **Liwi / libi:** terms applied in parts of the Andes to a weighted throwing or entangling weapon. They should not be retroactively substituted for the particular words recorded by Betanzos and Sarmiento without qualification.

### 1.2 The bola or *ayllos*

The earliest description is functional rather than technical. Betanzos says Mama Guaco struck a man “*con unos ayllos*,” killed him, immediately opened him, removed his lungs and heart, and inflated the lungs in view of the settlement. He gives no dimensions, number of weights, stone type, cord material, color, decoration, maker, or prior history. The plural *unos ayllos* is grammatical evidence for the weapon’s name, not secure evidence for the number of balls.

Sarmiento’s later *haybinto* is more narrowly described:

> “*un haybinto—que es una piedra atada en una soga, con que ella peleaba*”

That is: **one stone attached to a rope, used by her in fighting**. This sounds more like a single-weight striking or throwing weapon than the familiar three-weight Patagonian boleadora. Calling it a three-ball boleadora, as some modern educational accounts do, adds a construction detail absent from Sarmiento.

A Toledan-era testimony concerning the Sawasiray ayllu gives yet another object: Mama Huaco reportedly attacked people with a **huincha bearing a piece of gold**. That testimony may describe a weighted strap or cord, but the surviving wording does not prove that it was the same object as Betanzos’s *ayllos* or Sarmiento’s *haybinto*. It is nevertheless an important parallel because it preserves a local claim that her weapon had a gold component. The testimony is discussed and quoted in an academic study of the Sawasiray tradition. [PUCP, *Anthropologica*](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/anthropologica/article/download/390/386/1534)

### 1.3 The sling or slings

In Betanzos, the Ayar men emerge from the Pacaritambo cave carrying:

- golden halberds;
- garments of fine wool woven with gold;
- elaborately worked wool-and-gold bags;
- within those bags, **slings made of sinews**: “*hondas de niervos*.”

The text therefore supplies two materials for the sling assemblage:

- the slings themselves: **sinew**;
- their containers: **wool and gold workmanship**.

It supplies no lengths, weights, inscriptions, colors, makers, or individual assignments. The women emerge with fine mantles, gold-worked belts, gold *tupu* pins, and gold cooking vessels; Betanzos does not say that Mama Guaco carries one of the sinew slings.

Later in the same origin sequence, Ayar Cachi—not Mama Guaco—loads a stone into his sling and strikes four mountains. Each impact allegedly opens a large ravine, at ranges of roughly one to one-and-a-half leagues “according to what they imagine.” His companions’ fear of this power motivates their plot to seal him inside the cave. [Betanzos text](https://americaindigena.com/sumainca.htm)

### 1.4 Comparative physical evidence

Real pre-Hispanic and historic Andean slings demonstrate that such weapons were commonly textile objects, but they cannot fill gaps in the legendary specimen’s description.

Examples include:

- Museo Larco object ML600056, a Chancay *honda/huaraca* made from cotton and camelid fibres. [Museo Larco catalogue](https://coleccion.museolarco.org/detail/43891)
- A pre-Hispanic sling described by Chile’s Museo Nacional de Historia Natural as woven from black, red, white, and brown animal fibre. [Museo Nacional de Historia Natural](https://www.mnhn.gob.cl/noticias/la-honda-prehispanica)
- Nasca sling no. 2928 in the Museo Chileno de Arte Precolombino, made from camelid fibre with a woven cradle, braided and wrapped cords, and tassels; published dimensions are approximately 1,750 × 200 mm. [Museum collection publication](https://museo.precolombino.cl/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/Sheila-Hicks.-Reencuentro.pdf)
- British Museum Am1937,0213.85, once catalogued as a probable Inca sling, is now identified by textile specialist Penny Dransart as a woman’s belt. This is a useful warning that even surviving braided Andean textiles can be misclassified. [British Museum catalogue](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Am1937-0213-85)

None has documented provenance connecting it with Mama Huaco.

### 1.5 Condition and surviving components

No component is known to survive. There are:

- no identified stones;
- no cords or sinew sling;
- no associated bag;
- no inscription;
- no pre-Hispanic image securely identified as the weapon;
- no colonial inventory purporting to list it;
- no relic shrine or museum claimant.

The weapons survive solely as words, copied manuscripts, printed editions, translations, scholarly interpretations, and modern images.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Legendary origin within the narrative

Betanzos places the slings at the moment of emergence from **Pacaritambo/Tambotoco**, the origin cave of the Ayar siblings. They are already finished when brought from the cave; the text identifies no maker, workshop, act of manufacture, or acquisition. Their presence among golden halberds, elite clothing, bags, seeds, and vessels marks the emigrating company as equipped for war, cultivation, domestic production, and rule.

The *ayllos* used by Mama Guaco enter the narrative later, near the coca-and-chili settlement outside Cuzco. Betanzos does not say whether she brought them from Pacaritambo, made them en route, received them from someone else, or took them locally.

Sarmiento similarly supplies no manufacture story for the *haybinto*. It appears in her hands during the violence against the Guallas.

### 2.2 Earliest securely datable textual attestation

The earliest presently identified attestation is **Juan Díez de Betanzos, *Suma y narración de los incas*, 1551**, especially Part I, chapters 3–4.

Betanzos was a Quechua-Spanish interpreter connected to Cuzco and married to Angelina Yupanqui, formerly associated with Atahualpa and Francisco Pizarro. He completed the work for Viceroy Antonio de Mendoza. The Biblioteca Nacional del Perú describes it as the earliest systematic Western-form history of the Inca past and explains that two manuscripts survive: the incomplete Escorial copy used for the 1880 first edition, and a fuller manuscript later identified and published. [Biblioteca Nacional del Perú](https://repositoriodigital.bnp.gob.pe/bnp/recursos/2/html/juan-de-betanzos-y-el-tahuantinsuyo/16/)

What Betanzos says:

- the Ayar men possess “slings of sinews” on emerging;
- Ayar Cachi uses a sling to break mountains;
- Mama Guaco later kills a local man with *ayllos*;
- she mutilates the body and inflates its lungs;
- the spectacle causes the inhabitants to flee.

What he does **not** say:

- that Mama Guaco’s *ayllos* were sacred relics;
- that they were forged or given by a deity;
- that they were a sling;
- that her weapon and Ayar Cachi’s sling were the same;
- that either object survived;
- that either entered a royal treasury or cult;
- that later Incas inherited it.

The relevant passage is available in a complete modern transcription and in an older public-domain edition. [1987 Martín Rubio transcription](https://libros.kichwa.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/suma-y-narracion-de-los-incas-1551-Juan-de-Betanzos.pdf) [Older transcription](https://www.biblioteca-antologica.org/es/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/BETANZOS-Suma-y-narraci%C3%B3n-de-los-Incas.pdf)

### 2.3 Sarmiento’s 1572 recension

Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa composed his history under Viceroy Francisco de Toledo’s government. The manuscript is dated at Cuzco, 4 March 1572. It recasts the origin traditions as part of a Toledan history intended to demonstrate that Inca rule was comparatively recent, violent, and tyrannical.

Sarmiento makes Mama Guaco and Manco Cápac war leaders. He attributes to her two distinct instrumental acts:

1. She throws two golden rods northward to test the soil. One fails to penetrate at Colcabamba; the other enters good soil at Guanaypata.
2. She goes against the Guallas holding a *haybinto*, “a stone tied to a rope.”

The manuscript therefore distinguishes the **gold rods** from the **corded stone weapon**. Modern summaries sometimes combine the rods, bola, sling, and later Inca sceptres into one symbolic complex; Sarmiento does not describe them as one artifact. [Sarmiento edition](https://intiyana.org/bibliodig/files/original/944d24060d55c779ac0747ce880a0ebad1deed50.pdf)

### 2.4 Independent and derivative variants

A later colonial history preserved in the Galapagueana digital collection recounts the testing of the soil with two rods, the killing of a Poque or Gualla, and the inflation of the victim’s lungs, but does not assign the violent episode’s weapon with Betanzos’s precision. It also preserves an alternative account in which Manco Cápac, rather than Mama Huaco, fashions and wears gold plates and a diadem to persuade the inhabitants that he is a child of the Sun. Such alternatives demonstrate how quickly the origin objects became transferable narrative attributes. [Digitized colonial chronicle](https://galapagueana.darwinfoundation.org/common/doc11.pdf)

Cristóbal de Molina’s *Relación de las fábulas y ritos de los incas*, written approximately 1573–1575, is more important for the ritual afterlife of Mama Huaco than for the weapon. Molina says that the field of Sausero, where she planted the first maize, was annually venerated in relation to her body or mummy. He does not establish preservation of her bola or sling. A modern critical English edition explains Molina’s access to elderly Cuzco informants familiar with Inca ritual. [University of Texas/JSTOR edition](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/10.7560/723832.4.pdf) [Smithsonian catalogue record](https://www.si.edu/object/account-fables-and-rites-incas-cristobal-de-molina-introduction-brian-s-bauer-translated-and-edited%3Asiris_sil_968265)

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because no physical specimen is identified, the following is a **story-internal custody chain**, followed by the chain of textual transmission.

### 3.1 Legendary custody: Betanzos

| Stage | Holder and location | Mechanism | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Emergence from Pacaritambo | Ayar men collectively; individual allocation unstated | Objects carried out of the origin cave | Betanzos, 1551 |
| Journey to Huanacauri | Ayar company | Continued possession presumed by narrative, not inventoried | Betanzos |
| Mountain-shattering episode | Ayar Cachi | Personal use of a sling and stone | Betanzos |
| Sealing of Ayar Cachi | Sling’s fate unreported | Custody chain breaks completely | Betanzos |
| Approach to Cuzco | Mama Guaco | Holds or has access to *ayllos*; acquisition unstated | Betanzos |
| Killing at coca-and-chili settlement | Mama Guaco | Weapon used to strike and kill a man | Betanzos |
| After the inhabitants flee | Unknown | The object disappears from the story | Betanzos |

There is no transfer to Manco Cápac, Sinchi Roca, a panaca, the Coricancha, or a later ruler. No theft, burial, sacrifice, inheritance, repair, or destruction is narrated.

### 3.2 Legendary custody: Sarmiento

| Stage | Holder and location | Mechanism | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Migration toward Cuzco | Mama Guaco | Already has *haybinto*; source unstated | Sarmiento, 1572 |
| Attack upon the Guallas | Mama Guaco | Carries the corded stone during intimidation/warfare | Sarmiento |
| Following Inca occupation | Unknown | No further mention | Sarmiento |

The two gold rods follow a different narrative path: Mama Guaco throws them; the first lands unsuccessfully at Colcabamba, while the second penetrates at Guanaypata. The text does not describe either rod’s recovery. Their story is therefore an episode of deposition or abandonment, not a continuous custody chain.

### 3.3 Traditional parallel: Sawasiray testimony

Between 1572 and 1575, witnesses associated with the Sawasiray ayllu reportedly told Toledan investigators that Mama Huaco attacked their forebear’s people using a *huincha* with a piece of gold tied to it. Sawasiray fled. The weapon’s fate is again unstated. This is not a documented transfer but an independent local memory or politically conditioned testimony linking her to violent dispossession.

### 3.4 Textual custody

The object’s only documentable custody is intellectual:

- **1551:** Betanzos completes the *Suma*.
- **Late sixteenth century:** an incomplete copy enters what became the Escorial manuscript tradition.
- **1572:** Sarmiento completes his *Historia índica* for Toledo; the work is sent toward Spain and remains unpublished for centuries.
- **1880:** Marcos Jiménez de la Espada publishes the incomplete Betanzos text.
- **Twentieth century:** editors repeatedly publish Sarmiento and Betanzos, normalizing variable spellings.
- **1987:** María del Carmen Martín Rubio publishes an edition based on the fuller Betanzos manuscript, materially improving access to the work.
- **Late twentieth and twenty-first centuries:** historians frequently gloss *ayllos* or *haybinto* as “boleadora”; popular accounts increasingly describe a standardized multi-ball weapon and sometimes attach “the sling” to Mama Huaco herself.

This is transmission of a **description**, not custody of a relic.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Did the named object physically exist?

Three propositions must be separated:

- **Documented:** bola-like and sling weapons existed in the Andes; chronicles and surviving textiles establish the general weapon classes.
- **Documented:** sixteenth-century writers recorded traditions in which Mama Huaco used an *ayllos* or *haybinto*.
- **Unestablished:** a particular historical woman called Mama Huaco used a particular recoverable weapon during the founding of Cuzco.

Archaeology can demonstrate settlement, conflict, migration, cult places, and weapon technologies in the Cuzco region. It cannot presently identify this individual weapon.

### 4.2 Is the bola the sling?

Probably not in the literal descriptions.

- A sling has a central cradle holding a detachable projectile and two cords.
- Sarmiento’s *haybinto* retains its stone on a rope.
- Betanzos differentiates *hondas* and *ayllos* when listing arms elsewhere.
- Colonial lexical evidence likewise treats *ayllo* as a bola-like weapon used to catch or strike enemies.

Thus “bola and sling” should denote **two weapon traditions**, not one object with two interchangeable names.

### 4.3 Is Betanzos’s *ayllos* Sarmiento’s *haybinto*?

They occupy the same narrative function: Mama Huaco bears a corded impact weapon during the terrorization of Cuzco’s earlier inhabitants. It is therefore reasonable to regard them as variant descriptions of the same **legendary weapon episode**. It is not possible to show that they describe the same physical object or identical construction.

### 4.4 Was the weapon made of three stone balls and leather?

That familiar modern reconstruction is **unverified**. Neither principal source says “three balls.” Sarmiento specifies one stone and one rope. Betanzos supplies neither number nor material. Leather cords and multiple stones are plausible for some South American bolas, but plausibility is not object-specific evidence.

### 4.5 Was there a gold weight?

Only the Sawasiray testimony, as preserved and discussed in later scholarship, reports a *huincha* with a piece of gold tied to it. Betanzos and Sarmiento describe no gold on the attack weapon. Sarmiento’s separately thrown soil-testing rods are gold. Later synthesis may have drawn these objects together.

### 4.6 Rival claimants and scientific tests

There are no known rival relics, museum claimants, laboratory examinations, radiocarbon dates, fibre analyses, metallurgical tests, or provenance investigations concerning an alleged original. No ecclesiastical, state, museum, or Indigenous authority currently authenticates a physical weapon as hers.

### 4.7 Historical core

Some scholars interpret the Ayar story as encoding the movement and alliance of social groups and the displacement of earlier Cuzco populations. Brian Bauer’s archaeological work around Pacariqtambo argues that Puma Orco may be the landscape identified with Tambotoco and that Maukallaqta was monumentalized by Cuzco elites in connection with the origin tradition. That is evidence for the political and topographic embedding of the myth, not for the weapon’s material survival. [Bauer, “Pacariqtambo and the Mythical Origins of the Inca”](https://www.periodicos.capes.gov.br/index.php/acervo/buscador.html?id=W2327537176&source=all&task=detalhes)

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Mama Huaco / Mama Guaco

**Connection:** legendary wielder of the *ayllos/haybinto*; war leader; in Sarmiento, thrower of the gold soil-testing rods.  
**Date of attestations:** 1551 and 1572.  
**Tier:** early colonial attestation of an Inca origin tradition, not contemporary documentation of the supposed founding event.

### Ayar Cachi / Ayarcache

**Connection:** husband or brother-partner of Mama Guaco in Betanzos and Sarmiento; owner-user of the narrative’s explicitly demonstrated sling. His shots break four mountains; the company later imprisons him in the origin cave.  
**Tier:** legend recorded in 1551 and 1572.

### Manco Cápac / Ayar Manco

**Connection:** leader of the migration and beneficiary of the conquest accomplished in part through Mama Huaco’s intimidation. In some later or alternative variants, he—not Mama Huaco—carries or throws the golden foundation staff. No principal source transfers her bola to him.

### Ayar Auca, Ayar Uchu, Mama Ocllo, Mama Cura/Ipacura, Mama Raua

**Connection:** companions in the migration. Their shifting pairings across chronicles show that the narrative was not transmitted as a single fixed genealogy.

### Guallas, Poques, Alcavizas and Sawasiray people

**Connection:** named populations represented as occupying the Cuzco region before the Inca founders. The weapon operates against them as an instrument of terror and territorial displacement. “Gualla” and related spellings should not be silently replaced by “Huaylas,” a different ethnogeographic name, though some translations have done so.

### Juan Díez de Betanzos

**Connection:** earliest known recorder of the *ayllos* episode and of the sinew slings. He did not witness the legendary event; his engagement is textual and translational.

### Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa

**Connection:** recorder of the *haybinto* and gold-rod variants. His history was written within Toledo’s project of collecting testimony about Inca origins and presenting Inca sovereignty as usurpation.

### Francisco de Toledo and his investigative network

**Connection:** commissioned or institutionalized a major body of testimony on Inca rule, including Sarmiento’s history and ayllu depositions. The resulting archive preserved variants of Mama Huaco’s weapon while framing them for colonial legal and political purposes.

### Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala

**Connection:** depicts and discusses “Mama Vaco Coya” in the *Nueva corónica y buen gobierno*, completed around 1615. His image establishes a major visual reception of Mama Huaco but does not document the bola’s survival. The autograph manuscript is held by the Royal Danish Library and has been on UNESCO’s Memory of the World register since 2007. [Royal Danish Library](https://www.kb.dk/en/find-materials/collections/manuscript-collection/chronicle-guaman-poma) [Digitized page/text](https://docs.enriquedussel.com/txt/Textos_200_Obras/Relacion_critica_oprimidos/Primer_cronica.1-Guaman_Poma.pdf)

### Cristóbal de Molina

**Connection:** records the continuing ritual association of Mama Huaco, her body, and Sausero’s maize. He supplies evidence for her cultic afterlife, but not for custody of the weapon.

### Divine beings

- **Viracocha:** in Sarmiento’s framing, ultimately orders or enables the creation and emergence of the founding peoples.
- **The Sun:** legitimates the founders in several variants; Betanzos has the newcomers tell Alcaviza that the Sun sent them.
- **Huanacauri:** both a mountain and a sacred being/place in the migration complex; Ayar Uchu becomes a stone or huaca there in important variants.

No source says that a deity forged Mama Huaco’s bola or that the weapon itself became a divine being.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Warfare and psychological terror

The weapon’s primary narrated function is violent conquest. Mama Huaco’s blow kills; the subsequent opening of the body and inflation or biting of the lungs transforms the killing into a spectacle. The intended narrative result is flight: the local people conclude that the newcomers may eat human flesh and abandon their settlement.

Sarmiento’s description is openly hostile, calling her purpose “diabolical.” That language belongs to the Christian colonial narrator’s judgment. It is not an Indigenous name for the rite.

### 6.2 Possible divinatory structure

Scholars have compared the inflated-lung episode with **kallpa**, a form of divination in which llama lungs were inflated and examined to answer questions of war, succession, or other major concerns. This is a plausible ritual interpretation: the victim is treated like a sacrificial animal, and the act occurs at the threshold of conquest. It remains an inference; Betanzos does not call the episode *kallpa* or say that Mama Huaco reads an omen from the lungs.

### 6.3 Territorial appropriation

The weapon and the gold rods perform complementary operations:

- the corded weapon removes or terrifies prior inhabitants;
- the rods test and select fertile land;
- planting makes the claimed territory productive;
- settlement and temple building convert conquest into political and ritual order.

The bola therefore functions in the story as an instrument of **foundational sovereignty**, but possession of the physical weapon is never made a later legal requirement for Inca kingship.

### 6.4 Agricultural reenactment and Mama Huaco’s body

Brian Bauer argues that later Cuzco planting and harvest ceremonies reenacted the origin story. Molina says Sausero, where Mama Huaco planted the first maize, was venerated annually in connection with her preserved body, and maize from the field supplied chicha for its service. The cult thus attached to Mama Huaco, her body, and the first field—not demonstrably to her weapon. [Bauer, “Legitimization of the State in Inca Myth and Ritual”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/379076683_Bauer_1996_Legitimization_of_the_State_in_Inca_Myth_and_Ritual_1)

### 6.5 Symbolic association with the *mascapaicha* and *yauri*

Rebeca Carrión Cachot proposed that the form of a corded or weighted weapon associated with Mama Huaco contributed to the morphology or interpretation of Inca insignia, including the *mascapaicha* and *yauri*. This is a scholarly iconographic hypothesis, not a colonial statement that the weapon was physically converted into regalia. Her essay usefully assembles Betanzos, Sarmiento, Toledan testimony, and images, but its reconstruction should not be converted into custody history. [Carrión Cachot, “La mascapaicha”](https://revistaletras.unmsm.edu.pe/index.php/le/article/download/1718/1572)

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 Emergence from the cave

**Tier:** early attested colonial record of an Indigenous origin narrative.

The Ayar siblings emerge from one of the openings of Tambotoco/Pacaritambo already furnished with the material basis of elite life: arms, fine garments, gold objects, seeds, and domestic vessels. Weapons do not receive a manufacture story; they cross directly from the origin place into the human landscape.

The sling’s power is first expressed through Ayar Cachi. At Huanacauri, he casts stones against four hills, opening ravines. His power threatens his companions, who persuade him to return to the cave and then seal him inside. The sling’s next destination is never stated. This story explains both landscape and political succession: excessive force must be removed before Manco can lead.

### 7.2 The killing of the Gualla

**Tier:** attested by Betanzos in 1551; elaborated differently by Sarmiento in 1572.

Near Cuzco, Mama Guaco strikes and kills a local man with *ayllos*. She opens him, removes the lungs and heart, and inflates the lungs before the other inhabitants. They flee to the valley later called Gualla. The settlers then negotiate with Alcaviza and occupy the future Cuzco.

In Sarmiento, Mama Guaco kills and dismembers a Gualla, holds the heart and lungs in her mouth, and advances with the *haybinto*. The Guallas flee. She then argues that the founders should kill the remaining Guallas so their own cruelty will not become known. This harsher conclusion suits Sarmiento’s Toledan portrayal of the founders as tyrannical usurpers.

### 7.3 The Sawasiray variant

**Tier:** Indigenous testimony collected in a coercive colonial inquiry, 1572–1575.

Mama Huaco attacks Sawasiray’s people with a strap or cord bearing gold. Sawasiray, intimidated by her cruelty and prowess, flees to the wilderness. Unlike the principal chronicles, this tradition centers the defeated group’s ancestor and explains the group’s displacement.

### 7.4 The foundation rods

**Tier:** early colonial variant; subsequently conflated with the weapon.

At Matagua, Sarmiento’s Mama Guaco throws two gold rods northward. The first fails to lodge securely in poor ground at Colcabamba. The second penetrates at Guanaypata, revealing fertile soil. This is a trial of land rather than an attack.

In the now-better-known Garcilasan Titicaca story, the foundation staff belongs to Manco Cápac and sinks at the place selected for settlement. Modern school narratives often transfer that version back into the Ayar story or omit Mama Huaco. Conversely, modern rehabilitations of Mama Huaco sometimes attach every martial and foundation object to her. Both operations simplify a variable archive.

### 7.5 “Sling of Mama Huaco”

**Tier:** late conflation.

The early narratives support:

- slings among the Ayar company;
- a named sling feat by Ayar Cachi;
- a bola-like weapon used by Mama Huaco.

They do not clearly support a personally identified “Mama Huaco sling.” That phrase is best understood as a modern shorthand for her status as a female warrior or as a blending of adjacent episodes.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Physical object

**Status:** unlocated and unverified; most likely purely legendary as an individually identifiable object.

**Location:** unknown and not reconstructable.

**Condition:** unknown.

**Controller or owner:** none established.

**Legal regime:** none specific to the supposed weapon.

**Access:** not applicable.

There is no published evidence that Peru, a museum, a religious body, an ayllu, or a private owner possesses an object claimed as Mama Huaco’s original bola or sling. There is therefore no current restitution, title, export, or repatriation proceeding concerning it.

### 8.2 Manuscripts that preserve the object’s textual existence

The physical witnesses that can be located are manuscripts and editions:

- An incomplete Betanzos manuscript is preserved at the Biblioteca del Real Monasterio de San Lorenzo de El Escorial.
- A fuller Betanzos manuscript underlies modern editions, notably María del Carmen Martín Rubio’s 1987 publication.
- Sarmiento’s history survives in manuscript and printed transmission after remaining unpublished for centuries.
- Guaman Poma’s autograph *Nueva corónica* is preserved in Copenhagen by the Royal Danish Library.

These are witnesses to the legend, not relics of the weapon.

### 8.3 Sites standing in for the narrative

Puma Orco/Tambotoco, Maukallaqta, Huanacauri, Guanaypata, Sausero, and the Cuzco/Coricancha landscape materialize different stages of the origin story. Archaeological identification of a site does not authenticate every event narrated there.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Guaman Poma’s early-seventeenth-century image

Guaman Poma presents “Mama Vaco Coya” in his sequence of Inca queens. The image helped establish her as a dynastic ancestress and royal woman. It is not an eyewitness portrait and does not provide a technical depiction of the *ayllos*. Modern reproductions sometimes crop or relabel such images in ways that imply more object specificity than the manuscript possesses.

### 9.2 Colonial and republican dynastic painting

Luis Ramos Gómez analyzed a painting held by the Museo de la Universidad Nacional de San Antonio Abad del Cusco in which a powerful female figure, identified through its inscription and imagery with Mama Guaco or a related female-warrior archetype, stands over a captive or defeated person. Ramos asks whether the imagery combines Mama Guaco with Chañan Cori Coca, another celebrated warrior woman. The uncertainty shows that an image of female martial domination is not automatically a portrait of the bola episode. [Ramos Gómez bibliographic record](https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/REAA/citationstylelanguage/get/chicago-author-date?publicationId=20651&submissionId=24878)

Around 1830, Tadeo Escalante painted Mama Huaco and Mama Ocllo in the “Molino de los Incas” at Acomayo as part of a dynastic and religious mural program. Mama Huaco is shown spinning beside a vicuña and accompanied by a parasol-bearing attendant, not necessarily wielding her legendary weapon. The representation domesticates or courtly reframes the martial founder. [Garver dissertation](https://escholarship.org/content/qt73c1s9wq/qt73c1s9wq_noSplash_ab95a46d0d3555e0ac31656b60c7fb3d.pdf) [BCP cultural-heritage publication](https://www.fondoeditorialbcp.com/assets/pdf/Nuestra-Memoria-Puesta-en-Valor.pdf)

### 9.3 Twentieth-century reinterpretation

Twentieth-century Peruvian scholarship, especially work concerned with women’s authority, Andean dual organization, and the political content of origin myths, restored Mama Huaco to prominence after popular Garcilasan narratives had centered the gentler civilizing couple Manco Cápac and Mama Ocllo.

María Rostworowski emphasized:

- Mama Huaco’s military command;
- her role in choosing the settlement through the golden rods;
- her violent seizure of the Cuzco region;
- the contrast between her and the domesticated feminine model associated with Mama Ocllo.

Rebeca Carrión Cachot connected her weapon to royal insignia. Brian Bauer connected her founding actions with later maize ritual. These are important scholarly constructions built from colonial testimony, not new physical evidence.

### 9.4 Contemporary gender and political reception

Mama Huaco has become a figure through whom artists and writers explore female command, gender nonconformity, colonial suppression of Indigenous memory, and alternative genealogies of authority. *Los espejos de Mama Huaco*, for example, uses her shifting identities—warrior, free woman, sorceress, and androgynous figure—in a contemporary transdisciplinary and gender-focused project. [REDI record](https://redi.cedia.edu.ec/document/337495)

This reception often depicts her with a visually standardized multi-weight boleadora. Such images are modern reconstructions. They should not be treated as evidence for the number, material, or appearance of the legendary *ayllos*.

### 9.5 Educational and tourism narratives

Popular summaries commonly say that:

- the *haybinto* was a three-ball boleadora;
- it was made of braided leather;
- Mama Huaco caught rather than struck the Gualla;
- she subsequently used a *tumi* to open his chest;
- she carried “a sling” as her regular weapon.

Some details are plausible or pedagogically convenient, but the principal texts do not jointly attest them. In particular, Betanzos says she struck the victim with *ayllos* and opened him; he does not name a *tumi*. Sarmiento defines one stone attached to a rope. Modern staged performances and illustrations have converted the episode into a repeatable visual tableau.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Source criticism

The main evidence was written approximately two decades after the Spanish seizure of Cuzco and generations after any historical events potentially encoded in the foundation narrative. The sources drew on oral testimony, elite memories, translation, and colonial political projects.

Betanzos is often valued for his Quechua ability and close access to Inca informants. That does not make the *Suma* a transparent transcription of an unchanged pre-Conquest account. Its Spanish prose, organization, biblical or classical analogies, and the interests of Cuzco factions all mediate the narrative.

Sarmiento’s evidentiary problem is more explicit: he worked for Toledo, whose regime needed historical arguments against Inca legitimacy. His detailed account cannot simply be discarded, but its repeated vocabulary of barbarism, tyranny, cruelty, and diabolism must be recognized as framing.

The Toledan ayllu testimonies are valuable because they preserve voices of groups positioned as pre-Inca or displaced Cuzco populations. They were nevertheless produced under colonial interrogation and for a predetermined legal-political inquiry.

### 10.2 Manuscript history

The Betanzos text’s editorial history materially affects study of the weapons. The Escorial manuscript used by Jiménez de la Espada for the 1880 edition contained only the first eighteen chapters of Part I. A fuller manuscript enabled later editors to place the origin passage within a substantially more complete history. The Biblioteca Nacional del Perú’s digital exhibition documents the two manuscripts and eleven editions. [BNP exhibition](https://repositoriodigital.bnp.gob.pe/bnp/recursos/2/html/juan-de-betanzos-y-el-tahuantinsuyo/16/)

Sarmiento’s manuscript, although completed in 1572, remained unpublished for centuries. Hence its date of composition must not be confused with the date when its version became influential in printed historiography.

### 10.3 Lexical debate

The most consequential lexical issue is whether *ayllos*, *haybinto*, *liwi*, *boleadora*, and *honda* can be used interchangeably. The evidence favors restraint:

- Betanzos lists *hondas* and *ayllos* as distinct arms.
- Cieza de León describes *aillos* as weapons with which enemies could be caught.
- Sarmiento supplies a singular retained stone-and-cord definition for *haybinto*.
- Later “boleadora” is a typological gloss with strong associations with better-documented southern-cone weapons.

A German-language historical lexicon preserves Betanzos’s weapon sense of *ayllo* and quotes his passage alongside other colonial usages. [Historical lexicon preview](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9783111624631_A36622233/preview-9783111624631_A36622233.pdf)

### 10.4 Ritual interpretations

Three major interpretations coexist:

1. **Conquest memory:** the story encodes the violent displacement or absorption of earlier Cuzco populations.
2. **Ritual charter:** the killing, lung inflation, soil test, and first planting establish models repeated in royal warfare and agricultural ceremony.
3. **Political ideology:** the story naturalizes Inca claims to land by presenting victory, fertility, divine authorization, and social hierarchy as simultaneous at the beginning of time.

These interpretations are compatible but remain reconstructions. None authenticates a specific weapon.

### 10.5 Gender debate

Earlier writers sometimes treated Mama Huaco’s “manly” conduct as an anomaly requiring explanation. More recent scholarship asks whether this assumption imposes colonial or modern gender categories upon Andean structures in which paired male and female leadership could each possess martial, ritual, agricultural, and political functions.

The chronicles themselves are not neutral: Sarmiento makes female violence especially monstrous, while Garcilaso’s influential origin narrative privileges Mama Ocllo’s civilizing and domestic instruction. Modern presentations that call Mama Huaco simply “the first feminist” are similarly interpretive rather than historical descriptions.

### 10.6 Archaeology and landscape

Bauer’s fieldwork in the Pacariqtambo region argues that the origin narrative was anchored in actual monuments and landscapes. His broader Cuzco Valley project combines archaeological survey with ethnohistory to reconstruct pre-Inca and Inca settlement. [University of Texas Press, *Ancient Cuzco*](https://utpress.utexas.edu/9780292702790/)

The most cautious conclusion is that Inca elites monumentalized and ritually activated places associated with their origin account. Archaeology has not recovered a weapon with a secure inscription, context, or custody trail identifying it as Mama Huaco’s.

### 10.7 Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

The following should not be repeated as established facts:

- that an original weapon is displayed in a Peruvian museum;
- that it has been scientifically dated;
- that it consisted of exactly three stones;
- that it was certainly made from llama wool or braided leather;
- that its stones were gold;
- that Mama Huaco and Ayar Cachi used the same sling;
- that the weapon became the *mascapaicha* or *topayauri* through a documented physical conversion;
- that every representation of an armed Inca woman depicts Mama Huaco;
- that the episode is a literal eyewitness account of Cuzco’s foundation.

## 11. Chronology

| Date or period | Object history |
|---|---|
| Possible pre-Inca/Inca era | Bola-like throwing weapons and textile slings exist as Andean technologies. No object-specific evidence identifies Mama Huaco’s weapon. |
| Legendary primordial era | The Ayar siblings emerge from Pacaritambo with weapons and elite goods. In Betanzos, the men carry sinew slings in wool-and-gold bags. |
| Legendary migration | Ayar Cachi uses a sling to open four ravines in mountains. His companions seal him in the origin cave; the sling’s fate is unreported. |
| Legendary approach to Cuzco | Mama Guaco kills a local man with *ayllos*, removes his organs, and inflates his lungs; the inhabitants flee. The weapon then disappears from Betanzos’s narrative. |
| Legendary foundation, Sarmiento variant | Mama Guaco tests land by throwing two gold rods and later confronts the Guallas with a *haybinto*, one stone tied to a rope. Neither object receives a subsequent custody history. |
| c. 1535 | An early Spanish witness observes a Cuzco maize ceremony later interpreted by scholars as part of the ritual complex reenacting the founders’ first planting. No weapon is reported. |
| 1551 | Betanzos completes the *Suma*, the earliest securely dated written attestation of Mama Guaco’s *ayllos* and of the Ayar company’s sinew slings. |
| 1572 | Sarmiento completes the *Historia índica*, naming the *haybinto* and assigning the two gold foundation rods to Mama Guaco. |
| 1572–1575 | Toledan investigators collect Sawasiray and related testimony describing Mama Huaco’s violence with a *huincha* bearing a piece of gold. |
| c. 1573–1575 | Molina records annual veneration of the Sausero field and ritual service to Mama Huaco’s body; he does not report survival of her weapon. |
| c. 1615 | Guaman Poma completes the *Nueva corónica*, including “Mama Vaco Coya” in his succession of queens. |
| 1880 | Jiménez de la Espada publishes the incomplete Escorial Betanzos manuscript, bringing the *ayllos* episode into modern print. |
| Early twentieth century | Sarmiento, Molina, and other chronicles receive expanding critical and popular publication; “bola/boleadora” becomes a common gloss for the weapon. |
| c. 1830, recognized more fully in later scholarship | Tadeo Escalante depicts Mama Huaco at the Molino de los Incas in Acomayo, without providing archaeological evidence for the legendary weapon. |
| 1940s–1950s | Carrión Cachot and other Peruvian scholars develop iconographic and structural interpretations connecting Mama Huaco, the weapon, and royal insignia. |
| 1987 | Martín Rubio’s fuller Betanzos edition improves the textual basis for analysis. |
| 1990s | Rostworowski and Bauer foreground Mama Huaco’s political, gendered, agricultural, and ritual significance. |
| 2001 | Ramos Gómez publishes a major study of Mama Guaco and Chañan Cori Coca in Inca-related iconography. |
| 2007 | Guaman Poma’s manuscript is placed on UNESCO’s Memory of the World register; digital access greatly expands modern reception. |
| 2010–2011 | Major critical Spanish and English editions of Molina contextualize the origin and ritual material. |
| 2010s–2020s | Artistic, feminist, queer, educational, and heritage projects increasingly portray Mama Huaco as a warrior founder. Multi-ball visual reconstructions proliferate, although their physical details remain unattested. |
| 6 September 2026 | No authenticated physical bola, *haybinto*, *ayllos*, or sling of Mama Huaco is known. The “object” survives as a cluster of related legendary weapons in textual and visual transmission. |

### Assessment

The fullest defensible reconstruction is therefore not the biography of a surviving relic but the history of an unstable legendary assemblage. Its earliest recoverable form consists of a bola-like weapon used by Mama Guaco and separate sinew slings carried by the male Ayars. Sarmiento reshaped the first as a one-stone *haybinto* and paired it narratively with Mama Guaco’s two gold foundation rods. Subsequent scholarship and popular culture increasingly merged these objects into “Mama Huaco’s bola and sling.” No continuous physical custody chain exists beyond the moment of legendary use, and no modern object has a credible claim to identity with any of them.
