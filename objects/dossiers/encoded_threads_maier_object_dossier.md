# Laura Renee Maier's "Encoded Threads": Object Dossier

## Scope, identity, and evidentiary caution

**Research cutoff:** 6 September 2026.

**Central finding:** *Encoded Threads* is not, on the presently accessible evidence, one sacred or legendary object. It is the title of a **two-work contemporary-art series** created by American textile artist Laura Renée Maier in 2022:

1. *Encoded Threads I*
2. *Encoded Threads II*

Both are physical, thread-on-canvas works incorporating 24-karat gold leaf. Maier describes each as a textile “with blockchain encoding” and reports both as held in private collections. She presents the series conceptually through occult and folkloric imagery, but no source identifies either work as an object of religious veneration, as an ancient or traditional sacred object, or as possessing supernatural powers. “Sacred/legendary object” is therefore a classification imposed by the research prompt, not an attested historical status of the artworks.

The available record is exceptionally thin. The artist’s own website is the only located source that individually catalogues the two objects. No museum accession record, auction catalogue, blockchain transaction, NFT marketplace page, conservation report, condition report, bill of sale, collector disclosure, exhibition checklist, or catalogue raisonné entry specifically naming either *Encoded Threads I* or *II* could be located through extensive English- and Spanish-language searches. Accordingly, much of the requested chain of custody remains unreconstructable.

The dossier distinguishes the following evidence levels:

- **Documented—primary artist source:** statements and object data published by Maier.
- **Documented—independent source:** information supplied by a gallery, fair, institution, newspaper, or broadcaster.
- **Artist’s interpretation:** iconographic or conceptual claims made by Maier but not independently verified.
- **Plausible inference:** a conclusion consistent with the evidence but not directly documented.
- **Unverified:** a claim lacking the information required for independent confirmation.
- **Unknown:** no usable evidence found.

### 1.4 Construction technique

The series page itself says only “thread on canvas.” A technical account of Maier’s established process, however, was given by the artist in a May 2022 interview with Oregon Public Broadcasting. Maier said that she uses an antique Singer hand-crank sewing machine dating from the early 1920s. She turns its crank with her right hand and manipulates the cloth with her left. She described a continuous thread or line as an underlying feature of her figurative work: an initial outline establishes bodily proportions, after which repeated passages build darker, heavier areas. Repeated needle penetration changes the weave, producing distortion, texture, and dimension. [OPB interview](https://www.opb.org/article/2022/05/19/art-collaboration-on-display-in-portland-celebrates-multicultural-expression/)

Maier’s biography similarly says that she stitches expressive forms with “one continuous line of thread” using an antique hand-crank machine. [Artist’s biography](https://laurareneemaier.art/bio)

This is strong evidence for the artist’s general working method in 2022, but neither interview nor biography names *Encoded Threads I* or *II*. It is therefore a **plausible but unconfirmed inference** that both were produced by precisely this process. No work-specific progress photographs, workshop notes, or fabrication records were located.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Documentary origin

The only secure object-level date is **2022**, supplied by the artist for both works. The series statement calls *Encoded Threads* Maier’s “first body of work” to extend textile processes into blockchain technology. [Artist’s series catalogue](https://laurareneemaier.art/encoded-threads)

The place of manufacture is not recorded. Context makes Bogotá a possibility: Maier’s contemporary writings document her artistic work in Colombia, and her current biography says she lives and works in Bogotá. ### 2.2 Conceptual prehistory, 2021–22

The series emerged from an explicitly documented development in Maier’s practice.

On 1 July 2021, Maier wrote that she had begun working extensively in digital art and NFTs. She described *Desapariciones* as her first mixed-media series combining large stitched textiles and digital paintings, and announced that links to newly minted NFTs would later be added to her site. [Artist’s journal](https://laurareneemaier.art/journal)

The current *Desapariciones* page says that series was Maier’s first to explore digital painting, animated textiles, and physical textile works “encoded on the blockchain.” It lists some digital pieces as NFTs and expressly identifies OpenSea as the platform for *Desapariciones V* and *VI*. That page provides evidence that the technical vocabulary used for *Encoded Threads* grew out of experiments already under way in 2021–22. It does **not** identify an *Encoded Threads* token or show that the same platform was used. [*Desapariciones* catalogue](https://laurareneemaier.art/desapariciones)

On 21 January 2022, Maier wrote that she was continuing to explore NFTs, was developing “concrete concepts” for applying the technology to a physical-work NFT, and was preparing for a gallery exhibition of NFT works. She said the contemplated works included textiles and digital paintings that would be rendered and animated. [Artist’s journal](https://laurareneemaier.art/journal)

This dated journal entry is the earliest located documentary precursor to *Encoded Threads*. It establishes that by January 2022 the physical/digital linkage remained in development. It does not name either numbered work.

### 2.3 Earliest object-specific attestation

The earliest located public source naming *Encoded Threads I* and *II* is the artist’s undated online series page. The page dates the works but does not disclose when the record was first published. Without an archived page capture or dated catalogue, the exact publication date of the names and specifications cannot be established.

### 2.4 Purpose

Maier says the series explores the extension of textile processes into blockchain technology. Its material and digital forms are described as “dually tethered” by cryptographic proofs stitched into both the physical and the “on-chain canvas.” She connects that linkage to occult and folkloric imagery and says it creates room for a new language and mythology around an “emerging subject.” [Artist’s series catalogue](https://laurareneemaier.art/encoded-threads)

This is an **artist’s conceptual statement**, not a technical audit. Important ambiguities remain:

- “on-chain canvas” may be metaphorical.
- The source does not say whether the digital artwork itself is stored on-chain or merely referenced in token metadata.
- “Cryptographic proofs” is not defined.
- The source does not identify the chain, protocol, token standard, smart contract, transaction hash, content hash, storage system, or physical encoding method.
- It is not demonstrated that a future examiner could authenticate the physical canvas solely from the asserted proof.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Physical existence

Their existence has not, however, been independently documented through:

### 4.2 Attribution

No competing attribution, copy, forgery, or rival object has been found. Attribution rests directly on the artist’s catalogue during her lifetime. No catalogue raisonné or authentication committee is known.

### 4.3 Physical/digital identity

This is the largest unresolved authenticity problem. Maier’s statement posits a tether between material and digital forms, but the public object record omits every identifier ordinarily needed to test that assertion:

- blockchain name;
- network or chain ID;
- minting date and block;
- smart-contract address;
- token ID;
- transaction hash;
- minting wallet;
- current token-owning wallet;
- content or image hash;
- IPFS, Arweave, or other storage address;
- token standard;
- embedded physical code;
- documented verification procedure.

No marketplace page returned by searches could securely be connected to either numbered work. Maier’s *Desapariciones* page names OpenSea for two different works, but *Encoded Threads* does not. It would be improper to infer an OpenSea mint merely from her prior practice.

Consequently, the claim of blockchain encoding is **unverified in its technical particulars**. It may be true, but the public record as found is insufficient to audit it.

### 4.4 Ownership of token versus ownership of canvas

No evidence establishes whether:

- the physical canvas and digital token were sold as one package;
- the private collector possesses both;
- transfer of one legally requires transfer of the other;
- the token confers copyright, reproduction rights, access rights, or only token ownership;
- the collector’s private title is recorded on-chain;
- a token has subsequently changed wallets while the canvas remained stationary.

A blockchain record, even if located, would document custody of a token, not automatically legal ownership or physical possession of the canvas. The artist’s “dually tethered” description should not be treated as a complete chain of title.

### 4.5 Scientific and technical examination

No radiography, infrared examination, fibre analysis, adhesive analysis, pigment or gold assay, microscopy, dendrochronology, radiocarbon dating, digital-forensic audit, or cryptographic-verification report has been found. Such procedures would be unusual for works this recent, but their absence matters because the prompt specifically asks whether authentication claims have been scientifically tested.

### 4.6 Authority to authenticate

The artist is presently the sole publicly identifiable authority attaching the titles, dates, dimensions, materials, and collection statuses to these objects. Private collectors may possess invoices or certificates, and a gallery may hold records, but none has been published. No museum, foundation, estate, laboratory, or committee is documented as having authenticated them.

### 6.3 Symbolic significance of the continuous line

In her OPB interview, Maier said the continuous line in her work represents time, infinity, and the way perceptions of time pass over and build upon themselves. She described following the line as a meditative maze. [OPB interview](https://www.opb.org/article/2022/05/19/art-collaboration-on-display-in-portland-celebrates-multicultural-expression/)

This is relevant to the symbolic grammar of *Encoded Threads* if that series uses her standard continuous-thread method, but the connection is inferential rather than object-specifically documented.

### 6.4 Gold leaf

Gold leaf is listed materially, but the artist provides no explanation of its symbolic function in this series. It cannot be assigned sacred, alchemical, solar, divine, funerary, or economic meaning without evidence.

### 6.5 Blockchain as symbolic and operative structure

The blockchain element is presented both as a technical tether and as an artistic subject. In the artist’s formulation, emerging technology is built on “archaic structures and storylines” lodged in collective history and consciousness. This juxtaposition creates a symbolic genealogy for blockchain technology; it does not claim that blockchain is literally ancient or occult.

Independent sources corroborate that Maier was exhibiting textile and NFT work in Colombia around 2021–22 and that her manual sewing process was publicly described at the time. They do not independently corroborate the creation, exhibition, sale, tokenization, or present custody of the two *Encoded Threads* works.

## 12. Sources

### A. Object-specific primary source

**Laura Renée Maier, “Encoded Threads.”** This is the indispensable object record and the only located source naming and separately cataloguing *Encoded Threads I* and *II*. It supplies date, materials, dimensions, private-collection status, and the artist’s conceptual statement. Its limitations are equally significant: it gives no publication date, collector, sale record, exhibition history, token identifier, chain, contract, wallet, condition, signature, or technical explanation of the encoding.

### B. Artist’s primary contextual sources

**Laura Renée Maier, “Journal.”** Dated entries document her NFT experimentation in July 2021 and the development of physical-work NFT concepts in January 2022.

**Laura Renée Maier, “Desapariciones.”** Documents an earlier mixed physical/digital series and identifies certain other works as OpenSea NFTs. It is valuable for chronology but does not establish the platform or token identity of *Encoded Threads*.

**Laura Renée Maier, “Bio.”** Supplies her general technique and current working location.

### C. Contemporary independent and institutional sources

**Oregon Public Broadcasting, 19 May 2022.** A lengthy interview with Maier and Myriam Marcela De Anda. It provides the strongest accessible first-person technical account of Maier’s hand-crank Singer machine and continuous-thread practice.

**Arte al Día, 23 November 2021.** Contemporary Spanish-language reporting on *Hilos Silenciosos*, documenting Maier’s participation with NFT work and the Bogotá gallery context.

**BogotArt, “Dechado.”** Event record giving dates, venue, curatorial theme, and participant list.

**Casa Hoffmann, *Dechado* catalogue.** Identifies Maier’s exhibited object as *Ichpochtli*, preventing the unsupported attribution of *Encoded Threads* to that exhibition.

**Úrsula Ochoa, El Espectador, 31 May 2022.** Contemporary review of *Dechado* and its framing of embroidery between manual, social, gendered, and technological practice.

**Montenegro Art Projects, Laura Renée Maier résumé.** Lists solo and group exhibitions through 2022 but does not furnish object-specific provenance.

**Artsy, Laura Renée Maier.** Provides market context for other Maier works; no accessible entry for the two objects under study was found.

## Assessment

The fullest presently reconstructable history is short but definite at its core. Laura Renée Maier produced two textile canvases in 2022 under the collective title *Encoded Threads*. One uses black thread; the other is catalogued simply as thread. Both incorporate 24-karat gold leaf. The works were conceived at the point when Maier was extending a slow, manually powered, continuous-thread practice into digital art and NFTs. She describes the physical and digital forms as cryptographically tethered and frames their imagery as occult, folkloric, and generative of a new mythology.

After creation, each entered an unidentified private collection by an undisclosed means. Everything beyond that—place of manufacture, exhibition, buyer, price, physical itinerary, digital token identity, present location, and condition—remains undocumented in publicly accessible sources. The blockchain claim is particularly notable because the public catalogue does not expose the identifiers needed to verify it. The surviving record therefore confirms the physical identities claimed by the artist but does not yet provide the cryptographic or legal chain of custody implied by the series’ title and concept.
