# The incorrupt tongue relic of St. Anthony of Padua: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary notation

This dossier concerns the object displayed as the tongue of St. Anthony of Padua in the Pontifical Basilica of St. Anthony, Padua—not the saint’s jaw, laryngeal cartilages, habit, skeleton, or traveling *massa corporis* relics except where their histories intersect its custody.

Evidence is labeled as follows:

- **Documented:** supported by contemporary or near-contemporary documentary, liturgical, archival, or institutional evidence.
- **Scientific/medical observation:** an examiner’s reported observation, distinct from conclusions about identity or miraculous causation.
- **Institutional tradition:** the account maintained by the basilica, Franciscan custodians, or Holy See.
- **Pious interpretation:** theological meaning assigned to the object or its condition.
- **Later tradition:** materially later than the event described.
- **Unverified:** repeated in modern accounts but not established by an accessible primary or scholarly source.
- **Unresolved:** the surviving evidence does not decide the question.

A crucial distinction governs the dossier: there is abundant evidence that a dried anatomical-looking object is preserved and venerated in Padua, and strong institutional continuity from the later Middle Ages onward. There is no published radiocarbon, DNA, histological, proteomic, toxicological, or conservation-science result establishing that the displayed tissue belonged to Anthony, died in 1231, or remained naturally unaltered. “Incorrupt” is therefore retained as the relic’s historical and devotional title, not adopted here as a laboratory diagnosis.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity and names

The object is a first-class Catholic relic: an organic object presented as the preserved tongue of the Portuguese Franciscan preacher Anthony of Padua, born Fernando Martins de Bulhões or Fernando de Bulhões and called in different traditions:

- Italian: **la Lingua incorrotta di sant’Antonio di Padova**, “the incorrupt tongue of St. Anthony of Padua”; also **la Lingua benedetta**, “the blessed tongue.”
- Latin: **lingua beati Antonii**, later **lingua sancti Antonii**; medieval descriptions include *incorrupta*, “uncorrupted,” and *rubicunda et pulchra*, “ruddy/red and beautiful.”
- Portuguese: **a língua incorrupta de Santo António de Lisboa/de Pádua**.
- Spanish: **la lengua incorrupta de san Antonio de Padua**.
- English: **the incorrupt tongue**, **incorruptible tongue**, or **blessed tongue of St. Anthony**.

“Anthony of Lisbon” and “Anthony of Padua” name the same historical person. The competing geographical titles do not denote rival tongues.

The object’s name is functional and theological. *Lingua* is literal anatomy, while *incorrupta* became both a claimed physical description and a sign of Anthony’s preaching. Medieval texts compare it to the “trumpet of Christ,” the instrument of the Holy Spirit, and a bronze tent-peg of the Tabernacle. The 20th-century title *Doctor Evangelicus*, officially associated with Anthony when Pius XII declared him a Doctor of the Church in 1946, reinforced this interpretation but did not originate it.

### The organic relic

**What is publicly visible.** The relic appears as a small, dark reddish-brown to brown-black, desiccated organic body inside a transparent vessel. Its present appearance is not the fresh red appearance claimed by medieval texts. Public photographs allow only external visual observation; color is affected by glass, illumination, age, and photography.

**Published measurements.** No reliable open institutional catalogue located for this dossier gives the tongue’s own length, width, thickness, or weight. The frequently quoted measurement of **81 cm** belongs to the entire 15th-century reliquary, not the tissue. Statements online that assign that dimension to the tongue are erroneous.

**Anatomical characterization.** The custodians identify the object as a tongue. The accessible summaries of the 1981 examination discuss preserved structures of Anthony’s “vocal apparatus,” especially laryngeal cartilages, but do not publish a modern histological identification of the tongue relic itself. The tongue had long since been housed apart from the sealed tomb. Consequently, the discovery of identifiable laryngeal tissues in 1981 is not a scientific authentication of the separate tongue.

**Condition.** Medieval witnesses described it as fresh-looking, intact, pointed, red, and like that of a recently dead or living man. Today it is visibly desiccated and contracted. No accessible source establishes whether it was embalmed, chemically treated, waxed, varnished, repaired, or consolidated. No conservation report giving humidity, temperature, biological condition, or prior interventions was located.

**Inscriptions on the tissue.** None.

### The present reliquary

The present reliquary was made by the Florentine goldsmith **Giuliano di Giovanni**, conventionally called **Giuliano da Firenze** and often identified with Giuliano di Giovanni da Poggibonsi. It was commissioned in 1433, governed by a surviving replacement or renewed contract dated 27 August 1434, completed in 1436, and has contained the relic since that year.

Its documented and catalogued characteristics are:

- height: approximately **81 cm**;
- principal material: **gilded silver**;
- transparent container: a **crystal vessel**;
- base: an elaborate stepped, six-lobed foot;
- decoration: Gothic openwork, enamel rosettes, foliage in sheet metal, architectural nodes, miniature niches, statuettes of saints, turrets, flying buttress-like elements, and adoring winged angels;
- summit: a blue-enamelled, Renaissance-style domed structure with lantern;
- later addition: a small gilded figure of St. Anthony at the summit, identified by Treccani as an 18th-century addition;
- heraldry: the arms of **Antonio Ovetari** on the base, marking his assumption of the financial burden and his successful claim to commemorative patronage.

The design combines late Gothic microarchitecture with early Tuscan Renaissance forms. Scholars have variously stressed Florentine/Ghibertian, Venetian, and possible Iberian elements. The reliquary’s cupola has often been compared to Brunelleschian architecture. [Treccani’s documented catalogue account](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/giuliano-di-giovanni-da-poggibonsi_(Dizionario-Biografico)/) describes the reliquary in detail and summarizes its archival documentation.

### Related but distinct objects

These must not be conflated with the tongue:

1. **Anthony’s mandible or “chin” relic (*Mento*)**, placed in a separate bust-shaped reliquary given by Cardinal Guy/Guido de Boulogne in 1349–50. This was stolen in 1991 and recovered. The stolen object was not the tongue.
2. **Laryngeal or phonatory structures**, recognized among the tomb contents in 1981 and placed in a modern book-shaped reliquary by Carlo Balljana.
3. **The principal skeleton and ashes**, preserved in the saint’s tomb-altar.
4. **A sesamoid bone, scalp skin, and hair**, displayed in a 1982 reliquary by Roberto Cremesini.
5. **Traveling relics**, normally fragments of the *massa corporis*. Statements that “the tongue travels the world” require case-by-case verification: basilica missions usually use other relics.
6. **Contact relics**, such as cloths described as having touched the tongue. These are not fragments of the tongue.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Biological origin claimed by the custodial tradition

**Institutional tradition:** The object is the anatomical tongue of Anthony, who died on 13 June 1231 near Padua and was buried on Tuesday, 17 June, in the Franciscan church of Santa Maria Mater Domini. The tongue therefore was not “manufactured.” It became a separately curated object at the opening of the grave during the translation of 1263.

No contemporary 1231 burial record cited in the accessible literature reports special preservation, removal of the tongue, embalming, or deliberate treatment. The original burial environment has not been reconstructed in a published conservation-science study available online.

### The event of 8 April 1263

The traditional date is **8 April 1263**, the octave of Easter. Anthony’s remains were moved from Santa Maria Mater Domini into the enlarging basilica built in his honor. Bonaventure of Bagnoregio, then minister general of the Friars Minor, was present.

The standard account says:

1. the coffin or grave was opened;
2. most soft tissue had disappeared;
3. the tongue appeared intact and red;
4. Bonaventure lifted it, wept, kissed it, pronounced a blessing, enclosed it in a sealed reliquary, and ordered it placed in an honored elevated position.

The basilica’s own account dates the transfer to 8 April and places the discovery during the examination preceding deposit in a new wooden chest. [The basilica’s history of the recognitions](https://www.santantonio.org/it/content/ricognizioni) and the [English account](https://www.santantonio.org/en/content/expositions) maintain this chronology.

One modern basilica article instead says Sunday **7 April 1263**. This conflicts with the Easter calculation preserved in the critical Franciscan tradition: Easter fell on 1 April, making its octave 8 April. The 8 April date is therefore better supported, while the 7 April wording should be treated as a modern calendrical error rather than evidence for a separate ceremony.

### Earliest textual attestation

The finding is not described in Anthony’s earliest life, the *Assidua*, which predates the 1263 opening. The earliest presently identified narrative witness is the Franciscan **Legenda Benignitas**, generally placed about a decade after the event, in the 1270s. It says that Bonaventure found and reverently handled the preserved tongue. The [Associazione Universale di S. Antonio’s source summary](https://www.santodeimiracoli.org/santantonio-di-padova/la-lingua-incorrotta) identifies *Benignitas* as the earliest testimony.

A late-13th-century sermon or panegyric adds that the phenomenon was seen not only by friars but by laypeople, especially **twelve trustworthy men chosen by the Commune of Padua**, who later testified to the pope. This is a significant claim of civic verification. The accessible source does not reproduce a surviving communal act, list the twelve men, identify the pope, or provide their sworn depositions. It is therefore an early reported verification procedure, not an extant forensic dossier.

The **Raimondina**, dated about 1293, calls the tongue formerly “the trumpet of Christ” and “instrument of the Holy Spirit” and says it was found so intact and pointed that it seemed to belong to a living man.

Recent work reported by *Messaggero di Sant’Antonio* says that medievalist **Maria Teresa Dolso** identified two sermons attributed to Bonaventure, including a Berlin manuscript text describing lay witnesses and twelve reliable persons chosen by the Commune. Because the full critical edition was not accessible here, this potentially important near-contemporary evidence should be treated as a reported scholarly discovery pending examination of manuscript, attribution, and edition. [The 2026 interview describing the sermons](https://messaggerosantantonio.it/content/lantonio-dei-frati-medievali?t=1) materially changes the older assumption that all detailed testimony was substantially later.

### The *Chronica XXIV Generalium*

The best readily accessible full text is the Franciscan *Chronica XXIV Generalium Ordinis Minorum*, compiled in the later 14th century, commonly dated 1369–74 and associated with Arnald of Sarrant. It is not contemporary with 1263.

Its English translation says the tongue was “still incorrupt” and of a “beautiful reddish colour.” It reports Bonaventure’s reverent handling, tears, praise, kiss, sealing of the relic in a reliquary, and command that it occupy a place of honor. It also mistakenly says the body had lain buried for “27 years or more”; an alternative passage gives 32 years. Modern editors calculate 32 years and several months from June 1231 to April 1263. [English translation of the *Chronica XXIV Generalium*, pp. 107–08 and 224](https://franciscanstudies.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/chronica_final.pdf).

The traditional Latin acclamation is:

> *O lingua benedicta, quae Dominum semper benedixisti, et alios benedicere fecisti: nunc manifeste apparet, quanti meriti exstitisti apud Deum.*

Translation: “O blessed tongue, which always blessed the Lord and caused others to bless him: now it is plainly apparent how great your merit was before God.”

The wording exists in slightly different forms. Some versions say that the tongue “asked Him to bless others”; others that it “made others bless” the Lord. Attribution to Bonaventure is early Franciscan tradition, but no autograph transcript of the spontaneous utterance survives.

### Creation of the present material ensemble

The relic and its current container have separate origins:

- **1231:** alleged biological origin ends with Anthony’s death.
- **1263:** alleged discovery and separation as a curated relic.
- **1263–1436:** housed in at least one earlier sealed reliquary, now not securely identified.
- **1433–36:** present gilded-silver reliquary commissioned and made.

The 1434 contract required a minimum weight of **twenty marks** and completion by 15 May 1435. The project ran about a year late. Materials were obtained partly from pious donations and partly through the sale or melting of chalices belonging to the basilica. On 5–8 May 1436 a commission of goldsmiths appraised the work at **11¾ gold ducats per mark**. The precise final total requires the final weight, which the accessible summaries do not supply.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 1231: burial at Santa Maria Mater Domini

**Institutional tradition supported by early hagiography:** Anthony was buried in the small Franciscan church of Santa Maria Mater Domini on 17 June 1231. The tongue remained within the corpse. No evidence shows that it had already been removed.

Custody lay with the Franciscan community at the burial church, within Padua’s ecclesiastical jurisdiction and under the intense civic cult that followed Anthony’s canonization in 1232.

### 1231–1263: undisturbed or undocumented interval

The basilica tradition treats the burial as undisturbed until 1263. No accessible inventory describes the corpse during these 32 years. This is the first important evidentiary gap: continuity depends on the integrity of the tomb and the identification accepted by the friars and city.

### 8 April 1263: exhumation, separation, and first reliquary

**Early attested tradition:** During translation into the new basilica, Bonaventure handled the tongue and ordered it placed in a sealed, honored reliquary. From this moment the tongue ceased to share the main body’s custody container.

This act created two parallel chains:

- the principal body, re-enclosed in a wooden chest;
- the isolated tongue, displayed or secured separately.

The exact design, maker, material, and location of the first reliquary are not established in the accessible documentation. The *Chronica*’s reference to a sealed reliquary indicates curated separation but does not securely identify any surviving container.

### 1263–1433: Franciscan and basilica custody

The object remained at Anthony’s shrine in Padua. The specific rooms, altars, locks, seal-holders, and successive containers cannot be continuously reconstructed from accessible online sources.

A translation of the saint’s principal remains occurred on **14 June 1310** into a new chapel. This does not necessarily mean the tongue was inside the body’s chest; the 1263 account says it had already been separated. Accounts that imply the tongue followed every bodily translation without distinction flatten the two custody chains.

In 1349–50 Cardinal Guy de Boulogne provided a separate reliquary for the mandible. This increased the shrine’s displayed ensemble of speech-related relics but did not transfer ownership of the tongue.

### 1433–1436: commission and enclosure in the surviving reliquary

The **massari** or officials of the *Arca del Santo* commissioned Giuliano da Firenze. The original 1433 contract is lost; a renewed agreement survives from 27 August 1434. The work was complete by early May 1436.

The relic was then transferred into the crystal vessel of the new gilded-silver reliquary. [Treccani’s Giuliano biography](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/giuliano-di-giovanni-da-poggibonsi_(Dizionario-Biografico)/) dates the work and summarizes the appraisal records.

Antonio Ovetari, one of the shrine officials, assumed responsibility for the expense and obtained permission to add his arms and crest. In 1437 the Franciscan minister general confirmed his commemorative privilege in perpetuity. The surviving heraldry documents patronage, not private ownership of the relic.

### 1436–1690: treasury custody

The relic remained in the Giuliano reliquary under the guardianship of the basilica’s Franciscan community and the institutional administration of the *Arca*. Published inventories form the principal evidence for this period.

An inventory quoted in modern scholarship describes:

> *Item unum tabernaculum magnum argenteum in quo posita fuit lingua gloriosissima patris nostri Sancti Antonii, mirabiliter laboratum, oblatum per nobilem virum Ser Antonium de Ovetaris cum armis suis pede.*

In substance: a great silver tabernacle, wonderfully worked, in which the most glorious tongue of St. Anthony was placed, offered by Antonio Ovetari, with his arms on the foot.

Padua’s conquest by Venice in 1405 predates the present reliquary, though other treasury losses are associated with that event. French occupation in 1797 caused major losses to the basilica’s treasury, but no source located says the tongue or its reliquary was confiscated. Its survival should not be embellished into an undocumented rescue story.

### 1690–1745: construction of the present Treasury Chapel

The existing Chapel of the Relics or Treasury was begun from **1690–91** to a design by Genoese sculptor and architect **Filippo Parodi**. Work, furnishings, and silver-fronted niches continued for decades. The reliquaries were transferred to the completed setting in **1745**.

The chapel placed the tongue within an elaborate visual program: St. Anthony in glory above; statues of Francis, Faith, Humility, Penitence, Charity, and Bonaventure; the tongue and jaw arranged as the saint’s preaching organs. [The basilica’s chapel description](https://basilicadelsanto.it/it/content/cappella-delle-reliquie-o-del-tesoro) identifies the present arrangement; the [Diocese of Padua’s Jubilee description](https://giubileo2025.diocesipadova.it/basilica-di-santantonio-padova/) dates the relics’ transfer to 1745.

### 1745–1981: continuous display claimed, incomplete handling record

The institutional chain places the reliquary in the Treasury Chapel. Bernardo Gonzati’s 1851 catalogue, *Il Santuario delle reliquie ossia il Tesoro della Basilica di S. Antonio di Padova*, supplied an early systematic description and inventory. [Centro Studi Antoniani’s catalogue record](https://www.centrostudiantoniani.it/node/9022) summarizes its contents and the chapel’s building records.

No complete publicly accessible log was found for:

- openings of the tongue reliquary;
- replacement or repair of its seals;
- organic conservation treatments;
- wartime evacuation in 1915–18 or 1940–45;
- temporary processional removals;
- restorations of the metalwork.

Absence from online sources is not proof that such records do not exist. They may remain in the basilica, *Veneranda Arca di Sant’Antonio*, or Franciscan archives.

### 1981: examination of the main tomb

Pope John Paul II authorized a recognition for the 750th anniversary of Anthony’s death. The process began on **6 January 1981** under a religious commission and a technical-scientific commission appointed by the Holy See.

The tomb yielded:

- a large fir-wood chest wrapped in textiles;
- a smaller fir-wood chest divided into compartments;
- most of the skeleton;
- the habit;
- ashes called the *massa corporis*;
- labels and wrappings;
- laryngeal or phonatory structures later displayed separately.

The tongue itself was already in the Treasury reliquary and is not described in accessible summaries as having been sampled or subjected to laboratory analysis.

Anthony’s reconstructed remains were placed in a crystal urn and exposed from 31 January to 1 March 1981. More than 650,000 people reportedly passed before them. The crystal urn was then enclosed in an oak chest and returned to the tomb-altar. [Official account of the 1981 recognition](https://www.santantonio.org/en/content/expositions).

The published proceedings are *Ricognizione del corpo di S. Antonio di Padova: studi storici e medico-antropologici*, edited by Virgilio Meneghelli and Antonino Poppi, 1981, 328 pages according to the publisher’s record. [Centro Studi Antoniani bibliography](https://www.centrostudiantoniani.it/catalogo/centro-studi-antoniani-csa/ricognizione-del-corpo-di-s-antonio-di-padova-studi-storici-e).

### 1991 theft: a necessary correction

On 10 October 1991 masked thieves stole the **mandible/chin reliquary**, reportedly at the direction of organized criminals; it was recovered near Rome/Fiumicino roughly two months later.

The tongue was not the object stolen. Some later retellings incorrectly call the stolen item “the incorrupt tongue.” This is a documented identity confusion generated by the visual and ritual association of the two relics. Claims that the tongue was “recently taken by thieves” are false insofar as they refer to the 1991 crime.

### 21st century

The tongue remains in the 1436 reliquary in the Treasury Chapel. The basilica continues to identify it as the relic found in 1263. It is publicly viewable behind the chapel’s protective architecture and glass but not ordinarily available for handling.

On the annual Feast of the Translation, popularly called the **Feast of the Tongue**, the processional object is normally the **chin/mandible reliquary**, not necessarily the tongue itself. The basilica’s announcement for 15 February 2026 explicitly scheduled the *Mento* to be carried inside the basilica by the *Pia Unione Macellai “Militi dell’Immacolata.”* [Official 2026 feast announcement](https://www.santantonio.org/it/content/domenica-15-febbraio-festa-della-traslazione-delle-reliquie-del-santo-basilica-di).

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Questions that can be answered

**Does a physical object exist?** Yes. A visibly organic-looking object is displayed in a documented 1436 reliquary at the Padua basilica.

**Was a tongue relic associated with Anthony by the later 13th century?** Yes. *Benignitas*, the *Raimondina*, late-century sermons, and later Franciscan chronicles attest the tradition. The earliest identified text is approximately a decade after the 1263 event, not centuries later.

**Has the present reliquary continuously contained a purported tongue since 1436?** The evidence is strong at the institutional and inventory level, though no complete seal-by-seal conservation history is publicly available.

### Questions that remain open

**Is the tissue human tongue?** Public-facing sources identify it anatomically, but no accessible modern histological, microscopic, or molecular report was located.

**Is it Anthony’s tissue?** No published genetic comparison or uniquely identifying test has established this. Even a human DNA result would require authenticated comparative material and a defensible chain for the samples.

**Does it date to the 13th century?** No published radiocarbon result was located.

**Was its preservation miraculous?** This is a theological interpretation beyond the competence of historical or natural-scientific demonstration. The basilica explicitly presents the preservation as inexplicable to science and accepted by faith. That statement is institutional and devotional, not the conclusion of a published experimental study. [Basilica’s 2026 statement](https://www.santantonio.org/en/content/feast-translation-saints-relics-sunday-15-february-we-celebrate-discovery-saint-anthonys).

**Was it artificially preserved?** No accessible evidence proves embalming or chemical treatment; equally, no analysis excludes desiccation, environmental preservation, historic treatment, replacement, or other non-miraculous processes.

### Continuity problems

The most vulnerable interval is **1263–1436**. The medieval sources say Bonaventure sealed the tongue in a reliquary, but the present container was made approximately 173 years later. The transfer into Giuliano’s crystal vessel is historically credible and institutionally documented, yet a complete physical chain through the earlier container has not been published online.

A second problem is the changing description. Medieval texts call the tongue red, beautiful, fresh, and lifelike; the present object is dark and desiccated. That change is compatible with long-term drying and oxidation, but without serial examinations or conservation records its course cannot be reconstructed.

### The 1981 examination and its limits

The 1981 recognition is often invoked online as “scientific proof” of the tongue. That overstates the published evidence.

The examination established or reported important information about the tomb ensemble, skeleton, textiles, ashes, and preserved laryngeal structures. It allowed an anthropological reconstruction of Anthony’s general physical characteristics. It did not, in the accessible accounts, establish by DNA or radiocarbon dating that the separate tongue belonged to the skeleton.

The finding of preserved laryngeal cartilages is striking but distinct:

- cartilage can survive differently from muscular tongue tissue;
- anatomical compatibility is not individual identification;
- symbolic congruence with Anthony’s preaching is a theological interpretation.

### Rival claimants and duplicates

No serious rival institution claiming possession of Anthony’s complete tongue was identified. Numerous churches possess fragments attributed to Anthony, but the Padua tongue’s singular identity is not presently the subject of a prominent competing-claimant dispute.

Popular confusion arises from:

- “Anthony of Lisbon” versus “Anthony of Padua,” who are the same saint;
- other saints named Anthony;
- photographs of the jaw mislabeled as the tongue;
- contact relics described imprecisely as pieces of the tongue;
- the 1991 theft of the jaw retold as theft of the tongue.

### Authorities to authenticate

Authority has shifted and overlapped:

- **1263:** Franciscan minister general Bonaventure, local friars, alleged communal witnesses, and possibly papal testimony.
- **Late Middle Ages:** the Franciscan Order, basilica custodians, Commune of Padua, and *Arca del Santo*.
- **Modern era:** the Holy See and the Pontifical Delegation, assisted by medical and university specialists.
- **Present canon-law framework:** ecclesiastical recognition establishes authorized cult and custody; it is not identical to laboratory proof of biological identity.

The Holy See’s 2017 instruction requires careful verification where authentic documents or seals are absent or compromised, prohibits sale and profane display, and regulates recognition, sampling, conservation, and transfer. [Instruction, “Relics in the Church: Authenticity and Conservation”](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/csaints/documents/rc_con_csaints_doc_20171208_istruzione-reliquie_en.html).

No evidence was found that the Padua relic has “failed” an authenticity test. More precisely, no decisive modern identity test appears to have been published.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Anthony of Padua / Lisbon

**Connection:** alleged source of the tissue; Franciscan preacher whose speech supplied the relic’s meaning.

**Dates:** c. 1195–1231; buried 17 June 1231; canonized 1232.

**Evidence:** early lives, sermons, canonization tradition, and the shrine’s continuous cult. His personal sermons survive, but they do not mention the posthumous relic.

### Bonaventure of Bagnoregio

**Connection:** alleged discoverer, handler, first ecclesiastical interpreter, and person who ordered the relic sealed and honored.

**Date:** 8 April 1263.

**Evidence:** *Benignitas*, later sermons, *Raimondina*, *Chronica XXIV Generalium*, and subsequent liturgy. His precise exclamation survives through reported tradition, not autograph minutes.

### The Friars Minor

**Connection:** principal custodial network from Anthony’s burial to the present; creators and transmitters of the written tradition; commissioners or institutional participants in reliquaries, ceremonies, inventories, and recognitions.

**Evidence:** Franciscan hagiography, basilica records, papal documents, and continuing custody.

### Commune of Padua

**Connection:** civic participant in the shrine and, according to a late-13th-century sermon, elector of twelve trustworthy lay witnesses to the 1263 recognition.

**Evidence:** early sermon tradition as summarized by modern scholars. The individual witnesses and original act remain unidentified in accessible sources.

### The *Veneranda Arca di Sant’Antonio*

**Connection:** historic administrative and financial body managing the shrine, commissions, offerings, and artistic works; its officials commissioned the present reliquary.

**Dates:** especially documented in the 15th-century contracts and accounts.

### Giuliano di Giovanni da Firenze/Poggibonsi

**Connection:** maker of the surviving reliquary.

**Dates:** commission 1433; renewed contract 1434; completion and appraisal 1436.

**Evidence:** contracts, payments, appraisal records, stylistic analysis, and the surviving object. This reliquary is his only securely documented surviving autograph work according to Treccani.

### Antonio Ovetari

**Connection:** shrine official and patron who assumed the financial liability for the reliquary and placed his arms on it.

**Dates:** 1436–37.

**Evidence:** accounting and privilege records; heraldry on the base. He did not privately acquire the tongue.

### Five goldsmith appraisers

**Connection:** professional valuation of the completed reliquary.

**Date:** 8 May 1436.

**Evidence:** documentary record summarized by Treccani. The work was valued unanimously at 11¾ ducats per mark.

### Filippo Parodi

**Connection:** designer of the Baroque Treasury Chapel that became the tongue’s permanent architectural setting.

**Dates:** design submitted in 1690; construction and furnishing extended into the 18th century.

### John XXIII

**Connection:** papal commemoration of the seventh centenary of the translation.

**Date:** 16 January 1963 letter.

The pope repeated the annalistic tradition that the tongue was found ruddy and like that of a recently living person, quoting Bonaventure’s acclamation and explicitly interpreting preservation as appropriate to an instrument of the Holy Spirit. [*Acta Apostolicae Sedis* 55 (1963), pp. 159–61](https://www.vatican.va/archive/aas/documents/AAS-55-1963-ocr.pdf).

### John Paul II and the 1981 commissions

**Connection:** papal authorization and ecclesiastical supervision of the modern recognition.

**Date:** decree dated 1 January 1981; tomb opened 6 January.

The commission included Cardinal Pietro Palazzini and senior ecclesiastical officers; specialists from Padua and other universities performed historical, anatomical, medical, and anthropological work. Antonino Poppi served as secretary-notary. [Contemporary retrospective on the recognition](https://www.messengersaintanthony.com/node/1744).

### Carlo Balljana

**Connection:** maker of the modern reliquary for the laryngeal/phonatory remains discovered in 1981.

**Importance:** his work visually extends the medieval theology of the tongue into a modern ensemble, but it does not contain the tongue itself.

### Divine beings

- **God:** medieval and modern Catholic interpretation holds that God preserved the tongue as testimony to Anthony’s preaching.
- **Christ:** medieval texts call the tongue Christ’s trumpet and understand Anthony’s preaching as proclamation of Christ.
- **Holy Spirit:** the tongue is described as the Spirit’s instrument; Pentecostal language underlies accounts of Anthony being understood by multilingual audiences.

These are theological relations asserted by the sources, not historical agents susceptible to material verification.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Display and veneration

The relic is displayed as an object of veneration, not as an ordinary anatomical specimen. Its transparent vessel makes visibility central: devotees see the claimed survival of the preacher’s organ.

Catholic doctrine distinguishes veneration offered to a saint from worship owed to God. The object supplies a material connection to the saint. Its canonical status restricts sale, unauthorized alienation, sampling, and profane display.

### Feast of the Translation / Feast of the Tongue

The annual feast is observed on a February Sunday around **15 February**, rather than on the actual 8 April anniversary. It is popularly called the **Festa della Lingua** and is described by the basilica as Anthony’s most important local feast after 13 June.

The observance includes Mass, preaching, veneration, and an internal procession. In current practice the mandible reliquary is carried, even though the feast commemorates the discovery of the tongue. In 2026 it was carried by members of the butchers’ pious union, the *Militi dell’Immacolata*. This division protects the more fragile tongue while allowing a major Anthony relic to function processionally.

### Bonaventure’s founding gesture

The *Chronica*’s sequence—lifting, weeping, praising, kissing, sealing, elevating—constitutes the first reported ritual use of the separated object. It converted an anatomical remainder into a liturgically legible relic.

### Symbolic function

Its principal symbolism is unusually specific:

- tongue as organ of preaching;
- preservation as divine ratification of truthful speech;
- red/fresh appearance as sign of continuing efficacy;
- elevation in a transparent architectural reliquary as visual continuation of preaching;
- association with jaw and larynx as a complete “apparatus of the Word.”

The relic helped stabilize Anthony’s image as preacher at a time when his cultic identity was less visually fixed than that of Francis of Assisi. Eszter Konrád’s research argues that the relic’s discovery was instrumental in constructing and transmitting Anthony’s preaching-centered memory. [Konrád dissertation](https://www.etd.ceu.edu/2018/konrad_eszter.pdf).

### Miracles and healings

No well-documented healing uniquely caused by physical contact with the tongue was identified in the sources consulted. Anthony’s shrine accumulated extensive miracle traditions, but these normally attach to the saint, his tomb, image, bread, intercession, or relics collectively.

Modern descriptions sometimes imply the tongue itself heals, protects speech, or cures throat disorders. Such claims should be treated as devotional extensions unless a dated testimony names the tongue as the operative object.

### Access rules

Public visitors may view the relic in the Treasury Chapel. Direct touching is restricted to authorized custodians and ecclesiastical handling. Sampling would require competent church authorization under current Vatican rules. The interior of the vessel and its seals are not available for ordinary public inspection.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The incorruption miracle

**Tier:** early attested pious interpretation.

The core story appears within roughly a decade of the event: thirty-two years after burial, the corpse had decomposed but the tongue remained intact. Its survival was interpreted as God’s approval of Anthony’s preaching.

The early sources do not supply:

- an account of soil chemistry;
- a physician’s examination;
- exact measurements;
- a treatment history;
- an uninterrupted written seal record;
- an explanation of why a wet muscular organ survived while other tissue did not.

Later retellings frequently strengthen “intact” into “perfectly fresh,” “still flexible,” or “unchanged to this day.” Only the medieval descriptors “red,” “beautiful,” “intact,” “pointed,” and “like a living/recently dead man’s” are traceable to early textual tradition.

### Bonaventure’s acclamation

**Tier:** early tradition with variant wording.

Bonaventure is said to have recognized the tongue’s preservation as proportional to its merit. The speech’s literary polish and variant forms suggest transmission through hagiographic and liturgical redaction. Its historical core—an act of praise by the minister general—is plausible and early attested; verbatim recovery is impossible.

### Twelve civic witnesses

**Tier:** late-13th-century attested claim, documentary corroboration unresolved.

A sermon says twelve trustworthy laymen chosen by Padua’s Commune witnessed the discovery and testified to the pope. The number twelve also carries obvious apostolic symbolism, but that does not disprove civic participation. Until a communal act or papal dossier is identified, the claim remains early testimony rather than independently verified procedure.

### “The tongue was the only surviving part”

**Tier:** oversimplified later retelling.

Bone survived, and the 1981 investigators reported other preserved anatomical material, including laryngeal structures. The story properly concerns unusual soft-tissue preservation, not literal survival of nothing else.

### “The tongue and vocal cords were found together in 1263”

**Tier:** modern conflation.

The medieval account foregrounds the tongue. The laryngeal cartilages or other phonatory remains were recognized among the tomb contents in 1981. Statements that all were selected and displayed at the original burial are unsupported.

### “The tongue was stolen in 1991”

**Tier:** demonstrably erroneous modern legend.

The stolen and recovered relic was the mandible/chin reliquary. Repetition of “stolen tongue” appears to arise from imprecise journalism and the ritual pairing of the objects.

### Speech, Pentecost, and the devil attacking Anthony’s throat

**Tier:** medieval hagiographic network.

Anthony’s eloquence generated stories that multilingual crowds understood him and that the devil attempted to strangle him because his preaching was dangerous. These stories predate or developed alongside the tongue cult and gave its preservation a narrative logic. They do not provide material evidence about the relic.

## 8. Current Status and Location

As of September 2026, the object is publicly displayed in:

**Cappella delle Reliquie / Cappella del Tesoro**  
Pontificia Basilica di Sant’Antonio di Padova  
Piazza del Santo  
Padua, Veneto, Italy

Within the chapel’s central arrangement, the tongue occupies Giuliano da Firenze’s gilded-silver and crystal reliquary. The jaw is displayed above it; the modern laryngeal reliquary is below. [Official chapel guide](https://basilicadelsanto.it/it/content/cappella-delle-reliquie-o-del-tesoro).

### Custodian and legal regime

The basilica is a pontifical shrine entrusted to the Friars Minor Conventual, with the involvement of the Pontifical Delegation and historic shrine administration. The relic is ecclesiastical property under Catholic canon-law restrictions and Italian cultural-property law insofar as its reliquary and historic ensemble qualify as cultural heritage.

No published museum-style accession number was located. It is catalogued through basilica treasury inventories and scholarly object catalogues rather than a conventional museum accession system.

### Condition and conservation

Publicly visible condition: desiccated, darkened organic object; reliquary apparently intact and in display condition.

Unpublished or not located:

- conservation treatment record for the tissue;
- environmental specifications;
- recent microscopy or chemical study;
- radiocarbon date;
- DNA result;
- CT or medical imaging;
- an exact physical measurement sheet;
- complete seal history.

### Dispute or repatriation claim

No active ownership, restitution, or Portuguese repatriation proceeding was identified. Portugal commemorates Anthony as Anthony of Lisbon, but the tongue’s long Padua custody does not appear to be the subject of a formal interstate claim.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Art and visual culture

The discovery became an iconographic episode. A notable representation is **Ferdinando Suman’s 1847 painting, “St. Bonaventure Finds the Tongue of St. Anthony,”** in the basilica sacristy. It visualizes an event whose early sources supply little spatial detail.

Photographs of the tongue and jaw circulate widely online. A recurrent modern problem is caption confusion: images of the jaw reliquary are labeled “the incorrupt tongue,” while composite photographs are treated as a single object.

### Papal reception

John XXIII’s 1963 letter for the seventh centenary elevated the relic’s traditional meaning in official papal discourse. He repeated the red, lifelike description, quoted Bonaventure, and called the tongue an instrument of the Holy Spirit.

The 1981 recognition authorized by John Paul II generated international media attention, new displays, anthropological reconstruction, and renewed pilgrimage.

### Tourism and pilgrimage

The Treasury Chapel is one of the basilica’s principal visitor destinations. The relic’s visibility is a central component of the shrine economy, though no reliable source isolated revenues attributable specifically to it.

The object functions simultaneously as:

- devotional relic;
- historical curiosity;
- masterpiece of goldsmith work;
- destination within Padua’s religious tourism;
- visual emblem of Anthony as preacher.

### Replicas and contact relics

Commercial medals, cards, photographs, and cloth contact relics circulate. A cloth labeled as touched to the tongue is a contact or third-class devotional object, not bodily material. No comprehensive authorized-replica register was found.

### Popular skepticism

Modern debate commonly asks whether the object is genuine or scientifically inexplicable. Three recurrent errors shape the debate:

1. assuming ecclesiastical recognition equals DNA authentication;
2. assuming lack of a natural explanation proves miracle;
3. assuming a general description of the 1981 examination means the tongue itself was laboratory-tested.

A responsible account can affirm the physical object and early cult while leaving biological identity and preservation mechanism unresolved.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Medieval sources

1. **Legenda Assidua:** earliest life of Anthony; predates the 1263 discovery and therefore cannot attest it.
2. **Legenda Benignitas:** approximately 1270s; earliest presently identified narrative testimony.
3. **Late-13th-century Franciscan sermon/panegyric:** supplies the claim of twelve civic witnesses.
4. **Raimondina:** c. 1293; develops the tongue’s theological metaphors.
5. **Chronica XXIV Generalium:** c. 1369–74; fullest readily available medieval narrative, but over a century after the event and internally inconsistent about years of burial.
6. **Additions to the Anthony vitae and later breviaries:** incorporated the discovery into liturgical memory.

The chief historiographical issue is not whether the tradition is merely modern—it demonstrably is not—but how close the extant textual forms stand to 1263 eyewitness testimony.

### Reliquary scholarship

Major landmarks include:

- Bernardo Gonzati, *Il Santuario delle reliquie ossia il Tesoro della Basilica di S. Antonio di Padova* (1851).
- Antonio Sartori, “Il reliquiario della lingua di s. Antonio di Giuliano da Firenze,” *Rivista d’arte* 34 (1959), pp. 123–149.
- Sartori, “I reliquiari della lingua di s. Antonio,” *Il Santo* 3 (1963), pp. 31–60.
- Giuseppe Fiocco, “Il reliquiario della lingua del santo,” *Il Santo* 3 (1963).
- L. Montobbio, study of Giuliano’s Paduan activity (1970–71).
- *La basilica del Santo. Le oreficerie* (1995), especially the catalogue entry by A. M. Spiazzi.
- Francesco Lucchini’s work on relic circulation and the economic/accounting structure of the commission.

Sartori’s archival discoveries displaced older anonymous or stylistic attributions by documenting Giuliano’s authorship. Debate continues over whether Giuliano da Firenze should be identified with Giuliano di Giovanni da Poggibonsi, a collaborator associated with Ghiberti’s Florentine milieu. The reliquary itself is secure; the maker’s fuller biographical identity is less so.

### Medical and anthropological scholarship

The central publication is:

Virgilio Meneghelli and Antonino Poppi, eds., *Ricognizione del corpo di S. Antonio di Padova: studi storici e medico-antropologici* (Padua: Edizioni Messaggero, 1981).

Its importance lies in systematic study of the tomb ensemble. Popular summaries often extract only the spectacular claim about the “vocal apparatus.” A full evaluation requires the volume’s individual specialist chapters, methods, photographs, and specimen descriptions.

No peer-reviewed modern paper was located reporting destructive or molecular testing of the tongue. “Science cannot explain it” is therefore too broad unless limited to the custodians’ assertion that no accepted preservation history has been supplied.

### Source losses and gaps

Known gaps include:

- original 1433 reliquary contract lost;
- no accessible original 1263 protocol;
- no published list of the twelve communal witnesses;
- no accessible complete series of reliquary-opening records;
- no public scientific dossier focused solely on the tongue;
- no online critical edition consulted here for the complete *Benignitas* and *Raimondina* texts;
- incomplete documentation of wartime movements and metalwork restoration.

### Discredited or unsupported popular theories

- **The tongue was stolen in 1991:** wrong object; it was the jaw.
- **The 1981 commission DNA-tested the tongue:** no supporting publication located.
- **The tongue is still pink and physically unchanged since 1263:** contradicted by its present desiccated appearance and unsupported by serial measurements.
- **The tongue is 81 cm long:** confusion with reliquary height.
- **The vocal cords were displayed from 1263:** conflation with the 1981 recognition.
- **A fresh corpse was found except for the rest being dust:** rhetorical compression of multiple medieval descriptions, not a clinical autopsy report.
