# Poseidon's Trident: Object Dossier

**Requested filename:** `poseidons_trident_object_dossier.md`  
**Research cutoff:** 6 September 2026

## Evidentiary note

Poseidon’s Trident is not a surviving archaeological artifact with a demonstrable material history. No ancient trident, fragment, treasury object, excavation find, or museum accession has a defensible claim to be the individual weapon described in Greek and Roman myth. Its reconstructable “life” therefore consists of:

1. the history of a **mythic object in texts**;
2. the history of its **visual form in ancient art**;
3. physical places and monuments said to preserve the effects of its blows;
4. later representations and symbols derived from it; and
5. modern fictional objects that reuse its identity.

Statements below are consequently labeled, where necessary, as **primary-text attestation**, **material evidence**, **scholarly inference**, **ancient local tradition**, **later elaboration**, or **modern invention**. Mythic chronology is not historical chronology: the dossier orders events first by the dates of the sources attesting them and only secondarily by their position within the stories.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity and class

Poseidon’s Trident is the three-pronged implement conventionally carried by Poseidon, Greek god of the sea, earthquakes, horses, and springs, and by his Roman counterpart Neptune. In ancient literature it functions variously as:

- a divine weapon;
- an instrument for moving, splitting, or striking rock;
- an implement for agitating or governing the sea;
- an earthquake-producing power;
- a badge identifying Poseidon;
- a metonym for Poseidon’s sovereignty and cult;
- and, in later visual and political traditions, a general emblem of maritime dominion.

It is not consistently treated as a separately named personality-bearing artifact. Unlike “Excalibur,” for example, it has no stable proper name independent of its owner. “Poseidon’s Trident” is principally a descriptive modern title.

### Names and terminology

**Ancient Greek:** τρίαινα, *tríaina* (also transliterated *triaina*), “trident; three-pronged fish-spear.” The standard Greek lexicon records it as both an ordinary three-pronged fishing implement and Poseidon’s badge, citing Homer and later authors. The word is related to the idea of “three,” but the precise prehistoric derivation and formation are not completely transparent; claims that it derives straightforwardly from an unattested form meaning “three-times” should be treated cautiously. See the [LSJ entry for τρίαινα](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/dictionaries/entry/urn%3Acite2%3Ascaife-viewer%3Adictionaries.v1%3Alsj-n104724/).

Relevant divine epithets include:

- **εὐτρίαινα**, *eutriaina*, “of the goodly/fine trident”;
- **χρυσoτρίαινα**, *chrysotriaina*, “golden-tridented” or “of the golden trident”;
- **ὀρσοτρίαινα**, *orsotriaina*, interpreted variously as “trident-raising,” “upright-tridented,” or a dialectal form connected with holding the weapon erect. The linguistic analysis remains disputed; García Ramón discusses competing constructions and the iconographic significance of an upright trident.

**Latin:** *tridens*, literally “three-toothed,” from *tri-* and *dens* (“tooth”); as a noun it means “trident,” and poetically “Neptune’s trident.” Latin authors also use *tridentifer*, “trident-bearing.”

**English and later European languages:** English *trident*, French *trident*, Italian *tridente*, Spanish *tridente*, German *Dreizack*, and related forms ordinarily denote the implement class, not exclusively Poseidon’s individual weapon.

### Physical description supplied by ancient sources

No ancient literary text gives the object a complete technical description, dimensions, weight, shaft wood, metal composition, inscriptions, maker’s marks, or decorative program.

What is actually attested is limited:

- It has three points, inherent in its name.
- It is sufficiently long to be held in one or both hands.
- Homer depicts Poseidon holding it while directing waters and grasping it with both hands while stirring the sea.
- Greek and Latin poetic epithets sometimes make it **golden**. “Golden,” however, is conventional divine-poetic language and is not a metallurgical report.
- It can strike or split stone, move foundations, raise waves, open or close springs, and cause seismic destruction.
- Ancient art normally gives it a long shaft with a compact three-pronged head. Some coin types render the prongs as barbed, strengthening the interpretation of its form as derived from a fish spear.

The implement’s depiction is not uniform. Its outer prongs can curve inward or outward; the central tine may be longer; barbs may appear; and the shaft may terminate below in a point, ferrule, or decorative end. These are artistic variants, not evidence for alterations to one physical weapon.

### Materials and construction

**Mythic evidence:** Pseudo-Apollodorus says that the Cyclopes “bestowed” the trident upon Poseidon but does not specify its material, fabrication process, dimensions, or individual maker. The familiar statement that it was “forged in the fires beneath the earth” is a modern synthesis inferred from the Cyclopes’ later reputation as smiths; it is not what the relevant passage says. The actual text only describes the gift and its use in the divine war ([*Library* 1.2.1](https://www.theoi.com/Text/Apollodorus1.html)).

**Alternative tradition:** Later sources and scholia associate the Telchines of Rhodes with making Poseidon’s trident. Surviving summaries frequently overstate this as a well-attested early account. Diodorus and Strabo establish the Telchines’ metallurgical reputation, but the explicit attribution of the trident’s manufacture belongs principally to later mythographic or scholastic tradition. It should not be silently merged with Pseudo-Apollodorus’s Cyclopean gift.

**Material conclusion:** Its composition cannot be established. “Gold,” bronze, iron, and divine or indestructible metal are literary or modern visual choices, not scientifically observed properties.

### Inscriptions, damage, repairs, and condition

No source reports an inscription on the mythic weapon. No ancient account securely describes it as broken, repaired, reforged, restored, or replaced. No authenticated fragment survives. Stories and modern franchises in which the trident is shattered, stolen, reconstructed, or made from a named imaginary metal are independent modern creations.

### Copies, representations, and alleged physical traces

This dossier tracks the **singular mythic weapon**, while distinguishing it from:

1. ancient images of Poseidon’s trident on pottery, sculpture, reliefs, mosaics, and coins;
2. the alleged “trident marks” in Athenian bedrock;
3. sculptural tridents held by statues of Neptune;
4. heraldic and commercial emblems;
5. modern replica weapons and fictional “Tridents of Poseidon/Neptune.”

None is the mythic original.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Earliest textual attestation

The oldest securely surviving literary appearance is in the Homeric epics, conventionally dated to the late eighth or early seventh century BCE, though their oral formation and textual stabilization were prolonged.

In the *Iliad* 12.17–33, Poseidon carries the trident while he and Apollo destroy the unauthorized Achaean defensive wall at Troy. Apollo concentrates the rivers; Zeus supplies continuous rain; Poseidon, “bearing his trident in his hands,” leads the work, sweeps the timber-and-stone foundations into the sea, sands over the beach, and restores the rivers to their former courses. The trident is present and operationally associated with his command of destructive water and terrain, although the grammar does not require every effect to be produced by a separate blow of the weapon ([*Iliad* 12](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HomerIliad12.html)).

In the *Odyssey* 5.291–296, Poseidon takes the trident in both hands, gathers clouds, churns the sea, releases winds from every direction, and darkens sea and land while attacking Odysseus’s raft. This is the earliest extended description of the trident as a storm-making instrument ([Dickinson College Commentary on *Odyssey* 5](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/homer-odyssey/intro/essays-book-5)).

In *Odyssey* 4.499–511, Poseidon strikes the Gyraean rock on which Ajax the Lesser has survived shipwreck. The rock splits; the portion Ajax grips falls into the sea; and he drowns. This establishes the weapon’s rock-breaking and punitive function.

These Homeric passages do **not** say:

- who made the trident;
- when Poseidon acquired it;
- what it was made of;
- that it was the unique source of all Poseidon’s powers;
- that another person could acquire his powers by possessing it;
- or that it could be stolen from him.

### Earliest visual evidence

Identifying an absolute “first image” is difficult because early representations of bearded male gods can be ambiguous unless accompanied by an attribute or inscription. Secure archaic images occur on sixth-century BCE painted pottery, where a trident identifies Poseidon in divine assemblies and in the Gigantomachy.

Examples include:

- black-figure scenes of Heracles wrestling Triton, attended by Poseidon with a trident;
- scenes of Poseidon attacking the Giant Polybotes;
- images of Poseidon holding both trident and fish;
- and coins of Poseidonia in southern Italy showing the god advancing or striking with a trident.

The Metropolitan Museum’s Corpus Vasorum publication describes an Attic black-figure neck-amphora on which Poseidon drives the trident into Polybotes’ chest while preparing to crush him with a rock ([Metropolitan Museum CVA](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/Attic_Black_Figured_Neck_Amphorae_Corpus_Vasorum_Antiquorum_Fascicule_4.pdf)). Such images demonstrate that by the sixth century BCE the implement was a firmly conventional identifying attribute and combat weapon.

A British Museum red-figure neck-amphora shows Poseidon standing with a fish in one hand and resting the other on the trident; its catalogue records Attic manufacture and its later Blacas provenance ([British Museum 1867,0508.1121](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/G_1867-0508-1121)). These images are representations, not relics of the weapon.

### Growth of the manufacture story

The origin narrative is much later than Homer.

**Pseudo-Apollodorus, probably first or second century CE:** After Zeus frees the Cyclopes from Tartarus, they give Zeus thunder, lightning, and a thunderbolt; Hades receives a helmet; Poseidon receives a trident. Armed with these gifts, the gods defeat the Titans. The object is therefore inserted into a coordinated set of sovereignty-conferring divine equipment. This is the clearest surviving ancient origin account ([*Library* 1.2.1](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0022%3Atext%3DLibrary%3Abook%3D1)).

The text says “gave,” not explicitly “forged.” Later retellings convert the Cyclopes from donors into named manufacturers—usually Brontes, Steropes, and Arges—and describe subterranean fabrication. That expansion is plausible within Greco-Roman conceptions of Cyclopean smithcraft but must be identified as an elaboration.

**Telchine variant:** A separate tradition credited the metalworking Telchines with the trident. It is associated especially with Rhodes and later learned mythography. Because the Cyclopes and Telchines were both superhuman artisan groups and were sometimes compared or confused, the traditions may reflect competing mythographic assignments rather than two documented stages in one manufacture.

### Relation to ordinary fishing technology

The Greek *triaina* was also a practical three-pronged fish spear. Ancient artistic versions sometimes show barbs arranged as on real fishing implements. This supports the conventional interpretation that Poseidon’s emblem was intelligible as a sea god’s fish spear.

An influential contrary theory, argued by Christian Blinkenberg in 1911, proposed that the trident descended from a three-pronged lightning symbol and only secondarily became understood as a fish spear. He compared Carian and Anatolian thunder weapons and noted the trident’s earth-shaking use. The theory is historically important but remains an inference, not an established genealogy of the object ([Blinkenberg, *The Thunderweapon in Religion and Folklore*](https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/blinkenberg1911/0067)).

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because no physical object exists, “custody” here means custody within each textual tradition.

### Mythic sequence reconstructed from the sources

#### 1. Manufacture or possession before the Titanomachy

No archaic source provides this link. The Homeric poems assume that Poseidon possesses the trident but do not narrate acquisition.

Pseudo-Apollodorus supplies a retrospective sequence:

- **Donors:** the Cyclopes.
- **Recipient:** Poseidon.
- **Mechanism:** gift in gratitude for liberation from Tartarus.
- **Context:** preparation for or turning point in the Titanomachy.
- **Evidence:** *Library* 1.2.1.
- **Tier:** late mythographic account, not archaic documentation.

Later retellings replace “gift” with “forging and gift” and identify the Cyclopes individually. The Telchine tradition substitutes different makers. No ancient source preserves a sale, commission, price, mine, workshop location, or written contract.

#### 2. Titanomachy

Pseudo-Apollodorus states that the Olympians defeated the Titans while armed with the Cyclopes’ gifts. It does not assign a specific named Titan whom Poseidon kills with the trident or describe a blow. Custody remains with Poseidon.

#### 3. Allocation of the cosmos

After victory, Zeus, Poseidon, and Hades cast lots for sky, sea, and underworld. The trident is not itself allotted; Poseidon already possesses it. Virgil later makes the weapon emblematic of the marine allotment: Neptune tells the winds that the sea and the “dread trident” were given to him, not to Aeolus ([Virgil, *Aeneid* 1](https://www.theoi.com/Text/VirgilAeneid1.html)).

#### 4. Destruction of the Achaean wall at Troy

In Homeric narrative time, after Troy falls and the Greeks depart, Poseidon carries the trident while directing the demolition and erasure of their wall. The object travels to the Troad/Hellespont in the story. No subsequent physical remains are identified as its trace.

#### 5. Punishment of Ajax the Lesser

At the Gyraean rocks, Poseidon strikes and splits the rock beneath Ajax. Custody never changes. Later art and commentaries elaborate the scene, but no ancient tradition places the trident in Ajax’s hands.

#### 6. Storm against Odysseus

Poseidon carries the trident while traveling from the Ethiopians, sees Odysseus from the Solymian mountains, and uses it with both hands to agitate sea and winds. The story’s geography is mythopoetic rather than a recoverable itinerary.

#### 7. Contest with Athena for Attica

Pseudo-Apollodorus 3.14.1 narrates that Poseidon arrives first in Attica, strikes the middle of the Acropolis with his trident, and produces a sea called the Erechtheis; Athena plants an olive. Cecrops testifies, and the gods award the territory to Athena. The trident does not pass to Athena, Cecrops, or the city. Poseidon retains it.

The alleged impact was localized in Athens and treated as a visible sacred token. By the Hellenistic period Hegesias could evoke “the mark of the huge trident” as a celebrated Acropolis sight, quoted by Strabo at 9.1.16 ([Strabo 9.1.16](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Strabo/9A%2A.html)).

Pausanias, writing in the second century CE, reports within the Erechtheion a “sea water” well which sounded like waves when a south wind blew and “the shape of a trident in the rock”; these were said to be Poseidon’s tokens in the contest. His testimony documents what guides and worshippers identified in his day, not the historicity of a divine impact.

#### 8. Erechtheus and Eumolpus

Euripides’ fragmentary *Erechtheus*, performed in the later fifth century BCE, connected Poseidon’s trident with the death or subterranean entombment of the Athenian king Erechtheus after Erechtheus killed Poseidon’s son Eumolpus. Athena orders Poseidon to withdraw the earthquake-making trident and institutes cults reconciling civic antagonists.

This tradition may intersect with the Acropolis trident mark, but whether Athenians distinguished a mark from the contest and a mark from Erechtheus’s death is disputed. The modern reconstruction of the play can be consulted in [Mary R. Lefkowitz and Maureen B. Fant’s presentation of *Erechtheus*](https://diotima-doctafemina.org/translations/greek/euripides-erechtheus/).

#### 9. Gigantomachy

Archaic and classical images regularly place the trident in combat against Giants, especially Polybotes. Pseudo-Apollodorus’s surviving prose says Poseidon broke off Nisyros from Kos and cast it upon Polybotes; it does not expressly say that the trident performed the breaking. Vase painters, however, portray direct trident combat. Text and image should not be collapsed into a single standardized event.

#### 10. Amymone and Lerna

In the Argive story, Amymone, searching for water, accidentally strikes or attracts a satyr. Poseidon intervenes; in some versions he hurls his trident, which becomes embedded in rock. After his union with Amymone he tells her to remove it, whereupon water springs forth. The spring is linked with Lerna and sometimes named for Amymone. This is one of the rare narratives in which a mortal woman touches or removes the weapon, but she does not acquire ownership: it returns to Poseidon.

#### 11. Roman literary custody

Greek Poseidon’s weapon transfers seamlessly, through interpretatio Graeca, to Roman Neptune. This is not narrated as an owner-to-owner conveyance; Neptune is treated as the corresponding deity.

In Virgil:

- Neptune uses the trident to free ships from rocks and open quicksand while calming Juno’s storm (*Aeneid* 1).
- During Troy’s destruction, Venus shows Aeneas Neptune using the trident to shake the city’s walls and foundations from below (*Aeneid* 2: [text](https://www.theoi.com/Text/VirgilAeneid2.html)).

Ovid uses the trident as Neptune’s conventional identifier and as an instrument affecting waters and earth. Such passages expand its Roman literary career but do not create a documentable chain of physical custody.

### No theft, loss, inheritance, or recovery in the ancient core

No securely cited ancient source reports that Poseidon permanently:

- lost the trident;
- lent it to a hero;
- bequeathed it;
- had it stolen;
- surrendered it to Odysseus;
- or deposited it in a temple treasury.

Modern stories frequently introduce these events because a transferable superweapon generates a quest plot. They are not restorations of a lost classical tradition.

### Custody gaps

The entire material chain is absent. There is no transition from mythic possession to a temple relic, no ancient inventory entry for “the trident of Poseidon,” and no excavated candidate. The apparent continuity is iconographic and literary, not physical.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Does the original object exist?

There is no evidence that a unique physical “Poseidon’s Trident” ever existed. The evidence establishes belief, narration, cult symbolism, and representation—not material existence.

Accordingly:

- there is no accepted original;
- no accession number;
- no authenticated fragment;
- no scientific dating;
- no metallurgical analysis;
- no DNA or residue study;
- and no legal owner.

### The Erechtheion marks

The strongest physical claimant is not a weapon but an alleged effect of it: fissures or holes in the bedrock beneath the north porch of the building conventionally called the Erechtheion.

The official Acropolis Museum interpretation states that the building incorporated “the marks in the bedrock left by Poseidon’s trident” and the salt spring associated with his blow ([Acropolis Museum](https://www.theacropolismuseum.gr/en/node/16472)). This accurately reports the monument’s traditional interpretation.

The identification is nevertheless disputed archaeologically. Tétaz, after nineteenth-century clearance, associated three bedrock indentations with the ancient mark. Jeppesen and other scholars objected that the irregular fissures are not aligned like a trident and are recognizable as one only with prior expectation. A recent reassessment argues that ancient references may point elsewhere on the Acropolis and that the conventional identification of both the trident mark and the Erechtheion itself demands reconsideration ([Kerns, “The Erechtheion on the Acropolis of Athens”](https://journals.openedition.org/kernos/3853)).

The correct evidentiary formulation is:

- **Documented fact:** ancient authors reported a displayed trident mark on the Acropolis.
- **Documented fact:** fissures exist beneath the north porch.
- **Institutional tradition:** the fissures are presented as Poseidon’s marks.
- **Disputed archaeological inference:** those particular fissures are the feature seen by Hegesias, Strabo, or Pausanias.
- **Mythic claim:** the fissures were physically made by a divine trident.

### The Artemision bronze

The over-life-size bronze recovered from the sea near Cape Artemision and dated around 460 BCE has been identified alternately as Zeus throwing a thunderbolt or Poseidon brandishing a trident. The missing held object is decisive: a long trident crossing the figure’s face and visual field would be compositionally awkward, leading many scholars to prefer Zeus; others retain Poseidon.

The National Archaeological Museum catalogues it cautiously as **“Zeus or Poseidon of Artemision,”** inventory Χ 15161, height 2.09 m, copper alloy, displayed in Hall 15 ([Hellenic Ministry of Culture catalogue](https://odysseus.culture.gr/h/4/eh430.jsp?obj_id=4507)).

This statue is not a claimant to being Poseidon’s body, and its missing implement is not the original trident. The dispute concerns iconographic identification only.

### Ancient images as copies

A vase, coin, relief, or statue can be an ancient witness to what artists believed the trident looked like, but it cannot establish that artists copied a common physical prototype. Variations among depictions make direct copying from one cult object unlikely as a universal explanation.

### Modern false identifications

Common unsupported claims include:

- that a particular archaeological trident is “Poseidon’s” because it was found underwater;
- that the Ukrainian *tryzub* is directly Poseidon’s trident;
- that the Greek letter psi, Ψ, was designed from the weapon;
- that the weapon is historically identical with Shiva’s *triśūla*;
- or that the trident symbol proves direct cultural transmission wherever it occurs.

Formal resemblance alone does not establish identity or descent. Cross-cultural comparison can be historically useful but must be supported by evidence of transmission, chronology, and context.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Cultic function

The trident’s primary ritual importance was as a recognizable attribute of Poseidon. Images, votives, coins, and sanctuary decoration could evoke the god through the implement alone.

At Athens, the trident mark and salt-water *thalassa* operated as **sacred tokens**—materialized proof within civic religion that Poseidon had asserted a claim to Attica. Their incorporation into monumental space reconciled the city’s devotion to Athena with the continuing power of Poseidon-Erechtheus. The marks’ ritual significance is historical even though their supernatural origin cannot be verified.

Euripides’ *Erechtheus* indicates that a standing or cultically imagined trident could receive garlands. Athena’s settlement of the conflict binds Poseidon and Erechtheus into Athenian cult. The object thus legitimates not an individual monarch but a civic accommodation among divine and heroic claims.

### Instrumental powers by earliest attestation

| Claimed power | Earliest surviving attestation | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Directing the destruction of walls and reshaping shorelines | *Iliad* 12 | Poseidon works with Apollo, rivers, and Zeus’s rain |
| Stirring sea, winds, clouds, and darkness | *Odyssey* 5 | Explicitly grasped in both hands |
| Splitting rock and drowning an offender | *Odyssey* 4 | Punitive use against Ajax |
| Creating or revealing a salt spring/“sea” | Classical/Hellenistic Athenian tradition; explicit later prose in Apollodorus | Not in the Homeric poems |
| Producing earthquakes or opening the earth | Euripides’ *Erechtheus* and Roman adaptations | Closely linked to Poseidon’s Earth-Shaker identity |
| Smashing fortifications | Virgil, *Aeneid* 2 | Roman poetic expansion |
| Freeing ships and calming waters | Virgil, *Aeneid* 1 | Constructive and pacifying function |
| Granting universal oceanic control to any holder | Modern fantasy | Not a general ancient rule |
| Making its possessor king of Atlantis or the seas | Modern comics and film | Modern invention |

### Political and civic symbolism

On Greek coins the trident could replace the god’s full figure, functioning as a compact emblem of:

- a city’s Poseidon cult;
- maritime identity;
- naval aspiration;
- local genealogy;
- or divine protection.

Recent scholarship emphasizes that its numismatic uses were more varied than a simple generic “sea” sign and must be interpreted issue by issue ([Guimarães and Laky, “A iconografia do tridente…”](https://revistas.usp.br/revmae/pt_BR/article/view/227430)).

In Rome, Neptune’s trident continued to denote command of marine forces. In early modern Europe the same visual grammar migrated to rulers, ports, fountains, navies, and national personifications.

### Access rules and taboos

No source establishes a universal taboo that mortals could not touch the trident. The Amymone narrative may have her remove it from rock. What is absent is a general ancient rule that only Poseidon could lift it or that it judged rightful sovereignty. Such mechanics derive largely from modern fantasy weapons.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The Cyclopes’ gift

**Tier:** late ancient origin myth.

After Zeus frees the imprisoned Cyclopes, they equip the three divine brothers: Zeus with thunder and lightning, Hades with a concealing helmet, and Poseidon with the trident. These instruments allow the Olympians to defeat the Titans. The story integrates the trident into a constitutional mythology of cosmic government.

**Later elaborations:** The Cyclopes explicitly forge the weapons; each named Cyclops contributes a quality; the work occurs below Etna or in a volcanic forge; the metal is divine, indestructible, or otherwise named. These details are not present in Pseudo-Apollodorus 1.2.1.

### The Telchines’ manufacture

**Tier:** rival later origin tradition.

The Telchines—Rhodian or pre-Rhodian supernatural artisans—make the trident. The story associates Poseidon’s weapon with island metalworking and the sea. Later compilations often combine this with his upbringing among the Telchines.

No evidence permits harmonizing the traditions by claiming that the Telchines forged it and the Cyclopes merely delivered it, unless a modern author expressly invents that reconciliation.

### The contest for Athens

**Tier:** classical civic myth with Hellenistic and Roman descriptions of its physical tokens.

Poseidon strikes the Acropolis and produces a *thalassa*, conventionally understood as salt water. Athena produces the olive. The gods, Cecrops, or the Athenians judge between them, depending on the version; Athena wins.

Variants include:

- Poseidon gives a salt spring;
- Poseidon gives the first horse;
- the voting is conducted by male and female citizens, with women’s votes securing Athena’s victory;
- Poseidon retaliates with flood, drought, or anger;
- Zeus prevents open conflict.

The horse-gift version is later and should not be substituted for the salt-water token in Apollodorus and Pausanias. The myth explained multiple cults within Athenian sacred topography and affirmed that Poseidon’s defeated claim remained dangerous and ritually recognized.

### The death of Erechtheus

**Tier:** fifth-century BCE tragic and civic myth.

After Erechtheus defeats and kills Eumolpus, Poseidon strikes him into the earth. Athena orders Poseidon to remove the trident and prescribes cult arrangements. This gives the weapon a role in explaining chthonic space, earthquake, dynastic death, and the composite identity Poseidon-Erechtheus.

The relationship between this blow and the contest’s trident mark remains unresolved. Some reconstructions assume two marks; others treat the traditions as alternative explanations of one sacred feature.

### Ajax and the Gyraean rock

**Tier:** archaic epic.

Ajax survives Athena’s storm and boasts that he has escaped the gods. Poseidon strikes the rock, breaks off the part to which Ajax clings, and drowns him. The scene makes the trident an instrument of divine enforcement against hubris.

Later retellings sometimes simplify this to Poseidon spearing Ajax. Homer instead has the weapon strike the rock.

### Odysseus’s storm

**Tier:** archaic epic.

Poseidon takes the trident in both hands and churns the sea against Odysseus. Modern retellings sometimes transform this into a personal duel in which Odysseus steals the weapon and wounds the god. No such duel occurs in Homer; Odysseus survives through divine assistance, endurance, and eventual Phaeacian transport.

### The Achaean wall

**Tier:** archaic epic.

Poseidon and Apollo erase the Greeks’ wall because it was built without proper divine sacrifices. The trident participates in obliterating a human structure so completely that no trace remains. The story also serves Homeric poetics: it explains why audiences cannot see a supposedly enormous Trojan War fortification.

### Amymone’s spring

**Tier:** ancient regional myth with variants.

Amymone searches for water during an Argive drought. Poseidon rescues her from a satyr, becomes her lover, and reveals or creates a spring. In the most object-centered form, his thrown trident remains embedded in rock; Amymone draws it out, releasing water. The weapon briefly changes hands but not ownership.

### Polybotes and Nisyros

**Tier:** archaic/classical iconography plus mythographic narrative.

Poseidon pursues the Giant Polybotes, breaks off part of Kos, and crushes him beneath the island Nisyros. Art frequently shows Poseidon using the trident directly against the Giant. Later summaries often state that the trident itself broke off the island, but the surviving Apollodoran text does not specify the tool.

### Horse creation and Hippios

**Tier:** later variant and retrospective synthesis.

Poseidon’s horse associations are ancient, but stories in which the first horse springs from a trident-struck rock do not belong to the earliest Athenian contest account. They combine the god’s Hippios aspect with his rock-striking implement. Individual versions must be dated rather than described generically as “the Greek myth.”

### Lost or hidden trident stories

Ancient literature does not preserve a major quest for the lost trident. Hidden-trident, stolen-trident, rightful-heir, and world-ocean-control plots are overwhelmingly modern.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Status of the mythic object

**Current status:** legendary; no physical existence established.  
**Current location:** none that can be documented.  
**Owner or controller:** not applicable in law or archaeology.  
**Condition:** not assessable.  
**Public access:** through texts, representations, cult sites, and modern replicas.

There is no serious scholarly hypothesis that it lies undiscovered in a named underwater site, temple treasury, cave, or volcanic forge.

### Physical stand-ins

#### Erechtheion bedrock and sea well

The traditional trident marks lie—or are presented as lying—beneath the north porch of the Erechtheion on the Athenian Acropolis. The site is controlled and conserved by the Hellenic state. Visitor access is to the monument, not unrestricted physical handling of the bedrock. The official presentation accepts the traditional association; archaeological identification of the visible fissures remains debated.

#### Ancient representations

Thousands of ancient representations survive across museums and collections. They are evidence for iconography, not competing originals.

Examples include:

- British Museum red-figure neck-amphora 1867,0508.1121;
- British Museum drinking cup 1836,0224.66, whose catalogue discusses whether the trident-bearing figure is Poseidon or Nereus ([British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/G_1836-0224-66));
- the National Archaeological Museum’s Artemision bronze, Χ 15161, whose lost attribute generates the Zeus/Poseidon dispute;
- and Attic vessels depicting Poseidon versus Polybotes.

#### Monumental sculptural tridents

Giambologna’s Neptune Fountain in Bologna, constructed under papal patronage in 1563–1567, includes a monumental bronze Neptune wielding a physical sculptural trident. The city’s history identifies architect Tommaso Laureti and sculptor Giambologna and explains the fountain’s original designation *Aqua Pia* ([Comune di Bologna](https://nettuno.comune.bologna.it/en/history/)). Its trident is a Renaissance representation, not an ancient relic.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Renaissance and Baroque political imagery

Early modern rulers and cities used Neptune with trident to signify control of water, harbors, navigation, civic order, and imperial power. Fountains translated the mythic object into actual bronze and stone, associating engineered water supply with a god who commands water.

Giambologna’s Bologna Neptune presents the trident-bearing god above an elaborate hydraulic monument. The project served papal urban renewal and good-government imagery. The trident’s gesture balances threatened force with the calming authority expressed by Neptune’s other hand.

### Britannia and maritime empire

Britannia did not always carry a trident. Roman coins personified the province, but the decisively nautical trident developed in modern British imagery. The Royal Mint identifies Conrad Heinrich Küchler’s 1797 coinage as marking the transition from spear to trident, with Britannia surrounded by sea. The implement signified British maritime power and continued on later pennies, the 1969 fifty-pence coin, and bullion issues ([Royal Mint](https://www.royalmint.com/stories/commemorate/britannia-the-icon-on-the-coin/)).

This is a political appropriation of Neptune’s attribute, not a claim that Britannia possesses Poseidon’s literal weapon.

### Barbados: the broken trident

Barbados’s colonial badge showed Britannia with a trident. At independence in 1966, Grantley Prescod’s national-flag design placed a black, deliberately broken trident head in the gold central panel. The missing shaft signifies the break with colonial rule. The symbol is therefore historically descended through Britannia’s maritime trident, itself adapted from Neptune/Poseidon, but its fracture reverses the old imperial meaning.

The flag is a documented modern reinvention, not an ancient Barbadian Poseidon tradition.

### Maserati

Mario Maserati designed the company’s trident in 1925, drawing on Giambologna’s Neptune in Bologna. It appeared officially on the 1926 Tipo 26. Maserati states that Mario’s original drawing survives in its Modena archives and that later logos changed framing, color, and proportions while preserving the three-pointed emblem ([Maserati/Stellantis history](https://www.media.stellantis.com/em-en/maserati/press/the-first-hundred-years-of-the-maserati-trident-an-iconic-logo-between-the-racetrack-and-legend)).

This gives the symbol a particularly clear chain of transmission:

**ancient Poseidon/Neptune iconography → Giambologna’s 1560s Neptune → Mario Maserati’s 1925 drawing → Tipo 26 and subsequent corporate marks.**

### Astronomy

The conventional planetary symbol ♆ represents Neptune’s trident. NASA explicitly identifies it as the sea god’s three-pronged weapon ([NASA Solar System Symbols](https://science.nasa.gov/resource/solar-system-symbols/)). The glyph is a symbolic descendant, not an image of a located artifact.

### Literature, comics, film, and games

Modern fantasy commonly turns the attribute into a transferable magical artifact with powers unavailable to ordinary holders in ancient myth.

Significant transformations include:

- **DC’s Aquaman:** multiple continuities distinguish or confuse tridents of Neptune, Poseidon, and the Atlantean king Atlan. They can validate kingship, command water, emit energy, or serve as quest objects. DC’s *Aquaman* #7 (2012) explicitly makes a golden trident the climactic trophy in a weapon-collecting plot ([DC Comics](https://www.dc.com/comics/aquaman-2011/aquaman-7)).
- **The 2018 film *Aquaman*:** the Trident of Atlan is a separate Atlantean royal artifact. Public imagery nevertheless strengthens the assumption that every sea ruler must possess a Poseidon-like trident.
- **Rick Riordan’s *Percy Jackson* universe:** the trident operates primarily as Poseidon’s identifying symbol and as the sign acknowledging his children; modern fan fiction frequently—but noncanonically—transfers the weapon to Percy.
- **Pirates of the Caribbean:** *Dead Men Tell No Tales* (2017) makes “Poseidon’s Trident” a hidden artifact able to break all curses of the sea. Its destruction resolves the plot. This custody and destruction history belongs wholly to the film’s fictional universe.
- **Michael Livingston, *The Shards of Heaven* (2015):** treats the trident as one of several surviving divine superweapons.
- **Video games:** franchises including *God of War*, *Assassin’s Creed*, and mythological strategy or role-playing games depict it as loot, a quest item, a power-granting weapon, or a copyable equipment class.

A pervasive modern convention gives the weapon autonomous magical energy, indestructibility, recall to its owner, or the ability to confer command of the sea on whoever holds it. Ancient texts instead show the trident as an attribute through which Poseidon acts; they do not articulate a general transferable power system.

### Contemporary musical retelling

Jorge Rivera-Herrans’s *EPIC: The Musical* introduces a confrontation in which Odysseus gains Poseidon’s trident and uses it against him. Online discussion has sometimes mistaken this sequence for Homeric narrative. It is a documented modern transformation: Homer’s Odysseus never defeats Poseidon in a trident duel.

### Tourism and commerce

Replicas are sold as:

- costume weapons;
- museum-shop and archaeological-site souvenirs;
- jewelry;
- tattoos;
- devotional objects in modern Hellenic polytheism;
- film and comic collectibles;
- and maritime décor.

These objects may copy Greek vase forms, Roman statues, the Bologna fountain, Aquaman props, or wholly modern fantasy designs. Without a stated model, “replica of Poseidon’s Trident” means an imaginative representation, not a measured reproduction of an original.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Principal questions

Scholarship has concentrated on five problems:

1. **Was the trident originally a fish spear, a lightning symbol, a sceptre form, or a composite?**
2. **How early did it become Poseidon’s exclusive identifier?**
3. **How do literary actions relate to the god’s marine and seismic functions?**
4. **What did the Athenian trident mark physically consist of, and where was it?**
5. **What political meanings did the emblem carry on coins and monuments?**

### Fish spear versus thunder weapon

The ordinary meaning of *triaina*, the marine context, and barbed coin depictions support the fish-spear interpretation. Blinkenberg’s comparative theory instead derived it from a thunder or lightning weapon, stressing Poseidon’s earth-shaking character and Anatolian parallels.

The disagreement is partly methodological. Functional resemblance to fishing gear is direct but may explain the classical form rather than remote origin; comparative lightning forms are suggestive but do not document a transmission chain. Neither interpretation justifies treating a modern replica as an archaeologically exact reconstruction.

### The trident as sceptre

Another line of interpretation sees the trident as differentiating out of early divine sceptres with floral or multiple-point finials. Again, iconographic development can explain a symbol’s visual genealogy without establishing a single prototype object.

### The Erechtheion problem

Older architectural scholarship often treated the north-porch fissures as decisive proof that the conventional Karyatid building was the Erechtheion described by Pausanias. Critics observed that:

- the holes are irregular;
- they do not naturally form a trident;
- viewing conditions are awkward;
- textual descriptions may point to another location;
- and multiple Athenian myths could compete to explain a mark.

Recent reassessment has reopened both the mark’s location and the building’s identification. The official archaeological presentation and revisionist scholarship therefore coexist. Neither changes the basic conclusion that ancient Athenians recognized some Acropolis feature as a trident mark.

### Iconography and identification

A trident often identifies Poseidon but not invariably:

- Triton and other marine beings may carry one;
- Nereus can be represented with a similar implement;
- gladiators used real tridents;
- and restored statues may receive conjectural attributes.

Museum catalogues accordingly preserve uncertainty. The Artemision bronze is the best-known example: identifying a missing weapon from pose alone can become circular.

### Numismatic scholarship

Earlier catalogues often treated the trident merely as a label for Poseidon. More recent work studies it as an active civic sign. Placement, accompanying dolphins, local cults, magistrates’ symbols, minting context, and political chronology can change its meaning. The 2025 Portuguese-language study by Vinícius Bonifácio Guimarães and Lilian de Angelo Laky is important for treating archaic and classical coin imagery systematically rather than as an appendix to the god’s biography.

### State of the primary evidence

The evidence is uneven:

- Homer survives through a long manuscript tradition, not contemporary inscriptions.
- Euripides’ *Erechtheus* is fragmentary and reconstructed from papyri and quotations.
- Hegesias’s description survives only because Strabo quotes it.
- Pausanias preserves a Roman-period visitor’s account of an older sacred landscape.
- Painted pottery and coins preserve independent visual evidence but do not explain themselves.
- The physical configuration beneath the Erechtheion has been altered by destruction, reuse, excavation, and restoration.
- No ancient sanctuary inventory yet identified records the supposed divine original as a movable treasury object.

### Discredited or unsupported popular theories

The following should not be presented as established:

- Hesiod narrates the Cyclopes making Poseidon’s trident. Hesiod describes the Cyclopes’ relation to Zeus’s thunderbolt; the coordinated trident-and-helmet distribution is explicit in Pseudo-Apollodorus.
- The Telchines’ manufacture is the sole or earliest origin story.
- The Artemision bronze certainly held the trident.
- The Erechtheion fissures have been scientifically proved to be artificial trident holes.
- The weapon was made of adamant, orichalcum, “Poseidon steel,” or another named substance in classical literature.
- Poseidon lent the trident permanently to Triton, Percy Jackson, or Aquaman in ancient myth.
- Odysseus stabbed Poseidon with it in the *Odyssey*.
- The trident was historically recovered from Atlantis.
- Every three-pronged religious sign is descended from this weapon.
