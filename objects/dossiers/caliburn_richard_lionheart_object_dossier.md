# "Caliburn"/Excalibur given by Richard the Lionheart to Tancred: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conclusion

This dossier concerns the sword that Roger of Howden reports Richard I of England gave to Tancred, king of Sicily, at Catania in March 1191, identifying it as Arthur’s sword, called *Caliburnum* by the Britons.

Three things must be kept distinct:

1. **Caledfwlch/Caliburnus/Escalibor/Excalibur, the literary sword of Arthur.** This is a legendary object whose textual career begins in medieval Welsh and Latin literature.
2. **The physical sword reportedly transferred in 1191.** Roger’s near-contemporary account is evidence that Richard possessed and presented a sword accepted or represented as Arthur’s. It is not proof that the sword belonged to a historical Arthur, nor does Roger describe how it was authenticated.
3. **Later representations and alleged Excaliburs.** No surviving sword has a documented chain of custody from Tancred, and no museum, treasury, church, archaeological collection, or Sicilian institution presently catalogs the 1191 object.

The most defensible reconstruction is therefore:

> A physical sword probably existed at the center of the 1191 gift episode, because Roger describes it among concrete diplomatic gifts and transport vessels. Its construction, age, acquisition by Richard, and fate after Tancred received it are completely undocumented. Its identity as Arthur’s sword belonged to medieval historical belief, diplomatic representation, or both; it cannot be archaeologically verified.

The assertion that Richard merely “claimed” the sword was Excalibur is a modern paraphrase. Roger does not distance himself in that way: he calls it Arthur’s sword directly. Conversely, the frequent modern statement that it was excavated from Arthur’s purported grave at Glastonbury is unsupported by Roger and chronologically problematic.

### Evidentiary labels

- **Documented report:** explicitly stated by a medieval or archival source.
- **Near-contemporary testimony:** written close to the event, possibly by an informed observer, but not independently corroborated.
- **Literary tradition:** a proposition about Arthur’s legendary sword within a particular text.
- **Scholarly inference:** a reconstruction proposed from circumstantial evidence.
- **Late accretion or modern conflation:** absent from the controlling medieval evidence.
- **Unknown:** no reliable evidence presently establishes the point.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Object class and identity

**Class:** sword; royal diplomatic gift; alleged heroic relic; literary regalia.

**Object tracked:** the physical sword described in Roger of Howden’s accounts of Richard I’s meeting with Tancred at Catania in March 1191.

Roger’s Latin description in the revised *Chronica* is:

> *Rex autem Angliae dedit ei gladium optimum Arcturi, nobilis quondam regis Angliae, quem Britones vocaverunt Caliburnum.*

A close translation is:

> “The king of England, however, gave him the excellent/best sword of Arthur, formerly a noble king of England, which the Britons called Caliburn.”

The earlier *Gesta regis Henrici Secundi et Gesta regis Ricardi* has substantially the same report but calls Arthur a king of the Britons rather than of England:

> *Rex autem Angliae dedit ei gladium optimum Arcturi, nobilis quondam regis Britonum, quem Britones vocaverunt Caliburnum.*

The change from *rex Britonum* to *rex Angliae* is historiographically significant: the revised wording appropriates the British Arthur into an English royal past. The relevant editions are William Stubbs, ed., *Gesta Regis Henrici Secundi Benedicti Abbatis*, II (1867), p. 159, and *Chronica Magistri Rogeri de Houedene*, III (1870), p. 97. The traditional attribution of the *Gesta* to Benedict of Peterborough has been displaced; modern scholarship generally associates both works with Roger of Howden. An accessible English rendering appears in T. A. Archer’s collection of Third Crusade sources, while Henry T. Riley’s translation supplies the fuller narrative context ([Archer PDF](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/TA_Archer-CrusadeofRichard%20I-1912.pdf); [Riley, vol. II](https://www.antipas.org/library/World%20History/The%20Annals%20of%20Roger%20de%20Hoveden%20-%20Volume%202.pdf)).

### Names and linguistic history

- **Welsh:** *Caledfwlch*, modern spelling unchanged; approximately “hard-cleft,” “hard breach,” or “hard-cleaving,” although the precise compound and its relationship to Irish sword names remain debated.
- **Middle Latin:** *Caliburnus*; accusative *Caliburnum*. Geoffrey of Monmouth’s form.
- **Anglo-Norman and Old French:** *Caliburne*, *Calibourne*, *Escalibor*, *Escalibur* and related manuscript spellings.
- **Middle English:** forms including *Excaliber*, *Excalibur*, *Excalibur(e)*.
- **Modern English:** *Excalibur*.
- **Modern French:** *Excalibur*, sometimes *Caliburne* when discussing Geoffrey or Roger.
- **Modern Italian:** *Excalibur*; *Caliburno* is occasionally used as a learned adaptation.

The common derivational sequence is Welsh *Caledfwlch* → Geoffrey’s Latin *Caliburnus* → French *Escalibor/Escalibur* → English *Excalibur*. A relationship with Irish *Caladbolg*, the name of Fergus mac Róich’s sword, has frequently been proposed. An alternative or contributing influence is medieval Latin *calibs/chalybs*, “steel.” The evidence supports linguistic relationship, not a recoverable story in which one physical Irish sword became Arthur’s sword. The National Library of Wales’s *Welsh Classical Dictionary* summarizes the Welsh and Irish parallels ([National Library of Wales](https://www.library.wales/fileadmin/docs_gwefan/new_structure/discover/digital_exhibitions/printed_material/welsh_classical_dictionary/03_C1.pdf)).

### Physical description of the 1191 sword

No medieval source presently identified supplies any of the following:

- blade length, width, profile, or weight;
- iron or steel composition;
- method or date of forging;
- maker, workshop, mine, or place of manufacture;
- hilt, guard, grip, pommel, scabbard, belt, or suspension;
- inscriptions, heraldry, gemstones, gold, silver, or other decoration;
- damage, repairs, polishing, sharpening, or alteration;
- whether it was a functional weapon, older trophy, ceremonial sword, or purpose-made presentation object;
- whether Richard personally wore or used it;
- whether it came from an English, Angevin, Norman, Breton, Welsh, Sicilian, or other treasury.

Roger’s *gladium optimum* is praise, not a technical typology. It can mean “the best,” “excellent,” or “finest sword.” It does not establish supernatural qualities or exceptional metallurgy.

### Physical description in the literary tradition

The literary sword accumulated features that cannot be transferred automatically to the 1191 artifact:

- In *Culhwch ac Olwen*, Caledfwlch belongs to Arthur and is included among the equipment or prestigious possessions of his court; the tale does not give the later lake, stone, or scabbard narratives.
- Geoffrey of Monmouth, writing about 1136–1138, says Arthur’s sword *Caliburnus* was made in the Isle of Avalon: *gladium Caliburnum, in insula Avallonis fabricatum*. Arthur bears it with his shield and spear, and uses it in battle.
- Wace’s *Roman de Brut* (completed 1155) converts the name into vernacular forms approaching *Escalibur* and says the sword was made in Avalon. Wace adds that in French it means “cuts iron, steel and wood,” a literary folk etymology rather than a reliable linguistic derivation.
- Chrétien de Troyes’ romances know *Escalibor* as a renowned sword; in some French traditions it can be wielded by Gawain, demonstrating that exclusive possession by Arthur was not yet fixed.
- The thirteenth-century prose cycles introduce or develop the Lady of the Lake, the supernatural hand, and the sword’s protective scabbard. Different texts distinguish or conflate the sword drawn from the stone with the sword received from the lake.
- Thomas Malory’s *Le Morte Darthur* (printed by William Caxton in 1485) gave English readers the influential synthesis: Arthur first proves his kingship through the sword in the stone, later receives Excalibur from the Lady of the Lake, loses the scabbard, and at his death commands that the sword be returned to the water. Malory’s narrative distinguishes the first sword from the lake sword, though later retellings commonly collapse them into one.

Nothing in Roger says the 1191 sword shone, sang, cut steel, carried an inscription, possessed a magical scabbard, had been drawn from a stone, or came from a lake.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### A. Literary object

#### Welsh precursor or cognate

*Caledfwlch* appears in *Culhwch ac Olwen*, the oldest surviving substantial Arthurian prose tale. The dating requires two levels of precision:

- The tale’s extant redaction is usually placed in the eleventh or twelfth century, with some scholars identifying older linguistic or narrative strata.
- The surviving manuscripts are later: principally the fourteenth-century White Book of Rhydderch and Red Book of Hergest traditions.

Thus the date of a manuscript witness is not the same as the date of composition. D. Edel describes *Culhwch* as the oldest preserved Arthurian tale and notes the case for a redaction in the second half of the eleventh century, while emphasizing the difficulties of the surviving transmission ([University of Reading](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/85043/1/RMS-1983-01_D._Edel%2C_The_Arthur_of_%27Culhwch_and_Olwen%27_as_a_Figure_of_Epic-Heroic_Tradition.pdf)).

#### First securely influential Latin attestation

Geoffrey of Monmouth’s *Historia regum Britanniae*—also transmitted as *De gestis Britonum*—is the first securely dated and enormously influential account to call the sword *Caliburnus* and to provide an origin:

> Arthur girded on *Caliburnus*, “made in the island of Avalon.”

What Geoffrey does **not** say:

- He does not identify a smith.
- He does not mention the Lady of the Lake.
- He does not say the sword was drawn from a stone.
- He does not describe a magical scabbard.
- He does not narrate its return to a lake after Arthur’s last battle.
- He does not supply a physical description adequate to identify an archaeological sword.

Geoffrey’s work was treated as history by many medieval readers, but modern historiography regards its Arthurian reign as legendary pseudo-history rather than a contemporary record of post-Roman Britain.

### B. The physical sword reported in 1191

Its manufacture is unknown. No contract, inventory, treasury roll, inscription, scientific test, or archaeological context survives.

The first and only secure notice of the physical object is Roger of Howden’s account of Richard’s Catania meeting. The event belongs to Roger’s contemporary chronicle-writing, not to Geoffrey’s fictionalized sixth-century setting.

### C. Proposed pre-1191 origin theories

#### Glastonbury-grave theory

**Modern conflation, not documented provenance.**

Arthur and Guinevere’s purported grave was reportedly discovered by the monks of Glastonbury in the early 1190s. Later writers have inferred that Richard’s sword came from this excavation. The inference is weak for several reasons:

1. Roger never connects the sword with Glastonbury.
2. The earliest accounts of the excavation do not establish that a sword identified as Excalibur was removed from the grave and delivered to Richard.
3. The event’s dating is disputed within approximately 1190–1191; some reconstructions place the exhumation after Richard’s March gift.
4. Richard had already left England for the crusade in 1190.
5. No transmission from Glastonbury to Richard in Sicily is recorded.

Statements that the sword was “found in Arthur’s grave” should therefore be labeled a late scholarly or popular inference, not a medieval fact.

#### Angevin heirloom or Wayland-forged sword theory

Henri Bresc’s “Excalibur en Sicile” surveys the possibility that the object was connected with a prestigious sword in Angevin dynastic memory, perhaps one of the legendary blades attributed to the smith Weland/Wayland and associated with earlier royal gift exchanges. This is an attempt to supply a plausible material biography for Richard’s relic, but no continuous documentary chain identifies such an heirloom with Roger’s *Caliburnum*. The proposal remains circumstantial ([Henri Bresc, “Excalibur en Sicile”](https://ddd.uab.cat/pub/medievalia/medievalia_a1987n7/medievalia_a1987n7p7.pdf)).

#### Purpose-made diplomatic object

It is possible that the sword was manufactured or selected shortly before the meeting and invested with Arthurian identity for presentation. Medieval royal treasuries contained weapons whose value depended on attributed owners, antiquity, inscriptions, or ceremonial history. No source, however, names such a commission or exposes the sword as a fabrication.

#### Genuine ancient or sub-Roman sword

No evidence supports this beyond the attribution itself. Without the object, a description, or scientific examination, the proposition cannot be tested.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Legendary custody: source-dependent chain

The sword’s legendary custody cannot be reduced to a single canonical sequence.

1. **Welsh tradition:** Caledfwlch belongs to Arthur. No manufacture, transfer to Arthur, or final deposition is securely described in *Culhwch ac Olwen*.
2. **Geoffrey of Monmouth:** Caliburnus is made in Avalon and borne by Arthur in his European wars. Geoffrey does not narrate a donor, prior possessor, or return to Avalon.
3. **Wace:** Escalibur remains Arthur’s Avalon-made weapon, with enhanced cutting reputation.
4. **Chrétien and related French romance:** the sword’s custody is less exclusive; Gawain can carry the famous blade in certain episodes.
5. **Prose-cycle traditions:** Arthur receives a sword through the Lady of the Lake; the scabbard receives special protective powers. The stone sword and lake sword are not consistently identical.
6. **Post-Vulgate and Malorian synthesis:** the lake sword becomes the principal Excalibur and is returned to the water after Arthur’s fatal battle, generally by Girflet in French tradition and Bedivere in Malory.

These are transformations in literary custody, not records of a physical artifact.

### Reconstructable physical chain

| Date | Holder/place | Event | Evidence and status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before March 1191 | Unknown; immediately before the gift, presumably Richard I or his household | Acquisition, manufacture, and prior custody unknown | No surviving evidence |
| 1 March 1191 | Richard arrives at Catania and meets Tancred | Roger dates the meeting by narrative sequence | Near-contemporary report |
| Fourth day of the visit, conventionally 4 March 1191 | Catania, probably Tancred’s palace after visits involving St Agatha’s shrine | Richard gives the sword to Tancred | Roger’s *Gesta* and revised *Chronica* |
| After March 1191 | Presumably Tancred’s custody or royal treasury | No recorded use or movement | Inference only |
| 20 February 1194 | Tancred dies at Palermo | Possible inheritance or treasury succession entirely unknown | No sword-specific evidence |
| 1194 onward | Unknown | Possible seizure, dispersal, concealment, reuse, destruction, or retention during Henry VI’s conquest | No sword-specific evidence |
| Present | Unknown | No authenticated survivor or institutional claimant | Confirmed absence from known documented custody |

### The Catania transfer in context

Roger says Richard reached Catania on 1 March 1191 and was received with honor. Richard and Tancred visited the shrine or tomb of St Agatha, after which Richard stayed in Tancred’s palace for three days and nights. On the fourth day Tancred sent gifts of gold and silver vessels, horses, and silken cloths. Richard reportedly accepted only a small ring as a sign of mutual affection. Richard then gave the Arthurian sword. Tancred additionally supplied four large horse-transports—called *Ufsers/Ursers*, that is, huissiers—and fifteen galleys.

This placement makes the sword part of a carefully narrated royal gift exchange. It was not necessarily a direct commercial payment for nineteen vessels: Roger distinguishes the refused luxury gifts, accepted ring, sword, and ships. Nevertheless, modern scholars reasonably interpret the exchange within negotiations over crusading transport, Joan’s financial rights, and the new alliance.

A modern English translation dates the meeting to 1 March and gives the chapter heading accordingly ([Chronicle of the Third Crusade](https://dokumen.pub/the-chronicle-of-the-third-crusade-crusade-texts-in-translation-1nbsped-9780754605812-0754605817.html)).

### Date discrepancy: 4 or 6 March 1191

The frequently given date **6 March 1191** appears in later secondary literature. Bresc dates the gift to **4 March**, consistent with Roger’s sequence: arrival on 1 March, three nights in the palace, gift on the fourth day. Because medieval inclusive counting and modern reconstructions can produce discrepancies, the safest formulation is:

> Catania, during Richard’s meeting with Tancred beginning 1 March 1191; conventionally 4 March, though 6 March also occurs in secondary literature.

It should not be confused with the separate October 1190 Treaty of Messina or with Richard and Philip II’s March 1191 settlement.

### Why the sword changed hands

The immediate documentary answer is that it was a counter-gift within royal hospitality. The larger political setting included:

- Tancred’s disputed accession after William II’s death;
- Tancred’s detention of William’s widow Joan, Richard’s sister, and his retention of her property;
- Richard’s seizure of Messina in October 1190;
- monetary and dynastic settlements negotiated between Richard and Tancred;
- recognition of Richard’s nephew Arthur of Brittany as heir;
- a contemplated marriage between Arthur and one of Tancred’s daughters;
- Tancred’s need for security against Henry VI and Constance;
- Richard’s need for ships and a safe departure for the Third Crusade.

Christopher Berard interprets the sword as an object securing Richard’s honor and credibility in his alliance with Tancred. Michelle Warren has suggested that the betrothal created a possible circular future: if Arthur of Brittany succeeded Richard and married Tancred’s daughter, Caliburn might return through dynastic inheritance. This is an illuminating hypothesis, not a documented plan. Adam Curry summarizes these interpretations in his study of royal Arthurian imagery ([Northumbria University thesis](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/169506325/curry.adam_phd_16024424_.pdf)).

### The broken chain after Tancred

No known Sicilian inventory, royal charter, testament, narrative chronicle, treasury list, confiscation record, or Hohenstaufen source securely mentions the sword after 1191.

Possible fates include:

- remaining in Tancred’s palace or treasury;
- passing to his sons Roger III or William III;
- seizure when Henry VI conquered the kingdom in 1194;
- removal to Palermo or into the Hohenstaufen treasury;
- diplomatic regifting;
- reuse after its Arthurian identification ceased to be recognized;
- destruction, loss, or melting;
- survival under another description.

All are hypotheses. None is presently evidenced.

Tancred’s dynasty fell rapidly. He died in February 1194; Henry VI invaded later that year, deposed the child William III, and confiscated the defeated regime’s wealth. This upheaval supplies a plausible setting for the sword’s disappearance but not a documented destination.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Did a physical sword exist?

**Strong probability, short of certainty.** Roger inserts the sword among ordinary material gifts and ships in a detailed account. A physical presentation best explains the passage. However, because the episode lacks an independent contemporary witness and no artifact or inventory survives, absolute confirmation is impossible.

### Was it Arthur’s actual sword?

**Not established and not scientifically testable.**

There is no securely verified historical King Arthur whose personal weapon can be traced. Even if a fifth- or sixth-century sword survived into 1191, neither age nor British origin could prove ownership by Arthur. No archaeological context, metallurgical report, inscription, or continuous provenance exists.

The attribution can be understood in several non-exclusive ways:

- sincere medieval belief;
- conventional relic authentication through royal or courtly tradition;
- diplomatic theater;
- dynastic propaganda;
- deliberate fiction;
- an inherited antiquarian identification whose original basis is lost.

Roger gives no authentication procedure and does not say Richard “claimed,” “pretended,” or “said” it was Arthur’s. His unqualified wording records the object’s accepted narrative identity, not its archaeological authenticity.

### Was the 1191 sword the same entity as Geoffrey’s Caliburnus?

Only at the level of asserted identity. Geoffrey’s Caliburnus is a literary object; Roger attaches that name and biography to a physical sword. There is no evidentiary bridge from Geoffrey’s imagined Arthurian age to Richard’s treasury.

### Was it from Glastonbury?

No controlling source says so. The association depends on temporal proximity between the sword gift and the monks’ discovery of Arthur’s alleged grave. Giraldus Cambrensis and Ralph of Coggeshall give important early accounts of the grave, but neither establishes the required chain:

> grave → excavated sword → royal possession → transport to Sicily → gift to Tancred.

Moreover, the excavation itself has long been understood in relation to Glastonbury’s prestige, fundraising needs after its 1184 fire, and Plantagenet interest in neutralizing Welsh and Breton expectations that Arthur would return. Those institutional and political motives do not by themselves prove fraud in every detail, but they make uncritical identification particularly hazardous.

Popular claims that monks found “Excalibur” beside Arthur’s bones and that Richard immediately gave it away should be rejected as unsupported.

### Was it a forgery?

No source documents a forger, workshop, confession, or exposure. “Forgery” therefore goes beyond the evidence. The sword may have been an ordinary medieval weapon assigned a legendary provenance, an older object sincerely misidentified, or an intentionally constructed relic. The available evidence cannot choose among those possibilities.

### Rival claimant objects

No extant sword has a defensible documentary claim to be Tancred’s 1191 gift. Modern places associated with Arthurian legend—including lakes where replicas are ceremonially thrown, swords embedded in stone-like settings, and museum reproductions—belong to other traditions.

Two recurrent confusions deserve explicit rejection:

- **The sword of San Galgano**, embedded in rock at Montesiepi in Tuscany, is a real medieval object associated with the saint Galgano Guidotti. It is not documented as Richard’s or Tancred’s sword.
- Modern “Excalibur” swords recovered from rivers, lakes, or archaeological sites lack a provenance connecting them to the 1191 exchange.

### Scientific examination

None is possible because the object has not been identified. No radiocarbon test, metallography, X-ray fluorescence, neutron imaging, blade typology, residue analysis, or conservation examination has been performed on a recognized Tancred-Caliburn.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Direct object associations

- **Arthur — legendary owner.** Geoffrey, Wace, and later romances make Caliburnus/Escalibor Arthur’s sword. Roger’s attribution to Arthur supplies the 1191 object’s entire exceptional value.
- **Richard I of England — documented reported donor, March 1191.** Roger says Richard gave the sword to Tancred at Catania. How Richard obtained it is unknown.
- **Tancred of Lecce, king of Sicily — documented reported recipient, March 1191.** No later source establishes what he did with it.
- **Roger of Howden — near-contemporary recorder.** Roger accompanied Richard’s crusading expedition and is the sole controlling source for the transfer. His earlier and revised formulations show the sword participating in the Anglicization of Arthur.
- **Joan of England, dowager queen of Sicily — political context.** Her detention and withheld property helped cause Richard’s confrontation with Tancred. She is not recorded as touching or owning the sword.
- **Arthur of Brittany — dynastic context.** Richard recognized his nephew as heir; the proposed marriage to Tancred’s daughter made Arthurian naming unusually resonant. His eventual inheritance of the sword is scholarly speculation.
- **Philip II Augustus — diplomatic context.** His rivalry with Richard and communications with Tancred shaped the Catania negotiations. He has no recorded custody of the sword.
- **Henry VI and Constance of Sicily — possible but unproven later custodial context.** Their conquest ended Tancred’s regime. No source says they acquired the sword.

### Authors who transformed the object without possessing it

- **Geoffrey of Monmouth, c. 1136–1138:** established *Caliburnus* as Arthur’s Avalon-made sword in influential Latin pseudo-history.
- **Wace, 1155:** transmitted it into Anglo-Norman literature as *Escalibur* and elaborated its cutting reputation.
- **Chrétien de Troyes, later twelfth century:** helped integrate the sword into courtly romance, sometimes associating it with Gawain.
- **Authors of the Vulgate and Post-Vulgate cycles, thirteenth century:** developed the lake, scabbard, and return narratives.
- **Thomas Malory, fifteenth century:** synthesized French and English traditions.
- **William Caxton, 1485:** printed Malory, enormously extending the later English form of the tradition.
- **Alfred Tennyson, 1859–1885:** made the modern English spelling and lake-return imagery culturally dominant through *Idylls of the King*.
- **John Boorman, 1981:** visually consolidated the shining magical sword, stone, lake, and sovereignty traditions in the film *Excalibur*.

### Institutions and networks

- **The Angevin/Plantagenet court:** the setting in which British legendary history could be converted into dynastic capital.
- **The Norman Sicilian monarchy:** recipient institution and likely immediate repository, although no treasury record survives.
- **Glastonbury Abbey:** institution associated with Arthur’s alleged bones, but not demonstrably with Richard’s sword.
- **Third Crusade transport and diplomatic networks:** ships, money, hostages, marriage agreements, and symbolic gifts surrounded the object’s only documented movement.
- **Medieval chroniclers and romance manuscript networks:** the principal custodians not of the metal sword, but of its transferable identity.

### Supernatural beings

- **Lady of the Lake:** donor or custodian of Excalibur in later French and Malorian traditions; absent from Roger and Geoffrey.
- **Avalon:** in Geoffrey, the place of manufacture; in later tradition, a supernatural or otherworldly island associated with Arthur’s departure.
- **Morgan le Fay:** linked to Avalon and, in later narratives, to the theft or disposal of Excalibur’s protective scabbard. She is not connected to the 1191 transfer.
- **Weland/Wayland:** a legendary smith proposed in some comparative or dynastic reconstructions, but not named by Roger or Geoffrey as Caliburn’s maker.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Literary function

Caliburn/Excalibur functions as:

- an attribute of Arthur’s kingship;
- an instrument of extraordinary martial force;
- proof of election or legitimacy in sword-in-the-stone traditions;
- a gift from an otherworldly authority in lake traditions;
- a paired relic with a protective scabbard;
- a token whose return marks the end of Arthur’s reign and withdrawal to Avalon.

The famous principle that possession of the sword legitimates kingship is primarily the product of later stone and lake narratives, not Geoffrey’s earliest account.

### Function in the 1191 exchange

The reported presentation was a diplomatic ceremony rather than a liturgical relic rite. Its likely functions included:

- sealing reconciliation after armed conflict at Messina;
- honoring Tancred as a royal equal;
- giving an object whose symbolic value exceeded ordinary monetary valuation;
- invoking a prestigious British past during negotiations concerning Arthur of Brittany;
- demonstrating Richard’s magnificence and freedom from attachment to luxury goods;
- obtaining or accompanying urgently needed maritime resources for crusade.

Anne E. Lester interprets the presentation as a gift that retained prestige for the donor even after transfer—a form of “keeping while giving.” In this reading, Richard relinquished the material sword while displaying that he possessed, authenticated, and could bestow the most famous weapon of Britain ([*Living by the Sword*](https://dokumen.pub/living-by-the-sword-weapons-and-material-culture-in-france-and-britain-6001600-9781501752148.html)).

### Actual use

No source records:

- Richard fighting with the sword;
- Tancred wearing it;
- the sword being drawn during treaty oaths;
- coronation use in Sicily;
- liturgical blessing;
- veneration, pilgrimage, or healing;
- its display before troops;
- its employment in a later battle.

Descriptions of Richard “taking Excalibur on crusade” are inferential. He took the object as far as Sicily if Roger’s account is accepted, but the source does not say he bore it as his personal combat weapon.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### Avalon manufacture

**Early literary tradition:** Geoffrey of Monmouth, c. 1136–1138.

The sword is forged or made on Avalon. No maker is named. Later retellings make Avalon a fairy island and sometimes connect manufacture with supernatural women or smiths. Those are elaborations.

### The unparalleled cutting blade

**Twelfth-century elaboration:** Wace.

Wace explains the name through the claim that the sword can cut iron, steel, and wood. This helped turn the sword’s proper name into a statement about its power, but it is not a physical report concerning Richard’s object.

### The sword in the stone

**Later medieval romance tradition.**

Arthur proves his right to rule by drawing a sword that no rival can remove. In many modern retellings that sword is automatically Excalibur. Medieval narratives are less uniform: Malory’s first sword breaks, and Arthur subsequently receives Excalibur from the Lady of the Lake.

### The Lady of the Lake and magical scabbard

**Thirteenth-century French cycle development.**

The Lady gives Arthur the sword; the scabbard prevents blood loss or otherwise protects the wearer. Morgan steals or causes the loss of the scabbard in several traditions. The sword’s blade consequently became more famous, although within the story the scabbard could be the more valuable object.

### Return to the water

**Later medieval tradition.**

After Arthur is mortally wounded, a surviving knight is ordered to cast the sword into water. A hand receives it. French sources often assign the act to Girflet; Malory assigns it to Bedivere. The episode supplies a narrative ending that Roger’s physical sword conspicuously lacks.

### Richard recovered the sword from Arthur’s tomb

**Late conflation.**

This story appears to arise from joining two events close in date: Glastonbury’s claimed discovery of Arthur’s grave and the Catania gift. Neither Roger’s sword passage nor the early grave accounts document the connection. Some modern retellings go further and say Richard removed the sword personally or received it from the monks; those details are inventions unless a presently unidentified source can be produced.

### Richard knowingly gave Tancred a fake

**Modern speculation.**

The idea answers an intuitive question—why surrender an authentic wonder—but no source records deception. It should not be presented as fact.

### The sword brought fortune or misfortune

Claims that Richard suffered bad luck after surrendering Excalibur or that Tancred prospered because he received it are modern folkloric constructions. Tancred died in 1194 and his dynasty was overthrown; Richard survived the crusade but endured capture, ransom, war, and death in 1199. Retrospective patterns do not demonstrate a medieval curse tradition.

### Arthur beneath Mount Etna

Sicily possessed its own medieval Arthurian geography. Gervase of Tilbury records a story placing Arthur in a marvelous palace within or around Mount Etna/Mongibello, and Caesarius of Heisterbach transmits another Sicilian Arthur story. Bresc argues that the gift entered a region already capable of localizing Arthur in an otherworldly volcanic landscape. This does not show that Tancred deposited the sword at Etna, but it helps explain why an Arthurian royal relic could carry meaning in Sicily. Italian scholarship discussing these traditions cites Gervase’s description of Arthur reclining in a marvelous palace at Mongibello ([Italian discussion and primary-text quotation](https://www.aicc-nazionale.com/app/download/32072108/Atene%2Be%2BRoma%2B2007.pdf)).

## 8. Current Status and Location

**Status:** lost or unidentifiable.

**Last reported location:** Catania, Kingdom of Sicily, in Tancred’s hands or immediate royal custody, March 1191.

**Last secure custodian:** Tancred, according to Roger of Howden.

**Present controller:** none known.

**Accession number:** none.

**Public display:** none.

**Scientific testing:** none.

**Legal or repatriation dispute:** none documented, because no candidate object has been identified.

**Surviving components:** none known—not blade, hilt, scabbard, fitting, inscription, reliquary, inventory label, drawing, cast, or contemporary image.

No serious evidence places it beneath Glastonbury, in a Sicilian royal treasury, at Palermo, at Catania, in the Hohenstaufen regalia, in the Imperial Treasury, or at Mount Etna. Those locations are either former contextual possibilities or legendary associations.

The appropriate catalog-style status is:

> **Object not located; identity and survival unverified; documentary provenance terminates with the reported Catania transfer of March 1191.**

## 9. Modern Reception

The specific Richard–Tancred object occupies a comparatively small but persistent branch of Excalibur’s modern reception.

### Historical writing

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century histories usually repeated Roger straightforwardly, sometimes calling the object “the famous Excalibur.” T. A. Archer’s 1912 translation anachronistically glossed Caliburn as “Excalibur, as it is called in the *Idylls of the King*,” showing how Tennyson’s vocabulary shaped the translation of a twelfth-century source.

Modern biographies divide in tone:

- some state that Richard gave Tancred Excalibur;
- some cautiously say “a sword said to be Excalibur”;
- others say Richard “claimed” it was Excalibur;
- popular accounts increasingly connect it to Glastonbury without primary evidence.

The shift from Roger’s unqualified identity statement to modern “claimed” language reflects changed standards of historicity, not additional documentary discovery.

### Political and cultural interpretation

Modern scholars have treated the gift as evidence for:

- Plantagenet appropriation of British legendary history;
- Richard’s courtly and chivalric self-fashioning;
- Anglo-Norman transmission of Arthurian material to Sicily;
- symbolic diplomacy between two recently hostile kings;
- connections between Arthur of Brittany’s proposed Sicilian marriage and the relic’s possible dynastic future;
- the transformation of a literary object into transferable material culture.

Martin Aurell’s study of Plantagenet ideology notes both diplomatic and darker prophetic explanations: Richard may have used the object to seal the Sicilian alliance, while some interpretations suggest he was shedding an ominous Arthurian inheritance. Aurell correctly presents these as interpretations rather than an established motive ([Presses universitaires de Rennes](https://books.openedition.org/pur/22103?lang=fr)).

### Popular inaccuracies

Several claims now circulate widely but lack primary support:

- the sword was excavated from Arthur’s Glastonbury tomb;
- Richard personally found it;
- the grave discovery certainly preceded the gift;
- the sword was exchanged at the October 1190 Treaty of Messina;
- the gift occurred in 1189;
- Richard received fifteen galleys as a simple purchase price for the sword;
- Tancred used it in battle;
- it disappeared into Mount Etna;
- its loss explains Richard’s later misfortunes.

The incorrect date “6 March 1189,” found in some discussions of Arthurian transmission, combines the wrong year with one variant date for the March meeting.

### Literature, film, games, and replicas

The general Excalibur tradition was transformed by Malory, Tennyson, Howard Pyle, T. H. White, John Boorman’s *Excalibur* (1981), and innumerable fantasy games and films. Those works usually depict the lake or stone sword, not Tancred’s object.

The Richard–Tancred episode appears in historical biographies, Arthurian reference works, online medieval-history writing, role-playing discussions, and modern fictionalizations of Richard. It has particular resonance in Japanese *Fate* fandom because that franchise connects Richard I with imitations or manifestations of Excalibur. Fan-created “Excalibur Lecce” material is a documented modern invention, not medieval tradition.

Commercial replicas labeled “Richard the Lionheart Excalibur” or simply “Caliburn” possess no provenance from the medieval object.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### The controlling primary evidence

The indispensable evidence consists of two related texts:

1. *Gesta Regis Henrici Secundi et Gesta Regis Ricardi*, II, p. 159 in Stubbs’s edition.
2. Roger of Howden’s revised *Chronica*, III, p. 97.

Their relationship matters. The episode is not two independent attestations: Roger reused and revised material. The wording change from Arthur as king of the Britons to king of England is evidence for ideological development, not corroboration by a second witness.

Roger is unusually valuable because he participated in Richard’s crusading expedition and was in the royal orbit. Yet proximity is not omniscience. His detailed report makes the gift credible, but historians must still allow for courtly narrative shaping.

### Major interpretive questions

#### 1. Literal relic or political fiction?

There is no requirement to choose between “real sword” and “symbolic story.” A physical sword can be real while its attributed biography is legendary. That hybrid reading best fits the evidence.

#### 2. Why surrender so potent an emblem?

Proposed answers include:

- reconciliation with Tancred;
- reciprocal generosity;
- payment or honorific compensation for ships;
- endorsement of the Arthur of Brittany marriage project;
- projection rather than abandonment of Richard’s Arthurian prestige;
- avoidance of unfavorable Merlin prophecies;
- lack of special personal attachment to the artifact.

Only the diplomatic setting is documented. The rest are interpretations.

#### 3. Did the gift reject or promote Arthurianism?

Scholars have read the same act in opposite ways. Giving away the relic might signal indifference or rejection; alternatively, only a ruler claiming access to Arthur’s inheritance could bestow it, so the gift broadcast Richard’s command over British legendary capital. Lester’s “keeping while giving” formulation captures the second possibility.

#### 4. Did Caliburn come from Glastonbury?

This remains popular but lacks a documentary bridge. The theory should be treated as unproven and probably chronologically untenable in its strongest form.

#### 5. Did the gift promote Arthur of Brittany?

The nephew’s name, heirship, and planned marriage make the interpretation attractive. Warren’s possible return-by-inheritance theory is elegant but speculative.

#### 6. Was the sword inherited from earlier Angevin rulers?

Bresc explores the possibility through legendary royal swords and the international circulation of prestige weapons. No surviving inventory proves it.

### Essential modern studies

- Henri Bresc, “Excalibur en Sicile,” *Medievalia* 7 (1987), pp. 7–21: the most focused object study; situates the gift within Sicily’s Arthurian traditions and surveys provenance hypotheses.
- John Gillingham’s work on Richard and Roger of Howden: important for the chronicler’s authority and crusading setting.
- Martin Aurell, studies of Plantagenet ideology: treats Arthurian culture as political language.
- Michelle R. Warren, work on Roger of Howden and British/English historiographical identity: emphasizes textual revision and dynastic implications.
- Christopher Michael Berard, *Arthurianism in Early Plantagenet England*: analyzes Richard’s reign and the diplomatic use of the sword.
- Anne E. Lester, *Living by the Sword*: interprets the gift as material and symbolic exchange.
- Rachel Bromwich and D. Simon Evans, edition and study of *Culhwch and Olwen*: fundamental for Caledfwlch’s Welsh textual history.
- Neil Wright and Michael D. Reeve’s work on Geoffrey of Monmouth’s textual transmission: essential for the Latin *Caliburnus* tradition.

### State of the evidence

The field has abundant literary evidence but only one dependent chronicle tradition for the physical transfer. No relevant artifact, medieval inventory, image, or scientific report has emerged. Consequently, scholarship can reconstruct the cultural mechanics of the gift far more fully than the sword’s material biography.

### Final assessment

The “Caliburn” given to Tancred has a sharply bounded documentary life. It appears once, vividly, in Roger of Howden’s account of a royal gift exchange and then vanishes. The source makes a physical sword probable, but everything material about it—age, maker, metal, decoration, prior history, and subsequent fate—is unknown. Its asserted identity came from a literary tradition already at least half a century old in Latin and older in Welsh form. Richard’s gift represents the point where that textual sword crossed into reported material culture.

The surviving evidence does not permit identification with an artifact now on display, with a sword from Glastonbury, or with any known Sicilian or imperial regalia. Its last reconstructable act is the transfer itself: a sword bearing Arthur’s name passed from Richard to Tancred in Catania in March 1191, functioning simultaneously as weapon, relic, political language, and diplomatic gift. After that moment its metal history is lost; its textual history continued.
