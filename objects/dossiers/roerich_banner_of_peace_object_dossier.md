# The Banner of Peace (Roerich): Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `the_banner_of_peace_roerich_object_dossier.md`  
**Research current to:** 6 September 2026

## Evidentiary note and scope

“The Banner of Peace” is not one unique relic with an unbroken material biography. It is, first, a **modern graphic emblem and flag design** devised within Nicholas Roerich’s campaign for an international cultural-protection treaty; second, the **distinctive flag legally specified in the 1935 Roerich Pact**; and third, a potentially unlimited family of cloth flags, paintings, printed images, badges, institutional signs, and ceremonial replicas bearing that design.

Accordingly, no defensible single chain of custody exists for “the Banner” as a whole. This dossier keeps four things separate:

1. the abstract design;
2. the treaty’s annexed official model;
3. individually documented flags and artworks bearing the emblem;
4. claims that the design reproduces an ancient universal sacred sign.

The physical biographies of early ceremonial banners are incompletely published. In particular, accessible catalogues do not provide textile analyses, dimensions, maker’s names, or accession numbers for the flag blessed at Bruges in 1931, the New York “world pilgrimage” banner dedicated that December, or most later expeditionary flags. Absence of those details must not be concealed by treating photographs of similar flags as proof that they are identical objects.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Object class

The Banner of Peace is simultaneously:

- a **distinctive protective flag** under an international treaty;
- an **emblem** of the Roerich Pact and associated “Pax Cultura” movement;
- a modern spiritual and cultural symbol in Roerich organizations;
- a design reproduced on numerous independently manufactured flags and objects.

It is not a crown, relic, weapon, or unique cult object. Its closest legal analogues are the Red Cross emblem and, after 1954, the Hague Convention’s Blue Shield.

### 1.2 Names and languages

Documented names include:

- **English:** *Banner of Peace*, *Roerich Banner of Peace*, *Peace Banner*, *Flag of Culture*, *Banner of Protection*.
- **Russian:** **Знамя Мира**, *Znamia Mira* or *Znamya Mira*, literally “Banner of Peace” or “Banner of the World.” Russian **мир**, *mir*, can mean both “peace” and “world,” enabling a secondary wordplay especially noticeable in accounts of the flag aboard the space station *Mir*.
- **French:** *Bannière de Paix* or *Bannière de la Paix*.
- **Spanish:** *Bandera de la Paz*.
- **Portuguese:** *Bandeira da Paz*.
- **Italian:** *Bandiera della Pace*.
- **Latin-associated motto:** **Pax Cultura**, variously translated “Peace through Culture,” “Cultural Peace,” or “Peace of Culture.” This phrase is frequently printed beside or beneath the emblem but is not required by Article III of the treaty.

The treaty itself does not call the sign *Pax Cultura*. It speaks simply of a “distinctive flag.” “Roerich Pact” is an informal name for the treaty, not its formal title.

### 1.3 Canonical design

Article III of the 1935 treaty specifies:

> “a distinctive flag (red circle with a triple red sphere in the circle on a white background) in accordance with the model attached to this treaty.”

The authentic Spanish formulation is *una bandera distintiva (círculo rojo, con una triple esfera roja dentro del círculo, sobre un fondo blanco)*. The treaty was executed in English, Spanish, Portuguese, and French. Its legal specification fixes:

- a white ground;
- an outer red ring or circumference;
- three solid red circular forms inside it;
- their triangular grouping, as shown in the attached model.

It does **not** fix, in its operative text:

- the overall proportions of the flag;
- the exact diameter or spacing of the disks;
- a standardized red pigment;
- a Pantone or other color value;
- textile, weave, stitching, printing process, weight, fringe, finial, or method of attachment;
- the words *Pax Cultura*.

The 1931 movement publication described the color more specifically as **amaranth**—a purplish or deep reddish hue—on white. Modern copies range from scarlet through dark red and magenta. These differences do not establish rival originals; they reflect the treaty’s lack of technical color and construction standards.

### 1.4 Geometry and orientation

The common form has two disks above and one below, all within an unbroken ring. Some reproductions reverse that orientation or vary the distances. The annexed treaty model, rather than later commercial artwork, is controlling for treaty interpretation.

Popular descriptions sometimes say “three circles in a circle.” Geometrically, the external form is normally a ring, while the internal forms are filled disks. The English treaty’s “triple red sphere” is pictorial rather than three-dimensional language.

### 1.5 Surviving related works and material exemplars

#### A. Treaty model and original treaty instrument

The closest thing to an official “original” is the model attached to the original multilateral treaty. The Organization of American States identifies itself as depositary of the **original instrument and ratifications**, under OAS Treaty Series C-3/No. 33. The physical treaty and annex therefore belong to the OAS diplomatic archive in Washington, D.C., not to any Roerich museum.

The published United States version is Treaty Series No. 899. No accessible source consulted for this dossier supplies conservation data, paper dimensions, ink analysis, or an archival item-level call number for the annex.

#### B. *Madonna Oriflamma* (1932)

Nicholas Roerich’s most important early painted embodiment of the flag is *Madonna Oriflamma*. It is not itself a cloth Banner of Peace, but an authoritative visual interpretation made during the campaign.

The Nicholas Roerich Museum catalogue records:

- date: 1932;
- medium: tempera on canvas;
- dimensions: 173.5 × 99.4 cm (68¼ × 39¼ in.);
- unsigned;
- stretcher inscription: “№ 17. MADONNA ORIFLAMMA. / N.ROERICH”;
- later label: “Property of Svetoslav Roerich 1959”;
- museum record number: **700101**.

The image shows a haloed female figure holding the white banner with the red triune emblem. Its documented custody is:

1. Foundation Roerich Pro Pace, Arte et Scientiae, Bruges—loan, 1932;
2. Les Amis de Bruges—loan, 1947;
3. Svetoslav Roerich—by descent;
4. Nicholas Roerich Museum, New York—on loan from 1960;
5. acquired by that museum in 1984.

This is the best-catalogued early material object associated with the Banner, but it should not be mislabeled “the original flag.”

#### C. Bruges ceremonial banner

A contemporary cable reproduced in the *Roerich Museum Bulletin* reports that during the first Bruges conference in September 1931 a “Roerich Peace Banner” was taken in procession to the Basilica of the Holy Blood, blessed, and “crowned” with the arms of Bruges. This establishes the existence of at least one physical ceremonial banner. Its maker, materials, dimensions, subsequent custody, and present whereabouts have not been established in the accessible record.

#### D. New York “world pilgrimage” banner

The Nicholas Roerich Museum archive lists a program titled *Dedication of the Roerich Banner of Peace for Its World Pilgrimage*, dated 27 December 1931. This proves an event and probably another material banner, but the published catalogue does not establish whether it was identical to the Bruges flag, where it traveled, or where it survives.

#### E. Expeditionary and space-flight flags

Several later cloth exemplars have separate biographies:

- a flag taken to the North Pole by Fyodor Konyukhov, reported as 6 May 1989 in the more detailed Russian chronology, although some later summaries say 1988;
- a Banner carried aboard *Mir* from February to August 1990;
- one or more Banners installed aboard *Mir* during the 1997–1999 project;
- a Banner raised at the South Pole on 8 January 2000;
- further flags taken to mountain summits, given to political leaders, or displayed by Roerich organizations.

Institutional sources sometimes shift between singular and plural. One Russian account explicitly says that **copies** or “examples” associated with *Mir* went to different recipients. They cannot all be the same cloth object.

### 1.6 Inscriptions

The core emblem has no required inscription. *Pax Cultura* is an accompanying motto rather than an integral part of Article III’s legal device. Later flags may add names, dates, institutional text, borders, or presentation inscriptions. Such additions create identifiable variants but do not alter the abstract emblem.

### 1.7 Condition, repair, and scientific analysis

No textile-conservation report, fiber analysis, dye analysis, radiocarbon study, or technical examination of an alleged prototype flag was located. No published evidence supports dating any surviving cloth Banner to 1928–1929 through laboratory methods. The design’s authenticity is documentary and graphic, not archaeometric.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Intellectual prehistory

Roerich had advocated protection of cultural monuments before devising the Banner. Movement chronologies place a significant appeal in 1915, when he reportedly addressed Emperor Nicholas II and Grand Duke Nicholas Nikolaevich concerning state protection for Russian cultural treasures. That episode belongs to the genealogy of the protection program; it is not evidence that the three-disk Banner already existed.

The destruction of cultural property during the First World War—regularly exemplified in Roerich publications by Reims Cathedral and the Library of Louvain—supplied the campaign’s principal historical argument.

### 2.2 Drafting date: 1928 or 1929

Two closely related datings circulate:

- A published reproduction identifies a **formal draft prepared by Georges Chklaver in August 1928**, “originated by Nicholas Roerich.”
- Chklaver’s foreword to the 1931 *Roerich Banner of Peace* says the international pact draft “was devised in the summer of 1929” and then presented to the U.S. Department of State and the League of Nations’ International Institute of Intellectual Cooperation.
- The Nicholas Roerich Museum archive cautiously catalogues a draft “1929(?)” and preserves Chklaver’s letter to George Roerich of 3 December 1929.

The safest reconstruction is that Roerich and Chklaver developed the proposal across **1928–1929**, with a formal version dated August 1928 in later publication and active international circulation documented in 1929. Sources should not be harmonized into an exact “birthday” unsupported by a securely catalogued original sketch.

### 2.3 Designer and legal collaborators

**Documented attribution:** Nicholas Roerich originated the emblematic and policy project. Jurist **Georges G. Chklaver**—also transliterated George Chklaver, Shklyaver, or Chklaver—gave the proposal legal form. **Albert Geouffre de La Pradelle**, an international lawyer, is identified in later accounts as an adviser in its formulation.

The textile makers of the first flags remain unidentified. No commission invoice, workshop record, fabric source, or cost has been published in the sources located.

### 2.4 Earliest public record

The museum archive identifies:

- a Buenos Aires pamphlet, *La Defensa del Mundo Cultural: El Pacto Roerich y la Bandera de la Paz*, dated 1929;
- the draft pact, dated 1929 with uncertainty;
- Chklaver’s correspondence of December 1929;
- a *New York Times* story of 16 March 1930, “Special Flag Is Suggested to Protect Art Treasures”;
- Roerich’s French article “The Banner of Peace,” 1930;
- another *New York Times* report, “To Protect Art by Neutral Flag,” August 1930.

The 3 March 1929 *New York Times* article often cited in Roerich chronologies concerns Roerich’s Nobel Peace Prize nomination. It does not by itself prove the final Banner design. The Nobel archive confirms that Chklaver nominated Roerich in 1929.

### 2.5 What the earliest proposal meant

The Banner was conceived as the cultural counterpart of the Red Cross:

- institutions and monuments would be registered;
- a neutral protective flag would identify them;
- combatants would respect the marked property and personnel;
- protection would operate in peace as well as war.

The project therefore began as a functional international marker, not merely a personal heraldic emblem or devotional image.

### 2.6 Claimed ancient ancestry

Roerich and later supporters assembled images of threefold circular forms from many periods and cultures. Frequently invoked analogues include:

- prehistoric three-dot motifs;
- tamgas and seals;
- the Buddhist *triratna* or Three Jewels;
- three stones or jewels in Christian art;
- the Trinity;
- Andrei Rublev’s *Trinity*;
- the threefold pattern on depictions of Christ or the Virgin;
- three circles associated with past, present, and future.

These are **comparanda**, not a chain of descent. Similar geometry is widespread and can arise independently. No cited archaeological or art-historical study demonstrates a continuous transmission from a Stone Age object, Buddhist emblem, Rublev icon, or Central Asian tamga to Roerich’s 1928–1929 design.

Roerich’s own explanations are nevertheless primary evidence for the meaning he assigned it. He interpreted the three forms as either:

- **Art, Science, and Religion** within the encompassing circle of Culture; or
- **past, present, and future** within Eternity.

The coexistence of these explanations was deliberate symbolic polyvalence, not necessarily a contradiction.

### 2.7 Later esoteric origin claims

Some modern Roerich-oriented interpretations connect the emblem with Shambhala, the Chintamani stone, the Mahatmas, or fragments of a celestial treasure. These are **late esoteric readings**, not provisions of the pact and not documented sources for the graphic design’s manufacture. The claim that Roerich simply copied one specific ancient object remains unproved.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because the Banner is reproducible, the relevant provenance consists of several parallel chains.

### 3.1 The design and treaty model

**1928–1929 — Roerich/Chklaver formulation.**  
The design and draft circulated through the Roerich Museum network, the U.S. government, and international cultural organizations.

**1930 — publication and committee circulation.**  
The proposal was printed and distributed to governments, universities, museums, and cultural bodies. Committees operated in New York, Paris, and Bruges.

**13–15 September 1931 — Bruges.**  
The first international conference considered the pact. A contemporary movement cable reports a procession, blessing of a physical Peace Banner in the Basilica of the Holy Blood, and its association with the Bruges coat of arms. Custody after the event is undocumented.

**27 December 1931 — New York and associated observances.**  
The New York archive records dedication of a Roerich Banner “for its World Pilgrimage.” Movement sources report related church services. Whether this banner survives is unresolved.

**9 August 1932 — second Bruges conference.**  
Seventeen countries reportedly participated. *Madonna Oriflamma* was loaned to the Roerich foundation in Bruges that year and functioned as a painted icon of the movement.

**17–18 November 1933 — Washington conference.**  
A third international convention promoted worldwide adoption. The Banner operated here as a conference emblem rather than a transferred unique artifact.

**16 December 1933 — Montevideo resolution.**  
The Seventh International Conference of American States recommended that American governments sign the pact. The resolution described the objective as universal adoption of a flag “already designed and generally known.”

**15 April 1935 — White House signature.**  
Plenipotentiaries of all twenty-one American republics then represented in the Pan-American Union signed the treaty in Franklin D. Roosevelt’s presence. Henry A. Wallace signed for the United States. Article III incorporated the distinctive flag and attached model into treaty law.

**1935 onward — diplomatic custody.**  
The Pan-American Union, succeeded by the Organization of American States, became depositary of the original treaty and ratifications. This is the clearest continuous institutional custody in the Banner’s history.

### 3.2 Treaty signature versus ratification

All twenty-one republics signed on 15 April 1935, but signature did not make every country a party. The OAS record shows ratification/deposit for:

- United States: ratified 10 July and deposited 13 July 1935;
- Cuba: deposited 26 August 1935;
- El Salvador: 1 May 1936;
- Brazil: 5 August 1936;
- Chile: 8 September 1936;
- Guatemala: 16 September 1936;
- Mexico: 2 October 1936;
- Dominican Republic: 2 November 1936;
- Venezuela: 11 November 1936;
- Colombia: 20 February 1937.

Honduras records ratification on 27 January 1936 but no deposit date in the OAS table. Argentina, Bolivia, Costa Rica, Ecuador, Haiti, Nicaragua, Panama, Paraguay, Peru, and Uruguay signed but show no ratification deposit. Thus the recurring statement that “twenty-one countries adopted the Pact” is accurate only as to signature, not binding ratification.

The treaty entered into force on **26 August 1935**, upon deposit of the second ratification.

### 3.3 *Madonna Oriflamma*

The painting’s unusually well-documented physical chain is given in §1.5. It moved from India or Roerich’s working custody into the Bruges movement’s exhibition network, then by inheritance to Svetoslav Roerich, and finally to the Nicholas Roerich Museum in New York. No theft or wartime destruction is reported in its catalogue entry.

### 3.4 The 1990 *Mir* Banner

The flag was reportedly transferred by Daniel Entin of the Nicholas Roerich Museum to a Soviet commission studying Roerich’s scientific and cultural heritage. Cosmonauts **Aleksandr Balandin** and **Anatoly Solovyev** carried it on Soyuz TM-9 to *Mir* on 11 February 1990. Institutional accounts state:

- it remained aboard until August 1990, approximately 179 days;
- it was exposed outside the station for nine days;
- during that interval it made 144 Earth orbits;
- a televised communication took place beneath it on 25 March 1990.

A display text later described an object in the Moscow Banner of Peace Hall as a **copy of the Banner that had been in open space**, not unambiguously the flown original. That distinction matters. A later claim that “the banner from the space station” was presented to the Indian Parliament in 2004 may refer to another flown example or presentation copy.

### 3.5 The 1997–1999 *Mir* project

A separate Banner was launched to *Mir* on **5 August 1997** for an international educational project. It remained associated with successive crews. Talgat Musabayev, Nikolai Budarin, and Yuri Baturin returned a Banner aboard Soyuz TM-27; one ceremony account dates the return to 25 August 1998, while movement chronologies often give the total project as continuing until 28 August 1999. This is an unresolved chronological inconsistency, possibly caused by several flags or by conflating the object’s return with the project’s formal end.

Cosmonauts and project representatives later presented the returned flag to the International Centre of the Roerichs for “perpetual custody.” It was displayed in the Centre-Museum’s Banner of Peace Hall in Moscow.

### 3.6 Distributed space-associated copies

An institutional account states that examples associated with *Mir* were:

- kept in the Nicholas Roerich Museum/International Centre collection;
- presented to Moscow mayor Yuri Luzhkov;
- presented to Kazakhstan president Nursultan Nazarbayev on 5 January 1999, with Alexei Leonov and Sergei Kapitsa participating;
- in the Kazakhstan case, placed in the presidential gifts museum;
- presented in 2004 to Somnath Chatterjee, Speaker of India’s Lok Sabha.

These multiple destinations demonstrate that “the Banner that went to space” became a class of flown, carried, displayed, or commemorative flags rather than one uniquely tracked object.

### 3.7 Polar flags

**North Pole:** movement chronologies disagree between 1988 and 6 May 1989 for Konyukhov’s raising of the Banner. The more precise date should be used provisionally, with the year conflict noted rather than suppressed.

**South Pole:** a Banner was raised on 8 January 2000 by the “Toward the Twenty-First Century” expedition. A related flag was presented on 28 January to UN Secretary-General Kofi Annan. Sources do not prove whether the flag handed to Annan was the exact cloth raised at the Pole or a companion example.

The former Moscow museum display stated that the North- and South-Pole Banners were kept in its hall. Their present inventory status after the 2017 closure and displacement of that museum has not been independently established.

### 3.8 Moscow museum rupture, 2017

The International Centre of the Roerichs operated its public museum in the Lopukhin estate until 2017. Following police and state actions connected with disputes over the Centre’s premises and works acquired through former Master Bank chairman Boris Bulochnik, the Centre was evicted and the site transferred to state control.

The Centre states that the Banner flying over the estate was removed during the night of 30 April–1 May 2017. Contemporary art reporting confirms that the Banner on the flagpole was replaced by the Russian tricolor. This was removal of a particular outdoor flag, not destruction or legal extinction of the design.

The subsequent custody and item-level inventory status of the Centre’s flown space and polar flags remain unclear in publicly accessible sources. Claims by either the Centre or Russian state institutions about the contents of displaced collections require confirmation from inventories or court records.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Is there an “original Banner”?

There is no documented unique prototype equivalent to a regimental color or miraculous relic. The strongest candidates for “original” status are different kinds of object:

- Roerich’s lost or unpublished first graphic sketch, if one survives;
- the design in the 1928–1929 draft;
- the first manufactured ceremonial flag;
- the Bruges flag blessed in 1931;
- the New York pilgrimage banner;
- the model attached to the 1935 treaty;
- Roerich’s 1932 painted interpretation in *Madonna Oriflamma*.

Each has a different claim. Only the treaty model possesses formal intergovernmental authority.

### 4.2 Ancient-symbol claim

The basic geometry certainly predates Roerich in unrelated artifacts. What is not established is that those earlier forms bore the name “Banner of Peace,” carried Roerich’s cultural-protection meaning, or formed a continuous tradition inherited by him.

Therefore:

- **documented fact:** comparable triadic-circle designs are older than Roerich;
- **documented modern creation:** the name, color arrangement, cultural-protection program, and treaty function crystallized in the Roerich campaign;
- **institutional/spiritual tradition:** Roerich recovered a universal primordial sign;
- **unproved:** descent from any one ancient prototype.

### 4.3 Variants and counterfeit status

A red rather than amaranth copy is not automatically a forgery because the treaty says “red.” A flag with *Pax Cultura* is an expanded movement version, not the bare treaty device. A gold-on-purple version used by the proposed World League of Culture is a related organizational emblem, not Article III’s white-and-red flag.

No documented commercial forgery of an alleged 1930s original was located. The greater authentication problem is **misidentification through imprecise captions**—calling a modern replica “the Banner,” or describing a display copy as the object exposed in space.

### 4.4 Trademark controversy

On 1 November 2002 the International Centre of the Roerichs registered graphic and verbal forms of “Знамя Мира” in Russia as trademarks, reportedly registrations **226539** and **226540**. The Centre said registration protected the symbol from improper or commercial misuse and implemented the Roerich family’s wishes.

Opponents within the wider Roerich movement argued that a universal cultural-protection emblem, incorporated into an international treaty, should not become the controlled mark of one organization. The Centre maintained a commission regulating use of its marks and renewed registrations.

Trademark status does not establish authorship of a particular cloth flag, ownership of the OAS treaty emblem worldwide, or a power to amend the 1935 treaty. It concerns specified commercial/service uses within the registration’s jurisdiction and classes.

### 4.5 Replacement under the 1954 Hague Convention

The International Committee of the Red Cross explains that, among states bound by both instruments, Article 36(2) of the 1954 Hague Convention supplements the Roerich Pact and the Hague **Blue Shield replaces the Roerich distinctive flag**.

Thus the Banner remains historically and, for the Roerich Pact, textually authentic, but it is no longer the operative conflict-marking emblem between states jointly bound by the later convention. Modern ceremonial display should not be confused with a current assertion of special protection under international humanitarian law.

### 4.6 Authority to authenticate

Authority is divided:

- **Nicholas Roerich and his early committees:** authorship and movement meaning;
- **Pan-American Union/OAS:** authentic treaty text, annexed model, signatures, and ratifications;
- **states parties:** lawful designation of protected institutions under the pact;
- **UNESCO and states parties to the 1954 Convention:** present cultural-property protection regime and Blue Shield;
- **museums:** identification and provenance of particular paintings and flags;
- **Rospatent and courts:** trademark rights in Russia;
- **Roerich organizations:** ceremonial and spiritual lineage, but not exclusive treaty interpretation.

### 5.3 Sacred figures

*Madonna Oriflamma* places the Banner in the hands of a Marian or World-Mother figure. The figure is an artistic-symbolic bearer, not a documented historical custodian.

Roerich literature relates the triad to:

- the Christian Trinity;
- the Buddhist Three Jewels;
- Sergius of Radonezh and Rublev’s *Trinity*;
- the Mother of the World;
- Shambhala and, in later esoteric accounts, the Mahatmas.

These are theological or symbolic associations. The 1935 treaty invokes no deity and makes adherence independent of religion.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Protective function

Under the original proposal and Article III, the flag identifies monuments and institutions intended to receive neutrality, protection, and respect. Article IV requires governments to send the Pan-American Union lists of protected sites; Article V removes privilege if a site is used for military purposes.

The Banner itself does not create immunity by private display alone. Treaty protection depends on state participation, designation, and compliance.

### 6.2 Peace-time function

The pact unusually required protection in peace as well as war. Roerich presented neglect, demolition, ignorance, and inadequate cataloguing as threats alongside bombardment. The Banner therefore became an educational and preservation emblem, not merely a battlefield marker.

### 6.3 Documented ceremonies

- **September 1931, Bruges:** procession, blessing, and association with the Basilica of the Holy Blood and Bruges arms.
- **27 December 1931:** dedication for “world pilgrimage,” with related services reported in churches.
- **1932 and 1933 conferences:** conference emblem and focus of addresses.
- **15 April 1935:** emblem publicly linked to the White House treaty ceremony.
- **1990 and 1997–1999:** displayed aboard *Mir* as part of educational-cultural projects.
- **1995:** presented to the Russian State Duma according to movement chronicles.
- **1999:** ceremonial presentation to Kazakhstan’s president.
- **2000:** polar raising and presentation to the UN secretary-general.
- **2004:** presentation to the Indian Parliament.
- numerous later municipal, school, museum, and “City of Peace” presentations in the Americas and elsewhere.

### 6.4 Taboos and access

The treaty establishes no taboo against touching or reproducing the flag. Movement rhetoric often calls it sacred and urges morally appropriate use. The International Centre’s trademark policy attempted to regulate use, but that is institutional and legal control, not an original ritual prohibition.

### 6.5 Political and legitimating role

Possession of a cloth Banner confers no sovereignty. Its political function is reputational: recipients present themselves as protectors of culture and peace. The White House signing gave the emblem diplomatic legitimacy; space and polar journeys supplied later prestige and quasi-relic biographies.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 “The oldest universal symbol”

**Story:** the emblem has existed since remote prehistory and appears in all great cultures.

**Earliest relevant layer:** Roerich’s own 1930s essays and movement publications assembled parallels.

**Assessment:** individual parallels may be real; the assertion of one continuous, universally understood sign is not demonstrated. The Banner of Peace as named emblem and legal marker is modern.

### 7.2 Rublev’s *Trinity* as source

**Story:** Roerich derived the three circles from Andrei Rublev’s icon or from three angels representing the Trinity.

**Evidence:** Roerich invoked the Trinity and discussed the antiquity of the Byzantine-Christian conception in a 1932 letter. However, no securely reproduced preliminary sketch or unequivocal contemporary statement proves that Rublev’s icon alone generated the design.

**Classification:** plausible spiritual/artistic influence; unproved exclusive source.

### 7.3 Chintamani and the three fragments

**Story:** the enclosing circle represents the legendary Chintamani stone and the inner disks three fragments traveling through the world in times of crisis.

**Classification:** later esoteric interpretation. It does not appear in Article III and should not be retrojected into the legal design without a dated primary citation.

### 7.4 The flag as supernatural protection

Roerich literature sometimes speaks as though raising the Banner spiritually dissolves the “field of war.” This expresses aspirational or esoteric belief. There is no documented combat test showing that a site was spared because belligerents recognized the Banner under the pact.

### 7.5 Cosmic Banner

The *Mir* journeys converted actual textiles into space-flown commemorative objects. Statements that the Banner “circled the Earth” are factual for the carried flags; statements that it thereby created a spiritual shield belong to movement interpretation.

### 7.6 Conflation with the United Nations

Some promotional accounts imply that the Banner is a UN flag or was adopted by the UN. It was not. The UN has its own emblem and flag; the Banner was adopted in a 1935 inter-American treaty whose depositary is the OAS. Presentation of a copy to Kofi Annan did not make it a UN emblem.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 The emblem

The design remains widely reproducible and actively used by Roerich museums, cultural organizations, schools, peace committees, and private adherents.

### 8.2 The treaty and official model

The Roerich Pact remains listed by the ICRC as **in force**. The OAS General Secretariat in Washington is depositary of the original instrument and ratifications. Among states bound by both treaties, the 1954 Hague Blue Shield replaces the Banner’s protective function.

### 8.3 *Madonna Oriflamma*

The Nicholas Roerich Museum’s collection database locates the painting in its collection in New York under record **700101**, acquired in 1984. It is the most securely identified early surviving object that depicts the Banner.

### 8.4 Early ceremonial flags

- Bruges 1931 flag: present location **not established**.
- New York pilgrimage banner: present location **not established**.
- Any original 1928–1929 design sheet: present location **not established in published catalogues**.

### 8.5 Space and polar flags

Published institutional claims place or formerly placed examples in:

- the International Centre of the Roerichs’ Moscow collection;
- a Kazakhstan presidential gifts collection;
- Indian parliamentary custody;
- custody associated with Moscow municipal authorities;
- a presentation to the UN secretary-general.

Item-level accession numbers and current public display status are generally absent. The 2017 loss of the International Centre’s Lopukhin-estate museum premises makes old webpages describing objects “in the hall” unreliable as proof of their location in 2026.

### 8.6 Legal control

No single body owns the underlying emblem globally. Relevant control is fragmented among:

- OAS custody of the treaty model;
- national treaty obligations;
- copyright, if any remains under applicable jurisdiction;
- Russian trademark registrations;
- ownership of particular canvases and textiles;
- institutional rules governing ceremonial presentation.

No current international repatriation claim concerning a unique “original Banner” was located.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Art and reproductions

*Madonna Oriflamma* established the most influential devotional image of the Banner: a hieratic female guardian holding the emblem like an oriflamme. Posters, postcards, badges, medals, books, websites, monuments, school banners, and commercial flags have multiplied this visual language.

“Oriflamme” derives from French *oriflamme*, historically the sacred banner of Saint-Denis and the French kings; in Roerich’s title it elevates the modern peace emblem through the vocabulary of sacred standards.

### 9.2 The Roerich movement

After Roerich’s death in 1947, the Banner remained central to institutions influenced by the Roerichs’ Living Ethics/Agni Yoga philosophy. Its function expanded from cultural-property identification to a general sign of peace, spiritual evolution, education, planetary unity, and “culture” in Roerich’s elevated sense.

### 9.3 Space, poles, and mountains

Late twentieth-century promoters consciously created new object biographies by carrying individual flags to:

- *Mir*;
- the North and South Poles;
- Everest, Elbrus, Belukha, Khan Tengri, and other summits.

These acts did not extend treaty jurisdiction. They transformed particular copies into traveled memorabilia and strengthened the emblem’s planetary imagery.

### 9.4 Governmental and municipal use

Copies have been presented to presidents, parliaments, mayors, universities, and cultural institutions. In parts of Latin America, modern Banner organizations conduct “Ambassador of Peace” and “City of Peace” ceremonies. These are civic campaigns, not equivalent to ratification of the Roerich Pact.

Chile has considered or held parliamentary discussion of commemorating the pact and raising the Banner, demonstrating continued regional political reception.

### 9.5 Trademark and institutional politics

The Russian trademark registration became a fault line within the Roerich movement: protection against misuse to one side, privatization of a universal symbol to the other. The 2017 replacement of the Banner by the Russian tricolor at the former Moscow Centre-Museum likewise acquired symbolic significance within disputes over Roerich heritage and museum control.

### 9.6 Relationship with the Blue Shield

Modern cultural-property professionals generally use the 1954 Hague Blue Shield. Roerich organizations often present the Banner as its moral or historical predecessor. That genealogy is substantially correct, but the symbols are not legally interchangeable.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Primary documentary base

The strongest evidence consists of:

- 1928–1929 draft texts;
- Chklaver correspondence;
- 1930–1931 pamphlets;
- contemporary newspaper coverage;
- Roerich Museum programs and bulletins;
- Bruges conference materials;
- the 1933 Montevideo resolution;
- the 1935 treaty, annexed model, signature record, and ratifications;
- Roerich’s essays and letters;
- museum catalogue records;
- photographs and testimony from later expeditions and space projects.

### 10.2 Institutional archives

The Nicholas Roerich Museum has digitized a large body of campaign literature. This is indispensable but institutionally interested: it preserves the movement’s documentary record while often repeating its universalist and sacred interpretations.

The OAS and U.S. Department of State provide the controlling diplomatic evidence. The ICRC supplies the clearest modern legal statement on the treaty’s relation to the 1954 Hague Convention.

### 10.3 Principal debates

#### Dating

Was the formal pact drafted in August 1928 or summer 1929? The evidence points to development across both years. Exact priority between sketches and legal drafts remains unresolved.

#### “First” treaty

The Pact is often called the first international treaty devoted exclusively to protection of artistic and scientific institutions and historic monuments. It was not the first international law to address cultural property: the Hague Regulations and earlier instruments contained protective provisions. Its distinctiveness lies in its exclusive subject and peace-and-war scope.

#### League of Nations support

Movement sources frequently say League bodies approved the project. Recent scholarship cautions that sympathy, referral, or committee discussion should not be equated with formal League adoption. No European or Asian state ratified the 1935 pact.

#### Ancient origin

The prehistory of individual motifs is real; a continuous universal tradition is not established. Scholarship should distinguish visual analogy from genealogy.

#### Legal efficacy

The treaty created a principled framework but had narrow participation and little documented wartime implementation. Its importance is chiefly normative and genealogical, leading toward the 1954 Hague system.

#### Object identity

The phrase “the Banner” encourages false singularity. Historians must assign identifiers to each cloth flag and distinguish it from photographs, replicas, presentation versions, and the treaty model.

### 10.4 Research gaps

Important unresolved matters include:

- location and catalogue description of Roerich’s first design;
- maker, materials, and fate of the Bruges banner;
- identity of the 1931 New York pilgrimage banner;
- lists of institutions actually registered under Article IV;
- whether any site displayed the Banner during an armed conflict and received documented recognition;
- complete mission manifests and textile identifiers for the *Mir* flags;
- inventories documenting the post-2017 location of the Moscow Centre’s polar and space flags;
- present validity and scope of every Russian trademark registration;
- accession data for flags presented to the UN, Kazakhstan, India, and Moscow.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and official sources

1. **Organization of American States, Treaty C-3: signatories and ratifications.** Controlling record for adoption, depositary, entry into force, and national ratification status.

2. **U.S. Department of State, *Foreign Relations of the United States*, 1935, vol. IV, document 234.** Treaty text, Article III description, plenipotentiaries, and U.S. ratification history.

3. **U.S. Department of State, 1935 diplomatic correspondence.** Records efforts to encourage European adherence and reservations expressed by foreign governments.

4. **International Committee of the Red Cross treaty database.** Current legal status and relation to the 1954 Hague Convention.

5. **Nicholas Roerich Museum digital archive.** Drafts, Chklaver correspondence, conference publications, pamphlets, and contemporary press reprints.

6. **Nicholas Roerich Museum collection database.** Physical description and provenance of *Madonna Oriflamma*.

7. **Roerich’s collected essays and diary leaves.** Primary evidence for his explanations of the symbol and contemporary conference reports.

8. **International Centre of the Roerichs electronic library.** First-person ceremony texts and institutional accounts of the space flags; useful but interested evidence requiring careful singular/plural distinctions.

### Secondary and institutional interpretation

9. **UNESCO and ICRC material on the 1954 Hague Convention.** Modern protective-emblem framework.

10. **Recent scholarship on interwar peace projects.** Corrects overstatements concerning formal League of Nations endorsement.

11. **Roerich movement histories in Russian, English, Spanish, and Portuguese.** Valuable for reception and later custody claims, but frequently repeat unsourced sacred-origin assertions.

### Source limitations

No accessible publication supplied:

- an accession number for a surviving 1928–1931 cloth prototype;
- textile testing or conservation analysis;
- complete Article IV registration lists;
- a reconciled inventory of all space-flown examples;
- post-2017 custody records for all Moscow-held flags.

Consequently, claims of identity among early, space-flown, polar, and presentation flags remain unverified unless separately described above.
