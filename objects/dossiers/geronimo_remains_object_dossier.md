# Geronimo's skull and femurs: Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `geronimos_skull_and_femurs_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary finding

“Geronimo’s skull and femurs” is the name given to an alleged assemblage of human remains and funerary objects said to have been removed from Geronimo’s grave at Fort Sill, Oklahoma, by members of Yale’s Skull and Bones society in May 1918 and carried to the society’s clubhouse—the “Tomb”—in New Haven, Connecticut.

The central conclusion is necessarily divided:

- **Documented:** Geronimo died at Fort Sill on February 17, 1909, and was buried the following day in the Apache prisoner-of-war cemetery at Beef Creek.
- **Documented contemporary allegation:** Private Skull and Bones correspondence from June, August, and September 1918 records that members believed or claimed they had stolen “Geronimo’s” skull. The June letter also names femurs, a bit, and a saddle horn.
- **Probable but unproved event:** The correspondence, military records, and later internal tradition together make some kind of Fort Sill grave robbery by Bonesmen plausible.
- **Unproved identity:** No publicly documented examination, excavation record, photograph with secure provenance, osteological assessment, radiocarbon test, isotope study, or DNA comparison establishes that any Skull and Bones object came from Geronimo.
- **Major contrary evidence:** Geronimo’s grave was unmarked and reportedly unknown to outsiders in 1918; the alleged society history describes entry through an iron door, while Geronimo’s original grave was an ordinary earth burial beneath the open sky. Other Fort Sill graves fit the architectural description better.
- **Present status:** Geronimo’s principal burial remains officially identified at Beef Creek Apache Cemetery. Whether his skeleton is complete has never been established by forensic examination. Skull and Bones and its property-holding corporation have permitted no public inspection of any alleged remains. Yale states that it neither possesses the remains nor controls or has access to the Skull and Bones building.
- **Legal status:** A 2009 repatriation suit was dismissed in 2010 on jurisdictional and statutory grounds. The court did not authenticate the alleged relic, decide that Skull and Bones possessed it, or determine whether Geronimo’s grave had been robbed.

Accordingly, this is not a securely identified relic with a continuous provenance. It is a **contested identity attached to one or more unexamined human-remains objects—if those objects still exist—against a documented background of contemporary boasting, institutional secrecy, inconsistent physical descriptions, and unresolved claims by Geronimo’s descendants.**

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Object class

The alleged assemblage belongs to several overlapping classes:

- human remains;
- contested ancestral remains;
- alleged stolen funerary remains;
- an alleged secret-society trophy or ritual relic;
- associated funerary or equestrian objects;
- and, in public reception, a legendary or conspiratorial object.

It is not a museum accession known to have a catalogue number. No reliable public inventory issued by Yale University, Skull and Bones, or RTA Incorporated identifies an object as Geronimo’s skull or femurs.

### 1.2 Names

The most common English designations are:

- “Geronimo’s skull”;
- “Geronimo’s bones”;
- “Geronimo’s skull and femurs”;
- “the skull of Geronimo”;
- “the Geronimo skull”;
- and, in the June 7, 1918 letter, “the skull of the worthy Geronimo the Terrible.”

The word **Geronimo** is the Mexican/Spanish-derived public name of the Bedonkohe Apache man known in Apache as **Goyaałé**, frequently rendered *Goyathlay*, *Goyahkla*, or *Goyathle* in older English sources. It is commonly translated approximately as “one who yawns.” Because the alleged relic entered public discussion through English-language military, Yale, journalistic, and legal records, there is no independently attested Apache name for the object itself.

“Skull and Bones” is both the common name of the Yale senior society and an unfortunate source of semantic confusion: references to “Geronimo’s skull and bones” may mean particular alleged remains, the society said to hold them, or both.

### 1.3 Components claimed in 1918

The earliest securely dated description is Winter Mead’s letter of June 7, 1918, to fellow Bonesman F. Trubee Davison:

> “The skull of the worthy Geronimo the Terrible, exhumed from its tomb at Fort Sill by your club & the K—t Haffner, is now safe inside the T— together with his well worn femurs[,] bit & saddle horn.”

The original survives in the **F. Trubee Davison Papers** in Yale University Library’s Manuscripts and Archives. It was located by Marc Wortman and published in facsimile and transcription in 2006. The letter identifies:

1. one skull;
2. “femurs,” grammatically suggesting both thigh bones, although the count is not stated;
3. a bit, presumably an equestrian bridle bit;
4. a saddle horn, whether detached from a saddle or used as shorthand for part of one;
5. Fort Sill as the asserted place of exhumation;
6. Skull and Bones’ New Haven “Tomb” as the asserted destination.

The letter supplies no measurements, anatomical observations, sex or age assessment, labels, inscriptions, condition report, packaging information, or explanation of how the objects were transported. It does not say that Mead witnessed either the excavation or the arrival. The full document and its archival context are reproduced in the [2006 Yale Alumni Magazine report](https://archives.yalealumnimagazine.com/images/issues/2006/05/Yale_Alumn_Magazine.pdf).

### 1.4 Later descriptions

In the mid-1980s, San Carlos Apache leader Ned Anderson received anonymously:

- a photograph said to depict a skull in a glass display case inside the Tomb;
- a text represented as part of F. O. Matthiessen’s 1933 centennial history of Skull and Bones;
- and an account derived from an alleged 1919 society log.

No publicly accessible forensic authentication of the photographed skull, photograph, Matthiessen document, or copied log has been published. Yale and Harvard archivists contacted in a later investigation could not locate the purported Matthiessen history in their Matthiessen holdings.

At a 1986 meeting, Bones representatives reportedly showed Anderson a glass case containing a small skull. They said it belonged to a boy about ten years old, not Geronimo, and offered it to Anderson. He declined, judging it different from the photographed skull and unwilling to accept a proposed agreement disclaiming the Geronimo attribution. This episode is known through Anderson’s account, summarized in the [Yale Alumni Magazine](https://archives.yalealumnimagazine.com/images/issues/2006/05/Yale_Alumn_Magazine.pdf), rather than through a published inventory or forensic report.

Alexandra Robbins reported after interviewing Bones alumni for *Secrets of the Tomb* (2002) that several described a skull in a glass case near the Tomb’s entrance that members called “Geronimo.” Such statements attest a society identification or nickname, not biological identity.

A covertly recorded 2001 Skull and Bones ceremony reportedly showed participants handling skulls and “femur-sized bones.” The recording cannot establish that these were genuine human remains, much less the remains named in 1918.

### 1.5 What is physically known

No publicly verified physical description exists for the alleged adult skull or femurs:

- **Material:** claimed human bone; untested publicly.
- **Individual represented:** unknown.
- **Number of femurs:** unstated beyond the plural.
- **Age and sex:** unknown.
- **Dimensions and weight:** unpublished.
- **Trauma, pathology, weathering, soil staining, preservatives, labels, repairs, mounts:** unpublished.
- **DNA, radiocarbon date, isotopes, or other biomolecular findings:** none publicly reported.
- **Present condition:** unknown.
- **Accession or private inventory number:** none published.
- **Documented photograph:** an anonymously supplied photograph has been described, but its provenance and subject remain unauthenticated.

The small skull shown to Anderson in 1986 is a separate candidate object unless continuity can be demonstrated. Bones representatives reportedly denied it was Geronimo’s. It has not been publicly examined or traced after the meeting.

## 2. Origin, Burial, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Biological origin

If authentic, the skull and femurs originated as parts of Geronimo’s body. That statement is conditional: no examined specimen has been linked to him.

Geronimo died of pneumonia at Fort Sill on February 17, 1909, while still held as a prisoner of war. The National Archives identifies his burial place as the Beef Creek Apache Cemetery at Fort Sill. Its catalogue also preserves historical photographs of the grave and of Geronimo at Fort Sill. See the [National Archives historical account](https://prologue.blogs.archives.gov/2022/11/22/geronimo-apache-chief/) and the [National Museum of the American Indian’s record for funeral photographs](https://americanindian.si.edu/collections-search/edan-record/ead_component%3Asova-nmai-ac-331-ref2?destination=%2Fcollections-search%2Fedan-record%2Fead_collection%253Asova-nmai-ac-331%3Fview%3Dgrid).

### 2.2 Funeral and original interment

Contemporary Oklahoma reporting states that Geronimo’s funeral occurred on February 18, 1909. The Reverend L. T. Legters conducted a Christian service modified to resemble Apache burial practice as far as his religious views allowed. The War Department set aside a holiday for Apache prisoners, and approximately two hundred joined the procession. Geronimo’s widow reportedly tried to have his favorite horse killed for burial with him, but was prevented from doing so. This account is preserved in the [Oklahoma State Register of February 25, 1909](https://gateway.okhistory.org/ark%3A/67531/metadc112638/m1/5/).

Later narratives say a riding whip and blanket were placed in the coffin. These details are plausible but less securely documented than the contemporary funeral report. Accounts that he was buried “in the dress of a chief with his possessions” were repeated in the 2009 federal complaint and summarized by the court, but the complaint is an allegation, not a burial inventory.

No contemporary source located for this dossier establishes that a bit, saddle horn, or saddle was placed inside the coffin. Mead’s 1918 mention of a bit and saddle horn therefore does not independently prove that the raiders reached Geronimo’s coffin. Equestrian gear could have been displayed or deposited near another grave.

### 2.3 Earliest evidence for theft

There are two different “earliest attestations”:

1. **Earliest alleged internal record:** a society log entry supposedly made in 1919 and later quoted in F. O. Matthiessen’s 1933 private history. The original log has not been produced publicly; consequently its date and wording cannot be independently authenticated.

2. **Earliest securely dated surviving evidence:** Winter Mead’s June 7, 1918 letter. It is contemporary correspondence in an established archival collection. Mead was not an eyewitness, so the document proves circulation of the claim within the society, not the identity of the remains.

Research published in 2023 added two more contemporary references:

- An August 1918 letter said that Captain Chauncey Jerome Hamlin regarded the “Gironamo stunt pulled at Sill” as wonderful and joked that surpassing it would require taking the Kaiser’s “ivory dome.”
- A September 1918 letter referred to the “stupendous crook” of Geronimo’s skull, said to have been dug up “one dark night in May,” and stated that it rested on the mantel—apparently in the Tomb.

These documents reinforce the conclusion that the story existed immediately after the alleged event. They do not independently identify the skeleton. The letters and their archival setting are described in the [2023 CT Insider investigation](https://www.ctinsider.com/news/article/yale-secret-society-steal-famous-apache-leader-s-18085455.php).

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because identity is unproved, the provenance must be presented as parallel chains.

### 3.1 Geronimo’s documented body

**February 17, 1909 — Fort Sill hospital.**  
Geronimo died of pneumonia. His body initially remained under military authority at Fort Sill.

**February 18, 1909 — Beef Creek Apache Cemetery.**  
His body was transported in a funeral procession and buried in the Apache cemetery. This is the first documented custody and location of the remains.

**1909–1914 — ordinary earth grave.**  
The grave was not originally the masonry monument visible today. The precise location was not generally disclosed to outsiders.

**1914 — reported disturbance.**  
Later Fort Sill research reported that local Apache people found the grave disturbed but apparently still intact. Rumors were circulated that the remains had been secretly moved—elsewhere at Fort Sill, to Arizona, or even to Florida—to deter grave robbers. These stories are historically important but do not prove a relocation.

**1921–1933 — identification kept confidential.**  
According to the records of Master Sergeant Morris Swett, Belle Nicholas identified the site to him in 1921. Geronimo’s cousin Nah-thle-tla confirmed it in 1929. Swett reportedly withheld public disclosure until after Nah-thle-tla’s death in 1933. A 1930 photograph records Nah-thle-tla and James Kaywaykla at the grave. This reconstruction is discussed in the [2023 investigation](https://www.ctinsider.com/news/article/yale-secret-society-steal-famous-apache-leader-s-18085455.php).

**Late 1920s–1930s — monument construction.**  
The Army placed concrete over the grave and erected a stone monument. Sources variously date the work to 1928 or broadly to the 1930s. The present structure is a conical or pyramidal fieldstone monument capped by an eagle, with an inscribed marker. It is a later grave monument, not the “tomb” described in the alleged raid narrative.

**Present — Fort Sill.**  
The grave remains at Beef Creek Apache Cemetery on federal military property. The federal government treats it as Geronimo’s burial place. A National Register photographic record calls it “Geronimo’s Grave” and identifies the Fort Sill Museum as the negative holder: [National Register property photograph](https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/b8fc3a7f-9082-44c2-8f5f-966cc24a9758).

No sanctioned exhumation has established whether the skull or femurs are present beneath the monument.

### 3.2 The alleged Skull and Bones assemblage

**May 1918 — alleged excavation at Fort Sill.**  
The September 1918 letter dates the act to “one dark night in May.” The purported 1933 society history attributes the raid to a group that included William Ellery Sedgwick James, Henry Neil Mallon, Prescott Sheldon Bush, and additional members identified partly by nicknames. Winter Mead’s June letter specifically mentions Charles C. Haffner Jr. as participating with Davison’s “club.”

Military and Yale records place Bush, James, Mallon, and Haffner at Fort Sill during the relevant period. That opportunity is corroborative but not proof of the act.

**Late May or early June 1918 — alleged movement to New Haven.**  
The June 7 letter says the skull, femurs, bit, and saddle horn were “now safe” in the Tomb. It does not disclose how they traveled approximately 1,500 miles from Oklahoma to Connecticut or who carried them. Haffner’s movement from Fort Sill provides one proposed route, but a shipping record, luggage record, railway record, or eyewitness account has not surfaced.

**1918 — alleged placement inside the Tomb.**  
The September letter says the skull rested on a mantel. This differs slightly from later descriptions of a skull in a glass case near the entrance, but both place an object in the New Haven clubhouse.

**1919 — alleged log entry.**  
A purported Skull and Bones log allegedly recorded the raid and later supplied material for the 1933 history. The original log is unavailable for independent study.

**1933 — alleged incorporation into institutional history.**  
F. O. Matthiessen, Yale class of 1923 and a Bonesman, reportedly wrote *Continuation of the History of Our Order for the Century Celebration, 17 June 1933*. An excerpt published by Alexandra Robbins names Bush, James, and Mallon. The reported narrative describes breaking through an iron door and identifies the skull as the society’s most valuable acquisition. No complete authenticated copy has entered a public archive.

**1933–mid-1980s — documentary gap.**  
There is no public custody record, inventory, conservation record, or authenticated photograph establishing continuous survival in the Tomb. Oral reports from members about a skull called Geronimo are evidence of institutional tradition, not object continuity.

**Mid-1980s — anonymous disclosure to Ned Anderson.**  
Anderson received the photograph and copied historical materials. The sender’s identity has not been established publicly.

**September 1986 — meeting with Bones representatives.**  
Anderson met representatives including Jonathan Bush, Prescott Bush’s son, and Endicott Peabody Davison, F. Trubee Davison’s son. A small skull in a display case was presented. The representatives reportedly called it the skull of a ten-year-old boy and denied possessing Geronimo’s skull. Anderson rejected the offered object.

This meeting created rather than resolved an identity problem:

- the skull shown might have been the photographed object;
- it might have been a substituted object;
- the photograph might never have depicted a Skull and Bones holding;
- or the adult skull might have been lost, removed, hidden, or never possessed.

None of these alternatives is proved.

**1988–1989 — public controversy.**  
Anderson brought the claim to the press and sought political assistance. Jonathan Bush called the story a hoax. The controversy appeared in national reporting, including the [Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/lifestyle/1988/10/01/skull-for-scandal/56da3338-e79c-456c-b988-f40b5fc52603/) and a broader discussion of repatriation in the [Los Angeles Times](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1989-10-08-vw-299-story.html).

**2002 — published internal narrative.**  
Alexandra Robbins reproduced a substantial passage from the purported 1933 history in *Secrets of the Tomb*, substantially expanding popular knowledge of the alleged raid.

**2006 — discovery and publication of Mead’s letter.**  
Marc Wortman found the June 7, 1918 document in the Davison Papers. Its publication materially changed the historiography: the story could no longer be dismissed simply as an invention originating in the 1980s.

**2009–2010 — litigation.**  
Twenty alleged lineal descendants led by Harlyn Geronimo sued federal officials, Yale University, and the Order of Skull and Bones. They requested an accounting, return of remains and associated objects, relocation and reburial, and damages. The litigation produced no inspection or discovery establishing custody.

**2010–present — chain unresolved.**  
No authenticated inventory, surrender, repatriation, forensic test, or destruction record has emerged. In 2023, Skull and Bones’ recent former president Wesley Spear said the letters would lead to “a dead end” and that there was “nothing of substance to find.” RTA Incorporated, the nonprofit owner of the Tomb, did not permit inspection or answer detailed inquiries. Yale said it did not possess the remains and had neither ownership of nor access to the building.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Evidence supporting a genuine Fort Sill theft

The strongest evidence is cumulative:

- Three letters written in 1918 refer to the exploit.
- The June letter names the skull, femurs, bit, saddle horn, Fort Sill, Haffner, and the Tomb.
- The September letter dates the excavation to a dark night in May and reports the skull on a mantel.
- Military and Yale records place named Bonesmen at Fort Sill.
- The later internal-history narrative names several of the same men.
- Later members reportedly described a skull inside the Tomb as “Geronimo.”
- “Crooking”—competitive acquisition by theft—is independently reported as part of Yale society culture.

This evidence strongly supports the proposition that members were telling one another in 1918 that a skull had been taken from Fort Sill.

### 4.2 Evidence against identification as Geronimo

The objections concern identity, not merely whether a grave was robbed:

1. **Unknown grave location.** Geronimo’s grave was unmarked, overgrown, and reportedly concealed from outsiders. Belle Nicholas did not disclose its location to Swett until 1921, three years after the alleged raid.

2. **Architectural mismatch.** The internal story reportedly describes a “tomb” protected by an iron door. Geronimo lay in an earth grave beneath the open sky. His present masonry monument had not yet been built.

3. **Distance.** The cemetery was roughly two miles from the principal base area where the Yale servicemen were stationed.

4. **Alternative graves.** Marc Wortman proposed that the raiders may have entered the grave of Comanche man Mark “Thomas” Perconnic, whose masonry structure could have appeared to have a blocked doorway. Kicking Bird’s grave also has a metal door, although its position near a road made it a less likely target in Wortman’s assessment.

5. **Possible prior relocation.** Some descendants maintain that Apache people may have moved Geronimo’s body shortly after burial. This is family or community tradition, not a scientifically verified event.

6. **No forensic comparison.** The alleged objects have never been made available for examination against Geronimo’s known descendants.

David H. Miller of Cameron University concluded that the Bonesmen may have dug up someone at Fort Sill but probably did not find Geronimo. Former Fort Sill museums director Frank Siltman, after examining Swett’s records, considered outsiders’ successful location of the grave in 1918 implausible. The principal arguments are collected in the [Yale Alumni Magazine report](https://archives.yalealumnimagazine.com/images/issues/2006/05/Yale_Alumn_Magazine.pdf) and [CT Insider investigation](https://www.ctinsider.com/news/article/yale-secret-society-steal-famous-apache-leader-s-18085455.php).

### 4.3 Competing claimant objects

At least four physical identities must be kept separate:

1. **The remains beneath the Fort Sill monument.** Officially Geronimo’s burial, but never forensically inventoried.

2. **The adult skull described in 1918.** Its existence is reported in contemporary letters; its biological identity and survival are unproved.

3. **The skull in the anonymous 1980s photograph.** Its location, age, sex, and continuity with the 1918 object are unproved.

4. **The small skull shown to Anderson in 1986.** Bones representatives reportedly identified it as a child’s skull and denied it was Geronimo’s. It has not been scientifically examined in public.

The plural femurs, bit, and saddle horn disappear from the public documentary trail after 1918. No later source securely describes or photographs them.

### 4.4 Scientific testing

No publicly reported scientific test has been performed on a Skull and Bones candidate:

- no DNA analysis;
- no radiocarbon dating;
- no osteological age or sex assessment;
- no stable-isotope analysis;
- no soil or mineral comparison with the Beef Creek cemetery;
- no trauma or pathology comparison with Geronimo’s medical history;
- no analysis of coffin residue or preservation chemicals.

Hope Gonzales and Pius LaCroix-Garcia, descendants interviewed in 2023, called for DNA testing and stated that they would cooperate. They also emphasized that any human remains stolen at Fort Sill should be returned even if they prove not to be Geronimo’s.

DNA testing would itself require careful consent, sampling, chain-of-custody control, and interpretation. A mismatch could exclude the proposed relationship; a match would require appropriate reference relatives and could establish kinship more readily than unique personal identity. DNA could not by itself prove the circumstances of excavation.

### 4.5 Institutional positions

- **Skull and Bones representatives, 1986:** reportedly denied possessing Geronimo and characterized the available skull as a child’s.
- **Jonathan Bush:** publicly called the allegation a hoax.
- **Wesley Spear, 2023:** dismissed the documentary trail as a dead end without providing an inventory or inspection.
- **Yale University:** states that it does not possess the remains and is legally and physically separate from Skull and Bones and RTA Incorporated.
- **Fort Sill/Army:** maintains Geronimo’s official grave but has not exhumed it to test completeness.
- **Geronimo descendants:** not unanimous about disturbing or relocating the Fort Sill burial, but several have demanded an accounting and return of anything held in New Haven.

### 4.6 Evidentiary judgment

The most defensible formulation is:

> In 1918, Skull and Bones members contemporaneously claimed that comrades at Fort Sill had exhumed Geronimo’s skull, femurs, and equestrian objects and deposited them in the Tomb. The documents make the boast historically real. They do not establish that the excavated grave was Geronimo’s or that the alleged objects survive in Skull and Bones custody.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Institutions

### Geronimo / Goyaałé

The person from whose body the relic is alleged to derive. He died and was buried at Fort Sill in February 1909. No ante-mortem record describes the later alleged relic.

### Zi-yeh

Geronimo’s widow. Contemporary reporting says she sought to have his favorite horse killed so it might accompany him. Her action belongs to the funeral history, not to the alleged 1918 custody chain.

### Reverend L. T. Legters

Missionary who conducted the 1909 funeral. Contemporary reporting characterizes the service as a negotiated Christian/Apache ceremony.

### Naiche

Chiricahua leader present in later accounts of Geronimo’s funeral and eulogy. He had no documented role in the alleged relic’s later custody.

### Belle Nicholas

Apache woman said to have privately shown Geronimo’s grave to Morris Swett in 1921. Her disclosure is central to the argument that outsiders did not know the grave’s location in 1918.

### Nah-thle-tla

Geronimo’s cousin, who reportedly confirmed the grave’s location in 1929 and was photographed there in 1930.

### James Kaywaykla

Apache man photographed with Nah-thle-tla at the identified grave in 1930.

### Master Sergeant Morris Swett

Fort Sill librarian, historian, and museum organizer. His records preserve the account of the grave’s identification and delayed public disclosure.

### Charles C. Haffner Jr.

Yale class of 1919, Skull and Bones member, and soldier stationed at Fort Sill. Mead’s June 1918 letter explicitly credits “K—t Haffner” and Davison’s club with the exhumation.

### Prescott Sheldon Bush

Yale class of 1917, Bonesman, soldier at Fort Sill, later senator, and father and grandfather of U.S. presidents. The surviving Mead letter does **not** name him. His identification as a raider comes from the purported 1933 history and later accounts. His presence gives him opportunity, not proven participation.

### William Ellery Sedgwick James

Yale class of 1917 and Bonesman stationed at Fort Sill. Named in the purported internal history as a participant.

### Henry Neil Mallon

Yale class of 1917 and Bonesman stationed at Fort Sill. Named in the purported history.

### Winter Mead

Yale class of 1919 and Bonesman. Author of the June 7, 1918 letter. He was in New Haven, not Fort Sill, and therefore documented the society’s belief rather than an eyewitness excavation.

### F. Trubee Davison

Yale class of 1918 and Bonesman. Recipient of Mead’s letter. His preserved papers supplied the first authenticated contemporary documentary evidence.

### Newell Garfield

Bonesman whose March and July 1918 letters place Bush, James, Mallon, and other Yale men at Fort Sill. His evidence establishes presence, not the excavation.

### John Eliot Woolley, Kenneth F. Simpson, and Chauncey Jerome Hamlin

Connected to the August 1918 letter discussing the “Gironamo stunt pulled at Sill.” Their correspondence documents rapid circulation of the story among Bonesmen serving in France.

### F. O. Matthiessen

Literary critic, Yale class of 1923, and Bonesman. Supposed author of the 1933 centennial history quoting a 1919 log and naming raiders. The attributed work’s absence from accessible archives limits its evidentiary weight.

### Ned Anderson Sr.

Former chairman of the San Carlos Apache Tribe. Recipient of the anonymous disclosure; organizer of the 1980s campaign; participant in the 1986 meeting. He transformed a private society story into a public repatriation dispute.

### Jonathan Bush

Son of Prescott Bush and Bones alumnus. Met Anderson and later dismissed the story as a hoax.

### Endicott Peabody Davison

Son of Trubee Davison, attorney, and Bones representative in the Anderson negotiations. He reportedly offered Anderson the small skull and a settlement denying possession of Geronimo’s skull.

### Marc Wortman

Researcher who discovered Mead’s 1918 letter while studying the Yale aviation group. He later examined possible alternative graves at Fort Sill.

### Kathrin Day Lassila and Mark Alden Branch

Authors of the 2006 Yale Alumni Magazine investigation that published Mead’s letter and carefully distinguished contemporary belief from proof of identity.

### Alexandra Robbins

Author of *Secrets of the Tomb* (2002). Published excerpts attributed to the 1933 history and recorded alumni recollections of a skull called Geronimo.

### Harlyn Geronimo and the 2009 plaintiffs

Twenty people asserting lineal descent who sought return and reburial of Geronimo’s remains. Their names and the case docket appear in the [federal docket summary](https://dockets.justia.com/docket/district-of-columbia/dcdce/1%3A2009cv00303/135239).

### Robert Geronimo and intervening descendants

Opposed disturbing the existing grave, invoking Apache custom. Their position demonstrates that “the descendants” did not speak with one voice about exhumation or relocation.

### Ramsey Clark

Former U.S. attorney general and counsel to Harlyn Geronimo’s group. He acknowledged publicly that the plaintiffs did not know whether Skull and Bones possessed the remains.

### Skull and Bones / Order of Skull and Bones

The alleged recipient and keeper. It is a private Yale senior society, not an administrative unit of Yale University.

### RTA Incorporated

Connecticut nonprofit that owns the Tomb property. Its separate corporate identity is relevant to control, access, and potential NAGPRA coverage.

### Yale University

Custodian of the Davison Papers and institutional setting of the society, but not the owner or controller of the Tomb. Yale denies possessing the alleged remains.

### United States Army and Fort Sill

Custodian of the land containing Geronimo’s official grave. Its historical control of Geronimo as a prisoner of war and its present control of the cemetery made it a principal defendant in the repatriation litigation.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Geronimo’s funeral

The securely documented ceremonial use concerns the intact body in 1909: a funeral combining Christian ministry, military permission, and Apache participation. The proposed sacrifice of Geronimo’s horse and burial of personal possessions reflect beliefs about accompanying the deceased, although the exact inventory remains uncertain.

### 6.2 Alleged Skull and Bones function

The 1918 letters frame the remains as a **“crook”**—an illicit acquisition whose prestige lay in audacity. The purported 1933 history calls the skull the society’s most valuable acquisition. This suggests trophy and institutional-memory functions.

Claims that initiates kissed the skull, drank from it, drank brandy from it, or used it in weekly ceremonies occur in popular and anonymous accounts. They have not been verified by a publicly authenticated ritual manual or inspectable object. The 2001 recording showing skulls and femur-sized objects supports the general proposition that skeletal imagery or possible remains figured in ceremonies; it does not identify Geronimo’s remains.

### 6.3 Symbolic force

The claimed relic acquired significance beyond any original society use:

- for Bonesmen, an alleged proof of daring and internal solidarity;
- for critics, an emblem of elite impunity and grave robbery;
- for Apache activists, a concrete focus for ancestral return and proper burial;
- for journalists, a link between secret-society culture, Yale, and the Bush political family;
- for legal scholars, an example of the uncertain property and repatriation status of privately held Native American human remains.

Its political force does not depend on authenticity. The unresolved refusal or inability to produce an object has itself sustained the symbol.

## 7. Myths, Legends, and Narrative Accretions

### 7.1 The fully developed raid legend

The mature story says that six Bonesmen stationed at Fort Sill during the First World War located Geronimo’s tomb, posted lookouts, entered through an iron door, opened the grave, and carried away the skull, femurs, bit, and saddle horn. Prescott Bush is normally made the leader or principal thief; the trophies are then taken to New Haven and incorporated into Bones ritual.

**Historical core:** contemporary letters establish that some Bonesmen believed or claimed a skull had been taken.

**Unverified elaborations:** exact number of raiders, Bush’s leadership, the iron door, precise excavation method, ritual drinking, and uninterrupted retention.

### 7.2 The wrong-grave hypothesis

This is a modern historical reconstruction rather than a legend. It proposes that the raiders opened another Indigenous person’s grave and falsely identified the remains as Geronimo’s.

Candidates include:

- Kicking Bird’s more visible grave with a metal door;
- Mark “Thomas” Perconnic’s masonry grave, partly bricked where a doorway might have been.

Wortman regarded Perconnic’s grave as the closer architectural match. No physical or documentary evidence ties a Skull and Bones specimen specifically to either man.

### 7.3 Secret Apache relocation

Versions circulated after the 1914 disturbance that Apache people had moved Geronimo:

- to another Fort Sill location;
- back to Arizona;
- or even to Florida.

The rumors may have been deliberate protective misinformation. Some Mescalero descendants continue to believe a relocation occurred. No excavation or DNA evidence confirms any version.

### 7.4 The substitution narrative

Anderson believed the small skull shown in 1986 was not the skull in his photograph. This generated a theory that the society substituted a child’s skull to avoid surrendering the real one.

The evidence establishes only:

- a small skull was reportedly shown;
- representatives denied it was Geronimo;
- Anderson considered it different;
- no neutral expert examined either object.

Substitution remains possible but unproved.

### 7.5 Presidential-family framing

Popular retellings often reduce the affair to “George W. Bush’s grandfather stole Geronimo’s skull.” That formulation exceeds the evidence. Prescott Bush is named in the later purported society history and was at Fort Sill, but not in Mead’s authenticated June 1918 account. Neither President George H. W. Bush nor George W. Bush has a documented role in the alleged 1918 event.

### 7.6 Conflation with other trophy-skull legends

Skull and Bones has also been rumored to possess the skulls of Pancho Villa and President Martin Van Buren. These stories have encouraged a generic image of the Tomb as a repository of famous heads. They must not be used as corroboration for Geronimo’s skull; each claim requires its own provenance.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Official burial

Geronimo’s recognized grave is in Beef Creek Apache Cemetery, also called the Apache prisoner-of-war cemetery, on Fort Sill military land near Lawton, Oklahoma. It is marked by a fieldstone monument capped with an eagle. The site is historically documented and administered within the Fort Sill reservation.

The statement that Geronimo is “buried there” is an official and historical location claim. It does not establish anatomical completeness.

### 8.2 Alleged removed objects

Their current status is **unknown**.

Possible states include:

- still inside the Tomb in New Haven;
- privately transferred by members;
- reburied;
- lost;
- destroyed;
- mislabeled;
- or never physically present as described.

No public evidence permits selection among these possibilities.

The Tomb is owned by RTA Incorporated. Public access is restricted. A 2023 reporter was not admitted. RTA did not provide an inventory or substantive reply.

Yale’s 2023 position was explicit: Yale does not possess the remains, does not own the Skull and Bones building or its land, and lacks access. This statement does not resolve what RTA or Skull and Bones may possess.

### 8.3 Repatriation dispute

The principal demands have included:

- an accounting of remains held by Skull and Bones or Yale;
- DNA testing;
- return of any Native American remains;
- recovery and consolidation of Geronimo’s remains;
- and relocation from Fort Sill to ancestral Apache land in Arizona or New Mexico.

Descendants disagree about disturbing the Fort Sill grave. Harlyn Geronimo’s group sought relocation; Robert Geronimo and other intervenors opposed disturbance under Apache custom.

### 8.4 Legal regime

NAGPRA applies to federal agencies and qualifying museums or institutions receiving federal funds. The National Park Service’s compliance guidance explains the broad meaning of receipt of federal assistance but does not automatically bring an entirely private, unfunded society within the statute: [NAGPRA compliance guidance](https://home.nps.gov/subjects/nagpra/compliance.htm).

The 2009 plaintiffs relied chiefly on NAGPRA § 3002. Judge Richard W. Roberts dismissed the action on July 27, 2010 because:

- NAGPRA did not itself waive federal sovereign immunity;
- the complaint did not identify qualifying final agency action for an Administrative Procedure Act waiver;
- § 3002 governs items excavated or discovered on federal or tribal land after November 16, 1990, while the alleged removal occurred in 1918;
- no NAGPRA provision required the federal government to excavate possible burial sites;
- and the pleadings did not sustain claims against the remaining defendants.

The opinion is available from [GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-dcd-1_09-cv-00303/pdf/USCOURTS-dcd-1_09-cv-00303-0.pdf) and [Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/district-of-columbia/dcdce/1:2009cv00303/135239/33/).

The dismissal was procedural and statutory. It was not a finding that:

- the grave was intact;
- the raid never occurred;
- the alleged objects were forged;
- Skull and Bones possessed no human remains;
- or the plaintiffs’ preferred burial arrangement was historically or culturally incorrect.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Investigative journalism

The story’s modern evidentiary history has three principal stages:

1. Anderson’s disclosures and press coverage in the 1980s;
2. Robbins’s publication of the alleged internal history in 2002 and Wortman’s discovery of Mead’s letter in 2006;
3. the discovery and publication of additional 1918 letters in 2023.

The 2006 and 2023 discoveries substantially strengthened the evidence for a contemporary society belief while leaving biological identity unresolved.

### 9.2 Literature

Important modern treatments include:

- Alexandra Robbins, *Secrets of the Tomb: Skull and Bones, the Ivy League, and the Hidden Paths of Power* (2002);
- Marc Wortman, *The Millionaires’ Unit* (2006), whose archival research led to the Mead letter;
- Kitty Kelley, *The Family* (2004), which placed the claim within Bush-family history;
- Kris Millegan, ed., *Fleshing Out Skull & Bones* (2003), a compendium influential in conspiracy literature.

Popular works often present the theft as settled fact and omit the unmarked-grave problem. Their role is therefore part of the legend’s transmission, not automatically evidence of authenticity.

### 9.3 Political reception

Because Prescott Bush later became a United States senator and his son and grandson became presidents, the object became attached to arguments about inherited privilege, secrecy, and elite accountability. This association dramatically increased the story’s visibility but can distort the chain of evidence by making Bush the focus rather than the unidentified remains.

### 9.4 Repatriation and museum ethics

The controversy anticipated broader public attention to Native American ancestral remains in museums, universities, medical collections, and private hands. Legal scholar Alix Rogers uses the Geronimo litigation to examine the exceptional property status of Native American remains under American law in [“Owning Geronimo but Not Elmer McCurdy”](https://bclawreview.bc.edu/articles/322).

Yale separately holds Native American ancestral remains in institutional collections and has current NAGPRA responsibilities. Those holdings must not be conflated with the alleged Skull and Bones relic. Yale’s Department of Anthropology has publicly discussed the history and review of skeletal collections: [Yale Anthropology and Human Skeletal Remains](https://anthropology.yale.edu/yale-anthropology-and-human-skeletal-remains).

### 9.5 Hoax, forgery, and replica status

No identified forger has confessed to fabricating the 1918 correspondence; the documents reside in a legitimate archival collection and are supported by contextual correspondence. Conversely, the anonymous 1980s material has not been fully authenticated.

No commercial or museum replica has acquired a documented independent provenance. Images circulated online are often generic skulls, illustrations, the 1980s display-case photograph, or photographs of Geronimo’s grave. They should not be treated as photographs of an authenticated relic.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Documentary phases

**1918:** private contemporary correspondence.  
The event existed as society talk immediately after the alleged raid.

**1919/1933:** alleged internal institutional record.  
A purported log and Matthiessen history developed the named-raider narrative. Their inaccessible originals limit verification.

**1980s:** public disclosure and repatriation activism.  
The central evidentiary object became an anonymous package sent to Ned Anderson.

**2002:** popular investigative synthesis.  
Robbins published an extended version of the society narrative.

**2006:** archival turning point.  
The Mead letter established that the allegation did not originate in the 1980s.

**2009–2010:** legal testing without factual discovery.  
The case clarified statutory obstacles but not authenticity.

**2011–2023:** renewed site and archive investigation.  
Researchers compared the architectural description with Fort Sill graves and found further contemporary letters.

### 10.2 Principal debate

The debate is best represented as four propositions of decreasing certainty:

1. **Certain:** Geronimo was buried at Fort Sill in 1909.
2. **Very well documented:** Bonesmen in 1918 said that fellow members had stolen Geronimo’s skull and other objects.
3. **Plausible but unproved:** Members actually removed human remains from a Fort Sill grave and transported them to New Haven.
4. **Unproved and substantially challenged:** Those remains were Geronimo’s.

### 10.3 Primary evidence still needed

Resolution would require some combination of:

- production and authentication of the alleged 1919 log;
- a complete authenticated copy of the 1933 Matthiessen history;
- full documentation of Anderson’s anonymous package;
- inspection and inventory of human remains inside the Tomb;
- forensic examination under descendant and tribal consultation;
- examination of the Fort Sill grave, only if authorized by the appropriate descendants, tribes, and federal authorities;
- study of cemetery records and the alternative Perconnic and Kicking Bird graves;
- transport, luggage, or correspondence records tracing Haffner or others from Fort Sill to New Haven;
- and corporate or society records documenting acquisition, removal, destruction, or reburial.

### 10.4 Discredited or unsupported formulations

The following should not be repeated as established fact:

- “Prescott Bush definitely stole Geronimo’s skull.”
- “The skull is certainly displayed in the Skull and Bones Tomb.”
- “DNA proved it was—or was not—Geronimo.”
- “The court ruled Skull and Bones innocent.”
- “Geronimo’s grave is known to be empty.”
- “The Army has confirmed that the body is complete.”
- “The skull shown in 1986 was conclusively a child’s.”
- “Yale University owns the alleged relic.”

None is supported by the available evidence.

## 12. Sources and Source Criticism

### Primary and near-primary sources

The most important primary document is Mead’s June 7, 1918 letter in the F. Trubee Davison Papers. Its archival setting and mundane surrounding correspondence strongly favor authenticity. Its limitation is equally important: Mead did not witness the excavation.

The August and September 1918 letters discovered in the same archival environment independently demonstrate circulation of the story. Their authors’ partially illegible identities and lack of eyewitness status limit them.

The 1909 Oklahoma newspaper account is contemporary evidence for the funeral. It should be preferred over later romanticized versions of the burial inventory.

The federal court opinion is authoritative concerning the litigation’s disposition and reasoning, but its background summary repeats allegations from the pleadings. Judicial recitation of a complaint is not factual authentication.

The purported 1919 log and 1933 Matthiessen history remain central but incompletely available. Published excerpts must be treated as evidence mediated through copies and later authors.

### Institutional and official sources

The National Archives, National Museum of the American Indian, National Register, Fort Sill, and National Park Service reliably document the burial site, archival images, and current legal framework. None has authenticated a Skull and Bones-held specimen.

Yale’s statements reliably establish its asserted lack of custody and legal separation from RTA. They cannot establish what is inside a privately controlled building to which Yale says it has no access.

### Journalism

The 2006 Yale Alumni Magazine report is the foundational documentary investigation and carefully separates belief from identity. The 2023 CT Insider investigation adds new primary correspondence, military contextual research, interviews, and competing grave hypotheses. Contemporary 1980s reporting documents what participants said but cannot resolve the object’s identity.

### Final assessment

The case is neither a substantiated relic provenance nor a legend without contemporary roots. It occupies an intermediate evidentiary category:

> **A likely or possible human-remains trophy, contemporaneously called Geronimo’s by its alleged takers, whose actual individual identity, continuous survival, and current custody have never been established.**

Any responsible catalogue entry should identify the purported relic as **“Geronimo’s skull and femurs, alleged; possible unidentified human remains removed from a Fort Sill grave in 1918; present location and identity unverified.”**
