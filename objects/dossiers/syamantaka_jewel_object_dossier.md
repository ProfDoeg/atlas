# The Syamantaka jewel: Object Dossier

**Requested filename:** `the_syamantaka_jewel_object_dossier.md`  
**Research status:** Completed 6 September 2026  
**Object class:** Legendary divine gemstone; narrative treasure, solar ornament, wealth-producing talisman, royal-prosperity object  
**Existence status:** Textually attested; no historically documented physical specimen or scientifically examined claimant is known  
**Last location within the principal narrative tradition:** In Akrūra’s custody at Dvārakā  
**Present physical location:** Unknown and not historically recoverable; no authenticated surviving object

## Evidentiary key

- **Primary text:** What a Sanskrit narrative itself states—not independent proof that its supernatural or historical events occurred.
- **Textual finding:** A conclusion supported by manuscripts, a critical edition, or comparison among recensions.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** A published researcher’s analysis.
- **Living tradition:** A continuing religious interpretation or practice.
- **Later elaboration:** Material absent from the earliest recoverable version but present in later texts or recensions.
- **Modern retelling:** Recent devotional, literary, artistic, or commercial adaptation.
- **Unsupported identification:** A proposed physical identity lacking provenance, examination, or a continuous documentary chain.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Core identification

The Syamantaka is a supernatural jewel in the Kṛṣṇa narrative cycle. Its Sanskrit name is:

- **स्यमन्तक**, *Syamantaka*, the spelling found in the constituted text of the *Harivaṃśa*.
- **स्यमन्तकमणि**, *Syamantaka-maṇi*, “the Syamantaka jewel.”
- Modern English variants include **Syamantaka jewel**, **Syamantaka gem**, **Syamantaka stone**, and **Syamantaka Mani**.
- Modern Indian-language spellings commonly include Hindi **स्यमन्तक मणि**, Bengali **স্যমন্তক মণি**, Kannada **ಸ್ಯಮಂತಕ ಮಣಿ**, Telugu **శ్యమంతక మణి**, Tamil **சியமந்தக மணி**, and Malayalam **സ്യമന്തക മണി**. Spellings with an initial *Śy-*—“Śyāmantaka” or “Shyamantaka”—are widespread but do not represent the spelling used in the *Harivaṃśa* critical text.

The word *maṇi* can mean a jewel, gem, precious stone, bead, or magical jewel. The sources do not provide a mineralogically precise identification. Calling the Syamantaka a **diamond**, **ruby**, or any other specific mineral is therefore a translation or later interpretive choice, not a finding established by the early Sanskrit text.

A secure historical etymology of *Syamantaka* has not been established in the consulted scholarship. It should be treated as a proper name. Popular divisions connecting it with *śyāma* (“dark,” “dark-blue,” or Kṛṣṇa) are linguistically uncertain and should not be presented as demonstrated derivations.

### 1.2 Description in the earliest recoverable source

The earliest presently recoverable, substantially complete account is in the *Harivaṃśa*, Harivaṃśaparvan 28–29 in P. L. Vaidya’s critical edition. The critically constituted narrative calls it:

> *mahāmaṇim divyaṃ syamantakaṃ nāma*  
> “a great, divine jewel named Syamantaka” (*Harivaṃśa* 28.12).

The constituted text does not specify:

- mineral species;
- cut or faceting;
- dimensions;
- weight;
- setting;
- maker;
- inscription;
- exact color;
- geological source.

It does establish several visible or functional characteristics:

1. **Wearability.** Prasena is “adorned with it” while hunting (*Harivaṃśa* 28.15). At the story’s end Akrūra binds or fastens it upon himself and shines “like the radiant sun” (29.40). Several recensional passages explicitly put it around the neck.
2. **Solar brilliance.** In the widespread Sun-origin interpolation, Satrājit wearing it is mistaken for the Sun.
3. **Gold production.** The constituted verse says, *sa maṇiḥ syandate rukmam*—“that jewel exudes/pours forth gold” (28.13). A widely transmitted but critically marked additional passage quantifies its output as **eight bhāras of gold every day**.
4. **Environmental protection.** In the Vṛṣṇi-Andhaka settlement it causes Parjanya to rain at the proper time and removes fear of disease (28.13).
5. **Portability.** It passes repeatedly by hand, is worn on the body, is carried into a cave, and in later accounts is concealed in clothing or wrapped in cloth.
6. **Durability.** No source says that it is chipped, altered, reset, repaired, divided, melted, or destroyed.

The electronic Sanskrit text reproducing Vaidya’s constituted text and starred variant passages permits the object’s earliest recoverable description and transmission history to be distinguished directly. In particular, the celebrated scene in which Sūrya removes the jewel from his own neck and gives it to Satrājit is printed as an additional passage after 28.12, whereas the constituted verse itself says that **Prasena obtained the jewel from the ocean**. [GRETIL critical *Harivaṃśa* text](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/1_sanskr/2_epic/mbh/ext/hv_conpu.htm)

### 1.3 Description in the *Viṣṇu Purāṇa*

The *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 4.13 gives the fully developed solar account:

- Sūrya wears the jewel at his neck.
- Its brilliance obscures the god’s bodily form.
- Sūrya removes it so that Satrājit can see him distinctly.
- Satrājit requests the jewel, and Sūrya gives it to him.
- When Satrājit enters Dvārakā wearing it, observers mistake him for the Sun.
- It yields eight loads or *bhāras* of gold daily.
- Proper possession averts drought, flood or excessive rain, famine, plague, and other calamities.

McComas Taylor’s modern translation describes the *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* as a composite oral-textual work composed approximately 1,500 years ago, while acknowledging the genre’s long transmission. [ANU Press edition](https://press.anu.edu.au/publications/textbooks/visnu-purana) The older Wilson translation remains useful for its comparative notes and explicitly observes that the story also occurs in the *Bhāgavata*, *Vāyu*, *Matsya*, *Brahma Purāṇa*, and *Harivaṃśa*. [Wilson, Book IV, chapter 13](https://sacred-texts.com/hin/vp/vp106.htm)

### 1.4 Description in the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa*

The *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 10.56–57 supplies the best-known devotional version. It describes the Syamantaka as:

- Sūrya’s gift to Satrājit;
- fastened to a chain and worn at Satrājit’s neck;
- brilliant enough for the wearer to be mistaken for the Sun;
- installed by Satrājit in a domestic shrine or altar;
- productive of gold every day;
- protective, when properly worshipped, against famine, calamity, untimely death, snake danger, mental or bodily affliction, and deceit;
- “shining like the sun” when Akrūra finally produces it.

The Sanskrit term remains *maṇi*. A modern description of it as a “ruby” or “diamond” exceeds the primary text’s mineralogical information. [*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 10.56](https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/10/56/) [*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 10.57](https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/10/57/)

### 1.5 No surviving physical description

No ancient inventory, inscription, excavated object report, treasury list, royal charter, mineralogical analysis, or museum accession record identifies a physical gemstone as the Syamantaka. Consequently, the following are all **unestablished**:

- composition;
- carat weight;
- dimensions;
- color;
- cut;
- hardness;
- geological origin;
- human workmanship;
- date of manufacture;
- present condition.

The object is reconstructable only as a narrated thing.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Earliest securely reconstructable textual appearance

The earliest substantial surviving narrative is the Syamantaka episode incorporated into the genealogical portion of the Sanskrit *Harivaṃśa*, traditionally an appendix (*khila*) to the *Mahābhārata*. Absolute dating of the work and of this particular layer remains disputed. The *Harivaṃśa* is earlier than the mature *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* account, but its long and highly interpolated manuscript history prevents assigning one uncontested year to the episode.

The episode occupies chapters 28–29 of Vaidya’s critical edition. Older vulgate numbering often places it in chapters 38–39; citations must therefore specify the edition.

The constituted reading at 28.12 says:

> *praseno dvāravatyāṃ tu niviśantyāṃ mahāmaṇim /  
> divyaṃ syamantakaṃ nāma samudrād upalabdhavān //*

A close translation is:

> “While Dvāravatī was being settled, Prasena obtained from the ocean a great divine jewel named Syamantaka.”

This is the earliest recoverable origin statement in the critical text. It does **not** say who made the jewel. It does **not** say that Sūrya forged it. It does **not** name a mine, workshop, or material.

### 2.2 The two-origin problem

The manuscript tradition preserves two origins:

1. **Ocean origin — constituted text.** Prasena acquires the jewel from the sea or ocean.
2. **Solar origin — widespread additional passage and later Purāṇas.** Sūrya is Satrājit’s intimate divine friend. Satrājit encounters him at the seashore; the god’s brilliance is initially indistinguishable from the jewel’s. Sūrya removes the jewel from his neck, and after Satrājit requests it, gives it to him.

The solar passage is widespread across manuscript groups but marked as an insertion in the critical edition. Its wide distribution shows antiquity and importance; its status as an addition shows that it cannot simply replace the shorter ocean-origin reading without comment.

André Couture treats this “double origin”—ocean and Sun—not as disposable inconsistency but as part of the episode’s larger sacrificial and cosmological design. In his reading, the jewel’s movements link solar and lunar dynastic structures, Agni and Soma, and Kṛṣṇa’s comprehensive sovereignty. [Couture, “L’affaire du joyau Syamantaka”](https://poj.peeters-leuven.be/content.php?id=2994462&journal_code=JA&url=article.php)

### 2.3 Later consolidation of the solar origin

The *Viṣṇu Purāṇa*, *Bhāgavata Purāṇa*, and most modern retellings select or elaborate the solar version:

- Satrājit worships or befriends Sūrya.
- Sūrya appears wearing the jewel.
- Satrājit requests it as a boon.
- Sūrya transfers it voluntarily.

No principal source describes an act of manufacture. Statements that Sūrya “created,” “forged,” or “mined” the stone are unsupported extrapolations. In the solar version, it is already in Sūrya’s possession when the narrated chain begins.

### 2.4 Dating limitations

The relevant works are composite and cannot be dated like dated inscriptions:

- The *Harivaṃśa* episode belongs to the evolving Sanskrit epic tradition and is preserved in divergent recensions.
- The *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* is conventionally assigned in broad terms to the first millennium CE; Taylor summarizes it as roughly 1,500 years old.
- Many scholars place the final formation of the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* around the ninth or tenth century, though proposals vary. Museum catalogues sometimes use later dates, including “about 1200,” reflecting uncertainty over compilation and surviving manuscript traditions rather than a single universally accepted date.
- The *Matsya Purāṇa* 45 and *Brahma Purāṇa* 14–15 preserve close cognate narratives, but both texts are composite and cannot safely be used as simple chronological witnesses without manuscript-level comparison.

Accordingly, “earliest attestation” here means the earliest recoverable textual layer among the extant narrative witnesses, not proof that a gem existed in a datable archaeological period.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

This section separates the **critically constituted *Harivaṃśa* chain** from significant recensional and later variants.

### 3.1 Primary *Harivaṃśa* chain

#### Link 1: Ocean → Prasena

- **Mechanism:** Acquisition; exact mechanism unspecified.
- **Place:** Dvāravatī during its settlement; jewel said to come from the ocean.
- **Evidence:** *Harivaṃśa* 28.12, constituted text.
- **Status:** Earliest recoverable narrative claim; mythic, not documentary provenance.

#### Variant Link 1: Sūrya → Satrājit

- **Mechanism:** Divine gift granted after Satrājit requests the ornament.
- **Place:** Seashore encounter, followed by Dvārakā.
- **Evidence:** Starred passage after *Harivaṃśa* 28.12; developed in *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 4.13 and *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 10.56.
- **Status:** Ancient recensional variant/later consolidation.

#### Link 2 in the solar version: Satrājit → Prasena

- **Mechanism:** Fraternal gift or loan.
- **Evidence:** Additional *Harivaṃśa* passage says Satrājit gave it lovingly to his brother; later accounts often simply say Prasena wore it.
- **Status:** Recensional/later narrative.

#### Link 3: Prasena → lion

- **Mechanism:** Taking from a killed bearer.
- **Event:** Prasena wears the jewel on a hunt. A forest lion kills him because of or in connection with the jewel.
- **Place:** Wilderness, later associated in the *Harivaṃśa* with the Ṛkṣavat/Vindhya mountain region.
- **Evidence:** *Harivaṃśa* 28.15, 28.19–21.
- **Status:** Primary narrative.

#### Link 4: lion → Jāmbavān

- **Mechanism:** Jāmbavān kills the lion and takes the jewel.
- **Place:** Mountain wilderness; thereafter Jāmbavān’s cave.
- **Evidence:** *Harivaṃśa* 28.16, 28.21–24.
- **Status:** Primary narrative.

#### Link 5: Jāmbavān → child/nursery custody

- **Mechanism:** Given as a plaything to Jāmbavān’s child.
- **Keeper:** A nurse guards or attends the child.
- **Evidence:** The nurse’s celebrated verse, *Harivaṃśa* 28.24:

> “The lion killed Prasena; Jāmbavān killed the lion. Do not cry, little one: this Syamantaka is yours.”

- **Status:** Primary narrative.
- **Object-historical significance:** The jewel passes from royal and predatory possession into domestic use as a toy.

#### Link 6: Jāmbavān → Kṛṣṇa

- **Mechanism:** Surrender following combat; accompanied by Jāmbavān’s consent to Kṛṣṇa’s marriage with Jāmbavatī.
- **Duration of combat:** Twenty-one days in the constituted *Harivaṃśa* (28.26); twenty-eight days in the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa*.
- **Purpose of Kṛṣṇa’s acquisition:** *ātma-viśuddhaye*, “for the purification/vindication of himself” (28.29), after the Vṛṣṇis and Andhakas suspect him of killing Prasena to obtain the jewel.
- **Status:** Primary narrative with variant duration.

#### Link 7: Kṛṣṇa → Satrājit

- **Mechanism:** Public restoration.
- **Purpose:** Vindication of Kṛṣṇa and return to the claimant.
- **Evidence:** The end of *Harivaṃśa* chapter 28 and parallels.
- **Status:** Primary narrative.

#### Offered transfer: Satrājit → Kṛṣṇa

- **Mechanism:** Offered with Satyabhāmā in atonement or as a marriage gift.
- **Outcome:** Kṛṣṇa accepts Satyabhāmā but returns or declines the jewel.
- **Status:** Explicit in later developed narratives; reflected in the *Harivaṃśa* transition to chapter 29.

#### Link 8: Satrājit → Śatadhanvan

- **Mechanism:** Murder and theft.
- **Event:** Śatadhanvan kills Satrājit at night and takes the jewel.
- **Motives:** Desire for the jewel and resentment over Satyabhāmā’s marriage; Akrūra and Kṛtavarman are implicated to differing degrees by the sources.
- **Evidence:** *Harivaṃśa* 29.1–3; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 10.57.3–6.
- **Status:** Primary narrative.

The *Bhāgavata* emphasizes that Satrājit is murdered in his sleep. A sixteenth-century illustrated manuscript makes the seizure visually explicit by depicting Śatadhanvan decapitating Satrājit and taking the jewel. This image is a witness to early-modern reception, not independent evidence for the event. [Smithsonian object F1966.31](https://asia.si.edu/explore-art-culture/collections/search/edanmdm:fsg_F1966.31/)

#### Link 9: Śatadhanvan → Akrūra

- **Mechanism:** Secret deposit or entrustment before flight.
- **Condition:** In the *Harivaṃśa*, Akrūra makes Śatadhanvan promise not to disclose that he has it.
- **Evidence:** *Harivaṃśa* 29.3–5; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 10.57.
- **Status:** Primary narrative.

#### Failed recovery: Śatadhanvan searched after death

- **Event:** Kṛṣṇa and Balarāma pursue Śatadhanvan. His mare dies from exhaustion after a hundred-plus yojanas. Kṛṣṇa continues on foot and kills him near Mithilā.
- **Search result:** No jewel is found on his body or in his clothing.
- **Consequence:** Balarāma distrusts Kṛṣṇa’s explanation and temporarily leaves for Mithilā.
- **Evidence:** *Harivaṃśa* 29.14–23; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 10.57.20–23.
- **Status:** Primary narrative.

#### Link 10: Akrūra’s long custody

- **Mechanism:** Continued possession following Śatadhanvan’s deposit.
- **Use:** Akrūra performs numerous sacrifices, spending wealth and using golden altars. The *Harivaṃśa* speaks of sacrifices extending over sixty years; the number functions within legendary chronology and cannot be converted into a historical date.
- **Protection strategy:** The *Harivaṃśa* says Akrūra adopts sacrificial initiation like armor, since a consecrated sacrificer is inviolable.
- **Evidence:** *Harivaṃśa* 29.24–27.
- **Status:** Primary narrative.

#### Removal from Dvārakā and return

The *Harivaṃśa* reports a complicated factional episode:

- Akrūra leaves Dvārakā with Andhakas.
- Drought follows his absence.
- The Kukuras and Andhakas placate him.
- When he returns to coastal Dvārakā, rain returns.

The *Bhāgavata* partly rationalizes this: citizens attribute the disturbances to Akrūra’s absence because his father Śvaphalka had once been associated with the ending of a drought at Kāśī, but Kṛṣṇa knows Akrūra’s absence is not the sole cause. This preserves two interpretations—popular causal belief and narrator-endorsed qualification.

#### Link 11: Akrūra → Kṛṣṇa → Akrūra

- **Mechanism:** Compelled public production, inspection, and return.
- **Event:** Kṛṣṇa announces that he knows the jewel is in Akrūra’s hands and asks that it be shown in the assembly. Akrūra produces it.
- **Legal complication:** In the *Bhāgavata*, Kṛṣṇa states that because Satrājit had no son, Satyabhāmā’s sons are the residual heirs after funeral obligations and debts. Nevertheless, Akrūra is the person fit to keep the dangerous or difficult jewel.
- **Public authentication:** Kṛṣṇa shows it to his relatives, clears the renewed suspicion against himself, and returns it to Akrūra.
- **Evidence:** *Harivaṃśa* 29.35–40; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 10.57.35–41.
- **Final wording:** The *Harivaṃśa* ends with Akrūra fastening the jewel on himself and shining like the Sun. The *Bhāgavata* likewise ends the object’s chain by saying Kṛṣṇa returned it to him. [*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 10.57.41, text and translation](https://vedabase.io/en/library/sb/10/57/41/)

### 3.2 Custody after Akrūra

No principal narrative consulted supplies a traceable post-Akrūra transfer. There is no documented inheritance, burial, loss, destruction, royal-treasury accession, temple installation, or archaeological discovery.

The recoverable chain therefore ends:

> **Akrūra, at Dvārakā — mythic narrative custody; subsequent fate unstated.**

Any proposed history linking it from Akrūra to a later historical gemstone contains an unbridged gap of many centuries and lacks a continuous evidentiary chain.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Does a physical Syamantaka exist?

No physical object has been authenticated as the Syamantaka jewel. The dossier found no:

- museum object bearing a defensible Syamantaka accession;
- temple treasury catalogue documenting continuous custody from antiquity;
- excavated gemstone bearing an identifying inscription;
- laboratory analysis tied to a credible claimant;
- premodern inventory bridging the narrative to a surviving stone;
- chain of ownership beginning with any datable historical custodian.

The correct status is therefore **legendary object, physical existence unverified**—not “proved nonexistent,” but unsupported as a recoverable artifact.

### 4.2 The central identity dispute is textual

The oldest identity problem is not between two modern gemstones but between two accounts of the jewel’s first human acquisition:

- **Prasena from the ocean** in the critical *Harivaṃśa* text.
- **Satrājit from Sūrya** in widely transmitted additions and later Purāṇas.

These accounts may reflect redactional combination. Couture calls attention to the story’s “double origin” and interprets the combination structurally and cosmologically. [Couture 2013](https://poj.peeters-leuven.be/content.php?id=2994462&journal_code=JA&url=article.php)

### 4.3 Internal authentication scenes

The story itself stages authentication twice.

#### First authentication

Kṛṣṇa traces a physical sequence:

1. Prasena’s footprints or path;
2. Prasena and his horse dead;
3. the lion dead nearby;
4. bear tracks leading onward;
5. the cave;
6. the nurse’s statement naming the chain;
7. the recovered jewel.

Within the narrative, this is forensic reconstruction used to rebut the allegation that Kṛṣṇa murdered Prasena.

#### Second authentication

After Śatadhanvan’s death, the jewel’s absence reopens suspicion. Kṛṣṇa identifies Akrūra as custodian; Akrūra produces the stone publicly; Kṛṣṇa exhibits it to the kin group and returns it. Here authenticity rests on recognition by the assembly and continuity within living memory, not scientific testing.

### 4.4 Mineral identity

Claims that the Syamantaka was a diamond or ruby are not supported by the primary Sanskrit designation *maṇi*. The sources describe radiance and supernatural production, not refractive index, crystal structure, inclusions, or hardness. A specific gemological identification cannot be made.

### 4.5 Alleged identifications with famous diamonds

Modern internet retellings sometimes conflate or compare the Syamantaka with famous Indian diamonds, particularly the Koh-i-Noor, because both are celebrated, mobile, contested jewels. No historical evidence establishes identity:

- the narratives supply no matching dimensions or mineral species;
- the Syamantaka’s chain ends with Akrūra;
- the historically reconstructable diamond traditions begin vastly later;
- no intermediate inventory or inscription joins the chains.

Such equivalence is an **unsupported modern identification**, not an unresolved scholarly provenance dispute.

### 4.6 Absence of scientific testing

Because no credible specimen has been produced, there has been no meaningful:

- Raman spectroscopy;
- X-ray diffraction;
- trace-element sourcing;
- inclusion analysis;
- radiocarbon dating—the latter would not ordinarily date a mineral gemstone itself;
- tool-mark study;
- custody-based forensic examination.

No church, crown, museum, laboratory, or archaeological authority currently has recognized standing over an authenticated Syamantaka artifact.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Domestic installation and worship

In the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa*, Satrājit does not merely store the jewel. He installs it on an altar in his house, with brahmins conducting worship. Its beneficial effects depend on its being properly honored and on a ritually qualified or morally pure keeper.

This makes it simultaneously:

- personal property;
- divine gift;
- household cult object;
- economic generator;
- public-welfare talisman.

### 6.2 Gold production

The early constituted *Harivaṃśa* says it “pours forth gold.” A widespread additional passage and later texts specify **eight bhāras daily**. Because *bhāra* is a variable traditional weight term and the story is supernatural, modern conversion into kilograms would create false precision.

Gold connects the jewel to:

- Satrājit’s wealth;
- Akrūra’s repeated sacrifices;
- golden sacrificial altars;
- the suspicion that conspicuous religious expenditure proves secret possession.

The object thus converts divine brilliance into sacrificial and political capital.

### 6.3 Protection and prosperity

The jewel is credited with preventing or alleviating:

- untimely or inadequate rain;
- drought;
- disease;
- famine;
- environmental calamity;
- premature death;
- snake danger;
- social or mental affliction.

The *Harivaṃśa* initially places these benefits at the level of the Vṛṣṇi-Andhaka settlement. The *Bhāgavata* emphasizes the condition of correct worship and a pure custodian. This development turns the object from an automatic talisman into an ethically conditioned sacred possession.

### 6.4 Royal and commonwealth function

Kṛṣṇa’s request that Satrājit give the jewel to Ugrasena frames it as too consequential for merely private retention. Possession raises a constitutional question:

> Is a prosperity-producing divine object the private property of its recipient, dynastic property, or a public resource properly entrusted to the ruler?

The texts do not resolve this abstractly. They resolve it pragmatically by repeatedly returning the jewel to an appropriate keeper while treating its benefits as collective.

### 6.5 Sacrificial function

Akrūra uses the gold associated with the jewel to sustain prolonged sacrifices. In the *Harivaṃśa*, sacrificial consecration also acts as legal and ritual armor: while initiated, he cannot easily be attacked for the object. The jewel thereby finances the ceremony that protects its possessor.

### 6.6 Reputation and juridical function

The Syamantaka’s most persistent narrative function is evidentiary. Its appearance before an assembly clears Kṛṣṇa’s name twice:

- after Prasena’s death;
- after Śatadhanvan’s death and Akrūra’s concealment.

The *Bhāgavata* closes with precisely this action: Kṛṣṇa displays the jewel to his relatives, removes the “stain” upon himself, and returns it to Akrūra. The object is therefore both alleged motive and exculpatory exhibit.

### 6.7 Recitation as ritual remedy

In later Gaṇeśa Caturthī tradition, seeing the Moon on the prohibited lunar date is believed to expose a person to false accusation. Kṛṣṇa’s Syamantaka ordeal is interpreted as the exemplary case. Hearing or reciting the story—and especially the verse summarizing the lion/Jāmbavān chain—serves as a remedy.

The commonly recited formula derives from the nurse’s *Harivaṃśa* verse:

> *siṃhaḥ prasenam avadhīt siṃho jāmbavatā hataḥ /  
> sukumāraka mā rodīs tava hy eṣa syamantakaḥ //*

This application is a later ritual integration of the Kṛṣṇa story with the Ganeśa-and-Moon cycle. It should not be projected into the earliest critical *Harivaṃśa* layer. An Indira Gandhi National Centre for the Arts publication attributes the Moon-curse connection to later Purāṇic festival tradition. [IGNCA, *Hindu Holidays: Ganeśa Caturthī*](https://ignca.gov.in/Asi_data/19776.pdf)

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The Sun’s embodied radiance

**Story:** Satrājit approaches Sūrya but complains that the god looks no different nearby than in the sky: he sees only a blaze. Sūrya removes the Syamantaka from his neck, revealing a more distinct anthropomorphic body. Satrājit asks for the very object whose light concealed the god, receives it, and returns wearing solar radiance himself.

**Tier:** Ancient recensional elaboration, subsequently canonical in later Purāṇas.

**Change introduced:** The jewel ceases to be merely ocean-found treasure and becomes transferable solar essence.

### 7.2 Mistaken identity at Dvārakā

**Story:** Citizens see Satrājit wearing the gem and believe Sūrya himself has entered the city. Kṛṣṇa correctly identifies him.

**Function:** Establishes the stone’s extraordinary brilliance and Kṛṣṇa’s superior discernment.

**Tier:** Solar-origin recension and later Purāṇas.

### 7.3 The jewel refused to the polity

**Story:** Kṛṣṇa asks that it be given to Ugrasena, ruler of the Yādavas, so that its prosperity will benefit the kingdom. Satrājit refuses through attachment or greed.

**Function:** Converts later disaster into a consequence of private monopolization; frames the jewel as public-benefit wealth.

**Tier:** Especially developed in the *Bhāgavata* and devotional retellings.

### 7.4 The accusation against Kṛṣṇa

**Story:** After Prasena disappears, Satrājit or the broader clan infers that Kṛṣṇa, who had wanted the gem, killed him. Kṛṣṇa undertakes an evidentiary search.

**Function:** The jewel tests reputation, kinship trust, and legitimate sovereignty.

**Tier:** Core early narrative.

### 7.5 The chain-of-death verse

The nurse’s verse is the narrative in miniature:

> Prasena is killed by the lion;  
> the lion is killed by Jāmbavān;  
> the jewel belongs to the child.

This verse is unusually important to the object’s history because it is both:

- an in-story statement of immediate provenance;
- a later liturgical formula against false accusation.

### 7.6 Battle in the cave

**Story:** Kṛṣṇa enters Jāmbavān’s cave and attempts to recover the stone. Jāmbavān fails initially to recognize him. They fight until Jāmbavān submits.

**Variants:**

- Twenty-one days in the critical *Harivaṃśa*.
- Twenty-eight days in the *Bhāgavata*.
- Later devotional accounts identify Kṛṣṇa explicitly with Rāma, whom Jāmbavān served, making recognition a theological reunion.
- The waiting party remains outside twelve days in an additional *Harivaṃśa* passage and the *Bhāgavata*.

**Object function:** The jewel precipitates recognition of Kṛṣṇa’s trans-incarnational identity.

### 7.7 Two marriages attached to one recovery

Jāmbavān gives Jāmbavatī with the jewel; Satrājit later gives Satyabhāmā while offering the jewel in expiation. The stone is not simply “dowry” in a consistent legal sense:

- Jāmbavān’s surrender combines treasure restitution and marriage alliance.
- Satrājit’s offer combines atonement, alliance, and property transfer.
- Kṛṣṇa repeatedly refuses permanent personal ownership.

### 7.8 The “curse” of the jewel

Modern summaries often call the Syamantaka a cursed gem because deaths and disputes follow it. The primary texts describe conditional auspiciousness more precisely:

- with a pure, properly worshipping keeper, it brings prosperity;
- greed, concealment, accusation, and unqualified custody produce conflict.

A freestanding curse inherent in the mineral is therefore a **modern simplification**, not the clearest formulation of the Sanskrit accounts.

### 7.9 The Moon curse and Gaṇeśa Caturthī

**Later legend:** The Moon laughs at Ganeśa and is cursed so that anyone who sees it on the relevant fourth lunar day will suffer false accusation. Kṛṣṇa sees the Moon and is subsequently accused in the Syamantaka affair. Recitation of the Syamantaka story dispels the fault.

**Tier:** Later Purāṇic/festival accretion and continuing living tradition.

**Historical caution:** The core critical *Harivaṃśa* episode contains no causal role for Ganeśa or a prohibited lunar sighting. The two cycles were joined later.

### 7.10 Treasure-survival legends

Claims that the jewel remains:

- underwater at submerged Dvārakā;
- hidden in a Himalayan or Jāmbavān cave;
- in a secret temple treasury;
- reincarnated as a famous diamond;

are not supported by a continuous premodern source chain in the consulted evidence. They belong to treasure folklore or modern speculation unless a particular claim supplies independently verifiable documentation.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Physical object

As of 6 September 2026:

- no authenticated physical Syamantaka has been identified;
- no institution publishes a credible accession number for it;
- no recognized archaeological excavation reports its discovery;
- no laboratory has examined an accepted claimant;
- no government, temple, dynasty, or museum has established legal title to an authenticated original;
- no active restitution or repatriation proceeding concerns an object demonstrably identified as the Syamantaka.

The correct present status is:

> **Legendary; physical existence and present location unestablished.**

### 8.2 Last textual location

The principal narrative ends with the jewel:

- publicly displayed by Kṛṣṇa at Dvārakā;
- returned to Akrūra;
- worn or kept by Akrūra.

No account consulted securely continues the custody chain beyond him.

### 8.3 Places associated with the narrative

These are narrative locations, not authenticated archaeological findspots:

- the seashore where Satrājit encounters Sūrya in the solar version;
- Dvārakā/Dvāravatī;
- Prasena’s hunting ground;
- the Ṛkṣavat/Vindhya region in the *Harivaṃśa*;
- Jāmbavān’s cave;
- the route toward Mithilā, where Śatadhanvan is killed;
- Mithilā, where Balarāma stays;
- Kāśī, invoked in the drought tradition surrounding Akrūra’s family.

No known “Jāmbavān cave” identification supplies an archaeological chain to the narrated jewel.

### 8.4 Real objects standing in for the legend

The most securely catalogued material objects associated with Syamantaka are **representations**, not claimants:

- A ca. 1520 Delhi-Agra *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* painting, *The Sleeping Shatrajit Murdered by Satadhanva*, Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art, accession **F1966.31**. It was acquired from Heeramaneck Galleries in 1966. [Smithsonian catalogue](https://asia.si.edu/explore-art-culture/collections/search/edanmdm:fsg_F1966.31/)
- *Illustration from a Bhagavata Purana Series: Story of the Syamantaka Gem*, ca. 1700, Madhya Pradesh, opaque watercolor on paper, Norton Simon Museum, accession **P.2003.02.06**. [Norton Simon Museum](https://www.nortonsimon.org/art/detail/P.2003.02.06)
- *A Lion Kills Prasena in the Jungle*, ca. 1775, an illustration emphasizing the jewel’s power to produce gold and avert calamity, Philadelphia Museum of Art, object **88523**. [Philadelphia Museum of Art](https://www.philamuseum.org/objects/88523)
- A painting attributed to Fattu, ca. 1760–65, *Jambavan Kills a Lion and Obtains the Syamantaka Jewel*, from the “Large” Guler-Basohli *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* series, sold through Christie’s. [Christie’s catalogue](https://www.christies.com/en/lot/lot-6528585)

These works document the visual reception of the story. None purports to be the jewel itself.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Painting and manuscript illustration

The Syamantaka narrative proved especially suitable for sequential painting because the object’s motion supplies a clear visual chain:

1. Satrājit’s solar encounter;
2. Prasena wearing the jewel;
3. lion attack;
4. Jāmbavān killing the lion;
5. jewel beside the child;
6. Kṛṣṇa’s cave combat;
7. Satrājit’s murder;
8. public exhibition.

The ca. 1520 Smithsonian folio compresses successive moments into one architectural composition: murder, possession, preservation of Satrājit’s corpse in oil, Satyabhāmā’s departure, and mourning women. Its catalogue history also shows how the scene’s identification improved: dealer documents initially called it only a generic murder scene before curatorial study identified the narrative. [Smithsonian catalogue](https://asia.si.edu/explore-art-culture/collections/search/edanmdm:fsg_F1966.31/)

The Norton Simon example, ca. 1700, belongs to a Rajput painting tradition and is currently not on view. Its published accession and measurements demonstrate the difference between securely catalogued **representations of Syamantaka** and the uncatalogued legendary stone. [Norton Simon Museum](https://www.nortonsimon.org/art/detail/P.2003.02.06)

### 9.2 Comics and children’s literature

Amar Chitra Katha published *The Syamantaka Gem: The Sun God’s Precious Ornament*, scripted by Kamala Chandrakant and illustrated by H. S. Chavan. The comic consolidates the familiar later version:

- solar gift;
- prosperity and famine prevention;
- refusal to transfer;
- murder and theft;
- Kṛṣṇa’s vindication;
- Akrūra’s final declaration.

The publisher’s current web retelling continues to present the story as one in which the object clears Kṛṣṇa’s name. [Amar Chitra Katha](https://www.amarchitrakatha.com/mythologies/the-syamantaka-jewel/)

### 9.3 Devotional publication

A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda retold the episode in *Kṛṣṇa, the Supreme Personality of Godhead*. This presentation strongly emphasizes:

- the spiritual danger of material attachment;
- Kṛṣṇa’s divine omniscience;
- Satrājit’s offense and atonement;
- the jewel’s daily gold production;
- the correctness of Akrūra’s eventual public disclosure.

This has materially shaped global English-language awareness of the object. [Prabhupāda’s retelling](https://www.krsnabook.com/ch55.html)

### 9.4 Festival culture

In contemporary Ganeśa Caturthī practice, the object’s story is widely recited as protection against false accusation after accidental sight of the Moon. The jewel therefore remains ritually operative as a **narrated object** even without a physical relic.

The transition is important:

- In the early narrative, the jewel itself is recovered and exhibited.
- In later ritual, hearing the jewel’s history becomes the remedy.
- The material object’s power is transferred to a text and act of recitation.

### 9.5 Commercial and internet reception

Commercial jewelry pages, mythology blogs, and social-media posts frequently describe Syamantaka as:

- “the world’s most powerful diamond”;
- a lost historical treasure;
- the ancestor of a known famous gem;
- a cursed stone;
- a source of unlimited gold.

Those descriptions often collapse textual layers and omit the ocean-origin reading. Unless accompanied by verifiable historical provenance, they are modern retellings rather than evidence about the object.

### 9.6 Modern moralization

Recent retellings interpret the story variously as teaching:

- truth will ultimately emerge;
- private greed harms society;
- wealth requires a righteous custodian;
- reputation should be cleared through evidence;
- material possession creates anxiety;
- divine property belongs to the community.

These themes are reasonable interpretations but should not be confused with physical provenance.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Text-critical history

The decisive modern advance was P. L. Vaidya’s critical edition of the *Harivaṃśa*. It distinguishes:

- the shorter constituted ocean-origin reading;
- the widely transmitted solar-donation passages;
- regional and manuscript-family additions;
- variant durations and narrative expansions.

The GRETIL transcription makes both constituted and starred material searchable but should be used with awareness that it is an electronic derivative of the printed edition. [GRETIL text](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/1_sanskr/2_epic/mbh/ext/hv_conpu.htm)

### 10.2 Genealogy versus biography

Christopher Austin studies why the episode appears at the end of a genealogical sequence rather than in the main continuous Kṛṣṇa biography. He argues that its position reveals the importance of genealogy as a framework for Kṛṣṇa’s identity and should not simply be dismissed as clumsy editing. [Austin](https://doi.org/10.1558/ROSA.V5I1/2.153)

### 10.3 Cosmological and sacrificial interpretation

André Couture argues that the narrative’s details form a structured whole:

- ocean and Sun origins;
- lion and bear intermediaries;
- gold and sacrifice;
- Akrūra’s elaborate sacrificial activity;
- solar and lunar dynastic symbolism.

On this interpretation the jewel’s circulation is not incidental adventure. It is a device for asserting that Kṛṣṇa’s sovereignty encompasses sacrificial and cosmic domains. [Couture](https://poj.peeters-leuven.be/content.php?id=2994462&journal_code=JA&url=article.php)

### 10.4 Source relationship among Purāṇas

The story occurs in closely related forms in:

- *Harivaṃśa* 28–29 critical edition;
- *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 4.13;
- *Matsya Purāṇa* 45;
- *Brahma Purāṇa* 14–15;
- *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 10.56–57;
- references or cognate material in other Purāṇic traditions.

Similarity does not by itself prove that one extant printed Purāṇa copied directly from another in its present form. These are composite texts with intersecting manuscript and oral traditions.

The *Brahma Purāṇa*’s placement is noteworthy: the Syamantaka episode appears separately from its regular Kṛṣṇa biography, a structural feature also noticed in scholarship on that Purāṇa. A published critical study remarks on this separation and compares the account with the *Harivaṃśa*, *Viṣṇu*, *Matsya*, and *Bhāgavata* traditions. [Critical study of the *Brahma Purāṇa*](https://www.wisdomlib.org/uploads/ocr/essays/brahmapurana-critical/vol1/14-chapter.pdf)

### 10.5 Translation problems

Major translation hazards include:

- rendering *maṇi* as a specific mineral;
- converting *bhāra* into a precise modern weight;
- silently mixing critical text with starred additions;
- treating the *Harivaṃśa*’s 21-day fight and the *Bhāgavata*’s 28-day fight as one datum;
- calling the offered jewel a straightforward dowry in every transfer;
- treating Akrūra’s culpability as identical across all versions;
- confusing Babhru, an epithet or alternate name used for Akrūra in this context, with a separate custodian;
- identifying narrative Dvārakā directly with a specific excavated layer without independent evidence.

### 10.6 State of the primary evidence

What survives:

- extensive Sanskrit manuscript traditions represented in critical and printed editions;
- early modern illustrated manuscripts;
- commentarial and devotional traditions;
- modern museum catalogues for representations.

What does not survive:

- a contemporary inscription naming the object;
- a datable inventory of a physical jewel;
- a continuous material provenance;
- a securely identified original specimen;
- scientific examination of an accepted claimant.

### 10.7 Discredited or unsupported popular theories

The following remain unsupported:

- Syamantaka is definitively a diamond.
- It is definitively a ruby.
- It has been scientifically identified.
- It is the Koh-i-Noor or another named historical diamond.
- It has been excavated at Dvārakā.
- It is held secretly by a specified modern temple or royal family.
- Its gold production describes a known ancient technology.
- The Moon-curse episode belongs to the earliest recoverable *Harivaṃśa* text.

### Final assessment

The Syamantaka’s fullest reconstructable history is a history of textual transformations and narrated transfers, not of a scientifically identifiable mineral. Its earliest recoverable form is a “great divine jewel” obtained from the ocean by Prasena. A widespread ancient variant relocates its origin to the neck of Sūrya and makes Satrājit its first human recipient. Thereafter its custody chain is unusually explicit: Prasena, lion, Jāmbavān, child, Kṛṣṇa, Satrājit, Śatadhanvan, Akrūra, Kṛṣṇa for public inspection, and Akrūra again.

Its identity changes with its keepers. It is successively ocean treasure, solar ornament, royal regalia, hunting adornment, predator’s spoil, child’s toy, disputed evidence, marriage gift, murder loot, concealed deposit, source of sacrificial gold, commonwealth talisman, and public exhibit. The chain ends in narrative Dvārakā with Akrūra. No documentary seam joins that chain to a surviving gemstone. The real and continuing object is consequently the transmitted story—recited, painted, published, and ritually invoked against false accusation.
