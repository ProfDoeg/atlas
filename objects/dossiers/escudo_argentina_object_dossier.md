# The Escudo (coat of arms) of Argentina: Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `escudo_nacional_argentino_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary note

The **Escudo Nacional Argentino** is not a single portable artifact with an uninterrupted chain of custody. It is an **emblematic design**—originally the image of an official seal—whose identity survives through many physical realizations: engraved matrices, wax impressions, painted plaques, manuscript and printed images, textiles, coins, medals, architectural ornaments, government stationery, and digital files.

This distinction is essential. The documentary record establishes:

1. a seal used by the **Soberana Asamblea General Constituyente** in February 1813;
2. an order of March 1813 directing the executive government to use the same design with a different inscription;
3. the rapid transplantation of the image into flags, public buildings, coins, and administrative papers;
4. almost a century and a half of variant renderings;
5. the adoption in 1944 of the Assembly seal as the official prototype.

It does **not** establish that one unique physical object called “the Escudo” has passed intact from 1813 to the present. Nor has a published scientific examination demonstrated that a particular surviving metal matrix is conclusively the unique matrix from which every early impression was made. The most defensible “object biography” is therefore the history of the **design, its legal identity, and its securely documented embodiments**.

Evidentiary labels used below are:

- **Documented:** supported by contemporary legislation, archival records, catalogues, or surviving objects.
- **Institutional attribution:** asserted by a responsible Argentine public institution, but not necessarily demonstrated by a surviving contract or technical examination.
- **Scholarly inference:** reasoned interpretation rather than an explicit contemporary statement.
- **Tradition:** a national, institutional, or popular account repeated after the event.
- **Disputed or unresolved:** competing accounts cannot presently be adjudicated from published evidence.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Identity and names

The principal modern name is **Escudo Nacional Argentino**, “Argentine National Coat of Arms.” Official texts also use:

- **Escudo Argentino**
- **Escudo de Armas de la Nación**
- **Escudo Nacional**
- **Sello de la Soberana Asamblea General Constituyente**
- **Sello de la Asamblea del Año XIII**
- **Armas de la Asamblea**
- **Gran Sello de la Nación**, for a related official form retaining the inscription-bearing outer or “coronary” zone.

In the language of the March 1813 decree the operative artifact was a **sello**, a seal used to authenticate documents. In subsequent orders it was described as the **armas** of the Assembly. Over time the image migrated from seal to state arms, and *escudo* consequently came to name both the graphic composition and its material reproductions.

Spanish *escudo* denotes both a defensive shield and, by extension, the shield-shaped or shield-like field bearing heraldic arms. The Argentine emblem is not a conventional medieval shield in outline: its central field is an upright oval or ellipse. The term nevertheless follows ordinary Hispanic heraldic usage.

### 1.2 The legally controlling identity

Article 5 of **Decree 10,302 of 24 April 1944** provides that the representation of the Argentine arms is to be the faithful reproduction of the seal used by the 1813 Assembly. It separately reserves as the **Gran Sello de la Nación** the design retaining the region between the two ellipses—the annular space in which the institutional inscription appears.

Thus two related forms must be distinguished:

- **National arms:** the central emblem without the Assembly’s circular institutional legend.
- **Great Seal:** the seal-form design retaining the outer annulus or “región coronaria.”

The attached authentic reproductions named in the decree formed part of administrative file **Expediente 19.974-F-1943**. The online text of the decree does not itself provide a modern conservation catalogue for those annexed originals.

### 1.3 Official visual composition

The current official description is an oval divided horizontally:

- upper field: **azur**, rendered as light blue;
- lower field: **plata**, conventionally represented as white;
- two bare human forearms entering from the lower flanks;
- two right hands clasped at the center;
- a vertical pike held by the hands;
- a red or **gules** liberty cap at its upper end;
- a rising, human-faced golden sun above the oval;
- two fruitless laurel branches surrounding the oval;
- a light-blue, white, and light-blue ribbon tying the branches below.

The Argentine government’s current descriptive page specifies:

- an upright oval;
- a rising sun with **21 rays**—11 straight and 10 undulating;
- fruitless laurels which do not meet at the top;
- on the heraldic right branch, 21 inner and 20 outer leaves;
- on the heraldic left branch, 23 inner and 25 outer leaves.

The oval is commonly described in modern heraldic literature as having a proportion of approximately **14:11**. That ratio is a rule of graphic construction, not a dimension of a unique artifact.

### 1.4 Inscriptions

The emblematic core itself has no motto or textual inscription. Early seal forms did.

The 1813 Assembly seal carried a circular institutional legend identifying the sovereign constituent assembly. Sources abbreviate or transcribe it in forms corresponding to:

> **Asamblea General Constituyente de las Provincias Unidas del Río de la Plata**

The March 1813 decree ordered the executive to use the same design, changing the circular inscription to:

> **Supremo Poder Ejecutivo de las Provincias Unidas del Río de la Plata**

The decree therefore proves that at least two institutionally distinct seal forms were contemplated. Treating every surviving early impression as the product of one unchanged matrix would be unsafe.

The closely related coin design carried different legends. The Assembly’s monetary act of 13 April 1813 prescribed:

> **PROVINCIAS DEL RÍO DE LA PLATA**

and, on the other face:

> **EN UNIÓN Y LIBERTAD**

The latter is not inscribed on the national arms but became inseparably associated with its political meaning.

### 1.5 Materials and surviving embodiments

Because the emblem is a reproducible design, no single material description applies. Important early embodiments include:

### 1.6 Damage, alterations, and conservation

The history of the emblem contains two different kinds of alteration:

1. **Physical change to particular artifacts**, such as fading, textile deterioration, repainting, corrosion, restoration, or overpainting.
2. **Iconographic variation**, in which artists altered ray counts, leaf counts, colors, anatomy, proportions, cap direction, or decorative surrounds.

An especially important technical finding concerns the Museo Histórico Nacional’s copper badge or plaque. Infrared imaging revealed that its Assembly imagery was **painted over an earlier badge of King Charles III**. That finding documents literal material reuse: an emblem of revolutionary sovereignty was superimposed on a monarchical support.

The Jujuy flag has been subject to restricted movement for conservation. Between 1894 and 1920 it travelled to national commemorations; later authorities prohibited its removal from San Salvador de Jujuy. It is now protected in a dedicated interpretation or exhibition space adjoining the historic Flag Hall.

No published evidence located for this dossier reports metallographic, X-ray fluorescence, or tool-mark analysis of the alleged original silver and bronze seal matrices. No radiocarbon analysis would be applicable to the graphic design itself.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Political and administrative setting

The Assembly of the Year XIII opened in Buenos Aires on **31 January 1813**. The revolutionary governments had not yet formally declared independence and had continued using royal Spanish arms in important administrative contexts. A sovereign constituent assembly required an authenticating device that represented its own claimed authority.

The traditional institutional account states that the Assembly commissioned the deputy for San Luis, **Agustín José Donado**, to arrange a new seal. Donado in turn engaged the Cuzco-born silversmith, engraver, and artist **Juan de Dios Rivera**.

The national government and museum institutions now regularly credit Rivera with the definitive engraving. Yet they are more cautious about the drawing itself: the design has variously been attributed to Rivera, Donado, **Antonio Isidro de Castro**, or **Bernardo de Monteagudo**. The Casa Rosada’s own historical presentation states plainly that no source conclusively certifies the design’s origin.

### 2.2 Earliest securely dated physical evidence

The earliest securely identified surviving impressions are on citizenship letters dated **22 February 1813**, before the decree conventionally celebrated as the arms’ “creation.”

One letter was issued to **Francisco de Paula Saubidet**; another, preserved by the Museo Histórico Nacional, was issued to **Antonio Olavarría** and signed by Assembly president **Carlos María de Alvear** and secretary **Hipólito Vieytes**.

These impressions establish that:

- the design existed materially by 22 February;
- the Assembly was already using it to authenticate formal acts;
- 12 March was not the date on which an artist first drew or engraved it.

The exact day of manufacture remains unestablished in the published material consulted.

### 2.3 The decree of March 1813

The Assembly’s recorded decision is conventionally dated **12 March 1813**, although printed records and later reproductions sometimes associate it with the session of 13 March or with the issue of *El Redactor de la Asamblea* dated 20 March. The operative text ordered:

> The General Assembly orders that the Supreme Executive Power use the same seal as this Sovereign Body, with the sole difference that the inscription in the circle shall be: “Supreme Executive Power of the United Provinces of the Río de la Plata.”

The decision was signed by president **Tomás Antonio Valle** and secretary **Hipólito Vieytes**.

Its precise legal effect should not be overstated. It directly prescribed an **executive seal** derived from the Assembly’s seal. It did not contain a modern heraldic blazon, color chart, construction grid, or declaration that one immutable image was henceforth the national coat of arms. The transformation from institutional seal into national arms occurred through official use and subsequent legislation.

### 2.4 Expansion to public arms

A decree of **27 April 1813** ordered royal arms on public buildings and on the arms or flags of public bodies to be replaced by those of the Assembly. This is the decisive step by which the seal-image became an outward emblem of the revolutionary polity.

The surviving painted-iron arms from the Assembly portal are material evidence of that architectural and public-facing use.

### 2.5 Manufacture and maker dispute

#### Rivera attribution

**Institutional attribution:** Juan de Dios Rivera is widely identified as the engraver of the definitive seal. He was a Cuzco-born artisan established in Buenos Aires and active as a portraitist, silversmith, and engraver.

#### Donado’s role

**Documented or strongly institutionalized:** Donado acted as intermediary or commissioner. That does not prove he designed the composition.

#### Antonio Isidro de Castro hypothesis

A rival reconstruction connects the design to drawings prepared by the Cuzco-born artist **Antonio Isidro de Castro**, reportedly sent from Santiago de Chile in 1812. The extent to which either drawing anticipated the adopted seal remains disputed.

#### Monteagudo hypothesis

Some accounts attribute an intellectual or artistic role to **Bernardo de Monteagudo**. No primary commission naming him as designer has been identified in the sources used here.

#### Conclusion

Rivera’s role as engraver is considerably better supported institutionally than any claim identifying a sole inventor of the iconography. Statements that he indisputably “created the Escudo” collapse several different tasks—political conception, drawing, adaptation, and engraving—into one.

### 2.6 Materials of the first matrices

The Casa Rosada reports that Donado negotiated with Rivera for **two seals, one silver and one bronze**. Published accounts do not consistently specify:

- which institution used which metal;
- their dimensions or weight;
- whether both survive;
- whether a surviving object has been technically matched to the February impressions.

Accordingly, “silver and bronze” is an institutional report concerning the original commission, not a complete scientific description of authenticated surviving matrices.

### 2.7 Iconographic sources

The joined hands, liberty cap, pike, wreath, and revolutionary sun belong to an international republican vocabulary, especially that of the French Revolution. A French revolutionary access pass or emblem showing clasped hands supporting a pike and liberty cap has often been proposed as a close visual precedent.

The similarities are real, but the precise path of copying is **scholarly inference**, not documented commission history. Claims that the model was specifically a “Jacobin club pass” should be presented cautiously unless the individual French object and its circulation to Buenos Aires are proven.

The sun introduced an American layer. The Museo Histórico Nacional explains that revolutionary suns could signify political renewal after tyranny while also evoking **Inti** and the Inca past for South American viewers. Monteagudo later described the sun as a historical expression of the country of the Incas. This supports an Americanist interpretation, but it does not prove that the Assembly formally commissioned “an Inca sun” under that exact name.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 Before 1813: predecessor imagery

The predecessor was not an earlier Argentine coat of arms but the Spanish royal arms used by viceregal and early revolutionary administrations. The copper badge now in the Museo Histórico Nacional gives unusually direct material evidence of the transition: revolutionary imagery was painted on top of an older royal badge.

### 3.2 January–February 1813: creation and first use

- **31 January:** Assembly begins sitting in Buenos Aires.
- **By 22 February:** its new seal is being impressed on citizenship letters.
- **Custody:** presumably held by Assembly officers or secretariat personnel responsible for authenticating documents. The sources consulted do not identify a named day-to-day seal keeper.
- **Transfer mechanism:** commission arranged through Donado; delivery details and payment have not been reconstructed here from a surviving original account.

### 3.3 March 1813: duplication for executive government

The March decree ordered the **Supreme Executive Power** to use the same emblem with a different annular inscription. This implies manufacture or allocation of a distinct executive matrix, since the legends could not be simultaneously identical and different on one fixed engraved seal.

The legal custody of the design thereby expanded from the Assembly to the executive. This was not a private transfer of ownership but an institutional replication authorized by the sovereign body.

### 3.4 April 1813: public buildings and corporations

The 27 April order required replacement of royal arms with Assembly arms on public buildings and corporate flags or shields. The emblem consequently passed into the hands of:

- government painters;
- metalworkers;
- embroiderers;
- military and civic standard-makers;
- provincial and municipal officials.

The painted-iron emblem formerly at the Assembly’s portal is a survivor of this process. It later entered the national historical collection and is now associated with the Museo Histórico Nacional. The publicly accessible museum summary does not provide its complete accession chain or number.

### 3.5 1813: Belgrano and Jujuy

Manuel Belgrano incorporated the Assembly arms into a white-satin banner, later called the **Bandera Nacional de Nuestra Libertad Civil**. On **25 May 1813** he had it blessed and delivered it to the Cabildo and people of Jujuy in recognition of their sacrifices and military contribution.

This is a genuine physical object with a separate, unusually strong provenance:

1. made under Belgrano’s authority;
2. ceremonially used in Jujuy;
3. given to the Cabildo and people of Jujuy;
4. preserved locally;
5. carried to other Argentine cities between 1894 and 1920;
6. thereafter restricted from further travel for conservation;
7. adopted as Jujuy’s provincial flag in 1994;
8. recognized by national Law 27,134 in 2015 as a **historic national symbol**;
9. presently preserved in the provincial government complex in San Salvador de Jujuy.

The banner is not the national arms themselves, but it is one of the earliest surviving, colored, monumental realizations of the design.

### 3.6 1813–1815: coinage

On **13 April 1813** the Assembly ordered new gold and silver coins at the Potosí mint. The prescribed design adapted the Assembly seal:

- the shield-like central image appeared without the rising sun above it;
- the other face bore a large radiant sun;
- the legends proclaimed the United Provinces and “En Unión y Libertad.”

A circular of 28 July 1813 concerning the first patriotic coinage survives in the Museo Histórico Nacional del Cabildo de Buenos Aires y de la Revolución de Mayo under catalogue identifier **AR-MC-CSXVIIIXIX01-UD-879**.

The emblem was therefore physically distributed through circulation. The “custody chain” became diffuse: mint officials made dies; governments issued coins; soldiers, merchants, and inhabitants handled them; later collectors and museums acquired surviving pieces. An 1815 specimen in the national patrimonial database is catalogued as **EJMMJN5042**.

### 3.7 Nineteenth-century diffusion and instability

During the post-revolutionary period the design was repeatedly redrawn. Variants appeared in:

- official seals;
- provincial arms;
- military flags;
- diplomatic and consular insignia;
- coins and medals;
- public buildings;
- books and newspapers.

There was no consistently enforced national master drawing. Variations affected:

- proportions of the oval;
- shades of blue;
- angle and anatomy of the arms;
- shape and direction of the cap;
- pike length;
- number and arrangement of solar rays;
- laurel structure and presence of fruits;
- flags, weapons, trophies, and other added ornaments;
- institutional inscriptions.

Under federal governments, especially in the political culture associated with Juan Manuel de Rosas, red and federal inscriptions could surround or modify national and provincial emblems. These products are historical variants or politically marked uses, not proof that the underlying 1813 emblem was legally extinguished.

### 3.8 1885 consular standardization

The Ministry of Foreign Affairs issued an order in **1885** with a model for Argentine consulates. This was the first important effort to standardize the emblem, but it applied only to the consular sphere. Domestic agencies continued to use divergent versions.

### 3.9 Zeballos’s investigation, 1890s–1900

Statesman and historian **Estanislao S. Zeballos** gathered examples of the arms and studied their accumulated alterations. His work, approved or supported by prominent historical authorities including **Bartolomé Mitre** and **Vicente Fidel López**, appeared in 1900 as *El escudo y los colores nacionales*.

Zeballos attempted to recover a historically defensible type from a field of inconsistent versions. His study became foundational, although later scholarship has rejected some of his arguments concerning other national symbols and colors.

### 3.10 1927–1928 official study

A commission chaired by the rector of the University of Buenos Aires examined the national symbols in 1927. The government accepted its conclusions by an agreement of **25 September 1928**. The 1944 decree expressly invoked this work as part of the evidentiary foundation for standardization.

### 3.11 1943–1944 standardization

Military governments issued several decrees in 1943 concerning the flag, sun, and presidential sash. On **24 April 1944**, de facto president **Edelmiro J. Farrell**, acting with his ministers, issued Decree 10,302.

The decree stated that variations had become arbitrary and that a fixed prototype was necessary. It adopted the authenticated reproductions attached to file 19.974-F-1943 and declared the faithful reproduction of the 1813 Assembly seal to be the Argentine arms.

This did not transfer a unique object. It moved **authority to define authenticity** into an executive legal instrument backed by expert and historical study.

### 3.12 Later legal status

The decree remains the central national legal authority. Later legislation modified provisions concerning other symbols but left Article 5’s definition of the arms intact.

The Trademark Law, **Law 22,362**, bars registration as private trademarks of symbols used or required to be used by the Nation, provinces, municipalities, and specified public bodies. The national arms are therefore not an ordinary alienable commercial mark.

Responsibility for the custody and use of the **Great Seal** in international instruments has historically belonged to the foreign ministry under ministerial legislation. The exact physical matrix presently used for each class of instrument is not described in the public sources consulted.

### 3.13 No documented theft or restitution history

No well-supported episode was located in which a unique “original Argentine coat of arms” was stolen, ransomed, restituted, or repatriated. Individual flags, plaques, coins, documents, and seals may each have separate provenance histories, but those histories should not be conflated with the emblem itself.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Is there an “original Escudo”?

There are at least four possible meanings:

1. the original drawing;
2. the first engraved Assembly matrix;
3. the first surviving impression;
4. the legal prototype adopted in 1944.

They are not the same object.

- The original drawing has not been securely identified.
- Rivera is institutionally credited with the engraving, but no published forensic comparison presented here proves that a surviving matrix made every early impression.
- The earliest securely identified surviving impressions date to 22 February 1813.
- The legally controlling emblem is a 1944 standard based on surviving Assembly documentation.

Any museum or popular claim to display “the original Escudo” must therefore specify which category is meant.

### 4.2 Designer dispute

The principal candidates are:

- **Juan de Dios Rivera:** strongest institutional attribution for engraving; possible designer or adaptor.
- **Agustín Donado:** commissioner and possible provider of the design.
- **Antonio Isidro de Castro:** proposed author of precursor drawings sent from Chile.
- **Bernardo de Monteagudo:** proposed intellectual or artistic contributor.

No source used here conclusively identifies one person as sole graphic inventor. The safest formulation is: **Donado arranged the commission; Rivera engraved the adopted seal; authorship of the underlying drawing remains unresolved.**

### 4.3 Date dispute

Official commemorations use **12 March 1813**. Some printed transcriptions label the pertinent sitting 13 March; the Assembly’s newspaper reported the matter later in March. None of this changes the fact that surviving impressions predate the decree.

Consequently:

- **22 February:** earliest presently identified material use;
- **12 March:** traditional and governmental date of official adoption or authorization;
- **27 April:** order generalizing the arms on public and corporate displays.

### 4.4 Seal versus coat of arms

A recurring historiographical error is to say that the Assembly enacted a fully specified national coat of arms on 12 March. The text actually authorized an executive **seal** based on the Assembly’s seal. National heraldic status emerged from expanded official use, the 27 April replacement order, and later state practice.

This distinction does not invalidate the modern symbol. It identifies the historical mechanism by which an administrative device became a national emblem.

### 4.5 Authenticity of the 1944 version

Decree 10,302 describes its model as a faithful reproduction of the Assembly seal and treats authenticated Assembly documents as authoritative. Yet “faithful” necessarily involved interpretation:

- early impressions are small and sometimes indistinct;
- seal imagery does not inherently encode color;
- numerous nineteenth-century colored versions differed;
- fine details such as leaf and ray counts require graphic reconstruction.

The 1944 image is therefore an **officially authenticated standard derived from early evidence**, not the physical survival of a single untouched 1813 drawing.

### 4.6 French-revolutionary-source dispute

The close resemblance to French revolutionary emblems supports influence. It does not prove:

- direct copying from one identified pass;
- membership of the designer in a particular French club;
- a specifically Masonic origin;
- that every element has one exclusive French meaning.

Masonic explanations are common in popular literature but remain unproven unless supported by a contemporary commission, drawing, diary, or correspondence. The composition is readily explicable within the broader Atlantic republican vocabulary.

### 4.7 Scientific and technical examinations

Known or reported examinations include:

- **Infrared examination of the Museo Histórico Nacional copper badge:** revealed earlier royal imagery under the Assembly design.
- **Textile conservation of the Jujuy flag:** its restricted movement and protected display reflect conservation policy, although a full technical report was not located in the public sources used.
- **Numismatic examination:** specialists compare dies, ray forms, and engravers’ practices in the 1813 and 1815 coinage, but these coins are adaptations rather than impressions from the Assembly document seal.

No DNA analysis is relevant. No published radiocarbon, alloy, or forensic tool-mark study establishing an original seal matrix was located.

### 4.8 Forgeries and false antiquity

No documented forged “original matrix” was identified in the sources consulted. The greater risk is imprecise attribution: later illustrations, enlarged reconstructions, or commemorative replicas are frequently captioned online as “the 1813 Escudo” without identifying whether they reproduce an actual impression, the 1944 standard, or a modern redrawing.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Authentication

The first function was juridical and administrative: the impressed seal certified that a document originated from the sovereign Assembly or executive government. The image’s authority derived not from supposed supernatural power but from the institution controlling the matrix.

### 6.2 Replacement of royal sovereignty

The removal of Spanish royal arms and their replacement by Assembly arms was an exercise in political substitution. The copper plaque painted over Charles III’s badge physically embodies this change.

The emblem declared that acts and buildings no longer drew legitimacy from the Spanish monarch but from the revolutionary political community represented by the Assembly.

### 6.3 Civic and military ceremony

Belgrano’s Jujuy banner was blessed and ceremonially delivered on 25 May 1813. The arms thus entered:

- Te Deum and blessing ceremonies;
- civic anniversaries;
- military display;
- municipal custody;
- commemorative travel.

The emblem on that banner represented what Belgrano and the Cabildo called **civil liberty**, later interpreted as constitutional order and equality before law.

### 6.4 Coinage and everyday sovereignty

By appearing on money, the design acted in the sovereign name of the United Provinces during countless transactions. The paired motto **En Unión y Libertad** supplied a verbal counterpart to the joined hands and liberty cap.

### 6.5 Conventional symbolism

The standard official interpretations are:

- **clasped hands:** union or fraternity of the provinces;
- **pike:** willingness and force to defend liberty;
- **red liberty cap:** freedom and republican emancipation;
- **rising sun:** birth of a new political order and, in Americanist readings, the Inca past;
- **laurels:** military victory and glory;
- **blue-and-white ribbon and fields:** Argentine nationality.

These meanings were not all enumerated in the 1813 decree. They developed through republican iconographic convention, state teaching, historical writing, and ceremonial practice.

### 6.6 The cap: Phrygian or pileus?

Modern usage calls it a **gorro frigio**, Phrygian cap. Historically, revolutionary artists often blended the curved Phrygian cap with the Roman **pileus**, the cap associated with manumitted slaves. The official Argentine interpretation emphasizes liberty rather than archaeological precision.

### 6.7 Access and handling rules

There is no evidence of a religious taboo forbidding ordinary people to view or reproduce the arms. Access restrictions concern:

- control of official seals;
- protection of state authenticity;
- conservation of historic embodiments;
- prevention of private trademark appropriation;
- protocol governing official use.

The Jujuy banner’s movement restriction is conservational rather than sacred in a formal religious sense.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 “Created on 12 March 1813”

**Type:** National commemorative simplification.

The familiar story states that the Escudo was created or first used on 12 March. Surviving 22 February impressions show that the image already existed and was in official use. The March decision formalized executive adoption; it did not mark the design’s first material existence.

The story’s endurance is explained by the utility of a single commemorative date. Argentina observes 12 March as **Día del Escudo Nacional**.

### 7.2 Rivera as sole creator

**Type:** Institutional tradition with a documented core and unresolved extension.

Rivera’s engraving role is credible and officially maintained. The assertion that he alone invented every component is not demonstrated. Donado, Castro, and Monteagudo remain part of the authorship debate.

### 7.3 Rivera as a relative of Túpac Amaru

**Type:** Biographical-national tradition requiring caution.

Modern accounts sometimes identify Rivera as a cousin or descendant in the extended lineage of Túpac Amaru. Such claims strengthen the interpretation of the sun as a deliberate Inca signature. The precise genealogy is not established by the object records used here and should not substitute for direct evidence of design intention.

### 7.4 The Jacobin pass

**Type:** Plausible iconographic-source hypothesis.

A French revolutionary pass or club emblem with clasped hands, pike, and liberty cap closely resembles the Argentine composition. Later writers have called it a Jacobin access pass to the Legislative Assembly.

The visual relationship deserves serious consideration; what remains unproven is the specific transmission event. No source presented here shows the pass in Donado’s or Rivera’s possession.

### 7.5 Masonic origin

**Type:** Unverified popular claim.

The Assembly’s political networks included members associated in later accounts with the Lautaro Lodge, and joined hands, radiant suns, and republican caps have been read as Masonic signs. These motifs were also widespread in non-Masonic revolutionary art. No contemporary design memorandum proving a Masonic program has been located.

### 7.6 The sun as Inti

**Type:** Historically grounded interpretation, not exclusive origin proof.

The sun could simultaneously signify dawn, revolution, regeneration, America, and the Inca past. The Museo Histórico Nacional explicitly presents this double European-revolutionary and Americanist meaning. Calling it simply a portrait or cult-image of Inti goes beyond the evidence.

### 7.7 A single unchanged original

**Type:** Modern misconception.

The emblem’s official identity is continuous, but its graphic form was not visually unchanged. Hundreds of variations arose before standardization. The 1944 decree itself acknowledges “arbitrary modifications” and sought to end them.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 The emblem

As of **6 September 2026**, the Escudo Nacional Argentino remains an official symbol of the Argentine Republic under Decree 10,302/1944. It is reproduced throughout state administration, diplomacy, courts, military and civic settings, public buildings, educational materials, currency-related imagery, and online government communications.

Because it is an intangible state emblem, it has no single physical address or accession number.

### 8.2 Principal early witnesses

#### Archivo General de la Nación, Argentina

The archive holds the March 1813 decree and identifies an early wax impression on the 22 February citizenship letter for Francisco de Paula Saubidet.

#### Museo Histórico Nacional, Buenos Aires

The museum preserves or publicly identifies:

- a citizenship letter for Antonio Olavarría bearing an early impression;
- the painted-iron national arms formerly placed at the Assembly portal;
- the oil-painted copper Assembly badge whose underlying royal image was detected by infrared examination;
- early revolutionary coins and related symbolic objects.

Not all public-facing museum pages provide accession numbers or complete conservation histories.

#### Museo Histórico Nacional del Cabildo de Buenos Aires y de la Revolución de Mayo

Its documentary collection includes the 28 July 1813 circular concerning replacement of the royal effigy with the Assembly seal on Potosí coinage, catalogue **AR-MC-CSXVIIIXIX01-UD-879**.

#### San Salvador de Jujuy

The original Bandera Nacional de la Libertad Civil, bearing a large painted form of the Assembly arms, is preserved by the Province of Jujuy in the government complex, now in a protected interpretive room adjoining the historic Flag Hall. Public access is by institutional display arrangements; removal is restricted.

### 8.3 Legal control

The Argentine state controls the emblem’s official definition. It is not private property capable of ordinary sale or inheritance. Its use as a private trademark is restricted by Law 22,362.

The **Great Seal of the Nation** is a governmental instrument; foreign-ministry legislation has assigned the ministry responsibility for its custody and use on international documents. Publicly available sources consulted here do not identify a current matrix by accession number, maker, metal, dimensions, or vault location.

### 8.4 Disputes

No active repatriation dispute over “the Escudo” itself was found. Individual early embodiments belong to Argentine public institutions or, in the case of the Jujuy banner, to a provincial custodial tradition recognized by national law.

The principal unresolved questions are intellectual rather than possessory:

- Who designed the original drawing?
- Which surviving matrix, if any, is the first Assembly matrix?
- Can the February impressions be matched to a known matrix?
- What precise French images were available to the designers?
- Where are the complete annexes and physical exemplars associated with file 19.974-F-1943?

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 State reproduction

The arms appear on official websites, stationery, seals, diplomatic material, signage, medals, publications, and institutional architecture. Government-brand systems sometimes use simplified monochrome or typographically integrated versions, but those administrative logos should not automatically be mistaken for amendments to the legal national arms.

### 9.2 Education and commemoration

The emblem is taught through a stable symbolic vocabulary: union, liberty, defense, national emergence, victory, and nationality. **12 March** functions as the annual commemorative day.

Educational diagrams have contributed to rigid numerical descriptions—ray and leaf counts—that were not articulated in the original March decree but form part of the modern standardized rendering.

### 9.3 Coins, medals, and banknotes

The seal-derived emblem has continued to appear in numismatics. Early 1813 and 1815 coins are especially influential because their motto, **En Unión y Libertad**, is used on modern Argentine currency even though it is not written within the arms.

Replicas of early coins and seal impressions circulate commercially. They possess no institutional authenticity merely because they reproduce the design.

### 9.4 Provincial derivatives

Many provincial arms adopted or adapted elements of the national composition:

- clasped hands;
- pike;
- liberty cap;
- oval field;
- sun;
- laurel wreath.

These are related emblems, not duplicate claimants to the identity of the national arms. Their differences encode provincial history and autonomy.

### 9.5 Political adaptation

Political movements have borrowed the emblem’s visual grammar. The **Justicialist or Peronist shield**, developed in the 1940s, is the most consequential example: it stylizes joined hands, national colors, cap-like or liberty symbolism, and rising-sun imagery into a partisan emblem. It should not be catalogued as an official version of the national arms.

### 9.6 The Jujuy banner’s modern national status

The survival of the 1813 banner allowed its painted Assembly arms to acquire a second modern legal life:

- provincial flag of Jujuy under Law 4,816 in 1994;
- more precisely regulated under provincial Law 5,772;
- historic national symbol under Argentine Law 27,134 in 2015.

The law requires reproductions to follow the arms actually painted on the surviving banner, not simply to insert the 1944 national-arms graphic. This preserves an early historical variant rather than erasing it.

### 9.7 Tourism and museum display

The emblem anchors exhibitions about the Assembly, independence, coinage, and the formation of state sovereignty. Significant sites include:

- Museo Histórico Nacional;
- Museo del Cabildo;
- Casa Rosada historical interpretation;
- Archivo General de la Nación exhibitions;
- Jujuy’s Flag Hall and interpretation center.

No reliable evidence was located for a large commercial pilgrimage economy comparable to that surrounding religious relics.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Primary evidence

The documentary base includes:

- citizenship letters bearing February 1813 impressions;
- proceedings and *El Redactor* of the Assembly;
- the March decree;
- the April replacement order;
- the April coinage enactment;
- the July coinage circular;
- early coins, plaques, flags, and official documents;
- nineteenth-century diplomatic and administrative usages;
- the 1885 consular model;
- studies and commission records culminating in the 1944 decree;
- administrative file 19.974-F-1943 and its annexed reproductions.

### 10.2 Estanislao Zeballos

Zeballos’s *El escudo y los colores nacionales* remains the foundational extended study. Its lasting contributions include:

- gathering variant representations;
- recognizing the problem of uncontrolled alteration;
- treating national symbols through documentary evidence;
- pressing for a uniform prototype.

Later scholarship has shown that some of Zeballos’s arguments—especially on the history of the national colors—are no longer sustainable. His work must be used as a historically important study, not as a final authority on every point.

### 10.3 Dardo Corvalán Mendilaharsu and institutional scholarship

Twentieth-century historical studies, including work associated with the Academia Nacional de la Historia, refined the distinction between the 1813 seal, monetary iconography, and later national arms. An Academy bulletin in 1966 still observed that Decree 10,302 regulated the emblem but that a definitive congressional statute remained desirable.

### 10.4 Iconographic scholarship

Modern research places the arms in the visual world of the Atlantic revolutions:

- clasped hands as fraternity and union;
- liberty cap and pike as republican emancipation defended by force;
- rising sun as political regeneration;
- laurels as classical victory;
- American solar meaning connected with the Inca past.

The strongest account is plural rather than exclusive. The emblem combines European classical and revolutionary forms with local political colors and Americanist meaning.

### 10.5 Numismatic scholarship

The patriotic coinage provides a parallel but nonidentical witness. Specialists examine:

- die varieties;
- changes between 1813 and 1815;
- treatment of the sun;
- transfer of the seal’s central elements;
- possible work by Rivera and other engravers.

Coins cannot simply be used as exact measurements for the arms because the Assembly expressly altered the seal composition to fit two coin faces.

### 10.6 Technical conservation history

The infrared discovery beneath the copper Assembly badge is particularly valuable because it demonstrates reuse rather than merely stylistic succession. Comparable publication of:

- alloy analysis of alleged seal matrices;
- high-resolution die comparison;
- pigment analysis of the iron portal arms;
- pigment and fiber analysis of the Jujuy banner;
- complete accession and restoration records

would materially improve the dossier. These were not found in publicly accessible form during this research.

### 10.7 Persistent popular errors

Discredited or oversimplified propositions include:

- that no form of the emblem existed before 12 March 1813;
- that the March decree supplied the modern detailed blazon;
- that the first coin exactly reproduced the full arms;
- that one universally recognized physical “original Escudo” has an unbroken custody chain;
- that Rivera’s sole authorship of every design element is conclusively documented;
- that French influence proves a specifically Jacobin or Masonic commission;
- that the modern standardized vector is visually identical in every detail to every 1813 impression.

2. **Decree 10,302/1944, original text.** Contemporary published form of the standardization measure.
3. **Law 22,362.** Trademark regime prohibiting private registration of symbols used by the Nation and other public institutions.

6. **Museo del Cabildo catalogue AR-MC-CSXVIIIXIX01-UD-879.** Record for the 28 July 1813 circular on the first patriotic coinage.
7. **1889 Brazilian arbitration treaty ratification.** An example of the Great Seal being used on an international instrument.

### Museums, archives, and public cultural institutions

8. **Archivo General de la Nación / Argentina.gob.ar, Día del Escudo Nacional.** Identifies the decree and early citizenship-letter impressions.
9. **Museo Histórico Nacional, “Tiempo de Revolución.”** Provides descriptions of the Assembly copper badge, infrared result, patriotic coins, and related revolutionary insignia.
10. **Museo Histórico Nacional institutional guide.** Identifies the painted-iron arms formerly installed at the Assembly portal.
11. **Casa Rosada, Estación del Escudo Nacional.** Discusses the silver and bronze seals, disputed design authorship, French precedent, and coinage.
12. **Argentina.gob.ar, official Escudo Nacional page.** Supplies the standardized physical description and official symbolic meanings.
13. **Argentina.gob.ar and Jujuy educational pages on the Bandera Nacional de la Libertad Civil.** Document its material, custody, ceremonial origin, movements, and present protection.

### Historical and scholarly works

14. **Estanislao S. Zeballos, *El escudo y los colores nacionales* (1900).** Foundational historical investigation and standardization argument.
15. **Academia Nacional de la Historia, *Investigaciones y Ensayos* 54.** Critical historiographical assessment of Zeballos and his predecessors.
16. **Academia Nacional de la Historia bulletin 39 (1966).** Discussion of the legal history and continuing problem of definitive statutory regulation.
17. **IFINRA study of the first patriotic coins.** Numismatic discussion of engravers, dies, the sun, and the seal’s transfer into coinage.
18. **Museo Histórico Nacional English exhibition text.** Useful explicit account of the infrared examination and the double revolutionary/Inca significance of the sun.

### Source limitations

The following were not located in publicly accessible form:

- a complete modern catalogue for both reported 1813 silver and bronze seal matrices;
- a technical report matching a surviving matrix to the February 1813 wax impressions;
- the full conservation dossier for the painted-iron Assembly portal arms;
- the full scientific conservation report for the Jujuy banner;
- a digitized complete version of administrative file 19.974-F-1943 with all authenticated visual annexes;
- a contemporary document conclusively naming the designer of the underlying drawing.

Their absence requires keeping the history of the **design**, the **seal matrices**, the **impressions**, and the **1944 prototype** analytically separate.
