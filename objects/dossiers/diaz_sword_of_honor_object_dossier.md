# The sword of honor presented to General Armando Diaz, 1919: Object Dossier

## Scope, identification, and evidentiary warning

This dossier tracks the **Venetian sword of honor commissioned for General Armando Diaz and presented at the Palazzo Ducale in Venice in September 1919**, not every ceremonial sword given to Diaz that year.

This distinction is essential. At least three separate presentation swords associated with Diaz and 1919 appear in contemporary or near-contemporary records:

1. **The Venetian sword**, commissioned by a Venetian honors committee, designed by Ettore Tito, executed principally by Renato Brozzi, and accompanied by a case supplied by the Venetian firm Da Ponte. This is the object reconstructed below.

2. **A sword commissioned by Italians in Argentina**, designed by the Italian-Argentine sculptor Troiano Troiani. It was Roman in form, had a gold hilt personifying Mother Italy with Trento, Trieste, and Istria, and was accompanied by a sculptured case. It was illustrated in *L’Illustrazione Italiana*, no. 36, 7 September 1919. It is a different object.

3. **The City of London sword of honor**, presented with the honorary Freedom of the City at Guildhall during Diaz’s visit to London in October 1919. It is also a different object. Contemporary official Italian news reports describe its presentation, while City of London biographical records identify the reception committee.

The recurring English caption “sword of honor presented to General Armando Diaz, 1919” can therefore be dangerously imprecise. The Venetian object can be identified securely by its inscriptions, its Brozzi–Tito authorship, and its Palazzo Ducale presentation.

The sword is not legendary in the sense of being known only from myth. Its physical existence in 1919 is documented by contemporary photography, newspaper reporting, an illustrated periodical, a full-size preparatory drawing, Brozzi’s accounts, and later institutional cataloguing. Its **post-presentation custody and present location, however, are not publicly documented in the sources located for this dossier**.

### Evidentiary labels used

- **Documented fact:** supported directly by contemporary records, surviving archival material, or an institutional catalogue.
- **Institutional attribution:** stated by a museum or archival institution but not independently verified here against the original record.
- **Plausible inference:** consistent with the evidence but not directly documented.
- **Unresolved:** the available evidence does not establish the point.
- **Incorrect conflation:** a claim produced by confusing the Venetian sword with another Diaz presentation sword.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Principal names

The object is called in Italian:

- **Spada d’onore per il generale Armando Diaz** — “Sword of honor for General Armando Diaz.”
- **Spada d’onore al generale Diaz** — “Sword of honor to General Diaz.”
- **Spada d’onore al generale Diaz, donata dal popolo veneziano** — “Sword of honor to General Diaz, donated by the Venetian people.”
- In descriptive English: **The Venetian sword of honor presented to General Armando Diaz**.

“Spada d’onore” is a functional designation rather than a unique proper name. It denotes a ceremonially presented, elaborately decorated sword made as a public testimonial of honor. Nothing in the located evidence suggests that the Venetian object acquired a separate traditional name.

### Object class

The sword is a **commemorative presentation weapon and civic honorific object**. It was conceived as a sword but functioned principally as a sculptural and political testimonial rather than as a service weapon.

It was accompanied by a purpose-built **cofano**, or presentation case/chest. The sword and case formed a single ceremonial ensemble, though they should be catalogued as distinct physical objects.

### Overall form

The surviving descriptions characterize the sword as a richly worked weapon combining sculpture, precious-metal work, steel decoration, inscription, and civic-war iconography. A surviving full-scale drawing at the Museo Renato Brozzi measures **1,245 × 452 mm**, but these are the dimensions of the sheet, not a published measurement of the completed sword.

No reliable published dimensions or weight for the finished sword were found. The blank “Misure” field in the Dizionario d’Arte Sartori entry should not be mistaken for evidence that measurements exist.

### Materials

According to the Museo Renato Brozzi:

- the sword as a whole was executed in **argento massiccio**, “solid silver”;
- the hilt was silver and was at least partly **gilded**;
- the blade was **steel decorated by agemina**;
- gold and silver were purchased for its manufacture;
- Brozzi’s account book records **2,600 lire spent on precious metals alone**.

“Agemina” refers to decorative inlay or damascening: grooves or prepared surfaces in a base metal are inlaid with a contrasting metal. The institutional description does not publish a metallurgical analysis establishing the exact composition of the inlay, the silver alloy, or the gilding.

No assay marks, maker’s punches, blade-smith’s marks, or metallurgical test results have been published in the located sources.

### Hilt

The hilt is the sword’s principal sculptural component. It consists of:

- a vigorous nude male figure;
- an Italian infantry helmet on the figure’s head;
- the figure caught in the act of throwing a hand grenade;
- beneath his feet, the double-headed Habsburg eagle;
- the eagle’s wings gathered or compressed into the functional guard of the hilt;
- the soldier standing upon or crushing the eagle’s shoulders.

The composition translates recent trench warfare into an allegorical format derived from classical heroic sculpture. The soldier is simultaneously a modern infantryman—helmet and grenade—and an idealized nude hero.

The gilding heightened the color contrast between the sculptural hilt and the steel blade. The exact distribution of gilded and ungilded surfaces cannot be established from the textual descriptions alone.

### Blade

The blade was steel with damascened or inlaid decoration. The institutional catalogue gives the following program.

#### Obverse

- an image or emblem of the **Lion of Saint Mark**, representing Venice;
- the inscription:

> VENEZIA AL DUCE DELL’ESERCITO LIBERATORE – XXIV GIUGNO MCMXVIII –

Translation:

> Venice to the leader of the liberating army — 24 June 1918.

The Italian word **duce** here had its older, generic sense of “leader” or “commander.” The inscription predates Benito Mussolini’s consolidation of *Il Duce* as a personal political title. Reading the inscription exclusively through its later Fascist usage would be anachronistic.

The date 24 June 1918 refers to the conclusion of the Second Battle of the Piave, the defensive victory that prevented the Austro-Hungarian advance into the Veneto. It is not the date of manufacture or presentation.

#### Reverse

The museum transcribes the Latin inscription as:

> FLUMEN PLAVIS VALLUM ITALICA VIRTUTE FINISSIMUM AD BARBARORUM IMPETUS ARCENDOS

A close provisional translation is:

> The River Piave, a rampart made most secure by Italian valor for warding off the assaults of the barbarians.

The spelling and grammar should ideally be checked directly against a high-resolution photograph of the blade. In particular, the published reading **finissimum** may require verification; no independent diplomatic transcription was located.

“Plavis” is the Latinized name of the Piave. “Barbarorum” casts the Austro-Hungarian enemy as “barbarians,” an explicit example of wartime and victory-era propaganda embedded in the object.

### The case or chest

The presentation case was offered by the Venetian firm **Da Ponte**. The located account says that Diaz commended the firm’s young representative at the ceremony.

The case bore an extensive relief program chosen by the organizing committee:

- the Basilica of Saint Mark;
- the gate of the Venetian Arsenal;
- both monuments represented as they had appeared in summer 1918, protected by timber coverings and sandbags against aerial or artillery attack;
- martial emblems;
- Savoyard eagles;
- the Lion of Saint Mark;
- on the top, a youthful personification of the River Piave.

The Piave figure carried an **idria**, a large three-handled water vessel, under his left arm. His right hand was extended, palm outward, commanding the enemy to stop. This deliberately rejected the conventional elderly, reclining, bearded river god. The Piave instead appeared as a young, active defender.

The case was consequently not mere packaging. It extended the sword’s narrative: threatened Venice below, the resisting Piave above, the modern infantryman defeating the Habsburg eagle on the sword itself.

No dimensions, materials, maker’s marks, present condition report, or confirmed current location for the case were found.

### Surviving related material

The Museo Renato Brozzi in Traversetolo preserves:

- the full-scale final preparatory drawing for the sword, graphite with white-heightened watercolor and additional watercolor on sage-green paper, 1,245 × 452 mm;
- a silver hilt for a gladius incorporating the Habsburg eagle;
- a detailed white-heightened preparatory drawing associated with that hilt.

The museum cautiously describes the gladius hilt as **possibly a variant** connected with the Diaz commission. It is not identified as the hilt removed from the completed presentation sword.

The surviving drawing is sometimes illustrated or indexed under the name of the sword. That does **not** mean the finished sword itself is in the Museo Renato Brozzi. The museum’s own descriptive tour identifies the displayed work as the *elaborazione grafica definitiva*—the definitive graphic design.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Commission

**Institutionally documented:** The sword was commissioned by the **Comitato Veneziano per le onoranze al generale**, the Venetian committee organized to honor Diaz after the Italian victory.

The Museo Renato Brozzi’s biography adds that Venetian politician and nationalist **Piero Foscari** involved Brozzi in the commission. Foscari had already engaged him for a gold plaque offered to Gabriele d’Annunzio by the Committee of Irredentists of Venezia Giulia and Dalmatia.

The commission’s political rationale was explicit: Diaz was celebrated as the commander whose army had stopped the Austro-Hungarian offensive at the Piave and subsequently won at Vittorio Veneto.

### Design and execution

The division of authorship given consistently in the strongest located sources is:

- **Ettore Tito:** designer or author of the governing design;
- **Renato Brozzi:** executing sculptor, metalworker, and modeller, especially of the hilt;
- **Da Ponte:** supplier of the presentation case.

The wording varies. Some sources call the sword a work “executed on a design by Ettore Tito”; others say Brozzi modelled the hilt from Tito’s design. The evidence therefore supports a collaborative attribution, with Tito responsible for the design and Brozzi for the principal sculptural-metal execution.

The broad label “by Renato Brozzi” is understandable but incomplete. Conversely, attributing the physical execution solely to Tito would be incorrect.

### Place of manufacture

The precise workshop or sequence of workshops in which the blade, hilt, gilding, damascening, assembly, and case were executed is **unresolved**.

Brozzi was then working in Rome and had wartime employment in the Ministry of War’s precision laboratory. The sword was commissioned through Venetian networks and presented in Venice. These facts do not establish that every component was fabricated in either Rome or Venice.

No blade supplier, silversmithing subcontractor, gilder, assayer, or damascener is named in the accessible institutional account.

### Date and duration

The museum dates the project **1918–1919**. Brozzi’s account book, dated September 1919, reportedly indicates approximately **seven months of work**.

That would place sustained manufacture approximately in early 1919, though design activity may have begun in 1918. The surviving drawing itself is catalogued as 1918.

### Cost

Brozzi recorded **2,600 Italian lire** in expenses for gold and silver. This is not stated to be the total commission price. It excludes, unless proven otherwise:

- Brozzi’s labor;
- any blade manufacture;
- subcontracting;
- gilding and damascening;
- transport;
- ceremony costs;
- the Da Ponte case.

No subscription ledger, full committee budget, contract, invoice, or final payment was located online.

### Earliest attestations

The evidence begins before presentation with the surviving design and workshop documentation:

- **1918:** full-scale preparatory drawing.
- **Early–September 1919:** manufacture over approximately seven months, documented retrospectively in Brozzi’s September account book.
- **4–5 September 1919:** reports in *Il Gazzettino* describing the sword and the public ceremony.
- **14 September 1919:** illustrated coverage, “Venezia per la vittoria delle nostre armi,” in *L’Illustrazione Italiana*, no. 37, p. 265.

The convergence of a surviving design, maker’s account, newspaper description, and contemporary photograph provides strong evidence that the physical sword existed and was completed.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 1918–1919: committee and maker custody

During design and manufacture the object, or its parts, was under the practical control of Brozzi and the commissioning committee. The exact legal ownership during manufacture is not stated in the located sources.

The Da Ponte case had a distinct manufacture and was presumably united with the sword before the presentation.

### September 1919: movement to and within Venice

The completed ensemble was brought to the Palazzo Ducale for the ceremony. Photographs catalogued by the Venetian civic museums show the presentation in the palace courtyard.

The reported dates conflict:

- the Museo Renato Brozzi gives **3 September 1919**;
- a modern scholarly reconstruction based on *Il Gazzettino* says Diaz reached Venice and received the sword on **4 September**;
- the Venetian photographic catalogue labels the ceremony **5 September 1919**.

The 5 September date may be a catalogue date derived from publication or processing rather than the ceremony itself, but that cannot be assumed. Contemporary newspaper chronology favors 4 September: *Il Gazzettino* previewed or described the sword on 4 September and reported the grand demonstration on 5 September. The museum’s 3 September date remains an institutional claim requiring reconciliation with the newspapers.

### Presentation mechanism

The transfer was a **public civic gift** from Venice—rhetorically “the Venetian people”—to Diaz. The committee’s president, identified as attorney-general **Umberto Castellani**, introduced Brozzi to the general.

Other speakers included:

- **Filippo Grimani**, associated with Venice’s civic leadership;
- **Antonio Fradeletto**, Venetian public figure and organizer;
- Diaz himself.

The sword was displayed and formally handed to Diaz in the courtyard of the Palazzo Ducale, with military veterans, disabled veterans, civic officials, and patriotic associations assembled around the Scala dei Giganti.

Participating associations reportedly included:

- Trento e Trieste;
- Società Dante Alighieri;
- Garibaldi Pro Venezia Giulia.

### Political intervention during the presentation

A girl dressed in white, wearing the colors of Fiume and a sash reading **“O Fiume o morte”** (“Fiume or death”), presented Diaz with flowers and appealed to him to “save” Fiume as he had saved Venice.

This episode did not alter custody of the sword, but it shows the object being used immediately as a platform for intervention in the Adriatic territorial crisis. The ceremony occurred days before d’Annunzio’s seizure of Fiume on 12 September 1919.

### From Diaz’s receipt to 1928

After formal presentation, the sword became Diaz’s property unless the deed of gift imposed conditions; none are reported.

No located source documents:

- the building in which Diaz kept it;
- whether it was displayed in his Roman residence;
- whether he carried or exhibited it later;
- any loan to a military exhibition;
- any conservation, repair, or alteration;
- whether sword and case remained together.

The chain of physical custody therefore becomes undocumented immediately after the 1919 presentation.

### Diaz’s death and presumed inheritance

Diaz died in Rome in February 1928. It is plausible that the sword passed into his estate and then to heirs, but **no will, probate inventory, family catalogue, donation instrument, or sale record identifying the Venetian sword was located**.

It should not be asserted that the object passed to a named descendant without such documentation.

### Later twentieth century to the present

The accessible Brozzi literature continues to reproduce and discuss the sword as a work, but the located sources focus on:

- the design;
- contemporary photographs;
- the associated variant hilt;
- the commission’s importance in Brozzi’s career.

They do not publish a later lender, collection name, accession number, or physical examination of the completed sword.

No documented theft, wartime evacuation, confiscation, sale, destruction, melting, restoration, or restitution claim was found. Absence of a report is not proof that none occurred.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Existence

The sword’s existence in 1919 is secure. It appears in:

- a full-scale preparatory drawing;
- contemporary press descriptions;
- contemporary photographic documentation;
- Brozzi’s account book;
- *L’Illustrazione Italiana*;
- early biographical literature, including the 1930 *Enciclopedia Italiana* article on Brozzi.

There is no serious basis for classifying it as a legendary or invented artifact.

### Continuity problem

What cannot presently be demonstrated is that any object now privately held or displayed under this name is the same sword presented in 1919. No current claimant was identified in the accessible sources, so this is a **provenance gap**, not a known contest between rival objects.

### Confusion with the preparatory drawing

The Museo Renato Brozzi webpage is headed with the sword’s title but catalogues the large drawing. Search snippets can make it appear that the completed sword is in Traversetolo. The museum’s materials distinguish the drawing from the completed sword and separately state that it preserves a possibly related variant hilt.

Thus:

- the drawing is authentic workshop evidence for the commission;
- it is not the sword;
- the variant gladius hilt is not established as a component of the finished sword.

### Confusion with the Argentine sword

Troiano Troiani’s sword is materially and iconographically different:

- Roman form;
- gold hilt;
- four female personifications—Italy, Trento, Trieste, and Istria;
- separate sculptured case.

The Venetian sword instead carries Brozzi’s grenade-throwing infantryman and crushed Habsburg eagle. Any catalogue describing the Diaz sword with the four women is tracking the Argentine object.

### Confusion with the London sword

The London sword was presented at Guildhall in October 1919 with the Freedom of the City. It belonged to the City of London tradition of gold- and silver-mounted swords of honor. It was not the gift of Venice and did not bear the Venetian Piave program described here.

### Scientific examination

No published evidence was found for:

- X-radiography;
- metallography;
- X-ray fluorescence;
- assay of the silver or gilding;
- microscopic examination of the damascening;
- conservation treatment;
- authentication by comparison of tool marks with other Brozzi works.

Accordingly, all material descriptions remain documentary or visual rather than scientific findings.

### Forgeries and replicas

No documented forgery or commercial replica of this exact sword was found. The full-scale drawing and possible variant hilt are authentic related works, not identified as deceptive copies.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Institutions

### Armando Diaz

**Connection:** recipient and first documented private owner.

**Date:** September 1919.

Diaz received the sword as the personification of the victorious Italian army. The object’s inscriptions address him not by surname but as **“Duce dell’esercito liberatore.”**

### Renato Brozzi

**Connection:** principal executing artist and modeller; keeper of related workshop designs and a possible variant hilt.

**Date:** 1918–1919.

The commission was pivotal in Brozzi’s career. Its public presentation brought his work to d’Annunzio’s attention and led to a long sequence of commissions for the poet.

### Ettore Tito

**Connection:** designer.

**Date:** 1918–1919.

Tito supplied the governing artistic design. The precise division between his drawing and Brozzi’s sculptural elaboration has not been reconstructed from contracts or correspondence.

### Piero Foscari

**Connection:** political intermediary associated by the Museo Renato Brozzi with Brozzi’s recruitment.

**Date:** 1918.

Foscari linked the commission to Venetian irredentist and nationalist patronage networks.

### Umberto Castellani

**Connection:** president of the Venetian honors committee and ceremonial intermediary.

**Date:** September 1919.

Castellani presented Brozzi to Diaz during the ceremony.

### Filippo Grimani and Antonio Fradeletto

**Connection:** civic speakers or organizers at the presentation.

**Date:** September 1919.

Their presence situated the sword within Venice’s municipal and cultural establishment.

### Da Ponte firm

**Connection:** provider of the sculptured presentation case.

**Date:** 1919.

The firm’s exact legal name, workshop address, craftsmen, invoice, and materials have not been established from the sources accessed.

### Gabriele d’Annunzio

**Connection:** viewer and admirer, not commissioner or custodian of the Diaz sword.

**Date:** September 1919.

The sword reportedly prompted d’Annunzio to seek an introduction to Brozzi through pianist Luisa Baccara and to invite the artist to dinner at the Casetta Rossa. This began their long working relationship.

D’Annunzio’s association belongs to reception history, not the sword’s chain of ownership.

### Luisa Baccara

**Connection:** intermediary in d’Annunzio’s contact with Brozzi.

**Date:** September 1919.

She is not documented as handling or owning the sword.

### Venetian Honors Committee

**Connection:** commissioner and donor.

The committee determined much of the case’s iconographic program. Its records—minutes, subscription lists, correspondence, or accounts—would be among the most important primary sources for future research.

### City of Venice and Venetian public

**Connection:** collective donor in the ceremony’s official rhetoric.

The formula “donated by the Venetian people” should be understood as civic representation through a committee, not proof that every resident subscribed.

### Patriotic associations

Trento e Trieste, Dante Alighieri, and Garibaldi Pro Venezia Giulia participated in the ceremonial environment. They did not acquire custody but helped define the sword’s irredentist-nationalist meaning.

### Museo Renato Brozzi and Comune di Traversetolo

**Connection:** present keepers of the principal preparatory drawing and possibly related variant material; modern institutional interpreters of the sword.

They do not, in the located public catalogue, claim custody of the completed sword.

### Civici Musei di Venezia

**Connection:** custodians or cataloguers of photographs documenting the ceremony.

The photographs are evidence for presentation, handling, and location in September 1919, not evidence that the museums ever owned the sword.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Actual ceremonial use

Only one physical use is securely documented: the sword was displayed and transferred during the public honors ceremony in the courtyard of the Palazzo Ducale.

It was not documented as being used in combat, for an oath, for investiture into an order, or in a recurring ritual.

### Civic gift ritual

The ceremony converted several institutions into symbolic actors:

- Venice became the grateful city;
- Diaz became the “leader of the liberating army”;
- the Italian infantryman became the agent of victory;
- the Piave became the defender of the city;
- the defeated Habsburg monarchy became the crushed eagle.

The gift also joined the modern army to the Palazzo Ducale, the monumental center of the former Venetian Republic. This supplied the new Italian national victory with Venetian historical depth.

### Symbolic geography

The sword and case map the war onto a compact ensemble:

- **Venice:** Lion of Saint Mark, Basilica, Arsenal.
- **Piave:** inscription and youthful river personification.
- **Austria-Hungary:** double-headed eagle.
- **Italy:** modern infantryman, Savoyard emblems, Diaz as commander.
- **Recovered or claimed territories:** the participating irredentist associations and the Fiume intervention.

### The Piave date

The inscription’s 24 June 1918 date makes the object memorialize the defensive victory at the Piave rather than only the final offensive at Vittorio Veneto. This is important to the sword’s Venetian function: the Piave battle was presented as the moment the threat to Venice was stopped.

### Legitimation

Possession did not confer formal military rank or political office. Its legitimating role was representational: the sword publicly ratified Diaz as the person who had defended and “liberated” Venice.

The Fiume appeal attempted to extend that legitimacy. Its speaker urged the celebrated savior of Venice to become savior of Venice’s supposed Adriatic “daughter.” The object therefore became the ceremonial focus of a contested territorial claim.

### Access and handling

No formal taboo or access rule is documented. At the presentation, committee representatives, makers, attendants, and Diaz necessarily had access. Later handling restrictions are unknown.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

The evidence located does not reveal a developed supernatural legend, miracle story, curse, prophecy, or cult around the sword. Calling it “sacred” is defensible only in the sociological sense of a civic-national relic.

### The “savior of Venice” narrative

The sword materially embodies the claim that Diaz personally saved Venice by stopping the enemy at the Piave. This was a patriotic condensation of collective military action rather than a literal account of one man’s physical defense of the city.

Its earliest material articulation is contemporary with the commission:

- the commander is addressed as leader of the liberating army;
- the infantryman defeats the Habsburg eagle;
- the Piave blocks the “barbarians”;
- Venice’s monuments appear fortified but preserved.

### Venice as mother and Fiume as daughter

At the ceremony, the Fiume representative declared that Fiume was Venice’s daughter and implored Diaz to save the daughter as he had saved the mother. This was a contemporary political-nationalist claim, not an ancient Venetian tradition established by the sword.

The episode’s historical significance lies in its timing and use of the ceremony. It does not prove that Diaz endorsed the appeal.

### Later “Duce” readings

A modern viewer may associate the inscription’s *Duce* with Fascism. The word was already a conventional Italian term for leader and was applied to military commanders before Mussolini monopolized it. Claims that the sword was designed as a Fascist object are therefore chronologically false.

Later Fascist celebration of Diaz and victory culture could make the object retrospectively compatible with regime imagery, but that is distinct from its documented 1918–1919 origin.

### No evidence for magical or martial use

No source establishes that Diaz wore the sword in battle, used it in a military command ceremony, or believed it to possess protective power. Such claims, if encountered without documentation, should be classified as modern embellishment.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Completed sword

**Current location: unresolved.**

No public museum accession number, current collection name, gallery location, conservation record, auction appearance, or documented private owner was found for the completed Venetian sword.

It should not be reported as “on display at the Museo Renato Brozzi.” That museum displays the definitive drawing and preserves related material.

The sword’s survival after Diaz’s death is not affirmatively established by the sources located. Nor is its destruction, loss, theft, or melting documented. The responsible conclusion is therefore:

> The Venetian sword existed and was presented in 1919; its publicly documented chain of custody presently ends with Armando Diaz, and its present physical whereabouts and condition remain unestablished.

### Presentation case

The Da Ponte case’s present location is likewise unknown. There is no evidence that it remains with the sword.

### Preparatory drawing

The definitive drawing is held by the **Museo Renato Brozzi**, Piazza Fanfulla 5/A, Traversetolo, Parma. The museum presents it in its public route.

The webpage does not expose a conventional accession number. Its online presentation identifies it as item **31** among the museum’s principal works, but this may be a display-list number rather than a formal accession identifier.

### Variant hilt and drawing

The museum also holds the silver gladius hilt with Habsburg-eagle motif and the related preparatory drawing. Their exact inventory numbers were not available in the consulted page.

### Legal status and disputes

No current ownership litigation, export claim, restitution demand, lien, or repatriation proceeding involving the sword was found.

Because its present owner is unidentified, its legal status cannot be specified beyond the historical fact that it was delivered as a gift to Diaz.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Brozzi’s career

The sword’s most consequential reception was immediate. D’Annunzio’s admiration led to his introduction to Brozzi and a creative relationship lasting until the poet’s death in 1938.

This association helped move Brozzi from a talented sculptor-metalworker into d’Annunzio’s sustained network of decorative and emblematic commissions.

### Early art-historical recognition

The 1930 *Enciclopedia Italiana* entry on Brozzi listed “the sword offered by the Venetians to Armando Diaz in 1919” among his notable works. The sword therefore entered Brozzi’s canon within eleven years of presentation.

### Photographic afterlife

Contemporary photographs preserve the ceremony and are catalogued by the Venetian civic museums. The visual record allows the sword’s public function to survive even though its later material whereabouts are obscure.

### Museum interpretation

The Museo Renato Brozzi treats the design as part of its interpretation of:

- Brozzi’s metalwork;
- First World War commemoration;
- the relationship between Brozzi and d’Annunzio;
- the mass production of victory and war-memory imagery after 1918.

Its accessible and multimedia catalogue has made the design more visible online than the completed object.

### Political imagery

The sword belongs to the broader Italian postwar “cult of victory,” but the located evidence does not show the exact sword reproduced on:

- coins;
- banknotes;
- official flags;
- Fascist Party insignia;
- later military unit badges.

Its iconography—infantry hero, grenade, crushed double-headed eagle, Piave, Lion of Saint Mark—belongs to a widely shared victory vocabulary, making direct influence difficult to prove.

### Replicas, film, literature, and games

No securely documented full-scale replica, cinematic prop, literary plot centered on the sword, or appearance in a major game was located.

This negative result should be distinguished from the substantial modern reception of Diaz, the Piave, and the Victory Bulletin generally.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Primary evidence base

The object is documented by several complementary source types:

1. **Surviving workshop art**
   - full-scale preparatory drawing;
   - variant hilt and related drawing.

2. **Maker’s accounting**
   - Brozzi’s September 1919 account book, cited by the museum.

3. **Contemporary press**
   - *Il Gazzettino*, 4 September 1919, “La spada d’onore al gen. Diaz”;
   - *Il Gazzettino*, 5 September 1919, “La grandiosa dimostrazione di Venezia all’esercito ed al suo Duce”;
   - *L’Illustrazione Italiana*, 14 September 1919, p. 265.

4. **Photography**
   - ceremony photographs catalogued in the Venetian civic-museum system.

5. **Early reference literature**
   - *Enciclopedia Italiana* entry on Brozzi, 1930.

### Principal historiographical problems

#### Exact ceremony date

The 3, 4, and 5 September dates have all entered institutional or scholarly descriptions. The likely reconstruction is a 4 September ceremony reported on 5 September, but direct examination of the newspapers and municipal ceremony program is needed before treating that as final.

#### Attribution

The strongest formulation is “designed by Ettore Tito and executed by Renato Brozzi.” Short-form attributions to Brozzi alone omit Tito’s documented role; treating Tito as sole maker omits Brozzi’s execution.

#### Object versus design

Modern web cataloguing often illustrates the drawing under the title of the completed sword. Researchers must identify whether a reproduction shows:

- the final sword;
- the full-scale design;
- the possible variant gladius hilt;
- the separate Argentine sword.

#### Present whereabouts

This is the central unanswered provenance question. Published Brozzi studies have concentrated on authorship and iconography rather than the Diaz estate and later custody.

### Archives and records requiring further examination

A definitive study should consult:

- the Venice municipal archives for the honors committee’s records;
- committee minutes, subscriptions, contracts, and ceremonial programs;
- complete files of *Il Gazzettino* for 3–6 September 1919;
- *L’Illustrazione Italiana*, nos. 36–37;
- the Brozzi archive and original account book at Traversetolo;
- correspondence among Brozzi, Ettore Tito, Piero Foscari, and committee members;
- Da Ponte business records, if they survive;
- Diaz’s will, estate inventory, and family papers;
- customs or export records if the estate dispersed objects abroad;
- exhibition and loan catalogues devoted to Diaz, victory culture, or Brozzi;
- image versos and original captions in the Venetian photographic archive.

### Scientific and conservation history

No scientific or technical dossier has been published in the located sources. Until the completed sword is physically located, questions of alloy, technique, condition, repair, and authenticity cannot be investigated directly.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and near-contemporary sources

- *Il Gazzettino*, “La spada d’onore al gen. Diaz,” 4 September 1919. Identified through later scholarship; a stable direct scan was not located.
- *Il Gazzettino*, “La grandiosa dimostrazione di Venezia all’esercito ed al suo Duce,” 5 September 1919. Identified and quoted through later scholarship; a stable direct scan was not located.
- *L’Illustrazione Italiana*, “L’omaggio degli italiani dell’Argentina al generale Diaz,” no. 36, 7 September 1919, p. 241.
- *L’Illustrazione Italiana*, “Venezia per la vittoria delle nostre armi,” no. 37, 14 September 1919, p. 265.
- Brozzi, Renato, account book entry, September 1919, cited by Museo Renato Brozzi.
- Venetian civic-museum photographic catalogue, ceremony of the presentation of the sword of honor to Armando Diaz.
- *Gazzetta Ufficiale del Regno d’Italia*, 24 October 1919, containing Stefani reports on Diaz’s London reception and forthcoming or completed Guildhall sword presentation.

### Institutional and scholarly sources

- Museo Renato Brozzi, detailed catalogue entry for the full-scale design. This is the fullest accessible account of materials, inscriptions, manufacture, cost, case iconography, and ceremony.
- Museo Renato Brozzi, biography of Renato Brozzi.
- Museo Renato Brozzi, descriptive museum tour.
- *Enciclopedia Italiana*, “Brozzi, Renato,” 1930.
- Dizionario d’Arte Sartori, object and artist entries for Brozzi and Troiano Troiani.
- University of Milan doctoral thesis on Venetian postwar commemoration and nationalism, citing and quoting the September 1919 *Gazzettino* reports.
- City of London historical biographical compilation, documenting the reception committee associated with Diaz’s separate London sword.
- Metropolitan Museum of Art essay/catalogue entry on the City of London sword-of-honor tradition, useful for distinguishing the London class of presentation swords from the Venetian object.

### Assessment of the source record

The evidence is strongest for:

- the sword’s existence;
- commission;
- Brozzi–Tito authorship;
- materials in general terms;
- inscriptions;
- iconography;
- presentation in Venice;
- importance in Brozzi’s career.

It is weakest for:

- exact dimensions and weight;
- complete workshop division of labor;
- precise ceremony date;
- full cost;
- hallmarks and assays;
- Diaz’s storage and later use;
- inheritance;
- survival and present location;
- condition and conservation.

The post-1919 provenance gap should remain explicit until a Diaz estate document, current collection record, or physically examinable candidate emerges.
