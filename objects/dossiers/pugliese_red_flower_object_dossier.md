# The red flower left on Osvaldo Pugliese's piano: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary finding

The object commonly called *el clavel rojo sobre el piano de Pugliese*—“the red carnation on Pugliese’s piano”—was not, on present evidence, one durable flower possessing a continuous material history. It was a repeatable assemblage and political performance:

1. a fresh red carnation, or occasionally several carnations;
2. a piano associated with Osvaldo Pugliese’s orchestra;
3. an intentionally empty piano bench or absent pianist;
4. performance by the remainder of the orchestra;
5. the audience’s knowledge that Pugliese was detained, prohibited, or otherwise forcibly absent.

Individual flowers were perishable and apparently discarded, gifted, or allowed to decay. No source located identifies an original carnation preserved in a collection, gives it an accession number, or establishes a chain of custody beyond a particular performance. References to “the carnation” consequently name a type-token tradition: many materially different flowers successively enacted the identity of one symbolic object.

The best-supported early setting is the cycle of political detentions and prohibitions that affected Pugliese in the 1940s and 1950s. A more precise origin story places a formative performance at Newell’s Old Boys in Rosario, probably during the 1955 carnival season, when the orchestra’s glosador Luis “el Negro” Mella reportedly declared, “el piano está mudo, el piano está preso” (“the piano is mute; the piano is imprisoned”) and threw a red carnation onto its closed lid. That account is retrospective, however, and conflicts with testimony placing the established practice earlier, including during or after the military coup of 1943 and Pugliese’s six-month imprisonment in 1948.

The defensible conclusion is therefore:

> **Documented historical core:** Pugliese’s musicians repeatedly placed red carnations on an unplayed piano when political detention or prohibition kept him from performing.  
> **Unresolved origin:** no contemporaneous program, newspaper notice, photograph, police record, or dated eyewitness document yet located securely identifies the first flower, first performance, or inventor.  
> **Later legendary compression:** repeated flowers and episodes have been fused into “the red carnation,” sometimes assigned specifically to Rosario in 1955 and to Luis Mella.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Names

The object or practice appears in Spanish under several closely related names:

- *el clavel rojo sobre el piano* — “the red carnation on the piano”;
- *el clavel rojo de Pugliese* — “Pugliese’s red carnation”;
- *los claveles rojos* — “the red carnations”;
- *la flor roja sobre el piano* — “the red flower on the piano”;
- *un clavel rojo sobre el teclado* — “a red carnation on the keyboard”;
- *un clavel rojo sobre la tapa del piano* — “a red carnation on the piano lid”;
- in a minority of recollections, *una rosa* or “a rose.”

*Clavel* is the ordinary Spanish name for a carnation, normally *Dianthus caryophyllus*. *Rojo* means red. No special historic cultivar, florist, grower, supplier, botanical specimen, or artificial construction has been identified.

The phrase’s singular form is symbolically useful but materially misleading: historical accounts employ both singular and plural, and the practice recurred across multiple performances. The 1940s–1950s carnations cannot have been one conserved specimen.

### 1.2 Object class

The carnation can be classified simultaneously as:

- a perishable botanical object;
- a theatrical prop;
- a political protest sign;
- a proxy or substitute for an absent person;
- a commemorative offering;
- a recurring emblem rather than a unique relic.

After Pugliese’s death, fresh carnations placed on representations of his piano or tomb acquired a votive or quasi-relic function. They invoke the original protest without claiming physical continuity with any 1940s or 1950s flower.

### 1.3 Physical description

No early source supplies measurements, weight, cultivar, number of petals, stem length, wrapping, wire support, or florist’s label. A normal fresh carnation would consist of a green stem and leaves with a densely petaled red flower head, but applying botanical norms to a particular historic token would be inference, not description.

Accounts disagree about exact placement:

- on the keyboard or keys;
- on the piano;
- on the closed lid or top;
- thrown onto the closed lid by Luis Mella;
- several carnations “adorning” the instrument;
- exceptionally, on an empty chair rather than the piano.

They also disagree about number: one carnation, carnations in the plural, or a bouquet. These variations probably reflect repeated performances rather than faulty descriptions of one event.

### 1.4 The piano component

The political meaning depended at least as much on the silent piano as on the flower. Eyewitness Saúl Cascallar recalled that nobody played it and that the orchestra performed without piano: “El clavel rojo estaba en reemplazo de Pugliese.” Other evidence shows that this was not universal. Bandoneonist Enrique Alessio recalled carrying Pugliese’s prison-written arrangements to the orchestra and stated that Armando Cupo sometimes replaced him at the piano. The scholarly dossier *Osvaldo Pugliese: tango y comunismo en el Río de la Plata* therefore cautions that the carnation-and-empty-piano configuration was occasional, not the invariant form of every performance ([Huella del Sur dossier, pp. 19–20](https://huelladelsur.ar/app/uploads/2021/04/dossier-pugliese.pdf)).

The piano used at any particular absence performance has not been individually identified. Sources sometimes conflate the stage piano with Pugliese’s historic personal instrument. A 2005 report identifies a “piano histórico” donated by Pugliese to the Casa del Tango, but does not establish that it was the instrument present at Rosario, Radio Splendid, or every politically interrupted engagement ([Página/12, 25 July 2005](https://www.pagina12.com.ar/diario/espectaculos/6-54138-2005-07-25.html)).

### 1.5 Condition and survival

No original flower is known to survive. There is no reported pressing, drying, taxidermic preparation, resin preservation, herbarium sheet, reliquary, private collection, or museum accession. Later fresh flowers are replacements or commemorative reenactments.

No scientific examination—botanical identification, pigment analysis, DNA testing, radiocarbon dating, or residue analysis—has been reported. Such testing would have little application unless a claimant specimen emerged with documented provenance.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Botanical manufacture

Each token was presumably a cut cultivated carnation obtained shortly before a performance. Its farm, greenhouse, flower market, florist, purchaser, price, and cutting date are unrecorded. Nothing establishes whether the performers consistently used carnations rather than whatever red flower was available.

The meaningful “manufacture” of the object occurred onstage: a common flower became the Pugliese carnation when placed on an unused piano in a context of forced political absence.

### 2.2 Political preconditions

Pugliese joined the Communist Party of Argentina in 1936. His political affiliation, union activity, and prominence among working-class tango audiences exposed him to surveillance, censorship, cancellations, and detention under several governments. The biographical chronology maintained by the Diccionario Biográfico de las Izquierdas Latinoamericanas identifies repeated confinement in Villa Devoto, a six-month imprisonment in 1948, another six-month detention beginning in January 1955, and arrest during the anti-communist Operación Cardenal in April 1957 ([CeDInCI dictionary](https://diccionario.cedinci.org/pugliese-osvaldo/)).

These episodes created the communicative problem solved by the flower: how could the orchestra fulfill engagements and make the director’s involuntary absence publicly legible without replacing or concealing him?

### 2.3 Competing origin chronologies

#### Version A: established by the mid-1940s, perhaps from 1943

Saúl Cascallar, interviewed in 2006, connected the beginning of Pugliese’s recurrent political difficulties with the coup of 4 June 1943. He remembered arriving at dances where the red carnation was already on the keyboard whenever Pugliese was imprisoned. He explicitly attributed placement to “the musicians who accompanied him” and described the flower as Pugliese’s replacement ([Página/12 interview with Cascallar](https://www.pagina12.com.ar/diario/sociedad/3-63009-2006-02-13.html)).

This is important eyewitness testimony, but it was recorded more than sixty years after the alleged beginning. Cascallar did not identify a first performance, buyer, or individual inventor.

#### Version B: securely associated with the 1948 imprisonment

Pugliese’s longest imprisonment before 1955 occurred in 1948. A contemporary obituary published immediately after his death reported that during that confinement the orchestra played without a substitute musician and left a red carnation on the keys ([El Tiempo, 26 July 1995](https://www.eltiempo.com/archivo/documento/mam-374526)).

A later account quotes Pugliese himself remembering 1948: he had effectively been erased from employment, yet the orchestra continued without a pianist and with a carnation on the piano. The underlying interview publication is not fully identified on the webpage, so the quotation should be treated as a secondary reproduction rather than a verified transcript ([Visión País](https://visionpais.com.ar/osvaldo-pugliese/)).

This version demonstrates use by 1948 if the recollections are accurate, but it still does not prove that 1948 was the origin.

#### Version C: Rosario/Newell’s Old Boys, early 1950s or carnival 1955

Ramón Rosa Roldán’s *Memorias de un Lepra* (Amalevi, 2009), summarized in later journalism, assigns the legend’s birth to a Newell’s Old Boys engagement in Rosario. The longer retelling says Pugliese was detained before a Sunday performance; the club insisted that the orchestra appear; Luis Mella gave a short spoken introduction ending with “the piano is mute; the piano is imprisoned” and threw a red carnation onto the closed lid.

A 2024 article places a documented-looking iteration at Newell’s during the 1955 carnivals and reproduces an archival photograph of the orchestra, although the visible image does not by itself authenticate the flower or Mella’s spoken words. It identifies Jorge Maciel and Miguel Montero beside the orchestra and describes Mella’s action as performative protest ([El Litoral](https://www.ellitoral.com/informacion-general/memorias-santa-fe-carnavales-detencion-maestro-pugliese_0_gRoYP4SGjy.html)).

The Rosario claim is the most detailed origin narrative but not the earliest testimony. It is incompatible with accounts of use in 1948 unless it describes not the invention but a particularly memorable codification of an existing practice. The phrase “the legend was born in Rosario” may be literally true of a canonical narrative while being false as a claim about the first physical use.

#### Version D: Radio Splendid during the 1957 Operación Cardenal detention

Some popular retellings make the decisive episode a Radio Splendid engagement while Pugliese and other communists were held aboard the prison ship *París*. The orchestra allegedly honored its contract without him and decorated the piano with red carnations. The red carnation was already an established sign by then; even sources telling this story call it a pre-existing “marca de la protesta” ([El Fénix Digital](https://www.elfenixdigital.com/nota-araca-la-cana-%7C-historia-de-las-detenciones-de-osvaldo-pugliese-200794)).

This event is therefore part of the practice’s continuation, not credible evidence of its origin.

### 2.4 Earliest presently located attestation

No contemporaneous 1940s or 1955 text located in this research explicitly reports the flower. The earliest located published attestations are retrospective. Hamlet Lima Quintana’s biography *Osvaldo Pugliese* first appeared in 1990 and was reissued by the Centro Cultural de la Cooperación in 2009. It is valuable because it grew out of personal acquaintance and interviews with Pugliese and Lydia Elman, but it remains a late literary-biographical source rather than a contemporaneous artifact record ([2009 edition](https://d1y502jg6fpugt.cloudfront.net/33463/archive/files/0199897bee6b63a336aabcd77bc73fe8.pdf?Expires=1778112000&Key-Pair-Id=K6UGZS9ZTDSZM&Signature=qgMS~KKwn0EmdR2KvnVF4rzJN~TJfbBy-dINm1~NNm645numF8~NCRjYDklrydhmJ1Z16CBK4NT0MQYm5ScylI1-4IOduvYchmli3jlXWTubFssp4yp-f~4bbrguNToRbCCFl2nyoKeS25vgT2y6E2kW1CSxsGI4XYi0FKMO7ckli1PmtNDqwaUme6XFkVgnCYa3uhSZDQz8eI2A5u8bSYvy~6KAKHW13ed~3v2~Mc~UndAwHwthaMysiAp3LYFiPjnlteSRggucG96qoGCtyQPcy8KsLqxQaQkd-g0dMU9iaaN~WXoGml-f52YmFsVGSHVb~pv4XE~uScOfoDp8Eg__)).

Until dated newspapers, club records, photographs, or radio documentation are produced, “first use” must remain unresolved.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because this was a serial practice rather than one conserved flower, a conventional ownership chain cannot be reconstructed. The following is a custody-and-use sequence for successive tokens.

### Before 1943: no flower securely attested

Pugliese’s orchestra debuted in 1939. He had already experienced political attention and possible detention, but no presently located primary source securely documents a red flower at those earliest episodes.

### 1943–1947: possible early use

Cascallar’s testimony places the carnation practice in the period beginning after the June 1943 coup. The musicians procured and placed the flowers; the orchestra, rather than Pugliese, was their temporary custodian. Where the flowers went after performances is unknown.

### 1948: repeated emblem during imprisonment

During Pugliese’s approximately six-month confinement, the orchestra continued to perform. Accounts describe a silent piano bearing a carnation, although no engagement-by-engagement list survives. The likely custody sequence for each flower was florist or market → unidentified purchaser among the ensemble or its associates → musicians or Luis Mella → stage piano → unknown disposal or gift.

No evidence establishes that a carnation traveled into Villa Devoto, belonged to Pugliese personally, or was returned to him.

### Early 1950s: intermittent use and substitution

The empty-piano practice was not mechanically repeated at every absence. Pugliese sometimes refused a substitute, but Enrique Alessio’s testimony establishes that Armando Cupo sometimes played in his place. Another witness remembered the band stamping their feet to compensate rhythmically when the closed piano remained silent. The object’s biography thus branches:

- performances with empty piano and carnation;
- performances with a substitute pianist;
- possibly performances with a red flower but no strict carnation identification.

### January–July 1955: Villa Devoto detention

Pugliese was detained for about six months. Solidarity action reportedly included graffiti and balloons bearing the slogan “El tango está preso. Libertad a Osvaldo Pugliese.” The carnation participated in the same campaign of visible absence ([Huella del Sur dossier](https://huelladelsur.ar/app/uploads/2021/04/dossier-pugliese.pdf)).

### Carnival 1955, Newell’s Old Boys, Rosario

This is the most fully narrated token:

- Pugliese was absent under detention.
- The club maintained the engagement.
- The orchestra performed.
- Luis Mella reportedly spoke of the mute or imprisoned piano.
- He threw a red carnation onto its closed lid.
- The public understood the action as protest.

The flower’s source before the event and fate afterward are undocumented. Nothing demonstrates that it was retained by Mella, the club, Pugliese’s family, or an audience member.

### April 1957: Operación Cardenal and the ship *París*

Pugliese was arrested with Rodolfo Ghioldi, Orestes Ghioldi, Héctor P. Agosti, and other communists. The prisoners were confined aboard the *París*. A rumor circulated that the ship would be sunk; the reported plot was never carried out and its actual existence is unproved. During this detention, accounts place red carnations on a piano at a Radio Splendid performance. Pugliese himself reportedly played Argentina’s national anthem on a piano aboard the ship, but that was a different instrument and no carnation is attached to it in the evidence ([Huella del Sur dossier, p. 20](https://huelladelsur.ar/app/uploads/2021/04/dossier-pugliese.pdf)).

### Later career

After detention ceased to be a recurring feature of Pugliese’s performances, the flower became biographical shorthand. There is no evidence that Pugliese conserved a historic token or made a particular carnation part of his personal possessions.

One circulating anecdote claims that on some occasions the carnation was present even though Pugliese subsequently appeared and gave it to a woman in the audience. This would convert a protest token into a stage prop or gift and weakens any claim that every appearance of the flower proves a contemporaneous arrest. The anecdote has been attributed indirectly to Ulyses Petit de Murat, Francisco García Jiménez, and Arturo Jauretche, but no precise original citations were located. It must remain unverified.

### 1995: death and transformation into memorial offering

Pugliese died on 25 July 1995. The carnation ceased to represent temporary forced absence and came to represent permanent physical absence, memory, political constancy, and continued artistic presence.

### 1996 onward: Chacarita mausoleum

A mausoleum was erected in the Chacarita Cemetery with a sculptural representation of Pugliese at a piano. Reports describe fresh red carnations continually placed on the sculpted piano. Each is a modern votive replacement, not an original prison-era specimen. The donors are generally anonymous, and the cemetery or memorial caretakers exercise only temporary custody ([Página/12, 2005](https://www.pagina12.com.ar/diario/espectaculos/6-54138-2005-07-25.html)).

### 2009: Teatro Avenida reenactment

At a Buenos Aires Tango Festival tribute, an empty piano bearing a red carnation presided over the concluding performance of “La yumba.” Eleven former Pugliese musicians participated; Emilio Balcarce conducted; audience members threw additional red carnations toward the stage. The act was a consciously commemorative reenactment, documented by a contemporary report ([Página/12, 17 August 2009](https://www.pagina12.com.ar/diario/suplementos/espectaculos/2-14966-2009-08-17.html)).

The carnation used on the piano and those thrown by the audience have no reported later provenance.

### Present

Fresh tokens continue to appear at Pugliese commemorations and at his mausoleum. There is no central owner, legal title, custody authority, or canonical specimen.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Is there a single authentic object?

No. All available evidence points to successive perishable carnations. Asking which extant flower is “the real one” imposes a relic model not supported by the historical practice.

An authentic modern token may be authentic as an act of commemoration, but it is not materially identical to a flower used during Pugliese’s imprisonment.

### 4.2 Carnation or generic red flower?

Most accounts consistently say *clavel rojo*. Some later witnesses use *flor roja*, *rosa*, or alternate between carnation and rose. Without close contemporary photographs or preserved specimens, botanical identity cannot be independently verified for every event. The carnation identification is strong as tradition and repeated testimony, not scientifically demonstrated for each token.

### 4.3 One flower or bouquet?

Sources variously report one carnation, plural carnations, or a bouquet. There is no reason to harmonize them. Different performances probably used different numbers. Modern retellings sometimes enlarge the single emblematic flower into a bouquet.

### 4.4 Who invented the gesture?

Candidates include:

- the orchestra collectively;
- Luis “el Negro” Mella individually;
- the audience or Pugliese’s followers;
- Pugliese, insofar as he consented to an empty piano and rejected substitution.

Cascallar credited the musicians. The Rosario narrative gives Mella the decisive spoken and physical action. Lydia Elman’s recollection, quoted in Lima Quintana’s biography, spoke more broadly of “the pressure of the people” placing the carnation on the piano. The documentary record does not justify exclusive attribution.

### 4.5 When was it invented?

The strongest rival datings are:

- shortly after 1943;
- during the 1948 imprisonment;
- in Rosario in the early 1950s;
- specifically at the 1955 Newell’s carnival performances.

Since a late eyewitness account recalls the practice before 1955, Rosario should not be presented unqualifiedly as the first use. It may have furnished the canonical staging and phrase.

### 4.6 Which piano?

No evidence shows that one instrument accompanied the flower throughout its history. The orchestra performed in clubs, radio studios, theatres, and dance halls that likely supplied different pianos. Pugliese’s personal historic piano—later donated to the Casa del Tango—is a real artifact, but the sources located do not prove it was the flower-bearing instrument at the important absence performances.

The mausoleum piano is sculpture. The Teatro Avenida piano used in 2009 was a commemorative stage instrument. Neither is the original in a material sense.

### 4.7 Scientific and institutional authentication

No museum, archive, laboratory, botanist, or Pugliese foundation has authenticated an original flower. No specimen claimant was found. Authority over the symbol remains dispersed among surviving musicians, family members, biographers, tango institutions, communist memory organizations, audiences, and performers.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Osvaldo Pedro Pugliese

Pianist, composer, director, Communist Party member, and referent of the cooperative orchestra. His forced absence gave the object its original meaning. He reportedly consented to performances without a replacement pianist and later recalled the flower as the sign used while he was imprisoned.

### Luis “el Negro” Mella

Glosador, presenter, or reciter attached to the orchestra. In the Rosario tradition he pronounced the piano mute and imprisoned, closed it, and threw the carnation onto the lid. The attribution is vivid but retrospective.

### The Orquesta de Osvaldo Pugliese

The principal maker and custodian of the symbolic assemblage. Its musicians continued engagements without their director and enabled the flower to function as a substitute body. As a cooperative organization, the orchestra’s collective identity reinforced the gesture: the ensemble continued working while refusing to make Pugliese’s disappearance invisible.

### Saúl Cascallar

Communist activist and eyewitness interviewed in 2006. He remembered the carnation on the keyboard at dances, credited Pugliese’s musicians with placing it, and said the public understood it as his replacement ([Página/12](https://www.pagina12.com.ar/diario/sociedad/3-63009-2006-02-13.html)).

### Enrique Alessio

Bandoneonist in Pugliese’s orchestra. His testimony complicates the purified legend: Pugliese sent arrangements from prison, Alessio carried them to rehearsals, and Armando Cupo sometimes served as substitute pianist.

### Armando Cupo

Pianist who sometimes replaced Pugliese. His participation demonstrates that the empty piano and carnation did not accompany every politically enforced absence.

### Lydia Elman de Pugliese

Pugliese’s wife and later custodian of his memory. In Lima Quintana’s biographical narrative she associated the carnation with popular pressure for his release and with the slogan “El tango está preso.”

### Beba Pugliese and Carla Pugliese

Pugliese’s daughter and granddaughter, both musicians and transmitters of family memory. Carla described the carnation as the unmistakable sign of Pugliese’s “present absence” under successive governments ([La Nación, 20 February 2024](https://www.lanacion.com.ar/espectaculos/musica/carla-pugliese-los-milagros-intercedidos-por-su-abuelo-y-el-secreto-de-los-ultimos-anos-de-su-abuelo-nid20022024/)).

### Ramón Rosa Roldán

Author of *Memorias de un Lepra* (2009), a source for the Newell’s Old Boys/Rosario origin tradition. His account is based in club memory but was written decades after the event.

### Hamlet Lima Quintana

Poet, friend, and biographer of Pugliese. His 1990 biography, reissued in 2009, preserves interviews and politically sympathetic memory. He was not the flower’s original custodian.

### Communist Party of Argentina

The political network to which Pugliese belonged. The red color could be read immediately within communist and broader workers’ symbolism. The party and its sympathizers participated in campaigns for his freedom, although no source proves that the party centrally ordered or designed the carnation gesture.

### Newell’s Old Boys

Rosario football and social club associated with the best-developed origin narrative and a photographed 1955 carnival engagement.

### Radio Splendid

Broadcast venue associated with a later carnation performance during Pugliese’s Operación Cardenal detention.

### Argentine police, prison, and censorship authorities

These state actors did not possess the flower but created the conditions in which it signified: arrest, prohibition, cancellation, surveillance, and an empty place onstage.

### Emilio Balcarce and former orchestra members

At the 2009 tribute, Balcarce conducted musicians associated with Pugliese beside a deliberately empty, carnation-bearing piano. Their direct professional connection gave the reenactment testimonial authority.

### “San Pugliese”

No historical divine being was attached to the original flower. In later popular culture, Pugliese became a joking but sincerely used secular saint invoked against bad luck: “Pugliese, Pugliese, Pugliese.” The carnation at his tomb can consequently resemble a votive offering. This cultic layer is a late popular reception, not a belief documented in the original prison-era performances. Radio Nacional explicitly framed this afterlife as “San Pugliese y los milagros de la dignidad” ([Radio Nacional](https://cdn.radionacional.com.ar/san-pugliese-y-los-milagros-de-la-dignidad/)).

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Substitution

The carnation occupied the place of a missing body. Cascallar’s formulation is exact: the flower was “in replacement of Pugliese.” It did not replace his musical function—the orchestra’s sound remained deprived of his piano—but made the deprivation visible.

### 6.2 Silent accusation

The object converted absence into an assertion: the pianist had not abandoned the audience, fallen ill, or been casually substituted; he had been prevented from performing. The silent instrument and red flower pointed toward state action without requiring a long speech that might itself be censored.

### 6.3 Refusal of normalization

A substitute pianist could make the engagement sound complete and render political interference invisible. The closed or unplayed piano refused that normalization. The flower marked the missing part as still belonging to Pugliese.

### 6.4 Continuity of collective labor

The orchestra continued performing and earning income while its director was imprisoned. The gesture reconciled continuity with solidarity: the collective did not dissolve, but it did not pretend that nothing had happened.

### 6.5 Color symbolism

The red carnation already possessed broad associations with socialism, communism, workers’ movements, sacrifice, and political remembrance. In the Pugliese setting its color plainly resonated with his communist affiliation. No evidence located, however, records a formal explanation by the original performers that selected the species or color according to an established party code. The political reading is compelling inference supported by context, not a recovered design memorandum.

### 6.6 Relation to “El tango está preso”

The carnation visually performed the same claim expressed verbally on walls, leaflets, and balloons: “The tango is imprisoned.” Mella’s reported phrase transferred imprisonment from person to instrument—“the piano is imprisoned”—and the flower made that sentence visible after speech ended.

### 6.7 Memorial and votive function

After 1995, placing red carnations at Pugliese’s mausoleum enacted remembrance rather than immediate protest. The act can carry several meanings simultaneously:

- honoring Pugliese;
- affirming antifascist or communist memory;
- identifying with tango culture;
- invoking the good-luck figure “San Pugliese”;
- repeating the historical sign of presence-through-absence.

No formal liturgy, access restriction, authorized florist, prescribed number of flowers, or canonical placement rule is documented.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 “The first carnation was thrown in Rosario”

**Tier:** later local tradition with plausible historical core.

The fullest form says that Pugliese was detained shortly before a Newell’s engagement; the club refused cancellation; the orchestra appeared without him; Mella spoke of the imprisoned piano and threw a red carnation onto its closed lid; the audience erupted.

The event is plausible and may be substantially accurate. Its elevation into the absolute origin conflicts with testimony that the practice existed in the 1940s. Rosario may instead be where the best-remembered theatrical form arose.

### 7.2 “The orchestra always played without piano”

**Tier:** legendary simplification contradicted by participant testimony.

The empty piano was central to the emblem, but Alessio recalled Armando Cupo replacing Pugliese on some occasions. “Always” is not supported.

### 7.3 “There was one famous carnation”

**Tier:** grammatical and commemorative compression.

The singular object never had material continuity. Every fresh flower was another token. The continuous entity is the sign or ritual.

### 7.4 “Pugliese was always actually in prison whenever the flower appeared”

**Tier:** doubtful generalization.

Some absences resulted from detention, others from official prohibition or interrupted employment. Later anecdote even claims that Pugliese sometimes appeared after the flower had been placed and gave it to an audience member. That anecdote lacks a located primary citation, but it warns against treating every flower appearance as documentary proof of incarceration.

### 7.5 “The flower never wilted”

**Tier:** poetic metaphor.

A commemorative article called it a carnation that “was never seen wilted.” Literally, every cut carnation would deteriorate. Figuratively, repeated replacement kept the symbol alive.

### 7.6 “The ship *París* was going to be sunk with Pugliese aboard”

**Tier:** contemporaneous or near-contemporaneous rumor; unproved plot.

The rumor intensified solidarity during Operación Cardenal. The ship was not sunk. The Huella del Sur study explicitly leaves open whether public pressure stopped a plan or whether the plan never existed. Retellings that state the intended mass killing as established fact exceed the evidence.

### 7.7 “San Pugliese and miraculous protection”

**Tier:** late twentieth- and twenty-first-century secular cult.

Musicians and performers invoke Pugliese’s name or display mock prayer cards to avert bad luck. The carnation joins this iconography but did not originate as a magical object. The modern cult transforms a political sign into an auspicious and sometimes quasi-sacred emblem.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Material original

No original prison-era carnation is known to exist. Its location is therefore not “lost” in the ordinary museum sense; it was a consumable object never documented as entering permanent custody. The most likely fate of each token was disposal, natural decay, or informal gifting, but no particular fate is recorded.

### Continuing tokens

Fresh red carnations continue to be placed at Pugliese’s mausoleum in Chacarita Cemetery, Buenos Aires. A 2005 report described the memorial’s piano as habitually bearing fresh carnations. This is a living replacement tradition, not a conserved original ([Página/12](https://www.pagina12.com.ar/diario/espectaculos/6-54138-2005-07-25.html)).

### Associated physical candidates

1. **Pugliese’s historic piano at the Casa del Tango.**  
   Reported as donated by Pugliese. It is materially authentic as his instrument, subject to institutional records, but its identity as the particular prison-era flower-bearing piano has not been demonstrated.

2. **The piano sculpture at the Chacarita mausoleum.**  
   A commemorative representation that receives fresh flowers. It is not a performance instrument or the historic piano.

3. **Pianos used in reenactments.**  
   The 2009 Teatro Avenida piano temporarily carried the emblem but has no reported continuing special status.

### Ownership and access

Fresh flowers belong initially to their donors and are effectively relinquished as offerings. Cemetery authorities control the memorial site. No current ownership or repatriation dispute was found. No accession number for a carnation exists.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Funerary commemoration

The most durable material afterlife is the repeated placement of fresh carnations on the mausoleum’s sculpted piano. The ritual preserves the original syntax: flower + piano + absent Pugliese.

### 9.2 Concert reenactment

The 2009 “Noche Pugliese” at Teatro Avenida reconstructed the empty piano and carnation before former members of his orchestra played “La yumba.” The audience’s shower of carnations expanded a once-minimal sign into collective commemoration.

### 9.3 Music

Dutch bandoneonist Carel Kraayenhof composed “Clavel Rojo,” recorded by Sexteto Canyengue. The composer’s album notes explicitly dedicate the piece to Pugliese and explain the title as the carnation placed on the piano during his imprisonments ([Carel Kraayenhof, *Por el Tango*](https://www.carelkraayenhof.nl/albums/por-el-tango/)).

### 9.4 Documentary and broadcast culture

Television and radio profiles repeatedly use the carnation as a compact image of persecution and dignity. Radio Nacional’s documentary *San Pugliese y los milagros de la dignidad* includes the carnation alongside prison solidarity and Pugliese’s later saint-like popular image.

### 9.5 Political memory

The flower remains legible within Argentine communist, labor, antifascist, and popular-cultural memory. A scholarly interpretation identifies three political operations:

1. the prohibition targeted Pugliese personally rather than the whole orchestra;
2. refusing replacement prevented the state’s action from being concealed;
3. the flower made a mute but effective denunciation.

This formulation appears in the extensively sourced Huella del Sur dossier and is analysis rather than an original performers’ manifesto.

### 9.6 Journalism and visual shorthand

Headlines such as “Siempre habrá claveles rojos” use the flower metonymically for Pugliese’s artistic and political legacy. Modern illustrations may show a pristine carnation centered on a piano even though historical performances varied in flower number and placement.

### 9.7 Secular sanctification

The red carnation, Pugliese prayer card, triple invocation of his surname, and title “San Pugliese” now overlap. This is a documented modern tradition. It should not be retrojected into the 1940s as the musicians’ original intention.

### 9.8 Commercialization

Recordings, posters, articles, and commemorative events use the image, but no registered trademark, licensed canonical replica, or commercial relic market was found. Because any red carnation can perform the symbol, exclusive ownership would be difficult to establish.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Source structure

The evidentiary record is unusually oral and retrospective. Its principal layers are:

- participant and fellow-prisoner recollections;
- Pugliese’s later interviews;
- family testimony;
- local club memory;
- biographies published decades later;
- scholarly studies of tango, communism, and Peronist cultural politics;
- anniversary journalism and reenactments.

The absence of a known surviving flower is not surprising. The greater archival problem is the absence, so far, of a contemporaneous text explicitly documenting the first use.

### 10.2 Hamlet Lima Quintana

Lima Quintana’s biography is indispensable because it emerged from proximity to Pugliese and Lydia Elman. Its conversational and literary construction also requires caution. It preserves memory rather than supplying an accession-style object history. The 2009 edition’s publisher described it as detailed and useful while emphasizing the friendship between author and subject.

### 10.3 Saúl Cascallar’s testimony

Cascallar supplies one of the clearest functional descriptions: the musicians placed the carnation; the piano remained unplayed; the flower replaced Pugliese; the public understood. Its weakness is the long interval between event and publication.

### 10.4 Ramón Rosa Roldán and Rosario memory

Roldán gives the object a named performer, venue, dramatic utterance, and local origin. This is precisely the sort of polished scene most vulnerable to later compression. It deserves preservation as an important variant, but not automatic priority over earlier-use testimony.

### 10.5 Huella del Sur synthesis

The dossier *Osvaldo Pugliese: tango y comunismo en el Río de la Plata* is the strongest accessible synthetic study located. It cites Pugliese’s own writings, Arturo Marcos Lozza, memoir literature, Mercedes Liska, and participant testimony. Crucially, it refuses the absolute version: Pugliese rejected replacement “on occasions,” while Alessio’s testimony shows that Cupo sometimes substituted. This correction materially changes the object’s history.

### 10.6 Political historiography

Accounts differ on the scale and agency of persecution under Perón. Some popular versions assign every arrest directly to Perón; others minimize or deny the repression; still others embed it in longer Argentine anti-communist policing that crossed regimes. The flower securely testifies that musicians and audiences perceived Pugliese’s absence as political. It cannot, by itself, identify the official who ordered each detention or cancellation.

### 10.7 Needed archival research

A fuller reconstruction would require:

- Buenos Aires and Rosario newspapers for Pugliese engagements, 1943–1957;
- Newell’s Old Boys board minutes, carnival programs, contracts, and photographic files;
- Radio Splendid schedules, scripts, studio photographs, and recordings;
- police and Villa Devoto prisoner files;
- Communist Party newspapers and freedom-campaign leaflets;
- correspondence and papers of Pugliese, Lydia Elman, Luis Mella, Enrique Alessio, and Armando Cupo;
- Casa del Tango accession records for Pugliese’s historic piano;
- negatives or prints showing the carnation with independently datable provenance;
- the original editions and interview tapes underlying Lima Quintana’s biography;
- precise original references for the anecdotes attributed to Petit de Murat, García Jiménez, and Jauretche.

Until such material is examined, the exact first flower remains unresolved.

### Final assessment

The red flower’s fullest reconstructable biography is not the history of a single preserved relic. It is the history of repeated cut carnations made momentarily identical through placement and context. Its physical chain repeatedly begins at an unknown florist or market and ends in undocumented dispersal. Its symbolic chain is much firmer: musicians placed a red flower on a piano deprived of Pugliese; audiences read forced absence; local memory supplied Mella’s phrase and Rosario scene; later performers reenacted the gesture; mourners transferred it to the mausoleum; and popular culture joined it to the secular cult of “San Pugliese.”

The historical object is therefore best catalogued as an **iterated political-performance object with no known surviving original token**. The unanswered archival question is not where “the” carnation is now, but when, where, and by whose hand the first documented iteration occurred.
