# "Domremy" (steganographic embroidery by Ekaterina Sinchinova): Object Dossier

> **Object tracked:** the unique hand-embroidered landscape titled *Domrémy*, completed in January 2023 by Ekaterina Sinchinova, professionally known as Katrin Vates, on commission from the pseudonymous Dr. Doeg.  
> **Not to be confused with:** the French village Domrémy-la-Pucelle; the lost medieval “Fairy Tree” or “Ladies’ Tree”; the Fontaine aux Groseilliers; the Château de Bourlémont; or the Basilica of Bois-Chenu. These are referents incorporated—sometimes imaginatively—into the composition, not surviving parts of the object.  
> **Evidence cutoff:** 6 September 2026.  
> **Public catalogue status:** no museum accession number or catalogue raisonné number has been located.  
> **Present whereabouts:** private custody; precise location and present legal owner are not publicly established by the sources found.

## Evidentiary key

- **Documented:** stated in a contemporary first-party record or independently verifiable institutional source.
- **Artist/commissioner statement:** first-party testimony, valuable but not independently audited.
- **Scientific or technical finding:** conclusion supported by an identified examination or reproducible technical record.
- **Plausible inference:** consistent with the available evidence but not expressly documented.
- **Tradition or legend:** historical belief or narrative whose circulation is documented, without treating its supernatural content as established fact.
- **Unverified:** asserted in the artwork’s accompanying text but not substantiated by an identified primary or authoritative source.
- **Unknown:** the public record located does not establish the fact.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity

*Domrémy* is a unique, rectangular work of contemporary hand embroidery representing an invented sacred-historical landscape associated with Joan of Arc’s childhood region. It was made by Russian-trained, Maryland-based embroidery artist Ekaterina Sinchinova, who exhibits and sells work under the name **Katrin Vates**. The artist’s commission catalogue dates the work to **2023**, gives its image dimensions as **20 × 30 cm (7.8 × 11.8 in.)**, and names the pseudonymous **Dr. Doeg (@drdoeg)** as the person who ordered it. The artist separately dates the piece more precisely to **January 2023** and describes it as her “first commissioned art.” These are the strongest first-party identification records presently available. ([commission catalogue](https://www.katrinvates.com/commissions); [artist’s essay](https://www.katrinvates.com/blog/blbrj44pjcc57mrf90eunqqwektyzb))

No museum accession number, public collection inventory, exhibition label, auction record, certificate number independent of the encoded blockchain transaction, or published verso photograph showing a signature or inscription was located.

### Title and names

The artist consistently publishes the title as **Domrémy**, including the acute accent. A Pinterest capture of a work-in-progress social-media post likewise reproduces the title and the Russian phrase **“Domrémy в процессе”**—“Domrémy in progress.” ([archived social-media capture](https://ru.pinterest.com/pin/ekaterina-sinchinova-on-instagram-domrmy-the-picture-is-called-domrmy-t--481251910199478122/))

The title refers to **Domrémy-la-Pucelle**, Joan of Arc’s birthplace in the present Vosges department of France. The commune’s official history records the forms **DOMUS REMIGIUS** and **DOM REMY** in an act of 1070. The name is a medieval hagiotoponym, effectively “(Saint) Remigius/Remi,” rather than a name derived from Joan. The suffix **-la-Pucelle**, “the Maid,” commemorates Joan and is not part of the embroidery’s published title. ([commune history](https://www.domremy-la-pucelle.fr/histoire/))

No alternative title in Russian has been documented. “Steganographic embroidery” is an accurate descriptive classification but has not been found as part of the formal title.

### Class

- Contemporary fiber artwork
- Hand-embroidered landscape
- Private commission
- Conceptual or cryptographically indexed artwork
- Sacred-historical imaginary landscape
- First work in what later became a small sequence of commissioned sacred-site embroideries containing 32-byte stitched insignias

The artist’s commission page places *Domrémy* before *La Verna* (2023) and *Monte Verità* (2024). In the accompanying text for *Monte Verità*, the latter is called the third work depicting European sacred sites and carrying a 32-byte digital certificate; *Domrémy* is therefore the first documented work in this sequence. ([commission catalogue](https://www.katrinvates.com/commissions))

### Construction technique

The accompanying first-party account says that Sinchinova manipulates fabric without a hoop or frame and works with a long, flexible needle. It identifies straight stitch, chain stitch, French knots, and stitches developed by the artist; the artist’s FAQ expands her normal repertoire to straight stitch, French knots, couching, trailing, chain stitch, and self-invented stitches. In skies she may create closely laid horizontal passages resembling a supplementary weft, while foliage is built from dense French knots at different depths to create relief. ([artist’s essay](https://www.katrinvates.com/blog/blbrj44pjcc57mrf90eunqqwektyzb); [FAQ](https://www.katrinvates.com/faq))

The object has not been examined by a textile conservator in any located publication. Stitch identification therefore remains based on artist and commissioner descriptions and published photographs.

The artist says a “regular picture” generally takes approximately **200–400 hours**, but she expressly declined to publish the hours for *Domrémy* while it was in progress because it was a priced commission. No exact labor total is therefore documented. ([FAQ](https://www.katrinvates.com/faq); [social-media capture](https://ru.pinterest.com/pin/ekaterina-sinchinova-on-instagram-domrmy-the-picture-is-called-domrmy-t--481251910199478122/))

### Composition

The published description and images establish the principal elements:

1. A large spreading tree dominates the left and central field.
2. A garland of blue and yellow flowers hangs from its lowest branch.
3. A well or spring occupies the foreground.
4. A green slope and heavily worked vegetation fill the lower register.
5. A small tower with one visible window stands in the background.
6. The sky is worked in pink and blue tones.
7. Thirty-two columns of binary stitches are concealed or visually absorbed in the grass to the right of the well.
8. The garland’s opening, the tower window, and the well are compositionally aligned.

The commissioner’s interpretive text calls the well **“Urd’s Well”** and the central tree a **“World Tree.”** These Norse-cosmological identifications are symbolic interpretations within the commission’s program; they are not names arising from the Joan of Arc trial records.

### Composite and imaginary topography

The scene is not a documentary reconstruction of one historical view. The commissioner explicitly says that the tree’s form is identical to an **ombú** beside Recoleta Cemetery’s Paseo de las Artesanías in Buenos Aires. The represented tree is simultaneously intended to signify the lost beech known from Joan’s trial as the Ladies’ Tree or Fairy Tree. Thus a South American visual model has been transplanted into an imagined French-Lorraine sacred landscape.

The historical tree described in the trial evidence was a beech. The embroidery’s tree, according to its commissioner, is visually modeled on an ombú. The two identities must not be collapsed.

The accompanying text also conflates or spatially assembles:

- the Fairy/Ladies’ Tree;
- a healing spring;
- Bois-Chenu;
- a “Bourlémont tower”;
- a dragon-haunted forest;
- the modern basilica’s sacred geography;
- Urd’s Well and the World Tree;
- blue-and-yellow floral symbolism;
- a cryptographic certificate.

The work is therefore better classified as a **constructed mnemonic landscape** than as an archaeological visualization.

### The steganographic inscription

The lower-right grass incorporates **256 binary decisions**, organized as **32 columns of eight stitches**, one byte per column. The published decoding table divides each byte into two four-bit nibbles. A raised or otherwise marked stitch is represented by `I`, an unmarked position by `.`, and each four-bit group maps to a hexadecimal digit.

The published result is:

```text
6da7a9a9d8d651c48e0a979ea6d1f00ce03cd1388ea390c5fa2050f9b2fb4910
```

That is a 64-character hexadecimal value, equivalent to 32 bytes or 256 bits. The artist’s commission page identifies it as a **Dogecoin transaction identifier**. ([commission catalogue](https://www.katrinvates.com/commissions); [decoding account](https://www.katrinvates.com/blog/blbrj44pjcc57mrf90eunqqwektyzb))

No conventional alphabetic signature is reported in the published material. The embedded transaction ID functions as an inscription, index, and machine-readable conceptual component, although it requires a decoding key and external blockchain infrastructure.

### Condition

Published photographs show a completed, intact work. No tears, losses, fading, insect damage, staining, loose threads, repairs, relining, cleaning, remounting, or conservation intervention has been reported. Because the present object has not been examined for this dossier and no formal condition report was located, its current condition must be recorded as **unknown beyond the published post-completion photographs**.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Commission

**Documented by artist statement:** *Domrémy* was commissioned by the pseudonymous Dr. Doeg. The artist calls it her first commissioned artwork. The commissioner supplied or developed the historical-symbolic program and the cryptographic component; the artist physically executed the embroidered landscape. ([artist’s essay](https://www.katrinvates.com/blog/blbrj44pjcc57mrf90eunqqwektyzb))

The division of labor appears to have been:

- **Dr. Doeg:** commissioner; historical and symbolic research; selection or provision of the 256-bit transaction identifier; accompanying interpretive text.
- **Ekaterina Sinchinova:** visual interpretation, design development, technical construction, stitch selection, and physical embroidery.

The artist states generally that she begins with a loose sketch on fabric rather than a fully determined cartoon, preserving room for alterations during stitching. This likely describes the making of *Domrémy*, but no object-specific preparatory drawing has been published. ([artist biography](https://www.katrinvates.com/about))

The artist’s essay identifies it as her **January 2023** piece, and the appended description is dated **21 January 2023**. The commission catalogue dates it more generally to 2023. The most defensible completion date is therefore **January 2023**, probably by 21 January, although the precise day on which the last stitch was placed is not established.

A social-media capture shows the work “in progress,” proving that at least one intermediate-state image circulated publicly. Its original Instagram timestamp was not recoverable from the accessible source.

### Place of manufacture

Sinchinova identifies herself as based in Maryland, United States, and her website gives Rockville, Maryland, as her business location. It is consequently plausible that *Domrémy* was made in or near Rockville. No work-specific studio record or shipping document establishes the location beyond doubt. ([artist biography](https://www.katrinvates.com/about))

### Material provenance

No manufacturer, supplier, thread brand, needle type, fabric mill, country of textile origin, or dye source is recorded for this object. The material chain cannot presently be reconstructed before its entry into Sinchinova’s studio.

### Earliest public attestations

The reconstructable sequence is:

1. **Work-in-progress social-media post:** an Instagram post, preserved indirectly through Pinterest, identifies the title and states that it is a commission. Exact original posting date is not established from the accessible capture.
2. **21 January 2023:** date attached to the commissioner’s description reproduced in the artist’s essay.
3. **2023 commission catalogue:** the artist’s site records title, date, dimensions, images, commissioner, subject, and cryptographic system.
4. **10 September, year not displayed in the page text:** the artist published “Domrémy. The story of the picture.” Internal content identifies the object as a January 2023 work. The page’s visible interface omits the year, so it should not be silently supplied.
5. **Later catalogue context:** *La Verna* (2023) and *Monte Verità* (2024) show that the 32-byte insignia became a repeated commission-series device.

### The Dogecoin record as an origin document

The commissioner and artist state that the stitched value is a Dogecoin transaction ID and that the transaction “serves as a certificate,” linking an image of the composition and details of the story. The Dogecoin project’s resource page identifies public blockchain explorers through which transactions may be inspected, while Dogecoin’s node documentation explains that a transaction can be retrieved by its hash. ([Dogecoin resources](https://dogecoin.com/resources/); [Dogecoin REST documentation](https://github.com/dogecoin/dogecoin/blob/master/doc/REST-interface.md))

Important limitations:

- A transaction ID establishes the identity of a blockchain transaction, not by itself the identity or authorship of a physical textile.
- The physical stitching can point to the transaction, but the transaction cannot prevent later copying of the stitched number.
- The claim that the transaction contains links to an image and narrative requires inspection and decoding of the transaction’s outputs or attached data.
- The accessible indexed sources did not expose the complete raw transaction record for independent verification here.
- No evidence has been located that a museum, conservator, cryptographic auditor, or legal authority authenticated the mapping between the transaction, the digital image, and the physical textile.
- The accompanying phrase “certificate of my authorship” is grammatically ambiguous because it occurs in a text attributed to the commissioner, while Sinchinova is the named maker. The safer conclusion is that the blockchain entry was intended to certify the commissioned work and its associated narrative—not that it independently resolves legal authorship.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is the illustrated object a real physical embroidery?

Yes, on the balance of the public evidence. Multiple photographs show completed and intermediate states; the artist claims the work; it appears in her commission archive; and later commissions repeat its unusual encoded-insignia concept. There is no evidence that *Domrémy* is merely a digital mock-up.

### Is the surviving private object identical to the photographed object?

Probably, but not independently proven. No present inspection, continuous custody record, signed verso, fiber analysis, or high-resolution comparison has been published. Because the work is privately held, continuity after completion rests on the absence of evidence of substitution rather than on a documented audit.

### Is it an accurate representation of medieval Domrémy?

No, if “accurate” means a documentary reconstruction of one historical view. The accompanying description openly identifies the central tree’s visual model as an Argentine ombú and overlays French, Norse, Christian, folkloric, and cryptographic elements.

It is authentic as Sinchinova’s contemporary artwork; it is not a surviving medieval textile, topographical record, or relic of Joan of Arc.

### Does the encoded number match the published hexadecimal value?

The artist and commissioner say that it does. The published diagram gives a coherent binary-to-hexadecimal method. Independent verification would require:

1. a perpendicular, high-resolution photograph of the entire stitched data field;
2. a declared reading direction;
3. a definition of marked versus unmarked stitch positions;
4. confirmation of byte and nibble order;
5. transcription by at least two independent readers;
6. comparison with the raw Dogecoin transaction record.

No such formal verification report was located. The correspondence is therefore a **first-party technical claim capable of verification**, not a published scientific finding.

### Does the blockchain record prove authorship?

Only in a limited evidentiary sense. A dated transaction may prove that someone controlling a particular key caused specified data to be recorded no later than a block timestamp. It does not automatically establish:

- who controlled the key;
- who conceived the composition;
- who stitched the object;
- whether the attached image was captured before or after the transaction;
- ownership of the textile;
- copyright ownership;
- whether the physical object later presented is the same one photographed.

The artwork’s conventional provenance—the artist’s documentation, process images, commissioner relationship, and physical continuity—remains essential.

### Errors and contestable assertions in the accompanying narrative

The interpretive statement should not be treated as a critical historical edition.

- Joan’s securely named voices are **Saint Michael, Saint Catherine, and Saint Margaret**. The inclusion of **Gabriel** in the commission text is not supported by the standard trial testimony cited in the available sources.
- Joan testified that children made garlands at the tree and that sick people sought water at the spring. She said she had heard stories of fairies but did not know whether they were true and denied that her revelations originated at the tree. The prosecution’s later articles amplified these customs into allegations of nocturnal invocations and magic. ([trial translation](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/basis/joanofarc-trial.asp); [French trial text](https://www.ste-jeannedarc.fr/condamnation/ordinaire_public1.html))
- The claim that the Catholic Church cut down the tree in the seventeenth century because of pagan worship was not corroborated by the authoritative monument record or the trial sources found. It should be labeled **unverified**.
- The phrase “Bourlémont tower overlooking the fairy tree” appears to compress distinct places. Château de Bourlémont is a real fortified site associated with the lords of Bourlémont, but the evidence located does not establish the embroidery’s small background tower as a faithful portrait of a tower that historically stood above the tree.
- The claim that the forest was “reported to be home to dragons” was not traced to a medieval trial statement or authoritative local history in the sources located.
- The tree’s asserted age of one thousand years is not supported by dendrochronology or an identified contemporary measurement.
- The official French heritage record says the Basilica of Bois-Chenu was built in campaigns extending from **1881 to 1934**, with later work in 1946; describing it simply as built in the last two decades of the nineteenth century is incomplete. ([French heritage register](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/merimee/PA88000042))
- The artwork essay calls Royer’s cycle seven panels, while other local descriptions count six large nave canvases and still others eight paintings. This reflects differing counting conventions or imprecision and should not be resolved without a work-by-work inventory.

### Forgeries and rival claimants

No forged *Domrémy*, authorized replica, rival physical claimant, or dispute over attribution has been located. Reproduction photographs circulate online, but no second textile claiming to be the original has been documented.

### Authority to authenticate

At present, authentication effectively rests with:

1. **Ekaterina Sinchinova**, as maker and first custodian;
2. **the commissioner**, as designer or supplier of the encoded program and probable first recipient;
3. **technical verification of the stitched data**, which could corroborate correspondence with a blockchain transaction;
4. **future physical examination**, if performed by a textile conservator or provenance specialist.

No museum authentication committee, catalogue raisonné board, estate, foundation, or laboratory presently appears to exercise authority over the work.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Physical function

No evidence indicates that the textile has been used in Catholic liturgy, pilgrimage ritual, oath-taking, healing, procession, or public ceremony. It is a privately commissioned artwork intended for visual contemplation and interpretive decoding.

### Symbolic functions

#### Memorial landscape

The embroidery gathers features associated with Joan’s childhood and posthumous cult while excluding Joan herself. Its subject is therefore less an episode than a place imagined as retaining memory across time.

#### Garland

In the trial testimony, children made garlands near the tree. Joan said she made garlands for the image of Our Lady of Domrémy rather than offerings to fairies. The prosecution transformed the custom into evidence of invocation and superstition. The embroidery’s single garland preserves this ambiguity: Christian votive craft, village festivity, fairy offering, national colors, or several at once.

#### Blue and yellow

The garland is described as blue and yellow. These colors may carry contemporary political resonances, especially because Sinchinova’s social posts use blue-and-yellow heart symbols. No first-party statement located explicitly defines the garland as Ukrainian political symbolism, so such a reading must remain an inference.

#### Well and spring

The medieval testimony associates the spring with people seeking relief from fever. The commissioner overlays it with Urd’s Well, converting a local healing spring into a cosmological source of memory and fate.

#### World Tree

The tree functions simultaneously as:

- Joan’s Fairy/Ladies’ Tree;
- a visually transplanted Argentine ombú;
- the Norse World Tree;
- a compositional anchor;
- the most technically dense area of raised embroidery.

This layered identity is central to the object’s “sacred” character but is a modern artistic synthesis.

#### Cryptographic certificate

The 32-byte field gives the object a second mode of legibility. At ordinary viewing distance it resembles grass; under close inspection it becomes binary information. Its ceremonial function is secular and evidentiary: it stages authentication as an act of reading, counting, and consulting a distributed ledger.

The certificate does not bestow legal title or ecclesiastical authenticity. Symbolically, however, it makes permanence reside not solely in fragile cloth but in replicated computational records.

### Access rules

No ritual taboo governs who may see or touch the work. As a privately held textile, practical access is controlled by its present custodian. The artist’s photographs provide public visual access, while the physical object itself is not documented as publicly exhibited.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The Fairy Tree

**Early attestation:** Joan’s 1431 trial.

Joan said that near Domrémy stood a tree called the **Arbre des Dames** and by others the **Arbre des Fées**, with a spring nearby. She had heard that sick people drank from the spring. Young people went there, particularly around the local spring festival, ate, danced, and made garlands. Joan acknowledged joining the other girls but denied seeing fairies herself and denied that her voices originated there. ([English trial text](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/basis/joanofarc-trial.asp); [French trial text](https://www.ste-jeannedarc.fr/condamnation/ordinaire_public1.html))

**Prosecutorial elaboration:** the accusation articles recast these customs as nocturnal dancing, invocations, sorcery, and dealings with evil spirits. Those articles are allegations, not neutral descriptions of proven conduct.

**Rehabilitation testimony, 1450s:** witnesses remembered the tree as a large, curved beech with branches descending toward the ground. Jean Morel said people formerly reported supernatural women or fairies dancing there, but that after the Gospel of John was read they came no more. He remembered Joan attending with other girls for play, not solitary magic. ([Jean Morel deposition](https://ste-jeannedarc.fr/rehabilitation/dep_jean_morel.html))

**Later romantic and nationalist elaboration:** nineteenth- and early twentieth-century writers developed the tree into a symbol of Celtic survival, Lorraine soil, feminine prophecy, or Christianized paganism. These readings affected the modern cultural landscape but should not be projected back into Joan’s own statements.

### Healing spring

The trial record documents a belief that people with fever sought water at the spring. It does not establish medical efficacy. The embroidery’s well is therefore a representation of an attested healing custom and a modern symbolic transformation, not a surviving relic or scientifically validated therapeutic source.

### Prophecy of the maiden from Bois-Chenu

Joan testified that people asked whether her country contained a wood called Bois-Chenu because a prophecy said that a maiden would come from around that wood and perform marvels. The tradition helped map Joan’s mission onto her native geography. The embroidery evokes the prophetic landscape without depicting the maiden.

### Tree destruction

The artwork essay says the Church cut down the tree in the seventeenth century because of its pagan associations. This version has not been confirmed in the primary trial evidence or the French monument inventory examined for this dossier. Other local accounts simply treat the medieval tree as lost and describe a sixteenth-century chapel or oratory that later fell into ruin. The date, agent, and motive of the tree’s destruction remain unresolved.

### Dragons in the forest

The commissioner’s description mentions a forest “reported to be home to dragons.” No cited medieval source accompanies the statement, and it was not corroborated by the trial texts or official local heritage records located. It is best classified as an **unverified legendary accretion within the artwork’s program**.

### One-thousand-year-old tree

The description calls the depicted tree a thousand years old. Because the medieval tree is lost and the depicted form derives from an ombú, this is symbolic hyperbole or tradition, not a dendrochronological fact.

### Basilica constructed upon the visionary place

The French heritage register describes the basilica as built where Joan heard voices, reflecting institutional tradition. Construction began in 1881 and continued through multiple campaigns. The exact location of Joan’s first auditory experience is not archaeologically demonstrable, and different narratives associate her voices with the family garden, church vicinity, and Bois-Chenu landscape. ([French heritage register](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/merimee/PA88000042))

### The blockchain as immortality or proof

A modern legend can form around technological permanence: that placing a reference on a blockchain makes the artwork’s history immutable. More precisely, the confirmed transaction is highly durable and replicated, but web links can decay, images can disappear, decoding conventions can be forgotten, and a transaction cannot physically preserve the embroidery. The work deliberately exploits the gap between digital persistence and textile vulnerability.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Publication by the artist

The object’s principal reception has occurred through Sinchinova’s own website and social-media ecosystem. The artist promoted it both as a finished commission and as a story-rich work whose visual program requires explanation.

Unlike her more naturalistic landscapes, *Domrémy* has a detailed commissioner-authored interpretive apparatus. Its public identity therefore depends unusually heavily on text.

### Place in the artist’s career

The work is important within Sinchinova’s documented practice because it is:

- her first commissioned artwork;
- the first documented collaboration with Dr. Doeg;
- apparently the first of three sacred-site landscapes using 32-byte stitched blockchain identifiers;
- an early professional work, completed about one year after the artist’s stated 2022 career beginning.

The artist’s later CV records awards and exhibitions from 2023 onward, but it does not identify *Domrémy* as an award-winning or exhibited piece. The 2023 Woodlawn prizes therefore must not be attributed to this work without an object list. ([artist CV](https://www.katrinvates.com/art-cv))

### Companion works and series formation

*La Verna* and *Monte Verità* retrospectively alter how *Domrémy* is understood. What began as a singular commission became the prototype for a format joining:

- a sacred European landscape;
- dense naturalistic embroidery;
- a researched historical-esoteric narrative;
- a 256-bit stitched identifier;
- a Dogecoin transaction serving as an external digital record.

### Wider media coverage

General profiles of Sinchinova emphasize the realism and dimensionality of her foliage, her self-taught embroidery technique, and her use of hand- and machine-embroidery threads. A 2025 DeMilked feature repeats her account of beginning landscape embroidery while living in Siberia and seeking visual greenery. It does not appear to discuss *Domrémy* individually. ([DeMilked profile](https://www.demilked.com/katrin-vates-embroidery))

No substantial independent critical review, exhibition catalogue entry, auction commentary, academic article, or conservation study devoted specifically to *Domrémy* was located.

### Replicas and commercial derivatives

No authorized replica, kit, pattern, print edition, NFT, cast, facsimile, or forged copy of *Domrémy* has been documented. Images on the artist’s website are reproductions, not separate object claimants.

### Political reception

The blue-and-yellow floral garland and the artist’s blue-and-yellow social-media markers permit a contemporary Ukraine-related reading, but no located first-party text explicitly assigns that meaning to this object. No documented nationalist, religious, or political organization has mobilized the embroidery.

### Religious reception

The work has no documented cult, shrine, indulgence, ecclesiastical blessing, pilgrimage use, or Church ownership. “Sacred” describes its subject and symbolic field, not an official canonical status.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### State of scholarship on the object

There is not yet a scholarly literature on *Domrémy*. Its historiography consists primarily of:

1. the artist’s commission catalogue;
2. the artist’s narrative blog post;
3. the commissioner’s text reproduced on both;
4. social-media process documentation;
5. the later companion commissions that define it retrospectively as the first member of a series.

No catalogue raisonné, archive finding aid, oral-history transcript, gallery file, conservation report, or academic analysis was found.

### Principal unresolved questions

1. What is the exact support fabric?
2. What fibers and thread brands were used?
3. Is any metallic or goldwork material present?
4. What are the overall mounted and framed dimensions?
5. Does the verso carry a signature, date, label, or certificate?
6. What was the price?
7. Where exactly was it made?
8. When and how was it delivered?
9. Who presently owns and physically holds it?
10. Has it ever been publicly exhibited?
11. Can the 256 stitches be independently transcribed from a high-resolution image?
12. What exact data are stored in the corresponding Dogecoin transaction?
13. Who controlled the transaction’s originating key?
14. When was the transaction confirmed relative to completion of the textile?
15. Does the transaction point to a content-addressed permanent file or an ordinary mutable web URL?
16. What documentary source supplied the claimed tower, dragons, thousand-year age, and seventeenth-century ecclesiastical felling of the tree?

### The principal interpretive debate

The most fruitful issue is not whether the work is “historically accurate,” because its composite method is openly declared. The issue is how its layers should be distinguished:

| Layer | Evidentiary character |
|---|---|
| Physical embroidery | Documented contemporary object |
| Artist and commissioner | Documented maker–commissioner relationship |
| Domrémy title | Historical place-name used symbolically |
| Joan’s tree, spring, garlands | Attested in trial and rehabilitation testimony |
| Fairies | Reported local belief; Joan did not claim to have seen them |
| Sorcery at the tree | Prosecutorial allegation |
| Church-directed seventeenth-century felling | Unverified |
| Dragon-haunted forest | Unverified legendary addition |
| Ombú form | Declared modern visual transplantation |
| World Tree and Urd’s Well | Declared modern syncretic interpretation |
| 256-bit inscription | Physical and conceptual feature, first-party decoded |
| Dogecoin certificate | Technically plausible; full content not independently audited here |
| Legal proof of authorship or ownership | Not established by the transaction alone |

### Problems in the published object text

The accompanying essay is the indispensable primary source for the commission, but it is not neutral cataloguing. It contains lyrical criticism, historical claims, symbolic exegesis, and commissioner-supplied narrative without consistent source citation. Future scholarship should preserve it as evidence of original intent while independently checking each factual assertion.

### Recommended future documentation

A minimally adequate object file should contain:

- recto and verso photography with scale and color target;
- macro photographs of all 32 byte columns;
- artist-signed materials and technique statement;
- commission contract or redacted invoice;
- delivery and custody statement;
- condition report;
- transcription protocol for the binary field;
- raw Dogecoin transaction and decoded scripts;
- archived copies of every linked digital file;
- clarification of copyright, physical title, and ownership of the cryptographic key.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and first-party sources for the object

**Ekaterina Sinchinova, “Domrémy. The story of the picture.”**  
The fullest first-party account: first-commission status, January 2023 date, technique description, composition, decoding table, hexadecimal value, and commissioner’s narrative. It combines artist testimony with text supplied by Dr. Doeg and therefore must be read as both documentation and original interpretive apparatus.

**Ekaterina Sinchinova, “Commissions.”**  
The closest available catalogue record. It supplies the 2023 date, 20 × 30 cm dimensions, images, commissioner identification, location narrative, transaction-certificate claim, and sequence with *La Verna* and *Monte Verità*.

**Ekaterina Sinchinova, “About Me,” “FAQ,” and “CV.”**  
These establish the artist’s training, Maryland base, career chronology, normal supports, thread categories, stitch repertoire, labor estimates, commission procedure, shipping practices, and exhibition history. They do not constitute an object-specific conservation record.

**Pinterest capture of the artist’s Instagram post.**  
Secondary preservation of first-party process text. Useful for confirming the work-in-progress state and title, but inferior to the inaccessible original Instagram record.

### Technical sources

**Dogecoin Core REST-interface documentation.**  
Explains that node software retrieves a transaction through its hash. It establishes the technical meaning and retrievability of a transaction identifier, not the authorship or ownership of the textile.

**Dogecoin resources page.**  
Identifies recognized public blockchain explorers. Useful for the intended verification context.

**BlockCypher developer documentation.**  
Explains the Dogecoin transaction-query interface and the distinction between transaction hashes, blocks, inputs, and outputs. It supports the technical framework but does not independently certify this particular physical embroidery.

### Primary and near-primary sources for the historical imagery

**Trial of Joan of Arc, English translation hosted by Fordham’s Internet Medieval Sourcebook.**  
Contains the interrogation and accusation materials regarding the Ladies’ or Fairies’ Tree, spring, garlands, fairies, and prophecy.

**French trial text hosted by Sainte-Jeanne-d’Arc.**  
Provides the relevant French wording and distinguishes Joan’s answers from the prosecution’s expanded allegations.

**Rehabilitation deposition of Jean Morel.**  
Records local recollections of supernatural women or fairies, reading the Gospel of John, village recreation, and Joan’s non-solitary attendance.

**Jules Quicherat, ed., *Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc*.**  
Foundational nineteenth-century edition of the trial documentation. The linked Google Books record supplies bibliographic access rather than a complete modern critical apparatus.

### Institutional and local heritage sources

**French Ministry of Culture, POP/Mérimée PA88000042.**  
The authoritative public heritage record for the Basilica of Bois-Chenu: location, architecture, construction campaigns, architects, and decorative artists.

**Official Domrémy-la-Pucelle commune history.**  
Supplies the medieval forms and historical explanation of the place-name.

**Local history of the Basilica of Bois-Chenu.**  
Useful for construction phases, diocesan involvement, pilgrimage administration, earlier chapel tradition, and Royer paintings; institutional and devotional framing should be distinguished from archaeological proof.

### Secondary contextual sources

**Georges Poull, *Pages d’Histoire au “Pays de Jeanne d’Arc”: le château et les seigneurs de Bourlémont, 1149–1412*.**  
Regional historical study relevant to the Bourlémont family and castle. It does not by itself validate every genealogical or spatial assertion in the commissioner’s narrative.

**DeMilked profile of Katrin Vates.**  
A general media interview confirming the artist’s self-description, inspirations, techniques, and career development. It contains no independent provenance evidence for *Domrémy*.
