# The Pantaleon blood ampoules: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

“The Pantaleon blood ampoules” are not one securely documented archaeological object. The name covers a family of glass vessels containing red or brown substances attributed to the blood of the Christian martyr Pantaleon/Panteleimon of Nicomedia. The principal claimant is the large ampoule in Ravello Cathedral, Italy. A smaller and independently documented claimant has been at Madrid’s Real Monasterio de la Encarnación since 1611. Other putative portions exist or have existed at Montauro, Martignano, Limbadi, Vallo della Lucania, Rome, Lanciano, Caiazzo, Irsina, Padua, Naples, Crema, and elsewhere.

No published biochemical, DNA, radiocarbon, or archaeological examination establishes that any substance came from Pantaleon, or even that the Ravello or Madrid substances are blood. Ecclesiastical “authentications” establish authorized relic identity and transfer within canon law; they do not establish biological origin.

Evidence is labeled as follows:

- **Documented:** supported by a dated document, institutional record, or direct modern observation.
- **Scientific observation:** an empirical observation or test, distinguished from interpretation.
- **Institutional tradition:** a claim maintained by the custodial church or monastery.
- **Pious tradition/legend:** a devotional narrative without a continuous documentary chain.
- **Hypothesis:** a proposed explanation not demonstrated by direct analysis.
- **Unresolved:** evidence is insufficient or contradictory.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Names

The martyr’s Greek name is Παντελεήμων, *Panteleḗmōn*, conventionally interpreted as “all-compassionate” or “merciful to all,” from *pan-* (“all”) and *eleēmōn* (“merciful”). Western forms include Latin *Pantaleon* or *Pantaleo*, Italian *San Pantaleone* and Venetian *San Pantalon*, and Spanish *San Pantaleón*. Late hagiography sometimes makes *Pantaleon* (“lion in everything”) his original name and *Panteleemon* a divinely bestowed name after his prayer for his executioners. That etymological narrative belongs to the saint’s legend, not to the documented history of the vessels.

Relevant object-names include:

- Italian: *ampolla del sangue di San Pantaleone*, “ampoule of Saint Pantaleon’s blood”;
- Spanish: *ampolla/reliquia de la sangre de san Pantaleón*;
- Latin descriptions in early-modern records: a vessel or *ampulla* containing *sanguis Sancti Pantaleonis*;
- canonical relic classification: *reliquia ex sanguine*, a relic “from the blood.”

### The Ravello claimant

**Documented physical object:** A large, transparent glass ampoule, partly filled with a dark red-brown heterogeneous substance, stands in a chamber at the center of the marble altar-dossal in the Chapel of Saint Pantaleon, Basilica ex-Cathedral of Ravello, Campania. It is visible through iron grilles and ordinarily is not handled. The custodial cathedral says that a seventeenth-century crack remains visible. Published sources consulted here provide no dependable dimensions, weight, glass analysis, maker’s mark, inscription, or accession number.

Observers associated with CICAP described the contents as filling approximately half the vessel and separating into four visually distinguishable layers. During the reported liquefaction, one layer becomes translucent ruby-red; the upper layer appears fatty and sometimes foamy; sediment remains below. This is an external visual description, not a compositional analysis. The vessel’s sealed state, precise closure, and internal atmosphere have not been published in a conservation report. [CICAP’s investigation](https://www.cicap.org/articolo/il-miracolo-del-sangue-di-san-pantaleone)

The immediate architectural container is itself part of the relic’s material history. The chapel has an altar of marble, alabaster, and mother-of-pearl; a white-marble dossal with four columns; civic arms displaying the saint with ampoule and martyr’s palm; a double iron barrier; and a 1638 painting of the martyrdom by Girolamo Imperiali. The cathedral records that the chapel was built after the Vesuvian earthquake of 16 December 1631, both as a civic vow and to give the relic safer housing. [Cathedral chapel history](https://www.duomoravello.it/la-cappella-di-san-pantaleone/)

### The Madrid claimant

**Documented physical object:** A small oval glass vial containing a brownish-red substance, mounted within a gold-trimmed or gold-mounted assembly and displayed in a glass-and-metal custodia/reliquary at the Real Monasterio de la Encarnación. A small bone relic is associated with it but is a separate object.

Luis Muñoz’s 1645 description calls it:

> “una ampollita de cristal, guarnecida de oro con sangre del Mártir”

—a little crystal/glass ampoule ornamented with gold and containing the martyr’s blood. He says it was normally “*quaxada*,” coagulated, but melted on the feast. This is the earliest presently located published description of this particular vessel. [1645 passage and discussion](https://alfayomega.es/madrid-venera-un-ano-mas-la-sangre-licuada-de-san-pantaleon-martir-asiatico-del-siglo-iv/)

Modern reporting describes the ampoule inside a small glass pyramid or custodia beside the bone fragment. Patrimonio Nacional scholarship says the present display combines elements from different periods; consequently, the seventeenth-century vial and its current outer mount should not be assumed to be a single contemporaneous manufacture. A 1993 handling accident damaged the outer relic assembly sufficiently that, in 1994, worshippers were no longer allowed to kiss the blood ampoule; the bone relic was substituted for direct veneration. The sources do not report that the inner vial broke or leaked. [Contemporary 1994 report](https://elpais.com/diario/1994/07/28/madrid/775394669_850215.html)

No public catalogue number, exact measurements, weight, glass composition, maker, or technical conservation report was found.

### Other claimant ampoules

The following must be treated as separate artifacts unless documentary transfer from a parent vessel is demonstrated:

- **Limbadi:** a small bottle containing coagulated material, donated to the parish by the heirs of Dr Antonino Saladino on 4 August 1996. Its prior provenance is not supplied by the parish account. [Limbadi parish](https://www.sanpantaleonedilimbadi.it/le-reliquie-di-san-pantaleone/)
- **Montoro:** a new glass vial prepared in 2022 from material extracted from a separately housed Naples ampoule authenticated by a 1756 ecclesiastical parchment. This is not documented as an extraction from the Ravello vessel. [Diocesan report](https://www.diocesisalerno.it/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/n%C2%B0-15_QSCRAS_settembre-2022-1.pdf)
- **Crema:** received a small blood relic in 2005.
- **Vallo della Lucania:** an ampoule reportedly contains permanently liquid material.
- **Padua, church of San Tomaso:** a small portion is reported always liquid and red.
- **Madrid, Montauro, Martignano and several southern Italian churches:** annual or seasonal liquefaction is claimed.

Most published lists repeat devotional claims without identifying documents, seals, dimensions, or present catalogue entries. They are therefore a map of claimed identities, not proof of descent from Ravello.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Traditional origin

Late Christian martyr narratives place Pantaleon’s death at Nicomedia under Diocletian or Maximian, commonly in 305, though dates vary. In the fullest legend he survives burning, molten lead, drowning, wild beasts, the wheel, and a bending sword before consenting to be beheaded. Blood and a milk-like liquid flow at his death; a witness or disciple, often named Adamantius, collects blood in a vessel. These details occur in late hagiography whose historical value was already judged poor by the *Catholic Encyclopedia*. Early evidence supports an ancient cult of a martyr Pantaleon, but does not authenticate any surviving blood vessel. [Catholic Encyclopedia](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/11447a.htm)

Thus the alleged fourth-century manufacture—collection of blood in Nicomedia immediately after martyrdom—is **legend**, not documented object history. No excavated vessel, ancient inscription, or continuous inventory connects the extant ampoules with Nicomedia.

### Constantinople attestation

A blood relic was known in medieval Constantinopolitan tradition. The Ravello cathedral attributes testimony to Constantine VII Porphyrogenitus (reigned 913–959), but its webpage does not give a work, chapter, original text, manuscript, or edition. Without that bibliographic control, “documented by Constantine Porphyrogenitus” remains an institutional claim requiring verification.

A firmer datable witness is Rupert of Deutz’s Latin *Sermo de pretioso martyre Pantaleone*, written approximately 1125–1129 at the request of Abbot Gerhard of St Pantaleon, Cologne. The sermon survives in medieval manuscripts and was edited by Maurice Coens in *Analecta Bollandiana* 55 (1937), pp. 254–267. Rupert reports a Constantinopolitan marvel involving Pantaleon’s blood and milk in vessels whose red and white levels or positions alternated annually. He was not an eyewitness to the original collection, and his sermon does not establish that the Constantinople vessel was the one later at Ravello. [Manuscript and edition record](https://geschichtsquellen.de/werk/4275)

A later notice places relics of the saint, including blood and head, at Hagia Sophia in 1157. Medieval evidence therefore establishes that a blood-relic tradition existed in Constantinople; it does not supply a documented export to Ravello.

### Earliest Ravello evidence

The cathedral argues that the relic was present by 1112 because a parchment associated with Bishop Constantine Rogadeo records the consecration of an altar and recognition of the relic. The accessible account is a modern institutional summary, not a diplomatic transcription or image of the parchment. The same summary notes a church dedicated to Pantaleon by 1138. [Ravello cathedral relic history](https://www.duomoravello.it/la-reliquia-del-sangue/)

The secure hierarchy is therefore:

1. **Documented medieval belief:** Pantaleon blood vessels were reported at Constantinople by the 1120s.
2. **Institutional archival claim:** a Ravello parchment dates recognition of its relic to 1112.
3. **Documented early-modern practice:** Ravello episcopal visitations describe the ampoule and its liquefaction from 1577 onward.
4. **Legendary bridge:** Amalfitan merchants brought the Constantinopolitan vessel west, generally placed in the eleventh century.
5. **Unproved identity claim:** the Ravello ampoule is the same vessel or contains the same material as the Constantinopolitan relic.

The vessel’s glass could be ancient, medieval, or later. No laboratory dating has been published.

### Madrid origin

Madrid’s object is first securely located at the Encarnación in 1611, when Aldonza de Zúñiga—later Sor Aldonza del Santísimo Sacramento—entered the convent and brought the *Relicario de San Pantaleón* as dowry. Patrimonio Nacional’s archival authority record states this explicitly. [Patrimonio Nacional archival record](https://archivos.patrimonionacional.es/index.php/autoridad-4136913)

Its prior history is disputed:

- a common institutional/devotional version says Pope Paul V gave it to Juan de Zúñiga, former viceroy of Naples, or to his family;
- some later reports incorrectly substitute Urban VIII;
- another version says it was extracted from Ravello’s larger ampoule;
- Patrimonio Nacional scholarship more cautiously says it probably came through the Zúñiga family from Naples;
- the 1645 primary description does not name a pope, Ravello, or an extraction. It places the vial among valuables contributed by Aldonza and her mother.

The documented seam is therefore 1611. All earlier links remain unverified.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Ravello

- **Fourth century, traditional:** Blood collected after Pantaleon’s execution at Nicomedia by Adamantius or unnamed Christians.
- **By the tenth–twelfth centuries, reported:** A blood or blood-and-milk relic venerated at Constantinople.
- **Eleventh century, legend:** Amalfitan merchants remove or receive the relic and sail west. A storm prevents departure until the vessel is left at Ravello, interpreted as the saint’s choice of the city. No contemporary shipping, donation, or translation record has been located.
- **1112, institutional archival claim:** Bishop Constantine Rogadeo recognizes the relic during an altar consecration.
- **Initially, local tradition:** kept in an earlier church of Saint Pantaleon, later incorporated into or superseded by cathedral custody. The exact sequence and building identification remain unclear.
- **By 1577, documented in a later-cited visitation:** Bishop Paolo Fusco records annual softening or liquefaction beginning at first vespers on 27 July and continuing through the octave.
- **Before 1617:** Ravello bishops reportedly removed and distributed portions to other churches. Most destination claims lack published instruments.
- **1617:** Bishop Michele Bonsio prohibited further extraction under automatic excommunication and initiated plans for secure housing.
- **1632–1633:** construction of the new chapel following the 1631 earthquake.
- **1643:** Bishop Bernardino Panicola ordered demolition of the old relic altar, transfer to the new chapel, and a double iron gate.
- **16 May 1661:** the physical translation into the new chapel was completed, according to a notarial act by Nicola Campanile. This date corrects summaries that collapse the chapel’s completion and the relic’s transfer into 1643.
- **1665:** Bishop Savo de Pannicolis inspected the ampoule during a pastoral visitation; it appeared like recently poured blood and was associated with Rogadeo’s parchment.
- **Seventeenth century:** the glass reportedly developed its surviving crack; no cause or repair record is published.
- **1695:** Bishop Luigi Capuano ordered central placement at the altar and instituted a translation feast on the third Sunday of May.
- **1718:** extraordinary liquefaction at the translation feast was recorded in local tradition.
- **21 May 1922:** partial liquefaction was interpreted locally as approval of the cathedral’s new electric lighting.
- **17 March 2020:** clergy announced an out-of-season liquefaction during streamed prayers amid the COVID-19 pandemic. The event was interpreted devotionally as protection, but no controlled observations were made.
- **2025–2026:** the ampoule remained in its chapel and in active cult; Ravello continued both the May translation commemoration and the July patronal observance. [2025 translation observance](https://www.positanonotizie.it/it/chiesa-40/ravello-celebra-la-traslazione-della-reliquia-di-s-155166/article)

No theft, wartime evacuation, sale, deaccession, or loss is documented in the consulted sources. Because the vessel is immured behind barriers, absence of such reports should not be converted into proof of uninterrupted material identity.

### Madrid

- **Before 1611:** probably in possession of María de Zúñiga Avellaneda and the Zúñiga family after a Naples connection; exact transfer and papal donor unresolved.
- **1611:** brought as Aldonza’s dowry when she entered the newly founded Encarnación.
- **1645:** described in print by Luis Muñoz as a gold-mounted little glass vial that liquefied on the feast.
- **1724–1730:** investigated by a commission appointed by Miguel Herrero Esgueva, archbishop of Santiago. The commission included clerics, canonists, and court physicians. Its 20 January 1730 statement affirmed that members had observed annual liquefaction and knew no natural cause. This was an early-modern testimonial inquiry, not controlled chemistry.
- **1836:** ownership of the convent property reverted to the Royal Patrimony under the disentailment regime.
- **1842:** the nuns were expelled and dispersed temporarily among other communities. The consulted archival summary does not specify where the vial was kept during this interruption; this is a custody gap requiring the convent archive.
- **Later nineteenth century:** the community and collections returned; exact date of the ampoule’s return was not found.
- **1914–1918, later devotional claim:** the substance allegedly remained liquid throughout the First World War. No continuous observational register was found.
- **From 1940:** Patrimonio Nacional held rights of patronage, government, and administration over the monastery, while the Augustinian Recollect community remained its religious keeper. [Convent archive description](https://www.patrimonionacional.es/coleccion/archivo-general-de-palacio/sistema-archivos/archivo-del-convento-de-la)
- **1965:** the monastery museum opened to public visitation.
- **1993:** a seminarian dropped or mishandled the reliquary during veneration, damaging it.
- **1994:** direct kissing of the blood relic was suspended; the bone relic was offered instead.
- **Twenty-first century:** the ampoule has been placed near the altar for the feast, with magnified live images supplied through a small camera and cold lamp. [Archdiocese description](https://www.archimadrid.org/index.php/oficina-de-informacion/noticias-madrid/las-reliquias-de-san-pantaleon-nos-reenvian-al-cuerpo-y-a-la-sangre-de-cristo)
- **2025:** the Archdiocese confirmed continued annual exposition and the continuing presence of both blood and bone relics. [2025 announcement](https://www.archimadrid.org/index.php/oficina-de-informacion/noticias-madrid/la-iglesia-del-real-monasterio-de-la-encarnacion-expone-un-ano-mas-la-reliquia-de-la-sangre-de-san-pantaleon)

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is the material blood?

**Unresolved.** No direct, published analysis of the Ravello or Madrid substance was found. No hemoglobin test, microscopy, proteomics, DNA analysis, species identification, radiocarbon date, or residue analysis has been reported.

CICAP investigators observed the Ravello vessel externally, measured temperatures near its chamber, and investigated climate records. They concluded that the appearance, stratification, timing, and lack of necessary movement were compatible with a mixture of fats, resins, waxes, or colorants having a low melting point. This is a **physical hypothesis**, not a laboratory identification. They emphasized that the substance had never been sampled scientifically.

Tixotropy—the movement-induced liquefaction proposed for some other blood relics—is a poor explanation for Ravello because its vessel normally remains stationary. Seasonal melting is more plausible: liquefaction can begin in June, persist past 27 July, and resembles the behavior of the Saint Lawrence relic at Amaseno, whose material reportedly melts near 30°C. Direct controlled temperature logging at Ravello remains absent. [Review of physical explanations](https://www.queryonline.it/2020/05/12/il-sangue-di-s-pantaleone-ovvero-nessuno-ha-un-termometro-a-ravello/)

Madrid is moved for display and historically was tilted, kissed, and handled. Both temperature-dependent melting and movement-sensitive behavior therefore remain possible. Assertions that temperature has been excluded are based on anecdotal comparisons, not published controlled experiments.

### Is Ravello’s ampoule the Constantinople ampoule?

**Unresolved and presently unproved.** Rupert’s sermon establishes a medieval Constantinopolitan tradition, while Ravello’s archive is said to establish a local relic by 1112. No contemporary transfer document links them. Moreover, Rupert describes blood and milk or red and white bands with alternating positions, whereas the Ravello vessel presents several heterogeneous layers. Similarity is insufficient to establish identity.

### Is Madrid’s substance a portion of Ravello’s?

**Pious/institutional tradition, not demonstrated.** Ravello reportedly distributed portions before the 1617 prohibition, so such a transfer is chronologically possible. But Madrid’s earliest printed notice does not say so, and no extraction certificate has been published. The contradictory attributions to Paul V and Urban VIII warn against harmonizing later accounts.

### Ecclesiastical authentication

Church documents authenticate an object for veneration by sealing it, identifying its asserted saint and relic class, and recording authorized transfer. They do not function as scientific proof. The 2022 Montoro operation is illustrative: a Naples relic was accepted on the authority of a parchment dated 13 February 1756, opened, pierced through wax closures, sampled by syringe, and resealed. Additional samples were said to be destined for DNA analysis, but no published result was found. The record also noted that the glass vial itself lacked wax seals before the operation, complicating strict forensic continuity.

No identified forgery of the Ravello or Madrid vessel has been proved. Equally, no scientific evidence authenticates them as fourth-century human blood.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

- **Pantaleon/Panteleimon:** asserted source of the material; historical martyr cult ancient, biological connection unproved.
- **Adamantius:** legendary collector and witness; absent from a continuous documentary chain.
- **Constantine VII Porphyrogenitus:** alleged tenth-century witness to the Constantinople relic; precise source not established in the custodial citation.
- **Rupert of Deutz:** author, c.1125–1129, of the earliest securely datable detailed Western account of the Constantinopolitan blood-and-milk miracle.
- **Amalfitan merchants:** collective actors in Ravello’s translation legend; unnamed and undocumented.
- **Bishop Constantine Rogadeo:** attributed author of Ravello’s 1112 authenticity parchment.
- **Bishop Paolo Fusco:** recorded the annual phenomenon in the 1577 visitation.
- **Bishop Michele Bonsio:** prohibited further subdivision in 1617 and promoted safer housing.
- **Bishop Bernardino Panicola:** supervised the chapel and ordered the relic’s transfer and iron protection.
- **Notary Nicola Campanile:** documented the 16 May 1661 translation.
- **Bishop Savo de Pannicolis:** inspected the object in 1665.
- **Bishop Luigi Capuano:** formalized central placement and the annual translation feast in 1695.
- **Juan de Zúñiga and María de Zúñiga Avellaneda:** probable pre-1611 possessors or transmitters of the Madrid relic through their Neapolitan viceregal network.
- **Sor Aldonza del Santísimo Sacramento:** documented conveyor of the relic into the Encarnación in 1611.
- **Luis Muñoz:** supplied the earliest located printed description in 1645.
- **Augustinian Recollect nuns:** continuous religious guardians at Madrid, apart from documented displacement.
- **Patrimonio Nacional:** civil administrator and collections authority for the Madrid monastery since 1940.

- **Christ, angels, and Hermolaus:** figures within the martyr legend, not actors in the documentary custody chain.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

At Ravello the ampoule is a palladium-like civic relic: it identifies Pantaleon as patron, occupies the chapel’s visual and liturgical center, and appears in civic heraldic decoration. Its principal observance is 27 July, with first vespers, Masses, prayer, and processional celebration. A separate May feast commemorates the 1661 internal translation. Because the vessel is secured behind grilles, processions generally employ the saint’s silver bust or image rather than carrying the cracked ampoule.

The perceived liquefaction is treated locally as a favorable sign; failure is popularly feared as a warning of misfortune. That omen interpretation is living tradition, not a formal predictive doctrine. Extraordinary liquefactions have been interpreted retrospectively in relation to plague, electricity, war, or epidemic.

At Madrid the vial is exposed on 26–27 July. Historically it was passed close enough to be kissed and tilted; after the 1993 accident, the associated bone became the contact relic. Modern video enlargement permits collective viewing without equivalent handling. The Archdiocese explicitly frames the relic as directing devotion toward Christ rather than as an independently magical substance. It has not issued a binding declaration that the annual change is a miracle.

The relic’s reported healing role derives partly from Pantaleon’s identity as physician and patron of doctors and the sick. Claims of cures require case-by-case documentary examination; none of the consulted sources supplied a medically verified cure caused by the ampoule.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### Blood gathered at martyrdom

**Late hagiographic legend.** Adamantius or faithful Christians collect Pantaleon’s blood after his beheading. Some versions add milk flowing from his neck or an olive tree suddenly fruiting. The blood-and-milk motif later explains the red and white substances reported at Constantinople.

### The alternating Constantinople vessels

**Medieval attested miracle narrative.** Rupert states that red and white contents or bands changed relative position annually; failure or irregularity could portend disaster. Later Ravello retellings sometimes compress two vessels into one or treat the white material as an ingredient of the present ampoule.

### The storm-bound ship

**Local translation legend.** Merchants carrying the relic from Constantinople are stopped by a storm near Ravello. Their vessel cannot depart until the ampoule is surrendered; calm follows, showing that Pantaleon chose Ravello. The story legitimates Ravello’s possession while avoiding an explicit account of purchase, theft, crusader seizure, or diplomatic gift. No early document for it was located.

### Annual liquefaction exactly on 27 July

**Attested practice with legendary precision.** Early-modern witnesses associate the change with the feast, but modern observations say it may begin weeks earlier and persist into September. The annual feast gives a socially fixed observation date to what may be a seasonally variable physical process.

### Failure predicts disaster

**Later popular accretion.** Widely circulated in tourism and devotional reporting; its earliest text was not established. It has materially reinforced annual public attention.

### Extraordinary signs

Reported changes in 1718, 1922, during 1914–1918, and on 17 March 2020 were interpreted as responses to communal events. The observations are incompletely documented and their providential meanings are devotional interpretations.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Ravello

As of July 2026, the principal ampoule remained in the Chapel of Saint Pantaleon, Basilica ex-Cathedral, Piazza Duomo, Ravello, Salerno, Italy. It is visible behind the double grille but is not an ordinarily movable exhibit. It remains in ecclesiastical custody and active ritual use. No museum accession number is published. Recent reporting states that the annual liquefaction was again proclaimed on 27 July 2026, but this was not accompanied by a scientific protocol. [2026 report](https://posideo.it/ravello-si-ripete-il-miracolo-della-liquefazione-del-sangue-di-san-pantaleone/)

### Madrid

The small vial remains in the relic collection of the Real Monasterio de la Encarnación, Plaza de la Encarnación, Madrid. The monastery is administered by Patrimonio Nacional and occupied by Augustinian Recollect nuns. The relic room is accessible only within the monastery’s managed, guided visitor regime; the vial receives special liturgical exposition around 27 July. [Current visitor information](https://www.patrimonionacional.es/visita/real-monasterio-de-la-encarnacion)

No current ownership, restitution, or repatriation proceeding concerning either principal ampoule was found. There is likewise no published proposal to return material to İzmit/Nicomedia or Istanbul. Absence from public reporting does not prove that no internal claim exists.

## 9. Modern Reception

The ampoules remain primarily pilgrimage and local-identity objects rather than globally commodified relics. Ravello reproduces the ampoule in chapel heraldry, festival imagery, parish publications, souvenirs, and tourism promotion. Madrid’s public image is dominated by photographs of the pyramidal custodia and annual news footage of the red substance moving.

Modern press often describes liquefaction as fact while shifting silently between “observed change,” “miracle,” and “blood.” Skeptical literature, conversely, sometimes states a wax/resin explanation too conclusively despite the absence of sampling. The defensible formulation is narrower: seasonal melting is a plausible explanation consistent with observations, but composition remains unknown.

The 1993 Madrid accident changed ritual practice, showing how a modern mishap altered access to the historic object. Camera magnification subsequently created a mediated form of veneration: viewers inspect an enlarged live image rather than touching the vial.

Ravello’s March 2020 event circulated through streaming and online news during the COVID-19 emergency, extending a local relic’s symbolic function to a global crisis. That interpretation belongs to modern devotional reception.

No documented commercial forgery marketed as the “original Pantaleon blood” was found. Modern distributions such as Limbadi’s 1996 bottle and Montoro’s 2022 extraction should not be called forgeries merely because their earlier provenance is incomplete.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

The evidence is uneven and largely controlled by custodial institutions.

Important primary or near-primary materials include:

- the manuscript tradition of Rupert’s *Sermo de pretioso martyre Pantaleone*;
- the alleged 1112 Rogadeo parchment;
- Ravello episcopal visitations of 1577, 1643, and 1665;
- Nicola Campanile’s 1661 notarial act;
- Ravello synodal or episcopal directives of 1617 and 1695;
- the Encarnación’s *Relaçión de la fábrica y adorno del relicario*, Archivo del Real Monasterio, caja 1-A;
- Luis Muñoz’s 1645 life of Mariana de San José;
- Madrid’s 1724–1730 commission proceedings;
- convent and Royal Patrimony records surrounding the 1842 displacement;
- the 1756 authentication and 2022 opening record for the Naples/Montoro relic.

Several decisive sources remain unpublished online or are cited only in modern summaries. A critical dossier would require diplomatic editions and images of the Rogadeo parchment, 1577 visitation, 1661 notarial act, 1730 Madrid report, seals, and all extraction certificates.

The principal debates are:

1. whether the Constantinople, Ravello, and Madrid relics share material continuity;
2. whether the Ravello vessel itself is medieval or an early-modern replacement;
3. whether the substances contain human blood;
4. whether observed changes correlate with temperature, motion, or another variable;
5. whether the white and red Constantinopolitan materials survive in Ravello’s layered contents;
6. how many satellite ampoules have verifiable extraction records.

No question can be resolved by external visual inspection alone. A minimally invasive modern program would document the vessels in high-resolution transmitted light, monitor temperature and phase continuously for at least two annual cycles, analyze glass non-destructively, image closures and sediment, and—with custodial permission—test micro-samples using Raman/FTIR spectroscopy, chromatography, proteomics, microscopy, and ancient-DNA contamination controls. Dating organic material would date the sampled substance, not automatically its attribution to Pantaleon.
