# The AK-pattern rifle Castro gave Allende: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary finding

The object conventionally called “Allende’s AK-47” was a real, Soviet-made, selective-fire rifle in 7.62×39 mm, presented by Cuban prime minister Fidel Castro to Chilean president Salvador Allende during Castro’s November–December 1971 visit to Chile. Contemporary photographs, eyewitness testimony, police records, and surviving forensic photographs establish the existence of a folding-stock Kalashnikov associated with Allende. The common designation “AK-47” is imprecise: visible construction features identify the documented weapon more closely as an **AKMS**, the underfolding-stock variant of the stamped-receiver AKM.

The rifle’s history after 11 September 1973 is less secure. Witnesses saw Allende carrying a dedicated AK-pattern rifle in La Moneda; police photographed a folding-stock rifle beside his body and recorded serial number **PL-1651**. General Javier Palacios then took possession of it and displayed an AK-pattern rifle to television cameras. Palacios testified that he delivered the weapon to Army Intelligence. Army Intelligence later sent a dedicated Kalashnikov and a magazine holding twenty-six cartridges to the military prosecutor. A February 1974 laboratory report says that it examined a Soviet 7.62 mm “AK-47,” number **1651**, and returned it to the First Military Prosecutor’s Office.

That is the last documented institutional custody. By 2011 the Chilean Army and military judiciary could not locate the rifle, its helmet-associated evidence, or a complete final case file. No museum currently publishes an accessioned weapon demonstrably bearing serial PL-1651. Rifles taken from Chile’s Naval Museum in 2011 were investigated and returned; none was authenticated as Allende’s weapon. The present location of the dedicated rifle is therefore **unknown**.

A separate modern allegation holds that Castro’s gift was a fixed-stock display rifle left at El Cañaveral and that the weapon beside Allende was another AK. This theory rests principally on Joan Garcés’s recollection as relayed by Víctor Pey and journalist Camilo Taufic. It conflicts with Beatriz Allende’s near-contemporary eyewitness statement that her father carried Castro’s dedicated AK in La Moneda. The documentary record does contain serious custody and identification defects after the rifle was removed from the death scene, but those defects do not prove the fixed-stock substitution theory.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Names and classification

The object appears under several overlapping names:

- **“El fusil de Allende” / “el fusil del Presidente”** — Allende’s or the President’s rifle.
- **“La metralleta de Allende”** — colloquial Chilean usage; technically misleading because the object was an assault rifle, not a pistol-calibre submachine gun.
- **“El AK-47 regalado por Fidel Castro”** — the overwhelmingly common journalistic and commemorative name.
- **“Fusil ametralladora marca AK-47”** — terminology of the 1973–74 Chilean police and military investigation.
- **AKMS** — the technically preferable identification based on the folding stock and stamped AKM-pattern receiver visible in photographs.
- Russian: **7,62-мм модернизированный автомат Калашникова со складывающимся прикладом**, *7,62-mm modernizirovannyy avtomat Kalashnikova so skladyvayushchimsya prikladom*: “7.62 mm modernized Kalashnikov automatic rifle with folding stock.”
- The abbreviation **АКМС / AKMS** expands as *Avtomat Kalashnikova Modernizirovannyy Skladnoy*: “modernized folding-stock Kalashnikov automatic rifle.”

“AK-47” is often used generically for the entire Kalashnikov family. Technically, the original service AK and its AKS folding-stock variant had substantially milled receivers in their most numerous early forms. The **AKM**, adopted by the Soviet Union in 1959, introduced a lighter stamped receiver, a small pressed magazine-well dimple, a ribbed receiver cover, and other changes. Its underfolding counterpart was the AKMS. These diagnostic distinctions are summarized by Armament Research Services and the Small Arms Survey. [ARES identification guide](https://armamentresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/ARES-Research-Note-No.-6-Introduction-to-Basic-AK-Type-Rifle-Identification.pdf); [Small Arms Survey identification sheets](https://www.smallarmssurvey.org/resource/weapons-identification-sheets).

### Individual identifying features

The strongest reconstructable description of the particular rifle is:

- Soviet manufacture, according to the military prosecutor’s submission quoted in the February 1974 ballistic report.
- Calibre: **7.62×39 mm**.
- Gas-operated, rotating-bolt, magazine-fed, selective-fire action, as standard for the AKM/AKMS family.
- Stamped-steel receiver with the short pressed indentation characteristic of an AKM-pattern receiver.
- Ribbed stamped-steel receiver cover.
- Curved detachable box magazine, nominal standard capacity thirty cartridges.
- Metal underfolding buttstock.
- Wooden fore-end/handguards and a wooden pistol grip.
- Serial reported at the scene as **PL-1651**; later reports shortened it to **1651**.
- A metal dedication plate attached to the right side of the wooden pistol grip.
- A sling was present in photographs, although the claim that Castro’s original dedication was handwritten on a light-coloured sling belongs to the disputed fixed-stock theory rather than the principal documented reconstruction.
- A magazine remained attached when the scene was examined. The police did not unload it there. Army Intelligence subsequently inventoried a magazine with twenty-six complete cartridges.
- The February 1974 report attributed a recovered projectile to the submitted rifle and stated that two shots had been fired from it.

Julián Aceitero Gómez’s photographic comparison identifies the death-scene weapon as an AKMS because it combines an AKM-type shallow receiver dimple with an underfolding stock. His work correctly exposes the loose official use of “AK-47,” although his further conclusion that the laboratory necessarily received a substituted rifle remains an argument rather than an established forensic finding. [Aceitero Gómez’s source-based analysis](https://piensachile.com/2014/08/05/el-arma-peritada-por-la-seccion-de-balistica-de-la-policia-tecnica-fue-la-que-se-hallo-junto-al-cadaver-de-allende/).

### Generic AKMS dimensions, not measured dimensions of PL-1651

No located inventory supplies exact measurements or weight for Allende’s individual rifle. Period military references describe the Soviet AKMS as approximately:

- Barrel: about 415 mm.
- Overall length: approximately 880–920 mm with stock extended, depending on measurement convention and production configuration.
- Folded length: approximately 645–655 mm.
- Empty weight: roughly 3.3–3.5 kg.
- Standard magazine: thirty rounds.
- Cyclic rate: roughly 600 rounds per minute.
- Effective sighted range: ordinarily stated as approximately 400 metres.

A U.S. Army equipment manual gives 868 mm and 699 mm for one AKM-family folding-stock configuration; the variation cautions against assigning generic catalogue dimensions to the missing rifle as though it had been measured. [U.S. Army, *The Soviet Army: Troops, Organization and Equipment*](https://www.trngcmd.marines.mil/Portals/207/Docs/MCIS/ITEP/RITC-East/FM%20100-2-3.pdf).

### The dedication

Several versions circulate:

1. **“A Salvador, de su compañero de armas, Fidel Castro.”**  
   This is the most frequently reported full text and closely matches the wording inventoried by Army Intelligence.

2. **“A Salvador de su compañero de Armas. FIDEL CASTRO.”**  
   This is the capitalization and punctuation quoted from the military-investigation inventory.

3. **“A mi compañero de armas.”**  
   Beatriz Allende’s abbreviated recollection in her 28 September 1973 Havana speech.

4. **“A Salvador, de su compañero de armas, Fidel.”**  
   A shortened journalistic and memorial form.

5. **“A Salvador, de su compañero de armas, Fidel” written on a pale sling.**  
   This belongs to Camilo Taufic’s claim that the genuine gift was a fixed-stock rifle left at El Cañaveral.

The death-scene and post-assault accounts place a metal plaque on the **pistol grip or empuñadura**, not on a wooden buttstock: an AKMS has no wooden buttstock on which to mount it. Later retellings frequently relocate the plaque to the “culata,” apparently through generic language or confusion with the rival fixed-stock account.

### Condition and surviving components

The rifle itself is not presently available for examination. Its last documented condition in February 1974 was sufficiently complete to undergo firing comparison and allegedly to discharge test shots. No conservation report, deactivation certificate, accession record, published measured drawing, or modern high-resolution serial-number photograph has been located.

Surviving representations and records include:

- Photographs taken in the Salón Independencia on 11 September 1973.
- A site sketch showing the rifle’s position relative to Allende’s body.
- The police scene report and graphical exhibit.
- A 20 September 1973 fingerprint report recording PL-1651.
- Television film of General Palacios presenting a rifle outside Morandé 80.
- A preliminary ballistic report dated 12 September 1973.
- The final ballistic report dated 25 February 1974.
- Photographs taken before the coup showing Allende using a folding-stock AK-pattern rifle.
- Photographs of Allende armed inside La Moneda, most famously the frame attributed to presidential photographer Luis Orlando Lagos.

The surviving images and documentary copies vary greatly in quality. This has prevented a reliable public reading of all factory marks, arsenal symbols, date code, and matching serials on subsidiary components.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Industrial origin

The rifle belonged to the AKM family designed under Mikhail Kalashnikov’s direction and adopted by the Soviet armed forces in 1959. Its major materials would have included stamped and machined steel, wood or laminated wood in the handguards and grip, steel stock struts, springs, and surface finishes intended to resist corrosion.

The available Chilean paperwork calls the submitted weapon “of Soviet manufacture.” No accessible source identifies:

- The precise Soviet factory.
- The production year.
- A factory or arsenal mark.
- The original Soviet or Cuban military inventory number.
- The route by which Cuba acquired it.
- Whether it was drawn from Cuban military stock or obtained expressly as a diplomatic gift.
- Who manufactured or attached the dedication plate.

Because the rifle is missing and no sufficiently detailed factory-mark photograph has been published, claims that it was specifically an Izhevsk- or Tula-made example remain unverified.

### Gift to Allende

Fidel Castro reached Chile on 10 November 1971 and was officially received by Allende at La Moneda on 11 November. His state visit continued into early December. Chilean and Cuban sources agree that Castro brought the rifle during this visit, but no located contemporary ceremony record, gift register, customs declaration, photograph of the handover, or exact date has surfaced. A reasonable reconstruction places the transfer during an official or private meeting in Santiago, but that is an inference, not a documented date.

The gift is securely attested by September 1973:

- Beatriz Allende, who had been inside La Moneda on the coup morning, publicly described her father carrying “un fusil automático AK que le había regalado el comandante Fidel” and bearing the legend “A mi compañero de armas” in Havana on 28 September 1973. [Beatriz Allende’s speech](https://www.archivochile.com/Experiencias/test_relat/EXPtestrelat0005.pdf).
- Fidel Castro, speaking immediately afterward at the same mass gathering, referred to the rifle as a Cuban gift and used it to interpret Allende’s last resistance.
- The Chilean death-scene investigation recorded a dedicated rifle on 11–20 September 1973.
- Several people close to Allende later recalled the gift and its inscription.

Thus the object was not a legend invented years later. What remains undocumented is the exact delivery episode and the rifle’s pre-gift industrial chain.

### Pre-coup use and residence

Photographs probably taken between late 1971 and 1972 show Allende and Policía de Investigaciones director Eduardo “Coco” Paredes handling or firing a folding-stock AK-pattern rifle at the country residence known as **El Cañaveral**, in the Andean foothills east of Santiago. The property, associated with Allende’s secretary and partner Miria Contreras Bell, was used as a retreat and as a location where members of his protective group practiced shooting.

Another reported sequence shows Allende firing a folding-stock AK while aboard the Chilean destroyer *Riveros* in southern waters. The available reproductions do not expose a readable dedication or serial number; identifying the gun in every pre-coup photograph as PL-1651 is therefore plausible but not independently proved.

Journalist Eduardo Labarca recalled the dedicated rifle being shown to visitors at El Cañaveral. [The Clinic’s account of El Cañaveral](https://www.theclinic.cl/2022/01/27/casona-el-canaveral-las-historias-del-celebre-refugio-de-allende-donde-boric-se-reunira-este-viernes-a-su-gabinete/).

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Before November 1971: Soviet and Cuban custody

**Documented only in outline.** The rifle was reportedly Soviet-made and reached Castro or the Cuban state before the 1971 Chile visit. There is no public factory ledger, Soviet export record, Cuban armoury card, or diplomatic-gift inventory linking serial PL-1651 to this movement.

Likely chain:

> Soviet manufacturer → Soviet or Cuban state supply system → custody of Fidel Castro or the Cuban government.

Every arrow before Chile is inferential.

### November–December 1971: gift to Salvador Allende

During Castro’s Chilean visit, the rifle passed by gift to Allende. No purchase price applied. The political language of the dedication made it a personal gift and a material statement of revolutionary fraternity.

The exact date and room of delivery are not established. Assertions that Castro handed it to Allende at the airport or openly at La Moneda should not be treated as fact without a contemporary photograph or record.

### 1971–10 September 1973: Allende and presidential-security environments

The rifle was associated with Allende personally and was apparently kept at least part of the time at El Cañaveral. Allende and associates practiced with it. It may also have circulated between El Cañaveral, the official residence at Tomás Moro, presidential vehicles, and protective personnel; no daily armoury log is known.

The rifle was not merely ceremonial. Photographic evidence indicates it was fired in practice, although the number of occasions and rounds is unknown.

### 11 September 1973, early morning: movement to La Moneda

After learning of the military uprising, Allende left Tomás Moro in a convoy of Fiat 125 automobiles and arrived at La Moneda at approximately 7:30–7:40 a.m. He and members of the Grupo de Amigos Personales, or GAP, carried AK-pattern rifles and other weapons.

Beatriz Allende later stated that inside the palace her father wore an olive-green helmet and held Castro’s dedicated automatic rifle. Because she spoke only seventeen days later and had personally seen him, hers is the strongest early identification of the rifle in La Moneda. [Beatriz Allende’s testimony](https://www.archivochile.com/Experiencias/test_relat/EXPtestrelat0005.pdf).

Photographs made inside the palace show Allende wearing a helmet and carrying a folding-stock Kalashnikov. The best-known image was later published by *The New York Times* and won the 1974 World Press Photo of the Year award. [World Press Photo catalogue](https://www.worldpressphoto.org/collection/photo-contest/1974/orlando-lagos/1).

Whether every visible frame shows PL-1651 cannot be demonstrated from serial marks, but the weapon’s configuration is consistent with the dedicated AKMS described at the death scene.

### Use during the defence of La Moneda

Witnesses described Allende moving between rooms, speaking with defenders, and carrying or firing an AK-pattern rifle. Accounts differ over the extent of his firing. Photographs establish possession; they do not by themselves establish how many shots he fired at attacking forces.

A later statement that he fought with the weapon for “four and a half hours” is commemorative shorthand. The record supports his participation in the armed defence but does not furnish an ammunition log traceable to PL-1651.

### Death scene in the Salón Independencia

After the air attack and fire, defenders and civilians were ordered to leave. Dr. Patricio Guijón stated that he returned to retrieve his gas mask and saw Allende shoot himself while seated on a sofa. Other witnesses entered immediately after hearing the shots and saw him dead with the rifle between or near his legs.

The 1973 scene documentation recorded:

- A folding-stock Kalashnikov near or held by the body.
- Its magazine attached.
- Serial **PL-1651** in the fingerprint examiner’s report.
- Two spent cartridge cases in the room.
- Projectile evidence.
- The dedicated metal plate.

The scene team did not unload the rifle there, reportedly at the suggestion of ballistic expert Carlos Davidson Letelier. Consequently, the number of rounds originally present was not independently fixed at the scene.

The 2011 international forensic investigation concluded that Allende died from a contact shot fired beneath the chin by a long firearm on automatic setting, producing two nearly simultaneous projectile paths, and found the injuries compatible with suicide. Its conclusion concerned the death mechanism; because the original rifle was missing, the investigators could not physically authenticate PL-1651 or match it afresh to the remains. [2011 SML presentation and documentary summary](https://archivoschile.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/Informe-SML_Pdte_Allende-19-julio-2011.pdf).

### General Javier Palacios

General Javier Palacios, commander of the troops entering La Moneda, acquired physical control of the scene and rifle. Police reported leaving the weapon and other items with him for transmission to the military prosecutor. Palacios was filmed outside Morandé 80 exhibiting an AK-pattern rifle and reading its dedication.

In his 22 October 1973 statement to prosecutor Joaquín Erlbaum Thomas, Palacios said that he had delivered the weapon to the Army Intelligence Service. This link is documented in the surviving military-case copy, though the ordinary evidentiary chain was compromised because the prosecutor did not personally seize and seal the rifle.

### Army Intelligence Service

After the police laboratory said on 7 December 1973 that it still lacked the weapon needed for a final report, Erlbaum requested the relevant effects from Army Intelligence. The Intelligence director, Colonel Julio Polloni Pérez, sent a weapon inventoried as:

> “1 metralleta con placa que dice ‘A Salvador de su compañero de Armas. FIDEL CASTRO’”

and one magazine with twenty-six complete cartridges.

This inventory is the strongest documentary bridge between Palacios’s possession and the later laboratory submission. It does not, however, include a serial number in the quoted description.

### First Military Prosecutor and LAPOTEC, December 1973–February 1974

On 27 December 1973 Erlbaum sent the weapon and magazine to the central technical-police laboratory, LAPOTEC. The laboratory’s 25 February 1974 report described what it received as:

- A Soviet-manufactured 7.62 mm “AK-47.”
- Number **1651**.
- A curved magazine holding twenty-six cartridges.

The report said that a projectile recovered near the body had been fired by that rifle and that the rifle was returned to the First Military Prosecutor’s Office. It reportedly concluded that Allende had fired two shots.

The report’s omission of the prefix **PL**, failure to publish a serial-number photograph, and generic identification as “AK-47” weaken—without automatically severing—the link to the photographed AKMS. The laboratory may simply have copied the prosecutor’s abbreviated description. Alternatively, critics argue that it examined another gun. The present record cannot conclusively decide between clerical abbreviation and substitution.

### After 25 February 1974: broken chain

The First Military Prosecutor’s Office is the last documented custodian. Its file does not record a later transfer, destruction order, museum accession, return to the family, or deactivation.

When retired brigadier Joaquín Erlbaum was questioned in the 2011 investigation, he said he did not know where the rifle went after examination. His suggestion that he had left the prosecution before the case ended conflicts with case papers showing his March 1975 closure submission. That inconsistency adds to the custody problem but does not disclose the rifle’s fate.

### Possible Naval Museum/Familia Merino route

In 2011, Allende-family counsel Pamela Pereira relayed information that the missing rifle might have entered a collection associated with Admiral José Toribio Merino and later the Naval and Maritime Museum in Valparaíso. Reports also connected the collection with a 2009 museum-storage theft. Judge Mario Carroza ordered AK-pattern rifles seized for comparison. Press accounts initially mentioned two guns; the Navy ultimately delivered eight. [Emol report on the seizure](https://www.emol.com/noticias/nacional/2011/07/06/491171/juez-carroza-incauta-fusiles-desde-museo-naval-que-fueron-donados-por-familia-merino.html); [SoyChile report](https://www.soychile.cl/Valparaiso/Politica/2011/07/07/25582/Armada-entrego-fusiles-AK47-a-la-PDI-por-el-caso-de-la-muerte-del-Presidente-Allende.aspx).

All eight were returned to the museum. No published finding identified one as PL-1651 or as the dedicated rifle. [Return of the rifles](https://www.emol.com/noticias/nacional/2011/07/25/494497/juez-ordena-devolucion-de-restos-de-allende-y-prepara-cierre-de-indagatoria.html).

The Naval Museum hypothesis therefore remains an investigated but unsubstantiated lead, not a provenance link.

### Army search in 2011

The Army General Staff informed Judge Carroza that it could not find the AK or Allende’s helmet. The Army ordered an internal investigation through the military prosecution. The missing objects and portions of the case record were not recovered publicly. [La Tercera, 12 August 2011](https://www.latercera.com/diario-impreso/ejercito-abre-investigacion--por-perdida-de-ak-47-y-casco-de-salvador-allende/).

No later authenticated recovery has been located.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Did Castro’s gift exist?

**Yes, beyond reasonable historical doubt.** Independent testimony, early speeches, photographs, the inscription, and official inventories establish a physical dedicated rifle.

### Was it an AK-47 or AKMS?

- **Official and popular position:** AK-47.
- **Technical photographic identification:** AKMS.
- **Assessment:** “AK-47” functions as a generic public name. The rifle visible at the death scene has the stamped receiver and underfolding stock of an AKMS. Unless better photographs reveal a nonstandard hybrid, AKMS is the more exact designation.

### Was the death-scene weapon Castro’s gift?

#### Evidence supporting identity

- Beatriz Allende personally saw her father in La Moneda with an AK which she explicitly identified as Castro’s gift and recalled its dedication.
- Contemporary police documentation records PL-1651 at the scene.
- Palacios publicly displayed a dedicated rifle after taking the palace.
- Army Intelligence inventoried a gun with the Castro dedication.
- The military prosecutor and laboratory received a Soviet rifle numbered 1651.
- The death-scene gun and pre-coup photographs of Allende’s folding-stock rifle are morphologically compatible.

#### Evidence raising doubt

- No surviving photograph publicly shows the serial number and dedication together.
- Police did not unload or seal the rifle at the scene.
- Palacios, rather than the prosecutor, took initial custody.
- Army Intelligence held it before sending a gun onward.
- The Army Intelligence inventory quoted in later research did not record a serial number.
- The prosecutor/laboratory papers shortened PL-1651 to 1651.
- The laboratory called it an AK-47 although the scene photographs show an AKMS.
- The laboratory did not include a serial-number photograph in the accessible file.
- The gun disappeared after its return to the military prosecutor.
- The 2011 ballistic expert never examined the original rifle.

These defects prevent archival-grade certainty about uninterrupted identity after Palacios took the weapon. They do not disprove Beatriz Allende’s identification of the rifle before she left the palace.

### The El Cañaveral “fixed-stock genuine rifle” theory

Camilo Taufic argued that Castro’s true gift:

- Had a fixed wooden buttstock.
- Bore a handwritten dedication on a pale sling or a plaque attached to wood.
- Remained hanging as a trophy at El Cañaveral throughout 11 September.
- Was confiscated when troops occupied the residence.
- Was different from the folding-stock weapon beside Allende.

Taufic cited Joan Garcés, reportedly speaking to Víctor Pey, for the assertion that the gift “never left El Cañaveral.” [Taufic’s article](https://www.archivochile.cl/Chile_actual/08_p_ich/chact_piz0045.pdf).

Problems with the theory include:

- Garcés was not inside El Cañaveral and La Moneda simultaneously and was not an eyewitness to the rifle’s location throughout the day.
- The statement is transmitted indirectly through Pey and Taufic.
- Beatriz Allende’s near-contemporary first-person testimony directly places the dedicated rifle with Allende inside La Moneda.
- A metal plaque could be mounted on the AKMS’s wooden pistol grip; it need not have been on a wooden buttstock.
- Pre-coup photographs show Allende using a folding-stock AKMS-type rifle.
- Palacios was filmed with a rifle carrying or presented as carrying the dedication immediately after the palace fell.

The theory identifies real weaknesses in the custody record, but the positive claim that a second, fixed-stock gift remained at El Cañaveral is unverified.

### Did the laboratory examine the same rifle?

Aceitero Gómez argues that it did not, chiefly because:

- The scene rifle was an AKMS, while the 1974 report says AK-47.
- The serial prefix disappeared.
- The report lacks an identifying photograph.
- In 2011 David Pryor used a North Korean AK-pattern comparison rifle, not the original.

The nomenclature problem is less decisive than the theory suggests: Chilean officials repeatedly used “AK-47” generically for stamped Kalashnikovs. Similarly, dropping a letter prefix can be a transcription error. Nevertheless, the absence of a sealed transfer record and serial photograph means sameness cannot now be independently verified. The proper conclusion is **documentary continuity claimed, but materially unconfirmable**.

### What did the 2011 examination prove?

The 2011 multidisciplinary team examined Allende’s exhumed remains, clothing-associated evidence, historic autopsy materials, photographs, and reports. It did **not** examine PL-1651.

The team concluded:

- The remains were Allende’s.
- Death resulted from a contact gunshot beneath the chin.
- The wound pattern was compatible with a high-energy long firearm on automatic setting.
- Two projectiles were discharged in a very short interval.
- The manner of death was suicide.
- No evidence of third-party intervention was found.

This finding supports Guijón’s basic account and the use of an AK-type rifle. It cannot independently prove that the gun was Castro’s gift, because the object itself was unavailable. [SML reports portal and links](https://www.biobiochile.cl/noticias/2011/07/19/dan-a-conocer-informe-completo-del-servicio-medico-legal-en-investigacion-sobre-muerte-de-allende.shtml).

The courts subsequently upheld closure of the investigation. The legal determination concerns the manner of Allende’s death, not a museum-standard authentication of the missing rifle.

### Present claimant objects

No presently known rifle has a published accession number and demonstrable claim to be:

- Serial PL-1651.
- The weapon photographed in the Salón Independencia.
- The dedicated Castro gift.
- The firearm used in Allende’s death.

Photographic reproductions, replicas, and anonymous AK-pattern display guns should not be mistaken for the original.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Institutions

### Mikhail Timofeyevich Kalashnikov

Designer associated with the AK family. He did not personally manufacture, commission, or present Allende’s particular rifle. His connection is design lineage, not individual custody.

### Fidel Castro Ruz

Cuban prime minister and first secretary of the Communist Party of Cuba; giver of the rifle, 1971. The dedication transformed military hardware into a personal and political object. On 28 September 1973 Castro publicly interpreted Allende’s use of the gift as fulfillment of the comradeship expressed by the inscription. [Cuban government speech archive](https://www.cuba.cu/gobierno/discursos/).

### Salvador Allende Gossens

Recipient, owner or personal custodian, documented 1971–73. He practiced with a folding-stock rifle, carried such a weapon in La Moneda, and, according to eyewitness and forensic findings, used an AK-pattern rifle in his suicide.

### Beatriz “Tati” Allende Bussi

Allende’s daughter and direct witness in La Moneda. On 28 September 1973 she supplied the earliest detailed public identification of the rifle as Castro’s gift and quoted the abbreviated inscription. Her testimony is important because it predates later disputes. [Speech text](https://www.archivochile.com/Experiencias/test_relat/EXPtestrelat0005.pdf).

### Miria Contreras Bell, “Payita”

Allende’s secretary and partner, associated with El Cañaveral, where the rifle was displayed and used in practice according to later accounts. She is not documented as its owner.

### Eduardo Paredes Barrientos

Director general of the Policía de Investigaciones and participant in shooting practice photographed with Allende at El Cañaveral. He appears to have handled or fired the rifle; identical serial identity cannot be read from the available photograph.

### Grupo de Amigos Personales

Allende’s armed protective network. GAP personnel carried similar Kalashnikov rifles, creating a genuine identification hazard in photographs and inventories. The gift rifle’s dedication and serial were therefore essential individual identifiers.

### Patricio Guijón Klein

Physician and principal eyewitness to Allende’s death. His account connected the rifle beside Allende to the shooting. The exact phrase “two or three shots” reflects the difficulty of distinguishing nearly simultaneous automatic fire.

### Óscar Soto

Presidential physician who escaped La Moneda before the final assault and later described the dedicated rifle. His recollection is corroborative but not eyewitness evidence for the instant of death.

### General Javier Palacios Ruhmann

Commander of the troops that entered La Moneda. First identified post-coup individual custodian. He took the weapon, displayed it on television, and testified that he passed it to Army Intelligence.

### Claudio Sánchez

Canal 13 journalist who interviewed Palacios outside Morandé 80. The resulting film preserves an early post-seizure visual record, although available reproductions do not resolve every identifying mark.

### Pedro Espinosa Valdés and Julio Navarro Labra

Brigada de Homicidios officers associated with the 12 September 1973 police report. Their record identified the rifle by the number 1651, omitting PL.

### Carlos Davidson Letelier and the LAPOTEC ballistic staff

Police ballistic personnel involved in the preliminary and final reports. Davidson reportedly advised against unloading the weapon at the scene, a decision that impaired the later ammunition-chain reconstruction.

### Joaquín Erlbaum Thomas

First Military Prosecutor responsible for the original case. He requested the final ballistics work and received the rifle back in February 1974. The surviving record does not document its later disposition. His inconsistent 2011 recollections are central to the provenance gap.

### Colonel Julio Polloni Pérez

Army Intelligence director and later military authority approving closure of the case. His service transmitted the dedicated weapon and magazine to Erlbaum.

### Judge Mario Carroza Espinosa

Investigating judge in the 2011 proceeding. He ordered Allende’s exhumation, forensic review, searches for the weapon, and seizure of Naval Museum rifles.

### Pamela Pereira Fernández

Lawyer for the Allende family who pursued the Naval Museum/Merino collection lead.

### David J. Pryor

British firearms specialist who prepared a 2011 ballistic opinion. Because the original rifle was missing, he worked from historic materials and a comparison rifle supplied by Chilean investigators. His work concerned compatibility, not authentication of PL-1651.

### Francisco Etxeberria, the Servicio Médico Legal, and international forensic team

Participants in the 2011 multidisciplinary examination. They established the medically and ballistically compatible suicide mechanism without access to the original rifle.

### Camilo Taufic

Journalist who developed the most influential rival-object theory, asserting that Castro’s genuine fixed-stock gift remained at El Cañaveral and that another rifle was used or planted in La Moneda. His contribution belongs to the historiography of dispute, not the documented custody chain.

### Joan Garcés and Víctor Pey

Garcés, Allende’s political adviser, is the attributed source for the El Cañaveral assertion; Pey reportedly transmitted it to Taufic. Neither supplied a contemporary inventory or photograph establishing the alleged second rifle.

### Luis Orlando “Chico” Lagos Vásquez

Presidential photographer traditionally credited with the best-known image of Allende armed in La Moneda. The photograph, initially published without his name, became World Press Photo of the Year. His connection is documentary and representational, not custodial. [World Press Photo](https://www.worldpressphoto.org/collection/photo-contest/1974/orlando-lagos/1).

### Institutions

- Soviet arms industry: probable manufacturer and original state custodian.
- Cuban government and armed forces: probable pre-gift custodial system.
- Presidency of Chile: political and functional environment of use.
- GAP: armed protective environment.
- Policía de Investigaciones/LAPOTEC: scene documentation and ballistics.
- Chilean Army Intelligence Service: post-seizure custodian.
- First Military Prosecutor’s Office: last documented custodian.
- Servicio Médico Legal: 1973 autopsy and 2011 forensic re-examination.
- Chilean judiciary: custodian of reconstructed case records after 2011.
- Museo Marítimo Nacional: investigated as a possible repository; no rifle there was authenticated.
- Fundación Salvador Allende and memory institutions: custodians of reproductions and documentary memory, not the original rifle.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Diplomatic gift

The rifle began its Chilean career as a state-to-state or leader-to-leader diplomatic gift. Unlike neutral ceremonial arms, its inscription deliberately called Castro and Allende **compañeros de armas**—comrades in arms. This carried several simultaneous meanings:

- Personal friendship.
- Cuba–Chile revolutionary solidarity.
- Recognition of Allende as a political comrade despite his constitutional, electoral road to socialism.
- An implicit warning that the Chilean process might require armed defence.

No contemporary gift protocol has been found establishing whether Chilean law treated it as Allende’s private property, a presidential gift belonging to the state, or military matériel. Later sources consistently speak of it as personally given to Allende.

### Practical firearm

Photographs indicate that it was fired recreationally or for familiarization before the coup. It was therefore not an unfired presentation piece.

On 11 September it served as a functional defensive weapon. It gave the president personal means to participate in the palace defence and, according to the judicially accepted account, to take his own life rather than be captured.

### Political relic

After Allende’s death the rifle acquired a relic-like status without being recoverable for veneration. Its sacred or legendary force rests on absence:

- It materializes the Castro–Allende alliance.
- It is associated with Allende’s last stand.
- It is identified as the instrument of his suicide.
- Its disappearance under military custody made it evidence of both the coup and the dictatorship’s opaque evidentiary practices.
- The missing dedication plate condensed the political conflict into one short phrase.

### Propaganda

The weapon served opposed narratives:

- **Cuban and revolutionary commemoration:** Castro’s gift had become the arm with which Allende honoured his promise to resist.
- **Military-junta presentation:** the dedicated Soviet rifle linked Allende visually to Castro, foreign Marxism, and the junta’s claims of armed revolutionary preparation.
- **Anti-suicide narratives:** alleged substitution of the rifle became evidence in claims that Allende was killed.
- **Constitutionalist memory:** the rifle represents an elected president physically defending constitutional authority.
- **Critical memory:** some commentators treat it as evidence of the armed, extra-institutional dimension surrounding the presidency.

These symbolic meanings are historical facts about reception; they do not independently establish events at the death scene.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### “Castro foresaw the coup and gave Allende the weapon for his last stand”

**Tier:** early commemorative interpretation, not documented purpose at manufacture or delivery.

Castro’s 28 September 1973 speech retrospectively represented the gift as providential or premonitory. Later retellings hardened this into a story that Castro specifically foresaw the coup and armed Allende for the moment of death. No located 1971 dedication text beyond “companion in arms,” letter, or presentation speech explicitly states that purpose.

### “Allende fought for hours with this exact rifle”

**Tier:** plausible core, narratively amplified.

Allende was photographed carrying a folding-stock Kalashnikov and witnesses described him participating in the defence. No shot-by-shot evidence identifies all cartridges he fired or proves that only PL-1651 remained in his hands throughout the assault. The popular formula compresses a changing sequence—carrying, moving, directing, perhaps firing, and finally dying—into continuous heroic combat.

### “The inscription was on the buttstock”

**Tier:** recurrent descriptive error or conflation.

A folding-stock AKMS lacks a wooden buttstock. Officially quoted records place the metal dedication on the grip. Later accounts saying “culata” may use the term loosely or may derive from the rival story of a fixed-stock gift.

### “The genuine dedication was handwritten on a white sling”

**Tier:** disputed late claim.

This detail is associated principally with Taufic’s second-rifle theory. The strongest scene-chain documents instead describe a metal plaque. Both could theoretically have existed on different objects, but no authenticated surviving rifle or contemporary comparative photograph demonstrates two dedicated guns.

### “The rifle was planted beside Allende”

**Tier:** allegation unsupported by affirmative physical evidence.

The mishandled scene and later disappearance permitted planting theories. Against them stand Guijón’s eyewitness account, the immediate post-event witnesses, Beatriz Allende’s identification, and the 2011 forensic conclusions. No witness has documented seeing troops bring the Castro rifle into the Salón Independencia after Allende’s death.

### “A different rifle was substituted before the 1974 laboratory test”

**Tier:** unresolved allegation grounded in real chain-of-custody defects.

The missing PL prefix, model-name discrepancy, lack of serial photograph, Army Intelligence interposition, and eventual disappearance make substitution possible. They do not demonstrate it. “AK-47” was routinely used as a generic label, and 1651 corresponds to the numeric portion of PL-1651.

### “Merino possessed Allende’s rifle”

**Tier:** investigated rumour.

A lead suggested that Admiral José Toribio Merino’s collection included Allende’s weapon and that it entered the Naval Museum. Eight rifles were examined in 2011 and returned. None was publicly authenticated. The rumour should not be converted into a custody link.

### “The rifle survives secretly in an Army vault or private collection”

**Tier:** unsourced survival legend.

Such survival is possible, but no post-1974 inventory, photograph, sale, collector testimony, or scientific examination proves it. Destruction, illicit retention, loss in military storage, and undocumented transfer remain equally unverified possibilities.

## 8. Current Status and Location

**Status as of 6 September 2026: missing; location unknown.**

The last documentary custody point is the First Military Prosecutor’s Office after LAPOTEC returned the rifle on 25 February 1974. The Chilean Army reported in 2011 that it could not locate the weapon. No official announcement of recovery has since been found.

There is:

- No verified current custodian.
- No museum accession number.
- No public display.
- No established legal title.
- No known deactivation status.
- No modern scientific materials examination.
- No accepted DNA, fingerprint, metallurgical, or renewed ballistic test on the object itself.
- No publicly authenticated fragment, dedication plate, sling, magazine, or matched component.

If recovered, authentication would require at minimum:

1. Comparison of the full serial **PL-1651** and its font/location with the 1973 fingerprint record.
2. Factory- and date-mark analysis.
3. Comparison with death-scene photographs, including receiver rivets, scratches, stock geometry, wood grain, and dedication-plate screw positions.
4. Examination of matching serials on bolt, carrier, dust cover, and other parts where applicable.
5. Toolmark comparison against preserved cartridge cases or projectile evidence, if those items retain a secure chain.
6. Residue and corrosion analysis interpreted cautiously after five decades.
7. Full reconstruction of any post-1974 custody claim.
8. Authentication of the inscription plate and attachment chronology.

Legal ownership would also be disputed. Potential claimants could include the Chilean state, Allende’s estate or family, and an institution asserting lawful governmental custody. Because it is a select-fire military weapon and potential judicial evidence, ordinary private possession would raise additional Chilean arms-law and evidentiary issues.

## 9. Modern Reception

### The iconic armed-Allende image

The most influential representation of the rifle is the photograph of helmeted Allende, armed and surrounded by defenders inside La Moneda. The image appeared on the front page of *The New York Times* on 26 January 1974 and received the World Press Photo of the Year award in March 1974.

World Press Photo credits Luis Orlando Lagos and dates the image to 11 September 1973. The negative’s early publication history and delayed attribution have generated secondary debate, but the institutional catalogue retains Lagos’s credit. [World Press Photo record](https://www.worldpressphoto.org/collection/photo-contest/1974/orlando-lagos/1).

The photograph transformed the rifle from one missing piece of evidence into a visual emblem reproduced in:

- Posters and political graphics.
- Murals and commemorative exhibitions.
- Book and documentary covers.
- Memorial ceremonies.
- Socialist and anti-dictatorship iconography.
- Online memes and decontextualized “last stand” imagery.

The famous photograph should not automatically be called Allende’s literal final photograph; other frames were made during the morning, and the exact sequence of surviving negatives has been debated.

### Cuban commemorative reception

In Cuba, the rifle became part of Allende’s heroic narrative almost immediately. Beatriz Allende’s testimony and Castro’s speech on 28 September 1973 fused the dedication, armed resistance, and death into a single commemorative account. That narrative remains influential in Cuban state publications.

### Chilean memory culture

In post-dictatorship Chile, the absence of the rifle has made it part of a broader class of missing Allende objects and compromised coup-era evidence. Its story is regularly paired with:

- Allende’s recovered spectacles in the Museo Histórico Nacional.
- His helmet, also reported missing from military custody.
- The sword associated with Bernardo O’Higgins.
- The damaged and dispersed furnishings of La Moneda.
- The lost or incomplete military-justice file.

Unlike Allende’s broken spectacles, the rifle has no authenticated material surrogate on public display.

### Documentaries

Films and television programs have repeatedly shown:

- Allende holding the rifle.
- Palacios displaying it.
- The photographed death scene.
- Reconstructions or generic AK-pattern weapons.

Walter Heinowski and Gerhard Scheumann’s East German documentary material preserves Palacios’s post-assault presentation. Later productions sometimes illustrate the story with an incorrect fixed-stock AK or unrelated Kalashnikov, reinforcing confusion between AK, AKS, AKM, and AKMS.

### Replicas and commercial imagery

Commercial posters, statuettes, T-shirts, and prints reproduce Allende with a generic AK. There is no authoritative replica documented as having been built from complete measurements of PL-1651. Representations should therefore be treated as iconographic, not facsimile, copies.

### Continuing controversy

The principal modern controversies are:

- Suicide versus homicide.
- One rifle versus two.
- AK-47 versus AKMS.
- Metal plaque versus handwritten sling.
- Whether the 1974 laboratory received the scene weapon.
- Whether military authorities lost, concealed, retained, or destroyed it.
- Whether a weapon once associated with Merino was a surviving candidate.

The 2011 forensic and judicial findings substantially resolved the manner of death in favour of suicide. They did not resolve the object’s post-1974 fate.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Primary evidentiary corpus

The rifle is unusually well represented visually but poorly preserved materially. The principal primary record consists of:

- Beatriz Allende’s 28 September 1973 speech.
- Fidel Castro’s speech at the same gathering.
- Eyewitness accounts of La Moneda’s defence and Allende’s death.
- Scene photographs and sketches.
- Brigada de Homicidios report of 12 September 1973.
- Preliminary ballistic report of 12 September 1973.
- Fingerprint report of 20 September 1973.
- Palacios’s 22 October 1973 military-prosecutor statement.
- Army Intelligence inventory.
- LAPOTEC final ballistic report of 25 February 1974.
- Original autopsy protocol 2449-73.
- Military proceeding Rol 1032-73.
- Judicial proceeding Rol 77-2011.
- 2011 multidisciplinary forensic reports.
- Court orders and closure decisions.

A digitized copy of the police scene report makes clear that the weapon and associated evidence were left with Palacios for delivery to military justice. [Digitized investigative document](https://archivommdh.cl/uploads/2/0/202183/00000134000007000017.pdf).

### Evidentiary losses

Research is limited by:

- The disappearance of the rifle.
- The disappearance of the helmet.
- Incomplete original military-court documentation.
- Initial military control of the scene.
- The prosecutor’s failure to secure the weapon personally.
- A gap between scene inspection and delivery from Army Intelligence.
- Lack of a published serial-number photograph from the 1974 test.
- Low-quality reproductions available to the 2011 expert.
- Terminological imprecision in historic reports.
- Uncertain survival and location of original negatives and television masters.
- The absence of accessible Soviet/Cuban export and gift records.

### The 1973–74 official account

The junta-era inquiry concluded that Allende committed suicide with the dedicated Soviet rifle. Its documentation contains evidence supporting that conclusion but was produced under military control, without transparent adversarial scrutiny and with a defective physical-evidence chain.

The later democratic-era inquiry did not simply adopt the original conclusion. It re-examined the remains and surviving records with national and international specialists. Its suicide conclusion is accordingly stronger than the junta’s press announcement, though its identification of the exact missing rifle necessarily relied on historic documentation.

### The 2011 scientific re-examination

The exhumation occurred on 23 May 2011. The multidisciplinary work included anthropology, odontology, genetics, ballistics, evidence analysis, and comparison with the 1973 autopsy. The team’s integrated finding was that a self-inflicted, contact-range discharge from a long firearm caused Allende’s death. [Servicio Médico Legal materials](https://archivoschile.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/Informe-SML_Pdte_Allende-19-julio-2011.pdf).

The principal limitation for this dossier is crucial: **the study authenticated the death mechanism, not the missing individual firearm**.

### Revisionist and dissenting literature

Luis Ravanal and other critics argued for an additional small-calibre wound or questioned aspects of the original autopsy. Those claims prompted public attention before completion of the multidisciplinary study but were rejected by the final team’s interpretation of the reconstructed remains.

Camilo Taufic developed a different-rifle/suicide-assistance theory. It drew attention to evidentiary irregularities but depends on late and indirect testimony for the central claim that Castro’s genuine rifle stayed at El Cañaveral.

Julián Aceitero Gómez conducted the most detailed public object-centred analysis. His contributions include:

- Correctly emphasizing the AKMS morphology.
- Recovering the PL-1651 scene notation.
- Mapping Palacios → Army Intelligence → prosecutor → laboratory.
- Demonstrating the absence of a published serial photograph.
- Exposing the original rifle’s absence from the 2011 tests.

His further propositions—that the rifle was certainly substituted and that the official object narrative was deliberately falsified—go beyond what the surviving evidence conclusively proves. His work remains valuable because it cites file folios and isolates the exact archival seam. [Aceitero Gómez](https://piensachile.com/2014/08/05/el-arma-peritada-por-la-seccion-de-balistica-de-la-policia-tecnica-fue-la-que-se-hallo-junto-al-cadaver-de-allende/).

### Present scholarly position

The most defensible reconstruction is:

1. Castro gave Allende a dedicated folding-stock Kalashnikov in 1971.
2. Allende kept and fired it before the coup.
3. Beatriz Allende saw him carrying the dedicated rifle in La Moneda.
4. A folding-stock AKMS, serial PL-1651, was photographed beside his body.
5. The medical evidence establishes suicide with an AK-compatible long firearm.
6. Palacios removed the rifle and sent it into Army Intelligence custody.
7. A dedicated Soviet rifle numbered 1651 was subsequently tested and returned to the military prosecutor.
8. The archival chain contains serious procedural defects, so uninterrupted physical identity after seizure cannot be independently demonstrated.
9. The rifle disappeared from documented custody after February 1974.
10. No current claimant has been authenticated.

## 12. Sources and Evidentiary Assessment

### Primary and near-primary records

- Beatriz Allende’s 28 September 1973 speech is the most important near-contemporary eyewitness identification of the gift rifle in La Moneda.
- Fidel Castro’s speech at the same event is primary evidence of the giver’s retrospective understanding of the gift.
- Police scene papers, fingerprint work, inventories, and ballistic reports constitute the core physical-object record, although created under post-coup military control.
- Palacios’s military-prosecutor testimony documents his role in removing and transferring the rifle.
- The 2011 Servicio Médico Legal reports are the strongest scientific evidence concerning the weapon class and mechanism of death but not the individual rifle’s identity.
- World Press Photo’s catalogue authenticates the international career of the central armed-Allende photograph.

### Strong secondary reporting

- Contemporary 2011 reports document the judicial search, Naval Museum seizures, return of rejected candidates, and Army’s inability to locate the original.
- Chilean heritage and memory institutions provide digitized newspapers, photographs, and investigation papers.

### Adversarial or revisionist sources

Taufic and Aceitero Gómez are not neutral institutional catalogues. They are useful because they identify inconsistencies and cite otherwise obscure records. Their documentary observations should be separated from their conclusions:

- The missing serial prefix is documented.
- The AKMS morphology is visible.
- The poor custody chain is documented.
- The claim that substitution definitely occurred remains inferential.
- The claim that the genuine rifle remained at El Cañaveral is unverified.

### Questions still answerable only through new evidence

- Where and when was PL-1651 manufactured?
- How did it enter Cuban custody?
- On what exact date and in what setting did Castro give it to Allende?
- Does a Cuban diplomatic or military gift register survive?
- Was PL-1651 marked on all major components?
- Was the rifle fired against attacking troops, and if so how many times?
- Did LAPOTEC receive PL-1651 or another rifle numbered 1651?
- Who received it when LAPOTEC returned it on 25 February 1974?
- Was it destroyed, retained unofficially, transferred to another service, stolen, or placed in an uncatalogued collection?
- Does the dedication plate survive separately?
- Was there also a second dedicated fixed-stock rifle at El Cañaveral?

Until the rifle or a new inventory is recovered, these questions remain unresolved.
