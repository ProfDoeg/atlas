# The Statue of Liberty: Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `the_statue_of_liberty_object_dossier.md`  
**Research status:** Current through 6 September 2026.  
**Object tracked:** The colossal copper statue *Liberty Enlightening the World*, its internal supporting structure, masonry-and-concrete pedestal, and the eleven-pointed Fort Wood base on Liberty Island. Detached surviving original components—especially the replaced 1876–1886 torch—are identified separately.

## Evidentiary key

- **Documented:** established by contemporary records, official instruments, photographs, engineering documentation, or institutional custody records.
- **Scientific finding:** based on materials analysis, structural examination, or conservation science.
- **Scholarly inference:** a reasoned reconstruction not directly stated in contemporary evidence.
- **Institutional tradition:** an account maintained by the National Park Service or another responsible institution but not necessarily supported at every point by contemporary documentation.
- **National or popular tradition:** a widespread commemorative account.
- **Disputed / unresolved:** credible sources or interpretations conflict, or documentation is insufficient.
- **Modern invention:** a demonstrably recent claim presented as older history.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity, class, and names

The Statue of Liberty is a colossal neoclassical civic monument: a personification of Liberty represented as a standing, robed woman bearing a torch and an inscribed tablet. The object is simultaneously sculpture, architectural envelope, iron-and-steel structure, former navigational beacon, national monument, UNESCO World Heritage property, museum site, and political emblem.

Its formal French title is **_La Liberté éclairant le monde_**, literally “Liberty Enlightening the World.” Bartholdi’s 1879 United States design patent calls it **“Liberty enlightening the world.”** The common English name, **Statue of Liberty**, became dominant; **Lady Liberty** is a popular personifying epithet. Emma Lazarus’s poem supplied **“Mother of Exiles,”** an epithet that was not part of Bartholdi’s original title or iconographic program.

Relevant names include:

- French: **_La Liberté éclairant le monde_**
- English: **Liberty Enlightening the World**
- Common English: **The Statue of Liberty**
- Popular personification: **Lady Liberty**
- Lazarus-derived epithet: **Mother of Exiles**
- Monumental unit: **Statue of Liberty National Monument**
- Island: originally **Bedloe’s Island** in the statue’s American period; officially renamed **Liberty Island** in 1956.

“Liberty” derives through English and Old French from Latin *lībertās*, “freedom” or the legal condition of being free. The figure belongs to a long European iconographic family that includes the Roman personification or goddess **Libertas**, French republican Liberté, and American allegorical figures such as Columbia. The monument is not a securely identified cult image of the ancient goddess, though its classical dress, diadem, and allegorical identity deliberately evoke that tradition.

### The sculptural figure

Bartholdi’s own patent description, filed 2 January and issued 18 February 1879 as U.S. Design Patent D11,023, is the closest thing to an authoritative contemporary verbal specification. It describes:

- an erect female figure;
- weight shifted principally onto the left leg;
- the right leg bent and drawn back;
- classical drapery comprising a stola or mantle over a tunic;
- a nude raised right arm holding a flaming torch;
- a lowered left arm holding an inclined tablet;
- a severe, calm, classical face;
- a diadem with seven diverging rays representing a halo;
- bare, sandal-strapped feet.

The finished statue also steps among a broken shackle and chain at its feet. These are partly concealed by the robes and pedestal and are difficult to see from ground level. The forward motion distinguishes the figure from a wholly static enthroned or columnar allegory.

The tablet bears:

> **JULY IV MDCCLXXVI**

That is a deliberately mixed English/Roman-numeral formulation meaning **4 July 1776**, the date adopted for the United States Declaration of Independence. It is an inscription incorporated into the statue itself, not the later Lazarus plaque.

Published official weight totals vary because different sources define the “statue,” framework, and foundations differently. The NPS statistical page gives approximately:

- copper: **176,000 lb** (about 79,800 kg);
- framework: **440,000 lb** (about 199,600 kg);
- concrete foundation: **54,000,000 lb** (about 24.5 million kg).

A newer NPS facts page estimates the statue at 560,000 lb and its copper at 179,200 lb. These should be treated as institutional estimates, not precision weighings of the assembled monument.

### Materials and construction

The visible figure is a thin, load-relieved **copper envelope**, originally about **3/32 inch or 2.4–2.5 mm thick**, formed by repoussé: copper sheets were hammered over full-sized molds and joined into panels.

The principal original supporting system designed under Gustave Eiffel’s direction consists of:

1. a central wrought-iron pylon or tower;
2. secondary framing;
3. numerous flat iron armature bars following the changing contours of the copper surface;
4. copper saddles riveted to the skin, originally isolating and connecting it to the armatures;
5. a system flexible enough to allow the skin to move under wind and thermal expansion without behaving as a rigid masonry shell.

The statue therefore is not solid copper, cast bronze, or a copper-clad stone figure. Its thin envelope and independently moving internal structure are central to its identity as an engineering work.

The internal design changed during manufacture. Eugène-Emmanuel Viollet-le-Duc, Bartholdi’s former teacher and first structural adviser, proposed a more massive arrangement involving internal masonry compartments and sand. He also promoted the repoussé method and armature concept. After his death in 1879, Eiffel and his engineer Maurice Koechlin developed the flexible iron-pylon solution.

The pedestal is a massive stone-faced masonry and concrete structure designed by American architect **Richard Morris Hunt**, with its engineering and construction overseen by **General Charles Pomeroy Stone**. It stands within **Fort Wood**, an early nineteenth-century eleven-pointed star fort. The resulting monumental object is thus composite in date and authorship: fort, pedestal, statue, internal stairs and elevators, lighting, later museum fixtures, and replacement torch.

### Surface and color

The original copper was brown-orange and metallic. Atmospheric exposure produced a layered green patina. This was not green paint. Conservation analysis has identified copper corrosion compounds including sulfates and carbonates. The mature patina now protects much of the underlying metal; the 1980s restoration did not “polish the statue back” to copper color.

### Orientation and movement

The statue faces southeast, toward the harbor entrance and arriving ships. The NPS reports wind movement of approximately three inches in the body and as much as six inches at the torch under specified high-wind conditions. Such motion is part of the intended structural behavior, not evidence that the copper shell is freestanding.

### Torch: original and replacement

The torch requires separate identity treatment:

1. **Original sculptural torch, 1876–1984:** The first major full-sized component completed. The arm and torch were shown at Philadelphia’s Centennial Exposition in 1876 and later in Madison Square, New York, before being incorporated into the completed statue. The torch was repeatedly altered, most consequentially in 1916, when Gutzon Borglum redesigned it with internal lighting and hundreds of colored glass panes. Those openings promoted leakage and corrosion. The object that came down in 1984 was therefore original fabric heavily modified in 1916 and afterward.

2. **Present torch, installed 1985–1986:** A copper reconstruction intended to recover Bartholdi’s external form, with a flame covered in 24-karat gold leaf and illuminated externally. It is not the physical 1876 torch.

3. **Surviving original torch:** Preserved rather than destroyed. It was displayed in the pedestal and moved in 2018 into the purpose-built Statue of Liberty Museum, which opened in 2019. It remains a detached fragment of the original monument.

### Other replaced or altered fabric

The monument retains most of its original copper envelope and Eiffel pylon, but continuity is material rather than absolute. Significant replacements include:

- almost all original contour-following iron armature bars, replaced by shaped stainless-steel bars in 1984–1986;
- the original torch, removed and preserved;
- interior stairs and visitor systems;
- elevators, lighting, ventilation, and life-safety equipment;
- localized copper patches and replacement pieces;
- fasteners, insulation, coatings, windows, and service installations.

The present monument is therefore the original statue under ordinary conservation standards—continuous site, envelope, core conception, substantial original material, and documented custody—but not an untouched 1886 assemblage.

### The Lazarus plaque

“The New Colossus” is not inscribed on the statue’s tablet. Emma Lazarus wrote the sonnet in 1883 for the Art Loan Fund Exhibition supporting the pedestal. A bronze plaque bearing the poem was installed inside the pedestal in 1903 following efforts by Lazarus’s friend Georgina Schuyler. Its physical addition helped shift the public interpretation of the statue toward immigration and refuge.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### The 1865 origin story: documented core and retrospective elaboration

**Institutional tradition:** The standard account begins at a gathering at Édouard René Lefèbvre de Laboulaye’s home at Glatigny in 1865. Laboulaye—jurist, admirer of American constitutionalism, opponent of slavery, and critic of France’s Second Empire—is said to have proposed that if a monument to American independence were built, it should be a joint work of the two nations.

**Caution:** Later retellings converted this remark into a settled 1865 commission and sometimes placed Bartholdi at the dinner. The surviving evidence does not establish that a complete statue project, its eventual form, or an organized gift was commissioned that night. The dinner functions partly as a foundation legend: it telescopes several years of political discussion, artistic development, travel, and fundraising into one scene.

The documented political setting is nevertheless important. The American Civil War had ended, slavery had been constitutionally abolished, and Laboulaye’s circle could invoke the American republic and Union victory as indirect arguments for republican government in France. The monument’s early meanings included emancipation, republican liberty, Franco-American friendship, and the centennial of independence.

### Egyptian and Suez antecedents

During and after travel in Egypt, Bartholdi conceived colossal harbor monuments. His unrealized proposal commonly called **_L’Égypte apportant la lumière à l’Asie_**—“Egypt Bringing Light to Asia”—imagined a robed female figure or fellah holding a light at the entrance to the Suez Canal.

**Documented:** Models, drawings, and archival references establish that an Egyptian lighthouse-monument project existed.

**Scholarly inference:** The Suez project contributed to Bartholdi’s visual vocabulary: colossal scale, robed female body, raised light, and placement at a maritime gateway.

**Unsupported leap:** The New York statue is not simply a completed Suez statue transferred to another patron. Its title, republican iconography, tablet, broken chains, diadem, setting, political program, and extensive redesign belong to the American project.

### Project formation

Bartholdi traveled to the United States in 1871, promoted the monument, and identified Bedloe’s Island as his preferred site because ships entering New York Harbor would pass it. The project became organized through the **Union Franco-Américaine**, formed in the 1870s under Laboulaye’s leadership and later associated prominently with Ferdinand de Lesseps.

The funding compact was effectively divided:

- French subscribers would finance and fabricate the statue;
- Americans would provide its site and finance and build its pedestal.

This was a public-subscription project rather than a purchase by the French state. Calling it “a gift from France” is broadly correct but can misleadingly imply that the French government alone ordered and paid for it. Money came through banquets, public appeals, entertainments, sale of models and images, and a government-authorized lottery. Contributions were collected from municipalities, civic groups, businesses, schoolchildren, and individuals.

### Design development and patent evidence

Bartholdi developed models at successively larger scales. Full-sized sections were translated from enlarged models into wooden forms on which artisans hammered copper sheets.

The 1879 U.S. design patent is decisive primary evidence that the recognizable iconography existed by then. It specifies the female figure, torch, tablet dated 4 July 1776, classical drapery, severe face, and seven-rayed halo. It also shows that Bartholdi sought legal control over reproductions in multiple media. He used patented busts, statuettes, photographs, and exhibition pieces as fundraising and publicity instruments.

### Manufacture in Paris

Construction occurred from approximately 1875 to 1884 at the Paris metalworking establishment usually identified as **Gaget, Gauthier et Cie**, later **Monduit et Béchet—Gaget, Gauthier successeurs**, at **25 rue de Chazelles**.

The production sequence included:

- sculpting small studies;
- enlarging the form;
- making sectional plaster models;
- constructing full-size wooden templates;
- hammering copper sheets over the molds;
- cutting, joining, and riveting the skin;
- designing and installing the internal ironwork;
- assembling the complete figure in Paris for verification.

Important intermediate movements were:

- **1876:** completed right arm and torch exhibited at the Centennial Exposition in Philadelphia;
- **after Philadelphia:** arm and torch displayed in Madison Square, New York, as a fundraiser;
- **1878:** head and shoulders exhibited at the Paris Universal Exposition;
- **1881–1884:** progressive full assembly in Paris near Parc Monceau, where it became known informally as the “Lady of the Park.”

### Copper provenance

No surviving purchase document yet establishes a complete shipment-level chain from mine to workshop.

**Scientific finding and documentary inference:** Trace-element comparison of copper samples from the statue and European sources, together with nineteenth-century production capacity and trade evidence, identified the **Visnes mine on Karmøy, Norway**, as the most likely source.

This is strong evidence, but it should not be inflated into an extant invoice proving that every sheet came from Visnes. The Smithsonian Museum Conservation Institute describes Visnes as the most likely massive single source on the available scientific and documentary evidence.

### American acceptance and site

A joint resolution passed Congress and was signed by President Ulysses S. Grant on **3 March 1877**. It accepted the French gift, authorized the president to select Governors or Bedloe’s Island, and directed provision for its care as both a beacon and a monument of art and Franco-American goodwill. President Rutherford B. Hayes subsequently designated Bedloe’s Island.

The site was federal military property containing Fort Wood. New York had ceded jurisdiction over the island to the United States in 1800 while reserving limited process-serving rights. The statue did not pass through a sequence of private owners: the critical transfer was from the French organizing body, acting for its subscribers, to the United States.

### Pedestal

Richard Morris Hunt designed the pedestal. Charles Pomeroy Stone was engineer in charge. American fundraising repeatedly stalled. Benefits, exhibitions, private gifts, state or municipal proposals, and sales failed to close the deficit quickly.

In March 1885, publisher **Joseph Pulitzer** used the *New York World* to solicit small donations and promised publication of contributors’ names. The campaign raised more than $100,000 in roughly five months from about 120,000 donors, many contributing less than one dollar. The episode is an early, well-documented form of mass-media crowdfunding, although it was also commercially useful to Pulitzer’s newspaper.

The pedestal was completed in 1886.

### Formal transfer and Atlantic shipment

On **4 July 1884**, the completed statue was ceremonially presented in Paris to U.S. minister **Levi P. Morton**. Contemporary diplomatic correspondence records French governmental participation, Bartholdi, Ferdinand de Lesseps, Lafayette and Rochambeau descendants, and the French navy’s offer to transport it.

The statue was then:

1. disassembled;
2. indexed and packed into **214 crates**;
3. transported aboard the French naval vessel **_Isère_**;
4. received in New York Harbor on **17 June 1885**;
5. held while the pedestal was completed;
6. reassembled on Bedloe’s Island in 1886.

The iron pylon was erected first. Copper sections were then hoisted by derricks and attached without a conventional full external scaffold of the kind later used in restoration.

### Dedication

The monument was dedicated on **28 October 1886** in heavy rain and fog. President Grover Cleveland accepted it on behalf of the United States. A military and naval salute, harbor flotilla, parade, speeches, and unveiling accompanied the ceremony. Contemporary photographs and the official inauguration program document the event.

The statue’s face was covered by a French tricolor. Bartholdi released it prematurely or in response to an agreed signal obscured by ceremony; gunfire, whistles, and cheers followed.

Women were largely excluded from the official island ceremony even though the allegorical figure was female. The New York City Woman Suffrage Association chartered a boat and protested from the harbor, describing the monument as a mockery while women lacked political liberty. This was engagement with the object, not custody of it.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Before 1884: project and workshop custody

**c. 1865–1874:** The object existed first as an idea and developing design associated with Laboulaye and Bartholdi. There was not yet a transferable monumental object.

**1874–1875 onward:** The Union Franco-Américaine organized French fundraising and acted as the project’s institutional sponsor.

**1875–1884:** Physical components were fabricated and held in the Paris workshop at 25 rue de Chazelles under Bartholdi’s artistic direction and the contractors’ industrial custody.

**1876:** The arm and torch crossed temporarily to the United States for the Philadelphia Centennial Exposition and later Madison Square. They were fundraising display objects but remained components of the French project.

**1878:** The head and shoulders were displayed at the Paris Universal Exposition.

**1881–1884:** The complete statue stood assembled in Paris. This was the first full physical manifestation of substantially the same copper monument now in New York.

### Transfer to the United States

**4 July 1884:** Ceremonial delivery in Paris to Levi P. Morton represented transfer to the United States. The French state provided transport, but the gift had been financed principally through public subscription.

**1885:** Disassembly did not extinguish the statue’s identity; it became a documented set of numbered parts in 214 crates aboard *Isère*.

**17 June 1885:** Arrival in New York Harbor. The crates passed into American custody pending erection.

**1886:** The United States, acting through the American Committee and federal military site administration, installed the statue on its pedestal within Fort Wood.

**28 October 1886:** Grover Cleveland formally accepted the erected monument.

### Lighthouse Board and War Department

The statue initially served as a lighthouse under the **United States Lighthouse Board**. Its light proved less effective as a navigational beacon than hoped, in part because the illumination was insufficient and the torch glazing and placement were not optimized for lighthouse service.

In **1901**, responsibility passed from the Lighthouse Board to the **War Department**, which already controlled the surrounding military reservation. This was an administrative transfer within the United States government, not a change of sovereign ownership.

### Physical additions and modifications, 1886–1933

- **1903:** The Lazarus plaque was attached inside the pedestal.
- **1907–1909:** An Otis elevator was installed, altering visitor circulation.
- **1916:** The Black Tom explosion damaged the statue and island structures. Public access to the torch ceased and has never resumed.
- **1916:** Gutzon Borglum modified the torch, cutting openings and installing colored glass panes for internal illumination. Floodlighting of the exterior was also developed. This was a major intervention into original fabric.
- **1918:** Repairs were made to copper skin.
- **1931:** Modernized elevators, electrical equipment, controls, and floodlighting were installed.

### Black Tom, 30 July 1916

German agents or agents acting in Germany’s interest sabotaged the Black Tom munitions terminal in Jersey City. Exploding ammunition sent shock waves and fragments across the harbor. The statue’s torch-bearing side sustained damage; windows and nearby installations were affected.

This was neither an attempt to steal nor deliberately target the statue. It was sabotage against wartime munitions destined for the Allied powers while the United States was formally neutral. The monument was collateral damage. The closure of the arm and torch became permanent, though subsequent safety concerns—not unrepaired 1916 shrapnel alone—also explain why visitors cannot enter it today.

### National-monument status and NPS custody

On **15 October 1924**, President Calvin Coolidge’s Proclamation 1713 declared the Statue of Liberty and the Fort Wood area a national monument under the Antiquities Act. The proclamation described the foundations as an eleven-pointed star and reserved about two and one-half acres.

In **1933**, an executive reorganization transferred national monuments administered by the War Department to the **National Park Service, Department of the Interior**. This is the beginning of present institutional custody.

In **1937**, the War Department relinquished the remainder of Bedloe’s Island, allowing the NPS to manage the entire island and develop a unified plan.

### 1930s–1940s rehabilitation

Water penetration and galvanic interaction between copper and iron had produced rusting, expansion, displaced rivets, and local skin damage. Federal public-works programs supported extensive improvements:

- repair or replacement of some iron armatures;
- waterproofing and drainage work;
- interior cleaning and coatings;
- pedestal improvements;
- island clearance and landscaping;
- seawalls, paths, docks, and visitor facilities.

These interventions were substantial but did not solve all concealed corrosion.

### Renaming and expansion of the monument

**1956:** Congress renamed Bedloe’s Island **Liberty Island**. This renamed the place, not the statue.

**1965:** President Lyndon B. Johnson added Ellis Island to Statue of Liberty National Monument. Johnson signed the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 at Liberty Island, materially reinforcing the monument’s immigration association.

### Protests, occupations, and attempted symbolic repossession

The statue has repeatedly been temporarily seized or draped by political protesters. These actions did not transfer legal custody but belong to the object’s use-history.

- **1886:** Suffragists protested from a chartered vessel during the dedication.
- **1970:** Women’s liberation activists hung a banner from the crown.
- **December 1971:** Vietnam Veterans Against the War occupied the statue for approximately two days.
- **June 1976:** Protesters occupied it while demanding justice and political change; after refusing a court order to leave, they were arrested on 7 June.
- **15 February 1977 and later:** Iranian dissidents mounted demonstrations against repression under the Shah.
- **25 October 1977:** Twenty-nine Puerto Rican nationalists took over the statue for about nine hours, displayed a Puerto Rican flag, and demanded independence, an end to discrimination, and release of imprisoned nationalists. Authorities arrested them.
- **1982:** Anti-intervention protesters opposing U.S. military action in Grenada chained themselves within or near the crown structure.
- **3 June 1980:** A time-delayed bomb exploded in the pedestal. The FBI suspected Croatian nationalists seeking independence from Yugoslavia. No one was injured; security increased.

In each instance, activists attempted to appropriate the monument’s symbolic authority. The events were uses “in its name,” not ownership changes.

### Centennial restoration, 1981–1986

French engineers and conservators began detailed investigation in 1981. In 1982 President Ronald Reagan appointed Chrysler chairman **Lee Iacocca** to lead a private fundraising initiative. The Statue of Liberty–Ellis Island Foundation worked with the NPS; the combined statue/Ellis Island campaign became a major public-private preservation effort.

A French-American technical committee issued an architectural and engineering assessment in 1983. Restoration began in 1984.

Documented work included:

- complete exterior scaffolding;
- close inspection and mapping of copper;
- removal of accumulated interior coatings;
- local repair of holes, cracks, and distorted copper;
- replacement of almost all deteriorated iron armature bars with Type 316L stainless steel;
- use of insulating interfaces designed to reduce galvanic interaction;
- retention and repair of the main Eiffel pylon;
- structural reinforcement around the raised arm and shoulder;
- replacement of internal stairs and visitor infrastructure;
- elevator, ventilation, electrical, and climate-control upgrades;
- removal of the Borglum-modified original torch;
- installation of a new copper-and-gold-leaf torch based on Bartholdi’s external design.

The original torch was not discarded. Its survival allows viewers to inspect an authentic 1876 component bearing evidence of the 1916 alteration.

The restored statue reopened on **5 July 1986**, after Liberty Weekend ceremonies marking the centennial.

### 2001–present closures and reopenings

- **11 September 2001:** The islands closed after the attacks on New York and Washington.
- **December 2001:** Liberty Island reopened, but the monument interior remained closed.
- **3 August 2004:** The pedestal reopened after security and safety work.
- **4 July 2009:** The crown reopened under restricted, reservation-only access.
- **29 October 2011:** The interior closed for a year-long life-safety renovation.
- **28 October 2012:** Interior reopened.
- **29 October 2012:** Hurricane Sandy flooded roughly 75 percent of Liberty Island and devastated utilities, docks, paving, and service infrastructure. The elevated statue and pedestal escaped comparable structural destruction.
- **4 July 2013:** Statue and island reopened while recovery continued.
- **2018:** Original torch relocated into the new museum.
- **May 2019:** Statue of Liberty Museum opened.
- **2020:** Pandemic-related public-health closures interrupted access; subsequent reopening was phased.
- **Present:** NPS retains custody; public access continues under security screening and reservation controls.

### Chain integrity

There is no documented disappearance, clandestine substitution, private sale, wartime looting, or ownership gap in the colossal statue’s history. Its chain is unusually strong:

**Paris workshop → Union Franco-Américaine/French project custody → ceremonial delivery to U.S. representative → French naval transport → United States at Bedloe’s Island → Lighthouse Board → War Department → National Park Service.**

The main identity complication is not missing custody but conservation replacement: particularly the torch, armatures, and service systems.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is the present statue the 1886 object?

**Yes, under ordinary art-historical and conservation definitions, with qualifications.** The site, most copper skin, Eiffel pylon, sculptural form, pedestal, and documented custody are continuous. The statue was dismantled only once as part of its planned transfer from Paris and was reconstructed from the indexed original components.

It is not materially untouched. Replacement of nearly all secondary iron armatures and the entire exposed torch means that some visually and structurally important components date from the 1980s. Conservation records openly document these substitutions; there is no institutional concealment.

### The two torches

The phrase “the Statue of Liberty’s torch” can refer to two different objects:

- the **original 1876 copper torch**, later cut and glazed by Borglum, now indoors in the museum;
- the **1986 replacement**, now attached to the statue.

Any claim that visitors see the untouched original torch atop the monument is false. Any claim that the original was destroyed is also false.

### Copper source

The Visnes identification rests on trace-element analysis plus historical-production evidence. The Smithsonian account properly frames Visnes as the most likely source. In the absence of definitive workshop invoices, “proved beyond all possibility” would exceed the evidence.

### Face-model claims

Several persons have been proposed as the model for the face:

- Bartholdi’s mother, Charlotte;
- Isabella Eugénie Boyer, widow of sewing-machine magnate Isaac Singer;
- Sarah Coblenzer;
- Bartholdi’s brother or a male model;
- an anonymous Egyptian woman;
- a generalized classical synthesis.

No surviving statement by Bartholdi securely identifies a sitter. Musée Bartholdi personnel have treated the familiar mother and Boyer identifications as unsupported speculation. Similarity arguments based on photographs are subjective and cannot establish a sculptural model. The responsible conclusion is **unresolved; no named model is documented**.

### “Originally a Black woman”

A modern claim holds that the statue originally represented a Black woman or freed slave and was changed because white Americans objected.

The NPS commissioned a research review of this claim. Its findings distinguish several propositions:

- **Supported context:** Laboulaye was an antislavery advocate; the project emerged after Union victory and emancipation; chains and emancipation symbolism belong to its early conceptual field.
- **Supported visual ancestry:** Bartholdi’s Egyptian monumental studies likely contributed to the later design.
- **Not supported:** no evidence shows that American patrons or the U.S. government rejected a Black portrait and forced Bartholdi to whiten it.
- **Important design change:** an early conception involving broken chains in a hand gave way to the tablet, while broken shackle and chain remained at the feet.

Thus abolition is not an invented association, but the internet narrative of an explicitly Black portrait censored into a white one is not documented.

### “A Muslim woman intended for Suez”

This claim exaggerates the real Egyptian antecedent. Bartholdi proposed a robed Egyptian/fellah lighthouse figure for Suez; the New York monument developed later through different patrons, inscriptions, models, and political purposes. No evidence establishes that the completed New York copper skin had first been manufactured for Suez.

### Seven rays

The patent calls the rays a halo. The popular “seven continents and seven seas” explanation is widespread but lacks equivalent contemporary documentation. It should be labeled a later institutional or popular interpretation.

### Was it a French government gift?

The shorthand is incomplete. The project was organized and funded predominantly through French civic subscription. French public authorities authorized fundraising mechanisms and the French navy carried it. The formal object was presented to the United States in a ceremony involving French officials. It was a gift from French people and institutions, supported but not simply purchased by the French government.

### “Gift for immigration”

The statue was not commissioned primarily as a memorial welcoming immigrants. The dominant initial program concerned republican liberty, American independence, abolition’s aftermath, Franco-American friendship, and enlightenment.

The immigrant meaning developed through:

- immigrants’ actual harbor experience;
- the opening of Ellis Island in 1892;
- installation of the Lazarus plaque in 1903;
- Americanization campaigns;
- World War I propaganda;
- 1930s–1950s reinterpretation;
- association of Ellis Island with the same national monument.

The immigrant symbolism is historically real and powerful, but largely a reception history rather than the sole original intention.

### Legal location: New York or New Jersey?

Liberty Island lies geographically on the New Jersey side of the harbor waters but is federal property within the territorial jurisdiction of New York under the interstate arrangements governing the original islands. New Jersey litigants challenged New York’s jurisdiction in the 1980s and failed. The Supreme Court’s 1998 division of original and filled portions of Ellis Island did not transfer Liberty Island to New Jersey.

Therefore:

- owner/controller: **United States of America, administered by the NPS**;
- state territorial jurisdiction: **New York**;
- geographic proximity: closer to New Jersey;
- part of the shared New York–New Jersey harbor landscape.

### Patina myths

The green surface is authentic weathered copper, not the statue’s original color and not green paint. Removing it wholesale would eliminate historically developed, protective material and damage the thin copper. Conservation has therefore treated the patina as both evidence and protection.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

| Person, institution, or being | Connection | Date/evidence |
|---|---|---|

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Ceremonial function

The statue has no regular liturgy and is not a relic in ritual worship. Its ritual life is civic:

- cornerstone and fundraising ceremonies;
- French presentation on 4 July 1884;
- harbor reception in 1885;
- dedication on 28 October 1886;
- anniversary ceremonies in 1936, 1986, and 2011;
- presidential speeches and citizenship ceremonies;
- public mourning and patriotic observance;
- illumination during national commemorations.

The annual anniversary on 28 October and naturalization ceremonies enact the monument as a civic shrine.

### Lighthouse and beacon

Congress’s 1877 acceptance contemplated its maintenance as a beacon. The Lighthouse Board operated it after dedication. The light was symbolically potent but technically disappointing as a navigational aid. Its lighthouse function ended, but the torch remained the defining emblem of enlightenment.

### Iconographic functions

- **Torch:** enlightenment, reason, illumination, and guidance.
- **Tablet:** law and the American Declaration of Independence.
- **Broken shackle and chain:** liberation from tyranny and servitude; in the post–Civil War context, capable of abolitionist reference.
- **Forward step:** emergence from bondage and motion toward liberty.
- **Crown/halo:** radiant liberty; seven rays universalize the image in later interpretation.
- **Classical robes:** transhistorical civic authority rather than contemporary fashion.
- **Harbor placement:** arrival, greeting, boundary crossing, and maritime visibility.

### Political legitimation

The statue legitimized several overlapping claims:

- French republicans used admiration for the American republic to argue indirectly against authoritarian government at home.
- American officials used it to present the United States as heir to universal liberty.
- Immigrant and refugee narratives recast it as a promise of asylum.
- War-bond and recruiting propaganda treated the crowned figure as the nation itself.
- Presidents used Liberty Island as a setting for immigration and citizenship policy.
- Protesters used occupation, banners, and flags to argue that American practice contradicted the monument’s promises.

Possession does not confer constitutional authority, but physical command of the statue’s visibility confers exceptional symbolic reach.

### Access rules and taboo-like restrictions

The statue is a regulated federal monument rather than a sacred object with priestly access. Nevertheless, access is highly stratified:

- island and museum: ordinary ticketed public access;
- pedestal: timed reservation;
- crown: limited advance reservation and physically demanding stair ascent;
- torch and raised arm: closed to the public since 1916;
- structural and conservation spaces: staff or authorized specialists only;
- touching the original torch and historic fabric: prohibited;
- flags, banners, occupations, climbing, and unauthorized attachment: subject to arrest and removal.

These rules arise from safety, conservation, and security, but they create a modern custodial hierarchy analogous to restricted access around treasured regalia.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The single dinner where the statue was “born”

**Tier:** National foundation tradition with a plausible documentary core and retrospective compression.

The fullest popular version says Laboulaye announced the entire gift at an 1865 dinner, Bartholdi immediately accepted, and the monument proceeded directly from that inspiration. The evidence supports Laboulaye’s commemorative suggestion and political milieu, but not a complete commission at that moment. Later biographies and institutions stabilized the episode because it supplied a memorable origin.

### Bartholdi’s sudden harbor vision

**Tier:** Later romantic elaboration.

Some accounts say Bartholdi sailed into New York Harbor in 1870 or 1871, suddenly envisioned Liberty on Bedloe’s Island, and sketched it immediately. His 1871 journey and site selection are documented. The instantaneous revelation belongs to biographical legend unless supported by a specific contemporary diary or letter.

### The rejected Suez statue

**Tier:** Modern oversimplification built on a documented precursor.

The story claims Egypt rejected the exact statue, after which Bartholdi sold or donated it to America. What survives instead is evidence for an unrealized Egyptian harbor-lighthouse concept that informed later work. The New York statue was separately developed, financed, manufactured, and inscribed.

### The Black or Arab original model

**Tier:** Modern disputed claim.

The Egyptian antecedent and emancipation politics are real; a racially specific finished portrait censored at American insistence is not documented. The claim gained currency through late twentieth-century and internet circulation. The NPS research project found no evidence for a forced transformation from a Black woman to a white one.

### The sculptor’s mother

**Tier:** Popular tradition, unverified.

Charlotte Bartholdi is often identified as the face. The resemblance is asserted retrospectively, and no decisive studio record confirms it. The Boyer and other named-sitter claims have the same problem.

### Seven continents and seven seas

**Tier:** Later interpretive tradition.

The statue unquestionably has seven rays. Bartholdi’s patent calls them a halo. Their equation with seven continents and seven seas is not securely attested in the design documentation.

### “Give me your tired…” as the original commission

**Tier:** Modern historical conflation.

Lazarus wrote the poem in 1883, after the design and much of the construction existed. The plaque was not installed until 1903. The poem profoundly changed reception but did not dictate Bartholdi’s original iconography.

### Green by accident, neglect, or paint

**Tier:** Modern misconception.

The green layer is natural corrosion patina. It was neither the original appearance nor evidence that custodians simply failed to clean the statue. Conservation science regards stable patina as protective historic material.

### Secret substitution or relocated-island stories

**Tier:** Modern conspiracy/Mandela-effect material.

Claims that the statue once stood on Ellis Island, arrived in the 1920s, had a differently placed torch, or was secretly replaced lack documentary support. Photographs, plans, the 1879 patent, shipping records, and continuous federal administration establish its form and Liberty Island location from 1886 onward. Confusion is encouraged by the fact that Ellis Island and Liberty Island now form one national-monument unit.

### “France can take it back”

**Tier:** Political rhetoric, legally baseless as restitution.

Modern commentators periodically demand that the statue be “returned” to France when U.S. policy is judged inconsistent with its symbolism. No serious ownership claim accompanies such rhetoric. The gift was formally accepted and installed on federal property; France retains cultural association, not title or a contractual right of repossession.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Location and control

The attached statue stands at:

**Liberty Island, New York Harbor, New York, United States**  
**Statue of Liberty National Monument**  
Administered by the **National Park Service, U.S. Department of the Interior**.

The object is federal property. No current private-ownership or repatriation proceeding is documented.

### Heritage designations

- United States national monument: **1924**
- National Park Service custody: **1933**
- Liberty Island incorporated into the full managed landscape: **1937**
- UNESCO World Heritage List: **1984**, property number **307**

UNESCO recognizes the statue under cultural criteria for its artistic and engineering importance and for the universal political ideas associated with liberty, democracy, abolition, and international exchange.

### Condition

Official and post-restoration technical accounts indicate:

- the principal copper envelope remains substantially original and generally stable;
- the green patina is retained;
- corrosion-prone original secondary iron armatures have largely been replaced;
- the main Eiffel pylon remains;
- local copper repairs and monitoring continue;
- the gilded replacement torch is maintained;
- visitor infrastructure has been repeatedly upgraded for fire safety, evacuation, accessibility, and security;
- island infrastructure has undergone major post-Sandy reconstruction.

The statue’s extreme marine exposure, dissimilar metals, wind loading, water intrusion, and visitor volume require continual inspection. “Restored in 1986” does not mean conservation ended then.

### Public access

As of the latest NPS material available in 2026:

- Liberty Island and the museum are open subject to operating schedules and screening.
- The official landing ferry concession is Statue City Cruises.
- Pedestal and crown entry require designated tickets.
- Crown tickets must be reserved in advance.
- There are 162 steps from the top of the pedestal to the crown platform; the full ascent from ground level is substantially longer.
- The original torch is publicly visible inside the Statue of Liberty Museum.
- The attached torch and raised arm remain closed to the public.

### Cataloguing

The monument is managed as a federal cultural resource and UNESCO property rather than as a portable museum object with one conventional accession number. Relevant documentary identities include:

- UNESCO World Heritage property **307**;
- U.S. Design Patent **D11,023** for Bartholdi’s design;
- National Park Service unit **Statue of Liberty National Monument**;
- Historic American Buildings/Engineering documentation held through the Library of Congress;
- museum collections and archives maintained by NPS and the Statue of Liberty–Ellis Island Foundation.

The detached original torch is treated as museum collection material within the monument, although the public-facing museum page does not publish a conventional accession number.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Replicas and editions

The statue’s replication began before completion. Bartholdi patented busts and the complete design and authorized statuettes as fundraising products. Replicas therefore are part of the object’s manufacture history, not merely its later merchandising.

Important descendants include:

- Bartholdi’s reduced bronze **_Liberté_**, model dated 1876 and cast in 1889, Musée d’Orsay inventory **RF 3878**;
- the Île aux Cygnes statue in Paris, dedicated in 1889;
- Colmar’s modern monumental replica;
- numerous “Little Sisters of Liberty,” civic copies, exposition versions, commercial miniatures, advertising images, and theme-park replicas.

The Musée d’Orsay bronze acquired its own object biography: purchased by the French state in 1900, displayed at the Musée du Luxembourg, deposited in the Luxembourg Garden, assigned to the Musée d’Orsay, damaged by theft of its torch in 2011, and moved indoors in 2012. It is a replica/version, not a rival claimant to the New York original.

### Print, film, and visual shorthand

The silhouette has become a rapid visual sign for New York, the United States, immigration, freedom, invasion, apocalypse, and national vulnerability. Films frequently damage, bury, animate, or replace it because recognition is instantaneous. Influential examples include:

- *The Immigrant* and other early immigrant cinema;
- Alfred Hitchcock’s *Saboteur* (1942), using the statue as a climactic national setting;
- *Planet of the Apes* (1968), where the ruined statue reveals Earth’s fate;
- *Superman IV* (1987);
- *Ghostbusters II* (1989), animating the monument as a walking civic talisman;
- *X-Men* (2000);
- *The Day After Tomorrow* (2004);
- *Cloverfield* (2008).

These fictions do not form part of the statue’s physical provenance, but they materially shape popular expectations about its scale, color, torch, and political meaning.

### War and propaganda

The figure appeared on recruitment and war-bond posters during World War I and later conflicts. Such images sometimes transformed the statue into an armed or threatened national woman. Governmental use helped consolidate it as an embodiment of the United States rather than solely a Franco-American memorial.

### Immigration politics

The Lazarus lines are invoked in debates about refugees, asylum, border policy, and national identity. Competing speakers cite either:

- the universal welcome of “Mother of Exiles,” or
- the statue’s earlier republican-national origins to argue that immigration was not its original subject.

Historically, both points describe different layers of the monument’s career. The dispute becomes misleading only when one layer is used to erase the other.

### Race and incomplete liberty

African American writers and activists have invoked the statue’s promises against segregation, lynching, disenfranchisement, and racial violence. The NPS historical review notes that the statue’s image was also appropriated by nativists and white supremacists in the early twentieth century. Its symbolic record is therefore not a single progressive trajectory.

### Women’s rights

From the 1886 suffrage protest onward, the paradox of a female Liberty representing a polity that denied women equal political rights made the monument a recurring feminist site. Later banner actions and women’s-liberation demonstrations continued that critique.

### Puerto Rico and anti-colonial use

The 1977 occupation converted the monument into a visible argument about U.S. sovereignty over Puerto Rico. Draping the face with the Puerto Rican flag simultaneously obscured federal Liberty and asserted a competing national symbol. The object’s physical capture was brief; the photographic afterlife was durable.

### Tourism and commerce

The monument is a major destination whose economic life includes ferry concessions, timed access, souvenirs, photography, guided tours, fundraising, licensing, and replicas. Commercialization began during construction through models, patents, exhibition admissions, and printed reproductions. It is not a purely twentieth-century addition.

The official NPS warns that many private boat sellers advertise “Statue of Liberty” tickets without landing rights. Control of actual physical access remains with the federal concession system.

### Digital misinformation

Recent misinformation includes:

- AI-generated “photographs” said to depict the statue’s human model;
- false claims that the statue was designed as an explicitly Black portrait and altered under white pressure;
- claims that it stands or formerly stood on Ellis Island;
- digitally recolored images presented as authentic historic photographs;
- captions treating the 1986 replacement torch as original.

These claims can often be tested against patent drawings, construction photographs, museum records, and the securely dated movement of components.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Primary evidence

The object is exceptionally well documented through:

- Bartholdi’s surviving models, drawings, patents, photographs, and correspondence;
- Union Franco-Américaine records and publications;
- American Committee for the Statue of Liberty correspondence, 1875–1901;
- diplomatic correspondence surrounding the 1884 transfer;
- congressional acceptance and presidential proclamations;
- *New York World* fundraising pages and donor lists;
- official 1886 inauguration program;
- construction photography in Paris and New York;
- Lighthouse Board, War Department, and NPS administrative records;
- Historic American Buildings/Engineering surveys;
- 1981–1986 inspection, conservation, and engineering reports;
- corrosion-science publications and post-restoration monitoring;
- UNESCO nomination, ICOMOS evaluation, and periodic reports.

The strongest gaps concern informal early conversations, precise procurement of every material, and the identity of any facial model.

### Major interpretive shifts

#### 1. Founders’ memorial and republican alliance

Early official histories foregrounded Laboulaye, Bartholdi, American independence, Franco-American friendship, and republican political ideals.

#### 2. Engineering monument

Twentieth-century scholarship increasingly treated the object as a key episode in skeletal construction and curtain-wall development. Eiffel’s central pylon and the independently supported copper skin made it relevant to histories of skyscraper construction, bridge engineering, and metal conservation.

#### 3. Immigration icon

After the Lazarus plaque, Ellis Island’s operation, and twentieth-century mass politics, historians documented how “Mother of Exiles” overtook earlier readings. The NPS administrative and interpretive histories emphasize that this was a historical development, not the statue’s only meaning in 1886.

#### 4. Abolition and race

Late twentieth- and early twenty-first-century disputes asked whether official storytelling had minimized slavery’s abolition. The NPS “Black Statue of Liberty” report reached a nuanced result: abolition belongs to the project’s political environment and early symbolism, but the alleged censored Black portrait is unsubstantiated.

#### 5. Conservation and material authenticity

The 1980s restoration generated debates over whether to preserve, reinforce, or replace corroded armatures and whether to retain the Borglum torch. Investigators concluded that galvanic corrosion and water penetration required sweeping replacement of secondary ironwork. The original torch was preserved as a museum object while a reconstructed version restored Bartholdi’s exterior concept.

### Scientific study

Materials work addressed:

- copper thickness and remaining structural capacity;
- chemical composition and formation of patina;
- corrosion of iron armatures;
- galvanic effects between copper and iron;
- stress at the raised arm and shoulder;
- behavior under wind and thermal movement;
- selection of stainless steel, copper interfaces, and protective coatings;
- trace-element comparison for copper provenance.

Post-restoration inspections found the new armature system and preserved copper generally performing well in the first decade after intervention.

### Problems in official history

Institutional summaries sometimes repeat attractive but weakly documented claims, particularly:

- the fully formed 1865 dinner proposal;
- exact symbolic meaning of the seven rays;
- simplified descriptions of “France” and “America” each paying one half;
- single-number weights and costs despite differing scopes;
- categorical Visnes attribution without stating the inferential element.

These are best corrected through qualification rather than wholesale rejection.

### Discredited or unsupported theories still circulating

- The New York statue was already manufactured for Suez.
- Its face is securely known to be Isabella Boyer or Bartholdi’s mother.
- An explicitly Black statue was changed after American objections.
- The green color is paint or a failed restoration.
- The 1986 restoration replaced the whole statue.
- The original torch was destroyed.
- The statue is legally in New Jersey.
- The Lazarus poem was part of the original 1870s commission.
- The seven rays were demonstrably designed as the seven modern continents.
