# The preserved head of Thomas More: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary key

This dossier concerns the human cranial remains kept in a lead container behind an iron grille in the Roper family vault beneath the former St Nicholas Chantry, now usually called the Roper Chapel, at St Dunstan’s Church, Canterbury. The remains have long been identified as the severed head of Sir Thomas More (1478–1535), canonized by the Roman Catholic Church in 1935.

The identification is historically plausible but scientifically unverified. No published radiocarbon date, DNA profile, osteological sex or age assessment, stable-isotope analysis, facial comparison, or chemical identification of alleged embalming substances has established that the Canterbury remains are More’s. The strongest evidence is circumstantial: a relatively early family-linked account that Margaret Roper obtained and preserved her father’s head; a Canterbury container and skull fragments documented from at least 1715; their position in the Roper vault; and the absence of a persuasive rival claimant. Against this stand a contradictory diplomatic report from November 1535, the silence or ambiguity of the earliest More biographies, an undocumented interval of more than a century, uncertainty over Margaret Roper’s original burial, and disturbance of the vault.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** directly recorded in contemporary evidence, an official record, or physical inspection.
- **Scientific or medical observation:** a finding made by an identified examiner, with the limitations of the examination stated.
- **Plausible inference:** a reconstruction consistent with the evidence but not directly documented.
- **Institutional tradition:** the identification maintained by St Dunstan’s, ecclesiastical bodies, or other custodians.
- **Confessional or pious tradition:** a Catholic or Anglican devotional claim.
- **Later tradition:** a post-event narrative whose source is appreciably later than the alleged event.
- **Disputed or unresolved:** evidence supports more than one conclusion or is insufficient.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity and names

The object is variously called:

- **the head of Sir Thomas More**
- **Thomas More’s head**
- **the skull of Sir Thomas More**
- **the head relic of St Thomas More**
- **the relics of St Thomas More**, the plural now preferred by the Diocese of Canterbury because only separated fragments and powder apparently remain
- Latin: **caput Thomae Mori** or **caput divi/sancti Thomae Mori**, “the head of Thomas More” or “the head of Saint Thomas More”
- German: **das Haupt des Thomas Morus**
- Spanish: **la cabeza** or **el cráneo de santo Tomás Moro**
- French: **la tête de saint Thomas More**
- Italian ecclesiastical usage: **il capo** or **il cranio di san Tommaso Moro**

“More” was Latinized by Renaissance writers as *Morus*. “Relic” is not a neutral anatomical description: in Catholic usage it makes the remains a first-class relic, part of a saint’s body. The remains existed before More’s beatification in 1886 and canonization in 1935; their status changed from the remains of an executed person, to a family-preserved memorial of an alleged martyr, to relics of a canonized saint.

### The surviving material

The most detailed published description derives from the July 1978 opening of the Roper vault. Dr P. H. Garrard was permitted only an external visual examination. He did not remove or handle the remains, and saw them from roughly one foot away through the iron grille and an opening in the lead container.

He identified:

1. a fragment from the cranial vault, approximately one inch across;
2. the hard palate;
3. an adjoining but separated portion of the maxilla, with one empty tooth socket;
4. what appeared to be thin bony structures from the posterior nasal cavity;
5. numerous dark-brown, crumbling pieces;
6. powdery and granular material covering the bottom of the container.

Some pieces bore a white deposit Garrard provisionally interpreted as a lead salt. The surviving report does not describe a mandible, intact teeth, skin, brain tissue, recognizable hair, or a complete cranium. The description therefore does not support the frequent modern shorthand that an intact “skull” lies beneath the chapel. What survives is better termed **fragmentary human cranial material and associated residue**.

Garrard reportedly thought the advanced deterioration was probably caused by exposure to air, whereas other bones in the vault survived better under earth. This was a visual medical opinion, not a laboratory taphonomic study. The material may also have been affected by moisture, salts from the lead container, earlier handling, disturbance, and changes in the vault environment.

### Container and installation

The cranial material lies in a lead container set within a wall niche and protected by an iron grille. Descriptions vary:

- the 1837 account called it a lead box “something of the shape of a bee-hive,” open at the front;
- the 1978 report described a container about one foot cubical, with a cut opening allowing a partial view;
- nineteenth- and twentieth-century summaries often call it a “leaden box,” “casket,” or “case.”

These terms may refer to the same irregularly shaped container as seen from different angles. No published metallurgical examination, precise measured drawing, maker’s mark, inscription, heraldic device, or date for the container has been found. The white deposits on some bones may reflect interaction with lead, but this has not been chemically tested.

The niche is in the north wall of the vault according to the 1978 report. The 1837 vicar described the relevant wall as being on the south side, illustrating either imprecise orientation, alteration, or inconsistent reporting. The grille and niche are probably later than More’s death. Archaeological interpretation suggests that the niche was made when the vault was enlarged late in the sixteenth century, possibly around the burial of Thomas Roper in 1597/98.

### Alteration and losses

If the object is More’s head, its material history includes:

- decapitation on 6 July 1535;
- probable boiling or parboiling for public display;
- exposure on a spike in the open air;
- alleged preservation with spices by Margaret Roper;
- enclosure in lead;
- one or more transfers or reinterments within the Roper burial complex;
- exposure through an open front or hole in the container;
- progressive fragmentation;
- possible vandalism or illicit handling between nineteenth-century openings and 1978;
- continuing decay into fragments and powder.

The 1978 investigators found the vault more than half full of rubble and disturbed. Their report indicated that the human remains had suffered considerably. There is no published inventory demonstrating that all fragments presently in the lead container were always together or derived from one individual.

### Inscriptions and catalogue status

No inscription on the lead container has been reported. A twentieth-century ledger slab in the chapel marks the traditional burial place. Historic England describes a 1932 slab commemorating the burial of More’s head in the Roper vault.

The remains have no known museum accession number. They form part of a burial in consecrated ground rather than a museum collection. The church, vault, memorials, and chapel fall within the Grade I-listed Church of St Dunstan Without the West Gate, National Heritage List for England entry **1241793**.

## 2. Origin, Formation, and Earliest Attestation

### The physical object’s origin

Unlike a manufactured relic, the object originated as part of Thomas More’s body. More was beheaded on Tower Hill on 6 July 1535 after conviction for treason arising from his denial of Henry VIII’s royal supremacy over the English Church. Contemporary and near-contemporary sources securely establish the execution and the public display of his head.

The head was placed on London Bridge, the customary display place for the heads of some persons executed for treason. Sources differ over whether it was displayed for approximately fourteen days, almost a month, or several months. The better-supported later More tradition says nearly a month.

The head was probably boiled or parboiled before display. Such treatment retarded decomposition and made a severed head suitable for prolonged exposure. “Parboiled” is repeatedly reported in later accounts, but no surviving execution or bridge account specifying the preparation process has been located.

### Earliest evidence for the displayed head

The public display belongs to the secure historical core. The earliest evidence does not, however, provide an uncontested bridge from London Bridge to Canterbury.

A November 1535 item calendared in *Letters and Papers, Foreign and Domestic, Henry VIII*, volume IX, reports that More’s head had become black and had been thrown into the river. This is close in time to the execution and must be taken seriously. It could be accurate; it could repeat a rumor; it could describe an expected official disposal; or it could conceal or misunderstand a private recovery. The original document and its transmission require renewed archival examination before treating the calendared phrase as definitive.

Sir Richard Morison, writing against Johann Cochlaeus in 1536, was understood by later historians to say that More’s head remained on the bridge. That conflicts chronologically with both the November report and the later recovery story. Polemical rhetoric may not preserve an exact observation date.

### Earliest attestation of Margaret Roper’s recovery

William Roper’s *Life of Sir Thomas More*, written in or around 1557, is the earliest family biography. In its surviving form it does **not clearly narrate the recovery and preservation of the head**. That silence matters: William was Margaret’s husband and would have been the closest obvious witness. It is not conclusive, since the work is selective and circulated under dangerous confessional conditions.

The first detailed, securely datable published account is Thomas Stapleton’s Latin *Tres Thomae*, printed at Douai in 1588. Stapleton stated that:

- the head remained on London Bridge for nearly a month;
- when it was to be removed to make room for other heads, Margaret Roper watched for the opportunity;
- she bribed the official or executioner charged with removing it;
- she obtained the “sacred relic” before it could be thrown into the Thames;
- identification was secured both by continuous observation and personal signs;
- one tooth had been missing during More’s life;
- his face remained surprisingly recognizable;
- his nearly white hair or beard appeared reddish-brown;
- Margaret preserved the head with spices and treated it with great reverence;
- at the time Stapleton wrote, it remained in the custody of one of More’s relatives.

The crucial Latin conclusion is commonly rendered from *et hodie adhuc per aliquem suorum custoditur*: “and even today it is still kept by one of his family/relatives.”

Stapleton wrote fifty-three years after the execution. He was a Catholic exile composing a martyrological biography, but he was connected to More’s family and associates and could have drawn upon living witnesses and family information. His account is therefore **early family-confessional testimony**, not contemporary administrative proof.

### Growth of the origin narrative

Later writers added or sharpened details:

- that Margaret “bought” the head rather than bribed an official;
- that it was lowered directly to her boat from London Bridge;
- that she was arrested or summoned before the royal council for possessing it;
- that she declared it should not become “food for fishes”;
- that she kept it constantly near her;
- that it was buried in her arms.

Some may preserve lost family testimony; none should be retrojected automatically into 1535. The boat-and-basket scene became particularly influential through nineteenth-century art.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 6 July 1535: separation from the body

**Documented fact:** More was beheaded on Tower Hill. His torso was buried at the Chapel Royal of St Peter ad Vincula inside the Tower of London. Claims that Margaret later transferred the body to Chelsea are old but unproven; they concern the body rather than the head.

**Documented fact:** the head was placed on London Bridge.

At this point the head was in Crown or civic penal custody, administered through the officers responsible for executions and the bridge display.

### July–November 1535: the first break in the chain

Two incompatible documentary lines follow.

**Line A — near-contemporary adverse report:** a November 1535 report says the blackened head was thrown into the Thames.

**Line B — later family tradition:** Stapleton says Margaret Roper secured it before disposal, through payment to the responsible official, after carefully monitoring it.

There is no known receipt, council minute, bridge account, official release, or contemporary letter from Margaret documenting the transfer. This is the first and most important break in the physical chain of custody.

### 1535–1544: alleged custody of Margaret Roper

According to Stapleton, Margaret retained the head, preserved it with spices, and revered it. Later versions introduce a lead box during this period. No contemporary inventory of her possessions, household account, or letter has yet been identified as recording the relic.

Margaret died in 1544, not 1577 as several modern webpages mistakenly state. Her death created another uncertain transfer. Stapleton’s statement that the relic remained with “one of her relatives” in 1588 shows a family custody claim, but does not identify the keeper or location.

### Margaret Roper’s burial: Chelsea or Canterbury

Sources disagree over Margaret’s original burial.

- One tradition places her in the More tomb at Chelsea Old Church.
- The Canterbury tradition places her with William Roper in the family vault at St Dunstan’s.
- The surviving Latin memorial to William and Margaret at St Dunstan’s uses *Hic iacet … Gulielmus Roper et Margareta uxor*—“Here lie William Roper and Margaret his wife.” This materially strengthens the Canterbury case.
- Archaeological interpretation in 1978 assigned the western tomb-chest and an earlier phase of the vault to William and Margaret; the tomb could have existed by the 1540s.
- A compromise reconstruction has Margaret first buried elsewhere and later translated to Canterbury, but direct documentation for a translation has not been produced.

Later traditions state that the head was buried with Margaret, sometimes “in her arms.” Stapleton does not say this. If it occurred in 1544, the relic was subsequently removed from her grave and placed in the niche.

### 1544–1588: family custody, precise keeper unknown

Stapleton’s 1588 statement supplies a terminus by which a relative was said to possess the head. Candidates include Margaret’s descendants, especially her eldest daughter Elizabeth Roper Bray, but this remains inference. The annotation in a modern edition proposing Elizabeth Bray as the person who deposited it at St Dunstan’s is plausible, not documented fact.

William Roper died in January 1578 and was buried in the Roper chapel. His will referred to More’s intended burial at Chelsea but implied that More’s body lay elsewhere. It did not provide a clear published inventory of the head.

### Late sixteenth century: possible transfer to the niche

The Roper vault developed in phases:

1. an early-sixteenth-century burial arrangement associated with John Roper and the rebuilt chapel;
2. a probable William-and-Margaret phase by the 1540s;
3. a larger later-sixteenth-century vault, perhaps completed for Thomas Roper’s burial in 1597/98.

The 1978 archaeological reconstruction proposed that during the enlargement, More’s alleged head was removed from Margaret’s tomb or an earlier location and placed in the specially made wall niche. Thomas Roper, More’s great-grandson through Margaret, is a plausible agent. This is an architectural inference; no transfer record survives.

### Seventeenth century: literary claims but incomplete physical documentation

Anthony Wood’s *Athenae Oxonienses* reported that the head was first kept in a lead box and later placed with devotion in the Roper vault beside St Dunstan’s, where it remained. Wood wrote well after the events, but his account demonstrates that the Canterbury identification circulated before the eighteenth-century opening.

William Somner recorded the Roper monuments and family associations in Canterbury. Later summaries differ over whether he explicitly saw or merely reported the head.

### 1715: first securely reported sighting in the vault

Richard Rawlinson reportedly informed the antiquary Thomas Hearne that when the vault was opened in 1715 for a Roper-family interment, a box enclosed by an iron grille was seen. This is the earliest currently identified physical sighting of the distinctive niche installation.

The evidence survives through later quotation rather than a modern scientific record. Nevertheless, the combination of lead container, grille, niche, and Roper vault gives substantial continuity from 1715 onward.

The vault continued to receive Roper-related burials into the eighteenth century. Catherine Strickland, sometimes said to have been the last burial there in 1741, requires confirmation from the parish register and vault plan.

### Late eighteenth century

Edward Hasted’s history of Kent described a skull in a hollow in the wall beneath the Roper chapel, behind an iron grate and beside Margaret’s coffin. He repeated the story that Margaret privately bought and preserved her father’s head in a lead casket.

*The Kentish Traveller’s Companion* likewise described the skull in the niche and the tradition that Margaret wished to be buried holding it. By this period the identification was established local antiquarian knowledge, although still not supported by scientific examination.

### 1824 versus 1835–1837: conflicting reports

The nineteenth-century literature is internally confused.

The old *Dictionary of National Biography* and the *Catholic Encyclopedia* state that an ancient lead box was found and opened in June 1824 and contained a head presumed to be More’s, citing the *Gentleman’s Magazine* for 1824. This claim has been repeatedly copied.

A more detailed first-person account, published in the *Gentleman’s Magazine* in May 1837, says that the vault was **accidentally opened while the chancel was repaved in summer 1835**. The vicar descended specifically to determine whether More’s skull was present and saw it in its old niche, inside the beehive-shaped lead box and behind the grille. He did not describe discovering an unknown box or conducting an anatomical examination.

Some later sources say the remains were seen in 1837, confusing publication date with the 1835 inspection. Until the alleged 1824 article is checked directly, the secure sequence should be stated as:

- a reported sighting in 1715;
- a documented inspection in 1835;
- publication of that inspection in 1837.

The vicar already considered the church and remains endangered and appealed for restoration of the building. His description suggests the container was open to the vault atmosphere.

### Nineteenth-century disturbance

By 1978 investigators believed the vault had been disturbed and perhaps vandalized between the 1830s and later nineteenth-century church works, including the installation of an organ. Exact losses cannot be reconstructed. There is no surviving before-and-after inventory of the skull.

The disturbance is important for both authenticity and integrity. It leaves open whether bones were removed, shifted, mixed, or damaged. It does not by itself show that the cranial fragments were substituted.

### 1932: memorial slab and institutionalization of the identification

A ledger slab installed in 1932 marked the traditional burial place. The inscription or commemoration made the identification visible above ground even though the remains remained inaccessible below.

### 1935 onward: a saint’s relic and pilgrimage object

Pope Pius XI canonized Thomas More and John Fisher in 1935. The Canterbury remains thereby became, within Catholic understanding, relics of a saint. St Dunstan’s remained an Anglican parish church, creating an unusual custodial situation: a Church of England parish guards what Catholics understand as a first-class relic of a saint executed for rejecting royal ecclesiastical supremacy.

The site increasingly received Catholic and ecumenical pilgrims. Public access was to the chapel and memorial slab, not to the vault or bones.

### July 1978: archaeological opening

To mark the quincentenary of More’s birth, the Reverend Hugh O. Albin arranged for the vault to be opened and recorded. Canterbury Archaeological Trust personnel, including Paul Bennett, worked with church architects Peter Marsh and Henk Strik. Tim Tatton-Brown published the architectural and archaeological results.

The vault was more than half filled with rubble. After clearance, investigators surveyed the architecture, coffins, burials, tombs, and niche. The lead container and cranial fragments remained in situ.

Witnesses included:

- Hubertus Schulte Herbrüggen;
- J. B. Trapp of the Warburg Institute;
- Nicholas Barker of the British Library;
- Gordon Rupp;
- Patrick Collinson;
- A. F. Butcher;
- Canon Alfonso de Zulueta;
- Margaret Pawley;
- Anne Roper;
- Bishop Anthony Tremlett;
- Bishop Morris Maddocks;
- Archbishop George Otto Simms;
- the vicar, Hugh O. Albin;
- churchwardens Terence Wright and Harold Drage;
- archaeological and architectural personnel.

Dr Garrard’s visual assessment confirmed that visible pieces were human cranial bones. The inspection did **not** authenticate them as More’s. No sample was taken. The vault was resealed.

The principal resulting publications were Tatton-Brown’s “The Roper Chantry in St Dunstan’s Church, Canterbury,” *The Antiquaries Journal* 60 (1980), 227–246, and Hubertus Schulte Herbrüggen’s German monograph *Das Haupt des Thomas Morus in der St. Dunstan-Kirche zu Canterbury* (1982).

### 1978–2025: sealed custody

The remains stayed sealed below the chapel. Photographs and explanatory panels made their presence known, while the physical material remained inaccessible. Annual commemorations continued at the ledger stone.

A stained-glass window by Lawrence Lee, funded by supporters associated with St Thomas More churches in Kansas City and Vienna and by Hugh Albin, depicts More and his family. Sources disagree on its date: local heritage material assigns a More window to 1973, while another local account mentions a 1909 window involving More, St Dunstan, and Lanfranc. These may be two different windows and should not be conflated.

### 2023–present: conservation campaign

Campaigners, notably lawyer Steven Brizek, argued publicly that continued exposure and deterioration demanded conservation. In July 2025 the Parochial Church Council announced a consultation and its intention to seek a faculty for exhumation and conservation.

Churchwarden Sue Palmer said the material might require several years to dry and stabilize. Options under discussion included:

- conservation followed by return to the vault, possibly in a new reliquary;
- placement of a reliquary in an aboveground shrine;
- a carved stone pillar or comparable structure in the Roper Chapel.

The Archdeacon of Canterbury, Will Adam, stated that the legal decision rested with the Commissary General of Canterbury. Expert advice and fundraising were still being sought. Approximately £50,000 was reported in secondary coverage as an anticipated requirement, but no final project budget or approved conservation specification has been published.

As of the latest official information located, consultation and permission-seeking were underway; exhumation, testing, conservation, and reinstallation had not been reported as completed.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### The affirmative case

The strongest case that the Canterbury remains are More’s rests on cumulative provenance:

1. More’s decapitation and display are secure.
2. Stapleton, writing in 1588 with access to More-family networks, reported that Margaret recovered, preserved, and revered the head.
3. Stapleton said it remained with a relative in his own day.
4. The Roper chapel was the burial place of Margaret’s husband’s family and probably of Margaret herself.
5. The vault’s later-sixteenth-century enlargement offers a plausible occasion for formal placement in a wall niche.
6. A lead container behind an iron grille was reported there in 1715.
7. The same distinctive arrangement was recorded in 1835 and 1978.
8. The visible material was medically identified in 1978 as human cranial bone.
9. No rival skull has a comparable claim.

This makes the Canterbury identification considerably more than an unattached legend. It is a coherent family-and-place tradition with physical continuity securely traceable for approximately three centuries.

### The negative or skeptical case

The identification remains unproved because:

1. A near-contemporary November 1535 report says the head was blackened and thrown into the Thames.
2. William Roper’s early biography does not clearly tell the recovery story.
3. Stapleton wrote fifty-three years later in a Catholic martyrological setting.
4. There is no contemporary record of Margaret paying an official, being prosecuted for the head, preserving it, or bequeathing it.
5. Stapleton did not identify the relative or place of custody in 1588.
6. The first presently secure report of the distinctive Canterbury installation dates to 1715.
7. The vault was enlarged, reopened, filled with rubble, and disturbed.
8. The surviving bones were never independently dated or biologically profiled.
9. The missing-tooth story cannot now be tested confidently because the 1978 examiner saw only one empty socket, which may represent postmortem tooth loss rather than the antemortem absence Stapleton described.
10. No secure More-family comparative DNA reference has been established and used.

### The Chelsea rival

Chelsea Old Church contains More’s 1532 monument and an inscription prepared during his lifetime. An unsubstantiated tradition says his body, or Margaret and the head, was transferred there.

Chelsea Old Church itself now distinguishes monument from burial evidence: it calls the transfer of More’s body an unsubstantiated tradition and says the head is believed to rest at St Dunstan’s. Chelsea is consequently a rival **burial tradition**, not a rival physical skull claimant.

### The missing tooth

Stapleton treated a tooth missing during life as an identifying feature. Garrard saw part of the maxilla with an empty socket. This correspondence is suggestive but weak:

- the exact tooth is not stated in the accessible account;
- teeth commonly fall from old or poorly preserved skulls after death;
- Garrard could not assess healing of the socket;
- the fragment was seen at a distance and not radiographed.

Only direct osteological and imaging examination could determine whether the socket shows antemortem remodeling.

### Hair-color tradition

Stapleton said More’s white or gray hair/beard appeared reddish or yellowish after exposure. This may reflect chemical alteration, weathering, heat treatment, decomposition, or hagiographical wonder. No hair was reported in the 1978 medical inventory. Earlier travelers sometimes claimed hair remained, but that evidence is neither controlled nor demonstrably continuous.

### Scientific examination: what has and has not happened

The 1978 inspection established only that visible fragments appeared to be human cranial bone. It did not determine:

- sex;
- age at death;
- ancestry or population affinity;
- date;
- cause or method of separation;
- boiling or heat exposure;
- embalming compounds;
- whether all fragments belong to one person;
- genetic relationship to More’s descendants;
- individual identity.

No published radiocarbon, DNA, proteomic, isotopic, CT, X-ray, histological, or residue study has been located. Statements that archaeology “proved” the skull to be More’s overstate the findings.

### Potential future tests and their limits

If a faculty permits examination, useful work could include:

- micro-CT and conventional radiography;
- osteological assessment of age, sex, pathology, and tooth loss;
- surface microscopy for cut, heat, or tool marks;
- elemental and molecular study of lead corrosion products;
- chromatography or mass spectrometry for spice, resin, wax, or embalming residues;
- radiocarbon dating, with attention to conservation contaminants;
- ancient DNA and proteomic sex identification;
- comparison among fragments to detect commingling.

Even successful dating to the sixteenth century and identification as an adult male would not alone prove More’s identity. DNA comparison would require a securely documented maternal or paternal reference line or authenticated remains of a close relative, creating its own chain-of-custody and consent problems.

### Authority to authenticate

Authority has shifted over time:

- the Tudor state controlled and publicly labeled the head as that of a traitor;
- Margaret and the Roper family, according to tradition, identified it through personal recognition and custody;
- Catholic biographers declared it a martyr’s relic;
- antiquaries relied on family monuments, location, and accumulated testimony;
- St Dunstan’s institutionalized the identification;
- the Roman Catholic Church canonized More but did not thereby scientifically authenticate the Canterbury bones;
- archaeologists and a physician in 1978 confirmed the nature and context of the material but not individual identity;
- the Church of England’s consistory jurisdiction now controls whether the burial can be opened.

The most accurate present formula is: **human cranial remains in the Roper vault, traditionally and plausibly identified as Thomas More’s head, but not scientifically authenticated.**

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Tudor penal function

The head’s first secure public function was punitive and political. Mounted on London Bridge, it:

- marked More as legally condemned for treason;
- displayed royal power over the former lord chancellor;
- warned travelers entering or leaving London;
- separated the dishonored head from the buried body;
- placed More among other executed opponents of the Henrician settlement.

The public display was therefore not originally a relic cult. It was an official anti-memorial meant to turn the body into a sign of state authority.

### Family memorial and proto-relic

In Stapleton’s account, Margaret reversed that meaning. By recovering and preserving the head, she transformed a state-controlled trophy into a family object and “sacred relic.” Her alleged use of spices combines practical preservation with the treatment of honored remains.

Whether Margaret conducted formal devotions before the head is not documented in detail. “Greatest reverence” is Stapleton’s characterization. No sixteenth-century liturgy, shrine inventory, indulgence, or recorded miracle cult centered on the head has been found.

### Concealed or restricted object

The niche, lead box, underground vault, and grille created layered access restrictions:

- the public could enter the church;
- only authorized people could enter the vault;
- the grille prevented direct access;
- the box partially concealed the remains;
- later sealing removed even visual access.

These restrictions reflect burial practice, security, family privacy, and eventually conservation. They also enhanced the relic’s unseen symbolic presence: pilgrims encounter a marked place rather than the bones themselves.

### Catholic relic

After canonization in 1935, the remains became a first-class relic in Catholic understanding. Yet they were not translated into a Catholic altar reliquary or subjected to a publicly documented Roman canonical authentication process. Their continued burial within an Anglican church makes veneration indirect and ecumenically distinctive.

Visitors pray at or near the ledger slab. Annual commemorations on or near 6 July have included services and gatherings by More societies, Anglican clergy, Catholics, and scholars. These are documented modern practices; claims of an uninterrupted public pilgrimage from the sixteenth century are unsupported.

### Ecumenical symbol

The object’s present symbolic importance derives partly from the confessional reversal surrounding it:

- More died opposing the king’s supremacy over the Church;
- his alleged head is now guarded by a Church of England parish;
- Anglican and Catholic participants jointly commemorate him;
- the proposed conservation project has been described by diocesan authorities as an opportunity for reconciliation and Christian unity.

This ecumenical role is modern. It should not be projected onto the violently divided sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

### Symbol of conscience

More’s head has become a material shorthand for conscience resisting political power. Pope John Paul II’s 2000 proclamation of More as patron of statesmen and politicians strengthened this association, although the apostolic letter does not authenticate or center the Canterbury relic.

The head’s movement—from state trophy to protected family remains to saint’s relic—gives unusual physical form to the competing claims of Crown, kinship, church, conscience, and historical memory.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### Margaret’s bribe

**Early attested family-confessional tradition:** Stapleton says Margaret bribed the officer responsible for removing the head. Later writers alternately say she bought it. The distinction may be semantic: an unofficial payment for a condemned body part could be both purchase and bribe.

Evidence for a factual core is moderately strong because Stapleton was close to More-family networks and wrote while descendants lived. No administrative corroboration survives.

### Rescue from the Thames

Stapleton says Margaret intervened before the head could be cast into the Thames. This directly contradicts the November 1535 report that it had already been thrown into the river. Later tradition resolves the conflict by treating the official report as false or mistaken, but that resolution is not documented.

### The boat and basket

**Later elaboration:** Victorian and modern depictions show Margaret in a boat beneath London Bridge while the head is lowered in a basket. Lucy Madox Brown’s 1873 painting became the definitive image.

Stapleton says Margaret watched and paid the responsible person; he does not supply the fully staged river scene familiar from the painting. The visual narrative is therefore not reliable evidence of the mechanics of transfer.

### Arrest and examination before the Council

Later biographies say Margaret was imprisoned or summoned for possessing the head and defended herself by saying she would not permit it to become food for fishes. The story appears in descendants’ and later martyrological accounts, including the seventeenth-century life associated with More’s great-grandson.

This may preserve authentic family tradition, but it lacks the kind of surviving council or prison record expected for a fully documented prosecution. It should be labeled **later family tradition**.

### The recognizable face and transformed hair

Stapleton said the face remained almost as beautiful as in life and the graying hair or beard had become reddish-brown. Devotional writers could regard this as remarkable or miraculous. Natural explanations include treatment, boiling, weathering, staining, and decomposition.

No surviving tissue permits confirmation. The story belongs to the earliest detailed relic narrative and is not a purely modern invention.

### The missing tooth

Stapleton’s identification sign—a tooth known to have been absent during life—functions almost like an early forensic marker. The empty socket seen in 1978 has encouraged believers in the identification. Without direct examination for healed bone, the match remains unresolved.

### Burial in Margaret’s arms

**Later tradition:** Margaret supposedly directed that she be buried holding her father’s head. Hasted and eighteenth-century guide traditions repeated this, sometimes as hearsay. The later location of the head in a separate niche requires either that the story is symbolic or that the box was subsequently removed from her coffin or tomb.

No contemporary burial account confirms the posture.

### Secret Catholic concealment

Modern defenders of authenticity argue that recusant danger explains the absence of explicit records and the discreet niche. This is historically plausible: overt preservation of a condemned opponent’s body as a martyr’s relic could be dangerous. It is nevertheless an explanatory inference, not evidence for a missing transfer.

### “The only surviving relic”

Modern reports sometimes call the head the only relic of More. This is too absolute unless “bodily relic” is meant. Objects associated with More—including a hair shirt, hat, cap, seals, crucifix, and other possessions—have separate traditions and collections. Their authenticity varies. The Canterbury material is the principal claimed surviving portion of his body.

### Miracles, cures, and curses

No well-attested medieval-style sequence of cures, visions, punishments, or officially investigated miracles attached specifically to the Canterbury head has been found. The relic’s legend is principally one of rescue, filial devotion, preservation, identity, and conscience—not thaumaturgy.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Location

The remains are believed to remain in the sealed Roper family vault beneath the Roper Chapel at:

**St Dunstan’s Church**  
St Dunstan’s Street/London Road  
Canterbury, Kent, England

Within the vault, the fragments lie in the lead container in a wall niche behind an iron grille. The public cannot normally see or touch them. Visitors can enter the church, view the chapel, memorial slab, window, photographs, and interpretive material, and pray at the marked location.

The parish’s current website states that St Dunstan’s is ordinarily open for prayer, pilgrimage, and visiting Monday through Saturday, 9:30 a.m.–3:00 p.m., and after Sunday services until 3:00 p.m. Opening arrangements can change.

### Condition

The last detailed direct observation located is from 1978. At that time the alleged skull was already mostly reduced to fragile dark-brown pieces and powder. Lead-salt deposition was suspected. The parish now believes controlled drying and stabilization could take years after exhumation.

Because the container has remained sealed below ground, the exact condition in September 2026 cannot be stated without a new inspection.

### Control and legal regime

The remains are held within a Church of England parish church and consecrated burial space. They are not ordinary movable museum property. Disturbing or exhuming them requires ecclesiastical authorization through the faculty jurisdiction. The Commissary General of Canterbury is expected to decide any petition.

The church itself is Grade I listed, so structural alteration to the chapel, vault, or memorial setting may also engage listed-building and ecclesiastical heritage controls. Human-remains ethics, archaeological standards, descendant consultation, and Catholic interests are relevant even where not independently determinative in law.

### Proposed exhumation and conservation

In July 2025 the parish announced that it wished, subject to all permissions, to:

1. exhume the fragments;
2. conserve and stabilize them;
3. decide through consultation whether to return them below ground or house them in a more visible reliquary or shrine;
4. complete the work in anticipation of the five-hundredth anniversary of More’s execution in 2035.

The Diocese of Canterbury described this as a consultation and proposed faculty application, not an approved exhumation. No located official update establishes that the faculty has been granted or that removal has begun.

### Ownership and repatriation disputes

No formal state-to-state or institutional repatriation claim has been located. Potential stakeholders include:

- St Dunstan’s PCC and the Diocese of Canterbury;
- the Roman Catholic hierarchy in England and Wales;
- More-family descendants;
- More societies and scholars;
- local heritage bodies;
- visitors and pilgrims.

Catholic commentators have expressed interest in the relic’s treatment, but this is not equivalent to a legal ownership proceeding. The parish has said it would welcome communication with the Vatican.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Victorian art

Lucy Madox Brown’s *Margaret Roper Receiving the Head of Her Father* (1873; exhibited at the Dudley Gallery in 1875) shows Margaret in a boat while the head is lowered from London Bridge in a basket. The painting supplied concrete visual details absent from Stapleton and strongly shaped later popular imagination.

The work is sometimes misattributed to Lucy’s father, Ford Madox Brown. Lucy Madox Brown is the documented artist.

Other nineteenth-century historical paintings of More’s final encounters with Margaret reinforced the daughter’s role as chief keeper of his memory, even where they did not show the head itself.

### Poetry and prose

Alfred Tennyson’s “A Dream of Fair Women” invoked Margaret as the woman “who clasped in her last trance / Her murdered father’s head.” The line helped popularize the tradition that she died physically embracing the relic. It is poetry, not burial evidence.

Catholic biographies of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries used the head as proof of Margaret’s filial piety and More’s early treatment as a martyr. Protestant and secular histories more often repeated the anecdote as Tudor pathos or antiquarian curiosity.

Robert Bolt’s *A Man for All Seasons* and its adaptations transformed More’s modern public image as a hero of conscience. They do not make the Canterbury skull central, but their influence increased pilgrimage and interest in More-associated sites.

### Canonization and pilgrimage

More and John Fisher were beatified in 1886 and canonized in 1935. The canonization converted St Dunstan’s local historical curiosity into a significant Catholic pilgrimage destination.

The Church of England added More to its calendar in 1980. Subsequent commemorations at St Dunstan’s became increasingly ecumenical.

### Memorials at St Dunstan’s

Modern material culture surrounding the hidden remains includes:

- the 1932 ledger slab;
- one or more stained-glass commemorations;
- photographs of the 1978 opening and cranial fragments;
- explanatory panels;
- annual services at the slab;
- organized pilgrimages;
- the surviving Roper Gate across the street, marked by a plaque.

The modern memorial setting should not be confused with a public display of the bones themselves.

### International devotional networks

More societies and parishes dedicated to St Thomas More in Britain, continental Europe, and the United States have visited or supported the Canterbury site. The Lawrence Lee window was funded partly by supporters connected with More churches in Kansas City and Vienna, giving the chapel a transnational devotional network.

### Conservation controversy

The present dispute is not primarily over whether the relic should be returned to another country. It concerns:

- whether burial should be disturbed at all;
- whether scientific testing is appropriate;
- how to balance conservation with reverence;
- whether the remains should be visible;
- whether display would serve pilgrimage or turn a church into a museum;
- who should be consulted;
- how far uncertain authenticity should be acknowledged in a shrine.

Campaigners emphasize continuing decay and the missed opportunity of 1978. Others emphasize the presumption against exhumation and the need to respect a burial.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Principal primary and early sources

1. **Contemporary execution records and chronicles**, including Charles Wriothesley and Edward Hall, establish More’s execution and the punitive setting.
2. ***Letters and Papers, Foreign and Domestic, Henry VIII*, volume IX**, contains the November 1535 report that the blackened head was thrown into the river.
3. **Richard Morison’s 1536 answer to Johann Cochlaeus** supplies a conflicting early polemical reference.
4. **William Roper’s *Life of Sir Thomas More***, composed around 1557, is the earliest family biography but does not clearly provide the developed head-recovery story.
5. **Thomas Stapleton’s *Tres Thomae***, Douai, 1588, gives the foundational recovery, identification, spice-preservation, and continuing-family-custody account.
6. **William Roper’s will**, dated 4 January 1577/78, informs the separate question of More’s intended and actual burial.
7. **Roper memorial inscriptions** at St Dunstan’s supply important evidence that William and Margaret were understood to lie there.

### Antiquarian tradition

Anthony Wood, Richard Rawlinson, Thomas Hearne, Edward Hasted, and successive Canterbury guidebooks transformed family memory into an antiquarian chain. Their accounts are valuable but often copy one another. Dates such as 1715, 1824, 1835, and 1837 became confused in later summaries.

The 1837 *Gentleman’s Magazine* account is especially important because it is a first-person description of the 1835 descent into the vault. The old *Dictionary of National Biography* should not be followed uncritically in its statement that the box was discovered and opened in June 1824.

### Archaeological scholarship

Tim Tatton-Brown’s 1980 report remains the essential architectural account. It reconstructed the chapel and vault in phases and provided a historically plausible mechanism by which a relic first buried with Margaret could later have been transferred to the niche.

The Canterbury Archaeological Trust annual report confirms the practical circumstances: the vault was more than half full of rubble, was cleared and surveyed, and contained the alleged skull in a lead box behind a grille.

### Schulte Herbrüggen’s identity study

Hubertus Schulte Herbrüggen’s *Das Haupt des Thomas Morus in der St. Dunstan-Kirche zu Canterbury* (1982) is the only full monograph devoted specifically to the relic. It assembled written sources and the 1978 observations and argued for the Canterbury identification.

Its strengths are source collation, attention to the vault, and eyewitness access. Its conclusion remains historical rather than biomolecular. The book’s chapter titles—“Opening of the Roper vault,” “The problem of the skull’s identity,” and “The solution of the riddle”—should not be mistaken for laboratory proof.

### Central unresolved questions

1. What precisely is the original November 1535 report saying, and how reliable was its informant?
2. Why did William Roper omit or fail to preserve a clear recovery narrative?
3. Who was Stapleton’s informant?
4. Who held the head in 1588?
5. Where was Margaret first buried?
6. When was the lead container made?
7. Was the head initially placed in Margaret’s coffin or tomb?
8. Who made the niche and moved the container there?
9. Is the alleged 1824 opening genuine, miscited, or confused with 1835?
10. What was removed or damaged during nineteenth-century disturbance?
11. Do all fragments in the container belong to one person?
12. Can a minimally destructive scientific program establish date, sex, treatment, and familial relationship?
13. Would testing materially improve identification, given the absence of a simple uncontested comparative DNA sample?

### Discredited or overstated claims

The following claims exceed the evidence:

- that archaeology has conclusively authenticated the remains;
- that a complete, recognizable skull survives;
- that DNA testing has confirmed More’s identity;
- that Margaret’s recovery was recorded contemporaneously;
- that she certainly received the head from a bridgekeeper while sitting in a boat;
- that she certainly died or was buried holding it;
- that the relic has remained demonstrably untouched since 1535;
- that the 1978 opening occurred in 1982—the monograph appeared in 1982, but the vault was opened in July 1978;
- that Margaret died or was buried in 1577—she died in 1544; 1577/78 relates to William Roper’s death and will;
- that the Canterbury fragments are More’s only surviving associated relics of any kind.

### State of the archive

The evidence is dispersed among:

- Tudor state papers and their nineteenth-century calendars;
- early printed Catholic lives;
- manuscript versions of William Roper’s biography;
- wills and parish records;
- monumental inscriptions;
- antiquarian correspondence;
- *Gentleman’s Magazine* volumes;
- St Dunstan’s parish records;
- Canterbury Archaeological Trust documentation;
- the 1978 photographs and medical note;
- diocesan faculty and consultation records.

A definitive modern study would need to reproduce the relevant documents in full, trace every antiquarian quotation to its first form, reconcile the 1824/1835 problem, publish the complete 1978 archive, and document any future examination to current archaeological and ancient-DNA standards.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and near-primary sources

- Thomas Stapleton, *Tres Thomae* (Douai, 1588), and the modern English translation, *The Life and Illustrious Martyrdom of Sir Thomas More*. This is the earliest detailed published account of Margaret’s recovery and preservation of the head.
- William Roper, *The Life of Sir Thomas More*, composed c.1557. Its lack of a clear developed head narrative is evidentially significant.
- *Letters and Papers, Foreign and Domestic, Henry VIII*, vol. IX (1535), for the contemporary report that the head had blackened and been thrown into the river.
- Richard Morison’s 1536 polemical response to Johann Cochlaeus, for the conflicting early reference to the head on London Bridge.
- Anthony Wood, *Athenae Oxonienses*, for the later seventeenth-century Canterbury custody tradition.
- Richard Rawlinson’s communication to Thomas Hearne concerning the 1715 vault opening, preserved through later quotation.
- Edward Hasted’s history of Kent, for the eighteenth-century niche, grille, and burial tradition.
- “The Scull of Sir Thomas More,” *The Gentleman’s Magazine*, new series 7 (May 1837), p. 497, describing the 1835 inspection.

### Archaeological, medical, and specialist studies

- Tim Tatton-Brown, “The Roper Chantry in St Dunstan’s Church, Canterbury,” *The Antiquaries Journal* 60, no. 2 (1980), 227–246.
- Canterbury Archaeological Trust, Annual Report 1978–79, recording clearance and survey of the vault.
- P. H. Garrard’s medical observations, reproduced in accounts of the 1978 opening.
- Hubertus Schulte Herbrüggen, *Das Haupt des Thomas Morus in der St. Dunstan-Kirche zu Canterbury* (Opladen: Westdeutscher Verlag, 1982), 133 pages.
- Historic England, National Heritage List entry 1241793, for the church, chapel, and 1932 memorial slab.

### Institutional and current-status sources

- Diocese of Canterbury, “Preserving the relics of St Thomas More: consultation begins,” 15 July 2025.
- St Dunstan, St Mildred and St Peter benefice website, for present access and institutional description.
- Canterbury Historical and Archaeological Society pages on More and St Dunstan’s, for local historical interpretation and visitor information.
- Chelsea Old Church, for the distinction between More’s monument, the unsupported body-transfer tradition, and the Canterbury head tradition.
- Historic Royal Palaces, for the execution and institutional presentation of the relic story.
- Vatican, John Paul II’s 2000 apostolic letter, for beatification, canonization, and modern Catholic symbolism.

### Source criticism

The central evidentiary asymmetry must remain visible. The execution and display are contemporary facts. Margaret’s recovery first appears in a detailed published form in 1588. Physical continuity at Canterbury is reasonably strong from 1715, excellent from 1835 to 1978, and institutionally maintained thereafter. The link between 1535 and the Canterbury remains is persuasive historical tradition, not demonstrated identity.
