# Joan of Arc's Battle Standard: Object Dossier

**Proposed filename:** `joan_of_arcs_battle_standard_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary key

This dossier concerns the large military standard made for Jeanne la Pucelle—Joan of Arc—in Tours in spring 1429. It does **not** silently combine that object with her smaller pennon, the clergy’s crucifix banner, the dove device reported by the clerk of La Rochelle, her coat of arms, or any modern reconstruction.

Evidence is labeled as follows:

- **Documented:** contemporary administrative or judicial record.
- **Contemporary report:** produced during Joan’s lifetime, but not necessarily by an eyewitness.
- **Retrospective testimony:** recollection given during the 1450–1456 nullification proceedings.
- **Scholarly inference:** reconstruction from incomplete or conflicting evidence.
- **Tradition/legend:** claim lacking a continuous documentary or material chain.
- **Modern reconstruction:** acknowledged later object inspired by textual or visual evidence.
- **Forgery or suspect object:** presented as medieval but rejected or seriously doubted by specialists.
- **Unresolved:** evidence permits no secure conclusion.

The decisive finding is straightforward: **Joan’s original battle standard is not known to survive. No present textile has a continuous provenance from 1429, and no candidate has undergone scientific examination capable of establishing such an identity.** Its existence and manufacture are securely documented, but its exact appearance and fate are only partly reconstructable.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Object class and names

The object was a painted military textile, normally called in the sources:

- Middle French **estandart / estandard / estendart**, modern French **étendard**;
- sometimes **banière / bannière**, used less technically in Joan’s interrogation;
- Latin **vexillum**, the term employed in the official Latin trial record;
- English **standard**, **battle standard**, or loosely **banner**.

The controlling administrative document calls it **“ung grant estendart”**, “a large standard,” and distinguishes it from **“ung petit”**, a smaller ensign. The singular popular expression “Joan of Arc’s banner” consequently hides an important plurality.

In late-medieval military usage, a standard was generally an elongated flag, often tapering and divided into one or more tails; a banner was commonly more nearly square or rectangular; a pennon was smaller and usually triangular. Contemporary speakers were not perfectly consistent, however, and Joan’s judges themselves glossed the object as an *estandart ou banière*. Modern reconstructions that assign every reported image to one textile therefore risk forcing fluid terminology into an artificially exact system.

### 1.2 The documented ensemble of ensigns

At least two, and possibly four, distinguishable objects appear in the evidence:

1. **The large standard tracked here.** Paid for in Tours in April 1429; white; painted with the Lord or “King of Heaven,” the world, angels, lilies, and the names *Jhesus Maria*; silk-fringed.
2. **The small standard or pennon.** Paid for in the same account. The *Journal du siège d’Orléans* describes an Annunciation: the Virgin with an angel presenting her a lily. It reportedly caught fire accidentally during Joan’s entry into Orléans.
3. **A clergy or devotional banner.** Jean Pasquerel’s retrospective testimony describes a banner bearing the Crucifixion around which the clergy assembled. Its maker and administrative history are unknown. It should not automatically be identified with either of Poulnoir’s two paid ensigns.
4. **The dove device.** The later-recovered *Relation du greffier de La Rochelle* says that Joan had made at Poitiers an ensign with a blue shield, a white dove, and a scroll reading *De par le Roy du Ciel* (“By command of the King of Heaven”). The report conflicts with the documented Tours manufacture and may concern a separate device, a misunderstood pennon, or confused information. It is not a secure description of the large standard.

Joan also received arms showing a sword between fleurs-de-lis supporting a crown. At her trial she denied that she personally used those arms as her regular identifying device. They are not the battle standard.

### 1.3 Materials

Joan’s most authoritative physical description was given under interrogation at Rouen on 27 February 1431. She said the standard was:

> “white, of white linen or boucassin … and fringed with silk.”

The Latin record gives *de tela alba vel boucassino … fimbriatum de serico*. The corresponding Middle French tradition has *toile blanche ou boucassin*.

What *boucassin* meant in this context is not completely settled. It denoted a fine or closely woven textile, frequently understood as cotton, linen, or a cotton-like cloth depending on period and locality. It should therefore not be translated with false precision as a scientifically identified fiber. The surviving account says that the painter supplied the *estoffes*, but no swatch, workshop bill, fiber sample, or technical examination survives.

The decoration was painted rather than documented as woven embroidery. No pigments, binding medium, stitching method, pole attachment, or textile weave can now be analyzed.

### 1.4 Dimensions, weight, and construction

No contemporary dimensions or weight survive. The term *grant estendart* establishes only that it was the larger of the two ensigns commissioned. Modern dimensional reconstructions derive from comparative fifteenth-century vexillology, not from measurements of Joan’s object.

It was probably an elongated standard suitable for mounted and field use, but the number, shape, and length of its tails are not securely stated. The familiar modern image of a very long, two-tailed white standard is a plausible period reconstruction, not a surviving blueprint.

No original pole, finial, cords, cases, or fittings have been identified.

### 1.5 Ground, inscriptions, and imagery

#### Joan’s own 1431 description

Joan said:

- the field was white;
- it was “sown with lilies”;
- “the world” was represented;
- two angels stood at its sides;
- the names **Jhesus Maria** were written on it, apparently “at the side”;
- it had silk fringe.

The spelling **Jhesus** is a common late-medieval form of Jesus. *Jhesus Maria*—“Jesus [and] Mary”—also headed a number of letters issued in Joan’s name. The words were devotional names, not a modern-style slogan.

When questioned again, Joan said the depicted figure was the Lord holding the world. She rejected attempts to identify the two angels specifically as Michael and Gabriel, explaining that they were present for the honor of the Lord. Modern captions confidently naming both angels therefore exceed her testimony.

#### Jean Pasquerel’s retrospective account

At the nullification proceedings, Joan’s former confessor Jean Pasquerel recalled the image of the Savior:

- seated in judgment in the clouds of heaven;
- accompanied by an angel holding a lily;
- the Savior blessing the lily.

This agrees broadly with the judicial record’s cosmic, Christological imagery but differs in the number or visibility of angels.

#### Jean, Bastard of Orléans/Dunois

Dunois remembered a white standard bearing the figure of Our Lord holding a lily. His brief description again supports the combination of whiteness, Christ, and lily, but cannot determine the whole composition.

#### Morosini chronicle correspondence

News letters copied into the chronicle of the Venetian merchant Antonio Morosini, based on information circulating through Bruges in July 1429, describe:

- a white standard;
- Our Lord represented “in the manner of the Trinity”;
- one hand holding the world;
- the other blessing;
- an angel on each side presenting fleurs-de-lis of the kind borne by the kings of France.

This is a contemporary report, but not Morosini’s eyewitness observation. “In the manner of the Trinity” may be an informant’s interpretation rather than the painter’s intended iconography.

#### Perceval de Cagny

Perceval de Cagny, associated with the duke of Alençon, describes Joan taking her standard at Jargeau. His surviving sentence is damaged or incomplete but says one side showed God in Majesty and the other an azure shield of France held by two angels.

This is the strongest evidence for different imagery on the two faces. It remains uncertain whether:

- the reverse truly bore the royal arms;
- Cagny confused a blue shield containing Joan’s dove device with the arms of France;
- he combined two separate ensigns;
- or the defective transmission has obscured the sentence.

#### *Journal du siège d’Orléans*

The *Journal* distinguishes standard and pennon. It associates the large standard with Jesus and Mary and the smaller ensign with the Annunciation. Its text underwent compilation and interpolation, so it is valuable but must not be treated as a verbatim daily field diary in every passage.

### 1.6 What cannot be reconstructed securely

The following familiar details remain uncertain:

- whether the divine figure was God the Father, Christ as judge, Christ in Majesty, or a Trinitarian image;
- whether both angels presented lilies, or only one did;
- whether the angels were Michael and Gabriel;
- whether the reverse duplicated the front, carried France’s arms, displayed the Annunciation, or bore another device;
- whether lilies covered the entire white field or occupied only a section or tails;
- the exact location, color, and orientation of *Jhesus Maria*;
- the exact shape and number of tails;
- the dimensions, pigments, weave, and attachment to the staff.

Any modern image resolving all these questions is interpretive.

### 1.7 Damage and present material condition

No authenticated portion survives. There are consequently:

- no recorded original repairs;
- no conservation history;
- no radiocarbon result;
- no fiber, dye, pigment, or weave analysis;
- no accession number;
- no authenticated relic fragment;
- no established surviving staff or finial.

The original’s physical condition after May 1430 is unknown.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Commission and manufacture

**Documented.** The accounts of Hémon Raguier, Charles VII’s treasurer of wars, record expenditure after Easter in April 1429:

> “Et à Hauves Poulnoir, paintre, demourant à Tours, pour avoir paint et baillé estoffes pour ung grant estendart et ung petit pour la Pucelle, XXV livres tournois.”

Translation:

> “And to Hauves Poulnoir, painter residing at Tours, for having painted and supplied materials for a large standard and a small one for the Maid, 25 livres tournois.”

The expenditure was retrospectively supported by Charles VII’s letters patent issued at Chinon on 10 May 1429 and processed on 15 May. The account was printed by Jules Quicherat from the royal financial record in volume V of his *Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation*.

This entry establishes:

- a real commission;
- two separate objects;
- manufacture at or through a Tours painter;
- the painter’s name, Hauves Poulnoir;
- royal financing;
- a total cost of 25 livres tournois for painting and materials.

It does **not** itemize the two prices, textiles, labor time, pole, pigments, dimensions, or iconographic instructions.

### 2.2 The painter’s identity

The account’s painter is **Hauves Poulnoir**, with spelling variants in later transcription including Hauvres, Hennes, or Heuves Poulnoir. A once-popular assertion that he was a Scottish painter named “James Power” is an attempted reinterpretation of the name, not something stated in the account. Philippe Contamine’s critical treatment retains the documentary French name and warns against overconfident elaboration.

No signed work, workshop archive, biography, or securely attributed second painting by Poulnoir has been tied to the standard. His role is securely documented; his nationality and broader career are not.

### 2.3 Place and date

Joan was equipped at Tours before the relief expedition to Orléans. The account places payment in April 1429 after Easter; the royal authorization followed in May. The standard must have been substantially complete before Joan’s arrival at Orléans on 29 April.

Later narrative sources state that an ensign was blessed at the collegiate church of Saint-Sauveur in Tours. This is credible but less administratively secure than the payment itself, and the narratives do not always maintain a clean distinction between the large standard and the smaller ensign.

### 2.4 Who designed it?

Joan told her judges that Saints Catherine and Margaret commanded her to take a standard “from the King of Heaven” and to place the King of Heaven upon it. That is her recorded religious explanation for the design.

The recoverable production sequence is therefore:

- **Documented human manufacture:** Poulnoir painted and supplied the two textiles for royal payment.
- **Joan’s attested belief:** her voices prescribed the standard and its divine image.
- **Scholarly inference:** Joan probably communicated the desired program orally to royal officers or the painter, who translated it into familiar late-medieval iconography.
- **Unestablished:** no surviving design contract shows which details came from Joan, Poulnoir, a cleric, or a royal official.

### 2.5 Earliest attestation

The earliest securely datable material attestation is the April 1429 royal expenditure, documented through the May authorization.

The standard then appears in near-contemporary reports of the Orléans campaign. On 10 May 1429, Parisian parliamentary clerk Clément de Fauquembergue drew an imagined armed maiden with a standard in the margin of his register after news arrived of Orléans. He had not seen Joan. His sketch is important evidence that the standard was already central to reports about her, but it is not a portrait or technically reliable rendering of the textile.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

## 3.1 April 1429: Tours workshop and royal payment

**Custody link:** Hauves Poulnoir → Joan and her household or military company.

**Mechanism:** commissioned production paid by Charles VII’s war treasury.

The account is the only unambiguous administrative transfer. Whether Poulnoir personally handed the objects to Joan, an officer, Jean de Metz, or another member of her household is not recorded.

## 3.2 Tours and Blois

Narrative sources place the standard with Joan as her expedition assembled at Tours and Blois. The army’s clergy marched and worshiped under religious imagery, but Pasquerel’s crucifix banner may have been distinct.

The standard’s practical keeper was not always Joan. Large ensigns were regularly carried by attendants. Retrospective testimony describes a page bearing her flying standard when she rode from Selles. Louis de Coutes served Joan as page and later recalled her military household; Jean d’Aulon was her squire. Sources often say that Joan “carried” the standard in the broader sense of possessing or deploying it, even where a standard-bearer physically held it.

## 3.3 Entry into Orléans, 29 April 1429

Joan entered Orléans at night amid a dense crowd. The *Journal du siège* says the people pressed close to horse and ensign. A torch reportedly touched and ignited part of an ensign, and Joan turned her horse and extinguished the fire.

Later local accounts identify the burned object as the small pennon. The incident does not demonstrate that the large standard was destroyed; both later campaigning and Joan’s testimony establish that a principal standard continued in service.

## 3.4 Orléans operations, 4–8 May 1429

The standard was carried during the attacks that culminated in the lifting of the siege:

- Saint-Loup, 4 May;
- the Augustins and southern works;
- the Tourelles and boulevard, 7 May;
- the English withdrawal, 8 May.

Retrospective witnesses made the standard a rallying object. Jean d’Aulon recalled that, during the climactic assault, he saw the standard reach or touch the edge of the fortification and called on the troops to enter. Other narratives describe Joan returning to the fight and the army rallying when the standard moved forward.

These accounts support an active command-and-rallying function. They do not prove that the cloth itself exerted a miraculous force.

## 3.5 Loire campaign, June 1429

The standard accompanied Joan’s company during the Loire offensive. Its presence is especially recorded at **Jargeau**, where Perceval de Cagny says Joan took it as the assault developed. It likely accompanied the forces through Meung-sur-Loire, Beaugency, and Patay, although evidence should not be inflated into a documented sighting at every engagement.

At Jargeau Joan was struck on the helmet by a stone and returned to the attack. Later images commonly show her personally holding the standard at the walls, but narrative syntax does not always reveal whether she or an attendant bore it at each instant.

## 3.6 March to Reims and coronation, June–July 1429

The standard traveled with the royal army through the campaign that brought Charles VII to Reims, including the approaches to Auxerre, Troyes, Châlons, and Reims.

At Charles VII’s coronation on 17 July 1429, the standard occupied a conspicuous position in or near the cathedral choir and altar. When the Rouen judges asked why it had been more honored than the standards of other captains, Joan replied in the famous formulation:

> “It had been in the travail; it was right that it should be in the honor.”

The episode is documented by the trial record, though later painters greatly expanded its ceremonial staging. Joan’s statement does not mean that she formally transferred the standard to Reims Cathedral.

An English translation of the trial sometimes renders her answer as saying the standard “was in the church of Reims, she thought, fairly near the altar.” The original context concerns its position **during the coronation**, not secure continuing custody by the cathedral in 1431.

## 3.7 Northern campaign and Paris, August–September 1429

The standard accompanied Joan’s company toward Paris. At the assault on Paris on 8 September she entered or approached the ditch and called on defenders to submit to the king. She was wounded in the thigh. Visual representations in Martial d’Auvergne’s late-fifteenth-century *Vigiles de Charles VII* show banners, but these miniatures were produced decades later and are not technical portraits of the Tours textile.

When Joan withdrew, she left armor at the abbey of Saint-Denis. There is no equivalently secure evidence that she deposited her battle standard there. The later fate of the Saint-Denis armor and the standard must not be combined.

## 3.8 Late 1429–spring 1430

The object apparently remained part of Joan’s field equipment during operations around the Loire, Saint-Pierre-le-Moûtier, La Charité, Lagny, and the route toward Compiègne. Documentation is much thinner than for Orléans and Reims.

Joan told her judges that her brothers had, as far as she knew, horses, swords, and other goods of considerable value. She did not clearly include the standard among those possessions.

## 3.9 Capture at Compiègne, 23 May 1430

The trial record establishes that Joan crossed the bridge from Compiègne, attacked Jean de Luxembourg’s forces, was cut off during retreat, and was captured near the boulevard and river. Questioned about “the banner she carried,” she confirmed that it displayed the world and two angels and said that she had only one such standard.

This connects the large standard to the Compiègne operation. It does not establish who seized it or what happened afterward.

Philippe Contamine emphasizes the conspicuous silence: Burgundian accounts do not celebrate capture of the famous standard as a trophy. Possible explanations include:

- a standard-bearer escaped back into Compiègne with it;
- it was dropped or destroyed in the confusion;
- it was captured but not considered worth recording;
- Joan’s wording referred to the ensign used generally rather than an object taken with her;
- a member of her household recovered it.

No hypothesis has a continuous evidentiary chain.

## 3.10 1430–1431: the chain breaks

No Burgundian inventory, English delivery record, ransom document, trial exhibit list, church inventory, or royal receipt securely identifies the standard after Compiègne.

It was not produced before the Rouen tribunal. The judges questioned Joan about its appearance, significance, and use rather than inspecting it. This strongly suggests it was not in their custody, though absence from the courtroom alone cannot prove its fate.

The chain of custody therefore ends in May 1430, or at the latest in Joan’s retrospective descriptions of an already absent object in 1431.

## 3.11 Alleged later destinations

### Reims Cathedral

**Tradition based on misunderstood testimony.** The standard’s documented presence at the coronation generated claims that it remained near the altar or was deposited in the cathedral. No continuous cathedral inventory supports survival there.

### Orléans

Orléans preserved the memory and ceremonial image of the standard, not a documented original. Early-modern festival banners and later museum reconstructions are successors, not proven continuations.

### English or Burgundian destruction

It is sometimes said that the captors burned the standard, perhaps with Joan or as a condemned superstitious object. No identified contemporary destruction order or eyewitness account establishes this. Its destruction is plausible but undocumented.

### Survival as fragments

No fragment has an accepted pre-modern provenance, compatible textile analysis, or continuous custody. Claims that an isolated piece of medieval-looking fabric came from Joan’s standard must be treated as unverified unless accompanied by documentation that presently does not exist.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Does a physical claimant exist?

No generally recognized claimant exists. Museums and churches hold reconstructions, festival standards, paintings, tapestries, and objects associated iconographically with Joan, but none has a secure claim to being Poulnoir’s 1429 textile.

Accordingly, there is no authentic “relic textile” available for scientific authentication. The historically secure object is **lost**, not a scientifically verified museum artifact.

### 4.2 Identity confusion among Joan’s ensigns

The primary authenticity problem begins inside the medieval evidence. Reports describe:

- the Lord holding the world;
- one or two angels;
- lilies;
- *Jhesus Maria*;
- an Annunciation;
- a Crucifixion;
- a blue shield and white dove;
- an escutcheon of France supported by angels.

Older reconstructions often solved the conflict by placing nearly all motifs on two faces of one flag. Modern critical scholarship, especially Contamine, treats this as unsafe. The royal account proves two paid ensigns, while Pasquerel implies an additional clergy banner. Descriptions may therefore belong to different textiles.

### 4.3 The Azeglio tapestry

In 1858 the marquis d’Azeglio acquired at Lucerne a tapestry purporting to represent Joan before Charles VII and gave it to Orléans. It influenced subsequent reconstructions of the standard.

Its value as evidence for the 1429 object is weak. Its provenance did not lead securely back to the fifteenth century, and its iconography formed part of a cluster of nineteenth-century “discoveries” around Lucerne and Strasbourg. Even if the textile itself contained old materials, that would not prove that its image recorded Joan’s actual standard.

It should be catalogued as a later representation or suspect historicizing work, not an eyewitness copy.

### 4.4 “Medieval” miniatures and the Spanish Forger problem

Several miniatures depicting Joan with a banner entered the market in the later nineteenth or early twentieth century and were used as medieval evidence. Specialists subsequently associated examples with modern historicizing forgery, including the anonymous workshop conventionally called the **Spanish Forger**, active in Paris around the turn of the twentieth century.

Philippe Contamine concludes that the rise of erudition and patriotic or religious enthusiasm produced both necessarily speculative reconstructions and false objects that achieved continuing popular success.

These miniatures cannot be used to restore lost details of the standard. Their importance belongs to the history of reception and forgery.

### 4.5 The 1909 Orléans reconstruction

For Joan’s beatification in 1909, the conservator of the Musée Jeanne-d’Arc at Orléans commissioned a reconstructed standard. It combined:

- trial and chronicle descriptions;
- the Notre-Dame de Paris reconstruction of 1894;
- the Azeglio tapestry;
- purported medieval miniatures then taken more seriously than they are now.

This is an acknowledged modern object. It has historical significance as a product of the beatification era but no material continuity with 1429.

### 4.6 Scientific examination

No scientific examination can presently answer the central identity question because no candidate with a credible chain exists. There is no published:

- radiocarbon date of an alleged original;
- microscopy of original fibers;
- pigment or binder analysis;
- isotopic study;
- medieval repair analysis;
- dendrochronology of a surviving pole;
- DNA or biological-residue investigation.

Scientific silence must not be converted into either proof of authenticity or proof that the 1429 standard never existed. Its existence is established by documents; its survival is not.

### 4.7 Authorities to authenticate

Authority has shifted over time:

- **1429 royal administration:** established payment and issue.
- **1431 ecclesiastical tribunal:** treated Joan’s account as evidence while attacking possible superstition, but possessed no authenticated textile.
- **Early-modern civic and ecclesiastical tradition:** preserved ceremonial imagery without proving custody.
- **Nineteenth-century antiquarians and museums:** attempted visual reconstruction and sometimes accepted dubious objects.
- **Modern historians, codicologists, textile specialists, and curators:** distinguish primary descriptions from later images and reject provenance-free “relics.”

No church declaration of Joan’s sainthood authenticated a surviving battle standard.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Hauves Poulnoir — maker, April 1429

**Documented.** Painter resident at Tours, paid 25 livres tournois for painting and supplying the large and small standards. Nothing securely establishes that he was Scottish or named James Power.

### Charles VII — financier and beneficiary, 1429

**Documented.** His treasury paid for the ensigns, and his letters patent authorized the expenditure. The standard supported the campaign to relieve Orléans and secure his coronation. Its divine imagery framed Charles as the earthly beneficiary or lieutenant of the King of Heaven.

### Hémon Raguier — financial custodian of the record

**Documented.** Treasurer of wars whose account preserves the payment. His connection is administrative, not physical ownership.

### Régnier de Bouligny — financial official

**Documented.** General councillor of finances named in the authorization processing the royal expenditures.

### Joan of Arc / Jeanne la Pucelle — commissioner, possessor, and user, 1429–1430

**Documented.** Directed or approved the imagery, preferred the standard to her sword, carried or caused it to be carried in battle, and explained it during her trial.

### Jean d’Aulon — squire and operational witness

**Retrospective testimony.** Connected the standard’s advance with the renewed assault at the Tourelles. His deposition is evidence about use, not later custody.

### Louis de Coutes — page

**Retrospective testimony.** Member of Joan’s household; pages and attendants could physically bear the standard. He should not automatically be called the sole permanent standard-bearer.

### Jean Pasquerel — confessor and interpreter of imagery

**Retrospective testimony.** Described the Savior-in-judgment image and the clergy’s crucifix banner. His testimony is indispensable but was given roughly twenty-seven years later in proceedings intended to nullify Joan’s conviction.

### Jean, Bastard of Orléans, later Dunois — commander and witness

**Retrospective testimony.** Recalled a white standard with Our Lord holding a lily and its role in the Orléans campaign.

### Perceval de Cagny — chronicler associated with Alençon

**Near-contemporary narrative.** Described the standard at Jargeau, including God in Majesty and, in a damaged passage, the shield of France held by angels.

### Clément de Fauquembergue — recorder of its public image

**Contemporary non-eyewitness.** His 10 May 1429 marginal sketch is the earliest datable image associated with Joan and a standard, but it is imaginative.

### Pancrazio and Mauro Giustiniani; Antonio Morosini

**Contemporary information network.** Merchant correspondence passing through Bruges and Venice transmitted a detailed description. Morosini preserved it in his chronicle; he did not personally inspect the object.

### Pierre Cauchon, Jean Le Maistre, Jean d’Estivet, and the Rouen assessors

**Documented judicial engagement.** They interrogated Joan about the object’s imagery, alleged power, popularity, and religious legitimacy. D’Estivet’s fifty-eighth article turned the standard into evidence of vanity, superstition, and possible improper attribution of sacred authority.

### Saints Catherine and Margaret

**Joan’s attested religious claim.** Joan said their voices ordered her to take the standard from the King of Heaven and place his image upon it. This is evidence for her stated belief, not independently demonstrable manufacture by divine beings.

### The “King of Heaven”

The phrase designated God or Christ as the supreme sovereign. Joan connected the standard with this heavenly kingship. The figure painted on it was described variously as Our Lord, the Savior, God in Majesty, or a Trinitarian representation.

### The Virgin Mary

Her name appeared in *Jhesus Maria*. The Annunciation was painted on the smaller pennon according to the *Journal*. Later reconstructions frequently transferred Marian imagery to the large standard’s reverse.

### Angels

Two angels flank the world or Lord in Joan’s account. Other reports say they present lilies. Joan did not affirm that they were specifically Michael and Gabriel; that identification is a later harmonization.

### Charles VII’s army and Joan’s company

**Documented network.** The standard marked Joan’s command, provided a rallying point, and distinguished her household within the royal host.

### Orléans civic and ecclesiastical institutions

**Documented commemorative network.** The city transformed the lost ensign into a recurring ceremonial emblem, eventually maintaining festival replacements and reconstructions.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Military identification

The standard identified Joan’s place and company in a crowded late-medieval army. It helped troops find her and served as a visible command and rallying point. Its size and white ground made it conspicuous.

### 6.2 Avoidance of personal killing

Joan told the tribunal that she preferred the standard to her sword and carried it when going among the enemy so that she would not kill anyone. Her statement gives the object an ethical function: it allowed her to participate visibly in combat while presenting herself as a leader and divine messenger rather than an executioner.

This should not be exaggerated into proof that she never used any weapon under any circumstances. It is her sworn trial assertion about habitual conduct.

### 6.3 Liturgical preparation

Narratives associate the ensigns with blessing at Saint-Sauveur in Tours and with clerical processions before the march from Blois. Pasquerel described priests gathering around a crucifix banner and singing or praying. Because several banners existed, the large standard’s exact liturgical role cannot always be isolated.

### 6.4 Rallying at Orléans

At the Tourelles, movement of the standard was remembered as the signal or visual focus of renewed attack. The material action—raising, advancing, or touching the fortification—is historically plausible and supported by retrospective testimony. Claims that supernatural forces emerged from the cloth belong to later interpretation.

### 6.5 Coronation precedence

Its place at Reims converted a field object into ceremonial testimony. Joan’s explanation that it deserved honor because it had shared the labor gave it an almost participant-like status. The standard symbolically carried the military campaign into the coronation.

It did not crown Charles, transfer a legal title, or substitute for the traditional regalia. Its function was political and representational, not a formal constitutional necessity.

### 6.6 Claimed fortunate or protective power

The Rouen prosecution alleged that soldiers made pennons modeled on Joan’s “fortunate” standard and expected victory through them. Asked whether hope rested in the standard or herself, Joan replied that it rested in Our Lord. Asked whether another carrier would have been equally fortunate, she declined certainty.

This distinction is important:

- **Documented allegation:** soldiers treated imitations as lucky.
- **Joan’s documented answer:** efficacy belonged to God, not the cloth.
- **Unestablished:** how many copies were made, by whom, or whether soldiers uniformly believed in talismanic power.

### 6.7 Political theology

The program joined:

- white and gold or yellow;
- lilies associated with France and the Virgin;
- the heavenly ruler holding the world;
- angels presenting lilies;
- the names Jesus and Mary.

Contamine interprets this as a forceful expression of Joan’s political theology: the King of Heaven sanctions a properly ordered France and Charles’s mission. This is scholarly interpretation grounded in the images, not a surviving written explanation by Poulnoir.

### 6.8 Access and handling

No taboo restricted the standard to Joan’s touch. Pages and attendants bore it. Joan sometimes held it herself. Its ceremonial prestige did not make it a liturgical relic during her lifetime.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The divinely commanded standard

**Early attested claim.** Joan told the 1431 court that Catherine and Margaret commanded her to take the standard “from the King of Heaven” and have his image painted upon it.

The claim is historically secure as testimony; its supernatural origin is a matter of faith, not documentary verification. The physical production account identifies Poulnoir as painter and royal funds as payment, so no contradiction is necessary: divine command was Joan’s explanation of inspiration, while human manufacture is documented.

### 7.2 A magical guarantee of victory

**Contemporary allegation.** The prosecution said troops copied the standard because it was considered fortunate. Later retellings turned this into an invincible talisman.

Joan expressly located hope in God. The tribunal ultimately omitted the standard controversy from the final twelve condensed articles, suggesting that the answers did not provide a decisive heresy charge.

### 7.3 The standard alone rallied and won the Tourelles

**Retrospective heroic narrative.** Witnesses remembered a decisive moment when the standard advanced and the French surged into the fortification. The standard was genuinely a rallying sign. The stronger proposition—that the textile itself caused victory—is devotional or literary interpretation.

### 7.4 “It had been in the labor; it was right that it should be in the honor”

**Secure judicial testimony, later emblematic saying.** The statement concerning Reims became the central aphorism of the standard’s legend. It encouraged painters to show Joan and the unfurled banner in the foreground of the coronation, often with details no contemporary observer recorded.

### 7.5 Deposition at Reims

**Later misunderstanding.** Joan’s description of its position near the altar during the coronation has been read as permanent deposition. No cathedral custody chain follows.

### 7.6 Capture and burning by the English

**Plausible but undocumented tradition.** The notion that the English seized and destroyed the standard creates a narratively satisfying ending but lacks a surviving trophy record or destruction account. Contamine notes that Burgundian silence about such a prestigious capture is itself striking.

### 7.7 Miraculous survival or hidden fragments

**Unverified.** No candidate textile bridges the documentary gap after Compiègne. A fragment without pre-modern documentation cannot be authenticated merely because its cloth is old or its design resembles a reconstruction.

### 7.8 The Scottish painter “James Power”

**Modern speculative etymology.** Some popular narratives transform “Hauves Poulnoir” into a Scottish name, often James Power. The royal account says only that Poulnoir was a painter residing at Tours. Until independent documentation is found, “James Power” is not a fact about the standard’s manufacture.

### 7.9 A single flag bearing every reported motif

**Modern harmonization.** Many replicas place Christ, the world, two angels, lilies, *Jhesus Maria*, the royal shield, the Annunciation, and sometimes the dove on one two-sided textile. This solves literary contradictions artistically, not historically. The account’s two ensigns and Pasquerel’s third banner make conflation likely.

### 7.10 Exact modern reconstructions

**Modern invention in the technical sense.** Replicas routinely fix dimensions, colors, lettering, tail shape, and arrangement that the sources leave open. They are honest reconstructions when labeled as such; they become misleading only when presented as facsimiles.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Original object

**Status:** lost; probably destroyed, decayed, or deliberately discarded at an unknown date.

**Last secure operational association:** Joan’s Compiègne campaign, 23 May 1430.

**Last direct descriptive evidence:** Joan’s Rouen trial testimony, February–March 1431, referring to an object no longer available to the court.

**Present location:** unknown.

**Owner or controlling institution:** none established.

**Accession number:** none.

**Public access:** impossible because no authenticated surviving object is known.

**Legal or repatriation dispute:** none concerning an accepted original.

### 8.2 Orléans reconstructions and ceremonial successors

The Maison de Jeanne d’Arc and the Centre Jeanne-d’Arc in Orléans interpret the standard through documents and reconstructions. The Centre holds a major research collection rather than the original textile.

Orléans’s annual Joan of Arc festivals employ a ceremonial standard. According to the French Ministry of Culture’s intangible-heritage inventory, the earlier combination of banner and standard was replaced in 2000 by a single standard inspired by a reconstruction produced by the Centre Jeanne-d’Arc in 1990. Civic authorities ceremonially transfer the standard to ecclesiastical custody and receive it back during the festivities.

This annual object is a living ceremonial successor, not Joan’s textile.

Sources differ over the dating of particular festival textiles: some local summaries refer to a 1936 standard based on the 1909 arrangement, while the intangible-heritage inventory records the adoption of a new single standard in 2000. These statements concern successive festival objects and designs, not rival claims to the medieval original.

### 8.3 Other replicas

Acknowledged replicas or reconstructions have been made for:

- Orléans museums and festivals;
- Notre-Dame de Paris in 1894;
- Joan’s beatification in 1909;
- canonization celebrations in 1920;
- the fifth centenary of the relief of Orléans in 1929;
- commemorations in Reims, Rouen, Lagny-sur-Marne, and elsewhere.

Their present repositories and conditions vary. None carries the original’s identity.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Rediscovery through publication

Jules Quicherat’s five-volume publication of the condemnation and nullification trials and related documents in 1841–1849 transformed research. For the first time, artists and antiquarians could coordinate Joan’s answers, witness depositions, chronicles, and financial accounts on a large scale.

The consequence was double:

- historically informed attempts to reconstruct the standard;
- increased demand for supposed medieval images and relics, encouraging misattribution and forgery.

### 9.2 Nineteenth-century reconstructions

Orléans displayed an early reconstructed “banner of Joan of Arc” in the mid-nineteenth century. Another version was reportedly made in 1876 and placed at Les Invalides.

In 1894 a standard was produced for Notre-Dame de Paris. Its designers tried to respect witness descriptions but put the Annunciation—better attested for the small pennon—on the reverse. This influential decision exemplifies the persistent merging of separate ensigns.

### 9.3 Beatification and canonization

The 1909 beatification prompted the Orléans museum reconstruction and another design proposed by Henri de Barenton, which accumulated multiple motifs on one face.

Joan’s canonization by Benedict XV on 16 May 1920 intensified production of church banners, standards, devotional images, medals, and postcards. The canonization authenticated Joan as a Catholic saint; it did not authenticate any surviving banner.

A monumental painted banner associated with Bishop Stanislas Touchet, probably produced between 1909 and 1919 and rediscovered at the Orléans bishopric in 2023, depicts Joan ascending among angels, one of whom carries her standard. Measuring approximately 3.87 by 2.52 metres, it is an artifact of the canonization movement, not a claimant to the 1429 textile.

### 9.4 Civic festivals

The standard has become a recurring actor in the Fêtes de Jeanne d’Arc at Orléans. The handing over and return of the modern standard dramatizes cooperation and negotiated precedence between municipal and ecclesiastical authorities.

The original object’s loss therefore did not terminate its ceremonial biography. Instead, its identity became reproducible: the “standard” now exists socially through successive authorized replacements.

### 9.5 Painting and sculpture

Major modern works helped establish a public image more definite than the documents permit. Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres’s *Jeanne d’Arc at the Coronation of Charles VII* (1854) shows Joan prominently with an upright standard near the altar. Nineteenth-century academic and religious art repeatedly made the white, fleur-de-lis-covered banner her chief attribute.

Such works document reception, not the textile’s precise appearance.

### 9.6 Literature, drama, opera, and film

The standard became shorthand for Joan’s divine mission in:

- Friedrich Schiller’s *Die Jungfrau von Orleans* (1801);
- Jules Michelet’s historical writing;
- Charles Péguy’s Joan works;
- Paul Claudel’s *Jeanne d’Arc au bûcher*;
- nineteenth- and twentieth-century opera and oratorio;
- silent and sound cinema, including Carl Theodor Dreyer’s *The Passion of Joan of Arc* (1928);
- later television, comics, animation, and games.

These works often give the standard a stable iconography assembled from nineteenth-century reconstructions rather than medieval evidence.

### 9.7 Political use

From the nineteenth century onward, Joan and her white standard were mobilized by incompatible political constituencies:

- Catholic royalists treated the lilies and King-of-Heaven imagery as sacred monarchy;
- republicans emphasized a popular national heroine;
- nationalists used the standard as an image of territorial resistance;
- the Catholic Church incorporated it into the cult of a canonized saint;
- the French Republic institutionalized a national Joan of Arc commemoration in 1920.

The object’s imagery was particularly adaptable because it united Christian, royal, military, and national signs without surviving in a fixed original form.

### 9.8 Commercial and popular replicas

Flags sold as “Joan of Arc’s banner” commonly display:

- a white field;
- Christ enthroned on a rainbow;
- two kneeling angels;
- gold fleurs-de-lis;
- *Jhesus Maria*;
- a long two-tailed form.

These are usually descendants of nineteenth- and twentieth-century reconstruction traditions. Product descriptions calling them “exact replicas” are unsupported.

Digital culture has further diversified the design. Fantasy games and animation sometimes replace the historical imagery with heraldic crosses, dark flags, armored-saint emblems, or invented Latin slogans. These designs influence contemporary search results but are not historical evidence.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Primary-source corpus

The principal documentary sources are:

1. **Charles VII’s war accounts**, preserving Poulnoir’s payment.
2. **The 1431 condemnation trial**, especially Joan’s interrogations and article 58.
3. **The 1450–1456 nullification proceedings**, especially Pasquerel, Dunois, d’Aulon, Louis de Coutes, and other campaign witnesses.
4. **The *Journal du siège d’Orléans***, valuable but textually layered.
5. **Perceval de Cagny’s chronicle**, particularly the Jargeau description.
6. **The Morosini chronicle correspondence**, transmitting international news from 1429.
7. **The *Relation du greffier de La Rochelle***, source of the dove device.
8. **Clément de Fauquembergue’s parliamentary register**, with the 10 May 1429 imagined sketch.
9. Later fifteenth-century visual narratives such as Martial d’Auvergne’s *Vigiles de Charles VII*.

### 10.2 Quicherat

Jules Quicherat’s edition remains foundational because it assembled the trials and many scattered records. It must nevertheless be supplemented by later critical editions, notably Pierre Tisset and Yvonne Lanhers for the condemnation trial, and by modern work on manuscript transmission and witness context.

### 10.3 Eugène de Certain

Eugène de Certain’s 1859 “Note sur l’étendard de Jeanne d’Arc” is an important document of early scholarly reconstruction. It belongs to the moment when the Azeglio tapestry and other purported images were reshaping the subject. Its arguments should be read historiographically rather than accepted uncritically.

### 10.4 Adrien Harmand

Adrien Harmand’s *Jeanne d’Arc: ses costumes, son armure* (1929) attempted a detailed archaeological reconstruction of Joan’s material equipment. His distinctions among standard, pennon, and panonceau remain useful, but some iconographic conclusions and precise dimensions are conjectural.

### 10.5 Philippe Contamine

Contamine’s “Remarques critiques sur les étendards de Jeanne d’Arc” is the most important modern corrective. Its chief contributions are:

- insisting on the plurality of ensigns;
- returning to exact wording and source dates;
- separating eyewitness, hearsay, and late imagery;
- questioning the unrecorded fate after Compiègne;
- identifying nineteenth-century reconstructions and false miniatures as a major source of modern certainty;
- interpreting the standard as political theology without pretending to recover a facsimile.

### 10.6 Running controversies

#### One standard or several?

**Current best-supported answer:** several ensigns existed. The royal account alone proves two. A third clergy banner is credible. Which descriptions attach to which object remains partly unresolved.

#### God the Father, Christ, or Trinity?

Joan’s own words and other contemporary reports differ. Iconographic vocabulary was fluid, and news transmission could transform “the Lord holding the world” into a Trinity. No surviving image settles the question.

#### One-sided or two-sided decoration?

Cagny supports different faces; ordinary flag-painting practice could also produce mirrored or related images. The reverse remains unresolved.

#### Fleurs-de-lis or a royal shield?

Both are attested, but perhaps on different zones, faces, or objects. Later heraldic logic cannot override the damaged textual evidence.

#### Where was *Jhesus Maria*?

Joan thought it was “at the side.” Modern banners place it centrally or along the upper edge. Exact placement is unknown.

#### What happened at Compiègne?

The standard may have escaped, been lost, or been taken. No hypothesis is documentarily complete.

#### Did the original survive?

No evidence supports continuous survival. Apparent visual or textile claimants emerge centuries too late.

### 10.7 Unpublished and lost evidence

The original workshop design, itemized bill, and delivery acknowledgment are not known. Many royal records from the period were lost, dispersed, or survive only through later editions. No Burgundian or English document yet identified supplies the missing post-capture chain.

Future discoveries could clarify a trophy, church deposit, or destruction, but no current conclusion should assume such a document exists.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and near-primary sources

- **Royal war accounts of Hémon Raguier**, printed in Jules Quicherat, *Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc*, vol. V, pp. 257–258. The decisive evidence for Poulnoir, Tours, two ensigns, and 25 livres tournois.
- **Condemnation trial, 1431.** Joan’s descriptions, preference for the standard, statements about avoiding personal killing, its divine command, Reims precedence, alleged fortune, and article 58.
- **Nullification trial, 1450–1456.** Depositions of Pasquerel, Dunois, d’Aulon, Louis de Coutes, and others.
- **Perceval de Cagny, chronicle.** Description at Jargeau.
- ***Journal du siège d’Orléans*.** Standard/pennon distinction, Annunciation, entry fire, and campaign use.
- **Antonio Morosini’s chronicle correspondence.** Contemporary international report of the white standard and divine imagery.
- ***Relation du greffier de La Rochelle*.** Dove-and-scroll device.
- **Clément de Fauquembergue’s parliamentary register, 10 May 1429.** Earliest datable imagined image of Joan with a standard.
- **Martial d’Auvergne, *Vigiles de Charles VII*, BnF Français 5054, c. 1484.** Later fifteenth-century visual reception.
