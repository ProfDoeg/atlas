# The Virgin of Antipolo: Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `the_virgin_of_antipolo_object_dossier.md`  
**Research current to:** 6 September 2026

## Evidentiary key

- **Documented:** supported by a contemporary document, archival citation, official decree, or securely dated physical record.
- **Near-contemporary report:** written sufficiently close to the event to carry historical weight, but not itself the originating record.
- **Later historical reconstruction:** a modern or substantially later account based on cited earlier material.
- **Institutional tradition:** maintained by the shrine, diocese, religious order, civil authority, or another named institution.
- **Pious tradition / miracle claim:** a devotional assertion whose supernatural interpretation is not historically testable.
- **Inference:** a reconstruction consistent with the evidence but not directly documented.
- **Unresolved:** the available evidence does not establish the claim.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity

The Virgin of Antipolo is an extant Roman Catholic cult image of the Virgin Mary, conventionally dated to the early seventeenth century and associated since 1632 with Antipolo, east of Manila. Its principal Spanish title is **Nuestra Señora de la Paz y Buen Viaje**, also written **Nuestra Señora de la Paz y del Buen Viaje** (“Our Lady of Peace and Good Voyage”). Common English forms are **Our Lady of Peace and Good Voyage**, **Our Lady of Antipolo**, and **the Virgin of Antipolo**. Filipino forms include **Birhen ng Antipolo** and **Mahal na Birhen ng Kapayapaan at Mabuting Paglalakbay**. Modern ecclesiastical Latin sometimes renders the title as *Nostra Domina de Pace et Bono Itinere* or similar variants, but no single historically fixed Latin title was found in the sources examined.

“Antipolo” is locational rather than an original Marian title: the image acquired the name from the town and shrine where it became established. The town’s name is traditionally derived from **tipolo** or **antipolo**, a Philippine tree now botanically identified as *Artocarpus blancoi* of the mulberry family. The city government and University of Santo Tomas plant database retain this derivation. The frequently repeated claim that the name embodies a Tagalog locative prefix plus *tipolo* was not substantiated in the authoritative sources located and should not be treated as established etymology. [University of Santo Tomas plant database](https://www.ust.edu.ph/ust-manila-plant-databse/tipolo/); [Antipolo City Government](https://antipolo.ph/index.php/about-antipolo/)

The dossier tracks the object presently enthroned in the International Shrine of Our Lady of Peace and Good Voyage–Immaculate Conception Parish, commonly called Antipolo Cathedral. Numerous processional, parish, household, and overseas replicas exist. Reports of “pilgrim images” traveling around the Philippines must not automatically be read as movements of the seventeenth-century original.

### Object class and construction

The image is a **dressed devotional statue of the *de bastidor* type**: carved head and hands are attached to a mannequin-like wooden framework concealed beneath textiles. This construction permits repeated dressing and redressing without requiring a completely carved anatomical body.

The Cultural Center of the Philippines’ *Encyclopedia of Philippine Art* gives:

- date: early seventeenth century;
- material: wood;
- height: approximately **120 centimetres**;
- construction: *de bastidor*;
- exposed portions: head and hands of dark hardwood;
- complexion: golden-brown to dark brown;
- present location: Antipolo City, Rizal Province.

An older Spanish description calls it **“vara y cuarto”** high, approximately 1.04 metres if calculated with the Castilian vara. The discrepancy with the modern estimate may reflect imprecise historical measurement, differing inclusion of pedestal or framework, or later reporting. No published technical measurement survey resolving the discrepancy was found. [Cultural Center of the Philippines](https://epa.culturalcenter.gov.ph/3/82/2248/); [Los Agustinos y el arte de María en Filipinas](https://revistas.agustinosvalladolid.es/index.php/archivo/article/download/1170/1162/1559)

The Virgin is represented standing, with long unbound hair and markedly enlarged dark eyes. Her hands are held below the breast. Modern vesture ordinarily includes an embroidered blouse, long skirt, and mantle; these are removable textiles, not carved permanent garments. The image wears or is displayed with:

- an imperial-style crown;
- a diadem;
- a surrounding aureole with twelve stars;
- rings and other jewels;
- a military baton or staff;
- richly worked, periodically changed textiles;
- a pedestal traditionally said to have been made from the legendary tipolo tree.

The military baton commemorates the cane or command staff traditionally presented by Governor Juan de Arechederra after the image’s final trans-Pacific voyage in 1746–1748. A pure-gold crown and rich dress are explicitly named in Jesuit Pedro Murillo Velarde’s certificate recording the image’s return to Antipolo on 20 February 1748. Whether either surviving accessory is the identical 1748 object has not been demonstrated in a published technical catalogue. [Carlos Villoria Prieto, “La Virgen de la Paz y Buen Viaje de Antipolo”](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/libro/998656/2.pdf)

### Wood, color, and manufacture

The exposed head and hands are described as dark hardwood. The *Encyclopedia of Philippine Art* states that repeated polishing with oil contributed to their darkening. The precise wood species has not been established by a published microscopic, chemical, dendrological, or isotopic analysis located for this dossier. “Mexican hardwood,” sometimes presented as fact, is therefore a traditional geographic attribution rather than a scientifically demonstrated species provenance.

The source occasionally cited for an ebony component concerns the **cross of a separate crucifix** damaged in 1639, not necessarily the Virgin’s wood. Murillo Velarde contrasted the ebony cross, which burned, with the Virgin’s supposedly “less strong” wood, which he said survived. Calling the Virgin herself ebony is consequently unsupported by that account.

No maker’s signature, workshop mark, inscription, dated internal document, or securely identified commission contract is published in the material located. The sculptor is unknown.

### Damage and alterations

Two marks on the right cheek and neck are traditionally identified as spear or lance wounds inflicted during the Chinese uprising of 1639. They remain visible according to the *Encyclopedia of Philippine Art*. The physical existence of the cuts is documented photographically and institutionally; their attribution to the 1639 attack rests primarily on later Jesuit narrative, especially Murillo Velarde’s 1749 history. No modern forensic examination establishing the date or tool that produced them was found. [Cultural Center of the Philippines](https://epa.culturalcenter.gov.ph/3/82/2248/)

A second fire episode was reported for 10–11 September 1745. According to Murillo Velarde, a candle bent toward the image while it was in the sacristy for redressing. Gold ornament melted and pearls burst; a hole the size of the candle was burned through the gold braid, outer garment, and lining, but not through an inner tunic or into the statue. This account records damage to vesture and jewelry, not necessarily to the wooden image. Its miraculous interpretation belongs to the source. [Villoria Prieto, reproducing Murillo Velarde](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/libro/998656/2.pdf)

The image survived the destruction of the Antipolo church in 1945 because it had been removed and concealed. The church and much of its artistic setting did not survive. Colonial reliefs representing episodes from the Virgin’s history were dispersed; at least one, depicting the 1639 fire episode, reached the Museo San Agustín in Intramuros. The old retablo, architectural setting, and much of the accumulated treasury are lost or dispersed.

### Treasury

Devotional gifts once formed a substantial treasury of garments, pearls, gemstones, crowns, and church plate. The *Encyclopedia of Philippine Art* says many jewels “no longer exist” but does not provide a complete inventory, loss date, or itemized account. This dossier found no adequate published reconstruction of:

- the pre-1768 Jesuit treasury;
- objects removed or transferred after the Jesuit expulsion;
- losses during the Philippine Revolution;
- losses, dispersals, or salvage connected with the destruction of 1945;
- the precise continuity of individual crowns, rings, and vestments.

Claims that particular present jewels are seventeenth- or eighteenth-century originals therefore require object-level documentation not presently public.

### Current condition

The statue remains an actively dressed, enthroned, processed, and venerated object rather than a museum accession. No museum-style accession number is published. No comprehensive conservation report, radiography, CT scan, pigment study, wood identification, dendrochronology, radiocarbon test, or comparative Mexican workshop analysis was found. Absence of a published report does not prove that no internal examination has occurred, but it leaves the material history unusually dependent on visual and devotional descriptions.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Physical origin: what can be established

The standard account says Governor-General **Juan Niño de Tabora** obtained the image at Acapulco, New Spain, and took it aboard the galleon *El Almirante* when he sailed for the Philippines on 25 March 1626. Its existence by that voyage is highly plausible and consistently maintained, but the surviving published evidentiary chain is later than the event.

No contemporaneous 1626 purchase receipt, sculptor’s contract, Acapulco parish inventory, cargo manifest explicitly naming the statue, or Tabora letter documenting its acquisition was located. The assertion that Tabora took it from “a parish church in Acapulco” appears in later accounts without identification of the parish or the legal mechanism by which the image left it. “Gift,” “purchase,” “loan,” and informal removal cannot presently be distinguished.

The earliest major accessible narrative is Pedro Murillo Velarde’s *Historia de la Provincia de Philipinas de la Compañía de Jesús*, printed in Manila in 1749, more than a century after 1626. Murillo was nevertheless unusually well placed: he was a Jesuit, served as rector of Antipolo in the 1740s, handled the image’s 1748 return, and could use Jesuit institutional records now difficult to reconstruct. His history is indispensable but combines documentary narrative, corporate memory, and explicitly miracle-oriented writing. [1749 edition record and scan](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL33173474M/Historia_de_la_Provincia_de_Philipinas_de_la_Compa%C3%B1ia_de_Jesus)

Modern Spanish scholarship identifies Murillo’s work as the central historical source and notes that nineteenth-century books specifically entitled *Historia de la Virgen de la Paz y del Buen Viaje*—published in 1847, 1866, 1876, and 1887—were largely extracts or reworkings of his account, not independent early witnesses. [Villoria Prieto](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/libro/998656/2.pdf)

### Conflicting arrival dates

Sources agree on departure from Acapulco on **25 March 1626**, but report different Manila arrival dates:

- **22 June 1626** in Villoria Prieto’s reconstruction;
- **18 July 1626** in widely circulated shrine and popular histories.

The vessel may have entered Philippine waters or Manila Bay before a formal Manila landing, but no source examined explicitly reconciles the two dates. Both should remain visible rather than silently harmonized.

### Manufacture

The sculpture is generally called Mexican or New Spanish because it came from Acapulco. That establishes the last known pre-Philippine location, not necessarily its workshop. No published evidence proves:

- the sculptor’s identity;
- an Acapulco workshop;
- the quarry, forest, or timber source;
- a precise manufacturing date;
- whether it had been made specifically for Tabora;
- whether it had an earlier devotional identity.

The early-seventeenth-century stylistic dating reported by the Cultural Center of the Philippines is the best available public classification. Some devotional sources call it a sixteenth-century image, but no technical study was found supporting that earlier date. The prudent description is therefore: **an anonymous New Spanish-associated wooden *de bastidor* image, probably early seventeenth century, present at Acapulco by March 1626**.

### Original dedication

It is unclear under what title the image was venerated at Acapulco. Later tradition says Tabora declared it **queen and governor of his vessel** and invoked it as protectress during storms and a shipboard fire. The formal title “Peace and Good Voyage” belongs to the later Philippine history and was reportedly bestowed after the 1653 voyage and public thanksgiving.

The *Encyclopedia of Philippine Art* identifies a public decree dated **8 September 1653**, under Governor Sabiniano Manrique de Lara, as the act conferring the title. The text or archival shelf mark of that decree was not reproduced in the sources consulted. Murillo says Archbishop Miguel de Poblete began calling the image the Virgin “del Buen Viage, y de la Paz” after the dangerous 1653 crossing. These related accounts may describe the same ceremonial naming but should not be treated as identical documentary evidence without the decree itself. [Cultural Center of the Philippines](https://epa.culturalcenter.gov.ph/3/82/2248/); [Villoria Prieto](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/libro/998656/2.pdf)

### Legendary localization at the tipolo tree

Institutional and pious tradition holds that while the Jesuits were constructing the Antipolo church, the image repeatedly disappeared from its temporary chapel and was found in a tipolo tree. The missionaries interpreted this as an instruction to relocate the church to that place. They cut the tree and made its trunk into the image’s pedestal.

The legend is not part of the presently located 1626 documentation. Its exact first textual appearance remains unresolved. Later versions add that pilgrims removed splinters from the pedestal as healing relics, forcing its custodians to cover or protect it with stone. The pedestal now shown as the “tipolo-tree pedestal” has not been scientifically dated or genetically matched to *Artocarpus blancoi*. Its identity is institutional tradition, not demonstrated material provenance.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Before March 1626: unresolved

The statue was reportedly in an unidentified Acapulco parish church. Its maker, commissioning patron, earlier title, duration there, and means of transfer to Tabora are unknown. This is the first break in the chain.

### March–June/July 1626: Acapulco to Manila

Tabora took the image aboard *El Almirante*, which left Acapulco on 25 March 1626. Later tradition says the ship survived storms and a fire and that Tabora credited the Virgin’s intercession. Upon arrival, the image was carried in procession to Manila Cathedral for a thanksgiving Mass attended by civil and ecclesiastical authorities. Some accounts place it subsequently in the Jesuit church of San Ignacio in Intramuros; the exact sequence and duration are not fully resolved.

**Mechanism of custody:** personal or gubernatorial custody by Tabora, followed by ceremonial ecclesiastical custody in Manila.

### 1626–1632: Tabora’s custody

The image remained associated with Tabora until his death on **22 July 1632**. One modern Spanish article misprints the year as 1732; the historical governor died in 1632, and the surrounding chronology requires that reading.

### 1632: transfer to the Society of Jesus

Tabora entrusted or gave the image to the Jesuits for their church at Antipolo, then under construction by Father Juan de Salazar. Because the permanent church was incomplete, it was placed in a chapel in the barrio of Santa Cruz.

**Mechanism:** bequest, deathbed entrustment, or posthumous donation according to later accounts. No will or deed was found.

**Custodian:** Society of Jesus, Philippine Province.

### 1630s: move to the church’s present site

According to the tipolo-tree legend, repeated disappearances caused the Jesuits to move the church to the tree’s location and make a pedestal from its trunk. The underlying relocation may be historical; the supernatural cause is pious tradition.

### 1639–1640: Chinese uprising, attack, and removal to Manila

During the 1639 uprising by Chinese residents—called *Sangleyes* in Spanish colonial records—the Antipolo church was attacked. Murillo’s later account says the insurgents:

1. struck the Virgin with lances;
2. threw it and a crucifix into a fire;
3. failed to burn the Virgin, although the crucifix’s ebony cross burned;
4. left the two images to be recovered by Spanish forces.

This episode must be situated within a violent colonial uprising and repression, rather than abstracted into a simple iconoclast legend. Later European accounts frequently portray the Chinese solely as desecrators; those accounts originated within the victorious colonial and missionary institutions.

Governor **Sebastián Hurtado de Corcuera** ordered both recovered images brought to Manila in solemn procession and placed in the **Capilla Real**. The crucifix stayed in Manila; the Virgin was directed to Cavite for use as patroness of voyages to New Spain. [Murillo passage reproduced by Villoria Prieto](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/libro/998656/2.pdf)

### 1641–1646: first documented sequence of galleon journeys

Murillo gives this sequence:

- **1641:** aboard *San Luis*, commanded by General Alonso García Romero; completed a voyage and returned.
- **1643:** again aboard *San Luis*, commanded by Lorenzo de Ugalde Orella; completed a round voyage.
- **1645–February 1646:** a third voyage aboard *San Luis*, commanded by Hernando López Perona; the ship made an **arribada**, returning without completing the crossing.

Modern retellings sometimes count only successful round trips and sometimes count every embarkation. This explains some claims of six, seven, or eight voyages, but published lists themselves also contain inconsistencies.

### 1646–1647: Cavite and Manila

Governor Diego Fajardo ordered the returned image placed in Cavite’s parish church. During the Dutch attack on Cavite in June 1647, commander Andrés López de Azaldigui placed the port’s defense under the Virgin’s patronage and prayers were offered before the image. Spanish success was subsequently attributed to it.

The Manila Cathedral’s institutional history says the Virgin was sheltered there in 1647 after the damage of 1639, until its eventual return to Antipolo. This may refer to a temporary Manila phase between Cavite-related movements, but the precise dates require further archival work. [Manila Cathedral history](https://www.manilacathedral.com.ph/seventh.aspx)

### 1648–1651: further galleon movements

Modern lists derived from Murillo and Monina Mercado name:

- **1648–1649:** *Encarnación*;
- **1650:** *San Diego*, sometimes described as an aborted voyage;
- **1651–1653:** *San Francisco Javier*.

One academic paper prints “1851–1853” and “1859–1662,” evident typographical errors for 1651–1653 and 1659–1661/1662. The commonly repeated statement that the image made “six successful round trips and two aborted voyages” needs verification vessel by vessel against original sailing records. [Mark Tallara paper](https://www.kuasu.cpier.kyoto-u.ac.jp/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/5-1.MarkTallara.pdf)

### 1653: ceremonial restitution to Antipolo

The *San Francisco Javier* left Acapulco on 3 March 1653 carrying incoming Governor-General **Sabiniano Manrique de Lara**, Archbishop **Miguel de Poblete**, officials, Jesuits, and Augustinian Recollects. Murillo says three violent storms threatened the ship between the Embocadero and Cavite. The governor and archbishop vowed to return the image to “its ancient mountains” if they reached port.

After arrival on 22 July, ceremonies began at the Jesuit college church in Manila. Civil and ecclesiastical corporations joined a river procession toward Antipolo. On or about **8 September 1653**, the image was ceremonially restored to its shrine and publicly acclaimed under the title Peace and Good Voyage. A locust infestation was said to disappear upon its entry into Antipolo.

### 1659–1661/1662: *San José*

After galleon losses and disruptions, Manrique de Lara requested that the image again accompany the Acapulco ship. Murillo says it embarked in 1659 and completed a successful journey. Modern chronologies give its return as 1661 or 1662. No exact return document was located.

### 1663 and 1684: narrative relief cycle

By 1663 the Antipolo church reportedly possessed an altarpiece or set of six carved reliefs representing the image’s chief miracles. In 1684 the reliefs were installed on the shrine’s side walls. This is material evidence that a developed visual legend existed by the later seventeenth century. Most were dispersed when the church was destroyed in 1945; at least one survives in the Museo San Agustín.

### 1745: candle fire

For a procession against locusts to Pinagcotaan, the image was removed from its niche and redressed. A candle burned against its clothing for several hours. Pearls burst and gold ornament melted, but Murillo described limited textile damage as miraculous. Ash from the damaged garment was applied to a lesion on rector Juan Anglés’s forehead; Murillo reported an immediate cure. The textile involved is not known to survive.

### 1746–1748: final trans-Pacific voyage

On **6 June 1746**, acting governor Juan de Arechederra removed the image from Antipolo for installation as protector aboard *Nuestra Señora del Pilar*. Manila’s trans-Pacific commerce had been badly disrupted after the British capture of *Nuestra Señora de Covadonga* in 1743.

Arechederra executed a public instrument promising restitution. The Jesuit superior general, **Franz Retz**, objected to removing images from Jesuit residences and on 20 March 1748 ordered the Philippine provincial to recover the Virgin, apparently unaware that it had already returned.

The image arrived back from Mexico on **23 January 1748**. It received artillery salutes and fireworks in Manila, then traveled by *champan* and river procession toward Antipolo. Murillo certified that Arechederra personally restored it to the Antipolo church on **20 February 1748**, clothed in a rich dress and pure-gold crown. The procession took two days and included decorated riverbanks, boats, music, gunfire, banners, and temporary altars.

This is the strongest individual custody link in the image’s early history because Murillo quoted a formal certificate dated 21 February 1748. [Villoria Prieto](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/libro/998656/2.pdf)

### 1748: endowment

Passengers of *Nuestra Señora del Pilar* contributed 500 pesos to an *obra pía*; Arechederra added 600. The capital was to grow to 6,000 pesos, with proceeds used for candles, church furnishings, the image’s cult, the congregation, and local poor. Arechederra also paid for a dress and gold crown. Murillo used related funds to print engravings and novenas in Spanish and Tagalog and commissioned medals in Rome.

### 1768: Jesuit expulsion

The royal decree expelling the Society of Jesus reached the Philippines in 1768. The Antipolo residence and image passed out of Jesuit control.

A Recollect history states:

- secular clergy administered Antipolo after 1768;
- the parish was entrusted to the Augustinian Recollects in 1863;
- the Recollects remained until 1898.

The precise dates and names of every intervening secular custodian have not been reconstructed. [Augustinian Recollect account](https://www.filcatholic.org/the-virgin-of-antipolo-and-the-augustinian-recollects/)

### Earthquakes and rebuilding

The church was seriously damaged in the earthquakes of:

- **1645**;
- **1824**;
- **1863**.

The National Historical Commission marker records these disasters. The image’s exact location during each emergency and the dates of each temporary removal are not publicly documented in the sources found. The Recollects’ first major work after receiving the parish in 1863 was reportedly restoration of the earthquake-damaged church. [NHCP Church of Antipolo registry](https://philhistoricsites.nhcp.gov.ph/registry_database/church-of-antipolo/)

### 1898 and the Philippine Revolution

Recollect administration ended in 1898. No source located for this dossier gives a sufficiently precise account of the statue’s custody during the Revolution, First Philippine Republic, or Philippine-American War. Claims of uninterrupted residence in its niche during those conflicts cannot be confirmed from the evidence examined. This is an important documented gap.

### 1904: Manila visit

A major fluvial translation brought the image to Manila in November 1904. Wenceslao Retana records that on **27 November 1904**, students of the Ateneo de Manila and Normal de San Javier sang part of José Rizal’s *Junto al Pásig* as the river procession passed San Pedro Makati. This is evidence for the object’s physical movement and for the reintegration of a Rizalian colonial school drama into public devotion. [Retana, *Vida y escritos del Dr. José Rizal*](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/P%C3%A1gina%3AVida_y_escritos_del_Dr._Jos%C3%A9_Rizal%2C_por_Wenceslao_Retana.pdf/479)

### 1925–1926: canonical coronation

Pope Pius XI authorized canonical coronation through a decree communicated to Manila Archbishop Michael J. O’Doherty on **18 June 1925**. The solemn coronation occurred in Manila in **November 1926**. Sources differ between **26 November** and **28 November**; the commonly accepted date is 28 November, but the NHCP marker gives the 26th. The liturgical program or original acta should be consulted to settle the discrepancy.

The image was taken from Antipolo to Manila, carried in fluvial procession, and crowned at the Luneta/Rizal Park in a mass public ceremony. This temporarily placed the object under the custody of the Manila archdiocesan organizers before its return to Antipolo. [NHCP image marker](https://philhistoricsites.nhcp.gov.ph/registry_database/nuestra-senora-de-la-paz-y-buen-viaje/)

### 1944–1945: burial, flight, and refuge in Quiapo

This is the best-recorded modern emergency transfer, although many details remain based on custodial testimony.

In 1944 Japanese forces used the Antipolo church as a garrison or arsenal. Chief sacristan **Procopio Ángeles** reportedly:

1. wrapped the statue in a heavy woolen blanket;
2. placed it inside an empty petroleum drum;
3. buried the drum beneath or near a kitchen adjoining the church.

As battle approached, Ángeles and local devotees exhumed it on **19 February 1945**. Its route is reported as:

- Antipolo;
- Sitio Colaique/Kolaike, on the Angono boundary;
- Angono;
- Santolan, then part of Pasig;
- Pasig town;
- the Ocampo-Santiago residence on Hidalgo Street, Quiapo;
- Quiapo Church.

The named private keeper was **Rosario Alejandro**, daughter of Pablo Ocampo. The image remained at Quiapo Church alongside the Black Nazarene for the remainder of the war. Allied bombardment destroyed the Antipolo church after the original had been evacuated.

The statue returned to Antipolo on **15 October 1945**, initially to a temporary church. Its wartime route later supplied the historical template for annual Quiapo–Antipolo rituals. [Manila Bulletin account](https://mb.com.ph/2022/12/07/the-nazarene-in-antipolo/); [Abrera, “City Rituals”](https://www.siefhome.org/downloads/wg/ry/RY13-1%282024%29-City%20Rituals.pdf)

### 1948–1954: reconstructed shrine

A national fundraising committee was organized after the war. Architect **José L. de Ocampo** designed the reconstructed shrine, completed in 1954. The image was installed within the new building; no source found describes a major restoration of the statue itself during this work.

On **14 January 1954**, the bishops of the Philippines declared the church the National Shrine of Our Lady of Peace and Good Voyage. Later official reporting calls it the first national shrine in the Philippines and Southeast Asia, though the exact wording and legal scope of the 1954 episcopal act merit consultation in the original CBCP records.

### 1983: cathedral custody

Pope John Paul II created the Diocese of Antipolo by the apostolic constitution published in the *Acta Apostolicae Sedis*. The parish church containing the image was raised to cathedral rank when the diocese was canonically erected on **25 June 1983**. Custody consequently became diocesan as well as parochial and shrine-based. [Acta Apostolicae Sedis 75 (1983)](https://www.vatican.va/archive/aas/documents/AAS-75-1983-I-ocr.pdf)

### 2023–2024: international shrine

Following a petition supported by the Catholic Bishops’ Conference of the Philippines, the Holy See elevated the cathedral-shrine to international-shrine status. The decree took effect on **25 March 2023**, the anniversary of the image’s 1626 departure from Acapulco. The solemn declaration took place on **26 January 2024**.

On 26 February 2024, Archbishop Rino Fisichella placed before the image a **Golden Rose given by Pope Francis**. This rose is a separate associated object and should not be confused with a physical alteration of the statue. [Dicastery for Evangelization](https://www.evangelizatio.va/content/pcpne/en/news/antipolo-messa-santuario.html); [Vatican News](https://www.vaticannews.va/en/church/news/2024-02/fisichella-says-mass-at-first-filippino-international-shrine.html)

### 2026: quadricentennial

The Diocese of Antipolo inaugurated a year-long commemoration of 400 years since the image’s 1626 arrival and the centenary of its canonical coronation. The original remains in active use; separate pilgrim replicas are employed for some extended journeys. [Philippine News Agency](https://www.pna.gov.ph/photos/80923)

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is the present statue the 1626 object?

The shrine, diocese, Philippine cultural authorities, and continuous devotional tradition identify it as the statue brought from Acapulco in 1626. Several features support continuity:

- the form is consistent with an early colonial *de bastidor* image;
- visible facial and neck cuts correspond to the damage described by Murillo in 1749;
- nineteenth- and early twentieth-century images portray a substantially similar object;
- the original was deliberately removed before the 1945 destruction, explaining its survival;
- named wartime custodians and a specific route connect its prewar and postwar locations.

However, the public evidentiary record lacks:

- a continuous, object-specific inventory from 1626 onward;
- technical comparison of the present image with the oldest securely dated visual representations;
- dendrochronological or radiocarbon dating;
- wood-species or provenance analysis;
- X-radiography documenting joins, replacements, nails, internal framework, or later interventions;
- a published conservation history.

Thus continuity is **strong institutional tradition supported by circumstantial historical evidence**, not laboratory-proven identity.

### Replacement or substantial rebuilding

No source examined alleges a documented wholesale replacement of the statue. Nevertheless, *de bastidor* images are inherently composite and repairable. Heads, hands, internal frames, wigs, garments, crowns, and pedestals can have distinct histories. Without technical examination, it is impossible to state that every present component dates from 1626.

At minimum, the clothing and much jewelry have been repeatedly replaced. The extent to which the mannequin framework, pedestal, fingers, hair, surface coatings, or crown have been renewed is unresolved.

### Mexican origin

“Brought from Mexico” is documented tradition; “made in Mexico” is a reasonable but unproven inference. Its style, wood, and technique have not been tied publicly to a named Mexican workshop or regional school.

### Tipolo pedestal

The pedestal’s identity as wood from the tree where the image repeatedly appeared is a pious and institutional tradition. No scientific dating or species verification was located. Even if the wood is *Artocarpus blancoi*, that would not establish the disappearance legend.

### 1639 scars

The marks exist, but their identification as 1639 lance cuts rests upon historical tradition. No forensic dating is available. Murillo’s 1749 description proves that a spear-attack story circulated by then and may derive from earlier Jesuit records; it does not independently prove the physical marks’ cause.

### Fire survival

The image has two distinct fire traditions:

1. attempted burning during the 1639 uprising;
2. the accidental candle fire of 1745.

Later summaries sometimes conflate them. Murillo distinguished the episodes. In the first, the wooden image allegedly survived a bonfire; in the second, its ornaments and garments were damaged while the figure and inner tunic were spared. No physical fire-science examination has tested either claim.

### Voyage counts

The number of ocean crossings is disputed in modern retellings:

- some speak of six successful round trips;
- others of eight voyages, including aborted sailings;
- some lists omit or combine journeys;
- secondary sources contain obvious date errors.

A definitive reconstruction requires consultation of Manila galleon sailing records, Cavite church records, and Jesuit house diaries for each embarkation and return. The safest formulation is that the statue made **multiple documented or traditionally documented Pacific voyages between 1641 and 1748**, including at least one aborted departure, and was repeatedly used as a shipboard patron.

### Authority to authenticate

Authority has shifted:

- seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: governor-general, archbishop, Jesuit custodians, and witnesses to translations;
- after 1768: secular clergy and later Augustinian Recollects;
- twentieth century: Archdiocese of Manila, Philippine bishops, shrine administration, and the Holy See for cultic recognition;
- since 1983: Diocese of Antipolo;
- art-historical description: Cultural Center of the Philippines and scholars such as Regalado Trota Jose and Monina Mercado.

Canonical coronation and shrine status authenticate and regulate **veneration**, not the statue’s wood species, workshop, or absolute date. No ecclesiastical decree located claims to be a scientific authentication of its 1626 material identity.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Shipboard patronage

Its defining historical use was as a movable patron image aboard Manila–Acapulco galleons. The object was not simply represented on the ship: sources say the statue itself was physically removed from Antipolo or Cavite, embarked, carried across the Pacific, and returned. Governors, archbishops, generals, sailors, and passengers prayed before it during storms and fires.

The statue thereby acquired a double function:

- **apotropaic:** protection from storm, fire, shipwreck, conflict, and disorder;
- **administrative-political:** ceremonial “queen and governor” of the vessel, integrating sacred patronage with colonial government and commercial risk.

Murillo repeatedly contrasted peaceful voyages made with the image and disasters suffered without it. That is devotional causal reasoning, not statistical proof, but it demonstrates how the image functioned in the institutional imagination of the galleon system.

### Military protection

During the Dutch attack on Cavite in 1647, the commander entrusted the port to the Virgin and organized prayers. Victory was credited to her. The association made the statue an emblem of Spanish Catholic defense as well as maritime security.

Its later baton or command staff retained this political-military register. The baton is an offering and symbol, not evidence that the statue was formally commissioned as a military image.

### Agricultural ritual

The image was carried in processions against locusts, including the proposed 1745 procession to Pinagcotaan. Murillo also attributed the disappearance of a locust swarm to its 1653 arrival in Antipolo. Such processions placed it within the agricultural cycle and connected it to subsistence, weather, and fertility as well as oceanic travel.

### Healing

Reported healing practices included:

- taking splinters from the tipolo pedestal;
- applying ash from the 1745 burned vesture;
- prayers for illness;
- petitions from mothers for sick children;
- votive gifts after recovery.

The material extraction of pedestal splinters reportedly became extensive enough that custodians covered or protected the wood. This is a documented consequence of belief even though the cures themselves remain pious claims.

### Processions and translations

Historically attested forms include:

- formal procession to Manila Cathedral after the 1626 arrival;
- removal to Manila following the 1639 uprising;
- transfers to and from Cavite;
- fluvial translations on the Pasig River;
- ceremonial return to Antipolo in 1653;
- the spectacular 1748 river procession;
- Manila fluvial procession in 1904;
- transfer to Manila for the 1926 coronation;
- wartime return from Quiapo in 1945;
- present annual visit to Quiapo and penitential walk back to Antipolo.

### Pilgrimage season

The modern pilgrimage season, **Ahunan sa Antipolo** (“going up to Antipolo”), begins around the end of April and first days of May. In the ritual called **Pagdalaw ng Ina sa Anak** or **Dalaw ng Ina sa Anak** (“the Mother’s Visit to her Son”), an image of Our Lady of Antipolo goes to Quiapo Church, where the Black Nazarene is venerated. A penitential night walk then covers roughly 33 kilometres from Quiapo to Antipolo.

Accounts often call the traveling image “the image” without specifying whether it is always the seventeenth-century original. Because the shrine maintains replicas and pilgrim images, object identity must be checked for each year. The Quiapo Church’s 2026 report records a dawn motorcade to Quiapo on 28 April followed by the night pilgrimage and arrival at Antipolo at 4:22 a.m. [Quiapo Church, 2026 observance](https://quiapochurch.com.ph/news/a-mothers-visit-to-her-son-quiapo-and-antipolo-united-in-a-yearly-pilgrimage-of-faith-and-peace/)

The annual rite consciously reenacts the 1945 refuge in Quiapo and return “up” to Antipolo, although mass May pilgrimage to Antipolo has older roots. Treating the entire modern form as an unchanged seventeenth-century practice would be inaccurate.

### Vehicle blessings and modern travel

The title has expanded from galleons to steamships, railways, motor vehicles, aircraft, and overseas migration. Motorists commonly bring new vehicles to Antipolo for blessing. The practice preserves the object’s protective function while translating “good voyage” into modern mobility.

### Canonical coronation

Canonical coronation in 1926 placed papally authorized crowns on the Virgin and associated regalia. It recognized an established cult; it did not manufacture the cult or certify the wood’s age.

### Access

The image is publicly visible above the main altar but physically controlled by shrine personnel. Handling, dressing, movement, and close access are restricted. No published detailed access protocol or modern conservation-handling manual was located.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The storm and shipboard fire of 1626

**Story:** Tabora took the image as the vessel’s queen and protectress. *El Almirante* encountered storm and fire but reached Manila safely.

**Earliest located substantial source:** Murillo Velarde, 1749.

**Historical core:** the voyage and the image’s presence are strongly traditional; the storm and fire are plausible but were not verified in a contemporary log.

**Function:** established the statue as a protector of Pacific crossings.

### The image chooses the tipolo tree

**Story:** while the Jesuits built a church, the Virgin repeatedly vanished and appeared in a tipolo tree. The church was moved, the tree cut, and its wood made into a pedestal.

**Variants:** some say the image disappeared nightly; others merely “several times.” Some emphasize that the tree gave Antipolo its name; the town’s tree-derived name may predate the legend’s mature form.

**Tier:** later pious tradition, exact first attestation unresolved.

**Material consequence:** shrine location, pedestal identification, healing splinter practice, and the fusion of the Virgin with Antipolo’s local landscape.

### Survival of the 1639 fire

**Story:** Chinese insurgents stabbed the image and cast it into a bonfire, but it did not burn. Murillo compared it to Moses’ burning bush.

**Earliest securely located narrative:** Murillo Velarde, 1749, describing an event of 1639.

**Possible factual core:** church destruction, removal of cult objects, and marks on the statue. The supernatural immunity is a devotional interpretation.

**Later elaboration:** visible cuts became permanent relic-like proof of the event. Colonial versions often removed the uprising’s fiscal and political causes and converted it into a drama of heathen assault and Christian triumph.

### Patroness of the galleons

**Story:** every crossing with the Virgin ended safely, peace prevailed aboard, and voyages without it suffered disaster.

**Evidence:** several voyages are named by Murillo; later voyage totals conflict. The universal claim exceeds what can presently be demonstrated.

**Function:** justified repeated removal from Antipolo, mobilized public processions, and linked Jesuit devotion to imperial commerce.

### Defense of Cavite

**Story:** prayers before the Virgin defeated the Dutch fleet in 1647.

**Historical core:** Dutch operations against Cavite and Spanish resistance are historical; the causal attribution is religious.

**Interest served:** sacralized colonial defense and strengthened the image’s public, military character.

### Vanishing locusts

**Story:** locusts threatening Antipolo disappeared when the image arrived in 1653; later it was processed to protect fields from locusts.

**Tier:** eighteenth-century attested miracle narrative.

**Historical consequence:** agricultural processions and offerings.

### The candle and the cancer

**Story:** in 1745 a candle burned the Virgin’s jewelry and clothing without consuming the image. Ash from the damaged garment cured rector Juan Anglés’s malignant forehead lesion.

**Tier:** near-contemporary Jesuit miracle report.

**Material history:** the episode is unusually concrete about damaged gold, pearls, and fabric. The garment and ash cannot now be identified.

### Justiniano Asunción paints on his knees

**Story:** commissioned to paint the Virgin, Asunción repeatedly failed to capture its living expression. An old man advised him to kneel and pray while painting; only then did he achieve the likeness.

**Tier:** nineteenth-century episode preserved as oral tradition in later art history.

**Historical core:** Asunción made a painting of the Virgin; the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas holds a work under that title. The supernatural or admonitory details are not independently documented. [National Museum/Javellana study](https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/The-Philippine-Colonial-Tradition-of-Sacred-Art-from-the-BSP-and-NMP-Rene-B.-Javellana-SJ.pdf); [BSP collection catalogue](https://www.bsp.gov.ph/Media_And_Research/Publications/Tanaw.pdf)

### José Rizal and his mother’s vow

**Story:** Teodora Alonso vowed to take the child José Rizal to Antipolo if she survived a difficult delivery. Rizal and his father fulfilled the vow in 1868.

**Tier:** biographical tradition with a specific pilgrimage date, 6 June 1868.

**Later significance:** Rizal’s *Junto al Pásig* incorporated the Virgin’s river procession into a drama about Catholic and precolonial spiritual claims.

### Wartime deliverance

**Story:** devotees saved the Virgin from Japanese occupation and Allied bombing through burial and clandestine movement.

**Tier:** modern historical memory supported by named keepers, route traditions, and the demonstrable survival of the statue despite the shrine’s destruction.

**Later effect:** annual Quiapo–Antipolo rites consciously reproduce the wartime custody route.

## 8. Current Status and Location

The original image is presently enthroned at:

**International Shrine of Our Lady of Peace and Good Voyage–Immaculate Conception Parish**  
Antipolo Cathedral  
P. Oliveros Street, Antipolo City, Rizal, Philippines

It is:

- extant;
- actively venerated;
- ordinarily displayed in the principal sanctuary or retablo;
- periodically dressed and ceremonially moved;
- controlled by the Roman Catholic Diocese of Antipolo and cathedral-shrine administration;
- without a published museum accession number.

The Holy See’s international-shrine decree took effect on 25 March 2023. The solemn public declaration followed on 26 January 2024. Vatican reporting describes it as the first international shrine in the Philippines, the eleventh worldwide at the time, the third in Asia, and the first Marian international shrine in Asia. Later additions to the worldwide list do not alter its historic priority in the Philippines. [Vatican News](https://www.vaticannews.va/en/church/news/2024-02/fisichella-says-mass-at-first-filippino-international-shrine.html)

A Golden Rose from Pope Francis has been placed before it since 26 February 2024.

No present ownership or repatriation litigation was found. The possibility that the image was originally removed from an Acapulco parish without a documented permanent transfer is a provenance question, but no Mexican ecclesiastical, governmental, or community claimant was identified.

No recent published scientific examination or conservation campaign was located. Its active cultic use—dressing, exposure, processions, incense, lighting, climate changes, and transport—creates conservation concerns, but it would be speculative to diagnose its condition without a technical report.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Reproductions and pilgrim images

Copies circulate in Philippine parishes, family shrines, chaplaincies, migrant communities, and Marian galleries overseas. A replica was given to the Marian sanctuary at Torreciudad, Spain, in 1995. Such replicas extend the title but do not share the original’s custody history. [Torreciudad](https://torreciudad.org/galeria-mariana/ntra-sra-de-antipolo-de-la-paz/)

The existence of replicas also creates reporting ambiguity. A news story may say “Our Lady of Antipolo traveled to Albay” while specifying only later that the visitor was a **pilgrim image**. [Philippine Information Agency](https://pia.gov.ph/features/our-lady-of-antipolo-na-tinaguriang-patron-ng-paglalakbay-nasa-albay/)

### Prints, medals, and novenas

Murillo’s 1748 program included:

- engravings of the image;
- a novena in Spanish;
- translation into the “language of the natives,” identified in modern discussion as Tagalog;
- medals struck in Rome.

These were early technologies of replication that allowed the image’s presence and protective reputation to circulate without physically moving the statue.

### Painting

Justiniano Asunción’s nineteenth-century *Virgin of Antipolo* became an important painted interpretation. The Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas collection includes a work under this title. The attendant kneeling legend reinforced the belief that the image’s face could not be represented adequately without devotion.

The Virgin also entered later Philippine painting, devotional printmaking, calendar art, roadside shrines, and commercial religious statuary. The large number of copies makes a complete catalogue impracticable without dedicated collection research.

### Literature and theater

José Rizal’s verse melodrama **_Junto al Pásig_**, first staged at the Ateneo Municipal in 1880, presents children awaiting the Virgin’s fluvial procession. A figure called Diwata attempts to turn Leonido from the Marian celebration; angels defeat the demonic forces, and the children salute the Virgin as queen of the sea and sign of peace.

The work is important because it puts the Antipolo procession inside an early literary negotiation of colonial Catholicism, indigeneity, and education. A portion was performed during the real 1904 procession, making literature part of the object’s later ritual history. [Study and edition in *Revista Filipina*](https://revista.carayanpress.com/resources/Zarzuela-Rizal.pdf); [Spanish text](https://www.textos.info/jose-rizal/junto-al-pasig/pdf)

### Film

**_Himala ng Birhen_**, also known as **_Himala ng Birhen ng Antipolo_** or **_Sa Antipolo_**, was released in 1947, directed by Susana C. de Guzmán and starring Rosa del Rosario and Rogelio de la Rosa. Its appearance shortly after the wartime destruction and return belongs to the shrine’s postwar cultural reconstruction. Detailed production and reception history requires consultation of Philippine film archives; surviving online summaries are sparse.

### National, civic, and diasporic identity

The image became:

- the defining emblem of Antipolo as a pilgrimage city;
- a patron of motorists and travelers;
- a Marian reference point for overseas Filipino workers;
- a symbol of trans-Pacific Philippine–Mexican history;
- an emblem through which wartime survival is locally narrated.

The title’s application to overseas workers is a modern extension from seafaring to migration. It is not an original seventeenth-century patronage.

### Pilgrimage economy

Pilgrimage supports transport services, food stalls, religious goods, vehicle-blessing services, lodging, and municipal tourism. The conflict between devotion, traffic control, and revenue has periodically become legal. In 1960 an Antipolo vice mayor and police chief were sentenced to jail for refusing to obey an order against collecting fees from pilgrimage motorists. That dispute concerned civic management around devotion, not ownership of the statue.

### Quadricentennial reception

The 2026 observance explicitly joins two anniversaries:

- 400 years since the Acapulco departure and Philippine arrival;
- 100 years since canonical coronation.

This anniversary framework further consolidates “1626” as the image’s public origin date, even though its manufacture and pre-Acapulco history remain unknown.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Primary and near-primary foundation

The chief printed source is Pedro Murillo Velarde’s:

*Historia de la Provincia de Philipinas de la Compañía de Jesús: Segunda parte, que comprehende los progresos de esta provincia desde el año de 1616 hasta el de 1716* (Manila: Imprenta de la Compañía de Jesús, Nicolás de la Cruz Bagay, 1749).

Despite its stated endpoint of 1716, the printed work and associated Marian history include later material connected with Murillo’s Antipolo rectorship and the 1748 restitution. It preserves names, vessels, dates, vows, ceremonies, miracle stories, and the text of Murillo’s certificate. It is both historical evidence and Jesuit devotional advocacy.

Murillo’s account must be used critically because:

- it postdates the 1626 and 1639 events;
- it interprets events through miracle theology;
- it advances the prestige of a Jesuit shrine;
- its hostile description of Chinese insurgents reflects colonial conflict;
- later editions often reproduce it without adding independent evidence.

### Archival traces

The modern Spanish study cites:

- **Archivum Romanum Societatis Iesu**, *Congre. 4 No.es, Philip. 3*, catalogue of Philippine houses begun in 1737, fol. 309v;
- **Archivo Histórico Nacional**, Códices, libro 340, fol. 295r, concerning General Franz Retz’s recovery order;
- **Archivo Histórico Nacional**, Códices, libro 1269 and libro 340, fol. 37, concerning the *obra pía*;
- Murillo’s certificate of 21 February 1748.

These citations indicate that a fuller archival reconstruction is possible. The relevant documents are not all digitized or transcribed in full in the sources used here.

### Nineteenth-century editions

Separate *Historia de la Virgen* editions appeared in Manila and Binondo in 1847, 1866, 1876, and 1887. Modern scholarship warns that these are extracts from Murillo rather than independent records. They remain important for studying how the narrative was edited and circulated in the nineteenth century.

### Major modern studies

Monina A. Mercado’s **_Antipolo: A Shrine to Our Lady_** (1980) is repeatedly cited for physical description, voyage history, wounds, regalia, coronation, and traditions. Regalado Trota Jose’s **_Simbahan: Church Art in Colonial Philippines, 1565–1898_** (1991) supplies the art-historical classification of the image as *de bastidor*. Both underpin the Cultural Center of the Philippines entry.

Carlos Villoria Prieto’s recent Spanish-language study returns to Murillo and cited archives, providing the most detailed accessible reconstruction of voyages, Jesuit promotion, Arechederra’s endowment, and the 1748 ceremonies.

Maria Bernadette Abrera and Mark Tallara examine the image’s ritual geography, wartime movements, Quiapo relationship, and May pilgrimage. These are especially valuable for distinguishing the shrine’s historical custody from the later social memory embodied in annual walking ritual.

### Colonial and postcolonial interpretations

Christina H. Lee’s scholarship reads the localization of the originally Spanish-galleon devotion among Tagalogs and Aetas as a reinterpretive process, possibly connected with indigenous sacred-tree traditions and resistance. Villoria Prieto disputes an overly resistance-centered interpretation and emphasizes Hispanic Catholic devotional continuities.

The evidence supports at least two simultaneous processes:

1. Jesuit and colonial authorities actively promoted a Marian protector of galleons, defense, and public order.
2. Local populations adopted and reinterpreted the image through landscape, tree, healing, agricultural, and pilgrimage practices.

The available sources do not justify reducing the cult to either colonial imposition or indigenous resistance alone.

### Material-study deficit

The central scholarly weakness is the absence of published technical study of the object. Major unresolved questions include:

- precise wood species;
- date of carving;
- Mexican regional workshop;
- internal construction and repairs;
- age of surface coatings;
- whether the present pedestal is seventeenth-century tipolo wood;
- chronology of crowns and jewelry;
- degree of replacement after earthquakes and war;
- comparison with New Spanish images at Pátzcuaro, Zapotlán, Talpa, and elsewhere.

A scientific campaign combining high-resolution photography, X-radiography, CT scanning, wood anatomy, pigment and coating analysis, textile study, metal assay, and archival inventory comparison could substantially refine the dossier without requiring destructive dating.

### Frequently repeated errors

- **“Made from tipolo wood.”** The pedestal, not the exposed head and hands, is traditionally tied to the tipolo tree.
- **“The image arrived 18 July 1626” as uncontested fact.** A modern Spanish reconstruction gives 22 June.
- **“Eight successful round trips.”** Some voyages were aborted; counts and dates vary.
- **“The Jesuits gave it to the Recollects in 1768.”** The transition included secular administration; Recollect custody in Antipolo is placed in 1863–1898.
- **“The church survived the Second World War.”** The statue survived because it was evacuated; the old church was destroyed.
- **“Canonical coronation proves the statue is authentic.”** Coronation recognizes cult, not material provenance.
- **“The original travels on every modern provincial visit.”** Some visits explicitly use a pilgrim replica.

- Pedro Murillo Velarde, *Historia de la Provincia de Philipinas de la Compañía de Jesús* (Manila, 1749). This is the principal early narrative, including the 1639 attack, galleon voyages, 1653 restitution, miracles, and 1748 ceremonies.
- Murillo-derived *Historia de la Virgen de la Paz y del Buen Viaje* editions (1847, 1866, 1876, 1887), important as reception history but not independent seventeenth-century testimony.
- Wenceslao E. Retana, *Vida y escritos del Dr. José Rizal*, documenting the 1904 river procession and performance of *Junto al Pásig*.
- *Acta Apostolicae Sedis* 75 (1983), recording the erection of the Diocese of Antipolo and cathedral status of the church.
- Holy See/Dicastery documentation and reporting for international-shrine status and the 2024 Golden Rose.

### Evidentiary limitations

The strongest historical documentation concerns the Jesuit period as reconstructed by Murillo and the 1746–1748 voyage, for which a formal restitution certificate and cited archival correspondence survive. The weakest links are:

- manufacture and pre-1626 ownership;
- the exact Acapulco parish and transfer mechanism;
- contemporary documentation of the 1626 voyage;
- exact first attestation of the tipolo-tree legend;
- complete vessel-by-vessel voyage chronology;
- custody during the Revolution and Philippine-American War;
- inventories of lost jewelry and vestments;
- technical conservation and scientific analysis;
- component-level continuity of the modern statue.

These are genuine lacunae and should not be filled by devotional repetition alone.
