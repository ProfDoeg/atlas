# Andvaranaut, Andvari's cursed ring: Object Dossier

**File name:** `andvaranaut_object_dossier.md`  
**Status as of:** 6 September 2026

## Evidentiary note

Andvaranaut is a **legendary literary object**, not an archaeologically authenticated artifact. No physical ring has a documented provenance connecting it to Andvari, Loki, the Völsungs, or the Niflungs. Consequently, “custody,” “location,” and “use” below ordinarily mean custody, location, and use **inside particular medieval narratives**.

The evidence must be divided into four categories:

1. **Surviving medieval texts and manuscripts:** physically real documents containing stories about the ring.
2. **Earlier oral or poetic material reconstructed from those documents:** plausible but not directly preserved at the proposed earlier dates.
3. **Medieval visual evidence:** carvings that may depict the ring or a cognate episode but do not name it.
4. **Modern adaptations and substitutes:** Wagner’s ring, Tolkien’s One Ring, replicas, commercial jewelry, and fictional objects bearing the name. These are receptions, not continuations of the medieval object.

The three principal Icelandic witnesses do not tell precisely the same story:

- The prose frame of *Reginsmál* names the ring but its verse curse concerns **Andvari’s gold or treasure collectively**.
- Snorri Sturluson’s *Skáldskaparmál* singles out the ring as wealth-producing and initially curses the ring itself, after which Loki extends the doom to the ring and gold together.
- *Völsunga saga* calls it a gold ring, incorporates it into Sigurðr and Brynhildr’s oath and bridal deception, and makes it evidence that exposes Sigurðr’s disguise. It does not repeatedly explain later deaths by invoking the curse.

That distinction is central. The familiar modern formula “a magic ring whose curse kills every successive owner” is a synthesis and intensification of the medieval variants, not the wording or narrative emphasis of any one source.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Classification

Andvaranaut is a legendary **gold ring or circular gold ornament**, initially part of the dwarf Andvari’s treasure. Depending on the text, it functions as:

- a retained and especially valued treasure;
- a supernatural means of increasing wealth;
- an object placed into a blood-compensation payment;
- a bearer of a fatal pronouncement;
- part of Fáfnir’s dragon-hoard;
- an oath, betrothal, bridal, or identity token;
- evidence exposing Sigurðr’s substitution for Gunnarr;
- and, ultimately, an element of the lost Niflung treasure.

It is not described as a signet ring. No medieval source supplies an inscription, gemstone, rune, maker’s mark, exact form, diameter, weight, purity, or construction technique.

### Names and spellings

The normalized Old Norse name is **Andvaranautr**. The accusative form appearing in narrative passages is **Andvaranaut**. Modern reference works frequently use the uninflected-looking *Andvaranaut* as the English headword.

Recorded or modernized forms include:

- Old Norse: *Andvaranautr*; accusative *Andvaranaut*
- Modern Icelandic: *Andvaranautur* may occur in grammatical modernization
- Norwegian/Danish/Swedish adaptations: *Andvaranaut*, *Andvarenaut*
- German: *Andvaranaut*, sometimes *Andwaranaut*
- Variant popular spelling: *Andvarinaut*

The name is a compound of the genitive **Andvara**, “of Andvari,” and **nautr**. The latter appears in Old Norse object names denoting something acquired from, associated with, or once possessed by the person named in the first element. Cleasby–Vigfusson gives comparable compounds for rings, garments, gloves, ships, and swords and glosses *Andvara-nautr* as the “charmed ring” associated with Andvari. “Andvari’s gift,” “Andvari’s possession,” and “Andvari’s treasure” are defensible contextual renderings; “Andvari’s need” is an etymological mistake caused by confusing *nautr* with *nauðr*, “need/distress.” [Cleasby–Vigfusson, *nautr*](https://norroen.info/dct/cleasby/n.html)

The dwarf’s own name, *Andvari*, is less securely explained. Dictionary tradition relates it to watchfulness or vigilance and also records *andvari* as a fish-name. Confident translations such as “the wary one” should therefore be treated as conventional rather than certain. [Cleasby–Vigfusson, *Andvari*](https://norroen.info/dct/cleasby/a2.html)

### Description by source

#### *Reginsmál*, prose frame, preserved in Codex Regius, ca. 1270

The narrator calls it simply **“one ring”** (*einn hringr*) retained after Andvari has surrendered the rest of his gold. Loki takes it. When one whisker of the otter skin remains uncovered, Óðinn produces “the ring Andvaranaut” and places it over the hair.

The prose does not state:

- who made it;
- whether Andvari made it;
- whether it bears a stone or inscription;
- its dimensions;
- whether it is worn on a finger or arm;
- or whether it can create gold.

The term *hringr* can designate a ring, while the related *baugr* can cover both finger-rings and larger circular ornaments or arm-rings. Modern illustrations that give Andvaranaut a gemstone, runes, knotwork, or a heavy masculine signet form add details absent from this witness. The Old Norse text and a Norwegian translation are available in the *Reginsmál* edition hosted by Heimskringla.no. [*Reginsmál*](https://www.heimskringla.no/wiki/Reginsm%C3%A5l)

#### Snorri, *Skáldskaparmál*, composed ca. 1220; surviving manuscripts principally fourteenth century and later

Snorri supplies the most specific early description: Andvari conceals **“one small gold ring”**—*einum litlum gullbaug*. It appears beautiful to Óðinn: *sýndisk honum fagr*. Andvari says that if he retains it, he can **increase wealth for himself from the ring**: *lézk mega œxla sér fé af bauginum ef hann heldi*.

This is the firm medieval basis for calling the ring wealth-producing. It is not said to locate gold, reveal mines, control other rings, confer invisibility, or grant universal power. Those are later extrapolations or adaptations.

Snorri subsequently describes it as a *gullbaugr*, “gold ring/hoop,” when Sigurðr gives it to Brynhildr as *línfé*, a bridal or morning gift. Guðrún later calls the object on Brynhildr’s hand *Andvaranautr*. [Anthony Faulkes’s critical edition, *Edda: Skáldskaparmál*](https://vsnr.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Edda-2_skaldskaparmal_combined.pdf)

#### *Völsunga saga*, composed in the later thirteenth century; principal manuscript ca. 1400

The saga repeatedly calls it a **gold ring**, *gullhringr*, and explicitly places it on a hand. After Sigurðr has earlier given it to Brynhildr, he returns disguised as Gunnarr and “took from her the ring Andvaranaut, which he had given her.” Guðrún later shows it to Brynhildr, who recognizes it. The context now strongly supports a wearable personal ring and not merely an arm-ring or bullion hoop.

The saga gives no supernatural manufacturing description and does not repeat Snorri’s claim that the ring multiplies wealth. [Old Norse *Völsunga saga*](https://www.heimskringla.no/wiki/V%C3%B6lsunga_saga)

### What is not established

No reliable source establishes:

- that Andvari forged the ring;
- that it came from a named mine, river, or workshop;
- that dwarfs collectively manufactured it;
- that it had a jewel;
- that runes were engraved on it;
- that it made its wearer invisible;
- that it controlled other rings;
- that it compelled greed or consciously manipulated owners;
- that the ring rather than the treasure caused every death in the cycle;
- or that any surviving ring is Andvaranaut.

The common phrase “Andvari’s cursed ring” is a useful modern identifier, but in the oldest recoverable version the doom is at least as much a curse or fatal prediction concerning the **whole hoard** as an enchantment lodged exclusively in one object.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Mythic origin

The medieval sources begin with Andvari already possessing the ring and treasure. They do not narrate its manufacture.

In *Reginsmál*, Andvari lives in or frequents the waterfall Andvarafors in fish form. Loki borrows Rán’s net, catches him, and forces him to surrender the “fire of the river”—gold—as the price of his life. After surrendering the hoard, Andvari attempts to retain one ring. Loki takes it, and Andvari withdraws into the rock while pronouncing the treasure’s destructive future.

Snorri relocates the encounter to **Svartálfaheimr** and says Andvari keeps his gold in his stone or rock dwelling. Loki physically catches him while he is a fish and imposes a ransom of all the gold in the rock. Nothing in Snorri says the ring was forged after the gold was stolen or that it was made from Rhine-gold. Those are Wagnerian developments.

### Earliest possible visual attestation: U 1163 Drävle

Several late Viking-Age Swedish stones depict episodes from the Sigurðr cycle. The most important for the ring is runestone **U 1163**, originally from Drävle in Uppland and now at Göksbo. It is commonly dated to the later eleventh century, although catalogues differ in precision. Its imagery includes Sigurðr stabbing the serpent and a running figure holding a large round object.

Interpretations of the ring-bearing figure include:

- **Andvari with the ring**;
- **Sigurðr carrying the ring toward a valkyrie**;
- or another narrative figure.

The stone’s runic inscription is an ordinary memorial text for Erinbjǫrn and does not name Andvari, Sigurðr, or the ring. Consequently, U 1163 is the earliest possible representation of Andvaranaut, but not a secure named attestation. The Swedish runic catalogue describes the imagery as Sigurðr, Fáfnir, Andvari, and Sigrdrífa, whereas more cautious research says the round-object identification remains interpretive. [U 1163 catalogue entry](https://www.runinskrifter.net/signum/U/1163) [NorseMap object record](https://www.norsemap.eu/s/main/item/521)

Related stones Gs 2 and Gs 9 also contain or once contained figures holding rings, but their interpretation is similarly dependent on comparison with the literary cycle. Marjolein Stern identifies the figure on U 1163 as Sigurðr presenting the ring to the valkyrie, while documenting competing readings. [Stern, *Runestone Images and Visual Communication*](https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/14291/1/M_Stern_Thesis_without_images_new.pdf)

### Earliest securely datable explicit textual attestation

The earliest surviving manuscript that explicitly names Andvaranaut is **Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda, GKS 2365 4to**, copied in Iceland in the second half of the thirteenth century, conventionally ca. 1270. In the prose following stanza 5 of *Reginsmál*, the text says:

> *Þá dró Óðinn fram hringinn Andvaranaut ok hulði hárit.*

“Then Óðinn brought out the ring Andvaranaut and covered the hair.”

Codex Regius belonged to Bishop Brynjólfur Sveinsson, who gave it to King Frederik III of Denmark in 1662. It remained in Copenhagen until returned to Iceland in 1971. Its manuscript history establishes the physical survival of the text, not of the ring. [Editiones Arnamagnæanæ description of Codex Regius](https://eae.ku.dk/q?p=eae%2Fvols%2Ftext%2F1) [Árni Magnússon Institute manuscript overview](https://www.arnastofnun.is/is/merk-handrit-i-naermynd)

The verse components of *Reginsmál* may be substantially older than the manuscript. Proposed dates extend into the Viking Age, but the work is composite: prose frames and poems were assembled by a medieval compiler, and the name **Andvaranaut occurs in prose, not in Andvari’s metrical curse**. It is therefore unsafe to date the explicit name to the tenth century merely because some verses may be that old.

### Snorri’s earlier composition, later witnesses

Snorri probably composed the *Edda* around 1220, making his written treatment potentially earlier than the copying of Codex Regius. No authorial manuscript survives. The earliest major surviving witness, Uppsala University Library **DG 11 4to**, was copied in the first quarter of the fourteenth century. Its independent and abbreviated redaction preserves the wealth-producing small gold ring and the fatal pronouncement. [Viking Society, *The Uppsala Edda*](https://vsnr.org/editions/the-uppsala-edda-dg-11-4to/)

Thus two different meanings of “earliest” must be retained:

- **Earliest surviving explicit physical witness:** Codex Regius, ca. 1270.
- **Probable earliest composition of an extant work narrating the ring:** Snorri’s *Edda*, ca. 1220, reconstructed through later manuscripts.

### Continental comparison: *Nibelungenlied*

The Middle High German *Nibelungenlied*, composed around 1200, contains a golden finger-ring taken from Brünhilt by Sîfrit and later shown by Kriemhilt as evidence. It does not call this ring Andvaranaut, give it to a dwarf, associate it with a wealth-producing power, or place Andvari’s curse on it.

The *Nibelungenlied* ring is originally Brünhilt’s own jewelry and is distinct from the Nibelung hoard. The formal resemblance lies in its evidentiary role during the queens’ quarrel. It is a cognate or parallel narrative object, not a documented earlier custody stage of Andvaranaut. The German epic survives in numerous manuscripts; the principal A, B, and C witnesses are recognized in UNESCO’s Memory of the World register. [German UNESCO Commission](https://www.unesco.de/staette/nibelungenlied/) [Vienna *Nibelungenlied* manuscript synopsis](https://www.germ.univie.ac.at/nibelungenwerkstatt/files/nibelungenwerkstatt.pdf)

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

The following is a **mythic chain of custody**, reconstructed separately for each source.

### A. *Reginsmál*

#### 1. Andvari

- **Place:** Andvarafors, a waterfall; his gold is associated with the water and a rock dwelling.
- **Title:** original owner in the surviving narrative, not documented maker.
- **Transfer mechanism:** coercive seizure or extorted ransom.
- **Evidence:** prose introduction and prose after stanza 4.

#### 2. Loki

Loki catches Andvari with Rán’s net and demands all his gold as the price of his life. Andvari retains one ring; Loki notices and takes it. This is neither a gift nor a voluntary sale.

Andvari’s stanza 5 says:

> *Þat skal gull, er Gustr átti,  
> brœðrum tveim at bana verða,  
> ok ǫðlingum átta at rógi;  
> mun míns fjár mangi njóta.*

In sense: the gold that Gustr possessed will kill two brothers, cause conflict among eight rulers, and benefit no one. “Gustr” is apparently an alternate self-reference or name in this context. The grammatical subject is **the gold**, not exclusively the ring.

#### 3. Óðinn

Loki returns with the hoard. Óðinn takes or holds back the ring while the rest is placed in and around Ótr’s skin. This constitutes a brief intra-divine transfer or appropriation.

#### 4. Hreiðmarr and the otter-payment

Hreiðmarr requires the flayed skin of his son Ótr to be filled with red gold and covered externally. One whisker remains visible. Óðinn uses Andvaranaut to cover it.

The ring thereby enters the **wergild** or ransom called:

- *otrgjǫld*, “otter-payment”;
- *nauðgjald Ásanna*, “the Æsir’s forced payment”;
- and, in Snorri’s poetic terminology, *rógmálmr*, “strife-metal.”

Hreiðmarr accepts the completed payment. Custody passes to him.

#### 5. Fáfnir

Fáfnir kills his father Hreiðmarr for the treasure and denies Reginn a share. He carries or controls it at Gnitaheiðr and becomes or assumes the form of a serpent/dragon over it.

The text does not describe a separate act in which Fáfnir puts on Andvaranaut. His possession follows from his seizure of the entire hoard.

#### 6. Sigurðr

Sigurðr kills Fáfnir. After also killing Reginn, he loads the treasure onto Grani. In the surviving *Reginsmál–Fáfnismál–Sigrdrífumál* sequence, the ring ceases to be individually tracked at this point.

The Poetic Edda does not provide the full ring exchange between Sigurðr, Brynhildr, and Guðrún preserved in Snorri and *Völsunga saga*. A lacuna of eight leaves in Codex Regius interrupts the heroic poems. Some lost material may have supplied additional links, but its exact content cannot be recovered.

### B. Snorri’s *Skáldskaparmál*

Snorri gives the most continuous custody narrative.

#### 1. Andvari → Loki

- **Object:** one small gold ring.
- **Transfer:** Loki’s coercive confiscation after Andvari pays the rest of his gold.
- **Special attribute:** Andvari says he can increase wealth from it.
- **Pronouncement:** whoever owns it will meet death through it.

Loki approves and says the pronouncement will remain effective because he will repeat it to the recipients.

#### 2. Loki → Óðinn

Loki shows the treasure to Óðinn. Óðinn finds the ring beautiful and removes it from the payment pile.

#### 3. Óðinn → Hreiðmarr

When one whisker remains exposed, Óðinn uses the ring to cover it. The divine party declares the compensation complete. Loki then repeats that the ring and gold will kill their possessor.

#### 4. Hreiðmarr → Fáfnir

Fáfnir and Reginn kill their father after he refuses them a share. Fáfnir takes the treasure, threatens Reginn, moves to Gnitaheiðr, assumes serpent form, and lies on the gold.

#### 5. Fáfnir → Sigurðr

Sigurðr kills Fáfnir, kills Reginn after understanding the birds’ warning, enters Fáfnir’s lair, loads the treasure on Grani, and rides away.

#### 6. Sigurðr → Brynhildr

Disguised in Gunnarr’s appearance, Sigurðr crosses the surrounding fire and spends the night with Brynhildr, placing the sword Gramr between them. In the morning he gives her the gold ring Loki had taken from Andvari as *línfé*, here a bridal or morning gift, and takes another ring from her as a remembrance.

This version does not narrate an earlier gift of Andvaranaut from Sigurðr to Brynhildr. Its exchange occurs during the disguised bridal visit.

#### 7. Brynhildr retains and wears it

During the queens’ dispute at the river, Guðrún identifies the ring on Brynhildr’s hand as Andvaranaut and says the man who gave it to her was Sigurðr, not Gunnarr. The ring becomes physical evidence of impersonation and sexual-marital deception.

#### 8. Brynhildr/Gjúkungar → Gunnarr and Hǫgni

After Sigurðr’s murder and Brynhildr’s death, Snorri states that Gunnarr and Hǫgni took **Fáfnir’s inheritance and Andvaranaut** and ruled their lands. This is the clearest express post-Brynhildr transfer.

#### 9. Concealment in the Rhine

Before going to Atli’s feast, Gunnarr and Hǫgni hide Fáfnir’s gold in the Rhine. Snorri concludes:

> *ok hefir þat gull aldri síðan fundizk*

“and that gold has never since been found.”

Because Snorri has just included Andvaranaut within their possession of Fáfnir’s inheritance, the natural narrative inference is that the ring went into the Rhine with the hoard. The text nevertheless says “the gold,” not “the ring Andvaranaut,” in the concealment sentence. Its deposition with the hoard is therefore a strong inference, not a separately narrated act.

### C. *Völsunga saga*

#### 1. Andvari → Loki → Óðinn → Hreiðmarr

The saga follows the Eddic sequence. Andvari retains one ring after yielding his gold; Loki takes it. Andvari says the gold ring and all the gold will kill whoever owns them. Óðinn takes Andvaranaut from his hand and uses it to cover the last whisker on the otter skin.

#### 2. Hreiðmarr → Fáfnir → Sigurðr

Fáfnir murders Hreiðmarr and takes the treasure. Sigurðr kills Fáfnir and Reginn and takes the hoard. The ring is not inventoried separately at the moment Sigurðr enters the lair, but its later reappearance establishes that the saga-author understood it to be among his spoils.

#### 3. Sigurðr → Brynhildr: oath-gift

At Brynhildr’s hall, Sigurðr and Brynhildr swear that he will marry her. He gives her a gold ring. The ring is not named in this sentence, but a later retrospective explicitly identifies it as Andvaranaut.

This gives the object a new function: it embodies renewed vows.

#### 4. Brynhildr → Sigurðr in Gunnarr’s shape

After Grímhildr’s drink makes Sigurðr forget Brynhildr and marry Guðrún, Sigurðr assumes Gunnarr’s appearance and crosses the fire for him. He spends three nights beside Brynhildr with Gramr between them.

He then takes from her **Andvaranaut, which he had earlier given her**, and replaces it with another ring from Fáfnir’s treasure.

The transfer is deceptive: Brynhildr believes the visitor is Gunnarr. The substitute ring is not Andvaranaut.

#### 5. Sigurðr → Guðrún

The saga never stages this transfer. During the river quarrel Guðrún possesses and displays Andvaranaut, so Sigurðr must have given or otherwise conveyed it to her offstage. The mechanism and date cannot be established.

#### 6. Guðrún displays it to Brynhildr

Guðrún says Sigurðr crossed the fire, lay beside Brynhildr, and took Andvaranaut from her hand. Brynhildr sees and recognizes it, turns pale, and later demands vengeance.

This is the ring’s most consequential attested use in the saga. It is not shown killing anyone by magical action. It authenticates Guðrún’s disclosure, triggering a human sequence of anger, conspiracy, murder, and suicide.

#### 7. Post-Sigurðr gap

After Sigurðr’s death, the saga ceases to identify Andvaranaut by name. Gunnarr and Hǫgni possess the great treasure from Gnitaheiðr. Atli seeks it; Gunnarr declares that the Rhine will rule the gold rather than the Huns. But the text does not explicitly say:

- when Guðrún surrendered the ring;
- whether Gunnarr confiscated it;
- whether it remained with Guðrún;
- whether it was burned with Sigurðr or Brynhildr;
- or whether it went into the Rhine.

The named chain therefore **breaks with Guðrún**. Linking it to the Rhine depends on importing Snorri’s more explicit account or assuming that all Fáfnir’s treasure was reunited.

#### A false but recurring identification

Later summaries sometimes identify the gold ring in which Guðrún ties wolf-hair to warn Gunnarr and Hǫgni against Atli as Andvaranaut. *Völsunga saga* calls that object merely **a gold ring**. It does not name it Andvaranaut, and the earlier custody gap makes the identification unprovable.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Does the object physically exist?

There is no evidence that a physical Andvaranaut ever existed. No museum, treasury, excavation, scientific publication, or documented private collection possesses a candidate with a continuous provenance to the medieval narratives.

Accordingly, there is:

- no accession number for the ring;
- no radiocarbon or thermoluminescence date applicable to it;
- no metallurgical assay;
- no provenance file;
- no conservation history;
- no authenticated fragment;
- and no legal ownership or repatriation dispute.

Claims that a modern ring, amulet, or excavated Viking ornament is “Andvaranaut” are symbolic identifications unless supported by evidence not presently known.

### Are all medieval “rings” in the Sigurðr tradition the same object?

No.

#### The *Nibelungenlied* ring

Sîfrit removes a golden finger-ring and belt from Brünhilt while helping Gunther overpower her. He gives them to Kriemhilt, who displays them in the queens’ quarrel. The ring’s evidentiary role resembles Andvaranaut’s later saga role, but:

- Brünhilt is its original owner;
- it does not come from Andvari;
- it is not part of the hoard;
- it is not cursed;
- and it has no wealth-producing power.

Treating it as “the same ring” is comparative reconstruction, not manuscript fact. [Gunter Grimm and Uwe Werlein, “Wagner und das Nibelungenlied”](https://www.nibelungenrezeption.de/musik/wagner/wagner-nibelungenlied.pdf)

#### The ring in *Þiðreks saga*

The ring associated with the Brynhildr episode in *Þiðreks saga* is likewise not called Andvaranaut and is not assigned Andvari’s provenance or curse. It belongs to a distinct narrative recension.

#### Other rings in Fáfnir’s treasure

*Völsunga saga* explicitly distinguishes Andvaranaut from “another ring from Fáfnir’s treasure” used as its replacement. Every ring in the hoard is therefore not automatically Andvaranaut.

#### Draupnir

Modern retellings sometimes merge Andvaranaut’s wealth-producing quality with Óðinn’s multiplying ring Draupnir. They are distinct named objects in Old Norse literature. Snorri says Draupnir produces eight equal-weight rings every ninth night. He says Andvaranaut enables Andvari to increase his wealth but does not specify the mechanism. Any identification between them is comparative speculation.

### Is U 1163 a portrait of the object?

The Drävle stone presents an authenticity problem of representation, not of the ring itself. A circular object is visible, but the stone provides no identifying caption. Scholars have variously interpreted the bearer as Andvari or Sigurðr. It can establish that a ring-bearing episode was associated visually with the Sigurðr cycle in Viking-Age or early medieval Sweden, but cannot establish the historical existence, exact appearance, or exclusive identity of Andvaranaut.

### Is the curse confined to the ring?

This is a textual identity dispute.

- **In *Reginsmál* stanza 5:** Andvari speaks of the gold and his property collectively.
- **In Snorri:** Andvari initially says the ring will kill every owner; Loki then states that “the ring and that gold” will kill their possessor.
- **In *Völsunga saga*:** the dwarf condemns both “that gold ring” and “all the gold.”
- **In subsequent narrative:** none of the three sources repeatedly invokes the curse as an active mechanism.

William Layher’s focused study concludes that only Snorri initially confines Andvari’s pronouncement to the ring, that Loki immediately expands it to the gold, and that nineteenth-century reception made “the curse” more central than it is in the medieval narration. [Layher, “Der Ring Andvaranaut und sein Fluch”](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9783110614510-010/html?lang=en)

### Forgery and claimant history

No historically significant forged “original Andvaranaut” has been documented in the consulted museum, manuscript, archaeological, or scholarly sources. Commercial objects sold under the name are replicas or fantasy jewelry, not usually presented with serious archaeological provenance.

The absence of a claimant is itself important: Andvaranaut differs from the Holy Grail, the Spear of Longinus, or saintly relics because no enduring institution has claimed physical custody or authority to authenticate it.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Blood-price and legal compensation

The ring’s first narrative function is juridical. It completes the compensation for Ótr’s death. Hreiðmarr demands that the skin be filled and externally covered with red gold. When a whisker remains visible, Andvaranaut closes the gap.

The detail gives the object a paradoxical status:

- it completes a formally exact settlement;
- it has been obtained through another act of coercion;
- and it transfers a fatal pronouncement into the compensation.

Loki’s designation of the gold as the Æsir’s forced payment preserves the episode in the poetic vocabulary for gold. Snorri includes the story partly to explain kennings such as “otter-payment,” “forced payment of the Æsir,” and “strife-metal.” The ring therefore functions inside a learned system of poetic language, not only as a plot device.

### Wealth production

Only Snorri explicitly says Andvari can **increase wealth from the ring**. The text does not prescribe a ritual, spoken formula, interval, or numerical yield. Later claims that it “finds gold” or automatically creates coins exceed the evidence.

### Curse or fatal pronouncement

The texts use a *formáli*, a binding pronouncement or condition, rather than presenting a detailed magical rite. Andvari does not engrave runes, perform a sacrifice, or forge the curse into the metal. Loki becomes its herald, saying it will remain effective because he will convey it to those who receive the treasure.

The distinction between prediction and causation remains unresolved. The stories show greed, deception, kin-murder, and oath-breaking around the treasure, but do not depict the ring emitting a force or controlling minds.

### Oath and betrothal token

In *Völsunga saga*, Sigurðr gives the ring to Brynhildr when they renew vows to marry. It becomes a material witness to an oath. Its later retrieval under disguise is therefore not merely theft of jewelry; it strips the earlier promise of its token and transfers proof of the broken oath into another household.

### Bridal or morning gift

Snorri describes Sigurðr’s gift to Brynhildr as *línfé*. The ring formally marks the disguised bridal encounter. Its circulation is embedded in aristocratic gift exchange even though the marriage itself is founded on substituted identity.

### Recognition evidence

The ring’s clearest practical power is not supernatural. Brynhildr recognizes it. Guðrún uses it to prove that Sigurðr—not Gunnarr—crossed the fire and shared Brynhildr’s bed.

In both the Norse saga and the related German epic, a ring converts private bodily deception into publicly intelligible evidence. Wendy Doniger treats this recurring narrative structure as a “ring of truth”: the object preserves knowledge that the participants suppress or misrepresent. [Doniger, “Siegfried’s Ring and Wagner’s Ring”](https://academic.oup.com/book/12242/chapter-abstract/161723254)

### Dynastic treasure and sovereignty

Possession of Fáfnir’s inheritance enhances Sigurðr’s and subsequently the Gjúkungar’s wealth and prestige. After Sigurðr’s death, Gunnarr and Hǫgni are described as ruling lands while possessing the hoard. Atli’s attempt to learn its location becomes a dynastic and military conflict.

The medieval texts do not say possession of Andvaranaut alone legally confers kingship. That “mastery of the world” function belongs principally to Wagner’s adaptation.

### Taboo and access

No source records a cult, priesthood, shrine, pilgrimage, or standing rule governing who may touch or see Andvaranaut. The effective taboo is narrative: property seized through coercion and kin-murder benefits no possessor.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The otter-ransom

**Tier:** early attested medieval narrative.

Óðinn, Hœnir, and Loki travel together. Loki kills an otter that has caught a salmon, not knowing—or disregarding—that it is Hreiðmarr’s son Ótr in animal form. At Hreiðmarr’s dwelling, the family captures the gods and demands compensation: the skin must be filled and covered with gold.

Loki catches Andvari and extorts his treasure. Andvari tries to save one ring. Loki takes it. The ring becomes the final piece covering the last whisker and passes into Hreiðmarr’s hands.

Important variants:

- *Reginsmál* gives Loki Rán’s net and locates Andvari in Andvarafors.
- Snorri sends Loki to Svartálfaheimr and emphasizes Andvari’s rock-hoard.
- Snorri alone says the ring can increase wealth.
- The Poetic Edda’s curse verse condemns the treasure collectively.
- Snorri initially condemns the ring, then ring and gold.
- *Völsunga saga* explicitly condemns the gold ring and all the gold.

### The deaths of Hreiðmarr and Fáfnir

**Tier:** early attested medieval narrative.

Hreiðmarr refuses to share the compensation with Fáfnir and Reginn. Fáfnir murders him, takes the treasure, drives Reginn away, and guards the gold as a serpent at Gnitaheiðr. Reginn raises or instructs Sigurðr and incites him to kill Fáfnir.

The story is often retold as if Andvaranaut directly transforms Fáfnir into a dragon. The sources do not say that. Fáfnir assumes serpent form in connection with guarding the hoard; the causal agent is not identified as the ring.

### Sigurðr’s acquisition

**Tier:** early attested medieval narrative.

Sigurðr kills Fáfnir from a pit in the serpent’s path. Fáfnir warns that the gold will kill whoever possesses it. Sigurðr accepts mortality and chooses the treasure. After learning from birds that Reginn plans to betray him, he kills Reginn and loads the hoard on Grani.

Again, the texts emphasize fatal wealth more than a separately acting ring.

### Brynhildr and the recognition ring

**Tier:** thirteenth-century prose elaboration, with related continental parallel.

In *Völsunga saga*, Sigurðr gives Andvaranaut to Brynhildr during their oath. After magically induced forgetfulness, he marries Guðrún and helps Gunnarr obtain Brynhildr. In Gunnarr’s form he crosses the fire, retrieves Andvaranaut, and substitutes another ring.

Guðrún later displays Andvaranaut. Brynhildr recognizes it, realizes she was deceived, and presses for Sigurðr’s death. The ring thus “causes” catastrophe through recognition, not demonstrated magical compulsion.

Snorri simplifies the chain: Sigurðr gives the ring during the disguised bridal visit rather than retrieving an earlier oath-ring. Guðrún identifies it on Brynhildr’s hand.

The *Nibelungenlied* has the comparable revelation through Brünhilt’s own ring and belt, but no Andvari provenance.

### The Rhine concealment

**Tier:** medieval ending; details vary.

Snorri says Gunnarr and Hǫgni hide Fáfnir’s gold in the Rhine before visiting Atli and that it has never been found. *Völsunga saga* has Gunnarr declare, after Hǫgni’s death, that the Rhine shall possess the gold rather than the Huns, but does not show the deposit or name Andvaranaut at this stage.

The “Rhine gold” ending encouraged later geographical and treasure-hunting associations. No archaeological discovery has been authenticated as Fáfnir’s hoard or Andvaranaut.

### The self-fulfilling curse

**Tier:** modern interpretive synthesis.

Modern retellings often arrange a clean sequence in which every possessor immediately dies because of the ring:

Andvari → Loki → Hreiðmarr → Fáfnir → Sigurðr → Brynhildr → Gunnarr → loss in the Rhine.

The medieval evidence is less mechanical:

- Loki and Óðinn briefly possess or handle it without dying because of it.
- Reginn seeks the hoard without securely possessing the ring.
- Guðrún demonstrably holds it in *Völsunga saga* and survives for many subsequent episodes.
- Brynhildr recognizes it, but her death follows human deception and suicide.
- Atli covets the treasure but never obtains the ring.
- The narratives stop reminding the audience of the curse once it has been pronounced.

The curse nevertheless provides a powerful interpretive frame: wealth acquired through uncompensated death initiates further uncompensated deaths.

### Wagner’s Rhine-gold and ring of world-power

**Tier:** documented nineteenth-century invention using medieval materials.

In Wagner’s *Der Ring des Nibelungen*:

- Alberich, not Andvari, steals Rhine gold from the Rhinemaidens.
- He forswears love.
- He forges a ring from the gold.
- The ring confers dominion and the power to compel labor.
- Wotan and Loge seize it.
- Alberich curses it.
- It passes through the giants, Fafner, Siegfried, and Brünnhilde.
- Brünnhilde finally restores it to the Rhine as the gods’ world collapses.

This ring fuses the Andvari episode with the German Nibelung hoard, the Rhine setting, a newly invented renunciation-of-love condition, political power, industrial domination, and apocalyptic restitution. It is the most influential modern descendant of Andvaranaut, but not the same object under a faithful alternative name. Paul Schofield’s source study sets Snorri’s account directly against Wagner’s changes. [Schofield, *Wagner and the Volsungs*](https://vsnr.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/VSNR_Studies_Wagner.pdf)

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Literal status

Andvaranaut is **not known to exist as a physical artifact**. It has no verifiable present location, owner, keeper, accession number, or legal title.

Within the medieval narratives:

- Snorri’s most complete chain leaves the ring presumptively with the Niflung treasure concealed in the Rhine.
- *Völsunga saga* last securely places the named ring in Guðrún’s possession during the river quarrel; its later fate is unstated.
- *Reginsmál* and the extant Eddic poems cease individually tracking it after it enters the treasure chain.

“Lost in the Rhine” is therefore the traditional present status supplied by Snorri’s synthesis, not an archaeological location claim.

### Sites and objects standing in for it

#### U 1163 Drävle

The granite runestone at Göksbo, Uppland, is a real eleventh-century object that may depict an Andvaranaut episode. It is not the ring and does not claim to contain it.

#### Other Sigurðr stones

Gs 2, Gs 9, and related stones preserve ring-bearing figures interpreted within the Sigurðr cycle. Their real custody belongs to Swedish heritage authorities or local sites; their rings are carved images.

#### Hylestad portal

The late twelfth- or early thirteenth-century carved wooden portal from Hylestad depicts Sigurðr-cycle scenes including Fáfnir’s death and Grani carrying the treasure. The surviving portal, museum number **C4321**, is held by the Museum of Cultural History in Oslo. No separately identifiable Andvaranaut is visible with sufficient specificity to treat it as a portrait. [Hylestad portal documentation](https://www.kornbluthphoto.com/OHMPortal.html) [Museum 3-D record](https://sketchfab.com/3d-models/the-hylestad-portal-and-sigurd-the-dragon-slayer-6d1441208ae448deaca6c14120263252)

### Serious current hypotheses

There is no serious archaeological research program attempting to locate the literal ring. Attempts to map Gnitaheiðr or the Rhine hoard onto a specific treasure deposit concern legendary geography or the broader historical background of Burgundian traditions, not a traceable artifact.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Early modern recovery of the textual object

The ring re-entered European learned culture through the collection, copying, edition, and translation of Old Norse manuscripts.

*Völsunga saga* survives principally in the Icelandic parchment manuscript **NKS 1824 b 4to**, copied ca. 1380–1420. Later paper manuscripts derive from it. Bishop Brynjólfur Sveinsson gave the codex to Frederik III; it entered the Danish royal collection in 1656 and remains in the Royal Danish Library. The saga itself was probably composed in Iceland, or possibly Norway, in the second half of the thirteenth century. [University of Strasbourg Gottfried Portal](https://gottfried.unistra.fr/nibelungen/islandische-und-norrone-fassungen/volsungasaga/) [Rowbotham, manuscript history](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/24017/1/Rowbotham%2C%20T.%20P.%20-%20Fornaldars%C3%B6gur%2C%20Prosimetrum%2C%20and%20History-Writing%20in%20Medieval%20Iceland.pdf)

### English translation: Morris and Magnússon, 1870

Eiríkur Magnússon and William Morris published *The Story of the Volsungs and Niblungs* in 1870. Their translation transmitted the named ring and its curse into English literary culture. It renders the dwarf’s declaration as a curse upon “that gold-ring, yea and all the gold withal.” [Digitized 1870 translation](https://deriv.nls.uk/dcn23/8206/82069584.23.pdf)

### Wagner’s *Der Ring des Nibelungen*

Wagner worked on the tetralogy’s text and music from the revolutionary era of 1848 through the 1870s. The four dramas are:

1. *Das Rheingold*
2. *Die Walküre*
3. *Siegfried*
4. *Götterdämmerung*

*Das Rheingold* was first performed separately at Munich in 1869. The complete cycle premiered at the Bayreuth Festspielhaus from 13 to 17 August 1876. [Bayreuth Festival history](https://www.bayreuther-festspiele.de/en/festival/history/) [Schott, *Das Rheingold* production score and history](https://schott-production.s3.eu-central-1.amazonaws.com/public_content/element/234514/dl/Rheingold.pdf)

Wagner materially changed the object:

- Andvari became Alberich.
- The already-owned small ring became an object Alberich manufactures from stolen Rhine gold.
- Wealth multiplication became world-rule.
- The curse became a dominant leitmotivic and dramatic force.
- The final loss in the Rhine became restitution to its original aquatic guardians.

Because Wagner titled the entire work after the ring and placed it at the center of politics, sexuality, labor, contract, and apocalypse, his version largely created the modern public image of “the Nibelung’s cursed ring.”

### William Morris, *Sigurd the Volsung*, 1876

Morris’s epic poem calls it **“Andvari’s Ring of Gain.”** He places it gleaming like a star on the center of Fafnir’s gold heap and has Sigurd put it on his hand. This is a vivid Victorian elaboration of the sparse medieval description. The medieval texts never compare it to a star or stage it as the conspicuous summit of the hoard. [Morris, *The Story of Sigurd the Volsung*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/13486/old/13486-h/13486-h.htm)

### Tolkien and the One Ring

Tolkien was professionally and creatively immersed in Germanic literature and knew the Völsung material. His posthumously published *Legend of Sigurd and Gudrún* directly retells the Norse heroic cycle.

The stronger claim that Andvaranaut was the single or direct “original” of the One Ring is unproved. The objects differ substantially:

- Andvaranaut is not forged by an evil overlord.
- It contains no maker’s life-force.
- It does not confer invisibility.
- It does not control subordinate rings.
- Destroying it is never proposed as the remedy.
- It has no will or voice.
- Its destruction would not destroy Andvari.

Thomas Honegger argues that Tolkien’s One Ring emerged from several ring traditions and was developed partly as a critical rewriting of Wagner, alongside the Ring of Gyges and other sources. Thus Wagner’s transformation may have mediated the Norse material more powerfully than Andvaranaut alone. [Honegger, “Tolkien and the Tradition of the Rings of Power”](https://scholar.valpo.edu/journaloftolkienresearch/vol13/iss1/1/)

### Film and visual culture

Fritz Lang’s two-part film *Die Nibelungen* (1924) helped establish a monumental visual language for the Nibelung story, although its plot follows the German tradition rather than faithfully reproducing Andvaranaut’s Norse custody chain. Later Nibelung and Wagner films frequently give “the ring” a centrality inherited from Wagner.

Modern fantasy illustration commonly supplies:

- gemstones;
- runic inscriptions;
- serpent or dragon ornament;
- knotwork;
- glowing metal;
- or a heavy signet profile.

None is based on a medieval verbal description of Andvaranaut.

### Comics

Marvel’s *Loki: Agent of Asgard* #15 (2015) used an object named Andvaranaut with a newly invented power: seeing through lies. In that continuity it passes through the Willis family, is swallowed, dissolves, and transmits its property to Verity Willis. This is a distinct modern fictional artifact, not a retelling of the medieval custody chain. [Marvel character/object database](https://marvel.fandom.com/wiki/Andvaranaut)

### Games

Named or adapted versions appear as collectible magical equipment in modern games, including:

- *RuneScape*, where “Andvaranaut” is represented as an item;
- *Titan Quest*, which uses the name for a ring with powerful but probabilistic gameplay effects;
- *Vikings: Wolves of Midgard*, which treats it as a cursed object to be recovered;
- and *Wyrmsun*, which introduced Andvaranaut as a unique ring.

These works convert literary curse and treasure symbolism into item statistics, quests, bonuses, or penalties. [RuneScape item image and metadata](https://runescape.wiki/w/File%3AAndvaranaut_detail.png) [Wyrmsun release notes](https://store.steampowered.com/news/posts/?appids=370070&enddate=1450622138)

### Commercial replicas and amulets

Numerous vendors apply the name to modern jewelry. No canonical design exists, so these products are interpretive creations.

A related commercial object is the “Andvari amulet,” a reproduction inspired by a tenth-century bronze find from Daugmale, Latvia. The figure has been interpreted as Andvari holding a ring, but that identification is disputed; it may depict a warrior or another figure. Marketing the replica as “Andvari” transforms an uncertain iconographic hypothesis into a product identity. [Commercial reproduction with acknowledgment of the disputed identification](https://www.celticwebmerchant.com/en-fr/products/andvari-amulet-daugmale-10th-century-bronze)

### Namesakes

The Japanese thoroughbred **Andvaranaut**, foaled in 2018, is a modern namesake. Her dam is named Gullveig, making the mythological naming intentional within the pedigree. This is reception of the name, not a material replica. [Japan Bloodhorse Information System](https://www.jbis.jp/horse/0001266054/)

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### The source relationship

Older scholarship frequently treated the surviving Icelandic texts as transparent windows onto one coherent ancient myth. Current textual practice is more cautious:

- *Reginsmál* is a composite of verse and prose.
- Its poetic units may be older than the prose compilation.
- Snorri reorganized inherited material to teach poetic diction.
- *Völsunga saga* converted fragmentary heroic poetry into continuous prose and filled gaps through synthesis and invention.
- The saga’s sole medieval manuscript is appreciably later than the work’s likely composition.
- Continental German versions preserve related but independently developed material.

The appropriate object history is therefore not a single corrected plot but a **history of variant rings**.

### Ring versus treasure

The key scholarly dispute concerns whether the destructive force belongs to the ring or the whole hoard.

Layher emphasizes that the curse is much less consistently ring-centered than nineteenth-century reception suggests. In *Reginsmál*, the curse verse concerns the gold. In *Völsunga saga*, it embraces ring and gold. Snorri briefly isolates the ring but immediately expands the condition.

This undermines popular accounts in which Andvaranaut behaves like an autonomous malevolent agent. Medieval narration more often represents destructive wealth, failed compensation, and conflict among kin.

### The meaning of *nautr*

Earlier popular etymologies sometimes translate the name too literally as “gift,” while others confuse it with “need.” The comparative compound evidence suggests an object associated with, obtained from, or formerly belonging to Andvari. “Andvari’s possession” is safer than insisting upon a voluntary gift—especially since he surrenders it under duress.

### Finger-ring or arm-ring

The word *baugr* is not by itself decisive. Snorri’s “small gold *baugr*,” the taking of the object from a hand, and its bridal use favor a finger-ring in the developed story. Earlier stages may have imagined a gold hoop without the sharply modern distinction between jewelry types. The visual stones show conspicuously oversized circles because recognizability in stone carving may require enlargement.

### Drävle iconography

The ring-bearing figure on U 1163 has been read as:

- Andvari;
- Sigurðr;
- or a messenger/other figure.

The scene’s coexistence with Sigurðr stabbing Fáfnir strongly locates it in the legendary cycle. The particular episode remains disputed. It cannot securely prove that the name *Andvaranautr*, the wealth-producing property, or the saga’s exchange plot was known to the carver.

### The “eight princes”

*Reginsmál* says the gold will become a cause of strife among eight rulers. Attempts to map exactly eight later victims or possessors are unstable because the surviving cycle contains multiple rulers, claimants, and deaths, and the prose compilations do not organize the custody chain around a numbered fulfillment. The line may derive from poetic tradition no longer fully recoverable.

### Historical background

The later Niflung and Atli portions of the heroic tradition contain distant memories of historical Burgundians and Attila, but this does not historicize the ring. No fifth-century inventory, chronicle, grave, or treasure deposit identifies Andvaranaut. Historical residues in human names and conflicts cannot be transferred automatically to a supernatural object.

### Wagner and ideological interpretation

Wagner scholarship treats the ring variously as an emblem of:

- political sovereignty;
- capital and accumulated wealth;
- industrial domination;
- loveless power;
- corrupted contract;
- or the artist’s own creative power.

Those readings concern Wagner’s newly constructed ring. They should not be projected backward as established meanings of the medieval Andvaranaut.

Wagner’s later appropriation by German nationalism and National Socialism also shaped reception of Germanic myth. The medieval Icelandic ring, Wagner’s nineteenth-century political-artistic symbol, and twentieth-century ideological uses must remain historically separate.

### Tolkien influence debate

Direct linear equations—Andvaranaut → Wagner’s ring → Tolkien’s One Ring—are too simple. Tolkien knew the relevant traditions, but the One Ring combines properties absent from Andvaranaut and participates in a much broader history of magic, recognition, authority, invisibility, and soul-bearing rings. Scholarly arguments favor plural sources and transformation rather than one-object derivation.

### State of the primary evidence

Principal textual witnesses and problems are:

- **GKS 2365 4to, Codex Regius:** earliest surviving named witness; contains *Reginsmál* but has a major lacuna later in the heroic sequence.
- **DG 11 4to, Uppsala Edda:** probably the earliest surviving manuscript of Snorri’s *Edda*, but presents a divergent, shortened redaction.
- **Codex Regius of Snorri’s Edda, GKS 2367 4to:** fuller *Skáldskaparmál* witness used heavily in editions, but copied later than Snorri.
- **NKS 1824 b 4to:** sole independent medieval witness of *Völsunga saga*, copied ca. 1400; later manuscripts ultimately derive from it.
- **U 1163 and related carvings:** earlier material evidence for the narrative complex, but without textual labels for the ring.

Anthony Faulkes’s critical edition remains fundamental for Snorri’s wording and manuscript variants. R. G. Finch’s edition and translation remains important for *Völsunga saga*. Digital manuscript and textual projects have improved access but have not produced a physical candidate for the ring.
