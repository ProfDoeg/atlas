# "Checkmate" and "Check" (Retzsch/Vibert): Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary finding

The names in the inquiry refer not to one sacred object but to two unrelated nineteenth-century artworks that both depict chess:

1. Friedrich August Moritz Retzsch’s *Die Schachspieler—Les joueurs d’échecs—The Chess Players*, first securely documented as a published etching in Leipzig in 1831 and represented also by a small oil painting, a preparatory drawing, later editions, copies, and adaptations. “Checkmate,” “The Devil’s Checkmate,” and “Faust and Mephistopheles Playing Chess” are later descriptive titles, not the documented title of the 1831 publication.

2. Jehan-Georges Vibert’s *Check*—also called *Échec !* and *Napoleon and the Cardinal*—an undated oil painting on panel showing Napoleon Bonaparte playing chess with his uncle, Cardinal Joseph Fesch. It belongs to the Haggin Museum in Stockton, California.

Only Retzsch’s composition has acquired a sacred or legendary afterlife. Its modern legend says that a chess master—usually Paul Morphy—recognized that the apparently doomed young man still had “one more move,” thereby turning the picture into a Christian allegory of salvation and hope. The oldest located account, published in 1888 and amplified in 1889, differs materially: Morphy reportedly examined a color lithograph in a private Richmond house in 1861, reconstructed its imperfectly legible position, and demonstrated a winning continuation. The encounter did not occur at the Louvre; the object examined was not necessarily Retzsch’s original oil; and neither the eyewitness account nor Retzsch’s title contains the phrase “the king has one more move.”

No documentary evidence connects Vibert’s *Check* to that legend. The two works should therefore be tracked independently.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Retzsch: *Die Schachspieler*

**Documented titles**

- German: *Die Schachspieler*—“The Chess Players.”
- French, printed on the publication: *Les joueurs d’échecs*.
- English, printed on the publication: *The Chess-Players*.
- American edition: *The Game of Life, or, The Chess-Players*.
- Later popular titles: *Checkmate*, *The Devil’s Checkmate*, *Satan Playing at Chess with Man for His Soul*, and *Faust and Mephistopheles Playing Chess for a Soul*.

“Checkmate” is interpretive. The trilingual title in the 1831 publication says only “The Chess Players.” Contemporary and near-contemporary explanations called the contest “the game of life” and described Satan playing for the man’s soul, which encouraged the later title.

**The composition rather than a single unique object**

“Retzsch’s *Chess Players*” is a work embodied in several materially separate objects:

| Witness or version | Material and size | Date and present status |
|---|---|---|
| Preparatory or related drawing | Pencil on paper, sheet 178 × 279 mm | 1831 or earlier; Kupferstich-Kabinett, Staatliche Kunstsammlungen Dresden, inventory C 1892-278 b |
| First known published edition | Etching with letterpress explanations in German, French, and English; the Met’s bound copy measures 28 × 36.3 × 0.2 cm overall | Leipzig, Ernst Fleischer, 1831; Met object 35.65.7, not on view |
| British Museum edition | Etching and engraving with letterpress; plate width 265 mm; sheet approximately 250 × 331 mm | Catalogued as Leipzig, Ernst Fleischer, 1836; BM 1863,0411.105, not on display |
| Boston edition | Printed booklet, approximately 13.5 × 9.75 inches, four text pages and a plate | Weeks, Jordan & Co., 1837; copies survive, including one catalogued through the Boston Public Library system |
| Retzsch oil version sold in 1999 | Oil on panel, 32.3 × 39 cm | Sold by Christie’s, London, 7 October 1999; present owner and location unpublished |
| Later painted and printed copies | Oil on panel or canvas, lithographs, engravings, and other reproductions | Numerous; attribution, date, and board position vary |

The Met identifies Retzsch as artist, Carl Borromäus von Miltitz as editor, Ernst Fleischer as publisher, and Alfred Launder as binder of its particular copy. It acquired that volume through the Harris Brisbane Dick Fund in 1935. The British Museum describes its copy as a book of prints containing one outline-style image and a trilingual letterpress explanation. These records establish that the celebrated image circulated as a reproducible publication, not solely as a unique painting. [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/354051); [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1863-0411-105)

**Visual construction**

The composition places two opponents across a chessboard cut or set into the lid of a stone sarcophagus in a vaulted, infernal interior. At the viewer’s left sits Satan, luxuriously dressed and self-possessed. Opposite him, a young man supports his head in anxious concentration. A winged guardian spirit or angel stands behind and between them. Satan can see and must tolerate the angel; the young man does not see it.

The painted version sold in 1999 used strong color: Satan wore a green mantle and a cap with a red feather. His throne bore a snarling lion’s head, and its feet rested by a skull. A spider approaching the young man supplied another image of entrapment. Christie’s described the board’s support as a sarcophagus and the chessmen as the agents of the allegory. [Christie’s](https://www.christies.com.cn/en/lot/lot-1543688)

**The allegorical chessmen**

Von Miltitz’s explanatory tradition, repeated in an 1837 Dutch account, identifies the dark army as Satan and the vices:

- King: Satan, resembling the seated adversary.
- Queen: Sensuality or voluptuousness.
- Sloth: a heavy, swine-headed figure.
- Wrath: a forward-rushing figure with a turkey-cock’s head.
- Pride: a peacock-associated figure displaying itself while unwittingly exposing its nakedness.
- Falsehood or Cunning: feline-headed, one hand in an apparently sincere gesture while the other conceals a dagger.
- Envy and Avarice: joined or compressed into one figure.
- Unbelief: horned, kicking or trampling aside the cross.
- Pawns: Doubts, figured as predatory heads or harpy-like creatures with sharp teeth and bat wings.

The young man’s light army represents the soul and virtues:

- King: the soul, figured with butterfly imagery.
- Queen: Faith.
- Principal pieces: Hope, Truth, Peace, Humility, Innocence, and Love; Love appears as embracing children.
- Pawns: Prayer, represented as heads or devotional figures.

The exact mapping of allegorical figures onto orthodox rooks, bishops, and knights is not consistently legible. That ambiguity is crucial: later attempts to reconstruct a legally exact chess position depend on subjective identification of the pieces. The 1837 Dutch description supplies a close period witness to the iconography, while Christie’s confirms such individual details as the cross-trampling dark piece and the apparently peaceable figure hiding a stiletto. [De Gids, 1837](https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/_gid001183701_01/_gid001183701_01_0123.php); [Christie’s](https://www.christies.com.cn/en/lot/lot-1543688)

**Inscriptions and marks**

The first edition’s wrapper and title identify the work in German, French, and English and state that it was a drawing by Moritz Retzsch explained by C. Borr. von Miltitz from the artist’s indications. No meaningful inscription on the depicted sarcophagus is reported in the consulted catalogues. No published technical report identifying the panel wood, pigments, ground, maker’s marks, or alterations of the 1999 oil was located.

**Condition**

- Dresden drawing: catalogued as extant; no detailed public condition report located.
- Met volume: extant, not on view; no damage or conservation history published in the online record.
- British Museum volume: extant, not displayed; no detailed condition statement supplied online.
- Christie’s oil: extant at the 1999 sale; the catalogue gives no conservation or technical-analysis report.
- Original copperplate: its survival and location could not be established.
- The state of the 1999 oil after sale is not publicly documented.

### 1.2 Vibert: *Check*

**Titles**

- English museum title: *Check*.
- French form in later references: *Échec !*
- Descriptive alternative: *Napoleon and the Cardinal* or *Napoléon et le cardinal*.

The French *échec* means “check” in chess and, by extension, setback or failure. The title therefore permits a political as well as a literal reading.

**Physical object**

The Haggin Museum catalogues the work as:

- Artist: Jehan-Georges or Jean-Georges Vibert, 1840–1902.
- Medium: oil on panel.
- Dimensions: 29 × 38½ inches, presumably approximately 73.7 × 97.8 cm.
- Date: undated.
- Collection: Haggin Museum, Stockton, California.

The museum’s public page does not state an accession number, signature, frame dimensions, panel species, weight, technical examination, or conservation history. [Haggin Museum](https://hagginmuseum.org/collections/vibert-jehan-georges/check/)

**Depicted scene**

Napoleon sits at the chessboard in his bedroom at Fontainebleau. Cardinal Joseph Fesch, his maternal uncle, has apparently just put him in check and takes snuff with visible satisfaction. The room includes imperial emblems: the letter **N**, Bonaparte bees, laurel wreaths, and olive branches. Both men wear carefully rendered period dress and insignia of the Legion of Honor. The museum interprets the scene as a possible satire on political clericalism and the vanity of power, but it appropriately phrases these readings as possibilities, not documented statements by Vibert.

The painting depicts a retrospectively imagined episode. The museum calls it “an event from the life of Napoleon,” but no contemporaneous record establishing this precise chess game, room, move, or gesture was located. It should be classed as historical genre painting rather than documentary eyewitness representation.

**Condition**

No public condition report, restoration history, radiography, infrared imaging, dendrochronology, or pigment analysis was located.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Retzsch

The firm terminus is the Fleischer publication at Leipzig in 1831. The Met catalogues its edition to that year, and an antiquarian description identifies the 1831 issue as the first edition, comprising an engraved plate and three unnumbered letterpress leaves in German, English, and French. [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/354051); [Antiquariat Banzhaf catalogue](https://www.antiquariat-banzhaf.de/wp-content/uploads/Banzhaf-Katalog-2-mp.pdf)

Dresden’s pencil drawing, dated “1831 or earlier,” provides a physical witness at or before publication. It is not described as a full painted study, and its catalogued title—*Die Schachspieler (Faust und Mephisto beim Schachspiel)*—includes the later or curatorial identification of the players as Faust and Mephistopheles. [Graphikportal/Kupferstich-Kabinett](https://www.graphikportal.org/document/gpo00112324)

The sequence among drawing, etching, and oil remains unresolved. A 2026 Sotheby’s catalogue, discussing a later canvas “after” Retzsch, states cautiously that the 1831 Fleischer issue appears to be the earliest known print and that Retzsch may have developed painted versions from it. The oil sold by Christie’s cannot simply be assumed to have preceded the print. [Sotheby’s](https://www.sothebys.com/en/buy/auction/2026/books-manuscripts-objects-from-three-important-collections/after-friedrich-moritz-august-retzsch-die?locale=it)

Retzsch designed and etched the image; Fleischer published it; von Miltitz turned the artist’s “indications” into the accompanying exposition. The image belongs to Retzsch’s characteristic outline manner. Its precise manufacturing costs, print run, copperplate history, panel source, and commission are unestablished in the consulted evidence.

**Relationship to Goethe’s *Faust***

Retzsch was celebrated for outline illustrations to Goethe, including twenty-six plates for an 1818 *Faust* publication. The chess scene, however, was not one of those *Faust* plates and does not illustrate a chess episode found in Goethe’s drama. Its infernal wager and imperiled soul are Faustian in theme, but identifying the youth literally as Faust and Satan literally as Mephistopheles is an interpretation, not a transcription of Goethe. Christie’s carefully called the work “not an explicitly Faustian episode” but rich in “Faustian flavour.” Sotheby’s likewise distinguishes it from the 1818 series. [Christie’s](https://www.christies.com.cn/en/lot/lot-1543688); [Sotheby’s](https://www.sothebys.com/en/buy/auction/2026/books-manuscripts-objects-from-three-important-collections/after-friedrich-moritz-august-retzsch-die?locale=it)

**Purpose**

The composition was conceived as a moral allegory: the spiritual contest within a human life rendered as a chess game between virtues and vices. The angel may warn but not choose for the human player; the pictorial mechanism therefore preserves moral agency while showing the unequal force of temptation. The publication’s multilingual apparatus indicates an intended international market.

### 2.2 Vibert

*Check* is undated. It must fall within Vibert’s working life and probably within his mature career, when he repeatedly produced meticulously staged historical and anti-clerical genre paintings for an international collecting market. No commission, Salon entry, dated invoice, dealer stock book, preparatory drawing, or first-sale record for this particular panel was located.

The painting’s detailed reconstruction of Napoleon’s Fontainebleau bedroom shows research or quotation from surviving interiors and portraits. That does not authenticate the depicted chess game as an historical incident.

No evidence establishes whether the Haggins commissioned the painting, purchased it directly from Vibert, or obtained it through a Parisian or New York dealer.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 Retzsch oil painting sold in 1999

The documented chain published by Christie’s is short and contains a major opening gap:

1. **Retzsch or his immediate market, nineteenth century:** manufacture attributable to Retzsch, but no commission, first purchaser, or early inventory was published.
2. **Queen Isabella II of Spain, before 1904:** Christie’s lists her as owner. The catalogue does not reproduce an invoice, inventory number, palace location, or acquisition date.
3. **Monsieur Chappuis:** received the panel as a gift from Isabella II. His full name, date of gift, and residence are not given.
4. **E. Constantin, Paris, 1898:** acquired it from Chappuis.
5. **Constantin family or heirs, 1898–1999:** “thence by descent.”
6. **Christie’s, London, 7 October 1999:** sold for £67,500 against an estimate of £50,000–£70,000.
7. **Anonymous purchaser, 1999–present:** present ownership and physical location have not been published.

This provenance is auction-house documentation, not a complete evidentiary chain. In particular, the path from Retzsch to Isabella II and the evidence supporting the Chappuis and Constantin links were not made public in the online lot record. [Christie’s](https://www.christies.com.cn/en/lot/lot-1543688)

There is no evidence that this panel entered the Louvre. The recurrent claim that “Checkmate hung in the Louvre” conflicts with the published provenance and with the absence of a Louvre collection record. It should be classified as an unsupported modern legend.

### 3.2 Retzsch drawing in Dresden

- Made by Retzsch in or before 1831.
- Entered the Kupferstich-Kabinett by or in 1892, as suggested by inventory number C 1892-278 b.
- Presently catalogued by the Staatliche Kunstsammlungen Dresden.

The online record does not state the donor, vendor, price, or exact accession event. [Graphikportal](https://www.graphikportal.org/document/gpo00112324)

### 3.3 Met copy of the 1831 publication

- Published by Ernst Fleischer in Leipzig, 1831.
- Later bound by Alfred Launder; date and circumstances unreported.
- Acquired by the Metropolitan Museum of Art in 1935 through the Harris Brisbane Dick Fund.
- Now in the Department of Drawings and Prints as 35.65.7; not on view.

The Met page does not expose an intervening dealer or collector provenance. [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/354051)

### 3.4 British Museum copy

- Printed and published at Leipzig by Ernst Fleischer; catalogued by the museum as 1836.
- Acquired by the British Museum in 1863.
- Registration number 1863,0411.105.
- Not on display.

The public record does not name the former owner in its summarized metadata. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1863-0411-105)

### 3.5 Richmond lithograph associated with Paul Morphy

This object’s identity is separate from the Christie’s panel.

According to Reverend R. R. Harrison’s account published in 1889, Harrison possessed a **color copy or lithograph** of Retzsch’s composition in his Richmond, Virginia, home. In autumn 1861 he and Paul Morphy tried to reconstruct its board position. Harrison acknowledged that they had to examine it and do “the best we could” to verify each chessman.

The lithograph’s printer, edition, purchase history, and later fate are unknown. No evidence places the Christie’s oil in Harrison’s house, and the earliest printed anecdote itself referred merely to “the picture.” The object in the Morphy episode should therefore be catalogued as a now-unlocated colored reproduction after Retzsch.

### 3.6 Vibert’s *Check*

The fullest responsible chain presently reconstructable is:

1. **Vibert’s studio or initial market:** undated; first purchaser and exhibition history unknown.
2. **Louis Terah and Blanche Butterworth Haggin collection:** probable family ownership, because the museum’s founding collection was assembled principally by them and included multiple Viberts. The exact acquisition record for *Check* has not been published online.
3. **Eila Haggin McKee / Robert T. McKee:** Eila inherited the bulk of her father’s estate after Louis Terah Haggin’s death in March 1929. She supplied the art collection around which the memorial galleries were formed.
4. **San Joaquin Pioneer and Historical Society / Haggin Museum, Stockton:** the galleries opened on 14 June 1931. A contemporaneous October 1931 museum letter says the institution held approximately 200 canvases given by Mrs. Robert T. McKee and specifically mentions “Viberts” among them.
5. **Present:** Haggin Museum, 1201 North Pershing Avenue, Stockton, California.

The inference that *Check* was part of the initial Haggin gift is strong but not conclusively established by the object page or the 1931 letter, neither of which names the painting individually. The museum’s accession ledger or Patricia Sanders’s 1991 collection catalogue would be needed to close the gap. [Smithsonian transcription of 1931 correspondence](https://transcription.si.edu/view/52959/AAA-jacqself00001-003181); [Haggin Museum](https://hagginmuseum.org/collections/vibert-jehan-georges/check/)

No theft, wartime seizure, forced sale, restitution claim, deaccession, or ownership litigation involving either identified principal painting was found.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 What is the “original” Retzsch object?

There is no demonstrated single-object answer.

- The Dresden drawing is an authentic, institutionally catalogued Retzsch drawing dated in or before 1831.
- The 1831 Fleischer publication is the earliest securely documented public form of the composition.
- The oil sold in 1999 was catalogued by Christie’s as by Retzsch, but the public record does not establish whether it preceded or followed the etching.
- Numerous later paintings reproduce the composition. Some are expressly catalogued “after Retzsch”; they are not interchangeable with the 1999 panel.
- Different painted and printed versions can alter figures, colors, perspective, and—most importantly for the legend—the apparent chess position.

Thus “the original Checkmate painting” is an imprecise popular label. The earliest known publication is an etching; the chronology and plurality of Retzsch’s painted versions remain unresolved.

### 4.2 Is “Checkmate” the authentic title?

No. It is a later popular title. The documented title is *Die Schachspieler—Les joueurs d’échecs—The Chess Players*. “The Game of Life” is an early interpretive title attested in the 1837 Boston edition. “Satan Playing at Chess with Man for His Soul” was already used in Britain in the 1830s as an explanatory title. “Checkmate” describes the apparent narrative but is not the original title.

### 4.3 Is the young player actually checkmated?

This cannot be answered uniquely from “the artwork” because:

- the symbolic pieces are not ordinary Staunton pieces;
- exact piece types and square occupancy are difficult to determine;
- the image exists in versions with differences;
- Harrison and Morphy reportedly reconstructed the position from a colored lithograph rather than reading an exact diagram;
- Charles A. Gilberg’s 1888 reconstruction was an interpretation of the image, not an artist-supplied score;
- later analysts produce different positions and conclusions, ranging from a win for the youth to a forced mate for Satan.

The image was designed as moral allegory, not published as a composed chess problem with a stipulated side to move. Any exact solution must identify the physical version analyzed, document an unambiguous transcription, and state whose turn it is.

### 4.4 Did Morphy see the work?

There is significant but retrospective evidence that he saw **a reproduction**.

- The first located publication appeared on 18 August 1888, more than twenty-six years after the alleged event and four years after Morphy’s death.
- The writer, “G.R.F.”, was later identified as Gilbert R. Frith.
- Critics immediately questioned whether the pieces were legible and whether details of Morphy’s Richmond visit were correct.
- Reverend R. R. Harrison then supplied an eyewitness account, dated the visit to autumn 1861, described the reconstruction, named other persons present, and cited corroboration from Captain Barney.
- Harrison’s account supports the essential episode but also proves that modern formulations—Louvre, original painting, a curator about to retitle the picture, a shouted discovery, or precisely “one more move”—are embellishments.

The strongest classification is: **late but substantially corroborated eyewitness tradition concerning a private color lithograph; exact game reconstruction unresolved**. [Google Books, *Columbia Chess Chronicle*](https://books.google.com/books/about/Columbia_Chess_Chronicle.html?id=Fv42AAAAYAAJ); [transcription and issue guide](https://www.one-more-move-chess-art.com/One-More-Move.html)

### 4.5 Vibert authenticity

The Haggin Museum accepts and catalogues the panel as a work by Vibert. No rival claimant, attribution controversy, forgery allegation, or scientific challenge was located. Conversely, no published technical examination was found. Museum attribution is the present institutional judgment, not a laboratory finding.

### 4.6 Conflation of the two paintings

Retzsch’s and Vibert’s works share only chess subject matter and nineteenth-century date. Their artists, narratives, materials, dimensions, custody histories, and symbolic programs differ:

- Retzsch: youth versus Satan for the soul; angel; virtues and vices; sarcophagus; German Romantic moral allegory.
- Vibert: Napoleon versus Cardinal Fesch; Fontainebleau interior; imperial symbols; French historical and political satire.

Any source illustrating the “king has one more move” sermon with Vibert’s Napoleon painting, or identifying Vibert as Retzsch’s copyist, is conflating separate objects.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

Neither work is documented as a consecrated image, relic, ritual implement, or object of regulated veneration.

### Retzsch

Its original function was didactic and contemplative. The chessboard translates Christian moral psychology into visible strategy:

- the soul is embattled rather than passively judged;
- virtues and prayer defend it;
- vices operate through seduction, pride, deceit, violence, and unbelief;
- the sarcophagus establishes death as the boundary of play;
- the guardian spirit’s inability to dictate moves visualizes free choice.

Its later ritual-like use occurred in Protestant preaching and motivational discourse. Speakers transformed the alleged chess solution into a salvific maxim: apparent defeat is not final while the king retains a move. This is an attested modern use of the image, but not Retzsch’s documented wording and not proof of an objectively solvable board.

### Vibert

The painting’s function is secular satire and historical genre. “Check” turns a chess move into a metaphor for a cardinal’s political leverage over an emperor. Imperial emblems magnify the irony: Napoleon commands an empire yet is momentarily constrained at a small board by a churchman whose rank and collection depended upon Napoleonic patronage. The interpretation is the Haggin Museum’s plausible curatorial reading, not a recorded statement from Vibert. [Haggin Museum](https://hagginmuseum.org/collections/vibert-jehan-georges/check/)

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Objects

### 7.1 The Retzsch salvation legend

**Earliest located form, 1888–89**

Frith’s 1888 “Anecdote of Morphy” says that after supper in “Mr. H.’s” Richmond home, Morphy studied the picture and modestly stated that he could take the young man’s game and win. A board was arranged; the company reconstructed the position; and “victory was snatched from the devil and the young man saved.”

Harrison’s 1889 letter identifies himself as the host, corrects the date to autumn 1861, and admits that the position had to be inferred from the engraving. Morphy asked whether Harrison believed it had been set up correctly. After re-examination, Morphy said he believed he could win and reportedly did so quickly.

**Later elaborations**

The story subsequently acquired several unattested elements:

- the work hung in the Louvre;
- a tour guide or curator explained that the youth was checkmated;
- an unnamed world champion stared at it for hours;
- the master shouted, “The king has one more move”;
- the museum retitled the painting;
- the angel is looking directly at the uniquely saving piece;
- the position constitutes a formal chess problem with one exact solution.

These should be labeled **modern retellings**, not features of the 1888–89 evidence.

**Factual core**

A credible factual core is possible: Morphy was in Richmond in 1861; Harrison supplied an eyewitness account; another alleged attendee corroborated it; and Retzsch reproductions were widely available. What cannot now be recovered is the exact colored lithograph, its precise board, the legal position set up that evening, the moves played, or whether the reconstructed game faithfully reproduced Retzsch’s image.

### 7.2 Faust identification

Calling the youth “Faust” and the devil “Mephistopheles” is an early and understandable interpretive overlay, reinforced by Retzsch’s fame as an illustrator of Goethe. It is not a scene in Goethe’s *Faust*, and the first publication’s title names neither character. This is best classified as **institutional and popular identification based on thematic affinity**.

### 7.3 Louvre legend

No Louvre catalogue, accession number, loan history, or exhibition record was found. The 1999 oil’s published provenance runs through Isabella II, Chappuis, and Constantin before anonymous sale; the Met and British Museum own printed copies; Dresden owns the drawing. The Louvre claim is therefore **unsupported and probably a modern migration of the anecdote from Harrison’s private house to a famous museum**.

### 7.4 Legend around Vibert’s scene

The Haggin Museum presents the game as an episode from Napoleon’s life but gives no primary source. The precise match, position, and Fesch’s snuff-taking gesture may be Vibert’s invention. The painting is historical fiction unless corroborating contemporary evidence emerges. It has not acquired a comparable supernatural or salvation legend.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Retzsch

The composition became famous through reproduction. Its reproducibility is part of its object history, not incidental to it.

- **1837 British political parody:** John Doyle’s *Retzsch’s Extraordinary Design of Satan Playing at Chess with Man for his Soul*, published by Thomas McLean and lithographed by Alfred Ducôte, substituted Daniel O’Connell for Satan, Lord Melbourne for the endangered player, and Britannia for the angel. The table is supported by a three-headed dog, while the chessmen caricature political forces. The Met dates its copy to 29 September 1837; the British Museum calls it a parody of Retzsch’s already “well-known and justly-celebrated” etching. [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/736612); [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1868-0808-11819)
- **American publication:** the 1837 Boston edition renamed it *The Game of Life*, reinforcing its religious-moral reception.
- **Civil War reception:** later commentary identifies a Harper’s Weekly adaptation of 3 June 1865 presenting Union victory over the Confederacy through the composition. This claim is reported in secondary sources; the original page was not independently recovered in the present search and should be treated cautiously.
- **Chess culture:** the 1888–89 *Columbia Chess Chronicle* debate converted the image into an analyzable position and attached Morphy’s authority to it.
- **Christian preaching:** twentieth- and twenty-first-century sermons converted the Morphy anecdote into “The King has one more move.” This wording is a homiletic condensation rather than a quotation from Morphy.
- **Commercial replicas:** modern bronzes, prints, posters, digital images, and decorative reliefs circulate under *Checkmate* or *One More Move*. They frequently omit the original trilingual title and reproduce altered colors or board positions.
- **Cinema analogy:** scholarship discussing chess in art compares Retzsch’s allegory with Ingmar Bergman’s *The Seventh Seal* (1957), in which a knight plays chess with Death. This is thematic reception, not a demonstrated direct adaptation. [Metropolitan Museum Journal](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/The_Metropolitan_Museum_Journal_v_47_2012.pdf)

### Vibert

Vibert’s painting circulates through museum photography, postcards, and online reproductions. A circa-1990 postcard in the University of the Pacific’s Holt-Atherton Special Collections identifies it as *Check, Jehan-Georges Vibert, Haggin Museum*. [University of the Pacific](https://scholarlycommons.pacific.edu/postcards/610/)

Its public image remains tied to Napoleoniana and Vibert’s satires of cardinals. Unlike Retzsch’s composition, it has not become a widely documented sermon image, political emblem, or independently replicated cult object.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Retzsch

The historiography rests on several different kinds of evidence:

1. **Primary publication:** Fleischer’s 1831 trilingual edition, with von Miltitz’s explanation based on Retzsch’s indications.
2. **Early critical reception:** the detailed Dutch discussion in *De Gids* in 1837 and British descriptions from the same decade.
3. **Museum object records:** Dresden drawing, Met and British Museum editions, and the 1999 Christie’s lot.
4. **Chess-periodical controversy:** the *Columbia Chess Chronicle* correspondence of 1888–89.
5. **Modern scholarship:** studies of Retzsch’s etchings and broader scholarship on chess imagery.

The principal debates are:

- Did the oil precede the etching or follow it?
- How many painted versions did Retzsch himself make?
- Which later painted versions are autograph, workshop, period copies, or modern reproductions?
- Is “Faust and Mephistopheles” a legitimate subject identification or only a Faustian analogy?
- Can any printed or painted version be transcribed into a legal and determinate chess position?
- What position did Harrison and Morphy actually reconstruct in 1861?
- At what point did “The Chess Players” become “Checkmate,” and when did “one more move” enter the sermon tradition?
- What documentary evidence underlies the oil panel’s ownership by Isabella II?

Evanghelia Stead’s recent work on Retzsch’s etchings treats the artist in the context of reproducible outline art and its remediations; this is a more useful framework than assuming the celebrated image was always a unique easel painting. [Brill, *Moritz Retzsch’s Etchings*](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004543010/BP000012.pdf)

No scientific study of the 1999 panel was located. The Christie’s attribution therefore remains a connoisseurial and provenance judgment. The public evidence is insufficient to adjudicate the panel’s precise place in the creation sequence.

### Vibert

The painting’s scholarship is much thinner. Its central sources are the Haggin Museum catalogue entry and Patricia B. Sanders’s *The Haggin Collection* (1991), cited in museum-related literature but not located in full online. Critical questions remaining open include:

- exact date;
- first exhibition;
- original title and language;
- first buyer;
- date and mechanism of Haggin acquisition;
- whether Vibert drew upon a reported Napoleonic anecdote;
- signature and inscriptions;
- panel construction and conservation history.

The museum’s iconographic reading is plausible but should not substitute for provenance research. Dealer archives, Paris Salon catalogues, Vibert’s correspondence, and Haggin family purchase records are the most promising avenues.

## 12. Sources and Research Assessment

### Primary and near-primary sources

- Retzsch/von Miltitz/Fleischer, *Die Schachspieler—Les joueurs d’échecs—The Chess Players*, Leipzig, 1831.
- Von Miltitz and Retzsch, *The Game of Life, or, The Chess-Players*, Boston, Weeks, Jordan & Co., 1837.
- “De schaakspelers van Moritz Retzsch,” *De Gids*, 1837.
- John Doyle, Alfred Ducôte, and Thomas McLean, *Retzsch’s Extraordinary Design of Satan Playing at Chess with Man for his Soul*, 29 September 1837.
- *Columbia Chess Chronicle*, volumes III–IV, 1888–89: Frith anecdote, objections, Gilberg reconstruction, and Harrison testimony.
- Christie’s, *German Pictures*, lot record for Retzsch’s *Die Schachspieler*, 7 October 1999.
- Haggin Museum collection record for Vibert’s *Check*.
- 6 October 1931 correspondence from Haggin galleries director Harry Noyes Pratt, preserved in the Jacques Seligmann & Co. records and transcribed by the Smithsonian.

### Evidentiary conclusions

- **Secure:** the Retzsch composition was published under the trilingual title *The Chess Players* in 1831; the Dresden drawing and multiple institutional impressions survive; an attributed oil panel passed through the published Isabella II–Chappuis–Constantin provenance and sold in 1999.
- **Secure:** Vibert’s distinct *Check* is an undated oil-on-panel in the Haggin Museum.
- **Strong but retrospective:** Morphy examined and played from a reconstructed Retzsch position in Harrison’s Richmond home in 1861.
- **Unresolved:** whether the Retzsch oil or etching was the first completed version; the exact game position; the present location of the 1999 oil; the fate of Harrison’s lithograph.
- **Unsupported:** that Retzsch’s painting hung in the Louvre or was officially titled *Checkmate* by Retzsch.
- **Modern elaboration:** the exact formula “The king has one more move” and the familiar museum-curator version of the story.
- **Unestablished:** Vibert’s date, initial buyer, individual Haggin acquisition record, and the historicity of the depicted Napoleon–Fesch game.
- **No evidence found:** scientific examination, theft, wartime displacement, restitution controversy, or authenticity litigation concerning either principal panel.
