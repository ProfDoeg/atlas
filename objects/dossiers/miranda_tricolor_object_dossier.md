# Francisco de Miranda's tricolor flag: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary finding

“Francisco de Miranda’s tricolor flag” is not one continuously preserved textile. The name conflates at least four historically distinct things:

1. Miranda’s projected flags for a “Columbian” army, documented in plans from 1801 and in a Spanish archival drawing dated 13 May 1806.
2. The “Columbian colours” ceremonially raised aboard the *Leander* at Jacmel on 12 March 1806.
3. One or more expeditionary flags carried toward Venezuela, some captured at Ocumare and publicly destroyed by Spanish authorities, while others accompanied Miranda to La Vela de Coro and disappeared after his withdrawal.
4. The yellow-blue-red national design adopted by the Venezuelan Congress in July 1811 and subsequently inherited, with extensive alterations, by Venezuela, Gran Colombia, Colombia, Ecuador, and other political entities.

No original yellow-blue-red textile securely identifiable as the flag raised aboard the *Leander* or at La Vela de Coro is known to survive. What survives physically from 1806 consists principally of two contemporary or near-contemporary colored documentary representations in the Archivo General de Indias (AGI), Seville. These are drawings on parchment, not expeditionary flags. They record two other or potentially related designs: a black-red-yellow military tricolor and a blue naval flag with sun, moon, and a revolutionary motto.

Accordingly, this dossier treats the sacred or national “object” as a lost group of expeditionary textiles whose identity survives through eyewitness testimony, hostile administrative records, documentary drawings, later legislation, replicas, and political commemoration.

Evidence labels used below are:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary records or securely catalogued physical evidence.
- **Near-contemporary testimony:** an account by a participant, published shortly afterward, whose precision or interpretation may still be questioned.
- **Archival representation:** a surviving drawing or record, not the lost textile itself.
- **Scholarly inference:** a reconstruction argued from incomplete or conflicting evidence.
- **Official tradition:** a later state-sanctioned account.
- **Legend or late accretion:** an origin story lacking contemporary support.
- **Unresolved:** the sources do not permit a confident conclusion.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Names

The principal Spanish names are:

- **Bandera tricolor de Francisco de Miranda**
- **Tricolor mirandino**
- **Bandera de Miranda**
- **Bandera de Colombia** or **colores colombianos**, in Miranda’s continental rather than modern Colombian sense
- **Bandera Madre**, a modern honorific emphasizing its supposed descent into the flags of Venezuela, Colombia, and Ecuador
- **Bandera del Ejército Colombiano**
- **Pabellón de Miranda**
- **Pavellón de Miranda en su corveta**, the spelling appearing in the title associated with AGI MP-BANDERAS,17
- **“Vandera de Miranda para su proyecto exército con el nombre de Columbiano”**, the contemporary orthography of the title recorded for AGI MP-BANDERAS,18

In English it is commonly called **Miranda’s tricolor**, **Francisco de Miranda’s flag**, or the **Columbian colours**.

“Colombia,” “Columbian,” and Spanish *Colombiano/Columbiano* did not here denote only the present Republic of Colombia. Miranda used the name for the projected political community of Spanish America. The terminology is therefore historically broader than its modern national meaning.

### 1.2 The lost yellow-blue-red expeditionary tricolor

**Object class:** military, naval, and revolutionary flag or group of flags.

**Documented colors:** James Biggs, a member of the expedition, wrote that on 12 March 1806 “the Columbian colours were displayed on board for the first time” and that the ensign comprised “the three primary colours which predominate in the rainbow.” He did not name their order in that passage. Biggs’s account first appeared in 1808; the Library of Congress holds a public-domain Boston edition dated approximately 1809. [Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/item/tmp96008026/)

Spanish colonial testimony is more specific but contradictory:

- Witnesses Antonio Navarrete and Francisco Labastidas described the colors as *encarnado, azul y amarillo*—red, blue, and yellow.
- Another formulation in the same proceedings placed blue above, yellow in the middle, and red below.
- Ramón de Alves, reporting to the captain general on 16 April 1806, listed yellow, red, and blue.
- Miranda’s London military plan of 24 May 1801 reportedly requested ten flags in red, yellow, and blue zones.

These statements establish the recurring three-color vocabulary much more securely than they establish stripe order. The modern sequence yellow-blue-red cannot simply be projected backward onto every flag in Miranda’s expedition.

**Dimensions, weight, fibers, sewing, dyes, maker’s marks, and condition:** unknown. No securely identified original cloth has been located, catalogued, or scientifically examined. Claims that the lost expeditionary flag had a standardized 2:3 proportion or modern Venezuelan color specifications are reconstructions, not observations of the object.

A later account says that after the *Leander* reached Jacmel, Captain Thomas Lewis obtained cloth at Port-au-Prince and a tailor aboard the ship made the flag. This is plausible and consistent with the expedition’s circumstances, but no surviving invoice, tailor’s name, textile sample, or uncontested primary order has been identified in the sources consulted. It must remain **unverified secondary reconstruction**.

### 1.3 AGI MP-BANDERAS,18: the black-red-yellow army design

This is a real, surviving documentary artifact—not demonstrably an expeditionary textile.

- **Repository:** Archivo General de Indias, Seville.
- **Reference:** ES.41091.AGI/1.16418.2//MP-BANDERAS,18.
- **Formal title:** “Vandera de Miranda para su proyecto exército con el nombre de Columbiano.”
- **Date:** known by 13 May 1806.
- **Format:** one colored map/drawing on parchment.
- **Condition:** catalogued as good.
- **Associated file:** CARACAS,105.
- **Reproduction:** microfilmed as C-7459 and digitized.
- **Cataloguing authority:** description prepared by María Antonia Colomar Albajar, 5 March 2003. [PARES](https://pares.cultura.gob.es/BicentenarioIndependencias/catalogo/description/6501382)

The represented design consists of three equal horizontal bands:

- black above;
- red or *encarnado* in the center;
- yellow below.

Later descriptions attach the labels *negros*, *pardos*, and *indios* to the three stripes. The frequently repeated assertion that the colors therefore embodied racial equality in Miranda’s projected army is consistent with the annotations and with his effort to recruit across colonial caste divisions. Whether Miranda himself devised this precise explanation, however, depends on the authorship and derivation of the drawing, neither of which is securely established by the catalogue.

The dating is especially important. The archival artifact is dated 13 May 1806, after the capture of the *Bacchus* and *Bee*. It is evidence that Spanish officials possessed or reproduced such a design; it does not prove that this was the flag raised at Jacmel or La Vela.

### 1.4 AGI MP-BANDERAS,17: the naval pavilion

A second surviving documentary artifact depicts a radically different flag:

- **Repository:** Archivo General de Indias.
- **Reference:** ES.41091.AGI/1.16418.2//MP-BANDERAS,17.
- **Title:** “Pavellón de Miranda en su corveta.”
- **Date:** 1806.
- **Medium:** colored drawing on paper; specialist literature gives approximately 18.8 × 24 cm, although the current PARES catalogue describes the unit generically as one map on parchment.
- **Condition:** good.
- **Associated file:** CARACAS,106.
- **Microfilm:** C-7459.
- **Digitized:** yes. [PARES](https://pares.cultura.gob.es/BicentenarioIndependencias/catalogo/description/6501381)

The drawing shows:

- a predominantly dark-blue rectangular field;
- a rising yellow or reddish sun near the hoist;
- a pale full moon toward the fly;
- a red streamer or upper band;
- a partially abbreviated inscription usually expanded as **“Muera la tiranía y viva la libertad”**—“Death to tyranny and long live liberty.”

The surviving drawing itself reportedly reads “Muera la Tiranía y viva la &.&.&.” The familiar fully expanded phrase is therefore partly interpretive, although contemporary revolutionary vocabulary makes the expansion plausible. Modern versions often regularize the positions, proportions, faces of the sun and moon, lettering, and shades.

The symbolism of a rising American sun and a declining Spanish moon is a later explanatory tradition rather than a securely recorded statement by Miranda. Modern official descriptions of the Falcón state flag have entrenched that interpretation.

### 1.5 The 1811 national flag

The design presented by Miranda and Lino de Clemente to the Venezuelan Congress consisted of horizontal yellow, blue, and red bands. Early descriptions and later reconstructions generally give the yellow stripe half the flag’s depth and the blue and red stripes one quarter each. A canton or device representing an Indigenous woman, liberty, or the confederation appears in descriptions and reconstructions of the First Republic’s state flag.

This 1811 flag is materially and legally distinct from any cloth carried in 1806. Its genealogy is ideological and chromatic, not a demonstrated chain of physical identity.

### 1.6 What survives

| Flag or flags landed at La Vela de Coro, August 1806 | No known textile | Removed with the expedition or otherwise lost |

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Documentary prehistory, 1801

The earliest firm documentary precursor is Miranda’s military planning in London in 1801. A plan dated 24 May reportedly specified ten flags divided into three zones of red, yellow, and blue. This evidence appears in the published *Archivo del General Miranda*, volume XVI, page 167, and is discussed in Venezuelan historical scholarship. The surviving Miranda archive, later called *Colombeia*, is now held by Venezuela’s Archivo General de la Nación and is registered by UNESCO’s Memory of the World program. [UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/es/memory-world/lac/colombeia-generalissimo-francisco-de-mirandas-archives-1771-1810)

This establishes that Miranda contemplated a red-yellow-blue tricolor years before the *Leander* expedition. It does not establish the eventual stripe order or show that any 1801 textile was manufactured.

The black-red-yellow design preserved in MP-BANDERAS,18 is often dated loosely to 1800 or 1801 in popular literature. The secure date attached to the surviving archival sheet is 13 May 1806. An earlier conceptual origin is possible but should not be confused with the date of the physical drawing.

### 2.2 Manufacture at Jacmel

The *Leander* left New York on 2 February 1806 and reached Jacmel in Haiti later that month. The expedition obtained the schooners *Bacchus* and *Bee*, provisions, recruits, and other supplies. A later reconstruction places the procurement of flag cloth in Port-au-Prince and its sewing aboard ship between 18 February and 12 March.

The artisan has not been securely named. No documented price, textile specification, dye source, or workshop survives in the sources examined. Statements that Miranda personally “made” the flag should normally be understood as design authorship, not literal cutting, dyeing, or sewing.

### 2.3 First secure display: 12 March 1806

Biggs’s participant narrative supplies the earliest detailed, securely datable attestation of the “Columbian colours” as an actual flag in use. On 12 March, aboard the *Leander* at Jacmel, the ensign was raised ceremonially. A cannon was fired, and the expedition drank to the success of a banner expected to carry liberty and humanity to an oppressed country.

The evidentiary limits are crucial:

- Biggs says the colors were first displayed that day.
- He calls them the three primary colors predominant in the rainbow.
- He does not state their order.
- He does not give dimensions, fabric, devices, or lettering.
- He does not prove that precisely the same cloth later reached La Vela.

Press reports in Britain later called Miranda’s emblems the insignia or colors of the ancient Peruvians. Reports published in July attributed to them the slogan “Viva el Rey de España, y muera el mal Gobierno”—“Long live the King of Spain and death to bad government.” These notices may reflect confused reporting, a different flag or cockade, a tactical constitutional-monarchist slogan, or journalistic conflation. They cannot safely be harmonized with MP-BANDERAS,17.

### 2.4 The 3 August 1806 land display

After the failure at Ocumare and British-supported reorganization in the Caribbean, Miranda’s expedition reached La Vela de Coro at the beginning of August. Biggs wrote that on 3 August the rising sun witnessed “the colours of Colombia” flying from the staff long occupied by the Spanish royal flag.

The exact site varies in later accounts:

- the Fortín de San Pedro;
- the parish church tower;
- more generally, La Vela de Coro.

The fort is the predominant historical reconstruction; the Venezuelan Foreign Ministry’s official account mentions both the parish tower and, when quoting historian Carmen Bohórquez, the Fortín San Pedro. This internal inconsistency demonstrates how commemoration has outgrown the precision of the evidence. [Venezuelan Foreign Ministry](https://mppre.gob.ve/index.php/detalle/2)

### 2.5 Origin legends

#### Goethe and color theory

**Late tradition / unsupported as a quoted conversation.** A widely circulated story places Miranda and Johann Wolfgang von Goethe at Weimar, where Goethe supposedly told him that his destiny was to create a country in which the primary colors would not be distorted. No contemporaneous letter, diary entry, or authenticated Goethe text containing the familiar speech has been identified.

Chronology also weakens its usual formulation: Goethe’s *Zur Farbenlehre* appeared in 1810, after Miranda’s documented 1801 plan and the 1806 expedition, although Goethe had investigated color before publication. Miranda visited Weimar and knew Goethe; that biographical fact does not authenticate the dialogue.

Carmen Bohórquez has proposed that Miranda’s scientific reading, including Newtonian discussions of light and contemporary color theory, helps explain his choice. This is a modern scholarly interpretation, not a recorded explanation by Miranda. [Ciudad CCS account of Bohórquez’s argument](https://ciudadccs.info/publicacion/27714-bohorquez-trabajo-de-newton-inspiro-a-miranda-a-disenar-la-bandera)

#### Catherine II

**Legend / romantic accretion.** Another story says yellow represented Catherine II’s blond hair, blue her eyes, and red her lips. Variants substitute another woman, sometimes Christina Hall. The tale has no identified contemporary documentary basis and turns a continental political emblem into a romantic souvenir. Its first circulation remains insufficiently traced.

#### Gold, sea, and blood

**Later national pedagogy.** The meanings “yellow for mineral wealth or gold, blue for the sea, and red for the blood of heroes” are familiar modern explanations of the Venezuelan, Colombian, and Ecuadorian flags. They are not recorded in Biggs’s account and should not be attributed to Miranda without qualification.

#### Indigenous rainbow or Inca emblem

**Plausible context, unproven direct derivation.** Contemporary newspapers called the expeditionary standards Peruvian or linked them to “ancient Peruvians.” Miranda’s continental name “Colombia,” his interest in Indigenous American antiquity, and the rainbow description made such an association intelligible. No surviving order states that he copied a particular Inca banner.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 Conceptual designs, 1801–1805

Miranda retained plans and papers among the archive he assembled as *Colombeia*. The proposal for ten red-yellow-blue flags belongs to this documentary—not textile—chain. The papers were ultimately preserved in sixty-three volumes, held for much of the twentieth century by Venezuela’s Academia Nacional de la Historia and transferred in 2010 to the Archivo General de la Nación. UNESCO describes the collection as a closed archive organized by Miranda himself. [UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/es/memory-world/lac/colombeia-generalissimo-francisco-de-mirandas-archives-1771-1810)

There is no continuous custody history for an 1801 cloth because no such cloth is securely attested.

### 3.2 New York to Haiti, February–March 1806

The *Leander*, reportedly financed through merchant Samuel Ogden and other supporters and commanded by Thomas Lewis, sailed from New York. Miranda and his expedition controlled whatever flags were aboard. At Jacmel, one “Columbian” ensign was ceremonially raised on 12 March.

Custody at this stage was corporate and military: Miranda as commander and the *Leander*’s officers and crew as bearers. No named flag keeper, ensign, or quartermaster has been securely identified.

### 3.3 Jacmel to Aruba and Ocumare, March–April 1806

The expedition sailed from Haiti with the *Leander*, *Bacchus*, and *Bee*. On 28 April Spanish guard vessels captured the two schooners and approximately fifty-eight to sixty expeditionaries near Ocumare. Arms, uniforms, proclamations, portraits, patents, and flags were taken as evidence.

The chain for the captured flags was therefore:

1. Miranda’s expedition;
2. crews of *Bacchus* and *Bee*;
3. Spanish naval captors;
4. royal authorities at Puerto Cabello and Caracas;
5. judicial custody as *cuerpos del delito*;
6. public exposure and destruction by order of the colonial government.

The exact number and design of the captured flags is not reliably established. It cannot be assumed that all were yellow-blue-red or that the principal *Leander* ensign was among them.

### 3.4 Judicial humiliation and destruction, July–August 1806

Ten captured expeditionaries were executed at Puerto Cabello on 21 July. The Spanish sentence ordered “Miranda’s flag” fixed ignominiously on the scaffold during the execution and for three hours afterward. It then ordered the flag, Miranda’s proclamation, Daniel Kemper’s commission, and Miranda’s effigy burned by the executioner.

The official *Gaceta de Madrid* published this account on 11 November 1806. It is the strongest primary evidence for the physical destruction of at least one captured expeditionary flag. [Gaceta de Madrid, 11 November 1806](https://www.boe.es/gazeta/dias/1806/11/11/pdfs/GMD-1806-92.pdf)

Other records and later historians place a burning of captured flags, proclamations, and Miranda’s portrait in the Plaza Mayor of Caracas on 4 August. The apparent difference may reflect multiple flags and multiple ritual destructions—Puerto Cabello and Caracas—rather than mutually exclusive accounts. The singular “Miranda’s flag” in the sentence should not automatically be taken as the unique tricolor first raised at Jacmel.

These were deliberate political destructions. The flags were treated as criminal evidence, publicly dishonored, and annihilated to dramatize royal authority. No fragment is documented as surviving.

### 3.5 Surviving flags with Miranda, May–August 1806

The *Leander* escaped the Ocumare action. Miranda regrouped through Bonaire, Grenada, Barbados, and Trinidad and obtained British naval protection and material assistance. The surviving flags followed the expedition’s mobile headquarters, although the sources do not name each transfer.

At La Vela, the “colours of Colombia” replaced the Spanish royal flag on 3 August. After failing to obtain local support, Miranda evacuated Coro and La Vela around 13 August. A modern scholarly account states that no flags remained there because Miranda took them away.

Their later disposition is undocumented. Possibilities include:

- continued use aboard the expedition;
- storage or disposal in Trinidad or the British Caribbean;
- transport back to Britain;
- decay, loss at sea, or unrecorded destruction.

No candidate textile with a documented chain from Miranda, the *Leander*, or La Vela has emerged. The chain therefore breaks conclusively after August 1806.

### 3.6 Custody of the AGI drawings

MP-BANDERAS,17 and 18 were created or acquired within the Spanish imperial administrative response to the expedition. Their archival producer is catalogued as the Secretaría de Estado y del Despacho de Hacienda. They were associated with the Caracas files numbered 106 and 105 respectively and eventually entered the Archivo General de Indias.

Carlos Benito Figueredo publicized the black-red-yellow design after finding the documentation in the AGI in 1896. His discovery triggered debate in the Venezuelan press, including *El Tiempo* and *El Liberal*. The drawings remained Spanish state archival property, were microfilmed, digitized, and formally redescribed in 2003.

They are not looted expeditionary textiles. Their precise authorship is unknown: they may be official copies drawn from captured evidence, witnesses’ information, or now-lost originals.

### 3.7 Adoption in 1811

On 5 July 1811 the Venezuelan Congress appointed Miranda, Lino de Clemente, and José de Sata y Bussy to devise the flag and cockade of the new confederation. Sata y Bussy was especially associated with the cockade; Miranda and Clemente presented the flag.

The design was considered on 9 July and approved in the sessions of 14–15 July. It was publicly borne and raised in Caracas during the proclamation of independence. [Fundación Empresas Polar](https://bibliofep.fundacionempresaspolar.org/dhv/entradas/c/congreso-de-1811/) [Venezuelan Foreign Ministry](https://mppre.gob.ve/index.php/detalle/2)

This was a new legal act and almost certainly involved newly manufactured flags. There is no proof that an 1806 cloth was physically reused. The 1811 republic inherited Miranda’s palette and political symbolism, not a demonstrated fabric object.

### 3.8 Nineteenth- and twentieth-century descendants

The tricolor passed through a succession of national and military specifications:

- First Republic of Venezuela, 1811;
- Venezuelan republican regimes after the collapse of the First Republic;
- Republic of Colombia or Gran Colombia from 1819;
- successor national flags of Venezuela, Colombia, and Ecuador;
- numerous military, provincial, municipal, and political derivatives.

Stripe proportions, emblems, stars, coats of arms, inscriptions, and shades repeatedly changed. These flags form a genealogical series, not a single conserved artifact.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Is there an “original Miranda flag” today?

No authenticated original textile is currently documented.

A responsible institutional label for MP-BANDERAS,17 or 18 should describe it as an 1806 archival drawing or representation. Calling either “the original flag” collapses the distinction between:

- a design recorded on paper or parchment;
- a textile aboard a ship;
- a captured flag destroyed in 1806;
- the flag raised at La Vela;
- and the 1811 national flag.

### 4.2 Which design flew at Jacmel?

Three principal reconstructions compete:

1. **Yellow-blue-red:** Venezuela’s official national tradition and the dominant modern interpretation.
2. **Red-yellow-blue:** inferred from Miranda’s 1801 written plan and a literal reading of Biggs’s “primary colours.”
3. **Blue-yellow-red:** reported by colonial witnesses concerning a flag observed in Venezuela.

Biggs does not settle the stripe order. Nor does MP-BANDERAS,18, because it shows black-red-yellow and postdates the flag-raising. The modern yellow-blue-red arrangement is historically plausible and was unquestionably used in 1811, but its exact physical identity with the Jacmel flag remains **unresolved**.

### 4.3 Which flag flew at La Vela?

The majority Venezuelan tradition identifies it as yellow-blue-red. Some historians, especially after Figueredo’s AGI discovery, argued that the black-red-yellow military standard was raised. Colonial witnesses reportedly described blue-yellow-red. Biggs merely called it the “colours of Colombia.”

The evidence supports the presence of a Miranda-associated flag, but not a uniquely recoverable layout. Assertions that the exact modern tricolor was first raised on Venezuelan soil should be labeled **official tradition grounded in incomplete contemporary evidence**.

### 4.4 Naval flag versus national tricolor

MP-BANDERAS,17’s sun-and-moon pavilion may have been:

- a ship’s flag distinct from the army flag;
- a revolutionary signal;
- a Spanish official’s reconstruction;
- or a design considered but not used as Biggs’s “Columbian colours.”

Its iconography and motto do not match the plain rainbow tricolor. The expedition could have carried several flags simultaneously; eighteenth- and early nineteenth-century ships normally distinguished national ensigns, command flags, signals, and pennants. There is no need to force the surviving designs into a single object.

### 4.5 Scientific examination

No published fiber microscopy, dye analysis, radiocarbon dating, spectroscopic examination, or conservation report for an original expeditionary cloth was located, because no such cloth is securely known.

The AGI drawings have archival descriptions and preservation status but no scientific material analysis was identified in the accessible catalogue. Their dates rest on administrative context rather than laboratory testing.

### 4.6 The black-versus-blue problem

A central historiographical issue is whether *negro* in the army design became *azul* in the expeditionary or national flag. Possible explanations include:

- genuinely different flags for different functions;
- a change of design;
- copying or reporting error;
- dark blue perceived as black;
- later national harmonization.

The sources do not justify choosing a single explanation. The safest conclusion is that Miranda entertained or used multiple designs, and the yellow-blue-red national genealogy was retrospectively made dominant.

### 4.7 Authority to authenticate

Authority has shifted among:

- Spanish colonial courts, which treated captured flags as evidence;
- the Archivo General de Indias, which authenticates the surviving sheets as archival records;
- Venezuelan historians and the Academia Nacional de la Historia;
- the Venezuelan legislature and executive, which define official national memory;
- modern vexillologists and conservators, who distinguish designs and material objects.

Legislation can authenticate an emblem’s legal status; it cannot establish that a surviving modern cloth is the textile flown in 1806.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Shipboard inauguration

The ceremony of 12 March 1806 functioned as the flag’s political consecration:

- the colors were raised;
- a cannon was fired;
- the expedition celebrated;
- toasts were made to liberty and humanity;
- personnel were bound to a projected “Colombia” that did not yet exist as a state.

The flag therefore created a temporary political community before it represented an established government. It served as a visual claim that Miranda’s force was an army of liberation rather than a private raiding expedition.

### 6.2 Military identification

The expedition needed to distinguish itself from British, American, French, Haitian, and Spanish forces. Flags marked ships, landing parties, headquarters, and military formations. The existence of several designs is compatible with different naval and army functions.

### 6.3 Replacement of royal sovereignty

At La Vela, Miranda’s colors occupied a staff formerly used by the Spanish royal flag. This was a performative transfer of sovereignty. Although Miranda held the site only briefly and gained little local support, the replacement of the royal emblem became the episode’s most durable political act.

### 6.4 Judicial counter-ritual

Spanish authorities answered the raising with an inverse ceremony:

- displaying the captured flag ignominiously at the scaffold;
- executing expeditionaries in its presence;
- leaving it exposed;
- burning it through the public executioner;
- burning Miranda’s effigy and writings alongside it.

The destruction did not merely dispose of evidence. It symbolically criminalized the projected Columbian polity.

### 6.5 National legitimation

In July 1811 the tricolor legitimized the newly independent Venezuelan confederation. Its public procession and raising converted revolutionary colors into a state emblem.

Later republics claimed descent from this act. The tricolor became a portable genealogy of sovereignty: governments could alter stars or arms while asserting continuity with Miranda and independence.

### 6.6 Flag Day

Venezuela formerly commemorated 12 March, the Jacmel shipboard raising, as Flag Day. In 2006 the government moved the observance to 3 August, presenting the La Vela raising as the first display of the national flag on Venezuelan soil. This change relocated the symbolic center from a Haitian harbor and multinational expedition to Venezuelan territory.

The official account explicitly frames Miranda’s 1806 banner as the ancestor of the national flag. [Venezuelan Foreign Ministry](https://mppre.gob.ve/index.php/detalle/2)

### 6.7 Access and taboos

No special eighteenth-century rule limiting who could see or touch Miranda’s flags is known. Modern Venezuelan flag law and military protocol regulate respectful display and treatment of the national emblem, but those rules govern successor flags, not the lost 1806 textile.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The single original flag

**Modern simplification.** Schoolbook and commemorative accounts often speak of “the flag” as though one cloth traveled intact from Jacmel to La Vela and then became the national flag of 1811.

Evidence instead indicates several flags aboard several vessels, different military and naval designs, the capture and burning of some, and no continuous material custody into 1811.

### 7.2 The three republics born from one cloth

**National metaphor, not material provenance.** Venezuela, Colombia, and Ecuador do share a flag genealogy through Venezuela’s 1811 tricolor and Gran Colombia. They did not inherit pieces of the *Leander* flag, nor is there evidence that the same textile passed among their governments.

### 7.3 Goethe’s prophecy

The polished dialogue in which Goethe identifies Miranda’s destiny through the primary colors is a late literary construction unless an authentic earlier source can be produced. It gives European philosophical sanction to the national emblem and converts an uncertain design process into a prophetic scene.

### 7.4 Catherine II’s portrait in color

The hair-eyes-lips explanation is an undated romantic story. It reflects Miranda’s famous association with Catherine II but is unsupported as an account of the flag’s creation.

### 7.5 The rainbow

This is the closest explanation to contemporary testimony because Biggs explicitly invoked the primary colors predominant in the rainbow. Even so, Biggs did not say Miranda chose them because of Newton, Goethe, Inca symbolism, or continental racial unity. Those are later interpretive layers.

### 7.6 Rising America and declining Spain

The sun-and-moon reading of MP-BANDERAS,17 is visually persuasive and now institutionalized in Falcón’s symbolism. The surviving 1806 caption does not itself supply the full modern interpretation. It should be called a later emblematic exegesis rather than Miranda’s documented words.

### 7.7 Gold, sea, and blood

This pedagogical explanation belongs to the national afterlife of the tricolor. Its enormous circulation has often displaced the earlier “primary colors of the rainbow” account.

### 7.8 A surviving original hidden in an archive or museum

No serious published provenance identifies a museum textile as the Jacmel or La Vela flag. Confusion commonly arises because the AGI drawings are called “the flags of Miranda.” They are authentic 1806 archival objects but not cloth flags.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 The expeditionary textiles

**Status:** lost or destroyed.

- At least one captured flag was publicly destroyed under a documented Spanish sentence in 1806.
- Other captured flags may have been burned at Caracas.
- Flags remaining with Miranda after La Vela disappear from the record.
- No accession number, scientific examination, or credible modern ownership claim for an original yellow-blue-red *Leander* textile has been located.

### 8.2 MP-BANDERAS,17

**Location:** Archivo General de Indias, Seville, Spain.

**Reference:** ES.41091.AGI/1.16418.2//MP-BANDERAS,17.

**Access:** catalogued and digitized through PARES; the original is subject to Spanish archival access and conservation rules.

**Condition:** “Bueno.”

**Legal control:** Spanish state archival custody.

### 8.3 MP-BANDERAS,18

**Location:** Archivo General de Indias, Seville.

**Reference:** ES.41091.AGI/1.16418.2//MP-BANDERAS,18.

**Access and condition:** digitized and catalogued; condition recorded as good.

There is no known restitution claim on the basis that these sheets were the expeditionary flags themselves. A broader debate could concern colonial records removed to metropolitan archives, but no active proceeding specific to these drawings was located.

### 8.4 Miranda’s documentary archive

The *Colombeia* archive is held by Venezuela’s Archivo General de la Nación. It entered UNESCO’s regional Memory of the World register in 2002 and the international register in 2007. It preserves the documentary context for Miranda’s flag planning, but not an authenticated original flag. [UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/es/memory-world/lac/colombeia-generalissimo-francisco-de-mirandas-archives-1771-1810)

### 8.5 Present national emblem

Venezuela’s current flag is legally inspired by the flag adopted by Congress in 1811. The 2006 law retained yellow, blue, and red, added an eighth star, and revised the arms. These specifications govern newly manufactured flags and do not claim literal textile continuity with 1806.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 National reproduction

The yellow-blue-red pattern became one of the most consequential chromatic schemes in the Americas. It appears, with different proportions and charges, in:

- Venezuela;
- Colombia;
- Ecuador;
- former Gran Colombia;
- military and naval standards;
- departmental, provincial, and municipal flags;
- political insignia, monuments, textbooks, stamps, coins, posters, and state ceremonies.

“Bandera Madre” is best understood as a commemorative title for this visual genealogy.

### 9.2 Falcón state flag

In 2006 Falcón adopted a flag modeled on the sun-and-moon naval pavilion:

- dark-blue field;
- rising sun;
- moon;
- red upper band;
- “MUERA LA TIRANÍA Y VIVA LA LIBERTAD.”

The modern flag regularizes and interprets MP-BANDERAS,17. It is a derivative replica-design with its own legal life, not a restoration of the *Leander* flag.

### 9.3 Miranda state flag

The Venezuelan state of Miranda adopted a modern emblem drawing on the black-red-yellow “Columbian Army” design, adding stars and regional devices. This elevated the once-obscure MP-BANDERAS,18 scheme into a contemporary subnational symbol.

### 9.4 Monuments at La Vela

The centenary in 1906 produced organized fundraising and a monument near the ruins of Fortín San Pedro. A later Monumento a la Bandera was constructed in 1976. A 2006 historical assessment reported that monument destroyed and its site in ruins, while the nearby statue of Miranda was in poor condition.

These commemorative structures demonstrate that the lost cloth’s symbolic location—La Vela—became a substitute relic site. Coro and its port are UNESCO World Heritage properties, although their inscription concerns colonial urban architecture rather than the authenticity of a flag location. [UNESCO, Coro and its Port](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/658/)

### 9.5 Digital reconstructions

Modern SVG and PNG representations commonly impose:

- standardized 2:3 dimensions;
- fixed modern color values;
- equal stripes for the 1806 tricolor;
- smooth typographic inscriptions;
- symmetrical celestial devices.

Such images are useful diagrams, not documentary facsimiles. One widely circulated Wikimedia SVG explicitly identifies itself as the contributor’s own work and has undergone repeated recoloring. [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AFlag_of_Francisco_de_Miranda_%281806%29.svg)

### 9.6 Political mobilization

Successive Venezuelan governments have used Miranda and the flag to claim revolutionary continuity. The 2006 change of Flag Day, during Hugo Chávez’s presidency, connected the modern “Bolivarian” state to the landing at La Vela and foregrounded continental liberation.

The emblem is therefore not politically neutral. Competing governments and movements may disagree about stars, arms, dates, and official narratives while all invoking Miranda as an ancestral authority.

### 9.7 Literature and visual art

Paintings and textbook illustrations frequently portray Miranda holding a fully standardized yellow-blue-red flag. Unless contemporary, these are retrospective iconography. They reveal how later societies visualized the event, not how the flag can be physically authenticated.

Modern commercial replicas of the “naval,” “army,” and tricolor designs are widespread. No commercial replica located has an independent provenance linking it to 1806.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Primary evidentiary pillars

The reconstruction rests chiefly on:

1. Miranda’s plans and papers in *Colombeia*.
2. James Biggs’s participant narrative.
3. Spanish naval, judicial, and administrative records.
4. The official sentence and its publication in the *Gaceta de Madrid*.
5. Witness testimony in AGI CARACAS,458 and ESTADO,68, no. 44.
6. MP-BANDERAS,17 and 18.
7. Proceedings of the Venezuelan Congress in July 1811.
8. Contemporary and near-contemporary newspapers.

### 10.2 Biggs’s value and limits

Biggs witnessed the expedition and supplies the most important ceremonial descriptions. His account was published close to the events. Nevertheless, he wrote a narrative shaped by disappointment and polemic; his failure to name the colors or order is consequential. Later writers have often quoted him as if he explicitly said “yellow, blue, and red.” He did not in the cited passage.

### 10.3 Figueredo’s 1896 discovery

Carlos Benito Figueredo’s publication of the AGI material destabilized the settled patriotic account. The black-red-yellow design seemed to contradict the familiar national tricolor and generated a debate over whether it was:

- Miranda’s first design;
- his military flag;
- the actual La Vela flag;
- or merely one flag among several.

The modern archival catalogue supports its existence as a 1806 documentary object but does not resolve its operational use.

### 10.4 Venezuelan historical positions

Historians including Francisco Javier Yánez, José Félix Blanco, and Arístides Rojas helped establish the yellow-blue-red tradition. Later scholars such as Tomás Polanco Alcántara, Carmen Bohórquez, and Inés Quintero broadly maintained the tricolor’s centrality.

Other writers, including Mario Briceño Perozo, Carlos Medina Chirinos, and Manuel Vicente Magallanes, gave greater weight to the black-red-yellow flag or the AGI drawings.

The 2006 study in the *Boletín de la Academia Nacional de la Historia* concluded that no expeditionary copy survived: those at Ocumare were burned and those at Coro departed with Miranda. It explicitly called for further research into the black/blue discrepancy. [Boletín de la Academia Nacional de la Historia](https://biblat.unam.mx/hevila/BoletindelaAcademiaNacionaldelaHistoriaCaracas/2006/vol89/no354/2.pdf)

### 10.5 Recent contextual scholarship

A 2022 article in the Academy’s *Boletín* emphasized the conflicts among:

- Biggs’s rainbow colors;
- British reports of “ancient Peruvian” insignia;
- reports of the slogan supporting the Spanish king while condemning bad government;
- the black-red-yellow army drawing;
- and the blue sun-and-moon pavilion.

This approach treats the expedition’s visual culture as plural rather than searching for one canonical design. [Academia Nacional de la Historia, Boletín no. 419](https://www.anhvenezuela.org.ve/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Boletin-N%C2%B0-419.pdf)

### 10.6 Unpublished or inaccessible evidence

Important unresolved work includes:

- complete diplomatic transcription of CARACAS,105, 106, and 458;
- identification of the hands and pigments of MP-BANDERAS,17 and 18;
- tracing their transfer into the AGI’s Mapas y Planos series;
- locating the earliest print appearances of the Goethe and Catherine stories;
- reconstructing the captured flags individually from inventories and trial papers;
- determining whether British naval logs distinguish the expedition’s flags;
- searching Miranda’s British papers for tailoring expenses, flag orders, or sketches;
- identifying all contemporary Caribbean and Atlantic newspaper variants.

### 10.7 Discredited or unsupported propositions still circulating

The following should not be repeated as established facts:

- that a single original Miranda flag survives in a museum;
- that the AGI drawings are original cloth flags;
- that Goethe certainly dictated the colors to Miranda;
- that Catherine II’s appearance supplied the colors;
- that Miranda documented yellow as gold, blue as sea, and red as blood;
- that the exact modern Venezuelan flag was raised in 1806;
- that one cloth passed physically from Miranda to Venezuela, Gran Colombia, Colombia, and Ecuador.

## 11. Chronology

| Date | Object history |
|---|---|
| **24 May 1801** | **Documented proposal:** Miranda’s London military plan reportedly specifies ten flags in three zones, red, yellow, and blue. No cloth survives. |
| **1801–1805** | Miranda develops the symbols of a projected continental “Colombia”; exact flag sequence and manufacture remain uncertain. |
| **2 February 1806** | *Leander* sails from New York with Miranda’s expedition. Flags aboard at departure are not securely described. |
| **18 February–March 1806** | At Jacmel and Port-au-Prince, the expedition acquires supplies; later accounts place the procurement and sewing of tricolor cloth here. |
| **12 March 1806** | **Near-contemporary eyewitness account:** the “Columbian colours,” comprising the three primary rainbow colors, are raised aboard *Leander*. Cannon salute and toasts follow. |
| **27 March 1806** | Expedition leaves Haiti with *Leander*, *Bacchus*, and *Bee*; the flags travel under expeditionary custody. |
| **11–16 April 1806** | Ramón de Alves reports Miranda’s colors as yellow, red, and blue after the expedition’s appearance near Aruba. |
| **28 April 1806** | Spanish forces capture *Bacchus*, *Bee*, their crews, and expeditionary material including flags near Ocumare. |
| **13 May 1806** | Date attached to AGI MP-BANDERAS,18, the black-red-yellow “Columbian Army” design. |
| **June–July 1806** | British newspapers report “ancient Peruvian” insignia and, in some versions, “Viva el Rey de España, y muera el mal Gobierno.” |
| **21 July 1806** | Ten captured expeditionaries are executed at Puerto Cabello. The sentence orders Miranda’s flag displayed ignominiously at the scaffold and burned. |
| **1–3 August 1806** | Miranda’s reorganized expedition lands at La Vela de Coro. On 3 August “the colours of Colombia” replace the royal flag. Exact design disputed. |
| **4 August 1806** | Records and later histories place the burning of captured flags, proclamations, and Miranda’s portrait in Caracas. |
| **13 August 1806** | Expedition withdraws. Flags still in Miranda’s possession leave with it; their subsequent fate is unknown. |
| **11 November 1806** | *Gaceta de Madrid* publishes the sentence describing the scaffold display and destruction of Miranda’s flag. |
| **1808** | James Biggs’s participant narrative appears in Boston, providing the foundational account of the flag ceremonies. |
| **5 July 1811** | Venezuelan Congress commissions Miranda, Clemente, and Sata y Bussy to create national symbols. |
| **9 July 1811** | Miranda and Clemente present the yellow-blue-red design. |
| **14–15 July 1811** | Congress approves the national flag. This is a new official design, not proven to be an 1806 textile. |
| **1819–1830** | Gran Colombia uses yellow-blue-red flags, institutionalizing the tricolor’s continental legacy. |
| **1830 onward** | Venezuela, Colombia, and Ecuador develop separate national specifications from the shared tricolor lineage. |
| **1896** | Carlos Benito Figueredo publicizes the AGI army-flag drawing, reopening the color and identity dispute. |
| **1906** | Centenary commemorations at La Vela produce organized fundraising and a monument near Fortín San Pedro. |
| **1917** | Venezuelan illustrated discussion helps circulate the AGI designs and the problem of Miranda’s multiple flags. |
| **1929–1950** | First major edition of the *Archivo del General Miranda* is published in twenty-four volumes. |
| **1976** | Monumento a la Bandera constructed at La Vela; a new edition project for *Colombeia* is ordered. |
| **2002** | *Colombeia* enters UNESCO’s regional Memory of the World register. |
| **2003** | María Antonia Colomar Albajar completes the modern PARES catalogue descriptions of MP-BANDERAS,17 and 18. |
| **2006** | Bicentenary: Venezuela moves Flag Day to 3 August; Falcón adopts a derivative of the naval pavilion; Miranda state adopts a derivative of the black-red-yellow design. |
| **2007** | *Colombeia* enters UNESCO’s international Memory of the World register. |
| **2010** | Venezuela transfers *Colombeia* from the Academia Nacional de la Historia to the Archivo General de la Nación. |
| **2026** | No authenticated expeditionary textile is publicly known. The two AGI drawings survive in Seville; Miranda’s written archive remains in Caracas; the tricolor continues as a national and transnational emblem. |

### Overall conclusion

The historically defensible “life” of Miranda’s tricolor is the life of a lost and partly destroyed group of flags, not of one extant relic. Its documented sequence begins in written military planning by 1801, becomes material at Jacmel in March 1806, divides among ships and military functions, suffers capture and ritual destruction at Spanish hands, briefly displaces the royal flag at La Vela, and then vanishes as cloth. Five years later, its colors were reconstituted as law and ceremony by Venezuela’s First Republic.

The surviving AGI drawings are the closest material witnesses but represent competing designs rather than the canonical yellow-blue-red textile. The flag’s later identity was produced through legislation, historical writing, monuments, replicas, and national ritual. Its power as a national symbol is fully documented; the precise appearance and fate of the original expeditionary cloth remain unresolved.
