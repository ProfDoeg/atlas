# Ganesha's broken tusk (Ekadanta): Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `ganeshas_broken_tusk_ekadanta_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary conclusion

Ganesha’s broken tusk is a **legendary divine body-part, weapon, writing implement, and iconographic attribute**, not a securely documented physical relic. No published temple catalogue, museum record, archaeological report, or scientific examination located for this dossier identifies a surviving elephant tooth or ivory object as the original tusk of Ganesha. Nor is there a continuous traditional chain assigning the detached tusk to a particular shrine, treasury, grave, or terrestrial findspot.

Its reconstructable history therefore has several distinct layers:

1. **The one-tusked body:** texts and images identify Ganesha as *Ekadanta*, “One-Tusked,” without necessarily explaining how the other tusk was lost.
2. **The detached attribute:** sculpture and painting frequently place a tusk-shaped object in one of Ganesha’s hands. This iconographic tusk can function as an identifying emblem or weapon without illustrating a specific narrative.
3. **Competing loss narratives:** later texts explain the asymmetry through incompatible stories—Paraśurāma cuts the tusk off; Ganesha tears it out as a weapon; he throws it at the Moon; or, in the now-dominant popular story, he uses it as a pen while writing the *Mahābhārata*.
4. **Modern surrogate relics:** museums preserve representations of Ganesha holding the tusk, while the 2017 video game *Uncharted: The Lost Legacy* invented a monumental golden “Tusk of Ganesh” as a recoverable treasure.

The most defensible conclusion is that **the one-tusked icon and epithet precede the surviving explanatory legends in their developed forms**. Those legends are best understood as successive narrative biographies imposed upon an already recognizable divine attribute.

Throughout this dossier:

- **Documented:** supported by a datable text, inscription, archaeological context, or catalogued object.
- **Traditional/textual claim:** asserted within a religious text or living tradition, without implying ordinary historical occurrence.
- **Scholarly inference:** an interpretation rather than a directly recorded fact.
- **Modern accretion:** widespread today but absent from the older source to which it is commonly attributed.
- **Unverified:** reported without adequate primary evidence.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Classification

The object belongs simultaneously to four legendary classes:

- a severed or voluntarily extracted part of Ganesha’s elephant head;
- a hand-held divine attribute or *āyudha*;
- a projectile weapon in several narrative traditions;
- a writing implement in modern and late popular retellings of the *Mahābhārata*’s composition.

It is not a separate deity, notwithstanding the fact that *Ekadanta* also became the name of a complete manifestation or form of Ganesha in Ganapatya theology.

### 1.2 Names and terminology

**Sanskrit**

- **एकदन्त — Ekadanta:** *eka*, “one,” plus *danta*, “tooth” or, for an elephant, “tusk”; hence “One-Tusked” or “He of One Tusk.”
- **एकदंष्ट्र / एकदंष्ट्रः — Ekadaṃṣṭra:** “One-Fanged/One-Tusked,” using *daṃṣṭrā*, a fang or prominent tooth.
- **दन्त — danta:** tooth or tusk; the usual general noun.
- **रद / रदः — rada:** tooth or tusk. The *Gaṇapati Atharvaśīrṣa* describes Ganesha as holding a *rada*.
- **भग्नदन्त / भग्नदंष्ट्र — bhagnadanta/bhagnadaṃṣṭra:** “broken-toothed” or “broken-tusked,” a descriptive formation rather than the best-known canonical name.
- **स्वदन्त — svadanta:** “his own tusk,” common in iconographic descriptions.
- **दन्तिन् — Dantin:** “the tusked one” or “possessor of a tusk.”

The *Amarakośa*, at 1.1.40–41 in the cited online arrangement, lists eight names together:

> *vināyako vighnarāja-dvaimātura-gaṇādhipāḥ /  
> apy ekadanta-heramba-lambodara-gajānanāḥ*

That is, Vināyaka, Vighnarāja, Dvaimātura, Gaṇādhipa, Ekadanta, Heramba, Lambodara, and Gajānana are names of Ganesha. This is direct lexical evidence that *Ekadanta* had become a standard designation by the period represented by the text, generally placed in late antiquity but not securely dated to a single year or reign. The Sanskrit and German lexical presentation are available in the [online *Amarakośa*](https://www.payer.de/amarakosa/amara101.htm).

**Modern Indian-language forms** include Hindi एकदंत (*Ekdant/Ekadant*), Marathi एकदंत, Bengali একদন্ত, Tamil ஏகதந்தர் (*Ēkatantar*, Sanskritic) alongside பிள்ளையார் (*Piḷḷaiyār*) and விநாயகர் (*Vināyakar*), Telugu ఏకదంతుడు (*Ēkadantuḍu*), and Kannada ಏಕದಂತ (*Ēkadanta*). These normally name Ganesha rather than an independently preserved object.

### 1.3 Legendary material

If treated literally within the narrative world, the tusk is elephantine dental tissue: ivory surrounding a pulp cavity and continuous originally with Ganesha’s elephant head. No early source supplies measurements, weight, surface texture, growth rings, or anatomical description.

The narratives add inconsistent physical qualities:

- In the Paraśurāma story it is the **left tusk**, severed by an axe.
- It falls blood-covered and with such supernatural mass or force that the earth shakes.
- In weapon narratives it is strong enough to injure or kill a supernatural adversary or strike the Moon.
- In the writing story it can be held like a stylus and used to inscribe an enormous text.
- Modern popular art often renders it as a short, smooth, curved ivory point.
- *Uncharted: The Lost Legacy* converts it into an enormous, detachable golden treasure. That material and scale are modern fiction, not features of the Sanskrit textual object.

### 1.4 The tusk on Ganesha’s body versus the tusk in his hand

Images commonly show:

- one complete tusk projecting from the mouth;
- the other shortened, absent, or broken at the base;
- a separate curved object held in a hand.

These features should not automatically be treated as a naturalistic sequence. The hand-held tusk is an iconographic sign: an image can display both the loss and the detached object simultaneously. The convention sometimes makes it difficult to determine whether an eroded hand once held a tusk, radish, fruit, stylus, or another tapering attribute.

That ambiguity matters in early sculpture. A Metropolitan Museum publication notes that an object sometimes retrospectively identified as a broken tusk may instead be a fibrous radish root, *mūlaka*, an early attribute associated with offerings and agricultural fertility. The publication warns against reading later tusk iconography mechanically into every early tapering object ([Metropolitan Museum, *Lost Kingdoms*](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/Lost_Kingdoms_Hindu_Buddhist_Sculpture_of_Early_Southeast_Asia.pdf)).

### 1.5 Prescriptive description in the *Gaṇapati Atharvaśīrṣa*

The text supplies one of the clearest literary “portraits”:

> *ekadantaṃ caturhastaṃ pāśam aṅkuśadhāriṇam /  
> radaṃ ca varadaṃ hastair bibhrāṇaṃ mūṣakadhvajam*

In sense: “One-tusked and four-handed, bearing noose and goad, holding a tusk and the boon-bestowing gesture, with the mouse as emblem.” The text also contains the Ganesha *gāyatrī*:

> *ekadantāya vidmahe vakratuṇḍāya dhīmahi /  
> tan no dantiḥ pracodayāt*

“May we know the One-Tusked; may we meditate upon the Curved-Trunked; may the Tusked One impel us.”

The Sanskrit text is available through [Sanskrit Documents](https://sanskritdocuments.org/doc_ganesha/saarthaatharva.gif). The work is traditionally affiliated with the Atharvaveda, but modern scholarship treats it as a relatively late sectarian Upanishadic text, not evidence that the complete Ekadanta iconography existed in the earliest Vedic period.

### 1.6 Surviving representations

These are not the legendary tusk itself, but material witnesses to its iconographic history.

- **Uttar Pradesh, eighth century:** a ten-armed sandstone Ganesha, 125.7 cm high, now in the Asia Society collection, object 1979.013, holds a broken tusk along with rosary, snake, and sweets. The museum identifies the tusk explicitly and associates it with both the writing and Moon legends ([Asia Society Museum](https://www.asiasocietymuseum.org/search_object.asp?ObjectID=518&keywords=ganesha)).
- **Odisha, thirteenth century:** British Museum 1872,0701.60, a schist Pañcamukha/Lakṣmī Ganesha, holds a *danta*. Its own physical history includes a nineteenth-century restoration and removal of a non-original lotus fragment during later conservation. The sculpture—not the tusk represented—is documented through the Stuart, Bridge, and British Museum collections ([British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_1872-0701-60)).
- **Vijayanagara period:** Archaeological Survey of India, Hampi Circle, accession R233, a granite standing Ganesha measuring 52 × 36 × 18 cm, holds a broken tusk in his front right hand. Its archaeological provenance is catalogued as unknown ([ASI Hampi Circle](https://www.asihampicircle.in/ganesha-2-2/)).
- **Odisha, personal-devotion sculpture:** a small seated Ganesha in the Metropolitan Museum holds his broken tusk and sweets ([Metropolitan Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/38516)).
- **Kolkata, 1878–83:** Metropolitan Museum 2021.205, a lithograph with watercolor, represents Ganesha transcribing the *Mahābhārata*. Significantly, it shows ordinary pens—including discarded worn pens—not the tusk as stylus ([Metropolitan Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/849505)).

These objects prove the longevity and geographical spread of the iconographic attribute. They do not establish that a physical divine tusk once circulated.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 No documentable manufacture

There is no forge, workshop, commission, quarry, donor, or human maker. Within Hindu theology the tusk grows as part of Ganesha’s elephant head. Because accounts of how Ganesha acquired that head themselves vary, even the legendary biological origin cannot be reduced to one narrative.

No located Sanskrit account states that a divine craftsman fabricated the tusk separately. The stories concern its **loss, severance, extraction, or reuse**, not its manufacture.

### 2.2 Earliest secure state of the evidence

The evidence must be divided into three questions:

1. When is an elephant-headed figure first visible?
2. When is a clearly one-tusked Ganesha visible?
3. When is a detached tusk narrated as an object with an action-history?

These do not have the same answer.

#### Disputed early image

An elephant-faced figure at the Kaṇṭaka Cetiya near Mihintale, Sri Lanka, has been proposed as an early prototype of Ganesha, possibly in the first centuries CE. Alice Getty reports S. Paranavitana’s observation that the figure has one tusk, but she emphasizes that it occurs among other *gaṇas* and may not yet be the classical god. This remains a prototype hypothesis, not a secure identification ([Getty, *Gaṇeśa: A Monograph on the Elephant-Faced God*](https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/getty1971/0061)).

An alleged elephant-headed deity on imitative coins associated with Hermaios was promoted as an even earlier Ganesha. Osmund Bopearachchi reviewed the photographs and numismatic context and argued that the supposed trunk is a misread nose, beard, and garment fold: the deity is Zeus-Mithra, “certainly not Gaṇeśa.” The coin therefore cannot securely attest the tusk ([Bopearachchi, 1993](https://www.persee.fr/doc/topoi_1161-9473_1993_num_3_2_1479)).

#### Secure classical iconographic horizon

Recognizable Ganesha images occur in Gupta-period contexts, approximately the fourth through sixth centuries CE. Varāhamihira’s *Bṛhat Saṃhitā*, conventionally dated to the sixth century, prescribes an elephant-faced, pot-bellied image with an axe, a single tusk, and a radish root. That prescription establishes the one-tusked condition; it does not narrate an accident or identify a detached tusk as writing instrument.

The Karpaka Vinayakar rock relief at Pillaiyarpatti is traditionally and art-historically assigned to an early medieval horizon; a nearby seventh-century inscription calls the deity *Dēsi Vināyakar*. The relief’s apparent broken-left-tusk feature is material evidence for the established type, but it is carved stone representing a divine body, not a relic of the lost tusk.

#### Lexical attestation

The *Amarakośa* lists *Ekadanta* as an ordinary synonym of Ganesha. Its date remains debated, often placed around the fifth or sixth century. It offers no story: the name simply presupposes that “one-tusked” was sufficient identification.

This silence is historically important. A named, visually stable asymmetry existed before the fullest extant explanatory narratives can be securely dated.

### 2.3 Earliest narrative biography

No presently identified early inscription or Gupta-period text tells a complete story of the tusk’s loss. Developed accounts appear in Purāṇic and sectarian materials whose texts accumulated over long periods and whose surviving manuscripts are often substantially later than their oldest strata.

The Paraśurāma episode occurs in closely related forms in the *Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa* and *Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa*. The surviving recension and precise layer-dates require caution: the title “Purāṇa” does not make every chapter equally ancient.

The Ganesha- and Mudgala-centered Purāṇas are medieval Ganapatya works. Their relative chronology is disputed; proposals range broadly from approximately the eleventh through later medieval centuries, with evidence of continued redaction and extensive early-modern manuscript circulation. Greg Bailey’s scholarly edition and translation describes the *Gaṇeśa Purāṇa* as medieval and notes the scale of its manuscript tradition ([Bailey, *Gaṇeśapurāṇa: Krīḍākhaṇḍa*](https://books.google.com/books?id=boDXAAAAMAAJ)).

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because this is a legendary object, “custody” means who possesses, bears, drops, throws, or receives it within each textual version. The versions cannot be combined into one seamless itinerary.

### 3.1 Stage A: integral part of Ganesha’s head

All principal stories begin with Ganesha possessing two tusks or at least with a tusk capable of being broken or removed. The object is bodily rather than separately owned. Its location is wherever Ganesha is: commonly Kailāsa in the Paraśurāma episode, the scene of Vyāsa’s dictation in the writing tale, or the road/night sky setting in the Moon tale.

### 3.2 Stage B1: severed by Paraśurāma at Kailāsa

In the *Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa* account, Paraśurāma arrives at Kailāsa and is stopped by Ganesha. Following their struggle, Paraśurāma hurls the axe given to him by Shiva. Ganesha deliberately receives it with his **left tusk** so that his father’s weapon will not be rendered futile.

The text’s object-sequence is unusually explicit:

1. Ganesha possesses the left tusk.
2. Paraśurāma’s axe strikes it.
3. The tusk is chopped off.
4. It falls to the ground covered with blood.
5. Its impact shakes the earth, oceans, continents, and mountains.
6. Pārvatī and Shiva arrive after hearing the crash.
7. Pārvatī sees Ganesha with one tusk.
8. Krishna explains that Paraśurāma has “made him Ekadanta.”

A translation of this passage appears in G. V. Tagare’s *Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa*, chapter 42 ([primary-text translation](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/the-brahmanda-purana/d/doc362899.html)).

**Custody after the fall:** not stated. The tusk remains on the ground at the scene and disappears from the narrative as an independently tracked object. No named person retrieves, preserves, enshrines, or inherits it.

The *Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa* elaborates the confrontation, Pārvatī’s anger, divine mediation, and Paraśurāma’s culpability. Again, no durable relic custody follows the severance. The narrative exists to explain Ganesha’s bodily epithet and negotiate relationships among Shiva, Pārvatī, Ganesha, Paraśurāma, and Krishna—not to establish a shrine provenance.

### 3.3 Stage B2: Ganesha extracts and wields the tusk

Other traditions convert bodily loss into voluntary weaponization. In such stories Ganesha pulls out or breaks off his own tusk and hurls or strikes with it. Proposed opponents differ by regional and textual retelling, including demons identified as Gajamukha/Gajamukhāsura or related figures.

This version makes the chain:

1. Ganesha’s head;
2. Ganesha’s hand;
3. target or battlefield;
4. unrecorded fate after impact.

Some narratives turn the defeated being into Ganesha’s mouse vehicle, but these accounts vary in the demon’s name, the means of defeat, and whether the tusk is actually the decisive weapon. Modern summaries often harmonize them without citing a chapter. Such harmonizations should be treated as unverified unless tied to a particular recension.

### 3.4 Stage B3: thrown at the Moon

The Moon story places the detached tusk in a cosmic trajectory.

A common form says that Ganesha, heavy with sweets, falls from his mouse after it is startled by a snake. His belly opens or the sweets spill; he binds his belly with the snake. The Moon laughs. Ganesha angrily tears out his tusk, throws it at the Moon, and curses the Moon or those who look at it on Ganesha’s festival.

The custody chain is:

1. Ganesha’s head;
2. Ganesha’s hand;
3. thrown toward or into the Moon;
4. no subsequent recovery.

The tusk may cause a wound, mark, diminished radiance, or the lunar phases, depending on the retelling. In other versions the Moon’s curse occurs without the tusk projectile. The *Gaṇeśa Purāṇa*’s Moon narrative belongs to a ritual complex explaining the prohibition on seeing the Moon on the fourth lunar day and the counter-rite involving the Syamantaka-jewel story. Later storytellers often merge these components.

### 3.5 Stage B4: writing implement in Vyāsa’s dictation

The most famous modern chain is:

1. tusk on Ganesha’s head;
2. ordinary pen breaks or fails;
3. Ganesha snaps off the tusk;
4. he writes the remainder of the *Mahābhārata* with it;
5. no manuscript or tusk is subsequently located.

There is a serious textual problem: the received northern *Mahābhārata* passage about Ganesha serving as Vyāsa’s scribe mentions a **writing instrument** and the condition that it must not stop, but it does not say that the pen breaks or that Ganesha substitutes his tusk. The Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute’s Critical Edition excludes the entire Ganesha-scribe episode from its reconstructed text and records it among secondary material because it lacks adequate support across the manuscript tradition. The southern transmission notably omits it.

Bruno Lo Turco accordingly calls the scribe episode a widely recognized late addition and interprets it as a reflection on authorship, writing technology, and scribal labor ([Lo Turco, 2018](https://iris.uniroma1.it/handle/11573/1077214)). The electronic Critical Edition is hosted by the [University of Göttingen’s Mahābhārata project](https://bombay.indology.info/mahabharata/welcome.html).

Therefore:

- **Late manuscript tradition:** Ganesha becomes Vyāsa’s scribe.
- **Absent from that passage:** broken pen, self-extracted tusk, tusk used as stylus.
- **Later popular synthesis:** the ancient one-tusk icon is fused to the scribal episode.
- **Present evidentiary status:** the exact date and first identifiable author of that synthesis remain unresolved.

The 1878–83 Kolkata print at the Metropolitan Museum demonstrates that even relatively modern Indian visual culture could depict the scribal episode with ordinary pens rather than the tusk. The tusk-pen was therefore not yet an obligatory visual component.

### 3.6 No post-mythic custody

No examined source provides:

- a gift of the original tusk to a king or sage;
- burial or rediscovery;
- installation in a temple reliquary;
- medieval inventory entry;
- colonial seizure;
- museum accession;
- scientific authentication;
- modern litigation or repatriation claim.

The custody chains terminate inside their respective stories. Any claimed physical tusk would therefore require an independently documented provenance beginning after a total gap of many centuries. No such claimant was located.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Does the object exist physically?

**Documentary answer:** no physical specimen has been authenticated or even securely catalogued as a claimant to the original divine tusk.

**Religious answer:** traditions may treat the tusk’s mythic reality as part of Ganesha’s sacred history. Historical method can document that belief and its expressions but cannot submit a divine body-part outside ordinary chronology to laboratory verification.

The two propositions are not equivalent. “No authenticated specimen exists” does not demonstrate that a theological event is false; it defines the limit of material-historical evidence.

### 4.2 One object or several narrative objects?

The stories are mutually incompatible if read as one literal biography:

- Paraśurāma cuts off the left tusk.
- Ganesha removes the tusk himself as a weapon.
- He removes and throws it at the Moon.
- He removes it to replace a failed pen.

Some modern retellings evade the conflict by presenting each as a symbolic “version.” Others try to sequence episodes or suppose that a remaining tusk could be broken again. No early source establishes such a harmonized chain.

The historically sound treatment is to regard them as **variant identities attached to the same iconographic absence**, not as four documented episodes involving a single recoverable specimen.

### 4.3 Right or left tusk

There is no universal agreement.

- The *Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa* translation explicitly says Ganesha received Paraśurāma’s axe with the **left tooth/tusk**.
- Many sculptures show the hand-held tusk on the right side, but a hand’s position does not prove from which side of the mouth it came.
- Modern illustrations vary and are sometimes laterally reversed during printing.
- Popular accounts frequently say “right tusk” without textual citation.

Accordingly, “Ganesha’s right tusk” should not be used as a universal identification.

### 4.4 Tusk or radish?

Early iconographic objects can be misidentified. The *Bṛhat Saṃhitā* associates Ganesha with *mūlaka*, a radish root. Early sculptures in India and Southeast Asia show long, fibrous plant forms. Later viewers familiar with the tusk stories may call any tapering attribute a tusk.

Authentication therefore proceeds by:

- examining the mouth for a corresponding loss;
- looking for curvature and smoothness versus leaves or root fibers;
- comparing the image to contemporary regional iconography;
- avoiding explanations drawn solely from much later myths.

### 4.5 The “ancient Mahābhārata tusk-pen” claim

The assertion that “the *Mahābhārata* says Ganesha broke his tusk to write it” fails textual scrutiny in two stages:

1. The Ganesha-scribe episode itself is not part of the Critical Edition’s reconstructed archetype.
2. Even the later received passage does not narrate a broken pen or tusk substitution.

Thus the tusk-as-pen is a genuine and influential tradition, but its routine description as an explicit episode in the Sanskrit *Mahābhārata* is inaccurate.

### 4.6 No scientific examination

There has been no radiocarbon dating, ivory-species identification, stable-isotope study, DNA analysis, microscopy, or provenance testing of “the tusk,” because no authenticated or institutionally recognized physical claimant is available.

Museum conservation analyses apply to sculptures and paintings representing the tusk, not to the legendary object itself.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Identification of the deity

The tusk is foremost an identifying feature. A single tusk at the mouth and a detached tusk in the hand allow viewers to recognize Ganesha even where inscriptions are absent. In multi-deity temple programs the attribute functions like Shiva’s trident or Vishnu’s discus.

### 6.2 Worship through the name Ekadanta

*Ekadanta* is invoked in:

- name-litanies;
- meditation verses;
- the Ganesha *gāyatrī*;
- domestic and temple worship;
- rites undertaken before education, travel, business, marriage, and other beginnings.

The object’s ritual power is generally indirect: worshippers address **Ganesha as Ekadanta**, not a separately enshrined tusk. The name converts visible incompleteness into a complete divine identity.

### 6.3 Tusk as weapon

As a hand-held attribute, the tusk participates in the wider logic of divine weapons. It signifies that Ganesha can turn part of himself into an instrument of overcoming obstacles. The weapon interpretation is securely attested as an iconographic and narrative tradition; philosophical explanations of “sacrifice,” “resilience,” or “using imperfection as a tool” are primarily modern homiletic developments unless a particular older commentary is cited.

### 6.4 Tusk as stylus and patronage of learning

The writing legend binds the object to:

- literacy;
- disciplined attention;
- fulfilled promises;
- comprehension before transcription;
- sacrifice for intellectual work;
- Ganesha’s modern role as patron of students and writers.

Historically, the association developed in layers. Ganesha’s late appearance as Vyāsa’s scribe supplied the literary setting; the older broken-tusk emblem was subsequently turned into the instrument of writing. The result is symbolically powerful but should not be backdated into the oldest recoverable *Mahābhārata*.

### 6.5 The Moon taboo

The Moon narrative helps explain a ritual prohibition against viewing the Moon on Ganesha Caturthī. In several traditions, seeing it brings false accusation or misfortune. Recitation or hearing of Krishna’s Syamantaka-jewel episode can serve as a remedy.

Here the tusk helps enforce a calendrical practice: even if it is absent from some textual forms of the curse, later narrative makes the thrown tooth the physical agent linking Ganesha’s anger to the Moon’s damaged or fluctuating appearance.

### 6.6 Philosophical symbolism

Common interpretations include:

- the intact tusk as unity or absolute truth, the broken one as divided phenomenal existence;
- retention of truth and abandonment of illusion;
- sacrifice of the body for knowledge;
- transformation of loss into a useful instrument;
- conquest of pride, especially where Ekadanta defeats Madāsura, the embodiment of intoxication or arrogance.

These should be classified as **theological exegesis**, not as explanations independently confirmed by early iconography. The ancient image does not carry an inscription saying that its broken tusk means “sacrifice” or “imperfection.”

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 Paraśurāma and Shiva’s axe

**Tier:** medieval Purāṇic narrative; one of the strongest explicitly sourced explanations.

Paraśurāma visits Kailāsa after his campaigns and wishes to see Shiva. Ganesha, acting as guardian, refuses him entry while Shiva and Pārvatī are secluded. Words lead to combat. Ganesha overpowers or humiliates Paraśurāma, who then hurls his axe.

Ganesha recognizes it as a gift from Shiva. Rather than nullify his father’s weapon, he allows it to strike his left tusk. The severed tusk falls blood-covered with cosmic force. Pārvatī reacts furiously; divine mediation prevents further destruction. Ganesha is thereafter called Ekadanta.

The story accounts for:

- the missing tusk;
- its side, at least in this recension;
- Ganesha’s filial deference;
- Paraśurāma’s dangerous anger;
- the coexistence of Shiva’s two powerful dependents;
- the tusk-shaped attribute beside an axe in art.

**Object fate:** abandoned by the text after it strikes the earth.

### 7.2 Ganesha as Vyāsa’s scribe

**Tier:** late epic interpolation for the scribal compact; tusk-as-pen is a still later accretion.

Vyāsa seeks a sufficiently capable scribe. Ganesha agrees on condition that his pen never cease; Vyāsa counters that Ganesha must understand each verse before writing it. Vyāsa composes difficult “knotted verses,” giving himself time to formulate what follows.

In the now-familiar extension, Ganesha’s pen breaks. To avoid violating the compact, he snaps off his tusk and continues writing.

The textual seam is visible:

- the received interpolation speaks of writing and a pen;
- it does not describe the pen breaking;
- it does not make the tusk a substitute;
- the whole scribe episode is secondary in the Critical Edition.

The tusk element nevertheless became dominant in children’s books, educational materials, devotional talks, museum explanations, yoga literature, and internet iconography. Its success appears to come from the unusually exact fit between two independent features: Ganesha is one-tusked, and Ganesha is a scribe.

### 7.3 The Moon’s laughter and curse

**Tier:** Purāṇic/regional narrative with numerous later expansions.

Ganesha overeats sweets and rides away on his mouse. A snake frightens the animal; Ganesha falls, and sweets spill or his belly bursts. He replaces the sweets and ties the snake around himself. The Moon laughs at the spectacle.

Ganesha breaks off or pulls out his tusk and throws it at the Moon. He curses the Moon to disappear, lose brilliance, wax and wane, or bring slander upon those who see it. The gods plead for mitigation, producing a monthly lunar cycle or an annual Ganesha-Caturthī taboo.

Variants change:

- whether the belly actually opens;
- whether the mouse or snake causes the fall;
- whether the tusk hits the Moon;
- whether the curse explains lunar phases, lunar markings, or ritual danger;
- whether looking at the Moon is forbidden always or only on one night;
- the prescribed expiation.

The story is a good example of a legend that changes ritual behavior even though the physical projectile has no recoverable terrestrial history.

### 7.4 The tusk as demon-killing weapon

**Tier:** sectarian and regional variants.

Ganesha breaks off his tusk in combat and uses it against a demon. Gajamukha or Gajamukhāsura is a frequent modern name; in some tellings the defeated demon assumes mouse form and becomes Ganesha’s vehicle. Other demon traditions connect Ekadanta with Madāsura, whom the Ekadanta manifestation subdues.

The *Mudgala Purāṇa* gives Ekadanta a larger theological identity: he is one of Ganesha’s manifestations and defeats Madāsura, the demon of pride or intoxication. Here “Ekadanta” no longer merely records an injury. It names a cosmic form whose one-tusked identity precedes and structures the adventure. The Sanskrit *Ekadantacarita* is available in the [Mudgala Purāṇa text collection](https://sanskritdocuments.org/sanskrit/purana/).

### 7.5 The parents’ union and the single tusk

A courtly Sanskrit verse discussed in modern scholarship plays on Ganesha imitating his parents’ androgynous union: his bride has supposedly taken the half of him where the face is tuskless. This is poetic explanation, not custody history. It shows how the missing half could be interpreted through Śiva–Śakti theology rather than battle or writing. Asko Parpola discusses the verse while surveying one-horn and one-tusk symbolism ([Parpola, 2011](https://tuhat.helsinki.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/127256248/Parpola_A_2011._Harappan_unicorn_in_Eurasian_South_Asian_perspectives.pdf)).

### 7.6 Symbolic-castration theory

Paul Courtright proposed reading Ganesha’s severed tusk in relation to sacrifice, sexual power, and symbolic castration. This became controversial, especially when decontextualized from a broader psychoanalytic interpretation of the myths. It is a modern scholarly theory, not an indigenous consensus and not a finding about a physical artifact.

The debate demonstrates that interpretive control over the tusk can itself become politically charged. Critics emphasize its continuing potency as weapon and emblem, rejecting the assumption that breakage necessarily signifies emasculation. Courtright’s monograph remains a major study of the narrative complex ([*Gaṇeśa: Lord of Obstacles, Lord of Beginnings*](https://books.google.com/books?id=4bREXvKRKV0C)).

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 The legendary original

**Status:** legendary; no authenticated physical survivor.

**Location:** indeterminate and version-dependent.

- Paraśurāma version: last seen on the ground at Kailāsa.
- Moon version: last seen thrown toward the Moon.
- demon-combat version: last seen on or near a mythic battlefield.
- writing version: last seen in Ganesha’s hand during the transcription of the *Mahābhārata*.
- iconographic form: continuously present as an attribute in images of Ganesha.

No version supplies a later terrestrial shrine location.

### 8.2 Candidate relics

No credible claimant was found. In particular, the following are not the original tusk:

- sculpted tusks held by stone or bronze Ganeshas;
- ivory carvings of Ganesha;
- naturally shed or extracted elephant tusks called “Ganesh” commercially;
- gold-colored decorative tusks;
- the fictional golden object in *Uncharted*.

### 8.3 Access and control

Since there is no located relic, there is no owner, guardian, accession number, legal title, export history, repatriation proceeding, or access restriction applicable to the legendary original.

Access to its **representations** is distributed:

- actively worshipped images in temples and homes;
- archaeological monuments under national or state protection;
- museum collections;
- manuscripts in libraries;
- digital and commercial reproductions.

### 8.4 Conservation issues

Conservation concerns belong to representations, not the tusk itself. Tusk attributes are especially vulnerable because they project from a hand or face. They are often broken, abraded, restored, or misidentified. Any study must distinguish:

- intentional representation of a broken tusk;
- later physical loss to the sculpture;
- restoration;
- incomplete carving;
- misidentified radish, fruit, or stylus.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Print and educational culture

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century printmaking helped standardize Ganesha as a benevolent patron of learning. Yet the Metropolitan Museum’s 1878–83 Kolkata print shows him writing with replaceable pens. This is evidence that the tusk-pen synthesis, although now pervasive, was not universally required in late nineteenth-century imagery.

Modern schoolbooks and children’s retellings frequently reverse the evidentiary order, presenting the writing story as the original reason for the icon. They seldom mention the Critical Edition or older Paraśurāma and Moon traditions.

### 9.2 Literature

Uma Krishnaswami’s *The Broken Tusk: Stories of the Hindu God Ganesha* uses the object as the title and organizing emblem for a collection of Ganesha stories. Such books helped detach “the broken tusk” from one specific Purāṇic narrative and make it shorthand for Ganesha’s entire mythology ([Google Books record](https://books.google.ca/books?id=Lj3IXqJkDgIC)).

### 9.3 *Uncharted: The Lost Legacy*

The most consequential modern material reinvention is Naughty Dog’s 2017 game.

The game presents a **Golden Tusk of Ganesh** as a monumental ancient treasure sought in India’s Western Ghats by Chloe Frazer, Nadine Ross, and the antagonist Asav. PlayStation describes the quest explicitly as a search for the “legendary Golden Tusk of Ganesh” ([official series page](https://www.playstation.com/en-us/uncharted/); [game page](https://www.playstation.com/en-ph/games/uncharted-the-lost-legacy/)).

This object is a modern invention:

- no cited Sanskrit source makes the tusk a monumental gold artifact;
- no historical Hoysala treasury is documented as possessing it;
- its recoverable archaeological location and political custody are fictional;
- the game prefers the Paraśurāma conflict as its mythic frame.

The adaptation materially changed global public perception by presenting the tusk as something like a crown jewel: portable, priceless, archaeologically recoverable, and politically contestable. That is precisely what the older source history does **not** provide.

### 9.4 Commercial symbolism

The tusk appears on:

- brass, resin, stone, and plastic Ganesha statues;
- pendants and devotional jewelry;
- posters and festival decorations;
- logos for educational, technological, and commercial enterprises;
- tattoos and yoga merchandise.

Commercial descriptions frequently call it a symbol of sacrifice, adaptability, or choosing wisdom over perfection. These meanings are modern explanatory conventions unless tied to an identifiable commentary.

### 9.5 Political and cultural use

Unlike some relics, the tusk itself has not become the focus of a major restitution claim or territorial sovereignty dispute. Its broader image participates in debates about:

- ownership and export of South Asian antiquities depicting Ganesha;
- colonial museum collections;
- commercialization of Hindu sacred images;
- representation of Hindu myths in global entertainment;
- scholarly freedom versus perceived denigration of living religious traditions.

These controversies concern representations and interpretations, not custody of an original tusk.

### 9.6 Replicas and fictional surrogates

There are innumerable iconographic replicas but no canonical full-scale replica of a known original. The *Uncharted* golden tusk is a replica-producing design without an ancient prototype: fan models, game merchandise, and digital images reproduce a fictional object that acquired its own modern visual identity.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Foundational surveys

Alice Getty’s monograph assembled literary, iconographic, and cross-Asian evidence and remains valuable for documenting the range of forms, although some dating and evolutionary hypotheses require revision. It already noted uncertainty over proposed prototypes and the difficulty of distinguishing a tusk from other attributes.

Robert L. Brown’s edited *Ganesh: Studies of an Asian God* broadened the study beyond a single Hindu textual genealogy to Buddhist, Jain, Tibetan, Japanese, and Southeast Asian forms ([Google Books](https://books.google.com/books?id=PpD-RjNsFE8C)).

Paul Courtright’s 1985 monograph remains central for myth, ritual, sexuality, and social interpretation. Its psychoanalytic readings became part of a wider controversy about applying modern Western theory to Hindu deities.

### 10.2 Was the one-tusked icon originally explained by a myth?

The main scholarly possibilities are:

- a story produced the visual feature;
- an already existing iconographic asymmetry later attracted explanatory stories;
- both developed interactively.

The evidence favors caution. The *Amarakośa* and *Bṛhat Saṃhitā* know the one-tusked form without supplying the famous explanations. Several later stories compete to explain it. This pattern supports—but does not prove—the inference that the icon or epithet came first and the etiologies multiplied afterward.

### 10.3 Earliest-image disputes

The Hermaios coin hypothesis demonstrates the danger of retrospective recognition. Once Ganesha’s elephant head and tusk are expected, ambiguous folds or facial features can be converted into a trunk. Bopearachchi’s reassessment shows why lighting, die study, chronology, and comparative numismatics matter.

The Mihintale figure remains more plausible as an elephant-faced *gaṇa*, but “prototype of Ganesha” is not the same as an inscribed, securely identifiable Ganesha.

### 10.4 Textual dating

The dossier cannot assign single composition dates to whole Purāṇas without distortion. Relevant texts are composite, and particular chapters may be later than the nominal age of the collection.

Especially important distinctions are:

- the *Mahābhārata*’s oldest recoverable textual layer;
- the later Ganesha-scribe interpolation;
- the still later attachment of the tusk-pen incident;
- medieval Ganapatya Purāṇas;
- early-modern manuscript and print recensions;
- modern devotional retellings.

### 10.5 Ganesha as scribe

Lo Turco’s work shifts discussion away from simply calling the episode sectarian propaganda. He argues that the scene dramatizes authorship, dictation, comprehension, and the technology and profession of writing. This approach explains why Ganesha’s intellectual and obstacle-managing qualities made him an apt scribe even before the tusk was made into a stylus.

### 10.6 Unresolved questions

- What is the earliest datable manuscript or artwork that explicitly shows the tusk being used as a pen?
- Who first narrated the broken-pen incident in its now-standard form?
- Which early sculptures securely show a detached tusk rather than a radish or fruit?
- Are the Paraśurāma passages independent developments, or did one Purāṇic recension borrow from another?
- How early did ritual explanations of the Moon taboo include the thrown tusk?
- Did regional oral traditions preserve weapon stories before their surviving written witnesses?

These remain suitable subjects for manuscript-based research. Popular repetition cannot resolve them.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and near-primary textual materials

- *Amarakośa* 1.1.40–41: Sanskrit list of Ganesha’s names, including *Ekadanta*.
- *Bṛhat Saṃhitā*: single-tusked iconographic prescription attributed to Varāhamihira.
- *Gaṇapati Atharvaśīrṣa*: Ganesha *gāyatrī* and meditation image naming Ekadanta and the hand-held *rada*.
- *Mahābhārata*, Ādiparvan scribal interpolation: Ganesha and Vyāsa’s writing compact; no tusk substitution in the received passage.
- *Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa*, cited chapter 42: Paraśurāma’s axe, severed left tusk, fall, earthquake, and naming as Ekadanta.
- *Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa*, Gaṇapati-khaṇḍa: expanded Paraśurāma confrontation.
- *Mudgala Purāṇa*, Ekadantacarita: Ekadanta manifestation and Madāsura cycle.
- *Gaṇeśa Purāṇa*: medieval Ganapatya narratives, including the Moon-curse ritual complex.

### Principal scholarship and catalogues

- Alice Getty, *Gaṇeśa: A Monograph on the Elephant-Faced God*.
- Paul B. Courtright, *Gaṇeśa: Lord of Obstacles, Lord of Beginnings*.
- Robert L. Brown, ed., *Ganesh: Studies of an Asian God*.
- Greg Bailey, trans., *Gaṇeśapurāṇa: Krīḍākhaṇḍa*.
- Bruno Lo Turco, “The Divine Scribe: A New Interpretation of the Gaṇeśa Episode from the Mahābhārata.”
- Osmund Bopearachchi, “On the So-Called Earliest Representation of Ganesa.”
- Metropolitan Museum, British Museum, Asia Society Museum, and Archaeological Survey of India object records.

### Source limitations

- Several primary texts are available online only through modern transcriptions or translations, not diplomatic manuscript editions.
- Purāṇic chapter numbering varies between editions.
- The date of an entire Purāṇa cannot automatically be assigned to every passage in it.
- Museum descriptions sometimes repeat popular explanations without establishing their earliest textual source.
- No physical object was available for scientific analysis.
- No archive or institution supplied a provenance file for a claimed original tusk because no such recognized claimant was located.
