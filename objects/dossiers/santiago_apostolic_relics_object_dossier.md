# The apostolic relics of Santiago, Compostela: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier concerns the group of human skeletal fragments now venerated beneath the high altar of the Cathedral of Santiago de Compostela as the bodily relics of the apostle James the Greater and his disciples Athanasius and Theodore. It also follows the successive tombs, cists, boxes, and silver reliquary through which those remains acquired their present material identity.

Three propositions must remain separate:

1. **Physical fact:** fragmented human bones exist in a sealed inner container inside a nineteenth-century silver shrine in the cathedral crypt.
2. **Ecclesiastical identification:** the archbishop of Santiago declared them authentic in 1883; the Sacred Congregation of Rites confirmed that judgment in 1884; Pope Leo XIII ratified and publicized it in the apostolic letter *Deus omnipotens*.
3. **Historical or scientific identification:** no published radiocarbon, ancient-DNA, isotope, or modern forensic study has established that these particular bones date to the first century, came from Judaea, or belonged to James, Athanasius, and Theodore. The only direct scientific examination of the principal group remains the limited anatomical and chemical investigation of 1879–1882.

The traditional chain extending from Jerusalem in the first century to Compostela in the ninth is therefore presented separately from the materially documented chain. The seams between them are central evidence, not incidental reservations.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Object class and identity

**Class:** composite bodily relic; archaeological assemblage of human skeletal fragments; cult deposit; cathedral treasury object; pilgrimage focus.

**Current institutional identity:** the relics of:

- **James the Greater**, Spanish and Galician *Santiago el Mayor* / *Santiago o Maior*;
- **Athanasius**, Spanish/Galician *Atanasio*;
- **Theodore**, Spanish/Galician *Teodoro*.

The cathedral and papal documentation collectively call them *reliquiae*, *sacra ossa*, *sagradas reliquias*, or “apostolic relics.” The physical ensemble is often metonymically called the *urna apostólica*, although strictly that name belongs to its silver outer reliquary.

### 1.2 Names and linguistic history

- **Hebrew/Aramaic:** יַעֲקֹב, *Yaʿaqōḇ*.
- **Greek New Testament:** Ἰάκωβος, *Iákōbos*.
- **Latin:** *Iacobus* or *Jacobus*.
- **Spanish:** *Santiago*, historically contracted from *Sant Iago* or *Santo Iago*; also *Jacobo* and *Jaime*.
- **Galician:** *Santiago*; *Santiago o Maior*.
- **Portuguese:** *São Tiago* or *Santiago*.
- **French:** *Saint Jacques*.
- **Italian:** *San Giacomo*; at Pistoia, *San Jacopo*.
- **English:** Saint James the Greater, Saint James son of Zebedee.
- **Medieval Latin shrine designation:** *locus Sancti Iacobi*, *ecclesia Sancti Iacobi*, and eventually *Compostella*.

The apostle is distinguished from James son of Alphaeus, “James the Less.” This matters because the cathedral possesses a separate skull-relic traditionally assigned to James Alphaeus. A modern forensic study of that separate relic found carbonized remains of one mature adult male with cranial trauma and expressly questioned the traditional identification. Those findings do **not** constitute a scientific examination of the bones in the crypt. Confusion between the two relic groups has repeatedly contaminated popular accounts. [Forensic Anthropology study](https://journals.upress.ufl.edu/fa/article/view/1801)

### 1.3 “Compostela”

The popular derivation from Latin *campus stellae*, “field of the star,” belongs to the developed discovery legend. Philologists have more commonly connected *Compostela* with Latin *componere/compositum*, in a funerary sense—approximately “burial place” or “small cemetery”—although the etymology remains debated. The archaeological presence of a Roman and late-antique cemetery gives the funerary derivation contextual plausibility but does not prove it.

### 1.4 The bones found in 1879

The excavators reported an “abundant collection” of human bones inside a rough cist beneath the apse or hemicycle east of the high altar. The deposit had no identifying inscription. It lay approximately 1.20 metres below a metal plate serving as an altar step, inside a hurriedly assembled enclosure made from unworked or reused granite and marble slabs and bricks. Drops of candle wax were observed on some remains and were interpreted by the discoverers as evidence of a nocturnal concealment. That interpretation is plausible but not independently dated. [Cathedral authenticity-process study, pp. 403–410](https://www.annuariumsanctiiacobi.org/sites/annuariumsanctiiacobi.org/files/002_alejandro_barral_separata.pdf)

The university commission described the bones as:

- mixed with soil and without anatomical order;
- wholly disarticulated;
- fragile and extensively fragmented;
- lacking cartilage and soft tissue;
- containing no completely intact bone;
- better preserved in the upper part of the deposit;
- reduced in the lowest layer to innumerable small particles.

Recognizable pieces were sorted into three skeletal groups. A separate collection comprised **365 fragments too small or damaged for anatomical assignment**. The commission judged that the recognizable material represented **three incomplete skeletons of individuals of different ages**: two at the transition from the middle to the final third of ordinary life, and one within the final third. Nineteenth-century terminology and methods prevent reliable conversion of these descriptions into modern age ranges.

The report does not establish sex by modern osteological criteria. Later institutional summaries often call all three individuals male, but the surviving published account of the original examination is substantially less precise. [Timoteo Sánchez Freire biography and report summary](https://consellodacultura.gal/persoa.php?id=22290)

### 1.5 Inner containers

After examination and washing in February 1879:

- recognizable fragments were placed in a **mahogany box divided into three compartments**;
- the 365 indeterminate fragments were placed in another box.

At the final ceremonial deposition, published accounts state that the fragments were individually removed from fine silver paper and separated into designated compartments. The present silver reliquary contains a **cedar box lined with red velvet**. Sources disagree on its internal arrangement:

- cathedral-oriented descriptions commonly say **three compartments**, one for each named body;
- another detailed devotional description reports **six divisions containing glass vessels**, with the fragments wrapped in fine cloth.

Because the shrine is not ordinarily opened for inspection and no current technical inventory has been published, the exact present arrangement cannot be independently resolved. [Cathedral/Google Arts & Culture description](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/urna-del-ap%C3%B3stol-santiago/wgFPBd67XNcCJQ?hl=es) [Detailed Galician description](https://parroquiacarballo.org/a-urna-relicario/)

### 1.6 Silver outer reliquary

The visible *urna apostólica* is a Neo-Romanesque silver shrine commissioned after the ecclesiastical authentication.

**Materials and manufacture:**

- silver in its natural colour;
- cast and chased relief;
- cedar inner chest;
- red-velvet lining;
- glass containers according to one description;
- bronze support or pedestal;
- marble altar or frontal.

**Dimensions:** approximately **130 cm wide, 50 cm high, and 70 cm deep**.

**Inscription on the back of the lid:**

> SE HIZO EN LOS TALLERES DE JOSÉ LOSADA / SANTIAGO / AÑO MDCCCLXXXVI

Translation: “Made in the workshops of José Losada / Santiago / Year 1886.”

The design is associated with cathedral silversmith **José Losada de Dios**. Because illness prevented Losada from completing the work, execution was finished by **Eduardo Rey Villaverde** and **Ricardo Martínez Costoya**, with designs or modelling attributed to **Ángel Bar Fernández** and **Manuel Corgo Hermida**. The object is consequently misdescribed when assigned to Losada alone. [Cathedral Archive catalogue](https://archivobiblioteca.catedraldesantiago.es/index.php/urna-de-plata-del-apostol-santiago-en-la-cripta-de-la-catedral-de-santiago-de-compostela) [Ana Pérez Varela, “Una tumba para el Hijo del Trueno”](https://ler.letras.up.pt/uploads/ficheiros/16957.pdf)

### 1.7 Decoration

The front and sides imitate Romanesque and early-Christian forms:

- Christ in Majesty within an oval mandorla;
- the four Evangelist symbols or Tetramorph;
- figures beneath trilobed arches;
- James carrying a tau-shaped staff and a scroll with the Gospel phrase *et imposuit eis nomina Boanerges*—“and he gave them the name Boanerges”;
- Peter, Andrew, Paul, John, Matthew, Theodore, Athanasius, and other apostolic figures;
- on the side panels, Christ, the Virgin and Child, Mary Salome, James Alphaeus, Thomas, Bartholomew, Simon, and Torquatus;
- scallop shells and stars on the pitched lid;
- a Christogram, Alpha and Omega;
- imagery adapted from the cathedral’s medieval sculpture and from the lost Romanesque *tabula retro altaris*, known through a seventeenth-century drawing by José Vega y Verdugo.

The reliquary is therefore not a survival of the medieval shrine. It is a nineteenth-century historicist reconstruction that deliberately materialized an imagined Romanesque continuity.

### 1.8 Current condition

The Cathedral Archive describes the silver shrine’s condition as good. It remains behind the altar grille in the crypt, visible to visitors but not normally handled or opened. No public accession number has been identified; it is a liturgical cathedral possession rather than an accessioned state-museum object.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

## 2.1 The historical James

The earliest surviving text about James’s death is Acts 12:1–2, written decades after the event. It says that Herod killed “James the brother of John” with the sword. It does not mention Spain, transportation of his body, disciples named Athanasius and Theodore, or burial in Galicia.

James’s execution is generally placed under Herod Agrippa I, conventionally c. 44 CE. A Jerusalem execution is historically plausible; the fate of the body is unrecorded.

## 2.2 Earliest Spanish association

The earliest known textual family connecting James with Spain is the Latin *Breviarium apostolorum*, compiled or translated from Greek apostle lists in the later sixth or seventh century. Its entry states, in one recension:

> *Iacobus qui interpretatur subplantator, filius Zebedaei, frater Ioannis, hic Spaniae et occidentalia loca praedicat et sub Herode gladio caesus occubuit sepultusque est in Achaia Marmarica VIII Kalendas Augusti.*

Approximately: “James, interpreted ‘supplanter,’ son of Zebedee and brother of John, preached in Spain and the western regions; slain by the sword under Herod, he died and was buried in Achaia Marmarica on the eighth day before the Kalends of August [25 July].”

This is the earliest attested Spanish mission tradition. It does **not clearly place his grave in Galicia**. “Achaia Marmarica,” transmitted in unstable forms, has been interpreted as a location in Libya, an unknown district, a corruption, or—much later—as an *arca marmarica*, “marble ark.” [Discussion of the early source tradition](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0304418180900263) [Latin wording and source discussion](https://dokumen.pub/the-pilgrims-guide-to-santiago-de-compost.html)

An interpolated passage in the pseudo-Isidorian *De ortu et obitu patrum*, and later martyrological and liturgical texts, reinforced the Spanish mission. The late eighth-century Asturian hymn *O Dei Verbum* invokes James as the “golden head” and protector of Spain. None securely records a Galician tomb before the ninth-century cult.

## 2.3 Earliest evidence for a Compostela shrine

The discovery is traditionally dated between c. 820 and 830, during:

- the episcopate of **Theodemir of Iria**, whose predecessor was still active in 818;
- the reign of **Alfonso II of Asturias**, 791–842.

The first surviving narratives describing the event in detail are later, principally the early-twelfth-century *Historia Compostellana*. It says that the tomb, long hidden by woods during Muslim domination, was revealed in Theodemir’s time; Theodemir inspected it and informed Alfonso II, who came to the site, built a church, and endowed it. [English/Spanish presentation of *Historia Compostellana*, Book I](https://literaturamedieval.academic.wlu.edu/historia-compostelana/)

The oldest diplomatic references are ninth-century royal instruments preserved in later cathedral cartularies, especially *Tumbo A*. Their textual transmission and authenticity have been debated. A diploma dated 834 is often treated as the first documentary mention, but it is not an original contemporary parchment and must be used with diplomatic caution. Modern scholarship consequently distinguishes:

- a cult and royal foundation certainly existing by the ninth century;
- a traditional discovery in Theodemir’s episcopate;
- later narrative details about Pelagius, stars, and miraculous revelation.

A recent biomolecular study of remains attributed to Theodemir found an older, gracile male dating by radiocarbon to 673–820 CE, with isotope and genetic evidence compatible with a Galician individual of the period. This materially strengthens Theodemir’s historical existence and the ninth-century context. It does not authenticate the apostolic bones. [Antiquity study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/unveiling-bishop-teodomiro-of-iria-flavia-an-attempt-to-identify-the-discoverer-of-st-jamess-tomb-through-osteological-and-biomolecular-analyses-santiago-de-compostela-galicia-spain/3D7EA1E8EEE9E2B0F9832547FCF1B545)

## 2.4 Developed translation legend

The fullest medieval origin narrative appears in the pseudo-papal letter attributed to Leo III and in Book III of the *Liber Sancti Iacobi*, compiled in the twelfth century and preserved most famously in the *Codex Calixtinus*. Its core sequence is:

1. James preaches in Spain.
2. He returns to Jerusalem.
3. Herod has him beheaded.
4. Disciples recover his body.
5. An angelically guided or providential ship carries it from Jaffa to Galicia.
6. It reaches Iria Flavia/Padrón.
7. The disciples confront the pagan queen or noblewoman Lupa.
8. Wild bulls become tame; a dragon is overcome.
9. Lupa converts and provides a burial place.
10. James is buried in a marble tomb with disciples, later named Theodore and Athanasius.

The *Liber Sancti Iacobi* manuscript at Santiago was produced between approximately 1138 and 1173. Its attribution to Pope Calixtus II is pseudonymous. It is a primary source for what twelfth-century Compostela taught, not a contemporary record of a first-century voyage. [Spanish Ministry of Culture overview](https://www.cultura.gob.es/cultura/archivos/difusion/registro-memoria-unesco/2017/codice-calixtino/liber-calixtinus.html)

## 2.5 Physical archaeological origin

Excavation under and around the cathedral established that the site was a cemetery from the Roman period through late antiquity, with continued or renewed medieval use. A monumental Roman-period burial structure—the edicule later identified as the apostolic tomb—probably dates to the first or second century CE. Archaeology therefore establishes:

- the existence of a genuine Roman funerary landscape;
- the existence of a substantial antique mausoleum;
- later Christian and medieval modifications;
- ninth-century churches associated with the shrine.

It does not identify the mausoleum’s occupant. A Roman-date tomb is compatible with a first-century burial but is equally compatible with many other occupants. [Museo das Peregrinacións](https://museoperegrinacions.xunta.gal/es/visita/que-ver-en-tu-visita/descubrimiento-e-identificacion-del-cuerpo-apostolico) [USC *Locus Iacobi* research record](https://investigacion.usc.gal/documentos/5d1df67429995204f766c6ad?lang=es)

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### c. 44 CE: Jerusalem — tradition joined to one documented fact

**Documented:** James was reportedly killed by the sword under Herod Agrippa I.

**Unrecorded:** the treatment and burial of his body.

**Later tradition:** disciples transported it by sea to Galicia. No first-century source documents this transfer.

### First–second centuries: Roman cemetery at the future Compostela

A mausoleum and associated cemetery existed on the site. Its original ownership and occupants are unknown. The funerary inscription of Atia Modesta and other Roman remains establish elite or at least substantial Roman-period burial activity nearby, but none securely names James.

### Third–eighth centuries: complete documentary gap

No contemporary record traces James’s body, Athanasius, or Theodore in Galicia. Later sources retrospectively say the tomb was forgotten during persecution, political disorder, or Muslim domination. This is legendary provenance, not a continuous custody record.

### c. 820–830: first *inventio*

According to later accounts, a hermit or religious man named Pelagius/Pelayo observed lights over a wooded cemetery at Libredón and informed Bishop Theodemir. Theodemir discovered a stone chamber with bodies and identified the principal one as James. Alfonso II visited and commissioned a church.

What is reconstructable is that a shrine identified with James arose in Theodemir’s and Alfonso II’s period. The recognition procedure is unknown. No contemporary excavation record, label, seal, or inscription survives.

### Ninth century: royal and episcopal custody

The relics became the cult property of the church founded above the tomb, under the bishops of Iria and royal protection. Alfonso II’s small sanctuary was succeeded by a larger church under Alfonso III, consecrated in 899. Later tradition says the three bodies were placed in cedar boxes or gold-lined tombs; the precise source and reliability of those container descriptions require caution.

### 997: al-Mansur’s raid

Al-Mansur/Ibn Abi Amir’s army sacked Compostela and destroyed or damaged the basilica. Later pro-authenticity reconstruction asserted that Bishop Pedro de Mezonzo removed the relics before the raid, perhaps to Sobrado or Curtis, and afterwards returned them. Other accounts say the tomb itself was spared.

No contemporary document traces the bones out of and back into the cathedral. This is a major break in the proposed chain. Even defenders of authenticity acknowledge that the three bodies could have become mixed during such a removal.

### Eleventh–twelfth centuries: enclosure beneath the new cathedral

Construction of the Romanesque cathedral began in 1075. Diego Gelmírez modified the funerary edicule, consecrated a new altar in 1105, and eventually enclosed or obstructed access to the chamber. The old basilica was demolished as the Romanesque cathedral advanced.

The *Historia Compostellana* and later physical reconstruction indicate that the relics remained the cathedral chapter’s central cult possession beneath the high altar, though pilgrims increasingly encountered an altar and liturgical superstructure rather than an open sarcophagus.

### c. 1138–1145: Pistoia fragment

Bishop Atto of Pistoia requested a relic through intermediaries. Archbishop Diego Gelmírez and the Compostela chapter sent a cranial fragment with authentication documents, conventionally dated 1144 or 1145. The relic became a civic as well as ecclesiastical possession at Pistoia; its arrival is depicted on Atto’s fourteenth-century marble shrine.

The Pistoia fragment constitutes the strongest separately transmitted medieval offshoot of the Compostela assemblage. However, its continuous history proves only that Pistoia received something represented as a fragment from Compostela. It cannot independently identify the original body. [Diocese of Pistoia](https://www.diocesipistoia.it/atto/)

### Other medieval distributions

Cathedral histories and later compilations report:

- a frontal or cranial fragment sent through Carolingian custody to Saint-Vaast at Arras;
- an arm or forearm fragment given in 1055–1056 to the abbey of Saint-Jacques at Liège;
- claimed Compostela-derived relics at Arles, Toledo, and elsewhere.

These claims have uneven documentation. They also create an anatomical problem: numerous institutions claimed major parts or an entire body of James. A comprehensive modern inventory matching all external claims to the Compostela skeletons has not been published.

### 1554 and 1572: late access to the old chamber

In 1554 the chapter offered Philip II the opportunity to descend into the vault. According to a later account, the king declined and ordered that it never be opened. In 1572 royal antiquary Ambrosio de Morales described the belief that the body lay under large slabs beneath the apostolic altar and referred to an opening or *catarata* communicating with the chamber.

These observations document a living belief and architectural location, not visual inspection of identified bones.

### 1589: alleged secret concealment

When Francis Drake’s English forces attacked A Coruña, cathedral acts for 9–11 May record fear, military preparations, and decisions to send documents, precious objects, and relics toward Torre de Camba and Ourense.

No surviving order plainly states: “the bones of James were moved to the apse cist.” The later reconstruction holds that Archbishop **Juan de Sanclemente** and a small trusted group removed the three bodies from the old tomb and buried them behind the high altar, leaving no record for security. The alleged secrecy explains both the absence of a document and the later loss of memory, but it also makes the link unfalsifiable.

Thus:

- **documented:** invasion fear and evacuation planning;
- **plausible inference:** the apostolic relics were concealed inside the cathedral;
- **unproved detail:** Sanclemente personally ordered the exact cist found in 1879.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries: memory lost, cult retained

Knowledge of the exact location disappeared. Reports preserved a vague tradition of a removal to a hidden cathedral location and devotional attention continued at the high altar and rear hemicycle. Baroque remodelling lowered floors and altered or destroyed much of the ancient eastern chamber, further compromising the archaeological context.

### 1878–1879: search and second *inventio*

Cardinal-Archbishop **Miguel Payá y Rico** authorized a search directed by canons **Antonio López Ferreiro** and **José María Labín Cabello**, assisted by master builders and workmen including **Juan Nartallo**.

At approximately 10 p.m. on 28 January 1879, López Ferreiro was removing soil around the cist’s front stone. Two joined bricks were raised. Human bones and cranial fragments became visible. The opening was replaced, sealed with hydraulic lime, covered by a metal plate, and reported to the archbishop.

This immediate resealing provided some custody control after discovery. By modern archaeological standards, however, the night excavation, prior disturbance, interpretive agenda, and subsequent washing and anatomical rearrangement substantially limit evidentiary value.

### February 1879: university examination

Payá appointed:

- **Antonio Casares/Gil Casares**, pharmacy and chemistry;
- **Francisco Freire Barreiro**, medicine;
- **Timoteo Sánchez Freire**, medicine and surgery.

They worked at night in the cathedral, principally from 9 to 21 February. They sorted the remains into three groups, chemically analyzed a fragment from the shaft of a femur in “group two,” washed the material, and boxed it.

The chemistry showed reduced organic matter relative to comparative bone. This was treated as evidence of antiquity, but nineteenth-century compositional analysis cannot yield a calendar date and is heavily affected by burial conditions.

### 1879–1883: canonical custody and trial

The relics were controlled by the archbishop and chapter while a canonical process assembled:

- excavation testimony;
- workmen’s depositions;
- the medical-pharmaceutical report;
- archaeological and documentary opinions by Fidel Fita and Aureliano Fernández-Guerra;
- comparison with the Pistoia relic;
- arguments by an advocate for authenticity;
- a formal contrary submission by promoter/fiscal Manuel Mariño.

The complete cathedral dossier, *Expediente Reliquias Apóstol*, is preserved as ACS–CF 51, approximately 249 pages. The Roman phase also generated documents, some of which are lost or known only through excerpts. [Consello da Cultura Galega biography of López Ferreiro](https://consellodacultura.gal/album-de-galicia/detalle.php?persoa=2262)

### 12 March 1883: archiepiscopal judgment

Payá declared that the relics truly belonged to James and his disciples and were worthy of cult, then submitted the process to Rome.

### 1884: Roman investigation

A special congress of the Sacred Congregation of Rites initially returned *Dilata et ad mentem*, requiring clarification. Pope Leo XIII sent **Agostino Caprara**, Promoter General of the Faith, with a curial notary to Compostela. Caprara inspected the bones, excavations, documents, and witnesses, including objectors.

During his journey he examined the Pistoia relic or reviewed its examination. Physician **Alberto Chiappelli** identified it as a piece of the right mastoid region. Supporters reported that the equivalent area was absent from one Compostela skull group and treated this as a “fit.” No modern published osteological demonstration establishes a direct physical refit.

Caprara’s full report is now missing. Selected passages survive through Cardinal **Domenico Bartolini’s** publication. This loss materially weakens reconstruction of the Roman evidentiary review.

### 3 August 1884: ceremonial return

Following the Congregation’s affirmative judgment, the bones were solemnly moved from the archiepiscopal chapel to the reconstructed crypt.

### 1 November 1884: *Deus omnipotens*

Leo XIII’s apostolic letter confirmed Payá’s sentence, ratified the cult, recited the traditional history, and urged renewed pilgrimage. It is printed in the *Acta Sanctae Sedis*. This is an ecclesiastical authentication, not a laboratory identification. [Vatican *Acta Sanctae Sedis* archive](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ass/index_po.htm)

### 1884–1891: final shrine

The chapter commissioned the silver shrine in 1884. Its inscription dates workshop completion to 1886. Sources variously say it was placed in the crypt in 1886 or installed in its definitive setting in 1891. The discrepancy may reflect completion, provisional placement, and final installation as distinct events.

### 30 August 1936: political procession

With the Spanish Civil War underway, Archbishop **Tomás Muñiz Pablos** authorized the silver shrine to be carried through Compostela in a procession praying for peace but explicitly associated in contemporary and later accounts with a rapid Nationalist victory. Photographs show the reliquary leaving through the Puerta Real and crossing the Praza da Quintana.

This was reportedly the only occasion after its nineteenth-century installation on which the entire silver shrine left the cathedral. The event demonstrates direct political mobilization of the relics in the Nationalist zone. [Study of cult politicization, 1936–1943](https://editorial.ucatolica.edu.co/index.php/RevClat/article/download/1754/1599)

### 1946–1966: archaeological excavation around the shrine

Manuel Chamoso Lamas, with Francisco Pons-Sorolla and at times José Guerra Campos, excavated approximately 750 square metres beneath the cathedral. They found:

- Roman structures and a Roman/late-antique cemetery;
- medieval burials;
- foundations of the churches attributed to Alfonso II and Alfonso III;
- the displaced tombstone and remains attributed to Theodemir.

These campaigns strengthened the ninth-century historical setting but did not scientifically test the sealed apostolic assemblage. [Arquivo de Galicia excavation collection](https://arquivosdegalicia.xunta.gal/es/arquivo-galicia/publicaciones/excavaciones-en-la-catedral-de-santiago)

### 1982–2010: papal engagements

John Paul II prayed at the tomb during his 1982 visit and again made it the focal point of World Youth Day in 1989. Benedict XVI visited in 2010. These engagements renewed ecclesiastical and international attention without reopening the authenticity process. [John Paul II, Santiago homily, 9 November 1982](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/es/homilies/1982/documents/hf_jp-ii_hom_19821109_santiago-compostela.html) [John Paul II, 1989 pilgrim rite](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/speeches/1989/august/documents/hf_jp_spe_19890819_santiago-pilgrim-rite.pdf)

### Present

The cathedral chapter remains custodian. The bones remain sealed within the cedar chest and silver shrine in the crypt.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 What is established

- Human remains were found in 1879.
- The assemblage was extensively fragmented.
- Nineteenth-century examiners divided it into three incomplete skeletal groups.
- It was discovered in a secondary, roughly constructed deposit, not in anatomical burials.
- The site contains a genuine Roman and late-antique cemetery.
- A James shrine existed there by the ninth century.
- The relics have remained under cathedral custody since 1879.
- The Catholic hierarchy canonically authorized their veneration.

### 4.2 What is not established

No modern scientific evidence establishes:

- first-century dates for the bones;
- Middle Eastern childhood or residence;
- genetic relationship among the individuals;
- biological sex under modern criteria;
- evidence of decapitation in the principal assemblage;
- continuity between the occupants of the Roman mausoleum, the bodies recognized in the ninth century, the medieval Pistoia source body, the 1589 concealment, and the 1879 bones.

### 4.3 Weaknesses of the 1879 scientific examination

The commission answered the questions asked of it, but its methods were not capable of identifying named ancient people. Its conclusion was carefully negative: it found nothing making the attribution “rash or improbable.” That is not equivalent to proving it.

Limitations include:

- unknown stratigraphic history;
- a secondary mixed deposit;
- no radiometric dating;
- no biomolecular study;
- no modern sex or age analysis;
- washing and sorting that may have removed residues;
- chemical “antiquity” estimates lacking calendar resolution;
- the investigators’ reliance on the expected number of three bodies.

The number “three” is itself vulnerable to circularity: tradition expected James and two disciples; fragmentary remains were then organized into three groups.

### 4.4 The Pistoia comparison

**Case for continuity:** Pistoia has documents and civic tradition connecting its fragment to a gift from Compostela in the twelfth century. In the nineteenth century it was identified as part of the right mastoid region; one Compostela cranial set allegedly lacked that portion.

**Problems:** no published modern three-dimensional refit or genetic comparison exists; absence of a fragment from an incomplete skull is not distinctive; the Pistoia piece’s own chain and anatomical description require modern verification. The reported “blood” on it was a nineteenth-century visual or chemical inference, not an accepted modern forensic finding.

### 4.5 The “Athanasius martyr” inscription

Isidoro Millán González-Pardo and Antonio Blanco Freijeiro proposed readings of graffiti or an inscription including a form of *martyr* and a reference to Athanasius. Advocates regard this as indirect confirmation of the traditional burial group.

The reading, date, and connection to the bodies are disputed. It is not an intact epitaph identifying the 1879 deposit. Even if the name Athanasius were secure, it would not establish the identity of James.

### 4.6 Louis Duchesne’s critique

In “Saint Jacques en Galice” (1900), church historian Louis Duchesne subjected the tradition to source criticism. He emphasized the silence of early sources, the late Spanish mission tradition, and the still later translation narrative. He suggested, cautiously, that an earlier local holy grave might have been reassigned to James.

Duchesne is often credited with asserting that the body was definitely Priscillian’s. His actual position was more tentative. [Original article record and text](https://www.persee.fr/issue/anami_0003-4398_1900_num_12_46)

### 4.7 Priscillian hypothesis

Priscillian, executed at Trier in 385, was reportedly carried home and honoured by followers. Because he was associated with Gallaecia and had a martyr-like following, some writers proposed that his remains became the nucleus of Compostela’s cult.

Evidence **for** is circumstantial:

- Priscillian’s western Iberian following;
- the transportation of his remains;
- the existence of a late-antique cultic cemetery;
- the lack of evidence for James before the ninth century.

Evidence **against or absent**:

- no source places Priscillian specifically at Compostela;
- no inscription identifies him there;
- no scientific test links the crypt bones to a fourth-century individual;
- modern specialist review concludes that proposed burial places, including Compostela, remain unsupported.

The hypothesis is possible as a literary reconstruction but unproved. It must not be stated as the discovered identity of the bones. [Diego Piay Augusto, “Le lieu d’enterrement de Priscillien”](https://portalinvestigacion.uniovi.es/documentos/5e57af622999527d991b3022?lang=ca)

### 4.8 Alternative occupants

Other possibilities include:

- one or more Roman or late-antique local burials;
- unnamed Christian martyrs;
- bodies deliberately translated to the shrine before or during the ninth century;
- authentic medieval cult relics whose earlier history cannot be reconstructed;
- a mixture of remains from different periods.

The assemblage’s condition and secondary deposition leave all these alternatives open.

### 4.9 Church authentication versus historical proof

Leo XIII’s decision established permission and authority for Catholic cult. It did not turn the matter into a dogmatic proposition binding historical or scientific judgment. Modern cathedral and Vatican practice generally maintains the traditional identification while placing spiritual emphasis on pilgrimage and apostolic memory.

### 4.10 No modern testing of the main relics

Published modern analyses concern:

- the separate James Alphaeus relic;
- Theodemir’s remains;
- other burials beneath the cathedral.

They do not amount to radiocarbon or DNA testing of the three crypt groups. Statements that DNA has “proved the apostle’s bones” confuse these separate investigations.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Pilgrimage focus

The relics are the terminal sacred object of the Camino de Santiago. Pilgrims traditionally:

1. enter the cathedral;
2. pray at the high altar;
3. embrace the enthroned statue behind it;
4. descend to the crypt;
5. pray before the silver urn.

The bones themselves are not exposed. Ritual contact is visual and spatial, mediated through the shrine, altar, tomb chamber, and apostolic image.

### 6.2 Liturgical feasts

- **25 July:** feast of Saint James.
- **30 December:** feast of the Translation, commemorating the body’s legendary journey from the Holy Land to Galicia.

The Translation feast includes a cathedral procession, the Santiago Coquatrix image-reliquary, episcopal liturgy, civic and royal representation, and sometimes the *botafumeiro*. The present processional object is not ordinarily the crypt urn. [2024 cathedral liturgy](https://catedraldesantiago.es/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/Guion_Traslacion2024GAL.pdf)

### 6.3 Holy Years and indulgence

A Compostela Holy Year occurs when 25 July falls on Sunday. Opening the Holy Door, pilgrimage, confession, communion, prayer, and specified dispositions are associated with the jubilee indulgence. The relics provide the shrine’s apostolic centre, although the juridical requirements attach to religious acts rather than physical contact with bone.

### 6.4 Access and taboos

The shrine is visible but sealed. Ordinary visitors may enter the crypt but may not touch or open the reliquary. Historically, access to the underlying chamber became progressively more restricted. Philip II’s reported order not to reopen it and the secrecy attributed to the 1589 concealment exemplify the intertwining of reverence and security.

### 6.5 Political legitimacy

Possession of an apostolic body elevated Iria/Compostela to exceptional standing. The shrine supported:

- the transfer and elevation of episcopal authority;
- Compostela’s rivalry with Toledo and claims to apostolic dignity;
- royal patronage;
- the “patron of Spain” ideology;
- the medieval Voto de Santiago;
- military imagery of Santiago *Matamoros*;
- twentieth-century Nationalist mobilization.

The relics did not themselves appear in all these political episodes, but their claimed presence supplied the material premise.

### 6.6 Symbolic reproduction

The scallop shell, star, cross-sword of Santiago, pilgrim staff, and tomb have become transnational symbols reproduced on:

- route markers;
- confraternity insignia;
- seals and certificates;
- civic heraldry;
- tourist material;
- UNESCO representations;
- religious and state ceremonial objects.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 Preaching in Spain

**Earliest tier:** seventh-century Latin tradition.

James evangelizes Spain before returning to Jerusalem. The New Testament does not say this, and earlier Christian writers do not clearly attest it.

### 7.2 Miraculous sea translation

**Tier:** medieval elaboration, fullest in eleventh- and twelfth-century sources.

After decapitation, disciples carry the body aboard an unmanned or divinely guided vessel. It crosses the Mediterranean and Atlantic and lands at Iria Flavia, sometimes on a stone boat.

The stone-boat motif is absent from the earliest James sources and belongs to a broader Atlantic hagiographic tradition.

### 7.3 Queen Lupa, dragon, and bulls

The disciples request land from Lupa. She sends them toward danger, expecting their death. A dragon is defeated by the sign of the cross; wild bulls become tame; Lupa converts and gives her palace or land for the burial.

The story sacralizes local topography and converts pagan authority into service of the apostolic body.

### 7.4 The star field

Pelagius sees stars or lights above the forgotten tomb. This produces the familiar *campus stellae* explanation of Compostela. The etymology is devotional rather than philologically secure.

### 7.5 Charlemagne and the Milky Way

In the pseudo-Turpin, Book IV of the *Liber Sancti Iacobi*, James appears to Charlemagne, points out the starry road or Milky Way, and commands him to liberate the route to the tomb.

This is pseudohistorical literature. Charlemagne died before the conventional discovery date. The story served to bind Compostela to Frankish crusading and pilgrimage networks.

### 7.6 Survival of al-Mansur

Tradition says Muslim attackers destroyed the city but respected, failed to find, or were prevented from harming the apostolic tomb. Later versions credit Pedro de Mezonzo with removing the relics. The shrine’s survival became evidence of divine protection, though the physical sequence cannot be reconstructed.

### 7.7 Concealment from Drake

The 1589 story combines a documented English threat with an undocumented secret burial. Wax drippings observed in 1879 were interpreted as material traces of a hurried nocturnal operation.

The legend affected the object directly: it guided López Ferreiro’s search and supplied the explanation linking the secondary cist to the medieval tomb.

### 7.8 Priscillian beneath the apostle

This is a modern historical hypothesis that entered popular legend. Its strongest form—“the cathedral knows the bones are Priscillian’s”—is unsupported. No testing has established it, and the church has not admitted a substitution.

### 7.9 “DNA proved Santiago”

This is a modern misinformation pattern produced by confusion with the 2024 study of Theodemir and the separate forensic examination of James Alphaeus. Neither study sampled the principal apostolic relics.

## 8. Current Status and Location

The relics remain in:

**Crypt beneath the high altar  
Cathedral of Santiago de Compostela  
Praza do Obradoiro / Praza das Praterías access  
Santiago de Compostela, Galicia, Spain**

The cathedral chapter controls the shrine as ecclesiastical property. The building and historic city are protected under Spanish heritage law and UNESCO designation, but no repatriation or ownership proceeding concerning the crypt bones is publicly active.

The basilica and sepulchre are normally open daily, presently advertised from approximately 7:00 a.m. to 9:00 p.m., subject to liturgy and security. Entry to the basilica and tomb is free; baggage restrictions apply. [Official cathedral visitor information](https://catedraldesantiago.es/en/visits/)

The shrine is:

- on permanent devotional display;
- behind a barrier or grille;
- still used as a focus of Catholic prayer;
- not normally opened;
- not catalogued through a published museum accession number;
- not subject to a published programme of modern scientific sampling.

The UNESCO-listed Camino network defines the apostolic tomb as its destination. [UNESCO Camino listing](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/669/) The old city itself is described by UNESCO as a city that grew around the “Holy Tomb.” [UNESCO Santiago listing](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/347/)

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Nineteenth-century revival

The rediscovery and papal authentication were components of a planned revival after declining pilgrimage and the disappearance of the relics from view. López Ferreiro’s Neo-Romanesque crypt and Losada workshop’s shrine created the visual form through which modern pilgrims encounter the medieval claim.

The installation is not neutral restoration. It is historicist staging intended to make apostolic continuity architecturally legible. [Ana Pérez Varela, historicism study](https://hispaniasacra.revistas.csic.es/index.php/hispaniasacra/article/view/920)

### 9.2 Scholarship, apologetics, and scepticism

The relics became an international test case in the conflict between confessional archaeology and historical criticism:

- López Ferreiro, Fita, Fernández-Guerra, Bartolini, and later Guerra Campos defended continuity.
- Duchesne challenged the source tradition.
- Sánchez-Albornoz accepted the historical importance of the cult while disputing proof of apostolic identity.
- Priscillian theories became popular despite their evidentiary weakness.

### 9.3 Francoist use

The 1936 procession placed the physical reliquary in a partisan political setting. Francoist culture further mobilized Santiago as patron and crusading warrior. The crypt relics themselves remained less visually aggressive than *Matamoros* imagery, but they supplied its claimed sacred body and territorial centre.

### 9.4 Papal and European symbolism

John Paul II’s 1982 “European” appeal from Santiago and the 1989 World Youth Day positioned the tomb as a symbol of Europe’s Christian memory. The Camino subsequently expanded as religious pilgrimage, cultural itinerary, heritage network, and tourism economy.

### 9.5 UNESCO and mass pilgrimage

UNESCO inscribed the old town in 1985, the Spanish Camino routes in 1993, and French routes in 1998. The relic shrine thus functions simultaneously as:

- a Catholic cult centre;
- destination of a transnational heritage system;
- tourism icon;
- anchor of local hospitality and service economies.

The Pilgrim Reception Office recorded more than half a million *Compostela* certificates in 2025, although certificate counts do not equal all visitors or exclusively religious pilgrims. [Pilgrim Office statistics portal](https://oficinadelperegrino.com/estadisticas-2/)

### 9.6 Literature, film, and popular culture

The tomb and relic tradition appear in medieval chansons, pilgrim literature, modern historical novels, travel writing, documentaries, television, and conspiracy fiction. The most materially influential modern representation has not been a single novel or film but the global heritage image of the scallop-marked road terminating at the silver shrine.

### 9.7 Replicas and images

Photographs and small replicas reproduce the silver reliquary. Many omit the candles and framed Latin texts seen in older photographs, simplifying the object into a self-contained silver chest. The cathedral’s digital presentation explicitly describes it as the shrine in which “tradition” locates James, Athanasius, and Theodore—wording more cautious than nineteenth-century canonical declarations.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Principal primary sources

- Acts of the Apostles 12:1–2.
- *Breviarium apostolorum*.
- Pseudo-Isidore, *De ortu et obitu patrum*.
- *O Dei Verbum*.
- Ninth-century royal diplomas copied in *Tumbo A*.
- Pseudo-Leo letter on the translation.
- *Historia Compostellana*.
- *Liber Sancti Iacobi/Codex Calixtinus*.
- Pistoia correspondence and authentication tradition.
- Ambrosio de Morales, *Viage santo*.
- Cathedral chapter acts of May 1589.
- Payá’s pastoral letters.
- ACS–CF 51, *Expediente Reliquias Apóstol*.
- Fita and Fernández-Guerra, *Recuerdos de un viaje a Santiago de Galicia*.
- Sacred Congregation of Rites proceedings.
- Leo XIII, *Deus omnipotens*.
- Manuel Chamoso Lamas’s excavation archive.

### 10.2 Major interpretive phases

**Medieval institutional historiography:** treated the apostolic identification as the premise of cathedral history and expanded the translation and miracle narratives.

**Early-modern antiquarianism:** described the shrine and architecture but could not inspect the bodies directly.

**Nineteenth-century confessional archaeology:** attempted to unite textual tradition, excavation, comparative anatomy, and papal jurisdiction.

**Modern source criticism:** separated the seventh-century Spanish mission, ninth-century shrine, and twelfth-century translation narrative.

**Twentieth-century archaeology:** established the real Roman/late-antique cemetery and early medieval churches while failing to identify the principal burial.

**Twenty-first-century biomolecular archaeology:** successfully investigated other cathedral remains, notably Theodemir, demonstrating what could theoretically be learned if the crypt bones were sampled.

### 10.3 State of the archives

The Compostela canonical file survives in the cathedral archive but has not been fully published as a modern critical edition. The Roman Caprara report is reported lost; Bartolini preserves selections. Chamoso Lamas’s photographic and excavation archive is held in Galicia, while the history of his field diary and incomplete publication complicates assessment.

### 10.4 Unresolved research questions

1. How many biological individuals are actually present?
2. What are their calibrated radiocarbon dates?
3. Where did they grow up, according to strontium and oxygen isotopes?
4. Are any genetically related?
5. Are all fragments human?
6. Do the Pistoia and other external relics physically or genetically match a Compostela individual?
7. Can burial-soil residues connect the bones to the original mausoleum rather than the 1589 cist?
8. Does the assemblage contain later contamination introduced during medieval or modern handling?
9. Can archival study date the secondary cist independently of the Drake narrative?
10. What is the exact current inventory and internal arrangement?

### 10.5 What a defensible conclusion can say

The cathedral contains genuine old human remains associated since 1879 with a cult whose demonstrable local existence reaches the ninth century and whose physical setting includes an authentic Roman mausoleum. Ecclesiastical authorities formally identify them as James and his disciples. Historical documentation does not bridge the first-to-ninth-century gap, and science has not identified the remains. The correct status is therefore:

> **A real human relic assemblage of unestablished date and origin, canonically authenticated in 1884 as James the Greater, Athanasius, and Theodore, but not historically or scientifically demonstrated to be those persons.**
