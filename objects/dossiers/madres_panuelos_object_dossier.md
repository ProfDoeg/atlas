# The Mothers of Plaza de Mayo's embroidered headscarves: Object Dossier

**Proposed filename:** `the_mothers_of_plaza_de_mayos_embroidered_headscarves_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary note

“The Mothers of Plaza de Mayo’s embroidered headscarves” are not one unique artifact with a single chain of custody. They are a **distributed class of individually owned and repeatedly manufactured textiles**, united by a recognizable form, mode of wear, inscriptions, and political function. The class began with improvised white cloths—reportedly including saved cloth diapers—worn during a pilgrimage to Luján in October 1977. It subsequently included purpose-made embroidered scarves, screen-printed or commercially produced examples, presentation or honorary scarves, museum specimens, and monumental reproductions in paint, ceramic, stone, mosaic, metal, and graphic design.

This dossier therefore tracks:

1. the earliest documented pañales/pañuelos used in 1977;
2. individually embroidered working scarves belonging to particular Mothers;
3. the divergent inscription traditions adopted after the Mothers divided organizationally in 1986;
4. ceremonial scarves transferred as honors or gifts;
5. accessioned museum examples;
6. the painted and tiled scarf emblems around the Pirámide de Mayo;
7. later replicas and derivative “pañuelo” movements.

No surviving scarf has been demonstrated to be **the first scarf** used at Luján. No contemporary list identifies every piece carried there. Claims that every participant wore an actual diaper formerly used by her child are contradicted by testimony from Carmen Lapacó, who said she had no saved diaper and used one of her husband’s white scarves. “The original headscarf” is thus a collective origin tradition, not an authenticated singular relic.

### Evidentiary labels used

- **Documented:** supported by contemporary records, catalogues, legislation, photographs, or attributable testimony.
- **Institutional tradition:** an account maintained by one of the Mothers’ organizations or a public institution, usually based on participant memory.
- **Retrospective testimony:** a participant’s later recollection; important but subject to ordinary problems of memory.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** an analytical conclusion rather than a discrete event.
- **Disputed/unresolved:** incompatible sources exist or the evidence is insufficient.
- **Incorrect or misleading compression:** a later formula that treats a heterogeneous class as a single artifact or makes all early scarves literal diapers.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Names

The principal Spanish names are:

- **pañuelo blanco** — “white scarf” or “white kerchief”;
- **pañuelo de las Madres de Plaza de Mayo** — “scarf of the Mothers of Plaza de Mayo”;
- **pañal-pañuelo** — “diaper-scarf,” a scholarly and commemorative compound emphasizing the transformation of a domestic infant textile into public political dress;
- **pañuelos de las Madres** — “the Mothers’ scarves”;
- **pañuelo bordado** — “embroidered scarf”;
- **pañuelo blanco de las Madres y Abuelas** — a frequent broad formulation, although the Mothers and Abuelas are distinct organizations.

*Pañuelo* derives from Spanish *paño*, cloth, and may mean a handkerchief, neckerchief, or headscarf. *Pañal* means diaper or swaddling cloth. In English the object is variously called a “white headscarf,” “kerchief,” “handkerchief,” or “white scarf.” “Shawl” is generally too large a term for the worn textile, though it is sometimes applied to the painted emblem.

“Embroidered headscarves” is accurate for many historical examples but not all. The earliest cloths were apparently uninscribed; subsequent pieces might be embroidered, handwritten, painted, printed, or plain.

### 1.2 Object class

The pañuelo is simultaneously:

- an article of dress;
- a protest identifier;
- a textile inscription;
- a portable memorial;
- an emblem of collective membership;
- a bearer of a disappeared person’s name and disappearance date;
- in the Asociación tradition, a bearer of collective slogans;
- a ceremonial award;
- a reproducible graphic sign;
- an urban heritage motif.

The textile is worn over the crown of the head, framing the face, with its ends normally tied beneath the chin or at the neck. This placement matters: photographs make the white outline visually inseparable from the Mother’s face.

### 1.3 General construction

No evidence establishes a mandatory original pattern, textile specification, or centralized workshop in 1977. The evidence instead indicates improvisation.

The earliest pieces were:

- white;
- sufficiently large to cover the head;
- folded or shaped into a head covering;
- made from saved diapers, white domestic cloth, or ordinary scarves;
- tied beneath the chin.

The often-repeated description “cloth diapers” is broadly supported by organizational testimony, but it should not be universalized. In a published recollection, Carmen Lapacó stated:

> “Yo no tenía ningún pañal. Entonces agarré un pañuelo que mi marido llevaba al cuello y me lo puse.”

She also recalled that other women without diapers made white cloth substitutes. This is strong retrospective evidence that the inaugural ensemble was materially heterogeneous. The white color was selected for visibility, especially at night, and because white infant diapers were readily recognizable domestic objects.

Later scarves were commonly white cotton, linen, gauze, or comparable lightweight woven cloth. They were sewn or hemmed into rectangles or elongated triangular/runner-like forms and embroidered in contrasting thread, frequently blue. There is no published evidence of a universal dimension, stitch standard, authorized typeface, maker’s mark, or thread specification.

### 1.4 Inscriptions and variants

The inscription history has several phases:

1. **Initial uninscribed or minimally marked pañal-pañuelo, 1977.**  
   Its immediate purpose was recognition in a crowd.

2. **Individual memorial inscription, later 1970s–1980s.**  
   Mothers embroidered or otherwise applied the name of a disappeared child or relative, sometimes a photograph and often the date of disappearance. Later testimony suggests this practice developed gradually rather than being present on every scarf at Luján.

3. **Collective inscriptions.**  
   Scarves acquired such formulations as:

   - `APARICIÓN CON VIDA`
   - `APARICIÓN CON VIDA DE LOS DESAPARECIDOS`
   - `MADRES DE PLAZA DE MAYO`
   - `ASOCIACIÓN MADRES DE PLAZA DE MAYO`
   - `MADRES DE PLAZA DE MAYO – LÍNEA FUNDADORA`

4. **Post-1986 divergence.**  
   Línea Fundadora members generally continued to use individually identifying names, dates, and family-centered formulations. The Asociación headed by Hebe de Bonafini removed individual children’s names from its scarves as part of its doctrine of **socialización de la maternidad**, or socialization of motherhood, replacing them with collective language such as “Aparición con vida.” One academic account dates adoption of the collective slogan to 1984; another describes the differentiation as becoming definitive with the 1986 split. These are compatible if 1984 is treated as the ideological and inscriptional transition and 1986 as the organizational consolidation.

The white ground and blue lettering have become conventional but are not immutable. Purple embroidery, floral ornament, black writing, printed text, and other variations are documented.

### 1.5 Catalogued specimens

- **Technique:** industrial weaving.
- **Inscription, seven lines:**

The catalogue does not publish a fiber analysis, conservation report, accession date, embroidery-stitch description, weight, or scientific dating. The typographical “nuestro hijos” in the database may reproduce the textile or may be catalogue transcription; the online record does not clarify this.

#### History Colorado: object 2015.31.8

[History Colorado object 2015.31.8](https://5008.sydneyplus.com/HistoryColorado_ArgusNet_Final/ViewRecord.aspx?record=479575c1-0616-4253-bf62-f86ada70682c&template=Object) is an unusually well-described foreign-held example.

- **Object name:** scarf.
- **Title:** *Madres de Plaza de Mayo pañuelo (Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo headscarf)*.
- **Country:** Argentina.
- **Material:** catalogued cautiously as embroidered cotton or fine linen.
- **Shape:** runner-like.
- **Embroidery:** purple lettering; small dark-blue and dark-pink flowers.
- **Name:** `Lillianna Galletti`.
- **Other inscription:** `Desapareció 13-6-1977`.
- **Attached item:** a calling card sewn through the card and textile, printed with the names Alfredo Galletti and Elida B. de Galletti.
- **Handwritten note:** `Que quien te recoga nos traiga la suerte`, catalogued with the approximate sense “May whoever picks you up bring us luck.”
- **Provenance:** presented to United States congresswoman Patricia “Pat” Schroeder.
- **Accession number:** 2015.31.8.

The date on this object is the date of disappearance, not necessarily the date the scarf was made. The catalogue describes Lillianna Galletti as the missing woman and tentatively identifies Alfredo and Elida as her parents. It does not claim that the piece was present at Luján in October 1977.

#### Museo Casa Rosada/Bicentennial Museum example

The permanent-display descriptions for the Museo de la Casa Rosada identify a scarf embroidered:

`Aparición con vida de los desaparecidos / Madres de Plaza de Mayo`

Public summaries do not provide a published accession number, precise measurements, donor, fiber analysis, or individualized ownership history. Its status as an authentic movement artifact is institutionally asserted, but its complete object biography remains unpublished online.

### 1.6 Condition and scientific examination

No located conservation or laboratory publication reports radiocarbon dating, fiber microscopy, dye analysis, DNA testing, isotopic analysis, or forensic comparison for an early pañuelo. Such work would have limited capacity to authenticate collective identity: ordinary commercially made cloth could establish a production range but not prove use by a particular Mother.

Published museum catalogues rely mainly on provenance, inscriptions, associated documentation, and donor history. No catalogue raisonné of all surviving pañuelos has been identified.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Political and material prehistory

The Mothers began meeting in Plaza de Mayo on 30 April 1977. During the earliest encounters, some reportedly used a carpenter’s nail in a lapel as a recognition device. Later recollections also mention a wooden cross and a proposed tree branch. None provided adequate long-distance visibility.

These objects were precursors, not forms of the headscarf itself. They demonstrate that the pañuelo arose from a practical problem: women arriving separately at a very large permitted gathering needed to find one another without an organizational uniform or conspicuous political badge.

### 2.2 The Luján event

The best-supported date is **Saturday, 1 October 1977**, continuing into Sunday, 2 October, during the annual youth pilgrimage from Buenos Aires to the Basilica of Our Lady of Luján. Línea Fundadora’s historical exhibition gives 1 October. Local research in *El Civismo*, drawing on contemporary reporting, states that the main pilgrimage departed San Cayetano in Liniers at 14:30 on 1 October and reached Luján the following morning. The event reportedly attracted roughly 300,000 pilgrims.

The Asociación Madres de Plaza de Mayo has published mutually inconsistent commemorative dates:

- one official page says [1 October 1977](https://madres.org/2-el-panuelo-blanco/);
- another is titled [“Nacimiento del Pañuelo – 7 de Octubre de 1977”](https://madres.org/nacimiento-del-panuelo-7-de-octubre-de-1977/);
- a later recollection attributed to Hebe de Bonafini says 9 October;
- a 2007 newspaper article called it Sunday, 7 October, although 7 October 1977 was a Friday.

The calendar, Línea Fundadora account, and contemporary local-pilgrimage reconstruction favor 1–2 October. The 7 and 9 October dates should be treated as retrospective commemorative drift, not equally secure alternatives.

### 2.3 Maker and manufacturing process

No individual inventor is securely identified. Retrospective accounts say “one of the Mothers” proposed a diaper. Some women remembered the idea but explicitly said they did not know who first voiced it. Assigning invention to Azucena Villaflor, Hebe de Bonafini, or another named Mother would exceed the evidence located.

The first pieces were not commissioned or made by a recognized workshop. They were selected or improvised by participants from domestic textiles. The frequently repeated claim that “all had kept a diaper as a remembrance” is an institutional-origin formula; Lapacó’s account shows that at least some had not.

Manufacture of later embroidered scarves was decentralized. Mothers and supporters sewed and embroidered them. A La Plata history records women continuing to embroider in the Plaza and identifies Ana Kovalch de Dimovich and Rosa de Camarotti with scarf-making. Individual handwork helps explain spelling, ornament, dimension, and layout differences.

### 2.4 Earliest attestation

The initial Luján action received little or no contemporary press notice. The Asociación itself says it was not reported in the media. Consequently, the event is principally documented through:

- later oral testimony from participants;
- organizational records and publications;
- subsequent press reconstructions using local 1977 pilgrimage reports;
- the continuous use of white headscarves in dated photographs from the dictatorship;
- foreign television and photographic coverage by 1978.

The absence of identified press photography from 1–2 October 1977 prevents matching any surviving textile to the first group.

The 30 September 1978 correspondence and human-rights documentation assembled by CELS confirms that the Mothers again entered the Luján pilgrimage wearing white scarves. This near-contemporary record establishes the emblem’s regular use by the following year.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 Why no single chain exists

The pañuelo’s provenance is a branching network:

```text
domestic diapers/scarves/white cloth
                │
       Luján participants, 1977
                │
     individually retained and worn
                │
       later purpose-made scarves
         ┌──────┴─────────┐
Línea Fundadora      Asociación
names/dates          collective slogans
         │                 │
 family inheritance   replacement/use
 museum donation      honorary presentation
         └──────┬──────────┘
        museums, archives,
     public monuments, replicas
```

Most working scarves remained personal effects. They accompanied their wearers through weekly rounds, marches, pilgrimages, press appearances, foreign tours, commemorations, funerals, and political events. Many were presumably replaced as cloth wore out, but no systematic replacement registry has been published.

### 3.2 1977: domestic custody to collective public use

Before the pilgrimage, actual diapers were held in family homes as saved infant belongings. At Luján they temporarily became worn collective identifiers. Ownership did not transfer to a central organization: each cloth remained with its wearer.

The route itself—Liniers or intermediate joining points to Luján—was the first documented physical journey of the object class. The women arrived separately, recognized one another by the white cloths, assembled, displayed photographs and banners, and called attention to the disappeared.

### 3.3 1977–1983: circulation during dictatorship

After Luján, white headscarves were worn in the Thursday rounds around the Pirámide de Mayo. They moved with Mothers to:

- Plaza de Mayo;
- police stations, courts, churches, and government offices;
- human-rights gatherings;
- the 1978 Luján pilgrimage;
- meetings with foreign journalists and delegations;
- demonstrations during the 1978 World Cup;
- the Marches of Resistance;
- international advocacy trips.

The scarf provided a visible identity under surveillance and repression. There is no evidence that police systematically confiscated every scarf, although Mothers were detained, assaulted, or forced from demonstrations. Individual loss and damage are probable but cannot be reconstructed without wearer-specific archives.

Azucena Villaflor and other women connected to the group were abducted in December 1977. No located source establishes the fate of Villaflor’s scarf at the time of her abduction; it should not be presumed to survive.

### 3.4 Embroidery and individualized memorial custody

As the practice developed, scarves carried names, dates, photographs, or demands. The textile thereby became both personal property and a public record of state disappearance. Unlike a centralized banner, it moved between the wearer’s home and the street and could be altered by the wearer.

The History Colorado Galletti scarf documents a transnational custody chain:

1. manufactured and embroidered in Argentina;
2. associated with Lillianna Galletti and probably her parents Alfredo Galletti and Elida B. de Galletti;
3. transferred as a presentation to US congresswoman Pat Schroeder;
4. eventually accessioned by History Colorado as 2015.31.8.

The public record does not state the exact presentation date or the mechanism by which Schroeder’s holdings entered the museum. That is a documented gap.

The Museo Histórico Nacional scarf has this published chain:

1. used by Haydeé Esther Gastelú de García Buela as a member of Línea Fundadora;
2. retained in family custody;
3. transferred by Diego Fernando García Gastelú;
4. accessioned and held by the Museo Histórico Nacional as MHNA17970.

The online catalogue does not give dates for manufacture, use, transfer, or accession.

### 3.5 1984–1986: alteration and ideological division

For Asociación members, removing individual names was not merely replacement of a worn garment. It was a deliberate alteration of the signifying system. Hebe de Bonafini explained that the organization removed the child’s name and ceased carrying individualized photographs so each Mother would represent all disappeared children.

The physical record after this transition therefore cannot be treated as homogeneous:

- a named scarf may preserve the earlier individualized practice or continue Línea Fundadora’s tradition;
- a collective “Aparición con vida” scarf may belong to the Asociación’s socialized-motherhood practice;
- a museum or gift scarf may combine features from several moments.

### 3.6 Honorary transfers

The Asociación transformed presentation of a pañuelo into its highest or one of its highest distinctions. Such gifts are newly made ceremonial objects rather than transfers of the donor Mother’s original 1977 cloth unless a source explicitly says otherwise.

Documented recipients include:

- **Oscar Parrilli**, 12 June 2015: presented a white scarf in recognition of commitment to the organization;
- **Dilma Rousseff**, 2017: presented a scarf at the Faculty of Humanities sports complex; Bonafini called it “the embrace of our children”;
- **Miguel Funes**, announced for 14 March 2019 as recipient of the Asociación’s “máxima distinción”;
- **Horacio Pietragalla Corti**, 2019: received a scarf from Taty Almeida and Vera Jarach of Línea Fundadora at the ex-ESMA;
- **León Gieco**, 27 April 2024: presented a scarf at ECuNHi on the 47th anniversary of the first Thursday March.

Horacio González also received a pañuelo from the Mothers; the National Library’s publication records his description of it as “el nácar más brillante de la congoja argentina.”

These transfers produce independent custodial branches. Public notices rarely supply dimensions, makers, inscription transcriptions, or later locations.

### 3.7 The pavement emblems: custody through removal and dispersal

White scarf outlines painted around the Pirámide de Mayo converted the wearable object into a fixed, repeated urban emblem. By 2005 the painted area was important enough to receive statutory protection.

Buenos Aires Law 1,653, sanctioned in 2005, declared the area surrounding the Pirámide de Mayo—where the scarves identifying the Mothers are painted—a **Sitio Histórico** under Law 1,227. The legal object was therefore the area and its memorial character, not each transient layer of paint.

During Plaza de Mayo renovations beginning in November 2017, the city removed paving slabs carrying the painted scarves. The Asociación characterized the removal as an attempt to erase history and demanded the slabs. In 2018 removed units were transferred to the Mothers and distributed to institutions, including:

- ECuNHi at the former ESMA;
- Universidad Nacional de Mar del Plata;
- Universidad Nacional de Moreno;
- Universidad Nacional de Quilmes;
- other human-rights and educational institutions reported by the organizations.

At the Universidad Nacional de Quilmes, slabs received in 2018 were permanently installed in 2023 when a university space was named “Espacio Madres y Abuelas de Plaza de Mayo.”

The scarf images around the Pirámide were repainted after renovation. The present painted ring is consequently **not materially identical** with the pre-2017 painted paving. Identity resides in location, form, legal designation, and repeated communal repainting, not original pigment or substrate.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Is there an “original”?

No singular original has been established. The founding object was a simultaneous group of diapers, scarves, and white cloths. Any museum label implying that one specimen is “the original scarf” would require evidence not found in the published record.

A credible claim for an early individual scarf would require:

- identification of its wearer;
- family or organizational provenance;
- photographs or documentation linking it to dated use;
- evidence distinguishing a later replacement from an inaugural cloth.

No published specimen currently satisfies all these conditions for 1–2 October 1977.

### 4.2 Literal diaper versus symbolic diaper

Three propositions must be separated:

1. **Documented by converging testimony:** actual saved cloth diapers were used by some Mothers at Luján.
2. **Documented by Lapacó’s testimony:** not every white cloth was an actual diaper.
3. **Later symbolic truth:** all subsequent white scarves refer to the diaper and to the absent child, even when made as scarves.

Accordingly, “the scarves were originally diapers” is valid as a concise account of the emblem’s material genealogy but false if read as a claim about every individual object.

### 4.3 Date dispute

The Asociación has commemorated 7 October, while stronger calendrical and local evidence identifies 1 October 1977. This appears to be a dispute in memorial chronology, not evidence for two separate inaugural uses. The first weekend of October is secure; 1–2 October is the most reconstructable date.

### 4.4 Inscription authenticity

An embroidered name does not by itself authenticate wear by a Mother. The emblem has been widely reproduced, and honorary pieces may use the same language as working examples. Museums therefore depend heavily on donor provenance.

The Galletti scarf’s sewn calling card materially strengthens its association with the family, but History Colorado’s wording remains appropriately cautious (“may have been” the parents). Its stated fiber—cotton or fine linen—is also explicitly uncertain.

### 4.5 Organizational identity after 1986

Both Asociación Madres de Plaza de Mayo and Madres de Plaza de Mayo–Línea Fundadora inherit the pre-split scarf tradition. Neither group’s version is a counterfeit. They are historically divergent continuations:

- Asociación: collective slogan and socialized motherhood;
- Línea Fundadora: continued individualized naming and family testimony.

Public accounts often collapse them, sometimes also merging the Abuelas. This is a naming and provenance error, not necessarily bad faith.

### 4.6 Painted scarf identity

The painted scarves constitute an iterable work or heritage practice. Each repainting destroys or covers prior pigment. The 2017–2018 paving replacement physically severed some painted forms from their original urban setting. The removed slabs became collection objects, while the repainted forms retained the site identity.

No evidence found identifies a single artist with exclusive authorship of the Plaza ring. The organizations describe the scarves as painted “by the people,” emphasizing collective execution.

### 4.7 Forgeries and unauthorized claims

No scientifically exposed forged “original 1977 pañuelo” was located. The larger authenticity risk is misdescription:

- replicas sold or exhibited without a documented wearer;
- honorary scarves confused with working scarves;
- disappearance dates mistaken for manufacturing dates;
- a later collective scarf described as an inaugural diaper;
- Mothers and Abuelas treated as interchangeable custodians.

These are catalogue and attribution problems rather than proven criminal forgery.

### Networks

The pañuelo passed through or served as an emblem for:

- Madres de Plaza de Mayo before 1986;
- Asociación Madres de Plaza de Mayo;
- Madres de Plaza de Mayo–Línea Fundadora;
- Abuelas de Plaza de Mayo, which developed a closely related white-scarf visual identity;
- Familiares de Desaparecidos y Detenidos por Razones Políticas;
- APDH, CELS, LADH, H.I.J.O.S., and the wider Argentine human-rights movement;
- international solidarity, exile, church, trade-union, university, artistic, and feminist networks;
- museums and state heritage agencies.

### Sacred associations

The object is secular and political, not a cult relic. Its inaugural public deployment nevertheless occurred within a Catholic pilgrimage to the Basilica of **Nuestra Señora de Luján**, patronal Marian shrine of Argentina. The Mothers used an authorized mass religious gathering to make a proscribed political absence visible.

Participant accounts stress tactical use rather than unanimous devotional intent. Some Mothers were Catholic; others distrusted the hierarchy. The pañuelo should therefore not be classified as a Marian garment, even though its inaugural route passed through a Marian ritual landscape.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Recognition

The first function was operational: identify fellow Mothers among hundreds of thousands of pilgrims. White was conspicuous at a distance and at night.

### 6.2 Weekly investiture

Putting on the pañuelo became a transition from private individual to public Mother. Later testimonies describe it as giving strength or energy. This is an attested subjective experience, not a claim of supernatural power.

The scarf is normally donned for:

- Thursday rounds;
- Marches of Resistance;
- human-rights commemorations;
- testimony and public speeches;
- international visits;
- funerals and memorial acts;
- institutional awards.

It is not ordinarily a complete everyday uniform.

### 6.3 Memorial inscription

A named scarf substituted materially for an absent body and unavailable grave. Scholars have compared the embroidered name to funerary and mnemonic practices: the textile makes the disappeared person publicly legible without declaring that person legally or morally resolved as dead.

The date of disappearance fixes the unresolved event in time. Photographs and names individualize; “Aparición con vida” keeps the demand grammatically active.

### 6.4 Socialized motherhood

The Asociación’s removal of individual names transformed the scarf from “my child” to all disappeared people. This was a documented political-theological shift within the movement’s maternal language. The cloth became less a personal memorial register and more a collective office or mantle.

Línea Fundadora’s retention of names represents a different political theory of testimony: collective action grounded in individually documented disappearances.

### 6.5 Political legitimacy

The right to wear or ceremonially receive the pañuelo carries moral authority derived from resistance during dictatorship. Presentation scarves operate much like civic decorations, but without being state orders. The conferring authority is the Mothers or one of their organizations.

The object’s force is not legally constitutive: wearing it does not confer formal office, sovereign power, or statutory immunity. Its authority is historical, organizational, and symbolic.

### 6.6 Access and use

There is no universal legal prohibition on reproducing or wearing a white scarf. Within organizational practice, however, an authentic working or honorary pañuelo may signal standing conferred by the Mothers. An uncontextualized replica is not equivalent to a scarf worn by a founding participant or awarded by an organization.

### 6.7 Pavement ritual

Painting and repainting white scarf outlines around the Pirámide turns the Thursday circuit into a permanent absent procession. Even when no Mothers are present, the ground records their path.

The images are repeatedly renewed after wear, renovation, or vandalism. Repainting is thus both conservation and reenactment.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

The pañuelo does not possess an ancient supernatural mythology, but it has accumulated powerful modern origin formulas.

### 7.1 “Every Mother wore her child’s first diaper”

**Tier:** institutional tradition with a documented factual core and overgeneralized later form.

Some women wore saved diapers. Others used ordinary white scarves or cloth. The strongest defensible formulation is: **the emblem originated in a collective decision to use white infant diapers or substitutes as head coverings**.

The “first diaper” detail sharpens the symbolism of birth and maternity, but sources vary between *a diaper*, *the first diaper*, and generic white cloth. No inventory confirms which woman wore which infant textile.

### 7.2 “All the Mothers had preserved a diaper”

**Tier:** commemorative generalization contradicted by participant testimony.

Lapacó explicitly said she did not have one. The statement is symbolically expressive but not literally universal.

### 7.3 “The scarf was invented on 7 October 1977”

**Tier:** late commemorative dating, probably erroneous.

The first weekend of October is secure, but 1–2 October fits the documented 1977 pilgrimage calendar. The 7 October anniversary propagated by one Asociación page should be cited as organizational commemoration, not accepted without qualification.

### 7.4 “The pañuelo is the embrace of the disappeared children”

**Tier:** explicit Asociación interpretation.

The Asociación describes the scarf as the embrace of the children around the wearer’s head. This is symbolic doctrine, not a historical claim about manufacture.

### 7.5 “The scarf is the children’s diaper transformed”

**Tier:** materially and historically grounded symbolic lineage.

Even purpose-made later scarves retain the identity of the diaper through form, color, narrative, and use. Material discontinuity does not invalidate this symbolism, but museums should distinguish literal diapers from later commemorative textiles.

### 7.6 Relic-like status

Scarves worn by individual Mothers, especially after their deaths, may acquire relic-like affective value. Ceremonial presentation likewise transfers a portion of the movement’s moral prestige. However, no central shrine, canonization procedure, miracle tradition, or ecclesiastical authentication governs them.

The best classification is **modern civic-sacred object**: a secular textile treated with forms of reverence, taboo, inheritance, and ceremonial bestowal.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 The object class

The tradition remains in active use. On 13 March 2026 the Asociación completed its 2,500th round, with participants still wearing white scarves. Contemporary coverage also documented Línea Fundadora Mothers wearing them at public events in 2026.

The class is therefore neither lost nor closed. New scarves continue to be made, worn, presented, reproduced, and deposited.

### 8.2 Known accessioned examples

- **MHNA17970:** Museo Histórico Nacional, Buenos Aires. Public catalogue confirms institutional custody; current gallery display status is not stated.
- **2015.31.8:** History Colorado, United States. Public online collection record; current display status is not specified.
- **Museo Casa Rosada example:** identified in permanent-exhibition descriptions, though a detailed accession record was not located.
- **Removed Plaza paving slabs:** dispersed among ECuNHi and universities including Mar del Plata, Moreno, and Quilmes. The Quilmes installation became permanent in 2023.

### 8.3 Plaza de Mayo

White scarf emblems remain painted around the Pirámide de Mayo. The area is legally designated a historic site under Buenos Aires Law 1,653. The physical paint is renewable and has been replaced repeatedly. Public access is ordinarily open, subject to fencing, policing, demonstrations, and maintenance.

### 8.4 Condition and conservation

Textile condition reports are not publicly available for the catalogued examples. Potential risks include:

- light damage;
- fiber embrittlement;
- perspiration and skin oils from repeated wear;
- abrasion at folds and ties;
- bleeding or fading of embroidery dyes;
- damage from pins, sewn cards, or photographs;
- loss of wearer-specific documentation.

The painted emblems experience foot traffic, weather, repainting, paving replacement, and vandalism.

### 8.5 Current disputes

No active ownership or repatriation claim concerning an accessioned scarf was located. Current disputes concern:

- the integrity of memory sites;
- government alterations to Plaza de Mayo;
- removal or destruction of public scarf monuments;
- vandalism;
- political attempts to redefine or discredit the human-rights symbolism.

These are custody-of-memory disputes more than title disputes over individual textiles.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Photography and film

Photographic and television reproduction made the white frame around the face internationally legible. Particularly influential records include:

- Dutch television interviews during the 1978 World Cup;
- photographs of the Thursday rounds;
- Adriana Lestido’s *Madre e hija de Plaza de Mayo* (1982), in which both woman and child wear white scarves;
- *Las Madres de la Plaza de Mayo* (1985), directed by Susana Blaustein Muñoz and Lourdes Portillo.

The scarf’s graphic economy—white triangular form, blue writing, human face—made it highly reproducible in monochrome newspapers and television.

### 9.2 Art and museum display

The pañuelo has appeared in:

- paintings and prints;
- embroidery projects;
- documentary photography;
- museum cases alongside *Nunca Más* and other “new relics of the nation”;
- monumental sculpture and mosaics;
- illuminated civic displays;
- quilts and textile art;
- street murals and stencils.

Rosario’s Museo de la Memoria exhibition *Legados: Pañuelos en lucha* traced the emblem’s migration into other Latin American struggles, including Mexican collective embroidery responding to disappearances and killings.

### 9.3 The green scarf

Argentina’s abortion-rights movement adopted a green triangular scarf in the early 2000s, consciously citing the Mothers’ white pañuelo. The derivative retained:

- portable triangular textile form;
- collective inscription;
- bodily display;
- easy mass manufacture;
- capacity to turn a crowd into a visible political body.

Nora Cortiñas famously wore the white scarf on her head and a green scarf on her wrist. This was an alliance and extension, not a merger of meanings.

Anti-abortion movements subsequently adopted light-blue scarves, demonstrating that the pañuelo had become a general Argentine grammar of public political identity.

### 9.4 The 2017 anti-“2×1” mobilization

After the Argentine Supreme Court’s May 2017 decision allowing a controversial sentence-reduction calculation in a crimes-against-humanity case, mass demonstrations adopted white scarves. Approximately 70,000 were reportedly manufactured in nonwoven spunbond material for distribution in Plaza de Mayo.

These were mass-produced protest replicas, not historical diapers or personalized embroidered scarves. Their political effectiveness depended on recognizability rather than authenticity as individual artifacts.

### 9.5 Monuments, vandalism, and iconoclasm

The emblem’s replication has made it a recurrent target.

Documented episodes include:

- black coverings or paint applied to Plaza scarf images by Cecilia Pando and associates in 2008; she was later convicted and sentenced to community service;
- red crosses and Nazi symbols painted over Plaza scarves in 2020;
- theft and hammer destruction of a ceramic scarf plaque in Buenos Aires in 2021, recorded and circulated online;
- black spots and a political slogan painted over scarf images in Bahía Blanca’s Plaza Rivadavia in June 2024; human-rights representatives repainted them;
- white scarf images painted outside an army office in Posadas in March 2024 and subsequently ordered covered by the local commander;
- removal of a large scarf monument at Marcos Juárez, Córdoba, in March 2024 on the municipal claim that it lacked authorization.

The destruction of a replica does not destroy the distributed symbol. Repainting and replacement have become characteristic responses.

### 9.6 Songs, language, and education

The scarf appears in music, poetry, school curricula, children’s songs, commemorative publications, tattoos, posters, and organizational logos. “El mundo es un pañuelo,” ordinarily an idiom meaning “it’s a small world,” was repurposed as the title of the Asociación’s history of its international travels.

The object is taught as a concise representation of memory, truth, and justice. Educational simplification sometimes erases the material variation and the post-1986 inscription dispute.

### 9.7 Commercial and unauthorized reproduction

Replicas circulate as souvenirs, protest merchandise, printed graphics, and tattoos. No comprehensive licensing history was found. Commercial replication should not be assumed to have organizational authorization merely because it reproduces the form.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Foundational narrative history

Ulises Gorini’s *La rebelión de las Madres* is a major narrative source, drawing heavily on testimony and organizational history. It records the pañuelo’s novelty: feminine but no longer fashionable, visually curious, and unlike conventional party insignia.

Its value is greatest when particular recollections are attributed. Statements synthesized into a seamless collective memory require comparison with Línea Fundadora, Asociación, local Luján, and archival records.

### 10.2 Anthropology of material memory

Ludmila da Silva Catela and related scholarship interpret the scarf as a material trace of absent bodies. The infant textile asserts the prior relation “if there was a diaper, there was a child,” while the embroidered name resists disappearance as administrative erasure.

This is a scholarly interpretation supported by object use; it is not evidence that every textile was literally a used diaper.

### 10.3 Performance and gender scholarship

Diana Taylor and other scholars treat the pañuelo as part of an embodied repertoire: dress, walking, maternal identity, repetition, and occupation of national space. The scarf allowed women to mobilize culturally intelligible motherhood against a regime that claimed to defend family order.

Scholars disagree over whether this strategy reinforced traditional gender roles, transformed them, or did both simultaneously. The textile supports all three readings:

- it used a domestic maternal object;
- moved that object into political space;
- eventually expanded from an individual child to a collective revolutionary identity.

### 10.4 Object versus sign

Museum studies reveal a methodological tension. A museum can accession a particular scarf, but much of the object’s historical force lies in:

- repeated wearing;
- collective visual multiplication;
- spoken testimony;
- the Thursday route;
- the wearer’s body;
- iterative reproduction.

Removing a scarf—or a painted paving slab—from those relations preserves material evidence but changes its function. The 2017–2018 paving dispersal made this problem explicit: the slabs became portable relics precisely because they had been detached from the protected site.

### 10.5 Unresolved archival questions

Research remains incomplete in several areas:

- identification of photographs from the 1–2 October 1977 pilgrimage;
- an itemized inventory of early surviving scarves;
- provenance of the Museo Casa Rosada example;
- manufacturing and accession dates for many museum-held pieces;
- records of confiscated, lost, or destroyed scarves during dictatorship;
- complete documentation of honorary scarf recipients;
- conservation assessments;
- oral histories focused specifically on sewing, textile sourcing, repairs, and replacement;
- the earliest date and authorship of the painted scarf ring around the Pirámide.

### 10.6 Discredited or misleading popular claims

The following should not be repeated without qualification:

- that there was one original pañuelo;
- that every inaugural cloth was the wearer’s child’s first diaper;
- that all scarves have always carried names;
- that all Mothers use the same inscription today;
- that a disappearance date embroidered on a scarf dates the textile’s manufacture;
- that the Mothers and Abuelas are one organization;
- that present Plaza paint is physically the original paint;
- that any white triangular scarf is an authenticated movement artifact.

## 12. Sources

### Primary, institutional, catalogue, and legal sources

The most valuable object-level records are the Museo Histórico Nacional and History Colorado catalogues. Organizational sources preserve indispensable participant testimony but must be compared because the Asociación’s own pages disagree on the exact inaugural date. Línea Fundadora’s digital exhibition supplies the strongest organizational date of 1 October 1977. Buenos Aires Law 1,653 documents the legal transformation of the painted scarf area into protected heritage.

### Scholarly and documentary sources

The Comisión Provincial por la Memoria’s *Puentes* issue connects the pañuelo’s material form to mourning and absent bodies and quotes Juanita de Pargament. University theses and peer-reviewed articles document the inscriptional change associated with socialized motherhood, the emblem’s performance, and its adaptation by later movements. Museum-studies literature usefully describes the scarf as one of Argentina’s “new relics of the nation.”

### Limits of the dossier

A complete object census is not presently possible from public records. This dossier found no:

- scientifically examined candidate for an inaugural Luján scarf;
- complete early-photographic sequence from 1–2 October 1977;
- centralized manufacturing archive;
- comprehensive register of presentation scarves;
- published national catalogue of every institutional pañuelo;
- complete conservation report for MHNA17970 or History Colorado 2015.31.8.

These absences should be treated as research gaps, not evidence that the artifacts or records do not exist.
