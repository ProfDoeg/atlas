# The Ikurrina (Basque flag): Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `the_ikurrina_basque_flag_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary note

“The Ikurrina” is not one continuously surviving piece of cloth. It is:

1. an abstract, reproducible flag design created in 1894;
2. the first documented physical flag made to that design and raised at Bilbao’s Euskeldun Batzokija on 14 July 1894;
3. innumerable later specimens used by political parties, military units, governments, municipalities, diaspora organizations, sports teams, demonstrators, and private individuals.

These identities must not be conflated. The design survives and has an unbroken documentary history. The first cloth specimen does not: it was confiscated in 1895, after which its securely documented chain of custody ends. Other historically important specimens survive in museums and private or institutional collections, but none is demonstrably the 1894 flag.

Evidence below is labeled where necessary:

- **Documented:** established by contemporary legislation, archival material, institutional catalogues, photographs, or contemporary reporting.
- **Retrospective testimony:** later recollection by a participant or observer.
- **Institutional tradition:** a claim maintained by a party, government, foundation, museum, or other interested institution.
- **Scholarly inference:** a reasoned reconstruction not directly stated in contemporary evidence.
- **Disputed or unverified:** reported but not securely established.
- **Legend or modern accretion:** symbolic narrative rather than documented material history.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Class and identity

The Ikurrina is a **bicruciferous rectangular flag**: a red field bearing a green diagonal saltire, over which lies a white upright cross. The white cross is visually uppermost at intersections. Under Article 5.1 of the 1979 Statute of Autonomy, it is the official flag of the Autonomous Community of the Basque Country—Euskadi—comprising Álava/Araba, Gipuzkoa, and Biscay/Bizkaia. Outside that jurisdiction it is widely used, but not universally accepted, as the national or cultural flag of the broader Basque Country, Euskal Herria, including Navarre and the French Basque territories.

It is therefore simultaneously:

- an official autonomous-community flag under Spanish law;
- a national emblem in Basque nationalist usage;
- a cultural or ethnolinguistic emblem throughout much of Euskal Herria and its diaspora;
- historically, the flag devised for Biscay by the brothers Luis and Sabino Arana Goiri;
- initially an emblem closely connected with the political movement that became Euzko Alderdi Jeltzalea–Partido Nacionalista Vasco, EAJ-PNV.

There is no canonical “sacred original” in continuous custody. The closest candidates for foundational objects are the Arana brothers’ surviving design sketch and the lost first cloth flag of 1894.

### 1.2 Names, spellings, and etymology

The principal modern Basque name is **ikurrina**, normally written without a tilde in standardized Basque. Spanish and French texts commonly retain the historically widespread form **ikurriña**, representing the palatal nasal /ɲ/ explicitly. Northern Basque pronunciation may have /n/ where southern varieties have /ɲ/.

The earliest Aranist spelling appears as **ikuŕiña**, using Sabino Arana’s proposed orthographic conventions. The word was coined in the early Basque nationalist milieu and was intended more generally to mean a flag or symbolic cloth. In ordinary modern use, however, *ikurrina* has become a proper or quasi-proper name for this particular flag.

The frequently repeated derivation from Basque *ikur*, “sign” or “symbol,” and *ehun*, “cloth,” is an **institutional and popular etymology**, but its precise word-formation history is less secure than many summaries imply. Linguistic scholarship consistently identifies the word as an Aranist neologism; it should not be projected into medieval or early-modern Basque. Even *ikur* belongs to the innovative lexical program associated with the period. The flag and its name are consequently modern creations, not survivals of an ancient named standard.

Common titles include:

- **Ikurrina / Ikurriña**
- **Euskadiko bandera** — “flag of Euskadi”
- **Euskal bandera** — “Basque flag”
- **Euzkadi’ko ikurriña**, in historical Aranist spelling
- **bandera bicrucífera** — “bicruciferous flag”
- **drapeau basque**, in French
- originally, **Bizkaiko ikurriña** or the **bizkaitarra** flag

### 1.3 Official geometry and colors

The first Government of Euskadi’s decree of 19 October 1936, corrected on 24 October and published in the *Diario Oficial del País Vasco* on 21 and 28 October, defined the flag as a vermilion-red field carrying a bright-green saltire and a superimposed white cross. The arms were to reach, respectively, the corners and the midpoints of the flag’s sides.

The corrected dimensions prescribed:

- fly or horizontal length: **5.00 metres**;
- height: **2.80 metres**;
- width of each green and white band: **0.43 metre**;
- proportional reduction or enlargement for other sizes.

This yields a ratio of **28:50**, conventionally reduced to **14:25**. The height is 56 percent of the fly; each band is 8.6 percent of the fly. The present Basque Government’s identity system retains these proportions and specifies approximately:

- red: **Pantone 485 C**;
- green: **Pantone 347 C**;
- white: unprinted or opaque white.

The modern Pantone specifications are administrative standardizations, not evidence about the chemical dyes of the 1894 specimen.

### 1.4 Symbolism assigned by the designers

In Sabino Arana’s 1895 dialogue “La bandera fenicia,” the flag is explicitly the flag of the “Bizkain nation,” not yet of all Basques. The design translates the components of the then-understood arms of Biscay into cloth:

- **red field:** Biscay itself, or the red field of the Biscayan arms;
- **green saltire:** the green oak of Gernika and the “Old Law,” *Lagi-Zaŕa*; Aranist explanation also connected the saltire with Saint Andrew and the legendary battle of Arrigorriaga;
- **white cross:** *Jaun-Goikua*, God or the Catholic faith;
- **superposition of white over green:** religion placed hierarchically above the political or customary law.

Together the design materialized the motto **Jaun-Goikua eta Lagi-Zaŕa**, usually modernized as *Jaungoikoa eta Lege Zaharra*, “God and the Old Law.”

The often repeated claim that the red symbolizes “Basque blood” is not the securely documented original explanation. It is a later popular reinterpretation.

### 1.5 The first physical specimen

Secondary accounts based on nationalist archival research describe the first flag as:

- made of **wool or lightweight wool cloth** (*lana*, *lanilla*);
- approximately **5 × 2.8 metres**;
- manufactured at the establishment or factory of **Gumersindo Bilbao Goyaga**;
- deliberately very large so that it would dominate other flags visible in central Bilbao.

The reported dimensions coincide strikingly with those adopted by decree in 1936, suggesting that the government standardized the proportions of the foundational specimen or the established design derived from it. No published textile examination, fiber analysis, dye study, conservation report, accession number, or present physical location is known for this first flag. It should be classified as **lost, probably destroyed or discarded after confiscation**.

### 1.6 Surviving related objects

Several related but non-identical artifacts survive:

- **Original design sketch:** the Sabino Arana Foundation states that its archive preserves the original design, probably drawn by Luis Arana, an architect. A complete public conservation or forensic report has not been located.
- **Sabin Etxea ceremonial material:** the Museum of Basque Nationalism preserves the streamers or ties associated with the Ikurrina raised at Sabin Etxea’s 1932 inauguration; these are not the 1894 flag.
- **Ajuria Enea flag:** the Museum of Basque Nationalism records the first Ikurrina flown at Ajuria Enea, donated by Iñaki Betanzos Soto. Again, this is a later specimen with its own provenance.
- **Aita Mari flag and other military, maritime, political, and exile flags:** preserved in the museum’s collections as individual historical specimens.
- **The Atotxa derby flag of 1976:** the cloth carried jointly by Real Sociedad captain Inaxio Kortabarria and Athletic Club captain José Ángel Iribar has continued to be exhibited ceremonially; its identity rests on participant and club custody rather than any relationship to the 1894 cloth.

No surviving specimen has a defensible claim to being “the original Ikurrina” unless “original” refers to a particular later episode rather than the 1894 founding flag.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Precursors

The Ikurrina was not the first proposed flag for a collective Basque identity. Historian Óscar Álvarez Gila has documented experimental Basque and Basque-Navarrese banners developed in Argentina, Uruguay, and Cuba from the 1880s. Pedro Manuel de Soraluce promoted one such composite emblem. These flags answered a diaspora need for a symbol transcending the separate provincial arms.

They were not Ikurrinas and should not be treated as earlier versions of the Arana design. Their existence demonstrates that the demand for a common Basque flag predated 1894 while the eventually dominant solution did not.

### 2.2 The Castejón/Gamazada banner

On the night of 17–18 February 1894, during public mobilization surrounding the Navarrese *Gamazada*, the Arana brothers and associates reportedly sketched a banner at Pamplona’s Café Iruña. Juana Irujo, wife of Estanislao Aranzadi, made it for a reception at Castejón. It bore a **red saltire on white**, with a greeting from Biscay to Navarre and the motto “God and the Old Law.”

This object has sometimes been called the first Ikurrina. Luis Arana later denied that identification. The banner lacked the canonical red field, green saltire, and superimposed white cross. It is best classified as the **earliest documented Aranist political banner and a compositional precursor**, not the first specimen of the Ikurrina proper.

A recently publicized interpretation by José María Esparza, drawing on Teodoro Galarza Arranbide’s later memoirs, argues that Sabino Arana displayed an Ikurrina-like flag months before July 1894. Because the claim depends on retrospective memoir evidence and has not yet displaced the established distinction between the Castejón banner and the bicruciferous design, it remains **a significant but unresolved revision claim**.

### 2.3 Design process and attribution

The design was produced jointly by **Luis and Sabino Arana Goiri** in 1894 for Biscay. Sources vary in allocating individual authorship:

- nationalist institutional histories generally credit both brothers;
- some sources call Sabino the conceptual author;
- others identify Luis, trained as an architect, as the draftsman and principal visual designer;
- the Foundation’s attribution of the surviving sketch as “probably” Luis’s is appropriately cautious.

The strongest reconstruction is collaborative: Sabino supplied or articulated the religious-political symbolism; Luis helped give it graphic and proportional form. There is no surviving manufacturing contract publicly cited, no confirmed price, and no identified seamstress in the best-supported account of the 1894 cloth. Claims about Gumersindo Bilbao Goyaga identify the manufacturing establishment, not necessarily the individual artisan who cut and stitched it.

### 2.4 First securely documented public raising

At **18:00 on Saturday, 14 July 1894**, the flag was raised from the balconies of the newly inaugurated **Euskeldun Batzokija**, on Bilbao’s Calle Correo at its corner with the Arenal. Sources differ between the contemporary street numbers 22 and 34, probably because of renumbering or descriptions of different entrances; this minor topographical discrepancy should not be converted into rival-event theories.

**Ciriaco de Iturri y Urlezaga**, aged about fifty and the oldest of the association’s ninety-four founders, raised it. He was a former Carlist officer of the sixth company of the Arratia battalion. The next day the organization held a Mass at Begoña and elected Sabino Arana president.

This is the earliest securely established raising of the complete red-green-white design.

### 2.5 Earliest textual exposition

Sabino Arana explained the design in “La bandera fenicia,” published in issue 31 of *Bizkaitarra* on **28 July 1895**. Written as a dialogue, it calls the flag that adopted by Biscayan nationalism and the “flag of the Biscayan Nation.” The article is indispensable because it shows what the creators then claimed the object meant. It does not support later assertions that they designed it in 1894 as the flag of all seven Basque territories.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 Chain of the first 1894 cloth

**1894 — Manufacture.**  
The Arana brothers’ design was reportedly made in wool or *lanilla* at Gumersindo Bilbao Goyaga’s establishment in Bilbao. No surviving invoice, maker’s label, or published workshop record has been identified.

**14 July 1894 — Transfer into public and institutional use.**  
The flag was installed at the Euskeldun Batzokija and raised by Ciriaco de Iturri. The precise legal owner—one or both Arana brothers, the association, or a donor—is not established in the readily accessible record.

**14 July 1894–12 September 1895 — Batzokija custody.**  
It remained associated with and apparently displayed at the Bilbao clubhouse.

**12 September 1895 — Confiscation.**  
Spanish authorities closed the Euskeldun Batzokija amid proceedings arising from its nationalist activity and seized the flag. This was a coercive state confiscation, not a voluntary transfer.

**After 12 September 1895 — Chain broken.**  
Later stories place it at the Bilbao provincial court or *Audiencia*. One widely repeated account says it was still there in 1932 and may have been used by painters as a dust sheet to protect furniture. This is **unverified anecdote**, not an established final disposition. The cloth has not been recovered, accessioned, or scientifically identified. Its fate is unknown.

### 3.2 Propagation of the design

Unlike a crown or reliquary, the design propagated by copying rather than by transfer of the first specimen.

- **19 March 1895:** the first reported raising away from the founding Batzokija occurred aboard the Bermeo yacht or sailing vessel *Aketxe*. A photograph is cited by the Sabino Arana Foundation.
- **Busturia:** Luis Arana gave an Ikurrina to the next Batzoki established there.
- **August 1908:** an Ikurrina at the Begoña Batzoki reportedly received a salute from Alfonso XIII, who apparently mistook its status. The anecdote is institutional tradition and should not be read as royal recognition.
- **First decade of the twentieth century:** examples appeared in diaspora publications and associations in the Americas.
- **1916:** Luis de Eleizalde carried one to the Third Congress of Nationalities at Lausanne. This was an early documented international political appearance.
- **9 July 1921:** Buenos Aires’s Laurak Bat center raised the Ikurrina alongside the Argentine flag. This episode was important because the originally Biscayan design was used to represent Basques collectively.
- **1923–1930:** under Primo de Rivera’s dictatorship, public display of non-state political flags was suppressed. Clandestine mountaineering groups nevertheless placed Ikurrinas at conspicuous sites, including a reported 1924 placement on the Pico del Fraile near Orduña.
- **29 August 1931 and afterward:** Basque municipalities asked Eusko Ikaskuntza–Sociedad de Estudios Vascos whether a politically neutral common flag existed. Its conclusion reportedly acknowledged that no single historical flag had represented the whole country, but recognized the Ikurrina’s achieved social use as an expression of Basque spiritual unity. This was recognition of contemporary adoption, not a claim of medieval continuity.
- **26–27 March 1932:** Luis Arana raised an Ikurrina at the restored Sabin Etxea immediately before the first Aberri Eguna. Material accessories from that raising survive, but not necessarily the entire flag.

### 3.3 Official adoption and war

The Statute of Autonomy approved in October 1936 did not itself define a flag. The new Basque Government under José Antonio Aguirre therefore acted separately.

On **19 October 1936**, at the proposal associated with socialist Industry Councillor **Santiago Aznar**, the Government adopted the Ikurrina as Euskadi’s official flag. The decree was published on 21 October; a corrective decree of 24 October, published on 28 October, settled the dimensions.

The decree’s preamble recognized a crucial transformation: although created by Sabino Arana, the flag had come to be accepted throughout Euskadi as a symbol of unity. This governmental act shifted custodial authority over the design from its partisan and Biscayan origin to a coalition autonomous government.

The flag flew over the government headquarters in Bilbao’s **Hotel Carlton** and was used by Basque Army formations, *gudari* battalions, auxiliary naval vessels, public offices, ambulances, propaganda, and refugee or evacuation ships. Each of those flags was a separate object; surviving military and maritime examples require individual catalogues.

After Bilbao fell to Francoist forces on **19 June 1937**, the government continued in exile. The flag’s official use persisted within that exile government and its delegations, while its public display inside Francoist Spain became punishable as separatist symbolism.

### 3.4 Dictatorship: seizure, concealment, and clandestine remanufacture

During the Franco dictatorship, Ikurrinas were:

- removed from captured buildings and military positions;
- confiscated in police operations;
- hidden in homes, churches, clubs, vehicles, and mountain equipment;
- sewn privately;
- painted on walls when possession of a recoverable cloth was too dangerous;
- raised briefly on towers, peaks, power lines, and public monuments;
- used by exiles and diaspora centers beyond Spanish jurisdiction.

This period provides no single custody chain. The object’s history is a mass history of reproduction, seizure, destruction, and replacement. Confiscated specimens generally lost their maker and ownership records unless retained as police or court evidence.

The clandestine flag also acquired a deadly instrumental use. ETA employed displayed Ikurrinas as lures, warning symbols, or components of traps:

- On **5 October 1975**, an Ikurrina at the Sanctuary of Arantzazu drew a Guardia Civil patrol; a remotely detonated bomb killed Esteban Maldonado Llorente, Jesús Pascual Martín Lozano, and Juan Moreno Chamorro as they returned.
- On **17 January 1976**, Guardia Civil officer Manuel Vergara Jiménez was killed when a charge linked to a flagpole exploded near Beasain/Ordizia.
- On **11 April 1976**, explosives technician Miguel Gordo García was electrocuted while removing a flag attached to high-voltage cables in Barakaldo.

The flags in these operations were expendable devices or lures, not relics. Their use is nevertheless part of what was physically done “with” the emblem and cannot be omitted from an object history.

### 3.5 Atotxa, legalization, and renewed public custody

On **5 December 1976**, before a Real Sociedad–Athletic Club match at Atotxa stadium, captains **Inaxio Kortabarria** and **José Ángel Iribar** entered carrying an Ikurrina jointly. It was still prohibited. Accounts credit Real Sociedad player José Antonio de la Hoz Uranga and family members or associates with arranging and sewing the flag and bringing it into the stadium clandestinely. Later stories say it traveled concealed in a car’s exhaust or another vehicle compartment; the precise concealment mechanism varies and should be treated as participant tradition unless supported by contemporaneous evidence.

The photographed event became one of the most consequential uses of a particular specimen: the cloth passed from clandestine makers and carriers into the captains’ hands and then into a durable public memory shared by the two clubs.

On **19 January 1977**, the Spanish government ceased treating public display of the Ikurrina as prohibited separatist display. The measure is often called its “legalization,” although accounts frequently describe an administrative decision rather than cite a stand-alone statute bearing the flag’s name. Lieutenant Colonel Antonio Tejero publicly protested the decision and was transferred from Gipuzkoa.

Municipalities soon raised it openly. Its appearance in Pamplona and other Navarrese town halls immediately made physical placement of flags a continuing jurisdictional controversy.

### 3.6 Re-establishment as an official flag

The pre-autonomous **Basque General Council** adopted the Ikurrina on **18 December 1978**, with publication on 15 January 1979. Article 5.1 of Organic Law 3/1979 then provided:

> “La bandera del País Vasco es la bicrucífera, compuesta de aspa verde, cruz blanca superpuesta y fondo rojo.”

This constitutionalized the design within the Autonomous Community of the Basque Country. Article 5.2 separately recognized the flags of its historical territories.

Under Spain’s Law 39/1981, it is flown with the Spanish flag on public civil buildings in Euskadi. Consequently, state and autonomous-community law regulate display order and institutional custody, but neither law makes any individual piece of cloth the uniquely authentic flag.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Is there one authentic object?

No. The official object is principally a **design fixed in law**, instantiated in replaceable cloth, printed, painted, and digital forms. Authenticity means conformity to the design and legal context, not descent from the 1894 textile.

A specimen may nevertheless be authentic as:

- the actual cloth raised at a documented event;
- a government-issued flag;
- a military unit’s standard;
- a clandestine flag seized in a particular operation;
- a replica made to historic proportions.

Such authenticity must be demonstrated separately by provenance.

### 4.2 The lost first flag

The secure provenance of the first cloth ends with its 1895 confiscation. No museum publicly claims it. The story of its later use as a protective sheet by court painters is plausible as a tale of institutional disregard but is not established by a published inventory, photograph, witness deposition, or surviving fragment.

Therefore:

- **existence in 1894–95:** securely documented;
- **confiscation:** documented;
- **presence at the Audiencia in 1932:** reported, not securely documented;
- **use by painters:** anecdotal;
- **survival today:** unsupported;
- **identity of any present candidate:** none known.

### 4.3 Castejón banner dispute

The red-saltire-on-white Castejón banner is a real precursor in the documentary tradition but not compositionally the same flag. Luis Arana expressly rejected the claim that it was the first Ikurrina. Calling it such collapses two distinct objects and two stages of design development.

### 4.4 Authorship dispute

The surviving evidence supports joint credit to Luis and Sabino Arana, but division of labor is unresolved. Institutional formulas that call Sabino sole “creator” often reflect his prominence as founder and ideologue. Attributing the drawn design solely to Luis likewise goes beyond the cautious “probably Luis” assigned to the surviving sketch.

### 4.5 “Ancient Basque flag” claims

There is no credible evidence that the Ikurrina existed in antiquity, the Middle Ages, the early-modern foral period, or the Carlist Wars. Claims that it was an immemorial national standard are **modern nationalist retrojection**. Eusko Ikaskuntza’s 1931 inquiry explicitly recognized the absence of a historical single flag for the whole Basque Country.

The flag’s green saltire may invoke Saint Andrew, the Gernika oak, customary law, or the legendary battle of Arrigorriaga, but symbolic reference does not establish material descent from a medieval banner.

### 4.6 Union Jack derivation

The visual resemblance to Britain’s Union Flag has prompted a persistent theory that the Aranas copied it, sometimes because Biscay’s maritime world had strong British connections. The combination of saltire and upright cross makes comparison natural. However, the designers’ contemporary explanation derives the components from Biscay’s arms and the JEL motto. No documentary evidence presently demonstrates direct copying. British influence is a **plausible visual hypothesis**, not an established manufacturing history.

### 4.7 Scientific examination

No published radiocarbon dating, textile microscopy, dye analysis, or forensic examination of the lost first flag is possible unless a candidate emerges. No comprehensive scientific report on the surviving original sketch has been identified in accessible sources. Authentication authority has therefore moved through documentary rather than laboratory channels:

1. the Arana brothers and Euskeldun Batzokija;
2. nationalist party institutions;
3. Eusko Ikaskuntza’s social and historical assessment;
4. the Basque Government’s 1936 decree;
5. the Basque General Council and 1979 Statute;
6. present archives and museums authenticating particular specimens.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Political inauguration and oath-like function

The first raising inaugurated Euskeldun Batzokija and visually announced its political creed. The following day’s Mass and election of officers integrated flag, Catholic worship, association, and political organization. The ceremony gave the cloth a quasi-liturgical setting without converting it into a church relic.

### 6.2 Party and national ceremonies

The Ikurrina became central to:

- Batzoki openings;
- Aberri Eguna observances;
- nationalist funerals and commemorations;
- party congresses and rallies;
- exile-government ceremonies;
- municipal inaugurations;
- demonstrations for autonomy, independence, amnesty, and language rights.

Formal salutes, flag dances, and public raisings gave individual cloths temporary ritual status. These practices vary by organization; no universal Basque flag rite governs all uses.

### 6.3 Military function

During the Civil War it served as:

- the Basque Government’s territorial flag;
- a unit and battalion emblem;
- a naval and maritime identifier;
- a marker on official premises and vehicles;
- a legitimating symbol for an autonomous government operating within the Spanish Republic.

Possession did not legally confer sovereignty, but raising it asserted the government’s authority and the existence of Euskadi as a political subject.

### 6.4 Prohibition as symbolic amplification

Under dictatorship, the act of sewing, possessing, painting, raising, or removing an Ikurrina acquired political force precisely because it was prohibited. A quickly replaced clandestine flag could compel the state to dispatch police or technical personnel. The cloth thus operated as a small material device capable of claiming public space, provoking official action, and producing photographs or oral histories after its removal.

### 6.5 Dance and homage

The **Ikurrin Dantza**, performed in political and cultural ceremonies, waves the flag above dancers or assembled participants. Court records concerning events connected with radical Basque nationalism show that the choreography could be read either as cultural homage or political theater, depending on context. The ritual is documented practice, but claims that it is an ancient, unchanged ceremony should be treated cautiously.

### 6.6 Sport

The 1976 Atotxa display redefined the flag for a mass audience. Two rival clubs’ captains jointly held one cloth, framing it as collective rather than partisan. Since then the design has been normalized in stadiums, club commemorations, cycling, pelota, rowing, and international sport.

### 6.7 Mountaineering

Clandestine placement on peaks made the flag visible at locations difficult for authorities to police. Later summit flags became celebratory rather than clandestine. When Martín Zabaleta became the first Basque to reach Everest’s summit in 1980, the Ikurrina associated the expedition with Basque collective achievement. Such summit specimens are distinct objects whose authenticity depends on expedition photographs and custody records.

### 6.8 Access and handling rules

There is no sacred taboo restricting who may touch the Ikurrina. In official use it is governed by flag protocol and by Spanish rules requiring the Spanish flag’s presence and precedence on public buildings. Party, military, or museum specimens may have conservation restrictions, but these arise from institutional custody rather than religious prohibition.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The medieval-continuity legend

**Story:** the Ikurrina is the ancient or immemorial flag of the Basque people.

**Assessment:** modern invention or retrospective simplification. The documented design dates to 1894. Earlier Basque territories possessed arms, seals, standards, maritime ensigns, and royal or provincial banners, but no identical red-green-white flag represented all Basques.

**Function:** ancient continuity strengthens national legitimacy and detaches the emblem from its PNV and Biscayan origin.

### 7.2 The Arrigorriaga legend

The green Saint Andrew’s saltire is sometimes tied to the legendary battle of Arrigorriaga, in which the Basques under Jaun Zuria supposedly defeated an invading force on Saint Andrew’s Day. The battle narrative itself is legendary and developed in medieval and early-modern historiography. What is documented is that Aranist symbolism drew upon such Biscayan foundational traditions. The flag is not evidence that the battle occurred.

### 7.3 The “Basque blood” interpretation

The red field is frequently said to symbolize blood shed by the Basque people. This is a later popular elaboration. The designers’ own explanation linked the red field to Biscay or its heraldry. Later symbolism is historically important because it shaped reception, but it is not original provenance.

### 7.4 The first flag as court dust sheet

**Story:** after confiscation, the 1894 flag survived in the Bilbao Audiencia until at least 1932 and painters used it to cover furniture.

**Tier:** late anecdotal tradition.

**Evidence for:** repeated by writers close to nationalist archival circles; institutional disdain for confiscated political material makes it conceivable.

**Evidence against or missing:** no cited accession, property register, photograph, court memorandum, fragment, or named eyewitness has been published in the sources examined.

**Effect:** the story turns the lost original into a narrative of desecration and endurance. It should not be stated as fact.

### 7.5 The accidental royal salute

**Story:** Alfonso XIII unknowingly saluted the Ikurrina at the Begoña Batzoki in 1908.

**Tier:** institutional anecdote.

**Meaning:** the episode humorously reverses political hierarchy by having the Spanish king honor a separatist emblem. Even if the physical gesture occurred, interpreting it as recognition would be false; the story depends on mistake.

### 7.6 The Atotxa smuggling story

The Atotxa flag is said to have been hidden in a vehicle or exhaust assembly before entering the stadium. Multiple retellings agree that its entry was clandestine and participant-organized, but differ in detail. The photographed joint carrying is fact; the exact smuggling method is a colorful subsidiary tradition.

### 7.7 The flag as sole authentic Basque symbol

Some nationalist traditions present the Ikurrina as the natural flag of all seven territories. Competing Basque and Navarrese positions instead favor the Navarrese chains, Arrano Beltza, provincial flags, or combinations of them. The dispute is not about textile authenticity but about representational jurisdiction: who is entitled to speak for “the Basque Country,” and whether a design created for Biscay and adopted officially by a three-province autonomous community can stand for Navarre and Iparralde.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 The design

As of September 2026, the design remains the official flag of the **Autonomous Community of the Basque Country** under unchanged Article 5.1 of Organic Law 3/1979. It is displayed by the Basque Parliament, Basque Government, provincial and municipal authorities, and other public institutions subject to the Spanish statutory framework.

The Basque Government controls its official graphic specifications but does not own the geometric design as a unique physical chattel.

### 8.2 The first cloth

**Status:** lost.

**Last securely documented location:** in the custody of authorities following the 12 September 1895 closure of Euskeldun Batzokija in Bilbao.

**Current owner or controller:** unknown.

**Condition:** unknown; presumed destroyed, discarded, or unrecognized.

**Serious recovery hypothesis:** it might once have remained among court or police effects, but no current archive or museum identifies a candidate. The painters’ story is insufficient to infer survival.

### 8.3 The sketch and museum objects

The Sabino Arana Foundation reports that its archive preserves the original design sketch, probably by Luis Arana. The Museum of Basque Nationalism, in Sabin Etxea, Bilbao, preserves and displays later objects including historic flag accessories and named ceremonial specimens. Its collections are institutionally controlled and publicly accessible through exhibitions and online presentations, subject to conservation requirements.

A complete publicly searchable object-by-object catalogue containing dimensions, fiber identifications, treatments, provenance documentation, and accession numbers for every historic Ikurrina has not been identified. Absence from the public web does not prove the records do not exist internally.

### 8.4 Navarre

The Ikurrina is not the official flag of the Chartered Community of Navarre. Navarre’s official flag bears its historic chains emblem.

Navarre’s Foral Law 24/2003 regulated public symbols in a manner used to challenge or prevent Ikurrina display by municipalities. Foral Law 3/2017 repealed it, expressly invoking tolerance toward symbols accepted by different local entities. Repeal did not make the Ikurrina an official Navarrese flag. Placement on town halls remains politically contested and may be reviewed under general principles governing institutional neutrality, official flags, and municipal competence.

### 8.5 French Basque Country

In Lapurdi, Lower Navarre, and Zuberoa, the Ikurrina has no status as a national or regional flag under French constitutional law. It nevertheless appears widely on town halls, cultural institutions, businesses, sports facilities, festivals, and private buildings. There it functions chiefly as a Basque cultural and territorial emblem. Its normalization in Iparralde is part of the design’s twentieth-century expansion beyond both Biscay and the Spanish autonomous community.

### 8.6 Repatriation and ownership disputes

There is no single repatriation dispute because there is no unique extant object. Possible issues concern individual flags taken as wartime trophies, police evidence, exile property, or donated artifacts. Such cases require specimen-level provenance. The broad political dispute concerns symbolic ownership: Euskadi’s legal title to an official flag versus claims that it belongs culturally to all Euskal Herria or, conversely, that it improperly projects an autonomous-community or nationalist symbol into Navarre and France.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Mass reproduction

The Ikurrina is reproduced on:

- public buildings and official documents;
- clothing, scarves, jewelry, stickers, and domestic textiles;
- sports merchandise;
- festival decorations;
- political posters and ballots;
- murals, street art, memorials, and gravestones;
- websites, emoji-like graphics, and social-media profiles;
- composite diaspora emblems.

Most such reproductions are symbolic images rather than flags made to statutory dimensions.

### 9.2 Cinema and art

The 1924 film *Edurne, modista bilbaína* reportedly included an Ikurrina, drawing governmental objection under Primo de Rivera. Its appearance makes it an early cinematic embodiment of the flag’s conflict with state censorship.

During the Civil War, the Ikurrina appeared in posters, illustrations, photographs, military iconography, and representations of *gudaris*. Antonio de Guezala and other artists incorporated it into allegories of a Basque republic or homeland.

Later artistic use frequently detached the design from strict flag geometry, allowing fragments of its crosses or colors to signify Euskadi without displaying a literal banner.

### 9.3 Football and collective memory

The Atotxa episode has been repeatedly photographed, exhibited, reenacted, and commemorated by Athletic Club, Real Sociedad, museums, and the press. Its historical importance does not arise from the cloth’s age or workmanship but from the convergence of:

- illegality;
- mass spectatorship;
- cooperation between rivals;
- the transition from dictatorship;
- the event’s proximity to public authorization in January 1977.

The specimen thereby became a relic of democratization in club and popular memory.

### 9.4 Diaspora

In Argentina, Uruguay, Mexico, Venezuela, Cuba, the United States, Australia, and elsewhere, the flag migrated through print and newly made textiles rather than movement of the first specimen. Diaspora use helped transform it from a Biscayan party flag into a general Basque emblem.

Buenos Aires’s Laurak Bat raising in 1921 is especially important. In Boise, Idaho, the Basque Center began flying an Ikurrina outside its building in 1972, amid renewed political awareness connected with events in Spain. Today it is prominent at Jaialdi and other diaspora festivals.

The unofficial flag of Saint-Pierre-et-Miquelon incorporates a small Ikurrina-derived panel alongside Breton and Norman components to represent settler heritage. This is derivative heraldic reception, not evidence that the islands fall within the Basque political nation.

### 9.5 Nationalist and non-nationalist appropriation

The PNV remains closely identified with the flag’s invention and early spread, but the emblem passed beyond PNV control before 1936. Socialists in the coalition government helped formalize it; left-nationalist organizations adopted it; non-nationalist Basque institutions came to use it as the statutory flag.

This diffusion explains the flag’s dual reception:

- for many, it is a civic emblem of Euskadi regardless of constitutional preference;
- for others, especially in Navarre or strongly Spanish-unionist settings, it remains an ideological nationalist sign;
- in Iparralde and diaspora communities, it may operate as a cultural marker with little immediate implication about constitutional change.

### 9.6 Commercialization

The design appears extensively on souvenirs, food packaging, restaurant signs, tourist goods, and sporting merchandise. No single commercial manufacturer controls it. Commercial familiarity has helped secularize a symbol whose creators assigned explicitly Catholic and political meanings.

### 9.7 Contemporary controversy

Current controversies include:

- whether municipal buildings outside Euskadi should display it;
- whether calling it the flag of “the Basque Country” wrongly conflates Euskal Herria with the Autonomous Community;
- whether its Aranist and PNV origin limits its inclusiveness;
- whether later civic acceptance has emancipated it from that origin;
- whether official graphic reproductions must follow the narrow 14:25 proportions;
- whether alternative Basque symbols, especially the Navarrese flag or Arrano Beltza, have stronger historical claims.

These are disputes over representation, not over the design’s date or documented creators.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Primary evidence

The central primary sources are:

- the surviving Arana design sketch;
- the Euskeldun Batzokija’s organizational records;
- contemporary nationalist newspapers, especially *Bizkaitarra*;
- Sabino Arana’s “La bandera fenicia” of 28 July 1895;
- police, court, and administrative papers concerning the Batzokija’s closure and confiscation;
- early photographs of Batzokis, ships, rallies, and diaspora associations;
- municipal correspondence with Eusko Ikaskuntza in 1931;
- the Basque Government’s decrees of 19 and 24 October 1936;
- military and naval photographs and surviving flags from 1936–37;
- Franco-era police and court files;
- photographs and participant testimony concerning Atotxa in 1976;
- the 1978 Basque General Council agreement;
- Article 5 of the 1979 Statute.

The documentary record is strongest for the design’s legal and ceremonial career and weakest for the material fate of the first cloth.

### 10.2 Major research tendencies

Three broad historiographical approaches can be distinguished:

1. **Nationalist institutional history:** emphasizes the Arana brothers, the first raising, suppression, clandestine survival, and eventual national acceptance. The Sabino Arana Foundation and Museum of Basque Nationalism preserve indispensable objects and records, but their institutional relationship to PNV history should be visible to readers.

2. **Academic nationalism and symbol studies:** examines how a partisan, Biscayan emblem became a collective national symbol, paying attention to ritual, mass politics, diaspora, and official adoption.

3. **Critical and competing-national-symbol historiography:** stresses the flag’s recent invention, the absence of an ancient pan-Basque flag, its original confessional and Biscayan meanings, and competition from Navarrese or other emblems. This corrective is valuable when documentary; it becomes misleading when “recent” is used to imply that the flag’s later social adoption is unreal.

### 10.3 Óscar Álvarez Gila’s contribution

Álvarez Gila’s work on flags before the Ikurrina materially changed the frame of inquiry. It showed that nineteenth-century Basque diaspora communities were already experimenting with common flags and that the Ikurrina’s eventual dominance was a historical selection among alternatives, not the automatic emergence of an ancestral emblem.

### 10.4 Eusko Ikaskuntza’s 1931 intervention

The society’s position is frequently quoted selectively. Its importance lies in holding together two propositions:

- no single historic flag had represented the entire Basque Country;
- by 1931, the Ikurrina had gained sufficient collective recognition to serve that purpose.

The first proposition refutes ancient-continuity mythology; the second explains why the 1936 government could adopt a flag born in one party and one province.

### 10.5 Discredited or unsupported theories

The following should not be repeated as facts:

- that the Ikurrina is medieval or ancient;
- that it was the historical flag of the Kingdom of Navarre;
- that the Castejón banner was physically identical to the 1894 flag;
- that the first cloth is preserved in a named museum;
- that red originally meant Basque blood;
- that direct copying of the Union Jack has been proved;
- that all Basques immediately accepted the design in 1894;
- that 1977 legalization made it the official flag of Navarre or the French Basque Country.

### 10.6 Research gaps

A fuller material history requires:

- publication of the original sketch’s catalogue record, paper analysis, measurements, and provenance;
- identification of any invoice or business papers of Gumersindo Bilbao Goyaga;
- police and court inventories from the 1895 Batzokija seizure;
- systematic review of the Bilbao Audiencia’s property and maintenance records around 1932;
- textile and dye studies of securely provenanced early specimens;
- a union catalogue of Civil War, exile, clandestine, municipal, maritime, and diaspora Ikurrinas;
- precise provenance for the Atotxa specimen;
- separation in museum language between “first flag at an event” and “first Ikurrina.”

## 12. Sources

### Primary and official sources

- **Sabino Arana, “La bandera fenicia,” originally published in *Bizkaitarra*, no. 31, 28 July 1895.** Digitized edition reproducing the dialogue and its statement that the flag was first flown by the nationalist circle on 14 July 1894.
- **Government of Euskadi, Decree of 19 October 1936**, *Diario Oficial del País Vasco*, no. 13, 21 October 1936, p. 100. Primary adoption decree.
- **Government of Euskadi, corrective Decree of 24 October 1936**, published 28 October 1936. Primary source for final dimensions.
- **Organic Law 3/1979, Statute of Autonomy for the Basque Country**, Article 5. Current foundational law.
- **Law 39/1981 on the use of the Spanish flag and other flags and ensigns.** Governs joint official display.
- **Navarre Foral Law 24/2003 on Symbols.** Primary evidence for the former regulatory regime.
- **Navarre Foral Law 3/2017.** Primary repeal of the 2003 law.
- **Basque Government corporate identity manual.** Current specifications for proportions and standardized colors.
- **Guardia Civil, *50 años, 50 historias: 1973–2023*.** Official account of explosive-disposal history, including Miguel Gordo García’s death.

### Archives, museums, and documentary collections

- **Sabino Arana Foundation:** institutional history of the first raising, the lost first flag, and the original sketch.
- **Museum of Basque Nationalism:** collection pages for historically specific later flags and Sabin Etxea artifacts.
- **PARES—Portal de Archivos Españoles:** catalogue material concerning Eusko Ikaskuntza’s 1931–32 meetings.
- **Bizkaia digital press and library repositories:** scans of the 1936 official journal and period newspapers.
- **Athletic Club and Real Sociedad foundations:** documentary and commemorative material on the 5 December 1976 Atotxa flag.

### Scholarship

- Óscar Álvarez Gila, **“Antes de la ikurriña: la primera bandera vasca en América (1880–1900)”**, on the diaspora precursors and competing collective flags.
- Óscar Álvarez Gila, ** *Antes de la ikurriña. Banderas, símbolos e identidad vasca en América (1880–1935)* **, on the broader migration and adoption history.
- C. Rubio Pobes, **“La primera bandera de Euskal-Erria,”** on earlier Basque collective symbolism and the historical distinctiveness of the Ikurrina.
- Research on the 1936 Statute and Basque Government, particularly the observation that the Statute itself omitted the flag and that official adoption followed by executive decree.
- Luis de Guezala’s archival and public-history work for the Sabino Arana Foundation, especially on the first cloth’s confiscation and the design’s diffusion.
