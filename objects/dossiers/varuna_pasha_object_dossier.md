# Varuna's pasha (noose): Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `varunas_pasha_noose_object_dossier.md`  
**Research current to:** 6 September 2026

## Evidentiary finding

Varuṇa’s pāśa is not a surviving physical artifact with a continuous material provenance. No museum, temple, treasury, excavation report, inscription, or scientific examination identifies a tangible rope or weapon as *the* original noose of Varuṇa. Its reconstructable history is therefore a history of texts, ritual concepts, narrative transfers, and images.

Moreover, the sources do not always describe one numerically singular thing. Three overlapping forms must be distinguished:

1. **The Vedic pāśas of Varuṇa:** usually plural “snares,” “bonds,” or “fetters” through which Varuṇa detects, confines, or afflicts transgressors and from which he can release penitents.
2. **The epic Varuṇapāśa or pāśa vāruṇa:** a summonable and withdrawable divine weapon, capable of capturing enemies and transmissible through instruction in mantras and procedures.
3. **The iconographic pāśa:** a looped cord, rope, or serpent-like attribute held by Varuṇa in sculpture and painting, especially after he became standardized as guardian of the western direction and lord of waters.

The texts encourage readers to perceive continuity among these forms, but they do not establish that every occurrence denotes one immortal, materially identical cord. Any “chain of custody” must consequently be reconstructed separately for each textual cycle.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Names

The principal Sanskrit terms are:

- **वरुणपाश — Varuṇapāśa:** “Varuṇa’s noose,” a genitival compound.
- **पाशं वारुणम् — pāśaṃ vāruṇam:** “the noose belonging to Varuṇa.”
- **वरुणस्य पाशाः — varuṇasya pāśāḥ:** “Varuṇa’s nooses/snares,” normally plural in Vedic contexts.
- **वारुणपाश — Vāruṇapāśa:** an adjectival form, “the Varuṇian noose.”
- **Nāgapāśa:** “serpent-noose.” This can overlap iconographically with Varuṇa’s weapon, but it is not consistently identical to it.
- Varuṇa’s epithets include **Pāśabhṛt**, “noose-bearer,” **Pāśin**, “provided with a noose,” and **Pāśavān**, “possessing nooses.” Nineteenth-century mythographic literature additionally records the names *Pulakāṅga* and *Viśvajit* for his noose, but these names are poorly anchored in the early sources and should not be projected into the Veda. [Dowson’s *Classical Dictionary*](https://rarebooksocietyofindia.org/book_archive/196174216674_10156901773376675.pdf)

**Pāśa** means a snare, noose, tether, cord, bond, or fetter. It is an ordinary Sanskrit word as well as a technical religious and iconographic term. A conventional etymology relates it to binding or fastening. The etymology of **Varuṇa** itself remains disputed. A historically influential proposal connected the name to a root meaning “cover, encompass, restrain, or bind,” while another line of interpretation linked Varuṇa especially with the oath. Rolf Hiersche, building on Heinrich Lüders, argued for an oath-centered etymology and adduced Varuṇa’s enclosing and binding functions; this remains a scholarly proposal, not a settled linguistic fact. [Hiersche, “Note additionnelle relative à l’étymologie…”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/reg_0035-2039_1958_num_71_334_3532)

### 1.2 What the sources say it looks like

No early source supplies dimensions, weight, fiber, color, maker’s mark, inscription, or construction technique.

The earliest Vedic evidence is conceptually spatial rather than materially descriptive:

- *Ṛgveda* 1.24 speaks of Varuṇa’s bonds in upper, middle, and lower positions.
- *Atharvaveda* 4.16 describes nooses “seven by seven,” set out threefold, and then “a hundred fetters.” They bind the speaker of falsehood but release the truth-speaker. The hymn’s numbers convey pervasive and inescapable constraint; they do not constitute a practical inventory of ropes. [Whitney’s annotated *Atharvaveda* 4.16](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Atharva-Veda_Samhita/Book_IV/Hymn_16)
- Epic sources make the noose an **astra**, a supernatural weapon activated and recalled through learned procedure. The *Mahābhārata* uses the plural: Varuṇa says his “nooses” are irresistible and gives Arjuna his “Varuṇa weapons.”
- Iconographic texts and artworks reduce the plurality to a readily recognizable hand-held loop, sometimes represented as a cord and sometimes as a serpent.

### 1.3 Material representations, not relics

Several real objects depict the noose, but none claims to be the weapon itself.

- The Metropolitan Museum of Art preserves **Varuna Holding a Noose and Riding a Swan**, object **19.90**, an Indian post-Gupta sandstone relief dated to the sixth–eighth century, measuring 31.8 × 27.9 × 7.6 cm. Acquired through the Rogers Fund in 1919, it is not presently on view. This is a depiction, not a candidate relic. [Metropolitan Museum catalogue](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/37533)
- British Museum **1994,1110,0.12**, a circa-1814 Thanjavur Company School gouache on watermarked paper, depicts Varuṇa astride a makara, holding a nāga in one hand and a pāśa in the other. Page size is 25.1 × 19.2 cm. Purchased from Henry Brownrigg in 1994, it is not on display. [British Museum catalogue](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_1994-1110-0-12)
- British Museum **1992,0410,0.1.47**, a circa-1830 Tiruchirappalli album painting, shows Varuṇa holding what looks like a snake. The curator observes that the artist may have converted or mistaken the characteristic noose for a snake—a valuable demonstration of the iconographic permeability between pāśa and nāga. [British Museum catalogue](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_1992-0410-0-1-47)
- British Museum **1993,0806,0.74**, circa 1850, substitutes an axe for the expected noose, showing that even relatively standardized iconography admitted local or workshop variation. [British Museum catalogue](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_1993-0806-0-74)

No surviving representation establishes the material from which a mythic original was supposedly made.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Earliest reconstructable textual layer

The earliest attested ancestor of the legendary weapon is the complex of Varuṇa’s bonds in the *Ṛgveda*, composed and orally transmitted in Vedic Sanskrit during the second millennium BCE. Exact dates remain debated, and the surviving manuscripts are much later than the compositions. The oldest evidence is therefore oral-textual, not an excavated inscription or contemporary manuscript.

In *Ṛgveda* 1.24.11–15, Śunaḥśepa, already physically bound to three posts, appeals to Varuṇa for release. Verse 13 asks the king to “loosen the nooses” (*vi mumoktu pāśān*); verse 15 distinguishes an upper, middle, and lower noose:

> **ud uttamaṃ varuṇa pāśam asmad avādhamaṃ vi madhyamaṃ śrathāya**  
> “Loosen from us the uppermost noose, remove the lowest, and release the middle one.”

The hymn closes by asking to remain sinless within Varuṇa’s ordinance. The textual page preserves the Sanskrit and Griffith translation, though modern philology does not accept every detail of Griffith’s Victorian rendering. [*Ṛgveda* 1.24, Sanskrit and translation](https://rigveda-online.github.io/1/24.html)

What this earliest evidence establishes:

- Varuṇa possesses or controls multiple binding powers called *pāśa*.
- They are associated with guilt, divine ordinance, supplication, and release.
- The hymn deliberately overlays literal sacrificial binding and divine-moral bondage.
- Varuṇa can loosen as well as impose the bond.

What it does **not** establish:

- a named singular artifact *Varuṇapāśa*;
- a manufacturer;
- a creation story;
- its material;
- an original transfer from another god;
- a water-producing function;
- a physical storage place.

### 2.2 Atharvavedic development

*Atharvaveda* 4.16 makes the nooses explicit instruments of moral surveillance. Varuṇa’s spies watch the world; his fetters are “seven by seven” and threefold. They are instructed to bind the person who speaks falsely and release the truth-speaker. The next verse asks Varuṇa to bridle the liar with a hundred fetters and describes the offender’s belly collapsing or distending like an unbound vessel—an allusion understood by the commentary tradition as **jalodara**, dropsy or abdominal swelling.

Whitney’s edition notes that the hymn survives with variants in the Paippalāda recension and was used by the *Kauśika Sūtra* in a hostile rite against a person who comes cursing. Thus the “object” passed from poetic theology into an operative ritual formula. [Whitney, *Atharvaveda* 4.16](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Atharva-Veda_Samhita/Book_IV/Hymn_16); [Bloomfield’s translation of the binding verses](https://www.sacred-texts.com/hin/sbe42/av094.htm)

A related Vedic complex associates Varuṇa’s bond with illness, especially dropsy, and with release obtained through confession, correct ritual, or divine favor. The Śunaḥśepa narrative in *Aitareya Brāhmaṇa* 7.13–18 expands the relation among a broken promise, a threatened human sacrifice, Varuṇa’s punishment, and release. Modern comparative scholarship stresses that dropsy is not simply “water magic”; within these sources it can be the somatic manifestation of violated obligation. [Aslan Cohen Mizrahi dissertation discussion](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/record/12798/files/Aslan%20Cohen%20Mizrahi%20PhD%20Dissertation.pdf)

### 2.3 No documented manufacture

The Vedic texts do not say that a divine smith forged or braided the nooses. No quarry, loom, forge, or celestial craftsman can be identified.

The *Bālakāṇḍa* of the *Rāmāyaṇa* embeds weapons generally in a genealogy of personified astras: Jaya and Suprabhā, daughters of Dakṣa and wives of Kṛśāśva, produce classes of radiant weapon-sons. Viśvāmitra knows them and can transmit them. This genealogy gives the weapon corpus a mythic family structure, but it does not plainly identify an individually born son as the material manufacture of Varuṇa’s pāśa. It is best classified as a **later epic genealogy of weapon-beings**, not the noose’s securely attested Vedic origin. [*Rāmāyaṇa*, Bālakāṇḍa overview and weapon genealogy](https://www.bmscw.edu.in/files/StudyMaterials/BBA/I-BBA/balakanda.pdf)

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because the noose is legendary and frequently plural, the following is a **source-by-source narrative custody chain**, not documentary provenance for a material artifact.

### 3.1 Vedic cycle: Varuṇa retains control

**Second millennium BCE, composition layer of the *Ṛgveda*.**  
Custodian: **Varuṇa**.  
Location: cosmic and ritual space, not a named repository.  
Action: his nooses constrain; worshippers ask him to loosen them. In the Śunaḥśepa hymn the human victim is tied to three posts, but the text distinguishes or overlays those physical bonds with Varuṇa’s supernatural bonds. No transfer occurs.

**Later Vedic period, *Atharvaveda*.**  
Custodian/operator: **Varuṇa**, invoked by a ritual practitioner.  
Action: the nooses are directed against a liar or enemy. The ritual voice can petition Varuṇa to deploy them but does not acquire title to them.

**Brāhmaṇa and Śrauta ritual literature.**  
The “noose of Varuṇa” becomes a recurrent interpretation of binding, ritual danger, edema, and release. It may be represented by an actual girdle, tether, or binding cord within a rite, but those perishable ritual implements are tokens of the divine bond, not authenticated fragments of one original celestial rope. The Government of India’s Vedic Heritage Portal still summarizes the Varuṇapraghāsa rite as directed toward release from Varuṇa’s noose and disorders including *jalodara*. [Vedic Heritage Portal ritual overview](https://vedicheritage.gov.in/hi/rituals/)

### 3.2 *Rāmāyaṇa* weapon cycle

The received *Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa* developed over centuries, conventionally placed broadly between the late first millennium BCE and early first millennium CE. Numbering differs among recensions and editions.

**Earlier narrative time: Viśvāmitra possesses the weapon.**  
The epic recounts Viśvāmitra’s weapon contest with Vasiṣṭha. Viśvāmitra discharges a series including the Vāruṇa weapon, but Vasiṣṭha’s Brahma-staff absorbs or neutralizes the astras. In the same catalogue appear the Brahma- and Kāla-nooses. This episode does not clearly say that the Vāruṇa weapon was destroyed; supernatural weapons in epic narrative are normally repeatable through retained knowledge. [*Rāmāyaṇa* 1.55](https://rikamrit.com/vidyakendra/resources/valmiki-ramayana/reader.php?chapter=1.55)

**Transfer: Viśvāmitra → Rāma.**  
At *Bālakāṇḍa* 1.27.8, Viśvāmitra says:

> **dharmapāśam ahaṃ rāma kālapāśaṃ tathaiva ca /  
> pāśaṃ vāruṇam astraṃ ca dadāmy aham anuttamam //**

“I give you, Rāma, the noose of Dharma and likewise the noose of Time/Death; I give you the excellent noose and weapon of Varuṇa.” [Sanskrit text and translation](https://srivalmikiramayana.com/en/1/27/8/)

Mechanism: **initiatory gift/instruction**, not handover of an ordinary rope. Rāma performs purification and receives the weapons; personified astras present themselves and promise obedience. He then asks to learn their *saṃhāra*, the means of withdrawal or neutralization. [*Rāmāyaṇa* 1.28.2](https://srivalmikiramayana.com/en/1/28/2/)

Later custody: **Rāma retains knowledge of the weapon.** The surviving narrative does not provide a final return, burial, destruction, inheritance, or transfer to Rāma’s descendants. Claims that a physical noose passed into an earthly royal treasury cannot be established.

### 3.3 *Mahābhārata* cycle

The *Mahābhārata* likewise accumulated over centuries, with substantial composition and redaction commonly placed between the late first millennium BCE and early first millennium CE. The narrative contains more than one appearance of Varuṇa’s noose.

**Tārakāmaya battle, mythic prehistory.**  
Custodian and wielder: **Varuṇa**.  
Action: according to Varuṇa’s later statement to Arjuna, he used the nooses to seize and bind thousands of Daityas during the conflict concerning Tārā. This is retrospective legendary provenance supplied by Varuṇa himself within the epic.

**Burning of the Khāṇḍava forest.**  
Custodian/wielder: **Varuṇa**.  
When the gods attempt to stop Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna, Varuṇa takes up “his noose and beautiful missile.” This is deployment against the heroes, not a transfer. [*Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parvan, section 230 in Ganguli’s numbering](https://sacred-texts.com/hin/m01/m01230.htm)

**Himalayan transfer: Varuṇa → Arjuna.**  
After Arjuna receives the Pāśupata weapon from Śiva and Yama’s mace, Varuṇa appears from the western summit. He tells Arjuna that his nooses are irresistible, that they bound thousands of Daityas in the Tārakāmaya war, and gives Arjuna the Varuṇa weapons together with “the mysteries of hurling and withdrawing them.” [*Mahābhārata*, Vana Parvan, section 41](https://sacred-texts.com/hin/m03/m03041.htm)

This link is unusually explicit:

- **Donor:** Varuṇa.
- **Recipient:** Arjuna.
- **Place:** a Himalayan summit, with Varuṇa appearing from the west.
- **Mechanism:** divine gift and technical initiation.
- **Object form:** plural nooses or “Varuṇa weapons.”
- **Operational knowledge:** both deployment and recall.
- **Prior history:** use in the Tārakāmaya battle.
- **Claimed power:** even Yama/Death could not escape them.

The text gives no wrapping, container, visible material, or transport procedure. “Custody” is principally command of the mantra-weapon.

**Later use and fate.**  
The epic repeatedly mentions Vāruṇa weapons, often water-producing or defensive astras, but not every Vāruṇāstra is necessarily the binding noose. Arjuna’s use of a Vāruṇa weapon against the Nārāyaṇa weapon, for example, should not automatically be catalogued as use of the pasha: the passage names the Vāruṇāstra, not necessarily Varuṇa’s binding cords. No securely identified passage reports Arjuna losing, returning, or transmitting this particular noose. Its narrative chain therefore ends with Arjuna’s acquired command.

### 3.4 Purāṇic circulation

Later Purāṇic and affiliated narratives treat “Varuṇa’s nooses” as divine equipment that other gods may use with Varuṇa’s authority.

- In the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa*’s Vāmana narrative, **Garuḍa**, acting on Viṣṇu’s intention, binds King **Bali** with Varuṇa’s cords. Bali remains composed and offers his head for Viṣṇu’s third step. The episode restores the old association between the noose, truth, promises, and sovereign punishment: Bali is bound during adjudication of his three-step gift. [*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 8.21](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/the-bhagavata-purana/d/doc1128835.html)
- In *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 10.50, **Balarāma** begins binding **Jarāsandha** with Vāruṇa and ordinary human ropes; Kṛṣṇa stops him because Jarāsandha still has a role to play. The distinction between divine and human ropes is explicit. [*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 10.50](https://shlokam.org/bhagavatam/canto-10-chapter-50.htm)
- In the *Harivaṃśa*, Varuṇa fights demons with strokes of nooses while Soma uses cold and dew. The cords operate as battlefield restraints alongside Varuṇa’s water power. [*Harivaṃśa*, “Battle of the Gods”](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/harivamsha-purana-dutt/d/doc485526.html)
- A later *Devī Bhāgavata* battle passage has **Vāruṇī** bind Daityas with her noose and cast them unconscious. This is the weapon of a Varuṇa-associated goddess, not demonstrably the same numerically singular noose given to Rāma or Arjuna. [*Devī Bhāgavata* 5.28.58](https://vedapath.app/en/devi-bhagavatam/skandha-5/28/58)

The Purāṇic pattern is better described as **authorized divine use of the category “Varuṇa’s cords”** than as physical loans documented from one hand to another.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Does the original object exist?

No physical original is documented. There is:

- no accessioned claimant;
- no shrine publicly claiming custody of the Vedic or epic noose;
- no chain of inventories;
- no relic inscription;
- no scientific dating or fiber analysis;
- no reported excavation;
- no authenticated fragment;
- no serious scholarly proposal identifying a surviving rope as the epic weapon.

Accordingly, the question “Is the object now bearing the name the same object described in the Veda?” presently has no material candidate to test.

### 4.2 Singular versus plural identity

The earliest sources favor plurality: *pāśān*, “nooses,” or innumerable cords distributed through the cosmos. The singular, portable “Varuṇa-noose” becomes clearer in epic weapon lists and iconography. Treating all these as one object is a **theological or narrative synthesis**, not a demonstrated textual fact.

### 4.3 Pāśa versus Vāruṇāstra

Modern summaries frequently collapse the noose into **Varuṇāstra**, imagined principally as a water missile that floods a battlefield and counters an Agneyāstra. The Sanskrit evidence requires more care:

- *pāśa* is a binding implement;
- *vāruṇāstra* may designate a broader Varuṇa-affiliated weapon, often associated with water;
- some passages pair or group Varuṇa’s noose with another missile;
- other passages clearly describe plural cords that seize bodies.

The two concepts overlap because both belong to Varuṇa, but they are not universally interchangeable.

### 4.4 Confusion with other divine nooses

Nooses also belong to Yama/Kāla, Dharma, Brahmā, Gaṇeśa, and several goddesses. The *Rāmāyaṇa* itself lists Dharma-pāśa, Kāla-pāśa, and Varuṇa-pāśa separately. An unidentified noose in art therefore cannot be attributed to Varuṇa without contextual evidence such as his makara or swan vehicle, western directional position, aquatic attendants, or inscription.

### 4.5 Cord versus serpent

Some textual and visual traditions describe a serpent-noose or render the cord as a nāga. The British Museum’s circa-1830 painting is a documented instance in which the curator thinks the characteristic pāśa may have become a snake. This is an iconographic ambiguity, not evidence that one historical rope was replaced with a serpent.

### 4.6 Modern fabricated precision

Internet lists often assign the weapon precise ranks, elemental “statistics,” exhaustive owner lists, or rules such as automatic destruction of an untrained user. Unless a passage and edition are supplied, these should be classified as **modern systematization or unverified compilation**. The early texts do not provide a canonical power-ranking table.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Binding falsehood and violated obligation

The earliest well-supported function is juridical and cosmological. Varuṇa observes what is concealed and binds false speakers or violators of his ordinance. *Atharvaveda* 4.16 explicitly contrasts the liar, whom the nooses should catch, with the truth-speaker, whom they should release.

The pasha is therefore more than an execution weapon. It:

- detects concealed wrongdoing;
- restrains the offender;
- makes the consequence of falsehood bodily;
- enforces oath or promise;
- permits confession, propitiation, healing, and release.

This explains its later aptness in the Bali story: the cosmic sovereign’s cord appears precisely when a ruler’s verbal gift is being measured.

### 6.2 Disease and healing

Varuṇa’s punitive disease is frequently understood as dropsy or abdominal swelling. The “binding” may thus be experienced as bodily constriction and accumulation of water. Release rituals ask Varuṇa to remove the bond. This is an attested history of belief and ritual language, not a medical finding about supernatural causation.

### 6.3 Sacrificial binding

Vedic ritual repeatedly handles real cords: victims are tethered, garments or girdles tied, and ritual participants released. Exegetes interpret some of these as Varuṇa’s noose. The physical cord employed in a rite is a ritual representation or localization of the divine constraint; no source establishes that it is the original celestial object.

### 6.4 Epic battlefield capture

In the epics and Purāṇas the noose becomes a non-ordinary capture weapon. Its characteristic action is:

1. invocation or discharge;
2. encirclement or seizure;
3. immobilization/binding;
4. possible withdrawal by one who knows the relevant procedure.

The *Mahābhārata* stresses that Arjuna receives both *prayoga* and *upasaṃhāra*—deployment and recall. This is closer to receiving controlled knowledge than acquiring an inert tool.

### 6.5 Directional and royal symbolism

As Varuṇa became guardian of the western quarter, the pasha became his identifying insignia. Iconographic scholarship records:

- *Bṛhatsaṃhitā* 58.57: Varuṇa rides a swan and carries a pasha.
- *Agni Purāṇa* 51.15: Varuṇa rides a makara and bears the pasha.
- *Viṣṇudharmottara Purāṇa* III.52: four-armed Varuṇa has lotus and noose in his right hands, conch and jewel-vessel in the left, with a makara banner and seven-swanned chariot. [Iconography of the Lokapālas](https://ignca.gov.in/Asi_data/70875.pdf)

The noose thereby functions as a visual label: in a directional sequence around a temple, it helps distinguish the western guardian.

### 6.6 Restrictions and taboos

The epics imply a competence restriction: divine weapons require initiation into their invocation and withdrawal. No text examined supplies a universal taboo that only Varuṇa may physically touch the noose; indeed Rāma, Arjuna, Garuḍa, and Balarāma can operate Varuṇa-affiliated bonds. The controlling principle is authorized knowledge or divine direction, not exclusive material possession.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The three nooses and Śunaḥśepa

**Tier:** early attested Vedic myth-ritual poetry.

Śunaḥśepa is bound at three sacrificial posts and calls on Varuṇa. The hymn’s closing movement asks that Varuṇa release the upper, middle, and lower bond. The scene unites three kinds of release:

- release from literal tethers;
- release from death as sacrificial victim;
- release from sin or Varuṇa’s displeasure.

Later prose narrative explains why the boy is endangered and links the episode to a royal promise and Varuṇa’s disease. The elaboration is later than the hymn and should not be read automatically into every detail of the *Ṛgvedic* poem.

### 7.2 The hundred nooses of the all-seeing god

**Tier:** Atharvavedic development.

Varuṇa sees the person who believes himself hidden. His spies watch the earth; his nooses, spread in all directions, catch the liar. The imagery turns the noose into a distributed surveillance-and-sanction system rather than one cord carried at Varuṇa’s belt.

### 7.3 Viśvāmitra’s weapon and Vasiṣṭha’s staff

**Tier:** epic narrative.

Viśvāmitra attacks Vasiṣṭha with a succession of divine weapons, including the Vāruṇa. Vasiṣṭha’s Brahma-staff absorbs them. The episode serves the narrative argument that Brahmanical spiritual force exceeds royal and martial weaponry. The pasha’s defeat is temporary within the story-world: Viśvāmitra later possesses the knowledge necessary to give Varuṇa’s noose to Rāma.

### 7.4 Rāma’s initiation

**Tier:** epic initiatory legend.

After Rāma obeys Viśvāmitra and kills Tāṭakā, the sage gives him a catalogue of supernatural weapons. The weapons appear in embodied form, salute him, and ask what they should do. Rāma touches or accepts them and dismisses them until needed. The pasha here is simultaneously:

- a named weapon;
- a divine agency capable of personification;
- mantra-governed knowledge;
- a transferable power stored in the hero’s competence.

### 7.5 Varuṇa’s Tārakāmaya victory and gift to Arjuna

**Tier:** epic retrospective legend.

Varuṇa claims that in the ancient struggle over Tārā his irresistible nooses captured thousands of mighty Daityas. He then gives them to Arjuna, including the secret of recall. The story gives the weapon a pre-Arjuna service history, demonstrates its capture function, and authenticates Arjuna by direct divine investiture.

### 7.6 Binding Bali

**Tier:** Purāṇic theological narrative.

After Vāmana covers earth and heaven in two strides, Bali cannot provide space for the third. Garuḍa binds him with Varuṇa’s cords. Bali says that he fears dishonor more than hell, punishment, or the noose. Because the story revolves around a promise, the deployment strongly recalls the Vedic bond between Varuṇa, truth, and oath—whether or not the Purāṇic redactor consciously intended a direct archaism.

### 7.7 Later “water weapon” synthesis

**Tier:** late popular synthesis and modern retelling.

Contemporary summaries often describe “Varuna Pasha” and “Varunastra” as a single flood-producing weapon that counters fire. Some epic passages certainly use Vāruṇa astras as water weapons, while other passages unmistakably describe binding nooses. A source-driven reconstruction should report both but not erase the distinction.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Legendary object

**Current physical location:** none established.  
**Condition:** not applicable; no surviving material original is documented.  
**Controller:** no human institution has demonstrated legal or physical custody.  
**Access:** through texts, recitation, ritual interpretation, and visual representations.

There is no substantiated theft, colonial seizure, sale, restitution demand, or ownership litigation involving an asserted original Varuṇapāśa.

### 8.2 Textual locations

The object remains textually “located” in:

- the *Ṛgveda* and *Atharvaveda*;
- Brāhmaṇa and ritual literature;
- the *Rāmāyaṇa* and *Mahābhārata*;
- the *Harivaṃśa*;
- Purāṇic narratives and iconographic manuals;
- continuing printed, digital, oral, and liturgical transmission.

These textual witnesses are not autograph manuscripts. They preserve recensional and editorial variation, and verse numbering must be cited by edition.

### 8.3 Representative physical stand-ins

The strongest museum stand-in is the Met’s sixth–eighth-century sandstone Varuṇa, object 19.90. Its carved noose documents how early-medieval artists materialized the literary attribute, but neither the Met nor the work’s title claims that the stone loop is the actual pasha.

The British Museum paintings document later regional variation, including a clearly looped noose, a snake-like substitution, and an axe replacing the expected attribute. None is venerated or catalogued as the original weapon.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Archaeology, museums, and art history

Modern cataloguing detached individual Varuṇa images from architectural and album contexts and made the pasha an identification aid. Museum titles such as “Varuna Holding a Noose and Riding a Swan” translate iconographic scholarship into public-facing object names. This has increased the popular impression of a fixed, singular weapon even though the early texts often use the plural.

### 9.2 Printed mythology and encyclopedias

John Dowson’s nineteenth-century *Classical Dictionary of Hindu Mythology* consolidated Purāṇic and Sanskrit lexicographic material for English readers, describing the noose as Varuṇa’s means of binding offenders and recording alternative names. Such dictionaries significantly shaped later English summaries, but they are secondary compilations rather than primary evidence for Vedic wording.

### 9.3 The modern Varunastra torpedo

The most consequential modern reuse is **Varunastra**, India’s electrically propelled heavyweight anti-submarine torpedo. This is not a replica or claimant; it is a namesake that combines Varuṇa’s aquatic jurisdiction with the martial category *astra*.

DRDO states that the system was developed by its Naval Science and Technological Laboratory, is ship-launched, can target quiet submarines in deep or shallow water, and entered Indian Navy service in 2016. [DRDO product page](https://drdo.gov.in/drdo/en/offerings/products/varunastra)

An official export brochure describes acoustic homing, speeds above forty knots, long-range maneuvering, and a weight of approximately 1,500 kg. [DRDO export catalogue](https://www.drdo.gov.in/drdo/sites/default/files/schemes_services/DRDO-Product-for-Export-2021_web.pdf) Bharat Dynamics’ brochure gives 7.780 m length, 533.4 mm diameter, operating depths from 8 to 600 m, and different exercise and combat weights. [Bharat Dynamics brochure](https://bdl-india.in/sites/default/files/2021-07/Brochure_02_July_2021.pdf)

The modern torpedo should never be entered into the legendary object’s custody chain. Its significance is nominal and symbolic: a contemporary state weapon was branded with the aquatic god’s weapon-name.

### 9.4 Popular culture and internet mythology

Modern websites, games, fan discussions, and weapon-ranking lists regularly:

- equate Varuṇapāśa with every Vāruṇāstra;
- describe it as a water cannon, flood, lasso, or serpent;
- assign it owners absent from cited primary texts;
- treat “possession” as if warriors carried a permanent physical inventory;
- give it quantitative destructive rankings.

These claims require passage-level verification. No single canonical list of all bearers was found in the primary texts examined here.

### 9.5 Contemporary religious meaning

Living Hindu explanations frequently interpret the noose as restraint of falsehood, ego, karma, or disorder, while Varuṇa is invoked in water-related rites. These are valid records of contemporary interpretation. The exact abstractions “ego-control” or “ecological responsibility,” however, should not be represented as verbatim meanings stated by the earliest Vedic verses unless a historical textual bridge is supplied.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 From “weapon” to relational symbol

Early Western translators commonly rendered *pāśa* as “fetter,” “bond,” or “noose.” Later scholarship emphasized that the imagery cannot be reduced to an ordinary rope: Varuṇa’s binding is a relation among truth, oath, cosmic order, illness, sacrifice, and release.

A central interpretive question is whether the bond represents:

- punishment imposed externally by Varuṇa;
- the intrinsic consequence of disrupting *ṛta*;
- ritual impurity or danger;
- bodily disease;
- an oath-curse;
- literal sacrificial restraint;
- or a poetic overlay of several of these.

The evidence supports different emphases in different passages.

### 10.2 Dating problems

Neither the *Ṛgveda* nor the epics can be assigned one simple composition year:

- Vedic hymns were orally composed and transmitted before surviving manuscripts.
- The *Rāmāyaṇa* and *Mahābhārata* are stratified works with recensional differences.
- Purāṇas continued to be revised.
- Iconographic manuals likewise have difficult textual histories.

Consequently, a statement such as “the Varuna Pasha was created in year X” is unsupportable.

### 10.3 Translation problems

Important distinctions include:

- singular *pāśa* versus plural *pāśāḥ/pāśān*;
- literal tether versus supernatural constraint;
- *vāruṇa*, “belonging to Varuṇa,” versus a fixed proper name;
- *astra* as missile, invoked weapon, or supernatural technique;
- “hurling” and “withdrawal” as conventional translations of technical weapon-control vocabulary.

Stephanie Jamison and Joel Brereton’s modern *Ṛgveda* translation and continuing commentary project represent a major philological advance over Griffith, though the freely accessible older translations remain useful for locating passages. [Jamison–Brereton commentary project](https://sjamisonsanskrit.wordpress.com/); [Oxford publication record](https://academic.oup.com/book/33632)

### 10.4 Etymological debate

The resemblance between Varuṇa’s name and roots of covering or binding encouraged interpretations of the noose as an etymological expression of the god. The derivation is disputed. It is safer to say that binding is an extremely old and characteristic function of Varuṇa than to claim his name incontrovertibly means “the binder.”

### 10.5 Iconographic development

Art-historical sources agree that the pasha is Varuṇa’s most characteristic attribute, but disagree or vary concerning:

- vehicle: swan, makara, or other aquatic creature;
- number of arms;
- complexion;
- accompanying lotus, conch, jewel-vessel, or trident;
- whether the noose is a plain cord or serpent;
- whether a local image substitutes another weapon.

The Met sculpture and British Museum paintings demonstrate that textual prescriptions were influential but not mechanically uniform.

### 10.6 Discredited or unsupported claims

The following have not been established:

- that a physical original survives secretly in a temple or underwater palace;
- that all Vāruṇa weapons are one object;
- that the noose was manufactured from a named metal or cosmic substance;
- that it had a fixed historical length;
- that it passed through every hero named on modern astra lists;
- that the modern torpedo embodies recovered ancient technology;
- that archaeological science has authenticated any “Varuna Pasha.”

## 12. Sources

### Primary and near-primary textual witnesses

- *Ṛgveda* 1.24, especially verses 11–15: Śunaḥśepa, Varuṇa, and the threefold bonds.
- *Atharvaveda* 4.16: Varuṇa’s surveillance, sevenfold and hundredfold nooses, falsehood, and dropsy.
- *Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bālakāṇḍa 1.27–28: Viśvāmitra’s transfer of the pasha to Rāma and instruction in recall.
- *Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa* 1.55–56: Viśvāmitra’s weapon contest with Vasiṣṭha.
- *Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parvan, Khāṇḍava episode: Varuṇa arms himself with his noose.
- *Mahābhārata*, Vana Parvan 3.41: Varuṇa’s Tārakāmaya account and gift to Arjuna.
- *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 8.21: Garuḍa binds Bali with Varuṇa’s cords.
- *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 10.50: Balarāma’s attempted binding of Jarāsandha.
- *Harivaṃśa*, battle of gods and demons: Varuṇa fights with nooses.
- *Devī Bhāgavata* 5.28.58: Vāruṇī binds Daityas.
- *Bṛhatsaṃhitā* 58.57, *Agni Purāṇa* 51.15, and *Viṣṇudharmottara Purāṇa* III.52: iconographic prescriptions.

### Museum, scientific, and institutional evidence

No scientific examination of a supposed original exists because no physical claimant has been identified. The museum records cited concern historical representations. The DRDO and Bharat Dynamics records concern the modern torpedo namesake and are not evidence for material continuity with the legendary object.

### Source limitations

Several accessible translations are nineteenth- or early-twentieth-century works. They are cited for passage access and checked against displayed Sanskrit where available. Epic and Purāṇic chapter numbering varies by recension and edition. Digital compilations that add unsourced symbolism were not treated as primary evidence.
