# "Joan of Arc Hearing Voices" (Merson, 1895): Object Dossier

**Proposed filename:** `joan_of_arc_hearing_voices_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier concerns the original drawing catalogued by the National Gallery of Art, Washington, as Luc Olivier Merson’s *Joan of Arc Hearing the Voices*, dated 1895, accession number 2013.89.1. It is not an oil painting, despite being repeatedly called a “painting” by commercial websites. It is a work on paper made as one of six compositions for an illustrated Roman missal devoted to Joan of Arc.

The object is physically extant and institutionally catalogued. Its manufacture, signature, 1921 ownership, 2012 appearance with a Paris dealer, and 2013 acquisition by the National Gallery are documented. Its whereabouts between 1921 and 2012, including what happened after the death of its documented owner Lucien Gougy in 1931, have not been established in the published sources located for this dossier.

Evidence labels used below:

- **Documented:** stated in a primary record or an authoritative institutional catalogue.
- **Direct physical evidence:** visible or materially present on the sheet.
- **Institutional attribution:** the current museum’s cataloguing judgment.
- **Strong inference:** follows closely from multiple records but is not expressly documented.
- **Unresolved:** the surviving public evidence does not establish the claim.
- **Reception or devotional tradition:** concerns the represented event or later use of the image, not the physical drawing’s custody.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Object identity

**Current institutional title:** *Joan of Arc Hearing the Voices*  
**Artist:** Luc Olivier Merson, usually styled Luc-Olivier Merson  
**Artist’s full name:** Nicolas Luc-Olivier Merson  
**Date:** 1895  
**Class:** Finished illustration drawing or presentation design on paper; one of a series of six compositions made for the *Missel romain dit de Jeanne d’Arc*  
**Current collection:** National Gallery of Art, Washington, D.C.  
**Accession number:** 2013.89.1  
**Credit line:** Ailsa Mellon Bruce Fund  
**Display status:** Not on view as of the National Gallery’s currently published record  
**Wikidata identifier:** Q64594054

The National Gallery catalogues the medium as “pen and black and brown ink, with white gouache and gray wash on buff paper” and gives the image dimensions as **34.1 × 23 centimetres** (13 7/16 × 9 1/16 inches). No published overall sheet, mount, or frame dimensions have been located. No weight is published. [National Gallery of Art object record](https://www.nga.gov/artworks/160496-joan-arc-hearing-voices)

### Names and title history

The current English title is descriptive rather than an inscription on the sheet. Relevant forms include:

- *Joan of Arc Hearing the Voices* — current National Gallery title.
- *Joan of Arc Hearing Voices* — shortened English form in modern circulation.
- *Jeanne d’Arc entendant les voix* — modern French descriptive form used in reproductions and collection indexing.
- *Jeanne d’Arc écoutant les voix* — a common French iconographic title, also used for other artists’ treatments of the episode.
- *Johanna von Orleans hört die Stimmen* — German commercial translation.
- *Jeanne entend les voix* — caption used for a reproduction identified with Merson’s missal composition.

The 1921 memorial-exhibition catalogue did **not** individually title this sheet. It grouped six works as “Six compositions. (*Missel de Jeanne d’Arc*)” under catalogue numbers 121–126. Consequently, the earliest securely documented title for the group is functional rather than narrative. The exact point at which the present sheet acquired the distinct title *Joan of Arc Hearing the Voices* has not been established.

The French name *Jeanne d’Arc* is the conventional modern form of the historical subject’s name. In the trial material she referred to herself as *Jehannette* in her birthplace and *Jehanne* in France. These linguistic facts concern the represented person, not a title inscribed by Merson.

### Signature and date

The National Gallery records the following inscription in black ink at the lower right:

> LUC OLIVIER MERSON  
> MDCCCLXXXXV

The second line is Merson’s additive Roman-numeral form of **1895**: MDCCC (1800) + LXXXX (90) + V (5). Although modern convention would normally render 1895 as MDCCCXCV, Merson’s spelling is intelligible and appears in other signed works of the same year. A drawing of *Rest on the Flight into Egypt* in the Morgan Library likewise bears “Luc Olivier Merson / MDCCCLXXXXV.” [Morgan Library record](https://www.themorgan.org/drawings/item/354313)

No additional inscriptions, collector’s marks, stamps, watermarks, verso inscriptions, or labels are disclosed in the National Gallery’s public catalogue. Their absence from the online record should not be taken as proof that the verso or mount bears none.

### Composition

The vertically arranged image shows a young, rural Joan standing in a wooded setting. Her body is frontal or nearly frontal while her face and eyes turn upward, giving visual form to listening rather than military action. A dog lies at her feet. Trees define the narrow, rising composition, and suspended or descending pale forms among the branches externalize the supernatural communication.

Merson differentiates the earthly and visionary registers materially:

- black and brown pen describe figures, vegetation, folds, and contours;
- gray wash establishes shade and atmospheric recession;
- the buff support supplies an intermediate earth tone;
- opaque white gouache produces highlights and makes the visionary elements appear luminous against the tinted paper.

The effect is therefore not merely monochrome. It depends on a restrained hierarchy of buff, brown, black, gray, and opaque white. The represented “voices” are made visible without being rendered as conventionally solid human figures. This is an artistic interpretation; Joan’s trial testimony distinguished voices, light, and identifiable saintly apparitions in ways that were neither simple nor fully consistent across the interrogations.

### Condition

The National Gallery publishes no detailed condition report, conservation history, ultraviolet or infrared study, paper-fibre analysis, watermark study, or treatment history. No tear, loss, stain, abrasion, fading, trimming, backing removal, or restoration is identified in the public record.

The high-resolution institutional image indicates that the sheet survives substantially intact as an image, but a digital reproduction cannot establish the paper’s structural condition. Any stronger statement would require examination of the object or its conservation file.

### Related objects and versions

The drawing belongs to a larger material complex:

1. **Six original Merson compositions for the Joan of Arc missal.** The 1921 exhibition catalogue lists six together, owned by Lucien Gougy. The National Gallery identifies the present sheet as part of catalogue numbers 121–126 but does not assign it one exact number.

2. **A separate study of a woman for a Joan composition.** The same 1921 catalogue lists an “Étude de femme pour une Jeanne d’Arc écoutant les voix,” signed “L.-O. M.” and owned by Xavier Fouques-Duparc. This was a distinct object and should not be confused with NGA 2013.89.1.

3. **Published reproductive versions in the missal.** Editions of the *Missel romain dit de Jeanne d’Arc* contain five or six full-page plates, depending on how title/frontispiece material is counted, together with page borders and vignettes. The printed plate reproducing this composition is not the same physical object as the original drawing.

4. **Other surviving missal drawings.** Drawings titled *Le sacre de Charles VII à Reims* and *Jeanne devant le tribunal* have appeared separately in the art market. They are companion designs, not alternative claimants to the present sheet.

5. **Modern digital derivatives and commercial prints.** Wikimedia Commons hosts the National Gallery’s institutional scan; Rawpixel created and distributed a digitally enhanced derivative; print sellers have transferred the image to canvas, notebooks, tapestries, and other supports. None is the original object.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Maker

Luc-Olivier Merson was born in Paris on 21 May 1846 and died there on 13 November 1920. He trained under Horace Lecoq de Boisbaudran, Chassevent, and Isidore Pils, attended the École des Arts décoratifs and École des Beaux-Arts, and won the Prix de Rome in 1869. He was elected to the Académie des Beaux-Arts in 1892.

Merson worked across painting, drawing, stained-glass design, mosaics, book illustration, banknotes, and postage stamps. The 1921 memorial catalogue emphasized his religious, medievalizing, and literary work and described his art in explicitly spiritual and national terms. The catalogue is commemorative rather than neutral, but it documents how Merson’s immediate professional circle understood his practice. [1921 memorial-exhibition catalogue](https://archive.org/stream/enba-exposition-merson-1921/ENBA_Exposition_Merson_1921_djvu.txt)

### Commission and intended function

**Documented:** The sheet formed part of a set of six compositions for the *Missel de Jeanne d’Arc*, an illustrated Roman Catholic missal issued by Alfred Mame et Fils of Tours.

The missal combined ordinary liturgical material with an extensive Joan of Arc decorative programme. Surviving copies and antiquarian descriptions identify:

- approximately 431 pages in common editions;
- full-page plates after Merson;
- numerous smaller scenes and chapter decorations;
- borders designed by Adolphe Giraldon, incorporating coats of arms, religious symbols, and persons or cities connected to Joan;
- engraving or reproductive work attributed to Thévenin and Quesnel;
- luxury bindings in morocco, gilt edges, metal monograms, and, in some copies, bronze-and-enamel clasps.

An 1895 copy records ecclesiastical approval by Guillaume-René Meignan, archbishop of Tours, dated 14 August 1895. [Description of an 1895 copy](https://labraderie.org/accueil/2935-missel-romain-dit-de-jeanne-d-arc.html)

The present drawing was therefore not originally an autonomous easel picture. Its design was intended for mechanical reproduction in a portable devotional book. Its carefully controlled grayscale and white heightening were particularly suitable for translation into photomechanical or engraved monochrome reproduction.

### Date problem: 1894 or 1895

The physical sheet is explicitly dated **1895** in Merson’s hand. The National Gallery accepts that date.

The 1921 retrospective’s summary of Merson’s publications lists “*Missel de Jeanne d’Arc, Mame, 1894*.” Extant copies are widely dated 1895, and one carries ecclesiastical approval from August 1895. Later editions appeared in 1899, 1904/1905, 1910, and probably other years.

The discrepancy admits several possibilities:

- work on the publication may have begun or been announced in 1894;
- the retrospective catalogue may contain a one-year bibliographic error;
- different issues or preparatory states may have circulated;
- the project may have crossed the 1894–1895 publication boundary.

**Conclusion:** the sheet itself is securely dated 1895. An 1894 project or edition is possible but not established by the sources examined.

### Material origin and fabrication

No invoice, commission contract, publisher’s ledger, correspondence, studio inventory, documented price, or paper supplier has been located. The sheet’s fibres, mill, and geographical manufacture remain unpublished.

The documented working sequence is nevertheless reconstructable in broad form:

1. Merson selected or received six Joan episodes for the missal.
2. He prepared figure and compositional studies; at least one separate female study for the “voices” composition existed by 1921.
3. He executed a finished design in pen, black and brown inks, gray wash, and opaque white on buff paper.
4. He signed and dated it.
5. The design was translated into a reproducible plate by the publication’s graphic collaborators.
6. Printed impressions entered successive editions of the missal, while the original sheet remained a separate, collectible work of art.

### Historical source for the represented episode

The represented event is not contemporary with Merson. Its fundamental documentary source is Joan’s 1431 condemnation trial.

During interrogation on 22 February 1431, Joan stated that at approximately thirteen she received a voice from God to help and guide her; the first occurrence frightened her; it came around noon in summer, in her father’s garden; she heard it on her right, toward the church; and she seldom heard it without light. In later questioning she associated the voices or apparitions principally with Saint Michael, Saint Catherine of Alexandria, and Saint Margaret of Antioch. [English translation of the trial proceedings](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/basis/joanofarc-trial.asp)

The record does **not** establish that the exact wooded tableau, dog, costume, or arrangement of luminous forms in Merson’s drawing existed as a fifteenth-century visual tradition. Those are later iconographic constructions.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 1895: Merson’s studio

**Documented by signature and institutional attribution:** Merson completed and signed the drawing in 1895.

Its immediate legal disposition is unknown. The publisher may have commissioned or temporarily held it for reproduction, but no contract or transfer record was found. It may have remained with Merson, passed to a collaborator, or been sold or given to Lucien Gougy. The mechanism cannot presently be stated.

### By May 1921: Lucien Gougy collection, Paris

The École Nationale des Beaux-Arts mounted a memorial exhibition of Merson’s work in May 1921, six months after his death. Entries 121–126 read:

> Six compositions. (*Missel de Jeanne d’Arc*).  
> Appartiennent à M. Lucien Gougy.

This is the earliest located, securely datable custody record for the group. The National Gallery connects the present drawing to these entries and lists Gougy among its associated provenance names. [Catalogue entry](https://archive.org/stream/enba-exposition-merson-1921/ENBA_Exposition_Merson_1921_djvu.txt)

Lucien Gougy, a Paris bookseller, bibliophile, occasional publisher, and officeholder in French booksellers’ organizations, was a plausible collector for finished book illustrations. The exact date, price, and means by which he acquired the six drawings are unknown.

The exhibition temporarily moved the sheet from Gougy’s custody to the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris. The catalogue establishes exhibition presence but does not document transport dates, insurance, framing, or display arrangement.

### 1921–1931: presumed return to Gougy

It is reasonable to infer that the drawing returned to Gougy after the exhibition, but a return receipt or later inventory has not been located.

Gougy died in Paris in June 1931. His library was dispersed through a series of sales between 1934 and 1936, but the present research found no auction entry conclusively identifying the drawing or the six-sheet set. Because the object is graphic art rather than a book, it need not have appeared in the library sales.

### 1931–2012: undocumented gap

This is the major break in the published chain.

No located source establishes:

- Gougy’s heir or legatee for the drawing;
- a posthumous sale;
- whether the six compositions stayed together;
- when the present sheet became separated from any companions;
- wartime location during 1939–1945;
- export or import history;
- any intermediary dealer or collector;
- the date or mechanism by which Nicolas Schwed acquired it.

Claims filling this interval without documentary evidence should be rejected as conjectural.

### 2012: Nicolas Schwed, Paris

The drawing appeared as number 55 in the Paris dealer Nicolas Schwed’s catalogue *Dessins et tableaux anciens et du XIXème siècle* in 2012. This is the first securely published reappearance after 1921.

The National Gallery writes the dealer’s name “Nicolas Schwed,” while its exhibition-history line renders the catalogue name “Nicholas Schwed.” The former appears to be the normal French spelling. The price, consignor, and date of the dealer’s own acquisition are not published in the NGA record.

### 2013: purchase by the National Gallery of Art

The National Gallery records:

> (Nicolas Schwed, Paris); purchased by NGA 2013.

The acquisition was funded by the **Ailsa Mellon Bruce Fund** and assigned accession number **2013.89.1**. The Gallery’s 2013 annual report lists the drawing among that year’s acquisitions, confirming institutional entry and accession. [National Gallery annual report for 2013](https://www.nga.gov/sites/default/files/migrate_images/content/dam/ngaweb/About/pdf/annual-reports/annual-reports/annual-report-2013.pdf)

The published record does not give the purchase price, invoice date, acquisition meeting, export licence, shipment itinerary, or condition at entry.

### 2013–present: National Gallery custody

The drawing has remained in the National Gallery’s collection. No subsequent loans or exhibitions are listed in its current online exhibition history. No deaccession, restitution request, lien, seizure, theft, loss, or ownership dispute has been located.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is the National Gallery sheet physically authentic?

No published authenticity controversy has been located. The attribution rests on:

- the full lower-right signature;
- an explicit date;
- medium and technique consistent with Merson’s finished illustration drawings;
- relationship to a documented publication project;
- identification with a group publicly exhibited in 1921;
- acceptance by the National Gallery after acquisition.

These constitute a strong conventional art-historical case. They do not amount to scientific proof. No pigment, ink, paper, fibre, handwriting, watermark, radiographic, or spectroscopic examination is publicly reported.

### Is it one of Gougy’s six 1921 drawings?

The National Gallery says the work was exhibited in 1921 as “part of no. 121–126.” The catalogue itself does not title or individually describe the six sheets. Identity therefore rests on later curatorial matching rather than an itemized 1921 description.

The case is strong because:

- the current sheet depicts one of the missal’s published compositions;
- it bears a date matching the publication;
- its dimensions and finish suit reproductive artwork;
- Gougy owned six such compositions;
- the NGA explicitly associates Gougy with the work.

What remains unresolved is which exact number, 121 through 126, it would have carried if the six had been individually numbered.

### Original versus printed image

The drawing and the printed missal plate are different objects:

- **Original:** unique mixed-media sheet, NGA 2013.89.1.
- **Published plate:** mechanically reproduced image printed in numerous copies of the missal.
- **Later digital image:** scan or photograph of the original.
- **Commercial derivative:** digitally altered file or reproduction printed on a new support.

A modern canvas print advertised as the 1895 “painting” is not a second original and has no claim to the National Gallery object’s identity.

### Title and medium misidentification

The most common modern error is calling the work a painting, often an oil painting. The National Gallery classifies and materially describes it as a drawing on buff paper using inks, gouache, and wash. White gouache is paint, but its presence does not turn the work into a canvas painting.

### Rival claimants or forgery

No rival sheet claiming to be this particular original, documented forgery, attribution lawsuit, restitution claim, or competing museum identity has been found.

The separately catalogued female study and printed plate should be treated as related works, not claimants.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Luc-Olivier Merson — maker, 1895

Merson designed and executed the drawing, then signed and dated it. Its handling of a supernatural subject fits his broader practice of combining precise draftsmanship, medieval or early-Renaissance visual language, and religious narrative.

### Alfred Mame et Fils — publisher, c. 1894–1895

The Tours publishing house produced the *Missel romain dit de Jeanne d’Arc*. The firm’s extensive religious-publication apparatus converted Merson’s unique sheet into a reproducible devotional image.

### Adolphe Giraldon — decorative collaborator

Giraldon designed the missal’s page frames or borders. His heraldic and decorative programme placed Merson’s large compositions inside an integrated national, historical, and devotional book design.

### Thévenin and Quesnel — reproductive craftsmen

Descriptions of the missal credit Thévenin and Quesnel with engraving or reproductive execution. Their work mediated between Merson’s original drawing and the printed plates. The precise division of labour and process used for this image have not been established.

### Cardinal Guillaume-René Meignan — ecclesiastical approver, 1895

The archbishop of Tours approved the missal on 14 August 1895. This approval concerned the publication’s religious contents; it was not a declaration that every visual detail was historically or mystically authentic.

### Lucien Gougy — owner by 1921

Gougy possessed the six missal compositions when they were lent to the Merson memorial exhibition. No evidence shows that he commissioned them.

### École Nationale des Beaux-Arts — exhibition institution, 1921

The school displayed the work as part of the posthumous survey that first securely documents its ownership and public exhibition.

### Nicolas Schwed — dealer, 2012–2013

Schwed catalogued the drawing in Paris and sold it to the National Gallery.

### National Gallery of Art — owner and custodian, since 2013

The Gallery accessioned, catalogued, digitized, and made the image available through its open-access programme.

### Ailsa Mellon Bruce Fund — acquisition funding mechanism

The purchase was made through this National Gallery acquisition fund. The fund did not previously own the sheet.

### Joan of Arc — represented historical person

Joan, born at Domrémy around 1412 and executed at Rouen in 1431, is the image’s subject. Her association with the physical sheet is representational; she neither owned nor encountered it.

### Saint Michael the Archangel, Saint Catherine, and Saint Margaret — represented or implied divine persons

Joan eventually named these as the principal identities of her voices and apparitions. Merson’s pale visionary forms invoke that testimony. Identification of each individual form in the drawing is not supplied in the Gallery catalogue, so assigning every shape to a particular saint would exceed the published evidence.

### Joan’s judges and trial notaries — source-producing network, 1431

The ecclesiastical court at Rouen and its notarial apparatus created the textual record from which later artists drew the “voices” episode. That tribunal is part of the image’s documentary genealogy, not the sheet’s ownership history.

### Roman Catholic Church — institutional and devotional context

The drawing was made for a Roman missal while Joan’s formal cause was progressing but before beatification and canonization. Joan was beatified in 1909 and canonized on 16 May 1920. The Vatican’s current saint record gives the canonization date and reproduces the institutional narrative of her life and cause. [Dicastery for the Causes of Saints](https://www.causesanti.va/it/santi-e-beati/giovanna-d-arco.html)

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Function of the original sheet

There is no evidence that the unique drawing itself was:

- placed on an altar;
- carried in procession;
- venerated as a relic or cult image;
- used in the canonization proceedings;
- touched to Joan relics;
- credited with miracles;
- used as a legal or heraldic insignia.

Its primary function was **design and reproduction**. It supplied imagery for a devotional book, after which it functioned as a collectible original and exhibition object.

### Function through the printed missal

The printed image entered a different ritual environment. A personal Roman missal could accompany Mass, private prayer, first communion, memorial use, or household devotion. The Joan cycle surrounded liturgical texts with scenes of a national heroine whose sanctity was not yet formally complete in 1895.

The publication thus joined three systems:

1. Roman Catholic liturgy;
2. private devotional ownership;
3. French national memory.

Specific surviving copies acquired their own ceremonial histories. One bookseller records loose devotional images and handwritten notes commemorating a 1961 solemn communion at the church of Sainte-Jeanne-d’Arc in Mulhouse. That is evidence for the later ritual life of one printed copy, not the National Gallery drawing.

### Joan as national and sacred symbol in the 1890s

The drawing was made during a period of intense competition over Joan’s meaning. Republicans could present her as a daughter of the people and defender of the nation; monarchists emphasized loyalty to the rightful king; Catholics emphasized divine mission and martyrdom; nationalists made her a personification of territorial defence.

The 1894 campaign for a public Joan festival described her as a national, transpartisan figure even as opposing political constituencies sought to appropriate her. [Study of the 1894 Joan festival](https://www.persee.fr/doc/rhmc_0048-8003_1973_num_20_3_2259)

Merson’s version concentrates on divine vocation before warfare. Joan is defined neither by armour nor battlefield victory but by receptivity to a supernatural command. In the context of a missal, this favoured the devotional interpretation while remaining compatible with national symbolism.

### Access and touch rules

No special restrictions are published for the drawing beyond ordinary museum handling controls. As a light-sensitive work on paper, it would normally receive limited illumination and restricted display periods, but no object-specific rotation or lux limit has been located.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

The physical drawing has attracted little independent legend. Most narratives attached to it derive from Joan’s visions.

### Joan’s first voice

**Early attested account:** Joan’s own answers in the 1431 proceedings.

She said that while approximately thirteen, at noon in summer, in her father’s garden, she heard a voice from her right, toward the church. She was frightened, and light commonly accompanied the voice. The voice first instructed her to behave well and attend church. Later communications directed her toward her public mission.

This account is evidence that Joan testified to the experience. It does not independently prove either supernatural intervention or a modern medical diagnosis.

### Identification of the voices

In subsequent questioning Joan identified Saint Catherine and Saint Margaret as voices that had guided her for years, while Saint Michael figured in her initial vocation. Her testimony sometimes distinguished “voice,” “angel,” “saints,” light, bodily appearance, crowns, and touch; at other moments she refused particulars.

Later imagery commonly converts these guarded and shifting answers into a single lucid vision of three recognizable celestial visitors. Merson instead uses ambiguity: pale forms within the wooded environment suggest a manifestation without turning it into an ordinary bodily encounter.

### The traditional site at Domrémy

The garden of Joan’s father and the nearby church form the textual geography of her testimony. Later devotional geography also associates the Bois-Chenu area near Domrémy with the voices, and the basilica there memorializes that tradition. The exact topography represented by Merson is not identified and should not be treated as a measured portrait of a surviving site.

### Medical and psychiatric reinterpretations

From the late nineteenth century onward, physicians retrospectively proposed hysteria, psychopathy, schizophrenia, epilepsy, migraine, tuberculosis, and other explanations. A modern historiographical survey describes changing diagnoses from 1882 to 2016. These are retrospective hypotheses based on documentary language, not scientific examinations of Joan. [Survey of medical hypotheses](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36857627/)

The American Journal of Psychiatry reproduced Merson’s image in 2017 alongside an argument favouring a seizure disorder. That association gave the artwork a modern psychiatric reception distinct from its original devotional context. The article itself cautioned, in effect, that the image is an interpretation rather than clinical documentation. [“Joan of Arc—Hearing Voices”](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2017.17080948)

### Misleading modern legend: “Merson’s famous painting”

Commercial pages increasingly describe the work as Merson’s “famous painting,” sometimes assigning it broad categories such as Realism or Symbolism without citing a catalogue raisonné. This is a modern metadata accretion. The original is a drawing, and no evidence was found that it enjoyed exceptional fame before its museum digitization.

## 8. Current Status and Location

The drawing is owned by the **National Gallery of Art, Washington, D.C.**, under accession number **2013.89.1**.

The National Gallery currently marks it **“Not on view.”** This means it is not presently installed in a public gallery; it does not mean it is lost or physically absent from the collection. Its precise storage room is properly unpublished.

The Gallery’s current record supplies:

- maker and date;
- medium;
- image dimensions;
- accession number;
- credit line;
- provenance from Nicolas Schwed;
- 1921 and 2012 exhibition references;
- inscription;
- high-resolution downloadable image.

No active ownership, restitution, Nazi-era provenance, export, or repatriation dispute is disclosed. The published 1931–2012 provenance gap nevertheless remains a legitimate research issue. A gap is not evidence of unlawful transfer, but it prevents a fully continuous chain from being asserted.

No recent conservation treatment or scientific examination is published. Access to the original would presumably require either a future exhibition or an appointment governed by the Gallery’s study-room policies.

The digital image is available through the Gallery’s open-access system and has been released for unrestricted public use. [National Gallery object record](https://www.nga.gov/artworks/160496-joan-arc-hearing-voices)

## 9. Modern Reception

### Publication and repetition in missal editions

Merson’s composition first circulated through editions of the Joan of Arc missal. Extant descriptions document editions or issues dated 1895, 1899, approximately 1904–1905, and 1910. Variations occur in binding, paper, plate count, and the number or state of reproductions.

A 1905 description identifies six Merson plates “in double state,” meaning impressions in two forms or states were bound into that copy. An 1895 description counts a frontispiece, title-frontispiece, and five additional plates. These differences do not necessarily indicate different original designs; they may reflect cataloguing conventions and edition structures. [Bibliographical descriptions of editions](https://www.livre-rare-book.com/book/5472601/GITj420)

### 1921 retrospective

The memorial exhibition at the École des Beaux-Arts reframed the six designs as autonomous works in Merson’s artistic corpus. Removed from their printed liturgical setting and displayed as originals, they became evidence of the artist’s method and draftsmanship.

The exhibition’s rhetoric also nationalized Merson himself. Its preface praised an art rooted in France’s spiritual and artistic past. Because it appeared shortly after the First World War and shortly after Joan’s canonization, this framing added retrospective patriotic resonance to an 1895 devotional illustration.

### Canonization context

Joan was canonized on 16 May 1920, approximately six months before Merson’s death and one year before the retrospective. The drawing therefore crossed a major classification boundary during its early life: it was created when Joan was formally a candidate for sainthood and exhibited after she had become a canonized saint.

The object itself did not change materially, but captions could now describe its subject as “Saint Joan” without anticipation.

### Museum digitization and open access

After accession in 2013, the National Gallery photographed or scanned the drawing and distributed the digital file through its open-access programme. Wikimedia Commons received the institutional image in 2019 and identifies its source, dimensions, medium, accession number, and Gallery credit. [Wikimedia Commons institutional image](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Luc_Olivier_Merson,_Joan_of_Arc_Hearing_the_Voices,_1895,_NGA_160496.jpg)

This digital release radically expanded the image’s audience. It now circulates independently of both the original missal and the physical sheet.

### Digitally enhanced derivative

Rawpixel issued a digitally enhanced version in 2021. A later Wikimedia file identifies this as a derivative of the National Gallery image rather than a new photograph of the original. Its colour, contrast, sharpening, or cleaning should not be used as evidence for the sheet’s current physical appearance. [Rawpixel derivative record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Joan_of_Arc_Hearing_the_Voices_(1895)_drawing_in_high_resolution_by_Luc-Olivier_Merson._Original_from_National_Gallery_of_Art._Digitally_enhanced_by_rawpixel._(51927755648).jpg)

### Commercial afterlife

The digitized design is sold as:

- canvas prints;
- framed posters;
- tapestries;
- notebooks;
- wall décor;
- downloadable image files.

Commercial sellers sometimes enlarge it to dimensions many times greater than the original 34.1 × 23-centimetre image, add colour casts, crop it, sharpen its lines, or call it a “painting.” Such objects are replicas or transformations with separate production histories.

### Psychiatric and hearing-voices discourse

The image has been republished in modern medical writing because its title appears to illustrate auditory hallucination. That use changes its framework from Catholic vocation to psychiatry or neurohistory. It should not be mistaken for evidence that Merson intended a medical diagnosis.

### Relation to other nineteenth-century Joan images

Merson’s composition belongs to a dense nineteenth-century iconography that includes works by Hermann Stilke, Léon-François Bénouville, Eugène Thirion, Jules Bastien-Lepage, Pierre Fritel, Diogenes Maillart, Pedro Américo, and others. The “hearing the voices” scene allowed artists to negotiate whether the supernatural should appear as:

- fully embodied saints or angels;
- beams or zones of light;
- transparent figures;
- environmental animation;
- an inward psychological event.

Merson’s fusion of forest observation with pale, partially immaterial signs places the drawing between literal apparition and symbolic visualization.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Core object documentation

The most important object-level source is the National Gallery’s catalogue entry. It establishes the current identity, materials, dimensions, inscription, accession, recent provenance, and exhibition history.

The second indispensable source is the 1921 memorial-exhibition catalogue. It independently establishes:

- the existence of six Merson compositions for the missal;
- their ownership by Lucien Gougy;
- their presence at the École des Beaux-Arts exhibition;
- a distinct female study for a Joan-listening composition;
- Merson’s broader publication history.

The Bibliothèque nationale de France catalogue confirms the memorial catalogue’s bibliographic identity: Paris, Imprimerie Frazier-Soye, 1921, 79 pages, with a preface by P.-A.-J. Dagnan-Bouveret. [BnF catalogue record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb403743247)

### Catalogue problems

The 1921 catalogue is partly frustrating as a provenance source:

- the six drawings are grouped rather than individually titled;
- dimensions and techniques are omitted;
- acquisition mechanisms are not recorded;
- the missal is dated 1894 in the publication list, conflicting with the present sheet and extant 1895 copies;
- it supplies no history before Gougy.

The National Gallery’s online provenance is also compressed. Its formal provenance line names only Nicolas Schwed, although Lucien Gougy is listed under associated names and is evidenced by the exhibition history. The difference reflects cataloguing format, not necessarily curatorial rejection of Gougy’s ownership.

### Catalogue raisonné and unpublished research

A widely accessible, complete modern catalogue raisonné of Merson’s drawings has not been located. Relevant research includes:

- François Thoraval’s 1989 master’s catalogue of Merson’s paintings;
- Thoraval’s 1990 study of Merson drawings in French museums;
- the 2008–2009 Rennes exhibition *L’étrange Monsieur Merson*;
- the 1921 memorial catalogue;
- Adolphe Giraldon’s 1929 *Luc-Olivier Merson: une noble vie d’artiste*.

The Thoraval studies may remain academic theses rather than standard published reference works. Consultation could clarify the six missal drawings’ later dispersal, but no digital object-level entries were found during this research.

### Primary evidence for the iconography

The 1431 trial transcript remains the essential early source for Joan’s voices. It is unusually rich but not transparent:

- it was created in an adversarial judicial process;
- questions were translated between French and Latin;
- notarial minutes and the later formal record have distinct textual histories;
- Joan strategically refused some answers;
- the trial’s theological vocabulary does not map neatly onto modern categories of vision, hallucination, or audition.

Merson’s image is therefore an interpretive synthesis, not a direct illustration of one simple sentence.

### Unresolved research questions

1. Who commissioned Merson’s six compositions, and what was he paid?
2. Did Merson deliver the physical originals to Mame?
3. How and when did Gougy acquire the set?
4. Were all six still together at Gougy’s death?
5. Do estate inventories or sale catalogues document their dispersal?
6. Who held the present sheet between 1931 and 2012?
7. When was the current English title first assigned?
8. Which of the 1921 numbers, 121–126, corresponds exactly to the sheet?
9. Does the verso carry labels, numbers, collector’s marks, or reproductive instructions?
10. What printing process reproduced the image in each missal edition?
11. Has the paper been watermarked or scientifically examined?
12. Has the drawing received any conservation treatment since 2013?

## 11. Chronology

**c. 1425 — represented event, not object history.** According to Joan’s 1431 testimony, at about thirteen she first heard a voice in her father’s garden, from her right toward the church, commonly accompanied by light.

**1431 — earliest primary textual basis.** Joan describes her voices and apparitions during the Rouen condemnation trial.

**1894 — possible beginning or announced date of publication project.** The 1921 Merson catalogue later dates the Mame *Missel de Jeanne d’Arc* to 1894. This may be a project date or catalogue error.

**1895 — creation.** Merson completes the present drawing in pen and black and brown ink, white gouache, and gray wash on buff paper; signs and dates it “MDCCCLXXXXV.”

**14 August 1895 — ecclesiastical approval of missal.** Archbishop Guillaume-René Meignan approves the publication’s religious text.

**1895 — first documented edition or issue.** Alfred Mame et Fils publishes the illustrated *Missel romain dit de Jeanne d’Arc*. Merson’s composition is reproduced as one of the major plates.

**1899–1910 — later editions.** The image continues circulating through reissues of the missal with varying bindings and plate descriptions.

**1909 — beatification of Joan.** The represented subject becomes formally Blessed Joan of Arc. The drawing itself remains unchanged.

**16 May 1920 — canonization.** Joan is canonized by the Roman Catholic Church.

**13 November 1920 — death of Merson.**

**May 1921 — first secure exhibition and ownership record.** The École Nationale des Beaux-Arts exhibits six *Missel de Jeanne d’Arc* compositions, catalogue numbers 121–126, belonging to Lucien Gougy. The National Gallery identifies the present drawing as one of them.

**June 1931 — death of Lucien Gougy.** The drawing’s subsequent disposition is not documented in the sources located.

**1931–2012 — provenance gap.** Holder, location, transactions, and wartime history unresolved.

**2012 — Paris dealer exhibition.** Nicolas Schwed publishes the sheet as no. 55 in *Dessins et tableaux anciens et du XIXème siècle*.

**2013 — National Gallery acquisition.** Purchased from Nicolas Schwed using the Ailsa Mellon Bruce Fund; accessioned as 2013.89.1.

**2013 — acquisition published.** The National Gallery’s annual report lists the sheet among the year’s acquisitions.

**2017 — psychiatric reception.** The *American Journal of Psychiatry* reproduces or discusses Merson’s interpretation in an article on Joan’s voices.

**2019 — institutional digital file uploaded to Wikimedia Commons.** The Gallery’s high-resolution image enters broad open-access circulation.

**2021 — digitally enhanced derivative.** Rawpixel distributes an altered digital version derived from the Gallery image.

**2026 — present status.** The original remains in the National Gallery of Art, Washington, accession 2013.89.1, and is listed as not on view. No active authenticity, ownership, or restitution dispute is published.
