# Jose de San Martin's curved saber (sable corvo): Object Dossier

> **Object tracked:** the single curved saber catalogued by Argentina’s Museo Histórico Nacional as **MHNA 1314**, traditionally and institutionally identified as the weapon carried by José de San Martín during the South American independence campaigns.
>
> **Status date:** 6 September 2026.
>
> **Evidence key:** **[D]** documentary or archival evidence; **[S]** scientific/technical finding; **[I]** institutional attribution or tradition; **[P]** plausible inference; **[A]** allegation or disputed claim; **[L]** legend or unverified later tradition.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity and names

The object is an early nineteenth-century—or possibly older—single-edged, strongly curved cavalry saber of Southwest or South Asian form, mounted with a cross-shaped brass guard and dark horn grip scales and accompanied by a black leather scabbard. It is neither a regulation Argentine military saber nor a richly inscribed presentation weapon. Its austerity, extreme curvature, and association with mounted campaigning are central to its public image.

Its principal names are:

- **Spanish:** *sable corvo de San Martín*, “San Martín’s curved saber”; *sable corvo del Libertador*; *sable del Libertador*; *el corvo*.
- **San Martín’s own testamentary description:** simply *“el sable que me ha acompañado en toda la Guerra de la Independencia de la América del Sud”*—“the saber that accompanied me throughout the War of Independence of South America.” He did not call it *corvo*, Persian, Damascus, or sacred.
- **Typological labels applied later:** *sable oriental*, *sable mameluco*, *shamshir/shamsir persa*, *kilij turco*, *talwar indio*, *cimitarra*, *alfanje*, and occasionally *sable de abordaje*. These are not interchangeable. Modern Argentine arms specialists commonly prefer **shamshir**, while “mameluke saber” is a broader European military category describing curved, cross-hilted weapons inspired by Ottoman and Middle Eastern forms.
- **Persian:** شمشیر, *šamšīr/shamshir*, the ordinary Persian word for “sword.” The often-repeated derivation “lion’s claw” is popular but linguistically uncertain and should not be treated as established etymology.

The Museo Histórico Nacional’s national collections database catalogs the object as **“Sable,” inventory MHNA1314**, in the museum’s *Armas blancas* collection, describing it as the campaign saber used by General José de San Martín in the War of Independence. This establishes the present institutional identity and catalogue number, not by itself every claimed episode of use. [Museo Histórico Nacional collections record](https://patrimonioargentino.cultura.gob.ar/index.php/Detail/objects/74499)

### Dimensions and weight

The fullest published technical measurements, attributed to examinations following the second theft and summarized by Capt. Oscar Augusto Blanco in 1967, are:

| Component | Measurement |
|---|---:|
| Blade length | 818 mm |
| Overall saber length | 948 mm |
| Maximum blade width | 27 mm |
| Maximum blade thickness | 5 mm |
| Scabbard length | 845 mm in the technical tabulation; an earlier descriptive publication gave approximately 87 cm |
| Saber without sword knot | approximately 890 g |
| Saber with sword knot | approximately 910 g |
| Scabbard | approximately 680 g |
| Saber, scabbard, and sword knot | approximately 1,570 g |

The small discrepancies between the earlier narrative measurement and the later technical tabulation probably reflect measuring conventions rather than different objects, but the original examination sheets should be consulted before treating either figure as definitive. [Ramallo’s documentary history and technical summary](https://institutorosas.cultura.gob.ar/media/uploads/site-35/multimedia/historia_del_sable_de_san_martin_jorge_maria_ramallo_ins_2009.pdf)

### Construction

**Blade.** The blade is polished high-carbon steel, single-edged, narrow, and markedly curved. Its curvature increases toward the distal portion rather than following a perfectly regular European arc. Its back is described as rounded. Technical examination found no welded assembly seam characteristic of pattern-welded imitation “Damascus” blades.

**Hilt.** The hilt has a plain cross guard with straight brass quillons. The brass components were reportedly cast and carry no published maker’s mark. The grip consists of black scales identified in historical technical descriptions as buffalo horn. A perforation at the grip’s terminal accommodated a dark red or maroon sword knot terminating in a small silver-colored tassel.

**Scabbard.** The surviving scabbard is black, grained leather with brass furniture: a plain throat or locket, two raised brass suspension bands and rings, and a decorated chape engraved or chased with linear ornament. A small steel wheel or roller is attached at the lower end through a projecting pin. The scabbard, box, and sword knot formed part of the ensemble sent from England in 1897.

**Inscriptions and marks.** No blade inscription, ownership inscription, date, maker’s signature, arsenal stamp, or assay mark has been published. The absence of a maker’s mark is important: neither the blade’s precise workshop nor the London seller can presently be identified.

### Condition, intervention, and missing or altered elements

The saber survived two political thefts, repeated handling, police recovery, technical testing, transfers between institutions, public display, and ceremonial transport. No complete current conservation report is publicly available.

The 1966–67 study was designed to be substantially non-destructive. Metallographic structure was examined optically by exploiting reflected-light contrasts already visible in the polished blade; radiography and other physical inspections were also reported. Separate institutions examined the wood, leather, and metals. The leather could not be taxonomically identified in the published summary.

A 2015 congressional request for information alleged that removal of the sword knot during preparation, copying, or transfer may have damaged or caused the disappearance of that component and may also have marked the scabbard. The request asked whether the knot had been cut and what conservation precautions had been taken. It documents a formal allegation and demand for disclosure, not proof that permanent damage occurred. No publicly located final technical answer settles the matter. [Argentine Chamber of Deputies request, expediente 4433-D-2015](https://www.hcdn.gob.ar/comisiones/permanentes/ccultura/proyecto.html?exp=4433-D-2015)

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Manufacture: what is known

**[S]** The 1966 technical program found a crucible-steel-type structure commonly called “Damascus steel”: relatively large iron-carbide particles distributed irregularly in bands. Daniel Vassallo of Argentina’s Comisión Nacional de Energía Atómica later explained that the light-reflecting bands corresponded to carbide-rich regions. The observation favored a preindustrial crucible-steel blade rather than a European pattern-welded decorative imitation. [CNEA repository record](https://nuclea.cnea.gob.ar/entities/publication/8f6c5073-a5d4-4e7e-a8fa-509b4828e410) [CNEA technical article](https://nuclea.cnea.gob.ar/bitstreams/68c8373c-13c4-4781-a0b1-bcce1ebc3931/download)

The popular formulation “forged in Damascus” is too specific. “Damascus steel” can describe patterned crucible steel whose ingots, blades, or trade routes involved India, Persia, Central Asia, the Ottoman world, or Syrian commercial centers. The examination supported an **Asian crucible-steel tradition**, and the blade’s shape supports comparison with Persian shamshirs, but it did not identify a particular city, forge, smith, mine, or date.

**[P]** A plausible reconstruction is that an older or imported Asian blade was furnished or refurnished with its present hilt and scabbard for the European market. London had a substantial trade in imported arms and in “mameluke”-style officers’ swords after the French Egyptian campaign and British activity in Egypt and India. No bill of sale, shop ledger, maker’s mark, or customs record has yet connected this particular saber to a named London dealer.

### Acquisition by San Martín

**[I/P]** The standard institutional history says San Martín acquired the weapon in London in 1811, between leaving Spain and sailing for the Río de la Plata. This is plausible and widely repeated, but the most detailed specialist treatment admits that no receipt or other direct purchase document has been found. The claim rests on reconstruction from his itinerary, the weapon’s presence early in his American career, and later biographical tradition—not on a surviving London sales record. [Argentine National Academy of History review](https://repositorio.anh.org.ar/bitstream/anh/246/1/academia-de-la-historia-boletin-digital-8-.pdf)

The saber’s manufacture must therefore be described as:

- **Blade:** Asian crucible steel, probably Persian or from a closely related production tradition; exact place and date unresolved.
- **Mounting:** undetermined; possibly assembled or refurbished for sale in Europe.
- **Purchase:** traditionally London, 1811; seller and price unknown.
- **Earliest secure personal identification:** San Martín’s 1844 will, which refers unmistakably to one particular saber that had accompanied him through the independence war.

### Earliest evidence of use

The weapon is traditionally identified in portraits and campaign narratives with the curved saber San Martín carried after arriving in Buenos Aires in March 1812. It is associated with San Lorenzo in 1813, the Army of the Andes, Chacabuco in 1817, Maipú in 1818, and the campaigns culminating in Peru.

The evidence must be qualified:

- San Martín’s 1844 wording is strong first-person evidence that a particular saber accompanied him throughout the war.
- The continuous identification of the surviving saber with that testamentary object is supported by the documented inheritance and donation chain after 1850.
- Claims that he personally **struck with it** in every named battle are usually patriotic shorthand. Presence on campaign is better documented than specific blows, kills, or occasions of unsheathing.
- No blood, residue, impact, or use-wear study establishing a particular battle has been published.
- The phrase “baptized by fire at San Lorenzo” is commemorative language, not a forensic finding.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### c. 1811–1812: London to Buenos Aires

**[I/P]** San Martín probably acquired the saber during his London stay in 1811. He sailed aboard the *George Canning* and reached Buenos Aires in March 1812. The saber is believed to have traveled in his personal effects. Documentation does not identify the shop, transaction, price, or shipboard packing.

### 1812–1824: South American campaigns

**[D/I]** San Martín’s testament declares that the saber accompanied him through the entire South American independence war. The surviving object is consequently associated with his movements through:

- Buenos Aires and San Lorenzo;
- the northwestern United Provinces;
- Mendoza and the Cuyo military establishment;
- the Andes crossings;
- Chile, including the Chacabuco and Maipú campaigns;
- the Pacific expedition;
- Peru and the Protectorate;
- his withdrawal from public command and departure from South America.

This is a broad itinerary, not a day-by-day object log. No separate quartermaster record has been located for the privately owned saber.

### 1824–1850: Europe in San Martín’s household

San Martín left for Europe with his daughter in 1824. The saber remained among his possessions during his residences in France and the Low Countries, ultimately at Boulogne-sur-Mer. The exact room, case, or storage history is undocumented.

On 23 January 1844, in Paris, he executed a holographic will. Clause three stated:

> “El sable que me ha acompañado en toda la Guerra de la Independencia de la América del Sud, le será entregado al General de la República Argentina don Juan Manuel de Rosas…”

He explained the bequest as proof of his satisfaction with Rosas’s firmness in sustaining the Republic’s honor against foreign powers. This clause transformed a campaign weapon into an explicitly political testamentary object: a material commendation of resistance to foreign intervention. [Facsimile/transcription of San Martín’s will](https://www.cultura.gob.ar/media/uploads/testamento_1818.pdf) [Instituto Nacional Sanmartiniano presentation](https://sanmartiniano.cultura.gob.ar/noticia/testamento-del-general-san-martin/)

San Martín died at Boulogne-sur-Mer on 17 August 1850. His son-in-law and executor, Mariano Balcarce, notified Rosas of the legacy on 30 August. The subsequent delivery is documented in the family and museum histories, although the exact transport itinerary and delivery date deserve renewed archival publication.

### 1850–1877: Juan Manuel de Rosas

**[D]** Rosas accepted the testamentary saber. After his fall from power in February 1852, he lived in exile near Southampton, England, and the weapon remained with him there.

**[L/A]** José María Rosa later wrote that Rosas intended to give the saber to Paraguayan president and marshal Francisco Solano López in imitation of San Martín’s own political bequest, but abandoned the plan after López’s death in 1870. This has circulated widely, but no executed transfer occurred, and the evidentiary basis is less secure than the wills. It should not be inserted as a custody link.

Rosas’s will directed that the saber pass to his friend Juan Nepomuceno Terrero, then to Terrero’s wife Juana Rábago, and after her death to their children in order of age. Because both designated adults predeceased Rosas, the weapon passed after Rosas’s death on 14 March 1877 to their eldest surviving child, Máximo Terrero.

### 1877–1897: Máximo Terrero and Manuela Rosas in England

**[D]** Máximo Terrero, married to Rosas’s daughter Manuela—usually called Manuelita—held the saber in England. Thus the physical object remained outside Argentina for roughly three-quarters of a century after San Martín’s departure.

In 1896 Museo Histórico Nacional founder and director Adolfo P. Carranza, assisted by former Rosas aide Antonino Reyes, sought its donation. Carranza wrote on 5 September 1896. Manuela replied on 26 November, accepting the patriotic object of the request and explaining the testamentary chain. A further letter dated 31 January 1897 described the Museo Histórico Nacional as the weapon’s intended final repository. The correspondence is central to the 2026 legal dispute over whether the donation imposed an enforceable condition or merely expressed the donors’ purpose. [Jorge María Ramallo, documentary account in the *Anales*](https://sanmartiniano.cultura.gob.ar/media/uploads/site-31/multimedia/tomo_17_anales.pdf)

The gift was made by the Terrero-Rosas family **to the Argentine nation for deposit in the Museo Histórico Nacional**, together with the scabbard and box, in the condition in which the family had received San Martín’s legacy. Sources differ casually in calling Manuela alone, Máximo alone, “the heirs of Rosas,” or the family the donor. The correspondence shows a family decision in which both spouses’ custody and wishes mattered.

### February–March 1897: Atlantic return

The saber left Southampton on 5 February 1897 aboard the Royal Mail steamship *Danube*. It arrived at La Plata on 28 February. It was transferred to the Argentine corvette *La Argentina*, reached Buenos Aires on 4 March, and was received at Dock 3 by a commission headed by Gen. Donato Álvarez. An act was drawn up at the Museo Histórico Nacional recording its delivery.

This is the best-documented transportation sequence in the object’s history and marks its conversion from privately inherited property into Argentine public patrimony.

### 1897–1963: Museo Histórico Nacional

For approximately sixty-six years the saber was exhibited or kept at the Museo Histórico Nacional in Buenos Aires. It became one of the museum’s principal “relics” of independence. Ernesto Quesada described it in *Las reliquias de San Martín* by 1901, giving the object a published museum-era physical description and a place in the early national relic canon.

### First theft: 12 August 1963

Shortly before 19:00, members of the Peronist Youth/Peronist Resistance entered the museum by deception after closing. Accounts identify the principal participants as Osvaldo Agosto, Manuel Gallardo, Arístides or Alcides Bonaldi, and Luis Sansoulet; some recollections describe five participants because an additional driver or support member is counted. They overpowered or confined museum personnel, broke or forced the display, removed the saber, and left political communiqués.

Their declared purpose was political and symbolic. Peronism had been proscribed since 1955, Arturo Illia had recently been elected in an election from which Peronism was excluded, and the thieves claimed the saber for “the Argentine youth.” Their demands included lifting political proscriptions, freeing political and CONINTES prisoners, returning Juan Perón, returning Eva Perón’s remains, punishment for the 1956 executions and the disappearance of Felipe Vallese, repudiation of petroleum and electricity agreements, and a break with the IMF. The demands vary by communiqué and should not be collapsed into a single list.

The search produced arrests and extensive police operations. Aníbal Demarco, to whom Agosto reportedly entrusted the weapon for concealment, negotiated through Capt. Adolfo César Philippeaux. The saber was returned secretly at Campo de Mayo. The exact day is inconsistently summarized as “days” or “weeks” later in popular accounts; Ramallo’s study, based on contemporary newspapers, is the preferable reconstruction.

After recovery, the weapon temporarily went to the Regimiento de Granaderos a Caballo for security. There were public and institutional efforts to have it returned to the museum, and it eventually was. [Commission for Memory archival collection](https://atom.comisionporlamemoria.net/index.php/informationobject/browse?collection=1824&creators=1753&media=print&sf_culture=es&showAdvanced=1&sort=referenceCode&sortDir=desc&subjects=67790&topLod=0) [Ramallo, *Historia del sable de San Martín*](https://institutorosas.cultura.gob.ar/media/uploads/site-35/multimedia/historia_del_sable_de_san_martin_jorge_maria_ramallo_ins_2009.pdf)

### Second theft: 19 August 1965–June 1966

On 19 August 1965 the saber was again stolen from the Museo Histórico Nacional by actors associated in the surviving institutional account with the Peronist Resistance. The second theft triggered thousands of searches and arrests during the first days.

The saber remained missing for roughly ten months and was recovered or surrendered in June 1966. The fullest published narratives differ over individual responsibility, hiding places, and the mechanism of return. Police and intelligence documentation should therefore be preferred to retrospective participant recollections wherever the two conflict.

The Policía Federal’s Scopometric Cabinet examined the recovered weapon to identify it and assess its characteristics. This recovery examination, followed by the multi-institutional 1966 technical program, materially strengthened the identification of the returned object as the museum’s saber. No published evidence has emerged that a copy was substituted during either disappearance.

### 1966–1967: examination and military custody

The Regimiento de Granaderos coordinated studies because no adequate technical dossier existed. The Comisión Nacional de Energía Atómica examined the blade; other state laboratories examined the leather, wood or horn, and nonferrous metal components; radiographic and physical measurements were undertaken.

Decree 8756 of 21 November 1967 transferred the saber’s definitive **guard and custody** to the Regimiento de Granaderos a Caballo “General San Martín.” The decree altered custody, not national ownership or the museum’s historical accession. A fortified display shrine or *templete blindado*, funded by the Banco Municipal de la Ciudad de Buenos Aires, was constructed for it at the Palermo barracks. [Decree 8756/1967 record](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/normativa/nacional/decreto-8756-1967-422951)

### 1967–2015: Palermo barracks

For forty-eight years the saber remained under military guard in the regiment’s Buenos Aires headquarters. It was displayed within the protected shrine rather than circulating as an ordinary service weapon. It acquired an additional ceremonial identity as a relic entrusted to the unit San Martín had founded.

### 2015: return to the Museo Histórico Nacional

Decree 843 of 18 May 2015 ordered the saber’s transfer to the Museo Histórico Nacional for permanent exhibition while leaving its security custody with the Granaderos. It returned publicly on 24–25 May 2015 as part of a San Martín exhibition. This was not a restitution of ownership—the state and museum collection identity had not ceased—but a return to the museum building under a divided arrangement: museum exhibition and conservation context, military security custody. [Decree 843/2015](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/normativa/nacional/decreto-843-2015-247033/texto)

### 2015–2026: museum display

The saber remained on display in the museum for approximately eleven years. The museum presented the two thefts as episodes in the political history of the object rather than omitting them. A copy or cast existed for interpretive and security purposes.

### 2025–2026: proposed removal and renewed dispute

In 2025 reports said President Javier Milei’s administration sought to use or transfer the saber for a political or commemorative ceremony. Museum director Gabriel Di Meglio opposed removing the original on conservation and institutional grounds and was dismissed in July 2025; the government did not publish a dismissal instrument stating that this dispute was the formal cause, so causation remains reported rather than conclusively documented.

On 2 February 2026 Decree 81/2026 ordered the saber moved to the regiment’s principal headquarters at Avenida Luis María Campos 554, Buenos Aires, for permanent custody, preservation, security, and integrity. It repealed Decree 843/2015. [Official text of Decree 81/2026](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/normativa/nacional/decreto-81-2026-422901/texto) [Official Gazette PDF](https://www.boletinoficial.gob.ar/pdf/aviso/primera/338115/20260203)

Acting museum director María Inés Rodríguez Aguilar resigned in disagreement. The Asociación Argentina de Investigadores en Historia objected that the transfer subordinated museum and public-access criteria to executive discretion. [AsAIH public statements](https://asaih.org.ar/category/declaraciones-y-documentos-publicos/)

Members of the Terrero family sought an interim injunction, arguing that the 1896–97 gift required the saber to remain in the Museo Histórico Nacional. On 5 February Judge Macarena Marra Giménez of Federal Contentious-Administrative Court No. 12 refused immediate suspension, finding at that preliminary stage that a legally enforceable donation “with charge” had not been demonstrated. She requested further information and controls; rejection of interim relief did not decide every underlying merits question. [Report on the ruling](https://noticiasargentinas.com/politica/sable-corvo--la-justicia-rechazo-una-cautelar-y-habilito-el-traslado-a-granaderos_a698523d7cbf033dd999a9497)

On 7 February 2026 the saber left the museum, traveled under security to San Lorenzo, Santa Fe—reportedly the first time the original had physically returned to the battlefield locality—and appeared at the 213th-anniversary ceremony. Milei ceremonially transferred it to regimental commander Lt. Col. Cristian Castellanos. It then went to the Granaderos’ Palermo headquarters. The museum retained and displays a cast or replica. [Official transfer notice](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/noticias/traspaso-del-sable-corvo-del-general-san-martin-granaderos) [Municipality of San Lorenzo account](https://prensa.sanlorenzo.gob.ar/acto_central/)

In April 2026 deputies introduced bill 1832-D-2026 to create a special patrimonial regime, repeal Decree 81/2026, and reinforce the saber’s museum context. As of the status date, the Chamber record lists referrals to the Culture, National Defense, and Constitutional Affairs committees, not enactment. [Chamber of Deputies, expediente 1832-D-2026](https://www.hcdn.gob.ar/proyectos/proyectoTP.jsp?exp=1832-D-2026)

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is the present saber the testamentary saber?

The evidence for continuity is unusually strong for a campaign weapon:

1. San Martín identified a particular saber in his 1844 will.
2. His executor notified Rosas after San Martín’s death.
3. Rosas possessed and devised it in England.
4. Máximo Terrero inherited it under Rosas’s will.
5. Terrero and Manuela Rosas corresponded with Carranza about its identity and sent it with its scabbard and box.
6. Its 1897 arrival and museum deposit were formally recorded.
7. It received an institutional catalogue identity, now MHNA1314.
8. Both recoveries after theft were followed by official inspection.

There is no published rival physical saber with a comparably documented claim to be the testamentary object.

### Vulnerable points

- The pre-1844 chain is largely biographical rather than administrative; no 1811 receipt survives.
- “Carried throughout the war” comes from San Martín himself, but no continuous campaign inventory independently tracks the object.
- Neither theft has a publicly available day-by-day forensic chain published in full.
- The possibility of substitution is theoretically conceivable because the weapon was missing twice, but no physical anomaly, competing object, confession, or documentary evidence supports it.
- The present scabbard and sword knot may have undergone repair or replacement. Public sources do not provide a complete component-by-component conservation genealogy.

### What the scientific work proved—and did not prove

The 1966 examination supports:

- a genuine crucible-steel or “Damascus” microstructure;
- preindustrial manufacture;
- affinity with Asian, probably Persian, blade-making traditions;
- absence of the regular welded symmetry expected in some European pattern-welded imitations.

It does **not** establish:

- manufacture in the city of Damascus;
- a named Persian smith;
- an exact century;
- that the hilt and scabbard were made with the blade;
- purchase in a particular London shop;
- participation in any specific battle;
- biological traces of combat.

No radiocarbon test is applicable to dating the steel in the straightforward way it would be to organic material. No published isotope, trace-element, dendrochronological, DNA, or blood-residue program has been located.

### Authenticating authorities

Authority over identity has moved among:

- San Martín and his testament;
- the Rosas and Terrero family;
- Carranza and the Museo Histórico Nacional;
- federal police examiners after the thefts;
- Army specialists and the Regimiento de Granaderos;
- CNEA metallurgists;
- the national executive, which governs custody;
- courts, which can determine legal consequences of the donation;
- professional museum and heritage bodies, which contest appropriate stewardship.

These authorities answer different questions. A metallurgist can characterize the blade; a museum establishes accession identity; a court interprets the donation; the executive can order custody subject to law. None alone proves the entire traditional biography.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

The saber’s original function was practical and military: a cavalry cutting weapon. Its pronounced curve places the center and cutting action well forward, favoring slashing from horseback. San Martín’s selection of a non-regulation oriental form also gave it visual distinctiveness.

Its later functions are more securely documented:

- **Testamentary commendation:** San Martín made it a material judgment on Rosas’s defense of sovereignty.
- **National relic:** Once accessioned in 1897 it became evidence of physical contact with the independence struggle.
- **Military lineage object:** Under Granaderos’ custody it materialized continuity between the regiment and its founder.
- **Political sign:** The 1963 and 1965 thieves claimed temporary possession as authority to link San Martín, Rosas, and Perón and to condemn proscription and foreign economic influence.
- **Presidential sign:** The 2015 museum return was framed as public recovery of national patrimony; the 2026 regimental return was framed by Milei as restoration of proper military-historical order.
- **Ceremonial relic:** At San Lorenzo in February 2026 the original was processed and formally handed to the unit commander. Milei called it “the most powerful material symbol of the Argentine nation” and a “sacred relic.” Those phrases document contemporary presidential sacralization, not an ancient title. [Presidential address](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/noticias/milei-definio-al-sable-corvo-como-el-simbolo-material-mas-poderoso-de-la-nacion-argentina)

No established traditional coronation, oath, healing, magical, or sacrificial ritual belongs to it. No curse is attested. Access is controlled for security and conservation rather than religious taboo.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### “Forged in Damascus”

**Tier: modern popular simplification.** Metallography found Damascus-type crucible steel; it did not locate the forge in Damascus. “Damascus” describes a metallurgical and visual tradition whose production networks extended well beyond that city.

### “A Persian saber”

**Tier: strong typological inference, not documented workshop provenance.** The blade’s curvature and carbide banding support Persian or related production. The lack of inscription or maker’s mark prevents a narrower attribution.

### “Bought in London in 1811”

**Tier: entrenched institutional tradition and plausible reconstruction.** The claim fits San Martín’s itinerary and the European trade in oriental arms, but no receipt identifies the transaction.

### “Carried in every battle”

**Tier: expansion of a first-person testamentary claim.** San Martín wrote that it accompanied him throughout the war. Later retellings convert accompaniment into personal use at every major engagement. The former is documented; the latter generally is not.

### “Invulnerable blade” or supernaturally sharp weapon

**Tier: romantic martial elaboration.** Good crucible-steel blades could possess excellent cutting properties, but no test establishes that this saber was unbreakable, uniquely sharp, or miraculous.

### Rosas intended it for Solano López

**Tier: later historical claim.** The story appears in revisionist historiography and is plausible within the politics of Rosas’s exile, but no transfer took place and the documentary basis requires stronger publication.

### The thefts “rescued” rather than stole it

**Tier: political rhetoric.** The object was unlawfully removed from a public museum. The participants described the act as temporary popular custody or revolutionary recovery. Legally and materially it was theft; politically they sought to redefine custody as symbolic reclamation.

### A replacement returned after a theft

**Tier: unsupported suspicion.** Periods of disappearance make the question legitimate, but police examination, institutional acceptance, and technical continuity support the recovered object. No rival original or substantiated substitution account exists.

### “San Martín’s saber never left its scabbard in civil conflict”

A genuine San Martín statement from 1819—that his saber would never leave its scabbard for political opinions—is frequently invoked in modern arguments about the object. It expresses a political ethic associated with the weapon, not a verifiable record that it was literally never drawn outside combat against royalists.

## 8. Current Status and Location

As of 6 September 2026, the original saber is under the permanent guard and custody of the **Regimiento de Granaderos a Caballo “General San Martín,” Avenida Luis María Campos 554, Palermo, Buenos Aires**, pursuant to Decree 81/2026.

It was transferred there after its ceremonial appearance at San Lorenzo on 7 February 2026. Government notices state that it is displayed in the regiment’s museum and remains accessible to the public in a controlled security setting. The Museo Histórico Nacional continues to display a cast or reproduction and retains the broader San Martín collection. Public sources consulted do not provide a stable accession-style gallery designation or detailed visiting schedule for the original; access should therefore be verified with the regiment before travel.

Legal ownership remains with the Argentine state as national historical patrimony. Decree 81 changed custody and location, not title. The object remains associated with MHN inventory **MHNA1314**.

The current controversy has three layers:

1. **Executive position:** the regiment supplies appropriate security, conservation, symbolic context, and continuity with the 1967 arrangement.
2. **Museum and historians’ position:** the Museo Histórico Nacional provides the proper professional, contextual, and publicly accountable setting; executive ceremonial priorities should not override museum stewardship.
3. **Terrero-family position:** the 1896–97 correspondence made the museum the final depositary and created an enforceable condition.

The February 2026 court decision refused emergency suspension because a binding charge was not then proven. Subsequent reporting states that a later request became moot after the transfer occurred. Neither that procedural outcome nor the physical move necessarily resolves every possible claim concerning the donation’s interpretation. Proposed national legislation to return or specially protect the saber remained at committee stage in the source record consulted.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Replicas and casts

Replicas are held or displayed by Argentine military, historical, and civic institutions. They serve three distinct purposes:

- security substitutes when the original cannot travel;
- interpretive objects allowing close viewing;
- ceremonial symbols capable of functioning independently of the original.

The Museo Histórico Nacional’s retained cast became especially important after February 2026 because visitors can still encounter the saber’s form within the museum collection from which the original was removed.

Replica makers sometimes reproduce the silhouette while substituting modern steel, standardized grips, invented decoration, or presentation inscriptions. Such objects should not be used as evidence for the original’s fine details.

### Visual and literary image

The curved saber became an attribute by which San Martín can be recognized in painting, monumental sculpture, schoolbook illustration, military insignia, commemorative graphics, documentaries, and political posters. This iconography often exaggerates its curvature or transforms it into a generic scimitar.

Ricardo Rojas’s epithet for San Martín, *El Santo de la Espada* (“The Saint of the Sword”), reinforced the civic-sacral vocabulary around his arms even though it did not technically identify this saber alone. Museum labels, official speeches, and journalism increasingly call the saber *reliquia*, culminating in Milei’s explicit 2026 phrase *reliquia sagrada*.

### Political mobilization

The saber’s strongest modern afterlife lies in repeated reinterpretation of San Martín’s 1844 bequest:

- Rosista and nationalist writers emphasize the validation of Rosas.
- Peronist militants in the 1960s used it to construct a San Martín–Rosas–Perón lineage centered on sovereignty and resistance to foreign power.
- The 2015 government emphasized democratic public museum access.
- The 2026 government emphasized the Army, Granaderos, liberty, and restoration of historical order.
- Critics of the 2026 transfer invoked professional museology, donor intent, institutional autonomy, and fear of partisan appropriation.

The object therefore has not carried one uncontested national message. Its material continuity has enabled successive groups to claim continuity with sharply different political projects.

### Film and commemoration

*El Sable*, a documentary about the 1963 theft, was recognized in a 2016 Chamber of Deputies cultural proposal. Anniversary journalism and participant interviews have transformed the thieves’ retrospective explanations into a substantial oral-history layer. These recollections are valuable but must be checked against police files and contemporaneous newspapers. [Chamber of Deputies project on the documentary](https://www.hcdn.gob.ar/comisiones/permanentes/ccultura/proyecto.html?exp=4116-D-2016)

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

The object’s historiography has developed in four principal phases.

### Relic cataloguing, 1897–early twentieth century

Carranza’s correspondence and museum documentation established the custody narrative. Ernesto Quesada’s *Las reliquias de San Martín* supplied an influential physical description and presented the weapon as national material testimony. These early treatments were close to the donors but often used reverential rather than modern conservation language.

### Documentary reconstruction

E. M. S. Danero’s 1950 article, “Así llegó a Buenos Aires el sable del Libertador,” reconstructed the 1897 return. Jorge María Ramallo’s *Historia del sable de San Martín* assembled wills, letters, and newspaper coverage; his later lecture on the centenary of the return reproduced key correspondence. This remains the essential narrative synthesis, though its patriotic vocabulary should be separated from its documentary quotations. The Archivo General de la Nación library catalogs a dedicated publication reproducing the wills, donation negotiations, and later vicissitudes. [AGN catalogue record](https://koha-agn.mininterior.gob.ar/cgi-bin/koha/opac-detail.pl?biblionumber=53095)

### Technical examination, 1966–69

The police Scopometric Cabinet, Army specialists, forestry/agricultural laboratories, public-works materials laboratories, and CNEA collectively examined the recovered ensemble. Capt. Oscar Augusto Blanco published a partial account in *La Prensa* on 14 May 1967. The regiment published *El sable corvo del Libertador de América* in 1969. Daniel Vassallo later explained the metallographic work.

Important limitations remain:

- full raw examination sheets and radiographs are not readily available online;
- sampling, magnification, imaging, and analytical tolerances are incompletely reported by modern standards;
- “Persian” is a typological conclusion, not a traceable production provenance;
- the component histories of grip, sword knot, and scabbard need modern conservation publication.

### Museum, memory, and political scholarship

Recent work treats the thefts as part of the Peronist Resistance and the saber as a contested national symbol. The Comisión Provincial por la Memoria holds a dedicated documentary grouping, “El robo del sable de San Martín,” covering 1958–65. A scholarly article in *Caravelle* analyzes the theft within political memory and militant performance. [CONICET-hosted *Caravelle* article](https://ri.conicet.gov.ar/bitstream/handle/11336/203698/CONICET_Digital_Nro.6020d9d5-e88a-455c-b241-8ec82a4bfff0_BB.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=5)

### Outstanding research questions

- Can a London arms dealer, auction, or importer be connected to the object?
- Can the blade be dated or geographically narrowed through modern non-destructive elemental, diffraction, or imaging techniques?
- Are the original 1966 radiographs and complete laboratory reports preserved?
- What repairs occurred before and after each theft?
- Are the current sword knot, scabbard furniture, and grip entirely those sent in 1897?
- What precise act and inventory entry formalized the museum accession?
- Did Rosas document an intended gift to Solano López?
- What is the complete judicial status of the Terrero-family action after the failed interim application?
- What security and environmental standards apply in the present regimental display?
- Will the full 2015 condition report and the documentation concerning the allegedly removed sword knot be published?
