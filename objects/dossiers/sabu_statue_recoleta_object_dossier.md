# The Liliana Crociati/Sabu bronze statue, Recoleta: Object Dossier

**Proposed filename:** `liliana_crociati_sabu_bronze_statue_recoleta_object_dossier.md`

**Research status:** 6 September 2026  
**Object type:** Outdoor funerary portrait group  
**Location:** Tomb of Liliana Crociati de Szaszak, Cementerio de la Recoleta, Junín 1760, Buenos Aires, Argentina  
**Artist:** Wifredo/Wilfredo Viladrich (1923–1976)  
**Date:** Probably 1972; commissioned after February 1970  
**Medium:** Cast bronze, chased and patinated  
**Subjects:** Liliana Crociati de Szaszak and her dog Sabú  
**Documentary caution:** The bronze is unquestionably real and remains in situ. Its associated history, however, has accumulated mutually incompatible accounts concerning Liliana’s age, the avalanche’s date and location, whether she was on her honeymoon, when Sabú died, and whether the dog was part of the original casting or added later. These questions are treated separately below.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Identity and scope

The object is a nearly life-size—or, according to one early local account, approximately two-metre-high—outdoor bronze funerary group representing Liliana Crociati de Szaszak standing beside a dog identified by an inscription as **Sabú**. It occupies a raised platform immediately outside the Crociati-Balduino family vault in the Cementerio de la Recoleta. The group, its architectural setting, and the nearby memorial inscription are commonly conflated under the names “the tomb of Liliana Crociati,” “Liliana and Sabú,” or simply “the statue of Liliana.” This dossier distinguishes:

1. the bronze figure of Liliana;
2. the bronze figure of Sabú;
3. their common base or podium and inscribed plaques;
4. the neo-Gothic vault behind them; and
5. the human remains and memorial furnishings inside the vault.

The physical monument tracked here is the bronze group, while the architectural and funerary ensemble is included where necessary to reconstruct the object’s commission, custody, meaning, and present condition.

No published municipal accession or inventory number for this particular bronze was found. That absence is unsurprising: it is a privately commissioned funerary work occupying a municipal cemetery concession, not an accessioned museum object.

### 1.2 Names

The following forms occur in sources:

- **Liliana Crociati de Szaszak**: the standard Argentine form, using *de Szaszak* as the married surname.
- **Liliana Crociati**: the shortened name most often applied to the sculpture.
- **Lili**: family nickname, documented by a later family source.
- **Liliana Szaszak**: the form used in Austrian and contemporary news reporting.
- **Lillian Szaszak**: an apparent German-language spelling or transcription in an Austrian avalanche report.
- **Sabú** or **Sabu**: the dog’s name. The accent is standard in most Spanish accounts; the base reportedly reads “Sabú, fiel amigo de Liliana.”
- **Wifredo Viladrich**, **Wilfredo Viladrich**, and occasionally corrupted **Wíeredovol Viladrich**: variant and erroneous renderings of the sculptor’s name. The artist’s dedicated website and Argentine museum sources favor **Wifredo Viladrich**; the Buenos Aires Legislature page uses **Wilfredo**.

No reliable source supplies an etymology for Sabú’s name. It should not be assumed to refer to the Indian actor Sabu Dastagir, despite the chronological possibility.

### 1.3 Form and iconography

The group is frontal and largely static. Liliana is represented as a slender young woman with a solemn, slightly lowered or distant expression. Her long, straight hair is centrally parted and falls on both sides of her face. She wears a long dress conventionally identified as her wedding gown. Ornament or lace-like motifs repeat around the sleeve ends, below the breast or waist, and at the hem. Her left ring finger bears a ring identified in popular accounts as either her wedding or engagement ring.

The hands are unusually elongated and visually prominent. Her right hand rests upon or strokes the dog’s head, making the human and animal figures a single emotional composition. Sabú stands or sits close against her right side, his head beneath her hand. The statue is not a literal death scene: it presents an idealized reunion of the deceased woman with a remembered companion.

A local architectural survey describes the figure as “languid and slender,” with especially long, delicate hands. A Brazilian scholarly discussion of funerary art identifies the work as signed **Wifredo Viladrich, 1972**, providing the strongest published date presently found. A Buenos Aires historical publication gives its height as approximately two metres and describes the wedding dress as having a fitted bodice and lace border. [Recoleta architectural survey](https://cementeriodelarecoletacaba.blogspot.com/2020/11/liliana-crociati-obra-de-wilfredo.html), [funerary-art study](https://pdfcoffee.com/historia-da-arte-funeraria-pdf-free.html), [Buenos Aires Legislature digital book](https://pergamo.legislatura.gob.ar/pgmedia/biblioteca/LIBROS%20DIGITALIZADOS/018085_D.pdf)

### 1.4 Material and manufacture

The group is bronze. A detailed Buenos Aires historical account describes the production sequence:

- Viladrich first modelled the head;
- he completed the figure in clay;
- a sectional negative mould was made in plaster;
- a positive plaster cast was obtained;
- the work was cast in bronze by the lost-wax process rather than sand or earth moulding;
- Viladrich personally supervised the work;
- the face and hands received particular attention during chasing;
- the surface was then patinated.

No published alloy analysis, foundry mark, foundry invoice, casting contract, weight, wall-thickness measurement, or metallographic examination was located. “Bronze” is therefore documentary and visually consistent identification, not a published scientific compositional result. [Buenos Aires Legislature digital book](https://pergamo.legislatura.gob.ar/pgmedia/biblioteca/LIBROS%20DIGITALIZADOS/018085_D.pdf)

### 1.5 Surface and condition

The bronze has developed a predominantly green to blue-green outdoor patina. Sabú’s muzzle is conspicuously brighter and more golden-brown because visitors repeatedly touch or rub it. This localized polishing is both physical wear and evidence of the monument’s continuing folkloric use.

Photographs made in 2001, 2005, 2007, 2018, 2022, and 2023 document the group in the same position. In the sources consulted, no break, major loss, detached limb, replacement casting, structural failure, or formal conservation campaign is securely documented. One recent visitor account reports that a barrier or tape had been placed around the statue, apparently to discourage touching, but this is an anecdotal observation rather than a municipal conservation notice.

The sculpture should not be described as undamaged: repeated handling has visibly altered the dog’s muzzle. The extent of corrosion, internal armature condition, base anchoring, cracking, wax coatings, and previous cleaning cannot be established from published material.

### 1.6 Inscriptions

The base identifies the dog as:

> **SABÚ, FIEL AMIGO DE LILIANA**

That is, “Sabú, faithful friend of Liliana.”

A plaque associated with the sculptural platform carries **“A mia figlia”** (“To my daughter”), an Italian lament composed by Liliana’s father, Giuseppe/Joseph/José Crociati. The poem repeatedly asks *perché?*—“why?”—and accuses envious nature or fate of destroying his beautiful daughter. It ends with the father affirming that he continually dreams of her and signs off as *Il tuo papà* (“Your father”). Published transcriptions contain grammatical, spelling, punctuation, and accent differences; no diplomatic transcription authenticated against the plaque or an original manuscript was found.

Another Latin text circulating in descriptions begins *Miserere nostri, Deus omnium, et respice nos…*, derived from Ecclesiasticus/Sirach 36:1 in the Vulgate: “Have mercy on us, God of all, and look upon us….” Some online accounts confuse this biblical text with Crociati’s Italian poem. They are separate inscriptions or textual elements. [Photographic transcription](https://www.flickr.com/photos/18212371%40N06/2913187959/), [Vulgate text](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?resource=bible-book-overview-Sir&search=Ecclesiasticus+36&tab=study&version=VULGATE%3BNR1994)

### 1.7 Architectural setting

The bronze stands before a markedly neo-Gothic vault with pointed arches and tall windows. A historical Buenos Aires account states that the Crociatis acquired an existing 1902 structure from the Congregation of the Daughters of the Divine Heart and rebuilt or substantially transformed it under the design and direction of Liliana’s mother, María Adriana Ana Balduino de Crociati.

The below-ground interior reportedly uses exposed brick and Spanish tiles and is organized with arched burial compartments recalling catacombs. Its furnishings have included photographs of Liliana, family oil paintings, a portrait attributed to one of her Fine Arts classmates, a ceramic dog made by her mother, and a red sari associated with Liliana’s travels to India. These objects are not components of the bronze but are part of its memorial environment. [Buenos Aires Legislature digital book](https://pergamo.legislatura.gob.ar/pgmedia/biblioteca/LIBROS%20DIGITALIZADOS/018085_D.pdf)

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Event prompting the commission

Liliana Crociati de Szaszak died following an avalanche in western Austria in February 1970. Her parents commissioned the funerary ensemble thereafter.

The best independent contemporary technical source is an Austrian federal forestry-research report cataloguing avalanche incidents. It records that on **25 February 1970** an avalanche struck the Hotel Piz Buin at Ischgl. It names Juan Szaszak, aged thirty-two, and his wife “Lillian,” aged twenty-six, both Argentine tourists. Juan was recovered after approximately fifteen minutes; she was recovered after an hour. Resuscitation initially succeeded, but she died in an Innsbruck clinic during the night of 26–27 February. The report places the couple in a first-floor room.

Contemporary Argentine press reports, preserved in reproduction, also date the avalanche to the 25th while appearing in newspapers on 26 February. They describe Juan as thirty-one, Liliana as twenty-four, and the room as being on the third floor. A funeral notice gives her death as **27 February 1970**. These contemporaneous records are stronger evidence than later tourism retellings, though they themselves disagree over ages and floor level. [Austrian avalanche report](https://www.zobodat.at/pdf/Mitteilungen-forstlichen-Bundes-Versuchsanstalt_95_1971_0001-0134.pdf), [reproduced Argentine reports and obituary](https://recoletacemetery.com/?cpage=1&p=646)

Later family testimony published by *La Nación* says that Liliana and Janos/Juan married on 17 October 1966 and were on a ski vacation, not a honeymoon. This directly disproves the most repeated tourism claim that she died on her honeymoon. The same testimony places the couple first at Val-d’Isère and then at Zürs am Arlberg, but the Austrian technical report places the Hotel Piz Buin event at Ischgl. The geographical discrepancy remains unresolved in the later family-derived narrative; the Austrian incident catalogue is the stronger source for the avalanche site. [La Nación investigation](https://www.lanacion.com.ar/lifestyle/la-tragica-historia-de-liliana-crociati-la-mujer-que-inspiro-la-escultura-emblema-del-cementerio-de-nid04122021/)

### 2.2 Commissioners and design brief

The commissioners were Liliana’s parents:

- **Giuseppe/Joseph/José Crociati**, a prominent Buenos Aires hairdresser, poet, and author of the memorial lament;
- **María Adriana Ana Balduino de Crociati**, a painter and the credited designer or director of the reconstructed vault.

They supplied Viladrich with extensive photographs of Liliana and their own recollections of her appearance. The requested iconography was a posthumous portrait in bridal dress, wearing a ring and accompanied by Sabú. The dress is not an exact sculptural copy of the surviving wedding photographs, according to later family testimony.

Ana Viladrich, the sculptor’s daughter, stated in 2021 that she retained her father’s manuscript describing the work and remembered the friendship that developed between Viladrich and the Crociati family during the commission. That manuscript is important unpublished primary evidence. Its contents, present location beyond family custody, and availability to scholars were not established. [La Nación investigation](https://www.lanacion.com.ar/lifestyle/la-tragica-historia-de-liliana-crociati-la-mujer-que-inspiro-la-escultura-emblema-del-cementerio-de-nid04122021/)

### 2.3 Date

The commission necessarily postdates February 1970. The most specific published object date found is the signature/date **1972**, recorded in a scholarly work on funerary art. A contemporary photograph reproduced by *La Nación* shows Viladrich beside the completed sculpture shortly before its installation but supplies no exact date.

Accordingly:

- **1970**: sometimes assigned by unsourced or student catalogues; probably shorthand for the death and commission.
- **1972**: strongest published date for the completed signed sculpture.
- **By 1976**: absolute latest possible date for direct completion by Viladrich, who died on 13 May 1976.

### 2.4 Artist

Wifredo/Wilfredo Viladrich was born in Buenos Aires on 18 April 1923, the son of Catalan painter Miguel Viladrich and painter Ana Morera. After childhood in Spain and Morocco, the family fled Spain in 1939 and returned to Argentina. He began modelling in Catamarca, received a National Salon prize in 1942, and subsequently developed a career in portrait and public monumental sculpture. His major commissions included monuments to General Juan Martín de Pueyrredón and Admiral Guillermo Brown. He died in Buenos Aires on 13 May 1976. [Buenos Aires Legislature biography](https://cultura.legislatura.gob.ar/cultura_posts/artistas-en-nuestra-casa-wilfredo-viladrich697.html), [artist’s award chronology](https://wifredoviladrich.com/trayectoria/premios-obtenidos/)

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 Before casting: source images and models

No evidence indicates that Liliana posed from life for this specific sculpture. The documented modelling sources were family photographs and parental descriptions supplied after her death. Sabú was likewise reconstructed from memory and photographs.

The physical sources of the bronze alloy—mine, supplier, scrap source, merchant, or foundry—are unknown. The clay original, plaster negative, working plaster, wax model, and any mould sections have not been located in published collections. Their survival is unresolved.

### 3.2 1970: temporary burial at Chacarita

Liliana’s funeral notice invited mourners to accompany her remains to Chacarita Cemetery. *La Nación* reported in 2021 that she was initially placed there and later transferred to Recoleta after the vault and sculpture were ready. This is the documented seam between death and final memorialization; accounts that imply immediate burial beneath the present statue omit the temporary Chacarita stage. [Reproduced obituary](https://recoletacemetery.com/?cpage=1&p=646), [La Nación investigation](https://www.lanacion.com.ar/lifestyle/la-tragica-historia-de-liliana-crociati-la-mujer-que-inspiro-la-escultura-emblema-del-cementerio-de-nid04122021/)

### 3.3 Acquisition of the Recoleta structure

A Buenos Aires historical account says Liliana’s parents acquired a structure dating from 1902 from the **Congregación Hijas del Divino Corazón**. Her mother directed its transformation into the present neo-Gothic memorial. No deed, concession title, sale price, permit, architectural drawing, or exact transaction date was found online.

This transaction concerned the burial concession and building, not an independent sale of the bronze. Under current Buenos Aires cemetery law, such plots are public administrative concessions for use rather than ordinary freehold land. [Buenos Aires Legislature digital book](https://pergamo.legislatura.gob.ar/pgmedia/biblioteca/LIBROS%20DIGITALIZADOS/018085_D.pdf), [Buenos Aires mortuary-law definitions](https://boletinoficial.buenosaires.gob.ar/files/digesto/Anexo_I_DServiciosDeLaAdministracion_2022.pdf)

### 3.4 Circa 1970–1972: workshop custody

Viladrich held or controlled the work through modelling, mould-making, lost-wax casting, chasing, and patination. No evidence identifies the external foundry, if any, or establishes whether the bronze was cast in one pour or multiple welded sections. A photograph showing Viladrich beside the completed work establishes the sculpture’s existence before installation and links the extant design to the artist.

### 3.5 Circa 1972: installation at Recoleta

The completed bronze was installed outside the reconstructed family vault. The best available dating is 1972. Liliana’s widower, Janos “Jancsi” Szaszak, reportedly attended the installation but objected to the monument, regarding the occasion as a spectacle and believing Liliana would not have approved. This claim comes through his later widow and should be treated as family testimony rather than a contemporary recorded objection. [La Nación investigation](https://www.lanacion.com.ar/lifestyle/la-tragica-historia-de-liliana-crociati-la-mujer-que-inspiro-la-escultura-emblema-del-cementerio-de-nid04122021/)

### 3.6 Later family burials

The vault also contains or has contained members of the Balduino-Crociati family, including Liliana’s maternal grandfather, architect Eduardo Balduino, and her parents. Their burial there did not move the bronze; instead the statue remained the ensemble’s dominant public identifier.

### 3.7 Custody from installation to the present

From installation through September 2026, every located dated photograph places the bronze at the same Recoleta vault. There is no documented sale, museum accession, loan, wartime evacuation, confiscation, theft, relocation, deaccession, or replacement.

Custody is divided conceptually:

- the memorial and burial concession are associated with the Crociati/Balduino concession holders or successors;
- the cemetery land and regulatory framework belong to the City of Buenos Aires;
- conservation and alteration occur within municipal cemetery and historic-area rules.

The current registered concession holder was not identified in public sources. No present ownership litigation specific to this vault was found.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Authenticity of the bronze

There is no serious published allegation that the extant bronze is a copy, forgery, modern replacement, or misattributed work. Its authenticity rests on mutually reinforcing evidence:

- the artist’s recorded signature and 1972 date;
- a photograph of Viladrich with the finished statue before installation;
- testimony from his daughter concerning the commission and surviving manuscript;
- a technical account of his modelling and casting process;
- continuous photographic documentation in situ.

No scientific examination has been published, but none is presently required to distinguish the work from a rival claimant: there is no rival sculpture.

### 4.2 Was Sabú part of the original group?

This is the principal object-identity dispute.

**Version A: integral original composition.** The commissioners asked Viladrich to portray Liliana with Sabú, and the sculptor produced the unified group. The physical placement of Liliana’s hand on the dog’s head strongly supports prior compositional planning. The Buenos Aires historical account describes the family photographs and casting as one continuous commission. Later family testimony says Sabú died years after Liliana, but that does not preclude his being depicted while still alive.

**Version B: dog added later.** Numerous English, Italian, Portuguese, and tourism accounts claim that Sabú’s statue was added after the dog’s death, sometimes alleging that Liliana’s right arm was altered to accommodate it. No dated photograph of Liliana standing without the dog, no second contract, no documented recasting, and no conservation evidence of an altered arm has been produced.

**Assessment:** The surviving evidence favors Sabú having been conceived as part of the original ensemble. The “added later” account is widespread but undocumented. It may have grown from the idea that the dog died after Liliana, combined with an assumption that memorialization followed each death sequentially.

### 4.3 Was the dress literally Liliana’s wedding dress?

The sculpture indisputably presents bridal iconography, and multiple sources identify the garment as her wedding dress. Family photographs published in 2021 reportedly show that the bronze dress is not an exact replica. The most defensible description is therefore **a sculptural interpretation of Liliana in bridal dress**, based on but not mechanically copying the wedding garment.

A separate municipal tourism claim says she was buried in that wedding dress. No burial record or coffin examination supporting that statement was found.

### 4.4 Date and age discrepancies

Published dates give Liliana’s birth year as either **1944** or **1945**, and contemporary press reported her age as twenty-four while an Austrian technical report gives twenty-six. The monument is commonly described as portraying a twenty-six-year-old. Without a civil birth certificate or legible monument date, the precise birth year remains unresolved here.

The death notice and Austrian report support death on 27 February 1970. Sources that give 26 February generally conflate the avalanche or hospitalization date with the death.

### 4.5 Location of the avalanche

Later narratives alternately place the incident at Innsbruck, Zürs, Ischgl, Switzerland, Italy, or vaguely “the Alps.” The Austrian federal incident report is specific: the Hotel Piz Buin event involving the Szaszaks occurred at **Ischgl, Tyrol**. “Innsbruck” was the location of the clinic, not the hotel. “Swiss Alps” and “Italy” are errors.

### 4.6 Scientific testing and conservation evidence

No radiography, alloy analysis, 3-D scan, corrosion survey, casting-seam study, archival foundry research, or paint/patina analysis was located. No published forensic study has tested the “later dog” hypothesis. Authority to authenticate the work remains art-historical and documentary, principally with the Viladrich family archive, municipal cemetery records, and the signature on the bronze.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

- **Liliana Crociati de Szaszak (c. 1944/45–1970)** — Subject of the portrait and person memorialized. The statue was made posthumously from photographs and recollections.

- **Sabú** — Dog represented beside Liliana. Reliable later testimony says he did not die simultaneously with her. An earlier Buenos Aires account instead calls him a childhood dog already dead when the work was commissioned. The dog’s actual lifespan remains unresolved.

- **Wifredo/Wilfredo Viladrich (1923–1976)** — Sculptor, modeller, supervisor of casting, chaser, and patinator. The strongest published object date is 1972.

- **Giuseppe/Joseph/José Crociati** — Commissioner, Liliana’s father, and author of the Italian lament fixed to the monument. His name appears in different national or linguistic forms.

- **María Adriana Ana Balduino de Crociati** — Commissioner, painter, and designer/director of the neo-Gothic vault. She supplied visual and personal information to Viladrich.

- **Janos/Juan “Jancsi” Szaszak** — Liliana’s husband and survivor of the avalanche. Later family testimony says he disliked the monument and attended only its installation. He neither owned nor subsequently moved the sculpture in any documented account.

- **Ana Viladrich** — Sculptor’s daughter and present or recent custodian of a manuscript in which her father described the commission.

- **Trixi/Trixie Bongartz Brunner** — Later wife of Janos Szaszak and principal family informant for the 2021 *La Nación* article. Her testimony corrected the honeymoon and simultaneous-death legends.

- **Congregación Hijas del Divino Corazón** — Previous holder of the older 1902 Recoleta structure acquired by the Crociatis.

- **Cementerio de la Recoleta / Government of the City of Buenos Aires** — Physical site, regulator, public custodian of the cemetery, and promoter of the sculpture as a visitor attraction.

- **Tour guides, photographers, cemetery bloggers, and visitors** — Not custodians in the proprietary sense, but collectively responsible for the object’s transformation into a famous urban icon and tactile good-luck object.

- **God, fate, and nature** — Not beings represented by the bronze. They enter the ensemble through the father’s poem, which questions divine justice and personifies envious nature and destructive destiny.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Original function

The group is a private funerary memorial. Its primary functions were portraiture, mourning, commemoration, and the symbolic restoration of companionship. It places Liliana outside the family vault not as a recumbent corpse or angelic allegory but as an apparently living young woman in ceremonial dress, physically reunited with Sabú.

The bridal gown freezes a socially charged moment of adult identity. The dog serves as an emblem of loyalty, childhood memory, and enduring companionship. The father’s poem supplies a voice of unresolved parental grief rather than conventional consolation.

### 6.2 Funerary use

The sculpture marks and visually identifies the burial place. Flowers have been placed on or around the figure; a 2023 academic photograph documents a bouquet supported by the statue’s arm. The vault has also been surrounded with planted roses or greenery, although accounts disagree on whether Liliana liked cut flowers.

There is no documented formal annual liturgy, municipal ceremony, blessing rite, or anniversary procession centered on the bronze.

### 6.3 Tactile good-luck practice

Visitors rub or touch Sabú’s muzzle for good luck. The practice has physically polished away the green patina at the nose, leaving a bright bronze surface. This is an informal vernacular ritual, not a rite prescribed by the family, church, artist, or cemetery administration.

The earliest date of the practice remains uncertain. Online photographic and travel accounts document it by the 2000s; later municipal pages explicitly promote or repeat it as Recoleta folklore. The city’s own retelling says the muzzle is worn by visitors who believe touching it brings luck. [Buenos Aires municipal folklore page](https://buenosaires.gob.ar/gcaba_historico/noticias/mitos-y-leyendas-del-cementerio-de-la-recoleta-el-lado-desconocido-de), [Buenos Aires heritage-tourism article](https://buenosaires.gob.ar/gcaba_historico/noticias/las-joyas-arquitectonicas-del-cementerio-de-la-recoleta-un-viaje-al)

### 6.4 Symbolic career

Sabú has become a symbol of fidelity, while the complete group functions as an emblem of tragic young love. These meanings partly depend on inaccurate stories—especially the honeymoon and simultaneous deaths—but their effect upon visitor behavior is real. The monument is now one of the cemetery’s most frequently photographed non-state memorials.

It confers no office, sovereignty, priesthood, dynastic legitimacy, or formal civic status. Its authority is affective and touristic rather than juridical or political.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 “Liliana died on her honeymoon”

**Classification:** Later popular legend; contradicted by family evidence.

The standard story says Liliana and her new husband were honeymooning in the Alps when the avalanche struck. Their wedding occurred on 17 October 1966, more than three years before the accident. They were on a ski vacation. The bridal dress of the bronze probably encouraged the retrospective conversion of the trip into a honeymoon.

### 7.2 “Sabú died at the same moment in Buenos Aires”

**Classification:** Urban legend; contradicted by the strongest family testimony.

The legend states that Sabú died in Buenos Aires at the precise moment Liliana died in Austria, demonstrating a supernatural bond. A 2021 family source says the dog died naturally many years later. The City of Buenos Aires now identifies the simultaneous-death story as legend.

There remains a conflict with an older Buenos Aires historical account, which says Sabú had already died and was remembered as Liliana’s childhood companion. Neither version has been supported by veterinary records or a dated photograph establishing the dog’s lifespan. What can be rejected is the supposed evidentiary certainty of supernatural simultaneity.

### 7.3 “The dog was added after its death”

**Classification:** Widespread but unverified elaboration.

This story depends on Sabú surviving Liliana and assumes that the sculpture initially represented Liliana alone. Some versions add that Viladrich altered her arm. No object-based or archival evidence was found. The composition and commission history favor an original paired design.

### 7.4 “The widower died or vanished in the avalanche”

**Classification:** False early web legend.

Janos/Juan survived, remarried, had children, and died of a heart attack in 1996. Contemporary press and the Austrian avalanche report both document his rescue. Online claims that his body was never recovered are demonstrably false.

### 7.5 “A mysterious man leaves flowers”

**Classification:** Unverified cemetery legend.

Several accounts say an unknown man, sometimes identified as the surviving husband or his ghost, regularly left flowers. One older Buenos Aires account reports an unidentified young man leaving a flower and English verses inside the vault. The source does not identify him or document the observation’s date and frequency. There is no evidence that the visitor was Szaszak, still less his spirit.

The City’s 2024 folklore article repeats a variant in which the husband’s spirit continues to visit and leave flowers, clearly presenting it as what “some maintain,” not as documented fact.

### 7.6 “Touching Sabú brings good luck”

**Classification:** Modern vernacular practice and belief.

No causal claim is testable from the historical record. The belief is nevertheless materially attested by the polished muzzle. Its practical consequence is localized wear to the bronze.

### 7.7 “The mausoleum reproduces Liliana’s bedroom”

**Classification:** Institutional tradition, incompletely documented.

The municipal tourism office states that the parents reproduced her bedroom in the vault. A more detailed historical description instead presents a catacomb-like burial interior decorated with photographs, paintings, ceramics, Spanish tiles, and personal objects. No architectural plan or contemporary commission document confirms a literal room-for-room reproduction. The municipal statement should therefore be treated as interpretive tradition rather than verified exact replication.

### 7.8 “She was killed in Switzerland, Italy, or Innsbruck”

**Classification:** Geographical corruption.

The documented avalanche occurred in Tyrol, Austria. Innsbruck was the hospital destination. Switzerland and Italy are later errors.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Tourism

The bronze has become one of Recoleta’s signature visitor objects. The official Buenos Aires tourism site singles out the tomb among the cemetery’s “curiosities,” while city heritage articles describe it as one of the most visited vaults. Its appeal differs from that of the cemetery’s presidential or military monuments: it is founded upon intimate grief, figurative accessibility, animal companionship, and a story easily adapted into guided-tour folklore.

### 9.2 Photography and digital circulation

The object has an extensive photographic afterlife:

- photographs from the early 2000s circulated through Flickr and cemetery blogs;
- the AfterLife Recoleta project began a dedicated discussion in 2009 and reproduced contemporary 1970 newspaper clippings;
- Wikimedia Commons now hosts a category and downloadable images;
- a University of Central Florida digital collection includes detailed photographs of the statues and plaque;
- social-media and travel-platform images continually reinforce the bright-nosed Sabú ritual.

These images have been essential for documenting continuity of location and surface appearance, but their captions often reproduce the honeymoon and simultaneous-death legends.

### 9.3 Scholarly and artistic treatment

The monument appears in studies of funerary discourse, cemetery communication, tourism, and memorial sculpture. A Brazilian study uses the father’s lament to illustrate how funerary inscriptions can foreground the survivors’ grief. A 2025 Mexican dissertation treats the group as an unusually popular non-national monument whose emotional communication rivals the cemetery’s official heroic memorials. [Funerary-art study](https://pdfcoffee.com/historia-da-arte-funeraria-pdf-free.html), [UASLP dissertation](https://repositorioinstitucional.uaslp.mx/xmlui/bitstream/handle/i/9675/TesisD.FCSyH.2025.COMUNICACI%C3%93N.REYNA.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1)

### 9.4 Replicas, merchandise, and forgery

No authorized full-size replica, reduced commercial bronze, museum cast, surviving plaster edition, or documented forgery was found. Tourist photographs, postcards, online illustrations, and likely informal souvenirs reproduce its image, but no catalogue raisonné of these derivatives exists.

No evidence indicates that the current Recoleta bronze is a replica replacing a stolen or destroyed original.

### 9.5 Fiction and supernatural reception

The monument appears in literary and online cemetery narratives that imagine Recoleta’s dead walking at night. Such works draw upon the real bronze but freely combine it with the “simultaneous death,” honeymoon, and ghostly-flower legends. These are modern creative uses, not sources for the commission history.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Evidence hierarchy

The available evidence falls into five principal groups:

1. **Contemporary primary reporting, 1970:** Associated Press and United Press reports in *La Nación* and *La Prensa*, plus Liliana’s funeral notice.
2. **Official Austrian technical record, 1971:** the federal avalanche incident catalogue.
3. **Local historical account:** the Buenos Aires publication digitized by the city legislature, apparently based on cemetery and family information and providing the fullest manufacturing narrative.
4. **Artist-family evidence:** Ana Viladrich’s recollection and her father’s unpublished manuscript.
5. **Later family testimony:** Trixi Bongartz Brunner’s account and photographs published in 2021.

Municipal tourism material is authoritative for location, access, and the city’s present institutional presentation, but it is not consistently reliable on personal details. Travel blogs and multilingual Wikipedia derivatives preserve the reception history but often repeat one another.

### 10.2 Principal disputes

#### Date of the bronze

The 1972 signed date is the strongest published claim. “1970” lacks equivalent object-level support.

#### Birth year

Both 1944 and 1945 circulate. A civil record is needed.

#### Avalanche chronology

The Austrian report records the avalanche on 25 February and death during the night leading to 27 February. Later retellings often use 26 February for one or both events.

#### Avalanche location

The official Austrian report’s Ischgl identification conflicts with later Zürs narratives. The likely mechanism of error is confusion among the couple’s itinerary, resort names, the hotel name, and Innsbruck hospital reporting.

#### Sabú’s lifespan

Three incompatible versions exist:

- died the same day as Liliana;
- died before Liliana and was remembered as a childhood dog;
- died naturally years afterward.

The first is explicitly identified as legend. The second and third require archival resolution.

#### Original versus later dog figure

No material study has resolved the question, but the integrated hand position and commission account favor a single original composition.

### 10.3 Lost or inaccessible evidence

Research would materially benefit from:

- Liliana’s civil birth certificate;
- the cemetery transfer and interment registers from Chacarita to Recoleta;
- the Crociati concession title and transaction with the religious congregation;
- building permits and plans for the reconstructed vault;
- Viladrich’s manuscript in his daughter’s possession;
- workshop photographs and correspondence;
- foundry accounts or invoices;
- a direct examination of the artist’s signature and date;
- early photographs showing the exact installation configuration;
- veterinary or family records dating Sabú’s death;
- municipal maintenance and conservation files.

### 10.4 Discredited but persistent theories

The following claims remain popular despite contrary evidence:

- that Liliana was on her honeymoon;
- that the husband died or vanished;
- that the avalanche occurred in Switzerland or Italy;
- that Sabú’s simultaneous death is documented;
- that the dog figure was certainly added later;
- that every flower at the tomb is left by the widower or his ghost.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and near-primary evidence

- Austrian federal forestry-research avalanche report, *Mitteilungen der Forstlichen Bundes-Versuchsanstalt*, no. 95 (1971). It provides the most independent technical account of the avalanche, identifies the Szaszaks, and records rescue intervals and death.
- Contemporary *La Nación* and *La Prensa* reports of 26 February 1970 and the subsequent funeral notice, reproduced by the AfterLife Recoleta documentation project.
- Photograph of Wifredo Viladrich beside the completed bronze before installation, reproduced in *La Nación*.

- Ana Viladrich’s testimony concerning the commission and her father’s surviving manuscript.
- The bronze, plaques, wear patterns, and dated photographs of the monument itself.

### Institutional and scholarly sources

- Government of the City of Buenos Aires pages for the cemetery, its heritage, visiting arrangements, legal regime, and official folklore.
- Buenos Aires Legislature biographical material on Viladrich.
- Buenos Aires historical publication digitized by the Legislature, giving the fullest technical account of the statue’s making and vault acquisition.
- Wifredo Viladrich estate or family website, especially its award chronology.
- Scholarship on funerary art and cemetery communication.
- University photographic collections and Wikimedia Commons for comparative visual documentation.

### Source-critical conclusion

The reconstructable object history is relatively short and stable: commissioned after Liliana’s 1970 death, modelled from photographs by Wifredo Viladrich, cast in bronze by lost wax, probably completed and installed in 1972, and continuously displayed at the Crociati family vault ever since. No theft, removal, reconstruction, or substitute casting is documented.

The legend history is more volatile. Bridal iconography converted a ski vacation into a honeymoon; the paired portrait generated a supernatural simultaneous-death story; uncertainty about Sabú’s lifespan produced the claim that his figure was added later; and ordinary flower offerings developed into stories of an anonymous lover or visiting ghost. These legends have materially changed the object through repeated touching, tourism, photography, and official promotion. The brightly polished muzzle is consequently both surface damage and the most direct physical archive of the statue’s second life.
