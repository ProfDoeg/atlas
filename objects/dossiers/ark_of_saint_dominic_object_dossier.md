# The Ark of Saint Dominic, Bologna: Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `the_ark_of_saint_dominic_bologna_object_dossier.md`  
**Research status:** Current to 6 September 2026.

## Scope and evidentiary note

“The Ark of Saint Dominic” is not a single object made at one moment. It is a composite reliquary-monument assembled, altered, moved, damaged, dismantled, conserved, and augmented between the thirteenth and eighteenth centuries. It contains—or is architecturally associated with—the relics traditionally and ecclesiastically identified as those of Dominic of Caleruega, founder of the Order of Preachers.

For clarity, this dossier distinguishes:

1. Dominic’s original earth grave of 1221;
2. the plain stone or marble tomb installed in 1233;
3. the sculpted marble sarcophagus made in 1264–67;
4. its lost or dispersed medieval supports;
5. the saint’s cypress-wood inner coffin;
6. the separately housed skull and its metalwork reliquary of 1383;
7. Niccolò dell’Arca’s marble crowning structure of 1469–73;
8. Michelangelo’s and Girolamo Coltellini’s completion sculptures;
9. Alfonso Lombardi’s predella of 1532;
10. Jean-Baptiste Boudard’s altar frontal of 1768;
11. the surrounding chapel and altar, which frame but are not wholly identical with the Ark.

Statements concerning miraculous fragrance, incorruption, healing, or supernatural intervention are reported as medieval or institutional testimony, not as scientific findings.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Names

The normal Italian name is **Arca di San Domenico**, literally “Ark of Saint Dominic.” In medieval and early-modern usage, Italian *arca* and Latin *arca* could designate a chest, coffin, reliquary, or monumental sarcophagus. The term here does not imply a boat.

Other forms include:

- **Arca di san Domenico**;
- **Arca del Santo**, “the Saint’s Ark”;
- **Sepolcro di san Domenico**, “Tomb of Saint Dominic”;
- Latin **arca sancti Dominici**, **sepulcrum sancti Dominici**, or equivalent formulations;
- English **Ark**, **Shrine**, or **Tomb of Saint Dominic**;
- French **Sé­pulcre de saint Dominique**;
- Spanish **Arca de Santo Domingo**;
- German **Arca di San Domenico** or **Grabmal des heiligen Dominikus**.

The national catalogue identifies the ensemble as **“Arca di San Domenico (monumento funebre)”**, catalogue identifier **0800024749**. The catalogue record treats it as a state-owned historic or artistic property in the Basilica of San Domenico, Piazza San Domenico 13, Bologna. [Italian national cultural-heritage catalogue](https://dati.cultura.gov.it/lodview-arco/resource/HistoricOrArtisticProperty/0800024749.html)

### Class and present composition

It is simultaneously:

- a reliquary tomb;
- a monumental marble sarcophagus;
- a sculptural hagiographic cycle;
- an altar ensemble;
- a continuing site of Catholic veneration;
- an institutional monument of the Order of Preachers;
- a civic monument associated with Bologna.

The present vertical ensemble, from bottom to top, comprises:

1. Boudard’s eighteenth-century marble altar frontal;
2. the altar and architectural base;
3. Alfonso Lombardi’s Renaissance predella or stele;
4. the thirteenth-century relief-covered sarcophagus;
5. Niccolò dell’Arca’s elaborate fifteenth-century crowning structure;
6. statues completed by Niccolò, Michelangelo, and Girolamo Coltellini.

The head reliquary stands behind the Ark in a protected recess and should be catalogued as a related but physically distinct object.

### Materials and techniques

Documented or visibly identifiable materials include:

- carved white marble, catalogued in individual sculptures as Carrara marble;
- gilded-glass or vitreous inlays in the backgrounds of the thirteenth-century reliefs;
- a cypress-wood inner coffin for the principal bodily remains;
- metal fittings and protective closures;
- separately, silver, gilding, enamel, engraving, and repoussé work in Jacopo Roseto’s head reliquary.

No published petrographic study locating every marble block to a particular quarry was found. “Carrara marble” is securely catalogued for Michelangelo’s *Saint Proculus*, but it should not automatically be extended to every component without material analysis. [Catalogue record for *Saint Proculus*](https://catalogo.beniculturali.it/detail/HistoricOrArtisticProperty/0800024759)

A dependable overall measurement or weight for the composite monument was not found in the consulted public catalogue material. Measurements sometimes circulated online refer to individual statues—Michelangelo’s *Saint Proculus*, for example, is about 58.5 cm high—not to the Ark as a whole. Claims giving a single measurement for “the Ark” must specify whether they mean the sarcophagus, the crowning structure, or the entire altar ensemble.

### The thirteenth-century sarcophagus

The rectangular marble chest is carved on all four sides. Six principal narrative reliefs recount Dominic’s life and miracles:

- the healing and reception into the Order of Reginald of Orléans;
- papal approval of the Order;
- the raising or healing of the young Napoleone Orsini after a fall from a horse;
- the miracle of the book rejected by fire;
- angels bringing bread to the friars’ table;
- Peter and Paul entrusting Dominic with the Order’s preaching mission.

Standing figures divide the scenes. Christ and the Virgin and Child occupy central positions on the long sides. Four corner figures were traditionally called Doctors of the Church, but modern scholarship has questioned that identification because their attributes do not correspond consistently to the four Latin Doctors. [Treccani, Nicola Pisano](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/nicola-pisano_(Dizionario-Biografico)/)

The relief backgrounds retain or once carried gilded vitreous inlay. The sculptural narrative is continuous and densely populated, combining Romanizing forms associated with Nicola Pisano with more angular Gothic handling attributed variously to members of his shop.

No securely attested maker’s signature survives on the sarcophagus. Attribution rests on documentary tradition and stylistic analysis, not a contemporary signature or surviving commission contract.

### Original supports: lost, replaced, or dispersed

The sarcophagus originally stood elevated on sculpted or columnar supports, making it visible and accessible from several sides. Medieval sources call the supports columns; a seventeenth-century Dominican account said the Ark had been carried by twelve angels, three on each side.

Cesare Gnudi proposed that two surviving groups of three acolytes or clerics—now in the Museo Nazionale del Bargello, Florence, and the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston—were original telamons from the Ark. This reconstruction has attracted substantial support, including Anita Moskowitz’s, but remains inferential.

John Pope-Hennessy proposed adding two archangels now in the Victoria and Albert Museum and a female Virtue now in the Louvre. Later scholarship has generally been more skeptical. The Louvre continues to catalogue its figure only as having been considered one of the supports. [Louvre collections record](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark%3A/53355/cl010093831)

Thus:

- the original support system is not intact;
- its number, arrangement, and iconography are unresolved;
- sculptures in Florence and Boston are strong but not universally accepted candidates;
- the London and Paris candidates are weaker or rejected in some recent accounts.

### Niccolò dell’Arca’s crowning structure

The marble *cimasa*, commissioned in 1469 and erected in 1473, transforms the formerly flat-topped chest into a tall, pyramidal reliquary monument.

Its program ascends from local saints to redemption and creation:

- eight saints or civic protectors around the lower cornice;
- the four Evangelists;
- the dead or suffering Christ between angels;
- putti, fruit garlands, dolphins, and volutes;
- God the Father with globes at the summit.

The eight lower saints are now identified as Francis, Petronius, Dominic, Florian, Agricola, John the Baptist, Proculus, and Vitalis. Several were unfinished or absent when Niccolò died.

The surviving contract was dated **20 July 1469**. It involved Niccolò “de Apulia,” Bologna’s governor Giovanni Battista Savelli, the city elders, and the Sixteen Reformers. Niccolò presented a model. The inclusion of civic officers shows that the Ark had become a Bolognese civic monument as well as a Dominican reliquary. It was erected on **16 July 1473**, still incomplete. [Treccani, Niccolò dell’Arca](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/niccolo-dell-arca_(Dizionario-Biografico)/)

### Michelangelo’s sculptures

In 1494–95 the young Michelangelo Buonarroti supplied or completed:

- *Saint Petronius*, holding a model of Bologna;
- *Saint Proculus*;
- the right-hand kneeling candle-bearing angel.

The records and early accounts leave open whether Michelangelo began every figure from a fresh block. An early custodian’s wording that he worked on the “clothes” of *Saint Petronius*, and stylistic observations concerning *Saint Proculus*, have supported the possibility that Niccolò began one or more blocks.

Michelangelo’s angel survives in the ensemble. *Saint Proculus* lacks the object formerly held in its raised right hand, variously identified as an axe, palm, or weapon. The national catalogue records the missing attribute and identifies the marble as Carrara marble.

### Damage to *Saint Proculus*

On **4 August 1572**, the eve of Dominic’s feast, a lay brother named Pellegrino knocked the statue down while working at the Ark. Fra Ludovico da Prelormo recorded that it shattered into more than fifty pieces and was repaired.

An older alternative held that the current statue was a replacement carved by Prospero Spani, called Clemente. Later scholarship and the national catalogue accept the present, visibly fractured and reassembled figure as substantially Michelangelo’s original. The replacement theory remains part of the historiography but is not the prevailing catalogue position. [Early printed discussion in Vasari’s *Lives*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/84/Le_vite_de%27_pi%C3%B9_eccellenti_pittori%2C_scultori_ed_architettori_%28IA_levitedepiueccel07vasa_0%29.pdf)

### Later additions

**Girolamo Coltellini**, in 1536–39, supplied the *Saint John the Baptist* that replaced the contractually intended Saint Vincent.

**Alfonso Lombardi**, in 1532, made the supporting marble predella or stele beneath the sarcophagus. Its central panel is the *Adoration of the Magi*; flanking panels show further episodes from Dominic’s life. A 1943 photographic record documents it dismantled during air-raid protection work. [Italian photographic catalogue](https://dati.cultura.gov.it/lodview-arco/resource/PhotographicHeritage/0800640723.html)

**Jean-Baptiste Boudard** received a commission from the Dominican chapter in 1766 for the remaining altar panel. His marble relief of Dominic’s death or burial was completed on **15 October 1768**. [Biographical record for Boudard](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Boudard)

### Inscriptions

No comprehensive modern epigraphic transcription of every inscription on the composite monument was located in the accessible sources. The monument’s identity is conveyed principally through imagery and saintly attributes rather than a single dedicatory inscription.

Niccolò’s separate signed works use the formulation **“Opus Nicolai de Apulia”**, but this should not be represented as a surviving signature on the Ark unless the precise component is specified.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### The relic body before the Ark

Dominic died at Bologna on **6 August 1221** and was buried in the choir of San Nicolò delle Vigne, traditionally “under the feet of his brothers.” This was an earth or pavement grave, not the surviving sculpted Ark.

On **24 May 1233**, under Master General Jordan of Saxony, the grave was opened and the remains transferred into a more accessible stone or marble tomb. Jordan’s nearly contemporary *Libellus de principiis Ordinis Praedicatorum* reports the opening and the sweet odor said to have issued from the body. Its claim is primary medieval testimony to what participants believed or reported; it is not independent physical verification. [Jordan of Saxony, translated *Libellus*](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/basis/jordansax1.asp)

The translation was closely associated with the movement for Dominic’s canonization and with Dominican preaching in Bologna. Dominic was canonized in 1234. The plain 1233 tomb was the immediate predecessor of the sculpted Ark, but it is not established that any of its stonework was reused in the present monument.

### Commission of the sculpted sarcophagus

The decision to create a more visible and “solemn” monument matured around 1264, during the generalate of **John of Vercelli**. No original commissioning contract with Nicola Pisano survives.

At the Dominican General Chapter at Montpellier in 1265, the work was already underway: convents were asked to send financial help so that the **structura solemnis** of Dominic’s tomb would not remain incomplete. This is the earliest secure contemporary administrative attestation of the project. The chapter acts survive in the *Monumenta Ordinis Fratrum Praedicatorum Historica*. [Digitized *Acta capitulorum generalium*](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AActa_capitulorum_generalium_Ordinis_Praedicatorum_%28IA_actacapitulorumg14domi%29.pdf)

The new sarcophagus was ready when the relics were placed in it on **5 June 1267**. One Italian national catalogue record says 5 July, but the Dominican liturgical and historical tradition and the main art-historical literature give 5 June. The July date is best treated as a catalogue error unless supported by an independent document.

### Makers and attribution dispute

A necrology from the Dominican convent of Santa Caterina at Pisa, compiled before 1407 but drawing on older information, attributes the work to **Nicola Pisano with Fra Guglielmo of Pisa**. This is the closest documentary attribution, but it is not a contemporary building contract.

Stylistic scholars have proposed participation by:

- Nicola Pisano, as designer and directing master;
- Fra Guglielmo of Pisa;
- Arnolfo di Cambio;
- Lapo;
- possibly Donato;
- possibly Giovanni Pisano;
- one or more anonymous assistants.

The evidence for Arnolfo, Lapo, Donato, and Giovanni is mainly stylistic or circumstantial. Nicola contracted for the Siena Cathedral pulpit in September 1265 and promised to reside in Siena from March 1266, so extensive workshop participation in Bologna is likely. It does not follow, however, that Nicola had no major role: workshop design, block preparation, and carving could overlap, and medieval authorship was collective.

Modern scholarship ranges from strong emphasis on Arnolfo to renewed emphasis on Nicola’s overall direction and Fra Guglielmo’s documented participation. No consensus allocation of every figure exists. [“Ancora sull’arca di S. Domenico a Bologna”](https://iris.uniroma1.it/handle/11573/1662753)

### Iconographic sources

Most scenes correspond to the Dominican life compiled by **Humbert of Romans**, approximately 1256–60. The Ark is the oldest substantially complete surviving monumental cycle devoted to Dominic.

Some writers have connected individual details with the recollections of Sister Cecilia, who claimed personal knowledge of Dominic. Serena Romano objects that Cecilia’s recollections were not yet formally recorded when the program was devised and were not accepted as official Dominican hagiography. Identification of an elderly woman in the Napoleone Orsini relief as Cecilia is therefore a hypothesis, not a documented portrait. [Discussion of the iconographic problem](https://www.finestresullarte.info/opere-e-artisti/arca-di-san-domenico-bologna-da-nicola-pisano-a-michelangelo?set_dark_mode=0)

### Materials’ provenance and cost

No quarry accounts, transport records, total cost, or full schedule of payments for the 1260s phase was found. The chapter’s request for contributions documents collective Dominican financing but not the final expenditure.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 1221–1233: first grave

- **6 August 1221:** Dominic died in the Dominican settlement at Bologna.
- He was buried in the choir of San Nicolò delle Vigne.
- Custody lay with the Bolognese Dominican community.
- The place was relatively humble and partly exposed to water and weather in later Dominican descriptions.

### 1233–1267: plain tomb

- **24 May 1233:** the grave was opened under Jordan of Saxony.
- Bishops, clergy, Dominican friars, and civic participants attended.
- The remains were transferred to a plain stone or marble tomb in a more accessible position.
- The reported fragrance became a major part of Dominic’s canonization memory.
- The plain tomb’s later material fate is undocumented.

### 1264/65–1267: manufacture and installation of the surviving core

- John of Vercelli, master general from 1264, was the institutional promoter.
- The project was underway by Pentecost 1265.
- On **5 June 1267**, the relics were deposited in the carved marble chest.
- The Ark stood in the right aisle, before the medieval rood screen, elevated on supports and accessible on several sides.

The mechanism was not transfer of ownership in a modern commercial sense. The Dominican Order commissioned and guarded the tomb, while its increasing civic significance brought Bologna’s government into later patronage.

### Alleged removal of a rib

A Dominican tradition claims that Fra Guglielmo secretly took one of Dominic’s ribs during the 1267 translation and carried it to Pisa, confessing the act near death. The story has been repeated in modern accounts, but no contemporary record establishing the removal was found. It should be classified as a later order tradition concerning the dispersal of a relic, not a secure custody event.

### 1377–1411: first chapel and relocation

In **1377**, Master General Elias of Toulouse reportedly laid the foundation stone of a chapel intended to give the saint’s tomb a more monumental setting. Work proceeded slowly.

On **11 November 1411**, workers moved the Ark into the completed or usable chapel. Accounts state that the wooden relic coffin was not opened during this transfer. At this date the chest still lacked Niccolò’s high crowning structure.

### 1383: separation of the head

On **15 February 1383**, under Master General Raymond of Capua, the inner coffin was opened and Dominic’s head was removed. It was installed in a great processional reliquary made by the Bolognese goldsmith **Jacopo Roseto**.

The reliquary bears Roseto’s authorship and was finished in February 1383. It was designed both to enshrine the skull and to permit its public procession. It is silver-gilt with repoussé, engraving, and enamel decoration. Although directly associated with the Ark, it thereafter followed a partly separate custody history, including residence in the chapel of Saint Thomas and, from 1950, a protected position behind the Ark. [Treccani, Iacopo Roseto](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/iacopo-roseto_(Dizionario-Biografico)/); [national catalogue record](https://catalogo.beniculturali.it/detail/HistoricOrArtisticProperty/0800051397)

### 1469–1473: civic-Dominican enlargement

- **20 July 1469:** Niccolò de Apulia contracted to make the crowning structure.
- The governor, elders, and Sixteen Reformers participated with Dominican authorities.
- **16 July 1473:** the crowning structure was erected.
- Some intended figures were missing.
- The principal bodily relics were by this period kept in a cypress-wood coffin within the marble Ark.

Niccolò’s work on the monument gave him the enduring name **Niccolò dell’Arca**.

### 1494–1539: completion

- **1494–95:** Michelangelo carved or completed *Saint Petronius*, *Saint Proculus*, and a candle-bearing angel.
- **1532:** Alfonso Lombardi installed the predella beneath the medieval chest.
- **1536–39:** Girolamo Coltellini completed *Saint John the Baptist*.

### 1572: accidental destruction and repair

On 4 August 1572, Fra Pellegrino knocked Michelangelo’s *Saint Proculus* to the ground. Fra Ludovico da Prelormo said it broke into more than fifty fragments. Craftsmen reassembled it. Its current fracture network is material evidence of that repair history.

The assertion that Prospero Spani replaced it with a copy conflicts with the early custodian’s statement that it was “adjusted” or repaired and with modern attribution. The exact proportion of original marble, fills, pins, and later restoration material has not been published in a modern technical report located for this dossier.

### 1597–1605: construction of the present chapel and transfer

The earlier chapel was replaced by the present, larger chapel designed by **Floriano Ambrosini**. Work began in 1597. Ambrosini received more than 1,100 lire for the project. [Treccani, Floriano Ambrosini](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/floriano-ambrosini_(Dizionario-Biografico)/)

On **25 April 1605**, the Ark was solemnly placed in its present chapel location. The inner coffin reportedly remained closed. This was the last major relocation of the intact monument before the Second World War.

### 1766–1768: final major sculptural addition

The Dominican chapter commissioned Boudard’s altar frontal in 1766. It was completed in 1768, giving the ensemble its last major figural component.

### Nineteenth century: state title and continued Dominican use

The basilica ultimately entered the Italian state’s Fondo Edifici di Culto following nineteenth-century suppression and confiscation legislation. The movable monument is catalogued as state property, while Dominican friars continue to administer worship and guard the shrine. This produces divided functional authority:

- the Italian state/FEC holds the property interest;
- the cultural-heritage administration regulates conservation;
- the Order of Preachers maintains liturgical and devotional custody.

### 1943–1946: wartime dismantling, refuge, examination, and restoration

Initial protection consisted of structural supports and sandbags. Officials concluded that this would not withstand heavy bombing.

In April–May 1943:

- the relic coffin was removed to a reinforced underground shelter, described as about seven metres below ground near the convent chapter room;
- the Ark was carefully dismantled;
- the sculptures were photographed;
- dismantled sculptural components were transferred to **Villa Aria at Marzabotto**;
- contemporary reports say the dismantling itself caused no damage.

The Villani photographic studio made at least two major series, including 21 and 18 glass-plate negatives. These provide component-by-component documentation of the dismantled Ark. [Catalogue series of 21 wartime negatives](https://catalogo.beniculturali.it/detail/PhotographicHeritage/0800640711); [second catalogue series](https://catalogo.beniculturali.it/detail/PhotographicHeritage/0800640712)

The relics were removed from the shelter on **23 August 1946**. Restoration and reassembly took place from July to September. The Ark was solemnly rededicated and the relics returned on **15 September 1946**.

The 1943 conservation estimate called for treatment of sculptures that had been fractured and poorly patched in earlier periods. Thus the postwar campaign was not merely reassembly: it addressed accumulated historical repairs.

### 1950 onward

The head reliquary was placed behind the Ark in 1950. The ensemble has remained in its present chapel.

No modern theft, sale, deaccession, restitution claim, or competing legal owner was found. It is still a functioning shrine rather than a museum accession divorced from worship.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is the present sarcophagus the 1267 object?

The evidence for continuity is strong:

- the work is documented as underway by 1265;
- relics were placed in a completed monumental tomb in 1267;
- its relief style is consistent with that date;
- subsequent records describe relocations and additions rather than wholesale replacement;
- its dismantling history reveals a materially composite but old structure.

There is no serious scholarly rival claiming that the entire existing chest is a later imitation. Its present configuration, however, is emphatically not its 1267 configuration.

### Attribution

The secure minimum is a Nicola Pisano workshop project involving Fra Guglielmo. Allocation among Nicola, Arnolfo di Cambio, Fra Guglielmo, Lapo, Donato, and Giovanni Pisano remains disputed.

The documentary hierarchy is:

1. pre-1407 necrological evidence for Nicola and Fra Guglielmo;
2. contemporary circumstantial evidence that Nicola’s Siena obligations required assistants;
3. stylistic attribution of particular passages to Arnolfo or others;
4. Vasari’s later naming of assistants, valuable but not contemporary.

### Supports

The present monument lacks its original support arrangement. The Florence and Boston cleric groups are plausible fragments, but their association is an attribution, not an unbroken provenance. The Louvre and V&A figures have also been proposed and contested.

Accordingly, the surviving Ark is both authentic and materially incomplete: some possible original parts are dispersed, while later components now occupy structural and iconographic positions never envisioned in 1267.

### Relic identity

The relics have a continuous institutional provenance from Dominic’s documented burial place through successive recorded translations. That is significant historical evidence, but it is not the equivalent of modern genetic identification.

The 1940s examination included radiography and physical study. Reports described bones in good condition and a calcified laryngeal structure; Fabio Frassetto modeled the skull, after which sculptor Carlo Pini added reconstructed soft tissues. The resulting face was presented as a scientifically informed reconstruction and differed from conventional iconography. [Bologna municipal chronology](https://www.bibliotecasalaborsa.it/bolognaonline/cronologia-di-bologna/1946/il_vero_volto_di_san_domenico)

Limitations:

- the examination did not provide DNA identification;
- there is no known comparative DNA sample proving personal identity;
- no radiocarbon result was located;
- the “true face” was a forensic reconstruction constrained by mid-twentieth-century methods, not a photographic recovery of appearance;
- claims about vocal power inferred from a calcified larynx exceed what the accessible reports independently establish.

Ecclesiastical authentication derives from documented custody, recognition at canonical openings, seals, and institutional continuity. Scientific authentication remains limited.

### *Saint Proculus*: original or replacement?

Two accounts exist:

- **repair account:** Michelangelo’s shattered pieces were gathered and reassembled; this is supported by Fra Ludovico’s near-contemporary statement and is now generally accepted;
- **replacement account:** Prospero Spani made a new statue after the accident; this appeared in older literature.

The national catalogue identifies the present figure as Michelangelo’s work and records the historical repair. It is therefore best described as Michelangelo’s heavily fractured and restored original, subject to the absence of a published modern structural analysis quantifying replacement material.

### Head reliquary

Father Alce questioned whether the small bust at the summit of Roseto’s reliquary was original. Later catalogue scholarship, especially Daniele Benati, rejected those doubts and attributed it securely to Roseto. This is a dispute about part of the container, not the skull’s identity.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

The Ark’s primary practical function is the containment and veneration of Dominic’s relics. Its elevation converted an inaccessible grave into a visible focal point for prayer, pilgrimage, and institutional memory.

Documented ceremonial uses include:

- the solemn translations of 1233 and 1267;
- removal of the head in 1383;
- processions of the head reliquary through Bologna;
- relocation into new chapels in 1411 and 1605;
- wartime removal and postwar return in 1943–46;
- Masses, feast observances, and Order gatherings at the shrine.

The head reliquary was deliberately designed to be carried. Its visibility allowed a relic normally enclosed within a tomb to move through civic space.

The Ark served the Order as:

- proof of physical continuity with its founder;
- the liturgical center of the founder’s cult;
- a monumental narrative of Dominican origins;
- a destination linking the worldwide Order to Bologna;
- a visual statement of papal authorization and apostolic mission.

For Bologna it became a civic palladium in a looser symbolic sense. The addition of local patron saints and the participation of civic magistrates in the 1469 contract integrated the founder’s tomb into the city’s sacred identity. Possession did not legally confer sovereignty, but patronage of the shrine publicly displayed civic legitimacy and religious prestige.

No reliable evidence was found for a curse, legally prescribed taboo, or supernatural penalty for touching the Ark. Physical access is now controlled for conservation and liturgical reasons.

Miracles associated with Dominic’s tomb—especially fragrance, healing, and intercession—belong to the cult’s attested belief history. Their historical effect is demonstrable: they encouraged translation, canonization, pilgrimage, and increasingly costly architectural treatment. Their supernatural causation is not historically testable.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The odor of sanctity

**Earliest tier:** near-contemporary Dominican testimony.

When the 1221 grave was opened in 1233, Jordan of Saxony reported that a marvelous fragrance came from the body, clung to hands and objects, and confirmed Dominic’s sanctity to those present.

The narrative follows an established Christian hagiographic sign, the *odor sanctitatis*. It also had a concrete institutional role: it answered friars’ reported fear that the poorly protected grave would reveal decay and undermine the founder’s reputation.

What is historically secure is that Jordan recorded the claim and that it became central to the translation narrative. The physical cause or supernatural status cannot be established.

### Incorruption

Later devotional summaries sometimes compress the fragrance episode into an assertion that Dominic’s body was “incorrupt.” The earliest translation narrative emphasizes fragrance; it should not automatically be converted into a modern anatomical claim of complete bodily preservation. By 1383 and certainly by 1943–46, the principal remains were skeletal.

### Fra Guglielmo’s stolen rib

**Tier:** later Dominican tradition; unverified.

Fra Guglielmo supposedly removed a rib during the 1267 deposition, carried it to Pisa, and confessed on his deathbed. The story explains or validates a Pisan relic while simultaneously presenting illicit removal as ultimately confessed. No contemporaneous judicial, inventory, or translation record was found.

### The Ark as an eyewitness portrait cycle

Some modern popular descriptions suggest the reliefs were based directly on surviving eyewitnesses or portray Sister Cecilia. The scenes instead depend principally on official Dominican hagiography. The proposed portrait of Cecilia is disputed and should not be presented as fact.

### Michelangelo’s wholly independent figures

Modern tourism often presents Michelangelo as arriving to “finish the Ark” with three entirely autonomous masterpieces. His involvement is documented, but early wording and unfinished blocks leave open whether he completed work already begun by Niccolò. The neat modern story of three wholly new sculptures is an oversimplification.

### The “true face” of Dominic

**Tier:** twentieth-century scientific-popular construction.

The 1946 reconstruction was promoted as Dominic’s “true face.” It was based on an examined skull and contemporary anthropological reconstruction methods, but soft-tissue depths, ears, hair, expression, and many surface details necessarily involved estimation and artistic modeling. Its value is historical and forensic; the title should not be read as absolute proof of appearance.

## 8. Current Status and Location

The Ark remains in the **Chapel of Saint Dominic**, opening from the right side of the Basilica of San Domenico, Piazza San Domenico 13, Bologna, Italy.

The principal sarcophagus and associated sculpture are catalogued under national identifier **0800024749**. The national catalogue describes the legal status of individual components as **state property**. The basilica belongs to the **Fondo Edifici di Culto**, administered by the Italian Ministry of the Interior. Dominican friars remain its religious custodians.

The object is:

- extant;
- in its historic church;
- still used as a shrine;
- publicly visible when chapel access is available;
- protected under Italian cultural-heritage law;
- not known to be subject to a repatriation or private-title dispute.

The skull remains in Jacopo Roseto’s separate reliquary behind the Ark. The other attributed bodily remains are in the cypress coffin within the monument.

As of September 2026, the basilica has been undergoing a major restoration campaign begun in 2025. Work was initially expected to conclude in July 2026. Reporting in June 2026 stated that access to the Ark remained possible despite extensive scaffolding elsewhere in the church. The available sources do not establish that the Ark itself was dismantled in this campaign. ## 9. Modern Reception

### Art-historical status

The Ark is frequently described as a compressed history of Italian sculpture because it unites:

- Nicola Pisano’s thirteenth-century classicizing Gothic;
- the styles associated with Arnolfo and the Pisano workshop;
- Niccolò dell’Arca’s late-fifteenth-century expression;
- Michelangelo’s early work;
- Lombardi’s sixteenth-century relief;
- Boudard’s eighteenth-century classicism.

This formulation is interpretive but materially grounded in the ensemble’s multi-century manufacture.

### Influence on later shrines

The raised, narrative-covered tomb became an important model for Italian saint shrines, including:

- the Ark of Saint Peter Martyr at Sant’Eustorgio, Milan;
- the Ark of Saint Augustine at Pavia;
- other fourteenth-century elevated reliquary tombs in Padua and Udine.

Influence concerns typology—an elevated, accessible chest with narrative relief and figures—not literal replication.

### Photography and reproduction

The Ark has been extensively reproduced in:

- engravings and guidebooks;
- nineteenth-century art-historical publications;
- photographs by Pietro Poppi and the Villani studio;
- plaster casts and detail studies;
- Michelangelo catalogues;
- modern tourism material.

The 1943 Villani photographs have unusual documentary value because they record normally obscured surfaces and the dismantled construction.

### Tourism and pilgrimage

The shrine remains both a tourism destination and an active pilgrimage site. Its public image often centers on Michelangelo, although his three sculptures form only a small part of the whole. This emphasis can obscure Nicola Pisano’s workshop and the Order’s long history of additions.

No consequential modern forgery marketed as the Ark itself was identified. Commercial reproductions of Michelangelo’s angel and saints exist, but none found in this research has acquired a competing relic identity.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Primary evidence

The principal documentary bodies are:

- Jordan of Saxony’s *Libellus* for the 1233 opening;
- canonization and translation materials;
- Dominican General Chapter acts for the 1265 fundraising order;
- the pre-1407 Santa Caterina necrology for Nicola and Fra Guglielmo;
- the 1469 contract with Niccolò dell’Arca;
- Dominican convent inventories and custodians’ notes;
- Fra Ludovico da Prelormo’s account of Michelangelo’s work and the 1572 accident;
- payment and building records for Ambrosini’s chapel;
- wartime Soprintendenza files, estimates, photographs, and restoration reports;
- modern Italian catalogue records.

The formal petition for Dominic’s canonization, reportedly carried to the papal court after the 1233 translation, does not survive. This loss limits reconstruction of the earliest official use of the relic body.

### Major lines of study

**Authorship of the thirteenth-century reliefs.** Early scholarship divided the work mainly between Nicola and Fra Guglielmo. Twentieth-century formal analysis increasingly assigned high-quality passages to Arnolfo di Cambio, sometimes minimizing both documented masters. Recent scholarship has warned against treating stylistic “hands” as settled identities.

**Original architecture.** Scholars dispute how many supports there were, whether they were columns, angels, clerics, or a combination, and which dispersed sculptures belonged to the structure.

**Narrative sources.** The degree to which the images depend on Humbert of Romans, eyewitness testimony, or the recollections attributed to Cecilia remains debated.

**Civic patronage.** Modern studies emphasize that the fifteenth-century enlargement was not only Dominican. Bologna’s government participated directly, converting the Ark into a civic-religious monument.

**Michelangelo.** Questions persist over which figures he began, which he completed from Niccolò’s blocks, and the material history of the shattered *Saint Proculus*.

**Relic science.** The 1940s radiographic examination and facial reconstruction were significant for their time but did not establish identity through modern biochemical methods.

### Important scholarship

Major works include:

- Cesare Gnudi, *Nicola, Arnolfo, Lapo* (1948);
- John Pope-Hennessy, “The Arca of St. Dominic: A Hypothesis” (1951);
- Stefano Bottari, *L’Arca di S. Domenico in Bologna* (1964);
- Anita Fiderer Moskowitz, *Nicola Pisano’s Arca di San Domenico and Its Legacy* (1994);
- Massimo Medica’s studies of the Ark and its possible fragments;
- Serena Romano’s iconographic reassessments;
- Gianluigi Viscione’s work on reconstructing the original supports;
- archival work on the basilica’s medieval relic inventories.

The Italian national catalogue is especially useful for legal status, component-level identification, and twentieth-century photographic documentation, but it contains at least one apparent date discrepancy—5 July rather than 5 June 1267—showing that catalogue entries must also be critically compared.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and documentary sources

- Jordan of Saxony, *Libellus de principiis Ordinis Praedicatorum*, for the 1233 translation and fragrance narrative.
- *Acta capitulorum generalium Ordinis Praedicatorum*, Montpellier chapter of 1265.
- Dominican necrology of Santa Caterina, Pisa, compiled before 1407.
- Contract of 20 July 1469 between Niccolò de Apulia, Dominican and Bolognese civic authorities.
- Fra Ludovico da Prelormo’s custodial notes concerning Michelangelo and the accident of 1572.
- Convent inventories discussed in modern archival scholarship.
- Italian Soprintendenza wartime records and Villani photographic negatives, 1943.
- Italian General Catalogue of Cultural Heritage, records 0800024749, 0800024759, 0800051397, 0800640711, and 0800640712.

### Modern scholarly and institutional sources

- Treccani biographical and medieval-art entries for Nicola Pisano, Niccolò dell’Arca, Iacopo Roseto, Floriano Ambrosini, and Dominic.
- Italian Ministry of Culture catalogue and ArCo knowledge graph.
- Bologna municipal historical chronology.
- Archivio di Stato di Bologna’s documentary exhibition on San Domenico.
- Louvre collections catalogue for the disputed support figure.
- Studies by Gnudi, Pope-Hennessy, Bottari, Moskowitz, Medica, Romano, Benati, and Viscione.
- Contemporary Bologna tourism and news reporting for access and restoration status.

### Evidentiary conclusions

- **Existence:** certain. The monument is physically present and nationally catalogued.
- **Continuity of the 1267 marble chest:** very strong.
- **Present form identical to 1267 form:** false; the ensemble is a multi-period reconstruction.
- **Nicola Pisano’s responsibility:** strongly supported, though not by a surviving contemporary contract.
- **Fra Guglielmo’s collaboration:** strongly supported by the early necrology.
- **Precise division of carving among workshop members:** unresolved.
- **Identification of dispersed supports:** disputed.
- **Continuity of relic custody:** strong as an institutional and documentary chain.
- **Scientific proof that the bones are Dominic’s:** unavailable.
- **Miraculous fragrance or incorruption:** medieval religious testimony, not scientifically verifiable.
- **Present *Saint Proculus* as Michelangelo’s repaired original:** prevailing catalogue and scholarly position.
- **Complete technical publication of materials, dimensions, repair pins, fills, and modern interventions:** not located and possibly unpublished in accessible form.
