# Jupiter Lapis (the sacred flint of Jupiter Feretrius): Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `jupiter_lapis_object_dossier.md`

## Evidentiary key

- **Documented ancient testimony:** what an extant Greek or Latin source actually reports.
- **Traditional history:** an event assigned to Rome’s regal or early republican past by much later authors.
- **Scholarly inference:** a modern reconstruction from incomplete literary, linguistic, or archaeological evidence.
- **Unresolved:** the evidence does not permit a choice between competing interpretations.
- **Unsupported modern identification:** a claim for which no securely provenanced physical object or ancient testimony supplies adequate support.

## Executive finding

“Jupiter Lapis” is not a securely identified surviving artifact. No museum possesses an accessioned stone demonstrably descended from the Roman object, and no excavated stone has been authenticated as it. The underlying evidence instead preserves three related but not necessarily identical things:

1. A stone held and then cast away in a Roman treaty oath, explicitly called “Jupiter Stone” by Polybius.
2. A *lapis silex*—a hard stone or flint—used to strike the sacrificial pig in the fetial treaty ceremony described by Livy and later commentators.
3. A *lapis silex* reportedly kept, with a ritual sceptre, in the Capitoline temple of Jupiter Feretrius and removed by the fetials when concluding treaties, according to the Festus–Paul tradition.

Much modern writing fuses these into one unique cult object permanently housed in the temple and embodying Jupiter. That reconstruction is possible but not established. The stone thrown away in Polybius’s formula was logically expendable, while the temple implement described by Festus could have been a curated ritual instrument. J. S. Reid therefore warned that “Jupiter Lapis,” the thrown oath-stone, and the sacrificial flint may have been conflated by ancient antiquarians and modern scholars. Later scholarship continues to debate whether *Iuppiter Lapis* means “Jupiter-as-Stone,” an epithet of the god, or preserves an asyndetic invocation of “Jupiter and the stone.”

The most defensible object history is consequently a history of an uncertain object-class and its transmitted identity—not the biography of a securely individuated stone.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Principal names

| Form | Language and script | Transliteration/translation | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Iuppiter Lapis** / **Iupiter Lapis** | Latin | “Jupiter Stone,” conventionally “Jupiter of the Stone” or “Jupiter-as-Stone” | Attested through Greek in Polybius and directly in later Latin writers |
| **Δία λίθον** | Ancient Greek, accusative | *Día líthon*, “Jupiter Stone” | Polybius, *Histories* 3.25.6–9; earliest surviving explicit account |
| **per Iovem Lapidem** | Latin oath formula | “by Jupiter Stone” | Cicero and Aulus Gellius |
| **lapis silex / lapidem silicem** | Latin | “flint stone,” “hard stone,” or “silex-stone” | Livy, Festus/Paul, Servius |
| **Iuppiter Feretrius** | Latin divine title | Jupiter Feretrius | Cult to whose Capitoline temple the stored flint was reportedly attached |

*Iuppiter* is the Latin sky-god’s name, historically related to *Diēspiter* and Indo-European expressions for “sky-father.” *Lapis* means stone. *Silex*, genitive *silicis*, can denote flint but is not a modern mineralogical classification; Latin usage may encompass exceptionally hard stone. Translating every occurrence as geological “flint” therefore states more than the texts prove.

### 1.2 Is “Lapis” an epithet?

Three readings must remain distinct:

1. **Jupiter embodied in or represented by a stone.** Servius says that the ancients regarded a flint stone as Jupiter’s *signum*, a term that can mean image, token, or manifestation.
2. **Jupiter under the title Lapis.** This is the conventional interpretation of Polybius’s *Día líthon* and the Latin oath *per Iovem Lapidem*.
3. **“Jupiter and the stone.”** Some modern scholars construe the two words as an old two-part formula without an expressed conjunction. The 2023 study *Il culto di Iuppiter a Roma* expressly notes this possibility and reports that *Lapis* has no confirming occurrence as an independent Jovian epithet in Rome’s surviving inscriptions. [The study and its epigraphic catalogue](https://openscience.ub.uni-mainz.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/02455401-a979-4d16-8553-4b595792038c/content)

Accordingly, “Jupiter Lapis” should not automatically be treated as the proper name of one individuated stone.

### 1.3 Physical description supported by the sources

No source supplies dimensions, weight, color, exact lithology, quarry, workmanship, inscription, or decoration.

The complete defensible description is:

- **Material:** *silex*, conventionally flint, but more cautiously a hard stone.
- **Form:** unspecified. It was small enough to be held in one hand, used to strike a pig, or thrown away, depending on which testimony is being considered.
- **Modification:** no ancient text says it was polished, knapped, hafted, perforated, mounted, or inscribed.
- **Associated object:** a sceptre reportedly kept in the same temple and used for swearing.
- **Cult-image status:** no early source describes an anthropomorphic statue in the temple. The later claim that the stone itself was Jupiter’s *signum* occurs in Servius’s commentary on *Aeneid* 8.641. [Servius’s Latin text](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/In_Vergilii_Aeneidem_commentarii/Liber_VIII)
- **Current condition:** unknown; no surviving candidate is recognized.
- **Images:** no ancient image has been securely identified as a portrait of this stone.

The suggestion that it was a prehistoric stone axe or “thunderstone” is a modern archaeological-religious interpretation, not an ancient physical description. It is plausible because polished prehistoric axes were widely understood in later cultures as fallen thunderbolts, and because Jupiter wielded lightning; but no excavated axe has a demonstrable chain of custody from the temple.

### 1.4 One stone or a supply of stones?

This is the dossier’s central identity problem.

- Polybius’s oath-maker holds a stone and casts it away.
- Festus/Paul says the fetials took “the flint stone” from Jupiter Feretrius’s temple to strike a treaty.
- Livy says the pig was struck with *silex* but does not say that this particular stone came from the temple.
- Servius says the ancients thought a flint stone was Jupiter’s image or token.

These accounts permit at least four models:

1. **Unique-relic model:** one permanent sacred flint was kept in the temple, carried to treaty ceremonies, used to strike victims, and returned.
2. **Temple-instrument model:** one curated sacrificial implement belonged to the temple, while ordinary disposable stones were used for the casting oath.
3. **Object-class model:** the temple maintained or sacralized a supply of flints, individual pieces being expendable.
4. **Antiquarian-conflation model:** the thrown stone and pig-killing flint originally belonged to different rites and were subsequently combined.

No model can presently be demonstrated by archaeology.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 No documented manufacture

There is no commission, maker, quarry, geological source, price, or manufacture record. The stone’s prehistoric origin, if it was a naturally formed flint or repurposed stone tool, cannot be reconstructed. No primary source attributes its fabrication to a god, hero, craftsman, or king.

Claims that Romulus “created” the Jupiter Stone confuse the traditional foundation of the temple with the origin of its ritual contents. The surviving foundation narratives concern the sanctuary and the *spolia opima*, not the manufacture or discovery of a specific flint.

### 2.2 Earliest surviving explicit testimony: Polybius

The earliest securely datable surviving reference is Polybius, writing his *Histories* principally in the mid-second century BCE. In 3.25.6–9 he describes the oaths attached to Roman–Carthaginian treaties. He says:

- In the first treaty, the Carthaginians swore by their ancestral gods.
- The Romans swore, “according to an ancient custom,” by **Δία λίθον**, Jupiter Stone.
- The oath-taker held a stone.
- He asked that, if faithful, he receive good; but if false in thought or deed, that he alone be cast out while others remained safe in their countries, laws, possessions, temples, and tombs.
- He then threw away the stone.

[Polybius, *Histories* 3.25](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/thayer/e/roman/texts/polybius/3%2A.html)

Polybius connects the formula with the first Roman–Carthaginian treaty, conventionally dated to the first year of the Republic, 509 BCE. That is the traditional date of the treaty, not the date of the surviving narrative. Polybius says that treaty texts were preserved on bronze tablets in the quaestors’ treasury beside the temple of Capitoline Jupiter; he does not say the oath-stone itself was preserved there.

What Polybius does **not** say:

- that the stone was flint;
- that it came from Jupiter Feretrius’s temple;
- that it killed a pig;
- that it was a unique relic;
- that it was returned after use;
- that it was Rome’s anthropomorphic or aniconic cult statue.

### 2.3 Traditional regal-period origin

Livy, writing under Augustus, presents the treaty between Tullus Hostilius’s Rome and Alba Longa as the earliest compact remembered by tradition. The fetial Marcus Valerius appoints Spurius Fusius as *pater patratus*. After the terms are recited, Fusius calls on Jupiter to strike the Roman people if they knowingly break the treaty, just as he strikes the pig, and kills or stuns the pig with *silex*. [Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 1.24](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0151%3Abook%3D1%3Achapter%3D24)

Livy dates the narrative to the reign traditionally assigned to Tullus Hostilius, 672–640 BCE. His wording is cautious: this was the earliest compact whose memory tradition preserved. It is not contemporary seventh-century documentation. The Oxford Classical Dictionary’s assessment is that Livy’s description is an antiquarian reconstruction, perhaps drawing on priestly materials. [OCD, “fetiales”](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548207335)

### 2.4 The temple tradition

Roman annalistic tradition said that Romulus founded the temple of Jupiter Feretrius on the Capitoline after killing Acron, king of Caenina, and dedicating his armor as the first *spolia opima*. Livy calls it Rome’s first temple and says Ancus Marcius later enlarged it. Dionysius of Halicarnassus described its longest dimension as under fifteen Roman feet and said an ancient trace of it remained.

Nothing in the extant Romulus narratives securely connects the temple’s first construction to the acquisition of the flint. The explicit temple-custody statement survives in the lexicographical tradition of Verrius Flaccus, epitomized by Festus and then by Paul the Deacon:

> *ex cuius templo sumebant sceptrum per quod iurarent et lapidem silicem quo foedus ferirent*

“From his temple they took the sceptre by which they swore and the flint stone with which they struck a treaty.”

[Festus/Paul passage and temple testimonia](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Places/Europe/Italy/Lazio/Roma/Rome/_Texts/PLATOP%2A/Jupiter_Feretrius.html)

This testimony is important but textually late and mediated: Verrius wrote under Augustus and Tiberius; Festus epitomized him probably in the second century CE; Paul further abbreviated Festus in the eighth century.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because a unique artifact cannot be established, the following is a chain of **reported ritual custody**, not a continuous museum-style provenance.

### Before the mid-second century BCE: alleged archaic use

- **Traditional seventh-century BCE setting:** Livy’s Tullus–Alba story places a *silex* in the hands of the Roman *pater patratus*. No contemporary object record survives.
- **Traditional 509 BCE setting:** Polybius assigns the Jupiter-Stone oath to the earliest Roman–Carthaginian treaty. His evidence is second-century BCE and does not document where the stone went after it was thrown.
- **Later republican treaty practice:** Roman accounts treat the rite as ancestral. Whether one flint persisted between ceremonies is unrecorded.

### Mid-second century BCE: first surviving textual custody scene

Polybius gives the earliest extant, reasonably contemporary observation of the practice’s remembered form. The “commissioner” or oath-taker holds the stone only for the duration of the formula and casts it away. If literal, custody terminates deliberately: the object passes from hand to ground and ceases to be ritually useful.

This is fundamentally incompatible with a permanent-relic biography unless the Polybian stone was a substitute rather than the temple’s stored flint.

### Late Republic: temple inventory tradition

By the antiquarian tradition eventually preserved in Festus/Paul, the flint and sceptre were stored inside Jupiter Feretrius’s temple. Their guardianship presumably belonged institutionally to Roman public religious authorities and operationally to the fetial college. No individual keeper, inventory number, or storage container is recorded.

### Mid-first century BCE: temple collapse

Cornelius Nepos reports that the sanctuary founded by Romulus had become unroofed and was collapsing through age and neglect. Titus Pomponius Atticus urged Octavian—later Augustus—to repair it. [Cornelius Nepos tradition, summarized with text](https://www.keytoumbria.com/ROMAN_REPUBLIC/Temple_of_Jupiter_Feretrius.html)

The flint’s condition and location during the roofless period are completely unrecorded. Possibilities include continued in-situ storage, removal to another treasury, replacement, loss, or purely retrospective attribution. None can be established.

### Circa 31 BCE: Augustan restoration and inspection

Augustus restored the temple, probably around 31 BCE. His *Res Gestae* 19 lists the temples of Jupiter Feretrius and Jupiter Tonans among his Capitoline works. [Translation of *Res Gestae*](https://ancientrome.ru/ius/library/august/ada.htm)

Livy 4.20 records Augustus’s claim that he entered the temple and read the inscription on Aulus Cornelius Cossus’s linen corselet, deposited as *spolia opima*. This proves, at most, that Augustus asserted direct inspection of the sanctuary’s contents. Livy never says Augustus saw, handled, restored, or inventoried the flint.

The restoration is nevertheless a major potential break in material continuity. A roofless, failing building was rebuilt, but there is no surviving account of how its small cult objects were conserved or whether replacements were made.

### Imperial period: literary rather than physical custody

Later sources repeat the ceremony and metaphysics of the stone:

- Aulus Gellius records *per Iovem Lapidem* as an emphatic old oath.
- Servius explains that the pig was struck with flint because the ancients regarded the stone as Jupiter’s *signum*.
- Festus/Paul preserves the assertion that the flint and sceptre were taken from the temple.

None supplies an observed custody event, a physical description, or a final sighting.

### Late antiquity and after

No surviving source records:

- the temple’s closure;
- confiscation of its treasury;
- Christian destruction of the stone;
- its burial, reuse, sale, or translation;
- survival in a medieval collection;
- rediscovery in excavation.

The chain therefore ends in antiquity without a recorded terminal event. The temple itself disappears from the securely traceable monumental record, and its exact site remains uncertain. The stone’s loss must be classified **unresolved**, not documented destruction.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Was there one historical Jupiter Stone?

**Case for a real, curated cult object**

- Festus/Paul explicitly uses the singular *lapidem silicem* and says it was taken from the temple.
- The stone appears with another curated insignium, the sceptre.
- Servius describes flint as Jupiter’s *signum*.
- The temple’s extremely small scale and absence of a securely attested anthropomorphic statue are consistent with a compact aniconic cult token.
- Roman religion preserved archaic implements and formulae as markers of institutional continuity.

**Case against assuming one continuous artifact**

- Polybius’s stone is thrown away.
- Livy calls the killing implement simply *silex* and does not locate it in the temple.
- Festus/Paul is mediated late antiquarian testimony, not a contemporary inventory.
- Ordinary oath-stones could be ritually interchangeable.
- There is no physical description allowing one stone to be distinguished from another.
- There is no archaeological find or post-antique provenance.
- Roman antiquarians frequently rationalized ritual vocabulary through etymology and reconstructed archaic institutions from later practice.

**Finding:** historical use of stones in Roman oath and treaty ritual is strongly attested. The existence of one unique, continuously curated artifact called “the Jupiter Lapis” is not.

### 4.2 Were the thrown stone and pig-killing flint identical?

J. S. Reid’s 1912 study remains the classic skeptical intervention. Reid distinguished:

- the stone thrown away as an enacted curse of expulsion;
- the flint that struck the pig as the instrument of Jupiter’s punitive blow;
- the separately alleged temple relic.

He argued that modern writers too readily created a god “Iuppiter Lapis” and identified all these elements. [J. S. Reid, “Human Sacrifices at Rome and Other Notes on Roman Religion,” *Journal of Roman Studies* 2 (1912), 34–52](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Journals/JRS/2/Human_Sacrifices_at_Rome%2A.html)

Reid did not disprove a relationship. His point was evidentiary: the sources do not furnish a secure identity equation.

### 4.3 Aniconic image or ritual tool?

Servius’s *signum* language supports an aniconic reading, but it is late. A stone can simultaneously be:

- a practical sacrificial implement;
- a token of lightning;
- a temporary embodiment of divine sanction;
- an image or material sign of Jupiter.

Modern categories such as “statue,” “relic,” “weapon,” and “symbol” may divide functions that Roman ritual did not separate. Nevertheless, saying that “the temple had no statue and Jupiter was worshipped as this stone” remains a reconstruction, not a verbatim early inventory.

### 4.4 Flint, stone axe, or thunderbolt?

Ancient authors say *silex*, not “Neolithic axe.” The axe/thunderstone interpretation rests on analogy:

- prehistoric polished axes were sometimes collected as thunderbolts;
- Jupiter’s punishment was imagined as a lightning strike;
- flint produces sparks;
- the treaty formula asks Jupiter to “strike” the oath-breaker.

No petrographic analysis or use-wear study can test this because there is no identified specimen. The stone-axe claim must be labeled plausible but unverified scholarly inference.

### 4.5 Rival claimants and forgeries

No serious rival physical claimants have been documented. No Roman, ecclesiastical, private, or museum collection possesses an object with a continuous provenance as Jupiter Lapis. No documented modern forgery has achieved scholarly standing under that identity.

This negative conclusion also excludes several frequently confused objects:

- **Lapis Niger:** a black pavement and archaic inscribed complex in the Roman Forum, not Jupiter Lapis.
- **Lapis Manalis:** a name applied in Roman antiquarian sources to stones associated with rainmaking or the gate of the underworld, not securely identical with the fetial flint.
- **Capitoline foundation stones:** structural tufa from the temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus, not portable oath-stones.
- **Baetyls and generic thunderstones:** useful comparanda, not authenticated candidates.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Striking the treaty pig

Livy’s full sequence is the clearest account:

1. The fetial requests authority from the king.
2. Sacred vegetation is brought from the citadel.
3. A *pater patratus* is constituted by touching his head and hair with it.
4. Treaty terms are recited.
5. Jupiter, the Alban *pater patratus*, and the Alban people are called as witnesses.
6. Rome promises not to be first to defect knowingly and by public decision.
7. The Roman officiant asks Jupiter to strike the Roman people as he strikes the pig, only with divine power proportionately greater.
8. He strikes the pig with flint.
9. The Alban side pronounces its own oath.

The object’s operative meaning is punitive analogy: victim, implement, oath-breaker, and Jupiter’s thunderous violence are aligned. The stone does not merely “witness” the oath; it helps stage in advance the destruction promised for perjury.

### 6.2 Casting away the oath-stone

Polybius preserves a different performative analogy. The oath-taker asks that, if he breaks faith, he alone be expelled while everyone else retains country, laws, possessions, shrines, and tombs; he then throws away the stone.

Here the stone represents the prospective fate of the perjurer—not necessarily Jupiter’s weapon. Its abandonment is the core symbolic act. The rite has no pig in Polybius’s account.

### 6.3 Temple storage and removal

Festus/Paul reports that the fetials removed two objects from Jupiter Feretrius’s temple:

- a sceptre, “through which” or “by which” they swore;
- a flint stone with which they “struck a treaty.”

The paired objects materialized authority and sanction. The sceptre marked the speaker as Jupiter’s or the Roman state’s authorized representative; the flint enacted punishment.

The sources do not state:

- who could enter the temple;
- whether the stone could be touched outside ritual;
- whether it underwent purification;
- how it was transported;
- whether it was washed after sacrifice;
- whether blood staining was retained;
- whether a damaged stone could be replaced;
- how often the actual temple object was used.

### 6.4 *Foedus ferire*

Latin commonly says *foedus ferire*, literally “to strike a treaty,” meaning to conclude or ratify it. Ancient scholars variously connected:

- *foedus* with the ugly or *foedus* death of the pig;
- *Feretrius* with *ferire*, “to strike”;
- *Feretrius* with *ferre*, “to carry,” especially carrying spoils;
- *Feretrius* with a Greek-derived explanation involving the *pheretron*, a carrying frame.

These ancient etymologies are themselves evidence for how Romans conceptualized the rite, but they are not all historically valid linguistic derivations.

### 6.5 Political and legal function

The rite did not transfer sovereignty through ownership of the stone. Its political force lay in making a public treaty religiously binding:

- the Roman community was exposed to Jupiter’s punishment;
- the authorized fetial spoke for the *populus Romanus*;
- a diplomatic compact became simultaneously legal, public, and sacred;
- the physical blow or cast made the conditional curse visible.

Possession of the object therefore represented ritual competence—the ability to bind Rome—not kingship in the manner of coronation regalia.

### 6.6 Private oath language

By Cicero’s day, “to swear by Jupiter Lapis” could be used in correspondence as an exceptionally forceful or archaizing oath. Gellius later treated it as a recognizable old formula. This is the object’s semantic expansion: a public treaty implement became a proverbial guarantor of personal truthfulness.

No evidence shows that private swearers physically borrowed the Capitoline flint. They invoked the formula.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 Romulean antiquity

**Traditional narrative:** Romulus kills Acron, takes the enemy king’s armor, carries it to the Capitoline, marks out a sanctuary, dedicates the spoils to Jupiter Feretrius, and establishes the precedent for *spolia opima*.

**Earliest surviving developed sources:** late republican and Augustan writers, many centuries after the alleged event.

**What it contributes to the stone:** only a sacred institutional setting. Later reconstructions place the flint in Rome’s “first temple” and infer that it belonged to the city’s original Jovian cult. No extant foundation narrative says Romulus brought the flint, received it from heaven, or installed it as a cult image.

### 7.2 The stone as lightning

**Later ancient interpretation:** Servius says the ancients regarded flint as Jupiter’s *signum*. This connects the stone to Jupiter’s thunderbolt and makes the pig’s death a miniature performance of a lightning strike.

**Modern elaboration:** the stone becomes a prehistoric axe or “thunderstone,” imagined as a physical bolt fallen from heaven.

**Assessment:** the lightning symbolism is plausible and has ancient support; the precise axe identity does not.

### 7.3 The first treaty of Rome

Polybius’s association with the first Roman–Carthaginian treaty gives the oath immense antiquity. Livy independently says no older treaty was remembered than the Roman–Alban compact. The two texts therefore produce rival “earliest” narrative settings:

- Polybius: an early republican Roman–Carthaginian treaty.
- Livy: a regal-period Roman–Alban treaty.

They need not describe the same ceremony or stone. Both are retrospective claims of ancestral continuity.

### 7.4 Jupiter Feretrius as the stone

The frequently repeated statement “Jupiter Feretrius was identical with Jupiter Lapis” is scholarly synthesis, not a sentence preserved in an early ritual text. The supporting seam is:

1. Festus/Paul places a flint in Feretrius’s temple.
2. Polybius names a Jupiter-Stone oath.
3. Livy uses flint under Jupiter’s sanction.
4. Servius calls flint Jupiter’s *signum*.

The synthesis is coherent but not compulsory. Reid’s critique shows why the seam must remain visible.

### 7.5 Survival legends

No well-attested medieval or modern legend identifies a hidden stone, secret custodian, church relic, or excavated claimant as the surviving Jupiter Lapis. Statements that a displayed axe or Capitoline stone “is” the object should be treated as modern speculation unless accompanied by provenance absent from the present record.

## 8. Current Status and Location

**Status as of September 2026:** lost, unlocated, and not securely identifiable as a unique artifact.

- **Current repository:** none.
- **Accession number:** none.
- **Recognized candidate:** none.
- **Scientific examination:** none attributable to the object.
- **Conservation history:** none.
- **Legal owner or custodian:** none presently identifiable.
- **Display status:** not displayed as an authenticated artifact.
- **Repatriation dispute:** none documented.
- **Last secure physical sighting:** none. The ancient authors describe ritual or transmit institutional lore but do not provide a dated eyewitness examination of a surviving flint.

The temple’s precise archaeological location has not been established, and no remains securely assigned to the sanctuary have produced the stone. Modern Capitoline excavations have documented the much larger temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus, whose surviving foundations lie principally beneath Palazzo Caffarelli; these should not be attributed to Jupiter Feretrius. [Musei Capitolini, Temple of Capitoline Jupiter](https://www.museicapitolini.org/en/sede/campidoglio_antico/tempio_di_giove_capitolino)

The 2023 topographical and epigraphic study *Il culto di Iuppiter a Roma* states directly that there are no archaeological data for the Feretrian temple. It also finds no urban inscription attesting Jupiter under the epithet *Lapis*. [Full study](https://openscience.ub.uni-mainz.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/02455401-a979-4d16-8553-4b595792038c/content)

Consequently, even finding an unprovenanced flint on the Capitoline would not authenticate it. Identification would require an archaeological context specifically attributable to the sanctuary, ideally with an inscription or closed ritual assemblage, plus material study. No such find has been published.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Scholarship and popular reference works

Modern reception has been dominated by handbooks of Roman religion, legal antiquities, and topography. These commonly regularized the evidence into a single object:

> a sacred flint, housed in Jupiter Feretrius’s temple, representing Jupiter, carried by the fetials, and used to kill the treaty pig.

This concise account is useful but suppresses the distinction between Polybius’s discarded oath-stone and Livy’s sacrificial *silex*.

Samuel Ball Platner and Thomas Ashby’s *Topographical Dictionary of Ancient Rome* embedded the synthesis in English-language topographical scholarship while acknowledging Reid’s dissent. [Platner and Ashby, “Jupiter Feretrius”](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Places/Europe/Italy/Lazio/Roma/Rome/_Texts/PLATOP%2A/Jupiter_Feretrius.html)

### 9.2 Visual reconstruction

The actual stone has no secure ancient representation. Visual culture instead reconstructs its temple, principally from:

- a late republican denarius of P. Cornelius Lentulus Marcellinus showing Marcellus and the *spolia opima* at a small tetrastyle sanctuary;
- literary testimony concerning the sanctuary’s tiny dimensions;
- generalized models of archaic Roman temples.

The coin does not show or label the flint. Images that portray a conspicuous altar-stone, polished axe, meteorite, or freestanding baetyl add details not supplied by the coin or texts.

### 9.3 Neopagan and popular religious reception

Modern Roman-religion revivals sometimes invoke Jupiter Lapis in oath-taking or describe a stone as an aniconic manifestation of Jupiter. Such usage is a modern reconstruction from literary testimonia. A newly selected stone used in a reconstructed rite is a modern ritual object, not the ancient artifact under continuous custody.

### 9.4 Confusion with other sacred stones

Internet summaries frequently merge Jupiter Lapis with:

- the Lapis Niger;
- the rainmaking Lapis Manalis;
- generic Roman baetyls;
- meteoric stones;
- prehistoric thunderstones.

None of those identifications is supported by a continuous ancient source tradition. The name *lapis* alone is not evidence of identity.

### 9.5 Cultural significance

The object’s modern importance lies less in popular fiction than in discussions of:

- aniconism in early Roman religion;
- the relationship of material ritual to international law;
- Rome’s fetial priesthood;
- oath curses as performative acts;
- the Augustan reconstruction of archaic tradition;
- whether Roman antiquarians preserved or invented early religious practice.

No major modern literary, operatic, cinematic, or gaming treatment appears to have materially displaced the scholarly image. Claims of a large modern entertainment afterlife cannot presently be established.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Ancient antiquarian construction

The evidence is already historiographical in antiquity:

- **Polybius** presents the oath while defending his reconstruction of Roman–Carthaginian treaties.
- **Livy** embeds the flint in a narrative about Rome’s earliest remembered treaty procedure.
- **Verrius/Festus/Paul** organizes ritual knowledge through etymological dictionary entries.
- **Servius** explains Virgil through late-antique antiquarian learning.

None is a contemporary archaic ritual manual. Even when these writers drew on priestly archives, their texts interpret early practice through later categories.

### 10.2 The traditional synthesis

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century scholarship generally treated:

- Jupiter Lapis as a divine title;
- the flint as an aniconic Jupiter;
- the fetial flint as resident in Jupiter Feretrius’s temple;
- the pig-killing and stone-casting rites as aspects of the same complex.

This interpretation remains common in dictionaries and general accounts.

### 10.3 Reid’s source-critical challenge

J. S. Reid argued in 1912 that the evidence had been over-harmonized. His enduring contributions were:

- separating the ritual act of throwing a stone from killing a pig with flint;
- questioning whether “Jupiter Lapis” denoted a separately worshipped god or permanent image;
- observing that the temple provenance depends heavily on the vulnerable antiquarian tradition;
- refusing to turn ritual stones automatically into one relic.

Reid’s position does not require denying that Jupiter, flint, oaths, and the Feretrian sanctuary were connected. It denies that identity and continuous custody follow automatically.

### 10.4 Etymological debate

The epithet *Feretrius* has been derived from:

- *ferire*, “strike,” whether in battle or treaty sacrifice;
- *ferre*, “carry,” referring to carrying *spolia opima*;
- *feretrum*, a carrying frame or bier;
- *ferre pacem*, “bring peace”;
- a Greek superlative notion, “the one who surpasses all.”

Ancient explanations conflict. The connection with *ferire* is attractive because the flint “struck” the treaty victim, but ancient wordplay is not by itself historical etymology.

### 10.5 Augustan intervention

The restoration under Augustus affects both sanctuary and evidence. Augustus revived an archaic temple at a moment when the eligibility rules for *spolia opima* had immediate political consequences. His claim to have read Cossus’s linen inscription supported a restrictive interpretation favorable to supreme commanders.

Dylan Sailor has argued that Livy’s treatment distances the historian from Augustus’s alleged discovery and invites doubt about the authority—and possible fabrication—behind it. This concerns the armor inscription rather than the flint, but it warns against assuming that all “archaic” contents seen in the restored temple were unbroken survivals. [Dylan Sailor, “Dirty Linen, Fabrication, and the Authorities of Livy and Augustus”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/236758856_Dirty_Linen_Fabrication_and_the_Authorities_of_Livy_and_Augustus)

### 10.6 Archaeological limits

The temple has no securely identified archaeological footprint. Dionysius’s statement that an ancient trace remained after the Augustan restoration has been interpreted in several ways:

- the restoration preserved the original dimensions;
- a larger temple enclosed the old outline;
- the original footprint was marked on the later floor.

Without an identified site, the alternatives cannot be tested. Nor can archaeology presently answer whether an archaic flint or stone axe was installed there.

### 10.7 Epigraphic limits

The absence of urban epigraphic attestations for *Iuppiter Lapis* matters. It does not prove the title never existed: many ritual formulae were transmitted orally or literarily. It does mean the material record does not corroborate a formal Roman cult under that exact title. The inscriptions for Jupiter Feretrius outside Rome are themselves rare.

### 10.8 Present state of the question

The strongest minimum consensus is:

- Roman treaty and oath ritual employed stones.
- A *silex* struck the sacrificial pig in the fetial ceremony.
- Jupiter guaranteed the compact and supplied the imagined punishment.
- A Roman antiquarian tradition located a flint and sceptre in Jupiter Feretrius’s temple.
- “Jupiter Stone” was an intelligible ancient oath expression.
- No surviving physical stone can be authenticated.

Open questions remain:

- whether *Iuppiter Lapis* was a divine epithet or “Jupiter and the stone”;
- whether the object was an image, symbol, weapon, or all three;
- whether the thrown and striking stones were the same object;
- whether the temple held a unique stone or replaceable implements;
- whether the implement was natural flint, a knapped tool, or a prehistoric polished axe;
- when and how the temple object disappeared.

## 12. Sources

### Primary ancient sources

- **Polybius, *Histories* 3.25.6–9.** Earliest surviving explicit account of the oath by *Δία λίθον*, including holding and discarding the stone.
- **Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 1.24.4–9.** Full Roman–Alban treaty ritual and killing of the pig with *silex*.
- **Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 1.10; 1.33.8; 4.20.** Foundation and enlargement of the Feretrian temple, Cossus’s spoils, and Augustus’s reported inspection.
- **Cicero, *Ad familiares* 7.12.2.** Republican colloquial or emphatic use of the Jupiter-Lapis oath.
- **Cornelius Nepos, *Atticus* 20.3.** Temple unroofed and collapsing; restoration urged by Atticus.
- **Augustus, *Res Gestae* 19.** Imperial record of work on the Capitoline temples of Jupiter Feretrius and Jupiter Tonans.
- **Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities* 2.34.** Romulus, the sanctuary, and its tiny dimensions.
- **Plutarch, *Marcellus* 8; *Romulus* 16.** *Spolia opima* tradition and ancient etymologies of Feretrius.
- **Festus/Paul the Deacon, *De verborum significatu*, entries on Feretrius and *lapidem*.** Temple custody of the sceptre and flint and an oath formula involving casting the stone.
- **Aulus Gellius, *Noctes Atticae* 1.21.4.** Later literary evidence for swearing by Jupiter Lapis.
- **Servius, commentary on *Aeneid* 8.641.** Flint as Jupiter’s ancient *signum* and implement for striking the pig.
- **Propertius 4.10.45–48.** Feretrian altar and Marcellus’s dedication, relevant to the object’s sanctuary context.
