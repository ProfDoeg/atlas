# The dollar sign's claimed origin in the Potosi "PTSI" mint mark: Object Dossier

**Proposed filename:** `potosi_ptsi_mint_mark_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary finding

The subject is not a single portable artifact. It is a **symbol-origin legend attached to a reproducible mint mark**: the claim that the currency sign `$` descends from the Potosí mint’s interlaced abbreviation of *Potosí*, conventionally expanded as **P–T–S–I** or **P–T–S**.

Three related but nonidentical objects must be kept separate:

1. **The historical Potosí mint marks:** first a simple **P**, then an interlaced **PTSI/PTS** monogram struck from dies onto many coins.
2. **The dollar sign:** a handwritten and subsequently typographic currency symbol, `$`, attested in late-eighteenth-century accounts and correspondence.
3. **The Potosí-origin story:** a twentieth-century historical hypothesis, first securely located in Florian Cajori’s 1912 article, where Cajori attributes it to Chilean numismatist José Toribio Medina and rejects it in favor of descent from the scribal abbreviation *pˢ/ps* for *peso(s)*.

The Potosí monogram is real, survives on coins in public and private collections, and sometimes looks strikingly like a one-stroke dollar sign. What cannot presently be demonstrated is a historical act of copying or a documentary sequence by which merchants transformed that mint mark into `$`. The strongest manuscript research—Cajori’s survey, refined by Eric P. Newman—supports the development of `$` from overlapping forms of **p** and superscript **s**, abbreviating *peso(s)*, *piastre(s)*, or *piece(s) of eight*. The Potosí legend is therefore best classified as a **plausible-looking but undocumented origin hypothesis**, not as an established etymology.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Class and names

**Class:** mint mark; monogram; impressed identifying device; later national and civic emblem; alleged ancestor of a currency sign.

Relevant names include:

- **Marca de ceca de Potosí** — “Potosí mint mark.”
- **Sigla de Potosí** — “Potosí abbreviation.”
- **Monograma PTS / PTSI** — “PTS/PTSI monogram.”
- **Anagrama de Potosí** — common modern Spanish description, although *monograma* is technically clearer.
- **Potosí mint mark** — standard English numismatic description.
- **Dollar sign / dollar mark**, `$`.
- **Signo del dólar** and **signo del peso** in Spanish.
- **Cifrão** in Portuguese usage for the related one- or two-stroke sign, historically employed with the *mil-réis* and escudo. Portuguese usage has its own documentary development and should not be treated automatically as evidence for Potosí derivation.

The spelling **Potosi** without an accent appears in older English-language sources and in Latin-letter mint-mark expansions; modern Spanish is **Potosí**.

The mark has frequently been described as containing all four letters **P, T, S, I**, with the dot above the composite sign representing the dot of the *i*. Some later coins omit the dot and are described as **PTS**. Individual dies vary sufficiently that not every component is equally legible.

### 1.2 What physically exists

No unique “original PTSI object” survives as a single relic. The physical record consists of:

- documentary drawings or models for the proposed mint mark;
- steel dies or punches, where preserved or documented;
- impressions of the mark on large numbers of silver and gold coins;
- later drawings, photographs, casts, replicas, and digital extractions;
- modern institutional uses of the monogram.

José Antonio Fuertes López reports that the mark’s design appears in the **1750 “Relación de los instrumentos”** sent from Spain for modernization of the Potosí mint. The underlying document is held by the Historical Archive of the Casa Nacional de Moneda. Its published discussion identifies this as a documentary appearance approximately seventeen years before the monogram was placed on Potosí coins.

### 1.3 Form

The mature colonial mark combines letters by superposition:

- a vertical stem and loop attributable to **P**;
- horizontal or cross-strokes attributable to **T**;
- a curved **S** crossing or winding around the vertical;
- on the 1767–1770 columnarios, a superposed dot interpreted as completing **I**.

The result can resemble `$`, especially when the P loop and T crossbar are weak, worn, incompletely struck, cropped, or visually ignored. That resemblance is genuine. Resemblance alone, however, does not establish descent.

The Potosí mark is not graphically invariant. It changes with:

- denomination;
- die engraver;
- date and political regime;
- hammered versus milled manufacture;
- completeness of strike;
- wear, clipping, corrosion, and later damage;
- modern redrawing.

Fuertes López distinguishes three broad historical forms:

1. **P**, used on colonial hammered coins from the opening of the mint through 1773;
2. **PTSI**, introduced on circular columnario coins beginning in 1767;
3. **PTS**, used in later colonial, revolutionary, and Bolivian issues, with further simplification into the early twentieth century.

### 1.4 Coins carrying the mark

The clearest early carriers are Potosí’s milled “two worlds” or **columnario** coins of Charles III, issued in silver denominations including 8, 4, 2, 1, and ½ real between 1767 and 1770. On 8- and 4-real pieces the PTSI monogram appears twice on the reverse, on either side of the date; on smaller denominations it appears once. The large pieces normally combine:

- the crowned royal arms or royal titulature;
- two hemispheres between the Pillars of Hercules;
- the motto **VTRAQUE VNUM**, “both [worlds are] one”;
- date;
- assayers’ initials;
- denomination;
- two Potosí monograms.

A British Museum catalogue record identifies a surviving silver 8-real coin issued at Potosí in 1770, accession **1849,0122.12**. The catalogue’s existence establishes that independently catalogued institutional specimens survive, but it should not be mistaken for “the” object from which `$` was copied.

From 1773 to 1825 the Potosí mint produced portrait coinage bearing a reduced or undotted PTS form, generally on the reverse near the assayer’s initials. Revolutionary issues of the Provinces of the Río de la Plata in 1813 and 1815 retained a Potosí place mark. Bolivian republican coins continued versions of PTS through the nineteenth century. Fuertes López dates the last regular national use of the traditional PTS family approximately to 1904, with a separate PT form on certain 1900–1908 commercial issues.

### 1.5 Materials and manufacture

The mark itself has no independent material: it is negative engraving in a die and positive relief on a struck coin.

For colonial coinage its material carriers were principally silver alloys, with gold issues also bearing Potosí marks in later periods. The manufacturing sequence was broadly:

1. silver ore extraction, especially from the Cerro Rico mining complex;
2. refining and alloying;
3. casting into bars;
4. reduction to coin metal;
5. preparation of blanks;
6. engraving of dies, including the mint and assayer marks;
7. striking.

Earlier Potosí **macuquinas** were hammered between dies and are characteristically irregular. Institutional accounts describe bars being hammered, blanks cut with shears, and dies struck by hammer. Mechanized circular or cordoned coinage was introduced experimentally while the second mint building was under construction; Potosí columnarios were issued from 1767 to 1770. Hammered macuquinas nevertheless continued until 1773.

The labor system behind the silver cannot be detached from the objects’ material history. Potosí’s mining economy used coerced Indigenous **mita** labor, while the mint’s workshops also employed enslaved Africans and other compelled or dependent labor. This does not prove anything about the origin of `$`, but it is part of the physical production history of the coins invoked by the legend.

### 1.6 The comparison object: `$`

The modern dollar sign is an abstract character rather than a unique artifact. Its principal graphic forms are:

- a capital-S-like curve crossed by **one vertical stroke**;
- a similar curve crossed by **two vertical strokes**.

Unicode unifies these as one character, **U+0024 DOLLAR SIGN**; the one- and two-bar forms are glyph variants, not separately encoded characters. Unicode also records its use for dollar, peso, mil-réis, and escudo currencies. Consequently, a modern font’s choice of one or two bars is not evidence for a particular historical origin.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

## 2.1 Documented origin of the Potosí mint

Spanish-American mints marked coins so that issuing workshops and responsible officials could be identified. A provision of the 1497 monetary ordinances required the first letter of the city of manufacture, with specified emblematic exceptions. This regulatory tradition preceded Potosí.

The mint at Potosí developed after the extraordinary expansion of silver production at Cerro Rico. The first mint building was begun in 1572 and entered operation in 1574 under Viceroy Francisco de Toledo. Alonso de Rincón, transferred from the earlier Peruvian operation, is identified as its first assayer or principal technical officer.

For almost two centuries, Potosí’s normal mint identifier was a **P**, not PTSI. This is a decisive chronological qualification: statements that “the PTSI mark was on Potosí coins from 1573” conflate the mint’s founding with a mark introduced much later.

## 2.2 Creation of the PTSI mark

The most specific published reconstruction presently available is Fuertes López’s:

- On **3 October 1750**, six royal orders concerned appointments, equipment, models, and modernization of the Potosí operation.
- A surviving **Relación de los instrumentos que se remiten…** includes early drawn forms of the proposed Potosí abbreviation.
- The developed monogram first appeared on Potosí’s milled columnario coinage in **1767**.
- On these coins the dotted monogram can be read as **PTSI**.
- After the change to portrait coinage in **1773**, the dot was normally omitted, yielding **PTS**.

The mark was therefore not ancient, indigenous, or spontaneously invented by North American merchants. It was a Spanish imperial administrative identifier designed for a mechanizing royal mint.

## 2.3 Earliest secure attestation of the Potosí-origin theory

The earliest source securely recovered for the origin claim is Florian Cajori’s **“The Evolution of the Dollar Mark,” December 1912**. Cajori wrote:

> An interesting hypothesis is advanced by the noted historian, T. F. [sic] Medina, of Santiago de Chile. He suggests that perhaps the dollar mark was derived from the stamp of the mint of Potosi in Bolivia.

The scholar intended was almost certainly the Chilean bibliographer and numismatist **José Toribio Medina** (1852–1930). Cajori’s initials “T. F.” appear to be erroneous.

No earlier printed passage by Medina explicitly stating the hypothesis was located in the sources consulted. Cajori supplies no footnote for it. It may have reached him through correspondence or conversation. Accordingly:

- **Secure earliest public attestation:** Cajori, 1912.
- **Attributed proposer:** José Toribio Medina.
- **Exact date and medium of Medina’s own formulation:** unresolved.
- **Claim that the theory was already traditional in colonial Potosí:** unverified.

Medina’s major *Las monedas coloniales hispano-americanas* appeared in 1919. Its post-1912 publication date means it cannot be the source from which Cajori first learned the idea unless related material circulated earlier.

## 2.4 Earliest attestation of `$`

Cajori examined printed works and hundreds of manuscripts from Spanish America, the Philippines, New Orleans, and British North America. He found a long family of abbreviations for *peso/pesos*:

- **Pes.**
- **p**
- **ps**
- **pˢ**, with the *s* raised;
- **pss.**
- **pos.**

A Mexican manuscript from Oaxaca dated **1696** used a *p* with a sign resembling a horizontal 8. Cajori showed paleographically that the upper element meant omitted letters *os*, because the same sign appeared in the abbreviation of *vezinos*. This was therefore evidence for scribal abbreviation, not an “8” copied from an eight-real coin.

Cajori’s most celebrated transitional evidence was a pair of contemporary copies of a **1778 letter from Oliver Pollock to George Rogers Clark**. In the accounts at the end, a recognizable `$` and a flourished *pˢ* both occur. Cajori interpreted the coexistence as the sign “in the making.”

Eric P. Newman’s later, wider study moved the earliest ligature stage somewhat earlier. His published abstract states that British merchants entering West Florida and Louisiana after the 1764 political transfer converted Spanish **pS** into a convenient ligature beginning about **1768**. Newman also concluded that the first conventional dollar sign in type dates to **1799**, not 1797.

The date sequence is therefore:

- scribal *p/ps/pˢ* abbreviations: centuries before the mature `$`;
- PTSI design in a Potosí administrative document: 1750;
- PTSI on coins: 1767;
- emerging `$` ligatures in Anglo-American mercantile writing: approximately 1768 onward in Newman’s reconstruction;
- unambiguous examples in Pollock copies: 1778;
- conventional typographic `$`: by the end of the 1790s.

Chronology alone cannot decide between PTSI and *pˢ*, because both visible forms existed shortly before `$` emerged. The crucial distinction is that the *pˢ* hypothesis possesses a documented series of handwritten intermediate forms and occurs in the same accounting contexts; the PTSI hypothesis presently possesses resemblance and opportunity but no demonstrable transmission.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because a mint mark is reproduced rather than transferred intact, its “chain of custody” is a chain of **design authority, die manufacture, coin circulation, collecting, and historiographical transmission**.

### 1497–1574: regulatory ancestry

The Spanish monarchy’s mint-mark regime required coins to identify the place of manufacture. This system supplied the institutional class to which Potosí’s P and later PTSI belonged.

### 1572–1574: establishment at Potosí

Viceroy Francisco de Toledo ordered minting capacity transferred or newly established near the silver source. The first mint was built near the Plaza del Regocijo. The Potosí archive preserves official documentation of the institution’s creation and operation.

### 1574–1766: the simple-P period

The mint used **P** on hammered macuquina coinage. Individual pieces left royal custody through wages, taxes, official payments, merchant transactions, bullion trade, remittance, smuggling, theft, shipwreck, hoarding, and recoining. No continuous custody can be reconstructed for the coin population.

The coins circulated through the Spanish Americas and entered Atlantic and Pacific trade. Potosí silver reached Europe and Asia, sometimes as coins and sometimes after melting or recoining. This circulation explains how North American merchants could encounter Potosí pieces, but it does not establish that they copied their mint mark.

### 1630s–1650s: fraud, seizure, and revalidation

Potosí’s great mint fraud involved systematic underweight or debased coinage and corruption among mint officials. Investigations produced prosecutions and severe sentences. Royal authorities ordered suspect issues withdrawn, countermarked, or replaced. For individual surviving coins, these interventions may appear as countermarks, overstrikes, clipping, or recoinage.

The scandal belongs to the custody history of Potosí coinage generally, but not specifically to PTSI: the monogram had not yet been introduced.

### 1750: design transmitted from Spain

Models and machinery were dispatched for a new circular-coinage establishment. The “Relación de instrumentos” reportedly contains the proposed Potosí monogram. Custody of the surviving paper lies with the Historical Archive of the Casa Nacional de Moneda in Potosí.

### 1767–1770: PTSI impressed on columnarios

Dies bearing the monogram were used at Potosí. Every surviving coin is a separate object with its own, usually incomplete provenance. Most lack records from mint issue to modern collection.

### 1773–1825: reduced PTS under Spanish rule

Portrait coins carried the PTS form. Control passed between royal and revolutionary forces during the wars of independence. The same mint, dies, bullion, or personnel could change political custody while the Potosí place identifier persisted.

### 1813 and 1815: Provinces of the Río de la Plata

The revolutionary government ordered new coin types at Potosí. A decree of **13 April 1813** required the coins to bear information identifying the assayer, place, date, and value. Issues conventionally regarded as the first national Argentine coinage retained the Potosí monogram.

### 1825–1904: Bolivian state use

After independence, Bolivia continued to coin at Potosí. Legislation of 17 August 1825 and 20 November 1826 preserved place and assayer identifiers. Coins with Simón Bolívar’s bust appeared from 1827, and later national arms types continued the PTS mark. The design gradually simplified and reportedly disappeared from ordinary national coinage after about 1904.

### Nineteenth–twentieth centuries: dispersal into collections

Potosí coins entered private collections, museums, auction inventories, excavated hoards, and shipwreck recoveries. A British Museum 1770 8-real specimen entered that museum in 1849. The Casa Nacional de Moneda and Banco Central de Bolivia retain national numismatic collections, including material from the mint’s earliest production onward.

There is no basis for choosing any surviving coin as the unique exemplar that inspired `$`.

### 1912 onward: custody of the legend

The idea passed through a textual chain:

1. José Toribio Medina allegedly proposed it, by an unidentified pre-1912 communication.
2. Cajori published and criticized it in 1912.
3. Cajori repeated the discussion in *A History of Mathematical Notations*, volume II (1929).
4. General reference works, numismatic writing, tourism, newspapers, and websites repeated it, often omitting Cajori’s rejection.
5. Modern images isolate and enlarge the mark from coins, making its resemblance to `$` more visually compelling than it is on a complete coin.
6. The story became part of Potosí civic and Bolivian heritage discourse and part of broader claims that the modern dollar descends from Spanish-American silver.

The legend’s chain is therefore substantially more reconstructable than any transmission from the eighteenth-century coin mark to the eighteenth-century scribal symbol.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Is the PTSI mint mark authentic?

Yes. The interlaced Potosí mark is documented on surviving eighteenth-century coins and in numismatic catalogues. Institutional examples leave no serious doubt that it was an official Potosí mint identifier.

Individual coins can, however, be:

- contemporary counterfeits;
- later casts;
- modern reproductions;
- altered or too worn for reliable attribution;
- misidentified auction or tourist pieces.

Authenticating one coin does not authenticate the origin legend.

### 4.2 Is PTSI the same object as `$`?

No identity has been established. They are distinct signs with some shared graphic components.

The Potosí mark encodes a place name. The dollar sign functions as a handwritten and typographic monetary abbreviation. A resemblance can be produced by:

- incomplete striking of PTSI;
- erosion of the P and T elements;
- selective modern tracing;
- the natural convergence of superimposed Latin letters around S and a vertical stem.

For the two signs to be historically identical, evidence would be needed that users saw the mint mark, understood or abstracted it, and adopted it in accounts. No such statement, copybook instruction, commercial manual, annotated coin, or sequence of drawings has been identified.

### 4.3 Case for Potosí derivation

The strongest arguments are:

- **Visual similarity:** the center of PTSI can closely resemble `$`.
- **Chronological possibility:** PTSI appeared on coins by 1767, near Newman’s earliest emerging-dollar-sign dates.
- **Commercial opportunity:** Spanish-American silver circulated widely in British North America.
- **Semantic proximity:** the mark appeared on coins English speakers called Spanish dollars.
- **Repetition:** on large columnarios, two conspicuous mint marks flank the date.

These considerations make the hypothesis conceivable. They do not make it documented.

### 4.4 Case against Potosí derivation

The objections are stronger:

- Spanish scribal **p/ps/pˢ** abbreviations for *peso(s)* predate the Potosí PTSI mark.
- Surviving manuscripts display intermediate forms connecting *pˢ* and `$`.
- The transition occurs in accounts where the monetary word was being abbreviated.
- The sign appeared in regions and hands not shown to be copying Potosí devices.
- Writers who advanced Potosí derivation did not produce manuscript evidence of a coin mark being imitated.
- Early `$` forms often retain features naturally explained by a written **p**, including one long descender, rather than by a deliberate four-letter mint monogram.
- Potosí was only one of several sources of Spanish dollars circulating in North America.
- The abbreviation hypothesis explains why essentially the same sign came to denote both pesos and dollars.

Cajori explicitly rejected the Potosí proposal because forms combining *p* and *s* as abbreviations for *peso* existed before the Potosí mint was established. His formulation somewhat oversimplifies the issue—the mature PTSI mark is later still—but the underlying paleographic objection remains.

### 4.5 Rival origin claims

Other proposed ancestors include:

- **U superimposed on S**, meaning “United States”;
- the **Pillars of Hercules** and their winding *Plus Ultra* scrolls;
- **P8**, for “piece of eight”;
- **R8**, for “eight reales”;
- a figure **8** between separator lines;
- Roman **HS/IIS** for the sestertius;
- the Christian **IHS** monogram;
- German thaler or Hamburg accounting signs;
- heraldic, religious, solar, or occult devices.

Most are products of retrospective visual analogy. The United States theory is chronologically untenable because peso-related signs existed before or independently of adoption of the federal dollar and early users did not consistently draw a U-shaped component. The Pillars theory, like PTSI, has abundant iconographic resemblance but lacks the requisite manuscript chain.

### 4.6 Typographic dispute: 1797 or 1799

Cajori identified Chauncey Lee’s *The American Accomptant* (1797) as the earliest known printed occurrence. Newman later argued that Lee’s special character was not the conventional `$` and placed the first normal typographic dollar sign in **1799**. This is a classification dispute about what counts as the same glyph, not a challenge to the earlier handwritten history.

### 4.7 Authority to authenticate

Authority has shifted among:

- the Spanish Crown and mint officers, who authorized dies and monetary standards;
- assayers, whose initials warranted weight and fineness;
- revolutionary and Bolivian governments, which legislated coin designs;
- museums and numismatic societies, which catalogue surviving pieces;
- paleographers and historians, who assess handwritten development;
- encoding bodies and type designers, which now define the abstract digital character and its permissible glyph variants.

No modern mint, museum, or encoding body can make the Potosí etymology true by institutional declaration. It is a historical proposition dependent on evidence.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Original function

PTSI’s primary function was administrative and fiduciary:

- identify Potosí as the issuing mint;
- distinguish its coins from those of Mexico, Lima, Santiago, and other workshops;
- permit responsibility for deficient manufacture to be traced;
- accompany date, denomination, and assayer marks;
- participate in the Crown’s warranty of weight and fineness.

It was not originally a sacred sign and had no documented ritual handling rules.

### 6.2 Political function

The mark’s endurance across regimes made it a compact sign of possession of the Potosí mint. When revolutionary armies occupied Potosí in 1813 and 1815, they replaced royal imagery and legends but retained a mark identifying the place of manufacture. Control of the mint provided:

- access to monetary metal;
- the ability to pay armies and administrations;
- a medium for proclaiming sovereignty;
- continuity with an accepted regional coin standard.

In this sense the mark acted as an institutional signature whose political authority could change while its geographical referent remained stable.

### 6.3 Symbol of Potosí and Bolivia

Modern use frequently detaches PTS from its technical function and presents it as:

- an emblem of Potosí’s global monetary importance;
- a symbol of Bolivian numismatic heritage;
- supposed evidence that Potosí “gave the dollar its sign”;
- a compressed reminder of Cerro Rico’s wealth and colonial exploitation.

The Banco Central de Bolivia’s 2023 commemorative issue for the second mint building’s 250th anniversary includes the mint mark as a heritage symbol. Such use documents the legend’s cultural importance; it does not settle the etymology.

### 6.4 Function of `$`

The dollar sign moved far beyond coinage. It has served as:

- abbreviation for pesos, dollars, mil-réis, and escudos;
- accounting and pricing sign;
- typographic character;
- symbol of wealth, finance, capitalism, greed, or commercial success;
- programming and command-language operator;
- spreadsheet, variable, interpolation, and string marker;
- component of logos and popular iconography.

Unicode’s unification of one- and two-bar designs confirms that current digital practice treats them as the same abstract character. Currency identity must be established contextually or by codes such as USD, CAD, MXN, and others, not from `$` alone.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The Potosí monogram legend

**Full form:** The letters P, T, S, and I in *Potosí* were superimposed as the Potosí mint mark. Spanish dollars bearing it circulated throughout the world and especially in British North America. Merchants copied or simplified it, producing `$`.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Cajori, December 1912, attributed to José Toribio Medina.

**Evidentiary tier:** early twentieth-century scholarly hypothesis; transmission mechanism unverified.

**Later elaborations:**

- Potosí coins “were the first dollars.”
- The mark appeared on all Potosí coins from the sixteenth century.
- North Americans simply adopted it from the reverse of the eight-real coin.
- The United States intentionally chose a Bolivian or Spanish-American emblem.
- The two vertical strokes derive simultaneously from PTSI and the Pillars of Hercules.

The claim that PTSI was present from the mint’s founding is demonstrably inaccurate: published mint histories describe simple **P** as the normal mark until the eighteenth-century modernization, with PTSI appearing on coins in 1767.

### 7.2 The Pillars of Hercules legend

**Story:** The two vertical strokes are the Pillars of Hercules, while the S-shaped curve is the ribbon carrying **Plus Ultra**.

**Early printed circulation:** nineteenth-century discussions cited by Cajori, including the *New American Cyclopaedia* and *Notes and Queries*.

**Tier:** nineteenth-century antiquarian hypothesis; visually persuasive, paleographically undocumented.

Pillar dollars were indeed widely circulated and showed pillars with banners. The evidentiary problem is the absence of manuscript stages showing accountants copying that device.

### 7.3 The United States monogram legend

**Story:** `$` is U written over S, for “United States.”

**Attestation:** circulating in nineteenth-century newspaper and reference literature; Cajori cites an 1876 *Notes and Queries* appearance drawn from an older clipping.

**Tier:** patriotic retrospective etymology.

The sign or its immediate antecedents were in use for Spanish-American money before the federal dollar was fully established. One-stroke early forms also do not preserve a recognizable U. No inventor or adoption decree is known.

### 7.4 The piece-of-eight legends

Variants derive `$` from:

- **P8**, “piece of eight”;
- **R8**, “eight reales”;
- an **8** between two pillars;
- an 8 with separator lines.

**Tier:** nineteenth-century lexicographical and antiquarian speculation.

These accounts correctly associate the dollar with the eight-real coin but lack a continuous written transformation.

### 7.5 Roman and religious ancestries

Proposed sources include the Roman sestertius signs **HS** or **IIS**, the Christian **IHS** monogram, Solomon’s Temple pillars, Phoenician pillars, sun worship, and other sacred emblems.

**Tier:** antiquarian or esoteric accretions. No unbroken documentary path connects them to late-eighteenth-century American accountancy.

### 7.6 The “first global dollar” narrative

Potosí’s eight-real coins formed part of a transoceanic monetary system and materially influenced the monetary environment from which the U.S. dollar emerged. That broader statement is much better supported than the narrow claim about the graphic sign.

The narrative becomes misleading when it collapses four propositions:

1. Potosí was a major silver source — documented.
2. Spanish-American eight-real coins were called dollars in English — documented.
3. The U.S. dollar was modeled on the circulating Spanish dollar — documented.
4. `$` was copied from Potosí’s PTSI — unproven.

The truth of the first three does not entail the fourth.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Status of the mark

The historical PTSI/PTS mark survives on many coins and in archival documents. There is no sole original and therefore no single present location.

Important repositories include:

- **Casa Nacional de Moneda, Potosí, Bolivia:** national museum and archive occupying the second mint complex; custodian of coin, document, machinery, art, and photographic collections.
- **Banco Central de Bolivia:** custodian of national numismatic holdings, including examples reaching back to early Potosí production.
- **British Museum, London:** holder of catalogued Potosí issues, including the cited 1770 silver 8 reales, accession 1849,0122.12.
- **American Numismatic Society, New York:** research collections and publisher of major studies.
- numerous other museums, universities, private collections, auction houses, shipwreck collections, and archaeological repositories.

The 1750 “Relación de instrumentos” is reported in the **Archivo Histórico de la Casa Nacional de Moneda**. A complete diplomatic edition and high-resolution public facsimile would be especially valuable because this document appears to contain the earliest design-stage evidence for PTSI.

### 8.2 Status of the dollar sign

`$` exists as a global abstract character rather than a controllable physical artifact. Its current standard identity is:

- **Unicode:** U+0024 DOLLAR SIGN;
- glyphs: one or two vertical strokes;
- uses: multiple dollar and peso currencies, with historic use for additional currencies.

No institution owns the character. Particular typeface designs may be copyrighted or otherwise protected, but the abstract sign is part of general writing and encoding systems.

### 8.3 Status of the origin claim

The Potosí claim remains unresolved only in the weak sense that absolute exclusion is difficult. In positive evidentiary terms it is unsupported: no historical transmission has been shown.

The prevailing explanation adopted by the U.S. Bureau of Engraving and Printing is evolution from the Spanish or Mexican **Ps** abbreviation for pesos, piastres, or pieces of eight. The Bureau notes that the sign was used before adoption of the U.S. dollar in 1785.

There is no present legal ownership, restitution, or repatriation dispute over “the PTSI mark” as a singular object. Individual coins may, of course, have separate provenance issues, including illicit excavation, colonial removal, shipwreck title, theft, or export-law disputes. None can be generalized to the whole sign without item-specific evidence.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Heritage and tourism

In Potosí, the monogram appears in museum interpretation, numismatic displays, journalism, souvenirs, and local historical narratives. The resemblance to `$` is exceptionally effective in tourism because it gives a familiar global symbol an immediately visible local ancestor.

This interpretive convenience has encouraged the hypothesis to migrate from “perhaps” to “is” in popular retellings.

### 9.2 Numismatic collecting

Potosí columnarios are marketed partly through:

- global circulation of the Spanish dollar;
- the short 1767–1770 Potosí columnario series;
- association with the Pillars of Hercules;
- resemblance of the mint mark to `$`;
- colonial and shipwreck histories.

Commercial descriptions often repeat the origin claim without supplying manuscript evidence. They are useful for documenting reception and the market life of the legend, but not as independent proof.

### 9.3 State commemoration

The Banco Central de Bolivia issued a commemorative coin for the **250th anniversary of the Casa Nacional de Moneda, 1773–2023**. Its design includes the Potosí mint mark. The coin is a modern official replica or revival of the emblem rather than continued use as a routine production mark.

### 9.4 Popular graphics

Modern diagrams commonly:

1. isolate a PTSI mark from a coin;
2. enlarge and rotate it;
3. darken the S and central stroke;
4. fade the remaining P and T elements;
5. place `$` beside it.

Such graphics demonstrate a possible visual reduction, not an eighteenth-century sequence. They can inadvertently manufacture an appearance of documentary evolution.

### 9.5 Digital afterlife

The dollar sign is among the most ubiquitous computer characters because it entered early character sets and ASCII, then Unicode. Its technical uses now include:

- shell variables;
- regular-expression anchors or substitutions;
- programming-language variables;
- string interpolation;
- spreadsheet absolute references;
- mathematical typesetting delimiters in some systems.

This vast nonmonetary career belongs to `$`, not to PTSI. Linking every digital `$` genealogically to Potosí repeats the disputed premise.

### 9.6 Political meaning

The story may be mobilized to emphasize that:

- U.S. monetary culture did not arise in isolation;
- Spanish-American and Indigenous labor histories lie beneath Atlantic money;
- Potosí was central to early modern globalization;
- Latin American peso usage of `$` is not derivative from the modern United States.

The last point is substantially sound: `$` was associated with the Spanish-American peso environment before it became exclusively identified in popular English with U.S. currency. That does not select PTSI as its graphic source.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 José Toribio Medina

Medina’s scholarship established foundational catalogues of Spanish-American coinage. Cajori’s 1912 attribution makes him the earliest named advocate of the Potosí theory, but the absence of a cited Medina text is a major evidentiary gap.

Further archival work should search:

- Cajori correspondence;
- Medina correspondence and papers in Chilean repositories;
- pre-1912 numismatic journals;
- manuscript drafts or notes associated with Medina’s colonial-coin studies.

Until such a source is found, it is safest to write “attributed by Cajori to Medina,” not “invented by Medina in [specific year].”

### 10.2 Florian Cajori

Cajori transformed the subject from visual speculation into paleographic investigation. His 1912 article:

- listed rival theories;
- demanded manuscript evidence;
- reproduced stages of *pˢ* development;
- identified the Pollock letter;
- treated the Potosí hypothesis directly;
- concluded that `$` was the lineal descendant of *pˢ*.

His tone toward rival theories is sometimes polemical, but his central methodological advance remains decisive: a symbol’s ancestry should be demonstrated through dated usages rather than similarity alone.

In 1929 Cajori revisited the subject in *A History of Mathematical Notations* and in “New Data on the Origin and Spread of the Dollar Mark.” The latter appeared in *The Scientific Monthly*, volume 29, pages 212–216.

### 10.3 Eric P. Newman

Newman’s “The Dollar Sign: Its Written and Printed Origins,” published in the American Numismatic Society conference volume *America’s Silver Dollars*, is the major modern extension of Cajori’s work. Bibliographies differ between the 1993 conference date and the volume’s 1995 publication date; both should be stated where precision matters.

Newman:

- assembled further manuscript and typographic evidence;
- placed an early ligature development among British merchants in West Florida and Louisiana about 1768;
- distinguished handwritten evolution from typographic manufacture;
- rejected claims of a single conscious inventor;
- revised the first conventional printed `$` to 1799;
- investigated Chauncey Lee’s 1797 specially constructed signs.

Newman’s research correspondence from 1988–1995 is digitized by the Newman Numismatic Portal, allowing parts of the research process to be inspected.

### 10.4 Potosí mint historiography

Humberto F. Burzio, José Antonio Fuertes López, Carlos Lazo García, Arnaldo Cunietti-Ferrando, and other Latin American numismatists have reconstructed:

- the foundation and administration of the mint;
- assayer successions;
- the 1640s fraud;
- building and machinery changes;
- die varieties;
- the transition from P to PTSI/PTS;
- revolutionary and republican coinage.

Fuertes López’s publication is particularly important for disentangling the simple-P period from the 1750 design proposal and 1767 coin debut.

### 10.5 Discredited but persistent propositions

The following formulations should not be repeated as facts:

- “PTSI was used from 1573 onward.”
- “Potosí coins carried the same exact symbol as `$`.”
- “The United States adopted the Potosí emblem officially.”
- “The two bars prove derivation from the two Pillars of Hercules.”
- “Chauncey Lee invented `$` in 1797.”
- “Robert Morris invented `$`.”
- “Oliver Pollock invented `$`.”

The surviving evidence is consistent with a decentralized scribal evolution, not a named inventor.

### 10.6 State of the primary evidence

Strong primary evidence includes:

- royal monetary ordinances;
- the 1750 equipment and model inventory;
- surviving dated coins;
- eighteenth-century account books and correspondence;
- Pollock letter copies;
- Robert Morris manuscripts;
- early printed arithmetic and commercial manuals;
- later government and institutional catalogues.

Weaknesses include:

- incomplete publication of the Potosí archive;
- uncertain identification of Medina’s original statement;
- dispersed private manuscripts;
- the difficulty of distinguishing a deliberately formed `$` from an idiosyncratic *pˢ*;
- cataloguing changes and inaccessible high-resolution images;
- modern redrawings that suppress details of the mint mark.

### 10.7 Best-supported present conclusion

The evidence supports the following hierarchy:

- **Certain:** Potosí used a real interlaced PTSI/PTS mint mark.
- **Certain:** some forms resemble `$`.
- **Certain:** Potosí silver circulated internationally and was part of the Spanish-dollar monetary world.
- **Certain:** the PTSI form appeared on coins before the clearest surviving `$` examples.
- **Probable to very probable:** `$` developed from the handwritten abbreviation *pˢ/ps* for *peso(s)* and related p-initial monetary terms.
- **Possible but unsupported:** exposure to coin devices, including PTSI or the Pillars, influenced some writers’ graphic choices.
- **Not established:** `$` derives specifically from Potosí’s PTSI mark.
- **False as commonly stated:** PTSI was the Potosí mark throughout the mint’s history from 1573.

## 11. Chronology

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **1497** | Spanish monetary ordinances require place-identifying mint marks; documentary institutional ancestry. |
| **1565** | Royal ordinance provides rules for the Peruvian mint system later applied at Potosí. |
| **1572** | Foundations of the first Potosí mint building laid. |
| **1574** | Potosí mint enters operation under Viceroy Francisco de Toledo; early coins use **P**. |
| **1574–1773** | Hammered macuquinas bearing the simple P are produced, with changing royal, date, and assayer devices. |
| **1617 onward** | Dates become regular control elements on Potosí coinage, according to published Bolivian monetary history. |
| **1630s–1650s** | Great Potosí mint fraud, investigation, punishment, withdrawal, countermarking, and replacement of deficient coins. |
| **1696** | Oaxaca manuscript later examined by Cajori shows a *peso* abbreviation resembling p plus horizontal 8; paleographic context identifies the upper element as omitted *os*, not the numeral 8. |
| **3 October 1750** | Royal orders and equipment documentation for modernizing Potosí; surviving “Relación de instrumentos” reportedly contains early PTS design drawings. |
| **1759–1773** | Second Potosí mint building constructed. |
| **1767** | PTSI, including a superposed dot, first appears on Potosí’s milled columnario coins. |
| **1767–1770** | Potosí columnario production; PTSI appears twice on large denominations and once on smaller ones. |
| **c. 1768** | Newman’s reconstruction dates early British mercantile conversion of Spanish *pS* into ligature forms in West Florida/Louisiana. |
| **1773** | Portrait coinage begins in the new mint; mark normally reduced to undotted PTS. Hammered macuquina production ends. |
| **1778** | Contemporary copies of Oliver Pollock’s letter to George Rogers Clark contain both a recognizable `$` and flourished *pˢ*. |
| **1785** | The United States adopts the dollar as its monetary unit; `$` or its antecedent was already in circulation. |
| **1792** | Robert Morris manuscript evidence includes a one-stroke dollar sign. |
| **1794** | First U.S. silver dollars coined. |
| **1797** | Chauncey Lee’s *American Accomptant* prints a special monetary character long described as the first printed dollar sign; Newman disputes its identity as the conventional glyph. |
| **1799** | Newman’s date for the first conventional `$` in type. |
| **1802** | William A. Washington uses `$20`, `$30`, and `$40` in a letter, according to Cajori. |
| **1813** | Provinces of the Río de la Plata decree new coinage at Potosí, retaining place identification through the PTS mark. |
| **1815** | Further revolutionary Potosí issues; accounting records reflect transition toward the title Casa Nacional de Moneda. |
| **1825** | End of Spanish rule; Bolivian legislation continues place and assayer identification. |
| **1827–1862** | Bolivian bust coinage uses PTS. |
| **1864–1904** | National-arms coinage continues increasingly simplified PTS forms. |
| **1876–1877** | *Notes and Queries* publishes several competing dollar-sign origin theories, including US and Pillars explanations. |
| **1897–1899** | Educational and numismatic literature calls attention to the early printed sign in Lee’s 1797 arithmetic. |
| **c. 1904** | Traditional PTS family ceases to be regularly identifiable on Bolivian national coinage, according to Fuertes López. |
| **Before Dec. 1912** | José Toribio Medina reportedly proposes Potosí derivation; original communication unidentified. |
| **December 1912** | Cajori publishes the earliest securely located account of the Potosí theory and rejects it in favor of *pˢ*. |
| **1919** | Medina publishes *Las monedas coloniales hispano-americanas*. |
| **1929** | Cajori publishes *A History of Mathematical Notations*, vol. II, and “New Data on the Origin and Spread of the Dollar Mark.” |
| **1945** | Burzio publishes *La Ceca de la Villa Imperial de Potosí y la moneda colonial*. |
| **1958** | Burzio’s *Diccionario de la moneda hispanoamericana* consolidates mint terminology. |
| **1988–1995** | Eric Newman conducts documentary and typographic research; correspondence is now digitized. |
| **1995** | Newman’s full study appears in *America’s Silver Dollars*, following the 1993 conference. |
| **2023** | Bolivia commemorates 250 years of the second Casa Nacional de Moneda with a coin displaying the historic mint mark. |
| **2025** | Fuertes López’s detailed Spanish-language study of the mint mark is republished online, emphasizing the 1750 documentary design and 1767 numismatic debut. |
| **Present** | PTSI survives materially on coins and archivally in Potosí; `$` is Unicode U+0024; the Potosí derivation remains a widely circulated but undocumented legend. |

### Assessment of source quality

The strongest evidence for the history of `$` is manuscript and paleographic. The strongest evidence for PTSI is archival and numismatic. Popular history and commercial coin pages are useful mainly for documenting the legend’s modern diffusion.

The central unresolved source question is the missing original statement by José Toribio Medina. Until it is recovered, Cajori’s 1912 report remains both the earliest secure attestation and the only evidence for Medina’s exact role.
