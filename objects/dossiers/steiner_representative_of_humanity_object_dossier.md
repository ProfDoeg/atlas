# Steiner's "The Representative of Humanity Between Lucifer and Ahriman": Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier tracks the monumental, unfinished elm-wood figure group now displayed in the Second Goetheanum at Dornach, Switzerland—not every artwork employing the same “middle motif.” The related pastel designs, cupola painting, engraved-glass window, surviving small studies, and full-size modelling-stage version are treated as associated works and documentary witnesses, not as interchangeable manifestations of the same physical object.

The sculpture unquestionably exists and has unusually strong continuity: historical photographs document its modelling, the assembly and transport of its laminated wood blocks, carving in the Goetheanum carpentry complex, and its later installation in the Second Goetheanum. No competing object claims to be the original. The important uncertainties concern exact dimensions, the respective authorship of Rudolf Steiner and Edith Maryon, the identities and shares of assistants, the chronology of individual models, how much remained unfinished, and whether the sculpture’s present room accords with Steiner’s final intentions.

Evidence labels used below:

- **Documented:** supported by contemporary correspondence, photographs, construction records, recorded lectures, or institutional custody.
- **Institutional account:** stated by the Goetheanum or Anthroposophical Society, sometimes without a published archival citation.
- **Later testimony:** recollection recorded after the event.
- **Interpretation/tradition:** an anthroposophical or scholarly reading rather than an empirically demonstrable property.
- **Unresolved:** evidence is incomplete or conflicting.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity and names

The object is a monumental, multi-figure carved wooden ensemble conceived for the eastern rear of the stage beneath the smaller cupola of the First Goetheanum.

Its principal German name is:

- *Der Menschheitsrepräsentant zwischen Luzifer und Ahriman*—“The Representative of Humanity between Lucifer and Ahriman.”
- Short forms include *Der Menschheitsrepräsentant* and, throughout the documentary record, *die Gruppe* (“the Group”).
- English institutional usage includes *The Representative of Humanity* and *The Representative of Humanity between Lucifer and Ahriman*.
- French usage includes *Le Représentant de l’humanité entre Lucifer et Ahriman*.

The now-standard title should not be mistaken for a single, fixed authorial title. Steiner called the central figure, at different times, *Holzstatue* (“wooden statue”), *Hauptfigur* (“principal figure”), *Idealgestalt* (“ideal form”), *Urmensch* (“primordial human”), *typischer Mensch*, *eine Art Menschheitsrepräsentant*, *Christus-ähnliche Figur*, *Christus-Mensch*, and simply *Christus*. This lexical variation is important: sometimes he emphasized a universal or archetypal human being, sometimes the Christ being, and sometimes resisted allowing a label to substitute for visual experience. The 2020 documentary monograph collects approximately eighty pages of his statements about the motif. [Stephan Stockmar’s detailed review of the monograph](https://wortgartenwerk.de/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Stockmar-Im-Spannungsfeld-von-Weltenkraeften.pdf)

*Menschheitsrepräsentant* is an ordinary German compound: *Menschheit* (“humanity”) plus *Repräsentant* (“representative”). It does not mean a portrait of a statistically representative individual; in Steiner’s usage it indicates an archetypal or universally significant human form. *Lucifer* derives through Christian Latin from Latin *lux* (“light”) and *ferre* (“to bear”), while *Ahriman* is the Middle Persian form associated with Zoroastrian Angra Mainyu. Steiner’s Lucifer and Ahriman, however, are distinctly anthroposophical beings and should not be treated as unmodified borrowings from either conventional Christianity or historical Zoroastrianism.

### Object class

Physically it is:

- a monumental carved and assembled wood sculpture;
- an architectural-stage ensemble designed for a specific building;
- an unfinished collaborative artwork;
- an institutional heritage object under cantonal monument protection.

Functionally, it occupies an ambiguous category. It presents Christ and spiritual beings and is contemplated within an esoteric Christian movement, but the Goetheanum does not formally classify it as a liturgical image or object of worship. Calling it a “cult object” therefore describes aspects of reception and setting more safely than its official institutional function.

### Dimensions, weight, and material

Published dimensions vary:

- “more than eight metres” in some institutional summaries;
- approximately nine metres in Steiner’s recorded descriptions and many later publications;
- approximately 9.5 metres in recent specialist publications.

The most defensible formulation is **approximately 9–9.5 metres high**. A frequently repeated mass is **about twenty metric tonnes**, but no published weighing report or engineering schedule was located in the accessible record. The discrepancy between “over eight,” “nine,” and “nine-and-a-half” metres probably reflects rounding, differences between the central figure and complete rocky ensemble, or imprecise secondary repetition—not rival objects.

The principal material is **elm**, German *Ulme* or *Rüster*. The object was not cut from one tree or monolithic trunk. Photographs and technical discussion document the production of enormous **laminated or glued elm blocks**, their transport into the high studio, rough carving by assistants, and finishing by Maryon and Steiner. Work on the wooden execution began at the end of May 1917. [Technical and chronological summary](https://wortgartenwerk.de/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Stockmar-Im-Spannungsfeld-von-Weltenkraeften.pdf)

The precise source of the timber—forest, supplier, individual trees, purchase price, seasoning history, and adhesive composition—has not been established in the accessible published sources. The available documentation establishes the species and laminated construction but not a quarry-like “material provenance.”

### Composition

The group contains six principal figural presences if repeated manifestations are counted separately:

1. **The central Representative of Humanity/Christ.** A standing, forward-moving human figure occupies the compositional middle. The left arm rises; the right arm extends downward. The figure neither strikes nor physically grapples with the adversarial beings.

2. **Upper or falling Lucifer.** At the viewer’s upper right, a winged being falls or collapses from a rocky zone.

3. **Lower Ahriman.** At the base, within a cave-like constriction, an emaciated, hardened, earth-bound figure is trapped in or by the materiality to which he is attached.

4. **A second Lucifer.** At the viewer’s left appears another luciferic figure, conceived as not yet directly overcome by the central figure.

5. **A second Ahriman.** A corresponding secondary ahrimanic presence occupies the left side.

6. **The rock or elemental being**, commonly called *Felsenwesen* and interpreted as *Weltenhumor* (“world humour”), emerges at the upper edge of the rocky structure.

The second Lucifer and second Ahriman were not part of the earliest conception. They appear in a sketch-like fourth design, probably May–June 1915. The humorous elemental being was also a developmental addition, reportedly prompted when a woman observing the design remarked on its compositional imbalance. This explanation is preserved in the documented tradition around the work; the exact identity of the observer and wording of the exchange remain uncertain. [Development of the models and later additions](https://wortgartenwerk.de/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Stockmar-Im-Spannungsfeld-von-Weltenkraeften.pdf)

### Surface, colour, inscriptions, and tool work

The sculpture is unpainted wood in its present state. No inscription, dedicatory text, signature, maker’s mark, or accession number has been publicly identified on the monumental object.

Its visual articulation depends on carved surfaces, changing planes, asymmetry, grain, shadow, and gesture. Steiner described the aim as making the whole body physiognomic—“the entire figure” was to carry soul-expression, rather than expression being concentrated in a symmetrical face. His 7 September 1921 illustrated lecture explicitly identifies the central figure, the repeated Lucifer and Ahriman beings, and the elemental being growing from the rock. [Steiner’s illustrated 1921 presentation](https://rsarchive.org/Lectures/GA289/English/SOL2022/19210907k01.html)

According to the Goetheanum’s 2020 account, assistants rough-carved the laminated blocks to within approximately two centimetres of the intended surface. Maryon and Steiner then undertook the detailed carving. Steiner’s own finishing work can be securely associated with the central figure and the lower Ahriman; much of the remaining execution was carried out by Maryon and assistants under the evolving design. No carving was added after Steiner’s death. [Goetheanum account of execution](https://goetheanum.ch/en/news/menschheitsreprasentant)

### Incompleteness and loss

The object is authentically unfinished, not a fragment of a completed sculpture. Maryon died in 1924; Steiner, already seriously ill, continued trying to advance the work but died on 30 March 1925. The Goetheanum states that no later artist completed the carving.

The surviving full-size modelling-stage version is also incomplete as evidence: its central figure has not survived, although the two Ahrimans, two Lucifers, rock forms, and elemental being preserve features shaped by Steiner. Comparisons between that full-scale model and the wooden group are therefore highly valuable but cannot provide a complete one-to-one reconstruction.

The group has no recorded textual inscription and no lost detachable attribute comparable to a sword, crown, or reliquary fitting. Its greatest “loss” is contextual: the architectural, painted, and glass environment for which it was created burned before the sculpture could be installed.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Conceptual origin, late 1914–early 1915

The decisive conception appears to have emerged around the turn of 1914–15, rather than at the initial planning of the First Goetheanum. Alfred Meebold later recalled that by autumn 1914 the artists had received Steiner’s sketches for the smaller cupola paintings, but the centre had deliberately remained blank and no one yet knew what would occupy it.

A lecture of 13 December 1914 discussed how bodily gesture could express cosmic and spiritual relationships, including the idea that “Lucifer is redeemed through Christ.” This is conceptual preparation, not yet secure proof of the finished sculptural composition.

Danish painter Arild Rosenkrantz arrived in Dornach around Christmas 1914. His recollections, published in August 1925, describe Steiner producing a pastel design of the Representative between Lucifer and Ahriman and, effectively at the same creative moment, conceiving the motif in sculpture and engraved glass. Research by Dino Wendtland identifies three Steiner–Rosenkrantz meetings in the first days of January 1915. [Reconstruction of the January 1915 conception](https://wortgartenwerk.de/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Stockmar-Im-Spannungsfeld-von-Weltenkraeften.pdf)

Thus the safest distinction is:

- **Preparatory ideas:** attested by December 1914.
- **Recognizable middle-motif conception:** first days of January 1915.
- **Plastic studies:** beginning early 1915.
- **Monumental wooden manufacture:** beginning at the end of May 1917.

The occasional institutional date “from August 1914” refers broadly to the artistic collaboration or preliminary phase. The specialist reconstruction placing the recognizable group’s development in early 1915 is more precise.

### Studies and modelling sequence

Steiner and Maryon developed the composition through individual studies and a succession of group models. The record includes:

- an initial, only lightly indicated study of the Christ head, early 1915;
- models for individual figures around Easter 1915;
- a roughly two-metre group model made from approximately May to October 1915 and signed “Rudolf Steiner – Edith Maryon”;
- several further group studies in changing scales and states;
- a full-size 1:1 modelling-stage version, apparently begun around August 1916;
- the laminated elm execution begun in late May 1917.

Published counts differ between **seven preliminary models** in visitor information and **nine total plastic group studies** in the 2020 monograph. This is most likely a difference in cataloguing—surviving exhibition models versus every identifiable developmental state—not evidence that either set is wholly erroneous. The Goetheanum’s current visitor page says seven preliminary models from the nine-year process are exhibited with the wooden group. [Current permanent-exhibition description](https://goetheanum.ch/de/campus/dauerausstellungen)

The full-size model was made from a modelling compound described in anthroposophical technical literature as involving plasticine, chalk or limestone flour, and beeswax. Exact formulations and proportions have not been published in the web-accessible sources examined here.

### Workshop and makers

The sculpture was made in the **Hochatelier**, the purpose-built high studio adjoining the *Schreinerei* or carpentry workshop on the Goetheanum campus. Its ceiling height and scaffolding accommodated the full-scale model and monumental wood blocks.

The principal makers were:

- **Rudolf Steiner:** originator and reviser of the artistic concept; modeller; carver of documented portions; director of the larger Goetheanum programme.
- **Edith Maryon:** academically trained English sculptor; modeller, planner, technical organizer, and major carver; Steiner’s close collaborator.
- **Workshop assistants:** prepared modelling material, erected support structures and scaffolding, laminated and moved blocks, and undertook rough carving. Institutional summaries name Oswald Dubach and Karl Kemper among significant participants, but a complete task-by-task payroll or workshop register has not been published online. [Anthroposophy Switzerland’s sculpture survey](https://anthroposophie.ch/en/arts-architecture/topics/articles/visual-arts/steiners-artistic-work/sculptures.html)

Maryon had written to Steiner from England in 1912 and offered her artistic abilities for the Goetheanum project in January 1914. She arrived in Dornach in 1914 and became his closest trained sculptural collaborator. The published Steiner–Maryon correspondence is consequently primary evidence for their working relationship, although many enclosures mentioned in the letters are lost or unidentified. [Published correspondence, GA 263/1](https://steiner.wiki/GA_263/1)

### Commission, purpose, and cost

No conventional arm’s-length commission contract is known. The work arose inside the First Goetheanum building enterprise and was intended as the artistic and spiritual focus of the smaller eastern cupola/stage zone. Financing, timber purchase, wages, and workshop costs were presumably borne through the Goetheanum building organization, but no reliable object-specific total cost has been found.

The group belonged to a three-medium “middle motif”:

- monumental sculpture below;
- cupola painting above the intended site;
- monochrome engraved-glass treatment in the First Goetheanum’s pink north window.

These were related translations of a motif, not replicas. The painting and original glass were destroyed in the 1922–23 fire; photographs and designs survive, and adapted windows were later produced for the Second Goetheanum. [Monograph description and publication record](https://goetheanum-verlag.ch/produkt/im-spannungsfeld-von-weltenkraeften/)

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 1915–1917: models in the Dornach workshop complex

The first plastic studies were made under Steiner and Maryon in Dornach. The high studio was erected for the full-scale modelling and later wooden work. Custody lay within the Goetheanum building enterprise rather than in either artist’s private possession.

### Late May 1917–1922: monumental group in the Hochatelier/Schreinerei

The laminated elm blocks were made, transported into the studio, rough-cut by assistants, and worked in detail by Maryon and Steiner. Historical photographs document these physical operations.

The intended destination remained the eastern rear of the stage under the First Goetheanum’s smaller cupola, but the work was never installed there. An important later report says Assja Turgenieff urged that it be placed in the building before the fire and Maryon resisted premature installation. That episode is **later testimony**, not a surviving formal order. Maryon’s caution may have saved the object, but the causal formulation should not obscure the basic fact: it survived because it was still unfinished and outside the main building.

### 31 December 1922–1 January 1923: survival of the Goetheanum fire

The First Goetheanum burned to its concrete foundations. The sculpture survived in the separate workshop complex.

Institutions commonly state that the building was destroyed by arson. Contemporary observations did indeed lead Steiner, fire officials, and investigators to suspect a deliberately set fire: fuses remained intact, a fire developed within wall construction, and a fire expert believed it had been started at a strategically selected point. Nevertheless, no perpetrator was identified and the complete causal chain has never been judicially established.

The rediscovered official investigation files—found in 1974, transferred to the Solothurn State Archives in 2014, and published in 2022—do not identify a culprit. Accordingly:

- **Documented:** the building burned and was destroyed.
- **Strong contemporary finding/allegation:** deliberate ignition was considered probable.
- **Unresolved:** perpetrator, motive, and exact mechanism.
- **False or unsupported popular claim:** the dead watchmaker Jakob Ott was proved to be the arsonist. No such proof exists. [Review of the published investigation papers](https://dasgoetheanum.com/die-dokumente-zum-brand-des-goetheanum/)

This dispute does not threaten the sculpture’s identity. Its survival away from the principal fire is documented under either causal theory.

### 1923–1925: continued work after loss of the intended building

Destruction of the architectural setting did not end the work. Maryon continued to be associated with it until illness and her death in May 1924. Steiner sought to carry the sculpture forward during his own final illness, beginning in September 1924, and reportedly worked on it until close to his death.

For the projected Second Goetheanum, Steiner initially retained the idea of placing the group at the eastern rear of the stage. Architects made relevant structural provisions. Ernst Aisenpreis’s conversation note of 13 April 1924 records an intention to reconcile modern stage technology with partial artistic treatment incorporating the group.

By late February or early March 1925, however, discussion had opened around a separate setting. The relevant statement is indirectly transmitted, and its motivation is unresolved. Possibilities include:

- protecting the work in a fire-resistant location;
- allowing work to proceed independently of an expensive, delayed stage interior;
- acknowledging that Steiner could no longer create the total interior needed to contextualize it properly.

Steiner died on 30 March 1925. No definitive final installation directive survives.

### 1925–1928: transfer into the Second Goetheanum

Construction of the reinforced-concrete Second Goetheanum began in 1925 and continued after Steiner’s death. A special southeastern “group room” was incorporated into the building.

A detailed later reconstruction places the sculpture in this room in **1927**, before the whole Second Goetheanum opened in 1928. That date is more precise than current summaries saying it has been in its present room “since 1935.” The likely reconciliation is:

- installed in the designated group room in 1927;
- the room architecturally reshaped into substantially its present form in 1935.

There is no documented sale, inheritance, seizure, or private transfer. Custody remained with the Goetheanum’s institutional building body and its successor, the General Anthroposophical Society.

### 1935: remodelling of the room

In 1935 the display room was plastically redesigned after a scheme associated with Mieta Pyle-Waller, with Albert von Baravalle as architect. A small urn room was incorporated nearby for Steiner’s ashes and those of associates. That adjacency intensified the space’s commemorative atmosphere but did not transform the sculpture into a reliquary.

Visitors originally entered at approximately the sculpture’s own platform level rather than through the exact circulation arrangement experienced today. [Source-based reconstruction of the group room](https://wortgartenwerk.de/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Stockmar-Im-Spannungsfeld-von-Weltenkraeften.pdf)

### 1939–1945

The Second World War did not produce a published evacuation, seizure, or concealment episode for the sculpture. The Goetheanum itself faced financial closure in 1939, but no evidence located here indicates that the group left the building. Its large laminated construction and purpose-built room would have made ordinary evacuation exceptionally difficult.

This is a documentary gap: uninterrupted presence is very probable, but a complete wartime custody or condition log has not been published online.

### 1960s: first comprehensive restoration

A first extensive restoration of the monumental group and its preparatory studies took place in the 1960s. Accessible institutional summaries do not identify the exact year, conservators, treatments, or materials applied. Later references to excessive wax suggest that past surface campaigns accumulated coatings, but it cannot be assumed without the treatment report that all such wax came from the 1960s intervention.

### 1969 and 1981: foundational cataloguing publications

Åke Fant, Arne Klingborg, and A. John Wilkes published *Die Holzplastik Rudolf Steiners in Dornach* in 1969; a second edition followed in 1981. This work became the foundational object study, combining photographs, model history, and interpretation. Later cataloguing, particularly *Das plastische Werk* (2011) and the 2020 monograph, corrected parts of its developmental dating.

### 2002: extensive photographic documentation

In cooperation with the Goetheanum building administration, photographer Michael Schnur made a new photographic campaign using a roughly ten-metre rolling scaffold and specially positioned lighting and cameras. The resulting views documented details and angles ordinarily inaccessible to visitors. [2002 photographic project](https://www.sixtina-verlag.de/d-menschheitsrepraesentant.htm)

### Approximately 2006–2020: research, conservation, and room renewal

Mirela Faldey and David Hornemann von Laer began plans for a comprehensive monograph around 2006. The project expanded into technical study, conservation/restoration of the sculpture, renovation of the room, new photography, and archival reconstruction.

The work included removal of an excessive wax layer and cleaning. The exact treatment dates, solvents, quantities, consolidants, glue repairs, environmental measurements, and whether old restoration material was sampled have not been published in the open sources located for this dossier. The conservation appears to have been complete before the monograph’s publication in 2020. [Swiss Anthroposophical Society annual report](https://www.anthroposophie.ch/files/anthroposophie.ch/content/docs/mitteilungen/mitteilungen_2021_1.pdf)

### 2011: legal heritage protection

The complete composition—the Representative, Lucifers, Ahrimans, rock structure, and elemental being—was placed under protection by the Canton of Solothurn in 2011. Public summaries do not give an ordinary museum accession number. Its legally significant identifier is therefore its protected-monument status and fixed location within the Goetheanum rather than a published catalogue accession.

### June 2024: condition inspection

From 3 to 16 June 2024 restorers **Mirela Faldey and Astrid Kübli** enclosed the group in scaffolding for a close condition survey. They investigated the behaviour of the wood and glued joints after the earlier removal of excess wax and cleaning. The room remained mostly accessible, though the scaffold obstructed views.

The public notice describes examination, not a completed new restoration campaign. No final 2024 technical report or scientific dataset was located. [Goetheanum condition-survey notice](https://goetheanum.ch/de/nachrichten/menschheitsrepraesentant-unter-der-lupe)

### Custody chain in compact form

1. **1915–1923:** Goetheanum building enterprise, Hochatelier/Schreinerei, Dornach.
2. **1923–1927:** same workshop-campus custody after the First Goetheanum’s destruction.
3. **1927–present:** group room/exhibition room, Second Goetheanum, Dornach.
4. **Institutional control:** present-day Goetheanum/General Anthroposophical Society.
5. **Legal overlay since 2011:** protected under the Canton of Solothurn’s heritage regime.

No sale, theft, wartime displacement, private inheritance, restitution demand, or rival ownership claim has been found.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is the displayed sculpture the original?

Yes, to the standard appropriate for a documented twentieth-century artwork. The chain from manufacture to present display is geographically short and photographically documented. No substitution story, competing monumental claimant, or credible forgery allegation exists.

The group is not:

- a reconstruction made after the First Goetheanum fire;
- a copy of a destroyed original;
- the full-size plasticine model;
- a later completion of Steiner’s design.

It is the unfinished laminated elm group begun in 1917.

### The principal identity complication: one motif, several objects

The label “Representative of Humanity” can refer to:

- the surviving monumental elm sculpture;
- individual sculptural studies;
- small group models;
- the 1:1 modelling-stage version;
- the destroyed cupola painting;
- the destroyed First Goetheanum glass design;
- later adapted glass executions;
- reproductions and photographs.

These are materially distinct. The full-size model is especially liable to confusion because it stood in the high studio and supplied forms for the wooden group. It is not a second completed wooden original.

### Attribution dispute: Steiner or Steiner–Maryon?

Older institutional and popular accounts regularly name the work as “Rudolf Steiner’s sculpture,” mentioning Maryon as assistant or collaborator. Recent research increasingly treats it as a genuine co-creation.

Several evidentiary points support Maryon’s major authorship:

- she was the professionally trained sculptor; Steiner was largely self-taught in the medium;
- the approximately two-metre model bears both names;
- she produced technical plans and oversaw prolonged modelling and execution;
- the years of sustained workshop labour were impossible for Steiner alone, given his extensive lecturing and travel;
- the Goetheanum now consistently describes the work as created by Steiner and Maryon.

Rembert Biemond has estimated that Maryon did “ninety percent” of the labour, while stressing that the percentage is not a literal accounting. That is a modern biographical estimate, not a surviving timesheet. It corrects an older erasure but cannot establish exact surface-by-surface authorship. [Interview on Maryon’s contribution](https://dasgoetheanum.com/en/edith-maryon/)

The most defensible attribution is:

> Conceived and repeatedly revised by Rudolf Steiner; designed, modelled, technically developed, and carved in close collaboration with Edith Maryon; rough-carved and physically assembled with workshop assistants.

### Unfinishedness versus authenticity

Because neither principal artist completed the work, “unfinished” is part of its authentic historical state. Completing unworked surfaces now would falsify that state. Goetheanum policy since 1925 has been not to add carving.

### Discrepancy in the central hand

Heinz Müller’s recollection of a July 1922 explanation says the index and middle fingers of the central figure stood vertically upward, with the thumb and fingers indicating three spatial directions and symbolizing thinking, feeling, and willing. The existing hand does not match that description: the fingers extend forward at approximately a right angle.

Possible explanations include faulty memory, description of a model or intended state, misunderstanding, or an unexecuted alteration. No evidence establishes that the present fingers are later replacements. The discrepancy remains unresolved. [Discussion of the hand problem](https://wortgartenwerk.de/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Stockmar-Im-Spannungsfeld-von-Weltenkraeften.pdf)

### Scientific examination

No published dendrochronological dating, radiocarbon analysis, adhesive chemistry, X-radiography, CT scanning, species-genetics study, or systematic tool-mark attribution was located. Such tests are unnecessary to establish the broad date and identity, which are amply documented, but they could clarify:

- number and orientation of laminated elements;
- timber seasoning and source;
- glue composition and failure;
- hidden fasteners;
- sequence of block assembly;
- later fills, coatings, and repairs;
- differentiation of carving campaigns.

The 2024 survey specifically investigated wood and glue behaviour, but publicly accessible results remain limited to the announcement.

### Who authenticates?

Authority has moved through overlapping forms:

- **1915–1925:** the makers and Goetheanum workshop community.
- **After 1925:** custodial testimony, photographs, and Anthroposophical Society records.
- **From the later twentieth century:** cataloguers, art historians, and conservators.
- **Since 2011:** the Canton of Solothurn additionally recognizes and regulates the ensemble as protected heritage.

There is no recorded market authentication because the object has not circulated commercially.

### Makers and technical participants

- **Rudolf Steiner (1861–1925)** — principal conceiver, modeller, reviser, and documented carver of the central figure and lower Ahriman; dated involvement, 1915–1925.
- **Edith Maryon (1872–1924)** — co-designer, technical planner, modeller, workshop organizer, and principal long-term carver; dated involvement, 1915–1924.
- **Oswald Dubach** — named in institutional accounts among artists or craftspeople significant to execution; precise tasks require further archival confirmation.

- **Assja Turgenieff (1890–1966)** — glass engraver and later memoir witness; associated with the related window and reportedly advocated installing the wooden group before the fire.

- **Mieta Pyle-Waller** and **Albert von Baravalle** — associated with the 1935 shaping of the present group room.
- **Mirela Faldey** — sculptor, conservator, researcher, co-editor of the 2020 monograph, and participant in the 2024 condition survey.
- **Astrid Kübli** — conservator participating in the June 2024 condition survey.
- **Dino Wendtland** — Goetheanum art-collection researcher who reconstructed the changing installation plans and the related painting and glass histories.
- **Michael Schnur** — photographer responsible for the 2002 scaffold-assisted documentation campaign.

### Institutions and networks

- **Johannesbauverein/Goetheanum building organization** — original manufacturing and custodial framework.
- **Anthroposophical Society and General Anthroposophical Society** — the social and legal institutional continuity within which the sculpture was preserved.
- **Goetheanum Kunstsammlung** — curatorial framework for the sculpture, studies, architectural models, and related visual material.
- **Section for the Visual Arts** — Maryon was named its leader during the 1923 Christmas conference; the work became foundational to its sculptural identity.
- **Canton of Solothurn heritage authorities** — legal protective authority since 2011.
- **Rudolf Steiner Archive** — keeper and publisher of lectures, correspondence, sketches, and documentary evidence relevant to the object.

### Represented spiritual beings

These entries describe Steiner’s iconography, not independently verified entities.

- **Christ/the Representative of Humanity** — central balancing presence. Steiner presented Christ as neither attacking nor coercively judging the adversarial beings.
- **Lucifer** — spiritual tendency toward elevation, expansion, illumination, pride, fantasy, or severance from earthly embodiment.
- **Ahriman** — spiritual tendency toward contraction, hardening, calculation, material fixation, and domination.
- **Elemental rock being/World Humour** — a being emerging from rock, introducing an element of cosmic or corrective humour.
- **The human being** — not merely a spectator outside the group but, in anthroposophical interpretation, the field in which luciferic and ahrimanic tendencies must be held in living balance.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Intended function

The sculpture was designed as the visual culmination of the First Goetheanum’s architecture. Steiner said in 1920 that the whole building could be regarded as an unfolding of the central group and the group as a synthesis of the building.

Its intended stage position connected it with:

- public lectures;
- productions of Steiner’s Mystery Dramas;
- eurythmy;
- the cupola’s painted programme;
- coloured engraved-glass windows;
- the building’s sculptural metamorphoses.

It was therefore neither a detachable gallery sculpture nor, strictly, a church altar image. It belonged to a *Gesamtkunstwerk* in which architecture, speech, theatre, movement, light, painting, and carving interacted.

### Actual ceremonial and contemplative use

Because it was never installed in the First Goetheanum, no documented ceremony occurred before it in its intended setting.

In the Second Goetheanum it has functioned as:

- a permanent exhibition object;
- a destination on Goetheanum tours;
- a focus for artistic study and drawing;
- a setting for lectures, conferences, and contemplative observation;
- a motif for eurythmic exploration.

A 2013 discussion records eurythmist Christian Merz describing the experience of performing eurythmy before the sculpture. This documents an experimental modern use, not a continuously transmitted ritual prescribed by Steiner.

The adjacent urn room created in 1935 gave the space a memorial dimension until the urns were reburied in the Goetheanum memorial grove in 1992. The sculpture itself did not contain the remains.

### Symbolic function: dynamic balance

The conventional anthroposophical interpretation is not a contest in which Christ violently defeats two devils. The central figure maintains an active, mobile balance. Lucifer and Ahriman bring themselves into collapse through their own unilateral tendencies when confronted by the free human middle.

Steiner compared this balance to a scale beam that continually moves rather than remaining mechanically fixed. Individuality lives in the infinitely variable achievement of equilibrium. In 1919 he called the relationship a trinity of the luciferic, Christ-conforming, and ahrimanic; in 1920 he called it “the trinity of future human culture.” [Source chronology and quotations](https://wortgartenwerk.de/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Stockmar-Im-Spannungsfeld-von-Weltenkraeften.pdf)

### Relation to Michelangelo’s *Last Judgment*

Anthroposophical readings frequently contrast the work with Michelangelo’s judging Christ. Steiner’s central being does not condemn through a commanding gesture; the adversarial beings reveal or judge themselves in relation to the human middle. This is an interpretive and artistic intention, not a claim that the sculpture historically replaced or directly copied Michelangelo.

### Access rules and taboos

No esoteric initiation rule barring non-members from seeing the sculpture is currently in force. It is publicly accessible during the Goetheanum’s exhibition hours and guided visits. Physical touching and climbing are naturally restricted by conservation and safety practice, not by a published religious taboo.

The work’s location, scale, fragility, and protected status prevent ordinary movement or loan. No evidence was found that the original monumental group has travelled outside Dornach.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

This is not an ancient legendary object, but a real twentieth-century work around which institutionally meaningful narratives have formed.

### “The one thing that survived the fire”

**Common retelling:** the sculpture was the sole survivor of the First Goetheanum.

**Factual core:** it survived while the principal wooden building, cupola painting, and original glass setting were destroyed.

**Correction:** it was not physically inside the First Goetheanum and was not literally the only surviving object or structure from the campus. It stood in a separate workshop, and other fragments, models, photographs, concrete foundations, and auxiliary buildings survived.

**Effect:** the narrative transformed an unfinished work into a tangible survivor and memorial embodiment of the lost building.

### Maryon’s refusal saved it

**Later tradition:** Assja Turgenieff requested installation; Maryon refused to hurry; this decision saved the work.

**Assessment:** plausible and consistent with its unfinished condition, but preserved mainly through later recollection. The sculpture survived because it remained in the workshop; whether Maryon alone determined that result cannot be proved.

**Effect:** the story has contributed to modern reassessment of Maryon as an autonomous artist whose judgment materially preserved the work.

### Maryon saved Steiner’s life while carving

Maryon is reported to have prevented Steiner from suffering a serious or fatal fall during workshop work around 1916. The episode appears in biographical traditions and the correspondence edition’s contextual material. Its broad substance may be credible, but the detailed mechanics and exact date are not securely documented in the publicly accessible evidence.

This is an event in the workshop history, not a miraculous property of the object.

### The sculpture as protection or balancing force

Anthroposophical viewers often describe the group as actively mediating or making perceptible spiritual forces. Steiner himself said the artistic work is completed in the viewer’s inner activity: what arises in the soul while following the lines and surfaces constitutes the artwork in a fuller sense.

These are theological-aesthetic claims internal to anthroposophy. No physical or medical protective power has been scientifically tested.

### The fire as occult or anti-anthroposophical attack

The First Goetheanum’s destruction generated narratives of targeted hostility. Deliberate arson by an opponent was suspected contemporaneously, and anti-anthroposophical threats form relevant context. Yet neither a named anti-anthroposophical conspiracy nor Jakob Ott’s guilt was established. Later accounts that present a particular perpetrator as known exceed the evidence.

The legend directly shaped the sculpture’s reception by casting its survival as providential or historically fateful.

### The fixed final intention

A widespread institutional impression is that the group simply belongs in its present room. The archival evidence is more complicated:

- First Goetheanum: stage/smaller-cupola placement was clear.
- Early Second Goetheanum planning: the east end of the stage remained likely.
- Final months of Steiner’s life: a separate room was considered.
- After his death: successors chose and developed the group room.

Claims that the current location is unquestionably Steiner’s final, fully specified intention are stronger than the surviving evidence warrants.

## 8. Current Status and Location

The original monumental sculpture is in the **Sculpture Group exhibition room**, South Wing of the **Second Goetheanum**, Rüttiweg 45, 4143 Dornach, Canton of Solothurn, Switzerland. Visitor descriptions variously call this the fourth or fifth floor, a difference likely produced by national floor-numbering conventions and changing visitor documentation. It is reached through the southern stair circulation.

The room also presents:

- preparatory models and studies;
- material connected with the First and Second Goetheanums;
- a walk-in, materially detailed 1:20 model of the First Goetheanum, built by Rudolf Feuerstack beginning in 1994.

The sculpture remains:

- unfinished;
- stationary;
- publicly viewable at designated times;
- controlled by the Goetheanum/General Anthroposophical Society;
- protected as cantonal heritage since 2011;
- subject to professional conservation monitoring.

No published museum accession number was found. No ownership, restitution, export, or repatriation dispute is current. There is an intermittent internal architectural debate about whether it should someday be integrated into a redesigned stage setting, but no relocation has occurred. Concerns include fidelity to Steiner’s first intention, the loss of the First Goetheanum context, stage technology, conservation risk, and the present room’s comparatively easy public access.

The latest located physical intervention was the June 2024 scaffold examination of the wood and glued joints. The Goetheanum has not publicly reported catastrophic structural failure or loss. [Official current exhibition information](https://goetheanum.ch/de/campus/dauerausstellungen)

## 9. Modern Reception

### Photography and reproduction

The sculpture’s modern public image depends heavily on photographs because it is too large and fragile to travel. Important reproductive moments include:

- early workshop photographs showing models, blocks, scaffold, and carving;
- the 1969 and 1981 photographic monographs;
- Michael Schnur’s 2002 high-scaffold campaign;
- new close-range conservation photography associated with the 2020 monograph;
- postcards, calendars, books, brochures, and online imagery.

These images are documents and commodities, not substitutes with independent claims to originality.

### Models and replicas

Multiple authentic preparatory models survive. Copies or casts of models are used for study and exhibition, including material retained in the Hochatelier. The Goetheanum’s use of casts must be distinguished from its presentation of the original monumental group.

No full-scale second elm replica with a competing identity has been identified.

### Exhibitions

Steiner’s broader visual work has entered mainstream museum histories through exhibitions such as *Rudolf Steiner: Alchemy of the Everyday*, presented in venues including the Kunsthal Rotterdam in 2014–15. Such exhibitions generally rely on drawings, furniture, architectural models, photographs, and portable studies rather than moving the monumental sculpture. [Kunsthal exhibition record](https://www.kunsthal.nl/en/plan-your-visit/exhibitions/rudolf-steiner/)

The Centre Pompidou’s 2008 exhibition *Traces du sacré* catalogued Steiner’s *Menschheitsrepräsentant* within a modern-art genealogy of sacred and esoteric imagery, demonstrating reception beyond anthroposophical institutions. [Centre Pompidou exhibition catalogue](https://cataloguedesexpositions.centrepompidou.fr/Traces_du_sacr%C3%A9)

### Maryon’s recovery as co-author

The most consequential recent reception shift is the restoration of Edith Maryon’s name. Earlier labels often subordinated her to Steiner; current Goetheanum publications normally identify both artists. Biographical work, conservation study, surviving plans, and the jointly signed model have made a one-author account increasingly untenable.

### Influence and analogy

Joseph Beuys’s “social sculpture” is deeply indebted to Steiner’s enlarged understanding of artistic activity, although a direct line from this particular wooden group to any single Beuys work should not be asserted without specific evidence. The National Gallery of Victoria’s exhibition *Imagination, Inspiration, Intuition: Joseph Beuys & Rudolf Steiner* documented the wider intellectual and artistic relationship. [NGV exhibition account](https://www.ngv.vic.gov.au/exhibition/imagination-inspiration-intuition/)

Contemporary anthroposophical artists continue to study the group through drawing, sculpture, painting, and eurythmy. Caroline Chanter and J. Georg Müller’s recent drawing-based publication explicitly treats drawing as a means of experiencing the group’s changing forms. [Publisher’s description](https://steinerbooks.org/products/9781621484103-the-representative-of-humanity)

### Political and religious reception

Unlike crowns, relics, or national regalia, the sculpture has not served as a state emblem, appeared on national currency, or become the object of a restitution movement. Its symbolic mobilization is primarily internal to anthroposophy: it represents the human task of maintaining freedom between spiritualized inflation and materialized hardening.

Outside anthroposophy, it is variously classified as:

- expressionist sculpture;
- esoteric Christian art;
- part of an architectural total artwork;
- visual theology;
- modern occult art;
- the product of an alternative religious movement.

The label “cult object” can illuminate its reverential reception but becomes misleading if it implies documented worship, sacrifice, relic veneration, or sacramental use.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Primary evidence

The strongest evidence consists of:

- dated photographs of models and work on the elm blocks;
- Steiner’s lectures and illustrated presentations;
- Steiner–Maryon correspondence;
- building plans and architectural conversation notes;
- Goetheanum building-association reports;
- eyewitness accounts by artists and workshop participants;
- the surviving models and tool-worked monumental object.

A major weakness is that Steiner’s lectures survive mostly through stenographic transcripts rather than author-revised art treatises. Later recollections must therefore be separated from contemporaneous statements.

### Major publications

1. **Åke Fant, Arne Klingborg, and A. John Wilkes, *Die Holzplastik Rudolf Steiners in Dornach* (1969; revised 1981).**  
   Foundational illustrated study. Its chronology has been partly corrected by later research.

2. **Rex Raab, *Edith Maryon: Bildhauerin und Mitarbeiterin Rudolf Steiners* (1993).**  
   Important biographical restoration of Maryon’s role.

3. **Rudolf Steiner, *Das plastische Werk* (GA K 12, 2011), edited by Walter Kugler and Roland Halfen with collaboration from Mirela Faldey.**  
   Re-catalogued the sculptural corpus and corrected dates assigned in earlier literature.

4. **Judith von Halle and A. John Wilkes, *Die Holzplastik des Goetheanum—Der Menschheitsrepräsentant zwischen Luzifer und Ahriman* (2008; English edition subsequently issued as *The Representative of Humanity*).**  
   An illustrated anthroposophical interpretation and introduction, useful but confessional rather than neutral catalogue scholarship. [Bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Representative_of_Humanity.html?id=myZBmu2o4z8C)

5. **Mirela Faldey and David Hornemann von Laer, eds., *Im Spannungsfeld von Weltenkräften* (2020).**  
   The fullest object monograph: 536 pages, extensive photographs, sketches, models, correspondence, diaries, plans, witness reports, building records, and specialist essays. It treats sculpture, cupola painting, and glass engraving together. [Publisher’s catalogue](https://goetheanum-verlag.ch/produkt/im-spannungsfeld-von-weltenkraeften/)

### Criticism of the 2020 monograph

Stephan Stockmar praised its documentation but identified significant limitations:

- insufficient synthesis among sculpture, painting, and glass;
- failure to engage fully with the 2011 catalogue’s dating arguments;
- unresolved discrepancies such as the central hand;
- minimal engagement with broader modern-art contexts;
- essays that often presuppose the work’s singular anthroposophical importance;
- notes reflecting an earlier, approximately 2013 state of research despite publication in 2020;
- unexplained conflict and delay in the book’s production.

These criticisms matter because the monograph is institutionally close to the object and extraordinarily rich, but not the final word. [Full review](https://wortgartenwerk.de/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Stockmar-Im-Spannungsfeld-von-Weltenkraeften.pdf)

### Central scholarly disputes

#### Date of origin

- Broad institutional dating sometimes begins in August or autumn 1914.
- Specialist reconstruction places recognizable sculptural work in early 1915 and monumental wood carving in May 1917.

The latter distinguishes concept, models, and final material execution more carefully.

#### Number and dating of models

Counts of seven, nine, or more depend upon whether individual studies, lost states, full group studies, and casts are included. Later cataloguing revised some dates given in 1969/1981.

#### Authorship

Older Steiner-centred historiography treated Maryon as an executant. Current work recognizes her as co-author and possibly the person responsible for most sustained modelling and carving labour. Exact quantitative division is unknowable.

#### Final placement

The evidence supports both a long-standing stage intention and a late consideration of a separate room. It does not permit certainty about what Steiner would finally have chosen had he lived.

#### “Christ” or universal human being

Steiner’s own terminology supports both descriptions. The ambiguity is not necessarily a contradiction: in his Christology, Christ could be presented as the universally representative human being. A catalogue should preserve the terminology rather than force a single secular or doctrinal identity.

#### Religious image or autonomous artwork

The object represents explicitly spiritual beings and stands at the heart of anthroposophical self-understanding. Yet Steiner insisted on an artistic effect that should not depend solely on prior knowledge of Christian history. The object consequently operates simultaneously as modern sculpture, architectural synthesis, and visualized esoteric Christology.

### Research still needed

The following would materially improve the dossier:

- publication of complete conservation reports from the 1960s and recent campaigns;
- a block-by-block structural survey;
- adhesive and coating analysis;
- dendrochronology or archival timber-supplier research;
- a complete prosopography of workshop assistants and their tasks;
- digitization of building-association accounts and payment books;
- precise legal citation for the 2011 protection order;
- reconciliation of floor, room, height, and weight measurements;
- a public report from the June 2024 condition examination;
- comparative stylistic and tool-mark study distinguishing Maryon, Steiner, and assistants;
- identification of the observer connected with the “world humour” addition;
- fuller study of the work in relation to European expressionist, symbolist, and esoteric sculpture.

### Assessment

The object’s existence, broad manufacture history, survival, and physical continuity are secure. Its documentary vulnerabilities are not the usual provenance gaps of a traded artifact; they arise from collaborative authorship, incomplete workshop records, evolving designs, loss of the intended architectural setting, and an institutional historiography that long privileged Steiner over Maryon. The sculpture now displayed is the original unfinished elm group. What cannot yet be reconstructed completely is whose hand cut each surface, the exact material history of its laminated blocks and coatings, and the final installation Steiner would have authorized.
