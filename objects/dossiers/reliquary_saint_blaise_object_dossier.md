# The Dubrovnik head reliquary of St. Blaise: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary key

This dossier distinguishes three things frequently collapsed under the name “the head of St. Blaise”:

1. the human cranial material accepted and venerated in Dubrovnik as a relic of St. Blaise;
2. the medieval container recorded by 1335 and destroyed or rendered unusable in the earthquake of 1667;
3. the extant composite reliquary made by Francesco Ferro in 1694, incorporating medieval enamels salvaged from several objects.

The surviving reliquary is unquestionably a real seventeenth-century artifact. Continuity of the enclosed bone with the relic documented in fourteenth-century Dubrovnik is institutionally maintained and historically plausible, but published scientific authentication of the bone has not been located. The claim that the relic reached Dubrovnik in 1026 rests on retrospective medieval or early-modern chronicle traditions whose precise textual date is disputed.

Labels used below:

- **Documented:** supported by an inventory, inscription, archival act, eyewitness, or securely identified object.
- **Scholarly finding:** conclusion from technical, stylistic, palaeographic, or historical analysis.
- **Scholarly inference:** plausible reconstruction not directly documented.
- **Institutional or pious tradition:** claim maintained by Dubrovnik’s church or festival tradition.
- **Legend:** narrative whose supernatural or early historical content cannot independently be verified.
- **Unresolved:** evidence does not presently permit a firm conclusion.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity and names

The object is a non-anthropomorphic head reliquary—more exactly a decorated skull-cap or domed capsule—preserved in the Treasury of the Cathedral of the Assumption of the Virgin Mary in Dubrovnik, Croatia. It contains cranial material venerated as the head, or part of the head, of St. Blaise, bishop and martyr of Sebaste.

Principal names include:

- English: **Head Reliquary of St. Blaise**, **Reliquary of the Head of Saint Blaise**.
- Croatian: **Relikvijar glave svetoga Vlaha**, **moćnik glave sv. Vlaha**, **moći glave svetoga Vlaha**.
- Latin documentary form: **Capud Beati Blasij epi. in bacino argenti**—“the head of Blessed Blaise, bishop, in a silver *bacinum*.”
- Italian: **reliquiario della testa di San Biagio** or, in Ragusan usage, **reliquiario del capo di San Biagio**.
- The saint’s Dubrovnik name, **Vlaho**, derives through the local Romance/Slavic history of Latin *Blasius*; standard Croatian elsewhere commonly uses **Blaž**. Dubrovnik calls him **Parac** (“patron/protector”) and traditionally the city’s first **Gospar** (“gentleman” or civic lord).

The Croatian word **moćnik** can mean an individual reliquary and, in Dubrovnik usage, the treasury or reliquary collection itself. Context is therefore essential.

### The surviving object

The extant metalwork is the signed work of the Venetian goldsmith Francesco Ferro, completed in 1694. His signature, worked in gold wire along the lower edge, reads:

> **Fran.co Ferro Venet.o F. A. 1694**

The abbreviated formula identifies Francesco Ferro as Venetian and records his making of the object in 1694. Ferro is documented in Dubrovnik from 1678; he also repaired the leg reliquary of St. Blaise in 1684 and the arm reliquary in 1712. [Ana Munk, “Deconstructing the Myth of Byzantine Crown”](https://hrcak.srce.hr/en/167081); [Dubrovnik Cathedral, “The Reliquary”](https://katedraladubrovnik.hr/the-reliquary/).

Its body is a low, rounded metal cap rather than a naturalistic head. The published descriptions identify silver, gilding or gold elements, filigree, cloisonné and other enamel work, precious stones, and a structural core or capsule. Early-twentieth-century observer F. Hamilton Jackson described gold-wire stalks and enamel-filled leaves and flowers attached around the skull capsule, with each plaque surrounded by twisted gold wire and pinned down. [F. Hamilton Jackson, *The Shores of the Adriatic*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/30548/pg30548-images.html).

No reliable published overall dimensions or weight were found in the accessible catalogue and institutional records consulted. A frequently returned measurement of 16 cm high and 16 cm in diameter belongs to the comparative head reliquary of St. James in Zadar, not to the Dubrovnik object, and should not be repeated as a measurement of Ferro’s reliquary.

### Composite enamel decoration

Twenty enamel plaques are attached to the surviving reliquary. Ana Munk divides them into three materially and stylistically distinct sets. Their heterogeneous origins demonstrate that Ferro’s object is a post-disaster assemblage rather than an intact eleventh-century Byzantine work.

- **Group I:** twelve plaques forming the principal series. Older scholarship classified them as Byzantine or Constantinopolitan. Munk instead attributes them to a Greek-trained or Greek artist working in Dubrovnik or southern Italy, most plausibly Dubrovnik during the period of restored Byzantine rule in Dalmatia, approximately 1164–1180. Her evidence includes non-Constantinopolitan iconography, stylistic departures from court enamel, and Latin/Beneventan rather than Greek inscriptional features. The identification remains an art-historical attribution, not a laboratory dating.

- **Group II:** four badly damaged, uninscribed medallions, traditionally dated to the tenth or eleventh century. Their original object is unknown. Munk argues that they are spolia from another work salvaged after 1667; their place and date of manufacture remain unresolved.

- **Group III:** four rectangular plaques removed from the older right-arm reliquary of St. Blaise. They belong to a Byzantine-influenced southern Italian or Sicilian milieu associated with the Norman royal workshops, approximately 1185–1192 in the dating cited by Munk. Their transplantation is evidence of Ferro’s deliberate reuse of surviving treasury fragments.

The twenty plaques could not form a coherent original iconographic program because several source objects were incomplete. Ferro arranged the salvaged pieces in three rows and integrated them through Venetian filigree and floral enamel. [Munk full-text record and indexed text](https://hrcak.srce.hr/en/file/246375).

Figures represented include Christ, the Virgin, apostles and saints; the series has been connected with imagery of martyrdom and celestial coronation. At least some plaques bear Latin saint-names in Beneventan-influenced script. A plaque of St. Peter and plaques identified with Sts. James, Matthew, and Blaise may have been transferred from the arm reliquary or another damaged object. Precise allocation differs in the literature and should not be treated as settled without a new component-by-component catalogue.

### Shape: “Byzantine crown” or domed reliquary?

The familiar description of the object as shaped like a Byzantine imperial crown remains current in cathedral, museum, and tourism literature. Vinicije Lupis argued that the head reliquary supplied a crown-like prototype for many later non-anthropomorphic head reliquaries in Dubrovnik; twenty-eight of the cathedral treasury’s forty-two head reliquaries are said to follow its rounded form. [Vinicije Lupis, “O moćnicima u obliku glave”](https://hrcak.srce.hr/81282).

Munk rejects the stronger proposition that the lost medieval container replicated or intentionally evoked an imperial *kamelaukion*. The 1335 phrase *in bacino argenti* does not securely mean “in a silver crown.” She proposes a cylindrical container with a domed lid, carried on a dish or basin, comparable to the early-twelfth-century head reliquary of St. James at Zadar. The present crown-like impression is largely the result of Ferro’s 1694 design and later interpretation. [Munk](https://hrcak.srce.hr/en/167081).

Accordingly:

- the object is visually crown-like;
- its use as the local prototype for later rounded head reliquaries is plausible;
- its identity as a copy of a Byzantine imperial crown is disputed and, in Munk’s analysis, unsupported.

### Relic contents and condition

The reliquary contains cranial material accepted by the Diocese of Dubrovnik as St. Blaise’s head relic. Accessible sources do not provide an osteological inventory, photographs of the uncovered bone, a canonical-recognition report, seals, radiocarbon dates, sex or age assessment, isotope results, or DNA data. Whether it contains an entire cranium, a calvarium, or selected cranial fragments cannot be established from the sources used here.

The metalwork survives sufficiently intact for continuing display and ceremonial carriage. It bears the effects of its composite construction: damaged medieval plaques, missing members of earlier enamel series, transplanted elements, and a seventeenth-century framework. Its precise conservation history after 1694 is not published in the consulted sources.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### The claimed relic

St. Blaise was traditionally a bishop of Sebaste in Armenia Minor, now Sivas in Türkiye, martyred by decapitation during the Licinian persecutions, conventionally around 316. The chronology and details of his life are hagiographic rather than contemporary biographical documentation. The mere fact that the Dubrovnik material is cranial would therefore be consistent with the martyrdom story, but it does not identify the bone.

### Traditional arrival in Dubrovnik: 1026

The chronicle verses attributed to Miletius say that, while Vital was metropolitan and Lampridius judge, the bodies of the martyrs Lawrence, Andrew, and Peter—not the famous deacon and apostles—were translated, and that Blaise’s head was found with them in the year 1026:

> *Cum quibus et Blasii constat caput esse repertum,  
> Millenus vicenus sextus cum foret annus.*

“Together with them, it is established that the head of Blaise was found, when the year was one thousand and twenty-six.”

The verses survive through the sixteenth-century annals of Nikša/Nicola Ranjina, not in an autograph from 1026. Miletius’s own date is disputed: proposals range from the eleventh to the fourteenth century. Linguistic scholarship has argued that he drew upon an early Dubrovnik ecclesiastical memorial source also related to information used by Constantine VII Porphyrogenitus, but that does not make the extant wording a contemporary 1026 record. [Munk indexed full text](https://hrcak.srce.hr/file/246375).

Later Ragusan chronicles elaborate the episode. Relics of Peter, Lawrence, and Andrew were allegedly revealed in visions to a woman named Catherine near the Kotor territory. After the people of Kotor failed to act, Ragusan fishermen or officials retrieved the remains. Blaise’s head was found with them, and the relics were carried to Dubrovnik. One version places their initial deposition in the church of St. Stephen; another places them in the cathedral. [Ranjina annals transcription](https://pdfcoffee.com/nika-andreti-ranjina-nicola-ragnina-annales-ragusini-nicolai-de-ragnina-anali-di-ragusa-gamulin-mice-5-pdf-free.html); [study of the Petilovrijenci cult](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/315991213_Crkva_i_kult_sv_Petilovrijenaca_u_srednjovjekovnom_Dubrovniku).

Munk characterizes the episode as a medieval *inventio* and *translatio*, probably involving what modern historians call a “holy theft”: appropriation of relics from neighboring territory re-described as providential discovery. That is a historical interpretation of the chronicle narrative, not direct documentation of a raid.

### Competing arrival tradition

Ranjina also preserves an inconsistent story in which a Greek from the Levant brought the head to Dubrovnik and received 500 ducats; Resti similarly mentions a Greek rewarded with a large quantity of silver. These versions may rationalize or replace the Kotor acquisition story. The identity of the Greek, his route, the source of the relic, the date, and the actual payment cannot be independently established. [Croatian Catholic Network report summarizing the sources](https://hkm.hr/vijesti/domovina/sveta-krada-kako-je-relikvija-glave-sv-vlaha-prije-1000-godina-stigla-u-dubrovnik/).

### First independent notice: 1323

The Franciscan pilgrim Symon Semeonis, or Simon Fitzsimmons, passed through Ragusa in 1323. He reported that the head of St. Blaise was among the city’s celebrated relics, although the wording indicates that he did not personally see it. This is the earliest securely datable external notice identified in the present research. It establishes that the claim was circulating internationally before the first surviving cathedral inventory; it does not verify the relic’s biological identity. [Dubrovnik Diocese report on Munk’s research](https://db.hr/predavanje-prof-munk-o-relikvijaru-glave-sv-vlaha/).

### First surviving inventory: 1335

The cathedral inventory of 1335 records:

> **Capud Beati Blasij epi. in bacino argenti.**

This is the first secure internal documentary record of the relic and its container. It establishes that the cathedral possessed something identified as the head of Bishop Blaise in or on a silver *bacinum*. It does not describe enamel, a crown, gemstones, a maker, a date, or a Byzantine donor. [Dubrovnik Cathedral](https://katedraladubrovnik.hr/the-reliquary/).

### Manufacture of the medieval enamels

No contract or commission for the medieval container or plaques survives in the published record.

Older scholarship and current institutional texts often attribute the reliquary or its oldest artistic layer to eleventh-century Constantinopolitan workshops. Munk’s re-examination separates the components:

- the bone’s arrival may, according to tradition, date to 1026;
- four damaged plaques may be tenth- or eleventh-century work of uncertain origin;
- the main enamel series is probably later, perhaps Dubrovnik, 1164–1180;
- four plaques belong to the late-twelfth-century arm reliquary;
- the extant setting is Venetian work of 1694.

Thus neither “eleventh-century Byzantine reliquary” nor “entirely seventeenth-century object” adequately describes the composite artifact.

### Manufacture in 1694

The 1667 earthquake destroyed the Romanesque cathedral and devastated its treasury. Salvaged relics and metalwork were removed from the ruins. The former head reliquary was evidently damaged beyond satisfactory continued use. Ferro created a replacement in 1694, setting surviving medieval enamels from at least three sources into a new silver-gilt, gold-wire, filigree, and enamel framework.

This was not a modern-style restoration preserving an object’s material state. It was reconstruction through reuse: a new reliquary that retained the sacred contents and precious fragments of several damaged medieval works.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Before 1026

No documented chain connects the Dubrovnik bone with Blaise’s tomb at Sebaste. No source establishes removal from the saint’s body, transport through Byzantium, or arrival in Kotor. This entire segment is missing.

### 1026: alleged Kotor-region acquisition

**Traditional claim:** under Archbishop Vital and Judge Lampridius, Ragusans found or took the head with the relics of Peter, Lawrence, and Andrew in Kotor territory and brought them to Dubrovnik.

**Variant:** a Greek from the Levant delivered the head and was paid 500 ducats or a substantial sum of silver.

**Custody destination:** Ranjina places the translated relics in St. Stephen’s church; Resti says the cathedral. The discrepancy is unresolved.

### Eleventh to early fourteenth century

The custody chain is undocumented. A container presumably existed if the relic was publicly recognized, but neither its form nor its location is securely recorded.

### 1323

Symon Semeonis heard of the head while passing through Ragusa. The report shows that the claim belonged to the city’s pilgrim-facing sacred reputation.

### 1335

The cathedral inventory securely places the head in a silver *bacinum*. From this point the relic belongs to the documented institutional custody of the Ragusan cathedral chapter, under the wider religious and civic protection of the Republic.

### Fourteenth to seventeenth century

The cathedral treasury, or *Moćnik*, was kept in a specially protected chapel above the vaults of a side aisle of the Romanesque cathedral. The Republic treated the treasury as a civic as well as ecclesiastical possession. On 2 December 1440 the Senate issued regulations for the treasury’s maintenance and security; the act includes a detailed description of its room. [Dubrovnik Cathedral](https://katedraladubrovnik.hr/the-reliquary/).

Pilgrims and diplomatic visitors were shown the collection. A fifteenth-century account attributed to Gabriele Capodilista claimed that Ragusa possessed enough relics to ornament four hundred friars—a rhetorical measure of abundance rather than an inventory count. Displaying the treasury functioned as late-medieval institutional self-representation.

The head relic took part in civic liturgy. Medieval regulations placed relics in processions involving the rector, Senate, clergy, and population. The 1272 Statute prescribed observance of St. Blaise’s feast; later rules allowed or expected a bishop to carry the head reliquary. Touching the relics was strongly desired, and later descriptions mention an attendant using a rod to keep crowds from pressing upon them.

### 6 April 1667: earthquake and rescue

The great Dubrovnik earthquake collapsed the Romanesque cathedral and damaged its treasury. Within days, civic and ecclesiastical authorities organized recovery of relics and valuables from the ruins. The rescued objects were first taken to the Revelin fortress and then to the chapter room of the Dominican monastery. [“Tristogodišnjica baroknoga Moćnika”](https://katedraladubrovnik.hr/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/NG61web-1.pdf).

The head relic apparently survived. Its medieval container did not survive intact as an independent object. Scattered enamel plaques were recovered and subsequently redistributed. The sources do not provide an itemized excavation record allowing every recovered fragment to be assigned to its pre-earthquake parent object.

### 1694: remaking by Francesco Ferro

While the treasury remained in temporary accommodation, Ferro made the present reliquary. He enclosed or reset the claimed cranial relic and reused medieval enamel fragments. The result became the continuing bearer of the old relic’s identity.

### 1694–1721: Dominican custody

The cathedral relic collection remained in the Dominican monastery’s chapter premises while the new Baroque cathedral was constructed and fitted. This period lasted fifty-four years from the earthquake.

### 3 February 1721: return to the cathedral

Although the new cathedral was blessed in 1713, the relics were ceremonially returned only on the feast of St. Blaise in 1721. The Ragusan government ordered a solemn penitential procession from the Dominican monastery to the new cathedral. The translation marked both the completion of the cathedral and a public assertion that the city’s sacred treasury had survived the disaster. [Dubrovnik Cathedral anniversary account](https://katedraladubrovnik.hr/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/NG61web-1.pdf).

### Nineteenth century

The French occupation and abolition of the Republic in 1808 did not produce a documented confiscation or removal of the head reliquary. Custody continued with the cathedral chapter under successive French and Habsburg political regimes.

Stjepan Skurla’s 1868 catalogue, *Moćnik Stolne crkve dubrovačke*, became a foundational study of the treasury. It documented the earthquake damage and surviving collection but participated in interpretations subsequently revised by technical art history.

### Early twentieth century

F. Hamilton Jackson inspected and photographed the treasury. He identified Ferro’s signature, described the gold-wire and enamel construction, and recognized that small “Byzantine” enamels had been reset in 1694. His account is valuable as an external observation predating recent reinterpretation. [Jackson](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/30548/pg30548-images.html).

### First World War

A proposed wartime evacuation of Dubrovnik’s relics to Graz was refused. The head relic remained in Dubrovnik. The cathedral’s institutional account presents this as adherence to the rule that the patron’s head must not leave the city. [Dubrovnik Cathedral, *Moćnik Katedrale*](https://katedraladubrovnik.hr/mocnik-katedrale/).

### 1943: attempted Fascist removal

According to the cathedral’s historical account, Italian Fascist authorities intended to remove the head relic from Dubrovnik, but Italy’s capitulation on 8 September 1943 prevented the operation. The accessible account does not name the officials, cite a requisition order, or specify a prepared destination. The episode should therefore be recorded as an institutional historical claim requiring archival corroboration, not as a completed theft.

### 1991–1992 siege and war

During the Croatian war, UNESCO reportedly offered to remove or shelter the treasury in Italy. Evacuation was refused and the relic remained in Dubrovnik. Again, the reason given was that the patron’s head could not leave the city even temporarily. [Dubrovnik Cathedral](https://katedraladubrovnik.hr/mocnik-katedrale/).

### 1999–2000 Vatican exhibition

The head relic was withheld from the Vatican exhibition *Hrvati—kršćanstvo, kultura i umjetnost*. This is a significant negative loan history: other national treasures could travel, but this object’s civic-sacral identity was judged incompatible with removal from Dubrovnik.

### Present custody

The reliquary remains under the control of the Cathedral of the Assumption and the Diocese of Dubrovnik. It is normally kept in the cathedral treasury and is taken out for liturgical display and the annual Festa of St. Blaise. In 2026 it was carried in a special procession marking the traditional thousandth anniversary of the relic’s arrival. [Diocese of Dubrovnik, 2026 procession](https://db.hr/svecana-procesija-s-relikvijom-glave-sv-vlaha/).

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is the surviving metalwork the medieval reliquary?

No. The extant setting is signed and dated 1694. It contains medieval components, but the silver-gilt assemblage as presently constituted is Ferro’s work.

The cathedral describes Ferro’s intervention as a “restoration” respecting the old form. Munk argues more precisely that he remade the reliquary following the earthquake and that the original shape cannot be recovered from the surviving object alone.

### Did Ferro copy an imperial crown?

This is disputed.

**Crown interpretation:** Lupis and institutional catalogues see the medieval object as derived from a Byzantine imperial crown or crown form. Its importance is reinforced by the numerous Dubrovnik head reliquaries that imitate its rounded outline.

**Domed-container interpretation:** Munk finds no evidence that *bacino* denoted a crown. She reconstructs a cylindrical reliquary with a domed lid placed on a dish, comparable to Zadar examples. The language of “crowns of martyrdom” may have contributed symbolically without implying reproduction of imperial regalia.

The latter analysis directly addresses and rejects the former; modern catalogues nevertheless continue to repeat “Byzantine imperial crown.”

### Are the enamels Byzantine?

Not as a single group. The older phrase “Byzantine enamels” conflates material from different times and workshops.

- Group I: likely Byzantinizing Dalmatian or southern Italian work; Munk favors Dubrovnik, 1164–1180.
- Group II: uncertain, possibly tenth or eleventh century; origin unresolved.
- Group III: late-twelfth-century southern Italian/Sicilian work related to Norman court production.
- Setting: Venetian, 1694.

No metallurgical, compositional, or isotopic analysis capable of independently locating the enamels was found. Present conclusions depend largely on style, iconography, technique, and palaeography.

### Is the bone genuinely the head of St. Blaise?

The Catholic institution accepts it as such, and a continuous documented cathedral custody can be reconstructed from 1335, interrupted in location but not apparently in institutional control after the 1667 disaster.

Against certainty:

- there is no contemporary record of Blaise’s burial or division;
- the chain from Sebaste to the Adriatic is absent;
- the 1026 story survives in later chronicle transmission;
- arrival traditions contradict one another;
- no published radiocarbon, osteological, isotope, proteomic, or DNA examination was located;
- numerous churches have claimed cranial relics or cranial fragments of St. Blaise.

Claims to heads of St. Blaise are historically reported at Naples, Saint-Maximin in Provence, Montpellier, Orbetello, and Dubrovnik. Maratea’s canonically examined shrine contains a fragment of cranium together with thoracic, femoral, and arm material. These claims are not necessarily anatomically exclusive if each contains a fragment, but historical descriptions often call several of them “the head.” [Italian cultural-property catalogue for Maratea](https://catalogo.beniculturali.it/detail/ArchitecturalOrLandscapeHeritage/17iccd_modi_3861675236271); [Maratea relic history](https://marateaclub.com/le-reliquie-di-s-biagio-conservate-a-maratea/).

No comparative scientific investigation has established that the Dubrovnik and other claimed relics derive from one individual, a fourth-century male, or a population associated with Sebaste.

### Authority to authenticate

In medieval and early-modern Dubrovnik, authentication was effectively exercised by possession, cathedral inventory, episcopal custody, civic recognition, seals, and liturgical use. Ferro’s signed work authenticated its maker and date, not the bone.

Under modern Catholic procedure, the diocesan bishop and competent ecclesiastical authorities oversee canonical recognition, conservation, sampling, and transfer of major relics. The Vatican’s 2017 instruction sets procedures but does not itself authenticate the Dubrovnik relic. [Vatican instruction on relics](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/csaints/documents/rc_con_csaints_doc_20171208_istruzione-reliquie_it.html).

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

- **St. Blaise of Sebaste** — claimed bodily source; bishop-martyr and divine intercessor represented through the relic. Association is devotional and institutional; biological identity is unverified.

- **Archbishop Vital of Dubrovnik** — named in the Miletius tradition as metropolitan when the relic was found and transferred in 1026. This is a retrospective chronicle association.

- **Judge Lampridius/Lampredio** — civic magistrate named alongside Vital in the same verses.

- **Catherine** — visionary woman in the later translation legend who learned the location of the Petilovrijenci relics. Her connection to Blaise’s head varies by version and is legendary.

- **Unnamed Ragusan fishermen** — instruments of the Kotor-region discovery and transport in one chronicle version.

- **Unnamed Greek from the Levant** — alleged bearer who sold or surrendered the head for 500 ducats or a large silver payment in the competing tradition.

- **Miletius** — chronicler or poet to whom the 1026 verses are attributed. His date and proximity to the event are disputed.

- **Nikša/Nicola Ranjina (1494–1582)** — compiler who preserved Miletius’s verses and alternative acquisition accounts. He is a documentary transmitter, not a participant in the transfer.

- **Junije/Junius Resti (1669–1735)** — later Ragusan chronicler preserving variants of the relic narratives.

- **Symon Semeonis/Simon Fitzsimmons** — Irish Franciscan pilgrim who recorded the head’s reputation in 1323 without seeing it.

- **Cathedral chapter of Dubrovnik** — principal documented keeper from at least 1335 to the present.

- **Rector, Senate, Great and Minor Councils of Ragusa** — civic bodies that regulated the treasury and incorporated the relic into state ceremony.

- **Dominican community of Dubrovnik** — temporary keeper of the rescued treasury from 1667 to 1721.

- **Francesco Ferro** — Venetian goldsmith who made the surviving reliquary in 1694 and transplanted medieval enamels into it.

- **Stjepan Skurla** — author of the important 1868 treasury catalogue.

- **Lazar Mirković and Viktor Novak** — twentieth-century scholars who classified the enamels and inscriptions; their datings and Byzantine attributions supplied the basis for later reassessment.

- **Vinicije Lupis** — scholar of Dubrovnik metalwork who maintains the crown-derived formal genealogy.

- **Ana Munk** — scholar whose 2015–2016 analyses dismantled the idea of a unitary Byzantine crown-reliquary and reconstructed its composite material history.

- **Diocese of Dubrovnik and Bishop of Dubrovnik** — current ecclesiastical custodians; bishops conventionally carry the reliquary at the front of the relic procession.

- **Festa officials, festanjuli, banner-bearers, confraternities, pilgrims, and citizens** — recurring civic-religious network through which the reliquary is displayed and mobilized.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Annual feast and procession

The reliquary’s principal active function is liturgical, not museological. On 2 February, Candlemas and the eve of the feast, St. Blaise’s head relic is exposed in the cathedral during First Vespers. On 3 February, after the principal Eucharistic celebration, it is carried through Dubrovnik in a procession of clergy, bishops, parish banners, civic authorities, confraternities, residents, and pilgrims. [Diocese festival program](https://db.hr/festa-sv-vlaha/).

The head reliquary traditionally leads the clerical group of relic bearers and is carried by a bishop, marking its precedence within the treasury. The route passes through the historic city and the Church of St. Blaise before returning to the cathedral. The 2026 procession followed Ulica od Puča, passed Onofrio’s Fountain, traversed Stradun, and entered the patron’s church. [IKA, 2026 procession](https://ika.hkm.hr/novosti/svecana-procesija-s-relikvijom-glave-sv-vlaha/).

### Healing and access

St. Blaise is invoked against diseases and injuries of the throat. The ritual blessing of throats, *grličanje*, uses crossed candles and is performed during the feast, but the available evidence does not show that the head bone itself is applied to the throat. The healing function belongs to the saint’s cult and festival complex.

Worshippers seek proximity to and sometimes touch the reliquaries. Historical crowd-control measures demonstrate both access and anxiety over physical damage: attendants reportedly drove back those pressing to touch the objects. Modern preparation and handling are undertaken by cathedral personnel. [HRT report on preparation](https://magazin.hrt.hr/kultura/kako-izgleda-priprema-relikvija-za-festu-svetog-vlaha--11333687).

### Civic and diplomatic function

In the Republic of Ragusa, the relic was effectively a civic palladium. St. Blaise’s image appeared on fortifications, gates, seals, flags, coinage, official buildings, and state documents. The head reliquary was the most concentrated material claim that the saint’s bodily presence resided within the city.

Showing the treasury to envoys and high-status visitors advertised Ragusa’s wealth, antiquity, orthodoxy, international connections, and divine protection. The collection’s artistic and relic abundance constituted diplomatic capital.

Possession did not confer legal sovereignty in a formal constitutional sense, but it supplied sacred legitimacy. The rule that the head must remain within Dubrovnik—invoked against removal during two wars and a Vatican exhibition—shows that the relic is treated as attached to the city rather than as a freely lendable artwork.

### UNESCO recognition

In 2009 UNESCO inscribed the Festivity of St. Blaise on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. UNESCO’s listed element is the living festival, not the reliquary as movable cultural property, but the procession of relics is central to the practice. UNESCO describes the eve’s bells, release of white doves, throat blessing, and the 3 February gathering of banner-bearers, bishops, diplomats, civic leaders, and residents. [UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/festivity-of-saint-blaise-the-patron-of-dubrovnik-00232?lang=en).

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The saint’s warning and Dubrovnik’s deliverance

According to the city’s patronal legend, in 971 or 972 St. Blaise appeared to a priest named Stojko at St. Stephen’s church. He warned that Venetian ships ostensibly taking on supplies were preparing a surprise attack. The city was alerted, the plan failed, and Blaise replaced or eclipsed earlier patrons, particularly Sergius and Bacchus.

This is a legend about the saint’s protective presence rather than the head relic itself. It predates the traditional arrival of the head by approximately half a century. Later civic religion joined the apparition and the relic: the saint who had defended Dubrovnik spiritually became bodily present through his head.

The story’s precise year varies between 971 and 972. The claim that the Festa has continued without interruption since that date is institutional tradition, not a year-by-year documented series.

### The providential discovery at Kotor

In the fullest version, martyrs appeared to Catherine and asked that their bodies be raised. The Kotorans disbelieved her. The saints then directed her toward the Ragusans, whose fishermen or authorities recovered the relics. Blaise’s head was found among them and taken to Dubrovnik.

The story performs several functions:

- it makes Ragusan possession divinely chosen;
- it transfers sacred favor from disbelieving Kotor to responsive Dubrovnik;
- it redescribes appropriation from neighboring territory as *inventio*;
- it joins the patron’s head to the local cult of Peter, Lawrence, and Andrew, the Petilovrijenci.

Its historical core may be a transfer or seizure of relics from the Bay of Kotor. The vision, saints’ instructions, and providential preference cannot be historically tested.

### The Greek bearer and payment

The alternative narrative says a Greek from the Levant brought the head and was rewarded with 500 ducats. This supplies an eastern provenance and a transaction rather than a Kotor raid. It may reflect a different oral tradition or an attempt to give the acquisition a less contentious form.

No named bearer, receipt, treasury payment, ship, or date survives. The amount may be formulaic. This version conflicts with, rather than securely supplements, the Kotor story.

### The “Byzantine imperial crown”

The belief that the reliquary reproduces an emperor’s crown is a scholarly and institutional interpretation that became a popular tradition. No early text says an emperor donated a crown, that the relic entered Dubrovnik in imperial regalia, or that the container was made from an actual crown.

The legend was visually persuasive because the surviving object resembles a jeweled cap and its enamels appeared Byzantine. Once the components are separated chronologically, the apparently unitary imperial object dissolves into a medieval relic, several medieval enamel groups, and Ferro’s Baroque reconstruction.

### The relic that cannot leave Dubrovnik

The prohibition on removal functions as a modern continuation of the palladium idea: while the protector’s head remains, the city remains under his guardianship. The rule has had verifiable effects—refused evacuations and loans—even though no supernatural consequence of removal can be tested. It is therefore simultaneously pious tradition and documented provenance-making practice.

## 8. Current Status and Location

As of 2026, the reliquary is preserved in the Treasury or *Moćnik* of the Cathedral of the Assumption of the Virgin Mary, Poljana Marina Držića, Dubrovnik, Croatia. It is controlled by the cathedral chapter and Diocese of Dubrovnik.

Its normal status combines:

- protected treasury object;
- ecclesiastical relic container;
- object of active Catholic veneration;
- ceremonial object carried annually;
- major work of Croatian and Adriatic goldsmithing.

Public access to the treasury is possible under cathedral arrangements, but access to the bone itself is restricted. The reliquary leaves the treasury for liturgy and procession; it is not ordinarily lent outside Dubrovnik.

No published accession number comparable to a state-museum inventory number was found. Cathedral lists identify the head reliquary as **II**, while numbers used in successive inventories are not necessarily equivalent to a modern accession system.

The 2026 millennium observance treated the year 1026 as the anniversary of the relic’s translation. Bishops and archbishops carried the object through the historic center; Orthodox clergy paid respect when the procession passed the Orthodox church. This was an ecumenical gesture toward the relic’s civic role, not an authentication of its biological identity. [Dubrovnik Diocese](https://db.hr/svecana-procesija-s-relikvijom-glave-sv-vlaha/).

No current ownership, restitution, or repatriation litigation was found. Kotor does not appear in the consulted sources as pursuing a formal claim based on the medieval “holy theft” narrative.

No recent invasive scientific examination has been published. The most important recent scrutiny has been visual and art-historical, especially Munk’s two examinations of the reliquary and its enamel groups.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Scholarship and tourism

The reliquary is routinely advertised as an eleventh-century Byzantine masterpiece shaped like an imperial crown. This compressed description remains powerful in tourism and museum publicity but obscures the 1694 setting and composite origins of the enamel.

High-resolution photography and digital presentations have made details normally difficult to see available to visitors. A public Wikimedia photograph describes the object as a skull-cap reliquary with relics of several saints, but that metadata appears to confuse it with another multi-saint Dubrovnik head reliquary and should not override specialist identification. [Wikimedia Commons object page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AHead_reliquary_Dubrovnik_Cathedral.jpg).

### Art and visual culture

The reliquary appears in photography, exhibition displays, church publications, festival posters, documentaries, and Croatian sacred-art surveys. Its form has also influenced the public image of the entire Dubrovnik treasury: jewel-like Byzantine enamel joined to Baroque filigree.

Ivo Dulčić’s modern imagery of the Festa and Dubrovnik’s patronal cult helped translate the relic procession into twentieth-century Croatian religious art, though the reliquary is represented as part of the ceremony rather than treated as an archaeological object.

### Civic and national mobilization

Under the Republic, the relic participated in Ragusan state identity. In modern Croatia it functions simultaneously as:

- a Catholic sacred object;
- an emblem of Dubrovnik’s historical autonomy;
- evidence of the city’s Mediterranean and Byzantine connections;
- an object of Croatian national heritage;
- a focal point in UNESCO-recognized living tradition.

The decisions not to evacuate it in the First World War, in 1991–1992, or for the 1999–2000 Vatican exhibition transformed immobility into a public statement of local identity.

### Replicas, forgeries, and hoaxes

No documented forgery masquerading as the Dubrovnik head reliquary was found. Reproductions exist in photography, printed souvenirs, miniatures, and digital visualization, but no replica was identified as having acquired independent ritual status.

No evidence was found for an occult reinterpretation specific to the reliquary. Popular internet material sometimes conflates the relic with other claimed skulls of St. Blaise or presents the crown interpretation as uncontested fact.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Principal documentary foundations

- Chronicle verses attributed to Miletius, preserved in Ranjina’s annals.
- Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Ragusan chronicles by Ranjina and Resti.
- Symon Semeonis’s 1323 pilgrimage account.
- Cathedral inventory of 1335.
- Senate regulation of 2 December 1440.
- Later cathedral inventories and procession regulations.
- Ferro’s signature and date on the object.
- Post-earthquake records concerning rescue, temporary custody, repair, and return.
- Stjepan Skurla’s 1868 treasury catalogue.

### Major interpretive phases

**Nineteenth-century cataloguing:** Skurla established the treasury as an art-historical subject and documented traditions concerning damage and restoration.

**Early-twentieth-century connoisseurship:** Jackson recognized the 1694 reset. Scholars such as Lazar Mirković and Viktor Novak classified the enamel groups, scripts, and probable dates. Byzantine origin was often used broadly.

**Late-twentieth- and early-twenty-first-century local synthesis:** Lupis placed the object within Dubrovnik’s unusually large family of head reliquaries and argued that its crown-like form served as their prototype.

**Recent revision:** Munk separated relic, lost container, surviving setting, and enamel spolia. She rejected the assumed imperial-crown form, proposed a domed cylindrical medieval predecessor, attributed the principal enamel group to Dubrovnik or the southern Adriatic rather than Constantinople, and emphasized the object’s remaking after 1667.

### Unresolved questions

1. What precisely survives inside the capsule?
2. Has the relic ever undergone formal canonical recognition with a recorded opening?
3. Can its seals and internal wrappings be dated?
4. Is the bone human and cranial; what are its age, sex, radiocarbon range, and geographic indicators?
5. Which enamel plaques belonged to the pre-1667 head reliquary?
6. What was the exact form of that medieval container?
7. Did Group I originate in Dubrovnik or southern Italy?
8. Where was Group II made?
9. Was the relic acquired from Kotor territory, purchased from an eastern bearer, or obtained by another route?
10. When was the Miletius passage composed?
11. Are archival records of the alleged 1943 removal plan and UNESCO’s 1991–1992 evacuation offer preserved?
12. What conservation treatments have occurred since Ferro’s intervention?

### Evidentiary state

The physical object has been closely viewed and photographed but apparently not subjected to a fully published interdisciplinary campaign combining metal analysis, enamel chemistry, radiography, dendrology of any core, examination of fasteners, textile analysis, canonical seals, and bioarchaeology. Such work could clarify manufacture and reconstruction but could not by itself prove identity with a named fourth-century saint without a securely authenticated comparative sample.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and near-primary evidence

- Miletius’s verses, surviving through Ranjina’s sixteenth-century annals, are the foundational source for the 1026 date but are not an original 1026 document.
- Ranjina and Resti preserve incompatible acquisition narratives and different initial deposit locations.
- Symon Semeonis’s 1323 travel account is the earliest securely dated external witness to the claim.
- The 1335 cathedral inventory is the earliest secure internal record.
- Ferro’s 1694 signature is the decisive primary evidence for the surviving setting.
- Cathedral and civic records document the treasury’s rescue, temporary locations, and 1721 return.

### Major modern studies

Ana Munk’s 2016 article is the most important object-specific reassessment. It treats the reliquary as a stratified composite, challenges the imperial-crown reconstruction, and distinguishes three enamel groups.

Vinicije Lupis’s work supplies the broader typology of Dubrovnik head reliquaries and represents the principal crown-prototype interpretation.

The Dubrovnik Cathedral’s treasury history provides valuable local institutional synthesis, especially for physical custody, wartime non-evacuation, and the collection’s present liturgical life. Its traditional and interpretive claims require distinction from archival transcription.

### Research limitations

Several indispensable works are accessible only through indexed PDF text or secondary citation. The cathedral’s complete historical inventories, the 1440 Senate act, Skurla’s full catalogue, conservation files, canonical recognition records, and internal treasury documentation were not available as fully searchable critical editions in the consulted web sources. No laboratory report on the bone or metalwork was found.
