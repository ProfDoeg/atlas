# Joan of Arc's distaff and spindle: Object Dossier

## Scope and principal finding

**Object-status finding:** no surviving object, medieval inventory, relic notice, shrine record, or continuous chain of custody has been identified for a particular distaff and spindle owned by Joan of Arc. The historical record establishes that Joan spun thread and therefore used spinning implements, probably more than one set, but it does not individuate any one pair. “Joan of Arc’s distaff and spindle” is consequently best classified as an **iconographic attribute founded on documented biography**, not as an authenticated historical relic.

Three materially different subjects have sometimes converged under the name:

1. **Joan’s unrecorded working implements** — ordinary distaffs and spindles she must have handled while spinning at Domrémy, Burey-le-Petit, and Vaucouleurs. Their manufacture, appearance, custody, and fate are unknown.
2. **An English anti-Joan standard of 1429** — a real but lost banner reportedly decorated with a flax-loaded distaff, a half-filled spindle, scattered empty spindles, and the challenge *Or viegne la belle!* This was not Joan’s property; it mocked her by ordering her back to women’s work.
3. **The later representational pair** — the distaff and spindle placed in Joan’s hands or beside her in manuscripts, prints, paintings, sculpture, banners, devotional objects, and popular imagery. These are multiple representations and fabricated accessories, not one transmitted object.

These three histories are kept separate below.

### Evidentiary labels

- **D1 — contemporary documentary evidence:** records made during Joan’s lifetime, notably the 1431 trial.
- **D2 — retrospective sworn testimony:** principally the 1455–1456 nullification inquiry.
- **D3 — near-contemporary narrative or image:** fifteenth- or early-sixteenth-century chronicles, drama, manuscripts, and prints.
- **D4 — later scholarly or institutional record:** critical editions, museum catalogues, inventories, and modern scholarship.
- **T — later tradition or iconographic convention.**
- **U — unresolved or unverified claim.**
- **N — negative finding:** no qualifying evidence located; this is not proof that no such evidence could exist.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Identity and classification

**Preferred identification:** *Joan of Arc’s distaff and spindle*; French *la quenouille et le fuseau de Jeanne d’Arc*; Middle French spellings in the sources include *quenuelle*, *fuisel*, and *fusiaus*. The corresponding Latin vocabulary is *colus* or *colucula* for a distaff and *fusus* for a spindle, although testimony about Joan more commonly uses forms of *nere*, “to spin,” without describing the implements.

The object is not a single catalogued artifact. It is a **composite iconographic attribute** derived from a type of domestic equipment Joan demonstrably used. No accession number, reliquary, canonical authentication, or recognized claimant presently embodies “the” pair.

A **distaff** (*quenouille*) is a staff or reed supporting unspun flax, hemp, wool, silk, or cotton so the fibres remain ordered while the spinner draws them out. The historical French lexicographical description gives a staff approximately three and a half feet long, with fibres fixed near its upper end, usable either with a hand spindle or a wheel. A **spindle** (*fuseau*) is a smaller, usually wooden rod rotated to twist the drawn fibres and wind the completed thread. These are functional definitions, not descriptions of Joan’s particular tools. [CNRTL/TLFi](https://www.cnrtl.fr/definition/quenouille); [Encyclopédie definition](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/L%E2%80%99Encyclop%C3%A9die/1re_%C3%A9dition/QUENOUILLE); [Académie definition of *fuseau*](https://www.cnrtl.fr/definition/academie8/fuseau).

### 1.2 What can be said about Joan’s historical implements

**Documented fact (D1–D2):** Joan knew how to spin; witnesses remembered spinning with her, and one remembered her spinning while minding livestock. Therefore implements existed in the ordinary sense whenever she performed that work.

**Not established (N/U):**

- whether she owned her own tools or used household tools;
- how many distaffs or spindles she used;
- whether they were made for her;
- whether they were carried from Domrémy to Burey or Vaucouleurs;
- their wood species, fibre, length, weight, decoration, maker, marks, repairs, or wear;
- whether any survived her departure for Chinon in February 1429;
- whether any object later displayed or sold was claimed as her authentic tool.

No source located calls a surviving pair a bodily or contact relic, records it in a church treasury, or supplies a custody history.

### 1.3 The conventional visual object

Later images usually combine:

- a long wooden or reed distaff;
- a mass of pale flax, hemp, or wool near the upper end;
- a suspended or handheld spindle;
- loose thread joining the two;
- sometimes a dropped spindle, indicating that Joan has suddenly heard her “voices”;
- rural costume, sheep, trees, a cottage, or her father at the plough.

These details vary by artist. They do not derive from a surviving model.

The earliest securely identified published image of the motif is a woodcut in an early printed edition of Martial d’Auvergne’s *Vigiles de la mort de Charles VII*. It shows Joan spinning beside Jacques d’Arc, who operates a plough. The image is commonly dated to the early sixteenth century; the exact impression illustrated online has not been tied securely to a particular surviving pair of tools. [Image record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AJeanne_d%27Arc_filant_-_Les_Vigiles_de_la_mort_de_Charles_VII.jpg); [description of the printed woodcut](https://boudewijnhuijgens.getarchive.net/amp/media/jeanne-darc-filant-les-vigiles-de-la-mort-de-charles-vii-224dc0).

### 1.4 The English standard as a separate object

A Lille chronicle reports that an English captain had a white standard made in July 1429. Its field contained:

- a distaff loaded with flax;
- a spindle hanging from it, about half wound with thread;
- numerous empty spindles scattered over the field;
- the inscription **“Or viegne la belle!”** — approximately, “Now let the fair one come!”

The chronicler explains the conceit: the captain would give Joan spinning to do. This was an antagonistic military image, not a depiction of a relic and not Joan’s own banner. Its dimensions, fabric specification beyond “white,” embroidery or painting technique, maker, captain, and subsequent fate are unrecorded. The surviving wording is preserved in a nineteenth-century publication of the chronicle: *“ung estendart tout blancq … une quenuelle chergié de lin … ung fuisel … à moitiet fusée … fusiaus wis … Or viegne la belle!”* [Published chronicle extract](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/09/Bulletin_de_la_Soci%C3%A9t%C3%A9_de_l%27histoire_de_France_%28IA_BulletinDeLaSocieteDeLHistoireDeFrance1857%29.pdf).

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Historical use: the 1431 testimony

The earliest direct evidence is Joan’s own examination at Rouen on 22 February 1431. Asked whether she had learned a craft in youth, she answered that she had learned to sew and spin and that, in sewing and spinning, she feared no woman in Rouen—meaning she considered herself at least their equal. The Latin trial record derives from proceedings conducted orally in French and recorded by notaries; the French working minute and Latin redaction must therefore be distinguished from a verbatim modern transcript. [English translation of the trial record](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/basis/joanofarc-trial.asp); [account of the French minute and Latin redaction](https://www.clerus.org/clerus/dati/2001-10/23-13/jeannearc.html).

What this proves:

- Joan practised spinning before leaving home.
- She regarded herself as proficient.
- Some spinning equipment necessarily existed.

What it does not prove:

- that she possessed a unique, personally commissioned pair;
- that the implement was present when she first heard a voice;
- that she carried it while shepherding;
- that any particular surviving implement is hers.

### 2.2 Retrospective testimony, 1455–1456

The nullification inquiry supplies corroborating but retrospective testimony, given roughly twenty-seven years after Joan left Domrémy.

**Mengette, wife of Jean Joyart (D2):** she had often spun with Joan and performed household work with her, by day and night. Joan also went to harvest and, when it was her household’s turn, sometimes guarded the village herd while spinning. This is the strongest source for the combined “spinner in the fields” motif. It does not say that she was a professional shepherdess or identify an individual distaff. [Pierre Duparc edition/transcription](https://jeanne.wiki/duparc-proces-nullite/chap5-2).

**Durand Laxart (D2):** while Joan stayed in his house she willingly worked, spun, went to the plough, watched animals, and performed other appropriate household tasks. A later edition quotes the underlying Latin: *libenter operabatur, nebat, ibat ad aratrum, animalia custodiebat…* [Quotation with Quicherat reference](https://www.traditio-op.org/biblioteca/Petitot/Sainte_Jeanne_d_Arc%2C_Fr._L._H._Petitot_OP.pdf).

**Catherine Le Royer (D2):** when Joan stayed in the Le Royer household at Vaucouleurs, Catherine remembered that Joan liked spinning, spun well, and spun together with her. [Deposition in translation](https://www.gildiacre.fr/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/ProcesJeanneDarc.pdf).

The convergence of Joan’s statement and several later witnesses makes her practice of spinning highly secure. The witnesses describe an activity and social setting, not a relic.

### 2.3 The earliest attested distaff-and-spindle image directed at Joan: 1429

The Lille chronicle’s English standard concerns events around July 1429. Although the surviving chronicle manuscript and its precise compilation history require further codicological study, the report preserves the earliest located **explicit visual program joining Joan, a distaff, and a spindle**. Its function was derisive: it opposed the feminine domestic implement to Joan’s military presence.

This episode is chronologically earlier than the surviving trial record, though its textual witness is not necessarily contemporary with the event. Vallet de Viriville used it in his nineteenth-century *Histoire de Charles VII* and associated the force with English reinforcements entering Paris under the Duke of Bedford and Cardinal Beaufort. [Vallet de Viriville](https://www.mediterranee-antique.fr/Fichiers_PdF/TUV/Vallet_Viriville/Charles_VII_2.pdf).

### 2.4 The growth of the domestic image

The *Mystère du siège d’Orléans*, probably composed or developed in the fifteenth century, stages Joan initially in a pastoral-domestic mode. Scholarship notes the direction “the Maid guarding her father’s sheep and sewing linen.” This shows how theatre joined rural labour, textile work, and the divine summons, even though the historical testimony does not place the first voice in a sheep pasture or explicitly beside a distaff. [Study of pastoral female imagery](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/354639321_Nobles_bergeres_et_pauvres_pastoures_Les_figures_pastorales_dans_les_representations_du_pouvoir_feminin).

Martial d’Auvergne composed the *Vigiles* between the late 1470s and 1484. The royal presentation manuscript, BnF Français 5054, was made in Paris around 1484–1485 and given to Charles VIII. It contains extensive Joan imagery, but the familiar scene of Joan spinning beside her father is associated with the work’s **printed woodcut tradition**, not securely with the surviving royal manuscript’s painted cycle. The work was first printed by Jean Du Pré in Paris in 1493 and went through numerous editions before 1515. [Biblissima manuscript record](https://portail.biblissima.fr/ark%3A/43093/mdatac7f1ba3e093a0deb91b36bb5ddfbbac3819214ce); [BnF printed-edition catalogue](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark%3A/12148/cb30893110x); [ARLIMA bibliography](https://arlima.net/mp/martial_d_auvergne/les_vigiles_de_la_mort_de_charles_vii.html).

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because there is no individuated relic, a conventional ownership chain cannot be reconstructed. The available chains are instead those of (A) hypothetical working tools, (B) the English standard, and (C) representations.

### 3.1 Joan’s working implements

**Before February 1429 — Domrémy household (inference from D1–D2).** Joan spun in and around her parents’ household. Implements were presumably owned communally by the d’Arc household or borrowed among women working together. No purchase, inheritance, maker, or household inventory survives.

**Before February 1429 — Burey-le-Petit (D2).** Durand Laxart remembered Joan spinning while staying with him. Whether she brought tools or used his household’s equipment is unknown.

**1428–1429 — Vaucouleurs (D2).** Catherine Le Royer remembered spinning with Joan in the Le Royer house. Again, custody of the implements is unspecified.

**February 1429 onward — documentary break (N).** None of the tools is recorded in the preparations for Chinon, royal accounts, military inventories, the 1431 proceedings, her post-capture property, the nullification case, or the later family material consulted here. No text says that Joan exchanged, gifted, abandoned, hid, or carried a particular distaff or spindle. The chain therefore ends before her military journey.

### 3.2 English mock standard

**July 1429 — manufacture and English military custody (D3).** According to the Lille chronicle, an unnamed captain with approximately 1,500 English men-at-arms dressed in white caused the standard to be made. Its place of manufacture is not given. It accompanied forces sent to reinforce Bedford.

**Later 1429 — disappearance from the record (N).** No capture, destruction, dedication, return to England, or later inventory has been located. Its chain breaks immediately after the reported campaign appearance.

Later writers sometimes phrase the emblem as an English flag “copied from Joan’s,” but the recorded design is a parody, not a literal copy of Joan’s religious standard. Joan’s own documented standard showed religious and royal imagery—Christ in Majesty, angels, lilies, the names Jesus and Mary, and, in some descriptions, the arms of France—not spinning tools. [Trial-era and chronicle descriptions of Joan’s standard](https://ste-jeannedarc.fr/histoire_wallon/appendice15.html).

### 3.3 The *Vigiles* image tradition

**1477–1484 — composition.** Martial d’Auvergne composed the poem.

**1484–1485 — royal manuscript.** The presentation manuscript BnF Français 5054 was offered by Martial to Charles VIII.

**Late fifteenth–sixteenth centuries — royal libraries.** Its recorded custody proceeded through the royal libraries at Amboise, Blois, and Fontainebleau and then the ancien fonds royal. Inventories cited in the manuscript record include Blois in 1518, the Fontainebleau transfer inventory of 1544, and a late-sixteenth-century royal inventory.

**1493 onward — printed multiplication.** Jean Du Pré’s printed edition and later editions transformed the narrative into reproducible woodcut cycles. In this branch there was never a single “original” distaff: every impression is ink on paper representing one.

**Present — BnF.** Français 5054 remains in the Bibliothèque nationale de France, Département des Manuscrits. The specific spinning woodcut belongs to the printed tradition and must be catalogued by edition and copy, not treated as a detached relic of Joan.

### 3.4 Nineteenth- and twentieth-century art objects

Each later artwork has its own provenance:

- **Jules Bastien-Lepage, *Jeanne d’Arc*, 1879:** oil on canvas, 254 × 279 cm; acquired by Erwin Davis, given to the Metropolitan Museum of Art in 1889; accession 89.21.1; presently displayed at The Met Fifth Avenue. Joan reaches toward the apparition while a spinning apparatus and household objects remain behind her. [Met catalogue](https://www.metmuseum.org/fr/art/collection/search/435621).
- **Anonymous or unattributed sculpture at Sens:** a 162 cm figure holding a distaff and spindle; formerly French state property/FNAC, ownership transferred to the municipality in 2008; Musée municipal de Sens, Joconde 01890012056. [French national museum record](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/joconde/01890012056).
- **Édouard Drouot model/cast, about 1900:** bronze on red marble, 76.8 cm high, weighing 21,950 g; Joan has dropped her distaff. Acquired by the Musée des Beaux-Arts de Reims in 1979 from the Maison de la Jeune Fille; inventory history otherwise incomplete. [Joconde record](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/joconde/03110041751).
- **Devotional folk figure, Dijon:** polychrome plaster figure with sword and distaff; formerly owned by Cécile Le Panse, donated in 1999 to the Musée d’Art Sacré, Dijon. [Joconde record](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/joconde/01390003052).
- **Processional banner associated with Saint-Dié:** depicts Joan as a kneeling shepherdess spinning at the manifestation of Michael, Catherine, and Margaret. It belongs to modern patriotic and pilgrimage culture and is not the medieval English standard. [French heritage inventory](https://patrimoine.auvergnerhonealpes.fr/dossier/IM63003623).

These objects transmit the motif, not Joan’s equipment.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Is there an authentic relic?

**Finding: no authenticated claimant was located.** No museum catalogue examined identifies a distaff or spindle as Joan’s personal property. No object has a published radiocarbon date, dendrochronology, wood identification, residue analysis, fibre analysis, or documented provenance leading to Joan.

Consequently, there is presently nothing to which scientific authentication could meaningfully be applied. A medieval date alone would not establish personal ownership; thousands of similar implements existed.

### 4.2 The nineteenth-century “relic” conditional

Eugène de Certain’s 1859 article on Joan’s banner offers unusually clear evidence that no famous distaff relic was then known. He wrote conditionally that one of her weapons, a fragment of her armour, or “even the distaff she carried to the fields” **would be** among the holiest French national relics. The grammatical and rhetorical form describes a wished-for discovery, not an extant object. [De Certain, 1859](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bec_0373-6237_1859_num_20_1_445642).

This passage may have encouraged later readers to imagine a specific implement, but it supplies no provenance.

### 4.3 Biographical core versus shepherdess legend

The iconography has a factual core: Joan spun and sometimes watched animals while spinning. The stronger stock image—Joan as a full-time shepherdess hearing her first revelation while holding or dropping a distaff—is not directly stated in her 1431 testimony.

Her own account placed the first voice around age thirteen in her father’s garden, around noon in summer, near the church. Later pastoral art merged:

- domestic spinning;
- occasional herd duty;
- rural social identity;
- the garden revelation;
- the conventional holy shepherdess.

The resulting picture is historically plausible as a genre scene but not documentary reportage.

### 4.4 Confusion with Saint Geneviève

The distaff is also an attribute of Saint Geneviève. A modern Geneviève iconography project observes that medieval and early-modern imagery increasingly represented Geneviève as a shepherdess with sheep and distaff, and that comparisons between the two French female protectors encouraged overlap. [Geneviève iconography](https://www.sainte-genevieve.net/iconographie/).

This makes attribution by the distaff alone unsafe. Joan is more reliably distinguished by armour, sword, banner, voices, or a transition from rural dress to military equipment.

### 4.5 The English standard’s historicity

The 1429 mock banner is supported by one particularly detailed chronicle tradition, then repeated by nineteenth-century historians. Its description is internally coherent and fits contemporary gendered polemic, but its present evidentiary weaknesses are substantial:

- the captain is unnamed;
- no English financial account for its manufacture has been identified;
- no second independent visual witness survives;
- no physical fragment is known;
- the Lille chronicle’s manuscript date and relation to the event require specialist re-examination.

Its existence should therefore be described as **near-contemporary chronicle testimony, plausible but not physically corroborated**, rather than as an extant historical artifact.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

- **Joan of Arc / Jeanne la Pucelle (c. 1412–1431):** documented spinner and sewer; the only person securely associated with the activity underlying the attribute. She does not identify an individual tool. D1, 1431.
- **Isabelle Romée:** Joan’s mother. Later biographies often say that Isabelle taught Joan to spin, a normal household inference, but the examined primary testimony does not individuate that act or a particular instrument. U/T.
- **Jacques d’Arc:** Joan’s father. Paired with Joan in the *Vigiles* woodcut—he at the plough, she at the distaff—creating a compact picture of the household division of labour. D3 image, not eyewitness portrait.
- **Mengette, wife of Jean Joyart:** childhood neighbour who testified that she repeatedly spun with Joan. D2, 1456.
- **Durand Laxart:** relative and host who testified that Joan spun while staying in his household. D2, 1456.
- **Catherine Le Royer:** Vaucouleurs hostess who remembered spinning together with Joan and praised her skill. D2, 1456.
- **Pierre Cauchon and the Rouen tribunal:** their questioning elicited Joan’s earliest surviving statement about her skill. The court did not seize or describe spinning tools. D1, 1431.
- **Unnamed English captain:** alleged commissioner and first custodian of the anti-Joan standard. D3, reported for July 1429.
- **John, Duke of Bedford, and Cardinal Henry Beaufort:** commanders/patrons associated by later historical reconstruction with the English reinforcement in which the mock standard appeared; neither is documented as its maker or bearer.
- **Martial d’Auvergne (c. 1430–1508):** author of the *Vigiles*, whose printed illustration helped establish the domestic-spinner image.
- **Jean Du Pré:** Paris printer of the 1493 *Vigiles*, central to the image’s mechanical reproduction.
- **Charles VIII:** recipient of the royal *Vigiles* manuscript; custodian of the textual and pictorial cycle, although not specifically of a surviving spinning woodcut.
- **Saint Michael, Saint Catherine of Alexandria, and Saint Margaret of Antioch:** divine/heavenly figures Joan said communicated with her. Later artists placed them above or around the distaff, turning the interrupted household task into the threshold of a divine mission. The distaff itself has no attributed miraculous power.
- **Jules Bastien-Lepage, Léon-François Bénouville, Albert-Lefeuvre, Édouard Drouot, and Luca Madrassi:** modern artists who materially renewed the attribute in painting or sculpture.
- **French museums, churches, municipal collections, publishers, and pilgrimage associations:** the institutions that converted a biographical implement into national, devotional, and commercial imagery.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Historical function

The historical implements’ function was ordinary textile production: holding prepared fibres, twisting them into thread, and winding the thread. The sources attribute no blessing, healing, divination, protection, curse, or military power to them.

There is no documented ritual in which Joan’s distaff or spindle was:

- processed;
- sworn upon;
- touched for healing;
- placed on an altar;
- enclosed in a reliquary;
- used in her beatification or canonization;
- carried in the Orléans commemorations as an authenticated possession.

### 6.2 Gendered polemic

In medieval and early-modern symbolic language, the distaff marked female domestic labour. The English standard weaponized that association: its message was that Joan should abandon arms and return to spinning. The several empty spindles and the half-filled one intensified the joke by promising her abundant unfinished work.

This symbolism intersects directly with the trial’s concern over Joan’s male clothing. The distaff represented the social role her opponents believed she had transgressed; later French imagery reversed the insult by making the abandoned distaff the prelude to patriotic action.

### 6.3 Domestic virtue

After rehabilitation and especially during the nineteenth-century Joan revival, the pair signified:

- diligence;
- filial obedience before the mission;
- rural simplicity;
- feminine competence;
- chastity and modesty;
- willingness to leave private duty for public sacrifice.

A 1905 women’s magazine explicitly called Joan’s distaff an enduring symbol of the discreet activity of the young woman and adapted it to contemporary ideals of female work. [*La Mode illustrée*, 1905](https://img01.decalog.net/decalog_images/numerisation_periodiques/LMI%20-%20la%20Mode%20Illustree/PDF/M5053JP_LMI_1905_037/PDF/M5053JP_LMI_1905_037.pdf).

### 6.4 Interrupted labour as visual syntax

Modern artists developed a recurring sequence:

1. Joan spins.
2. The heavenly voices appear.
3. Her hand stops or releases the spindle.
4. Sword, banner, armour, or burning city anticipates the mission.

The dropped implement is therefore a narrative hinge: domestic time ceases and sacred-national history begins. This symbolism is artistic interpretation, not a miracle recorded in the trials.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 “Joan was a shepherdess with her distaff when the voices came”

**Classification:** later medieval elaboration consolidated by modern art.

The fullest familiar version says that the adolescent Joan was spinning while tending her father’s sheep when Michael, Catherine, and Margaret appeared; startled, she dropped her spindle or abandoned the distaff, then exchanged it for a sword.

Evidence for a factual core:

- Joan did spin.
- Mengette said she sometimes guarded livestock while spinning.
- Joan reported hearing a first voice in a rural setting near home.

Evidence against treating the composite scene as an observed event:

- Joan located the first voice in or near her father’s garden, not explicitly in a sheep pasture.
- Neither she nor the early witnesses say that she held or dropped a distaff at that instant.
- Witnesses describe only occasional turns guarding the communal herd.
- Later images often converted a comparatively prosperous peasant household into a generalized pastoral idyll.

### 7.2 “She exchanged the distaff for the sword”

**Classification:** symbolic formula, not a documented exchange.

Nineteenth-century prose frequently speaks of Joan exchanging *la quenouille contre la lance* or sword. No literal transaction is recorded. The phrase condenses a change in role: domestic worker to armed royal servant.

### 7.3 “The distaff she carried to the fields was a national relic”

**Classification:** counterfactual antiquarian wish, sometimes liable to misreading.

De Certain’s 1859 sentence says such an implement, **if recovered**, would rank with France’s most precious national relics. It does not report its discovery or veneration.

### 7.4 “The mock standard was Joan’s banner”

**Classification:** modern confusion.

The two standards differ categorically:

- Joan’s banner: religious and royal imagery, carried by her party.
- English mock standard: distaff and spindle, carried by an enemy unit to insult her.

Treating the latter as “Joan’s distaff banner” erases its polemical ownership.

### 7.5 “Distaff and spindle are canonical saintly attributes”

**Classification:** partially established modern convention.

Some devotional lists give Joan armour, sword, cross-shaped sword, banner, pyre, distaff, and spindle. Yet systematic iconographic indexing more commonly prioritizes armour, banner, lance, and sword. Distaff and spindle identify one episode or aspect—Joan before the mission—rather than functioning as universally sufficient attributes. [Modern devotional attribute list](https://www.sancteo.org/fr/saints/1768-sainte-jeanne-d-arc-pucelle-orleans); [Iconclass-style index](https://www.arkyves.org/HIM/FOLGER?language=en&notation=11HH%28...%29).

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 The alleged original

**Status:** unlocated and not demonstrated ever to have been preserved as a discrete relic.

**Location:** unknown; more precisely, no qualifying claimant has been found.

**Condition, owner, custodian, accession number, legal status, and access:** not applicable or unknown.

**Scientific study:** none identified.

**Restitution or ownership dispute:** none identified.

The safest present statement is not “destroyed,” because destruction is undocumented, but **“ordinary historical implements once existed; no specific specimen can now be traced.”**

### 8.2 The English standard

**Status:** lost after its reported appearance in 1429.

**Location:** unknown.

**Survivals:** only textual description; no accepted fragment, drawing made from life, or early copy is known.

### 8.3 Principal surviving substitutes

The physical objects now carrying the motif are works of art:

- BnF copies of the *Vigiles* and its printed descendants;
- Bastien-Lepage’s 1879 painting at the Metropolitan Museum of Art;
- the Sens sculpture;
- the Reims Drouot bronze;
- the Dijon devotional figure;
- processional and commemorative banners;
- countless prints, statuettes, postcards, book illustrations, and church furnishings.

None claims an evidential chain back to Joan’s household equipment.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Nineteenth-century national revival

After the Revolution and especially after the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–1871 and the loss of Alsace-Lorraine, Joan became a powerful national symbol. Artists from Lorraine had particular reason to emphasize her local rural origin.

**Léon-François Bénouville, *Jeanne d’Arc écoutant ses voix* (1859):** Joan holds a distaff while the spindle lies at her feet; heavenly figures offer the martial future. The painting makes the dropped spindle a direct sign of interrupted domestic vocation.

**Albert-Lefeuvre, *Jeanne d’Arc* (shown at the 1878 Exposition universelle):** contemporary criticism described a young rustic Joan standing in a short skirt, spinning at her distaff and listening upward. [Contemporary exposition account](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/65/Getty_Research_Institute_%28IA_lartetlindustrie00expo%29.pdf).

**Jules Bastien-Lepage, 1879:** the garden is rendered with naturalistic specificity while translucent saints enter the scene. The work was exhibited at the Salon of 1880 and in New York in 1881; Erwin Davis gave it to the Met in 1889. The painting’s fame helped establish the rural revelation scene internationally.

### 9.2 Sculpture and serial reproduction

Édouard Drouot’s approximately 1900 figure shows the distaff fallen at Joan’s feet. The composition was cast in multiples; the Reims example is expressly described as a cast after an original. Its seriality demonstrates that a “Joan distaff” in sculpture is part of a reproducible model rather than an attempted medieval relic.

Luca Madrassi’s late-nineteenth-century biscuit figure similarly shows Joan seated as a shepherdess with a distaff beside her. Surviving examples circulate in the art market, sometimes described in devotional language but without medieval provenance. [Market record](https://www.anticstore.art/60281P).

### 9.3 Popular print and children’s culture

Épinal imagery converted Joan’s biography into inexpensive coloured prints and paper-doll sets. A municipal collection records a sheet in which a shepherdess costume and distaff form one stage of her “marvellous existence,” followed by soldier, armour, horse, and banner. [Musée de l’Image record](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/joconde/M0537072641).

The sequence taught a simplified biography through objects: distaff equals Domrémy; sword and banner equal mission; chains or prison equal captivity; pyre equals martyrdom.

### 9.4 Religious use

Following Joan’s beatification in 1909 and canonization in 1920, the rural-spinner image entered church sculpture, stained glass, banners, holy cards, and parish decoration. It was particularly suited to youth organizations and women’s devotional associations because it joined domestic work to vocation.

The object did not acquire a formal liturgical use. It remained representational.

### 9.5 Political use

The distaff allowed competing political readings:

- conservative Catholic imagery presented orderly domestic femininity interrupted only by divine command;
- republican-national imagery treated the abandoned domestic tool as the emergence of popular patriotism;
- feminist interpretation could emphasize Joan’s movement beyond prescribed gendered labour;
- hostile satire could reactivate the original English implication that a woman bearing arms belonged back at the distaff.

The same symbol could therefore defend conventional gender hierarchy or dramatize its suspension.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Documentary foundations

The dossier rests principally on:

- the 1431 condemnation trial;
- the 1455–1456 nullification testimony;
- the Lille chronicle passage about the English standard;
- the *Mystère du siège d’Orléans*;
- Martial d’Auvergne’s *Vigiles* and its visual transmission.

Jules Quicherat’s five-volume nineteenth-century edition first made the trial corpora broadly available in full. Pierre Duparc’s twentieth-century edition of the nullification process improved the documentary basis and manuscript comparison.

### 10.2 Major debates

**Was Joan a shepherdess?** Modern scholarship generally distinguishes occasional herd duty from the professional or archetypal shepherdess of art. Mengette’s testimony supports spinning while watching animals, but it also says this happened when the household’s turn came.

**Does the spinning image preserve memory or create legend?** It does both. Spinning is historically attested; the exact composition is created. The distinction is essential.

**Which is the earliest image?** The 1429 mock standard is the earliest described distaff-and-spindle image associated with Joan, if the Lille chronicle report is accepted. The earliest surviving visual artifact located for the positive domestic motif is the early printed *Vigiles* woodcut. The 1484 royal manuscript must not automatically be cited as the carrier of that particular woodcut.

**Did Joan carry a distaff into the fields?** Mengette’s testimony makes this plausible and perhaps probable, but it does not identify one unique implement. De Certain’s 1859 phrase reflects that testimony, not sight of a relic.

**Are distaff and spindle formal attributes of Saint Joan?** They appear regularly enough to constitute secondary attributes, especially in childhood and vocation scenes. Armour, sword, and banner remain more stable identifiers.

### 10.3 State of the evidence

The textual evidence is rich for Joan’s activity but empty for the tools’ individuality. This asymmetry explains why popular iconography can be vivid while material provenance is nonexistent.

No known document supplies:

- a commission or purchase;
- an inventory entry;
- a donor or inheritor;
- a church authentication;
- a theft or recovery;
- an antiquarian discovery;
- a scientific examination;
- a museum accession of an alleged original.

### 10.4 Research still needed

A definitive specialist study should:

1. establish the manuscript date, compiler, and textual affiliations of the Lille chronicle passage;
2. search English Exchequer, military, and wardrobe accounts for the alleged 1429 unit and standard;
3. identify every early edition of the *Vigiles* containing the spinning woodcut and compare recut blocks;
4. survey nineteenth-century Joan relic catalogues, diocesan archives, and auction records for otherwise forgotten claimants;
5. catalogue the motif’s transfer between Joan and Saint Geneviève;
6. separate works showing hand spindle and distaff from those showing a spinning wheel, which belong to different technological and symbolic traditions.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and near-primary texts

- The condemnation trial record, especially Joan’s examination of 22 February 1431.
- The 1455–1456 nullification depositions of Mengette, Durand Laxart, Catherine Le Royer, and other Domrémy/Vaucouleurs witnesses.
- The chronicle conventionally called the *Chronique de Lille*, as published in the *Bulletin de la Société de l’histoire de France*.
- The *Mystère du siège d’Orléans*.
- Martial d’Auvergne, *Les Vigiles de la mort de Charles VII*, manuscript and early printed traditions.

### Critical editions and documentary scholarship

- Jules Quicherat, ed., *Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc*, 5 vols., Paris, 1841–1849.
- Pierre Duparc, ed., *Procès en nullité de la condamnation de Jeanne d’Arc*, Paris, 1977–1988.
- Eugène de Certain, “Note sur l’étendard de Jeanne d’Arc,” *Bibliothèque de l’École des chartes* 20 (1859), 355–368.
- Auguste Vallet de Viriville, *Histoire de Charles VII*.
- BnF, Biblissima, JONAS/IRHT, POP/Joconde, and Metropolitan Museum collection records.

### Assessment of source limitations

The 1431 and 1456 proceedings prove the activity of spinning but do not describe a particular object. The Lille chronicle supplies the fullest material description, but of an enemy standard rather than Joan’s tools. The *Vigiles* and later artworks document the iconographic object’s development, not the physical survival of a medieval relic. Museum records substantiate individual modern artworks and their custody only.
