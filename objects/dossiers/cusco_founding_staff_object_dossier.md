# The golden staff that founded Cusco: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conclusion

The “golden staff that founded Cusco” is not a securely documented surviving artifact. No museum, church, government treasury, excavation report, colonial inventory, or scientific publication identifies a physical object with a continuous provenance from Manco Cápac to the present. Its recoverable history is therefore principally a history of narratives, images, landscapes, and later representations.

Nor do the colonial sources describe one perfectly stable object. At least three related but not demonstrably identical traditions became intertwined:

1. **Golden weapons or halberds** carried from the Pacaritambo cave by the Ayar founders, recorded by Juan de Betanzos around 1551.
2. **One or two gold testing-stakes** thrown or driven into soil to locate fertile land near Cusco, recorded most explicitly by Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa in 1572.
3. **A single slender bar of gold**, given by the Sun to Manco Cápac and Mama Ocllo and swallowed completely by the earth at Huanacauri, in Inca Garcilaso de la Vega’s *Comentarios reales* of 1609.

A fourth account, written by Joan de Santa Cruz Pachacuti Yamqui in the early seventeenth century, makes the **Tupayauri** a staff left by the culture hero Tunapa and subsequently taken up by Manco Cápac. That is a royal-insignia genealogy, not transparently the same narrative as Garcilaso’s irrecoverably buried bar.

The safest identification is consequently:

> **A colonial-period textual complex concerning a gold foundation implement associated with Manco Cápac, Mama Huaco/Mama Ocllo, Huanacauri, Huanaypata, and the establishment of Inca possession in the Cusco valley—not a presently identifiable archaeological artifact.**

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Object class

Depending on the source, the object is:

- a **testing rod or stake**, used to probe the agricultural quality of soil;
- a **foundation implement**, whose penetration into the ground discloses the divinely chosen settlement;
- a **royal staff or sceptre**, expressing Inca authority;
- a **golden halberd or pole weapon**, one of several insignia carried by the Ayar founders;
- or, in later retellings, a **magic wand** whose disappearance marks the exact foundation of Cusco.

These classifications should not be collapsed. “Golden staff” is a convenient modern umbrella term, not the invariant name of a single object in all the primary accounts.

### 1.2 Names and terminology

#### Spanish forms

The principal colonial descriptions include:

- *alabardas de oro* — “halberds of gold” (Betanzos);
- *varas de oro* — “rods/wands of gold” (Sarmiento’s two implements);
- *estaca de oro* — “stake of gold” (Sarmiento’s alternative version);
- *barra de oro* — “bar of gold” (Garcilaso);
- *barrilla* or *barreta de oro* — later editorial and popular variants;
- *palo* — “staff/stick,” in Santa Cruz Pachacuti’s account of the Tupayauri;
- *cetro* — “sceptre,” common in later explanations and iconography.

English renderings include *golden staff*, *golden rod*, *golden wand*, *golden bar*, *golden cane*, and *golden stake*. These translations can obscure the differences among the Spanish nouns.

#### Quechua-associated forms

The related royal staff is variously written:

- **Tupayauri**
- **Topayauri**
- **Tupa Yauri**
- **Tupaq yawri / T’upaq yawri** in modernized spelling

Colonial orthography is unstable. *Tupaq* can carry senses including “royal,” “noble,” or “splendid.” Colonial dictionaries and narratives use *yauri* for a staff, sceptre, needle-like implement, or metal point, depending on context. “Royal sceptre” is therefore a reasonable functional gloss, but proposed exact etymologies vary. It cannot be established that **Tupayauri was the proper name of Garcilaso’s disappearing bar**; that identification is a later harmonization of separate sources.

Other royal pole insignia, especially the **suntur páucar**, also appear in chronicles and modern imagery. They must not automatically be identified with the foundation rod.

### 1.3 Descriptions by source

#### Juan de Betanzos, *Suma y narración de los Incas*, composed ca. 1551

Betanzos says that four men and their wives emerged from the cave at Pacaritambo. The men brought out “*unas alabardas de oro*”—golden halberds—while wearing fine wool garments woven with gold. The women carried gold dress pins and gold domestic vessels. No dimensions or weights are supplied. The plural matters: Betanzos describes a set of founding insignia, not clearly the unique rod of Garcilaso’s story. [Betanzos, digitized text](https://www.biblioteca-antologica.org/es/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/BETANZOS-Suma-y-narraci%C3%B3n-de-los-Incas.pdf)

#### Pedro Cieza de León, *El señorío de los incas*, written ca. 1550–1553

Cieza’s Ayar account mentions rich garments, abundant gold service, and one founder carrying a **golden sling with a stone in it**. In the surviving passage, he does not give Garcilaso’s episode of a solar bar disappearing at Huanacauri. This omission is significant: gold insignia belong to the early narrative complex, but the familiar single-rod episode was not universal. [Cieza, Spanish edition](https://www.biblioteca-antologica.org/es/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/CIEZA-DE-LE%C3%93N-Cr%C3%B3nica-del-Per%C3%BA-II.pdf)

#### Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa, *Historia de los Incas*, 1572

Sarmiento gives two versions:

- Mama Huaco takes **two rods of gold** and throws them northward. One falls at Colcapampa but does not penetrate properly; the other reaches the area of Huanaypata and fixes securely, revealing fertile ground.
- “Others say” Manco Cápac himself performed the test with **the gold stake he carried**, which sank into the soil at Huanaypata.

The implement functions as a soil probe. Sarmiento does not say that this stake vanished forever. His text explicitly preserves disagreement over the actor, number of implements, and manner of use. [Sarmiento, English edition, chapter 13](https://web.seducoahuila.gob.mx/biblioweb/upload/History-of-the-Incas.pdf)

#### Inca Garcilaso de la Vega, *Comentarios reales de los Incas*, 1609

Garcilaso’s Sun gives his son and daughter a **barra de oro**. They are to test the ground wherever they stop. At Huanacauri, south of Cusco, it sinks “with much ease” at the first blow and is never seen again:

> “Allí procuró hincar en tierra la barra de oro, la cual con mucha facilidad se les hundió al primer golpe que dieron con ella, que no la vieron más.”

Garcilaso supplies no dimensions, weight, cross-section, ornament, inscription, alloy, maker’s mark, or fabrication technique. He calls it gold, but nothing permits metallurgical confirmation. [Official Cusco museum edition](https://museogarcilaso.culturacusco.gob.pe/mediaelement/pdf/3-ComentariosReales.pdf)

#### Joan de Santa Cruz Pachacuti Yamqui, *Relación de antigüedades deste reino del Pirú*, early seventeenth century

Santa Cruz Pachacuti relates that Manco Cápac takes a staff left by **Tunapa**, called *Tupayauri*, together with two small gold cups or *aquillas*. This narrative furnishes the staff with a history before Manco and connects it to inherited sacred authority. It does not clearly reproduce Garcilaso’s solar gift or state that the staff disappears into the soil. [Peruvian Ministry of Education repository record and edition](https://repositorio.minedu.gob.pe/handle/20.500.12799/8421)

#### Felipe Guamán Poma de Ayala, *Primer nueva corónica y buen gobierno*, ca. 1615

Guamán Poma depicts Manco Cápac with royal regalia, holding a special staff in one hand and a weapon in the other. The drawing documents early-seventeenth-century visualization of Manco as bearer of pole insignia; it does not prove that the pictured implement is the vanished foundation bar. [Royal Danish Library manuscript portal](https://www.kb.dk/en/find-materials/collections/manuscript-collection/chronicle-guaman-poma) and [image discussion](https://smarthistory.org/guaman-chronicle/)

### 1.4 Reconstructable physical properties

Only the following can be responsibly extracted:

| Property | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Material | Described as gold in all relevant rod/stake variants; untested |
| Number | One in Garcilaso and Sarmiento’s alternative report; two in Sarmiento’s Mama Huaco version; several golden halberds in Betanzos |
| Size | Unrecorded |
| Weight | Unrecorded |
| Shape | Bar, rod, stake, staff, or halberd, depending on source |
| Construction | Unrecorded |
| Decoration | Unrecorded for the land-testing object |
| Inscription | None reported |
| Power | Reveals suitable/fertile land by penetrating it |
| Final condition | Garcilaso: wholly swallowed and invisible; Sarmiento: embedded, with no later fate stated |
| Surviving fragments | None documented |

Modern pictures frequently add a knob, spearhead, sunburst, ornamental head, great length, or European-looking sceptre. These details derive from later artistic convention or the iconography of Inca royal insignia, not from Garcilaso’s physical description.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 No documented manufacture

There is no contract, workshop record, mine attribution, metal analysis, excavated context, or pre-Hispanic inventory for the foundation staff. No source names a human goldsmith. Nothing establishes whether “gold” would mean native gold, a gold-silver alloy, hammered sheet over a core, or solid metal.

Andean archaeology confirms sophisticated pre-Hispanic goldworking, but that general fact cannot authenticate this particular object.

### 2.2 Earliest securely datable textual evidence

The earliest known colonial text in the relevant object-family is Betanzos’s *Suma y narración*, composed around 1551. It attests **golden halberds carried from Pacaritambo**, but it does not unambiguously attest Garcilaso’s unique, soil-testing bar.

Cieza’s roughly contemporary narrative attests a golden sling and other gold property but likewise not the canonical disappearing rod.

The earliest presently located explicit version of a **gold implement used to test the future settlement’s soil** is Sarmiento’s *Historia de los Incas* of 1572. His account is especially important because he records alternatives within the tradition instead of presenting a single uniform story.

The familiar Sun–Titicaca–single-bar–Huanacauri form is first fully and unmistakably attested in Garcilaso’s *Comentarios reales*, printed at Lisbon in 1609. Garcilaso says the story was told to him during his youth by an elderly Inca relative. That statement establishes his claimed oral source; it does not independently date the story to the pre-Hispanic period.

### 2.3 Traditional creation accounts

#### Garcilaso’s solar gift

The Sun supplies the bar directly to his two children, Manco Cápac and Mama Ocllo. No forging occurs in the narrative. Its material and authority both come from the solar father.

#### Pacaritambo emergence tradition

In Betanzos, the founders bring gold weapons from inside the cave. The cave is a *paqarina*, an origin place, so the implements enter human geography already complete. Their maker remains unnamed.

#### Tunapa–Tupayauri genealogy

Santa Cruz Pachacuti’s staff is a relic of Tunapa. Manco retrieves or assumes it along with other insignia. This makes authority transmissible through possession: the founder does not merely receive a tool but inherits an older sacred mandate.

### 2.4 Archaeological context and limits

Archaeology supports neither a literal creation nor the survival of the rod. It does establish that:

- the Cusco valley had a long pre-Inca occupation;
- state formation there was gradual;
- regional consolidation occurred during approximately CE 1000–1400;
- rapid imperial expansion followed after about 1400.

Bauer and Covey’s archaeological model therefore contradicts any literal reading in which an empty valley suddenly became a city at one miraculous moment. It does not disprove the possibility that the tradition preserves memories of migration, conquest, agricultural testing, or claims to land. [Bauer and Covey, “Processes of State Formation in the Inca Heartland”](https://anthrosource.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1525/aa.2002.104.3.846)

UNESCO likewise describes Cusco as having more than three millennia of pre-Inca occupation and emphasizes its major fifteenth-century remodelling under Pachacútec. [UNESCO, City of Cuzco](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/273/)

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because no surviving physical staff can be identified, this section separates **custody inside each story** from **custody of the story and its documentary witnesses**.

### 3.1 Garcilaso version: narrative chain

1. **The Sun** possesses or produces the gold bar.
2. **The Sun gives it jointly to Manco Cápac and Mama Ocllo** at or in connection with Lake Titicaca.
3. The pair carry it northward.
4. At every halt they attempt to drive it into the ground.
5. They pass through Pacárec Tampu.
6. At **Huanacauri**, Manco drives it into the earth.
7. It sinks completely and is never seen again.
8. The bar has no later keeper, owner, shrine, inventory, or recovery in Garcilaso’s narrative.

The only asserted transfer is divine gift. Its final disposition is immediate, complete burial or supernatural absorption.

### 3.2 Sarmiento version A: Mama Huaco’s two rods

1. Mama Huaco possesses two gold rods.
2. From Matahua she throws them toward the north.
3. One lands at Colcapampa but fails the test.
4. The second fixes firmly in Huanaypata near Cusco.
5. Its successful penetration identifies fertile land.
6. Sarmiento gives no subsequent custody or recovery.

This is selection by trial rather than disappearance by miracle.

### 3.3 Sarmiento version B: Manco Cápac’s stake

1. Manco carries a gold stake.
2. He tests soils with it.
3. It sinks at Huanaypata.
4. The party recognizes the land’s fertility.
5. Its subsequent fate is unstated.

### 3.4 Betanzos version: golden halberds

1. The halberds are inside Pacaritambo’s cave.
2. The four male founders bring them out.
3. They carry them during the migration toward Huanacauri and Cusco.
4. Betanzos does not single out one as the soil-testing object.
5. No continuous later custody is supplied.

Betanzos also says other gold goods remained in the cave and that Ayar Cachi was induced to return for them before being sealed inside. That episode demonstrates the narrative importance of retained cave treasure, but it supplies no history of the later “golden staff.”

### 3.5 Santa Cruz Pachacuti version: sacred succession

1. **Tunapa** possesses or leaves the staff.
2. The staff remains associated with a place of origin or sacred deposit.
3. **Manco Cápac takes the palo called Tupayauri**, along with gold drinking vessels.
4. It becomes part of his authoritative regalia.
5. In later passages and interpretations, the name is associated with Inca royal succession.

This is the only major version with something resembling a multigenerational pedigree. Even here, continuity between the original staff and later royal sceptres is traditional rather than physically documented.

### 3.6 The conquest does not supply a missing final chapter

Spanish accounts document the seizure, division, concealment, and melting of enormous quantities of Inca gold after 1532. Betanzos describes gold taken from Cusco and sacred places; Garcilaso discusses gold objects hidden from Spaniards. But no reliable conquest-period source identifies the founding staff among Atahualpa’s ransom, Cusco temple property, Pizarro’s allotment, or confiscated sacred objects.

Therefore it is unsound to claim that:

- Spaniards captured it;
- it was melted into bullion;
- priests hid it;
- it remains beneath the Qorikancha;
- or treasure hunters nearly recovered it.

Those are possibilities or folklore, not established custody links.

### 3.7 Documentary custody

- Betanzos’s work circulated incompletely and remained unpublished for centuries; the full manuscript tradition was reconstructed only in modern scholarship.
- Sarmiento prepared his history in 1572 in the context of Viceroy Francisco de Toledo’s program to establish a Spanish legal-historical case against independent Inca sovereignty.
- Garcilaso published the first part of the *Comentarios reales* at Lisbon in 1609.
- Santa Cruz Pachacuti’s *Relación* survives as an early-colonial manuscript and first appeared in print in the nineteenth century; a modern critical edition is available through Peru’s Ministry of Education.
- Guamán Poma’s illustrated manuscript, completed around 1615, is held by the Royal Danish Library and is digitally accessible.

This is the demonstrable modern chain: not custody of one gold artifact, but custody and reproduction of the witnesses through which it is known.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Did the object physically exist?

**Unresolved, with no affirmative physical evidence.** A real gold staff could have underlain one or more traditions, but no excavated object has been connected to the narrative by provenance, inscription, scientific analysis, or continuous custody.

### 4.2 Are all the recorded objects the same?

Probably not as narrated:

- Betanzos has multiple halberds.
- Cieza has a golden sling.
- Sarmiento has two thrown rods in one version and one carried stake in another.
- Garcilaso has one solar bar that disappears forever.
- Santa Cruz Pachacuti has Tunapa’s named staff.

Scholars may compare these as transformations of an insignia or foundation motif, but no source permits a material identity equation.

### 4.3 Is the foundation bar the Tupayauri?

This is frequently asserted in popular summaries. The evidence is insufficient. Garcilaso never calls his bar *Tupayauri* in the foundation passage. Santa Cruz Pachacuti’s named staff has a different origin and does not undergo Garcilaso’s final disappearance. Betanzos’s golden halberds may be typologically related to royal pole insignia, but that does not make any one of them Garcilaso’s bar.

The correct classification is **plausible literary/iconographic association, not demonstrated object identity**.

### 4.4 Candidate physical objects

No credible museum candidate was found. Existing Inca or Inca-period staffs, mace heads, axes, gold miniatures, wooden funerary markers, and colonial sceptres lack the required provenance. For example, the Metropolitan Museum’s Inca funerary staff is a wooden funerary marker, not a claimant to the foundation rod. [Metropolitan Museum catalogue](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/313053)

### 4.5 Scientific examination

No radiocarbon dating, metallography, elemental analysis, lead-isotope study, microscopy, or provenance examination has been performed on “the” foundation staff because no physical claimant has been authenticated or even securely proposed.

Scientific claims about its purity, mine source, dimensions, or age are therefore unsupported.

### 4.6 Authority to authenticate

In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, credibility rested with:

- Inca nobles and other Andean informants;
- interpreters such as Betanzos;
- Spanish chroniclers;
- indigenous and mestizo authors;
- colonial officials who solicited or certified testimony.

Modern authority rests with textual criticism, archaeology, anthropology, linguistics, museum provenance standards, and materials science. None has authenticated a surviving staff.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Selection of fertile ground

Sarmiento explicitly rationalizes the action as testing land. Penetration indicates usable soil; failure indicates an unsuitable location. The motif ties political foundation to agricultural productivity.

This is not merely a modern agricultural interpretation: Sarmiento’s own text says the founders learned from the test that Huanaypata was fertile.

### 6.2 Divine election of place

Garcilaso transforms or presents the test as an oracle. The Sun has determined the sign in advance, and the bar’s effortless disappearance discloses divine will. Cusco is therefore not selected solely by human calculation; its foundation is cosmologically mandated.

### 6.3 Taking possession

In the broader Ayar cycle, poles, projectiles, the planting of objects, and transformation into stones mark possession of territory. The staff belongs to a cluster of acts through which landscape becomes ancestry and political title.

### 6.4 Legitimation of sovereignty

Possession of the implement signifies:

- authorization from the Sun or an older sacred being;
- the bearer’s right to select the capital;
- mastery of productive land;
- continuity between founding hero and later Inca rulers.

Santa Cruz Pachacuti’s Tupayauri is especially explicit as transmissible authority. Later royal images turn the staff into an attribute of office.

### 6.5 Huanacauri and ritual landscape

Garcilaso locates the decisive event at Huanacauri and says a temple commemorated the founders’ passage and benefaction. Other chroniclers describe Huanacauri as a major huaca associated with Ayar Uchu, initiation, sacrifice, and Inca identity. The staff itself is not documented as an exposed cult relic there; the **place**, rather than a recoverable implement, became the enduring sacred object.

### 6.6 No documented recurring ceremony with the original bar

There is no attested annual procession, coronation, libation, or initiation in which Garcilaso’s buried bar was retrieved or displayed. Later royal sceptres may have participated in ceremonies, but their use cannot be assigned to the vanished bar without conflating objects.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The canonical Garcilasan legend

**Tier: early colonial attestation; most influential later version.**

The Sun, seeing humanity without proper religion, law, settlement, agriculture, clothing, or social order, sends his son Manco Cápac and daughter Mama Ocllo from Lake Titicaca. He gives them a gold bar and orders them to establish their court where it sinks with one thrust. They travel north, testing it repeatedly. It does not enter the ground until Huanacauri, where it vanishes completely. The pair recognize the sign, enter the valley, gather inhabitants, divide their civilizing work by gender, and establish Cusco.

The story presents Inca rule as paternal, solar, rational, and civilizing. Garcilaso was writing for an early-modern European and colonial readership and organized the account in terms intelligible to Christian-humanist political thought. That context does not prove fabrication, but it affects how the narrative must be read.

### 7.2 Mama Huaco’s thrown rods

**Tier: early colonial variant, attested 1572.**

Mama Huaco, not Manco, is the active diviner. She hurls two golden rods. The first fails; the second finds the productive territory. This version gives a founding woman technological, ritual, and military agency. Sarmiento subsequently depicts the settlers’ seizure of land from the Huallas in violent terms, unlike Garcilaso’s largely consensual civilizing narrative.

### 7.3 Manco’s testing stake

**Tier: alternative explicitly reported in 1572.**

Sarmiento notes that other informants assign the experiment to Manco Cápac. The staff sinks at Huanaypata and proves fertility. The location, actor, and action differ from Garcilaso:

- Huanaypata rather than the summit or slopes of Huanacauri;
- soil testing rather than total disappearance;
- an already carried stake rather than necessarily a fresh solar gift.

### 7.4 Pacaritambo’s golden weapons

**Tier: earliest related written attestation, ca. 1551.**

The founders emerge carrying golden halberds. These objects help distinguish them as extraordinary and authoritative beings. Later retellings often turn one halberd into “the” golden staff, but Betanzos himself does not make that equation.

### 7.5 Tunapa’s Tupayauri

**Tier: early-seventeenth-century indigenous-Christian sacred-history variant.**

Tunapa precedes Manco and leaves a staff that becomes part of the founder’s regalia. The story establishes succession from an earlier sacred traveler. Because Tunapa was interpreted through colonial Christian categories, sometimes as an apostolic or saint-like preacher, the narrative is evidence of a complex early-colonial religious synthesis, not a transparent transcript of timeless pre-Hispanic belief.

### 7.6 Fertility, penetration, and gendered interpretations

Modern scholarship has interpreted the planting or penetration of the staff as:

- agricultural testing;
- symbolic fertilization of earth;
- political seizure of productive territory;
- an axial act connecting solar authority, ruler, and landscape.

These are scholarly interpretations, not meanings explicitly agreed upon by all chroniclers.

### 7.7 Treasure and survival stories

Popular claims that the rod remains under Huanacauri, the Qorikancha, or central Cusco are **unverified modern folklore** unless tied to a specific documented local narrative. Garcilaso’s text supports only the literary proposition that it disappeared into the earth. It gives no recoverable coordinates, depth, chamber, guardian, prophecy, or later sighting.

### 7.8 “Exact foundation spot” claims

Three principal places compete in retellings:

- **Huanacauri**, Garcilaso’s location;
- **Huanaypata/Guaynapata**, Sarmiento’s successful soil test;
- central Cusco or the **Qorikancha**, in harmonizing modern versions.

The last often arises from compressing a regional sacred landscape into one tourist-visible urban point. No excavation has found the staff at any of them.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Status

**Legendary or lost, with physical existence unverified.**

No accession number, recognized custodian, legal owner, conservation file, or authenticated fragment exists. There is no current repatriation case concerning the original staff because no possessable claimant has been established.

### 8.2 Most precise possible “location”

Under Garcilaso’s narrative, its last location is underground at **Huanacauri**, south of Cusco. Under Sarmiento, a successful rod or stake reaches **Huanaypata**, near Cusco. These are narrative terminal points, not verified archaeological findspots.

### 8.3 Real places standing in for it

- **Huanacauri** preserves the landscape identity tied to the foundation cycle.
- **Cusco** itself functions as the object’s monumental outcome.
- **Pacaritambo/Puma Orco and Maukallaqta** have been examined archaeologically in relation to the Ayar origin tradition. Brian Bauer argues that the Puma Orco outcrop may correspond to mythic Tambotoco and that nearby Maukallaqta may have contained an oracle of Manco Cápac. This is an archaeological-topographic correlation, not proof of the staff. [Bauer study record](https://www.periodicos.capes.gov.br/index.php/acervo/buscador.html?id=W2327537176&source=all&task=detalhes)
- **Museums and monuments** display images or substitute staffs, not the original object.

### 8.4 Legal and heritage framework

Cusco’s historic center is protected under Peruvian law and has been a UNESCO World Heritage property since 1983. UNESCO recognizes surviving Inca urban and architectural fabric but makes no claim for a recovered foundation staff. [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/273/)

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 School and national narrative

Garcilaso’s version became the dominant textbook story: Manco Cápac and Mama Ocllo rise from Lake Titicaca, carry a golden rod, and found Cusco where it sinks. Modern simplification frequently:

- suppresses Mama Huaco’s role;
- merges Titicaca and Pacaritambo traditions;
- calls the rod *Tupayauri* without source qualification;
- moves the event from Huanacauri to central Cusco;
- and treats “the Incas founded Cusco” as a single datable event.

These are harmonizations, not the form of any one early source.

### 9.2 Visual art

Guamán Poma’s ca. 1615 portrait is an early and enormously influential model of Manco as a ruler bearing staff and weapon. Colonial dynastic paintings subsequently combined Inca insignia with European portrait and heraldic conventions.

The eighteenth-century double-sided Cusco School painting catalogued by the Museo Larco includes a sceptre as part of Manco’s dynastic symbolism. It is not a portrait from life or an image of a surviving staff. [Museo Larco](https://www.museolarco.org/pt-br/exibi%C3%A7%C3%A3o/exposicao-permanente/exposicao-online/o-sincretismo/escudo-de-armas-e-retrato-de-manco-capac/)

### 9.3 Monument to Manco Cápac in Lima

David Lozano’s bronze monument, donated by Peru’s Japanese community and inaugurated in 1926, represents Manco holding a large staff. It emerged from centennial-era indigenism and debates about Peruvian origins. The monument materially fixed the staff as a public emblem even though it is not a replica based on archaeological measurements. [Study of Peruvian twentieth-century Incaist art](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0717-71942020000100101&script=sci_arttext)

The monument’s iconographic program has been described as showing Manco fulfilling the Sun’s command by driving the gold bar at Huanacauri. Its bronze staff is therefore a **modern representation with its own custody and conservation history**, not the legendary object itself.

### 9.4 Tourism and performance

Tourism literature routinely describes the rod as a detector of fertile soil and links it to Huanacauri, Lake Titicaca, and Cusco. These retellings sustain the story’s economic and place-making importance. Their frequent confidence about exact geography exceeds the early sources’ agreement.

Modern historical spectacles, school performances, illustrations, and reenactments employ gilded staffs as stage properties. Unless separately catalogued, these are disposable or local replicas rather than identity claimants.

### 9.5 Games and popular fantasy

Modern games and fantasy treatments often transform the staff into:

- a weapon;
- a magical artefact with continuing supernatural power;
- a treasure that can be recovered;
- or a generalized “Inca sceptre.”

Those attributes are modern inventions when not tied to the narrow powers described by the chronicles. A recent Peruvian university game-development project, for example, equips an heroic Ayar Manco with a golden staff and fantasy-style character statistics. That documents continuing reception, not ancient tradition. [Project repository](https://repositorio.unamba.edu.pe/bitstream/handle/UNAMBA/1610/T-1610.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1)

### 9.6 Esoteric interpretations

Modern occult and neo-gnostic writers interpret the staff as a spinal column, phallic or kundalini-like axis, or instrument of spiritual transformation. Such readings are modern esoteric accretions. One explicit example equates the golden rod with the spinal column traversed by an ascending “serpent of fire.” [Modern gnostic interpretation](https://ciagweb.com/elementos-gnosticos-en-la-civilizacion-inca/)

No early colonial source gives that explanation.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 The primary-source problem

No pre-conquest alphabetic Inca account survives. The narratives were written after Spanish invasion and translated through colonial categories. Their authors had different informants, purposes, and political situations:

- **Betanzos** knew Quechua and had close connections with Inca elites.
- **Cieza** questioned Inca nobles and interpreters but evaluated their accounts through Christian demonological and classical historiographic assumptions.
- **Sarmiento** worked within Toledo’s campaign to characterize Inca expansion as relatively recent and tyrannical.
- **Garcilaso**, of Inca and Spanish ancestry, wrote in Spain and defended an idealized, providential image of Inca civilization.
- **Santa Cruz Pachacuti** and **Guamán Poma** wrote as indigenous Christian intellectuals, combining Andean categories with colonial religious and political argument.

Their disagreements are evidence about the history of the tradition, not defects to be silently repaired.

### 10.2 Was Garcilaso recording an old tradition or constructing a synthesis?

Garcilaso attributes the story to an elderly Inca relative and embeds it in remembered family discourse. The broad complex of gold insignia, founder migration, soil selection, and sacred landscape is attested before 1609, especially by Betanzos and Sarmiento. Thus Garcilaso did not invent every element from nothing.

However, the exact canonical combination—solar children from Titicaca, one gold bar, repeated testing, effortless disappearance at Huanacauri, benevolent civilizing mission—is most securely documented in Garcilaso’s literary formulation. Its pre-conquest age cannot be independently demonstrated.

### 10.3 Myth as political geography

Modern ethnohistory treats Inca origin traditions as claims encoded in landscape:

- Pacaritambo establishes a place of emergence;
- Huanacauri anchors sacred transformation;
- Huanaypata marks entry into productive land;
- Cusco becomes a divinely and ancestrally authorized center;
- rival local groups are incorporated, subordinated, or displaced.

Bauer’s archaeological work is important because it compares chronicled routes and places with actual settlements and topography without treating miraculous episodes as excavation reports. [Bauer, “Pacariqtambo and the Mythical Origins of the Inca”](https://www.periodicos.capes.gov.br/index.php/acervo/buscador.html?id=W2327537176&source=all&task=detalhes)

### 10.4 Foundation versus pre-existing settlement

Betanzos explicitly describes inhabitants at Cusco before the Ayar migrants. Sarmiento names the Huallas and other groups and narrates coercive displacement. Archaeology likewise demonstrates extensive pre-Inca occupation.

Accordingly, “the staff founded Cusco” is a statement internal to a charter myth. Historically, the narrative may concern an Inca group’s arrival, assertion of sovereignty, or later ideological refoundation—not the first human settlement of the valley.

### 10.5 The “agricultural probe” theory

The idea that the rod tested soil is strong for Sarmiento because he expressly connects penetration with fertility. Applying that explanation to Garcilaso is plausible but inferential: Garcilaso makes the event primarily a divine sign.

An early twentieth-century archaeological synthesis already proposed that the golden staff was a practical implement for testing soil depth. That interpretation has remained attractive because it links the myth to migration and cultivation, but it does not prove that a literal gold probe existed. [Historic archaeological synthesis](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/96/South_American_archaeology_-_an_introduction_to_the_archaeology_of_the_South_American_continent_with_special_reference_to_the_early_history_of_Peru_%28IA_soamericanarchae00joyce%29.pdf)

### 10.6 Gold as a historical clue

Colonial sources consistently associate Inca founders and rulers with gold textiles, vessels, weapons, dress pins, seats, images, and staffs. Gold marked solar and royal status. This cultural plausibility makes a gold or gilded insignia possible, but contextual plausibility is not object-level proof.

### 10.7 Current state of research

Research can responsibly pursue:

- manuscript relationships among the chronicles;
- Quechua terminology for staff and royal insignia;
- archaeological study of Pacaritambo, Huanacauri, and early Cusco;
- colonial and republican iconography;
- the political uses of the foundation story.

It presently cannot provide:

- an accession number;
- physical measurements;
- alloy composition;
- a verified mine;
- an excavated findspot;
- or a continuous chain of custody for the staff itself.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and early textual witnesses

- Juan de Betanzos, *Suma y narración de los Incas*, composed ca. 1551. Especially the Pacaritambo emergence account and its plural “golden halberds.”
- Pedro Cieza de León, *El señorío de los incas*, composed ca. 1550–1553. Comparative Ayar account with golden sling and cave treasure.
- Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa, *Historia de los Incas*, 1572. Principal early witness for the two rods of Mama Huaco and alternative stake of Manco Cápac.
- Inca Garcilaso de la Vega, *Comentarios reales de los Incas*, Lisbon, 1609, book I. Principal witness for the single solar bar that disappears at Huanacauri.
- Joan de Santa Cruz Pachacuti Yamqui Salcamaygua, *Relación de antigüedades deste reino del Pirú*, early seventeenth century. Principal witness for Tunapa’s Tupayauri.
- Felipe Guamán Poma de Ayala, *Primer nueva corónica y buen gobierno*, ca. 1615. Visual evidence for Manco Cápac’s royal staff and weapon.

### Archaeology, heritage, and modern reception

- Brian S. Bauer, “Pacariqtambo and the Mythical Origins of the Inca,” *Latin American Antiquity* 2 (1991): 7–26.
