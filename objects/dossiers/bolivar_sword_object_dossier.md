# Simon Bolivar's sword: Object Dossier

**Proposed filename:** `simon_bolivars_sword_object_dossier.md`  
**Research cutoff:** 6 September 2026

## Scope and evidentiary note

“Simón Bolívar’s sword” is not a unique, self-identifying artifact. Bolívar owned, carried, received, and gave away several swords. At least four substantially documented objects—and numerous replicas—have been called *la espada de Bolívar*:

1. **The Quinta de Bolívar sword**, Bogotá, collection number **05-475**: the comparatively plain sword inventoried in 1924, stolen by the M-19 guerrilla organization in 1974, returned in 1991, displayed at Gustavo Petro’s inauguration in 2022, kept at the Casa de Nariño during his presidency, and returned to the Quinta on 24 July 2026. This is the object principally reconstructed here.
2. **The Sword of Peru**, Caracas: the gold-and-gem presentation sword commissioned by the municipality of Lima in 1825, inscribed with Bolívar’s name and the name of its maker, Chungapoma. It has the strongest early documentary provenance of any object popularly called Bolívar’s sword.
3. **The 1822 campaign sword given to Rafael Arboleda**, whose transfer was reportedly accompanied by a letter from Bolívar identifying it as the sword used in the southern Colombian campaign.
4. Other reported military swords, including examples associated with Alexandre Pétion, Rafael Urdaneta, the battle of Junín, and private or museum collections. Their histories are unevenly documented and sometimes mutually confused.

The familiar proposition that the M-19 object was “the sword with which Bolívar liberated five nations” is therefore not established. The earliest located inventory calls it only a sword “believed to have belonged to the Liberator.” No published metallurgical, forensic, or documentary examination has demonstrated that Bolívar carried it in battle—or, on the evidence presently available, that he owned it at all.

### Evidentiary labels

- **Documented:** supported by a contemporary official record, inventory, insurance schedule, photograph, film, or contemporaneous press report.
- **Institutional attribution:** asserted by the museum, Ministry of Culture, Presidency, or another custodian, but not independently proven.
- **Testimonial reconstruction:** based on later recollections of participants, often recorded after clandestine events.
- **Plausible inference:** consistent with the object and circumstances but not independently documented.
- **Disputed:** supported by incompatible accounts.
- **Legend or propaganda:** a claim principally serving symbolic narrative rather than verifiable provenance.
- **Unresolved:** presently insufficient evidence.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Principal object

**Current institutional name:** *Espada de Bolívar* or *Espada del Libertador Simón Bolívar*.

**Collection:** Casa Museo Quinta de Bolívar, Bogotá.

**Registration number:** **05-475**.

**Institutional dating:** approximately **1821**, nineteenth century.

**Recorded maker:** unknown.

**Recorded materials:** bronze, steel, and wood.

**Recorded technique:** casting (*fundición*); the available summary does not identify which components were cast or describe the blade-forging technique.

**Recorded dimensions:** **85 × 15 × 12 cm**. These appear to be maximum object dimensions, possibly including the projecting hilt and scabbard rather than simply blade length.

These particulars appear in the Colombian Ministry of Culture’s 2022 insurance schedule, which identified the insured object as collection number 05-475 and covered it against theft during its proposed movement on 7 August 2022. The policy valued the insured interest at a reported one billion Colombian pesos in contemporary coverage, but insurance valuation is not an authentication or market appraisal. [Insurance schedule](https://static-uat.cambiocolombia.com/s3fs-public/2022-08/str_1001780-0_original.pdf)

### 1.2 Construction and visible form

The weapon has:

- A substantially **straight steel blade**, with curvature concentrated toward the point rather than the pronounced continuous curve of a sabre.
- A cast, gold-colored or gilded **hilt and guard**, catalogued materially as bronze.
- A perforated or openwork hand guard (*guarnición calada*) protecting part of the hand.
- The **arms of Gran Colombia** incorporated into the guard.
- Vegetal ornament.
- A knuckle guard (*guardamano*).
- A projecting, curved quillon (*gavilán*).
- A wooden component, presumably in the grip or scabbard structure.
- A metal scabbard historically described as copper.

Museum director Elvira Pinzón Méndez supplied these diagnostic features when distinguishing the museum object from the curved weapon shown in a much-circulated photograph of Pablo Escobar’s son. Cultural-heritage specialists at Universidad Externado independently concluded from the photographs that the weapons were different. [AFP comparison and museum description](https://factual.afp.com/doc.afp.com.32GN98Y)

### 1.3 Earliest known inventory description

A commission appointed on 3 December 1924 to evaluate the Quinta’s holdings reportedly entered:

> “una espada con empuñadura dorada y vaina de cobre que se cree perteneció al Libertador”

That is: “a sword with a gilded hilt and copper scabbard which is believed to have belonged to the Liberator.”

The cautious phrase *se cree* is critical. It establishes that the Bolívar attribution was already a belief, not a demonstrated provenance, when the object entered the museum inventory. Later inventories, reportedly including that of 1932, shortened the designation to *Espada de Bolívar*, allowing an explicitly qualified attribution to harden into an object name. [Discussion of the 1924 inventory](https://historiamilitar.wordpress.com/espadas-de-bolivar/) [Later review of the museum acquisition](https://cambiocolombia.com/cultura/articulo/2024/1/la-espada-de-bolivar-un-objeto-que-construye-historias-metaforas-y-discursos)

### 1.4 Inscriptions, marks, condition, and alterations

No reliable published catalogue located for this dossier transcribes an inscription, maker’s mark, serial number, arsenal mark, ownership engraving, or dedicatory text on collection 05-475. This absence should not be mistaken for proof that there are no marks; it means that the accessible institutional description does not publish them.

No public technical report was located concerning:

- alloy composition;
- steel microstructure;
- tool marks;
- gilding analysis;
- wood identification;
- corrosion layers;
- repairs or replacement components;
- maker or national-school attribution;
- comparison with dated military patterns;
- conservation treatment after burial, coating, or clandestine storage;
- proof that the scabbard now paired with the blade is its original scabbard.

The clandestine accounts describe potentially damaging interventions: cleaning, coating with petroleum jelly, wrapping in tow and tar, encasement in cement, burial, and repeated transport. Yet no accessible condition report identifies which corrosion, abrasion, accretion, discoloration, or repair can be attributed to those episodes.

### 1.5 Associated pieces

Reports of the 1974 theft repeatedly mention **a pair of Bolívar’s spurs or rowels** (*espolines*) taken with the sword. At least some clandestine accounts say the spurs were wrapped and moved with it. The later record overwhelmingly concerns the sword; the present identity, accession status, and current location of the spurs were not established from the located institutional material. [Contemporaneous retrospective on the search](https://www.eltiempo.com/archivo/documento/MAM-39926)

### 1.6 Objects not tracked as the principal sword

#### The Sword of Peru

This is a different, far more sumptuous object. It was commissioned by Lima in 1825 after the victories of Junín and Ayacucho. Its steel blade bears, in published transcription:

- “SIMON BOLIVAR”
- “UNION Y LIBERTAD”
- “Año 1825”
- “Libertador de Colombia y Perú”
- “Chungapoma me fecit en Lima”

The scabbard is reported as solid 18-carat gold, weighing about sixty-four ounces, with a nine-inch serpent whose eyes are rubies. The hilt includes gold, diamonds and other stones, Peruvian arms, a personification of Liberty with a Phrygian cap, and the dedication “El Perú a su Libertador.” It also names **C. Freyre** as commissioner. This is emphatically not the bronze, steel, and wood object numbered 05-475. [Sword of Peru description](https://www.monedasdevenezuela.com/joyas-de-bolivar/joyas-del-libertador-espada-del-libertador/) [Peruvian cultural presentation](https://bicentenario.gob.pe/exposiciones/independencia/personajes4.html)

#### Venezuelan replicas

During Hugo Chávez’s government, replicas of the Sword of Peru were presented to foreign leaders and institutions. Two Venezuelan replicas reportedly entered Colombian collections in 2005, one at the Quinta de Bolívar and another at Quinta de San Pedro Alejandrino. These must not be confused with 05-475 merely because they occupy Bolívar memorial sites. [Survey of Bolívar’s swords and replicas](https://www.elespectador.com/el-magazin-cultural/las-espadas-de-bolivar-eran-al-menos-diez/)

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Documented origin of collection 05-475

The sword’s maker, workshop, place of manufacture, original purchaser, cost, and commission are unknown.

The museum dates it to approximately 1821, but no published technical argument supporting that date was located. The date should therefore be treated as **institutional cataloguing**, not a securely documented manufacture date.

A modern account says the Sociedad de Embellecimiento de Bogotá donated the sword to the newly constituted museum collection in 1924. The museum itself arose after the Colombian state reacquired the Quinta de Portocarrero in 1922 to turn it into a memorial to Bolívar. [Museum-context account](https://cambiocolombia.com/cultura/articulo/2024/1/la-espada-de-bolivar-un-objeto-que-construye-historias-metaforas-y-discursos) [History of the Quinta](https://www2.culturarecreacionydeporte.gov.co/sites/default/files/convocatorias_cartillas_y_anexos/fichas_tecnicas_espacios_sugeridos.pdf)

The earliest securely located attestation of the particular object is thus the **1924 inventory**, not a campaign order, receipt, portrait, will, estate division, or letter from Bolívar. The pre-1924 chain remains blank.

### 2.2 Traditional origin

Museum and governmental language often says that the sword “accompanied Bolívar in his campaigns” or identifies it without qualification as *the sword of the Liberator*. On 24 July 2026 the Colombian Foreign Ministry repeated the campaign association while reporting its return to the Quinta. That language is an **official tradition**, not newly disclosed provenance evidence. [Foreign Ministry account](https://cancilleria.gov.co/sala-de-prensa/noticias/la-canciller-rosa-yolanda-villavicencio-mapy-acompano-al-presidente-de-la-republica-en-la-entrega-de-la-espada-de-bolivar-la-quinta-de-bolivar)

A 2024 review states that the sword was presumed to have reached Bolívar around 1822 but adds that visitors imagining it in battle were probably imagining something that did not occur. No underlying contemporary record was cited there for the proposed 1822 association. [Cambio review](https://cambiocolombia.com/cultura/articulo/2024/1/la-espada-de-bolivar-un-objeto-que-construye-historias-metaforas-y-discursos)

### 2.3 The evidentiary seam

The seam is unusually clear:

- **Before 1924:** no reconstructed, document-based custody chain.
- **1924:** a museum commission records a sword *believed* to have belonged to Bolívar.
- **1930s onward:** the qualified attribution becomes the categorical museum name *Espada de Bolívar*.
- **1974 onward:** theft and political use make the museum attribution culturally more consequential than its uncertain provenance.
- **Present:** the object is unquestionably the historical sword of the M-19 episode, even if its personal ownership by Bolívar remains unproved.

Thus the artifact has two different authenticity questions. Its identity as **the sword stolen in 1974** is strongly documented; its identity as **a sword once owned or carried by Bolívar** is not.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 Unknown beginnings to 1924

No continuous account links the sword to Bolívar’s lifetime, death in 1830, estate, relatives, officers, or nineteenth-century collectors. The chain breaks entirely before the Sociedad de Embellecimiento’s reported 1924 donation.

This gap cannot legitimately be filled by transferring the well-documented provenance of the Sword of Peru to the Bogotá object.

### 3.2 1924–1974: museum custody

The sword was accessioned or inventoried at the Casa Museo Quinta de Bolívar in 1924. It was displayed in a glass case, reportedly with spurs, and became one of the museum’s best-known relics. By 1932 the collection terminology reportedly called it *Espada de Bolívar* without the 1924 qualification.

No evidence located indicates that it left the museum during these fifty years, although detailed loan and conservation files were not available.

### 3.3 Theft, 17 January 1974

The M-19 theft was both an unlawful taking and a planned political performance.

Later participant reconstructions disagree on the number of people immediately involved. One 1990 report described twenty-two young people entering the Quinta during the day; other accounts speak of a small operational group. Álvaro Fayad commanded or led the extraction. Vera Grabe and María Eugenia Vásquez have been identified with advance surveillance cells. The group remained until closing time, threatened or subdued the guard, broke or forced access to the display, and removed the sword and spurs.

The exact means of opening the case varies: one retrospective says Fayad smashed the glass with an iron rod; another says a padlock or fastening was broken. The weapon was concealed in a large ruana. A communiqué left behind declared, in its fuller reported form:

> “Bolívar no ha muerto. Su espada rompe las telarañas del museo y se lanza a los combates del presente. Pasa a nuestras manos. Y apunta ahora contra los explotadores del pueblo.”

A shorter slogan became canonical: **“Bolívar, tu espada vuelve a la lucha”**—“Bolívar, your sword returns to the struggle.” [1997 reconstruction](https://www.semana.com/especiales/articulo/la-ruta-de-la-espada/34708-3/) [National memory account](https://www.senalmemoria.co/articulos/espada-de-bolivar-memoria)

The getaway also exists in variants. A 1990 reconstruction says Fayad reached a Renault 12, whose occupants could not operate its lights or windshield wipers in the rain; suspicion at a service station led them to abandon it and carry the sword north on foot before delivering it to Jaime Bateman. Other summaries specify a Renault 6 and name Carlos Sánchez Méndez and a French companion. These details remain testimonial rather than police-file certainties.

The first issue of the magazine *Alternativa*, dated February 1974, published a photograph of the sword over an M-19 flag or map with wording to the effect that it had appeared and was “in Latin America.” The photograph is important continuity evidence: a detailed comparison of the photographed object with 05-475 could potentially test later substitution claims.

### 3.4 First hiding places, 1974–1976

The clandestine chain is contradictory.

One account says the sword first spent weeks in a central Bogotá house or a brothel. Roughly six to eight weeks after the theft, Bateman retrieved it near Calle 22 and Carrera 3, reportedly remarking that it had been “where the prostitutes are,” and carried it to poet **León de Greiff’s** house at Carrera 16A No. 23-35 in the Santa Fe neighborhood.

According to the militant known as “El Indio,” the group cleaned it, wrapped it in a blanket, placed it in a duffel, and left it on books upstairs. Another participant remembered it beneath an old dining table. Bateman reportedly removed it twice for special meetings, though no corroborating record was identified.

A competing 1990 report says the first old central-city house held it for nearly four years under Bateman’s responsibility, which does not fit neatly with the De Greiff chronology. The later, more detailed 1997 reconstruction tentatively places it with De Greiff until illness prompted his family to ask that it be removed in 1976.

Even De Greiff’s death date is inconsistently reported in journalistic retellings. The custody claim should be separated from biographical chronology and checked against civil and archival records before assigning exact terminal dates.

### 3.5 The 1976–1979 gap

This is the least secure Colombian phase. Later testimony proposed that **Gustavo “Boris” de Greiff**, the poet’s son and a prominent chess player, may have removed it, but no confirmation was obtained. Other participants said artists and intellectuals guarded it. **Luis Vidales** is the only additional custodian consistently named.

Reported concealment methods include:

- a specially constructed sofa;
- a PVC tube;
- a cement block placed in a garden;
- an apartment within a house in the Santa Bárbara neighborhood;
- concealment behind books in a library.

The cement story has a documented propagandistic variant. When captured in January 1979, Iván Marino Ospina reportedly told interrogators that the sword had been sealed in cement and thrown into the Magdalena River. According to later testimony, the first half was based on an actual hiding method, while the river was misdirection.

Authorities searched intensely, including reported searches of De Greiff’s tomb and detention of Boris de Greiff and the elderly Luis Vidales. These actions show the practical force of the relic’s symbolism, but they do not themselves establish where it was.

### 3.6 Burial and conservation improvised by the M-19, 1979

Between May and June 1979, a compromised hiding place allegedly required another movement. Bateman, Fayad, Carlos Toledo, a physicist, and Afranio Parra reportedly prepared the sword by:

1. coating it in petroleum jelly;
2. wrapping it in tow;
3. applying additional grease;
4. adding tar;
5. enclosing it in a wooden box.

They transported it out of Bogotá with household goods as cover, briefly unpacked it for photographs, and buried it at an unidentified rural property—possibly, according to unverified testimony, a farm belonging to a prominent politician.

These measures were intended as corrosion protection but cannot be evaluated without a conservation report. Tar, cement, fluctuating humidity, soil contact, and aggressive cleaning could all alter metal surfaces.

### 3.7 Removal from Colombia, approximately 1980

Following Fayad’s October 1979 arrest, Bateman reportedly decided to remove the object from Colombia. Later M-19 accounts say that, during 1980, a woman guardian and another militant carried it from the burial site, after which Cuban diplomatic or intelligence channels assumed custody.

The proposed route varies:

- Bogotá to the Cuban embassy or a Cuban official;
- Colombia to Cuba directly or through diplomatic channels;
- Cuba to the Cuban mission in Panama;
- temporary presence in Panama during the December 1989 United States invasion;
- return to Cuba;
- Cuba to Venezuela and then Colombia in January 1991.

Antonio Navarro Wolff later said the sword had been in Cuba from the late 1970s until its return. Otty Patiño and Carlos Sánchez gave broadly compatible Cuban-custody accounts. [AFP account](https://factual.afp.com/doc.afp.com.32GN98Y)

### 3.8 The incompatible Colombia-based account, 1985–1990

A 1990 *Semana* investigation reported something different. It said Fayad and Carlos Pizarro moved the sword through Bogotá in 1985 in a cardboard box filled with pineapples, placing it with an unnamed trusted civilian. On 23 April 1990, days before Pizarro’s assassination, Pizarro and three confidants allegedly saw the sword in a wooden box in the custodian’s northern Bogotá apartment. It was then rewrapped in petroleum-jelly-coated plastic and returned to an old wardrobe. [Contemporaneous 1990 investigation](https://www.semana.com/nacion/articulo/que-nos-la-devuelvan/13814-3/)

That account cannot be reconciled literally with continuous Cuban custody. Possibilities include faulty recollection, deliberate operational misinformation, an undisclosed temporary return from Cuba, or the presence of a replica. No accessible documentary evidence resolves the issue.

### 3.9 Panama and the 1989 invasion

A 1997 investigation, relying on two non-M-19 sources connected with the peace process, placed the sword in the Cuban embassy in Panama in December 1989. The mission was evacuated after the United States invasion began on 20 December. Rumors held that the sword was lost or seized by American forces; another account says it returned safely to Cuba.

The later recovery of a sword does not prove which of these accounts is correct because the identity continuity was never publicly tested. No United States military seizure record, Cuban diplomatic inventory, transportation manifest, or contemporaneous photograph was located.

### 3.10 Public uncertainty, 1990

After the M-19’s disarmament on 9 March 1990, pressure increased for the relic’s return. Carlos Pizarro reportedly intended to return it, but he was assassinated on 26 April.

In congressional questioning during August or September, Antonio Navarro said: **“Sabemos cómo llegar a ella pero no sabemos quién la tiene”**—“We know how to reach it, but we do not know who has it.” In November or December the M-19 placed newspaper advertisements seeking the sword. On 17 December 1990, newspapers reported the incongruity of a demobilized movement publicly searching for the relic it had stolen. [Contemporaneous report](https://www.eltiempo.com/archivo/documento/MAM-39926)

The advertisements may reflect genuine loss of contact under compartmentalization, a means of signalling a clandestine custodian, political theatre, or a cover for negotiations with Cuba. The evidence does not decide among these alternatives.

### 3.11 Recovery and return, January 1991

The most repeated reconstruction is that **Arjaid Artunduaga** travelled to Cuba in mid-January 1991, collected the sword, entered Venezuela with it, and brought it clandestinely to Colombia. Patiño’s version says he first approached the Cuban ambassador in Caracas because Colombia and Cuba lacked normal diplomatic relations.

On 29 January Otty Patiño and **Laura García de Pizarro**, Carlos Pizarro’s widow, coordinated the ceremony with museum director Diana Torres de Ospina.

On **31 January 1991**, at the Quinta:

1. Laura de Pizarro presented the sword.
2. It passed to Antonio Navarro Wolff.
3. Children of deceased M-19 commanders participated in the symbolic transfer.
4. President César Gaviria and government officials witnessed the ceremony.
5. Dissident former militants protested outside, arguing that Navarro lacked authority to return it.
6. The government transferred the object to secure custody at the Banco de la República.

The ceremony was filmed and contemporaneously reported. It proves delivery of the presently recognized object, but not an unbroken clandestine chain. [El Tiempo report, 1 February 1991](https://www.eltiempo.com/archivo/documento/MAM-18139)

### 3.12 1991–2000/2002: bank vault

The sword remained in a Banco de la República vault or safe-deposit box for most of the 1990s. Accounts differ slightly about when it returned to the Quinta:

- one retrospective says **2000**;
- a 2002 report says it had been in a Banco de la República vault “for a decade” and remained there while the Quinta underwent expansion.

The discrepancy may distinguish legal return to the collection, intermittent exhibition, and actual secure storage. [2002 report](https://www.eltiempo.com/archivo/documento/MAM-1344948) [2010 retrospective](https://www.elespectador.com/colombia/cartagena/el-robo-de-la-espada-article-219336/)

### 3.13 2000s–2022: renewed museum custody

The sword resumed its place in the Quinta’s collection but was subject to heightened security and limited movement. The published 2022 insurance schedule confirms that the Ministry of Culture remained the insured party and the museum registration remained 05-475.

### 3.14 7 August 2022: presidential inauguration

President-elect Gustavo Petro requested that the sword appear at his inauguration in Plaza de Bolívar. Outgoing president Iván Duque’s administration withheld authorization, publicly attributed to security and insurance concerns despite preparation of a one-day policy.

After taking the oath, Petro issued his first instruction as president: **“Como Presidente de Colombia, le solicito a la Casa Militar traer la espada de Bolívar.”** After a delay of approximately ten to twenty minutes, soldiers carried the sword in a glass case to the platform.

Petro declared that it should not be buried or retained and should be sheathed only when Colombia had justice. The ceremony created a new, directly documented layer of meaning: the object stolen by a movement to which Petro had belonged became the central material emblem of the first moments of his presidency. [Presidential transcript](https://petro.presidencia.gov.co/prensa/Paginas/Quiero-que-la-espada-de-Bolivar-se-envaine-cuando-haya-justicia-en-este-pa-220807.aspx)

Claims that King Felipe VI of Spain deliberately refused to honor the sword are interpretations of televised conduct. He remained seated while others stood, but no evidence establishes his intention, nor was standing necessarily prescribed by agreed protocol.

### 3.15 2022–2026: Casa de Nariño

After the inauguration the sword was retained under presidential custody at the Casa de Nariño. It appeared as a symbol in Petro’s political rhetoric and at least one later political event, including its public use in 2025 amid debate over a proposed popular consultation. Critics argued that a national museum object was being converted into a partisan emblem; Petro and supporters presented it as a popular and emancipatory symbol.

No transfer of legal ownership to Petro or the Presidency occurred. The museum registration and state patrimonial status remained.

### 3.16 Return to the Quinta, 24 July 2026

On Bolívar’s birthday, **24 July 2026**, Petro returned the sword ceremonially from the Casa de Nariño to the Casa Museo Quinta de Bolívar. The route passed the monument to Policarpa Salavarrieta before reaching the museum. Cavalry and a historical guard accompanied the object.

The Foreign Ministry described the event as a formal delivery. A subsequent government public-information notice stated that the sword would again be accessible to citizens, students, and researchers as part of the Quinta’s collection. [Official return notice](https://cancilleria.gov.co/sala-de-prensa/noticias/la-canciller-rosa-yolanda-villavicencio-mapy-acompano-al-presidente-de-la-republica-en-la-entrega-de-la-espada-de-bolivar-la-quinta-de-bolivar) [Public access notice](https://www.urnadecristal.gov.co/actualizate/la-espada-de-bolivar-regreso-la-quinta-de-bolivar-y-retomo-su-lugar-como-patrimonio-historico-de-la-nacion)

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Did it belong to Bolívar?

**Case supporting the attribution:**

- The sword was accepted into a Bolívar memorial collection in 1924.
- It is typologically compatible, at least at a broad level, with a nineteenth-century military or ceremonial weapon.
- Museum tradition dates it to about 1821.
- Successive Colombian cultural institutions have maintained the attribution.

**Case against certainty:**

- The first located inventory says only that it was *believed* to have belonged to Bolívar.
- No pre-1924 owner or donor provenance has been established.
- No letter, estate inventory, portrait, presentation inscription, or campaign record has been tied to it.
- No published laboratory examination has established an early nineteenth-century date.
- Bolívar owned numerous swords, making generic family or patriotic attribution particularly hazardous.

**Finding:** ownership by Bolívar is **possible but unproved**.

### 4.2 Is the returned sword the one stolen in 1974?

**Evidence for continuity:**

- The state and former M-19 leadership accepted the 1991 object without recorded public objection to its appearance.
- Photographs exist from before or shortly after the 1974 theft, from the clandestine period, from the 1991 return, and from modern display.
- Museum specialists recognize the present sword through specific morphological features.
- No documented confession, manufacturing record, or forensic finding demonstrates substitution.

**Reasons continuity cannot be considered forensically closed:**

- The sword passed through undocumented hands for seventeen years.
- Its custodians deliberately compartmentalized information.
- Public statements in 1990 admitted uncertainty about who held it.
- Accounts place it simultaneously or successively in incompatible locations.
- It may have been at risk during the 1989 invasion of Panama.
- No published pre-theft measurements, metallurgical fingerprint, microscopic comparison, or distinctive-damage study has been matched against the returned object.
- No public authentication report accompanied the 1991 return.

**Finding:** continuity between the 1974 and 1991 objects is strongly accepted institutionally and historically plausible, but **not scientifically demonstrated**.

### 4.3 Was it replaced by a replica?

Substitution rumors arose naturally from secrecy, Cuba’s alleged custody, Panama, the M-19’s public search, and the existence of many Bolívar-sword replicas. No located source proves a replacement.

The possibility could be investigated through high-resolution comparison of:

- pre-1974 museum photographs;
- the February 1974 *Alternativa* image;
- photographs reportedly taken by Fayad, Bateman, and Toledo in 1979;
- any Cuban custody photograph sent for the Guardian replicas;
- 1991 news footage;
- the present object.

Unique nicks, corrosion islands, casting flaws, engraving details, and guard contours could establish or undermine continuity without destructive testing.

### 4.4 The Pablo Escobar claim

The Netflix series *Narcos* and later popular accounts portrayed or suggested the sword passing to drug trafficker Pablo Escobar. A photograph of Escobar’s son holding a curved weapon was circulated as proof.

The claim fails at the level of object identification:

- the pictured weapon has a different hilt;
- it lacks 05-475’s openwork guard, Gran Colombian arms, knuckle guard, and quillon;
- its blade curvature differs;
- museum staff and Universidad Externado specialists concluded it is not the museum sword;
- Otty Patiño and Darío Villamizar denied that the M-19 transferred the relic to Escobar.

The pictured weapon may be another sword or sabre, but it is not collection 05-475. [AFP fact-check](https://factual.afp.com/doc.afp.com.32GN98Y)

### 4.5 Confusion with the Sword of Peru

The most persistent identity error is the transfer of the Sword of Peru’s secure 1825 biography to the Quinta weapon. Statements that “Bolívar’s sword” was:

- commissioned by Lima;
- made by Chungapoma;
- supervised by C. Freyre;
- made of solid gold;
- set with rubies and diamonds;
- inherited by Juana Bolívar;
- bought by Venezuela in 1889;
- kept by the Banco Central de Venezuela

describe the Sword of Peru, not the M-19 sword.

### 4.6 Authority to authenticate

Institutional authority has shifted:

- In 1924, a historical commission and the museum accepted a cautious attribution.
- Museum inventories normalized that attribution.
- In 1991, the Colombian executive, museum officials, and former M-19 leaders jointly recognized the returned object.
- Today, the Ministry of Culture and Casa Museo Quinta de Bolívar control the catalogue identity.
- Heritage specialists can distinguish the object morphologically, but no public scientific program has tested its Bolívar-era provenance.

Institutional recognition makes 05-475 the legally and culturally recognized relic. It does not retroactively supply its missing nineteenth-century provenance.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Institutions

### Simón Bolívar

**Connection:** alleged original owner; no direct record tied to 05-475.  
**Date:** conventionally circa 1821–1822.  
**Tier:** institutional tradition, unproved.

### Sociedad de Embellecimiento de Bogotá

**Connection:** reported donor or source of the sword when the Quinta’s museum collection was organized.  
**Date:** 1924.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Museum relic

Before 1974, the sword’s primary function was commemorative. Displayed with other personal relics in Bolívar’s former residence, it materialized the national founder. Visitors supplied the imagined campaign history that the inventory did not prove.

### 6.2 Foundational emblem of the M-19

The theft served as a declaration that Bolívar’s struggle had passed from the museum into contemporary insurgency. The M-19 used the object or its image:

- in its inaugural communiqué;
- in photographs published by *Alternativa*;
- across maps of Colombia or South America;
- in slogans shouted in combat and military trials;
- as an emblem of the multinational *Batallón América*;
- as an argument for a nationalist, Latin American revolutionary identity distinct from orthodox Soviet-oriented iconography.

This symbolic function is documented whether or not Bolívar ever owned the artifact.

### 6.3 “Order of the Guardians of the Sword”

In 1986–1987, during a period of military and political difficulty, the M-19 created the **Orden de los Guardianes de la Espada**. Twelve people or organizations selected for anti-imperialist, democratic, or sovereignty-related reputations were to receive miniature gold replicas and certificates.

Named recipients in available accounts include:

- Omar Torrijos, posthumously;
- Bishop Sergio Méndez Arceo;
- Eduardo Galeano;
- Mario Benedetti;
- the Mothers of Plaza de Mayo.

Galeano and Benedetti reportedly did not receive their miniatures; Torrijos’s went to his heirs. Since those commissioning the pieces had not seen the original, they asked Cuban custodians for a photograph. The order was publicly announced on 24 July 1987. [Detailed reconstruction](https://www.semana.com/especiales/articulo/la-ruta-de-la-espada/34708-3/)

### 6.4 Return as peace ritual

The 1991 ceremony transformed the object from insurgent trophy into a restitution token. The participation of commanders’ children projected generational closure; transfer to state custody represented the M-19’s movement from clandestine armed organization to legal politics. Dissident protests demonstrated that the meaning was contested even within the former organization.

### 6.5 Presidential investiture

In 2022 the sword acquired a quasi-regalia function without becoming constitutional regalia. Petro’s order paused the inauguration until the object arrived. Soldiers carried it in a transparent case; its presence visually linked Bolívar, the M-19 peace process, the 1991 Constitution, and Petro’s electoral victory.

The object conferred no legal authority. Its efficacy was theatrical and mnemonic: it made a claim about the historical genealogy of the new administration.

### 6.6 Sheathing and unsheathing

Petro repeatedly invoked a saying that the sword should not be sheathed until liberty or justice was secured. Versions of this formula circulate under Bolívar’s name, including language used in Colombia’s military decoration “Protectors of the Liberator’s Sword.” The precise textual origin and wording require separate verification before treating it as an authenticated Bolívar quotation. [Decree creating the military medal](https://sidn.ramajudicial.gov.co/SIDN/NORMATIVA/TEXTOS_COMPLETOS/5_DECRETOS/DECRETOS%202021/Decreto%20093%20de%202021%20%28Espada%20del%20Libertador%29.pdf)

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 “The sword that liberated five nations”

**Category:** national and institutional simplification.

The phrase collapses Bolívar’s military career into one blade and assumes 05-475 was that blade. Bolívar demonstrably possessed multiple swords; some were ceremonial, some military, and some were given away. No documentary evidence makes the Quinta object the constant weapon of the independence campaigns.

### 7.2 The sword escaping the museum’s “cobwebs”

**Category:** contemporaneous M-19 propaganda, 1974.

The communiqué personified the object as an actor that broke free from dead institutional memory and entered present combat. It is not an account of the weapon’s earlier history; it is the founding myth of its second, politically documented life.

### 7.3 The river story

**Category:** deliberate clandestine misinformation with a partial factual core.

Iván Marino Ospina reportedly said the blade had been sealed in cement and thrown into the Magdalena. Later testimony maintained it had indeed been hidden in cement but remained in a garden. The story protected the relic by converting a real concealment technique into a false destruction narrative.

### 7.4 Burial in a politician’s estate

**Category:** unverified testimony.

The sword was reportedly buried on a farm owned by an unnamed prominent politician. Because neither the place nor owner was identified, the allegation cannot be checked and should not be converted into a factual custody link.

### 7.5 American seizure in Panama

**Category:** unresolved rumor generated by the December 1989 invasion.

The sword was allegedly left vulnerable in the evacuated Cuban mission. Some believed American forces obtained it; others said it returned to Cuba. Since a sword was delivered in 1991, the seizure theory implies later substitution or recovery, neither of which has been demonstrated.

### 7.6 Pablo Escobar possessed the sword

**Category:** late narco legend amplified by television.

The account gained public force through *Narcos* and a family photograph, but visual comparison disproves identification of the pictured curved weapon as 05-475. M-19 witnesses deny the transfer. The story remains significant as modern reception, not provenance.

### 7.7 The M-19 returned a copy

**Category:** unresolved suspicion without affirmative proof.

The long undocumented interval makes substitution possible in the abstract, but no claimant has produced the alleged original, identified a copyist, found a manufacturing record, or demonstrated physical disagreement between the pre-1974 and post-1991 sword.

### 7.8 “Recovered” rather than stolen

Former M-19 members and some modern official programming call the 1974 act a *recuperación*—a recovery. The Museo Nacional used that language in announcing a 2024 commemorative exhibition. Legally and physically, the group removed a museum object without authorization through coercion: it was a theft. *Recovery* records the perpetrators’ political interpretation, not the transfer mechanism. [Museo Nacional announcement](https://www.museonacional.gov.co/noticias/Paginas/Espada_Simon_Bolivar.aspx)

## 8. Current Status and Location

As of **6 September 2026**, collection **05-475** is again controlled by the **Casa Museo Quinta de Bolívar**, Calle 20 No. 2-91 Este, Bogotá, under Colombia’s national cultural administration.

The object returned there on 24 July 2026 after four years at the Casa de Nariño. Government information states that it is again available for public appreciation and research, although ordinary gallery hours, temporary removal, and security requirements may affect actual access.

No current ownership or repatriation litigation was identified. The principal dispute is interpretive: whether an object of national patrimony should be mobilized as a presidential or former-guerrilla symbol.

No post-return technical examination, conservation report, environmental specification, or updated condition assessment was located. Given the sword’s history of burial, cement concealment, grease, tar, repeated handling, and public transport, a published conservation study would materially improve the record.

The scabbard’s precise condition and the current status of the spurs remain unestablished from available sources.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Logos and graphic reproduction

The sword crossing a map became one of the M-19’s principal graphic signs. Its image outlived the physical guerrilla organization and remains present in memoirs, exhibitions, anniversary journalism, and political controversy.

### 9.2 Miniatures and replicas

There are at least three distinct replica traditions:

1. M-19’s small gold Guardian swords of 1986–1987.
2. Venezuelan reproductions of the Sword of Peru given by Hugo Chávez to foreign leaders and Colombian memorial institutions.
3. Commercial, ceremonial, and decorative “Bolívar swords,” often poorly identified and liable to be confused with either principal relic.

The second category reproduces the gold Peruvian sword, not 05-475.

### 9.3 Museum exhibition as contested memory

The Quinta’s exhibition *Historia de una ausencia: la espada de Bolívar y el M-19* was developed with former combatants, particularly women presenting individual and collective memory. Its panels explicitly represented their authorship rather than neutral museum consensus. The exhibition treated the sword as a speaking witness to identity, unity, dignity, peace, audacity, and political struggle. [Exhibition document](https://es.scribd.com/document/615782932/Casa-Museo-Quinta-de-Bolivar-Historia-de-una-ausencia)

A 2024 exhibition and public program at the Museo Nacional commemorated fifty years since the M-19 action and connected the object’s history to disarmament and peacebuilding. The choice of the word *recovery* itself became part of the politics of memory.

### 9.4 Film and television

Netflix’s *Narcos* materially strengthened the false or unverified narrative that the sword reached Pablo Escobar. The series’ dramatic representation should not be cited as custody evidence.

News film in the Señal Memoria archive preserves:

- retrospective television coverage of the 1974 theft;
- the 1991 return ceremony.

These audiovisual records are important for future object-comparison research. [Señal Memoria](https://www.senalmemoria.co/articulos/espada-de-bolivar-memoria)

### 9.5 Literature and journalism

Major narrative reconstructions include:

- the 1990 *Semana* inquiry while the sword was still missing;
- *Semana*’s 1997 “La ruta de la espada”;
- participant memoirs and interviews;
- Patricia Lara Salive’s later book-length reconstruction, *La espada de Bolívar: del M-19 a la Casa de Nariño*.

Their value lies in access to participants; their limitation is reliance on memories shaped by clandestinity, political allegiance, elapsed time, and deliberate compartmentalization.

### 9.6 Petro-era political reception

From 2022 to 2026, the sword was alternately presented as:

- an emblem of popular sovereignty and completed peace;
- a bridge between Bolívar, the M-19, the 1991 constitutional settlement, and the Petro presidency;
- an improperly partisan use of national heritage;
- a continuing glorification of armed insurgency;
- a symbol successfully transformed from weapon into democratic memory.

The dispute concerns the object’s public ownership in the symbolic sense, not merely its legal title.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 State of the primary evidence

Important surviving or reported primary evidence includes:

- the 1924 Quinta inventory;
- subsequent museum inventories, including that of 1932;
- pre-theft photographs and catalogue records;
- police, intelligence, and museum reports from 17 January 1974;
- M-19’s founding communiqué;
- the photograph published in *Alternativa*;
- photographs reportedly made during the 1979 rural transport;
- interrogation and detention records associated with the search;
- M-19 conference records;
- Cuban diplomatic or security documentation, if preserved and accessible;
- congressional proceedings from 1990;
- advertisements seeking the sword;
- film and photographs of the 1991 restitution;
- Banco de la República custody records;
- museum accession and conservation files;
- the 2022 insurance policy and movement authorizations;
- Presidency and Ministry of Culture transfer documentation, 2022–2026.

Much of the decisive evidence remains unpublished or was not located online.

### 10.2 Dominant historical approaches

Three approaches recur:

1. **Relic history:** assumes the object belonged to Bolívar and treats later events as adventures of an authenticated national treasure.
2. **M-19 political history:** treats the sword principally as an insurgent symbol and reconstructs its route through oral history.
3. **Memory and material-culture history:** asks how a weakly proven museum attribution became politically real through display, theft, secrecy, restitution, and reuse.

The third best accommodates the evidence: the sword’s modern authenticity as a political relic does not depend on proving Bolívar’s ownership.

### 10.3 Running controversies

- Who first proposed the theft: Luis Otero or Jaime Bateman?
- How many people participated directly?
- Was the display case smashed, forced, or unlocked?
- What was the first hiding place?
- Did León de Greiff knowingly accept custody, and for how long?
- Who held it between 1976 and 1979?
- When did it leave Colombia?
- Was it continuously in Cuba, or back in Bogotá by 1985?
- Was it in Panama during the 1989 invasion?
- Why did the M-19 publicly search for it in 1990?
- Is the 1991-returned object the 1974 object?
- Did Bolívar ever own it?
- Should state ceremonies employ it as a national emblem or is that partisan appropriation?

### 10.4 Needed research

A responsible authentication project would combine:

- full publication of the 1924 accession documentation;
- donor and Sociedad de Embellecimiento archives;
- typological study by arms historians;
- non-destructive X-ray fluorescence of bronze, gilding, and blade;
- radiography and metallography where ethically possible;
- wood-species identification;
- tool-mark and manufacturing analysis;
- conservation mapping;
- forensic comparison of all dated photographs;
- search for Gran Colombian military-pattern parallels;
- review of Bolívar’s inventories, correspondence, estate papers, and gift records;
- Cuban, Venezuelan, Panamanian, Colombian, and United States archival work.

Until such work is published, categorical claims about the blade’s manufacture or Bolívar’s use exceed the evidence.

## 12. Sources

The source base is strongest for the 1974 theft, 1991 restitution, and 2022–2026 public movements. It is weakest where the most consequential attribution lies: before the 1924 inventory.

The 1990 and 1997 *Semana* investigations are indispensable but incompatible at important points. They should be read as oral-history compilations produced first while the relic was missing and later after additional witnesses spoke—not as a seamless archival chain.

Government and museum sources reliably document catalogue identity, present custody, official ceremonies, and institutional language. They do not independently prove Bolívar-era provenance. Recent official statements that the sword accompanied Bolívar through South American campaigns repeat national tradition and should not override the cautious 1924 inventory.

The Sword of Peru literature is useful principally to prevent conflation. Its maker’s inscription, commission, materials, and family-to-state provenance belong to a different object.
