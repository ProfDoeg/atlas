# The Sainte Ampoule (Holy Ampulla): Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

“The Sainte Ampoule” has denoted several materially distinct things:

1. the small glass or crystal vial documented at Reims by the Middle Ages and destroyed in 1793;
2. the aromatic, dark-brown substance traditionally identified as heaven-sent chrism;
3. the medieval/early-modern gold-and-vermeil reliquary that enclosed the vial and was dismantled or destroyed during the Revolution;
4. alleged fragments of the vial and balm recovered in 1793;
5. the Restoration-period rock-crystal replacement vial and Jean-Charles Cahier reliquary made in 1819–22 for those alleged remnants;
6. a separate glass vial into which Cardinal Louis-Joseph Luçon transferred the claimed balm in 1906.

These components do not have equally secure chains of identity. The pre-Revolutionary vial certainly existed, but its manufacture and connection with Clovis are undocumented. It was deliberately broken in 1793. The nineteenth-century vial and reliquary are documented replacements. Continuity survives only through minute quantities of substance and glass whose rescue histories rest principally on post-Revolutionary declarations rather than continuous forensic custody.

Evidence labels used below:

- **Documented:** supported by contemporary records, an extant artifact, or an official collection record.
- **Scientific/institutional assessment:** a finding or interpretation published by a museum, heritage authority, or specialist.
- **Plausible scholarly inference:** compatible with the material and documentary evidence but not demonstrated.
- **Ecclesiastical tradition:** formally or informally maintained within the Church of Reims.
- **Legend:** supernatural or retrospective narrative not established by contemporary evidence.
- **Disputed/unverified:** testimony exists, but continuity or accuracy cannot independently be demonstrated.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Names

The principal French name is **la Sainte Ampoule**, conventionally translated as **the Holy Ampulla**, **Holy Ampoule**, or **Holy Phial**. Medieval Latin expressions include *ampulla*, *ampullula* (“small flask”), *sacrosancta ampulla*, and descriptions of its contents as *sanctum chrisma* or chrism *caelitus*/*de caelo*—“from heaven.”

French *ampoule* descends from Latin *ampulla*, a small rounded flask or bottle. The occasionally proposed derivation from a Germanic or Saxon form such as *ampel* is unnecessary and linguistically secondary. “Sainte” initially marked the vessel’s alleged heavenly contents and association with Saint Remigius; in later usage vessel, substance, reliquary, and institutional cult were frequently compressed into the same name.

### The destroyed pre-1793 vial

The fullest widely accessible physical description derives from Jean-François Lacatte-Joltrois’s *Recherches historiques sur la Sainte Ampoule* (1825), drawing on earlier descriptions, particularly Dom Guillaume Marlot and the *Almanach de Reims* for 1771:

- a small vial of **glass or crystal**;
- approximately **1½ French inches high**, roughly 4 cm, depending on the historical measure employed;
- neck circumference approximately **seven lignes**;
- base circumference approximately **thirteen lignes**;
- sealed at the neck with **red taffeta**;
- about two-thirds filled with a **dark-brown balm**, congealed against the inner walls.

Lacatte-Joltrois’s description is useful but not a modern conservation examination: it was published more than thirty years after the vessel’s destruction and belonged to the literature preparing the restored relic for Charles X’s coronation. It nevertheless preserves substantially more physical detail than the medieval narratives. [Lacatte-Joltrois, digitized text](https://pdfs.bibliotheque-catholique.com/pdfs/histoire-de-france/Jacques%20Lacatte-Joltrois%20-%20Recherches%20historiques%20sur%20la%20Sainte%20Ampoule.pdf)

No securely published weight, maker’s mark, inscription, compositional assay, or archaeological drawing of the naked vial has been identified. “Glass or crystal” is therefore the historical description, not a laboratory determination. Sources calling it opaque, reddish, or ordinary glass are not wholly consistent.

### Its pre-Revolutionary reliquary

By the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the vial was mounted:

- inside a gold dove;
- within a rose- or plate-shaped frame of vermeil;
- behind a transparent crystal cover;
- surrounded by precious stones;
- with a silver-gilt or gold border and a heavy silver chain;
- accompanied by a gold needle or stylus, about thirty-seven lignes long;
- backed by a removable silver paten on which the balm and newly consecrated chrism were mixed.

Lacatte-Joltrois gives the mount as about forty-five lignes long and the dove as thirty-eight lignes. The assembly opened in two parts. Fastenings at its rear were undone for a coronation so that the vial, needle, and paten could be reached.

Prosper Tarbé’s nineteenth-century history likewise describes the ampulla between or within a silver dove, enclosed in a circular or oval frame, with a chain by which the grand prior carried it at his neck. [Tarbé, *Notre-Dame de Reims*](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark%3A/12148/bpt6k54702863.pdf)

Its construction date is uncertain. A late-twelfth-century attribution is sometimes proposed, but no surviving commission or hallmark establishes it. Patrick Demouy has observed that Marlot’s description of the vial itself is of an unadorned, ordinary-looking container, not an object securely attributable to a sophisticated twelfth-century royal workshop.

The pre-Revolutionary vial, dove, frame, fittings, and most of their contents no longer survive as an intact artifact.

### The 1820–22 replacement ensemble

The present display object is a Restoration-period reliquary by the Parisian royal goldsmith **Jean-Charles Cahier**, reportedly after designs by **Louis Lafitte**. Official and institutional sources differ slightly between “completed in 1820” and a date of 1822; this likely reflects stages between manufacture, completion, and delivery rather than two different reliquaries.

The French Ministry of Culture records:

- gilded silver and crystal;
- a mausoleum-shaped reliquary;
- rectangular base on four claw feet;
- four angel statuettes at the corners;
- four bas-reliefs on the lower chest;
- an upper chest decorated with pilasters and ribbons;
- a lid with four angel heads;
- a crowning dove;
- inside, a small pear-shaped vial on a circular silver foot, capped by a royal crown with fleurs-de-lis;
- base dimensions: approximately **47.2 × 32 cm**.

A Mobilier national catalogue gives the ensemble as approximately **36 cm high, 46.5 cm wide, and 32 cm deep**, made of gilded silver, rock crystal, emeralds, and rubies. It identifies scenes of the baptism of Clovis and the coronation of Louis XVI, angels carrying royal emblems and instruments of the Passion, and medallions of kings crowned at Reims. Its inventory/deposit identifier is reported as **D-TAU 1905000223/4**. [French Ministry of Culture, Palissy PM51000716](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/palissy/PM51000716); [Mobilier national catalogue](https://www.mobiliernational.culture.gouv.fr/sites/default/files/2025-04/2514_MobilierNational_CharlesX_LivretPedagogique_V6.pdf)

The official Palissy notice describes the listing as “2 reliquaires,” evidently treating the monumental outer reliquary and the inner liturgical vial as two catalogued objects. It records state ownership, classification as a *monument historique* on 28 February 1896, and present conservation in the treasury room of the Palais du Tau.

The Centre des monuments nationaux says that the inner vial is **rock crystal**. It no longer contains the disputed balm, which was removed in 1906. [Palais du Tau](https://www.palais-du-tau.fr/decouvrir/le-reliquaire-de-la-sainte-ampoule)

### Inscriptions

A pamphlet devoted to the new reliquary’s inscriptions was published in 1825 and attributed to the abbé d’Auribeau. Its existence is catalogued by the French *Catalogue collectif de France*, but the complete inscriptional programme was not recoverable from the accessible catalogue record. [CCFr catalogue record](https://ccfr.bnf.fr/portailccfr/ark%3A/16871/0014406137)

No inscription has been documented on the destroyed vial itself.

### Condition

- **Original vial:** destroyed in 1793; only alleged chips or particles survive.
- **Original contents:** largely lost; claimed minute remnants survive through disputed rescue accounts.
- **Original reliquary:** destroyed, dismantled, or sent as precious metal for recovery during the Revolution.
- **1820–22 outer reliquary and rock-crystal vial:** extant and catalogued.
- **Claimed balm:** held separately under ecclesiastical custody after its 1906 transfer; its present quantity and exact container are not fully described in the public heritage catalogue.
- **Golden extraction needle:** a nineteenth-century needle is displayed with or associated with the replacement ensemble.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### The baptism of Clovis: what the early evidence does not say

Clovis’s conversion and baptism are narrated by Gregory of Tours in the later sixth century, but Gregory does **not** mention a dove, a supernatural flask, or heaven-sent chrism. Other early texts connected with Clovis and Remigius likewise fail to attest the object.

Consequently, there is no contemporary fifth- or sixth-century evidence that a special vessel was used at Clovis’s baptism, that the oil arrived miraculously, or that any such vessel was preserved afterward.

The baptism itself is conventionally dated to Christmas 496, but modern scholarship allows a range extending into the early sixth century. The uncertainty in the baptism’s date compounds, rather than resolves, the absence of evidence for the vessel.

### Hincmar’s ninth-century formulation

The first secure literary horizon is the work of **Hincmar, archbishop of Reims (845–882)**. The story appears in increasingly explicit forms in texts associated with him, culminating in his *Vita sancti Remigii*, generally dated about 876–78.

Hincmar’s Latin formulation reads:

> *Et ecce subito columba nive candidior attulit in rostro ampullulam chrismate sancto repletam.*

A close translation is: “And behold, suddenly a dove whiter than snow brought in its beak a little flask filled with holy chrism.”

The narrative says that the cleric carrying the ordinary chrism could not penetrate the crowd; Remigius prayed; the dove appeared; and an extraordinary fragrance filled the baptistery. This is the earliest securely datable, fully developed account of the miraculous delivery. A modern critical note cites Bruno Krusch’s edition in *Monumenta Germaniae Historica, Scriptores rerum Merovingicarum*, III, pp. 296–97. [Critical textual discussion](https://libra.unine.ch/server/api/core/bitstreams/9e3257a2-f9bc-4969-a11c-1e8d0a3b9756/content)

An ivory plaque of the **Miracles of Saint Remigius**, generally dated to the ninth century and plausibly commissioned in Hincmar’s milieu, provides early visual evidence. Its lower register shows Remigius baptizing Clovis with the ampulla/dove motif. The dating and patronage remain art-historical attributions, but the object demonstrates that the legend was visualized near the time of Hincmar’s writing. [INHA iconographic record](https://omci.inha.fr/s/ocmi/item/153)

### Alleged discovery in Remigius’s tomb

A later reconstruction holds that, during Hincmar’s translation of Remigius’s relics in 852, one or two small aromatic vials were found in or near the saint’s sarcophagus. Hincmar then supposedly identified one with the baptismal vessel.

The Centre des monuments nationaux offers a cautious institutional interpretation: Hincmar may have recognized the miraculous ampulla in an **aromatic flask probably left by embalmers in Remigius’s sarcophagus**. This is an inference, not an archaeologically documented find. [CMN object sheet](https://www.palais-du-tau.fr/var/cmn_inter/storage/original/application/680863b1a81a9d75e727e3c242cb2ba5.pdf)

Francis Oppenheimer later compared the vessel with Roman or Gallo-Roman balsamaria commonly placed in graves. The hypothesis is materially plausible: an old funerary scent bottle could have become the physical nucleus of the legend. But the vessel was destroyed before archaeological glass typology or chemical examination could test that proposal.

### Flodoard and consolidation

Flodoard’s tenth-century *Historia Remensis ecclesiae*, compiled from the Reims archives and Hincmar’s writings, transmitted the legend in a developed ecclesiastical history. Flodoard is not an independent fifth-century witness; he is evidence for the story’s consolidation in tenth-century Reims.

### First documented ritual use

Potential use at earlier Reims coronations—sometimes extended back to Odo in 888/893—is conjectural. The first commonly accepted documentary evidence for use of the sacred balm in a royal consecration is the coronation of **Louis VII at Reims in 1131**, performed by Pope Innocent II. The vessel itself becomes explicit in thirteenth-century coronation ordines.

By about 1230–50, an ordo specifies the **sacrosancta ampulla** brought as a relic by the abbot of Saint-Remi. This is the point at which the ampulla’s place in a stable, elaborated coronation apparatus becomes unambiguous. Scholarship emphasizes that Reims, its archbishop, and its ampulla became an uncontested package only gradually, particularly from the thirteenth century. [Study of coronation institutions](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-0752/9/4/111)

### Parallel origin histories

| Question | Documented or supportable history | Traditional history |
|---|---|---|
| When did the vessel originate? | Unknown; a small old vial was associated with Saint-Remi by the ninth century. | It appeared at Clovis’s baptism, conventionally dated 496. |
| Who made it? | Unknown. | No human maker: heaven supplied it. |
| How did it reach Reims? | Possibly recovered or reinterpreted in Hincmar’s ninth-century translation of Remigius’s relics. | A snow-white dove, understood as the Holy Spirit or an angelic agent, delivered it to Remigius. |
| What did it originally contain? | An aromatic, dark-brown, resinous substance; composition untested. | Miraculous holy chrism. |
| Why was its identity asserted? | It materially linked Remigius, Clovis, Reims, and later kings. | It demonstrated divine selection of the Frankish/French monarchy and Reims’s consecrating office. |

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Fifth–sixth centuries: traditional custody only

In the legend, the dove delivered the vessel to Remigius during Clovis’s baptism. No source contemporary with Clovis documents what happened to it afterward. Any claim that Remigius preserved it continuously until his death belongs to retrospective tradition.

### Ninth century: documentation begins at Reims

Under Hincmar, the heavenly-chrism tradition became textual and visual. The putative physical vessel was associated with Remigius’s relics at the abbey later known as **Saint-Remi of Reims**. The monastery—not the cathedral—became its normal custodian.

The seam in the provenance is therefore clear:

- **Legendary chain:** heaven → dove → Remigius → Clovis’s baptism → preservation by Remigius’s church.
- **Documentable chain:** Hincmar’s ninth-century Reims → abbey of Saint-Remi and its relic treasury.

### Tenth–twelfth centuries

Flodoard transmitted the Reims tradition. The vial remained attached to the saint’s tomb and cult.

Its use before 1131 remains uncertain. The sacred balm is securely associated with Louis VII’s consecration in 1131; later in the twelfth century, the ampulla’s coronation role is more visible, including at Philip Augustus’s coronation in 1179.

### Thirteenth century: regulated ceremonial custody

The coronation ordines formalized an unusually controlled temporary transfer:

1. the vessel remained at Saint-Remi;
2. the abbot, and later the grand prior, opened the saint’s tomb or treasury;
3. the reliquary was suspended from his neck;
4. monks escorted it under a canopy;
5. four great lords served as **hostages of the Sainte Ampoule**, guaranteeing its return;
6. it traveled from Saint-Remi to Notre-Dame Cathedral;
7. the archbishop used a minute portion;
8. the object was returned to the abbey.

Thus the king never owned the ampulla. Even during a coronation, custody was divided: Saint-Remi guarded and carried it; the archbishop administered its contents; lay hostages guaranteed restitution.

### Fourteenth–fifteenth centuries

The ampulla acquired prominent monumental imagery. The Gallery of Kings on Reims Cathedral, dating mainly from the fourteenth century, placed the baptism of Clovis and the heavenly ampulla within the façade’s royal programme. [Reims Cathedral, Gallery of Kings](https://www.cathedrale-reims.fr/decouvrir/la-galerie-des-rois)

In 1429 the object was brought for **Charles VII’s coronation**, achieved after Joan of Arc’s campaign opened the route to Reims. The ampulla’s presence made the ceremony more than a crown placement: it enacted continuity with Clovis and the divinely sanctioned French kingship claimed by the Valois.

In 1483 **Louis XI**, gravely ill at Plessis-lès-Tours, ordered the ampulla brought to him. A royal *mandement* concerning its translation survives in bibliographical record. This exceptional journey is commonly described as the only removal from Saint-Remi for a purpose other than coronation. The king did not thereby acquire permanent ownership; the vessel returned to Reims. [BnF record of the 1483 mandate](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark%3A/12148/cb33810296s)

### Sixteenth–eighteenth centuries

The custody protocol continued. A surviving record from the coronation of Francis II in 1559 concerns the oath of the four noble hostages. The object’s movement remained a legal and ritual transaction, not an informal devotional outing.

At coronations the route became increasingly elaborate. The grand prior could ride a white palfrey; monks and officers accompanied him; the keys, tools, and fastenings needed to open the reliquary traveled under separate control. Disputes arose over places in the procession, canopy-bearing, horses, and the relative rights of monks and lay lords.

The ampulla was used at successive Reims coronations through that of **Louis XVI on 11 June 1775**. Louis XVI was the last pre-Revolutionary monarch anointed with the intact old vessel.

### Revolutionary confiscation and destruction, 1793

The old reliquary and vial passed into revolutionary control after the suppression of the abbey’s corporate religious life and seizure of ecclesiastical property.

On **7 October 1793**—some official summaries say 8 October—the Convention’s representative-on-mission **Philipp Jakob Rühl** had the relic brought to Place Royale in Reims. Before assembled municipal officers and a crowd, he struck and broke the vial against the pedestal of the former statue of Louis XV. The act was deliberately public and political: the material link between heaven, Clovis, sacral monarchy, and Reims was to be rendered unusable.

Rühl transmitted a procès-verbal to the National Convention. In the Convention’s published proceedings, the destruction was represented as the “eternal annihilation” of the ampulla and the apparatus belonging to that “sacred bauble.” This is the strongest contemporary evidence for the destruction of the historical vessel. [Rühl’s letter and parliamentary record](https://www.persee.fr/doc/arcpa_0000-0000_1910_num_76_1_42571_t1_0339_0000_15)

The Assemblée nationale’s biographical record adds that pieces were sent to the Convention via public courier, wrapped in a damaged new shirt intended for volunteers—a pointed example in Rühl’s report of administrative waste. [Assemblée nationale biography](https://www2.assemblee-nationale.fr/sycomore/fiche/12266?legislature=2)

The precious-metal reliquary was removed for metal recovery. Accessible accounts do not establish that identifiable components survive.

### Alleged rescues

Three overlapping rescue traditions must be separated.

1. **Jules-Armand Seraine and Philippe Hourelle:** According to later declarations, the constitutional parish priest and a municipal officer opened or accessed the relic before its public destruction and extracted part of the balm with the gold needle. Portions were allegedly entrusted to named custodians, including Bouré, curé of Berry-au-Bac, and Lecomte, a judge.

2. **Louis Champagne Prévoteau:** He allegedly retrieved two shards from the place of destruction, with balm adhering to them.

3. **Anonymous or cathedral witnesses:** Some institutional retellings state more generally that spectators collected fragments and hid them in the cathedral.

These accounts are not equivalent to an uninterrupted, scientifically documented chain of custody. They became formalized through nineteenth-century testimony, notably a procès-verbal of **25 January 1819**. The Hourelle portion was reportedly lost by his heirs. The Centre des monuments nationaux labels more than one version as a hypothesis rather than settled fact. [Palais du Tau](https://www.palais-du-tau.fr/decouvrir/le-reliquaire-de-la-sainte-ampoule)

### Restoration and reconstitution, 1819–25

In 1819 Archbishop **Jean-Charles de Coucy** commissioned Jean-Charles Cahier to make a new reliquary. It was completed in stages by 1820–22.

On **22 or 25 May 1825**, immediately before Charles X’s coronation, Archbishop **Jean-Baptiste de Latil** assembled the recognized remnants. The claimed particles and glass fragments were incorporated into fresh chrism and placed in the new rock-crystal vial. This was a liturgical reconstitution, not restoration of the shattered bottle.

On **29 May 1825**, the replacement ampulla supplied the anointing substance for Charles X. The ceremony asserted dynastic and sacramental continuity despite the object’s documented physical rupture.

After the coronation the replacement ensemble did not return permanently to Saint-Remi. It remained with the cathedral treasury, marking a shift from monastic to cathedral/state heritage custody.

### 1896 onward

The ensemble was classified as a protected historic object on **28 February 1896**. The French state is its recorded owner; it is deposited at the Palais du Tau under the care of the Centre des monuments nationaux.

### 1906 separation of container and substance

During implementation of the 1905 separation of Church and State and Cardinal **Louis-Joseph Luçon’s** expulsion from the archiepiscopal residence, Luçon feared another profanation. In 1906 he transferred the claimed balm from the rock-crystal ampulla into a small glass vial and retained it under archiepiscopal custody.

This created the present divided chain:

- **state-controlled material monument:** Cahier reliquary and empty rock-crystal vial;
- **church-controlled claimed contents:** the separate small vial held by the archbishopric.

### 1914–18 and 1937

The Palais du Tau and cathedral complex suffered severe First World War damage. Accessible collection records do not provide a detailed evacuation or conservation itinerary for the Cahier reliquary. Its survival is documented by its present existence, but the precise wartime custody chain remains to be reconstructed from unpublished or unconsulted local conservation files.

In **1937**, when the restored cathedral’s high altar was consecrated, seals placed on the ecclesiastical vial were broken and a small portion of the alleged balm was mixed with chrism for the altar’s dedication. The Palais du Tau describes this as the only subsequent use after Charles X’s coronation. It was not a royal anointing.

### Present custody

The Cahier ensemble remains in the treasury room of the Palais du Tau, Reims. The claimed balm remains separately controlled by the Archdiocese of Reims. Public sources do not document any current ownership litigation or repatriation demand.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Did the Clovis ampulla exist?

There is no contemporary evidence that it did. Gregory of Tours does not mention it; Hincmar wrote roughly three and a half centuries after the conventional date of Clovis’s baptism. The supernatural delivery must therefore be classified as a ninth-century-attested legend, not a documented fifth-century event.

Defenders historically argued that Hincmar used older Reims materials now lost and that silence in earlier narratives did not disprove an established local tradition. Critics responded that numerous writers who discuss Clovis, Remigius, or the baptism omit precisely the miracle that would have been most memorable. A seventeenth- and eighteenth-century controversy developed around these arguments. [Study of early-modern debate](https://www.persee.fr/doc/versa_1285-8412_1998_num_1_1_983)

### Was the medieval object ancient?

Possibly, but unproved. Oppenheimer’s comparison with Gallo-Roman funerary balsamaria supplies a plausible route by which a genuinely old bottle entered Remigius’s shrine. It does not demonstrate:

- that the destroyed vessel was found in Remigius’s tomb;
- that it was contemporary with Remigius;
- that it contained baptismal chrism;
- or that the object described in the seventeenth century was the same vessel present in Hincmar’s day.

No surviving fragment has a published analytical pedigree sufficient to resolve these questions.

### Was Hincmar inventor, transmitter, or identifier?

Three positions recur:

- **Traditional:** Hincmar faithfully recorded an older miracle and recognized its surviving vessel.
- **Critical:** Hincmar created or decisively shaped the legend to support Reims’s primacy and sacral kingship.
- **Intermediate:** Hincmar combined older motifs—Remigius’s miracles, miraculous oils, a dove as Holy Spirit, aromatic tomb vessels, and Clovis’s baptism—into the form subsequently regarded as ancient.

Modern scholarship generally accepts Hincmar’s decisive formative role while leaving open whether every motif originated with him.

### Is the present vial “the Sainte Ampoule”?

Materially, no: it is a nineteenth-century replacement. Institutionally and liturgically, it inherited the name because alleged remnants were placed in it. The Ministry of Culture dates it explicitly to 1820 and attributes it to Cahier’s workshop.

### Are the surviving contents authentic remnants?

The strongest positive case is cumulative testimony:

- persons claimed to have removed balm before destruction;
- a witness claimed to have collected glass with balm attached;
- declarations were recorded in 1819;
- ecclesiastical authorities examined and combined the recognized portions in 1825;
- the substance was sealed and thereafter institutionally guarded.

The strongest negative case is the break in custody:

- rescue accounts became formalized decades after 1793;
- at least one portion disappeared;
- multiple independently held portions were amalgamated;
- fresh chrism was deliberately added in 1825;
- the contents were transferred again in 1906;
- later sampling in 1937 further disturbed the sealed assemblage;
- no published chemical, radiocarbon, botanical, residue, or glass analysis confirms age or identity.

Accordingly, “the surviving balm” is an **ecclesiastically authenticated relic with a disputed documentary chain**, not a scientifically authenticated sample of a fifth-century substance.

### Scientific examination

No credible published scientific analysis of the claimed balm or alleged original shards was located in the consulted record. No radiocarbon result, spectrometric characterization, glass compositional study, or securely documented laboratory sampling appears in the official Palissy or Palais du Tau documentation.

Testing could at most characterize materials and perhaps approximate the date of glass or organic ingredients. It could not verify supernatural origin or prove use at Clovis’s baptism.

### Authority to authenticate

- **Ninth–eighteenth centuries:** abbey of Saint-Remi and archbishop of Reims.
- **Royal period:** ecclesiastical custody reinforced by coronation law and court ceremony.
- **1793:** revolutionary state claimed authority to extinguish the object’s public identity through destruction.
- **1819–25:** witnesses and the archbishopric reconstructed authenticity through sworn testimony and canonical recognition.
- **Since 1896:** the French state authenticates and protects the Cahier ensemble as a nineteenth-century historic object; the archbishopric maintains the religious identity of the alleged contents.

These are different kinds of authenticity: historical-artistic, legal, institutional, and devotional.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Preparation and transport

The old ampulla normally remained inside or near Remigius’s tomb at Saint-Remi. Keys were controlled by the abbot. On coronation day the abbey’s officers opened the shrine and prepared the reliquary.

The abbot or grand prior wore the reliquary suspended by its chain. Monks escorted it beneath a canopy from the abbey to Notre-Dame Cathedral. The four noble hostages rode or walked around it and pledged that it would be returned.

The ritual emphasized that the king did not command the relic absolutely. He received a sacramental benefit temporarily mediated by Remigius’s successor and guarded by the saint’s monastery.

### Extraction and mixing

The archbishop detached an extremely small particle of the congealed balm with the gold needle—variously compared to a grain of wheat or a small pea. He mixed it on the paten with chrism consecrated on Maundy Thursday.

This procedure accomplished two practical objectives:

- the small reserve was conserved;
- ordinary renewable chrism received the identity or efficacy attributed to the relic.

After the anointing, residue could be scraped from the paten and returned. This likely helped generate reports that the balm never diminished, although no quantitative inventory proves miraculous replenishment.

### Anointing

The king received multiple anointings. The exact number and bodily locations changed with the ordo, but commonly included the head, chest, shoulders, and joints of the arms. The rite framed the king as chosen and consecrated without making royal ordination identical to priestly ordination.

The queen did not ordinarily receive the Sainte Ampoule’s substance; she was anointed with ordinary consecrated chrism.

### Political function

The ampulla linked four claims:

1. Clovis was a providential Christian ruler.
2. French kings were continuous with Clovis.
3. divine grace entered kingship through anointing.
4. the archbishopric and city of Reims possessed the unique material means of completing that continuity.

Its political effectiveness did not depend on proving the miracle. Once institutions, ordines, processions, images, and coronations treated it as indispensable, the object shaped conduct as a legal-ritual fact.

### Access rules

Ordinary access was highly restricted:

- custody at Saint-Remi;
- keys under the abbot’s authority;
- removal chiefly for a coronation;
- opening with designated tools;
- handling by the abbot/prior and archbishop;
- lay nobles serving as guarantors, not proprietors.

Louis XI’s deathbed request was an exceptional breach or extension of normal function.

### Symbolic and heraldic career

The dove-bearing ampulla appeared:

- in manuscripts and ivory;
- in cathedral sculpture;
- on coronation prints and medals;
- in images of Clovis’s baptism;
- on the Cahier reliquary itself;
- in the insignia and retrospective mythology of groups styled after the Sainte Ampoule.

The dove made the Holy Spirit visually legible; the flask materialized divine election; Remigius represented episcopal mediation; Clovis supplied dynastic antiquity.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The heavenly delivery

**Tier:** ninth-century attested legend.

A dense crowd prevented the chrism-bearer from reaching Remigius. The bishop prayed. A dove whiter than snow descended bearing a small flask of sacred oil; an incomparable perfume filled the church. Remigius anointed Clovis.

The dove could be understood as the Holy Spirit itself, its visible symbol, or an angel in dove form. Later art often eliminated this ambiguity by giving the bird a radiant, celestial descent.

### The inexhaustible balm

**Tier:** medieval/early-modern elaboration.

Because only a minute amount was removed and unused mixture might be returned, the balm seemed never to diminish. Devotional accounts treated this as continuing miracle. No serial measurement survives to test the claim.

### The tomb-vial tradition

**Tier:** historical inference attached to legend.

One or two aromatic vessels were supposedly discovered at a translation of Remigius’s remains. The story could reconcile physical discovery with heavenly origin: the vessel delivered at the baptism had later been buried with the saint. Alternatively, an old funerary balsamarium may have inspired the identification.

### The ampulla as guarantee of French monarchy

**Tier:** political-theological tradition.

Possession of heaven-sent chrism was treated as evidence that French kings enjoyed a distinctive sacred status. Polemical writers used it to elevate France over rival monarchies and Reims over rival coronation centers, particularly Saint-Denis.

The claim did not mean that a claimant physically holding the bottle automatically became king. Legitimation arose from its use within the full coronation rite by authorized clergy.

### Survival through revolutionary destruction

**Tier:** disputed post-1793 rescue tradition.

The most consequential modern legend—or potentially true clandestine episode—is that loyal or prudent witnesses saved part of the balm before and after Rühl shattered the bottle. Unlike the heavenly-dove narrative, this has named actors and nineteenth-century documentary testimony. Yet its delayed recording, divided custody, missing portions, and deliberate admixture prevent complete verification.

The story had an evident Restoration function: it allowed Charles X’s monarchy to claim continuity across regicide, confiscation, and revolutionary iconoclasm. That political use does not by itself prove the rescue false.

### Modern “Order of the Holy Ampulla”

Claims that Clovis founded an order of chivalry associated with the ampulla are late and historically unsupported. The documented “hostages” were ceremonial guarantors at coronations, not proof of a fifth-century chivalric order.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Cahier reliquary and replacement ampulla

- **Location:** Palais du Tau, Reims, treasury room.
- **Owner:** French state.
- **Custodian/display authority:** Centre des monuments nationaux.
- **Heritage reference:** Palissy **PM51000716**.
- **Inventory/deposit number reported by Mobilier national:** **D-TAU 1905000223/4**.
- **Protection:** classified as a historic object, 28 February 1896.
- **Condition/status:** extant; the inner rock-crystal vial is no longer the container of the claimed balm.
- **Access:** museum access is subject to the Palais du Tau’s opening and exhibition arrangements.

The Palissy record was last modified on 9 June 2026 and identifies the Palais du Tau as the current conservation site. [Official collection record](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/palissy/PM51000716)

### Claimed remnants of the old contents

- **Location:** reported under the custody of the Archbishop/Archdiocese of Reims.
- **Container:** small glass vial used for Cardinal Luçon’s 1906 transfer.
- **Access:** restricted ecclesiastical custody, not ordinary museum display.
- **Identity:** disputed remnant of the 1793 substance, diluted or reconstituted with later chrism in 1825.
- **Recent scientific work:** none identified in published sources consulted here.
- **Current dispute:** no active judicial ownership or restitution proceeding located.

### What is irretrievably lost

- the intact small medieval or earlier vial;
- most of its contents;
- the complete pre-Revolutionary gold/vermeil mounting and precious-metal apparatus;
- a demonstrable, uncontaminated sample with continuous custody predating 1793.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Restoration monarchy

The Cahier reliquary was not a neutral reconstruction. Its mausoleum form, angels, royal medallions, Passion instruments, baptismal scene, Louis XVI imagery, jewels, and crowned vial turned revolutionary loss into a programme of Bourbon continuity and expiation.

Cahier undertook other Restoration commissions connected with remembrance of Louis XVI and Marie-Antoinette. His Sainte Ampoule reliquary belongs to this broader political-religious goldsmithing. [Study of Cahier](https://www.narthex.fr/dossiers/tresors-deglise/un-tresor-d2019orfevrerie-de-jean-charles-cahier-orfevre-du-roi/)

### Historical art and reproduction

The object’s public image is now usually the Cahier reliquary or an early-modern engraving of the destroyed mount—not the naked old vial. This substitution has encouraged visual conflation between:

- the plain historical bottle;
- its elaborate old casing;
- and the much larger nineteenth-century shrine.

A nineteenth-century drawing by Maquart depicting the Cahier reliquary entered the Reims museum collections and was commercially reproduced. [Musées de Reims object page](https://musees-reims.fr/oeuvre/reliquaire-de-la-sainte-ampoule-present-de-charles-x)

### 1996 and national-memory debate

The fifteenth-centenary celebrations of Clovis’s conventional baptismal date in 1996, including John Paul II’s visit to Reims, revived argument over whether Clovis and the ampulla represented Christian origins, French national origins, or a later political myth. Historians stressed the distinction between the poorly documented historical baptism and its long afterlife in national memory.

The event’s importance to the ampulla lies less in physical use—the relic was not employed for a monarch—than in renewed circulation of its imagery and claims.

### Tourism and pilgrimage

The Palais du Tau presents the Cahier reliquary as part of the coronation treasury; the cathedral and basilica provide the architectural route linking Clovis, Remigius, and French kingship. The “voie des sacres” and related interpretation turn the former custody journey into a heritage itinerary. [Modern Reims interpretive brochure](https://www.renaissancedesaintremi.fr/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Brochure_La-voie-des-sacres-BIS-1_compressed.pdf)

The object thus operates simultaneously as:

- a protected state-owned artwork;
- an emblem of French royal ritual;
- a Catholic relic tradition;
- a case study in medieval political theology;
- and a tourism symbol for Reims.

### Popular distortions

Frequently repeated but misleading claims include:

- that the extant crystal vial is the one used at Clovis’s baptism;
- that the original vial is intact;
- that scientific testing has proved fifth-century date or miraculous composition;
- that every king of France was anointed with it;
- that it was permanently kept in Reims Cathedral rather than normally at Saint-Remi;
- that the rescue of the contents is either wholly proved or conclusively disproved.

None of these formulations reflects the mixed evidence.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Primary evidence

The most important bodies of evidence are:

- Gregory of Tours’s account of Clovis, significant for its silence about the ampulla;
- Hincmar’s liturgical texts and *Vita sancti Remigii*;
- Flodoard’s *Historia Remensis ecclesiae*;
- medieval coronation ordines;
- abbey and cathedral inventories;
- coronation procès-verbaux, including hostages’ oaths;
- Louis XI’s 1483 mandate;
- early-modern descriptions by Marlot and others;
- Rühl’s 1793 procès-verbal and Convention correspondence;
- the 1819 testimony concerning alleged fragments;
- the 1825 reconstitution and coronation records;
- the extant Cahier reliquary;
- modern Palissy and Centre des monuments nationaux records.

### Major modern interpretations

**Francis Oppenheimer, *The Legend of the Sainte Ampoule* (1953):** a major English-language monograph that treated the development of the legend and proposed comparison with ancient funerary balsamaria.

**Patrick Demouy:** emphasizes coronation ritual, the object’s ordinary-looking historical form, and the gradual construction of Reims’s ceremonial monopoly.

**Jacques Le Goff and scholars of coronation ordines:** locate the ampulla within the thirteenth-century elaboration of French sacral monarchy rather than projecting the mature ceremony unchanged into the early Middle Ages.

**Jean-Claude Bonne and iconographic scholarship:** study the ninth-century ivory not simply as illustration but as an instrument joining divine grace, episcopal action, and royal authority.

### Running questions

1. Did Hincmar invent the dove-and-flask episode or transmit an earlier local account?
2. Was a genuinely ancient funerary vial found with Remigius’s remains?
3. When was the destroyed vial first treated as the specific bottle delivered at Clovis’s baptism?
4. Did Louis VII receive balm from that exact object in 1131?
5. When was the old jeweled dove reliquary manufactured and altered?
6. How much, if any, material was removed before Rühl’s destruction?
7. Can the present ecclesiastical sample be securely connected to the pre-1793 vessel?
8. Would church authorities permit non-destructive materials analysis?
9. Where are the detailed First World War movement and conservation records for the Cahier ensemble?

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- a fifth-century eyewitness record of the dove;
- an unbroken, fully documented custody chain from Clovis to the present;
- scientific proof that the balm is supernatural or incorruptible;
- survival of the original vial as the complete vessel displayed in the Palais du Tau;
- foundation by Clovis of a chivalric Order of the Sainte Ampoule.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and near-primary texts

- Hincmar of Reims, *Vita sancti Remigii*, especially the dove-and-ampulla episode, in Bruno Krusch’s *Monumenta Germaniae Historica* edition.
- Flodoard, *Historia Remensis ecclesiae*.

- Louis XI’s 1483 mandate concerning transportation of the ampulla.
- Proceedings and correspondence of the National Convention concerning Rühl’s destruction of the object.
- Jean-François Lacatte-Joltrois, *Recherches historiques sur la Sainte Ampoule* (Reims, 1825).
- Abbé d’Auribeau, *Inscriptions du nouveau et magnifique reliquaire de la Sainte Ampoule* (1825).
- Prosper Tarbé, *Notre-Dame de Reims*.

### Institutional and catalogue sources

- French Ministry of Culture, Base Palissy, PM51000716.
- Centre des monuments nationaux, Palais du Tau object history and object sheet.
- Mobilier national catalogue for Charles X’s coronation.
- Bibliothèque nationale de France catalogue.
- Catalogue collectif de France.
- Musées de Reims and cathedral interpretation records.

### Source assessment

The official modern catalogues are strongest for the Cahier reliquary’s materials, attribution, dimensions, legal status, and location. Hincmar is indispensable for the legend’s ninth-century form but cannot establish a fifth-century event. Rühl’s correspondence establishes destruction of the old object. The rescue narratives possess documentary form by 1819–25 but lack continuous contemporary custody and scientific corroboration. Devotional sources preserve details of ecclesiastical recognition but cannot independently authenticate the recovered substance.
