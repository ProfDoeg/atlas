# The Sigillum Dei Aemeth: Object Dossier

**Proposed filename:** `sigillum_dei_aemeth_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary note

“Sigillum Dei Aemeth” names three related but non-identical things:

1. A medieval ritual diagram called the *Sigillum Dei*, *sigillum Dei vivi et veri*, or “Seal of the Living and True God,” transmitted in manuscripts of the *Liber iuratus Honorii*.
2. John Dee’s substantially reworked diagram, recorded in 1582 in British Library MS Sloane 3188 and called by Dee the *Sigillum Dei*, *Sigillum Aemeth*, or *Sigillum Dei Aemeth*.
3. A group of three surviving engraved wax discs in the British Museum—one large and two small—traditionally identified as remnants of the five wax seals made for Dee’s angelic consultations.

The medieval diagram unquestionably existed as a manuscript design. Dee’s manuscript version is also securely documented. The British Museum wax discs physically exist and closely match Dee’s instructions, but an unbroken provenance from Dee to the museum has not been proved. Accordingly, this dossier distinguishes throughout between the **diagrammatic tradition**, the **documented objects prescribed and apparently used in Dee’s records**, and the **three surviving candidate objects**.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Names

The principal forms are:

- **Latin:** *Sigillum Dei* — “Seal of God.”
- **Latin:** *Sigillum Dei vivi* or *sigillum Dei vivi et veri* — “Seal of the Living [and True] God.”
- **Dee’s forms:** *Sigillum DEI*, *Sigillum AEMETH*, and *Sigillum Dei Aemeth*.
- **English:** “Seal of God,” “Seal of the Living God,” “Seal of Truth,” or “Seal of God’s Truth.”
- **Hebrew element:** אֱמֶת, *ʾemet* or *emeth*, “truth.” Dee normally Latinized or graphically represented the word as **AEMETH**. The spelling is not evidence that the entire design is an ancient Jewish artifact: its structure belongs principally to medieval Latin Christian ritual magic and Dee’s Elizabethan reconstruction of it.

Latin *sigillum* means a seal, sign, or impressed device. Here it denotes primarily an authoritative sacred figure or talisman rather than an instrument for impressing documentary wax. “Aemeth” evokes a passage quoted by Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa: the divine name *Emeth*, interpreted as “Truth,” is called the seal of God. Dee’s title therefore means approximately “Seal of God: Truth” or “Seal of the Truth of God,” not simply “Dee’s Enochian seal.”

### 1.2 Class and form

The object belongs to several overlapping classes:

- ritual-magic diagram;
- consecrated talisman or *character*;
- inscribed wax tablet;
- component of a ritual table;
- support for a crystal or other “shew-stone”;
- protective boundary between operator and invoked beings.

The commonly reproduced Dee form is an elaborate concentric figure built from:

- an outer circle divided into forty segments;
- divine and angelic names or letter-series;
- a large heptagon;
- a heptagram;
- a pentagram;
- an inner heptagon;
- planetary numeration and letter-derived names;
- a central cross or cross-like figure;
- on the physical great disc, a cross and the letters associated with **AGLA** on the reverse.

The diagram is not purely “Enochian” in the narrow modern sense. It incorporates Latin Christian divine names, Hebrew or pseudo-Hebrew elements, planetary correspondences, medieval ritual-magic geometry, and names peculiar to Dee and Edward Kelley’s recorded revelations.

### 1.3 The British Museum candidate objects

The British Museum catalogues three engraved wax discs together as **1838,1232.90.a–c**.

#### Large disc: 1838,1232.90.a

- **Material:** wax, conventionally described as beeswax.
- **Technique:** cast or formed as a thick cake and engraved on both faces.
- **Date assigned by museum:** late sixteenth century.
- **Diameter:** 23.20 cm.
- **Depth:** 3.40 cm.
- **Weight:** 1,322 g.
- **Front:** the complex Sigillum Dei Aemeth figure.
- **Reverse:** a cross incorporating or accompanied by the AGLA formula.
- **Additional identifiers:** OA.105; 1838,XVI.5; codex ID 29403.
- **Condition:** intact enough for the principal design to remain legible, but with surface wear, cracking, abrasion, discoloration, softened details, edge losses, and the deformation expected of old wax.

Its diameter is remarkably close to the prescribed nine inches: nine English inches equal 22.86 cm. Its 3.4-cm thickness is also close to Dee’s recorded prescription of an inch and “half a quarter,” normally understood as approximately 1⅛ inches or 2.86 cm. Agreement is close rather than exact, and old wax may deform.

#### Smaller discs: 1838,1232.90.b and 1838,1232.90.c

These are surviving members of a prescribed set of four tablets intended to stand beneath the four feet of the ritual table.

The two surviving examples are approximately 12–12.5 cm in diameter and about 2 cm thick. The British Museum/Google Arts & Culture record for **1838,1232.90.c** gives:

- **Diameter:** 12.00 cm.
- **Depth:** 2.00 cm.
- **Weight:** 223 g.
- **Material:** wax.
- **Technique:** engraved.

Only two of the four prescribed or formerly reported small seals now survive as identifiable complete objects. The other two are lost or may survive only among fragments once reported with the Cotton collection. No secure identification of those fragments has been published.

### 1.4 What is lost

The following parts of the original working apparatus are not securely extant:

- two of the four small supporting seals;
- Dee’s original wooden “table of practice,” prescribed as square, two cubits across, and made of “sweet wood”;
- any original covering or wrapping used with the wax seals;
- a fully secure association between the seals and any surviving crystal, mirror, lamen, or later “Dee” object.

A marble “Holy Table” in the History of Science Museum, Oxford, inventory **15449**, is not Dee’s original table. It is a later copy, probably derived from the engraving published by Meric Casaubon in 1659, and was presented to the Bodleian Library by Richard Rawlinson in 1750.

### 1.5 Inscriptions

A complete diplomatic transcription is difficult because the design encodes names by geometrical extraction rather than ordinary linear writing. Major components include:

- forty outer characters received in sequence during Dee and Kelley’s sessions;
- names derived through sevenfold planetary arrangements;
- forms associated with the seven traditional planets and their angels;
- the central word or principle **AEMETH/EMETH**, “Truth”;
- the apotropaic formula **AGLA**, conventionally expanded from Hebrew *Atah Gibor Le-olam Adonai*, “Thou art mighty forever, O Lord.”

Individual manuscript and printed reproductions differ. Dee himself introduced copying or indexing errors, and later occultists produced “corrected” versions. A modern diagram should therefore not be assumed to reproduce the inscriptions on the museum disc or Sloane 3188 exactly.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 The medieval Sigillum Dei

The oldest securely datable textual tradition is the *Liber sacratus sive iuratus*, usually called the *Liber iuratus Honorii* or *Sworn Book of Honorius*. It is pseudonymously attributed to “Honorius, son of Euclid” or Honorius of Thebes, not securely to any historical pope.

Jean-Patrice Boudet identifies British Library **Sloane MS 3854**, fols. 112ra–139ra, written on parchment in the middle of the fourteenth century, as the oldest and best principal witness. Other major witnesses include:

- Sloane MS 313, second half of the fourteenth century;
- Sloane MS 3885, sixteenth century;
- Royal MS 17 A XLII, an incomplete English translation made after 1533.

The work itself may have originated in the late thirteenth or early fourteenth century. Dating remains inferential. Its prologue presents the book as the product of a council of eighty-nine masters who supposedly condensed magical knowledge while facing ecclesiastical persecution. No independent evidence establishes that such a council met. It is a legitimating literary fiction or foundation legend.

The medieval text directs the operator to draw a circle whose diameter is three, five, seven, or nine fingers—symbolically related to the nails of Christ, Christ’s wounds, the sacraments, or the angelic orders—with five fingers said to be usual. It adds concentric circles, a cross, long letter-series, pentagonal and sevenfold geometry, divine names, and prayers. The seal belongs to a prolonged Christian theurgical program aimed ultimately at the *visio Dei*, the vision of God.

The earliest manuscripts therefore attest a **textual and drawn design**, not any surviving medieval wax object. No physical tablet from the medieval *Liber iuratus* tradition has been identified as the archetype of Dee’s museum seals.

### 2.2 Sources available to Dee

Dee owned or consulted manuscripts containing related seal traditions. Sloane MS 313 has been associated with his library and contains a medieval form of the Sigillum Dei. Related figures also circulated in versions of the *Clavicula Salomonis*, in magical miscellanies, and in works drawing upon planetary and cabalistic magic.

The seal Dee received in 1582 was therefore not created in an intellectual vacuum. Its geometry and function have recognizable medieval predecessors. What is distinctively Dee-Kelley is the detailed angelic redelivery, reorganization, naming system, and integration into their table-and-shew-stone apparatus.

### 2.3 Dee’s recorded reception, March 1582

The primary record is John Dee’s **Mysteriorum Libri Quinque**, British Library MS Sloane 3188.

On **10 March 1582**, the spirit identified through the scryer as Uriel instructed Dee to use:

- a four-square table;
- two cubits square;
- made of sweet wood;
- with the Sigillum Dei set upon it;
- and with smaller seals under its feet.

Uriel referred to a seal “already perfected in a book of thine.” This probably indicates that Dee already possessed a related design, although identifying the exact book is disputed. Some interpreters connect the remark to the *Book of Soyga*; the medieval *Liber iuratus* tradition is a more direct formal antecedent.

On **14 March**, Kelley reported seeing a perfected form of the seal. Over the sessions recorded between approximately **15 and 21 March 1582**, the being identified as Michael and a succession of forty white-clad figures supplied, explained, or corrected the characters. Dee titled the second book “The Mysteries most marvelous of Sigillum DEI, otherwise called Sigillum AEMETH.”

The instructions specify:

- purified or “perfect” wax;
- no necessary ritual significance for its color;
- a great seal nine inches in diameter;
- a circumference of about twenty-seven inches;
- thickness of one inch and “half a quarter”;
- a cross on the reverse;
- four smaller seals for the table legs;
- reverent handling and restricted viewing.

This is the documented conceptual origin of Dee’s version. The source reports the instructions as angelic communications mediated by Kelley. That supernatural attribution is a claim inside the primary source, not an independently verifiable fact.

### 2.4 Manufacture

No surviving workshop account, invoice, named wax-worker, or record of material purchase has been found. Dee may have made the tablets, supervised their manufacture, or commissioned an unnamed artisan. The close match between the great museum disc and the manuscript implies manufacture by someone with access to the design.

The surviving wax has been described macroscopically, but no published radiocarbon determination, chemical provenance study, DNA analysis, or secure isotope study has dated it to 1582. The British Museum assigns a late-sixteenth-century date stylistically and contextually. That is consistent with Dee’s lifetime but does not prove personal ownership.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 Prescribed and recorded ritual custody, 1582–1589

Dee’s manuscripts document the seal as part of his working apparatus from 1582. The normal arrangement was:

```text
shew-stone or crystal
        ↓
large wax Sigillum Dei Aemeth
        ↓
Holy Table
        ↓
four small wax seals beneath the legs
```

Dee was the owner and ritual director; Edward Kelley was the principal scryer during the best-known sessions. Other scryers and witnesses entered the broader angelic-conversation network, but the seals remained components of Dee’s equipment.

The records were begun at Mortlake. Dee and Kelley subsequently travelled through the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth and Habsburg lands, including Kraków and Prague. It is not possible to prove from the surviving object record that the three museum discs accompanied every journey. Claims that the present discs were used at every continental session exceed the evidence.

### 3.2 Dee’s return and dispersal of his possessions

Dee returned to England in 1589 and found his Mortlake library and household collections badly depleted. Books and manuscripts were removed or dispersed during his absence. Some were later traceable through Nicholas Saunder and other possessors. The surviving evidence does not identify the wax seals individually in this episode.

After Dee’s death in 1608/09, portions of his papers and library passed through poorly documented hands. Sir Robert Bruce Cotton acquired numerous Dee manuscripts, both through sales in 1625–26 and through earlier dispersals. It is plausible that the wax objects entered the Cotton collection with related papers, but no acquisition document establishes this.

**Documented gap:** approximately Dee’s death to the first certain observation in 1692. The gap is roughly eighty years if measured from the seals’ prescribed manufacture in 1582 and more than eighty years from Dee’s death to their first explicit description.

### 3.3 The Cotton manuscripts and Casaubon

In the 1650s Sir John Cotton, grandson of Sir Robert Cotton, encouraged Meric Casaubon to transcribe Dee’s deteriorating spiritual diaries. Casaubon published selections in 1659 as *A True & Faithful Relation of What Passed for Many Yeers between Dr. John Dee … and Some Spirits*.

Casaubon’s purpose was hostile to enthusiasm: he presented the records as evidence of demonic deception rather than authentic revelation. His volume nonetheless preserved and publicized Dee’s ritual world. It included an engraving of the Holy Table but did not provide a secure catalogue-level provenance for the surviving wax discs.

The often-repeated claim that the seals were demonstrably given by Dee directly to Robert Cotton is not supported by a transfer record.

### 3.4 First secure sighting: 1692

The first presently known description of physical wax instruments occurs in a letter dated **15 October 1692** from Richard Lapthorne to Richard Coffin. Visiting the Cotton Library, Lapthorne reported seeing “Dr Dee’s instruments of Conjuration, in cakes of bees wax almost petrified, with the images, lines, and figures, on it.”

This description is important because it establishes that Dee-associated engraved wax cakes were in the Cotton Library by 1692. It does not prove that they were the very cakes made in 1582, but the number, material, imagery, location, and attribution make them plausible ancestors of the museum set.

### 3.5 Cotton catalogues, 1696–late eighteenth century

The chain becomes clearer but remains imperfect:

- **1696:** early Cotton Library catalogues mention Dee’s letters and manuscripts but do not clearly inventory the wax discs.
- **1701:** Sir John Cotton’s library was transferred or bequeathed to the nation under statute.
- **1703:** Humfrey Wanley’s survey of the Cottonian charters records “3 Large Seals of Wax, one lesser and fragments of 3 others” and material lying near or upon “Dr Dee’s conjuring Table.” This is suggestive, but the count does not map neatly onto the present one-large/two-small set.
- **About 1750:** Richard Widmore recorded “two or three Cakes of Wax Markd with figures.”
- **1753:** the Cottonian Library became one of the statutory foundation collections of the British Museum.
- **Late eighteenth century:** Samuel Ayscough’s handwritten catalogue made the attribution explicit: “Three Cakes of Wax marked with magical names and numbers which were used by John Dee in his conversations with Spirits.”

The evolution of the catalogue wording matters. The earliest physical observation calls them Dee’s instruments; later entries fluctuate in number and description; only Ayscough explicitly states their alleged use in Dee’s conversations.

### 3.6 Departmental transfer, 1838

In **March 1838**, the three discs were transferred internally from the British Museum’s Department of Manuscripts to the department responsible for antiquities. Their present registration number, **1838,1232.90.a–c**, reflects this transfer, not a new purchase from a private collector.

The museum record nevertheless lists:

- **Donor:** Sir John Cotton, 3rd Baronet;
- **previous owner/collection:** Sir Robert Bruce Cotton;
- **acquisition note:** transferred from the Department of Manuscripts.

Thus “1838” is not the date at which they first entered public custody. They had already descended institutionally with the Cottonian collection.

### 3.7 Scholarly description and display

O. M. Dalton discussed “Wax discs used by Dr. Dee” in the *Proceedings of the Society of Antiquaries* in 1907, helping establish their modern antiquarian identity.

During the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the seals became display objects and exhibition loans rather than active ritual implements. Documented appearances include:

- Florence, 1980, in an exhibition connected with the history of science and Medicean Tuscany;
- British Museum, *Shakespeare: Staging the World*, 19 July–25 November 2012;
- British Museum Enlightenment Gallery, Room 1, floor case 20;
- contextual discussion in the 2016 Royal College of Physicians exhibition *Scholar, Courtier, Magician: The Lost Library of John Dee*. Some “Dee objects” in that exhibition came from other institutions; the records should not be read as proof that every object normally grouped with Dee has the same provenance.

No theft, wartime loss, commercial sale, restitution proceeding, or deaccession of the wax discs has been documented.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 What is secure

The following are documented facts:

- Three old engraved wax discs exist in the British Museum.
- They were in the British Museum’s manuscript collections before their 1838 departmental transfer.
- Engraved wax instruments attributed to Dee were seen in the Cotton Library in 1692.
- Their dimensions and designs closely correspond to instructions and diagrams in Dee’s 1582 manuscript.
- Their physical type fits the prescribed arrangement of one great seal and smaller table-foot seals.
- The museum dates the great disc to the late sixteenth century.

### 4.2 What is not secure

The following remain unproved:

- that Dee personally made or commissioned these exact discs;
- that Kelley saw visions over this particular surviving great disc;
- that the present two small tablets are two of the original four;
- that the discs passed directly from Dee to Sir Robert Cotton;
- that they accompanied Dee to Kraków, Prague, or Třeboň;
- that all three surviving discs were manufactured at the same time;
- that no later copy or substitution entered the Cotton collection.

### 4.3 The strongest case for authenticity

Silke Ackermann and Louise Devoy’s 2012 study judged the wax seals to have the strongest potential connection to Dee among the British Museum objects traditionally associated with him. Supporting evidence includes:

- near agreement between the great disc’s 23.2-cm diameter and the prescribed nine inches;
- close agreement in thickness;
- the appropriate larger-and-smaller grouping;
- close correspondence between the engravings and Sloane 3188;
- the AGLA cross on the reverse;
- a Cotton Library sighting by 1692;
- Cotton’s documented acquisition of numerous Dee manuscripts.

Taken together, these make a late-sixteenth-century origin within or near Dee’s circle plausible.

### 4.4 The strongest case for caution

The cautions are substantial:

- the first secure physical description is 110 years after the 1582 instructions;
- no contemporary receipt, inventory, maker’s account, or letter identifies the present discs;
- Cotton’s early manuscript catalogues fail to list them clearly;
- the reported number of whole and fragmentary discs changes;
- the objects could have been made from Dee’s surviving diagrams after his death;
- “correspondence” to a design demonstrates access to that design, not necessarily ritual use by Dee;
- the museum’s older display language sometimes stated Dee’s ownership more firmly than the scholarly evidence permits.

The responsible conclusion is therefore **probable or plausible association, not demonstrated identity**.

### 4.5 Scientific examination

The published object study compares dimensions, workmanship, designs, manuscripts, collecting history, and archival descriptions. No conclusive laboratory dating of the wax has been published. Beeswax radiocarbon dating would also face sampling and contamination problems and could at best establish the wax’s age range, not its maker or ritual use.

No maker’s mark is reported. The engravings themselves are the principal forensic link.

### 4.6 Rival objects and replicas

No rival museum object presently has a comparably strong claim to be Dee’s great wax Sigillum. Rivalry instead arises from:

- multiple manuscript drawings;
- reconstructions correcting Dee’s apparent transcription errors;
- modern wax, wood, metal, resin, and printed replicas;
- confusion between the wax seal, the Holy Table, the gold “Vision of the Four Castles” disc, and Dee-associated mirrors or crystals.

The British Museum’s gold disc **1942,0506.1** is not the Sigillum Dei Aemeth. It carries a different design based on a 1584 vision and may have been made much later. Likewise, the obsidian mirror and crystal balls grouped with Dee have separate and less secure provenances.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Medieval function

In the *Liber iuratus*, the seal is embedded in a Christian theurgical operation culminating in the vision of God. It does not function as a free-standing decorative emblem. Its preparation is coordinated with:

- purification and confession;
- fasting;
- prayers and invocations;
- consecration;
- divine names;
- ritual circles;
- regulation of the operator’s conduct.

The text attributes extraordinary power to the correctly prepared seal and its associated rites, including authority over created beings below the highest angelic ranks. These are claims made by the ritual text, not experimentally established effects.

### 6.2 Dee’s apparatus

In Dee’s reconstructed system, the seal’s material placement was central:

- the great wax seal lay at the center of the Holy Table;
- the shew-stone or crystal rested upon it;
- four smaller seals supported or protected the table’s legs;
- the structure established a sacred, ordered, and protected ritual space.

The great tablet was thus simultaneously an image, base, protective talisman, and material interface for visionary work.

### 6.3 Rules of manufacture and access

The communications prescribe “perfect,” meaning purified, wax. Color was said not to matter. The object was to be approached with reverence and devotion and not treated as an ordinary drawing. Later occult instructions often amplify this into prohibitions against casual sight or touch, but not every modern taboo is explicitly present in Dee’s earliest text.

No evidence shows that possession of the physical seal conferred political office, sovereignty, or ecclesiastical authority. Its legitimating function was intellectual and spiritual: it represented authorization from the beings Dee believed he consulted and helped distinguish his operations from illicit or merely coercive conjuration.

### 6.4 Documented use versus prescribed use

The diaries securely document the seal as prescribed equipment within Dee’s sessions. They do not supply a modern laboratory-style log identifying today’s museum disc by an inventory number. Consequently:

- **Attested practice:** a seal of this design was integrated into Dee’s working system.
- **Strong inference:** wax seals were made to the instructions.
- **Unresolved identity:** the surviving discs may be those instruments but cannot be proved to be them.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 Angelic revelation

**Tier: contemporaneously recorded visionary claim.**

The central story is that angels, principally Uriel and Michael, revealed, perfected, and interpreted the Sigillum Dei Aemeth through Kelley in March 1582. Forty beings supplied its outer characters.

The record is contemporary or near-contemporary and written by Dee. It proves that Dee recorded and apparently believed or seriously entertained this process. It cannot independently prove the beings’ ontological identity.

### 7.2 Adamic and Enochian antiquity

**Tier: ritual-text tradition and later esoteric elaboration.**

Dee’s angelic records connect portions of his wisdom tradition with Adam, Paradise, Enoch, and primordial divine revelation. Later practitioners consequently describe the seal as “Enochian,” sometimes implying that its exact geometry originated with the biblical Enoch.

The historical record instead shows:

- medieval Latin predecessors;
- access by Dee to manuscript magic;
- a distinct 1582 reconfiguration;
- later classification of the whole Dee-Kelley corpus as “Enochian magic.”

No ancient Hebrew, Second Temple, or early Christian artifact bearing Dee’s exact seal has been found.

### 7.3 The eighty-nine-magicians council

**Tier: medieval foundation legend.**

The *Liber iuratus* claims that eighty-nine masters, threatened by ecclesiastical suppression, assembled and entrusted their wisdom to Honorius. No documentary evidence corroborates this council. The story authorizes secrecy, oath-bound transmission, and the compression of supposedly ancient wisdom into one book.

### 7.4 “The original” museum seal

**Tier: institutional tradition resting on substantial but incomplete evidence.**

Popular captions, photographs, and commercial replicas frequently call 1838,1232.90.a “the original Sigillum used by John Dee and Edward Kelley.” The phrase collapses a genuine but incomplete provenance into certainty. The accurate formulation is: **the surviving great wax seal traditionally associated with Dee and closely matching his 1582 manuscript instructions**.

### 7.5 Protective or supernatural efficacy

**Tier: ritual claim and continuing practitioner belief.**

Medieval and modern texts attribute protection, angelic access, spiritual authority, and cosmological harmony to the seal. Modern practitioners reproduce it for invocation, altar work, meditation, jewelry, tattoos, and personal protection. These beliefs are historically real as practices and reception, but their claimed supernatural effects are not established by the object’s provenance.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 From discrediting evidence to cultural treasure

Casaubon’s 1659 publication sought to portray Dee as deceived by spirits. It unintentionally made the angelic records accessible and furnished later occultists with a textual corpus. The seal’s reception consequently changed several times:

- seventeenth century: evidence in debates over spirits, enthusiasm, and demonic deception;
- eighteenth century: cabinet curiosity and residue of “conjuration” in the Cottonian collection;
- nineteenth century: antiquarian artifact and source for the occult revival;
- twentieth century: central emblem of “Enochian magic”;
- twenty-first century: museum object, commercial occult image, tattoo, digital graphic, and popular symbol of Dee.

### 9.2 Golden Dawn and Thelema

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn incorporated Dee-derived angelic materials into its late-nineteenth-century synthesis. Golden Dawn manuscripts and later publications recast the seal within a graded initiatory and magical curriculum.

Aleister Crowley’s publication of Golden Dawn-derived material in *The Equinox* helped detach Dee’s imagery from restricted manuscript circulation. Crowley and later Thelemic practitioners used versions of the Sigillum Dei Aemeth in connection with angelic invocation and Enochian workings.

These modern versions are not exact neutral copies. They may normalize letters, repair perceived errors, alter line weights, regularize geometry, or reinterpret the planetary structure.

### 9.3 Modern editions and reconstructions

Important modern transmitters include:

- O. M. Dalton’s 1907 discussion of the wax discs;
- Israel Regardie’s publication of Golden Dawn materials;
- modern editions of Dee’s journals;
- Deborah Harkness’s historical study of the angel conversations;
- Joseph H. Peterson’s transcription and edition of the *Five Books of Mystery*;
- Colin D. Campbell’s monograph on the seal;
- digital reconstructions by Clay Holden and others.

Because Dee’s own notes contain ambiguities and apparent copying errors, “corrected” seals embody editorial decisions. A geometrically tidy online image may therefore be further from the physical museum disc than a less polished manuscript facsimile.

### 9.4 Museum and exhibition culture

The three discs are commonly displayed beside a crystal ball, obsidian mirror, and gold disc. This grouping powerfully constructs the public image of “Dr Dee’s magic,” although the objects have different provenance strengths.

Ackermann and Devoy observed that the seals have the best potential connection to Dee within the British Museum group, while a direct link remains unproved. Contextual exhibitions, particularly the Royal College of Physicians’ 2016 *Scholar, Courtier, Magician*, have increasingly presented Dee’s magic alongside his mathematics, astronomy, collecting, medicine, and court service.

### 9.5 Popular culture and commerce

The seal now appears in:

- fantasy and occult fiction;
- television, games, album art, and graphic design;
- commercially manufactured altar tablets;
- engraved metal pendants;
- laser-cut wood and acrylic copies;
- resin or colored-wax reproductions;
- posters, clothing, and tattoos.

A frequent modern invention is the claim that the familiar exact design is an immemorial “ancient Hebrew” or “Atlantean” symbol. The securely documented genealogy is medieval Latin Christian ritual magic followed by Dee’s sixteenth-century transformation.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Primary evidence

The essential primary witnesses are:

- British Library Sloane MS 3854, the oldest major Latin witness of the *Liber iuratus*;
- Sloane MS 313, containing a medieval seal diagram and associated with Dee’s book collection;
- Sloane MS 3188, Dee’s *Mysteriorum Libri Quinque*, especially fol. 30r;
- Cotton Appendix XLVI, containing later angelic-conversation records;
- Casaubon’s 1659 *True & Faithful Relation*;
- Richard Lapthorne’s letter of 15 October 1692;
- Humfrey Wanley’s 1703 Cotton catalogue, Harley MS 7647;
- Richard Widmore’s catalogue notes;
- Samuel Ayscough’s British Museum catalogue;
- the three wax objects themselves.

### 10.2 Critical study of the medieval tradition

Gösta Hedegård’s 2002 critical edition, *Liber Iuratus Honorii*, established a modern Latin text from the principal British Library manuscripts. Boudet’s 2002 study situated the work within medieval theurgy, angelology, and the pursuit of the beatific vision.

Major problems remain:

- the exact date and place of composition;
- the relation among Latin and English witnesses;
- the sources of its divine-name sequences;
- whether its purported council reflects any real network;
- the direction and date of transmission into related Solomonic traditions.

### 10.3 Study of Dee’s ritual system

The decisive modern shift has been away from treating Dee’s angelic work as an embarrassing aberration. Scholars including Frances Yates, Peter French, Deborah Harkness, Stephen Clucas, Nicholas Clulee, and Egil Asprem have examined it as part of early modern natural philosophy, Christian cabala, apocalypticism, court culture, and ritual practice.

This does not settle whether Kelley consciously fabricated material, experienced visions sincerely, or combined performance with belief. Those psychological questions cannot authenticate the wax discs.

### 10.4 Object biography

Ackermann and Devoy’s 2012 article remains the principal object-centered study. Its central conclusion is deliberately limited: comparison of the museum artifacts with Dee’s manuscripts gives the wax seals the strongest potential link to him, but **direct association remains to be proved**.

That conclusion corrects two popular extremes:

- categorical museum-romance claims that the seals are unquestionably Dee’s originals;
- categorical debunking claims that they are known modern forgeries.

The evidence supports neither certainty.

### 10.5 Errors that persist

Several recurrent claims should be rejected or qualified:

- **“The seal is ancient Hebrew.”** Incorrect as a historical description of the complete figure.
- **“Enoch made this exact design.”** A religious/esoteric claim without ancient documentary support.
- **“The British Museum scientifically proved Dee made it.”** No such conclusive analysis is published.
- **“The wax seals came from Hans Sloane.”** The institutional record points principally to the Cottonian collection; confusion arises because Sloane manuscripts preserve Dee material and Sloane’s collection also founded the museum.
- **“MS Sloane 1388 contains the design.”** This number appears in some museum-derived text, but the standard manuscript reference is **Sloane 3188**.
- **“Kircher reproduced it in 1562.”** Athanasius Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* was published in the 1650s; “1562” is a transposed or repeated modern error.
- **“All five seals survive.”** Only one great and two small discs are presently catalogued together.
- **“The gold disc is another Sigillum Dei Aemeth.”** It is a separate object with the “Vision of the Four Castles.”
