# The bells of Santiago Cathedral: Object Dossier

**Suggested filename:** `the_bells_of_santiago_cathedral_object_dossier.md`

## Evidentiary key

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary or near-contemporary evidence independent of the developed bell legend.
- **Medieval attestation:** a medieval source makes the claim, without implying that the narrated event occurred.
- **Scholarly inference:** a reasoned reconstruction not directly established by surviving physical or documentary evidence.
- **Institutional tradition:** a cathedral, museum, municipal, or heritage body presents the claim as part of institutional history.
- **Legend:** a narrative first recorded substantially after the alleged event, or containing demonstrably fabulous elements.
- **Unresolved:** surviving evidence does not permit a conclusion.

The central finding is stark: **no bell or lamp presently preserved at Santiago de Compostela or Córdoba has been authenticated as one of “the bells of Santiago” allegedly seized in 997.** Their dimensions, number, inscriptions, founders, alloy, and ultimate physical fate are unknown. What survives securely is a developing sequence of medieval texts. Those texts disagree about whether the bells were returned immediately, sent to Mecca, installed in Córdoba, transformed into lamps, restored to Santiago in 1236, or otherwise disposed of. Dana E. Katz’s object-centered study accordingly describes the bells as **nonextant** and treats their “conversion” primarily as a powerful historiographical and material-religious narrative [S1].

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Object class and scope

“The bells of Santiago Cathedral” are a purported group of early medieval church bells associated with the pre-Romanesque basilica of Saint James at Compostela. According to later medieval narratives, forces commanded by Muḥammad ibn Abī ʿĀmir al-Manṣūr—Almanzor—removed them during the sack of Compostela in 997. The developed thirteenth-century form of the story says that they were transported to Córdoba, inverted or converted into mosque lamps, and returned to Santiago by Ferdinand III after Córdoba’s capitulation in 1236.

They are commonly called:

- Spanish: **las campanas de Santiago**, **las campanas de la catedral de Santiago**, **las campanas de Compostela**.
- Galician: **as campás de Santiago**, **as campás da catedral de Santiago**, **as campás de Compostela**.
- Medieval Latin formulations include **campanae ecclesiae Sancti Iacobi**, “the bells of the church of Saint James,” with variations in case and word order.
- Alfonso X’s vernacular historiographical tradition calls them **las campanas menores**, “the lesser bells.”
- Modern English scholarship generally uses **the bells of Santiago** or **the bells of Santiago de Compostela**.

They are not “relics” in the narrow canonical sense of bodily remains or contact relics. Medieval sources present them as church furnishings, booty, trophies, lamps, and instruments of symbolic humiliation and restitution. Their proximity to the apostolic shrine and their place in the legend give them a relic-like cultural status, but no located medieval source formally classifies them as relics.

### 1.2 What can be said physically

No securely identified component survives. Consequently:

| Attribute | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|
| Number | Unknown. Sources use a plural; no reliable count is supplied. |
| Dimensions | Unknown. |
| Weight | Unknown. |
| Founder or workshop | Unknown. |
| Date of casting | Unknown; if real and present during the 997 attack, necessarily no later than that event. |
| Inscriptions | None recorded. |
| Decoration | None recorded. |
| Alloy | Not analyzed. Bronze or another copper alloy is plausible for early medieval bells but remains an inference, not an established fact about these objects. |
| Clappers, yokes, or suspension fittings | Not described. |
| Repairs or recastings before 997 | Not documented. |
| Present condition | Nonextant or unidentified. |
| Museum or cathedral inventory number | None. |

The earliest relevant narrative, Book IV, chapter 25 of the *Liber Sancti Iacobi* or *Codex Calixtinus*, mentions “the codices, silver tables, bells, and other ornaments” removed from the basilica. It gives no number, form, measurements, metal, inscription, or manufacture date [S2].

The later Alfonsine *Estoria de España* specifies “lesser bells.” That adjective is an important textual variant but cannot safely be converted into measurements. It may distinguish them from a principal bell, emphasize portability, or simply reflect the redactor’s imaginative elaboration.

### 1.3 Bell-to-lamp transformation

The sources do not describe a technically coherent manufacturing operation.

Three materially different possibilities became blurred in later retellings:

1. **Inversion without remanufacture:** intact bells were hung or stood mouth-up and used as lamp basins.
2. **Adaptation:** handles, clappers, or fittings were removed and oil-lighting apparatus installed.
3. **Melting and recasting:** bell metal was remade into separate lamps.

Medieval formulations commonly say that the bells were placed or hung in the mosque “serving as lamps.” Modern accounts often sharpen this into “they were melted down” or “lamps were fabricated from them.” Those statements are not equivalent. If the bells were melted into new vessels, the claim that Ferdinand returned “the same bells” would require another recasting, which no early text explains. Katz emphasizes precisely this instability: the narrative asserts continuity of identity while imagining radical change in form and religious use [S1].

No lamp in the Mosque-Cathedral of Córdoba has been identified archaeologically, epigraphically, or metallurgically as one of these objects. The medieval mosque possessed many lamps, but the existence of lamps there does not establish a Compostelan origin.

### 1.4 Objects explicitly excluded

The famous **Berenguela** and the other surviving or replaced bells of the present cathedral are not candidates.

The documented original Berenguela now in the cathedral cloister was cast by Pedro de Güemes in 1729. The inventory records:

- diameter: 254 cm;
- height: 195 cm;
- lip thickness: 25 cm;
- estimated weight: 6,211 kg;
- founder: Pedro de Güemes;
- casting date: 24 July 1729;
- use: striking the hours;
- condition: vertically cracked, reportedly after corrosion expanded the clapper staple;
- subsequent intervention: an internal profile mould was taken by Eijsbouts for a reproduction, and a metal sample was removed for compositional analysis [S3].

Its extensive Spanish and Latin inscriptions, Marian, Eucharistic, royal, episcopal, and Jacobean imagery, and explicit 1729 date rule out identity with any object allegedly carried away in 997 or returned in 1236.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Possible physical origin

If actual bells were present in the basilica destroyed in 997, they belonged to the pre-Romanesque sanctuary developed before the existing Romanesque cathedral. No surviving casting contract, payment, consecration rite, inventory, founder’s inscription, or archaeological find securely documents them.

The *Liber Sancti Iacobi* also tells, in its legendary Carolingian history, that Charlemagne endowed the basilica with bells, textiles, books, and ornaments. This is not evidence of an eighth- or ninth-century commission. Book IV is the Pseudo-Turpin, a twelfth-century work presenting fictive Carolingian history. Its account belongs to Compostela’s construction of an apostolic and imperial past [S2, S4].

Thus two purported origins must be separated:

- **Physical possibility:** a pre-997 basilica likely possessed some means of liturgical signaling, possibly bells.
- **Legendary commission:** Charlemagne supplied bells to the shrine. This appears in the Pseudo-Turpin and is not independently documented.

### 2.2 Earliest surviving textual attestation of the removal

The earliest located developed witness is not contemporary with 997. It is the **Pseudo-Turpin Chronicle**, incorporated as Book IV of the *Liber Sancti Iacobi*, surviving in the mid-twelfth-century Codex Calixtinus.

Chapter 25 states that Almanzor:

- reached Santiago;
- forcibly took what he found;
- devastated the basilica;
- removed codices, silver tables, bells, and other ornaments;
- suffered blindness and intestinal affliction with his soldiers as divine punishment;
- promised the Christian God and Saint James that he would return everything;
- restored the goods **doubled** after fifteen days;
- withdrew praising the power of the Christian God and Saint James [S2].

This earliest narrative therefore **does not contain the later, familiar custody history**. It does not send the bells to Córdoba or turn them into lamps. On the contrary, it says that the removed property was returned, doubled, within fifteen days.

Its miraculous content and chronological distance make it evidence for the legend’s existence in the twelfth century, not direct proof of the bells’ 997 journey.

### 2.3 The *Chronica Naierensis*

The *Chronica Naierensis*, composed in the later twelfth century, gives a different destination: Almanzor sent the bells to **Mecca**, for the dishonor of Christ and Saint James, and had them placed like lamps before an image of his god.

This version presents several problems:

- Mecca was geographically and politically distinct from Córdoba.
- The description reflects Christian polemical misunderstanding of Islamic worship, including the premise of a divine image before which the lamps could be placed.
- It contradicts the Pseudo-Turpin’s immediate restitution.
- It supplies no independently checkable transfer record.

Nevertheless, it is crucial evidence for the development of the object-story: by the late twelfth century, the bells had become enduring trophies placed in an Islamic sacred center rather than temporarily stolen furnishings [S5].

### 2.4 Contemporary or near-contemporary evidence for the 997 attack

Arabic and Christian historical traditions securely support a devastating campaign against Compostela in 997. Accounts associated with Ibn ʿIdhārī’s *al-Bayān al-Mughrib* and the *Dhikr bilād al-Andalus* describe destruction, plunder, captives, and the campaign’s political-military setting. Their testimony supports the sack but does not by itself authenticate the developed bell-lamp-return narrative.

Some modern summaries state that the Arabic sources explicitly mention the bells. Other source-critical treatments observe that no contemporary Arabic report supplies the familiar Córdoba-lamp episode. The distinction matters:

- **Attack, destruction, and plunder:** strongly attested.
- **Removal of specific cathedral bells:** first clearly attested in later Christian literary tradition.
- **Installation as lamps in Córdoba:** thirteenth-century or later narrative development.
- **Return in 1236:** asserted by thirteenth-century Christian chroniclers, but unsupported by a surviving delivery receipt, cathedral inventory, or identifiable bell.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Before 997: Santiago de Compostela

**Possible physical custody; undocumented object identity.** If the bells existed, their keeper was the clergy of the basilica of Saint James. No individual bells can be reconstructed. There is no known inventory linking a named or described bell to the later story.

### 997: seizure during Almanzor’s campaign

**Event context documented; specific bell seizure later attested.** Almanzor’s forces attacked Compostela in August 997, plundering and destroying much of the sanctuary complex. The bells’ alleged removal is recorded only in later narratives.

The commonly repeated detail that Christian captives bore the bells on their shoulders to Córdoba is not established by a contemporary transport record. It functions as the first half of a later symmetrical humiliation-and-restitution story.

### Pseudo-Turpin version: return within fifteen days

**Twelfth-century legend.** Book IV, chapter 25 says Almanzor returned twice what he had stolen after miraculous punishment. In this version the chain is:

Santiago basilica → Almanzor’s possession in Galicia → restitution to Santiago after fifteen days.

There is no Córdoba phase [S2].

### *Chronica Naierensis* version: dispatch to Mecca

**Late-twelfth-century legend.** The chain becomes:

Santiago → Almanzor → Mecca → installation as lamps.

No return is described in the passage, and the later Córdoba recovery cannot be joined to it without silently changing Mecca into Córdoba.

### Early thirteenth century: Lucas of Tuy

Lucas of Tuy’s *Chronicon mundi*, completed around 1236, develops the Córdoba restitution story. The relevant formulation states that the Catholic king Ferdinand caused the bells to be carried back to Saint James on Saracen shoulders: **“et rex catholicus Fernandus fecit eas Sarracenorum humeris ad ecclesiam sancti Iacobi reportari.”**

Lucas is roughly contemporary with Córdoba’s conquest, making his statement much closer to the alleged restitution than the earlier sources were to the 997 seizure. But proximity does not solve the identity problem:

- he provides no inventory or physical description;
- there is no route or arrival date;
- no cathedral receipt is cited;
- no surviving bell is traceable to the delivery;
- his work incorporates and expands a miracle-laden historical tradition.

### 1236–1243: Rodrigo Jiménez de Rada

Rodrigo Jiménez de Rada’s *Historia de rebus Hispaniae* gives the developed symmetrical account. Almanzor had taken the bells to Córdoba and placed them in the mosque as lamps to shame Christianity; Ferdinand III had the same bells returned to Santiago, carried by Muslims as Christians had previously carried them south [S6].

The account is significant because Rodrigo was a leading churchman and royal political actor close to Ferdinand III. It is still a historiographical narrative rather than an administrative custody record. Rodrigo’s presentation integrates the bells into the Christian purification of the mosque and the reversal of conquest.

The object-chain claimed by Rodrigo is:

Santiago → seizure by Almanzor → transport by Christians → Great Mosque of Córdoba → use as lamps → seizure or recovery by Ferdinand III → transport by Muslims → Santiago.

### *Chronica latina regum Castellae*

The *Chronica latina regum Castellae*, probably associated with Juan of Soria, is a major near-contemporary source for Córdoba’s capitulation and the mosque’s consecration. It describes the purification, holy-water aspersion, first Mass, royal entry, and institutional reorganization. Its evidentiary relationship to the bell episode requires care: the account of the conquest is strong contemporary context, but the most elaborated bell restitution comes from Lucas and Rodrigo and then from vernacular compilations [S7].

### Late thirteenth century: the Alfonsine *Estoria de España*

The history project initiated under Alfonso X and continued under later redactors consolidates the story for a vernacular courtly audience. Its account calls the objects the **lesser bells**, says Almanzor took them as a sign of victory, and says he placed them as lamps in Córdoba’s mosque. The later conquest narrative repeats their restitution.

This version fuses earlier strands:

- actual 997 devastation;
- bells as selected trophy;
- Córdoba replacing the *Naierensis*’s Mecca;
- lamp use;
- symmetrical forced porterage;
- Ferdinand III’s restoration of Christian order.

### After the alleged return

Here the physical chain breaks completely.

No securely cited medieval Compostelan inventory identifies returned “Almanzor bells.” No bell now at Santiago has a continuous provenance from 1236. No documented medieval recasting connects their metal to a later named bell. No fragments, clappers, yokes, lamp chains, moulds, inscriptions, or metallurgical samples have been accepted as theirs.

Possible physical fates include:

- the bells never existed as described;
- bells were seized in 997 but never reached Córdoba;
- bells reached Córdoba but were melted or lost before 1236;
- objects identified in 1236 as the bells were returned but later broke and were recast;
- other mosque lamps or metal objects were ceremonially identified as the bells;
- the return story was literary and no objects traveled north in 1236.

None can presently be established.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Did bells exist in the 997 basilica?

**Plausible but unproved for this particular set.** Churches used bells in early medieval western Europe, and a major shrine could have possessed them. The absence of a contemporary inventory prevents identification of number, form, and date.

### 4.2 Were they seized in 997?

**Possible; first clearly narrated substantially later.** The campaign and plunder are secure. The earliest detailed bell narrative appears in a twelfth-century legendary work and says the objects were promptly restored. That weakens confidence in the developed Córdoba chain.

### 4.3 Mecca or Córdoba?

The traditions are incompatible:

- *Chronica Naierensis*: Mecca.
- Pseudo-Turpin: returned in Galicia after fifteen days.
- Lucas of Tuy, Jiménez de Rada, and the Alfonsine tradition: Córdoba and eventual restitution.

“Mecca” may be a polemical universalization of Islam’s sacred center, later rationalized into Córdoba, the actual capital from which Almanzor ruled. That is a plausible literary-development hypothesis, not a documented correction by an eyewitness.

### 4.4 Were the bells intact lamps or lamp metal?

Unresolved. Medieval verbs and later translations vary among placing, hanging, serving as lamps, making lamps from them, and converting them into lamps. No technical description establishes whether they remained recognizable bells.

### 4.5 Were the objects allegedly returned in 1236 the same objects?

Unresolved and probably untestable without a surviving candidate. The word “same” in Rodrigo’s account is a claim of narrative identity. There is no serial inscription, inventory correlation, distinctive damage description, or scientific test supporting it.

### 4.6 Were they actually returned?

The return is a **thirteenth-century chroniclers’ claim**, not a modern invention. Lucas and Rodrigo wrote close to Ferdinand III’s reign, so it cannot simply be dismissed as recent folklore. Yet no located administrative or cathedral document closes the custody chain.

The strongest case **for** a historical restitution is:

- multiple thirteenth-century Christian narratives;
- the episode appears in sources close to the royal court;
- trophy bells and the reuse of captured bells as lamps are materially plausible elsewhere in medieval Iberia;
- the action fits the documented consecration of the mosque after conquest.

The strongest case **against certainty** is:

- no contemporary evidence for the original transport to Córdoba;
- the earliest versions contradict the Córdoba story;
- the narrative is highly symmetrical and politically useful;
- no identifiable returned object survives;
- no known receipt or early Compostelan inventory verifies arrival;
- the texts reproduce misunderstandings and polemical stereotypes about Islam.

### 4.7 Scientific examination

None has been performed on “the bells of Almanzor,” because there is no accepted physical candidate. There is consequently:

- no radiometric or thermoluminescence dating;
- no metallographic study;
- no lead-isotope provenance analysis;
- no alloy comparison between Santiago and Córdoba objects;
- no residue analysis for lamp oil;
- no epigraphic examination;
- no authenticated medieval sample.

The sampled 1729 Berenguela is a separate object [S3].

### 4.8 Authority to authenticate

In the Middle Ages, narrative authentication rested with chroniclers, royal historiography, and ecclesiastical authority. Modern authenticity would require convergence among archival provenance, campanological study, archaeology, epigraphy, and materials science. No cathedral declaration can by itself establish material continuity, and neither Santiago nor Córdoba presently publishes an authenticated candidate.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Original liturgical function

No source records the bells’ actual ringing schedule. As cathedral or basilica bells, they may have:

- summoned clergy and worshippers;
- marked offices, Masses, vigils, feasts, deaths, emergencies, or civic time;
- projected the Christian shrine’s acoustic authority over Compostela.

These are comparative possibilities, not documented uses of the particular bells.

### 6.2 Silencing as conquest

The removal of bells has an evident symbolic function in medieval religious conflict: taking them silences the defeated sacred institution and removes a public Christian sound from the landscape. Katz interprets the Santiago story as an account of material and religious “conversion”: objects formerly activated by Christian sound become silent supports for Islamic light [S1].

### 6.3 Lamp use

In the developed narrative, turning the bells into mosque lamps reverses their orientation, sensory medium, and institutional allegiance:

- sound becomes light;
- moving or struck objects become stationary vessels;
- Christian liturgical instruments serve an Islamic prayer space;
- trophies announce Almanzor’s victory without needing to be rung.

The idea is symbolically exact even though the technical process is unknown.

### 6.4 1236 restitution ritual

The alleged recovery is attached to the documented Christian dedication of Córdoba’s congregational mosque. Cross and royal banner were raised; the building was purified with blessed water; an altar to the Virgin was established; Mass and the *Te Deum* marked the transfer of power [S7].

Within that setting, the bells’ return functions as:

- reversal of Almanzor’s victory;
- restitution to Saint James;
- legitimation of Ferdinand III;
- conversion of an Islamic sacred building into a Christian cathedral;
- humiliation of Córdoba’s defeated Muslims through forced porterage;
- completion of a providential historical cycle.

The forced carrying of sacred trophies by religiously defined captives should be reported as an act of domination in the narrative, whether or not it occurred physically.

### 6.5 Access and handling rules

No surviving source establishes formal taboos governing who could touch the bells. The tradition itself emphasizes compelled handling by captives rather than privileged clerical access.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The Pseudo-Turpin punishment and restitution

**Tier: early-attested twelfth-century legend.**

After the basilica is profaned, Almanzor’s soldiers suffer diarrhea and blindness. Almanzor himself becomes ill and blind. A captive cathedral priest counsels him to invoke the Christian God. Almanzor promises to renounce Muḥammad, cease attacking Saint James’s land, and return what he stole. His health is restored, and after fifteen days he returns the property doubled [S2].

This story turns the bells into evidence of Saint James’s territorial power. It also contradicts the later belief that they remained in Islamic custody for more than two centuries.

### 7.2 The Mecca lamp story

**Tier: late-twelfth-century elaboration.**

The *Chronica Naierensis* says the bells were sent to Mecca and displayed like lamps before an image of the Muslim deity. The story intensifies the desecration by transporting the objects to Islam’s holiest city. Its imagined cult image reflects Christian polemic, not Islamic practice [S5].

### 7.3 The Córdoba lamp story

**Tier: thirteenth-century consolidation.**

Mecca is replaced by Córdoba, Almanzor’s actual political center. The bells are placed in the congregational mosque. This localization makes their recovery by Ferdinand III possible.

### 7.4 The symmetrical burden

**Tier: thirteenth-century triumphal elaboration.**

Christian captives carry the bells south in 997; Muslim captives carry them north in 1236. The perfect reversal is the story’s most memorable feature and also a reason for historical caution. It dramatizes providential justice in bodily and geographic form.

### 7.5 “The same bells rang again”

**Tier: later traditional inference.**

Popular accounts often imply that the returned bells resumed ringing at Santiago. The medieval restitution claim does not provide a documented installation, consecration, first ringing, or later inventory. The statement therefore exceeds the evidence.

### 7.6 Confusion with Berenguela

**Tier: modern popular conflation.**

Visitors sometimes associate the present monumental Berenguela with Almanzor’s booty. The existing original Berenguela was cast in 1729 and is materially unrelated. Its tower and clock history belong to a much later cathedral [S3].

### 7.7 Historical effects of the legends

Even if no bells traveled as narrated, the legend had material consequences:

- it shaped royal and ecclesiastical memory of the conquest of Córdoba;
- it linked the two cathedrals in a shared story of appropriation and restitution;
- it supplied painters, writers, heritage interpreters, and tourism with a vivid object biography;
- it reinforced Santiago’s representation as a Christian territorial patron;
- it transformed anonymous, lost furnishings into enduring political symbols.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 The legendary bells

**Status:** lost, destroyed, never physically present as narrated, or otherwise unidentified.

**Location:** unknown.

**Custodian:** none identifiable.

**Accession number:** none.

**Public display:** no authenticated bell or lamp.

**Legal status:** no present ownership or restitution proceeding is known, because there is no identified object over which Santiago and Córdoba could assert competing title.

The safest current catalogue statement is:

> A nonextant or unidentified group of supposed pre-997 bells, first described in twelfth-century legendary historiography and given a Córdoba–Santiago custody chain by thirteenth-century Christian chroniclers.

### 8.2 Present substitutes and memorial objects

The story is represented rather than embodied by the bells themselves.

A principal material surrogate is Juan Luis López’s **1929 gouache on canvas, *Traslado de las campanas***:

- dimensions: 100 × 150 cm;
- location: Museo Catedral de Santiago;
- owner or depositor: Deputación Provincial da Coruña;
- placed on deposit with the Fundación Catedral in 2019;
- displayed in the Sala Manrique of the Palace of Gelmírez;
- originally one of eight panels commissioned for the Galicia pavilion at the 1929 Ibero-American Exposition in Seville;
- subject: four Christian captives carrying the bells after Almanzor’s destruction of the church [S8].

The existing Berenguela is another, frequently confused, material focus. The cracked 1729 original is in the cathedral cloister; a reproduction carries out its timekeeping function. It is not a claimant to the legendary identity [S3].

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Historiography and heritage interpretation

The legend remains a standard component of public narratives about both Santiago Cathedral and the Mosque-Cathedral of Córdoba. Modern heritage treatments range from straightforward repetition to explicit source criticism.

The 2024 *Paisajes sonoros históricos* entry reconstructs the 1236 dedication and cites Lucas, Rodrigo, the *Chronica latina*, the *Estoria de España*, and modern architectural scholarship. It presents the rescue of the bells as part of the event while identifying the textual basis [S7].

A more skeptical municipal presentation emphasizes:

- the lack of contemporary evidence for the bell journey;
- the *Chronica Naierensis*’s Mecca destination;
- disagreement among later accounts;
- the modern Berenguela’s 1729 date;
- the absence of material continuity [S9].

### 9.2 Painting

Juan Luis López’s 1929 panel gave the legend a durable modern image. Created for the Ibero-American Exposition, it embedded the story in a regionalist visual history of Galicia. Its current display in the cathedral museum gives a legendary transport scene institutional visibility [S8].

### 9.3 Literature

Serafín Linares Roldán’s **El viaje de las campanas** was published in Córdoba in 2004 in the context of the twinning of Córdoba and Santiago and a Jacobean Holy Year. It fictionalizes a 1240 expedition returning the bells, gives the journey named participants and a detailed timetable, and concludes with emancipation for the Muslim carriers. Those narrative details are modern literary invention, not medieval documentation [S10].

### 9.4 Civic reconciliation

The legend’s structure has made it adaptable to modern intercity and intercultural commemoration. What medieval texts framed as humiliation and religious reversal can be retold as a shared historical bond between Córdoba and Santiago. The object thus serves mutually incompatible symbolic programs: triumph, restitution, pilgrimage, reconciliation, and tourism.

### 9.5 Scholarship on material conversion

Katz’s 2021 article is the most directly object-centered modern treatment located. Rather than merely deciding whether the story is “true,” it examines what the bells’ narrated transformations reveal about Christian and Islamic material cultures, sensory regimes, and the chroniclers’ desire to preserve object identity through conversion [S1].

### 9.6 Modern misinformation

Common unsupported assertions include:

- that the bells’ exact number is known;
- that they were certainly melted down;
- that surviving Córdoba lamps are demonstrably made from them;
- that the present Berenguela is one of the returned bells;
- that they can still be heard at Santiago;
- that an unbroken cathedral inventory proves their return;
- that Muslim sources contemporary with 997 narrate the complete lamp-and-return episode.

These claims require evidence that has not been produced.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Primary textual corpus

The essential texts form a sequence rather than a single coherent tradition:

1. **Arabic accounts of the 997 campaign:** establish devastation and booty but do not securely establish the complete bell narrative.
2. **Pseudo-Turpin / *Liber Sancti Iacobi*, Book IV, chapter 25:** bells stolen, followed by miraculous punishment and prompt doubled restitution.
3. ***Chronica Naierensis*:** bells sent to Mecca and used as lamps.
4. **Lucas of Tuy, *Chronicon mundi*:** Ferdinand returns the bells on Muslim shoulders.
5. **Rodrigo Jiménez de Rada, *Historia de rebus Hispaniae*:** Córdoba mosque, lamp use, and symmetrical restitution.
6. ***Chronica latina regum Castellae*:** strong contextual account of Córdoba’s conquest and consecration.
7. **Alfonsine *Estoria de España*:** vernacular consolidation, including the “lesser bells.”

These texts should not be collapsed into a composite “medieval account.” Each preserves a different state of the legend.

### 10.2 Dating and transmission problems

The Codex Calixtinus is a manuscript compilation, while its constituent texts have separate composition histories. Book IV’s false attribution to Archbishop Turpin and its legendary Carolingian material require especially cautious use. Its value here is not eyewitness authority but proof that a bell-seizure narrative circulated by the twelfth century.

The *Chronica Naierensis* demonstrates that the story remained unstable. Its Mecca episode may precede the localization at Córdoba.

Lucas and Rodrigo wrote around the time of Ferdinand III and were connected to ecclesiastical and royal institutions. Their relative proximity makes the 1236 claim more serious than late folklore, but both wrote ideological histories and inherited earlier legendary material.

### 10.3 Political interpretation

Modern scholarship situates the bells inside the rhetoric of conquest and architectural conversion. In Rodrigo’s history, the return helps explain Córdoba’s passage from Islamic capital and mosque-city to a Christian royal and episcopal possession. The bells render historical reversal tangible [S6].

The scene also strengthens Ferdinand III’s sacral kingship: he does not merely conquer territory but restores sacred property to the apostle.

### 10.4 Material-culture debate

Comparative examples show that captured bells could be reused as lamps in Islamic contexts. That makes the basic operation possible, but analogy is not provenance. It cannot prove that the Córdoba lamps described by Christian chroniclers originated at Santiago.

Conversely, the story’s symbolic perfection does not prove fabrication. Actual trophy objects often acquire rhetorically symmetrical histories. The correct conclusion is unresolved identity, not categorical impossibility.

### 10.5 Archival gaps

Research priorities include:

- unpublished or incompletely catalogued medieval inventories of Santiago Cathedral;
- Córdoba chapter records relating to movable mosque furnishings after 1236;
- royal administrative documents from Ferdinand III’s court;
- early modern inventories that might preserve a tradition of returned bells;
- comparison of manuscript variants of Lucas and Rodrigo;
- closer examination of whether later Arabic authors borrowed bell details from Christian traditions;
- archaeological records of medieval lamp fittings from the Mosque-Cathedral.

Katz acknowledges assistance from the Archivo Capitular de la Catedral de Córdoba, but no authenticated object emerged from that research [S1].

### 10.6 State of the question

The best-supported synthesis is:

- Almanzor’s 997 destruction and plunder of Compostela are historical.
- Bells appear in the narrative record by the twelfth century.
- The earliest developed version says they were promptly returned.
- Another twelfth-century source sends them to Mecca.
- Thirteenth-century sources relocate them to Córdoba and connect their return to Ferdinand III.
- No physical object proves any version.
- The legend is medieval, not modern, but its medieval origin does not authenticate its object-chain.
- The bells’ documented history after their supposed 1236 return is absent.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and medieval texts

**[S2] Pseudo-Turpin Chronicle, *Liber Sancti Iacobi* / Codex Calixtinus, Book IV, chapter 25.**  
The earliest located developed narrative mentioning Almanzor’s seizure of the bells. It says the stolen property was returned doubled after fifteen days; it does not contain the later Córdoba captivity.

**[S4] Official Camino de Santiago description of the Codex Calixtinus.**  
Useful for the status of Book IV as literary, pseudo-Carolingian history rather than straightforward contemporary annal.

**[S5] *Chronica Naierensis*.**  
Later-twelfth-century witness sending the bells to Mecca. The online synthesis used here reproduces the relevant translated passage and identifies its place in the chronicle.

**Lucas of Tuy, *Chronicon mundi*.**  
The crucial early-thirteenth-century formulation of return on Saracen shoulders. Consulted through the textual and bibliographical presentation in *Paisajes sonoros históricos* and related scholarship.

**Rodrigo Jiménez de Rada, *Historia de rebus Hispaniae*, Book IX.**  
The major thirteenth-century source for the mature Córdoba-lamp-restoration narrative. The critical Latin edition is Juan Fernández Valverde, *Roderici Ximenii de Rada Opera omnia I: Historia de rebus Hispanie sive Historia Gothica*, Corpus Christianorum Continuatio Mediaevalis 72, Turnhout, 1987 [S11].

**Alfonsine *Estoria de España* / *Primera Crónica General*.**  
Later-thirteenth-century vernacular consolidation. The relevant passages are cited and partly transcribed by the modern studies below.

### Modern scholarship, catalogues, and institutional records

**[S1] Dana E. Katz, “The Conversion of Iberia’s Bells,” *Material Religion* 17, no. 1 (2021), 81–110.**  
The most important object-centered study located. It identifies the bells as nonextant and analyzes the chronicular transformations of their substance, use, and religious identity.

**[S3] Francesc Llop i Bayo and collaborators, “De horas, Berenguela (B),” Inventario de campanas de las Catedrales de España.**  
Detailed field record of the 1729 bell, including dimensions, inscriptions, iconography, damage, sampling, and current cloister location. Essential for excluding Berenguela as a candidate.

**[S6] “Royal Entries in Conquered Towns: Mosques, Cathedrals and the Power of Buildings.”**  
Scholarly analysis of royal entries and mosque conversions, including Jiménez de Rada’s bells narrative and its place in the political theology of Ferdinand III’s conquest.

**[S7] Juan Ruiz Jiménez, “Consagración de la mezquita de Córdoba y rescate de las campanas de la catedral de Santiago (1236),” *Paisajes sonoros históricos* 10, art. 63 (2024).**  
A source-based account of the dedication ceremonies and bell-restoration claim, with bibliography and references to Lucas, Rodrigo, the *Chronica latina*, and the *Estoria de España*.

**[S8] Museo Catedral de Santiago, catalogue entry for Juan Luis López, *Traslado de las campanas* (1929).**  
Primary institutional record for the painting’s medium, dimensions, commission, exhibition history, ownership, deposit, and subject.

**[S9] Santiago municipal cultural-history page, “Leyendas de Santiago: lo que es verdad y lo que no.”**  
A current skeptical public-history treatment emphasizing the late and contradictory sources and the nonidentity of Berenguela.

**[S10] Storyca database entry for Serafín Linares Roldán, *El viaje de las campanas* (2004).**  
Documents a modern literary elaboration tied to the Córdoba–Santiago twinning and the 2004 Holy Year.

**[S11] Brepols catalogue for Fernández Valverde’s critical edition of Jiménez de Rada.**  
Bibliographical authority for the modern critical Latin edition and its manuscript basis.
