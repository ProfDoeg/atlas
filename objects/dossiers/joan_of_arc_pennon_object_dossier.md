# Joan of Arc's Pennon: Object Dossier

**Proposed filename:** `joan_of_arcs_pennon_object_dossier.md`

## Scope and evidentiary finding

The object conventionally called **Joan of Arc’s pennon** was a small painted military ensign commissioned for Joan at Tours in 1429 together with a larger standard. The two objects are repeatedly confused in medieval testimony and almost completely conflated in later art. The surviving royal account nevertheless establishes that there were two: “*ung grant estendart et ung petit pour la Pucelle*”—one large standard and one small one. The term *pennon* or Middle French *panon* is supplied most clearly by the later recension of the *Journal du siège d’Orléans*.

The pennon’s probable decoration was an **Annunciation**, with the Virgin Mary and an angel presenting a lily. One source instead describes a blue shield, white dove, scroll, and the words *De par le Roy du Ciel*. Philippe Contamine argues that this discrepant report probably concerns the same smaller ensign and has imperfectly transmitted its Annunciation imagery.

The original textile does not survive. A near-contemporary narrative says that fire caught it during Joan’s entry into Orléans on 29 April 1429 and that she turned her horse and extinguished it. Its condition afterward is not documented. References to Joan’s ensign after that date overwhelmingly concern the large standard, not the pennon. No documented medieval custody chain continues beyond the Orléans incident, and no museum, church, private collection, or scientific laboratory possesses an authenticated fragment. The strongest defensible conclusion is therefore:

> **Documented existence; incompletely reconstructable appearance; damaged by fire on 29 April 1429; subsequent fate unknown, probably discarded or destroyed; no surviving claimant with a continuous provenance.**

A modern ceremonial pennon made in 2016 is used in the annual Joan of Arc festivities at Orléans. It is explicitly a reconstruction, not a claimed medieval relic.

### Evidentiary labels used below

- **Documented:** supported by a contemporary administrative record, trial record, or closely contemporary narrative.
- **Contemporary report:** written during Joan’s lifetime or soon afterward, but not necessarily by an eyewitness.
- **Retrospective testimony:** testimony given in 1455–1456 about events of 1429–1430.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** a conclusion inferred from several imperfect sources.
- **Institutional reconstruction/tradition:** a modern commemorative representation maintained by an institution.
- **Unverified:** possible but not established.
- **Legend or modern accretion:** unsupported by an adequate custody or documentary chain.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Object class and names

The object was a **painted textile military ensign**, smaller than the larger standard made for Joan at the same time.

Relevant terminology includes:

- **Middle French:** *panon*, *pennon*, *petit estendart*, *enseigne*.
- **Modern French:** *pennon de Jeanne d’Arc*, *petit étendard de Jeanne d’Arc*.
- **English:** *Joan of Arc’s pennon*, *small standard*, *small banner*.
- **Latin trial vocabulary:** *vexillum*, a broad term meaning an ensign or banner and not reliably distinguishing the two objects.
- **Modern vexillological usage:** *pennon* usually denotes a relatively small, elongated, often single-pointed or single-tailed ensign, historically associated with a knight or lesser commander.

The royal payment account does not call the small object a *pennon*. It calls it simply “*ung petit*,” grammatically dependent on *estendart*: a small standard. The identification as a pennon depends principally on the *Journal du siège d’Orléans* and on comparison with fifteenth-century flag practice. [The account is reproduced in Henri Wallon’s documentary appendix](https://ste-jeannedarc.fr/histoire_wallon/appendice15.html), while the distinction between the two ensigns is critically analyzed by [Philippe Contamine](https://journals.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/index.php/fr/article/view/45032).

The name “Joan of Arc’s Pennon” therefore describes a historically real class of object, but it is not a stable medieval proper name. Medieval narrators alternated among *estendart*, *bannière*, *enseigne*, *panon*, and Latin *vexillum*. Statements that Joan possessed three clearly differentiated objects—banner, standard, and pennon—usually impose later technical classifications on sources that securely document only **two commissioned textiles**.

### 1.2 What can be said physically

The original object is lost. No dimensions, weight, weave count, surviving pigment sample, maker’s mark, seam, fastening, pole, or textile fragment are known.

The most defensible reconstruction is:

- **Support:** cloth supplied by Hauves Poulnoir; the precise fabric of the smaller ensign is not separately specified.
- **Technique:** painted, not securely demonstrated to have been embroidered.
- **Ground color:** probably white, by association with Joan’s larger standard and the entrance narrative, but not independently established for every part of the pennon.
- **Shape:** probably smaller and single-tailed; exact outline and proportions unknown.
- **Principal subject:** an Annunciation—Mary facing an angel who offers or holds a lily.
- **Possible additional elements:** a dove representing the Holy Spirit; a blue shield or blue terminal field; fleurs-de-lis; a scroll.
- **Possible text:** *De par le Roy du Ciel* (“On behalf of the King of Heaven”) or an Annunciation formula; neither is certain.
- **Pole and fittings:** unrecorded.
- **Condition on 29 April 1429:** briefly on fire, then extinguished.
- **Condition after that event:** unknown.

### 1.3 The Annunciation description

The most explicit distinction occurs in the *Journal du siège d’Orléans*, compiled in its surviving form around 1467 from older local material:

> “*Et faisoit porter devant elle son estandard, qui estoit pareillement blanc, ouquel avoit deux anges tenant chacun une fleur de liz en leur main, et ou panon estoit painte comme une Annonciation, c’est l’image de Nostre-Dame ayant devant elle ung ange luy presentant ung lis.*”

Translation:

> “She caused her standard to be carried before her; it was likewise white, and on it were two angels, each holding a fleur-de-lis in his hand; and on the pennon was painted something like an Annunciation, that is, the image of Our Lady with an angel before her presenting her with a lily.”

This is the clearest evidence that Joan’s **large standard** and **pennon** were separate textiles with different principal scenes. The online transcription and source history are available in the [*Journal du siège d’Orléans*](https://ste-jeannedarc.fr/chroniques/journal_siege_orleans.html), while Contamine discusses the wording and its implications in his [critical study](https://journals.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/index.php/fr/article/view/45032).

### 1.4 The La Rochelle variant

A report attributed to the clerk of La Rochelle, written in 1431, says that Joan had an ensign made at Poitiers bearing:

- an azure shield;
- a white dove inside it;
- a scroll in the dove’s beak;
- the words *De par le Roy du Ciel*.

This version is problematic:

1. The administrative account locates the painter at Tours, not Poitiers.
2. The text is not an eyewitness workshop record.
3. The dove and scroll fit an Annunciation more naturally than a Last Judgment.
4. *De par le Roy du Ciel* may be a paraphrase of Joan’s claimed divine commission rather than a verbatim inscription.
5. The blue shield may be a confused memory of a blue, fleur-de-lis-bearing tail or of royal arms.

Contamine’s reconstruction treats this as an imperfect description of the **pennon**, not the large standard. He cautiously proposes a single-tailed object with Gabriel and Mary near the hoist and a blue, fleur-de-lis-bearing terminal area. This is a scholarly hypothesis, not a recoverable blueprint.

### 1.5 Details belonging to the large standard, not securely to the pennon

The following familiar features belong principally to Joan’s **large standard**:

- white cloth or *boucassin*;
- a field “sown with lilies”;
- the world or globe;
- the King of Heaven or Christ in judgment;
- two angels;
- *Jhesus Maria* written along a side;
- silk fringe;
- possibly Christ on a rainbow displaying his wounds.

At her trial on 27 February 1431 Joan described an ensign with a white field, lilies, the world, two angels, *Jhesus Maria*, and silk fringe. She called it the ensign she had when she went to Orléans. This testimony does not describe the Annunciation pennon separately and should not be transferred wholesale to it. The French and Latin versions are preserved in the [fourth public interrogation](https://www.ste-jeannedarc.fr/condamnation/interro_public4.html).

### 1.6 Claimed dimensions

No medieval source gives the pennon’s measurements.

The French Ministry of Culture’s inventory of the Orléans festivities assigns the reconstructed large standard dimensions of approximately 3.56 metres by “0.80 cm.” The latter is plainly an internal unit error and must mean approximately **0.80 m**, not eight millimetres. Those measurements concern a **modern reconstruction of the large standard**, not the pennon. The same inventory does not publish dimensions for the 2016 pennon. [French Ministry of Culture inventory](https://www.culture.gouv.fr/Media/Thematiques/Patrimoine-culturel-immateriel/Files/Fiches-inventaire-du-PCI/Les-fetes-de-Jeanne-d-Arc-a-Orleans).

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Commission

**Documented, spring 1429.** Joan’s military equipment was prepared after Charles VII’s court accepted her for service and before the relief expedition to Orléans. A surviving treasury entry records payment:

> “*Et à Hauves Poulnoir, paintre demourant à Tours, pour avoir paint et baillié estoffes pour ung grant estendart et ung petit pour la Pucelle, XXV livres tournois.*”

Translation:

> “And to Hauves Poulnoir, painter residing at Tours, for having painted and supplied the cloth for one large standard and one small one for the Maid, 25 livres tournois.”

The payment was included in Hémon Raguier’s accounts as treasurer of war. Royal letters patent dated at Chinon on 10 May 1429 and financial authorization issued on 15 May supported settlement of the expense. The wording establishes:

- one contractor;
- his occupation as painter;
- his residence at Tours;
- his supply of the textiles as well as the painting;
- two separate objects;
- a combined price of 25 livres tournois;
- Joan as intended user.

It does **not** divide the price between the two, state their measurements, identify assistants, describe the loom or textile merchant, or enumerate pigments. [Documentary transcription](https://ste-jeannedarc.fr/histoire_wallon/appendice15.html); [Contamine’s discussion](https://journals.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/index.php/fr/article/view/45032).

### 2.2 Maker’s identity

The maker’s recorded name appears as **Hauves Poulnoir**, with later variants including Hauvres, Hennes, James Poulnoir, and Poulvoir. The modern identification “Hamish Power,” implying Scottish origin, is frequently repeated but is not established by the payment entry itself. No securely identified second work by him survives. Consequently:

- **Documented:** a painter named Hauves Poulnoir lived and worked at Tours in 1429.
- **Plausible:** he directed or personally executed the painted decoration.
- **Unverified:** that his name represents “Hamish Power” or that he was Scottish.
- **Unknown:** workshop assistants, textile supplier, exact workplace, and duration of manufacture.

Joan later intervened with Tours authorities on behalf of Poulnoir’s daughter Héliote at the time of her marriage. This demonstrates a continuing association with the painter’s household, but not custody of the pennon. A Tours account excerpt concerning gifts for Héliote’s wedding is reproduced with Joan’s letter in the [Joan of Arc Archive](https://joan-of-arc.org/archive/joanofarc_letter_p_Jan_19_1430.html).

### 2.3 Date and place

The ensigns were probably made during Joan’s period at **Tours in April 1429**, before she joined the relief convoy at Blois. The exact completion day is not preserved.

A later chronicle says she caused the standard and her equipment to be blessed at the collegiate church of Saint-Sauveur at Blois. Whether the smaller pennon was included in that collective blessing is plausible but not separately stated.

### 2.4 Materials

The payment entry says Poulnoir supplied the *estoffes*, meaning cloth or materials. Joan’s 1431 trial testimony describes her ensign as white cloth or *boucassin* and silk-fringed. Medieval and early modern writers used *boucassin* inconsistently for a fine textile, commonly linen or cotton-like cloth. It should not be translated with certainty as modern cotton, buckram, or burlap.

The following hierarchy is warranted:

- **Secure for the commissioned pair:** textile support painted by Poulnoir.
- **Secure for the large standard:** white cloth or *boucassin*, with silk fringe, according to Joan.
- **Probable but unproved for the pennon:** a related white textile from the same commission.
- **Unproved:** silk fabric, satin, embroidery, gold thread, exact pigments, gilding, or a specific weave.

Several later writers called the ensigns white silk or satin. These descriptions may reflect normal luxury-banner vocabulary rather than inspection of the objects.

### 2.5 Design authority

At the 1431 trial, Joan attributed the command for her principal standard to Saint Catherine and Saint Margaret, who told her to take it boldly and have the King of Heaven painted upon it. This claim explicitly concerns the large standard’s divine king imagery. No surviving statement by Joan explains who devised the pennon’s Annunciation.

Possible contributors to the design include Joan, Poulnoir, her confessor Jean Pasquerel, and clerical advisers at Tours or Blois. None can be documented as the independent iconographic author of the pennon.

### 2.6 Earliest attestation

The earliest securely dated evidence for the physical pair is the **1429 royal accounting record**, ultimately authorized in May. The earliest narrative evidence that distinguishes the smaller ensign’s imagery is later in surviving form: the *Journal du siège d’Orléans*, compiled around 1467 but incorporating material related to the events of 1429.

Perceval de Cagny, writing around 1438 and drawing on his service with the duke of Alençon, says Joan made an ensign bearing an image of Our Lady. Contamine interprets this as the pennon. Cagny’s terminology does not itself make the identification certain.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because the object disappeared almost immediately, the chain is short and the uncertainties unusually important.

### April 1429: Poulnoir’s workshop, Tours

**Documented.** Hauves Poulnoir supplied and painted the large and small standards for Joan. Until delivery, the small ensign was in his or his workshop’s physical control.

**Transfer mechanism:** commissioned delivery financed by the royal administration.

**Price:** part of a combined 25 livres tournois payment.

**Exact legal owner:** unclear. The Crown paid, but the account calls the objects “for the Maid.” Whether they were royal equipment issued to Joan or her personal movable property is not stated.

### April 1429: Joan’s household and military entourage

**Documented by purpose; exact bearer unknown.** After delivery, the pennon entered the custody of Joan or members of her household. Medieval standards were often carried by designated bearers, although Joan sometimes carried her principal standard herself.

No source identifies the pennon’s regular bearer. Statements naming Jean d’Aulon, Louis de Coutes, or another member of the household as its custodian require episode-specific evidence that has not survived.

### Blois, late April 1429

**Plausible.** Joan assembled with the Orléans relief force at Blois. The *Chronique de la Pucelle* says her standard and equipment were blessed at Saint-Sauveur. The pennon may have accompanied the larger standard, but the text does not itemize it.

### Road from Blois to Orléans, 27–29 April 1429

**Documented in general.** Both ensigns evidently accompanied the expedition, because the Orléans narrative describes the standard and pennon during Joan’s entry.

### Orléans, evening of 29 April 1429

**Closest thing to a terminal documented event.** Joan entered Orléans through the Porte de Bourgogne amid a dense torch-bearing crowd. According to the *Journal du siège*, fire caught the pennon near the larger standard. Joan spurred and turned her horse toward it and extinguished the fire herself. The feat impressed soldiers and townspeople.

The episode is significant because the source calls the burning object *ce pennon*, explicitly identifying the smaller ensign. Contamine therefore separates it from the large standard carried later in the campaign. The modern City of Orléans educational dossier states directly that the Annunciation pennon burned accidentally during Joan’s entrance. [City of Orléans educational dossier](https://www.orleans.fr/fileadmin/orleans/MEDIA/document/culture/musees/offre_pedagogique/2022/mja/dossier/vdh_jeannedarc.pdf); [critical source synthesis](https://www.jeannedarc.com.fr/maison/maisonexpoldetandard.htm).

The degree of damage is not recorded. “Fire caught” does not necessarily mean complete destruction. Later writers have variously described it as burned, partly burned, scorched, or rendered unusable.

### After 29 April 1429

The custody chain breaks completely.

Adrien Harmand argued in 1929 that the damaged pennon was probably taken out of service and not replaced, noting that later campaign records speak of Joan’s standard but not clearly of the pennon. This is a reasonable inference, not documented disposal. [Harmand’s study](https://jeanne.wiki/harmand-costumes-armure/etendard).

Possible fates, in descending order of evidentiary caution:

1. **Damaged and discarded at Orléans:** plausible, but undocumented.
2. **Retained briefly as damaged equipment:** possible, without evidence.
3. **Repaired and reused under another name:** possible but unsupported.
4. **Completely consumed in the 29 April fire:** not what the narrative explicitly says, since Joan extinguished it.
5. **Captured at Compiègne in 1430:** unlikely if the pennon was already out of use; no Burgundian inventory or trophy notice identifies it.
6. **Preserved as a relic:** no medieval or early modern custody evidence.
7. **Surviving in a church, museum, or private collection:** no credible claimant.

### Distinction from the large standard’s later itinerary

The large standard—not the burned pennon—appears in accounts of:

- the fighting at Orléans;
- Jargeau;
- the Loire campaign;
- the march to Reims;
- Charles VII’s coronation;
- the attack on Paris;
- subsequent campaigning;
- possibly Joan’s capture at Compiègne.

At the trial Joan said she had never had more than one standard with the world and two angels. This means one principal standard, not that the royal account’s “small” ensign never existed. The interrogation itself switches among broad banner terms. [First private interrogation, 10 March 1431](https://www.ste-jeannedarc.fr/condamnation/interro_prive1.html).

The fate sometimes given for “Joan’s banner”—capture by the Burgundians at Compiègne and subsequent destruction—belongs to the large standard and remains conjectural even for that object. Contamine notes the odd absence of any documented Burgundian trophy or later record.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Did the pennon exist?

Yes, to the normal historical standard of proof.

The administrative record for one large and one small standard is contemporary and specific. The *Journal du siège* independently distinguishes an *estandard* from a *panon*. The evidence does not depend on a nineteenth-century relic claim.

### 4.2 Was it the Annunciation ensign?

**Strong scholarly reconstruction, not absolute certainty.**

Evidence in favor:

- The *Journal du siège* explicitly puts an Annunciation on the *panon*.
- Perceval de Cagny reports an ensign with Our Lady.
- The La Rochelle dove-and-scroll description is iconographically compatible with an Annunciation.
- A smaller, single-tailed ensign accompanying the principal standard fits contemporary military usage.

Complications:

- The *Journal* survives in a recension compiled decades later.
- Cagny calls the Marian object an *estendart*, not necessarily a pennon.
- Joan said in one interrogation that she had only one standard, but the question and answer referred to the standard with the world and angels.
- Later authors freely merged the Annunciation with the large standard’s Last Judgment imagery.
- No original image or material survives for comparison.

### 4.3 Is any extant textile the original?

No authenticated surviving candidate is known.

There has been:

- no published radiocarbon dating of an alleged fragment;
- no fiber, dye, pigment, or weave analysis on an accepted claimant;
- no DNA testing;
- no medieval ecclesiastical inventory tracing an extant textile to Joan;
- no royal, municipal, Burgundian, or English custody record after 1429;
- no museum accession with a continuous chain to Poulnoir’s commission.

Any modern textile displayed as “Joan’s pennon” is therefore a reconstruction, commemorative object, or unrelated later banner unless an independently documented provenance is produced.

### 4.4 Standard-versus-pennon conflation

This is the central identity problem.

Henri Wallon and numerous later authors reconstructed:

- a large white standard with the King of Heaven and angels;
- a reverse with royal arms;
- a separate Annunciation pennon.

Contamine’s technical objection is that different paintings on the two faces of a thin painted cloth would be difficult because the paint penetrated the fabric. He regards many “reverse” descriptions as evidence that witnesses or later compilers mixed the two ensigns. He also stresses that two flags, two faces, furled cloth, moving folds, and distant observation created ideal conditions for confusion.

Thus the familiar modern composite—Christ and angels on one side, the Annunciation or French arms on the other—cannot be treated as a faithful copy.

### 4.5 The “one standard only” statement

On 10 March 1431 Joan was asked whether the world and two angels were painted on the standard she carried. She answered yes and added that she had never had but one. The Latin reads *nec unquam habuit nisi unicum*.

Possible interpretations:

- She had only one **principal standard of that description**.
- She denied having successive or magical replacements.
- She used *standard* narrowly and did not count the small pennon.
- The answer was simplified in the official record.
- Less plausibly, the account’s small ensign was never delivered or was not hers in practice.

Because the payment account expressly names a large and small standard, Joan’s answer cannot safely erase the smaller object.

### 4.6 Alleged medieval images

Clément de Fauquembergue drew Joan holding an ensign in the margin of a Paris Parlement register on 10 May 1429. He had never seen her. His sketch is contemporary evidence for Joan’s immediate association with a banner and the abbreviation “IHS,” but it is not a portrait or measured image of the pennon. The Bibliothèque nationale de France describes it as an imaginary representation in its [educational study of Joan’s imagery](https://classes.bnf.fr/pdf/heros_2.pdf).

Contamine warns especially against purportedly detailed “medieval” miniatures whose dates are uncertain and whose motifs reproduce information only widely disseminated after Jules Quicherat published the trial sources between 1841 and 1849. Such images may be later alterations or nineteenth-century creations. They cannot repair the broken material provenance.

### 4.7 Authority to authenticate

Authority has shifted among:

- the royal treasury, which authenticated the commission as an expense;
- Joan herself and trial notaries, who recorded descriptions;
- chroniclers and rehabilitation witnesses;
- antiquarians and heraldists;
- churches and civic committees commissioning replicas;
- modern medievalists, textile historians, archivists, and conservators.

No church canonization decree authenticates any surviving pennon. Joan’s sainthood authenticates neither a particular reconstruction nor any alleged textile relic.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Joan of Arc — commissioner, intended possessor, user

**1429. Documented.** The royal account says the pair was made “for the Maid.” The Orléans narrative places the pennon next to her on 29 April and describes her extinguishing the fire.

### Hauves Poulnoir — maker and material supplier

**Tours, 1429. Documented.** Paid for painting and providing cloth for the large and small ensigns. No other securely attributed work is known.

### Charles VII — funding authority

**1429. Documented institutional connection.** Royal funds paid for the commission. Whether Charles personally approved the pennon’s iconography is unknown.

### Hémon Raguier — financial administrator

**1429. Documented.** Treasurer of war whose account preserves the payment. His connection is administrative, not physical custody.

### Regnier de Bouligny — financial authorization

**May 1429. Documented.** Royal financial officer addressed in the letters authorizing payment.

### Jean Pasquerel — chaplain and later witness

**1429; testimony in 1456. Retrospective eyewitness for manufacture of the large standard.** Pasquerel said he was at Tours when Joan’s standard was painted. He described the Savior in judgment and an angel with a lily but did not separately describe the pennon.

### Perceval de Cagny — chronicler and possible eyewitness

**Circa 1438.** As a member of the duke of Alençon’s household, he was well positioned to observe Joan’s campaigns. His report that Joan had an ensign made with the image of Our Lady is important evidence for the Marian pennon.

### Anonymous or composite author of the *Journal du siège d’Orléans*

**Material compiled by circa 1467.** Supplies the clearest distinction between the large standard and Annunciation pennon and records the fire incident.

### Clerk of La Rochelle

**1431. Contemporary report, not demonstrated eyewitness.** Supplies the blue shield, dove, scroll, and *De par le Roy du Ciel* variant.

### Jean, count of Dunois

**1429; testimony in 1456.** Associated with Joan at Orléans and later described her white standard. His testimony concerns the larger ensign rather than securely identifying the pennon.

### Saint Catherine of Alexandria and Saint Margaret of Antioch

**Divine association claimed by Joan in 1431.** Joan said these voices commanded her to take the principal standard boldly and place the King of Heaven upon it. No equally direct testimony connects them to the Annunciation design of the pennon.

### Virgin Mary

**Painted subject, according to the *Journal du siège* and possibly Cagny.** Mary is depicted at the Annunciation, not documented as a supernatural owner of the textile.

### Archangel Gabriel

**Probable painted subject.** Although the *Journal* calls him simply an angel, the angel presenting a lily to Mary in an Annunciation is Gabriel.

### Holy Spirit

**Possible painted subject.** The La Rochelle account’s white dove probably represents the Holy Spirit. Its presence on the original pennon is plausible but not certain.

### “King of Heaven”

This title can denote God or Christ in Joan’s language. On the large standard it was represented by the enthroned divine figure. On the pennon it may have appeared in the formula *De par le Roy du Ciel*. The exact inscription is disputed.

### Orléans civic institutions

The medieval city preserved and elaborated the memory of Joan’s entry. Modern municipal and associational bodies commission and maintain replacement standards and a reconstructed pennon for the annual festivities.

### Association *Jeanne, son étendard et son message*

**Founded 2000. Institutional custodian of modern reconstructions.** The Ministry of Culture inventory identifies the association as keeper of the shorter processional standard and the modern pennon.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Military identification and rallying

A pennon visually identified Joan and her household within an army containing numerous noble and royal ensigns. Its small scale would have made it more manageable on horseback than the principal standard.

The sources do not preserve a tactical manual for its use. General claims that it gave commands, marked headquarters, or assigned formations are plausible from contemporary military practice but not specifically documented for this pennon.

### 6.2 Sacred imagery

The Annunciation united several themes:

- divine message delivered through an angel;
- virginity;
- obedient acceptance of a mission;
- the lily as Marian, royal, and purity symbolism;
- correspondence between Gabriel’s message to Mary and Joan’s claimed angelic commission.

This interpretation is historically plausible, but no surviving explanation by Joan or Poulnoir states it.

### 6.3 Blessing

A chronicle says Joan’s standard and equipment were blessed at Saint-Sauveur in Blois before the expedition. If the pennon was included, the blessing transformed military equipment into a publicly sacralized ensemble. The inclusion remains inferential.

### 6.4 Entry into Orléans

On 29 April 1429 the standard and pennon participated in Joan’s public entrance with the relief convoy. This was both military deployment and civic spectacle. The fire incident occurred amid the torches of the welcoming crowd.

The object’s documented ceremonial function therefore preceded its disappearance: it announced Joan’s arrival as a divinely commissioned military figure.

### 6.5 Claimed protection and “fortunate” ensigns

At Joan’s trial, the prosecution alleged that soldiers had made pennons modeled on her “fortunate” standard and believed these would bring victory. The charge attempted to frame banner devotion as superstition or magic. Joan did not admit enchanting the objects.

This episode concerns the symbolic family of ensigns generated by Joan’s standard, not necessarily literal copies of the Annunciation pennon. It nevertheless shows that *pennons* associated with her design were reportedly reproduced during her lifetime.

### 6.6 Joan’s preference for the standard

Joan said she loved her principal standard forty times more than her sword and carried it during attacks to avoid killing anyone. This famous statement belongs to the large standard, not demonstrably the smaller pennon. [Trial text](https://www.ste-jeannedarc.fr/condamnation/interro_public4.html).

### 6.7 Modern ceremonial substitution

The 2016 Orléans pennon is used on 29 April when the young woman chosen to represent Joan enters the city on horseback. It is therefore not merely displayed in a museum context: it is an active ceremonial substitute for the lost object.

According to the Ministry of Culture inventory:

- the reconstruction was created in 2016;
- it has one tail;
- it bears the Annunciation;
- it is used for the 29 April entrance;
- it is kept by *Jeanne, son étendard et son message*;
- its use is coordinated with municipal, religious, military, and voluntary organizations.

This is an **institutional commemorative function**, not uninterrupted medieval ritual custody.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 Divine command

**Early attested claim, recorded in 1431.** Joan said Catherine and Margaret told her to take the standard on behalf of the King of Heaven, carry it boldly, and have the heavenly king painted upon it. This concerns the principal standard. Later retellings extend the same divine design authority to every component of Joan’s banner ensemble, including the pennon, without separate evidence.

### 7.2 A miraculous or invincible banner

**Contemporary allegation.** The Rouen prosecution alleged that soldiers copied Joan’s banner in the belief that such pennons were fortunate and would ensure victory. This proves circulation of the belief as an accusation; it does not prove Joan taught magical efficacy.

The distinction matters: an object serving as a religious rallying sign is documented, while an enchanted talisman is a hostile prosecutorial characterization.

### 7.3 Fire mastered by Joan

**Attested narrative with legendary potential.** The *Journal du siège* says she maneuvered her horse and extinguished the burning pennon so skillfully that experienced soldiers marveled. The event is physically ordinary—a torch igniting cloth—but narratively functions as an early demonstration of martial composure.

Later retellings sometimes enlarge this into the pennon surviving miraculously or Joan being unharmed by fire in symbolic anticipation of her execution. Those theological connections are later interpretation, not part of the basic incident.

### 7.4 The pennon as the complete “banner of Joan”

**Later conflation.** Popular reconstructions frequently combine:

- the Annunciation;
- Christ or God holding the world;
- two angels;
- French arms;
- fleurs-de-lis;
- *Jhesus Maria*;
- *De par le Roy du Ciel*.

No medieval source describes all these elements on one face of one object. The composite is the product of harmonizing mutually inconsistent accounts.

### 7.5 “The original survives”

**Unsupported claim.** No candidate has a documentary chain to Tours in 1429. Tapestries, church banners, painted miniatures, ceremonial flags, and commercially sold replicas are witnesses to later reception, not surviving parts of the pennon.

### 7.6 A Scottish maker named Hamish Power

**Popular but unverified identification.** Hauves Poulnoir is sometimes transformed into the Scottish name “Hamish Power,” reinforcing the narrative of Scottish participation in Charles VII’s armies. The account itself gives only the French-form name and Tours residence. Until independent archival proof is supplied, Scottish nationality and the modernized name should be labeled conjectural.

### 7.7 The pennon at Reims or Compiègne

**Common object-confusion.** Images and narratives showing “Joan’s banner” at Charles VII’s coronation or at her capture generally concern the large standard. The documentary trail gives no secure post-Orléans appearance of the Annunciation pennon.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Original

**Status:** lost, probably destroyed.

**Last documented location:** Orléans, during Joan’s entry on the evening of 29 April 1429.

**Last documented condition:** caught fire and was extinguished; extent of damage unknown.

**Owner or controller:** none now identifiable.

**Accession number:** none.

**Public display:** impossible; no authenticated material survives.

**Scientific examination:** none possible on an identified original.

**Legal or repatriation dispute:** none documented.

**Serious location hypotheses:** no evidence-supported hypothesis identifies a surviving location. “Discarded at Orléans after fire damage” is a practical inference, not an archival fact.

### 8.2 Modern Orléans reconstruction

**Date:** 2016.

**Status:** ceremonial reconstruction.

**Custodian:** the association *Jeanne, son étendard et son message*, according to the French Ministry of Culture inventory.

**Use:** carried by the annual representative of Joan during the 29 April ceremonial entrance into Orléans.

**Design:** single tail and Annunciation scene.

**Claimed identity:** a reconstruction “on the model” of Joan’s pennon, not the medieval object.

**Published accession number:** none found.

**Access:** seen during the annual festivities; ordinary off-season public-access arrangements are not specified in the inventory.

The inventory was prepared as documentation of the **intangible cultural heritage of the Orléans Joan of Arc festivals**, rather than as a museum catalogue of medieval material. [Ministry inventory](https://www.culture.gouv.fr/Media/Thematiques/Patrimoine-culturel-immateriel/Files/Fiches-inventaire-du-PCI/Les-fetes-de-Jeanne-d-Arc-a-Orleans).

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Publication of the sources

Jules Quicherat’s publication of the trial and related sources between 1841 and 1849 transformed the banner from a loosely remembered attribute into an object that antiquarians and artists attempted to reconstruct line by line. Contamine identifies this documentary publication as the turning point after which detailed modern designs proliferated.

### 9.2 Nineteenth-century reconstructions

Major episodes in the larger banner’s reconstruction history include:

- **1855, Orléans:** a white silk processional banner created for festivities surrounding the inauguration of Denis Foyatier’s statue; principally derived from Joan’s and Pasquerel’s descriptions.
- **1876:** another version on the same general model, deposited at Les Invalides.
- **1894, Notre-Dame de Paris:** a reconstruction seeking fidelity to the written descriptions but placing pennon-related imagery on the reverse.
- **1909, Orléans:** another reconstruction influenced by the so-called Azeglio tapestry and the Notre-Dame banner.

These were reconstructions of “Joan’s standard” rather than secure copies of the pennon. Their importance lies in establishing the modern composite iconography. [Contamine and the Maison de Jeanne d’Arc synthesis](https://www.jeannedarc.com.fr/maison/maisonexpoldetandard.htm).

### 9.3 Twentieth-century reconstructions

Further versions were produced for:

- Joan’s canonization celebrations in 1920;
- the 500th anniversary of the relief of Orléans in 1929;
- commemorations at Reims, Rouen, and Lagny-sur-Marne;
- a very large English-made standard presented to Reims Cathedral in 1931;
- the Orléans festivities from the 1930s onward.

The 1931 Reims example reportedly relied heavily on the La Rochelle account. It is a commemorative reconstruction without medieval material continuity.

### 9.4 Orléans civic standardization

The Ministry of Culture inventory traces successive Orléans ceremonial objects:

- a processional banner offered by subscription from the women of Orléans in the nineteenth century;
- replacement in 1894;
- a new standard entrusted to the annual representative of Joan from 1947;
- replacement of competing municipal, clerical, and associational versions around 2000–2002 by a unified design informed by a 1990 Centre Jeanne d’Arc reconstruction;
- manufacture of two practical copies of that standard, one for presentation and one for horseback procession;
- creation of the separate reconstructed pennon in 2016.

The rule that the two copies of the principal standard should not appear simultaneously preserves a symbolic fiction of singularity. This is an explicitly managed modern representational convention.

### 9.5 Hymn and ritual symbolism

The *Hymne à l’étendard*, composed by Abbé Marcel Laurent in 1899, is sung during the Orléans festivities. The hymn addresses the standard as Joan’s enduring symbolic presence. It concerns the banner tradition generally, but contributes to public conflation of the lost standard and pennon.

### 9.6 Art and image

Joan’s banner became a central attribute in:

- nineteenth-century history painting;
- church stained glass;
- statuary;
- nationalist prints;
- schoolbook illustration;
- early and later cinema;
- civic pageants;
- canonization imagery;
- commercial flags and devotional objects.

The artistic image usually favors a large white bifurcated standard, because it is visually more dramatic than a small single-tailed pennon. Annunciation scenes are frequently placed on a reverse or incorporated into the large flag, perpetuating the source conflation.

Clément de Fauquembergue’s 1429 sketch is often reproduced as though it documented the textile’s appearance. The BnF correctly identifies the sketch as imaginative: Fauquembergue recorded news reaching Paris but never saw Joan. [BnF study](https://classes.bnf.fr/pdf/heros_2.pdf).

### 9.7 Political and national reception

The banner tradition has served Catholic, monarchist, republican, military, and nationalist appropriations of Joan. The physical pennon’s short documented life is rarely distinguished in political imagery; “the standard of Joan” functions instead as a portable sign of divine mission, French resistance, national recovery, or religious fidelity.

This symbolic appropriation is historically real, but it does not add links to the original textile’s provenance.

### 9.8 Commercial replicas

Modern replicas are sold in multiple incompatible designs. Their variation reflects the contradictory source dossier:

- white versus partly blue fields;
- one tail versus two;
- Annunciation versus Last Judgment;
- French arms versus scattered fleurs-de-lis;
- *Jhesus Maria* versus *De par le Roy du Ciel*;
- painted versus embroidered construction.

No commercial design can properly be advertised as an exact facsimile.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Primary evidence base

The principal evidence consists of:

1. the royal financial account for Poulnoir’s large and small standards;
2. Joan’s interrogations at Rouen in 1431;
3. the 1431 La Rochelle report;
4. Perceval de Cagny’s chronicle, circa 1438;
5. Jean Pasquerel’s 1456 testimony;
6. Dunois’s 1456 testimony;
7. the *Journal du siège d’Orléans*, in its later fifteenth-century compilation;
8. the *Chronique de la Pucelle*;
9. other contemporary or near-contemporary reports by Fauquembergue, the anonymous Parisian bourgeois, Antonio Morosini, Eberhard Windecke, Enguerrand de Monstrelet, and others.

These sources differ because they describe different objects, different sides, furled or moving textiles, or information received at second hand.

### 10.2 Quicherat

Jules Quicherat’s five-volume documentary edition, published from 1841 to 1849, made the central texts widely available. It remains historiographically foundational, although later editions by Pierre Tisset and Yvonne Lanhers offer a more modern treatment of the condemnation trial.

Quicherat’s publication also unintentionally enabled historical-looking reconstructions and possible pseudo-medieval images to incorporate newly publicized details.

### 10.3 Wallon

Henri Wallon synthesized the sources into a coherent two-object reconstruction: large standard plus Annunciation pennon. His documentary appendices preserve useful transcriptions, but his finished visual scheme is more certain than the sources permit. [Wallon’s discussion](https://ste-jeannedarc.fr/histoire_wallon/appendice15.html).

### 10.4 Adrien Harmand

Harmand’s 1929 study of Joan’s costume and arms treated the pennon as a normal smaller companion to a captain’s standard. He concluded that it was scorched or partly burned at Orléans and then disappeared from the record. His assertion that it was certainly not replaced exceeds the evidence, but his separation of the pennon from the later campaign standard is methodologically useful. [Harmand](https://jeanne.wiki/harmand-costumes-armure/etendard).

### 10.5 Colonel de Liocourt

A 1974 study by Colonel de Liocourt offered the first extensive modern technical investigation of fifteenth-century standards and Joan’s ensigns. Contamine acknowledges its foundational collection of evidence while rejecting many of its conclusions. Its importance is historiographical rather than dispositive.

### 10.6 Philippe Contamine

Contamine’s “Remarques critiques sur les étendards de Jeanne d’Arc,” published in *Francia* 34/1, is the central modern critical treatment. Its major contributions are:

- insistence that Joan had two painted ensigns;
- separation of the Annunciation pennon from the Last Judgment standard;
- attention to how cloth folds and battlefield display distorted observation;
- rejection of mechanically combining all testimony into a two-sided image;
- warning about detailed images of uncertain date;
- reconstruction of a probable Gabriel–Mary pennon with a possible blue, fleur-de-lis-bearing tail;
- acknowledgment that Poulnoir’s artistic work cannot be replicated exactly;
- refusal to treat any reconstruction as definitive.

The article’s conclusion is appropriately limited: surviving texts permit several conceptions of the originals, not exact copies. [Journal record and PDF access](https://journals.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/index.php/fr/article/view/45032); [abridged notice in the *Bulletin de la Société nationale des Antiquaires de France*](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bsnaf_0081-1181_2011_num_2004_1_10892).

### 10.7 Outstanding questions

The following cannot presently be established:

- exact completion date;
- the small ensign’s fabric;
- exact dimensions and shape;
- whether it was fringed;
- whether its ground was wholly white;
- complete iconography;
- exact inscription;
- whether a dove appeared;
- whether a blue shield or blue tail appeared;
- whether it had fleurs-de-lis;
- whether it was blessed at Blois;
- identity of its bearer;
- severity of the Orléans fire damage;
- whether it was repaired;
- date and mechanism of final destruction or loss;
- whether any unrecognized fragment survives;
- whether Poulnoir was Scottish;
- whether Joan personally dictated the Annunciation program.

### 10.8 Discredited or weakly supported theories

The following should not be presented as facts:

- that an extant church or museum flag is the original;
- that modern dimensions derive from a medieval measurement;
- that the original pennon’s two faces are exactly known;
- that it accompanied Joan to Reims and Compiègne;
- that it was definitely captured and burned by the Burgundians;
- that Joan personally embroidered it;
- that Poulnoir’s name is securely equivalent to “Hamish Power”;
- that Fauquembergue drew the actual pennon from life;
- that the pennon was a magical talisman;
- that a modern reconstruction possesses relic status through ceremonial use.

### Assessment of the record

The object is not legendary in the strict sense: the contemporary payment account demonstrates that a small standard was physically manufactured. Its **exact appearance**, however, is reconstructive; its **post-fire survival** is unresolved; and every presently existing textile bearing its identity is modern. The seam between artifact and tradition is unusually sharp: physical history ends at Orléans in April 1429, while representational and ceremonial history continues and expands from the fifteenth century to the present.
