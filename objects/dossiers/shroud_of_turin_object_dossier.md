# The Shroud of Turin: Object Dossier

**Object tracked:** the linen cloth presently conserved in Turin Cathedral, not every burial cloth, sudarium, mandylion, or acheiropoietic image historically associated with Jesus.

**Research cut-off:** 6 September 2026.

**Evidentiary key**

- **Documented:** supported by contemporary documents, physical inspection, or an institution’s custody records.
- **Scientific finding:** an observation or measurement published with an identified method and sample.
- **Institutional or pious tradition:** maintained by a church or devotional community but not independently demonstrated.
- **Scholarly inference:** a reconstruction from incomplete evidence.
- **Disputed claim:** seriously contested interpretation of evidence.
- **Legend:** narrative lacking a demonstrable chain to the Turin cloth.
- **Unresolved:** evidence does not presently permit a secure conclusion.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Identity, class, and names

The Shroud of Turin is a rectangular linen textile bearing faint, approximately life-sized frontal and dorsal images of a naked adult male, arranged head-to-head as though a cloth had been laid lengthwise beneath a body, passed over the head, and returned over its front. Red-brown marks occur at locations interpreted as wounds from scourging, punctures around the head, nails through the wrists or hands and feet, and a wound in the side. Christian tradition identifies it as the burial cloth of Jesus of Nazareth. The scientifically dated textile, however, yielded a medieval radiocarbon range, and its secure documentary history begins only in fourteenth-century France.

Relevant names include:

- **Italian:** *Sindone di Torino*, *Santa Sindone*. *Sindone* derives through Latin *sindon* from Greek σινδών, *sindōn*, a fine linen cloth or sheet.
- **French:** *Suaire de Turin*, historically *Saint Suaire* and *Suaire de Lirey*. French *suaire*, from Latin *sudarium*, properly means a sweat-cloth or face-cloth but came to denote a funerary shroud.
- **Latin:** *sindon*, *sudarium*, *pannus sepulturae dominicae* (“cloth of the Lord’s burial”), and in the 1390 papal documentation *figura seu representatio sudarii* (“figure or representation of the shroud”).
- **English:** *Shroud of Turin* or *Turin Shroud*.
- **Spanish:** *Sábana Santa de Turín*.
- **German:** *Turiner Grabtuch*.

These terms are not interchangeable in early Christian sources. The canonical Gospels variously mention a *sindōn*, *othonia* (“linen cloths”), and a *soudarion* associated with Jesus’s burial. None describes a miraculously formed body image. Likewise, the later Image of Edessa or Mandylion was normally described as a facial image. Applying “Shroud of Turin” retrospectively to any ancient *sindōn*, *soudarion*, or Mandylion therefore begs the question of identity.

### 1.2 Dimensions and textile

Published measurements vary because the cloth is irregular, stretched differently when mounted, and was relaxed and re-supported in 2002. Traditional dimensions were about **436 × 110 cm**. Measurements associated with the 2002 conservation produced side lengths of approximately **441.5 and 442.5 cm**, and widths of about **113.0 and 113.7 cm**. A 2026 analysis described the principal textile as approximately **4.30 × 1.08 m**, with an attached side strip about **3.80 × 0.08 m**. The variation reflects measurement conventions, not evidence that substantial modern fabric was added to its full length.

The main fabric is linen woven in a conspicuous **three-to-one herringbone twill**. The yarn is hand-spun; published descriptions commonly report Z-twist, though the precise textile terminology and the technological implications of the weave have been debated. A narrow strip runs along one long side, attached by a seam except where a portion is missing. The original loom, workshop, flax source, and weaver are unknown.

There is no known maker’s mark, identifying inscription, securely demonstrated signature, or museum accession number. Alleged Greek, Latin, or Aramaic lettering detected photographically has not achieved general acceptance and should not be treated as a physical inscription.

### 1.3 Images and marks

The cloth contains several visually and materially distinct classes of mark:

1. **Body image:** pale yellow-brown, extremely low-contrast discoloration forming the front and back of a bearded male figure. The image is most legible in photographs and especially in a photographic negative. It has no conventional painted background and is discontinuous at the microscopic level.
2. **Red-brown “blood” areas:** marks at the scalp, forearms, wrists, feet, back, and side. Their chemistry is disputed; blood-derived material, pigment, or a mixture have each been reported.
3. **1532 fire damage:** paired longitudinal scorch lines and symmetrically repeated holes created while the cloth was folded in a reliquary. Molten material from the heated silver container penetrated the folded layers.
4. **Earlier burn holes:** smaller sets of approximately L-shaped holes, represented in a painted copy dated 1516 and therefore predating the Chambéry fire. Their cause and date are unknown.
5. **Water stains:** repeated rhomboidal and other tide marks, some plausibly connected with extinguishing the 1532 fire and some possibly older.
6. **Losses and sampling cuts:** missing corner or edge material, the “Raes” sampling area from 1973, and the adjacent strip removed for radiocarbon dating in 1988.

No persuasive evidence establishes the height, ethnicity, blood group, or individual identity of the represented man. Anthropometric reconstructions depend on where an investigator places indistinct image boundaries and on assumptions about projection.

### 1.4 Repairs and current fabric assembly

In 1534 Poor Clare nuns at Chambéry:

- covered the fire holes with triangular or irregular patches;
- stitched the Shroud to a full-length supporting linen conventionally called the **Holland cloth**;
- stabilized burned and weakened areas.

In the 2002 conservation intervention directed technically by textile conservator Mechthild Flury-Lemberg:

- the 1534 patches were removed;
- charred debris beneath them was collected and conserved separately;
- the sixteenth-century Holland backing was removed;
- a new, lighter supporting cloth was attached;
- wrinkles and accumulated tension were reduced;
- the Shroud was thereafter stored flat rather than rolled.

This was a material alteration of the historical object, not merely cleaning. It improved access to the reverse and reduced mechanical strain but removed a sixteenth-century layer that was itself part of the artifact’s history. Critics objected to the limited prior public consultation and loss of contextual information; custodians presented the work as necessary conservation. The removed backing, patches, and charred fibers survive under archiepiscopal control rather than having been discarded. The official Turin account documents the fire damage and restoration history.[Official physical account](https://www.sindone.org/telo/index.html) [Official restoration account](https://sindone.org/en/home-english/the-shroud/the-fire/the-restoration/)

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 What can be established about manufacture

**Documented:** The cloth’s maker, commissioner, workshop, production place, and original purpose are unknown.

**Scientific finding:** Accelerator-mass-spectrometry measurements made in 1988 at Arizona, Oxford, and Zürich dated the tested linen to **AD 1260–1390 at at least 95% confidence** under the calibration and statistical procedures then used. The result fits the period immediately before its documented appearance at Lirey.[Damon et al., *Nature*](https://www.shroud.com/nature.htm)

The test dated flax cellulose, not the image. Even if the textile is medieval, radiocarbon dating alone cannot identify its maker or image-forming method. Conversely, proof that an image-forming mechanism is unusual would not by itself make the cloth ancient or identify it with Jesus.

### 2.2 Earliest secure attestation of the Turin/Lirey cloth

The first secure documentary complex belongs to **Lirey in Champagne in the 1350s and 1389–1390**.

Geoffroi I de Charny founded a collegiate church at Lirey. The surviving foundation and indulgence documents refer to the church and its relics but do not identify this shroud. Later documents report that the cloth had been publicly exhibited around 1355–1356. A fourteenth-century pilgrim badge recovered from the Seine in 1855 depicts a head-to-head frontal and dorsal body image above the arms of Charny and Vergy; it is strong material evidence that a cloth closely resembling the Turin Shroud was exhibited under Charny patronage.

The earliest surviving narrative accusation is an undated draft memorandum conventionally assigned to 1389, written for Pierre d’Arcis, bishop of Troyes, to the Avignon claimant Clement VII. D’Arcis alleged that:

- the Lirey clergy had procured a cloth “cunningly painted” with front and back images;
- it had been falsely presented as Christ’s actual burial shroud;
- his predecessor Henri de Poitiers investigated it;
- an unnamed artist confessed to making it.

The manuscript is a draft, not a sealed papal submission. No independent record of Henri’s alleged investigation or the artist’s confession survives, and the artist is never named. Thus the memorandum is contemporary evidence of an accusation, not surviving proof of a confession. Its language nevertheless shows that medieval church authorities already considered deliberate manufacture a live possibility.[D’Arcis memorandum and Thurston translation](https://www.priory-of-sion.com/biblios/links/memorandum.html)

Clement VII authorized renewed displays but ordered that the clergy announce plainly that the object was not Christ’s true burial cloth, but a **figure or representation** of it. His 6 January 1390 letter also constrained Bishop d’Arcis’s opposition. Clement did not issue a scientific authentication; he regulated a devotional and jurisdictional dispute during the Western Schism.[Clement VII’s 1390 letter](https://www.shroud-of-turin.com/timeline/1390-letter-pope-clement-vii-to-pierre-darcis-troyes/)

### 2.3 The proposed ancient lineage

Advocates of authenticity have attempted to bridge the roughly thirteen centuries between Jesus and Lirey through the following sequence:

**Jerusalem → Edessa → Constantinople → disappearance during the Fourth Crusade → western Europe → Lirey.**

Every identification in that sequence is disputed.

#### Gospel burial cloths

The Gospels are the earliest sources for cloths used in Jesus’s burial. They do not say that an image appeared upon them, do not describe the Turin cloth’s head-to-head format, and do not provide a custody history after the empty-tomb narratives.

#### Abgar and Edessa

Eusebius of Caesarea’s early-fourth-century *Ecclesiastical History* transmits correspondence between Jesus and King Abgar of Edessa and a healing mission by Thaddeus. It contains **no image and no cloth**.

The Syriac *Doctrine of Addai*, generally dated around the late fourth or early fifth century, adds a painted portrait made by the messenger Hannan. This is a human-made image, not yet the Turin-type bodily imprint.

By the sixth century, Evagrius Scholasticus described an Edessan image “made without hands.” Later Greek versions say Jesus pressed his face onto a cloth, and the 944 *Narratio de imagine Edessena* developed the legend surrounding the Image’s translation to Constantinople.

Ian Wilson and other Shroud-authenticity advocates proposed that the Mandylion was actually the Turin Shroud folded repeatedly—interpreting Greek *tetradiplon* as “doubled in four”—so that only its face was visible. Historians including Andrea Nicolotti and Emmanuel Poulle reject the identification: the Mandylion tradition normally concerns a small facial image; sources distinguish it from burial linens; and Constantinopolitan inventories or narratives can mention the Mandylion and burial relics separately. The proposed fold is ingenious but does not establish identity.[Review of Nicolotti’s source study](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/tmr/article/view/20176) [Poulle’s source analysis](https://philpapers.org/rec/POULSD-4) [Doctrine of Addai bibliography](https://www.nasscal.com/e-clavis-christian-apocrypha/doctrine-of-addai/)

#### Constantinople in 1203–1204

The crusader Robert de Clari reported a *sydoine* at the church of St Mary of Blachernae “where Our Lord was wrapped,” which was raised upright every Friday so that his figure could be seen. After Constantinople’s capture in 1204, Robert said no one knew what became of it.

This is an important independent witness to a claimed burial cloth bearing or revealing a figure in Constantinople. It does **not** supply dimensions, weave, damage pattern, or a transfer to the Charnys. The object might have been the Turin cloth, another relic, a painted liturgical cloth, or something described inaccurately by Robert.

A frequently cited 1205 letter attributed to Theodore Angelos complaining that sacred objects, including Christ’s burial linen, had been taken to Athens has a contested textual history and does not provide a chain to Lirey. Proposed Templar, Othon de la Roche, Besançon, or Charny transmission routes remain hypotheses without an unbroken documentary series.

### 2.4 Origin conclusions

Three propositions should not be conflated:

- The **traditional origin** is Jerusalem around AD 30 as Jesus’s burial cloth.
- The **earliest secure appearance of the extant candidate** is medieval Lirey.
- The **best direct age measurement yet performed** gives AD 1260–1390, though its sampling design and statistics remain contested.

There is currently no document identifying a quarry, loom, workshop, commission, named maker, or first purchaser.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 Lirey and the Charnys, c. 1355–1418

**c. 1355–1356 — Lirey:** Later ecclesiastical documents state that exhibitions occurred at the collegiate church. Geoffroi I de Charny and Jeanne de Vergy are the first family association supported by the badge and later records. How Geoffroi obtained the cloth is unrecorded.

**1356:** Geoffroi died at Poitiers. The object’s exact physical location immediately afterward is not continuously documented.

**1389:** Geoffroi II de Charny and the Lirey clergy renewed exhibitions. Bishop Pierre d’Arcis attempted to stop them, leading to secular enforcement actions and the appeal to Clement VII.

**1390:** Clement VII permitted display only under conditions designed to prevent presentation as the authentic burial cloth. This was neither confiscation nor authentication: custody remained associated with Lirey and Charny.

**1398:** Geoffroi II died. Custodial interests passed through his daughter Marguerite de Charny.

**1418:** During the insecurity of the Hundred Years’ War, Lirey’s canons entrusted the cloth to Marguerite and her husband Humbert de Villersexel for safekeeping, reportedly at Montfort. It was supposed to be returned but never was.

### 3.2 Marguerite de Charny’s itinerant custody, 1418–1453

Marguerite moved with the cloth among family strongholds and displayed it outside Lirey. Documentary notices or later legal proceedings place it or its exhibition at locations including Saint-Hippolyte/Montfort, Chimay, Mons, Liège, and Germolles. Not every stop is equally secure, and “presence at court” should not automatically be treated as a public exposition.

Lirey’s canons sued for restitution. Judgments were issued at Dole in 1443 and Besançon in 1447. Marguerite maintained that the cloth was her family’s property, whereas the canons treated it as church property temporarily removed for safety.

On **22 March 1453**, at Geneva, Marguerite transferred the cloth to Duke Louis I of Savoy and Anne of Cyprus. The juridical instruments describe benefits bestowed upon her—Varambon castle and revenues from Miribel—in return for “valuable services.” Whether styled gift or donation, the exchange had material consideration. Because canon law restricted commerce in relics, euphemistic drafting is unsurprising. It is therefore safest to call this a compensated transfer rather than an uncomplicated pious gift.

The Lirey chapter did not consent. Ecclesiastical pressure culminated in Marguerite’s threatened or pronounced excommunication in 1457. The cloth was not restored. The conflict ended practically, though not by consensual restitution.[Official historical outline](https://sindone.org/en/home-english/the-shroud/historical-outline/) [Chronological documentary compilation](https://www.shroud.com/history.htm)

### 3.3 Savoy custody and travel, 1453–1578

The Shroud became a dynastic relic of the House of Savoy and moved with the itinerant court. Locations reported in the later fifteenth century include Geneva, Chambéry, and sites in Piedmont. Its custody becomes firmer in Savoy chapel inventories; a 1483 inventory is an important documentary anchor.

**1502:** It was deposited in the ducal Sainte-Chapelle at Chambéry.

**1506:** Pope Julius II approved a proper Mass and Office of the Holy Shroud and fixed its feast on 4 May. This institutionalized the cult; it did not resolve the cloth’s historical identity. Indulgences increased the chapel’s status as a pilgrimage center.

**4 December 1532 — Chambéry fire:** Fire reached the Sainte-Chapelle. The Shroud was folded inside a silver reliquary. Heat melted or softened metal that penetrated successive folds, producing repeated holes and scorches. Water used during the response contributed stains.

**1534:** Poor Clare nuns repaired and backed the cloth. Their work remained attached until 2002.

Political and military conditions caused further travel. The relic accompanied, or was removed with, the Savoy court and treasury through parts of Savoy and Piedmont. Assertions that it occupied one fixed chapel continuously between 1534 and 1578 oversimplify an itinerant dynastic treasury.

### 3.4 Transfer to Turin and the Guarini Chapel, 1578–1939

In 1578 Duke Emmanuel Philibert transferred the Shroud from Chambéry to Turin. The public explanation was to shorten the penitential pilgrimage promised by Archbishop Carlo Borromeo of Milan after the plague. The move also embedded the leading Savoy relic in the duchy’s new capital. It arrived on 14 September and was displayed at Turin.[Official account of the 1578 transfer](https://sindone.org/ostensioni/1578-2/)

The move became permanent despite Chambéry’s earlier status. The cloth was kept in and around Turin Cathedral and the royal complex, with repeated public and private ostensions for dynastic marriages, births, military events, jubilees, and devotional occasions.

Architect Guarino Guarini designed the monumental Chapel of the Holy Shroud joining the cathedral and royal palace. In **1694** the cloth was installed there in a purpose-built altar-reliquary. The chapel’s architecture proclaimed joint royal and sacred possession.

Sebastiano Valfrè undertook repairs in 1694, reinforcing weakened areas and adjusting earlier work. The object remained normally folded or rolled within nested reliquaries, conditions later judged suboptimal for the textile.

### 3.5 Photography and early twentieth-century custody

During the 1898 exposition, lawyer and amateur photographer **Secondo Pia** made the first successful photographs. While developing his plates, he observed that the photographic negative appeared as a more natural positive portrait. This did not prove that the cloth was itself a “photographic negative” in the technical sense; it showed that light and dark values in the body image are broadly reversed relative to ordinary visual expectations. Pia’s result was confirmed by Giuseppe Enrie’s more controlled photographs in 1931.[Official 1898 exhibition record](https://sindone.org/en/home-english/expositions/2057-2/) [Metropolitan Museum record for an Enrie photograph](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/687615)

Important public displays included 1898, 1931, and 1933. The expanding photographic archive transformed the Shroud from a rarely visible royal relic into a globally reproducible image.

### 3.6 Wartime evacuation, 1939–1946

In September 1939, with war beginning, the Shroud was secretly removed from Turin. After stops in Rome and Naples it reached the Benedictine abbey of Montevergine near Avellino on 25 September. Only a small number of monks were informed of its identity. It was concealed there through the war rather than left in a prominent northern target. It returned to Turin on 28 October 1946.

Stories that Adolf Hitler or an SS unit specifically sought the Shroud are later claims with weak documentary support. Wartime removal is documented; a special Nazi relic hunt is not.

### 3.7 Scientific access and transfer to the Holy See, 1969–present

**1969 and 1973:** Cardinal Michele Pellegrino authorized expert commissions and photography. In 1973 Gilbert Raes removed textile samples from an edge for examination. The 1973 television exposition brought the cloth to a mass audience.

**1978:** The cloth was publicly displayed for 43 days; official figures reported more than three million visitors. Forty-four researchers then received approximately 120 hours of direct access in the Shroud of Turin Research Project, or STURP. They photographed, spectroscopically examined, imaged, and applied adhesive sampling tapes to selected locations.[Official 1978 record](https://sindone.org/ostensioni/1978-2/)

**1983–1984:** Former king Umberto II died in 1983. Under his testament the Shroud passed from the House of Savoy to the Holy See; the Vatican describes itself as legal owner from 1984. The archbishop of Turin serves as pontifical custodian. This transfer separated legal ownership from physical location: the cloth remained in Turin.[Vatican press-office ownership statement](https://press.vatican.va/content/salastampa/it/bollettino/pubblico/2014/11/05/0820/01723.html)

**21 April 1988:** An approximately 8 × 2 cm edge strip adjacent to the Raes area was removed in Turin Cathedral’s sacristy, divided, and supplied to laboratories at Arizona, Oxford, and Zürich. The British Museum coordinated sample identification and statistical treatment. Control textiles accompanied the Shroud specimens.

**11–12 April 1997:** Fire devastated the Guarini Chapel and part of the adjacent palace. Because the Shroud had been moved from its normal altar during restoration works, it was in a cathedral display case. Firefighter Mario Trematore and colleagues smashed 39-mm protective glass and removed the reliquary. An inspection on 14 April found no damage to the cloth.[Contemporary fire and inspection dossier](https://www.shroud.com/fire.htm)

**1998, 2000, 2010, 2015:** Major public expositions took place. Televised or digital presentations occurred in 1973, 1983, 2013, and during the COVID-19 period.

**2002:** The conservation intervention described above removed the 1534 repairs and backing and installed a new support.

**2008:** A high-resolution digital photographic campaign produced detailed imagery without reopening the question of authenticity.

**2025:** Turin’s Jubilee initiative used a digital presentation rather than exposing the original. The cloth stayed in its conservation case.[2025 digital exhibition](https://www.diocesi.torino.it/ufficiostampa/aperta-la-tenda-di-avvolti-in-piazza-castello-mons-giraudo-lincontro-con-la-sindone-digitale-unesperienza-vera/)

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 The propositions under dispute

“Authenticity” can mean several different things:

1. Is the Turin cloth the same physical object documented at Lirey?
2. Is it the Constantinopolitan burial cloth described by Robert de Clari?
3. Is it the Edessa Mandylion?
4. Is it genuinely ancient linen?
5. Did it wrap an actual crucified person?
6. Did it wrap Jesus specifically?
7. Was its image formed naturally, artistically, or miraculously?

A positive answer to one does not logically establish the others.

Continuity from Lirey to Turin is well documented after the late fourteenth century. Continuity before Lirey is not.

### 4.2 The medieval documentary case

The strongest historical evidence for medieval manufacture is cumulative:

- no secure reference to this particular object before the mid-fourteenth century;
- its sudden appearance amid a western European relic cult;
- the near-contemporary d’Arcis allegation that an artist made it;
- Clement VII’s insistence that it be called only a representation;
- a radiocarbon interval spanning AD 1260–1390.

The weaknesses are that d’Arcis’s named evidence has not survived, his memorandum was polemical and jurisdictional, and no maker, contract, payment, or workshop record has been found.

### 4.3 Radiocarbon dating

The 1988 measurements were:

- Arizona: weighted average approximately **646 ± 31 radiocarbon years BP**;
- Zürich: **676 ± 24 BP**;
- Oxford: **750 ± 30 BP**.

The combined calibrated range was published as **AD 1260–1390, 95% confidence**. Control samples produced acceptable dates. The measurements remain the most direct replicated dating of authenticated material removed under formal supervision.[Damon et al., *Nature*](https://www.shroud.com/nature.htm)

Objections include:

- all material came from one edge rather than several regions;
- the sampling protocol was reduced from seven laboratories and multiple methods to three AMS laboratories;
- the sampled edge may have experienced unusual handling, water, heat, or repair;
- laboratory subsamples were statistically heterogeneous;
- later analyses of released raw data reported a spatial age gradient;
- Raymond Rogers argued that fibers associated with the sample contained dye and gum suggesting a rewoven repair.

Counterarguments include:

- textile experts observed no woven discontinuity indicating an “invisible reweave”;
- Rachel Freer-Waters and A. J. Timothy Jull examined retained Arizona material and found linen consistent with the main cloth, not a demonstrable patch;
- Bella, Garlaschelli, and Samperi reported no mass-spectrometric evidence supporting Rogers’s specific invisible-mending interpretation;
- ordinary contamination would have to be quantitatively large and resistant to pretreatment to move genuinely first-century flax into the medieval period.

A 2020 statistical review concluded that the 1988 design would not meet current best practice and that heterogeneity complicates the combined confidence interval; it explicitly did **not** determine a different age. A sound new program would require authenticated samples from multiple regions, blanks and historical controls, disclosed raw data, and pre-registered treatment rules.[2020 statistical review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7597180/)

A **2026 peer-reviewed analysis** examined two tiny fragments retained from the 1988 Arizona material. It investigated the textile fragments rather than the image. Its importance lies in renewed direct work on authenticated radiocarbon-sample remnants, not in overturning the medieval result.[2026 textile-fragment study](https://doi.org/10.1038/s40494-026-02530-7)

### 4.4 Image chemistry: STURP versus McCrone

STURP researchers reported that the body-image color resides largely in extremely superficial yellowing of linen fibrils, caused by oxidation, dehydration, and conjugation of cellulose. Their final summary said the image was not produced by conventional painting and represented a real human form. They found no quantity of pigment adequate, in their judgment, to account for the entire body image.

Microscopist Walter McCrone, examining STURP adhesive tapes, reached the opposite conclusion. He reported:

- red ochre particles concentrated in image areas;
- vermilion in selected red “blood” areas;
- a proteinaceous tempera medium;
- no persuasive blood.

He interpreted the object as a fourteenth-century painting made shortly before its appearance. His papers remain a genuine scientific counter-position, not a rumor.[McCrone and Skirius, microscopy I](https://mccroneinstitute.org/28-3-4-light-microscopical-study-of-the-turin-shroud-1/) [McCrone, microscopy II](https://mccroneinstitute.org/28-3-4-light-microscopical-study-turin-shroud-2/) [McCrone et al., microscopy III](https://mccroneinstitute.org/29-1-microscopical-study-of-the-turin-shroud-3/)

The disagreement concerns whether particles are image-forming material, later contamination, or additions to pre-existing stains. The lack of new, independently distributed image-area samples prevents decisive replication.

### 4.5 “Blood” findings

John Heller and Alan Adler reported spectroscopic and chemical evidence of heme-derived porphyrin and concluded that blood was present in the red-stained areas. Other investigators reported proteins, albumin, bilirubin, or immunological reactions and proposed human blood, sometimes blood group AB.[Heller and Adler, *Applied Optics*](https://opg.optica.org/ao/abstract.cfm?uri=ao-19-16-2742)

Reservations include:

- some tests were performed on extremely small tape-derived samples;
- several chemical responses are not uniquely diagnostic of blood;
- pigment contamination is independently present;
- serological typing of old, contaminated material is especially vulnerable to nonspecific reactions;
- no validated DNA profile identifies one injured individual, much less Jesus.

A 2017 study proposed that blood-derived material had later been reinforced with pigments. That hybrid explanation illustrates why “blood or paint” need not be an exhaustive binary.[Blood-and-pigment study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1296207417300092)

### 4.6 Pollen, dust, and biological traces

Max Frei-Sulzer collected surface material using adhesive tapes in 1973 and 1978 and identified pollen he regarded as indicating Palestine, Anatolia, and Constantinople. His work is disputed because identification was based largely on light microscopy, sample custody was imperfectly documented, and later reviewers could not reproduce all claimed taxa.

Plant-image claims advanced by Avinoam Danin and others—flowers allegedly visible photographically and diagnostic of the Jerusalem region—depend heavily on subjective pattern recognition and have not become a historical provenance method.

DNA analyses of dusty surface samples have detected a broad mixture of human, plant, animal, and microbial sequences. Such diversity records centuries of handling, display, repairs, brushes, containers, and environmental exposure. It cannot normally locate flax manufacture or identify the represented individual.

A 2025/2026 metagenomic study of 1978 samples reported diverse biological material, including human lineages interpreted by its authors as compatible with South Asian connections. The samples are mixed surface deposits, however, not a sealed biological assemblage. The result is evidence of contamination history, not proof that the linen was woven in India or traveled there.

### 4.7 WAXS and alternative dates

In 2022 Liberato De Caro and colleagues applied wide-angle X-ray scattering to a claimed Shroud thread, measuring cellulose crystallinity and comparing degradation with historically dated linens. Under assumed average storage temperature and humidity, they argued for an age compatible with roughly two millennia.

Limitations are substantial:

- the sample’s custody and representativeness are disputed;
- cellulose degradation depends on humidity, heat, chemical treatments, microorganisms, and storage, all incompletely known;
- the method is not an established replacement for radiocarbon dating;
- its model is calibrated with a limited reference series;
- the 1532 fire and later conservation history complicate environmental assumptions.

The WAXS result is therefore a disputed experimental age estimate, not a generally accepted redating.

### 4.8 Anatomical and image-formation arguments

Pro-authenticity arguments emphasize:

- superficially discolored fibrils;
- lack of obvious brush strokes;
- negative-like tonal reversal;
- correspondence with wounds of crucifixion;
- apparent encoding of intensity information usable to make relief-like images;
- difficulty reproducing all reported characteristics simultaneously.

Medieval-manufacture arguments emphasize:

- the radiocarbon age;
- the Lirey context and d’Arcis accusation;
- elongated anatomy and iconographic conventions;
- image geometry more similar to orthogonal projection than to cloth wrapped around a three-dimensional body;
- known medieval technologies using pigment, rubbing, bas-relief, scorching, or combinations of techniques.

Experimental replicas made by Joe Nickell, Luigi Garlaschelli, Nicholas Allen, and others demonstrate that recognizable negative-like shroud images can be produced without modern photography, though none is universally accepted as matching every microscopic characteristic. Producing an approximation refutes claims of absolute impossibility but does not identify the actual technique.

### 4.9 Ecclesiastical position

The Catholic Church venerates the Shroud but has not promulgated a dogmatic judgment that it is Jesus’s authentic burial cloth. Modern popes frequently call it an **icon**, **sign**, or **mirror of the Gospel**. The Holy See owns the object and authorizes exhibitions and destructive sampling; legal custody and spiritual interpretation have therefore converged in the papacy, but scientific authenticity has not been canonically decreed.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

| Person, institution, or being | Association | Date and evidentiary status |
|---|---|---|
| Jesus of Nazareth | Traditionally the man whose corpse was wrapped in the cloth | c. AD 30; pious tradition, not historically demonstrated |

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Lirey pilgrimage object

At Lirey, the cloth functioned as a pilgrimage attraction. It was displayed by clergy using ceremonial lights and drew offerings. D’Arcis presented this as commercial deception; the Charny and collegiate position framed it as legitimate devotion. The pilgrim badge demonstrates an organized cult with reproducible souvenirs.

Clement VII’s settlement preserved the ritual while controlling its language. Public exposition was permitted, but an announcement had to distinguish the cloth from Christ’s authentic shroud. This is a notable case of an institution permitting veneration while withholding literal authentication.

### 6.2 Savoy dynastic relic

After 1453 the Shroud served the House of Savoy as:

- a sacred treasure validating the dynasty’s religious prestige;
- a focus for court liturgy;
- a relic displayed for marriages, births, treaties, victories, jubilees, and visits;
- a symbol joining Chambéry and later Turin to the Passion of Christ.

Its permanent transfer to Turin supported the new capital’s ceremonial centrality. The Guarini Chapel physically linked cathedral and palace, expressing the partnership of ecclesiastical veneration and dynastic possession.

### 6.3 Liturgical cult

Julius II’s 1506 authorization established a Mass and Office and the feast of 4 May. The present Archdiocese of Turin continues liturgical observance. The cult is directed doctrinally toward Christ, whether the textile is called relic or icon.[Turin liturgical office](https://sindone.org/vita-di-fede/liturgia/)

### 6.4 Ostension

An **ostension** is a formal public display. The Shroud is normally invisible inside its container and is displayed only by papal authorization. Historical ostensions could involve balconies, scaffolds, cathedral chancels, royal ceremonies, armed security, and enormous crowds.

Modern major ostensions include:

- 1898;
- 1931 and 1933;
- 1978;
- 1998;
- 2000;
- 2010;
- 2015.

The 1978 exposition attracted a reported three million visitors. Recent displays emphasize controlled lighting, timed admission, accessibility, video interpretation, and pastoral programming.

### 6.5 Access and touch

Historically, rulers, clergy, conservators, and selected investigators handled the cloth. Copies could become “contact relics” by being laid upon or brought close to it. Modern access is far stricter:

- the public cannot touch it;
- it is normally sealed and hidden;
- destructive sampling requires owner and custodian approval;
- conservation, movement, and photography occur under multidisciplinary supervision.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The Resurrection image

**Pious interpretation:** Christ’s body produced the image at or near the Resurrection.

The Gospels do not describe such an event. No early Christian text securely connects a miraculous full-body imprint with the Turin cloth. Radiation, corona discharge, proton or neutron emission, and earthquake-generated effects are modern physical speculations, not ancient traditions and not experimentally established explanations.

### 7.2 The Mandylion theory

**Modern scholarly/devotional hypothesis:** The Shroud was folded so that only its face showed and was revered as the Image of Edessa. It traveled to Constantinople in 944 and disappeared in 1204.

The theory was popularized especially by Ian Wilson from the 1970s. Its effects are historically real: it reorganized Shroud advocacy, encouraged fold-mark analysis, and made Byzantine iconography central to popular authenticity arguments. Its identity claim remains disputed and is rejected by specialists who find the sources distinguish the objects.

### 7.3 Templar custody

**Modern legend/hypothesis:** Knights Templar acquired the Shroud during or after the sack of Constantinople, secretly revered it, and transmitted it through a family connected with Geoffroi de Charny. The resemblance between “Charny” and the name of the Templar preceptor Geoffroi de Charney has encouraged the story.

No surviving Templar inventory or trial record securely identifies the Turin cloth. The theory fills a documentary gap with association and should not be presented as a custody link.

### 7.4 The missing-corners story

Some writers propose that missing corner pieces were cut as relics, samples, or evidence of ownership. The precise date and purpose of all edge losses are unknown. One damaged corner became the site of the Raes and radiocarbon sampling, but this does not explain the original loss.

### 7.5 The Chambéry fire as miracle

Devotional accounts emphasize that the Shroud survived molten silver and fire. Its survival is documented; interpreting it as miraculous is theological. Folding concentrated damage into repeated areas while leaving most fabric intact, providing a physical explanation for the pattern.

### 7.6 Nazi pursuit

The Shroud’s wartime concealment at Montevergine is documented. Claims that Hitler personally wanted it or that an SS search narrowly failed to seize it rest principally on later recollection and dramatic retelling. No firm seizure order has been produced.

### 7.7 Photographic “revelation”

Pia’s 1898 negatives are often described as revealing Christ’s “true face.” What they objectively revealed was a striking tonal reversal and much greater photographic legibility. Identification of the face remains a devotional conclusion.

### 7.8 Coins, flowers, and writing

Investigators have claimed to see:

- coins over the eyes, sometimes attributed to Pontius Pilate;
- Palestinian flowers;
- Greek, Latin, or Aramaic words;
- teeth, internal bones, or other anatomically hidden features.

Most depend on contrast enhancement of low-resolution, mottled imagery and are vulnerable to pareidolia. They have not been established by reproducible imaging consensus.

### 7.9 Leonardo da Vinci

The claim that Leonardo made the Shroud using proto-photography is a modern invention inconsistent with chronology: the cloth was documented before Leonardo’s birth in 1452, and its transfer to Savoy occurred in 1453. Leonardo cannot have created the object first displayed in the 1350s unless the present cloth were a later substitution, for which no evidence exists.

## 8. Current Status and Location

As of 6 September 2026, the Shroud is conserved **flat inside a sealed, climate-controlled conservation case in Turin Cathedral, Piazza San Giovanni, Turin, Italy**, in the last chapel of the left aisle beneath the Royal Tribune.

The case is enclosed in a larger metal protective box. The original cloth is **not normally visible**. Visitors see the closed installation and interpretive screens; they do not see the Shroud itself. The nearby Museo della Sindone exhibits documents, instruments, photographs, and related objects, not the original cloth.

The **Holy See owns it**. The **archbishop of Turin**, as pontifical custodian, controls day-to-day custody on the pope’s behalf. No active repatriation lawsuit is known. Lirey and Chambéry possess strong historical associations but no current legal claim recognized by the owner.

There is no fixed schedule for exhibitions. The pope decides when an ostension occurs. The 2025 Jubilee event was digital, and no next public display had been announced by the research cut-off.[Official current-location and access statement](https://sindone.org/en/home-english/the-shroud/where-is-the-shroud-now/)

Its condition is stable enough for continued controlled storage but permanently altered by:

- old burn holes and scorches;
- missing textile;
- water and handling stains;
- centuries of folding and rolling;
- historic stitching;
- removal of samples;
- the 2002 backing replacement.

The object has no conventional accession number because it is ecclesiastical property rather than a museum accession.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Photography and mass reproduction

Secondo Pia’s 1898 negative created the modern Shroud. Before photography, viewers encountered a faint textile during rare ceremonies; afterward, a sharp, reproducible face circulated in books, devotional prints, postcards, exhibitions, and newspapers.

Giuseppe Enrie’s 1931 images became the standard twentieth-century visual record. Later milestones included:

- Giovanni Battista Judica Cordiglia’s early color photography;
- STURP’s multispectral and ultraviolet work in 1978;
- Gian Durante’s 1997–2002 photography;
- high-definition digital imaging in 2008.

The Turin Shroud Museum preserves these photographic series and instruments.[Shroud Museum collections](https://www.sindone.it/en/percorso)

### 9.2 Replicas and contact copies

Painted and textile copies circulated widely from the sixteenth century onward, especially through Savoy territories, Spain, France, Italy, and religious houses. Some carry inscriptions stating that they were touched to the original. Such copies can acquire independent devotional status, but they are not rival claimants to physical identity.

Modern full-scale photographic replicas support traveling exhibitions because the original is rarely exposed. Their educational utility can also blur the distinction between direct observation and processed photography.

### 9.3 Scholarship, advocacy, and organizations

The object generated “sindonology,” an interdisciplinary but institutionally uneven field involving historians, theologians, chemists, physicians, photographers, physicists, textile specialists, and amateur researchers.

Important organizations include:

- the Centro Internazionale di Studi sulla Sindone in Turin;
- STURP in the United States;
- the British Society for the Turin Shroud;
- the Shroud of Turin Education and Research Association;
- confraternities and diocesan commissions.

Some work appears in mainstream peer-reviewed journals; much appears in specialist proceedings, devotional presses, or advocacy websites. These categories should not be assigned equal evidentiary weight.

### 9.4 Literature, film, and popular culture

The Shroud appears repeatedly in documentary television, apologetic literature, skeptical investigations, novels, and thrillers. Major modern public narratives cluster around:

- the photographic-negative discovery;
- forensic reconstruction of Jesus’s face;
- the 1988 carbon date;
- alleged Vatican secrecy;
- lost Templar custody;
- miraculous radiation at the Resurrection.

These treatments have shaped public understanding more powerfully than medieval documents. Claims that “science cannot explain the image” often mean that no single proposed process has won consensus, not that all human manufacture has been excluded.

### 9.5 Tourism and civic identity

Ostensions bring large numbers of visitors to Turin and involve municipal, regional, transport, hospitality, medical, volunteer, and security networks. The cloth functions simultaneously as sacred object, cultural heritage, scientific controversy, and tourism asset. The Archdiocese’s own reporting has acknowledged the economic and international-visibility effects of exhibitions.

### 9.6 Living controversies in 2026

Current debates concern:

- whether new multi-site radiocarbon sampling should be authorized;
- whether the 1988 data remain sufficient;
- whether WAXS constitutes a credible dating method;
- how much evidentiary value mixed DNA and pollen samples possess;
- whether the 2002 intervention sacrificed historical information;
- whether image formation can be reproduced with medieval techniques;
- how the Church should speak of an object it venerates but has not authenticated.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Medieval and early-modern historiography

The foundational archive is adversarial:

- Lirey and Charny records support possession and exhibition;
- d’Arcis attacks fraudulent presentation;
- Clement VII regulates display;
- later Lirey records argue for restitution;
- Savoy inventories document custody and ceremonial use.

The d’Arcis memorandum survives in French manuscript collections as an undated draft. Its report of an artist’s confession has never been corroborated by the confession itself. Conversely, attempts to dismiss it entirely because d’Arcis had jurisdictional or financial interests confuse bias with nonexistence; biased contemporary evidence must be weighed, not erased.

Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Savoy histories increasingly supplied illustrious or ancient antecedents. These works are sources for the cult’s development, not independent proof of first-century provenance.

### 10.2 Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century source criticism

Canon Ulysse Chevalier assembled and published medieval documentation and argued that the Shroud was a medieval fabrication. Herbert Thurston translated and analyzed the d’Arcis material for English readers. Paul Vignon developed photographic and physiological arguments supporting authenticity.

Their dispute established a pattern that persists: documentary medievalism versus physical-image exceptionalism.

### 10.3 Scientific era

The major turning points were:

- **1898:** Pia’s photography.
- **1931:** Enrie’s improved photographs.
- **1969–1973:** appointed commissions, color photography, and sampling.
- **1978:** STURP’s direct multidisciplinary examination.
- **1980–1981:** opposed publications by Heller and Adler versus McCrone.
- **1988–1989:** radiocarbon sampling, announcement, and *Nature* publication.
- **1997:** fire and emergency inspection.
- **2002:** major conservation and reverse-side access.
- **2017 onward:** recovery and reanalysis of radiocarbon raw data.
- **2022:** WAXS alternative dating claim.
- **2026:** renewed analysis of retained radiocarbon fragments.

### 10.4 Primary evidence still needed

A decisive modern campaign would ideally include:

- samples from multiple securely mapped locations;
- complete documentation of each sample’s removal and custody;
- independent textile examination before destructive testing;
- replicated AMS dating with published pretreatment chemistry and raw data;
- untargeted spectroscopy and microscopy of body-image, red-stain, and control fibers;
- sequencing controls able to distinguish environmental DNA from endogenous material;
- open, high-resolution imaging datasets;
- analysis of the removed 1534 backing, patches, charred threads, and retained Raes material as historical controls.

The owner has not authorized such comprehensive resampling.

### 10.5 State of the debate

The most defensible concise assessment is:

- **Physical existence:** certain.
- **Continuity from late-fourteenth-century Lirey to present Turin:** substantially documented.
- **Existence of a similar image-bearing cloth at Lirey in the mid-fourteenth century:** strongly supported.
- **Manufacture in AD 1260–1390:** supported by the only replicated direct radiocarbon test, but challenged on sampling and statistical grounds.
- **Identification with Constantinople’s Blachernae shroud:** possible but unproved.
- **Identification with the Edessa Mandylion:** disputed and rejected by many relevant historians.
- **First-century origin:** not established.
- **Contact with an actual corpse:** disputed.
- **Presence of blood-derived material:** supported by some chemical studies and rejected or qualified by others.
- **Specific identification with Jesus:** beyond what existing historical or scientific evidence can demonstrate.
- **Exact image-forming method:** unresolved.
