# The imiut fetish: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

“The imiut fetish” is not the name of one unique artifact with a single continuous chain of custody. It denotes an ancient Egyptian class of ritual emblem—known from images, hieroglyphic writing, textual references, and a small number of surviving three-dimensional examples—whose form and interpretation changed over more than three millennia.

This dossier therefore tracks three related but distinct subjects:

1. the **imiut as an ancient Egyptian ritual-object type and divine emblem**;
2. individual surviving imiut objects, principally:

   - the Middle Kingdom ensemble from the tomb enclosure of Imhotep at Lisht South, now divided between the Metropolitan Museum of Art and Cairo;
   - the two gold-and-calcite imiut emblems from Tutankhamun’s tomb, Carter nos. 194 and 202, historically Egyptian Museum JE 61374 and JE 61375 and now understood to belong to the Tutankhamun collection displayed at the Grand Egyptian Museum;

3. the **myths and scholarly interpretations** attached to the form.

No evidence supports treating the Lisht object, either Tutankhamun object, or any later representation as the unique original imiut. Each is an instantiation of an older emblem.

### Evidentiary labels

- **Documented:** established by an excavated object, contemporary inscription, archival record, or museum accession record.
- **Scientific/material finding:** based on physical examination or conservation analysis.
- **Scholarly inference:** a reasoned interpretation not explicitly stated by the ancient evidence.
- **Ancient theological interpretation:** an explanation supplied by an Egyptian religious text, sometimes centuries after the emblem first appeared.
- **Institutional attribution:** the identification or date used by the holding museum.
- **Traditional or popular claim:** repeated without sufficient primary evidence.
- **Unresolved:** evidence does not presently permit a secure conclusion.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Definition

The imiut was an upright ritual emblem conventionally represented as:

- a headless animal skin or skin-shaped bundle;
- suspended by its tail or rear extremity from the top of a vertical pole;
- its forelegs or elongated forward portions hanging downward;
- the pole planted in a jar, pot, or rounded base;
- the upper end sometimes shaped like a lotus bud;
- the skin sometimes stuffed or shaped into a dummy animal body.

The Spanish national cultural-heritage thesaurus defines it as an Egyptian fetish or symbol consisting of a headless, inflated animal skin, sometimes apparently feline, tied to a pole placed over a vessel. It classifies the form among ritual and ceremonial objects and notes its First Dynasty attestations and later association with Anubis. This is a useful concise institutional definition, although it does not resolve the species of animal or original function ([Tesauros del Patrimonio Cultural de España](https://tesauros.cultura.gob.es/tesauros/bienesculturales/1192033.html)).

“Fetish” is the conventional Egyptological term inherited from older comparative-religion vocabulary. It should not be mistaken for an Egyptian technical classification or for the modern sexual sense of the word. “Emblem,” “ritual standard,” “cult object,” or “skin emblem” is often more descriptively neutral.

### 1.2 Ancient name

The conventional Egyptological transliterations include:

- **jmy-wt**
- **imy-wt**
- **imiut**
- less commonly **imyut**, **imi-out**, or French **imy-out**

The expression is written with phonetic signs corresponding approximately to *jmy-wt* and sometimes with an emblematic determinative.

The most familiar translation is:

- **“the one who is in the wrappings”**
- **“he who is in the place of embalming”**
- **“the one who is in the wt”**

The uncertainty lies chiefly in *wt*. Depending on context and analysis, it has been understood as “wrappings,” an embalming place or enclosure, a skin or container, or as an older opaque noun subsequently reinterpreted through mortuary theology. Accordingly, “the one in the wrappings” is a conventional and plausible rendering, not a fully settled etymology.

Anubis bears **jmy-wt** as an epithet in offering formulas. This linguistic overlap helped modern scholars name the object the “Anubis fetish,” but it does not prove that every early occurrence represented Anubis. The relationship may have developed historically rather than existing from the emblem’s inception.

### 1.3 Animal identity

The animal cannot be identified uniformly.

Suggested identifications include:

- canid or “jackal”;
- leopard, panther, or other large feline;
- bovine or bull/cow skin;
- a deliberately composite or generalized sacrificial skin;
- a symbolic skin no longer intended to represent a particular species.

Representations are stylized, while surviving models do not necessarily contain a complete, zoologically diagnostic hide. The common popular formula “a jackal skin sacred to Anubis” is therefore too definite. German Egyptological summaries often call it a headless, mutilated jackal pelt, while iconographic studies have noted that some examples look feline and others bovine. A study of feline representation observes explicitly that scholars disagree whether the imiut skin is leopard or bull and that late Eighteenth Dynasty versions can look feline ([Castel, “Panthers, leopards and cheetahs”](https://mmolipol.webs.ull.es/TdE1/TdE1-2Castel.pdf)).

### 1.4 Standard physical components

Across images and excavated examples, the canonical components are:

1. **Skin or pseudo-carcass.** A headless body with pendant forelimbs or limb-like strips. The hindquarters may be absent or visually merged with the suspension point.
2. **Bindings.** Linen bands secure the form to the pole.
3. **Pole.** Normally wood in functional examples; gold or gilded material in elite models.
4. **Finial.** Often interpreted as a lotus bud, though not every depiction preserves it clearly.
5. **Vessel or base.** A jar-shaped support that may contain ointment or resin.
6. **Optional enclosure.** Some examples were placed inside a shrine or naos.
7. **Optional guardians.** The Lisht ensemble included two standing guardian figures.

The object’s precise status varied. It could be:

- a full-sized or near-full-sized ritual implement;
- a funerary deposit;
- a miniature precious-metal model;
- a hieroglyphic or emblematic image;
- a divine standard in temple decoration;
- a painted symbol on coffins or papyri.

### 1.5 The Lisht South imiut

The best documented surviving organic/composite example is Metropolitan Museum of Art **14.3.19–.20**.

**Institutional identification**

- Title: *Anubis Fetish (Imiut)*
- Date: approximately 1919–1885 BCE
- Period: Middle Kingdom, Dynasty 12
- Reign assigned by the museum: Amenemhat II
- Provenance: tomb of Imhotep, Lisht South, Memphite region
- Excavation: Metropolitan Museum Egyptian Expedition, 1913–14
- Materials: travertine or Egyptian alabaster, cedar, animal skin and linen structure, and ointment/resin residue
- Vase height: 9.2 cm
- Vase diameter: 10 cm
- Overall height of rod and vase: 59.3 cm
- Present display: Metropolitan Museum of Art, Fifth Avenue, Gallery 111

The museum describes the upper element as a **dummy animal**, made from a linen form enclosed in animal skin, without a head or hind legs, attached by linen strips to a staff. The staff ends in a travertine jar that formerly held ointment, now decayed ([Metropolitan Museum object 14.3.19–.20](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/545547)).

The associated shrine is separately accessioned as **14.3.18**:

- wood with paint;
- height 59.3 cm;
- width 32 cm;
- depth 23 cm;
- found with the imiut and two guardian figures;
- currently displayed in Gallery 111 ([Metropolitan Museum shrine 14.3.18](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/545546)).

The ensemble was uninscribed. Its identification rests on form and context rather than a label naming it *jmy-wt*.

### 1.6 The Lisht guardians

Two wooden male figures stood in relation to the shrine. One is at the Metropolitan Museum; its counterpart went to Cairo under the division of finds.

The surviving Met figure is made from multiple cedar components joined with dowels, mortise-and-tenon connections, adhesive, and plaster. Conservation study identified pigments including red ochre, yellow orpiment, Egyptian blue, calcium-carbonate white, and carbon black. It had an ancient wood repair at the back of the left shoulder. A beard and an object once held in the right hand are missing.

These material findings concern the guardian rather than the imiut itself, but they demonstrate that the ensemble was a carefully constructed, painted ritual installation rather than an isolated skin deposited casually.

### 1.7 Tutankhamun’s imiut emblems

Two imiut emblems were placed in Tutankhamun’s burial chamber:

- **Carter no. 194**, Egyptian Museum **JE 61374**
- **Carter no. 202**, Egyptian Museum **JE 61375**

The Griffith Institute handlist describes both as **Anubis emblems of gold on alabaster bases** and associates them with Harry Burton photographs 590 and 673 ([Griffith Institute Cairo concordance](https://www.griffith.ox.ac.uk/gri/cairo1to8_3.pdf)).

They are elite miniaturized or symbolic versions, not preserved carcasses. Published descriptions identify:

- a gold skin-shaped body;
- a gold-covered or gold pole;
- a calcite—traditionally “alabaster”—jar or base;
- royal inscriptions naming Tutankhamun and invoking Anubis.

Translations published in modern cataloguing literature vary slightly. One base invokes:

> The perfect god Nebkheperure, son of Re, Tutankhamun, ruler of southern Heliopolis, given eternal life, beloved of Anubis who presides over the embalming house.

The other gives a closely related form referring to Anubis “who is in the place of embalming.” Exact readings should be checked against Carter’s object cards and a modern epigraphic edition; the translations circulating in exhibition literature are not fully standardized.

Carter’s photographs show the two emblems standing in the western corners or western side of the burial chamber, in the constrained space outside the nested burial shrines.

### 1.8 Condition and losses

For the object type as a whole:

- Most ancient functional examples are lost.
- Most evidence consists of images rather than the objects represented.
- Organic skins, bindings, oils, and wooden poles were intrinsically vulnerable.
- No continuous ancient temple imiut is known to have remained in cult use into modern times.
- Surviving pieces are funerary deposits preserved through burial.

For the Lisht example:

- the ointment has decayed;
- the organic body survives but cannot be treated as an intact animal carcass;
- museum records do not publish a definitive zoological species determination;
- the object, shrine, and guardians are divided between New York and Cairo.

For Tutankhamun’s examples:

- the gold-and-calcite emblems survive;
- no major ancient destruction is reported;
- their current individual GEM catalogue identifiers were not located in the public sources consulted;
- their older Carter and *Journal d’Entrée* numbers remain the secure identifiers.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 No single manufacture event

The imiut type cannot be assigned to one inventor, workshop, or commission. It appears before surviving continuous Egyptian historical narrative and was repeatedly manufactured.

Its origin must therefore be reconstructed from iconographic sequence rather than from a foundation inscription.

### 2.2 Predynastic candidate

Thomas J. Logan identified the earliest possible antecedent on a decorated lug handle dated to **Naqada IIc–d**. The scene apparently shows a bird being opened and its viscera or internal matter attached to a standard-like object. Logan interpreted this as evidence that the earliest imiut may have involved the exposed internal parts of a sacrificial victim rather than an embalming emblem.

This interpretation is influential but not certain:

- **Documented:** the decorated object and scene exist.
- **Scholarly inference:** the scene represents the production of an imiut.
- **Further inference:** the suspended material is viscera from a sacrificed bird.
- **Unresolved:** whether this is genuinely the same object type as later *jmy-wt* emblems.

Logan’s article remains a central treatment: Thomas J. Logan, “The Origins of the Jmy-wt Fetish,” *Journal of the American Research Center in Egypt* 27 (1990), 61–69 ([JSTOR record](https://www.jstor.org/stable/40000074)).

### 2.3 Naqada animal-skin imagery

Gwenola Graff compared Naqada-period images of animal skins stretched or suspended on poles with the later imiut. She proposed an iconographic and conceptual continuity involving regeneration: a dead person enclosed in an animal hide could be imagined as undergoing a new gestation.

The argument is interpretive. Later Egyptian mortuary symbolism makes rebirth through enclosure plausible, but it is hazardous to project later theology backward automatically. A recent synthesis of early Egyptian mortuary religion emphasizes precisely this problem: representations of hides on Predynastic pottery cannot be assumed to have carried the same regenerative meaning later Egyptian texts assigned to skins and wrappings ([Graff, “La peau animale nagadienne et la nébride IMY-WT”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/250139297_La_peau_animale_nagadienne_et_la_nebride_IMY-WT); [Smith, *Following Osiris*, preview discussion](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780191089756_A30390504/preview-9780191089756_A30390504.pdf)).

### 2.4 First Dynasty attestations

The first secure historical cluster belongs to the First Dynasty. Imiut-like standards occur on royal labels and sealings associated with:

- Hor-Aha;
- Djer;
- Djet;
- Den.

In these scenes the emblem can accompany royal ceremony, the opening or inauguration of structures, Sed-festival activity, and episodes interpreted as ritual killing or execution of bound enemies.

The record does **not** identify an individual Anubis cult object moving from reign to reign. Rather, it demonstrates that the emblematic form already belonged to royal ceremonial culture.

Some readings remain contested. A First Dynasty label corpus notes, for example, that a sign interpreted by one publication as “birth of the imiut” differs substantially from recognizable First Dynasty imiut forms and may instead belong to oil-processing imagery. The identification cannot simply be repeated as settled fact ([First Dynasty Labels Corpus](https://www.francescoraffaele.com/egypt/hesyra/labels/xxdjet16.htm)).

### 2.5 Old Kingdom development

By the Old Kingdom the imiut becomes increasingly connected with the funerary sphere and Anubis. Anubis’s epithet *jmy-wt* appears in mortuary formulas, alongside titles such as:

- *tpj ḏw=f*, “he who is upon his mountain”;
- *ḫnty sḥ-nṯr*, “foremost of the divine booth” or embalming pavilion;
- lord or guardian of the necropolis.

The commonly repeated claim that the Anubis association begins specifically in the Fourth Dynasty is a useful chronological approximation, not an event documented by a dated decree. The association emerges through formulaic and iconographic evidence.

### 2.6 Manufacture of the Lisht specimen

The Lisht imiut was manufactured in the Middle Kingdom, probably during the early Twelfth Dynasty. The Met currently dates it to Amenemhat II, approximately 1919–1885 BCE.

Its construction involved:

- carving a small travertine jar;
- shaping a cedar staff;
- making a linen-filled animal form;
- enclosing the form in animal skin;
- fastening it to the staff with linen;
- placing ointment or resin in the jar;
- enclosing the emblem in a painted wooden shrine.

No maker, workshop, cost, or commission document survives. The high-quality guardians and ritual arrangement have led to the inference that the ensemble may have been a royal gift or royal-workshop product for the high official Imhotep, but this remains a scholarly reconstruction. A Met publication notes that site records are sparse and that even the identity of the guardian figures—king, deity, or ritual guardian—was uncertain ([Metropolitan Museum Bulletin](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/Egyptian_Art_The_Metropolitan_Museum_of_Art_Bulletin_v_41_no_3_Winter_1983_1984.pdf)).

### 2.7 Manufacture of Tutankhamun’s pair

Tutankhamun’s two emblems were produced for a royal burial in the late Eighteenth Dynasty, approximately 1332–1323 BCE under one commonly used chronology.

Their materials and inscriptions distinguish them from a working skin-and-pole implement. They are precious funerary simulacra: gold embodies imperishability and divine flesh, while calcite jars evoke ritual containers for unguents.

No named goldsmith or workshop document survives. Their close pairing, inscriptions, and symmetrical burial-chamber placement indicate a coordinated funerary commission. Whether they were made specifically after Tutankhamun’s death or prepared during his reign cannot be established.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

## 3.1 History of the object type

### Naqada IIc–d, fourth millennium BCE

- Candidate precursor appears on a decorated lug handle.
- Custody before archaeological collection is not reconstructable from the sources consulted.
- Identification as an imiut is scholarly, not epigraphically demonstrated.

### First Dynasty, approximately 3100–2890 BCE

- The emblem appears in royal documentary art under Hor-Aha, Djer, Djet, and Den.
- It is associated with kingship, ceremonies, bounded sacred space, and scenes of violence against captives.
- These are depictions of an object type, not a record of one inherited object.

### Old Kingdom

- The form becomes associated with mortuary protection and Anubis.
- *Jmy-wt* functions as an epithet or manifestation of Anubis.
- Imiut images appear in funerary and offering contexts.

### Middle Kingdom

- Physical models were deposited in elite funerary contexts.
- The Lisht example demonstrates that the emblem could be enclosed in its own shrine and attended by guardian statues.
- The combination of ointment vessel, skin, bindings, and staff shows that the image corresponded to an actual manufactured ritual object.

### New Kingdom

- The imiut occurs in temple relief, royal funerary equipment, tomb painting, and mortuary papyri.
- Hatshepsut’s mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahari shows the emblem in the Anubis chapel.
- Tutankhamun received two precious-metal versions.
- The object became increasingly integrated with Osirian mortuary ritual while retaining its Anubian connection.

### Third Intermediate and Late Periods

- Imiut images continued on coffins, cartonnage, and ritual scenes.
- A 21st/22nd Dynasty coffin panel formerly in a Hamburg private collection shows an imiut between Thoth and Osiris. Its modern provenance begins only with “private collection, Hamburg, acquired before 1977,” followed by descent, Christie’s sale in 2016, and Sotheby’s resale. This is an image of an imiut, not the cult implement itself ([Christie’s lot record](https://www.christies.com/lot/lot-an-egyptian-polychrome-painted-wood-coffin-panel-6009327/); [Sotheby’s lot record](https://www.sothebys.com/buy/c6a2f3b3-e847-430e-983c-f17227fbb16c/lots/179b6110-bf56-4dc1-9d45-254bec45d8bd)).

### Ptolemaic period

- The Papyrus Jumilhac supplied extended theological explanations involving animal skin, bodily restoration, Anubis, Horus/Anti, Hesat, Osiris, and Seth.
- These texts are late explanations of a very old emblem, not eyewitness accounts of its origin.

### Roman period and after

- Anubian and Osirian imagery continued, but no continuous post-pharaonic custody of an operating imiut shrine is documented.
- The ritual object passed into archaeological, museum, artistic, and modern religious reception rather than an unbroken institutional cult.

## 3.2 The Lisht ensemble: chain of custody

### Ancient manufacture and deposition

- **Date:** early Twelfth Dynasty; the Met presently assigns approximately 1919–1885 BCE.
- **Place:** Lisht South, in the enclosure of the tomb of an elite official named Imhotep.
- **Mechanism:** deliberate ritual deposition in a chamber within the southern enclosure wall.
- **Context:** imiut in a shrine, associated with two guardian figures.
- **Purpose:** probably funerary protection or rites connected with Imhotep’s burial; exact ceremony is not recorded.

### Burial interval

From the Middle Kingdom until 1914 the ensemble remained buried. There is no evidence of later ancient or medieval handling in the available excavation history.

### Excavation, 1913–14

The Metropolitan Museum’s Egyptian Expedition found the ensemble during excavation of the tomb enclosure. Albert M. Lythgoe directed the Museum’s Lisht work during this period. Excavation, documentation, and removal converted the objects from a sealed ritual deposit into archaeological finds.

### Division of finds

Under the antiquities regime then operating in Egypt, the assemblage was divided:

- the imiut, its shrine, and one guardian entered the Metropolitan Museum;
- the second guardian entered the Egyptian Museum in Cairo.

The division physically dismembered the ancient ensemble. It was not a private sale or clandestine export; it was an officially administered archaeological division under the period’s Egyptian antiquities system. Nevertheless, it means the reconstructed shrine group no longer survives in one collection.

### Metropolitan Museum accession, 1914

- Imiut: **14.3.19–.20**
- Shrine: **14.3.18**
- Met guardian: **14.3.17**
- Credit line: Rogers Fund and Edward S. Harkness Gift, 1914

### Modern museum custody

The imiut and shrine have remained in the Metropolitan Museum’s collection. They are currently listed as on public display in Gallery 111.

No theft, deaccession, restitution proceeding, or rival ownership claim involving 14.3.19–.20 was located in the consulted sources.

## 3.3 Tutankhamun’s pair: chain of custody

### Manufacture and burial

- Produced in the late Eighteenth Dynasty.
- Placed in Tutankhamun’s burial chamber, one at either western corner or along the western side outside the outermost burial shrine.
- Their paired position created a protected ritual zone around the royal burial.

### Ancient tomb intrusions

KV62 was entered by robbers in antiquity and then resealed. The available documentation does not indicate that either imiut was taken, dismantled, or displaced beyond recognition. Both remained in the burial chamber until the modern excavation.

### Discovery and recording

Howard Carter’s expedition discovered Tutankhamun’s tomb in November 1922. The burial chamber was opened in February 1923. The imiuts received Carter numbers **194** and **202**.

Harry Burton photographed them:

- object 194: photographs 590 and 673;
- object 202: photograph 673.

The Griffith Institute preserves Carter’s excavation archive, including object records, photographs, journals, drawings, conservation notes, and concordances. The archive is documentary custody, distinct from physical custody of the artifacts ([Tutankhamun Spatial Archive overview](https://tutankhamun.griffith.ox.ac.uk/about)).

### Removal and Egyptian state custody

The objects were removed from KV62 during Carter’s clearance and entered the Egyptian Museum in Cairo:

- Carter 194 → **JE 61374**
- Carter 202 → **JE 61375**

Unlike the Lisht finds, the Tutankhamun tomb contents were not divided with Carter or the Metropolitan Museum. The pair remained Egyptian state property.

### Egyptian Museum display and storage

For most of the twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, the two emblems were in the Egyptian Museum system in Cairo. Public catalogues do not provide a complete continuous record of every gallery move, period of storage, or conservation intervention.

### Transfer to the Grand Egyptian Museum

The Egyptian government transferred Tutankhamun’s collection from the Egyptian Museum and storage facilities to the Grand Egyptian Museum at Giza as part of the plan to reunite the tomb group.

The Grand Egyptian Museum opened fully to the public in November 2025. Its two Tutankhamun halls present more than 5,000 tomb objects together for the first time. Official visitor information confirms that admission includes the Tutankhamun galleries ([GEM Tutankhamun Galleries](https://gem.eg/en/collection/tutankhamun-galleries/); [official GEM ticketing site](https://tickets.gem.eg/); [Associated Press report on the opening](https://apnews.com/article/f5bf88997dff98cc3c3e95743ababdbd)).

The public sources consulted do not individually identify JE 61374 and JE 61375 on the GEM website. Their inclusion in the transferred and reunited Tutankhamun collection is strongly supported institutionally, but the exact case, gallery coordinates, and new GEM accession identifiers remain **unverified in the published sources consulted**.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Is there a single “real” imiut?

No. The premise of a unique original is unsupported. The word designates a reproducible emblem or ritual implement. Authenticity must be assessed separately for every physical specimen and representation.

### 4.2 Continuity of form

The principal scholarly dispute is typological:

- Are Naqada suspended skins, First Dynasty standards, Middle Kingdom skin-and-jar objects, and New Kingdom gold models manifestations of one continuous emblem?
- Or have modern scholars grouped superficially similar objects that carried different meanings?

There is strong formal continuity: suspended animal-derived matter, a pole, and a base or bounded setting recur. But continuity of appearance does not prove unchanged meaning.

### 4.3 The earliest candidate

The Naqada IIc–d lug-handle scene is not labeled *jmy-wt*. Its identity depends on iconographic comparison. It is therefore a **candidate precursor**, not the first certain named imiut.

### 4.4 Species disputes

No consensus establishes one animal species for every imiut.

Arguments include:

- **Canid:** encouraged by the later Anubis association and some elongated forms.
- **Feline:** supported by spotted or catlike representations and myths of Anubis flaying Seth in panther form.
- **Bovine:** supported by particular representations and possible links with Apis, Hesat, or regenerative bovine symbolism.
- **Variable species:** perhaps the most cautious conclusion; Egyptian artists and ritualists may have used different hides over time.

Without published biomolecular analysis of the Lisht skin, claims that it is definitively jackal, leopard, or bull should be marked unverified.

### 4.5 The Lisht object

The Lisht object’s authenticity as an ancient Middle Kingdom artifact is secure because it was excavated in a recorded sealed archaeological context.

Questions concern interpretation rather than genuineness:

- whether the tomb owner was certainly the Imhotep named by surrounding evidence;
- whether the ensemble dates to Senwosret I or Amenemhat II;
- whether the guardian figures are royal, divine, or ritual personifications;
- whether the object was used in a ceremony before deposition or manufactured as funerary equipment.

The Met’s dating has evolved with study. An older museum bulletin emphasized affinities with Senwosret I’s reign and called the analysis incomplete; the present database assigns the ensemble to Amenemhat II. This is a genuine attribution history, not evidence of forgery.

### 4.6 Tutankhamun’s objects

Their authenticity is secure through excavation in situ, Carter numbering, Burton photography, and Egyptian Museum registration.

No rival “true” Tutankhamun imiuts or substitution theory supported by serious evidence was located. Modern replicas and souvenirs exist, but they do not possess competing archaeological provenances.

### 4.7 Scientific examination

The published museum record for the Lisht object identifies cedar, travertine, ointment, linen, and skin, but it does not supply:

- radiocarbon dates;
- species identification by microscopy, proteomics, or ancient DNA;
- chemical characterization of the ointment;
- provenance analysis of the cedar or stone.

Consequently, the object’s date continues to rest primarily on archaeological context and art-historical assessment.

No published scientific examination specific to Tutankhamun’s two imiuts was located in the consulted material. The absence of a published analysis should not be converted into a claim that none was ever undertaken; internal conservation documentation may exist.

### 4.8 Authority to authenticate

Authenticating authority has shifted:

- ancient priests and royal institutions determined correct ritual manufacture and use;
- excavators identified finds through comparison with Egyptian imagery and texts;
- the Egyptian Antiquities Service controlled excavation and division;
- museums now exercise cataloguing and conservation authority;
- philologists interpret the name and inscriptions;
- laboratories could determine skin species, pigments, binders, and residues, but published results remain limited.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Royal protection

Early images place imiut-like standards in royal settings. Logan interpreted the emblem as protective of the king during public appearances and ceremonies. Its location beside royal platforms or sacred precincts may mark:

- protection;
- ritually controlled space;
- the presence of sacrificial power;
- royal authority over enemies and disorder.

This function is inferred from placement, not explained by an accompanying First Dynasty text.

### 6.2 Sacrifice and sanctioned violence

First Dynasty scenes associate the emblem with captive killing or ritual execution. Logan’s reconstruction proposes that the earliest object displayed the internal organs of a sacrificial victim, making the emblem a visible product of ritual killing.

The chain of reasoning is plausible but not certain:

1. an animal or bird is killed;
2. its interior matter is removed;
3. that matter is attached to a pole;
4. the charged pole becomes a royal protective standard;
5. the later term *jmy-wt* may have been reinterpreted in embalming language.

The popular statement that “bird entrails were tied to the imiut” should therefore be attributed specifically to Logan’s reading rather than presented as a directly translated ancient instruction.

### 6.3 Foundation and inauguration rites

Early royal images place the emblem in scenes understood as inaugurating buildings or sacred establishments. It may have protected newly delimited space or embodied the violent neutralization of hostile forces.

### 6.4 Sed festival

The imiut occurs in royal jubilee imagery and may have been carried or erected during the Sed festival, when kingship was ritually renewed. Its presence suits both royal protection and regeneration. No surviving procedural manual provides a complete imiut rite for the First Dynasty.

### 6.5 Embalming and funerary protection

In later periods the emblem belongs decisively to funerary ritual. Its associations include:

- Anubis and the embalming pavilion;
- wrapping and transformation of the corpse;
- ointment and anointing;
- protection of the mummy;
- regeneration of the dead;
- Osiris’s restoration;
- the ritual night before burial.

The Met states that the Lisht imiut and its guardians played a role in rites performed over the mummy on the night before the funeral. This is a modern institutional reconstruction from archaeological and textual comparison, not a surviving label from Imhotep’s tomb.

### 6.6 Ointment and bodily restoration

The pole of the Lisht specimen stood in a jar containing ointment. This is materially important. The base was not merely an inert stand: it could preserve or symbolize substances used in anointing.

Later myths connect animal skin, milk, and ointment with the reconstitution of a dismembered or flayed deity. The physical arrangement therefore brought together:

- skin;
- wrapping;
- aromatic or preservative substance;
- upright support;
- divine embodiment.

### 6.7 Boundary protection

Imiuts were placed around sacred or funerary spaces. Tutankhamun’s pair stood at the west side of the burial chamber; their duplication may have created bilateral protection for the king and shrines.

### 6.8 Osirian regeneration

In later funerary imagery, the imiut often stands near Osiris. It can signify the successful conversion of the dead into an Osirian, preserved body. This does not mean that the emblem “belonged to Osiris” from its beginning; it was incorporated into an expanding Osirian funerary system.

### 6.9 Political function

Possession of an imiut is not known to have conferred sovereignty in the manner of a crown or throne. Its political function was indirect:

- it accompanied the king;
- marked his ritual authority;
- displayed the defeat of captives or chaotic forces;
- renewed and protected kingship;
- contributed to the royal burial’s claim of eternal legitimacy.

There is no documented war or succession dispute fought specifically for possession of an imiut.

### 6.10 Access and taboos

The evidence suggests controlled access:

- working emblems belonged to temple, royal, or mortuary ritual;
- the Lisht example was enclosed in a shrine and guarded;
- Tutankhamun’s pair was sealed within a royal burial chamber.

No surviving text consulted specifies a universal prohibition such as “only the king may touch the imiut.” Claims about exact access rules should therefore be marked unresolved.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The restoration of Anti/Horus

**Source:** Papyrus Jumilhac, Ptolemaic period, Louvre E 17110.

In the story conventionally called “the mystery of the nebride,” Anti—identified or assimilated with Horus in the text’s local theology—commits an offense involving the decapitation of Hathor/Isis. He is punished by having his skin and flesh separated from his bones.

His mother, the cow goddess Hesat:

1. takes or receives the skin;
2. places or gathers his bodily remains within it;
3. produces milk;
4. mixes or applies an ointment;
5. restores him to life.

The resulting skin-object is set before Osiris and becomes an etiological explanation for the imiut or related nebride.

**Tier:** ancient, late theological explanation.

It is not a historical account of the emblem’s Predynastic manufacture. It demonstrates how Ptolemaic priests understood or re-explained a form already ancient in their time.

A scholarly analysis of mortuary constellations summarizes the episode: Hesat places the remains within the skin, pours in milk, applies ointment, and brings her son back to life, after which the imiut stands before Osiris ([Némeš, ENiM 13](https://www.enim-egyptologie.fr/revue/2020/1/Nemes_ENiM13_p1-61.swf.pdf)).

### 7.2 Anubis flays Seth

The Papyrus Jumilhac also preserves narratives in which Seth attacks or attempts to seize the bodily fluids of Osiris. Seth transforms into a panther. Anubis captures him, brands or marks him, flays him, and wears his skin.

This story explains several related phenomena:

- why the panther has spots;
- why the sem-priest wears a leopard or panther skin;
- why Anubis is master of dangerous animal skin;
- why a flayed hide signifies the defeat of hostile power;
- why the skin can become protective rather than polluting.

**Tier:** ancient late-period theological myth.

It is associated with the imiut’s symbolic field, but the sem-priest’s worn panther skin and the pole-mounted imiut are not physically identical objects.

### 7.3 Protection of Horus

Later tradition connects the imiut with the protection and regeneration of the infant Horus. This rests upon the broader Jumilhac complex of skin, maternal milk, concealment, and revival. Popular accounts sometimes compress separate episodes into the claim that the imiut itself sheltered Horus in Chemmis. That formulation should be treated as a modern synthesis unless tied to a precise textual passage.

### 7.4 The skin as womb

Modern scholars have interpreted the hide as a womb or matrix:

- the dead body is enclosed;
- fetal posture evokes gestation;
- burial becomes pregnancy;
- emergence from the hide becomes rebirth.

This is not an explicitly preserved Predynastic myth. It is a modern interpretation informed by later Egyptian ideas of bodily renewal.

### 7.5 Apis-skin theory

Some popular and secondary accounts say the original imiut was the hide of the Apis bull and therefore embodied regeneration. The evidence is insufficient to establish this as the single original meaning. Bovine associations are real, but the Apis claim competes with feline, canid, sacrificial-viscera, and generalized-skin theories.

### 7.6 The tekenu theory

Alexandre Moret and later writers compared the imiut with the **tekenu**, a bundled form drawn in funeral processions and sometimes represented as a crouching or wrapped person on a sledge. One theory holds that the tekenu preserved the memory of a person or sacrificial remnant enclosed in an animal skin and ritually revived.

Similarities exist—skin, enclosure, corpse-like form, transformation—but no source proves that the imiut “became” the tekenu or vice versa.

### 7.7 Modern occult accretions

Modern esoteric presentations sometimes call the imiut:

- a device for storing magical energy;
- a necromantic instrument;
- a portal to Anubis;
- a talisman with specific supernatural powers;
- evidence of a secret priestly technology.

These claims do not derive from a demonstrable Egyptian ritual manual. Ancient Egyptians certainly regarded the object as ritually potent, but modern technical language about “energy” or portals is a modern invention.

No ancient “curse of the imiut” comparable to twentieth-century Tutankhamun curse folklore is attested.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 The object type

The ancient imiut cult object is extinct as a continuously documented temple implement. Its surviving material record consists of:

- excavated ritual models;
- precious funerary emblems;
- reliefs;
- paintings;
- papyri;
- coffin decoration;
- modern reconstructions and replicas.

### 8.2 Lisht imiut

**Current location:** Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York.

**Accession:** 14.3.19–.20.

**Display:** Gallery 111, according to the museum’s current collection record.

**Condition:** composite object survives with travertine jar, cedar pole, linen-filled animal-skin form, bindings, and traces or remains of the formerly present ointment. The associated shrine survives as 14.3.18.

**Control:** Metropolitan Museum of Art.

**Access:** publicly viewable when the gallery is open; online images are designated public domain by the museum.

**Legal dispute:** none located.

### 8.3 Divided Lisht ensemble

- Imiut: New York.
- Shrine: New York.
- One guardian: New York.
- Second guardian: Cairo.

The ensemble is therefore materially dispersed. Digital or exhibition reconstruction can restore its visual arrangement, but the ancient group has not been physically reunited in the evidence consulted.

### 8.4 Tutankhamun imiuts

**Secure historical identifiers**

- Carter 194 / JE 61374
- Carter 202 / JE 61375

**Current institutional context:** Egyptian state Tutankhamun collection, Grand Egyptian Museum, Giza.

**Display status:** the GEM states that the Tutankhamun galleries are open and that the collection has been reunited. The exact display cases and new inventory numbers for these two objects were not established from the public catalogue.

**Ownership and legal regime:** property of the Egyptian state; no foreign ownership chain exists.

**Access:** through admission to the GEM Tutankhamun galleries. Handling is restricted to authorized museum staff and conservators.

### 8.5 Papyrus Jumilhac

The physical manuscript that preserves the fullest later myths is:

- Louvre **E 17110**;
- Ptolemaic, broadly dated by the museum between 332 and 30 BCE;
- held by the Département des Antiquités égyptiennes;
- decorated with Anubis, Osiris, Horus figures, Seth, Apis, the nebride, and other local religious subjects ([Louvre collection record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010003518)).

Its exact early modern provenance is incomplete. Jacques Vandier reported that the Louvre acquired it in 1945; its ancient findspot and prior chain are not securely documented. It is a textual witness to imiut mythology, not itself an imiut.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Archaeological discovery and museum display

The Lisht discovery transformed the imiut from a primarily iconographic sign into a demonstrably manufactured composite object. The survival of skin, linen, pole, jar, and ointment confirmed that Egyptian images were not simply abstract hieroglyphs.

Tutankhamun’s pair then supplied unusually luxurious royal examples. Burton’s photographs fixed the modern public image of the imiut as a golden, elegant, pole-mounted emblem standing in a royal tomb.

### 9.2 Replicas

Replicas occur in:

- museum education;
- travelling Tutankhamun exhibitions;
- commercial Egyptian-revival décor;
- resin and metal souvenirs;
- modern Kemetic altars;
- digital 3-D reconstructions;
- historical illustration.

A replica is not an alternative claimant to the ancient object unless falsely marketed as ancient. No major documented imiut forgery scandal was located.

### 9.3 Modern Kemetic religion

Some practitioners of reconstructed or revival Egyptian religion employ imiut images as symbols of Anubis, embalming, protection, transformation, or the honored dead. These are modern religious uses informed by Egyptology. They should not be represented as an unbroken survival of ancient priestly practice.

### 9.4 Popular culture

The imiut remains less prominent than the ankh, Eye of Horus, scarab, or jackal-headed Anubis. It appears primarily where creators reproduce tomb and temple settings with unusual fidelity.

Popular representations often introduce errors:

- showing a complete living jackal on the pole;
- calling it Anubis’s body;
- treating it as a unique magical relic;
- identifying the skin unequivocally as leopard or jackal;
- assigning it an invented curse;
- confusing it with the Abydos fetish of Osiris;
- confusing it with the sem-priest’s leopard-skin garment.

### 9.5 Digital dissemination

The Met’s public-domain photographs and the Griffith Institute’s digitized Carter archive have greatly expanded access. Wikimedia and museum-derived databases reproduce these images widely, sometimes without the archaeological qualifications attached to the original records.

The Griffith Institute’s new Tutankhamun Spatial Archive is especially important because it reconnects objects with:

- original room position;
- Carter numbers;
- Burton photographs;
- notebooks;
- conservation records;
- Egyptian Museum numbers.

### 9.6 Tourism

The Lisht object contributes to the Met’s Middle Kingdom presentation, while Tutankhamun’s pair forms part of Egypt’s flagship museum economy. Their political and economic significance arises from participation in the Tutankhamun collection rather than from independent fame.

The 2025 opening of the complete Tutankhamun galleries gave the pair a new museological context: the tomb group can be interpreted together rather than dispersed between limited displays and storage.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Early cataloguing

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century Egyptologists recognized the pole-and-skin form in monuments and linked it with Anubis. Older authors regularly used “fetish,” “jackal skin,” or French *nébride*. Early discussions were often shaped by evolutionary theories of religion, which treated animal-derived objects as survivals of “primitive fetishism.”

That framework is historically important but analytically limiting. The object belonged to sophisticated state, temple, and funerary systems.

### 10.2 Ursula Rößler-Köhler

The most extensive dedicated study is:

Ursula Köhler, later cited as Ursula Rößler-Köhler, *Das Imiut: Untersuchungen zur Darstellung und Bedeutung eines mit Anubis verbundenen religiösen Symbols*, Göttinger Orientforschungen IV.4, Wiesbaden, 1975.

It comprises two volumes and more than 500 pages, with catalogues, tables, figures, plates, and analysis. Its subtitle correctly signals the central issue: the imiut is a religious symbol “connected with Anubis,” not automatically reducible to Anubis himself ([bibliographic record](https://ci.nii.ac.jp/ncid/BB29950873)).

The work remains foundational but has been criticized on particular interpretations and must be read alongside subsequent studies of early iconography and animal remains.

### 10.3 Thomas J. Logan

Logan’s 1990 article shifted attention toward the Predynastic and Early Dynastic record. His principal contributions were:

- identification of a Naqada IIc–d possible precursor;
- emphasis on sacrificial internal organs;
- analysis of First Dynasty royal labels;
- interpretation of the imiut as a protective royal emblem;
- separation of the early emblem from its later, fully developed Anubian embalming theology.

The article’s strength is its historical sequencing. Its weakest point is that the earliest scene lacks a written label; the continuity is iconographic and inferential.

### 10.4 Gwenola Graff

Graff examined Naqada animal-skin motifs and proposed continuity with the later nebride and imiut. Her regenerative-womb interpretation integrates Predynastic imagery with later mortuary theology, but critics warn that it may retroject later beliefs into poorly documented early contexts.

### 10.5 Jacques Vandier and the Papyrus Jumilhac

Vandier’s publication of the Papyrus Jumilhac opened a local priestly archive of explanations for:

- Anubis’s battles with Seth;
- the use of panther skins;
- bodily relics of Osiris;
- the restoration of a flayed god;
- the nebride/imiut;
- local place names and cult institutions.

The papyrus itself is late, composite, and locally focused. It preserves ancient Egyptian exegesis, not necessarily the original meaning of every custom it explains.

### 10.6 Principal debates

#### Meaning of *jmy-wt*

Competing readings include:

- “he who is in the wrappings”;
- “he who is in the embalming place”;
- “that which is inside”;
- an older untranslatable cult term reinterpreted through embalming theology.

#### Original contents

Possibilities include:

- stuffed skin;
- entire flayed hide;
- viscera;
- linen dummy;
- symbolic divine body;
- ointment-associated ritual bundle.

#### Species

Canid, feline, bovine, or variable.

#### Earliest date

- Naqada IIc–d if the lug-handle identification is accepted;
- First Dynasty if only recognizable imiut standards are accepted;
- later Old Kingdom if one requires the written name and established Anubis association.

#### Original function

- royal protective standard;
- sacrificial trophy;
- boundary marker;
- foundation emblem;
- regenerative skin;
- embalming symbol;
- container or embodiment of a deity.

These need not be mutually exclusive across time.

#### Relation to Anubis

The strongest historical model is developmental:

1. pre-Anubian or not demonstrably Anubian emblem;
2. early royal-protective and sacrificial use;
3. growing association with funerary practice;
4. identification with Anubis through *jmy-wt*;
5. integration into Osirian resurrection theology;
6. late priestly myths explaining skin, ointment, and regeneration.

### 10.7 Evidence still needed

A modern research program should include:

- non-destructive species identification of the Lisht skin by ZooMS proteomics;
- ancient DNA testing if sampling is ethically and technically justified;
- fiber analysis of the stuffing and bindings;
- GC-MS analysis of ointment residues;
- radiocarbon dating of skin and linen;
- stable-isotope or dendrological study of the cedar;
- complete publication of the original Lisht excavation notes;
- updated epigraphic publication of Tutankhamun 194 and 202;
- publication of their conservation histories and GEM identifiers;
- a digital corpus of every dated imiut representation.

No such integrated modern scientific project was identified in the sources consulted.
