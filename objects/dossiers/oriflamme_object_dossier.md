# The Oriflamme: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary finding

The Oriflamme of Saint-Denis was not demonstrably one continuously surviving medieval textile. The evidence is better explained by a succession of sacred red banners made, damaged, lost, replaced, reconsecrated, and retrospectively treated as one enduring insignia. “The Oriflamme” therefore denotes three overlapping things:

1. the historically attested *vexillum beati Dionysii*, the banner of the abbey and martyrs of Saint-Denis, first securely documented in 1124;
2. the royal French war insignia into which that banner developed between the twelfth and fifteenth centuries;
3. a legendary banner of Charlemagne—sometimes traced farther back to Saint Peter, Constantine, Clovis, or Dagobert—whose name and history were progressively attached to the Saint-Denis banner.

No accepted medieval Oriflamme survives. No museum has an accessioned textile demonstrably belonging to the medieval chain. Accordingly, there has been no radiocarbon dating, fibre analysis, dye analysis, or other scientific examination of an authenticated original. Modern banners displayed or reproduced under the name are reconstructions, commemorative replacements, or artistic representations.

The central identity problem is thus not simply “where is the original?” but “how many physical Oriflammes existed, and at what dates did contemporaries begin treating replacements as the same enduring sacred object?” The sources do not permit a final count.

Evidence labels used below:

- **Documented:** supported by a contemporary act, inventory, account, or near-contemporary narrative.
- **Probable inference:** a reconstruction accepted or advanced by modern scholarship but not expressly recorded.
- **Institutional tradition:** a claim maintained by Saint-Denis or the French monarchy.
- **Literary tradition:** evidence for the history of the story or name, not necessarily for the physical object.
- **Later legend:** first attested substantially after the alleged event.
- **Disputed/unresolved:** evidence admits materially different interpretations.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity and class

The Oriflamme was a sacred ecclesiastical banner converted into, or adopted as, a royal war standard. Medieval sources variously call it:

- Latin *vexillum beati Dionysii* — “banner of Saint Denis”;
- *vexillum sancti Dionysii*;
- *signum* or *signum regale* — “standard” or “royal standard”;
- Old and Middle French *enseigne Saint-Denis*, *gonfanon Saint-Denis*, *gonfanon le roi*, *oriflambe*, *auriflamme*, *oriflamme*, and occasionally *oriflande*;
- Latin *aurea flamma* — literally “golden flame”;
- French *Oriflamme de Saint-Denis*, *Oriflamme du roi*, and *Oriflamme de France*.

The earliest evidence calls the object a *vexillum*, not an Oriflamme. The name was imported from epic literature and attached to the Saint-Denis banner only later.

### Etymology

The conventional derivation is Latin *aurea flamma*, “golden flame,” mediated through Old French *orie flambe*. The phrase occurs in the *Chanson de Roland* as the name or description of Charlemagne’s banner. It does not there securely identify the historical banner at Saint-Denis.

The name is paradoxical because the historically described Saint-Denis banner was red, not gold. Proposed explanations include:

- the “gold” referred to a gilded lance rather than the cloth;
- the red or orange-red silk resembled fire;
- the cloth or later representations bore gold decoration;
- the name was transferred whole from Charlemagne’s literary banner without regard to the Saint-Denis object’s appearance.

Philippe Contamine concluded that medieval descriptions and images were too variable to support a single literal explanation. The combination of a luminous red textile, a gilded staff, flame-like tails, and the prestige of the Carolingian name probably sustained several explanations simultaneously. [Contamine’s full study](https://www.mgh-bibliothek.de/dokumente/a/a131248.pdf)

### Materials

The most dependable descriptions identify the field as silk:

- *cendal*, *sendal*, or *sandal*: a fine silk textile whose precise weave could vary;
- red, vermilion, scarlet, or fiery red;
- in one late description, unusually thick;
- attached to a lance or staff, commonly described as gilded;
- sometimes described with green silk tufts or fringe.

The 1505 Saint-Denis inventory called it “*un estandard de sandal fort caducque*”—a standard of cendal, very decayed. The 1534 inventory described “*un estandard d’un sendal fort espais, tendu par le milieu en façon d’un gonfanon, fort caducque*”—a standard of very thick sendal, extended through the middle in the manner of a gonfanon, very decayed. These are the closest surviving archival descriptions of a physical object in the abbey. [Omont’s inventory publication record](https://agorha.inha.fr/ark:/54721/6691b794-1e2c-415e-90a3-f9bdf88eaff2)

### Form

The reconstruction usually shown today—a horizontally projecting red banner terminating in two or three long points—combines inconsistent evidence.

Guillaume le Breton, writing in the early thirteenth century, described a plain, shining red silk banner like those carried in ecclesiastical processions. In the *Philippide* he wrote that it was made from a simple cloth of brilliant red silk and resembled processional church banners. [Latin–French edition of the *Philippide*](https://remacle.org/bloodwolf/historiens/guillaumelebreton/philippide11.htm)

Later sources offer:

- a square or rectangular cloth;
- two tails in some early-modern descriptions;
- three lower points or tails in influential early-modern reconstructions;
- multiple flame-like lower divisions;
- attachment “through the middle” like a gonfanon;
- green tufts or fringes;
- no figure or heraldic charge in Guillaume Guiart’s description;
- gold flames or stars only in certain later accounts and representations;
- the inscriptions “MONTJOIE” and “SAINT-DENIS” only on modern reconstructions, not securely on the documented medieval cloth.

The banner should not be confused with the king’s blue, fleur-de-lis-covered banner. At Bouvines and elsewhere the royal fleurdelisé and the banner of Saint-Denis could be distinct objects with different tactical and symbolic functions.

### Dimensions, weight, and construction

No reliable medieval dimensions or weight survive. Assertions that it was small are inferences from accounts in which the folded cloth was placed around the king’s or bearer’s neck before being mounted on its lance. There is no surviving workshop contract, tailoring account, technical drawing, or securely scaled contemporary representation for the first banner.

A transverse bar has often been reconstructed because later writers compared it with parish processional banners and the Roman *labarum*. The 1534 phrase *tendu par le milieu en façon d’un gonfanon* permits such an arrangement but does not settle exactly how the cloth was suspended.

### Decoration and inscriptions

No inscription is securely documented on the surviving medieval object. Guillaume Guiart explicitly described a red silk banner “without any figure.” Gold flames, suns, stars, crosses, *S. DENIS*, and *MONTJOIE SAINT DENIS* belong to particular later images, texts, heraldic interpretations, or reconstructions and cannot be projected backward indiscriminately.

### Damage and alteration

The physical history implies repeated severe damage:

- exposure to wind, rain, mud, and close combat;
- probable loss or destruction during unsuccessful campaigns;
- possible loss at Mons-en-Pévèle in 1304;
- loss or destruction at Poitiers in 1356;
- replacement and new blessing after a loss;
- extreme decay by 1505 and 1534;
- disappearance of the cloth by the early eighteenth century;
- temporary survival, at most, of a lance or staff associated with it.

There is no securely identified medieval fragment.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Documentary origin of the historical object

The historical record begins shortly after 3 August 1124. Emperor Henry V threatened the kingdom, and Louis VI went to Saint-Denis. A surviving royal diploma records that the king raised a banner from the altar of the martyrs Denis, Rusticus, and Eleutherius. The act survives as an original parchment formerly bearing a pendant seal, now Archives nationales, K 22A, no. 4; it was edited as no. 220 in Jean Dufour and Robert-Henri Bautier’s *Recueil des actes de Louis VI*. [Diploma catalogue and synopsis](https://caro.huma-num.fr/corpus/France/Louis_6/220a)

The act connects the banner with the county of Vexin, which Louis held in fief from Saint-Denis, and says that he assumed the right or duty of standard-bearer as the counts of Vexin had formerly done. Its important Latin clause describes the king as taking the banner from the martyrs’ altar and preserving the ancient custom of his predecessors as *signifer*, standard-bearer.

Suger’s *Vita Ludovici Grossi* supplies the narrative setting: Louis went to Saint-Denis, invoked the martyrs’ protection, and took their banner before assembling his army. The German force withdrew without a battle. Suger does not call the banner *Oriflamme*. The contemporary evidence establishes a Saint-Denis banner, a royal taking ceremony, and a claimed Vexin connection; it does not establish that Suger personally designed or commissioned a new textile in 1124.

### Was it manufactured in 1124?

**Unresolved.** Modern summaries frequently say that Suger “created” the Oriflamme in 1124. No known contemporary manufacture account says this. At least three possibilities remain:

1. an existing abbey processional or military banner was taken from the altar;
2. a banner associated with the Vexin advocacy was already kept at Saint-Denis;
3. Suger arranged or formalized the use of a newly made banner whose production was not recorded.

The royal diploma asserts an older custom of the counts of Vexin, but this may be juridical legitimation rather than a demonstrable chain for the cloth. Documentation of the physical object does not extend backward beyond 1124.

### Earliest attestation of the name

The *Chanson de Roland*, in its surviving late-eleventh- or early-twelfth-century form, names Charlemagne’s banner *Romaine* and says it was later called *Montjoie*; nearby it uses *orie flambe*. This is literary evidence for a Carolingian banner-name, not proof that Charlemagne owned the later Saint-Denis textile.

Around 1170, *Fierabras* appears to apply the Oriflamme name to Saint-Denis. Around 1200, Gervase of Canterbury, describing events of 1184, called the banner taken by Philip Augustus “the standard of King Charles,” an emblem of “death or victory.” In 1221–1224 Guillaume le Breton’s *Philippide* explicitly called the red Saint-Denis banner by the popular name *aurea flamma*. The fusion of historical Saint-Denis banner and literary Carolingian banner was therefore substantially complete by the early thirteenth century.

### Legendary origin lineages

#### Charlemagne and Pope Leo III

**Later literary and institutional tradition.** The banner was said to have been given to Charlemagne by Pope Leo III. No contemporary Carolingian text or inventory connects such a banner with Saint-Denis. The story enhanced both Capetian continuity with Charlemagne and Saint-Denis’s claim to be the kingdom’s pre-eminent royal abbey.

#### Saint Peter and “Romaine”

In the *Roland* tradition the banner’s earlier name, *Romaine*, was explained through Rome or Saint Peter. Later writers converted this literary genealogy into material provenance: Peter, Rome, the pope, Charlemagne, and finally Saint-Denis. This is a history of the legend, not an authenticated chain of custody.

#### Constantine and the labarum

Late medieval interpretations associated the Oriflamme with Constantine’s heavenly sign and labarum. Some texts altered the visionary lance from white to gold or made a flame stream from it. This strengthened the analogy between a Christian emperor’s divinely sanctioned victory and the French king’s war under Saint Denis.

#### Clovis and Dagobert

Around 1300 Saint-Denis traditions pushed the banner’s custody back to Dagobert, legendary founder and benefactor of the abbey. Jean Golein and other late medieval authors incorporated it into narratives of Clovis’s heavenly insignia. In 1625 Jacques Doublet claimed that Clovis II, son of Dagobert, had the red banner made. These claims are centuries later than the events alleged.

#### Blood of Saint Denis

The frequently repeated story that the cloth was red because it had been dipped in the martyr’s blood is a late devotional explanation without support in the earliest physical descriptions or in early accounts of Denis’s martyrdom.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Before 1124

No physical chain is demonstrable. The 1124 diploma says that the counts of Vexin had customarily been Saint-Denis’s standard-bearers. This supports an institutional office or claimed right but does not prove that the same textile existed under earlier counts. The proposition that the crown inherited custody when the French Vexin entered the royal domain is a legal-historical reconstruction, not a textile provenance.

### 1124: Louis VI

**Documented.** Louis VI took the banner from the martyrs’ altar during Henry V’s invasion. The abbey remained owner or sacred custodian; the king took it in a feudal and devotional capacity. The army assembled, but no battle occurred. The record does not say whether the king personally returned the cloth or by what procedure.

### 1147: Louis VII and the Second Crusade

**Documented by near-contemporary narrative.** Before departing on crusade, Louis VII went to Saint-Denis. Odo of Deuil records the shrine ceremony in which relics were exposed and the king received sacred insignia. The event strengthened the pattern: the object left the abbey temporarily for an extraordinary royal expedition and remained notionally the martyrs’ property.

### 1190: Philip II Augustus and the Third Crusade

**Documented.** Philip took silken standards from the altar or over the martyrs’ bodies before departure. Later sources and art identify one as the Oriflamme. The precise number and distinction among the textiles are not always clear. The Louvre and Versailles preserve nineteenth-century representations of the ceremony, not the banner itself. [Louvre drawing catalogue](https://arts-graphiques.louvre.fr/detail/oeuvres/1/527483-Philippe-Auguste-prend-loriflamme-a-Saint-Denis-24-juin-1190)

### 1214: Bouvines

Guillaume le Breton associates the Oriflamme with the royal army, but distinguishes it from the king’s fleurdelisé. The historical banner’s precise deployment at Bouvines is not described consistently. Later iconography commonly places it at the head of the communal contingents.

### Louis IX: crusading uses

Louis IX took the banner for the Seventh Crusade in 1248. Joinville and Jean Sarrazin call it the banner or gonfanon of Saint Denis rather than consistently using *Oriflamme*. A banner may have been lost during the Egyptian campaign. If so, a replacement must have been made before later documented uses; no manufacture record has been identified.

A further taking was associated with the Tunis crusade of 1270. These crusading episodes helped turn a local abbey banner into a Christian royal palladium.

### 1297–1304: Anseau de Chevreuse and the Flemish wars

Contamine identifies Anseau de Chevreuse as guardian in 1297 and 1304. At Mons-en-Pévèle in 1304 the banner was endangered or lost. Guillaume Guiart later said that he had seen the Oriflamme at Saint-Denis, but the object he saw need not have been the cloth taken into the battle.

Contamine proposed that, after the 1304 loss, another old or replacement banner in the treasury may have been brought forward and identified as the ancient banner of Dagobert and Charlemagne. That is a plausible reconstruction, not a documented confession of substitution.

### 1315: Louis X

**Documented.** Louis X took the banner for a new Flemish expedition and entrusted it to Raoul, called Herpin, lord of Erquery.

### 1316: Philip V as regent

Philip took the banner while still regent. The abbot, objecting to what he regarded as an invasion of a royal privilege, refused to celebrate Mass or bring out the martyrs’ relics. The bishop of Saint-Malo instead celebrated the service and blessed the banner.

Contamine treats this first explicit recorded blessing as strong evidence that the banner was a *res nova*, a new thing needing consecration, rather than an untouchably continuous relic. It is one of the clearest signs of physical replacement.

### 1328: Philip VI and Cassel

**Documented.** Philip VI raised the banner in July 1328 for the Flemish campaign, entrusted it to Mile de Noyers, and returned it after the victory at Cassel.

### 1338–1340: probable uses

Royal itineraries place Philip VI at Saint-Denis immediately before the 1338 Amiens expedition, the 1339 Buironfosse campaign, and the 1340 Bouvines campaign. Taking the banner is probable but not directly stated in each case. A visit on 24 October 1340 may mark its return.

### 1346: Crécy

**Taking documented; fate disputed.** Philip VI came to Saint-Denis on 22 or 23 July before pursuing Edward III. Mile de Noyers was guardian. Some later histories say the Oriflamme was lost at Crécy; the available evidence does not securely describe an English capture of an identifiable cloth. A subsequent banner existed.

### 1347: Sangatte/Calais

Richard Lescot’s chronicle supports another taking for the attempted relief of Calais. Geoffroi de Charny was guardian in 1347.

### 1355–1356: Geoffroi de Charny and Poitiers

Jean II formally entrusted custody to Geoffroi de Charny by letters of 25 June 1355. The Oriflamme probably accompanied the Amiens campaign and was certainly present at Poitiers on 19 September 1356. Charny died fighting near the king.

Later writers say that the English captured the banner. Its bearer was killed and the French army collapsed, so physical loss is highly probable, but no securely proven English trophy or later English inventory entry identifies the cloth. A later French Oriflamme must consequently have been another textile.

### 1368–1370: Arnoul d’Audrehem

Charles V, who did not personally lead field armies, assigned the guardianship to Marshal Arnoul d’Audrehem. This shows that “guardian of the Oriflamme” could exist as an office even when the banner was not immediately deployed.

### 1372–1382: Pierre de Villiers

Charles V’s letters of 15 October 1372 appointed Pierre de Villiers, lord of L’Isle-Adam, to guard the king’s Oriflamme and carry it whenever the king rode personally into battle. The wording also reflects a shift: the abbey’s banner had become “our Oriflamme” in royal administration.

Under Charles VI, Villiers bore it in the 1382 Flemish campaign. At Roosebeke on 27 November, Froissart says it was unfurled. The French victory became the object’s most famous late-medieval triumph.

### 1383: Gui de La Trémoille and Guillaume des Bordes

Gui de La Trémoille carried or guarded it during the Bourbourg campaign. Guillaume des Bordes was appointed guardian on 27 October 1383 and held the office until his death in 1397.

### 1397–1414: Hutin d’Aumont

Hutin d’Aumont became guardian on 28 July 1397 and retained the office until his death on 13 March 1414. In 1412 Charles VI ceremonially took the folded banner at Saint-Denis and placed it around Aumont’s neck, to remain folded until military necessity required mounting and unfurling it.

### 1414–1415: Guillaume Martel

Guillaume Martel, lord of Bacqueville, was appointed guardian on 28 March 1414 and died at Agincourt on 25 October 1415.

Older histories therefore say that the Oriflamme was carried and captured at Agincourt. Contamine and later historians distinguish office from actual deployment: Martel’s status as guardian and his death in the battle do not prove that the cloth was present. Since Charles VI was not personally in the army and the normal premise for deployment was the king’s personal expedition, its presence remains disputed.

### 1417–1418: civil war and multiple custodians

Contamine records:

- Olivier de Mauny in 1417;
- Morelet de Montmor in 1418;
- an otherwise obscure man called Chapelain in November 1418.

These rapid appointments occurred amid the Armagnac–Burgundian struggle. The sources indicate that an object or office called the Oriflamme still functioned, but continuity with the pre-Poitiers or even pre-1304 textile cannot be established.

### 1420–1436: English occupation

Claims that the Oriflamme disappeared during the English occupation of Saint-Denis are difficult to reconcile with its later inventory history unless “disappeared” means a campaign cloth was taken while another, older banner remained. No securely identified English-held Oriflamme survives.

### 1465: Louis XI

Louis XI had the Oriflamme raised on 30 August 1465 during the War of the Public Weal. It was apparently not taken into the battle of Montlhéry itself or used thereafter as a battlefield standard. This is generally treated as the last royal raising of the Oriflamme.

### 1505 and 1534: the decayed treasury banner

The 1505 inventory located a very decayed cendal standard at Saint-Denis, reportedly on or near a pillar behind the treasury or relic cabinets. The 1534 inventory again listed a thick, very decayed sendal gonfanon. Whether this was the textile raised in 1465, an older banner, or an object retrospectively identified with the series is unresolved.

### 1594 and seventeenth century

A further Saint-Denis inventory in 1594 reportedly mentioned the standard. Jacques Doublet described it in 1625, combining observation, institutional tradition, and historical reconstruction. Its fabric progressively perished. Early-modern descriptions suggest that a staff with remnants of cloth remained near the martyrs’ altar.

### 1706–1721: final traces

Michel Félibien’s 1706 history describes the traditions and disposition of Saint-Denis’s treasures. A 1715 guide reported the Oriflamme’s lance or associated staff above the eighth treasury cabinet. The 1721 treasury description no longer lists it.

There is no documentary basis for saying that revolutionaries destroyed an intact Oriflamme in 1793. By then the documented medieval or late-medieval textile had already disappeared. Stories of its destruction during the Revolution conflate its fate with the well-documented dispersal and destruction of other Saint-Denis treasures.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Was there one original?

Almost certainly not in the strict textile-conservation sense. Silk carried repeatedly on campaign could not plausibly survive intact through multiple losses and centuries of exposure. More importantly, the sources themselves imply replacement:

- banners were reported lost in failed expeditions;
- a banner was newly blessed in 1316;
- the 1505 and 1534 inventories call the surviving textile extremely decayed;
- custodial and ceremonial identity continued after probable physical losses.

The Oriflamme’s continuity was therefore institutional and ritual, comparable to an office, seal type, or repeatedly renewed liturgical textile.

### Was the 1505 banner the object of 1124?

There is no proof. Arguments for continuity rest on the abbey’s own identification and the absence of an admitted replacement. Arguments against include the military losses, the 1316 blessing, the fragility of silk, and the centuries separating the records. The safest designation is “a late-medieval or earlier Saint-Denis banner identified by the abbey as the Oriflamme.”

### Was it Charlemagne’s banner?

No contemporary Carolingian evidence supports this. The identification arose through the convergence of epic literature, Capetian Carolingianism, and Saint-Denis institutional memory between the late twelfth and thirteenth centuries.

### Was it carried at Agincourt?

**Disputed, probably not established.** Guillaume Martel was guardian and died there. That proves an association between the office-holder and the battle, not the banner’s presence. The king was absent, and surviving contemporary battle narratives do not unambiguously place the Oriflamme on the field.

### Was it captured at Poitiers?

Physical loss is probable; documented English custody is not. Charny died while bearing or guarding it, but no authenticated English-held fragment, treasury entry, or later recovery is known.

### Modern claimants

No modern textile has a credible provenance to the Middle Ages. Current banners bearing the name are avowed or evident reconstructions. None has undergone scientific authentication as the original because no serious institutional provenance claim warrants such testing.

### Scientific investigation

There is no surviving candidate accepted for fibre, dye, weave, radiocarbon, or metallurgical investigation. Consequently:

- no scientific manufacture date exists;
- no material provenance is known;
- no original dimensions can be recovered scientifically;
- no museum accession number belongs to the medieval object;
- modern imagery cannot substitute for lost physical evidence.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Divine and saintly associations

- **Saint Denis of Paris:** sacred patron and notional owner; the banner represented his protective presence with the royal army.
- **Saint Rusticus and Saint Eleutherius:** Denis’s fellow martyrs; their altar and relics formed part of the taking and blessing ceremonies.
- **God and Christ:** victory under the banner was represented as divine judgment or protection.
- **Saint Peter:** literary or legendary prior owner of *Romaine* in the Carolingian epic genealogy.
- **Constantine:** later typological model through comparison with the labarum and visionary lance.
- **Saint Michael:** his banner eventually replaced or displaced the Oriflamme in late royal military symbolism.

### Institutional networks

- **Abbey of Saint-Denis:** owner, custodian, ritual authority, and principal producer of the banner’s institutional history.
- **Counts of Vexin:** asserted earlier standard-bearers of the abbey.
- **Capetian and Valois monarchy:** temporary recipient and increasingly dominant interpreter of the object.
- **Royal household and military officers:** supplied the guardians who bore or kept it.
- **Saint-Denis chroniclers:** preserved documentary memory while selectively accommodating Carolingian and Merovingian legends.
- **English and Burgundian opponents:** helped give the banner its international reputation as the distinctive French sacred standard.
- **Antiquarians and heraldists:** Galland, Du Cange, Doublet, Félibien, and later scholars reconstructed its appearance and history from divergent records.

### Principal royal users

- Louis VI, 1124;
- Louis VII, 1147;
- Philip II Augustus, 1190 and later campaigns;
- Louis IX, 1248 and 1270;
- Louis X, 1315;
- Philip V as regent, 1316;
- Philip VI, 1328, 1346, and probably other campaigns;
- Jean II, 1355–1356;
- Charles V as appointing authority;
- Charles VI, particularly 1382 and 1412;
- Louis XI, 1465.

### Documented guardians

Contamine’s reconstructed list for the later Middle Ages is:

1. Anseau de Chevreuse — 1297 and 1304.
2. Raoul/Herpin, lord of Erquery — 1315.
3. Mile de Noyers — possibly 1304; certainly 1328 and 1346.
4. Geoffroi de Charny — 1347; formally appointed 25 June 1355; killed at Poitiers in 1356.
5. Arnoul d’Audrehem — 1368–1370.
6. Pierre de Villiers, lord of L’Isle-Adam — appointed 15 October 1372; bearer at Roosebeke in 1382.
7. Gui de La Trémoille — Bourbourg campaign, 1383.
8. Guillaume des Bordes — 27 October 1383–1397.
9. Hutin d’Aumont — 28 July 1397–13 March 1414.
10. Guillaume Martel, lord of Bacqueville — 28 March 1414–25 October 1415.
11. Olivier de Mauny — 1417.
12. Morelet de Montmor — 1418.
13. Chapelain — November 1418.

“Porte-Oriflamme” is a convenient later title. Contamine preferred the contemporary and broader *garde de l’oriflamme*. It should not automatically be treated as a stable great office of the Crown equivalent to constable or marshal.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Taking the banner

The developed “liturgy of the Oriflamme” involved some or all of the following:

1. the king came personally to Saint-Denis;
2. the martyrs’ relics were exposed or elevated;
3. Mass and prayers were celebrated;
4. the banner was placed on or taken from the altar;
5. the abbot delivered it to the king;
6. the king might carry the folded cloth around his neck;
7. a chosen knight swore to guard and return it;
8. it was mounted on its lance and unfurled only when battle was imminent.

Not all elements are documented at every date. The fully developed ritual is clearest in fourteenth- and early-fifteenth-century narratives.

### Ownership expressed through ritual

The king did not originally take the banner as an unrestricted possession. He received it from the saint, through the abbey, for a particular emergency. Its expected return emphasized continuing Saint-Denis ownership.

The 1316 dispute demonstrates that ritual authority mattered materially: the abbot could not prevent the regent from taking the cloth but could refuse the normal Mass and relic ceremony. Resort to another bishop showed both royal power and the abbey’s continuing claim.

### Battlefield function

The banner:

- marked a specially solemn royal campaign;
- embodied Saint Denis’s presence and protection;
- served as a rallying point;
- ranked before other French banners in ceremonial descriptions;
- proclaimed that the king fought for the realm under divine patronage;
- bound aristocratic military service into a collective royal cause.

It was not the ordinary positional standard of the king. The blue fleurdelisé could remain beside the monarch while the Oriflamme occupied another position in the host.

### “No quarter”

Medieval and modern scholars have connected unfurling the red banner with an intention to wage battle without quarter. Gervase’s “death or victory” formula and later associations of red with severity support the reputation. Some military historians, including Maurice Keen, accepted a functional no-quarter meaning.

The evidence should nevertheless be stated narrowly. No surviving general ordinance says that every raising of the Oriflamme automatically prohibited taking prisoners. Medieval battles fought under it did produce captives. The object’s reputation communicated extremity, divine judgment, and exceptional war; later writers hardened that symbolism into a categorical rule. Modern legal histories have continued to treat it as an ancestor of the red “no quarter” flag, but that is a historical interpretation rather than a uniformly recorded medieval procedure. [Oxford chapter abstract](https://academic.oup.com/book/7963/chapter-abstract/153289167)

### Coronations

The Oriflamme was not one of the regular coronation regalia at Reims and was not normally used in the coronation liturgy. Its function was departure for war, not accession or anointing. Nineteenth-century historical paintings sometimes blur these categories through generalized royal ceremonial imagery.

### Cry and emblem

“Montjoie Saint Denis!” became the Capetian battle cry. The exact derivation of *Montjoie* is disputed: explanations include a mound or marker, a place-name, Charlemagne’s sword or banner, and a corruption of Latin expressions. The cry and banner reinforced one another even though their earliest histories were not identical.

In modern French, *oriflamme* broadened to mean an elongated decorative banner, often with pointed ends, severing the word from the unique medieval object.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The banner of Charlemagne

**Early literary tradition, later material identification.** Charlemagne’s epic banner was called *Romaine*, *Montjoie*, or *orie flambe*. By the thirteenth century writers treated the Saint-Denis banner as this same thing. The claim supplied the Capetians with Carolingian inheritance and supplied Saint-Denis with an imperial antiquity.

### Gift of Pope Leo III

**Later legend.** Leo III supposedly gave the banner to Charlemagne. No contemporary papal, imperial, or monastic record confirms it.

### Saint Peter’s banner

**Epic-sacral genealogy.** By giving the banner a Petrine or Roman origin, tradition made French kingship the beneficiary of apostolic authority.

### Constantine’s visionary standard

**Late medieval typological elaboration.** The Oriflamme was compared with, or genealogically connected to, Constantine’s labarum. Variants describe a white, golden, or blazing lance. The point was symbolic equivalence: Christian sovereign, heavenly sign, victory.

### Clovis’s heavenly insignia

**Late medieval elaboration.** Jean Golein and related royal mythographers connected the banner with the celestial gifts of French kingship associated with Clovis. This placed the Oriflamme alongside the fleur-de-lis and holy oil in a divinely ordained national regalia complex.

### Dagobert and Saint-Denis

**Monastic tradition, attested around 1300 and later.** Dagobert supposedly entrusted the banner to the abbey. The legend served Saint-Denis’s claim that royal use depended on an ancient monastic possession.

### Clovis II as maker

**Early-modern institutional history.** Jacques Doublet asserted in 1625 that Clovis II had the red banner made. Auguste Galland and Du Cange rejected this and similar stories as unsupported.

### Blood of Denis

**Late devotional legend.** The red textile was supposedly dipped in Denis’s blood. Early sources merely describe red silk and do not supply this origin.

### Roosebeke miracle

**Near-contemporary reported portent.** At Roosebeke in 1382 mist or cloud allegedly obscured the battlefield; when Pierre de Villiers unfurled the Oriflamme, sunlight broke through on the French side. Froissart and later chroniclers treated this as encouragement or divine favour. The weather event cannot be independently reconstructed, but the interpretation is historically important: it made victory proof of the banner’s efficacy.

### Certain victory unless lost through sin

The banner developed the character of a palladium: properly received and defended, it promised saintly protection; defeat was explained by sin, improper procedure, an unworthy bearer, or physical loss rather than failure of the saint.

### Destruction in the French Revolution

**Modern conflation.** Saint-Denis’s treasury was indeed dispersed and much was destroyed during the Revolution, but the Oriflamme cloth had ceased to be recorded by 1721. No evidence establishes that revolutionaries destroyed an intact medieval banner in 1793.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### Medieval object

**Status:** lost or destroyed through decay, probably after multiple medieval replacements.

**Last reasonably secure location:** the abbey church of Saint-Denis, where a severely decayed cendal standard was inventoried in 1505 and 1534.

**Final material trace:** a lance or staff associated with the Oriflamme was reportedly kept above a treasury cabinet in the early eighteenth century. It disappears from descriptions after 1715 and is absent in 1721.

**Current owner or legal controller:** none identifiable. No extant object supports a present ownership, restitution, or repatriation claim.

**Scientific status:** unexamined because no authenticated candidate survives.

### Modern substitutes and representations

Modern Oriflammes at Saint-Denis or in ceremonial and educational contexts are reconstructions. A banner reportedly made under Louis-Philippe and another associated with 1913 do not replicate the securely known medieval object in all respects. Their inscriptions and decoration should not be treated as archaeological evidence.

The French Ministry of Culture’s collections database includes objects and images titled *Oriflamme de Saint-Denis*, but these are later prints, paintings, cards, or commemorative material. One chromolithographic advertising card, for example, is catalogued as inventory 995.1.140.6 A / BV2.17.6 and dates only to the late nineteenth or early twentieth century. [French collections catalogue](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/joconde/5002E003616)

## 9. Modern Reception

### Bourbon Restoration and July Monarchy art

The nineteenth century converted the Oriflamme into a highly visible scene of national history. Pierre Révoil’s *Philippe-Auguste prend l’oriflamme à Saint-Denis* was commissioned by Louis-Philippe in 1840 for the historical galleries at Versailles and exhibited in 1841. It reconstructs a ceremonial medieval interior and banner; it is not a documentary image. [Versailles/POP catalogue](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/joconde/000PE004421)

Jean-Baptiste Mauzaisse painted Louis VII taking the Oriflamme before the Second Crusade. Aristide Cholet engraved Révoil’s composition. School prints, advertising chromolithographs, and popular histories standardized a red, pointed, often gold-decorated banner.

### National symbolism

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century writers treated the Oriflamme as an ancestor of the French national flag. Its red was retrospectively incorporated into genealogies of the tricolour. This is symbolic reception, not a direct documented transfer of cloth, design, or legal status.

Royalist and Catholic memory organizations continue to invoke it as an emblem of Saint-Denis, Christian kingship, and French continuity. Such uses preserve the medieval association but often reproduce modern banner designs.

### Literature

The Oriflamme appears prominently in:

- Robert Southey’s *Joan of Arc*;
- nineteenth-century historical novels and histories;
- Arthur Conan Doyle’s *The White Company*;
- popular Hundred Years’ War narratives;
- modern fantasy, heraldry, gaming, and alternate-history settings.

These works commonly amplify three traits: flame-like tails, supernatural victory, and “no quarter.” None should be used as evidence for the medieval textile.

### Joan of Arc

Joan’s association is indirect. She venerated Saint Denis and deposited armour at the abbey, but there is no sound evidence that she bore the historical Oriflamme. Artistic and literary conflation with her own painted standard is modern.

### Military naming and commercial use

French vessels and organizations have borne the name *Oriflamme*. The word is also commercially used for vertical advertising banners. That generic usage derives from the medieval banner’s remembered shape but no longer implies Saint-Denis or royal war.

### Replicas

Replicas vary substantially:

- plain red;
- red with gold flames;
- red with a gold sun;
- red with crosses or stars;
- two-, three-, or many-tailed;
- bearing *MONTJOIE SAINT DENIS*.

The diversity accurately reflects modern uncertainty but is sometimes falsely presented as a single historically verified design.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Early institutional histories

Jacques Doublet’s *Histoire de l’abbaye de S. Denys* (1625) preserved valuable late descriptions but accepted Merovingian and Carolingian traditions too readily.

Auguste Galland’s *Des anciennes enseignes et estendarts de France* (1637) was more sceptical. He explicitly declined to adjudicate stories that the banner came from heaven or originated under Clovis, Dagobert, or Charlemagne.

Charles du Fresne, sieur du Cange, published his major dissertation on the banner in 1668. He rejected the remote legendary chain and interpreted it as the ordinary military banner of Saint-Denis, entrusted to the abbey’s lay advocate. His thesis dominated scholarship for centuries.

Michel Félibien’s 1706 history documented the abbey and treasury before their revolutionary destruction and dispersal. It remains indispensable but mixes direct observation, archival transcription, and received tradition. [Digitized Félibien volume](https://books.google.com/books?hl=fr&id=b92MaD_LHGEC)

### Inventory scholarship

Henri Omont published the 1505 and 1739 Saint-Denis inventories in 1901. The 1505 entry is critical because it records a material object—already “very decayed”—rather than merely an episode in royal legend.

The modern Saint-Denis treasury project and the published 1634 inventory reconstructed the wider physical and documentary setting of the banner. The Oriflamme was part of a treasury that joined relics, royal insignia, antiquities, and political memory. [Review of the 1634 inventory edition](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ccmed_0007-9731_1975_num_18_70_2003)

### Philippe Contamine

Contamine’s *L’Oriflamme de Saint-Denis aux XIVe et XVe siècles: étude de symbolique religieuse et royale* (1975) remains the central documentary study. Its major contributions were:

- separating the abbey banner from the legendary Carolingian Oriflamme;
- reconstructing individual campaign takings and returns;
- identifying the guardians from royal letters and chronicles;
- treating replacement after loss as a real material problem;
- analysing the liturgy surrounding the object;
- showing the gradual royal appropriation of what remained formally Saint-Denis’s banner;
- comparing textual descriptions with manuscript imagery;
- refusing to assume that a single cloth survived.

Bernard Guenée’s contemporary review emphasized Contamine’s demonstration of two interacting traditions: the monks’ legal history of an abbey banner connected with the Vexin, and the broader royal-popular legend of Charlemagne, Constantine, and Rome. [Guenée review](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ahess_0395-2649_1976_num_31_6_293779_t1_1170_0000_001)

### Later debates

Anne Lombard-Jourdan connected the Oriflamme and fleur-de-lis to deeper sacred geography and cultic traditions around the Paris basin. Reviewers regarded parts of her reconstruction as speculative. Her work is important to the historiography but does not supply a secure material provenance before 1124. [Scholarly review](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bulmo_0007-473x_1993_num_151_3_3404_t1_0547_0000_5?pageId=T1_548)

Hervé Pinoteau emphasized royal symbolic systems, rejected the Oriflamme’s inclusion among coronation regalia, and stressed discrepancies among later reconstructions.

Olivier Bouzy examined the Oriflamme beside the coronation sword and Holy Lance as political symbols whose legendary antiquity exceeded their documentable histories.

### Outstanding questions

1. Was the 1124 textile newly made or already an abbey banner?
2. How literal was the diploma’s claim of earlier Vexin custom?
3. Which campaign losses resulted in total destruction rather than temporary separation?
4. Was the 1316 banner necessarily new, or could an existing textile receive a renewed blessing?
5. What object did Guillaume Guiart see after Mons-en-Pévèle?
6. Was any Oriflamme actually present at Agincourt?
7. Was the 1505 banner a campaign replacement, an older treasury banner, or a ceremonial simulacrum?
8. When precisely did the final cloth perish?
9. Did any part of the associated staff survive anonymously in another collection?

The surviving record cannot resolve these questions.
