# The Brazen Head (Roger Bacon legend): Object Dossier

**Proposed filename:** `the_brazen_head_roger_bacon_legend_object_dossier.md`

**Research status:** completed 6 September 2026.

## Evidentiary key

- **Documented fact:** supported by a datable primary record, surviving edition, institutional catalogue, or physical artifact.
- **Textual fact:** a statement about what a particular source says, not evidence that the narrated event occurred.
- **Scholarly inference:** a reconstruction accepted or proposed by modern editors but not directly documented.
- **Tradition/legend:** a historical claim transmitted without contemporary evidence.
- **Modern retelling:** a deliberate literary, artistic, occult, or popular adaptation.
- **Unresolved:** the surviving evidence does not permit a firm conclusion.

The central finding is straightforward: **no physical artifact has been identified, catalogued, excavated, scientifically examined, or credibly documented as Roger Bacon’s Brazen Head.** The object’s reconstructable life is textual and performative. Its “chain of custody” runs through medieval analogues, a lost Elizabethan narrative source, Robert Greene’s play, surviving seventeenth-century chapbooks and illustrations, stage performances, antiquarian discussion, political satire, Romantic and modern fiction, and Oxford place-lore. Real brass heads, architectural ornaments, and sites have subsequently become proxies for it, but none possesses a credible material provenance from Bacon.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Object class and identity

The Brazen Head is a **legendary anthropomorphic speaking object**, variously classifiable as:

- a magical image;
- an oracular statue or bust;
- an artificial speaking head;
- an astrological or alchemical construct;
- a demonically animated image;
- an automaton or proto-robot in modern interpretation;
- a failed national-defense instrument in the specifically Baconian legend.

The dossier tracks **the head attributed to Roger Bacon**, not every medieval talking-head tradition. Closely related heads attributed to Gerbert of Aurillac/Sylvester II, Robert Grosseteste, Albertus Magnus, Virgil, Enrique de Villena, and others are treated as antecedents and parallel claimants rather than as the same object.

### 1.2 Names

The principal historical English forms are:

- **“an head of brass”** — the wording used by John Gower for Robert Grosseteste’s related object in *Confessio Amantis*, Book IV, lines 234–43;
- **“a brazen head” / “the Brazen Head”** — the normal early-modern designation;
- **“Brasen head” / “Brasen-head”** — spellings in seventeenth-century print;
- **“brass head”** — later modernization;
- **“speaking head”**, **“talking head”**, **“oracular head”**, and **“mechanical head”** — descriptive modern terms;
- **“brazen android”** — William Douglas O’Connor’s nineteenth-century technological reinterpretation, not the original object’s historical name.

“Brazen” in Middle and Early Modern English ordinarily means **made of brass, bronze, or copper alloy**, not necessarily the standardized copper–tin alloy now called bronze. No surviving recipe provides proportions, and no specimen exists for metallurgical testing. Accordingly, statements that the head was specifically “bronze” exceed the evidence.

The name has no demonstrated connection to **Brasenose College’s** historic brass or bronze door-knocker. The college states that “Brasenose” probably derives from a prominent nose on a brazen door-knocker. Greene exploited the pun by placing Bacon at “Brazen-nose” and giving his head a conspicuous nose, but linguistic resemblance is not material identity. [Brasenose College history](https://www.bnc.ox.ac.uk/about-brasenose/history/)

### 1.3 Physical description by source

#### John Gower’s Grosseteste analogue, late fourteenth century

In *Confessio Amantis*, Book IV, lines 234–43, Gower says Robert Grosseteste used his learning to “make and forge” a **head of brass** that would “tell / Of such things as befell.” He worked seven years but lost the result through the negligence of half a minute. Gower supplies:

- material: brass;
- form: a head;
- technique: “make and forge,” broadly artisanal rather than technically precise;
- intended faculty: foretelling events;
- duration of manufacture: seven years;
- dimensions, weight, facial features, inscriptions, internal mechanism, and final physical condition: unstated.

This is the immediate surviving English model for the Baconian failure story, but its owner is Grosseteste, not Bacon. [John Gower Society editions](https://johngower.org/online-editions/) [University of Michigan text record](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/c/cme/Confessio?view=toc)

#### The anonymous *Famous History of Friar Bacon*

The fifth episode of the prose *Famous History* gives the fullest quasi-technical description. Bacon and Friar Bungay construct a **head of brass** because Bacon believes that, if it can speak and he can hear its message, he will learn how to wall England in brass. A summoned devil supplies the secret by which it can be made to speak.

The prose account explains the supposed operation in natural-magical terms: Bacon reasons that human speech proceeds by an “effusion of vapours” and attempts to reproduce it through the continuous fumes of the “six hottest simples.” This is not a reproducible recipe: the six substances are not securely enumerated, proportions and apparatus are absent, and no mechanism is described.

The head:

- is made of brass;
- is sufficiently substantial to fall and break;
- remains inert during a prolonged watch;
- speaks three times, at half-hour intervals;
- says, in sequence, **“Time is,” “Time was,” and “Time is past”**;
- falls after the final utterance amid noise, fire, smoke, or “strange flashes”;
- is found broken on the floor.

Miles’s comic song claims that it had formerly been a kettle: “Time was when thou a Kettle wert.” This is mockery, not an independent provenance statement. It nevertheless introduces the possibility, internal to the fiction, that the head’s brass was recycled domestic metal. [1629 text and catalogue](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eebo/A01692.0001.001/1%3A1?rgn=div1%3Bview%3Dfulltext) [Folger catalogue](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/395577)

#### Robert Greene’s *Friar Bacon and Friar Bungay*

Greene calls it a “monstrous head of brass,” set on a pedestal in Bacon’s Oxford study. Its dramatic attributes are:

- a notably large or comic nose;
- placement at the center of the stage;
- supernatural animation “by the enchanting forces of the devil”;
- a voice capable of three brief pronouncements;
- destruction by an external supernatural hand wielding a hammer.

The stage direction after “TIME IS PAST” reads: “Then there is a flash of lightning and a hand appears, which breaks the Head with a hammer.” The shattered pieces remain visible long enough for Bacon to lament over them. Greene therefore differs from the prose tale: the head does not simply fall; a visible hand destroys it. [Complete play text](https://www.luminarium.org/renascence-editions/greene2.html)

No surviving early stage property is known. Every performance necessarily employed a newly made prop, but surviving records do not identify its materials or maker. Wood, plaster, pasteboard, painted cloth, or thin metal are all conceivable; none is documented.

#### The 1629/1630 woodcut

The 1629 chapbook and 1630 second quarto of Greene’s play reproduce a woodcut showing:

- a large human head on or above a shelf of books;
- speech bands carrying the temporal utterances;
- Miles playing pipe and tabor;
- Bacon and Bungay asleep below.

The image accords more closely with the prose narrative, in which both friars sleep, than with Greene’s scene. It is therefore evidence for seventeenth-century visualization, not a portrait of a medieval artifact and not necessarily a record of Elizabethan staging. The block was first used for Elizabeth Allde’s 1629 *Famous History* and reused for the 1630 play. [Queen’s Men Editions textual introduction](https://lemdo.uvic.ca/qme/emdFBFB_TextIntro.html) [Christie’s description of a 1666 edition](https://www.christies.com/en/lot/lot-5320112)

### 1.4 Dimensions, weight, inscriptions, and condition

No early source supplies measurements or weight. No permanent inscription is reported. The words “Time is,” “Time was,” and “Time is past” are speech, not inscriptions, although woodcuts render them visually in ribbons.

Within the legend, the object’s final condition is **destroyed and fragmented**. Outside the legend, no fragments are documented. Claims that remains were preserved in Oxford or Lincoln derive from later local lore and cannot be attached to a catalogued object.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Historical Roger Bacon: negative evidence

Roger Bacon lived in the thirteenth century and wrote extensively on language, optics, mathematics, natural philosophy, experiment, astrology, and the powers of art and nature. He discussed ships without rowers, self-moving vehicles, flying machines, optical devices, artificial thunder, and other marvels. These passages made later mechanical interpretations of the head seem plausible.

No authenticated work by Bacon says that he made a speaking metal head. No thirteenth-century inventory, Franciscan record, university record, letter, chronicle, workshop account, or eyewitness report documents one. The absence is especially important because the surviving Baconian legend appears centuries after his lifetime.

Robert Recorde, in *The Castle of Knowledge* (1556), argued that Bacon’s mathematical accomplishments were misclassified as necromancy by people who did not understand their causes. Recorde mentions Bacon’s reputation as a necromancer but does not securely attest this particular head story. [Robert Recorde resource](https://www.cosmopoetics.uni-bayreuth.de/en/textbooks/recorde_robert_the-castle-of-knowledge/index.html)

### 2.2 Earlier speaking-head traditions

The Baconian object belongs to a long, mobile motif rather than an independently documented invention.

#### Gerbert of Aurillac/Sylvester II

William of Malmesbury’s *Gesta regum Anglorum*, completed in the early twelfth century, relates that Gerbert cast the head of a statue under a calculated conjunction of the stars. It answered questions truthfully in the affirmative or negative. Gerbert asked whether he would become pope and whether he would die before celebrating Mass “at Jerusalem.” He misunderstood the second answer because “Jerusalem” proved to be the name of a Roman church.

This source predates the Baconian attribution by centuries and supplies several durable elements:

- a learned cleric accused of magical knowledge;
- a cast metal head;
- astrological manufacture;
- short, truthful but ambiguous answers;
- failure caused by the operator’s misunderstanding rather than mechanical error.

William’s account does not identify the object as Roger Bacon’s and does not contain “Time is / Time was / Time is past.” [William of Malmesbury translation](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AWilliam_of_Malmesbury%27s_Chronicle.djvu/196)

#### Robert Grosseteste

Gower’s *Confessio Amantis*, composed in the later fourteenth century and surviving in multiple recensions, gives the closest English antecedent: Grosseteste forges a brass head over seven years but loses its disclosure through half a minute’s negligence. The placement in Book IV, a treatment of sloth or negligence, makes the head an exemplum: intellectual labor is defeated by a brief lapse in vigilance.

The transfer from Grosseteste to Bacon is undocumented. The men’s historical intellectual association, their Oxford connections, and Bacon’s later magical reputation provided conditions for confusion or deliberate reassignment. The exact author and date of that reassignment remain unresolved.

### 2.3 Earliest Baconian attestation

The earliest securely datable **surviving substantial Baconian head narrative** is Robert Greene’s play, probably written between 1588 and 1591 and first printed in 1594. Its performance history proves that the legend was in circulation by the early 1590s.

The bibliographical problem is significant:

1. The anonymous prose *Famous History* is generally considered Greene’s major narrative source.
2. Its surviving editions postdate Greene.
3. It entered the Stationers’ Register on 12 January 1624.
4. Early surviving editions are dated 1627 and 1629; one bibliographical study reports a 1625 copy described as a second edition.
5. Greene must therefore have used either a lost manuscript, a lost print, oral material, or some combination.
6. Because the presumed source is lost, scholars cannot know how closely the surviving Caroline chapbooks preserve the sixteenth-century version.

The **earliest attestation is thus split**:

- **Earliest securely surviving dramatic witness:** Greene’s play, composed approximately 1588–91, printed 1594.
- **Presumed earlier narrative witness:** a lost sixteenth-century form of the *Famous History*.
- **Earliest surviving prose editions:** 1620s.
- **Earlier analogues:** Gerbert and Grosseteste, not Bacon.

The Science History Institute catalogues an undated early copy broadly as sixteenth century, but its own description uses cautious language. That institutional dating should not override the more specific bibliographical evidence for the surviving 1620s editions. [Science History Institute copy](https://digital.sciencehistory.org/works/54pkvzr) [Queen’s Men Editions](https://lemdo.uvic.ca/qme/emdFBFB_TextIntro.html)

### 2.4 Manufacture within the mature legend

The mature prose narrative gives this sequence:

1. Bacon conceives a national project: learn from the head how to encircle England with brass.
2. He enlists Friar Bungay.
3. Their unaided learning does not suffice.
4. They summon or constrain a devil.
5. The devil reveals the secret of animation.
6. They fashion the brass head and expose it to fumes.
7. They watch it for weeks; Greene expands the total labor to seven years and the final watch to sixty days.
8. Exhaustion forces them to sleep.
9. Miles receives orders to awaken them when it speaks.
10. The head pronounces its three temporal formulas.
11. Miles treats them as nonsense.
12. The decisive moment passes.
13. The head is destroyed.

Nothing in this description has the status of a manufacturing record.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

Because no physical head is documented, two chains must be kept separate:

- the **internal legendary chain**, describing who holds the thing inside each narrative;
- the **external transmission chain**, describing manuscripts, printed books, theatrical properties, images, and places that carried its identity.

### 3.1 Internal legendary custody

#### Baconian prose version

- **Creator/first possessor:** Roger Bacon, working in Oxford.
- **Co-maker and co-keeper:** Friar Bungay.
- **Supernatural consultant:** an unnamed devil, compelled to disclose the animating secret.
- **Temporary guardian:** Miles, Bacon’s servant, during the friars’ sleep.
- **Final physical state:** broken after Miles fails to wake Bacon.
- **Later custody:** none stated. The fragments have no subsequent narrative career.

No sale, gift, inheritance, theft, burial, or recovery occurs. The head never enters royal custody and never completes its intended service to England.

#### Greene’s play

- Bacon and Bungay construct and watch the head.
- Bacon leaves it under Miles’s observation.
- A supernatural hand breaks it with a hammer.
- Bacon examines or mourns the fragments.
- It disappears from the action thereafter.

The anonymous hand is an agent of destruction, not a documented claimant. Bacon blames hostile infernal powers—Asmenoth and Demogorgon—for grudging that a mortal should accomplish so much, but the stage direction does not identify the hand by name.

### 3.2 External transmission and representational custody

#### Before approximately 1588: lost source stage

A Baconian prose or oral story must have circulated before Greene’s dramatization, but no physical witness has been securely identified. This is the first and largest documentary gap.

#### Approximately 1588–91: Queen’s Men repertory

Greene’s play was written for or performed by the Queen’s Men. If the head appeared in these performances, it existed as a theatrical property owned or controlled by the company. No inventory identifies that prop, and its construction and fate are unknown.

#### April 1594: Rose Theatre performance records

Philip Henslowe’s records note performances of “friar bacone” by the Queen’s Men during the Easter season. The record demonstrates performance of a Bacon play but cannot prove that the performed text was exactly the surviving Greene play or describe its head property. [Queen’s Men Editions](https://lemdo.uvic.ca/qme/emdFBFB_TextIntro.html)

#### 14 May 1594: Stationers’ Register

The “Historye of ffryer Bacon and ffryer Boungaye” was registered for printing. Adam Islip’s name was struck out and the entry transferred to Edward White. Islip printed and White published the first quarto later that year. This transfer concerns printing rights, not the fictional object.

#### 1594 first quarto

The first surviving printed play makes the head reproducible in text. Unlike later editions, its title page used Islip’s fleur-de-lis rather than the talking-head woodcut.

#### 12 January 1624 and the 1620s prose editions

The *Famous History* was entered in the Stationers’ Register to Francis Grove. The earliest surviving copies are exceptionally rare and bibliographically imperfect. The Folger records the 1629 edition as printed by Edward Allde for Grove, 52 pages, signatures A–G⁴ with missing blank leaves in the reproduced copy. [Folger catalogue](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/395577)

#### 1629 woodcut

Elizabeth Allde’s prose edition introduced the influential woodcut of the head, sleeping friars, and Miles. The physical printing block thereafter became the earliest identifiable material object specifically carrying the Baconian head’s image.

The block’s own origin and survival are unknown. It was reused in 1630 and possibly in later editions, but no current repository identifies the original block.

#### 1630 second quarto

Elizabeth Allde printed the second quarto of Greene’s play after the copyright passed from Edward White’s estate to Edward Allde and then to his widow. It reused the 1629 head woodcut and advertised the work as lately played by the Prince Palatine’s Servants.

#### 1640–55 copyright transfers

By 1640 the copyright passed to Richard Oulton, Allde’s son-in-law, who sold it to Moses Bell. Bell’s wife Jean or Jane printed the third quarto in 1655. This is a chain of textual-commercial custody, not possession of a medieval object.

#### 1666 sixth prose edition

Edward Cotes printed an edition sold by Thomas Passinger. A surviving copy described by Christie’s retains the title woodcut and is one of only two copies cited by ESTC for that edition. Christie’s notes that most preceding editions survive in only one or two copies. [Christie’s catalogue](https://www.christies.com/en/lot/lot-5320112)

#### Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries

Chapbook publishers repeatedly reset, abridged, and modernized the story. Surviving copies include Scottish printings, among them an 1807 Glasgow edition. These versions altered wording and illustration while maintaining the basic custody sequence: Bacon and Bungay create; Miles watches; the head speaks and is lost. [1807 Glasgow scan](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/6e/Famous_history_of_the_learned_Friar_Bacon_%282%29.pdf)

### 3.3 Oxford place-lore

A medieval gate-tower at the south end of Oxford’s old South Bridge later became known as **Friar Bacon’s Study**. Tradition identified it as Bacon’s observatory and sometimes as the head’s workshop.

The structure’s documented history is independent of the legend:

- It was a defensive tower, probably built in the thirteenth century.
- Medieval civic records concern timber, locks, ironwork, repairs, residence, and defensive access.
- A circa-1500 estate map depicts it.
- Thomas Waltham or Welcome added an upper storey after 1611.
- Samuel Pepys visited “Friar Bacon’s study” in 1669 and paid a shilling to go up.
- Artists recorded it repeatedly in the eighteenth century.
- It was demolished in 1779 to widen the bridge approach.

No archaeological report or inventory links the tower to a metal head. Its importance lies in giving the legend a stable physical address. [Oxford Cabinet account](https://www.cabinet.ox.ac.uk/friar-bacons-study-and-folly-bridge) [South Oxford local history](https://southoxfordhistory.org.uk/interesting-aspects-of-grandpont-and-south-oxford-s-history/folly-bridge) [Historic England image record](https://historicengland.org.uk/images-books/photos/item/CC72/02348)

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Does the object exist?

There is no positive material evidence that it did. Specifically, no source provides:

- a medieval inventory entry;
- a commission or payment;
- a workshop attribution;
- a contemporary eyewitness report;
- a Franciscan custody record;
- a university or royal acquisition;
- a burial or excavation record;
- authenticated fragments;
- a continuous physical provenance;
- an accession number for a claimed original;
- metallurgical, radiographic, residue, thermoluminescence, or other scientific analysis.

The proper classification is therefore **purely legendary object with later material representations**, not “lost medieval artifact” in the archaeological sense.

### 4.2 Competing creators

The speaking metal head was attributed to several learned clerics and magicians. These are rival narrative allocations of a mobile motif, not rival museums presenting the same surviving artifact.

- **Gerbert/Sylvester II:** astrologically cast head giving yes/no answers.
- **Robert Grosseteste:** prophetic brass head ruined by a half-minute’s negligence.
- **Albertus Magnus:** speaking head or complete artificial man, later destroyed by Thomas Aquinas in expanded versions.
- **Roger Bacon:** head intended to reveal how England could be surrounded with brass, lost because Miles failed to wake him.
- **Virgil and others:** related oracular images in medieval romance.

Chronology favors literary transmission and reassignment: the Gerbert story predates Bacon; Gower’s Grosseteste story closely anticipates Bacon’s seven years and missed moment. This does not prove a single linear source, but it weighs heavily against independent historical manufacture by each scholar.

### 4.3 Brasenose College’s brass head or knocker

Brasenose College possesses a historic “brazen nose” tradition and displays brass-head imagery. The college’s name is traced to Brasenose Hall, documented before the present college’s foundation, and to a face-shaped knocker with a conspicuous nose.

That object is not Bacon’s oracle:

- it is a door fitting, not an automaton;
- it has no speaking mechanism;
- its provenance concerns Oxford and Stamford hall communities;
- no early source identifies it as Bacon’s work;
- Greene’s comic emphasis on the head’s nose likely plays upon Brasenose’s name.

The association is a punning and local-traditional convergence, not identity.

### 4.4 Friar Bacon’s Study

The gate-tower was real, but Bacon’s occupation of it is traditional rather than documented. Even if Bacon used it—which has not been established—that would not authenticate the head. The tower’s destruction in 1779 did not destroy a documented Brazen Head, and no recovery of fragments was recorded.

### 4.5 Natural magic versus demonic magic

Early-modern sources dispute not whether a particular specimen is genuine but what sort of operation such a head would represent.

- The prose narrative combines demonic instruction with a quasi-natural vapor theory.
- Greene foregrounds necromantic charms and the “enchanting forces of the devil.”
- Robert Recorde’s broader defense of Bacon treats apparently magical wonders as misunderstood mathematics.
- Richard Browne’s 1683 life says that some reported Bacon’s speaking head and attributed it to the devil, but argues that Albertus and Boethius achieved speaking-metal effects through “Natural” magic or art.
- Thomas Browne treats the popular tale as an error arising from misunderstanding artificial and alchemical operations.

Richard Browne invokes Cassiodorus’s description of Boethian devices in which metals roar, a bronze serpent hisses, and artificial birds sing. That passage supports the historical existence of acoustic automata in general; it does not authenticate Bacon’s particular head. [Richard Browne’s 1683 life](https://ota.bodleian.ox.ac.uk/repository/xmlui/bitstream/handle/20.500.12024/A28790/A28790.html?isAllowed=y&sequence=5)

### 4.6 Scientific examination

None has occurred because no credible candidate object exists. Claims that the head was a medieval robot, pneumatic engine, phonographic device, alchemical vessel, or concealed human ventriloquist are interpretations of a story, not laboratory findings.

### 4.7 Forgeries and hoaxes

No documented forged artifact has successfully entered a major collection as “Roger Bacon’s original Brazen Head.” Numerous later props, illustrations, souvenirs, and occult reconstructions exist, but they are not forgeries unless represented as medieval originals. No such claimant with a traceable provenance was located in the consulted sources.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### Roger Bacon

**Connection:** legendary designer, maker, and possessor; historically a thirteenth-century Franciscan scholar.  
**Date of association:** securely attested by the late sixteenth century, not during Bacon’s lifetime.  
**Tier:** legend attached posthumously.

### Friar Bungay

**Connection:** co-maker and co-watcher in the *Famous History* and Greene’s drama.  
**Date:** late-sixteenth-century narrative tradition.  
**Tier:** literary character incorporating the name of a historical Franciscan, Thomas Bungay, but the head episode is legendary.

### Miles

**Connection:** servant appointed to watch the head; fails to wake the friars; mocks its words; becomes the proximate cause of the project’s failure in the prose version.  
**Date:** lost Elizabethan source and surviving seventeenth-century chapbooks; dramatized by Greene.  
**Tier:** fictional or comic legendary agent.

### Unnamed devil

**Connection:** compelled to reveal how the head may be animated.  
**Tier:** supernatural actor internal to the prose legend.

### Asmenoth and Demogorgon

**Connection:** Greene’s Bacon names infernal powers that resent his attempted mastery.  
**Tier:** dramatic supernatural attribution, not part of the physical chain.

### Robert Grosseteste

**Connection:** owner-maker of the closest Middle English precursor in Gower.  
**Date:** *Confessio Amantis*, late fourteenth century.  
**Tier:** early attested analogue; no contemporary evidence of manufacture.

### Gerbert of Aurillac, Pope Sylvester II

**Connection:** maker of an earlier oracular cast head in William of Malmesbury.  
**Date:** early twelfth-century text describing a tenth-/eleventh-century figure.  
**Tier:** medieval legend and literary antecedent.

### Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas

**Connection:** later tradition assigns Albertus a speaking artificial head or man; Thomas destroys it in expanded accounts.  
**Tier:** parallel legend.

### Robert Greene

**Connection:** dramatist who gave the Baconian head its most influential theatrical form.  
**Date:** approximately 1588–91; printed 1594.  
**Tier:** documented literary engagement, not custody.

### Queen’s Men

**Connection:** theatrical company associated with the earliest performances and therefore with an unknown stage representation of the head.  
**Date:** late 1580s to 1594.  
**Tier:** documented performance network; the prop is lost or unidentified.

### Adam Islip and Edward White

**Connection:** printer and publisher of the 1594 first quarto.  
**Tier:** documented custodians of the printed text.

### Francis Grove, Edward Allde, and Elizabeth Allde

**Connection:** publisher and printers of the 1629 prose edition and 1630 second quarto; their editions fixed the influential woodcut visualization.  
**Tier:** documented print transmission.

### Richard Oulton, Moses Bell, and Jean/Jane Bell

**Connection:** later owners and printers of the play’s publishing rights; Jane Bell printed the 1655 quarto.  
**Tier:** documented bibliographical chain.

### Thomas Browne

**Connection:** recorded in 1646 that “every ear” was filled with the story and discussed it as a vulgar error.  
**Tier:** documented critical reception. [*Pseudodoxia Epidemica*](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/pseudodoxia/pseudodoxia.shtml)

### Richard Browne

**Connection:** repeated and naturalized the report in his 1683 life of Bacon.  
**Tier:** documented antiquarian reception.

### Brasenose College

**Connection:** provides a punning location and real brass-head imagery; later local tradition linked its “brazen noses” to Bacon.  
**Tier:** institutional and literary association, not authentic custody.

### Oxford civic authorities

**Connection:** controlled and ultimately demolished the real gate-tower known as Friar Bacon’s Study.  
**Tier:** documented custody of an associated site, not of the head.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 No sacred cult or liturgical use

Despite the object’s inclusion here as sacred/legendary material, the Brazen Head was not a documented relic, cult image, or liturgical implement. No worship, offering, procession, pilgrimage rite, consecration, or clerically authorized veneration is attested.

Its religious force is cautionary. The story places forbidden or suspect knowledge in the hands of friars and resolves the experiment in failure, destruction, and, in extended narratives, repentance.

### 6.2 Oracular function

The intended function is revelation:

- Gerbert’s head answers yes/no questions.
- Grosseteste’s would foretell events.
- Bacon’s should utter “aphorisms” or disclose hidden principles.
- Its actual Baconian speech is temporal and enigmatic rather than directly informative.

The three utterances may be read as a countdown, an opportunity structure, or a moral formula about irreversible time. Within the plot they are operational signals: Bacon must be awakened before the final declaration. Miles’s failure converts prophecy into retrospective judgment.

### 6.3 National-defense function

The most distinctive Baconian development is the planned **wall of brass around England**. The head is not itself the wall; it is an information-producing device expected to reveal how the wall can be made.

Greene’s dating near the Spanish Armada has encouraged political interpretations:

- If written before July 1588, the project could dramatize anxiety about invasion.
- If written after, it could participate in patriotic celebration.
- The play’s date is uncertain, and the wall motif alone cannot decide between those positions.

The Queen’s Men’s national touring and royal patronage gave the story a political setting in which Bacon’s mastery glorified English learning while his failure limited unlawful power. The peer-reviewed Queen’s Men edition cautions that supposed Armada allusions remain circumstantial. [Queen’s Men Editions](https://lemdo.uvic.ca/qme/emdFBFB_TextIntro.html)

### 6.4 Symbol of dangerous or failed knowledge

The head came to symbolize:

- intellectual overreach;
- knowledge acquired through illicit intermediaries;
- the fragility of long labor;
- the missed moment of discovery;
- technological ambition defeated by human negligence;
- the ambiguous boundary between natural philosophy, engineering, magic, and fraud.

Greene intensifies the moral structure by having Bacon later renounce magic following deaths seen through his perspective glass. The head’s destruction anticipates that renunciation but is not itself the sole cause.

### 6.5 Access and taboos

No historical access rules exist. Inside the fiction:

- Bacon and Bungay alone understand the project;
- Miles may guard but not interpret it;
- Bacon must hear the head at the proper moment;
- demonic knowledge must be compelled;
- sleep, delay, and ridicule violate the project’s operational conditions.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The missed moment

**Early attested analogue:** Gower’s Grosseteste loses seven years’ labor through half a minute’s negligence.

**Baconian elaboration:** Bacon and Bungay watch for weeks but sleep at the decisive moment. Miles hears the words and fails to wake them.

**Greene’s change:** the failure becomes a theatrical spectacle, and a supernatural hand destroys the head.

The shared seven-year labor and minute-scale failure strongly connect the Grosseteste and Bacon stories. The mechanism of transfer remains undocumented.

### 7.2 “Time is; Time was; Time is past”

These seven words are the Baconian head’s signature. They are absent from the earlier Gerbert account and are not stated by Gower. In narrative sequence:

1. “Time is” announces the present opportunity.
2. “Time was” signals that part of the opportunity has elapsed.
3. “Time is past” closes it irreversibly.

Miles treats the utterances as trivial grammar and answers them with jokes. Bacon understands only afterward that they were the required summons. Later writers detached the formula from the plot and used it proverbially for wasted opportunity or obsolete learning.

### 7.3 The wall of brass

The proposed wall transforms an oracle into an instrument of national security. No physical wall is begun. The head never reveals the method.

The image has parallels in Renaissance drama, including aspirations to encircle territories with brass, but the specific English project belongs to the Baconian legend. It allowed Bacon to appear simultaneously patriotic and dangerously ambitious.

### 7.4 The devil as technical consultant

In the prose tradition, the friars’ own learning cannot animate the head. A devil discloses the necessary secret. Yet the subsequent vapor explanation resembles natural philosophy. This duality enabled opposing readings:

- a literal demonological warning;
- a story about natural effects misread as magic;
- a satire on useless scholastic labor;
- an allegory of illicit knowledge;
- a proto-science-fiction tale.

No single reading exhausts the early texts.

### 7.5 The head as former kettle

Miles’s song says the head was once a kettle “filled with better matter” until Bacon battered its sides. This is an internal comic insult. Later retellings sometimes treat it as an origin detail, but the text does not independently certify that the head was actually forged from a kettle.

### 7.6 Friar Bacon’s Study

Oxford tradition localized construction in the gate-tower at South/Folly Bridge. The tower’s real medieval fabric gave the story architectural credibility. Its demolition in 1779 encouraged picturesque and antiquarian memorialization.

A second legend claimed the tower would stand until a wiser man than Bacon passed beneath it. Its eventual demolition by workmen could be treated as either practical refutation or comic fulfillment. This is a place-legend, not evidence for the head. [Charles Mackay’s 1840 account](https://www.thames.me.uk/Mackay.htm)

### 7.7 Brasenose imagery

Greene places Bacon at “Brazen-nose” and lets Miles joke about the head’s nose. Later commentators connected Brasenose College’s real brass heads and knocker to the oracle. The direction of influence is uncertain, but the documentary evidence supports literary wordplay and retrospective association, not survival of Bacon’s artifact.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Original object

**Status:** legendary; no verified physical existence.

**Location:** none.

**Condition:** within the principal narratives, destroyed. No authenticated fragment survives.

**Legal owner or custodian:** none.

**Accession number:** none.

**Scientific examination:** none.

**Repatriation or ownership dispute:** none.

### 8.2 Surviving material witnesses

The object survives indirectly in:

- copies of the 1594, 1630, and 1655 quartos of Greene’s play;
- seventeenth- to nineteenth-century editions of the *Famous History*;
- the 1629/1630 woodcut and its later impressions;
- promptbooks, modern theatre designs, and props;
- paintings and engravings of Friar Bacon’s Study;
- Brasenose College’s separate brass-head traditions;
- modern literary and artistic reconstructions.

The Science History Institute holds a chapbook copy in the Roy G. Neville Historical Chemical Library under shelfmark **B765.B24 M678**. This is a printed witness, not the legendary head. [Science History Institute](https://digital.sciencehistory.org/works/54pkvzr)

The Folger catalogue describes an electronic reproduction of the Huntington Library’s 1629 copy. [Folger catalogue](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/395577)

Princeton University Library holds Thomas Rowlandson’s 1784 etching *A Peep into Friar Bacon’s Study*. [Princeton Graphic Arts](https://www.princeton.edu/~graphicarts/2012/01/a_peep_into_friar_bacons_study.html)

Worcester College, Oxford, is reported to hold Michael Angelo Rooker’s eighteenth-century painting of Friar Bacon’s Study. The Oxford Cabinet presents it and related engravings as records of the now-demolished tower. [Oxford Cabinet](https://www.cabinet.ox.ac.uk/friar-bacons-study-and-folly-bridge)

### 8.3 Associated site

Folly Bridge occupies the vicinity of the old South Bridge and tower. The original gate-tower is gone. Public access is to the modern bridge and surrounding streets, not to a surviving “study.”

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Seventeenth-century ubiquity

Thomas Browne’s remark that “every ear” was filled with the tale indicates that by 1646 it was proverbial. His treatment helped recast it as an example in the history of error: wondrous art, alchemy, and acoustics became exaggerated into necromancy.

Daniel Defoe’s *A Journal of the Plague Year* (1722), set during 1665, says that fortune-tellers used signs such as “the speaking head” or “Friar Bacon’s head.” Whether every detail reflects actual plague-year signage is not independently established, but Defoe documents the eighteenth-century association between the head and commercial divination.

### 9.2 Cervantes and the fraudulent talking head

In Part II of *Don Quixote* (1615), Don Antonio Moreno exhibits a supposedly enchanted brass head that answers questions. It is exposed as a contrivance operated by a concealed person speaking through a tube. The episode is not Roger Bacon’s object, but it shows how readily the speaking-head motif could be converted into a technological fraud narrative.

### 9.3 Political satire

Thomas Rowlandson’s *A Peep into Friar Bacon’s Study* (1784) casts George III as Bacon and uses the speaking head and magical apparatus to satirize ministerial and parliamentary change. Here the object becomes a machine of opaque statecraft. Princeton identifies the print as an etching in its Graphic Arts Collection. [Princeton Graphic Arts](https://www.princeton.edu/~graphicarts/2012/01/a_peep_into_friar_bacons_study.html)

### 9.4 Romantic-period circulation

Chapbook reprints kept the legend in popular circulation. Carla Pomarè’s study traces references and transformations in Southey, Byron, William Godwin, and Mary Shelley, relating them to contemporary fascination with automata and debates over legitimate science.

Relevant developments include:

- Byron’s allusion to Bacon’s head in *Don Juan*;
- Godwin’s critical retelling in *Lives of the Necromancers*;
- removed Bacon references in an early *Frankenstein* draft;
- related magical-scientific motifs in Shelley’s later writings and theatrical adaptations.

These are documented engagements with the legend, not episodes in the original head’s custody. [Pomarè, “Exploded Knowledge?”](https://iris.uniupo.it/handle/11579/178263)

### 9.5 *The Brazen Android*

William Douglas O’Connor’s “The Brazen Android,” published in *The Atlantic Monthly* in 1891 and collected in *Three Tales* in 1892, converts Bacon’s object into a steam-driven artificial being. O’Connor uses technological spectacle and political reform rather than literal medieval demonology. Correspondence preserved by the Walt Whitman Archive documents its proposed serialization and publication history. [Whitman Archive](https://whitmanarchive.org/item/med.00008.001) [Publication note](https://whitmanarchive.org/item/loc.08244)

### 9.6 Children’s literature

James Baldwin’s “Roger Bacon and the Brazen Head,” in *Thirty More Famous Stories Retold* (1905), supplied an accessible prose version for children. Illustrations and retellings often make the head enormous, solemn, and classically sculptural—details not present in the earliest Baconian sources.

### 9.7 John Cowper Powys

Powys’s historical novel *The Brazen Head* was published by Macdonald in 1956. It places Bacon and Peter Peregrinus in an imaginative thirteenth-century world and gives the head a vatic role. The novel is a major modern reinvention, not a reconstruction based on newly discovered evidence. A contemporary review appeared in *The Spectator* on 7 December 1956. [*Spectator* review](https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/7th-december-1956/24/new-novels)

### 9.8 Science fiction and artificial intelligence

Modern writers and commentators frequently call the head:

- a robot;
- an android;
- a computer;
- an artificial intelligence;
- a voice assistant;
- a medieval chatbot.

These analogies describe narrative function—an artificial object answering questions—but are anachronistic if presented as historical technology. The early sources do not describe computation, stored information, logical machinery, or autonomous learning. They describe prophecy, natural magic, demonic animation, and theatrical wonder.

### 9.9 Occult and esoteric reuse

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century occultists have reconstructed “Brazen Head” rituals or interpreted the object as an initiatory symbol, artificial familiar, golem analogue, or vehicle for an invoked intelligence. Such practices are modern appropriations. They are not evidence that Bacon performed a surviving ritual or that an authentic medieval manual for the head exists.

### 9.10 Commercial and institutional names

“The Brazen Head” has become a pub, periodical, and business name. These names trade on the general speaking-head or brass-head motif and should not be treated as claimants to Bacon’s artifact.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 From biography to folklore

Early biographies often repeated the head while trying to rehabilitate Bacon:

- some accepted the report but naturalized it;
- some blamed popular ignorance for turning mechanics into necromancy;
- others treated it as proof of magical reputation;
- nineteenth-century historians increasingly compared it with older Gerbert, Grosseteste, and Albertus legends.

Richard Browne’s 1683 account is characteristic: “Some report” that Bacon made the head, but comparable speaking devices could arise through natural art. This is not testimony from Bacon’s lifetime; it is late reception explicitly framed as report. [Richard Browne](https://ota.bodleian.ox.ac.uk/repository/xmlui/bitstream/handle/20.500.12024/A28790/A28790.html?isAllowed=y&sequence=5)

### 10.2 Priority of chapbook or play

The major bibliographical dispute concerns the relationship between Greene and the *Famous History*.

**Traditional reconstruction:** Greene adapted an earlier prose narrative now lost.

**Evidence supporting it:**

- five prose episodes closely correspond to scenes in the play;
- the prose organization often appears more episodic and jestbook-like;
- a pre-1590 manuscript or lost edition is plausible.

**Limitations:**

- the surviving prose copies are decades later;
- revision and accretion cannot be measured;
- some material may have moved from drama into later prose;
- oral circulation may have influenced both;
- the earliest edition’s exact date and status remain bibliographically unsettled.

The Queen’s Men Editions therefore treats the lost prose source as a strong scholarly inference, not an extant primary witness. [Textual introduction](https://lemdo.uvic.ca/qme/emdFBFB_TextIntro.html)

### 10.3 Dating Greene’s play

Proposed dates range principally from 1588 to 1591.

Arguments include:

- a reference to St James’s Day falling on Friday, once used to argue for 1589;
- patriotic language associated with the Armada;
- possible composition for the clown Richard Tarlton before his death in September 1588;
- comparison with *Doctor Faustus*, whose own date is disputed.

No argument is decisive. The secure anchors are the 1594 performance and print records.

### 10.4 Meaning of the wall

Scholars have treated the brass wall as:

- pre-Armada anxiety;
- post-Armada celebration;
- national Protestant propaganda;
- a comic impossibility;
- an emblem of intellectual hubris;
- a secularized version of magical enclosure.

Its function may be deliberately overdetermined. Dating uncertainty prevents a confident one-to-one political reading.

### 10.5 Natural philosophy and necromancy

Older popular histories often treated Bacon as a scientist centuries ahead of his time and the head as a distorted memory of real machinery. More recent history of science situates him within thirteenth-century scholastic, Franciscan, astrological, optical, and alchemical cultures. This approach does not deny his technical imagination, but it rejects the inference that later automaton legends are eyewitness memories.

The head’s supposed pneumatic basis may draw plausibility from genuine traditions of hydraulic and acoustic automata. General technological possibility, however, is not object-specific provenance.

### 10.6 Brasenose hypothesis

Kevin LaGrandeur has argued that Brasenose College’s brass heads and its name may have stimulated Greene’s treatment, particularly the comic nose imagery. The case is one of literary and visual influence, not the survival of Bacon’s machine. The theory remains plausible but does not explain the whole motif, which long predates Greene.

### 10.7 State of the primary evidence

The strongest primary witnesses are:

- William of Malmesbury for Gerbert;
- Gower for Grosseteste;
- the 1594 quarto and associated Stationers’ records for Greene;
- Henslowe’s performance notices;
- surviving 1620s *Famous History* editions;
- the 1629/1630 woodcut;
- subsequent quartos and chapbooks;
- early-modern critical references;
- maps, records, and images of Friar Bacon’s Study.

Critical losses include:

- Greene’s manuscript;
- the presumed pre-Greene prose source;
- early Queen’s Men promptbooks;
- original theatrical properties;
- the woodblock’s provenance and present whereabouts;
- potentially many ephemeral chapbook editions.

The evidence supports a detailed history of the legend’s transmission, but not a material biography of a medieval machine. The Brazen Head’s only continuous “substance” is reproducible representation: first an inherited narrative motif, then words on the page, an ephemeral stage property, a woodcut, an antiquarian commonplace, and finally a durable metaphor for artificial intelligence, dangerous knowledge, and the discovery missed at the moment when “Time is” becomes “Time is past.”
