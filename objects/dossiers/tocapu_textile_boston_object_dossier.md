# The Boston MFA t'ocapu textile: Object Dossier

## Scope note and evidentiary key

This dossier concerns the large all-over *tukapu* tapestry conventionally called the “mantle” in the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, accession no. **1988.325**, and called **“MFA325”** by Gary Urton. It does **not** concern either the MFA’s fragmentary Inka tunic 1977.131 or the better-known All-T’oqapu Tunic at Dumbarton Oaks, PC.B.518. Confusion among these objects is common because all three appear in scholarship on Inka *tocapu/t’oqapu/tukapu*.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented:** established by a museum record, direct examination, publication, or historical text.
- **Material observation:** reported from physical examination of the textile.
- **Scholarly inference:** a reasoned interpretation not independently demonstrated.
- **Hypothesis/disputed:** explicitly contested or presently untestable.
- **Unestablished:** evidence needed to decide the claim has not been published or located.
- **Traditional/contextual:** an account of Inka textile practice that may illuminate the object but does not document this individual textile.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Identity

**Current repository:** Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, Massachusetts, United States.

**Accession number:** **1988.325**.

**Principal published names:**

- *Mantle*
- *Tapestry mantle*
- *Cloth mantle MFA325*
- *MFA325*
- “Boston MFA *t’ocapu* textile”
- “Multi-year *tukapu* calendar” or “commemorative calendar”—Gary Urton’s interpretive designation, not an established original title or function.

The object is a large rectangular Andean tapestry, attributed broadly to the **late Inka or early Spanish-colonial period**, probably the late fifteenth or sixteenth century. Its precise date and findspot are unknown. It is exceptional because virtually its entire field is organized into **1,824 geometric design units** interpreted by Urton as *tukapus*.

### Names and terminology

The term appears in modern scholarship in several orthographies: **tocapu, tocapo, tokapu, t’oqapu, toqapu, tuqapu,** and **tukapu**. Urton used *tukapu*. The variation reflects Spanish colonial spellings and modern attempts to represent Quechua pronunciation; it does not establish a single historically exclusive spelling.

The word’s early documented semantic range was broader than the modern art-historical definition “a square containing a geometric motif”:

- An anonymous Quechua vocabulary of **1586** glossed *tocapo* as decorative work embroidered or woven on vessels, boards, and other surfaces.
- Diego González Holguín’s **1608** Quechua dictionary defined *tocapu* as “garments of precious workmanship, or figured woven cloths.”
- Ludovico Bertonio’s **1612** Aymara vocabulary glossed *tocapu isi* as an astonishingly fine garment or clothing of the Inka and noted that the expression was being extended to velvet, figured silks, and brocades when speakers wished to praise them.

Consequently, the now-familiar use of *tocapu* for each individual quadrangular compartment may be a later metonymic narrowing from the fine garment or figured fabric to its component designs. Rodolfo Cerrón-Palomino has emphasized that the term’s etymology remains obscure; popular decompositions that derive it mechanically from *apu* (“lord,” “sacred mountain”) should not be treated as demonstrated etymology. [Historical lexical discussion](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0718-22012024000100074&script=sci_arttext)

### Dimensions and construction

**Documented/material observation:**

- Overall dimensions: approximately **119 × 171 cm**.
- The textile was woven as **two pieces**, subsequently joined.
- The warps run along the textile’s long, horizontal axis as reproduced in the published illustrations.
- The design was executed in **interlocking tapestry weave**.
- Published descriptions identify **cotton and camelid fiber** in the warp system and camelid-fiber wefts.
- Reported density:
  - approximately **32 warp threads per square inch**;
  - approximately **195–200 weft threads per square inch**.
- Rebecca Stone-Miller characterized the weft density as nearly the upper limit attainable with handspun yarn in this medium.
- Average design-unit dimensions reported from Alan Sawyer’s examination are approximately **3.18 × 2.54 cm**.
- There is a joining seam after column 23: one woven panel carries 23 columns and the other 25, rather than two equal groups of 24.
- Susan Niles observed compression of motifs near column 23 and expansion near columns 47–48. She inferred that the panels were woven in the direction represented as left to right and that the weaver or weavers adjusted motif dimensions to achieve a predetermined total. That remains a **scholarly inference**. [Urton, “A Multi-Year Tukapu Calendar,” pp. 247–66](https://www.ictal.org/public/downloads-old/2013-2017/meso-urton.pdf)

### Design inventory

Urton counted **1,824 units**, arranged as **38 rows × 48 columns**. His classification is:

1. **Type A:** 912 concentric yellow, black, and red diamond designs.
2. **Type B:** 684 varied designs in red, brown, green, and indigo, without substantial white areas.
3. **Type C:** 228 varied designs incorporating substantial areas of white.

The arithmetic is highly structured:

- Type A = 912, exactly half the total.
- Types B + C = 684 + 228 = 912.
- Type B is exactly three times Type C.
- The entire design field contains 1,824 units.

Urton further distinguished 26 principal—or 57 variant—Type C designs. He identified white-bearing designs arranged into visually prominent diamond formations with X-shaped, N-shaped, or reversed-N centers.

Alan Sawyer classified the units differently. He agreed on 912 Type A elements but assigned 454 to Type B and 458 to Type C. John Howland Rowe questioned whether the 912 Type A diamonds should be called *tukapus* at all, suggesting that they may be fillers between the more individually differentiated signs. Thus even the statement that the textile has precisely “1,824 *tukapus*” depends on a contested classification: **it certainly has 1,824 counted design units; whether every unit is properly a *tukapu* is disputed**.

### Color, condition, damage, and alteration

Published descriptions identify yellow, black, red, brown, green, indigo, and white fibers. The textile is generally well preserved but bears several conspicuous areas of staining.

No inscription in an alphabetic or securely deciphered script is present. Claims that the motifs constitute writing, calendrical notation, heraldry, identities, or place names are interpretations of the textile’s visual organization, not translations of a demonstrably deciphered text.

No published evidence located for this dossier establishes:

- its weight;
- the species of camelid represented by the animal fibers;
- dye identifications;
- fiber-isotope results;
- radiocarbon dates;
- ancient repairs;
- modern conservation treatments or backing;
- whether the seam is entirely original;
- chemical identification of the stains.

Urton stated explicitly that the stains had **not undergone chemical analysis** when he published his study.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Physical origin

**Documented:** The manufacture site and archaeological findspot are unknown. There is no published excavation record, tomb record, export history, or contemporaneous document securely attached to the textile.

**Attribution:** Its materials, interlocking tapestry technique, color organization, and *tukapu* vocabulary place it within the artistic and technological world of the central Andes under late Inka and early colonial rule. Urton cautiously described its conventions as those prevailing from late pre-Hispanic Inka times into the early colonial period over a broad central-Andean area.

### Date

Three principal positions appear in the literature:

1. **Early colonial date—Alan Sawyer.** Sawyer compared the textile with the unusually well-preserved Dumbarton Oaks All-T’oqapu Tunic and inferred that both might be colonial. This was a stylistic and preservation-based argument, not a scientific date.

2. **Early colonial possibility—Susan Niles.** Niles compared motifs on the MFA textile with *tukapus* depicted on garments in Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala’s early-seventeenth-century illustrated chronicle. Urton found this suggestive but not decisive.

3. **Late Inka or early colonial, unresolved—Gary Urton.** Because neither the textile nor the Dumbarton Oaks tunic has a secure archaeological context, Urton concluded that there was insufficient evidence to assign MFA325 confidently to either side of the Spanish invasion.

No radiocarbon determination for MFA 1988.325 was located. Descriptions that give a narrow date without qualification exceed the presently published evidence.

### Maker and workshop

The weaver or weavers are unknown. No maker’s mark is reported. The extreme fineness, controlled interlocking tapestry, calculated distribution, and adjustments in motif width demonstrate highly trained specialist production.

It is tempting to associate such work with **qompi/cumbi**, the exceptionally fine cloth produced within Inka state institutions, and with specialist weavers conventionally identified from colonial accounts as *acllas* or *acllakuna* (“chosen women”) and *qompi kamayuq*. That association is **contextually plausible but not documented for this individual textile**. No source identifies its maker’s sex, legal status, workshop, ethnic community, patron, or intended recipient.

### Earliest attestation of this particular object

The textile’s pre-1988 history is undocumented in the published sources consulted. The accession number shows its registration by the MFA in **1988**, but the precise acquisition date, vendor or donor, price, previous collection, and import history could not be reconstructed from the publicly retrievable sources.

The object was catalogued and reproduced by Rebecca Stone-Miller in the **1992** MFA exhibition catalogue *To Weave for the Sun: Ancient Andean Textiles in the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston*, plate 67a and pp. 179–82. That publication is the earliest securely identified public scholarly treatment located here. The exhibition ran at the MFA from **7 August to 15 November 1992**. [Smithsonian catalogue record](https://www.si.edu/object/siris_sil_435836)

### Earliest attestation of the class and term

The artifact itself lacks a textual history before modern collecting, but the class of fine figured cloth is documented soon after the Spanish invasion. Sixteenth- and early-seventeenth-century vocabularies and chronicles record *tocapu* as precious figured cloth or Inka clothing. Guaman Poma’s manuscript, conventionally dated about **1615**, depicts Inka rulers and royal women wearing garments with compartmentalized geometric designs. These sources establish a historically real Inka and colonial design vocabulary; they do **not** identify MFA 1988.325.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### Reconstructable chain

1. **Late fifteenth or sixteenth century, central Andes—manufacture.**  
   Attribution is based on technique and design, not excavation documentation. Maker, patron, and exact place are unknown.

2. **Original use—unknown.**  
   Possible functions include mantle, display textile, ceremonial cloth, record-bearing textile, or burial wrapping. None is securely documented.

3. **Possible funerary use—date and place unknown.**  
   Alan Sawyer inferred from the size and distribution of stains that it had wrapped a person about four feet tall, perhaps approximately thirteen years old. He called the individual male, but Urton observed that Sawyer did not explain how sex was inferred. No chemical, DNA, protein, soil, or residue analysis has confirmed a burial use.

4. **Unknown date through 1988—complete provenance gap.**  
   There is no published information on excavation, removal from Peru or another Andean country, dealers, collectors, sale, inheritance, export permit, or movement into the United States. This is the central break in the object’s chain of custody.

5. **1988—accession by the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston.**  
   The accession number is 1988.325. The accession mechanism and consideration paid, if any, are unestablished in the sources available here.

6. **By 1992—cataloguing and exhibition.**  
   Stone-Miller included the textile in the MFA’s *To Weave for the Sun* catalogue and associated exhibition.

7. **Before Urton’s publication—direct scholarly examination.**  
   Gary Urton examined the object with the assistance of Pamela A. Parmal, then curator in the MFA’s Department of Textile and Fashion Arts. He also consulted Alan Sawyer’s unpublished notes and manuscript. Urton’s study appeared in *Skywatching in the Ancient World*, published in 2007.

8. **Present—MFA custody.**  
   Published scholarship continues to identify the textile as part of the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston collection.

### What cannot presently be reconstructed

No evidence located establishes:

- an archaeological excavation;
- the country or region from which it was exported;
- a pre-MFA collection;
- a dealer or auction;
- legal export documentation;
- seizure, theft, restitution, or repatriation proceedings;
- a loan history after 1992;
- deaccession or change in title.

The absence of such evidence is not proof of illicit excavation or export. It is, however, a substantial provenance gap. Because archaeological context was already absent when scholars examined the textile, claims about its original owner, wearer, burial, political affiliation, and exact date cannot presently be verified.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Is it an ancient or early colonial Andean textile?

The published debate concerns **date and function**, not a documented accusation of modern forgery. Its structure, materials, yarn fineness, interlocking tapestry, and Inka-associated motif system have persuaded specialists that it belongs to the late Inka/early colonial Andean textile tradition.

Nevertheless, the absence of archaeological provenance and scientific dating means that the attribution is art-historical rather than independently chronometric.

### Is it properly a mantle?

“Mantle” is a museum and scholarly classification, not a documented Indigenous name for this individual object. Its rectangular format is compatible with a mantle, but its all-over organization is unique among presently known mantle-sized *tukapu* textiles. No surviving tie, fastening, neck opening, or documented wear pattern has been cited to prove that it was worn.

### Was it a burial shroud?

**Sawyer’s hypothesis:** The stain configuration and dimensions suggested that the textile wrapped a roughly four-foot-tall adolescent, perhaps about thirteen years old.

**Problems:**

- the stains were not chemically analyzed;
- no human remains or excavation documentation accompany it;
- the inference that the person was male was unexplained;
- the textile could have acquired stains in another use or after excavation;
- Urton stressed that funerary use, even if correct, need not describe the object’s entire life.

Verdict: **plausible but unverified scholarly inference**.

### Was it made before or after the Spanish conquest?

Sawyer favored an early colonial date; Niles found affinities with Guaman Poma’s colonial drawings; Urton concluded that the evidence did not discriminate securely between late pre-Hispanic and early colonial manufacture. No scientific date resolves the issue.

Verdict: **open question**.

### Is it a calendar or writing?

Urton proposed that the 1,824 units encode a five-year structure: five solar years contain 1,825 days, one more than the design count. He divided the composition into paired fields around a central strip and argued that 26 principal or 57 variant signs might locate events, groups, titles, persons, *ayllus*, or places within a recurring five-year framework. He connected this with Martín de Murúa’s report that Inka censuses occurred every five years.

This is an ingenious structural hypothesis, not a decipherment. There is:

- no bilingual key;
- no independently readable sign sequence;
- no demonstrated one-to-one correspondence between particular designs and words, dates, places, or names;
- no proof that the five-part partition was intended calendrically;
- no explanation for why a five-year count should contain 1,824 rather than 1,825 units beyond the near match;
- no securely identified event cycle encoded by the object.

Urton himself could not specify the calendar’s content and acknowledged uncertainty over its public or private use.

Verdict: **important but unverified hypothesis**. Calling the textile “the Inka five-year calendar” as fact is not warranted.

### Is every counted element a *tukapu*?

Rowe and Sawyer questioned whether the 912 repetitive concentric diamonds are true *tukapus* or patterned fillers. Urton retained them because the total count underlies his calendrical analysis.

Verdict: **terminological and analytical dispute**.

### Rival objects

There is no rival claiming the identity of MFA 1988.325. There are, however, comparable objects that must not be conflated with it:

- **Dumbarton Oaks PC.B.518:** a complete, much smaller All-T’oqapu Tunic, 90.2 × 77.15 cm, woven as a garment and currently not on view. [Dumbarton Oaks object record](https://museum.doaks.org/objects-1/info/23071)
- **MFA 1977.131:** a damaged Inka tunic or tunic fragment, approximately 76.2 × 60.9 cm, acquired with Landon T. Clay funds; not the 119 × 171 cm mantle. [Christine Beaule’s illustrated discussion](https://www.scielo.cl/pdf/eatacam/n59/0718-1043-eatacam-01301.pdf)

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Established significance of its material class

Fine textiles were major stores and media of value in the Inka polity. Colonial accounts describe cloth as tribute, state property, elite apparel, diplomatic gift, sacrificial offering, and a marker of office and hierarchy. All-over or banded *tukapu* designs occur on elite garments and in colonial depictions of Inka rulers.

This context supports an elite or institutional setting for a textile of MFA325’s extraordinary fineness. It does not establish who commissioned or used it.

### Proposed functions for MFA325

1. **Mantle or wearable cloth:** conventional classification; not independently demonstrated.
2. **Elite or royal textile:** plausible from quality and imagery, but no named royal association exists.
3. **Public or restricted display:** Urton speculated that it might once have hung in a palace, temple precinct, or community setting where its signs could be read or contemplated. No mounting evidence or textual record confirms this.
4. **Commemorative five-year record:** Urton’s hypothesis.
5. **Census-related textile:** a possible refinement of the five-year hypothesis, based on Murúa’s statement that Inka census-taking recurred every five years. No direct linkage exists.
6. **Burial shroud:** Sawyer’s stain-based hypothesis.
7. **Prestige wrapping or funerary offering:** conceivable, but unverified.

No attested ritual procedure, taboo, miracle, curse, procession, coronation, sacrifice, or continuing veneration is associated with this individual object.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

MFA325 has no documented premodern legend of its own. Its modern “legendary” career consists principally of interpretive claims generated after museum acquisition.

### “The burial shroud of an Inka noble boy”

**Earliest located source:** Alan Sawyer’s unpublished manuscript, known through Urton.

**Claim:** Stains map the body of an adolescent male about four feet tall and thirteen years old.

**Status:** Modern scholarly conjecture. Neither burial context nor biological sex is established. Urton specifically questioned the unexplained sex attribution.

### “The Inka five-year calendar”

**Earliest full formulation located:** Gary Urton, “A Multi-Year Tukapu Calendar,” 2007.

**Claim:** The textile’s 1,824 motifs approximate five 365-day years and organize historically determined identities or events in five sectors.

**Status:** Published scholarly hypothesis, neither deciphered nor independently confirmed. Spanish-language discussions have repeated it as a possible calendrical textile, sometimes with less caution than Urton used. [Spanish discussion of the 1,824-unit hypothesis](https://qhapaqnan.cultura.pe/sites/default/files/mi/archivo/QuipuYTocapu.pdf)

### “An Inka writing system”

The idea that *tocapus* encode language, heraldry, offices, places, calendrical information, or social categories long predates Urton’s object-specific study. MFA325’s ordered diversity makes it attractive in this debate, but no accepted decipherment exists.

The most disciplined formulation is that the designs may have communicated socially structured information to knowledgeable viewers. Calling them alphabetic writing, translating individual motifs as words, or claiming to read a continuous message is unsupported.

### “Royal ownership”

Because colonial images associate elaborate *tocapu* garments with Inka rulers, modern descriptions may call exceptional all-over pieces “royal.” No source links MFA325 to Atahualpa, Huáscar, Manco Inka, or another named sovereign. The royal designation is categorical and inferential, not a provenance.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Exhibition and publication

The textile entered broad scholarly visibility through the MFA’s 1992 exhibition and catalogue *To Weave for the Sun*. The catalogue framed ancient Andean textiles as primary evidence for artistic thought and social organization rather than merely as archaeological craft products.

Urton’s 2007 analysis transformed MFA325 from an exceptional but enigmatic tapestry into a frequently cited candidate for nonalphabetic Andean record keeping. Subsequent Spanish-language literature has used it in discussions of:

- *tocapu* as notation or writing;
- calendrical structures;
- the organization of Andean space and time;
- comparison between textile grids, architecture, and state administration;
- numerical patterning in non-European information systems.

### Replicas and commercial reception

No authorized full-scale replica, commercial reproduction, forged claimant, or ritual substitute for MFA325 was located. The object is less visible in popular culture than the Dumbarton Oaks All-T’oqapu Tunic, whose image is widely reproduced in teaching materials and museum merchandise. Images of the two should not be substituted for one another.

### Digital and educational afterlife

Urton’s label “MFA325” has become a practical scholarly identifier. Its apparent numerical regularity has encouraged its treatment as a “calendar textile,” especially in secondary sources. The strongest popular distortion is the conversion of a conditional hypothesis—“may represent”—into a claim that the object is a deciphered Inka calendar.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Alan Sawyer

Sawyer conducted an early close material and formal examination. His unpublished report remains important because it recorded:

- thread counts;
- average motif dimensions;
- staining;
- motif classes;
- the possible shroud function;
- an early colonial attribution.

Because the report is unpublished and was accessed through the MFA, most readers know it through Urton’s quotations and summaries. Its evidence should not be treated as independently reproducible without consulting the archival manuscript and textile.

### Rebecca Stone-Miller and the 1992 MFA catalogue

Stone-Miller provided the foundational published catalogue description, including:

- scale and materials;
- exceptional fineness;
- the count of 1,824 motifs;
- formal interpretation;
- comparison with Inka and colonial textiles.

Susan Niles’s contribution considered the textile’s date and relationship to colonial imagery. The catalogue remains the central object-specific publication, although a freely accessible full text was not located.

### Gary Urton

Urton made the most elaborate numerical and semiotic analysis. Its strengths are the explicit count, transparent classification, diagrams, attention to panel construction, and clear acknowledgment of uncertainty.

Its vulnerable points are:

- dependence on counting repetitive Type A units as *tukapus*;
- the one-unit discrepancy between 1,824 and five 365-day years;
- absence of an external key linking signs to referents;
- speculative reconstruction of display and use;
- uncertain dating;
- lack of archaeological context.

Urton presented his conclusion assertively but still labeled its content and use uncertain. Later citations should preserve that distinction.

### Broader *tocapu* debate

Major interpretive schools have treated *tocapu* as:

- elite ornament and status display;
- emblematic or heraldic signs;
- representations of ethnic groups, territories, offices, or conquered peoples;
- mnemonic or semasiographic notation;
- astronomical or calendrical patterning;
- a full or partial writing system;
- polysemic images whose significance depended on garment, wearer, event, and visual context.

There is no consensus that *tocapus* constitute glottographic writing—that is, signs systematically recording spoken language. Colonial dictionaries describe valuable figured cloth but do not supply a sign list or phonetic decipherment. Guaman Poma and Murúa depict patterned garments, but their images do not furnish a bilingual “Rosetta Stone.”

### Outstanding research needs

The most consequential future work would include:

- accelerator mass spectrometry radiocarbon dating of securely sampled fibers;
- fiber-species identification;
- dye and mordant analysis;
- microscopy of yarn and panel joins;
- chemical and proteomic analysis of stains;
- residue, soil, pollen, and mineral comparison;
- archival reconstruction of the 1988 acquisition;
- identification of dealer, collector, export, and import records;
- full publication of Sawyer’s notes;
- high-resolution recto, verso, seam, edge, and stain imaging;
- blind replication of motif counts and classifications;
- comparison with securely excavated Inka and early colonial textiles.

Until those investigations occur, the textile is physically authentic as a highly accomplished Andean-style tapestry in the judgment of specialists, but its exact date, place, maker, original function, and pre-1988 custody remain unresolved.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and near-primary sources

- Anonymous Jesuit vocabulary, *Arte y vocabulario en la lengua general del Perú* (1586): earliest located lexical definition of *tocapo*, cited in modern lexical scholarship.
- Diego González Holguín, *Vocabulario de la lengua general de todo el Perú llamada lengua Qquichua o del Inca* (Lima, 1608): defines *tocapu* as valuable figured garments or woven cloth.
- Ludovico Bertonio, *Vocabulario de la lengua aymara* (1612): records *tocapu isi* as extraordinarily fine Inka clothing.
- Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala, *El primer nueva corónica y buen gobierno*, c. 1615: comparative images of patterned royal and elite garments.
- Martín de Murúa, *Historia general del Perú*, text originating in the late sixteenth/early seventeenth century: account of five-year Inka censuses, used by Urton as contextual support.
- Alan R. Sawyer, unpublished manuscript and notes on MFA325: consulted and partially quoted by Urton.

### Research limitations

The central limitation is not lack of interpretive literature but lack of object documentation before accession. The known scholarly history begins after the textile had been detached from archaeological context and entered the museum. The inaccessible or unpublished evidence—MFA accession files, dealer correspondence, conservation records, Sawyer’s complete manuscript, and any export documentation—may materially alter this dossier.

No scientific dating, dye study, fiber-species report, stain analysis, DNA study, or complete modern conservation report for MFA 1988.325 was located. Absence from the sources consulted should not be confused with proof that no internal museum examination exists.
