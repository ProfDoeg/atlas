# Shiva's linga: Object Dossier

**Requested filename:** `shivas_linga_object_dossier.md`  
**Research current to:** 6 September 2026

## Scope and evidentiary finding

“Shiva’s linga” is not the proper name of one uniquely identifiable artifact. It denotes a class of cult objects and, at a theological level, Shiva’s manifesting “sign” or form. There is consequently no single manufacture date, chain of custody, or present location to reconstruct. Thousands—probably millions—of consecrated, portable, naturally formed, archaeological, reconstructed, and newly made lingas exist.

This dossier therefore keeps four histories distinct:

1. the Sanskrit concept **liṅga** and the developing textual traditions that identify a linga with Shiva;
2. the archaeological history of securely identifiable Shiva lingas;
3. the ritual “life” prescribed for an individual consecrated linga;
4. the individual histories of important specimens or successive objects bearing the same sacred identity.

No securely demonstrated material or documentary chain connects any Indus-civilization stone to a later Shiva linga. The earliest presently known, broadly accepted monumental candidate is the still-worshipped **Gudimallam linga**, but even its date ranges in scholarship from approximately the second century BCE to several centuries CE. The twelve **jyotirliṅgas** are twelve sacred identities attached to different shrines and, in several cases, to successive replacement objects; they are not surviving fragments of a demonstrable prehistoric object.

### Evidentiary labels used

- **Documented:** supported by an inscription, excavation report, object catalogue, legal record, or contemporary account.
- **Archaeological inference:** interpretation of excavated material, style, or stratigraphy.
- **Textual tradition:** what a datable religious text states, without treating its narrated event as material history.
- **Institutional or temple tradition:** a shrine’s received account.
- **Living religious claim:** a theological identification accepted by practitioners.
- **Disputed:** materially conflicting evidence or substantial scholarly disagreement.
- **Legend/late accretion:** a narrative not independently established as historical.
- **Modern claim:** recent interpretation, political use, or commercial representation.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Names and meanings

The principal Sanskrit term is:

- **लिङ्ग — liṅga** (neuter; nominative/accusative singular *liṅgam*): “mark,” “sign,” “token,” “characteristic,” “evidence,” “distinguishing organ,” and, in relevant religious usage, Shiva’s emblem or manifest form.
- **शिवलिङ्ग — śivaliṅga**: “Shiva’s sign/emblem.”
- **शिवलिङ्गम् — śivaliṅgam**: the same compound with its Sanskrit neuter ending.
- Common modern forms include Hindi **शिवलिंग / शिवलिङ्ग**, Bengali **শিবলিঙ্গ**, Tamil **சிவலிங்கம்** (*civaliṅkam*), Telugu **శివలింగం**, Kannada **ಶಿವಲಿಂಗ**, Malayalam **ശിവലിംഗം**, Indonesian/Javanese **lingga**, and English **linga**, **lingam**, or **Shiva lingam**.

“Lingam” is not a separate indigenous object class; it reflects preservation of the Sanskrit case ending in English usage. “Phallus” describes an undeniable formal and semantic dimension of many examples, especially Gudimallam and several early Mathura specimens, but it does not exhaust the object’s meanings. Sanskrit theological sources also explain the linga as Shiva’s sign, an axis, the cosmic principle into which creation dissolves, and a limitless pillar of radiance. Conversely, attempts to declare that the object has *never* had a phallic dimension are inconsistent with early material evidence and several Sanskrit myths. The historically defensible description is therefore **polysemous**, not exclusively sexual or exclusively abstract.

Other technical names include:

- **svayambhū-liṅga**: “self-manifested” linga, normally a naturally occurring formation rather than an artisan-made icon;
- **bāṇaliṅga / narmadeśvara-liṅga**: naturally rounded river stones, especially those collected from the Narmada;
- **mānuṣa-liṅga**: an artisan-made linga;
- **mukhaliṅga**: linga carrying one or more anthropomorphic faces;
- **ekamukhaliṅga**: one-faced linga;
- **caturmukhaliṅga**: four-faced linga, conventionally implying a fifth, upward or transcendent aspect;
- **jyotirliṅga**: “linga of radiance/light,” especially the twelve celebrated Indian pilgrimage manifestations;
- **ātma-liṅga**: a linga embodying Shiva’s own essence in certain regional legends;
- **liṅgodbhava**: Shiva’s “emergence from the linga,” principally the limitless-pillar narrative and its iconography;
- **liṅga-pīṭha**, **yoni**, or **yoni-pīṭha**: the pedestal or receptacle supporting the shaft.

### 1.2 Standard material form

A monumental temple linga commonly consists of:

1. a vertical shaft, normally rounded at its visible upper end;
2. a pedestal with a basin and projecting drainage channel;
3. a buried or concealed lower portion anchoring the shaft;
4. sometimes one or more carved faces, a vertical line conventionally called the *brahmasūtra*, applied metal coverings, textiles, serpent ornaments, flowers, or painted markings.

Iconographic manuals divide a developed artisan-made linga into:

- **Brahmabhāga**, the usually square lower section;
- **Viṣṇubhāga**, the normally octagonal intermediate section;
- **Rudrabhāga** or **pūjābhāga**, the cylindrical visible portion.

The exact ratios vary by textual school and type. T. A. Gopinatha Rao’s synthesis of Sanskrit iconographic texts records numerous proportional systems rather than one universal design. The lower sections are frequently invisible after installation. A database entry from India’s National Mission on Monuments and Antiquities describes, for example, a damaged Badami linga whose shaft and circular yoni-pīṭha are made from the same stone, with the customary depression and outlet channel; it assigns the work stylistically to the Late Chalukya period.

Materials prescribed or actually used include stone, clay, sand, wood, metal, gems, crystal, ice, earth, butter, grain, flowers, ash, and naturally shaped river stones. Permanent temple objects are usually stone; temporary ritual lingas may be formed and dissolved during one observance. A surviving object may therefore range from a many-ton stone installation to a hand-sized portable icon or an ephemeral mound with no intended archaeological afterlife.

### 1.3 The yoni-pīṭha

The basin receives water, milk, and other liquids poured over the shaft and conducts them away through a spout. Text and practice associate the base with the goddess—variously Pārvatī, Umā, Śakti, or a cosmic feminine principle—and the combined installation with generative totality.

The paired sexual anatomy is explicit in some objects and interpretations, but the pedestal is also a working piece of ritual hydraulic design. Calling every base simply a “vulva” omits its liturgical construction and broader cosmology; denying the sexual symbolism altogether is equally reductive.

### 1.4 Inscriptions and marks

Most worshipped lingas bear no inscription on their exposed shaft. Dating therefore depends on:

- archaeological context;
- stylistic comparison;
- inscriptions on a pedestal, shrine, enclosure, foundation deposit, or associated architecture;
- historical descriptions;
- recorded reconstruction or consecration.

Prolonged ablution can polish, erode, or obscure working traces. Renovation may replace the basin without replacing the shaft, reset an old shaft at a different floor level, cover lower portions, or incorporate an excavated linga into a later temple.

### 1.5 Representative surviving specimens

#### Gudimallam linga

**Location:** Paraśurāmeśvara temple, Gudimallam, Andhra Pradesh, India; still in worship.

**Material and form:** a tall stone linga with a strongly naturalistic phallic upper profile and a full-length male deity carved in high relief on the front. Published measurements vary according to whether buried portions are counted. Doris Srinivasan reported approximately five feet visible above ground and a shaft about one foot thick. The lower shaft is polygonal and terminates in a square fixing section.

The relief figure stands upon a crouching dwarf or *guhya*. He holds a ram or antelope, a water vessel, and an axe; his hair and bodily treatment differ from the later standardized Shiva image. Identification as an early form of Shiva/Rudra is widely accepted but rests on iconography, not a naming inscription.

Excavation in 1973–74 exposed concealed portions and concentric stone rings. The Archaeological Survey of India reported a polished brownish sandstone pedestal, an approximately 1.35-metre-square railing, associated early pottery, and an initially open-air installation. A later rectangular pedestal had altered what could be seen.

#### National Museum, New Delhi, caturmukhaliṅga

**Accession:** 65-172.  
**Find-place:** Mathura.  
**Date assigned by the museum:** second century CE.  
**Material:** stone.

Four Shiva heads are carved around the shaft; one wears a Kushan-style turban. This is a documented museum object and important evidence that anthropomorphic and columnar representations were being deliberately combined in Kushan-period Mathura.

#### Metropolitan Museum, southern Thai linga

**Accession:** 1992.150.3.  
**Date:** seventh–eighth century.  
**Material:** sandstone.  
**Dimensions:** 31.8 × 16.5 × 15.2 cm.  
**Provenance published by the museum:** gift of Steven Kossak, Kronos Collections, 1992.  
**Status in 2026:** displayed at the Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York.

Its conical upper body and square shaft exemplify a peninsular Thai form that differs from common Indian profiles. Its pre-market archaeological findspot and removal history are not supplied in the online catalogue, leaving a provenance gap before private ownership.

#### Metropolitan Museum, Afghan ekamukhaliṅga

**Date:** ninth century, Shahi period.  
**Material:** white marble.  
**Place assigned:** Afghanistan.  
**Status:** displayed at the Metropolitan Museum.

A single face of Shiva emerges from a phallic shaft. Its marble, hairstyle, and facial treatment combine regional Shahi, Kashmiri, Gupta-derived, and possibly Sasanian visual elements. The published collection page does not establish an excavated context.

#### British Museum eastern Indian linga

**Accession:** 1872,0701.119.  
**Date assigned:** 1700–1800.  
**Material:** sandstone.  
**Description:** a snake coils around the shaft; the base is unfinished, indicating that it was probably once set into a shrine floor.

**Custody:** collected in India between 1777 and 1828 by Major-General Charles Stuart of the East India Company; sold at auction after Stuart’s death; acquired by John Bridge; donated in 1872 by members of the Bridge family. The catalogue documents the British chain after Stuart but not the circumstances under which he removed or purchased it in India. It is therefore an object of colonial-period acquisition with incomplete source-site provenance, not one that can responsibly be called either a documented purchase or documented theft.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 The Vedic evidence: what it does and does not establish

The noun *liṅga* has non-cultic meanings—sign, characteristic, evidence—in early Sanskrit. Occurrence of the word alone does not attest a Shiva icon.

Two often-repeated origin claims require caution:

- The **Atharvavedic Skambha hymn** praises a cosmic support or pillar. Later interpreters could relate *skambha* to the Shiva linga, but the hymn itself does not describe the standard Shiva cult object.
- The Rigvedic term **śiśnadeva** has been invoked as evidence for “phallus worshippers.” Its interpretation is contested, and it does not securely identify a Shiva linga.

Accordingly, neither passage establishes a continuous, demonstrable Vedic linga cult.

### 2.2 Indus-civilization claims

Excavators at Harappa and other Indus sites recovered polished, rounded, ring, and pillar-like stones. John Marshall and later writers interpreted some as phallic cult objects and connected them to Shiva. That reconstruction remains disputed.

Doris Srinivasan’s examination identified critical breaks in the argument:

- the stones lack inscriptions identifying them;
- their archaeological function is uncertain;
- the famous so-called “proto-Shiva” seal does not itself demonstrate linga worship;
- there is no continuous two-millennium sequence connecting Indus objects to securely identifiable early historic Shiva lingas;
- early undisputed lingas can be more anatomically explicit than the abstract stones alleged to be their prototypes.

Thus:

- **Documented:** rounded and ring-like objects were excavated at Indus sites.
- **Unverified:** that they were cult objects.
- **More specifically unverified:** that they were Shiva lingas.
- **Not established:** a custody, ritual, or manufacturing tradition connecting them to later Shaivism.

Claims that a particular Indus stone is “the world’s oldest Shiva linga” exceed the evidence.

### 2.3 Earliest secure or near-secure material horizon

Gudimallam is usually treated as the earliest surviving major Shiva linga, but its date is not fixed.

I. K. Sarma interpreted excavation stratigraphy, pottery, railing, and architectural phases as supporting an origin around the second century BCE. Other scholars proposed approximately the first century BCE or first century CE. Stylistic critics have suggested dates as late as the third or fourth century CE. The Italian archaeological survey in *Treccani* observes that the early occupation of the site is secure, but the excavation did not conclusively show an early layer sealing the sculpture and pedestal; later construction may have cut into earlier levels. Early pottery near an object is not by itself the manufacture date of that object.

The correct conclusion is:

> Gudimallam is an early and exceptionally important surviving linga, probably of the late centuries BCE or early centuries CE, but the frequently repeated second-century-BCE date is an archaeological argument, not a direct inscriptional or scientific date.

There is no published radiocarbon date for the stone itself—stone cannot ordinarily be radiocarbon dated—and no maker’s inscription or quarry analysis securely identifying its workshop.

Other early material evidence includes realistic or face-bearing lingas from Mathura, Bhita, Reh, Nand, and related north Indian sites, many assigned to approximately the first–third centuries CE. By the Kushan and Gupta periods, the association with Shiva is materially clear.

### 2.4 Early epigraphic setting

The undated Udayagiri Cave 4 inscription from the reign of Chandragupta II records dedication of a Shaiva cave by his minister Vīrasena. The cave’s ekamukhaliṅga and inscription belong to a securely historical Gupta royal environment, around the turn of the fifth century CE. This does not establish the first linga, but it demonstrates court-supported Shaiva installation by that period.

The Mathura pillar inscription of Gupta year 61, generally placed in the fourth century CE, records the teacher Ārya Udita’s installation of **Upamiteśvara** and **Kapileśvara** in a teachers’ shrine. Scholarly interpretation commonly understands these as named memorial lingas associated with deceased teachers. The inscription provides unusually concrete evidence for manufacture, naming, installation, lineage, institutional guardianship, and threatened supernatural penalties for interference.

### 2.5 Textual formation

No single securely dated Sanskrit text created the linga tradition. The relevant strata accumulated across centuries:

- The **Mahābhārata** contains praise of Shiva and references to worship of his *liṅga*, but the epic is composite. Individual passages cannot automatically be projected into the text’s earliest oral stage.
- Early **Śaiva Āgamas** and related ritual traditions codified consecration, proportions, placement, worship, repair, and disposal.
- The **Śivadharma** textual corpus helped make Shiva worship available to wider lay communities. Its linga-origin material explains the cosmic prototype as neither gold, silver, copper, crystal, nor pearl; ordinary installed lingas imitate it.
- The **Liṅga Purāṇa**, **Śiva Purāṇa**, **Skanda Purāṇa**, and regional *māhātmyas* elaborate creation stories, pilgrimage geographies, materials, ritual rewards, and named sacred lingas. Their surviving recensions are layered and substantially later than the archaeological beginnings of the cult.
- Tamil Shaiva hymns of Appar and Campantar, conventionally placed in the seventh century, refer to the story in which Brahmā and Viṣṇu cannot find Shiva’s upper and lower limits. They are important witnesses to the myth’s established circulation in South India.

### 2.6 Mythic manufacture

In the limitless-pillar story, the divine prototype is not manufactured. Shiva appears between Brahmā and Viṣṇu as an immeasurable column of fire or radiance. Brahmā flies upward as a bird—usually a goose or swan—and Viṣṇu digs downward as a boar. Neither reaches an end. Shiva then reveals himself, establishing his supremacy.

Later texts explain terrestrial lingas as imitations or localized manifestations of this unmade cosmic form. Other narratives instead derive linga worship from:

- Shiva’s severed or fallen phallus;
- the sages’ curse in the Daru forest;
- the gods’ need to contain Shiva’s generative energy in the goddess’s receptacle;
- particular divine gifts, including the ātma-liṅga carried by Rāvaṇa;
- spontaneous manifestation from earth, river, rock, ice, or light.

These are parallel theological etiologies, not sequential events in one material object’s biography.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

## 3.1 Why no universal custody chain exists

A linga’s sacred identity ordinarily begins with manifestation, discovery, naming, or consecration, not legal title in the modern museum sense. A permanent temple linga normally belongs to or is controlled by a shrine, endowed institution, hereditary trustees, monastic body, royal foundation, statutory board, or public trust. Portable household lingas pass through gift, inheritance, initiation, sale, excavation, colonial collection, and the art market.

The history is consequently a branching forest of objects. The following chains are the most reconstructable and representative.

### 3.2 Gudimallam: in-situ continuity with architectural alteration

#### Initial installation

**Archaeological inference, disputed date:** the linga was installed with concentric ring stones and enclosed by a small square railing. Sarma reconstructed an open-air sacred enclosure, later supplied with brick architecture.

No maker, commissioner, purchase price, quarry, or original priestly lineage is known.

#### Early and medieval rebuilding

Excavation identified multiple architectural phases. A brick shrine and later stone temple successively enclosed the cult object. Between approximately the ninth and twelfth centuries, destruction or rebuilding of earlier brick fabric, removal of the railing, and raising of the floor altered the linga’s visible proportions. Parts of the shaft and relief were concealed. Chola-period structures and inscriptions attest later institutional occupation.

This is not a clean museum-style chain. The best-supported reconstruction is continuous or repeatedly renewed worship at the same sacred point, with changing architecture around an older image. It remains possible that the linga was reset during a later phase; stratigraphy does not exclude every relocation scenario.

#### Scholarly “discovery”

The object was not discovered in the sense of being unknown locally. It remained in worship. Archaeologist T. A. Gopinatha Rao brought it into modern scholarly literature in the early twentieth century—often dated to his 1903 inspection and subsequent publication. “Discovery” therefore means documentation by colonial-era archaeology, not recovery of an abandoned artifact.

#### 1973–74 intervention

The Archaeological Survey of India removed or dismantled later fabric around the base sufficiently to excavate the concealed installation and examine its lower section. This was both archaeological investigation and conservation intervention. The linga was not accessioned into a museum; it remained in its temple and in ritual use.

#### Present custody

The monument is protected by the Archaeological Survey of India while functioning as a living Hindu shrine. Ritual custody and heritage control therefore overlap. Published sources do not supply a conventional accession number for the linga itself.

### 3.3 Somnath: sacred identity through destroyed and replacement bodies

Somnath illustrates why a shrine’s linga identity must not be equated automatically with one continuous block of stone.

#### Before 1026

Texts and inscriptions establish Prabhas/Somnath as an important sacred centre, but the manufacture and physical description of the pre-1026 linga are not securely recoverable. Temple tradition gives it cosmic antiquity and identifies it as the first jyotirlinga.

#### Mahmud of Ghazni’s raid, 1025–26

Persianate chronicles describe Mahmud’s assault on Somnath, plunder of the sanctuary, and destruction of its cult image. Details grew in later retellings: that the image was suspended magnetically, hollow and filled with jewels, offered for ransom, personally smashed after Mahmud rejected payment, or cut into threshold stones sent to Ghazni, Mecca, and Medina.

The raid and destruction are historical; many dramatic particulars are literary accretions serving triumphalist, colonial, nationalist, or apologetic arguments. No authenticated fragment of the pre-1026 linga has been scientifically identified.

#### Later reinstatements and destructions

Successive rulers and communities rebuilt the shrine and installed new lingas. Government and temple histories record renewed destruction or desecration under forces associated with the Delhi and Gujarat Sultanates and a later Mughal order. The details, dates, and number of “destructions” differ among chronicles and modern accounts. Claims that Somnath was destroyed exactly seventeen times are popular but are not a secure object-by-object inventory.

The essential custody fact is that each destruction breaks any demonstrable physical continuity. Later lingas inherit the **Somnath identity**, not a proven unbroken material body.

#### Ahilyabai Holkar’s temple

In the eighteenth century, Queen Ahilyabai Holkar sponsored a nearby temple and consecrated a linga, restoring active worship without reconstructing the ruined monument as it now stands. That linga and shrine constitute one branch of Somnath’s ritual continuity.

#### 1947–51 reconstruction

Vallabhbhai Patel called for reconstruction after visiting the site in November 1947. The Somnath Trust was established; K. M. Munshi became a principal organizer. The project was financed through public contributions rather than direct state construction funds. Traditional Sompura builders erected the present temple.

On 11 May 1951, President Rajendra Prasad conducted or presided over the consecration of a **new linga** in the reconstructed sanctuary. The present cult object is therefore demonstrably a twentieth-century installation embodying an ancient sacred title. Claims that it is physically the linga broken in 1026 are unsupported.

#### Current control

The Shree Somnath Trust controls the modern shrine. As of 2026, India’s prime minister serves as the Trust’s chair. Government commemorations mark both the 1026 raid and the 1951 consecration, making the present linga simultaneously a cult image and a symbol in Indian public memory.

### 3.4 Kashi Vishvanath: loss, claimed concealment, and replacement

A Vishveśvara/Vishvanath linga at Varanasi is textually and ritually old, but the present stone cannot be carried through a continuous documented chain from antiquity.

A Mughal order dated 9 April 1669 directed demolition of major temples or schools; Persian chronicles report the destruction of the Vishvanath temple. The Gyanvapi mosque incorporated earlier architectural fabric. The precise fate of that temple’s principal linga is unknown.

A widely repeated tradition states that a priest hid the old linga in the **Gyan Vapi**, the “Well of Knowledge,” sometimes by leaping into it with the object. No documented contemporary recovery, examination, or scientific identification establishes that the old linga remains in the well.

Ahilyabai Holkar constructed the present Kashi Vishvanath temple in the eighteenth century and installed a linga there. This is a replacement installation carrying the Vishvanath identity. Later Sikh and Hindu patrons enriched its architecture and endowments.

Modern litigation over the neighboring Gyanvapi complex concerns architecture, worship rights, and the identity of a stone form found during a court-directed survey. Hindu claimants have called that form a linga; mosque representatives have called it part of a fountain. This disputed object is not automatically identical either with the seventeenth-century Vishvanath linga or with the linga now worshipped in Ahilyabai’s temple. Those are three separate identity questions.

### 3.5 Khmer royal lingas

Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscriptions record named lingas endowed with land, labor, vessels, livestock, and ritual personnel. Royal and divine names were combined: a king or donor could establish a linga whose name joined a personal element to **-īśvara**, creating an enduring ritual relation without necessarily asserting that the stone was a portrait.

The Sdok Kak Thom inscription, K.235, dated 1053, retrospectively narrates a ritual instituted for Jayavarman II by the Brahmin Hiraṇyadāman and transmitted in Śivakaivalya’s priestly lineage. Older scholarship identified the **devarāja** directly with a royal linga transported with the court. More recent analysis warns that the inscription describes rites and a divine institution but does not securely identify one surviving stone as “the devarāja linga.”

Therefore:

- **Documented:** named gods, priestly offices, royal patronage, property, and ritual transmission.
- **Probable:** lingas were central to numerous royal Shaiva foundations.
- **Disputed:** that one portable stone was the empire’s continuous palladium.
- **Unidentified:** no surviving museum linga can presently be authenticated as the unique devarāja object of Jayavarman II.

At Vat Phou in Laos, Phou Kao mountain itself was understood as **Liṅgaparvata**, a naturally manifest linga landscape. Water from a spring was directed through the sanctuary to bathe the cult image. Here the “object” includes mountain, waterworks, temple, and installed icon rather than an easily transferable stone.

At Mỹ Sơn in Vietnam, Cham rulers installed named lingas associating Shiva with royal founders. Warfare, weathering, reuse, excavation, looting, and twentieth-century bombing destroyed or displaced much of the sanctuary. UNESCO documentation records that Temple B1 was largely destroyed by bombing. Surviving pedestals and fragments cannot all be matched to the named lingas in inscriptions.

### 3.6 Museum acquisitions and colonial transfer

Lingas entered European and American collections through:

- East India Company officers’ collecting;
- antiquarian excavation;
- removal from ruined or abandoned sites;
- purchase from dealers;
- gifts from private collections;
- undocumented movement from South and Southeast Asia.

The British Museum’s Stuart/Bridge linga has a documented British chain after collection but no source-shrine documentation. The Met’s Thai sandstone linga has a recorded 1992 donor and collection association but no published excavation context. The absence of early provenance is evidence of a gap, not proof of lawful export or proof of theft.

Museum accession deconsecrates an object institutionally in the museum’s understanding, but it does not necessarily extinguish religious recognition among viewers. Conversely, an archaeologically linga-shaped object is not known to remain ritually consecrated merely because it is catalogued as a linga.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 There is no single “authentic linga”

A newly carved and properly consecrated linga can be entirely authentic as a cult object without being ancient. Antiquity, original fabric, iconographic classification, legal title, and ritual efficacy are separate questions.

Traditional authority to authenticate has included:

- revelation or sacred geography for *svayambhū* forms;
- priests and initiatory lineages;
- Āgamic specialists conducting installation;
- royal patrons and temple assemblies;
- hereditary trustees or monastic authorities;
- archaeological departments;
- courts deciding property or worship rights;
- museums assigning date and provenance;
- scientific laboratories characterizing material.

These authorities answer different questions and need not agree.

### 4.2 Gudimallam’s identity

**Strong case:** the explicitly phallic shaft, early male relief with later-Shiva attributes, continuing shrine identity, and archaeological enclosure make it a very early Shiva linga.

**Reservations:** no inscription names the figure; dating is disputed; some attributes do not match later canonical Shiva; and the stratigraphic relationship between sculpture and earliest occupation is not conclusive.

The object is genuine ancient stonework and not a modern forgery. The dispute concerns date and iconographic stage, not whether the visible artifact exists.

### 4.3 Indus claimants

No Indus candidate meets the same evidentiary standard. Shape alone cannot distinguish a cult linga from a pestle, gaming object, weight, architectural fitting, or object of unknown function. Neither Shiva’s name nor a deciphered ritual context accompanies them. Their identification remains conjectural.

### 4.4 Jyotirlinga continuity

The twelve-site lists occur in later Sanskrit religious literature and differ in some details and geographical identifications. Even when a shrine’s sacred identity is old, its present material linga may be:

- a documented replacement;
- a reconstruction after destruction;
- one of two local claimants;
- an older stone reset in later architecture;
- a naturally occurring feature whose outline changes seasonally;
- inaccessible to scientific examination.

The authenticity claim is typically one of sacred manifestation and cult continuity, not a museum claim that the stone has remained physically untouched since a datable ancient manufacture.

### 4.5 Somnath fragments and Ghazni’s “Gates”

No authenticated piece of the linga destroyed by Mahmud has been recovered. In 1842, Governor-General Lord Ellenborough ordered wooden doors from Mahmud’s tomb at Ghazni transported to India as the alleged “Gates of Somnath.” Material and stylistic scrutiny showed them to be Islamic workmanship and not the ancient temple gates. Their ceremonial “restoration” was an officially promoted colonial fiction, not recovery of part of the linga or even a demonstrated part of Somnath temple.

### 4.6 Amarnath ice formation

The Amarnath cave in Kashmir contains a seasonally forming ice stalagmite revered as a *svayambhū* Shiva linga. Its existence and seasonal physical formation are observable; claims about divine manifestation belong to living religion. Variations in size do not establish fraud, and natural formation does not negate sacred identity. Stories assigning discovery to a particular shepherd or remote antiquity vary and lack a single securely documented origin.

### 4.7 Marketed “Narmada lingams”

Naturally rounded Narmada cobbles are gathered, polished, traded, and installed worldwide. Sellers frequently describe them as a unique “cryptocrystalline quartz,” meteorite, egg of the cosmos, or scientifically powerful source of electromagnetic energy. The commercial objects are geologically variable river stones; universal mineral-composition claims require testing specimen by specimen. No credible evidence establishes advertised healing or electromagnetic powers. Their ritual status may nevertheless be genuine within a practitioner’s tradition.

### 4.8 Scientific examination

Unlike a biological relic, a linga generally offers no useful DNA test. Relevant methods include:

- petrography and quarry-source comparison;
- tool-mark analysis;
- residue analysis;
- stratigraphic excavation;
- thermoluminescence for associated fired material;
- radiocarbon dating of associated organic deposits, not stone manufacture;
- inscriptional palaeography;
- archival provenance research;
- three-dimensional recording of erosion and damage.

No comprehensive scientific census exists. The most famous active lingas are often inaccessible to invasive examination, while excavated and museum pieces frequently lack secure find context.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

| Subject | Connection | Date/evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Shiva / Śiva / Rudra / Maheśvara** | God represented, manifested, or ritually present in the linga | Archaeological and textual evidence secure by the early centuries CE |
| **Pārvatī / Umā / Śakti** | Associated with the pedestal and the receptive/generative divine principle | Āgamic and Purāṇic theology; dates vary by text |
| **Brahmā** | Searches upward for the top of the limitless pillar; associated with the square lower section in later iconography | Purāṇic and Āgamic narratives |
| **Viṣṇu** | Searches downward as Varāha; associated with the octagonal middle section | Purāṇic and Āgamic narratives |

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Consecration as the object’s ritual “birth”

An artisan-made stone is not necessarily a functioning deity merely because it has a linga profile. Āgamic installation may include:

- selection and testing of material;
- carving to prescribed proportions;
- preparation of foundation deposits;
- installation in the pedestal;
- ritual purification;
- mantras and fire offerings;
- *nyāsa*, placing divine powers into the image;
- opening or activation rites;
- **prāṇa-pratiṣṭhā**, establishment of living presence;
- assignment of priests, land, revenue, vessels, lamps, and service schedules.

The legal owner may possess the stone, but the installed deity can be treated juridically in India as a legal person represented by a guardian or manager. This makes removal, sale, or replacement more than ordinary movement of property.

### 6.2 Daily worship

The central act is **abhiṣeka**, bathing or anointing. Substances may include:

- water;
- milk and yogurt;
- clarified butter;
- honey or sugar solutions;
- coconut water;
- sandal paste;
- sacred ash;
- flowers and bilva leaves.

The liquid flows across the shaft into the pedestal and out through its channel. Mantras, commonly including forms of the *Śatarudrīya*, accompany bathing. The linga may then be dried, marked, dressed, crowned, masked, garlanded, and presented with lamps, incense, food, music, and circumambulation.

This ritual produces physical change: polish, mineral film, staining, surface loss, fractures from temperature change, repair cement, and wear around the pedestal. Conservation and active worship can conflict when museum standards would prohibit liquids but liturgy requires them.

### 6.3 Access and taboos

Rules vary radically:

- some temples allow devotees to touch and bathe the linga;
- others permit only initiated or hereditary priests to enter the innermost sanctuary;
- some expose the object only during specified hours;
- certain lingas are normally concealed under silver or gold covers;
- pilgrimage crowds may view only from a distance;
- photography is often prohibited;
- damaged or ritually compromised icons may require re-consecration, burial, immersion, or replacement.

There is no universal caste or gender rule applicable to all linga shrines. Local access regimes must be documented shrine by shrine.

### 6.4 Temporary lingas

A linga may be made for one ceremony from clay, sand, rice, butter, flowers, ash, or other substances. Its dissolution or immersion is completion, not vandalism. Such objects demonstrate why survival bias distorts archaeological history: permanent stone specimens represent only part of actual practice.

### 6.5 Festivals

**Mahāśivarātri** is the principal pan-regional festival associated with nightlong worship, fasting, recitation, and repeated ablution. Later traditions connect the night with Shiva’s marriage, cosmic dance, or manifestation as the limitless linga; these explanations coexist.

Individual linga shrines possess their own annual processions and bathing festivals. In many temples the immovable linga remains in the sanctum while a portable anthropomorphic image is carried publicly. Thus the linga may be the central divine presence without being physically moved.

### 6.6 Political legitimation

Installing a named linga could:

- mark conquest or foundation;
- associate a king with Shiva;
- claim territory by creating a sacred centre;
- memorialize an ancestor or teacher;
- receive tax-free land and dependent labor;
- stabilize a royal priesthood;
- present rebuilding after iconoclasm as restoration of sovereignty.

Khmer royal lingas and Somnath’s 1951 installation are especially clear cases. In neither should symbolism be confused with proof that the stone itself was an ancient hereditary crown-like palladium.

### 6.7 Funerary and memorial use

Named **-īśvara** lingas could preserve the name or merit of patrons, teachers, and rulers. The Mathura installation of Upamiteśvara and Kapileśvara links sacred objects to a teacher lineage. This is not necessarily worship of the dead person as Shiva; it creates a ritualized bond between person, deity, institution, and place.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The limitless pillar

**Tier:** early medieval textual tradition with earlier possible strata; securely represented in Purāṇic, Āgamic, Tamil hymn, and temple-art traditions.

Brahmā and Viṣṇu dispute supremacy. An endless fiery column appears. Brahmā assumes avian form and flies upward; Viṣṇu takes boar form and burrows downward. Neither finds an end. In some versions Brahmā falsely claims success with the ketakī flower as witness; Shiva condemns the lie and restricts Brahmā’s worship. Vishnu admits failure and is honored. Shiva appears from the column.

The story explains:

- the linga as immeasurable divinity rather than merely anatomy;
- Shiva’s supremacy over Brahmā and Vishnu;
- vertical temple symbolism;
- the placement of *liṅgodbhava* reliefs on the rear wall of South Indian sanctums.

The Metropolitan Museum’s late twelfth- or thirteenth-century Tamil granite relief preserves the mature iconography: Shiva emerges in the pillar while Brahmā flies and boar-headed Vishnu descends.

### 7.2 The Daru-forest episode

**Tier:** Purāṇic and sectarian narrative with significantly varying recensions.

Shiva enters the sages’ forest as a provocative ascetic, often naked and erotically compelling. The sages or their wives respond; curses or ritual attacks cause his phallus to fall or become a destructive force. The cosmos is endangered until the linga is stabilized in the goddess’s yoni and worshipped.

Versions differ on:

- whether Shiva is testing the sages;
- whether the sages recognize him;
- who issues the curse;
- whether the phallus falls, grows, burns, or becomes infinite;
- whether Pārvatī, the gods, or ritual installation contains it;
- whether the tale criticizes sterile ritualism, affirms sexuality, or subordinates both to Shiva.

Online retellings often splice episodes from different Purāṇas into a synthetic “ancient story.” Claims should be checked against a named recension and chapter.

### 7.3 The twelve jyotirlingas

**Tier:** Purāṇic and pilgrimage tradition, geographically elaborated over time.

A celebrated hymn names twelve sites commonly identified today as Somnath, Mallikarjuna, Mahakaleshwar, Omkareshwar, Kedarnath, Bhimashankar, Vishvanath, Trimbakeshwar, Vaidyanath, Nageshwar, Rameshwaram, and Grishneshwar. Variant readings and competing local identifications exist, especially for Vaidyanath, Bhimashankar, and Nageshwar.

The list constructs a sacred geography. It does not provide archaeological manufacture dates for twelve extant stones, nor does it say that they were cut from one pillar.

### 7.4 Rāvaṇa and the ātma-liṅga

**Tier:** regional and later textual traditions.

Rāvaṇa performs austerities and receives Shiva’s own linga, conditional on never setting it down before reaching Lanka. The gods fear its power. Gaṇeśa, disguised as a boy, takes temporary custody and places it on the ground at Gokarna when Rāvaṇa fails to return promptly. The linga becomes immovable. Rāvaṇa’s efforts to pull it free damage it or scatter associated pieces, explaining a network of regional shrines.

No physical examination can establish custody by Rāvaṇa. The legend supplies Gokarna with a divine provenance and binds surrounding temples into one sacred landscape.

### 7.5 Somnath and the Moon

**Tier:** Purāṇic/temple tradition.

Dakṣa curses Soma, the Moon, for favoring one wife over his other daughters. Soma worships Shiva at Prabhas; Shiva alleviates the curse, producing the Moon’s waxing and waning. Soma establishes Somnath, “Lord of Soma.” Later temple tradition adds successive temples made of gold by Soma, silver by Rāvaṇa, sandalwood by Krishna, and stone by historical rulers. These material sequences are legendary and do not correspond to an excavated inventory.

### 7.6 The Amarnath secret

**Tier:** later pilgrimage legend.

Shiva chooses the cave to reveal immortality’s secret to Pārvatī, abandoning companions and ornaments along the route. A pair of pigeons overhear and become immortal. Pilgrims still report pigeons as signs of the story. The narrative maps stations in the journey but is not evidence for the physical age of the ice formation.

### 7.7 Colonial and modern legends

Modern claims include:

- lingas were prehistoric nuclear reactors;
- the form encodes advanced electricity or atomic science;
- all lingas are meteorites;
- Narmada stones emit scientifically measured healing energy;
- the Somnath linga floated through magnetism;
- every Indus rounded stone is a Shiva linga;
- the object is only an abstract “cosmic egg” and never phallic;
- conversely, it is only a sexual organ and has no other indigenous meaning.

None is an adequate historical account. Some recycle medieval marvel tales or nineteenth-century polemic; others arose through modern nationalist, occult, New Age, or commercial writing.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 The class as a whole

Shiva lingas are currently:

- actively worshipped in temples and homes across South Asia and the global Hindu diaspora;
- formed temporarily in festivals;
- gathered from rivers;
- installed in new temples;
- preserved in archaeological sites;
- held in museum stores and galleries;
- circulating in international religious and antiquities markets;
- represented in disputed sacred spaces.

There is no central registry and no single custodian.

### 8.2 Gudimallam

The linga remains *in situ* in the Paraśurāmeśvara temple at Gudimallam and in active worship. The Archaeological Survey of India protects the monument. Its ritual use restricts the forms of examination and conservation that would be possible for an unvenerated museum object.

### 8.3 Somnath

The principal object currently worshipped at the Shree Somnath temple is the linga consecrated in 1951. It is controlled by the Shree Somnath Trust and remains a major public pilgrimage object. The eighteenth-century Ahilyabai shrine provides a separate ritual locus nearby. The pre-1026 linga is destroyed or lost; no authenticated fragment is known.

### 8.4 Kashi Vishvanath and Gyanvapi

The present Kashi Vishvanath linga is actively worshipped in Ahilyabai Holkar’s eighteenth-century temple under a statutory administrative regime. The alleged older linga in the Gyan Vapi remains an unverified tradition. The structure identified by Hindu litigants as a linga in the adjacent mosque complex remains under legal and physical restrictions. It should not be represented as conclusively authenticated while litigation and expert disagreement continue.

### 8.5 Amarnath

The ice formation appears seasonally in the Amarnath cave and remains a highly controlled pilgrimage destination. Its condition depends upon temperature, water flow, and human pressure. It is a recurring natural formation vested with continuing sacred identity, not one permanent stone.

### 8.6 Museum examples

- **National Museum, New Delhi 65-172:** documented stone caturmukhaliṅga from Mathura, displayed or held in the national collection.
- **Metropolitan Museum 1992.150.3:** southern Thai sandstone linga, on display.
- **Met Afghan ekamukhaliṅga:** ninth-century white marble, on display.
- **British Museum 1872,0701.119:** eastern Indian sandstone linga, colonial-period provenance documented only after Charles Stuart’s collection.
- Numerous additional pieces remain in Indian state museums, site museums, university collections, and foreign institutions.

### 8.7 Legal and repatriation context

No universal repatriation claim attaches to the whole class. Individual lingas may, however, be subject to:

- temple-property claims;
- illicit-export investigations;
- claims under Indian or Southeast Asian antiquities laws;
- community requests concerning sacred handling;
- disputes over colonial removal;
- museum provenance review.

Cambodia’s recent recovery of sculptures trafficked during war demonstrates the scrutiny now applied to undocumented Khmer sacred art, although the prominently publicized returns have not all been lingas. A museum label stating only “Cambodia” or “Thailand; private collection” is not a complete provenance.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Colonial interpretation

European missionaries and administrators frequently treated the linga as proof of “obscene” or “degraded” religion. Other Orientalists spiritualized it into an entirely abstract emblem. Both approaches often selected one indigenous strand while suppressing others.

Charles Stuart, sometimes called “Hindoo Stuart,” collected Hindu sculpture while serving the East India Company. His British Museum linga illustrates a different colonial engagement: admiration and collection still embedded within unequal imperial access and undocumented removal.

### 9.2 Indian reform and apologetics

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century Hindu interpreters frequently emphasized *liṅga* as “sign,” cosmic pillar, or formless absolute in response to missionary attacks. Those meanings are textually legitimate, but categorical denial of the form’s phallic history is apologetic overcorrection.

### 9.3 Nationalism and Somnath

Somnath’s reconstruction became a major symbol of post-independence civilizational restoration. Jawaharlal Nehru was concerned about official identification of the secular state with the project; Patel, Munshi, and Rajendra Prasad presented reconstruction as recovery after conquest. Private funding was politically important.

The 1951 linga thus acquired a precisely documented modern career:

- replacement cult image;
- symbol of independence;
- site of debate over secular government;
- emblem of Hindu historical injury and recovery;
- continuing pilgrimage focus.

In 2026 the Government of India’s **Somnath Swabhiman Parv** explicitly commemorated one thousand years since the 1026 raid and seventy-five years since the 1951 consecration. Its official language is national and civilizational, not neutral archaeological description.

### 9.4 Art, museums, and popular culture

Modern art reproduces the linga as:

- a temple icon;
- abstract sculpture;
- erotic sign;
- cosmic axis;
- nationalist emblem;
- meditation object;
- commercial décor.

The *liṅgodbhava* story appears in film, devotional television, calendar art, comics, and digital animation. These frequently add flames, galaxies, anatomically stylized yoni imagery, or explicit Trinitarian diagrams not present in the earliest surviving objects.

### 9.5 Global commerce

“Narmada Shiva lingams” are sold internationally as religious articles and New Age crystals. Machine polishing, standardized sizing, and mass distribution create a modern commodity chain:

river collection → wholesaler → polishing workshop → exporter → spiritual retailer → household shrine or decorative market.

Commercial descriptions often conflate geological fact, Hindu tradition, tantra, chakra healing, and invented scientific language. An object may be a genuine Narmada cobble and sincerely consecrated while its seller’s mineralogical or medical claims remain false.

### 9.6 Iconoclasm and political media

Images of damaged lingas are routinely circulated without find context and assigned to particular conquerors. Verification requires a site report, inscription, excavation context, or contemporary account. Conversely, documented destruction should not be euphemized: cult images at Somnath and numerous other temples were deliberately broken during military and religious campaigns.

Modern political narratives may:

- collapse several replacement lingas into one immortal object;
- multiply uncertain destruction episodes;
- deny well-documented iconoclasm;
- attribute every damaged sculpture to religious attack when weathering, reuse, theft, or accidental loss is possible.

The object’s history is repeatedly used to argue present political claims, making evidentiary separation especially important.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Principal questions

Scholarship has revolved around six problems:

1. Does the linga cult descend from the Indus civilization?
2. How early is Gudimallam?
3. Was the earliest linga primarily phallic, aniconic, anthropomorphic, or some combination?
4. When did the limitless-pillar theology attach to the material form?
5. How did named lingas organize kingship and institutions?
6. How should sacred continuity be discussed when the physical stone was replaced?

### 10.2 Early iconographic scholarship

T. A. Gopinatha Rao assembled Sanskrit textual prescriptions and archaeological examples in *Elements of Hindu Iconography*. His work remains fundamental for terminology, measurements, and early documentation, although individual dates and evolutionary schemes have been revised.

Older accounts tended to arrange forms into a linear progression: natural phallus → conventional pillar → symbolic abstraction. The actual record is less orderly. Natural stones, realistic shafts, abstract pillars, anthropomorphic faces, and composite forms overlapped.

### 10.3 Gudimallam debate

Sarma’s excavation transformed understanding by exposing the pedestal, railing, and concealed lower relief. His early date became widely repeated.

Later criticism focused on:

- whether associated pottery dates the sculpture;
- whether medieval builders cut earlier deposits when installing or resetting it;
- whether its style fits the proposed date;
- whether the relief represents a local deity being assimilated to Shiva;
- whether the icon is evidence of already established linga worship or of a transitional attempt to identify a phallic object by adding a divine figure.

The defensible consensus is importance and antiquity, not a universally agreed exact year.

### 10.4 Indus continuity debate

Marshall’s “proto-Shiva” and phallic-stone interpretations exerted enormous influence. Srinivasan’s 1984 critique showed that resemblance, especially selected resemblance, is insufficient to bridge long chronological and iconographic gaps. Indus religion remains undeciphered; Shaiva identification is possible speculation, not demonstrated provenance.

A discredited popular method proceeds backwards: because later Hindus worship lingas, every earlier rounded or pillar-like stone must be one. Archaeology requires contextual evidence independent of the desired continuity.

### 10.5 Phallic versus symbolic readings

The dispute has often been falsely framed as a choice:

- Western sexual misreading, or
- purely indigenous abstraction.

Early objects such as Gudimallam possess unmistakable anatomical form. Sanskrit narratives also speak of Shiva’s generative organ. Other Sanskrit sources explicitly define the linga as sign, cosmic support, subtle principle, or infinite radiance. Ritual practitioners may foreground neither anatomy nor philosophical abstraction but Shiva’s immediate presence.

The historical object therefore cannot be reduced to one interpretation valid for every region and century.

### 10.6 Devarāja debate

Georges Cœdès and related scholarship popularized the image of a Khmer “god-king” cult centered on a royal linga. Subsequent scholars questioned whether **devarāja** means “god-king,” “king of gods,” a ritual, a deity, or an institution, and whether any single transferable linga embodied it.

The K.235 inscription remains primary evidence, but it was carved in 1053 to memorialize and legitimate a priestly lineage while describing events approximately two centuries earlier. Its retrospective and interested character must be retained in any reconstruction.

### 10.7 Primary-evidence limitations

Major limitations include:

- composite religious texts with uncertain redaction dates;
- inscriptions naming a deity but not describing the stone;
- active sanctums inaccessible to excavation;
- excavated objects lacking original labels;
- museum objects without findspot or export documentation;
- destruction and rebuilding that erase old contexts;
- ritual renewal interpreted as either continuity or replacement depending on disciplinary assumptions;
- colonial publications that call locally known objects “discoveries”;
- modern websites presenting temple tradition as exact chronology.

### 10.8 Needed research

The field would benefit from:

- publication of complete Gudimallam stratigraphic records and conservation documentation;
- non-invasive petrography and high-resolution scanning;
- a concordance of early linga inscriptions and securely excavated specimens;
- object-level provenance audits in foreign collections;
- scientific characterization of Narmada trade specimens;
- careful mapping of variant jyotirlinga lists by manuscript date;
- legal-historical distinction between deity identity and material continuity;
- documentation of surface change caused by continuing abhiṣeka.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and near-primary textual sources

- **Electronic Mahābhārata, Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute critical text.** Useful for locating *liṅga* passages without assuming that every later vulgate passage belongs to the critical text.
- **Liṅga Purāṇa Sanskrit text, GRETIL.** A searchable Sanskrit recension documenting ritual, theology, material classifications, and narratives. Its online metadata reflects the source edition, not a claim that the Purāṇa was composed in a single period.
- **Śivadharma Purāṇa, chapter 3.** Provides the account of the non-material cosmic linga and terrestrial imitations of differing substances. The web presentation is recent; the underlying Sanskrit tradition is medieval.
- **Sdok Kak Thom inscription K.235.** Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscription dated 1053; indispensable but retrospective and institutionally interested.
- **Gupta-period inscriptions.** Records of Vīrasena’s Shaiva dedication and the Mathura teacher-lineage installations provide firmer chronological anchors than undated myths.

### Archaeological and museum sources

- **Archaeological Survey of India/IGNCA, “Gudimallam Linga,” in *Indian Archaeology Today*.** Excavation summary, pedestal, railing, associated pottery, and early-date interpretation.
- **T. A. Gopinatha Rao, *Elements of Hindu Iconography*.** Historic synthesis of Āgamic measurements and material examples.
- **National Museum, New Delhi, accession 65-172.** Official catalogue for the Mathura caturmukhaliṅga.
- **Metropolitan Museum of Art collection records.** Object measurements, dates, materials, accession numbers, display status, and immediate provenance.
- **British Museum collection and exhibition records.** Colonial collection chain for the Stuart/Bridge linga.
- **UNESCO nomination dossiers for Vat Phou and Mỹ Sơn.** Landscape, architectural, religious, and damage history.

### Major interpretive scholarship

- **Doris Srinivasan, “Unhinging Śiva from the Indus Civilization,” 1984.** A foundational critique of attempts to identify Indus artifacts as direct Shiva prototypes.
- **I. K. Sarma’s Gudimallam research.** Supports the early chronology and reconstruction of architectural phases; its conclusions remain debated.
- **Recent EFEO reassessment of devarāja.** Reconsiders older “god-king linga” formulations through the inscriptional evidence.

### Source-quality warning

Official temple and government pages are authoritative for current administration, institutional tradition, and modern ceremonies. They are not automatically critical authorities for events placed in mythic time. Museum catalogues reliably document accession and current condition but may reproduce older stylistic dates and may leave pre-market provenance blank. Religious texts document the history of claims and narratives; they are not by themselves excavation reports.
