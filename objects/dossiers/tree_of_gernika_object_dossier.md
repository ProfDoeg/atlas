# The Tree of Gernika: Object Dossier

**Proposed filename:** `the_tree_of_gernika_object_dossier.md`  
**Research cut-off:** 6 September 2026

## Scope and evidentiary note

“The Tree of Gernika” is not one continuously living organism. It is a civic object constituted by:

1. a succession of pedunculate oaks planted at the same ceremonial site;
2. the preserved trunk and detached fragments of earlier trees;
3. the adjoining oath-tribune and Assembly House, which make the tree usable as a political object;
4. a claimed biological lineage maintained through acorns and seedlings;
5. an institutional identity that treats successive trees as continuations of one symbol.

The dossier therefore distinguishes the **individual organisms** from the **serial civic object**. The present oak is biologically a descendant, not the same organism as the medieval or early-modern trees. Institutional language nevertheless speaks of a continuous *Árbol de Gernika*. No published DNA study proving an unbroken pedigree was located. “Descendant” normally means that the seedling was raised from an acorn attributed to an earlier official tree and certified through the nursery and custody practices of the provincial institutions.

Evidence labels used below:

- **Documented:** supported by a contemporary record, manuscript, official institutional record, catalogue, or closely sourced historical study.
- **Scientific/technical finding:** based on botanical, pathological, or conservation examination.
- **Institutional tradition:** a claim presently maintained by the Juntas Generales or Basque public institutions, sometimes without publication of the underlying primary documentation.
- **Scholarly inference:** a reasoned reconstruction rather than a directly recorded event.
- **Traditional or legendary:** culturally important but not established as literal history.
- **Disputed:** incompatible versions survive.
- **Unverified:** repeated in secondary literature but not confirmed in the sources examined.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Identity and object class

The Tree of Gernika is a **living civic monument, oath-tree, assembly-tree, territorial emblem, and serial biological object** at the Casa de Juntas de Gernika, Gernika-Lumo, Bizkaia, in the Basque Autonomous Community of Spain. It stands outside the chamber of the Juntas Generales de Bizkaia, beside the neoclassical oath-tribune and opposite a circular pavilion containing the principal surviving trunk of an earlier oak.

Its primary institutional function developed from the practice of holding the General Assemblies of the Lordship of Biscay beneath or beside an oak. The Lord of Biscay was required to confirm the territory’s usages, liberties, and fueros in the Gernika assembly setting. Since 1936, the place has also served for the oath or promise of office of the *lehendakari*, president of the Basque Government.

The Juntas Generales call the oak “the heart of the Lordship” and present the organism planted on 2 March 2015 as the current continuation of the old tree’s lineage. Their own account expressly acknowledges that the present tree is a successor and that the preserved “Old Tree” was itself not the first Tree of Gernika. [Juntas Generales de Bizkaia](https://jjggbizkaia.eus/es/sedes/arbol-de-gernika)

### 1.2 Names

- **Basque:** *Gernikako Arbola*, literally “the Tree of Gernika.”  
  - *Gernikako* is the relational/genitival form of *Gernika*: “of Gernika.”
  - *arbola* means “tree.” The synonymous *zuhaitz* also occurs, especially in modern oath formulas as *Gernikako zuhaitzpean*, “under the tree of Gernika.”
- **Spanish:** *Árbol de Gernika*; historically and under older Spanish orthography, *Árbol de Guernica*.
- **French:** *Arbre de Gernika* or *Arbre de Guernica*.
- **English:** *Tree of Gernika*, *Tree of Guernica*, or *Oak of Guernica*.
- **Functional titles:** *Árbol Foral* (“Foral Tree”), *roble foral* (“foral oak”), and *árbol juradero* or oath-tree.
- **Names of particular generations:** *Árbol Padre* (“Father Tree”), *Árbol Viejo* / *Aritz Zaharra* (“Old Tree”), *Árbol Hijo* (“Child Tree”), and, retrospectively, *Árbol Breve* (“Brief Tree”) for the short-lived official specimen of 2005–2015.

The spelling **Guernica** represents the older Spanish convention in which *gu* before *e* gives a hard /g/. **Gernika** is the present official Basque spelling. The change in spelling does not identify a different object.

The spelling *arbola*, rather than standard modern Basque *arbola* being translated or modernized away, has been stabilized by José María Iparragirre’s 1853 song, originally printed in nineteenth-century spellings such as *Guernicaco arbola*. The song’s title consequently became almost inseparable from the object’s public name. A scholarly study of the anthem documents its textual and musical variants. [Xabier Zabaltza, “Gernikako Arbola, un himno huérfano”](https://ojs.ehu.eus/index.php/HC/article/download/17583/15411/65205)

### 1.3 Species and material

The documented preserved trees and official successors are identified as **pedunculate or English oak, *Quercus robur* L.** The physical material is therefore living or preserved oak wood, bark, roots, foliage, acorns, and reproductive descendants.

No inscription, maker’s mark, or artificial decoration belongs to the living trunk itself. The civic presentation of the tree, however, includes:

- a planted bed or parterre;
- a metal railing on a stone base;
- a neoclassical stone oath-tribune;
- a circular, seven-columned pavilion protecting the principal remnant of the Old Tree;
- plaques and heraldic representations;
- the adjacent Casa de Juntas.

The geoEuskadi survey gives the site coordinates as approximately **UTM 30N ETRS89 X 525952, Y 4795626**. It describes the oath-tribune as a stone structure with frontal and lateral columns, an entablature, a pediment bearing the arms of Bizkaia, stone seats, and attached rear columns. [geoEuskadi object sheet](https://www.geo.euskadi.eus/cartografia/DatosDescarga/Documentacion/Geocuriosidades/Castellano/48-27%20El%20%C3%A1rbol%20de%20Gernika.pdf)

### 1.4 Enumerated physical generations

Because dates vary among sources, the following table separates reasonably secure events from institutional shorthand.

| Designation | Approximate life or official tenure | Physical status |
|---|---:|---|
| Predecessor or primordial assembly oak(s) | Before the securely surviving fifteenth-century texts | No known physical remains securely identified |
| *Árbol Padre* | Traditionally medieval; replacement discussed in 1564; usually said to have ceased as the principal tree in 1742 | Lost; exact dates disputed |
| *Árbol Viejo* / *Aritz Zaharra* | Traditionally planted or designated in 1742; gradually superseded in the nineteenth century; died in 1892 | Main trunk displayed in a pavilion; catalogued fragment at the Museo Vasco |
| *Árbol Hijo* / *Árbol Nuevo* | Planted or set between 1858 and 1861; official sources conventionally use 1860; declared clinically dead in April 2004 | Removed; at least one catalogued fragment survives |
| 2005 successor / *Árbol Breve* | Born in 1986 according to press accounts; planted 25 February 2005; declared dead 15 January 2015 | Removed; institutional plans called for part of the trunk to be preserved |
| Present tree | Germinated from the 2000 acorn crop; planted 2 March 2015 | Living at the Casa de Juntas as of the latest official references located |

### 1.5 Preserved Old Tree

The most prominent surviving historical material is the trunk of the *Árbol Viejo*, displayed upright under the circular pavilion in the garden. The public institution sometimes calls it “fossilized,” but this should not be taken as a geological description. It is preserved wood, not necessarily wood mineralized into a true fossil.

A separate fragment is catalogued by the Museo Vasco de Bilbao:

- **Object:** fragment of the trunk of the Old Tree, including the beginnings of principal branches;
- **Species/material:** *Quercus robur*, oak wood;
- **Collection:** Cultural Anthropology;
- **Inventory number:** **AAAA/5120**;
- **Dimensions:** height 150 cm; diameter 60 cm;
- **Catalogue date:** 1742–1892;
- **Provenance:** Casa de Juntas de Gernika.

The museum catalogue calls this the second tree “under scholarly control,” while also conceding a presumed predecessor from the fourteenth to sixteenth century. [Museotik catalogue, AAAA/5120](https://museotik.euskadi.eus/titulo-fragmento-del-arbol-de-gernika-arbol-viejo-/objeto-tronco-/museotik-ca-192370/webmus00-contenedor/es/)

### 1.6 The 1858/1860–2004 tree

This oak reached approximately **12 metres in height**. The exact year of installation is inconsistent:

- the Juntas Generales and several official summaries say **1860**;
- a 2015 Juntas press release says it was “fixed” or established in **1858**;
- other reconstructions describe an unsuccessful planting in 1859, followed by replacement in 1861;
- its reported age of 146 years at death in 2004 points to approximately 1858.

This is a genuine documentary inconsistency, probably reflecting the distinction between germination, preliminary planting, transplantation to the place of honour, and the failure or replacement of an initial sapling. “1860” remains the institutional conventional date, but it should not be treated as exact beyond dispute.

A fragment is catalogued by the Museo Vasco:

- **Object:** trunk fragment from the *Árbol Foral* or *Árbol Nuevo*;
- **Species:** *Quercus robur*;
- **Inventory number:** **00/3447**;
- **Provenance:** Casa de Juntas;
- **Official tenure:** from the conventional planting in 1860 to clinical death in April 2004.

[Museotik catalogue for the Árbol Nuevo fragment](https://museotik.euskadi.eus/titulo-fragmento-del-arbol-de-gernika-arbol-nuevo/objeto-tronco-/museotik-ca-183909/webmus00-contenedor/es/)

### 1.7 The 2005–2015 tree

The fourth official organism was reportedly born in 1986 from the previous oak’s acorn crop and transplanted to the ceremonial site on 25 February 2005. It suffered severe root-system failure. A December 2014 examination involving the University of the Basque Country concluded that the branches and interior of the trunk were dead and that degeneration of the root system had prevented water uptake, causing complete desiccation. Excess water, poor drainage, shade, clayey confinement, and root constriction were identified as central factors. It was officially declared at the end of its life cycle on 15 January 2015. [Contemporary report on the technical findings](https://cadenaser.com/emisora/2015/01/15/radio_bilbao/1421321727_782403.html)

### 1.8 Present tree

The present oak was raised from an acorn of the 1858/1860–2004 tree’s crop. It germinated in **2000**, spent its first two years in a Basalan nursery near Bilbao, and then grew in a provincial plot in Arratia. It was a biological sibling of the 2005–2015 tree: both were described as offspring of the same predecessor, not one as the offspring of the other.

It was planted at the Casa de Juntas on **2 March 2015**, after work intended to correct the planting site’s drainage, irrigation, soil, and root-space deficiencies. Its present exact height, crown spread, trunk circumference, and botanical condition were not published in the official materials located. It remains visibly present in official photographs and was the site of ceremonies in 2024 and 2026. [Juntas Generales planting notice](https://jjggbizkaia.eus/fitxategiak/JJGG/deiadarra/gabinete/documentos/210215%20NP%20Arbol%20Gernika.pdf)

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 There was no single manufacture

The object was not commissioned or fabricated in the ordinary sense. Its origins have three layers:

1. the natural life of one or more local oaks;
2. the selection of a particular oak as the place of assembly;
3. the later institutional production of successor trees, preserved trunks, and a monumental setting.

No source located identifies the original acorn, parent stand, planter, cost, or planting ceremony of the first assembly oak.

### 2.2 Earliest documentary horizon

Modern scholarship reports meetings of the General Assemblies at Gernika from the **early fourteenth century**, but the surviving evidence does not permit the easy identification of that assembly tree with any preserved trunk.

A tradition connects an oak at Gernika with the recognition of **Alfonso XI** as Lord of Biscay before the foundation charter of the town in 1366. Later writers placed a primordial tree’s origin around 1334 or earlier. The exact planting year is not established.

The earliest readily verifiable explicit legal formulation examined here is the **Fuero Viejo de Vizcaya**, compiled in 1452 and later confirmed and published. A sixteenth-century manuscript copy, Valladolid University Library MS 040, begins its promulgation formula:

> “So el Arbol de Guernica donde se acostumbra de fazer junta general…”

That is, “Under the Tree of Guernica, where it is customary to hold the General Assembly.” The wording is important: by 1452 the tree was not introduced as a novelty but as the customary assembly place. The manuscript catalogue identifies the text as the Fuero composed in 1452, confirmed in 1457, and publicly issued under the tree in 1463. [Universidad de Valladolid, MS 040 catalogue](https://uvadoc.uva.es/handle/10324/198)

The Fuero’s ritual itinerary required the incoming lord to be received at Aretxabalagana, swear at the church of Saints Emeterius and Celedonius, proceed to Gernika beneath the customary assembly tree to confirm or amend fueros with the agreement of the Biscayans, and continue to Bermeo. This is documented legal-ceremonial prescription, not evidence that every ruler performed every stage personally.

### 2.3 The “Father Tree”

The institutional and popular narrative presents a medieval *Árbol Padre* as the first member of the tracked lineage. Its chronology is unstable:

- one tradition places it in the fourteenth century and gives it roughly 450 years of life;
- some catalogues state that a first controlled tree was planted in **1564** and died in **1811**;
- the more usual succession says the Father Tree ceased in **1742**, when the Old Tree was planted or selected;
- a nineteenth-century account says the penultimate tree, traditionally dating from the mid-fourteenth century, died on **2 February 1811**.

These dates cannot all describe a clean sequence of exclusive official trees. The likely explanation is overlap: a declining older oak could remain standing while a successor grew nearby, and different writers may date “succession” by planting, ceremonial adoption, biological death, or removal. No dendrochronological report resolving the chronology was located.

### 2.4 Deliberate reproduction by 1564

A later historical tradition reports that in **1564** officials considered felling and replacing an old tree and already maintained young trees or shoots for succession. If accurate, this is significant: by the sixteenth century the object was understood not merely as one ancient organism but as a renewable lineage. The practice anticipated the modern provincial nursery system.

### 2.5 The Old Tree of 1742

The *Árbol Viejo* is conventionally dated from **1742**. Its planter, precise parentage, and original nursery are not securely identified in the sources consulted. Its importance lies partly in survival: its trunk is the oldest material now publicly presented as belonging to an official Tree of Gernika.

Claims that the preserved Old Tree is literally the medieval assembly oak are incorrect. The Juntas themselves explicitly state that it is not the first tree. [Juntas Generales institutional history](https://jjggbizkaia.eus/es/sedes/arbol-de-gernika)

### 2.6 Monumentalization of the site

Buildings gradually accumulated around the assembly oak and the hermitage of Santa María la Antigua. In 1826 work began on the current neoclassical complex under architect Antonio de Etxebarria; the Assembly House was completed in the early 1830s. The stone oath-tribune replaced an earlier structure around 1828.

This construction altered the object’s physical context. The tree ceased to be merely an oak in an open assembly landscape and became the centre of a designed parliamentary monument. [Casa de Juntas institutional history](https://jjggbizkaia.eus/es/sedes/casa-de-juntas-de-gernika)

### 2.7 No legendary supernatural origin

Unlike a relic said to have descended from heaven, the tree has no stable ancient supernatural manufacture story. Its sacral language is primarily metaphorical and political. The clearest declaration that God planted or blessed it belongs to the nineteenth-century anthem, not to the medieval Fuero.

Iparragirre’s song says that the tree was “planted by God” and calls it blessed, beloved, holy, and perpetually fruitful. This is poetic theology and political symbolism, not a botanical provenance claim.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 Custody model

The tree was not an heirloom passed through private hands. Its provenance is tied to the political community and institutions of Bizkaia:

- the communities and representatives of the Lordship;
- the General Assemblies;
- the Diputación or provincial government during institutional interruptions;
- the restored Juntas Generales from 1979;
- provincial forestry and nursery personnel responsible for descendants.

The individual organisms remained rooted at the assembly precinct. Their “transfers” were plantings, substitutions, removals, preservation of trunks, and distribution of acorns or seedlings.

### 3.2 Medieval and early-modern custody

**Early fourteenth century onward — documented at the level of site, not organism.** Assemblies are reported at Gernika. The surviving sources do not establish that one biological tree endured throughout this period.

**1452/1463 — securely attested legal object.** The Fuero Viejo identifies the tree as the customary place for the General Assembly. The community of Biscayan representatives, rather than a monarch as private owner, exercised the relevant political control.

**1476 — Ferdinand’s oath and homage.** Ferdinand the Catholic came to Gernika as Lord of Biscay and confirmed the fueros. Historical accounts place him beneath the tree after mass at Santa María la Antigua.

**1483 — Isabella’s personal visit.** Alicia Gould Quincy’s study transcribes documentary evidence for Isabella’s oath in Gernika on 17 September 1483. Older summaries sometimes state that she acted only by proxy, but the documentary case for her personal presence is strong. The ceremony did not transfer the tree to the monarch; it made the ruler’s authority conditional, in the local constitutional understanding, upon an oath at the community’s assembly place. [Alicia Gould Quincy, 1933](https://www.eusko-ikaskuntza.eus/PDFAnlt/riev/24/24654659.pdf)

**Sixteenth–eighteenth centuries — institutional maintenance.** References to contemplated replacement in 1564 and to later successors show the Lordship maintaining a reserve lineage. Documentation is insufficient to reconstruct every horticultural intervention.

### 3.3 French Revolutionary occupation, 1795

During the War of the Pyrenees, French revolutionary troops under General Bon-Adrien Jeannot de Moncey temporarily occupied Biscayan territory. A scholarly reconstruction reports that they visited Gernika and honoured the oak as an “arbre de la liberté,” accompanied by pro-French Basque intermediaries. This is a documented political appropriation, not evidence that the French acquired the tree.

The episode linked an older foral assembly symbol to the Revolutionary liberty-tree tradition. [Luengo Teixidor and Delgado Cendagortagalarza](https://www.cepc.gob.es/sites/default/files/2021-12/26790felixluengoteixidoryinbaloferhyp15.pdf)

### 3.4 Nineteenth-century succession and damage

**1811 — disputed death date.** A nineteenth-century account dates the death of a traditional old tree to 2 February 1811. Museum and institutional chronologies instead preserve an “Old Tree” dated 1742–1892. The discrepancy indicates overlapping trees or inconsistent naming.

**1826–1833 — construction intervention.** The current Assembly House and oath-tribune reorganized the ground around the trees.

**1839 — last royal foral oath in the traditional series.** Regent María Cristina swore or confirmed the fueros for the young Isabella II. Institutional tradition treats this as the last performance of the old monarchical rite.

**1858–1861 — successor planting.** A young oak was installed in front of the oath-tribune while the Old Tree remained. Sources variously give 1858, 1859, 1860, and 1861. One reconstruction states that a two-year-old planted in 1859 died after two years and another was planted in 1861. Official memory simplifies the history to a successful planting in 1860.

**Circa 1864 — physical attack.** Carmelo Echegaray reported that, following the Senate controversy in which Manuel Sánchez Silva attacked the fueros, the recently planted successor was found one night “degollado”—cut through or beheaded. It had to be cut back and replanted or treated to keep it from dying. This is the clearest reported deliberate assault on an official Tree of Gernika before the Civil War. The account is retrospective and should not be inflated into a fully documented criminal case: the attacker, motive, police response, and exact date remain unknown.

**1875 — Carlist ceremonial use.** On 3 July, the Carlist claimant Carlos de Borbón y Austria-Este, styling himself Carlos VII, swore to maintain the Fueros of Biscay under the tree. The ceremony was organized during the Third Carlist War, probably with the involvement of the foralist publicist Arístides de Artiñano. It did not amount to internationally recognized accession to ownership or sovereignty; it was a political-legitimist use of the site.

**1876 — institutional interruption.** The law of 21 July 1876 dismantled the traditional foral regime after the Carlist defeat. The tree remained physically at Gernika but ceased to preside over the old functioning General Assemblies. This was not the tree’s confiscation or destruction. It transformed it from an operative constitutional site into a monument of lost, altered, or disputed liberties.

**1892 — death of the Old Tree.** The Old Tree died or was finally retired. The already established young successor assumed sole ceremonial primacy.

**1926 — preservation in pavilion.** The main surviving Old Tree trunk was installed in the circular pavilion beside the Assembly House. It thereby changed category from dead biological organism to conserved historical exhibit. A separate fragment ultimately entered the Museo Vasco collection as AAAA/5120.

### 3.5 Civil War and bombing

**7 October 1936 — oath of José Antonio Agirre.** Following approval of the Basque autonomy statute, Agirre took office as the first *lehendakari* beneath the oak. His formula invoked God, Basque soil, ancestors, the Tree of Gernika, and the people’s representatives. This use expanded the object’s operative meaning from a specifically Biscayan foral tree to the oath-site of the autonomous Basque executive.

**26 April 1937 — bombing of Gernika.** Aircraft of the German Condor Legion and Italian forces, supporting the insurgent Nationalists, bombed the town. The Assembly House and tree precinct survived, while much of the town was destroyed. The oak’s survival is a documented physical fact; claims that it survived by miracle or because the attackers intentionally protected it are not established.

**Late April 1937 — Carlist guard and alleged Falangist threat.** After insurgent forces occupied Gernika, filmed or photographed requetés guarded the tree. Jaime del Burgo Torres later said that rumours circulated that Falangists intended to cut it down and that he ordered armed requetés of the Tercio de Begoña to surround and protect it.

The evidence supports the presence of a Carlist guard and the political use made of it in Nationalist propaganda. The intended felling rests principally on retrospective testimony and rumour; no identified group of axe-bearing Falangists, order, or attempt has been independently demonstrated. Francoist newsreels used images of the guard, accompanied by *Gernikako Arbola*, to claim that “legitimate,” Catholic, Spanish Vasconia—not the Republic or Basque nationalism—was protecting the historic symbol. The protection episode is thus partly documented action and partly contested narrative.

### 3.6 Franco period

The tree remained at the provincial site under institutions controlled by the Francoist order. It was not abolished as a symbol. Carlist and other regime-aligned writers presented it as a symbol of Catholic, traditional, and Spanish Biscay while rejecting nationalist interpretations. The Assembly institution itself was not restored in its historic form.

In 1964 part of the Assembly House complex was enclosed for museum purposes. In 1985 a large stained-glass roof depicting the tree, fueros, and Biscayan localities was added to the covered patio.

### 3.7 Democratic restoration

**29 December 1978 — Statute drafting.** The Assembly of Basque Parliamentarians held its final session in the Casa de Juntas to complete the proposed autonomy statute. The resulting 1979 law became popularly known as the Statute of Gernika.

**1979 — restoration of the Juntas Generales.** After a 102-year institutional suspension, the General Assemblies of Bizkaia were restored. Custodial and ceremonial responsibility for the tree again belonged to a functioning elected foral parliament.

**1980 — Carlos Garaikoetxea.** Garaikoetxea took the oath as *lehendakari* at the tree, reviving Agirre’s 1936 precedent. Successive elected presidents continued the practice.

### 3.8 Illness, death, removal, and replacement: 1970–2005

The 1858/1860 tree had serious health problems by approximately 1970. From 1992 it received intensive observation and treatment. Reports identified *Armillaria mellea*, a root-rotting honey fungus, together with a foliar parasite. The hot summer of 2003 accelerated its decline. It failed to leaf in spring 2004 and was declared clinically dead on 20 April.

Press accounts described replacement work including:

- removal and replacement of contaminated soil;
- enlargement of the planting space;
- improved drainage;
- preparation of a pit roughly six metres by four metres and two metres deep;
- rejection of an intended replacement that had succumbed to the same fungal problem.

The dead tree was removed, and material was retained. Fragment **00/3447** is catalogued at the Museo Vasco. [Contemporary report on the 2004 death](https://elpais.com/elpais/2004/04/20/actualidad/1082449031_850215.html)

On **25 February 2005**, a 19-year-old or approximately 18–19-year-old descendant was planted at the ceremonial site.

### 3.9 Failure of the 2005 tree and 2015 succession

In summer 2014 the oak deteriorated sharply. Basalan and University of the Basque Country personnel attempted emergency drainage and nutrient treatment. The setting was described as effectively a clay “flowerpot”: shaded, waterlogged, and restrictive to root development.

In December 2014, investigators concluded that root degeneration had prevented water absorption. Winter rains aggravated the waterlogging. On 15 January 2015 the Juntas declared that the tree’s life cycle had ended.

After subsurface work, its sibling from the 2000 acorn crop was moved from Arratia and planted on 2 March 2015. The change was performed as an official parliamentary act. The Juntas framed it not as replacement of the symbol but as continuation of the lineage.

### 3.10 Present custody

The current tree and preserved Old Tree remain under the control of the **Juntas Generales de Bizkaia**, within the Casa de Juntas precinct. Basalan and provincial technical services have been responsible for cultivation and maintenance of the living trees and reserve seedlings.

The latest official material located records ceremonies beside the current tree in July 2026. No current ownership, repatriation, or adverse-possession dispute was found.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Is the present tree “the” medieval tree?

**No, not as an individual organism.** It germinated in 2000 and was planted at the site in 2015.

**Yes, institutionally, as a successor object.** The Juntas treat the name as belonging to a continuous office or lineage filled by successive oaks. This resembles the identity of a repeatedly renewed sacred fire, regimental colour, or periodically recast civic image: material discontinuity does not end the institutional object.

Any account saying the present oak itself witnessed medieval assemblies, royal oaths, Agirre’s 1936 oath, or the 1937 bombing is false. Those events involved predecessors at the same site.

### 4.2 Biological lineage

Official sources call the present specimen a genetic descendant by acorn of the 1858/1860–2004 tree. This language supports direct maternal descent but not clonal identity. Oaks reproduce sexually; an acorn normally contains genetic material from both the seed-bearing tree and an unknown pollen parent.

No peer-reviewed microsatellite, genomic, ancient-DNA, or parentage study of the successive official trees was located. No published test was found comparing:

- the preserved Old Tree;
- fragment AAAA/5120;
- fragment 00/3447;
- the 2005 tree;
- the present tree;
- internationally distributed “descendants.”

Accordingly, the lineage is best classified as **institutionally documented horticultural provenance**, not scientifically published genetic proof.

### 4.3 Chronological contradictions

The principal unresolved contradictions are:

- whether the first controlled Father Tree dates from the fourteenth century or 1564;
- whether it ceased in 1742 or died in 1811;
- how a tree allegedly planted in 1564 and dying in 1811 relates to the Old Tree dated 1742–1892;
- whether the nineteenth-century successor was planted in 1858, 1859, 1860, or 1861;
- whether a failed early sapling and the successful 2004 tree have been conflated.

These discrepancies do not undermine the existence of the later trees. They do prevent a precise, gap-free biological genealogy back to the medieval oak.

### 4.4 Preserved-trunk identity

The principal Old Tree trunk in the pavilion and museum fragment AAAA/5120 are institutionally identified as material from the 1742–1892 tree. No published dendrochronology, radiocarbon test, conservation report, or chain-of-custody file for the separation of the museum fragment was found.

The Museo Vasco’s inventory number and physical dimensions establish an accessioned object. They do not independently prove every date attributed to the source tree.

### 4.5 “Fossilized” terminology

Official and popular sources sometimes call the Old Tree’s trunk a *fósil*. This is commemorative language. The sources examined do not report geological fossilization or laboratory-confirmed petrification. The object should be described as a preserved dead trunk unless a conservation report establishes a particular mineralizing or consolidating process.

### 4.6 The 1937 protection narrative

Three propositions should be separated:

1. **Documented:** the tree and Assembly House survived the bombing.
2. **Documented:** Carlist requetés guarded the tree after insurgent occupation, and Francoist media displayed that action.
3. **Disputed/unverified in detail:** Falangists were actually preparing to cut it down and were physically prevented at the last moment by Jaime del Burgo.

The third claim is plausible in the political climate but rests on retrospective testimony and rumour in the sources located. It should not be stated as an uncontested thwarted theft or destruction attempt.

### 4.7 No rival “true tree”

No competing living oak currently has a serious institutional claim to replace the Casa de Juntas specimen as the official Tree of Gernika. Hundreds of descendants and purported descendants exist elsewhere, but they are commemorative offspring, not rival originals.

The only meaningful identity tension is internal: whether “the Tree” means one organism, one maternal lineage, the rooted place, the office of civic tree, or the entire tree-and-assembly complex.

### 4.8 Authority to authenticate

Authority has moved through:

- the customary political community and General Assembly;
- officers of the Lordship and provincial administration;
- the Diputación during the suppression of the historic Juntas;
- the restored Juntas Generales;
- museum registrars for detached fragments;
- Basalan and collaborating university specialists for botanical health;
- institutional certificates accompanying official seedlings.

No ecclesiastical authority is required to authenticate it, despite the “holy tree” language of the anthem.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings

### 5.1 Rulers and claimants

- **Alfonso XI of Castile — fourteenth century, traditional/documentary reconstruction.** Later histories associate his recognition as Lord of Biscay with the Gernika assembly. The exact tree and ceremony remain incompletely documented.
- **Ferdinand the Catholic — 30 July 1476, documented.** Confirmed the fueros and received homage at Gernika.
- **Isabella I of Castile — 17 September 1483, documented.** Personally performed the Gernika oath according to the documentary study by Alicia Gould Quincy.
- **María Cristina of Bourbon — 1839, institutional tradition.** As regent for Isabella II, performed the last royal oath in the traditional line.
- **Carlos de Borbón y Austria-Este (“Carlos VII”) — 3 July 1875, documented political ceremony.** Used the tree to legitimate his Carlist claim during civil war.
- **Alfonso XIII — September 1922, documented engagement.** Visited during the Third Congress of Basque Studies; the visit generated both official ceremony and nationalist protest.

### 5.2 Foral institutions

- **Juntas Generales del Señorío de Vizcaya — medieval period to 1876.** The tree’s central institutional network; representatives assembled there, enacted or confirmed norms, and received rulers.
- **Diputación de Vizcaya/Bizkaia — nineteenth and twentieth centuries.** Maintained the site and successor trees during periods when the old assemblies were suspended or transformed.
- **Juntas Generales de Bizkaia — restored 1979 to present.** Current controlling institution and certifier of the official lineage.
- **Basalan — late twentieth century to present.** Provincial forestry company responsible for cultivation and care of reserve seedlings and official trees.
- **University of the Basque Country specialists — 2014.** Participated in the examination of the failing 2005 tree.

### 5.3 Writers, artists, and scholars

- **Tirso de Molina — seventeenth century, literary engagement.** Referred to the tree’s antiquity and its refusal of shade to tyrants, criminals, and traitors. This is literary characterization, not custody evidence.
- **Jean-Jacques Rousseau — eighteenth century, frequently alleged association.** His well-known image of peasants wisely conducting public affairs under an oak is often applied to Gernika. The passage does not name Gernika; treating it as a direct eyewitness description is unjustified.
- **Wilhelm von Humboldt — early nineteenth century, documented visitor/writer.** Described a weathered oak with a cracked, twisted trunk and dead branches. The description probably concerns the late Old Tree generation.
- **William Wordsworth — 1810, literary engagement.** Wrote “The Oak of Guernica,” comparing it with Dodona’s sacred oak and addressing it as guardian of Biscay’s liberty. His note relied on Alexandre de Laborde rather than personal custody or inspection. [Wordsworth’s 1815 printed text](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Poems_%28Wordsworth%2C_1815%29/Volume_2/The_Oak_of_Guernica)
- **José María Iparragirre — 1853 onward, transformative symbolic engagement.** Wrote the lyrics and claimed the melody of *Gernikako Arbola*, turning a principally Biscayan emblem into a mass Basque symbol.
- **Juan María Blas de Altuna — 1853, musical association.** Accompanied the earliest documented Madrid performance and transcribed or corrected the melody. Claims that he was the sole composer remain disputed.
- **Carmelo Echegaray — late nineteenth/early twentieth century, historian.** Recorded the alleged cutting attack on the young tree.
- **Sabino Arana — 1890s–1903, ideological engagement.** Retained the oak in nationalist imagery but rejected Iparragirre’s anthem as insufficiently nationalist and too available to Spanish foralists.
- **Tomás Meabe — 1902–1904, polemical engagement.** Mocked the physical tree’s condition yet defended the song’s universalist language against nationalist restriction.
- **Pablo Picasso — 1937, indirect association.** His painting *Guernica* refers to the bombed town, not specifically to the oak. The painting nevertheless enlarged the international semantic field in which the tree became a symbol of survival and peace.
- **Félix Luengo Teixidor and Ander Delgado Cendagortagalarza — 2006, historiography.** Produced the leading source-driven study of the tree’s modern political transformations.

### 5.4 Basque presidents

- **José Antonio Agirre — 7 October 1936.** First *lehendakari* to swear office under the tree.
- **Jesús María Leizaola — 1960.** Took office in exile and therefore did not use the Gernika site.
- **Carlos Garaikoetxea — 1980.** Revived the Gernika oath after democratic restoration.
- **José Antonio Ardanza, Juan José Ibarretxe, Patxi López, Iñigo Urkullu — 1985–2020.** Used the tree precinct for their respective oaths or promises, with variations in religious and constitutional wording.
- **Imanol Pradales — 22 June 2024.** Swore beneath the present tree over copies of the Statute of Gernika and Fuero Viejo. The official formula began *Eusko lur gainean zutunik… Gernikako zuhaitzpean…* [Official record of Pradales’s inauguration](https://www.euskadi.eus/eleccion-lehendakari/web01-s1ezaleh/es/)

### 5.5 Military and political actors in 1937

- **German Condor Legion and Italian aviation — 26 April 1937.** Conducted the bombing in which the tree precinct survived.
- **Jaime del Burgo Torres and the Tercio de Begoña — late April 1937.** Guarded the tree after capture of the town; later credited with preventing a rumoured Falangist attack.
- **Francoist film and propaganda services — 1937 onward.** Presented the guarded oak as evidence of insurgent respect for “legitimate,” Catholic, Spanish Basque tradition while falsely attributing the town’s destruction to Republican or Basque forces.

### 5.6 Divine associations

The tree has no demonstrated pre-Christian cult or named divine owner.

- **Christian God:** invoked in Iparragirre’s anthem as planter and protector and in the 1936 oath formula.
- **Virgin Mary:** appears in later stanzas of the anthem, probably added around the 1854 proclamation of the Immaculate Conception.
- **Dodona’s divine oak:** Wordsworth’s classical comparison; a poetic analogy, not a historical cult link.

Claims that the Tree of Gernika directly continues a Celtic, druidic, or pagan sacred-oak cult are speculative. Assembly under trees was widespread in medieval Europe and does not by itself establish ritual continuity from antiquity.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Assembly function

The tree marked the customary place where Biscayan representatives met. The 1452 Fuero describes the General Assembly as customarily held beneath it. The tree provided a visible centre for a political community whose constituent towns and *anteiglesias* sent representatives.

It should not be imagined that every session throughout history was conducted entirely outdoors beneath its branches. The adjoining hermitage and later Assembly House increasingly accommodated meetings. The tree remained the legal and symbolic centre even as business moved indoors.

### 6.2 Summoning the assemblies

The Fuero speaks of the “five horns” or trumpets being sounded when the assembly met. A later romantic tradition relocated the sounding to five “horn mountains” across Bizkaia and imagined animal horns transmitting the summons throughout the territory.

Recent institutional historical work distinguishes the traditions: medieval documentation indicates that metal horns were sounded in Gernika to gather those already assembled, while the mountain-to-mountain network was substantially elaborated in nineteenth-century foralist culture. The later mountain ritual is historically influential but should not be projected unchanged into the Middle Ages.

### 6.3 The lord’s oath

The ruler’s progress combined sacred and constitutional acts:

1. recognition at Aretxabalagana;
2. oath before the consecrated host at a church;
3. appearance beneath the Gernika tree;
4. confirmation or consensual amendment of fueros;
5. further oath at Santa Eufemia in Bermeo.

The Gernika stage expressed that the lord governed through a recognized body of local liberties and with the agreement of the Biscayans. Later ideologies interpreted this differently:

- monarchists and moderate foralists saw a historic pact compatible with Spanish monarchy;
- Carlists saw Catholic and traditional legitimacy;
- federal republicans saw local self-government;
- Basque nationalists interpreted pact-making as evidence of prior sovereignty.

### 6.4 Lehendakari oath

Agirre’s 1936 wording, translated approximately, was:

> Humbled before God, standing on Basque soil, in memory of the ancestors, beneath the Tree of Gernika, before you, representatives of the people, I swear faithfully to discharge my office.

Later presidents retained the place and essential sequence while changing language concerning God, society, oath versus promise, and constitutional texts. In 2024 Pradales swore over the Statute of Gernika and the Fuero Viejo.

The tree is therefore not merely decorative. Physical presence at the site supplies historical legitimacy to the office, even though the legal validity of election comes from the modern constitutional and parliamentary system.

### 6.5 Heraldic function

The oak became the central charge of Bizkaia’s arms. The mature traditional design combined:

- a green oak;
- the arms or wolves associated with the House of Haro;
- a cross behind the crown, documented by at least the seventeenth century;
- a border with heraldic devices in some versions.

In 1936 the Basque Government’s arms used the Gernika oak without the Haro wolves. In 1986 the Juntas Generales adopted a revised Bizkaia shield likewise omitting the wolves. The change reflected a long dispute over whether dynastic emblems of the lords belonged in the civic arms. [Study of Bizkaia’s heraldic development](https://www.bizkaia.eus/fitxategiak/04/ondarea/Kobie/PDF/6/KOBIE_Anejo25_web.pdf)

The current Basque governmental shield is surrounded by oak leaves recalling the Tree of Gernika. [Basque Government symbols](https://www.euskadi.eus/web01-a2inguru/es/contenidos/informacion/01_simbolos/es_simbolos/escudo.html)

### 6.6 Anthem and ritual song

Iparragirre’s first stanza, in historical spelling, begins:

> *Guernicaco arbola / Da bedeincatuba…*

It calls the tree blessed and beloved, commands it to spread its fruit throughout the world, and calls it holy. The preserved twelve-stanza version includes four “new verses,” probably added after the initial composition.

The first documented performance took place at Madrid’s Café de San Luis in 1853, probably before 30 June. A performance at the University of Madrid is recorded on 30 June. A claim that Iparragirre had already composed and sung it in the 1840s lacks foundation. The possibility of an earlier 1852 performance in Bilbao is a scholarly inference from inconsistent biographical chronology, not a documented premiere.

The melody’s attribution remains debated. Iparragirre said Altuna merely transcribed it and corrected small faults; Altuna did not claim authorship. Another theory derives it from a Durangaldea dance melody allegedly documented in 1711. The lyric’s authorship is much more secure.

The song became a practical ritual around the object: performed at official visits, foralist demonstrations, Carlist gatherings, socialist meetings, diaspora celebrations, and commemorative plantings. Bizkaia declared it its official anthem in 2007; it is not the statutory anthem of the Basque Autonomous Community.

### 6.7 Access and touch

The current planted bed is partly protected by a railing. Public visitors can enter the Casa de Juntas complex subject to opening hours, reservations, security, and closure for plenary sessions. No evidence was found of an absolute religious taboo against viewing or touching the tree. Restrictions are protective, ceremonial, and institutional rather than sacerdotal.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The immemorial single tree

**Story:** One oak lived from the remote Middle Ages through centuries of assemblies and royal oaths.

**Classification:** Later simplification of a real institutional continuity.

**Evidence:** Assemblies under a Gernika tree are medieval; the current and preserved trees are successors. The single-organism narrative collapses several overlapping oaks into one immortal witness.

**Effect:** The fiction of organic continuity made the tree an unusually strong symbol of historical rights: roots signified antiquity, trunk continuity, branches territorial community, and acorns regeneration.

### 7.2 Jaun Zuria and primordial liberty

Later legendary histories connect the assembly tree with **Jaun Zuria**, the supposed first Lord of Biscay, and the legendary Battle of Arrigorriaga. In these accounts, Biscayans elected or acclaimed their lord, who swore their customs beneath the tree.

The Jaun Zuria complex is medieval and early-modern political legend rather than securely recoverable ninth-century history. Projecting the documented fifteenth-century oath procedure into his supposed lifetime produces a legendary provenance for the institution.

The legend served to claim that lordship arose from Biscayan election and compact, rather than unilateral royal grant.

### 7.3 Rousseau at Gernika

**Modern claim:** Rousseau visited or specifically described the Biscayans governing beneath the Tree of Gernika.

**Evidence:** Rousseau wrote admiringly of peasants conducting state affairs under an oak, but the passage does not name Gernika. Later foralist writers naturalized the image as a reference to the Biscayan assemblies.

**Classification:** Attribution by later association, not secure direct testimony.

### 7.4 “While the tree lives, liberty lives”

There is no securely identified medieval prophecy stating that Bizkaia or the Basque people will remain free while the oak stands. Nineteenth-century verse and political rhetoric repeatedly equated damage to the tree with abolition of the fueros and regeneration of the tree with recovery of liberty.

The effective “prophecy” is therefore metaphorical and cumulative rather than an ancient oracle. It nevertheless influenced preservation and succession: reserve seedlings ensured that political enemies, disease, or age could not terminate the symbol by killing one organism.

### 7.5 Planted by God

In Iparragirre’s anthem, God planted the tree “about a thousand years ago.” The tree is addressed as blessed and holy and asked to bear fruit worldwide.

**Classification:** Nineteenth-century poetic sacralization.

**Factual core:** The medieval assembly tradition was already centuries old in 1853.

**Non-factual element:** No documentary or scientific evidence identifies divine planting or establishes a one-thousand-year-old organism.

### 7.6 Miraculous survival in 1937

The tree’s survival amid the destruction of Gernika became a powerful narrative of providential or national endurance.

**Documented core:** The precinct survived the bombing.

**Unresolved factors:** The exact reasons may include bombing patterns, target selection, construction, chance, and the site’s position. Survival does not demonstrate supernatural intervention.

**Historical effect:** After 1937 the tree’s older meaning—local law and foral liberty—merged with Gernika’s international meanings of civilian suffering, antifascism, peace, and survival.

### 7.7 Del Burgo saving the tree

The fullest form says that Falangists approached with axes to destroy a separatist symbol, whereupon Jaime del Burgo ordered armed Carlists to encircle the oak and saved it.

**Earliest evidentiary basis located:** Del Burgo’s later recollection and subsequent retellings.

**Documented elements:** Carlist custody and propaganda imagery.

**Unverified elements:** the assailants’ identities, actual presence, axes, timing, and imminence.

**Interest served:** Carlists could claim to be the true defenders of Basque fueros while remaining Spanish traditionalists and participants in Franco’s coalition.

### 7.8 The five horn mountains

The romantic form describes five horns sounded from five mountains to summon all Bizkaia.

**Documented core:** Five horns were sounded in association with the Gernika assembly.

**Later elaboration:** simultaneous or relay sounding from five mountains with animal horns. Recent historical work attributes much of this form to nineteenth-century romantic foralism.

### 7.9 Pagan or druidic origin

Modern popular writing sometimes treats the oak as the survival of an ancient Celtic tree cult.

**Classification:** Speculative modern interpretation.

Assemblies under trees occurred across Europe; sacred-oak imagery is widespread; Wordsworth compared Gernika to Dodona. None of this demonstrates an unbroken pagan cult at the site. No excavation, inscription, or ancient text tying the Gernika oak to a named pre-Christian deity was found.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 From Biscayan emblem to Basque national symbol

Before the nineteenth century, the oak primarily represented Biscayan institutions. Its extension to all Basque territories accelerated during the foral controversies of the nineteenth century. Important causes were:

- the absence of an equally fixed assembly monument shared by Álava, Gipuzkoa, and Navarre;
- the visual power of a living, regenerating tree;
- Iparragirre’s song;
- the Carlist Wars and abolition or modification of traditional fueros;
- Basque migration and diaspora organizations.

Luengo and Delgado date its consolidation as the principal general Basque symbol to the mid-nineteenth century rather than immemorial antiquity. That does not make the underlying assembly tree modern; it dates the expansion of its territorial meaning.

### 9.2 Competing political appropriations

The tree has never possessed one uncontested ideology.

- **Moderate liberals** treated the fueros as local forms of constitutional liberty compatible with Spain.
- **Carlists** presented the tree as Catholic, traditional, monarchical, and Spanish.
- **Federal republicans** used it as an emblem of government by the people.
- **Basque nationalists** interpreted it as evidence of original sovereignty but often preferred newer party-national symbols.
- **Socialists** sometimes ridiculed the physical “log,” yet other socialists celebrated the anthem’s command to spread liberty through the world.
- **Francoists**, especially Carlist-derived sectors, retained it as a symbol of regional tradition while rejecting separatism.
- **Post-1978 institutions** use it as a symbol of democracy, historic rights, self-government, and plural Basque society.

### 9.3 Political conflict around the anthem

In 1893, during protests against Finance Minister Germán Gamazo, *Gernikako Arbola* was sung in Pamplona and at mass meetings. On 27 August 1893 in San Sebastián, refusal by a municipal band to perform it contributed to a demonstration that police suppressed violently, leaving three dead and numerous wounded according to the cited scholarly reconstruction.

By the early twentieth century, Sabino Arana’s followers criticized the song as too foralist, insufficiently nationalist, linguistically impure, and too readily used by Spanish monarchists and Carlists. Proposals even changed “spread your fruit through the world” to “through Euskadi.” Socialists defended the original universalist line.

The tree thus remained a shared visual symbol while its song became a field of ideological competition.

### 9.4 Descendant trees

Official sources say **approximately half a thousand** descendants have been planted across five continents since the nineteenth century. This total appears to combine multiple institutional and private distributions; a complete, audited register was not located.

Named locations include:

- Gasteiz, at Lehendakaritza;
- Padrón;
- Laudio;
- Pforzheim;
- Gothenburg;
- Sydney;
- Washington;
- Auschwitz;
- Nairobi;
- Rome;
- Montevideo;
- São Paulo;
- Celaya;
- Rosario;
- Mexico City;
- Montalivet;
- La Garriga.

A 2017 report documented **66 officially delivered seedlings since 1975**, a narrower count than the broader “approximately 500” planted since the nineteenth century. The figures are compatible if they describe different periods and certification standards. [Official report on the Lehendakaritza descendant](https://www.irekia.euskadi.eus/es/news/59853-retono-del-arbol-gernika-crece-lehendakaritza)

These trees perform several functions:

- diaspora connection;
- municipal twinning;
- commemoration of war victims;
- claims of peace and reconciliation;
- regional and institutional friendship;
- material enactment of the lyric “spread your fruit through the world.”

An official certificate commonly accompanies an institutional seedling. Certification establishes the documented transfer through the Juntas’ distribution system, not necessarily DNA-confirmed paternity.

### 9.5 Commercial propagation

The private project **Gernikako Haziak**, established in 2014, has marketed descendants with certificates and extended the tree’s name to products such as perfume and air freshener. This is a commercial afterlife of the emblem, distinct from official custodianship. [EITB interview on Gernikako Haziak](https://www.eitb.eus/es/radio/radio-euskadi/programas/vivirparaver/detalle/5883955/gernikako-haziak-retonos-arbol-gernika-mundo/)

### 9.6 Literature and visual art

- Tirso de Molina used the oak as a literary emblem of untarnished liberty.
- Wordsworth’s 1810 sonnet transformed it into an international Romantic monument, comparing it favourably with the oracular oak of Dodona.
- Numerous nineteenth-century Basque and Spanish-language poets produced obligatory odes for floral games and political festivals.
- Iparragirre’s 1853 song became the decisive popular representation.
- Sabino Arana’s nationalist hymn retained a sacred Biscayan oak beneath the cross even while displacing Iparragirre’s song.
- Picasso’s *Guernica* did not depict the official oak as its subject, but the painting caused the town’s name—and by association the tree—to signify aerial terror and antiwar memory globally.
- Fernando Arrabal’s film *L’Arbre de Guernica* / *El árbol de Guernica* (1975) used the name for a Surrealist treatment of the Spanish Civil War. The film helped detach the title further from literal arboreal history.

### 9.7 Honours and official derivatives

The Basque Government created the **Cross of the Tree of Gernika** by Decree 86/1983. It recognizes exceptional service to Euskadi, its identity, or its social, economic, and cultural life. The decoration reproduces the tree as shown in the arms of the Lordship. [Basque Government description](https://www.euskadi.eus/cruz-del-arbol-de-gernika/web01-s1ezaleh/es/)

The tree also appears:

- in the arms of Bizkaia;
- in the Basque Government’s heraldic environment;
- in municipal arms;
- in institutional seals, stationery, medals, plaques, and stained glass;
- in diaspora-centre emblems and commemorative planting certificates.

### 9.8 Tourism

The tree and Casa de Juntas are among Gernika-Lumo’s principal visitor destinations. Tourism combines at least three historical narratives that should remain distinguishable:

1. medieval and early-modern foral government;
2. the 1937 bombing;
3. Picasso’s painting and international peace memory.

The tree belongs directly to the first and survived the event central to the second; it is only indirectly related to the third.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Primary evidence

The principal classes of primary evidence are:

- medieval and early-modern fueros and assembly records;
- royal oath instruments;
- records of the Juntas and Diputación;
- construction documentation for the Assembly House;
- nineteenth-century newspapers and political pamphlets;
- travellers’ descriptions;
- song printings and Iparragirre’s correspondence;
- photographs, newsreels, and propaganda films from 1937;
- botanical reports and institutional press releases;
- museum accession records.

The Valladolid MS 040 is particularly important because it preserves the explicit formula locating the General Assembly beneath the tree. Its manuscript is sixteenth-century, while the legal text it transmits belongs to the fifteenth-century redaction and publication history.

### 10.2 Major interpretive shift

Older histories often treated the tree’s general Basque meaning as immemorial. Recent scholarship distinguishes:

- a medieval Biscayan institutional object;
- early literary emblematic uses in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries;
- a dramatic nineteenth-century expansion into a pan-Basque symbol;
- a twentieth-century fusion with nationalism, the bombing, exile, and peace memory.

Luengo and Delgado argue that the song’s success, the foral crisis, and the lack of rival shared sites explain the tree’s nineteenth-century ascendancy. This interpretation avoids both extremes: it neither denies the medieval assembly evidence nor projects modern nationalism unchanged into it.

### 10.3 Disputed chronology of the organisms

No fully satisfactory critical catalogue of the individual trees was located. Official institutions, the Museo Vasco, newspapers, and older local histories disagree on planting and death dates. A future definitive study would need:

- archival horticultural accounts;
- assembly expenditure records;
- dated photographs and drawings;
- dendrochronology of preserved trunks and fragments;
- conservation treatment files;
- genetic comparison among retained tissues and authenticated offspring.

### 10.4 Botany and pathology

The 2004 and 2015 deaths generated extensive technical attention, but the underlying full reports are not prominently published online. Public accounts identify:

- *Armillaria mellea* and an additional foliar parasite in the older tree;
- heat stress in 2003;
- long-term disease from around 1970;
- waterlogging, clay confinement, shade, poor drainage, and root constriction in the 2005 tree;
- failure of roots to absorb water despite wet conditions.

“Death by drowning” is journalistic shorthand for a technically described process of root degeneration and loss of water uptake in waterlogged or otherwise unsuitable soil.

### 10.5 Musical authorship debate

The anthem’s text is attributed to Iparragirre. The melody has been assigned variously to:

- Iparragirre;
- Altuna;
- joint composition;
- an older Durangaldea dance tune adapted by one or both men.

Iparragirre’s own testimony supports his authorship with Altuna acting as transcriber and corrector. The absence of a claim by Altuna weighs against the later sole-Altuna theory but does not conclusively settle the matter.

### 10.6 Discredited or unsupported popular theories

The following should not be repeated as established:

- the current oak is the same organism under which medieval lords swore;
- the preserved Old Tree is certainly the original medieval oak;
- Rousseau explicitly visited or named the Gernika tree in his oak passage;
- the five mountains relayed medieval summons exactly as modern commemorations depict;
- the tree is a proven survival of a Celtic or druidic sanctuary;
- it was intentionally spared by the German and Italian bombers;
- Falangists unquestionably reached it with axes and were stopped in an immediately documented confrontation;
- every tree sold or displayed as a descendant has been genetically tested;
- “fossilized trunk” necessarily means petrified wood.

### 10.7 Unresolved research questions

1. What is the earliest surviving original document, rather than later copy or summary, explicitly naming the tree?
2. Can the early-fourteenth-century assembly references be tied to a particular organism?
3. What happened in 1564: planting, planned felling, or establishment of a reserve grove?
4. How should the 1742, 1811, and 1892 dates be reconciled?
5. Was the successful nineteenth-century successor planted in 1858, 1860, or 1861?
6. Does an archival police or administrative record survive for the circa-1864 cutting attack?
7. Where are the complete conservation files for the pavilion trunk?
8. Was material from the 2005–2015 oak accessioned, and if so under what number?
9. Can DNA still be obtained from the Old Tree, 2004 fragment, and living descendants?
10. Is there an authoritative, public register of certified descendants and their survival status?

## 12. Sources

### Primary and near-primary documentary sources

- **Fuero Viejo de Vizcaya, Valladolid University Library MS 040.** Sixteenth-century manuscript transmission of the 1452 Fuero, including the formula “under the Tree of Guernica where it is customary to hold the General Assembly.”
- **Isabella’s oath instrument as transcribed and analysed by Alicia Gould Quincy.** Evidence for her personal visit and oath in 1483.
- **William Wordsworth, “The Oak of Guernica.”** Primary literary evidence for international Romantic reception in 1810–1815.
- **José María Iparragirre, *Gernikako Arbola*.** Primary poetic text; early text documented in José Antonio Santesteban’s 1862 collection and analysed in the modern critical study.
- **Juntas Generales de Bizkaia institutional pages and planting releases.** Primary institutional statements on succession, present custody, and ceremonies.
- **Museo Vasco/Museotik catalogues.** Primary collection records for preserved fragments.
- **Basque Government inauguration records.** Primary institutional records for modern oaths.
- **Contemporary press reports, 2004–2015.** Near-primary reporting of official botanical findings and replacement work.

### Principal modern studies

- Félix Luengo Teixidor and Ander Delgado Cendagortagalarza, “El árbol de Gernika. Vicisitudes del símbolo foral de los vascos,” *Historia y Política* 15 (2006). Essential for the nineteenth- and twentieth-century political career of the symbol.
- Xabier Zabaltza, “Gernikako Arbola, un himno huérfano,” *Historia Contemporánea* 54. Essential for the anthem’s authorship, premiere, textual variants, ideological use, and later legends.
- Alicia Gould Quincy, “Isabel la Católica y su juramento so el Arbol de Guernica,” *Revista Internacional de los Estudios Vascos* 24. Documentary analysis of the 1483 oath.
- Museo Vasco catalogue records for the physical fragments.
- geoEuskadi’s site description for the present monumental arrangement and coordinates.

### Source limitations

The most important unavailable or unpublished bodies of evidence are the complete horticultural files, pathology reports, conservation records for the preserved trunks, and a critical archival register of all officially distributed descendants. The absence of published scientific testing is itself material: claims of lineage presently depend on institutional propagation records rather than publicly reported DNA or dendrochronological proof.
