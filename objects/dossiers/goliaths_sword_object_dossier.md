# Goliath's sword: Object Dossier

> **Object type:** Legendary or literary sword; possible memory of an ancient war trophy  
> **Earliest surviving physical attestation of the sword tradition:** 4QSamᵇ (4Q52), a Hebrew parchment manuscript from Qumran Cave 4, conventionally dated about 250 BCE and now more broadly assigned to the early Hellenistic period  
> **Narrative setting:** Traditionally the late eleventh or early tenth century BCE  
> **Last narrated custody:** In David’s possession after the priest Ahimelech gives it to him at Nob  
> **Present status:** The biblical sword is unlocated and not securely identifiable with any surviving artifact  
> **Principal modern claimant:** A 101-centimetre sword called *al-Battār*, associated in later Islamic relic tradition with Muhammad and, secondarily, with David and Goliath; the surviving object is materially and stylistically many centuries too late to be Goliath’s sword

## Method and evidentiary scale

This dossier distinguishes five different objects or levels of evidence that popular accounts frequently collapse:

1. **The sword inside the narrative world of Samuel.**
2. **The sword tradition preserved in surviving ancient manuscripts.**
3. **Any historical sword that may lie behind that tradition.**
4. **Later relics or claimants identified with Goliath’s sword.**
5. **Artistic, literary, religious, and political representations of it.**

The following labels are used throughout:

- **Textually attested:** stated by an ancient primary text.
- **Manuscript fact:** established from a surviving physical manuscript.
- **Plausible inference:** consistent with the text but not stated by it.
- **Later tradition:** an interpretation or additional detail supplied by a later writer.
- **Institutional or devotional attribution:** an identification maintained in a relic or museum context without continuous documentary provenance.
- **Disputed scholarly reconstruction:** a conclusion supported by some specialists but not universally accepted.
- **Unverified modern claim:** a recent assertion lacking adequate primary documentation.
- **Unknown:** the evidence does not permit an answer.

Dates attached to biblical episodes are therefore dates of the stories’ settings or traditional chronology, not securely documented dates for movements of a surviving artifact.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### 1.1 Names

The Hebrew Bible does not give the weapon an individual proper name. It calls it:

- **חֶרֶב גָּלְיָת הַפְּלִשְׁתִּי** — *ḥerev Golyat ha-Pəlištî*, “the sword of Goliath the Philistine” (1 Samuel 21:10 in Hebrew/Jewish numbering; 21:9 in many English Bibles).
- **חַרְבּוֹ** — *ḥarbô*, “his sword,” when David takes it from the fallen Philistine (1 Samuel 17:51).
- Goliath himself speaks of coming with a **חֶרֶב**, *ḥerev*, “sword,” alongside a spear and another weapon (1 Samuel 17:45).

The common noun *ḥerev* can designate a sword or large fighting blade but does not determine blade length, profile, metallurgy, or whether the weapon was straight or curved. It should not be translated more precisely as “broadsword,” “falchion,” “scimitar,” or “two-handed sword” without independent evidence.

The Old Greek or Septuagint calls it:

- **ἡ ῥομφαία Γολιαθ τοῦ ἀλλοφύλου** — *hē rhomphaia Goliath tou allophylou*, “the sword of Goliath the foreigner/Philistine.”

The Greek noun *rhomphaia* can refer to a large sword, but in this translation it functions as a rendering of Hebrew *ḥerev* and cannot by itself establish the weapon’s archaeological type. The Old Greek calls Philistines *allophyloi*, literally “people of another tribe.”

The Latin Vulgate tradition uses **gladius Goliath Philisthaei**, “the sword of Goliath the Philistine.” European forms—*Goliath’s sword*, *l’épée de Goliath*, *Schwert Goliaths*, *spada di Golia*, *espada de Goliat*—derive from the biblical identification rather than from an ancient individual name.

The name **al-Battār** or **al-Battār/al-Battar** (Arabic البتّار, approximately “the cutter,” “the severer,” or “the sharp, decisive sword”) belongs to a different, later relic tradition. No biblical manuscript calls Goliath’s weapon *al-Battār*.

### 1.2 What the earliest texts physically describe

The biblical text supplies remarkably little information about the sword itself.

**Textually attested:**

- It had a **sheath or scabbard**: David “drew it from its sheath” (*mittaʿrāh*, מִתַּעְרָהּ) in 1 Samuel 17:51.
- It was usable as a cutting weapon and was employed to decapitate Goliath.
- At Nob it was **wrapped in a garment or cloth**—Hebrew *lûṭāh baš-śimlāh*—and placed “behind the ephod.”
- David said, “There is none like it,” or “There is nothing like it” (1 Samuel 21:10/9).
- Goliath carried it into the confrontation in the Valley of Elah.

**Not stated:**

- blade length;
- total length;
- width;
- weight;
- metal;
- number of cutting edges;
- curvature;
- hilt material;
- guard or pommel form;
- maker;
- forge;
- place or date of manufacture;
- inscription;
- decoration;
- supernatural property;
- whether it was unusually large;
- whether David retained its original scabbard;
- whether “none like it” refers to size, workmanship, symbolic value, suitability, or merely the absence of another available weapon.

Popular illustrations often transfer Goliath’s extraordinary scale to his sword. The text does not. It quantifies Goliath’s armor and the **iron head of his spear**, not his sword. The spearhead is said to weigh six hundred shekels of iron, while the helmet, scale armor, greaves, and another weapon are described as bronze. None of those material statements grammatically governs the sword. Assertions that “Goliath’s sword was iron” are plausible only as historical speculation, not as a biblical datum.

### 1.3 Relationship to Goliath’s other weapons

In the Masoretic form of 1 Samuel 17:4–7, Goliath’s equipment includes:

- a bronze helmet;
- bronze scale armor;
- bronze greaves;
- a **כִּידוֹן**, *kîdôn*, carried between or behind his shoulders;
- a large spear whose shaft is likened to a weaver’s beam;
- an iron spearhead;
- a shield bearer;
- and, from 17:45 and 17:51, a sword.

The identity of the *kîdôn* is disputed. It has been rendered javelin, spear, dagger, or curved sword. Some reconstructions identify it with an Aegean or Near Eastern curved blade. That does not make it identical to the separately named *ḥerev* drawn from the sheath in 17:51. One military reconstruction consequently treats the *kîdôn* as a curved side weapon and the *ḥerev* as a conventional straight infantry sword. This remains a reconstruction, not an excavated identification.

Azzan Yadin argued that the equipment is an eclectic literary assemblage and that the narrative, in its extant form, reflects later knowledge of Greek or Aegean warfare. Jeffrey Zorn instead proposed that the equipment can be understood coherently as that of an elite Iron Age I Philistine chariot warrior. The disagreement concerns the story’s historical horizon, not a recovered sword. [Yadin’s study](https://blogs.bu.edu/aberlin/files/2011/09/Yadin-Goliaths-armor.pdf) and [Zorn’s study](https://doi.org/10.1086/BASOR41104416) illustrate the two positions.

### 1.4 State of preservation

No object with a secure archaeological context, inscription, ancient inventory, or continuous provenance has been authenticated as Goliath’s sword. Consequently:

- no original blade, hilt, scabbard, cloth wrapping, or fragment is known;
- no scientific test has been performed on the biblical object;
- no museum accession number belongs to an authenticated sword of Goliath;
- depictions in art are iconographic inventions;
- the Topkapı-associated *al-Battār* is a separate medieval or early Islamic composite relic claimant discussed below.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### 2.1 Narrative origin

Samuel supplies no manufacture story. Within the narrative, the weapon first appears as part of the equipment Goliath brings from the Philistine camp into the Valley of Elah. Nothing identifies:

- a Philistine smith;
- Gath as its manufacturing place;
- a royal commission;
- inherited ownership;
- a divine maker;
- captured earlier provenance;
- metal source;
- date or cost.

Calling it “Philistine-made” therefore goes beyond the source. It is Goliath’s sword and Goliath is identified as a Philistine from Gath; manufacture in a Philistine workshop is possible but not stated.

The historically reconstructed setting traditionally falls around the turn of the first millennium BCE, but the extant narrative cannot simply be treated as a contemporary inventory. Yadin emphasizes the story’s layered composition and the long interval between its alleged setting and textual preservation. Zorn argues that the armament can nevertheless fit an Iron Age I chariot warrior. Neither position demonstrates the physical existence of this particular sword.

### 2.2 Earliest surviving manuscript witness

The most important physical witness is **4QSamᵇ (4Q52)**:

- **Material:** parchment.
- **Language:** Hebrew.
- **Script:** square Hebrew script.
- **Findspot:** Qumran Cave 4.
- **Conventional date:** approximately 250 BCE or late third/early second century BCE, classified as early Hellenistic.
- **Surviving contents:** parts of 1 Samuel 16, 19–21, 22, and 23, including portions of 1 Samuel 21:1–10 and the sword passage.
- **Current documentary access:** high-resolution images in the Israel Antiquities Authority’s Leon Levy Dead Sea Scrolls Digital Library.

The official digital record classifies 4Q52 as an early Hellenistic Hebrew scriptural manuscript on parchment. [Israel Antiquities Authority record](https://www.deadseascrolls.org.il/explore-the-archive/manuscript/4Q52-1)

This makes the sword tradition materially attested by the Hellenistic period. It does **not** date the alleged sword or prove that the episode occurred. A manuscript copied around 250 BCE establishes that a version of the tradition was then in circulation.

Recent radiocarbon and computational-paleographic work has reopened the dating of some early scrolls. Reported probability ranges for 4Q52 include substantially earlier possibilities than its conventional paleographic date. Such modeling dates the parchment/manuscript, not the events narrated on it, and should not be converted into an archaeological date for the sword.

### 2.3 Old Greek witness

The Old Greek translation of Samuel, titled **1 Reigns**, preserves both essential episodes:

- David takes the Philistine’s *rhomphaia*, draws it from its sheath, and decapitates him.
- At Nob the priest offers David “the sword of Goliath the allophyle,” wrapped in a garment; the Greek version then explicitly adds, “And he gave it to him.”

A modern critical translation is available in the [New English Translation of the Septuagint](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/nets/edition/09-1reigns-nets.pdf).

The Old Greek version of 1 Samuel 16–18 is considerably shorter than the Masoretic form. Its David-and-Goliath narrative includes 17:51, so the taking and use of the sword are not among the most conspicuous long-form expansions. Emanuel Tov’s influential reconstruction regards the shorter Greek form as reflecting an earlier Hebrew edition and the Masoretic form as a later expanded edition. Other scholars have defended alternative models, including abbreviation in the Greek tradition or more complicated interaction between editions. [Tov’s study](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004275973/B9789004275973-s024.pdf)

### 2.4 Masoretic witness

The medieval Masoretic tradition gives the fullest familiar form. The Hebrew of 1 Samuel 21:9/10 reads:

> וַיֹּאמֶר הַכֹּהֵן חֶרֶב גָּלְיָת הַפְּלִשְׁתִּי אֲשֶׁר־הִכִּיתָ בְּעֵמֶק הָאֵלָה הִנֵּה־הִיא לוּטָה בַשִּׂמְלָה אַחֲרֵי הָאֵפוֹד אִם־אֹתָהּ תִּקַּח־לְךָ קָח כִּי אֵין אַחֶרֶת זוּלָתָהּ בָּזֶה … וַיֹּאמֶר דָּוִד אֵין כָּמוֹהָ תְּנֶנָּה לִּי׃

A close translation is:

> The priest said, “The sword of Goliath the Philistine, whom you struck down in the Valley of Elah—here it is, wrapped in the garment, behind the ephod. If you would take it for yourself, take it, for there is no other here besides it.” David said, “There is none like it; give it to me.”

The Hebrew text and a modern Jewish translation can be compared at [Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/I_Samuel.21.1-22.16).

### 2.5 Josephus

Flavius Josephus, writing in Greek in the late first century CE, adds a crucial detail absent from Samuel: after the victory, David **dedicated Goliath’s sword to God**. When retelling the visit to Nob, Josephus has the priest identify the weapon as the sword David had dedicated after killing Goliath.

This is the earliest clear surviving explanation for how the sword reached a sanctuary. It is a **later interpretive bridge**, not an explicit statement in 1 Samuel 17. Josephus’ wording helped establish the enduring conception of the weapon as a votive trophy. [Josephus, *Antiquities* 6](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/josephus/ant-6.html)

### 2.6 Pseudo-Philo

The *Biblical Antiquities* attributed to Pseudo-Philo, probably composed around the turn of the era, retells the duel and states that David drew Goliath’s sword and removed his head. It elaborates the theological and miraculous surroundings of the episode but does not supply an independent manufacturing history or a traceable later custody. [Pseudo-Philo, *Biblical Antiquities*](https://catholiclibrary.org/library/view?chunk.id=00000121&docId=%2FAntiquities-EN%2FAntiquities.0106.html)

### 2.7 Qurʾanic attestation and later Islamic elaboration

Qurʾan 2:251 says that David killed **Jālūt**—the Arabic form of Goliath—and that God gave David kingship, wisdom, and knowledge. It does not mention Goliath’s sword. [Qurʾan 2:251](https://quran.com/en/2/251)

Elsewhere the Qurʾan associates David with divinely enabled metalworking and armor manufacture, particularly Qurʾan 34:10–11. Later traditions about Davidic swords could combine these separate motifs, but the Qurʾan does not say that David forged Goliath’s sword or preserved it.

The identification of a relic called *al-Battār* as the sword David took from Goliath is therefore a **post-Qurʾanic accretion**, not Qurʾanic testimony.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 3.1 Goliath’s possession

**Claim:** Goliath possesses and carries the sword into the Valley of Elah.

**Evidence:** 1 Samuel 17:45 and 17:51.

**Mechanism of acquisition:** Unknown.

**Place immediately before transfer:** With Goliath on the battlefield, traditionally in the Valley of Elah between Socoh and Azekah.

### 3.2 David’s battlefield seizure

**Claim:** After Goliath falls, David runs to him, takes the sword, draws it from its sheath, kills or finishes killing him, and cuts off his head.

**Evidence:** 1 Samuel 17:49–51 in Hebrew and Greek textual traditions.

**Mechanism:** Battlefield capture or spoil.

**Action performed with the sword:** Killing/dispatching and decapitation.

There is a compositional tension between verses 50 and 51:

- Verse 50 summarizes that David killed the Philistine with sling and stone and that no sword was in David’s hand.
- Verse 51 then says he took the Philistine’s sword, killed him, and severed his head.

Possible readings include:

- the stone caused the fatal collapse and the sword completed the killing;
- verse 50 anticipates the result while verse 51 narrates it;
- “killed him” in verse 51 intensifies or confirms the death;
- the wording reflects the joining of related narrative layers.

The sword’s use in the decapitation is clear; the exact medical cause or narrative instant of death is not.

### 3.3 Trophy phase after the battle

1 Samuel 17:54 says David took Goliath’s head to Jerusalem and put “his weapons” or “his armor/equipment” in his tent. The Hebrew possessive can naturally refer to Goliath’s equipment, but the verse does not itemize it.

The sword may be included among those weapons, but this is a **plausible inference**, not an explicit inventory entry. The reference to Jerusalem is chronologically awkward because, within Samuel’s larger narrative, David does not capture Jerusalem until later. Proposed explanations include:

- proleptic narration;
- later editorial updating;
- a different referent or stage of the tradition;
- evidence of narrative combination.

Josephus converts the uncertainty into a coherent provenance: David keeps the head but dedicates the sword to God.

### 3.4 Transfer to the sanctuary at Nob

**Textually attested fact within the story:** By the time David flees Saul, the sword is at Nob in the custody of a priest, wrapped in a cloth and placed behind the ephod.

**How it arrived:** Samuel does not say.

**Traditional mechanism:** David dedicated it to God as a victory offering or votive trophy.

**Earliest explicit source for dedication:** Josephus, late first century CE.

The description “behind the ephod” is itself debated. “Ephod” may mean:

- a priestly garment;
- a cultic object;
- an oracular apparatus;
- an image or object standing within the sanctuary.

The sword’s placement near or behind it indicates protected sacred storage but does not establish that the blade had become an object of worship. Older scholarship sometimes described the non-garment ephod as a standing cult image; the lexical and archaeological problem remains unsettled. [Jewish Encyclopedia, “Ephod”](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/5790-ephod)

### 3.5 Ahimelech’s custody and transfer to David

At Nob, David asks the priest Ahimelech for a spear or sword, explaining that he left without his own weapons because the king’s business was urgent. Ahimelech replies that only Goliath’s sword is available.

**Custodian:** Ahimelech, son of Ahitub, and the priestly sanctuary at Nob.

**Recipient:** David.

**Mechanism:** Gift, release from sanctuary custody, or return of an object uniquely associated with David.

**Price:** None recorded.

**Conditions:** The priest says that if David wishes to take it, he may. No ritual, oath, payment, or authorization from Saul is reported.

David answers, “There is none like it; give it to me.” The Old Greek then expressly states that Ahimelech gave it to him.

Some medieval and early modern commentators inferred that the sword properly belonged to David as the captor who had deposited it, so no one else had an equivalent claim. Others understood Ahimelech to be releasing sacred property in an emergency. Neither interpretation is expressly defined by the narrative.

### 3.6 Doeg’s observation and report

Doeg the Edomite witnesses or learns of the assistance given to David. In 1 Samuel 22:9–10 he reports to Saul that Ahimelech:

- inquired of God for David;
- gave David provisions;
- gave him Goliath the Philistine’s sword.

Saul then accuses Ahimelech of conspiracy. Ahimelech defends David’s loyalty and denies knowledge of a rebellion. Saul orders the priests killed; his guards refuse, but Doeg carries out the execution. Nob is then put to the sword.

The weapon thus becomes evidence in a political prosecution. The text does not say that Goliath’s sword itself was used in the massacre. “Put Nob to the sword” is a description of the mode of destruction, not an identification of a particular blade.

### 3.7 David’s flight to Gath

Immediately after receiving the sword, David flees to Achish, king of Gath. Gath is Goliath’s city. The narrative does not explicitly say that David displayed the sword there, but sequentially he has just received it and no surrender or concealment is narrated.

Its presence would make the journey particularly charged, and commentators have often emphasized the irony of David entering Goliath’s home city with Goliath’s sword. Because the text never names the sword in the Gath scene, physical transport into Gath remains a strong narrative inference rather than an independently stated custody event.

David feigns madness before Achish and escapes. The sword is not mentioned.

### 3.8 Disappearance from the record

After 1 Samuel 22:10, the sword vanishes from the biblical narrative.

It is not explicitly mentioned:

- at the Cave of Adullam;
- in Moab;
- in the forest of Hereth;
- at Keilah;
- in the wilderness of Ziph;
- at En-gedi;
- in David’s later Philistine service;
- at Ziklag;
- during David’s accession;
- in Jerusalem;
- in a royal treasury;
- among Solomon’s Temple furnishings;
- during the Babylonian conquest;
- in post-exilic inventories.

The chain therefore breaks completely while David is a fugitive. Statements that it became David’s royal sword, passed to Solomon, entered the Jerusalem Temple, was seized by Babylonians, or survived into later Jewish or Islamic custody are traditions or speculation, not continuations documented by Samuel.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### 4.1 Did the sword physically exist?

Three positions must be kept distinct.

#### A. Historical artifact remembered by the story

A historical duel or military event may have generated a remembered trophy. Ancient warfare included the stripping of an enemy’s weapons, decapitation, display of heads, and dedication of captured arms. The story’s sequence is culturally intelligible.

This establishes plausibility, not identity. No contemporary inscription, excavation, or external chronicle records the blade.

#### B. Literary object built from historical practices

The sword may be a narrative object assembled from conventional war-trophy motifs. It performs several literary tasks:

- turns Goliath’s own superiority against him;
- supplies David the blade he deliberately lacked;
- makes the enemy’s weapon the instrument of his defeat;
- materially links the Elah victory to David’s later flight;
- makes priestly assistance politically incriminating;
- marks David as the proper beneficiary of victory.

Under this model, asking for the metal or present location exceeds what the evidence can answer.

#### C. Legendary elaboration around an otherwise historical David

Even scholars accepting a historical David often treat the detailed duel as legendary or propagandistic. Yadin notes that Baruch Halpern, despite defending substantial historicity in the David tradition, classifies the Goliath combat as unhistorical. Yadin further argues that the extant narrative retrojects features of sixth-century BCE Palestine and Greek culture into an earlier setting.

Zorn counters that the equipment can fit an Iron Age I chariot-warrior context and need not demand a late date. The armament debate leaves the individual sword unverified.

### 4.2 David versus Elhanan

The largest identity problem is not a rival sword but a rival Goliath slayer.

- **1 Samuel 17:** David kills Goliath and takes his sword.
- **2 Samuel 21:19:** In the Masoretic Hebrew, Elhanan son of Jaare-oregim the Bethlehemite kills “Goliath the Gittite,” whose spear shaft is like a weaver’s beam.
- **1 Chronicles 20:5:** Elhanan son of Jair kills Lahmi, “the brother of Goliath the Gittite.”

Major scholarly reconstructions include:

1. **Elhanan priority:** The short notice about Elhanan preserves an older tradition; the exploit was later transferred to David. Chronicles then harmonized the contradiction by making Elhanan kill Goliath’s brother.
2. **Corruption in Samuel:** Chronicles preserves the better text, while scribal error caused “Lahmi, brother of Goliath” to become “the Bethlehemite … Goliath” in 2 Samuel.
3. **Separate traditions and progressive combination:** A story about an unnamed Philistine champion, an Elhanan-Goliath notice, and Davidic heroic tradition interacted through successive editorial stages.
4. **Traditional harmonization:** “Elhanan” is another name or epithet for David, or the two passages concern Goliath and his brother.

Yadin describes the priority of the Elhanan notice as widely accepted among critical scholars and sees the name “Goliath” as entering the David story from that tradition. Confessional commentators often prefer textual corruption or harmonization. The surviving Hebrew of 2 Samuel remains a genuine problem; the sword’s historical ownership depends on which reconstruction is accepted.

If Elhanan originally killed Goliath, the weapon’s assignment to David may be part of the same literary transfer of heroic credit. If 2 Samuel is corrupt, it has no effect on the sword’s Davidic provenance. The evidence does not permit recovery of an artifact independently of these texts.

### 4.3 Textual plurality in 1 Samuel 16–18

The Masoretic narrative contains duplications and tensions concerning:

- how David entered Saul’s service;
- whether Saul knew David;
- why David was at the battlefield;
- who introduced him after the victory;
- whether Goliath was killed by the sling or the sword.

The shorter Old Greek edition omits substantial material found in the Masoretic form. Tov argues that this is evidence for two literary editions. The sword-taking episode survives in the short form, making it comparatively well embedded in both major editions, but the historical identity of the slain warrior remains entangled with the Elhanan problem.

### 4.4 The Topkapı-associated *al-Battār*

A sword called *al-Battār* is circulated in Islamic relic literature and on modern websites as:

- one of Muhammad’s swords;
- spoil from the Jewish tribe Banū Qurayẓa;
- a sword previously belonging to biblical prophets;
- the sword David took from Goliath.

The reported physical object is approximately **101 cm long**. It bears Arabic writing naming David, Solomon, Moses, Aaron, Joshua, Zechariah, John, Jesus, and Muhammad, as well as a second inscription often called Nabataean. It is sometimes said to show David’s victory over Goliath.

The surviving object fails as a Bronze or Iron Age Israelite/Philistine artifact:

- its hilt has been classified as **Mamluk in type**, many centuries later than David;
- even proposals assigning the blade to the early Umayyad period place it roughly seventeen centuries after the traditional Davidic setting;
- hilt, blade, and inscriptions need not be contemporary;
- the alleged Nabataean inscription lacks a securely published translation and has been questioned as an antiquarian-looking addition;
- no continuous custody connects the biblical sanctuary at Nob to early Islam, Banū Qurayẓa, Damascus, the Ottoman treasury, or the present collection;
- the Qurʾan does not identify Goliath’s weapon with *al-Battār*;
- the seventeenth-century Ottoman inventory cited in scholarship for sacred swords lists weapons attributed to companions of Muhammad, but the published list does not establish a David–Goliath provenance for *al-Battār*.

A modern study of sacred-sword iconography cautiously suggests that the pseudo-archaic inscription may have been added to reinforce the blade’s antiquity. It describes the hilt as Mamluk in type and treats the Goliath connection as something “said,” not demonstrated. [Study of Islamic sword symbolism and *al-Battār*](https://katalog.marmara.edu.tr/veriler/yordambt/cokluortam/2B/62c419ece8f61.pdf)

Accordingly, *al-Battār* is significant as a **later claimant and relic tradition**, but it cannot responsibly be catalogued as the same object described in Samuel.

### 4.5 Scientific testing

No published radiocarbon, metallographic, isotopic, residue, or ancient-DNA test authenticates a sword as Goliath’s. Metal cannot ordinarily be radiocarbon-dated unless associated organic material survives, and even a correct Iron Age metallurgical date would not establish personal ownership without contextual evidence.

For *al-Battār*, published discussion is primarily typological, epigraphic, and art-historical. A full modern technical report specifying alloy composition, forging method, blade phases, inscriptions, restorations, and accession history was not located in the accessible evidence. Claims that scientific analysis proved it to be Goliath’s sword are unverified.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### 6.1 Battlefield function

Its primary narrated function is ordinary but decisive: it is Goliath’s close-combat blade. Goliath approaches with sword, spear, and *kîdôn*. After the sling strike, David uses the sword for close-range killing and decapitation.

The action is an example of enemy-weapon reversal: the defeated warrior’s own armament confirms his defeat. The narrative explicitly states that David previously had no sword in his hand.

### 6.2 Trophy and votive function

At Nob the sword is no longer in normal military storage. It is:

- wrapped;
- set apart;
- placed beside or behind an ephod;
- the only weapon of its kind immediately available.

This strongly suggests trophy or votive status. Josephus makes that implication explicit by saying David dedicated it to God. The biblical text itself does not use a technical verb for dedication, and no associated offering formula or inscription survives.

Ancient dedication of captured weapons in sanctuaries is historically plausible. In this particular case, however, “votive trophy” should be marked as a strong contextual inference confirmed only by a later retelling.

### 6.3 Oracular and sanctuary context

The sword’s placement behind the ephod ties it materially to a priestly and possibly oracular setting. In the next chapter Doeg says Ahimelech both inquired of God for David and gave him the sword. Saul treats the combined acts as political aid.

The weapon thus stands at the junction of:

- sacred custody;
- priestly consultation;
- battlefield victory;
- royal legitimacy;
- political rebellion.

Possession is not said to confer kingship automatically, but narratively it reinforces David’s identity as the divinely successful champion whom Saul cannot securely control.

### 6.4 “There is none like it”

David’s statement generated several symbolic readings:

- no sword equals it because it embodies YHWH’s victory;
- it is uniquely suited to David because he won it;
- it is physically exceptional;
- it is providentially available in an emergency;
- it foreshadows David’s continuing victories.

Only the statement itself is anciently attested. Its explanation is open.

### 6.5 Legitimation

The object forms a material bridge between David’s heroic victory and his fugitive career. When Ahimelech hands it over, a weapon formerly belonging to Israel’s enemy passes from sanctuary custody to the future king. Doeg’s report shows that Saul’s administration recognizes the political significance of supplying David with it.

The sword therefore functions as a **portable credential of heroic legitimacy**, although Samuel never formulates a rule that its holder is rightful king. Later Christian and artistic traditions further moralized it as the weapon by which humility destroys pride.

### 6.6 Taboos and access

No biblical rule states who may touch or view the sword. Its wrapping and sanctuary location imply restricted or careful storage but do not prove a taboo. Ahimelech releases it without a described purification rite.

Later art commonly shows it unwrapped, exposed, oversized, and freely handled. Those choices belong to iconography rather than ancient ritual evidence.

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### 7.1 The enemy slain by his own sword

**Tier:** Early attested biblical narrative.

David rejects Saul’s armor and carries no sword into the fight. Goliath’s own weapon then becomes the instrument of his killing and decapitation. The reversal is the central and oldest recoverable sword legend.

### 7.2 The dedicated trophy

**Tier:** Implicit in Samuel; explicit late-first-century elaboration.

Samuel places the wrapped sword at Nob without explaining how it arrived. Josephus supplies the missing action: David dedicated it to God. This became the standard traditional provenance and explains the weapon as sacred war spoil.

### 7.3 A uniquely powerful or peerless blade

**Tier:** Exegetical inference.

David says, “There is none like it.” Later readers often convert this into a claim of exceptional size, supernatural power, perfect craftsmanship, or unmatched lethality. None of those qualities is specified. The phrase may simply express David’s eagerness or recognition of the famous weapon.

### 7.4 David carries it back into Goliath’s city

**Tier:** Strong narrative inference and later dramatic elaboration.

David’s next destination is Gath. The irony of arriving with the city’s champion’s sword is powerful, but the text does not stage a recognition scene around the blade. Modern dramatizations frequently make the sword visible to Achish’s court; that is supplied theater.

### 7.5 A royal or dynastic sword

**Tier:** Later extrapolation.

The biblical record never says it became part of David’s regalia or passed to Solomon. Arguments that the sword subsequently entered a Davidic royal treasury depend on what “David’s possession” is imagined to mean after the narrative silence. There is no inventory link.

### 7.6 Identity with *al-Battār*

**Tier:** Later Islamic relic attribution; historically unsupported as object continuity.

The fullest modern form of the story says:

1. Goliath possessed *al-Battār*.
2. David took it after killing him.
3. It passed through prophets or Israelite custodians.
4. Muhammad acquired it as spoil from Banū Qurayẓa.
5. It eventually entered the Ottoman sacred relic collection.
6. It survives in Istanbul.

Every link beyond David’s battlefield capture is absent from Samuel. The Qurʾan does not name the sword. The surviving weapon’s early Islamic or medieval characteristics break the proposed Iron Age continuity. The tradition is important evidence for the expansion of the sword’s identity across Abrahamic sacred history, but not for the biblical artifact’s survival.

### 7.7 David as divinely instructed metalworker

**Tier:** Qurʾanic motif later conflated with sword lore.

Qurʾan 34 associates David with softened iron and armor manufacture. Later sacred-weapon stories sometimes make David the maker of named swords. Goliath’s sword, however, is already Goliath’s possession when David encounters him. Saying David forged it contradicts the simplest reading of Samuel unless an additional lost-and-recaptured provenance is invented.

### 7.8 The sword as a cross and moral emblem

**Tier:** Christian artistic interpretation.

Renaissance and Baroque artists frequently gave the weapon a conspicuous crossguard, allowing it to resemble the Christian cross. In some paintings its long blade is shouldered like a cross. Caravaggio’s Borghese version bears letters interpreted as abbreviating **humilitas occidit superbiam**, “humility kills pride.” This is not an ancient inscription on the sword; it is a Christian moral program attached to a painted representation.

## 8. Current Status and Location

### 8.1 Biblical object

As of September 2026, the sword described in Samuel has:

- no verified location;
- no verified remains;
- no accession number;
- no continuous provenance after David’s flight;
- no scientifically examined candidate accepted by archaeologists or textual historians.

“Lost” is conventional but slightly stronger than the evidence permits, because it presupposes a physical original. The strict status is:

> **A textually attested legendary or possibly historical object, disappearing from its narrative chain of custody after David receives it at Nob. No surviving artifact has been authenticated.**

### 8.2 The manuscript witness

The earliest material object directly preserving the sword tradition is not the sword but manuscript **4Q52**. Its fragments are under the authority of the Israel Antiquities Authority and digitally accessible through the Leon Levy Dead Sea Scrolls Digital Library. The manuscript provides evidence for the tradition, not the weapon.

### 8.3 *Al-Battār*

A sword presented in modern sacred-relic literature as *al-Battār* is generally associated with the Topkapı Palace sacred-arms tradition in Istanbul. Published descriptions report:

- total length: approximately 101 cm;
- straight blade;
- Arabic names of prophets and Muhammad;
- an additional allegedly Nabataean or archaizing inscription;
- a hilt of Mamluk type;
- non-contemporaneous components or decoration.

The exact public display status, gallery placement, and published Topkapı accession number could not be securely established from an authoritative current collection record. It should therefore not be stated unconditionally that visitors can presently see “Goliath’s sword” in a specified gallery.

The strongest responsible identification is:

> A later Islamic sacred-sword claimant, possibly composite and repeatedly modified, whose Goliath provenance is devotional or legendary and contradicted by its evident post-Iron-Age features.

## 9. Modern Reception

### 9.1 Visual art

The sword acquired a much more definite appearance in art than it ever possessed in scripture.

#### Donatello, Verrocchio, and Renaissance David imagery

Renaissance statues of David commonly place a sword in his hand or beside Goliath’s head. The blade helps identify the instant after decapitation. Artists frequently enlarge it to contrast David’s youth with the defeated warrior’s scale.

These works are representations, not copies based on an ancient relic.

#### Caravaggio

Caravaggio treated David and Goliath repeatedly around 1600–1610. His versions helped establish the modern image of David holding both Goliath’s head and a large sword. In the Borghese painting, letters on the painted blade are interpreted as **H-AS OS**, from *humilitas occidit superbiam*. The painting transforms the sword into a moralizing emblem and, through its cross-like form, a Christian typological device. [Galleria Borghese collection discussion](https://borghese.gallery/collection/paintings/david-with-the-head-of-goliath.html)

#### Aert de Gelder

Aert de Gelder’s **Ahimelech Giving the Sword of Goliath to David**, about the 1680s, is unusually important because it depicts not the duel but the sword’s custody transfer at Nob. The J. Paul Getty Museum catalogues it as oil on canvas, object **78.PA.219**, about 90 × 132 cm. [Getty collection authority record](https://www.getty.edu/cona/CONAFullSubject.aspx?subid=700002109)

The sword is rendered through seventeenth-century visual culture rather than Iron Age archaeology.

#### Small bronzes and replicas

The Fitzwilliam Museum holds a copper-alloy statuette, **David Drawing Goliath’s Sword**, accession **M.20-1980**, 23.1 cm high. The figure draws an exceptionally large sword from a sheath, preserving in miniature the early modern convention of the oversized blade. [Fitzwilliam Museum catalogue](https://data.fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/id/object/14001)

### 9.2 Political and military memorialization

The Machine Gun Corps Memorial at Hyde Park Corner, unveiled in 1925 and designed by Francis Derwent Wood, uses a nude David beside Goliath’s enormous sword. The biblical weapon is converted into a symbol of technologically enabled victory over a stronger opponent and a memorial to machine-gun troops killed in the First World War.

The appropriation is ironic: the memorial celebrates a modern projectile arm using a figure whose projectile victory is completed by a captured blade.

### 9.3 Literature, film, television, comics, and games

Modern retellings commonly make four changes:

1. the sword is made gigantic;
2. it is given bronze or iron material without qualification;
3. it becomes David’s continuing personal weapon;
4. it acquires a supernatural or dynastic identity.

Biblical films and illustrated Bibles often show David laboring to lift an enormous sword. Fantasy games use “Goliath’s Sword” as a generic name for oversized weapons or strength-enhancing items. These conventions derive from Goliath’s perceived gigantic stature and later fantasy vocabulary, not the measurements in Samuel.

The 2025 television series *House of David* renewed popular speculation about the sword’s manufacture, symbolism, and subsequent career. Online discussion often treats dramatic additions from the series as biblical details. They are modern narrative inventions unless independently present in ancient sources.

### 9.4 Replica and commercial culture

Commercial sword makers sell “David and Goliath swords,” wall hangings, costume props, and miniature letter openers. Their designs range from Bronze Age-inspired leaf blades to medieval arming swords and fantasy greatswords. No design has documentary authority as a replica.

Images of *al-Battār* are also reproduced commercially as “the actual sword of Goliath” or “one of the Prophet’s swords.” Such captions suppress the object’s composite chronology and legendary provenance.

### 9.5 “Goliath” as symbolic vocabulary

The phrase “David and Goliath” now denotes conflict between unequal opponents. In many political cartoons, David’s sling rather than the sword carries the metaphor. When the sword appears, it usually signifies:

- victory completed;
- the strong defeated by their own power;
- captured enemy technology;
- humility overcoming pride;
- justified resistance;
- or, in opposing propaganda, violence concealed beneath underdog imagery.

The symbolic object is therefore widely reproduced even when the physical sword is omitted from the verbal allusion.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 10.1 Premodern harmonization

Jewish and Christian commentators generally accepted a continuous narrative:

1. David took the sword at Elah.
2. He deposited or dedicated it at the sanctuary.
3. Ahimelech later returned it to him.
4. It thereafter remained with David.

Their main questions concerned why it was at Nob, what “behind the ephod” meant, and why David called it unique. Josephus’ dedication explanation became especially influential.

### 10.2 Nineteenth- and twentieth-century source criticism

Critical scholarship highlighted tensions between:

- 1 Samuel 16 and 17;
- the shorter Greek and longer Hebrew editions;
- David’s apparent deaths of Goliath in 1 Samuel;
- Elhanan’s killing of Goliath in 2 Samuel;
- Chronicles’ harmonizing “brother of Goliath.”

The sword became evidence within a broader debate about how the History of David’s Rise was composed. Because the sword appears both in the duel and in the Nob episode, it links textual units that some reconstructions otherwise separate.

### 10.3 Textual criticism after Qumran

The discovery of Samuel manuscripts at Qumran demonstrated that the medieval Masoretic form was not the only ancient Hebrew edition. 4Q52 physically attests the Nob passage in early Hellenistic Hebrew. 4QSamᵃ and other Samuel fragments also showed affinities with readings known from the Old Greek.

The central lesson is not that “the Dead Sea Scrolls proved the Bible unchanged.” Rather, they documented both stability and textual plurality. The sword at Nob is an old feature; the complete shape of the David narrative remained fluid.

### 10.4 Tov and the two editions

Emanuel Tov argued that the shorter Old Greek form of 1 Samuel 16–18 reflects an earlier Hebrew edition and that the Masoretic form contains later additions. His work remains foundational. Since the sword episode survives in the shorter edition, its literary antiquity is stronger than that of some omitted episodes, although that still does not establish the story’s historicity.

### 10.5 Archaeological weapon debate

The detailed armament has generated competing historical readings:

- Karl Galling and later Azzan Yadin stressed that Goliath’s equipment does not reproduce a single known Philistine kit and may be eclectic or Greek-influenced.
- Yadin argued that the story’s final form is late and best read in relation to Greek heroic literature.
- Jeffrey Zorn reconstructed Goliath as an elite Iron Age I chariot warrior and argued that the equipment does not require a late setting.
- Other studies parse the *kîdôn* as javelin, curved sword, or chariot weapon.

None can identify the separate *ḥerev* typologically with confidence.

### 10.6 The Tell es-Safi/Gath inscription

An Iron Age inscription found at Tell es-Safi, widely identified with Gath, contains names or name forms sometimes compared with “Goliath.” Its discovery showed that broadly comparable non-Semitic name forms circulated in Philistine Gath. Early publicity sometimes overstated it as evidence for the biblical Goliath.

Later philological treatment rejected a direct identification. The inscription does not name the biblical warrior and says nothing about a sword. It is contextual evidence for naming practices, not an ownership label.

### 10.7 The “Philistine sword” problem

Several archaeological blades have been labeled “Philistine” on typological or cultural grounds. A notable British Museum-associated sword traditionally linked with a “Shardanian” or Philistine type was re-examined technologically and typologically, illustrating the instability of ethnic labels applied to unprovenanced weapons. [Shalev, “Redating the ‘Philistine Sword’”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1468-0092.1988.tb00183.x)

Even an authentic Iron Age sword from Philistine territory would not become Goliath’s without a secure inscribed or archaeological chain.

### 10.8 Relic scholarship

Ottoman sacred-relic collections developed through conquest, gift, inheritance, ceremonial transfer, and dynastic claims. An official register dated 1 September 1686 lists a group of swords attributed to companions of Muhammad in the Privy Chamber. This is valuable evidence for Ottoman custody and classification, but it begins millennia too late to validate Davidic ownership. [Study citing the 1686 register](https://isamveri.org/pdfdrg/D247848/2014/2014_AGCAS.pdf)

Modern internet accounts frequently merge:

- traditional lists of Muhammad’s named swords;
- extant Ottoman relics;
- later images;
- Qurʾanic traditions about David’s metalworking;
- and the biblical sword of Goliath.

This composite story is not supported by a single early chain of evidence.

### 10.9 State of the primary evidence

The principal evidence consists of:

- Hebrew manuscript fragments, especially 4Q52;
- the Masoretic Hebrew tradition;
- the Old Greek text of 1 Reigns;
- 2 Samuel 21:19 and 1 Chronicles 20:5;
- Josephus;
- Pseudo-Philo;
- later translations and commentaries;
- Islamic sacred-weapon literature and Ottoman relic inventories;
- surviving artistic representations.

What is missing:

- contemporary Iron Age documentary reference;
- excavated sword linked to Goliath, David, Elah, or Nob;
- ancient inscription on the weapon;
- continuous custody after David;
- Temple inventory;
- Babylonian, Persian, Hellenistic, Roman, Byzantine, or early Islamic transfer record;
- reliable technical dossier authenticating *al-Battār* as an Iron Age object.

## 12. Sources

### Primary textual and manuscript sources

- **1 Samuel 17, 21–22, Hebrew and translation.** The foundational passages for capture, use, sanctuary storage, transfer, and Doeg’s report.
- **4QSamᵇ/4Q52.** Earliest surviving Hebrew manuscript witness to the Nob episode.
- **Old Greek 1 Reigns.** Ancient Greek witness to both the battlefield and Nob episodes.
- **2 Samuel 21:19 and 1 Chronicles 20:5.** Competing or harmonized traditions about Goliath’s slayer.
- **Josephus, *Jewish Antiquities* 6.** Earliest explicit statement that David dedicated the sword to God.
- **Pseudo-Philo, *Biblical Antiquities*.** Early expanded Jewish retelling.
- **Qurʾan 2:251.** Islamic scriptural reception of David’s killing of Jālūt, without a sword.
- **Ottoman Topkapı inventories.** Evidence for early modern custody of Islamic sacred swords, but not for an Iron Age chain.

### Principal modern studies

- Emanuel Tov, “The Composition of 1 Samuel 16–18 in Light of the Septuagint.”
- D. Barthélemy, D. W. Gooding, J. Lust, and E. Tov, *The Story of David and Goliath: Textual and Literary Criticism*.
- Azzan Yadin, “Goliath’s Armor and Israelite Collective Memory.”
- Jeffrey R. Zorn, “Reconsidering Goliath: An Iron Age I Philistine Chariot Warrior.”
- A. Kuruvilla, literary and military analysis of 1 Samuel 17.
- S. Shalev, technological and typological reassessment of the so-called Philistine sword.
- Studies of the Topkapı Sacred Trusts and Islamic sword symbolism used to assess *al-Battār*.

### Evidentiary conclusion

The fullest defensible reconstruction is short at its physical core and extensive in its textual afterlife. Samuel knows a sheathed sword carried by Goliath, seized and used by David, later wrapped and kept beside a cultic or priestly ephod at Nob, then released to David by Ahimelech. Doeg’s report makes that transfer part of the evidence in Saul’s destruction of Nob. The object then disappears.

The sword’s manufacture, metal, dimensions, later royal use, passage to Solomon or the Jerusalem Temple, survival through antiquity, and identity with *al-Battār* cannot be established. The *al-Battār* claimant belongs to a demonstrably later material and devotional history. What survives securely is not the blade but an early Hellenistic parchment witness, subsequent manuscripts, and an unusually rich succession of interpretations in which the weapon became trophy, relic, royal credential, moral allegory, monumental symbol, and fantasy greatsword.
