# Jose Marti's shackle-ring: Object Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

José Martí’s celebrated shackle-ring was a finger ring traditionally—and, in its basic outline, credibly—described as having been fashioned from part of the iron restraints Martí wore as a political prisoner in colonial Cuba. It bore the word **CUBA** and became a portable memorial of imprisonment and a declaration of allegiance to Cuban independence.

The original ring is lost. No museum presently claims to possess it; no accession number, measured catalogue record, laboratory examination, or uncontested post-1895 chain of custody is known. Its documented history therefore has two unequal halves:

1. A physical object attested by Martí’s correspondence, eyewitness descriptions, and images during his lifetime.
2. A missing relic whose supposed presence on Martí’s body at Dos Ríos and subsequent removal rest on retrospective testimony and inference.

The dossier distinguishes the following evidentiary classes:

- **Primary contemporary evidence:** Martí’s correspondence and images made during his lifetime.
- **Eyewitness retrospective testimony:** accounts by people who knew or saw Martí, written afterward.
- **Documented institutional research:** findings published by the Centro de Estudios Martianos, Fragua Martiana, museums, or archival researchers.
- **Institutional or national tradition:** propositions repeatedly asserted in Cuban commemorative literature but not always supported by a cited contemporary record.
- **Inference:** a conclusion consistent with available evidence but not directly recorded.
- **Legend or late elaboration:** symbolic or narrative material that cannot presently be traced to contemporary documentation.

A recurrent source of confusion must be identified at the outset. Martí preserved more than one iron object connected with his imprisonment. Miguel Tedín described both:

- a finger ring, which Tedín remembered as **silver**, engraved “Cuba”; and
- a “rough iron ring” from Martí’s prison restraint, kept as an ornament or relic on his office desk.

The second object was probably an actual shackle-ring or iron restraint component, not the wearable “Cuba” ring. Later retellings sometimes conflate the two.

## 1. Identification and Physical Description

### Names

The object appears in Spanish under several overlapping names:

- **sortija de hierro** — “iron finger ring”;
- **sortija Cuba** or **sortija “Cuba”** — “Cuba ring”;
- **anillo de hierro de Martí** — “Martí’s iron ring”;
- **anillo del grillete** — “ring of/from the shackle”;
- **anillo de la patria** — “ring of the homeland,” principally a commemorative or pedagogical name;
- **shackle-ring** — an English descriptive rendering rather than a demonstrated nineteenth-century title.

*Sortija* and *anillo* both mean “ring,” although *sortija* more specifically suggests a finger ornament. *Grillete* means a fetter or shackle, while *argolla* or *eslabón* may denote a ring or link belonging to the restraint. Sources alternate between saying that the ring came from an **eslabón de la cadena**, a chain link, and from a fragment of the **grillete**, the shackle assembly.

### Class

The lost object was simultaneously:

- a personal finger ring;
- a converted prison relic;
- an autobiographical memorial;
- a political emblem;
- and, in later Cuban reception, a quasi-sacred national relic.

It was not a formally canonized religious relic and is not known to have possessed any juridical or ceremonial authority.

### Physical form

The fullest retrospective description comes from Blanche Zacharie de Baralt’s *El Martí que yo conocí*, first published in 1945. She described:

- an iron finger ring;
- approximately **one centimetre wide**;
- made from a link of the chain Martí had worn in prison;
- with **CUBA** cut or carved in large letters.

Baralt’s language is compatible with a broad band or signet-like ring. A later investigation by Fragua Martiana museologist Regino Sánchez Landrián concluded from documentary and visual evidence that the letter-bearing portion was **quadrangular or rectangular**, not the rounded or oval “cigar-band” panel reproduced in some modern replicas. The finding was publicized in 2006, but no technical imaging report or measured reconstruction accompanied the newspaper account. It should therefore be treated as institutional visual interpretation rather than metrology of the lost original ([Juventud Rebelde, 2006](https://www.juventudrebelde.cu/cuba/2006-08-22/curiosos-hallazgos-sobre-la-sortija-de-marti)).

### Material dispute

Most retrospective sources identify the ring as **iron**, consistent with Martí’s own phrase *mi sortija de hierro*. That is strong evidence for the intended material identity.

Miguel Tedín, however, remembered that in early 1888 Martí wore “an anillo de plata” engraved with “Cuba.” His account first appeared in *La Nación* of Buenos Aires on 1 December 1909 and is reproduced in the Centro de Estudios Martianos collection *Yo conocí a Martí*. Tedín simultaneously described a separate, rough iron ring from Martí’s prison shackle on the office desk ([Centro de Estudios Martianos, *Yo conocí a Martí*](https://sos-de-fra-1.exo.io/patria-libros/books/2_Centro_de_Estudios_Martianos/Yo_conoci_a_Marti_2018/Yo_conoci_a_Marti_SIN_MARCAS.pdf)).

Several interpretations remain possible:

1. Tedín misremembered the dark or polished iron as silver.
2. Martí possessed a second “Cuba” ring in silver.
3. The finger ring incorporated iron with silver-colored finishing.
4. Later writers merged Tedín’s two objects.
5. Tedín’s use of *plata* was simply erroneous.

No materials analysis can resolve the matter because the object is missing.

A twentieth-century interpretive article asserts that “CUBA” appeared in gold, but the accessible primary and eyewitness descriptions do not securely establish gold inlay. This should not be repeated as physical fact without a supporting catalogue, photograph, or early testimony.

### Inscription

The only consistently reported inscription is:

> **CUBA**

Latin capital letters; no punctuation, date, or maker’s mark is recorded. Sources disagree about whether the word was on a rectangular external face, engraved into the band, or “inside” the ring. The images presently available online do not permit a dependable reading of the inscription itself.

### Finger and orientation

The strongest recurring tradition places the ring on Martí’s **left ring finger** (*dedo anular de la mano izquierda*). The 2006 Fragua Martiana account adopts that identification. Less careful popular accounts place it on the left index finger or even the right hand. The painted portrait and enlarged Key West photograph are better evidence than modern prose, but image reversal, reproduction quality, and pose should be considered before treating handedness as completely settled.

### Images

Two images are repeatedly cited as visual attestations:

- Herman or Herrman Norrman’s 1891 portrait of Martí, in which the ring is visible.
- A 1894 Key West photograph showing Martí with Fermín Valdés Domínguez and Francisco “Panchito” Gómez Toro. Regino Sánchez Landrián reported that the ring becomes clear in a 24 × 34 cm enlargement supplied by the Oficina de Asuntos Históricos del Consejo de Estado.

A seated photograph recovered or digitally restored at the Museo de Guanabacoa has also been reported as showing the ring clearly, though the online notice gives neither a catalogue number nor a complete photographic provenance ([Radio Florida report](https://floridadecuba.wordpress.com/2013/05/18/un-marti-considerado-inedito-en-el-museo-de-guanabacoa/)).

### Dimensions, weight, construction, and condition

Known or reported:

- Width: approximately 1 cm, according to Baralt.
- Base material: iron, according to Martí’s correspondence and the dominant tradition.
- Form: broad finger ring with a rectangular inscription-bearing section, according to later visual research.
- Inscription: CUBA.

Unknown:

- internal diameter and ring size;
- weight;
- exact iron composition;
- whether the metal was a chain link, shackle ring, or another restraint component;
- forging, welding, casting, or soldering method;
- presence of gold, silver, plating, or inlay;
- exact letter style;
- maker’s marks;
- repairs or resizing;
- corrosion state in 1895;
- present condition.

## 2. Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation

### Material prehistory: Martí’s imprisonment

Martí was arrested in Havana in October 1869, prosecuted for political disloyalty, and sentenced in 1870 to imprisonment with forced labor. He worked in the San Lázaro quarries wearing prison restraints. The iron injured him and became one of the defining physical memories of his youth. His 1871 polemic *El presidio político en Cuba* documents the penal system and its cruelties, although it is not by itself evidence that a finger ring had yet been made.

The preserved prison shackle now associated with the Museo Casa Natal de José Martí has its own custody history: Cuban institutional accounts say Martí retained it, later left it with Carmen Miyares and her family in New York in 1895, and that María Mantilla donated it to Cuba in 1953. That shackle is not the lost “CUBA” finger ring. Its survival does, however, demonstrate Martí’s deliberate preservation of prison iron as a personal and political relic ([Juventud Rebelde, “Fragua de espíritus”](https://www.juventudrebelde.cu/cuba/2012-01-26/fragua-de-espiritus)).

### Commission

The earliest secure textual evidence for the finger ring is Martí’s own undated or editorially dated correspondence to **Gabriel de Zéndegui**:

> “regaña a Agustín, porque no me ha querido hacer mi sortija de hierro, que es la única que ajustará bien a mi dedo”

Translation:

> “scold Agustín, because he has not wanted to make my iron ring, which is the only one that will fit my finger well.”

The letter is reproduced in Martí’s *Epistolario*, with an editorial note identifying it as the ring made from part of the restraints he had dragged in political prison and wore until his death ([Martí, *Epistolario*](https://biblioteca.clacso.edu.ar/Cuba/cem-cu/20150114054555/Vol20.pdf)).

The wording establishes:

- Martí requested an iron ring;
- Agustín de Zéndegui was expected to make it;
- Martí attached personal significance to its “fit.”

It does **not**, in the quoted passage alone, expressly say that the supplied metal came from his shackles or that “CUBA” was to be inscribed. Those details come from annotations, later witnesses, and institutional reconstruction.

### Maker and place

Cuban research identifies the maker as **Agustín de Zéndegui**, brother of Gabriel de Zéndegui. He is described as a poet or writer and an amateur or self-taught jeweler rather than as the proprietor of a documented commercial workshop.

The prevailing reconstruction is:

- In Havana in 1878, Martí gave Agustín a link or fragment of his prison chain.
- The work was performed or arranged in connection with the Zéndegui family’s quinta in El Cerro, a site of literary gatherings.
- Agustín made the ring but did not promptly deliver it.

No surviving commission, workshop invoice, assay, drawing, or receipt has been identified. “1878” is an institutional reconstruction repeated by Fragua Martiana and *Juventud Rebelde*, not a date engraved on the object.

### Delivery

Martí’s mother, **Leonor Pérez Cabrera**, traveled to New York in November 1887. Baralt says she gave her son the ring on arrival. A later Cuban anecdotal compilation dates the gift to **22 November 1887**, while another modern article gives **17 November**. The difference is unresolved; the exact day should not be stated without qualification ([*Entre espinas, flores: Anecdotario martiano*](https://sos-de-fra-1.exo.io/patria-libros/books/23_Oficina_de_Asuntos_Historicos/Entre_espinas_flores_Anecdotario_Martiano.pdf)).

Baralt, writing decades afterward, reported that Leonor placed it on Martí’s finger and that someone told her he said:

> “Ahora que tengo una sortija de hierro tengo que hacer obras férreas.”

“Now that I have an iron ring, I must perform iron deeds.”

Baralt explicitly introduced the words as hearsay—*Me cuentan que dijo*. The statement is therefore an early-twentieth-century remembered attribution, not a surviving contemporary quotation.

## 3. Provenance and Custody History

### 1870–1871: prison iron

**Documented context:** Martí wore iron restraints while imprisoned and at forced labor in Havana.

**Traditional object link:** A link or fragment was saved and later converted into the ring. The precise moment at which Martí or his family recovered the metal is not documented in the accessible sources.

### 1871–1878: preserved fragment

The future ring’s raw material was reportedly retained by Martí or his family after his deportation to Spain. No continuous inventory survives for these years. The place where the fragment was kept—Havana, Madrid, or within family effects—is not established.

### 1878: transfer to Agustín de Zéndegui

**Institutional reconstruction:** During Martí’s return to Havana, he delivered a piece of prison iron to Agustín de Zéndegui for transformation into a finger ring. Martí’s letter to Gabriel corroborates the commission but, in the quoted form, not every material detail.

Custody passed temporarily to Agustín as artisan. No completion date is known.

### 1878–1887: the nine-year gap

Later accounts say nine years elapsed before Martí received the ring. Its location during that interval is unknown. Plausible custodians include Agustín de Zéndegui, Gabriel de Zéndegui, or Martí’s family, but no source currently establishes the successive hands.

### November 1887: Leonor Pérez to José Martí

**Eyewitness-based tradition:** Leonor carried the completed ring from Cuba to New York and gave it to Martí. Baralt’s narrative is the fullest source, though published in 1945.

The mechanism was a maternal gift or delivery. It is unclear whether Leonor legally owned the object, merely transported it, or was returning property commissioned by Martí.

### 1887–1895: Martí’s possession

The ring is attested in Martí’s possession through:

- his own prior correspondence requesting it;
- Tedín’s account of seeing a “Cuba” finger ring in early 1888, although he called it silver;
- Norrman’s 1891 portrait;
- the 1894 Key West photograph;
- Baralt’s recollection that Martí did not part with it.

During this period it traveled with Martí within the United States and probably on his revolutionary journeys. A city-by-city itinerary cannot be safely equated with the ring’s itinerary, because no source states that he wore it on every trip.

### January–April 1895: departure for the Cuban expedition

The traditional chain assumes Martí carried the ring from New York through the Dominican Republic and Haiti and during the expedition that landed at Playitas de Cajobabo on 11 April 1895. No embarkation inventory or diary entry specifically names it.

Its continued presence is plausible from the wear tradition but remains an inference.

### 19 May 1895: Dos Ríos

Martí was killed during the fighting at Dos Ríos. Spanish troops obtained his body and possessions.

Later literature frequently states as fact that the ring was on his finger and was looted. The best direct testimony is less definite. Colonel José Ximénez de Sandoval later wrote to Gonzalo de Quesada y Aróstegui:

> “Respecto a la sortija de hierro que dice llevaba Martí, debió serle quitada cuando le despojaron del revólver, reloj, cinto, polainas, zapatos y papeles…”

Translation:

> “As for the iron ring which you say Martí wore, it must have been taken from him when they stripped him of the revolver, watch, belt, gaiters, shoes, and papers…”

A fuller reported version adds that when Sandoval encountered and ordered the body searched, without dismounting, he found money, an emblem, Clemencia Gómez’s letter and ribbon, and a pocket case—but not the ring.

This testimony establishes:

- Quesada believed or had information that Martí wore the ring.
- Sandoval did not personally inventory it.
- Sandoval inferred that someone had removed it earlier.
- The identity of that person was unknown to him or unstated.

It does **not** establish who took it, whether removal happened before or after the body entered Spanish custody, or whether Martí had actually worn it that day.

### 19–20 May 1895: probable disappearance

The most defensible reconstruction is that the ring disappeared between Martí’s fall and Sandoval’s examination of the body. Possible actors include:

- Spanish soldiers who first reached or searched the body;
- irregular auxiliaries or guides;
- persons who removed other effects;
- conceivably, although without affirmative evidence, someone on the Cuban side before final loss of the body.

Calling the event “theft” or “looting” is reasonable if the ring was removed without lawful authority as personal spoil. The documentary record does not name a thief.

### After 1895

No reliable sighting, sale, inheritance, donation, police recovery, museum accession, or scientific identification of the original has surfaced. Its chain of custody ends at Dos Ríos.

Sandoval retained or transferred other objects. In 1913 he delivered several Martí-associated effects to the Cuban legation in Madrid, including a bloodstained pocketknife, spectacles, a small manuscript, Clemencia Gómez’s blue ribbon, and an emblem associated with Carlos Manuel de Céspedes. The surviving list does not include the ring. This negative evidence reinforces its earlier disappearance but does not reveal its fate.

## 4. Authenticity and Identity Disputes

### Did the ring exist?

**Conclusion: yes, with high confidence.**

Martí’s own request for *mi sortija de hierro*, Tedín’s sighting of a “Cuba” ring, multiple retrospective witnesses, and visual evidence collectively establish a real wearable object. The symbolic story did not originate wholly after Martí’s death.

### Was it made from his prison restraints?

**Conclusion: probable, but not demonstrated by scientific analysis.**

The attribution is repeated by witnesses and Martí specialists and coheres with the separate preservation of his prison shackle. No metallurgical comparison has been performed between the lost ring and the surviving restraint. The proposition rests on provenance testimony, not laboratory proof.

### Iron or silver?

This is the most important material contradiction.

- Martí called the commissioned object an **iron ring**.
- Baralt and the dominant tradition call it iron.
- Tedín called the worn “Cuba” ring **silver**.

Because Tedín also saw a rough iron restraint-ring on the desk, his description may indicate two distinct objects. Martí’s first-person wording gives iron the stronger evidentiary position, but Tedín’s testimony prevents categorical dismissal of a second ring or a silver-colored finish.

### One ring or two?

At least two physical iron-associated objects must be distinguished:

1. The wearable ring inscribed CUBA.
2. A rough iron ring or shackle component displayed on Martí’s desk.

Tedín’s reference to a silver finger ring potentially adds a third object, though this may be simple material misidentification.

### Was “CUBA” in gold?

Some secondary interpretations state that gold letters were set into the iron. The claim is not securely supported by the principal evidence reviewed here. It remains unverified.

### Did Martí wear it continuously?

Baralt said he “never separated himself from it.” Images show it on particular occasions, not every day. “Always” or “until his death” is biographical tradition, not continuous documentation.

### Was it on the corpse?

Ximénez de Sandoval’s phrasing is expressly conditional: “the ring which you say Martí wore” and “must have been taken.” There is no presently identified first-search inventory listing it. Its presence at Dos Ríos is likely but not conclusively documented.

### Do any surviving replicas have a claim to be the original?

No. The twentieth- and twenty-first-century rings discussed below are acknowledged commemorative replicas. Their differences in metal and design are not evidence of competing authenticity claims.

### Scientific examination

None is possible on the missing original. No published metallography, X-ray fluorescence, microscopy, dimensional scan, or comparison with Martí’s surviving shackles is known.

## 5. Associated Persons, Networks, and Institutions

- **José Julián Martí Pérez (1853–1895):** commissioner, wearer, and principal owner. His letter supplies the earliest secure textual attestation.

- **Leonor Pérez Cabrera:** Martí’s mother and reported courier or giver. She carried the completed ring to New York in November 1887.

- **Agustín de Zéndegui:** reported maker. Martí’s letter identifies him as the person expected to make the iron ring.

- **Gabriel de Zéndegui:** intermediary and recipient of Martí’s request that Agustín finish the commission.

- **Miguel Tedín:** eyewitness in early 1888. He described a silver “Cuba” finger ring and a separate rough iron prison ring on Martí’s desk. His evidence is indispensable precisely because it complicates the later standardized account.

- **Blanche Zacharie de Baralt:** knew Martí in New York and supplied the fullest later physical and symbolic description. She also asked publicly what became of the ring.

- **Herman/Herrman Norrman:** Scandinavian artist whose 1891 portrait depicts the ring.

- **Fermín Valdés Domínguez and Panchito Gómez Toro:** appear with Martí in the 1894 Key West photograph used to reconstruct the ring’s appearance. Their connection is pictorial, not custodial.

- **José Ximénez de Sandoval y Bellange:** Spanish commander at Dos Ríos. He did not claim to have possessed the ring; his later letter inferred that it was removed before his examination.

- **Gonzalo de Quesada y Aróstegui:** Martí’s literary executor and the inquirer to whom Sandoval discussed the missing ring.

- **Carmen Miyares and María Mantilla:** custodians of Martí’s separate surviving prison shackle, not documented custodians of the finger ring.

- **Fragua Martiana:** Cuban memorial and educational institution built around the former San Lázaro quarry area. It researched the ring’s visual form and developed the *Sortija Cuba* educational project.

- **Centro de Estudios Martianos:** editor and repository of Martí scholarship, letters, and witness testimonies.

- **Sociedad Cultural José Martí:** promoter of commemorative replicas and their use as awards.

- **Asociación Cubana de Artesanos Artistas, Matanzas branch:** participating institutional network in the 2009 silver replica project.

- **Museo Casa Natal de José Martí:** holds related Martí relics, including prison restraints and objects associated with Dos Ríos, but not the lost original ring.

- **Spanish colonial prison administration:** the institutional source of the iron, assuming the accepted material provenance.

- **Partido Revolucionario Cubano and Cuban émigré communities:** the political network in which the ring acquired public visibility as Martí’s personal emblem.

## 6. Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function

### Martí’s personal function

The ring joined pain, memory, and political obligation in one object. If manufactured from his restraint, colonial penal iron was literally reworked into an emblem naming the nation for whose independence he labored.

The attributed phrase about performing *obras férreas* makes a pun on:

- *hierro* — iron;
- *férreo* — ironlike, unyielding, resolute;
- and the moral work expected of the wearer.

Because Baralt introduced the statement as something told to her, it should be classified as an early retrospective tradition, not a verbatim contemporary record.

### “Marriage” to Cuba

Baralt interpreted Leonor’s placement of the ring as Martí’s symbolic marriage to the homeland, comparing it to the Venetian doge’s ritual marriage with the Adriatic. She expressly presented this as symbolism. It is not evidence that Martí conducted a formal oath or invented a documented rite.

Later writers expanded the analogy into claims that the ring was Martí’s “true wedding ring.” These are literary and nationalist interpretations, not legal or ceremonial facts.

### Amulet

Tedín said the rough prison ring on Martí’s desk had been given by his mother to serve as an “amulet” in his pilgrimage for Cuban freedom. The syntax of his account attaches this description to the rough desk object, not unambiguously to the CUBA finger ring. Later accounts frequently transfer the amulet function to the wearable ring.

No specific supernatural protection, cure, curse, or miracle is contemporaneously attributed to it.

### Political emblem

Unlike a seal, the ring did not authenticate documents. Its political efficacy was visual and autobiographical: the word CUBA was worn on Martí’s body, while its material recalled colonial imprisonment. Possessing it did not confer office in the Partido Revolucionario Cubano.

### Modern ceremony

Replicas have been awarded to Cuban schoolchildren and young people for study of Martí and civic distinction. Here the ring’s function changed from an individual private emblem to a repeatable initiatory or honorific token. The recipient is symbolically charged to emulate the attributed “iron deeds.”

## 7. Myths and Legends Attached to the Object

### The iron-vow saying

**Tier:** retrospective attributed saying.

The standard story relates that upon receiving the ring Martí declared that, now possessing an iron ring, he must perform iron deeds. Its earliest source has not been securely placed in 1887; Baralt recorded it in 1945 as hearsay.

### Marriage to the homeland

**Tier:** Baralt’s documented literary interpretation, later national tradition.

The ring becomes Martí’s wedding band with Cuba. Later criticism elaborated the image into a mystical nuptial union, sometimes contrasting it with his marriage to Carmen Zayas Bazán. That development belongs to Martí reception, not the custody history of the artifact.

### He never removed it

**Tier:** eyewitness-based tradition, incompletely verifiable.

Baralt said it was never separated from him after 1887. Photographs corroborate recurrent use but cannot establish uninterrupted wear.

### It disappeared at the instant of martyrdom

**Tier:** compressed modern retelling.

The object likely disappeared during the stripping or searching of Martí’s corpse, but the exact instant and agent are unknown. “Disappeared on 19 May 1895” is a useful chronological shorthand, not a witnessed event.

### A Spanish soldier took it

**Tier:** plausible but unverified inference.

Spanish troops held the body and other possessions were distributed or transferred. Sandoval believed the ring “must have” been removed during the stripping. No soldier has been identified, and no descendant’s collection has been documented.

### Secret survival

**Tier:** unresolved possibility, not an attested provenance.

The ring could survive unidentified in a private collection, or it may have been discarded, melted, corroded, or passed through heirs who lost its history. No serious candidate has been published with a chain of custody.

### Masonic meanings

Modern essays connect the ring’s iron, geometry, inscription, and Martí’s reported Masonic affiliations to esoteric symbolism. These interpretations are not supported by a documented lodge ritual involving the ring, a statement by the maker, or a contemporary explanation by Martí. They belong to modern symbolic exegesis.

## 8. Current Status and Location

**Status:** lost since 19 May 1895, or shortly thereafter.

**Last probable location:** the Dos Ríos–Remanganaguas zone of eastern Cuba, in or among the effects removed from Martí’s corpse under Spanish military control.

**Present location:** unknown.

**Legal control:** none presently enforceable over an identified object. If discovered, ownership could involve questions of personal inheritance, Cuban patrimonial law, military spoil, archaeological context, and national cultural heritage. No active proceeding concerning a located claimant is known.

**Museum status:** no accession number for the original has been published because no museum is known to hold it.

**Condition:** unknown. If the object survives and was substantially iron, corrosion would depend upon storage. If melted or materially altered, visual identification might be impossible without exceptional documentary provenance.

**Authentication requirements for any future claimant:**

1. a custody history reaching back toward Spanish participants at Dos Ríos;
2. comparison with period photographs and Norrman’s portrait;
3. dimensional and manufacturing analysis;
4. metallurgical comparison with Martí’s surviving prison restraints, while recognizing that matching alloy would not alone establish identity;
5. examination of the CUBA inscription’s tooling and corrosion;
6. archival research into Spanish military family papers, Martí’s estate, and early Cuban museum correspondence.

No such candidate has yet passed these tests.

## 9. Modern Reception

### Educational replicas

By the early twenty-first century, Cuban educational projects were distributing replicas to children completing Martí-related study. Some replicas incorrectly used a rounded or oval inscription panel.

In 2009, twelve Matanzas artisans produced **100 silver rings** for the Sociedad Cultural José Martí. The project involved:

- 480 grams of donated silver;
- three months of work;
- molding, casting, and polishing;
- supervision by jewelry instructor Jorge Rodríguez;
- participation by the Matanzas branch of the Asociación Cubana de Artesanos Artistas;
- presentation to children and young people distinguished for engagement with Martí.

The artisans knowingly used silver rather than iron for durability and adjustability as children grew. The report candidly stated that their documentary and photographic search had been inconclusive. These objects are commemorative interpretations, not facsimiles in material or necessarily in geometry ([Juventud Rebelde, 2009](https://www.juventudrebelde.cu/cuba/2009-04-25/artesanos-matanceros-reproducen-anillo-de-jose-marti)).

### Institutional replicas

The Museo del Tabaco in Havana reports possessing a replica among honors connected with Martí and Cuban tobacco culture. Its display history demonstrates how the ring’s image migrated beyond Martí museums into institutions representing Cuban national culture ([Excelencias Cuba](https://www.excelenciascuba.com/generales/museo-del-tabaco-en-la-habana-vieja-33-anos-mostrando-cubania)).

### Literature and scholarship

The ring has become a recurring figure in Martí biography and criticism:

- a condensed emblem of *El presidio político en Cuba*;
- a “wedding ring” linking Martí to Cuba;
- evidence of his construction of personal symbols;
- and a central image in mystical or archetypal readings.

The 1969 *Anuario Martiano* published an interpretation likening Martí to an initiate who died and was reborn through prison, with the iron ring functioning as a nuptial emblem joining him to Cuba ([*Anuario Martiano* 1](https://metro.inter.edu/cai/jose_marti/anuario_1969-1977/AnuarioM01.pdf)).

A later essay, “El Anillo de Hierro: Apuntes para una mística martiana,” extended the esoteric reading while acknowledging the ring’s unresolved fate. Its symbolic claims should be separated from its useful compilation of provenance traditions ([ResearchGate text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/328914825_El_Anillo_de_Hierro_Apuntes_para_una_mistica_martiana)).

### Theater

The stage work *Hierro*, by Carlos Celdrán, uses the ring and the prison iron as part of a deliberate demythologizing exploration of Martí. In publicity surrounding the production, Celdrán repeated the tradition that Martí carried the ring into the Cuban campaign and that nobody knows what became of it ([Artburst Miami](https://www.artburstmiami.com/articulos-en-espanol/hierro-desmitificacion-de-jose-marti-en-el-black-box-del-miami-dade-county-auditorium)).

### National political symbolism

The ring is repeatedly invoked in Cuban public writing as the material conversion of colonial suffering into revolutionary duty. This reception is political and pedagogical, but it does not render the underlying physical history fictitious. The original object and its later ideological uses must be studied separately.

### Commercial and informal copies

Silver versions and privately held school replicas circulate outside their original award context. They can usually be distinguished by modern workmanship, their acknowledged silver composition, and lack of pre-1895 provenance. No documented forgery marketed as the original has been identified in the sources reviewed.

## 10. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Source structure

The evidence is unusually narrow:

1. Martí’s correspondence establishes a requested iron ring and names the intended maker.
2. Tedín’s 1909 testimony records a CUBA finger ring but calls it silver.
3. Visual works from 1891 and 1894 document that Martí wore a broad ring.
4. Baralt’s 1945 memoir supplies the standard origin, measurements, maternal delivery, symbolic marriage, attributed saying, and lost-object question.
5. Sandoval’s retrospective letter establishes only an inference about removal at Dos Ríos.
6. Later institutional scholarship standardized the 1878 manufacture and 1887 delivery narrative.
7. Modern newspaper and commemorative writing frequently converts probabilities into certainties.

### Major unresolved questions

- What is the exact date of Martí’s letter to Gabriel de Zéndegui?
- Did Martí personally give Agustín the iron in 1878?
- What precise restraint component supplied the metal?
- Was the completed finger ring iron, silver, plated iron, or one of two rings?
- Were the letters CUBA incised, raised, or inlaid?
- Was the inscription on the exterior or interior?
- Which finger did Martí ordinarily use?
- Did Leonor deliver it on 17 or 22 November 1887?
- Is the “iron deeds” phrase traceable earlier than Baralt?
- Was the ring on Martí when he fell?
- Who removed it, and what happened afterward?

### Historiographical cautions

The ring’s origin story is strong enough to be treated as historical tradition grounded in primary evidence, but not every familiar detail has equal standing. In particular:

- Martí’s phrase *sortija de hierro* is primary.
- Agustín’s role is strongly supported by that letter.
- The shackle-metal origin is traditional and highly plausible but scientifically unverified.
- Leonor’s delivery is an eyewitness-circle recollection.
- Continuous wear is an overstatement of intermittent visual evidence.
- Presence at Dos Ríos is probable, not inventoried fact.
- Removal by a named Spanish soldier is unsupported.
- Gold lettering and mystical coding are later claims.

### Archives and further research

A definitive study would require consultation of:

- the manuscript and editorial apparatus of Martí’s Zéndegui correspondence;
- Gonzalo de Quesada y Aróstegui’s papers;
- the original Ximénez de Sandoval letter and any preceding inquiry;
- 1895 Spanish military inventories and unit papers;
- Martí family correspondence concerning Leonor’s 1887 journey;
- the Zéndegui family archive;
- original prints or negatives of the 1894 Key West photograph;
- the Norrman portrait’s conservation and provenance records;
- accession and donor files for Martí’s surviving shackles;
- the 1913 Cuban-legation transfer act;
- records of early Martí exhibitions and the 1925 Casa Natal museum inauguration.

No published catalogue raisonné devoted solely to the ring is known from the reviewed material.

## 12. Sources

### Primary and near-primary evidence

- José Martí, letter to Gabriel de Zéndegui, reproduced in *Epistolario*, containing the request for *mi sortija de hierro*.
- Miguel Tedín, “José Martí,” originally published in *La Nación*, Buenos Aires, 1 December 1909; reproduced by the Centro de Estudios Martianos in *Yo conocí a Martí*.
- Blanche Zacharie de Baralt, *El Martí que yo conocí*, first published 1945.
- José Ximénez de Sandoval, retrospective correspondence to Gonzalo de Quesada y Aróstegui concerning Martí’s possessions; quoted in later historical studies.
- Norrman’s 1891 portrait of Martí.
- Key West group photograph, 1894.

### Institutional and scholarly sources

- Centro de Estudios Martianos editions of Martí’s correspondence and witness testimonies.
- *Anuario Martiano* and *Anuario del Centro de Estudios Martianos*.
- Fragua Martiana research reported by Luis Hernández Serrano in 2006.
- 2009 documentation of the Matanzas replica project.
- Cuban archival reporting concerning the 1913 return of Martí-related objects.
- Modern analytical study “El Anillo de Hierro: Apuntes para una mística martiana.”

### Source assessment

The strongest sources prove that an iron ring was commissioned and that Martí wore a CUBA ring. They do not furnish a complete nineteenth-century manufacture record or postmortem inventory. The complete familiar narrative—shackle link, Agustín’s workmanship, Leonor’s gift, iron-deeds pronouncement, uninterrupted wear, and battlefield theft—is a composite assembled from evidence of different dates and strengths. Its central outline is credible; its individual links must not be presented as uniformly documented.
