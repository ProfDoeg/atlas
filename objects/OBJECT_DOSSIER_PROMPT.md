# OBJECT_DOSSIER_PROMPT.md

Standing prompt for commissioning a sacred-object research dossier — specific, named, real or legendary physical objects: regalia, relics, weapons, textiles, banners, national and civic symbols, and particular artworks or monuments with independent legend status (a specific named object qualifies; Mjolnir qualifies, "hammers" in general does not, though the Sudarshana Chakra does and so does one particular bronze dog in Recoleta). Prose Markdown, stage one of the pipeline (dossier first; any structured extraction comes later as a separate step). Sibling to the person prompt at `working/journeys/dossiers/PROMPT_TEMPLATE.md`, the network prompt at `working/networks/NETWORK_DOSSIER_PROMPT.md`, and the pantheon prompt at `working/pantheon/GOD_DOSSIER_PROMPT.md`. Drafted by Fable 5, 2026-09-06.

---

[OBJECT/SYMBOL NAME] ([NATURE, e.g. "a legendary sword" or "a real 3rd-century BCE inscribed stela"])
[ONE-LINE DESCRIPTION]

Please conduct a comprehensive, source-driven research dossier on this object. I want the fullest reconstructable history of the object itself — not of its owners, though they will crowd the page, but of the thing: what it is or was, what it is made of, where it has physically been, whose hands it has passed through, what has been done to it, with it, and in its name, from its creation or earliest attestation to the present moment. An object has no birth and death in the human sense, but it has something just as reconstructable: a documented origin (a quarry, a forge, a loom, a commission, a decree) or, for a purely legendary object, an earliest textual attestation; and then a chain of custody — ownership, transfer, gift, inheritance, theft, loss, recovery, destruction, dismemberment, restoration, forgery, and dispute — that is the object's actual life. Reconstruct both the thing and the chain.

Objects of this kind range from securely documented artifacts with museum accession numbers to things that never physically existed at all, with every gradation between: real objects wrapped in heavy legend, legendary objects with a real candidate on display, lost objects with rival claimants, destroyed objects surviving only in drawings and inventories. The evidentiary discipline must therefore carry an extra question the person dossier never faces: not only "did this event happen" but "does, or did, this object exist — and is the object now bearing the name the same object the sources describe?" Never assume the answer in either direction. For a real artifact, document its physical existence like an archivist: where it is, how it is catalogued, who has examined it, what scientific analysis (dating, materials, DNA, provenance research) has been performed and what it found. For a legendary object, treat the earliest attested source exactly as the pantheon dossier treats a god's first inscription: what the oldest datable text or image actually says, in what language, dated how, and how the object's description, powers, and story changed across later sources — the variants are the history. For the many objects in between, keep the physical thing and the legend about it visibly distinct on the page while giving both full weight. Throughout, distinguish clearly: documented fact, scientific finding, plausible scholarly inference, institutional tradition, pious or national tradition, allegation, disputed claim, unverified claim, propaganda, legend, myth, modern invention (including material that circulates as "ancient" but is demonstrably recent, with the inventor named where known), and unresolved question. Where sources disagree — on origin, on materials, on whether the object survived a fire or a revolution, on which of two rival objects is "the real one" — give the competing versions with names, dates, and sources rather than silently choosing. Do not sanitize inconvenient material: loot is loot, colonial seizure is colonial seizure, a forged relic is a forgery, a political theft is a theft; state what is documented plainly, and what is alleged as alleged. Mark anything that cannot be established explicitly.

Organize the dossier with sections along the following lines, adapting as the object's actual character requires:

1. **Identification and Physical Description.** What the object is, in one dense orienting passage; its class (crown, sword, relic, banner, stela, cult image, manuscript, monument, embalmed body, seal, emblem); its names, titles, and epithets in every relevant language and script, with transliterations and etymologies including disputed ones, and how the name itself has shifted over time. Then the fullest available physical description: materials, dimensions, weight, construction technique, inscriptions (with text, translation, and script), decoration, maker's marks, damage, repairs, alterations, and current condition; what survives versus what is lost, destroyed, replaced, or disputed (a stone missing its fragments, a sword missing its scabbard, a regalia set dispersed). For a purely legendary object, give the description the sources give — in full, by source, with each source's date — and note where later tradition or modern art supplied details the early texts lack. For an object that exists in multiple copies, versions, or claimants, enumerate them here and say which one the dossier tracks.

2. **Origin, Manufacture, and Earliest Attestation.** For a real object: when, where, by whom, for whom, and why it was made — the commission, the workshop, the materials' own provenance where known (the quarry, the mine, the mint), the documented cost, and the evidence for all of this (contracts, inventories, chronicles, scientific dating), including any scholarly dispute over the date or attribution. For a legendary object: the oldest securely datable text, image, or inscription in which it appears, with what exactly that earliest evidence does and does not say, and then the growth of the origin story across later sources — who forged it in the myth, from what, and how that account varies. For the frequent hybrid case — a real object with a legendary origin (a shield fallen from heaven, oil brought by a dove, a stone from Eden) — present both lineages side by side and clearly labeled: the documented earliest appearance of the physical thing, and the traditional origin story with its own earliest attestation, who circulated it, and what work the legend did for whoever held the object.

3. **Provenance and Custody History.** The spine of the dossier. A chronological chain-of-custody narrative from origin or first attestation to the present: every documented owner, keeper, guardian institution, and place of residence; every transfer, with its mechanism — gift, sale, dowry, inheritance, bequest, tribute, conquest, confiscation, colonial seizure, theft, ransom, restitution — its date, its price where recorded, and its evidence; every loss, disappearance, and recovery; every theft in full detail (who took it, how, what happened to the thieves, how and whether it came back); every destruction, partial destruction, dismemberment, melting-down, burial, hiding, and exhumation; wartime evacuations and concealments; museum accessions, deaccessions, and loans; and the documented gaps — say plainly where the chain breaks, for how long, and what rival reconstructions bridge the gap. Distinguish the documented chain from the traditional chain: many objects carry a legendary provenance (back to a saint, a prophet, a god, a founding hero) grafted onto a documentable one, and the dossier must show the seam — where documentation actually begins, and when and by whom the earlier links were asserted. For a legendary object, the custody chain runs through the stories: who holds it in each source, who takes it from whom, and how that sequence varies by version. Treat each link in the chain as a discrete, checkable claim with its own evidence, the way the network dossier treats an edge.

4. **Authenticity and Identity Disputes.** A dedicated section, distinct from the general fact/legend labeling, because for objects this is so often the central drama. Is the object now bearing the name the object the sources describe? Cover, wherever applicable: rival claimants to the same identity (competing "true" relics of the same type, with each claimant's own provenance and champions); continuity disputes (whether the present object is the original, a medieval replacement, a post-fire reconstruction, or a copy — and whether the substitution was ever admitted); scientific examinations and their results (radiocarbon and other dating, materials analysis, DNA, forensic and provenance research), with dates, examiners, findings, and the disputes those findings provoked; documented forgeries and fabrications, including the forger, the motive, and the exposure; officially maintained fictions (an institution venerating or displaying an object its own experts doubt); and cases where authenticity was tested and failed — say so flatly, as with a skull venerated for a century and then DNA-disproven. Where the question is genuinely open, present the strongest case on each side with its evidence and partisans, and do not adjudicate beyond what the sources support. Note also who has had standing to authenticate — church, crown, museum, laboratory — and how the authority to declare the object genuine has itself moved over time.

5. **Associated Persons, Networks, and Divine Beings.** The object's documented relations to named people and named collective or divine subjects: makers, commissioners, owners, bearers, wielders, keepers, thieves, forgers, finders, venerators, scholars, and destroyers; the institutions, orders, guilds, dynasties, and networks through whose systems it moved or which it served as emblem; and, for cult objects and mythic objects, the gods, angels, or divine beings to whom it belongs, whom it represents, or in whose stories it acts. For each significant association: name the person, network, or being; state the nature of the connection in a line or two; date it; and give the source, labeled by the same evidentiary tiers used throughout. Where an associated subject is itself the kind of figure this atlas covers — a historical person, a bounded historical network, a named divine being — make the connection explicit and citable rather than folding it into narrative prose; these named, dated, sourced connections are among the most valuable material in the dossier. One discipline throughout: an episode belongs to the person who was actually there. If someone's only connection to the object is that they later wrote about it, painted it, or made a pilgrimage to it, record that as their documented engagement with the object, not as a scene in the object's custody chain.

6. **Ritual, Ceremonial, and Symbolic Function.** What the object is for, beyond possession: its role in coronations, anointings, oaths, processions, liturgies, pilgrimages, sacrifices, healings, and magic, with the documented occasions of actual use, dated, and the procedures where recorded; the beliefs about its powers, protections, and curses, each with its earliest attestation; its function as legitimator — the way holding it confers or is claimed to confer sovereignty, priesthood, or victory, and documented cases where possession or loss of the object mattered politically; its symbolic and heraldic career, where the object became an emblem reproduced far beyond itself (on coins, arms, flags, letterheads); and taboos and access rules — who may touch, see, or move it, and what happens when the rules are broken. Distinguish attested practice from later tradition about the practice.

7. **Myths and Legends Attached to the Object.** Myth is as important as fact, and for many of these objects it is most of the record. Tell the principal stories fully — origin legends, curse narratives, miracle accounts, prophecies tied to the object ("while it stands, the city stands"), treasure and survival legends, conspiracy narratives about its hiding place or secret substitution. For each significant story: what it says in its fullest form; its earliest attested source and date; the significant variants and what each changes; who circulated it and what interest it served; what evidence exists for and against any factual core; and how it shaped the object's treatment — legends move objects, motivate thefts, and fund shrines, and those effects are documented history even when the legend is not. Label every story by tier — early attested, later elaboration, regional variant, late accretion, modern invention, modern retelling — and keep the labels tracking the history of the story, not merely its truth.

8. **Current Status and Location.** Where the object is right now, stated as precisely as the sources allow, and its condition: on display (institution, city, gallery, accession number where published), in a treasury or vault, in continuing ritual use, actively venerated, buried, lost, destroyed (when, how, and what if anything survives — fragments, casts, photographs, drawings, inventories), or disputed. Who controls it and under what legal regime; access — public, restricted, forbidden; any current custody, ownership, or repatriation dispute, with the parties, their claims, the proceedings, and the state of play; recent scientific work, conservation, or examination; and for lost objects, the serious current hypotheses about fate or location, sourced, and separated from treasure-hunting folklore. For a purely legendary object, state that plainly — and then cover the real things that stand in for it: the candidate objects, sites, and shrines that claim or receive its identity today.

9. **Modern Reception.** The object's afterlife in the modern world, treated as seriously as the older evidence: replicas, casts, and copies, official and commercial, and any replica that acquired a life of its own; documented modern forgeries and hoaxes trading on the object's name; the object in literature, art, opera, film, comics, and games where those treatments have materially shaped its public image, with the works dated; political, nationalist, and religious mobilization — the object on flags and banknotes, invoked in independence movements, restitution campaigns, and identity politics, with the documented episodes; esoteric and occult reinterpretation where it exists, with its key texts and figures dated; tourism, pilgrimage economics, and commercial use; and scholarly or popular controversies alive right now. Say plainly where a modern "tradition" about the object is a documented modern invention, and by whom.

10. **Historiography and Scholarly Debate.** How the object has been studied: the major scholarly works, catalogues raisonnés, excavation and conservation reports, and the running controversies (dating fights, attribution disputes, authenticity wars, restitution debates); the state of the primary evidence — which archives, inventories, chronicles, and images document it, what was lost or destroyed, what remains unpublished; key discoveries and examinations that changed the picture; and discredited theories that still circulate popularly, identified as such.

11. **Chronology.** A compact timeline — year-by-year, century-by-century, or period-by-period as the evidence allows — of the object's own history: creation or earliest attestation, transfers, thefts, recoveries, destructions, restorations, examinations, disputes, down to the present. Every entry anchored to its evidence, and entries resting on tradition rather than documentation labeled as such inline.

12. **Sources.**

Search broadly and deeply; prefer primary and archival material — inventories, chronicles, charters, wills, sale and auction records, court and police records, museum catalogues and accession files, excavation and conservation reports, scientific publications, contemporaneous newspapers, and for legendary objects the primary texts themselves in critical editions — over encyclopedia summaries. Wikipedia and general encyclopedias may orient, but follow their citations into the underlying sources. Search in every language relevant to the object's history — the languages of its makers, its keepers, its thieves, and its scholars, not only English. Look for obscure material, contradictory accounts, local reporting, forgotten episodes, and details that disappear from short summaries. Write without editorial overlay — no moralizing, no romantic inflation, no debunker's sneer, no treating any living tradition's claims about the object as either true or false; report what is attested, by whom, and when. Favor documentary presentation. Do not compress for elegance; detail over brevity.

End with one deduplicated list of every URL actually used (full https:// URLs, one per line, no shortened links, no duplicates). Output as a downloadable .md file named [slug]_object_dossier.md, where [slug] is the object's name in lowercase with underscores.
