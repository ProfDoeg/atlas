# Andrew Carnegie (1835-1919): research report

*2026-08-21. From the verified research pool (chronology, geography, quotes, interlock, afterlife) for slug `andrew_carnegie`. Companion: `andrew_carnegie.journey.json`, 8 segments, 45 stops.*

Legend: **[A]** attested, source named. **[R]** reconstruction, tradition, or self-report only. Contradictions between the pool's lenses are printed, not resolved; section 10 collects the corrections made.

**The subject's special problem.** The primary source and the myth are the same document. The *Autobiography of Andrew Carnegie* (broken off 1914, published 1920, ed. John C. Van Dyke) is the origin of most of what anyone repeats about him: the Working Boy letter, the first dividend, the "first blood" of the Civil War, the McLuckie note, the St. Nicholas memorandum. It was read directly this pass (Gutenberg 17976), which is how several circulating paraphrases were corrected. Being verbatim in it makes a claim [A] as to what Carnegie wrote and [R] as to what happened.

## 1. Dunfermline, 1835-1848

- Born 25 November 1835 in a rented half of a weaver's cottage at Moodie Street and Priory Lane, to William Carnegie, damask hand-loom weaver, and Margaret Morrison [A, Britannica; carnegiebirthplace.com]. The building is now the Andrew Carnegie Birthplace Museum.
- Boyhood formation under the Abbey, Bruce and Wallace and Burns, uncle George Lauder as tutor [R, Autobiography].
- Pittencrieff Glen closed to the Morrisons [R, family tradition via the Autobiography and carnegie.org]. No contemporary document of an exclusion was reached, so the tradition is carried without a scene of its own; the attested 1902-1903 purchase carries it instead.
- 17 May 1848, the family leaves for Glasgow and sails on the *Wiscasset*, twenty pounds borrowed to complete the fare [A, PBS American Experience timeline; ship name corroborated by Wikipedia]. No passenger list was consulted, and the lender's name survives only in the family's retelling.

## 2. Allegheny and the wire, 1848-1859

- Crossing of about fifty days, landing at New York, then Hudson and canal westward [R]; no arrival date established. Settlement in Allegheny, **September 1848** [A, Wikipedia], correcting the pool's bare "1848".
- Bobbin boy at $1.20 a week, end of schooling [A, PBS; Wikipedia]; boiler-firing and bobbin-oiling in the cellar [R, Autobiography].
- 1849, telegraph messenger for the Ohio Telegraph Company, operator within about a year, reading the sounder by ear [A, Wikipedia].
- Colonel James Anderson's library of about four hundred volumes and the newspaper letter signed Working Boy [R, Autobiography, with an editorial corroboration attributed to the telegraph historian David Homer Bates].
- 1853, hired by **Thomas A. Scott** of the Pennsylvania Railroad [A, Wikipedia]. The train-order incident and Scott's "white-haired Scotch devil" are [R, Autobiography].
- 1 December 1859, superintendent of the Western Division at $1,500 a year [A, Wikipedia].

**Wage contradiction, unresolved.** The pool gives "about $4 a week" (chronology lens) and "about $35 a month" (geography lens), calling both attested. Four dollars a week is roughly seventeen a month.

**Adams Express, two pool problems.** (a) Date: Wikipedia dates the purchase 1855 and describes $500 raised on a $600 mortgage of his mother's house; the dossier dates it 1856 and describes "ten shares". (b) Tag: the pool marked it [A] while its own source field said [R]. Adopted: the purchase [A], the "revelation about capital income" framing [R], the date disputed.

## 3. The war, the oil, the bridges, 1861-1874

- 1861, accompanies Scott to Washington when Scott takes charge of Union military railroads and telegraphs [A in general terms, Wikipedia; detail from the Autobiography].
- The telegraph wire that cut his cheek, "first blood of the war", is [R] and explicitly legend: Wikipedia presents it as Carnegie's own later claim and joke, not a casualty record.
- **Keystone Bridge.** Piper and Schiffler dates from about 1862 and was reorganised as the **Keystone Bridge Company in 1865** [A, Wikipedia]; both pool lenses had 1863 or 1862-1863.
- **Eads Bridge.** Nothing places Carnegie at the bridge; it is carried as the object his company built and whose bonds he sold, the licensed maker's-object pattern. The pool's own source warns this is the connection later biography folds into "Carnegie invented steel".

**Oil: a three-way contradiction, printed not resolved.** The chronology lens has Storey Farm, Venango County, 1864; the geography lens has Storey Farm on Oil Creek near Titusville, 1861-1864, which is Crawford County; the verifier has the **Columbia Oil Company**, Venango, 1864. Storey Farm is the property, Columbia Oil the company, and Titusville sits in Crawford. Venango and 1864 are used; the "roughly $1 million in a single year" figure remains [R, Autobiography].

## 4. New York, the memorandum, the steel, 1867-1886

- 1867 move to New York, Broad Street office, lodging with his mother at the St. Nicholas Hotel [R, Autobiography via dossier].
- **December 1868, the memorandum** [A, footnote 30, discussed in Wall and Nasaw]. The quote lens verified the wording against Gutenberg 17976; it differs from paraphrases in circulation.
- 1873, the partnership behind the Edgar Thomson works, partners including Coleman, McCandless, Stewart, Thomson and Scott [R, dossier]; 1875, in production [A, Wikipedia; PBS].
- 1882, the *Servia* crossing with **Herbert Spencer** [R, Autobiography]. Spencer's remark on visiting the Pittsburgh works is standard in the literature but was **not** in the verified pool.
- 1886, *Triumphant Democracy* [A, Wikipedia and dossier concur].

**Dunfermline library: the pool's "1881" is neither the gift nor the opening.** Per Wikipedia's Dunfermline Carnegie Library article, funding plans and 8,000 pounds date to **1879-80**, Margaret Carnegie laid the **foundation stone on 27 July 1881**, and the library **opened 29 August 1883**. Wikipedia's Andrew Carnegie article dates the gift to 1880. All three are printed.

## 5. The deaths, the marriage, the Gospel, 1886-1890

- **Thomas Morrison Carnegie died 19 October 1886** at Pittsburgh of pneumonia, having left work on the sixteenth thinking he had a cold [A, Wikipedia, 'Thomas M. Carnegie']; the pool's "1886-10" is sharpened to the day. **Margaret Morrison Carnegie died 10 November 1886** [A, same source]. **Place contradiction, unresolved**: the chronology lens puts her death in New York City, the geography lens and the standard accounts at the Cresson residence where Andrew lay ill with typhoid. Cresson is used.
- 22 April 1887, marriage to **Louise Whitfield**, private, at the Whitfield family home [A, Wikipedia]; the exact address was not confirmed [R]. Her prenuptial waiver is in the standard accounts, not checked here.
- June 1889, **"Wealth,"** *North American Review* [A]. Five passages verified verbatim against a full-text mirror. The popular "the man who dies rich dies disgraced" is a paraphrase; the printed sentence is "The man who dies thus rich dies disgraced".
- Allegheny library, offered 1886, opened 1890, $250,000, conditional on public maintenance [A, NPS], the model for every later grant. Library counts differ by scope (about 1,689 in the United States, about 2,509 worldwide).

## 6. Johnstown and Homestead

**The dam.** Breach between 2:50 and 2:55 pm, **31 May 1889**; **2,208** dead [A, Wikipedia]. Carnegie's **membership** of the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club is confirmed, alongside Frick, Mellon, Knox and Phipps [A]; the club's lawyers, Knox and Reed, defeated all suits [A]. Visit of 28 November 1889 and about $55,000 toward a Johnstown library [R, dossier via NPS, not reverified].

**"He was in Paris at the time" is dropped.** The dossier attributes it to the National Park Service; the cited nps.gov/jofl page returns 404, and neither the flood article nor the club article says where he was, though the pool's geography lens gave Paris its own stop. **No Paris stop is staged and no location asserted**: a gap, printed as a gap.

**The mill**, all [A] from Wikipedia, 'Homestead strike': consolidation as the **Carnegie Steel Company** in 1892 with Frick as chairman; **28 June**, lockout of the plate mill and an open-hearth furnace; **6 July**, about three hundred Pinkertons, the barge landing, some ten hours of fighting, at least ten dead, commonly seven workers or supporters and three Pinkertons, counts varying and not reconciled; **12 July**, Pattison's militia, about 8,500; **23 July**, Alexander Berkman shoots and stabs Frick, who survives; **20 November**, the lodge votes **101 to 91** to return, after **143 days** (the pool said 144). The fence, its rifle slots and the name Fort Frick come from that article rather than the pool. Berkman's attack gets **no stop of its own**: Carnegie was in Scotland and Frick's office is Frick's scene.

Carnegie himself is at a rented castle on **Loch Rannoch**, reachable only by cable, "inaccessible to all" [A, PBS]. The Autobiography sentences quoted there ("No pangs remain... The men were outrageously wrong") are a retrospective judgement written years later, not a contemporary reaction.

## 7. The McLuckie material: two pool errors corrected

The quote lens carried John McLuckie's line, "Well, that was damned white of Andy, wasn't it?", as verbatim, which it is (Gutenberg 17976, chapter XVII). Two pieces of metadata were wrong. **Date**: not 1893. Van Dyke met McLuckie "in the spring of 1900" and the remark came at a later meeting, "a year later, or perhaps it was in the autumn of the same year", so about **1900-1901**. **Provenance**: not "a letter from a mutual acquaintance", but a signed first-person narrative by **Prof. John C. Van Dyke of Rutgers College**, the autobiography's own editor, reporting an exchange he had face to face at **Guaymas, Sonora**.

Van Dyke's Mexican journey is Van Dyke's, so **no Sonora stop is staged**. Carnegie's own act, the pencilled note "Give McLuckie all the money he wants, but don't mention my name", sits at his Skibo pin, dated 1900 only to order the segment, with Van Dyke's narrative cited there.

## 8. The sale and the institutions, 1901-1913

- **2 March 1901**, Morgan buys Carnegie Steel into U.S. Steel; about $225.64 million to Carnegie in gold bonds [A, Wikipedia]. The written-price-on-a-slip story is widely repeated and **not** verified here.
- **Carnegie Institution of Washington, 29 January 1902**, $10 million, later about $22 million [A]; the chronology lens gave a bare "1902".
- **Carnegie Mansion**, built 1899-1902, occupied about the end of 1902 [A, cooperhewitt.org]; the purchase of surrounding lots is house tradition, flagged. **Pittencrieff Park** conveyed 1902-1903 [A, carnegie.org], its **1914 statue** [A, Wikipedia] undated as to unveiling, with nothing placing Carnegie there.
- **St Andrews.** Rector **1901-1907**, elected 1901 and re-elected 1904 [A, Wikipedia], not "1903, 1905". **1905** is the second rectorial address, printed 1906 as *A League of Peace*.
- **Tuskegee, 1906** [A, carnegiebirthplace.com]: solicitation refused 1890, about $20,000 for a library by 1900, later about $600,000 in U.S. Steel securities including a personal trust for Booker T. Washington, a reported figure. The **Edinburgh** address of 16 October 1907 is [A] as to the address, [R] as to the venue building.
- **Carnegie Endowment.** The pool paired "on his seventy-fifth birthday" with 1910-12-14, which is false: birthday and **announcement, 25 November 1910**, **deed of gift and establishment, 14 December 1910**, $10 million in first-mortgage five percent bonds [A, Wikipedia]. The instruction to move on to the next greatest evil once war is abolished is the standard account of the deed's language; the deed was not read.
- **Carnegie Corporation, 1911** [A]. **Figure contradiction, unresolved**: carnegie.org gives $135 million at founding, the Autobiography's footnote 61, which carries the quoted object, $125,000,000.
- **Peace Palace, opened 28 August 1913**, $1.5 million secured through Andrew Dickson White [A].
- **Kenyon College Stanton address, 1906** [A, publication record], cut from the journey for stop count and recorded only here.

## 9. Death and afterlife

**11 August 1919**, Shadowbrook, bronchial pneumonia, at eighty-three [A, Wikipedia; obituary; Louise Carnegie's 1920 preface]. Wikipedia places the estate site, now the Kripalu Center, in **Stockbridge** while noting it is often described as being in **Lenox**. The mansion burned in 1956.

**Sleepy Hollow Cemetery** [A]: granite Celtic cross, Skibo stone, sculptor George Henry Paulin; coins left by visitors [A, verbatim]; lifetime giving about $350.7 million; Louise d. 1946, Margaret d. 1990. **Claim reduced**: "interred only a short distance from Samuel Gompers" is **not** supported, the cited source saying nothing about the plots. Only the shared cemetery is claimed.

**Diplodocus carnegii**: type specimen CM 84, Sheep Creek, Wyoming, 1899, collected under Jacob L. Wortman, named by John Bell Hatcher 1901, London cast 1905, composite mount 1907 [A, Wikipedia]. Casts distributed "from 1905 to 1928" per Wikipedia, which gives **no per-museum years**; the per-city year list comes only from the Carnegie Museums Magazine piece and its Mexico City date of 1930 falls outside Wikipedia's window, so the cities are named without years. The **Museo de La Plata** cast was installed **1912** by Carnegie Museum director **William J. Holland**, who travelled to Argentina to assemble it [R, Spanish Wikipedia]. Carnegie never went there and Holland's voyage is Holland's, so the stop is for the object he paid for and gave and stages no arrival scene. It is kept as this subject's strongest hook for an Argentine reader.

**Renaming confirmed, pool hedge lifted.** carnegie.org's news feed carries "Carnegie Corporation of New York Changes Its Name to the Andrew Carnegie Foundation", dated **9 June 2026** [A], and confirms the 1911 founding.

## 11. Interlocks

**Named in a campa**, each because Carnegie himself stands in a real relation, not because of shared ground:

- **`tchaikovsky`**. Carnegie built the Music Hall; Tchaikovsky was brought over for its inaugural festival and conducted on opening night, 5 May 1891. Patron and inaugural conductor is a direct relation. Canonical Carnegie Hall pin **40.7651, -73.9799** inherited byte-identical from seven existing files. The pool does not document Carnegie's own presence that night and nothing asserts it.
- **`alexander_graham_bell`**. Carnegie money is one of three funding streams (Harriman, Rockefeller, Carnegie) behind the Eugenics Record Office at Cold Spring Harbor, founded 1910, whose Board of Scientific Directors Bell chaired from about 1910 to 1918 [A, via `alexander_graham_bell.journey.json` and `prescott_bush.journey.json`]. A funding relation between the two men's activities, not co-location, which is why it may be named. It is narrated inside the Carnegie Institution stop; **no stop is staged at Cold Spring Harbor**, where Carnegie never went.

**Considered and deliberately not named**, with the reason in each case. `george_washington`: shared geography only, the pool itself calling the Forks of the Ohio link "inferred, not stated in either file"; that pin is unused. `felix_adler`, `stebbins`, `martha_graham`, `bethsabee_de_rothschild`, `elsa_gindler`, `gurdjieff`: all at Carnegie Hall after 1891 and mostly after his death, so the building is their stop, with two cited instead in his own hall stop's `suggested_refs`. `john_ruskin`: speculative, no textual link. `thomas_edison`: that file already flags the thread as finance with no direct contact surfaced. `mark_brodsky`, `mohammad_mosaddegh`: at the Peace Palace decades later. `abraham_lincoln`: speaking on Stanton is not a relation to Lincoln.

**Queued but unresearched, so no slug to name**: **Henry Clay Frick** (QUEUE.md line 382) and **J. P. Morgan** (line 171), named in the journey's prose as ordinary historical figures. When those files land, the Homestead segment and the 1901 sale stop are where they interlock back. Likewise Spencer, Booker T. Washington and Scott.
