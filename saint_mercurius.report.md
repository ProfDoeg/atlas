# Saint Mercurius of Caesarea (Philopator Mercurius, Abu Sayfayn)

*Research report, 2026-08-18, for `saint_mercurius.journey.json`. The Cappadocian soldier-martyr traditionally executed under Decius c. 250, and, from the sixth century onward, the posthumous killer of the emperor Julian.*

**Legend.** **[A]** attested, source named. **[R]** reconstruction, tradition, or legend, including anything surviving only inside hagiography. Contradictions are flagged in place and left open; twelve are collected at the end of section 9. Unreachable sources are named with the reason.

**Standing caution.** There is no third-century document of any kind about this man: no inscription, no letter, no contemporary act, no mention in any secular historian. Every biographical statement below is [R] by construction, and an [A] tag in the life-phase sections attaches to *what a given text says*, never to the event described.

## 1. Origins and family (c. 210-225)

- **[A as text]** Budge, Preface p. ix, on the Coptic Martyrdom: "He was the son of Gordianus, a native of Scythia, and before he enlisted was known by the name of 'Philopator'."
- **Contradiction, not resolved.** The Greek Passion (BHG 1274/1275) names the father **Gordianos**, primicerius of the Martenses. The Coptic and Arabic Acts name him **Yares**, baptized **Noah**, mother **Saphina/Safina**. Budge's own volume carries both the enlistment-naming (Summaries p. cxv) and, in the Coptic text, a variant giving the name Mercurius at *baptism* by a bishop. Three naming traditions, none reconcilable.
- No source names a home city; **Eskentos** is traditional and not locatable. The file uses **Tomis (44.1733, 28.6383)** as a declared stand-in for the Scythian frontier and pins the birth at Caesarea.
- **The hunt and the cynocephali. [R]** Yares and his own father are attacked by two dog-headed beings; the grandfather is devoured, the father saved by an angel who rings the pair in fire, converts, is baptized Noah, and vows a son. Confirmed as story content at en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahrakas_and_Augani, citing Piankoff (1942, 1947) and Basset (1909). **Correction:** the pool placed this in "Egypt", where the tradition was transmitted; it is set in the family's own Cappadocian country. The pair, named **Ahrakas and Augani**, are then subdued, Christianized, kept as household servants, and later accompany Mercurius into battle. All of it is absent from the Greek Passion.

## 2. The soldier (c. 249-250)

- **[A as text]** Unit: the **Martenses**. Province: **Armenia Prima**. Commander: **Satorninos**. Father in the same unit as primicerius. War against unnamed barbarians, undated.
- **Two impossibilities inside the source, not resolved.** (1) Tradition puts enlistment at seventeen in the reign of Decius; Decius reigned September 249 to June 251, and birth in 224/225 plus seventeen gives 241/242, in Gordian III's reign. The pool carried "242" and it cannot stand; the file follows Decius and says in `date_confidence` that the recruit is then about twenty-five years old. (2) The promotion to stratelates is granted by "Decius and Valerian" jointly, who never ruled together. Oxford CSLA treats this as the signal that the text is not a court record.
- **Province anachronism. [A]** *Armenia Prima* dates from the reforms of c. 293, decades after Decius: a fourth or fifth century army called third-century.
- **Coordinate correction.** No fort is named. **Satala** stands as a representative Late Roman base in Armenia Prima: **40.02611, 39.59472**. The pool gave 39.9667, 39.55, about 7.5 km southwest. Corrected in all four garrison stops.

## 3. The passion and the death (c. 250)

- **[A as text]** Before Decius he refuses to sacrifice to Artemis, strips off cloak and military belt, declares he came into the world naked. The text calls Decius enraged and fascinated at once, and Mercurius young, blond, courageous.
- **Five tortures, each reversed but the last. [R]** Four stakes, swords and fire, the blood quenching the flames; near death in prison, restored overnight by an angel; red-hot spears through both cheeks, sweet fragrance instead of burning; hung upside down under a stone and flogged; then carried toward Caesarea on a pack animal. **Internal geography does not close:** the court sits at Caesarea and the prisoner is then sent *toward* Cappadocia. Flagged, not resolved. **Tyana (37.8422, 34.5967)** is a declared stand-in for the unnamed waystations.
- **Death date, corrected.** The pool gave "250-11-25", fusing a feast onto an execution. en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Mercurius gives the martyrdom as **4 December 250** and **25 November** as the Latin and Eastern Orthodox **feast**. The file uses 0250-12-04 and states the split in `date_confidence`. **[A]** The apparatus is in fact plural and non-reconciled: 25 November, 4-5 December (Coptic, 25 Hathor), 16-17 June (translation of relics, 9 Paoni), 1-2 August. Four dates is itself evidence of regional accretion rather than descent from one fixed record.

## 4. The cult at Caesarea, and Basil's silence (370-518)

- **[A, attested as an absence]** Basil is bishop of Caesarea 370-379, intensely engaged with the martyr shrines of his own city, promotes the Forty Martyrs of Sebaste there, and **never mentions Mercurius anywhere in his surviving corpus** (Oxford CSLA on E02774). A documented silence is not proof that no cult existed, and is not treated as one.
- **[A]** The pilgrim **Theodosius**, *De situ terrae sanctae*, shortly after **518**, names Mercurius as one of the two great saints of Caesarea alongside **Mamas**: the first solid external attestation, 268 years after the traditional martyrdom.
- **[R, flagged unproven by its own proposers]** The Oxford hypothesis deriving the cult from the Forty Martyrs of Sebaste via a drift Kyrion to Mar Qurios to Merkourios. Not in the file as fact; it sits in `suggested_refs` on the Theodosius stop.
- **[R]** Vision-discovery of the body: a poor man is told where the corpse lies by a visitor naming himself "Mercurius, the Martyr of the Lord". **Coordinate harmonization:** Caesarea is **38.7225, 35.4875** throughout; one lens of the pool carried 38.7312, 35.4787, 1.3 km away.

## 5. The killing of Julian (363, told from the sixth century)

- **Attested baseline. [A]** Ammianus Marcellinus, a serving officer on the campaign, *Res Gestae* XXV.iii: Julian rides out without his cuirass during the retreat, is struck by a cavalry spear into the liver, cuts his own fingers on the blade trying to draw it, is carried to his tent, and **expires quietly about midnight of the same night** (XXV.3.23). He names no attacker. **Correction:** one lens of the pool had Julian dying "days later". **26 June 363**, near Samarra/Maranga, **34.1983, 43.8746** (approximate; the exact site is disputed).
- **The legend. [A as text, R as event]** John Malalas, *Chronographia*, sixth century: on the night of Julian's death Basil sees heaven open and Christ enthroned order the already-martyred Mercurius to kill the emperor; Mercurius appears in polished iron, departs, returns, reports. **Glitch, flagged:** Malalas needs Basil to be bishop in 363, and he was consecrated in **370**. Left standing in `date_confidence` as a property of the legend, which requires both Cappadocian archetypes already in their finished roles.
- **Coptic elaboration. [R]** Basil, imprisoned before the campaign, prophesies Julian will not return; that night Mercurius leaves his own martyrium, **takes the spear out of his own image**, and departs. Budge, Preface p. x, gives what the witnesses heard: "Shall I permit this lawless man (i. e. Julian) to blaspheme the God of heaven in this manner?" **Two corrections:** the pool cited p. xi, and it is **p. x**; and the pool attached the "spear returns bloodied" motif to this vision, where Budge says the opposite, that the spear was found **missing** and "Three days later came the news of Julian's death, but nothing is said about the spear coming back." The bloodied return belongs to the **Eutychius/Arabic** variant and to the **Ethiopic** version, and the file separates the two into two stops.
- **Eutychius variant. [A as text]** Sa'id ibn Batrik, tenth century, in Budge, Preface **p. xi** (not pp. xi-xii): the painted figure leaves the tablet, the panel stays blank about an hour, the figure returns with blood on the spear-point. Budge's footnote gives Pococke, *Contextio Gemmarum, sive Eutychii Annales* (Oxford, 1656), i, 484-7.
- **Antioch is not a stop.** The Coptic elaboration stages Basil's confrontation with Julian at the campaign's staging point, where Mercurius is not present in any version. That scene belongs to Basil and Julian; it is carried in the martyrium stop's campa and sources, not staged.

## 6. The miracle cycle: an officer of his own shrine

Source throughout: the **Coptic pseudo-Basilian Encomium**, possibly sixth century, earliest dated witness **Pierpont Morgan M.588, copied 842**; translated in Budge, vol. I (1915); Oxford CSLA E01861. All **[A as text, R as event]**. The characterization drives the whole file: he investigates, disciplines, threatens, beats, heals, settles accounts and enforces promises, in person, on his own building site.

- **Agathonicus** vows to enlarge the shrine, procrastinates, is warned in a dream; construction begins. Retrojected to the reign of **Jovian**. The overcrowded three-day festival at which the preacher breaks off his sermon is folded into this stop.
- **The ox** that gores its fellow: the wounded beast healed by the saint's foot, the guilty one sentenced to lose its horns. Summaries **p. cxx** (pool said cxxi). Fourth Miracle.
- **The timber thief**, whose mind is made to wander until he carries the stolen plank to the custodian's own door. Summaries **pp. cxxi-cxxii** (pool said cxxii-cxxiii). Sixth Miracle.
- **The fruiting beam**: a workman's joke about the apse timber taken seriously; the fruit kept as **phylakteria**. Summaries p. cxxiii.
- **Gaipios**, a Jewish skeptic speared in the martyrium, then healed, baptized Zacharias, commissioning a gold stele of the saint spearing him. Summaries pp. cxxiii-cxxiv. **Anti-Jewish polemic standard in the genre; reported as the text has it, stated as such in `date_confidence`, not endorsed.**
- **Hermapollo's daughter**, afflicted by a rejected suitor's hired magician; Mercurius appears as an enraged general, strikes the young man three times with the flat of the sword, and forces the spell undone.
- **The walking corpse and the talking mule.** Preface **p. ix**, and again in the Summaries at **p. cxix** (pool said p. x, which does not carry it): "Sing praises to the martyr." **Place contested:** Budge's Edfu manuscript (**BL Or. MS 6802**, probably eleventh century) sets this at **Edfu**, the earlier cycle at Caesarea. The file pins Caesarea and records Edfu in `date_confidence` rather than inventing a coordinate.

## 7. Egypt: relics, and the house of the Two Swords

- **[R]** Translation of *part* of the relics on **9 Paoni (16 June)**, after contact between an Armenian Catholicos and the Patriarch of Alexandria. **No year, no route, no named agent.** The file's year is an ordering device asserted by nobody.
- **[A]** **Dayr Abu Sayfayn**, Old Cairo: a walled enclosure holding **three churches and a convent of nuns**, his church the largest in the district of ancient Babylon.
  - **Coordinate correction, topographically significant.** The complex is at **30.012444, 31.230806**, just north of the Babylon fortress. The pool carried 30.0043/31.2308 and 30.0059/31.2302, both *inside* Qasr al-Sham, contradicting the Coptic Encyclopedia's own "north of Qasr al-Sham". One lens also placed it in **Harat Zuwayla**, a different Coptic quarter. Deleted.
- **[A]** The **History of the Patriarchs**, on the tenure of **Mark VII (1745-1769**, confirmed), names "the monastery of the great martyr Philopator Mercurius, Father of the Two Swords".
- **[R]** Enclosure wall attributed to the twelfth century and to **Ibn Abu al-Fada'il ibn Faruj** (also *ibn Faruq* in the pool; one form used throughout). "1150" is a synthetic midpoint, and the Claremont Coptic Encyclopedia would not serve its text this pass, so the attribution is unverified at first hand.
- **[R]** Modern devotional claim: abbess **Tenmav Irini** and the nuns of the Abu Sefein convent credit the saint with reversing a Ministry of Defence confiscation (no year in the source). Teja and Acerbi count more than twenty-five churches to him across Egypt **[A]**.

## 8. Iconography

- **[A]** **Faras cathedral, Nubia, ninth century**: mounted Mercurius spearing Julian, among the earliest datable examples. The site is now under Lake Nasser; the plaster is in Khartoum and Warsaw.
- **[A]** **Monastery of Saint Anthony, Red Sea**, fresco of roughly the tenth to thirteenth centuries: mounted saint, fallen Julian, angel with the second sword, Ahrakas and Augani behind the horse. Meinardus (1992) proposed an Armenian origin.
- **[A/R]** **Biete Qeddus Mercoreus, Lalibela**, one of the eleven rock-hewn churches; King Lalibela reigned c. 1181-1221, so c. 1200 holds. **But** the name **Oleonus** as the Ethiopian form of Julian, the black horse, and cynocephalic attendants **could not be confirmed in any accessible source**. The pool tagged this [A], which overstates it; carried as reported tradition.
- **[A]** **Hosios Loukas**: Mercurius among the military saints in the katholikon mosaics, katholikon tentatively dated **1011-12**. **Coordinate correction: 38.395361, 22.746833**; the pool gave 38.4166, 22.9351, about 18 km east. The Byzantine type is the **standing** soldier, not the mounted avenger. A reported fresco of **1295** at **Ohrid** was bundled into the same pool item under one coordinate pair; it is kept in `suggested_refs`.
- **[A]** **Coptic Museum, Cairo**: eighteenth-century icon attributed to **Ibrahim al-Nasikh**, the surviving image that *names* Ahrakas and Augani. His workshop partner **Yuhanna al-Armani** lived **1726-1786**; the pool called him seventeenth-century.

## 9. The other Mercurius, and the contradictions collected

**[A as scholarship]** Relics venerated at **Benevento** under this name belong, per Delehaye and Walter after him, to **Mercurius of Aeclanum**, a distinct saint confused with the Cappadocian martyr. The two are kept separate, and the Benevento stop says so in its campa. Walter's text was **not reachable** (403); reproduced from the brief.

**The twelve contradictions carried forward, not resolved**, each set out in place above: father's name, Gordianos against Yares/Noah; when the name Mercurius was given, at enlistment against at baptism; enlistment at seventeen against the reign of Decius; "Decius and Valerian" ruling jointly; Armenia Prima named decades before it existed; court at Caesarea with the prisoner then sent toward Cappadocia; day of death, 25 Hathor / 4 December against 25 November; Basil bishop in 363 against 370; the spear missing and never mentioned again against returned bloodied; the walking corpse at Edfu against Caesarea; Ibn Faruj against ibn Faruq; and the pool's "five textual strata", of which it enumerated three.

## 10. Apparatus relations for the operator

Saint Mercurius is **not** listed in `EXCEPTIONS.md`, so nothing has been applied backward as a framework, and nothing was silently deleted. **No backward apparatus relation was found**, and none was invented.

- **Forward, unlicensed, kept as sources rather than stops.** Several relations in the pool are other people's travels and other people's work: Vansleb (1672-73), Butler (1884), Coquin and Casanova and Simaykah on topography, Delehaye and Walter on Benevento, Binon (1937), Budge, the **Pierpont Morgan Library** holding M.588, the **National Museum in Warsaw** holding the Faras plaster. None is staged as a scene; each is a `source` or `suggested_ref` on the stop for the place or object it concerns. Flagged because a Pierpont Morgan stop and a Butler stop were both available and both declined.
- **Borderline, kept as stops; the operator may want to rule.** Six stops are places where an *image or relic* of the subject is, not places he went in any tradition: Faras, Saint Anthony's, Lalibela, Hosios Loukas, the Coptic Museum icon, Benevento. They are in because this cult's own doctrine is that the image is operational: the spear leaves the painted tablet in Eutychius, the saint steps out of his own martyrium in the Coptic vision. If that reads as over-reach, these are the stops to cut, at a cost of six stops and one segment.

## 11. Interlocks considered and declined

Fifteen candidates were surfaced; one is named in a campa, because shared geography is not a relation and the map already shows co-location.

- **`jesus.journey.json`, named twice.** Christ appears personally to Mercurius at Caesarea before the execution (Greek Passion), and Christ enthroned issues the order to kill Julian by name (Malalas). A direct relation.
- **Declined for want of any relation:** `san_cristobal`, `saint_george`, `saint_blaise`, `saint_acacius` (same Cappadocian or cynocephalic field, no meeting, no text, no citation either way); `maimonides`, `matias_carbone`, `laura_renee_maier`, `gustave_flaubert`, `fyodor_yevtishchev`, `ibn_battuta`, `cyrus` (shared district, register, or province). `catorce_santos_ayudantes.journey.json` was checked directly: **Mercurius does not appear in it**.
- **False friends:** `benjamin_netanyahu` (Caesarea Maritima) and the Caesarea Philippi stop in `jesus.journey.json`. Only Caesarea Mazaca is this subject's. The Abu Serga pin was **not** inherited: a different building in the same quarter.

## 12. Honest gaps

No contemporary evidence of any kind for the person; everything before 518 is text about text. The shrine at Caesarea has never been located archaeologically, and the "gardens near the royal palace" cannot be pointed at. The relic translation to Egypt has no year, no route, no named agent. The Coptic Encomium's cycle may be set at Caesarea or at an Egyptian foundation, and the manuscripts pull both ways. Teja and Acerbi is cited throughout the pool without page numbers, and the Ethiopian material rests on it alone. And **Simon Binon, *Essai sur le cycle de Saint Mercure* (1937)**, named in the brief as the foundational modern study of the legend-cycle, could not be located in any digitized copy: the most valuable unread item in the dossier, flagged as an open task.
