# Abu Karib As'ad, king of Himyar: research report

*Slug: `abu_karib`. Compiled 2026-08. The journey file runs in the register "national mythology: the canon is true". This report is the audit behind it and smooths nothing.*

**Legend.** **[A]** = attested, source named, claim supported by that source as written. **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition, inference, or legend. Where sources disagree, both readings are printed and neither is chosen.

**The problem in one line.** Almost everything colourful about Abu Karib comes from Arabic narrative written three to five centuries after his death, through a book that no longer exists. Almost everything datable about him comes from Sabaic inscriptions that mention not one episode the narrative describes. The two bodies of evidence cover the same reign and never touch. This report keeps them apart on the page.

---

## 1. The name and the titles

**[A]** `en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Karib` gives the reign as 390 to 420 CE, a co-regency from 375, a father named Malkikarib Yuhamin, the Kaaba kiswa attribution, and successors Hassan Yuha'min and Sharhabil Yafar. *Correction on the record:* an earlier pass reported this article unretrievable. Only the exact title `Abu_Karib_As%27ad` returns 404; the base title resolves, and the most relevant source was missed on the first pass.

**[A]** The royal titulature, from the inscriptions and not the chronicles: *king of Saba', Dhu Raydan, Hadramawt and Yamnat, and of their Arabs on the coast and in the highland* (Wikipedia, `Himyarite Kingdom`). The firmest single fact in the dossier. Each clause records a formerly separate South Arabian polity now held from Zafar.

**[R]** The regnal name As'ad al-Kamil and the dynastic style Tubba' belong to Islamic-era Arabic usage, not to the inscriptions.

**[A]** Zafar was the Himyarite capital from 110 BCE to 525 CE, at 14.2114 N, 44.4033 E (Wikipedia, `Zafar, Yemen`). *Correction:* the geography pass used 14.1958 / 44.42 at six stops, 2.4 km off and inconsistent with its own afterlife pass. The journey uses 14.2114 / 44.4033 throughout.

## 2. The inheritance: what his father did, and when

The central contested question, not settled here.

**[A]** The epigraphic transition happens in the **mid-fourth century**. Wikipedia, `Himyarite Kingdom`: inscriptions "suddenly transition from polytheistic invocations to those mentioning the high god Rahmanan". Almaqah, Ta'lab and dhu-Samawi drop out; Rahmanan, glossed there as "Lord of the Heavens and Earth", the "God of Israel" and "Lord of the Jews", replaces them.

**[R]** *Correction:* the pool dated this shift to 380 and credited Malkikarib Yuhamin (r. c. 375-400) with being first to drop polytheistic formulas. Both wrong by a generation; the formulas had changed before he acceded. What Wikipedia credits to him is a later, different step: "the conversion from polytheism and the institutionalization of Judaism as the official religion".

**[A]** Under Malkikarib a *mikrab* (prayer house) called **Barik** is built at Marib, attested by Ja 856 = Fa 60, naming the king and his sons Abikarib As'ad and Dhara''amar Ayman (Wikipedia, `Malkikarib Yuhamin`). Abikarib As'ad is Abu Karib. This is the only contemporary religious inscription in the dossier that names our subject, and it names him as somebody's son.

**[A]** Almaqah is **not** securely a moon god. Wikipedia, `Almaqah`, records that Ryckmans, Garbini and Pirenne moved the scholarship toward solar and warrior attributes.

**[A]** Mainstream scholarship dates the introduction of Judaism at court to **Malkikarib's reign**, one generation before the legend puts it. Wikipedia, `Himyarite Kingdom`, separates "historically" from "later tradition".

**[R]** Later Arab-Islamic tradition credits **Abu Karib himself** with the conversion.

> **Contradiction 1, not resolved.** Father or son? The inscriptions favour the father. The narrative tradition insists on the son. No source consulted reconciles them, and the journey file carries both without adjudicating.

**[R]** Co-regent from 375, sole ruler from 390, while the father's reign runs to c. 400. The father is therefore still reigning during the son's "sole" rule. An earlier version of this research described 390 as a clean succession on the father's death; that is not what the sources say.

## 3. The reign: what the stones attest

**[A]** A war against the **Ma'add** confederation of central Arabia, fought by Abu Karib with his son as co-regent, allied with Saba', Hadramawt and Kinda. Attested in Sabaic royal inscriptions from **Ma'sal al-Jumh**, discussed in Robin's chronology. The only northern campaign with epigraphic support.

**[R]** *Correction:* the pool framed the expedition as directed "against Byzantine-aligned influence in the Arabian interior". No source consulted says this. The inscription records a war on Ma'add and an alliance with three South Arabian powers; Byzantium is absent.

**[R]** *Coordinate correction:* the pool placed the campaign at Turabah, 21.2167 / 42.65, calling it "central Najd". Turabah is at 21.17 / 41.60 (Wikipedia, `Turubah`), a degree west, "on the border between the topographic regions of Hejaz and Najd". The pool's pin lands in empty desert 110 km off. The journey uses 21.17 / 41.60 and flags Ma'sal al-Jumh, the actual findspot, as a different site not pinned.

**[A]** Hebrew-influenced and Sabaean inscriptions of the fourth and fifth centuries praise the dynasty for helping and empowering "the People of Israel" (Wikipedia, `History of the Jews in Yemen`).

**[R]** *Correction:* the afterlife pass cited these as "Ry 508 and related texts". Ry 508 belongs to the Ma'sal al-Jumh group associated with the Ma'add campaign. Two distinct inscription sets were conflated: the formula is real, the shelfmark attached to it was wrong.

**[R]** Nothing in the inscriptional record mentions Yathrib, Mecca, the Kaaba, two rabbis, an illness, or an ordeal by fire.

## 4. The march north: what the narrative supplies

The source is Ibn Ishaq's *Sirat Rasul Allah*, lost in the original and surviving through Ibn Hisham. Cited from A. Guillaume, *The Life of Muhammad* (Oxford University Press, 1955), read from the archive.org scan.

**[A, quoted verbatim]** Guillaume, p. 7, opening the section headed "How Abu Karib Tiban As'ad took possession of the kingdom of the Yaman and his expedition to Yathrib":

> It was Tiban As'ad Abu Karib who went to Medina and took away to the Yaman two Jewish rabbis from thence.

**[R]** The king reaches Yathrib, installs a son as governor, and marches on. The son is killed by the townspeople. He returns to destroy the oasis.

**[A, quoted verbatim]** Guillaume, p. 7. The trigger is a single killing in a date grove: Ahmar of B. 'Adiy b. al-Najjar catches one of the king's men cutting his dates and kills him, saying, "The fruit belongs to the man who cultivates it."

**[A, quoted verbatim]** Guillaume, p. 7, on the Ansar fighting his men by day and hosting them by night: "Tubba' was amazed at this and used to say: 'By God our people are generous!'"

**[A, quoted verbatim]** Guillaume, p. 7, the two rabbis of B. Qurayza: "O King, do not do it, for if you persist in your intention something will happen to prevent your carrying it out and we fear that you will incur speedy retribution."

**[R]** The rabbis' argument, per Wikipedia's `Medina`: Yathrib is "the place to which a prophet of the Quraysh would migrate in time to come". A pre-Islamic king foretelling Muhammad is retrojection on its face. Guillaume does not flag it here; his interpolation footnote ("Either the poem has suffered interpolation or it is the product of a later age", p. 9 n. 3) attaches to Subay'a's elephant poem, a different text.

**[R]** The illness during the siege, and the two Jewish scholars who nurse him. Some versions name them **Ka'b and As'ad**; others give different names. Not resolved. The conversion of king and army at Yathrib is dated to 390 in the strand summarized by Wikipedia's `History of the Jews in Yemen`.

> **Contradiction 2, not resolved.** The same body of tradition that converts him at Yathrib elsewhere credits the kingdom's turn to Judaism to his father's reign at Zafar. Both are in the tradition. Neither cancels the other in any source consulted.

### 4a. The page-number correction

The research pool cited eight verse passages to **Guillaume p. 9**. All eight are on **p. 11** or **pp. 11-12**. Verified against the archive.org djvu text, where the running page headers fall at p. 7 @ line 2856, p. 8 @ 2915, p. 9 @ 2983, p. 10 @ 3057, **p. 11 @ 3117**, p. 12 @ 3194. The al-Tabari-transmitted poem, marked "T 906", begins at line 3117, immediately after the page-11 header. The wording of all eight is **[A]**; only the citations were wrong.

All at **p. 11** unless noted, with djvu line numbers: "Why, O soul, is thy sleep disturbed" (3129-3131); "I had sworn a steadfast vow... striplings and fruitful" (3150-3155); "Stand back from a city preserved" (3160-3162, semicolon in print); "So I forgave them without reproach" (3164-3168); "Some of our people I left there for him" (3170-3176); "A house of ancient wealth in Mecca" (3184-3185); "I wanted to seize them but my Lord said nay" (**pp. 11-12**, 3187-3191 and 3196); "Dhu'l-Qarnayn before me was a Muslim" (**p. 12**, 3197-3201).

**[R]** The Dhu'l-Qarnayn lines are **not** the poem's close, as the pool claimed. Two couplets follow, ending "Before him Bilqis my father's sister / Ruled them until the hoopoe came to her."

**[R]** In the poem the Hudhaylis approach the king at "al-Duff of Jumdan above al-Masnad", not in Mecca. The prose parallel at p. 8 has them describe "an ancient treasury which former kings have overlooked... pearls, topaz, rubies, gold, and silver".

## 5. Mecca and the kiswa

**[A, quoted verbatim]** Guillaume, p. 8, the rabbis when the Hudhayl try to lure the king into sacking the Ka'ba: "We know of no other temple in the land which God has chosen for Himself, and if you do what they suggest you and all your men will perish."

**[A]** The kiswa attribution is confirmed. Wikipedia, `Kiswah`: "Abu Karib later covered the Kaaba in a striped red woolen garment, layering it atop the already existing hangings." The tradition ties this to his return from Yathrib with the rabbis.

**[R]** *Correction:* the pool framed the kiswa as connected to "an attempted invasion of the city" and dated it to 395. Neither is supported; 395 is invented precision. The journey dates the Meccan episode only relative to the Yathrib march. The Zamzam offering appears in some recensions and not others, and is carried as a variant.

## 6. Death, succession, and the road to Dhu Nuwas

**[R]** Three regnal brackets circulate and no source consulted chooses between them: **390-420** (Wikipedia, `Abu Karib`, and the body text of `Himyarite Kingdom`); **400-445** (given in `Himyarite Kingdom` as "listed as r. c. 400-445 in one source"); **390-440** (Wikipedia's ruler list via `Tubba'`, "ruler #98, reigned 390-440 CE").

*Correction:* the pool called this a two-way contradiction between a Wikipedia infobox (390-440) and body text (390-420). Not the shape of it: three brackets from three places, and 390-440 comes from the ruler list, not an infobox.

**[A]** Successors: "His sons Hassan Yuha'min and Sharhabil Yafar succeeded him" (Wikipedia, `Abu Karib`). **[R]** *Correction:* the pool claimed he died leaving children too young to succeed. The minors motif belongs to the Dhu Shanatir legend, which attaches to c. 490 and to Sharhabil Ya'fur's death.

**[A]** Dhu Nuwas (Yusuf As'ar) reigned 522-530 and persecuted the Christians of Najran in 524 (Wikipedia, `Dhu Nuwas`).

> **Contradiction 3, not resolved.** Earlier Arab scholars and the Jewish Encyclopedia make Dhu Nuwas Abu Karib's **son**. Ibn al-Kalbi makes him son of Sharhabil Yakkuf and so a **great-grandson**. Internal wrinkle: Sharhabil Yakkuf is elsewhere given as Abu Karib's own son, which would make Dhu Nuwas a **grandson**. Three degrees of descent are on the table.

**[R]** *Correction:* the pool called the century between them a gap left blank by the sources. Overstated. The intervening kings are named: Hassan Yuha'min, Sharhabil Yafar, Sharhabil Yakkuf, Ma'dikarib Ya'fur, Lakhni'ah Yanuf Dhu Shanatir (c. 490-517). Sparsely attested, not empty. The genealogy itself is **[R]**, not **[A]**: a lineage argued between Ibn al-Kalbi, "earlier Arab scholars" and a nineteenth-century encyclopaedia is reconstruction.

## 7. Afterlife: transmission, scripture, spade

**[A]** The transmitter. **Wahb ibn Munabbih**, d. c. 728-729 or 732-733, is the named source for the elaborated Tubba' material. Wikipedia, `Wahb ibn Munabbih`, gives his father as "a Persian knight who had been expelled from Herat" and his mother as Himyarite.

**[R]** *Correction, twice over:* the pool called Wahb of "reputed Jewish or Persian descent" (no Jewish descent in the source) and named his lost work *Kitab al-Muluk al-Himyariyya*. It is *Kitab al-Muluk al-Mutawwadjin min Himyar*; the surviving *Kitab al-Tijan fi Muluk Himyar* was compiled by **Ibn Hisham**, who cites Wahb. The geography pass cited "Wahb ibn Munabbih, Kitab al-Tijan", wrong in both author and book.

**[A]** Qur'an 44:37 (*Ad-Dukhan*) and 50:12-14 (*Qaf*) name "the people of Tubba'" among destroyed nations, beside the people of Pharaoh. **[R]** Identifying that Tubba' with Abu Karib is **exegetical**, from the ninth and tenth century tafsir tradition including al-Tabari's *Jami' al-Bayan*. The Qur'anic text names no king.

**[A]** Zafar was excavated **1998-2009** by Heidelberg under Paul Yule (Wikipedia, `Zafar, Yemen`). *Correction:* the pool twice gave 1998-2010. No royal tomb has been identified; tradition supplies no burial site and archaeology has not found one.

**[A]** A carved ring-stone from Zafar bearing the Jewish-Aramaic name **Yishaq bar Hanina** is "the earliest possible evidence for Jews in South Arabia", dated **330 BCE to 200 CE or later**. **[R]** *Correction, and it matters:* the pool dated it to the fourth or fifth century CE and called it "contemporary with or shortly after" Abu Karib's reign. It predates him by centuries. The most striking material object in the dossier is evidence **against** the story that a king imported Judaism to Himyar.

**[A]** Modern reassessment, chiefly Robin and collaborators from the 1990s, treats "the conversion of Abu Karib" as a later Arabic-Islamic construction laid over a real but gradual, top-down monotheistic shift attested independently in inscriptions.

**[A]** There is no text by Abu Karib. The nearest thing to an edition of him is an edition of a source narrating him: Wustenfeld's Ibn Hisham (Gottingen, 1858-60), Guillaume (1955), the SUNY al-Tabari (1985-1999).

## 9. Honest gaps

1. **No birth or death date.** Only reign brackets, and three of those.
2. **No tomb, no grave goods, no named structure.** Zafar has been dug; he is not in it.
3. **No text by him**, and one contemporary inscription naming him at all, Ja 856, where he is his father's son.
4. **The Yathrib siege has no independent attestation.** It rests on Ibn Ishaq, through Ibn Hisham, drawing on Wahb ibn Munabbih, whose book is lost.
5. **The kings between Abu Karib and Dhu Nuwas are named but not dated**, and the genealogy joining the two is contested three ways.
6. **Wadi al-Dawasir, Tayma, Dedan and Khaybar are geographic inference**, marked as road and not record in their `date_confidence` fields. **Hajr al-Yamama** is thin in the tradition and disputed as to site.
7. **When Jewish settlement in northern Arabia began is unknown** (Wikipedia, `Khaybar`). Every claim about pre-existing Jewish communities in the Hijaz at this date is softer than it looks.
