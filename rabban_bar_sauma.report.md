# Rabban Bar Sauma (c. 1220 - January 1294): research report

*Compiled 2026-08-13 for the Atlas of Journeys. Queued as "the reverse Marco Polo: Beijing-born Nestorian monk sent west, met kings and popes" (QUEUE.md line 338). No prior journey file; nothing in `working/journeys` mentions him by name.*

**Method.** Four research lenses (chronology, geography, quotations, atlas-interlock) were gathered, then verified adversarially against the cited pages; the verification corrected eleven items. Where sources genuinely disagree, the disagreement is printed, not adjudicated. The whole narrative rests on one medieval text in one recension, so the evidence ceiling is low.

**Legend.** **[A]** = attested, source named · **[R]** = reconstruction or tradition · *flag* = contradiction left open.

## 0. The single source problem

Everything biographical about Bar Sauma descends from one Syriac text, the *History of Mar Yahballaha and Rabban Sauma*, itself an abridgment by an anonymous later hand that folds his travel account into a biography of his disciple Markos, later Patriarch Yahballaha III **[A: Wikipedia, "Rabban Bar Sauma"; standard framing in Montgomery 1927 and Budge 1928]**. The fuller original, believed written in Persian, is lost **[A: same]**. The compilation was dated 1330 in the gathering pass, which nothing consulted supports **[flag]**; it is normally placed shortly after Yahballaha III's death on 13 November 1317, so "c. 1318 or after" is used here.

The consequence runs through every section below: where the account is our only witness, we have Bar Sauma's words filtered by an editor writing a generation later, interested in the patriarch at least as much as in the traveler.

## 1. Origins: Zhongdu, c. 1220

Born c. 1220 in or near the city then called Zhongdu, later Khanbaliq, now Beijing **[A: Wikipedia, "Rabban Bar Sauma"]**. Family wealthy; of Uyghur origin per Bar Hebraeus, Öngüd in Chinese accounts **[A: same]**. Three corrections attach here.

1. "Yuan China" is anachronistic for 1220: the Yuan was proclaimed in 1271 and the city was not called Khanbaliq until c. 1272. In 1220 this is Mongol-held former Jin territory **[A]**.
2. The father's office was reported twice and differently, "chief examiner of religious teaching under the Mongol court" in one lens and "chorbishop" in another. Neither appears in the cited article **[flag; both dropped]**. Two unsourced and incompatible offices for one man is reason to say nothing of him beyond the family's wealth and confession.
3. The birth address is unknown; the pin marks the historic city, not a house **[R]**.

## 2. The hermitage, c. 1240 - c. 1275

At about twenty he renounces marriage and withdraws to a cell in the hills southwest of the capital **[A: Wikipedia, "Rabban Bar Sauma"]**. Tradition identifies the site with the Monastery of the Cross in modern Fangshan District; the cave is not archaeologically fixed **[R]**. Two decades of seclusion follow, and he acquires a reputation as a teacher **[A]**.

The disciple Markos, an Öngüd youth, seeks him out **[A: Wikipedia, "Yahballaha III"]**. His birthplace, Koshang, is a live problem: Wikipedia places it "near modern Dongsheng District, Inner Mongolia", about 39.82 N, 109.98 E, inside the Ordos loop **[A]**, while the rival identification with Olon Süme sits at 41.96 N, 110.486 E and is called in its own article only "a possible northern capital of the medieval Ongut kings" **[R]**. The coordinates first supplied, 41.4386 / 110.05, match neither.

## 3. The road west, c. 1275 - c. 1279

Master and disciple leave together on pilgrimage toward Jerusalem **[A: Wikipedia, "Yahballaha III", citing the *History*]**. Neither returns east. Route stations named in the sources: Tangut country (former Xi Xia), Khotan, Kashgar, Taraz, Samarkand, Bukhara, Khorasan, Tus **[A: Wikipedia, "Rabban Bar Sauma"; "Yahballaha III"]**. Kashgar is recorded as desolate at their passing, the country between Kublai's Mongols and Kaidu's being at war **[A]**.

Two corrections. Tus was tagged a reconstruction, a "representative waypoint on the standard road"; it is not, the Yahballaha III article naming it explicitly, so the stop is **[A]** and the coordinates move from 36.3667 / 59.5333 (effectively Mashhad) to about 36.4864 / 59.5236. Taraz was placed in "the Syr Darya valley"; the Talas is endorheic, drying in the Muyunkum desert **[A: Wikipedia, "Talas River"]**, so this becomes "the Talas valley".

## 4. The pilgrimage abandoned, c. 1279 - 1281

**The contradiction, printed and not resolved.** One lens has the monks received at Maragha by Denha I, who then sends them to Abaqa; the other has the reception at Baghdad. The Denha I article settles the patriarchal side: on their arrival, "Denha held the patriarchal seat in Baghdad. He requested the two monks to visit the court of Abaqa in order to obtain confirmation letters" **[A: Wikipedia, "Denha I"]**. Baghdad is therefore where the patriarch was and Maragheh where they were sent. Whether they touched Maragheh first on the road in from Khorasan stays open **[flag]**.

Warnings of danger on the southern Syrian roads, the Mongol-Mamluk war being in progress, turn them back from Jerusalem **[A: Wikipedia, "Rabban Bar Sauma"]**. The pilgrimage is never completed. No source gives the place of the decision; the pool offered "northern Iraq/Syria border region" without coordinates, so the stop sits at Baghdad rather than on an invented pin **[gap stated]**.

Markos is consecrated bishop of Cathay and the Öngüd, taking the name Mar Yahballaha, and never takes up the see **[R: Wikipedia, "Rabban Bar Sauma"; "Yahballaha III"]**. Denha dies in 1281 **[A]**; Markos is elected and consecrated at Kokhe on 21 November 1281 as Yahballaha III, reportedly favored by the Mongol court partly for his Mongol fluency and Öngüd origin **[A: Wikipedia, "Yahballaha III"]**. Bar Sauma becomes his advisor **[A]**.

Kokhe's coordinates were given as 33.094 / 44.5809, which is Taq Kasra, the Sasanian palace on the east bank at Ctesiphon. Kokhe stood at Seleucia on the west bank, about 33.09 / 44.52 **[A]**. Corrected, footprint still unverified **[R]**.

## 5. Three Ilkhans, 1282 - 1287

Abaqa dies in 1282; his brother Tekuder takes the name Ahmad and converts to Islam, straining relations with the Christian clergy **[A: Wikipedia, "Rabban Bar Sauma"; general Ilkhanate historiography]**. Arghun overthrows him in 1284, restores ties with the Church of the East, and continues the search for a Christian alliance against the Mamluks **[A: same]**.

In 1287 Arghun chooses Bar Sauma, then about sixty-seven, as envoy to the Byzantine emperor, the Pope, and the kings of France and England, proposing a joint campaign and offering Jerusalem to the Christians once taken together **[A: Wikipedia, "Rabban Bar Sauma"]**. The commissioning seat is Maragha in one lens and Tabriz in another, both Ilkhanid seats in these years **[flag]**. The retinue of thirty riding animals is **[R]**.

## 6. The embassy west, 1287 - 1288

### 6.1 The Black Sea crossing, and a direction problem

The Bar Sauma article names Simisso, that is Samsun, as the port where he took ship **[A]**. The Yahballaha III itinerary begins "Trabzon, Constantinople, Kingdom of Naples, Rome, Florence, Genoa, Lombardy, Paris, Bordeaux, back to Genoa and Rome" **[A]**, putting Trebizond outbound. The gathering pass had made it the *return* crossing, which no source supports **[flag; corrected]**. Trabzon is treated as an outbound landfall, Samsun as the port of embarkation, the return port as unrecorded.

### 6.2 Constantinople, Etna, and the sea fight

Received by Andronikos II Palaiologos, viewing the relics of the city and Hagia Sophia **[A: Wikipedia, "Yahballaha III"; Budge]**. Etna in eruption, 18 June 1287 **[A: Wikipedia, "Rabban Bar Sauma"]**.

The naval battle carries a three-part correction, stated in full because the atlas would otherwise carry the error forward. **Date:** the Battle of the Counts was fought 23 June 1287 **[A: Wikipedia, "Battle of the Counts"]**, while his account renders it 24 June, Saint John's Day **[A: Budge]**; two lenses split on this and both readings are printed. **Place:** normally the Bay of Naples, the Angevin fleet beached near Castellammare di Stabia, about 40.70 N, 14.48 E; "Bay of Sorrento" is the Bar Sauma article's phrasing and is not where the battle is usually put **[flag]**. **Belligerents:** Charles II was not commanding, being an Aragonese prisoner from 1284 to 1288; the Angevin fleet was led by Reynald Quarrel of Avella under Narjot de Toucy against Roger of Lauria **[A: same]**. The twelve thousand dead is Bar Sauma's own figure; the battle article records forty galleys and about five thousand crew taken.

### 6.3 Rome, first visit

He arrives to find Honorius IV recently dead and the throne vacant, and deals instead with the College of Cardinals, who question him closely **[A: Wikipedia, "Rabban Bar Sauma"]**. Five verbatim passages from Budge belong to this exchange: the announcement of the embassy, the eastern mission field ("many of our Fathers have gone into the countries of the Mongols, and Turks, and Chinese"), the refusal of Roman precedence ("No man hath come to us Orientals from the Pope."), the dispute over Trinitarian procession, and the compact confession ("The Father is the Begetter, the Son is the Begotten, and Holy Spirit proceedeth.") **[A]**. Four are carried into the journey file; the cardinals then concede they were testing him **[A]**.

### 6.4 Genoa, the winter, and a misidentified interlocutor

He winters in Genoa **[A]**. His complaint about the vacancy ("Behold I have been a year of days [since I came], and a Pope hath not sat.") is verbatim at Budge, pp. 187-188 **[A]**, but two attached details were wrong **[flag; corrected]**: the man addressed is not "John of Tusculum" but the *Periodeutes*, the Visitor of Mar Papa from Allemagne, whom Budge's footnote has Chabot identifying with John of Jerusalem; and the exchange falls at the end of the winter, early 1288, not 1287.

### 6.5 Paris and Bordeaux, with an unresolved year

Paris: about a month with Philip IV, who shows him the relics and the learning of the city and declares willingness to join **[A for the month and reception: Budge; R for the Sainte-Chapelle, unnamed in the Syriac]**. Gobert de Helleville is appointed to accompany him east, departing 2 February 1288 **[A]**.

Bordeaux: Edward I, then in his French territories, receives him, answers warmly, and takes communion from his hand **[A: Budge, p. 186]**. The quotation was cited to p. 232 and printed "King Arghen"; both are wrong, Budge p. 186 reading "King Arghon" **[flag; corrected]**. Budge also hedges the location, printing "Kasonia (Gascony?)" and "their city [BORDEAUX?]", so Bordeaux is an editorial conjecture **[R]**.

**Year unresolved.** 1287 in one lens, winter 1287-1288 in another, early 1288 by the Wikipedia ordering **[flag]**. Nothing consulted fixes it; both stops are dated 1288 with the conflict printed in the confidence field.

### 6.6 Rome, second visit

Nicholas IV is elected, ending the vacancy **[A]**. Bar Sauma returns, is formally received, and takes communion from the Pope's own hand on Palm Sunday **[A]**; his account treats this as the height of the embassy **[R: an editorial reading]**. The source gives the feast, not the day. Julian Easter 1288 falls on 28 March, so Palm Sunday is 21 March, stated in the confidence field rather than in prose.

The leave-taking and the relics are verbatim in Budge at p. 195, not p. 241 as first cited **[flag; corrected]**. Two fidelity notes: Budge prints an editorial query, "for your service (?)", and the marked ellipsis elides a clause about showing the kings at home what the Pope had done for him. The relics are a piece of Christ's apparel, a piece of the Virgin's kerchief, and fragments of saints; the claim that they went into a church at Maragheh is **not** in the passage **[flag]**.

## 7. The return, the letter, the silence, 1288 - 1294

He is back in Baghdad by the summer of 1288 with letters, relics, and gifts for Arghun **[A]**.
**Arghun's letter of 1289.** Dated 11 May 1289 to Philip IV, carried west by Buscarello de Ghizolfi on a separate third mission **[A: Wikipedia, "Buscarello de Ghizolfi"]**. The gathering pass had it promising action "by spring"; it names instead the end of the Tiger year's winter, that is winter 1290-1291, and offers Jerusalem **[flag; corrected]**.

**The quotation from that letter is the weakest item in the pool and is dropped from the journey file.** The English wording ("Under the power of the eternal sky... I shall give you Jerusalem") is a real circulating text carried by the English Wikipedia article, referenced there only to "Letter from Arghun to Philippe le Bel, 1289, France royal archives". The attribution offered to Mostaert and Cleaves, "Trois documents mongols des Archives secretes vaticanes", *HJAS* 15 (1952), is self-contradictory on its face: that article concerns *Vatican* documents, while this letter is in the Archives Nationales de France. The Mostaert-Cleaves edition that does cover it (1962) gives a *French* translation. There is also an unmarked elision, and the colophon places the writing at Ho'ndlon, not Tabriz **[flag; quote dropped]**.

No joint campaign materializes. Acre falls in 1291 **[A as an event: Wikipedia, "Siege of Acre (1291)", and molay.journey.json; R as a consequence of the embassy's failure]**.

**The last years, and the church.** One lens has him at Baghdad serving Yahballaha III and building a church there **[R]**; another sends the relics into a church at Maragheh **[flag; unsupported]**. He is called the Ilkhanate's visitor-general for the Church of the East in these years **[A: Wikipedia, "Rabban Bar Sauma"]**. One stop carries the church, tagged reconstruction, with the split printed.

He composes, or has composed under his direction, the account of his travels, in Persian **[A]**. He dies in Baghdad in January 1294, aged roughly seventy-three or seventy-four **[A]**; no source gives the day. Yahballaha III dies at Maragheh on 13 November 1317 **[A: Wikipedia, "Yahballaha III"]**, outliving his teacher by twenty-three years, not the "nearly three decades" one lens gave **[flag; corrected]**.

## 8. Afterlife of the text and of the name

- Burial reportedly inside the church of Darath Rhomaye, the Convent of the Romans, in Baghdad, north of the altar **[R: Grokipedia, AI-compiled tertiary, not cross-checked]**.
- No tomb, marker, or identified modern site was located **[gap stated, not a finding of absence]**; Baghdad's Church of the East fabric was destroyed repeatedly after the Ilkhanid period.
- Translations **[A]**: Montgomery, *The History of Yaballaha III, Nestorian Patriarch, and of His Vicar Bar Sauma*, 1927, the first in English; Budge, *The Monks of Kublai Khan, Emperor of China*, 1928, the more widely read edition and the source of every quotation used here; Borbone's critical edition in English by Laura E. Parodi, 2021 (year of the Italian original unconfirmed); Carlson, 2025.
- Morris Rossabi, *Voyager from Xanadu*, generally cited to 1992, credited with returning him to non-specialist English readers **[A for the book; year provisional]**.
- "The reverse Marco Polo" is a modern label applied retrospectively **[R]**; no medieval cult, veneration, or legend cycle was found in anything consulted. The distinction matters for register, and the phrase is kept out of the campa voice.

## 9. Contradictions carried forward, not resolved

Each is flagged in place above and printed in the relevant stop's confidence field.

1. **Denha's seat.** Baghdad versus Maragha; whether the monks reached Maragheh first stays open.
2. **Koshang.** Dongsheng versus Olon Süme, about 240 km apart; the claim text ("north of the Yellow River bend") fits Olon Süme, Wikipedia's identification fits Dongsheng.
3. **The father's office.** Chorbishop versus chief examiner of religious teaching. Neither sourced, both dropped.
4. **The sea fight.** 23 June versus 24 June; Bay of Naples versus Bay of Sorrento.
5. **Paris and Bordeaux.** 1287 versus early 1288. **The commissioning seat.** Maragha versus Tabriz.
6. **The church.** Baghdad versus Maragheh, the latter unsupported in the passage cited for it.
7. **Trebizond.** Outbound versus return crossing; return port unrecorded.

## 10. Gaps stated as gaps

- Day of death unknown; only January 1294. Burial site unlocated on the ground.
- The Persian original is lost. Every word we have of his passed through an anonymous compiler.
- No independent witness to the embassy was located: no Byzantine, French, or English chancery record of the 1287-1288 visits was consulted, which is the obvious next line of work. The 1289 Arghun letter in the Archives Nationales is the one western-archived document adjacent to the mission.
- Etna's 1287 eruption was not checked against a volcanological catalogue. Whether Bar Sauma ever met Marco Polo, or knew of him, is nowhere attested and is not claimed.

## 11. Atlas interlocks

Six existing travelers touch this life and are named in campa where the paths cross. In each case the atlas file's own content is **[A]** and the connection to Bar Sauma is **[R]**, no existing file mentioning him.

- **marco_polo.** Polo's road ran Baghdad to Tabriz to Kashgar, with Nestorian churches noted at Kashgar and Ganzhou: the same ground in reverse. He escorted Kokotchin toward Arghun in 1291 and learned at Hormuz that Arghun, Bar Sauma's patron, was dead. He dictated his book at Genoa in 1298, where Bar Sauma had wintered eleven years earlier.
- **kublai_khan.** Sorghaghtani Beki, Kublai's mother, was a Kerait Christian of the Church of the East. Kublai charged the Polos to bring back a hundred men learned in the Christian law, and sent to his Ilkhanid kin for the Xiangyang engineers just before Bar Sauma made the same Khanbaliq-to-Persia leg. He dies in February 1294, weeks after Bar Sauma, in the city where Bar Sauma was born.
- **genghis_khan.** The Baljuna covenant of 1203 bound nineteen companions of six peoples and three faiths, Nestorian Christian, Muslim, and shaman: where the tolerance that made a Zhongdu-born Nestorian a plausible Mongol envoy begins.
- **molay.** Molay staked the Temple on a Franco-Mongol alliance with Ghazan, Arghun's son; pleaded the crusade with Edward I; was arrested by Philip IV. Both kings had received Bar Sauma.
- **ramon_llull.** Llull carried a recovery plan including a mission to the Tatar khans to Nicholas IV, the pope who gave Bar Sauma communion, and Llull too found a dead pope waiting in Rome.
- **kircher.** Kircher printed the Nestorian Stele of Xi'an in *China Illustrata*, 1667, the material evidence for the church Bar Sauma was born into.
