# Andrew Jackson (1767-1845): research report
*2026-08-10. Compiled for the Atlas of Journeys from a five-lens verified pool (chronology, geography, quotes, interlock, afterlife). Seventh President; major general of the Tennessee militia and then of the U.S. Army; signer of the Indian Removal Act; destroyer of the Second Bank of the United States.*

**Legend.** **[A]** = attested, source named. **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition, or attribution with no located primary. Where two lenses disagree, the disagreement is printed and left standing. Coordinates given are the corrected values used in the journey file; where the raw pool was wrong, the error is noted.

---

## 1. The Waxhaws and the Revolution (1767-1781)

**Birth, 15 March 1767 [A date / R place].** Born to Scots-Irish parents in the Waxhaws on the Carolina border. The date is not disputed; the *state* is. Both Carolinas claim the cabin and no contemporary record fixes it, because neither colony had run a survey line through the district (Wikipedia; Miller Center; History.com). **Left unresolved.** The journey file pins Andrew Jackson State Park, Lancaster County SC (34.841, -80.807) as the conventional marker, not as an adjudication. *Pool coordinate 34.8968, -80.7423 fell about 8.5 km northeast, in open country.*

**The father's grave [R].** Andrew Jackson Sr. is said to lie in the churchyard of Old Waxhaw Presbyterian Church (34.858, -80.766) (cemetery records; Remini vol. 1, secondhand). Reconstruction, not document.

**Hugh Jackson, 20 June 1779 [A].** The eldest brother dies of heat exhaustion after Stono Ferry (32.7789, -80.198). **Correction:** the chronology lens placed all three family deaths in 1781. Hugh dies June 1779; Robert late spring 1781; Elizabeth November 1781 (Wikipedia, *Elizabeth Hutchinson Jackson*). The action itself was an American assault on an entrenched British covering force at the Stono ferry and was repulsed (Wikipedia, *Battle of Stono Ferry*).

**The Charleston inheritance, c. 1783 [R].** A legacy from his grandfather Hugh Jackson in Carrickfergus reaches him in his teens and is spent in Charleston on horse racing and gaming. Standard in Parton and Remini; no primary accounting survives, so the sum and the manner of its loss are tradition.

**Camden, 16 August 1780 [R]. Dropped.** Tradition places the teenage brothers near the militia lines at the defeat at Camden; no contemporary document confirms it, and the claim descends through Parton (1860). The better-attested engagement is Hanging Rock, August 1780. Too thin to pin. *Pool coordinate 34.2551, -80.5748 pointed at neither the town nor the battlefield, nine miles north near 34.35, -80.61.*

**The saber, April 1781 [A].** Dragoons take Andrew and Robert at a relative's farmhouse (34.905, -80.75); ordered to clean an officer's boots, Andrew refuses and is slashed across head and hand. The source is Jackson's own retelling decades later, in Parton, corroborated by Remini: attested as testimony, not as independent record. **Camden gaol, spring 1781 [A]:** marched some forty miles to Camden (34.2578, -80.607), both take smallpox in the jail and Robert dies of it soon after release.

**Charleston, November 1781 [A].** Elizabeth Jackson dies at Charleston (32.7765, -79.9311) after nursing American prisoners on British ships. **Contradiction flagged:** the geography lens gives cholera; other standard accounts give ship fever or typhus. Not adjudicated. Her burial place is never recovered.

## 2. The law and the western district (1784-1806)

**Salisbury, 1784-1787 [A].** Reads law in Rowan County NC (35.6709, -80.4742) under Spruce Macay then Colonel John Stokes, with a local reputation for tavern brawling alongside the study (Remini vol. 1, secondhand). **Admitted to the North Carolina bar, September 1787 [A]** (Wikipedia).

**Nashville, October 1788 [A].** Practises briefly at Jonesborough, then arrives at Nashville (Fort Nashborough, 36.1636, -86.7744) as public prosecutor, boarding with the widow Donelson and her daughter Rachel. **Anachronism corrected:** the chronology lens called the district the Southwest Territory. That territory was not created until 1790; in October 1788 this was the **Western District of North Carolina**.

**The marriage, 1791 [R] / 1794 [A].** The pool contains this in two incompatible forms.
- *Traditional account:* Jackson marries Rachel Donelson Robards at Springfield Plantation near Fayette MS in August 1791, believing her divorce from Lewis Robards final; it is not, and they remarry in January 1794. The geography lens tagged this **[A]**.
- *Documentary position:* per Wikipedia, *Springfield Plantation (Fayette, Mississippi)*, **there is no documentary evidence that any 1791 ceremony took place at all**: not at Springfield, not elsewhere in Mississippi, not anywhere. The **January 1794 Nashville ceremony is documented [A]** (Miller Center).

The journey file carries Springfield as tradition, with the hedge in `date_confidence`, and does not assert the 1791 wedding occurred. *Pool coordinate 31.5432, -91.142 and locality "Bruinsburg" are both wrong; Springfield is at 31.7461, -91.1931.* No source disputes that the irregularity is used against Rachel for the rest of her life.

**Congress, 1796-1798 [A].** Helps draft the Tennessee constitution; first U.S. Representative from the state, 1796; senator, 1797; resigns 1798 for a judgeship on the Tennessee Superior Court (History.com; Miller Center). The federal government sits at Philadelphia in these years, so the journey file uses the atlas's existing Philadelphia pin (39.9526, -75.1652).

**Major general, 1802 [A]. Correction.** The chronology lens dated his election as major general of the Tennessee militia to 1812. He held the rank from **1802** (Wikipedia).

**The Hermitage, from 1804 [A].** Buys the Davidson County tract (36.22, -86.61), worked by an enslaved population growing past a hundred by his death (Andrew Jackson Foundation).

**The Dickinson duel, 30 May 1806 [A].** Kills Charles Dickinson on the Red River near Adairville KY (36.67, -86.86), taking a bullet that stays near his heart for thirty-nine years.

**The Benton brawl, 4 September 1813 [A].** A gun battle outside the City Hotel, Nashville (36.1627, -86.7816) with Jesse Benton leaves a second bullet in his shoulder. Thomas Hart Benton later becomes the Senate ally who moves to expunge his censure. The pool does not remark on the reversal; it is left as juxtaposition.

## 3. The Creek War (1813-1814)

**Fort Mims, 30 August 1813 [A].** Red Sticks overrun the stockade in Baldwin County AL, killing hundreds of settlers and allied Creeks; the massacre becomes the justification for the campaign (Alabama Historical Commission). *Pool coordinate 31.235, -87.748 was about 11 km off; corrected to 31.181, -87.838.*

**Tallushatchee, 3 November 1813 [A].** Coffee's detachment destroys the town, killing roughly 186 warriors; Jackson sends an orphaned infant found among the dead, Lyncoya, to the Hermitage. *Pool placed the town "near Jacksonville, AL" at 33.814, -85.8; it was near present-day **Alexandria**, 33.8123, -85.9069.*

**Talladega (9 Nov 1813), Fort Strother (winter 1813-14), Emuckfau and Enitachopco (Jan 1814) [A].** Relief of besieged allied Creeks; near-mutiny of starving militia at the forward base; two nearly disastrous engagements on the retreat (Remini vol. 1, secondhand). *Fort Strother corrected from 33.79, -86.15 to 33.7636, -86.0475.* Compressed in the journey file rather than pinned separately.

**Horseshoe Bend, 27 March 1814 [A].** Jackson's force, including Cherokee and allied Creek fighters, destroys Tohopeka in a bend of the Tallapoosa (32.9799, -85.7377), killing over 800 warriors and breaking Creek military power (NPS).

**Treaty of Fort Jackson, 9 August 1814 [A]. Figure disputed.** The cession is taken from the whole Creek Nation, **including the allies who fought beside him**. Size given as **23 million acres** by Remini and as **over 20 million** by Wikipedia and history.state.gov. Neither is adopted; the journey file hedges. *Coordinate corrected from 32.531, -86.213 to 32.5033, -86.2564.*

## 4. The Floridas and New Orleans (1814-1821)

**Pensacola, 7 November 1814 [A].** Storms Spanish-held Pensacola (30.4213, -87.2169) with no authorization from Washington, driving out a British force using the harbour; not disciplined for it.

**New Orleans, December 1814 [A].** Declares martial law over the city (29.9511, -90.0715, the atlas's existing New Orleans pin) and takes into his line regulars, militia, two battalions of free men of colour, Choctaw fighters, and Lafitte's Baratarians.

**Chalmette, 8 January 1815 [A]. Casualty claim corrected.** The geography lens claimed "over 2,000 British troops killed": wrong by an order of magnitude. **British killed numbered 291**; 2,037 was killed, wounded, and missing or captured together, against **71 American casualties, 13 of them dead** (Wikipedia, *Battle of New Orleans*). The journey file says casualties, not dead. Fought after the Treaty of Ghent had ended the war, before news reached either army. *Pool coordinate 29.9377, -89.9989 sat about 700 m south of the battlefield (29.9433, -89.9958).*

**San Marcos de Apalache, 7 April 1818 [A].** Takes the Spanish fort at St. Marks (30.158, -84.201) and executes two British subjects, Arbuthnot and Ambrister, for aiding the Seminoles. **Pensacola again, 24 May 1818 [A]:** a second unauthorized occupation provokes an international incident and pressures Spain into the Adams-Onis cession. Jackson serves briefly as territorial governor in 1821.

## 5. The corrupt bargain and the presidency (1823-1830)

**Senate, 1823 [A].** Returns to Washington as senator from Tennessee (Miller Center; Wikipedia).

**The House election, February 1825 [A].** Leads in both popular and electoral vote in a four-way race without a majority; loses in the House to John Quincy Adams after Henry Clay throws his support to Adams and is then named Secretary of State. Jackson resigns his seat and campaigns on the corrupt bargain for four years (History.com; Miller Center). **1828 [A]:** defeats Adams in a campaign that prints attacks on Rachel over the irregular marriage.

**Rachel Jackson, 22 December 1828 [A].** Dies of a heart attack at the Hermitage weeks after the election. Jackson blames his political enemies and never remarries (Andrew Jackson Foundation; History.com).

**Inauguration, 4 March 1829 [A].** Sworn in on the Capitol's East Portico (38.8899, -77.0091); the crowd later overruns the White House reception, in what critics call the reign of King Mob (White House Historical Association). **The Eaton affair, 1829-1831 [A]** splits the cabinet, elevates Van Buren, and ruins Calhoun's standing with him (Miller Center); he governs through the circle the press names the Kitchen Cabinet.

## 6. Removal (1830-1839)

**Indian Removal Act, 28 May 1830 [A].** Authorizes negotiated exchange of tribal land east of the Mississippi for territory west of it; nearly seventy removal treaties follow (National Archives, act text; history.state.gov). **Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek, 27 September 1830 [A]:** first under the act, the Choctaw ceding their last Mississippi land (33.115, -88.595).

**Cherokee Nation v. Georgia (1831); Worcester v. Georgia (1832) [A].** The Court first holds the Cherokee a "domestic dependent nation" unable to sue Georgia directly, then holds Georgia's laws without force in Cherokee territory. Jackson declines to enforce Worcester (Miller Center; history.state.gov). **"John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it" [R]:** widely repeated, **not located in any contemporary primary source**, regarded by scholars as apocryphal. Carried in no `quote` field; described in prose as an attribution, because the non-enforcement it summarizes is real.

**Treaty of New Echota, 29 December 1835 [A].** A minority faction, unauthorized by the national council, cedes all Cherokee land east of the Mississippi (34.5394, -84.9086); John Ross and the great majority oppose it. **Ratified by the Senate in May 1836 by a single vote** (NPS; National Archives). *Pool coordinate 34.4258, -84.9394 was about 13 km off.*

**The removal, 1838-1839 [A]. Death toll contradiction flagged.** Scott's troops begin the roundup May 1838; Ross negotiates control of the migration; removal is declared complete March 1839. The **chronology lens** says "more than a thousand people" died. The **geography lens**, citing the same NPS source, says **4,000**. The two lenses contradict each other on one event. The standard estimate is approximately **4,000 dead, published range 4,000 to 8,000** (NPS; Wikipedia, *Trail of Tears*); the journey file uses it and names it as an estimate. The lower figure is recorded here as an error in the pool, not as a live scholarly alternative.

## 7. The Bank War (1832-1837)

**The veto, 10 July 1832 [A].** Congress passes an early recharter at Biddle's urging, with Clay and Webster, to force the issue into the election. Jackson vetoes and returns the bill to the Senate, in which it originated. Full text from the Avalon Project: the best-sourced material in the pool, and every Bank-War quotation in the journey file is verbatim from it. **Note corrected:** the pool called "I have now done my duty to my country..." the closing sentence. It is not; two sentences and the signature follow. It is near the close. **Reelection, November 1832 [A]** is read by Jackson as a mandate to destroy the Bank outright.

**Nullification, November 1832 to 1833 [A].** South Carolina's convention (34.0007, -81.0348), with Calhoun behind it, declares the tariffs void within the state. Jackson answers with a proclamation and secures force authority; the state backs down after a compromise tariff (History.com; SC Dept. of Archives and History).

**Removal of the deposits, September-October 1833 [A]. Sequence corrected on three points.** On **18 September 1833** Jackson reads his removal paper to the cabinet; **Taney was not yet Treasury Secretary.** William Duane held the office until 22 September and was dismissed for refusing; Taney was appointed 23 September; removals began 1 October. The pool's "two previous Treasury secretaries he had already fired for refusing the order" is false: Ingham was purged in 1831 over the Eaton affair and McLane was transferred to State, so **only Duane was dismissed over the deposits**. And the afterlife lens called Taney the **third** Treasury Secretary; he was the **fourth**, after Ingham (1829-31), McLane (1831-33), and Duane (May-Sept 1833). Source: Wikipedia, *United States Secretary of the Treasury*.

**Pet banks and Biddle's panic, 1833-1834 [A].** Deposits go to selected state banks, seven initially and past thirty within a few years, Girard Bank among the first. Biddle contracts credit nationwide to force a recharter; the manoeuvre backfires politically (Remini vol. 3 and Sellers, both secondhand).

**Censure, 28 March 1834 [A].** Clay carries a censure for an unconstitutional assumption of power, the first of a sitting president; Jackson calls the vote itself unconstitutional (U.S. Senate Historical Office). **Expunged January 1837 [A]** at Benton's urging.

**The national debt, January 1835 [A]. Framing corrected.** The chronology lens said Jackson "signs legislation paying off the entire national debt." **No such act exists.** The debt was extinguished out of accumulated surplus from public land sales and tariff revenue under existing law: the only time in the nation's history it reached zero, resting on the same land boom running through the pet banks that the Specie Circular is issued to stop eighteen months later. The journey file states a retirement, not a signing.

**Richard Lawrence, 30 January 1835 [A]. Age corrected.** Both pistols misfire at the Capitol; Jackson beats him with his cane. The pool said "in his seventies." He was **67**.

**Specie Circular, 11 July 1836 [A].** Gold or silver only in payment for federal land (National Archives). *Treasury coordinate corrected from 38.8934, -77.0322, near the Ellipse, to 38.8976, -77.0343.*

**Panic of 1837 [A as event, R as causation].** A severe crisis breaks weeks after he leaves office. **Causation contested:** Peter Temin and others attribute it substantially to international bullion flows rather than to Jackson's order. The journey file states the sequence and names the disagreement.

**Farewell Address, 4 March 1837 [A].** Full text from the Miller Center; three sentences carried verbatim. **Rejected as a misquote:** the pool's "It will foster this eager desire to amass wealth without labor, and will stimulate that spirit of speculation, and inevitably lead to corruption, which will find its way into your public councils" is a **splice of two separate passages** and is not used. The "spirit of speculation" language belongs to an earlier paragraph entirely.

**"The Bank is trying to kill me, but I will kill it" [R].** Attributed in conversation with Van Buren and repeated across the Bank War literature; **not located verbatim in any primary document**. Not carried as a quotation. Same status for "I killed the Bank."

## 8. Death and afterlife (1845-2021)

**Death 8 June 1845 [A]; burial 10 June 1845 [A, one confirming lookup still wanted].** Dies at the Hermitage of chronic illness including heart failure, tuberculosis, and complications from the Dickinson bullet. **Correction:** the afterlife lens said "buried the next day." He died Sunday 8 June; funeral and interment were Tuesday **10 June**. That lens could not re-fetch a primary source (Hermitage and UCSB pages 404, search budget exhausted), so this rests on standard biographies. Buried in the garden tomb beside Rachel; the epitaph names only "General Andrew Jackson." *Tomb coordinate corrected from 36.2206, -86.6082, off the developed grounds, to approximately 36.2148, -86.6124.*

**The parrot [R]. Not carried.** Legend holds that his African grey, Poll, was removed from the funeral for swearing. The only source is a secondhand recollection attributed to Rev. William Menefee Norment, uncorroborated by any contemporary 1845 account.

**Clark Mills's equestrian statue, 8 January 1853 [A].** Lafayette Square: first equestrian statue cast in the United States and the first anywhere balanced on two hind legs without support (Smithsonian American Art Museum; NPS). *The pool record was malformed, carrying `lat 38.9` with **no `lng` field at all**, so it would not have plotted.* Further casts at Jackson Square, New Orleans (1856) and the Tennessee capitol grounds (1880). **Monument politics, 2010s-2020s [A]:** the New Orleans bronze has been defaced and petitioned against on account of the Removal Act and, unlike several Confederate monuments the city removed in the same years, remains standing.

**The twenty-dollar bill from 1928 [A];** Treasury's Tubman redesign announced 2016, delayed, revived 2021, no bill issued as of the mid-2020s. **Trail of Tears National Historic Trail, 1987 [A]:** established by Congress, multiple routes across nine states, NPS-administered.

**The Papers of Andrew Jackson [R].** University of Tennessee Press, begun in the 1970s, more than a dozen volumes, coverage into the 1830s. Volume count and current terminus not verified against the press catalogue; recorded as approximate.

## 9. Interlocks with the existing corpus

**Attested from file text [A]:**
- **`marques_de_lafayette`** states that an equestrian Jackson stands at the centre of Lafayette Square, with Lafayette's standing bronze (1891) at one corner alongside Rochambeau, Kosciuszko, and von Steuben. Its line, "Andrew Jackson rides the middle of the square on his horse," is carried on Jackson's Lafayette Square stop.
- **`thomas_jefferson`** documents the Jefferson-Hamilton quarrel over Hamilton's national bank. The Second Bank Jackson kills in 1832 succeeds Hamilton's First Bank (1791-1811, re-chartered 1816); the veto is the second act of a fight the atlas already stages. That file's Hamilton sentence is carried on Jackson's Philadelphia stop.
- **`sitting_bull`** and **`low_dog`**, the corpus's only completed Native American travelers, both cross-reference **`julio_argentino_roca`**, setting the 1881 Fort Buford surrenders against the Conquista del Desierto. Jackson's 1830 statute is the earlier American ancestor of the same apparatus; Low Dog's 1881 Leavenworth line is carried on Jackson's Ross's Landing stop.
- **QUEUE.md** names his interlock target as "sitting_bull, low_dog, pocahontas already done/queued." `pocahontas.journey.json` does not yet exist.

**Reconstructed [R]:** **`george_washington`** documents Hamilton drafting Washington's Farewell Address, which Jackson's own Farewell of 1837 answers; the journey file sets the two side by side. **`symmes`** places John Cleves Symmes Jr as a garrison officer at New Orleans in the early 1800s, captain of infantry in 1813: the same theatre and war in which Jackson wins the battle that makes him.

**Gaps, stated as gaps.** **No completed atlas journey covers the Bank War.** A grep of all `*.journey.json` for "Bank of the United States," "Specie Circular," "pet bank," and "Biddle" returns nothing; the only banking material committed is Jefferson's Hamilton feud and `salomon_oppenheim`'s unrelated Rhineland house, and the QUEUE's banking cluster (fuggers, medicis, warburgs, barings, morgans, william_paterson, johan_palmstruch, adam_smith, alexander_hamilton) **exists nowhere as files**. **The requested canonical pins do not apply:** no source places Jackson at the Kaaba, Temple Mount, Paris, or Buenos Aires, and no journey file connects him. Recorded as absence, not forced. The pins that do recur, all inherited byte-identical, are Washington D.C. (38.8913, -77.0261), Philadelphia (39.9526, -75.1652), New Orleans (29.9511, -90.0715), and Lafayette Square (38.8995, -77.0365).
