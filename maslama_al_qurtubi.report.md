# Maslama b. Qasim al-Qurtubi (d. 353/964)

*Research report. Cordoba; the man of charms and talismans; the Rutbat al-Hakim and the Ghayat al-Hakim; six centuries under another man's name.*

Legend: **[A]** = attested, source named. **[R]** = reconstruction, inference, or scholarly tradition. Contradictions are flagged where found and left standing.

A warning that governs the whole file: **the single most important source for this subject, Maribel Fierro's 1996 Studia Islamica article, was not reachable** (JSTOR paywall, 403 on direct fetch). Every claim credited to Fierro below arrives through the footnote apparatus of the English Wikipedia "Picatrix" article, not through her prose.

---

## 1. The man himself: what exists

Almost nothing. This is the finding, not a preface to the finding.

- Full name **Maslama ibn al-Qasim ibn Ibrahim ibn Abd Allah ibn Hatim al-Qurtubi al-Zayyat** **[A: Spanish Wikipedia, "Picatrix", seen verbatim; the chain derives from Ibn al-Faradi via Fierro, neither reached directly]**.
- He **died in 353 AH / 964 CE** **[A: Fierro 1996, relayed via English Wikipedia "Picatrix" fn. 3; the same date on the "Maslama al-Majriti" page corroborates the relay, not the underlying source]**.
- The tenth-century Cordoban biographer **Ibn al-Faradi** describes him as **"a man of charms and talismans"** (sahib al-ruqa wa-l-tilasmat) **[A: phrase verbatim on English Wikipedia "Picatrix", attributed there to Ibn al-Faradi via Fierro; the Arabic was not consulted]**.
- Fierro places him in **Andalusian Batini and Sufi-adjacent currents** **[R: Wikipedia's gloss, "If this suggestion is correct it would place the work in the context of Andalusian sufism and batinism", not a quotation of Fierro]**.

**Stated gaps, not papered over.** No birth date. No birthplace beyond what the nisba implies. No teacher, no student, no house, no quarter. **No burial place is recorded for him anywhere, and none for al-Majriti either** **[R: absence across every source consulted; the silence is worth noting, since biographical-dictionary entries often do record burial]**.

### The nisba does not do the work usually assigned to it

**"Al-Qurtubi" does not distinguish the two Maslamas**, though the opposite is widely repeated. The astronomer's own full name is *Abu 'l-Qasim Maslama ibn Ahmad al-Faradi al-Hasib al-Majriti al-Qurtubi al-Andalusi* **[A: English Wikipedia, "Maslama al-Majriti"]**. Both men are "the Cordoban". What separates them is the **patronymic** (ibn Qasim versus ibn Ahmad) and the **death date** (353/964 versus 398/1007). A further trap: the astronomer also carries **al-Faradi**, one letter of context away from **Ibn al-Faradi**, the biographer whose single line is the whole evidentiary basis for the other Maslama. Any source that abbreviates can produce a chimera.

## 2. The two books

- **Rutbat al-Hakim** ("The Sage's Step" / "The Rank of the Wise"), alchemical. Standard Fierro/Pingree dating: **composed 339-342 AH / 950-953 CE** **[R: standard modern dating; the figure most often relayed]**.
- **Ghayat al-Hakim** ("The Goal of the Sage"), the astral-magic compendium later known in Latin as *Picatrix*. Its own text **states completion in 348 AH / c. 959 CE** **[A: internal statement of the Arabic text, reported in English Wikipedia "Picatrix" citing David Pingree, "Some of the Sources of the Ghayat al-hakim", JWCI 43 (1980), p. 2]**. Standard dating of its composition: **343-348 AH / 954-959 CE**.

### Three contradictions on the dating, flagged and left standing

1. **The date 343 AH.** English Wikipedia's "Picatrix" states that the Ghaya's author "began after finishing Rutbat al-Hakim in 343 AH (~954 CE)". Read carelessly this makes 343 the *completion date of the Rutbat*; read against the standard Fierro/Pingree scheme it is the *start of the Ghaya*. The two readings are not compatible and are not adjudicated. The Rutbat is presented as a range (339-342), never as a colophon year.
2. **"Circa 1009" for the Rutbat.** English Wikipedia's **"Maslama al-Majriti"** dates the Rutbat to **"circa 1009"** **[A: "the two works are the 'Sage's Step/The Rank of the Wise' (Rutbat al-hakim, ?1009) and the Picatrix"]**. A third figure, incompatible with 343/954, with 339-342/950-953, and with al-Majriti's own 1007 death date on the same page.
3. **The eleventh-century consensus.** Both the English and Spanish "Picatrix" articles report scholarly consensus placing the original in the **middle of the eleventh century**: "a 400-page Arabic book of magic and astrology, which most scholars assume was originally written in the middle of the 11th century" **[A: seen verbatim; the Spanish article says the same]**. This flatly contradicts the internal 348/959 date reported in the same articles, and it is the tension the whole Majriti/Qurtubi debate turns on.

## 3. The other Maslama: al-Majriti the astronomer

- **Born c. 950 at Majrit (Madrid)**; **died at Cordoba, conventionally 1007** (398 AH), with 1004-1008 given across reference works **[A: English and Spanish Wikipedia, "Maslama al-Majriti"]**.
- Astronomer, mathematician, and surveyor at Cordoba during the reign of **al-Hakam II (r. 961-976)**, later called "the Euclid of Spain"; **adapted and corrected al-Khwarizmi's zij** and **worked on Ptolemy's Planisphaerium** **[A: English Wikipedia, "Maslama al-Majriti"]**. Said al-Andalusi, *Tabaqat al-Umam*, calls him "the best mathematician and astronomer of his time in al-Andalus" **[A: sentence seen verbatim on the same page; Said's own text not reached]**.
- **Caution on patronage:** the sources reached establish that he was *active during* al-Hakam II's reign, not that he held formal caliphal patronage; "under the patronage of the caliph" overstates what is attested **[R]**. Spanish Wikipedia separately makes him an astrological adviser in the circle of the hajib **al-Mansur** **[A: "Maslama al-Mayriti"; not corroborated in English]**.

### Correction: the student list is contaminated

English Wikipedia lists his students as "Ibn al-Saffar, Abu al-Salt, and at-Turtushi". **Two of the three are chronologically impossible.** **Abu al-Salt** (Umayya ibn Abd al-Aziz) was born c. 1068 at Denia, died 1134 at Bejaia, and was taught by al-Waqqashi (1017-1095) **[A: Wikipedia, "Abu al-Salt"]**; **al-Turtushi** was born 1059 at Tortosa, died 1126 at Alexandria, and was taught by al-Baji **[A: Wikipedia, "Al-Turtushi"]**. Both were born fifty to sixty years **after** al-Majriti's death. The defensible students are **Ibn al-Saffar** (d. 1035 at Denia) and **Ibn al-Samh** (per Spanish Wikipedia) **[A]**. The corrected list is what the journey file carries.

### The argument from silence

**No surviving biographical notice of al-Majriti by contemporaries or near-contemporaries credits him with either book** **[A: English Wikipedia "Picatrix", summarizing Pingree and Fierro]**. Those same notices are detailed about his tables, his instruments, and his school, which is what gives the silence weight.

### Correction: the arithmetic of the chronological objection

English Wikipedia's "Picatrix" says the 348/959 completion date "predates al-Majriti's birth by roughly five decades". **This is wrong on its own encyclopedia's numbers.** A birth of c. 950 makes him **about nine years old in 959**, not unborn. The objection survives intact, a nine-year-old not having written a four-book compendium of astral magic, but it must be stated correctly; the "five decades" figure would require a birth c. 1009, which nothing supports. Similarly, "more than fifty years" between 959 and the death in 1007 should read **forty-eight years**.

**Also flagged:** Fierro's case is not chronological alone. It rests on the **positive identification** of a real Cordoban talisman-practitioner in Ibn al-Faradi, so framing it as "chronology independent of any positive case" overstates the argument and is avoided here **[R]**.

## 4. The misattribution: how the name got attached

- The **earliest known manuscript attribution** of the work to al-Majriti comes from the Mamluk alchemist **Aydamur al-Jildaki**, working at Cairo **[A: English Wikipedia, "Picatrix", traceable to the Ritter-Plessner and Pingree apparatus via Holmyard]**.
- **Contradiction flagged on his death date.** English Wikipedia's "Picatrix" (after Holmyard, after Brockelmann) says he "died shortly after 1360"; Wikipedia's **"Al-Jildaki"** gives the standard **743 AH / 1342-43 CE**, with 762 AH / 1361 as Brockelmann's alternative **[A]**. Both are reported, neither adopted. On the mainstream date the gap from the Ghaya's composition is about 385 years, not "four centuries".
- **Ibn Khaldun repeats the attribution** in the Muqaddima, drafted at **Qal'at Ibn Salama** during his retreat there **1375-1379** and dated to **1377** **[A: Wikipedia, "Qalat Ibn Salama"]**. He then spends his final decades at Cairo and dies in **1406**.
- **Two corrections.** (i) Ibn Khaldun is often called the source of the attribution. He is not: **al-Jildaki is the earliest known witness, Ibn Khaldun the amplifier** whose authority fixed a pre-existing claim. (ii) The "roughly two decades" said to separate them misreads Holmyard's "Ibn Khaldun died some 20 years later", which compares two **deaths** and is wrong regardless, Ibn Khaldun having died in 1406. On the mainstream al-Jildaki date the 1377 draft comes about thirty-five years after his death.

## 5. Transmission into Europe

- **Alfonso X of Castile** commissioned a **Castilian translation between 1256 and 1258**, per the Latin prologue's own testimony **[A: English Wikipedia "Picatrix", citing Pingree, *The Latin Version*, 1986, p. 3; corroborated by Spanish Wikipedia, "En torno a 1256, Alfonso X ordeno traducir al castellano y al latin el tratado arabe de magia talismanica"]**. A **Latin version was made from the Castilian**, not from the Arabic, and became the vehicle of European circulation **[A: same, plus Bakhouche et al., *Picatrix: Un traite de magie medievale*, Brepols, pp. 32-33]**. Only a **fragment of the Castilian survives**; the Latin survives complete **[R: standard account, not re-verified by live fetch]**.

### Correction: the translator and the title

Two claims in circulation were stripped: that **Yehuda ben Moshe** was the translator, and that the Castilian Picatrix is the **Libro de las formas e las ymagenes**. Neither the English nor the Spanish article names a translator, and the *Libro de las formas* is a separate Alfonsine compilation linked to the Picatrix only through manuscript context (Vatican Reg. lat. 1283a). **The Castilian translator is not securely identified** **[R, a gap]**. Whether the work was done at Toledo, Seville, or elsewhere in the itinerant court is **not specified** anywhere reached; Toledo is labelled in the journey file as a marker for the translation culture.

- **Reception:** Marsilio Ficino's **De vita libri tres (1489)**, whose third book is *De vita coelitus comparanda*, works the same astral-magic ground; **Giovanni Pico della Mirandola** numbers the book among his magical authorities **[R: standard in D. P. Walker and in Yates, NOT carried by Wikipedia's Ficino article, so kept sourced to Walker]**.
- A single Latin manuscript passed through a chain of English practitioners: **Simon Forman, Richard Napier, Elias Ashmole, William Lilly** **[A: as recorded in maslama_al_majriti.journey.json; none of the four has a journey file, noted as a gap]**.
- **El Escorial:** the Saadian sultan Zidan's library was **seized at sea in 1612 and incorporated in 1614** **[A]**. **Whether any Ghayat al-Hakim manuscript is among the seizure is not established** **[R; the tag is downgraded from the pool's [A], a stop whose only connection to the subject is unestablished not being attested]**.

## 6. Recovery of the Arabic, and the reattribution

- **Wilhelm Printz** identified an Arabic manuscript of the Ghaya at Istanbul in the **1920s**, the first Arabic text of the work known to European scholarship **[R: English Wikipedia "Picatrix", crediting Ritter's account]**.
- **Hellmut Ritter's Arabic edition**: *Studien der Bibliothek Warburg* **12**, **1933** **[A]**. **Correction:** a "1927 or 1933" conflict in circulation resolves to **1933**, the 1927 being an apparent footnote misreading, dropped rather than presented as a live conflict; the imprint is **Leipzig/Berlin (Teubner)**, not Hamburg, though the series belonged to the Hamburg library; and **Ritter was resident in Istanbul from 1926 to 1949** **[A: Wikipedia, "Hellmut Ritter"]**, so he was not in Germany when it appeared.
- **Ritter and Martin Plessner**, *"Picatrix": Das Ziel des Weisen von Pseudo-Magriti* (Warburg Institute, **1962**) **[A]**. The qualifier signals doubt thirty-four years before Fierro proposes another name.
- **Correction on the Warburg Institute's addresses:** relocated from Hamburg **1933**, installed at **Thames House 1934**, moved to the **Imperial Institute Buildings 1937**, reaching **Woburn Square only in 1958** **[A: Wikipedia, "Warburg Institute"]**. The 1962 and 1986 publications belong to Woburn Square; the 1933 arrival does not.
- **David Pingree**: "Some of the Sources of the Ghayat al-hakim", *JWCI* 43 (1980), pp. 1-15 **[A]**; "Between the Ghaya and Picatrix. I: The Spanish Version", *JWCI* 44 (1981), p. 27 **[R]**; *Picatrix: The Latin Version*, London, **1986**, in **Studies of the Warburg Institute 39**, not the "Surveys and Texts 6" series sometimes cited **[A]**.
- **Maribel Fierro**, "Batinism in Al-Andalus. Maslama b. Qasim al-Qurtubi (died 353/964), Author of the Rutbat al-Hakim and the Ghayat al-Hakim (Picatrix)", *Studia Islamica* **84 (1996), pp. 87-112** **[A as to existence, title, and journal, via English Wikipedia "Picatrix", fnn. 3, 24, 26, 27; full text NOT reached]**.
- **Julio Samso**, "Lunar mansions and timekeeping in Western Islam" (2017), reportedly distinguishes "Qurtubi (d. 353/964)" from the astronomer as settled ground **[R: Google Scholar snippet only]**.

### The reattribution has not been fully adopted

- **Dan Attrell and David Porreca**, *Picatrix: A Medieval Treatise on Astral Magic* (Penn State University Press, **2019**), the first complete modern English translation from Pingree's Latin, is **catalogued with "Maslamah ibn Ahmad al-Majriti" as author of record** twenty-three years after Fierro, whom the same volume cites **[A: English Wikipedia "Picatrix", fnn. 1 and 15]**.
- A JSTOR chapter, "From Occult Ekphrasis to Magical Art" (2018), still refers flatly to "the Muslim astronomer, alchemist and mathematician Maslama al-Majriti (d. c. 1008)" **[R: Google Scholar snippet only]**. Meanwhile some scholars treat the Arabic Picatrix as **effectively anonymous** pending fuller confirmation of the Qurtubi attribution **[A: English Wikipedia, "Picatrix"]**.

**Assessment, stated as such:** the dispute is live in practice even where it is settled in principle. The journey file carries al-Qurtubi as author because Fierro's argument is the best available, and carries the traditional attribution beside it because that is what the record still mostly says.

## 7. Source-tradition stops, and what they are not

The Ghaya's sources are Hermetic, Sabian, Ismaili, Indian, Nabataean, and encyclopedic **[A: English Wikipedia, "Picatrix"]**. The journey file includes four eastern stops for these (Kufa, Harran, old Basra, Baghdad). **None is a travel stop.** There is no evidence that Maslama al-Qurtubi left al-Andalus, and none that he did not; the sources are silent on his movements entirely. The eastern stops trace the movement of texts and are tagged as reconstruction.

Coordinate and dating corrections applied in the journey file:

- **Old Basra** 30.5085/47.7835 (modern city) to approximately **30.42 / 47.70**: the original garrison settlement lay about 15 km southwest, near al-Zubayr **[A: Wikipedia, "Basra"]**. The Brethren's Basra base and the dating of the Rasa'il are both debated, so the tag was downgraded from [A] to [R].
- **Madinat al-Zahra** 37.8814/-4.8501, open ground east-southeast of the site, to **37.888 / -4.867** **[A: Wikipedia, "Medina Azahara"]**. **Jagiellonian Library** 50.0247/19.9058 to **50.0615 / 19.9236**, an error of 4.4 km, the present building dating from 1931-1939. **Warburg-Haus** 53.5875/9.9847 to **53.5877 / 9.9961** (Heilwigstrasse 116).
- **Qal'at Ibn Salama** 35.0667/1.05, which is Frenda town itself, to approximately **34.99 / 1.04**, the fortress standing about three miles south of Frenda near Taoughzout.
- Ibn al-Faradi's eastern journey re-dated to the **early 990s**, his Mecca stop rewritten as **study at Mecca and Medina**, no source consulted attesting that he performed the hajj.

Canonical pins already in the atlas are reused byte-identical: **Cordoba 37.8882 / -4.7794**, **Toledo 39.8628 / -4.0273**, **Cairo 30.0444 / 31.2357**, **Mecca 21.4225 / 39.8262**, **Florence 43.7696 / 11.2558**, **London 51.5072 / -0.1276**.

## 8. Named links to other travelers

Two are named in campa text, each on a real relation: **maslama_al_majriti**, the astronomer whose name carried al-Qurtubi's books for six hundred years, the relation being the misattribution itself; and **giovanni_pico_della_mirandola**, a reader who numbered the Ghaya, in Latin dress and under the wrong name, among his own magical authorities. Deliberately **not** named, despite sharing Cordoba or al-Andalus: Maimonides, Ziryab, Ibn Arabi, Benjamin de Tudela, Abd al-Rahman al-Ghafiqi. Shared geography is not a link.
