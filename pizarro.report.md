# Pizarro — Trujillo to Cajamarca — report

**Sources.** Primary-canon backbone: Francisco de Xérez's *Verdadera relación de la conquista del Perú* (1534, Pizarro's own secretary, eyewitness at Cajamarca — the source of the one substantial Pizarro speech quoted here) and Agustín de Zárate's *Historia del descubrimiento y conquista del Perú* (1555, source for the dying "¡Jesús!" and the cross of blood). Secondary spine: Wikipedia/Britannica/EBSCO entries on Pizarro, Balboa, Almagro, the Battles of Cajamarca and Las Salinas, and the History of Cusco/Lima, cross-checked against Grokipedia summaries and Spanish-language efemérides (CEHMP) for exact dates of the Empresa del Levante (10 Mar 1524), the Capitulación de Toledo (26 Jul 1529), the founding of San Miguel (15 Aug 1532), and Lima (18 Jan 1535). The coda leans on Ancient Origins, Atlas Obscura, and a 1984 UPI wire for the forensic resolution of the "two Pizarros" (the false 1891 glass-coffin body vs. the 1977 lead-boxed skull, forensically matched 1984, reburied 26 Jan 1985).

**Judgment calls.** (1) *Coordinates without a fixed marker* — Puerto de la Hambre, the Urabá stockade of San Sebastián, and the Darién sighting-ridge above the Gulf of San Miguel have no single agreed pin; I used the best-attested approximate region (mouth of the Río San Juan; Gulf of Urabá; Darién highlands) and marked confidence accordingly. (2) *The line-in-the-sand quote* is flagged explicitly as tradition (Gómara/Zárate, popularized by Prescott 1847) rather than a verbatim eyewitness record — the event (thirteen men crossing) is solid; the wording is chronicle legend, which the register asks to be narrated as fact regardless. (3) *Cusco pinning* — per the curator's direction I pinned Huacaypata/Plaza de Armas and the Coricancha precisely and flagged the plaza as the site for essay 207's Plaza San Francisco catacombs and the San Cristóbal hill constellation, without importing that mythos wholesale — it's noted only as a future interlock in suggested_refs. (4) *Quote scarcity* — Pizarro was illiterate and left almost no words of his own outside chronicle paraphrase; three genuine-canon quotes survive the register's own honesty test (the line in the sand, Xérez's rendering of his words to the fallen Atahualpa, and his dying cry), versus dozens in Joan-of-Arc-style trial-transcript figures. That thinness is itself true to the man.

**Time-folds / gaps.** The Hispaniola decade (1502–1509) is compressed into two stops — the sources genuinely go quiet on Pizarro specifically until Ojeda's 1509 Urabá expedition. The 1536–37 Manco Inca uprising and siege of Cusco (Hernando Pizarro's captivity in Sacsayhuamán) is skipped entirely to keep the arc on Francisco's own itinerary, which stayed in Lima through it — it belongs more properly to a future Manco Inca or Hernando Pizarro journey. Ten years (1519–1524) as comfortable alcalde of Panamá are folded into a single stop; nothing dramatic survives of them, which is the point — the whole arc turns on his choosing to give that comfort up.

**Five richest episodes.** (1) Isla del Gallo, 1527 — the line in the sand and the Thirteen of Fame, the hinge where the whole conquest almost doesn't happen. (2) Cajamarca, 16 Nov 1532 — the ambush, Valverde's thrown-down missal, Atahualpa seized by Pizarro's own hand. (3) The ransom room, filled once with gold and twice with silver, at the Inca's own measured word — the largest ransom in recorded history, paid to the letter, honored with a garrote. (4) The assassination, 26 June 1541 — the cross drawn in his own blood, the single cried word "¡Jesús!", almost liturgical in its economy. (5) The coda — the century-long case of mistaken remains (1891 glass coffin, 1977 lead box, 1984 forensic match, 1985 reburial), a genuinely modern miracle of identification answering a genuinely medieval crime.

**Connections to the atlas.** Shares the Santo Domingo pin with **columbus** (same Torre del Homenaje harbor, 1502 — Pizarro crosses on Ovando's fleet while the Admiral is dying his own slow way home). Shares Trujillo-kinship language with **cortes** (both this same fleet's Extremaduran conquerors, per the tradition that makes them cousins of one hard country). The Almagro-and-brothers civil war anticipates the world **lautaro** is born into a decade later in Chile — Pedro de Valdivia, Lautaro's great enemy, served his apprenticeship as a lieutenant under exactly these Pizarro brothers and inherited their war-to-the-death habits of conquest. Cusco's plaza is pinned precisely (Huacaypata / Coricancha) against the day a Manco Inca, Mama Huaco, or San Cristóbal-hill journey needs to interlock with it, per essay 207's Plaza San Francisco catacombs. And the Lima this journey founds (18 Jan 1535) is the same Ciudad de los Reyes that **sucre**, **bolivar**, and **valientes** will walk three centuries later.
