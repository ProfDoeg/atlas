# Pedro de Mendoza — report

**Sources.** Ulrich Schmidl's *Viaje al Río de la Plata* (the only eyewitness chronicle, via the Biblioteca Nacional Mariano Moreno transcription and secondary quotation in Canal26/Infobae) anchors the famine material — the eaten shoes, the three hanged horse-thieves, the night cannibalism, Charles V's absolution. Ruy Díaz de Guzmán's *La Argentina* (1612) is the source, via modern citation (Página/12, the UBA-FADU archaeology site), for the five mares and seven horses left at the 1541 abandonment and their multiplication into "moving hills" by the 1580s. Standard modern scholarship (arrecaballo.es; the Universidad de Almería's Ideimand project; the Real Academia de la Historia's *Diccionario Biográfico* entry on Juan Osorio; Infobae's two long-form pieces) supplies the biographical spine: Guadix, the sack of Rome, the capitulación, Sanlúcar, the killing of Osorio, the founding, Diego's death at Corpus Christi, the departure, the will at sea, the death off Cape Verde. Essay 218 ("Atavismo y los niños perdidos") and its two Instagram extractions supply the coda's argument and are cited directly at the three stops where the atlas's own atavism thread — ombú/Bourlemont tree, mozos perdidos, gaucho etymology — is invoked.

**Judgment calls / time-folds.** The biggest one is deliberate and directed: the canonical, well-documented "freeing of the herds" is an event of 1541 (Irala's forced abandonment, five mares and seven horses turned loose), four years *after* Mendoza's own April 1537 departure. Per the curator's brief — Mendoza's own exit is the reason this journey exists — the freeing is narrated as a mythic event of the 1537 departure stop, with the historically precise 1541 count folded into its own later coda stop as confirmation rather than correction. This is flagged in-line (the 1537 stop hedges with "in the canon's own telling") and unpacked here for the record; the two stops together make the fold legible rather than hiding it. A second, smaller fold: two unrelated forts share the name "Corpus Christi" on the same day, 15 June 1536 — Ayolas's post on the Carcarañá and the battle where Diego de Mendoza died near Buenos Aires are different places connected only by the feast-day calendar; the dataset keeps them as sibling stops rather than merging them, since conflating them would be the actual error. Third: the exact site of the Combate de Corpus Christi is a live historiographical dispute (Laguna de Rocha vs. the Luján river, ~60 km apart); Laguna de Rocha was used and marked traditional. Fourth: Mendoza's birth year (1487 vs. 1499) and the precise coordinates of Buena Esperanza (near, but not certainly at, Puerto Aragón) are genuinely contested in the sources and marked traditional/inferred rather than attested.

**Gaps.** No surviving quotation is attributed to Mendoza himself except the Osorio death-sentence — a documented but ugly first utterance to give him in the canon, and an honest one. Direct primary-source phrasing for the three-hanged-and-eaten episode could not be retrieved verbatim (paywalled/blocked chronicle scans); the campa narrates it as canon fact per the secondary sources and leaves the quote field null rather than paraphrase-as-quote.

**Five richest episodes.** (1) The killing of Juan de Osorio at Guanabara — a written kill-order, later ruled unjust by the Crown itself, that seats Ayolas in power and seeds every later disaster. (2) The famine at Buen Ayre — boots eaten, three men hanged for a horse and their own corpses eaten that night, Charles V's grim absolution. (3) Corpus Christi, 15 June 1536 — Diego de Mendoza's death naming a killing-ground while, upriver, a fort of the same name opens the road to Asunción. (4) The departure and the loosing of the herds — the adelantado sailing home to die, and (in the canon's compressed telling) leaving behind the free herd that will outlive every fort he built. (5) 1541/1580/1617/1872 as one continuous coda — Irala's seven horses and five mares, Garay's baguales, Hernandarias's mozos perdidos, and Martín Fierro's freed bird, four centuries of the same abandonment ripening into a national image.

**Connections in the atlas.** This is the departure-side hinge of the Buenos Aires cluster: belgrano, alvear, eva_peron and charly_garcia all play out on ground this journey opens and, in 1541, empties. That is a shared-ground note only, not a documented relation to Mendoza himself — the Parque Lezama campa was revised (2026-08-14 house-style pass) to drop the four names, since nothing ties them to Mendoza but the coordinate, and to close instead on the horses themselves. The coda's atavism thread (ombú "Lighthouse," mozos perdidos, feral herds) directly continues essay 218 and, through it, the Bourlemont fairy-tree material already load-bearing in joan_of_arc.journey.json and the pierre_bourlemont/joffrey_bourlemont/pica_bourlemont sub-family — two trees, two feral commons, one thread. Mendoza's cousin-kinship with Francisco Pizarro (both Trujillo/Extremadura Mendozas by blood, noted in cortes.journey.json's opening stop) is carried forward as a passing detail rather than a shared pin.

**Dates/chronology fixed (two):**
- *Santander 1522*: the stop had Charles sailing **from** Santander to England on 1522-05-26 — inverted; Charles reached England from Calais in May 1522 and landed **at** Santander from England on 16 July 1522. Canon (arrecaballo) says only "como paje, acompañó al soberano en su viaje a Inglaterra en 1522," so the stop was reframed as the homecoming landing (date → 1522-07-16, still "inferred"), keeping the Santander pin and the Treaty of Windsor ref.
- *Buen Ayre besieged and burned*: was 1536-12-01 "traditional" — but the tradition (Schmidl's ~23,000-strong assault, fire-arrows, four ships burned) is dated to St John's day, **24 June 1536**, nine days after Corpus Christi. Date corrected, stop moved before Buena Esperanza (Sept 1536) to keep segment chronology, campa reworked to the fire-arrow/four-ships telling. This also repairs the implied causality: Mendoza went upriver *after* the burning, not before.
- *Parque Lezama monument*: 1937-01-01 → **1937-06-23** — the monument was inaugurated on the 400th anniversary, to the day, of Mendoza's death at sea; the campa now says so (a canon gift the placeholder date was hiding).
