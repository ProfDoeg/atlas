# Arshag Shorjian (1898 - after November 2000): research report

*Atlas of Journeys, working file. Compiled 2026-08-12 from a four-lens verified research pool (chronology, geography, quotes, interlock, afterlife), each claim re-checked against the source that carries it.*

**Legend:** **[A]** = attested, source named · **[R]** = reconstruction, inference, tradition, or an explicit gap. Contradictions are flagged where they stand and are not adjudicated.

**The evidentiary situation in one paragraph.** Essentially the whole documentary basis for this life is a single magazine article: David Zenian, "The Armenians of Argentina," *AGBU Magazine*, November 2000, in AGBU's "Ola Buenos Aires" feature (agbu.org/ola-buenos-aires/armenians-argentina). Shorjian appears as an interviewee, aged 102, in an Armenian home for the elderly in Buenos Aires. There is no birth register, no ship manifest, no naturalization file, no obituary, no identified photograph, no memoir, no scholarly treatment. Everything below either comes from that one article, is regional context from other sources, or is flagged as a gap. A journey built on this material is a journey built on one afternoon's testimony given by a man of a hundred and two about things that happened to him when he was eleven.

---

## 1. Hadjin, 1898-1903

**Birth. [A]** "Arshag was born in the once heavily Armenian populated south central Turkish town of Hadjin in 1898" (AGBU Magazine, Nov. 2000, verbatim). No month or day is given anywhere; the journey defaults the date to 1 January and marks the hedge in `date_confidence`. No birth record has been located, and none would be expected: Ottoman Armenian parish registers of the Adana vilayet largely did not survive 1909 and 1915.

**Where Hadjin is. [A, with a correction carried through all four lenses]** Hadjin is the modern town of Saimbeyli, Adana Province, Turkey, in the Taurus range of Cilicia; a kaza of the sanjak of Kozan (Sis) within the vilayet of Adana (Virtual Genocide Memorial, entry for Kaza Hacin).

> **Coordinate error, flagged and corrected.** Three lenses placed Hadjin in three different spots, none right: chronology 37.7167 / 35.9333 (about 30 km south-west, near Kozan), geography 38.1122 / 36.1289 (about 14.5 km north-east, on open mountainside), afterlife 37.9944 / 36.1275 (about 3.5 km east, outside the town). Correct: **37.9846 / 36.0888** (OSM/Nominatim, "Saimbeyli, Adana, Akdeniz Bölgesi, 01740, Türkiye"; Wikipedia gives 37°59'N 36°05'E). The journey uses the corrected pair at every Hadjin stop.

**What kind of town. [A]** Roughly 30,000 Armenians and six churches in the early twentieth century (Wikipedia, "Saimbeyli"); the Virtual Genocide Memorial gives 27,850 Armenians in the kaza for 1914-15 and between 13,550 and 30,000 in the town proper. The town was settled in part by Armenians displaced after the fall of the Cilician kingdom in 1375, and preserved a distinct Armenian dialect. Nothing in the source addresses Shorjian's family's origin within Hadjin.

**The family house. [R]** The source names the town and nothing smaller. No street, no quarter, no house. The father's trade, the mother's name, and the number of siblings are all unrecorded. This is stated as a gap, not filled.

**Departure for Adana. [A]** The family relocates from Hadjin to Adana in 1903, when Shorjian is about five (AGBU, Nov. 2000). No reason for the move is given.

## 2. Adana, 1903-1914

**Coordinates. [A/R]** The chronology and afterlife lenses place Adana at 37.0000 / 35.3213, the geography lens at 37.0017 / 35.3289. Both are inside the modern city, under a kilometre apart. The journey unifies on **37.0017 / 35.3289**: housekeeping, not a finding.

**The Armenian quarter and its churches. [R]** Sourp Boghos Bedros (Sts. Peter and Paul) was one of Adana's principal Armenian churches in the period. No source confirms the Shorjian family's parish, and the church's exact address in the old quarter is not fixed in reachable sources. Tagged as reconstruction in the journey.

**The massacres of April 1909. [A, as regional context]** An estimated 20,000 to 30,000 Armenians and about 1,300 Assyrians were killed across the Adana vilayet (Wikipedia, "Adana massacre"; corroborated by genocide-museum.am). Hadjin's monastery was destroyed in the same events and later rebuilt as a school (Virtual Genocide Memorial). Neither figure is a claim about Shorjian personally.

**What happened in his house. [A, first person]** From the interview:

> "I was a little boy then, but I can never forget that day. They came into our house and killed my grandfather and uncle. I was very small and was able to hide under a pile of firewood."

He is about eleven. The grandfather and the uncle are the only two family members named in the whole record, and they are named only as the dead.

**The neighbours. [A, first person]**

> "The few of us who survived were protected by some of our Turkish neighbors but the situation deteriorated again with the start of the second wave of massacres in 1914."

He dates the second wave to 1914, not 1915. Zenian's narration elsewhere uses the phrases "those dark days" and "death marches." His dating is preserved as given, and the tension with the standard chronology is flagged below.

**The five years between. [R]** Nothing is recorded of 1909-1914. The family presumably remains in Adana, since the deportation narrative begins there, but the source says nothing.

## 3. The deportation, 1914

**Departure point. [R, tag corrected]** The geography lens carried this as **[A]** while its own text conceded the point was inferred. AGBU names only Mersin, the destination; it does not name Adana as the origin. Adana is the reasonable inference because the family was living there, but an inference is **[R]**.

**The march. [A]** He is forcibly deported and marched to the Ottoman port of Mersin (AGBU, Nov. 2000). No month given. Tarsus lies on the Adana-Mersin road and is the likeliest waypoint, but no source names it for him: **[R]**.

**Who marched with him. [A, with an embellishment struck]** The geography lens wrote that "Shorjian and his surviving family are put aboard" the French boat. Not supported. The AGBU sentence is "the long march to the Turkish port of Mersin and the journey on a French boat to Beirut." No family member is mentioned as accompanying him at any point after 1909, and he ends in an orphanage, which cuts against surviving family. Struck; the journey says only that he boards.

**The boat. [A]** A French vessel carries him from Mersin to Beirut. No ship name, no sailing date, no manifest located.

**The date, contradiction flagged.** AGBU puts the deportation in 1914; the Cilician deportations are conventionally dated to 1915; Shorjian's own words say "the start of the second wave of massacres in 1914." The afterlife lens hedged to "1914-1915," the other lenses used 1914 flat. **Not adjudicated.** The journey uses 1914 and marks the conflict in `date_confidence`.

## 4. Jounieh, c. 1914-1919

**The orphanage. [A]** Five years in an orphanage at Jounieh, on the Lebanese coast north of Beirut, where he learns Arabic and shoemaking (AGBU, Nov. 2000). Coordinates 33.9808 / 35.6178, consistent across lenses.

**Near East Relief attribution, struck. [R]** The geography lens described the orphanage as "run under the Near East Relief system." Re-fetching the article confirms the orphanage "is not named or attributed to any particular organization." The NER attribution is unsourced and anachronistic: NER's Lebanese network is documented as founded at Aintab after the armistice and relocated to Jounieh only in 1920, six years after Shorjian's arrival there per AGBU. The attribution is removed; the **contradiction is preserved as a stop in the journey** rather than resolved, since either the interview's chronology is compressed or the orphanage was a different institution, and no reachable source decides.

**Exit year. [R]** 1919 is arithmetic only: 1914 plus five years. No source states it. The afterlife lens gave "c. 1915-1920" for the same span, contradicting the chronology lens and AGBU's own narrative sequence. The journey uses 1914-1919 and tags both endpoints as reconstruction.

**The trade. [A]** Shoemaking learned at Jounieh, later specialized to orthopedic shoes.

**Hadjin destroyed while he is in Lebanon. [A, corrected]** About 8,000 survivors returned in 1919; France ceded Cilicia; Kemalist troops besieged the town from March 1920 and it fell that October, with only a few hundred escaping (Virtual Genocide Memorial, Kaza Hacin). The geography lens's bare "1920" and "after 1918" are corrected accordingly. Whether Shorjian learned of it is not recorded: **[R]**.

## 5. The gap, 1919-1926

**[R], stated as a gap.** Roughly seven years are undocumented. No source says where he lived, whether he worked as a shoemaker in Lebanon, how he arranged passage, or when the uncle in Argentina emigrated. Nothing is invented to fill it; the journey carries a stop that says so.

**Embarkation and route. [R]** Beirut is the plausible port of departure given his residence; Marseille was the standard transshipment port for Levantine emigrants bound for South America in the 1920s. Neither is attested. Both appear in the journey tagged as reconstruction, because the shape of a route matters to the atlas even when the record is silent.

## 6. Buenos Aires, 1926-1998

**Arrival. [A]** 1926, to join an uncle already settled there; one of an estimated 10,000 Armenian refugees who reached Argentina between 1909 and 1938 (AGBU Magazine, Nov. 2000).

**The city. [A, hedge deleted]** The chronology lens noted that the article never explicitly places him in Buenos Aires. It does: he moved in July 2000 to "the Armenian home for the elderly in Buenos Aires," and Buenos Aires municipal authorities honoured him at 100. The hedge is removed.

**Where the community lived, mis-sourced claim corrected. [R]** The geography lens credited AGBU for a claim that early Armenian arrivals settled in conventillos around San Juan and Jujuy. The article contains no mention of conventillos, tenement housing, or those streets. The only settlement locus it names is **Acevedo Street, later renamed Armenia Street**, where the lot for the community's church and institutions was bought in 1922. The conventillo detail is dropped and the attested one replaces it.

**Institutions, [R] as to Shorjian personally.** The Cathedral of San Gregorio el Iluminador (Armenia 1353, Palermo), designed by Israel Arslan and consecrated in 1938, and the Centro Armenio de la República Argentina are the community's central institutions across the decades he lived in the city. No source records his attendance at either.

**The trade. [A]** Orthopedic shoemaker in Buenos Aires until the age of 100, that is, until about 1998. No workshop address survives.

**Unmarried. [A]** He never marries. The source gives no elaboration, and none is supplied here.

**The hundredth birthday. [A]** The municipal social security department gives him a special reception for his hundredth birthday. His words:

> "I had never had a birthday like that. I would have continued working but the pay was not good."

## 7. The last documented year, 2000

**July 2000. [A]** He moves into an Armenian home for the elderly in Buenos Aires. Name and address not given.

**November 2000. [A]** David Zenian interviews him there. He is 102, in reasonable health, walking briskly, not needing glasses.

> "I might get back to work if I find a well-paying job. I made orthopedic shoes all my life, and I'm still very good at my profession."

> "The Armenians of this country are the product of a generation which paid a high price to keep its identity and that in itself has kept us unique. Our collective memory has not faded."

The second is the closing line of the interview, introduced as what "Shorjian told me as I left him."

## 8. Afterlife

**Death. [R, gap]** No date, no place, no circumstances, no obituary, no cemetery record. He was 102 in November 2000; death presumably followed within a few years, but that is inference. Whether he lies in the Armenian section of a Buenos Aires cemetery, as is customary, is unattested.

**Works, monuments, legend. [R, gaps]** No memoir, book, or authored text; his testimony survives only as quotation inside another man's article, so there is nothing to edit or translate. No plaque, street, or memorial naming him individually. No later folk elaboration, retelling, or dramatization; the record appears frozen at the single AGBU account.

**Digital survival. [A, overclaim reduced]** The afterlife lens asserted, tagged **[A]** and sourced to the November 2000 article itself, that the profile is "cited by researchers of the Armenian community in Argentina as an example of a rare centenarian survivor interview." A 2000 article cannot attest its own later reception, and no scholarly citation was found. Reduced to what is attested: the profile persists in AGBU's online archive as an oral testimony.

**Item cut.** The chronology lens carried a Facebook post from the Compatriotic Union of Hadjin (Lebanon) group mentioning a "Garin Shorjian," described as the daughter of "Hajintsi Sdepan & Maro Shorjian." Re-fetching the post shows it identifies Garin Shorjian as the **leader** of that compatriotic union, and contains neither the parentage detail nor the "diaspora program in Armenia" attributed to it. Rare surname plus shared town of origin is suggestive and nothing more; a Facebook group post is below the evidentiary floor for an atlas entry. **Cut.**

## 9. Interlocks with the existing atlas

Recorded because the atlas is a single fabric, not because Shorjian met any of these people. No source places him in a room with any of them.


- **emin_arslan** [A]. Arslan's weekly *La Nota*, founded in Buenos Aires in August 1915, was "among the first papers in Argentina to bring a general public word of the deportations and killings then under way against the empire's Armenian subjects" (emin_arslan.journey.json). He had been Ottoman consul general in Buenos Aires 1910-1914, representing the government under which the Adana massacres occurred, and in *Recuerdos de Oriente* (1918, p. 216) wrote of Enver Pasha "exterminando casi toda una raza." Shorjian, arriving in 1926, walks into a city where the news of what happened to him had been printed eleven years earlier by an Ottoman ex-consul. Named in the journey campa.
- **alberto_gerchunoff** [A]. A contributor to *La Nota*; his own journey file names Arslan directly. His Once stops give the atlas the Buenos Aires neighbourhood where the Jewish and Middle Eastern colectividades overlap. Named in the journey campa.
- **janos_czetz** [R as to any real crossing]. Queued in the atlas; Armenian ancestry noted by a Hungarian National Library source ("örmény származású") and by the Armenian Embassy in Argentina, which gives his Armenian name as Hovhannés Tzetzian. He died in Buenos Aires in 1904, twenty-two years before Shorjian landed, and was named in the journey campa precisely as a man already dead, with the campa's own date_confidence stating outright "no connection to Shorjian exists or is claimed." **No journey.json exists for him yet.** Cut by name on the house-style pass of 2026-08 as a textbook coincidental crossing; the stop now describes the colectividad Shorjian actually walks into.
- **saint_blaise** [R]. Bishop-physician martyr of Sebastea in Lesser Armenia, the atlas's only existing pin inside the Armenian highlands (census_real_persons_2026-08-02.md line 172). Sebastea is about 200 km north-east of Hadjin. Was named once as a geographic anchor, not as a connection; cut by name on the house-style pass of 2026-08 for the same reason (sixteen centuries apart, no citation).

**Canonical pins inherited byte-identical:** Buenos Aires centre -34.6037 / -58.3816; Puerto Madero -34.6118 / -58.3626; Once -34.6037 / -58.4085.

## 10. Contradictions and errors, listed

1. **Hadjin coordinates.** Three lenses, three wrong answers. Corrected to 37.9846 / 36.0888 throughout (a factual correction, not a source conflict).
2. **Deportation year.** 1914 (AGBU, and Shorjian's own words) against the standard 1915 chronology of the Cilician deportations. Not adjudicated; journey uses 1914.
3. **Jounieh orphanage dates.** AGBU implies c. 1914-1919; Near East Relief sources date the Jounieh facility's opening to 1920. Not adjudicated; preserved as a stop.
4. **Orphanage years, lens against lens.** Chronology gave 1914-1919, afterlife gave c. 1915-1920. Neither is sourced; journey uses 1914-1919 as reconstruction.
5. **Departure point of the 1914 march** tagged [A] while the lens's own text called it an inference. Corrected to [R].
6. **"Shorjian and his surviving family" aboard the French boat.** Unsupported. Struck.
7. **Near East Relief attributed to the Jounieh orphanage.** Unsourced and anachronistic. Struck.
8. **Conventillos in San Juan and Jujuy sourced to AGBU.** Not in the article. Replaced with Acevedo/Armenia Street.
9. **"Cited by researchers" tagged [A] on the 2000 article itself.** Circular. Reduced.
10. **Facebook "Garin Shorjian" item.** Misdescribed, below the floor. Cut.
11. **Adana coordinates** differ between lenses by under a kilometre. Unified on 37.0017 / 35.3289.
