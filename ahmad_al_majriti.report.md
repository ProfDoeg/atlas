# Ahmad al-Majriti: research report

*Compiled 2026-08-14. Subject as queued: "Ahmad al-Majrītī, of the Majrītī school, the name the Rutbat al-Ḥakīm and Picatrix circle travels under." Companions: `maslama_al_majriti` (the astronomer, written); `maslama_al_qurtubi` (queued, unwritten). Transliteration is kept plain, matching the journey file.*

Legend: **[A]** = attested, source named · **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition, inference, or a claim relayed through a summarizing intermediary rather than read at the source. Contradictions are flagged and left standing; unreachable sources are named with the reason.

**The finding that governs everything below.** No source located in this research documents an independent person named Ahmad al-Majriti. **[R]** Searches of en.wikipedia.org and britannica.com in August 2026 return no standalone entry, and the bare nisba `al-Majriti` redirects to the article on the astronomer Maslama ibn Ahmad al-Majriti. On the most economical reading the circulating name is that astronomer's **patronymic**, *ibn Ahmad*, detached in copying or cataloguing and read as a given name. This is a negative finding, not proof that no such man existed. The journey file is therefore built as the itinerary of a **name**, with the two candidate authors and the chain of attributors as its stages.

---

## 1. The nisba: Majrit, and what a name from there carries

**[A: Encyclopaedia of Islam, 2nd ed., s.v. "al-Madjriti"]** The nisba *al-Majriti* means "of Majrit", the Umayyad frontier fortress on the Manzanares that became Madrid, and it is the only element common to every name in this dispute.

**[R]** The foundation of Majrit is placed in the reign of Muhammad I (852-886), with no year fixed by any source consulted; the surviving Arab wall at the Cuesta de la Vega (40.4139, -3.7136) is the physical anchor and is dated only broadly to the ninth and tenth centuries.

**[A: the Andalusi biographical dictionaries as summarized in Wikipedia, "Maslama al-Majriti"]** The astronomer's full name is given as **Abu al-Qasim Maslama ibn Ahmad al-Majriti** by Sa'id al-Andalusi, Ibn Bashkuwal, Ibn al-Faradi and Ibn Abi Usaybi'a. **[R]** Nothing is recorded of the father Ahmad beyond the patronymic. A father with no biography, sitting in the middle of a much-copied name, is the mechanism the whole problem most plausibly runs on, and Ibn Khaldun's attributing sentence preserves that patronymic verbatim (see §5). It is the best circumstantial support the detachment hypothesis has, and it is still circumstantial.

## 2. The documented astronomer (the man the name was taken from)

**[A: Wikipedia, "Maslama al-Majriti", summarizing the biographical dictionaries]** Born c. 950 at Majrit (**[R]** for the year: given as circa, no month or day); active in Cordoba under al-Hakam II (r. 961-976). He adapted al-Khwarizmi's zij from the Persian to the Hijri calendar, worked on the Arabic *Almagest* and Ptolemy's *Planisphaerium*, developed surveying methods used in al-Andalus, and founded a school of the exact sciences. Sa'id al-Andalusi, *Tabaqat al-Umam* (1068), calls him "the best mathematician and astronomer of his time in al-Andalus". **Correction:** the surveying is credited to al-Majriti alone, and Wikipedia's own entry calls Ibn al-Saffar a **colleague** who "worked at the school founded by his colleague Al-Majriti", not a pupil.

**Contradiction, not resolved:** he dies in Cordoba, the sources giving 395-398 AH, that is **1004-1008 CE**, most commonly 1007 or 1008; the journey file uses 1007 with the range in `date_confidence`. No grave or burial tradition is recorded, a notable absence given how elaborate the later legend became.

**Corrupt data explicitly refused.** The student list circulating on Wikipedia ("Ibn al-Saffar, Abu al-Salt, al-Turtushi") holds two chronological impossibilities and is not reproduced: **Abu al-Salt** (c. 1068-1134, taught by al-Waqqashi of Toledo) was born 61 years after al-Majriti died, **Abu Bakr al-Turtushi** (1059-1126, taught by al-Baji at Zaragoza) 52 years after. Only **Ibn al-Saffar** (d. 1035, Denia) is possible, and the sources call him a colleague; his attested work is the astrolabe treatise, a commentary on the *Zij as-Sindhind*, and the coordinates of Mecca for qibla calculation.

## 3. The two anonymous books

**[A: internal dating of the Arabic texts, reported by Ritter, Pingree 1980, Fierro 1996, de Callataÿ 2013]**

- *Rutbat al-Hakim* ("The Rank of the Sage") states in its own preface that it was completed in **343 AH (c. 954 CE)**, after which the author begins the companion work.
- *Ghayat al-Hakim* ("The Aim of the Sage") states in its own text that it was completed in **348 AH (c. 959 CE)**.
- **The preface is anonymous.** The authorial persona gives a course of study and a plan of work and no name. "al-Majriti" is an external attribution, never a self-identification in the manuscript. This is the load-bearing fact of the whole file.

Two prologue passages were recovered verbatim in Spanish translation and are quoted in the journey file:

> "Ya hemos presentado...51 epístolas en las que hemos reflexionado a fondo sobre esas ciencias" (fol. 2b) · "Este libro nuestro...lo hemos concebido como un resumen de aquellas numerosas epístolas" (fols. 3a-3b)
> *Rutbat al-Hakim*, MS Beşirağa 505, trans. Godefroid de Callataÿ, *Al-Qantara* 34/2 (2013) **[A]**

**Method note and gap:** the CSIC PDF of the 2013 article was fetched through a reader proxy, SSL on the CSIC domain being broken. The Spanish wording is reliable as printed; it has not been collated against the manuscript here.

**[A: Rutbat al-Hakim, trans. E. J. Holmyard, *Isis* 6.3 (1924)]** The same anonymous book carries the mercury-calcination passage read by some historians of chemistry as an early statement of conservation of mass ("The weight remaining as it was originally"). It belongs to the disputed corpus, not to the astronomer.

### 3a. Two distinct chronological objections, flagged and not merged

Popular summaries collapse these into one. They are opposite arguments. **(1) The age argument:** astronomer born c. 950, so about 4 when the *Rutba* closes (954) and about 9 when the *Ghaya* closes (959). **[R]** The arithmetic is sound, but calling it "the central objection" misstates the scholarship. **(2) Ritter's argument, the dominant one:** he proposed *raising* the internal dates by roughly a century, to **443-448 AH (1050/51-1056/57 CE)**, which makes the text **post-date** the astronomer (d. 1007) rather than pre-date his adulthood. **[A: de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picatrix]** en.wikipedia's Maslama article follows him: "the original Arabic text dates probably from the middle of the eleventh century." Both sit in `date_confidence` on the relevant stops; neither is adopted as settled.

## 4. The rival candidate: Maslama ibn Qasim al-Qurtubi

**[A: Maribel Fierro, "Batinism in al-Andalus. Maslama b. Qasim al-Qurtubi (d. 353/964), author of the Rutbat al-Hakim and Ghayat al-Hakim (Picatrix)", *Studia Islamica* 84 (1996), 87-112; citation confirmed exactly]**

- A second Cordoban Maslama, born **906**, died **353 AH / 964 CE** aged 58, five years after the *Ghaya*'s own stated completion date. That is why the internal chronology works for him and breaks against the astronomer.
- Ibn al-Faradi's dictionary describes him as **"a man of charms and talismans"**. **[R:** twice mediated, Ibn al-Faradi via Fierro via Wikipedia via a summarizing fetch; Fierro's own PDF at CSIC is behind an anti-bot wall.**]**
- He travelled east **before 932**, through (among other places) **Syria, Mecca, Baghdad and Yemen**, losing his eyesight in the Yemen, and returned to Spain before his death. **[A: de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picatrix]**

**Corrections carried from the verification pass, all applied:**

- The eastern itinerary belongs to **c. 920s-930s**, not the 990s. The 990s dating that circulates attaches to **Ibn al-Faradi's** journey (Kairouan, Cairo, Mecca, Medina), a different man on a comparable road sixty years later. The journey file separates them into two segments.
- There is no "348-360 AH composition window" in any source; the texts give 343 AH and 348 AH only.
- Paola Carusi is **Paola**, not Paolo, and her 2000 publication is a study of Islamic alchemy and religious legitimation cited *alongside* Fierro, not a reattribution study itself. **[A]** Her form of the candidate's name keeps the nisba attached ("Maslama Ibn al-Qasim ... al-Magriti"), so the reattribution does not evict the word *al-Majriti* from the case. It detaches it from the astronomer.

**[A: de Callataÿ and Moureau, *Al-Qantara* 37/2 (2016), abstract fetched verbatim from the Semantic Scholar Graph API]** The position of the scholars now doing primary manuscript work: the books are "now both correctly ascribed to Maslama Ibn Qasim al-Qurtubi (d. 353/964)", and medieval Andalusi scholars habitually credited the astronomer not only with those but with the whole *Rasa'il* corpus. **[R]** That the question is live is shown by de Callataÿ's 2021 follow-up, arguing that Maslama had no access to the *Rasa'il* before beginning the *Ghaya*.

## 5. The attribution fastened on, four centuries late

**[A: modern scholarship summarized via Wikipedia, "Picatrix" and "Maslama al-Majriti"]** No medieval biographical notice of the astronomer, not Sa'id al-Andalusi, Ibn Abi Usaybi'a, Ibn Bashkuwal, or Ibn al-Faradi (whose dictionary predates his death in 1013), credits him with either book; his recorded output is astronomical, mathematical and administrative.

**[A: Wikipedia, "Picatrix", citing Holmyard]** The earliest manuscript attribution to an "al-Majriti" is by the Cairo alchemist **Aydamur al-Jildaki**.

- **Contradiction, unresolved:** his death is given as **743/1342** by Brockelmann and as **shortly after 1360** in the manuscript literature; the journey file pins 1360 with both variants in `date_confidence`.
- **Arithmetic corrections:** Wikipedia's "al-Jildaki, who died shortly after 1360, while Ibn Khaldun died some 20 years later" measures **Ibn Khaldun's death** (1406) from about 1360, not the *Muqaddimah*'s composition; with the 1342 variant the gap to the *Muqaddimah* (1377) is 35 years, so the figure is dropped. And 959 to c. 1360 is about **four centuries**, not "roughly three and a half".

**[A: Ibn Khaldun, quoted in Spanish translation in de Callataÿ, *Al-Qantara* 34/2 (2013); words traced verbatim]**

> "Luego vino Maslama ibn Aḥmad al-Mayrītī, el imam de los andalusíes por las matemáticas y por la magia. Hizo el resumen de todas esas obras y sintetizó sus métodos en un libro suyo llamado 'El Objetivo del sabio' (Gāyat al-hakīm)."

**Citation correction:** de Callataÿ cites this to **Ibn Jaldun, *Kitab al-'ibar*, I, p. 497**, not to the *Muqaddima* (the prolegomenon to that same work), and it appears in the **2013 single-author article**, not the 2016 piece to which it is often attached. Note what the sentence contains: **ibn Ahmad**. The patronymic sits inside the very passage that fixes the misattribution for six centuries.

**[A]** Ibn Khaldun's dates: born Tunis 1332; drafted the *Muqaddimah* at Qal'at Ibn Salama near Frenda in a retreat of **1375-1378** (not 1379); reached Cairo in Sha'ban 782 AH = **1380** (not 1382); died 17 March 1406. **[R]** His al-Azhar teaching is reported secondhand by al-Sakhawi; he was made professor of the Qamhiyya madrasa in 1384.

## 6. The Latin road

**[A: Latin *Picatrix* prologue, as reported in Wikipedia, "Picatrix"]** The text "was translated into Spanish from the Arabic by order of Alphonso X of Castile at some time **between 1256 and 1258**." **Correction, applied:** that is a **range from a prologue**, not two dated events. Splitting it into a 1256 commission and a 1258 completion manufactures two events out of one undated interval; the window is carried in `date_confidence` on both Castilian stops.

**[A: Wikipedia, "Alfonso X of Castile"]** The translator was **Yehuda ben Moshe**, who rendered a small group of magical works, the *Lapidario*, the *Picatrix*, and the *Libro de las formas et las ymagenes*, of which "only the *Lapidario* survives in its entirety". The Castilian *Ghaya* is **lost**; the Latin descends from it, not from the Arabic. **[R]** The Latin translator and date are unrecorded (placed shortly after 1258), and the city of the Castilian work is **not established**: the court was itinerant, so Seville is as defensible as Toledo, and both pins are carried.

**[R: holdings unverified]** Latin manuscripts are asserted at the Vatican, Munich, Krakow and Paris; none was confirmed by shelf-mark research here, so the journey file keeps **one** such stop (Krakow) to represent the unverified diffusion. **[R]** Ficino's *De Vita Coelitus Comparanda* (1489) draws on the same current, though the degree of **direct** debt is debated. **[A]** The Latin text circulated among the Elizabethan and Stuart occultists, Forman, Napier, Ashmole, Lilly, each believing he read a named Andalusi author.

## 7. The philologists

- **1898-1949** Brockelmann's *Geschichte der Arabischen Litteratur*, the catalogue against which the later sorting is argued. **[R:** reference status only, entries unverified.**]**
- **c. 1920** Wilhelm Printz locates a complete Arabic *Ghaya*, the first available to Europe. **[A]**
- **1933** Hellmut Ritter, critical Arabic edition, *Studien der Bibliothek Warburg* XII, Teubner. **[A]**
- **1962** Ritter and Martin Plessner, German translation, *Picatrix: Das Ziel des Weisen von Pseudo-Magriti*, Studies of the Warburg Institute 27. **[A]**
- **1980 / 1986** David Pingree, "Some of the Sources of the Ghayat al-hakim" (*JWCI*), then the critical Latin edition, Studies of the Warburg Institute 39. **[A]**
- **1996 / 2000** Fierro, *Studia Islamica* 84; Paola Carusi, *Oriente Moderno* 19/3. **2013 / 2016 / 2021** de Callataÿ, with Moureau in 2016: *Al-Qantara* 34/2 and 37/2, *Arabica* 68/5-6. **[A]**

**Corrections applied to the standard summary:**

- The **1933** edition belongs to *Studien der Bibliothek Warburg* (the Hamburg-era German series, Teubner imprint), **not** to "Studies of the Warburg Institute" (the later London series); crediting it to the Warburg *Institute* is an anachronism. Flag, unresolved: en.wikipedia separately dates the Warburg Library's first publication of the Arabic text to **1927**.
- The 1962 co-translator is **Martin** Plessner, not "Hellmut Plessner" (Hellmut is Ritter's own first name, duplicated onto Plessner in the circulating summary), and "Pseudo-Majriti" is formalized in that **1962 title**, not coined in 1933.
- The 2021 article appeared in **Arabica** 68/5-6, not in *Arabic Sciences and Philosophy*. Fierro's institute in 1996 was CSIC's **Instituto de Filologia**; the Instituto de Lenguas y Culturas del Mediterraneo name is anachronistic for that year.

**Coordinate corrections** were applied to nine circulating pins; values are in the journey file.

**Istanbul, demoted to [R].** That the manuscript behind Ritter's edition surfaced in the **Süleymaniye** is not sourced: en.wikipedia says only that Printz "discovered an Arabic version in or around 1920", with no location. Ritter did catalogue Istanbul manuscripts from 1926 to 1949, and MS **Beşirağa 505** is an Istanbul manuscript, but that is inference; the stop is framed as an unfilled gap.

## 8. Contradictions carried forward, not adjudicated

1. **Astronomer's death year:** 395-398 AH / 1004-1008 CE, with 1007 and 1008 both common (§2).
2. **The two chronological objections** (age 4 and 9 versus Ritter's plus-100-years) point in opposite directions and are both in circulation (§3a).
3. **al-Jildaki's death:** 743/1342 (Brockelmann) versus shortly after 1360 (manuscript literature) (§5).
4. **First Warburg publication of the Arabic text:** 1927 (en.wikipedia, outlier) versus 1933 (standard citation) (§7).
5. **Basra as the home of the Ikhwan al-Safa** is contested ("not everyone accepts the contemporary evidence that gives the Brethren as inhabitants of Basra"), so the circulating [A] tag overstates the case. Unverified too: that **Sa'id al-Andalusi credits the astronomer with first bringing the *Rasa'il* into al-Andalus.** The recovered Sa'id passage credits **al-Kirmani**: "Hasta donde yo sé, nadie las había introducido en al-Andalus antes que él."
6. **Whether al-Qurtubi is settled.** De Callataÿ and Moureau write "now both correctly ascribed"; the broader synthesis calls it strong but not universally settled. The file does not choose.

## 9. Gaps, stated as gaps

- **No independent biography of an "Ahmad al-Majriti" exists in anything reachable:** no dates, no city, no works, no teacher, no student. **No grave** is recorded for the astronomer, and none for al-Qurtubi.
- **The Castilian *Ghaya* is lost**, so the Arabic-to-Latin descent cannot be checked at its middle link; the Latin translator is unnamed and the title *Picatrix* has never been satisfactorily explained.
- **Read only through intermediaries:** Fierro 1996 (CSIC PDF behind an anti-bot wall) and Ibn al-Faradi's entry on al-Qurtubi, whose "man of charms and talismans" is twice mediated and tagged [R]. **Unverified here:** manuscript holdings (Vatican, Munich, Paris, Krakow), the provenance of Ritter's Ghaya manuscript, and Brockelmann's GAL entries.

## 10. Interlock

The only existing atlas traveller named inside a campa is **Maslama ibn Ahmad al-Majriti** (`maslama_al_majriti`): the subject's name *is* his patronymic, and the disputed corpus is attached to both. Canonical pins are inherited byte-identical from that file: Cordoba 37.8882, -4.7794; the school 37.8795, -4.781; and Mecca, Cairo, Qal'at Ibn Salama, Toledo, Seville, London, Madrid, Madinat al-Zahra, Baghdad, Basra.

Deliberately **not** named in any campa, though the map shows the overlap: Ibn Arabi, Maimonides, Ziryab and Roger Bacon share Cordoba or the Toledo translation apparatus but have no documented relation to the subject or to either candidate author, and Leo Africanus is a structural parallel rather than a connection. Christian Rosenkreuz and Cagliostro were checked directly: a grep for `picatrix|grimoire|talisman|astral magic` returns no matches in either file, so that occult link is closed off as a gap.
