# Edward Bulwer-Lytton (1803-1873): research report

*Compiled 2026-08-02 for the Atlas of Journeys. Slug: `bulwer_lytton`. Approved from QUEUE.md line 42 ("Victorian novelist, author of Vril", 11 prior document mentions in the wider corpus).*

Legend: **[A]** attested, source named · **[R]** reconstruction, tradition, or secondary attribution only. Contradictions are printed as contradictions and left standing. Where nothing was reachable, the gap is named.

He is famous twice for sentences and once for a word, and all three have detached from him; much of the work here was tracing where each departure happened, which is why the afterlife section is nearly as long as the life.

## 1. Birth and the fatherless childhood, 1803-1820

**Born 25 May 1803, 31 Baker Street, Marylebone [A: Wikipedia; Victorian Web].** Youngest son of General William Earle Bulwer of Heydon Hall, Norfolk, and Elizabeth Barbara Lytton of Knebworth, Hertfordshire: two counties, and a surname question unsettled for forty-one years. **Father dies 1807 [A: Wikipedia; Victorian Web].** Victorian Web gives 7 July 1807. *Flag:* the geography lens asserted Heydon Hall as the place of death, **not independently corroborated**. The hall as family seat is attested; the deathbed is inference.

**Removal to Kent [R: DNB].** The widow settles her sons near her own father at St Lawrence, outside Ramsgate. The reading-before-writing precocity comes from his own account, relayed by the DNB, uncorroborated.

**Schools, in this order [A sequence, R dates]:** Dr Ruddock's at Fulham (Victorian Web: 1812; DNB: withdrawn within a fortnight); Dr Hooker's at Rottingdean; classics under the Rev. Charles Wallington at Ealing.

> **Contradiction flagged, not resolved.** The pool carried the Rottingdean date as "1812/1820". It cannot be both, and 1820 is excluded because Victorian Web puts him at Ealing in summer 1820, so Rottingdean falls roughly 1815-1818. The journey pins 1816, with the hedge in date_confidence. **Reconstruction, not attestation.**

**1820: Ealing, and *Ismael: An Oriental Tale, with Other Poems* [A: Victorian Web].** *Flag:* the pool claimed he "first publishes verse at fifteen"; he was seventeen. The pool dated the Wallington tuition c.1818-1822; Victorian Web says **summer 1820**.

## 2. Cambridge, 1822-1826

**Trinity College, Easter term 1822; transfers to Trinity Hall [A: Wikipedia; Victorian Web].**

**Chancellor's Gold Medal for English verse, July 1825, for *Sculpture* [A].** **BA 1826, *Weeds and Wild Flowers* privately printed [A].** **Army commission bought 1826, sold 1829 without service [R: Wikipedia]**, stated without a document behind it. **Ullswater, the grave of "Lucy" [R: DNB]**, his own account only.

**A tour of Scotland [R, dropped].** The DNB names the country, no town; the pool proposed Edinburgh as a stand-in. **Cut rather than invented:** a pin whose only content is "he went" has nothing under it.

## 3. France, marriage, the making of a novelist, 1826-1830

**Faubourg Saint-Germain, and a retreat to the woods of Versailles [A: DNB].** **Boulogne-sur-Mer, second in a duel [R: DNB]**, which gives the fact and withholds principals, cause, and outcome. **Marries Rosina Doyle Wheeler, St James's Piccadilly, 30 August 1827, against his mother's wishes [A: Victorian Web].** The allowance stops; that is the proximate cause of the professional writing career. **Settles at Woodcot near Pangbourne [R: DNB; Victorian Web gives "Reading" generally].**

> **Correction.** The pool called *Falkland* (1827) a "moderate success". Wikipedia says the opposite: "The novel enjoyed success in Germany, but was criticised in Britain as immoral." It failed at home. The breakthrough is *Pelham* (1828), the year daughter Emily was born.

> **A non-contradiction, corrected.** The pool flagged a source disagreement over the 1829 novel. There is none: Wikipedia's bibliography lists **both** *The Disowned* and *Devereux*. He published two that year.

**1830: *Paul Clifford* [A].** The opening is verified verbatim against Gutenberg 7735, and the journey carries it in full: "It was a dark and stormy night; the rain fell in torrents, except at occasional intervals, when it was checked by a violent gust of wind which swept up the streets (for it is in London that our scene lies)..."

**"The great unwashed", traced to primary source [A].** It is in the **Dedicatory Epistle of the 1830 first edition** (Internet Archive `paulclifford01lytt`, footnote pp. xix-xx), which Bulwer **dropped from later collected editions**, which is why it is missing from the Gutenberg "Complete" text:

> "For though he is certainly a man who bathes and 'lives cleanly,' (two especial charges preferred against him by Messrs. the Great Unwashed,) yet he is also brave, generous, just; a true friend, an active citizen..."

The popular citation drops "For though" unmarked and treats "he" as generic. "He" is **Pelham**, whom reviewers had identified with the author. Wiktionary cites this epistle as the first published use.

## 4. Parliament, Italy, the wreck of the marriage, 1831-1839

**MP for St Ives, Cornwall, 1831 [A].** Son Robert born 8 November 1831 [A: Victorian Web]. **MP for Lincoln 1832-1841 [A].** *Eugene Aram* 1832 [A]. **1833: Italy with Rosina; acrimonious separation follows [A].** **Milan, Pinacoteca di Brera: Karl Briullov's *The Last Day of Pompeii* [A: Wikipedia]**, the *confirmed* trigger of the novel.

> **Contradiction flagged, not resolved.** The pool said he "writes the greater part of The Last Days of Pompeii" at Naples, and gave two incompatible ranges for the Italian journey (winter 1832-33; autumn 1833 to spring 1834). Wikipedia credits the Briullov canvas and **does not support the Naples composition claim**. The journey keeps the pin and asserts nothing about where the book was written.

**Pompeii, House of the Tragic Poet [R].** Traditional model for Glaucus's house. **No source fetched confirms Bulwer entered the excavation.** Kept explicitly as reconstruction, the campa saying so. **1834: *The Last Days of Pompeii* [A].**

**1836: legal separation finalised [A].** *Precision withdrawn:* the pool's "April" is corroborated by nothing reached; the journey dates it to the year. **1838: baronet, of Knebworth [A].** **1839: Rosina publishes *Cheveley, or the Man of Honour*, satirising him [A], and he publishes *Richelieu; or, The Conspiracy* [A].** Verbatim from the 1839 first edition, Act II Sc. II:

> "True, — THIS! / Beneath the rule of men entirely great / The pen is mightier than the sword. Behold / The arch-enchanter's wand! — itself a nothing! — / But taking sorcery from the master-hand / To paralyse the Caesars — and to strike / The loud earth breathless! — Take away the sword — / States can be saved without it!"

> **Correction.** The commonly quoted extended form ("...the pen is mightier than the sword. Take away the sword; States can be saved without it!") is **not an edition variant**. It is a conflation of two passages four lines apart, joined with no ellipsis; the original has no comma after "great", and "States" follows a dash.

> **Venue caveat.** The premiere is conventionally placed at Covent Garden with Macready, but the verified pool fixes only the year and the act-and-scene. The journey's Covent Garden pin is reconstruction, and date_confidence says so.

## 5. The name, the house, the cure, 1840-1849

**8 December 1840: *Money* premieres at the Theatre Royal, Haymarket [A].** **1841: leaves Parliament, travels [A].** **1842: *Zanoni* [A].**

**Germany and the Schiller translations [R].**
> **Contradiction flagged.** The pool dated a German residence 1841-1843 and framed it as "following the separation from Rosina", which does not survive its own chronology: the separation was 1833 and 1836. The DNB names **"Germany"** and no town; the Schiller translations appear in the mid-1840s. The journey places a **reconstructed** Weimar pin at 1844, says in the campa that no source names the town, and takes the coordinates from the atlas's existing Schiller pin so the reconstruction stays legible as one.

**December 1843: mother dies, Knebworth inherited [A].** **20 February 1844: surname changed by royal licence, per her will [A].** House rebuilt 1843-1845 into the Tudor-Gothic pile [A: Wikipedia].

**The water cure [A: Victorian Web].** Great Malvern (nine or ten weeks); Petersham under a Doctor Weiss (seven weeks); Boppard on the Rhine under a Doctor Schmidt. All three undated in the source beyond "mid-century"; the journey spaces them 1845-1847, date_confidence recording that the source supplies no dates. Petersham is folded into the Malvern campa for stop count.

**1849: *The Caxtons: A Family Picture* [A].**

## 6. The second career, 1852-1866

**1852: Conservative MP for Hertfordshire, held to 1866 [A].** **Guild of Literature and Art [A: DNB; Wikipedia].** Founded with Charles Dickens and others, **incorporated 1854**, dissolved by Act of Parliament 1897. Bulwer-Lytton gave land at Knebworth; Wikipedia records "funds and landed property (at Knebworth)", not at Stevenage.

**Rector of Glasgow.** *Correction:* the pool gave 1856; Wikipedia gives **1856-1859**, a three-year term. **Secretary of State for the Colonies [A].** *Correction:* the pool's phrasing ("from 5 June to 11 June 1858 handover and through June 1859") is garbled. The span is **5 June 1858 to 11 June 1859**. He selects Richard Clement Moody to found British Columbia [A]. **Fort Dallas at Camchin is renamed Lytton by Governor James Douglas in 1858 [A]**, making the naming Douglas's act, not his; he never crossed the Atlantic.

**1859: *The Haunted and the Haunters* [A]. 1862: *A Strange Story* [A]. 1866: Baron Lytton of Knebworth, moves to the Lords [A].**

## 7. The Rosicrucian question, and Vril, 1867-1871

**The occult-patron claim [R], and the pool's own pin was wrong.** Tradition names him patron, Grand Patron, or secret adept of the Rosicrucian revival; the pool proposed Freemasons' Hall, Great Queen Street.

> **Two corrections.** **Wikipedia's article on the Societas Rosicruciana in Anglia does not mention Bulwer-Lytton at all.** And the location is wrong even as reconstruction: the SRIA was formed by Robert Wentworth Little between 1865 and 1867 and formally established **1 June 1867 in Aldermanbury** (approx. 51.517, -0.093). The journey moves the stop there and states in the campa that the society's own founding account does not name him.

**18 January 1870: Knight Grand Cross of St Michael and St George.**
> **Contradiction flagged, not resolved.** Wikipedia gives 18 January; Victorian Web gives 15 January. Both are in date_confidence. The journey uses the 18th, which produces a coincidence stated plainly rather than dressed up: **he dies on 18 January, three years later to the day.**

**1871: *The Coming Race*, published anonymously by Blackwood in May, later retitled *Vril: The Power of the Coming Race* [A].** He tells John Forster the force is electricity developed to uses only dimly guessed at, not mesmerism.

Verified verbatim against Gutenberg 1951 [A]: "I should call it electricity, except that it comprehends in its manifold branches other forces of nature, to which, in our scientific nomenclature, differing names are assigned, such as magnetism, galvanism, &c."; and "The fire lodged in the hollow of a rod directed by the hand of a child could shatter the strongest fortress, or cleave its burning way from the van to the rear of an embattled host."

> **Two pool quotes corrected before use.** (1) The "destroy like the flash of lightning" passage was **truncated at both ends and mid-sentence-capitalised**, and sits in **Chapter IX**, not the Chapter VII vril definition; the sentence continues ", and on it they chiefly rely for the cure of disease...". The journey quotes from "It can destroy" onward, with a trailing ellipsis. The twentieth-century Vril Society derives from the novel generally, **not from this sentence**. (2) The Vril-ya proverb was garbled: Chapter IX has "No happiness without order, no order without authority, no authority without unity", and the pool dropped "authority without", breaking the chain. The journey uses the full form and records that it is the narrator's admittedly lossy paraphrase.

**Quotes carried as [R]**, resting on Wikiquote and never checked against a primary edition: "Man is arrogant in proportion to his ignorance" (*Zanoni*, Bk IV); "Even genius itself is but fine observation strengthened by fixity of purpose" (*Caxtoniana*, 1863); "What men want is not talent, it is purpose..." (*Lucretia*, 1846); "Master books, but do not let them master you..." (*The Caxtons*, 1849). Each carries its [R] status in the journey's quote_source field.

## 8. Death and burial, 1873

**Dies at Torquay, about 2 a.m., 18 January 1873, aged 69 [A: Wikipedia; ODNB via scalar.lehigh.edu, which names the house as Argyll Hall].** *Kenelm Chillingly* and *The Parisians* appear the same year [A]; *Pausanias the Spartan*, unfinished, is published by his son in 1876 [A].
> **Correction.** The pool gave "complications from ear surgery". The standard account is a **cerebral abscess arising from chronic middle-ear disease**: a long-standing complaint, not a surgeon's error.

**Buried Westminster Abbey, 25 January 1873 [A].** The Dean refuses; **John Forster** argues that if a writer of this standing is excluded no one can be included; a letter appears in *The Times*; the Dean relents. Placed in **St Edmund's Chapel, not Poets' Corner**, near the tomb of Sir Humphrey Bourchier, killed at Barnet, who appears in one of Lytton's own romances; a black slab carries the two dates [A: Westminster Abbey; British History Online]. Burial against his wishes (he wanted Knebworth) is reported by Victorian Web and the Abbey both, though the wish rests on report.

> **A pool claim deleted outright.** The pool said the Abbey "holds Dickens... laid to rest one year later in the same nave." Wrong twice: **Dickens died 9 June 1870 and was buried that month, nearly three years BEFORE Lytton**, and lies in Poets' Corner, from which Dean Stanley deliberately excluded Lytton.

## 9. Afterlife

**The mausoleum [A: Wikipedia].** Octagonal neoclassical, by John Buonarotti Papworth, built **1817**, commissioned by Edward's mother for **her** parents. Elizabeth (1770-1843) lies there, as do the ashes of his granddaughter **Constance Bulwer-Lytton (1869-1923)**, suffragette and hunger striker, and his son **Robert**, Viceroy of India 1876-1880.

**The joke [A].** Scott Rice founds the **Bulwer-Lytton Fiction Contest** at San Jose State University in **1982** and ends it in **January 2025**, citing his age.

**The word [A, then R].** Theosophy takes vril as a fact of nature; Hollow Earth and Nazi-occult writers follow. The claim that he disclosed rather than invented the force **has no basis in his stated intention** (the Forster letter). The "Vril Society" of 1920s-30s Germany is **[R] and contested**: no contemporaneous document, meeting note, or membership list has been produced. The earliest lead is a **1947 recollection by rocket engineer Willy Ley** of a fringe group, the *Wahrheitsgesellschaft*, seeking vril by contemplating an apple cut in half.
> **Contradiction flagged, not resolved.** Popular esoteric sources assert the society as fact; Historic Mysteries and Gizmodo treat it as postwar legend. Both recorded, neither adopted.

**Bovril.** Wikipedia gives the etymology as Latin *bovinus* plus his vril (previously "Johnston's Fluid Beef") and dates Bovril Ltd's incorporation to 1889. It does **not** support the pool's "1886" rename, which is dropped.

**"One of the most eminent occultists of the Victorian age" [R].** Widely repeated; sourced only to general search summaries. Nothing primary found.
