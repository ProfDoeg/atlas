# Ibrahim ibn Adham: research report

*2026-08-09. Subject: Ibrahim ibn Adham (Ebrahim b. Adham), ascetic of Balkh and Syria, c. 718 to c. 778/783. Prepared for the Atlas of Journeys, slug `ibrahim_ibn_adham`.*

**Legend.** **[A]** = attested, source named in place. **[R]** = reconstruction, hagiographic tradition, or inference. Contradictions between sources are flagged and **not** adjudicated. Sources that could not be reached are named with the reason.

**The shape of the problem.** There is a historical man and there is a legend, and they do not sit on top of each other. The historical man is an Arab of the Banu Ijl, born at Balkh into a Kufan settler family, who moved west with his tribe and died on a Byzantine campaign. The legend is a prince of Balkh who heard a voice while hunting and gave his throne away at the palace door. Both are in the record. This report keeps them apart by tag; the journey file keeps them together, which the register of the atlas requires.

---

## 1. Origins, name, and the nasab

**The correct name chain [A].** *Encyclopaedia Iranica* VIII/1, pp. 62-63 (15 December 1997), via Wayback snapshot 20260414123119: **EBRAHIM B. ADHAM b. Mansur b. Yazid b. Jaber 'Ejli**. Adham is his father, Mansur his grandfather, the nisba 'Ijli.

**The garbled chain [R].** English and Arabic Wikipedia both circulate "Ibrahim ibn Mansur ibn Zayd ibn Jabir al-Ijli", which drops his own father Adham out of the chain (leaving a man called ibn Adham who is not the son of Adham) and reads Yazid as Zayd. That is the form that reached the research pass, and it is wrong on both counts.

**Birth [A/R].** Balkh, Khurasan, c. 100 AH, which runs 718-719 CE, so the standard "c. 718" is arithmetically sound. *Iranica*: **"born to a notable Kufan family in Balkh"**.

**Origin class [A], and a contradiction.** A "notable Kufan family" is not an Iranian noble house. English Wikipedia traces the family's presence at Balkh to an earlier migration of the Banu Tamim or Banu Ijl **out of Kufa and eastward** into conquest-era Khurasan. The royal framing therefore contradicts the lineage his own biography carries: he descends from Arab settlers planted in a Persian city, not from its dynasts.

## 2. Balkh and the kingship

**The kingship [R].** Sufi tradition, principally through Attar, makes him king or prince of Balkh. No contemporary Balkh source confirms any kingship. *Iranica* treats the abdication stories as **"Sufi legends"** and, where it grants them administrative substance at all, attaches them to the **governorship** rather than a throne.

**Coordinates, corrected [A].** The pass pinned "Balkh, royal citadel (Bala Hisar)" at 36.7551, 66.8974: that is the town centre, not the citadel. English Wikipedia places the Bala Hissar **"at the north of the site"**, oval, roughly 1,500 by 1,000 m, putting a citadel pin near 36.766 N, 66.902 E. The pass also used two longitudes (66.8974 and 66.8998) for one place in two lenses. The journey uses the canonical atlas Balkh pin, **36.755, 66.897**, plus a citadel pin at 36.766, 66.902.

## 3. The awakening

**Two incompatible versions, both [R].** Arabic and English Wikipedia both give a hunting story and disagree on its mechanism.

1. A **voice** cries out while he rides down a gazelle. Burton, *Personal Narrative*, vol. III, footnote to ch. XXVIII (Wikisource): **"Man! it is not for this that Allah made thee!"** Burton gives it as legend and cites no earlier authority.
2. **Khidr** appears, in some tellings twice, and warns him.

Not harmonized in any reachable source, and not harmonized here. Both are carried into the journey as separate stops.

**The shepherd's coat [R].** Arabic Wikipedia: he exchanges his fine clothes for a shepherd's coarse garment and abandons his rule over Balkh. English Wikipedia summarizes the same renunciation **without** the clothes-exchange.

**Dating [R, contradictory].** English sources put the abdication and the Khidr warning at approximately **750 CE**. This collides with the atlas's own material: `rabia.journey.json` stages the Mecca-road meeting on **5 April 737**, after, in the legend's logic, fourteen years of walking. If both stand, the renunciation must precede 723. Flagged in the journey through `date_confidence`.

## 4. The migration west

**The correction that matters most [A].** *Iranica*: he **"migrated with his tribe from Khorasan to Syria before 137/754"**. That is a collective migration of an Arab settler group, not a renunciant's solitary flight. It is the most consequential correction available here, and the pass missed it because it never reached *Iranica*. Both versions are in the journey file, adjacent and unreconciled.

**Nishapur: struck as biography [gap].** The pass placed him at Nishapur, "Shadyakh", at 36.213, 58.66. Three failures: the coordinate sits about 12 km west of the city in open farmland; Shadiyakh is in the **southern** part of Nishapur near Attar's tomb; and **no source places Ibrahim ibn Adham at Nishapur at all**. The association is with **Attar**, his thirteenth-century biographer, a native of the place. The journey keeps a Nishapur stop only because Attar is there, and says so in the campa.

**Kufa: struck as a station, kept as an origin [gap].** The pass gave "Kufa, Great Mosque" as a place he visited, reasoning from Sufyan al-Thawri. English Wikipedia never mentions Kufa in connection with his travels; its only Kufa reference is to his **ancestors' migration out of Kufa**, the opposite movement. Sufyan is named only by Arabic Wikipedia and ended his life at Basra. The journey uses Kufa once, for the ancestral departure.

**Basra: struck [gap].** The pass placed grain-grinding at Basra, then conceded the attribution was unconfirmed. The grain-grinding is real in English Wikipedia and carries **no location**. Its coordinate (30.5085, 47.7835) is modern Basra; eighth-century Basra stood 15 km southwest near Az-Zubayr. No Basra stop appears here.

## 5. Syria: the labor of the hands

**The labor ethic [A].** English and Arabic Wikipedia concur: in Syria he supported himself grinding grain, tending orchards, harvesting and gardening, refusing alms and inherited wealth on principle. No location is fixed for it.

**Gaza [A, with a caveat].** English Wikipedia: he travelled "as far south as Gaza". The pass then tagged the Great Mosque of Gaza, which **no source names**, and called Gaza "the southernmost point of his recorded travels", contradicted by the same pool placing him at Mecca, 1,100 km further south. The journey calls it the southern limit of his **Syrian** range.

**Tyre [A as a grave only].** Listed among the purported graves, cited to *Encyclopedia of Islam* III, 985. The pass's added claim that he "lives and supports himself by manual labor in Tyre" has **no source whatever**. Residence cut; grave kept.

**The monk Simeon [A as dialogue, unlocated].** Quoted verbatim in English Wikipedia from Cyril Glassé, *The Concise Encyclopedia of Islam* (1989), p. 178: the seventy years in the cell; "Every night, one chickpea...They come to me one day in every year and adorn my cell and process about it, so doing me reverence"; and "Do thou, O Hanifite, endure the labor of a year for the glory of eternity." The **location is invented**: Glassé says only that Ibrahim visited the monk "in his cell", naming no mountain, no monastery and no Lebanon. The journey pins the encounter in the Syrian interior and calls the cell unlocated in the campa. Glassé names no medieval hagiography behind the anecdote, so the chain stops at a 1989 encyclopedia.

## 6. Mecca and the ascetic circle

**The Kaaba [R].** Attar's tradition has him circling it for years, and has Khidr instruct him there in the greatest name of God. English Wikipedia confirms Khidr's role in the conversion but does **not** fix it at Mecca.

**The wandering Kaaba [R].** Already in the atlas: `rabia.journey.json`, stop "Outskirts of Mecca, Ibrahim ibn Adham and the wandering Kaaba", 21.47, 39.95, dated 0737-04-05, with Rabia's answer quoted from Jami's *Nafahat al-Uns*. The motif is **not unique to Ibrahim**; it attaches to several saints, a point retellings do not always flag.

**Al-Fudayl ibn Iyad: direction probably reversed [R; the pass over-tagged this as A].** English Wikipedia's sole support is that "records such as those of the Chishti Order note that he was taught by ... al-Fudayl ibn Iyad", footnoted to *Siyar ul Auliya i Chisht*, an 1884 reprint of a hagiography compiled six centuries after the fact. *Iranica* does not mention Fudayl at all. The chronology inverts: Fudayl was born in the 720s and died in 803, **younger** than Ibrahim and his survivor by decades. English Wikipedia's al-Fudayl article states the opposite relation. Not resolved; demoted to [R].

**Sufyan al-Thawri [R].** Named only by Arabic Wikipedia. No date, no place.

**Shaqiq al-Balkhi [R].** English Wikipedia: "Tradition makes him the disciple of Ibrahim ibn Adham." Shaqiq died in 810 (194 AH, which converts correctly). Place and timing of contact unspecified anywhere reachable. `rabia.journey.json` already stages Shaqiq at Rabia's deathbed.

## 7. The frontier and the death

**Military activity [A].** English and Arabic Wikipedia both describe frontier campaigning (ghazw) against Byzantium in his final period, including a **naval** expedition. English Wikipedia footnotes the death and campaign to **Abu Nu'aym al-Isfahani, *Hilyat al-awliya* vii, 388**, a tenth-to-eleventh century source; *Iranica* grounds them in Abu Nu'aym **and** Ibn Asakir jointly.

**Ibn Asakir is cited for one thing only [correction].** The pass attributed the death date, the naval campaign, and the Jerusalem, Baghdad and Tyre graves to Ibn Asakir (1105-1176). English Wikipedia cites him for **exactly one** item: the island burial. Tyre, Baghdad and "the city of the prophet Lot" are footnoted to *Encyclopedia of Islam* III, 985; the Jerusalem cave carries **no citation at all**.

**The date, and an arithmetic error [correction].** Arabic Wikipedia gives 162 AH and glosses it as 776-777 CE. **This is wrong.** 1 Muharram 162 falls in late September 778, so 162 AH = 778-779 CE. (Checked against anchors that do convert correctly: 187 AH = 803, 194 AH = 810, 281 AH = 894.) Arabic Wikipedia then contradicts itself two clauses later with "some sources cite 161 AH/778 CE", and 161 AH = 777-778.

The four figures on offer: Arabic Wikipedia's 162 AH = **778-779** (not the 776-777 it prints); its own cited alternative 161 AH = 777-778; English Wikipedia's c. 165 AH = c. 782; *Iranica*'s 166 AH = 782-783, with a campaign-death range of 160-166 AH = 776-783. QUEUE.md, written before this pass, carries "d. 777". The journey uses 0778 and states the spread in `date_confidence`.

**The bow [R].** Dying on campaign, he asked his companions to string his bow and died gripping it. Repeated everywhere in Arabic tradition, traced to no one.

## 8. The graves

Seven mutually exclusive claims, not the six the pass reported. No source privileges one.

1. **An unnamed Byzantine island.** Ibn Asakir; the only item he is actually cited for. No source names the island.
2. **Tyre.** *EI* III, 985.
3. **Baghdad.** *Encyclopedia of Islam* III, 985. Baghdad was founded in **762**, so this grave is reachable only in his last two decades at the earliest. The pass anchored it on the Abu Hanifa Mosque at Adhamiyya, which dates to 375 AH (985-986 CE) and is connected to the claim by no source. Anchor dropped.
4. **The city of the prophet Lot.** *EI* III, 985. Dropped entirely by the pass. Unlocated; carried in prose, not as a pin.
5. **A cave of Jeremiah at Jerusalem.** **Uncited in English Wikipedia.** The pass tagged it [A] and pinned it at 31.7823, 35.2287, which is **Zedekiah's Cave**, beneath the Old City wall; that article connects it neither to Jeremiah nor to Ibrahim. The "cave of Jeremiah" is **Jeremiah's Grotto**, north of Damascus Gate. Demoted to [R].
6. **Jableh, Syrian coast.** The **Mosque of Sultan Ibrahim ibn Adham**, confirmed by Arabic and English Wikipedia and the Jableh article ("It contains the tomb and mosque of Ibrahim Bin Adham"). **Ibn Battuta records visiting it in the fourteenth century.**
7. **Sur (Sur al-Batha), Oman.** A shrine kept as a pilgrimage site, disconnected from his attested range. **No source explains the connection.**

**A second standing mosque, missed by the pass [A].** English Wikipedia's page "Sultan Ibrahim Ibn Adham Mosque" is **not** the Jableh mosque. It is an older building at **Beit Hanina, East Jerusalem** (31.8290 N, 35.2254 E), completed **336 AH / 947-948 CE** per an on-site inscription. Two mosques carry his name; the pass knew of one.

## 9. The afterlife of the legend

**Attar [A].** Farid al-Din Attar (c. 1145 to c. 1221) includes him as the **tenth** of ninety-six lives in the *Tadhkirat al-Awliya*, composed in the 1210s, running from Ja'far al-Sadiq to Mansur al-Hallaj. A **late literary source**, four and a half centuries after the death, and the principal channel by which the story travels.

**Translations [A, with a date corrected].** Pavet de Courteille, *Le Mémorial des saints*, 1889, **from the Uyghur manuscript** in the Bibliothèque nationale, not from the Persian. Arberry, *Muslim Saints and Mystics*, **first published 1966**; the pass gave three years for this one book across three lenses (1966, 1990 reprint, 1979). Losensky, 2009.

**Rumi [A].** Retells the legend at length in the *Masnavi*. **Flahooley [A].** The 1951 Broadway musical has a genie named "Abou Ben Atom", played by Irwin Corey.

**Leigh Hunt [A, with a date corrected].** English Wikipedia's "Abou Ben Adhem" cites only *The Poetical Works of Leigh Hunt* (Moxon, **1844**), which the pass took as the poem's date. The **Leigh Hunt** article dates the poem to **1834**, first printed in S. C. Hall's annual *The Amulet*, and 1834 is the date that matters.

**Dhaka [A; the pass contradicted itself].** A mausoleum and mosque at Azimpur, Old Dhaka, for a "Hazrat Ibrahim Adham" said to have settled there in 281 AH (894-895 CE). The pass's geography lens claimed sources leave the identity unresolved. **English Wikipedia resolves it outright**: the passage sits under a heading "Namesakes" and opens "Among the **later namesakes** of Ibrahim ibn Adham...". Its own afterlife lens got this right.

**Historicity [R; the pass overstated it].** The pass concluded he was "very likely a genuine historical figure". English Wikipedia is hedged: "Ibrahim **may have been** a historical Sufi of the 8th century whose legend was embellished in later accounts." *Iranica* is firmer, treating him flatly as historical and quarantining only the abdication tales. The conclusion survives on *Iranica*, not on the source cited.

## 10. Quotations gathered

Verbatim, chains stated as far as they go. **None reaches a critical edition of an Arabic or Persian original.**

- **"Man! it is not for this that Allah made thee!"** Burton, *Personal Narrative*, vol. III, ch. XXVIII footnote. Legend, no earlier authority named.
- **"O God! In my eyes heaven itself is less than a gnat in comparison with the love of Thee..."** Al-Ghazali, *Alchemy of Happiness*, trans. Field, ch. VIII (Wikisource). The 1911 *Britannica* "Sufiism" (no translator named) gives what is **very likely the same prayer** in wholly different English ("the eight Paradises are little beside the honour which Thou hast done unto me"). Neither identifies the original. Not resolved.
- **"Thou dost not practise what thou knowest; why, then, dost thou seek what thou knowest not?"** Hujwiri, *Kashf al-Mahjub*, trans. Nicholson. Words on a stone that told him to turn it over; Hujwiri presents it as already old in the eleventh century.
- **The verse epistle to Harun al-Rashid**, trans. Epiphanius Wilson, verified line by line against Wikisource. Two quatrains, and they are two: the pass ran them together as prose and mislabelled the source, which is Wilson's ***Arabian* Literature**. Nothing exists beyond Wilson's English. **A further problem the pass missed: Harun becomes caliph in 786, at least four years after the latest death date offered.**
- **The three Simeon quotations** (section 5), Glassé p. 178 via English Wikipedia. The ellipsis in "Every night, one chickpea...They come to me" is in the extracted text.
- **Five sayings** from *The Sayings and Teachings of the Great Mystics of Islam* (2004), pp. 26-28, via Wikiquote, opening "If you like to be saint of God, then desire not the things of this world or the next." Three read as clauses of one rebuke. **The compiler and translator were not established.** None is used in the journey.

## 11. Contradictions carried forward, not resolved

**Death date**, 161 / 162 / 165 / 166 AH (table, section 7), with Arabic Wikipedia miscomputing its own figure. **The awakening's mechanism**, a disembodied voice against Khidr appearing twice. **The direction of the Fudayl discipleship**, stated in opposite directions by two English Wikipedia articles, with the dates favoring neither cleanly. **The character of the westward move**, solitary renunciant flight against tribal migration before 137/754. **The abdication date against the atlas's own Rabia file**, c. 750 against a meeting dated 5 April 737 that requires a renunciation before 723. **The burial**, seven mutually exclusive claims (section 8). **The two prayers** (section 10), likely one saying in two irreconcilable renderings. **Origin class**, Iranian royal house against Kufan Arab settler family of the Banu Ijl. **The Harun al-Rashid poem** against every death date on offer.

## 12. Gaps, stated as gaps

- **Attar's own text was never read.** Arberry's *Muslim Saints and Mystics* (archive.org, `muslimsaintsmyst0000atta`) returned **HTTP 401** on the full-text endpoint. Every "Attar says" here runs through a secondary summary: the most-cited primary source for this subject is the one source not consulted.
- **Britannica's biographical entry** returned **HTTP 403**. ***Encyclopedia of Islam* III, 985**, behind three of the seven grave traditions, and ***Siyar ul Auliya i Chisht*** (1884), the only support for the Fudayl discipleship, were not consulted directly.
- **The Oman shrine at Sur has no explanation in any source**, and **the island he died on is unnamed** in every reachable source.
- **Nothing is known of what he did at any specific place in Syria.** The labor is attested; the locations are not. Every Syrian pin in the journey is an approximation, and the campas say so.
