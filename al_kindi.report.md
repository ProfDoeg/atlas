# Abu Yusuf Ya'qub ibn Ishaq al-Kindi (c. 801 - c. 873)
*Research report, 2026-08-14. Kufa, Basra, Baghdad, Samarra, and the afterlife of one short treatise on ciphers.*

**Legend.** **[A]** = attested, source named. **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition, or interpretive framing. Contradictions are flagged and **not adjudicated**. Absences of evidence are stated as absences.

**Method note.** What was read directly is listed under Sources. Everything traced to Ibn al-Nadim, al-Qifti, Ibn Abi Usaybi'a, Abu Rida, Rashed and Jolivet, Lindberg, Farmer, Kahn, Bloom, or Al-Kadi's 1992 *Cryptologia* article reaches this report **through secondary quotation only**. For this subject that distinction is the whole difference between what is known and what is repeated.

---

## 1. The name before the man

**Kinda. [R]** The epithet means "of Kinda" and attaches him to a South Arabian tribal aristocracy, not to a city. Wikipedia and the genealogical tradition make Kinda a pre-Islamic kingdom seated at Qaryat al-Faw, abandoned around 300 CE when the tribe relocated to Hadramawt; the family traced descent from the chieftain al-Ash'ath ibn Qays. The first fact about him is a claim of rank, not of residence.

**Birth, c. 801. [A]** Wikipedia, MacTutor and New World Encyclopedia agree on c. 801 into the Kinda aristocracy. The epithet "the Philosopher of the Arabs" follows from the lineage and is carried by the 1911 *Britannica*, on the authority of Flügel (1857) and de Boer (1901).

**The father's post: a live contradiction.** MacTutor and New World Encyclopedia have his father **Ishaq al-Sabbah as governor of Kufa**, as the grandfather had been. Wikipedia says verbatim: *"His father Ishaq was the governor of Basra and al-Kindi received his preliminary education there."* The pool tagged Kufa **[A]** on New World Encyclopedia alone, a minority reading stated as fact; **downgraded to [R]**. Both cities are carried in the journey file.

**Early education. [A]** Qur'an, Arabic grammar, mathematics, theology, at Kufa and Basra both (New World Encyclopedia; Wikipedia). Basra in these decades is the capital of Arabic philology and of Mu'tazilite rationalist theology. That his later method grows out of a philologist's habit of counting the parts of a text is this report's own observation **[R]**.

## 2. Baghdad and the translation circle

**Arrival, 810s-820s. [A]** He completes his education in Baghdad and earns a name for scholarship and fine calligraphy (New World Encyclopedia; SEP).

**Al-Ma'mun, r. 813-833. [A]** The accession is secure; that he *founded* the House of Wisdom is not. Wikipedia has it founded as a library of Harun al-Rashid or a collection of al-Mansur, with al-Ma'mun turning it into "a public academy and a library". The pool's "established or greatly expanded" becomes expansion only.

**Appointment to the House of Wisdom. [R]** MacTutor reports it on the strength of "his learning and aptitude for study", alongside al-Khwarizmi and the Banu Musa. Not corroborated elsewhere in the sources reached.

**The translation circle: the strong claim and the weak one.**
- **[A]** SEP: he oversaw *one of the two main groups of translators in the ninth century*, working with men "many of whom were Syrian Christians or of Syrian extraction". He did not translate from Greek himself; his work was to rebuild their renderings into Arabic philosophy.
- **[R]** That this was housed in Bayt al-Hikma. **SEP's al-Kindi entry never mentions the House of Wisdom.** Wikipedia carries Gutas's argument that it may have been a storehouse, or "a metaphor for the larger Academic community in Baghdad". The pool cited SEP for this link twice; **that citation is false and is withdrawn.**

**Paper. [A]** Baghdad manufactured paper from Harun al-Rashid's reign, and the Suq al-Warraqin supplied sheets and copyists (Bloom, via the pool). **[R]** for the inference that al-Kindi's output depended on it.

## 3. The peak: al-Mu'tasim, 833-842

**[A]** Al-Ma'mun dies in 833; al-Mu'tasim succeeds; al-Kindi transfers his patronage and is appointed **tutor to the caliph's son Ahmad**, which SEP calls the peak of his philosophical career. **[A]** *Fi al-Falsafa al-Ula*, **On First Philosophy**, is dedicated to al-Mu'tasim and argues the legitimacy of philosophical inquiry inside an Islamic frame (SEP; Wikipedia).

**Quotations used. All reach this report through SEP, none checked against the critical editions SEP cites.** *"We must not be ashamed to admire the truth..."* (*On First Philosophy* §1, citing Rashed and Jolivet 1998). *"Thus the true One possesses no matter, form, quantity..."* (SEP §3.1, same edition, p. 95); *correction:* the pool clipped the opening "Thus" and mislabelled the location "Section 4". *"We say that the true, first act is the bringing-to-be of beings from non-being..."* (*On the True, First, Complete Agent...*, citing Abu Rida 1950, pp. 182-4). All **[A]**.

*Correction, withdrawn quotation:* the pool gave "First philosophy is the knowledge of the first truth who is the cause of all truth" as al-Kindi's sentence. SEP quotes only the inner phrase; "First philosophy is" is the encyclopedia author's connective. The wider quotation is withdrawn, and the idea appears in the journey file as prose.

## 4. Samarra

**[A]** In 836 al-Mu'tasim founds a capital at Samarra and moves the government upriver (Wikipedia). **[R]** That al-Kindi relocated is inference. A tutor in the caliph's household would follow the court, but no source reached places him at Samarra on any date, and the biographers rarely say which city they mean for these decades.

**The Banu Musa rivalry. [R]** The pool tagged this **[A]** and sourced it to the *Fihrist* as recording the friction "plainly". **Downgraded:** MacTutor and Wikipedia route the story through the al-Qifti tradition of later biographical dictionaries, and the pool's own chronology tags the identical episode [R]. Not in dispute: the Banu Musa commanded their own translators and patronage.

**Al-Wathiq, 842-847. [R]** The pool tagged the decline **[A]** while conceding in the same sentence that "the record for this specific interval is thin". No source reached documents his fortunes under al-Wathiq; the decline is read backwards from the al-Mutawakkil episode. **Downgraded.**

---

## 5. The Risala on deciphering

**[A]** *Risala fi Istikhraj al-Mu'amma* contains the **first known explanation of cryptanalysis by frequency analysis** in any language (Wikipedia "Frequency analysis"; Kahn, via the pool). The priority claim is confirmed. **No source records the year, occasion, or patron.** The journey file dates it to the productive middle of his career and marks the date_confidence undated.

> "One way to solve an encrypted message, if we know its language, is to find a different plaintext of the same language long enough to fill one sheet or so, and then we count the occurrences of each letter."

> "Then we look at the cipher text we want to solve and we also classify its symbols. We find the most occurring symbol and change it to the form of the 'first' letter of the plaintext sample, the next most common symbol is changed to the form of the 'second' letter, and so on, until we account for all symbols of the cryptogram we want to solve."

**[A]** as text, with a caveat: both are reproductions of Al-Kadi's 1992 translation, seen in Cozzens and Miller (2013), p. 5 and on muslimheritage.com. **Neither that article nor a facsimile was fetched.**

**The Qur'an. [R]** The pool says al-Kindi "illustrates" the method "using close statistical study of Qur'anic and other Arabic text". Wikipedia advances something weaker: *"it has been suggested that a close textual study of the Qur'an first brought to light that Arabic has a characteristic letter frequency"*, a hypothesis about how the property was noticed and not evidence about the treatise. The stronger version is **withdrawn**.

## 6. The range of the corpus

**Mathematics. [A]** Indian numerals, carrying the decimal positional system into Arabic mathematical writing; geometry; the theory of parallels; paradoxes of infinity and space (MacTutor).

**Optics. [A]** *De aspectibus*, vision built on rays travelling in straight lines, translated into Latin and formative for medieval European optics; Wikipedia names **Grosseteste, Witelo and Roger Bacon** among those it reached. *Correction, twice:* (a) the pool conflated it with *De radiis stellarum*, which Wikipedia calls **"the apocryphal work"**, "likely written around the 13th century by a Latin author". Only *De aspectibus* is used. (b) New World Encyclopedia names "Francis Bacon" as the recipient; the figure in the literature is the thirteenth-century **Roger** Bacon.

**Pharmacology. [A]** *De Gradibus*, a mathematical scale for the strength of compound drugs, credited as the first systematic application of mathematics to dosage (New World Encyclopedia).

**Music. [R]** Notes have measurable pitch; sound reaches the ear as waves striking the drum (New World Encyclopedia). That he is the first Arabic writer to represent pitch by letters comes through Farmer via the pool. Almost nothing survives complete.

**Astrology and astronomy. [A]** Applied natural philosophy, tied to a causal chain running from the celestial spheres into earthly events, alongside meteorology and tides (via the pool). Later falsafa, al-Farabi included, attacks the premise he defends.

**The count. [A]** *Fihrist* (c. 987): **at least 260 books**. New World Encyclopedia: 241. The 1911 *Britannica*: "they are said to have numbered over two hundred, but fewer than twenty are extant." Not fixed, and this report does not fix it. *Correction:* the pool quoted this as "His writings ... are said to have numbered over two hundred", stitching a subject onto a clause whose subject is "they".

**Ibn al-Nadim's assessment. [R]** *"The best man of his time, unique in his knowledge of all the ancient sciences. He is called the Philosopher of the Arabs."* Widely attributed to the *Fihrist*, seen only in modern secondary paraphrase; neither the Arabic nor Dodge's translation was reached.

## 7. The orthodox turn, and the silence

**[A]** Al-Mutawakkil becomes caliph in 847. Wikipedia records his "violent persecution of unorthodox Muslims" and that al-Kindi "was beaten and his library temporarily confiscated". *Correction:* the Mihna was reversed around 848-851, not on accession.

**[R]** The episode itself. The pool tagged it [A] in one section and [R] in another, no contemporary account existing and the story travelling through al-Qifti and his successors. **The [R] reading is adopted throughout,** including the attribution of the disgrace to Banu Musa intrigue.

The 1911 *Britannica*, read verbatim: *"In the orthodox reaction under Motawakkil, when all philosophy was suspect, his library was confiscated, but he himself seems to have escaped."* Note what it **does not** contain: a beating. Not evidence against the beating, but a source that had the story and told it differently, recorded rather than smoothed.

**An unresolved contradiction inside one source.** Wikipedia also states he was **employed as a calligrapher by al-Mutawakkil** across 847-861, while carrying the persecution narrative for the same reign. Nothing reconciles them. **Flagged, not resolved.**

**[R]** The library is eventually returned (al-Qifti, whose account modern scholarship treats with suspicion). **[A]** His student **Ahmad ibn al-Tayyib al-Sarakhsi** became tutor and boon companion to al-Mu'tadid, and was executed in 899 (*Fihrist*, via the pool).

**Death. [A]/[R]** In Baghdad under al-Mu'tamid (r. 870-892). SEP says only "died after 866", "usually put in the early 870s"; c. 873 is most often cited. Wikipedia, citing Corbin, has him dying "a lonely man". **Grave: gap.** No source reached names a burial site, shrine, or memorial tradition.

**Loss of the corpus. [A]/[R]** Wikipedia, citing Klein-Franke, attributes it to the persecution, the Mongol destruction of Baghdad's libraries in 1258, and his eclipse by al-Farabi and Avicenna. The sack is [A]; that it took his texts specifically is [R], since by 1258 much had already thinned to fragments.

## 8. Alkindus, and the manuscript that waits

**Toledo. [A]** Gerard of Cremona translates al-Kindi at Toledo; the Toledo School article lists "Al-Kindi's *On Optics*" among his works, with chemical and medical texts. The Latin West knows him as **Alkindus**. *Correction:* the pool made Gerard work under Archbishop Raymond's patronage. Wikipedia is explicit that this is impossible: Raymond died in 1151 and "Gerard arrived in Toledo in 1167".

**Oxford. [R]** Roger Bacon builds his optics on *De aspectibus*. Two secondary sources report his estimate and do **not** agree word for word: the 1911 *Britannica* has "Kindī is placed by Roger Bacon in the first rank after Ptolemy as a writer on optics", Deming (2012, p. 92) has "Roger Bacon placed him in the first rank after Ptolemy as a writer on the topic". The pool blended them into a third wording and attributed it to Bacon. **Neither is Bacon's own Latin**; *Opus Majus* and *Perspectiva* were not fetched. The journey file quotes the Britannica, credited to the Britannica.

**Cardano. [R], kept out of the journey file.** That Gerolamo Cardano ranked him among the twelve greatest minds is Wikipedia's own prose, cited to Sarton (1927) **with no page number**. The phrase is Sarton's gloss, not a quotable line from *De Subtilitate*, which was not fetched.

**The Arabic cryptanalytic lineage.**
- **[A] Ibn Adlan (1187-1268)** addresses the **minimum sample size**: twenty groups of rules, ciphers without word spacing, a roughly 90-character floor below which the frequencies lie. *Correction:* the pool placed him at Mosul; he was born there but taught at the Al-Salihiyya Mosque in **Cairo**, where he died.
- **[A] Ibn al-Durayhim (1312-1359/62).** *Correction, twice:* the pool's "lost, known only through later quotation" framing is out of date, Wikipedia reporting the book "was recently discovered, but has yet to be published"; and his contribution is stronger than stated, comprising eight cipher systems and "the earliest suggestion of a tableau of the kind that two centuries later became known as the Vigenère table". His working cities are **not** fixed: the nisba al-Mawsili points to Mosul, his career to Damascus as much as Cairo.
- **[A] Al-Qalqashandi (1355-1418)** folds a cryptology section drawn from Ibn al-Durayhim into *Subh al-A'sha*, completed 1412.

**Istanbul. [R], the weakest link in the chain.** Twenty-four works believed lost were located in a Turkish library in the mid-twentieth century **[A]**, with neither library nor discoverer **named in the source**. The much-repeated story that the *Risala* was rediscovered in the Suleymaniye archive in **1987** and identified by **Ibrahim A. Al-Kadi** is **[R]**: Wikipedia "Cryptanalysis" credits his 1992 *Cryptologia* article but gives no date, no archive, no finder. Wikipedia "Al-Qalqashandi" notes that Istanbul archive discoveries turned up "earlier 9th-century cryptological works by al-Kindi", corroborating the archive and neither the date nor the person. That Al-Kadi personally located it is **unsupported anywhere reached**: the Arabic edition was prepared by Damascus-based scholars, and his role is bringing the treatise to Anglophone attention. The journey file credits the 1992 publication, not a 1987 find. That nobody in the Suleymaniye across four Ottoman centuries recognised the volume is **[R]**, written as inference.

**Shannon. [R]** *Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems* (1949) puts cryptography on an information-theoretic footing. Shannon **does not cite al-Kindi**, and the pool itself flags the transmission as retrospective historiography, not a documented chain of textual influence. The journey file writes it as the descent of a method.

**Riyadh. [A]** Al Kindi Plaza opened in the Diplomatic Quarter in 1986.

## Contradictions carried, not resolved

1. **Governorship and birthplace.** Kufa (MacTutor, New World Encyclopedia) against Basra (Wikipedia), for both the father's post and the birthplace.
2. **Persecuted or employed.** Wikipedia carries both the beating and confiscation under al-Mutawakkil and employment as his calligrapher, same 847-861 span.
3. **Beaten, or only robbed of his books.** The 1911 *Britannica* has the confiscation and "he himself seems to have escaped"; the modern accounts have a beating.
4. **How many books.** 260 (*Fihrist*), 241 (New World Encyclopedia), "over two hundred" with "fewer than twenty" extant (1911 *Britannica*).
5. **Bayt al-Hikma or not.** SEP never mentions it; MacTutor and Wikipedia place him there; Gutas questions what the institution was.
6. **The 1987 rediscovery.** Universal in popular cryptography history; absent from every encyclopedia entry reached.
7. **De radiis stellarum.** Circulated for centuries as his; Wikipedia calls it apocryphal and thirteenth-century Latin.

## Gaps, stated as gaps

- **No date for the cryptanalysis treatise**: no year, patron, or occasion for the work that made his name outside philosophy.
- **No burial site**, and **no death date better than a range**: SEP commits only to "after 866" and "usually the early 870s".
- **Nothing on 842-847**: the record for al-Wathiq's reign is empty of him.
- **Which city**: after 836 the sources do not say whether an event happens at Baghdad or at Samarra.
- **The mother, wives, children**: no source reached names any of them.

**Coordinates corrected.** Qaryat al-Faw to 19.765, 45.163 (105 km off). Basra to the corpus garrison-site pin 30.3833, 47.7083. Baghdad to 33.3475, 44.335, and 33.3152, 44.3661 for the translation stops. Samarra to 34.1983, 43.8742, its palace and mosque pins having been swapped. Ibn Adlan to Cairo, 30.0444, 31.2357.
