# Ibn Adlan (Afif al-Din Ali ibn Adlan al-Mawsili, 1187-1268): research report

*2026-08-14. Subject: the Mosul-born grammarian, poet and cryptologist who taught Arabic at the Salihiyya of Cairo and who wrote, for an Ayyubid prince titled al-Malik al-Ashraf, the treatise that put a floor under frequency analysis: about ninety letters of ciphertext, below which the counting cannot be trusted.*

**Legend.** **[A]** = attested, with the source named in the bracket. **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition, or inference (mine or a source's), flagged as such. Contradictions between sources are recorded and left standing; where an earlier draft of this research invented a date or a detail, the correction is stated openly rather than quietly applied.

**Sourcing situation, stated up front.** Ibn Adlan is a thin subject in reachable material. The load-bearing scholarship is Ibrahim A. Al-Kadi, "The Origins of Cryptology: The Arab Contributions," *Cryptologia* 16:2 (1992), 97-126 (verified real via Crossref DOI 10.1080/0161-119291866801), and the Damascus critical edition of Mrayati, Meer Alam and al-Tayyan (1987) with its 2004 English translation. **Neither was reachable in full text this session.** What was reachable: the English and Arabic Wikipedia entries on Ibn Adlan plus related entries, which cite those two works at page level. This report therefore rests on an encyclopedia layer that itself cites the primary edition, and every quotation below is that layer's wording. **No sentence of Ibn Adlan's treatise was read directly for this account.**

## 1. Before him: the counting tradition he inherits

- **[R: Wikipedia, 'Al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi'; 'Al-Kindi']** Al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi (717-786, dates attested) works at Basra on what is described as the first Arabic book of cryptographic messages, using permutation and combination to enumerate all possible Arabic words with and without vowels. The book does not survive. The line that al-Kindi "credits" him is not what the source says; Wikipedia says only that al-Kindi built on the work. The date "c. 750s" in the research pool is unsourced: the work is undated.
- **[A: Wikipedia, 'Al-Kindi'; Al-Kadi 1992]** Al-Kindi, born at Kufa around 801, writes *Risala fi Istikhraj al-Mu'amma*, on deciphering cryptographic messages, in the mid-ninth century. It states the frequency method plainly and is the earliest known use of statistical inference against a cipher.
- **[R]** Two claims the pool carried that do **not** hold: (a) that al-Kindi's father was governor *of Kufa* (English Wikipedia says Basra; the sources conflict and it should not be stated flatly); (b) that the *Risala* was written inside the Bayt al-Hikma. No source places its composition there, and no site of the House of Wisdom is archaeologically identified. The journey file uses the corpus's canonical Baghdad pin (33.3475, 44.335) and describes the city, not the building.
- **[R: Michal Biran, cited in Wikipedia, 'House of Wisdom']** The image of the Tigris running black with ink in 1258 is partly a literary trope magnifying Mongol barbarity. The journey file says "destroys or scatters an unknown quantity," as far as the evidence goes.

**Interval error, corrected everywhere.** The pool repeatedly said al-Kindi wrote "two and a half centuries" before Ibn Adlan. Mid-ninth century to 1229-1237 is **nearly four centuries**. The error ran through the chronology, geography and afterlife lenses alike. Related: al-Qalqashandi in 1412 stands **almost six** centuries after al-Kindi and **under two** after Ibn Adlan, not "three and six."

## 2. Mosul, 1187: birth and formation

- **[A: English Wikipedia, 'Ibn Adlan'; Arabic Wikipedia, 'Ali ibn Adlan']** Born at Mosul in 583 AH / 1187 CE. Full name Ali ibn Adlan ibn Hammad al-Rabi'i al-Mawsili; honorific Afif al-Din; kunya Abu al-Hasan. No month or day is recorded, so the journey file dates the stop 1187-01-01 and says so in `date_confidence`.
- **[A: English Wikipedia]** Three epithets: **al-Mawsili** (of Mosul), **al-Nahwi** (the Grammarian), **al-Mutarjim**. The pool's fourth, *al-Mu'ammi*, is not in the source and is dropped. *Al-Mutarjim* literally means the translator or interpreter; the gloss "the Cryptanalyst" reflects the medieval Arabic use of *tarjama* for decipherment and is flagged rather than treated as the literal sense.
- **[R: Arabic Wikipedia]** Raised in Mosul, trained in hadith and grammar. The pool's "al-Makbari circle" is dropped: the Arabic article as retrieved contains no such circle, and al-Makbari and al-'Ukbari are two different names, one of which belongs to the separately attested Baghdad studentship below.
- **[R: Arabic Wikipedia, undated]** An early appetite for *alghaz*, linguistic riddles. He later writes a lost book on them (*Uqlat al-mujtaz fi hall al-aljaz*). Later sources connect this taste to his turn toward cryptanalysis; that connection is a source's inference, not a document.
- **[A: Wikipedia, 'Great Mosque of al-Nuri']** Coordinate correction: the Great Mosque of al-Nuri is at 36.34306, 43.12667. The pool's 36.3346, 43.1189 sits about 1.2 km southwest of it. The journey file uses 36.3431, 43.1267.
- **Name contamination, corrected.** The pool's geography lens called him "Ali ibn Muhammad ibn Adlan." That is wrong: *ibn Muhammad* belongs to **Ibn al-Durayhim** (Ali ibn Muhammad ibn Abd al-Aziz). Two Mosul cryptologists a century and a quarter apart, both carrying al-Mawsili, had bled into one another.

## 3. Baghdad: the studentship

- **[A: English Wikipedia, 'Ibn Adlan']** He travels to Baghdad and studies Arabic grammar under **Abu al-Baqa al-'Ukbari**. No years are given.
- **[R]** The Nizamiyya is the plausible address and is used as the pin (33.3417, 44.3901), but no source places al-'Ukbari's classroom inside it. The campa says so.
- **[A: Al-Kadi 1992, p.113; Mrayati et al. 2004, p.18, via the encyclopedia layer]** The treatise reproduces **al-Kindi's Arabic letter frequencies, identical numbers, with no attribution**. This is the strongest evidence for the dependence, and it is indirect: nothing records him reading al-Kindi, in Baghdad or anywhere. Stated as a gap in the campa.

## 4. The Syrian circuit: Aleppo and Damascus

- **[A: Arabic Wikipedia]** He travels to Aleppo and Damascus. No years for either.
- **Direction corrected.** The pool called this "a documented eastward trajectory from Mosul toward Egypt." Mosul (43.1 E) to Aleppo (37.2 E) to Damascus (36.3 E) to Cairo (31.2 E) is consistently **westward**. Corrected everywhere.
- **Patron misdated, corrected.** The pool put the Aleppo residence at c. 1215-1225 under **al-Zahir Ghazi**, who ruled 1186-1216 and died in 1216. The journey file narrows the Aleppo window to before 1216 and says in `date_confidence` why.
- **[R: Arabic Wikipedia]** In Aleppo his reputation is literary: poetry and puzzles. Writers close to his lifetime rank him grammarian and poet first, decipherer second; historians of cryptography invert that order. Both are recorded, neither adjudicated.
- **[A: coordinates]** Great Umayyad Mosque of Aleppo: 36.19949, 37.15691. The pool's value fell within 165 m and is kept in rounded form.

## 5. The treatise: *al-Mu'allaf lil-Malik al-Ashraf*

**Composition date: unknown.** **[R]** The pool's chronology lens asserted **1233** with an [A] tag while its own source note conceded "precise composition year not given." That year is not in any source and appears to have been invented to fill the field. The journey file uses 1233-01-01 as a **midpoint marker inside the attested 1229-1237 bracket** and says exactly that in `date_confidence`, where the hedge belongs. No prose sentence anywhere in the file asserts 1233 as a fact.

**Dedicatee: contested, not resolved.**
- **[A: English Wikipedia, 'Ibn Adlan']** The treatise is dedicated to **al-Ashraf Musa**, Ayyubid emir of **Damascus**, whose reign 1229-1237 brackets the composition.
- **[R: Wikipedia, 'Al-Ashraf Musa, Emir of Homs'; Al-Kadi 1992]** An alternative reading makes the patron the **later al-Ashraf Musa of Homs** (holding the city 1246-1263 as an Ayyubid vassal under Mamluk and briefly Mongol overlordship), who inherited at about seventeen and surrendered Homs to a cousin within two years. On that reading Ibn Adlan writes near sixty rather than near fifty.
- The secondary literature does not choose. **Both readings appear in the journey file as separate stops** (Damascus Citadel; Homs Citadel), each stating that the other exists.
- Also left standing: the pool's geography lens placed the writing of "at least two works on cryptanalysis" in **Cairo, c. 1250s-1260s**, irreconcilable with a dedication to a Damascene emir dead in 1237. That reading is not adopted; the conflict is named.
- **[A] Overstatement corrected:** "at least two works on cryptanalysis" rests on *al-Mu'lam*, which English Wikipedia lists as a **lost** work merely "said to have contained" cryptanalytic material. One cryptanalytic book survives.

**Contents, all [A: English Wikipedia, 'Ibn Adlan', citing Al-Kadi 1992 p.113 and Mrayati et al. 2004 pp.18-19]:**
1. Twenty cryptanalytic techniques arranged in nine thematic sections.
2. Cryptanalysis of **no-space monoalphabetic cryptograms** (*al-mudmaj*), called his most original contribution. **The motive claim is dropped:** the pool asserted that word boundaries were removed "specifically to defeat earlier frequency-analysis attacks of the al-Kindi type." No source says this. The campa says the sources do not say why.
3. A **minimum sample size**: about 90 characters, approximately three times the length of the Arabic alphabet, below which letter occurrence will not follow the frequency distribution. This is the specific advance on al-Kindi. The pool's earlier verdict of UNABLE TO VERIFY was itself wrong: the claim is in the live article, footnoted at page level. **But** the gloss "28/29-letter alphabet" is a writer's addition, not in the source, and the campa does not attach a letter count to the source's phrasing.
4. Letters stratified into **seven common, eleven medium, ten rare**.
5. Analysis of **consecutive letter co-occurrence**.
6. A table of common **two- and three-letter words**.
7. Probable stock openings, e.g. **Bismillah**.
8. A separate set of principles for **enciphered poetry**.

## 6. Cairo: the Salihiyya decades

- **[A: English Wikipedia]** He relocates to Cairo and teaches Arabic at the **al-Salihiyya** mosque/madrasa for the rest of his career.
- **Anachronism corrected.** The pool said Cairo was "then under Mamluk rule." The Mamluk Sultanate begins in **1250**; the Salihiyya was founded in **1242** by the Ayyubid sultan al-Salih Najm al-Din Ayyub **[A: Wikipedia, 'Madrasa of al-Salih Ayyub']**. He arrives in **Ayyubid** Cairo and lives to see the Mamluk takeover. The journey file dates the arrival stop 1242 to that foundation.
- **[R: English Wikipedia]** "He was in contact with various rulers of his time, and in this capacity he gained practical experience in cryptanalysis." No ruler, message, occasion or commission is specified anywhere. Recorded as tradition, gap named in the campa.
- **[A: English Wikipedia]** Three further lost works: *al-Mu'lam* (said to contain cryptanalysis algorithms), *al-Intihab li-kashf al-abyat al-mushkilat al-i'rab*, and *Uqlat al-mujtaz fi hall al-aljaz*. Titles only.
- **[A]** He dies at Cairo in **666 AH / 1268 CE**, aged about eighty-one. No month, no day, no circumstance.
- **[R] Burial: a genuine gap.** No source consulted names a grave, tomb, shrine, or later legend attached to him. The journey file includes an al-Qarafa stop (30.027, 31.274) whose campa says the cemetery is offered as a plausible district and nothing more. Arabic biographical dictionaries, above all al-Suyuti's *Bughyat al-wu'ah*, were **not** consulted and could hold material English-language sources do not surface. A stated gap, not a confirmed absence.

## 7. After him: the line to al-Qalqashandi

- **[A: Wikipedia, 'Ibn al-Durayhim']** Ibn al-Durayhim, born Sha'ban 712 AH / 1312, dies 1359 or 1361/62. His cryptology book, *Maqasid al-fusul al-mutarjama an hall al-tarjama* ("Clear Chapters Goals and Solving Ciphers"), "includes the use of the statistical techniques pioneered by Al-Kindi and Ibn 'Adlan." It contains the earliest known suggestion of a substitution tableau, two centuries before Vigenere.
- **Two corrections.** (a) The Arabic title in the pool, *Miftah al-kunuz fi idah al-lughz*, matches neither encyclopedia; English Wikipedia gives the *Maqasid* title, Arabic Wikipedia gives *Idah al-Mubham fi Hall al-Mutarjam*. (b) **Survival:** the pool's lenses contradicted each other. The specific article governs: the book "was recently discovered, but has yet to be published," while *History of cryptography* still calls it lost. The contradiction is named in the journey file, not hidden.
- **[R]** His birthplace at Mosul is inferred from the nisba, not stated by the cited source. His time at **Mardin** is general biographical notice with no document and no date; the 1330s placement is a guess. Coordinate corrected to 37.3131, 40.735 (the pool's value sat 1.3 km northwest).
- **[A: Wikipedia, 'Al-Qalqashandi']** Al-Qalqashandi (1355/56-1418), Katib al-Darj of the Mamluk chancery in Cairo, completes *Subh al-A'sha* in 14 volumes in **1412**; its cryptology section transmits material descending from Ibn al-Durayhim, and thereby from the al-Kindi and Ibn Adlan line. **In the surviving citations Ibn Adlan is not named alongside Ibn al-Durayhim.** English Wikipedia does not place his work at the Citadel specifically; that caveat sits in `date_confidence`.

## 8. Rediscovery

- **[A: English Wikipedia, 'Ibn Adlan']** A manuscript copy survives in the **Süleymaniye** collection, Istanbul (41.0161, 28.9639, exact). Nothing records when it entered the library or through what chain; no autograph is known.
- **Attribution corrected.** The pool credited the modern find to **Ibrahim A. Al-Kadi in 1987**. Wrong on both counts: the 1987 milestone is the **Damascus critical edition** by Mrayati, Meer Alam and al-Tayyan (Arab Academy, vol. I of *Ilm at-Ta'miyah wa Istikhraj al-Mu'amma Ind al-Arab*), and Al-Kadi's 1992 *Cryptologia* paper is a survey of the field, not a find report.
- **[A]** English translation, **2004**, as vol. 2 of the *Series on Arabic Origins of Cryptology*, King Faisal Center with King Abdulaziz City for Science and Technology; tr. Said M. al-Asaad, rev. Mohammed I. al-Suwaiyel, Ibrahim A. al-Kadi and Marwan al-Bawab; ISBN 9960-890-18-X. The pool hedged this as unverified; the article states it directly, so the hedge is dropped.

## Contradictions carried forward, not resolved

1. **Which al-Ashraf.** Damascus (Musa ibn al-Adil, d. 1237) versus Homs (Musa, 1246-1263). Two stops, both flagged.
2. **Where the cryptanalysis was written.** Damascus before 1237 (dedication) versus Cairo in the 1250s-60s (one lens of the pool). Only the first is adopted; the conflict is stated.
3. **Ibn al-Durayhim's book: lost or found.** Two Wikipedia articles disagree. The specific article governs; the disagreement is named.
4. **Ibn al-Durayhim's death year.** 1359 / 1361 / 1362 across sources; the place of death is stated by none of them.
5. **Al-Kindi's father's governorship.** Kufa or Basra. Not adjudicated.
6. **The order of Ibn Adlan's own priorities.** Grammarian and poet first (near-contemporary biographers) versus cryptologist first (modern histories). Both stand.

- No named teacher of the Mosul years; no named student anywhere, at Aleppo, Damascus or Cairo.

## Named links to other travelers in the corpus

Named in the campas because a real relation exists, not because of shared geography:

- **al_kindi**. Ibn Adlan's treatise reproduces his letter frequencies number for number and answers the question his *Risala* left open. Direct, one-directional dependence.
- **al_khalil_ibn_ahmad_al_farahidi**. The permutational treatment of Arabic that every later grammarian, Ibn Adlan included, works inside. One-directional, and stated as tradition.
- **ibn_al_durayhim** (queued, no file yet). He names Ibn Adlan explicitly as one of the two men whose statistical techniques his own book uses. The reverse citation across a century and a quarter, and the second Mosul-born cryptologist of the line.
- **al_qalqashandi** (queued, no file yet). He transmits the tradition in *Subh al-A'sha*, though in surviving citations he names Ibn al-Durayhim and not Ibn Adlan.

**Deliberately not named**, despite appearing in the interlock pool: Ibn Arabi (Damascus, 1223-1240), Maimonides (Cairo, d. 1204), al-Kamil (Ayyubid sultan of Egypt), Richard the Lionheart (Acre, 1192). Every one is shared place or shared decade with no attested contact, no correspondence, no citation either way. Claude Shannon's file frames itself against al-Kindi rather than Ibn Adlan, and no line of transmission runs from the ninety-letter rule to the 1945 memorandum, so he is left out too, although the question the two men ask is recognizably the same one.
