# Maslama al-Majriti (c. 950 - 1007), research report

*Compiled 2026-08-14. Journey file: `maslama_al_majriti.journey.json`, 9 segments, 39 stops.*

**Legend.** **[A]** = attested, source named. **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition, or inference. Contradictions are flagged and left open; unreachable sources are named with the reason.

**The problem in one line.** Two men in tenth-century Cordoba were called Maslama, and the more famous one has spent a thousand years carrying the other one's books. Almost every difficulty below descends from that, and it is not resolvable from the sources reachable here. The journey keeps two strands apart: the documented career, and the attribution history of works fastened to his name later.

---

## 1. Mayrit and the frontier (c. 950 - c. 968)

- Born at Mayrit (Madrid), a small Umayyad fortress-town of the Marca Media, traditionally **c. 950**. **[R: Wikipedia (En), 'Maslama al-Majriti'; Wikipedia (Es), 'Maslama al-Mayriti']** No source fixes a month, a day, a quarter, or a house. The year is circa in every source consulted.
- Full name **Abu 'l-Qasim Maslama ibn Ahmad al-Faradi al-Hasib al-Majriti al-Qurtubi al-Andalusi**, carrying the nisba of the town that bore him and the city that claimed him. **[A: Wikipedia, 'Maslama al-Majriti']** `al-Faradi` = divider of inheritances; `al-Hasib` = calculator.
- **Gap.** No source records a return to Madrid. The date and route of the move south are **[R]**, estimated from his floruit.

## 2. Cordoba under al-Hakam II (c. 968 - 976)

- He works inside Cordoba's scientific culture under the caliphate. **[R: Wikipedia (En/Es)]** The pool's anchor of c. 970 is an estimate, not an attestation.
- Al-Hakam II (r. 961-976) expands the Great Mosque and builds its gold-mosaic mihrab. **[A: Wikipedia, 'Al-Hakam II']** Maslama's presence there is **[R]**, inferred from the sciences being taught in that setting.
- Al-Hakam II assembles a library reported at hundreds of thousands of volumes, buying in Damascus, Baghdad, Constantinople, Cairo, Mecca, Medina, Kufa and Basra, and sponsors a joint Muslim and Mozarab committee translating Greek and Latin into Arabic. **[A: Wikipedia, 'Al-Hakam II']**
  - **Honest gap.** That source **does not name al-Majriti among the figures of al-Hakam II's court.** His use of the library is inference. The journey states the absence rather than papering over it.
- **Madinat al-Zahra**, the caliphal palace-city and seat of patronage. **[A]** for the site and its dates (built from 936-940, sacked 1010-1013); **[R]** for any presence of Maslama, whom no source puts on those terraces.
  - **Coordinate correction.** The pool gave 37.8607, -4.8465, roughly 3.5 km off in open country southeast of the site. Journey uses **37.888, -4.867**.

## 3. The tables: al-Khwarizmi recast for a western horizon

- **Al-Khwarizmi compiles the Zij al-Sindhind at Baghdad, c. 820. [A: Wikipedia, 'Al-Khwarizmi']**
  - **Coordinate correction.** The pool gave 33.3406, 44.3922 for the House of Wisdom, about 5.3 km east of the Round City, across the Tigris in modern Rusafa. Journey uses **33.3475, 44.3350**.
- **Maslama revises the zij. [A: Wikipedia, 'Al-Khwarizmi'; 'Maslama al-Majriti']** The best-attested fact about him: the Arabic original is lost, and al-Khwarizmi's tables survive **only** through Maslama's recension.
- **Date contradiction, flagged, not resolved.** The pool dated the revision to **985**; the al-Khwarizmi article dates it to **around 1000 CE**. The journey file carries 1000 with the conflict recorded in `date_confidence`.
- **Source discipline.** The much-repeated detail that he converted the Persian calendar dates to the Hijri is **not** in the articles cited for it; it descends from the Suter/Neugebauer edition, not opened. **[R]** The meridian shift is standard in secondary literature, not verified against a primary source.
- **Ptolemy's Planisphaerium and the Almagest.** He translates and corrects the Arabic Planisphere and improves the Arabic Almagest. **[A: Wikipedia, 'Maslama al-Majriti']** The Alexandria stop is background geography **[R]**; he never travelled there.
- **Commercial arithmetic and taxation; surveying and triangulation. [A: same]** Undated; known by report, no text traced.

## 4. The school and the students

- **He founds a school of astronomy and mathematics at Cordoba**, credited by Said al-Andalusi with beginning organized exact-sciences research in al-Andalus. **[A: Wikipedia, 'Maslama al-Majriti']** Date and address unrecorded **[R]**.
- **Student list corrected.** The pool's list (Ibn al-Saffar, Abu al-Salt, at-Turtushi) holds two chronological impossibilities, both struck:
  - **Abu al-Salt**, b. c. 1068, d. 1134, sixty-one years after al-Majriti died. Wikipedia's Ibn al-Saffar article calls him a *later* scholar influenced by the circle, not a fellow student.
  - **Abu Bakr al-Turtushi**, b. 1059, d. 1126, fifty-two years after.
  - List used: **Ibn al-Saffar (d. 1035), Ibn al-Samh (979-1035), Abu al-Hakam al-Kirmani (b. c. 970). [A]**
- **Ibn al-Saffar**: teaches on at Cordoba; astrolabe treatise still in use into the fifteenth century; determines the coordinates of Mecca (date not in the source, **[gap]**); d. Denia 1035. **[A: Wikipedia, 'Ibn al-Saffar']**
- **Ibn al-Samh**: b. Cordoba 979; instruments, equatoria, theory; enters Zirid service under Habus ibn Maksan at Granada; d. 1035. **[A: Wikipedia, 'Ibn al-Samh']**
- **Abu al-Hakam al-Kirmani**: b. Cordoba c. 970; studies at Harran; settles at Zaragoza; said to have fled to Morocco, death uncertain, 1066 commonly cited. **[A: Wikipedia, 'Abu al-Hakam al-Kirmani']** Harran coordinates (36.8656, 39.0306) are standard geography, not from the cited sources.

## 5. The Brethren of Purity

- The **Rasa'il Ikhwan al-Safa** are compiled by an anonymous fraternity in the Islamic east in the mid-tenth century; scholarship divides between **Basra and Baghdad** for the place. **[A: Wikipedia, 'Brethren of Purity']** Contradiction noted, not resolved.
- **Maslama's circle edits and alters parts of the Rasa'il after they arrive in al-Andalus. [A: Wikipedia, 'Maslama al-Majriti']** This is the defensible form of his connection to the Epistles.
- **The claim that he brought them to Spain is contested. [A: SEP, 'Ikhwan al-Safa']** The SEP separates two men previously conflated: **Maslama al-Qurtubi al-Majriti (d. 964)**, now argued to have brought the Rasa'il west in the first half of the tenth century, and **the scientist (d. 1007)**. It credits **al-Kirmani** with introducing the *complete* Rasa'il, and places the transmission "under a false attribution" to al-Majriti at the start of the eleventh century, isolated and partial.
- **Hamdani's dating, relocated.** His argument that the Ghaya quotes the Rasa'il between 954 and 960 is confirmed **[A: Wikipedia, 'Brethren of Purity']**. The pool sited it at "Maslama's school" (37.8795, -4.781), an anachronism the pool argues against elsewhere: in those years Maslama was a child and no school existed. Moved to Cordoba, 37.8882, -4.7794.
  - **What it does and does not show.** The Hamdani sentence calls the Ghaya "the anonymous (Pseudo-Majriti) work". It is evidence **against** his authorship of the Ghaya, not evidence **for** his transmission of the Rasa'il, and cannot carry the second claim.
- **Gap.** Nothing traced documents his circle physically carrying the Rasa'il into al-Andalus. muslimheritage.com and Encyclopaedia Iranica returned **404 and 403**.
- **Zaragoza as relay point [R]**: recurs in secondary literature, confirmed by no source consulted. Carried with the gap declared.

## 6. The regent, the alchemy, the death

- **Astrological adviser to al-Mansur (Almanzor)**, choosing hours to launch campaigns. **[R: Wikipedia (Es)]** No source documents contact between them; the regency (981-1002) is **[A]**, the relationship is not.
- **The mercury experiment**, from the **Rutbat al-Hakim**, read later as early documentation of conservation of mass. The block quote is verbatim on Wikipedia, 'Maslama al-Majriti', cited there to **E. J. Holmyard, *Isis* 6, no. 3 (1924), 293-305, at p. 302.**
  - The article is confirmed to exist via Crossref (p. 302 inside the range), but **not opened**: Isis 6(3) is missing from the Internet Archive SIM run (6(2) and 6(4) are present) and JSTOR is paywalled. The wording is verified against Wikipedia's transcription only.
  - **Tag correction.** The pool tagged this **[A]** in one lens and **[R]** in another. It must be **[R]**: the Rutbat belongs to the same pseudo-Majriti corpus as the Ghaya, and treating it as attested would contradict this file's own attribution argument.
  - **Quotation integrity.** A sentence sits between the two lines usually run together: *"The outer pot was then in such a degree of heat that I could bear my hand upon it."* The journey marks the cut with `[...]`.
- **Death: 1007 at Cordoba. [A: Wikipedia, 'Maslama al-Majriti']** **Contradiction flagged:** the Picatrix article gives **1005-1008 CE (398 AH)**. Both are in `date_confidence`; neither is preferred.
- **No grave.** No burial site, mausoleum, or cult of the tomb is attested in the Arabic biographical dictionaries or the scholarship consulted. Stated as a gap.

## 7. The verdict, written sixty-one years late

- **Date correction.** The pool dated Said al-Andalusi's judgment **995**. Said lived **1029-1070** and wrote the *Tabaqat al-Umam* in **1068**, born twenty-two years *after* al-Majriti died. The stop is dated 1068 and the campa says the verdict is retrospective (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Said_al-Andalusi).
- **This is not a quotation.** The wikitext reads, with no quotation marks: *According to [[Said al-Andalusi]], he was the best mathematician and astronomer of his time in al-Andalus.* That is Wikipedia's paraphrase, not Said's translated words. The underlying citation, **Salem and Kumar, *Book of the Categories of Nations* (Texas, 1991), p. 64**, was **not opened**. The journey therefore carries **no `quote`** on this stop.

## 8. The Ghayat al-Hakim / Picatrix: the attribution dispute

**The traditional attribution**
- **Ibn Khaldun**, *Muqaddimah* (1377), ascribes the Ghaya to Maslama al-Majriti. **[R: Wikipedia, 'Picatrix']** Verbatim in the wikitext, but footnoted **not to Ibn Khaldun** and instead to **Eugenio Garin, *Astrology in the Renaissance* (Routledge, 1983), p. 47**: a tertiary paraphrase two removes from the Arabic, which was not opened. The stop is pinned at **Qal'at Ibn Salama** (34.94, 1.05), the traditional site of composition; the pool gave only "North Africa" and the pin is declared approximate.
- The **earliest surviving manuscript attribution** is by the alchemist **al-Jildaki (d. c. 1360)**, about two decades before Ibn Khaldun repeats it. **[A: same]** The Cairo pin follows al-Jildaki's known Egyptian activity; the city is **not** in the source and is flagged as a placement.

**The case against**
1. **The text dates itself.** The introduction gives completion at **348 AH, c. 959 CE**, after finishing the **Rutbat al-Hakim in 343 AH, c. 954**. With a birth of c. 950, al-Majriti was about **nine** at the Ghaya's completion and about **four** at the Rutbat's.
   - **Arithmetic correction.** The pool said "about five" in two lenses, "about nine" in a third. **Nine** is correct for 959; five follows only from 954. Reconciled to nine throughout.
   - **Source quality.** The 348 AH sentence is footnoted **not** to Ritter (1933), Ritter and Plessner (1962), or Attrell and Porreca (2019), but to a bare Internet Archive link to an unedited Arabic scan (`archive.org/details/majritighayat/page/n20`). **[R]**
2. **The "Rutbat c. 1009" date is unsupported and was dropped.** The pool built an internal-contradiction argument on a tradition dating the Rutbat to 1009, two years after his death. The Picatrix article states plainly that the Rutbat **precedes** the Ghaya, at **c. 954**. The 1009 figure appears only on the al-Majriti article and matches no standard dating (older literature after Holmyard read the manuscript dates as 439-442 AH / 1047-1051). The real impossibility rests on 954/959.
3. **No biography of al-Majriti mentions the work. [R: same]**

**The counter-attribution**
- **Maslama ibn Qasim al-Qurtubi (d. 964 CE / 353 AH)**, called by the biographer **Ibn al-Faradi** "a man of charms and talismans", proposed as the author, placing the work in Andalusi Sufi and Batini currents. The study is **Maribel Fierro, 'Batinism in Al-Andalus. Maslama b. Qasim al-Qurtubi (died 353/964), Author of the Rutbat al-Hakim and the Ghayat al-Hakim (Picatrix)', *Studia Islamica* 84 (1996), 87-112**, confirmed to exist via Crossref; **the article was not fetched. [R as sourced here]**
- **Other scholars hold the Arabic text is simply anonymous.** Wikipedia's summary of the field: *"More recent attributions of authorship range from 'the Arabic version is anonymous' to reiterations of the old claim that the author is 'the celebrated astronomer and mathematician Abu l-Qasim Maslama b. Ahmad Al-Majriti'."* (footnoted to Kahane et al., *Romance Philology* xix (1966), p. 575, and Holmyard, *Isis* vi (1924), p. 294).
  - **Truncation correction.** The pool cut this sentence and the Fierro sentence mid-clause, losing the substantive content and closing each with a period not in the original. Both are restored in full in the journey's `quote` fields.

## 9. Latin afterlives

- **Toledan Tables**, c. 1080, from a group around al-Zarqali, for Toledo's meridian. **[A: Wikipedia, 'Toledan Tables']** **Gap:** no source confirms a direct line from Maslama's recension, though both sit in the same meridian-adaptation tradition. Said as a gap in the campa.
- **Adelard of Bath, 26 January 1126**: the Latin of **Maslama's revision** of the zij. **[A: Wikipedia, 'Al-Khwarizmi']** The pool hedged this; **the hedge is closed.** The article states that the lost Arabic survives through Maslama's revision and that the Latin of it was made "presumably by Adelard of Bath (26 January 1126)". Strongest documented transmission link in the file, and the journey asserts it.
  - **Counter-correction.** The pool's "years of study in Islamic Spain" is overstated: the article lists only "possibly Spain" and says historians merely *believe* he learned Arabic in Sicily or Spain. The campa claims only that he learned Arabic somewhere on his travels.
- **Alfonso X** commissions the Castilian Ghaya, **1256-1258**; the Latin **Picatrix** follows, with Christianizing changes. **[A: Wikipedia, 'Picatrix']** **Gap:** no city is named. Toledo is standard, Seville equally plausible given the itinerant court; both are carried, Seville declared unresolved.
- **Chaucer's *Treatise on the Astrolabe* (1391)** works from a Latin manual circulating under **Mashallah's** name, translated by John of Seville. **Kunitzsch established the author was Ibn al-Saffar.**
  - **Date correction:** the pool gave 1980; the Astrolabe article gives **1981**, matching the paper (*AIHS* 31, 1981), not opened.
  - **Source correction:** the footnote cites **Helaine Selin (ed.), *Encyclopaedia of the History of Science, Technology, and Medicine in Non-Western Cultures* (Springer, 2008), p. 1335**, not Kunitzsch directly. The journey cites Selin, with Kunitzsch as the scholarship reported.
- **Destombes / Barcelona astrolabe: cut from the journey file.** The pool dated it 1050 and credited al-Majriti's tradition; the dating is contested (eleventh-century Portugal per the Astrolabe article, c. 980 Catalonia per Destombes) and **no source asserts the causal link.**
- **English occult afterlife**: a British Library Picatrix manuscript passes through **Forman, Napier, Ashmole, Lilly. [A: Wikipedia, 'Picatrix']** The London pin stands for England, not an address.
- **Wilhelm Printz** identifies an Arabic manuscript of the Ghaya around 1920. **[A: same]** **No stop made**: the source gives no location, and inventing a city would be worse.

Fourteen corrections were carried from the research pool into the journey file; each is documented above at the point where it changed the map.

## 10. Fatima de Madrid, and honours

- **Fatima de Madrid**, the supposed daughter and collaborator credited with correcting tables, an astrolabe treatise, and work on the Almagest, **cannot be traced earlier than the Enciclopedia Espasa-Calpe entry of the 1920s (given as 1924)** and is unattested in the medieval tradition. Arabists including **Manuela Marin** call her a modern fabrication kept alive by repetition. **[R: Wikipedia, 'Fatima of Madrid', citing Requena Fraile and Marin]** Carried as iconographic afterlife, not as a fact about his family.
- Called **el Euclides de España** in later Spanish tradition **[R: Wikipedia (Es)]**; in **2015** the IAU NameExoWorlds project names an exoplanet orbiting **Titawin** (Upsilon Andromedae) **Majriti** **[A: Wikipedia (Es); NameExoWorlds 2015 results]**.

## Interlocks

The interlock pass surfaced Cordoba as a dense node: **Maimonides**, **Ibn Arabi**, **Ziryab**, **Aristotle** (via Ibn Rushd and the Toledo translators) and **Hypatia** (via the Alexandrian Ptolemaic lineage).

**None is named in a campa, deliberately.** Each connection is shared geography, discipline, or tradition, and none is a documented relation: he did not meet, correspond with, cite, or write about any of them, and none cites him. Ziryab precedes him by a century in the same court; Maimonides and Ibn Arabi follow him by a century and a half in the same city; Hypatia stands upstream in a lineage running through Ptolemy, not her. Naming them would be name-dropping co-location, which the map already shows.

The figures in genuine direct relation to him have no journey files: al-Khwarizmi, Ptolemy, his three students, Said al-Andalusi, al-Jildaki, Ibn Khaldun, Adelard of Bath, Alfonso X. They are named freely in the campas as ordinary historical figures. **Interlock count: 0.**
