# Alexander the Great — Pella to Babylon: the Son of Ammon and the Ends of the Earth

**45 stops · 9 segments · 15 quotes · 356–323 BC (julian_bce)** *(quote count revised down from 16 by the verification pass of 2026-07-24; see below)*

## Sources

Three canons braided, as the tradition demands equal weight for all three:

- **Arrian, *Anabasis of Alexander*** — the spine for the campaign narrative: Granicus, Issus, Tyre, Gaugamela, the death of Darius, Bessus, Cleitus, the Sogdian Rock, the Hyphasis speech, the Mallian wound, the Gedrosian march, Pasargadae, Opis, and the death at Babylon (Books 1–7). Arrian, writing from the lost eyewitness memoirs of Ptolemy and Aristobulus, is the most sober of the ancient sources and anchors every "attested" date.
- **Plutarch, *Life of Alexander*** — the intimate register: Olympias's dream and Herostratus's fire, Bucephalus, Aristotle at Mieza, Diogenes at Corinth, the tomb of Achilles, the tent of Darius, the Susa/Siwa material, and Cleitus's death. Plutarch is a moralist working a century and a half later, so his best lines (the "kingdom equal to yourself," the Achilles/Homer remark, "I will not steal my victory") are treated as the tradition's own voice, not filed as uncertain.
- **The Alexander Romance (Pseudo-Callisthenes)**, its Syriac, Persian, and Latin daughters, and the **Letter of Alexander to Aristotle** — canon here exactly as directed, on equal footing with the historians: the glass diving-bell at Tyre, the Land of Darkness and the Water of Life with the cook Andreas/al-Khidr, the Trees of the Sun and Moon, and the griffin-flight. Diodorus Siculus and Quintus Curtius Rufus fill gaps (Thebes, Persepolis and Thaïs, Gaza, the Susa weddings, the last words). The **Qur'an, Surat al-Kahf 18:83–98** supplies the Dhul-Qarnayn/Gog-and-Magog episode verbatim, per the curator's explicit instruction that this is the edge into the Muslim cluster.

## Judgment calls, time-folds, and gaps

- **The Romance is interwoven, not ghettoized.** Rather than clumping the four fantastical episodes into one legendary appendix, each was pinned to the geographic/thematic point in the historical itinerary the tradition itself associates with it: the glass bell at Tyre (explicitly tied there by the Problemata/Romance tradition, "to scout the harbor defenses"), the Land of Darkness and Water of Life at Alexandria Eschate on the Jaxartes (the literal edge of the mapped world in 328 BC), the Gate against Gog and Magog at the Caucasus/Derbent gap, and the Trees of the Sun and Moon plus the griffin-flight in furthest India, just before the Hyphasis mutiny — so the trees' prophecy of death-by-poison-in-Babylon becomes the dramatic hinge the whole return march hangs on.
- **One deliberate fold**: the Land of Darkness and the Gate of Gog/Magog are dated November–December 328 BC, when Alexander was actually in Bactria/Sogdia, not the Caucasus — the Romance's own geography is looser than the historians', and the curator's brief calls the Romance canon, so the fold is narrated straight rather than hedged, exactly as the register instructs.
- **Cyrus's tomb, two visits folded to one.** Arrian records Alexander weeping at the tomb-epitaph and, separately, finding it looted and ordering Aristobulus to restore it — but both events belong to the single documented visit in Jan/Feb 324 BC on the return from India (there is no solid second visit on record in 330 BC, despite some popular retellings implying one). The single stop here carries both the grief and the restoration, and shares its exact coordinate (30.194, 53.178) with the tomb stop in `cyrus.journey.json` — the same little stone house, visited two centuries apart.
- **Quotes were held to a strict rule**: only Alexander's own recorded words qualify, per the schema. This meant dropping several famous *lines about him* that aren't *his* lines — Philip's "seek a kingdom worthy of you," Aristander's satyr-pun on Tyre, the Cyrus epitaph itself — even though all are narrated as color inside the campa prose. The Qur'anic Dhul-Qarnayn quote (18:98) is the one case where the "traveler's own words" are scripture's own words, since the curator names this the Islamic-canon voice for this same figure.
- **Coordinates**: firm real-world sites (Pella, Chaeronea, Troy/Hisarlik, Gordium, Tyre, Gaza, Memphis, Alexandria, Siwa, Gaugamela/Tell Gomel, Babylon, Susa, Persepolis, Pasargadae, Ecbatana, Samarkand/Maracanda, Taxila) are firm. Genuinely contested ground — the Hyphasis mutiny site, the Mallian town, the Gedrosian route, Darius's death-spot near Hecatompylos, Opis — sits on the traditional/best-guess location and is marked accordingly. Babylon, Susa, Ecbatana, and Pasargadae reuse the exact coordinates from `cyrus.journey.json` to share pins across the atlas.
- **Gap**: no stop for the burning of the Temple of Artemis itself (Ephesus is not on Alexander's own itinerary — it is narrated as backstory within the Pella birth-stop, the way the tradition itself frames it as an omen of his birth, not a place he visited as a child).

## The five richest episodes

1. **The glass bell at Tyre** — the king descending alone into the feeding grounds of the deep, surfacing to tell his officers the world below is "damned and lost," the great fish devouring the small: the Romance's cruelest and best image, folded into the real siege.
2. **The Sogdian Rock** — "you would need men with wings" answered literally, by night, on tent-pegs and flaxen rope, thirty men lost to the climb before the herald points up.
3. **Pasargadae, twice** — Alexander weeping at Cyrus's own epitaph, then finding the tomb robbed and the king's bones scattered, and quietly ordering it mended rather than avenged: the conqueror as custodian of the conquered.
4. **The Trees of the Sun and Moon** — the male tree promising him lordship of the whole earth, the female tree naming, in the same breath, poison and Babylon and a year left to live; the single most explicit prophecy-fulfilled moment in the whole dataset.
5. **The death at Opis and Babylon together** — the mutiny, the two days of silence, the banquet where "kinsmen" is spoken over nine thousand men from every conquered nation, followed within a year by "to the strongest": the whole arc of universal empire collapsing into a single unanswered pronoun.

## Connections in the atlas

Per the curator's brief: **Aristotle** is his tutor at Mieza, quoted in his own letter of complaint (Plutarch 7) — the one moment the philosopher answers back. **Cyrus**, already in the atlas, is the hinge explicitly built here: Alexander stands at the same tomb, reads the same epitaph, and restores what looters broke, making Pasargadae a literal shared pin between the two journeys. The **Dhul-Qarnayn** episode (Qur'an 18:83–98) is written as the edge into the Muslim cluster exactly as directed, with the Gate of Gog and Magog placed at the Caspian/Derbent gap that later Islamic geography identifies as his wall. **Genghis Khan**, the atlas's other world-conqueror, is not given a stop here (per the brief, he is an edge/connection rather than a shared scene) but the two journeys rhyme structurally: both begin with a portentous birth and a father's murder, both end with the conqueror's empire dissolving among quarreling successors the moment breath leaves the body. Downstream of Alexander sits the whole Hellenistic and Roman world this atlas has not yet reached; upstream, through Cyrus, sit **Zoroaster's Persia** and the scriptural quarter Cyrus's own journey opens onto.

**Quotes — 16 checked against the canon, 12 changed, 1 nulled.** The governing rule was the researcher's own: only words the sources actually carry as Alexander's.

- **Nulled.** *Thebes* — "Spare the house of Pindar, and the roof that sheltered the poet" appears nowhere. Arrian 1.9 has only "It is said that Alexander preserved the house and the descendants of Pindar the poet, out of respect for his memory," with no direct speech; Plutarch 11 likewise. The order survives in the campa, where it belongs; the quote field is now null. Tally falls 16 → 15.
- **Restored to the canon's wording** (verified against Perrin at LacusCurtius and Chinnock at Wikisource): Bucephalus, the letter to Aristotle ("those doctrines," not "these"), the Diogenes remark, the Hyphasis speech (the invented "for a man who is a man, toil… is itself good" replaced by Arrian's actual "to a brave man there is no end to labours except the labours themselves, provided they lead to glorious achievements"), the Sogdian Rock herald's shout, and the Opis reconciliation ("But all of you without exception I consider my kinsmen, and so from this time I shall call you").
- **Re-sourced.** *Gordium* — "It makes no difference how they are loosed" is Curtius 3.1.18, not Arrian 2.3, which carries only the linchpin version and the remark that the cord "had been untied by him." *Siwa* — "I have heard what my own soul desired" was filed as a paraphrase; Arrian 3.4 in fact carries it as Alexander's own account ("having heard what was agreeable to his wishes, as he himself said"), so the wording now follows Arrian and the campa sentence was aligned to match. *Issus* — Curtius 3.12.17 reads *et hic Alexander est*, "he too **is** Alexander," not "is called Alexander"; the small word matters, since the point is that Hephaestion is a second self, not a namesake. *Gaugamela* — "I will not steal my victory" confirmed verbatim in Plutarch 31; Arrian 3.10 demoted to a secondary, indirect witness.
- **Upgraded.** *Troy* — Plutarch 15 gives the Achilles remark only in indirect speech, so the dataset's direct rendering was unsupported. Replaced with the line the canon does carry directly: Cicero, *Pro Archia* 10.24, *o fortunate adulescens, qui tuae virtutis Homerum praeconem inveneris!* — spoken, Cicero says, at that very tomb at Sigeum. *Hydaspes* — Arrian 5.19 gives Alexander's question indirectly but his reply directly; the reply is now the quote, and Porus's "in a kingly way" stays in the campa where the schema wants it.
- **Left standing.** The Qur'anic 18:98 (Yusuf Ali's "He will make it into dust" is a real rendering, not a slip), "To the strongest," and the Tyre glass-bell line. The last is legend quoting legend, so its source note was tightened to say plainly that the great-fish-devour-the-small moral belongs to the medieval Western retelling rather than the Greek recension — a provenance note, not a debunking. In this register the Romance is canon and the line stays.
