# San Cristóbal (Christopher of Lycia): research report

*2026-08-02. Subject: the martyr called Christopher, born Reprobus or Adokimos; in the Eastern strand a conscript of the numerus Marmaritarum out of Marmarica, in the Western strand a giant ferryman of an unnamed Lycian river. Traditional martyrdom c. 250 under Decius; earliest firm evidence of cult, 452. Queue item 38, flagged "dog-headed in the Eastern tradition; patron of travelers", 38 documents in the census, first in the corpus survey.*

**Legend: [A] = attested, source named · [R] = tradition, hagiography, or scholarly reconstruction.** Nothing here is attested in the sense a historian would want: no document contemporary with a third-century Christopher exists. In this file [A] means *the text, object, building or decree exists and is dated*; [R] means *the tradition says so*.

---

## 0. The evidentiary floor

Modern historical-critical scholarship treats Christopher as legendary: no contemporary record, no archaeologically confirmed relic site, no text within two centuries of the events **[A: scholarship on the Passio Sancti Christophori]**. The Roman church made this the stated ground for the 1969 removal from the universal calendar **[A: General Roman Calendar of 1969]**.

What is dated and attested:

- a martyrium at Chalcedon consecrated **22 September 452** **[A: consecration record, via Wikipedia "Saint Christopher"]**, the earliest evidence of an established cult;
- terracotta plaques from **Vinica**, North Macedonia, 5th-6th century, one showing a dog-headed soldier saint **[A: Wikipedia]**;
- a Greek *Passio* (BHG 309-310c), probably 6th century, "possibly 5th century or later" in Wikipedia's more cautious phrasing **[A as a text; R as to its date]**;
- the earliest Latin passion, **8th century**, BHL 1764 **[A: Wikipedia]**.

Everything narrative sits below that floor. Two hundred years separate the traditional martyrdom from the Chalcedon stone, and nothing fills them.

## 1. Origins: two incompatible birthplaces

**The Eastern strand.** The early Greek Passion places his origin among the **Marmaritae**, a tribe west of Egypt described as giants, cannibals and *cynocephaloi*, dog-headed. His pre-baptismal name is **Reprobus**, Greek **Adokimos**, "the rejected one" **[A: early Greek Passion (BHG), summarized in Orthodox Arts Journal]**. No settlement is pinpointed; the journey pins Paraetonium (Mersa Matruh), the nearest fixed point in known Marmaritae country.

**Conscription.** David Woods (UCC, 1999) argues that Christopher was a Marmarita taken in Diocletian's campaign of **late 301 / early 302** and forced into the **numerus Marmaritarum**, garrisoned in or near **Antioch** **[A as to Woods's argument; R as to the events]**. Woods reads the dog's head as a marker of peripheral peoples, not as a scribal accident.

> **Correction.** The pool attributed the *Cananeus / canineus* etymology to Woods. It is not his, and Wikipedia runs the confusion in the opposite direction: Greek *kunokephalos* gave Latin *canineus*, misread afterwards as *Cananeus*, the Canaanite.

**Rival birthplaces, unresolved.** Legend gives **Marmarica (Libya)**, **"Barbaria"**, or **Canaan** **[R: Wikipedia; Orthodox Arts Journal]**. The Canaan version is downstream of the *Cananeus* reading and is therefore not independent evidence. Noted, not adjudicated.

**The Coptic root.** Wikipedia states the legend "seems to have its roots in" the **Coptic Acts of Bartholomew** (4th-5th c., Egypt): a dog-headed cannibal who calls himself "Bewitched" or "the Damned", is baptized, receives human speech from an angel, and is named *Christianus* / *Christomaios* **[A: Wikipedia]**.

> **Correction.** The pool called this "the earliest surviving account of Christopher". It is not: the figure is not Christopher there, and Wikipedia claims ancestry, not identity.

**Gap.** The Coptic text names no place; the journey pins the Egyptian national centroid and declares it one.

## 2. Baptism and preaching: the strands separate for good

**Eastern:** baptized at **Antioch**, then preaching "in Lycia and Samos" **[A: Orthodox Church in America, *Lives of the Saints*, May 9; Omnesmag]**. **There is no ferryman episode in this strand at all**: no river, no child, no staff.

**Western:** a Canaanite giant converted by a riverside hermit in Lycia, baptized in connection with the Christ-child crossing **[R: Jacobus da Voragine, *Legenda Aurea*, c. 1260]**.

**Contradiction flagged, not resolved.** The two accounts differ on origin, on conversion, and on the whole central episode. No source reconciles them; the journey carries both as parallel unmerged arcs, segments 1 and 2.

**Which Samos?** The sources say "Lycia and Samos" and do not specify **[R]**. An island detour fits nothing else in the narrative, and the supposed Lycian town of the same name cannot be pointed at. The journey pins the island, the commoner assumption, with the doubt in `date_confidence`.

## 3. The Western legend: king, devil, hermit, river

The sequence, all **[R: *Legenda Aurea*, Caxton trans. 1483]**, corroborated by Catholic Online, EBSCO Research Starters and the OCA Life:

1. Reprobus, a giant of twelve cubits, serves the greatest king he can find; leaves when the king crosses himself at the word *devil*.
2. Serves the Devil, met on the road as "a knight cruel and horrible"; leaves when the Devil detours around a wayside cross.
3. A hermit, told he will not fast and cannot pray, sets him to carry travellers over a dangerous river.
4. He carries a child who grows unbearably heavy; the child is Christ, bearing the weight of the world. He is renamed Christopher, Christ-bearer.
5. His planted staff blossoms.

**No river is ever named** in the *Legenda Aurea* **[A: absence in the text itself]**. The Caxton text supplies the journey's quotes verbatim; nine stops carry one, unrestyled.

> **Correction.** The pool labelled the crossing coordinates (36.3392, 29.3392) "Xanthos river mouth". They sit some 8 km inland: the city is at 36.3561, 29.3186, the mouth near 36.28, 29.32. The journey keeps the pin, relabelled "Xanthos valley", a declared regional anchor.

## 4. Passion and martyrdom

- Imprisoned by **Dagon** (OCA, Omnesmag) or **Dagnus** (Golden Legend), acting under Decius **[R]**. Neither is attested outside hagiography.
- **Callinika and Aquilina**, sent to seduce him, convert and are martyred **[R: OCA, May 9; BHG 309-310c names the same pair]**.
- Torture by fire and a red-hot iron seat; archers, one arrow striking the emperor's eye **[R: Passio tradition; OrthodoxWiki; OCA]**.
- Beheading; the ruler is healed by clay tempered with the martyr's blood, and converts **[R: Golden Legend]**.
- The OCA Life gives the year as **250**; the Golden Legend gives none **[A as to what each text says]**.

**Contradictions flagged, not resolved:**

1. **Lycia vs. Antioch** as the site. The Hieronymian Martyrology, earliest Western witness, has Lycia under Decius; another strand of the *Passio* puts the trial at Antioch **[R]**.
2. **Decius (r. 249-251) vs. Diocletian (r. 284-305)** as persecutor. Later Latin tradition shifts the emperor, moving the martyrdom half a century **[A: the divergence is in the texts]**.
3. **Blood vs. milk at the neck.** The Golden Legend requires blood, since the healing clay is tempered with it. The tradition carried in this atlas's own `catherine_alexandria.journey.json` says milk ran from Christopher's neck as from Catherine's. Both are in the beheading stop; neither is preferred.

## 5. Cult, image, and the argument about the dog

**The image does not follow the text.** The chronology of depictions:

| Date | What is shown | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| 5th-6th c. | Vinica terracotta: dog-headed soldier saint | [A: Wikipedia] |
| 10th c. | Synaxarion of Constantinople: "wild and fearsome looking", **not** canine | [A: Sanidopoulos, 2018] |
| 11th c. | Cappadocia (Göreme): young, beardless soldier, no dog, no child | [A: Sanidopoulos] |
| 13th-15th c. | Athos, Patmos, Tarnovo: beardless soldier, sometimes with child on shoulder | [A: Sanidopoulos] |
| late 15th c. | Tarnovo, SS Peter and Paul: **bearded**, child on shoulder, post-Byzantine | [A: Sanidopoulos] |
| 17th c. | panels with an unmistakable canine snout on a bearded warrior | [A: JSTOR Daily] |
| late 17th-early 18th c. | the literal dog-headed icon appears in surviving *Greek* painting | [A: Orthodox Arts Journal; Sanidopoulos] |

> **Corrections.** (a) The Synaxarion, Cappadocia, Athos, Patmos and Tarnovo material is Sanidopoulos (2018), not the Orthodox Arts Journal. (b) The pool merged the Palaiologan beardless soldier at Tarnovo with the late-15th-century bearded Christ-bearer in the same church: two phases, a century and a conquest apart, separated in the journey stop.

**Coordinate corrections applied:** Docheiariou to **40.2664, 24.1733** (pool off by ~9 km); Karakallou to **40.2239, 24.3083**; Vinica, centroid replaced, to **41.8783, 22.5089**; Havana to **23.1424, -82.3494** (El Templete); Šibenik to **43.7358, 15.8892** (the pool pin sat in the harbour).

**Theories of the dog's head, all live, none adjudicated here:**

- **Woods (1999):** the dog-head marks a peripheral people **[A as a published argument]**.
- **Etymological:** *kunokephalos* to *canineus*, misread as *Cananeus* **[A: Wikipedia gives this direction]**.
- **Hellenistic-Egyptian substrate:** descent from Anubis and Hermanubis imagery **[R: Wikipedia links the Vinica plaque to Hermanubis]**.
- **Deliberate symbolic layering** of Cainite, Canaanite and canine associations, not error **[R: Jonathan Pageau, Orthodox Arts Journal, 2015]**.
- **The dog as slur:** Emily Rebekah Huber notes "dog" as a medieval insult for non-Christians, so the cynocephalus absorbed Muslims, Jews and heretics **[A: Huber, via JSTOR Daily]**.

**The theological question.** Augustine, *De Civitate Dei* XVI, ruled that whatever is born of man descends from the first man and that no Christian may doubt it **[A: via JSTOR Daily]**. Around **863-865** Ratramnus of Corbie answered Rimbert, later Ansgar's successor at Hamburg-Bremen, on whether the dog-headed peoples of the north were rational and fit for mission; citing the fathers and a hagiography of Christopher, he concluded that they were **[A: *Epistola de cynocephalis*, via JSTOR Daily]**.

> **Correction.** The pool dated Ratramnus to 850. The letter is c. 863-865.

## 6. Relics: two skulls, one body

- **Rab, Croatia.** A skull in an 11th-century gold-plated reliquary; Bishop Raduald displays it from the walls during the Italo-Norman siege of **1075**, the arrows turn back on the attackers, and the town keeps 9 May as *Dies Victoriae* **[A as to cult, reliquary and commemoration; R as to the miracle: Brepols/Ikon; kristofor.hr; rab-visit.com]**.
  > **Correction.** The geography lens put the reliquary in the Cathedral of the Assumption. It is in the **Museum of Sacred Art at St Justine's** (Sveta Justina), as the afterlife lens had it; two concurring sources.
- **Karakallou, Mount Athos.** Skull fragments venerated as Christopher's, with a skull of Bartholomew and a fragment of the True Cross **[A: Monastiriaka; Athos Forum]**. **Contradiction flagged:** Rab and Karakallou both hold the skull. Neither account mentions the other; both are carried.
- **Šibenik.** The leg, from Constantinople by way of Corfu in **1484**, originally destined for Korčula; the altar plaque reads "the foot, shin and femur of St Christopher the Martyr". The cathedral is dedicated to St James **and St Christopher** **[A: Wikipedia]**.
- **Vercelli.** A molar enshrined in silver and gold by the Humiliati; Juan Luis Vives (1492-1540) calls it "a molar tooth bigger than a fist". The Humiliati are suppressed in 1571, the Barnabites keep the veneration to the end of the 18th century, when a naturalist identifies it as a **hippopotamus** tooth; it leaves the altar **[A: British Dental Journal, 2019]**.
  > **Correction.** The geography lens said mammoth. The BDJ says hippopotamus.
- **Toledo Cathedral.** A reliquary associated with Louis IX recorded as holding a relic of Christopher, body part unspecified **[A: ToledoSpain.Click]**. Undated; dropped rather than padded with a guessed year.

## 7. The Western giant: statues, print, and the Fourteen

- **Norton Priory, Runcorn:** the largest medieval Christopher left in Britain, 3.37 m, late 14th c. **[A: Wikipedia, "Statue of St Christopher, Norton Priory"]**.
- **Notre-Dame de Paris:** a colossal statue, c. 28 feet, commissioned **1413** by Antoine des Essarts, chamberlain to Charles VI; **knocked down in 1785** to admit light to the nave **[A: Wikidata; Penelope.uchicago.edu]**.
  > **Correction.** The pool gave 1786 (it is 1785) and "nearly ten metres" (c. 8.5 m). Accounts blaming the Cult of Reason are four years out.
- **Buxheim, 1423:** the earliest dated European woodcut depicts Christopher **[A: Wikipedia]**.
- **The Fourteen Holy Helpers.** Formed in Franconia and the Rhineland around the Black Death. Regensburg 1350 and the Vierzehnheiligen vision of 28 July 1446 are already pinned by `saint_barbara.journey.json` and `giles.journey.json` **[A as to the stops]**. Christopher's membership is standard hagiography but is **named in neither existing stop** **[R]**; both new campas say so.

## 8. Calendars, bans, and the modern afterlife

- **c. 1550:** the reformed Roman Tridentine calendar commemorates the feast on **25 July** **[A: Wikipedia]**. **1954:** extended to all Masses of the day **[A]**.
- **1722:** the Russian Synod bans dog-headed depictions **[A: Wikipedia]**. The Old Believers continue the type; **the ban on their practice is lifted in 1971** **[A: Wikipedia]**.
  > **Correction.** The pool inverted 1971, reading it as the year the practice stopped, and invented a "1750" Moscow proscription out of the Orthodox Arts Journal's undated "proscribed in the 18th century by Moscow". That date is deleted, the item merged into 1722.
- **19th c.:** Emanuel Max's Christopher on the south side of the Charles Bridge, Prague **[A: Wikipedia]**. No year given; 1850 is a declared placeholder.
- **1969:** removed from the General Roman Calendar, "leaving the memorial of Saint Christopher to local calendars"; 25 July passes to St James. Not a decanonization, and his name stays in the Roman Martyrology **[A: General Roman Calendar of 1969; Catholic Answers]**.
  > **Correction.** The geography lens called this a downgrade from obligatory to optional. It was removal from the universal calendar.
- **Feasts kept:** 25 July in the West, **9 May** in the East **[A: Wikipedia; OrthodoxWiki]**.
- **Motorists:** dashboard medals and key-ring medallions become his chief modern form and outlive the reform **[A: The Automobile and American Life; Bikerringshop]**. No origin site exists, so the practice gets no stop; it is folded into the 1969 campa.
- **Rab:** the Rabska Fjera, 25-27 July, for St James, St Anne and St Christopher, with crossbowmen's games **[A: Rab heritage pages]**. The journey ends there.
