Gana

Rivers, trees, etymologies, and the genealogies of families, philosophies, and morals all contain the same morphological structure. These are the branching flows of ganas and the desiring form of primitive life energy. Ganas originates from the Proto-Indo-European root *gen-, meaning to give birth or beget. It is the source of the words genus, genius, generate, engineer and genital, coming through the Latin ganare, meaning to produce or earn. The Castilian ganar (to win), gana (desire), and ganado (cattle) also share this root. Here, we confront a conspiracy of language that places the cow, production, and fluid desire in a complex of meaning. Like Deleuze's desiring-production, it is a form of wanting based on a generative positive rather than a lack.

Goethe began the Italian Journey leaving from [image: 762043aaae…], or Karlovy Vary. The enclave is a small Bohemian spa town, built on the geothermal springs at the banks of the Ohře and Teplá crossing, founded in 1349 after Bohemian King Charles IV, in the third year of his minor coronation, came on the springs while hunting. In 1786 Goethe would have walked at night along Vrídelní, the boardwalk of the Teplá, holding a thick ceramic cup filled with hot mineral water from one of the sixteen springs in Carlsbad. Its handle would be a straw and draw the warmest water from the bottom of the vessel.

When Goethe sets out from the bath town of the Roman-German emperor, he traveled to Egra, and down to Bavaria. Once over the border he visits the Imperial Waldsassen Abbey, first inhabited by Thuringian monks of Volkenroda Abbey in the mid 12th century. The journey passes through Tirschenreuth, [image: 762043aaae…], Regenstauf, Bad Abbach, and Saal, and as he passes over the landscape comments on the quality of the roads, grade of the ground, run of the water, bounding rivers, constituent rocks and minerals, geological properties, and fertility of the soil.

I reached the Walchensee about half past 4. About three miles from this place I met with a pretty adventure. A harper came before me with his daughter, a little girl, of about eleven years, and begged me to take up his child… I spoke with her on very many subjects, she was at home with all of them, and made most pertinent remarks. Thus she asked me once, what tree one we came to, was. It was a huge and beautiful maple, the first I had, seen on my whole journey. She narrowly observed it, and was quite delighted when several more appeared, and she was able to recognize this tree. She was going, she told me, to Botzen for the fair, where she guessed I too was hastening.

— J. W. von Goethe, Italian Journey (Walchensee)

After midnight the wind blows from north to south, and he who wishes to go down the lake must travel at this time, for a few hours before sunset the current of air changes, and moves northward. At this time, the afternoon, it blows strongly against me, and pleasantly qualifies the burning heat of the sun. Volkmann teaches me that this lake was formerly called "Benacus," and quotes from Virgil a line in which it was mentioned: Fluctibus et fremitu resonans, Benace, marino — resounding with the waves and the roar, Benace, of the sea (Virgil, Georgics, Book II, line 160). In Hindu language, the conspiracy reaches beyond etymology and takes root in the meaning of the root sounds themselves. Lord Shiva on Mount Kailash provides the locus for an effluent mountain of meaning. Ganas are the names of Shiva's fierce and loyal attendants on Mount Kailash, led by his elephant-headed son, Lord Ganesha. Apart from his human form, Shiva incarnates in his most primitive form as the oblate ellipsoid phallus linga. He is depicted seated, accompanied by his mount (vahana), the Nandi cow. From Shiva's head sprouts the river Ganges in personified form, spouting milky water down over the linga, as the source of the sacred river. In his "South American Meditations," the German philosopher Count Hermann Alexander Graf Von Keyserling (1880–1946) explores the matrix of meaning in his seventh meditation on Argentina, titled "Gana," presenting the Río de la Plata as the ultimate manifestation of primordial unconscious desire, not only as the home of great populations of cattle, but more so in the blind poetic expressions of wanting.

In Argentina, the word Gana manifests itself more impressively than anywhere on earth, because its intrinsic passivity is concomitant with outward progressiveness, intellectual alertness, and great refinement of feeling.

— Hermann Graf von Keyserling, South American Meditations (1932)

From 1913 to 1916, Jung began to explore embodied practices he called "Active Imagination," which served as ritual conduits for channeling the raw generative gana. He wrote that the practice "could be done in a number of ways …dramatic, dialectic, visual, acoustic, or in the form of dancing, painting, or modeling." Over the next 17 years, Jung directed more attention toward performative and embodied rituals, writing on the dance of the Pueblo Indians, the dance of the Hindu Maya, Zoroastrian dance, and the practice of yoga.

You can dance, not only to produce the union with yourself or to manifest yourself, but in order to produce rain, or the fertility of women, or of the fields, or to defeat your enemy. The idea of an effect, or something produced, is always connected with the idea of dancing.

In 1933, the same year Jung became president of the General Medical Society for Psychotherapy, the year the Reichstag burned after appointment of the Chancellor, and while Laban was working to integrate Jung's concepts into his dance productions at the Berlin Opera, Olga formalized the Eranos Conferences with Jung as a founding member. Olga described the conferences as "rituals" or "dances" of "spirit ganas". Eranos was the only meeting ground of its kind to remain open during the course of the second world war.

In 1947 Miguel Serrano traveled to Antarctica with Carl Jung's 1928 'The Relations Between the Ego and Unconscious'. He studied the book intently and slept with it under his pillow, as Napoleon had done with Goethe's "Young Werther". He saw in Antarctica something of an archetypical domain, and the study of Jung's works influenced Serrano to study yoga, travel to India as the Chilean Ambassador, and read Richard Wilhelm's translation of the 'I Ching'. Like Braille's scheme, the 'Book of Changes' used a six element hexagram, which enumerated 64 different variations, the same number of blocks on the chessboard. In 1957, Serrano started a written correspondence with Jung and finally on February 28, 1959, the two met at Hotel [image: 762043aaae…], overlooking Lake Maggiore, in Locarno, Ticino, next to the town of Ascona.
