# Saadia Gaon (Sa'id ben Yosef al-Fayyumi), c. 882-942

*Research report for the Atlas of Journeys. Compiled 2026-08-14. Egypt to Palestine to Iraq: the first systematic Jewish theologian, the first Hebrew lexicographer, the man who put the Bible into Arabic and lost his office rather than countersign an exilarch's ruling.*

**Legend.** **[A]** = attested, source named. **[R]** = reconstruction, inference from a stated age, tradition, or scholarly synthesis. Where two sources disagree, both are printed and neither is adjudicated. Sources not reached are named with the reason.

**Standing warning on dates.** Many dates in circulation for Saadia are not attested years but arithmetic on a contested birth year: the literature says "at the age of 20", "at the age of 23", and compilers convert ages into years using whichever birth year they favour. Since the birth year is disputed by a decade, every derived date carries ten years of slack. Derived dates are marked as derived; in the journey file the hedge lives in `date_confidence`, never in campa prose.

---

## 1. Birth and the contested decade (Fayyum)

**[A]** Born in the Fayyum of Middle Egypt, at Dilaz or near it, son of Joseph. The district gives him his Arabic name, al-Fayyumi. Wikipedia, "Saadia Gaon"; Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (SEP), "Saadya".

**Contradiction, flagged, not resolved.**
- **882 CE**, resting on a Cairo Genizah list of his writings compiled by his sons, dated 1113. Wikipedia states the 892 date "was exclusively cited before 1921" and that modern scholarship rests on 882.
- **892 CE**, the older reference-book date, still printed.

**882 is adopted as primary here, 892 noted, on one internal ground:** all sources give his age at death in 942 as sixty, which fits 882 and contradicts 892. That is a judgement about internal consistency, not new evidence, and is marked **[R]** wherever it does work. The pool this report corrects was internally inconsistent, anchoring 892 in one lens and 882 in two others.

**Consequence.** The commonly printed 912 (Agron) and 915 (first anti-Karaite work) are **[R]**, not [A]: the source gives ages, not years. On an 882 birth they fall c. 902 and c. 905. Note also that 915 is separately given for the emigration to Palestine, so the received chronology pins two different events to one derived year.

**Gap.** No teacher of the Egyptian years is named anywhere reachable. That he absorbed Arabic philology and kalam is **[R]**, reconstruction from the character of his later work (SEP), not a documented apprenticeship.

## 2. The first books (Egypt, c. 902-912)

**[R, derived from an age]** At about twenty, the **Agron**, a Hebrew dictionary arranged for poets, words listed by initial and again by final letters so a composer can find rhymes. Wikipedia.

**[R, derived from an age]** At about twenty-three, a polemic against the followers of **Anan ben David**, his first anti-Karaite work. Same source, same derivation.

**Correction.** The Egyptian polemic was against the Ananites. **Abu al-Surri ben Zuta** belongs later: Wikipedia introduces him with "Later, one of Saadia's chief disputants was the Karaite by the name of Abu al-Surri ben Zuta", after his rise. Any pin placing Ben Zuta in Egypt c. 905-915 is wrong.

**[R]** Location within Egypt for these years is not firmly attested; Fustat is conventional, the Fayyum the alternative. The journey file uses the canonical Fustat pin and says so.

## 3. Palestine (c. 915-920)

**[A]** He leaves Egypt for the Land of Israel and settles for a period in **Tiberias**, studying under the Masoretic scholar **Abu Kathir Yahya al-Katib**. Wikipedia.

**[R]** That the Tiberian Masoretic and grammatical tradition shaped his philology is reconstruction from the shape of his work (SEP; MyJewishLearning), not a documented curriculum.

**[R], with a date correction.** His father **Joseph** "was compelled to leave Egypt and died in Jaffa" (Wikipedia). That is the entire notice. **No year is given.** A "c. 915-920" bracket in circulation is fabricated. The journey file places the stop within the Palestinian years for sequence only and says so. What compelled the departure is not stated; whether father and son travelled together is not stated.

**Gap.** No wife or child is named, though he later writes of separation from "my wife and children" (section 5).

## 4. The calendar war with Aaron ben Meir (921-922)

**[A]** **Aaron ben Meir** of the Palestinian gaonate proclaims a calculation moving Passover and the festivals **two days earlier** than the Babylonian reckoning (Wikipedia, "Aaron ben Meir"). The threat is schism.

**[A]** "Saadia was in **Aleppo**, on his way **from the East**, when he learned of Ben Meir's regulation of the Jewish calendar" (Wikipedia, "Saadia Gaon"). Two corrections follow from that sentence:
1. "From the East" means he was travelling **westward**, away from Babylonia. Pool material describing him as travelling "from the east toward Babylonia" inverts the direction.
2. He **learned** of the decree at Aleppo. The source does not say he composed his treatise there; **Sefer ha-Mo'adim** was written later "at the request of the exilarch", which places it in Babylonia.

**Correction on the opponent's seat.** Ramla is **not** Ben Meir's seat, and the article usually cited for it gives no location at all. Wikipedia, "Palestinian Gaonate", says the gaonate moved from Tiberias to Jerusalem only "by the mid-tenth century", so in 921-922 it sat at **Tiberias**; Ben Meir is traditionally described as proclaiming from the Mount of Olives. Ramla was the Muslim capital of Jund Filastin, a different institution. Any Ramla pin should be removed.

**[A]** Fall 921: peace at Baghdad between the exilarch **David ben Zakkai** and **Mar Kohen-Zedek** of Pumbedita (Wikipedia, "David ben Zakkai"). David writes to Ben Meir urging him not to cause a schism; the excommunication that follows is **David's act**, not Saadia's alone.

**[A]** **Sefer ha-Mo'adim**, 922. The Babylonian reckoning prevails and the fixing of the Jewish year passes to Babylonia for good.

## 5. The Genizah letters

Recovered from the Cairo Genizah, printed by **Solomon Schechter, Saadyana (1903)**, translated in **Henry Malter, Saadia Gaon: His Life and Works (JPS, 1921)**, which was fetched and read directly in full text on archive.org.

**[A]** Travel prayer written on the road, Baghdad to Arbela to Mosul to Aleppo, c. 920/921 (Saadyana pp. 133-135; Malter pp. 61-62): "Now Thy servant has set his face to go into the land of Canaan and the land of [Babylonia] ... And now, O Lord, that Thou hast taken me out of my city, mayest Thou lead me to my desire, and bring me back in peace to the house of my father."

**[A]** Letter to his former pupils in Egypt, written in Babylonia, dated by Malter to the winter of 922 (Saadyana pp. 24ff.; Malter pp. 55-56): "As I have been desolate ever since I left my wife and children, so I have grieved over my separation from you ... It is now six and a half years that no word from you has reached me."

**Citation correction, carried into the journey file.** The much-quoted "you have probably thought that I am still in Palestine" is real but is **not** on Malter p. 56. It stands in Malter's **Postscript, p. 425**, paraphrasing the letter. Malter's actual translation at p. 56 reads: "I presume that you wrote to him, and not to me, only because, in accordance with previous reports, you thought that I was still in Palestine." The journey file quotes only the p. 55-56 passage verbatim.

## 6. Sura (c. 922-928)

**[A]** He settles permanently in Lower Mesopotamia; Wikipedia gives **926** for the arrival at Sura, at Mata Mehasya.

**Correction.** "Under Gaon Yom Tov Kahana" in 926 fails on the sources' own chronology: Wikipedia, "Sura Academy", lists Yom-Tob Kahana at **c. 924**, and Wikipedia, "David ben Zakkai", states that "a weaver filled the office of gaon for two years (926-928)". The **alluf** title is conventionally dated 922; no source consulted gives it a year. Tag **[R]**.

**COORDINATE CORRECTION, the most consequential fix in the dossier.** Wikipedia, "Sura (city)": **31 deg 53 min N, 44 deg 27 min E = 31.883, 44.450**, expressly east of the Euphrates. A pin at 32.4, 43.8 that propagated through the working pool sits about 85 km northwest, on the **wrong side of the river**, toward Karbala. Every Sura stop in the journey file uses 31.883, 44.450. The death pin is one of them.

**[A]** **Sefer ha-Tamyiz** (Book of Distinction), his principal anti-Karaite work, **926**.

**[A]** **928**: David ben Zakkai appoints him **Gaon of Sura**. Sources call this unconventional, a "contested rise" (SEP), the office having gone by custom to Babylonian scholarly families rather than to an Egyptian immigrant. **Overstatement corrected:** neither Wikipedia nor SEP says "first non-Babylonian to hold the post". That superlative is not used.

**[A]** **Pumbedita**, the rival academy, conventionally located near modern Fallujah (33.3489, 43.793); its gaon in this period is **Kohen-Zedek**. "Joseph ben Jacob" in some compiled material is a conflation with **Joseph b. Jacob b. Satia**, installed **at Sura** in 933 to replace Saadia: different academy, later date.

## 7. The work of the gaonate

**[A]** The **Tafsir**: Judeo-Arabic Bible with commentary, Torah first, then Isaiah, Psalms, Job, Proverbs and others, rendered for sense. SEP; Wikipedia; MyJewishLearning. **Gap:** no source gives composition dates for individual books. Treated as a body of work across the Babylonian years.

**[A]** **Kitab al-Amanat wa'l-I'tiqadat** (Book of Beliefs and Opinions, Emunot ve-Deot), completed **933**, the first systematic work of Jewish philosophical theology, argued in Mu'tazilite kalam terms. **Attribution correction:** SEP gives **no** completion date; 933 comes from Wikipedia and is cited to Wikipedia in the journey file.

**[A]** A **Siddur**, Kitab Jami al-Salawat wal-Tasabih, with Arabic rubrics and his own liturgical poetry, including Azharot on the 613 commandments.

**[A]** **Kutub al-Lughah**, and **Tafsir al-Sab'ina Lafza**, a list of some seventy rare biblical words, frequently reprinted.

**[R]** A commentary on the **Sefer Yetzirah**, Tafsir Kitab al-Mabadi. SEP lists it but gives **no date and no place**. The c. 931 date from older reference literature is not attested in the sources cited here, and Baghdad as its place of composition is not attested at all.

**[R]** Refutations of **Hiwi al-Balkhi**; date and place unconfirmed.

**[R]** Responsa and polemics against **Salmon ben Yeruham** and **Ibn Saqawayhi**; locations of individual exchanges unspecified.

## 8. The rupture with David ben Zakkai (c. 932-937)

**[A]** An inheritance case comes before the exilarch. David rules in a manner sources call self-interested and irregular, and needs both geonim to countersign. Kohen-Zedek signs; Saadia refuses (Wikipedia, "David ben Zakkai").

**Date correction.** The case is **not** datable to 928, which is the year of his appointment. Wikipedia gives no date for the case but dates the outcome precisely: "Saadia was deprived of his gaonate (**beginning of 933**)." The case is therefore c. 932-933, independently supported by the caliphal chronology below.

**[A]** **Beginning of 933**: deposition, and **Joseph b. Jacob b. Satia** installed. A pool date of 930 is wrong; the c. 930 in Wikipedia, "Saadia Gaon", refers to the outbreak of mutual excommunication, not the deposition.

**[A]** Saadia answers by declaring David deposed and proclaiming David's brother **Josiah, called Hasan**, exilarch. This follows the deposition, so **933**, not 930.

**[A]** Both parties petition the Abbasid court, **al-Muqtadir** and, after his death, **al-Qahir**. Judgment goes to David. Josiah is banished to **Khorasan** and dies there. *Note:* al-Muqtadir died in 932 and al-Qahir reigned 932-934, which independently pushes the litigation's onset into 932.

**Coordinate correction.** Khorasan is pinned conventionally at **Nishapur, 36.2133, 58.7958**. A pool value of 58.4926 sits about 27 km west of the city, in open country. **Hedge corrected:** the identity of the banished man is not contested; it is Josiah Hasan, and the journey file says so.

**[A]** From **Nathan ha-Bavli's Report**, in Malter pp. 110-111:
- David to Saadia by his son Judah: "Go and tell him in my name that he shall at once endorse the documents."
- Saadia's reply: "Tell your father that it is written in the Torah, 'Ye shall not respect persons in judgment.'" (Deuteronomy 1:17)
- David's second message: "Sign and don't be a fool!"

**Over-quotation corrected.** Malter p. 111 reads: *Judah went home defeated, and, "with tears running from his eyes," reported to his father what had happened.* Only the phrase inside the inner quotation marks is Nathan's; the rest is Malter's narration. The journey file paraphrases.

**[R]** Malter's characterization of the exilarchal Letter of Excommunication ("In the baseness of its tone and the vileness of its accusations this document has its equal only in the diatribe of Sarjadah mentioned below") is **Malter's prose, not the Letter's**, and stands at **p. 112**, not p. 111. The Letter itself (Harkavy, Zikron, V, p. 232) was not fetched.

**Duration correction.** Out of office from the beginning of 933 to 27 February 937: **four years**, not "roughly seven"; Wikipedia says they were reconciled "four years later". Also, **no source consulted names his residence during the deposition**. Baghdad is a map convenience and the journey file states that in `date_confidence`.

**[A]** **27 February 937**: reconciliation, precipitated by David's maltreatment of a litigant whose case had favoured Saadia and by the intercession of **Bishr ben Aaron**, whose son-in-law Sarjadah was feeding the quarrel. Malter pp. 121-123 preserves both the community's charge to Bishr and Bishr's admonition to David. Date from Wikipedia, "David ben Zakkai".

**[A]** He is restored as Gaon of Sura for the rest of his life.

## 9. Last years and death (940-942)

**[A]** David ben Zakkai dies, **940**; his son **Judah** succeeds and dies **seven months** later.

**Major correction, and the best detail in the dossier.** The grandson did **not** die. Wikipedia, "Exilarch": "his son Judah died seven months afterward. Judah left a son (whose name is not mentioned) twelve years of age, whom **Saadia took into his house and educated** ... His generous treatment of the grandson of his former adversary was continued until Saadia's death in 942."

**[A]** He dies **16 May 942**, at about sixty, of "black gall", melancholia, per his son **Dosa** as reported by **Abraham ibn Daud, Sefer ha-Qabbalah**.

**Contradiction, flagged, not resolved.** Wikipedia gives **Sura** as the place of death; SEP says he "died in Baghdad in 942". Incompatible, unadjudicated in anything consulted. Both appear as separate labelled stops in the journey file.

**[R], weakened from the pool.** A Wikipedia infobox names the **Safed Old Jewish Cemetery** as his burial place. That field is **uncited and its internal link is dead**. No consulted source describes a tomb, marker, or pilgrimage there; claims of a "venerated tomb" and a "site of later pilgrimage" overstate the evidence and were removed. Wikipedia, "Sura Academy", records no tomb or remains at Sura either.

**Correction.** "Sura closed roughly 45 years after Saadia's tenure" misreads the source: Sura functioned from 225 CE to **1033 CE**, and the "about 45 years" refers to one temporary closure. Sura closed permanently **91 years** after his death.

## 10. Afterlife of the texts

**[A]** The **Tafsir** was preserved chiefly by continuous copying in **Yemenite** communities after it fell out of use elsewhere.

**[A]** **Yosef Qafih** (Kapach), Yemenite-Israeli, produced modern critical editions of the Tafsir and of the Book of Beliefs and Opinions. **Date correction:** the 2011 imprint in Wikipedia's bibliography cannot be the edition's date, since **Qafih died in 2000**. His Judeo-Arabic edition with facing Hebrew first appeared in **1970**; 2011 is a posthumous reprint.

**[A]** **Judah ibn Tibbon** translated the Book of Beliefs and Opinions into Hebrew as **Emunot ve-Deot**, traditionally late twelfth century (MyJewishLearning). The Lunel pin is the Tibbonid family seat, conventional, and marked as such.

**[A], upgraded from the pool's "unverified" flag.** **Abraham ibn Ezra's** critique was checked against the primary text, his commentary on **Genesis 2:11** (Hebrew, Piotrkow 1907-1911 ed., via Sefaria). Published English (Strickman and Silver, Menorah, 1988): "The fact of the matter is that he had no tradition concerning these things ... Perhaps these things were revealed to him in a dream. He definitely erred in a number of them as I will point out in their proper place. So we will not rely on his dreams." The widely circulated English mistranslates *bemiktzatam* ("in some of them", i.e. some identifications) as "with respect to certain places". The journey file marks the rendering as the editor's.

**[R]** A late legend in **Sefer Hasidim** credits him with settling a disputed-lineage inheritance by a bone-and-blood test. Folklore; given no stop.

**[A]** Israeli commemoration is **toponymic, not figurative**: a street named Se'adya Ga'on in Tel Aviv at its junction with HaHashmona'im. The further claim that other Israeli cities carry his name is **not** in the cited source and was dropped.

**[R]** **No** portrait, icon, or likeness of Saadia is attested in anything consulted. Stated as a gap.

**[R]** Fragments of his Bible translation, responsa, grammar, and the letters of section 5 came out of the **Cairo Genizah** at Fustat and were published from the 1890s (Schechter, Saadyana, 1903; later Genizah scholarship).

## 11. Relations to other travellers in the atlas

The rule applied: a name enters a campa only where Saadia himself stands in a real relation to that person, not because two lives touched one city.

**Named: Maimonides**, once, at the Tafsir stop. The relation is direct influence and inheritance: Saadia's Judeo-Arabic Bible and his Judeo-Arabic philosophical theology are the enterprise Maimonides works inside two and a half centuries later, in the same Egyptian community, in the same language, on the same problem.

**Considered and rejected:** **Benjamin de Tudela** (his Baghdad exilarch and academies are institutional continuity, not a relation between persons); **Rashi** (the code-versus-commentary parallel is structural, and nothing in the pool shows him citing Saadia); **Nebuchadnezzar II** (the 586 BCE deportation founded the community Saadia later headed, which is causation at a distance, and the Tafsir's coverage of Daniel is not attested in the pool); **Martin Buber, Rebecca Jefferson, Kenneth Stow, David Sassoon** (modern figures at the end of a textual or geographic chain, with no relation to Saadia himself).

**Verified negative.** A grep across `working/journeys/*.journey.json` returns no existing file mentioning Saadia by name (two false positives, in leo_africanus and maslama_al_qurtubi).

**Canonical pins inherited byte-identical:** Fustat **30.0059, 31.2312** (maimonides, benjamin_de_tudela); Baghdad exilarch's court **33.3152, 44.3661**; Baghdad academies **33.333, 44.363**; Baghdad general **33.335, 44.413** (all benjamin_de_tudela).

## 12. Honest gaps

1. **The birth year is open.** 882 is adopted on internal consistency with the death notice, not on independent evidence.
2. **Every early date is derived from an age**, not attested.
3. **No teacher, school, or year of departure from Egypt is named.**
4. **The father's death has no date.** One sentence, one city, no year.
5. **The Tafsir's composition dates are unknown**, book by book, as are the dates and places of the Sefer Yetzirah commentary and the Hiwi al-Balkhi refutations.
6. **His residence during the deposition (933-937) is not attested anywhere.** Baghdad is a map convenience.
7. **Place of death is contradicted** between the two principal reference sources.
8. **There is no attested grave**, at Sura or at Safed.
9. **There is no likeness**, and no description of his person survives.
10. **The Letter of Excommunication was not fetched**; only Malter's characterization of it was read.
