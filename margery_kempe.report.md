# Margery Kempe (c. 1373 to after 1438): research report

*Bishop's Lynn (King's Lynn), Norfolk. Brewer, mother of at least fourteen, pilgrim to Jerusalem, Rome, Compostela, Wilsnack and Danzig, repeatedly examined for heresy and never convicted, dictating author of the earliest surviving autobiography in English.*

**Legend.** **[A]** = attested, source named on the line. **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition, or scholarly approximation. Contradictions are flagged where they occur and left unresolved. Unreachable sources are named with the reason.

**Method.** The spine of the life is the Book, which is not a chronicle: it narrates thematically, drops years, and circles back. Almost every date in every modern Kempe chronology is therefore an editorial placement. Where the Book supplies a year (23 July 1436, 28 April 1438) it is marked [A]; where a chronology supplies one by inference it is marked [R], even when every encyclopedia repeats it as fact. Middle English follows Lynn Staley's TEAMS edition (metseditions.org), checked line by line; several widely circulated quotations proved half-modernized hybrids and are corrected below.

---

## 1. Lynn: birth, marriage, and the businesses (c. 1373 to c. 1402)

- Born Margery Brunham at Bishop's Lynn, daughter of John Brunham, merchant and five times mayor of the town. **[A: Wikipedia "Margery Kempe"; Britannica]** The year c. 1373 is back-calculated from her own statement of her age at marriage, and is **[R]** as a hard date.
- Married John Kempe, a burgess of Lynn, c. 1393. **[A: Wikipedia; Britannica]** Her own words: *"Whan this creatur was twenty yer of age or sumdele mor, sche was maryed to a worschepful burgeys and was wyth chylde wythin schort tyme."* **[A: Book I ch. 1, Staley/METS]**
- John Kempe became a town official of Lynn in 1394. **[A: Wikipedia]** The pool as gathered called him *chamberlain*; the cited source does not. **Corrected: the office is unsupported**, and the ODNB, which would settle it, was not reachable.
- First child born, the first of at least fourteen. **[A: the Book]** Fourteen is itself a reconstruction from scattered references; the Book keeps no roster of children. **[R: Wikipedia summarizing the Book]**
- After the birth she breaks down for roughly eight months, tormented by devils, until a vision ends it. Her words: *"sche dyspered of hyr lyfe ... this creatur went owt of hir minde and was wondyrlye vexid and labowryd wyth spyritys half yer eight wekys and odde days."* **[A: Book I ch. 1, Staley/METS]** *Note: the widely quoted form "she went owt of hir minde" is wrong; the text has "this creatur went owt of hir minde." The Book almost never says "I."*
- The vision that ends it: *"owyr mercyful Lord Crist Jhesu ... aperyd to hys creatur ... in lyknesse of a man ... clad in a mantyl of purpyl sylke ... And anoon the creature was stabelyd in hir wyttys and in hir reson."* **[A: Book I ch. 1, Staley/METS]**
- The brewery: *"sche gan to brewyn and was on of the grettest brewers in the town N a three yer or four tyl sche lost mech good"*, and then *"the berm wold fallyn down that alle the ale was lost every brewyng aftyr other."* **[A: Book I ch. 2, Staley/METS]** *Note: the edition prints "the town N", the manuscript's abbreviation for Lynn; quotations that ellipse after "town" silently swallow it.*
- The horse-mill, her second venture: *"this hors wold drawe no drawt in the mylle for nothing the man mygth do ... he wold rather gon bakward than forward."* **[A: Book I ch. 2, Staley/METS]** She reads both failures as punishment for pride. **[A: the Book; Britannica]**
- **Dating of the businesses is a gap.** The Book gives no years. The c. 1400 placement is a scholarly interpolation between the marriage and the devotional turn. **[R: Britannica chronology reconstruction]**
- The heavenly melody, and the beginning of the weeping at services. **[A: the Book]** **Flagged: the gathered pool dated this 1401 and made it the start of her loud crying. Neither holds.** No consulted source fixes a year, and the Book itself separates two events: the melody and weeping begin at Lynn, while the violent roaring begins at Calvary in 1414. The 1401 date is **[R]** and the conflation is removed.
- The refrain of these years, spoken in her own mouth: *"Alas, that evyr I dede synne, it is ful mery in hevyn."* **[A: Book I ch. 3, Staley/METS]** The half-modernized form in circulation ("Alas, that ever I did sin! It is full mery in Heaven") is cited to an EETS page via Wikiquote; the line is now traced to the primary text.

## 2. The turn: 1413

- **The vow of chastity.** Walking from York toward Bridlington, traditionally on Midsummer Eve, Margery bargains with John Kempe: *"Grawntyth me that ye schal not komyn in my bed, and I grawnt yow to qwyte yowr dettys ... makyth my body fre to God ... I schal etyn and drynkyn on the Fryday at yowr byddyng."* **[A: Book I ch. 11, Staley/METS; Wikipedia]** They remain legally married. The precise spot on the road is not fixed by the text. **[A: the Book, by omission]**
- **Julian of Norwich.** Margery goes to the anchoress at her cell in Norwich to have her visions and her tears tested. Julian answers that the tears come of the Holy Ghost and that the test of a revelation is whether it works charity. **[A: Book I ch. 18; Wikipedia]** No journey file for Julian exists in the working directory, so this is a meeting the map cannot yet show from the other side.
- **Philip Repyngdon, Bishop of Lincoln, and Thomas Arundel, Archbishop of Canterbury.** Both audiences belong to 1413, before the Jerusalem journey. **[A: the Book; Wikipedia]** **Contradiction flagged and corrected:** the gathered pool put Arundel and Repyngdon in a 1417 examination alongside Henry Chichele's circle. That is impossible: Arundel died 19 February 1414, and Chichele became archbishop only in 1414. The entry is split into a 1413 pair (Repyngdon, Arundel) and a later, undated Chichele-era scrutiny. The Book does not present the 1413 audiences as heresy trials.

## 3. To Jerusalem (1413 to 1414)

- Sails from Yarmouth, then Zierikzee, Constance, Bologna, Venice. **[A: the Book, Bk I chs. 26-28]**
- At Zierikzee her companions cut her a sackcloth apron to shame her. **[A: Bk I ch. 26]**
- At Constance a papal legate hears the English party's complaints against her and takes her side. **[A: Bk I ch. 27]**
- **Bologna corrected upward from [R] to [A]:** the pool tagged Bologna as a route reconstruction. The Book names it outright; Bologna is where the company agrees to take her back on condition she not speak of the Gospel. **[A: Bk I ch. 27]**
- Thirteen weeks in Venice waiting for a galley, taking communion daily. **[A: Bk I ch. 28]**
- Jaffa, then inland. **[A: Bk I ch. 28]** The stop at Ramla is standard pilgrim itinerary rather than a named stop in the Book; **[R]**, and dropped from the journey file for that reason.
- **Calvary.** At the Church of the Holy Sepulchre she cries out for the first time with the roaring that marks her for life. **[A: Bk I ch. 28]** This, not 1401, is where the crying begins.
- Mount of Olives, held up by two pilgrims; Bethlehem; the Jordan; Mount Zion; the tomb of Lazarus at Bethany, all on the Franciscan-guided circuit. **[A: Bk I chs. 28-29]**
- **Coordinate correction:** the gathered Jordan pin fell about a kilometre east of the river, on the Jordanian bank. The pilgrim baptism site sold on the extended tour, Qasr el Yahud, is near 31.837, 35.535.

## 4. Rome and the way home (1414 to 1415)

- **Assisi corrected upward from [R] to [A].** The pool called Assisi disputed and said the Book does not name the town. It does: Book I ch. 31, where she is present for the Lammas Day indulgence and meets Dame Margaret Florentyne. **[A: Bk I ch. 31]**
- Rome: she lodges at the hospital of St Thomas of Canterbury, is expelled by an English priest offended at her weeping, and is later taken back. **[A: Bk I chs. 31-33]**
- **Date correction:** the Roman stay is 1414-1415, not 1414. She left Rome at Easter 1415. **[A: Wikipedia]**
- Home by way of Middelburg in Zeeland. **[A: Bk I ch. 44]**

## 5. Compostela, and the year of the courts (1417 to 1418)

- Bristol, weeks waiting for a ship, lodging at Henbury with Thomas Peverel, Bishop of Worcester. **[A: Bk I ch. 45; Wikipedia]**
- The voyage: *"And so sche toke hir schip in the name of Jhesu and seylyd forth wyth hir felaschip ... thei comyn to Seynt Jamys on the sevenyth day."* **[A: Bk I ch. 45, Staley/METS]** A fortnight at Compostela, told in almost no detail. **[A: Bk I ch. 45]**
- Hailes Abbey, for the relic of the Holy Blood. **[A: Bk I ch. 46]** **Contradiction flagged, not resolved:** the geography pool dates Hailes 1417, the chronology pool dates it 1418. Both derive from the same reconstruction of a return leg the Book does not date. Recorded as 1417 in the journey file with the disagreement carried in `date_confidence`.
- **Leicester.** Arrested, examined by the mayor, who calls her a false strumpet, a false Lollard and a false deceiver of the people; held roughly three weeks. **[A: Bk I chs. 46-48; Wikipedia]** Her weeping there, on seeing a crucifix: *"the fyer of lofe kyndelyd so yern in hir hert that sche myth not kepyn it prevy ... it cawsyd hir to brekyn owte wyth a lowde voys and cryen merveylowslyche and wepyn and sobbyn ful hedowslyche."* **[A: Bk I ch. 46]** Her answer on the Eucharist before the Abbot of Leicester: *"what man hath takyn the ordyr of presthode ... I beleve that it is hys very flesch and hys blood."* **[A: Bk I ch. 48]**
- **York.** Examined before the Archbishop of York, traditionally Henry Bowet, and acquitted despite a crowd that wanted her burned. **[A: Bk I chs. 51-52; Wikipedia]** From the York chapters: *"for pacyens is more worthy than myraclys werkyng"* **[A: Bk I ch. 51]**; *"I am non heretyke, ne ye schal non preve me."* **[A: Bk I ch. 52]**; and to a weeping-hating cleric, *"Syr, ye schal welyn sum day that ye had wept as sor as I."* **[A: Bk I ch. 52]** All three circulate in modernized spelling cited to Wikiquote page numbers; all three are now traced to the primary text.
- To a priest who confronted her over the white clothes: *"ye schulde kepe the comawndmentys of God and not sweryn so necgligently as ye do."* **[A: Bk I ch. 50]**
- Beverley, examined again amid a crowd calling for burning **[A: Bk I ch. 53]**; then arrested a further time at the Humber ferry near Hessle **[A: Bk I ch. 54]**.

## 6. The undated middle years (c. 1418 to 1431)

This is the largest gap in the life and should be named as one. The Book narrates Canterbury, Walsingham, and the Norfolk and Yorkshire shrines without a usable chronology. **[R: scholarly consensus in Wikipedia and Britannica on the Book's non-linear structure]** The gathered pool's 1420 is a placeholder. One anchor sits at the far end: John Kempe and their eldest son both died in 1431. **[A: Wikipedia; Bk II chs. 1-2]**

The Corpus Christi cry, *"I dey, I dey"*, belongs to this undatable body of material. **[A: Bk I ch. 45, Staley/METS]**

## 7. Prussia, Wilsnack, and the writing down (1431 to 1438)

- At about sixty she sails to Danzig with her widowed daughter-in-law. **[A: Bk II chs. 2-3]**
- Wilsnack, for the bleeding hosts of the Wunderblutkirche, a shrine whose traffic was said to rival Compostela's until it was destroyed in 1552. **[A: Bk II ch. 5]** The gathered pin sat 5.3 km north of the town.
- Aachen, for the showing of the great relics. **[A: Bk II chs. 6-7]** Home by Calais, ill and out of money. **[A: Bk II ch. 7]**
- Syon Abbey at Isleworth. **[A: Bk II ch. 9]** **Date corrected:** the gathered "1431 or later" conflates the abbey's move to Isleworth with the visit; Wikipedia places the visit on the return leg of the 1433-1434 journey, so 1434.
- **The two scribes.** The first, an Englishman who had lived in Germany, probably her son John, produced a draft the Book calls barely legible, and died before finishing. **[A: the Book, Proem; Wikipedia]** Since the son died in 1431, **that draft must predate 1431**; the gathered pool's 1436 for it is wrong.
- The priest, possibly her confessor Robert Springold, **began** the rewriting on 23 July 1436. **[A: the Book's own dating; Wikipedia]** **Corrected: the gathered pool said he completed it on that date.**
- Book Two begun 28 April 1438. **[A: internal dating; Britannica]**
- A "Margery Kempe" is admitted to the Trinity Guild of Lynn in 1438, generally but not certainly the same woman. **[A: Lynn guild records, via Wikipedia and Windeatt's introduction]**

## 8. The silence after 1438

Nothing. No date of death, no place, no burial, no will traced in any source consulted. **[A: Wikipedia, by explicit absence]** The Trinity Guild admission is the last documentary trace. Any chronology offering a death date is offering a guess.

## 9. The manuscript (1934 to the present)

- Identified in 1934 in the library of Lieutenant-Colonel William Erdeswick Ignatius Butler-Bowdon by the American scholar Hope Emily Allen. **[A: Wikipedia, "The Book of Margery Kempe"]**
- **Contradiction resolved, with the resolution shown.** The gathered pool hedged between Derbyshire and Yorkshire. The encyclopedic record has no Yorkshire variant: Wikipedia's article on Allen names Southgate House, Chesterfield, Derbyshire. The hedge is dropped and the pin moved from 53.14, -1.55 (13 km out in the Peak District) to roughly 53.23, -1.43.
- **Mechanism corrected.** The gathered pool had Allen recognizing the book from marginalia matching printed 1521 extracts. The sources describe something plainer: Albert Van de Put of the Victoria and Albert Museum borrowed the manuscript and showed it to Allen, who identified it. De Worde's c. 1501 print and Pepwell's 1521 extracts are real; the identification story attached to them is not.
- **The ping-pong story is flagged, not repeated as fact.** That the manuscript surfaced in a cupboard among sporting equipment is widely retold and confirmed by no primary or scholarly source reachable here. **[R: popular anecdote]**
- Four distinct annotating hands are identified, the most recognizable in red ink. **[A: British Library description via Wikipedia]** **Flagged:** the Mount Grace Priory and Carthusian attribution is **not** in the cited article. It rests on the ownership inscription *Liber Montis Gracie*, documented in the manuscript scholarship (Windeatt; the BL catalogue). **[R]** pending a direct citation.
- W. Butler-Bowdon's own modernized version, with an introduction by R. W. Chambers, Jonathan Cape, 1936, four years before the scholarly text. **[A: Open Library catalogue record; upgraded from the gathered pool's [R]]**
- The first scholarly Middle English edition, EETS Original Series 212, Oxford University Press, 1940, **edited by Sanford Brown Meech with a prefatory note by Hope Emily Allen**. **[A: Wikipedia]** **Corrected:** the gathered pool made Allen a co-editor. A planned second volume was never completed.
- Sold at Sotheby's, London, June 1980, by Captain Maurice E. Butler-Bowdon; bought by the British Library; now Additional MS 61823. **[A: Wikipedia]**
- Modern translations: Windeatt, Penguin, 1985; Staley, Norton Critical Edition, 2001; Bale, Oxford World's Classics, 2015. **[A: Wikipedia]**
- Memorials, all **[A: Wikipedia]**: a bench in the Saturday Market Place, King's Lynn, 2018, by Toby Winteringham, unveiled by Mayor Nick Daubney; a statue at a medieval bridge in Oroso, Galicia, 2020; the sculpture "A Woman in Motion" at King's Lynn Minster, 2023. The Margery Kempe Society was founded in 2018.

## 11. Interlocks

Only two are relations rather than coincidences of map and calendar, and only those two are named inside the campa:

- **Saint Francis of Assisi** (`saint_francis.journey.json`). Margery is at Assisi for the Lammas Day indulgence, in the church built over him, and the Jerusalem circuit she walks is Franciscan-guided from beginning to end. A devotional relation, not a shared pin.
- **Mary Magdalene** (`mary_magdalene.journey.json`). The Magdalene is the pattern Kempe's tears are measured against by her defenders and by herself: weeping as a sign of grace rather than of madness. A cited model.

Real but **not** named in campa, being co-location or period parallel only: Richard the Lionheart (the 1192 Jaffa treaty opening Jerusalem to unarmed pilgrims), al-Kamil, Jacques de Molay, Joan of Arc, Yolande of Aragon, Egeria, Rabban Bar Sauma, Dante, William the Conqueror. The map already shows co-location; prose need not.

## 12. Honest gaps

1. **Death.** Unknown date, place, and grave.
2. **The children.** Fourteen is a reconstructed total. Only one child, the son who was her first scribe, is individuated at all.
3. **The businesses.** No years in the Book; the c. 1400 dating is editorial.
4. **The 1420s.** Undatable; a decade of shrines with no calendar.
5. **John Kempe's office in 1394.** Attested that he held one; the office itself is not attested in any reachable source.
6. **Santiago.** Sailing dates not preserved; only "the sevenyth day" of the crossing and a fortnight there.
7. **The ping-pong story.** Unverified.
8. **Mount Grace.** The Carthusian provenance is real scholarship, unreachable in the cited source.
