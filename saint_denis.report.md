# Saint Denis of Paris (Dionysius, Denys): research report

*2026-08-08. Subject: the first bishop of the Parisii, beheaded on Montmartre, patron of France, one of the Fourteen Holy Helpers, and the accidental namesake of a Greek mystical theologian who never set foot in Gaul.*

**Evidence legend.** **[A]** = attested, with the source named. **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition, or hagiographic elaboration. Contradictions between sources are flagged and left open. Where a source could not be reached, it is named with the reason.

**The shape of the problem.** Almost everything a reader thinks they know about Denis comes from two texts written long after him: an anonymous *Passio* of about 600 and Abbot Hilduin's Carolingian *Passio* of the 830s. The only early witness is Gregory of Tours, and Gregory gives four things: a date, a mission, a see, and a sword. The companions, the prefect, the tortures, the walking head, the widow, and the Athenian philosophy all arrive afterward and grow in the telling. This report keeps the growth visible rather than pruning it, because the journey file is written in the register where the canon is true.

---

## 1. Name, birth, origin

- Denis is **born in the third century in Italia [R]** (Wikipedia, "Saint Denis of Paris", following the *Passio* tradition). **No source in the pool gives a birth year, and none gives a birthplace more precise than Italia.** The commonly circulated figure of 200 has no textual basis and is recorded here as an **editorial inference to be dropped**, not a fact. Honest gap.
- He is **sent from Italia to convert Gaul [R]** (*Passio*, via Wikipedia). French Wikipedia, "Denis de Paris", puts the sending "vers 250".

## 2. The sending (c. 250)

- **Gregory of Tours, *Historia Francorum* I.30 [A]** (Brehaut translation, Fordham Medieval Sourcebook) dates the mission to **"the consulship of Decius and Gratus", that is AD 250**, and lists **seven bishops ordained and sent to seven separate cities**: Catianus to Tours, Trophimus to Arles, Paul to Narbonne, Saturninus to Toulouse, **Dionysius to Paris**, Stremonius to Clermont, Martial to Limoges.
- **Gregory names no sender and never mentions Rome. [A, by absence]** The attribution of the commission to **Pope Fabian** is a later Gallican gloss preserved in the Catholic Encyclopedia, "St. Denis" **[R]**, and it is **chronologically impossible as usually framed**: Fabian's dates (236 to 250) are correct, but he died on 20 January 250 as the first victim of the Decian persecution, so he cannot have sent missionaries to repair Gaul *after* that persecution. Corrected here to c. 250 and reworded as "at the outset of".
- **Frequently repeated error, flagged:** that Denis travelled with **six companions**. French Wikipedia's "avec six autres évêques" means six *other bishops, each with his own see*. They are Gregory's seven minus Denis, not a travelling party. **Rusticus and Eleutherius belong to a separate and later strand and are not among Gregory's seven. [A, from Gregory's own list]**
- **The names Rusticus and Eleutherius are judged non-historical by modern scholarship [A]** (Wikipedia, "Saint Denis of Paris", citing modern scholarly assessment).

## 3. The road through Gaul (all [R])

None of this is in Gregory. The itinerary is Carolingian and later.

- **Embarkation at Ostia [R]:** general hagiographic reconstruction. **No ancient text names a port.** Gap, stated as a gap.
- **Landing at Massilia [R]:** Provençal local tradition, absent from Gregory, possibly borrowed from the Lazarus and Trophimus landing legends of the same coast.
- **Passage through Arles [R]:** Hilduin's elaborated itinerary; no corroboration outside the ninth-century *Passio*.
- **Orléans [R]:** local ecclesiastical tradition; may conflate Denis with other Loire valley missionary saints. Not carried into the journey file.
- **Sanctinus at Meaux [R]:** local hagiography naming a companion of Denis as first bishop there. Standard padding in later cults seeking apostolic pedigree.
- **Miracles at Trier [R]:** Hilduin again, extending the saint's geography into Germania five hundred years after the events described. An open inflation, recorded as one.

## 4. Bishop of the Parisii

- **Denis is bishop of Paris. [A]** This is Gregory's core claim and the load-bearing historical statement of the whole dossier.
- **He settles on the island in the Seine and builds a church there [R]** (Catholic Encyclopedia). A date of 246 circulates for this; it is **unsupported and predates the mission itself** under Gregory's chronology. Corrected to c. 250 or later.
- **Conversions provoke the pagan priests, who press the governor Fescenninus Sisinnius against the missionaries [R]** (Catholic Encyclopedia). **The prefect's name appears only from Hilduin onward; earlier passiones do not name him** (Wikipedia, French Wikipedia). The name may be invented.
- **Tortures before execution [R/A]:** Gregory says only *diversis pro Christi nomine adfectus poenis*, various pains suffered for Christ's name. The particulars (gridiron, furnace, cross, the lion in the amphitheatre, Christ in the cell with the Eucharist) come from Hilduin and the *Legenda Aurea* **[R]** and are stock motifs of the genre.

## 5. The martyrdom

- **Manner: beheading by sword. [A]** Gregory of Tours: *"Beatus Dionysius Parisiorum episcopus diversis pro Christi nomine adfectus poenis praesentem vitam gladio immente finivit."* In Brehaut's English: "the blessed Dionisius, bishop of Paris, after suffering divers pains in Christ's name, ended the present life by the threatening sword."
- **Date: not fixed. Sources disagree and this report does not adjudicate.** Candidates in circulation: **c. 250** (Decius, favoured by modern scholarship assuming Denis's historicity, Wikipedia), **258** (Valerian), **270 to 275** (Catholic Encyclopedia and others). The *Golden Legend* is wilder still and places the martyrdom under **Domitian, c. 96**, a consequence of the Areopagite conflation. **Four incompatible datings, left open.**
- **Place: the hill north of Lutetia, Mons Martis or Mons Martyrum, later Montmartre [R]** (Wikipedia, French Wikipedia).
- **Contradiction flagged:** a **variant local tradition drowns Rusticus and Eleutherius in the Seine** rather than beheading them beside Denis. Sources disagree on whether the three died together.
- **Second contradiction flagged:** **two Montmartre sites both claim the exact spot**, the Chapelle du Martyre on rue Yvonne-Le-Tac and the crypt of the church of Saint-Pierre de Montmartre. Local tradition supports both. Not resolved.

## 6. The cephalophore

- **After beheading, the body rises, takes up the head, and walks north preaching repentance, about six kilometres, to the place of burial. [R]** (*Legenda Aurea*; Butler's *Lives*; French Wikipedia.) A later French strand adds an angel as guide.
- **Unit error corrected:** the figure is **six kilometres**, not six Roman miles, which would be about 8.9 km. Caxton's *Golden Legend* says "two leagues".
- **Gregory of Tours, three centuries closer to the event, has no head-carrying whatever.** The motif appears in full only with Hilduin. This is the single clearest measure of how the legend grew.
- **A pious woman named Catulla recovers the body and buries it at Catulliacus [R]** (Catholic Encyclopedia). Gregory attests only that a basilica dedicated to Denis existed near Paris in his own century, and does not credit the widow-burial story. The place-name Catulliacus becomes the town of Saint-Denis **[R]**.

## 7. Shrine, abbey, kings

- **A shrine near the present basilica exists by the fifth century, attested indirectly through the *Vita Sanctae Genovefae* (composed shortly after 502). [A]** Genevieve of Paris has a basilica built or expanded over the tomb, c. 475.
- **Dagobert I founds the royal abbey over the tomb. [A]** *Gesta Dagoberti I Regis Francorum* (ninth century), with surviving charters. **Date contradiction, flagged and harmonized:** the dossier carried **three different years for one event** across its lenses (630, a 628 to 636 window, and 636). Wikipedia, "Basilica of Saint-Denis", gives **632**, which is adopted in the journey file with the disagreement recorded in `date_confidence`. **Dagobert's reign dates were also wrong in the working pool** and are corrected from Wikipedia, "Dagobert I": Austrasia 623 to 634, Neustria and Burgundy 629 to 639, king of all the Franks 632 to 639, died 19 January 639.
- **Fulrad becomes abbot c. 749/750 and builds a new church. [A]** (Wikipedia.)
- **Pope Stephen II anoints Pepin the Short and his sons at Saint-Denis, 754. [A]** (Inherited from `charlemagne.journey.json`.) Stephen also carries the veneration of Denis back to Rome **[A]**.
- **Pepin is buried face down at the threshold of the church, 768. [A]** (`charlemagne.journey.json`.)
- **Charlemagne attends a rededication of the rebuilt abbey church, 775. [A]** (Carolingian annals.)
- **Suger rebuilds from 1135; the new choir is dedicated 11 June 1144 in the king's presence, with the relics installed beneath the new high altar. [A]** (Wikipedia; Suger, *De Administratione* and *De Consecratione*.) **Corrected:** the pool gave 1140 for the translation; 1144 is the dedication year and is used.
- **The abbey is the necropolis of the kings of France from the Merovingians to the Bourbons. [A]**

## 8. The Areopagite graft

This is the largest single distortion in the record, and it is a distortion with an author and a date.

- **Acts 17:34 [A]** names Dionysius the Areopagite among Paul's Athenian converts.
- **Eusebius, *Historia Ecclesiastica* IV.23.3 [A]**, quoting Dionysius of Corinth, reports that the Areopagite **became first bishop of Athens**. **Correction to the working pool: Eusebius nowhere says the Areopagite was martyred.** The martyred Athenian bishop in that same passage is **Publius** (IV.23.2), succeeded by Quadratus. The claim "and was martyred there" is struck.
- **The confusion of the two Dionysii begins in the ninth century, not the eighth. [A]** (Wikipedia.) The commonly quoted year 775 has no source and is dropped.
- **Hilduin, abbot of Saint-Denis, formalizes the identification. [A]** Louis the Pious commissions the biography in **835** (Wikipedia, "Hilduin of Saint-Denis"). **Two distinct works are routinely conflated and are separated here:** the *Rescriptum* or *Areopagitica*, a report to the emperor, c. 835 to 836; and the *Passio sanctissimi Dionysii*, a separate hagiography of 835 to 840.
- **Three figures are fused into one saint [A]:** the third-century Gallic martyr, the first-century Athenian convert, and the anonymous late-fifth or early-sixth-century mystical theologian now called **Pseudo-Dionysius**.
- **The channel of the confusion is a manuscript. [A]** Byzantine emperor Michael II gives a Greek codex of the Dionysian corpus to Louis the Pious in **827**; Louis donates it to Saint-Denis. Hilduin's own Latin version (c. 838) is judged "almost unintelligible"; **John Scotus Eriugena** produces a cleaner translation in **862** for Charles the Bald, criticized for excessive literalness. Later: **John Sarrazin** (commentary 1140, translation 1165), **Robert Grosseteste** (c. 1240 to 1243), **Albertus Magnus**, and a thirteenth-century Paris compilation merging the Old and New Translations with the glosses of Maximus the Confessor, John of Scythopolis, and Thomas Gallus. **[A, Wikipedia, "Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite"]**
- **Pierre Abelard challenges the identification publicly while a monk at Saint-Denis, c. 1121, and is met with fury. [A]** (Wikipedia.)
- **Correction to the working pool:** the statement that the identification was "not definitively rejected by scholarship until the mid-20th century" is **too late**. The Dionysian corpus was shown to postdate the Areopagite independently by **Koch and Stiglmayr in 1895**.
- **The 1969 revision of the General Roman Calendar under Paul VI formally separates the two. [R/A]** An official retraction of a ninth-century error that stood for roughly a millennium.

## 9. Cult, feast, Fourteen Holy Helpers

- **Feast of 9 October, shared with Rusticus and Eleutherius. [A]**
- **The feast is celebrated liturgically by around 800. [A]**
- **Pius V's Tridentine Calendar of 1568 carries it. [A]** **Wording corrected:** he **codifies** a long-standing observance and does not **add** or introduce it, since the dossier's own chronology has the feast kept since c. 800.
- **Denis is counted among the Fourteen Holy Helpers, invoked against headache. [R]** The closed Franconian list of about 1500 is attested through `catorce_santos_ayudantes.journey.json`.
- **Relic claim at Regensburg [R/A]:** St Emmeram's Abbey documents a Saint Denis altar and a relief of about 1052, consistent with the eleventh-century Regensburg claim to hold Denis's body. **The "ninth-century translation" in circulation is unsupported** and is recorded as a gap.
- **Iconographic contest [R]:** rival relic claims between Saint-Denis and Notre-Dame of Paris shape how he is drawn, whether holding an entire severed head, a skull, or a head still crowned, depending on which house commissioned the image.
- **Patron of Paris and of France. [R]** (Catholic Encyclopedia.)

## 10. Revolution and after

- **1790: the Montmartre abbey is demolished. [A]** **Correction:** the pool gave 1794, which is the year the last abbess was guillotined; Wikipedia, "Montmartre", dates the demolition to **1790**.
- **August and October 1793: revolutionary authorities exhume the kings and queens at Saint-Denis and pit the bones. [A]**
- **1817: Louis XVIII gathers the surviving royal remains into a common ossuary in the crypt. [A]** **1819: the relics of Denis, sheltered in a Paris parish church, return to the abbey. [A]**
- **Nineteenth-century restorations by François Debret and then Eugène Viollet-le-Duc. [A]**
- **1855: an oratory is built at 11 rue Yvonne-Le-Tac; renovated 1994; today the Crypte du martyrium de saint Denis. [A]**
- **1876 to 1919: Sacré-Coeur rises at the summit of Montmartre, near but not because of the martyrdom site. [A]**
- **Sumner McKnight Crosby's photogrammetric survey and the posthumous 1987 monograph *The Royal Abbey of Saint-Denis*. [A]** **Gap:** the reachable source does not say whether his fieldwork located or verified an original grave. Stated as a gap.

## Contradictions carried, not adjudicated

1. **Martyrdom year:** c. 250 / 258 / 270 to 275 / (Golden Legend) c. 96 under Domitian.
2. **Who sent him:** Gregory names nobody; the Gallican tradition names Pope Fabian, who was dead by 20 January 250.
3. **Companions:** seven bishops to seven cities (Gregory) versus six companions travelling with Denis (a misreading of French Wikipedia) versus Rusticus and Eleutherius (a later *Passio* strand, judged non-historical).
4. **Manner of the companions' death:** beheaded with Denis, or drowned in the Seine.
5. **The exact spot on Montmartre:** Chapelle du Martyre versus the crypt of Saint-Pierre de Montmartre.
6. **Dagobert's foundation year:** 630, 628 to 636, 636, or 632. The journey file uses 632 and records the disagreement.
7. **One person or three:** Gregory's Gallic bishop, Eusebius's Athenian bishop, and the anonymous author of the Corpus Areopagiticum. Hilduin says one. Everyone since Abelard says otherwise.

## Honest gaps

- **No birth year and no birthplace beyond "Italia".**
- **No port of embarkation** in any ancient text.
- **No fixed martyrdom year.** Four candidate datings, none decisive.
- **Nothing between the mission and the martyrdom** except the *Passio* material, legendary by its own editors' admission.
- **The Regensburg relic translation is undated**; the ninth century is asserted in circulation and unsupported by the reachable article.
- **No archaeological confirmation of an original grave.** Crosby measured the church, not the tomb, so far as the reachable source says.
- **The composition place of the c. 600 *Passio* is unknown**; "Gaul" is as precise as the sources get, and the journey file pins it at the abbey with that hedge stated.
