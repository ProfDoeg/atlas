# Robert Finley (1772-1817): research report
*Basking Ridge pastor, schoolmaster, prime mover of the American Colonization Society, president of the University of Georgia for five months. Prepared 2026-08-10 for the Atlas of Journeys.*

**Method.** English Wikipedia ("Robert Finley," "American Colonization Society") was used as an index of claims only, then checked line by line against the near-contemporary primary biography: Isaac V. Brown, *Memoirs of the Rev. Robert Finley, D.D.* (New-Brunswick: Terhune & Letson, 1819), retrieved in full from the Internet Archive and read this session. Brown prints the documents themselves: letters, presbytery minutes, the Georgia trustees' resolutions, the pamphlet, the obituary.

**Legend.** **[A]** attested, source named. **[R]** reconstruction or tradition. Contradictions are flagged; inaccessible sources are named with the reason.

---

## 1. Princeton, the weaver's son (1769-1787)

**The parents.** James Finley: born Glasgow 1737, yarn merchant and then inspector of the yarn factories of Glasgow, emigrated 1769 at John Witherspoon's invitation, worked at Princeton as a **weaver**, held lay office as a **ruling elder**. **[A: Brown pp. 9-11.]** Wikipedia calls him "Rev. James Finley," a minister, and hedges his origin as "Scotland/Ireland." Both are wrong on Brown's evidence: never ordained, and Scotland only. The mother's maiden name is **Angres** (p. 11); Wikipedia's "Angrest" and "Anderson" are artifacts of the same word. **[A]**

**Birth.** "Robert Finley was born at Princeton, New-Jersey, A. D. 1772." **[A: Brown p. 14.]** **Gap: day and month unrecovered;** Geni and WikiTree returned no usable content. The journey defaults to 1772-01-01 with the hedge in `date_confidence`.

**College and Charleston.** Enters the College of New Jersey at eleven (1783), graduates at fifteen (1787). **[A: Wikipedia, consistent with Brown.]** The five years before he returned to Princeton were spent teaching, including a year in **Charleston, South Carolina**: "In the early part of my life I spent a year at Charleston, South Carolina, and brought away with me a partiality for the South" (21 February 1817). **[A: Brown p. 113.]** Wikipedia omits this; the attachment to the slaveholding South predates the plan by twenty-five years.

## 2. The pulpit and the school (1792-1809)

**Return and theology.** In "the Autumn of 1792" he left Charleston, resumed the Princeton grammar school, and began theology under Witherspoon. **[A: Brown p. 24.]** Wikipedia dates the return to 1793. **Flagged; Brown's date is documented.** The tutorship followed "very soon," on the joint recommendation of Witherspoon and Samuel Stanhope Smith.

**Licensure.** Licensed by the Presbytery of New Brunswick, **16 September 1794**. **[A: Brown p. 28.]** **Gap: the meeting place is not named,** presbyteries then meeting by rotation in member congregations. The journey pins the town and marks the venue unattested; an earlier draft placing it at First Presbyterian Church, New Brunswick, was guessing.

**Ordination and tenure.** Presbytery met at Basking Ridge 16 June 1795 and "on the following day" ordained and installed him: **17 June 1795**; the relation was dissolved at Allentown **22 April 1817**, the minute quoted verbatim. **[A: Brown pp. 29-30, 117.]** That is **twenty-two years**. Wikipedia's "approximately 19 years" is an arithmetic error that propagates through the secondary literature. **Corrected wherever it appears.**

**Marriage.** "In May 1798, Mr. Finley was united in marriage to Miss Esther Caldwell, a daughter of the Rev. James Caldwell." **[A: Brown p. 31.]** Three years **after** the installation; a tradition dating it c. 1794-1795 is wrong by three or four years. **Gap: Brown names no venue,** and the tradition of the Caldwells' home church needs care, James Caldwell having died in 1781 and his Elizabethtown church having been burned in January 1780. The journey keeps the Elizabethtown pin for the family connection and marks the venue unattested. Esther's brother was **Elias Boudinot Caldwell**, Clerk of the Supreme Court: Finley's brother-in-law and his Washington host in December 1816. **[A: Brown pp. 96-97.]** Wikipedia's ACS article calls Mercer's contact "John Caldwell." **Resolved against Wikipedia:** Elias B. Caldwell is documented; "John" is an error.

**The school.** "Soon after Mr. Finley's settlement at Basking Ridge, he was induced to undertake the instruction of a small number of boys... Thus was laid the foundation of a school, which became permanent." **[A: Brown p. 31.]** A founding by accretion, not the "re-establishment" Wikipedia implies. Brown says it ran "during twenty years" and that no classical seminary on the continent surpassed it (p. 32); students came from Virginia and New York City. The two-storey **Brick Academy** was raised in **1809**. **[A: NRHP record, crediting Finley with the building and recording enrolment growing from under a dozen to an average of twenty-five, sometimes forty; coordinate 40.70667, -74.55000.]**

**Trustee.** Elected to the Board of Trustees of the College of New Jersey **1806**, held "till his removal from New-Jersey." **[A: Brown p. 54.]** Credited also with an early Sunday School curriculum. **[R: Wikipedia; untraced.]**

**Enslavement.** The New Jersey Slavery Records project (records.njslavery.org), citing Somerset County Clerk's Office documents, records that Finley enslaved people while living in Bernards Township. **[R: cited secondhand; record images not retrieved, no date of finding established.]** **Carried into the journey as a gap,** because it stands directly against the pamphlet's moral self-description.

## 3. The plan (February to November 1816)

The scheme is on paper almost two years before the founding meeting, in a private letter: Finley to John P. Mumford, Basking Ridge, **14 February 1816** **[A: Brown p. 17]**:

> Could not the rich and benevolent devise means to form a colony on some part of Africa, similar to the one at Sierra Leone... Our fathers brought them here, and we are bound if possible to repair the injuries inflicted by our fathers.

> Could they be sent back to Africa, a three-fold benefit would arise. We should be cleared of them : - we should send to Africa a population partially civilized and christianized for its benefit : - our blacks themselves would be put in a better situation.

The same letter floats an alternative, a district "on the shores of the Pacific Ocean." All these passages sit on p. 17; a citation giving pp. 17-18 is wrong, since p. 18 opens with Brown's narrative. The named model is the British settlement at Freetown (1787). **[A for the naming; R for any inference about what he knew of its conduct.]**

**Mercer, and who moved whom.** Charles Fenton Mercer, having uncovered the Virginia removal debates that followed Gabriel Prosser's 1800 rebellion, began advocating colonization in early 1816. **[A: Wikipedia, "American Colonization Society."]** Wikipedia has Mercer's circle then putting Finley in touch with colonization, after which he endorses the plan and becomes its organizer. **Brown's eyewitnesses reverse the arrow.** A Washington participant, writing 10 November 1818, records that Finley arrived about 1 December 1816 with the plan already formed and "opened to me his views on the subject" (pp. 80-81); a second is blunter, "he was the sole mover and promoter of it; nor did he leave the District until it was organized" (p. 82). **[A]** **Adjudicated toward the eyewitnesses,** who were in the room.

**The pamphlet.** *Thoughts on the Colonization of Free Blacks*: written in New Jersey before departure, **published in Washington in December 1816** as campaign material. A District correspondent tells Brown that Finley "reviewed a piece, 'Thoughts, &c.' which he had written previous to his coming here, which we published"; Brown reprints it and calls it "probably the first [treatise] on this great subject ever published in the United States." **[A: Brown pp. 80-83.]** This upgrades an earlier **[R]** and corrects the place of publication.

Verbatim, as reprinted: "What shall we do with the free people of colour? What can we do for their happiness consistently with our own?" (p. 83); the aim, "the gradual separation of the black from the white population" (p. 83); "Is it a practicable thing to form a colony of free blacks in our own wild lands, or on the coast of Africa?" (p. 83, **not** pp. 83-84); and on p. 85 (**not** pp. 85-86), "expense must be incurred, in fair and honourable purchase from the natives." **Four page citations corrected**, and one em-dash previously mistranscribed as a semicolon.

## 4. December 1816, Washington

**Arrival** about **1 December 1816**; he called personally on members of Congress, the heads of departments, and President Madison. **[A: Brown pp. 80-82.]** A companion recalls him saying, as they walked, "I know this scheme is from God!" **[A as reported speech: Brown p. 82. Not his own hand.]**

**21 December, Davis's Hotel.** The meeting appointed a committee "to prepare a constitution and rules for the government of the society, to be organized at a subsequent meeting." **[A: Brown pp. 98-99.]** Clay chaired; Elias B. Caldwell and John Randolph of Roanoke spoke. It **did not** constitute the society or elect Bushrod Washington president, though Wikipedia dates the presidency to it. **Adjudicated toward the minute.**

**28 December, the founding proper.** The adjourned meeting saw "the expected constitution reported and unanimously adopted, the 'American society for colonizing the free people of colour,' completely organized, and officers appointed." **[A: Brown pp. 98-99.]** **Finley was elected a vice-president** and closed the meeting in prayer (p. 99). **Gap: Brown does not name the venue.** An earlier draft placed it in the Capitol's House chamber; that belongs to the anniversary meetings, the first on 1 January 1818 (p. 104).

**Backers.** Bushrod Washington (president from 28 December), Clay, Randolph, Richard Bland Lee, Francis Scott Key, Mercer; Jefferson, Madison and Monroe lent support. **[A: Wikipedia, corroborated in part by Brown; the presidents not checked against their papers.]**

**Samuel John Mills.** Wikipedia has Finley co-founding the Society with Mills. **Contradicted by the eyewitness record.** Mills's service came after Finley's death: he sailed from Philadelphia in November 1817 as the Society's agent to find land on the African coast and died at sea in May 1818. The December 1816 co-organizers were Caldwell and Mercer. **[A: Brown pp. 81-82.]**

## 5. The road home, and the road south (January to May 1817)

**Philadelphia, January 1817.** The most consequential correction in the set. An earlier reconstruction had Finley recruiting funds among northern clergy and merchants; Brown records the opposite errand: he "was grieved to find the people of colour in that city filled with painful alarms and apprehensions, respecting the nature and object of the colonization society." **[A: Brown pp. 99-100.]** He went to allay an alarm his own society had caused; Black Philadelphians organized against the ACS in exactly this window. The fundraising story is discarded.

**New Jersey, 1817.** He petitioned the legislature and helped constitute an auxiliary society at Trenton with its own constitution and officers. **[A: Brown pp. 99-104.]** Wikipedia's national organizing "through 1817" overstates a short, local effort.

**The Georgia appointment.** Appointed unanimously at Athens **21 December 1816** to the presidency of the University of Georgia, in the place of the Rev. John Brown, resigned, at $1500 per annum. Governor Peter Early's covering letter was dated 15 January 1817, postmarked 29 January, and reached Basking Ridge 20 February; the acceptance is datelined "Basking Ridge, Feb. 21, 1817." **[A: Brown pp. 109-113.]** **Wikipedia's "July 1817 accepts the presidency" is wrong by five months and inverts the sequence: by July he had been in Athens two months.** He was elected to the Georgia chair on the same day the Davis's Hotel meeting appointed the constitution committee, and did not learn of it for two months.

**Nomenclature, release, route.** Chartered as the University of Georgia in 1785, with Franklin College the name of its collegiate body; both appear in the 1817 documents, so "later renamed" is inaccurate. **[A]** The pastoral relation was dissolved at Allentown **22 April 1817**. **[A: Brown p. 117.]** No itinerary south survives; the journey carries one reconstruction stop on the Virginia post road. **[R]**

## 6. Athens, May to October 1817

**Arrival, May 1817.** "When therefore, Dr. Finley arrived at Athens in May, 1817, he found upon a thorough investigation of the affairs of the institution, that its funds were good only in prospect." **[A: Brown p. 137.]** The state bank holding the endowment had opened in summer 1816 on seventeen per cent of its capital: a financial ruin, not the half-built campus of an earlier atmospheric draft.

**The good summer, then the illness.** His Athens letters of 12 and 24 June, 1 and 16 July 1817 are energetic, and a Georgia observer wrote that "the success which attended the few months of his presidency, gave us the most animating hope." From one postscript (p. 132): "With men of reflection the colonizing scheme is as popular here, as with you in Jersey." He fell ill only after an August tour, returning home 8 September; the obituary specifies "a painful illness of eighteen days," placing onset about 15 September. **[A: Brown pp. 132-137, 153.]** "Within weeks of arriving" is wrong by three months.

**Death: 3 October 1817.** The contemporary Georgia obituary (Brown p. 153): "Died on Friday Morning, the 3d inst. ... after a painful illness of eighteen days." **Only 3 October fell on a Friday in 1817; 3 November was a Monday.** A letter datelined Washington, Georgia, 14 October already treats the death as a fortnight old. Wikipedia gives 3 November. **Adjudicated: Wikipedia is the outlier and is wrong.** The journey carries 1817-10-03 with the disagreement recorded in `date_confidence`.

Tenure: about five months, the shortest completed presidency in the institution's history, standing alone between John Brown (1811-1816) and Moses Waddel (1819-1829). **[A: UGA presidential list.]**

**Burial.** Jackson Street Cemetery: the town's working burying ground c. 1810-1856, which the university later grew around. **[A: notable-interments record.]** Authoritative coordinate 33.95583, -83.37361; three different coordinates circulated in the working pool (33.9538/-83.3762, 33.9548/-83.3733, and the correct pair) and are reconciled in the journey file. **Gap: the marker.** Find a Grave memorial 7717 returned **HTTP 403 Forbidden**; no inscription or photograph verified.

**The trustees' resolution, 12 December 1817.** The board appropriated **$200** for "a suitable tomb-stone, commemorative of his worth and services," resolved to educate **all** of Finley's sons at the college's expense, having been asked only for the eldest, and gave Mrs Finley her choice of two Athens town lots. **[A: Brown pp. 166-167.]**

## 7. Afterlife

**The biography.** Brown's *Memoirs* (New-Brunswick: Terhune & Letson, 1819; printer W. Myer): title plural. **[A: read in full, Internet Archive memoirsofrevrobert00brow.]** His expanded 1857 *Biography of the Rev. Robert Finley, D.D.* (archive.org biographyofrevro00brow) was located, not read line by line. Reprint editions and HathiTrust digitizations exist. **[A: catalogue listings.]**

**The colony.** The Elizabeth sailed from New York **6 February 1820** with 86 emigrants, the Society's first voyage: two years four months after the death, so "three years" is wrong. Cape Mesurado was acquired from King Peter in **December 1821** by Robert Stockton and Eli Ayres, under coercion; settlers moved onto the cape **7 January 1822**; Monrovia was named later that year, so "named Liberia in 1822" conflates separate acts. Emigration ran from January 1822 through the Civil War, moving more than 15,000 free and freed Black Americans; of about **4,571** arrivals between 1820 and 1843, about **1,819** were recorded alive in 1843. **[A: Wikipedia, "American Colonization Society" and "Robert Finley"; standard Liberia chronology.]**

**The brother, the papers.** Josiah Finley is said to have governed Mississippi-in-Africa, 1837-1838. **[R: Wikipedia; unconfirmed, and the fraternal relation unchecked against Brown.]** The Presbyterian Historical Society, 425 Lombard Street, Philadelphia (39.9430, -75.1501), is cited as holding Finley sermons and a Latin manuscript. **Finding aid unreachable: history.pcusa.org/collection/robert-finley-papers 301-redirects to a path returning 404.** **[A for the institution; R for the contents.]**

**Monuments. Gap.** No marker, statue, or named place in New Jersey, Georgia, or Liberia commemorating Finley specifically was located: a failure to find, not a documented absence. **[R]** The Washington eyewitness had asked on 10 November 1818 that "some town or district in the colony be not called Finley." **[A: Brown pp. 82-83.]** No such toponym exists.
