# James Lewis Thomas Chalmers Spence (1874-1955), research report

*Slug: `james_lewis_thomas_chalmers_spence`. Journalist, mythographer, occult encyclopaedist,
Scots-language poet, and the first Scottish Nationalist ever to contest a United Kingdom
parliamentary seat. Published as **Lewis Spence**.*

Legend: **[A]** = attested, source named · **[R]** = reconstruction, inference, or a claim resting
on a single contested source. Contradictions below are **flagged, not adjudicated**.

---

## 1. Birth and origins (1874), the place is contested three ways

**Date [A].** Born 25 November 1874. English Wikipedia's infobox gives `birth date|1874|11|25` and
no source consulted disagrees.

**Place [R], an unresolved three-way disagreement.** The pool arrived tagging the birthplace [A];
that tag is not defensible and has been downgraded:

| source | birthplace given |
|---|---|
| English Wikipedia, "Lewis Spence" | Monifieth, Angus |
| Wikidata Q325589 (P19) | Forres, Moray |
| German Wikipedia | "Forfarshire" only (the old county name for Angus) |
| popular / occult reference literature | Broughty Ferry |

Monifieth and Broughty Ferry are four kilometres apart on the same coast, so those two are
reconcilable as birth-versus-boyhood. **Forres is not**: Moray, 150 km north across the
Grampians, and nothing explains how it entered Wikidata. **No parish register has been
consulted.** The file pins the birth at Monifieth town centre (56.4796, -2.8194) with the whole
disagreement in `date_confidence`, and carries a separate Broughty Ferry stop (56.4676, -2.8700,
corrected from the pool's 56.465/-2.8778) flagged [R] as the popular tradition, not reconciled.

**Family.** The four forenames are attested; nothing about his parents is in any source
consulted. **A gap, left as one.**

## 3. The newspaper years (1899-1909)

**[A]** Married Helen Bruce, 1899 (Wikipedia). **Venue, church, witnesses and her family are
absent from every source consulted.** A gap.

**[A]** Sub-editor then editor at *The Scotsman*, 1899-1906 (Wikipedia). The Cockburn Street
office is attested for the paper, not separately for his desk (Wikipedia, "The Scotsman", for the
Peddie & Kinnear building).

**[A]** *The Scotsman* moved on **19 December 1904** to its purpose-built North Bridge premises,
designed by Dunn & Findlay after three years' construction (Wikipedia, "The Scotsman"), Spence
being on staff for the move. **Coordinate corrected** from the pool's 55.9518/-3.1885 (about
100 m north, past the Waverley Bridge corner) to **55.9509, -3.1877**, 20 North Bridge.

**[R]** Editor of *The Edinburgh Magazine*, 1904-05 (Wikipedia). One line, no address, no
contents, no circulation. Tagged [R] for want of corroboration.

**[A]** Editor of *The British Weekly*, 1906-09 (Wikipedia), the nonconformist paper founded by
William Robertson Nicoll and published by Hodder & Stoughton. **[R] as to address**: the London
office is nowhere confirmed, and Paternoster Row (51.5142, -0.0977), the publisher's historic
house, is used as an approximate pin and flagged as such.

**[R]** That *The Popol Vuh* (1908) was written in London is an **inference** from the posting.
**[A/R]** The 1909 return to Edinburgh: career dates attested, the return itself inferred from
every subsequent activity being Edinburgh-based. Both flagged in the file.

## 4. The mythologies and the reference works (1908-1922)

All [A] from Wikipedia's bibliography unless noted:

- *The Popol Vuh* (1908)
- *A Dictionary of Mythology* (1910)
- *The Myths of Mexico and Peru* (1913)
- *The Myths of the North American Indians* (1914)
- *Myths and Legends of Babylonia and Assyria* (1916), also Wikisource author page
- *An Encyclopaedia of Occultism* (1920)
- *An Introduction to Mythology* (1921)

**Title correction, 1920.** The pool called the 1920 book *An Encyclopaedia of Occultism and
Parapsychology*. **That is a conflation.** Spence's 1920 book is *An Encyclopædia of Occultism*
(George Routledge & Sons); *Encyclopedia of Occultism and Parapsychology* is Leslie Shepard's
later, different multi-volume expansion built on Spence and Nandor Fodor, indexed separately by
Open Library (2003/2010/2014 imprints). The date is right; "and Parapsychology" is removed.

**Offices, both undated.** **[A]** vice-president of the Scottish Anthropological and Folklore
Society (Wikipedia); **[A]** Fellow of the Royal Anthropological Institute (Wikipedia, German
Wikipedia). **No source gives a year for either.** The pool's notes on both ended "hence R" while
the tag field still read "A"; resolved in favour of the notes. Both sit in one stop dated
1922-01-01 **purely to order the segment**, with the invention stated in `date_confidence`.

**The RAI is not a Spence stop.** The fellowship is an institutional tie and **no source places
Spence physically at the Institute** (whose coordinates the pool also got wrong by a kilometre:
51.5194/-0.127 is Russell Square; the RAI is 51.5239/-0.1397). A stop belongs to whoever was
actually there, so the fellowship sits at his Edinburgh pin and London is named in prose only.

## 5. Atlantis and Lemuria (1923-1943)

**[A]** *The Problem of Atlantis* (1924); *Atlantis in America* (1925, Ernest Benn); *The Problem
of Lemuria* (1932); *The Occult Sciences in Atlantis* (1943, IA `occultsciencesin00spen`).

**Date contradiction, not resolved: *The History of Atlantis*.**

Wikipedia says 1927; Open Library carries a distinct Rider edition record at **1926** alongside
1927 printings; the Internet Archive's London: Rider copy `historyofatlanti00spenuoft` is
catalogued "[n.d.]" with the note "exact publication date unknown". The pool's own two lenses
disagreed with each other on this one book. The file uses **1926 consistently in both the stop
date and the prose**, with 1927 recorded in `date_confidence`. The pool's "revised, expanded"
has been **dropped**: no source describes it as a revision of the earlier Atlantis books.

**The Donnelly lineage [A].** Wikipedia explicitly names Ignatius Donnelly's *Atlantis: The
Antediluvian World* (1882) as what Spence takes up: a direct one-directional citation relation,
written into the file. **Donnelly is in `QUEUE.md` (lines 424-426) with no journey file yet**;
when he gets one it should inherit this interlock forward.

**The contradiction inside Spence's own work, flagged not resolved.** *The Myths of Mexico and
Peru* (1913) opens chapter I: "There is now no question as to the indigenous origin of the
civilisations of Mexico, Central America, and Peru." *Atlantis in America* (1925) argues that a
drowned Atlantic landmass seeded them. **No source records Spence reconciling the two**, and the
file says so rather than smoothing it.

**Reception, [R] as to the printed page.** *The Geographical Journal* 64:2 (August 1924),
pp. 181-182, reviewer "R.N.R.B.", on *The Problem of Atlantis*: industrious, has done service in
marshalling the evidence, an entertaining volume well worth reading, but "in reading this book
one cannot help feeling that the author believes more than the evidence warrants", and rash "in
stating that there is proof that Greenland has moved 2500 yards in forty years". **The JSTOR
scan is paywalled and was not inspected**; the whole review reaches this file through Wikipedia's
citation alone, and both quotations carried into the journey file are tagged [R] for that reason.

## 6. The Scots verse (1923-1953)

**[A]** *The Phoenix* (1923, Porpoise Press); *Plumes of Time* (1926, Allen & Unwin); *Weirds and
Vanities* (1927, Porpoise Press); *Collected Poems* (1953). Source: Scottish Poetry Library poet
page, corroborated by Wikipedia.

**Correction.** The pool called the 1953 *Collected Poems* "posthumous". **It is not.** Spence
died 3 March 1955; the book appeared two years before his death, as the pool's own geography lens
correctly had it. The word has been deleted.

**[A]** The verse is in a Scots modelled on the sixteenth-century makars, and the Scottish Poetry
Library places him in the revival alongside **Hugh MacDiarmid**, who has no journey file and is
named as a plain fact of the movement, not as an inherited pin.

## 7. Nationalist politics (1926-1929)

**[A]** Founded the Scottish National Movement, an Edinburgh group drawn mainly from literary
figures (Wikipedia). Dated 1926 by one lens and "c. 1926-1928" by the other; the file uses 1926
and notes the softer range.

**[A]** The National Party of Scotland was inaugurated at **Stirling on 23 June 1928**
(Wikipedia, "National Party of Scotland"). **Correction to the merging bodies:** the pool listed
four; Wikipedia names **three**, the Scots National League, the Scottish National Movement and
the Glasgow University Scottish Nationalist Association. The pool's fourth, "the Scottish Home
Rule Movement", is really the Scottish Home Rule **Association**, which the article places in the
consensus that a party was needed, **not in the merger**. The file follows the three and words
the Association's role as agreement. The venue within Stirling is unspecified; the pin is
town-centre level.

**[A] and exact.** The **Midlothian and Peebles Northern by-election, 31 January 1929**. Spence
stood for the National Party of Scotland and **was the first nationalist to contest a United
Kingdom parliamentary seat**. Fourth of four: Clarke (Lab) 7,917; Colville (Unionist) 6,965;
Keir (Lib) 3,130; **Spence 842, 4.5 per cent**; turnout 66.0 per cent (Wikipedia, "Midlothian and
Peebles Northern (UK Parliament constituency)"; "National Party of Scotland"). The pool had this
as a general election; **it was a by-election**, and the date has been added.

**[R] as to site.** Dalkeith (55.8958, -3.0693) is the constituency's traditional counting town;
**no source confirms the declaration venue**, and the pool's own note said so while its tag field
still read "A". Corrected to [R].

## 8. *The Mysteries of Britain*, the pool's largest error, corrected

The pool carried, in two separate lenses, "*Mysteries of Celtic Britain* (1905)". **Both the title
and the date are wrong.** The book is ***The Mysteries of Britain: Secret Rites and Traditions of
Ancient Britain Restored***, London: Rider & Co., **1928** (Open Library `first_publish_year` 1928
with a Rider & Co. edition record; Internet Archive scan `in.ernet.dli.2015.283526` dated 1928).
*Mysteries of Celtic Britain* is a **1998 retitled reprint** (Senate / Siena). Wikipedia's
"(1905)" is a long-standing error **mirrored by the Wikisource author page**, so those two are
**not independent** and must not be counted as corroboration.

The consequence is not only bibliographic. In 1905 Spence was a sub-editor with nothing
published; 1928 puts the book in the year the National Party of Scotland was founded, among his
mature Celtic work. The journey file moves it and explains the move in `date_confidence`.

## 9. Late books, widowerhood, death (1940-1955)

**[A]** *Occult Causes of the Present War* (1940); Helen Bruce Spence died 1942 (Wikipedia); *The
Occult Sciences in Atlantis* (1943); *Scottish Ghosts and Goblins* (1952).

**Date contradiction: *The Magic Arts in Celtic Britain*.** The pool gave 1949, from Wikipedia's
bibliography. **Open Library's edition years run 1938, 1945, 1946, 1970, 1988, 1992, 1995, 1999,
with Rider among the publishers, and no 1949 printing appears at all.** Wikipedia's bibliography
is the same one carrying the wrong 1905 for *The Mysteries of Britain*. The file uses **1945**
and records 1949 as the alternative. **Not resolved, only weighted.**

**[A], single-source.** A royal Civil List pension for his literary work, 1951, **German
Wikipedia only**; English Wikipedia contains neither "Civil List" nor "pension" anywhere. The pool
tagged it [A] while its own note called the London administrative link [R]; the file keeps the
Edinburgh award at [A] on de.wikipedia alone, says so, and drops the London link.

**[A]** Died in Edinburgh, 3 March 1955, aged 80, having published over forty books. **No source
consulted names the street or the house** where he died. A gap.

**Burial [A] as to fact and description, coordinates reconciled.** Wikipedia: "buried in the
north-west section of the 20th century northern extension to Dean Cemetery in western Edinburgh",
and "His wife, Helen S. Bruce (d. 1942) lies with him." The interments list carries him under
"Twentieth century extension" as "Lewis Spence (1874-1955) journalist, author and poet."

**The three lenses gave three different coordinates for the one grave**, 55.9497/-3.2265,
55.9525/-3.2247 and 55.9497/-3.2276, the first and third outside the cemetery altogether, down
toward the Water of Leith. All three are discarded for the value in Wikipedia's own "Dean
Cemetery" article, **55.95333, -3.22222**, used everywhere the grave appears.

**No burial date is attested.** The file dates it 1955-03-07 **solely to order it after the
death**, and says so in `date_confidence`.

**[R]** No monument, statue or plaque to Spence is documented anywhere consulted; the shared
gravestone appears to be the whole of it. Dean Cemetery's own site was unreachable, so this is an
argument from absence and is tagged as one.
