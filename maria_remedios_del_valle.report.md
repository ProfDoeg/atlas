# María Remedios del Valle — build report

**Sources.** No single scholarly biography exists; the canon here is assembled from Argentina's recent public-history wave (2020-2026), triangulated across ~15 outlets: El Historiador, Infobae (two long features, 2020 and 2026), Cultura.gob.ar and the Museo del Cabildo, ENDEPA, La Tinta/Agencia Paco Urondo (the fullest account of her Mondongo origins and paternity), Pensamiento Nacional's biographical entry (the most granular on the 1826-1828 legislative file, including Anchorena's testimony and the pension figures), and the Spanish and English Wikipedia articles (best on precise dates and the Rosas-era pension). Battle coordinates for Vilcapugio and Ayohuma came from the Spanish Wikipedia battle infoboxes. Her actual 1826 petition text ("Doña María Remedios del Valle, capitana del Ejército... a V.S. debidamente expone...") surfaced via a genealogy blog (geneasud) quoting the expediente itself — the one moment her own dictated words survive.

**Judgment calls.** (1) Birth year floats between 1764, 1766, and 1767 across sources, and the Monserrat parish wasn't keeping registers until 1770 — I used 1766 (traditional) and said so in the campa rather than pick a false precision. (2) The army's departure date splits 20 June vs. 6 July 1810 across sources; I went with 6 July (two independent sources) but marked it traditional rather than attested. (3) "Las niñas/mujeres de Ayohuma" is used by some sources as if it meant her literal daughters — I treated it as the group epithet the canon actually uses (a company of brave women, not a family claim), since it doesn't square with the husband-and-two-sons-lost-at-Huaqui thread that's otherwise consistent. (4) The 1814-1820 stretch with Güemes and Arenales, and the entire "trail goes cold" gap, is confirmed only in outline by secondary sources with no dates attached — I built four inferred stops there rather than skip six years of her adult life, flagging the uncertainty inside the prose itself (the "trail goes cold" stop is explicitly about the archive's silence).

**Time-folds / gaps.** The biggest is 1818-1826: total documentary silence between the end of active campaigning and her reappearance as a beggar. The captivity chapter (nine days of flogging, "en capilla siete veces," aiding officers' escapes, her own escape) is also underdated in every source — I sequenced it Nov 1813-May 1814 as the most defensible order without pretending to day-level precision, and marked all four stops inferred except the flogging itself (attested as an event, not as to exact dates).

**Five richest episodes.** (1) The Reconquista/Defensa of 1806-07 — she'd already fought two street wars in Buenos Aires before Alto Perú ever started. (2) Huaqui, 20 June 1811 — husband and both sons lost in one afternoon, and she stays with the army instead of going home; the canon's real hinge. (3) Tucumán, 24 September 1812 — Belgrano refuses her leave to nurse at the front, she goes anyway, and the soldiers, not the general, are the ones who first call her Madre de la Patria. (4) The nine days of public flogging plus "siete veces en capilla," ordered by name by three royalist commanders — the physical cost the later scar-count (six wounds, entered into legislative testimony in 1828) can only gesture at. (5) Plaza de la Recova, August 1827 — Viamonte recognizing a woman selling pastries and begging alms as the captain who marched with the army to the Alto Perú, the single scene that turns fifteen years of silence back into a name.

**Connections to the atlas.** Direct edge to belgrano.journey.json — she serves under him from Tucumán through Ayohuma, and both journeys end the same way the canon likes: real poverty, an unpaid or barely-paid pension, a state that recognizes its own hero only once it's nearly too late (Belgrano dies owning less than a shopkeeper in 1820; she dies a sergeant major who took her patron's surname in gratitude for a salary the republic itself had refused her). San_martin.journey.json is the wider independence-cluster sibling, operating on a different front of the same war. Juana Azurduy and Martín Güemes are named in her own story (the "with Arenales" and "Güemes' gauchos" segments) but have no journey file yet in the atlas — natural next builds, since both would share several coordinates and dates with this one (Salta frontier, 1814-1817). She is, as far as this corpus goes so far, the atlas's first entry from the Afro-Argentine thread specifically, rather than the independence-cluster generally.
