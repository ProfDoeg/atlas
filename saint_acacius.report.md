# Saint Acacius (Agathius, Achatius, Ahac, Sant'Agazio, San Acato)
*Research report, 2026-08-08. Prepared for the Atlas of Journeys. Slug: `saint_acacius`.*

**Legend:** **[A]** = attested, source named · **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition, or inference. Contradictions are flagged where they occur and are **not** adjudicated. Sources that could not be reached are named with the reason.

---

## 0. The central problem, stated first

There are at least two Acaciuses here, and the tradition never separated them.

1. **Acacius (Agathius) of Byzantium**, a Cappadocian Greek centurion, arrested at Perinthus in Thrace and executed at Byzantium c. 303, inside the Diocletianic persecution. **[A: en.wikipedia, 'Acacius of Byzantium'; it.wikipedia, 'Acacio di Bisanzio']**
2. A legendary **Acacius / Achatius**, leader of a Roman legion crucified with ten thousand companions on Mount Ararat, in a tradition naming the emperor as **Hadrian** and placed in the second century. **[R: de.wikipedia, 'Zehntausend Märtyrer', which states outright that the story *ist als historische Begebenheit nicht belegt*]**

Sources disagree about which is the Acacius counted among the **Fourteen Holy Helpers**. Standard modern lists identify the member as Agathius of Byzantium, feast 7 May, invoked against headache **[A: en.wikipedia, 'Fourteen Holy Helpers', citing Richard Stracke, *Christian Iconography*, 20 October 2015]**, but the Ararat legend supplies nearly all the surviving imagery of the cult: Dürer, Carpaccio, the Bern Münster glass.

A **third** Acacius threatens the file: **Acacius, bishop of Melitene** (d. c. 438), real and distinct. The gathering pass anchored the centurion's service to Melitene on "the Anatolian-legion martyr topos." That anchor is **dropped**: unsourced, and it manufactures exactly the conflation the file warns against. It may also explain Dürer's label "Bishop Acacius." The genuine Melitene topos belongs to Legio XII Fulminata and the Forty Martyrs of Sebaste. Nothing below resolves the double identity, and the Roman calendar does not either: 7 May for the centurion, 22 June for the ten thousand, with no ruling on whether the two names are one man.

## 1. Origin: Cappadocia, late third century

- Born in **Cappadocia**, late third century. **[A: en.wikipedia, 'Acacius of Byzantium']**
- **Correction applied.** The gathering pass carried a birth year of **280**. No source supports it; the sources say only "late 3rd century in Cappadocia" and name **no town, no parents, no year**. The journey carries `0280-01-01` **as a pin-forcing placeholder only**, with the hedge in `date_confidence`, never in prose.
- The coordinate, **Caesarea Mazaca / Kayseri (38.7225, 35.4875)**, is a **regional proxy** for the province's chief city, not a claimed birthplace. **[R]**
- **Greek**, of Cappadocia, serving as **centurion in the imperial Roman army**. **[A]**

## 2. Arrest and execution, c. 303-304

- Arrested at **Perinthus in Thrace** (later Heraclea; Marmara Ereğlisi, 40.9714, 27.9531) by the **tribune Firmus**, charged with being a Christian; brought to **Byzantium**, scourged, refusing to renounce, **beheaded**. Dated **c. 303**, some sources **304**. **[A: en.wikipedia, citing the *Passio*; the one-year spread flagged]**
- The persecution's machinery is independently attested in the atlas: Lactantius dates the demolition of the Nicomedia church to **23 February 303**, the first edict to the next morning (`constantino_el_grande.journey.json`). **[A for the edict; the link to Acacius's arrest is [R]]**

### The two quotations

Both come from **one** source: the English *Lives of the Saints* of the **Orthodox Church in America**, rendering the Greek Synaxarion, under "Martyr Acacius the Centurion at Byzantium," 7 May. Both **fetched and confirmed verbatim on oca.org**, and **[A as texts]** only.

1. **"Courage, Acacius, and be strong!"**: a heavenly voice on the forced march; fellow prisoners are said to have heard it and converted.
2. **"My power and strength are given me by the Lord Jesus Christ, Who has healed my wounds."**: his reply to the officer Vivianus (Bibianus), who found him healed after seven days in prison and suspected a bribed physician.

**No Roman-era document carries either sentence.** That is the general condition of this file (see §11).

### Contradiction: how he died

**Beheading** is the standard account **[A: en.wikipedia]**, but **Socrates of Constantinople** reports a chapel where "a walnut tree stood, on which the martyr was supposed to have been hanged" **[A as a quotation of Socrates; the manner of death is [R]]**. The two deaths are incompatible and neither source acknowledges the other. Socrates's own phrasing is a hedge, not a record. **The chapel and the tree have never been located.** The journey pin (41.0, 28.96) is an admitted round number inside the old peninsula. The gathering pass tagged this **[A]**; that was wrong, and it is carried as **[R]**.

## 3. The Church of St Acacius at Constantinople

- The church existed, "possibly" named for this martyr. **[A for the church; the dedication is hedged by the source itself]** **Correction:** the gathering pass dated it to the **5th century** and gave Arcadius as *r.* 395-408. En.wikipedia, 'Church of St. Acacius', places it among the **earliest churches of the city after the 328 foundation** (4th century) and gives Arcadius as augustus **from 383**; 395 is only his sole rule in the east.
- **The collapse:** when **Arcadius** visited the walnut-tree chapel the building fell in, and the crowd outside was unharmed. **[A as reported by Socrates]** **Doubt carried, not suppressed:** **David Woods** argues the patron is a *different* Acacius, a *comes* under Constantine and no martyr. **[A: cited within en.wikipedia]**
- **The Heptaskalon martyrium restored under Justinian** is **[R]**: in no atlas file and in no source gathered here. It comes from outside knowledge of Constantinopolitan topography, sits on the canonical city pin, and is flagged in `date_confidence`.

## 4. Feast dates: unharmonized

**8 May** in the older Latin reckoning and on the Vierzehnheiligen Mercy Altar **[A: de.wikipedia, 'Basilika Vierzehnheiligen']** · **7 May** in the modern Roman calendar **[A: en.wikipedia, 'Fourteen Holy Helpers', Stracke]** · **17 April** in Orthodox use **[A: de.wikipedia, 'Achatius'; it.wikipedia]** · **16 January** for the translation of the relics **[A: en.wikipedia, 'Acacius of Byzantium']**. Italian and German sources disagree by a day (7 vs 8 May) and neither notices. **Flagged, not resolved.**

## 5. The relics go west: Calabria and Castile

### Squillace, the sharpest internal contradiction

- Relics translated to a **spring near Cassiodorus's Vivarium** at Squillace; venerated as **Sant'Agazio**, feast **7 May**. **[A for cult and name: it.wikipedia, 'Squillace', names him patron]**
- **Date irreconcilable.** en.wikipedia and es.wikipedia give **c. 630**. it.wikipedia gives the **XI secolo**. Four hundred years apart, no cross-citation. The journey carries **two stops on the same pin**, one per date, rather than choosing. The gathering pass let its chronology lens say 630 while its afterlife lens said the eleventh century; that silent self-contradiction is now explicit.
- **Name:** "San Agario" is an en.wikipedia variant or typo; the confirmed local form is **Sant'Agazio** (it.wikipedia, 'Squillace'). **"Remain there in the cathedral" is unsupported:** that same article names the Concattedrale di Santa Maria Assunta and mentions no relics. **Coordinates:** Squillace is **38.783 / 16.517**; the gathering pass's 38.7719/16.5133 sits ~1.3 km south.

### Guardavalle, Cuenca, Ávila, Slovenia

**Guardavalle:** an **arm relic** brought in **1584**, installed by **Bishop Marcello Sirleto**; town patron Sant'Agazio, feast 7 May. **[A for patronage and feast: en.wikipedia, 'Guardavalle'; the 1584 date and Sirleto come from es.wikipedia alone, unconfirmed]** **Cuenca and Ávila:** relics venerated in both under the name **San Acato** **[A: es.wikipedia]**, with **no church, no date, no account of the transfer** for either; journey coordinates are city-centre proxies and the dates explicit placeholders. **Slovenia:** venerated as **Ahac** since **at least the 16th century** **[A: en.wikipedia]**, see §8.

## 6. The Ten Thousand Martyrs of Mount Ararat

From **de.wikipedia, 'Zehntausend Märtyrer'**, checked line by line and confirmed **[A as legend text]** throughout: **9,000** Roman soldiers under **Achatius** are sent to Armenia to suppress a revolt; trapped by a superior force, they see a vision, convert en masse, and win; a barbarian contingent sent to punish them converts too, bringing the total to **10,000**; all are tortured with **thorns** and **crucified on Mount Ararat** by an emperor "often identified as **Hadrian**." German Wikipedia states outright that the story **"ist als historische Begebenheit nicht belegt."** **[A]**

**Correction 1.** The gathering pass wrote that the legend "emerges around the time of the Crusades." It does not: en.wikipedia attributes the written account to **Anastasius Bibliothecarius**, ninth century, two centuries earlier. The crusading era is when the *cult spread*, not when the *story appears*.

**Correction 2.** The gathering pass offered "others place the episode under **Diocletian**." Diocletian is **not attested in the Ararat dossier at all**; en.wikipedia names no emperor there, and the Diocletianic date belongs to the Byzantium centurion. The genuine third-emperor variant is **Decius**.

**Three unharmonized regional forms, all [A: en.wikipedia, 'Ten Thousand Martyrs']:** the Roman, **22 June**, Mount Ararat, Hadrian · the Greek Orthodox, **1 June**, **Antioch**, **Decius** · and "Myriads of Holy Martyrs, by the sword," **18 March**, **Nicomedia**, in the Diocletianic context. Three cities, three emperors, three dates, one number. Later calendars confuse them. No source reconciles them and neither does this report.

**Textual basis.** Attributed to **Anastasius Bibliothecarius**, ninth century, some seven hundred years after the purported events. **[A]** The tradition running through the Catholic Encyclopedia calls it "a legend containing many historical inaccuracies and utterly improbable details," a phrase surviving unchanged into the modern reference works. **[A: quoted within en.wikipedia]**

**Golden Legend, a rejected waypoint.** The gathering pass proposed **Genoa, "seat of Archbishop Jacobus de Voragine."** The *Legenda Aurea* is dated c. **1259-1266**, while Jacobus became Archbishop of Genoa only in **1292** (en.wikipedia, 'Golden Legend'; 'Jacobus de Voragine'), so Genoa cannot be his seat for a compilation made as a Dominican decades earlier. Separately, **the negative finding is unverified**: no Ten Thousand Martyrs chapter was located, but the full table of contents could not be reached. The waypoint is **dropped**.

## 7. The Fourteen Holy Helpers

- The group forms as a fixed company in the German dioceses during the **Black Death, mid-14th century**. **[A: en.wikipedia, 'Fourteen Holy Helpers']**
- **Coordinate correction.** The gathering pass pinned "the Rhineland" at 50.7374/7.0982, calling it the Cologne area. That is **Bonn** (Cologne is 50.9375/6.9603). The journey instead inherits the canonical **Regensburg pin, 49.0134 / 12.1016, dated 1350-01-01**.
- Acacius's place: **feast 7 May, invoked against headache** **[A: Richard Stracke via en.wikipedia; the gathering pass misnamed him "C. Stracke"]**. Italian tradition widens the patronage to **soldiers, the dying, migraine and toothache** **[A: it.wikipedia; Catholic Online]**; the wording varies between "headache" and "migraine and toothache," **flagged, not harmonized**.
- A collective feast on **8 August** was kept locally but **never entered the General Roman Calendar**. Several individual feasts were **removed in the 1969 reform**; **none decanonized**. **[A]**

### Vierzehnheiligen

- **24 September 1445**: the shepherd **Hermann Leicht**, near **Kloster Langheim**, sees a crying child in a field. **28 June 1446**: the final apparition, the child with a red cross among thirteen others, declaring themselves the fourteen helpers. **Eighteen days later**: a dying maid at Langheim recovers after invoking them, and the abbey recognizes the visions. **[A: de.wikipedia, 'Basilika Vierzehnheiligen']** **Correction:** the gathering pass dated the third apparition to **1445**, which belongs to the first vision alone; `catorce_santos_ayudantes.journey.json` already carries 1446-06-28.
- **Two places collapsed into one, corrected.** The gathering pass put Kloster Langheim at 50.1167/11.05, effectively **on the basilica**. de.wikipedia gives **50.11219 / 11.11011**, in Klosterlangheim (Lichtenfels), **~4.4 km east**. The journey keeps two distinct pins.
- **Basilica built 1743-1772 by Balthasar Neumann** **[A]**, canonical pin **50.1157 / 11.0541**; the afterlife lens's 50.1097/11.1128 is ~4 km east and wrong. **On the Gnadenaltar** Acacius appears under the **double name "Agathius (or Acacius)"**, feast **8 May**, invoked **against headache** **[A: de.wikipedia]**. The double name is carved into the furniture: the shrine does not decide which Acacius it honours.
- **Obernburg am Main:** the **Nothelferaltar** sets the fourteen in four ranked rows with Christ at centre, with a plaque keeping the rhyme of the three virgin martyrs. **[A: shared canonical pin 49.8385 / 9.1472, 1780-01-01]**

## 8. Ahac in Carniola

- **Idrija, first sighting:** a bucket-maker finds liquid mercury in a spring, year given as **1490, 1492 or 1497**. **[A: en.wikipedia, 'Idrija', which makes no saint's-day link]** **Idrija, the lode:** "on the saint's feast day in 1508 a productive mercury vein opened in Idrija." **[A: sl.wikipedia, 'Ahac']** Coordinates confirmed exact, **46.00250 / 14.02750**.
- **Attribution corrected, and a false contradiction dissolved.** The gathering pass credited the 1508 claim to **es.wikipedia, 'Acacio de Bizancio'**, which contains **no Idrija and no mercury whatsoever**; it comes from **sl.wikipedia, 'Ahac'**. And the dates are **not** in conflict: 1490 (var. 1492/1497) is the *first sighting of liquid mercury in a spring*, 1508 the *opening of the rich cinnabar lode*, which is what tradition ties to St Ahac's day. **Two events, eighteen years apart.**
- **Battle of Sisak, 22 June 1593**, the feast of Ahac. **[A: en.wikipedia, 'Battle of Sisak'; the feast-day linkage from sl.wikipedia]** **Correction:** command was **joint**, **Ruprecht von Eggenberg, Ban Tamás Erdődy, and Andreas von Auersperg** ("the Carniolan Achilles"), where the gathering pass named Auersperg alone. The site is the **Sava-Kupa confluence, 45.4706 / 16.3861**; 45.4881/16.3719 is Sisak town, ~2 km off.
- **Church of St Ahac on Gora**, built to commemorate the victory. **[A: sl.wikipedia, 'Ahac']** **Coordinate correction, ~10 km:** it stands on **Gora peak (748 m) above Mali Ločnik near Turjak**, **45.89083 / 14.61291**; the gathering pass's 45.8/14.65 falls well south. **Founding year not fixed** in the sources, and the journey carries a hedged placeholder.

## 9. Afterlife in art

- **Dürer, woodcut, Nuremberg, c. 1497** (catalogued c. 1496-1498, "approximately ten years" before the panel). A **bishop identified as Acacius** appears with "his eyes stripped through a drill." **[A: en.wikipedia, 'Martyrdom of the Ten Thousand']** The gathering pass's flat 1496 overstated the precision. **The "Bishop Acacius" label contradicts the centurion tradition and is kept as a contradiction**, possibly contaminated by Acacius of Melitene (§0).
- **Dürer, panel, 1508**, commissioned by **Frederick the Wise** for the Wittenberg relic collection (Schlosskirche, 51.8664 / 12.6378, which held relics of the Ten Thousand among Frederick's hoard). Dürer paints **himself** and his late friend **Conrad Celtis** at the centre; Acacius is **bound in chains**, the drilled eyes dropped. **[A]** The panel now hangs in the **Kunsthistorisches Museum, Vienna**; **gap:** no source dates the transfer, so the journey pins the museum's opening year and says so.
- **Carpaccio, 1515**, commissioned by **San Giorgio Maggiore, Venice**. **[A]** **Gap:** present location **could not be established**; the Accademia collection pages returned no record and no other holding institution was found.
- **Bern Münster glass, 1448-1450**: originally **40 rectangular panels, 8 medallions, 3 decorative elements**; **eleven rectangles and one medallion survive**. **[A: de.wikipedia]** The same source links the cult to two Bernese battles fought on or near 22 June (**Laupen 1339**, **Murten 1476**). It also names **Santi Diecimila Martiri**, Palermo, and the **Kirche zu den Zehntausend Rittern**, Lenthe; Lenthe **not geolocated**, and the Palermo date in the journey is a placeholder.

## 11. The honest gaps

1. **No primary Roman-era acta survive** for either Acacius from within a century of the claimed events. Both dossiers rest on later hagiographic compilation. Not resolved by anything reachable, and the single most important fact in the file.
2. **The two Acaciuses are never separated** by any authority; the calendar preserves the split rather than closing it.
3. Further gaps, each stated as such in the journey's `date_confidence` fields: the **Squillace translation date** (630 or the eleventh century, by language edition) · **Cuenca and Ávila**, relics attested but church and date not · **Carpaccio's altarpiece**, commission attested, location unfound · **the Lenthe church**, named but not geolocated · **the Golden Legend**, no Acacius chapter found but the table of contents unreached, so the absence is unproven · **the Gora church founding year**, unfixed · **the walnut-tree chapel**, never located, its coordinate an admitted guess.
