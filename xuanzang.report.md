# Xuanzang (602-664): research report
*Compiled 2026-08-13 for the Atlas of Journeys. Slug: `xuanzang`. Companion artifact: `xuanzang.journey.json` (9 segments, 45 stops).*

Chen Hui of Luozhou, ordained Xuanzang, who left Tang China illegally in 629 (or 627), reached Nalanda by way of the Gobi, the Tian Shan, Sogdiana and Gandhara, returned in 645 with 657 Sanskrit manuscripts, and spent his last nineteen years translating them in Chang'an under imperial patronage. The Ming novel *Journey to the West* buried the pilgrim under the fictional Tang Sanzang, and the fiction is what most of East Asia knows.

**Legend.** **[A]** = attested, source named. **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition or inference, including anything resting on Huili's devotional biography alone. Contradictions are flagged and **not adjudicated**.

**Method.** Worked from a verified research pool of five separately sourced, adversarially corrected lenses. Where two lenses disagree, the disagreement is recorded here and carried into the journey file's `date_confidence`, never smoothed away in campa prose.

---

## 1. Luoyang and the doubt (602-628)

- Born Chen Hui, some records Chen Yi, near Luoyang in Henan, into a family of officials and Confucian scholars **[A** Wikipedia 'Xuanzang'; New World Encyclopedia**]**. Year 602 traditional, month and day unrecorded, village given as Chenhe, Luozhou.
- Father dies; the boy moves to Jingtu Monastery in Luoyang to live with his elder brother, a monk, 611 **[A]**. Flees the Sui collapse to Chang'an, then Chengdu, 618 **[A]**; fully ordained at Chengdu, 622, age twenty **[A**, both sources agreeing**]**.
- **Corrected within the pool.** The chronology lens had him "formally taking the monastic name Xuanzang" at full ordination in 622. Wrong: Wikipedia records an earlier **novice (sramanera) ordination at thirteen** at Jingtu, when the dharma name was conferred; 622 is the bhiksu ordination only. Both ordinations **[A]**; Huili's account of the examination **[R]**.
- In Chang'an by about 626, studying Sanskrit, comparing Chinese recensions, dissatisfied above all with the state of the *Yogacarabhumi-sastra* **[R** NWE; a process, not a datable event**]**.

## 2. The illegal departure (627 or 629)

- Petitions the court to go west; refused under a Tang ban on foreign travel; departs anyway **[A** NWE; Wikipedia**]**.
- **Contradiction, unresolved.** Traditional biographies date the departure **627**, modern scholarship generally **629** on court records. The journey file uses 629 and carries the dispute. Not cosmetic: the identity of the qaghan met at Suyab depends on it (section 3).
- Gansu stages from Huili: Qinzhou, Lanzhou, the Yellow River, Liangzhou (preaching draws crowds, the governor orders him back), Guazhou (the prefect Li Chang tears up the arrest warrant); then the Gobi crossing, the guide Shi Pantuo, the five beacon towers, the arrow at the first tower, the spilled water skin, four waterless nights. All **[R]**, all Huili, no independent Tang-era corroboration reached.

**The largest map correction in the dossier.** The geography lens pinned the Jade Gate crossing at the Han **Yumenguan ruins** west of Dunhuang (40.3652, 93.8783). Those ruins are at 40.3535, 93.8640, and Wikipedia 'Yumen Pass' records the Han pass **abandoned by the sixth century** as caravans took the northern route via Hami: no Tang checkpoint in 629. The gate crossed by night was the **Tang Jade Gate, relocated east near Guazhou on the Hulu river**, some 200 km away. The journey file pins it there (40.3, 96.2) and flags the site as inference.

## 3. The oasis kingdoms (629-630)

- **Gaochang (Turfan)**: Qu Wentai detains him, becomes patron, provisions the onward journey **[A** Wikipedia 'Gaochang': "the successor Qu Wentai welcomed the Tang pilgrim Xuanzang with great enthusiasm in 629 AD"**]**. **Contradiction, unresolved:** the chronology lens dates Gaochang **630**, the geography lens and the Gaochang article **629**; the journey file uses 629 and flags it. The **sworn brotherhood, gold, thirty horses and twenty-four letters** are **[R]**, Huili alone.
- **Agni (Karashahr), Kuchi (Kucha), Aksu**, then the Tian Shan **[A** Wikipedia; *Records* book 1 for Kucha: the colossal Buddhas outside the western gate, the debate with Moksagupta**]**.
- **The Ice Mountain (Ling Shan)**: seven days on glacier, a third of the party dead of cold **[A** Huili**]**; the pass identification **[R]**. The geography lens labelled it "Bedel Pass (Muzat river crossing)" at 41.8, 78.3, tagged [A]. Both corrected: Bedel sits at roughly 41.53, 78.42, and Wikipedia treats **Bedel and Muzart as two distinct passes**, scholars disputing which he used. Conflating them erases the dispute.
- **Issyk-Kul (Qingchi)** and **Suyab**: audience with the qaghan of the Western Turks in a tent worked with gold flowers, interpreter and letters provided **[A** Huili**]**. Coordinates corrected: Ak-Beshim is at 42.8052, 75.1999, 8 km west-southwest of Tokmok, not the pool's 42.7864, 75.3153, which sat on the modern town. Tag corrected: naming the ruler **Tong Yabghu Qaghan** cannot stand as [A]. He died around 628-630, and scholars dispute whether Xuanzang met him or his son Si Yabghu, which turns on the 627/629 question. Tagged **[R]**.

## 4. Sogdiana and Tokharistan (630)

- Route: **Samarkand, Iron Gates (Buzgala), Termez / Amu Darya, Balkh, Bamiyan** **[A** *Records* book 1**]**.
- **Bukhara does not belong on the line of march.** The chronology lens listed "Samarkand, Bukhara, and Balkh". Bukhara lies northwest of Samarkand, the wrong direction for Balkh, and the geography lens, drawn from *Records* book 1, has no Bukhara stop. The *Records* describe it as a country he did not traverse. **It is therefore not a stop**, despite the Genghis Khan pin on it.
- **Iron Gates**: Wikipedia places it at 38.2111, 66.9731, Surxondaryo Region, **Uzbekistan**, west of Boysun, on the Samarkand-Balkh road. The geography lens called it the "Uzbekistan-Tajikistan border" and pinned it 8 km north. Both corrected.
- **Balkh**, which he calls "little Rajagriha": about a month at the **Nava Vihara**, the finest monastery in Tokharistan by his judgement, holding relics including what the monks tell him is a tooth of the Buddha **[A** *Records* book 1**]**.
- **Bamiyan, 30 April 630**: two colossal standing Buddhas in the cliff, a third reclining figure in a nearby monastery **[A** *Records* book 1; Wikipedia 'Buddhas of Bamiyan'**]**, his account the earliest textual evidence for them. Latitude corrected to 34.8319 (the pool's 34.8453 sat 1.5 km north of the niches). His "140 to 150 feet" is an underestimate: the western Buddha stood 55 m, the eastern 38 m. **The reclining figure has never been archaeologically confirmed**, and is recorded as testimony, not fact.
- **Kapisa (Begram)**: winters there, taking part in a court-arranged debate between schools **[A]**.

## 5. India: entry, Gandhara, Kashmir (630-633)

- **The entry sequence in the chronology lens was geographically impossible.** It read "Kapisha, then Lampa, Nagarahara, and Gandhara before reaching Laghman". **Lampa and Laghman are the same place** (Lampaka = Laghman), so the list duplicated it, and Laghman lies **west** of Nagarahara and Gandhara. Correct order: **Kapisha, Lampaka/Laghman, Nagarahara, Gandhara**. Lampaka is where he reckoned himself arrived in India, which is why it cannot come last.
- **Purushapura (Peshawar)**: the ruined stupa of Kanishka, over 400 feet by his account, the old capital emptied of monks **[A** *Records* book 2**]**. **Uddiyana (Swat)**: 1,400 monasteries, mostly deserted **[A** book 3**]**, and the only **[A]** interlock in the pool. **Taxila**: a subject province of Kashmir, monasteries ruined, the Dharmarajika stupa standing **[A** book 3**]**.
- **Kashmir**: about two years, invited by the king, Abhidharma and logic, twenty scribes copying the kingdom's scriptures **[A** NWE; Huili**]**. Then Chinabhukti, Jalandhara, the Kulu valley, Bairat, Mathura **[A** book 4**]**; Matipura beyond the Ganges **[R]**.

## 6. The Buddhist sites and Nalanda (636-643)

- **Kanyakubja (Kanauj)**, monasteries visited, study among non-Mahayana traditions **[A** NWE**]**. **"Theravada" in the pool's wording is an anachronism**: that is the Pali school of Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia, no presence at seventh-century Kanyakubja. His *Records* use the Hinayana/Mahayana division, and the non-Mahayana schools of the plain were **Sammatiya** and **Sarvastivada**.
- **Lumbini, Kusinagara, Sarnath**, then Nalanda **[A** NWE; *Records* books 6-7**]**.
  - **Contradiction, unresolved, on Sarnath.** The geography lens dates the visit 636-637; Wikipedia 'Sarnath' gives about **640**. Worse, 636-637 against 637-638 for Lumbini and Kushinagar **reverses the itinerary**: he reached those two first, then turned south to Varanasi. The journey file keeps the chronology lens's order and flags the dating. The 1,500 Sammatiya monks and the 100-foot Ashokan stupa are confirmed from his own text.
- **Nalanda**: student of **Silabhadra**; receives the Indian name **Mokshadeva**; studies grammar, logic and Sanskrit besides Buddhist philosophy; later lectures there, and reaches the level of the roughly ten teachers able to expound fifty collections **[A** Wikipedia 'Nalanda'**]**. **Corrected:** the geography lens had him studying continuously 637-642, where Wikipedia records **two separate visits, 637 and 642, totalling around two years**, with travel through eastern, southern and western India between them. The journey file splits Nalanda into two stops.
- **Kanchipuram** and **Valabhi**: described at length in the *Records*, **presence disputed among historians [R]**. Kanchipuram is a stop, dispute flagged, as the southern limit of the record. Valabhi is left out: same doubt, less weight.
- **Harsha**: hosts him at Kanyakubja, with a twenty-one-day assembly and daily ritual before a life-sized golden Buddha **[A** Wikipedia 'Harsha', citing Xuanzang**]**. **The exact date is not given in the sources consulted**: inside Harsha's reign (606-647), generally placed around 643, unconfirmed **[R]**. The Prayaga quinquennial almsgiving is **[A** Huili; *Records* book 5**]**.

## 7. Return, translation, death (643-664, and after)

- **Departure from India was 643, not 645.** The chronology lens gave 645, with 657 texts and about 150 relics in 520 cases on twenty horses. Wikipedia 'Nalanda' is explicit: he left in **643**, and **645 is the arrival at Chang'an**. **The cargo figures are confirmed exactly.**
- **Honest gap: no stop-by-stop return itinerary exists.** The sources note the Pamir and Tarim recrossing and nothing like the outbound detail. The journey file carries three return stops (Pamir, Khotan, Chang'an), all reconstruction, and **drops Dunhuang**: the pool concedes it "is not named explicitly in the surviving account of this final leg".
- **Khotan**: about eight months, writing to Taizong to ask pardon for the illegal departure and leave to return with the cargo **[R** Huili**]**.
- **Chang'an, 645**: welcomed by large crowds; Taizong receives him and, rather than punishing him, encourages the travel account **[A** NWE; Wikipedia**]**. One source credits his diplomatic value, having moved Harsha to send an emissary to the Tang court, as easing the reception **[A]**.
- **The bureau was at Hongfu Temple, 645-648, not at Ci'en.** Da Ci'en did not exist in 629 or 645: founded **648** by decree of Prince Li Zhi, later Gaozong, in memory of Empress Zhangsun; Xuanzang moved there only then.
- **646**: Bianji compiles the dictated account into the **Great Tang Records on the Western Regions**: twelve volumes, roughly 120,000 characters, 110 countries **[A]**.
- **Translation output**: 74 works in one source, 75 in another, 1,335 chapters, out of roughly 657 texts collected **[A]**. The discrepancy is left standing.
- **652**: the Great Wild Goose Pagoda built at Ci'en in the third year of Yonghui to hold manuscripts, relics and figurines: five storeys, brick over an earthen core, about 200 feet **[A]**. **704**: rebuilt under Wu Zetian as a seven-storey pavilion tower, today 64.1 m with later Ming refacing **[A]**.
- **648**: Taizong sends Wang Xuance to India answering Harsha's embassy; Harsha had died in 647 **[A** Wikipedia 'Harsha'**]**.
- **Death, 5 February 664, age 61, at Yuhua Palace near Tongchuan**, after completing the 600-scroll Mahaprajnaparamita **[A** Wikipedia; Britannica; Huili**]**. **Contradiction, unresolvable from the pool:** the two lenses give **different coordinates for one site**, geography 35.15, 109.13 and afterlife 35.06, 108.94, about 20 km apart, and neither is right. Yuhua Palace stood in **Yijun County**, seat at 35.3986, 109.1169, the palace northwest of it. The journey file uses the county seat and says so. **The ruin itself was not pinpointed in this pass.**
- **669**: remains reinterred south of Chang'an; **Xingjiao Temple** founded over them with a five-storey pagoda flanked by smaller pagodas for Kuiji and Yuance **[A**, coordinates 34.0914, 109.0339 confirmed**]**. Two corrections: Wikipedia puts the temple in **Shaoling Yuan** ("Fanchuan" names the district of the eight famed Tang temples, not the site), and calls the Tang structure a **stone** pagoda, not brick. **2014**: inscribed by UNESCO within the Silk Roads: Chang'an-Tianshan Corridor **[A]**.
- **Afterlife of the text**: Julien's French translation, Paris 1857-1858; Beal's *Si-Yu-Ki*, London 1884; Watters, *On Yuan Chwang's Travels in India*, 1904 **[A]**. The *Records* guided excavators to Rajagrha, Sarnath, the Ajanta Caves, Vasu Vihara and Nalanda **[A]**.
- **Alexander Cunningham [R], dating corrected.** Archaeological Surveyor 1861, first Director-General 1 January 1871, but his reliance on Xuanzang runs from the **1830s**: Sarnath excavated 1837, "Verification of the Itinerary of the Chinese Pilgrim" published 1848. The pool's "c. 1861-1871" is far too late and narrow, and Wikipedia does not confirm he excavated Nalanda himself.
- **Unverified, recorded as tradition [R]**: the Xuanzang Memorial Hall at Nalanda (existence confirmed, the PRC funding and 2007 inauguration not); the skull relics said to have been unearthed near Nanjing in 1942 and divided among temples in Nanjing, Taiwan, Nara and Nalanda.
- **16th century**: Wu Cheng'en's *Journey to the West* recasts him as Tang Sanzang with Sun Wukong, Zhu Bajie and Sha Wujing **[A]**. This, not the pilgrimage, is his widest image.

## 8. Corpus interlocks

The interlock lens found **one [A] link; the rest are [R]**, inferred by laying his route against another traveler's pin rather than asserted by any file.

- **[A] Giuseppe Tucci.** `giuseppe_tucci.report.md` line 145 names Xuanzang and notes his absence from the corpus. Tucci's 1955 Swat reconnaissance and the Butkara dig were a hunt for **Uddiyana**, the kingdom Xuanzang described. Pin inherited byte-identical: **34.7571, 72.3622**.
- **[R] Alexander**, three inherited pins, all named in campa: Balkh (**36.755, 66.897**), Samarkand (**39.654, 66.96**), Taxila (**33.746, 72.787**); plus the Sogdian Rock near Baysun, in the Iron Gates campa. **Genghis Khan** shares the Samarkand pin (1220) and is named there.
- **[R] Marco Polo**: the Pamir (**37.5, 74.5**) and Khotan (**37.1184, 79.9273**), inherited and named in campa. **Kashgar (39.4704, 75.9898) was dropped** for stop budget: three [R] stops are as many as the undocumented return leg carries.
- **[R] Swami Vivekananda**, Bodh Gaya 1902: **24.6959, 84.9917**, inherited over the geography lens's 24.6961, 84.9911, named in campa.
- **[R] Matteo Ricci** (Beijing 1601), **Kircher** (the Nestorian Stele of Xi'an, 1667), **Ibn Battuta** (Delhi 1334), **Max Muller** (*Sacred Books of the East*): parallels, not shared sites, each named once, at the 645 return, the Ci'en pagoda, the Kanauj assembly and the Hongfu bureau.
- **Xi'an.** The corpus carries 34.3416, 108.9398 (`mao_zedong`, 1936) and 34.2556, 108.9855 (`bjarne_stroustrup`); either may anchor Chang'an. **34.3416, 108.9398 is used** for 626, 629 and 645; Hongfu (34.26, 108.94) and Ci'en (34.2197, 108.9594) keep their own pins. Mao is **not** named in campa: sharing a city across thirteen centuries is not a crossing.
- **Refused inheritance.** Kaaba (21.4225, 39.8262), Temple Mount (31.778, 35.2354), Paris and Buenos Aires are not on his route, and **Bukhara (39.7747, 64.4286) was refused too**, despite the Genghis Khan pin. A near-miss is not a shared site.

## 9. Quotes

Nine of 45 stops carry quotes, all verbatim. Two page citations were corrected within the pool by running-head verification against the Archive.org scan (id `b28983841`):

- The oath at the Jade Gate ("cut up as small as the very dust") is Beal, Book I, **p. 18, not p. 17**.
- The mother's dream ("You are my son, where then are you going?") is **pp. 10-11, not 11-12**, straddling a page break, and Beal says she dreamt it **at** his birth, not before it. **Flagged as hagiography and kept:** the register of this atlas is that the canon is true, and the campa reports the dream as the biographies do.
- Also used, from Beal Book I unless noted: Li Chang at Guazhou (p. 14), the cry at the watch-tower (p. 19), the desert vow (p. 22), the Nalanda hierarchy naming Silabhadra (Book III, p. 112), and his answer to Harsha from *Si-Yu-Ki* Book V.
- **Chain of custody.** The two Li Yung-hsi (1959) quotations were seen **only through Wikipedia's quotation with citation**, never against the volume. Both are used, and `quote_source` says so.

## 10. Honest gaps

1. **No primary Tang-era source** for the Gobi crossing. Everything dramatic between Chang'an and Turfan rests on Huili, a disciple writing devotionally.
2. **The return journey is nearly blank**: three stops for two years of travel.
3. **Yuhua Palace has no confirmed coordinates**; two lenses disagree by 20 km, neither right.
4. **Kanchipuram and Valabhi**: presence disputed. The southern reach of the map may be reportage, not travel.
5. **The 1942 Nanjing relics tradition could not be reverified**; the Wikipedia section was inaccessible.
6. **74 versus 75 translated works**, and **twenty versus thirty horses** (Wikipedia's twenty for the homeward cargo, Huili's thirty for the Gaochang gift): both left standing as reported.
