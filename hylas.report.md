# Hylas, pantomime dancer of Augustan Rome: research report

*Compiled 2026-08-29 for `hylas.journey.json`. Subject: the pantomime dancer Hylas, pupil and rival of Pylades of Cilicia, active at Rome under Augustus (27 BCE - 14 CE). Birth, birthplace, parentage, legal status, death and burial are all unrecorded.*

**Legend.** **[A]** = attested, source named. **[R]** = reconstruction, modern hypothesis, tradition, or inference. Contradictions are flagged and left standing, not adjudicated. Unreachable sources are named with the reason.

**The size of the dossier.** Everything securely known about this man fits in two ancient passages: Suetonius, *Divus Augustus* 45.4 (one subordinate clause), and Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 2.7.12-19 (four short anecdotes, three of them about somebody correcting him). A third passage, Cassius Dio 54.17.4-5, is routinely dragged in and does not name him. The journey file is built accordingly: nine of its twenty-nine stops are about what is missing.

---

## 1. Origin: Salmacis, or nothing

**[R] Birthplace unknown.** No ancient source states where Hylas was born, when, or to whom. The one candidate is epigraphic.

**[R] The Theoros inscription, CIL VI 10115 = ILS 5197.** A pantomime named Theoros boasts of victory over four rivals: *Pylades aus Kilikien, Nomius aus Syrien, Pierus aus Tibur und Hylas aus Salamakis* (verbatim from the Theatrum lexicon entry, theatrum.de/2670, which is what was reachable). If this Hylas is Pylades's pupil, his origin is Salmacis, the spring and quarter adjoining Halicarnassus in Caria (modern Bodrum).

**Contradictions and corrections carried forward, not resolved:**

- *Object type.* Descriptions of the piece as an inscribed **terracotta object found near Rome** are **unsupported** in the source actually consulted, which calls it a **Grabinschrift aus Tibur**, a grave inscription from Tibur (Tivoli). Both phrases have been dropped from the journey file.
- *Findspot.* The CIL **VI** number assigns the stone to the **Rome** corpus; the lexicon says **Tibur**. Direct tension, not resolved here. EDCS, which might settle it, has migrated from `db.edcs.eu` to `edcs.hist.uzh.ch` and **no record page was reachable this session**. The journey file pins the stop at Tibur and states the dispute in `date_confidence`.
- *Praenomen.* "Gaius Theoros" is **unverified**; the reachable source gives only "Theoros".
- *The identification itself.* The inscription does **not** call its Hylas anyone's pupil, and Hylas was a reusable stage name. A modern epigraphic hypothesis, tagged **[R]** throughout.

**[R] The stage-name custom.** Modern scholarship (Edith Hall, in Hall and Wyles, eds., *New Directions in Ancient Pantomime*, Oxford, 2008, **not consulted directly this session**) notes that imperial pantomimes repeatedly bore mythological names: Pylades, Bathyllus, Paris, Hylas. No ancient source states that "Hylas" was adopted rather than given.

**[A] The mythological Hylas is a different figure.** Heracles's companion, taken by nymphs at a spring, referred to by Juvenal, *Satires* 1.164. Distinct from the dancer and must not be conflated. Juvenal's text was **not re-fetched this session**; the content is standard and flagged as not independently re-verified.

## 2. Training: the school of Pylades

**[A] Pylades taught him.** Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 2.7.12: *Nec Pylades histrio nobis omittendus est, qui clarus in opere suo fuit temporibus Augusti et Hylam discipulum usque ad aequalitatis contentionem eruditione provexit.* "Nor should we omit the actor Pylades, famous in his art in the time of Augustus, who by his teaching brought his pupil Hylas up to a contest of equality." The sole surviving connected account of Hylas's training, and undated.

**[A] The public divided.** *Saturnalia* 2.7.13: *Populus deinde inter utriusque suffragia divisus est.*

**[R] What a pantomime did.** The technical account used in the journey file (one masked solo dancer taking all roles, a sung libretto carried by a singer or chorus, instrumental accompaniment including the *scabellum*) rests on **modern scholarship not consulted directly this session**: Jory 1981 and 2004, Lada-Richards 2007, Hall and Wyles 2008. **No ancient source describes Hylas's own technique.**

**Dating.** Macrobius gives no year for anything. Every date in the journey file's segments 2 through 7 is arbitrary, placed loosely inside Augustus's reign to order the sequence, and says so in its own `date_confidence` field.

## 3. The Agamemnon

**[A] The performance.** *Saturnalia* 2.7.13. Hylas dances a *canticum* ending on the Greek phrase for "the great Agamemnon" and represents greatness as physical size: *sublimem ingentemque Hylas velut metiebatur*, "Hylas seemed as it were to measure out something lofty and huge."

**[A] The correction.** Pylades, watching from the audience, shouts in Greek: Σὺ μακρὸν οὐ μέγαν ποιεῖς, "You are making him tall, not great."

**Evidence caveat on the Greek.** The Latin narration was located directly in an Internet Archive OCR scan of the *Saturnalia*, in which **the Greek letters are corrupted and read as nonsense**. The Greek wording used here comes from the Penelope/Thayer text. The Greek clausula of the song itself (*ton megan Agamemnona*) could **not** be verified character-for-character in any reachable scan and is not quoted in the journey file.

**[A] The crowd forces the master to dance.** *Saturnalia* 2.7.14: *Tunc eum populus coegit idem saltare canticum cumque ad locum venisset quem reprehenderat, expressit cogitantem, nihil magis ratus magno duci convenire quam pro omnibus cogitare.* Pylades portrays Agamemnon thinking, on the grounds that nothing suits a great commander better than to think on behalf of everyone.

**[R] Venue.** **No source names a theatre for any performance of Hylas.** The journey file uses the Theatre of Pompey for the Agamemnon stops, Rome's principal permanent stone theatre in the period, and says so in each `date_confidence`; the pin is inherited byte-identical from `julius_caesar.journey.json` (41.8953, 12.4769).

**[R] The language question.** That the exchange is reported in Greek is attested. What it implies about the libretto's language, or about Hylas's own first language, is inference. Jory reads it as evidence that meaning had to arrive through gesture whatever the sung text's language; it does **not** prove Hylas was Greek-literate or a native speaker. Jory not consulted directly.

## 4. The Oedipus

**[A] The episode, in full.** *Saturnalia* 2.7.15: *Saltabat Hylas Oedipodem, et Pylades hac voce securitatem saltantis castigavit: Σὺ βλέπεις.* Hylas was dancing Oedipus, and Pylades rebuked the dancer's *securitas* with the cry "You are looking."

**Correction carried forward, and this one matters.** A widely repeated gloss has Pylades objecting that Hylas "moves too confidently for a blind man," i.e. that the episode danced was post-blinding. **Macrobius says none of that.** He says only that the dancer's *securitas* was rebuked, never mentions blindness, and never specifies an episode. The blind-Oedipus reading is a **modern inference [R]**, plausible and probably right, but not attested content. The journey file states this in the stop's `date_confidence` and keeps the campa on the attested side of the line.

**[A] No title survives** for either the Oedipus or the Agamemnon piece. **[R/gap] No imperial pantomime libretto survives complete**, a fact of the genre and not of Hylas in particular.

## 5. The riot and the emperor

**[A] The seditio.** *Saturnalia* 2.7.19: *Idem cum propter populi seditionem pro contentione inter se Hylamque habita concitatam indignationem excepisset Augusti, respondit: Καὶ ἀχαριστεῖς βασιλεῦ· ἔασον αὐτοὺς περὶ ἡμᾶς ἀσχολεῖσθαι.* A popular rising arose from the contention between Pylades and Hylas; Augustus's indignation fell on Pylades; Pylades replied, "And you are ungrateful, king: let them busy themselves about us."

**Corrections carried forward:**

- *The wording of the reply.* Macrobius does **not** say it is "to the emperor's advantage," and says **nothing** about "rather than politics." That framing is imported from Dio and does not belong in a quotation of Macrobius. The two texts are quoted apart in the journey file.
- *The transliteration.* An earlier rendering gave ἀχάριστος (adjective). Jan's edition (archive.org `bub_gb_IjNCAAAAIAAJ`, p. 256, line 31465) prints **ἀχαριστεῖς**, a second-person verb. Corrected transliteration: *Kai acharisteis, basileu; eason autous peri hemas ascholeisthai.* With the clean Greek now in hand this quotation moves from **[R]** to **[A]**.
- *The dating.* There is **no** warrant for placing the seditio in 18 BCE. See section 6.

**[A] The Suetonius context, three punishments in one passage.** *Divus Augustus* 45.4 records in immediate succession: the actor **Stephanio** whipped through all three theatres and banished for having a married woman disguised as a boy wait on him; **Hylas** scourged in his own atrium on a praetor's complaint; **Pylades** expelled from Rome and from Italy for pointing out a heckler with his finger. **Suetonius does not state that these three cases are connected**, and gives no date for any of them. Stephanio's and Pylades's punishments are other people's, carried in Hylas's file only inside his own stop for Augustus's discipline of performers, never staged as scenes of their own.

## 6. The contradiction that must not be resolved: Macrobius vs Dio

**[A] Dio's text.** Cassius Dio, *Roman History* 54.17.4-5 (verified via LacusCurtius): Pylades, previously exiled for causing sedition, is restored; Augustus rebukes him for quarrelling with **Bathyllus**, the favourite of Maecenas; Pylades replies that it is to Caesar's advantage that the people devote their spare time to performers. **Dio never mentions Hylas.**

**The conflict.** Macrobius attaches a reply of the same *kind* to a **Pylades-Hylas** rivalry, in **different words**; Dio attaches it to a **Pylades-Bathyllus** rivalry. Two traditions about one witticism, not reconcilable from the sources. **Neither is used to fill the other's gaps.** The journey file gives the conflict its own stop and quotes both texts separately.

**Correction to the dating warrant.** It has been said that Dio dates the episode by the consulship of **Gaius Furnius and Gaius Silanus**. He does not. Those consuls belong to **17 BCE**, and Dio names them at **54.18.1**, which *opens* the following year ("These were the occurrences of that year. In the consulship of Gaius Furnius and Gaius Silanus..."). The anecdote sits at **54.17.4-5**, inside Dio's account of the *preceding* year, **18 BCE**. So 18 BCE is right for **Dio's episode**, but by narrative sequence, not by that consulship, and 18 BCE has **no** warrant at all as a date for anything involving Hylas.

**[R] The modern legend.** Retellings have merged the two passages and produced an **exile of Hylas in 18 BCE**. No ancient source reports any exile of Hylas. Flagged as a modern inference, not an ancient claim.

## 7. The flogging

**[A] Suetonius, *Divus Augustus* 45.4:** *Hylan pantomimum querente praetore in atrio domus suae nemine excluso flagellis verberarit.* Rolfe's Loeb (1913, via LacusCurtius): "Hylas, a pantomimic actor, was publicly scourged in the atrium of his own house, on complaint of a praetor."

**Correction carried forward.** The transmitted Latin reads **verberarit**, perfect subjunctive, the clause sitting inside the *ut*-clause governed by *coercuit*; many modern quotations silently print *verberavit*. It is a **subordinate clause, not a freestanding sentence**. Text checked at `thelatinlibrary.com/suetonius/suet.aug.html`.

**Attested:** the flogging; its location in Hylas's own atrium; that nobody was excluded (*nemine excluso*); the praetor's complaint; that Augustus ordered it. **Not attested, and stated as gaps:** the charge, the praetor's name, whether there was a hearing, guilt, the number of blows, injuries, the date, the outcome, and **any connection whatever to the riot**. The journey file's flogging stops make no causal claim linking the seditio to the punishment.

**[A/gap] After the flogging: nothing.** No later performance, no retirement, no exile, no death notice, no tomb, no epitaph, no year. Checked directly against both primary passages; the absence held up.

## 8. Gaps, stated as gaps

- **[R/gap]** No ancient source gives Hylas's birth name, birth date, birthplace (independent of the disputed inscription), parents, legal status (freeborn, enslaved, or freed), owner, patron, spouse, or children; nor any date or circumstance of death, retirement, or activity after the Augustan notices.
- **[R/gap]** No papyrus, contract, libretto manuscript, letter, household record, legal dossier, or securely labelled portrait, bust, mask or relief of the dancer has been identified by modern scholarship. Independently re-checked by search; nothing surfaced.
- **[A]** No monograph or biography of Hylas exists; he appears in modern scholarship only as a supporting case inside studies of the genre. Independently corroborated: searches returned no dedicated study, and the standard bibliography (Jory 1981, Jory 2004, Lada-Richards 2007, Hall and Wyles 2008, Webb 2008) offers only chapters treating him inside larger arguments. **[R]** He has no dedicated entry in general reference literature in any language checked; the name is occupied by the myth.

## 9. Homonyms, kept apart

Three men and one myth share the name and are **not** the dancer:

1. **[A] Gnaeus Pomponius Hylas**, freedman, of the columbarium on the Via di Porta Latina, Rome. Tomb c. 14-54 CE (dated by niches for a freedman of Tiberius and one of Claudia Octavia), bought and redecorated by him for himself and his wife; excavated 1831 by Pietro Campana. Mosaic: *CN(aei) POMPONI HYLAE ET POMPONIAE CN(aei) L(ibertae) VITALINIS*. **Correction carried forward:** the praenomen is **Gnaeus**, not Publius, and the road is the **Via di Porta Latina**, not the Via Latina.
2. **[R] Marcus Ulpius Hylas**, a performer of the Trajanic period (98-117 CE). Distinction drawn in prosopographical scholarship (Leppin, *Histrionen*, 1992); **no primary inscription or study naming him was reachable this session**.
3. **[A] The mythological Hylas**, companion of Heracles, taken by nymphs at a spring (Juvenal, *Satires* 1.164).
4. **[A] The modern crowding-out.** J. W. Waterhouse, *Hylas and the Nymphs* (1896), Manchester Art Gallery. **Correction carried forward:** the gallery's coordinates are **53.4786, -2.2414** (Mosley Street), not 53.4794, -2.2453, which sits c. 280 m WNW toward Albert Square.

## 10. Interlocks

**Named in a campa (real relations only):**

- **`pylades_of_cilicia`**: teacher and rival, the other half of every attested episode. Named unavoidably and repeatedly: there is no account of Hylas that is not also an account of Pylades. Queued, not yet researched.
- **`bathyllus_of_alexandria`**: named once, in the stop carrying the Macrobius/Dio conflict. Not a life relation and not presented as one: in Dio's version Bathyllus occupies exactly the position Hylas occupies in Macrobius's, sharing the same rival and the same anecdote, which is why the two have been conflated for centuries. Queued, not yet researched.

**Considered and deliberately not named** (shared geography or shared reign is not a relation, and the map already shows co-location): `herodotus` (Halicarnassus, five centuries earlier; his pin 37.0355, 27.4249 inherited byte-identical, his name in no campa); `virgil`, `strabo`, `cicero`, `julius_caesar` (same city, adjacent decades, no attested contact; the Rome pin 41.8925, 12.4853, the Palatine pin 41.8894, 12.488 and the Theatre-of-Pompey-complex pin 41.8953, 12.4769 all inherited byte-identical); `mnester` (queued, a pantomime of the next generation, no contact claimed anywhere).

Two pool items describe **other people's** presence and were handled under the stop-ownership rule rather than deleted. **Stephanio's whipping and Pylades's banishment** (Suetonius 45.4) get no scenes; they are folded into Hylas's own stop for Augustus's discipline of performers. **Dio's Pylades-Bathyllus episode** is not staged as a scene either; it became a stop about the state of *Hylas's own record*, pinned at Rome and dated to Dio's composition, because flagging the contradiction is required and burying it would misrepresent the evidence.
