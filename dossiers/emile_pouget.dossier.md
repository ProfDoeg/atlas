# Émile Pouget: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier concerns the French anarchist journalist and revolutionary syndicalist **Jean Joseph Émile Pouget (1860–1931)**. It draws principally on the revised *Maitron* biographical notice reproduced by the Centre international de recherches sur l’anarchisme (CIRA), contemporary trial reporting, CGT congress records, Pouget’s publications, library catalogues, and testimony by associates such as Paul Delesalle.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a civil record, judicial record, congress proceeding, contemporaneous publication, or comparably direct evidence.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a named contemporary or responsible later historian but not independently established here from a primary record.
- **Disputed:** credible sources offer incompatible versions.
- **Allegation:** an accusation made without a conclusive judicial finding.
- **Interpretation:** a historian’s characterization rather than a directly verifiable event.
- **Myth or error:** a recurrent story contradicted by stronger evidence.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full civil name:** **Jean Joseph Émile Pouget**.
- **Usual name:** **Émile Pouget**.
- **Birth:** **12 October 1860**, Pont-de-Salars, Aveyron, France.
- **Death:** **21 July 1931**, at Lozère, then a locality in Seine-et-Oise and now part of Palaiseau, Essonne.
- **Nationality:** French.
- **Occupations:** department-store employee; bookseller’s representative or book broker; journalist; pamphleteer; newspaper editor; trade-union official; independent publisher and advertising/catalogue agent.
- **Political descriptions used by Pouget or historians:** anarchist, libertarian communist, antimilitarist, revolutionary syndicalist; frequently classified retrospectively as an early anarcho-syndicalist.
- **Principal editorial identities and aliases:**
  - **Le Père Peinard**, the fictional cobbler-narrator and editorial persona of his newspaper.
  - **“Gniaff journaleux”**, roughly “cobbler-newspaperman,” a self-description attached to that persona.
  - **Émile Boiteaux**, clandestine correspondence name used in London.
  - **Blacksmile**, name used for receiving mail at his London address.
- **Contemporary nickname:** **“l’Éminence grise de la CGT”**, used by Maurice Leclercq and Édouard Girod de Fléaux to describe his unobtrusive but substantial influence.
- **No verified title of nobility, academic degree, state office, decoration, patent, or formal award has been located.**

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and social background

**Documented/reported fact:** Pouget was born into a provincial bourgeois household. His father, **Jean Joseph Louis Pouget**, was a notary; his mother was **Marie Clémentine Boissonnade**.

The older *Maitron* notice placed his father’s death in **1863**. The revised notice by Colette Chambelland, Guillaume Davranche, and Josef Ulla gives **1866**. The civil record should govern, but it was not directly legible through the consulted online materials; the date therefore remains **disputed between 1863 and 1866**.

His mother married **Philippe Vergely**, a conductor or works supervisor in the Ponts et Chaussées, in **1868**. Some derivative accounts call him “Jean Vergely”; the revised *Maitron* notice identifies him as Philippe. Vergely was an ardent republican and founded the departmental weekly **L’Aveyron républicain**. His writing reportedly caused his dismissal from public employment. He died in **1875**.

### Sibling

Pouget had a brother, **Isidore Pouget**, who became a lycée and later university teacher at Rennes and Algiers. Isidore died at Salles-la-Source, Aveyron, on **30 June 1933**.

No reliable evidence of additional siblings was found.

### Partners and marriage

Pouget’s long-term companion was **Stéphanie Boiteux**, called affectionately **“la mère Peinard”** by associates. She helped him through the years of anarchist journalism and shared his London exile. She died on **29 July 1904**. The exact legal status of their relationship is unclear; sources consistently call her his *compagne*, not his wife.

His later partner was **Augustine Caroline Jamaux**, born in Paris’s 2nd arrondissement on **6 September 1867**. She had reportedly been a friend of Stéphanie Boiteux. Pouget and Jamaux married in Paris’s 18th arrondissement on **17 April 1915**. Their witnesses were the artists **Maximilien Luce**, **Paul Rousseau**, and **Hippolyte Petitjean**.

No children of either relationship are identified in the consulted records. It is therefore safest to state that **no children are presently documented**, not that none existed.

Following Pouget’s death, Augustine moved by late 1931 to **12 chemin du Telemly, Algiers**, where members of the Pouget family lived. She died in 1937 according to later biographical references.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1860–1877

Pouget spent his early childhood in Aveyron. His father’s early death, his mother’s remarriage, and Vergely’s militant republicanism created a politically active household.

In November **1871**, Vergely took the eleven-year-old Pouget to sessions of the Rodez trial of participants in the **Narbonne Commune**. Pouget was reportedly especially impressed by the testimony of the former Communard **Émile Digeon**. Digeon later became his mentor and described Pouget as a **“spiritual son.”**

As a pupil at the **lycée de Rodez**, Pouget produced a tiny handwritten newspaper, **Le Lycéen républicain**, traditionally dated to about **1875**, when he was fourteen or fifteen. It is the earliest known expression of his lifelong interest in oppositional journalism.

Vergely’s death in 1875 made continued schooling financially impossible. Pouget left the lycée without a known diploma. In **1877**, aged sixteen or seventeen, he moved to Paris and obtained employment at the **Bon Marché** department store.

## Education and Formation

Pouget had a partial secondary education but no documented university training. His political and literary formation was largely autodidactic.

Important formative influences included:

- **Philippe Vergely:** militant republican journalism and hostility to conservative authority.
- **Émile Digeon:** direct connection to the Commune tradition, organizer and mentor.
- **La Révolution sociale:** an anarchist periodical that the revised *Maitron* notice identifies as decisive in Pouget’s conversion to anarchism at about seventeen.
- **Le Révolté:** the anarchist newspaper associated with Peter Kropotkin and Jean Grave.
- **Jacques-René Hébert’s Père Duchesne:** stylistic precedent for a fictional popular voice using slang, invective, and profanity.
- **Henri Rochefort’s La Lanterne:** model for the small pamphlet format of *Le Père Peinard*.
- British trade-union practice, especially **boycotting** and **“go canny”**, encountered or studied during exile and translated into Pouget’s vocabulary of *boycottage* and *sabotage*.

His writings also drew on the First International’s division between anarchist and parliamentary or “authoritarian” socialism. Pouget repeatedly opposed the electoral Marxism of **Jules Guesde**, although his mature syndicalism adopted class-struggle language also associated with Marxism.

## Early Employment and Organization, 1877–1883

After work at the Bon Marché, Pouget attended radical meetings and anarchist groups. In **1879**, at Digeon’s urging, he participated in creating the **Syndicat des employés de commerce parisiens**, described in some accounts more narrowly as a union of textile or shop employees.

In April **1882**, he was listed as proprietor-manager of the first issue of the **Bulletin de la chambre syndicale fédérale des employés**, headquartered at **347 rue Saint-Martin**.

After the Communard amnesty, Pouget and Digeon joined an anarchist circle meeting in the premises of **“père Rousseau,”** a wine merchant at **131 rue Saint-Martin**. Within this circle Digeon principally composed the insurrectionary antimilitarist pamphlet **À l’armée**. Pouget arranged its publication through the employees’ union and distributed copies sent from Geneva by **Georges Herzig**.

Pouget was exempted from military service because of a **varicose vein**. This is the only clearly documented medical condition from his early life.

By 1883 he was working as a **book broker or travelling bookseller’s representative**.

### Alleged 1881 London congress

The older *Maitron* biography and many derivative accounts say that Pouget represented French anarchists at the London congress of **14 July 1881**.

**Revised finding:** the updated *Maitron* notice rejects this identification. It traces the claim to an undated police report probably written about ten years later. Pouget’s attendance should therefore be treated as a **discredited or at least unproved claim**, not a documented journey.

## The Unemployed Demonstration, Trial, and Imprisonment, 1883–1886

On **9 March 1883**—frequently but incorrectly given as 8 March—the Paris carpenters’ union convened an outdoor meeting of unemployed workers on the Esplanade des Invalides. Police dispersed the meeting. Pouget and former Communard **Louise Michel** then led a column toward the Faubourg Saint-Antoine by way of Boulevard Saint-Germain.

Three bakeries were looted while demonstrators shouted **“Du pain, du travail ou du plomb”** (“Bread, work, or lead”). At Place Maubert, police confronted the march. Pouget was arrested; contemporary sympathetic testimony says he attempted to prevent Michel’s arrest.

A search of Pouget’s residence produced:

- a six-shot revolver;
- bottles containing chemical or explosive substances;
- approximately 600 copies of *À l’armée*;
- according to contemporary trial reporting, files sharpened like daggers, copying equipment, money, and alleged incendiary devices.

The existence of these objects is documented in trial reportage. Their intended use was disputed. No completed bombing or attack was attributed to Pouget in that proceeding.

The trial took place before the Seine assize court on **22–24 June 1883**. Pouget openly declared his anarchism. The defendants were tried partly for directing the bakery pillaging and partly for distributing antimilitarist propaganda advocating military insubordination.

- Louise Michel received six years’ imprisonment.
- Pouget was convicted of incitement to armed pillage and distribution of antimilitarist propaganda.
- His sentence was **eight years’ imprisonment plus ten years of police surveillance**.
- He was confined as an ordinary criminal at **Melun prison**.

Pouget served approximately three years and was freed under the **1886 amnesty**. Claims that he served the full eight years are erroneous.

## Return to Journalism, 1886–1889

After prison, Pouget resumed book representation and associated with the literary and anarchist milieu of Montmartre, including **Charles Malato**.

In May **1888**, with associates including **Constant Martin**, he launched the anarchist fortnightly **Ça ira**. It opposed General Boulanger and attacked Blanquists who had rallied to Boulangism.

To transform the paper into a daily, Pouget helped establish a **variable-capital limited company** in September 1888, planning to issue **10,000 bonds at five francs each**. This is the clearest business or investment structure connected to him. The project collapsed after prosecution over the article **“Silence aux pauvres”** in the tenth and final issue, January **1889**. There is no evidence of fraud; it was a failed, politically exposed publishing venture.

## *Le Père Peinard*, 1889–1894

Pouget issued the first *Père Peinard* on **24 February 1889**. Initially a sixteen-page pamphlet sold for two sous, it used a fictional cobbler’s Parisian argot to address workers as *bons bougres* and *bonnes bougresses*. Pouget was the sole or principal author of the earliest issues, but unsigned contributions and later collaborators make attribution of every article uncertain.

Editorial and residential locations included:

- **120 rue Lafayette**;
- **31 rue Cadet**, from September 1890;
- **4 bis rue d’Orsel**, from January 1891;
- Pouget’s home at **14 rue Clauzel** and later **24 rue Véron**.

The paper changed size several times to facilitate street sales. Its back page carried work by artists including **Maximilien Luce**, **Henri-Gabriel Ibels**, **Lucien Pissarro**, and **Adolphe Willette**.

The paper attacked the state, parliament, army, Church, employers, colonialism, and electoral socialism. It treated the **expropriatory general strike** as the preferred revolutionary method. Pouget’s issue of 3 November 1889 declared that the general strike was now the essential route to social revolution.

Police files recorded about **500 subscribers in 1894**, but Paul Delesalle recalled a much broader circulation through street sales, posters, and worker-to-worker distribution. Some *Père Peinard au Populo* placards reportedly exceeded **20,000 copies**.

Pouget participated in the **Cercle anarchiste international**, a major Paris meeting place. Prosecutions were frequent. He served intermittent terms at **Sainte-Pélagie prison**, continuing to submit copy from his cell. A succession of nominal managers also faced prosecution.

### Revolutionary violence

**Documented position:** Pouget’s language could celebrate revolt and legitimize property damage, but he did not consistently advocate the individual bombing campaign of 1892–1894. In September 1892, after Ravachol’s execution, *Le Père Peinard* emphasized the general strike rather than “propaganda by the deed.”

**Contradictory evidence:** on 1 January 1893, Pouget praised Ravachol for applying “little pots” to the social question and opening a path for individual initiative. His journalism could therefore romanticize a bomber even while strategically preferring collective economic action.

No evidence consulted establishes that Pouget personally planned or carried out a bombing.

## Repression, Algeria, London, and the Trial of the Thirty, 1894–1895

Police searched and damaged the *Père Peinard* office and Pouget’s home in January **1894**. The almanac was seized, and the newspaper ceased after issue 253 on **21 February 1894**.

Pouget fled. A period in **Algiers** before London is reported but not fully resolved:

- The revised *Maitron* notice says he apparently remained there until September 1894.
- Records identify an Émile Pouget as secretary of the Algiers Bourse du Travail between April 1894 and April 1896.
- That term overlaps his documented London residence and return to France, making either the dates or the identification uncertain.

The Algerian episode is consequently a **probable but incompletely documented stay**.

Pouget and Stéphanie Boiteux settled at **23 King Edward Street, London**, and apparently hosted **Augustin Hamon**. Pouget later shared premises on Pentonville Road, Islington, with the Italian anarchist **Antonio Agresti**. He received mail as “Blacksmile” and used “Émile Boiteaux” to evade police surveillance.

At the **Trial of the Thirty**, 6–12 August 1894, prosecutors attempted to connect anarchist intellectuals, journalists, and illegalists as an *association de malfaiteurs*. Pouget was tried in absentia. On **31 October 1894**, he received a default sentence of **twenty years’ forced labor**.

This was a judicial conviction by default, not proof that Pouget personally committed the thefts or bombings associated with other defendants. The prosecution’s broader conspiracy theory failed against most defendants and has generally been treated as part of the exceptional-law campaign against anarchism.

In London Pouget produced eight miniature issues of *Le Père Peinard*, September 1894–January 1895. In November 1894 he expressly criticized the failed **Greenwich Observatory bombing**, distancing himself from *bombisme*.

## Return to France and *La Sociale*, 1895–1896

Following an amnesty, Pouget returned through Boulogne-sur-Mer and was arrested on **5 February 1895**. He challenged the default judgment, was retried, and was **acquitted on 18 February**.

He settled at **15 rue Ravignan**—rendered “Lavieuville” in some OCR-derived accounts—in Paris’s 18th arrondissement. A police report of 16 April said that anarchism was reconstituting itself at Montmartre, where Pouget had “planted his flag.”

From **11 May 1895 to 18 October 1896**, he published 76 issues of **La Sociale**, based at **23 rue des Trois-Frères**. He also resumed the *Almanach du Père Peinard*, publishing annual volumes through 1898.

With **Fernand Pelloutier**, **Bernard Lazare**, **Augustin Hamon**, and **Errico Malatesta**, Pouget pursued an alliance of anarchists and antiparliamentary socialists based on syndicalism and the general strike. An attempt to establish a daily, **La Clameur**, with **Jean Allemane** failed in spring 1896.

At the international socialist congress in London in July **1896**, Pouget held mandates from metalworkers in Beauvais, masons and stonecutters in Cognac, and slate workers in Angers-Trélazé. He became a spokesman for the French Allemanist-libertarian coalition and participated in parallel meetings at St Martin’s Hall. He distributed **Variations guesdistes**, a compilation designed to expose contradictions in Jules Guesde’s statements.

## Return of *Le Père Peinard* and the Turn to Syndicalism, 1896–1900

The renewed *Père Peinard* appeared from **25 October 1896**.

At the CGT congress in Toulouse, **20–25 September 1897**, Pouget represented the hand-sewn shoemakers’ union of Paris, the Vienne union federation, and Amiens Bourse du Travail. He joined the commission on boycott and sabotage with **Paul Delesalle**, Pierre Narcisse, Rémy Thierrart, and Bernadac.

The commission’s report was **probably drafted in advance by Pouget**, but that authorship is an informed historical attribution rather than a signed fact. The congress adopted it unanimously.

### Who introduced “sabotage”?

The simplified claim that Pouget single-handedly “invented sabotage” is misleading.

- The practices predated him.
- British workers used **“go canny”** for deliberate restriction of output.
- Pouget publicized the practice in France and helped render it as **“sabottage”**, later *sabotage*.
- Paul Delesalle appears to have presented or read the report at Toulouse.
- Pouget became its most influential French theoretician.

His formula **“À mauvaise paye, mauvais travail”** (“Bad pay, bad work”) was connected to the story of workers shortening their shovels after a wage reduction—“small pay, small shovel.” Pouget’s sabotage encompassed slow work, obedience to rules, refusal of extra effort, disclosure of adulteration or fraud, and in some situations damage to production. He urged avoiding injury to consumers and workers, although not every example in his writings was non-destructive.

## The Dreyfus Affair

Pouget initially regarded the campaign for **Captain Alfred Dreyfus** as a bourgeois diversion. After Émile Zola’s *J’accuse*, the 16 January 1898 *Père Peinard* demanded comparable concern for imprisoned anarchists.

Some scholarship and later summaries report that Pouget’s early commentary employed **antisemitic stereotypes**. Such language was present in parts of the nineteenth-century radical press. The consulted revised notice documents his political reversal more clearly than it inventories every slur; individual unsigned articles therefore require attribution caution.

In July **1898**, with assistance from the young lawyer **Léon Blum**, Pouget published an analysis of the 1893–1894 exceptional laws in *La Revue blanche*. **Francis de Pressensé**, president of the Ligue des droits de l’homme, provided a preface when it appeared as *Les Lois scélérates de 1893–1894* in 1899.

Following exposure of Lieutenant-Colonel Henry’s forged evidence, Pouget joined the Dreyfusard struggle. On 9 October 1898 he called for workers to oppose antisemites and nationalists. He co-signed the **Coalition révolutionnaire** manifesto and joined the radical defense of republican liberties.

He did not become an admirer or personal associate of Dreyfus. His support was framed as opposition to military, clerical, nationalist, and judicial reaction.

## *Journal du peuple* and the End of *Le Père Peinard*, 1899–1900

In May **1899**, with *Le Père Peinard* in financial difficulty, Pouget became editorial secretary of **Sébastien Faure’s Journal du peuple**, a daily paper of the Dreyfusard extreme left.

On **11 June 1899**, he participated in the republican demonstration at Longchamp after nationalists attacked President Émile Loubet. Pouget kept the struggling daily operating until it closed on **3 December 1899**.

A third series of *Le Père Peinard* began on **14 January 1900**. Pouget also helped organize an international antiparliamentary congress scheduled for 19–22 September 1900, but the French government prohibited it.

## CGT Leadership, 1900–1908

The CGT decided at its Paris congress to create a confederal newspaper. **La Voix du peuple** first appeared on **1 December 1900**, with Pouget effectively responsible for its editorial work. CGT historical collections list him as editor from 1900 to 1908.

In June **1901**, Pouget led a CGT delegation to London for a joint demonstration with British trade unions against the prospect of Anglo-French war. He co-chaired a meeting of approximately 2,000 people at Shoreditch Town Hall.

In September 1901 he answered **Jean Jaurès’s** attack on the “illusion” of the general strike. Articles published under the name of the CGT General Strike Committee were, according to Achille Leroy, actually composed by Pouget. They later formed **Grève générale révolutionnaire et grève générale réformiste**.

On **26 November 1901**, nominated by the South-East federation, Pouget was elected CGT assistant secretary by **52 votes out of 82**. The position and editorship of *La Voix du peuple* were combined. His salary was **250 francs per month**, the only precisely documented regular remuneration found.

After the 1902 Montpellier unity congress he remained editor-in-chief. Pouget, **Victor Griffuelhes**, and **Georges Yvetot** represented the CGT at the 1903 international trade-union conference in Dublin, advocating antimilitarism and the general strike.

Pouget and Griffuelhes became the organizational center of the CGT’s revolutionary majority. Pouget also wrote for:

- **L’Action directe**, July 1903–February 1905;
- **L’Avant-Garde**, April 1905–March 1906;
- **Le Mouvement socialiste**, including extensive congress analyses.

### Opposition within the CGT

From 1903, reformists accused anarchists of capturing the confederal bureau and using *La Voix du peuple* for excessively antigovernmental purposes. Pouget was a principal target. This was a documented internal political accusation, not a criminal finding.

At the **Bourges congress of 1904**, Pouget argued that the reformist–revolutionary division could be overcome through common class action toward capitalist expropriation. He drafted the amendment calling for workers, beginning 1 May 1906, to refuse work beyond eight hours.

He increasingly avoided publicly identifying himself specifically as an anarchist. He did not repudiate anarchism; instead, he argued that union unity should supersede ideological labels. He described the CGT as the **“parti du Travail”**, with syndicalism as its own doctrine.

Pouget unsuccessfully advocated admitting professional journalists to the CGT and argued for inclusion of intellectual workers, small peasants, fishers, and artisans who owned tools but remained economically proletarianized.

On **22 November 1904**, the miners’ federation nominated him successfully for assistant secretary of the federations section.

### International isolation

In 1905, German trade-union secretary **Carl Legien** refused Pouget and Griffuelhes’s request to place the general strike, antimilitarism, and the eight-hour day on the agenda of an international conference. The CGT boycotted it, contributing to its isolation from predominantly reformist European union centers.

## The Eight-Hour Campaign and Charter of Amiens

Pouget was a principal strategist of the eighteen-month campaign culminating on **1 May 1906**, when workers were urged to stop after eight hours. Delesalle later praised Pouget’s sustained inventiveness in keeping the campaign before workers.

At the CGT congress in Amiens in October **1906**, Pouget, Griffuelhes, Delesalle, and **Louis Niel**, among others, helped formulate the resolution on relations between unions and political organizations.

The resulting text asserted:

- immediate economic improvement and eventual complete emancipation as labor’s **“double task”**;
- capitalist expropriation as the long-term goal;
- the general strike as the transformative method;
- the union as both present organization of resistance and future unit of production and distribution;
- organizational independence from political parties and sects.

The motion passed by **830 votes to 8, with one abstention**. The label **“Charter of Amiens”** became customary only later, around 1912.

Pouget was an important co-drafter, not its sole author. Claims that he alone wrote the Charter exceed the documentation.

Despite his antimilitarist history, Pouget abstained on Yvetot’s separate antimilitarist motion in the interest of congress unity. He described organized labor as *a-patriotic*, *a-religious*, and *a-political*. This neutralist formulation provoked libertarian critics.

In July **1907**, **Émile Janvion** accused him of making syndicalism insufficiently antiparliamentary and ideologically empty. Pouget replied that the CGT had moved beyond the old formulation and was revolutionary through the direct action of its members.

Pouget declined a reported invitation to the 1907 Amsterdam anarchist congress and allowed the younger **Pierre Monatte** to defend French revolutionary syndicalism there.

## Prosecutions and the Draveil–Villeneuve-Saint-Georges Crisis, 1907–1908

The CGT published the poster **Gouvernement d’assassins** during the southern winegrowers’ conflict of June 1907. Pouget and eleven other militants were tried before an assize court on **20–22 February 1908**. Defended by Maître Bonzon, all twelve were **acquitted**.

After violent confrontations connected with the Draveil quarry strike and the deaths at Villeneuve-Saint-Georges on **30 July 1908**, the Corbeil prosecutor issued arrest warrants for roughly thirty CGT leaders.

Pouget was arrested at the Maison des fédérations on the morning of **1 August 1908** and imprisoned at Corbeil with Griffuelhes, Yvetot, Marie, Maucolin, and Bousquet. The government alleged responsibility for fomenting the disorders; no judicial determination established Pouget’s personal direction of violence. He received a **dismissal/non-lieu** and was released on **31 October**.

His imprisonment prevented normal participation in the Marseille CGT congress. After release he did not resume the editorship of *La Voix du peuple* or his confederal duties.

## *La Révolution* and Withdrawal from CGT Leadership, 1908–1909

Pouget’s long-standing ambition was a revolutionary daily. He initially planned to revive Jules Vallès’s title **Le Cri du peuple**, anticipating financial support from **Charles Malato**, who had received an inheritance. The arrests delayed the project.

With **Amédée Dunois**, Pierre Monatte, and other collaborators, Pouget launched **La Révolution** on **1 February 1909**. It was begun without adequate capital, became insolvent, and closed on **28 March after 56 issues**.

There is no evidence of fraudulent financing. The failure was a conventional shortage of working capital and circulation income. Monatte later wrote that the defeat wounded Pouget permanently. It marked the end of his central organizational role.

## Later Writing and Intellectual Development, 1909–1914

With electricians’ union leader **Émile Pataud**, Pouget published the utopian or anticipatory novel **Comment nous ferons la Révolution** in 1909. It portrayed a syndicalist transformation through general strike, disintegration of state authority, communal provisioning, and worker administration.

Jaurès publicly mocked the book at the Socialist School’s opening session on **29 November 1909**. Pouget answered in *La Guerre sociale* on 1 December. Even Griffuelhes called it literary fantasy rather than a practical revolutionary program. A 1911 edition contained a preface by **Peter Kropotkin**.

Pouget supported the campaign for the Spanish educator **Francisco Ferrer**, threatened and ultimately executed in 1909.

From **31 August 1910**, he wrote a trade-union column for **La Guerre sociale**. His pamphlets systematized:

- direct action;
- the union’s immediate and revolutionary roles;
- active minorities;
- boycott and sabotage;
- general strike;
- rejection of dependence on parliamentary parties or state arbitration.

In **1911 and again in 1914**, he opposed xenophobic agitation against foreign labor. In **1912**, he criticized narrow workerism, favored tactical unity with the Socialist Party against war, and defended the unionization of women, including **Emma Couriau**.

When *La Guerre sociale* issued a May 1912 manifesto against anarchist abstentionism and antiparliamentarism within the CGT, Pouget, Francis Delaisi, and Vigné d’Octon refused to sign it. His position therefore cannot be reduced either to orthodox anarchist abstentionism or to conversion to parliamentary socialism.

He sold three social serial novels to *L’Humanité*:

- **Les Épaves de Paris**, beginning 10 February 1912;
- **Nico**, beginning 25 January 1913;
- **Fleurette**, beginning 6 July 1914.

His **L’Organisation du surmenage**, written in 1913 and generally catalogued as published in 1914, criticized Taylorism as a technology for intensifying labor rather than a neutral efficiency science.

## First World War and Political Retreat, 1914–1918

In 1914 Pouget and Augustine rented an apartment at **18 rue du Mont-Cenis**, Paris. They purchased land at **Lozère near Palaiseau** and built a small house.

At the outbreak of war, Pouget aligned himself with **national defense**. He attended Jaurès’s funeral on **4 August 1914** with CGT leaders and registered no public opposition to Léon Jouhaux’s union-sacrée speech.

In the now-daily *Guerre sociale*, Pouget abandoned his trade-union column and contributed a “things seen” feature, **“La Rue,”** until 7 September. Whether the couple temporarily left the region during the German advance is unknown.

He subsequently wrote patriotic serials for *L’Humanité*:

- **Vieille Alsace**, beginning 14 May 1915;
- **L’Emmuré**, beginning 14 August 1916.

This wartime patriotism was a substantial reversal from his earlier antimilitarist propaganda. Sources do not reveal a detailed theoretical justification. It placed him among prewar syndicalists and anarchists who accepted defense of France, while other former comrades remained internationalist or antiwar.

## Later Life, Work, and Finances, 1919–1931

Pouget did not return to major political activity after the war. In an *Humanité* interview of **23 September 1920**, Amédée Dunois brought together Pouget, Griffuelhes, Delesalle, and Yvetot to discuss the Charter of Amiens and CGT divisions. Pouget spoke little but supported Griffuelhes’s declaration favoring the confederal minority.

During the 1920s he worked independently as a **publisher and advertising/catalogue agent**. Through friendships in avant-garde painting, he produced:

- the annual catalogue of the **Salon des Indépendants**;
- the **Annuaire de l’art décoratif moderne**.

He remained in contact chiefly with **Paul Delesalle** and **Dr. Marc Pierrot**, visiting Delesalle’s bookshop weekly. He otherwise lived privately at Lozère.

In **1929**, Maurice Chambelland proposed publishing Pouget’s memoirs in installments in *Le Cri du peuple*. Pouget refused categorically. No diary or memoir is known.

### Property and wealth

The couple’s land and small house at Lozère are documented. No evidence of substantial investments, corporate holdings, inherited fortune, foreign accounts, or concealed wealth has emerged. His repeated newspaper failures, salaried CGT employment, later advertising work, and modest funeral indicate limited means, although an exact estate inventory has not been located.

## Death and Burial

Pouget died at Lozère on **21 July 1931**, aged seventy. Paul Delesalle wrote that declining health and illness had increasingly restricted him, but the consulted records do not give a precise diagnosis or medically certified cause of death.

He was buried locally with a small attendance including:

- Paul Delesalle;
- Pierre Monatte;
- Maurice Chambelland.

Some later accounts add **Daniel Guérin**. A frequently reproduced Guérin footnote says that a pauper’s hearse carried Pouget in **1920**, an impossible date eleven years before his death. This is evidently a typographical or editorial error; it weakens the precision of that specific recollection but not necessarily Guérin’s presence.

Obituaries appeared in *Le Cri du peuple*, Dr. Pierrot’s *Plus loin*, Victor Méric’s *Le Soir*, and the German FAUD paper *Der Syndikalist*, where Max Nettlau devoted two pages to him. Delesalle collected memorial texts in a small **In Memoriam/Ad Memoriam** volume.

## Political, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

1. **Republican formation:** inherited from Vergely and the memory of the Commune.
2. **Anarchism, approximately 1877 onward:** rejection of state, Church, army, parliamentary government, and wage labor.
3. **Militant antimilitarism:** distribution of *À l’armée* and later participation in antimilitarist journalism.
4. **Collective rather than exclusively individual revolt:** general strike increasingly preferred over isolated attentats.
5. **Syndical turn, 1894–1897:** labor organizations became the durable mass base that affinity groups lacked.
6. **Revolutionary syndicalism:** direct economic action would unite workers beyond party doctrine; unions would conduct present struggles and form the institutions of a future society.
7. **Neutralism:** the union should be independent of political parties, religions, and patriotic institutions while remaining socially revolutionary.
8. **Pragmatic broadening:** inclusion of intellectual workers, women, immigrants, peasants, and artisans; selective cooperation with socialists against war.
9. **National defense in 1914:** an unresolved departure from prewar antimilitarism.
10. **Private retirement:** no documented repudiation of syndicalism, but little postwar political intervention.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Criminal Proceedings

### 1883 pillage conviction

- **Established:** Pouget led part of the unemployed march, was arrested, possessed antimilitarist pamphlets and potentially dangerous materials, was convicted, and served three years.
- **Not conclusively established:** that he personally looted a bakery, used a weapon, or intended to deploy the chemicals or alleged incendiary devices.
- **Contemporary dispute:** defenders considered the armed-pillage theory exaggerated and the eight-year sentence political.

### Advocacy of violence

Pouget’s journalism contained threats, profanity, approval of revolutionary force, and occasional praise of Ravachol. His “sabotage” doctrine could include deliberate damage to capitalist property.

Conversely, he criticized bomb attacks such as Greenwich, prioritized mass economic organization, and sometimes distinguished revolutionary action from indiscriminate destruction.

The evidence supports **rhetorical and strategic acceptance of certain political and industrial violence**, not personal participation in an attentat.

### Trial of the Thirty

- **Formal accusation:** membership in a criminal association linking anarchist propagandists to illegalist offenders.
- **Default conviction:** twenty years’ forced labor, 31 October 1894.
- **Final outcome:** after return, Pouget was retried and acquitted on 18 February 1895.
- **Historical assessment:** the initial proceeding was deeply shaped by the exceptional *lois scélérates* and an effort to criminalize anarchist association and journalism.

### Press prosecutions

*Le Père Peinard* generated repeated prosecutions and imprisonments for press offenses. A complete case-by-case judicial list has not been reconstructed here. The frequency is documented; claims that every prosecution concerned direct incitement to murder would be inaccurate.

### Alleged anarchist “capture” of the CGT

Reformists alleged from 1903 that Pouget and other anarchists manipulated the CGT bureau and its newspaper. Pouget undeniably exercised influence disproportionate to his public visibility. However, he was repeatedly elected, represented mandated organizations, and the Amiens resolution received an overwhelming congress vote. “Capture” was a political accusation, not proof of clandestine or fraudulent control.

### Sabotage and public safety

Critics treated Pouget’s doctrine as criminal destruction. Pouget presented it more broadly as workers matching effort to pay, enforcing rules, revealing employer wrongdoing, and controlling their labor. Some techniques he discussed would nevertheless have constituted property offenses under contemporary law. No evidence reviewed connects him personally to a fatal act of industrial sabotage.

### Dreyfus and antisemitism

Pouget’s initial refusal to support Dreyfus and reported use of antisemitic stereotypes are a significant part of his record. By late 1898 he explicitly mobilized against antisemitic and nationalist forces and participated in the Dreyfusard press.

This was a **documented political evolution**. It should neither erase the earlier material nor be treated as proof that he remained an anti-Dreyfusard.

### Sex and gender

The language of *Le Père Peinard* frequently employed masculine bravado, sexualized abuse, and stereotypes that modern scholarship may characterize as sexist. Authorship of unsigned pieces must be treated cautiously.

Pouget also published attacks on workplace sexual exploitation, defended women workers, and in 1912–1913 supported women’s unionization and Emma Couriau. The record contains both gendered popular invective and concrete pro-union advocacy for women.

### First World War reversal

Pouget’s adherence to national defense contradicted his earlier antimilitarism. It was not a prosecution or scandal in law, but it was politically contentious within anarchist and syndicalist circles. No evidence found indicates that he became a government agent or received state payment.

### Fraud, corruption, intelligence connections, and financial crime

The dedicated adversarial searches—including French and English combinations of Pouget’s name with scandal, allegation, fraud, conspiracy, lawsuit, corruption, and criticism, as well as searches restricted conceptually to 2024–2026—produced **no credible accusation of personal enrichment, fraud, corruption, money laundering, intelligence employment, or embezzlement**.

His files were extensively maintained by police, but being surveilled is not evidence of serving the police. A 1895 article shows him disputing reckless allegations that another anarchist was an informer, illustrating his awareness of the damage caused by unsupported denunciations.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Pouget invented sabotage”

- **Story:** Pouget invented both the word and practice of industrial sabotage.
- **Circulation:** union histories, short biographies, popular discussions of workplace resistance.
- **Evidence:** he imported and translated “go canny,” helped secure the Toulouse resolution, and became the best-known French theorist. The practice and related vocabulary had earlier roots; Delesalle presented the congress report.
- **Finding:** **heroic simplification**, based on genuine major influence.

### “A worker threw a wooden shoe into a machine”

- **Story:** *Sabotage* derives literally from workers stopping machinery with wooden clogs.
- **Evidence:** Pouget’s immediate usage derived primarily from “working in a sabot-like, clumsy way” and the British slowdown idea, not from a documented founding incident involving a shoe.
- **Finding:** **popular etymological legend**, not an established event in Pouget’s life.

### The 1881 London congress

- **Story:** Pouget was a French delegate.
- **Source:** an older police-based attribution repeated by biographers.
- **Counterevidence:** the revised *Maitron* notice identifies the underlying report as undated and apparently retrospective.
- **Finding:** **unproved and probably erroneous**.

### Algerian labor official, 1894–1896

- **Story:** Pouget served as secretary of the Algiers Bourse du Travail for two years.
- **Evidence for:** a name and office appear in labor records; Pouget likely passed through Algiers.
- **Evidence against full version:** he was demonstrably in London and France during much of the stated term.
- **Finding:** **identity or date problem unresolved**.

### “Author of the Charter of Amiens”

- **Story:** Pouget personally wrote the Charter.
- **Evidence:** he was one of its principal formulators and the “double task” bears his intellectual imprint.
- **Against sole authorship:** Griffuelhes, Delesalle, Niel, and others participated; it was a commission and congress product.
- **Finding:** **substantially connected but falsely individualized**.

### The cobbler image

- **Story:** Pouget was himself professionally a cobbler.
- **Origin:** the fictional Père Peinard was a *gniaff*, and Pouget represented a hand-sewn shoemakers’ union at Toulouse.
- **Evidence:** Pouget’s documented occupations were department-store employee, book broker, editor, union official, and publisher.
- **Finding:** **persona confused with biography**.

### Pauper’s funeral in 1920

- **Story:** Daniel Guérin recalled Pouget’s pauper’s hearse in 1920.
- **Evidence against date:** Pouget died in 1931.
- **Finding:** **obvious dating error** in a later note. The modest funeral itself is consistent with contemporary memorial accounts.

### The silent mastermind

- **Story:** Pouget secretly controlled the CGT as its “grey eminence.”
- **Origin:** hostile or fascinated contemporary portraits emphasized his silence, tactical intelligence, and editorial control.
- **Evidence:** he was influential and often drafted collective texts; he was also elected and worked within commissions.
- **Finding:** **reputation grounded in real influence but inflated into conspiratorial imagery**.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Pouget’s immediate funeral and press reception were modest. His reputation survived principally through syndicalist networks, anarchist libraries, and the continued institutional authority of the Charter of Amiens.

His legacies include:

- popularization of **sabotage** as a consciously theorized workers’ tactic;
- development of **direct action** as a transnational political concept;
- formulation of the union’s **“double task”**—immediate gains and future social reorganization;
- insistence on union autonomy from political parties;
- a model of militant journalism written in working-class slang rather than formal doctrinal language;
- influence on later French, Italian, Spanish, Latin American, British, and American syndicalist movements;
- continuing disputes over whether the Charter of Amiens signifies neutrality, independence, antiparliamentarism, or an autonomous form of working-class politics.

Later editions have alternately presented him as an anarchist precursor, CGT founder, theorist of workplace resistance, stylist of popular French, or advocate of destructive sabotage. Each captures only part of his activity.

The CIRA and revised *Maitron* work has corrected several inherited errors, especially the alleged 1881 London delegation, while identifying previously neglected positions against xenophobia and for women’s unionization.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by consulted sources are included. Mere contemporaneity has been excluded.

### Captain Alfred Dreyfus — documented political connection, not a personal relationship

Pouget initially dismissed the Dreyfus campaign as concern for a member of the elite while anarchist prisoners were ignored. After exposure of the Henry forgery in 1898, he joined the Dreyfusard mobilization, opposed antisemitic and nationalist street movements, co-signed the Coalition révolutionnaire manifesto, and worked for the Dreyfusard *Journal du peuple*. No meeting or correspondence between Pouget and Dreyfus has been documented.

### Karl Marx — documented intellectual adversarial reference

Pouget’s syndicalist writing repeatedly opposed the political and organizational tradition he associated with “authoritarian” Marxism and with Jules Guesde. This was an intellectual relationship to Marx’s legacy, not a personal connection: Marx died in 1883, when Pouget was a young imprisoned militant, and no meeting is documented.

### Mikhail Aleksandrovich Bakunin — documented intellectual lineage

Pouget and associates situated libertarian socialism in the Bakuninist side of the First International’s conflict with Marx. The connection is ideological and retrospective; Bakunin died in 1876 before Pouget’s mature activism, and there was no personal contact.

### Georges Sorel — reported shared intellectual scene; direct relationship unproved

Pouget and Sorel were both important contributors to the intellectual formation of revolutionary syndicalism around *Le Mouvement socialiste*. Pouget supplied congress reports and organic union theory; Sorel developed a separate philosophical interpretation of violence and myth. The consulted sources do not establish a close friendship, correspondence, or teacher–student bond. This is therefore a **shared journal and intellectual milieu**, not a proven personal alliance.

### Friedrich Nietzsche — no documented connection found

Later scholarship sometimes compares anarchist ideas of active minorities or force with Nietzschean themes, but no consulted source established that Pouget read Nietzsche or acknowledged him as an influence.

### Vladimir Ilyich Ulyanov, Rosa Luxemburg, Antonio Gramsci, Walter Benjamin, Erich Mühsam, Gustav Landauer, Rudolf Rocker, Lucy Parsons, José Buenaventura Durruti, and other rostered revolutionaries — no direct connection established

Several occupied overlapping international socialist or anarchist worlds, and some later encountered syndicalist ideas influenced by Pouget. The materials consulted did not establish a meeting, correspondence, collaboration, or explicit personal influence sufficient for an atlas connection.
