## Works by Anni Albers

### Functional textiles, prototypes, and architectural fabrics

- **1923–1926:** *Upholstery Material*. A Bauhaus furnishing-fabric design intended for repeatable manufacture rather than autonomous display; MoMA catalogues the surviving sample under this functional title.[1]
- **1923–1926:** *Drapery Material*. A woven furnishing textile: an industrial-design obra whose identity lies in its repeat and material structure, not in a unique “original.”[1]
- **1925:** *Design for Smyrna Rug*. A preparatory design for a knotted rug; it is a project on paper rather than the rug itself.[1]
- **1925:** *Design for Wall Hanging*. A weaving diagram or project, preserving instructions and visual structure rather than constituting the intended textile.[1]
- **1925:** *Wallhanging*. A unique handwoven textile whose architectural and pictorial functions resist the later division between design object and fine art.[2]
- **1926:** *Wallhanging*. A woven architectural hanging, distinct from the several surviving paper designs bearing the generic title *Design for Wall Hanging*.[1][3]
- **1926/1964:** *Black-White-Red*. A cotton-and-silk wall hanging designed at the Bauhaus but rewoven in 1964; its double date records the gap between project and later manifestation.[4]
- **1927:** *Design for a Wall Hanging*. An annotated multilayer-weave diagram: simultaneously image, technical protocol, and project for a textile.[1]
- **1929:** *Wall-Covering Material*. Albers’s Bauhaus diploma project, combining light-reflective cellophane with sound-absorbing chenille; it is a functional architectural prototype rather than simply a decorative cloth.[1]
- **1930:** *Wall Hanging for the ADGB Trade Union School, Bernau*. An architectural textile commissioned for Hannes Meyer and Hans Wittwer’s building; sources distinguish Albers’s design from later reconstructions and editions.[5]
- **ca. 1934:** *Untitled*. A textile work retained under a non-title; the absence of a fixed name should not be repaired editorially.[3]
- **1936:** *Ancient Writing*. A textile design whose linear organization evokes an unreadable script without becoming linguistic text.[3]
- **1930s–1960s:** *Untitled textile samples*. Numerous experimental swatches survive without individual titles or secure dates. They resist treatment as autonomous “works”: some are trials, teaching specimens, or protocols for possible manufacture.[3][6]
- **1944:** *Temple*. A furnishing-textile design later manufactured by Knoll; repetitions and later authorized productions are manifestations of the design, not new compositions by Albers.[1]
- **1946:** *With Verticals*. A woven hanging in which structure and image are inseparable; it is both textile construction and abstract composition.[3]
- **1947:** *Knot*. A woven textile organized around a knotlike linear figure.[3]
- **1949:** *Free-Hanging Room Dividers*. A series of five open-weave architectural textiles made for Albers’s MoMA exhibition. The series is a spatial project—“pliable planes” that divide rooms without functioning as fixed walls—and individual surviving manifestations are not consistently titled.[1]
- **1949:** *City*. A small linen-and-cotton weaving: an autonomous textile image rather than yardage or an architectural screen.[7]
- **1950s–1970s:** *Knoll textile designs*. A body of industrial furnishing fabrics designed over roughly three decades. It resists itemization as unique objects because samples, colorways, production runs, and later reissues are manifestations of repeatable designs.[1]
- **1958:** *Rail*. A linen casement material designed for Knoll Textiles; its proper category is industrial textile design, not singular tapestry.[8]
- **1982:** *Textiles for Sunar*. A commercially commissioned range rather than one unique object; the Foundation chronology records the project but does not supply a complete public list of its patterns and colorways.[9]
- **1984:** *Wallhanging*. A wool hanging catalogued under a generic title; no more specific title should be inferred.[10]

### Pictorial weavings

- **1947:** *La Luz I*. A pictorial weaving: Albers’s term for an autonomous textile image without an assigned utilitarian function.[11]
- **1948:** *Tapestry*. A directly composed weaving whose generic title identifies its medium but does not individualize its subject.[12]
- **1950:** *Black-White-Gold I*. A pictorial weaving built from contrasting bands and metallic color.[11]
- **1954:** *Red Meander*. A pictorial weaving; it is distinct from the later screenprints titled *Red Meander I* and *Red Meander II*.[11][13]
- **1954:** *Red and Blue Layers*. A pictorial weaving whose interlocking structure is simultaneously material construction and abstract image.[11]
- **1957:** *In Orbit*. A pictorial weaving, not a textile pattern intended for yardage.[11]
- **1958:** *Open Letter*. A pictorial weaving whose title invokes writing while the textile remains nonverbal—an image behaving like an unreadable text.[3][11]
- **1958:** *Pasture*. A cotton pictorial weaving; the title is evocative rather than evidence of conventional landscape representation.[14]
- **1958:** *South of the Border*. A pictorial weaving associated with Albers’s sustained encounter with Latin American textile structures.[11]
- **1961:** *Haiku*. A pictorial weaving named after a poetic form but containing no poem; it is not itself a literary text.[11]
- **1962:** *Intersecting*. A cotton-and-rayon pictorial weaving organized through crossing linear systems.[11]
- **1963:** *Under Way*. A cotton, linen, and wool pictorial weaving.[15]
- **1965–1966:** *Six Prayers*. Six separate woven panels commissioned by the Jewish Museum as a Holocaust memorial. The obra is both textile ensemble and commemorative spatial work; its serial, devotional character makes it more than six independent tapestries.[9][16]
- **1968:** *Epitaph*. Albers’s final pictorial weaving; the title deliberately marks an ending before her shift from loom work to printmaking.[17]

### Rugs and repeatable floor textiles

- **1925:** *Design for Smyrna Rug*. A rug project preserved as a design; where no executed rug is documented, the intention itself is the work.[1]
- **1950s–1960s:** *Rug designs*. A body of hand- and machine-producible floor-textile projects. Different weavings and later authorized editions should be understood as manifestations rather than automatically counted as new Albers compositions.[6]
- **1979:** *Red Lines on Blue*. A rug translating a graphic linear system into pile textile; its industrial realization makes it neither merely print nor unique weaving.[6]
- **1970:** *Study for an unrealized print*, later issued as *Temple Emanu-El/Temple Berry* rug designs. The surviving color study was originally a print project that remained unrealized in Albers’s lifetime; posthumous rugs are authorized manifestations, not objects physically made by her.[18]

### Jewelry and anti-luxury objects

- **ca. 1940–1941:** *Necklace of washers and grosgrain ribbon*. An anti-luxury necklace devised from hardware-store materials with Albers’s student Alex Reed; authorship is collaborative.[19]
- **ca. 1940–1941:** *Necklace of drain strainer and paper clips*. A collaborative Albers–Reed jewelry object that transforms domestic hardware without disguising it.[19]
- **ca. 1940–1941:** *Necklace of bobby pins on plated chain*. A collaborative Albers–Reed necklace; its serial construction lies between ornament, found-object assemblage, and material demonstration.[19]
- **ca. 1940–1941:** *Hardware-jewelry collection*. A larger collaborative body employing curtain rings, bottle caps, corks, erasers, sieves, washers, nuts, hairpins, and other inexpensive components. Surviving records do not establish a complete, uniquely titled object-by-object corpus, so the collection resists a false catalogue of invented titles.[9][19]
- **1941:** *Anni Albers and Alex Reed: Exhibition of Necklaces*. The exhibition was a public manifestation of the collaborative jewelry project, not itself a new set of objects.[20]

### Prints and printed portfolios

- **1963–1984:** *The prints of Anni Albers*. The catalogue raisonné documents 102 prints—lithographs, screenprints, etchings, offset impressions, and inkless embossings. This is the only medium for which a published catalogue raisonné supplies an authoritative complete numerical corpus; impressions, proofs, and color variants must not automatically be mistaken for separate compositions.[21]
- **1964:** *Line Involvements*. Her first print portfolio, a suite of lithographs made after her initial Tamarind experiments. Sources disagree in summaries over whether the suite contains six or seven works; the catalogue raisonné should govern item-level numbering.[22]
- **1964:** *Line Involvement I*. A lithograph from *Line Involvements*.[23]
- **1964:** *Line Involvement II*. A lithograph from *Line Involvements*.[23]
- **1964:** *Line Involvement III*. A lithograph from *Line Involvements*; its identity is as one leaf of a portfolio as well as an individual print.[22]
- **1964:** *Line Involvement IV*. A lithograph from *Line Involvements*.[24]
- **1964:** *Line Involvement V*. A lithograph from *Line Involvements*.[22]
- **1964:** *Line Involvement VI*. A lithograph from *Line Involvements*.[25]
- **1969–1970:** *Red Meander I*. A screenprint produced through repeated rotations and overprinting of a single screen; it translates weaving-like iteration into a specifically graphic process.[13]
- **1969–1970:** *Red Meander II*. A related screenprint, distinct from both *Red Meander I* and the 1954 weaving *Red Meander*.[21]
- **1970:** *TR I*. A lithograph.[26]
- **1970:** *TR II*. A print in the *TR* group; the abbreviated title should not be expanded without documentary authority.[21]
- **1970:** *TR III*. A zinc-plate embossing over screenprint, joining printed color to relief without becoming a textile.[27]
- **1973:** *DO I*. A screenprint whose short title is not an instruction or protocol.[21]
- **1973:** *DO II*. A screenprint.[28]
- **1973:** *PO I*. A hybrid graphic work combining workshop screenprinting with commercial press processes.[9]
- **1973:** *PO II*. A hybrid print made through the unprecedented combination of screenprint and commercial printing processes.[9][21]
- **1973:** *WCO*. A print made through Albers’s experiments at Fox Press; the archival abbreviation remains the title.[9]
- **1976:** *Triangulated Intaglio I–VI*. A six-print intaglio series exploring a repeated geometric system. Each sheet is an individual obra, while the sequence is also a serial project; *Triangulated Intaglio V* and *VI* are independently documented examples.[21][29]
- **1979:** *Orchestra*. A graphic composition later represented in the *Connections* portfolio through *Orchestra III*; sources should not collapse the various numbered manifestations into one impression.[21][30]
- **1983–1984:** *Connections*. A portfolio of nine screenprints revisiting motifs from six decades of Albers’s weaving, design, and printmaking. It is an autobiographical Gesamtkunstwerk in miniature: not a memoir in prose, but a deliberately retrospective translation among media.[9][31]
- **1983:** *Study for an Unexecuted Wallhanging*. A screenprint in *Connections*, based on a 1926 textile project; its title preserves the important fact that the intended wall hanging was not executed.[6]
- **1983:** *Orchestra III*. A screenprint included in *Connections* and derived from an earlier graphic motif.[30]
- **1984:** *Connections 1925–1983*. The portfolio’s alternative extended title records the dates of the source designs rather than nine new composition dates; catalogues vary between 1983, the printing date, and 1984, the publication date.[9][31]

### Drawings, studies, and projects on paper

- **1920s–1980s:** *Untitled drawings and textile studies*. A large body of graph-paper diagrams, ink drawings, gouaches, and working studies survives. Many are protocols or projects for weaving and printing rather than autonomous works, and many have neither fixed titles nor securely assigned dates.[1][6]
- **1925–1927:** *Designs and preliminary designs for wall hangings*. Multiple sheets survive under nearly identical generic titles. They are separate paper objects, but the titles alone cannot safely establish how many distinct intended textiles they represent.[1]
- **1970:** *Color study for an unrealized print*. A triangular gouache project that never became its intended print during Albers’s lifetime; its incompletion is part of the obra, not a defect to be silently corrected.[18]
- **1970–1980:** *Notebook*. A working notebook containing serial drawings and graphic investigations rather than a conventional authored book; its later facsimile publication is an edition of this private work object.[32]

### Books and book-length works

- **1959:** *On Designing*. A constructed collection of Albers’s theoretical essays on materials, craft, art, architecture, and design; later editions are manifestations of the same book, not additional works.[33]
- **1965:** *On Weaving*. A book-length synthesis of weaving’s structures, histories, materials, and design principles. It functions partly as theory and partly as protocol, while refusing a simple separation between technical manual and aesthetic argument.[33]
- **1970:** *Pre-Columbian Mexican Miniatures: The Josef and Anni Albers Collection*. A jointly authored collection catalogue with Ignacio Bernal and Michael D. Coe. Albers helped make the book and the collection it articulates, but she did not make the pre-Columbian objects themselves.[33]
- **2000:** *Anni Albers: Selected Writings on Design*. A posthumously edited collection prepared by Brenda Danilowitz. Its constituent essays are Albers’s works, but the selection and book-form compilation are editorial manifestations rather than a new book made by Albers in 2000.[33]

### Essays, statements, and lectures

- **1924:** *Bauhausweberei*. An essay on Bauhaus weaving; some archival descriptions date it 1926, while the scholarly bibliography identifies publication in the November 1924 Bauhaus issue of *Junge Menschen*, so the dating is disputed in the accessible records.[33][34]
- **1924:** *Wohnökonomie* (bibliographies also print *Wöhnökonomie*). A short design text published in a special Bauhaus supplement; the spelling varies among later references.[33]
- **1938:** *Work with Material* (also cited as *Work with Materials*). A programmatic text for the Black Mountain College bulletin; the singular/plural title varies in institutional records.[33][34]
- **1938:** *Weaving at the Bauhaus*. A historical and theoretical contribution to the Museum of Modern Art’s Bauhaus publication.[33]
- **1941:** *Handweaving Today: Textile Work at Black Mountain College*. An essay describing weaving pedagogy and practice.[33]
- **1942:** *On Jewelry*. A lecture or statement associated with the Albers–Reed hardware-jewelry experiment; it is a contextual text, not a jewelry object.[19]
- **1943:** *Designing*. An essay published in *Craft Horizons*.[33]
- **1944:** *We Need the Crafts for Their Contact with Materials*. A manifesto-like argument: the text advocates renewed material experience rather than merely describing craft.[33][34]
- **1946:** *Constructing Textiles*. An essay on textile structure and design published in a Black Mountain College issue of *Design*.[33]
- **1947:** *Design: Anonymous and Timeless*. A manifesto for design whose value lies in useful, unselfconscious form rather than the designer’s signature.[33]
- **1948:** *Fabrics*. A short text published in *Arts & Architecture*.[33]
- **1949:** *Weavings*. A short text published in *Arts & Architecture*.[33]
- **1957:** *The Pliable Plane: Textiles in Architecture*. An essay reconceiving textiles as flexible architectural planes; it is theory for a spatial project as much as description.[33]
- **1961:** *Conversations with Artists*. A published contribution to the American Craftsmen’s Council forum.[33]
- **1969:** *A Start*. A late manifesto-like essay about beginning, experimentation, and artistic work.[33]
- **1969:** *On Walter Gropius*. An obituary and critical remembrance.[34]
- **1982:** *Material as Metaphor*. Albers’s named statement for a College Art Association panel; the archive notes that her other preserved lectures are untitled, so they cannot responsibly be converted into invented works.[34]
- **1942–1982:** *Untitled lectures*. A surviving body of typescripts and publicity records. These are textual events and working documents, but their lack of authorial titles makes them resist item-by-item naming as finished works.[34]

### Collections, pedagogy, and works that resist objecthood

- **1935–1967:** *Latin American journeys and textile study*. A sustained research project undertaken through repeated travel, collecting, photography, and technical observation. It generated textiles, writings, and teaching but is not itself one bounded art object.[17]
- **1930s–1960s:** *Weaving pedagogy at Black Mountain College and elsewhere*. Courses, material exercises, and teaching protocols were central made structures in Albers’s practice, yet authorship is distributed among teacher, students, materials, and repeated classroom enactments.[1][34]
- **1949:** *Anni Albers: Textiles*. The MoMA exhibition was a designed public manifestation bringing together texture studies, yardage, pictorial weavings, and hanging screens. It was not a single artwork, although its installation made an architectural argument across media.[9][20]
- **1959–1960:** *Pictorial Weavings*. A traveling exhibition and curatorial manifestation of Albers’s autonomous weavings, not a separate body newly made in 1959.[20]
- **1970:** *The Josef and Anni Albers collection of pre-Columbian miniatures*. A jointly assembled collection rather than objects authored by the Alberses; collecting, arranging, and publishing it were their project, while the ancient works retain their own makers and histories.[33]
- **1970 onward:** *Shift from weaving to printmaking*. This is not a work in the narrow object sense but a deliberate reorganization of practice: Albers abandoned the loom and used graphic processes to continue structural problems first developed in thread.[9][22]
