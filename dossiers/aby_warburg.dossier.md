# Aby Moritz Warburg: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

Aby Moritz Warburg (1866–1929) was a German-Jewish independent scholar, historian of art and culture, collector, photographer, lecturer, and founder of the library that became the Warburg Institute. His principal subjects were the transmission and transformation of ancient images, symbols, gestures, and cosmologies; Renaissance Florence; astrology; religious practice; and the historical psychology of images.

This dossier uses the following labels:

- **Documented fact**: supported by archival material, contemporaneous records, Warburg’s writings or correspondence, institutional records, or strong scholarly documentation.
- **Reported fact**: stated in credible secondary literature but not independently verified here from the underlying document.
- **Disputed interpretation**: the underlying evidence is accepted, but its meaning remains contested.
- **Retrospective diagnosis**: a modern or historical medical interpretation that cannot be treated as definitive.
- **Legend or family story**: a memorable narrative transmitted after the event, not necessarily verifiable in every detail.
- **Rumor or conspiracy narrative**: unsupported or distorted claims, ordinarily arising from confusion between Aby and his banker brothers or from antisemitic mythology.

No evidence located in the dedicated adversarial search indicated that Aby Warburg was charged with or convicted of fraud, corruption, financial crime, political violence, or any criminal offense.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Aby Moritz Warburg.

**Birth name:** The name is normally recorded as Aby Moritz Warburg. “Aby” was associated with the Hebrew name Abraham; he is consequently sometimes indexed as **Abraham Moritz Warburg**.

**Common scholarly name:** Aby M. Warburg; Aby Warburg.

**Self-description:** Warburg described himself in Italian as:

> “Amburghese di cuore, ebreo di sangue, d’anima Fiorentino.”

Usually translated: “Hamburger at heart, Jew by blood, Florentine in spirit.”

**Birth:** 13 June 1866, Hamburg, then a free city within the North German Confederation.

**Death:** 26 October 1929, Hamburg, Weimar Republic.

**Cause of death:** Reported as a sudden heart attack or cardiac seizure.

**Burial:** Ohlsdorf Cemetery, Hamburg, in the Warburg family burial ground.

**Nationality and civic identity:** German; strongly identified with Hamburg’s civic culture.

**Religion and religious position:** Born into an observant Jewish banking family. As an adult he rejected substantial elements of Orthodox Jewish observance, declined to recite the Kaddish as eldest son after his father’s death, and married a Protestant. He did not, however, erase his Jewish ancestry or experience from his self-understanding. His relation to Judaism was ambivalent, historically situated, and affected by German assimilation and antisemitism.

**Primary occupations:**

- Independent scholar or *Privatgelehrter*
- Art historian and cultural historian
- Historian of images
- Lecturer
- Collector and librarian
- Photographer and ethnographic observer during his American journey
- Founder of the Kulturwissenschaftliche Bibliothek Warburg

**Principal residences:**

- Hamburg, childhood and much of adult life
- Bonn, Strasbourg, Berlin, and Florence during education
- Florence, especially 1897–1901
- Hamburg, principally Heilwigstraße 114 from 1909
- Hospitals or clinics in Hamburg and Jena, approximately 1918–1921
- Bellevue Sanatorium, Kreuzlingen, Switzerland, 1921–1924
- Rome and other Italian locations, 1928–1929

## Family and Ancestry

### The Warburg family

**Documented fact:** Aby was the eldest son of banker Moritz M. Warburg and Charlotte Esther Warburg, née Oppenheim. The family controlled M. M. Warburg & Co., established in Hamburg in 1798 and descended from banking enterprises associated with brothers Moses Marcus and Gerson Warburg.

Family tradition traced the Warburgs through Jewish families who moved from Italy into German-speaking lands, subsequently settled in the Westphalian town of Warburg, and adopted the town’s name. Some genealogical summaries give 1557 as the year of settlement in Warburg. The longer lineage contains uncertainties typical of early-modern family genealogy.

### Parents

**Father:** Moritz M. Warburg (1838–1910), Hamburg banker and head of M. M. Warburg & Co.

**Mother:** Charlotte Esther Oppenheim (1842–1921), from another prominent German-Jewish banking family.

The household combined wealth, civic standing, family obligation, and Jewish religious observance. Aby’s resistance to the commercial and religious future expected of him became a defining early conflict.

### Siblings

The usually accepted sibling group comprises:

- **Max Moritz Warburg** (1867–1946), banker, later head of M. M. Warburg & Co.
- **Paul Moritz Warburg** (1868–1932), banker in Germany and the United States; later a member of the first US Federal Reserve Board.
- **Felix Moritz Warburg** (1871–1937), banker and philanthropist in New York, partner in Kuhn, Loeb & Co.
- **Olga Charlotte Warburg** (1873–1904), married Paul Kohn-Speyer. Her death is described in medical-historical scholarship as suicide.
- **Louisa or Louise Warburg** (1879–1973), married Julius Derenberg.
- **Fritz Moritz Warburg** (1879–1964), banker.

Aby was therefore related directly to major German and American financial networks. He did not become a banker, sit on the Federal Reserve Board, direct the Reichsbank, or run his brothers’ American businesses.

### Marriage

**Wife:** Mary Warburg, born Mary Hertz (13 October 1866–4 December 1934), painter and sculptor.

Mary was the daughter of Hamburg merchant, shipowner, and senator Adolph Ferdinand Hertz and Maria or Marie Hertz, née Gossler. She received private artistic instruction, produced drawings, pastels, watercolors, portraits, figurative work, and sculpture, and was encouraged by Hamburg Kunsthalle director Alfred Lichtwark. In Florence she entered circles around sculptor Adolf von Hildebrand and painter Arnold Böcklin.

Aby and Mary married in Hamburg in 1897. Religious difference complicated the match: Mary was Protestant, while Aby’s family was Jewish. The marriage joined two wealthy Hamburg mercantile families but also embodied Aby’s movement away from inherited religious observance.

Medical records and later accounts depict Mary carrying an exceptionally heavy burden during Aby’s illnesses and periods of agitation. Psychiatrist Heinrich Embden’s surviving characterization was more sympathetic to Mary than to Aby and described her life as subordinated to her husband’s moods. This is a clinician’s reported judgment, not a neutral account of the marriage.

### Children

Aby and Mary Warburg had three children:

- **Marietta Warburg**, later Braden (1899–1973)
- **Max Adolph Warburg** (1902–1974), classical philologist and artist
- **Frede Charlotte Warburg**, later Prag (1904–2004)

### Wealth and family financing

Warburg’s personal scholarly independence rested substantially on family capital. His brothers and the family bank financed his book collecting, household, research, employees, and eventually the institutional library.

The relationship was not merely passive patronage. Aby negotiated budgets, cultivated the family’s sense of civic responsibility, and presented the library as the family’s intellectual equivalent of a banking house. The library nevertheless depended materially on commercial wealth that Aby himself did not produce.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1866–1885

Warburg grew up in a prosperous Hamburg household shaped by banking, Judaism, international commerce, and the civic ambitions of the city’s mercantile elite. He attended the Realschule des Johanneums, as did his brothers Max and Paul.

He showed an early appetite for reading, history, literature, and images. Later family accounts portray him as physically delicate, anxious, imaginative, and resistant to the career ordained for the eldest son.

### The “birthright for books” story

**Legend or family story, based on later testimony:** At approximately thirteen, Aby offered his younger brother Max the right to inherit leadership of the family bank if Max would promise to buy every book Aby required.

Max repeated the story later in life, and it became the founding myth of both brothers’ careers: Max received the bank; Aby received an unlimited scholarly credit line. The transaction was not a legally enforceable sale of inheritance, and its precise wording cannot be independently reconstructed. It nevertheless reflects the documented outcome: Max entered and ultimately headed the bank, while the family financed Aby’s collecting.

The phrase “birthright sold for books” consciously resembles the biblical story of Esau selling his birthright to Jacob. Later accounts often sharpen this literary parallel.

### Early psychological and religious conflict

Medical-historical scholarship based on family and clinical records reports childhood and adolescent anxieties, fear of illness, and obsessive ideas. During his student years, conflict over Jewish dietary observance was reportedly accompanied by the obsessive fear that he possessed a “Janus head.” Bromide treatment is mentioned in the clinical reconstruction.

These episodes are **documented through retrospective clinical and autobiographical records**, but their precise psychiatric meaning is unresolved.

## Education and Formation, 1886–1893

### University of Bonn

Warburg entered the University of Bonn in 1886. He studied art history, archaeology, history, and related subjects. Teachers commonly associated with his Bonn formation include:

- **Carl Justi**, historian of Spanish and Renaissance art
- **Henry Thode**, art historian
- **Hermann Usener**, classical philologist and historian of religion
- **Karl Lamprecht**, historian associated with cultural and psychological approaches to history

Usener’s attention to ancient religion and survivals and Lamprecht’s broad cultural history helped direct Warburg beyond connoisseurship and formal style.

### Florence, 1888–1889

Warburg studied Renaissance sources and works in Florence and used the Kunsthistorisches Institut. Florence became his primary historical laboratory. He examined paintings together with poetry, festival culture, dress, patronage, archival documents, domestic objects, commercial relations, and the social world of patrons.

### Strasbourg

Warburg continued at Strasbourg under **Hubert Janitschek**. He completed a doctoral dissertation on Botticelli’s mythological paintings, published in 1893 as:

*​Sandro Botticellis “Geburt der Venus” und “Frühling”: Eine Untersuchung über die Vorstellungen von der Antike in der italienischen Frührenaissance.*

The dissertation argued that Botticelli’s moving figures, flying hair, and animated drapery were not incidental decoration. They arose through Renaissance engagement with ancient literary descriptions and expressive conventions.

This work established several enduring problems:

- the reactivation or *Nachleben* of antiquity;
- the migration of motifs between words and images;
- expressive movement as a bearer of historical memory;
- the Renaissance as a negotiation between pagan intensity and Christian or rational restraint.

### Berlin and medical or psychological study

After the doctorate, Warburg attended courses in medicine and psychology in Berlin for approximately two semesters. Gertrud Bing later called this an abortive attempt to study medicine. His interest was less in qualifying as a physician than in the physiological and psychological foundations of expression.

### Military service

Warburg completed a period of military service after his university training. Accounts differ in how much importance to assign it. He did not pursue a military career.

### Intellectual influences

Among Warburg’s formative authors and fields were:

- Jacob Burckhardt on Renaissance civilization
- Friedrich Nietzsche, especially the tension between Apollonian order and Dionysian intensity
- Charles Darwin’s *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals*
- Tito Vignoli on myth and fear
- Ewald Hering and Richard Semon on memory
- Hermann Usener on religion and myth
- Renaissance poetry, especially Poliziano
- Classical philology
- Anthropology and ethnology
- Psychology of expression
- Astrology and cosmology

Warburg borrowed from these writers selectively. His mature work was not reducible to any one philosophical system.

## American Journey, 1895–1896

### Origin and itinerary

In 1895 Warburg traveled to the United States for the wedding of his brother Paul to Nina Loeb in New York. Instead of returning promptly to Europe, he undertook an extended journey through the United States.

His documented or strongly reported route included:

- New York
- Washington, DC, including the Smithsonian and Bureau of American Ethnology milieu
- Colorado
- New Mexico
- Arizona
- Pueblo communities including Acoma, Cochiti, Laguna, San Ildefonso, and Zuni
- Hopi country, including Oraibi and Walpi
- Keams Canyon and other reservation or agency locations
- California before return travel

He met anthropologists and intermediaries including **Franz Boas**, **James Mooney**, **Frank Hamilton Cushing**, and Presbyterian missionary **Henry Voth**. Warburg and Boas corresponded from 1895 to 1928.

### What he studied

Warburg collected notes, photographs, drawings, books, and Indigenous objects. Subjects included:

- Pueblo architecture
- pottery and ornament
- Katsina or Kachina figures
- masked dances
- children’s drawings
- the relation of snakes, lightning, and rain
- Indigenous ritual under conditions of missionary and federal intervention
- the coexistence of Indigenous, Catholic, and modern technological systems

He commissioned Pueblo children to draw lightning and related motifs. He studied pottery associated with the Hopi-Tewa artist Nampeyo, though the exact circumstances of every encounter or acquisition are not equally documented.

### The snake dance

Warburg did not simply “discover” the Hopi snake ritual. It was already observed, described, photographed, and sometimes exploited by missionaries, anthropologists, tourists, and government personnel. Whether Warburg personally witnessed the principal public snake dance at Oraibi has been debated; accounts of his journey and surviving material require careful differentiation among ceremonies he saw, ceremonies described to him, and later synthetic interpretation.

The famous photograph in which Warburg appears beside an Indigenous dancer is sometimes reproduced as if it proves full ritual participation. It establishes presence and interaction, not mastery of the ritual’s meaning.

### Intellectual result

The journey destabilized a simple opposition between “primitive” magic and modern rationality. Warburg later interpreted the lightning-serpent relation as an attempt to obtain psychic and symbolic distance from overwhelming natural forces. Telegraph wires and electric technology became, in his later formulation, modern forms of controlling or displacing the serpent-like forces of nature.

### Colonial and ethical context

**Documented fact:** Warburg traveled as a wealthy European visitor through Indigenous communities then subjected to US territorial expansion, reservation administration, missionary pressure, forced schooling, tourism, and ethnographic extraction.

**Disputed interpretation:** Older scholarship frequently represented the trip primarily as a heroic breakthrough in interdisciplinary method. Recent scholarship asks how Warburg’s access depended on colonial institutions and how his photography and collecting participated in asymmetrical possession and representation.

The Warburg Institute now consults Pueblo representatives regarding culturally sensitive material. It has imposed a moratorium on reproduction and permissions for specified images and records and withheld some objects or images from exhibition at the request of community authorities. This is not evidence that Warburg committed a prosecutable offense. It recognizes that legal possession does not settle cultural authority, sacred restriction, consent, or continuing harm.

### 1897 lecture

After returning to Europe, Warburg lectured in Hamburg in January 1897 on his journey through Pueblo territory. His observations remained largely unpublished during his lifetime.

## Marriage, Florence, and Renaissance Research, 1897–1901

Warburg married Mary Hertz in 1897, and the couple lived principally in Florence until 1901. This was a productive period of archival investigation and participation in expatriate and scholarly society.

He researched:

- Botticelli
- Florentine festival culture
- Medici patronage
- portraiture
- the Tornabuoni and Sassetti families
- exchanges between Florence and Flanders
- costume and expressive movement
- the survival of ancient motifs in domestic, civic, and religious settings

Mary continued her artistic work. The couple’s position combined wealth, scholarship, family life, and social contact with German and Italian artistic circles.

Warburg developed the image of the animated female figure—the later “nymph”—as a bearer of intensified movement inherited from antiquity. The “nymph” was both an art-historical motif and a recurring figure in Warburg’s imaginative vocabulary.

## Hamburg Scholar and Civic Intellectual, 1901–1913

The family returned to Hamburg in 1901. Warburg pursued scholarship without a conventional university post.

### Publications and lectures

Important studies from this period included:

- “Bildniskunst und florentinisches Bürgertum” on portraiture and Florentine civic culture
- “Dürer und die italienische Antike”
- “Francesco Sassettis letztwillige Verfügung”
- work on Burgundian-Flemish and Florentine cultural exchange
- studies of Medici buildings, festivals, astrology, and Renaissance imagery

In the 1905 Dürer lecture Warburg used **Pathosformel**—“pathos formula”—for inherited visual formulas encoding extreme emotion or movement. Such formulas could survive across centuries and undergo reversal: triumph could become mourning; violence could be absorbed into religious or political imagery.

### Library formation

Warburg had collected books since his student years. By 1900 he was discussing a formal “Warburg-Bibliothek für Kulturwissenschaft” with Max. The difficulty of assembling his Botticelli research from specialized libraries reinforced his desire for a collection organized around problems rather than academic departments.

In 1909 the family moved into Heilwigstraße 114 in Hamburg. The growing library occupied the residence and adjoining spaces.

Its evolving intellectual order was eventually expressed through four broad categories:

1. **Bild — Image**
2. **Wort — Word**
3. **Orientierung — Orientation**
4. **Handlung — Action**

Books were placed according to Warburg’s “law of the good neighbor”: a reader seeking one volume should encounter another that redirected or deepened the problem. This was an intellectual method embodied as shelving, not a universal cataloguing system.

### Hamburg civic activity

Warburg worked with Hamburg scholars, museum officials, teachers, and cultural administrators. He supported the creation of a university in Hamburg and promoted the city as a center of serious research.

In 1912 he rejected a call to a professorship at Halle. Hamburg appointed him professor in connection with the developing university project, but he continued to function primarily as an independent scholar rather than a standard departmental academic.

### The 1912 Rome lecture

At the International Congress of Art History in Rome, Warburg presented research on the frescoes of the Palazzo Schifanoia in Ferrara. He identified the complex astrological and mythographic sources behind the fresco cycle, tracing imagery through Greek, Arabic, medieval, and Renaissance channels.

The lecture is frequently treated as a foundational moment in iconology because it joined formal analysis to texts, astrology, transmission, patronage, and historical function.

## War, Politics, and Breakdown, 1914–1921

### First World War

Warburg reacted intensely to the First World War. He gathered an enormous collection of press clippings, photographs, maps, pamphlets, and propaganda. The activity was simultaneously scholarly documentation, political engagement, and a means of managing anxiety.

His political position was broadly that of an assimilated German liberal bourgeois patriot. He supported Germany during significant phases of the war, monitored foreign propaganda, and hoped that disciplined historical inquiry might expose the irrational images and rumors mobilized by conflict.

He was not a pacifist throughout the war, a revolutionary socialist, or a Zionist political leader. His position became increasingly strained as military catastrophe, revolution, antisemitism, and the collapse of Imperial Germany accumulated.

### Luther and astrology

His wartime and postwar investigations culminated in *Heidnisch-antike Weissagung in Wort und Bild zu Luthers Zeiten* (1920), on pagan-antique prophecy in the age of Luther. Warburg analyzed astrological images and predictions surrounding Martin Luther and the Reformation, treating political prophecy as a battlefield between fatalistic cosmology and attempts at rational self-direction.

### Psychiatric crisis

**Documented fact:** Warburg suffered a severe psychiatric breakdown beginning around the end of the war. Symptoms recorded by family members and physicians included:

- intense agitation
- insomnia and screaming
- paranoid delusions
- fears that relatives were being imprisoned, tortured, killed, dismembered, or fed to him
- fear of international conspiracies and spies
- belief that he might be held responsible for Germany’s defeat
- violent outbursts
- threats or attempts involving self-harm and harm to family members

A later account states that he took up a revolver, believing he could save the family only by killing them and himself, but was disarmed. Clinical records also report that he nearly killed a nurse on one occasion. These are **documented clinical or family reports**, not criminal convictions.

Warburg entered care in Hamburg, then was treated in Jena under neurologist Hans Berger. Berger diagnosed *dementia praecox* or schizophrenic psychosis and asked that Warburg be transferred because his disturbance was extremely difficult to manage.

## Bellevue Sanatorium, 1921–1924

Warburg was admitted to the Bellevue Sanatorium at Kreuzlingen, Switzerland, on 16 April 1921. Bellevue was directed by psychiatrist **Ludwig Binswanger**.

### Diagnoses

- **Hans Berger:** dementia praecox or schizophrenic psychosis.
- **Ludwig Binswanger:** schizophrenia or a related schizoid psychosis.
- **Emil Kraepelin, consultation of February 1923:** mixed manic-depressive state, with a favorable prognosis.

These diagnoses belonged to early twentieth-century psychiatric systems and are not interchangeable with modern criteria. Modern writers have proposed bipolar disorder, schizoaffective disorder, psychotic depression, schizophrenia, or severe mood disorder with psychosis. No retrospective diagnosis can be definitive.

### Treatment and institutional life

Records mention rest, supervision, medication including opium treatment after Kraepelin’s consultation, and management of agitation. Later criticism by Carl Georg Heise accused Binswanger of therapeutic insufficiency. A medical-historical reassessment argues that the central issue was not failure to use later shock therapies—which did not yet exist—but Binswanger’s limited psychotherapeutic engagement following a pessimistic schizophrenia diagnosis.

Warburg wrote thousands of pages during the illness. One estimate cited in the clinical literature gives approximately 7,345 manuscript pages from his sanatorium period. These materials register both delusion and an effort to reconstruct causal order.

### Cassirer, Saxl, Bing, and recovery

The philosopher **Ernst Cassirer**, who had used Warburg’s library and whose work concerned symbolic forms, visited him. Warburg reportedly felt that Cassirer understood his continuing research and treated him as a scholar rather than merely a patient.

**Fritz Saxl** maintained and developed the library in Hamburg, while **Gertrud Bing**, who joined the institution in 1921, became increasingly important to Warburg’s research and eventual return.

### The serpent-ritual lecture

On 21 April 1923, Warburg delivered his lecture on Pueblo serpent ritual to patients, staff, and invited listeners at Bellevue. The event has frequently been described as a “self-cure” or examination proving his sanity.

That formulation is **partly mythic**:

- The lecture was real.
- Warburg treated the work as a continuation of serious scholarship and a demonstration of intellectual competence.
- It did not itself produce immediate discharge.
- Kraepelin’s revised diagnosis, gradual clinical improvement, family negotiations, and institutional judgment all contributed.
- Warburg explicitly objected to reducing the lecture to a medical performance.

He was discharged in August 1924.

## Return to Hamburg and Final Productive Period, 1924–1929

### Reorganization of the library

On returning to Hamburg, Warburg resumed work with Saxl and Bing. In 1924 the collection acquired the library of classicist and historian of astrology **Franz Boll**.

The Kulturwissenschaftliche Bibliothek Warburg became a public research institution rather than merely a private book collection. A purpose-built library at Heilwigstraße 116 opened in May 1926 beside Warburg’s residence. Its elliptical reading room facilitated lectures and displays.

The building bore the inscription **MNEMOSYNE**, the Greek name of memory and mother of the Muses.

### Academic network

The library attracted or supported scholars including:

- Fritz Saxl
- Gertrud Bing
- Ernst Cassirer
- Erwin Panofsky
- Edgar Wind
- Karl Reinhardt
- Franz Boll
- Carl Georg Heise
- Gustav Pauli
- Alfred Lichtwark

Cassirer used its resources for his philosophy of symbolic forms. Panofsky and Saxl’s work on Dürer’s *Melencolia I* developed within the library’s intellectual environment. Warburg’s role included patron, critic, organizer, and provider of research infrastructure.

### Teaching and lectures

From 1925 to 1929 Warburg gave lectures and seminars in the library and through the University of Hamburg milieu. His 1926 seminar on Jacob Burckhardt examined the historian as a figure able to perceive the dangerous return of historical forces while maintaining intellectual distance.

He also participated in the Hamburg Orientalists’ Congress and sponsored cultural-historical displays.

### “Idea Vincit”

In 1926 Warburg commissioned Otto Heinrich Strohmeyer to design a postage-stamp image bearing the motto **“Idea Vincit”**—“the idea conquers.” It expressed faith in transnational intellectual communication during a period of nationalism and political fragmentation. The stamp was a cultural project, not an adopted state issue.

### Planetarium project

Warburg and his circle developed a photographic exhibition on astrology and astronomy for the Hamburg Planetarium: *Bildersammlung zur Geschichte von Sternglaube und Sternkunde*. It opened in 1930, after his death. The project contrasted magical-fatalistic orientation with mathematical and technological command of the heavens without presenting history as a simple linear victory of reason.

## The *Bilderatlas Mnemosyne*

Warburg’s last and most famous project was the *Bilderatlas Mnemosyne*. Its principal working phase ran from approximately 1924 or 1925 until his death.

### Form

He pinned photographic reproductions, diagrams, maps, manuscript images, advertisements, newspaper photographs, coins, stamps, artworks, and other visual material to large black-cloth-covered panels. He repeatedly moved and regrouped them.

The final documented series comprised 63 principal panels and nearly one thousand images, although earlier states and alternative panels survive photographically.

### Aim

The Atlas was intended to map:

- the migration of ancient expressive formulas;
- the polarity between ecstatic identification and rational distance;
- transformations between pagan and Christian imagery;
- relations among the ancient Mediterranean, the Middle East, medieval Europe, the Renaissance, and modern mass culture;
- how gestures preserve and reverse emotional energies;
- collective memory embedded in images.

Warburg called his method an “iconology of the interval” or *Ikonologie des Zwischenraumes*: meaning emerged not only from individual objects but from tensions and transitions between them.

### Status

**Documented fact:** The Atlas was unfinished when Warburg died.

Modern printed versions are editorial reconstructions from photographs, notes, captions, and surviving source images. They are not conventional books completed and authorized by Warburg.

In 2020 the Haus der Kulturen der Welt and Warburg Institute presented all 63 panels reconstructed with almost all the original photographic material. Researchers located most of the approximately 971 source images within a collection of over 400,000 items.

---

## Final Italian Journey, 1928–1929

Warburg traveled to Italy with Gertrud Bing and assistant Franz Alber in 1928. He worked in Florence and Rome and continued revising the Atlas.

On 19 January 1929 he lectured at the Bibliotheca Hertziana in Rome. Large horizontal screens, rather than ordinary slides, allowed him to present sequences related to the Atlas.

Warburg’s closeness to Bing during this period was intellectual and practical. She acted as research assistant, organizer, interlocutor, and later editor. No adequate documentary basis located in this research establishes a sexual relationship; descriptions that turn their collaboration into an affair should be treated as speculation unless supported by additional private evidence.

He returned to Hamburg in June 1929.

---

## Death

Aby Warburg died suddenly in Hamburg on 26 October 1929, aged sixty-three. The cause is consistently reported as a heart attack or cardiac event.

He left:

- an unfinished *Mnemosyne Atlas*;
- a major photographic collection;
- tens of thousands of books;
- manuscripts, index cards, diaries, photographs, and correspondence;
- a functioning research institute;
- a family;
- a scholarly method that remained only partly systematized in print.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### M. M. Warburg & Co.

Warburg was born into the family owning the Hamburg bank. He relinquished the expected banking career to Max. The bank and family financed his work. No evidence establishes that Aby exercised executive control over the bank’s financial operations or shared responsibility for the later political and corporate activities of Max, Paul, or Felix.

### Kulturwissenschaftliche Bibliothek Warburg

Founded from Aby’s private collection and developed into a research library. Important stages were:

- book collecting from 1886;
- conception of a cultural-science library by approximately 1900;
- organization at Heilwigstraße 114 after 1909;
- Saxl’s appointment in 1913;
- conversion toward a public research institute from 1921;
- opening of the new building at Heilwigstraße 116 in 1926.

### University of Hamburg

Warburg supported the movement for a Hamburg university. Founded in 1919, it drew on the library and Warburg circle. He held professorial standing but rejected the normal model of a permanent departmental career.

### Later institutional migration

After the Nazi seizure of power, Fritz Saxl, Gertrud Bing, the Warburg family, and supporters moved the library, photographs, papers, and furniture from Hamburg to London in 1933. The institute opened there in 1934, became part of the University of London under a 1944 trust arrangement, and moved to its Bloomsbury building in 1958.

The move occurred after Aby’s death, but it determined his posthumous influence.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Judaism and assimilation

Warburg’s Jewish identity cannot be described simply as observance or rejection.

- He resisted his father’s Orthodox expectations.
- He rejected dietary restrictions.
- He married a Protestant.
- He refused the eldest son’s expected Kaddish after his father’s death.
- He nevertheless identified himself as “Jew by blood.”
- Antisemitism remained an object of concern and a force shaping his political world.
- His scholarship repeatedly considered the tension between minority memory, assimilation, civic identity, and European culture.

Charlotte Schoell-Glass argues that antisemitism and Jewish identity are structural concerns within his scholarship, not peripheral biographical facts. Other interpreters caution against making all of Warburg’s image theory a coded response to Jewish politics.

### German patriotism and liberal civic culture

Warburg shared many values of Hamburg’s educated liberal bourgeoisie: civic institutions, scholarship, commerce, international exchange, and cultural cultivation. During World War I his patriotism became entangled with propaganda work and fear. The experience ultimately sharpened his concern with mass irrationality and the political power of images.

### Rationality and magic

Warburg did not write a simple story in which enlightened reason permanently defeats superstition. Astrology, ritual, and myth were recurring human attempts to orient the self in a dangerous cosmos. Rational distance was an achievement, but one always vulnerable to collapse.

His recurring term **Denkraum**—“thought-space”—described the interval humans create between stimulus and response, self and world, fear and action. Symbols could create that distance, but they could also reactivate fear and ecstatic identification.

### Art history as cultural science

Warburg opposed the isolation of “fine art” from religion, commerce, science, technology, festival, politics, and everyday visual culture. He preferred *Kulturwissenschaft*, cultural science, and sometimes called himself a historian of images rather than an art historian.

### Core concepts

- **Nachleben der Antike:** the afterlife, survival, or continued life of antiquity.
- **Pathosformel:** inherited expressive formula carrying intensified emotional energy.
- **Denkraum:** reflective space or distance between human beings and compelling forces.
- **Polarität:** polarity; an image may move between opposed meanings.
- **Wanderstraße:** migration route through which images and symbols travel.
- **Ikonologie des Zwischenraumes:** iconology of the interval.
- **Mnemosyne:** cultural memory understood through mobile, material images.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Mary Warburg

Spouse, artist, household manager, mother of his children, and central support during illness. Her independent artistic career has often been obscured by biographies centered on Aby.

### Max Warburg

Younger brother, banker, financial supporter, negotiator with doctors and institutions, and a major guarantor of the library. Their relationship joined affection, conflict, money, and civic ambition.

### Paul and Felix Warburg

Younger brothers in American banking. Paul’s wedding occasioned Aby’s American trip. Both contributed to the transatlantic family network and financial backing surrounding the library.

### Fritz Saxl

Austrian art historian who joined Warburg in 1913. Saxl preserved the library during Warburg’s illness, transformed it into an institute, collaborated intellectually with him, and later led its move to London.

### Gertrud Bing

Joined the library in 1921 after studying Lessing and Leibniz under Cassirer. From 1924 she became Warburg’s closest research assistant and traveled with him in Italy. She later edited and interpreted his work and directed the London institute from 1955 to 1959.

### Ernst Cassirer

Philosopher at Hamburg, user of the library, and intellectual interlocutor. Cassirer’s philosophy of symbolic forms and Warburg’s history of images developed in productive proximity. His visits during Warburg’s hospitalization helped reestablish Warburg’s scholarly confidence.

### Erwin Panofsky

Art historian associated with Hamburg and the Warburg Library. Panofsky’s iconology developed in dialogue with the Warburg circle, though the later Panofskian system was more methodically codified than Warburg’s mobile, psychological approach.

### Edgar Wind

A younger scholar of the institute who participated in the editorial and theoretical transmission of Warburg’s work, particularly in the English-speaking world.

### Franz Boas

Anthropologist whom Warburg met in the United States and with whom he corresponded from 1895 to 1928. Boas advised and exchanged literature with him; both opposed simplistic racial-nationalist models of culture, although their methods and field authority differed.

### Ludwig Binswanger

Psychiatrist and director of Bellevue. Their relationship was simultaneously custodial, adversarial, and intellectually charged. Binswanger’s diagnosis and treatment became a later controversy.

### Emil Kraepelin

Consulting psychiatrist who rejected the pessimistic schizophrenia prognosis in favor of a mixed manic-depressive state and predicted recovery.

### Carl Georg Heise

Art historian and personal acquaintance who later wrote recollections of Warburg and criticized Binswanger’s treatment.

### Alfred Lichtwark and Gustav Pauli

Hamburg museum directors and cultural organizers connected to Warburg’s civic and artistic network.

### Jacques Mesnil

Pseudonym of Belgian art historian and physician Jean-Jacques Dwelshauvers. He corresponded with Warburg about Flemish and Florentine culture.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Contested Questions

### 1. Indigenous materials and colonial extraction

**Documented facts:**

- Warburg photographed Indigenous people and ceremonies.
- He collected Indigenous cultural objects and information.
- His access depended partly on anthropologists, missionaries, reservation personnel, and colonial administrative structures.
- Some Pueblo authorities regard portions of the archive as culturally sensitive.
- The Warburg Institute has restricted reproduction of identified material and consulted descendant communities.

**Disputed questions:**

- Whether consent given in the 1890s can authorize perpetual public circulation.
- Whether some photographs or objects should ever have been collected.
- Whether Warburg’s scholarship challenged European hierarchy or ultimately reproduced it.
- Whether publication of the serpent-ritual material violated Warburg’s own wishes as well as Indigenous restrictions.

No single label—pioneer, appropriator, colonial observer, or ally—captures the entire record.

### 2. Publication against Warburg’s wishes

The serpent-ritual lecture was first published in English in the *Journal of the Warburg Institute* in 1939, a decade after his death. Evidence cited by later critics indicates that Warburg did not wish the text circulated as a definitive scientific publication. The posthumous text was edited from a lecture and notes. Consequently, quotations from “A Lecture on Serpent Ritual” should not automatically be treated as a polished book authorized by Warburg.

### 3. Psychiatric violence

Clinical records report violent behavior, threats to family, a firearm episode, and a near-fatal assault on a nurse.

**Evidentiary status:** documented in medical or family accounts; no criminal prosecution or judicial finding located. The events occurred during severe psychosis and institutional confinement. That context explains their documentation but does not erase the danger to others.

### 4. Treatment and diagnosis

Binswanger’s schizophrenia diagnosis has been criticized as mistaken and therapeutically pessimistic. Kraepelin’s manic-depressive diagnosis offered a more favorable prognosis. Modern reassessments remain retrospective. Claims that the snake lecture single-handedly cured Warburg are hagiographic simplifications.

### 5. Family money and scholarly independence

Warburg’s institution was intellectually innovative but privately financed by banking wealth. His self-image as a “scholarly private banker” acknowledged the parallel. Scholarship that presents the library as the achievement of an isolated genius can conceal the labor of Mary Warburg, Saxl, Bing, librarians, photographers, assistants, and the family’s financial apparatus.

### 6. Nationalism and wartime propaganda

Warburg collected and analyzed propaganda but also participated emotionally in German wartime nationalism. His later reputation as a cosmopolitan critic of irrational politics should not retroactively erase his initial patriotic commitments.

### 7. Alleged sexual relationship with Gertrud Bing

**Unverified interpretation:** Their emotional and intellectual closeness has encouraged speculation. Available institutional accounts document an intensive scholarly partnership and shared travel, not a proven affair.

### 8. Claims of occultism

Warburg extensively studied astrology, magic, divination, ritual, and esoteric imagery. This has led popular accounts to call his library an “occult library” or Warburg an occultist.

**Finding:** He studied occult and magical systems historically. No credible evidence establishes that he founded an occult order, practiced ceremonial magic as a religious commitment, or considered astrological prediction scientifically valid.

### 9. Banking and political conspiracy narratives

Because his brothers Max and Paul were prominent bankers, Aby appears in narratives alleging that the “Warburg family” secretly controlled Germany, the Federal Reserve, international finance, communism, Zionism, or opposing sides in World War I.

**Finding:** Such narratives characteristically merge distinct relatives, institutions, and dates. Aby was a financially dependent family member and library founder, not an executive architect of the Federal Reserve or a controller of German state finance. Max advised German authorities and Paul served on the Federal Reserve Board; these documented roles do not establish the larger conspiratorial claims. The narratives descend substantially from antisemitic myths about coordinated Jewish financial control.

### 10. Lawsuits

No lawsuit involving Aby personally was located. A major 2011–2014 High Court dispute concerned the later Warburg Institute and University of London’s obligations under the 1944 trust. It occurred more than eighty years after his death and should not be attributed to him.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Self-Created Mythology

### “The birthright sold for books”

**Story:** Thirteen-year-old Aby gave the family bank to twelve-year-old Max in return for every book he wanted.

**Appearance and circulation:** Told retrospectively by Max and repeated by institutional histories, biographers, and journalists.

**Evidence:** Consistent with the brothers’ eventual careers and family financing, but not preserved as a legally operative childhood contract.

**Reputational effect:** Cast Aby as an intellectual Jacob or anti-Esau, converting banking inheritance into a library.

### “The serpent ritual cured him”

**Story:** Warburg proved his sanity by delivering the Pueblo lecture and was released.

**Evidence for:** The lecture was a major demonstration of sustained intellectual work during recovery.

**Evidence against literal version:** It occurred in April 1923; discharge came in August 1924. Kraepelin’s consultation, changing prognosis, gradual improvement, and continued treatment intervened.

**Effect:** Made Warburg’s scholarship and illness appear symbolically identical and encouraged romantic accounts of madness cured by knowledge.

### Warburg as “mad genius”

**Story:** His fragmentary writing, associative library, Atlas, and psychosis were products of the same visionary mind.

**Evidence:** His illness and unconventional practices are documented.

**Problem:** The story can reduce scholarship to pathology, overlook disciplined archival research, and treat violence or suffering aesthetically. Conversely, sanitized institutional histories sometimes detach the work too completely from his psychiatric experience.

### Warburg as founder of iconology

**Story:** Warburg single-handedly invented iconology.

**Evidence:** His 1912 Schifanoia lecture and interdisciplinary method were foundational.

**Qualification:** Iconographic interpretation had older histories, and the more standardized three-level iconological method is especially associated with Panofsky. Warburg’s method was less a closed system than an experimental cultural science.

### Warburg as inventor of the internet, Google Images, or the meme

**Modern analogy:** Commentators compare the Atlas and associative library to hyperlinks, image search, databases, or memes.

**Evidence:** His method did juxtapose reproducible images and trace repeated motifs.

**Status:** Retrospective analogy, not literal technological invention. He designed no electronic network, search algorithm, or digital protocol.

### Warburg as prophet of fascist image politics

His studies of emotional formulas, mass imagery, astrology, propaganda, and political irrationality have been read as anticipating fascism. He died before Nazi rule and did not analyze fascist visual culture as a completed political phenomenon. His framework proved useful to later analysis, but “prophecy” is retrospective.

### The Atlas as completed masterpiece

The Atlas is often displayed as a finished canonical work. It remained under revision and lacked a final authorized text, sequence, and publication. Every modern edition is partly reconstructive.

## Works by Aby Warburg

Warburg’s output consists of lifetime publications, lectures, unpublished manuscripts, correspondence, notebooks, photographs, and posthumously edited collections. The following is a substantial rather than absolutely exhaustive analytical bibliography.

### Lifetime publications and major lectures

1. **1893 — *Sandro Botticellis “Geburt der Venus” und “Frühling”: Eine Untersuchung über die Vorstellungen von der Antike in der italienischen Frührenaissance*.**  
   Published doctoral dissertation. Genuine lifetime publication.

2. **1893 — “Matteo de’ Strozzi.”**  
   Short Renaissance-historical study; later collected.

3. **1895 — “Die mittelalterliche Vorstellungswelt im Festzuge des Lorenzo de’ Medici.”**  
   Study of medieval imagery and Medicean festival culture.

4. **1897 — “Eine Reise durch das Gebiet der Pueblo-Indianer in Neu-Mexico und Arizona.”**  
   Hamburg lecture based on the American journey. Not fully published as an authorized book during his lifetime.

5. **1898 — “Sandro Botticelli.”**  
   Published contribution for an art-appreciation series.

6. **1899 — “Die Bilderchronik eines florentinischen Goldschmiedes.”**  
   Study of a Florentine goldsmith’s picture chronicle.

7. **1902 — “Bildniskunst und florentinisches Bürgertum.”**  
   Major essay on portraiture, patronage, and Florentine bourgeois society.

8. **1905 — “Delle ‘Imprese Amorose’ nelle più antiche incisioni fiorentine.”**  
   Italian-language study of amorous devices in early Florentine engravings.

9. **1905/1906 — “Dürer und die italienische Antike.”**  
   Lecture delivered in 1905 and published in 1906; an important formulation of *Pathosformel*.

10. **1907 — “Francesco Sassettis letztwillige Verfügung.”**  
    Study of Sassetti’s will, religious outlook, family, and patronage.

11. **1908 — “Der Baubeginn des Palazzo Medici.”**  
    On the beginning of construction of the Medici palace.

12. **1911 — “Eine astronomische Himmelsdarstellung in der alten Sakristei von S. Lorenzo in Florenz.”**  
    Identification and interpretation of an astronomical ceiling.

13. **1912/1922 — “Italienische Kunst und internationale Astrologie im Palazzo Schifanoia zu Ferrara.”**  
    Originated as the 1912 Rome congress lecture; published in expanded form later.

14. **1914 — “Der Eintritt des antikisierenden Idealstils in die Malerei der Frührenaissance.”**  
    On the entry of the classicizing ideal style into Early Renaissance painting.

15. **1920 — *Heidnisch-antike Weissagung in Wort und Bild zu Luthers Zeiten*.**  
    Major lifetime monograph on pagan prophecy, astrology, image, and word in Luther’s age.

16. **1923 — “Bilder aus dem Gebiet der Pueblo-Indianer in Nord-Amerika” / serpent-ritual lecture.**  
    Delivered at Bellevue; posthumously published in edited form. Not an authorized lifetime book.

17. **1926 — Burckhardt seminar and related lectures.**  
    Surviving through notes and later editions.

18. **1926 — “Idea Vincit” stamp project.**  
    Designed by Otto Heinrich Strohmeyer on Warburg’s commission; Warburg originated the intellectual program, not the graphic execution.

19. **1927 — Rembrandt and Italian antiquity lecture.**  
    Survives in archival and edited form.

20. **1928–1929 — Roman and Atlas lectures.**  
    Working presentations associated with *Mnemosyne*.

### Unfinished and posthumously constituted works

21. ***Bilderatlas Mnemosyne*.**  
    Unfinished at death. Modern versions reconstruct panel arrangements from 1928–1929 photographs and surviving materials.

22. ***Grundlegende Bruchstücke zu einer pragmatischen Ausdruckskunde*.**  
    Fragments toward a theory or science of expression; posthumously edited.

23. ***Tagebuch der Kulturwissenschaftlichen Bibliothek Warburg*.**  
    Institutional diary containing entries by Warburg, Gertrud Bing, and Fritz Saxl; posthumous scholarly edition.

24. ***Briefe*.**  
    Correspondence, 1873–1929; genuine letters published posthumously in edited form.

25. ***Bilderreihen und Ausstellungen*.**  
    Posthumous edition of image sequences and exhibitions.

26. ***Bilder aus dem Gebiet der Pueblo-Indianer in Nord-Amerika: Vorträge und Fotografien*.**  
    Critical posthumous edition of lectures and photographs.

27. ***Bildersammlung zur Geschichte von Sternglaube und Sternkunde im Hamburger Planetarium*.**  
    Exhibition assembled by Warburg and collaborators; opened posthumously in 1930.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### 1929–1933

Saxl, Bing, Edgar Wind, and colleagues prepared the first *Gesammelte Schriften*, published in 1932. The edition established Warburg’s Renaissance essays but could not publish the whole archive.

### Exile of the library

In December 1933, approximately 60,000 books, photographs, papers, and furnishings were shipped to London. The rapid transfer saved the institution from Nazi seizure or destruction. Staff and users included Jewish and politically threatened scholars.

### London institutionalization

The library became the Warburg Institute. Its work influenced:

- iconography and iconology;
- Renaissance studies;
- history of astrology and magic;
- history of science;
- classical reception;
- anthropology of images;
- visual studies;
- media studies;
- memory studies;
- intellectual history.

### Eclipse and rediscovery

Warburg’s reputation was initially mediated by Saxl, Bing, Wind, and Panofsky. Panofsky’s clearer methodological formulations often overshadowed Warburg’s fragmentary and experimental style.

Gombrich’s 1970 biography stimulated an international revival. In West Germany, Dieter Wuttke and Hamburg scholars helped restore Warburg to academic discussion. From the 1980s onward, French, Italian, German, Spanish, Portuguese, and English scholarship transformed him into a major theorist of images and cultural memory.

### Competing reinterpretations

Warburg has been reconstructed as:

- founder of iconology;
- cultural historian;
- image anthropologist;
- theorist of collective memory;
- historian of religion;
- media theorist before electronic media;
- diagnostician of modern mass politics;
- Jewish intellectual negotiating assimilation;
- colonial ethnographic observer;
- patient whose medical history informed his concepts;
- institution-builder whose greatest work was a library;
- fragmentary scholar later turned into a systematic master.

These interpretations are not mutually exclusive, but each selects different portions of an unusually heterogeneous archive.

### Institutional dispute

In 2011–2014, the Warburg Institute and University of London litigated the meaning of the 1944 trust. The High Court largely protected the collection’s maintenance as an independent unit. The dispute concerned stewardship and funding, not Aby’s conduct.

### Current status

The Warburg Institute remains part of the University of London. A major redevelopment has expanded its spaces and public profile. The original Hamburg building operates as the Warburg-Haus. The Atlas continues to be reconstructed, digitized, exhibited, and criticized.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted evidence are included. Mere common interests, contemporaneity, or posthumous comparison are excluded.

### Max Moritz Warburg

**Evidentiary label: documented fact.**

Aby’s younger brother, born in Hamburg in 1867. Max assumed the banking role Aby rejected, financed Aby’s books and institution, dealt with his medical care, and helped ensure the library’s survival. The celebrated childhood bargain is a documented family story but not a verifiable legal transaction.

### Paul Moritz Warburg

**Evidentiary label: documented fact.**

Younger brother. Paul’s 1895 New York wedding to Nina Loeb occasioned Aby’s journey to America. Paul’s banking career and Federal Reserve role became a source of later confusion and conspiracy narratives attaching financial power to Aby.

### Friedrich Nietzsche

**Evidentiary label: documented intellectual influence; no personal meeting.**

Warburg read Nietzsche and worked with a related polarity between disciplined distance and ecstatic or Dionysian force. His later lectures placed Nietzsche alongside Jacob Burckhardt as a thinker endangered by the forces he sought to understand. No evidence supports a personal meeting.

### Charles Robert Darwin

**Evidentiary label: documented intellectual influence; no personal meeting.**

Darwin’s *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals* contributed to Warburg’s interest in bodily gesture, inherited expression, and the biological dimension of emotion. This was textual influence, not personal contact.

### Martin Luther

**Evidentiary label: documented historical subject; no personal relationship.**

Luther was a central subject of Warburg’s 1920 study of prophecy and astrology. The connection is scholarly and posthumous: Warburg investigated the use of astrological imagery around Luther’s life and political-religious reputation.

### Sigmund Freud

**Evidentiary label: indirect documented institutional connection; no verified personal relationship.**

Binswanger discussed Warburg’s illness within a psychiatric world strongly affected by Freud and communicated about the case in that context. Modern comparisons between Warburg and Freud are extensive. No direct meeting or sustained correspondence between Warburg and Freud was established in the consulted sources.

### Frances Amelia Yates

**Evidentiary label: documented posthumous institutional influence.**

Yates joined the London Warburg Institute in 1941, twelve years after Warburg’s death. She developed what she called “Warburgian history,” integrating thought, science, art, imagery, and symbolism. They never met.

### Giordano Bruno

**Evidentiary label: documented posthumous institutional connection, not a personal connection.**

Bruno was not established as a major personal subject in the sources consulted for Warburg’s biography, but became central to Frances Yates’s work at the Warburg Institute. This is a connection through Warburg’s posthumous institution, not through Warburg personally.

### Other roster names

No sufficiently documented direct connection was established in the consulted sources with Walter Benjamin, Carl Gustav Jung, Albert Einstein, Martin Buber, Max Weber, Theodor W. Adorno, Rudolf Laban, or other roster members sometimes placed near Warburg in later intellectual histories. Their absence here is deliberate: analogy, shared period, or later reception does not establish meeting, correspondence, collaboration, or acknowledged influence.
