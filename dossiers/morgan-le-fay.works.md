## Works by Morgan le Fay

### Healing projects and created states

- **c. 1150:** Arthur’s projected cure. In Geoffrey of Monmouth’s *Vita Merlini*, Morgen examines Arthur’s wound and undertakes to cure him if he remains under her treatment; the poem ends before the outcome, so this is an unfinished **project**, not a completed cure or surviving object. ([Geoffrey of Monmouth, *Vita Merlini*](https://ancienttexts.org/library/celtic/ctexts/merlini.html); [Lucy Allen Paton, *Studies in the Fairy Mythology of Arthurian Romance*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/Studies_in_the_fairy_mythology_of_Arthurian_romance_%28IA_cu31924072606621%29.pdf))

- **c. 1167–1169:** Arthur’s restored and immortal body. Étienne de Rouen’s *Draco Normannicus* says that Morgan received, cured, nourished, revived, and made her wounded brother immortal: a bodily **transformation**, not an independently titled work. ([Michael Twomey, “Morgan Le Fay, Empress of the Wilderness”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/arthurian-literature-xxv/morgan-le-fay-empress-of-the-wilderness-a-newly-recovered-arthurian-text-in-london-bl-royal-12cix/52628C5A846D10A25DC6AA86FAE6BE38); [Carolyne Larrington, *King Arthur’s Enchantresses*](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780857714060_A24413993/preview-9780857714060_A24413993.pdf))

### Medicaments

- **c. 1170:** Morgan’s wound ointment. In Chrétien de Troyes’s *Erec et Enide*, Arthur has brought an ointment expressly said to have been made by his sister Morgan; applied daily, it is supposed to heal wounds to nerves or joints within a week. This is the clearest early material **obra** securely attributed to her. ([Base de français médiéval, critical-text record for *Erec et Enide*](https://txm-bfm.huma-num.fr/txm/catalog/ErecKu); [text and translation of the episode](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/French/DeTroyesErecEnidePartIII.php))

- **c. 1177–1181:** Morgan the Wise’s anti-madness ointment. In Chrétien’s *Yvain, ou le Chevalier au lion*, a lady produces an ointment that Morgan had given her with instructions that it would cure madness; it restores Yvain’s reason and also treats his injuries. The text securely makes Morgan its donor and authority, but—unlike *Erec et Enide*—does not unequivocally say that she manufactured it, so its status as her obra remains uncertain. ([Laine E. Doggett, *Love Cures*](https://www.psupress.org/books/titles/978-0-271-03531-4.html); [Judy Shoaf, “Yvain: Sources and Analogues”](https://people.clas.ufl.edu/jshoaf/2014/07/01/yvain-sources-and-analogues/))

- **c. 1180s–early 1190s:** Fâmurgân’s healing plaster or bandage. Hartmann von Aue’s German *Erec* transforms Chrétien’s brief ointment notice into a long account of a uniquely effective dressing left by Arthur’s deceased sister Fâmurgân. Hartmann calls her a goddess and magician but does not state as plainly as Chrétien that she made the dressing herself; it is therefore an adapted **manifestation** of Morgan’s medicament with qualified authorship. ([*Erek – digital*, dating and textual tradition](https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/en/erd/index.html); [Lucy Allen Paton, *Studies in the Fairy Mythology of Arthurian Romance*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/Studies_in_the_fairy_mythology_of_Arthurian_romance_%28IA_cu31924072606621%29.pdf); [“Zwischen Wunde und *twalm*”](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41244-020-00184-x))

### Textile work

- **c. 1170:** Gold-embroidered secular garment later remade as a chasuble. *Erec et Enide* attributes the embroidery or decorative “work” of a rich garment to Morgan in the Vale Perilous; she intended it for a lover, but Guinevere acquired it and converted or repurposed it as a liturgical vestment, which Enide subsequently placed on an altar. The surviving thing is thus a collaborative, mobile **obra** whose medium and function changed after it left Morgan’s hands. ([E. Jane Burns, *Sea of Silk*](https://dokumen.pub/sea-of-silk-a-textile-geography-of-womens-work-in-medieval-french-literature-9780812291254.html); [Base de français médiéval, *Erec et Enide*](https://txm-bfm.huma-num.fr/txm/catalog/ErecKu))

### Gifts that resist classification as Morgan’s works

- **c. 1155–1160:** Hector’s exceptionally beautiful horse. Benoît de Sainte-Maure’s *Roman de Troie* says that a fairy—named Orva in the transmitted text but widely identified by scholars as Morgan—gave the horse to Hector. Neither its manufacture nor even the identification of Orva with Morgan is textually secure, so it is a disputed possession or gift, not safely an obra by Morgan. ([Lucy Allen Paton, *Studies in the Fairy Mythology of Arthurian Romance*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/Studies_in_the_fairy_mythology_of_Arthurian_romance_%28IA_cu31924072606621%29.pdf); [Warburg Institute edition of *Le Roman de Troie*](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/pdf/neh1720b2329688E.pdf))

- **late twelfth or early thirteenth century:** The Fairy of Gibel’s protective ring. In the Occitan romance *Jaufre*, the subterranean Fairy of Gibel gives Jaufre a protective ring; comparison with the later Montgibel tradition has led scholars to identify her as Morgan. The romance does not say she made the ring, and the identification itself is inferential, so the object resists inclusion except as a possible Morgan-associated manifestation. ([Lucy Allen Paton, *Studies in the Fairy Mythology of Arthurian Romance*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/Studies_in_the_fairy_mythology_of_Arthurian_romance_%28IA_cu31924072606621%29.pdf); [Roger Sherman Loomis, discussion of *Jaufre* and the Fairy of Gibel](https://nlp.fi.muni.cz/projekty/ahisto/portal/book/1054?esearch=Prose+Lancelot%40persName&lpage=VII))

- **late twelfth or early thirteenth century:** The Fairy of Gibel’s collapsible pavilion. The same *Jaufre* episode associates the fairy with a marvellous tent capable of being reduced to a small compass, but it is presented as her gift rather than explicitly as her handiwork. Its attribution to Morgan depends on identifying the unnamed Fairy of Gibel with her, so it cannot securely be called Morgan’s obra. ([Roger Sherman Loomis, discussion of the pavilion and Morgan identification](https://nlp.fi.muni.cz/projekty/ahisto/portal/book/1054?esearch=Prose+Lancelot%40persName&lpage=VII); [Lucy Allen Paton, *Studies in the Fairy Mythology of Arthurian Romance*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/Studies_in_the_fairy_mythology_of_Arthurian_romance_%28IA_cu31924072606621%29.pdf))
