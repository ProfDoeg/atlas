# El Inca Garcilaso de la Vega: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier concerns the Peruvian-born chronicler, translator, soldier, cleric, and landholder born **Gómez Suárez de Figueroa** in 1539, later known as **Garcilaso de la Vega**, **Inca Garcilaso de la Vega**, or **El Inca**.

Because much of his early history comes from his own retrospective writings, evidence below is differentiated as follows:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary legal, notarial, ecclesiastical, testamentary, or publication records.
- **Reported fact:** stated by Garcilaso or early witnesses but not independently documented in full.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** inference from several documentary or textual sources.
- **Disputed claim:** competing interpretations exist.
- **Legend or myth:** culturally influential but unsupported as literal history.

Biographical dates and documentation are drawn especially from Garcilaso’s testament and codicils, property records, correspondence, early editions, the reconstructed postmortem inventory of his library, and the work of Aurelio Miró Quesada, Raúl Porras Barrenechea, José Durand, John Grier Varner, Antonio Cornejo Polar, and later specialists.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father: Sebastián Garcilaso de la Vega y Vargas

**Documented and reported facts.** Sebastián was an Extremaduran conquistador, probably born at Badajoz around 1507. He reached Peru in 1534 with forces associated with Pedro de Alvarado. He participated in the unstable military politics following the conquest and eventually served as corregidor of Cuzco, conventionally dated 1554–1556.

His ancestry connected him with the Vargas and Suárez de Figueroa families and, more remotely, with distinguished Iberian literary and aristocratic lineages. The son later emphasized descent from the medieval warrior Garci Pérez de Vargas and association with the lineage of the Spanish poet Garcilaso de la Vega.

During Gonzalo Pizarro’s rebellion, Sebastián’s conduct became politically damaging. Chroniclers said he gave Gonzalo Pizarro his horse “Salinillas” at the battle of Huarina in 1547. Garcilaso later maintained that the battle had effectively ended and his father acted under compulsion or ordinary assistance rather than treason. Sebastián subsequently joined Pedro de la Gasca’s royalist forces shortly before the decisive battle of Jaquijahuana in 1548, producing the hostile epithet **“leal de tres horas”**—“loyal for three hours.”

That episode later obstructed Gómez Suárez’s effort to obtain royal favor in Spain.

### Mother: Chimpu Ocllo / Isabel Suárez

Chimpu Ocllo was an Inca **palla**, a woman of royal or high noble lineage. She received the Christian name **Isabel Suárez**.

Garcilaso described her as daughter of Huallpa Túpac and granddaughter of Túpac Inca Yupanqui. Genealogical formulations differ over whether she was niece, great-niece, or close collateral kinswoman of Huayna Cápac and how exactly she was related to Huáscar and Atahualpa. The secure point is that she belonged to Cuzco’s Inca dynastic nobility; the precise European-style degree of kinship is not uniformly reconstructed because Andean kin categories and later genealogical representations do not map cleanly onto Spanish terminology.

Sebastián and Chimpu Ocllo lived together for about a decade but did not contract a canonical marriage. Their relationship existed within the asymmetrical colonial system that joined conquistadors to Indigenous noblewomen. The surviving evidence does not establish whether it should be described as consensual partnership, concubinage, coercive possession, or some combination conditioned by conquest. Later romantic accounts of an uncomplicated “love union” are not documentary findings.

Around 1548–1549 Sebastián married the Spanish woman **Luisa Martel de los Ríos**, under pressure from Crown and church policies encouraging Spaniards to end nonmarital Indigenous unions. Chimpu Ocllo then married **Juan del Pedroche**, apparently with property or a dowry supplied by Sebastián. Her testament, found in Cuzco in the twentieth century, documents her later family and material circumstances. She died in 1571.

### Siblings and half-siblings

- **Isabel**, usually identified as Garcilaso’s full sister, was born approximately a year after him. Details of her life remain sparse.
- Through Chimpu Ocllo’s marriage to Juan del Pedroche, Garcilaso had maternal half-sisters, commonly identified as **Ana Ruiz** and **Luisa de Herrera**.
- Sebastián and Luisa Martel had daughters, giving Garcilaso paternal half-sisters. The surviving popular genealogies vary in naming and should not be treated as uniformly secure.

### Paternal protector: Alonso de Vargas

After Gómez Suárez reached Spain, his paternal uncle **Alonso de Vargas**, a veteran of imperial campaigns in Europe, received him at Montilla. Alonso became his principal protector, military model, and material patron. His household and estate gave the immigrant stability and entry into Andalusian landed and aristocratic society.

### Son: Diego de Vargas

Garcilaso never married. Older biographies frequently described him as childless or treated Diego de Vargas merely as a servant or ward.

**Documented revision.** Cathedral chapter records examined in Córdoba identify **Diego de Vargas as Garcilaso’s natural son** and **Beatriz de la Vega** as Diego’s mother. Garcilaso’s testament calls Diego a person he had raised throughout his life and grants him a lifetime annuity of eighty ducats, potentially doubled if Beatriz died. Diego became a sacristan at Córdoba Cathedral and handled aspects of his father’s burial.

Garcilaso did not explicitly call Diego his son in the surviving testament. That silence probably reflected illegitimacy, clerical status, inheritance strategy, or social discretion; the exact motive is unresolved.

### Beatriz de la Vega

Beatriz was a member of Garcilaso’s household and the mother of Diego. The testament calls her his **criada**, a term that could mean servant, household dependent, or a person raised within a household. It bequeathed her furnishings, kitchenware, bedding, and an annual income. The evidence establishes a household and parental relationship but does not fully reconstruct the duration or character of her relationship with Garcilaso.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1539–1560

Garcilaso was born only seven years after the capture of Atahualpa and while Spanish authority in the Andes remained unstable. His childhood coincided with civil wars among conquistadors, Indigenous resistance, the rebellion of Gonzalo Pizarro, and the consolidation of viceregal government.

He grew up in Cuzco among both Spanish conquistador society and his mother’s Inca relatives. Quechua was a childhood language. He later claimed privileged access to family recollections, ceremonies, genealogies, place-names, and linguistic distinctions unknown or misunderstood by monolingual Spanish chroniclers.

His narrative of childhood contains recollections of elderly Inca relatives mourning the lost polity. The famous scene in which an old maternal relative recounts Inca history to the boy is a foundational episode in the *Comentarios reales*. It is autobiographical testimony shaped many decades later into a literary scene; no independent record confirms its exact wording.

He witnessed or remembered:

- continuing consequences of Manco Inca’s rebellion;
- elite Indigenous families adapting to Spanish rule;
- conquistador factional conflict;
- the execution of rebel leaders;
- Catholic evangelization;
- the transformation of Cuzco’s sacred and political landscape;
- the use of Inca buildings by Spanish institutions.

Garcilaso says that during dangerous fighting his mother and household sought protection with relatives. Some episodes, including the family’s danger during Gonzalo Pizarro’s revolt, are plausible and internally detailed but rest largely on his later account.

### Education

Garcilaso studied with other sons of conquerors and Indigenous noblewomen under **Juan de Cuéllar**, a canon of Cuzco Cathedral. Cuéllar reportedly taught grammar and Latin and lamented that Peru’s first-generation mestizo pupils lacked more advanced educational opportunities.

His education was mixed:

- oral Quechua histories from maternal relatives;
- horsemanship and military culture from his father’s circle;
- Christian doctrine and Spanish letters;
- elementary Latin;
- familiarity with colonial legal hierarchies;
- firsthand knowledge of Indigenous and Spanish speech.

His later classical learning was principally acquired in Spain through sustained reading, conversation with humanists and clergy, and translation work.

## Migration to Spain, 1560–1561

Sebastián died in 1559. His will provided Gómez Suárez with money—often reported as approximately 4,000 pesos—to study or establish himself in Spain.

Garcilaso left Peru in 1560, probably traveling from Cuzco toward Lima and Callao and then through the Caribbean-Atlantic imperial route. Reconstructions commonly give Panama, Cartagena, Havana, the Azores, and Lisbon as stages, but not every stop is equally documented in contemporary travel records. He reached Spain later in 1560 and never returned to the Americas.

He visited paternal family territories in Extremadura and proceeded to the royal court.

### Petition at court and rejection

At Madrid he sought recognition, compensation, or permission connected with his father’s services. The Council of the Indies official **Lope García de Castro** confronted him with accounts of Sebastián’s aid to Gonzalo Pizarro at Huarina, particularly the horse episode. Gómez defended his father, but the petition failed.

This was a major biographical rupture:

- his anticipated reward from imperial service did not materialize;
- his father’s reputation remained compromised;
- he abandoned, or at least deferred, plans to return to Peru;
- his later *Historia general del Perú* repeatedly rehabilitated Sebastián as a fundamentally loyal royal servant.

The interpretation that this rejection alone caused a lifelong psychological crisis is a scholarly reconstruction, not a diagnosis.

## Montilla, Name Change, and Military Career, 1561–1591

Gómez settled at Montilla under Alonso de Vargas’s protection. He entered the social world of the Marquisate of Priego, landholding, horse breeding, military obligation, ecclesiastical learning, and provincial humanism.

### Adoption of “Garcilaso de la Vega”

Between approximately 1563 and 1565, documents show him experimenting with names before settling on **Garcilaso de la Vega**. Later he increasingly added **Inca**.

The change joined several claims:

- recovery of his father’s name;
- identification with the prestigious Spanish poetic and military lineage;
- displacement of the name Gómez Suárez de Figueroa;
- later public assertion of Inca descent.

It should not be reduced either to simple assimilation or simple Indigenous resistance. His authorial name deliberately combined both genealogies.

### War of the Alpujarras

**Documented fact.** From March to December 1570, Garcilaso served in the campaign against the Morisco rebellion in Granada, in forces connected with the Marquis of Priego and ultimately commanded by Don Juan of Austria. He attained or used the rank of **captain**.

The rebellion followed coercive royal measures suppressing Morisco language, clothing, customs, and religious practices. Royal forces used executions, forced removals, enslavement, and destruction; rebel forces also committed killings and atrocities. The surviving records establish Garcilaso’s service in the repressive royal campaign, but do not identify particular killings or abuses personally committed by him.

Later patriotic representations sometimes turn this service into heroic defense of Spain; modern literary reconstructions sometimes imagine that a mestizo soldier must have identified sympathetically with the Moriscos. Neither inner position is established by direct testimony.

Garcilaso did not build a lasting professional military career after the campaign.

### Landholding and finances

His uncle’s patronage and inheritance made him a person of property rather than a dependent professional writer. He managed agricultural and financial interests around Montilla and later Córdoba. Notarial records associate him with:

- inherited property;
- land and houses;
- horses;
- agricultural income;
- censos, annuities, loans, or rent-producing obligations typical of early modern Spain;
- purchases and sales made through local notaries.

He was comfortable enough to assemble a library inventoried after death at **188 entries**, fund publication, maintain a household, provide annuities, and purchase a cathedral chapel. He was not an imperial magnate, and no dependable modern equivalent for his wealth can be calculated.

A surviving 1593 letter to antiquarian **Juan Fernández Franco** refers candidly to the “miseries of Spain” and considers a return to Peru if a suitable appointment could be obtained. It demonstrates that his position, while propertied, was not free of financial or vocational dissatisfaction.

## Intellectual Formation and Early Writing

### Humanist and religious environment

At Montilla, Córdoba, and nearby towns, Garcilaso developed connections with clergy, antiquarians, Jesuits, aristocratic patrons, printers, and learned readers. His library included classical authors, church books, histories, poetry, Italian literature, dictionaries, and works about the Americas.

Important intellectual currents included:

- Renaissance humanism;
- Christian providential history;
- Neoplatonism;
- philology and etymology;
- classical models of empire and civic virtue;
- Spanish genealogical culture;
- oral Andean historiography;
- colonial chronicles.

### León Hebreo

His first major publication was a translation of Judah Abravanel/León Hebreo’s Italian *Dialoghi d’amore*. The work’s Neoplatonic account of love as a cosmic principle offered Garcilaso both a linguistic exercise and a philosophical vocabulary for harmony, unity, hierarchy, and desire.

The translation was finished in the mid-1580s and published in Madrid in 1590 as *La traduzión del indio de los tres Diálogos de Amor de León Hebreo*. The self-description **“del indio”** publicly advertised American authorship.

It was not an original philosophical treatise, but a substantial authorial translation. Garcilaso compared versions, explained difficult passages, and fashioned Spanish prose from Italian. Later censorship of León Hebreo complicated circulation of the translation.

## Córdoba and Mature Career, approximately 1591–1616

Following family deaths and inheritance arrangements, Garcilaso moved to Córdoba around 1591. He lived in the cathedral parish of Santa María and entered minor ecclesiastical orders, probably around 1597. His testament calls him a cleric, but there is no secure evidence that he became a priest or regularly celebrated Mass.

### Gonzalo Silvestre and *La Florida del Inca*

Garcilaso repeatedly visited **Las Posadas**, where he interviewed the veteran **Gonzalo Silvestre**, participant in Hernando de Soto’s expedition through southeastern North America. He also used accounts associated with **Alonso de Carmona** and **Juan Coles**, and compared published histories.

A 1593 letter documents his movements between Córdoba and Las Posadas and his network with Juan Fernández Franco.

*La Florida del Inca* was therefore not eyewitness history. It was a literary-historical reconstruction produced through prolonged oral interviews, written memoranda, comparison, and classical narrative techniques. Garcilaso presented Indigenous North Americans as politically organized, courageous, and capable of eloquence, while retaining the framework of Spanish conquest and Christian expansion.

The book appeared at Lisbon in 1605.

### Blas Valera

The mestizo Jesuit **Blas Valera** was Garcilaso’s most important Andean written source. Garcilaso reported that Father Pedro Maldonado de Saavedra gave him surviving fragments of Valera’s damaged manuscript around 1598. Much of Valera’s work was said to have been destroyed during the English sack of Cádiz in 1596.

Garcilaso quoted or paraphrased Valera extensively, especially about language, religion, government, and Inca moral order. The degree of dependence later became a plagiarism controversy; see below.

### *Comentarios reales*

The first part of the *Comentarios reales* appeared at Lisbon in 1609. Its nine books cover:

- Inca origin traditions;
- rulers and expansion;
- political and social institutions;
- religion and ritual;
- agriculture and technologies;
- roads, bridges, architecture, and communication;
- Quechua vocabulary and etymology;
- relations among different Andean peoples;
- Spanish misunderstandings of Indigenous terms.

Garcilaso positioned himself as “commentator” and “glossator,” correcting Spanish historians through Quechua knowledge and family memory. His Inca realm is orderly, paternal, civilizing, and in some respects providentially prepared for Christianity.

### *Historia general del Perú*

The second part, licensed by 1613 and published posthumously at Córdoba in 1617, narrates:

- the Spanish invasion and conquest;
- the deaths of Atahualpa and later Inca rulers;
- disputes among the Pizarros and Almagros;
- Gonzalo Pizarro’s rebellion;
- Pedro de la Gasca’s pacification;
- subsequent revolts;
- the execution of Túpac Amaru I in 1572.

It is more openly personal than the first part, particularly in its defense of Garcilaso’s father and its address to “Indians, mestizos and creoles” as brothers and compatriots.

## Religious, Philosophical, and Political Development

Garcilaso remained an affirming Roman Catholic. His testament professed the Trinity and obedience to the Roman Church. He funded Masses, religious observances, and a chapel dedicated to souls in Purgatory.

His writings nevertheless preserved extensive information about Inca religion. He tended to divide pre-Inca peoples from the Incas in a developmental scheme: earlier peoples were represented as disordered or idolatrous, while the Incas approached natural religion, solar monotheism, moral law, and preparation for Christianity.

This scheme served several purposes:

- defense of Inca rationality against descriptions of barbarism;
- compatibility between noble Andean antiquity and Christian universal history;
- comparison of Cuzco with Rome;
- justification of his own dual inheritance;
- critique of Spanish linguistic ignorance and some colonial abuses.

It also distorted Andean diversity. Modern ethnohistory and archaeology do not support his harmonious imperial model in full. Inca expansion involved warfare, forced population transfers, tribute, hierarchy, and coercion. Garcilaso’s limited treatment of human sacrifice and internal conflict is a major evidentiary omission.

Politically, he remained a subject of the Spanish monarchy and did not advocate independence. At the same time, his reconstruction of legitimate Inca sovereignty, his account of Túpac Amaru I as rightful heir, and his address to Peru’s Indians, mestizos, and creoles later supplied anti-colonial readers with usable political language.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Documented or strongly supported

- **Alonso de Vargas:** paternal uncle, guardian, patron, military influence, and source of inheritance.
- **Gonzalo Silvestre:** friend and principal oral informant for *La Florida del Inca*.
- **Juan Fernández Franco:** antiquarian and correspondent; surviving letter dated Córdoba, 20 May 1593.
- **Pedro Maldonado de Saavedra:** clerical intermediary who supplied remnants attributed to Blas Valera.
- **Blas Valera:** intellectual predecessor and extensively acknowledged textual source; no known personal meeting.
- **Bernardo de Aldrete:** Córdoba humanist and philologist who praised or cited Garcilaso.
- **Francisco Fernández de Córdoba, Abbot of Rute:** learned contemporary who cited him.
- **Marquis of Priego and household:** regional patrons and military framework for his Alpujarras service.
- **Beatriz de la Vega:** household member and mother of his son.
- **Diego de Vargas:** natural son, dependent, cathedral sacristan, and participant in burial arrangements.

### Luis de Góngora

Notarial and inheritance matters placed Garcilaso in a limited commercial or legal relationship with **Luis de Góngora**. This should not be inflated into a close literary friendship. Their shared Córdoba environment and indirect family dealings are documented more securely than sustained personal intimacy.

### Miguel de Cervantes

Cervantes operated as a royal provisions commissioner in Andalusian towns, including the Montilla region, while Garcilaso maintained connections there. Claims that the two met are possible but not supported by a surviving letter, witness statement, or unequivocal record. Their near-simultaneous deaths later generated a powerful literary parallel.

## Property, Household, and Financial Arrangements

Garcilaso’s testament and inventory show a materially established household.

He possessed:

- real property and income-bearing rights;
- books and manuscripts;
- household furniture, bedding, kitchen copper, ceramics, glassware, and storage vessels;
- religious objects;
- weapons or objects associated with gentlemanly status;
- sufficient capital to establish perpetual religious obligations.

In 1612 he acquired or secured the Chapel of the Souls in Purgatory in Córdoba Cathedral. His foundation financed chaplains and commemorative Masses and provided his burial site.

His will gave lifetime incomes of eighty ducats each to Diego de Vargas and Beatriz de la Vega, with survivorship provisions. It also provided for servants, executors, religious foundations, funeral expenses, and publication-related matters.

No evidence found in the dedicated adversarial search supports fraud, money laundering, corrupt business structures, criminal prosecution, imprisonment, or civil litigation against Garcilaso himself. “Money laundering” and modern corporate categories are anachronistic to his documented affairs.

## Controversies, Accusations, and Disputed Questions

### 1. His father’s alleged treason

**Documented underlying event; disputed interpretation.** Sebastián Garcilaso aided Gonzalo Pizarro with a horse during the rebel victory at Huarina. Royal officials later treated this as compromising service.

Garcilaso argued that the aid occurred after the fighting or under circumstances that did not make his father a rebel. His mature history systematically rehabilitated Sebastián.

The facts of assistance are strongly attested; its voluntariness, military importance, and legal-moral meaning remain disputed.

### 2. Garcilaso’s participation in repression of the Moriscos

**Documented fact.** He served the Crown during the Alpujarras campaign and became a captain.

**Unestablished:** which individual actions he performed in battle or in the removal and punishment of Morisco civilians. Claims that he personally committed atrocities require evidence not presently identified. Conversely, romantic stories that he secretly sympathized with the rebels are literary speculation.

### 3. Plagiarism of Blas Valera

In the early twentieth century, historian **Manuel González de la Rosa** argued that Garcilaso had appropriated Valera’s history and that Valera deserved recognition as the foundational historian.

**Documented fact:** Garcilaso used Valera extensively.

**Counterevidence:** he repeatedly named Valera, quoted him, described the manuscript’s provenance, and distinguished Valera’s text from his own commentary. José de la Riva-Agüero and later scholars therefore rejected a simple plagiarism verdict.

Current discussion ranges among dependence, compilation, co-production across texts, appropriation under early modern conventions, and original transformation. Modern plagiarism rules cannot be mechanically imposed on seventeenth-century historiography, but the scale of borrowing is a legitimate authorship question.

### 4. Historical reliability

From the nineteenth century onward, scholars challenged the *Comentarios reales* on several grounds:

- an idealized, centralized, benevolent Inca state;
- projection of Roman and Renaissance political concepts;
- Christianization of Inca theology;
- silencing or minimizing human sacrifice;
- reduction of ethnic and regional diversity;
- dynastic chronology unsupported by archaeology;
- hostility to Atahualpa and favor toward the Cuzco/Huáscar line;
- retrospective family apologetics;
- writing roughly half a century after leaving Peru.

Defenses stress:

- childhood fluency in Quechua;
- access to royal-family oral history;
- careful correction of linguistic errors;
- transparent citation of many written sources;
- preservation of information unavailable elsewhere;
- the impropriety of judging oral or humanist historiography exclusively by modern positivist standards.

The strongest conclusion is that Garcilaso is indispensable but not self-verifying. Each claim requires comparison with archaeology, administrative records, and other chroniclers.

### 5. Reliability of *La Florida del Inca*

The work contains chronological and geographic problems and elevates oral reminiscence into classical epic narrative. Its Indigenous speeches cannot be treated as stenographic records.

Nevertheless, its dependence on expedition veterans and its preservation of otherwise lost traditions make it historically valuable. Scholarship remains divided over particular episodes rather than accepting or rejecting the work wholesale.

### 6. Maternal royal genealogy

Garcilaso’s Inca descent is not reasonably in doubt, but the exact dynastic line is disputed. Later nationalism and commemoration often simplified him into the literal son of an “Inca princess” closely related to every famous ruler. “Princess” is an approximate European rendering of **palla/ñusta**, not an exact constitutional title.

### 7. Identity politics and self-presentation

Garcilaso variously called himself Indian, mestizo, Inca, Peruvian, and Spaniard. Some interpreters see strategic changes designed for different audiences; others see a coherent composite identity. His celebrated claim that he called himself mestizo “with a full mouth” is genuine, but it coexists with his pursuit of Spanish gentlemanly and clerical status.

### 8. Silence concerning his son

Older scholarship accepted Garcilaso’s public celibacy and silence. Córdoba cathedral documents now make his paternity of Diego de Vargas difficult to dispute.

The concealment or euphemism is documented; its cause is not. Explanations involving clerical reputation, illegitimacy, inheritance, or status are hypotheses.

### 9. No modern scandal discovered

The requested 2024–2026 adversarial searches for scandal, fraud, lawsuit, criminality, conspiracy, and accusations produced no credible new personal allegations. Recent controversy remains historiographical: colonial violence, the Alpujarras, authorship, mestizaje as national ideology, and reliability as a source.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Propaganda

### “The first mestizo”

**Story:** Garcilaso was the first biological or cultural mestizo of the Americas.

**Evidence:** False if literal; many mixed-ancestry children preceded him.

**Historical function:** The phrase marks his stature as the first major American-born writer to make a public, canonical authorial identity from both Indigenous and Spanish descent.

### “Perfect synthesis of two worlds”

**Story:** His life reconciled Spanish and Inca civilizations harmoniously.

**Origin and circulation:** Particularly influential in twentieth-century Peruvian Hispanist and nationalist criticism, including Riva-Agüero and celebratory state discourse.

**Counterevidence:** His life included illegitimacy, maternal displacement, racial hierarchy, failed royal claims, permanent migration, colonial warfare, and repeated defense of his father.

**Effect:** Made him a national symbol but sometimes obscured coercion and conflict.

### “Inca prince”

His maternal nobility is real; representations of him as a straightforward hereditary prince or claimant to the imperial throne are unsupported. He never advanced a claim to rule Peru.

### Encounter with Cervantes

**Legend or unresolved possibility.** Their Andalusian movements could have crossed, but no direct meeting is established. Literary commemorations often turn geographical possibility into fact.

### Death on the same day as Cervantes and Shakespeare

Garcilaso and Cervantes died within roughly a day of each other in April 1616. Shakespeare died on 23 April under the Julian calendar then used in England, corresponding to 3 May Gregorian. Thus the familiar “all died the same day” story is symbolically powerful but calendrically misleading.

Garcilaso’s own death may have occurred late on 22 April or on 23 April. The testament dates from 18 April.

### The harmonious Inca utopia

The *Comentarios* generated the enduring image of an orderly, communitarian empire ruled by benevolent civilizers. Later writers treated this alternately as accurate memory, utopia, proto-socialism, authoritarian collectivism, or nationalist myth.

Archaeology and comparative ethnohistory document both sophisticated administration and imperial coercion. The utopian image is historically influential but cannot stand as a literal institutional description.

### The concealed “true history” of Blas Valera

Later theories, especially those surrounding controversial Miccinelli manuscripts, allege that Valera survived his official death, wrote secret works, collaborated with or manipulated other chroniclers, and encoded alternative histories in quipus.

The manuscripts’ authenticity has been intensely disputed. They cannot presently overturn the secure publication and archival record of Garcilaso’s authorship. The controversy remains significant as modern scholarly mythology and as a debate over Indigenous textuality.

### Alonso Sánchez de Huelva as pre-Columbian discoverer

Garcilaso transmitted the story that a Spanish pilot, Alonso Sánchez de Huelva, accidentally reached lands across the Atlantic before Columbus and communicated the route to him.

No reliable contemporary documentation establishes Sánchez’s voyage. The story belongs to early modern anti-Columbian or pre-discovery legend, though Garcilaso’s transmission helped preserve it.

## Works by Garcilaso

### Major works

1. **La traduzión del indio de los tres Diálogos de Amor de León Hebreo**  
   Madrid: Pedro Madrigal, 1590.  
   Translation from Italian of Judah Abravanel’s *Dialoghi d’amore*. Genuine translation by Garcilaso; not an original underlying treatise.

2. **La Florida del Ynca. Historia del adelantado Hernando de Soto, gobernador y capitán general del reino de la Florida, y de otros heroicos caballeros españoles e indios**  
   Lisbon: Pedro Crasbeeck, 1605.  
   Original historical-literary work based principally on veterans’ testimony and written chronicles.

3. **Primera parte de los Comentarios reales, que tratan del origen de los Yncas, reyes que fueron del Perú…**  
   Lisbon: Pedro Crasbeeck, 1609.  
   Genuine work. Commonly called *Comentarios reales de los incas*.

4. **Historia general del Perú. Trata el descubrimiento dél, y cómo lo ganaron los españoles; las guerras civiles que hubo entre Pizarros y Almagros…**  
   Córdoba: viuda de Andrés Barrera, 1617.  
   Genuine work, licensed before Garcilaso’s death and published posthumously. It is the second part of the *Comentarios reales*.

### Genealogical and shorter writings

5. **Relación de la descendencia de Garci Pérez de Vargas**  
   Written approximately 1596. A genealogical memorial concerning his paternal lineage. Genuine manuscript work; published much later.

6. **Anotaciones a la Historia general de las Indias de Francisco López de Gómara**  
   Marginal annotations in Garcilaso’s copy. Genuine reading notes, not an independently published book during his lifetime.

7. **Carta a Juan Fernández Franco**, Córdoba, 20 May 1593  
   Genuine surviving correspondence concerning Las Posadas, prospects for travel to Peru, patronage, and practical affairs.

8. **Dedications, prologues, and addresses** attached to the major books  
   Genuine authorial texts important for his identity and intended audiences.

9. **Testament and codicils**, April 1616  
   Not literary publications but central primary documents for his household, faith, property, son, burial, and final arrangements.

### Lost, projected, or uncertain works

Garcilaso referred to projected historical and genealogical undertakings. Not all survived or can be distinguished from material absorbed into his published books. No secure corpus of poetry, drama, patents, inventions, technical papers, or visual art is known.

## Death and Burial

Garcilaso became seriously ill in 1615 or early 1616. The precise diagnosis is unknown. No secure evidence supports addiction, psychiatric illness, poisoning, violent death, or accident.

On **18 April 1616**, “ill in body” but of sound mind, he executed his testament. He professed Catholic faith, organized debts and bequests, protected Diego and Beatriz, endowed religious observances, and directed burial in his chapel.

He died during the night of **22–23 April 1616**. The conventional date is 23 April. Diego de Vargas participated in arranging the burial.

Garcilaso was interred in the Chapel of the Souls in Purgatory in Córdoba’s Mosque-Cathedral. His second history appeared the following year.

In 1978, a portion of remains or funerary material attributed to him was ceremonially transferred from Córdoba to Cusco and deposited in the cathedral. The attribution is commemorative and ecclesiastical; no modern genetic identification establishes the remains individually.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries

Garcilaso quickly became an authority on Inca history and Peru. European translations and adaptations spread his account. Until the nineteenth century, many readers treated his dynastic narrative as the central history of the Incas.

The 1723 edition helped renew interest in Europe and Spanish America.

After the Túpac Amaru II rebellion, Charles III’s government ordered officials in 1782 to collect Garcilaso’s history, stating that Indigenous readers had learned dangerous or politically suggestive material from it. Earlier restrictions in 1729 and 1741 also affected circulation of works associated with conquest history.

The link between the book and rebellion is documented as a belief of colonial authorities. Precisely how Túpac Amaru II read and used Garcilaso is less completely documented than later patriotic narratives imply.

### Independence era

Garcilaso’s language of legitimate Inca sovereignty and common Peruvian belonging influenced creole and Indigenous historical imagination. José de San Martín sponsored or encouraged renewed publication of the *Comentarios*. Writers associated with independence read it as an American historical patrimony.

### Nineteenth-century criticism

William H. Prescott used Garcilaso extensively but distrusted aspects of his Indigenous memory and idealization. Marcelino Menéndez y Pelayo admired his prose while describing the *Comentarios* in terms approaching a political utopia or novel.

The discovery of additional chronicles and the rise of archaeology weakened his position as unquestioned factual authority.

### Twentieth-century national symbol

Peruvian intellectuals variously constructed him as:

- father of Peruvian literature;
- first great Hispano-American prose writer;
- emblem of mestizaje;
- Christian humanist;
- Indigenous cultural witness;
- divided colonial subject;
- architect of a conciliatory national myth.

Riva-Agüero’s “harmonious mestizaje” interpretation became especially influential in official commemoration. Cornejo Polar and other critics later emphasized conflict, heterogeneity, silences, and unequal colonial power.

### Continuing influence

His work remains important in:

- Andean history and ethnohistory;
- colonial literary studies;
- translation studies;
- Quechua linguistics;
- Indigenous and mestizo intellectual history;
- studies of oral testimony;
- Florida and southeastern North American colonial history;
- debates over national identity.

Institutions bearing his name include the **Universidad Inca Garcilaso de la Vega** and Peru’s **Centro Cultural Inca Garcilaso**. The modern university’s licensing and governance scandals concern that institution, not the historical person.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by family relationship, documentary contact, acknowledged textual dependence, or demonstrable later influence are included. Mere contemporaneity is excluded.

### Atahualpa

**Evidentiary label: reported family relationship; exact degree disputed.**

Garcilaso presented his maternal family as belonging to the Inca royal dynasty and treated Atahualpa as a relative of his mother. Modern summaries variously call Chimpu Ocllo Atahualpa’s cousin or niece. The exact degree is uncertain. Garcilaso never met Atahualpa, who died in 1533, six years before his birth.

Atahualpa is a major character in Garcilaso’s historical reconstruction. Garcilaso’s Cuzco dynastic loyalties contributed to a markedly hostile portrayal of Atahualpa as usurper and destroyer of Huáscar’s family.

### Blas Valera

**Evidentiary label: documented textual relationship; no documented meeting.**

Garcilaso received and extensively used fragments of Valera’s history, acknowledged him by name, and preserved material otherwise lost. The relationship generated the later plagiarism controversy. There is no secure evidence that the two men personally met.

### Francisco Pizarro González

**Evidentiary label: documented family-historical association, not documented personal meeting.**

Garcilaso’s father participated in the conquest society created by Pizarro and later in conflicts among the conquerors. Francisco Pizarro remained alive during Garcilaso’s infancy, dying in Lima in 1541, but no source consulted documents a personal encounter between the child and Pizarro. Pizarro is central to Garcilaso’s *Historia general del Perú*.

### Felipe Guamán Poma de Ayala

**Evidentiary label: parallel contemporary authorship; no documented personal connection.**

Both produced major accounts of Andean history and colonial rule in the early seventeenth century. No surviving evidence shows meeting, correspondence, or direct textual borrowing between them. Their juxtaposition is a later scholarly comparison.

### Miguel de Cervantes Saavedra

**Evidentiary label: possible geographical crossing; meeting unverified.**

Both had connections with Andalusia and Montilla. Later writers have suggested an encounter while Cervantes collected provisions for the Crown, but no direct documentary proof has been identified. Their deaths occurred within approximately a day in April 1616, encouraging a lasting symbolic association.

### Luis de Góngora

**Evidentiary label: documented or strongly reported limited legal-commercial connection.**

Family inheritance and financial dealings in Córdoba indirectly connected Garcilaso and Góngora. The evidence supports a transaction-level relationship, not a close literary friendship.

### José Gabriel Condorcanqui Noguera / Túpac Amaru II

**Evidentiary label: reported intellectual influence; colonial authorities treated it as fact.**

Garcilaso died more than a century before Condorcanqui’s birth. Later evidence and official policy associated the *Comentarios reales* with the intellectual environment of the 1780 rebellion. The Crown’s 1782 order to collect the book explicitly stated that Indigenous people had learned politically consequential material from it. The precise extent of Condorcanqui’s personal use remains less certain.

### Alexander von Humboldt

**Evidentiary label: documented literary and intellectual reception.**

Humboldt read and referred to Garcilaso while interpreting Andean geography, monuments, and history. This is posthumous influence, not personal contact.

### José de San Martín

**Evidentiary label: documented posthumous political-cultural reception.**

During the independence period, San Martín’s government encouraged republication and circulation of Garcilaso as part of the historical recovery of Peru’s Inca past. They were separated by two centuries; the connection is explicitly one of political and literary reception.

### Micaela Bastidas Puyucahua

**Evidentiary label: indirect posthumous contextual connection only.**

As a leader of the Túpac Amaru rebellion, Bastidas participated in the movement after which colonial authorities suppressed Garcilaso’s history. No individual reading or use of Garcilaso by Bastidas was established in the sources consulted.

### Absences

No documented personal connection was found with Christopher Columbus, Hernán Cortés, Miguel de Cervantes beyond the unresolved possibility noted above, or other roster members merely because they appeared in Garcilaso’s histories, lived in the same century, or later wrote about the Incas.
