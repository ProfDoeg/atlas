# Apostle Peter: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 24 August 2026  
**Scope:** Historical reconstruction, source criticism, traditions, attributed literature, reception, and legacy  

## Source-Critical Key

Peter is unusually well attested for a non-elite first-century Galilean, but the evidence is uneven.

- **Earliest evidence:** Paul’s undisputed letters, especially *Galatians* and *1 Corinthians* (c. 50s CE). Paul knew Cephas personally. These are the strongest evidence for Peter’s existence, leadership, travel, marriage, resurrection claim, and conflict with Paul.
- **Canonical narrative:** Mark (commonly dated c. 65–75), Matthew and Luke–Acts (c. 80–90), and John (c. 90–100, with debated compositional layers). These preserve earlier traditions but are theological narratives written decades after Jesus’s ministry.
- **Early post-New Testament witnesses:** *1 Clement* (usually c. 95, though broader dates are proposed), Ignatius, Papias as quoted by Eusebius, Dionysius of Corinth, Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, Tertullian, Origen, and Eusebius. Their distance from Peter and apologetic purposes vary.
- **Apocrypha and hagiography:** *Acts of Peter*, *Gospel of Peter*, *Apocalypse of Peter*, Pseudo-Clementines, later martyrdoms and saints’ lives. These are evidence for Peter’s developing image, not straightforward eyewitness biography.
- **Archaeology:** Capernaum and the Vatican necropolis illuminate settings and early veneration. Neither can identify Peter by modern forensic standards.

Dates below are approximate unless an ancient source supplies a secure synchronism. “Peter” and “Cephas” are treated as the same person, the dominant ancient and modern identification; the minority two-person hypothesis is noted below.

## Basic Identifying Information

| Religion | Jew of Galilee; follower and apostle of Jesus; leader in the earliest Jewish Jesus movement |

Peter was not called “pope” in his lifetime. The monarchical episcopate and the later papal office developed over time. Calling him “the first pope” accurately states Catholic retrospective doctrine and institutional genealogy, but it is anachronistic if treated as his first-century job title.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father and ancestry

The only claimed parent is the father embedded in the patronymic: Jonah/Jona in Matthew, John in John. No mother, tribe, extended ancestry, or social pedigree is recorded. Attempts to supply a genealogy are later speculation.

### Andrew

The canonical Gospels identify **Andrew** as Peter’s brother. Mark and Matthew call the brothers together while fishing; John makes Andrew a prior follower who brings Simon to Jesus. These are different narrative sequences and cannot be harmonized without inference.

### Wife, household, and possible children

Peter was married. Mark 1:29–31, followed by Matthew and Luke, describes Jesus healing Peter’s mother-in-law in the house associated with Simon and Andrew at Capernaum. Paul’s question in 1 Corinthians 9:5—whether he lacked the right to travel with “a believing wife, as do the other apostles and the brothers of the Lord and Cephas”—is the earliest direct evidence and suggests Peter’s wife accompanied missionary travel.

Her name is not preserved in an early reliable source. Clement of Alexandria, quoted by Eusebius centuries later, relays a story that Peter watched his wife being led to martyrdom and encouraged her, “Remember the Lord.” It is an early Christian tradition about their marriage, not independently corroborated biography. Later names assigned to her are not historically secure.

No child of Peter is securely attested. “My son Mark” in 1 Peter 5:13 is normally understood as a spiritual or mentoring relationship, not biological paternity. Later claims of descendants or particular Levantine families lack demonstrable first-century documentation.

### Social and financial position

Peter’s household was not necessarily destitute. Fishing required boats, nets, access rights, labor cooperation, processing, and markets. The Gospel traditions associate the brothers with nets and a boat; Mark portrays James and John as working with hired men, showing that the lakeside fishing economy could include small enterprise. Nothing establishes Peter’s personal property portfolio, net worth, debt, taxation, or ownership of the Capernaum house. Acts later idealizes apostolic sharing and depicts Peter disclaiming silver and gold. There are no surviving accounts, contracts, or property records attributable to him.

## Childhood and Early Life

No source describes Peter’s childhood, mother, schooling, adolescence, or precise date of migration. His origin at Bethsaida and later household at Capernaum imply movement within the northern Sea of Galilee region. Bethsaida’s exact archaeological identification remains debated; candidates include et-Tell and el-Araj.

Galilee was ruled during much of Peter’s early life by Herod Antipas under Roman supremacy. Its fishing economy was connected to local elites, taxation, tolls, salting, transport, and Greek-speaking markets. Peter’s occupation therefore joined manual skill to commercial networks.

Acts 4:13 calls Peter and John *agrammatoi kai idiōtai*. This can mean people without formal scribal/rabbinic education and without official standing, not necessarily total illiteracy. Nothing names a childhood teacher. Claims that he attended a rabbinic academy are unsupported.

## Education and Formation

Peter’s demonstrable formation was occupational, Jewish, communal, and then itinerant:

- practical training in fishing, boats, weather, nets, labor, and trade;
- synagogue and household exposure to Jewish scripture and practice;
- discipleship under Jesus, presented by all four Gospels as the decisive formation;
- leadership experience within the Twelve;
- post-Easter preaching, conflict, imprisonment, mission, and negotiation with other leaders.

John’s Gospel says Andrew had followed John the Baptist before introducing Simon to Jesus. It does not explicitly say Peter was John’s disciple. Later assertions should not convert Andrew’s biography into Peter’s.

## Early Career: Fisherman and Disciple

### Calling traditions

Mark 1:16–20, generally regarded as the earliest extant Gospel account, has Jesus call Simon and Andrew while they cast a net; they leave their nets. Matthew closely follows. Luke 5 relocates the call around a miraculous catch and Peter’s confession of sin. John 1 places the introduction through Andrew and has Jesus immediately confer the name Cephas. These accounts agree on Peter’s early and prominent attachment but differ in setting and sequence.

### Name and symbolic commission

The naming tradition is ancient: Paul already calls him Cephas. Matthew 16:16–19 gives the fullest “rock” commission: Peter confesses Jesus as Messiah, receives the name’s wordplay, the keys of the kingdom, and authority to bind and loose. Mark records the confession but not the keys or church saying; Luke abbreviates it; John supplies a naming scene at the first meeting and a pastoral commission after resurrection.

The historical core may be that Jesus gave Simon the Aramaic byname Kepha. The scope of authority encoded in Matthew is disputed:

- Catholic interpretation connects personal commission, keys, and succession in the Roman see.
- Orthodox interpretation grants Peter primacy or preeminence among apostles without universal jurisdiction in later papal form.
- Many Protestant readings identify the rock as Peter’s confession, Christ, or Peter in a nontransferable apostolic role.
- Historical criticism distinguishes an early Petrine tradition from later institutional conclusions.

### Inner circle and failures in the Gospel narratives

Peter belongs with James and John in an inner group at the raising of Jairus’s daughter, the Transfiguration, and Gethsemane. He frequently speaks for the disciples. The same narratives emphasize instability:

- he walks on water in Matthew but sinks in fear;
- he confesses Jesus, then rebukes the passion prediction and is answered “Get behind me, Satan”;
- he resists or misunderstands foot washing in John;
- he promises loyalty and then denies Jesus three times;
- he sleeps at Gethsemane;
- John identifies him as cutting off Malchus’s ear, while the Synoptics leave the assailant unnamed.

These are not modern criminal records. In the narratives, the sword attack is an act of violence during arrest; no trial of Peter for it is reported. The repeated exposure of the movement’s leading apostle as fearful and mistaken became central to Christian themes of failure, forgiveness, and restoration.

## Chronological Life History

### c. 27–30 CE: Ministry with Jesus

Peter leaves or repeatedly suspends fishing work to travel with Jesus through Galilee and, in the Gospel sequence, toward Jerusalem. His exact itinerary cannot be independently reconstructed. Major episodes include the call, healings in his household, appointment among the Twelve, mission activity, confession near Caesarea Philippi, Transfiguration, final journey, Last Supper, Gethsemane, arrest, and denial.

The Synoptics say Peter denied association with Jesus three times in the high priest’s courtyard. Mark’s manuscripts differ over the cockcrow details; the four accounts vary in the identity of questioners and setting. The common tradition is strong, while exact staging is literary and disputed.

### Resurrection traditions

Paul’s formula in 1 Corinthians 15:3–8, received before he wrote it, says the risen Christ appeared first to Cephas and then to the Twelve. This is the earliest extant claim connecting Peter to a resurrection appearance. Luke 24:34 contains a similar brief report. The Gospels narrate women at the empty tomb, Peter’s visit or race to the tomb, and appearances in differing sequences. John 21 stages a Galilean appearance, miraculous catch, threefold “Do you love me?”, and “Feed/tend my sheep,” commonly read as reversing the three denials.

What Peter subjectively experienced cannot be independently recovered. Historically, he was a principal claimant and transmitter of resurrection faith.

### c. 30s: Jerusalem leadership

Acts portrays Peter as spokesman in selecting a replacement for Judas, preaching at Pentecost, healing, judging Ananias and Sapphira, confronting authorities, and participating with John in Samaria. These speeches are Luke’s literary compositions, though they may preserve traditions.

The deaths of Ananias and Sapphira immediately after Peter exposes deception are among the most severe episodes attached to him. Acts presents divine judgment, not an execution ordered or physically carried out by Peter. There is no external investigation or legal record.

Acts reports arrests by Jerusalem authorities, detention, interrogation, flogging, and release. Acts 12 places Peter’s imprisonment under Herod Agrippa I, the execution of James son of Zebedee, an angelic escape, a visit to the house of Mary mother of John Mark, and departure “to another place.” The miracle is a faith narrative; the arrest under Agrippa is historically plausible but uncorroborated.

### Paul’s first meeting with Cephas

Paul says that three years after his call he visited Jerusalem, stayed with Cephas fifteen days, and saw James, the Lord’s brother (Galatians 1:18–19). Depending on the dating of Paul’s conversion, this is often placed in the mid-to-late 30s. It proves direct acquaintance and Cephas’s importance.

### Missions, Lydda, Joppa, and Caesarea

Acts 9–10 traces Peter through Lydda and Joppa and to the Caesarea household of the centurion Cornelius. A vision overturns purity boundaries, the Spirit falls on gentiles, and Peter orders baptism. Acts 11 has him defend the action in Jerusalem. Historically, Paul independently confirms Peter’s willingness to eat with gentiles before the Antioch confrontation. The specific visions and speeches belong to Acts’ theological narration.

### c. late 40s: Jerusalem agreement and Antioch dispute

In Galatians 2 Paul calls James, Cephas, and John “acknowledged pillars.” They recognize Paul’s gentile mission and extend fellowship, while Peter is associated with mission “to the circumcised.” The agreement includes remembering the poor.

At Antioch, Paul says Cephas had eaten with gentiles but withdrew when people came from James, fearing the circumcision group. Paul confronted him publicly and called the behavior hypocrisy. This is the clearest contemporary evidence of conflict. Paul does not record Peter’s reply or the outcome. Interpretations range from a temporary tactical retreat to a fundamental dispute over table fellowship and Torah. Claims of a permanent Peter–Paul schism go beyond the evidence; later traditions repeatedly pair them.

Acts 15 gives Peter a speech supporting gentile inclusion and James the concluding judgment. Its relationship to Galatians 2 is disputed: the passages may describe the same meeting reshaped by Luke, or different meetings.

### Corinth and itinerancy

1 Corinthians names factions saying “I belong to Paul,” “Apollos,” or “Cephas,” but does not prove Peter visited Corinth. Dionysius of Corinth, c. 170 and preserved by Eusebius, later associated both Peter and Paul with Corinth and Italy. Paul’s reference to Cephas traveling with a believing wife indicates mobile mission.

### Antioch as a see

Peter’s presence in Antioch is certain from Galatians. His status as its first bishop and a seven-year episcopate belongs to later church tradition. The Feast of the Chair of Peter at Antioch preserved that institutional memory. A first-century tenure with defined patriarchal office is not documentable.

### Rome

No undisputed writing of Peter says unambiguously that he was in Rome. Romans, written before Paul’s arrival there, greets many people but not Peter; this argues against a permanent 25-year Roman episcopate, not against any later visit. If 1 Peter is Petrine or carries a genuine Petrine setting, “Babylon” in 5:13 probably means Rome, but authorship and dating are disputed.

Evidence accumulates after his death:

1. *1 Clement* names Peter’s labors and death by “witness” or martyrdom in a Roman letter, without explicitly saying where he died.
2. Ignatius tells Roman Christians he does not command them “as Peter and Paul did,” implying a special Roman association but not supplying itinerary or death details.
3. Dionysius of Corinth says Peter and Paul taught in Italy and suffered martyrdom at the same time; “same time” need not mean the same day.
4. Irenaeus associates the Roman church’s foundation and organization with Peter and Paul and says they handed its episcopate to Linus.
5. Gaius of Rome, quoted by Eusebius around 200, points to the apostles’ “trophies” at the Vatican and Ostian Way.
6. Tertullian associates Peter’s passion at Rome with a death like the Lord’s.
7. Origen, quoted by Eusebius, supplies upside-down crucifixion.

The cumulative case makes a final Roman ministry and martyrdom probable to many historians, but does not validate every later detail. A minority rejects the Roman tradition or proposes an earlier death elsewhere. J. D. G. Dunn and other scholars have described Peter as a mediating “bridge” between James-oriented Jewish believers and Paul’s gentile mission; this is an interpretive model, not an office Peter claimed.

## Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

Peter belonged to no company in the modern sense. Relevant institutional formations were:

- a Galilean fishing household or partnership;
- the Twelve, an eschatological and missionary circle around Jesus;
- the Jerusalem assembly (*ekklēsia*);
- missionary networks using household hospitality;
- later communities that claimed Petrine foundation or teaching, especially Jerusalem, Antioch, and Rome.

Acts depicts common funds, distribution to the needy, and offerings laid at apostles’ feet. It also depicts Peter condemning Simon Magus’s attempt to buy spiritual power—the narrative source of the later term **simony**. No evidence ties Peter to personal enrichment, investments, fraud, money laundering, patents, or commercial ventures after his call.

## Religious, Political, and Intellectual Development

Peter remained a Jew within the earliest Jesus movement. The sources show development from messianic discipleship to resurrection proclamation and negotiation of gentile inclusion.

Politically, proclaiming an executed “Messiah” or “Lord” had potential conflict with both local authority and Roman imperial order, but Peter left no political manifesto. Acts depicts him telling the Jerusalem council that obedience to God supersedes human commands, while 1 Peter—if connected to him—urges submission to imperial and gubernatorial authority, honor for the emperor, endurance under slavery, and nonretaliation. The household code’s instruction that enslaved people submit even to harsh masters has had a long and controversial reception. Whether it represents Peter’s teaching depends on the letter’s authorship.

The Antioch incident shows neither simple legalism nor simple abolition of Torah. Peter initially ate with gentiles, then withdrew under pressure. His position may have been transitional, pragmatic, or inconsistent; Paul’s hostile description is the only firsthand account.

## Controversies, Accusations, Violence, and Disputed Questions

### Denial and abandonment

**Status:** multiply attested canonical tradition, exact details divergent. Peter denies Jesus under threat despite prior vows. The tradition is unlikely to be a later flattering invention, but its form is theological narrative.

### Sword violence at the arrest

**Status:** canonical narrative; John names Peter, earlier Synoptics do not. Peter cuts off a slave’s ear; Jesus rebukes the violence and, in Luke, heals the injury. No independent charge or trial survives.

### Ananias and Sapphira

**Status:** Acts narrative only. Peter confronts each over concealed proceeds; each dies. Acts attributes the deaths to divine judgment and does not describe Peter ordering physical punishment. Modern allegations that this was an apostolic execution are speculative.

### Ethnic/table separation at Antioch

**Status:** documented firsthand accusation by Paul. Paul says Peter withdrew from gentile meals out of fear and acted hypocritically. Peter’s defense and the resolution are lost. This is the strongest evidence of a serious fault alleged against him by a contemporary.

### Peter versus Paul

**Status:** real disagreement plus later ideological amplification. Galatians proves conflict; the Jerusalem handshake proves cooperation. Pseudo-Clementine literature later constructs a strongly anti-Pauline or Peter-versus-Simon framework, and modern interpreters sometimes identify its Simon Magus with a cipher for Paul. That identification is debated, not established.

### Primacy and papal claims

**Status:** theological and historical dispute, not a fraud investigation. Peter’s prominence is early and clear; a first-century monarchical episcopate in Rome is not. Catholic doctrine views the Petrine commission and Roman succession as continuous. Orthodox, Protestant, and historical-critical accounts distinguish primacy, local episcopacy, and later universal jurisdiction differently.

### Cephas and Peter as separate people

**Status:** minority scholarly hypothesis. Paul mostly says Cephas but uses Peter in Galatians 2:7–8; a few ancient and modern writers have argued for two figures. John explicitly translates Cephas as Peter, and the names’ semantic equivalence plus early reception favor one person. Textual or redactional questions remain.

### Literacy and pseudonymous writings

**Status:** unresolved for 1 Peter; broad critical consensus against direct authorship of 2 Peter. Secretarial assistance, especially the reference to Silvanus, is invoked in defense of 1 Peter’s authenticity. Counterarguments cite polished Greek, Septuagintal use, social setting, and distance from an eyewitness voice. Second Peter differs in style, depends closely on Jude in common scholarly reconstruction, treats apostolic tradition retrospectively, and had weak early attestation. Ancient doubts are recorded by Origen and Eusebius.

## Death, Martyrdom, and Burial

### What can be said with graded confidence

| Claim | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Peter died | Certain, date unknown. |
| He died as a martyr | Probable; *1 Clement* is early but vague, and later testimony converges. |
| He died in Rome under Nero | Probable but not contemporary eyewitness fact; usually located in 64–68 CE. |
| He was crucified | Plausible and relatively early; John 21:18–19 may allude to an imposed martyr death, while Tertullian and later witnesses specify a passion like Jesus’s. |
| He was crucified upside down | Later tradition first explicit in the late-second-century *Acts of Peter*; possible but unverified. |
| He chose inversion because unworthy to imitate Jesus | Later explanatory hagiography; the *Acts of Peter* itself gives a more cosmic symbolism of inversion. |
| He died on 29 June, the same day/year as Paul | Liturgical tradition, not securely established chronology. |
| Identified bones survive beneath St. Peter’s | Unproved. The site shows early veneration; particular remains lack a secure chain of custody and unique identification. |

### Vatican archaeology

Excavations beneath St. Peter’s in 1940–49 uncovered a Roman necropolis, a modest second-century aedicula commonly connected with Gaius’s “trophy,” the Constantinian memorial, graffiti, and complicated bone assemblages. Constantine’s basilica was engineered around this focal point at major cost, strong evidence that fourth-century Christians regarded it as Peter’s grave.

Margherita Guarducci argued that bones removed from a graffiti-wall niche belonged to a robust man of approximately 60–70 and were Peter’s relics. Antonio Ferrua and others disputed the archaeological sequence, inscriptions, custody, and identification. Pope Paul VI announced in 1968 that the relics had been identified in a manner judged convincing, but the announcement was carefully not a scientific certainty. No DNA comparison or identifying inscription can prove the individual. The defensible conclusion is an early and important Petrine memorial at the Vatican; the grave and bones’ personal identity remain uncertain.

Other relic traditions include Peter’s skull at St. John Lateran, chains at San Pietro in Vincoli, and remains or temporary deposits at the catacombs of San Sebastiano. Their documentary chains are late or contested. They are historical evidence for cult and pilgrimage, not forensic identifications.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Upside-down crucifixion

1. **Story:** Peter asks to be crucified head downward.
2. **Appearance:** Explicit in the late-second-century *Acts of Peter*; Origen’s version survives in Eusebius.
3. **Circulation:** Apocryphal acts, patristic writers, preaching, art, and liturgy.
4. **Evidence:** No contemporary account. The general martyrdom tradition is earlier; inversion is not.
5. **Effect:** The inverted cross became Peter’s attribute. In modern popular culture it is often misidentified exclusively as satanic, although its Petrine use is Christian.

### Quo vadis?

1. **Story:** Fleeing Rome, Peter meets Christ on the Appian Way and asks, “Lord, where are you going?” Christ answers that he is going to Rome to be crucified again; Peter returns to martyrdom.
2. **Appearance:** *Acts of Peter*, later localized at the church of Domine Quo Vadis.
3. **Circulation:** Hagiography, pilgrimage, Henryk Sienkiewicz’s novel *Quo Vadis*, films, sermons.
4. **Evidence:** No first-century support; it is a theological martyr narrative.
5. **Effect:** It made Peter’s second failure and final courage a durable story of repentance.

### Contest with Simon Magus

1. **Story:** Peter battles the magician Simon, whose flight over Rome ends when Peter’s prayer causes him to fall.
2. **Appearance:** Acts 8 contains only the Samaritan encounter and attempted purchase; Roman flight and miracle contest appear in later apocrypha and heresiology.
3. **Circulation:** *Acts of Peter*, Pseudo-Clementines, church fathers, medieval art.
4. **Evidence:** Historical Simon and encounter are not independently confirmed; spectacular Roman episodes are legendary.
5. **Effect:** Generated the word “simony” and a template of apostolic truth defeating magic/heresy.

### Peter as gatekeeper of heaven

1. **Story:** Peter personally guards heaven’s gate and checks entrants.
2. **Appearance:** Popular elaboration of Matthew’s “keys of the kingdom,” developed in medieval and modern folklore rather than early biography.
3. **Circulation:** Art, jokes, literature, film, popular theology.
4. **Evidence:** Matthew gives keys and binding/loosing authority; it does not narrate a postmortem gatekeeping job.
5. **Effect:** Made Peter one of the most recognizable Christian figures outside formal doctrine.

### Tears, rooster, and forgiveness

The rooster symbolizes denial; keys symbolize authority; a boat and nets symbolize fishing and church; an inverted cross symbolizes martyrdom. Art often shows the aged, short-bearded, balding apostle, a stable iconographic type rather than a portrait from life.

### Mamertine Prison and miraculous spring

Roman tradition says Peter and Paul were confined in the Mamertine/Tullianum prison and that Peter produced a spring to baptize guards. No first-century record connects Peter to the site. The legend shaped pilgrimage and sacred topography.

### “First pope for twenty-five years”

Later catalogues assign Peter a long Roman episcopate beginning in the 40s. This conflicts with the absence of Peter from Paul’s mid-50s letter to Rome and retrojects later episcopal structure. It remains a traditional regnal chronology rather than demonstrable biography.

### Hostile and alternative Peters

Some Gnostic or alternative Christian texts portray Peter as obtuse, jealous of Mary, hostile to women, or unable to grasp revelation. *Gospel of Thomas* 114 puts misogynistic language in Simon Peter’s mouth; *Gospel of Mary* has Levi rebuke him. These second-century or later texts reveal competition over gender and apostolic authority, not verified quotations. Conversely, Pseudo-Clementines make Peter the supreme repository of true teaching and opponent of error.

Jewish folklore in some later sources recasts Simon Peter as a covertly loyal Jew who deliberately separated Christianity from Judaism; the authorship of prayers such as *Nishmat* has also been attributed to him in legend. These traditions are late and historically unsupported but show interreligious attempts to domesticate or explain a foundational Christian figure.

Islamic scripture names no apostle individually. Later Muslim commentary sometimes identifies Peter as a disciple or one of the messengers connected to Antioch in interpretations of Qur’an 36. These are reception traditions, not independent first-century evidence.

## Works by or Attributed to Peter

Peter left no work whose autograph or direct dictation can be demonstrated. The bibliography must therefore be graded.

### Canonical works

1. **First Epistle of Peter** (*Petrou A*; 1 Peter), usually dated c. 70–100. Claims Peter as sender and Silvanus as intermediary; addressed to Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia; closes from “Babylon.” Traditional authorship remains defended, often using an amanuensis theory. Many critical scholars regard it as Petrine-school or pseudonymous writing after Peter’s death.
2. **Second Epistle of Peter** (*Petrou B*; 2 Peter), often dated c. 100–130, sometimes later. Claims “Simeon Peter,” presents a testament before death, recalls the Transfiguration, and refers to Paul’s letters as a collection. Widely judged pseudepigraphal; authenticity continues to have defenders. Its canonical status was disputed in antiquity.

### Speech and saying collections embedded in other works

- Speeches of Peter in Acts 1–5, 8, 10–11, and 15: Luke’s literary presentations, possibly containing inherited traditions; not stenographic transcripts.
- Sayings and dialogues in the four Gospels: transmitted by evangelists, not writings by Peter.
- Petrine material in Paul’s letters: Paul’s testimony, not Peter’s composition.

### Works linked through an “interpreter”

- **Gospel according to Mark**: anonymous in the text, conventionally dated c. 65–75. Papias, as quoted by Eusebius, says Mark became Peter’s interpreter and wrote remembered sayings/deeds accurately but not in order. Clement of Alexandria and Irenaeus extend the association. Modern scholarship debates the Papias tradition; Mark should not simply be catalogued as Peter’s memoir.

### Noncanonical pseudepigrapha and fragments

- **Gospel of Peter** (*Euangelion kata Petron*), probably second century; surviving Passion fragment from Akhmim. Not by Peter.
- **Acts of Peter** (*Praxeis Petrou*), late second century, originally Greek, largely surviving in Latin Codex Vercellensis; contains Simon Magus contest, *quo vadis*, and inverted crucifixion. Not by Peter.
- **Apocalypse/Revelation of Peter**, early-to-mid second century; Greek fragments and Ethiopic transmission; tour of rewards and punishments; nearly canonical in some communities. Not by Peter.
- **Preaching/Kerygma of Peter** (*Kērygma Petrou*), early Christian fragments quoted by Clement of Alexandria and others; lost; authorship pseudonymous.
- **Judgment of Peter**, known only through references and possibly related to the Apocalypse; lost/disputed identity.
- **Epistle of Peter to Philip**, Nag Hammadi text, probably second or third century; pseudonymous.
- **Letter of Peter to James** and **Contestatio**, prefatory Pseudo-Clementine documents; pseudonymous.
- **Pseudo-Clementine Homilies and Recognitions**, fourth-century forms drawing on earlier sources; Peter is protagonist and teacher, not author.
- **Acts of Peter and Andrew**, **Acts of Peter and the Twelve Apostles**, **Acts of Peter and Paul**, and assorted martyrdoms, church orders, dialogues, and Coptic/Ethiopic/Arabic Petrine texts: late, composite, and pseudonymous.
- **Gospel of Thomas**, **Gospel of Mary**, **Secret Book of James**, and other dialogues contain sayings assigned to Peter but do not claim genuine Petrine composition.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Church office and doctrine

Peter became the central apostolic symbol of unity, authority, repentance, pastoral care, and martyrdom. Rome’s bishops increasingly grounded their authority in Peter, especially in late antiquity. Catholic doctrine identifies the pope as successor to Peter; Vatican I defined papal primacy and infallibility in particular conditions. Eastern churches honor Peter as preeminent or chief among apostles while rejecting later Roman universal jurisdiction. Protestant traditions honor the apostle but commonly deny that Matthew 16 creates a hereditary papal monarchy.

### Cult and feast days

The shared feast of Saints Peter and Paul is 29 June in major Western and Eastern calendars. Western observances also include the Chair of Saint Peter (22 February), the Confession of Peter in Anglican/Lutheran calendars, and historically Peter’s Chains. He was never canonized through a later formal process; his cult predates centralized canonization.

### Patronage, names, and places

Peter is patron of fishermen, net makers, shipbuilders, locksmiths and others; keys made him a natural patron of locksmiths and gatekeepers. St. Peter’s Basilica, innumerable churches, cities, schools, and the name Peter/Pierre/Pietro/Pedro/Pyotr derive prestige from him. The crossed keys became an emblem of papal authority and Vatican City.

### Art and literature

Major image cycles include the calling, walking on water, confession, delivery of keys, Transfiguration, denial and rooster, repentance, release from prison, conflict with Simon Magus, *quo vadis*, crucifixion, and enthronement. Important works include Masaccio’s Brancacci Chapel scenes, Perugino’s *Delivery of the Keys*, Caravaggio’s *Crucifixion of Saint Peter*, and representations by Rubens, Rembrandt, El Greco, and many others.

Sienkiewicz’s *Quo Vadis* globalized the Appian Way legend. “St. Peter at the pearly gates” became a stock narrative frame in jokes and popular culture.

### Historical reinterpretation

Modern research shifted from harmonized saint’s life to memory analysis: the “historical Peter” recoverable from Paul; multiple Gospel portraits; Luke’s apostolic ideal; Roman martyr memory; competing apocryphal Peters; and later institutional appropriation. Feminist scholarship has examined texts that stage Peter against Mary; social historians emphasize fishing economies, household mission, ethnicity, and table fellowship; ecumenical scholarship separates Peter’s New Testament prominence from disputed forms of succession.

## Atlas Connections

### Jesus of Nazareth

- **[T5]** Peter was a disciple and close associate of Jesus in Galilee and Jerusalem, conventionally c. 27–30 CE. Their underlying teacher–disciple relationship is a **documented fact** of historical reconstruction; particular conversations, the conferral of the name Cephas, Peter’s denial, and his restoration are **reported facts** preserved in the canonical narratives.

### Paul the Apostle

- **[T5] [T1+] [T3]** Paul personally visited Cephas in Jerusalem for fifteen days, later recognized him as a “pillar,” and confronted him at Antioch, probably between the later 30s and early 50s CE. Paul also wrote extensively about Cephas/Peter in *Galatians* and *1 Corinthians*, making Peter the earlier, written-about party and creating one-sided surviving literary engagement. **Documented fact**, based on Paul’s firsthand testimony; Paul’s account of the Antioch dispute supplies only his side. [Galatians 1:18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Galatians+1%3A18&version=NRSVUE)

### Mary Magdalene

- **[T5]** John 20:1–3 depicts Mary Magdalene going directly to Simon Peter and the beloved disciple in Jerusalem to report that Jesus’s body was missing, prompting their journey to the tomb; both also belonged to Jesus’s immediate follower milieu. **Reported fact** in a late-first-century Gospel, not independently documented as a specific conversation. [John 20:1–8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+20%3A1-8&version=NRSVUE)

### Simon Magus

- **[T5]** Acts 8:14–24 places Peter and Simon together in Samaria, where Simon offers money for the apostles’ power and Peter condemns him. **Reported fact** from Acts, without independent contemporary corroboration. Their later Roman miracle contest is **myth**, first elaborated in apocryphal literature rather than established by the Acts encounter. [Acts 8](https://search.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts+8&version=NRSVUE)

### Tertullian

- **[T1+]** Around 200 CE, Tertullian wrote about Peter’s dispute with Paul, apostolic authority, connection with Rome, and death “like” the Lord’s in works including *Prescription Against Heretics*. Peter is therefore the earlier, written-about figure. **Documented fact** that Tertullian wrote these passages; his notice of Peter’s Roman crucifixion is a **reported fact** deriving from later Christian tradition. [*Prescription Against Heretics*](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0311.htm)

### Origen of Alexandria

- **[T1+]** In the third century, Origen wrote that Peter preached to the circumcised and was crucified head downward at Rome; the relevant passage from his lost commentary is transmitted by Eusebius in *Church History* 3.1. Peter is the earlier, written-about figure. The textual transmission is a **documented fact**; the head-downward crucifixion itself remains a **reported fact** based on late tradition. [Eusebius, *Church History* 3](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm)

### Augustine of Hippo

- **[T1+]** Augustine repeatedly interpreted Peter in sermons and theological works, including a sermon on Matthew 14 in which Peter’s faltering on the water and confession symbolize the Church. Composed in North Africa around the late fourth or early fifth century, this is a **documented fact** and makes Peter the earlier, written-about figure. [Augustine, Sermon 26](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/160326.htm)

### Dante Alighieri

- **[T1+]** Dante made Peter a speaking character in *Paradiso* 24 and 27, composed in the early fourteenth century: Peter examines Dante on faith and denounces corruption in the papacy. Peter is the earlier, written-about figure. **Documented fact**; the encounter inside the poem is literary fiction, not a claimed historical meeting. [University of Texas, “Peter: Fixed Stars, Paradiso 24”](https://danteworlds.laits.utexas.edu/textpopup/par2401.html)

### Martin Luther

- **[T1+]** Luther preached an exposition of the First Epistle of Peter at Wittenberg in 1522; Caspar Cruciger recorded it and published it in 1523 as *The First Epistle of St. Peter, Preached and Explained*. Because the exposition treats the apostle and a work traditionally attributed to him, Peter is the earlier, written-about figure. **Documented fact**; Luther’s acceptance or interpretation of Petrine authorship is not independent evidence that Peter composed the epistle. [Luther’s exposition of 1 Peter](https://www.martinluther.dk/1pet1.html)

### Constantine the Great

- **[T9+]** In the fourth century Constantine constructed the first monumental basilica around the Vatican memorial then revered as Peter’s tomb, physically enclosing the earlier “Trophy of Gaius.” Peter is the earlier/originating party in this site connection; Constantine is the later monumentalizer. Constantine’s building project and its orientation around the memorial are **documented facts**; identification of the underlying grave as Peter’s actual burial place is a **reported fact** grounded in ancient Roman Christian tradition, not a forensic certainty. [St. Peter’s Basilica, “The Tomb of Saint Peter”](https://www.basilicasanpietro.va/en/san-pietro/the-tomb-of-saint-peter)

### Michelangelo

- **[T9+]** Between 1546 and his death in 1564, Michelangelo reshaped the new St. Peter’s Basilica and designed its dome at the Vatican site traditionally identified with Peter’s burial and already monumentalized in Peter’s name. Peter is the earlier party in this shared-site connection. Michelangelo’s work on the basilica is a **documented fact**; the site’s attribution to Peter remains a **reported fact** rather than a conclusive identification of his remains. [St. Peter’s Basilica, “Who designed Saint Peter’s Basilica?”](https://www.basilicasanpietro.va/en/faq/who-designed-st-peters-basilica)

No defensible personal, textual, prophetic, or shared-object connection was found for the remaining roster members. Mere later devotion to Peter, use of his name, appearance in generic Christian art, or presence in places associated with his cult was not counted without a specific documented crossing.
