# André Breton: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier concerns the French poet, essayist, editor, collector, and principal organizer of historical Surrealism, André Breton (1896–1966).

Labels used for contested material:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a primary document, archive, contemporaneous record, or convergent authoritative sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a credible secondary source but not independently confirmed here from the underlying document.
- **Interpretation:** a scholarly or critical reading rather than a directly verifiable event.
- **Allegation or disputed claim:** asserted by an interested party or contested among sources.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story important to reputation but inadequately evidenced.
- **Unresolved:** available evidence does not permit a firm conclusion.

The institutional chronology maintained by the Association Atelier André Breton, the Bibliothèque nationale de France collections, the four-volume Pléiade edition, and Henri Béhar’s documentary chronology provide the principal chronological scaffolding. Dates attached to informal relationships sometimes remain approximate.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full civil name:** André Robert Breton is frequently given in catalogues and biographical records; he normally published and signed as **André Breton**.
- **Early pseudonym:** **René Dobrant**, an anagrammatic pen name used for poems published in his school review.
- **Common titles and nicknames:** “the pope of Surrealism,” “the Surrealist pope,” “magus,” and “pontiff.” These were journalistic or hostile labels, not offices he accepted. Breton, an atheist and anti-clericalist, disliked the papal metaphor.
- **Birth:** commonly recorded as **19 February 1896**, Tinchebray, Orne, Normandy, France.
- **Date discrepancy:** some biographical chronologies say he was born late on **18 February 1896** and registered on 19 February. The BnF and standard scholarly reference works use 19 February.
- **Death:** **28 September 1966**, Hôpital Lariboisière, Paris, aged seventy.
- **Burial:** Cimetière des Batignolles, Paris. His tomb bears the sentence **“Je cherche l’or du temps”** (“I seek the gold of time”), taken from *Introduction au discours sur le peu de réalité*.
- **Nationality:** French.
- **Occupations:** poet, essayist, critic, editor, publisher, art adviser, collector, exhibition organizer, radio broadcaster, and movement organizer.
- **Education:** Lycée/Collège Chaptal; medical studies in Paris beginning in 1913, interrupted by military service and never completed as a medical degree.
- **Languages and cultural range:** wrote in French; read German; worked with translated German psychiatric and psychoanalytic literature; participated in international literary networks across Europe, Mexico, the Caribbean, and the United States.
- **Principal residences:** Tinchebray; Saint-Brieuc during portions of early childhood; Pantin; wartime postings at Pontivy, Nantes, Saint-Dizier, and Paris; **42 rue Fontaine, Paris**, from 1922 to 1966 except during wartime exile; Marseille in 1940–41; New York in 1941–46; and a summer house at Saint-Cirq-Lapopie from 1951.
- **Principal affiliations:** *Littérature*; Paris Dada; Bureau de recherches surréalistes; Surrealist group; French Communist Party, briefly and contentiously; Fédération internationale de l’art révolutionnaire indépendant; *VVV*; postwar Paris Surrealist group; signatory networks opposing colonialism, Stalinism, fascism, and the Algerian War.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and grandparents

**Documented or strongly corroborated facts:**

- Breton was the only child of **Louis-Justin Breton** (26 March 1867–10 November 1955) and **Marguerite-Marie-Eugénie Le Gouguès** (1 July 1871–1946).
- Louis-Justin was born at Vincey in the Vosges. He served as a gendarme at Tinchebray and later became an accountant, then a managerial employee at the Legras glassworks in Pantin.
- Marguerite Le Gouguès came from Lorient. Henri Béhar’s chronology identifies her father as a carpenter at the Lorient naval arsenal and her mother, born Le Miloch, as a home embroiderer.
- The paternal line was rooted in the Vosges and included agricultural or wine-growing family connections; the maternal line was Breton in the regional sense, centered on Lorient and Saint-Brieuc.
- The family’s social position was modest but upwardly mobile: disciplined lower-middle-class respectability rather than poverty.

### Family culture

Biographers describe Louis-Justin as secular or atheist and Marguerite as Catholic, morally strict, possessive, and anxious about respectability. Breton’s adult anti-clericalism and revolt against domestic and bourgeois authority have often been traced to this household.

That psychological connection is an **interpretation**, although Breton’s own writings repeatedly oppose conventional family morality, religion, work discipline, and utilitarian education. Accounts portraying the mother as emotionally cold depend partly on Breton’s recollections and later biographical reconstruction.

Breton appears to have been especially attached to time spent with maternal relatives in Brittany. Saint-Brieuc and Lorient contributed to the maritime, Celtic, legendary, and object-centered imaginative geography that recurs in his work.

### Marriages, partners, and child

1. **Simone Kahn, later Simone Collinet (1897–1980)**  
   Breton married Kahn on **15 September 1921**. She came from a prosperous Jewish family whose fortune had connections to the Amazonian rubber trade. Their family reportedly resisted her marrying an impecunious poet. Recent archival research has recovered her role as organizer, typist, correspondent, mediator, collector, and participant in the Surrealist circle—not merely “Breton’s wife.” The couple separated in 1928; the legal endpoint is variously dated 1929 or 1931 in summaries. They later recovered a measure of friendship and shared antifascist commitments. They had no children.

2. **Suzanne Muzard (1900–1992)**  
   Breton’s intense affair with Muzard began while both were attached to others. It contributed to the collapse of his marriage to Simone and informed *Nadja*, *Les Vases communicants*, and the ideal of “mad love.” The relationship was intermittent and conflictual rather than a settled partnership.

3. **Léona-Camille-Ghislaine Delcourt, called “Nadja” (1902–1941)**  
   Breton met Delcourt in Paris on **4 October 1926**. Their intense association lasted roughly ten days, followed by letters and intermittent contact. It supplied the central encounter of *Nadja*. This was not a marriage or stable partnership. Delcourt was hospitalized in 1927 and remained institutionalized until her death in 1941. The ethical and factual controversies surrounding Breton’s treatment of her are examined below.

4. **Jacqueline Lamba (1910–1993)**  
   Breton met Lamba in 1934, in circumstances mythologized in *L’Amour fou*. They married on **14 August 1934**. Lamba was an artist in her own right, although Breton’s narrative and later Surrealist historiography long obscured her independent career. Their daughter **Aube Elléouët-Breton** was born in December 1935. The marriage deteriorated during the American exile; Lamba formed a relationship with American sculptor and editor David Hare. Breton traveled to Reno in 1945 to obtain a divorce.

5. **Elisa Bindhoff Enet, usually Elisa Breton (1906–2000)**  
   Chilean-born Elisa was previously married to the Chilean politician Benjamin Claro Velasco. Her daughter Ximena had drowned before Elisa met Breton in New York in late 1943. Breton and Elisa traveled together in Canada in 1944; the grief, landscape, and their relationship entered *Arcane 17*. They married in Reno in 1945 and remained together until his death. Recent museum research stresses Elisa’s active part in selecting, acquiring, arranging, and documenting the collection at rue Fontaine.

### Child and descendants

- **Aube Elléouët-Breton** (born 1935), Breton’s only acknowledged child, became a visual artist and custodian of his archive.
- Aube married the poet and painter **Yves Elléouët**.
- She later enabled major archival access and donated or transferred important manuscripts and the preserved “Breton Wall” to French public collections.

No well-supported evidence emerged of other children.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1896–1913

Breton was born at Tinchebray while his father was posted there as a gendarme. The family left Normandy around 1900 and settled in **Pantin**, then an industrial suburb northeast of Paris.

From 1902 the family lived near rue Montgolfier and rue Étienne-Marcel; in 1913 it moved to avenue Édouard-Vaillant. Louis-Justin’s progression from policing to accounting and management placed André within an environment of wage labor, factory organization, bourgeois aspiration, and material insecurity.

Breton initially attended a religious school, Sainte-Élisabeth, then Pantin’s municipal school. In 1907 he entered the Collège Chaptal in Paris as a day student. He performed particularly well in German and developed interests in natural science, insects, unusual stones, popular imagery, and poetry.

His early reading included:

- Charles Baudelaire
- Stéphane Mallarmé
- J.-K. Huysmans
- Arthur Rimbaud
- Maurice Barrès
- Gustave Moreau
- Paul Valéry

In 1912 he published poems in the school journal *Vers l’idéal*, some signed **René Dobrant**. In 1913 he began corresponding with Valéry, submitting poems for criticism. His early verse was still Symbolist and technically controlled, far from the anti-literary and automatic experiments associated with his later work.

## Education and Formation, 1913–1918

### Medical education

In 1913 Breton enrolled in the preliminary scientific-medical course required for medical studies in Paris. Family expectations favored a secure professional career. His interests increasingly centered on neurology, psychiatry, abnormal psychology, dreams, and language disorders.

He did not complete the medical degree. Claims that he was formally a psychiatrist or practicing physician are therefore inaccurate. He acquired substantial wartime clinical experience under military supervision but never became a licensed specialist.

### First World War

Breton was mobilized in 1915. He trained with the artillery at **Pontivy**, which he later described as an environment of blood, stupidity, mud, and military regimentation.

His medical background led to hospital work:

- **Nantes:** He served as a military medical orderly or intern in a neurological ward.
- **Saint-Dizier:** He worked at a neuropsychiatric center under **Dr. Raoul Leroy**, examining shell-shocked and psychotic soldiers.
- **Paris:** He later worked at the neurological center of the Pitié hospital under **Joseph Babinski**, whose theory of “pithiatism”—symptoms susceptible to persuasion—was influential in French wartime neurology.

Breton encountered the works of Sigmund Freud through psychiatric literature, including translations and summaries then available in French. He experimented with free association and close transcription of patients’ speech. The war therefore joined three formative experiences: mechanized slaughter, institutional authority, and language produced outside deliberate rational control.

### Jacques Vaché

In Nantes in 1916 Breton met **Jacques Vaché**, a wounded soldier, draftsman, writer, and devotee of Alfred Jarry. Vaché’s cultivated indifference—his “umour” without an initial “h”—his contempt for artistic careers, and his refusal of patriotic seriousness profoundly affected Breton.

Breton later called Vaché the man he had loved most in the world. Vaché died in Nantes in January 1919 from an opium overdose. The coroner treated the death as poisoning; whether it was intentional suicide, an accidental overdose, or a staged gesture remains **unresolved**. Breton helped construct the enduring Vaché legend by editing *Lettres de guerre* and treating the death as a terminal anti-bourgeois act.

### Guillaume Apollinaire and Louis Aragon

Breton met **Guillaume Apollinaire** in 1915 after sending him the dramatic poem *Décembre*. Through Apollinaire he encountered a living avant-garde encompassing Cubism, visual poetry, theatrical experiment, and modern urban imagery.

At the Val-de-Grâce military hospital he met **Louis Aragon** in 1917. Their friendship became one of the main organizational partnerships of postwar Dada and early Surrealism. Aragon helped direct Breton toward Lautréamont’s *Les Chants de Maldoror*, a decisive discovery.

## Early Career and Paris Dada, 1918–1923

### Return to literary Paris

Breton worked briefly with Apollinaire and met Philippe Soupault, Paul Éluard, Théodore Fraenkel, Pierre Reverdy, Francis Picabia, and other writers and artists. Apollinaire’s death in November 1918 and Vaché’s death two months later left Breton seeking a collective method capable of continuing their rupture with received literature.

### *Littérature*

In March 1919 Breton, Aragon, and Soupault founded the review ***Littérature***. Its deliberately bland title concealed an increasingly radical program. The review published modern and avant-garde writers, surveys, dream accounts, automatic texts, and Dada material.

### *Les Champs magnétiques*

In May–June 1919 Breton and Soupault conducted sustained experiments in rapid, uncensored writing. Published in 1920 as ***Les Champs magnétiques***, these texts later acquired canonical status as the first major work of Surrealist automatic writing.

The manuscripts contain corrections, variants, and compositional choices. Accordingly, “purely automatic” should be treated as a programmatic description, not proof that the published text passed directly from unconscious speech to print without revision.

### Dada

Tristan Tzara arrived in Paris in 1920. Breton helped stage Dada performances, provocations, mock trials, and attacks on established literary authority. Among the most notorious was the 1921 “trial” of nationalist writer Maurice Barrès.

Breton increasingly wanted discipline, intellectual investigation, and a constructive revolutionary direction; Tzara defended Dada’s negativity and refusal of stable programs. Their conflict culminated around the disorderly performance of Tzara’s *Le Cœur à gaz* in July 1923, during which Breton used a cane and reportedly broke actor Pierre de Massot’s arm. The episode marked the practical end of Paris Dada’s unity.

### Employment and finances

Breton did not possess independent wealth. He undertook literary and art-market work, including:

- reading and editorial tasks;
- advising couturier and collector **Jacques Doucet**;
- preparing memoranda on modern literature and art;
- helping Doucet acquire works, including Picasso’s *Les Demoiselles d’Avignon*;
- buying and reselling paintings and objects;
- relying at times on Simone Kahn’s family resources.

Recent reporting based on Simone’s family archive indicates that obtaining regular employment with Doucet helped Breton overcome her family’s objections to the marriage.

## Founding and Direction of Surrealism, 1924–1929

### Bureau of Surrealist Research

In 1924 Breton and associates opened the **Bureau de recherches surréalistes** at 15 rue de Grenelle. It solicited reports concerning dreams, coincidences, compulsions, unusual perceptions, and everyday speech.

Antonin Artaud served briefly as an especially forceful director. The periodical ***La Révolution surréaliste*** began publication in December 1924 under Pierre Naville and Benjamin Péret, later under Breton’s direction.

### Competing manifestos

Yvan Goll published a rival Surrealist manifesto shortly before Breton. Breton’s ***Manifeste du surréalisme***, published in October 1924 as the theoretical preface to *Poisson soluble*, prevailed institutionally because it was attached to an active group, bureau, review, and continuing program.

Breton defined Surrealism as “pure psychic automatism” intended to express the actual functioning of thought in the absence of rational control and moral or aesthetic constraint. He predicted resolution of dream and waking life in a higher reality—*surréalité*.

### Techniques and projects

The early group experimented with:

- automatic writing;
- dream narratives;
- hypnotic “sleeping sessions”;
- exquisite-corpse drawings and sentences;
- collective games and questionnaires;
- urban wandering;
- found objects;
- verbal collage;
- simulation of mental disorders;
- investigations of sexuality, suicide, love, and chance.

The group eventually stopped the hypnotic sessions after participants became physically or psychologically endangered and interpersonal manipulation became difficult to control.

### Anti-colonial turn

In 1925 Breton and the Surrealists opposed France’s Rif War in Morocco and collaborated with Communist and anti-colonial organizations. The tract **“La Révolution d’abord et toujours!”** linked psychic revolt to social revolution. The group denounced colonial exhibitions and French imperial violence, although its collectors simultaneously removed non-European objects from their original contexts and interpreted them through European categories.

### Communist Party relationship

Breton entered the **French Communist Party (PCF)** in January 1927. The relationship was antagonistic from the start:

- Party officials distrusted Surrealism’s eroticism, individualism, Freud, occult interests, and autonomous art.
- Breton rejected demands that poetry submit to party doctrine.
- He was assigned routine cell work and repeatedly required to justify Surrealist publications.
- His attempt to reconcile Marx, Freud, Rimbaud, and revolutionary desire never became accepted Communist orthodoxy.

### Internal exclusions

Breton helped expel or break with several participants, including Philippe Soupault, Antonin Artaud, Roger Vitrac, Robert Desnos, Michel Leiris, Raymond Queneau, André Masson, Jacques Prévert, and others at different moments.

Reasons varied: refusal of Communist engagement, journalism, theatre work, alleged literary careerism, personal antagonism, or resistance to collective discipline. Treating all departures as formal “expulsions” would be inaccurate; some resigned, drifted away, or formed rival circles.

### *Nadja*

Breton met Léona Delcourt in October 1926. He wrote *Nadja* at the Manoir d’Ango in Normandy in August 1927; it appeared in 1928, combining narrative, photographs, documentary traces, philosophical inquiry, and autobiographical material.

Delcourt was hospitalized at the Perray-Vaucluse asylum in March 1927, later transferred to northern France, and died in 1941. Breton did not identify her civil name in the book. Her letters and drawings show her as an active correspondent and artist, not simply the passive apparition constructed by the narrative.

## Reorganization, Political Conflict, and Major Works, 1929–1937

### Second Manifesto and *Un Cadavre*

The ***Second Manifeste du surréalisme*** appeared in December 1929 and book form in 1930. It attacked former collaborators, tightened the group’s revolutionary claims, and moved from the open experimentalism of 1924 toward ideological adjudication.

Dissidents responded with the 1930 pamphlet ***Un Cadavre***, whose contributors included Georges Bataille, Robert Desnos, Michel Leiris, Jacques Prévert, and Raymond Queneau. They depicted Breton as authoritarian, hypocritical, puritanical, and pontifical. The “pope of Surrealism” image became one of the most durable hostile myths about him.

### Georges Bataille

Breton attacked Bataille’s “base materialism,” fascination with excrement, sacrifice, death, and degraded matter. Bataille in turn characterized Breton as an idealist moralist who sought transgression while policing its acceptable forms.

Their dispute was theoretical and personal:

- Breton pursued dialectical reconciliation, convulsive beauty, and transformative love.
- Bataille emphasized irreducible expenditure, abjection, erotic violence, and the impossibility of final synthesis.

They later collaborated tactically in the antifascist group **Contre-Attaque** in 1935, but the reconciliation was short-lived.

### Violence as rhetoric

The Second Manifesto includes the sentence that the simplest Surrealist act would be to go into the street and fire a revolver blindly into the crowd. It is a **documented quotation**, usually detached from its surrounding qualification that the person who had never felt such a desire belonged outside Surrealism.

There is no evidence that Breton carried out such an attack or organized indiscriminate shooting. The sentence is nonetheless not a fabricated quotation: it expresses the movement’s cultivation of scandal, absolute revolt, and rhetorical proximity to political violence.

### *L’Immaculée Conception* and simulation

With Paul Éluard, Breton published *L’Immaculée Conception* (1930), including simulations of different psychiatric states. The authors maintained that mentally healthy writers could enter and leave such linguistic states.

Later criticism questions the appropriation and aestheticization of psychiatric suffering. The experiment was literary, not clinical research, and offers no proof that psychosis can be voluntarily assumed without consequence.

### Art criticism and collecting

Breton promoted Giorgio de Chirico’s early metaphysical paintings, Max Ernst, Joan Miró, André Masson, Yves Tanguy, Alberto Giacometti, Pablo Picasso, and later many younger artists. His essays collected under ***Le Surréalisme et la peinture*** resisted academic art history and treated paintings as agents of psychic revelation.

Breton also traded and brokered art. The boundary between critic, collector, adviser, and market participant was therefore porous. No substantial evidence found in the adversarial search establishes fraud, forged authentication, money laundering, or criminal market manipulation by Breton.

### *Les Vases communicants*

Published in 1932, *Les Vases communicants* attempted to connect dream and waking causality, psychoanalysis, dialectical materialism, intimate crisis, and social revolution. Freud objected to aspects of Breton’s interpretation. Their correspondence shows mutual interest but not intellectual agreement.

### Aragon rupture

Aragon increasingly accepted PCF authority and socialist realism. His poem “Front rouge” led to prosecution in 1932; Breton defended him against state repression while criticizing the poem’s subordination to propaganda. Aragon then definitively chose the Communist Party over the Surrealist group.

### Jacqueline Lamba and *L’Amour fou*

Breton met Jacqueline Lamba in May 1934. His subsequent account in *L’Amour fou* represented the meeting as the fulfillment of a phrase and image anticipated by chance. They married that August and traveled to the Canary Islands in 1935.

*L’Amour fou* developed the concepts of objective chance, erotic recognition, reciprocal revelation, and convulsive beauty. Its autobiographical mythology is central to Breton’s self-construction, but it should not be mistaken for a neutral record of Lamba’s experience.

### The 1935 Ehrenburg affair and René Crevel’s death

Soviet writer **Ilya Ehrenburg** described the Surrealists in abusive terms, including sexual slurs. On 14 June 1935 Breton encountered and slapped him.

The organizers of the International Congress of Writers for the Defense of Culture then refused Breton a speaking place. René Crevel, who had attempted to mediate between Communists and Surrealists, died by suicide on 18 June.

**Documented sequence:** insult, slap, exclusion from the platform, Crevel’s death, and eventual reading of the Surrealist statement by Éluard.

**Unresolved causal claim:** Crevel was ill with tuberculosis, had attempted suicide before, and faced personal, political, and sexual pressures. It is reductive to attribute his death solely to Breton, the PCF, his bisexuality, or the congress crisis. The crisis was an immediate context, not a demonstrable single cause.

Breton’s break with Communist institutions became final in 1935. He subsequently denounced Stalinism, the Moscow Trials, and state direction of culture.

## Mexico, Trotsky, and Revolutionary Art, 1938–1939

In April 1938 Breton traveled to Mexico under a French cultural commission. He met **Diego Rivera**, **Frida Kahlo**, and the exiled revolutionary **Leon Trotsky**.

Breton and Trotsky debated revolutionary art at length. Their ***Manifeste pour un art révolutionnaire indépendant*** was finalized on **25 July 1938**.

### Authorship and signatures

- **Documented fact:** the published manifesto bore the signatures of André Breton and Diego Rivera.
- **Documented through drafts and testimony:** Trotsky was a principal co-author.
- **Reason for concealment:** political prudence and the desire to present a broader artistic alliance.
- Breton later said the manifesto owed more to Trotsky than to Rivera or himself, especially the rejection of any political exception to artistic liberty.

The manifesto’s formula joined revolutionary transformation with independence of artistic creation. It led to the short-lived **Fédération internationale de l’art révolutionnaire indépendant (FIARI)**.

Breton helped organize the 1939 Paris exhibition **Mexique**, presenting works by Kahlo, Manuel Álvarez Bravo, and Mexican popular and Indigenous artists. Kahlo rejected being reduced to a Surrealist label, although she participated in Breton’s network.

Breton’s relationship with Rivera became strained amid political and logistical disputes. Trotsky’s assassination in 1940 ended the personal connection but not Breton’s anti-Stalinism.

## War, Marseille, Martinique, and New York, 1939–1946

### Mobilization and censorship

Breton was mobilized in 1939 as a medical orderly. The Vichy regime banned his ***Anthologie de l’humour noir***, first printed in 1940 but prevented from normal distribution until after the war.

After German victory he reached **Marseille**, where displaced writers and artists gathered around the Villa Air-Bel and Varian Fry’s Emergency Rescue Committee.

In December 1940 Vichy police detained Breton preventively during Marshal Pétain’s visit, regarding him as a potentially disruptive anarchist.

### Departure from France

On **25 March 1941**, Breton, Jacqueline Lamba, Aube, Claude Lévi-Strauss, Victor Serge, Anna Seghers, Wifredo Lam, and other refugees sailed from Marseille aboard the **Capitaine-Paul-Lemerle**.

Conditions were cramped and punitive. On reaching Martinique, passengers were confined at the Lazaret. Breton encountered the journal ***Tropiques*** and met **Aimé and Suzanne Césaire**. He recognized in Césaire’s poetry a major anti-colonial and revolutionary voice.

Breton and his family continued through the Caribbean to New York.

### New York exile

Breton arrived in New York in July 1941. Because his English remained weak, he did not integrate easily into American literary culture. His principal activities included:

- broadcasting in French for the Office of War Information/Voice of America;
- lecturing and organizing exhibitions;
- editing ***VVV*** with Marcel Duchamp, Max Ernst, David Hare, and others;
- maintaining contacts among European émigrés and American artists;
- collecting Indigenous American and Oceanic art;
- traveling through the American Southwest and Canada.

His radio employment was wartime propaganda work for the Allied cause, but no evidence found supports claims that Breton served as an intelligence officer or clandestine operative.

### Separation from Jacqueline

Jacqueline Lamba developed an independent artistic network and formed a relationship with David Hare. The marriage with Breton ended. Accounts that make her merely the woman who abandoned him reproduce Breton-centered historiography; her career and dissatisfaction with his expectations require independent consideration.

### Elisa and *Arcane 17*

Breton met Elisa Bindhoff Claro in New York in late 1943. They traveled to the Gaspé Peninsula in 1944. At Percé Rock Breton composed ***Arcane 17***, combining Elisa’s mourning, wartime catastrophe, the Tarot Star, Mélusine, feminism, occult symbolism, and anticipated liberation.

After divorcing Jacqueline in Reno, Breton married Elisa in 1945.

### Haiti

Breton traveled to Haiti in December 1945 at the invitation of French cultural attaché Pierre Mabille. His lectures were publicized by radical students and writers including René Depestre.

A January 1946 student revolt helped precipitate the fall of President Élie Lescot. Breton’s speeches were one stimulus within a much larger crisis involving authoritarian rule, race and class hierarchy, censorship, labor conflict, and student organization.

The statement that Breton “caused the Haitian Revolution of 1946” is therefore a **mythic exaggeration**. Participants such as Depestre remembered his presence as electrifying, but Haitian actors—not a visiting French poet—organized the movement.

Breton also visited the painter and Vodou priest **Hector Hyppolite**, whose work he collected and promoted. His writing valued Haitian popular religion against colonial and Catholic hierarchies, while also passing it through a European Surrealist framework.

## Postwar Return and Later Life, 1946–1966

### Return to Paris

André and Elisa returned to France in spring 1946 and reoccupied 42 rue Fontaine. Breton found a cultural environment dominated by Communism, existentialism, Resistance credentials, and abstraction. His anti-Stalinism and wartime absence reduced his institutional authority in France, but he reconstituted an international Surrealist group.

### 1947 international exhibition

With Marcel Duchamp, Breton organized ***Le Surréalisme en 1947*** at Galerie Maeght. The exhibition used labyrinths, altars, ritual forms, and references to myth and initiation. Critics treated its occult dimension either as expansion of revolutionary imagination or retreat from politics.

### Anarchism

Breton moved closer to organized anarchism during the late 1940s and early 1950s. He contributed to or supported the newspaper ***Le Libertaire*** and declared that Surrealism had first recognized itself in the black mirror of anarchism.

This did not mean abandonment of Marx. Breton continued to seek a non-Stalinist revolutionary synthesis: Marxist analysis, Freudian emancipation, poetic transformation, anti-colonialism, and libertarian organization.

### Saint-Cirq-Lapopie

In 1951 Breton purchased a former inn or mariners’ house in **Saint-Cirq-Lapopie**, Lot. He spent summers there with Elisa, collected stones and natural objects along the Lot, and received artists, writers, and younger Surrealists.

### Camus controversy

Albert Camus’s ***L’Homme révolté*** (1951) criticized Surrealism’s nihilistic rhetoric and its proximity to revolutionary murder. Breton answered in *Arts*, attacking Camus’s interpretation of Lautréamont and Surrealism. Camus replied in “Révolte et conformisme.”

This was an intellectual polemic, not a close friendship or organizational rupture. Camus nevertheless later credited Breton with writing profoundly about love.

### Break with Max Ernst

In 1954 Max Ernst accepted the Venice Biennale’s Grand Prize for painting. Breton and the Paris group treated acceptance of an official honor as incompatible with Surrealist refusal of cultural institutions and announced Ernst’s exclusion.

Ernst replied that he had left the group long before and denounced those speaking in Breton’s name. The affair reinforced the image of Breton as an aging disciplinarian unable to control the independent careers of major artists.

### Algerian War

Breton opposed French colonial warfare and torture. In September 1960 he signed the **Manifesto of the 121**, declaring the right to refuse service in the Algerian War and defending those assisting Algerian independence.

### Final period

Breton continued to organize collective inquiries, reviews, and exhibitions:

- ***Médium***
- ***Le Surréalisme, même***
- ***Bief***
- ***La Brèche***
- the 1959–60 international exhibition ***EROS***
- the 1965 exhibition ***L’Écart absolu***

He supported younger figures including Jean Benoît, Toyen, Mimi Parent, Jean-Jacques Lebel, Radovan Kraguly, and others, while maintaining friendships with Benjamin Péret until Péret’s death in 1959.

He rejected official literary honors and never joined the Académie française. His authority remained informal: it rested on writing, correspondence, selection, publication, meetings, and the power to recognize or repudiate affiliation.

---

## Death

In September 1966 Breton was at Saint-Cirq-Lapopie when severe respiratory or cardiopulmonary illness required emergency transportation to Paris.

- **Documented fact:** he died at Hôpital Lariboisière on the morning of **28 September 1966**.
- A contemporaneous *Le Monde* notice described the immediate event as a heart attack.
- Later accounts emphasize serious respiratory insufficiency before evacuation from Saint-Cirq.
- These descriptions are compatible with a terminal cardiopulmonary crisis; the publicly accessible evidence consulted does not establish a more precise medical diagnosis.

He was buried at the Cimetière des Batignolles. No credible evidence supports rumors of suicide, poisoning, assassination, or political foul play.

The organized Paris Surrealist group continued briefly and declared its dissolution in 1969.

## Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Journals and organizations

- ***Littérature*** (1919–24): co-founded with Aragon and Soupault.
- **Paris Dada** (c.1920–23): organizer, performer, polemicist.
- **Bureau de recherches surréalistes** (1924–25): founder and central authority.
- ***La Révolution surréaliste*** (1924–29): contributor and later director.
- ***Le Surréalisme au service de la révolution*** (1930–33): director.
- **Contre-Attaque** (1935): antifascist collaboration with Bataille’s circle; short-lived.
- **FIARI** (1938–39): international federation initiated following the Breton–Trotsky manifesto.
- ***VVV*** (New York, 1942–44): co-organizer and contributor.
- Postwar reviews including ***Médium***, ***Le Surréalisme, même***, ***Bief***, and ***La Brèche***.

### Gallery Gradiva

Breton opened the **Galerie Gradiva** at 31 rue de Seine in 1937. Marcel Duchamp designed a striking doorway in the form of a double silhouette. The gallery joined exhibition, sales, Surrealist display, and group promotion. It was not a durable commercial success.

### Art advising, dealing, and authentication

Breton’s income at different times came from literary work, art advice, gallery activity, sales from his collection, and radio employment. His eye for modern and non-European art became highly respected. Claude Lévi-Strauss later recalled Breton’s remarkable ability to identify significant objects in New York flea markets.

No evidence consulted establishes that Breton created a corporate business empire, held patents, made technological inventions, or accumulated conventional industrial investments.

### Property and estate

His two principal properties were the rented or occupied apartment-studio at **42 rue Fontaine** and the house at **Saint-Cirq-Lapopie**.

The rue Fontaine collection included modern art, Oceanic and Indigenous American objects, pre-Columbian works, folk art, found objects, minerals, insects, manuscripts, photographs, and books. Its monetary value increased enormously after his death, but the arrangement was not simply an investment portfolio. Objects were continuously exchanged, sold, repositioned, or incorporated into working environments.

After Elisa Breton’s death, efforts to preserve the entire studio as a foundation failed. In 2003 thousands of items were dispersed through a major auction. Aube Elléouët-Breton transferred the wall behind Breton’s desk to the Musée national d’art moderne. Sources variously describe **212** displayed works or **255** constituent works and objects, depending on cataloguing method.

### Non-European objects and human remains

Recent object-by-object research identified sensitive holdings, including painted human skulls from the Sepik River region of New Guinea.

- **Documented fact:** Breton possessed such materials and displayed non-European sacred, ceremonial, and funerary objects alongside modern art and natural specimens.
- **Unresolved:** many objects lack secure acquisition histories. It is not presently possible to determine whether every object was lawfully exported, acquired under colonial coercion, or passed through legitimate contemporary markets.
- **Interpretation:** Breton’s anti-colonial politics coexisted with a European avant-garde collecting practice dependent on colonial movement of objects. Current scholarship treats that tension as substantive rather than cancelling either side.

## Political, Religious, Philosophical, and Artistic Development

### Religion and occultism

Breton rejected Catholicism, clerical authority, and conventional religious faith. He nevertheless drew on:

- alchemy;
- Tarot;
- astrology;
- Celtic and Arthurian myth;
- hermeticism;
- Gnosticism;
- Indigenous cosmologies;
- Romantic occultism;
- Charles Fourier’s utopian social theory.

He did not simply “convert to occultism.” His later esotericism functioned as poetic knowledge, analogical method, revolt against positivism, and search for non-instrumental relations.

### Psychoanalysis

Breton’s psychoanalytic formation was heterodox. He took from Freud dreams, free association, displacement, condensation, and unconscious desire, but rejected therapeutic normalization and adapted psychoanalysis into collective poetic practice.

He visited Freud at **19 Berggasse, Vienna, on 10 October 1921**. Breton found the interview disappointing; Freud regarded Surrealist appropriations with reserve. Their correspondence continued intermittently through 1937.

### Marxism and Communism

Breton regarded social revolution as necessary to liberation. He joined the PCF but resisted socialist realism, party discipline, nationalism, and Stalinist cultural authority. His break with the party was not a renunciation of revolution; it produced anti-Stalinist Marxist, Trotskyist, and later anarchist alliances.

### Anti-fascism and anti-colonialism

Breton opposed:

- the Rif War;
- French colonialism;
- fascism and Nazism;
- Stalin’s Moscow Trials;
- Vichy;
- state-directed art;
- colonial war and torture in Algeria.

Surrealist rhetoric could nevertheless exoticize colonized peoples and treat their art as evidence for European psychic theories. Political opposition to colonialism did not automatically dissolve asymmetrical collecting and representation.

### Love, sexuality, and feminism

Breton elevated reciprocal erotic love as a revolutionary force and praised women’s emancipation in parts of *Arcane 17*. He opposed conventional marriage, prostitution, and bourgeois sexual hypocrisy at different moments.

At the same time, his writing repeatedly casts women as muse, child-woman, medium, fairy, witch, or portal to revelation. Feminist scholarship argues that this granted symbolic power while restricting women’s status as autonomous creators.

The contradiction is documentary: Breton supported or promoted numerous women artists, but his narratives often subordinated their independent work to his mythology of desire.

## Controversies, Allegations, Violence, and Ethical Questions

### Authoritarian leadership and “purges”

**Documented fact:** Breton signed, directed, or endorsed numerous exclusions and denunciations.

**Hostile characterization:** dissidents represented him as a dictatorial pope conducting purges.

**Qualification:** Surrealism was a voluntary avant-garde association without state power, employment control, or legal sanctions. “Purge” is metaphorical, but exclusion could damage friendships, publishing access, reputation, and group participation.

**Assessment:** the underlying pattern of ideological and personal policing is well documented; the image of an omnipotent dictator exaggerates his actual power and ignores repeated defections he could not prevent.

### Homophobia

The published Surrealist “Researches into Sexuality” record Breton expressing categorical hostility toward male homosexuality and associating it with moral or mental deficiency.

- **Documented fact:** the remarks exist and were not invented by later critics.
- **Context, not exculpation:** French homosexuality was stigmatized and medicalized, while the group simultaneously claimed sexual liberation.
- His hostility contributed to conflict surrounding Jean Cocteau and made the group inhospitable to openly queer men.
- René Crevel’s bisexuality formed part of the social tension around him, though claims that Breton expelled or caused Crevel’s death simply because Crevel was bisexual are **overstated and causally unproved**.
- Breton later maintained relationships with some gay or bisexual writers, but no clear public retraction of the earlier position was found.

### Women, misogyny, and muse-making

**Documented pattern:** women were central as artists, writers, organizers, lovers, and patrons, but early group leadership and theoretical authority were overwhelmingly male.

Critics argue that Breton:

- converted actual women into signs of male revelation;
- valued the *femme-enfant*, medium, hysteric, or muse;
- obscured Simone Kahn’s organizational labor;
- narrated Jacqueline Lamba primarily as a predestined lover;
- transformed Léona Delcourt’s crisis into literary material;
- advocated free love while exercising possessive authority.

Countervailing evidence includes his promotion of women artists, his attack on patriarchal family structures, and the explicitly emancipatory passages of *Arcane 17*. The appropriate conclusion is not that either feminism or misogyny wholly defines him, but that both emancipatory argument and patriarchal representation are present in the record.

### Léona Delcourt/Nadja

The most serious biographical ethical dispute concerns Delcourt.

**Documented facts:**

- Breton recognized her psychological instability.
- They had an intense but brief association.
- She continued writing to him.
- She was institutionalized in March 1927.
- He did not secure her release and appears not to have visited her after institutionalization.
- He used her speech, drawings, and life circumstances in *Nadja*.
- The book condemned asylum psychiatry and predicted that confinement could worsen her condition.
- Delcourt died in an institution in 1941.

**Critical allegation:** Breton exploited and abandoned a vulnerable woman, aestheticizing her illness while protecting his own life.

**Qualification:** the surviving correspondence does not support a simple claim that Breton single-handedly caused her hospitalization. Nor is there proof that he possessed the legal, medical, or financial power to free her. His failure to sustain personal responsibility remains distinct from causal responsibility for her illness and confinement.

### Physical violence

Known episodes include:

- the 1923 Dada theatre brawl, in which Breton struck Pierre de Massot with a cane and reportedly broke his arm;
- the 1935 slap delivered to Ilya Ehrenburg.

These are **documented acts**, not merely rhetorical violence. No evidence found indicates a sustained criminal career, conviction for violent crime, or organized terrorist activity.

### Suicide rhetoric

Breton and the Surrealists repeatedly examined or aestheticized suicide. Vaché, Crevel, and other associates died by suicide or probable overdose.

The claim that Breton operated a “suicide cult” is **hostile mythology**, unsupported as an organizational fact. The group’s rhetoric could romanticize lethal refusal, however, and therefore had consequences beyond abstract literary play.

### Communism, Trotskyism, and political violence

Breton joined the PCF and defended revolution, but later opposed Stalinist repression and the Moscow Trials. He collaborated with Trotsky without joining a stable Trotskyist party.

No evidence found supports allegations that he was a Soviet agent, financed by Soviet intelligence, involved in Trotsky’s assassination, or an agent of French or American intelligence. His Voice of America work was open wartime broadcasting, not proof of espionage.

### Colonial collecting

Breton’s anti-colonial activism is documented. So is his acquisition of objects displaced through colonial commercial networks, including human remains.

The precise provenance of many objects is **unresolved**. It would be unwarranted either to accuse him categorically of illegal trafficking or to assume clean provenance in the absence of records.

### Money, fraud, and art-market accusations

The dedicated adversarial searches located no substantiated lawsuit, criminal investigation, conviction, tax fraud case, forged-authentication scheme, corruption case, or money-laundering allegation against Breton.

He participated in the art market and benefited when artists he supported appreciated in value. That financial relationship is documented; criminal manipulation is not.

### Alcohol, drugs, and mental illness

Breton smoked and appears frequently with a pipe. The available sources do not establish clinical addiction, chronic alcoholism, or illicit drug dependency.

He studied mental illness and explored altered states but was not documented as psychotic. Claims portraying Surrealism as the product of Breton’s insanity are rhetorical pathologization, not diagnosis.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Self-Created Mythology

### “Pope of Surrealism”

- **Story:** Breton ruled Surrealism like an infallible pope.
- **Origin and circulation:** intensified in the dissident pamphlet *Un Cadavre* and repeated by journalists, former associates, and later histories.
- **Evidence for:** manifestos, exclusions, loyalty tests, control of reviews, and public denunciations.
- **Evidence against:** no formal office, no coercive state power, frequent successful rebellion against him, and multiple Surrealisms outside Paris.
- **Effect:** made Breton synonymous with Surrealism while narrowing understanding of the movement’s collective and international character.

### The spontaneous birth of automatic writing

- **Story:** Breton and Soupault directly transcribed an uncontaminated unconscious in *Les Champs magnétiques*.
- **Evidence:** rapid composition and deliberate suspension of revision were genuine experimental procedures.
- **Contrary evidence:** manuscripts contain corrections and variants.
- **Effect:** provided Surrealism with an origin myth of language escaping authorship.

### Nadja as supernatural muse

- **Story:** a mysterious clairvoyant woman appeared by objective chance, opened Paris’s marvelous underside, and vanished into madness.
- **Underlying history:** Nadja was Léona Delcourt, a mother, worker, correspondent, and vulnerable woman with a documented life.
- **Effect:** made *Nadja* canonical while effacing Delcourt’s biography. Recovery of her letters has altered the ethical balance of scholarship.

### Predestined meeting with Jacqueline Lamba

- **Story:** Breton’s encounter with Lamba fulfilled a poetic phrase and prior image.
- **Evidence:** the meeting occurred; Breton deliberately organized its details in *L’Amour fou*.
- **Status:** autobiographical myth-making rather than independently demonstrable predestination.
- **Effect:** established “objective chance” as lived doctrine while subsuming Lamba’s perspective.

### Breton caused the Haitian Revolution of 1946

- **Story:** his lectures directly overthrew Lescot.
- **Evidence for:** radical students attended him; his words were publicized during the immediate pre-revolt atmosphere.
- **Evidence against:** Haitian social, racial, labor, student, and political conflicts preceded his arrival; Haitian organizers conducted the movement.
- **Status:** influential presence, not sole cause.
- **Effect:** magnified Breton’s international revolutionary aura but risks reproducing a colonial “great white visitor” narrative.

### “Avida Dollars”

Breton’s anagram of **Salvador Dalí** as **Avida Dollars** (“eager for dollars”) is documented polemical wordplay. It crystallized the mythology of Dalí as commercial traitor and Breton as guardian of revolutionary purity.

### “I seek the gold of time”

The phrase on Breton’s tomb became the governing legend of his life: collection, love, chance, poetry, and marvelous objects as attempts to recover non-monetary value from chronological existence. It was Breton’s own writing, later transformed into epitaph and biographical emblem.

### Magical infallibility as an art connoisseur

Lévi-Strauss and others circulated stories of Breton identifying exceptional objects in flea markets almost intuitively. Breton demonstrably possessed broad visual knowledge, but the image of infallible divination belongs partly to admirer mythology. Recent study found many non-European holdings aesthetically powerful but not necessarily rare or of exceptional ethnographic quality.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Breton’s death did not settle whether Surrealism was a historical movement, a continuing ethical disposition, or a visual style. Competing legacies developed almost immediately.

### Canonization

Breton entered the Bibliothèque de la Pléiade in four volumes between 1988 and 2008. *Nadja*, the two Manifestos, *L’Amour fou*, and *Arcane 17* remain widely translated and taught. The BnF describes the 1924 Manifesto as one of the most influential twentieth-century texts.

### Dissolution and continuation

Jean Schuster announced the dissolution of the Paris group in 1969. Other Surrealist groups rejected the declaration and continued in Prague, Chicago, Madrid, Stockholm, Leeds, Paris, and elsewhere.

Thus the proposition “Surrealism ended in 1969” is an organizational claim made by one Paris faction, not a universally accepted historical fact.

### International influence

Breton’s influence extends through:

- poetry and experimental prose;
- psychoanalytic and automatic methods;
- visual art and exhibition design;
- photography and film theory;
- revolutionary Marxism and anarchism;
- anti-colonial writing;
- Caribbean, Latin American, Czech, Arab, Japanese, and Anglophone Surrealisms;
- Situationism and postwar avant-gardes;
- Beat, punk, countercultural, occult, and experimental artistic movements;
- contemporary interest in archives, objects, ecology, chance, dreams, and collective authorship.

### Reinterpretation of women

Recent research has shifted attention from Breton’s representations of women to the work and testimony of Simone Kahn, Jacqueline Lamba, Elisa Breton, Léona Delcourt, Suzanne Césaire, Leonora Carrington, Remedios Varo, Toyen, Meret Oppenheim, and others.

This has not made Breton irrelevant; it has changed the unit of analysis from a solitary male genius to a network whose labor, authority, and visibility were unevenly distributed.

### Postcolonial reassessment

Breton’s opposition to empire and promotion of Césaire, Lam, Hyppolite, and non-European art remain historically important. Scholars and museums now also investigate:

- provenance;
- colonial trade;
- sacred and funerary status;
- anonymous makers;
- human remains;
- the category “primitive art”;
- the conversion of ritual objects into modernist aesthetic capital.

### The 2003 dispersal

The auction of the contents of 42 rue Fontaine provoked controversy. Supporters of an intact foundation regarded dispersal as a cultural loss; others emphasized legal ownership, conservation costs, and the impossibility of freezing a living collection.

The transfer of the Wall to the Centre Pompidou preserved one crucial ensemble. Major manuscripts entered public institutions through purchase, preemption, donation, or dation.

### Centenary reassessment, 2024–26

The centenary of the 1924 Manifesto produced exhibitions, new editions, manuscript digitization, documentaries, and reassessments. The recent tendency is dual:

- restoration of Breton’s political, poetic, and anti-colonial seriousness;
- greater attention to homophobia, gender hierarchy, psychiatric appropriation, colonial provenance, and authoritarian group practice.

No major new criminal allegation against Breton emerged in the 2024–26 adversarial search. Current controversy concerns historical interpretation, ethics, attribution, provenance, and institutional memory.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by consulted sources are included. The roster contains several historical influences whom Breton read but never met; those are identified as influences rather than personal encounters.

### Albert Camus — documented fact

Camus discussed and criticized Breton and Surrealism in *L’Homme révolté* (1951). Breton attacked Camus’s treatment of Lautréamont and Surrealism in *Arts*; Camus answered with “Révolte et conformisme.” This was a documented public polemic in France in 1951.

### Alfred Jarry — documented intellectual influence

Breton read Jarry during the First World War. Jacques Vaché’s devotion to Jarry strengthened this influence. Jarry’s pataphysical humor, theatre, and anti-bourgeois posture entered Breton’s prehistory of Surrealism. They could not meet: Jarry died in 1907.

### Antonin Artaud — documented fact

Artaud joined the Surrealist group, directed the Bureau de recherches surréalistes, and wrote militant declarations. Breton and the group broke with or expelled him in 1926–27 amid disputes over Communism, theatre, and revolutionary strategy. Artaud subsequently attacked the Surrealists.

### Carl Gustav Jung — documented intellectual opposition/influence

Breton knew Jungian concepts but privileged Freud. Later Surrealist occult and archetypal readings sometimes overlap with Jung, yet no documented personal meeting was found. Any claim of a direct Breton–Jung collaboration is unsupported.

### Claude Lévi-Strauss — documented fact

Breton and Lévi-Strauss traveled together aboard the *Capitaine-Paul-Lemerle* from Marseille to Martinique in 1941 and were confined at the Lazaret. In New York they shared interests in Indigenous American objects; Lévi-Strauss later testified to Breton’s connoisseurship.

### Diego Rivera — documented fact

Rivera hosted and worked with Breton in Mexico in 1938. The *Manifesto for an Independent Revolutionary Art* appeared under their signatures, although Trotsky was a principal actual co-author. Relations later cooled.

### Frida Kahlo — documented fact

Breton met Kahlo in Mexico in 1938, promoted her work, wrote about it, and helped organize her Paris exhibition in 1939. Kahlo resisted being assimilated straightforwardly to Surrealism and criticized aspects of the Paris circle.

### Friedrich Hegel — documented intellectual influence

Breton repeatedly invoked Hegelian dialectic in attempting to reconcile dream and waking, subject and object, poetry and revolution. There was necessarily no personal meeting.

### Friedrich Nietzsche — documented intellectual influence

Nietzsche belonged to the constellation of anti-rationalist and anti-bourgeois thinkers read by the Surrealists. Breton’s relationship was selective rather than systematic; no personal connection was possible.

### Jacques Derrida — reported intellectual reception, not a life crossing

Derrida later wrote within a French theoretical environment substantially affected by Surrealist problems of writing, presence, and automatism. No documented personal meeting with Breton was established in the consulted sources. This is reception, not a direct biographical link.

### Jacques Lacan — reported fact

Lacan moved in interwar Surrealist and psychiatric circles and published in *Minotaure*. Recent reporting concerning Simone Kahn’s family states that Lacan sheltered members of her Jewish family under false names during the Occupation. Breton and Lacan belonged to overlapping Paris networks, although the dossier found no evidence of a formal teacher-student relationship.

### Jean-Paul Sartre — documented shared intellectual field; no close association established

Breton and Sartre were rival reference points in postwar French culture. Sartre’s circle’s dispute with Camus overlapped with Breton’s separate attack on *L’Homme révolté*. No close friendship or sustained collaboration was found.

### Leon Trotsky, listed as Lev Davidovich Bronstein — documented fact

Breton met Trotsky in Mexico in 1938. They jointly drafted *Pour un art révolutionnaire indépendant*, which advocated revolution alongside complete artistic independence. Trotsky’s authorship was concealed behind Diego Rivera’s published signature.

### Pablo Ruiz Picasso — documented fact

Breton knew Picasso, reproduced and defended his work, and helped Jacques Doucet acquire *Les Demoiselles d’Avignon*. Picasso remained adjacent to rather than disciplined by the Surrealist group and participated selectively in its publications and exhibitions.

### René Descartes — documented intellectual antagonist

Breton positioned Surrealism against restrictive Cartesian rationalism. This is an intellectual relationship across centuries, not a personal connection.

### Sigmund Freud — documented fact

Breton encountered Freud’s work during wartime neuropsychiatric service, visited him at 19 Berggasse in Vienna on 10 October 1921, and corresponded intermittently with him through 1937. Freud remained skeptical of Surrealism’s artistic use of psychoanalysis.

### William Blake — documented artistic and poetic influence

Breton and the Surrealists treated Blake as a precursor in visionary poetry, anti-rational imagination, and image-text composition. No personal meeting was possible.

### Walter Benjamin — reported shared network and documented critical engagement

Benjamin studied and wrote on French Surrealism, especially in his 1929 essay “Surrealism: The Last Snapshot of the European Intelligentsia.” Both inhabited interwar Paris intellectual networks. The connection is securely one of critical reception; a significant personal relationship was not established from the sources consulted.

### Other roster names

No adequately sourced direct crossing was established here for the great majority of the 896-name roster. Mere contemporaneity, residence in Paris, shared opposition to fascism, or generic influence on twentieth-century culture was not treated as a connection.

## Compact Chronology

- **18/19 February 1896:** Born at Tinchebray, Orne.
- **c.1900:** Family settles in Pantin.
- **1907:** Enters Collège Chaptal in Paris.
- **1912:** Publishes school poems, including work under “René Dobrant.”
- **1913:** Begins medical studies; corresponds with Paul Valéry.
- **1915:** Mobilized; meets Guillaume Apollinaire.
- **1916:** Serves in Nantes neurological hospital; meets Jacques Vaché.
- **1916–17:** Works at Saint-Dizier neuropsychiatric center; studies Freud.
- **1917:** Meets Louis Aragon.
- **1918:** Returns to Paris avant-garde circles; Apollinaire dies.
- **January 1919:** Jacques Vaché dies.
- **March 1919:** Founds *Littérature* with Aragon and Soupault.
- **May–June 1919:** Automatic-writing experiments leading to *Les Champs magnétiques*.
- **1919:** Publishes *Mont de piété* and edits Vaché’s *Lettres de guerre*.
- **1920:** *Les Champs magnétiques* published; Paris Dada activity intensifies.
- **1921:** Mock trial of Maurice Barrès; marries Simone Kahn; visits Freud in Vienna on 10 October.
- **1 January 1922:** Moves to 42 rue Fontaine.
- **1922:** Hypnotic-sleep experiments.
- **July 1923:** Violent disruption of Tzara’s *Le Cœur à gaz*; break with Dada.
- **1924:** Bureau de recherches surréalistes; *Manifeste du surréalisme*; *Poisson soluble*; *La Révolution surréaliste*.
- **1925:** Anti–Rif War activism; “La Révolution d’abord et toujours!”
- **4 October 1926:** Meets Léona Delcourt/Nadja.
- **1927:** Joins PCF; Delcourt institutionalized; writes *Nadja*.
- **1928:** *Nadja* published; separation from Simone.
- **1929–30:** Second Manifesto; major exclusions; dissidents publish anti-Breton *Un Cadavre*.
- **1930:** *L’Immaculée Conception* with Éluard.
- **1932:** *Les Vases communicants*; definitive political rupture with Aragon.
- **1934:** Meets and marries Jacqueline Lamba.
- **1935:** Tenerife journey; Ehrenburg slapped; Breton barred from writers’ congress; René Crevel dies; break with Communist institutions.
- **December 1935:** Daughter Aube born.
- **1937:** *L’Amour fou*; Galerie Gradiva.
- **April–August 1938:** Mexico; meets Kahlo, Rivera, and Trotsky.
- **25 July 1938:** Finalizes *Pour un art révolutionnaire indépendant* with Trotsky.
- **1939:** Organizes Mexican exhibition in Paris; war mobilization.
- **1940:** *Anthologie de l’humour noir* suppressed by Vichy; reaches Marseille.
- **December 1940:** Preventively detained during Pétain’s Marseille visit.
- **25 March 1941:** Leaves Marseille on *Capitaine-Paul-Lemerle*.
- **April 1941:** Confined in Martinique; meets Aimé Césaire.
- **July 1941:** Arrives in New York.
- **1942–44:** Works on *VVV* and Allied French-language radio.
- **1943:** Meets Elisa Bindhoff.
- **1944:** Travels to Gaspé; writes *Arcane 17*.
- **1945:** Divorces Jacqueline at Reno and marries Elisa.
- **December 1945–1946:** Lectures in Haiti during crisis leading to Lescot’s fall.
- **Spring 1946:** Returns to Paris.
- **1947:** Organizes international Surrealist exhibition with Duchamp.
- **1948:** Publishes *Martinique, charmeuse de serpents* with Masson.
- **1949:** Moves within 42 rue Fontaine from fourth to third floor.
- **1951:** Acquires Saint-Cirq-Lapopie house; polemic with Camus.
- **1952:** Radio interviews with André Parinaud; close alignment with anarchism.
- **1954:** Public rupture with Max Ernst after Venice Biennale prize.
- **1957:** Publishes *L’Art magique*.
- **1959:** Benjamin Péret dies; EROS exhibition prepared.
- **1960:** Signs Manifesto of the 121 supporting refusal in the Algerian War.
- **1961:** Launch of *La Brèche*.
- **1963:** Revised edition of *Nadja*.
- **1965:** Organizes *L’Écart absolu* exhibition.
- **27 September 1966:** Transported from Saint-Cirq-Lapopie to Paris in severe respiratory distress.
- **28 September 1966:** Dies at Hôpital Lariboisière.
- **1969:** One Paris faction declares the Surrealist group dissolved.
- **1988–2008:** Four-volume Pléiade *Œuvres complètes* published.
- **2003:** Rue Fontaine collection dispersed; Breton Wall enters Centre Pompidou.
- **2017–21:** Major manuscripts, including the Manifestos, enter the BnF through acquisition.
- **2024:** BnF makes its Breton manuscripts freely accessible through Gallica; centenary reassessments and exhibitions.
- **2024–26:** New scholarship emphasizes Simone Kahn, Elisa Breton, provenance, gender, homophobia, psychiatry, and the international movement.
