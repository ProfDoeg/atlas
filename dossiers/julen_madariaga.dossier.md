# Julen Madariaga: Research Dossier

*Prepared 31 August 2026. This is a documentary dossier, not a moral evaluation. Statements based primarily on Madariaga's recollections are identified as such; allegations, charges, convictions, and later retrospective claims are kept distinct.*

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Julen Kerman Madariaga Agirre. Basque-language sources and his publications commonly use **Iulen Madariaga**, **Iulen K. Madariaga**, **Madariaga-ko Iulen**, or the older orthography **Madariaga-tar Iulen K.** Spanish sources sometimes write the second surname as *Aguirre* and insert the aristocratic-style particle *de*: **Julen de Madariaga y Aguirre**. Library authority records normalize him as *Madariaga, Julen, 1932–2021*.
- **Clandestine names:** He said his internal name was **German** (possibly a play on *Kerman*); Txillardegi joked that he would “Germanize” ETA. Secondary histories also identify **Kerman** as his habitual nom de guerre and **Ahmed** as a name adopted in Algeria. The names are not separate people or titles.
- **Born:** 11 October 1932, Abando, Bilbao, Biscay.
- **Died:** 6 April 2021, at his home in Senpere/Saint-Pée-sur-Nivelle, Labourd, Pyrénées-Atlantiques, France, aged 88, after a long illness. *Enbata* alone dated the death to 5 April; contemporaneous reports by Berria, RTVE, El País, and La Vanguardia say the early hours of 6 April. The latter date is followed here.
- **Nationalities:** Spanish by birth; Chilean connections and, according to his 2012 oral testimony, three nationalities; naturalized French in 1986. The available record does not securely establish the legal basis or date of any Chilean nationality, so it should not be asserted beyond his own statement.
- **Languages:** Spanish was his childhood and early political language; French and English were acquired through schooling and exile; he became a committed adult learner of Basque, studying with Xabier Peña and Alfonso Irigoien and practicing in Nabarniz. His memoir and theoretical book were written in Basque.
- **Occupations:** Lawyer, lawyer-economist, travel-agency founder, company director, political organizer, political writer, party official.
- **Principal affiliations:** Ekin; Euskadi Ta Askatasuna (ETA); Herri Batasuna (HB); Elkarri; Aralar; Committee for the Defense of Human Rights in the Basque Country (CDDHPB).
- **Historical significance:** Cofounder of Ekin and ETA; early head of ETA's action/military branch and a principal theorist of revolutionary violence; later internal critic, public advocate of ending ETA's armed campaign, participant in Aralar and Elkarri, and supporter of ETA's disarmament.

## Family and Ancestry

Madariaga was born into a prosperous, legally trained, politically engaged Basque family. In a five-hour oral-history interview recorded by Ahotsak in 2012, he said the paternal Madariagas had lived in Bilbao for six generations and traced their origin to Elexalde in Larrabetzu, Txorierri, as far back as 1488; he added that church records older than that had been destroyed during the War of the Convention. This is family reconstruction, not independently verified genealogy.

His father was **Nicolás de Madariaga Astigarraga** (born in Bilbao in 1901), a lawyer and Basque nationalist. Nicolás was associated first with the PNV milieu and then with the more secular, left-leaning Acción Nacionalista Vasca/Eusko Abertzale Ekintza (ANV/EAE). He served as a legal adviser to the Basque Government's Defense Department during the Civil War. Julen remembered him as religious but anti-clerical in politics—“Christian, but not Catholic”—and critical of the PNV's old requirements of Catholic orthodoxy and “eight Basque surnames.” Nicolás's education in Switzerland, France, and Britain and his liberalism shaped Julen's rejection of clerical and racial definitions of Basque identity.

His mother was **Esther Aguirre Arrigorriaga** (c. 1903–10 November 1999). Family-history material links her to a Chilean branch of the Aguirre family, which helped make Chile a feasible refuge. Julen said both parents and five children went into exile, indicating that he had at least four siblings. **Jon Madariaga**, a brother, is specifically documented: Julen involved him in saving Ekin/ETA's valuable Danish duplicating machine during the 1960–61 police crackdown, and Jon was arrested. The complete sibling list is not established in the accessible sources.

His paternal grandfather, **Ramón Genaro de Madariaga Azcuénaga**, was a lawyer, provincial deputy (1911), member of Eusko Ikaskuntza, and one of the jurists who worked on an early Basque autonomy statute. Julen kept at his Senpere home the table on which, according to family tradition, the 1931 draft had been worked out. Ramón was also an alternative candidate when José Antonio Aguirre was chosen lehendakari at Gernika in 1936. The grandfather's statute role is supported by Julen's testimony and repeated local accounts, but the superlative sometimes used—“the principal author”—is harder to substantiate.

The family was interwoven with the Aguirre political network. Nicolás's brother Juan married María Teresa Aguirre Lekube, sister of lehendakari José Antonio Aguirre. This social position mattered: the founders of Ekin were often young men from educated and relatively comfortable nationalist families, not an underground proletarian cadre.

### Marriages and children

Madariaga married twice. He did not name his first wife in the accessible interviews; he said she had died by 2014. His second wife was **Yvette** (surname not securely established in the consulted material), a nurse at the Bayonne hospital. He acknowledged that both wives suffered from his absences, clandestinity, flights, and imprisonments, and that he had not given his children the time they needed.

He had **eleven children—five with his first wife and six with his second**, described in one obituary as five sons and six daughters. Two of the younger six predeceased him. The best-documented tragedy was **Iraia**, aged nineteen months (some contemporary wording says one and a half), who drowned accidentally in the swimming pool at the Senpere family home on 23 September 1988 while Madariaga was held at Fresnes. The authorities let him view her body under guard but not attend the public funeral ceremony. He later said that he contemplated suicide afterward. Enbata names two other children living at home in 1991, **Loritz** and **Ainize**. Publishing a full list would require family records not available in the consulted sources.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1932–1949

Madariaga's earliest years coincided with the Second Republic and Civil War. He remembered Bilbao air raids and sheltering during bombardment. In March 1937, when he was four, his mother and the children were evacuated by sea first to the French Basque Country. The family then embarked from La Rochelle for Chile, where relatives lived. Madariaga said the ship reached Chile on his fifth birthday, 11 October 1937.

Sources disagree on the return. Short biographies often give **1942**, sometimes saying Julen and Jon returned alone for secondary school. Madariaga's detailed 2012 oral history and 2014 Berria interview say he spent **nine years in Chile and returned at fourteen in 1946**, flying from Chile to Buenos Aires and sailing from Buenos Aires to Bilbao. The specificity and first-person character of the latter account make 1946 the stronger date, though the discrepancy remains unresolved.

He attended a Marist school in Chile and remembered harsh corporal punishment. Chile marked his speech: upon returning, he spoke Spanish with an American accent. He contrasted “technicolor” America with the grey, damaged, hungry Bilbao of 1946—black bread, decaying buildings, frightened people with lowered heads. At sixteen, on a reward visit to relatives in Donostia/San Sebastián, he met **José Luis Álvarez Enparantza, Txillardegi**, through an older cousin's circle. They discussed Basque history and later met again as students. That friendship became one of the founding relationships of Ekin and ETA.

## Education and Formation

Madariaga studied at the University of Deusto and the University of Oviedo, in a combined course he called **“abogado-economista”**; standard accounts call it a law degree. At Deusto, the Jesuit **Father Urrutia**, who taught legal and commercial law, brought together students with shared Basque concerns: Madariaga, José Manuel Agirre, Gurutz Ansola, and, in the broader technical-student circle, Rafael Albisu, Txillardegi, Mikel Barandiaran, Iñaki Larramendi, and others. Madariaga later joked that Urrutia unknowingly helped create Ekin and ETA.

He pursued postgraduate or doctoral work at Cambridge. Ahotsak dates the doctorate to **1962**, and obituaries call him “doctor por Cambridge.” No thesis title or Cambridge catalogue record was located in this research, and published chronologies conflict with the fact that he also described returning from Cambridge into the 1960–61 arrest cycle. The degree is well attested by people and institutions close to him, but its field, college, thesis, and exact conferral date remain unverified.

His intellectual formation combined:

- the secular and liberal nationalism of his father;
- the memory of defeat and exile after 1936;
- adult recovery of Euskara as a personal and national task;
- anti-colonial movements in Ireland, Tunisia, Algeria, Vietnam, Cuba, and later Palestine;
- admiration for Israel's establishment and revival of Hebrew, and for Irgun's urban underground methods;
- **Federico Krutwig's** *Vasconia* and theory that national liberation must precede social liberation;
- contact in Brussels with Trotskyist economist **Ernest Mandel** and Fourth International militants;
- direct exposure in Algeria to the FLN state and Third World liberation movements.

Madariaga's ideological position was not stable Marxism-Leninism. He favored national liberation first, then social revolution; called himself socialist; and later distrusted both the Soviet Union and China as empires. This priority repeatedly placed him against ETA's workerist tendency.

## Early Career: Ekin, Business, and the Birth of ETA, 1952–1961

### Ekin

In 1952–53 Madariaga, Agirre, Ansola, Txillardegi, Benito del Valle, Albisu, Larramendi, and others formed **Ekin** (“to act/do”). It began as a study and cultural group: Basque history seminars, Euskara teaching, lectures, internal bulletins, and clandestine organization. Madariaga regarded Ekin and ETA as one continuous organization whose name changed after rupture with the PNV; most historians distinguish the Ekin phase from ETA proper.

Ekin joined or worked closely with EGI, the PNV youth, in the mid-1950s. At the 1956 World Basque Congress in Paris, Ekin representatives presented a report on Basque youth. Relations worsened over the PNV leadership's caution, confessional inheritance, autonomy-first strategy, and security practices. Madariaga complained that PNV interlocutors spoke members' full names on tapped telephones. After Benito del Valle's expulsion in 1958, the Ekin group broke away.

### Sireika and financing

Madariaga founded or helped found a travel agency, usually given as **Sireika**, both as a livelihood and to raise funds for Ekin. In 2014 he openly described the logic: propaganda and money were indispensable to a revolutionary organization. Public sources do not provide corporate filings, ownership shares, revenue, or the agency's end date.

### Founding date and founders

The date “ETA was founded” is genuinely disputed:

1. **Madariaga's continuity thesis:** Ekin's founding in 1952 was the substantive birth; ETA was a renaming.
2. **December 1958:** At Deba, after the final break with the PNV, the group agreed to create a distinct organization and asked Txillardegi to find a name. This is now the strongest historiographical date.
3. **1959:** The initials appeared in a document sent to Caracas and publicly in a letter to lehendakari Jesús María Leizaola dated 31 July 1959. For years this public letter supplied ETA's official anniversary.

Madariaga's 2014 list of seven founders was **José María Benito del Valle, José Manuel Agirre, Mikel Barandiaran, Txillardegi, Rafael Albisu, Iñaki Larramendi, and Madariaga**. Other lists add or substitute Eneko Irigarai, David López Dorronsoro, or Alfonso Irigoien, reflecting differing definitions of the founding circle.

### First clandestine actions and first arrest

An unpublished 1964 history by Madariaga, preserved at Lazkao, retrospectively attributed to ETA tar-and-paint propaganda, flags, and homemade bombs at the newspaper *Alerta* in Santander, the Civil Government in Vitoria, and police headquarters in Bilbao in late 1959. A December 1959 US consular report also attributed the explosions to Basque nationalists identified as ETA. These actions were not publicly claimed at the time, which explains why later ETA chronologies often began in 1961.

Madariaga was arrested in the 1960–61 crackdown. He said Melitón Manzanas and other political-police officers held and tortured him for four days in San Sebastián, breaking three ribs, before a 22-hour train journey to Madrid. He also said he was abused again on arrival at Carabanchel. Ahotsak records six months in Carabanchel with Iñaki Larramendi, Imanol Laspiur, and Rafael Albisu. These are first-person allegations supported by the broader documented record of Manzanas's torture methods and by later witness statements, but no surviving medical certificate was located.

ETA's attempted derailment of a train carrying Francoist veterans toward San Sebastián on 17–18 July 1961 produced no casualties but triggered mass arrests and near-destruction of the organization. Madariaga was not shown by the consulted record to have physically carried out that operation; his role at this stage was leadership, finance, propaganda, and preparation for armed action.

## Chronological Life History

### 1961–1964: exile, the First Assembly, and the military branch

After prison Madariaga moved to the French Basque Country. In May 1962 the First ETA Assembly met at the Benedictine monastery of Belloc near Urt. It declared ETA a Basque national-liberation movement and created a collegial executive. Madariaga became the first head of the **action or military branch**, charged with obtaining weapons; Sabin Uribe assisted him. The assembly did not clearly define limits on armed action, but Madariaga soon supplied the strongest answer: Euskadi was in a state of war with a foreign occupier.

At the 1963 Aberri Eguna at Itsasu/Itxassou, organized by Enbata, Madariaga and Ximun Haran planted a shoot of the Tree of Gernika and participated in the declaration commemorated by the *Itsasuko Agiria* stone. The scene later became part of his northern-Basque patriotic iconography.

In autumn 1963 Madariaga, Juan Luis Irusta, and an engineer called Jaime sought contact in Paris with representatives of Menachem Begin's Herut/Irgun milieu. The story is documented mainly through later historical reconstruction: via Alberto de Onaindía and journalist Elie Meisi they met a representative named Steinberg and asked for military help. Begin reportedly declined because assistance hostile to de Gaulle's France was politically impossible. The general claim fits Madariaga's own identification of Israel, Tunisia, and Ireland as models; the exact dialogue remains secondary-source reconstruction.

At the Third Assembly in 1964, ETA approved Madariaga's **“La insurrección en Euzkadi”** (ETA Training Notebook no. 20). It envisioned revolutionary war combining psychological warfare, urban/rural guerrilla, and political revolution; treated deception, compulsion, and killing as potentially necessary; and created the militant ideal in which commitment ended in victory, prison, or death. A notorious sentence held that shooting traitors was “less scandalous” than shooting enemies. This text is the clearest documentary basis for assigning him a formative role in ETA's doctrine of political violence.

Mikel Azurmendi later alleged that Madariaga organized a cell vote on whether to kill workerist dissident **Patxi Iturrioz**. The proposed killing did not occur. Azurmendi told the story in Jon Viar's documentary *Traidores*; the claim is therefore a named participant's retrospective testimony, not a court finding or contemporaneous order presently available for inspection.

### 1964–1966: French prosecution, Belgium, and Algeria

French authorities restricted Madariaga and other ETA leaders from the border departments in 1964. In 1965 they arrested Madariaga and Eneko Irigarai; Irigarai recalled six-month sentences followed by expulsion. They fled to Antwerp and Brussels, met Mandel and Belgian Trotskyists, and, short of work and money, decided to try revolutionary Algeria.

Their route ran by car through Belgium and Italy, ferry to Tunisia, then overland to Algiers, arriving in early 1965 (Irigarai alternately said February and March). Communications minister **Bachir Boumaza** found both men state jobs: Irigarai at a hospital outside the capital and Madariaga as an official of the new Algerian state. They witnessed Che Guevara's February 1965 Algiers speech and the June coup replacing Ahmed Ben Bella with Houari Boumédiène. “Ahmed” became one of Madariaga's clandestine names.

In March 1966 Madariaga reportedly asked the Algerian government for weapons, money, training, and a radio station for ETA. Boumédiène's government declined. The Algerian exile nonetheless put ETA in proximity to the FLN, Black Panthers, Palestinian, African, and Latin American movements; Irigarai became the durable international representative, while Madariaga's movements were more varied.

### 1966–1975: assemblies, splits, Burgos, and northern activism

Madariaga remained aligned with the nationalist-revolutionary faction against the workerist tendency around Iturrioz. The Fifth Assembly (1966–67) split the organization. Sources simplify his role differently: some call him an architect of the assembly's embrace of violence; others emphasize that his older Third Worldist strategy was displaced by younger leaders. He sat on the *Biltzar Ttipia* leadership emerging from the second part of the assembly with Edur Arregi, J. J. Etxabe, Emilio López Adán, Federico Krutwig, and others.

ETA's first acknowledged killings occurred in 1968—Civil Guard José Antonio Pardines, militant Txabi Etxebarrieta, and police chief Melitón Manzanas. Madariaga had procured early weapons and supplied the ideological framework for armed struggle; one sympathetic review of his memoir says he bought the weapon used in ETA's first planned lethal action. No consulted record proves he ordered or physically participated in a specific 1968 killing, and individual criminal responsibility should not be inferred solely from institutional leadership.

At the disputed Sixth Assembly in 1970, the dominant interior current expelled or rejected the claims of the five older leaders—Arregi, Etxabe, Krutwig, López, and Madariaga—who issued an August manifesto claiming continuity with the Fifth Assembly. Madariaga was charged in the **Burgos Process** but tried *in absentia* and declared in rebellion; he was not one of the sixteen defendants physically in the courtroom whose death sentences created international protest.

His known writing in this period includes *Ekai-arauketa dialektikorrari buruz* (1971), a Basque-language theoretical work on dialectical materialism, and essays such as **“Aldjeria”** and **“Lutte révolutionnaire en Euskadi: état de guerre larvée.”** He was associated with *Zutik!* and the broader exile press, although a complete article-level bibliography has not been reconstructed.

In 1973 he established **Aturri S.A.** in Biarritz. He described himself as its head and general problem-solver; at its height it employed 18–20 people. The business type is not clearly specified in accessible sources, though the 1988 prosecution involved invoices for magnets and alleged metal parts, implying industrial or technical trade.

In 1974 he participated in a hunger strike in Bayonne Cathedral, part of northern Basque mobilization around refugees and prisoners. Marc Légasse reportedly served as an improvised interpreter between strikers and police.

### 1975–1987: HB, Aturri, violence around the exile milieu, and internal criticism

After Franco's death Madariaga did not accept the Constitution or the Gernika Statute as resolving the national question. He helped build **Herri Batasuna** in 1978 and remained connected to ETA. Unlike younger leaders of ETA militar, he did not exercise day-to-day operational command in the democratic period, but he publicly defended the rupture strategy and described PNV, EA, and Euskadiko Ezkerra as reformist or traitorous.

Aturri and its milieu were themselves attacked during the “dirty war” period:

- 2 August 1979: employee or associate “Pantu” Lopategi was killed at Anglet; Ángel Iturbe and Sasiain Etxabe were wounded. The Batallón Vasco Español claimed the attack.
- 3 August 1979: Aturri's offices were bombed during Lopategi's funeral.
- September 1979: Periko Elizaran was killed in Biarritz in an attack claimed by Acción Nacionalista Española.
- 30 December 1980: former Aturri employee José Martín Sagardia was killed by a car bomb in Biarritz.

These attacks are documented in a 1991 Enbata defense dossier and should be understood as claims of responsibility by far-right/parapolice groups; they do not establish who commissioned each attack.

In the early and mid-1980s Madariaga wrote two internal letters criticizing ETA's leadership for becoming detached from the population and for mishandling relations with the northern group Iparretarrak. He later said that car bombs, attacks that ruined mass initiatives, and the 1986 murder of former ETA leader **Yoyes (Dolores González Katarain)** deepened his doubts.

There is an important contradiction. Several hostile or derivative profiles say that in 1987, around the presentation of *Artapalo: ETA después de Txomin*, Madariaga justified Yoyes's killing and regretted that it had not happened sooner. The original recording or contemporaneous transcript was not located. His 2014 account instead cited the murder as one cause of estrangement, though he softened blame by saying the perpetrators probably did not act in bad faith. The dossier cannot resolve whether his position changed rapidly, whether the 1987 quotation has been transmitted accurately, or whether both statements are compatible with a strategic rather than ethical objection. The allegation must not be presented as a proven verbatim quotation without its primary source.

In 1986 Madariaga met ETA leaders Txomin Iturbe, Juanjo Aranburu, and **Josu Urrutikoetxea (“Zekorra”/“Ternera”)** to present criticism. An argument erupted when he called the USSR and China imperial powers. He later met Txomin and Urrutikoetxea separately. These contacts demonstrate continued insider access even as his strategic dissent sharpened.

He obtained French nationality in 1986.

### 1988–1991: Aturri case, imprisonment, family tragedy, and rupture with ETA

French police arrested Madariaga on **30 May 1988**. Investigators seized Aturri invoices for magnets that Spanish police alleged had been used in ETA bombs. On 12 July a concealed space at his Senpere house, Zamaldegia, yielded deteriorated wooden boxes containing metal objects potentially usable in submachine-gun parts. Madariaga consistently denied knowing of the deposit and said it had been placed during construction.

Judge Michel Legrand initially charged unauthorized possession of explosives, weapons, and ammunition plus terrorist criminal association. The weapons/explosives counts were later dismissed (*non-lieu*), but the association case proceeded. Madariaga argued that he was being prejudged because the charging document contrasted his French naturalization with his status as an ETA founder.

He was held at Fresnes. Iraia's drowning in September 1988 produced the severest documented psychological crisis of his life. He said he contemplated suicide. A second crisis arose from his relations with Urrutikoetxea and another cellmate. Madariaga alleged that ETA withheld the normal family allowance from him while investigating whether he had made a police deal. He described humiliating task assignments, ostracism, and at least one physical assault by Urrutikoetxea; transfer to another cell prevented his suicide, he said. ETA eventually cleared him and restored support. These details are Madariaga's retrospective account; Urrutikoetxea's response to the physical-assault allegation was not located.

Madariaga dated his final departure from ETA to **1989**. The rupture was therefore simultaneous with, not prior to, his prosecution for collaboration.

On 22–23 June 1989 he and Patxi Noblia, José Luis “Azkoitti” Arrieta, and Juan Rego were tried in Paris in a case linked to the Sokoa arms cache. On 13 July the court convicted Madariaga of terrorist criminal association/collaboration and sentenced him to **four years' imprisonment and ten years' exclusion from specified territory**. On 21 November the Paris Court of Appeal confirmed four years but reduced the exclusion to five. The Court of Cassation on 18 July 1990 upheld the appellate court's refusal to hear certain defense witnesses. His defenders included Henri Leclerc/de Felice (sources vary on exact counsel list), Koko Abeberry, and González; supporters included local mayors, clerics, Gilles Perrault, Max Simeoni, Denis Laborde, and Walter Luyten.

He campaigned in prison for recognition and grouping of Basque political prisoners, access to Basque-language media, and improved conditions. In April 1990 he and Arrieta refused hand-and-foot shackling for a short hospital transfer. He was moved from Fresnes to Bois-d'Arcy in June 1991 and released in August after roughly 1,170 days. The territorial order barred him from fourteen departments, including the southwest, Brittany, and Paris, preventing return to Yvette and the children at Senpere.

Aturri, deprived of its director, deteriorated and closed. Madariaga described the company as lost rather than sold; no bankruptcy records or final accounts were located.

### 1991–2000: Bilbao lawyer and public dissident

Unable to live or work south of the Dordogne in France, Madariaga returned to Bilbao. He practiced law in offices shared with former HB lawyer **Txema Montero**, but later said the practice failed for lack of clients.

From 1993 he moved criticism into public view because his internal messages had been ignored. He argued that ETA had lost the struggle for self-determination as an armed organization and should stop. He feared retaliation and equated ETA's vague warning letter with the danger of being classified a traitor.

On 23 January 1995 ETA murdered PP politician **Gregorio Ordóñez** in San Sebastián. Herri Batasuna would not condemn the killing; Madariaga left HB. He later described the post-Ordóñez strategy as objectively disastrous and said attacks on elected officials strengthened Spanish nationalism and the PP.

He called repeatedly for a unilateral ceasefire in 1997–98. He supported the 1998 Lizarra-Garazi/Estella process and thought ETA should have made its ceasefire permanent then. ETA's unilateral resumption of attacks in 1999, without consulting the nationalist parties that had taken political risks, was for him a major betrayal of the opportunity.

In September 2000 he was among 768 signers of the Zaramaga document advocating dialogue, absence of violence, human rights, civil disobedience, and citizen participation. He was active in or close to **Elkarri**, the civil-society movement for dialogue.

### 2001–2005: Aralar and electoral politics

Madariaga joined the Aralar current in 2001. When it became an independent party in June 2002, he became head of its **Guarantees Commission** and a member of the executive. Aralar maintained Basque independence and left politics but explicitly rejected ETA violence.

He was Aralar's candidate for **deputy general of Biscay** in the May 2003 local/foral elections; he did not win the office. He also appeared on Aralar's lists for the Senate in the March 2004 general election and the European Parliament in June 2004; he was not elected. His public prominence was symbolic—an ETA founder endorsing an abertzale party without armed tutelage—more than electoral.

He appeared as himself in Julio Medem's controversial documentary *La pelota vasca: la piel contra la piedra* (2003), part of a large set of conflicting voices on Basque politics.

### 2004–2007: alleged mediation, the extortion investigation, and competing accounts

Madariaga said that before the March 2004 election PSE president **Jesús Eguiguren** spent roughly eight hours asking him and a friend to connect the Socialists with ETA's vicinity, with José Luis Rodríguez Zapatero's knowledge. In Madariaga's fuller version, he was willing to facilitate contact but awaited a “green light” that never came; in another press rendering he refused to be an interlocutor. The PSE denied his account. Later evidence established that Eguiguren did hold secret contacts with Arnaldo Otegi, but it does not independently prove every detail of the Madariaga meeting.

On **20 June 2006**, French and Spanish authorities arrested twelve people in an operation against ETA's alleged extortion-finance apparatus associated with the Bar Faisán investigation. Madariaga was questioned and formally placed under investigation in France for terrorist criminal association, financing terrorism, and complicity in organized extortion. He was released under judicial supervision rather than remanded; Judge Laurence Le Vert confined him to the Paris area for a period before allowing his return to Senpere.

The factual core is contested:

- Police investigators alleged that two Navarrese businesspeople gave Madariaga **€54,000** and that another €54,000 moved through PNV figure Gorka Agirre toward Joseba Elosua.
- Madariaga acknowledged that extorted businesspeople approached him as a lawyer and former insider and that he tried to prevent harm and provide guarantees. He denied being part of ETA's collection network.
- At the later Faisán trial, an investigator testified that police expected to find €108,000 but found no ETA material or money in the targeted search.
- Contemporary notices document investigation and judicial supervision, not a conviction of Madariaga in the 2006 matter. No final French dismissal order was located in the consulted sources. Claims that “charges were dropped within days” conflate release with exoneration and should not be repeated as established fact.

Madariaga argued that the arrest was designed to discredit a public critic of ETA and possibly punished his refusal to mediate. That is his allegation, not a court finding. The separate Faisán “tip-off” case ultimately convicted two Spanish police officers of revealing secrets to avoid disrupting the peace process; it did not convict Madariaga.

During the Zapatero process he said ETA, like all aggressors, should ask victims for forgiveness. Yet his formulation remained conditional and conflict-based: Spain and France should conduct self-criticism first, followed by Basque actors. This was not the unconditional ethical rejection voiced by some former ETA prisoners.

### 2008–2021: memoir, disarmament, illness, and final years

In a 2009 interview Madariaga attacked radicals for remaining under ETA's tutelage and favored reconciliation. He continued to support independence and to interpret the history as a conflict initiated by Spanish and French occupation.

He suffered a **stroke in 2012** and thereafter used a cane, though interviewers found him mentally and physically active in 2014. Over twelve to fourteen months he wrote his memoir because, he said, he needed to do so before dying and because younger Basques had a right to know the pleasant and ugly episodes.

*Egiari zor* appeared from Erein in 2014. It combined family history, Ekin/ETA origins, prison, exile, internal dissent, and appendices reproducing his critical letters. He said that with hindsight the founders might not have created ETA had they understood what it would become, yet remained proud of founding what he believed was necessary in the 1950s. He described himself, in Koldo Izagirre's phrase, as an **“ETA member without ETA.”** He celebrated the 2011 termination of armed activity as an achievement harder than beginning it.

At the April 2017 Bayonne gathering supporting ETA's disarmament, Madariaga appeared publicly despite poor health. He favored handing weapons to an international body or Basque institutions, not to Madrid or Paris. ETA dissolved in 2018.

The Spanish translation of the memoir, *En honor a la verdad: autobiografía*, translated by Jela Martínez Urmeneta and published by Pol·len Edicions, was presented on 16 February 2021. Madariaga died less than two months later at Senpere after a long illness.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Sireika travel agency

Founded in the Ekin period. It functioned as both a commercial livelihood and a financing mechanism for clandestine propaganda. Ownership, capitalization, and ultimate disposition remain undocumented.

### Aturri S.A.

Founded in Biarritz in 1973; directed by Madariaga; 18–20 employees at its peak. The company bought technical materials including magnets, which later became evidence in the 1988 investigation. It was repeatedly attacked in 1979–80 and collapsed after his arrest. Madariaga reported no sale proceeds and presented its closure as a personal financial loss.

### Law practice

After 1991 he practiced in Bilbao with Txema Montero. He said he stopped for lack of clients. His role as lawyer later became central to his defense in the 2006 extortion affair: he characterized contacts with threatened businesspeople as protective legal/intermediary work.

### Wealth and property

The family background was affluent and professional. Madariaga owned or occupied the Senpere house **Zamaldegia**, with a pool, and preserved his grandfather's statute-drafting table there. No reliable net-worth estimates, investment portfolio, real-estate register, offshore structure, corruption finding, fraud allegation, or money-laundering case was located. The relevant financial controversies concern organizational financing, Aturri materials, and the alleged 2006 transfer of extortion funds.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

Madariaga's continuity was Basque independence; what changed was his view of armed means and organizational authority.

1. **Family nationalism without racial-confessional orthodoxy:** He inherited Basque nationalism but rejected the old PNV linkage of patriotism with Catholicism and genealogical purity.
2. **Cultural reconstruction:** Euskara study, history, and cultural organization were not ancillary; they created the nation capable of political action.
3. **Anti-colonial revolutionary nationalism:** By 1962–64 he interpreted Spain and France as occupying powers and violence as imposed self-defense. Israel/Irgun, Ireland, Tunisia, Algeria, Vietnam, and Cuba supplied models.
4. **National before social liberation:** Influenced by Krutwig's two-stage “missile,” he maintained that independence had to precede class transformation. He accepted socialism but resisted workerism that dissolved the national subject.
5. **Strategic dissidence:** His early 1980s criticism concerned democratic responsiveness, northern relations, operational mistakes, and loss of popular support—not an immediate moral renunciation of all violence.
6. **Public rejection of armed tutelage:** From 1993–95 onward he argued that ETA damaged independence, left HB over Ordóñez, backed Elkarri and Aralar, and demanded an end to armed action.
7. **Qualified retrospective position:** He asked ETA to apologize and supported disarmament, but still called Spain and France aggressors, rejected surrendering arms to them, defended the original necessity of ETA, and did not adopt the Spanish constitutional settlement as legitimate national self-determination.

His childhood faith is documented—he prayed not to die before returning to the Basque Country—but no later institutional religious practice is established.

- **Txillardegi:** childhood acquaintance turned cofounder; linguistic and political collaborator; later fellow critic received by Aralar. They shared national primacy but differed in temperament and emphasis.
- **José María Benito del Valle, José Manuel Agirre, Rafael Albisu, Iñaki Larramendi, Mikel Barandiaran, Gurutz Ansola:** Ekin/ETA founding network.

- **Federico Krutwig:** theorist and collaborator whose *Vasconia* informed Madariaga's insurrectionary doctrine.
- **Eneko Irigarai:** fellow founder and companion in French arrest, Belgian exile, and the 1965 journey to Algeria.

- **Ximun Haran, Jakes and Koko Abeberry, Enbata circle:** northern Basque political, legal, and solidarity network.

- **Txema Montero:** shared Bilbao legal office and a parallel trajectory away from HB orthodoxy.

- **Yvette Madariaga:** second wife, nurse, wage earner during imprisonment, and mother of six children.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Founder of ETA in one day, 31 July 1959”

- **Story:** Seven men founded ETA on St Ignatius's Day.
- **Origin/circulation:** ETA anniversary culture and short reference works.
- **Evidence:** The 31 July letter is real, but Madariaga and later historians describe a process from Ekin (1952), the Deba decision (December 1958), early use of the initials (early 1959), and the public letter.
- **Effect:** It supplied a ceremonial birthday while obscuring organizational continuity and contested membership.

### “Grandfather” or “Englishman” of ETA

- **Story:** Media portray him as ETA's patriarch; the fictional composite **El Inglés** in *La línea invisible* evokes him as an external arms-and-money organizer.
- **Evidence:** His education, age, exile, procurement role, and doctrinal authorship support the archetype, but operational command passed to younger generations long before ETA's deadliest democratic-era campaign.
- **Effect:** The image exaggerates continuous personal control while correctly identifying formative influence.

### “From terrorist to pacifist”

- **Story:** A clean conversion from violence to peace.
- **Evidence:** The change was gradual: internal strategic criticism in the early 1980s; claimed exit in 1989; public dissent in 1993; HB departure in 1995; Aralar after 2001. He continued to defend ETA's founding, national-conflict framing, and dignified disarmament.
- **Effect:** Spanish obituaries used the arc as repentance; abertzale obituaries used it as proof that he understood before others that armed action had expired.

### “Traitor” within the movement

- **Story:** His dissent meant accommodation with the enemy.
- **Circulation:** ETA's internal investigation, prison ostracism, warning letters, and later polemics.
- **Evidence:** ETA investigated him and withheld support, but cleared him; he never abandoned independence.
- **Effect:** The threat of the traitor category helps explain his fear, psychological crisis, and years-long delay in making criticism public.

### “The state framed him because he sought peace”

- **Story:** The 1988 and especially 2006 arrests were political operations against a dissident intermediary.
- **Circulation:** Madariaga, Aralar/abertzale media, and later peace-process controversy.
- **Evidence:** The 1988 case produced a conviction after dismissal of some counts; the 2006 case produced charges but no located conviction, and the wider Faisán affair did involve an unlawful police warning designed not to disrupt talks. None of this proves the personal-retaliation theory.
- **Effect:** It reinforced his status among supporters as a man punished by both states and ETA orthodoxy.

### Posthumous epic/hagiography

Enbata placed him in a Basque national “Pantheon,” compared him to adventurer Lezo Urreiztieta, predicted a future pastoral drama, and framed his life as epic resistance. Otegi called him a committed patriot. Hostile outlets instead reduced him to founder, hardliner, and alleged Yoyes apologist. Both memory regimes select real episodes but turn a contradictory life into a symbolic type.

## Death, Posthumous Reputation, and Legacy

Madariaga died at home in Senpere after years of illness and a 2012 stroke. No more specific cause of death was publicly documented. His family held the private position in the later years of a man who had spent much of his adult life absent through clandestinity, exile, travel, or prison.

His posthumous reputation divides along several axes:

- **As founder and theorist:** His role in transforming a cultural-nationalist student network into an organization prepared for armed struggle is unusually well documented. “La insurrección en Euzkadi” and his weapons-procurement office place him nearer the center than founders who departed earlier.
- **As dissident:** His early-1980s letters and 1989 departure preceded the broad left-abertzale break with armed tutelage by roughly two decades. Aralar converted that dissent into an electoral structure.
- **As incomplete penitent:** He rejected ETA's later strategy, asked it to apologize, and supported disarmament, but never renounced the founding project, the description of Spain and France as occupiers, or independence. Different audiences therefore call him pacifist, strategic dissident, insufficiently repentant, or prophetic critic.
- **As a bridge in left-abertzale memory:** Aralar later reconverged with the broader sovereignist left in EH Bildu. Otegi's praise at his death symbolized the reintegration of a once-suspect dissident. Patxi Zabaleta argued that Madariaga and Txillardegi entering Aralar, and Aralar later joining Bildu, closed the historical cycle begun in 1959.
- **As warning and symbol:** His life joins ETA's full institutional arc—cultural recovery, antifranco clandestinity, revolutionary violence, exile diplomacy, democratic-era persistence, internal coercion, strategic failure, party dissent, peace advocacy, disarmament, and dissolution—without fitting cleanly into either redemption narrative.

## Chronology

- **11 Oct 1932** — Born in Abando, Bilbao.
- **Mar–Oct 1937** — Evacuated through France; reaches Chile on fifth birthday.
- **1946 (stronger account; some sources say 1942)** — Returns via Buenos Aires to Bilbao.
- **c. 1948–50** — Meets Txillardegi; begins adult political and Euskara formation.
- **1952–53** — Cofounds Ekin at Deusto.
- **1956** — Ekin participates in World Basque Congress in Paris.
- **1958** — Break with PNV/EGI; Deba meeting chooses new organization and commissions its name.
- **1959** — ETA initials enter use; early propaganda and homemade bombs; 31 July letter to Leizaola.
- **1960–61** — Arrested, alleges torture by Melitón Manzanas, imprisoned six months at Carabanchel.
- **17–18 Jul 1961** — ETA train-derailment attempt; crackdown and exile.
- **May 1962** — First ETA Assembly at Belloc; executive member and military/action chief.
- **1962** — Reported Cambridge doctorate; exact thesis/date unverified.
- **1963** — Plants Gernika oak shoot at Itsasu; seeks Irgun/Begin help in Paris.
- **1964** — Authors “La insurrección en Euzkadi”; Third Assembly adopts it.
- **1964–65** — Restricted, arrested, and expelled by France.
- **1965** — Belgian exile; travels with Irigarai to Algiers; works for Algerian state.
- **Mar 1966** — Reportedly requests Algerian arms/training/radio support; refused.
- **1966–67** — Fifth Assembly leadership struggles.
- **1968** — ETA begins acknowledged killing; Madariaga remains senior exile leader.
- **1970** — Sixth Assembly split; Burgos Process charges him in absentia.
- **1971** — Publishes *Ekai-arauketa dialektikorrari buruz*.
- **1973** — Establishes Aturri S.A. in Biarritz.
- **1974** — Bayonne Cathedral hunger strike.
- **1978** — Helps found/build Herri Batasuna.
- **1979–80** — Aturri staff and offices suffer BVE/ANE-attributed attacks.
- **Early/mid-1980s** — Sends critical internal letters to ETA leadership.
- **1986** — French naturalization; confrontational meetings with ETA leaders; Yoyes murdered.
- **30 May 1988** — Arrested in France; Aturri and home searched.
- **23 Sep 1988** — Daughter Iraia drowns while he is at Fresnes; suicide crisis.
- **1989** — Says he leaves ETA; convicted in Paris and sentenced to four years.
- **21 Nov 1989** — Appeal confirms imprisonment, reduces exclusion order.
- **Aug 1991** — Released after about 1,170 days; returns to Bilbao because of exclusion zone.
- **1991–93** — Practices law with Txema Montero; Aturri closes.
- **1993** — Begins public criticism of ETA.
- **Jan 1995** — Leaves HB after murder of Gregorio Ordóñez and HB's refusal to condemn it.
- **1997–98** — Calls for unilateral truce; supports Lizarra-Garazi process.
- **1999** — Condemns ETA's ending of ceasefire.
- **2000** — Signs Zaramaga nonviolence/dialogue document.
- **2001–02** — Joins and helps constitute Aralar; heads Guarantees Commission.
- **2003** — Candidate for deputy general of Biscay; appears in *La pelota vasca*.
- **2004** — Senate and European Parliament candidate; alleged meeting with Eguiguren.
- **20 Jun 2006** — Arrested in alleged extortion-finance operation; investigated and released under supervision.
- **2009** — Publicly attacks continued ETA tutelage and favors reconciliation.
- **2011** — Welcomes ETA's definitive end of armed activity.
- **2012** — Stroke; records extensive Ahotsak oral history.
- **2014** — Publishes *Egiari zor* and gives final major interviews.
- **Apr 2017** — Attends Bayonne disarmament event.
- **2018** — ETA dissolves.
- **16 Feb 2021** — Spanish memoir edition presented.
- **6 Apr 2021** — Dies at Senpere, aged 88.
