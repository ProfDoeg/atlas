# Peter von Cornelius: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier concerns the German painter and academy director **Peter Joseph von Cornelius (1783–1867)**, not his nephew, the composer and poet Peter Cornelius (1824–1874).

The reconstruction draws principally on museum catalogues, archival finding aids, historical biographical dictionaries, digitized correspondence, contemporary or near-contemporary biographies, and specialist art-historical studies. Nineteenth-century biographies are valuable testimony but frequently combine documentation with aesthetic judgment and nationalist commemoration. Such judgments are identified below as contemporary or retrospective interpretation rather than fact.

No documented criminal prosecution, fraud, corruption investigation, lawsuit, imprisonment, intelligence connection, political violence, or comparable scandal involving Cornelius was found. The principal controversies concern patronage, artistic control, delegation to pupils, confessional and nationalist ideology, technical criticism of his paintings, and his later construction as a Prussian “state artist.”

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Peter Joseph Cornelius; after ennoblement, **Peter Joseph von Cornelius**.
- **Usual name:** Peter von Cornelius.
- **Variants:** Peter Cornelius; Peter Joseph Cornelius; Peter Ritter von Cornelius; Pierre de Cornelius in some French-language publications.
- **Attributed pseudonym:** The Deutsche Digitale Bibliothek authority record lists **“Raphael.”** The accessible record does not explain whether this was an actual working pseudonym, a catalogue-derived alias, or a comparison with Raphael; it should therefore not be treated as a securely documented public pseudonym.
- **Title:** “von” following Bavarian ennoblement. Sources disagree slightly over whether the decisive ennoblement occurred in **1823** or **1825**. The Deutsche Biographie authority heading says “seit 1823,” while the Rhineland historical portal connects the honor with Ludwig I’s satisfaction after 1825. The discrepancy may reflect the date of an order conferring personal nobility versus later formal usage.
- **Profession:** Painter, draftsman, illustrator, fresco designer, teacher, professor, academy director, administrator of artistic education.
- **Principal classification:** History painter; leading representative of the Nazarene movement and of nineteenth-century monumental fresco revival.
- **Religion:** Roman Catholic.
- **Born:** **23 September 1783**, Düsseldorf, Duchy of Berg, Holy Roman Empire.
- **Died:** **6 March 1867**, Berlin, Kingdom of Prussia, aged 83.
- **Burial:** Old cathedral cemetery of the Roman Catholic St Hedwig congregation, Berlin. The original grave did not survive; a simplified memorial grave was subsequently established.
- **Principal residences and working centers:** Düsseldorf; Frankfurt am Main; Rome; Düsseldorf and Munich seasonally; Munich; Berlin; later Rome again; final residence in Berlin.
- **Nationality and political geography:** “German” in later national terminology, but born before a unified German state. His successive professional worlds included Electoral/ducal Düsseldorf, Napoleonic-era Frankfurt, the Papal States, Bavaria, and Prussia.

Principal institutional summaries are supplied by [Deutsche Biographie](https://www.deutsche-biographie.de/sfz69592.html), the [Portal Rheinische Geschichte](https://www.rheinische-geschichte.lvr.de/Persoenlichkeiten/peter-joseph-von-cornelius/DE-2086/lido/5e414955dc4274.32308507), the [Bavarian State Painting Collections](https://www.sammlung.pinakothek.de/de/artist/vDn4Zv64aK/peter-von-cornelius-1783), and the [Staatliche Museen zu Berlin](https://visit.smb.museum/object/obj-2262528).

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and paternal ancestry

**Father:** Johann Christian Aloys Cornelius, usually called **Aloys Cornelius** (1748–1800), painter, teacher, and inspector at the Düsseldorf Academy. He belonged to the late-Baroque/classicizing academic tradition associated with Anton Raphael Mengs.

**Mother:** The sources disagree over her surname:

- Deutsche Biographie gives **Anna Helene Cosse**.
- The Portal Rheinische Geschichte calls her **Anna Cosse**, dying about 1809.
- Other later compilations give **Anna Helena Corsten**.

The difference has not been conclusively resolved in the sources consulted. “Cosse” has the stronger institutional authority here, but “Corsten” may represent a variant transcription or a separate genealogical correction.

**Paternal grandfather:** Peter Christian Cornelius, described as a Düsseldorf wine and seed merchant and innkeeper.

**Paternal grandmother:** Anna Gertrud Bonrath.

The family was therefore not aristocratic. Cornelius’s nobiliary particle was acquired through royal honor, not inherited ancestry.

### Siblings

His older brother **Lambert Cornelius (1778–1823)** succeeded their father as inspector of the Düsseldorf Academy and helped support the family. According to the Rhineland portal, five sisters and two brothers survived to relatively mature ages, but the consulted summary does not name all of them.

The father’s early death left a large family with limited means. Peter and Lambert undertook paid artistic work in part to support the household.

### Marriages and children

Cornelius married three times, all apparently to Italian women:

1. **Carolina or Caroline Grossi**, married in Rome in **1814**.  
   - Some sources give her death as **1832**.
   - Another tradition says that she and a daughter died on **24 October 1834**.
   - Cornelius painted her portrait in about 1813–1814.
   - The difference between 1832 and 1834 remains unresolved in the consulted institutional material.

2. **Gertruda, Geltrude, or Gertrude Fervatini/Ferretini**, married in Rome in **1835**, reportedly initially by proxy before she joined him in Munich. She died in **June 1859**.

3. **Teresa Giampieri**, a younger woman from Urbino, married in **1860** according to Deutsche Biographie and the Rhineland portal; some secondary compilations say 1861.

Deutsche Biographie records **one son and two daughters**, apparently from the first marriage, but the consulted concise sources do not provide a fully reliable list of their names and dates. One daughter is reported to have died with Carolina. No evidence located suggests that Cornelius founded a continuing artistic or commercial dynasty through his children.

### Extended family

His nephew **Peter Cornelius (1824–1874)** became a composer, poet, critic, and associate of Franz Liszt and Richard Wagner. In 1844 the younger Cornelius entered his uncle’s Berlin household, where he encountered prominent artists and intellectuals. The two men are frequently confused in catalogues and search results.

Another relative mentioned in genealogical records was **Carl Adolf Cornelius**, a historian and church historian.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1783–1798

Cornelius was born into the physical and institutional environment of the Düsseldorf Academy. His father’s position gave him access to its collection of antiquities and to the Düsseldorf gallery, later transferred in large part to Munich. Nineteenth-century biographer Friedrich Pecht reported that Rubens especially impressed the child, while his father directed him toward Raphael.

These recollections are **reported facts from near-contemporary biography**, not independently documented childhood testimony. They accord with Cornelius’s later synthesis of large-scale Baroque or Renaissance composition with German religious and literary themes.

He initially drew battle and hunting scenes. The family’s economic circumstances became more difficult as his father’s health or employment declined and then deteriorated sharply after Aloys’s death.

---

## Education and Formation, 1798–1809

Cornelius entered the Düsseldorf Academy in **1798** and studied there until approximately **1805** under **Johann Peter Langer (1756–1824)**. One institutional account states that Langer judged him deficient in talent. This is a documented biographical tradition but should not be exaggerated into a formal expulsion: Cornelius continued his education and professional work.

His training was uneven. Later critics argued that he never fully mastered anatomical observation, modeling, perspective, foreshortening, color, or traditional fresco technique. Pecht attributed this partly to the need to earn money before completing systematic academic formation. This is an influential **critical interpretation**, not a neutral academic record.

After his father’s death, Cornelius earned money by:

- painting church banners;
- drawing illustrations for calendars;
- making portraits;
- accepting minor church commissions;
- producing biblical and classical compositions.

### Weimar Prize Competitions

Between **1803 and 1805**, Cornelius entered the art competitions organized by Johann Wolfgang von Goethe and Heinrich Meyer:

- *Odysseus bei Polyphem* / *Odysseus with Polyphemus* (1803);
- *Das Meeresgeschlecht* / *The Race of the Sea* (1804);
- *Herakles in der Unterwelt* / *Heracles in the Underworld* (1804).

He won no major prize. Goethe did not become an uncomplicated admirer. Later accounts state that Goethe recognized Cornelius’s compositional force but objected to the tendency to blur the boundaries between poetry and painting.

### Early contacts and influences

Around 1803 Cornelius encountered the brothers **Sulpiz and Melchior Boisserée**, collectors and advocates of medieval and Early Netherlandish painting. Their influence appears to have increased gradually rather than producing an immediate conversion from classicism.

The Cologne canon and collector **Ferdinand Franz Wallraf** obtained for him a commission at St Quirin in Neuss. In **1807–1808**, Cornelius decorated the crossing dome in grisaille with the Evangelists, Apostles, and allegories of the cardinal virtues. The work was partly damaged by 1859 and whitewashed in 1865; only preparatory drawings survive.

Early paintings included *The Fourteen Holy Helpers* (1804), portraits, and biblical subjects.

---

## Frankfurt, the Faust Cycle, and Romantic Reorientation, 1809–1811

After his mother’s death—usually placed around **June or autumn 1809**—Cornelius left Düsseldorf, traveling through Koblenz to **Frankfurt am Main**. He lived in the household of publisher and collector **Friedrich Wilmans**.

For the Frankfurt banker Schmidt’s house on the Zeil, later known as Villa Mumm, he painted six mythological decorations in oil on canvas within illusionistic architectural settings in 1810. These are lost.

For Prince-Primate **Karl Theodor von Dalberg**, Cornelius produced a *Madonna* and a *Holy Family*. The latter displeased Dalberg, and Cornelius lost his patronage. This was an early documented professional failure.

### Illustrations to Goethe’s *Faust*

The decisive work of the Frankfurt years was a cycle of twelve pen drawings inspired by the first part of Goethe’s *Faust*, published in 1808.

- Seven drawings were made in Frankfurt by 1811.
- The remaining drawings and title page followed in Rome, principally in 1813–1815.
- Ferdinand Ruscheweyh engraved the first edition, published by Ferdinand Wenner in Frankfurt in 1816.
- A second edition appeared in 1825 with an additional *Osterspaziergang* plate engraved by Julius Thaeter.
- Cornelius dedicated the published cycle to Goethe.

Cornelius informed Goethe that he had studied Dürer’s marginal drawings for Emperor Maximilian’s prayer book. His *Faust* illustrations replaced the smooth academic idiom of his youth with angular contour, expressive compression, sixteenth-century German graphic references, and an increasingly “national” historical style.

The cycle brought him greater recognition than his paintings for Dalberg and became one of his most durable achievements.

---

## Rome and the Nazarene Circle, 1811–1819

### Journey to Italy

Cornelius and his close friend **Johann Christian Xeller** left for Italy at the end of August 1811 and reached Rome on **14 October 1811**.

They joined the **Brotherhood of St Luke**, or Lukasbund, centered on **Johann Friedrich Overbeck**. The group later acquired the nickname **Nazarenes**, initially a somewhat mocking reference to the members’ long hair and religious appearance.

The Nazarenes rejected contemporary academic routine and sought renewal through:

- late-medieval and early-Renaissance religious art;
- Dürer, Perugino, Raphael, and the Italian Quattrocento;
- communal workshop practice;
- religious seriousness;
- monumental mural painting;
- the reconciliation of art, faith, and national culture.

Cornelius never simply duplicated Overbeck. He was more forceful, dramatic, and receptive to Michelangelo and Raphael’s mature monumental language. He also proved less willing than Overbeck to subordinate art completely to Roman Catholic devotional revival.

### National politics and the Wars of Liberation

The anti-Napoleonic uprisings of **1813** strongly affected the German artists in Rome. Cornelius reportedly contemplated returning to enlist but was persuaded not to do so.

In a letter to the publisher Wenner, he described national faith and moral conviction, rather than armies alone, as the real defense of a people and called for a “German temple of art.” The surviving language documents a politically charged cultural nationalism. It does not establish membership in a political party or clandestine organization.

His Nibelungen drawings arose in this setting. They joined medieval German literature to the anti-Napoleonic search for a national culture.

### Marriage and financial hardship

Cornelius married Carolina Grossi in 1814. Near-contemporary accounts describe severe poverty during the early marriage. The Casa Bartholdy commission reportedly reimbursed costs rather than providing a large profit. No full household accounts, property schedule, or estate valuation were located.

### Programmatic letter to Joseph Görres

On **3 November 1814**, Cornelius wrote a major programmatic letter to journalist and intellectual **Joseph Görres**. He advocated the revival of fresco as the means of giving German art a public, monumental, nationally and spiritually formative purpose.

The letter is central evidence for his mature position:

- art should not remain a private luxury or a flattering servant of wealthy individuals;
- public monuments could educate and morally form the people;
- fresco would reunite architecture, painting, religion, and collective memory;
- the period from Giotto to Raphael offered the technical and institutional model.

### Casa Bartholdy

Prussian consul **Jakob Salomon Bartholdy** commissioned the Nazarene artists to decorate rooms in the Palazzo Zuccari, his Roman residence, with the story of Joseph.

The participating artists were:

- Cornelius;
- Overbeck;
- Wilhelm Schadow;
- Philipp Veit.

Cornelius painted:

- *Joseph Interprets Pharaoh’s Dreams*;
- *Joseph Reveals Himself to His Brothers*.

The paintings were completed in **1816–1817**, became widely accessible to visitors, and established the group’s reputation. They were removed from the walls in the late nineteenth century, transferred to Berlin in 1887, and displayed in the Nationalgalerie from 1888. They are now held by the Alte Nationalgalerie.

The museum’s reconstruction of the cycle documents the division of labor among the four artists.

### Villa Massimo and Dante

Marchese Carlo Massimo commissioned the Nazarenes to decorate rooms with scenes from Dante, Ariosto, and Tasso. Cornelius was assigned Dante’s *Divine Comedy*. He produced designs and two full-scale cartoons but abandoned the commission when called to Munich.

### Niebuhr and separation from the Nazarene core

Prussian diplomat and historian **Barthold Georg Niebuhr**, arriving in Rome in 1816, became an important patron, friend, and intermediary with the Prussian government.

Pecht’s 1876 biography credits Niebuhr with helping Cornelius distance himself from what Pecht polemically called the “Romanizing” and “ultramontane” tendencies of the Nazarene group. The friendship is documented; the claim that Niebuhr was principally responsible for an ideological break is an **interpretation by a liberal-national nineteenth-century critic**.

Cornelius remained Catholic and continued producing religious art. His divergence from Overbeck concerned artistic control, politics, confessional exclusivity, and the balance between national and Roman Catholic purposes—not a rejection of Christianity.

---

## Düsseldorf and Munich: Dual Appointments, 1818–1825

In January 1818, Bavarian crown prince **Ludwig**, visiting Rome, encountered Cornelius and the Casa Bartholdy frescoes. In April he commissioned Cornelius to decorate major rooms of the new **Glyptothek** in Munich.

At nearly the same time, the Prussian government offered Cornelius the directorship of the revived Düsseldorf Academy. Because the communication was delayed, the offer initially missed him in Rome. He ultimately undertook both responsibilities.

Cornelius worked seasonally:

- winters in Düsseldorf, reorganizing the academy;
- summers in Munich, designing and supervising the Glyptothek frescoes.

### Düsseldorf Academy reforms

He established or strengthened a master class oriented toward monumental painting. His Düsseldorf pupils included:

- Hermann Anschütz;
- Wilhelm von Kaulbach;
- Adam Eberle;
- Hermann Stilke;
- Karl Ferdinand Sohn or closely associated younger Düsseldorf artists;
- Ernst Förster;
- Franz Xaver Glink and others in the expanding workshop network.

The precise year he formally assumed the Düsseldorf directorship varies in sources between **1819 and 1821**, likely reflecting appointment, arrival, and effective service dates.

His teaching rested on large historical compositions, cartoons, group execution, and integration with architecture. Pupils frequently performed the physical transfer and coloring of designs.

### Projects delegated to pupils

Cornelius encouraged fresco projects in the Rhineland and gave pupils substantial responsibilities. This created professional opportunities and helped form the Düsseldorf school, but it also made attribution difficult. “A work by Cornelius” may mean:

1. a conception or sketch by Cornelius;
2. a full-scale cartoon made under his authority;
3. a mural transferred or painted by pupils;
4. a project supervised by him but physically executed by others.

This distinction is essential throughout his oeuvre.

---

## Munich Career and the Glyptothek, 1819–1830

Cornelius began work in Munich in autumn 1819. The Glyptothek program encompassed Greek mythology and the Trojan cycle:

- **Hall of the Gods**, completed by 1826;
- **Trojan or Heroic Hall**, completed in 1830;
- **Vestibule**, completed in 1830.

He designed mythological wall and ceiling schemes including Olympus, the underworld, the marine world, the times of day, the anger of Achilles, the destruction of Troy, Priam before Achilles, and related episodes.

The designs were stylistically indebted not primarily to ancient vase painting but to Michelangelo, Raphael, and Annibale Carracci. Monumental outlines, crowded figure arrangements, and philosophical allegory were more important to Cornelius than archaeological reconstruction.

### Workshop structure

Cornelius assigned extensive execution to pupils. This was normal for monumental painting but became controversial because the assistants varied in skill. King Ludwig approved the earlier Hall of the Gods but criticized the later vestibule and heroic rooms, particularly their color.

Supporters saw the workshop as a revival of Renaissance collective practice and a school for public artists. Critics charged that Cornelius valued invention while neglecting execution and allowed inferior assistants to compromise finished murals.

### Salary and compensation

A nineteenth-century commemorative source claimed Cornelius received **10,000 gulden** for the Glyptothek compositions while silk wall coverings for the same rooms cost 80,000 gulden. The comparison circulated as an example of artists’ poor compensation. It has not been independently checked against the complete Bavarian accounts here and should be labeled a **reported historical claim**, not a verified balance sheet.

### Munich Academy directorship

After Johann Peter Langer’s death, Cornelius was called to direct the Munich Academy. The appointment is variously dated **1824 or 1825**. He dissolved his Düsseldorf contract in December 1824 and resided fully in Munich from 1825.

He also held responsibility for teaching art history and aesthetics. Many Düsseldorf pupils followed him, while Munich recruits included:

- Moritz von Schwind;
- Bernhard von Neher;
- Clemens von Zimmermann;
- Wilhelm Lindenschmit;
- Johann Georg Hiltensperger;
- Karl Hermann;
- Wilhelm von Kaulbach.

### Ennoblement

Ludwig raised Cornelius into the Bavarian nobility after expressing satisfaction with the Glyptothek’s first completed section. The surname “von Cornelius” thereafter became standard. As noted above, reference works differ between 1823 and 1825 in dating the honor.

---

## Pinakothek, Hofgarten, Odeon, and Bavarian Historical Art

Cornelius developed a large program for the loggias of the Alte Pinakothek: twenty-five architectural bays divided into Italian and German sections, narrating art history through artists’ lives and allegorical theory.

He worked on the designs from approximately **1827 to 1840**. On 25 December 1827, the royal building administration informed him that he would have no control over the cartoons or final execution. The work was assigned largely to **Clemens von Zimmermann**, who executed it between 1831 and 1840.

Cornelius considered this a personal and artistic rebuff and contemplated leaving Munich. Critics later generally preferred his printed or drawn inventions to Zimmermann’s painted execution.

He also devised or supervised:

- a cycle of Bavarian/Wittelsbach history for the Munich Hofgarten arcades;
- three mythological ceiling paintings for the Odeon;
- designs for additional state and dynastic decorative programs.

Correspondence between Cornelius and Ludwig I shows the king revising titles and historical formulations for the Wittelsbach scenes. This documents royal participation in what subjects were represented and how their political meaning was stated.

---

## The Ludwigskirche and Break with Ludwig I, 1829–1841

To retain Cornelius after the Pinakothek conflict, Ludwig commissioned him to decorate the church of St Ludwig, designed by **Friedrich von Gärtner**.

Cornelius originally conceived a Christian epic encompassing the entire church. Architectural, financial, or royal constraints reduced the program to the choir and crossing.

### Design and execution

Designs and cartoons were prepared from **1830 to 1836**, partly during Roman stays. The program included:

- God the Creator in the apse vault;
- the Nativity;
- the Adoration of the Magi;
- the Crucifixion;
- the Holy Spirit;
- the Communion of Saints;
- the enormous *Last Judgment* on the altar wall.

Execution occurred chiefly from **1836 to 1840**. Pupils painted much of the scheme. Cornelius is credited with personally executing the *Last Judgment*, approximately 1,500 square feet and deliberately rivaling Michelangelo’s Sistine Chapel composition.

### Illness, 1835–1836

A near-contemporary account reports that Cornelius suffered a severe illness during the winter of 1835–1836, attributed to overwork, and that his life was considered endangered. He recovered sufficiently to begin painting the *Last Judgment* in summer 1836.

No clinical diagnosis was found. “Caused by overwork” is a historical attribution rather than a medically established cause.

### Final conflict with Ludwig

Ludwig again disliked the coloring. Relations were also affected by Cornelius’s deteriorating friendship with architect Gärtner and the king’s increasingly direct intervention.

One hostile-to-Ludwig anecdote says that when Cornelius invited the king to inspect the completed *Last Judgment*, Ludwig entered with Gärtner while a doorkeeper prevented Cornelius from following. This appears in later nineteenth-century biography. It is best classified as a **reported incident**, vivid but not independently corroborated here.

The conflict culminated in Cornelius’s departure. The simplified version—“the king rejected the fresco, so Cornelius left”—obscures a longer accumulation of disagreements over:

- control of execution;
- color;
- use of assistants;
- architectural constraints;
- reductions in programs;
- the Pinakothek decision;
- royal artistic micromanagement;
- Cornelius’s sensitivity about authority and authorship.

In later years Cornelius and Ludwig partially reconciled, and Cornelius acknowledged Ludwig’s importance as a patron of German art.

---

## Paris, Stuttgart, and International Contacts

Cornelius made a documented trip to **Paris in autumn 1838**. In spring 1839 he traveled to Stuttgart for the unveiling of the Schiller monument.

In **1839**, he received the knight’s cross of the French Legion of Honour. His monumental cartoons were subsequently exhibited in Paris, Brussels, Berlin, and elsewhere.

These international displays contributed to his reputation even when many of the intended frescoes remained unrealized.

---

## Berlin and Prussian Royal Patronage, 1840–1853

On **15 August 1840**, shortly after Frederick William IV became king of Prussia, Cornelius offered his services. The king invited him to Berlin with the title “director” but apparently without routine administrative obligations. He arrived in **April 1841**.

His first Berlin task included translating or adapting **Karl Friedrich Schinkel’s** designs for the vestibule of the Altes Museum into fresco form.

### London journey and Parliament consultation

Cornelius traveled to London after his Berlin appointment. A projected decoration of Gatton Park failed when the commissioner died. He nevertheless consulted **Sir Charles Lock Eastlake** concerning fresco decoration for the new Palace of Westminster.

The conversations were published in the **1842 Parliamentary Papers** and conveyed information about German fresco practice. Thus Cornelius’s influence entered the British debate over monumental painting even though he did not execute Westminster murals himself.

### Smaller Prussian commissions

Projects included:

- six Tasso drawings for the grand duke of Mecklenburg;
- seven cartoons for stained-glass windows in the princely crypt at Schwerin, 1843–1844;
- Old Testament prophet medallions for St Nicholas’s Church, Potsdam, 1850;
- the painting *Christ in Limbo*, commissioned by Count Atanazy Raczyński, 1843;
- *Blessed Are Those Who Hunger and Thirst for Righteousness*, also for Raczyński, 1851.

---

## Campo Santo: The Unfinished Final Project, 1841–1867

Frederick William IV planned a new Berlin Cathedral and a dynastic burial complex commonly called the **Campo Santo**. Cornelius’s designs for its frescoes became the defining labor of his last quarter-century.

The theological center was Romans 6:23: death as the wages of sin and eternal life as God’s gift through Christ. The program combined:

- biblical history;
- Last Things and the Apocalypse;
- Lutheran or broader Protestant theological concerns;
- Catholic Romantic visual traditions;
- Hohenzollern dynastic commemoration;
- the king’s personal religious concepts.

Principal designs included:

- *The Four Horsemen of the Apocalypse* (1846; missing since 1945);
- *The Arrival of the New Jerusalem* (1849);
- *The Fall of Babylon* (1852–1853);
- *Christ after the Resurrection* (1863–1865);
- *The Outpouring of the Holy Spirit* (1865–1866);
- works of mercy and associated socle scenes.

The first four major drawings were substantially developed by 1845 and engraved by Julius Thaeter. Cornelius’s treatment of the Apocalypse drew explicitly on Dürer.

### Failure of the project

The Revolution of **1848** stopped the cathedral and burial-complex building program. It was never revived in Cornelius’s lifetime. Formally, however, he continued to receive authorization or support for work on the cartoons and an apse design.

This distinction matters:

- **Documented fact:** the architectural project ceased.
- **Documented fact:** Cornelius continued producing cartoons for it.
- **Interpretation:** the drawings became an autonomous monument to an unrealized theocratic or dynastic vision.
- **Unresolved financial question:** the accessible summaries do not provide a complete account of continuing salary, project payments, studio costs, or the value of the royal commitment after 1848.

The Campo Santo was his largest conception and greatest practical failure. Its non-realization occupied decades of labor, yet the surviving cartoons became central to his posthumous museum status.

---

## Later Rome and Berlin, 1843–1867

Cornelius worked in Rome in **1843–1844** and **1845–1846**. In **1853**, he effectively relocated there for an extended period, continuing the Campo Santo work.

His later designs were shown internationally:

- Paris, 1844;
- Berlin, 1859;
- Brussels, 1859;
- another French showing in or near the same period.

After the death of Gertruda in 1859, Cornelius married Teresa Giampieri in 1860. He returned to Berlin in **1861**, still hoping that the Campo Santo project might resume. It did not.

He continued drawing full-scale cartoons into old age. A near-contemporary biography says he worked on them until shortly before his death and died quietly and without obvious pain. No reliable medical cause of death was located. Claims of a “peaceful death” derive from commemorative biography rather than a death certificate.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

Cornelius was not an entrepreneur in the modern sense. No companies, patents, industrial investments, or inventions are documented.

His economic structure consisted mainly of:

- academy salaries;
- royal commissions;
- court titles;
- honoraria for designs;
- patron-financed projects;
- sales or commissions for paintings and portraits;
- reproduction of designs through engravers and publishers;
- the labor of academy pupils and studio assistants;
- state acquisition of cartoons.

### Institutions

- Düsseldorf Academy;
- Munich Academy of Fine Arts;
- Bavarian royal building and art administrations;
- Prussian Ministry of Culture;
- Prussian court under Frederick William IV;
- Casa Bartholdy;
- Villa Massimo;
- Glyptothek, Munich;
- Alte Pinakothek, Munich;
- Ludwigskirche, Munich;
- Altes Museum, Berlin;
- planned Berlin Cathedral and Campo Santo;
- St Nicholas’s Church, Potsdam;
- Schwerin princely crypt;
- academies and learned societies across Europe;
- Order Pour le Mérite;
- Freies Deutsches Hochstift.

### Property and wealth

The early Roman household experienced significant hardship. In maturity, Cornelius possessed a secure court-supported professional status and could maintain residences or long stays in Munich, Berlin, and Rome. He died highly honored, but no reliable net-worth figure, estate inventory, real-estate portfolio, investment structure, or evidence of unusual wealth was found.

The state’s acquisition of his cartoons should not be confused with proof that Cornelius personally amassed their later institutional value.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Academic classicism

His earliest phase combined Düsseldorf academic classicism, Raphael, Rubens, antiquity, and miscellaneous historical subjects.

### German Romantic historicism

The *Faust* and Nibelungen cycles marked a turn toward Dürer, sixteenth-century German graphics, medieval literature, and a national cultural program.

### Nazarene religious reform

In Rome, Cornelius joined the effort to renew Christian art through communal living, early-Renaissance models, and fresco. He shared the Nazarenes’ rejection of academic routine but gradually separated from their more exclusivist Roman Catholic tendencies.

### Monumental public art

By 1814 he argued that painting should function as public moral education rather than private decoration. His preferred medium became fresco or the full-scale cartoon for a fresco.

### Nationalism

The nationalism in his 1813 correspondence was anti-Napoleonic, cultural, Christian, and institutional. It sought a unified German art before political unification. Later Bavarian and Prussian commissions placed this vocabulary in royal and dynastic settings.

There is no evidence that Cornelius participated in electoral politics, revolutionary organizations, or political administration. His politics operated chiefly through art, education, royal patronage, religious symbolism, and historical subject matter.

### Confessional position

Cornelius remained Catholic but did not follow Overbeck into an increasingly Roman-centered, sectarian art. His Berlin Campo Santo designs served a Protestant monarch and blended confessional traditions. Historians have variously described this as ecumenical, court-directed, Romantic-Christian, or politically conservative.

### Philosophy of artistic authorship

Cornelius gave primacy to the **idea**, composition, and linear design. He accepted collective execution by pupils and considered the cartoon capable of embodying the work’s essential conception. This explains both his influence and the recurring criticism that the final color and surface were inferior to his drawings.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Family and formative figures

- **Aloys Cornelius:** father and first artistic guide.
- **Lambert Cornelius:** older brother; academy inspector and family supporter.
- **Johann Peter Langer:** academy teacher; reportedly doubted Cornelius’s talent.
- **Ferdinand Franz Wallraf:** secured an important early church commission.
- **Sulpiz and Melchior Boisserée:** transmitted enthusiasm for medieval and Early Netherlandish art.
- **Friedrich Wilmans:** Frankfurt publisher, host, collector, and facilitator.
- **Ferdinand Wenner:** publisher of the *Faust* prints.

### Friends and collaborators

- **Johann Christian Xeller:** close friend and traveling companion to Rome.

## Controversies, Accusations, and Adversarial Findings

### 1. Delegation and attribution

**Documented fact:** Cornelius delegated extensive mural execution to pupils.

**Criticism:** Contemporaries and later historians argued that assistants of unequal quality produced inconsistent results, especially in the Glyptothek and Ludwigskirche.

**Cornelian position:** The artist’s decisive contribution was the invention, compositional design, and cartoon; collective execution was compatible with Renaissance workshop practice.

**Assessment:** This is a genuine authorship and quality-control controversy. It is not evidence of fraud. Nineteenth-century monumental art routinely employed workshops, but catalogue entries should distinguish design from physical execution.

### 2. Technical weaknesses

Pecht’s 1876 biography delivered a sustained hostile technical assessment:

- insufficient study from nature;
- weak anatomy and foreshortening;
- rigid contours;
- poor modeling;
- inadequate chiaroscuro;
- harsh or disunited color;
- inability to translate powerful conception into convincing painted bodies.

Even admirers often considered the drawings and cartoons superior to the frescoes. Modern museum presentations similarly emphasize his linear imagination and the autonomous importance of the cartoons.

These are **documented critical judgments**, not factual misconduct.

### 3. State artist and court ideology

Cornelius worked for Bavarian and Prussian monarchs, decorated dynastic histories, taught in royal academies, and spent decades designing a Hohenzollern burial complex.

The Berlin Nationalgalerie later displayed more than seventy cartoons in rooms effectively constituting a Cornelius “personal museum.” Museum historian Birgit Verwiebe describes this as the elevation of Cornelius into a **Staatskünstler**, with his oeuvre officially presented as the culmination of nineteenth-century art.

This is a well-supported institutional interpretation. It does not imply that every Cornelius composition was dictated by government. His conflicts with Ludwig demonstrate that he asserted considerable artistic autonomy.

### 4. Nationalism and anti-French sentiment

**Documented fact:** His 1813 correspondence expressed cultural nationalism during the struggle against Napoleon.

A centenary article characterized this as “hatred of everything foreign.” That wording belongs to nationalist commemoration and is not a neutral summary of his entire career. Cornelius’s enduring reliance on Italian art, long residence in Rome, Italian marriages, French honor, and international exhibitions complicate any claim of consistent xenophobia.

### 5. Confessional reaction and “ultramontanism”

Nazarene religious art was attacked by liberal and modernist critics as politically reactionary or “ultramontane.” Pecht distinguished Cornelius from Overbeck and claimed Cornelius resisted Romanizing tendencies.

**Documented fact:** Cornelius was Catholic, joined the Nazarenes, and pursued Christian public art.

**Interpretive dispute:** Whether this constituted political reaction, confessional revival, ecumenical Romanticism, or a theory of public culture depends on the period and project. The Berlin designs in particular combined Catholic and Protestant sources.

### 6. Conflict with Ludwig I

The dispute is documented in commissions, administrative decisions, and correspondence. Later stories—such as Cornelius being barred by a doorkeeper while Ludwig inspected the *Last Judgment* with Gärtner—should remain labeled **reported anecdotes** unless corroborated from court records.

Neither evidence of bribery nor financial corruption was found. The conflict concerned power over state-funded art.

### 7. Gender and representation

Period criticism observed that Cornelius had difficulty representing female beauty and rendered women and children with hard, aged, or masculinized anatomy. This was an aesthetic criticism of his drawing style, not an allegation about personal conduct.

No reliable evidence of sexual scandal, abuse, addiction, or domestic violence was located.

### 8. Criminal, legal, and financial allegations

The dedicated searches combined Cornelius’s name with scandal, allegations, lawsuit, fraud, corruption, conspiracy, criticism, and recent dates.

**Finding:** No significant criminal case, lawsuit, fraud allegation, corruption inquiry, financial scandal, or conspiracy narrative concerning the historical painter emerged. Results instead returned:

- the Ludwig I patronage dispute;
- criticism of workshop delegation;
- attacks on Nazarene aesthetics;
- debate over monarchic and Christian-national ideology;
- confusion with unrelated people named Peter Cornelius.

Absence of search results is not absolute proof of absence, but there is no evidentiary basis for adding a criminal or financial scandal to the biography.

### 9. Most recent two years

Searches for 2025–2026 controversy produced updated museum records, collection pages, and older scholarly materials newly digitized or indexed—not a new substantive accusation against Cornelius. The recent record concerns preservation, cataloguing, and reassessment of his cartoons.

## Myths, Legends, Hagiography, and Hostile Mythology

### “Founder of modern German national art”

**Story:** Cornelius single-handedly founded a new German art and restored monumental painting.

**Circulation:** Mid- and late-nineteenth-century biographies, commemorative speeches, the Nationalgalerie display, and the Düsseldorf monument.

**Evidence:** He unquestionably exerted major institutional influence, trained important painters, and promoted fresco. The revival was nevertheless collective, involving Overbeck, Schadow, Veit, Schnorr von Carolsfeld, patrons, architects, pupils, and royal administrations.

**Reputational effect:** It elevated him to heroic national-master status and obscured collaborative authorship.

### “The new Raphael” or “Raphael” alias

**Story:** Cornelius was a modern German Raphael or used “Raphael” as a name.

**Evidence:** Raphael was a declared model, and authority data list “Raphael” as a pseudonym. No consulted primary document establishes systematic use of it as a signature or secret identity.

**Label:** Catalogue claim or honorific association; unverified as a genuine pseudonym.

### The starving inspired Nazarene

**Story:** Cornelius and his companions lived in saintly poverty while restoring Christian art.

**Evidence:** Real financial hardship is reported, especially after the 1814 marriage. Later biographies shaped hardship into a hagiographic narrative of sacrifice.

**Effect:** Supported the image of spiritually pure artists opposed to commercial modernity.

### Rejected genius versus philistine monarch

**Story:** Ludwig I repeatedly humiliated a superior genius because he did not understand the Ludwigskirche frescoes.

**Evidence:** The disputes and Ludwig’s dissatisfaction are documented. The complete record also shows that Ludwig employed, promoted, ennobled, and funded Cornelius over many years, while raising defensible concerns about execution and color.

**Effect:** Later biographies cast Cornelius as an autonomous artist resisting arbitrary power; royal histories emphasize the patron’s indispensable role.

### “Largest Last Judgment since or larger than Michelangelo’s”

**Story:** Cornelius’s Ludwigskirche *Last Judgment* surpassed Michelangelo’s in physical size.

**Evidence:** Institutional accounts put it at about 1,500 square feet and describe it as among the world’s largest frescoes. Precise comparisons depend on what surfaces and dimensions are counted.

**Label:** Plausible size comparison, often rhetorically amplified.

### Artist who “foresaw German national greatness”

**Story:** His art prophetically announced later German political unification or national power.

**Origin:** Nationalist nineteenth-century criticism, especially post-1871.

**Evidence:** His letters document early cultural nationalism; no evidence shows literal prophecy of the German Empire.

**Effect:** Bound his reputation to imperial-era national teleology, contributing to his later rejection when that framework lost authority.

### The forgotten master

**Story:** Modernism erased Cornelius solely through fashion or prejudice.

**Evidence:** Hugo von Tschudi’s Nationalgalerie policy and the 1906 Century Exhibition did remove Cornelius from the canonical center as Impressionism and individual brushwork became dominant criteria. Yet technical weaknesses, loss of murals, changing religious culture, and the failure of the Campo Santo also contributed.

**Label:** Partly documented institutional history, partly restorationist mythology.

## Works by Peter von Cornelius

Cornelius was principally a visual artist, not a conventional book author. Many publications under his name reproduce drawings engraved by others. Authorship must therefore be divided among original design, engraving, workshop execution, and later editorial publication.

### Major independent paintings and early works

- *Die vierzehn Nothelfer* / *The Fourteen Holy Helpers* (1804).
- *Odysseus bei Polyphem* (1803), competition design.
- *Das Meeresgeschlecht* (1804), competition design.
- *Herakles in der Unterwelt* (1804), competition design.
- Portrait of Georg Teichmann (c. 1806).
- Portrait of Theodor Glasmacher (1808–1809).
- *Minerva lehrt die Malerei* / *Minerva Teaches Painting* (1809).
- Six mythological decorations for the Schmidt house, Frankfurt (1810; lost).
- *Madonna* (1810–1811).
- *Heilige Familie* / *Holy Family* (1811).
- Portrait of Carolina Grossi (1813–1814).
- *Das Gleichnis von den klugen und törichten Jungfrauen* / *The Wise and Foolish Virgins* (1813–1816; unfinished).
- *Grablegung Christi* / *Entombment of Christ*, several versions or designs (c. 1815–1819).
- *Die drei Marien am Grabe* / *The Three Marys at the Tomb* (1815–1822).
- *Christus in der Vorhölle* / *Christ in Limbo* (1843).
- *Selig sind, die da hungert und dürstet nach Gerechtigkeit* (1851).
- *Pietà* (1856).
- *Hagen versenkt den Hort der Nibelungen* (1859).

### Published illustration cycles

#### *Bilder zu Goethe’s Faust*

- Original drawings: principally 1810–1815.
- First engraved edition: Ferdinand Wenner, Frankfurt am Main, 1816.
- Engraver: Ferdinand Ruscheweyh.
- Dedicated to Goethe.
- Second edition: 1825, adding *Osterspaziergang*, engraved by Julius Thaeter.
- Authorship: Cornelius designed the images; the printed plates were executed by named engravers.

#### *Aventiure von den Nibelungen*

- Drawings: principally 1812–1817.
- Six engraved plates published by Reimer, Berlin, 1817.
- Title page completed later and published in 1821.
- Engravers included Johann Heinrich Lips, Heinrich Ritter, Samuel Amsler, Karl Barth, or collaborators identified in catalogue records.
- Dedicated to Barthold Georg Niebuhr.
- Authorship: original visual conception by Cornelius; printed realization by engravers.

#### *Le Paradis du Dante*

- Full title: *Le Paradis du Dante. Avec texte explicatif, dessiné au trait par Pierre de Cornelius*.
- Published in Leipzig, 1830.
- Derived from the Villa Massimo Dante designs.
- Authorship: Cornelius’s designs reproduced in outline; explanatory text and reproductive production require edition-specific attribution.

#### *Entwürfe zu den kunstgeschichtlichen Fresken in den Loggien der Königlichen Pinakothek zu München*

- Designs produced c. 1827–1840.
- Posthumous print publication: Leipzig, Dürr, 1875.
- Genuine designs by Cornelius; publication is a posthumous reproductive compilation.

### Fresco and mural cycles

#### St Quirin, Neuss

- Grisaille decorations of Evangelists, Apostles, and virtues, 1807–1808.
- Partly destroyed by 1859; painted over in 1865.
- Preparatory drawings survive.

#### Casa Bartholdy, Rome

- *Joseph deutet die Träume des Pharao*.
- *Joseph gibt sich seinen Brüdern zu erkennen*.
- 1816–1817.
- Executed by Cornelius as part of a collaborative eight-scene cycle.

#### Villa Massimo, Rome

- Dante designs and cartoons, c. 1817–1818.
- Incomplete contribution; other artists continued the decorative program.

#### Glyptothek, Munich

- *Die Götter Griechenlands* / *The Gods of Greece* and Trojan cycle.
- Designed and executed with pupils, 1818/1819–1830.
- Original frescoes did not survive the post-Second World War reconstruction.
- Forty-two of the original forty-eight Glyptothek cartoons survive, according to the Berlin museum.

#### Munich Hofgarten arcades

- Bavarian/Wittelsbach history cycle.
- Designs by Cornelius; extensive execution by pupils and collaborators.
- Political wording adjusted through correspondence with Ludwig I.

#### Odeon, Munich

- Three major mythological ceiling compositions.
- Design/supervisory authorship must be distinguished from workshop execution.

#### Alte Pinakothek loggias

- Twenty-five-bay history of art.
- Designs by Cornelius, 1827–1840.
- Painted principally by Clemens von Zimmermann, 1831–1840, without the degree of Cornelius’s control he desired.

#### Ludwigskirche, Munich

- Designs and cartoons, 1830–1836.
- Frescoes, 1836–1840.
- *Last Judgment* substantially or wholly executed by Cornelius.
- Other sections extensively painted by pupils.

#### Schwerin princely crypt windows

- Seven stained-glass cartoons, 1843–1844.

#### St Nicholas, Potsdam

- Medallions of Old Testament prophets, 1850.

#### Berlin Campo Santo

- Extensive drawings and cartoons, 1841–1867.
- Never translated into the intended architectural fresco cycle.
- Engraved selections published by Julius Thaeter in 1846–1848.
- Major cartoons listed above.

### Letters and theoretical writings

Cornelius did not publish a large systematic treatise. His intellectual record survives principally through letters:

- letter to Joseph Görres, 3 November 1814, advocating revival of fresco and public monumental art;
- letters to Ferdinand Wenner;
- correspondence with Goethe;
- extensive correspondence with Ludwig I;
- letters to the Prussian Ministry of Culture, 1818–1858;
- letters to artists, pupils, patrons, publishers, and administrators.

Important editorial collections include:

- Ernst Förster’s two-volume biography, which drew on Cornelius’s manuscripts, oral testimony, and friends’ recollections;
- *Künstlerbriefe über Kunst* (Dresden, 1926), which printed the Görres letter;
- archival holdings identified through the Deutsche Digitale Bibliothek, Kalliope, Bavarian State Library, state archives, and museum archives.

Individual edited letters are authentic documentary material, but nineteenth-century biographies sometimes abridged, modernized, or framed them polemically. Edition criticism is required before quotation.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate commemoration

Cornelius died as an internationally honored artist. The projected purchase of his remaining cartoons was already under discussion. After his death, the Prussian state acquired the major Glyptothek, Ludwigskirche, and Campo Santo cartoons.

When the Nationalgalerie opened in **1876**, more than seventy Cornelius cartoons occupied two large central halls. Their scale influenced the architecture of the display. A gilded colossal bust reinforced his position as the official culmination of German art.

Düsseldorf made him an honorary citizen in 1862 and unveiled Donndorf’s monument in 1879. The monument names Düsseldorf, Rome, Munich, and Berlin, turning his movements into a four-city national narrative.

Munich’s **Corneliusbrücke** commemorates both the painter and his composer nephew.

### Decline after 1900

By the early twentieth century, Cornelius’s standing fell sharply. Nationalgalerie director **Hugo von Tschudi**, appointed in 1896, considered his Renaissance-derived academic art superseded. The 1906 Century Exhibition presented nineteenth-century development as a movement toward realism and Impressionism.

Under criteria emphasizing:

- direct observation;
- painterly individuality;
- natural light;
- color;
- visible brushwork;
- autonomous easel painting,

Cornelius’s intellectual programs, workshop execution, hard contours, and court commissions appeared obsolete. His works went into storage.

### Loss and destruction

His reputation suffered materially because:

- the St Quirin frescoes were painted over;
- the Glyptothek murals were lost or not preserved during postwar reconstruction;
- the *Four Horsemen* cartoon disappeared in 1945;
- the Campo Santo was never built;
- cartoons of enormous size are difficult to display.

His oeuvre therefore often survives in preparatory or reproductive form rather than in the intended architectural environment.

### Reassessment

Late twentieth- and twenty-first-century scholarship has reconsidered Cornelius as:

- an institutional reformer;
- an architect of collective artistic labor;
- a theorist of public art;
- a major Romantic draftsman;
- a mediator between Catholic revival, Protestant monarchy, and cultural nationalism;
- a precursor of state-sponsored museum and mural programs;
- an artist whose cartoons may be more successful than his frescoes.

The Berlin restoration and exhibition of the Glyptothek cartoons in 2005 was an important act of recovery. Forty-two of forty-eight cartoons survived, some measuring up to approximately 4.5 by 8.5 meters.

His lasting influence lies less in a continuously popular body of paintings than in:

- the revival of monumental mural programs;
- the Düsseldorf and Munich academy systems;
- the status of the full-scale cartoon as an autonomous work;
- nineteenth-century visualizations of Goethe, the Nibelungen, mythology, Christian history, and apocalypse;
- the international transfer of fresco theory, including to Britain.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Shared nationality, period, city, religion, or general intellectual climate is not treated as a connection.

### Johann Friedrich Overbeck — documented fact

Cornelius joined the Roman Lukasbund around Overbeck after arriving in Rome on 14 October 1811. They collaborated on the Casa Bartholdy Joseph cycle in 1816–1817 and participated in the Villa Massimo program. Cornelius later diverged from Overbeck’s increasingly Roman Catholic artistic position, although the timing and severity of a personal “break” are interpreted differently by biographers.

### Johann Wolfgang von Goethe — documented fact

Cornelius entered the Weimar prize competitions organized by Goethe and Heinrich Meyer in 1803–1805, without winning. He later corresponded with Goethe about his *Faust* illustrations, explicitly discussed his use of Dürer’s Maximilian prayer-book drawings, and dedicated the 1816 engraved *Faust* cycle to Goethe. Goethe admired aspects of his talent but reportedly warned against confusing poetic and pictorial modes.

### Friedrich Schiller — documented fact, limited

Cornelius traveled to Stuttgart in spring 1839 for the unveiling of the Schiller monument. This establishes participation in Schiller commemoration, not a personal relationship: Schiller had died in 1805, and no meeting is documented.

### Napoleon Bonaparte — documented influence, no meeting

The Wars of Liberation against Napoleon directly shaped Cornelius’s 1813 nationalist correspondence and the cultural-political setting of his Nibelungen work. No personal meeting is documented. The connection is an explicitly documented historical influence, not interpersonal contact.

### Dante Alighieri — documented artistic influence

Cornelius designed scenes from Dante’s *Divine Comedy* for the Villa Massimo and later published or authorized reproductions associated with *Le Paradis du Dante*. This is a direct artistic engagement across centuries, not a personal connection.

### Michelangelo di Lodovico Buonarroti Simoni — documented artistic influence

Cornelius consciously modeled monumental forms and the Ludwigskirche *Last Judgment* in dialogue or rivalry with Michelangelo. Institutional sources expressly describe this engagement.

### Johann Wolfgang von Goethe and Albrecht Dürer roster caveat

Goethe is on the Atlas roster; Dürer is not. Dürer was nevertheless one of Cornelius’s most important acknowledged sources and is retained elsewhere in the dossier but not counted as a roster connection.

### Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling — reported shared institutional-intellectual network

Schelling and Cornelius both belonged to the intellectual and educational constellation assembled under Ludwig I in Munich. Sources concerning the university and court identify Schelling, Görres, and Cornelius within Ludwig’s Christian-oriented cultural program. No specific personal meeting or correspondence between Schelling and Cornelius was established in the consulted material. This is therefore a **reported shared institutional network**, not a documented friendship.

### Alexander von Humboldt — documentary catalogue connection, scope unresolved

The Edition Humboldt Digital maintains a person record for Cornelius and records his letters to the Prussian Ministry of Culture from 1818 to 1858. This establishes relevance to the Humboldt-era Prussian institutional archive, but the material consulted did not establish direct correspondence or a meeting between Cornelius and Alexander von Humboldt. No stronger connection is claimed.

### Absence finding

No adequately sourced personal or intellectual crossing was found with the overwhelming majority of the 896-name roster. In particular, mere coexistence within Romanticism, nineteenth-century Germany, Catholic culture, royal patronage, or later art history was not treated as proof of contact.

## Chronology

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 23 Sep. 1783 | Born in Düsseldorf to Aloys Cornelius and Anna Helena Cosse/Corsten. |
| 1798 | Entered the Düsseldorf Academy. |
| 1800 | Father died; family finances became precarious. |
| 1803 | Entered Goethe and Meyer’s Weimar competition with *Odysseus bei Polyphem*. |
| 1804 | Produced *Das Meeresgeschlecht*, *Herakles in der Unterwelt*, and *The Fourteen Holy Helpers*. |
| c. 1803–1806 | Encountered the Boisserée circle and medieval-art revival. |
| 1807–1808 | Painted grisaille decorations at St Quirin, Neuss. |
| 1809 | Mother died; Cornelius moved to Frankfurt via Koblenz. |
| 1810 | Decorated the Schmidt house in Frankfurt; began major *Faust* work. |
| 1811 | Completed several *Faust* drawings; left with Xeller for Italy; reached Rome on 14 October and joined the Lukasbund. |
| 1812–1817 | Developed Nibelungen illustrations. |
| 1813 | Anti-Napoleonic political excitement; considered returning to join the war. |
| 1814 | Married Carolina Grossi; wrote programmatic letter to Joseph Görres on 3 November. |
| 1816–1817 | Painted two Casa Bartholdy Joseph frescoes with Overbeck, Schadow, and Veit. |
| 1816 | First engraved *Faust* cycle published. |
| 1816 onward | Friendship and patronage relationship with Barthold Georg Niebuhr. |
| 1817 | Six Nibelungen prints published; began Villa Massimo Dante designs. |
| Jan.–Apr. 1818 | Met Crown Prince Ludwig; received Glyptothek commission. |
| 1818–1821 | Appointed/called to Düsseldorf Academy directorship; exact formal date varies. |
| Autumn 1819 | Began sustained work in Munich while retaining Düsseldorf duties. |
| 1820 | Glyptothek cartoons exhibited in Berlin. |
| 1821 | Nibelungen title page published. |
| 1823/1825 | Ennobled in Bavaria; date varies by source. |
| Dec. 1824 | Ended Düsseldorf contract. |
| 1825 | Settled fully in Munich; became Munich Academy director. |
| 1826 | Hall of the Gods substantially completed. |
| 25 Dec. 1827 | Informed that he would not control final execution of Pinakothek loggia frescoes. |
| 1827–1840 | Produced Alte Pinakothek loggia designs. |
| 1830 | Glyptothek vestibule and Trojan Hall completed; returned to Rome for Ludwigskirche designs. |
| 1830 | *Le Paradis du Dante* published. |
| 1830–1836 | Prepared Ludwigskirche designs and cartoons. |
| 1832 or 1834 | Death of first wife Carolina; one account also records a daughter’s death. |
| 1835 | Married Gertruda Fervatini/Ferretini in Rome. |
| Winter 1835–1836 | Severe illness, reportedly from overwork. |
| 1836–1840 | Ludwigskirche frescoes executed. |
| Autumn 1838 | Traveled to Paris. |
| 1839 | Attended Schiller-monument unveiling in Stuttgart; received Legion of Honour. |
| 1840 | Completed *Last Judgment*; final rupture with Ludwig I. |
| 15 Aug. 1840 | Offered services to Frederick William IV. |
| Apr. 1841 | Arrived in Berlin with title of director. |
| 1841 onward | Began Campo Santo designs. |
| 1842 | Joined and became vice-chancellor of the peace class of the Pour le Mérite; fresco consultations appeared in British Parliamentary Papers. |
| 1843 | Returned from London; completed *Christ in Limbo* for Raczyński. |
| 1843–1844 | Rome stay; designed Schwerin crypt windows. |
| 1844 | Campo Santo designs exhibited in Paris; received honorary doctorate at Münster. |
| 1845–1846 | Further Rome stay. |
| 1846 | *Four Horsemen of the Apocalypse* cartoon; initial Campo Santo engravings. |
| 1848 | Revolution stopped the Berlin cathedral and Campo Santo building project. |
| 1849 | *Arrival of the New Jerusalem*. |
| 1850 | Prophet medallions for St Nicholas, Potsdam. |
| 1851 | Raczyński commission, *Blessed Are Those Who Hunger and Thirst for Righteousness*. |
| 1852–1853 | *Fall of Babylon*. |
| 1853 | Relocated for an extended period to Rome. |
| 1859 | Campo Santo designs exhibited in Berlin and Brussels; second wife Gertruda died; appointed master of the Freies Deutsches Hochstift. |
| 1860 | Married Teresa Giampieri. |
| 1861 | Returned to Berlin. |
| 1862 | Made honorary citizen of Düsseldorf and chancellor of the Pour le Mérite peace class. |
| 1863–1865 | Produced *Christ after the Resurrection*. |
| 1865–1866 | Produced *Outpouring of the Holy Spirit*. |
| 6 Mar. 1867 | Died in Berlin. |
| 1874 | Ernst Förster’s two-volume memorial biography published. |
| 1876 | More than seventy Cornelius cartoons displayed prominently at opening of Berlin Nationalgalerie. |
| 1879 | Donndorf’s Düsseldorf Cornelius monument unveiled. |
| c. 1900–1906 | Reputation sharply declined under modernist and Impressionist-oriented museum history. |
| 1945 | *Four Horsemen* cartoon became missing; other works suffered wartime loss. |
| 2005 | Major Glyptothek-cartoon exhibition in Munich and Berlin aided reassessment. |
| 2023–2026 | Continued institutional scholarship, digitization, conservation, and catalogue revision. |
