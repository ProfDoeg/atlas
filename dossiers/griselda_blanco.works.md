## Works by Griselda Blanco Restrepo

### Designed objects

- **Early 1970s:** Cocaine-concealment lingerie. Bras and other garments were manufactured with hidden compartments for couriers; this was a functional product line serving an illicit trafficking scheme, not fashion authored as an artistic collection. The surviving accounts credit Blanco’s design or direction but do not identify individual garments, fixed titles, or precise production dates ([UNODC](https://sherloc.unodc.org/cld/uploads/pdf/Issue_Paper_Organized_Crime_and_Gender_1.pdf), p. 45).

### Criminal enterprises as projects

- **1972–1975:** New York cocaine-importation and distribution ring. This was a collaborative criminal infrastructure rather than a singly authored obra: testimony described Blanco as a participant, while an indictment named her alongside thirty-seven others; her subsequent conviction established conspiracy but does not make every operation of the ring her individual creation ([United States v. Blanco](https://sherloc.unodc.org/cld/case-law-doc/drugcrimetype/usa/1988/united_states_v_blanco.html)).

- **Late 1970s–1985:** Multi-city cocaine-trafficking organization. Blanco organized shipments supplying New York, Miami, San Francisco, and Los Angeles and directed money laundering, couriers, maritime transport, and distribution; sources variously call this her organization, empire, or part of the Medellín network, so its boundaries and sole authorship remain unsettled ([InSight Crime](https://insightcrime.org/colombia-organized-crime-news/griselda-blanco-alias-la-madrina/); [DEA retrospective](https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/Digitization/147278NCJRS.pdf), pp. 54–55).

- **By 1984:** *Pistoleros*. This was the name reported for the enforcement group assembled around Blanco’s Miami organization. It is an organized instrument of coercion, not an artwork or text, and the DEA’s retrospective account supplies lurid initiation claims without a membership catalogue or independent attribution for every act ([DEA retrospective](https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/Digitization/147278NCJRS.pdf), p. 55).

### Violent acts that resist classification as works

- **11 July 1979:** *Dadeland Massacre*. The killings of trafficker Germán Jiménez Panesso and Juan Carlos Hernández at a Miami shopping centre have been traced or attributed to gunmen associated with Blanco, but the language of attribution varies and the cited sources do not establish that she personally carried them out. This was a criminal event—not an obra—and its inclusion records alleged commissioning rather than artistic authorship ([DEA retrospective](https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/Digitization/147278NCJRS.pdf), p. 55; [InSight Crime](https://insightcrime.org/colombia-organized-crime-news/griselda-blanco-alias-la-madrina/)).

- **1982:** Three ordered killings prosecuted together. Authorities charged Blanco with ordering three Miami-area murders, including the killing of a young child struck while the intended target was his father; she later pleaded guilty in 1998. These deaths emphatically resist being called “works”: they are recorded here only because ordering them was an intentional production of consequences, and broader claims that she masterminded scores or hundreds of killings remain allegations rather than a securely enumerable corpus ([UNODC](https://sherloc.unodc.org/cld/uploads/pdf/Issue_Paper_Organized_Crime_and_Gender_1.pdf), p. 45; [InSight Crime](https://insightcrime.org/colombia-organized-crime-news/griselda-blanco-alias-la-madrina/)).

### No attributable textual or media corpus

- No scholarly bibliography, catalogue raisonné, collected-works edition, or discography exists for Blanco, and the consulted legal, governmental, and organized-crime sources identify no books, manifestos, protocols, songs, films, or other authored texts by her. Films, television series, documentaries, biographies, music, and merchandise bearing her name are representations made by other people and therefore are not included as her works.
