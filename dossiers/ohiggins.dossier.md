# Bernardo O'Higgins Riquelme: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier reconstructs the documented life, public career, private relationships, property, writings, controversies, mythology, and posthumous uses of Bernardo O’Higgins Riquelme.

Evidence labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary records, correspondence, official documents, or convergent archival evidence.
- **Reported fact:** stated in reputable secondary scholarship but not independently verified here from the underlying document.
- **Disputed claim:** supported by competing interpretations or incomplete evidence.
- **Allegation or theory:** an accusation or explanatory theory without proof sufficient to establish it as fact.
- **Rumor or myth:** folklore, propaganda, dramatization, or a traditional story unsupported by adequate documentary evidence.

Dates follow the Gregorian calendar. Nineteenth-century titles retain their original spelling where bibliographically useful.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father: Ambrosio O’Higgins

**Documented fact.** Bernardo’s father was **Ambrosio O’Higgins**, born in Ireland, probably at Ballynary near Ballinrobe, County Mayo, around 1720. He entered Spanish imperial service, became a military engineer and colonial administrator, served as governor of Chile from 1788 to 1796, and as viceroy of Peru from 1796 until 1801.

Ambrosio never married Isabel Riquelme and did not publicly establish a conventional household with Bernardo. Colonial law and the political demands of his office made formal acknowledgment of an illegitimate son delicate. Nevertheless, he financed the boy’s upbringing and education through intermediaries and ultimately left him a substantial inheritance.

Bernardo apparently saw Ambrosio only rarely. A meeting in 1788, when the elder O’Higgins was traveling to assume the governorship, may have occurred without the child being told that the visitor was his father. The psychological importance attributed to this remote father—admiration, resentment, and a desire to “merit” the paternal name—is partly inferential, although Bernardo’s later insistence on the surname and his correspondence support the importance of the relationship.

### Mother: Isabel Riquelme y Meza

**Documented fact.** His mother was **María Isabel Riquelme y Meza**, a young member of a locally established Chillán family. She remained close to Bernardo throughout his adult life. His surviving letters address her with marked affection and report military and household affairs. She eventually accompanied him into exile in Peru and died there in 1839.

### Maternal ancestry and household

The Riquelme family belonged to the provincial landholding and municipal society of the Chillán area. Bernardo’s maternal grandparents are usually identified as **Simón Riquelme de la Barrera** and **María Mercedes de Meza y Ulloa**. Precise genealogies differ in spelling and in the inclusion of collateral lines.

His early household was not a conventional two-parent household. He passed among caregivers selected or financed by Ambrosio:

- his mother and maternal relatives near Chillán;
- the household of the Portuguese-born merchant **Juan Albano Pereira Márquez** and his wife **Bartolina de la Cruz y Bahamonde** at Talca;
- Franciscan educators at Chillán;
- commercial guardians in Lima, Cádiz, and London.

### Siblings and close kin

**Documented fact.** Bernardo had at least one maternal half-sister, **Rosa Rodríguez Riquelme**, usually called **Rosita**, daughter of Isabel Riquelme and Félix Rodríguez. Rosita became one of his closest domestic companions in Peru and was named residuary heir in his last testament.

Some family reconstructions also identify **Nieves Rodríguez Riquelme** as a maternal half-sister. The relative obscurity of women and children born outside formal marriages has produced discrepancies in later genealogies. Her existence is treated in modern literary and family-history discussions but is less securely documented in the readily accessible institutional record than Rosa’s.

**Petronila Riquelme**, a younger female relative who accompanied the household to Peru, is variously described in popular genealogies as Bernardo’s daughter, niece, goddaughter, or dependent. Claims that she was his biological daughter are not established by conclusive documentary evidence. She should not be listed as a proven child.

### Partner: María del Rosario Puga y Vidaurre

**Documented core; details disputed.** O’Higgins did not marry. His best-documented intimate relationship was with **María del Rosario Melchora Puga y Vidaurre**, a woman from a prominent Concepción family who was separated from her husband, José Antonio Pérez Cotapos.

Their relationship existed during O’Higgins’s government, approximately 1817–1820, although its precise beginning and ending are uncertain. Because Rosario was legally married and O’Higgins held supreme office, the relationship was politically and socially sensitive.

Their separation produced a struggle over their son. Pedro Demetrio was placed with Bernardo’s mother, Isabel Riquelme. Rosario protested and attempted to recover the child. A reported angry exchange between O’Higgins and Rosario, in which she blamed his political circle for the deaths of the Carrera brothers and Manuel Rodríguez, comes through later reconstructions; its exact wording cannot be treated as a verbatim contemporary record. [La Tercera](https://www.latercera.com/culto/2023/11/17/cuando-bernardo-ohiggins-termino-su-relacion-con-rosario-puga-en-pleno-gobierno-y-con-un-hijo-en-disputa/?outputType=base-amp-type)

### Child: Pedro Demetrio O’Higgins Puga

**Documented fact.** Their son, **Pedro Demetrio**, was born at Concepción on **29 June 1818**. In childhood he appeared under the surnames Puga and Jara, a method of concealing or managing his parentage. He later used **Pedro Demetrio O’Higgins**.

Pedro accompanied the O’Higgins household to Peru in 1823. Bernardo educated and provided for him, treated him as his son in private life, and secured his inheritance. Pedro later became a landowner and politician in Peru and died in Lima on 24 November 1868.

The son’s changing surnames have become a central element in cultural representations of illegitimacy in the O’Higgins family. The claim that Bernardo first acknowledged him only in his final will is too simple: the paternal relationship was practically recognized earlier, even if public and legal nomenclature evolved.

### Other alleged children and relationships

Stories periodically assign other children or lovers to O’Higgins. No additional biological child is established with evidence comparable to that for Pedro Demetrio. Claims concerning Petronila Riquelme should remain classified as **disputed genealogy**, not fact.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1778–1794

### Birth under conditions of secrecy

Bernardo was born while Ambrosio O’Higgins was a rising Spanish colonial official and Isabel Riquelme was unmarried. The boy’s delayed baptism and movement among guardians reflect the need to shield both parents from scandal and possible professional consequences.

The epithet **“huacho Riquelme”** later used by political opponents combined two injuries: exclusion from legitimate paternal descent and relegation to his mother’s provincial surname. In colonial Chile, illegitimacy could obstruct inheritance, office, clerical status, and elite marriage. Bernardo nevertheless received privileges unavailable to most children in the same legal category because Ambrosio financed his care.

### Talca, 1783–1788

From about 1783 he lived in Talca with Juan Albano Pereira and Bartolina de la Cruz. The Albanos were commercially connected and personally trusted by Ambrosio. Their son, **Casimiro Albano**, later a priest and patriot, became a childhood companion.

This household offered Bernardo a socially respectable upbringing while maintaining distance between him and his biological parents.

### Franciscan education at Chillán, approximately 1788–1790

He was sent to the Franciscan establishment usually called the **Colegio de Naturales de Chillán**, created principally for sons of Mapuche leaders. He studied basic literacy, Christian doctrine, and probably Latin rudiments.

The importance of his Mapuche classmates is sometimes exaggerated into a fully formed childhood doctrine of indigenous liberation. What can be stated is that he encountered indigenous elite students directly. Later republican language—his adoption of “Lautaro” for a revolutionary society and his 1819 declaration that indigenous inhabitants should be called Chileans—gave this experience retrospective importance.

## Education and Formation, 1790–1802

### Lima, 1790–1794

In September 1790 Bernardo was sent to Lima and placed under the protection of the Irish merchant **Juan Ignacio Blake**. He attended the **Colegio del Príncipe**, an institution intended for sons of Inca nobles, and later the **Real Convictorio de San Carlos**.

A modern scholarly interpretation argues that the Colegio del Príncipe exposed him to descendants of Andean nobility and memories of the Túpac Amaru rebellion. This is plausible context, but direct evidence that particular classmates transmitted a defined revolutionary ideology to him is limited. [“Un acercamiento a la construcción del pensamiento político americanista…”](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0717-88322021000200017&script=sci_arttext)

### Cádiz, 1794–1795

At sixteen he sailed to Cádiz and entered the guardianship of **Nicolás de la Cruz y Bahamonde**, later Count of Maule, a Chilean-born merchant with extensive transatlantic interests. Relations with de la Cruz were difficult. The guardian regarded Bernardo as an expensive obligation and sent him onward to England.

### England, 1795–1799

Bernardo lived and studied at Richmond, Surrey, at the Catholic school of **Timothy Eeles**. The curriculum advertised for the school included Latin, Greek, English, French, writing, arithmetic, bookkeeping, geography, history, mathematics, and the use of globes.

His London financial agents were the watchmakers or manufacturers **Spencer and Perkins**. Correspondence records disputes over money. In one surviving letter Bernardo indignantly rejected allegations that he had sold his books or squandered his allowance.

He traveled in southern England and spent time at Margate and Falmouth. His English education furnished commercial knowledge, languages, and exposure to Britain’s constitutional and maritime power. [National Maritime Museum Cornwall](https://nmmc.co.uk/2022/03/bernardo-ohiggins/)

### Francisco de Miranda

**Documented fact, with disputed institutional details.** In London Bernardo met the Venezuelan revolutionary **Francisco de Miranda**. O’Higgins later wrote that he had been converted to the doctrines of this “intelligent and indefatigable apostle of the cause of South America.” Miranda became his principal political mentor.

Miranda instructed young Spanish Americans in clandestine organization, continental cooperation, and the practical requirements of independence. A set of instructions attributed to Miranda urged O’Higgins to cultivate prudence, learn military science, and conceal revolutionary aims until conditions were favorable.

The often-repeated story that Miranda formally initiated O’Higgins into a regular Masonic lodge is not established. Evidence supports participation in a secret independence network variously called the **Great American Reunion**, **Caballeros Racionales**, or later **Lautaro Lodge**. Scholarship distinguishes these political societies from regular Freemasonry. [“La actitud religiosa de don Bernardo O’Higgins”](https://revistahistoria.uc.cl/index.php/rhis/article/view/15505)

### Return through Portugal and Cádiz, 1799–1802

O’Higgins left Falmouth in April 1799, spent several weeks in Lisbon, and reached Cádiz in June. He became involved with Spanish American independence sympathizers.

In 1800 he attempted to return to Chile aboard the frigate *Confianza*. The voyage was interrupted by British naval action and illness. He returned to Cádiz, reportedly suffering yellow fever. His revolutionary associations became known after the informer Pedro José Caro disclosed Miranda’s plans to Spanish officials.

Ambrosio, then viceroy of Peru, initially reacted by cutting or threatening to cut financial support. Shortly afterward Ambrosio died in Lima on 18 March 1801. His testament left Bernardo the estate of **San José de Las Canteras**, livestock, and other property. Bernardo sailed for South America in 1801 and reached Chile in 1802.

## Landowner and Provincial Political Formation, 1802–1810

### San José de Las Canteras

O’Higgins took possession of Las Canteras near Quilleco, approximately forty kilometers from Los Ángeles. It comprised extensive land, livestock, buildings, and dependent labor.

He operated it as a commercial agricultural estate producing:

- wheat and barley;
- flour;
- cattle and horses;
- milk products;
- charqui;
- hides;
- tallow and grease.

He developed expertise in stock breeding, irrigation, accounts, and regional commerce. He also organized and trained the local militia. This was not a period of idle aristocratic retirement: the estate was his economic base and the setting of his first sustained public leadership.

### Political contacts

O’Higgins became associated with reformers around **Juan Martínez de Rozas**, a lawyer and political leader from Concepción. He maintained clandestine independence commitments while publicly functioning inside colonial society.

When Napoleon’s invasion of Spain displaced Ferdinand VII and produced a legitimacy crisis, O’Higgins supported the movement for a Chilean governing junta. The initial public argument was government in the captive king’s name; more radical participants, including O’Higgins, contemplated independence.

## Chronological Life History

## The First Congress and the Patria Vieja, 1810–1813

### Entry into national politics

The Santiago cabildo of 18 September 1810 established the First Government Junta. In 1811 O’Higgins was elected deputy for **Los Ángeles** to the First National Congress.

He belonged to the more radical or “exalted” faction and supported measures moving beyond temporary autonomous administration. His legislative presence was not dominant, but it placed him inside the emerging revolutionary elite.

### Relationship with José Miguel Carrera

**Documented fact.** The return of **José Miguel Carrera** from Spain transformed Chilean politics. Carrera used military force in the coups of 4 September and 15 November 1811 and eventually dominated the government.

O’Higgins initially cooperated with Carrera’s regime and accepted military responsibilities. Their rivalry later involved:

- competing regional and family networks;
- disagreement over military command;
- Carrera’s concentration of power;
- distrust between Santiago and Concepción factions;
- later claims over responsibility for the disaster at Rancagua;
- radically opposed exile networks.

Simple descriptions of Carrera as liberal and O’Higgins as conservative are historically unstable. Both used military power and both sponsored republican reforms, while their supporters constructed mutually hostile traditions.

### War begins, 1813

A royalist expedition under Brigadier Antonio Pareja landed in southern Chile in 1813. O’Higgins began the campaign as a militia colonel.

He participated in the actions at Linares, Yerbas Buenas, San Carlos, Chillán, El Roble, Membrillar, and Quechereguas. His estate at Las Canteras lay inside the war zone and was repeatedly damaged or lost.

At the **Battle of El Roble**, 17 October 1813, O’Higgins rallied disordered patriot troops. The traditional cry attributed to him—“¡O vivir con honor o morir con gloria!”—became one of his signature legends. That he took a conspicuous part in restoring the line is well attested; the exact wording of the cry is not securely recoverable.

In November 1813 the governing junta replaced Carrera with O’Higgins as commander-in-chief. Carrera resisted the change, cementing the rivalry.

## Civil Conflict, Rancagua, and Exile, 1814

### Treaty of Lircay

O’Higgins and Juan Mackenna negotiated with the royalist commander Gabino Gaínza, producing the **Treaty of Lircay** on 3 May 1814. It nominally recognized Ferdinand VII and promised withdrawal of royalist forces. Neither side fully trusted or carried out the settlement.

Carrera returned to power in another coup. Patriot factions fought each other at **Tres Acequias** on 26 August 1814, where Carrera’s forces defeated O’Higgins. News of a new royalist invasion under Mariano Osorio compelled a temporary reconciliation.

### Disaster of Rancagua, 1–2 October 1814

O’Higgins occupied Rancagua in an effort to block Osorio’s route to Santiago. Royalist forces surrounded the town. After two days, with ammunition, food, and water nearly exhausted, O’Higgins led a mounted breakout.

Carrera, holding the reserve outside the town, did not provide the reinforcement O’Higgins expected. Whether Carrera could have intervened effectively remains disputed. O’Higginist narratives call his inaction betrayal; Carrerist accounts argue that O’Higgins disobeyed the strategic plan and placed the army in an indefensible position. [Chilean Army account](https://ejercito.cl/efemerides/efemerides/MjA1)

The defeat ended the Patria Vieja. Patriots and civilian refugees crossed the Andes to Mendoza.

### Mendoza and subordination to San Martín

In Mendoza, Governor **José de San Martín** disarmed or marginalized Carrera’s faction and recognized O’Higgins as the principal Chilean military partner. The two developed a durable alliance rooted in their shared continental plan.

O’Higgins accepted a subordinate command in the **Army of the Andes**. During 1815–1816 he helped recruit exiles, gather intelligence, plan routes, and prepare the invasion. His surviving Mendoza diary and correspondence document this work.

## Liberation Campaign, 1817–1818

### Crossing of the Andes

In January–February 1817 the Army of the Andes crossed through multiple passes. O’Higgins commanded a division on the principal route with San Martín.

### Battle of Chacabuco, 12 February 1817

O’Higgins’s division attacked before San Martín’s planned coordinated movement had fully developed. The premature assault created danger but contributed to the eventual patriot victory when the other division under Miguel Estanislao Soler entered effectively.

A Santiago cabildo offered supreme authority first to San Martín, who refused, and then to O’Higgins. O’Higgins became **Supreme Director of Chile on 16 February 1817**.

### Early government and continuing war

O’Higgins combined executive government with military campaigning. Royalist forces retained the south, Talcahuano, Valdivia, and Chiloé.

His assault on Talcahuano in December 1817 failed with heavy losses. This was a significant military failure, though later official narratives sometimes pass quickly over it.

### Declaration of Independence

The declaration was dated at Concepción on **1 January 1818** and solemnly proclaimed on **12 February 1818**, the first anniversary of Chacabuco.

Authorship requires distinction:

- O’Higgins ordered and signed the political act.
- **Miguel Zañartu** drafted or revised the Act.
- **Bernardo de Vera y Pintado** wrote the accompanying manifesto.
- The surviving ceremonial document is therefore not a solely authored O’Higgins text. [Memoria Chilena](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/602/w3-article-96486.html)

### Cancha Rayada and Maipú

At the **Second Battle of Cancha Rayada**, 19 March 1818, royalists surprised and scattered the patriot army. O’Higgins received a serious gunshot wound to his right arm.

Despite the wound, he organized forces in Santiago and went to the battlefield of **Maipú** on 5 April. San Martín commanded the decisive battle. O’Higgins arrived with reinforcements after the main victory was effectively secured. Their meeting became the celebrated **Abrazo de Maipú**.

The statement “O’Higgins won Maipú” is therefore imprecise. His government, recruits, and military leadership formed part of the victory, but San Martín exercised battlefield command. [Chilean Army account](https://ejercito.cl/efemerides/efemerides/NQ%3D%3D)

## Supreme Director of Chile, 1817–1823

### Constitutional position

O’Higgins governed under emergency conditions, initially with extensive executive discretion. The **Constitution of 1818** created a five-member Senate whose members were selected by the Director. The **Constitution of 1822** provided a more elaborate institutional structure but permitted continued executive dominance and was widely interpreted as enabling O’Higgins to prolong his rule.

Contemporaries and historians have called the regime a dictatorship. This is accurate in the older constitutional sense of concentrated, extraordinary executive authority, but should not be confused without qualification with twentieth-century military dictatorships.

### State construction

Documented initiatives under his administration included:

- reopening the **National Institute**;
- reopening the **National Library**;
- founding the **Military Academy**, predecessor of the modern Military School;
- creating a naval officer training institution;
- organizing the **Chilean Navy**;
- establishing or reorganizing ministries, treasury offices, courts, and provincial administration;
- creating the **Legion of Merit**;
- sponsoring primary schools;
- supporting vaccination and public-health measures;
- establishing the General Cemetery in Santiago;
- authorizing a cemetery for non-Catholic foreigners at Valparaíso;
- developing the Alameda de las Delicias;
- creating a public market and other urban works;
- founding or reorganizing towns including San Bernardo, Vicuña, and La Unión;
- developing diplomatic missions to Britain, the United States, and the Holy See;
- establishing national ceremonies and symbols.

### Equality legislation and limits

O’Higgins ordered the removal of noble coats of arms from public display and abolished titles of nobility, describing them as remains of feudal inequality. He attempted to end **mayorazgos**, or entailed estates, but resistance from property-holding elites and the Senate prevented full implementation.

In 1819 his government declared that indigenous inhabitants should not be designated by legally degrading colonial categories but should be regarded as Chilean citizens. This was a formal equality measure. It did not abolish frontier warfare, land inequality, or the state’s instrumental use of indigenous symbols. [Study of constitutional equality](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0716-54552024000100295&script=sci_arttext)

### Religion

O’Higgins remained publicly Catholic. His government supported the Church while seeking republican control over patronage and ecclesiastical administration. The cemetery for Protestants and diplomatic approaches to the Holy See reveal pragmatic toleration rather than a clear rejection of Catholic establishment.

Claims that he sought to destroy Catholicism arise largely from later confessional polemic and the disputed identification of the Lautaro society with Freemasonry.

### The navy and the expedition to Peru

O’Higgins and his ministers made the creation of a fleet a strategic priority. Ships were purchased or captured, and **Thomas Alexander Cochrane** was appointed naval commander.

The navy under Cochrane captured Valdivia in February 1820. It also blockaded royalist ports and made possible the **Liberating Expedition of Peru**, which departed Valparaíso on 20 August 1820. San Martín held overall command of the expeditionary army.

Financing the army and navy placed severe pressure on Chile’s small treasury. The government used forced loans, requisitions, confiscated royalist property, customs revenue, and foreign credit. Soldiers and sailors frequently went unpaid.

O’Higgins’s support for the expedition was both ideological and strategic: an independent Chile remained insecure while the Spanish viceregal center survived in Peru.

### Relations with Cochrane

O’Higgins and Cochrane shared the objective of maritime independence but their relationship deteriorated over pay, prize money, political authority, and San Martín’s conduct in Peru. Cochrane accused governments of withholding contractual compensation; Chilean officials regarded him as insubordinate and financially demanding.

These conflicts generated claims of fraud on both sides, but they were principally disputes over prize law, arrears, and divided command rather than adjudicated criminal fraud by O’Higgins.

### War to the Death

After central Chile was secured, warfare continued in the south against royalist regulars, guerrillas, Indigenous allies, and armed bands. The conflict known as the **Guerra a Muerte** involved executions, confiscation, destroyed settlements, forced recruitment, and violence against civilians by multiple forces.

O’Higgins’s government authorized harsh emergency measures and delegated authority to commanders including Ramón Freire. The historical record does not support depicting this phase as a clean conventional war.

## Political Violence and Major Controversies

## The Carrera brothers

### Juan José and Luis Carrera, 1818

The brothers attempted to cross from Argentina into Chile amid plans directed against the O’Higgins–San Martín political order. They were arrested, tried in Mendoza, and executed on **8 April 1818**.

**Documented fact:** the executions were carried out by authorities in Mendoza, not physically by O’Higgins.

**Documented complication:** O’Higgins and San Martín’s allies regarded the brothers as conspirators and security threats. Bernardo de Monteagudo and the Lautaro network operated in the political environment of the prosecutions.

**Evidence against a simple murder-order claim:** O’Higgins sent a letter dated 11 April asking that indulgence be shown to the brothers, apparently unaware that they had already been executed.

**Hostile interpretation:** Carrera supporters alleged that this appeal was theatrical and that O’Higgins already knew the outcome. No conclusive surviving order signed by O’Higgins directing their execution has been established.

Verdict: **O’Higgins’s political system benefited from and helped create the conditions for their elimination; his personal issuance of an execution order is unproved.**

### José Miguel Carrera, 1821

José Miguel Carrera became a participant in the Argentine civil wars while attempting to acquire the means to overthrow O’Higgins. Captured after defeat, he was executed at Mendoza on **4 September 1821**.

The execution was an Argentine governmental act. O’Higgins remained Carrera’s political enemy and benefited from his death, but direct authorship of the sentence has not been demonstrated.

## Arrest and killing of Manuel Rodríguez

**Documented facts:**

- Rodríguez had served as San Martín’s intelligence agent during the Reconquest.
- He became a popular military and political figure.
- He opposed the O’Higgins government and had close Carrerist associations.
- O’Higgins’s authorities arrested him in 1818.
- Soldiers escorting him toward Quillota killed him near Tiltil on **26 May 1818**.
- The official claim that he was shot while trying to escape was immediately doubted and is generally rejected by historians.

**Responsibility:**

The direct killers were military escorts; Lieutenant **Antonio Navarro** is normally identified as the officer most directly implicated. Later testimony and investigations connected the operation to senior officers and the Lautaro political network.

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century authors have assigned planning responsibility to **Bernardo de Monteagudo**, Rudecindo Alvarado, San Martín, O’Higgins, or the Lautaro society collectively. No surviving written order from O’Higgins has conclusively proved that he commanded the killing.

The available evidence establishes state custody, political motive, and killing by officers serving the regime. It does not conclusively establish O’Higgins’s personal order. His government did not conduct a convincing contemporary prosecution of those responsible. [Memoria Chilena: Manuel Rodríguez](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/602/w3-article-740.html)

## The regime’s coercive practices

The O’Higgins government employed:

- preventive detention and exile;
- censorship and pressure on political printing;
- confiscation of royalist property;
- compulsory loans and requisitions;
- military tribunals;
- surveillance of Carrerist and royalist networks;
- executive appointment of legislators;
- harsh counterinsurgency in the south.

These were documented practices of a revolutionary wartime government. Whether the regime should be categorized primarily as an emancipatory republic, military dictatorship, or transitional emergency state has changed with historians’ political frameworks.

No evidence establishes that O’Higgins operated a private financial corruption system comparable to a modern kleptocracy. Complaints focused more often on arbitrary government, favorites, military expenditure, and his minister **José Antonio Rodríguez Aldea**, accused by opponents of manipulation and corruption.

## Fall from Power, 1822–1823

### Accumulating opposition

O’Higgins lost support among several overlapping groups:

- landholding elites threatened by reforms and compulsory finance;
- Carrerists;
- advocates of a representative constitution;
- provincial leaders resentful of Santiago;
- unpaid officers and soldiers;
- opponents of Rodríguez Aldea;
- southern commanders around **Ramón Freire**;
- clergy and Catholics suspicious of religious policy;
- merchants affected by fiscal instability.

The 1822 earthquake and economic difficulties further weakened the regime.

### Constitution of 1822

The new constitution was promulgated on 30 October 1822. Although it created representative institutions, its provisions were widely read as arranging O’Higgins’s continuation in office. The constitution did not settle the legitimacy crisis.

### Abdication, 28 January 1823

Provincial opposition and the threat of civil war reached Santiago. At an open meeting O’Higgins was confronted by civic and military leaders. He surrendered authority on **28 January 1823**.

The famous scene in which he bares his chest and asks his accusers to take revenge is based on later recollection and patriotic dramatization. The fact of a tense personal defense and voluntary surrender is secure; the polished theatrical language is less certain.

O’Higgins hoped to remain in Chile and return to private property, but political pressure and fear of prosecution made departure necessary.

## Exile and Life in Peru, 1823–1842

### Journey

O’Higgins embarked from Valparaíso aboard the British corvette **Fly** on **17 July 1823** and sailed on 19 July. His household included Isabel Riquelme, Rosa Rodríguez Riquelme, Pedro Demetrio, Petronila Riquelme, and domestic dependents. They reached Callao in August.

He never again saw Chile.

### Reception and property grants

Peru honored him as Grand Marshal and recognized his contribution to the liberation expedition. The Peruvian state granted him the haciendas **Montalván** and **Cuiva** in the Cañete valley. The grant originated in recognition made while San Martín governed Peru, although possession and title involved administrative complications.

### Agriculture, sugar, and slavery

O’Higgins turned Montalván into an increasingly productive sugar and aguardiente estate. Reported annual revenues reached approximately **10,000–12,000 pesos** in successful periods, though war, debt, management expenses, and market fluctuations reduced disposable income.

He invested in:

- sugar-cane production;
- a mechanized mill or trapiche;
- livestock and estate buildings;
- coastal transport through Cerro Azul;
- commercial relations in Lima and Cañete.

A new mill installed in 1838 was a source of pride in his correspondence.

**Documented fact:** Montalván relied on enslaved African-descended labor. Scholarship reports roughly fifty enslaved workers during O’Higgins’s ownership. Records also document purchase, management, punishment, family life, illness, and sale within this labor system. O’Higgins therefore became a slave-owning plantation proprietor in Peru, notwithstanding the egalitarian language of his Chilean government. [Study of O’Higgins in Peru](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0717-88322021000200053&script=sci_arttext)

No evidence suggests he invented the machinery, held patents, or originated sugar-processing technology. He was an adopter and investor.

### Domestic life

His mother, half-sister Rosa, son Pedro Demetrio, and other dependents formed his household. Summers were sometimes spent near Cerro Azul. O’Higgins divided his time between Montalván and Lima.

He remained unmarried. References in estate correspondence to a “wife” require caution; terminology could refer to a household companion or editorial interpretation and does not establish a legal marriage.

### Continuing political activity

Exile did not mean political inactivity. O’Higgins:

- corresponded extensively with San Martín and Chilean associates;
- watched for a possible restoration by Chilean O’Higginists;
- advised political and military figures;
- defended continental cooperation;
- promoted Chilean settlement of the Strait of Magellan;
- advocated control of southern territory and Pacific strategy;
- followed Peruvian and Chilean revolutions;
- offered service during regional wars.

O’Higginist officers remained an identifiable Chilean political current after his fall, though attempts to restore him failed.

### Simón Bolívar

O’Higgins admired Bolívar’s victories and continental role and met him in Peru. He offered his services during the final campaigns against Spanish power. Bolívar treated him ceremonially as an important liberator but did not give him central operational command. Their connection was respectful rather than an intimate partnership.

### Peru–Bolivian Confederation war

During the conflict between Chile and the Peru–Bolivian Confederation, O’Higgins supported the Chilean restoration expedition commanded by **Manuel Bulnes** and furnished local knowledge, contacts, and advice. He accompanied or assisted Bulnes’s movement in Peru despite his age and illness.

This placed him against Andrés de Santa Cruz’s Confederation and in alignment with exiled Peruvian opponents of Santa Cruz.

### Possibility of return

Chilean attitudes gradually softened. President Bulnes authorized or encouraged O’Higgins’s return. In 1842 O’Higgins prepared to travel to Chile, but declining health prevented departure.

## Wealth, Property, and Financial Relationships

### Las Canteras

His original fortune derived principally from Ambrosio O’Higgins’s inheritance. Las Canteras gave him regional standing, livestock, agricultural revenue, and a militia base. Warfare devastated or separated him from the estate. After 1823, recovery and disposal of Chilean property became a prolonged financial matter.

### Government finances

O’Higgins did not clearly separate personal reputation from state credit, but no strong evidence shows personal enrichment from the Chilean treasury. His government’s major financial liabilities came from:

- maintaining two armies;
- creating a fleet;
- supporting the expedition to Peru;
- foreign arms and ship purchases;
- unpaid salaries and prize claims;
- public works;
- a narrow tax base damaged by war.

The first major Chilean foreign loan was negotiated in London during the final phase of his government. Its proceeds and charges became a national financial controversy, but available evidence does not show that O’Higgins personally embezzled the loan.

### Montalván and Cuiva

The Peruvian estates gave him an economically comfortable but not uniformly liquid existence. Profit estimates must be distinguished from net personal wealth. Estate debts, management costs, war losses, and investment in machinery were substantial.

His participation in plantation slavery is an essential component of the financial structure: enslaved people were treated legally and economically as estate property and labor assets.

### Testament

On **8 October 1842**, gravely ill, O’Higgins executed a final testament before the Lima notary Jerónimo Villafuerte. He made provisions for Pedro Demetrio and other dependents and designated Rosa as residuary heir after specified and confidential obligations.

The existence of “secret instructions” has encouraged speculation about unknown children or relationships. Secret testamentary commissions were not extraordinary, and their presence alone does not prove those claims.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Americanism

O’Higgins moved from colonial reform to complete independence. His mature political geography was continental: Chile’s freedom required the destruction of royalist power in Peru, maritime command of the Pacific, cooperation among new American states, and control of strategic southern passages.

### Republicanism and executive power

He opposed hereditary nobility and publicly endorsed legal equality, education, representative institutions, and civic merit. Simultaneously, he believed wartime independence required centralized executive command.

This combination produced the core contradiction of his government:

- republican social symbolism and administrative modernization;
- limited electoral legitimacy and extensive military-executive control.

### Indigenous policy and symbolism

The choice of “Lautaro” for the secret independence organization and official language calling Indigenous people citizens reflected an Americanist appropriation of Mapuche resistance.

This did not create Indigenous self-government. State symbolism could celebrate Lautaro while republican institutions continued territorial ambitions in Mapuche country. The government’s 1819 arms also removed the Indigenous figures previously used in patriotic iconography. [“Arauco, matriz retórica de Chile”](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0719-09482013000200009&script=sci_arttext)

### Religious position and the Masonic question

O’Higgins’s Catholic identity is supported by upbringing, correspondence, public worship, patronage, and death observances.

The Lautaro organization used secrecy, oaths, grades, and lodge terminology resembling Masonry. Institutional historians differ:

- Masonic traditions retrospectively claim O’Higgins as a brother.
- Other scholars regard Lautaro as a political secret society influenced by Masonic forms but not a regular lodge.
- Documentary proof of his initiation in a recognized Masonic lodge has not been established.

The safest classification is: **documented member of a secret independence society; formal Freemasonry disputed.**

## Illnesses, Injuries, and Death

O’Higgins experienced several documented medical episodes:

- severe illness, usually identified as yellow fever, during his attempted return from Europe around 1800;
- battlefield injuries during the independence campaigns;
- a serious gunshot wound to the right arm at Cancha Rayada in March 1818;
- recurrent illness in later life, including cardiac and respiratory symptoms;
- increasing weakness in 1842.

He did not have a documented addiction. Later psychological interpretations emphasizing paternal rejection, illegitimacy, or compensatory ambition are speculative and should not be treated as diagnoses.

In October 1842 his condition deteriorated in Lima. He received religious attention, completed testamentary arrangements, and died on **24 October 1842**, aged sixty-four. Accounts commonly describe a cardiac condition, sometimes hypertrophy of the heart, complicated by illness affecting breathing. Without a modern clinical record, the precise biomedical cause cannot be stated with certainty.

He reportedly spoke or repeatedly invoked “Magallanes” near death. This has been interpreted as evidence of his continuing concern that Chile occupy the Strait of Magellan. The episode is plausible and early enough to have historical weight, but its exact final-word form belongs partly to patriotic deathbed tradition.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The father of Chilean independence”

**Story:** O’Higgins single-handedly liberated Chile and founded the republic.

**Origin and circulation:** State education, military commemoration, monuments, biographies, and official anniversaries, especially from the late nineteenth century onward.

**Evidence:** He was indispensable as military commander, head of state, organizer of the navy, and sponsor of the Peruvian expedition. He did not act alone. Carrera, San Martín, Rodríguez, Cochrane, civilian officials, ordinary soldiers, women, Indigenous allies, and Afro-descended troops were also essential.

**Reputational effect:** Established O’Higgins as the principal Chilean “Father of the Fatherland,” while subordinating rival traditions.

### “El huacho Riquelme”

**Story:** The illegitimate provincial son overcame paternal abandonment to earn the O’Higgins name.

**Evidence:** His irregular birth, separate upbringing, delayed baptism, and early use of Riquelme are documented. Claims that every political decision derived from a psychological need for paternal approval are retrospective speculation.

**Effect:** Used both as abuse and as a redemptive national story of merit overcoming birth.

### The El Roble cry

**Story:** O’Higgins rallied his soldiers with “¡O vivir con honor o morir con gloria!”

**Evidence:** His conspicuous rallying action is well supported; verbatim wording depends on later accounts.

**Effect:** Became a military motto and a core scene of heroic biography.

### The embrace of Maipú

**Story:** The wounded O’Higgins arrived at the moment of victory and San Martín greeted him with words honoring the “savior of Chile.”

**Evidence:** Their post-battle meeting is documented and was witnessed. Exact dialogue and idealized staging were polished by art and commemoration.

**Effect:** Symbolized Chilean–Argentine fraternity and obscured tensions in the campaign.

### Personal order to kill the Carreras and Rodríguez

**Story:** O’Higgins deliberately ordered all principal rivals murdered.

**Circulation:** Carrerist polemic, opposition literature, nineteenth-century histories, theater, film, and popular political memory.

**Evidence:** His regime arrested Rodríguez; Rodríguez was killed by regime officers; O’Higgins’s allies benefited from the Carrera executions. No conclusive signed personal execution order has been produced. The regime’s institutional responsibility for Rodríguez’s custody and failure to punish the killers is much stronger than proof of O’Higgins’s personal command.

**Effect:** Created the enduring hostile image of O’Higgins as a jealous dictator who eliminated more charismatic patriots.

### Formal Freemason and lodge conspiracy

**Story:** Miranda initiated O’Higgins into Freemasonry; a transnational Masonic lodge secretly directed South American independence and political killings.

**Evidence:** Secret political societies and oath-bound coordination are documented. Formal regular Masonic status is contested. Claims of a unified international Masonic command exceed the evidence.

**Effect:** Masonic institutions incorporated him into their genealogy; Catholic and conspiracy literature used the same association to portray independence as an anti-religious plot.

### Abdication with bared chest

**Story:** Confronting hostile citizens, O’Higgins tore open his clothing and offered his chest to those accusing him.

**Evidence:** He personally defended his record and surrendered power rather than begin civil war. The set-piece dialogue derives from later narration and should be considered partly dramatized.

**Effect:** Converted political defeat into a final act of self-sacrificing patriotism.

### “Magallanes” as his final word

**Story:** His final thought was Chilean occupation of the Strait of Magellan.

**Evidence:** O’Higgins had repeatedly advocated southern expansion and occupation of the strait. The precise deathbed word is not independently certain.

**Effect:** Later linked him to Chile’s 1843 occupation of the Strait and to a prophetic national-territorial vision.

### Poor exile versus wealthy planter

Two opposed myths circulate:

1. O’Higgins died abandoned and impoverished.
2. O’Higgins became an extremely wealthy Peruvian magnate.

The evidence indicates a middle position. Montalván generated substantial agricultural income and supported a comfortable household, but estate expenses, war damage, debts, and investment meant he was not uniformly cash-rich. He owned enslaved laborers and productive property and was not destitute.

## Works by Bernardo O’Higgins

O’Higgins was not principally a book author. His corpus consists of correspondence, diaries, proclamations, manifestos, military orders, speeches, decrees, state papers, and texts issued under his authority. Authorship is often collective: secretaries and ministers drafted documents that he approved or signed.

### Contemporary or near-contemporary texts

- **Letters from England and Cádiz**, 1790s. Genuine private correspondence, including disputes with Spencer and Perkins and letters to guardians.
- **Letters to Francisco de Miranda and independence associates**, late 1790s–early 1800s. Genuine correspondence where preserved; some associated revolutionary instructions were Miranda’s, not O’Higgins’s.
- **Letters to Isabel Riquelme**, especially 1812–1813. Genuine private correspondence.
- **Military correspondence and orders**, 1813–1814. Genuine or officially authenticated operational documents.
- **Diario llevado en Mendoza por O’Higgins**, 3–31 July 1816. A genuine working diary kept while San Martín was in Córdoba.
- **Correspondence with José de San Martín**, 1814–1842. Genuine letters, later collected and edited.
- **Manifiesto del Capitán General de Ejército Don Bernardo O’Higgins a los pueblos que dirige**, 1820. Official political manifesto published under his name.
- **Carta de Bernardo O’Higgins al pueblo de Chile**. Political letter or address; editions differ in date and presentation.
- **Proclama del Excelentísimo Señor Brigadier General don Bernardo O’Higgins, Supremo Director del Estado de Chile, Gefe de la Legion de Mérito**. Official proclamation.
- **El Director Supremo del Estado de Chile…**, numerous decrees and proclamations beginning in February 1817. These are official acts under his authority; drafting was often ministerial.
- **Al Ejército de Lima**, 30 January 1818. Official proclamation.
- **El Supremo Director de Chile a los españoles liberales**, 1 February 1818. Official proclamation.
- **Acta de la Independencia de Chile**, dated 1 January and proclaimed 12 February 1818. Signed and authorized by O’Higgins but principally drafted by Miguel Zañartu; not sole authorship.
- **Manifiesto de la Independencia**, 1818. Authorized by O’Higgins but written principally by Bernardo de Vera y Pintado.
- **Constitution of 1818**. Promulgated and signed under O’Higgins; produced by a commission, not personally authored.
- **Constitution of 1822**. Promulgated under O’Higgins; collective constitutional authorship.
- **Government decrees concerning titles of nobility, coats of arms, Indigenous citizenship, education, cemeteries, urban works, the military, and navy**, 1817–1823. Official acts; drafting attribution varies.
- **Correspondence from Peru**, 1823–1842, including letters concerning Montalván, Pedro Demetrio, San Martín, Chilean politics, the Confederation war, and Magellan.
- **Testaments**, including an 1829 testament and final testament of 8 October 1842. Genuine legal documents.

### Major edited collections

- **Epistolario de D. Bernardo O’Higgins, Capitán General y Director Supremo de Chile, Gran Mariscal del Perú y Brigadier de las Provincias Unidas del Río de la Plata, 1798–1823**, edited by Ernesto de la Cruz, 1916. Posthumous scholarly collection.
- **Archivo de don Bernardo O’Higgins**, Santiago, beginning 1946. Multi-volume documentary archive containing correspondence, government papers, newspapers, military organization, operations, confiscations, diplomacy, and Cochrane papers. It is an edited archive, not a work authored as a whole by O’Higgins.
- **Pensamientos del Libertador Capitán General Bernardo O’Higgins**, various editions. Posthumously selected quotations; not an original standalone book.
- **Correspondencia O’Higgins–San Martín**, in several edited editions. Genuine letters, posthumously arranged.
- **Proclamas y discursos de Bernardo O’Higgins**, various institutional compilations. Mixed official authorship and editorial selection.

Memoria Chilena provides digitized access to letters, proclamations, the Mendoza diary, images, and the documentary archive. [O’Higgins author collection](https://www.memoriachilena.gob.cl/602/w3-propertyvalue-166018.html)

## Films, Television, Theater, Interviews, and Archival Material

### Screen representations

- **El húsar de la muerte** (1925), directed by and starring Pedro Sienna. Silent film about Manuel Rodríguez. O’Higgins appears within the political world that arrests Rodríguez; the film contributed to the popular heroic Rodríguez/authoritarian-government contrast.
- **Héroes: O’Higgins, vivir para merecer su nombre** (Canal 13, 2007), directed by Ricardo Larraín, with Julio Milostich as O’Higgins. Covers Cancha Rayada through abdication with childhood and exile flashbacks. It dramatizes Rosario Puga, illegitimacy, executive authoritarianism, and political killings. Dialogue and private scenes are dramatized, not documentary evidence.
- **Carrera, príncipe de los caminos** (2007) and **Rodríguez, hijo de la rebeldía** (2007), companion telefilms in *Héroes*. Both construct O’Higgins from the viewpoint of his rivals and include invented confrontations.
- **El niño rojo** (2014), directed by Ricardo Larraín. Film centered on the childhood and formation of O’Higgins; part of the international *Libertadores* project. It necessarily reconstructs poorly documented private scenes.
- **Algo habrán hecho por la historia de Chile** (television series, 2010). Popular historical dramatization incorporating the independence era and O’Higgins.

### Visual art and monuments

- José Gil de Castro’s portraits of O’Higgins are foundational contemporary or near-contemporary representations.
- Pedro Subercaseaux’s **El abrazo de Maipú** (1908) fixed the canonical visual form of O’Higgins and San Martín’s meeting.
- Numerous equestrian statues, busts, military portraits, stamps, currency images, and school illustrations converted a historically changing appearance into a standardized national icon.

### Archives

- **Archivo de don Bernardo O’Higgins**, Biblioteca Nacional de Chile and Academia Chilena de la Historia.
- **Memoria Chilena**, digitized letters, proclamations, biographies, newspapers, and iconography.
- **Biblioteca del Congreso Nacional**, parliamentary biography, constitutional history, and legislative records.
- **Archivo Nacional de Chile**, governmental and notarial materials from the independence period.
- **Dundonald/Cochrane Papers**, used in later volumes of the O’Higgins archive.
- Peruvian notarial and hacienda records relating to Montalván, Cuiva, slavery, and O’Higgins’s testaments.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Repatriation and state cult

O’Higgins was initially buried in Peru. In January 1869 the Chilean state repatriated his remains aboard a naval squadron including the corvettes *O’Higgins*, *Esmeralda*, and *Chacabuco*, under Manuel Blanco Encalada. He was buried in Santiago’s General Cemetery.

His remains were transferred in 1979 to the **Altar de la Patria**, a monumental civic-military complex associated with the government of Augusto Pinochet. After redesign of central Santiago, they were placed in the restored crypt at the Plaza de la Ciudadanía in 2006.

### Military commemoration

The Chilean Army’s officer academy bears his name, as do military units, naval vessels, decorations, streets, towns, schools, a university, parks, and the Antarctic administrative region **Región del Libertador General Bernardo O’Higgins**.

### Competing political appropriations

O’Higgins’s symbolism has been used by:

- conservatives emphasizing order, military command, Catholicism, and state authority;
- liberals emphasizing abolition of titles, education, constitutional government, and civic equality;
- socialists and communists emphasizing anti-aristocratic measures, Indigenous citizenship, and anti-imperial independence;
- the Pinochet regime, which stressed military hierarchy, sacrifice, and national unity;
- democratic governments, which stressed abdication rather than civil war and republican institution building.

A scholarly study of his reputation from 1970 to 2008 documents appropriation by both left and right rather than exclusive ownership by one political tradition. [“Bernardo O’Higgins entre izquierda y derecha”](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0719-12432013000200005&script=sci_arttext)

### O’Higgins versus Carrera and Rodríguez

Public memory often operates as a three-sided contest:

- O’Higgins as state builder and victorious director;
- Carrera as the charismatic founder of the Patria Vieja;
- Rodríguez as the popular guerrilla destroyed by authority.

These traditions select different episodes and sometimes convert unresolved responsibility into certainty. The rivalry remains active in schoolbooks, monuments, historical novels, television, and political rhetoric.

### Historical reassessment

Modern scholarship has complicated both official hagiography and blanket denunciation by emphasizing:

- the collaborative and transnational character of independence;
- his dependence on San Martín and a broader military network;
- the constitutional ambiguity of emergency executive rule;
- coercion and political killings under his government;
- genuine anti-noble and educational reforms;
- the limits of formal Indigenous equality;
- the fiscal crisis of wartime state building;
- his role as a slave-owning sugar planter in Peru;
- the political construction of the “Father of the Fatherland.”

## Atlas Connections

Only relationships supported by the consulted sources are included. Shared era or geography alone is excluded.

### Francisco de Miranda — documented fact

O’Higgins met Miranda in London in the late 1790s and explicitly acknowledged him as the mentor who converted him to the cause of South American emancipation. Miranda supplied political instruction and connected him to a secret revolutionary network. Claims of formal Masonic initiation are disputed.

### José de San Martín — documented fact

O’Higgins and San Martín worked together from the Mendoza exile of 1814 through the Army of the Andes, Chacabuco, Maipú, and the expedition to Peru. San Martín was the senior strategist and battlefield commander; O’Higgins supplied Chilean political authority, forces, ships, and finance. They remained correspondents and friends in exile.

### Simón Bolívar — documented fact

O’Higgins met Bolívar in Peru and offered service during the final independence campaigns. Bolívar acknowledged his status as a fellow liberator, but their relationship was more ceremonial and political than operationally close.

### Antonio José de Sucre — reported fact

O’Higgins was present in the Peruvian political-military world in which Sucre completed the campaign against Spanish power. Both were honored as high-ranking liberators. The consulted sources do not establish a sustained friendship, extensive correspondence, or joint command; the connection should therefore remain limited to documented participation in the same liberation settlement rather than inflated into collaboration.

### Carlos María de Alvear — reported fact

Alvear and O’Higgins belonged to the broader network of Río de la Plata and Chilean revolutionary secret societies associated with the Caballeros Racionales/Lautaro organization. Direct personal intimacy is not demonstrated by the consulted material.

### Manuel Belgrano — reported fact

Belgrano and O’Higgins belonged to the allied revolutionary movement of the United Provinces and Chile. O’Higgins held the rank of brigadier of the United Provinces, and both were connected to San Martín’s continental strategy. No specific meeting or correspondence was established in the sources consulted for this dossier.

### Napoleon Bonaparte — documented influence, not personal contact

Napoleon’s invasion of Spain and displacement of Ferdinand VII created the imperial legitimacy crisis from which Chilean juntas and independence emerged. O’Higgins had no documented personal meeting with Napoleon. The connection is causal and historical, not interpersonal.

### Augusto Pinochet Ugarte — documented posthumous appropriation

During Pinochet’s government, the 1978 bicentenary of O’Higgins’s birth was commemorated through military ceremonies, portraits, medals, exhibitions, and diplomatic gifts. In 1979 O’Higgins’s remains were transferred to the Altar de la Patria. This was a posthumous symbolic appropriation, not a lifetime relationship.

### Salvador Guillermo Allende Gossens — documented posthumous political use

Before and during Allende’s era, sectors of the Chilean left presented O’Higgins as an anti-oligarchic, anti-feudal, and anti-imperialist precursor. This was a posthumous ideological interpretation. No lifetime connection was possible.

### Pedro Subercaseaux Errázuriz — documented posthumous artistic connection

Subercaseaux painted canonical scenes from O’Higgins’s life, most notably **El abrazo de Maipú** (1908). His image helped establish the standard visual mythology of O’Higgins and San Martín’s battlefield meeting.

### Lautaro — documented symbolic connection

O’Higgins encountered Mapuche history during his Chilean formation and belonged to the revolutionary society named for **Lautaro**, the sixteenth-century Mapuche military leader. The connection was an explicit symbolic invocation, not a personal relationship.

No adequately supported personal connection was found with the overwhelming majority of the remaining atlas roster. Their absence is a finding; contemporaneity, general intellectual resemblance, or later use of O’Higgins’s name is insufficient.

## Chronology

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 20 Aug. 1778 | Born at Chillán, son of Ambrosio O’Higgins and Isabel Riquelme |
| 23 Jan. 1783 | Baptized at Talca |
| 1783–1788 | Raised principally in the household of Juan Albano and Bartolina de la Cruz |
| c. 1788–1790 | Studies with Franciscans at the Colegio de Naturales, Chillán |
| Sept. 1790 | Sent to Lima |
| 1790–1794 | Studies at the Colegio del Príncipe and Convictorio de San Carlos |
| 1794 | Sent to Cádiz under Nicolás de la Cruz |
| 1795–1799 | Studies and lives in England, principally Richmond and London |
| c. 1798–1799 | Meets Francisco de Miranda and joins the independence network |
| Apr.–June 1799 | Travels from Falmouth through Lisbon to Cádiz |
| 1800 | Failed return voyage; illness and political suspicion |
| 18 Mar. 1801 | Ambrosio O’Higgins dies, leaving Bernardo an inheritance |
| 1802 | Returns to Chile and assumes Las Canteras |
| 1802–1810 | Farmer, rancher, merchant, and militia officer near Los Ángeles |
| 18 Sept. 1810 | Chilean First Junta established |
| 1811 | Elected deputy for Los Ángeles to the First National Congress |
| 1813 | Joins war against royalist expedition |
| 17 Oct. 1813 | Rallies patriot forces at El Roble |
| Nov. 1813 | Appointed commander-in-chief in place of Carrera |
| 3 May 1814 | Treaty of Lircay |
| 26 Aug. 1814 | Defeated by Carrera’s forces at Tres Acequias |
| 1–2 Oct. 1814 | Commands defense and breakout at Rancagua |
| Oct. 1814 | Exile across Andes to Mendoza |
| 1815–1816 | Helps San Martín prepare Army of the Andes |
| Jan.–Feb. 1817 | Crosses Andes with liberation army |
| 12 Feb. 1817 | Victory at Chacabuco |
| 16 Feb. 1817 | Becomes Supreme Director |
| Dec. 1817 | Failed assault on Talcahuano |
| 1 Jan. 1818 | Declaration of Independence dated |
| 12 Feb. 1818 | Independence solemnly proclaimed |
| 19 Mar. 1818 | Wounded at Cancha Rayada |
| 5 Apr. 1818 | Maipú victory and celebrated meeting with San Martín |
| 8 Apr. 1818 | Juan José and Luis Carrera executed in Mendoza |
| 26 May 1818 | Manuel Rodríguez killed near Tiltil while in military custody |
| 23 Oct. 1818 | Constitution of 1818 sworn |
| 1818–1819 | Navy, military academy, educational institutions, and republican administration organized |
| 1819 | Noble titles abolished; Indigenous citizenship declaration issued |
| 1819–1822 | War to the Death in southern Chile |
| Feb. 1820 | Cochrane captures Valdivia |
| 20 Aug. 1820 | Liberating Expedition of Peru sails from Valparaíso |
| 4 Sept. 1821 | José Miguel Carrera executed at Mendoza |
| 1822 | Political and fiscal crisis; earthquake; opposition expands |
| 30 Oct. 1822 | Constitution of 1822 promulgated |
| 28 Jan. 1823 | Abdicates as Supreme Director |
| 19 July 1823 | Sails into exile aboard *Fly* |
| Aug. 1823 | Arrives in Peru |
| 1820s–1830s | Develops Montalván and Cuiva; corresponds with American political leaders |
| 1829 | Executes an earlier testament |
| 1838 | Installs improved sugar-milling machinery at Montalván |
| 1838–1839 | Assists Manuel Bulnes during war against the Peru–Bolivian Confederation |
| 1839 | Isabel Riquelme dies in Peru |
| 8 Oct. 1842 | Executes final testament |
| 24 Oct. 1842 | Dies in Lima |
| 11 Jan. 1869 | Repatriated remains arrive in Valparaíso |
| 1869 | Reburied in Santiago |
| 1978 | Bicentenary commemorations conducted throughout Chile |
| 1979 | Remains transferred to the Altar de la Patria |
| 2006 | Coffin reinstalled in the present crypt at Plaza de la Ciudadanía |
