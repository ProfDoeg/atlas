# Siméon Denis Poisson: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 24 August 2026  
**Scope:** Reconstructable life history, institutional career, scientific work, networks, controversies, myths, writings, and legacy  
**Evidence note:** Dates and claims below are tied wherever practical to primary or near-primary records and specialist historical studies. Arago's biography, read to the Académie des sciences in 1850, is indispensable but is also a commemorative institutional portrait written by a friend and colleague. Later anecdotes are identified as such. Genealogical claims unsupported by civil or notarial records are treated cautiously.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Siméon Denis Poisson; nineteenth-century printing also uses **Siméon-Denis Poisson** and **S. D. Poisson**. “Denis” was commonly the identifying forename.
- **Titles and styles:** French mathematician, geometer, mathematical physicist, professor, examiner, academician, university councillor, geometer of the Bureau des longitudes, **baron** by royal grant (a dignity he did not regularize or ordinarily use), and **pair de France** from 1837.
- **Birth:** **21 June 1781**, Pithiviers, then in the Orléanais/Gâtinais and now Loiret, France. A modern secondary webpage gives 27 June, but Arago’s near-contemporary notice and the standard institutional record give 21 June; 21 June is the stronger date.
- **Death:** **25 April 1840**, Sceaux, south of Paris, aged 58.
- **Burial:** Père-Lachaise Cemetery, Paris, division 19. His funerary inscription identifies him as peer of France and member of the Institut.
- **Nationality/citizenship:** French.
- **Primary fields:** analysis, mechanics, celestial mechanics, potential theory, electricity and magnetism, elasticity, hydrodynamics, capillarity, heat, optics, probability, and the administration of mathematical education.
- **Best-known eponyms:** Poisson distribution, Poisson process (later terminology), Poisson equation, Poisson bracket, Poisson kernel/integral, Poisson summation formula, Poisson ratio, Poisson spot, Poisson’s constant and several less universally used formulas and coefficients.
- **Attributed maxim:** “La vie n’est bonne qu’à deux choses : à faire des mathématiques et à les professer” (“Life is good only for two things: doing mathematics and teaching it”). The source is Arago’s posthumous recollection, not a surviving autograph statement.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and social origins

Poisson was the son of **Siméon (or Siméon-François) Poisson** and a woman Arago calls **Mlle Franchetère**; genealogical compilations expand her name as **Edmée/Aimée Marie Francheterre**. The precise spelling varies. Arago says the elder Poisson had served as a common soldier in the Hanoverian campaigns, then acquired a small administrative post at Pithiviers analogous to that of a later justice of the peace. During the Revolution he became president of the district and received the *Journal de l’École polytechnique*, which the young Poisson read.

The frequently repeated story that the father deserted because aristocratic officers mistreated him appears in later biographical tradition. Arago states the service and modest civil office but does not, in the passages consulted, establish desertion. It is therefore best treated as **an unverified biographical elaboration**, not as proven fact.

Arago says Poisson’s elder siblings died in infancy. Online genealogical compilations propose an elder boy, Siméon Denis Marie (1779–c.1780), and a younger sister, Edmée Louise (born 1786). These details are useful leads but require verification against parish or civil registers. His mother was described as delicate in health.

### Marriage and children

In **1817**, Poisson married **Nancy de Bardi**, described in a major biographical dictionary as an orphan born in England to émigré parents. Standard biographies state that they had **four children**. A FamilySearch summary confirms at least one son but is not a substitute for civil acts. The consulted sources do not securely provide a complete, verified list of the children’s names and life dates, so none is invented here.

Marriage changed the organization of his time: biographers describe a domestic life concentrated on family, research, teaching, and official obligations. No credible evidence located in this research indicates extramarital partnerships, scandal, or family estrangement.

### Property and estate evidence

Poisson’s simultaneous public appointments produced what Arago called “grande aisance”—substantial comfort or affluence. A **notarial inventory after death**, opened at Sceaux on 25 April 1840 in the minutes of notary Hippolyte Lejeune, survives in the French archival system. It is the strongest identified route for exact information about household goods, securities, debts, and real property; the online archival notice does not itself enumerate the assets. No evidence was found of private business ventures, patents, speculative companies, inherited estates, or corruption.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1781–1798

Poisson was born into a modest provincial household during the last decade of the Ancien Régime. Because his mother was in fragile health, he was sent to a wet nurse living in an isolated house outside Pithiviers. Arago tells a dramatic survival story: the father visited, found the infant hanging by his swaddling bands from a nail after the nurse had gone out, and concluded that the child’s constitution had been tested by danger. This is a **near-contemporary anecdote transmitted by Arago**, not independently documented.

His father sought a secure profession for him. Poisson was sent to an uncle, a surgeon at Fontainebleau, to learn medicine and surgery. The experiment failed. Accounts emphasize his poor manual coordination: he could neither manipulate surgical instruments reliably nor draw well. A darker version says that a patient entrusted to him for bleeding died. Arago’s narrative presents the surgical episode humorously and as evidence of physical awkwardness; the patient-death story is not independently documented in the sources consulted and should be regarded as **biographical lore**.

The failure was formative rather than ruinous. A school friend, **Vanneau**, exposed him to mathematical problems from the École centrale at Fontainebleau. Poisson solved them and entered that school. His decisive early teacher was **M. Billy**, whom Arago depicts as patient, generous, and willing to study more advanced mathematics in order to keep ahead of his pupil. Billy recognized that Poisson rapidly surpassed him and encouraged the young man toward the École Polytechnique.

## Education and Formation, 1798–1800

Poisson entered the **École Polytechnique in 1798**, reportedly ranking first in the competitive entrance examination. Institutional memory records that classmates collected money to enable this poor student to continue. The episode is plausible and repeated by the school itself, though the exact amount and participants were not found.

His formation placed him at the center of post-Revolutionary French mathematical science:

- **Joseph-Louis Lagrange** and **Pierre-Simon Laplace** recognized and patronized his talent.
- **Adrien-Marie Legendre** noticed his early memoir on finite differences.
- **Sylvestre-François Lacroix** participated in evaluating that work and later preceded him as exit examiner.
- **Gaspard Monge’s** descriptive geometry remained a problem because Poisson could not draw. The school excused the graphical requirements because he was directed toward science rather than engineering service.

At about eighteen he wrote two unusually advanced memoirs: one concerning **Bézout’s elimination method/theory of equations**, and another on the **number of complete integrals of finite-difference equations**. Legendre and Lacroix recommended publication of the latter in the *Recueil des savants étrangers*. The achievement helped him complete his studies in **1800 without the usual final examination** and move immediately into teaching.

Poisson was not a doctoral student in the modern sense, and modern lists naming Laplace or Lagrange as “doctoral advisers” are retrospective mappings onto an institutional world without the later PhD structure.

## Early Career, 1800–1812

### École Polytechnique

- **1800:** appointed *répétiteur* (tutor/assistant) in analysis, an exceptional direct Paris appointment.
- **1802:** became a substitute or deputy professor.
- **1806:** obtained the chair of mechanics vacated after Fourier’s departure for Grenoble. Some institutional summaries compress this as succession to Fourier beginning in 1802; the distinction is between deputy service and the full chair.
- He taught at the school until the reorganization of **1814**, after which he moved into the powerful role of exit examiner.

Poisson became known as a highly effective lecturer. His aphorism about doing and teaching mathematics captures an identity built around research and pedagogy. He worked rapidly, published continuously, and treated applied problems by deriving equations from a Laplacian molecular-mechanical picture.

### Other institutions

- He participated in the **Société Philomatique de Paris**, including editorial work for its mathematical bulletin.
- **1808:** became an astronomer attached to the **Bureau des longitudes** (the precise grade varied before his later appointment as *géomètre*).
- **1809:** appointed professor of rational mechanics in the newly organized **Faculté des sciences de Paris**.
- **1812:** elected to the **Académie des sciences/Institut de France**, after an earlier unsuccessful candidacy. He was still only thirty.

### First major scientific program

His *Traité de mécanique* (1811) organized analytical mechanics for teaching and research. In electricity, his 1811–1813 memoirs extended Laplace’s potential methods to the distribution of charge on conducting bodies. The equation now written in a standard form as \(\nabla^2 V=-4\pi\rho\) (with constants depending on units) generalized Laplace’s equation from charge-free regions to regions containing source density.

## Chronological Life History

### 1812–1819: Academy authority, electricity, and Fresnel

Admission to the Academy made Poisson both a producer and a judge of science. He developed mathematical theories of electrostatics and magnetism using action-at-a-distance fluids and molecular forces. George Green later explicitly acknowledged the elegance and importance of his electrical memoirs.

The best-known optical episode arose from the Academy’s **1817 diffraction prize competition**. Poisson served on a committee that included Laplace, Biot, and Arago. He belonged to the Laplacian/Newtonian corpuscular tradition and examined Augustin-Jean Fresnel’s wave-theory memoir critically. From Fresnel’s equations he deduced that a small circular opaque disk should create a bright point at the exact center of its geometrical shadow. Popular retellings say he proposed this as an absurd consequence. Arago tested the prediction and saw the spot; the committee then awarded Fresnel the prize in March 1819.

What is documented is Poisson’s deduction and the experimental confirmation. The theatrical version—Poisson confidently declaring the result impossible and being instantly humiliated—is a **later heroic narrative of wave optics**. The spot had also been observed earlier by Delisle and others, though Fresnel’s theory explained it. The episode was a failure for the corpuscular program but also an example of Poisson extracting a sharp, testable implication from an opponent’s mathematics.

### 1814–1827: examiner and national administrator

After political and institutional change in 1814–1816, Poisson ceased holding his Polytechnique professorship and became the school’s **exit examiner**, replacing Lacroix. Arago gives **1816**; an École summary gives **1815**. He held the examining office until death. The role gave him influence over careers in the state technical corps and helps explain his place in later stories about gatekeeping.

On **26 July 1820**, he became a councillor of the University. From **1822** he served as treasurer of the Royal Council of Public Instruction. He advocated mathematics for students in the humanities as well as sciences and sought a unified *agrégation* examination for mathematics and physics. This was not a private educational business; it was state administration.

He married Nancy de Bardi in 1817. In **1818** he was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society of London. Foreign honors followed.

His research during these years included:

- celestial perturbations and variation of constants;
- lunar libration;
- definite integrals and trigonometric series;
- propagation of waves and sound;
- elasticity and fluids;
- magnetic potential;
- heat conduction.

### Fourier relationship and heat controversy

The Poisson–Fourier relationship combined succession, transmission, rivalry, and dispute. Poisson succeeded Fourier at the École Polytechnique. In **1808**, as an editor of the Société Philomatique bulletin, Poisson published a signed-“P” account that was the first printed exposition of Fourier’s heat theory. From 1814 onward Poisson developed his own work on trigonometric series and heat.

In **1815** a bitter priority dispute broke out. Fourier complained to Laplace that Poisson and Biot had not produced results different from his but claimed a superior mode of exposition. Poisson criticized gaps in Fourier’s derivations and pursued a molecular-mechanical foundation, whereas Fourier intentionally constructed a macroscopic mathematical theory without committing to a microscopic caloric mechanism. Modern historians generally give Fourier priority for the decisive theory and series method while also recognizing that some of Poisson’s criticisms addressed real issues of proof and that Poisson extended the heat equation to variable conductivity. Poisson’s *Théorie mathématique de la chaleur* appeared in 1835.

### 1821–1827: title, honors, and Laplace’s institutional succession

Royal government offered Poisson the dignity of **baron**—sources variously date it to **1821** or **1825**. He did not obtain the formal letters patent/diploma and did not ordinarily use the title. This matters because later lists that simply call him “Baron Poisson” overstate his personal adoption of the style.

On **11 April 1827**, after Laplace’s death, Poisson became *géomètre* at the Bureau des longitudes. This was a direct institutional succession to his mentor. It did not mean that he inherited Laplace’s estate, noble rank, or political following.

### 1828–1833: elasticity, capillarity, Galois, and Copley Medal

Poisson’s elasticity work assumed matter composed of molecules interacting through central forces. He derived relations among elastic constants, including a transverse-to-longitudinal strain ratio. The modern **Poisson ratio** remains fundamental, although the universal numerical value suggested by his molecular model is not generally correct; real materials exhibit a broad range.

His *Nouvelle théorie de l’action capillaire* appeared in **1831** and received the **Royal Society’s Copley Medal in 1832**. The work is also an example of his physical methodology: use a hypothesized molecular force law to derive observable continuum behavior.

#### The Galois report

Évariste Galois submitted a memoir on conditions for solvability of equations by radicals to the Academy on **17 January 1831**. **Poisson and Lacroix** reported on it on **4 July 1831**. Their report judged the argument insufficiently clear and the principal condition not presented in a form that allowed them to verify it; they recommended that Galois develop and clarify the work before it could be judged.

Later legend often says Poisson simply “rejected Galois’s genius” or destroyed the paper. The documentary distinctions are:

1. **Cauchy**, not Poisson, handled earlier Galois submissions in 1829 and encouraged revision; the story that Cauchy maliciously lost them is unproved.
2. A prize submission delivered in 1830 was associated with Fourier, who died before adjudication; its disappearance is not attributable to Poisson on available evidence.
3. The 1831 Poisson–Lacroix report was unfavorable, but it was written, reasoned, and preserved. Caroline Ehrhardt’s study shows that many submissions received no report at all; a detailed negative report could signify serious engagement.
4. With hindsight the referees failed to recognize a foundational theory. That is a documented failure of evaluation, not evidence of fraud, theft, political retaliation, or a conspiracy against Galois.

Galois, already alienated from academic institutions and radicalized politically, experienced the decision as another rejection. The episode later became a central martyr narrative: revolutionary genius versus conservative establishment. Poisson’s role was real, but later compression gives him responsibility for a chain of lost manuscripts and exclusions in which several people and institutions were involved.

### 1830–1837: regime change, probability, peerage

Poisson’s politics were cautious and institutionally adaptive. His father’s republican convictions reportedly shaped him, and one tradition says Poisson initially refused the oath required under Napoleon. Yet he served continuously under the Consulate/Empire, Bourbon Restoration, and July Monarchy. This is best described as concentration on science and educational administration, not absence from politics: his offices were political appointments and made him a powerful state functionary.

The **July Revolution of 1830** placed some Restoration appointments at risk. Arago later told a colorful story that ministers were considering Poisson’s removal; Arago arranged for him to dine at the Palais-Royal, where Louis-Philippe publicly recognized him, making dismissal politically awkward. This is a friend’s retrospective anecdote and should not be treated as a verbatim cabinet record.

Poisson’s probability work culminated in **Recherches sur la probabilité des jugements en matière criminelle et en matière civile** (1837). It generalized Bernoulli’s law of large numbers to varying probabilities and studied jury decisions. In the limiting calculation for many trials with small individual probability, he obtained the law later named the **Poisson distribution**. The distribution was a relatively small part of a large book about legal judgment, evidence, error, and social statistics.

The text must be read in its nineteenth-century context. It attempted to quantify probabilities of correct verdicts and conviction from aggregate judicial data, making assumptions about independence, stable propensities, and juror competence that are not empirically secure in modern legal systems. It was neither a court finding nor a legal code and did not demonstrate the justice of particular verdicts. Later statisticians, especially **Ladislaus von Bortkiewicz** in *Das Gesetz der kleinen Zahlen* (1898), made the rare-event law famous through applications such as deaths from horse kicks.

In **1837**, Poisson joined the Royal Council of the University and was created a **peer of France**, formally bringing him into the upper chamber of the July Monarchy. His peerage was justified as representation of science, but it was necessarily a political office. No evidence located shows sustained parliamentary leadership, party organization, intelligence work, political violence, or personal enrichment from legislation.

### 1837–1840: final synthesis and death

Poisson continued teaching, examining, and publishing at extraordinary volume. His late books included the theory of heat, probability of judgments, and projectile motion with attention to shape, rotation, air resistance, and Earth’s diurnal motion. He planned a broad *Traité de physique mathématique* synthesizing his molecular mechanics, but did not complete it.

He died at Sceaux on **25 April 1840**. The sources consulted establish the place and date but do **not establish a specific medical cause**. It would be unsafe to repeat an unsourced diagnosis. No well-supported evidence was found of addiction, psychiatric crisis, suicide, accident, or violence. His death triggered an inventory of his estate and burial at Père-Lachaise.

### Technologies and practical projects

Poisson was primarily a theorist, not a patenting inventor. His work nevertheless entered practical technology through:

- projectile and ballistic calculation;
- elasticity and material characterization;
- electrostatic potential and later field engineering;
- capillarity and fluid mechanics;
- heat conduction;
- celestial mechanics and navigation-related computation;
- examination and training of engineers in French state corps.

No patents or private companies under his name were found. The major “business structure” around him was the French state scientific-educational system, which paid him through multiple appointments.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Politics

Poisson inherited a family memory hostile to aristocratic military privilege and was described as republican in upbringing. His own career shows pragmatic continuity across regimes. He did not become a public ideologue like Arago, nor did he withdraw from state power. His baronial offer, royal education offices, and peerage indicate accommodation with monarchy; his service under Napoleon and reported reluctance over an oath complicate any simple royalist label.

No party manifesto, sustained election campaign, police file, or ideological tract by Poisson was found. Political claims should therefore be limited to documented appointments, oath stories attributed by biographers, and his educational policy.

### Religion

The consulted evidence does not establish a strong confessional identity, conversion, anticlerical campaign, or religious authorship. Silence should not be converted into atheism or orthodoxy. His intellectual writings operate within mathematical physics rather than theology.

### Intellectual method: the Laplacian program

Poisson’s mature physical philosophy belonged to the **Laplacian program**: physical phenomena should be reduced to forces between hypothetical molecules and treated analytically. This produced powerful equations and unified research across electricity, capillarity, elasticity, fluids, heat, and magnetism. It also isolated him as field and continuum approaches developed. Fourier’s phenomenological heat theory and Fresnel’s wave optics exposed limits of Poisson’s preferred ontology.

His work therefore combines enduring mathematics with physical mechanisms later discarded. The equations often survived the microscopic stories used to derive them.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Teachers, patrons, and predecessors

- **M. Billy:** first sustained mathematics teacher at Fontainebleau; the central formative mentor before Paris.
- **Lagrange:** teacher, evaluator, analytical model, and lasting supporter.
- **Laplace:** mentor, patron, scientific leader, and predecessor at the Bureau des longitudes. Poisson extended Laplacian potential theory and molecular physics.
- **Legendre and Lacroix:** evaluators of his precocious work; Lacroix later his predecessor as examiner and co-referee in the Galois case.
- **Monge:** institutional influence whose descriptive geometry highlighted Poisson’s inability to draw.

### Colleagues, collaborators, and rivals

- **Fourier:** predecessor, colleague, subject of Poisson’s early summary, and later rival in heat theory and priority.
- **Arago:** colleague, experimental tester of the bright spot, political protector in the 1830 anecdote, and posthumous biographer.
- **Cauchy:** contemporary analyst and Academy colleague. Both worked on waves, elasticity, and mathematical physics; their programs overlapped and competed. Cauchy’s earlier handling of Galois’s manuscripts must not be conflated with Poisson’s 1831 report.
- **Biot:** fellow Laplacian and participant in disputes over heat and optics.
- **Fresnel:** scientific opponent whose diffraction mathematics Poisson tested; the predicted bright spot became evidence for Fresnel’s theory.
- **Sophie Germain:** another central contributor to elasticity; her plate theory and Poisson’s molecular elasticity intersected within a contested Academy field.
- **George Green:** intellectual successor who cited Poisson’s electrical work and extended potential theory.

### Galois

There is no evidence of a close personal relationship. Their consequential connection was institutional: Poisson and Lacroix refereed Galois’s 1831 memoir. Later culture transformed this narrow contact into a symbolic antagonism.

### Dirac and the twentieth-century lineage

Poisson and **Paul Dirac** never overlapped in time. The relationship is conceptual:

1. Poisson formulated the bilinear operation later called the **Poisson bracket** in his work on variation of orbital elements and mechanics.
2. Hamiltonian mechanics made this bracket central to canonical dynamics.
3. In 1925–1926 Dirac recognized that quantum commutators correspond structurally to classical Poisson brackets and made this analogy foundational to canonical quantization.
4. Dirac later introduced the **Dirac bracket** for constrained Hamiltonian systems, a modification of the Poisson bracket.

Thus “Poisson–Dirac” is an intellectual lineage, not collaboration or direct influence through personal contact.

## Scientific Work and Named Concepts

### Mechanics and celestial mechanics

Poisson’s mechanics texts helped standardize analytical dynamics. His celestial work addressed secular inequalities, variation of arbitrary constants, planetary elements, lunar libration, and the rotation of Earth. The bracket associated with his name emerged from calculations involving variation of orbital elements; its modern abstract interpretation as a Lie bracket on functions came later.

### Analysis

He worked on definite integrals, finite differences, equations, Fourier/trigonometric series, and interpolation. The **Poisson integral/kernel** gives harmonic extension from boundary values in a disk or half-space. The **Poisson summation formula** connects a sum over a lattice with a sum over Fourier transforms, though precursors and independent formulations complicate simple invention narratives.

### Potential theory, electricity, and magnetism

The **Poisson equation** introduces a source term into Laplace’s equation. It is now fundamental in electrostatics, gravitation, fluid mechanics, diffusion, and numerical analysis. Poisson’s own electricity was based on two fluids and action at a distance; modern electromagnetic fields use the equation without that ontology.

### Elasticity

The **Poisson ratio** measures transverse contraction relative to longitudinal extension. Poisson’s derivation from central molecular forces implied special relations among elastic constants that are not universal. Cauchy and later continuum mechanicians developed broader theories.

### Probability and statistics

The **Poisson distribution** is the limiting distribution of rare events over many opportunities, with mass function

\[
P(X=k)=e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}.
\]

Poisson derived the limiting form in 1837 while studying verdicts. De Moivre had obtained related limiting mathematics much earlier, so “discovered by Poisson” is conventional attribution rather than an uncontested priority claim. Modern Poisson processes, regression, point processes, queuing, epidemiology, insurance, astronomy, and counting experiments extend far beyond Poisson’s legal application.

### Optics

The **Poisson/Arago bright spot** is a diffraction maximum at the center of the shadow of a circular obstacle. Its eponym commemorates a prediction Poisson drew from a theory he opposed.

### Heat and fluids

Poisson extended heat equations to variable conductivity and developed mathematical treatments of capillarity, waves, hydrodynamics, and rotating projectiles. His physical hypotheses were often molecular and later superseded; his methods fed mathematical physics.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Financial Questions

### Documented scientific disputes

1. **Fourier priority and rigor:** documented, sometimes virulent. Poisson criticized and competed with Fourier while also publicizing Fourier’s early theory. Modern accounts generally reject Poisson’s implicit priority over the central heat theory but credit extensions and legitimate technical criticism.
2. **Fresnel and wave optics:** documented theoretical opposition. The bright-spot prediction was confirmed against Poisson’s preferred corpuscular picture. The humiliation motif is exaggerated.
3. **Galois report:** documented adverse report with Lacroix. It failed to recognize the eventual importance of the work. There is no evidence Poisson stole, destroyed, or politically suppressed Galois’s mathematics.
4. **Elasticity model:** scientifically influential but physically overrestrictive. The limitation is a later technical judgment, not misconduct.
5. **Probability of judicial decisions:** ambitious and historically important, but dependent on strong assumptions about jurors, independence, and latent guilt. It should not be presented as empirical proof of judicial accuracy.

### Institutional power and gatekeeping

Poisson accumulated unusual authority as professor, academician, examiner, councillor, and peer. His exit examinations and Academy reports affected careers. This is documented structural power. No evidence located supports bribery, sale of appointments, plagiarism, embezzlement, money laundering, intelligence activity, abuse, criminal charge, civil lawsuit, or formal investigation.

### Wealth

Multiple salaries brought substantial comfort. Exact income, securities, and estate totals require consultation of the notarial inventory. There is no evidentiary basis here for calling him either impoverished in maturity or exceptionally wealthy by aristocratic standards.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. The infant hanging from a nail

- **Story:** A nurse left the swaddled infant suspended by his bands; his father rescued him.
- **Appearance/circulation:** Arago’s 1850 Academy biography, then popular biographies.
- **Evidence:** Arago may have heard it from Poisson or family, but no independent record is known.
- **Reputational function:** A providential survival prelude to genius.
- **Classification:** Near-contemporary anecdote; unverified.

### 2. The disastrous medical apprenticeship

- **Story:** Poisson was so clumsy at bleeding patients that medicine had to be abandoned; stronger versions add a patient’s death.
- **Appearance/circulation:** Arago and later mathematical biographies.
- **Evidence:** The failed apprenticeship and poor coordination are well attested; the death detail lacks independent documentation.
- **Function:** Comic contrast between manual ineptitude and analytical brilliance.
- **Classification:** Core fact with embellished variants.

### 3. “Life is good only for mathematics and teaching it”

- **Story:** Poisson repeatedly expressed total devotion to mathematics.
- **Appearance:** Attributed by Arago after Poisson’s death.
- **Evidence:** Credible witness attribution but no autograph source.
- **Function:** Defines the tireless savant and excuses political detachment.
- **Classification:** Attributed saying, probably authentic in substance but not textually secure.

### 4. Poisson was “proved wrong” by his own spot

- **Story:** He mocked Fresnel by predicting an absurd bright spot; Arago immediately showed it, humiliating Poisson.
- **Appearance:** Later textbooks and popular histories of optics.
- **Evidence:** Prediction and confirmation are documented. Motive, dialogue, surprise, and theatrical humiliation are less secure; earlier observers had seen the phenomenon.
- **Function:** A clean parable of experiment defeating authority and corpuscular dogma.
- **Classification:** Documented event with dramatized framing.

### 5. Poisson destroyed or rejected Galois out of incomprehension or malice

- **Story:** A conservative examiner suppressed a revolutionary genius and lost his masterpiece.
- **Appearance:** Romantic biographies of Galois and general histories.
- **Evidence:** Poisson and Lacroix issued an adverse report in 1831. Other lost submissions involved Cauchy and Fourier. The report requested clearer development and was more engagement than many submissions received.
- **Function:** Establishes Galois as doomed outsider and Poisson as bureaucratic antagonist.
- **Classification:** Real adverse decision expanded into unsupported conspiracy.

### 6. Poisson “invented” every Poisson eponym

- **Story:** The modern distribution, process, bracket, equation, kernel, ratio, and spot all appeared in their present form in Poisson’s writings.
- **Evidence:** Several derive directly from him, but names and abstractions were standardized later; the process is twentieth-century, the spot had earlier observers, and probability has de Moivre precursors.
- **Function:** Eponymic compression of complicated lineages.
- **Classification:** Pedagogical shorthand, historically misleading if read literally.

## Works by Poisson

### Bibliographical warning

Poisson published **more than 200 distinct works**, and older counts approach 300 or 400 because abstracts and reprints were counted as separate entries. The most authoritative compact primary bibliography is the **“Catalogue des travaux laissés par Poisson, rédigé par lui”** appended to Arago’s biography. The following is a substantial, organized list of major books and memoir clusters rather than a claim to reproduce every notice and abstract. Titles retain original French where established.

### Books and major monographs

- **Traité de mécanique** (Paris, 1811, 2 vols.; substantially revised second edition, 1833). Genuine authored treatise.
- **Nouvelle théorie de l’action capillaire** (Paris, 1831). Genuine authored monograph; basis of the 1832 Copley Medal.
- **Théorie mathématique de la chaleur** (Paris, 1835); **Supplément** (1837). Genuine authored treatise.
- **Recherches sur la probabilité des jugements en matière criminelle et en matière civile, précédées des règles générales du calcul des probabilités** (Paris, 1837). Genuine authored monograph; contains the rare-event limiting law.
- **Recherches sur le mouvement des projectiles dans l’air, en ayant égard à leur figure et leur rotation, et à l’influence du mouvement diurne de la terre** (Paris, 1839). Genuine authored monograph.
- **Lehrbuch der Mechanik** and other translations/adaptations of the *Traité* appeared in German; these are translations, not separately authored new works unless revised text is specified.

### Early algebra, finite differences, and analysis

- Memoir on Bézout’s method/elimination and the theory of equations (c.1799–1800).
- **Mémoire sur le nombre d’intégrales complètes dont les équations aux différences finies sont susceptibles** (early career; evaluated by Legendre and Lacroix).
- **Mémoire sur les intégrales définies** (1813).
- **Mémoire sur la manière d’exprimer les fonctions par des séries de quantités périodiques, et sur l’usage de cette transformation dans la solution de différents problèmes** (1820).
- **Suite du mémoire sur les intégrales définies et sur la sommation des séries** (1823).
- **Mémoire sur le calcul numérique des intégrales définies** (1826).
- Numerous notes on series, residues, definite integrals, finite differences, partial differential equations, and the calculus of variations in the *Journal de l’École polytechnique*, *Bulletin de la Société philomatique*, and Academy memoirs.

### Mechanics and celestial mechanics

- **Sur les inégalités séculaires des moyens mouvements des planètes** (1809).
- **Sur la variation des constantes arbitraires dans les questions de mécanique** (1809).
- Memoirs on planetary perturbations and variations of orbital elements, including the structures later called Poisson brackets.
- **Sur la libration de la lune** (published in *Connaissance des temps*, 1821).
- **Sur le mouvement de la terre autour de son centre de gravité** (Academy memoir, published 1827).
- Memoirs on pendulums, rotation, projectiles, sound, waves, and mechanics of systems.

### Electricity and magnetism

- Two major memoirs on the distribution of electricity on conducting bodies/spheres (presented 1811–1812; published in Academy memoirs).
- **Mémoire sur la théorie du magnétisme en mouvement** / closely related title conventionally translated as a memoir on the theory of magnetism (Academy volume associated with 1823/1824 publication).
- Further notes on electrical attraction, potential, magnetic induction, and surface/source cases of the potential equation.

### Heat

- Signed-“P” review/exposition, **“Mémoire sur la propagation de la chaleur dans les corps solides, par M. Fourier”** (1808). This is Poisson’s editorial account of Fourier’s work, not original authorship of Fourier’s theory.
- Series of memoirs on heat and trigonometric expansions, 1814–1825.
- **Mémoire sur la distribution de la chaleur dans les corps solides** (1823), including variable conductivity.
- The 1835 treatise and 1837 supplement listed above.

### Elasticity, fluids, capillarity, and acoustics

- **Mémoire sur l’équilibre et le mouvement des corps élastiques** (Academy memoir, 1829).
- **Mémoire sur les équations générales de l’équilibre et du mouvement des corps solides élastiques et des fluides** (1829/1831 publication context).
- Memoirs on deep-water waves, propagation of sound, hydrodynamics, and fluid equilibrium.
- **Sur l’attraction des sphéroïdes** (1829).
- **Sur l’attraction d’un ellipsoïde homogène** (published in Academy memoirs, 1835).
- *Nouvelle théorie de l’action capillaire* (1831), listed above.

### Probability and social mathematics

- Academy papers announcing/generalizing the **law of large numbers** (1835–1837 context).
- *Recherches sur la probabilité des jugements* (1837), genuine authored book.

### Reports, teaching, and institutional writing

- Academy referee reports, including with Lacroix the **Rapport sur le mémoire de M. Galois relatif aux conditions de résolubilité par radicaux** (read 4 July 1831). Co-authored institutional report, not a research memoir.
- Examination questions, educational reports, Academy evaluations, and council work survive dispersed in institutional archives.
- Poisson’s self-compiled catalogue, published posthumously by Arago, is genuine autobiographical bibliography but was edited and appended after his death.

### Manuscripts and unfinished work

At death Poisson was assembling a broad **Traité de physique mathématique**. It remained unfinished. Posthumous collections or reproductions of individual memoirs should not be confused with a completed “collected works” edition authored as such.

## Books and Major Works About Poisson

### Primary and near-primary biographical sources

- **François Arago**, “Poisson,” biography read in extracts to the Académie des sciences on 16 December 1850; printed in *Œuvres complètes de François Arago*, vol. 2 (1854). Essential near-primary narrative and catalogue; commemorative and partisan in places.
- **Royal Astronomical Society**, obituary notice (1841 era), linked through the MacTutor resource list.
- Academy minutes, reports, Bureau des longitudes records, École Polytechnique personnel records, and the 1840 notarial inventory.

### Modern collective volume and specialist biography

- **Yvette Kosmann-Schwarzbach (ed.)**, *Siméon-Denis Poisson* (Éditions de l’École Polytechnique, original commemorative volume 1981; expanded/reissued 2013), 548 pp. Includes Pierre Costabel on the man and work, Ernest Coumet on Poisson as a Polytechnique student, and specialist chapters across mathematics and physics.
- **Yvette Kosmann-Schwarzbach**, “Seven Concepts Attributed to Siméon-Denis Poisson,” *SIGMA* 18 (2022), 092. Useful corrective history of eponyms and priority.
- **D. H. Arnold**, “The Mécanique Physique of Siméon Denis Poisson: The Evolution and Isolation in France of his Approach to Physical Theory (1800–1840),” multi-part series, *Archive for History of Exact Sciences* 28–29 (1983–1984). The major study of his physical program, Fourier dispute, Fresnel episode, elasticity, and unfinished synthesis.

### Focused scholarship

- **Caroline Ehrhardt**, “A Social History of the ‘Galois Affair’ at the Paris Academy of Sciences (1831),” *Science in Context* 23 (2010): 91–119. Reconstructs the Academy’s algebraic and refereeing practices.
- **Amy Dahan-Dalmédico**, study of deep-water wave propagation and developments in Poisson and Cauchy, in *The History of Modern Mathematics II* (1989).
- **I. J. Good**, “Some Statistical Applications of Poisson’s Work,” *Statistical Science* 1 (1986): 157–180.
- **J. R. Hofmann**, “Poisson’s 1812 Electricity Memoir,” in *André-Marie Ampère* (1995).
- **Lorraine Daston**, studies of classical probability, including nineteenth-century moral and judicial applications.
- **Stephen M. Stigler**, histories of statistics that situate Poisson’s legal probability and later distribution.
- **Ivor Grattan-Guinness**, works on French mathematical physics and analysis around 1800–1840.
- **Jesper Lützen**, histories of mechanics and differential equations relevant to the Poisson bracket and analytical mechanics.
- **E. T. Whittaker**, *A History of the Theories of Aether and Electricity* (1910 and later editions), influential retrospective assessment of Poisson’s electrostatics.
- **W. W. Rouse Ball**, *A Short Account of the History of Mathematics*, biographical sketch of Poisson; useful but old and derivative.
- **MacTutor History of Mathematics**, “Siméon-Denis Poisson,” modern reference biography with extensive bibliography.

### Hostile, sympathetic, and interpretive tendencies

No major modern “hostile biography” devoted solely to Poisson was located. Hostile portrayals occur mainly inside heroic narratives about Fourier, Fresnel, or Galois, where Poisson appears as rival, dogmatist, or gatekeeper. Sympathetic institutional accounts emphasize prodigious output and service to education. Arnold’s scholarship is interpretive rather than celebratory: it treats Poisson’s molecular mechanics as productive and ultimately isolating.

## Films, Documentaries, Interviews, and Archival Material

No substantial theatrical feature film or major broadcast documentary devoted exclusively to Poisson was identified. He appears indirectly in educational media about probability, Fresnel diffraction, Galois, classical mechanics, and the history of mathematical physics.

Important accessible archival/digital materials include:

- Full text and page images of Arago’s biography and Poisson’s self-catalogue on Wikisource/Internet Archive.
- École Polytechnique biographical and exhibition pages; the school’s 2013 volume and its table of contents.
- FranceArchives authority record pointing to Poisson’s **1840 inventory after death**.
- Royal Society election and Copley Medal records.
- Digitized editions of *Traité de mécanique*, *Théorie mathématique de la chaleur*, *Recherches sur la probabilité des jugements*, and Academy memoirs in Gallica, Google Books, HathiTrust, Internet Archive, and specialist digital libraries.
- Academy minutes containing the Poisson–Lacroix report on Galois, cited in modern critical scholarship.
- Photographs and historical engravings of the Père-Lachaise tomb in Wikimedia Commons.
- The 2014 exhibition **“S.-D. Poisson, les mathématiques au service de la science”**, mounted in Paris and subsequently shown in the United States; exhibition pages and later École presentations preserve parts of its interpretive program.

Short online videos titled “Siméon Denis Poisson” exist, but none located met the evidentiary standard of an important documentary. No surviving sound recording or filmed interview is possible because he died before recording technologies.

## Death, Burial, and Estate

Poisson died at his residence or place of stay in Sceaux on 25 April 1840. A precise illness is not established in the consulted documentary sources. He was buried at Père-Lachaise. The surviving funerary monument has been photographed and catalogued; reports about its later physical deterioration vary, so present condition should be verified on site rather than inferred from old visitor pages.

The notarial inventory after death is catalogued in French archives. Nancy de Bardi and the children survived him, but exact dates and disposition of the estate were not reconstructed from the online notice.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate reputation

Arago portrayed Poisson as an extraordinarily productive savant and public teacher. His textbooks remained useful, and his equations circulated through mechanics and physics. His own catalogue documented a publishing output so large that later bibliographers had to remove duplicate abstracts and reprints.

### Eponymic survival

Poisson’s name became unusually durable because it attached to tools used daily across unrelated fields. Many users of a Poisson equation or distribution know little of the molecular physics or jury statistics from which they came. This produces both prestige and historical flattening.

### Mathematical physics

The Poisson equation became a central elliptic partial differential equation. Poisson brackets became the algebraic language of Hamiltonian dynamics, symplectic geometry, Lie–Poisson systems, integrable systems, and canonical quantization. The twentieth-century path through Dirac made Poisson foundational to quantum theory without validating Poisson’s own corpuscular or molecular models.

### Statistics

The distribution named for him became one of the basic discrete laws. Its canonical modern uses—radioactive counts, call arrivals, defects, insurance claims, disease incidence, astronomical photons—were not his original examples. Historical scholarship increasingly stresses de Moivre precursors, Poisson’s legal context, and Bortkiewicz’s role in popularization.

### Commemoration

- Poisson is one of the **72 scientists and engineers whose names are inscribed on the Eiffel Tower**.
- A statue by Deligand, cast by Charnot, was inaugurated at Pithiviers on **15 June 1851**. It was later destroyed/melted, commonly dated to the German occupation in the Second World War.
- Pithiviers preserves his name in public space, including Place Denis-Poisson and educational commemoration.
- A lunar crater and asteroid **(12874) Poisson** bear his name.
- The **Fédération Denis Poisson**, a French mathematical-physics research federation, carries the eponym.
- Exhibitions and the 1981/2013 specialist volume periodically reassessed his work.

### Historical reinterpretation

Older histories often ranked Poisson principally by the number of his works and his succession to Laplace. Later historians examined the physical assumptions beneath the mathematics and the institutional power beneath the career. Three revisions are especially important:

1. **From lone inventor to lineage:** many eponyms have precursors and later reformulations.
2. **From neutral savant to administrator:** his examination and council posts shaped French education and professional access.
3. **From villain in other men’s stories to historically situated referee/rival:** Fourier, Fresnel, and Galois controversies remain genuine, but their popular mythologies compress documents and chronology.
