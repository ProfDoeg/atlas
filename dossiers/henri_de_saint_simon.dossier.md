# Henri de Saint-Simon — Research Dossier

**Subject:** Claude-Henri de Rouvroy, comte de Saint-Simon (1760–1825), French aristocrat, soldier, speculator, social theorist, theorist of industrial society, and posthumous namesake of Saint-Simonianism.  
**Not to be confused with:** Louis de Rouvroy, duc de Saint-Simon (1675–1755), the court memoirist of Louis XIV and the Regency.

## Research note and evidentiary method

Saint-Simon's biography is unusually difficult to reconstruct because several layers of evidence overlap:

- contemporary military and administrative records;
- Saint-Simon's own retrospective autobiographical fragments and self-descriptions;
- printed works issued under his name or in collaboration with secretaries;
- correspondence, much of which became systematically available only with Pierre Musso's 2025 edition of the *Correspondance (1782–1825)*;
- disciple-written biographies and the enormous nineteenth-century Saint-Simonian edition of his works;
- later biographies that repeated anecdotes whose documentary foundations are weak;
- posthumous ideological appropriations by socialists, positivists, technocrats, liberals, Catholics, Marxists, sociologists, industrialists, and critics of technocracy.

The 2025 correspondence is especially important because its editor explicitly states that the letters reveal a life "quite different" from the biography repeatedly told by earlier biographers and because the volume adds two previously unpublished or unidentified texts issued under pseudonyms during the Revolution, in 1790 and 1795. The critical *Œuvres complètes* edited by Juliette Grange, Pierre Musso, Philippe Régnier, and Franck Yonnet (PUF, 2012; publisher pages sometimes give 2013 for the completed set) is the principal modern critical edition.

Accordingly, this dossier distinguishes:

- **documented fact** — supported by contemporary or strong archival evidence;
- **self-report** — Saint-Simon's own retrospective account;
- **later testimony** — especially recollections by disciples or acquaintances;
- **scholarly reconstruction** — conclusions of modern historians based on scattered evidence;
- **legend / self-mythology** — stories important to his reputation but not independently verifiable;
- **retrospective label** — concepts such as "utopian socialist" or "technocrat" applied after his lifetime.

Exact dates are given where the documentary record permits them. Where major sources disagree, the disagreement is retained rather than silently harmonized.

## Basic Identifying Information

### Full name and principal forms

**Most standard modern form:** Claude-Henri de Rouvroy, comte de Saint-Simon.

Other forms found in catalogs, publications, correspondence, revolutionary usage, and later literature include:

- Claude Henri de Rouvroy de Saint-Simon
- Claude-Henri de Saint-Simon
- Henri de Saint-Simon
- Henri Saint-Simon
- Henry de Saint-Simon
- comte de Saint-Simon
- Rouvroy de Saint-Simon
- **Claude Bonhomme** / **citoyen Bonhomme** — Revolutionary name used when he abandoned overt noble styling
- **Simon** — another simplified Revolutionary identity appearing in the biographical tradition
- **M. Falvy** — a name associated by modern Saint-Simon scholarship with one of the identities he used under the Empire
- **Un habitant de Genève** — authorial persona of his early Genevan letters; the BnF treats "Habitant de Genève, Un" as a rejected/alternative author form
- **Le Père** — an authorial/pseudonymous form indexed by the BnF and later resonant with the quasi-religious language of Saint-Simonianism

In his later life he increasingly preferred **Henri Saint-Simon**. France Mémoire notes that he chose to call himself Henri Saint-Simon, a useful reminder that the long aristocratic form is partly a bibliographic normalization.

### Birth

- **17 October 1760**
- **Paris, Kingdom of France**

The date and place are supported by the Bibliothèque nationale de France authority record and by essentially all modern scholarship.

### Death

- **19 May 1825**
- **Paris**
- A detailed modern reconstruction based on the correspondence gives approximately **10:00 p.m.**
- Residence at death: **9, rue du Faubourg-Montmartre, Paris**
- Age: 64

The consulted sources do not provide a sufficiently well-documented medical cause of death to justify a specific diagnosis. He had survived a severe self-inflicted gunshot wound in 1823 and had lived for years under recurrent financial, physical, and psychological strain, but a direct causal connection between those conditions and his death should not be asserted without stronger medical documentation.

### Burial

- **22 May 1825**
- Civil funeral at **Père-Lachaise Cemetery**, Paris
- Present monument: **Division 28**, chemin Camille-Jordan, first line, according to the Association des Amis et Passionnés du Père-Lachaise
- His family was reportedly absent from the funeral.
- Friends, former collaborators, and future propagators of his thought attended.

### Nationality and social origin

French. Born into an old noble Rouvroy de Saint-Simon branch, but not into the wealthiest line of the family. He was a remote relation of the much better-known court memoirist Louis de Rouvroy, duc de Saint-Simon.

### Principal occupations and roles across his life

- aristocratic military officer;
- participant in the American War of Independence;
- military/engineering-project entrepreneur;
- speculator in confiscated *biens nationaux* during the French Revolution;
- salon host;
- independent student of mathematics, physics, medicine, physiology, history, political economy, and social organization;
- pamphleteer and journal founder;
- political and social theorist;
- theorist of "industrial" society;
- religious-social reformer in his last work.

He held no long-term university chair, elected office, ministerial office, or stable institutional academic appointment.

## Family and Ancestry

### The Rouvroy de Saint-Simon family

Saint-Simon belonged to the **Rouvroy de Saint-Simon** family, a noble lineage associated particularly with Picardy. He came from the **Sandricourt** branch.

The famous memoirist **Louis de Rouvroy, duc de Saint-Simon (1675–1755)** belonged to the same extended family but was not Henri's father or grandfather. Henri is variously called a distant cousin, younger-branch relative, or "petit cousin" in later literature. Modern summaries agree on kinship while differing in how they describe the exact degree.

### Parents

Compiled genealogical sources identify his parents as:

- **Balthazar-Henri de Rouvroy de Saint-Simon de Sandricourt (1721–1783)** — military officer; described in Saint-Simon scholarship as a captain and later *mestre de camp*.
- **Blanche-Isabelle de Rouvroy de Saint-Simon de Vaux (born 1737; death date uncertain in the genealogical material consulted)**.

Pierre Musso's biographical reconstruction describes the father as having nine children and Claude-Henri as the eldest of six sons. This is compatible with the surviving sibling roster: Claude-Henri had an elder sister but was the eldest male child.

### Siblings

A compiled Geneanet genealogy gives the following children of the parental couple:

1. **Adélaïde Blanche Marie de Rouvroy de Saint-Simon de Sandricourt** (1759–1820)
2. **Claude-Henri de Rouvroy de Saint-Simon de Sandricourt** (1760–1825)
3. **Claude-Henri René** (1762–1763)
4. **Marie-Louise** (1763–1804 in this genealogy; other secondary references disagree on later details)
5. **Eudes Claude Henri** (1766–1785)
6. **Adrienne Émilie Josèphe** (1767–1853)
7. **Louis Claude Jean** (1769–1814)
8. **André Louis** (born 1771; death date not established in the consulted genealogy)
9. **Herbert Claude Louis** (1774–1852)

This sibling list comes from a modern compiled genealogy rather than from direct inspection of each parish or civil record and should therefore be treated as a genealogical reconstruction, not as a substitute for archival proof of every entry.

### Claimed descent from Charlemagne

A recurring Rouvroy/Saint-Simon genealogical tradition traced the family back to **Charlemagne**, often through the medieval counts of Vermandois.

**Status:** disputed genealogical tradition / later self-mythology.

Modern genealogical compilations themselves warn that the bridge linking the later Rouvroy family to the Carolingian/Vermandois line is doubtful. Richard Swedberg's study of Saint-Simon's European thought states more strongly that the descent claim has been disproved. The claim nevertheless became psychologically and symbolically important because Saint-Simon incorporated Charlemagne into his own autobiographical mythology.

### Marriage

On **7 August 1801 in Paris**, Saint-Simon married:

**Alexandrine-Sophie Goury de Champgrand (1773–1860)**, later known as **Sophie de Bawr / Baronne de Bawr**.

She was a writer, dramatist, composer, and later memoirist in her own right. Her own later life makes clear that she should not be treated merely as an appendage to Saint-Simon's biography.

The marriage lasted less than a year in practical terms and ended in divorce in **1802**.

Later reminiscences say Saint-Simon conceived the marriage partly as an intellectual experiment: he wanted a wife capable of organizing a salon where he could encounter leading minds. This interpretation is prominent in later biographical traditions and in Saint-Simon's own theatrical way of narrating his life, but the precise motives cannot be reduced to a single documented statement.

### Children

No children of the Saint-Simon–Champgrand marriage have been identified in the standard biographies, genealogical sources, or the material consulted for this dossier.

Sophie de Bawr had a child connected with an earlier secret marriage; that child was not Saint-Simon's.

### The Madame de Staël marriage story

A famous anecdote says Saint-Simon considered divorcing Sophie in order to marry the widowed **Germaine de Staël**, because he supposedly reasoned that she was Europe's most intelligent woman and he its most extraordinary man.

**Status:** later recollection / anecdote, not established documentary fact.

The story appears in **Léon Halévy's 1832 recollections**, written after Saint-Simon's death. It is historically useful as evidence of how Saint-Simon narrated his own ambition and how disciples remembered his grandiosity, but it should not be treated as proof that a serious marriage negotiation with Staël occurred.

## Childhood and Early Life

### Picardy and Paris

The documentary record of Saint-Simon's childhood is thin. Modern biographers describe movement between Paris and family properties or family environments in Picardy, including **Falvy**. The later Saint-Simonian life-writing greatly magnified this period, creating a precocious heroic childhood around a child already conscious of a world-historical mission.

The 2025 correspondence edition is particularly important because its publisher emphasizes how little of the conventional biography survives unchanged when one works outward from actual letters.

### Temperament in later accounts

Later biographies describe him as:

- turbulent;
- intellectually restless;
- resistant to conventional discipline;
- enormously ambitious;
- fascinated by large-scale works, hydraulics, and organization.

These character traits are plausible in light of his adult conduct but are often reconstructed backward from the adult Saint-Simon.

### The valet wake-up story

The most famous childhood/youth anecdote says he ordered a servant to wake him every morning by reminding him that he had "great things to do."

**Status:** famous later biographical anecdote; primary provenance weak.

The story occurs in nineteenth- and early twentieth-century biographies and encyclopedia entries, including the 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica*. No contemporary childhood document or early independent witness was located in the sources consulted. Its endurance reflects the later image of Saint-Simon as a man possessed by an almost comic sense of destiny.

## Education and Formation

### Private education

Saint-Simon was educated privately, as was normal for a noble child of his class. He later characterized his education as careful but badly directed, with too many teachers and insufficient intellectual unity.

### Was d'Alembert his tutor?

Saint-Simon later claimed, or was understood to claim, that his education had been "directed" by the mathematician and Encyclopedist **Jean Le Rond d'Alembert**.

**Status:** disputed.

- The old biographical tradition often states as fact that d'Alembert directed his education.
- Later historians already expressed doubt in the nineteenth century about whether this meant literal personal tutoring.
- No solid documentary evidence was located in this research establishing a sustained formal teacher-pupil relationship.
- The claim may record occasional intellectual contact, indirect educational influence, or retrospective construction of an Enlightenment pedigree.

It is safer to say: **Saint-Simon associated his education with d'Alembert's direction or influence; literal sustained tutoring is unproven.**

### Rousseau

Some later accounts add **Jean-Jacques Rousseau** to the list of men who personally taught or influenced the young Saint-Simon.

**Status:** weakly documented / legendary as a personal pedagogical relationship.

Rousseau's intellectual influence on the generation is obvious, but direct teaching of Saint-Simon is not established by the material consulted.

### Military engineering and mathematics after America

After his American service, Saint-Simon was associated with **Mézières**, home of the royal engineering school. Later sources say he followed mathematical instruction associated with **Gaspard Monge**, one of the great figures of the École Royale du Génie.

The safest formulation is that Saint-Simon **studied mathematics and engineering in the Mézières environment and encountered Monge's teaching**, rather than that he necessarily completed a formal engineering-school curriculum equivalent to that of enrolled military engineers.

### Scientific re-education in Paris, c. 1798 onward

Around 1798, after his break with large-scale speculation, Saint-Simon deliberately set out to re-educate himself.

His program included:

- mathematics and physics in the environment of the **École Polytechnique**;
- physiology and medicine near the **École de Médecine**;
- contact with or study of the work of figures such as **Monge**, **Lagrange**, **Cabanis**, **Bichat**, **Vicq d'Azyr**, and the physician **Jean Burdin**;
- efforts to connect physical science, physiology, psychology, history, and social organization into a new "science of man."

This late self-education is more central to his intellectual formation than the picturesque stories about famous childhood tutors.

## Early Career: Soldier, Atlantic World Officer, and Engineering Projector, 1777–1789

### Entry into the army: 1775, 1777, or 1778?

Sources disagree on the exact beginning of his military career.

- Saint-Simon's own later account says he entered service in **1777**.
- Pierre Musso's reconstruction likewise places the start in **January 1777**.
- An *Encyclopedia of the American Revolution* military entry gives **1775** as the year he entered as a second lieutenant.
- Other reference works give 1777 or 1778.

**Conclusion:** 1777 is the strongest date in the modern Saint-Simon specialist literature, but 1775 appears in military-reference tradition and should be retained as a source discrepancy.

### Touraine Regiment

By **3 June 1779**, military-reference material places him as a **captain of cavalry**; on **14 November 1779** he transferred into the **Touraine infantry regiment**.

He sailed from **Brest** toward the Caribbean theater, reaching the French Atlantic imperial world centered on **Saint-Domingue**.

### Caribbean warfare, 1779–1781

The Touraine regiment served in the Caribbean. Military summaries connect Saint-Simon with operations around **Barbados in 1780** and with the French-Spanish coalition war against Britain.

This period matters intellectually only retrospectively: decades later, Saint-Simon would portray the American conflict as the event that taught him that a new industrial liberty was replacing the old feudal-military order.

### Yorktown, 1781

Saint-Simon participated in the **Yorktown campaign**, the decisive Franco-American siege that ended with the surrender of Cornwallis in October 1781.

Military-reference accounts state that he **commanded his regiment's gunners** during the siege.

A recurring source of confusion must be eliminated:

- **Claude-Henri de Rouvroy, future social theorist**, was a young officer.
- **Claude-Anne de Rouvroy, marquis de Saint-Simon-Montbléru**, an older relative, was the senior Saint-Simon military commander who brought troops from the Caribbean to Yorktown.

They are distinct people. The presence of two Saint-Simons in the same expedition has repeatedly contaminated short biographies.

Saint-Simon left Virginia with **de Grasse's fleet on 4 November 1781** according to the military-reference chronology.

### St. Kitts and Brimstone Hill, 1782

In **February 1782**, he took part in the French siege of **Brimstone Hill** on St. Kitts. Military accounts credit him with engineering/siege activity.

### Battle of the Saintes and imprisonment, 1782–1783

On **12 April 1782**, during the **Battle of the Saintes** / action in the Saints Passage, the French fleet suffered a major defeat.

Saint-Simon was captured by the British and sent to **Jamaica** as a prisoner of war.

He remained a prisoner until the peace settlement of 1783.

Later biographies sometimes transform the episode into a dramatic wound-and-capture narrative. The capture is well supported; specific embellishments about wounds require more caution.

### Mexico and an interoceanic canal proposal

After release, Saint-Simon presented or attempted to present to the **viceroy of Mexico** a plan for an **interoceanic canal**.

**Documentary status:** well established in the biographical tradition, though the precise engineering dossier is less accessible than the fact of the proposal.

The project should not be anachronistically called a design for the later Panama Canal. It belongs to a much older history of schemes to connect the Atlantic and Pacific worlds across Spanish America.

This is one of the earliest examples of a lifelong Saint-Simon pattern: **geopolitical problems translated into vast engineering systems**.

### Return to France and military rank

After returning to France:

- he was made **mestre de camp en second** in the **Aquitaine regiment**, effective **1 January 1784**, according to the military reference;
- by **1788** he was a colonel attached to cavalry in the surviving reference chronology;
- in **1790** he was made a **chevalier of the Order of Saint-Louis**.

These military honors sit awkwardly beside the later image of a thinker who rejected hereditary idleness. They are part of the pre-Revolutionary life he subsequently reinterpreted.

### Netherlands, c. 1785

Biographical traditions place Saint-Simon in the **Netherlands in 1785**, where he observed political conditions and, characteristically, canal engineering and hydraulic works.

### Spain, 1787–1789

Saint-Simon spent time in **Spain**, where he involved himself in large development schemes.

The biographical tradition often calls his project a canal "from Madrid to the sea." This should be understood against the contemporary **Canal del Guadarrama** / Madrid-to-Guadalquivir-and-Atlantic planning environment, a real Spanish hydraulic-development project associated with Carlos Lemaur and the Banco de San Carlos.

Saint-Simon was not the original designer of the entire Guadarrama system. His role is better described as **an entrepreneurial-project involvement in Spanish canal and economic-development schemes**.

A later specialist summary describes a plan in which he expected to direct thousands of workers.

### Formation of the "great works" mentality

By the eve of the French Revolution, several durable features were already visible:

- fascination with canals, transportation, and infrastructure;
- preference for continental-scale or global-scale projects;
- belief that technical elites could reorganize material life;
- willingness to move between military, financial, diplomatic, and engineering worlds;
- a personal style of extreme ambition.

These traits preceded his published social philosophy.

## Chronological Life History

### 1789–1792: Revolution and abandonment of overt aristocratic identity

Saint-Simon accepted the Revolution rather than joining the émigré opposition.

He dropped or deemphasized his noble identity and used names such as **Claude Bonhomme** and **citoyen Bonhomme**.

This was not merely verbal. His financial activity depended on the revolutionary transformation of property.

### National-property speculation

With the confiscation and sale of **church and émigré property** as *biens nationaux*, Saint-Simon entered property speculation on a substantial scale.

His principal associate was the Prussian nobleman/diplomat **Jean Sigismond Ehrenreich, comte de Redern**.

The enterprise involved:

- purchasing large blocks of confiscated property;
- reselling property;
- using financial intermediaries;
- connections with the **Perrégaux bank**, which financed operations of this type;
- treating the Revolution as a massive restructuring of landed ownership.

Later socialist hagiography sometimes minimized this period; hostile accounts sometimes reduced him to a profiteer. Neither reduction is sufficient. The land business was real, consequential, and central to his financial biography.

### Revolutionary political position

Saint-Simon was not a Jacobin political leader, elected revolutionary legislator, or major officeholder. He tried to survive and profit within the Revolution while developing a language that opposed inherited feudal privilege.

Jean Dautry's archival study emphasizes that he voluntarily mixed his life into the Revolution and later interpreted it as a historically necessary "critical" phase.

### Revolutionary playing cards

Later biographical literature reports that in **1793** Saint-Simon designed a revolutionary deck of cards in which:

- "geniuses" replaced kings,
- "liberties" replaced queens,
- "equalities" replaced jacks.

**Status:** reported in later literature; not independently established here from a surviving primary artifact.

It is plausible within Revolutionary dechristianizing and republican iconography, but should remain an attributed episode rather than an unquestioned fact.

### Arrest, 1793

Saint-Simon was arrested during the Terror.

The reasons are not completely transparent. Possibilities in the sources include:

- suspicion generated by his aristocratic background;
- his relationship with Redern, a Prussian;
- the political vulnerability of property speculation;
- general-security measures rather than a substantiated counterrevolutionary plot.

Jean Dautry describes the detention as a **measure of general security**.

### Sainte-Pélagie and Luxembourg prisons

The strongest modern reconstruction gives:

- first imprisonment at **Sainte-Pélagie**;
- transfer to the **Luxembourg prison in May 1794**;
- release **28 August 1794**;
- total detention roughly **eleven months**.

Older Larousse material gives a contradictory "November 1793–June 1794" range while also saying that the fall of Robespierre caused his release. Because Robespierre fell on 27 July 1794, the "June" endpoint cannot fit that explanation. The modern specialist date of 28 August is therefore preferable.

### The Charlemagne prison vision

Saint-Simon later told a story that while imprisoned at Luxembourg, **Charlemagne appeared to him** and declared that Saint-Simon would equal or surpass his ancestor in philosophy as Charlemagne had done in politics.

**Status:** self-reported visionary/autobiographical story; not independently verifiable.

This episode belongs both to his psychological self-conception and to the mythology of his movement. It is treated in detail below.

### 1794–1797: wealth after prison

After the Terror, Saint-Simon's property position made him wealthy.

By **October 1795**, modern correspondence-based reconstruction places him in Paris near the **Palais-Royal**, living in a private mansion with a household of roughly **twenty servants** and operating an intellectual/political salon.

The exact value of his fortune is uncertain. Some later sources give round figures such as one million francs/livres, but these figures should not be treated as audited net worth.

### Redern rupture and financial loss

The Saint-Simon–Redern partnership deteriorated into a major dispute over the division of profits and property.

Older scholarship devoted separate studies to Saint-Simon's land speculation and his litigation/financial contest with Redern.

The essential facts are:

- the association had generated substantial assets;
- Saint-Simon believed he had been deprived of his proper share;
- Redern emerged from liquidation in a stronger financial position;
- the dispute continued to haunt Saint-Simon;
- later Saint-Simon writings include material connected to his contest with Redern.

The common popular story that Redern simply "stole his entire fortune" is too clean. The breakup was a complex business and legal dispute involving assets, agreements, and competing claims.

### 1798: the declared intellectual turn

Saint-Simon later dated a decisive transformation to the period after his break with Redern.

He said, in effect, that he now wanted to act directly on humanity's moral and scientific condition rather than merely make money.

He reorganized his life around systematic study:

- physical sciences;
- mathematics;
- physiology;
- medicine;
- intellectual history;
- social organization.

This is the beginning of the Saint-Simon recognizable as a theorist.

### Salon as a research instrument

He used money socially and intellectually, hosting scientists, artists, financiers, and intellectuals.

The salon had several functions:

- access to expertise;
- testing ideas;
- reputation-building;
- recruitment of collaborators;
- an attempt to make himself the organizer of a new encyclopedic synthesis.

Later stories emphasize extravagant dinners, luxury, and sexual relationships. The luxurious scale of life is well attested; details about individual liaisons are much less securely documented.

## 1801–1802: Marriage, Salon Experiment, Divorce, and Travel

### Marriage to Sophie de Champgrand

Saint-Simon married Alexandrine-Sophie Goury de Champgrand on **7 August 1801**.

Sophie was already a culturally accomplished woman. She later became a successful author and composer under the name **Sophie de Bawr**.

Later testimony portrays the marriage as partly instrumental: Saint-Simon wanted a brilliant salon hostess and believed organized social interaction could accelerate his intellectual project.

### Expense and grandiosity

Léon Halévy, writing in 1832, reported Saint-Simon's claim that the marriage and associated social life cost an enormous sum, sometimes translated into approximately 300,000 francs.

**Status:** later recollection of Saint-Simon's own claim, not independently audited.

The story is best used as evidence of the financial extravagance and self-dramatization surrounding this phase.

### Divorce

The marriage ended in **1802**.

No evidence found in the consulted material supports a scandalous divorce proceeding in the modern sense. The intellectual incompatibility, Saint-Simon's unstable finances, and his instrumental expectations of marriage all appear in later accounts.

### Travel after the marriage

Biographies place him in **England**, **Germany**, **Switzerland**, and especially **Geneva** in the early 1800s. The itinerary is not documented day by day in the public sources consulted.

Geneva became symbolically important because his first famous published intervention assumed the voice of an **"inhabitant of Geneva."**

## 1802–1814: From Scientific Religion to a Science of Society

### Earliest known pamphlet: *À la Société du Lycée*

Richard Swedberg, drawing on bibliographical research, identifies **À la Société du Lycée**, probably from **March 1802**, as Saint-Simon's earliest known printed pamphlet. It is a short, rare item recovered by historian Jean Dautry in the twentieth century.

This complicates the old statement that *Lettres d'un habitant de Genève* was simply "his first work."

### Two Genevan-letter forms and a dating problem

Bibliographical scholarship distinguishes:

- *Lettres d'un habitant de Genève à l'humanité* — 1802;
- *Lettres d'un habitant de Genève à ses contemporains* — commonly dated 1802 or 1803 depending on edition and state.

Later collections often regularized the title and date.

### The Newtonian council

In the Genevan letters Saint-Simon imagined a new spiritual-scientific order organized around leading scientists and artists. Newton becomes a quasi-sacred figure in a projected scientific religion.

The proposal is not modern secular technocracy in a simple sense. It combines:

- scientific authority;
- hierarchical selection of superior minds;
- moral organization;
- quasi-religious symbolism;
- transnational organization.

### Social hierarchy in the early writings

Saint-Simon's early schemes were emphatically **not egalitarian in the later democratic sense**.

He imagined stratified social functions and believed exceptional scientific/intellectual capacity should command authority.

Oxford research on his "technocratic internationalism" emphasizes that the expert class he envisioned was strongly shaped by existing assumptions about educated, property-owning men.

### Racial hierarchy: suppressed or overlooked material

One of the most important inconvenient elements in Saint-Simon's early thought is a **racial hierarchy applied to Black people**.

Richard Swedberg notes that a passage/note in the *Lettres d'un habitant de Genève* was omitted from standard later editions. In it, Saint-Simon argued that Revolutionaries had wrongly generalized equality to Black people and appealed to contemporary physiology to claim unequal intellectual capacities.

In his **1808** scientific writings he again used physiological racial hierarchy.

**Status:** documented in his texts; later editorial suppression/omission contributed to its disappearance from short intellectual histories.

This is not merely a matter of anachronistic terminology. It is evidence that Saint-Simon's universalist language of humanity coexisted with hierarchical racial anthropology.

### 1804: *De l'organisation sociale* / letter to Fourcroy

Bibliographic traditions list a **1804** text on social organization addressed to **Antoine François de Fourcroy**, a major chemist and state educational figure.

The text belongs to Saint-Simon's effort to gain recognition from established scientific administrators.

### 1807: *Introduction aux travaux scientifiques du dix-neuvième siècle*

The BnF catalogs:

**Claude-Henri de Saint-Simon, *Introduction aux travaux scientifiques du dix-neuvième siècle*, Paris, J.-L. Scherff, 1807.**

The work attempts a general map and reorganization of scientific labor.

It expresses several durable Saint-Simon convictions:

- science develops historically;
- scientific activity should be organized rather than left atomized;
- a general intellectual architecture could guide humanity;
- the organizer of knowledge performs a political-spiritual function.

### Financial collapse

By the middle of the 1800s Saint-Simon's private wealth had substantially evaporated.

Larousse and the older biographical tradition report that around **1807** he worked as a **copyist at the Mont-de-Piété**, an extraordinary reversal from his Directory-era luxury.

### Diard

An important figure in the poverty years was **Diard**, described as a former servant/employee who gave Saint-Simon housing and material assistance.

Diard's support is one of the least glamorous but most consequential relationships in Saint-Simon's life. The theorist of social organization depended materially on an ex-servant.

After Diard's death around 1810, Saint-Simon again faced severe hardship.

### Health crisis

The early 1810s brought poverty and illness. Some later accounts place him in a sanatorium in 1813, but the exact medical chronology is not adequately established in the strongest public sources consulted. It is safe to say that he suffered a significant health and financial crisis.

### *Histoire de l'homme* and physiology

Works and fragments from approximately **1809–1813** include:

- projects for a new encyclopedia;
- *Histoire de l'homme*;
- writings later grouped as *De la physiologie sociale*;
- *Mémoire sur la science de l'homme*.

He tried to construct a science of humanity continuous with physiology and the natural sciences.

### Archival manuscript: *Mémoire sur la science de l'homme*

FranceArchives catalogs manuscript material for *Mémoire sur la science de l'homme* and a Saint-Simon letter to the physician/naturalist **André Marie Constant Duméril**, dated **6 November 1813**.

This is valuable because it places Saint-Simon's "science of man" directly in a contemporary scientific network rather than only in later edited collections.

### Napoleon

Some texts of this period contain appeals to or praise of Napoleon, but Saint-Simon was never simply a Bonapartist ideologue. Like many projectors, he repeatedly addressed whoever possessed power in hopes of winning patronage for reorganization schemes.

His political method was frequently **petitionary and advisory rather than electoral**: persuade a prince, minister, scientist, banker, industrialist, or public that a new system was historically necessary.

## 1814–1815: Europe, Augustin Thierry, and Restoration Politics

### Augustin Thierry

Young historian **Augustin Thierry** became Saint-Simon's secretary, pupil, and collaborator.

Their collaboration is important because some works traditionally cataloged under Saint-Simon's name were drafted or heavily shaped by Thierry.

### *De la réorganisation de la société européenne* (1814)

The BnF catalogs:

*De la réorganisation de la société européenne, ou De la nécessité et des moyens de rassembler les peuples de l'Europe en un seul corps politique, en conservant à chacun son indépendance nationale* (1814).

The title page associates **Saint-Simon** and **Augustin Thierry**.

Authorship is nuanced:

- Saint-Simon supplied the central political project and intellectual framework.
- Scholarship summarized by Richard Swedberg indicates that Thierry probably performed much of the actual drafting and contributed historical material.
- The original presentation emphasized Saint-Simon and called Thierry his pupil.

The work proposed a European political structure with a common parliament while retaining national existence.

### Not a modern European Union blueprint

Later European federalists have celebrated Saint-Simon as a prophet of European union.

That is partly justified: he did formulate a concrete supranational European project in 1814.

But the historical proposal differed sharply from the later EU:

- leadership was expected especially from Britain and France;
- political representation was socially restricted;
- educated/property-owning elites had privileged authority;
- the plan was embedded in an imperial and colonial world;
- Saint-Simon's contemporary racial assumptions remained operative.

It is therefore best understood as an early nineteenth-century elite European federation proposal, not as a fully formed democratic EU.

### Colonial expansion

Swedberg shows that the 1814 European plan connected European political organization with **colonial expansion** and assumptions of European racial/civilizational superiority.

This imperial component is often omitted in celebratory accounts of Saint-Simon as a European visionary.

### The Hundred Days and the Arsenal librarian claim

Larousse states that during the **Hundred Days in 1815** Saint-Simon aligned with **Benjamin Constant** and accepted a position as **librarian at the Arsenal**, losing it after Waterloo.

There is a documentary problem:

- in a **1821 letter to Robin Scévole**, Saint-Simon reportedly claimed that he had accepted **no office from governments since 1789**.

These accounts conflict.

**Status:** unresolved in this research.

Without independent personnel records from the Arsenal, the librarian appointment should be presented as a traditional biographical claim contradicted by Saint-Simon's own later statement.

## 1816–1819: *L'Industrie*, the Industrial Class, and the Liberal Network

### *L'Industrie*

From **December 1816 to May 1818**, Saint-Simon issued *L'Industrie* irregularly in a series of cahiers/volumes.

This is a crucial turning point.

The key social division becomes less:

- noble versus commoner,

and more:

- **productive / industrial** versus **idle / parasitic**.

### Meaning of "industrial"

For Saint-Simon, the industrial class was much broader than factory owners.

It could include:

- scientists;
- engineers;
- physicians;
- artists;
- bankers;
- merchants;
- manufacturers;
- artisans;
- farmers;
- workers engaged in useful production.

This breadth is one reason later interpreters could depict him either as a theorist of capitalism or as a precursor of socialism.

### Property

Saint-Simon did not initially call simply for abolition of all private property.

His recurring distinction was between:

- property and power justified by socially useful production;
- unearned privilege, inherited rank, rentier idleness, and political dominance without productive function.

His later critique of hereditary property became sharper. The full Saint-Simonian doctrine of abolishing inheritance was systematized more emphatically by his disciples.

### Liberal milieu

During the Restoration Saint-Simon tried to educate or influence the **liberal opposition**.

He was close enough to liberal networks to publish and recruit among them, while simultaneously criticizing conventional parliamentary politics as secondary to the deeper reorganization of production and knowledge.

### Patronage: Ternaux

Larousse identifies the industrialist **Guillaume Louis Ternaux**, a major textile manufacturer, as an important industrial patron/subscriber who subsidized Saint-Simon.

Saint-Simon's entire late intellectual career depended on such patronage, subscriptions, collaborators, family assistance, and credit.

---

## 1817–1824: Auguste Comte

### Beginning of collaboration

**Auguste Comte**, still very young, became Saint-Simon's secretary/collaborator around **1817** after Comte's departure from the École Polytechnique environment.

Their collaboration lasted roughly seven years.

### What each supplied

Simplified accounts often turn the relationship into a contest over who "invented sociology" or who stole whose ideas.

The historical relationship was reciprocal and unequal in changing ways.

Saint-Simon supplied:

- an already-developed theory of historical transformation;
- the project of a positive science of society;
- the organic/critical period distinction;
- networks, publishing ventures, and material support;
- the industrial-reorganization framework.

Comte supplied:

- exceptional analytical rigor;
- systematic philosophical drafting;
- a more disciplined positive-philosophy architecture;
- texts that appeared inside Saint-Simonian publication structures.

### Authorship problems

Nineteenth-century collected editions helped blur authorship.

A later bibliographical review specifically notes that older editions could intercalate:

- an Augustin Thierry text into *L'Industrie*;
- Comte's work into the *Catéchisme des industriels* corpus.

The **third cahier** of the *Catéchisme des industriels* is especially associated with Comte's *Système de politique positive* / early positive-philosophy program rather than with Saint-Simon's sole authorship.

### Break, 1822–1824

The Saint-Simon–Comte relationship broke down between **1822 and 1824**.

Michel Bourdeau's study of the rupture emphasizes mutual dependence and the difficulty of Comte's emancipation because Saint-Simon had provided material and institutional support.

Points of conflict included:

- priority and attribution;
- editorial control;
- Comte's intellectual independence;
- different conceptions of political versus spiritual reorganization;
- incompatible personalities and working styles.

Comte left in **1824**.

### Later priority wars

Comte later minimized Saint-Simon's intellectual contribution; Saint-Simonians and later historians pushed back.

**Status:** real influence plus contested priority.

It is untenable either to reduce Comte to a plagiarist of Saint-Simon or to describe Saint-Simon as merely an eccentric patron whom Comte transformed from scratch.

---

## 1819–1820: *L'Organisateur*, the "Parable," and Criminal Prosecution

### *Le Politique*

Saint-Simon issued political writings under the title *Le Politique* in 1819, part of his effort to intervene in Restoration public debate through serial publication.

### *L'Organisateur*

A prospectus for *L'Organisateur* appeared in **August 1819**. Installments followed in late 1819 and 1820.

The publication proposed a reorganized political system centered on productive and intellectual capacity.

### Three-chamber organization

Saint-Simon imagined functional political bodies roughly corresponding to:

- invention/creative design;
- scientific examination;
- execution/industrial implementation.

This scheme is one major basis for the retrospective description of him as a **technocratic** thinker.

### The text later called the "Parable of Saint-Simon"

The famous **"Parabole de Saint-Simon"** originated as a passage in the first delivery of *L'Organisateur*.

It asked readers to imagine France suddenly losing its most capable:

- scientists;
- engineers;
- artists;
- manufacturers;
- bankers;
- artisans;
- other productive leaders.

France would be crippled.

By contrast, if the country lost large numbers of princes, grandees, courtiers, senior dignitaries, and socially prestigious nonproducers, Saint-Simon argued, the material damage would be much less.

The piece was not originally a standalone pamphlet titled *Parabole*. The label became canonical through later Saint-Simonian editing, especially Olinde Rodrigues's 1832 collection.

### Assassination of the duc de Berry and prosecutorial context

After the assassination of the **duc de Berry** in February 1820, Restoration authorities were highly sensitive to writing that appeared to threaten social elites.

The "Parable" became the basis for a prosecution.

### Trial

Saint-Simon defended himself in:

*Lettres de Henri Saint-Simon à MM. les jurés qui doivent prononcer sur l'accusation intentée contre lui* (1820).

The bibliographical history of *L'Organisateur* shows:

- a trial originally scheduled for March 1820;
- publication of his letters to the jurors and of defense material;
- an initial acquittal;
- continuing procedural/legal pressure over the defense publication itself.

A modern historical summary places his **acquittal at the Assize Court on 23 March 1820**.

### Legal status

There is no basis for describing Saint-Simon as convicted for advocating assassination.

**Documented:** prosecution and trial over politically provocative publication.  
**Outcome:** acquittal on the central accusation.

### Subscription finance

A **26 March 1820** circular sought "founder" subscriptions to keep *L'Organisateur* alive, illustrating his recurrent model of intellectual finance: publications funded through networks of subscribers and patrons rather than stable institutional income.

### June 1820 arrest

Larousse also reports a **brief arrest in June 1820** connected with protest against the law of the "double vote."

This episode is separate from the *L'Organisateur* prosecution and is less often mentioned in short biographies.

---

## 1821–1822: *Du système industriel*

Saint-Simon's multi-part *Du système industriel* appeared across **1821–1822**.

Its core argument was that the decisive social fact of the modern age was the rise of productive industry and that political institutions needed to be reconstructed around it.

### Government and administration

Saint-Simon increasingly subordinated "politics" in the traditional sense to administration, production, and expertise.

The famous formula about replacing "government of men" with "administration of things" is often quoted as if it were a single canonical sentence by Saint-Simon.

The textual situation is more complicated:

- Saint-Simon used closely related formulations contrasting government of persons with administration of things and direction of productive operations.
- Later Saint-Simonians sharpened the doctrine.
- **Friedrich Engels** made the formula famous in later socialist literature.
- Scholarship sometimes explicitly says that Engels "lends" the definitive formula to Saint-Simon.

**Conclusion:** the concept is genuinely Saint-Simonian and substantially present in Saint-Simon's writings, but the most polished aphoristic form should not automatically be treated as a verbatim standalone Saint-Simon quotation.

### The state

It is therefore misleading to say simply that Saint-Simon wanted "no state."

He wanted to transform coercive-political rule into productive-administrative coordination. Depending on the text, this could imply an extremely powerful coordinating apparatus run by experts, producers, and intellectual elites.

This ambiguity later made him usable both by:

- critics of the state;
- theorists of planning and technocratic administration.

---

## 1823–1824: Suicide Attempt, *Catéchisme des industriels*, and Rupture with Comte

### Suicide attempt: 9 March 1823

On **9 March 1823**, Saint-Simon attempted suicide by shooting himself in the head.

He survived but lost the sight of one eye.

**Documented core:** date, firearm suicide attempt, survival, loss of one eye.

A popular modern story says he fired six bullets into his head. This spectacular detail circulates widely, but it is not necessary to the documented event and should not be repeated as fact without a securely traced primary source.

### Context

Sources connect the attempt with:

- extreme poverty;
- frustration at the limited impact of his writings;
- personal exhaustion;
- despair.

A modern psychiatric diagnosis cannot responsibly be made from these facts.

### Olinde Rodrigues

After the suicide attempt, mathematician and financier **Olinde Rodrigues** became one of Saint-Simon's most important supporters.

Rodrigues helped materially sustain him and later became indispensable to the preservation and propagation of his works.

### *Catéchisme des industriels*

The *Catéchisme des industriels* appeared in **1823–1824** in multiple cahiers.

Its catechism form systematized the identity of the "industrial" class and the principles by which productive society should be organized.

### Who counts as an industrial?

Again, the term was broad. It did not mean only capitalist factory owner.

The category encompassed people who contributed productively to society, though Saint-Simon frequently assigned leadership to the most capable and knowledgeable members of that broad productive world.

### Collaboration problem

The work must not be cited indiscriminately as if every cahier were solely written by Saint-Simon. The third cahier is especially tied to Comte's contribution.

---

## 1824–1825: Last Circle and *Nouveau Christianisme*

### Léon Halévy

After Comte's departure, **Léon Halévy** became Saint-Simon's final secretary in 1824–1825.

Halévy later published recollections of Saint-Simon in 1832. They are a major source for anecdotes but must be read as posthumous memory.

### Final religious turn

Saint-Simon's last major work was:

**_*Nouveau Christianisme. Dialogues entre un conservateur et un novateur*_ (1825).**

It is often misdescribed as an abandonment of his earlier industrial philosophy.

A better reading is that he tried to supply the industrial order with a moral-religious principle.

### Religion in the final work

Saint-Simon stripped Christianity toward an ethical-social core centered on improving the condition of the poorest class.

He criticized existing churches for failing to fulfill what he considered Christianity's social mission.

This was not a simple return to orthodox Catholicism.

### From Newtonian religion to New Christianity

Across his career, religion changed form but never disappeared from his political imagination:

1. **early 1800s:** scientific/Newtonian quasi-religion;
2. **middle period:** positive science of society and industrial organization;
3. **late period:** a renewed Christianity made into a social principle.

Science, social organization, and spiritual authority were repeatedly entwined.

### Publication and death

*Nouveau Christianisme* appeared in **April 1825**, only weeks before Saint-Simon's death on **19 May**.

The work was unfinished in the larger sense: it opened a religious-social program that his disciples would radicalize after his death.

## Death / Later Life / Present Status

### Last address and death

Correspondence-based modern research places Saint-Simon's death at approximately **10 p.m. on 19 May 1825**, at **9 rue du Faubourg-Montmartre in Paris**.

He died with only a small immediate circle compared with the size of the movement that would soon bear his name.

### Last conversations and succession

Saint-Simon's late collaborators, especially **Olinde Rodrigues**, interpreted themselves as charged with continuing his work.

Posthumous recollections turned the deathbed into a succession scene. The historical core is that Rodrigues and others immediately began planning a vehicle to propagate the doctrine.

### Funeral, 22 May 1825

The funeral was:

- civil rather than ecclesiastical;
- held at Père-Lachaise;
- attended by a small circle;
- reportedly unattended by his family.

Sources name combinations of:

- Olinde Rodrigues;
- Auguste Comte;
- Augustin Thierry;
- Prosper Enfantin;
- Léon Halévy;
- Dr. Bailly and other associates.

Attendance lists vary among later accounts and should not be treated as an exact official roll unless backed by a contemporary funeral register or newspaper report.

### Contemporary press

Later cemetery and biographical sources say the death/funeral was noticed by:

- *Le Constitutionnel*;
- *Courrier des Pays-Bas*;
- *Le Globe*.

He was not a mass public celebrity at death.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Military institutions

- French royal army
- Touraine regiment
- Aquitaine regiment
- French forces in the Caribbean and American War of Independence
- Mézières military-engineering environment

### Revolutionary property enterprise with Redern

This was Saint-Simon's most substantial business activity.

**Partner:** Jean Sigismond Ehrenreich de Redern.

**Business:** acquisition and resale of *biens nationaux* — confiscated church and émigré properties.

**Finance:** relationship with the Perrégaux banking network.

**Result:** substantial temporary wealth followed by acrimonious division, loss, and litigation/claims.

### Stagecoach / transport enterprise

France Mémoire notes that Saint-Simon used part of his resources to launch enterprises including a **stagecoach company**.

This fits his persistent interest in transportation networks, though the surviving public summaries do not provide enough corporate detail to reconstruct incorporation, routes, capitalization, or duration.

### Canal and infrastructure proposals

Saint-Simon's project portfolio included:

- an **interoceanic canal** proposal in Spanish America/Mexico;
- Spanish canal/economic-development schemes related to connecting **Madrid to the sea**;
- interest in Dutch hydraulic works;
- later intellectual advocacy of large infrastructure systems as means of social integration.

### Notre-Dame metal-roof scheme

Modern Oxford scholarship on his early technocratic internationalism notes an extraordinary scheme associated with the Revolutionary/entrepreneurial period: using or selling metal from the roof of **Notre-Dame**.

Popular retellings sometimes connect this to **Talleyrand** and describe it flatly as a plan to buy the cathedral and strip the roof for scrap.

**Status:** a reported financial/project scheme in modern scholarship and later biography; details and Talleyrand's exact participation require care.

It belongs to the context in which confiscated ecclesiastical property had become an economic asset.

### Encyclopedic/scientific organization projects

Saint-Simon repeatedly proposed:

- a new encyclopedia;
- councils of scientists;
- scientific hierarchies;
- organized research;
- integration of scientific and social knowledge.

These were institutions conceived in print rather than stable organizations he successfully founded.

### Periodicals and serial publishing

His publishing ventures included:

- *L'Industrie*
- *Le Politique*
- *L'Organisateur*
- serial *Système industriel* and *Catéchisme* projects

These publications were organizational experiments as much as texts: they assembled editors, subscribers, patrons, secretaries, and intellectual contributors.

### Patents and inventions

No patent portfolio or clearly documented patented mechanical invention by Saint-Simon was identified in the sources consulted.

His technological importance lies primarily in:

- infrastructure proposals;
- organization of technical expertise;
- theorizing networks and industrial society;
- advocacy of large-scale technical systems.

## Wealth, Finances, Property, and Financial Relationships

### Family wealth

Saint-Simon was noble but not born into the richest branch of the Saint-Simon family.

Money was a recurrent problem even before the Revolution.

### Revolutionary fortune

The Revolution created his largest wealth opportunity through national-property speculation.

The scale was large enough to finance:

- a mansion;
- a large staff;
- extravagant hospitality;
- intellectual patronage;
- scientific self-education.

### Was he a millionaire?

Some later sources say he accumulated roughly one million in fortune.

**Status:** plausible order-of-magnitude tradition, not an audited figure established by the present research.

Currency units, revolutionary inflation, asset values, partnership ownership, and later recollection make apparently precise net-worth figures deceptive.

### Redern settlement

The financial break with Redern was decisive. Saint-Simon believed he had been unfairly deprived of the profits of their enterprise.

Older dedicated scholarship on the land speculation confirms the seriousness and complexity of the dispute.

### Consumption of capital

Saint-Simon also consumed wealth through:

- lavish living;
- salon maintenance;
- travel;
- support of intellectual projects;
- publishing;
- failed or unrealized schemes.

Therefore his impoverishment cannot be explained solely as theft by Redern.

### Poverty

By the late 1800s/early 1810s he experienced real poverty.

Reported sources of survival included:

- work as a copyist;
- assistance from Diard;
- a family pension;
- subscriptions;
- patronage from Ternaux and others;
- later support from Rodrigues.

### Intellectual financing model

Saint-Simon repeatedly tried to convert ideas into funded institutions by appealing to:

- bankers;
- industrialists;
- scientists;
- aristocrats;
- governments;
- subscribers.

This pattern foreshadows the Saint-Simonian movement's later deep engagement with finance and infrastructure.

## Political / Religious / Intellectual / Artistic Development

### 1. Enlightenment noble reformer

The young Saint-Simon inherited:

- noble military culture;
- Enlightenment universalism;
- fascination with science;
- the era's belief in rational improvement.

### 2. American revolutionary interpretation

He later treated American independence as a revelation that an old military-feudal civilization was yielding to industrial liberty.

This retrospective interpretation may exaggerate how fully formed the idea was in the 1780s, but it genuinely became part of his mature self-understanding.

### 3. Revolutionary anti-feudalism

The French Revolution destroyed the corporate-legal order into which he had been born.

Saint-Simon accepted:

- abolition of hereditary privilege;
- nationalized property redistribution;
- social mobility;
- a new property-owning order.

Yet he never became an egalitarian democrat in the modern sense.

### 4. Positive/scientific phase

From approximately 1798 he sought a new science capable of organizing all knowledge.

He treated society as a field susceptible to:

- observation;
- historical law;
- physiological analogy;
- rational organization.

### 5. Industrialism

From the Restoration onward, "industry" became the central category.

Society should be organized by those who do useful work rather than those who inherit status or monopolize political ceremony.

### 6. Historical theory: organic and critical epochs

Saint-Simon distinguished periods of:

- **organic** social coherence;
- **critical** dissolution.

The French Revolution was necessary as a destructive/critical transition, but endless criticism could not create a stable new society.

A new organic order required:

- a shared intellectual system;
- productive organization;
- moral authority.

### 7. "Law of three stages" question

Later scholarship notes striking anticipations of the three-stage historical schema usually associated with Auguste Comte.

Saint-Simon distinguished theological/feudal, transitional critical/metaphysical, and positive/industrial tendencies in forms that influenced Comte.

**Priority status:** Saint-Simon clearly supplied important antecedents; Comte systematized the "law of three states" into his own philosophical system. Exclusive ownership claims oversimplify collaborative intellectual development.

### 8. Technocracy — retrospective but useful

Saint-Simon died before "technocracy" became a standard political term.

Calling him a technocrat is therefore **retrospective**.

The label nevertheless identifies genuine features:

- rule/coordination by technical competence;
- scientists and engineers as social organizers;
- politics converted into administration;
- functional chambers rather than traditional estates;
- large-scale planning.

### 9. Socialism — retrospective and contested

Saint-Simon did not belong to a socialist party and died before later socialism acquired its mature meanings.

He was canonized as one of the great **"utopian socialists"** especially by **Marx and Engels**.

But he also:

- valued bankers and industrial entrepreneurs;
- defended hierarchy of capacity;
- did not advocate simple worker ownership in his own lifetime;
- imagined productive elites directing society.

This explains why scholarship can locate him simultaneously in the ancestry of socialism, managerial capitalism, technocracy, sociology, and economic liberalism.

### 10. Religion

Saint-Simon's system remained structurally religious even when it exalted science.

He repeatedly sought:

- a spiritual authority;
- a socially unifying creed;
- a hierarchy of intellectual/moral leadership;
- ritualized symbolic legitimacy.

*Nouveau Christianisme* made that structure explicit.

### 11. Art

Artists appear in his schemes as a major social force because they can:

- imagine the future;
- mobilize feeling;
- communicate social purposes.

The Saint-Simonians later developed this into the idea of the avant-garde, helping give the word a modern artistic-political career.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### 1. Revolutionary profiteering / speculation

**Documented fact:** Saint-Simon speculated in confiscated national property and made substantial money.

**Interpretive dispute:** whether to call this ordinary legal investment, revolutionary profiteering, *agiotage*, or predatory speculation.

The activity existed in a rapidly changing legal and monetary environment. His business relationship with Redern and Perrégaux is documented in historical studies. No consulted source establishes a criminal fraud conviction arising from these transactions.

### 2. "Bande noire" characterization

Some later accounts associate national-property buyers who dismantled estates for resale materials with the **bande noire** phenomenon.

Saint-Simon is sometimes rhetorically placed in this world.

**Status:** useful contextual analogy; not evidence that he belonged to a formal criminal organization called a "Black Band." The phrase was a contemporary/later social label for speculative demolition networks, not necessarily a single incorporated conspiracy.

### 3. Notre-Dame roof scheme

The reported idea of monetizing Notre-Dame's metal belongs to the same controversial property context.

**Documented status:** reported scheme.  
**Not established:** that Saint-Simon actually executed a stripping of Notre-Dame or was convicted over it.

### 4. Revolutionary arrest

**Documented:** arrest and imprisonment under the Terror.

**Not established:** a proven counterrevolutionary crime.

Dautry's formulation of detention as a general-security measure is important.

### 5. Redern dispute

**Documented:** major business and financial dispute.

Later retellings sometimes say Redern "stole" Saint-Simon's fortune.

**Status:** Saint-Simon's grievance / simplified later narrative. The surviving history supports an acrimonious and disadvantageous settlement for Saint-Simon, not a simple judicial finding of theft.

### 6. 1820 prosecution

**Documented:** criminal prosecution over *L'Organisateur* / the text later called the Parable.

**Outcome:** acquittal on the central accusation.

### 7. Political surveillance / arrest in 1820

Larousse reports a brief June 1820 arrest over protest against the double-vote law.

No broader conspiracy conviction was found.

### 8. Authorship controversies

Some works published in Saint-Simon's orbit were drafted by or contain substantial contributions from:

- Augustin Thierry;
- Auguste Comte;
- other collaborators.

Nineteenth-century collected editions blurred these distinctions.

Modern critical editing is essential before using any passage as evidence of Saint-Simon's personal wording.

### 9. Race

Saint-Simon's early writings contain racial hierarchy, including a claim about Black intellectual inferiority grounded in the physiology of his era.

This is **documented text**, not hostile propaganda.

Later standard editions omitted at least one relevant note/passage, contributing to a sanitized intellectual portrait.

### 10. Colonialism

His European plans included colonial expansion and civilizational hierarchy.

This is likewise part of the documentary record and should be retained when discussing his "Europeanism."

### 11. Elitism and democracy

Saint-Simon advocated abolition of hereditary privilege but did not advocate modern political equality in all respects.

Authority in many schemes belongs to:

- the scientifically competent;
- productive leaders;
- property-holding or educated elites.

The common equation "enemy of aristocracy = democrat" does not fit him.

### 12. Women

The mature Saint-Simonian movement became famous for debates over the emancipation of women.

These doctrines were developed especially **after Saint-Simon's death**, and Saint-Simon's own expert institutions were often imagined through male elite categories.

The later movement's feminism should not be projected backward wholesale onto him.

### 13. Sexual libertinism

Modern biographical descriptions, including Pierre Musso's publisher characterization, sometimes call Saint-Simon "libertin."

Older sources describe numerous liaisons and lavish social life.

**Status:** broad reputation with anecdotal support; a detailed catalog of lovers cannot be responsibly reconstructed from the public sources consulted.

### 14. No evidence found for modern-style corruption/money laundering/intelligence crimes

No documentary evidence was found in the consulted sources for:

- money laundering in the modern legal sense;
- organized criminal conspiracy;
- espionage conviction;
- bribery conviction;
- embezzlement conviction.

His life did involve speculation, political suspicion, wartime capture, imprisonment, and prosecution, but those should not be inflated into unsupported criminal categories.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Myth 1: Direct descendant of Charlemagne

**Story:** Saint-Simon descended directly from Charlemagne and inherited a world-historical mission.

**Where it appears:** family genealogy, Saint-Simon's self-conception, later biographies.

**Who circulated it:** Saint-Simon himself and later Saint-Simonian/biographical traditions.

**Evidence:** the claimed medieval genealogical bridge is doubtful; modern scholarship rejects a secure Carolingian descent.

**Effect on reputation:** it gave Saint-Simon a dynastic mythology even as he rejected hereditary political privilege. It helped him transform aristocratic ancestry into prophetic authority.

---

### Myth 2: Charlemagne appeared to him in Luxembourg prison

**Story:** while imprisoned during the Terror, Charlemagne appeared and told Saint-Simon that his philosophical fame would equal Charlemagne's political greatness.

**Earliest status:** Saint-Simon's own later autobiographical/dedicatory narrative.

**Evidence for:** Saint-Simon reported the story.

**Evidence against / limits:** no independent witness can verify a private vision.

**Historical significance:** exceptionally high. It condenses his lifelong fusion of aristocratic genealogy, revolutionary rupture, prophecy, and intellectual mission.

---

### Myth 3: d'Alembert personally educated him

**Story:** the Encyclopedist d'Alembert was Saint-Simon's tutor.

**Circulation:** standard nineteenth- and early twentieth-century biographies.

**Evidence:** Saint-Simon associated his education with d'Alembert's direction.

**Against literal version:** no firm documentation of sustained personal tutoring was located; later historians have explicitly doubted the literal reading.

**Reputational effect:** places Saint-Simon in a direct apostolic succession from the Enlightenment.

---

### Myth 4: Rousseau personally taught him

**Story:** Rousseau was a direct teacher or intimate formative guide.

**Evidence:** weak.

**Historical significance:** illustrates how later biographies compressed broad Enlightenment influence into personal-contact legends.

---

### Myth 5: The servant's morning command

**Story:** his valet woke him daily with a reminder that he had great things to accomplish.

**Evidence:** appears in later biographies and encyclopedia tradition, not in a contemporary childhood record located here.

**Effect:** perhaps the single most effective anecdote for representing his grandiosity and self-appointed mission.

---

### Myth 6: He "invented socialism"

**Story:** Saint-Simon was the founder of socialism in the modern doctrinal sense.

**Origin/circulation:** especially nineteenth-century socialist genealogy; Engels helped canonize Saint-Simon, Fourier, and Owen as "utopian socialists."

**Evidence for:** he profoundly influenced socialist thought; centered productive classes; attacked hereditary privilege and idleness; subordinated politics to economic organization; ended with a doctrine centered on the poorest.

**Against literal form:** "socialism" as a mature named ideological family postdates much of his work; he valued bankers and industrial entrepreneurs and defended hierarchy of function.

**Effect:** made Saint-Simon a canonical pre-Marxian socialist while obscuring his compatibility with managerial capitalism and technocracy.

---

### Myth 7: He was simply a capitalist prophet

**Story:** Saint-Simon was fundamentally a prophet of organized capitalism, not socialism.

**Evidence for:** strong role for entrepreneurs, bankers, industrialists, productive hierarchy, credit, investment, infrastructure.

**Against reduction:** he attacked inherited idleness, sought reorganization of property and power, increasingly prioritized the poor, and inspired movements explicitly socialist.

**Effect:** a counter-myth to socialist appropriation.

---

### Myth 8: He invented technocracy

**Story:** Saint-Simon founded technocracy.

**Evidence for:** expert rule, scientific councils, engineering governance, administration replacing conventional politics.

**Limit:** the word and twentieth-century technocracy movement postdate him.

**Effect:** allows modern technological-governance debates to claim him as ancestor.

---

### Myth 9: The exact phrase "administration of things instead of government of men" is a verbatim Saint-Simon slogan

**Story:** Saint-Simon coined one polished aphorism in precisely the form repeated today.

**Evidence:** Saint-Simon wrote closely related formulations, and the concept is authentically Saint-Simonian.

**Complication:** later Saint-Simonians and Engels standardized/popularized the memorable wording. Some scholarship explicitly says Engels attributed the definitive formula to Saint-Simon.

**Effect:** a complex corpus became condensed into one maxim.

---

### Myth 10: Saint-Simon designed the Suez Canal

**Story:** Saint-Simon directly conceived or designed the Suez Canal.

**Evidence for:** he was obsessed with canals and global infrastructure; Saint-Simonian disciples later became central advocates of great isthmian and railway projects, including the Suez idea.

**Against:** his own early interoceanic proposal concerned Spanish America/Mexico, and his Spanish schemes concerned Madrid/Atlantic connectivity. The organized Suez campaign belonged especially to the **posthumous Saint-Simonian movement**, not to a Saint-Simon engineering plan for the canal ultimately built by Ferdinand de Lesseps.

**Effect:** retrospectively merges founder and disciples into one infrastructure genealogy.

---

### Myth 11: Saint-Simon was the direct author of every text in the old collected works

**Story:** passages appearing in the nineteenth-century *Œuvres* can all be cited as Saint-Simon's words.

**Evidence against:** modern bibliography identifies Thierry and Comte contributions embedded within the corpus.

**Effect:** distorted priority debates in sociology, positivism, and socialism.

---

### Myth 12: He was either mad or purely rational

Two opposite mythologies coexist.

**Hostile mythology:** eccentric aristocrat, megalomaniac, quasi-mad prophet.

**Hagiographic mythology:** pure scientific rationalist who logically foresaw the industrial future.

The documentary life contains elements feeding both:

- grandiose self-mythology;
- a prison vision;
- extravagant schemes;
- a suicide attempt;
- but also decades of serious reading, publication, collaboration, and remarkably prescient analysis of infrastructure and industrial organization.

No single psychiatric or rationalist label exhausts the evidence.

## Works by Saint-Simon

### General bibliographic warning

Saint-Simon's bibliography is difficult because:

- early pamphlets survive in rare forms;
- titles vary between issues and later collections;
- periodicals were collaborative;
- Thierry and Comte drafted material in Saint-Simon's projects;
- disciples republished extracts under new titles;
- posthumous collected editions mixed Saint-Simon and Enfantin;
- later editors sometimes silently omitted material;
- the 2025 correspondence identifies pseudonymous Revolutionary pieces not part of the old standard bibliography.

The **2012 critical *Œuvres complètes*** and **2025 *Correspondance*** should be treated as the modern starting points for attribution.

### Revolutionary and early material

#### Two pseudonymous Revolutionary texts, 1790 and 1795
- Identified/published in the appendices to Pierre Musso's 2025 correspondence edition.
- The public publisher description confirms the existence and dates of these texts but does not provide sufficient bibliographic detail to reproduce their full titles confidently here.
- **Status:** genuine Saint-Simon texts newly integrated into the corpus.

#### *À la Société du Lycée* — probably March 1802
- Approximately eight pages.
- Identified by later bibliographical scholarship as the earliest known printed Saint-Simon pamphlet.
- Rediscovered by Jean Dautry.
- **Status:** genuine; rare.

#### *Lettres d'un habitant de Genève à l'humanité* — 1802
- Early version/state of the Genevan project.
- **Status:** genuine.

#### *Lettres d'un habitant de Genève à ses contemporains* — 1802/1803
- The most famous early publication.
- Later bibliographies disagree between 1802 and 1803 because of edition/state history.
- Scientific-spiritual reorganization around an elite of savants and artists.
- **Status:** genuine.

### Scientific and encyclopedic phase

#### *De l'organisation sociale, lettre au conseiller d'État Fourcroy* — 1804
- Listed in major biographical bibliographies.
- **Status:** genuine attribution in standard corpus.

#### *Introduction aux travaux scientifiques du dix-neuvième siècle* — 1807
- BnF catalog: Paris, J.-L. Scherff, 1807.
- **Status:** genuine.

#### *Lettres au Bureau des longitudes* — early nineteenth-century scientific phase
- Indexed in institutional library catalogs of Saint-Simon's works.
- Exact issue history should be checked against the 2012 critical edition before quotation.

#### *Esquisse d'une nouvelle Encyclopédie* / encyclopedia projects — c. 1809–1811
- Part of the effort to reorganize knowledge.
- Some material survives as fragments and later editorial groupings.

#### *Histoire de l'homme* — c. 1810–1811
- Includes an *Avertissement* dated 1810 in later scholarly collections.
- **Status:** genuine material, complex publication history.

#### Writings later grouped as *De la physiologie sociale* — c. 1812 onward
- "Physiologie sociale" is also an editorial category in later collections.
- Do not assume every modern volume title was a standalone title issued by Saint-Simon.

#### *Mémoire sur la science de l'homme* — 1813
- Manuscript material survives in French archival collections.
- A 6 November 1813 Saint-Simon letter to Duméril is cataloged with related material.
- **Status:** genuine.

#### *Travail sur la gravitation universelle* — early 1810s
- Part of the scientific-system corpus.
- **Status:** genuine attribution in complete works; publication-state details should be taken from critical apparatus.

### European and political reorganization

#### *De la réorganisation de la société européenne, ou De la nécessité et des moyens de rassembler les peuples de l'Europe en un seul corps politique, en conservant à chacun son indépendance nationale* — 1814
- Saint-Simon and Augustin Thierry.
- **Authorship:** Saint-Simon's project; substantial drafting/contribution by Thierry.
- **Status:** collaborative, not safely "Saint-Simon alone."

#### *Vues sur la propriété et la législation* — 1814-era Restoration corpus
- Listed in standard biographical accounts.
- **Status:** genuine attribution; consult critical edition for exact publication form.

### Industrial periodicals

#### *L'Industrie, ou Discussions politiques, morales et philosophiques, dans l'intérêt de tous les hommes livrés à des travaux utiles et indépendants* — 1816–1818
- Irregular serial/cahiers.
- **Authorship:** collaborative; contains material by Augustin Thierry and others.
- **Status:** Saint-Simon-directed publication, not all words his.

#### *Le Politique* — 1819
- Serial political intervention.
- **Status:** Saint-Simonian publication project with collaborative context.

#### *L'Organisateur* — 1819–1820
- Prospectus August 1819; deliveries through 1820.
- Includes passage later known as the "Parable."
- **Status:** genuine Saint-Simon-directed publication; bibliographical complexity across issues.

#### "Parabole de Saint-Simon" — 1819 text, posthumous title
- The famous passage first appeared within *L'Organisateur*.
- The standalone title *Parabole* is a later editorial label, made canonical by Olinde Rodrigues and other posthumous editors.
- **Status:** genuine underlying passage; title/history requires distinction.

#### *Lettres de Henri Saint-Simon à MM. les jurés qui doivent prononcer sur l'accusation intentée contre lui* — 1820
- Connected to the prosecution over *L'Organisateur*.
- Includes defense material.
- **Status:** genuine; legal-publicistic text.

#### *Circulaire relative à l'Organisateur* — 26 March 1820
- Subscription/funding circular.
- **Status:** documentary publishing artifact.

### Mature industrial system

#### *Du système industriel* — 1821–1822
- Multi-part/cahier publication.
- **Status:** major genuine Saint-Simon corpus, with normal collaborative/editorial cautions.

#### *Catéchisme des industriels* — 1823–1824
- Multiple cahiers.
- **Authorship warning:** the third cahier is substantially associated with Auguste Comte and must not be cited indiscriminately as Saint-Simon's sole text.
- **Status:** Saint-Simon-directed project with major collaborative authorship issue.

#### *Opinions littéraires, philosophiques et industrielles* — 1825
- Connected with Saint-Simon's last collaborative circle.
- **Status:** collective/collaborative publication context; consult critical edition before assigning individual passages.

### Final religious work

#### *Nouveau Christianisme. Dialogues entre un conservateur et un novateur* — 1825
- Paris: Bossange père / A. Sautelet et Cie in surviving bibliographic records.
- Published shortly before death.
- **Status:** genuine Saint-Simon work; foundational for the religious phase of the posthumous movement.

### Autobiographical fragments

#### *Histoire de ma vie* / fragments of his life
- Saint-Simon left autobiographical materials rather than a fully published, continuous lifetime autobiography.
- Olinde Rodrigues's 1832 collection printed *Fragmens de l'histoire de sa vie écrite par lui-même*.
- **Status:** genuine autobiographical material, but posthumously selected/edited.

### Correspondence

#### *Correspondance (1782–1825)* — edited by Pierre Musso, Manucius, 2025
- Posthumous scholarly edition.
- First letter in the volume: 1782, from the young officer in the American-war period.
- Last: 1825, to a collaborator.
- Includes many letters previously unknown and two Revolutionary pseudonymous texts.
- **Status:** modern scholarly edition of genuine correspondence/material; not a book published by Saint-Simon in his lifetime.

### Major posthumous collected editions

#### Olinde Rodrigues, ed., *Saint-Simon* — 1832
Full title begins: *Saint-Simon: Son premier écrit; Lettres d'un habitant de Genève à ses contemporains, 1802; sa Parabole politique, 1819; Le nouveau christianisme, 1825; précédés de Fragmens de l'histoire de sa vie écrite par lui-même*.
- Paris: Librairie Saint-Simonienne.
- **Status:** disciple-edited posthumous selection; historically crucial in forming the canonical Saint-Simon.

#### *Œuvres de Saint-Simon & d'Enfantin* — 1865–1878, 47 volumes
- Monumental Saint-Simonian edition.
- Includes historical notices, Saint-Simon's works, Enfantin's works, doctrine, sermons, and trial material.
- **Status:** posthumous disciple corpus; indispensable but not a modern critical edition.

#### *Œuvres complètes* — PUF, 2012, 4 volumes
Edited by **Juliette Grange, Pierre Musso, Philippe Régnier, Franck Yonnet**.
- **Status:** principal modern critical edition.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

## 1825–1826: Immediate organization

After the funeral, Rodrigues and associates moved quickly to continue the project.

They founded **Le Producteur**, a journal devoted to philosophy, industry, science, and the arts.

This marks the transition from:

- **Saint-Simon's thought**
to
- **Saint-Simonianism as an organized movement**.

The distinction is essential.

## 1828–1830: Doctrine becomes a school

**Saint-Amand Bazard** delivered systematic expositions of the doctrine.

The school developed ideas more explicit than Saint-Simon's own in areas such as:

- planned organization of production;
- inheritance;
- social association;
- historical stages;
- gender;
- religious hierarchy.

## Enfantin and the Saint-Simonian "church"

**Prosper Enfantin** became one of the movement's "Pères" / supreme fathers.

The movement developed:

- rituals;
- ranks;
- distinctive clothing;
- communal life;
- a search for a female messianic counterpart;
- the doctrine of the rehabilitation of flesh;
- public scandals and prosecutions.

These developments became part of the mythology of "Saint-Simon," even though Saint-Simon himself had died before they occurred.

## Women and feminism

The Saint-Simonian movement became an important site for early nineteenth-century debates on women's emancipation.

Women associated with or responding to Saint-Simonianism developed their own newspapers, writings, and critiques.

This legacy is historically connected to Saint-Simon but should be credited primarily to the **posthumous movement and its women**, not projected backward as a complete doctrine of the founder.

## Finance and banking

Former Saint-Simonians became major actors in nineteenth-century finance.

The **Pereire brothers**, for example, helped create new forms of industrial credit and large financial institutions.

Saint-Simon's idea that bankers could coordinate productive society found a concrete afterlife here.

## Railways and infrastructure

Saint-Simonian networks were deeply involved in:

- French railways;
- engineering schools;
- industrial development;
- communication systems;
- infrastructure finance.

Figures associated with the broad Saint-Simonian orbit included engineers and entrepreneurs such as:

- Paulin Talabot;
- Émile Clapeyron;
- Gabriel Lamé;
- Stéphane Mony;
- Michel Chevalier;
- the Pereire brothers.

## Suez

Enfantin and Saint-Simonian engineers promoted the idea of linking seas and reorganizing world commerce through great works.

The Suez Canal later became one of the most powerful symbols of Saint-Simonian infrastructural imagination.

**Caution:** Ferdinand de Lesseps's successful canal enterprise should not be reduced to "Saint-Simon's project," nor should every person involved be treated as a direct disciple.

## Algeria and colonialism

Some Saint-Simonians became active in the French colonization and economic organization of **Algeria**.

This posthumous imperial practice has roots in the founder's own willingness to combine European organization, technical development, and colonial hierarchy.

## Positivism

Auguste Comte carried forward and transformed:

- positive social science;
- historical stages;
- spiritual reorganization;
- scientific hierarchy.

The exact division of intellectual property became a long controversy.

## Sociology

Saint-Simon became a recurrent ancestor in histories of sociology.

**Durkheim** devoted major attention to him, helping establish a genealogy from Saint-Simon through Comte to modern social science.

## Socialism and Marxism

**Engels** treated Saint-Simon, Fourier, and Owen as the great pre-Marxian "utopian socialists."

Engels admired in Saint-Simon:

- perception of class forces;
- recognition that political institutions rest on economic organization;
- historical breadth.

Marxist classification also fixed the contrast:

- "utopian" socialism based on projects and elite persuasion;
- "scientific" socialism based on material class development.

That schema had enormous influence on Saint-Simon's reputation.

## Liberal and capitalist appropriations

Saint-Simon has also been read as:

- a prophet of industrial capitalism;
- an advocate of credit and banking;
- a theorist of entrepreneurial leadership;
- a precursor to management science.

These readings are not simply falsifications. His "industrial" order included capitalists and bankers as productive actors.

## Hayek and anti-technocratic criticism

**F. A. Hayek**, in *The Counter-Revolution of Science*, placed Saint-Simon within a genealogy of scientism: the attempt to transfer methods or prestige of natural science into social planning and administration.

This became a major hostile interpretation of Saint-Simon's legacy.

## Foucault and later critical genealogy

Later twentieth-century critics sometimes placed Saint-Simon in genealogies of:

- biopolitics;
- managerial social organization;
- expert power;
- modern governmental rationality.

Such chains are interpretive genealogies, not evidence that Saint-Simon directly caused later regimes.

## Network society

Pierre Musso's work has emphasized Saint-Simonian thinking about:

- networks;
- circulation;
- communication;
- industrial organization.

The founder's canal and infrastructure imagination becomes, in this reading, an ancestor to modern network ideology.

## European integration

Saint-Simon is periodically revived as an early thinker of European political union.

The 1814 text undeniably warrants inclusion in histories of European federation.

But modern celebration often omits:

- elite suffrage assumptions;
- Anglo-French leadership;
- colonial expansion;
- racial hierarchy.

## "Utopian" reputation

Saint-Simon rarely described detailed communal architecture in the manner of Fourier.

His "utopia" was often procedural and institutional:

- reorganize knowledge;
- reorganize credit;
- reorganize production;
- assign authority by capacity;
- build networks;
- create a new spiritual principle.

This helps explain why he could influence both utopian movements and highly practical bankers and engineers.

## Major Historical Interpretations: A Documentary Map

### Saint-Simon as "utopian socialist"
**Promoters:** Marxist and socialist historiography, especially Engels.  
**Basis:** producer class, anti-idleness, historical stages, social reorganization, poor-centered final ethics.  
**Limitation:** underplays bankers, hierarchy, and entrepreneurial leadership.

### Saint-Simon as technocrat
**Promoters:** twentieth-century political theory and histories of expertise.  
**Basis:** scientists, engineers, administrative rule, functional political chambers.  
**Limitation:** term is anachronistic and can understate his religious dimension.

### Saint-Simon as founder of sociology
**Promoters:** sociological genealogies, Durkheimian and positivist histories.  
**Basis:** science of society, historical law, organic/critical phases, influence on Comte.  
**Limitation:** sociology did not yet exist as the later institutional discipline.

### Saint-Simon as industrial capitalist prophet
**Promoters:** histories of industrialism, banking, management, network society.  
**Basis:** productive elites, entrepreneurs, banks, infrastructure, credit.  
**Limitation:** underplays his attack on inherited property/power and final concern for the poor.

### Saint-Simon as religious prophet
**Promoters:** Saint-Simonians themselves and historians of political religion.  
**Basis:** Newtonian cultic language, spiritual power, *Nouveau Christianisme*.  
**Limitation:** risks projecting Enfantin's later church backward onto the founder.

### Saint-Simon as megalomaniac eccentric
**Promoters:** hostile and picturesque biography.  
**Basis:** Charlemagne vision, grand schemes, expensive salon, prophetic self-conception, suicide attempt.  
**Limitation:** can trivialize a large documentary intellectual corpus and real historical influence.

### Saint-Simon as liberal
**Promoters:** recent scholarship emphasizing markets, industrial freedom, anti-feudalism, and civil society.  
**Basis:** hostility to hereditary privilege, productive freedom, business networks.  
**Limitation:** his elite administrative order is not reducible to classical laissez-faire liberalism.

## Chronology

### 1760
- **17 October:** born in Paris into the Rouvroy de Saint-Simon family, Sandricourt branch.

### 1760s–1770s
- Private education.
- Later claims connect his education to d'Alembert; direct sustained tutoring remains unproven.
- Childhood partly associated in later biographies with Falvy/Picardy.

### 1775 / 1777
- Military career begins. Sources disagree; Saint-Simon's own retrospective chronology and modern specialist reconstruction favor **1777**, while one military reference gives **1775**.

### 1779
- **3 June:** captain rank in military-reference chronology.
- **14 November:** transfer to Touraine infantry.
- Sails from Brest into Caribbean theater.

### 1780
- Campaigning in Caribbean; military-reference tradition connects him with Barbados operations.

### 1781
- Joins de Grasse expedition to North America.
- **September–October:** participates in Yorktown siege; reportedly commands regimental gunners.
- **4 November:** leaves Virginia with de Grasse's fleet.

### 1782
- **February:** participates in siege of Brimstone Hill, St. Kitts.
- **12 April:** captured after Battle of the Saintes.
- Prisoner of war in Jamaica.
- Earliest letter in the 2025 correspondence dates from 1782.

### 1783
- Released following peace.
- Presents/advocates an interoceanic canal project to the viceroy of Mexico.
- Returns to Europe.
- Father Balthazar-Henri dies.

### 1784
- **1 January:** appointment as *mestre de camp en second* in Aquitaine regiment, according to military reference.
- Associated with Mézières and engineering/mathematical study.

### 1785
- Travels to the Netherlands; observes canals/hydraulic techniques in later accounts.

### 1787–1789
- Spanish economic and canal-development projects.
- Connected with the Madrid-to-sea / Guadarrama development environment.

### 1788
- Military-reference chronology gives colonel attachment to cavalry.
- Relationship with Redern develops.

### 1789
- French Revolution begins.
- Saint-Simon accepts the new order rather than emigrating.

### 1790
- Uses Revolutionary identity.
- Later awarded/holds chevalier status in Order of Saint-Louis according to military reference.
- One pseudonymous Revolutionary text from 1790 identified in 2025 correspondence edition.

### 1790–1793
- Large-scale acquisition/resale of *biens nationaux* with Redern.
- Financial relationship with Perrégaux.
- Uses name **Claude Bonhomme**.
- Later literature reports revolutionary playing-card project.

### 1793
- Arrested during Terror.
- Imprisoned first at Sainte-Pélagie.

### 1794
- **May:** transferred to Luxembourg prison in modern specialist reconstruction.
- Later self-narrative places Charlemagne vision in prison.
- **27 July / 9 Thermidor:** fall of Robespierre.
- **28 August:** preferred modern release date.

### 1795
- Returns to large-scale financial/social life.
- By October, correspondence-based reconstruction places him in a Paris mansion near Palais-Royal with a large household.
- A second pseudonymous Revolutionary text from 1795 identified in 2025 correspondence edition.

### 1797–1799
- Partnership with Redern collapses into dispute.
- Saint-Simon progressively turns from speculation to intellectual project.
- Settlement/disposition of property interests leaves him much less wealthy.

### 1798
- Self-declared scientific/intellectual reorientation.
- Studies physics, mathematics, medicine, physiology.

### 1801
- **7 August:** marries Alexandrine-Sophie Goury de Champgrand in Paris.
- Salon experiment.

### 1802
- Marriage dissolves/divorce follows.
- *À la Société du Lycée*, probably March.
- *Lettres d'un habitant de Genève à l'humanité*.
- First states of Genevan-letter project.
- Travels associated with Geneva/Europe.

### 1803
- Conventional date often assigned to *Lettres d'un habitant de Genève à ses contemporains*.

### 1804
- *De l'organisation sociale*, letter to Fourcroy, in standard bibliography.

### 1807
- *Introduction aux travaux scientifiques du dix-neuvième siècle*.
- Poverty deepens; later tradition says he works as a copyist at Mont-de-Piété.

### 1809–1811
- New encyclopedia and *Histoire de l'homme* projects.
- Diard provides material support.
- Diard dies around 1810 in standard biographies, worsening Saint-Simon's condition.

### 1812–1813
- Physiology/social-science writings.
- *Mémoire sur la science de l'homme*.
- **6 November 1813:** letter to Duméril preserved/cataloged with manuscript material.
- Period of illness and severe poverty.

### 1814
- Collaboration with Augustin Thierry.
- *De la réorganisation de la société européenne*.
- European parliamentary/federal project, also connected to colonial hierarchy.

### 1815
- Traditional account: supports Benjamin Constant during Hundred Days and briefly accepts Arsenal library appointment.
- **Disputed:** Saint-Simon later claimed he had accepted no governmental office since 1789.

### 1816
- *L'Industrie* begins in December.

### 1817
- Auguste Comte becomes secretary/collaborator.
- "Industrial" class becomes central concept.

### 1818
- *L'Industrie* publication cycle ends in May.
- Mature historical theory of critical/organic phases develops.

### 1819
- *Le Politique*.
- August: prospectus for *L'Organisateur*.
- November: first deliveries and the passage later called the "Parable."

### 1820
- Political prosecution over *L'Organisateur*.
- March: *Lettres ... à MM. les jurés*.
- **23 March:** acquittal on central charge in modern historical summary.
- **26 March:** funding/subscription circular.
- June: reported brief arrest over double-vote law protest.

### 1821
- *Du système industriel* begins/continues.
- Letter to Robin Scévole contains retrospective account of American war and claim of not taking post-1789 government office.

### 1822
- *Du système industriel* continues.
- Saint-Simon–Comte conflict intensifies.

### 1823
- **9 March:** suicide attempt by gunshot; survives and loses sight in one eye.
- Olinde Rodrigues becomes crucial supporter.
- *Catéchisme des industriels* begins.

### 1824
- *Catéchisme* continues.
- Break with Auguste Comte completed.
- Léon Halévy becomes final secretary.

### 1825
- **April:** *Nouveau Christianisme*.
- **19 May, about 10 p.m.:** death in Paris, 9 rue du Faubourg-Montmartre.
- **22 May:** civil funeral at Père-Lachaise.
- Associates move toward creating *Le Producteur*.
- Last letter in the 2025 correspondence dates from this year.

### 1825–1826
- *Le Producteur* propagates the developing Saint-Simonian doctrine.

### 1828–1830
- Bazard's doctrinal lectures and *Exposition de la doctrine de Saint-Simon*.
- Saint-Simonian school becomes increasingly organized and religious.

### 1829–1832
- Enfantin/Bazard leadership, *Le Globe*, quasi-church organization, gender controversy, Ménilmontant community, state prosecutions.

### 1832
- Olinde Rodrigues publishes canonical posthumous Saint-Simon selection with autobiographical fragments, Genevan letters, "Parable," and *Nouveau Christianisme*.
- Léon Halévy publishes early recollections.

### 1833

### 1865–1878
- Monumental 47-volume *Œuvres de Saint-Simon & d'Enfantin* begins publication, consolidating the disciple canon.

- Engels's *Socialism: Utopian and Scientific* fixes Saint-Simon in the canonical triad of utopian socialism with Fourier and Owen.

## Research Conclusions Without Editorial Overlay

The documentary record supports the coexistence of the following without requiring them to be reconciled into a single ideological label:

- Saint-Simon was an aristocrat who repudiated hereditary political privilege but retained habits of hierarchy and personal grandeur.
- He fought in the American War of Independence and later made that war part of his own intellectual origin story.
- He was a large-scale Revolutionary property speculator who became rich from the liquidation of church and émigré property.
- He was imprisoned during the Terror without a demonstrated counterrevolutionary conviction.
- He lost or consumed most of his fortune and lived through severe poverty.
- He conceived unusually large infrastructure and administrative projects.
- He pursued scientific authority while repeatedly using religious structures and prophetic symbolism.
- He advocated rule by productive and scientific capacity more readily than rule by undifferentiated popular sovereignty.
- He condemned hereditary idleness while treating bankers, manufacturers, engineers, scientists, and workers as parts of one productive order.
- His texts include racial hierarchy and colonial assumptions that later celebratory accounts often omit.
- His work profoundly influenced socialism, but the later Saint-Simonian movement added doctrines not fully present in his own writings.
- His thought influenced Auguste Comte, while important works from their collaborative period cannot be cleanly assigned without critical textual work.
- He attempted suicide in 1823, survived with loss of one eye, and continued writing.
- He died with a small circle in 1825; within a few years his name had become the center of an organized intellectual-religious movement.
- His afterlife in banking, railways, Suez advocacy, positivism, sociology, socialism, technocracy, European federalism, and network ideology is substantially larger than his public fame during his lifetime.

## Atlas Connections

### Adam Smith

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Saint-Simon identified himself with the political economy of Adam Smith and Jean-Baptiste Say and used their analysis of productive activity when developing his industrial doctrine. The precise extent of his direct reading is uncertain; scholarship suggests he may have encountered Smith principally through Say rather than through *The Wealth of Nations* itself. [University Jean Monnet Saint-Simonian economics archive](https://www.univ-st-etienne.fr/fr/gate-lse/liens-utiles/saint-simonisme-et-economie/saint-simonisme-et-economie-textes-economiques-saint-simoniens.html), [contemporary historical discussion](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Le_Fondateur_du_socialisme_moderne_-_Saint-Simon)

### Alexander Hamilton

- **[T4]** **Documented fact:** Hamilton and Saint-Simon were both serving with the allied army during the siege of Yorktown, Virginia, in October 1781. Hamilton commanded the American assault on Redoubt No. 10, while Saint-Simon served with the French Touraine regiment in the same siege. No evidence located establishes a personal meeting between them. [National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/york/learn/historyculture/hamiltonbio.htm)

### Apostle Paul

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Saint-Simon discussed Pauline Christianity in *Nouveau Christianisme* (1825), explicitly invoking the Epistle to the Romans while evaluating established Christian doctrine and its social application. This is textual engagement, not evidence of any claimed visionary encounter. [Text of *Nouveau Christianisme*](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Nouveau_Christianisme)

### Charlemagne

- **[T2- unresolved]** **Rumor or myth / Saint-Simon’s self-report:** Saint-Simon related that Charlemagne appeared to him during his imprisonment in the Luxembourg prison in 1794 and foretold that Saint-Simon would achieve in philosophy a distinction comparable to Charlemagne’s in politics. The apparition and prophecy are unverifiable, while the alleged fulfillment—Saint-Simon’s eventual intellectual stature—is inherently interpretive. The story nevertheless helped construct his prophetic identity and may have reinforced his sense of mission. [Richard Swedberg’s scholarly discussion](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-journal-of-sociology-archives-europeennes-de-sociologie/article/saintsimons-vision-of-a-united-europe/74C837C79926FA67080067F3DF4553FF)

### Friedrich Hayek

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Hayek devoted a substantial historical critique to Saint-Simon in the essays collected as *The Counter-Revolution of Science* (1952). He presented Saint-Simon as a formative representative of scientism, social engineering, hierarchical expert government, and the common ancestry of positivism and modern planning. This is a later writer’s sustained engagement with Saint-Simon, not a personal crossing. [Text of Hayek’s study](https://www.mises.at/static/literatur/Buch/hayek-the-counter-revolution-of-science.pdf)

### George Washington

- **[T4]** **Documented fact:** Washington and Saint-Simon occupied the same military theater during the Franco-American siege of Yorktown in September–October 1781. Saint-Simon’s Touraine regiment belonged to the French contingent integrated into the allied siege commanded by Washington and Rochambeau. Surviving Washington records prominently concern Saint-Simon’s older relative and commander Claude-Anne de Rouvroy; they do not establish a personal meeting between Washington and Claude-Henri. [George Washington’s October 1781 diary and editorial notes](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Washington/01-03-02-0007-0006), [National Park Service campaign history](https://www.nps.gov/york/learn/historyculture/history-of-the-siege.htm)

### Jesus of Nazareth

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Saint-Simon’s *Nouveau Christianisme* (Paris, 1825) reinterpreted the religion attributed to Jesus as a social ethic whose central obligation was human fraternity and improvement of the poorest class. Saint-Simon accepted Christianity’s divine origin while sharply reducing its essential doctrine and criticizing the historical churches’ implementation of it. [Text of *Nouveau Christianisme*](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Nouveau_Christianisme), [scholarly analysis](https://www.persee.fr/doc/assr_0003-9659_1965_num_20_1_1783)

### Joseph-Louis Lagrange

- **[T5]** **Reported fact:** Biographical sources report that, during Saint-Simon’s Directory-era scientific re-education and salon activity in Paris, he gathered scholars including Gaspard Monge and Lagrange around him. This supports personal social contact, although the accessible account does not document particular conversations or dates and therefore does not justify describing them as close friends or formal collaborators. [Catholic Encyclopedia biography](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/13377a.htm)

### Karl Marx

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Marx repeatedly wrote about Saint-Simon and Saint-Simonianism. With Engels, he discussed Saint-Simon’s life and industrial theory in *The German Ideology* (1845–1846), while the *Communist Manifesto* (1848) placed the systems of Saint-Simon, Fourier, and Owen within the early history of “Critical-Utopian Socialism and Communism.” This was retrospective intellectual classification; the two men could not have met because Marx was seven years old when Saint-Simon died. [*The German Ideology*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1845/german-ideology/ch04c.htm), [*Communist Manifesto*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/61/61-h/61-h.htm)

### Marquis de Lafayette

- **[T4]** **Documented fact:** Lafayette and Saint-Simon served in the same allied campaign at Yorktown in 1781. Lafayette commanded American forces in Virginia and then a division during the siege; Saint-Simon arrived with the French Caribbean contingent and served in the Touraine regiment. Later summaries sometimes say they “fought alongside” one another, but the evidence located supports shared campaign proximity rather than a documented friendship or specific personal meeting. [National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/york/learn/historyculture/lafayette-and-the-virginia-campaign-1781.htm)

### Napoleon Bonaparte

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Saint-Simon wrote about Napoleon in his scientific and political writings of the imperial period. His early-1810s *Mémoire sur la science de l’homme* and related material praised Bonaparte’s creation or support of scientific and educational institutions, while Saint-Simon treated Napoleonic power as a possible vehicle for intellectual and social reorganization. There is no securely documented personal meeting in the sources examined. [Catholic Encyclopedia biography](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/13377a.htm), [FranceArchives manuscript record](https://francearchives.gouv.fr/fr//facomponent/df7dcde5a956407d1a4b1134d7879f916988f18e)

### Sir Isaac Newton

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Newton was the central historical figure in Saint-Simon’s *Lettres d’un habitant de Genève* (1802–1803). Saint-Simon imagined Newton occupying the place nearest God in a scientific spiritual order and proposed a transnational “Council of Newton” composed of leading scientists and artists. The connection is entirely posthumous and textual: Newton died in 1727, decades before Saint-Simon’s birth. [Stanford’s Saint-Simon exhibit](https://exhibits.stanford.edu/super-e/feature/claude-henri-de-rouvroy-de-saint-simon-1760-1825), [BnF authority and works record](https://data.bnf.fr/fr/see_all_activities/11923405/page1)
