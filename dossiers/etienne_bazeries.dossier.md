# Étienne Bazeries: Research Dossier

## Scope, method, and evidentiary cautions

This dossier reconstructs the documented life and work of French army officer and cryptanalyst Étienne Bazeries (1846–1931). The surviving record is uneven. His military chronology can be recovered in considerable detail from his French Légion d’honneur file (Archives nationales, LH//150/51); his cryptologic career is best documented by his own publications, contemporary proceedings, and later histories by David Kahn, Christopher Andrew, Rosario Candela, William F. Friedman, and specialist historians. His private life, finances, and final illness are poorly documented in accessible sources.

Three recurring claims require particular care:

1. **The Great Cipher:** Bazeries did break the Rossignol family’s principal Louis XIV nomenclator. That achievement is documented and technically reconstructable.
2. **The Man in the Iron Mask:** Bazeries’s claim that the cipher revealed the prisoner to be General Vivien de Bulonde depends on his conjectural reading of two otherwise unattested code groups, especially `330 = masque`. The Great Cipher solution is real; the Bulonde identification is not established and is rejected by most later specialists.
3. **The Panizzardi telegram in the Dreyfus affair:** later literature often states that Bazeries personally solved or mistranslated it. The documentary position is less certain. Bazeries was placed at the Foreign Ministry in September 1894, but David Kahn found no surviving record proving that he participated in the telegram’s solution and thought his later account too sketchy to establish participation. Christopher Andrew attributes some faulty Quai d’Orsay work of the period, probably including the Panizzardi error, to Bazeries. Both positions are preserved below.

Dates transcribed from handwritten military papers are marked as such. Where the handwriting or later accounts conflict, the competing readings are given rather than silently harmonized.

### Name and rank

No pseudonym is documented. “Commandant” in French sources is both a rank-address and a durable authorial label. His Légion file calls him *chef d’escadron en retraite* when promoted to Officier. English writers sometimes translate *chef d’escadron* as “major.” Bazeries published principally as “Commandant Bazeries.”

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and origins

Bazeries was born in the Mediterranean port of Port-Vendres, near the Spanish border. David Kahn, drawing partly on material later deposited in the Légion file, describes his father as a mounted policeman. The handwritten civil-status sheet in LH//150/51 identifies Bazeries as a son of an Étienne Bazeries and a mother whose surname appears to be **Salle** or **Sallé**; the faded script does not permit a fully confident transcription from the online scan. No reliable deeper ancestry has been located.

The family intended him for agriculture. According to Kahn, Bazeries enlisted five days after his seventeenth birthday specifically to escape the agricultural future planned for him. The source does not establish whether the family owned land or expected him to work for others.

### Marriage

He married **Marie-Louise-Élodie Berthon** in 1876 after returning from his first Algerian tour. His service sheet gives the marriage date as **26 April 1876**; the locality is difficult to read in the digital scan and should be checked against the original civil record before being treated as certain. Some secondary summaries give 1877, but both Kahn and the military papers support 1876.

### Children

The couple had three daughters:

- **Césarine Bazeries**
- **Fernande Bazeries**
- **Paule Bazeries**

Their birth dates, marriages, and later lives were not established in the sources consulted. In 1913 Bazeries bought a house at Céret, reportedly because one daughter needed a healthier climate. The daughter is not identified in the principal published account.

### Household life

Kahn preserves a small domestic portrait of Bazeries at Céret: his wife and daughters walking behind the bearded, cane-swinging officer on excursions to upland farms; Bazeries speaking Catalan to farmers and trying to persuade the daughters to appreciate Roussillon wine, which his wife disliked. This is biographical anecdote, not a contemporaneous diary account, but Kahn’s inquiries and correspondence concerning Bazeries survive in the Légion file.

No spouse other than Marie-Louise-Élodie Berthon, no extramarital partner, and no son is documented. No reliable evidence of the family’s religion or Bazeries’s personal religious practice was found.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1846–1863

Bazeries grew up in Port-Vendres, then a small fishing and military-commercial port in French Catalonia. Kahn states that he learned Catalan together with French. No school, diploma, private tutor, or formal mathematical training has been identified. Later descriptions consistently present him as self-taught in cryptology and weak in formal education. Christopher Andrew, using Quai d’Orsay evidence, calls him ill-educated and ignorant of foreign languages; this is consistent with, but harsher than, the absence of documented advanced schooling.

His formation was practical rather than academic: bilingual border-country childhood, early army service, horse and supply work, and later recreational solving of newspaper cryptograms. No evidence places him at the École Polytechnique, Saint-Cyr, or another officer academy.

## Education and Intellectual Formation

### Formal education

No advanced formal education is documented. His commission came through promotion from the ranks rather than an elite military school.

### Self-education through newspaper ciphers

Bazeries later said, and Kahn reports, that he developed his interest by solving encrypted notices in newspaper personal columns. Some concealed adulterous appointments. He reportedly entertained fellow officers by revealing their contents in the mess. This activity trained the habits that defined his later work: rapid hypothesis-building, probable-word attacks, linguistic pattern recognition, and relentless testing rather than formal mathematical theory.

### Influences and contemporaries

His cryptologic environment included:

- **Auguste Kerckhoffs**, whose principles of military cryptography—including the principle that a system should remain secure even if the apparatus is known—shaped the era.
- **Gaëtan de Viaris**, aristocratic mathematician and cryptographer, both professional peer and technical antagonist.
- **Félix Delastelle**, tobacco administrator and inventive cryptographer.
- **Paul Valério**, military author on cryptanalysis.
- **General Charles-Alexandre Fay**, the senior officer who tested and then supported Bazeries.
- **Édouard Lucas**, mathematician who presented Bazeries’s cylindrical cryptograph to the 1891 French Association for the Advancement of Science congress at Marseille.

Bazeries’s practice was strongest in natural-language cryptanalysis and weakest where scientific detachment required him to accept that an opponent had broken one of his own designs.

## Military Career and Chronological Life History

## 1846–1870: Enlistment and rise from the ranks

Bazeries was born on 21 August 1846. On **26 August 1863**, five days after turning seventeen, he enlisted as a volunteer for seven years. Kahn identifies his first unit as the **4th Supply Squadron** (*4e escadron du train des équipages militaires*). The Légion file records his progression:

- Volunteer enlisted: 26 August 1863.
- Noncommissioned officer: 14 August 1866.
- Re-enlisted for seven years without bounty: 3 March 1870.

The train arm handled transport, animals, materiel, and logistical support. This was neither an academic commission nor a staff appointment; Bazeries learned soldiering from the ranks.

## 1870–1871: Franco-Prussian War, capture, escape, Loire campaign

His archival campaign summary records service as a noncommissioned officer in the **2nd Corps of the Army of the Rhine**, from 21 July to 27 October 1870. He was captured following the fall/capitulation of Metz. The service paper gives captivity from **28 October to 6 November 1870**, followed by escape and return on **7 November**. Kahn adds the memorable detail that he escaped disguised as a bricklayer or mason.

He then served with the **Army of the Loire** from 7 November 1870 to 7 March 1871. He was commissioned **sous-lieutenant on 13 October 1870**—a date that falls during the Metz crisis and is preserved in his later service résumé. The administrative date and the practical circumstances of assuming the commission may not have coincided.

His record also lists interior service at **Lyon, 30 April–12 May 1871**, during the final phase of the Commune-era disturbances. The accessible papers do not specify his actions. No allegation of abuse or disciplinary proceeding arising from this service was found.

## 1871–1877: Officer advancement and first Algerian tour

Bazeries became lieutenant on **26 April 1874**. His campaign résumé records African service approximately **8 May 1875–9 February 1876**; this was the first of three Algerian tours described by Kahn.

The Légion file records a wound or injury “in commanded service” on **19 January 1876**, but the nature, severity, and circumstances are not legible in the available summary. It should not be embellished into a combat wound without further documentation.

He returned to France and married Marie-Louise-Élodie Berthon in 1876. He became captain on **12 January 1877**.

## 1878–1888: Extended African service and conventional military advancement

The archival campaign résumé records service as a captain in Africa from **19 March 1878 to 6 June 1882**, credited as five campaigns. His detailed unit list is difficult to read in the scan but confirms repeated assignments in train squadrons and administrative/logistical roles.

In later life Bazeries became known almost entirely through cryptology, but by the late 1880s he had accumulated more than two decades of army service, including war, captivity, escape, and extensive North African duty. He was appointed **Chevalier of the Légion d’honneur by decree of 28 December 1888**, while described as a captain in the train arm. The file does not isolate cryptologic work as the sole reason; length and character of military service formed the award context.

## 1890: Nantes demonstrations against the official cipher

In 1890 Bazeries was stationed at Nantes at the headquarters of the **11th Army Corps**. He told fellow officers that the official French military cipher—a complex transposition system—could be read without the key. The claim was initially greeted with laughter. General **Charles-Alexandre Fay**, the corps commander, supplied test cryptograms. Bazeries solved them. When the system was strengthened and tested again, he read the new messages before the replacement system entered general service.

This episode established his reputation as an operational cryptanalyst. It also created the recurring conflict of his career: he could repeatedly demonstrate defects in official systems, but his own proposed replacements did not satisfy the War Ministry.

## 1891: Bord challenge, Foreign Ministry assignment, and cylinder proposal

On **8 January 1891**, an inventor from Nantes named **Bord** sent Bazeries eight messages produced by a printing cryptograph that Bazeries himself called “a jewel of an instrument.” By 31 January, after progressively strengthened challenges, Bazeries had read the decisive message: “I want to be hanged if you decipher this.” Bazeries advised Bord not to carry out the penalty and observed that if everyone whose cipher had been solved were hanged, hanging would lose its significance.

On **12 February 1891**, with Fay’s support, Bazeries submitted his cylindrical cryptograph to the Ministry of War. The device used twenty removable disks, each bearing a differently mixed alphabet of twenty-five letters. Disk order supplied part of the key; a plaintext row was aligned across the cylinder and another row read as ciphertext.

On **19 September 1891**, mathematician Édouard Lucas presented the device at the twentieth congress of the Association française pour l’avancement des sciences in Marseille. The published description appeared as “Cryptographe à 20 rondelles-alphabets (25 lettres par alphabet),” *Comptes rendus*, vol. XX, pp. 160–165.

In **August 1891**, the Army placed Bazeries temporarily at the disposal of the **Bureau du Chiffre of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs**. His handwritten service résumé gives 22 August 1891 for this assignment. This began the period in which he operated between the Army, Quai d’Orsay, archival research, and police consulting.

## 1891–1893: Historical ciphers and the Great Cipher

Commandant **Louis Gendron** of the General Staff, researching Marshal Nicolas de Catinat’s campaigns, had found five ciphered dispatches from Louis XIV and two from War Minister Louvois that other cryptologists had failed to read. In 1891 he gave them to Bazeries.

The corpus contained **12,125 number groups** and a repertory reaching into the high 500s. Bazeries first considered digraphic encipherment—numbers representing letter pairs—but failed. He then inferred that most groups represented French syllables, mixed with letters, whole words, nulls, and punctuation. He counted frequencies: Kahn reports `22` 187 times, `124` 185 times, `42` 184 times, `311` 145 times, and `125` 127 times. Without a syllable-frequency table, he guessed frequent French elements and searched for repeated military phrases.

His decisive probable phrase was **les ennemis**. Repeated sequences such as `124-22-125-46-345`, with variations in the last and middle groups, suggested syllabic values and homophones. By inserting and extending these tentative values, he reconstructed a two-part nomenclator of **587 equivalents** for letters, syllables, words, nulls, and punctuation. One sign erased the preceding sign. The method and reconstructed tables were later published.

The solution took about three years in the standard account. Bazeries’s core cryptanalytic achievement—recognizing the syllabic structure and rebuilding the Great Cipher—is accepted.

## 1892: Anarchist cryptograms and promotion

French authorities arrested anarchists in 1892 and introduced cryptograms as evidence. Bazeries solved six messages enciphered in a Gronsfeld-type system. Kahn notes that nulls at the beginning and end delayed him for approximately a fortnight—an unusually long delay for him—and that Bazeries paradoxically regarded this comparatively routine solution as his best.

He became **chef d’escadron on 10 July 1892**. This promotion gave him the rank by which he was thereafter commonly known as Commandant Bazeries.

## 1893: Publication of the Bulonde theory and rejection of the cylinder

On **9 February 1893**, Bazeries resubmitted a simplified cylinder to the Military Cryptography Commission. The commission rejected it as too complicated. Bazeries believed that an officer whose own cipher he had broken was predisposed against it.

The same year, with **Émile Burgaud**, he published *Le Masque de fer: Révélation de la correspondance chiffrée de Louis XIV*. Most of the book argued that General **Vivien L’Abbé, seigneur de Bulonde**, was the Man in the Iron Mask. Its documentary appendix published Great Cipher material and the reconstructed tables.

Also in 1893, the Paris firm A. Hermann published *Tables chiffrantes et déchiffrantes, ou Chiffre Bazeries*, a four-part combined item including Bazeries tables and an application of A. Hermann’s secret cipher.

## 1893–1894: Constantine and another African campaign

The service résumé places him in command of the train at **Constantine** from late 1893, with a third African tour credited from **30 January to 3 July 1894**. On 29 September 1894 he was again placed at the disposal of the Minister of Foreign Affairs.

## 1894: Dreyfus affair and the Panizzardi telegram—documented uncertainty

On 2 November 1894, after Alfred Dreyfus’s arrest became public, Italian military attaché **Colonel Alessandro Panizzardi** telegraphed Rome. The message asked that, if Dreyfus had not had relations with the Italian service, the ambassador issue an official denial “to avoid press comment.” The Foreign Ministry’s first substantial decrypt was correct except at the end, which was rendered as “our emissary has been warned.” A corrected and accurate version followed on 10 November. The erroneous version reached Dreyfus’s judges; the correction did not.

The attribution to Bazeries must be separated into levels:

- **Documented:** Bazeries had been lent to the Foreign Ministry in September 1894.
- **Documented:** the Bureau du Chiffre made an initially erroneous, then corrected, solution of the Panizzardi telegram.
- **Unresolved:** the surviving records examined by Kahn did not say whether Bazeries was serving inside the bureau or personally participated. Kahn thought Bazeries’s brief discussion in *Les Chiffres secrets dévoilés* made participation seem unlikely.
- **Later scholarly attribution:** Christopher Andrew describes the faulty decrypt as among Bazeries’s errors and more broadly treats him as the Quai d’Orsay’s leading cryptanalyst. This likely rests on French diplomatic archives and institutional history, but it does not erase Kahn’s warning about the missing individual attribution.

Thus it is inaccurate to state without qualification either that “Bazeries exonerated Dreyfus and was suppressed” or that “Bazeries fabricated the incriminating ending.” The Bureau’s error and the judicial suppression of the correction are established; Bazeries’s exact hand remains disputed.

## 1896: Napoleon’s 1813 ciphers

Bazeries published *Les “Chiffres” de Napoléon pendant la campagne de 1813: Épisodes du siège de Hambourg*, a 57-page study first appearing in the Fontainebleau newspaper *L’Abeille de Fontainebleau*. His quotation marks around *chiffres* expressed contempt for the weakness of Napoleon’s field systems. The work drew on documents at Aix-la-Chapelle, the Archives nationales, and the Dépôt de la guerre.

## 1898: Fashoda-era error and Orléanist conspiracy traffic

### Fashoda decrypt

Christopher Andrew reports that on 30 September 1898 an Italian diplomatic telegram was partly misread, probably by Bazeries, as stating that the British ambassador had instructions to issue an ultimatum over Major Marchand’s withdrawal from Fashoda and threaten war. No such ultimatum existed. Foreign Minister Théophile Delcassé reacted emotionally to the supposed threat and afterward mistook its non-delivery for a diplomatic triumph. Andrew treats this as a major example of policy distorted by insufficiently qualified cryptanalysis.

### Orléanist messages

Police seized encoded messages in the quarters of **Chevilly**, a supporter of Philippe, Duke of Orléans and claimant to the French throne. The traffic used four-digit groups between 1111 and 3737, a paired-number representation of letters, polyalphabetic keys, and—in one set—successive lines of Alfred de Musset’s *La Nuit de décembre*. Another set used the day and date as a key. Bazeries eventually deduced “the key to the keys.”

Among the traffic was a garbled incoming message that even the duke could not decipher, followed by the duke’s short reply of 13 December 1898. After removing seven null `q` characters, Bazeries read the meaningful word: **Merde**. He later testified about these solutions before the High Court of Justice during proceedings against the conspirators.

## 1899: Retirement from the active army and final War Ministry rejection

Bazeries retired from the active army on **18 March 1899**, after approximately 35 years, 6 months, and 22 days of active service according to his résumé. He entered the territorial army as commander of the 1st territorial train squadron on 31 May 1899 and was returned to civilian life at his own request on 29 February 1904.

The Foreign Ministry retained him as a cryptanalyst after army retirement. He worked partly at home and partly in the office, spending substantial time at Versailles.

His last major paper-and-pencil military proposal combined:

- a message-specific monoalphabetic substitution derived from a two-letter numerical key;
- reversal of three-letter groups;
- optional vowel nulls.

On **19 April 1899**, the War Ministry rejected it because it did not provide sufficient security. Kahn judges this rejection technically correct: a monoalphabetic substitution could not remain secure under heavy traffic, even when combined with such transposition.

## 1899–1901: Police consulting, Sûreté relationship, and polemical book

Andrew reports that the Sûreté employed Bazeries part-time on a **300-franc monthly retainer**, initially for anarchist correspondence and later diplomatic work. This is the one specific non-salary financial arrangement found in the sources.

In 1901 Bazeries published his principal book, *Les Chiffres secrets dévoilés*. It was simultaneously:

- a survey of historical and contemporary cryptography;
- a report of his solutions of anarchist and Orléanist traffic;
- an argument for his own cipher systems;
- an attack on the General Staff’s cryptographic routine and “willful blindness”;
- a sharply personal review of competing writers.

He wrote that official claims of progress were actually retreat and called weak military ciphers a public danger. He accused figures such as Josse and de Viaris of “heresy” when they disagreed. Kahn considers the book’s personality and readability central to its enduring place in the literature, while noting its polemical and episodic character.

## 1904–1905: Japanese diplomatic code failure and German successes

At the beginning of the Russo-Japanese War, Sûreté agents obtained fragments concerning a Japanese diplomatic code. Bazeries examined them for a week and declared reconstruction impossible. Police commissaire **Haverna** then took over and, with an assistant, spent two months reconstructing almost the entire Japanese codebook—reported as about 1,600 pages with 100 lines per page. French police thereafter read Tokyo–Paris embassy traffic and shared copies with Russian authorities.

This was a clear Bazeries failure and a clear Haverna success. Capsule biographies often invert or blur the episode by saying Bazeries helped “break Japanese codes.”

The Quai d’Orsay did, however, begin regularly reading some German diplomatic traffic from 1904. The surviving institutional record does not assign every solution individually to Bazeries.

## 1907–1908: Promotion in the Légion d’honneur

Bazeries was promoted **Officier of the Légion d’honneur by decree of 30 December 1907**. His file records dispatch of the decoration and brevet in January 1908. The promotion sheet identifies him as a retired chef d’escadron.

## 1913: House at Céret

In 1913 he bought a house in **Céret**, not far from Port-Vendres, seeking a healthier environment for one daughter. He worked there during later years, sometimes receiving cipher material from Paris. Kahn’s account says he hired illiterate servants when working on secret documents, sustained long attacks on ciphers with pipes and coffee, and recovered with excursions in the nearby mountains. This operational-security anecdote comes from later biographical reconstruction and should not be treated as proof that every servant was illiterate or that the practice was permanent.

## 1914–1918: First World War work

Although retired from the army, Bazeries assisted in solving German military cryptograms during the First World War. The open sources consulted do not give a complete message-by-message inventory or establish that he led the wartime Section du Chiffre. France’s wartime cryptanalytic organization included younger figures such as Georges Painvin and a more institutionalized structure than the ad hoc world in which Bazeries had risen.

Claims that Bazeries personally broke every major German system of the war are unsupported. His contribution was real but insufficiently itemized in accessible records.

## 1919–1924: Final working years

Bazeries continued assisting the cipher services after the armistice and did not retire fully from cryptanalytic work until **1924**, aged 78. No detailed list of his postwar assignments has been found.

## 1924–1931: Final retirement and death

The evidence for his final seven years is sparse. He died at **Noyon on 7 November 1931**, aged 85. His Légion file and authority records confirm the date; later biographical accounts specify Noyon. No reliable cause of death, final illness, hospitalization, addiction treatment, or deathbed account was found.

## Cryptanalytic Work, Technologies, and Projects

## The Great Cipher of the Rossignols

### Nature of the system

The “Grand Chiffre” was a two-part nomenclator associated with Bonaventure Rossignol and the cryptographic dynasty that served Louis XIII and Louis XIV. Bazeries reconstructed a table of 587 values representing letters, syllables, words, nulls, punctuation, and special operations. Multiple code groups could represent the same plaintext element, frustrating simple frequency analysis.

### Bazeries’s method

His attack combined:

1. counting all 12,125 groups;
2. ranking their frequency;
3. inferring syllabic rather than alphabetic structure;
4. guessing recurrent military language, especially *les ennemis*;
5. identifying homophones through variants of repeated sequences;
6. propagating tentative values through the corpus;
7. reconstructing separate encoding and decoding order.

### Achievement and limit

The general reconstruction was a major, verifiable success. It opened previously unreadable correspondence and supplied a historical case study in the solution of syllabic nomenclators. It did **not** make every group unambiguous, especially groups appearing only once.

## The cylindrical cryptograph / Bazeries cylinder

### Construction

- Twenty removable disks on a spindle.
- Twenty-five letters on each circumference, omitting one letter.
- A different mixed alphabet on each disk.
- Disk order determined by a key.
- Plaintext aligned on one generatrix; ciphertext taken from another.

### Submission and rejection

Submitted 12 February 1891; publicly described 19 September 1891; rejected as too complicated; simplified and resubmitted 9 February 1893; rejected again.

### De Viaris attack

Gaëtan de Viaris developed a valid method against the device when the cryptanalyst possessed an example of the apparatus. By aligning all `a` characters and tabulating the possible equivalents of a probable fragment such as `-ation`, he could infer disk identities and arrangements. Bazeries refused to acknowledge that de Viaris had successfully broken the principle as implemented. A later commentator quoted by Kahn described this as a failure of intellectual generosity in the face of a cherished theory’s collapse.

### Jefferson and M-94 relationship

Thomas Jefferson had described a similar “wheel cypher” about a century earlier, but Bazeries apparently did not know it. Parker Hitt later developed related strip and cylinder ideas, and Joseph Mauborgne refined the U.S. Army device adopted as the **M-94** in 1922. Historical accounts differ on whether Hitt directly borrowed from Bazeries or independently rediscovered the principle. William Friedman recalled that Hitt knew nothing of Bazeries; other summaries describe a Bazeries influence. What is secure is a technological lineage of closely related removable mixed-alphabet disks, not a Bazeries patent licensed to the United States.

### Patents and commercial exploitation

No Bazeries patent or company formed to manufacture the cylinder was found. A. Hermann published his tables, and Bazeries promoted the device, but the accessible record does not document royalties, investment income, or a manufacturing business.

## The paper-and-pencil “Bazeries cipher”

This is distinct from the cylinder. In its commonly described form:

1. a numerical key is spelled out;
2. repeated letters are removed to construct a mixed alphabet;
3. plaintext is divided into groups according to the digits of the key and reversed within groups;
4. a substitution alphabet is applied.

It combines transposition with monoalphabetic substitution. It is historically important and useful pedagogically but not secure by modern standards; the War Ministry’s 1899 security objection was substantially correct.

## Historical cipher projects

Bazeries reported solutions or reconstructions involving:

- Francis I;
- Francis II;
- Henry IV;
- Mirabeau;
- Napoleon I and the 1813 campaign;
- Louis XIV and the Rossignol nomenclators.

His habit was to move from current operational traffic into archives and to treat historical ciphers as live cryptanalytic problems rather than merely antiquarian objects.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Politics

Bazeries served successive French regimes as a professional soldier and intelligence specialist. His writings display forceful French patriotism and institutional criticism, not a developed party program. He worked against anarchists and Orléanist conspirators because of official assignments; this does not by itself establish a comprehensive personal ideology.

He was willing to challenge senior officers and bureaucracies. Kahn relates that as a lieutenant he told a general that the regimental harness injured the horses. The same disposition informed his cryptographic campaigns against official routine.

### Religion

No dependable evidence of his religious affiliation, observance, anticlericalism, or conversion was found.

### Intellectual style

Contemporaries and historians consistently describe:

- exceptional intuition in natural-language cryptanalysis;
- rapid generation of hypotheses from probable subject matter;
- persistence and intense concentration;
- limited formal theoretical contribution;
- strong patriotism;
- polemical confidence and personal combativeness;
- difficulty accepting valid attacks on his own designs.

Christopher Andrew’s account, based on diplomatic-service material, adds that he was alcoholic, a compulsive gambler, poorly educated, ignorant of foreign languages, and considered “un singulier bohème.” These are serious biographical claims from a scholarly intelligence history, apparently grounded in French archival descriptions; accessible independent medical, financial, or police records confirming the alcoholism and gambling were not found. They should therefore be treated as documented contemporary/research characterization rather than quantified clinical diagnoses.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### General Charles-Alexandre Fay

Fay tested Bazeries’s claim against official army ciphers, validated his success, and recommended his cylindrical cryptograph. He was the most important early patron identified.

### Commandant Louis Gendron

Gendron brought the Catinat/Louis XIV cryptograms to Bazeries and initiated the Great Cipher project. Some popular accounts call him an “historian”; he was a General Staff officer conducting historical military research.

### Émile Burgaud

Co-author of the 1893 Iron Mask book. Burgaud developed the historical argument; Bazeries supplied the cryptologic reconstruction. Later retellings sometimes misname him Louis Burgaud.

### Gaëtan de Viaris

Peer, rival, and effective cryptanalyst of Bazeries’s cylinder. Bazeries had broken systems associated with de Viaris; de Viaris answered by showing how the cylinder could be attacked. Their technical contest became personal and illustrates the gap between Bazeries the brilliant breaker and Bazeries the defensive inventor.

### Édouard Lucas

Presented the cylinder at Marseille in 1891 and may have contributed to practical choices in its use. The exact depth of collaboration is not known.

### Bord

Nantes inventor whose printing cryptograph Bazeries broke in January 1891. The available sources provide only the surname.

### Charles-Marie Darmet and the Bureau du Chiffre

Darmet headed the seven-person Foreign Ministry bureau when the Panizzardi telegram arrived. The bureau collectively solved the superenciphered commercial code; whether Bazeries was present is uncertain.

### Commissaire Haverna

Police cryptanalyst who succeeded in reconstructing Japanese diplomatic code material after Bazeries declared the task impossible. Haverna represents an important corrective to the legend that Bazeries invariably succeeded.

### Théophile Delcassé

Foreign minister and consumer of the Quai d’Orsay’s cryptanalytic product. A probably Bazeries-associated false decrypt misled him during the 1898 Fashoda crisis.

### Family

Marie-Louise-Élodie Berthon and daughters Césarine, Fernande, and Paule formed his stable household. No evidence suggests that they participated technically in his work.

## Controversies, Errors, and Adverse Material

## 1. Rejection of his cipher systems

**Documented fact:** the War Ministry repeatedly rejected his proposed systems.

**Competing interpretations:** Bazeries saw bureaucracy, routine, and personal hostility. Later cryptologic analysis agrees that the administration could be rigid but also finds that de Viaris could attack the cylinder as configured and that the 1899 monoalphabetic/transposition proposal was inadequate under heavy traffic.

## 2. Refusal to accept de Viaris’s solution

**Documented controversy:** de Viaris published a valid attack assuming possession of the apparatus. Bazeries insisted that a particular Marseille cryptogram remained unsolved and would remain so. Later commentators treated his response as intellectually ungenerous.

## 3. Panizzardi telegram

**Documented:** an erroneous interim decrypt was sent to military intelligence; a correct version followed; the wrong version was shown to Dreyfus’s judges while the correction was withheld.

**Disputed:** Bazeries’s personal role. Kahn found it unproven and perhaps unlikely. Andrew assigns the error within a pattern of Bazeries’s brilliant and mistaken hypotheses. Claims that Bazeries alone exonerated Dreyfus, or alone framed him, exceed the evidence.

## 4. Fashoda telegram

**Scholarly attribution:** Andrew reports a partially inaccurate Italian decrypt, probably produced by Bazeries, that falsely announced a British ultimatum. It distorted Delcassé’s understanding of the crisis. This is one of the clearest examples of harm caused by unqualified confidence in a plausible decrypt.

## 5. Japanese code reconstruction

**Documented failure in Andrew’s account:** Bazeries declared reconstruction impossible after a week. Haverna and an assistant substantially rebuilt the huge codebook in two months.

## 6. Alcohol and gambling

**Archival/scholarly characterization, not clinical record:** Andrew describes him as alcoholic and a compulsive gambler and quotes the description *un singulier bohème*. No accessible medical diagnosis, arrest, debt judgment, or family testimony was located. The claims belong in the dossier but should not be expanded beyond their source.

## 7. Temperament and polemics

His 1901 book attacks the General Staff and rival authors in sarcastic terms. This did not constitute a crime or formal scandal, but it shaped institutional resistance and his later reputation as both truth-teller and abrasive combatant.

## 8. Criminality, corruption, and litigation

No credible evidence was found that Bazeries committed fraud, corruption, money laundering, espionage for a foreign power, political violence, or other ordinary crime. He served intelligence and police institutions and testified in political-conspiracy proceedings, but that is not evidence of personal criminality. No lawsuit involving him was identified.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## Myth 1: Bazeries revealed the Man in the Iron Mask’s name

### What the story says

The story says that Bazeries decrypted a 1691 order to imprison General Vivien de Bulonde at Pignerol and allow him daytime exercise on the battlements “wearing a mask,” thereby identifying Bulonde as the Man in the Iron Mask.

### Appearance and circulation

The theory appeared publicly in 1893 in Émile Burgaud and Bazeries’s *Le Masque de fer*. It entered popular histories of cryptography and Iron Mask candidate lists and has been repeated in encyclopedias, popular books, classrooms, and documentaries.

### Evidence for it

- Bazeries genuinely decrypted the surrounding dispatch.
- Bulonde genuinely disobeyed orders at the siege of Cuneo/Coni and angered Louis XIV.
- The dispatch genuinely ordered his arrest and confinement at Pignerol.
- The sequence `330 309` occurs at the end of the disputed clause.

### Evidence against it

- `330` appears only once in the relevant corpus, so its value cannot be cross-checked.
- Bazeries conjectured `330 = masque`; it was not derived from repeated occurrences.
- A repertory of only 587 military values contains no regular code for the common word *ennemis*, making a dedicated value for the uncommon *masque* improbable.
- The syntax as reconstructed is awkward: critics argued Louvois would more naturally have written *en masque* rather than *avec un masque*.
- Bulonde was reportedly alive and at liberty in 1708 or died in 1709, whereas the famous masked prisoner died in 1703.
- Bulonde’s offense was public and gave no apparent reason for life-long identity concealment.
- The later prisoner’s known custody history under Saint-Mars does not fit Bulonde securely.
- David Kahn’s 2005 re-examination rejected the `masque` reading and reviewed alternatives such as a custodian/guard sense; the relevant original archival letter could no longer be located when he investigated.

### Historical effect

The claim made Bazeries famous outside cryptology. It fused a real technical triumph with a fragile historical inference. Popular memory often retains “Bazeries solved the Iron Mask” while losing the distinction between decoding the nomenclator and guessing one singleton value.

## Myth 2: The Great Cipher had remained continuously “sealed for two centuries”

The phrase is broadly true as popular shorthand: the Rossignol table was lost, the dispatches became unreadable, and Bazeries recovered them in the 1890s. It is not proof that every professional cryptanalyst had worked continuously on the same documents for two hundred years. The better formulation is that documents written in the abandoned system remained unreadable in the archives until Bazeries reconstructed it.

## Myth 3: Bazeries invented the cylinder first

Bazeries independently developed and publicized his cylinder in 1891. Thomas Jefferson had described a similar wheel cypher in the 1790s, and other antecedent multi-disk devices complicate priority further. There is no evidence Bazeries copied Jefferson. “Independent reinvention” is more accurate than either “first inventor” or “plagiarist.”

## Myth 4: The French Army rejected a perfect cipher and America simply adopted it

The French Army did reject Bazeries’s cylinder, and the U.S. Army later used the related M-94. But the cylinder as Bazeries built it was not perfect; de Viaris demonstrated an attack, mnemonic alphabet construction created patterns, and later U.S. development passed through Hitt and Mauborgne. The real story is independent invention, institutional resistance, technical modification, and eventual use of a related design—not direct adoption of an unaltered French device.

## Myth 5: Bazeries personally solved the Panizzardi telegram and proved Dreyfus innocent

This version conflates the Bureau du Chiffre’s collective work, the initial error, the later correction, and judicial misuse. Bazeries’s exact participation is unproven. The corrected telegram supported the absence of Italian contact but did not by itself end the case.

## Myth 6: The infallible “natural cryptanalyst”

The titles “Lynx of the Quai d’Orsay,” “Napoleon of Ciphers,” “Magician,” and “Champollion of codebreakers” encode a reputation for almost supernatural intuition. His verified successes justify an extraordinary reputation. The Panizzardi/Fashoda questions, Japanese failure, and defense of insecure personal designs show that intuition could produce both discoveries and confident errors.

Works by Georges Mongrédien, Jean-Christian Petitfils, Marcel Pagnol, and other Iron Mask historians discuss or reject the Bulonde hypothesis. They are about the larger mystery rather than biographies of Bazeries, but they are important to the reception of his most famous disputed claim.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Cryptanalysis

Bazeries is remembered as an exceptional intuitive cryptanalyst rather than a foundational theorist. Kahn’s formulation—“the great pragmatist of cryptology”—captures the consensus: small formal theoretical contribution, extraordinary practical gift.

### Historical cryptography

His Great Cipher reconstruction remains his most durable scholarly achievement. It demonstrated how frequency, probable language, homophones, and syllabic structure can recover an abandoned nomenclator without the original key.

### Machine and field ciphers

His cylinder became one of the canonical ancestors or independent instances of the multi-disk cipher family. Its relationship to Jefferson’s earlier manuscript and the later M-94 makes Bazeries central to histories of recurring invention in cryptographic machinery.

### Institutional lesson

His career illustrates two opposite intelligence failures:

- institutions that ignored accurate warnings about weak systems;
- institutions that accepted ingenious but uncertain decrypts without adequate confidence grading.

The same force of intuition that exposed official weakness could overreach when evidence was sparse.

### Public memory

Port-Vendres commemorates him with a plaque associated with its war memorial. Cryptologic museums display his cylinder and book. The Iron Mask claim remains the main route by which non-specialists encounter his name, even though it is the least secure part of his famous work.

### Reassessment

Later scholarship has moved from heroic summary toward a divided assessment:

- **secure successes:** official-cipher demonstrations, Great Cipher reconstruction, multiple historical and police solutions;
- **important designs:** cylinder and Bazeries cipher, though neither invulnerable;
- **institutional frustrations:** repeated rejection, some of it bureaucratic and some technically justified;
- **serious errors or disputed attributions:** Panizzardi, Fashoda, Japanese code;
- **unsupported legend:** definitive identification of Bulonde as the Man in the Iron Mask.

## Atlas Connections

### Julius Caesar

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Bazeries discussed substitution methods associated with Julius Caesar in the historical survey opening *Les Chiffres secrets dévoilés* (Paris, 1901), explicitly classifying “Jules César” under substitution cryptography. This is a later author writing about an ancient figure, not evidence of any stronger connection. [Bazeries, *Les Chiffres secrets dévoilés*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/0b/Les_chiffres_secrets_d%C3%A9voil%C3%A9s%2C_%C3%89tienne_Bazeries%2C_1901.pdf)

### Blaise de Vigenère

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** In *Les Chiffres secrets dévoilés* (1901), Bazeries described the *chiffre carré* as the “chiffre de Vigenère,” identified Blaise de Vigenère in a note, and analyzed methods bearing his name. The connection is textual and retrospective; no claim of direct intellectual transmission beyond Bazeries’s engagement with Vigenère’s cryptographic legacy is warranted. [Bazeries, *Les Chiffres secrets dévoilés*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/0b/Les_chiffres_secrets_d%C3%A9voil%C3%A9s%2C_%C3%89tienne_Bazeries%2C_1901.pdf)

### Louis XIV

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Beginning in 1891, Bazeries reconstructed the Rossignol Great Cipher from Louis XIV-era dispatches; with Émile Burgaud, he then wrote *Le Masque de fer: Révélation de la correspondance chiffrée de Louis XIV* (Paris, 1893), publishing decrypted royal correspondence and advancing the disputed Bulonde theory. The cryptanalytic reconstruction is documented; the identification of Bulonde as the masked prisoner remains a theory. [Digitized 1893 volume](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k1523532j)

### Napoleon Bonaparte

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Bazeries wrote *Les “Chiffres” de Napoléon pendant la campagne de 1813: Épisodes du siège de Hambourg* (Fontainebleau, 1896), analyzing Napoleon’s campaign ciphers from archival documents found at Aix-la-Chapelle, the Archives nationales, and the Dépôt de la guerre. This is a direct later-author/earlier-subject connection.

### Auguste Kerckhoffs

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Bazeries engaged in writing with Kerckhoffs’s cryptographic work and terminology in *Les Chiffres secrets dévoilés* (1901), within the late-nineteenth-century French debate over military cipher security. The evidence supports one-sided written engagement and membership in the same professional discourse, but not a documented personal meeting or correspondence. [Bazeries’s 1901 text](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/0b/Les_chiffres_secrets_d%C3%A9voil%C3%A9s%2C_%C3%89tienne_Bazeries%2C_1901.pdf)

### Captain Alfred Dreyfus

- **[T3] Reported fact:** Bazeries briefly addressed the Panizzardi telegram associated with the Dreyfus affair in *Les Chiffres secrets dévoilés* (1901), creating a one-sided textual connection to Dreyfus. Bazeries was attached to the Foreign Ministry when the telegram was processed in Paris in November 1894, but surviving evidence does not securely establish that he personally produced either the erroneous first decrypt or its correction; the connection should therefore not be elevated to a personal meeting or direct correspondence.

### Thomas Jefferson

- **[T9-] Documented fact:** Bazeries’s twenty-disk cylindrical cryptograph, presented in France in 1891, independently reproduced the central removable-disk principle of Jefferson’s earlier wheel cipher, described in the 1790s but rediscovered among Jefferson’s papers only in 1922. Sources indicate that Bazeries did not know Jefferson’s design; their connection is the shared cipher-device lineage, with Jefferson the earlier originator and Bazeries the later independent reinventer. [NSA Friedman–Callimahos technical history](https://www.nsa.gov/Portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/publications/FOLDER_257/41751819079101.pdf), [museum collection history](https://onlinesammlung.museumsstiftung.de/detail/collection/f10ac0df-fc93-4aaa-b6e9-3bbd8b267d52)
