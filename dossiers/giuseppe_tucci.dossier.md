# Giuseppe Tucci: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier concerns the Italian Orientalist, Tibetologist and historian of religions Giuseppe Tucci (1894–1984), not later namesakes.

Labels used for contested matters:

- **Documented fact:** supported by an archival record, official institutional record, Tucci’s publication or contemporaneous document.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a generally reliable secondary source but not independently verified here from the underlying record.
- **Allegation:** an accusation made by an identifiable person or publication.
- **Disputed claim:** credible sources disagree, or the available documents do not settle the question.
- **Rumor or myth:** a recurrent story whose documentary foundation is absent or inadequate.
- **Inference:** a conclusion suggested, but not directly established, by the evidence.

The most detailed modern chronological bibliography, Oscar Nalesini’s *Giuseppe Tucci’s Chronological Bibliography* (2018), records more than 700 writings by or involving Tucci and approximately 370 writings about him. The selected bibliography below identifies his principal books, serial works, expedition reports, editions and major institutional publications; it is not a transcription of all 700 entries.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and childhood household

**Documented fact:** Tucci was the only child of Oscar Tucci and Ermenegilda Fermani. Oscar was first secretary in the provincial financial administration (*Intendenza di Finanza*). Both parents’ families had moved from Apulia to the Marche. Some later sources spell his mother’s surname “Firmani”; Alice Crisanti’s *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani* entry gives **Fermani**.

The family was conventionally Catholic and middle-class. No evidence located for this dossier supports claims of aristocratic ancestry, inherited wealth or a commercial dynasty.

The household moved to Novara in 1902, returned to Macerata in 1903 and settled in Ancona in 1917, by which time Giuseppe was already based chiefly in Rome.

### Marriages, partners and child

Tucci married three times:

1. **Rosa Di Benedetto (also printed De Benedetto)** — married in 1920.  
   Their only known child, **Ananda** or **Ananda Maria Tucci**, was born in 1923. Later accounts say the marriage had been promoted or chosen by Tucci’s father, but the paternal-arrangement detail is a **reported fact**, not established here from a marriage or family document. The marriage was annulled before 1927.

2. **Giulia Nuvoloni** — married in 1927.  
   Nuvoloni accompanied Tucci on Himalayan journeys, including the 1928 Ladakh and 1929 Nepal expeditions, and contributed to the expeditionary record. Their marriage was in practice over by 1941 or 1942; sources give both years for the separation.

3. **Francesca Bonardi (1917–2014)** — Tucci’s partner from approximately 1941–42 and his wife from 1971, after Italian divorce law made termination of the second marriage possible.  
   Bonardi accompanied missions, made an important photographic record, helped maintain Tucci’s later household and legacy, and assembled or preserved the Bonardi-Tucci collection. Her later donation included Asian objects and manuscripts intended for teaching and public knowledge.

No reliable evidence found in the consulted sources establishes additional children, acknowledged or otherwise. Tucci’s relations with his son Ananda are insufficiently documented in accessible institutional biographies.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1894–1913

Tucci was born in Macerata on 5 June 1894. His earliest intellectual interest reportedly concerned local antiquities, Picene archaeology, inscriptions and names. This local antiquarian beginning preceded his concentration on Asia.

In 1907 he entered the Liceo classico Giacomo Leopardi in Macerata. He studied the classical curriculum intensely and also began oriental languages, including Sanskrit, unusually early. He completed the liceo on 25 June 1912, being discharged “with honor.”

In March 1913 he enrolled in the Faculty of Letters at the Regia Università di Roma, following the classical-philology curriculum.

## Education and Formation

### Teachers and mentors

- **Giovanni Vacca:** introduced or guided Tucci into Chinese language and culture. Sinology became Tucci’s first systematic route into Asian intellectual history.
- **Carlo Formichi:** principal mentor in Indian studies, Sanskrit and Buddhism; later secured the opportunity for Tucci to accompany him to Śāntiniketan.
- **Luigi Pigorini:** influenced Tucci’s early interest in ethnography and Asian prehistory.
- **Giovanni Gentile:** intellectual patron, correspondent and later institutional collaborator. Gentile’s political position inside Fascism was crucial to the establishment of IsMEO and to Tucci’s university and cultural network.
- **Raffaele Pettazzoni:** collaborator in Italian history-of-religions publishing, notably *Studi e materiali di storia delle religioni*.

Tucci initially worked on Picene Latin epigraphy and anthroponymy, then turned toward Chinese thought. From Chinese studies he moved increasingly into Buddhism, Sanskrit and Tibetan intellectual and religious history.

### First World War

**Documented fact:** Called to military service, Tucci served as a lieutenant in the Regina Brigade. The war interrupted but did not terminate his university studies. No reliable account consulted documents imprisonment, a major wound or a decoration for combat.

On 12 July 1919 he graduated with the highest marks. His thesis was titled *Sull’importanza e dello stato attuale degli studi di storia della filosofia orientale* (“On the importance and present state of studies in the history of Oriental philosophy”).

---

## Early Career, 1919–1925

Tucci briefly taught as a substitute at the Liceo-Ginnasio Stabili in Ascoli Piceno, approximately 1919–21.

Through the intervention of former prime minister Luigi Luzzatti, he became secretary in the Library of the Chamber of Deputies in January 1921. He remained formally associated with its staff until 1 November 1930, although he left for India in 1925.

During these years he:

- published translations and studies of Buddhist texts;
- collaborated with the publisher Il Solco of Città di Castello;
- founded or directed *Alle fonti delle religioni* in 1921;
- helped merge that venture into *Studi e materiali di storia delle religioni* in 1925;
- obtained the *libera docenza* in languages and literatures of the Far East in 1923;
- taught at the University of Rome, first in Far Eastern languages and literature, then in religions and philosophies of India and the Far East;
- published *Storia della filosofia cinese antica* in 1922.

These activities established him as a young specialist before his extended Asian fieldwork.

## Chronological Life History

## India and the first Himalayan journeys, 1925–1930

### Śāntiniketan

Rabindranath Tagore invited Carlo Formichi to teach Sanskrit at Visva-Bharati in Śāntiniketan. Formichi asked the Fascist government to finance a younger Italian scholar who could teach Italian culture; Tucci was selected. A Foreign Ministry memorandum shows that his journey was financed from a 35,000-lire fund allocated to foreign propaganda work.

Tucci reached India late in 1925 and taught Italian language, art and literature at Visva-Bharati. This placement had both scholarly and cultural-diplomatic purposes.

### Break between Tagore and Fascist Italy

Tagore’s 1926 disillusionment with Fascist Italy disrupted the Śāntiniketan arrangement. Tucci subsequently moved through Dhaka and Calcutta, teaching Chinese, Tibetan or Italian in differing institutional arrangements and forming relations with both Indian intellectuals and the British colonial administration.

This placed him in overlapping networks: Bengali and Indian scholarship, anticolonial intellectual society, Italian state cultural diplomacy and British Indian officialdom.

### Early journeys

Nalesini’s reconstruction distinguishes journeys that were later compressed into a simpler “eight Tibetan expeditions” narrative:

- February 1926: Dhaka toward Agartala and Gauhati, with Formichi;
- May 1926: Darjeeling and Temi, Tucci’s first direct contact with Tibetan cultural environments;
- June–October 1928: Taxila, Rawalpindi, Murree, Srinagar and Ladakh, with Giulia Nuvoloni;
- April–September 1929: Kathmandu, Bhatgaon/Bhaktapur and Patan, with Nuvoloni;
- February 1930: Darjeeling and Ghoom;
- 1930: another Ladakh journey.

The familiar aggregate—eight expeditions in Tibetan regions and five or six in Nepal—depends on whether preliminary journeys and later missions are counted separately. “Eight Tibet, five Nepal” is the most consistently used recent institutional count.

---

## Professorship and institutional ascent, 1930–1933

In 1930 Tucci was appointed, *per chiara fama* (“by eminent reputation”), to the chair of Chinese at the Regio Istituto Orientale in Naples. He taught there in 1931. In 1932 he moved to the University of Rome as professor of religions and philosophies of India and the Far East, a position he retained until retirement in 1969.

Carlo Formichi’s support helped secure Tucci’s appointment in 1929 to the Reale Accademia d’Italia. The academy supplied status and substantial support for his expeditions.

### Founding of IsMEO

In 1931 Tucci proposed to Benito Mussolini an institute that would mediate between Italy and Asia through:

- language instruction;
- scholarships;
- research and expeditions;
- cultural diplomacy;
- commercial and political relationships.

The **Istituto Italiano per il Medio ed Estremo Oriente (IsMEO)** was formally founded on 16 February 1933. Giovanni Gentile became president; Tucci was vice-president from 1934 until the institute’s 1944 commissarial suspension.

**Documented fact:** IsMEO was simultaneously a scholarly institute and an instrument of Italian state cultural policy. Its purposes cannot be reduced exclusively either to neutral scholarship or to covert operations.

## Tibetan expeditions and the 1930s

### Principal expeditions

Tucci’s most productive field period included:

- **1931:** Western Himalayan circuit through Srinagar, Ladakh, Rupshu, Lahul, Manali, Kunawar, Shipki Pass, Chini, Sarahan and Simla; followed by Nepal.
- **1933:** Western Tibet expedition, accompanied by the physician, photographer and cartographer Eugenio Ghersi. The mission produced a major chronicle and film record.
- **1935:** Western Tibet and Himalayan research.
- **1937:** Central Tibet.
- **1939:** Central and southwestern Tibet, including Sakya, Ngor and Shalu/Zhvalu. The expedition returned to Gangtok on 5 September, just after Britain and France entered the European war.
- **1948:** final Tibet expedition, reaching Lhasa and central Tibetan monasteries.

Tucci travelled with Italian physicians, photographers and cartographers, locally hired interpreters and lamas, porters, animal handlers and guides. The 1948 expedition included Tenzing Norgay, later famous for the first confirmed ascent of Everest with Edmund Hillary.

### Research and collections

The expeditions documented temples, inscriptions, murals, sculpture, ritual, oral accounts, historical geography and monastery libraries. At Sakya, Ngor and Shalu, Tucci located thousands of Sanskrit manuscript pages, including works believed lost in India. Expedition members photographed or copied many texts.

He acquired manuscripts, paintings, ritual objects and other art. These materials later formed important parts of Italian state and IsMEO collections. His photographs are now often the only surviving visual evidence for buildings or works damaged or destroyed during later political upheavals.

His principal synthesis of this phase was the four-part *Indo-Tibetica* (1932–41). The monumental *Tibetan Painted Scrolls* followed in 1949.

---

## Fascist cultural diplomacy and Japan, 1933–1943

Tucci worked within the Fascist state’s cultural apparatus. His relationship with Fascism was practical, institutional and at particular moments openly propagandistic.

### Japan, 1936–1937

From November 1936 to January 1937 he travelled in Japan as a representative of Mussolini’s government. His task was to improve Italian-Japanese cultural and political relations. On 27 April 1937 he delivered a Japanese-language radio address associated with Mussolini’s government.

His mission formed part of the diplomatic environment preceding Italy’s adhesion to the Anti-Comintern Pact on 6 November 1937. Claims that Tucci personally “caused” Italy’s entry are an **overstatement**; he contributed to cultural and diplomatic preparation but did not possess sole negotiating authority.

Tucci later served on the editorial board of *Yamato*, launched in 1941 to cultivate the Italian-Japanese alliance. IsMEO’s periodical evolved from the *Bollettino* into *Asiatica* in 1936 and became increasingly entangled with state foreign policy.

### Relations with political figures

His network included:

- Benito Mussolini, whose government approved and financed projects;
- Giovanni Gentile, his principal institutional partner;
- Carlo Formichi;
- Luigi Federzoni, president of the Royal Academy of Italy, with whom surviving correspondence from 1939–43 documents professional and personal contact;
- officials of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Ministry of Popular Culture;
- British Indian political authorities;
- Indian scholars and public figures.

Tucci was not a party theorist comparable to Gentile, nor has evidence emerged that he held an elected Fascist office. Nevertheless, descriptions of him as politically uninvolved are incompatible with his documented propaganda mission, state-financed cultural diplomacy and senior position in Fascist institutions.

### Second World War

Unable to continue normal expeditions, Tucci concentrated on research and writing. He was exempted from Second World War military obligations. He did not formally join the Italian Social Republic after September 1943, and no evidence located establishes participation in the Resistance. He remained intellectually connected to Gentile until Gentile’s assassination in April 1944.

---

## Purge proceedings, 1944–1946

After Allied forces entered Rome, Tucci was subjected in July 1944 to university purge proceedings.

The accusations were first framed as “apologia” and then as **active participation in Fascist politics**. Tucci submitted detailed defense memorials. As the Italian purge process narrowed, the Central Purge Commission acquitted him and restored him to active service on 8 January 1946.

A separate review of his 1930 appointment “by eminent reputation” began in October 1945. The Higher Council initially questioned or opposed the appointment, but the case ended on 30 June 1946 with his confirmation. Former prime minister Francesco Saverio Nitti intervened in his favor.

**Legal status:** investigated administratively and temporarily removed; not convicted of a crime; formally cleared and reinstated.

---

## Postwar reconstruction and final Tibetan expedition, 1947–1950

Tucci became president of the revived IsMEO in 1947. Political support from Giulio Andreotti was important to the institute’s restoration and subsequent public funding.

In 1948 Tucci undertook his last central-Tibetan expedition and reached Lhasa. He met the young Fourteenth Dalai Lama, then approximately thirteen rather than fifteen under conventional dating; some later RAI descriptions call him fifteen. The discrepancy probably reflects Tibetan age reckoning or an imprecise retrospective caption.

The Chinese People’s Liberation Army’s entry into Tibet and the subsequent closure or transformation of access ended Tucci’s Tibetan fieldwork. He published *The Tombs of the Tibetan Kings* in 1950 and consolidated his documentation in *Tibetan Painted Scrolls*.

---

## Nepal, Pakistan, Afghanistan and Iran, 1950–1978

### Nepal

Tucci conducted major western Nepal expeditions in 1952 and 1954. These produced research on the Malla dynasties and the history, art and religious geography of regions then little studied by European scholars. The results appeared in *Preliminary Report on Two Scientific Expeditions in Nepal* (1956) and popular accounts.

### Pakistan and Swat

A 1955 reconnaissance of the Swat Valley initiated IsMEO’s long-term archaeological program in Pakistan. Work included Butkara I, Udegram and related Buddhist and historic sites. Tucci functioned increasingly as organizer, negotiator, fundraiser and scientific director rather than solely as a field philologist.

### Afghanistan

Beginning in 1957, IsMEO developed archaeological, architectural and restoration work in Afghanistan, particularly at Ghazni. Tucci coordinated agreements and the overall Eurasian research program.

### Iran

From 1959 IsMEO extended work into Iran, including Sistan, Persepolis and Isfahan. The institute combined excavation with architectural restoration.

These programs created a durable Italian school of Asian archaeology. They also relied on bilateral state agreements and public institutional finance. No evidence consulted indicates that Tucci personally owned the excavation enterprises or treated them as private businesses.

### Museum and publishing institutions

Tucci helped establish the Museo Nazionale d’Arte Orientale in Rome in 1957. The museum later bore his name; its collections have since been integrated into the Museo delle Civiltà.

He directed or shaped:

- *East and West* from 1950;
- *Serie Orientale Roma* from 1950;
- *Reports and Memoirs* of IsMEO’s archaeological center from 1962;
- the *Restorations* series from 1969;
- the scholarly series *Il Nuovo Ramusio*, approximately 1950–73.

---

## Retirement and Final Years, 1969–1984

Tucci retired for age limits in November 1969 and was appointed professor emeritus in 1970. He remained president of IsMEO until 1978 and then became honorary president.

He married Francesca Bonardi in 1971.

On 2 June 1973 the Italian Republic appointed him Knight Grand Cross of the Order of Merit of the Italian Republic.

He received the Jawaharlal Nehru Award for International Understanding for 1976. Some publisher biographies date the presentation to 1978, but the award year cited by Treccani is 1976. In 1979 he received the Balzan Prize for History.

Tucci lived in later years at San Polo dei Cavalieri, near Tivoli. He died there on 5 April 1984, two months before his ninetieth birthday. The consulted authoritative records state neither a specific terminal illness nor a more precise medical cause of death. Claims about deathbed declarations should therefore be treated cautiously unless tied to an identifiable witness.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects and Financial Interests

Tucci was an institutional entrepreneur, not a known commercial businessman.

### Documented institutional interests

- Library of the Chamber of Deputies: salaried secretary.
- University positions in Rome and Naples.
- Visva-Bharati and other Indian teaching appointments under Italian Foreign Ministry arrangements.
- Reale Accademia d’Italia: member and beneficiary of expedition financing.
- IsMEO: co-founder, vice-president, president and honorary president.
- National Museum of Oriental Art: leading advocate for its creation.
- Government-supported archaeological and restoration missions in Pakistan, Afghanistan and Iran.
- Periodical and monograph publishing programs.

### Finance

Known funding sources included:

- Tucci’s public salaries;
- Foreign Ministry cultural-propaganda appropriations;
- Royal Academy of Italy expedition support;
- Fascist-government support to IsMEO;
- postwar Italian public support secured partly through Andreotti;
- bilateral arrangements with Asian governments.

The 1925 India mission was supported by a documented 35,000-lire foreign-propaganda allocation.

No reliable evidence located shows:

- substantial private business holdings;
- patents or inventions;
- securities or corporate investments of historical significance;
- personal enrichment through an archaeological company;
- money laundering, embezzlement or financial fraud;
- a judicial finding that expedition funds were misappropriated.

His house, library and collections were valuable cultural property, but accessible sources do not provide a reliable net-worth history.

## Political, Religious and Intellectual Development

### Intellectual program

Tucci’s career moved through overlapping phases:

1. local archaeology and Latin epigraphy;
2. Chinese philosophy and Sinology;
3. Sanskrit, Indian philosophy and Buddhist textual studies;
4. Tibetan history, art and religion;
5. comparative Eurasian cultural history;
6. archaeological and restoration programs spanning South, Central and West Asia.

His mature thesis was that Europe and Asia formed a historically interconnected Eurasian field rather than sealed civilizations. He emphasized migrations, trade, conquest, religious transmission and artistic exchange.

### Method

Tucci combined:

- close philology;
- direct consultation of Tibetan and Sanskrit texts;
- field observation;
- oral evidence from learned lamas;
- art-historical comparison;
- photography and copying;
- archaeological reconnaissance;
- broad comparative synthesis.

His work contains period vocabulary now avoided, including “Lamaism,” and occasionally severe judgments about Tibetan monastic “decadence,” cleanliness or institutional decline. These statements belong to his historical observational framework and sometimes reproduce hierarchical assumptions of European Orientalism.

### Buddhism and personal belief

Several accounts describe Tucci as more than an external scholar of Buddhism.

- Edward Conze reportedly regarded him as a believing and to some degree practicing Buddhist.
- Enrica Garzilli reports a late private statement that Tucci had become Buddhist after disappointment with the Catholic Church and clergy.
- Other memorialists emphasize his inward knowledge of Buddhist practice.

**Evidentiary status:** **reported fact / unresolved personal claim.** No public conversion certificate, ordination record or single dated declaration has been established. “Tucci was a Buddhist” is plausible but cannot be specified institutionally from the consulted documentation.

### Freemasonry

Former Grand Master Gustavo Raffi named Tucci among Italian scholars of India who “are or were Freemasons.”

**Evidentiary status:** **reported allegation**, not conclusively documented here. No lodge register, initiation date, degree or lodge affiliation was located. It should not be treated as established solely on Raffi’s retrospective statement.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Carlo Formichi

Teacher, patron, travelling companion and facilitator of Tucci’s Indian appointment. Formichi’s support was decisive in Tucci’s early career and Academy recognition.

### Giovanni Vacca

Teacher of Chinese and an important influence in Tucci’s transition from classical to Asian studies.

### Giovanni Gentile

Long-term correspondent, intellectual patron and IsMEO collaborator. Gentile’s political authority enabled the institute’s foundation. Their association continued until Gentile’s death in 1944.

### Rabindranath Tagore

Tagore’s invitation to Formichi created Tucci’s Śāntiniketan opportunity. Tucci taught at Tagore’s university, but the relationship was institutionally disrupted when Tagore broke with Fascist Italy in 1926. Evidence located does not establish a lasting intimate friendship between Tagore and Tucci.

### Benito Mussolini

Mussolini’s government financed the India mission, approved IsMEO and sent Tucci on the Japanese cultural-diplomatic mission. Their connection was one of political patronage and service rather than documented personal intimacy.

### Raffaele Pettazzoni

Colleague in history-of-religions scholarship and periodical publication.

### Luigi Luzzatti

Helped Tucci obtain his Chamber of Deputies library position.

### Luigi Federzoni

Political and academic associate; their surviving correspondence, 1939–43, concerns both professional and personal matters.

### Giulio Andreotti

Postwar political patron whose support helped revive IsMEO. Garzilli published their correspondence as part of her biography.

### Giulia Nuvoloni and Francesca Bonardi

Both were expeditionary collaborators as well as spouses. Accounts that reduce them to domestic roles obscure their photographic and logistical work.

### Eugenio Ghersi

Physician, photographer, cartographer and principal collaborator on the 1933 western Tibet mission.

### Fosco Maraini

Student and expedition collaborator, notably in 1937 and 1948. *Segreto Tibet* was dedicated to Tucci as Maraini’s master. Maraini could also criticize him and later defended his collecting against accusations of theft.

### Luciano Petech, Raniero Gnoli and later pupils

Tucci trained or institutionally supported generations of Italian Asianists. Luciano Petech became a leading historian of Tibet; Raniero Gnoli became a major Sanskritist and historian of Indian thought. Their memorial accounts tend to emphasize Tucci’s scholarly achievement and delimit the political significance of his Fascist work.

### Ernst Bernhard

**Reported fact with first-person testimony:** The Jewish Jungian psychoanalyst Ernst Bernhard credited Tucci with obtaining his release from the Ferramonti internment camp, enabling him to return home and live in hiding. This does not erase Tucci’s Fascist institutional participation, but it is material evidence against a simple portrait of consistent personal antisemitism.

### The Fourteenth Dalai Lama

Tucci met the young Dalai Lama at Lhasa in 1948.

### Mircea Eliade

Tucci invited Eliade to give two lectures at IsMEO in Rome in March 1950. Eliade later wrote an obituary or memorial assessment of Tucci. In Eliade’s novella *Youth Without Youth*, and Francis Ford Coppola’s 2007 film adaptation, “Professor Giuseppe Tucci” appears as the named authority consulted about an apparently ancient Asian language.

## Controversies, Allegations and Investigations

## Fascist participation

**Documented fact:** Tucci accepted Fascist state financing, became an Academy of Italy member, co-founded a state-supported cultural-policy institute, carried out propaganda and diplomatic work in Japan, and participated in publications associated with the Axis relationship.

**Disputed interpretation:** Scholars disagree about whether he should be understood principally as:

- a committed Fascist intellectual;
- an ambitious scholar who used the regime to finance research;
- a cultural diplomat whose scholarship and politics were inseparable;
- or an essentially universalist scholar operating opportunistically inside dictatorship.

The archival record defeats the strongest exculpatory claim that he had “nothing to do with politics.” It does not, by itself, prove that every part of his scholarship was created to advance Fascist doctrine.

Gustavo Benavides interpreted Tucci as an example of “Buddhology in the age of Fascism.” Enrica Garzilli’s *L’esploratore del Duce* presents a sustained political reconstruction. Oscar Nalesini criticized errors, overreach and methodology in Garzilli’s biography while not denying the documented institutional relationship with the regime.

## The Manifesto of Race

A recurring controversy claims that Tucci signed or endorsed the 1938 *Manifesto della razza*.

### What is established

- Tucci was a member of the Royal Academy of Italy.
- Annalisa Capristo’s research places him on an Academy study commission dealing with “problems of race.”
- Jewish-community representatives Leone Paserman and Victor Magiar publicly objected in 2010 to naming a Roman street after Tucci, citing his alleged adherence.
- Tucci’s name has appeared in later lists of intellectual supporters.

### What is not established

The original manifesto was associated above all with ten named racial scientists. No signed copy bearing Tucci’s verifiable signature has emerged in the material consulted. Later lists often combine original drafters, later adherents, public supporters and institutional participants without distinguishing them.

**Finding:** Tucci’s signature is a **disputed and unproven claim**. Membership in the Academy commission and participation in the Fascist system are documented; formal personal signature of the manifesto is not.

The controversy resurfaced in 2010 during the dedication of Largo Giuseppe Tucci in Rome.

## Espionage and intelligence connections

British India’s Political and Secret Department maintained a file titled **“Tibet (via Kashmir): Professor G Tucci,”** now British Library reference IOR/L/PS/12/4247. Surveillance and scrutiny of foreign travellers in strategically sensitive Tibet were routine.

Postwar accusations described Tucci as a spy, and his travels necessarily involved colonial political officers, diplomatic permissions and state reporting.

**Finding:** The existence of a British secret/political file is a **documented fact**. Tucci’s official cultural-diplomatic activity is documented. The stronger claim that he was a formally enrolled intelligence agent, with a service designation and operational chain of command, remains an **unproven allegation** in the sources consulted.

## Collecting, removal and provenance of Tibetan objects

Tucci bought and transported manuscripts, thangkas, sculptures and ritual materials from impoverished monasteries and dealers. His own writing describes bargaining with monks. Nalesini observes that some of his collecting methods may be questionable by modern standards and that poverty lowered prices.

Fosco Maraini acknowledged the accusation that Tucci removed “too many” treasures but defended him, arguing that acquisitions saved works later threatened by Chinese destruction. That defense reflects a twentieth-century salvage rationale; it does not resolve present questions of consent, unequal bargaining power, sacred status or provenance.

A story says Tucci tore a manuscript into pieces before an Indian customs officer. Lo Bue repeats it as something **reported**, not as a verified event.

**Finding:**

- Acquisition and export: **documented fact**.
- Universal characterization as theft or looting: **not established case by case**.
- Ethical concern arising from colonial conditions, unequal prices and incomplete provenance: **documented contemporary scholarly issue**.
- Claim that every exported object would otherwise have been destroyed: **counterfactual and unprovable**.
- No consulted source records a court judgment finding Tucci guilty of antiquities theft.

The materials have also preserved texts and images otherwise lost. The historical value of preservation and the provenance problem coexist.

## Orientalism and representations of Tibet

Tucci resisted purely romantic images of Tibet and treated Tibetan intellectual history seriously. At the same time, his accounts sometimes described monks and institutions in disparaging physical or civilizational language and judged religious communities by European conservation ideals.

Recent scholarship evaluates him through Edward Said’s concept of Orientalism and through decolonial museum and provenance studies. This is an academic interpretive controversy, not a criminal accusation.

## Purge and academic appointment review

These were actual investigations, not rumors. He was accused administratively of active Fascist politics, cleared on 8 January 1946, and confirmed in his chair on 30 June 1946. Acquittal establishes the official outcome of the proceedings; it does not mean his documented propaganda work did not occur.

## Financial, criminal and personal allegations

The dedicated adversarial searches combined Tucci’s name with scandal, fraud, lawsuit, corruption, money laundering, crime, abuse and conspiracy, in English and Italian, including 2024–26 results.

No credible evidence was found of:

- fraud or embezzlement;
- money laundering;
- private financial corruption;
- criminal conviction;
- sexual or domestic criminal allegation;
- addiction;
- a civil lawsuit of major biographical importance;
- political violence personally committed by Tucci.

Search results concerning the 2023 killing of a firefighter also named Giuseppe Tucci concern a different person.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda and Disputed Narratives

### “Tucci was the greatest Tibetologist in the world”

This appears frequently in Italian commemorative and journalistic literature. It expresses his exceptional international standing but is an honorific judgment, not an independently measurable fact. His documentation, language skills and synthesis were unquestionably influential; Tibetology was nevertheless an international field with other major specialists.

### “He knew forty or fifty languages”

Versions of this prodigious-polyglot story circulate without a dated inventory or testing standard. Tucci demonstrably worked in numerous classical and Asian languages. Exact high numbers should be classified as **reputational legend**.

### “The scholar untouched by politics”

This postwar image emphasizes pure scholarship and portrays state relationships as incidental. Archival documentation of propaganda funding, IsMEO’s political role and the Japanese mission contradicts the literal version.

### “Nothing more than Mussolini’s spy”

The inverse mythology turns every expedition into espionage. It draws plausibility from geopolitical circumstances, state patronage and British surveillance, but no consulted document establishes that all or most of his philological and archaeological work was a cover for intelligence operations.

### “Signer of the Manifesto of Race”

Widely repeated after the 2010 street controversy. His Fascist institutional position and race-commission membership are relevant, but an authenticated signature has not been produced. The label must remain disputed.

### “He stole Tibet’s treasures”

The story combines documented acquisitions with an undifferentiated criminal conclusion. Some objects were bought, some copied or photographed and some may have been presented; provenance is uneven. Modern criticism of the unequal collecting environment is supported, but each object requires separate provenance research.

### “He saved everything from the Chinese Cultural Revolution”

Maraini’s defense gained force after massive destruction in Tibet. It influenced Tucci’s reputation as a rescuer. It is partly supported by the survival of unique photographs, texts and objects but cannot demonstrate that each acquired object faced certain destruction.

### Buddhist sage or initiate

Late portrayals sometimes transform Tucci from scholar into esoteric initiate. His deep engagement with Buddhism and possible personal practice are credible; claims of secret initiation, extraordinary realization or authoritative lama status lack adequate documentation.

### Freemason

Repeated chiefly from Gustavo Raffi’s statement. Without lodge documentation, it remains a reported but unresolved affiliation.

## Illness, Injury, Addiction and Death

No authoritative consulted source identifies a major chronic illness, psychiatric crisis, addiction or severe expedition injury. His Himalayan travels exposed him to substantial environmental danger, but later adventure literature sometimes dramatizes risk without medical documentation.

Tucci died at San Polo dei Cavalieri on 5 April 1984. A specific medical cause of death was not located. Assertions beyond “death in advanced age at his residence” should therefore remain unverified.

## Works by Giuseppe Tucci

### Authorship note

The following are genuine works authored, edited or co-authored by Tucci unless otherwise noted. Modern reprints with new popular titles, selections and translations are not new original works. *Nel paese delle donne dai molti mariti*, for example, is a posthumous gathering of travel journalism whose editorial titles and captions have been criticized.

### Early studies and editions

- *Animadversiones indicae* (series of early Indological papers, 1910s–20s).
- *Sull’importanza e dello stato attuale degli studi di storia della filosofia orientale* (1919; university thesis; not originally a commercial monograph).
- *Storia della filosofia cinese antica* (1922).
- *Il Buddhismo* (1926; later editions and revisions).
- *Pre-Dinnāga Buddhist Texts on Logic from Chinese Sources* (1929).
- *The Nyāyamukha of Dignāga* (1930).
- Studies and editions of Buddhist logical, Madhyamaka, Yogācāra and tantric texts published in Italian and international journals.

### Himalayan and Tibetan works

- *Indo-Tibetica*, 4 parts in multiple fascicles/volumes (Rome, 1932–41):
  - *Mc’od rten e ts’a ts’a nel Tibet indiano ed occidentale* (1932).
  - *Rin c’en bzan po e la rinascita del buddhismo nel Tibet intorno al Mille* (1933).
  - Studies of the temples of Western Tibet and related monuments.
  - Studies of Gyantse and Tibetan religious art.
- With Eugenio Ghersi, *Cronaca della missione scientifica Tucci nel Tibet occidentale (1933)* (1934).
- English adaptation: *Secrets of Tibet: Being the Chronicle of the Tucci Scientific Expedition to Western Tibet, 1933* (1935).
- *Santi e briganti nel Tibet ignoto: diario della spedizione nel Tibet occidentale 1935* (1937).
- *Tibetan Painted Scrolls*, 3 volumes (1949; often described as two text volumes plus plates; bibliographic descriptions vary).
- *A Lhasa e oltre: diario della spedizione nel Tibet MCMXLVIII* (1950).
- *To Lhasa and Beyond* (English translation/adaptation, 1956).
- *The Tombs of the Tibetan Kings* (1950).
- *Teoria e pratica del mandala* (1949; later translated as *The Theory and Practice of the Mandala*).
- *The Tibetan Book of the Dead* / *Il libro tibetano dei morti* (Italian presentation/translation associated with Tucci; not original authorship of the Tibetan source text).
- *Minor Buddhist Texts*, volumes I–II (1956–58; editions/translations).
- *Deb t’er dmar po gsar ma* and other edited Tibetan historical texts.
- *The Religions of Tibet* (German original synthesis *Die Religionen Tibets*, 1970; later English and Italian translations).
- *Tibet* (1973; in the *Archaeologia Mundi* series; translated into several languages).

- *Forme dello spirito asiatico* (1940).
- *Asia religiosa* (1946).

- *Rati-Līlā: An Interpretation of the Tantric Imagery of the Temples of Nepal* (1969).
- *East and West* essays on Indian philosophy, Buddhist logic, tantra, Tibetan historiography and Asian archaeology.

### Travel, cultural history and public writing

- *Italia e Oriente* (1949).
- *Tra giungle e pagode* (1953).
- *Le grandi vie di comunicazione Europa-Asia* (1958).
- *La via dello Swat* (1963).
- Numerous articles in newspapers and magazines about Tibet, Nepal, India, Japan, archaeology, religion and cultural politics.
- “Earth in India and Tibet,” delivered at Eranos in 1953 and published in the *Eranos-Jahrbuch*.
- Essays in *Asiatica*, *East and West*, *Studi e materiali di storia delle religioni*, *Bollettino dell’IsMEO* and other journals.

### Editorial and institutional work

- Founder/director, *Alle fonti delle religioni* (1921–24).
- Co-founder or early collaborator, *Studi e materiali di storia delle religioni* (from 1925).

### Posthumous and derivative publications

These should not be mistaken for books newly composed by Tucci:

- *Nel paese delle donne dai molti mariti* — posthumous collection of previously published travel pieces; criticized for altered or fanciful titling and some inaccurate captions.

### Sympathetic, critical and mixed traditions

The literature divides broadly into:

- pupil and institutional memorials emphasizing philology, discovery and mentorship;
- political biographies emphasizing Fascist cultural diplomacy;
- Tibetological reassessments of his discoveries and errors;
- provenance and museum studies addressing unequal acquisition conditions;
- popular adventure biographies that sometimes blur expedition reconstruction with legend.

No single work should be treated as exhaustive or neutral. Garzilli and Nalesini should be read together because their disagreement concerns both factual details and historical interpretation.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Tucci’s lasting contributions include:

- establishment of Tibetan studies as a major Italian academic field;
- recovery, photography and edition of Sanskrit and Tibetan texts;
- early systematic art-historical documentation of western and central Tibetan monasteries;
- photographic evidence for monuments subsequently damaged or destroyed;
- training of scholars including Luciano Petech and Raniero Gnoli;
- creation of durable archaeological missions in Swat, Ghazni and Iran;
- establishment of publication series and the National Museum of Oriental Art;
- promotion of a connected Eurasian approach to cultural history.

His posthumous reputation has undergone several phases:

1. **Immediate commemoration:** the master explorer, philologist and institution-builder.
2. **Political reassessment:** greater attention to Fascist finance, propaganda and diplomacy.
3. **Archival correction:** reconstruction of itineraries, personnel, correspondence and purge proceedings.
4. **Provenance reassessment:** examination of collecting under colonial and economically unequal conditions.
5. **Renewed public commemoration:** documentaries, exhibitions and Chinese editions of *Indo-Tibetica*.
6. **Continuing dispute:** whether political compromise was instrumental to scholarship or constitutive of his intellectual enterprise.

The former Museo Nazionale d’Arte Orientale carried his name. Its collections now form part of the Museo delle Civiltà. Macerata and San Polo dei Cavalieri have commemorated him, and Rome named Largo Giuseppe Tucci amid the 2010 controversy over the alleged Race Manifesto signature.

In 2026 the exhibition *Abbracciando l’infinito. L’eterna ricerca di Giuseppe Tucci* circulated in Beijing and Ulaanbaatar, accompanied by expedition films and the new RAI documentary. This demonstrates continuing international interest while political and provenance questions remain active.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere overlap in interests or membership of a broad intellectual milieu is excluded.

### Benito Mussolini — documented fact

Mussolini’s government approved and financed the 1925 India mission involving Tucci and Formichi. Tucci later proposed the institute that became IsMEO, which Mussolini’s government established in 1933. Tucci represented the regime in Japan in 1936–37 and delivered propaganda associated with Mussolini. This was a relationship of state patronage, cultural diplomacy and political service; personal intimacy is not established.

### Rabindranath Tagore — documented fact

Tagore invited Carlo Formichi to Visva-Bharati at Śāntiniketan. That invitation led to Tucci’s selection as Italian lecturer in 1925. Tucci taught at Tagore’s university. The connection weakened after Tagore’s 1926 repudiation of Fascist Italy. A lasting personal friendship is not documented.

### Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi — no supported direct connection found

Tucci inhabited Indian intellectual and political networks during Gandhi’s prominence, and Fascist Italy separately cultivated relations with Gandhi. No consulted source documents a Tucci-Gandhi meeting, correspondence, collaboration or acknowledged intellectual influence. Absence is the finding.

### Carl Gustav Jung — reported institutional connection, not a documented personal relationship

Tucci lectured at Eranos in 1953, a conference circle strongly shaped by Jung. Both therefore belonged to the documented Eranos intellectual environment. The consulted sources do not establish a direct meeting or correspondence between Tucci and Jung. A personal bond should not be inferred.

### Olga Fröbe-Kapteyn — documented institutional connection

Fröbe-Kapteyn founded and organized Eranos. Tucci participated in the 1953 conference and contributed “Earth in India and Tibet” to the resulting *Eranos-Jahrbuch*. This constitutes a documented organizer-contributor connection; detailed personal correspondence was not located.

### Mircea Eliade — documented fact

Tucci invited Eliade to Rome to lecture twice at IsMEO in March 1950. Eliade later wrote about Tucci, and inserted a character named Professor Giuseppe Tucci into *Youth Without Youth*. The connection combined institutional patronage, comparative-religion scholarship and posthumous literary commemoration.

### Julius Evola — unresolved / no sufficiently documented direct connection

Tucci and Evola wrote about Asian religion in overlapping Italian intellectual environments, and secondary literature sometimes places them in the same “East-West” or esoteric field. The consulted sources do not establish a direct Tucci-Evola meeting, correspondence or collaboration. No atlas connection is asserted.

### René Guénon — no supported direct connection found

Their writings share subject matter concerning Asian traditions, and both influenced European discussions of “East” and “West.” No direct meeting, correspondence or acknowledged personal influence was established.

### Alexandra David-Néel — no supported direct connection found

Both were prominent European travellers and interpreters of Tibetan Buddhism, but no consulted source establishes that they met or corresponded.

### Nicholas Roerich — no supported direct connection found

Shared geographical and scholarly interests do not establish contact. None was found.

### Xuanzang — documented scholarly influence, not a personal connection

Tucci’s reconstruction of Buddhist transmission and Eurasian routes necessarily engaged the historical record of Chinese Buddhist pilgrims such as Xuanzang. This is a text-mediated scholarly connection across centuries, not a biographical encounter. It is included only as a documented intellectual-historical influence on Tucci’s field.

### Odoric of Pordenone, Marco Polo, Giovanni da Pian del Carpine and Matteo Ricci — documented tradition-level influence

Tucci and IsMEO situated modern Italian Asian studies within the earlier Italian history of travel, mission and textual exchange represented by these figures. His direction of *Il Nuovo Ramusio* explicitly cultivated the documentary tradition of European travel to Asia. The connection is intellectual and editorial, not personal.

### Francis of Assisi and Dante Alighieri — reported comparative references, not biographical connections

Later presentations of Tucci’s Eurasian outlook invoke Italian religious and literary traditions, including Francis and Dante, but no distinctive documented influence sufficient to reconstruct a personal intellectual genealogy was located. They are not counted as firm atlas connections.
