# Olga Fröbe-Kapteyn: Research Dossier

**Subject:** Founder of the Eranos conferences at Ascona; artist, editor, image archivist, and religious-intellectual organizer  
**Prepared:** 24 August 2026  
**Scope note:** This dossier distinguishes documented facts, later recollections, interpretation, and unresolved claims. Fröbe-Kapteyn left a large but dispersed archive—at Eranos, the Warburg Institute, the Wellcome Collection, and in correspondences of Jung and Bollingen participants—but no full conventional autobiography. Her first book-length scholarly biography appeared only recently; several family details remain inconsistently reported.

## Family and Ancestry

Olga was the eldest of three children of Dutch parents. Her father, **Albertus Philippus Kapteyn/Kapteijn (1848–1927)**, was an engineer, inventor, senior executive of the Westinghouse Brake & Signal enterprise in Britain, and an enthusiastic photographer. He was an elder brother of the astronomer **Jacobus Cornelius Kapteyn**. Albertus moved to London in 1881 for Westinghouse; by 1887 he had become director-general of its London operation and is later described as vice-president. The family’s industrial income explains the material independence that later enabled Olga to acquire or maintain property, travel to major collections, commission photographic reproductions, entertain scholars, and subsidize an institution without a university base.

Her mother, **Geertruida Agneta “Truus” Muysken Kapteyn (1855–1920)**, was a writer and activist in women’s emancipation, social reform, dress reform, and anarchist or philosophical-anarchist circles. Her Zürich salon included reform intellectuals; biographical accounts connect her with **George Bernard Shaw** and **Peter Kropotkin**. Olga therefore grew up at the junction of industrial modernity, photography, women’s reform, free-thinking politics, and cultivated sociability.

The exact identities and trajectories of Olga’s two younger siblings are not securely reconstructed in the sources consulted. Likewise, public accounts rarely integrate her daughters into the Eranos story. This absence is itself significant: later portrayals often convert her from mother and widow into the nearly disembodied “Great Mother” of Eranos.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1881–1900

Born in London, Olga grew up in prosperous circumstances. She attended **North London Collegiate School**, an important institution for girls’ education. A school friendship with the future birth-control pioneer and palaeobotanist **Marie Stopes** is reported in biographical literature. The family darkroom was formative: she watched and assisted her father as he developed photographs, acquiring the technical and visual familiarity that later underlay her monumental image archive.

By the end of the nineteenth century the Kapteyn family had moved to **Zürich**. Truus Kapteyn created another reform-minded intellectual circle there. Olga became a keen skier and mountaineer as well as an art student. These details place her within the Alpine *Lebensreform* milieu that later converged on nearby Monte Verità.

## Education and Formation, 1900–1909

In 1900 she entered Zürich’s **Kunstgewerbeschule** (School of Applied Arts). Training included sewing, embroidery, jewelry, design, and related crafts. She also studied art history, reportedly at the University of Zürich, but accessible sources do not establish a degree. Describing her as a university “scholar” can therefore overstate the formal credential: her later authority came from self-directed learning, visual research, multilingual networks, editing, patronage, and institution-building.

Her formation combined applied art with her family’s photographic practice. That conjunction remained visible throughout her work: she treated visual forms as knowledge-bearing structures and photographic reproduction as a comparative research technology.

## Marriage, Artistic Work, War, and Widowhood, 1909–1919

In 1909 Olga married **Iwan Hermann Fröbe**, a musician of Croatian/Slovenian background in the Habsburg world, active as a flautist with Zürich’s Tonhalle/opera orchestra and as a conductor. His career took the couple through Braunschweig or Munich and then, by late 1910, to **Berlin**. The exact sequence varies among short biographies.

In Berlin Olga worked with the Dutch-German philologist **André Jolles**, who founded the *Nadel* (“Needle”) sewing circle in 1911. Jolles designed garments inspired by classical Greek dress; Olga executed or embellished them with fine embroidery. This episode belongs to European dress reform and to the attempt to reunite art, ritual, everyday life, and antiquity.

At the outbreak of the First World War the couple returned to Zürich. Olga organized a literary-cultural salon called the **Table Ronde**, an early prototype of her later work: intimate gathering, hospitality, structured exchange, and cross-disciplinary conversation.

The family suffered two crises in 1915. Twin daughters were born, usually dated May. In September Iwan died in an aviation accident at **Fischamend**, near Vienna. Technical accounts say he was testing an aerial camera for the Austro-Hungarian military when the aircraft crashed. Some retellings place the twins’ birth after his death, contradicting the May/September sequence. The safest reconstruction is that the twins were born in 1915 and he died that September; the precise birth date needs civil-record confirmation. One twin is said to have been seriously disabled and later to have died, but accessible evidence is too thin for a date or diagnosis.

Olga was thus widowed in her early thirties while caring for children. Later spiritual narratives treat the bereavement as a decisive initiation. It clearly preceded her retreat to Ticino, but claims about a direct mystical conversion should be treated as interpretation rather than documented causation.

## Ascona, Monte Verità, and the Creation of a Spiritual Center, 1919–1932

In 1919–20 Olga and her father went to the **Monte Verità sanatorium** above Ascona for natural treatment or convalescence. Monte Verità already carried a mythology of vegetarianism, naturism, anarchism, dance, psychoanalysis, and alternative religion. Olga’s later center was geographically adjacent but institutionally distinct.

Accounts differ over the property transaction. One version says Albertus Kapteyn bought **Casa Gabriella**, an old farmhouse at Moscia near Ascona, for Olga; another says she acquired the Moscia property from the ophthalmologist Hoffmann with paternal inheritance in 1919. Both versions agree that family wealth made the purchase possible. Casa Gabriella became her permanent home and the nucleus of Eranos. A later lecture building, **Casa Eranos**, was erected in 1928.

During the 1920s she studied Indian philosophy, yoga, meditation, Theosophy, and comparative religion. Major contacts included:

- **Ludwig Derleth**, German poet and religious visionary;
- **Rudolf Otto**, historian of religion and theorist of the *numinous*;
- **Hermann Keyserling** and his *Schule der Weisheit* at Darmstadt;
- **Richard Wilhelm**, translator of the *I Ching* and mediator of Chinese thought;
- members of the **Marburg ecumenical circle**;
- **Alice A. Bailey** and Bailey’s Arcane School milieu;
- the psychiatrist **Roberto Assagioli**, later associated with psychosynthesis.

From roughly 1928 to 1932 Olga collaborated with Bailey. Her geometric color screenprints—now usually called **Theosophical Prints**—belong to this period. Their visual vocabulary includes pyramids, rays, vessels, mandalas, crowns, hearts, black/gold polarity, and abstract diagrams of spiritual ascent. The Metropolitan Museum dates its complete fourteen-print set to about 1930.

She originally imagined her new hall as a spiritual study center or “ashram.” Rudolf Otto proposed the Greek name **Eranos**, meaning a shared feast or banquet to which each guest contributes. The often-repeated claim that **Carl Gustav Jung** told her to make it a “meeting place between East and West” is plausible and appears throughout Eranos institutional memory, but the exact wording and date vary. Sources commonly place their first meeting around 1930 at Keyserling’s School of Wisdom.

Her relationship with Jung altered the project. Later Jungian biographies say analysis with Jung freed her from Bailey’s Theosophy. That formulation originates within Jungian retrospective narrative and should not be treated as a neutral clinical finding. Documentary evidence does show a shift from explicitly Bailey-style esoteric graphics toward Jungian active imagination and archetypal research.

## Eranos Founded and Consolidated, 1933–1939

The first Eranos meeting took place at Casa Eranos in **August 1933**, under the theme *Yoga und Meditation im Osten und im Westen* (“Yoga and Meditation in East and West”). Olga selected the theme, recruited speakers, housed and fed participants, arranged the setting and images, and edited the papers. She was not merely a patron: she controlled the intellectual program and material conditions.

Early speakers and associates included Jung, Heinrich Zimmer, Erwin Rousselle, Károly Kerényi, Walter F. Otto, Mircea Eliade, Louis Massignon, Gershom Scholem, Henry Corbin, Adolf Portmann, Paul Tillich, Martin Buber, D. T. Suzuki, and others across psychology, history of religions, mythology, Indology, Islamology, Jewish mysticism, biology, anthropology, and philosophy. Not all attended in the 1930s; this network accumulated over decades.

The annual **Eranos-Jahrbuch**, published in Zürich by Rhein-Verlag, preserved lectures in German, French, Italian, and later English translation. HathiTrust’s catalogue records Olga as editor for 1933–61. She edited thirty annual volumes, a major publishing labor often obscured by emphasis on the male lecturers.

Eranos was deliberately non-university and non-doctrinal. Its method sought a conjunction of rigorous specialist knowledge and personally transformative experience. The setting—garden, round table, lecture room, Lake Maggiore, shared meals—was part of its epistemology. Critics regarded this as an elite, anti-rational, or syncretic enclave; supporters saw a rare space for interdisciplinary inquiry.

### The Eranos Archive

Beginning in the 1930s, initially to illustrate conference themes and Jung’s researches in alchemy and archetypes, Olga assembled photographs and reproductions of symbolic images from many civilizations. She worked at or obtained material from the Vatican Library, British Museum, Warburg Institute, Morgan Library, Bibliothèque nationale, and museums in Athens, among others.

Her classifications juxtaposed objects by visual analogy—Great Mother figures, masks, fish, trinities, three-headed gods, Navajo sand paintings—rather than only by conventional chronology or region. She described images as a respite from verbal logic and invoked the “magic wand of analogy.” The archive reached more than 6,000 core images in her hands; later ARAS holdings expanded far beyond that number.

The method’s power and problem are inseparable. It enabled striking cross-cultural comparison and supplied imagery for Jungian works, but it could detach sacred and ethnographic objects from local histories, collapse difference into universal archetypes, and reproduce colonial museum taxonomies. These are later scholarly critiques, not accusations of fraud.

### Visions and active imagination

Between approximately 1934 and 1938 Olga produced more than 300 drawings or pastels generally called the **Visions** series, using Jung’s practice of active imagination. Correspondence shows that the archive’s images and her own psychic imagery began to interpenetrate. Jung warned her about identification with archetypal forces; later correspondence with Erich Neumann explored her relation to the Great Mother. The unpublished album now called the **Blue Book** combines earlier theosophical images and later visionary works. It was not a book commercially published by Olga.

## War Years, Politics, and American Suspicion, 1939–1945

Neutral Switzerland allowed Eranos to continue during the Second World War, although travel restrictions changed participation. Olga repeatedly represented Eranos as a counterforce to National Socialism. Most of its major participants were not Nazis, and several were exiles or targets of Nazi racial policy. Alfons Rosenberg later praised her courage in sustaining such a forum in 1933.

The stronger claim—that Eranos functioned as an organized anti-Nazi institution—is harder to demonstrate. Hans Thomas Hakl’s history notes the difficulty of measuring Olga’s retrospective assertions. Eranos generally avoided explicit politics, offered no coordinated resistance program, and cultivated “inner” or spiritual renewal. The correct classification is therefore: **documented anti-Nazi self-understanding and refuge-like internationalism; disputed degree of active political resistance**.

The circle also carries the political ambiguities of twentieth-century depth psychology and religious studies. Some participants’ ideas were conservative, anti-modern, or susceptible to fascist appropriation; Jung’s conduct and writings in the early Nazi period remain controversial. Association does not establish Olga’s endorsement of every participant’s politics. No evidence consulted establishes that she joined a fascist party, collaborated with Nazi authorities, or engaged in political violence.

During the war, American authorities apparently viewed her with suspicion, likely because of transatlantic correspondence, international financial contacts, and Jung’s disputed reputation. William McGuire’s history of Bollingen says a sympathetic U.S. official familiar with Jungian thought cleared the suspicion. This is a **documented administrative suspicion in a secondary archival history**, not a charge or conviction, and no evidence located establishes espionage.

## The Mellons, Bollingen, and Institutional Finance, 1938–1950s

**Mary Conover Mellon** and **Paul Mellon**, both connected with Jung, entered Olga’s circle before the war. Mary and Olga became close friends. Mary’s wealth and commitment to Jungian publishing helped translate the Ascona network into the American **Bollingen Series** and eventually the Bollingen Foundation. **Cary F. Baynes**, translator and editor, worked on Eranos and Bollingen materials; her Wellcome archive preserves extensive correspondence with Olga.

The relationship was intellectual, emotional, and financial. Olga sought American support for Eranos, its image archive, and its publications. She hoped Bollingen or Paul Mellon might buy her property and retain her as custodian, thereby securing the institution and her livelihood. After Mary Mellon’s death in 1946 this hope collapsed. This episode complicates the mythology of Eranos as purely gift-based: it depended on inherited Kapteyn wealth, unpaid or underrecognized labor, publishing income, hospitality, donors, and negotiations with very wealthy patrons.

No evidence consulted indicates embezzlement, fraudulent accounting, money laundering, or a criminal financial structure. Exact budgets, ownership arrangements, tax status, and transfers require examination of Eranos property and foundation records.

## Postwar Archive, Eranos at its Height, and Later Years, 1946–1962

From 1946 Olga began sending photographic material to the **Warburg Institute** in London. Accounts differ on whether the collection was “given” in 1946 or accepted in full in 1956; the best synthesis is a staged transfer beginning in 1946 and formal institutional acceptance/completion around 1956. Duplicate or related collections developed through Jungian institutions in Zürich and the United States.

In 1960 the American archive took the name **Archive for Research in Archetypal Symbolism (ARAS)**. Its later expansion was driven by three women in particular: Olga, Mary Mellon, and **Jessie Fraser**. The archive became a research tool for analysts, artists, and scholars and eventually a digital database.

Eranos reached extraordinary international prestige after the war. Olga continued choosing themes, corresponding with speakers, managing the houses, staging the annual gathering, selecting papers, and editing every yearbook through the 1961 volume. She cultivated an atmosphere at once familial and hieratic. Participants repeatedly described her as the “guiding genius,” hostess, or mother of the feast.

This maternal role had tensions. A later quantitative study reports that fewer than five percent of Eranos speakers between 1933 and 1988 were women—seven of 154—and records a reputation that Olga was especially ill-disposed toward women. The statistic is verifiable within that study; the characterization of her attitude is a reported reputation requiring case-by-case archival testing. Her own leadership did not translate into gender parity at the lectern.

Her later art and correspondence increasingly centered on the **Great Mother**. Chloë Sugden interprets this self-identification as a strategy for claiming feminine spiritual authority through hosting, archiving, and image-making. That is a modern scholarly interpretation grounded in her works and correspondence, not a psychiatric diagnosis.

Olga outlived Jung by less than a year. She died at Casa Gabriella on 25 April 1962. The specific illness and cause of death are not established by the consulted sources. Claims that she was 81 are arithmetically mistaken if the October 1881 birth date is accepted; she was 80.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, Property, and Financial Interests

### Casa Gabriella and Casa Eranos

Casa Gabriella was her residence, family property, archive workplace, guesthouse, and administrative center. Casa Eranos, built in 1928, served as lecture hall and communal space. Their integration made private property into an intellectual institution. Ownership chronology and inheritance arrangements are incompletely published.

### Eranos conferences and yearbooks

Eranos was neither a conventional university nor initially a modern grant foundation. It functioned through Olga’s ownership and direction, visiting scholars, publishing arrangements with Rhein-Verlag, participant contributions, and private patronage. “Eranos” itself signaled a common feast, but authority was centralized in Olga’s invitations and editorial decisions.

### Eranos Archive / ARAS

This was her most technically innovative project: photography, cataloguing, index cards, thematic cross-referencing, and comparative iconography created an analogue image database decades before digitization. It generated no patent and was not a conventional commercial company. Its institutional descendants include the Warburg-held Eranos collection and ARAS centers in the United States.

### Publishing

Olga edited rather than authored most volumes bearing her name. The *Eranos-Jahrbücher* are collective proceedings; English selections such as *Man and Time*, *Man and Transformation*, *Man and Rite*, and *Spiritual Disciplines* compile other lecturers’ papers. Catalogue records that list her as an author must be read as editorial responsibility.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

1. **Reform household:** feminism, anarchist and social-reform sociability, applied art, photography.
2. **Dress reform and classicism:** the Jolles circle joined craft, ancient form, and transformed daily life.
3. **Bereavement and Lebensreform:** widowhood and Monte Verità preceded an intensified spiritual search.
4. **Theosophical phase:** Bailey collaboration, esoteric cosmology, disciplined meditation, geometric prints.
5. **Comparative East–West project:** Otto, Wilhelm, Keyserling, and the planned “ashram.”
6. **Jungian-archetypal phase:** analysis, active imagination, Visions, comparative image archive.
7. **Great Mother identification:** maternal hosting became both an institutional role and self-symbolization.

She was not a systematic academic theorist. Her distinctive contribution lay in assembling situations, people, images, and publications through which theory could be produced.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Unresolved Questions

### Political quietism and anti-Nazi claims

- **Documented:** Eranos began in 1933, welcomed an international network, and Olga described it as countervailing National Socialism.
- **Supported context:** many speakers opposed Nazism or were exiles.
- **Disputed:** whether Eranos amounted to political resistance rather than inward, elite cultural withdrawal.
- **Not established:** Nazi membership, collaboration, intelligence work, or political violence by Olga.

### Wartime American suspicion

- **Documented in McGuire’s archival institutional history:** U.S. authorities suspected her and an official cleared the matter.
- **Unresolved:** exact agency, file, legal category, and evidence.
- **Not established:** arrest, indictment, prosecution, espionage, or conviction.

### Gender hierarchy

- **Documented:** women were a very small minority of speakers despite a woman founder.
- **Reported claim:** Olga had a reputation for hostility toward women.
- **Assessment:** the numerical imbalance is firm; motive and personality claims remain interpretive without a fuller correspondence study.

### Universalism and cultural extraction

- **Documented method:** images from many cultures were removed from original sequences and rearranged under archetypal headings.
- **Modern critique:** the archive can dehistoricize objects and reproduce colonial collecting structures.
- **Counterpoint:** Olga recorded sources, traveled to collections, and intended a research instrument rather than ownership of original artifacts. No evidence located indicates illicit antiquities trafficking.

### Finances and patronage

- **Documented:** inherited wealth and Mellon/Bollingen support were essential; Olga unsuccessfully sought a property arrangement after Mary Mellon’s death.
- **Unresolved:** detailed annual budgets and property title history.
- **No evidence located:** fraud, corruption, money laundering, or criminal financial conduct.

### Psychological interpretation

Jung’s warnings about identification with archetypes, and Olga’s accounts of images entering her psychic life, have encouraged retrospective diagnoses. No responsible diagnosis can be made from those materials. “Possession by the Great Mother” belongs to Jungian interpretive language, not a documented medical finding.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### “The mother of Eranos”

1. **Story:** Olga spontaneously generated a sacred intellectual family around her round table.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** participant memoirs, institutional histories, Eranos tributes, and Jungian writing, especially after the war.
3. **Evidence:** extensive correspondence, editing, hospitality, property, and program control substantiate her centrality.
4. **Against literalization:** Eranos also depended on family capital, domestic labor, publishers, donors, translators, and famous speakers.
5. **Effect:** the myth preserved her importance but often converted concrete administrative labor into feminine “nurture.”

### The divinely destined meeting place of East and West

1. **Story:** Jung gave an empty hall its destined mission; Otto supplied the providential name.
2. **Circulation:** Eranos origin narratives and later biographies.
3. **Evidence:** both men demonstrably influenced the project; the first theme enacted the East–West program.
4. **Qualification:** Olga had planned a study center by 1927–28 and had Bailey/Keyserling networks before Jung’s decisive involvement.
5. **Effect:** the compressed story magnifies Jung and understates Olga’s prior conception.

### Eranos as anti-Nazi resistance

1. **Story:** founding Eranos in 1933 constituted cultural opposition to Hitler.
2. **Circulation:** Olga’s later declarations and sympathetic recollections, including Rosenberg.
3. **Evidence for:** international and exile participation; refusal of Nazi ideological control.
4. **Evidence against a strong version:** political reticence and absence of organized resistance activity.
5. **Effect:** gave Eranos a morally heroic wartime memory; historians retain a more limited formulation.

### Olga as archetypal Great Mother

1. **Story:** her identity and institution embodied the universal maternal archetype.
2. **Circulation:** Olga’s own images and correspondence, Neumannian/Jungian interpretation, participant reminiscence, recent scholarship.
3. **Evidence:** explicit imagery, self-description, maternal hosting, and recurring symbolic identification.
4. **Qualification:** this was a self-fashioning and interpretive framework, not a factual supernatural status.
5. **Effect:** strengthened her authority while obscuring biography, motherhood’s difficulties, money, and administration.

### A Jungian “conversion” from Theosophy

1. **Story:** analysis by Jung liberated Olga from Theosophy and redirected her toward psychology.
2. **Circulation:** Jungian biography, notably Gerhard Wehr and derivative accounts.
3. **Evidence:** chronological shift in affiliations and art; documented Jung relationship.
4. **Qualification:** her later work retained esoteric, comparative-religious, and visionary continuities.
5. **Effect:** supports a triumphalist Jungian genealogy that minimizes Bailey and Olga’s own agency.

## Works by Olga Fröbe-Kapteyn

### Artworks and unpublished primary works

- **Theosophical Prints** (c. 1928–30), a set of fourteen color screenprints. Titles recorded in museum/exhibition sources include *Kether, The Crown*; *The Chalice in the Heart*; *The Cosmic Incarnation*; *The Creation*; *The Grail*; *The Light of the Soul*; *The Divine Breath*; and related untitled or differently titled sheets. Genuine artworks; later cataloguing and titles may vary.
- **Visions**, Series I and II (c. 1934–38), more than 300 pastel, pencil, and mixed-media works generated through active imagination. Genuine works, largely unpublished during her lifetime.
- **The Blue Book** (modern descriptive title), a privately assembled album/anthology combining two periods of art. It is not evidence of a lifetime publication under that title. Facsimile and study published posthumously by Riccardo Bernardini in 2025.
- **The Eranos Archive** (1930s–1950s), more than 6,000 selected, photographed, mounted, annotated, and classified images. This is a curatorial/research work, not original authorship of the reproduced art.
- **“The Eranos Archive”**, unpublished manuscript, Cary Baynes Archive, Wellcome Collection, PP/CBA/A.1/3.
- **“Draft of Preface to American Eranos Anthology”** (July 1941), Cary Baynes Archive, PP/CBA/C.1/2.
- Extensive **correspondence** with Jung, Mary Mellon, Cary Baynes, Ximena de Angulo, Fritz Saxl, Erich Neumann, Ernst Bernhard, speakers, publishers, and institutions. Genuine letters; dispersed and incompletely published.

### Editorial work

- **Eranos-Jahrbuch**, vols. I–XXX (conference years 1933–1961), Zürich: Rhein-Verlag. Fröbe-Kapteyn was founder-editor and organizer; the substantive essays are by individual lecturers.
- English-language selections from the yearbooks, including **Man and Time** (Bollingen Series XXX, 1957), **Man and Transformation** (1964), **Man and Rite** (English edition/selection associated with 1950s publication), **Man and His Symbols/Man and Meaning**-type catalogue variants, and **Spiritual Disciplines: Papers from the Eranos Yearbooks** (1960). Exact title/date data vary by edition. Her role was selection/organization/editorial, not sole authorship.

### Authorship warning

Library catalogues and booksellers sometimes place “Olga Fröbe-Kapteyn” in the author field for collective Eranos volumes. These should be catalogued as **edited works** unless a specific preface, introduction, or essay bears her name.

### Exhibitions

- Posthumous exhibitions began by 1978, though a full exhibition list has not been reconstructed here.
- **The Keeper**, New Museum, New York (2016), included her archival/artistic practice.
- **Women in Abstraction**, Centre Pompidou, Paris (2021), repositioned her as a modern abstract artist rather than only Jung’s collaborator.
- **Olga Fröbe-Kapteyn – Tiefes Wissen**, Kunsthalle Mainz (2023).
- **Olga Fröbe-Kapteyn: Artista–Ricercatrice**, Museo Casa Rusca, Locarno (2024).
- Her prints entered the Metropolitan Museum of Art collection in 2023; market and museum visibility increased sharply in the mid-2020s.

### Archives

- **Eranos Foundation Archive**, Casa Gabriella/Casa Eranos, Ascona: correspondence, organizational files, photographs, artworks.
- **Warburg Institute Archive**, London: Eranos image collection and correspondence with Fritz Saxl/Warburg staff.
- **Wellcome Collection**, London, Cary F. Baynes papers: extensive Eranos/Bollingen work correspondence, including Olga manuscripts and letters.
- **Library of Congress**, Bollingen Foundation records: Mellon and foundation correspondence.
- **ETH Zürich Research Collection**: recent dissertation/article research and digital scholarship.
- **ARAS**, New York and affiliated centers: derivative and expanded archetypal image archive.
- **Metropolitan Museum of Art**, New York: complete set of fourteen theosophical screenprints.

No dedicated theatrical film or major broadcast documentary about her was located in the sources consulted. Recent exhibition videos, lectures, and online art features exist but should not be confused with archival documentary films.

## Death and Posthumous Reputation

Olga died at Casa Gabriella on 25 April 1962. Eranos continued under other organizers, including Adolf Portmann and later generations. Her death did not end the meetings, yearbooks, property institution, or archive, but for decades her identity was subordinated to the celebrated men she convened.

Her reputation has moved through several phases:

1. **Participant memory (1930s–60s):** formidable hostess, organizer, editor, “guiding genius.”
2. **Jungian institutional memory:** disciple/collaborator who supplied archetypal images and a setting for Jung’s mature work.
3. **Intellectual history:** creator of a para-academic international forum important to religious studies, depth psychology, and the humanities.
4. **Critical revision:** scrutiny of political quietism, universalism, gender hierarchy, elite patronage, and colonial image taxonomies.
5. **Art-historical rediscovery:** recognition since the 2010s as an abstract and visionary artist with an oeuvre independent of Jung.
6. **Feminist and media-archaeological reinterpretation:** emphasis on invisible organizational labor, female patronage networks, and the archive as an early image database.

The most defensible legacy claim is institutional: she created durable infrastructures—place, conference, publication series, network, and image archive—that changed how twentieth-century scholars compared religion, myth, psyche, and symbol.

## Research Limitations

- Civil certificates, probate records, land registers, and medical/death records were not accessible in the web corpus consulted.
- Family sources disagree on the birth day and the sequence/details concerning the twins.
- Archival correspondence is dispersed and only partly digitized; several conclusions depend on published archival studies.
- A complete item-level catalogue of every letter, drawing, and editorial preface would require on-site archival work.
- No reliable evidence was found for criminal conduct, imprisonment, formal political office, awards, patents, addiction, or a diagnosed psychiatric disorder.

## Atlas Connections

### Aby Warburg

- **[T9-] Documented fact:** Fröbe-Kapteyn had no documented personal contact with Warburg, who died in 1929. The connection is institutional and posthumous: beginning in 1935 she consulted and corresponded with staff of the photographic collection descended from Warburg’s work, visited it repeatedly, and in the 1950s deposited/donated her Eranos image archive to the **Warburg Institute** in London. She is therefore the later party in a shared-site/archive lineage. [Visual Resources](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/01973762.2014.936097); [Eranos Foundation](https://www.eranosfoundation.org/page.php?page=19&pagename=iconographic+collection)

### Carl Gustav Jung

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Fröbe-Kapteyn first met Jung around 1930 at Hermann Keyserling’s School of Wisdom in Darmstadt. Jung subsequently became her analyst, adviser, and the principal recurring intellectual presence at the Eranos conferences she organized in Ascona. [Eranos Foundation history](https://www.eranosfoundation.org/page.php?page=4&pagename=history)
- **[T3] Documented fact:** Their relationship included extensive correspondence and direct intellectual collaboration. At Jung’s request, Fröbe-Kapteyn began assembling symbolic images in the mid-1930s; the resulting archive supplied illustrations and research material for Jung’s *Psychology and Alchemy* and related work. [Eranos iconographic collection](https://www.eranosfoundation.org/page.php?page=19&pagename=iconographic+collection)

### Charlotte Bara

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Fröbe-Kapteyn and the dancer Charlotte Bara met at the second Eranos conference at Ascona in August 1934; Bara appears on its participant list. Both also created purpose-built cultural spaces near Monte Verità—Casa Eranos and Teatro San Materno—and shared acquaintances including Leo Kok. [Fondazione Cini/Eranos conference abstracts](https://eranosfoundation.org/images/uploads/attachments/1668418374_FondazioneCini-Eranos-2022.pdf)

### Erwin Schrödinger

- **[T4] Documented fact:** Schrödinger lectured at Fröbe-Kapteyn’s Eranos meeting in Ascona, 2–9 August 1946, presenting “Der Geist der Naturwissenschaft”; she organized the conference and edited the yearbook containing his contribution. [Eranos lectures catalogue](https://eranosfoundation.org/page.php?page=12&pagename=lectures)
- **[T3] Documented fact:** Archival holdings record a letter from Fröbe-Kapteyn to Schrödinger dated 27 October 1946 at Ascona, enclosing a draft program for the coming Eranos meetings, as well as an undated related letter/program draft for 1947. [University of Vienna Schrödinger-document inventory](https://services.phaidra.univie.ac.at/api/object/o%3A2085354/download)

### Giuseppe Tucci

- **[T4] Documented fact:** Tucci attended Fröbe-Kapteyn’s Eranos conference at Ascona, 19–27 August 1953, and delivered “Earth in India and Tibet.” Fröbe-Kapteyn organized the meeting and edited *Eranos-Jahrbuch* XXII in which the lecture appeared. This establishes shared presence at Eranos, but the consulted evidence does not independently establish friendship. [Eranos lectures catalogue](https://www.eranosfoundation.org/page.php?page=12&pagename=lectures)

### Hermann Keyserling

- **[T4] Documented fact:** During the late 1920s or around 1930, Fröbe-Kapteyn participated in the milieu of Keyserling’s **Schule der Weisheit** at Darmstadt, where she first met Jung and encountered a circle pursuing the comparative “common root” of religions. The school also provided an institutional model for her developing Eranos project; the available source establishes the shared institutional milieu more securely than a specific personal meeting with Keyserling. [Eranos Foundation history](https://www.eranosfoundation.org/page.php?page=4&pagename=history)

### Ludwig Derleth

- **[T5] Reported fact:** Eranos institutional history identifies the Munich poet Derleth as Fröbe-Kapteyn’s first profound personal contact with symbolism and as an important influence on Eranos’s gestation during the 1920s. The accessible account does not supply precise meeting dates or surviving correspondence. [Eranos Foundation historical account](https://eranosfoundation.org/PRE/history_b.htm)

### Martin Buber

- **[T4] Documented fact:** Buber attended the second Eranos gathering at Ascona, 20 August–1 September 1934, delivering two connected lectures on symbolic and sacramental existence in Judaism; Fröbe-Kapteyn organized and hosted the meeting and edited the resulting yearbook. [Eranos Foundation](https://www.eranosfoundation.org/page.php?page=17&pagename=aragno)
- **[T3] Documented fact:** The Martin Buber Archives preserve multiple letters from Fröbe-Kapteyn to Buber—including letters dated 1 October 1933, 7 and 9 March 1934, 3 and 13 August 1934, and 27 July 1935—concerning invitations, participants, programs, and Buber’s inability to return in 1935 after Nazi restrictions on his teaching. [University of Chicago dissertation citing the archival files](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/record/445/files/Shonkoff_uchicago_0330D_14542.pdf); [Journal of Religion article](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/715776)

### Mircea Eliade

- **[T4] Documented fact:** Eliade repeatedly lectured at Fröbe-Kapteyn’s Eranos conferences in Ascona from 1950 through her final years as organizer, including the meetings of 1950, 1952, 1953, 1954, 1956, and 1957. She hosted the gatherings and edited the volumes containing his papers. [Eranos lectures catalogue](https://eranosfoundation.org/page.php?page=12&pagename=lectures)
- **[T3] Documented fact:** Eliade explicitly reflected in print on the Eranos meetings and quoted Fröbe-Kapteyn’s metaphor comparing their intellectual movement to a dance. Her image archive also furnished research material for his *The Forge and the Crucible* (1956), creating a documented two-way intellectual exchange between organizer-archivist and scholar. [Fondazione Cini/Eranos abstracts](https://eranosfoundation.org/images/uploads/attachments/1668418374_FondazioneCini-Eranos-2022.pdf); [Eranos iconographic collection](https://www.eranosfoundation.org/page.php?page=19&pagename=iconographic+collection)

### Rudolf Ritsema

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Ritsema belonged to Eranos during Fröbe-Kapteyn’s lifetime and became part of its succession structure. As her health and active participation declined around 1960–61, she reportedly entrusted him with overall administrative authority so that the meetings would continue; after her death he assisted Adolf Portmann and later directed and edited Eranos activities. [Historical study of Eranos](https://www.rdpc.uevora.pt/bitstream/10174/37094/1/Doutoramento-Filosofia-Christian_Carlos_Kuhn.pdf); [Eranos yearbooks catalogue](https://www.eranosfoundation.org/page.php?page=10&pagename=yearbooks)

No reliable documented crossing was found for the remaining roster members. In particular, broad overlap with Monte Verità, Jungianism, Theosophy, comparative religion, or the Warburg tradition was not counted without evidence of correspondence, encounter, precise shared presence, written engagement, or a qualifying shared object/site.
