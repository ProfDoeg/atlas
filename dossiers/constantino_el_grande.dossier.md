# Constantine the Great: Research Dossier

## Scope, identity, and evidentiary method

This dossier concerns the Roman emperor conventionally known as Constantine the Great: **Flavius Valerius Constantinus**, born at Naissus in the central Balkans, probably on 27 February in the early 270s, and emperor from 306 until his death on 22 May 337.

The evidence is substantial but uneven. Constantine is known through:

- Contemporary or near-contemporary Christian writers, especially Lactantius and Eusebius of Caesarea.
- Official letters and enactments preserved by Eusebius, Lactantius, Optatus, Athanasius, the *Codex Theodosianus*, and the *Codex Justinianus*.
- Latin imperial panegyrics, coins, inscriptions, monuments, and archaeological remains.
- Concise later narratives such as Aurelius Victor, Eutropius, the *Epitome de Caesaribus*, the *Origo Constantini Imperatoris* or first *Excerptum Valesianum*, Socrates, Sozomen, Philostorgius, Jerome, and Zosimus.
- Later hagiography and medieval political legends.

Eusebius’s *Vita Constantini* is indispensable but overtly celebratory, the Christian equivalent of an imperial encomium. It suppresses Constantine’s executions of Crispus and Fausta and presents military and political conflicts providentially. Lactantius is closer to the western court but likewise polemical. Pagan or anti-Constantinian accounts—particularly Zosimus—preserve hostile traditions, sometimes uniquely, but were written considerably later. Official laws and letters are direct evidence of promulgated policy, not proof that policy was uniformly enforced.

The labels used below are:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary documentary, material, or multiple strong narrative sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a historical source but not independently demonstrated.
- **Allegation or theory:** an ancient or modern explanatory claim for which decisive evidence is absent.
- **Rumor or myth:** legendary, folkloric, propagandistic, forged, or supernatural material presented for its historical influence rather than as verified biography.

### Birth-date problem

**Documented fact:** Ancient sources agree that Constantine was born on 27 February, but the year is not securely established. Calculations from Eusebius’s statements about his age support approximately 272/273, the conventional dating. Other reconstructions extend toward 280 or later. Claims that he was born as late as 285 make parts of his early military career difficult to accommodate. “27 February c.272/273” is therefore the strongest working conclusion, not an absolutely demonstrated date.

### Physical representation

Coins and the surviving colossal marble portrait from Rome show a tall-faced emperor with large, upward-looking eyes and an increasingly idealized, clean-shaven image. These portraits are political constructions rather than neutral likenesses. The later Constantinian style deliberately evoked Alexander the Great and broke with the blocklike collegiate imagery of the Tetrarchy.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father: Constantius I

**Documented fact:** Constantine’s father was **Flavius Valerius Constantius**, commonly called Constantius Chlorus, a successful Illyrian military officer. He became Caesar under Maximian in 293 and Augustus in 305. “Chlorus,” meaning pale or greenish, is a later distinguishing epithet rather than a normal name used during his life.

Constantius’s ancestry is obscure. Later Constantinian propaganda connected the family to the third-century emperor Claudius II Gothicus. Most modern scholars treat this as a manufactured dynastic pedigree intended to supply an established imperial ancestor.

### Mother: Helena

**Documented fact:** Constantine’s mother was **Helena**, later Flavia Julia Helena Augusta and Saint Helena. Her birthplace is uncertain. Procopius later associated her with Drepanum in Bithynia, subsequently renamed Helenopolis; that association does not prove that she was born there. Ambrose’s description of her as a *stabularia* has been translated as innkeeper, stable-maid, or woman connected with a posting station. It signals modest origins but is too late and rhetorically charged to establish her occupation.

The legal form of Constantius and Helena’s relationship is disputed. She is variously described as wife, concubine, or a partner in a customary union later dissolved for dynastic reasons. Constantine’s legitimacy was politically questioned by enemies, but no contemporary legal record resolves the marital status.

Around 289, Constantius separated from Helena and married **Theodora**, the daughter or stepdaughter of Emperor Maximian, consolidating his place in the tetrarchic system. Helena then disappears from the record until Constantine’s ascendancy. Constantine later elevated her to Augusta, issued coins in her name, gave her access to imperial resources, and commemorated her publicly. Recent scholarship emphasizes that her visibility was part of Constantine’s deliberate dynastic reconstruction as well as evidence of personal honor.

### Half-siblings

Constantius and Theodora produced six children conventionally identified as:

- **Flavius Dalmatius**
- **Julius Constantius**
- **Hannibalianus**
- **Constantia**, who married Licinius
- **Anastasia**, who married Bassianus
- **Eutropia**, mother of Nepotianus

Constantine incorporated several members of this branch into his regime. Most adult male members or their sons were killed in the dynastic purge following Constantine’s death in 337. Whether Constantine designed that outcome is unknown.

### Partners, wives, and children

#### Minervina

**Documented or strongly reported:** Constantine’s earliest known partner was **Minervina**. The nature of their union is uncertain: ancient terminology and later dynastic presentation do not show conclusively whether she was a legal wife, concubine, or partner in a marriage later set aside.

Their son was:

- **Flavius Julius Crispus**, born probably c.295–305; Caesar from 317; executed in 326.

#### Fausta

Constantine married **Flavia Maxima Fausta**, daughter of Maximian and sister of Maxentius, at Trier in 307. The marriage sealed a political alliance with Maximian and gave Constantine a tetrarchic dynastic connection. Fausta became Augusta in 324 and died violently in 326.

Their securely identified children were:

- **Constantine II**, born in 316, Caesar in 317, emperor 337–340.
- **Constantius II**, born 7 August 317, Caesar in 324, emperor 337–361.
- **Constans**, born probably c.320–323, Caesar in 333, emperor 337–350.
- **Constantina**, later wife of Hannibalianus and then Gallus Caesar.
- **Helena**, later wife of the emperor Julian.

A child referred to in a law of 322 is sometimes reconstructed as an otherwise unknown offspring. The evidence is inconclusive.

### Dynastic marriages

Constantine used marriage to contain rivals and join competing imperial houses:

- His marriage to Fausta linked him to Maximian.
- His half-sister Constantia married Licinius in 313.
- His half-sister Anastasia married Bassianus, whom Constantine later attempted to install as Caesar.
- His daughter Constantina married Hannibalianus.
- His daughter Helena later married Julian, although that occurred after Constantine’s death.

These arrangements did not prevent civil war, executions, or the near-destruction of collateral branches of the family.

## Childhood and Early Life

Almost nothing reliable is known about Constantine’s childhood at Naissus. His father’s career probably meant repeated movement through the Balkan and Danubian military environment.

By 293, Constantius had entered the imperial college as Caesar. Constantine, already a young adult under the conventional chronology, was retained at the eastern courts of Diocletian and Galerius. This has often been described as “hostage” status. The word captures the political leverage involved, but no source calls him a formally imprisoned hostage. He was also an imperial officer and prospective dynastic asset.

### Early residences and movements

The likely sequence includes:

- Naissus and the Balkan provinces during childhood.
- Nicomedia, Diocletian’s eastern capital.
- The Danube frontier.
- Syria and Mesopotamia during campaigns against Persia.
- Palestine or Egypt according to less certain reconstructions.
- Departure through the Balkans and Gaul to join Constantius in 305–306.
- Britain in 306.

### Military formation

**Reported fact:** Constantine served under Diocletian and Galerius, campaigning on the Danube and in the Persian war of the later 290s. Later panegyrics represent him as physically imposing, energetic, brave, and naturally fitted to rule. Such descriptions belong to court rhetoric but accord with the speed and personal direction of his later campaigns.

No formal school, curriculum, or identifiable teacher is documented. His education was that of a late Roman military aristocrat: Latin administration, court ceremonial, military command, imperial ideology, patronage, lawmaking, and familiarity with Greek-speaking eastern institutions. He later spoke to Greek audiences but probably relied on interpreters or prepared translations for sophisticated theological communication.

### The Great Persecution

Constantine was present in the eastern imperial environment when Diocletian and Galerius began the Great Persecution in 303. Eusebius insists that he did not share the persecutors’ conduct. No contemporary evidence shows that Constantine initiated persecution, but neither can his precise role or response be reconstructed. Later Christian presentation had a strong interest in separating him from the persecuting court.

## Education and Formation

Constantine’s formation came from institutions and political experiences rather than a documented literary academy.

### Principal formative influences

- **Constantius I:** military command, frontier government, and a comparatively restrained image toward Christians.
- **Diocletian:** mobile imperial government, court hierarchy, fiscal reorganization, the separation of civil and military roles, and the concept of several emperors governing one empire.
- **Galerius:** the dangers of dynastic exclusion and military patronage.
- **Maximian:** an initially useful patron and father-in-law who became a rival.
- **Solar imperial religion:** Constantine publicly favored Apollo or Sol, especially after separating his legitimacy from the tetrarchic Hercules.
- **Christian clergy and intellectuals:** after 312, Hosius of Corduba, Eusebius of Caesarea, Eusebius of Nicomedia, Lactantius, and numerous bishops served as advisers, interpreters, petitioners, or court intellectuals.
- **Civil war:** repeated victories taught Constantine to present political enemies as tyrants, sacrilegious persecutors, or enemies of divine order.

### Lactantius

The Christian rhetorician Lactantius was eventually associated with Constantine’s court and reportedly taught Crispus. His direct personal influence on Constantine is possible but cannot be measured.

### Hosius of Corduba

Hosius was one of Constantine’s most important ecclesiastical advisers in the 310s and 320s. He carried imperial communications in the dispute surrounding Arius, presided or held a leading place at councils, and connected the court with western episcopal practice.

### Eusebius of Caesarea

Eusebius was not a lifelong intimate adviser in the modern sense. He met Constantine in ecclesiastical and ceremonial settings and became the principal literary architect of the emperor’s posthumous Christian image. Constantine commissioned fifty copies of scripture for churches in the new capital through Eusebius, according to a letter preserved in the *Life*.

## Early Career and Accession, c.293–306

### Service under Diocletian and Galerius

**Reported fact:** Constantine served as a military tribune or senior officer in the east, participated in Danubian operations, and took part in campaigning connected with the Persian settlement of 298. The later sources magnify his courage and Galerius’s jealousy; stories that Galerius deliberately exposed him to lethal combat may reflect retrospective heroic narrative.

### The succession crisis of 305

On 1 May 305, Diocletian and Maximian abdicated. Constantius and Galerius became Augusti; Severus and Maximinus Daia became Caesars. Constantine and Maxentius, adult imperial sons with plausible dynastic expectations, were excluded.

Constantine obtained permission to leave Galerius’s court and join his father. Lactantius tells an adventure story in which he departed at speed and disabled post horses behind him to prevent pursuit. The core fact—his rapid journey west—is credible; the sabotage detail is unverified.

### Britain and proclamation at York

Constantine joined Constantius for a northern British campaign, probably against Picts or other groups beyond the imperial frontier. Constantius died at Eboracum, modern York, on 25 July 306.

**Documented fact:** The army proclaimed Constantine emperor. Later sources claim Constantius designated him. Galerius refused to recognize him as Augustus but accepted him as Caesar, while promoting Severus to Augustus. Constantine temporarily accepted this formal compromise while continuing to display imperial authority in Gaul and Britain.

## Chronological Life History

## 306–310: Rule in Gaul, Britain, and Spain

Constantine’s initial power base comprised Britain, Gaul, and probably Spain. His principal residences included Trier—Augusta Treverorum—and the mobile military court.

### Rhine wars

He campaigned against Franks and Alamanni and repaired or defended Rhine installations. A panegyric reports that two captured Frankish kings, Ascaric and Merogaisus, were thrown to wild beasts in the Trier arena.

- **Reported fact:** The execution is described approvingly in contemporary court rhetoric.
- **Context:** It was exemplary imperial violence against defeated foreign leaders, not an exceptional scandal to the panegyrist.
- **Uncertainty:** Exact circumstances and whether the captives had violated a treaty cannot be independently verified.

### Building and residence at Trier

The surviving imperial baths and basilica at Trier belong to the monumental development of Constantine’s court city, although attribution and construction phases require archaeological qualification. Trier was an administrative, minting, and military center rather than a modern fixed national capital.

### Marriage to Fausta and alliance with Maximian

In 307 Maximian recognized Constantine as Augustus and married Fausta to him. Constantine set aside or had already lost Minervina. The alliance improved his dynastic standing but tied him to a volatile former emperor.

### Carnuntum, 308

At the conference of Carnuntum, Diocletian, Maximian, and Galerius attempted to restore order. Licinius was made Augustus. Constantine was formally reduced or left with the rank of Caesar. He did not surrender his practical position and increasingly styled himself Augustus.

### Maximian’s last revolt, 310

While Constantine campaigned on the Rhine, Maximian proclaimed that Constantine was dead and seized authority at Arles. Constantine returned rapidly; Maximian fled to Massilia, was surrendered, and died.

- **Documented fact:** Maximian died after his failed rebellion.
- **Reported fact:** Sources describe suicide by hanging after Constantine permitted or ordered him to choose his death.
- **Later allegation:** Lactantius says Fausta exposed a subsequent assassination plot in which Maximian planned to murder Constantine in bed.
- **Assessment:** Constantine was responsible at least for the political circumstances and probably the sentence. The dramatic bedroom plot may be court justification.

### The Apollo vision at Grand

A Latin panegyric of 310 says Constantine saw Apollo accompanied by Victory, offering laurel wreaths marked with the number thirty. This is contemporary evidence for a visionary or rhetorically constructed solar experience two years before the Christian victory narrative.

The episode matters because it shows that imperial visions were not uniquely Christian and that Constantine’s religious public language evolved through solar monotheism rather than by a documented instantaneous replacement of paganism.

---

## 311–313: From toleration to the Milvian Bridge

### Galerius’s toleration edict

In 311, the dying Galerius ended the Great Persecution and permitted Christians to assemble, asking them to pray for the empire. Constantine was not the first emperor to legalize Christian worship. His later settlement with Licinius broadened restoration and toleration.

### Invasion of Italy

In 312 Constantine crossed the Alps with a mobile field army. He defeated Maxentius’s forces at Segusium, Turin, Brescia, and Verona. At Verona, Maxentius’s prefect Ruricius Pompeianus was killed. Constantine advanced toward Rome rather than attempting a long siege.

### The vision or dream before the Milvian Bridge

The evidence comes in stages:

1. **Lactantius, written relatively soon after the battle:** Constantine was instructed in a dream to mark the “heavenly sign of God” on his soldiers’ shields. Lactantius describes a crossed or superimposed form, commonly understood as an early Chi-Rho.
2. **Eusebius’s *Ecclesiastical History*:** Gives a general providential victory but not the developed sky vision.
3. **Eusebius’s later *Life of Constantine*:** Constantine and his army saw a luminous trophy of the cross above the sun with the message “By this conquer”; Christ then appeared in a dream and ordered a standard made. Eusebius says Constantine confirmed the account to him under oath.
4. **The Arch of Constantine, dedicated in 315:** Attributes victory to *instinctu divinitatis*, “by the prompting/inspiration of a divinity,” without identifying Christ.
5. **Coins:** Constantine continued issuing Sol imagery for years after 312.

**Historical conclusion:** Constantine associated his victory with the Christian God and adopted a Christ-associated military sign by or soon after the battle. The precise visual phenomenon and the expanded public sky vision are not independently verifiable. The variations may reflect evolving memory, theology, and imperial presentation.

### Battle of the Milvian Bridge, 28 October 312

Maxentius crossed the Tiber and fought north of Rome. His army collapsed toward the river; a bridge arrangement failed, and Maxentius drowned. His severed head was paraded in Rome and sent to Africa as proof of his defeat.

Constantine entered Rome, dissolved the Praetorian Guard that had supported Maxentius, and dismantled its camp. He retained much of Maxentius’s building program, rededicating or completing monuments in his own name. The colossal statue and the Basilica of Maxentius and Constantine exemplify this appropriation.

### Arch of Constantine

Dedicated on 25 July 315, the arch commemorated the victory and Constantine’s decennalia. It reused reliefs from monuments of Trajan, Hadrian, and Marcus Aurelius. Older interpretations treated this “spolia” as evidence of artistic decline; later research emphasizes deliberate ideological selection, economy, workshop practice, and Constantine’s presentation as successor to admired emperors.

The inscription calls Maxentius a tyrant and Constantine the liberator who fought a just war. It is official victory propaganda, not a neutral account.

### Milan, 313

Constantine and Licinius met at Milan. Licinius married Constantia. Their policy statement granted Christians and all others freedom to follow their chosen religion and required restoration of confiscated Christian property.

The familiar name “Edict of Milan” is conventional. No freestanding edict with that title survives. The text is preserved in Lactantius’s Latin account and Eusebius’s Greek version as an imperial rescript or directive following the Milan agreement. It did not make Christianity the empire’s official religion; it established broad toleration and restitution.

## 313–316: Patronage of churches and the Donatist conflict

Constantine began granting Christian clergy privileges, returning property, financing basilicas, and treating episcopal organizations as useful corporate bodies.

Important foundations or patronage traditionally associated with his regime include:

- The Lateran basilica in Rome.
- Old St Peter’s over the venerated tomb of Peter.
- A basilica associated with Paul on the Ostian Way, initially smaller than its later replacement.
- Churches in Jerusalem, Bethlehem, Mamre, Antioch, Nicomedia, and Constantinople.
- The Church of the Holy Sepulchre complex, developed after clearance of the site occupied by a Roman temple.

Precise initiative is sometimes divided among Constantine, Helena, bishops, and later builders. “Constantinian” can refer to imperial funding or construction phase rather than Constantine’s personal architectural design.

### Donatist controversy

North African Christians disputed the legitimacy of Caecilian, bishop of Carthage. His opponents alleged that his consecrator had been a *traditor*—someone who surrendered scripture during persecution. Constantine first used episcopal adjudication:

- Roman council under Miltiades, 313.
- Council of Arles, 314.
- Imperial appellate involvement afterward.

When Donatists rejected adverse decisions, Constantine ordered coercive measures and confiscations. Violence followed. By 321 he retreated from direct coercion and urged patience, though the schism survived for generations.

**Significance:** This was Constantine’s first sustained attempt to use state resources to decide an internal Christian dispute. It shows movement from toleration to imperial management and, when unity failed, coercion.

---

## 314–317: First war with Licinius

Relations between Constantine and Licinius deteriorated. A plan to appoint Constantine’s brother-in-law Bassianus as Caesar ended in an alleged conspiracy associated with Bassianus’s brother Senecio and Licinius.

Constantine defeated Licinius at Cibalae, conventionally dated 316 though 314 has also been argued, and fought him again near Campus Ardiensis. The settlement transferred much of the Balkans to Constantine while Licinius retained Thrace and the east.

On 1 March 317, the emperors appointed three Caesars:

- Crispus
- Constantine II
- Licinianus, son of Licinius and Constantia

Constantine II was an infant; the promotions were dynastic rather than merit appointments.

---

## 317–323: Divided empire and dynastic government

Crispus governed in Gaul and campaigned on the Rhine. Constantine remained active along the Danube and in the Balkans.

Constantine’s religious policy became more explicitly Christian:

- Clergy received exemptions, although these were adjusted to prevent municipal elites from escaping civic burdens.
- Churches received gifts and legal capacities.
- Bishops acquired judicial roles by imperial authorization.
- Christian symbols entered military and court representation.
- Laws used Christian-influenced moral language.

At the same time:

- Sol continued on coinage into the 320s.
- Pagan cults and temples generally continued functioning.
- Constantine retained the traditional supreme priestly title *pontifex maximus*.
- The regime used religiously ambiguous formulas when politically useful.
- Some particular sacrifices, divination practices, or morally stigmatized cults were restricted, but claims that Constantine universally outlawed paganism are unsupported.

### Sunday legislation

On 7 March 321, Constantine ordered judges, urban inhabitants, and workshops to rest on the “venerable day of the Sun,” while allowing agricultural work. The language was accessible to both solar and Christian interpretation. Later laws linked Sunday observance more explicitly to Christian worship.

This was not the invention of Christian Sunday observance, which predated Constantine. It did give the day imperial civil status.

---

## 324: Defeat of Licinius and sole rule

Conflict resumed in 324. Constantine defeated Licinius near Adrianople. Crispus led the fleet to victory in the Hellespont, defeating Licinius’s admiral Abantus or Amandus. Constantine then crossed into Asia and won decisively at Chrysopolis on 18 September.

Licinius surrendered through Constantia’s mediation after receiving assurances of life. He was sent to Thessalonica.

### Deaths of Licinius and Licinianus

In 325 Constantine had Licinius executed, officially on grounds that he plotted with barbarians or renewed conspiracy. Licinius’s young son, Licinianus, was also killed, probably in 326.

- **Documented fact:** Both were put to death after the surrender.
- **Documented political issue:** Constantine did not honor the life guarantee in its ordinary sense.
- **Official allegation:** Licinius continued plotting.
- **Unresolved question:** No independent record proves the alleged conspiracy.

Eusebius presents the civil war as liberation of Christians from a persecuting tyrant. Licinius had indeed adopted anti-clerical and restrictive measures late in his reign, but Eusebius’s story also served the victor’s legitimacy.

---

## 324–330: New Rome

After defeating Licinius, Constantine selected Byzantium on the Bosporus for an expanded imperial city. Construction began in 324 or 325, and the city was dedicated on 11 May 330.

### Names

The city was styled **New Rome**, but **Constantinople**, “the city of Constantine,” became its enduring name. It did not legally erase Rome’s identity overnight, nor did Constantine create a wholly new eastern empire. He established a favored imperial residence and institutional center in the strategic east.

### Strategic and administrative purposes

The site offered:

- Control over routes between the Black Sea and Mediterranean.
- Access to the Danube and eastern frontiers.
- Strong natural defenses.
- A harbor on the Golden Horn.
- Distance from Rome’s entrenched aristocratic and ceremonial networks.
- A location near the territory where Constantine defeated Licinius.

### Major works

Constantinian construction included or initiated:

- New land walls.
- The Great Palace.
- Expansion of the Hippodrome.
- The Forum of Constantine and its porphyry column.
- A senate house.
- Baths, cisterns, streets, porticoes, and grain-distribution arrangements.
- Churches, notably Hagia Eirene and the first Holy Apostles complex.
- Traditional or classically styled monuments and images.

The city was neither purely Christian nor a conventional pagan capital. Its population and monuments combined Christian patronage, Roman civic forms, Greek urban traditions, imperial cultic imagery, and transported statuary.

### “Capital transfer” qualification

Modern shorthand says Constantine “moved the capital from Rome.” The empire had no single modern constitutional capital, and emperors had governed from Trier, Milan, Sirmium, Thessalonica, Nicomedia, and other residences. Constantinople’s founding was nonetheless a structural turning point because court, senate, patronage, population, and strategic investment accumulated there. Its later position as the center of the eastern Roman Empire amplified Constantine’s decision.

## 325: Council of Nicaea

The dispute began when Arius, a presbyter of Alexandria, opposed Bishop Alexander’s account of the Son’s eternal relationship to the Father. Constantine first sent Hosius with a letter urging reconciliation and treating the dispute as needlessly divisive. When that failed, he summoned a general council.

The council met at Nicaea in Bithynia in 325. Traditional attendance figures include 318 bishops, but contemporary estimates vary and were lower. Most attendees came from the Greek-speaking east.

Constantine:

- Supplied transport and logistical support.
- Attended in ceremonial imperial dress.
- Addressed the bishops and urged agreement.
- Supported the resulting creed.
- Enforced exile against principal dissenters.
- Promoted a common Easter settlement.
- Hosted the bishops in an imperial banquet interpreted by Eusebius as an image of the kingdom of God.

The creed declared the Son “of one substance” (*homoousios*) with the Father and condemned specified Arian propositions. Constantine did not personally invent the Trinity, determine the biblical canon, suppress belief in reincarnation, or remove books from the Bible at Nicaea. Those are later misconceptions.

The surviving conciliar and postconciliar history is more complex than a permanent victory over Arius. Constantine later recalled Arius or permitted his rehabilitation after receiving an acceptable statement of faith; he exiled anti-Arian leaders including Athanasius for disciplinary and political causes; and his ecclesiastical circle increasingly included Eusebius of Nicomedia. These actions do not establish that Constantine became doctrinally “Arian” in a precise theological sense. They show that unity and obedience remained his overriding administrative objectives and that alliances shifted.

### Anti-Jewish language

Constantine’s letter on Easter used hostile language about Jews and urged Christians not to share the Passover calculation of those he called responsible for Jesus’s death. This is documentary evidence of anti-Jewish imperial-Christian rhetoric. It contributed to the long history of Christian separation from and hostility toward Judaism.

Constantine also issued measures affecting Jewish ownership of Christian slaves, conversion, and communal relations. Exact dating and attribution are complicated because the surviving codes were compiled later. His reign did not begin all Roman restrictions against Jews, nor did it establish the more complete disabilities of later Christian emperors, but it materially connected imperial law with Christian religious hierarchy.

## 326: Crispus and Fausta

This is the most opaque and damaging episode in Constantine’s domestic history.

### Execution of Crispus

Crispus, Constantine’s adult son, successful Caesar, and naval commander, was killed at Pola in 326. Some sources specify poison. His name was subjected to erasure from inscriptions and public memory.

### Death of Fausta

Fausta died soon afterward. Later accounts say she was killed in an overheated bath. The manner is plausible but not confirmed by a contemporary judicial or medical record. Her public memory was likewise suppressed.

### Surviving explanations

1. **Sexual accusation:** Zosimus says Crispus was suspected of relations with Fausta. Later versions say Fausta accused him of attempted seduction after he rejected her.
2. **Incest/adultery:** Some modern scholars take the sexual charge seriously because of the sequence and Constantine’s contemporary marriage legislation.
3. **Dynastic conspiracy:** Crispus’s success and seniority may have threatened Fausta’s sons or Constantine’s control.
4. **False accusation followed by revenge:** A hagiographic or dramatic narrative says Helena exposed Fausta’s lie, after which Constantine killed Fausta.
5. **Pregnancy or failed abortion theory:** A modern reconstruction proposes that Fausta died during an abortion intended to conceal pregnancy, but it rests on conjectural reading of the evidence.
6. **Political treason:** Crispus may have been accused of plotting or disobedience, with sexual stories developing later.

### Evidentiary assessment

- **Documented fact:** Constantine caused or authorized the deaths of Crispus and Fausta.
- **Documented fact:** Their commemoration was suppressed.
- **Unresolved:** Motive, charge, trial procedure, and Fausta’s exact manner of death.
- **Later allegation:** Sexual misconduct between Crispus and Fausta.
- **Later legend:** Helena proved Crispus innocent and forced or inspired punishment of Fausta.
- **Unsupported claim:** That Constantine certainly repented, founded churches specifically as expiation, or converted because pagan priests refused absolution.

Eusebius’s complete silence is itself evidence of controlled commemoration but does not reveal what occurred. David Woods’s study demonstrates the chronological and textual problems: Jerome misdates Fausta’s death, and the earliest notices report killings without the elaborate later plot.

### Licinianus

Licinius’s young son by Constantia was also killed around this period. He was Constantine’s nephew. His removal completed the elimination of Licinius’s direct line.

---

## 327–337: Final decade

### Church politics after Nicaea

Constantine attempted to restore communion through shifting settlements. Arius’s readmission remained contested. Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, resisted imperial and conciliar demands involving the Meletian schism and Arius’s supporters. A council at Tyre deposed Athanasius in 335; Constantine exiled him to Trier.

The emperor’s interventions established no simple doctrine that emperors could dictate theology at will. Bishops negotiated, resisted, petitioned, and used imperial procedure against one another. Nevertheless, Constantine made imperial enforcement a permanent element of Christian institutional politics.

### Helena’s journey and death

Helena traveled to Palestine, probably in 326–328 rather than after 330 as some chronologies imply. Eusebius describes her piety, charity, and church patronage but does not say she discovered the True Cross. She supported construction at Bethlehem and the Mount of Olives and was associated with the Jerusalem building program.

She probably died around 328–330. Constantine buried her in a mausoleum at Rome, now associated with Tor Pignattara, in a porphyry sarcophagus later moved to the Vatican Museums.

### Danubian campaigns

Constantine and his sons campaigned against Goths and Sarmatians.

- In 332, Roman forces under Constantine II or under imperial direction defeated Goths who had attacked Sarmatian territory. The *Origo* claims nearly 100,000 Goths died from hunger and cold; the figure is rhetorical and unverifiable.
- A treaty required Gothic military support and created a durable relationship across the Danube.
- In 334, internal conflict among the Sarmatians displaced large numbers. Constantine reportedly resettled approximately 300,000 people across Thrace, Scythia, Macedonia, and Italy. The number may be exaggerated.

These policies combined warfare, hostage-taking, treaty relations, military recruitment, and population transfer.

### Armenia and Persia

Constantine cultivated Christian Armenia and presented himself as protector of Christians beyond Roman borders. He wrote to Shapur II of Persia in language preserved by Eusebius, praising Christian subjects and warning through the example of persecuting rulers.

By 337 he was preparing a major Persian expedition, perhaps involving the disputed Armenian succession and the Mesopotamian territories obtained under Galerius. He planned to receive baptism in the Jordan during the campaign according to one tradition. Illness prevented departure. War between Constantius II and Shapur followed Constantine’s death; the extent to which Constantine’s policies precipitated Persian suspicion of Christians remains historically significant but cannot be reduced to a single cause.

## Government, Institutions, Economy, and Projects

## Imperial structure

Constantine did not abolish Diocletian’s system so much as alter it. He maintained a large bureaucracy, multiple prefectures, mobile field forces, and court hierarchy while returning the empire to dynastic monarchy.

Major developments included:

- Greater distinction between mobile field troops, the *comitatenses*, and frontier forces, although the system developed over time.
- Expansion of palace offices and the imperial consistory.
- Continued separation of civil governors from many military commands.
- Elevation of new senatorial elites.
- Creation or expansion of a senate at Constantinople.
- Use of sons, nephews, and in-laws as Caesars and regional rulers.
- Replacement of tetrarchic adoption with hereditary Constantinian succession.

### Praetorian prefects

After eliminating the Praetorian Guard, Constantine retained praetorian prefects as powerful civilian administrators rather than commanders of the old guard. The transformation was gradual and not solely his invention.

## Currency and finance

### The solidus

Constantine introduced the gold **solidus**, conventionally dated to 309–310 in the west and extended empire-wide after 324. Struck at 72 to the Roman pound, it became one of the longest-lived stable gold standards in Mediterranean history.

Its stability chiefly benefited the state, army, officials, and groups operating in gold. Bronze currency remained vulnerable to depreciation, and tax burdens were uneven. The solidus should not be described as universal economic prosperity.

### Taxation

The regime continued and refined Diocletianic assessments of land and persons. Tax collection supported the army, court, bureaucracy, building programs, and grain distributions. Constantine granted exemptions to favored groups, including clergy, then limited entry into clerical office when exemptions threatened municipal obligations.

The **chrysargyron**, a levy on commercial and artisanal activity usually associated with Constantine, was collected periodically and became deeply unpopular; Anastasius abolished it in 498. Details of its Constantinian origin remain debated.

### Property and church wealth

Constantine transferred imperial money, land, buildings, and legal privileges to churches. He enabled churches to receive bequests. This did not create a uniform centralized “church corporation,” but it materially accelerated ecclesiastical accumulation of property and patronage networks.

### Personal wealth

No reliable modern monetary estimate exists for Constantine’s net worth. Imperial and private property were not separated like the accounts of a modern head of state. He controlled:

- Crown estates and palaces.
- Tax revenue.
- Confiscated property.
- Precious-metal stocks and mints.
- Labor obligations and military resources.
- Land and movable wealth redistributed to relatives, officials, cities, and churches.

Assertions that his church patronage was funded only by seized pagan treasure, or conversely only by ordinary revenue, exceed the evidence. Both confiscation and taxation contributed to imperial resources.

## Law and social policy

Constantine’s surviving laws are numerous but often preserved in later abridged form.

### Christianity and clergy

He:

- Restored confiscated church property.
- Granted selected clerical fiscal and civic immunities.
- Recognized manumission performed in churches.
- Permitted episcopal arbitration or adjudication.
- Authorized churches to receive legacies.
- Subsidized councils and basilicas.
- Used exile and confiscation against selected Christian dissidents.

### Slavery

Constantine did not abolish slavery. His laws:

- Facilitated Christian church manumission.
- Protected some slave families from separation in certain estate divisions.
- Prescribed severe punishment for enslaved people in sexual or household offenses.
- Continued the legal treatment of enslaved persons as property.
- Restricted Jewish ownership or circumcision of Christian slaves in some enactments.

The legislation combined limited humanitarian provisions with preservation of the slave system.

### Crucifixion and branding

Later tradition credits Constantine with abolishing crucifixion out of reverence for Christ. The evidence suggests that crucifixion declined or was prohibited under his rule, but the precise enactment is lost and other aggravated death penalties continued.

A law prohibited branding condemned persons on the face because the face was made in the divine image; branding elsewhere remained permissible. This was modification of punishment, not abolition of coerced labor or bodily penalties.

### Family and sexual legislation

Constantine issued severe laws regarding adultery, elopement or abduction, guardianship, marriage, and household sexuality. Some increased women’s property or family protections; others imposed brutal penalties and strengthened patriarchal control.

A notorious law on abduction punished not only the abductor but, under specified circumstances, the woman and attendants. Molten lead is mentioned as punishment for a complicit nurse. These enactments demonstrate the violence of late Roman penal law.

### Exposure of infants

Constantine authorized aid or arrangements intended to prevent impoverished parents from exposing newborns. Other rules treated exposed children through ownership-like or dependency relationships. He did not abolish infant exposure by a single comprehensive decree.

### Gladiators

A 325 constitution has often been summarized as abolishing gladiatorial games. It declared that bloody spectacles were inappropriate in civil peace and directed criminals toward the mines, but gladiatorial contests persisted. Constantine restricted rather than effectively ended them.

### Colonate

Constantinian laws tied some tenant farmers more firmly to estates and penalized flight. This contributed to the later hereditary colonate. Describing the system as medieval serfdom already completed under Constantine is anachronistic.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

## From tetrarchic religion to Christianity

Constantine’s trajectory was gradual and politically public:

1. **Tetrarchic phase:** Participation in traditional imperial religion.
2. **Solar phase:** Prominent attachment to Apollo/Sol and a supreme-divinity vocabulary.
3. **312 onward:** Conviction that the Christian God granted victory; Christian symbols and patronage increased.
4. **Sole rule after 324:** Explicit Christian letters, legislation, councils, and extensive church building.
5. **337:** Baptism during final illness.

The survival of Sol on coins after 312 does not prove that his Christian allegiance was fictitious. Nor does Christian patronage prove that every traditional association immediately vanished. Roman religious identity, imperial representation, and coin circulation did not operate through a modern binary conversion certificate.

### Sincerity versus political calculation

Two reductive positions recur:

- Constantine was a sincere Christian whose policy followed conversion.
- Constantine invented or adopted Christianity solely to manipulate the empire.

The sources support neither in exclusive form. His letters, long-term patronage, theological engagement, and baptism support personal belief. His use of religion for victory, unity, legitimacy, and administration is equally documented. Personal conviction and political utility were compatible in Roman emperorship.

### Baptism

Constantine remained a catechumen until his final illness. Delayed baptism was not extraordinary because Christians feared post-baptismal sin and because rulers remained involved in warfare, executions, and coercion.

Eusebius of Nicomedia baptized him near Nicomedia. Since that bishop had supported Arius and later anti-Nicene positions, polemicists called Constantine’s baptism “Arian.” The label requires qualification: Eusebius of Nicomedia formally participated in the church’s shifting settlements, and the later doctrinal categories had not yet stabilized. Constantine professed the Christian faith and died in communion with bishops then accepted at court.

## Violence, Controversies, Allegations, and Adversarial Findings

A dedicated adversarial search using combinations of Constantine’s name with “controversy,” “scandal,” “allegations,” “fraud,” “conspiracy,” “criticism,” and related terms produced no newly documented scandal from 2024–2026 that changes the ancient evidentiary record. Recent discussion largely recycles the following established controversies or later conspiracy narratives.

## Execution of defeated or rebellious relatives

### Maximian

- **Documented:** Died after rebellion and capture in 310.
- **Strong inference:** Constantine ordered or compelled his death.
- **Official/Christian narrative:** Maximian plotted murder and was permitted to choose suicide.
- **Unresolved:** Whether the murder plot occurred as described.

### Licinius

- **Documented:** Executed after surrender and promise of safety.
- **Official allegation:** Renewed plotting or dealings with barbarians.
- **Unresolved:** Whether the allegation was genuine, manufactured, or exaggerated.

### Licinianus

- **Documented:** The young son of Licinius and Constantia was killed.
- **Political interpretation:** Dynastic elimination.
- **Unresolved:** Formal charge and precise date.

### Crispus and Fausta

- **Documented:** Both were killed on Constantine’s authority in 326.
- **Allegations:** Adultery, attempted rape, false accusation, incest, conspiracy, or succession conflict.
- **Finding:** No surviving explanation rises to established fact.

## Violence against foreign captives

The reported exposure of Frankish kings to beasts and large-scale killing or starvation of Goths show that Constantine’s rule used violence typical of Roman imperial warfare and spectacle. Court sources celebrate rather than conceal some of it. Exact casualty figures are unreliable.

## Religious coercion

Constantine ended persecution of Christianity and protected broad worship in 313, but later:

- Confiscated dissident Christian property.
- Exiled bishops.
- Used soldiers and officials in church disputes.
- Ordered destruction or closure of selected cult sites according to Christian sources.
- Restricted certain sacrifices and divinatory practices.
- Employed degrading anti-Jewish rhetoric.

There was no uniform empire-wide forced conversion under Constantine. Pagan temples and officeholders persisted. The trajectory toward a privileged imperial Christianity was nonetheless unmistakable.

## “He made Christianity the official religion”

**False as stated.** Constantine legalized, privileged, financed, and institutionally empowered Christianity. Theodosius I’s Thessalonian edict of 380 is the conventional marker for an official Nicene imperial confession. Even that later process was not a single-step replacement of every other practice.

## “He created the Catholic Church”

**Myth or polemical simplification.** Structured episcopal churches, catholic self-description, canon formation, creeds, sacraments, and Roman episcopal authority all predated Constantine. He transformed their material scale and relationship with government.

## “He invented the Trinity at Nicaea”

**False.** Trinitarian debates and formulations long predated 325. Constantine summoned and enforced the council but was not the originator of its theology.

## “He chose the books of the Bible”

**Unsupported modern legend.** Nicaea’s documented agenda concerned Arius, the creed, Easter, clerical discipline, and related matters. No reliable ancient record says the council selected the biblical canon. Constantine later ordered fifty scriptural codices for Constantinople; their exact contents are not recorded.

## “He removed reincarnation from Christianity”

**Modern conspiracy narrative.** No evidence connects Constantine or Nicaea with deleting a formerly orthodox doctrine of reincarnation from scripture. The story often conflates Nicaea in 325 with the Second Council of Constantinople in 553 and with disputes over Origen.

## “He converted only on his deathbed”

**Misleading.** He was baptized on his deathbed, but documentary patronage and Christian declarations extend back roughly twenty-five years. Baptism and conversion were not synonymous moments in fourth-century practice.

## “He never truly converted”

**Unresolved psychological claim presented too confidently.** His continued solar imagery, political opportunism, and delayed baptism support interpretations of gradual or syncretic development. His sustained Christian patronage, letters, theological interventions, and baptism are evidence against treating the conversion as a late clerical fabrication.

## “Constantine established Christmas on 25 December”

**Unproven.** The Roman celebration of Christ’s nativity on 25 December is attested in the fourth century, but no surviving decree of Constantine establishes it. Connections to Sol Invictus remain debated.

## Judicial status

No modern categories of lawsuit, indictment, criminal conviction, corporate fraud, money laundering, or intelligence service apply straightforwardly. Constantine was the supreme imperial legal authority and victor in civil wars. His executions were state actions recorded by sources, not verdicts from an independent court. The absence of prosecution is not evidence that the killings did not occur.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## The heavenly cross and “In this sign, conquer”

- **Story:** Constantine saw a cross of light over the sun and the words conventionally Latinized as *in hoc signo vinces*; Christ then instructed him to use the sign in battle.
- **Earliest layers:** Lactantius reports a dream and shield sign; Eusebius’s developed public vision appears later in the *Life*.
- **Evidence for:** Constantine’s post-312 Christian alignment, the early military sign, Lactantius’s near-contemporary account, Eusebius’s claimed personal testimony, and later imperial Christian symbolism.
- **Evidence against literal certainty:** Differing accounts, delayed publication of the full sky vision, solar imagery, and absence of explicit Christian wording on the Arch of 315.
- **Reputational effect:** Became the archetype of divinely sanctioned Christian victory and one of the most influential conversion scenes in European art and political theology.

## Constantine as a new Moses

Eusebius repeatedly compares Constantine with Moses: raised near tyrants, escaping their court, defeating an impious ruler drowned in water, and leading God’s people. This is not simple factual biography. It is typological Christian historiography that made Maxentius’s drowning correspond to Pharaoh’s destruction.

## Helena and the True Cross

- **Story:** Helena traveled to Jerusalem and discovered Christ’s cross, sometimes by interrogating a Jew named Judas Cyriacus; a miracle identified the true relic.
- **Earliest evidence:** Eusebius records Helena’s pilgrimage and construction but does not mention discovery of the Cross.
- **Later circulation:** The story appears in later fourth-century writers and expands through Rufinus, Socrates, Sozomen, Ambrose, and medieval hagiography.
- **Assessment:** Helena’s pilgrimage and patronage are historical; her personal discovery of the Cross is a later tradition not confirmed by the closest source.
- **Effect:** Made Helena the archetypal Christian archaeologist and relic-finder and linked Constantine’s dynasty to Jerusalem.

## Constantine’s leprosy, the infants’ blood, and Pope Sylvester

- **Story:** Pagan priests told a leprous Constantine to bathe in infants’ blood. He spared the children; Peter and Paul appeared in a dream; Pope Sylvester baptized and cured him.
- **Appearance:** The *Actus Silvestri*, developed from late antique traditions and widely circulated in medieval languages.
- **Evidence against:** Contemporary sources place Constantine’s baptism near Nicomedia under Eusebius of Nicomedia in 337; Sylvester died in 335.
- **Effect:** Recentered Constantine’s conversion at Rome, elevated the Roman bishop, and supplied the narrative foundation for the Donation of Constantine.

## Donation of Constantine

- **Story/document:** Constantine, cured and baptized by Sylvester, supposedly granted the pope primacy, imperial insignia, the Lateran, Rome, Italy, and authority in the western provinces before retiring east.
- **Documentary status:** Medieval forgery, probably composed in the eighth century and later included in Pseudo-Isidorian materials.
- **Use:** Supported papal temporal and jurisdictional claims and played a major role in medieval church-state controversy.
- **Exposure:** Nicholas of Cusa questioned it; Lorenzo Valla’s 1440 philological analysis demonstrated anachronistic language, titles, institutions, and historical circumstances.
- **Assessment:** No property transfer of this kind occurred. The forgery is not evidence for Constantine’s acts but is major evidence for his medieval political afterlife.

## Constantine founded Constantinople because Rome was irredeemably pagan

This is an overdrawn later interpretation. Religious motivation mattered, but strategy, dynastic victory, trade, defense, and the late Roman practice of governing from frontier residences were at least as important. The early city contained both churches and classically religious monuments.

## Trojan foundation and the Palladium

Later Byzantine sources claimed Constantine first considered Troy or began construction there, and that sacred objects such as the Palladium were brought from Rome and placed beneath his column. The Oxford Classical Dictionary notes that early evidence for the city is sparse and later accounts are already saturated with myth. These stories connected New Rome to Troy and Old Rome’s legendary ancestry.

## Statue as Apollo, Helios, or Christ-associated emperor

The statue on Constantine’s porphyry column was remembered as a radiate figure and variously understood as Constantine in the guise of Helios, an older Apollo statue refashioned with Constantine’s head, or a Christianized cosmic ruler. Later stories placed relics beneath or within it. The monument’s mixed symbolism became a focal point for competing pagan, Christian, and antiquarian memories.

## Baptism by Sylvester versus Eusebius

- **Historical account:** Baptism by Eusebius of Nicomedia near death.
- **Latin hagiographic replacement:** Baptism by Sylvester in Rome.
- **Reason for replacement:** It removed the embarrassment of baptism by a bishop later branded Arian and strengthened Roman ecclesiastical claims.
- **Result:** The legendary version dominated western medieval art despite contradicting earlier testimony.

## Helena as a British princess

Geoffrey of Monmouth and later British traditions made Helena the daughter of King Coel or another British ruler and placed Constantine within a British royal genealogy.

- **Assessment:** Medieval origin; contradicted by earlier associations with the eastern empire and Helena’s modest status.
- **Effect:** Incorporated Constantine into British legendary history and the folklore of “Old King Cole.”

## Constantine’s remorse

Later stories describe Constantine tormented by the deaths of Crispus and Fausta, rejected by pagan priests, or seeking Christian absolution.

- **Evidence:** No contemporary source securely records such remorse.
- **Function:** The story explained conversion as guilt, supplied moral causation for church patronage, or attacked the authenticity of his faith.
- **Status:** Unverified late tradition.

## Secret pagan Constantine or destroyer of ancient religion

Opposed confessional traditions constructed two exaggerated Constantines:

- A pagan opportunist who falsified Christianity for power.
- A Christian hero who immediately eradicated idolatry.

Coins, laws, temples, churches, and letters instead show an extended transition: increasingly exclusive Christian allegiance at the imperial center alongside pragmatic survival of traditional institutions.

## Works by or Under the Name of Constantine

Constantine was not an author in the modern bibliographical sense. His surviving corpus consists chiefly of speeches, letters, edicts, rescripts, and laws produced through imperial secretariats. “Authored by Constantine” may mean personally dictated, approved, issued in his name, translated, or reshaped by officials.

## Speech

### *Oratio ad sanctorum coetum* — “Oration to the Assembly of the Saints”

- Original language: Preserved in Greek; traditionally presented as translated from Constantine’s Latin.
- Date: Disputed, commonly placed in the 310s or 320s.
- Contents: Christian apologetic, monotheism, prophecy, Christ, and a Christianized interpretation of Virgil’s Fourth Eclogue.
- Attribution: Disputed. Defenders accept a Constantinian core or court speech; critics identify literary and theological mediation.
- Transmission: Appended to manuscripts of Eusebius’s *Life of Constantine*.

## Imperial letters preserved by Eusebius and others

Important items include:

- Letter to Anulinus on restoring church property.
- Letter concerning clerical immunities.
- Letters in the Donatist dispute.
- Letter summoning or addressing councils.
- Letter to Alexander and Arius urging concord.
- Letter to the churches following Nicaea.
- Letter concerning Easter.
- Letter against Arius and orders affecting Arian writings.
- Later letters permitting Arius’s reconsideration or return.
- Letter to Eusebius of Caesarea commissioning fifty copies of scripture.
- Letter to Macarius concerning construction at Jerusalem.
- Letter to Shapur II concerning Christians in Persia.
- General proclamation to eastern provincials after Licinius’s defeat.
- Letters regarding restoration of churches and property.

**Authorship qualification:** These are genuine or plausibly genuine state documents transmitted by partisan literary sources. Exact wording may reflect chancery drafting, Greek translation, excerpting, or Eusebius’s editorial arrangement.

## Laws and constitutions

Hundreds of enactments issued under Constantine’s name survive, principally in:

- *Codex Theodosianus*
- *Codex Justinianus*
- *Fragmenta Vaticana*
- *Collatio Legum Mosaicarum et Romanarum*
- Ecclesiastical histories and documentary collections

Subjects include taxation, municipal office, slavery, family law, criminal penalties, religion, clergy, courts, inheritance, the army, and imperial administration.

These are not “books by Constantine.” They are official products of government. Dating and authenticity must sometimes be corrected because later compilers abbreviated texts or assigned misleading headings.

## Edict or agreement of Milan

- Date: 313.
- Parties: Constantine and Licinius.
- Survives through Lactantius and Eusebius.
- Bibliographical status: Imperial religious settlement, conventionally called an edict; not a sole-authored Constantinian manifesto.

## Nicene communications

Constantine’s address, conciliar letters, and enforcement orders survive in several textual traditions. The Nicene Creed was the council’s corporate product, not a work written by Constantine.

## Misattributed or forged works

### *Constitutum Constantini* or Donation of Constantine

- Date: Probably eighth century.
- Attribution: Forged in Constantine’s name.
- Historical importance: Enormous; genuine authorship: none.

### Legendary decrees in the *Actus Silvestri*

These are hagiographic constructions, not authentic imperial acts.

### “Thirteen Letters of Constantine” and later collected correspondence

Modern editions assemble genuine, disputed, translated, and fragmentary documents. The compilation itself is posthumous editorial scholarship.

## Digital and archival resources

- New Advent and CCEL host public-domain English translations of Eusebius and other church fathers.
- Fordham University’s Internet History Sourcebooks provides translations of the 311 and 313 toleration documents and Eusebius’s *Life*.
- LacusCurtius hosts the *Origo Constantini Imperatoris* and epigraphic/topographical material.
- The Metropolitan Museum of Art, British Museum, Dumbarton Oaks, and major numismatic collections preserve searchable Constantinian coins and objects.
- Cambridge Core and Oxford Academic provide current scholarly studies, including work on Fausta, inscriptions, Eusebius, Constantinople, and the Constantinian dynasty.
- The Vatican Museums preserve Helena’s porphyry sarcophagus and major Constantinian-period objects.
- Archaeological sites at Trier, Rome, Niš, York, Istanbul, Jerusalem, Bethlehem, and Nicomedia/İzmit preserve material contexts for the reign.

No surviving voice recording, interview, diary, or autograph manuscript by Constantine exists.

## Death and Burial

During preparations for the Persian war, Constantine fell ill, reportedly first at Helenopolis and then near Nicomedia. He sought baptism and removed the imperial purple, according to Eusebius’s ritualized account.

**Documented or strongly reported sequence:**

- Illness during spring 337.
- Baptism by Eusebius of Nicomedia and associated clergy.
- Death on Pentecost, 22 May 337.
- Body carried to Constantinople.
- Lying in state under imperial ceremony.
- Burial in the Church of the Holy Apostles.

His mausoleum contained his sarcophagus surrounded by twelve cenotaphs or markers representing the apostles. The arrangement encouraged an image of Constantine as a thirteenth apostolic figure or Christ-associated ruler. Whether he personally designed every symbolic implication is debated.

No reliable diagnosis survives. Later claims of leprosy contradict the near-contemporary death narrative and derive from the Sylvester legend. Natural illness is the only defensible cause; a specific disease cannot be established.

### Succession

Constantine left no single Augustus designated as supreme. His sons and nephews held regional commands:

- Constantine II in the west.
- Constantius II in the east.
- Constans in the central territories.
- Dalmatius as Caesar in Balkan regions.
- Hannibalianus with an unusual royal title connected to Armenia and Pontus.

After months of uncertainty, soldiers killed Dalmatius, Hannibalianus, Julius Constantius, and other male relatives. Constantine’s three sons emerged as Augusti in September 337.

- **Documented fact:** A dynastic massacre followed his death.
- **Unresolved:** Whether Constantius II directed it, accepted it, or was unable to prevent it.
- **Unproven theory:** Constantine secretly arranged the purge through a forged or concealed testament.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

## Eastern Christian sainthood

Constantine and Helena are venerated as saints and “Equal to the Apostles” in Eastern Orthodox and several Eastern Catholic traditions. Their feast associates imperial conversion with the Christianization of the Roman world.

This cult emphasizes:

- The end of persecution.
- The cross vision.
- Nicaea.
- Church construction.
- Helena’s pilgrimage and relic tradition.
- Constantinople as Christian New Rome.

It normally minimizes or explains the deaths of Crispus, Fausta, and Licinius.

## Western Christian memory

The Latin West honored Constantine as the first Christian emperor but did not generally develop the same universal liturgical sainthood. The Sylvester legend and Donation made him central to medieval papal political theory. Dante condemned the supposed Donation’s consequences while accepting the traditional attribution available in his time.

## Byzantine imperial model

Later eastern Roman emperors repeatedly invoked Constantine as:

- Founder of the imperial city.
- Model Christian emperor.
- Convenor of the archetypal ecumenical council.
- Restorer or refounder of Roman order.

“New Constantine” became a title of praise. The name Constantine remained prominent through the last eastern Roman emperor, Constantine XI Palaiologos.

## Roman and European state history

His reign permanently affected:

- The geographic orientation of imperial government.
- Christian institutional wealth and public authority.
- Relations between rulers and councils.
- The gold currency system.
- Military and administrative development.
- Dynastic succession.
- Roman law and later European legal compilations.
- The symbolic union of victory, monarchy, and Christianity.

## Reformation and Enlightenment reinterpretation

Protestant writers attacked the Donation and sometimes viewed Constantine as the point at which an allegedly pure church became wealthy and coercive. Catholic scholars revised their use of the Donation while retaining Constantine’s importance. Enlightenment and anticlerical histories emphasized political calculation, family murders, and the cost of church-state alliance.

Edward Gibbon’s treatment helped fix the image of Constantine as ambitious, luxurious, and morally compromised. Burckhardt’s nineteenth-century interpretation strengthened the portrait of a calculating political genius rather than a religious convert.

## Modern scholarship

Modern research has moved away from a single “sincere saint versus cynical opportunist” question. Principal areas of debate include:

- How quickly and exclusively Constantine became Christian.
- Whether solar and Christian symbolism overlapped in his thinking.
- The authenticity and editorial state of documents in Eusebius.
- The practical enforcement of anti-pagan and pro-Christian measures.
- The cause of the Crispus-Fausta killings.
- The political purpose of Constantinople.
- The relationship between consensus and coercion.
- Constantine’s part in the emergence of Christian antisemitic law and rhetoric.
- Whether “revolution,” “conversion,” “transformation,” or “accommodation” best describes the reign.

## Long-term negative and contested legacies

Documented developments associated with his reign include:

- State-backed enforcement of Christian ecclesiastical decisions.
- Material privileging of clergy and churches.
- Coercion against religious dissidents.
- Anti-Jewish rhetoric and discriminatory measures.
- Dynastic executions and suppression of memory.
- Intensified sacral presentation of monarchy.
- Greater linkage of warfare with Christian divine favor.

These effects coexist with:

- Termination of state persecution of Christians.
- Broad toleration in 313.
- Restoration of seized property.
- Durable monetary reform.
- Major urban and architectural patronage.
- Administrative continuity after prolonged civil war.
- Institutional foundations that sustained the eastern Roman state for centuries.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the researched record are included. Mere shared geography or later inclusion in the same historical tradition is excluded.

### Jesus of Nazareth

- **Nature:** Explicitly acknowledged religious and ideological influence.
- **Dates and places:** Especially from the campaign of 312 onward; Rome, the Balkans, Nicaea, Constantinople, Palestine, and the imperial court.
- **Evidence label:** **Documented fact as professed influence; supernatural intervention is a religious claim.**
- **Basis:** Constantine attributed victory and imperial protection to the Christian God and Christ, funded churches associated with Jesus’s birth, death, and resurrection, summoned Nicaea to resolve the status of the Son, and received Christian baptism.

### Paul the Apostle

- **Nature:** Posthumous cultic and architectural connection.
- **Dates and place:** Rome, principally the early fourth-century shrine or basilica on the Ostian Way associated with Paul’s tomb.
- **Evidence label:** **Reported/documented patronage, with archaeological and later textual qualification.**
- **Basis:** Constantine’s regime is traditionally credited with constructing or sponsoring an initial basilica at the venerated burial place of Paul. The much larger basilica known from later history was principally a late-fourth-century rebuilding.

### Alexander the Great

- **Nature:** Documented iconographic and imperial-model influence, not a personal relationship.
- **Dates and places:** Constantinian portraiture and Constantinople, especially after 324.
- **Evidence label:** **Scholarly interpretation grounded in material evidence.**
- **Basis:** Constantine’s upward-looking eyes, diademed portrait conventions, universal-monarch imagery, and placement of Alexander’s image in Constantinople have been read as deliberate engagement with Alexander’s imperial model. The strength of any private personal identification cannot be measured.

### Origen of Alexandria

- **Nature:** Indirect intellectual connection through fourth-century theological disputes.
- **Dates and places:** Nicaea and the eastern church, particularly 324–337.
- **Evidence label:** **Documented intellectual background; no personal connection.**
- **Basis:** Origen’s earlier theological vocabulary and exegetical legacy formed part of the intellectual background to disputes over the Father and Son. Constantine did not meet Origen, who died decades before his birth, and no evidence makes Origen a direct teacher of Constantine.

### Constantine XI Palaiologos

- **Nature:** Dynastic-imperial namesake and inheritor of the Constantinopolitan political tradition.
- **Dates and places:** Constantinople, 1449–1453.
- **Evidence label:** **Documented legacy connection, not a lifetime interaction.**
- **Basis:** Constantine XI ruled from the city founded by Constantine I and bore a regnal name embedded in the imperial tradition Constantine created. No direct genealogical descent from Constantine I is implied.

### Justinian I

- **Nature:** Institutional and legal successor.
- **Dates and places:** Constantinople, sixth century.
- **Evidence label:** **Documented legacy connection.**
- **Basis:** Justinian ruled from Constantine’s city, rebuilt major Constantinian church sites, and incorporated Constantinian constitutions into the *Codex Justinianus*. They were separated by two centuries and never met.

### Aurelius Augustinus

- **Nature:** Intellectual and ecclesiastical consequence of Constantinian policy.
- **Dates and places:** Roman North Africa, late fourth and early fifth centuries.
- **Evidence label:** **Documented institutional influence, not personal contact.**
- **Basis:** Augustine was born in 354, after Constantine’s death. His confrontation with Donatism operated within the church-state framework created by Constantine’s original adjudication and coercive interventions.

### Saint Acacius of Byzantium, Catherine of Alexandria, Saint George of Lydda, Saint Barbara of Heliopolis, Pantaleon of Nicomedia, and other martyr figures on the roster

- **Nature:** Sometimes placed by later hagiography in the persecutions immediately preceding Constantine or in locations important to his reign.
- **Evidence label:** **No securely documented personal connection found.**
- **Finding:** Shared late-antique Christian memory is insufficient to establish meeting, correspondence, patronage, or influence. Catherine’s story concerns Maxentius in later legend, but that does not document a meeting with Constantine.

For the remaining roster names, no source consulted established a qualifying direct relationship, correspondence, meeting, family tie, collaboration, rivalry, or explicitly acknowledged influence. Absence is particularly expected for figures living centuries before or after Constantine; later comparison or occupation of Constantinople alone was not treated as a personal crossing.
