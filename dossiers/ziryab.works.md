## Works by Ziryab

### Poetry

- **Date unknown, before 852:** An untitled four-verse love poem. This is the only poetry attributed to Ziryab that survives, preserved much later by al-Maqqarī from an anthology compiled more than a century after Ziryab’s death; its attribution is therefore doubtful, and the poem has no transmitted title. [Reynolds, “Al-Maqqarī’s Ziryāb”](https://doi.org/10.1080/14752620802223756); [Reynolds, *Medieval Arab Music and Musicians*, pp. 147–48](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004501546/BP000012.pdf)

### Musical compositions and repertory

- **Before 822:** An unnamed setting or performance of verses by ʿAntara. Later accounts associate this song with Ziryab’s expulsion from the Aghlabid court, but the earliest report says that he sang it, not that he composed either its words or melody; it therefore resists classification as his work. [Reynolds, “Ziryāb in the Aghlabid Court”](https://brill.com/previewpdf/book/edcoll/9789004356047/BP000022.xml); [Arslan and Erkoçoğlu, “Ziryâb”](https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/ziryab)

- **Before 852:** An unnamed musical composition subsequently altered by his son ʿUbayd Allāh. Ibn Ḥayyān’s account implies that a composition by Ziryab existed but supplies neither words, musical notation, nor title; even its medium—melody, mode, rhythm, or some combination—is uncertain. [Reynolds, *The Musical Heritage of al-Andalus*, ch. 4](https://www.routledge.com/The-Musical-Heritage-of-Al-Andalus/Reynolds/p/book/9780367243142); [Reynolds, “Al-Maqqarī’s Ziryāb”](https://doi.org/10.1080/14752620802223756)

- **c. 822–852:** Ziryab’s unnamed repertory and performance style. Ibn Ḥayyān records its transmission through Ziryab’s children, pupils, and Manṣūr the Jew, but “repertory” and “style” are intertwined in the Arabic terminology, and some songs originally belonging to earlier singers were subsequently ascribed to Ziryab; this is consequently a body of practice, not a recoverable catalogue of authored compositions. [Reynolds, *Medieval Arab Music and Musicians*, pp. 139–42](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004501546/9789004501546_webready_content_text.pdf)

- **Date unknown:** The purported corpus of 10,000 songs. The anonymous *Kitāb akhbār Ziryāb* says that Ziryab knew their words and melodies from memory, but does not say that he made them; no early biographical source identifies even one song as his composition, so the corpus is remembered repertory rather than an obra by Ziryab. [Reynolds, introduction to “The Biography of Ziryāb,” pp. 110–11](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004501546/BP000011.pdf)

- **Traditionally dated c. 822–852:** The Andalusi *nawba* or twenty-four *nūbāt*. Modern tradition frequently makes Ziryab their creator, but the surviving early sources do not document that claim; even the connection between his performance practice and the later suite is tenuous, so these cannot responsibly be catalogued as securely his works. [Reynolds, *The Musical Heritage of al-Andalus*, ch. 4](https://www.routledge.com/The-Musical-Heritage-of-Al-Andalus/Reynolds/p/book/9780367243142); [Somerville, “Ziryab and Us”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8372298/)

### Instrument designs

- **c. 822–852:** Five-string lute. The anonymous *Kitāb akhbār Ziryāb* credits Ziryab with adding a red middle string, symbolizing the soul, to the four-string lute; Ibn Ḥayyān explicitly says he found no corroborating authority, and modern scholarship treats the story as possibly mythical. This is an attributed instrument modification rather than a surviving instrument. [Reynolds, “The Biography of Ziryāb,” pp. 127–28](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004501546/BP000012.pdf); [Reynolds, introduction, p. 111](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004501546/BP000011.pdf)

- **c. 822–852:** Eagle-quill lute plectrum. The same solitary biography credits Ziryab with substituting an eagle’s quill for the usual wooden plectrum; no specimen survives and independent confirmation is lacking, making this an attributed implement design rather than a securely documented object. [Reynolds, “The Biography of Ziryāb,” p. 128](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004501546/BP000012.pdf)

### Pedagogical and performance protocols

- **c. 822–852:** Three-stage singing curriculum. Ziryab reportedly taught rhythm, poetic meter, and words first, the basic melody second, and ornament or nuance last; this is a protocol embodied in teaching rather than a surviving written treatise. [Arslan and Erkoçoğlu, “Ziryâb”](https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/ziryab)

- **c. 822–852:** Vocal-admission and remedial exercises. The reported procedure tested a pupil’s range by sustained shouting and prescribed bodily supports or a wooden mouthpiece for weak projection or articulation; it survives only as a description attributed to al-Maqqarī, not as Ziryab’s own written manual. [Arslan and Erkoçoğlu, “Ziryâb”](https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/ziryab)

### Culinary works

- **c. 822–852:** *Tafāyā*. The anonymous Ziryab biography calls this coriander-based preparation his culinary innovation, but a thirteenth-century Andalusi cookbook gives numerous *tafāyā* recipes without connecting the dish to him; authorship is therefore disputed. [Reynolds, “The Biography of Ziryāb,” p. 132 and n. 63](https://brill.com/downloadpdf/book/9789004501546/BP000012.xml)

- **c. 822–852:** *Baqliyya attributed to Ziryab*. A vegetable-and-meat dish preserved in a later Andalusi cookbook carries an explicit attribution to him, although the chronological gap prevents certainty; unlike most dishes in his legend, this one has an independent culinary witness. [Reynolds, “The Biography of Ziryāb,” p. 133 and n. 65](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004501546/BP000012.pdf)

- **c. 822–852:** Ziryab’s method for frying beans. The anonymous biography describes a distinct procedure—moistening and working the beans before slowly frying them in ceramic—and says it remained attached to his name; it is a culinary protocol rather than a titled dish. [Reynolds, “The Biography of Ziryāb,” pp. 133–34](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004501546/BP000012.pdf)

- **c. 822–852:** Recipes attributed to Ziryab. The anonymous biography says that he wrote recipes for bread-soups, cold dishes, meat and poultry croquettes, pastries, sweets, wafers, and preserves, but no manuscript of this recipe corpus is known to survive and individual authorship cannot usually be separated from his reputed introduction of eastern dishes. This is a lost textual obra whose extent and titles are unknown. [Reynolds, “The Biography of Ziryāb,” p. 133](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004501546/BP000012.pdf)

### Cosmetic and domestic formulations

- **c. 822–852:** Refined *murdāsanj* deodorant. The anonymous biography credits Ziryab with a preparation of litharge refined with salt and bleached for use against body odor; it is a hazardous lead-based formula, and the claim has no independent early corroboration. [Reynolds, “The Biography of Ziryāb,” p. 130](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004501546/BP000012.pdf)

- **c. 822–852:** Perfumes, incense blends, aromatic woods, incense sticks, and cosmetic powders. The biography praises Ziryab’s production and composition of these substances but transmits neither names nor formulas; they constitute an attributed, lost body of material creations rather than separately identifiable works. [Reynolds, “The Biography of Ziryāb,” p. 134](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004501546/BP000012.pdf)

### Fashion and domestic protocols

- **c. 822–852:** Short coiffure worn by Ziryab’s household. The design cut the hair across the forehead, exposed the ears, and retained locks at the temples; the complete Ibn Ḥayyān text says Cordoban elites adopted it chiefly for servants, eunuchs, and singing girls, whereas al-Maqqarī’s abridgment enlarged it into a court-wide fashion. It is a collectively manifested household style, not securely an individual design by Ziryab. [Reynolds, “The Biography of Ziryāb,” p. 130 and n. 55](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004501546/BP000012.pdf)

- **c. 822–852:** Seasonal dress calendar. The anonymous biography attributes to Ziryab a protocol prescribing white clothing from late June through September, colored garments during the rest of the year, and seasonally adjusted fabrics; it is an attributed rule system rather than a collection of surviving garments. [Reynolds, “The Biography of Ziryāb,” pp. 134–35](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004501546/BP000012.pdf)

- **c. 822–852:** Glass-drinking-vessel protocol. Ziryab is said to have preferred fine glass to gold and silver vessels, but the source describes selection and use, not his manufacture of the vessels; it therefore resists being called a made work except as a courtly protocol. [Reynolds, “The Biography of Ziryāb,” p. 134](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004501546/BP000012.pdf)

- **c. 822–852:** Leather table-covering protocol. The anonymous biography credits him with prescribing decorated, wipe-clean leather coverings over wooden tables; this is a designed practice, not evidence that he personally made the coverings. [Reynolds, “The Biography of Ziryāb,” p. 134](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004501546/BP000012.pdf)

- **c. 822–852:** Leather bed-covering protocol. The same source says he preferred smooth leather bed coverings to stuffed linen ones; preference alone does not establish authorship, so this is at most an attributed domestic-design intervention. [Reynolds, “The Biography of Ziryāb,” p. 134](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004501546/BP000012.pdf)
