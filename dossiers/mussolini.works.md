## Works by Benito Mussolini

### Pamphlets, essays, fiction, and early autobiography

- **1904:** *L’uomo e la divinità*. Antireligious pamphlet developed from Mussolini’s public debate with the Protestant pastor Alfredo Taglialatela; it combines a revised speech, a summary of the reply, a written rebuttal, and an appendix on evangelism. [[2]](#source-2)

- **1908:** *La filosofia della forza*. Essay or lecture on Nietzsche, first published in installments rather than conceived as a conventional book; it belongs to the miscellaneous writings gathered chronologically in the *Opera omnia*. [[1]](#source-1)

- **1909:** *La santa di Susà*. Investigative, aggressively anticlerical newspaper report about Rosa Broll, subsequently issued as a pamphlet. It is therefore both journalism and a separately circulating polemical text. [[3]](#source-3)

- **1910:** *Claudia Particella, l’amante del cardinale*. Anticlerical historical feuilleton serialized in *Il Popolo* from January to May 1910. Mussolini later tried to suppress the novel’s circulation; it did not receive an Italian book edition during his lifetime. [[3]](#source-3)

- **1910, projected but unrealized:** *Il fucilato di Querétaro*. Proposed historical novel and intended second part of a four-novel anti-Habsburg sequence beginning with *Claudia Particella*; only the title and project are known, so this is an unfinished intention rather than a surviving text. [[3]](#source-3)

- **1910, projected but unrealized:** *L’imperatrice Elisabetta*. Proposed historical novel in the same projected quadrilogy. No completed manuscript is known, and it resists classification as an obra except insofar as an explicitly named project is itself a made intellectual object. [[3]](#source-3)

- **1910, projected but unrealized:** *Franz Joseph intimo*. Proposed fourth novel in the anti-Habsburg sequence. It survives as a title or plan, not as a demonstrably completed text. [[3]](#source-3)

- **1911:** *Il Trentino veduto da un socialista: note e notizie*. Political, ethnographic, and polemical essay assembled from observations made during Mussolini’s 1909 residence in Trentino; portions appeared in *La Voce* before book publication. [[3]](#source-3)

- **1911–1912:** *La mia vita dal 29 luglio 1883 al 23 novembre 1911*. Early autobiography begun in prison in December 1911 and completed the following year. It is distinct from the English-language *My Autobiography* of 1928, although later editions have sometimes shortened both titles to *La mia vita*. [[4]](#source-4)

- **1913:** *Giovanni Huss il veridico*. Anticlerical historical-biographical pamphlet on Jan Hus, written for the series “I martiri del libero pensiero.” Its mixture of biography, political exemplum, and propaganda makes it closer to a militant secular hagiography than to disinterested history. [[1]](#source-1)

- **1914:** *La guerra per la libertà e per la fine della guerra: lettera ai socialisti d’Italia*. Interventionist political letter-pamphlet issued after Mussolini resigned as editor of *Avanti!*; later printings added his public declarations. It is a manifesto-like act of political separation as much as an explanatory text. [[5]](#source-5)

- **1924:** *Preludio al Machiavelli*. Political essay first published in *Gerarchia*, using Machiavelli to formulate a theory of force, consent, and the state. Its later life as a separate pamphlet should not be mistaken for a wholly new work. [[1]](#source-1)

### Diaries, memorial works, and retrospective narratives

- **1915–1917:** *Il mio diario di guerra*. Wartime diary first published serially in *Il Popolo d’Italia* and issued in book form in 1923. The book text was edited and softened in places, so neither the serial nor the later volume can automatically be treated as an untouched field diary. [[4]](#source-4)

- **1932:** *Vita di Arnaldo*. Memorial biography of Mussolini’s brother Arnaldo. It combines family remembrance, political commemoration, and the construction of a fascist moral exemplar. [[6]](#source-6)

- **1941:** *Parlo con Bruno*. Memorial text addressed to or organized around Mussolini’s son Bruno after Bruno’s death in an aircraft accident. It is simultaneously private lament, public commemoration, and regime text. [[6]](#source-6)

- **1943:** *Pensieri pontini e sardi*. Prison and internment notes written after Mussolini’s removal from power, principally on Ponza and La Maddalena. The title designates an editorially stabilized diary-like group of reflections rather than a book published by Mussolini at the time. [[6]](#source-6)

- **1944:** *Storia di un anno: il tempo del bastone e della carota*. Political memoir and apologetic history of the period leading to Mussolini’s fall on 25 July 1943, developed from articles published during the Italian Social Republic. Its retrospective narrative is also an act of self-exculpation and renewed propaganda. [[6]](#source-6)

### Texts published under Mussolini’s name but made collaboratively

- **1928:** *My Autobiography*. English-language propaganda autobiography published first in the *Saturday Evening Post* and then as a book. It cannot securely be called a text written by Mussolini: Richard Washburn Child and Luigi Barzini Jr. ghostwrote it in English from materials supplied by Arnaldo Mussolini and Margherita Sarfatti, with Mussolini authorizing the result. [[7]](#source-7)

- **1932:** *La dottrina del fascismo*. Encyclopaedia article and subsequently an independent doctrinal pamphlet published under Mussolini’s name. Authorship is divided or disputed: Giovanni Gentile drafted the philosophical “Idee fondamentali,” while the political-historical portion is generally assigned to Mussolini, who revised and authorized the whole; it is therefore an official manifestation of doctrine rather than an uncomplicated single-author essay. [[8]](#source-8)

### Manifestos and operative political texts

- **1919:** *Programma dei Fasci italiani di combattimento* (also called the *Programma di San Sepolcro* or *Manifesto dei Fasci italiani di combattimento*). A manifesto in the strict sense: it sought to constitute and direct a movement rather than merely describe one. It was published by the Fasci’s central committee in *Il Popolo d’Italia* on 6 June; Alceste De Ambris made a major contribution, and elements also derived from Futurist political demands, so exclusive attribution to Mussolini is untenable. [[9]](#source-9)

- **1901–1945:** *Political programs, proclamations, orders, government statements, prefaces, messages, interviews, and occasional writings*. These hundreds of short operative texts do not form one authored book and often resist the category “work”: some are administrative acts, some collective state or party products, and some stenographic or journalistic manifestations of Mussolini’s speech. The Susmels printed them chronologically in volumes 1–32 and added newly located writings in volumes 35 and 37. [[1]](#source-1)

### Historical dramas and Gesamtkunstwerk-like collaborations

- **1930:** *Campo di maggio*. Historical drama about Napoleon’s Hundred Days, written through Mussolini’s collaboration with playwright and director Giovacchino Forzano. Forzano performed the material writing from Mussolini’s scenario and political instructions; even the degree of Mussolini’s textual intervention remains disputed. The subsequent film was directed by Forzano under Mussolini’s supervision, making the project extend across scenario, stage propaganda, performance, and cinema. [[10]](#source-10)

- **1931–1932:** *Villafranca*. Historical drama about Cavour and Italian unification, credited to Mussolini and Forzano and later adapted as a film. Contemporary and archival accounts describe Forzano working from Mussolini’s outline, so “co-authorship” names a hierarchical political-theatrical collaboration rather than demonstrably equal writing. [[10]](#source-10)

- **1934–1939:** *Cesare*. Historical drama on Julius Caesar, conceived for both stage and screen and completed for the theatre with Forzano. A short scenario by Mussolini survives alongside hundreds of manuscript pages by Forzano; the planned film was never realized, making the larger stage-and-cinema project incomplete even though the play was performed. [[10]](#source-10)

### Editorial projects and periodical bodies of work

- **1910–1911:** *La lotta di classe*. Socialist weekly founded and directed by Mussolini at Forlì. It is an editorial project and collective periodical rather than a single Mussolini text, although his articles and editorial direction give it the character of an extended political obra. [[11]](#source-11)

- **1912–1914:** *Avanti!* under Mussolini’s editorship. Mussolini did not create the newspaper, which predated him, but he remade its editorial voice and produced a large body of signed and unsigned journalism for it. The periodical itself therefore resists attribution as his work, while his contributions belong to his corpus. [[11]](#source-11)

- **1913–1914:** *Utopia*. Ideological review founded and directed by Mussolini as a forum for revolutionary socialism. It is best classified as an editorial project and collective intellectual manifestation, not as a book by Mussolini. [[1]](#source-1)

- **1914–1943:** *Il Popolo d’Italia*. Daily newspaper founded by Mussolini as an interventionist paper and subsequently transformed into the principal journalistic organ of his movement and regime. It is a collective institution rather than a single-authored work, but founding, directing, and repeatedly writing its editorials constituted one of Mussolini’s central continuing projects. [[9]](#source-9)

- **1922–1943:** *Gerarchia*. Political and cultural review founded by Mussolini and initially directed with Margherita Sarfatti. Like his newspapers, it is a collective editorial machine rather than a unitary text; Mussolini nevertheless used it for signed and unsigned essays and doctrinal interventions. [[11]](#source-11)

### Bodies of writing and speech preserved by the collected edition

- **1901–1945:** *Journalistic and miscellaneous writings*. The chronological core of the *Opera omnia* preserves articles, editorials, notes, polemics, interviews, messages, prefaces, and other short texts rather than a sequence of separately conceived books. Treating every item as a “book” would reproduce the error the collected edition avoids; together they form a body of work, while individual attribution—especially for unsigned official journalism—can remain uncertain. [[1]](#source-1)

- **1903–1945:** *Carteggio*. Mussolini’s surviving correspondence, printed in six appendix volumes of the *Opera omnia*. This is an incomplete archival corpus rather than a work planned for publication: survival, selection, recipient archives, secretarial drafting, and editorial recovery determine its boundaries. [[12]](#source-12)

- **1901–1944:** *Discorsi and other oratory*. Speeches, addresses, parliamentary interventions, rally performances, radio addresses, and reported remarks occupy the chronological volumes, with further material gathered in the final appendix. They are manifestations produced through voice, crowd, stenography, broadcasting, and editorial transcription; the printed wording is not always identical with what was spoken. [[12]](#source-12)

- **1951–1980, posthumous editorial construction:** *Opera omnia di Benito Mussolini*. Forty-four-volume collected edition prepared by Edoardo and Duilio Susmel: thirty-two chronological volumes, a volume of juvenile works, a volume of major works, additions and indexes, followed by appendices of writings, correspondence, and oratory. It is indispensable as the standard corpus but is not itself a work made by Mussolini, and “omnia” should not be read as a guarantee that every attribution is certain or every surviving text was found. [[1]](#source-1)
