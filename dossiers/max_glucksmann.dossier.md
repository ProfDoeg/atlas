# Max Glücksmann: Research Dossier

## Scope, identity, and evidentiary method

This dossier concerns the Austro-Hungarian-born Argentine and Uruguayan cinema, phonograph, record, publishing, and theatrical entrepreneur generally known as **Max Glücksmann**, not the South African anthropologist Max Gluckman or other similarly named persons.

The documentary record is strongest for Glücksmann’s commercial career after 1891. Information about his parents, childhood, schooling, children, and private life is sparse. Several colorful accounts of his youth derive from later interviews, descendants, or entrepreneurial profiles and are therefore identified below as reported family narrative rather than independently established fact.

The labels used are:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporaneous records, surviving artifacts, archival descriptions, or modern scholarship grounded in such sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a generally credible secondary or retrospective source but not independently verified here from the underlying record.
- **Claim or allegation:** an assertion made by a contemporary opponent or later writer, without proof sufficient to treat it as fact.
- **Tradition, myth, or legend:** a story important to the subject’s reputation but not securely documented.
- **Unresolved:** evidence is contradictory, incomplete, or unavailable.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and ancestry

No consulted source supplies securely documented names, occupations, or birthplaces for Glücksmann’s parents. His birth in Czernowitz and Jewish communal identity situate his family within the multilingual Jewish population of Bukovina, but a more exact ancestry—Ashkenazi lineage, family town of origin, religious denomination, or occupational class—cannot presently be established.

The recurrent description of him as “Romanian” reflects the later political geography of Bukovina or imprecise retrospective usage. In 1875 Czernowitz was within Austria-Hungary, not Romania. It passed to Romania after the First World War and later to Soviet Ukraine.

### Siblings

The surviving record indicates that several brothers participated in the family’s international business:

- **Bernardo Glücksmann — documented fact.** Max’s brother and manager of the Uruguayan branch from approximately 1913. Bernardo developed the firm into the leading Uruguayan distribution and exhibition circuit of its period.
- **Jacobo Glücksmann — documented fact.** A contemporary Chilean film-trade report identified him as Max’s representative in New York during negotiations with Triangle Film Corporation. A genealogical compilation gives 1881 as his birth year, but this has not been independently verified.
- **Enrique Glücksmann — documented fact.** Contemporary reporting on the Electric Palace identified Enrique as Max’s brother and spokesman while Max was in Europe.
- **Carlos Glücksmann — reported fact.** A recent University of Maryland dissertation identifies Carlos as another brother involved in the difficult negotiations separating the Argentine and Uruguayan family businesses in the 1930s.

Some secondary accounts say “Max and his three brothers” ran the firm. The combination of Bernardo, Jacobo, Enrique, and Carlos creates a numerical inconsistency. Carlos may have been an alternate name, a less continuously involved brother, or a confusion in the secondary record. This remains unresolved.

### Marriage

**Documented fact:** Glücksmann married **Rebeca Lerman** in Buenos Aires on or about **15 April 1907**. *Caras y Caretas* reported the wedding in its issue of 20 April and mentioned a bachelor’s farewell banquet held by his friends the preceding Saturday.

Little reliable material about Rebeca’s origins or independent activities was located. A saying concerning *tzedakah* is sometimes attributed online to “Rebeca Lerman de Glücksmann,” but its provenance was not established and it should not be treated as authenticated.

### Children and descendants

A genealogical entry reproduced by cinema historian Jean-Claude Seguin lists an **Enrique Glücksmann** and a **Jacobo Glücksmann**, followed by Gerardo Glücksmann and Andrés Max Glücksmann, but the formatting does not make every generation unambiguous. Other sources refer generically to “one of his grandsons” as the source of recollections about the Libertad synagogue.

Accordingly:

- That Max and Rebeca had children is **reported**, but a reliable complete list was not established.
- Gerardo Glücksmann and Andrés Max Glücksmann appear in a descendant list, but their exact relationship to Max should be treated as **reported, not independently documented**.
- No reliable evidence was located concerning extramarital partners or additional children.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1875–1890

### Czernowitz

Glücksmann was born into the Jewish community of Czernowitz on 8 March 1875. The city was the administrative and cultural center of Bukovina, characterized by German-language institutions and substantial Jewish, Romanian, Ukrainian, Polish, and other populations.

No dependable record was found for:

- the family home;
- his parents’ economic status;
- a synagogue or congregation attended;
- his formal school;
- named childhood teachers;
- apprenticeship in photography or mechanics before emigration.

### The inkwell story

**Tradition or family narrative:** A frequently repeated account says that a teacher called Jewish pupils “dogs,” whereupon the adolescent Mordechai threw an inkwell at him. Fearing punishment, he decided to leave for America with a friend.

The story appears in modern biographical compilations and is likely derived from Glücksmann family recollection or later profiles. No school disciplinary record, passenger testimony, or contemporaneous account has been located. Its central motif—a Jewish youth resisting humiliation and converting expulsion into entrepreneurial success—became part of Glücksmann’s self-made-man mythology.

### Emigration and the murdered companion

**Reported family narrative:** Glücksmann and a friend allegedly arrived in Buenos Aires on **27 July 1890**, during the Revolution of the Park. Soon after arrival, the friend was reportedly shot and killed in the political disorder.

The date is plausible: the armed uprising began on 26 July 1890. The broader fact of Glücksmann’s arrival in Argentina in July 1890 is repeated in specialist accounts. The identity of the companion, circumstances of the shooting, ship, port record, and police or death documentation were not established. The death story therefore remains unverified.

Glücksmann was fifteen and reportedly spoke no Spanish.

## Education and Formation

No formal higher education is documented.

His effective education was commercial and technical:

1. **Manual employment:** He reportedly began as a fireman or furnace assistant in a foundry.
2. **Photography:** He moved into work as a photographer’s assistant.
3. **Casa Lepage:** By 1891 he was employed at the photographic-supplies business of the Belgian immigrant Henri or Enrique Lepage.
4. **Commercial apprenticeship:** Lepage became the decisive professional mentor. Later profiles represented Glücksmann as Lepage’s “other self” and “prince heir.”
5. **Technical associates:** The French photographer and cameraman Eugène or Eugenio Py provided practical access to moving-picture technology.
6. **International business education:** Glücksmann traveled to Europe and maintained purchasing houses or representatives in Paris, London, and New York. His knowledge of machines, film rights, distribution, and recording came primarily through this commercial network.

By his own 1931 account, the Casa Lepage staff grew from three people when he entered to about 150 by the time he bought the business. A 1914 profile said he became manager at eighteen, approximately 1893.

Later claims that he possessed “cultivated knowledge of architecture” arise principally in accounts of the Libertad synagogue. No architectural education has been documented.

## Early Career: Casa Lepage, Photography, and the Arrival of Cinema

### Employment at Casa Lepage

**Documented or strongly corroborated fact:** In 1891 Glücksmann joined Casa Lepage, a photographic apparatus and supplies establishment at Bolívar 375 in Buenos Aires. He initially worked as an assistant and salesman and rose rapidly into management.

The firm imported cameras, plates, paper, phonographs, and eventually moving-picture apparatus. Its personnel brought together three complementary figures:

- Lepage, the proprietor and importer;
- Py, the photographer and cameraman;
- Glücksmann, the organizer, salesman, and later owner.

### First Buenos Aires screenings

The first generally recognized Lumière-program exhibition in Buenos Aires took place at the Teatro Odeón on **18 July 1896**, organized by businessman Francisco Pastor and journalist Eustaquio Pellicer. Glücksmann, Lepage, and Py reportedly attended.

Later biographies sometimes state or imply that Glücksmann himself opened Argentina’s first cinema in 1896. That is incorrect. The Grand Splendid did not open until 1919, and film exhibitions occurred in various theaters and adapted rooms well before then.

### Acquisition of a camera

Lepage, Py, and Glücksmann reportedly tried unsuccessfully to obtain a Lumière cinematograph. Casa Lepage instead acquired an **Elgé chronophotograph** associated with Georges Demenÿ and Léon Gaumont, and later Pathé apparatus.

The connection to the work of Étienne-Jules Marey is technological rather than personal: Marey’s chronophotography and Demenÿ’s adaptations formed part of the apparatus lineage used by Casa Lepage.

### Early films

The division of authorship among Py, Lepage, and Glücksmann is often blurred in later retellings. Py generally operated the camera; Lepage’s house supplied capital and apparatus; Glücksmann participated commercially and organizationally.

Works connected to Casa Lepage included:

- **La bandera argentina** (1897), approximately three minutes, usually credited to Eugenio Py and frequently called the first Argentine-made film.
- **Viaje del doctor Campos Salles a Buenos Aires** or film of Brazilian president Manuel Ferraz de Campos Salles’s visit (1900), commonly described as an early Argentine documentary.
- **Visita del general Mitre al Museo Histórico Nacional** (1901).
- **Revista de la Escuadra Argentina** (1901).
- **Tango argentino** and other short views, approximately 1900–1906.
- Political, military, ceremonial, street, and social “actualities.”

A surviving filmography database credits Glücksmann personally as producer of the Mitre film, but early-company credit should be treated cautiously because modern catalogs often translate firm sponsorship into individual producer credit.

### Chilean boundary mission, 1902

**Documented in cinema history:** During the Argentina–Chile boundary crisis, Glücksmann sent Py and the Spanish journalist Enrique Casellas across the Andes. They recorded Chilean military preparations, the German military mission under General Emilio Körner, and events surrounding British boundary mediator Sir Thomas Holdich.

This was an early example of Glücksmann treating news film as an organized transnational business rather than an occasional novelty.

### Phonograph-cylinder activity

A Biblioteca Nacional publication reports that the young Glücksmann rode a tricycle carrying blank wax cylinders to performers’ homes. Artists such as payador Arturo de Nava and **Ángel Villoldo** used phonographs placed there to make early commercial recordings, after which Glücksmann collected the cylinders for customers who had ordered through Casa Lepage catalogs.

This story is specific and institutionally published, but the exact dates and surviving cylinder numbers remain uncertain.

## Acquisition of Casa Lepage, 1906–1908

Henri Lepage returned permanently to Belgium in approximately 1906. Glücksmann remained in charge and, by his own retrospective account, bought the business in **1908**. The firm then traded as **Casa Lepage de Max Glücksmann**.

Sources differ between 1907 and 1908:

- 1907 appears in some general histories as the year Glücksmann acquired control.
- Glücksmann himself told *Atlántida* in 1931 that he purchased Casa Lepage in 1908.
- Advertising from 1908 identifies him as successor.

The best reconstruction is that operational control passed gradually after Lepage’s 1906 departure, while the formal purchase or public succession was completed in 1908.

Casa Lepage had already expanded beyond Bolívar 375, including a branch on Avenida de Mayo. Under Glücksmann it became a vertically integrated organization importing and selling apparatus, renting and repairing equipment, producing films, acquiring foreign-film rights, distributing films, and operating theaters.

## Chronological Life History

## 1904–1913: Entry into the Record Business

### Odeon representation

The Berlin-based International Talking Machine Company, founded by Max Straus and Heinrich Zuntz, introduced the Odeon label in 1903. Glücksmann became an Argentine representative or agent for Odeon records and machines. The date is often given as **1904**, although recent research on Uruguay places a key concession for recording and distributing Odeon material in **1913**. These may represent different stages: early importation followed by a fuller regional manufacturing or recording concession.

Odeon’s double-sided discs provided a commercial advantage over one-sided formats. Glücksmann also dealt at various times in Columbia, Fonotipia, Victor, Lioret, and other products.

### Recording and manufacturing

Glücksmann moved from importing recordings to commissioning Argentine performances and producing records carrying his name. The business evolved through labels and inscriptions including:

- Discos Glücksmann;
- Disco Nacional;
- Disco Nacional–Glücksmann;
- Nacional-Odeón;
- records “manufactured exclusively for Max Glücksmann”;
- Argentine Talking Machine Works.

Accounts commonly say Odeon helped establish a pressing operation in Buenos Aires and supplied a Berlin-trained engineer. The precise ownership and financing structure changed over time and is inadequately represented by the later claim that the factory was simply “his.”

### Early synchronized-film experiments

Between approximately 1907 and 1911, Casa Lepage reportedly produced about **32 short phonoscenes**, synchronizing projected images with separately played records. Filming occurred at the Teatro San Martín or on the Casa Lepage roof.

The performers reportedly included Ángel Villoldo, Alfredo and Flora Gobbi, Rosa Bozán, and other early tango artists. Synchronization defects were considerable, and few or none of these films survive in complete form. The series was technologically transitional rather than fully sound-on-film.

## 1913–1919: Expansion into a Regional Entertainment Empire

### Electric Palace and Palace

By 1913 Glücksmann operated the Palace and opened the **Electric Palace** on Lavalle Street between Suipacha and Esmeralda. A contemporary *Caras y Caretas* report described the Palace as the elite venue and the Electric as a luxurious but deliberately popular theater. Enrique Glücksmann gave the interview while Max was in Europe “preparing surprises.”

### Actualidades Argentinas

From April or later in **1913**, Casa Lepage/Cinematografía Max Glücksmann issued a weekly screen-news format generally titled **Actualidades Argentinas**, **Actualidades Max Glücksmann**, or **Variedades Max Glücksmann**.

The films recorded:

- state ceremonies and presidential activity;
- military displays;
- physical-education demonstrations;
- sporting events;
- public works;
- elite social occasions;
- Jewish communal institutions;
- national commemorations.

Two surviving films analyzed by Eduardo Galak show:

- a children’s physical-exercise competition organized by Club Mar del Plata, filmed on 23 February 1913;
- Argentine army cadets performing gymnastics, filmed in 1915.

Their ordered formations, institutional settings, and intertitles placed the camera inside contemporary programs of discipline, physical education, nationalism, and public pedagogy. This interpretation concerns the films’ function; it is not evidence of a written political doctrine authored by Glücksmann.

### Regional network

By the later 1910s the firm operated or supplied theaters across Argentina and in Uruguay, Chile, Paraguay, Bolivia, and Peru. Glücksmann’s model combined:

- exclusive territorial acquisition of foreign films;
- owned or leased cinemas;
- equipment sales and maintenance;
- local news production;
- live variety performances;
- record promotion;
- newspaper and trade advertising.

In Uruguay, Bernardo Glücksmann arrived in Montevideo around 1913 to manage the branch. In Chile, Carlos Battier managed or represented the distributing house.

### Triangle Film Corporation contract

**Documented reported transaction:** A contemporary Chilean trade journal reproduced news that Jacobo Glücksmann, acting in New York, negotiated with Triangle Film Corporation for rights to approximately:

- 175 photodramas;
- 100 two-reel Mack Sennett comedies;
- another 100 Triangle comedies;
- productions covering 1916–1919.

The exact final performance of this contract has not been reconstructed, but it demonstrates the scale of the firm’s purchasing ambitions.

### Fiction production

Glücksmann was principally a distributor and exhibitor, not a sustained fiction-film director. Nevertheless, he backed several important productions.

#### *Amalia* (1914)

The adaptation of José Mármol’s novel was directed and scripted by Enrique García Velloso. Surviving catalog data credit Glücksmann as assistant director and editor; Eugenio Py handled cinematography and Enrique Luchetti the camera. Production was associated with the Sociedad del Divino Rostro, a charitable society involving members of elite families.

It premiered at the Teatro Colón on **12 December 1914**. It is frequently called the first Argentine feature-length fiction film, though definitions of “feature” and the incomplete early-film record complicate absolute priority claims.

#### *Mariano Moreno y la Revolución de Mayo* (1915)

Glücksmann is credited with production and editing. Enrique García Velloso directed; actors associated with the production included César Fiaschi, Elías Alippi, Camila Quiroga, and Lola Marcó del Pont.

### First World War

The war weakened European film supply and accelerated the dominance of United States producers. Glücksmann responded by negotiating large American packages and expanding his theater network. This increased his power but also exposed him to the risks of expensive exclusivity contracts.

## 1917–1930: Gardel, Tango, and Vertical Integration

### Signing Gardel and Razzano

José González Castillo, employed by Glücksmann translating intertitles for imported films, admired the duo **Carlos Gardel–José Razzano** and persuaded Glücksmann and recording manager Mauricio Godard to audition them.

The duo began recording for Nacional-Odeón on or around **9 April 1917**. Sources differ on the first matrix, but “Cantar eterno,” by Ángel Villoldo, is frequently identified with the opening session.

The contract paid a royalty of approximately **four centavos per double-sided disc sold**. By the later 1920s Gardel’s rate had reportedly risen to around thirty centavos, seven or eight times the original amount.

In 1917 Gardel recorded “Mi noche triste,” usually treated as a decisive event in the development of the sung tango. The commercial system mattered alongside the performance: records, sheet music, cinema appearances, touring, and publicity reinforced one another.

### Other recording artists

The Glücksmann/Odeon roster included or distributed work by:

- Carlos Gardel and José Razzano;
- Roberto Firpo;
- Francisco Canaro;
- Ignacio Corsini;
- Francisco Lomuto;
- Juan Maglio;
- Osvaldo Fresedo;
- Ada Falcón;
- Sofía Bozán;
- Ángel Villoldo;
- the Gobbis;
- payadores and opera singers.

Exclusivity varied by performer, date, territory, and repertoire. It should not be assumed that every artist listed was continuously under a comprehensive personal contract.

### Royalty reputation

Later accounts credit Glücksmann with introducing record and sheet-music royalties to Argentine popular musicians. Gardel reportedly called him “my Columbus and my Juan de Garay,” saying that Glücksmann had risked substantial money when others did not believe in him.

The exact quotation’s earliest source was not verified. The broader commercial fact—a per-copy royalty in Gardel and Razzano’s contract—is well attested. Claims that Glücksmann invented Argentine musical royalties single-handedly are more difficult to prove and should be understood as part of the favorable entrepreneurial tradition.

### Cross-promotion

Artists under record contract performed in Glücksmann theaters:

- between silent films;
- as “fin de fiesta” attractions;
- at record-release events;
- on provincial and international tours designed to promote sales.

This allowed one corporate network to monetize performers through live admission, recorded music, sheet music, and cinema advertising.

### Grand Splendid

Construction began around 1917, and the **Splendid Theatre**, later Grand Splendid, opened on Avenida Santa Fe 1860 on **14 May 1919**.

The building is associated with architects Peró and Torres Armengol, with construction credited in some accounts to Pizoney and Falcope. Nazareno Orlandi painted the dome in 1919. The site had previously held the Teatro Nacional Norte and, earlier, a carriage establishment.

It served successively or simultaneously as:

- theater;
- cinema;
- recording studio;
- broadcasting site;
- headquarters or showcase for Glücksmann’s music operations.

Gardel and other performers recorded there. The building was converted into the El Ateneo Grand Splendid bookstore in 2000 and became the most visible surviving physical monument associated with Glücksmann.

The repeated assertion that the Grand Splendid was Argentina’s “first cinema” is false. Its historical importance lies in its architectural scale, integrated entertainment functions, and continued survival.

### Capitol theaters

Glücksmann operated a Capitol in the 1910s and inaugurated or relaunched a major **Cine-Teatro Capitol** in July 1927. The Teatro Capitol also hosted Albert Einstein’s Jewish-community lecture during his 1925 visit, demonstrating the overlap between Glücksmann’s commercial venues and communal activity.

### Electrical recording

In November 1926 the Grand Splendid studio adopted microphone-based electrical recording. Gardel and his guitarists were reportedly the first artists to use the new process for Nacional-Odeón.

The first attempts were defective and not commercially issued. Gardel temporarily returned to acoustic recording for six titles. From 30 November 1926 his sessions were regularly electrical.

This was an important failure-and-correction episode: Glücksmann introduced a major technology promptly, but the initial implementation did not work.

### Tango competitions

Beginning in **1924**, Disco Nacional organized annual public competitions at the Grand Splendid. There were reportedly:

- seven Buenos Aires competitions, 1924–1930;
- two parallel Montevideo competitions, 1927 and 1928;
- separate categories for tangos with and without lyrics;
- audience voting rather than decision solely by a professional jury.

Notable results included:

- **1924:** “Sentimiento gaucho,” by Francisco and Rafael Canaro and Juan Andrés Caruso, first prize in the instrumental category.
- **1924:** “Organito de la tarde,” by Cátulo Castillo and José González Castillo, third prize.
- **1926:** “Bajo Belgrano,” music by Francisco Anselmo Alita and lyrics by Francisco García Jiménez.
- **1928:** Luis Visca’s music later became “Muñeca brava,” with lyrics by Enrique Cadícamo.
- **1929:** “De todo te olvidas,” by Cadícamo, first prize in the lyric category.

A major festival on 21 December 1926 announced and performed the winning works. Gardel sang the first three selections. These events were cultural showcases and market-development mechanisms for records, sheet music, artists, and the Grand Splendid.

### San Isidro company celebration

On **19 December 1926**, Glücksmann held a rural-style company celebration at San Isidro. Gardel, Razzano, the guitarists, and orchestra leaders Francisco Canaro, Roberto Firpo, Osvaldo Fresedo, Francisco Lomuto, and Juan Maglio attended; the Canaro and Lomuto orchestras supplied music.

### Factory at Montañeses

A purpose-built Argentine Talking Machine Works facility at **Montañeses 2130/50**, Buenos Aires, was completed around November **1928**. Architect Paul Ploetz is credited. Its covered area was approximately 1,690 square meters. The building no longer survives.

Claims that this was unquestionably “Argentina’s first record factory” appear in promotional and commemorative accounts. It was an important domestic pressing plant, but absolute priority depends on the definition of manufacture and on earlier operations by competing companies.

## 1923–1942: Jewish Communal Activity

Glücksmann was not merely a donor. Contemporary Jewish press evidence places him inside institutional leadership.

### Association Hebraica and Einstein invitation

In October 1923, the Asociación Hebraica appointed **Mauricio Nirenstein, Max Glücksmann, and Isaac Starkmeth** to a special committee to invite Albert Einstein to Argentina.

Glücksmann contributed **1,000 Argentine pesos**, one of the two largest donations reported after the committee began fundraising. The invitation was coordinated with the University of Buenos Aires and other universities. Einstein postponed the trip planned for 1924 but visited Argentina in March–April 1925.

The sources establish Glücksmann’s role in the invitation committee and financing. They do not, in the material consulted, establish a private friendship or specific one-to-one meeting between Glücksmann and Einstein.

### Congregación Israelita de la República Argentina

Around the period of Einstein’s visit, Glücksmann was president of the **Congregación Israelita de la República Argentina**, described as the country’s oldest and socially elite Jewish institution. He also belonged to the first executive committee of the newspaper *Mundo Israelita*.

The institutional milieu favored Spanish-language Jewish cultural integration, cosmopolitanism, and participation in Argentine high culture. It faced criticism from Yiddish-speaking, Zionist, leftist, Sephardic, and other sectors. Sources describe accusations of “assimilationism” against the Hebraica circle. No consulted record demonstrates a detailed personal manifesto by Glücksmann on these disputes.

### Libertad synagogue

The present Libertad synagogue was expanded or rebuilt in the 1920s and early 1930s. Later congregational testimony says Glücksmann:

- made an important financial contribution;
- worked closely with architect Alejandro Enquin on the design;
- influenced the installation of the organ;
- brought musical and architectural interests to the project.

The material contribution is a **reported institutional fact**. The extent of his architectural authorship rests on retrospective testimony and should not be overstated.

### Jewish documentary films

Films associated with the company include **Moisés Ville** (1925) and **Asilo de huérfanos** (1927), valuable records of Jewish colonization and welfare institutions. Precise directorial attribution is uncertain; they are safer to describe as company productions than as films personally directed by Glücksmann.

## 1929–1936: Crash, Financial Crisis, and Retrenchment

The Wall Street crash and subsequent depression damaged the value of Glücksmann’s investments. A recent dissertation, drawing on the Uruguayan film-business record, describes his financial position as severely strained.

He sought capital from the Uruguayan family business. Bernardo refused to provide or lend the requested funds. Carlos Glücksmann participated in the negotiations. The Argentine and Uruguayan branches’ formal partnership was quietly dissolved around **1936**.

During the 1930s Max:

- rented the Grand Splendid to other operators;
- sold his remaining Argentine theaters;
- lost the vertically integrated dominance of the 1910s and 1920s;
- concentrated increasingly on records and related interests.

This complicates the simplified legend that “the 1929 crash made him sell the cinemas and keep only the label.” The decline was not a single transaction. It involved devalued investments, family conflict over capital, the sound transition, changing distribution practices, foreign studios establishing direct branches, and continuing sales or leases through the 1930s.

### Separation from the Uruguayan branch

Bernardo’s organization remained powerful in Uruguay and expanded after its separation from Max’s Argentine interests. It eventually controlled a large circuit, reportedly reaching 41 theaters—21 in Montevideo and 20 in the interior.

Later Uruguayan venues such as Radio City (1937), Trocadero (1941), Coventry (1943), and Eliseo (1949) belonged to the Glücksmann business tradition, but not all should be treated as Max’s personal property. Eliseo opened after Max’s death. The evidence points increasingly to Bernardo’s autonomous Uruguayan enterprise.

## 1931–1946: Odeon Reorganization, Gardel Dispute, Archive Seizure, and Final Years

### EMI and label changes

In 1931, the international merger that created Electrical and Musical Industries affected the Odeon network. Argentine products subsequently appeared under names including:

- Disco Nacional-Odeón;
- Disco Criollo, after official restrictions on private use of “Nacional”;
- Industrias Eléctricas y Musicales Odeón S.A.

Between approximately 1934 and 1936 Glücksmann’s name and the Argentine Talking Machine Works inscription disappeared from many labels. This reflects corporate and legal reorganization, although the precise extent of Glücksmann’s residual ownership and management is not fully documented.

### Scale claimed in 1931

In a July 1931 interview, Glücksmann said that his organization employed about **1,500 people** in sales, recording, factories, printing, and cinematography. He called it “a small republic.”

He claimed:

- branches in Rosario, Córdoba, Santa Fe, Montevideo, and Santiago de Chile;
- purchasing houses in Paris, London, and New York;
- control of seventy cinemas.

These figures are valuable contemporary self-report but were also corporate publicity. Other later accounts give sixty or one hundred theaters. The safest conclusion is that the network controlled dozens of venues through a mixture of ownership, lease, programming contracts, and distribution dependency. The round figure of one hundred should not be read as one hundred buildings personally owned outright.

### Gardel, RCA Victor, and the alleged lawsuit

When Gardel made recordings in the United States in association with RCA Victor while reportedly still subject to Nacional-Odeón commitments, a contractual conflict arose.

Later accounts say:

- Glücksmann began judicial proceedings;
- the dispute was settled amicably;
- the parties arranged for the American matrices to be shared or commercially handled in Argentina.

No court file, docket, pleading, or signed settlement was located among the consulted sources. Therefore:

- **Contractual dispute:** strongly reported.
- **Formal lawsuit:** reported but not independently verified.
- **Settlement terms:** reported, precise wording unresolved.
- **Criminal wrongdoing:** none alleged or established.

### Death of Gardel and corporate consequences

Gardel’s death in the Medellín air crash on 24 June 1935 removed the company’s most commercially valuable and culturally significant recording artist. Odeon continued issuing his records and cataloging his repertoire. No reliable evidence was found that Glücksmann exploited the death through illegal or fraudulent means.

### Expropriation of the film archive, 1944

In April **1944**, Argentina’s military government ordered approximately **20,000 meters of film**, accumulated by Casa Glücksmann across roughly forty years, declared of public interest.

Research by Laura Fernández Cordero, Maximiliano Scheggia, and Nicolás Testoni stresses that “public interest” in this instance meant compulsory taking or expropriation. The order bore the authority of President Edelmiro Farrell’s government.

This was a conflict over control of historical images: material initially created or acquired as private commercial property was redefined as national memory. Much of the transferred material was subsequently lost through inadequate preservation.

The consulted summary does not establish:

- that Glücksmann received fair compensation;
- the exact legal objections he filed;
- whether anti-Jewish motives contributed;
- whether the seizure was directed personally against him rather than toward archival centralization.

Any claim that the action was a proven antisemitic confiscation would therefore exceed the available evidence, although the political context warrants further archival investigation.

### Final years and death

By the time of his death, Glücksmann’s distribution company had lost its earlier preeminence. Direct operation by American studios, corporate restructuring, financial losses, and the independent course of Bernardo’s Uruguayan branch had reduced Max’s control.

He died in Buenos Aires on **20 October 1946**, reportedly of a heart attack, and was buried in the Jewish cemetery at Ciudadela.

No reliable evidence was found of prolonged illness, addiction, psychiatric crisis, imprisonment, criminal prosecution, or violent death.

### Business model

Glücksmann’s principal innovation was organizational rather than a patented machine. He integrated:

```text
international rights and equipment
                ↓
local recording and film production
                ↓
pressing, printing, and distribution
                ↓
owned/leased theaters and retail outlets
                ↓
live appearances and promotional competitions
                ↓
record and sheet-music sales
```

This reduced dependence on outside exhibitors and allowed successful artists or titles to be promoted through several media at once.

### Patents and inventions

No patent registered personally to Max Glücksmann was identified in the consulted material.

He was an importer, adopter, and commercial organizer of technologies including:

- photographic cameras and supplies;
- phonographs and gramophones;
- wax-cylinder recording;
- double-sided shellac discs;
- motion-picture cameras and projectors;
- Elgé/Gaumont-Demenÿ chronophotography;
- synchronized disc-and-film presentation;
- local record pressing;
- electrical microphone recording.

Calling him the “inventor” of cinema, the phonograph, sound film, or the record industry in Argentina would be inaccurate.

### Wealth

Glücksmann became wealthy enough to finance large theaters, international film contracts, manufacturing, and major charitable contributions.

A recurrent claim says that *Fortune* magazine listed him among the great fortunes of the Americas in **1914**. This is chronologically impossible: *Fortune* was founded in 1929. The claim is therefore either a confusion with another publication, a wrong date, or later legend.

No audited personal net-worth figure, estate inventory, complete property schedule, or shareholding register was located. Descriptions of him as one of the richest men in the Americas should be treated as promotional unless linked to a specific contemporary financial source.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Politics

No evidence was found that Glücksmann:

- held elected political office;
- joined a political party;
- financed a documented party organization;
- engaged in espionage or intelligence work;
- supported political violence;
- was imprisoned or exiled for politics.

His newsreels often recorded presidents, military activity, state ceremonies, patriotic holidays, and disciplined physical culture. They participated in the visual production of official national life, but this does not by itself establish his personal party allegiance.

### Jewish identity

His Jewish identification is firmly documented through marriage reporting, communal office, philanthropy, burial, and institutional work.

The institutions with which he was associated promoted a Spanish-speaking, culturally integrated, often liberal and cosmopolitan Jewish identity. This brought them into conflict with sectors that regarded them as socially elitist or assimilationist.

It is not known how strictly Glücksmann observed Jewish religious law. The installation of an organ in the Libertad synagogue indicates involvement in a modernizing, Central European-style congregational culture rather than traditional Orthodox practice.

### Artistic outlook

Glücksmann was not primarily an artist-theorist. His artistic influence operated through selection, financing, technical mediation, and market organization.

His catalog joined:

- European opera;
- Argentine popular and rural song;
- instrumental tango;
- sung tango;
- documentary film;
- literary adaptations;
- imported European and American cinema;
- live theater and variety entertainment.

His competitions and royalty contracts helped make composition and recording commercially legible activities. At the same time, exclusivity concentrated gatekeeping power in his firm.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

## Market concentration

**Documented structural fact:** Glücksmann controlled a large vertically integrated network and used long-term exclusive contracts with artists, composers, producers, and theaters. Contemporary and later writers frequently described this as monopoly or near-monopoly.

What is established:

- The firm controlled major catalogs and many cinemas.
- It bundled films into monthly programs.
- It obtained territorial exclusivities.
- It signed prominent musicians and composers.
- It used its theaters to promote its records and artists.

What is not established:

- A judicial finding that the company constituted an illegal monopoly.
- A criminal antitrust prosecution.
- A complete quantitative market share for Argentina or the continent.
- That Glücksmann literally owned every theater described as part of his “circuit.”

## The *La Película* campaign, 1916–1918

A major dedicated adversarial source is the Buenos Aires trade journal *La Película*, founded in September 1916. Its inaugural attack, “Grandes monopolizadores de la cinematografía: La Casa Max Glücksmann,” announced a campaign to expose what it called the “organization, purposes and conduct” of the firm.

The journal alleged that Glücksmann’s monthly subscription system:

- forced suburban exhibitors to accept old or undesirable films;
- charged excessive prices;
- reserved important premières for favored venues;
- failed to respect some theater contracts;
- made false or misleading agreements;
- used market power to obstruct independent exhibitors.

It mocked the supplied programs as “rancid” leftovers and compared them to unappetizing sausages.

**Evidentiary assessment:**

- The attacks are **documented contemporary allegations**.
- They reveal genuine resentment against vertical integration and bundling.
- The publication initially used opaque or “phantom” direction and staff, which complicates evaluation of its commercial interests.
- The campaign was polemical and “systematic and ruthless,” in the assessment of later scholarship.
- No consulted material establishes that a court validated the allegations.
- The allegations should neither be suppressed as mere propaganda nor converted into proven fraud.

## Artist contracts and copyright

Exclusive agreements gave Glücksmann leverage over performers and repertoire. Favorable accounts emphasize that he paid royalties; critical accounts emphasize long duration and concentration of copyrights.

Both may be true:

- royalty payment could materially improve artists’ income;
- the same contract could restrict an artist’s freedom to record elsewhere;
- control of composers and sheet music could reinforce control of performers and theaters.

An additional attribution problem existed in early recording: Gardel and Razzano were listed as authors of many songs whose true authorship was uncertain. This practice occurred before robust authors’ societies and reliable registration systems. No source consulted shows that Glücksmann personally ordered false attribution, but his company published and profited within that imperfect system.

## Gardel/RCA dispute

This was a contractual and civil-commercial dispute, not a criminal accusation. A judicial proceeding is reported, followed by settlement, but the primary file was not located. It should not be described as Gardel committing proven fraud or Glücksmann winning a judgment.

## Family-business conflict

The disagreement with Bernardo over access to Uruguayan capital is documented in recent scholarship as an internal financial conflict. Bernardo’s refusal to lend or transfer capital was not itself wrongdoing. The result was a quiet separation around 1936.

## 1929 losses

Accounts consistently associate Glücksmann’s decline with the crash and devalued investments. No evidence was found of bankruptcy fraud, concealment of assets, insider trading, embezzlement, or criminal insolvency.

## 1944 archive expropriation

The state’s compulsory acquisition of 20,000 meters of film was an official measure, not a criminal seizure following conviction. It represents a property and archival controversy. Subsequent state loss of much of the collection damaged the surviving record of early Argentine film.

## Searches for other wrongdoing

A dedicated search combined Glücksmann’s name with Spanish and English terms for scandal, lawsuit, fraud, accusations, corruption, conspiracy, criticism, and allegations, including material indexed or published in 2024–2026.

No credible evidence was found connecting him personally to:

- organized crime;
- corruption or bribery;
- money laundering;
- intelligence agencies;
- political violence;
- sexual abuse;
- narcotics;
- imprisonment;
- criminal conviction;
- fraudulent securities;
- wartime collaboration.

The significant negative record consists instead of market-power criticism, contractual disputes, trade hostility, business failure, family financial conflict, and the ambiguity of corporate credit.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The penniless immigrant who arrived during a revolution”

**Story:** Glücksmann reached Buenos Aires without money, unable to speak Spanish, as the Revolution of the Park erupted.

**Appearance and circulation:** Retrospective interviews, self-made-man profiles, Jewish immigrant commemorations, and later biographies.

**Evidence:** His 1890 arrival and rapid employment are well supported. His exact assets at arrival are not documented.

**Effect:** It established the standard rise-from-nothing narrative.

### The inkwell thrown at an antisemitic teacher

**Story:** He attacked a teacher who called Jews dogs and fled Europe.

**Evidence:** No contemporary record located; probably oral or family tradition.

**Effect:** Casts emigration as an act of resistance to antisemitism rather than solely economic migration.

### The murdered traveling companion

**Story:** His companion was shot shortly after their arrival during the 1890 revolution.

**Evidence:** Historically plausible context, but no identified victim or official record.

**Effect:** Intensifies the drama of Glücksmann’s arrival and isolation.

### “He brought cinema to Argentina”

**Story:** Glücksmann personally introduced or invented Argentine cinema.

**Evidence against literal form:** The 1896 screening was organized by Pastor and Pellicer using imported Lumière films. Py was the principal early cameraman. Other exhibitors and experimenters were active.

**Underlying fact:** Glücksmann was an unusually important early organizer of film equipment importation, production, distribution, newsreels, and exhibition.

### “The Grand Splendid was Argentina’s first cinema”

**Story:** Glücksmann opened the first Argentine cinema in 1896 or later built the first cinema as the Grand Splendid.

**Evidence against:** The Grand Splendid opened in 1919, more than two decades after initial screenings and after numerous film venues existed.

**Effect:** The survival and fame of the building have displaced less visible earlier venues in popular memory.

### “One hundred cinemas”

**Story:** He owned one hundred theaters across Argentina, Uruguay, Chile, and Paraguay.

**Evidence:** Contemporary self-report in 1931 gives seventy; other sources give more than sixty or approximately one hundred. Networks included leases, programming arrangements, distribution clients, and family branches, not only outright ownership.

**Assessment:** A large multinational circuit is documented; the round number and ownership form are unresolved.

### “Listed by Fortune in 1914”

**Story:** *Fortune* listed Glücksmann among the Americas’ great fortunes in 1914.

**Evidence against:** The magazine did not exist until 1929.

**Assessment:** Chronologically false as stated. It may preserve a distorted memory of a different financial or society publication.

### “Inventor of royalties in Argentina”

**Story:** He introduced royalties to Argentine record and sheet-music production.

**Evidence:** The Gardel-Razzano per-copy contract is supported. Absolute national priority has not been demonstrated.

**Effect:** Supported a benevolent “Don Max” image among tango musicians, offsetting criticism of exclusivity.

### “Unchallenged tango monopoly”

**Story:** Glücksmann controlled all Argentine tango recording.

**Evidence:** He commanded a dominant roster and powerful catalog, but Victor, Columbia, and other companies remained or re-entered the market.

**Assessment:** “Virtual monopoly” is a useful characterization of market power in a limited period, not a literal absence of competitors.

### Confusion between Max’s empire and Bernardo’s later circuit

Later accounts frequently attribute every Glücksmann-branded Uruguayan theater to Max. Bernardo increasingly ran an autonomous business; venues opened after 1936, and especially after Max’s death, belong to the family-company legacy rather than Max’s personal career.

## Death, Estate, and Immediate Aftermath

Glücksmann died on 20 October 1946. The available record does not disclose:

- a probate file;
- estate valuation;

- disposition of company shares;

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Cinema

Glücksmann is remembered as one of the organizers of the industrial infrastructure of Argentine and regional cinema. His contribution was not a single invention but a system linking apparatus, cameras, production, news film, international rights, distribution, and exhibition.

The destruction or disappearance of most early silent footage has distorted this legacy. Approximately 90 percent of Argentine silent production is estimated to be lost. The 1944 expropriation followed by further loss of the Glücksmann archive removed much of the direct evidence by which his production could be judged.

### Recorded music and tango

His longest cultural afterlife comes through records. The Nacional-Odeón catalog:

- preserved early tango performance styles;
- amplified Gardel’s emergence;
- circulated Argentine music internationally;
- helped normalize artist royalties;
- encouraged composition through competitions;
- connected performers to reproducible consumer media.

His role is sometimes submerged beneath Gardel’s fame or later EMI branding.

### Architecture

The Grand Splendid is now internationally famous as a bookstore. Its preservation maintains Glücksmann’s name in architectural histories, although tourist retellings frequently introduce inaccurate priority claims.

The Libertad synagogue provides a second surviving institutional monument, although his exact design contribution remains partly dependent on oral testimony.

### Jewish communal history

He represents a strand of affluent, Germanophone or Central European-influenced Jewish civic integration in Buenos Aires. His role in bringing Einstein to Argentina linked commercial wealth, Jewish institutional ambition, and Argentine academic culture.

### Uruguay and regional cinema

The Glücksmann circuit shaped filmgoing in Uruguay for decades. Historians increasingly separate Max’s initiating and financial role from Bernardo’s sustained management and later independent control.

### Historical reinterpretation

Earlier accounts generally treated Glücksmann as an uncomplicated self-made pioneer. Recent scholarship has added:

- the distinction between ownership and network control;
- the role of brothers and employees;
- the polemics over monopoly;
- the politics embodied in newsreels;
- the financial rupture after 1929;
- the coercive 1944 transfer of his archive;
- the need to distinguish Max’s Argentine company from Bernardo’s Uruguayan successor.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere co-presence in Argentina or shared Jewish, artistic, or technological contexts has not been counted.

### Albert Einstein — documented fact

Glücksmann served with Mauricio Nirenstein and Isaac Starkmeth on the Asociación Hebraica committee formed in Buenos Aires in October 1923 to invite Einstein. He contributed 1,000 pesos to the effort. Einstein eventually visited Argentina in March–April 1925 and addressed Jewish audiences, including a lecture associated with the Teatro Capitol.

The sources document institutional and financial participation but do not establish a private friendship or specific bilateral meeting.

### Carlos Gardel — documented fact

Glücksmann’s company signed Gardel and José Razzano in 1917, recorded and distributed their work through Nacional-Odeón, paid per-disc royalties, promoted performances in its theaters, and adopted electrical recording with Gardel in 1926. A later contractual conflict arose over Gardel’s American RCA Victor recordings and was reportedly settled.

### Ada Falcón — documented fact

Falcón was among the Odeon artists whose recordings were promoted by Glücksmann’s company. A 1930 advertisement for Enrique Santos Discépolo’s “Yira… yira…” offered five versions by exclusive Nacional-Odeón performers, including Gardel, Canaro, Corsini, Ada Falcón, and Sofía Bozán. The evidence establishes a company-artist relationship, not necessarily a close personal friendship.

### Cátulo Castillo — documented fact

Cátulo Castillo’s “Organito de la tarde,” with lyrics by his father and Glücksmann employee José González Castillo, won third prize in the instrumental category of Glücksmann’s 1924 competition at the Grand Splendid. José González Castillo also helped bring Gardel and Razzano to Glücksmann’s attention.

### Enrique Santos Discépolo — documented fact

Glücksmann’s company commercially promoted multiple recorded versions of Discépolo’s “Yira… yira…” in 1930. No close personal relationship was established.

### Ángel Villoldo — documented fact

Biblioteca Nacional material reports that Glücksmann transported blank cylinders to performers including Villoldo for early recording. Gardel and Razzano’s first Nacional-Odeón session is frequently said to have included Villoldo’s “Cantar eterno.” Villoldo also participated in early synchronized-film experiments associated with Casa Lepage.

### Étienne-Jules Marey — documented technological connection, not personal contact

The Elgé chronophotograph used by the Casa Lepage group belonged to the technological lineage of Marey’s chronophotographic research and Georges Demenÿ’s commercial adaptations. No meeting or correspondence between Marey and Glücksmann is documented.
