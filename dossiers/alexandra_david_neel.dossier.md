# Alexandra David-Néel: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier concerns Alexandra David-Néel, born Louise Eugénie Alexandrine Marie David (1868–1969), the French-Belgian writer, Buddhist practitioner, orientalist, singer, feminist, anarchist, and traveler.

The reconstruction draws on institutional records, surviving archival descriptions, her published correspondence and travel narratives, contemporary documentation discussed in scholarship, museum collections, specialist studies, and later biographies. Her autobiographical writings remain indispensable but are not neutral records: she selected, revised, dramatized, and sometimes destroyed evidence concerning her own life.

Evidentiary terms used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by archival, official, photographic, bibliographic, or contemporaneous documentary evidence.
- **Reported fact:** stated by reputable biographers or institutions but not independently verified here from the underlying document.
- **Disputed claim:** substantial competing accounts exist.
- **Allegation:** an accusation made by an identifiable person or publication without adequate proof.
- **Rumor or myth:** a circulating narrative whose literal truth is unproved or contradicted.
- **Inference:** a reasoned interpretation, explicitly identified as such.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Louis Pierre David**, her father, was French, of Huguenot or Protestant family background. Sources variously describe him as a teacher, journalist, republican activist, printer, or man connected with educational and publishing work. These occupational descriptions may represent successive activities rather than contradictions. He was a republican, anticlerical, and Freemason.

**Alexandrine Borghmans**, her mother, was Belgian and Roman Catholic. The Borghmans family had greater financial resources than Louis David, but the marriage was unhappy and affected by financial disagreement.

The contrast between her Catholic mother and republican, anticlerical father supplied David-Néel with an early model of religious and political conflict. Later narratives portray her home as emotionally cold. That characterization rests principally on her recollections and sympathetic biographies and should be treated as **reported fact**, not independently observable fact.

### Siblings

She is generally described as the couple’s only surviving child. Some biographies mention an earlier son who died in infancy or childhood. The exact dates and circumstances are inconsistently reported and were not securely established from an accessible civil record in this research.

### Political family memory

A frequently repeated story says that in 1871 Louis David took his two-year-old daughter to the Père-Lachaise cemetery after the suppression of the Paris Commune, showing her the site of the Communards’ executions so that she would remember human cruelty. This is best classified as a **reported autobiographical or family story**. It accords with Louis David’s politics but has acquired the structure of a formative legend.

### Marriage and children

David-Néel married Philippe Néel at Tunis on 4 August 1904. She did not have biological children. Her surviving writings indicate that she regarded maternity and conventional domesticity as incompatible with the independent intellectual life she wanted.

In 1929 she legally adopted Aphur Yongden, a Sikkimese lama of Tibetan parentage whom she had met in the Himalayan region around 1914. He became Albert Aphur Yongden David in French civil life. Their relationship combined the roles of attendant, interpreter, pupil, teacher, collaborator, expedition partner, household member, and son.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1868–1885

Alexandra was born in Saint-Mandé but spent much of her childhood in Belgium, especially around Brussels and Ixelles. Her parents’ troubled marriage, differences of religion, and recurring financial tension form the background to her early life.

Accounts based substantially on her later testimony describe her as solitary, resistant to confinement, and fascinated by death, asceticism, religious ritual, travel, and self-testing. She read accounts of saints and hermits but reacted against dogmatic Catholicism.

She later recounted repeated attempts to escape the physical and social limits imposed upon her:

- At about fifteen she reportedly left home and traveled toward England.
- At seventeen she attempted or completed another solitary excursion toward Switzerland or Italy, crossing Alpine terrain with little money.
- Some versions describe her reaching the Saint-Gotthard region; others compress several youthful journeys into a single dramatic “flight.”

These episodes are **reported facts with uncertain itineraries**. Their recurring presentation as proof of a destiny for exploration belongs partly to retrospective self-fashioning.

She attended both Protestant and Catholic boarding schools. Her mother prevented her from studying medicine, according to the institutional biographical reconstruction in [AGORHA/INHA](https://agorha.inha.fr//ark%3A/54721/76318b15-fff2-45b4-8d27-4189cf39a9b8).

## Education and Formation

### Musical and theatrical training

In 1886 she enrolled in the Royal Conservatory of Brussels or associated Brussels conservatory instruction, studying singing, piano or music theory, Italian, declamation, gesture, deportment, and theatrical expression. Institutional research reports that she received a first prize in singing in 1889.

Her formal education was therefore strongest in music and theatrical performance rather than in the modern university disciplines with which she later became associated.

### Self-directed Oriental studies

She studied philosophy, comparative religion, Sanskrit, and Asian religions through:

- Libraries and lectures in Paris;
- The Musée Guimet;
- Courses and contacts associated with the Sorbonne and Collège de France;
- The Société Théosophique;
- Personal instruction in India, Sikkim, China, and Tibetan monasteries;
- Tibetan-language instruction from Kazi Dawa Samdup and others;
- Practice and textual work with Lachen Gomchen Rinpoche and Aphur Yongden.

Claims that she held an ordinary European doctorate in Tibetology are false or misleading. She received honorific religious recognition in Tibet, described in her account as titles equivalent to lama and doctor of Buddhist philosophy, but not a modern accredited university doctorate.

### Philosophical influences

Her early reading included Stoicism, especially Epictetus; European freethought; anarchism; feminism; the philosophy of the Buddha; Vedānta; and the popular occult and comparative-religion literature of late nineteenth-century Europe.

She kept notebooks from at least 1882 and an intimate journal in 1889–1892. The [AGORHA archival inventory](https://agorha.inha.fr/detail/761) notes that she destroyed older papers on more than one occasion, including a significant destruction in 1910. Consequently, the surviving archive is a deliberately shaped remainder, not a complete record.

## Theosophy, Freemasonry, Anarchism, and Feminism

### Theosophy

David joined the European Section of the Theosophical Society in London on 7 June 1892. The movement offered access to Asian religious texts, women intellectual organizers, international networks, and the Adyar headquarters in India.

An archival study of early French Theosophy reproduces evidence that Annie Besant corresponded with her in March 1893 about admission to the Society’s Esoteric Section. Whether she was finally admitted to that inner section is uncertain. The available Adyar information cited by the study suggests that she may not have completed admission.

She later criticized Theosophical romanticism and claims of supernatural authority. Her mature Buddhism was therefore not simply Theosophy under another name, although Theosophy was an important institutional bridge.

### Freemasonry

She became associated with mixed or adoptive Freemasonry. Later accounts credit her with advancement as high as the thirtieth degree of the Ancient and Accepted Scottish Rite. The exact lodge, initiation date, and sequence of degrees are not securely established in publicly accessible documentation. A French Masonic account explicitly states that the place where she was initiated remains unknown. The claim should therefore be treated as **reported fact with incomplete documentation**, not as a fully reconstructed Masonic career.

### Anarchism and socialism

During the 1890s she wrote for *L’Étoile socialiste* and other radical publications. She associated with the anarchist geographer Élisée Reclus, who encouraged or prefaced *Pour la vie*. Her anarchism emphasized personal autonomy, opposition to coercive institutions, free intellectual development, and skepticism toward marriage, church, state, and property privilege.

She did not become a major party organizer or revolutionary conspirator. Her political work was primarily intellectual and journalistic.

### Feminism

She contributed to feminist debate, including in *La Fronde*, the newspaper founded and operated by women. Her *Le Féminisme rationnel* criticized both male domination and versions of elite feminism that, in her view, ignored working women’s material conditions.

Her life challenged prevailing gender restrictions, but she did not consistently formulate her public identity in the language of later feminist movements. Her private correspondence could also be hierarchical and severe toward female assistants.

## Early Career: Writing and the Operatic Stage, 1889–1904

### Early journey to India

After receiving money through an inheritance—usually attributed to a godmother or relative, Elisabeth Morgan—she traveled in 1890–1891 or 1891–1892 through Ceylon and India. Exact chronology differs among biographies. She visited the Theosophical headquarters at Adyar and encountered Indian religious practice beyond European texts. She returned when her money was exhausted.

A longer residence at Adyar followed in 1893. There she encountered the institutional culture of international Theosophy and studied Sanskrit and religious philosophy.

### Journalism

From 1893 onward she published articles under her own name and as **Mitra**, including in *Le Lotus bleu* and *L’Étoile socialiste*. Her subjects included social emancipation, religion, Asian philosophy, feminism, and individual freedom.

### Opera in French Indochina

Under the stage name **Alexandra Myrial**, she was engaged as a leading singer in Hanoi and Haiphong during the 1895–1896 and 1896–1897 seasons. Her repertory reportedly included:

- Violetta in Verdi’s *La traviata*;
- Roles in *Carmen*;
- *Lakmé*;
- *Thaïs*;
- *Faust*;
- *Mireille*;
- *Les Noces de Jeannette*.

Contemporary theatrical reconstruction confirms that “Alexandra Myrial” was an important performer in the Tonkin opera circuit, rather than merely an amateur later exaggerating a theatrical past. See Samuel Thévoz’s study, [“The Star of Tonkin”](https://ifi.vnu.edu.vn/uploads/news/2022_11/fap-n3-final03thevoz-myrial.pdf).

### Jean Haustont and *Lidia*

From approximately 1897 to 1900 she lived or worked closely with the musician Jean Haustont in Paris, at 3 rue Nicolo according to later reconstruction. She wrote the libretto to the one-act lyric drama *Lidia*; Haustont composed the music. Sources describe them as partners or lovers, but the precise private terms of the relationship rest on correspondence and biography.

She performed in Athens between November 1899 and January 1900 and went to Tunis in July 1900.

### Tunis and Philippe Néel

In Tunis she met Philippe Néel, a railway engineer, apparently a distant cousin. Their intimate relationship began around 15 September 1900. She gave up full-time singing around 1902, briefly directed artistic programming at the Tunis casino, and continued research and writing.

They married on 4 August 1904. She possessed a meaningful personal capital at marriage; one later reconstruction gives 77,696 francs, but that amount should be treated as **reported**, pending inspection of the marriage contract or notarial inventory.

---

## Marriage and Intellectual Work, 1904–1911

The marriage was unconventional and often conflictual. Philippe expected more domestic stability than Alexandra was prepared to give; Alexandra wanted financial and geographical independence. Nevertheless, they maintained a durable intellectual and financial bond.

She traveled, studied, and published essays on Asian religion and philosophy. Among her early scholarly-popular subjects were Korea, Tibetan religious power, the Chinese philosopher Mozi, Buddhist modernism, and feminism.

Philippe financed a significant portion of her later travel, directly or through management of her money. Assertions that she was a wholly self-financed solitary adventurer are therefore inaccurate. She also sought or received support from French ministries and learned societies for the 1911 expedition.

On 3 August 1911 she sailed for Asia, promising to return in roughly eighteen months. She did not return to France until 1925.

## The Long Asian Journey, 1911–1925

## India, Ceylon, and Sikkim, 1911–1914

She traveled through Ceylon and India, studying Buddhism and Hindu philosophy. She visited Benares/Varanasi, northern India, and Sikkim.

In 1912 she became closely associated with **Sidkeong Tulku Namgyal**, crown prince of Sikkim. He was educated partly at Oxford, interested in Buddhist reform, and later briefly became chogyal. David-Néel advised him on religious reform; he helped arrange access, teachers, and interpreters.

Kazi Dawa Samdup became her principal Tibetan-language instructor and interpreter. He accompanied her to Kalimpong, where on 15 April 1912 she received an audience from the exiled Thirteenth Dalai Lama, Thubten Gyatso. She asked doctrinal questions and was advised to learn Tibetan. The audience is a **documented fact**; claims that she was categorically “the first Western woman ever received by a Dalai Lama” are frequently repeated but harder to prove universally.

In the waiting room she met the Japanese monk and traveler Ekai Kawaguchi, who had entered Lhasa in disguise. She met him again in Japan. His achievement helped demonstrate the practical possibility of clandestine entry.

### Sidkeong relationship

Their correspondence establishes intellectual and emotional intimacy. Later biographies have suggested that they were lovers. The surviving evidence supports a close relationship and mutual confidence but does not conclusively establish a sexual affair. This is a **disputed or unresolved claim**.

Sidkeong died in December 1914, soon after becoming chogyal. Rumors alleged poisoning in Sikkimese court politics. David-Néel was not accused of participation. The case affected her emotionally and helped end the reforming partnership.

## Lachen, Yongden, and tantric training, 1914–1916

Around 1914 she met the adolescent Aphur Yongden. His reported birth year varies, usually 1899; consequently he was approximately fourteen or fifteen. He became her attendant, translator, travel partner, collaborator, and ultimately adopted son.

She lived in a hermitage near Lachen in northern Sikkim, at roughly 3,900–4,000 meters, near **Lachen Gomchen Rinpoche**. She undertook intensive meditation and studied Tibetan tantric practice, including:

- *Tummo*, the discipline associated with inner heat;
- Extended retreat or *tsam*;
- Visualization;
- Tibetan language and scriptures;
- Ritual and oral instruction.

Descriptions of supernatural accomplishments come mainly from her later writings and cannot be independently tested. The fact that she undertook extended retreat and religious instruction is well supported.

## Unauthorized Tibet journey and the Panchen Lama, 1916

On 13 July 1916 she entered Tibet without British authorization, accompanied by Yongden and another monk. She reached Tashilhunpo near Shigatse on 16 July and was received by the Ninth Panchen Lama, Thubten Chökyi Nyima, on 19 July.

She reported access to scriptures, audiences with high religious figures, and receipt of honorary lama and learned titles. She departed on 26 July. British authorities subsequently required her to leave Sikkim because of the unauthorized crossing.

Descriptions that the British “imprisoned” her are unsupported. She was expelled or ordered away from the frontier region, not incarcerated.

## Japan, Korea, and China, 1917–1921

Unable or unwilling to return through wartime Europe, she and Yongden traveled through Burma or Southeast Asian ports to Japan. She visited Kyoto, Tokyo, and Nara and renewed contact with Ekai Kawaguchi. She proceeded through Korea and reached Beijing in October 1917.

From Beijing they crossed northern China with a Tibetan lama, traveled through regions affected by warlord conflict and banditry, and reached Xining and Kumbum in 1918. They remained at or around Kumbum monastery until early 1921.

At Kumbum, David-Néel deepened her study of monastic life and Tibetan literature. With Yongden’s linguistic assistance, she translated or interpreted Buddhist texts, including Prajñāpāramitā material.

Claims that she alone produced philologically exact translations directly from every original language understate Yongden’s and other teachers’ contribution. Her work combined language study, oral explanation, paraphrase, compilation, and interpretation.

## Across China and eastern Tibet, 1921–1923

They left Kumbum on 5 January 1921. Their movements included Gansu, Mongolia or the Gobi borderlands, monasteries and caravan routes of western China, and Tibetan cultural regions under shifting Chinese, Tibetan, or local authority.

By 1922 they were moving through difficult passes and returned to or paused near Ganzhou. In 1923 they traveled through Lanzhou and reached Chengdu on 18 June. They left again on 14 July toward the southwest, ultimately preparing the clandestine approach to central Tibet.

Civil war, brigandage, inadequate funds, illness, weather, and repeated obstruction made this phase less linear than later heroic summaries suggest.

---

## The Lhasa Journey, 1923–1924

David-Néel and Yongden approached Tibet from Yunnan and the Sino-Tibetan borderlands. She disguised herself as a Tibetan beggar or pilgrim; Yongden wore clothing consistent with his identity as a lama. They minimized baggage and concealed her European appearance by darkening exposed skin, wearing worn clothing, and avoiding prolonged conversations.

They crossed high passes and river systems, suffered hunger and cold, and traveled partly on foot. Their route passed through eastern Tibet toward the Tsangpo/Brahmaputra system.

They entered Lhasa in February 1924, during the Monlam or Great Prayer Festival, and remained for roughly two months. They saw the Potala and visited major sites in and around the city.

The [Musée Guimet](https://www.guimet.fr/fr/actualites-du-musee/alexandra-david-neel-sur-le-toit-du-monde-voyage-lhassa-1924) preserves and exhibits photographs associated with their stay, including an image of David-Néel, Yongden, and a Tibetan child before the Potala. The existence of photographs, letters, itinerary evidence, and contemporary recognition makes the entry into Lhasa a strongly documented event.

Some picturesque details are less secure. The often-repeated story that she was discovered because she washed daily in a river, making her suspiciously clean for a beggar, belongs to the anecdotal tradition surrounding the journey. Authorities appear eventually to have learned or suspected her identity, but she was allowed to depart rather than arrested.

She exited Tibet through Gyantse and Sikkim and reached India. Her arrival generated international press attention.

### “First woman” qualification

She is routinely called “the first Western woman” or “first European woman” to enter Lhasa. The safest formulation is:

> She was the first European woman known to have entered and stayed in Lhasa whose journey was widely documented and publicized.

She was not the first woman in Lhasa, nor necessarily the first non-Tibetan woman. Missionary women and travelers had reached Tibetan border regions, and earlier identities and clandestine passages are not exhaustively recoverable.

## Return to Europe and Public Celebrity, 1925–1936

She returned to France in May 1925 after nearly fourteen years. Philippe and Alexandra reunited briefly but did not resume a conventional marriage. Yongden accompanied her. Alexandra and Philippe nevertheless continued an extensive correspondence until Philippe’s death.

She lectured in France and elsewhere in Europe and was received as an explorer, religious authority, and public intellectual. Press coverage amplified the image of an elderly Parisian woman who had defeated imperial border controls through disguise and endurance.

In 1927 she published *Voyage d’une Parisienne à Lhassa*, the work that fixed the public form of the adventure. It was translated internationally.

In May 1928 she purchased a property at Digne. She called it **Dhyāna Vihāra** in Sanskrit and **Samten Dzong** in Tibetan, usually translated as “Fortress of Meditation” or “Fortress of Concentration.” A notarial record of the purchase survives, according to the [AGORHA research entry](https://agorha.inha.fr//ark%3A/54721/76318b15-fff2-45b4-8d27-4189cf39a9b8).

In 1929 she legally adopted Yongden. They wrote, translated, maintained correspondence, cultivated the property, and traveled for lectures.

She learned to drive and obtained a license in her sixties. Popular accounts often date this to about 1935.

Her principal successes in this period included:

- International publication and translation;
- The gold medal of the Société de Géographie;
- Appointment as a chevalier of the Legion of Honour;
- Recognition from scholarly and Buddhist audiences;
- Financial independence derived from property, royalties, lecture fees, savings, and Philippe’s continuing assistance.

No evidence found suggests that she controlled a commercial company, patents, or industrial investments. Her economic structure was personal: inherited capital, marital financial support, grants, fees, publishing contracts, and real property.

## Second China Journey, 1937–1946

On 9 January 1937, aged sixty-eight, she left again with Yongden, traveling through Brussels, Moscow, and the Trans-Siberian Railway to Beijing.

She went to Wutai Shan and stayed at the Pusading monastery, intending to study traditions of Mañjuśrī, Tibetan Buddhism, and Taoism. The Sino-Japanese War transformed the research journey into prolonged displacement.

They left Wutai Shan on 20 September 1937. At Taiyuan they encountered bombing and received assistance from Baptist missionaries. During an alarm they reportedly lost much of their baggage. They continued through Hankou and Chongqing, repeatedly relying on Protestant and Catholic mission networks and French consular assistance despite David-Néel’s anticlericalism.

They reached Chengdu in March 1938. In July they arrived at Tatsienlu, now Kangding, a major Sino-Tibetan frontier town. They lived there from approximately 1938 to 1943, sometimes in poor conditions and amid refugees, inflation, food shortages, and war.

Her experiences informed:

- *Sous des nuées d’orage*;
- *À l’ouest barbare de la vaste Chine*;
- Later reflections on Chinese and Tibetan political change.

She returned to Chengdu around 1943 and remained in China through the end of the war. Philippe Néel died on 8 February 1941 at Saint-Laurent-d’Aigouze. Alexandra learned of the death while stranded in Asia. His death ended a correspondence of exceptional scale and deprived her of both an emotional interlocutor and financial administrator.

She and Yongden left China after the war, traveling through Kunming, India, and other transit points. She returned to France by air in 1946.

A detailed summary of this wartime route, including missionary assistance, is available in [Histoire de Chine’s documentary reconstruction](https://wiki.histoire-chine.fr/index.php/Alexandra_David-N%C3%A9el_et_les_missionnaires_en_Chine).

## Later Life at Samten Dzong, 1946–1969

David-Néel resumed life at Digne and continued writing, editing earlier material, translating, and corresponding. Her advanced age did not end her planning for travel, although further major expeditions did not occur.

A series of secretaries and household assistants worked for her. Relations were sometimes difficult. Accounts portray her as demanding, suspicious of inefficiency, exacting about food and household order, and capable of dismissing assistants abruptly. These descriptions recur in memoirs by people close to the household but should not be converted into psychiatric diagnosis.

### Yongden’s death

Yongden died at Digne in 1955. Some accounts attribute his declining health to heavy smoking and alcohol use, but the exact medical cause remains uncertain. His death was one of the principal crises of David-Néel’s later life.

His ashes remained in the Tibetan shrine at Samten Dzong for eventual joint disposition with hers.

### Marie-Madeleine Peyronnet

In 1959 Marie-Madeleine Peyronnet joined the household as secretary, housekeeper, and caregiver. David-Néel called her “Tortue.” Peyronnet remained through David-Néel’s death and later became guardian and interpreter of the house and archive.

The relationship was close but hierarchical. Peyronnet’s later books are valuable eyewitness testimony, though shaped by affection and by her custodial role.

### Health

David-Néel experienced recurrent illness during her travels, including fevers, digestive trouble, exhaustion, exposure, and age-related frailty. Some popular biographies describe recurrent depression and suicidal thoughts in youth; these derive from diaries and retrospective interpretation, not a modern clinical diagnosis.

She retained considerable mental activity into extreme old age. The famous story that she renewed her passport at age one hundred is substantially supported by institutional commemoration. It functions both as an administrative fact and as a symbol of her lifelong readiness to depart.

### Death and ashes

She died at Samten Dzong on 8 September 1969. Institutional summaries describe natural causes; no more precise publicly documented cause was established here.

She was cremated. In 1973 Marie-Madeleine Peyronnet carried David-Néel’s and Yongden’s ashes to Varanasi and dispersed them in the Ganges.

David-Néel gave or bequeathed Samten Dzong to the municipality of Digne. Her Tibetan library went principally to the Musée Guimet; other philosophical books and objects were divided among French institutions, including the Musée de l’Homme.

Principal locations, not every transit point:

2. **Childhood–young adulthood:** Brussels and Ixelles, Belgium; schooling in Belgium and France.
3. **Late 1880s–1890s:** Paris and London; Theosophical and musical circles.
4. **Early 1890s:** Ceylon and India, including Adyar.
5. **1895–1897:** Hanoi, Haiphong, and other French Indochina theatrical locations.
6. **1897–1900:** Paris; then Athens.
7. **1900–1911:** Tunis, with European travel.
8. **1911–1912:** Ceylon and India.
9. **1912–1916:** Sikkim, Kalimpong, Darjeeling, Himalayan monasteries, Lachen, and unauthorized travel to southern Tibet.
10. **1917:** Southeast Asian ports, Japan, Korea, Beijing.
11. **1918–1921:** Northern China, Xining, Kumbum.
12. **1921–1923:** Gansu, borderlands, Lanzhou, Chengdu, Yunnan, eastern Tibetan regions.
13. **1923–1924:** Overland clandestine journey to Lhasa and exit through southern Tibet and Sikkim.
14. **1925–1928:** France, European lecture circuit, temporary southern residences including the Toulon region.

16. **1937–1946:** Moscow and Trans-Siberian route; Beijing; Wutai Shan; Taiyuan; Hankou; Chongqing; Chengdu; Kangding/Tatsienlu; Kunming; India.
17. **1946–1969:** principally Digne, with limited travel.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Philippe Néel

Husband, correspondent, financial supporter, critic, and administrator. Although geographically separated after 1911, the marriage did not simply end. Their letters reveal continuing intimacy, irritation, dependence, and collaboration. He enabled much of her travel through remittances and management of funds.

### Aphur Yongden

Adopted son and indispensable collaborator. Publicity frequently made him secondary to the celebrated European traveler, but he supplied language, cultural competence, religious legitimacy, navigation, negotiation, and physical assistance. Several books were explicitly co-authored; many others depended on his explanations.

### Sidkeong Tulku Namgyal

Sikkimese crown prince, Buddhist reformer, patron, friend, and possible lover. Correspondence supports an intense intellectual bond; sexual intimacy is unproved.

### Lachen Gomchen Rinpoche

Tantric teacher and mentor during her northern Sikkim retreat. Her descriptions helped make him a central figure in the Western image of the Tibetan hermit.

### Kazi Dawa Samdup

Interpreter and Tibetan teacher. His linguistic labor was crucial to her early access to Tibetan authorities and texts.

### Thirteenth Dalai Lama and Ninth Panchen Lama

Important religious audiences rather than continuing personal friendships. These meetings strengthened her authority in Europe.

### Annie Besant and Theosophical networks

Besant corresponded with and encouraged her during her early Theosophical formation. The relationship supplied access to international religious networks, though David-Néel eventually distanced herself from Theosophical supernaturalism.

### Élisée Reclus

Anarchist geographer, mentor, and supporter of *Pour la vie*. He provided an intellectual model joining geography, travel, social critique, and personal liberty.

### Jean Haustont

Musical and intimate partner in Paris; collaborator on *Lidia*.

### Sylvain Lévi

Major French Indologist who wrote the preface to the David-Néel/Yongden work on Gesar. His support conferred scholarly legitimacy.

### Marie-Madeleine Peyronnet

Final secretary, caregiver, executor of aspects of the memorial legacy, and author of eyewitness memoirs.

## Institutions, Projects, Property, and Financial Interests

### Institutions

David-Néel was associated at different periods with:

- Royal Conservatory circles in Brussels;
- Theosophical Society lodges in London, Paris, and Adyar;
- Musée Guimet;
- French feminist and socialist periodicals;
- Hanoi/Haiphong operatic companies;
- Tunis theatrical and casino management;
- French geographical societies;
- French ministries supporting Oriental research;
- Buddhist monasteries in Sikkim, Tibet, Japan, China, and India;
- French consular networks in wartime China;
- Samten Dzong and its later municipal museum organization.

### Property

The best-documented major asset was Samten Dzong, purchased in 1928 in her own name. She enlarged and adapted it as residence, library, shrine, writing center, garden, and prospective meditation center.

### Income and support

Sources identify:

- Inherited money in youth;
- Earnings as a singer and theatrical director;
- Personal capital at marriage;
- Philippe Néel’s remittances and financial management;
- Government or institutional travel assistance;
- Lecture fees;
- Book royalties and translation income;
- Property ownership.

No documented evidence was found of corporate holdings, patents, speculative business structures, secret trusts, or illicit financial arrangements.

### Archival project

Her papers include notebooks, manuscripts, extensive correspondence with Philippe, photographs, travel documents, and Tibetan materials. Because she destroyed some early material and curated the surviving papers, researchers must distinguish archival survival from a complete documentary record.

## Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

David-Néel’s intellectual trajectory was cumulative rather than a simple conversion:

1. **Catholic childhood:** fascination with saints, ritual, asceticism, and death, accompanied by rejection of dogmatic authority.
2. **Stoicism and freethought:** emphasis on self-command and personal independence.
3. **Anarchism and feminism:** resistance to imposed institutions and gender roles.
4. **Theosophy:** institutional access to Asian religions and an international network, later tempered by skepticism.
5. **Early Buddhism:** preference for what she regarded as the rational, psychological, and non-theistic teaching of the historical Buddha.
6. **Tibetan Buddhism:** direct engagement with monastic learning, ritual, tantra, and oral instruction.
7. **Vedānta and Taoism:** continuing comparative study without exclusive doctrinal confinement.
8. **Late synthesis:** Buddhism presented as method, mental discipline, philosophy, and path of experiential investigation.

She frequently contrasted an “original” or philosophical Buddhism with popular ritual and superstition. Modern scholars regard that distinction as partly characteristic of Buddhist modernism: it selected elements compatible with European rationalism while marginalizing others integral to lived Asian traditions.

At the same time, her practice of retreat, Tibetan study, and dependence on Asian teachers went beyond purely armchair Orientalism.

---

## Political Activity and Views

David-Néel’s early politics were anarchist, individualist, feminist, anticlerical, and sympathetic to workers’ emancipation. She did not maintain an easily classifiable twentieth-century party identity.

Her Asian writings contain criticism of:

- British frontier controls and imperial administration;
- French colonial complacency;
- Tibetan hierarchy, monastic wealth, serf-like obligations, and superstition;
- Chinese militarism and bureaucratic disorder;
- Missionary claims of religious superiority;
- European ignorance about Asian philosophy.

Her judgments could also reproduce racialized, civilizational, or essentializing categories common in European Orientalism. She sometimes generalized about “Chinese,” “Tibetans,” “Orientals,” servants, peasants, and religious populations. These passages are documentary evidence of her historical vocabulary; they should neither be erased nor treated as proof that every part of her work had one uniform political purpose.

She was not documented as an intelligence officer. Her geographic knowledge, official contacts, and reports naturally interested governments in an imperial border environment, but no reliable evidence found here establishes recruitment by French, British, Russian, Chinese, or other intelligence services.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Unresolved Questions

## Did she actually reach Lhasa?

### The accusation

In 1972 Jeanne Denys published *Alexandra David-Néel au Tibet: une supercherie dévoilée*, alleging that David-Néel had not reached Lhasa and had fabricated or borrowed the journey. Jean Marquès-Rivière subsequently repeated or developed the accusation.

Denys had briefly assisted the elderly David-Néel with her library in the late 1950s. Her case emphasized perceived geographic inconsistencies, literary resemblance to earlier travelers, and the implausibility of some descriptions.

### Evidence supporting the journey

- Photographs associated with David-Néel and Yongden in Lhasa, including an image before the Potala;
- Correspondence and itinerary documentation;
- The contemporaneous appearance of the travelers in India after crossing Tibet;
- Detailed route reconstruction by later geographers and biographers;
- Recognition by people with knowledge of the region;
- The failure of Denys to produce documentary evidence placing David-Néel somewhere incompatible with the journey.

### Assessment

The accusation is a **historically significant but unsupported allegation**. The journey is overwhelmingly accepted by modern scholarship and museum institutions. Individual details in David-Néel’s literary account may be dramatized without invalidating the underlying entry into Lhasa.

The charge affected her posthumous reputation by making authenticity itself part of the David-Néel story. It also illustrates how extraordinary female travel writing was subjected to forms of skepticism not always applied equally to male adventurers.

## Use of Yongden’s knowledge

David-Néel’s fame overshadowed Yongden’s contribution. He is explicitly credited on several works but was essential to many more.

This is not evidence of legal plagiarism by itself. It is a **documented asymmetry of authorship and publicity**. The surviving record supports treating their Tibetan work as a collaboration whose division of labor was not always transparently represented to readers.

## Scientific authority and occult claims

In *Mystiques et magiciens du Tibet*, David-Néel reported or discussed:

- *Tummo* heat practices;
- *Lung-gom* runners;
- Telepathy;
- Thought-forms or *tulpas*;
- Oracles, apparitions, and ritual magic.

She sometimes said she personally practiced or observed unusual phenomena while also offering psychological explanations. Critics accused her of presenting marvels without adequate verification.

The correct label varies by claim:

- Her study of the beliefs and practices: **documented fact**.
- The existence of practitioners making such claims: **reported fact**.
- Paranormal efficacy: **unverified claim**.
- Later Internet “tulpa” culture derived from her vocabulary: **posthumous reinterpretation**, often detached from Tibetan doctrine.

## Orientalism and colonial framing

Modern scholarship argues that David-Néel helped create a Western image of Tibet as secret, timeless, spiritually pure, and “forbidden.” Fabienne Le Houérou identifies her as part of the European construction of a “virgin Tibet” paradigm. Samuel Thévoz and other scholars offer more qualified readings: her work participated in Orientalist systems while also criticizing European superiority and entering sustained relationships with Asian teachers.

This is a **documented scholarly controversy**, not an allegation of a crime. Her disguise as a beggar could undermine colonial separation by forcing her to share local conditions, but the resulting text still converted Tibet into a consumable European literary spectacle.

Chinese translations of her work have also been selectively modified. Yunfei Bai’s study argues that some translations reshaped her writing to support Chinese representations of Tibet. This concerns the later political use of her texts rather than misconduct by David-Néel.

## Tibetan politics and later propaganda

Statements attributed to David-Néel about Tibetan social hierarchy, slavery or serfdom, monastic power, and Chinese rule have been used by competing political narratives:

- Pro-Chinese sources cite her criticisms as evidence against idealized pre-1950 Tibet.
- Tibetan-independence advocates emphasize the political selectivity of those quotations and her recognition of distinct Tibetan culture.
- Western spiritual literature often removes both Chinese and Tibetan political conflict from her work.

Her observations are primary testimony filtered through a foreign traveler. They do not by themselves settle the legal or historiographical status of Tibet.

In 2024, the centenary of her Lhasa journey became adjacent to a controversy over French museums’ terminology for Tibet and alleged Chinese pressure. This was a dispute over contemporary museum policy; there was no new allegation of personal misconduct by David-Néel. [Le Monde reported the competing claims and the Guimet administration’s denial of Chinese interference](https://www.lemonde.fr/culture/article/2024/09/26/le-risque-d-effacement-du-tibet-a-l-uvre-dans-les-musees-francais_6334308_3246.html).

## Relations with employees and self-presentation

Memoirs and biographies describe her as imperious and difficult with secretaries and servants. She controlled her papers and cultivated the image of the solitary, indomitable traveler.

These are **reported character assessments**. They are relevant to household history and reputation but do not establish abuse in a legal sense. No lawsuit or criminal proceeding arising from these relationships was found.

## Criminality, lawsuits, and investigations

No evidence was found of:

- Criminal conviction;
- Fraud prosecution;
- Financial-corruption investigation;
- Money laundering;
- Patent litigation;
- Civil lawsuit of major biographical importance;
- Participation in political violence;
- Formal espionage charge.

Her unauthorized border crossings violated British-imposed travel restrictions and Tibetan entry controls. The practical consequence was expulsion from Sikkim in 1916; she was not formally tried or imprisoned.

## Adversarial search findings, including 2024–2026

Dedicated searches combining her name with controversy, scandal, fraud, lawsuit, accusations, conspiracy, racism, colonialism, and recent dates produced four substantive categories:

1. The old Denys allegation that the Lhasa journey was fraudulent;
2. Scholarly criticism of Orientalist representation;
3. Disputes over Chinese political appropriation or translation of her Tibetan writing;
4. The 2024 French museum terminology controversy.

No credible recent evidence of hidden criminal activity, fraud, espionage, financial misconduct, or a newly authenticated scandal was located.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Self-Created Mythology

## “The first Western woman in Lhasa”

**Story:** David-Néel was the first Western woman ever to enter the forbidden city.

**Appearance and circulation:** International press coverage from the 1920s onward; publishers, biographies, museums, children’s books, and documentaries.

**Evidence:** Her 1924 stay is strongly documented. The universal “first” is harder to prove because clandestine and poorly recorded earlier movements cannot be exhaustively excluded.

**Effect:** It became the central shorthand for her life, eclipsing her political writing, music, and scholarship.

## The perfect Tibetan disguise

**Story:** She became visually indistinguishable from a Tibetan beggar and deceived everyone.

**Evidence:** Disguise and concealment are documented. Her success also depended on avoiding scrutiny, Yongden’s presence, festival crowds, linguistic discipline, and the willingness or indifference of people along the route. She was not necessarily believed to be Tibetan by every observer.

**Effect:** Reinforced a transformation myth in which a European woman crossed not only a frontier but an identity boundary.

## Discovery because she washed too often

**Story:** Officials exposed her because her habit of washing made her implausibly clean for a beggar.

**Evidence:** Repeated in popular narratives; the precise causal chain is not firmly documented.

**Label:** **Anecdote or legend based on an uncertain incident.**

**Effect:** Humanized the adventure and gave it a comic climax.

## The “lama degree”

**Story:** The Panchen Lama awarded her the Tibetan equivalent of a doctorate, proving she was formally a Tibetan lama-scholar.

**Evidence:** She was honored and religiously recognized. Direct equivalence to a Western PhD or a standard monastic *geshe* degree is misleading.

**Effect:** Enhanced her authority with European readers.

## The tulpa

**Story:** David-Néel created a thought-form in the shape of a jovial monk; it became autonomous and sinister, and she required months to dissolve it.

**Source:** Her account in *Mystiques et magiciens du Tibet*.

**Evidence:** Her testimony establishes that she told the story, not that an independently existing entity was created.

**Label:** **Self-reported occult experiment; unverified paranormal claim.**

**Effect:** The episode became one of the most influential Western occult stories about Tibet and later inspired New Age, horror, Internet, and “tulpamancy” subcultures.

## The superhuman *lung-gom-pa*

**Story:** She saw Tibetan adepts moving at extraordinary speed in trance and capable of traveling immense distances.

**Evidence:** Tibetan traditions of disciplined running exist, but supernatural claims and the precise observations cannot be independently verified.

**Effect:** Helped establish the image of Tibet as a storehouse of secret psychophysical technologies.

## Sidkeong as lover

**Story:** The crown prince of Sikkim and David-Néel conducted a love affair.

**Evidence:** Intimate correspondence and spiritual-political partnership; no conclusive proof of sexual relations.

**Label:** **Possible but unresolved relationship claim.**

**Effect:** Added romance to biographical treatments and complicated the story of her marriage.

## The entirely solitary explorer

**Story:** A lone European woman walked into Lhasa through willpower alone.

**Evidence against:** Yongden was continuously indispensable; Philippe financed her; teachers, monks, porters, missionaries, diplomats, and local hosts enabled her movement.

**Label:** **Publicity myth built around a documented achievement.**

## The passport at one hundred

**Story:** At one hundred she renewed her passport because she remained ready to leave for Asia.

**Evidence:** Repeated by the Maison Alexandra David-Néel and official commemorative sources and likely based on an actual administrative act.

**Label:** **Documented or strongly reported fact that became symbolic legend.**

Documented or strongly reported distinctions include:

- Gold Medal of the Société de Géographie de Paris;
- Chevalier of the Legion of Honour;
- Later promotion within the Legion of Honour, commonly reported as officer;

## Works by Alexandra David-Néel

No fully exhaustive bibliography of her journalism and short contributions has yet been established. The [AGORHA research project](https://agorha.inha.fr//ark%3A/54721/76318b15-fff2-45b4-8d27-4189cf39a9b8) explicitly notes that the scale of her periodical production makes completeness difficult.

Dates below refer to first publication where reasonably established; later editions often changed subtitles and spelling of “Tibet/Thibet.”

### Early political, philosophical, and historical writings

- **1898 — *Pour la vie*.** Anarchist social and philosophical essay; associated with Élisée Reclus’s support or preface. Genuine authorship.
- **1904 — *Notes historiques sur la Corée*.** Historical article or pamphlet; genuine authorship.
- **1904 — *Le Pouvoir religieux au Thibet*.** Study of Tibetan religious authority; genuine authorship.
- **1906 — *Le Féminisme rationnel*.** Some bibliographies date publication to 1909; likely reflects periodical versus collected publication. Genuine authorship.
- **1907 — *Le Philosophe Meh-ti (ou Mo-tse) et l’idée de solidarité*.** On Mozi and solidarity.
- **1909 — *Les Théories individualistes dans la philosophie chinoise: Yang-Tchou*.** Genuine authorship.
- **1911 — *Le Modernisme bouddhiste et le Bouddhisme du Bouddha*.** Later reworked or republished in forms titled *Le Bouddhisme du Bouddha*.

### Travel, Buddhism, Tibet, and fiction

- **1925 — *Souvenirs d’une Parisienne au Thibet*.** Early publication connected with the Lhasa journey.
- **1927 — *Voyage d’une Parisienne à Lhassa: à pied et en mendiant de la Chine à l’Inde à travers le Thibet*.** Principal Lhasa narrative; genuine authorship, drawing on letters and expedition experience with Yongden.
- **1929 — *Mystiques et magiciens du Tibet*.** Ethnographic, religious, autobiographical, and occult material; genuine authorship.
- **1930 — *Initiations lamaïques: des théories, des pratiques, des hommes*.** Study of Tibetan initiation and teachers.
- **1930 — *Arsenia Trueba*.** Fiction; bibliographic status and exact edition vary.
- **1931 — *La Vie surhumaine de Guésar de Ling, le héros thibétain, racontée par les bardes de son pays*.** Co-authored or produced “with the collaboration of Lama Yongden”; preface by Sylvain Lévi.
- **1933 — *Au pays des brigands-gentilshommes: Grand Tibet*.** Travel narrative.
- **1935 — *Le Lama aux cinq sagesses*.** Novel; collaboration with or dependence on Yongden is sometimes acknowledged.
- **1938 — *Magie d’amour et magie noire: scènes du Tibet inconnu*.** Fiction or fictionalized Tibetan narratives, associated with Yongden.
- **1939 — *Le Bouddhisme: ses doctrines et ses méthodes*.** Synthetic exposition.
- **1940 — *Sous des nuées d’orage: récit de voyage*.** Account shaped by the Sino-Japanese War period.
- **1947 — *À l’ouest barbare de la vaste Chine*.** Travel and frontier account.
- **1949 — *Au cœur des Himalayas: le Népal*.**
- **1951 — *Aṣṭāvakra Gītā: discours sur le Vedānta Advaita*.** French translation or interpretive rendition from Sanskrit.
- **1951 — *Les Enseignements secrets des bouddhistes tibétains: la vue pénétrante*.** Genuine authorship based on Tibetan teachings; English title often *The Secret Oral Teachings in Tibetan Buddhist Sects*.
- **1951 — *L’Inde hier, aujourd’hui, demain*.**
- **1952 — *Textes tibétains inédits*.** Anthology and translation/interpretation, including poems attributed to the Sixth Dalai Lama.
- **1953 — *Le Vieux Tibet face à la Chine nouvelle*.** Political and historical reflections.
- **1958 — *La Connaissance transcendante d’après le texte et les commentaires tibétains*.** Translation and exposition of Prajñāpāramitā material.
- **1961 — *Immortalité et réincarnation: doctrines et pratiques en Chine, au Tibet, dans l’Inde*.**
- **1964 — *Quarante siècles d’expansion chinoise*.** Historical synthesis.
- **1968 — *En Chine: l’amour universel et l’individualisme intégral*.** Late Chinese-philosophy work or compilation.

### Operatic work

- **Circa 1897–1900 — *Lidia*.** One-act lyric drama; libretto by Alexandra David/Myrial, music by Jean Haustont. Genuine collaborative work; performance and publication history is obscure.

### Periodical work

She contributed essays, reviews, political commentary, and religious studies to publications including:

- *Le Lotus bleu*;
- *L’Étoile socialiste*;
- *La Fronde*;
- Geographical and Orientalist journals;
- Newspapers and lecture publications.

A complete title-by-title inventory remains a research desideratum.

### Correspondence, diaries, and posthumous editorial publications

These are genuine source materials but posthumously selected and edited:

- ***Journal de voyage: lettres à son mari, 11 août 1904–27 décembre 1917*.** Edited correspondence.
- ***Correspondance avec son mari*, later volumes covering the Asian years.**
- ***Le Sortilège du mystère*.** Journal or autobiographical material assembled from surviving papers.
- ***La Lampe de sagesse*.** Maxims and reflections selected from notebooks and correspondence.
- ***Le Féminisme rationnel* and *Pour la vie*.** Modern editions restoring early political writings.
- ***Voyages et aventures de l’esprit* (2021).** Posthumous selection of unpublished writings, edited from material found at Samten Dzong.
- ***Grand Tibet et vaste Chine* (1999).** Omnibus volume containing previously published books and editorial biographical material; not a newly authored 1999 work.
- Various collections prepared by Marie-Madeleine Peyronnet, Marc de Smedt, and other editors.

Editorial collections must not be confused with books designed and published by David-Néel herself.

## Death, Estate, and Memorial Institutions

David-Néel died at Digne on 8 September 1969. No evidence of suspicious death was found. Her house and collections were divided according to gifts, bequests, and municipal or museum arrangements.

Samten Dzong became a museum and interpretive center. The institutional form has changed over time, involving an association and the municipality of Digne-les-Bains. Restoration and new museum presentation have emphasized the house as both a historic residence and a constructed space of Buddhist identity.

The Fourteenth Dalai Lama later visited and blessed the site, strengthening its role as a place of transnational Buddhist memory.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

David-Néel’s reputation has passed through several overlapping forms:

### Explorer-hero

The dominant popular image remains the elderly woman disguised as a beggar entering Lhasa. It is historically grounded but compresses a fifty-year intellectual and artistic career into one episode.

### Pioneer of Buddhism in Europe

She helped present Buddhism as philosophy, psychology, disciplined practice, and lived Asian tradition. She influenced French and international readers seeking alternatives to Christianity and institutional religion.

### Popularizer of Tibetan esotericism

Her accounts of *tummo*, hermits, *lung-gom*, telepathy, and thought-forms profoundly shaped Western occult and New Age images of Tibet. Later readers often detached these stories from her skepticism and from Tibetan doctrinal contexts.

### Travel-writing and feminist icon

Her resistance to marriage conventions, solitary travel, late-life independence, and public intellectual authority made her a feminist symbol. That posthumous use can obscure her sometimes elitist attitudes and the labor of Yongden and other assistants.

### Object of postcolonial reassessment

Recent scholarship studies how her work simultaneously:

- challenged European religious superiority;
- depended on imperial transportation and consular networks;
- crossed colonial racial boundaries through disguise and apprenticeship;
- converted Tibet into an exotic literary object;
- preserved observations unavailable elsewhere;
- contributed to myths of an isolated, timeless Tibet.

### Tibetan and Chinese political afterlives

Her writings are quoted by opposing sides in debates about Tibet. Selective translation and quotation have produced David-Néels adapted to incompatible political projects: critic of old Tibet, admirer of Tibetan civilization, witness to Chinese expansion, opponent of British interference, and cosmopolitan Buddhist.

### Influence

Readers and writers influenced by the public example of David-Néel include explorers, travelers, Buddhist converts, feminists, novelists, occultists, and scholars. Direct, documentable personal influence should be distinguished from later comparison. Her cultural importance lies as much in the forms of life she made imaginable as in any single doctrinal school.

| 1914 | Meets Aphur Yongden; undertakes Himalayan retreat and tantric study. |
| December 1914 | Sidkeong Tulku Namgyal dies. |
| 1915–1916 | Extended retreat near Lachen with instruction from Lachen Gomchen. |
| 13–26 July 1916 | Unauthorized Tibet journey; visits Tashilhunpo and meets the Ninth Panchen Lama. |
| Late 1916–1917 | Ordered to leave Sikkim; travels through India and Southeast Asia. |
| 1917 | Travels in Japan and Korea; reaches Beijing in October. |
| 1918 | Crosses northern China; arrives at Kumbum in July. |
| 1918–January 1921 | Lives and studies at Kumbum monastery; works on Tibetan texts with Yongden. |
| 5 January 1921 | Leaves Kumbum. |
| 1921–1923 | Travels through Gansu, western China, and Tibetan borderlands. |
| 18 June 1923 | Reaches Chengdu. |
| 14 July 1923 | Leaves Chengdu toward the southwest and Tibetan route. |
| February 1924 | Enters Lhasa in disguise with Yongden. |
| February–April 1924 | Stays in Lhasa and visits major religious sites. |
| 1924 | Exits through southern Tibet and Sikkim; journey receives international attention. |
| May 1925 | Returns to France after nearly fourteen years; reunites briefly with Philippe. |
| 1927 | Publishes *Voyage d’une Parisienne à Lhassa*. |
| May 1928 | Purchases the Digne property later known as Samten Dzong. |
| 1929 | Legally adopts Aphur Yongden; publishes *Mystiques et magiciens du Tibet*. |
| 1930–1935 | Publishes major books on Tibetan initiation, Gesar, frontier travel, and fiction. |
| Circa 1935 | Obtains a driving license in her sixties. |
| 9 January 1937 | Departs with Yongden for China via Moscow and the Trans-Siberian Railway. |
| 1937 | Lives at Wutai Shan; Sino-Japanese War forces flight south. |
| 20 September 1937 | Leaves Wutai Shan. |
| March 1938 | Reaches Chengdu. |
| July 1938 | Reaches Tatsienlu/Kangding. |
| 1938–1943 | Lives principally at Kangding amid war and displacement. |
| 8 February 1941 | Philippe Néel dies in France. |
| 1943–1945 | Lives principally at Chengdu. |
| 1946 | Returns to France by air after travel through Kunming and India. |
| 1946–1955 | Writes and edits at Samten Dzong with Yongden. |
| 1955 | Yongden dies at Digne. |
| 1959 | Marie-Madeleine Peyronnet joins her household. |
| 1960s | Continues writing, correspondence, and publication into her nineties. |
| 1968 | Celebrates centenary and renews passport. |
| 8 September 1969 | Dies at Samten Dzong, Digne, aged 100. |
| 1972 | Jeanne Denys publishes the unsuccessful “Lhasa fraud” accusation. |
| 1973 | Peyronnet disperses David-Néel’s and Yongden’s ashes in the Ganges at Varanasi. |
| Late twentieth–twenty-first century | Samten Dzong becomes a museum; her work undergoes feminist, Buddhist, postcolonial, and political reinterpretation. |
| 2024 | Centenary of the Lhasa entry; her memory intersects with French museum disputes about the representation and naming of Tibet. |
