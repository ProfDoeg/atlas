# Khwaja Mu'in al-Din Hasan Chishti: Research Dossier

## Scope, Method, and Evidentiary Limits

Khwaja Muʿin al-Din Hasan Chishti lived in the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries, but no securely contemporary biography of him is known. He is not given a substantial notice in the principal surviving chronicles of the Ghurid conquest or early Delhi Sultanate. Most detailed narratives of his childhood, travels, miracles, encounters with rulers, marriages, and teachings were written from the mid-fourteenth century onward.

This dossier therefore uses the following labels:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary or near-contemporary evidence, physical evidence, official records, or multiple critically evaluated sources.
- **Probable reconstruction:** accepted by critical historians but not directly documented during Chishti’s lifetime.
- **Reported fact:** stated by relatively early Chishti tradition but not independently confirmed.
- **Hagiographic tradition:** transmitted to describe sanctity, spiritual authority, or miraculous power; historically important but not literal evidence by itself.
- **Disputed attribution:** assigned to Chishti, but modern scholarship questions authorship or compilation date.
- **Modern allegation:** asserted in political, sectarian, or legal controversy but not established as historical fact.
- **Myth or folklore:** a narrative important to devotion, polemic, or collective memory but unsupported by contemporary evidence.

The fullest critical treatment remains P. M. Currie’s *The Shrine and Cult of Muʿīn al-Dīn Chishtī of Ajmer*. Currie concluded that the surviving material permits only a limited historical biography and that much of the received life was constructed retrospectively. An Indian university history unit similarly describes *Anīs al-arwāḥ* and *Dalīl al-ʿārifīn* as fabricated or apocryphal malfūẓāt composed after Chishti’s death.

## Source History

### Near-contemporary silence

The most important limitation is absence. Minhaj-i Siraj Juzjani’s *Ṭabaqāt-i Nāṣirī*, a major thirteenth-century chronicle, records Muhammad of Ghor’s campaigns, Prithviraj’s defeat, and the early Delhi Sultanate but does not supply the elaborate Chishti narrative found in later sources. This does not prove that Chishti was absent from India; it does mean that claims that he was a prominent military or political participant require evidence outside the contemporary chronicles.

### Principal later sources

1. **Amir Khurd Kirmani, *Siyar al-awliyāʾ***, composed approximately 1351–82. This is the earliest extensive Chishti collective biography and was written more than a century after Chishti’s death. Its value is greatest for institutional memory and the early order’s self-understanding, not for exact eyewitness biography.

2. **Jamali, *Siyar al-ʿārifīn***, early sixteenth century. It expands earlier accounts and embeds Chishti in a developed sacred history.

3. **ʿAbd al-Haqq Dihlawi, *Akhbār al-akhyār***, completed in the late sixteenth or early seventeenth century. Influential but several centuries removed.

4. ***Siyar al-aqṭāb*** and later shrine biographies. These supply many of the narratives now used in devotional and political argument.

5. ***Anīs al-arwāḥ***, attributed to Chishti as a record of his master’s discourses, and ***Dalīl al-ʿārifīn***, represented as Chishti’s discourses collected by Qutb al-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki. Modern historians generally regard both as later pseudepigraphic compositions. An academic overview dates their likely manufacture to approximately 1324–55 and 1355–85 respectively.

6. **Inscriptions, imperial chronicles, farmāns, revenue records, architectural remains, and modern statutes** become much stronger sources for the shrine’s history after the saint’s death.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

Later tradition names Chishti’s father as Khwāja Ghiyath al-Din Hasan and his mother as Umm al-Wara, also called Bibi Mah-i Nur or Mahe Noor.

**Evidentiary status:** hagiographic or genealogical tradition. No contemporary record independently establishes the names.

### Sayyid ancestry

Shrine genealogies describe him as a descendant of Muhammad through Fatima and Ali, commonly joining a Husayni line through his father and a Hasani line through his mother.

**Evidentiary status:** traditional claim, not independently verifiable. Sayyid genealogies were important forms of religious authority in the medieval and early modern Islamic world. The surviving pedigrees are late and sometimes vary in names or generations.

### Siblings and extended family

Some devotional biographies mention relatives in Sistan and identify Khwaja Fakhr al-Din Gardezi as a cousin or close attendant who accompanied Chishti to India. The descendants of Gardezi and associated families became hereditary khādims at Ajmer.

**Evidentiary status:** the later existence of hereditary service families is documented; their precise biological relationship to Chishti is genealogical tradition.

### Marriages

Later accounts assign two wives:

- **Bibi Asmatullah**, commonly described as a daughter of Sayyid Wajih al-Din Mashhadi.
- **Bibi Ummatullah**, sometimes represented as a local ruler’s daughter, a Hindu convert, or a woman taken captive and subsequently married to Chishti.

The order of the marriages, dates, parentage, and allocation of children differ across sources. Some accounts date a marriage around 590 AH/1194; others around 605–06 AH/1209–10 or 615 AH/1218–19.

**Evidentiary status:** reported in late family and shrine tradition. The captive-princess version cannot be confirmed from contemporary evidence and has become a major point of modern polemic.

### Children

Tradition usually gives three sons and one daughter:

- Khwaja Fakhr al-Din Abu’l-Khayr
- Khwaja Ziya al-Din Abu Saʿid
- Khwaja Husam al-Din Abu Salih
- Bibi Hafiza Jamal or Bibi Jamal

Some accounts identify Fakhr al-Din as the principal biological and spiritual continuator at Ajmer. Others claim that Husam al-Din disappeared from ordinary life or entered occultation. Bibi Jamal acquired a local reputation for sanctity.

**Evidentiary status:** family tradition, later genealogies, and continuing descent claims; individual maternity and some biographical details remain disputed.

## Childhood and Early Life

### Birthplace and date

Three geographic formulations recur:

1. **Sistan/Sijistan**, the broad region spanning parts of present-day Iran and Afghanistan.
2. **Sanjar or Sanjār**, sometimes described as a village in Sistan and sometimes confused with Sanjar near Mosul.
3. **Isfahan**, found in some later biographies.

The nisba **Sijzī** supports an origin in or family connection with Sistan more strongly than the precise village traditions. A birth in Chisht itself is unlikely to be the meaning of “Chishti.”

Birth dates range from 530 AH/1135–36 to 537 AH/1142–43. The widely repeated 1 February 1143 is a modern conversion of a traditional Hijri date, not a date recorded by a contemporary witness.

### Orphanhood and inherited property

The standard devotional narrative says his father died while Muʿin al-Din was about fifteen. He inherited an orchard, garden, and grinding mill. A wandering mystic—usually Ibrahim Qanduzi or Qundūzī—visited the orchard. Chishti offered him grapes; the visitor chewed sesame cake or another morsel and placed it in the youth’s mouth. Chishti underwent an immediate spiritual awakening, sold or distributed his property, and left in search of knowledge.

**Evidentiary status:** hagiographic conversion story. Its structure—saintly stranger, blessed food, sudden renunciation—is a recognizable Sufi literary motif. No independent property record survives.

The story nevertheless encodes themes central to later Chishti identity: renunciation of inherited wealth, hospitality, initiation through embodied blessing, and preference for poverty over property.

## Education and Formation

### Samarqand and Bukhara

Later accounts say Chishti studied Qurʾan, hadith, jurisprudence, grammar, and theology at Samarqand and Bukhara, major centers of Sunni scholarship.

Some biographies name teachers such as Husam al-Din Bukhari and Sharaf al-Din or Sharf al-Din. The names and sequences vary.

**Evidentiary status:** probable in general, unverified in detail. A Sistan-born religious seeker of the period could plausibly have studied in these centers, but no contemporary register or certificate survives.

### Uthman Harwani

The most stable element in the received spiritual biography is discipleship to **Khwaja Uthman Harwani**, also rendered Haruni, Harvani, or Hārūnī. Chishti tradition says Muʿin al-Din met him at Harwan or Nishapur, underwent initiation, and accompanied him for approximately twenty years.

The disciple is said to have carried his master’s bedding and water vessel, endured retreats and fasts, and received formal authorization or *khilāfat*.

**Evidentiary status:** probable core relationship preserved by the Chishti silsila, though the detailed itinerary and twenty-year duration come from late sources.

### Spiritual genealogy

The standard Chishti chain runs, with variant links, from Muhammad through Ali, Hasan al-Basri, Ibrahim ibn Adham, Fudayl ibn ʿIyad, Ibrahim ibn Bashshar, Abu Ishaq Shami, Abu Ahmad Abdal Chishti, Abu Muhammad Chishti, Abu Yusuf Chishti, Mawdud Chishti, Sharif Zandani, Uthman Harwani, and Muʿin al-Din.

Some South Asian versions incorporate Abu Bakr or other early figures differently.

**Evidentiary status:** institutional lineage, not a continuous biographical record. Early sections contain chronological and encounter problems typical of retroactively formalized Sufi chains.

### Intellectual influences

Later teaching collections associate him with:

- Qurʾanic piety and prophetic imitation
- ascetic poverty (*faqr*)
- remembrance of God (*dhikr*)
- service to the hungry and distressed
- spiritual audition (*samāʿ*)
- detachment from rulers
- disciplined obedience to a master
- generosity without discrimination
- the Persian Sufi literature of Khurasan

Later writers also connect him with ʿAbd Allah Ansari of Herat, Junayd of Baghdad, Abu Yazid Bistami, Mansur al-Hallaj, ʿAbd al-Qadir al-Jilani, and other earlier mystics. These are principally lineal, literary, or legendary influences rather than securely documented personal relationships.

## Major Journeys and Geographical Movements

The complete traditional itinerary includes Sistan, Samarqand, Bukhara, Nishapur, Harwan, Baghdad, Mecca, Medina, Isfahan, Tabriz, Kharaqan, Balkh, Herat, Ghazni, Lahore, Multan, Delhi, and Ajmer.

Only the broad movement from the Persianate east through Afghanistan into northern India is historically persuasive. Exact dates and the inclusion of particular cities largely derive from hagiography.

### Khurasan and Iran

He is said to have encountered major ascetics and visited shrines throughout Khurasan and Iran. Claims that he met Farid al-Din Attar or Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi are chronologically possible in some reconstructions but lack contemporary corroboration.

### Baghdad

Tradition says he visited Baghdad, studied or conversed with leading Sufis, and in some versions met ʿAbd al-Qadir al-Jilani.

The reported meeting is problematic. ʿAbd al-Qadir died in 1166. It would require an early chronology for Chishti’s travels, and the story appears in late devotional sources rather than contemporary Baghdadi records.

**Evidentiary status:** hagiographic tradition; possible chronologically under some birth dates, unproven historically.

### Mecca and Medina

Chishti is said to have performed the hajj, circumambulated the Kaʿba, and spent time in Medina. In the central mission legend, he heard Muhammad’s voice or saw him in a dream and was ordered to go to Hindustan, where Ajmer was shown to him spiritually.

**Evidentiary status:** pilgrimage is plausible; the prophetic commission is visionary hagiography and the theological charter for the Indian Chishtiyya.

### Ghazni, Lahore, and Delhi

Tradition places him at:

- **Ghazni**, an important route into the subcontinent.
- **Lahore**, where he performed a retreat at the tomb of Ali Hujwiri, author of *Kashf al-maḥjūb*.
- **Delhi**, where Qutb al-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki became his principal successor.
- **Ajmer**, his permanent residence.

The Lahore-to-Delhi-to-Ajmer route is probable, although the precise sequence and dates are not documented.

## Chronological Life History

### c. 1135–43: Birth and early household

Born somewhere in the Sistan cultural region, probably into a Persian-speaking Muslim family possessing at least modest property. The claim of aristocratic Sayyid descent is traditional.

### c. 1150s–60s: Orphanhood and renunciation

Later tradition places the death of both parents in his adolescence, followed by the orchard episode and distribution of his inheritance.

### c. 1160s–80s: Study and initiation

He allegedly studied in Samarqand and Bukhara, entered the discipline of Uthman Harwani, and traveled widely through the central Islamic lands.

No precise chronology can be recovered. The purported encounters with many famous saints may compress decades of Sufi memory into one exemplary journey.

### Late twelfth century: Pilgrimage and eastward movement

The hajj and Medina commission belong here in traditional chronology. Chishti then traveled through Ghazni and Lahore toward Delhi and Ajmer.

### Arrival in India: competing chronologies

Three models exist:

1. **Before 1192:** Later hagiographies place him in Ajmer while Prithviraj III was still ruler. This permits the miracle contests and confrontation narratives.
2. **With or immediately after Muhammad of Ghor’s conquest in 1192:** Some colonial and later writers say he accompanied, followed, or benefited from the Ghurid army.
3. **After 1206:** Critical reconstructions place his arrival or settlement only after Turkish authority and a Muslim population were established at Ajmer.

The third model is adopted by some modern historians because it avoids relying on late miracle narratives and better fits the weak early evidence. Currie notes chronological problems in the traditional account. A conclusive arrival year cannot presently be established.

### Settlement at Ajmer

Ajmer, then a recently conquered Chahamana capital, lay near the major Hindu pilgrimage center of Pushkar. Chishti established a household and teaching circle there.

Later narratives say he first camped beneath a tree associated with royal camels, moved toward Ana Sagar, and ultimately occupied the place where the shrine stands. These stories dramatize displacement of royal and sacerdotal authority by saintly power.

Historically, his choice of Ajmer placed a Khurasanian Sufi community in an important frontier city of the emerging Ghurid-Delhi polity.

### Community and teaching

Chishti appears to have trained disciples rather than to have created a centralized “order” with modern bureaucratic structure. The Chishtiyya developed retrospectively through linked masters, hospices, family networks, oral teaching, and authorization documents.

Probable immediate or early disciples include:

- Qutb al-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki
- Hamid al-Din Sufi Nagauri
- Fakhr al-Din Gardezi
- Muhammad Yadgar Sabzawari
- Chishti’s son Fakhr al-Din

### Relationship to political power

Later Chishti ideology emphasized distance from rulers and dependence upon God rather than state salaries. Chishti himself is not securely documented as holding an office, taking government employment, receiving a military command, or serving at court.

The later cult became deeply connected with rulers, but that cannot simply be projected back onto his lifetime.

### 1220s–30s: Final years

Traditional accounts portray him as continuing teaching, fasting, distributing food, arranging succession, and periodically visiting Delhi.

Qutb al-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki is said to have received Chishti’s cloak, prayer rug, staff, sandals, and formal spiritual authorization. Hamid al-Din Nagauri developed another important center in Rajasthan.

### Death

The received account says that on 6 Rajab Chishti entered his cell, instructed that he not be disturbed, and remained in prayer. Music or a mysterious sound was heard during the night. In the morning he was found dead, and words meaning “This is God’s beloved, who died in God’s love” appeared miraculously on his forehead.

**Evidentiary status:** hagiographic death narrative.

The date is disputed:

- **6 Rajab 627 AH**, approximately 21 May 1230, is given by *Mirʾāt al-asrār* and some later works.
- **6 Rajab 633 AH**, approximately 15–16 March 1236, is the more widespread shrine chronology and appears in *Siyar al-ʿārifīn*, *Siyar al-aqṭāb*, and later compilations.

A 1949 official inquiry into shrine affairs reportedly acknowledged that the exact date was not ascertainable. The annual *ʿurs* is observed in early Rajab, culminating on the sixth.

Cause of death is not medically documented. There is no reliable evidence of murder, accident, chronic illness, addiction, or psychiatric crisis.

## Religious and Intellectual Development

### Poverty and non-accumulation

The Chishti ideal was not simply personal destitution. It involved refusal to store wealth, prompt distribution of gifts, feeding visitors, and reliance on voluntary support. Later sayings attributed to Chishti compare generosity to a river, affection to the sun, and hospitality to the earth.

Because the principal collections are pseudepigraphic or late, such sayings should be understood as formulations of mature Chishti ethics rather than verbatim transcripts.

### Service to the poor

The title Gharīb Nawāz crystallized the saint’s image as protector of the poor. The shrine’s enormous cooking vessels, charitable food distribution, and offerings transformed this ethical theme into an institution.

### Samāʿ and music

The Chishtiyya became known for controlled spiritual audition—poetry and music intended to intensify remembrance and produce ecstasy. Later tradition presents Chishti as an advocate of *samāʿ* subject to spiritual discipline.

Modern qawwali cannot be projected unchanged into his lifetime. Its repertory and performance institutions developed over subsequent centuries, especially around Delhi and Ajmer.

### Approach to non-Muslims

Devotional literature portrays Chishti as serving anyone in need, and the later shrine attracted Muslims, Hindus, Sikhs, and others. Other hagiographic passages present him as a missionary confronting “unbelievers,” defeating Hindu ritual specialists, and predicting Muslim conquest.

These strands coexist. Universal hospitality and triumphalist sacred history are both present in the textual tradition; neither should be erased. There is no contemporary statistical evidence showing how many people he personally converted or by what methods.

### Relation to rulers

The remembered maxim of avoiding sultans became central to later Chishti self-definition. His successors, however, maintained varied relations with political elites. Even where a shaykh declined office, rulers and nobles might visit, donate, seek blessings, or cultivate association with his lineage.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Uthman Harwani

**Nature:** spiritual master.

**Status:** probable historical core; detailed travels and miracles are hagiographic.

### Qutb al-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki

**Nature:** principal disciple and successor in Delhi.

Kaki transmitted the Chishti lineage to the Delhi region. He died in 1235, traditionally after entering ecstasy during *samāʿ*. If Chishti died in 1236, he survived Kaki briefly; the 1230 death chronology reverses that order.

**Status:** documented institutional relationship in early Chishti memory, though exact episodes are late.

### Hamid al-Din Sufi Nagauri

**Nature:** disciple associated with Nagaur.

He became known for agrarian simplicity, vegetarian restraint in some accounts, and resistance to elite wealth.

**Status:** probable historical relationship.

### Fakhr al-Din Gardezi

**Nature:** cousin, attendant, or early follower according to khādim genealogies.

His descendants or claimed descendants became a major hereditary service community at the shrine.

**Status:** reported fact; exact kinship is genealogical tradition.

### Iltutmish and early Delhi rulers

Some later stories connect Chishti or Kaki with Sultan Iltutmish. Kaki’s relationship to Delhi’s political society is better attested than any direct Chishti–Iltutmish relationship.

**Status:** Chishti’s direct political association remains unverified.

### Prithviraj III

**Nature:** protagonist in later conflict legends.

No contemporary evidence establishes a meeting. Their coexistence in Ajmer depends on accepting a pre-1192 arrival, itself disputed.

**Status:** hagiographic tradition and modern political controversy.

### Muhammad of Ghor

**Nature:** alleged ally, fellow traveler, beneficiary of Chishti’s prayer, or commander summoned by him, depending on the source.

No reliable contemporary evidence establishes a personal meeting or correspondence.

**Status:** disputed late claim.

## Institutions, Property, and Financial Interests

### During Chishti’s life

No reliable account survives of a formal corporation, commercial enterprise, investment, patent, salaried appointment, landed estate, treasury, or government grant controlled by Chishti.

His inherited orchard and mill belong to hagiography. His Ajmer household may have depended on gifts, family resources, and community support, but accounts cannot be reconstructed.

### The posthumous endowment

The shrine eventually accumulated:

- buildings and courtyards;
- shops, houses, and other real property;
- villages and revenue assignments;
- offerings or *nazr*;
- imperial and princely gifts;
- cooking vessels and charitable funds;
- offices claimed by the sajjāda-nishīn, dīwān, khādims, and government-appointed administrators.

These assets belong to the posthumous institution, not to Chishti’s personal wealth.

### Modern statutory administration

India’s **Durgah Khawaja Saheb Act, 1955**, in force from 1 March 1956, vested administration of the endowment in a statutory Dargah Committee. The Act defines the endowment to include the shrine complex, movable property, land, houses, shops, the jagir villages of Hokran and Kishanpur, income dedicated for religious or charitable purposes, and authorized offerings.

The committee is a corporate body capable of suing and being sued. The central government appoints its members and a *nazim*. The Act separately recognizes the sajjāda-nishīn and regulates offerings, audits, reports, and administration.

This formal arrangement reflects centuries of jurisdictional conflict among shrine families, hereditary officers, state authorities, and donors.

## The Shrine and Posthumous Cult

### Initial grave

The earliest grave was probably modest. The monumental complex is a layered product of later patronage rather than a building completed by Chishti.

### Sultanate development

By the fourteenth century, Chishti’s tomb had become a recognized pilgrimage center. Muhammad bin Tughluq and later sultans are associated with visits or patronage. Sultan Mahmud Khalji of Malwa constructed or enlarged the Buland Darwaza in the fifteenth century.

### Mughal patronage

Mughal engagement transformed Ajmer into an imperial shrine.

- Humayun is traditionally credited with early construction or rebuilding.
- Akbar undertook repeated pilgrimages. A modern study reconstructs **seventeen journeys between 1562 and 1579**, sometimes connected with vows, military victories, imperial mobility, and the production of kingship.
- Akbar’s association with Chishti saints became linked to his desire for an heir. Prince Salim—later Jahangir—was named after Shaykh Salim Chishti of Fatehpur Sikri, a later member of the same broad order, not after Muʿin al-Din.
- Akbar built a mosque and donated one of the great cauldrons.
- Jahangir continued patronage and used Ajmer as an imperial residence.
- Shah Jahan added marble architecture and a congregational mosque.
- Princess Jahanara Begum sponsored architectural and literary expressions of Chishti devotion.
- Aurangzeb visited after defeating Dara Shikoh in 1659.

Imperial devotion also served political purposes. Ajmer was a military and administrative base, and patronage created relationships with regional elites, religious specialists, and pilgrimage networks.

### Rajput and Maratha patronage

As Mughal power receded in the eighteenth century, Rajput rulers and Maratha authorities continued to support the shrine. Scholarship shows that patronage of Ajmer and nearby Pushkar could function as a proxy for political competition among Jaipur, Marwar, and Maratha powers.

### British period

The colonial state inherited the problem of managing a wealthy, internally divided religious endowment. Litigation and administrative inquiries concerned hereditary rights, offerings, custodianship, and public order.

Queen Mary’s 1911 visit was commemorated through the Victoria Tank, illustrating that even a Christian imperial government used patronage of the shrine in public ceremonial politics.

### Independent India

The 1955 Act established the modern statutory structure. The shrine remains a major center of pilgrimage, charity, qawwali, vows, political symbolism, and ceremonial offerings from public figures.

## Political Activity and Political Appropriation

### Chishti’s own political activity

No secure evidence shows that Chishti:

- held office under Prithviraj or the Ghurids;
- commanded troops;
- collected taxes;
- negotiated a treaty;
- wrote to Muhammad of Ghor;
- personally administered conquered territory;
- participated in a documented battle.

Later accounts give him a sacral role in conquest, but these should not be converted into documented military biography.

### Political uses of the shrine

Successive powers—Delhi sultans, Mughals, Rajputs, Marathas, the British, and governments of independent India—have treated Ajmer as a site of public legitimacy.

Prime ministers and other officials regularly send a ceremonial *chādar* for the *ʿurs*. The practice is an act of state symbolism and outreach, not evidence about Chishti’s political position in the thirteenth century.

## Controversies, Allegations, Violence, and Financial Questions

### Alleged participation in the Ghurid conquest

**What is alleged:** Chishti accompanied Muhammad of Ghor, invited him to invade, prayed for Prithviraj’s defeat, fought in the army, or personally delivered Prithviraj to the “army of Islam.”

**Source history:** versions occur in late Chishti hagiographies, particularly works such as *Siyar al-aqṭāb*, and were repeated in colonial-era and modern polemical literature.

**Evidence for:** later texts explicitly connect saintly power with the Muslim victory and sometimes use conquest language.

**Evidence against:** contemporary conquest chronicles do not document Chishti as a military participant; critical scholarship questions whether he was in Ajmer before 1192 and places his settlement as late as the early thirteenth century.

**Finding:** disputed late claim, not documented fact. The narrative is important evidence for how later Chishtis sacralized the political transformation of Ajmer.

### Cow slaughter and desecration at Ana Sagar

**What the story says:** after Hindu ritual specialists or royal camel-keepers opposed his settlement, Chishti’s followers slaughtered cows, cooked meat near a temple or sacred lake, and challenged local religious authority.

**Source history:** late hagiographic material, subsequently circulated in anti-Chishti social-media campaigns.

**Evidence:** no contemporary archaeological, legal, or narrative source establishes the episode. Late texts may preserve memories of religious conflict but cannot establish the literal events unaided.

**Finding:** hagiographic-conflict narrative, now employed as a modern political allegation.

### Capture or marriage of a Hindu princess

**What is alleged:** followers captured a Hindu ruler’s daughter, converted her, and presented her to Chishti as Bibi Ummatullah.

**Source history:** late family biographies differ. Some call her a ruler’s daughter or war captive; other genealogies provide a Muslim family background or treat the marriage without captivity.

**Evidence:** no contemporary source, identifiable ruler, dated marriage record, or independent genealogy confirms the capture.

**Finding:** disputed family legend. It should not be stated as established abduction or sexual enslavement.

### Destruction or usurpation of a Hindu temple

Two distinct questions are often conflated:

1. whether Chishti destroyed temples during his lifetime;
2. whether his tomb or later dargah was constructed over a Shiva temple.

No contemporary source securely documents Chishti demolishing a temple. The present shrine was constructed in phases centuries after his death.

In September 2024, Hindu Sena leader Vishnu Gupta filed a civil suit seeking recognition of the shrine as a Shiva temple and an archaeological survey. The petition reportedly relied heavily on Har Bilas Sarda’s 1911 *Ajmer: Historical and Descriptive* and arguments about reused material, local tradition, and alleged subterranean remains. In November 2024 an Ajmer court issued notices to the Dargah Committee, Archaeological Survey of India, and Ministry of Minority Affairs. Multiple groups subsequently sought to join the proceedings.

**Evidentiary status as of the consulted reporting:** unresolved civil allegation. Issuance of notice or admission of a pleading is not a factual finding that a temple existed or was destroyed.

The Dargah’s representatives deny the claim, stating that the burial ground was open land. Critical discussion notes that Sarda recorded traditions rather than presenting a modern archaeological excavation. No final court judgment or completed archaeological survey establishing the petition’s claim was located in the sources consulted.

### Sectarian Muslim criticism

Salafi and other reformist critics condemn:

- supplication at the tomb;
- vows and petitions addressed through the saint;
- prostration or kissing;
- tying threads;
- music and qawwali;
- mixing of men and women;
- hereditary custodianship;
- state sponsorship.

They classify some practices as religious innovation (*bidʿa*) or associationism (*shirk*). Chishti-oriented Sunnis distinguish worship of God from reverence, intercession, and visitation of a saint.

**Finding:** documented theological dispute about the posthumous cult. It does not establish which practices Chishti personally instituted.

### 1992 Ajmer sexual-exploitation scandal

A large blackmail and sexual-abuse case emerged in Ajmer in 1992. Several accused belonged to influential khādim families associated with the shrine and had political connections, including the local Youth Congress.

Victim totals vary sharply in journalism and public retelling—from approximately one hundred to several hundred—and inflammatory accounts sometimes claim more than one thousand. The scheme involved coercion, sexual assault, photography, and blackmail of girls and young women. Criminal proceedings and convictions followed over many years.

**Necessary distinction:** the offenses occurred more than seven centuries after Chishti’s death. They implicate individual perpetrators and networks, some connected to hereditary shrine-service families; they are not acts of Chishti and do not establish institutional authorship by the dargah as a religious entity.

The scandal has nevertheless affected the shrine’s reputation and has been used in communal propaganda. The 2023 feature film *Ajmer 92* generated objections that it might generalize criminal culpability to Chishti or Muslims.

### 2007 Ajmer Dargah bombing

On 11 October 2007, during Ramadan iftar, a bomb exploded in the shrine courtyard, killing three people and injuring others. Investigators attributed the attack to Hindu-extremist conspirators. A special National Investigation Agency court convicted two defendants in 2017 and imposed life sentences; other defendants were acquitted or remained disputed.

**Finding:** documented terrorist attack against pilgrims at Chishti’s shrine, unrelated to conduct by the medieval saint.

### Modern threats and misconduct by individual khādims

Individual hereditary attendants have periodically faced criminal accusations or public criticism. A prominent example was Salman Chishti’s 2022 video offering a reward for violence against politician Nupur Sharma, followed by his arrest.

**Necessary distinction:** hereditary or claimed devotional association is not evidence that Chishti authorized such conduct.

### Administration, offerings, and custodial conflict

The shrine’s high volume of offerings has repeatedly produced disputes concerning:

- who may solicit and retain offerings;
- the division between statutory administration and hereditary khādims;
- succession to the sajjāda-nishīn office;
- audit authority;
- property leasing;
- maintenance and structural safety;
- political representation by competing dargah bodies.

The 1955 Act’s detailed treatment of offerings, audits, endowment property, succession, and arbitration demonstrates that such conflicts were institutional rather than imaginary. Particular allegations of corruption require case-specific proof; no basis exists for attributing modern financial misconduct to Chishti personally.

### Waqf controversy, 2025

In 2025, Ajmer khādim organizations divided over India’s Waqf Amendment legislation. Some prominent Chishti representatives supported aspects of the bill; the principal khādim body condemned that position and accused supporters of aligning with organizations hostile to Muslim interests.

**Finding:** documented contemporary institutional and political dispute, not a biographical controversy involving the medieval saint.

## Myths, Legends, Folklore, and Disputed Narratives

## The orchard awakening

**Story:** Ibrahim Qanduzi transmits spiritual grace through food; Chishti abandons his orchard and mill.

**Earliest setting:** later Chishti biography, not a contemporary document.

**Function:** converts economic inheritance into spiritual inheritance and makes hospitality the beginning of sainthood.

### The Medina commission

**Story:** Muhammad orders Chishti in a dream or audition to travel to India and shows him Ajmer.

**Circulation:** shrine biographies, *tazkirahs*, sermons, and pilgrimage literature.

**Evidence:** visionary claims are not historically testable. The late textual record cannot date the vision.

**Effect:** presents the Indian mission as prophetic authorization rather than migration following conquest.

### The royal camels

**Story:** Chishti camps where Prithviraj’s camels rest. Camel-keepers evict him; he declares that the camels may have the ground. The animals become unable to stand until forgiveness is sought.

**Evidence:** late hagiography.

**Function:** saintly speech immobilizes royal military wealth and symbolically subordinates temporal power.

### Ana Sagar in a water-skin or cup

**Story:** Hindu priests prevent Chishti’s followers from drawing water. A disciple places a vessel in Ana Sagar and the entire lake enters it, leaving the city dry. Chishti restores the water after submission or intercession.

**Evidence:** miracle legend; no contemporary environmental record.

**Function:** demonstrates control over nature and the saint’s power to withhold or restore common resources.

### Ajaypal Jogi and the flying sandals

**Story:** Ajaypal, a powerful yogi or magician, attacks Chishti with serpents, fire, flying objects, or occult forces. Chishti’s sandals fly through the air and strike him until he submits. In some versions Ajaypal converts and becomes Abd Allah Bayabani.

**Source development:** elaborated in late hagiographies and visual folklore.

**Effect:** stages competition between Sufi and yogic charisma while allowing the defeated rival to become a disciple rather than simply disappear.

### Prithviraj’s defeat foretold

**Story:** after the ruler rejects Chishti, the saint says he has delivered Prithviraj alive to the army of Islam.

**Evidence:** late retrospective prophecy; no contemporary attestation.

**Effect:** ties Chishti’s sanctity to one of North India’s foundational conquest narratives and fuels present-day accusations that he was a military jihadist.

### Roshni or forehead inscription at death

**Story:** after his death, luminous writing appears on his forehead declaring him God’s beloved.

**Evidence:** hagiographic death sign.

**Effect:** certifies sainthood without the need for an earthly judge.

### Six-day bridal *ʿurs*

The death anniversary is an *ʿurs*, literally a wedding, symbolizing union with God. Ceremonies include flag raising, shrine opening, qawwali, food distribution, sandal rites, and the culminating *qul*.

This ritual interpretation is a developed Sufi theology of death. It is not evidence of the precise circumstances of Chishti’s death.

### “4606 miracles”

Some popular biographies assign thousands of miracles to Chishti. Such numeric precision has no critical historical basis and functions rhetorically to signify inexhaustible sanctity.

## Works by or Attributed to Chishti

No surviving work can presently be identified as an autograph manuscript or securely contemporary composition by Chishti.

### *Anīs al-arwāḥ* — انیس الأرواح

Purported record by Chishti of the sayings of Uthman Harwani.

- **Traditional attribution:** Chishti as compiler.
- **Modern assessment:** probably a fourteenth-century pseudepigraphon, perhaps composed between approximately 1324 and 1355.
- **Historical value:** evidence for later Chishti memory, doctrine, and attempts to formalize early lineage.
- **Manuscripts and editions:** Persian manuscripts survive; later lithographed and printed versions appeared in South Asia.

### *Dalīl al-ʿārifīn* — دلیل العارفین

Purported discourses of Chishti collected by Qutb al-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki.

- **Traditional attribution:** Chishti as speaker, Kaki as recorder.
- **Modern assessment:** probably fabricated or compiled between approximately 1355 and 1385.
- **Historical value:** mature Chishti doctrine projected backward onto the founder.
- **Archival presence:** a Persian manuscript is catalogued by the Library of Congress.

### *Dīwān-i Muʿīn* or collected Persian poetry

Poems, including ghazals and devotional verses, circulate under Chishti’s name.

- **Assessment:** disputed attribution. No secure early manuscript corpus establishes an authorial divan.
- The famous Persian quatrain beginning **“Shāh ast Husayn, bādshāh ast Husayn”** is frequently attributed to Chishti, especially in South Asian Muharram culture, but attribution varies and lacks decisive early manuscript proof.

### Letters, prayers, and counsels

Later collections publish *maktūbāt*, prayers, aphorisms, and counsels under his name.

- **Assessment:** unverified unless an early manuscript provenance can be shown.
- Frequently quoted sayings about the river’s generosity, the sun’s affection, and the earth’s hospitality should be cited as “attributed to Chishti,” not as verbatim documentary quotations.

### *Ganj al-asrār*, *Risāla-yi wujūdiyya*, and other titles

Bibliographies sometimes assign mystical treatises bearing these or similar names to Chishti.

- **Assessment:** disputed or insufficiently studied attribution.
- Catalogue attribution alone proves that a manuscript circulated under his name, not that he composed it.

## Films, Documentaries, Music, and Archival Material

### Films and dramatic representations

- **Mere Gharib Nawaz** (1973), Hindi devotional film directed by G. Ishwar. Uses the Ajmer cult as a framework for faith, petition, and divine assistance.
- **Sultan-e-Hind** and similarly titled television or devotional productions. Versions and dates require title-by-title verification.
- **Ajmer 92** (2023), Hindi crime drama concerning the 1992 sexual-exploitation scandal. It is not a biography of Chishti, but controversy surrounding the film directly affected discourse about the shrine.
- Documentaries and broadcast features produced around the annual *ʿurs*, qawwali, Mughal patronage, and interreligious pilgrimage.

### Recorded devotional repertory

Thousands of qawwalis, manqabats, and film songs invoke Gharīb Nawāz. Major themes include arrival at Ajmer, charity, intercession, pilgrimage, and the saint’s spiritual sovereignty over India.

Notable performers associated with Ajmer or Chishti repertory include hereditary Ajmeri qawwals, the Sabri Brothers, Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan, Aziz Mian, and numerous Indian and Pakistani ensembles. Performance of a text does not establish that Chishti authored it.

### Visual archives

- Mughal *Akbarnāma* paintings depict Akbar’s pilgrimage to Chishti’s grave.
- British Library, Victoria and Albert Museum, IGNCA, and South Asian manuscript collections preserve manuscripts, paintings, photographs, gazetteers, and shrine plans.
- The British Museum catalogue records visual material relating to the saint and shrine.
- The Library of Congress holds a Persian manuscript catalogued as *Dalīl al-ʿārifīn*.
- Parliamentary and governmental archives contain the 1949 inquiry material, debates preceding the 1955 Act, annual reports, rules, and notifications.

## Death, Burial, and Succession

Chishti died at Ajmer and was buried within or beside his residence. The lack of a contemporary inscription means that the exact original form of the grave cannot be recovered.

His spiritual succession was not identical to hereditary control of the grave:

- **Qutb al-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki** carried the principal initiatic line to Delhi.
- **Hamid al-Din Nagauri** represented an early Rajasthan branch.
- **Fakhr al-Din**, described as Chishti’s son, continued a family line.
- **Fakhr al-Din Gardezi’s descendants or claimed descendants** became associated with hereditary shrine service.
- The **sajjāda-nishīn/dīwān** came to claim genealogical and ritual authority.
- The **khādims** claimed service rights and shares of offerings.
- The modern **Dargah Committee** holds statutory administrative authority.

Disputes among these roles should not be flattened into a single “descendant who owns the shrine” model.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Formation of the Indian Chishtiyya

The durable initiatic sequence is:

Muʿin al-Din Chishti  
→ Qutb al-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki  
→ Farid al-Din Masʿud Ganj-i Shakar  
→ Nizam al-Din Awliya  
→ later Nizami branches

Other branches developed through Sabir Kaliyari and regional successors.

Chishti was not the creator of Sufism in India, nor necessarily the first Chishti to enter the subcontinent. His posthumous cult nevertheless made him the foundational saint of the most influential South Asian Chishti genealogy.

### Ajmer as a transregional pilgrimage center

Ajmer became a meeting place for:

- ordinary petitioners;
- merchants and travelers;
- Sufi disciples;
- musicians;
- rulers and diplomats;
- Muslims of multiple legal and theological orientations;
- Hindu, Sikh, and other non-Muslim pilgrims;
- state officials seeking public legitimacy.

This cross-community use is documented as a feature of the shrine’s later history. It should not be retroactively treated as proof of every modern claim about Chishti’s own language or social program.

### Political symbolism

For Mughal rulers, Ajmer connected sacred authority with imperial mobility and western military frontiers. In modern India it functions variously as:

- a symbol of composite culture;
- a Sunni Sufi center;
- a target of reformist Muslim criticism;
- a target of Hindu-nationalist historical claims;
- a setting for official secular or interfaith ceremony;
- a contested endowment with substantial material interests.

### Historical reinterpretation

Four broad images now coexist:

1. **Devotional:** prophetic envoy, miracle-worker, Gharīb Nawāz, and spiritual ruler of India.
2. **Academic-critical:** historically elusive Khurasanian migrant whose biography was substantially constructed by later Chishti institutions.
3. **Pluralist-national:** emblem of interreligious hospitality and composite Indian culture.
4. **Hostile-polemical:** religious agent of Ghurid conquest, temple destruction, coercive conversion, or foreign domination.

The first and fourth rely heavily on the same late hagiographies but interpret them oppositely. Critical history neither automatically accepts devotional miracles nor converts triumphalist sacred narratives into contemporary military reportage.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are listed. Shared religion, geography, or later ceremonial association alone has not been counted.

### Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani

- **Nature:** alleged personal meeting in Baghdad and later spiritual influence.
- **Date/place:** Baghdad, presumably before al-Jilani’s death in 1166.
- **Label:** **rumor or hagiographic tradition**.
- **Assessment:** chronologically possible only under some birth and travel reconstructions; no contemporary source establishes the meeting.

### Abu Yazid Tayfur ibn Isa al-Bistami

- **Nature:** intellectual and initiatic predecessor in the wider Khurasanian Sufi tradition.
- **Label:** **reported doctrinal influence**, not personal contact.
- Chishti lived centuries later; no meeting was possible.

### Abu al-Qasim al-Junayd ibn Muhammad al-Baghdadi

- **Nature:** doctrinal predecessor whose sober Sufism entered later Chishti teaching and genealogies.
- **Label:** **documented influence through tradition**, not a personal relationship.
- No direct meeting was possible.

### Abu Bakr as-Siddiq

- **Nature:** included in some variants of broad Sufi spiritual ancestry.
- **Label:** **institutional lineage claim**, not biological or personal connection.
- The principal Chishti chain more commonly runs through Ali.

### Ibrahim ibn Adham

- **Nature:** early ascetic predecessor appearing in Chishti spiritual genealogies.
- **Label:** **traditional initiatic connection**, not personal contact.

### Farid al-Din Attar

- **Nature:** some later biographies claim a meeting in Nishapur or general influence from the Khurasanian Sufi milieu.
- **Date/place:** purportedly Nishapur, late twelfth or early thirteenth century.
- **Label:** **unverified tradition**.
- The chronology permits a meeting, but no strong contemporary evidence confirms one.

### Ibn Arabi

- **Nature:** claimed encounter in some expansive travel narratives and later shared place in the Persianate Sufi canon.
- **Label:** **unverified tradition**.
- No adequately early source consulted establishes a personal meeting.

### Nizam ad-Din Auliya

- **Nature:** spiritual descendant through Qutb al-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki and Farid al-Din Ganj-i Shakar.
- **Dates/places:** Chishti died by 1236; Nizam al-Din was born about 1238, so no meeting occurred.
- **Label:** **documented institutional lineage**, indirect influence.
- Nizam al-Din’s Delhi community was decisive in preserving and expanding Chishti memory.

### Amir Khusrow Dehlavi

- **Nature:** disciple of Nizam al-Din Awliya and therefore a later member of Chishti devotional culture.
- **Dates/places:** born in 1253, after Chishti’s death.
- **Label:** **documented indirect institutional connection**.
- Khusrow’s poetry and later musical reputation profoundly affected Chishti *samāʿ* and the cultural reach of the order, but he never met Chishti.

### Muhammad ibn Abdullah

- **Nature:** ultimate prophetic authority of the lineage; Chishti is said to have received his Indian commission through a dream or spiritual audition at Medina.
- **Label:** the historical religious allegiance is **documented**; the direct commission is **visionary hagiography**.

### Ali ibn Abi Talib

Ali is not separately named in the supplied roster, though he is essential to Chishti genealogical and initiatic claims. The biological Sayyid descent and unbroken spiritual chain are traditional, not independently verifiable.

### Other roster findings

No documented personal crossing was found with Jalal al-Din Rumi, Shams-i Tabrizi, Baha al-Din Walad, Mansa Musa, Ibn Battuta, Nizami Ganjavi, or the other atlas figures. Ibn Battuta visited South Asia after Chishti’s death and belongs to a later historical setting. Mere membership in Islamic, Sufi, Persianate, or South Asian history has not been counted as a connection.
