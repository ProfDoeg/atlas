## Works by Diego María de la Concepción Juan Nepomuceno Estanislao de la Rivera y Barrientos Acosta y Rodríguez

### Murals and architectural painting cycles

- **1922–1923:** *La creación (Creation)*. Rivera’s first completed Mexican mural: an encaustic-and-gold architectural painting in the Bolívar Amphitheater of the Escuela Nacional Preparatoria, executed with assistants and therefore both an individual obra and a collective production. ([Colegio de San Ildefonso](https://sanildefonso.org.mx/espiritudel22/rivera.html); [MoMA chronology](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/mobile/chronology.html))
- **1923–1928:** *Visión política del pueblo mexicano (Political Vision of the Mexican People)*, also called the *Secretaría de Educación Pública mural cycle*. This is an architectural narrative comprising 235 fresco panels across the Courtyard of Labor and Courtyard of the Fiestas; individual scenes have titles, but the made thing is also the building-encompassing cycle. ([MoMA chronology](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/mobile/chronology.html); [SEP building account](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretariat_of_Public_Education_Main_Headquarters))
- **1924–1927:** *Murals in the administrative building of the Escuela Nacional de Agricultura, Chapingo*. A fresco cycle devoted to agricultural labor and social revolution; it forms one half of Rivera’s larger Chapingo project and resists clean separation from the chapel cycle. ([MoMA chronology](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/mobile/chronology.html))
- **1924–1927:** *La tierra fecundada (The Fertile Earth)*, also called the *Chapingo Chapel cycle*. A chapel-wide fresco Gesamtkunstwerk—walls, vault, entrance, and architectural symbolism—whose component scenes collectively turn a former religious interior into a secular revolutionary sanctuary. Sources differ between 1923–27 and 1924–27 for its dating. ([MoMA chronology](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/mobile/chronology.html); [El País](https://elpais.com/mexico/2024-12-18/la-capilla-laica-de-diego-rivera-recupera-los-colores.html))
- **1927:** *Mural decoration for the Ministry of Health and Welfare, Mexico City*. An architectural cycle centered on health, fertility, and bodily life; it is sometimes catalogued panel by panel and sometimes as a single commission. ([MoMA, *Diego Rivera*, chronology](https://assets.moma.org/documents/moma_catalogue_1979_300369443.pdf))
- **1929–1930:** *Historia del estado de Morelos: conquista y revolución (History of the State of Morelos: Conquest and Revolution)*. A fresco cycle in the Palacio de Cortés, Cuernavaca, organized as a sequential history of conquest, exploitation, and agrarian revolution. ([MoMA chronology](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/mobile/chronology.html))
- **1929–1935:** *Historia de México: de la conquista al futuro (History of Mexico: From the Conquest to the Future)*. The principal stairwell cycle in Mexico’s Palacio Nacional, comprising interdependent walls commonly distinguished as Mexico’s Indigenous past, the history from conquest to 1930, and the world of today and tomorrow. ([Mexican Ministry of Finance](https://www.hacienda.gob.mx/cultura/Documents/Pol%C3%ADpticos_Palacio_Nacional.pdf); [Smarthistory](https://smarthistory.org/artist/diego-rivera/))
- **1930:** *Allegory of California*. A fresco for the Pacific Stock Exchange’s Luncheon Club in San Francisco; an architectural allegory whose central personification was modeled by Helen Wills Moody. ([MoMA chronology](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/mobile/chronology.html))
- **1931:** *The Making of a Fresco Showing the Building of a City*. A fresco at the California School of Fine Arts, now the San Francisco Art Institute, that makes its own construction process part of its subject and architectural form. ([MoMA chronology](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/mobile/chronology.html); [MoMA artist record](https://www.moma.org/artists/4942-diego-rivera))
- **1932–1933:** *Detroit Industry*. A room-encompassing cycle of twenty-seven fresco panels in the Detroit Institute of Arts; it is simultaneously industrial panorama, architectural environment, and collective portrait of modern production. ([MoMA chronology](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/mobile/chronology.html))
- **1933:** *Man at the Crossroads Looking with Hope and High Vision to the Choosing of a New and Better Future*. An unfinished Rockefeller Center fresco project terminated after Rivera refused to remove Lenin’s portrait; the wall painting was subsequently destroyed, so the obra survives through studies, photographs, documentation, and its later reworking rather than as an intact mural. ([MoMA artist record](https://www.moma.org/artists/4942-diego-rivera); [MoMA chronology](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/mobile/chronology.html))
- **1933:** *Portrait of America*. Twenty-one portable fresco panels made for the New Workers School in New York; the series is a political-historical cycle rather than a conventional easel-picture suite, and it was later dispersed. ([MoMA chronology](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/mobile/chronology.html))
- **1934:** *Man, Controller of the Universe*, also called *Man in the Time Machine*. A revised reconstruction in the Palacio de Bellas Artes of the destroyed Rockefeller Center composition, not an exact copy and therefore a separate obra. ([MoMA chronology](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/mobile/chronology.html))
- **1935:** *The History of Mexico: The Agustin de Iturbide Palace mural*. A mural commission in Mexico City recorded in the general mural chronology; its identity is sometimes subsumed under Rivera’s broader historical cycles rather than treated as a freestanding canonical title. ([MoMA, *Diego Rivera*, chronology](https://assets.moma.org/documents/moma_catalogue_1979_300369443.pdf))
- **1936:** *Dictatorship* and *The Dance of the Huichilobos*, also known collectively as the *Hotel Reforma murals*. Rivera made these movable panels for the hotel’s bar; their political caricatures led to censorship and delayed installation, making the commission both mural and contested public manifestation. ([MoMA chronology](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/mobile/chronology.html))
- **1939:** *Portrait of the Bourgeoisie*. Rivera participated in the Sindicato Mexicano de Electricistas mural project, but the surviving cycle is principally attributed to David Alfaro Siqueiros and his collective; Rivera’s precise material contribution is disputed, so it should not be treated unqualifiedly as a Rivera obra. ([MoMA, *Diego Rivera*, chronology](https://assets.moma.org/documents/moma_catalogue_1979_300369443.pdf))
- **1940:** *Pan American Unity*, formally *The Marriage of the Artistic Expression of the North and of the South on This Continent*. A ten-panel portable fresco painted publicly for the Golden Gate International Exposition; it is a continental historical-technological synthesis and was conceived for later architectural installation. ([SFMOMA](https://www.sfmoma.org/publication/diego-riveras-america/); [MoMA chronology](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/mobile/chronology.html))
- **1942–1943:** *Murals for the Hotel del Prado dining room*. A decorative architectural commission distinct from Rivera’s later lobby mural at the same hotel; accounts often subordinate or omit it in favor of the more famous *Alameda* work. ([MoMA, *Diego Rivera*, chronology](https://assets.moma.org/documents/moma_catalogue_1979_300369443.pdf))
- **1943–1944:** *History of Cardiology*. A two-part fresco cycle in Mexico’s Instituto Nacional de Cardiología, presenting ancient and modern histories of medical knowledge. ([MoMA chronology](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/mobile/chronology.html))
- **1945–1951:** *México prehispánico y colonial (Pre-Hispanic and Colonial Mexico)*. A series of first-floor corridor frescoes in the Palacio Nacional, including views of Indigenous cities, cultures, markets, conquest, and colonial rule; it extends, but is materially distinct from, the 1929–35 stairwell history. ([Mexican Ministry of Finance](https://www.hacienda.gob.mx/cultura/Documents/Pol%C3%ADpticos_Palacio_Nacional.pdf); [MoMA chronology](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/mobile/chronology.html))
- **1947–1948:** *Sueño de una tarde dominical en la Alameda Central (Dream of a Sunday Afternoon in Alameda Central Park)*. A portable fresco originally made for the Hotel del Prado; after earthquake damage to the hotel it was transferred into a purpose-built museum, so its status moved from architectural mural to independently housed monument. ([Smarthistory](https://smarthistory.org/artist/diego-rivera/); [MoMA chronology](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/mobile/chronology.html))
- **1951:** *El agua, origen de la vida (Water, Origin of Life)*. An underwater and architectural painting-and-sculpture environment in the Cárcamo de Dolores hydraulic complex, designed to be traversed by the water it allegorizes; it is better understood as a Gesamtkunstwerk than as a wall picture. ([MoMA chronology](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/mobile/chronology.html))
- **1951:** *The People’s Demand for Better Health*. A mural for Hospital La Raza in Mexico City, integrating medical care, social history, and labor into an institutional public image. Some chronologies give the larger hospital commission as 1953. ([MoMA chronology](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/mobile/chronology.html); [MoMA, *Diego Rivera*, chronology](https://assets.moma.org/documents/moma_catalogue_1979_300369443.pdf))
- **1952:** *Pesadilla de guerra y sueño de paz (Nightmare of War and Dream of Peace)*. A large movable mural commissioned for a European touring exhibition but withheld by Mexican authorities because of its political content; its subsequent whereabouts are unknown, and reported destruction or survival has not been conclusively settled. ([MoMA, *Diego Rivera*, chronology](https://assets.moma.org/documents/moma_catalogue_1979_300369443.pdf))
- **1952–1953:** *El pueblo en demanda de salud (The People’s Demand for Health)*. A glass-mosaic mural for Hospital de La Raza; because sources apply closely related English titles and dates to the hospital commission, its relationship to the preceding entry must be treated cautiously rather than silently collapsed. ([MoMA chronology](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/mobile/chronology.html))
- **1952–1956:** *La Universidad, la familia mexicana, la paz y la juventud deportista (The University, the Mexican Family, Peace, and Athletic Youth)*. A polychrome stone-relief “sculpture-painting” on the Estadio Olímpico Universitario; only part of Rivera’s much larger encircling design was realized, so the project is an unfinished Gesamtkunstwerk rather than a completed mural. ([El País](https://elpais.com/mexico/2026-10-09/la-unam-y-el-world-monument-fund-preparan-una-restauracion-de-murales-en-ciudad-universitaria-es-un-sueno-compartido.html))
- **1953:** *Teatro de los Insurgentes façade mosaic*. A glass-mosaic architectural image commonly known through its central figure of Cantinflas and its theatrical history of Mexico; it is a public façade rather than an easel painting. ([MoMA, *Diego Rivera*, chronology](https://assets.moma.org/documents/moma_catalogue_1979_300369443.pdf))
- **1953:** *La historia del teatro en México (History of Theatre in Mexico)*. The Teatro de los Insurgentes mosaic’s descriptive title; catalogues do not always agree whether this is its formal title or a designation for the same façade work, so it should not automatically be counted as a second object. ([MoMA, *Diego Rivera*, chronology](https://assets.moma.org/documents/moma_catalogue_1979_300369443.pdf))
- **1954:** *Gloriosa victoria (Glorious Victory)*. A large portable political painting about the 1954 Guatemalan coup; although mural-like in scale and rhetoric, it is a transportable tempera work and therefore resists a strict mural/easel division. ([Pushkin State Museum exhibition record](https://pushkinmuseum.art/events/archive/2024/exhibitions/gloriosa_victoria/index.php))
- **1956:** *Unity of the Working Class*. A mosaic relief for the Social Security complex in Mexico City, conceived as architecture-bound public art. ([MoMA, *Diego Rivera*, chronology](https://assets.moma.org/documents/moma_catalogue_1979_300369443.pdf))
- **1956–1957:** *Historia de México* for the Museo Nacional de Historia, Chapultepec. Rivera’s final historical mural project, left incomplete at his death; the project’s unfinished state is part of the obra and should not be normalized into a fictive completion date. ([MoMA, *Diego Rivera*, chronology](https://assets.moma.org/documents/moma_catalogue_1979_300369443.pdf))

### Portable frescoes

- **1931:** *Agrarian Leader Zapata*. A transportable fresco made for Rivera’s Museum of Modern Art exhibition, translating a figure from the Cuernavaca cycle into a deliberately autonomous mural panel. ([MoMA exhibition checklist](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/content/Final_press_checklist.pdf))
- **1931:** *Indian Warrior*. A portable fresco panel made for the MoMA exhibition, representing conquest through a compressed monumental fragment. ([MoMA exhibition checklist](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/content/Final_press_checklist.pdf))
- **1931:** *Sugar Cane*. A portable fresco developed from the Cuernavaca cycle’s plantation imagery rather than physically removed from that building. ([MoMA exhibition checklist](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/content/Final_press_checklist.pdf))
- **1931:** *Liberation of the Peon*. A portable fresco made for MoMA whose staged revolutionary scene operates as a self-contained political image. ([MoMA exhibition checklist](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/content/Final_press_checklist.pdf))
- **1931:** *The Uprising*. A portable fresco portraying an urban confrontation between workers and armed authority. ([MoMA exhibition checklist](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/content/Final_press_checklist.pdf))
- **1931:** *The Proletarian Revolution*. A portable fresco panel made for the MoMA installation; its montage-like revolutionary imagery makes it closer to a pictorial manifesto than a descriptive scene. ([MoMA exhibition checklist](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/content/Final_press_checklist.pdf))
- **1931:** *Frozen Assets*. A portable fresco conceived specifically in and about Depression-era New York, combining skyline, municipal shelter, and bank vault in a vertical social anatomy. ([MoMA exhibition checklist](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/content/Final_press_checklist.pdf))
- **1931–1932:** *Electric Power*. A portable fresco panel presenting the machinery and labor behind metropolitan energy. ([MoMA exhibition checklist](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/content/Final_press_checklist.pdf))
- **1931–1932:** *Pneumatic Drilling*. A portable fresco centered on mechanized construction labor; it belongs to the New York group added after the MoMA exhibition opened. ([MoMA exhibition checklist](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/content/Final_press_checklist.pdf))

### Easel paintings, drawings, watercolors, and prints

- **1889–1957:** *Obra de caballete y dibujos (easel paintings and drawings)*. This is a corpus of thousands of separately made objects—not one work—and it resists honest compression into a short selection: the standard *Catálogo general de obra de caballete* records works from 1889 through 1957, including titled, untitled, lost, privately held, and attribution-sensitive objects. Without reproducing that 339-page catalogue entry by entry, no internet-derived list can truthfully claim item-level completeness. ([WorldCat description](https://search.worldcat.org/title/Diego-Rivera-catalogo-general-de-obra-de-caballete/oclc/24065696); [Google Books bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Diego_Rivera.html?id=1wI3AQAAIAAJ); [Centro de Investigación y Documentación Alberto Beltrán](https://cid-albertobeltran.cultura.gob.mx/catalogo-biblioteca/diego-rivera-catalogo-general-de-obra-de-caballete/))
- **1913:** *Adoration of the Virgin and Child*. A Cubist oil painting from Rivera’s European period. ([MoMA artist catalogue](https://www.moma.org/artists/4942-diego-rivera))
- **1914:** *The Eiffel Tower*. A Cubist painting whose title identifies its Parisian motif without making it documentary topography. ([MoMA, *Diego Rivera*, catalogue](https://assets.moma.org/documents/moma_catalogue_1979_300369443.pdf))
- **1914:** *Sailor at Lunch*. A Cubist still-life portrait also known in French and Spanish title variants; translations should not be counted as separate works. ([MoMA, *Diego Rivera*, catalogue](https://assets.moma.org/documents/moma_catalogue_1979_300369443.pdf))
- **1915:** *Zapatista Landscape—The Guerrilla*. A Cubist landscape/still life that functions as an allegorical portrait of the Mexican Revolution. ([Museo Nacional de Arte collection](https://munal.mx/es/obra/paisaje-zapatista))
- **1916:** *Still Life with Gray Bowl*. A Cubist still life; English, Spanish, and French catalogue titles refer to the same object rather than distinct obras. ([MoMA, *Diego Rivera*, catalogue](https://assets.moma.org/documents/moma_catalogue_1979_300369443.pdf))
- **1918:** *The Mathematician*. A portrait marking Rivera’s turn away from high Cubism toward a more classical figurative construction. ([MoMA, *Diego Rivera*, catalogue](https://assets.moma.org/documents/moma_catalogue_1979_300369443.pdf))
- **1931:** *The Rivals*. A large oil painting of a Mexican festival made for Abby Aldrich Rockefeller; it is an easel picture despite its mural-like density and scale. ([Christie’s](https://www.christies.com/en/lot/lot-6143507))
- **1935:** *The Flower Carrier*. A tempera-and-oil painting on Masonite, not a mural, that monumentalizes an episode of physical labor. ([SFMOMA collection](https://www.sfmoma.org/artwork/35.8/))
- **1936:** *Weaving*. An easel painting of the Nahua model and weaver Luz Jiménez at a backstrap loom. ([Art Institute of Chicago](https://www.artic.edu/artworks/151363/weaving))
- **1942:** *Vendedora de alcatraces (Calla Lily Vendor)*. An easel painting whose compressed figures and flowers adopt the monumentality of Rivera’s fresco language. ([Smarthistory](https://smarthistory.org/diego-rivera-calla-lily-vendor/))
- **1944:** *The Flower Seller*. An easel painting; it is one of several distinct flower-vendor compositions and should not be collapsed with similarly titled works from other years. ([National Gallery of Art collection search](https://www.nga.gov/collection/artist-info.2726.html))
- **1948:** *Portrait of Natasha Gelman*. A society portrait whose authorship is secure but whose place beside Rivera’s public revolutionary imagery makes it resist a single political characterization of his oeuvre. ([Museo Frida Kahlo bibliography](https://www.museofridakahlo.org.mx/wp/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/Bibliografia-Diego-Rivera-ingles.pdf))
- **1956:** *Portrait of Dolores Olmedo*. A late portrait painting associated with Rivera’s patron and collector. ([Museo Dolores Olmedo collection](https://www.museodoloresolmedo.org.mx/))
- **undated and variously dated:** *Untitled portraits, landscapes, still lifes, studies, mural cartoons, sketchbooks, lithographs, and watercolors*. These are individual obras, not ancillary “illustrations” by default, but many have no stable public title, date, present location, or uncontested attribution; the catalogue’s uncertainty must be retained rather than repaired with auction-house titles. ([*Catálogo general de obra de caballete* bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Diego_Rivera.html?id=1wI3AQAAIAAJ))

### Stage work and performance design

- **1927–1932:** *H.P. (Horse Power)*. Rivera designed sets and costumes for Carlos Chávez’s “Pan-American ballet”; twenty-four surviving watercolor-and-pencil designs show that his obra was the visual and spatial project for a performance, not the musical composition or ballet as a whole. ([MoMA Magazine](https://www.moma.org/magazine/articles/1416); [MoMA chronology](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2011/rivera/mobile/chronology.html))
- **1932:** *Costume and scenery designs for H.P.*. The twenty-four individual drawings constitute surviving objects as well as components of the unrealized or altered stage environment; treating them solely as preparatory material understates their independent status. ([MoMA Magazine](https://www.moma.org/magazine/articles/1416))

### Architecture, collecting, and Gesamtkunstwerk projects

- **1941–1957:** *Museo Anahuacalli*. Rivera conceived, financed, and—with architect Juan O’Gorman and others—developed a volcanic-stone building to house his collection of pre-Columbian objects; unfinished at his death and completed posthumously, it is a collaborative architectural and museological project rather than a building authored by Rivera alone. ([Museo Anahuacalli](https://museoanahuacalli.org.mx/en/history/))
- **1941–1957:** *Anahuacalli pre-Hispanic collection and display project*. Rivera assembled a large collection and designed its museological manifestation; because many objects were made by ancient artists and merely collected by him, the collection itself is not his artwork, while its selection, arrangement, and institutional project are things he made. ([Museo Anahuacalli](https://museoanahuacalli.org.mx/en/history/))
- **1940s–1957:** *Ciudad de las Artes*. Rivera’s projected arts complex around Anahuacalli remained incomplete; as an intention embodied in plans, construction, and institutional activity, it is properly a project even where no finished obra exists. ([Museo Anahuacalli](https://museoanahuacalli.org.mx/en/history/))

### Book and publication images

- **1930:** *Illustrations for The Underdogs / Los de abajo*. Rivera supplied images for Mariano Azuela’s novel; the novel is Azuela’s text, while Rivera made a distinct illustrative suite embedded in particular editions. ([Enciclopedia de la Literatura en México](https://www.elem.mx/autor/obra/directa/108476//genero/))
- **1932:** *Illustrations for The Discovery and Conquest of Mexico*. Rivera’s images accompany Bernal Díaz del Castillo’s historical narrative and must not be mistaken for authorship of that text. ([MoMA, *Diego Rivera*, catalogue](https://assets.moma.org/documents/moma_catalogue_1979_300369443.pdf))
- **1933:** *Portrait of America*. The book issued with Bertram D. Wolfe converts Rivera’s mural imagery into a printed political-historical sequence; authorship is collaborative, and the publication is distinct from the twenty-one fresco panels bearing the same collective title. ([Internet Archive bibliographic record](https://archive.org/details/portraitofameric00rive))
- **1936:** *Popol Vuh illustrations*. A projected series of watercolors interpreting the K’iche’ Maya narrative; publication plans changed and the cycle’s exact completeness is uncertain, so it is better described as an illustration project than as a finished book by Rivera. ([Diego Rivera Foundation catalogue](https://www.diego-rivera-foundation.org/the-complete-works.html?page=1&q=&sort=popularity))
- **various dates:** *Bookplates, covers, posters, broadsides, and periodical illustrations*. These constitute a dispersed graphic corpus; no complete item-level list is supplied by the general literary bibliography, and titles attached to the publications should not automatically be assigned to Rivera’s images. ([Enciclopedia de la Literatura en México](https://www.elem.mx/autor/obra/directa/108476//genero/))

### Manifestos and collective declarations

- **1922–1923:** *Manifesto of the Union of Technical Workers, Painters and Sculptors*. A collective political-artistic text associated with Rivera, David Alfaro Siqueiros, Xavier Guerrero, and their union; editions differ over its date and signatory presentation, so it is not safely classed as Rivera’s sole-authored writing. As a manifesto, it seeks to organize artistic action rather than merely describe it. ([Getty Research Institute, Mexican muralist documents](https://www.getty.edu/research/exhibitions_events/exhibitions/mexican_muralists/))
- **1938:** *Manifesto for an Independent Revolutionary Art*. A manifesto published under the names André Breton and Diego Rivera but substantially composed with Leon Trotsky; Trotsky withheld his signature for political reasons, making the printed authorship deliberately tactical and the underlying authorship disputed. ([Marxists Internet Archive bibliographic introduction](https://www.marxists.org/subject/art/lit_crit/works/rivera/manifesto.htm))
- **1938:** *Declaration of the International Federation of Independent Revolutionary Art (FIARI)*. A collective organizational text connected to the federation created around the manifesto; Rivera’s participation does not make every FIARI declaration a securely sole-authored Rivera work. ([Getty Research Institute, Mexican muralist documents](https://www.getty.edu/research/exhibitions_events/exhibitions/mexican_muralists/))

### Essays, polemics, memoirs, and attributed speech-texts

- **1920s–1957:** *Articles, lectures, interviews, letters, political statements, and art criticism*. This is a heterogeneous textual corpus collected in modern editions of Rivera’s writings; many pieces began as speeches, interviews, collaborations, or ephemeral publications and resist being treated as stable, sole-authored “books.” ([University of California finding aid, “Writings by Diego Rivera”](https://cdn.calisphere.org/data/13030/m6/tf838nb2m6/files/tf838nb2m6.pdf); [*Diego Rivera: Illustrious Words* publisher catalogue](https://sep.turbifycdn.com/ty/cdn/artbook/DAPFall08.pdf))
- **1924:** *The Revolutionary Spirit in Modern Art*. An essay-manifestation of Rivera’s account of revolutionary art, first appearing in English translation in *The Arts*; its title and wording vary in later collections. ([University of California finding aid](https://cdn.calisphere.org/data/13030/m6/tf838nb2m6/files/tf838nb2m6.pdf))
- **1929:** *The Revolution in Painting*. A polemical essay connecting pictorial form with revolutionary politics; later reprinting does not create a new work. ([University of California finding aid](https://cdn.calisphere.org/data/13030/m6/tf838nb2m6/files/tf838nb2m6.pdf))
- **1932:** *The Revolution in Mexico*. A political text associated with Rivera’s United States lectures and publications; bibliographies vary in treating lecture, article, and pamphlet manifestations as one text or several documents. ([University of California finding aid](https://cdn.calisphere.org/data/13030/m6/tf838nb2m6/files/tf838nb2m6.pdf))
- **1933:** *What Is Art For?*. A polemical essay or lecture-text arguing for art’s social function; it is an intervention rather than a neutral account of aesthetics. ([University of California finding aid](https://cdn.calisphere.org/data/13030/m6/tf838nb2m6/files/tf838nb2m6.pdf))
- **1933:** *I Paint What I See*. Rivera’s public defense concerning the Rockefeller Center controversy, manifested through statements and later printed forms; variant publication titles make it difficult to delimit as one fixed text. ([University of California finding aid](https://cdn.calisphere.org/data/13030/m6/tf838nb2m6/files/tf838nb2m6.pdf))
- **1935:** *The Revolutionary Movement in Mexico*. A political essay whose publication history belongs to Rivera’s shifting relationship with Communist organizations; it should not be confused with similarly titled speeches or later editorial compilations. ([University of California finding aid](https://cdn.calisphere.org/data/13030/m6/tf838nb2m6/files/tf838nb2m6.pdf))
- **1939:** *The Fourth International and the Mexican Revolution*. A political polemic attributed to Rivera within the Trotskyist press; its periodical manifestation, rather than any later collected edition, is the work’s originating form. ([University of California finding aid](https://cdn.calisphere.org/data/13030/m6/tf838nb2m6/files/tf838nb2m6.pdf))
- **1956:** *Apunte de Diego Rivera*. A short published text recorded by Mexico’s national literary encyclopedia; “apunte” describes a note or sketch rather than a book-length work. ([Enciclopedia de la Literatura en México](https://www.elem.mx/autor/obra/directa/108476//genero/))
- **1960:** *My Art, My Life: An Autobiography*. A posthumously published autobiography assembled with journalist Gladys March from interviews and Rivera’s storytelling; it is a collaborative, editorially mediated memoir whose factual reliability has repeatedly been questioned, not an independently written manuscript completed by Rivera. ([Museo Frida Kahlo bibliography](https://www.museofridakahlo.org.mx/wp/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/Bibliografia-Diego-Rivera-ingles.pdf); [publisher description of collected writings](https://sep.turbifycdn.com/ty/cdn/artbook/DAPFall08.pdf))
- **posthumous collected form:** *Diego Rivera: Illustrious Words, 1886–1921* and *Diego Rivera: Illustrious Words, 1921–1957*. These volumes are editorial collections of earlier writings, interviews, letters, memoir material, and unpublished documents; the collections themselves were made after Rivera’s death and must not be mistaken for two books authored and arranged by him. ([publisher catalogue](https://sep.turbifycdn.com/ty/cdn/artbook/DAPFall08.pdf))
