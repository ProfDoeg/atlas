# Arshag Shorjian: Research Dossier

## Scope, Identity Control, and Evidentiary Limits

This dossier concerns **Arshag Shorjian**, an Armenian survivor born in Hadjin in 1898 who settled in Argentina and was interviewed in Buenos Aires in 2000. He must not be confused with **Archag/Arshag Tchobanian (Արշակ Չօպանեան, 1872–1954)**, the Constantinople-born Armenian writer, translator, editor, and political advocate.

The reconstructable record is unusually narrow. Extensive exact-name and variant-name searches in English, Spanish, French, Armenian, and transliterated Armenian located only one substantive person-specific account: David Zenian’s interview and community history, “The Armenians of Argentina,” published in the November 2000 issue of *AGBU Magazine*. AGBU and UGAB France now host duplicate versions of the same article; they are not independent sources.

Independent archival and academic sources confirm the historical environments through which Shorjian said he passed—the 1909 Adana violence, deportations from the Adana region, and Armenian orphanages around Jounieh—but they do not independently identify him. Accordingly:

- Statements directly attributed to Shorjian are labeled **first-person reported testimony**.
- Statements supplied by Zenian’s narrative are labeled **reported fact**.
- Historical contextual material not individually tied to Shorjian is labeled **documented context**.
- Unsupported deductions are avoided or explicitly marked as **inference**.
- Birth, immigration, residence, naturalization, employment, and death certificates were not found in accessible indexed sources.
- No passenger manifest, orphan register, census return, electoral record, trade directory, civil-status document, probate file, or cemetery record identifying him was located.
- His exact death date and final disposition remain undetermined.

## Family and Ancestry

Very little identifying family information survives in the accessible record.

### Parents

Shorjian moved from Hadjin to Adana “with his parents” in 1903. Their names, occupations, ages, religious affiliation, later whereabouts, and deaths are not given.

The wording establishes that both parents—or at least a parental household described collectively—were involved in the move. It does not establish that both were alive during the 1909 violence or the later deportation.

**Status:** Reported fact for the 1903 family migration; all later details unresolved.

### Grandfather and uncle killed in 1909

Shorjian told Zenian that attackers entered the family home during the 1909 violence and killed his grandfather and an uncle. He said that he survived by hiding beneath a pile of firewood.

His formulation was:

> “They came into our house and killed my grandfather and uncle. I was very small and was able to hide under a pile of firewood.”

The source does not identify:

- whether the grandfather was maternal or paternal;
- whether the uncle was a paternal uncle, maternal uncle, or an adult addressed by a kinship term;
- the victims’ names;
- the date within the April 1909 violence;
- the house’s address;
- whether Shorjian personally witnessed the killings or saw their immediate aftermath;
- who else was present.

**Status:** First-person reported testimony, not independently corroborated at the individual level.

### Surviving uncle in Argentina

Zenian reports that Shorjian migrated to Argentina in 1926 “to join his uncle.” The source does not say whether this was the brother of one of his parents, a more distant relative called “uncle,” or a relative by marriage. This plainly cannot have been the uncle said to have been killed in 1909.

No name, address, occupation, date of migration, household record, or later history was located for this Argentine uncle.

**Status:** Reported fact.

### Siblings and other relatives

No siblings are named. Shorjian referred to “the few of us who survived,” but the antecedent is imprecise: it could mean survivors of his household, immediate family, neighborhood group, or the local violence more generally.

No documentary bridge has been established between Arshag Shorjian and later persons carrying the surname in Lebanon, France, Armenia, or Argentina. Shared surname alone is insufficient evidence of kinship.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1898–1909

### Birth in Hadjin

Shorjian was born in **Hadjin** in 1898. Hadjin was a heavily Armenian town in the mountainous northern part of the Ottoman Adana Vilayet. It is now Saimbeyli in Turkey.

Contemporary and later historical sources document a dense Armenian institutional life in Hadjin, including churches, schools, Protestant missions, and orphan-relief work. The United Orphanage and Mission began operating there in 1898, the year of Shorjian’s birth. No evidence places Shorjian or his family inside that institution.

**Status:** His birth is reported fact; institutional description is documented context.

### Move to Adana in 1903

Zenian states that Shorjian moved with his parents to “neighboring Adana” in **1903**, when he would have been approximately five.

No reason is supplied. Possible economic, family, or security motives cannot be responsibly assigned.

No record was found for:

- his parents’ work;
- their property or tenancy in either city;
- the route taken;
- his schooling before or after the move;
- the family’s church or denomination.

**Status:** Reported fact.

## The Adana Violence of 1909

### Shorjian’s account

In April 1909, extensive anti-Armenian violence occurred in Adana and elsewhere in Cilicia. Shorjian said attackers entered his family home, killed his grandfather and uncle, and failed to find him because he hid under firewood. He also recalled protection from Turkish neighbors:

> “The few of us who survived were protected by some of our Turkish neighbors.”

This is significant because it records two simultaneous levels of experience:

1. lethal violence directed at members of his Armenian household; and
2. intervention or concealment by local Turkish neighbors.

The neighbors are unnamed, and the duration and form of their protection are unspecified.

### Documentary context

Contemporaneous correspondence, missionary reports, Ottoman materials, and later scholarship document mass violence against Armenians in Adana in April 1909. The Amherst College archive describes contemporary reports collected by the missionary William Nesbitt Chambers. Clark University’s Guerguerian Archive includes Ottoman documents and interrogations concerning the Adana events, including lists and material relating to Armenians in Adana and Hadjin.

These collections establish that Shorjian’s broad setting is historically plausible. They do not, on the evidence located, name him or the relatives he described.

### Age discrepancy

If born in 1898, Shorjian was approximately ten or eleven during the April 1909 violence. Zenian nevertheless quotes him as saying, “I was very small.”

This is not necessarily contradictory: “very small” may refer to physical size or may be a translated recollection of childhood rather than a claim to infancy. It should not be converted into a different birth year without further evidence.

**Status:** The general violence is documented fact. His household story and rescue are first-person reported testimony.

## Deportation, Mersin, and Removal to Lebanon

### The date problem: “1914” versus the standard 1915 chronology

Shorjian told Zenian that conditions deteriorated with “the start of the second wave of massacres in 1914.” Zenian then describes:

- forced deportation;
- a long march to Mersin;
- passage on a French boat;
- arrival in Beirut;
- placement in an orphanage at Jounieh.

The reference to **1914** requires caution. The principal Ottoman deportation program against Armenians is conventionally dated from spring 1915, after the Ottoman Empire entered the First World War in late 1914. Several possibilities remain:

- Shorjian remembered late-1914 deterioration followed by later deportation;
- Zenian compressed a multiyear sequence;
- “1914” was an interview, transcription, or editorial error;
- Shorjian’s group experienced a local removal not precisely aligned with the best-known national chronology.

No transport document or dated orphan record was found to resolve this.

**Status:** Deportation sequence is first-person/reported testimony; exact year unresolved.

### Route to Mersin

The reported route ran from the Adana area to **Mersin**, an Ottoman Mediterranean port. Zenian calls it a “long march.” The article does not identify:

- departure point;
- date or duration;
- guards or authorities;
- members of Shorjian’s convoy;
- deaths or injuries en route;
- whether surviving parents or relatives accompanied him;
- whether the journey was directly from Adana to Mersin.

Clark University’s Guerguerian Archive catalogs Ottoman material concerning the dates and identities of people deported from Adana and Hadjin. The publicly searchable description does not identify Shorjian.

### French ship to Beirut

Zenian says Shorjian traveled from Mersin “on a French boat” to Beirut. Neither ship nor operator is named.

The phrase may signify:

- a vessel flying the French flag;
- a French naval or relief vessel;
- a ship operating under French occupation or mandate authority;
- a vessel colloquially remembered as French.

No passenger list was located.

### Status as an orphan

Shorjian was placed in an orphanage, but the record does not explicitly say when or how his parents died or disappeared. Placement in an orphanage strongly suggests loss, separation, destitution, or inability of surviving relatives to care for him; it does not by itself prove that both parents were dead.

**Status:** Orphanage placement is reported fact; circumstances of orphanhood unresolved.

## Jounieh Orphanage and Formation

### Five years in Jounieh

Shorjian reportedly spent **five years** in an orphanage in Jounieh, on the Lebanese coast. He learned:

- Arabic;
- shoemaking.

The vocational training became the basis of his lifelong occupation.

If his Jounieh stay began around 1920, five years would fit his migration to Argentina in 1926. If it began as early as 1914 or 1915, the chronology leaves an unexplained interval before 1926. The article does not give admission or discharge years.

### Which orphanage?

Sources document at least two Armenian orphan operations associated with Jounieh:

- **Jounieh/Vorpakhnam**, founded in 1920 and operating until 1928, which housed children transferred from Ayntab and admitted both sexes;
- a **Near East Relief-supported Jounieh orphanage**, described as housing hundreds of girls and closing in 1925.

Because Shorjian was male, the mixed-sex Armenian-run institution is the more plausible candidate. This remains an inference. Zenian did not name the institution, and no located register identified Shorjian.

A Haigazian University repository document separately confirms that an Armenian-managed orphanage operated at Djounieh and that Near East Relief also supported an orphanage there. Academic work on Armenian orphan care records that Ayntab orphans were transferred to Jounieh in July 1920 and that the institution provided educational and artisanal instruction. Again, no evidence establishes that Shorjian arrived with the Ayntab cohort.

### Teachers and mentors

No teacher, director, missionary, craftsman, or administrator associated personally with Shorjian has been identified. The unnamed instructor or workshop that taught him shoemaking was nevertheless his clearest documented vocational influence.

**Status:** Residence, Arabic acquisition, and shoemaking instruction are reported facts. Institutional identification is unresolved.

## Migration to Argentina, 1926

Shorjian arrived in Argentina in **1926** to join an uncle. His port of entry is not explicitly stated, although Buenos Aires is the probable arrival point given the migration pattern and his later residence.

No accessible passenger manifest was found under:

- Arshag or Arshak Shorjian;
- plausible French spellings;
- plausible Spanish spellings;
- likely Armenian transliterations.

This negative search does not disprove the arrival. Armenian immigrants from the Ottoman Empire were often recorded under variant personal names, misspelled surnames, or nationality categories such as “Turkish,” “Syrian,” or “Ottoman.”

### Immigration context

Zenian placed Shorjian within an estimated movement of about 10,000 Armenian refugees to Argentina between 1909 and 1938. He also noted that official passenger statistics undercounted Armenians because some were classified as Turks by country of origin.

Shorjian entered an Armenian community that already possessed:

- an AGBU branch;
- churches and clergy;
- political parties;
- compatriotic unions;
- early Armenian schools;
- organizations for immigrants from places including Hadjin.

The **Union of Hadjin Residents**, reportedly founded in Argentina in 1915, would have been a natural communal institution for a Hadjin-born immigrant. No evidence was found that Shorjian joined it.

**Status:** Arrival year and purpose are reported facts. Port, ship, immigration category, and organizational memberships are unresolved.

## Working Life in Argentina

### Shoemaking and orthopedic work

Shorjian said he made **orthopedic shoes throughout his working life**. The trade drew directly on the craft training received in the Jounieh orphanage.

The record does not establish:

- employer names;
- workshop addresses;
- whether he was an employee, contractor, or proprietor;
- dates of individual jobs;
- professional licenses;
- clients or medical collaborators;
- whether he manufactured entire shoes or performed specialized modifications;
- business assets or ownership;
- union membership;
- income;
- inventions or patents.

No patent or technology publication under his name was located.

### Longevity in employment

Zenian reports that Shorjian continued working until age **100**, approximately 1998. Shorjian joked that he would have kept working but that the pay was inadequate:

> “I would have continued working but the pay was not good.”

In 2000 he added:

> “I might get back to work if I find a well-paying job. I made orthopedic shoes all my life, and I’m still very good at my profession.”

The remarks support several limited conclusions:

- he retained a strong occupational identity after retirement;
- he considered his technical ability intact;
- compensation, rather than an explicitly stated disability, figured in his account of stopping work.

They do not reveal whether late-life employment was full-time, occasional, formal, or informal.

### Finances and property

The single source implies that pay remained relevant to him at age 100. It supplies no salary, pension, savings, property, debts, business interests, or inheritance information.

His move to a communal home for older people might reflect a need for care, housing, or companionship, but the article does not specify his financial circumstances. Poverty or destitution should not be inferred.

**Status:** Profession and longevity at work are reported facts; business structure and finances unknown.

## Later Life and Public Recognition

### Centennial reception

For his 100th birthday, the social-security department of the Buenos Aires municipal authorities reportedly held a special reception in his honor.

Shorjian said:

> “I had never had a birthday like that.”

No municipal press release, photograph, award certificate, official resolution, or contemporary newspaper report was located. The exact agency is not identified; “social security department” may be Zenian’s English rendering of a municipal social-services or older-persons office.

The recognition appears to have honored his centenary and exceptional continuation in employment rather than a political or artistic achievement.

**Status:** Reported fact, based solely on the AGBU article.

### Health at 102

In 2000 Zenian described Shorjian as:

- healthy;
- not wearing eyeglasses;
- walking briskly.

This is a journalist’s observation, not a medical evaluation. No diagnoses, medication history, disability records, hospitalizations, addictions, psychiatric crises, accidents, or serious injuries were found.

### Move to an Armenian elder home

Shorjian entered an Armenian home for older people in Buenos Aires in **July 2000**, a few months before his 102nd birthday.

The article does not name the home. Buenos Aires’s Armenian community operates the Casa de Descanso Onnig Bodourian–Ohannés Diarbekirian, founded in 1975 in Palermo and associated with the Armenian Relief Society/HOM. It is a plausible candidate, but no source located expressly connects Shorjian to it.

**Status:** Move to an unnamed Armenian home is reported fact; specific institutional identification unresolved.

## Religious, Political, Ideological, and Intellectual Development

### Religion

His Armenian background, placement in an Armenian orphanage, and final residence in an Armenian communal home suggest lifelong participation in Armenian communal structures. No source identifies:

- denomination;
- baptism;
- church membership;
- clerical relationships;
- devotional practice;

### Politics

No political-party membership, public office, voting activity, lobbying, military service, revolutionary activity, or ideological affiliation has been documented.

Zenian’s article lists the Armenian Revolutionary Federation, Armenian Democratic Liberal Party, Social Democrat Hunchakian Party, AGBU, and regional unions as institutions in Argentine-Armenian life. These are contextual references, not evidence of Shorjian’s membership.

### Historical outlook

His clearest documented public statement concerned diasporic continuity:

> “The Armenians of this country are the product of a generation which paid a high price to keep its identity and that in itself has kept us unique. Our collective memory has not faded.”

This demonstrates that he interpreted his life within a collective Armenian history of persecution, refuge, migration, and cultural persistence. The statement does not establish a detailed political program.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

No independent folklore, hagiography, hostile legend, conspiracy narrative, or self-created public mythology about Shorjian was located.

### The centenarian survivor narrative

The AGBU article presents him symbolically as an embodiment of the founding immigrant generation: survivor, orphan, artisan, long-lived worker, and custodian of collective memory. This framing is editorially visible in Zenian’s transition from Shorjian’s individual life to the general history of Armenians in Argentina.

What the narrative says:

- he survived two periods of anti-Armenian violence;
- passed through deportation and orphanage life;
- built a working life in Argentina;
- remained vigorous at 102;
- personified Armenian continuity.

Evidence:

- principally Shorjian’s testimony and Zenian’s observation;
- historical archives independently support the general settings;
- no individual civil or institutional file was found.

Effect on reputation:

- it preserved Shorjian as a representative witness to Armenian migration and survival;
- it also caused his biography to be generalized, leaving many personal particulars unrecorded.

### The firewood concealment story

The story of the child hiding under firewood functions like a concentrated survivor episode, but there is no evidence that it circulated before the 2000 interview or developed into community folklore.

Classification: **first-person reported testimony**, neither independently corroborated nor contradicted.

### Rescue by Turkish neighbors

This episode complicates any simplified ethnic narrative by recording Turkish individuals protecting Armenian survivors during anti-Armenian violence. No names or corroborating testimonies were found.

Classification: **first-person reported testimony**.

Shorjian was alive and interviewed at approximately age 102 in 2000. He had moved into an Armenian home for older people in Buenos Aires in July of that year. Zenian observed him walking briskly and not using eyeglasses.

No subsequent dated reference was found. If alive today he would be approximately 127 or 128, beyond the oldest securely validated human lifespan; he must therefore be presumed deceased. That is a demographic inference, not a documented death record.
