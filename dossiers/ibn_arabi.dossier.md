# Ibn Arabi: Research Dossier

## Scope, identity, method, and evidentiary conventions

This dossier concerns **Muḥyī al-Dīn Ibn ʿArabī (560–638 AH/1165–1240 CE)**, the Andalusian Sufi author, and not **Abū Bakr Ibn al-ʿArabī al-Maʿāfirī** (1076–1148), the earlier Mālikī jurist.

The surviving evidence is unusually rich for a medieval mystic but uneven. Ibn ʿArabī embedded autobiographical notices, dated visions, travel memories, names of teachers, correspondence, and composition records throughout his writings. Important first-person sources include:

- *Al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya*
- *Rūḥ al-quds fī munāṣaḥat al-nafs*
- *Al-Durrat al-fākhira*
- *Al-Fihrist* or *Fihrist al-muʾallafāt*
- the *Ijāza* prepared for al-Malik al-Muẓaffar
- *Tarjumān al-ashwāq* and its commentary
- *Al-Isrāʾ ilā al-maqām al-asrā*
- *Mashāhid al-asrār*
- the *Dīwān*
- hearing certificates, ownership notes, colophons, and other manuscript evidence.

Claude Addas reconstructed his movements largely by collating such internal evidence. Her chronology remains the principal modern foundation, although revisions continue as manuscripts are re-examined. The Muhyiddin Ibn Arabi Society’s archive distinguishes holographs, autographs, copies made from autographs, works authenticated through internal textual evidence, doubtful works, and spurious attributions. It therefore provides a firmer basis than undifferentiated traditional lists.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by Ibn ʿArabī’s writings, contemporary manuscript evidence, or strong near-contemporary documentation.
- **Reported fact:** transmitted by a generally credible later source but not independently established.
- **Disputed claim:** supported by conflicting evidence or interpretation.
- **Visionary claim:** an experience reported by Ibn ʿArabī; the report is documentary evidence that he narrated it, not external verification of its supernatural content.
- **Hagiography, legend, or myth:** a reputation-forming narrative not established as ordinary history.
- **Hostile allegation:** an accusation made in polemical literature, not necessarily an established description of his teaching or conduct.

Dates converted from the Islamic calendar vary by one or more days between modern authorities. His birth is variously rendered as 27 or 28 July 1165. His death is variously converted as 8, 9, 10, or 16 November 1240; the Hijrī date itself is also reported as either 22 or 28 Rabīʿ II 638. These discrepancies are retained rather than silently harmonized.

## Basic Identifying Information

### Full name

The form most securely reconstructed from his own usage is:

**Abū ʿAbd Allāh Muḥammad ibn ʿAlī ibn Muḥammad ibn al-ʿArabī al-Ṭāʾī al-Ḥātimī**

Longer genealogical forms add Aḥmad and ʿAbd Allāh among his ancestors. Variants include:

- Muḥammad ibn ʿAlī ibn Muḥammad Ibn ʿArabī
- Muḥammad ibn al-ʿArabī
- Ibn ʿArabī
- Ibn al-ʿArabī
- Ibn Suraqa, reportedly an Andalusian designation
- al-Ḥātimī or al-Ṭāʾī, referring to claimed descent from the Arab tribe Ṭayy and the line of Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī
- Abenarabi and related Hispanicized spellings
- Ibn Arabí in Spanish
- İbnü’l-Arabî or İbn-i Arabî in Turkish
- Ibn al-ʿArabī al-Ḥātimī in manuscript catalogues.

### Kunyas and titles

- **Abū ʿAbd Allāh**
- **Abū Bakr**, also found in the biographical tradition
- **Muḥyī al-Dīn**, “Reviver of Religion”
- **al-Shaykh al-Akbar**, “the Greatest Master” or *Doctor Maximus*
- **Sulṭān al-ʿārifīn**, “Sultan of the Knowers”
- **Kibrīt al-aḥmar**, “the Red Sulphur,” an alchemical metaphor for the supremely rare saint
- **Ibn Aflāṭūn**, “son of Plato” or “the Platonist,” in later usage
- **Khātim al-awliyāʾ al-Muḥammadiyya**, “Seal of Muhammadian sainthood,” a status he associated with himself through visionary-symbolic language.

Some exalted titles are clearly products of later Akbarian reverence rather than contemporary civil or religious offices.

### Birth

- **Hijrī:** 17 Ramaḍān 560.
- **Common Gregorian conversions:** 27 or 28 July 1165.
- **Place:** Murcia, then the principal city of the Tudmīr region in al-Andalus.
- **Political setting:** Murcia was passing through the instability of the later Almoravid and taifa period into Almohad control.

### Death

- **Hijrī:** most often 22 Rabīʿ II 638; al-Maqqarī reportedly gives 28 Rabīʿ II.
- **Gregorian conversions:** commonly 9 or 10 November 1240; 8 and 16 November also occur.
- **Place:** Damascus, probably in the Banū Zakī residence.
- **Burial:** the Banū Zakī family cemetery at al-Ṣāliḥiyya, beneath Mount Qāsiyūn.
- **Cause of death:** no securely documented illness, accident, violence, or specific medical cause has been identified.

## Family and Ancestry

### Claimed ancestry

Ibn ʿArabī identified himself as **al-Ṭāʾī al-Ḥātimī**, claiming descent from the tribe of Ṭayy and the celebrated pre-Islamic exemplar of generosity, Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī. Some later genealogies make him descend from ʿAbd Allāh, brother of the Companion ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim.

**Evidentiary status:** Ibn ʿArabī’s self-identification with Ṭayy is documented. The uninterrupted genealogy back to Ḥātim cannot be independently verified from surviving contemporary records.

### Social background

His family was prosperous, politically connected, and sufficiently established to enter Almohad administrative and military society. It was not the impoverished background sometimes retrojected from generic Sufi hagiography.

### Father

His father was **ʿAlī ibn Muḥammad**, reportedly a soldier or government official who entered the service of the Almohad ruler Abū Yaʿqūb Yūsuf after the family relocated to Seville. He knew senior political and intellectual figures. Ibn ʿArabī’s meeting with Ibn Rushd was arranged through his father, who was acquainted with the philosopher-jurist.

Ibn ʿArabī later described being present during his father’s final illness and reported a spiritual perception associated with the moment of death. His father died around **590 AH/1193–94**, though the exact date is uncertain.

### Mother

His mother’s personal name is not securely established in the principal modern biographies. She survived her husband only briefly and died around **591 AH/1194–95**.

Ibn ʿArabī’s use of maternal imagery is better documented for his spiritual teacher Fāṭima of Córdoba, whom he called his “divine mother,” than for his biological mother.

### Maternal uncle

A maternal uncle identified as **Yaḥyā ibn Yughan**—the spelling varies—was reportedly a ruler or officeholder who renounced power for an ascetic life after an encounter with a holy man.

**Status:** reported from Ibn ʿArabī’s spiritual-biographical material. The episode has the literary structure of a conversion exemplum and lacks independent political documentation.

### Siblings

He had **two sisters** and apparently no brother. After their mother’s death, he took responsibility for them and brought them from al-Andalus to Fez. A surviving reference to his correspondence indicates sustained attachment to them. Their names and later lives are not securely known.

### Wives

The sources suggest at least three marriages, but the sequence and maternity of his children are incompletely reconstructed.

1. **Maryam bint Muḥammad ibn ʿAbdūn**

   His first known wife, from a socially prominent Andalusian family. Ibn ʿArabī presents Maryam as spiritually serious and as having dreams or experiences of her own. The date of marriage is usually placed during his Andalusian years.

2. **A woman connected with Makīn al-Dīn al-Iṣfahānī’s family**

   Some reconstructions identify a Meccan marriage within the household of his host and teacher Makīn al-Dīn Abū Shujāʿ Zāhir ibn Rustam al-Iṣfahānī. It is important not to confuse this wife with **Niẓām**, Makīn al-Dīn’s daughter, who inspired *Tarjumān al-ashwāq*. Whether Ibn ʿArabī married Niẓām herself is disputed and should not be stated as fact.

3. **The widow of Majd al-Dīn Isḥāq**

   A widely repeated reconstruction holds that after Majd al-Dīn’s death, Ibn ʿArabī married his widow and became stepfather or guardian to her son, **Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnawī**. The exact legal and domestic relationship is less firmly documented than the later master-disciple relationship between Ibn ʿArabī and Qūnawī.

4. **Mother of ʿImād al-Dīn**

   Ibn ʿArabī refers to the mother of his son ʿImād al-Dīn. Whether she was identical with one of the women above is unresolved.

### Children

- **Muḥammad Saʿd al-Dīn**, born in Ramaḍān 618 AH/1221 at Malatya; died in 656 AH/1258. He transmitted or recited his father’s work and reportedly composed poetry.
- **Muḥammad ʿImād al-Dīn**, who survived his father and was bequeathed the authoritative second recension or an important copy of *Al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya*.
- Some sources also mention a daughter, **Zaynab**, but the documentation is thinner.
- **Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnawī** was Ibn ʿArabī’s foremost disciple and may have been his stepson; the step-parentage is reported rather than entirely secure.

No evidence supports a dynastic accumulation of wealth or an enduring commercial family enterprise.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1165–c.1182

Ibn ʿArabī was born at Murcia during political reorganization in al-Andalus. Around **568 AH/1172–73**, when he was about seven or eight, his family moved to **Seville**, the Almohad capital in Iberia.

Seville provided:

- courtly and administrative connections through his father;
- formal instruction in Qurʾān, Arabic, grammar, law, and hadith;
- exposure to the Almohad political order;
- access to ascetics and Sufis operating outside formal court culture.

Later accounts sometimes describe him as a court page, secretary, soldier, or young man preparing for government service. There is reasonable contextual support for an elite administrative-military formation, but no surviving appointment document. Claims that he formally served in the army and resigned in precisely **1184** are **reported**, not firmly established.

He retrospectively called his pre-conversion condition a form of **jāhiliyya**, or ignorance. This need not mean irreligion; it marks the contrast he drew between conventional youth and conscious spiritual commitment.

## Education and Formation

### Formal education

Ibn ʿArabī was trained in the standard disciplines expected of a learned Andalusian Muslim:

- Qurʾānic recitation and interpretation
- hadith and its transmission
- Arabic grammar and literature
- jurisprudence
- theology
- devotional and ascetic literature.

He received authorizations from numerous teachers. Lists derived from his *Fihrist*, *Ijāza*, and autobiographical writings contain dozens of names, although not every named transmitter was a long-term personal master.

### Legal orientation

He is frequently described as a Mālikī because Mālikism dominated al-Andalus. His mature legal method, however, was unusually independent:

- he rejected uncritical adherence to a school;
- favored direct return to Qurʾān and hadith;
- held that binding consensus was essentially the consensus of the Prophet’s Companions;
- sometimes reached positions resembling Ẓāhirī literalism;
- nevertheless gave symbolic and metaphysical readings alongside literal legal ones.

Calling him simply a Mālikī, Ẓāhirī, or “without madhhab” therefore compresses a more complicated record.

### Early spiritual turning

The conversion is generally placed around **580 AH/1184–85**, when he was approximately nineteen. Ibn ʿArabī associated his transformation with a divine summons and with visions involving Jesus, Moses, and Muhammad.

**Documented fact:** he later narrated these experiences and organized his spiritual autobiography around them.

**Visionary claim:** the supernatural encounters themselves are not independently verifiable.

### Principal Andalusian teachers

Among the ascetics and Sufis he described were:

- **Abū al-ʿAbbās al-ʿUryabī of al-ʿUlyā**, often treated as his first formal spiritual director. An unlettered or minimally lettered peasant saint, al-ʿUryabī represented knowledge received through spiritual realization rather than book learning.
- **Abū Yaʿqūb Yūsuf ibn Yakhlaf al-Kūmī**, a major early guide.
- **Abū ʿImrān Mūsā ibn ʿImrān al-Mīrtulī**
- **Abū ʿAbd Allāh al-Sharafī**
- **Abū ʿAlī al-Shakkāz**, associated with his introduction to foundational Sufi literature.
- **Abū Madyan Shuʿayb**, the Maghribi master whom Ibn ʿArabī revered deeply but apparently never met physically. Their connection was spiritual and through disciples and writings.
- **Fāṭima bint Ibn al-Muthannā of Córdoba**, an elderly woman whom he served for roughly two years. He called her his spiritual or “divine” mother and described her command of the opening chapter of the Qurʾān as a charismatic power.
- **Shams Umm al-Fuqarāʾ of Marchena**, another elderly woman saint, admired for spiritual strength and discernment.
- **Yasmin or Yasmina of Marchena** may be another rendering of Shams in some secondary accounts rather than a demonstrably separate person.

The prominence of women in these reminiscences is not a modern invention: it derives from Ibn ʿArabī’s own notices, although later popular treatments sometimes romanticize the relationships.

### Ibn Rushd/Averroes

Around **1180s**, probably in Córdoba, the young Ibn ʿArabī met **Abū al-Walīd Ibn Rushd (Averroes)** through his father.

Ibn ʿArabī’s later account depicts a symbolic exchange:

- Ibn Rushd asked whether mystical unveiling confirmed rational inquiry.
- Ibn ʿArabī answered, “Yes and no,” adding that spirits fly from the places between yes and no.
- The philosopher reportedly paled at the implications.

Ibn ʿArabī later said he saw Ibn Rushd’s coffin transported in **1198**, balanced against his books.

**Documented fact:** Ibn ʿArabī recorded both stories.

**Interpretive caution:** the dialogue survives only in his retrospective literary account. It dramatizes the relationship between rational philosophy and unveiling and cannot be treated as a transcript.

## Chronological Life History

## Andalusian journeys, c.1182–1194

After his spiritual turn, Ibn ʿArabī traveled repeatedly through southern and central al-Andalus. Places connected with these years include:

- Seville
- Córdoba
- Carmona
- Marchena
- Morón
- Ronda
- Algeciras
- Almería
- Granada
- possibly Jaén and other Andalusian towns.

He sought ascetics, visited teachers, practiced retreats, transmitted hadith, and began writing. His autobiographical portraits emphasize poor, obscure, disabled, socially marginal, and female saints as well as formally learned men.

Around **586 AH/1190**, at Córdoba, he reported a vision assembling all prophets from Adam to Muhammad. He later interpreted this as an announcement of his own spiritual office. The prophetic structure of *Fuṣūṣ al-ḥikam*, written decades later, has often been linked to this vision.

### North Africa, 1193–1195

He crossed to the Maghrib more than once.

In **590 AH/1193–94**, he traveled to **Tunis**, where he associated with ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz al-Mahdawī and other Sufis. He heard or studied Ibn Barrajān’s *Kitāb al-Ḥikma* and composed or developed works including *Mashāhid al-asrār al-qudsiyya*.

While in Tunis, he reported a visionary encounter with al-Khiḍr. Similar Khidr narratives recur throughout Sufi literature.

**Status:** autobiographically documented as a reported vision; supernatural identification is not externally verifiable.

His father died around this period, requiring his return to Seville. His mother died several months later.

### Removal of his sisters and residence at Fez, 1195–1198

After his mother’s death, Ibn ʿArabī escorted his two sisters to **Fez**, then a major Almohad intellectual center. He spent extended periods there.

At Fez he:

- attended circles of hadith and Qurʾānic learning;
- maintained contacts with Andalusian and Maghribi Sufis;
- undertook retreats;
- reported a vision or spiritual ascent in **593 AH/1197**;
- composed or developed *Kitāb al-Isrāʾ*, *Mawāqiʿ al-nujūm*, and other early works.

In **594 AH/1198**, he was back in al-Andalus and witnessed Ibn Rushd’s funeral at Córdoba. The famous image of the philosopher’s coffin, with his books counterbalancing his body, became one of Ibn ʿArabī’s most cited symbolic recollections.

### Final western period, 1198–1201

His movements included Granada, Almería, Murcia, Fez, and Tunis. Among the early works assigned to this phase are:

- *ʿAnqāʾ mughrib fī khatm al-awliyāʾ wa-shams al-maghrib*
- *Mawāqiʿ al-nujūm*
- *Inshāʾ al-dawāʾir*
- *Al-Tadbīrāt al-ilāhiyya*
- *Mashāhid al-asrār*
- *Al-Isrāʾ ilā al-maqām al-asrā*.

In **598 AH/1201–02**, while again in Tunis, he completed or presented *Inshāʾ al-dawāʾir* for a companion. He then left the Islamic West permanently.

No documentary evidence establishes that this departure was a legal exile. Explanations include pilgrimage, spiritual vocation, intellectual opportunity, political instability, or dissatisfaction with Almohad conditions. “Exile” is therefore inappropriate unless used metaphorically.

---

## Eastward migration and pilgrimage, 1201–1202

His route east is reconstructed through internal dates and composition notices and may have included:

- Tunis
- Alexandria
- Cairo
- Hebron
- Jerusalem
- Medina
- Mecca.

He visited sacred sites associated with Abraham and other prophets at Hebron, Jerusalem’s prophetic sanctuaries, and Muhammad’s tomb at Medina. He reached Mecca in **598 AH/1202**, in time for pilgrimage.

This migration divided his life:

- approximately thirty-five years in al-Andalus and the Maghrib;
- approximately thirty-eight years in the central and eastern Islamic lands.

---

## Mecca, 1202–1204

Mecca was decisive for his mature work.

### Makīn al-Dīn’s household and Niẓām

He became close to **Makīn al-Dīn Abū Shujāʿ Zāhir ibn Rustam al-Iṣfahānī**, a learned Persian associated with the sanctuary. Makīn al-Dīn’s daughter **Niẓām**, also called ʿAyn al-Shams wa-l-Bahāʾ, became the human focus of *Tarjumān al-ashwāq*.

Ibn ʿArabī described her as:

- learned in Qurʾān and religious knowledge;
- eloquent;
- beautiful;
- spiritually accomplished;
- an embodiment or locus of divine wisdom.

### Nature of the relationship

**Documented fact:** Ibn ʿArabī said Niẓām inspired the poems.

**Unresolved question:** whether the relationship involved marriage or physical romance. The evidence does not establish either.

**Later polemic:** critics accused the poems of being ordinary erotic verse disguised as mysticism. Ibn ʿArabī answered by composing a prose commentary explaining their symbolic meanings. Neither the erotic surface nor his mystical commentary should be erased.

### Major Meccan compositions

- He began *Al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya*, an encyclopedic work eventually comprising 560 chapters.
- He wrote *Rūḥ al-quds fī munāṣaḥat al-nafs* in 600 AH/1203, addressing ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz al-Mahdawī and preserving biographies of western Sufis.
- He produced *Tāj al-rasāʾil*, poems concerning the Kaʿba.
- He developed material later incorporated into *Tarjumān al-ashwāq*.
- He reported visions of the Kaʿba, Muhammad, and symbolic “bricks” associated with the seal of sainthood.

## Iraq, Anatolia, Syria, and repeated travel, 1204–1223

### Baghdad and Mosul, 1204–1205

Leaving Mecca with **Majd al-Dīn Isḥāq al-Rūmī**, Ibn ʿArabī traveled through Iraq, including Baghdad and Mosul.

At Mosul he reportedly received the **khirqa**, or Sufi mantle, associated with al-Khiḍr through a chain connected to ʿAlī ibn Jāmiʿ. He later discussed transmission of the mantle as a bond of companionship rather than proof of subordination to a single institutional order.

### Anatolia and Konya, c.1205

Majd al-Dīn Isḥāq had connections to the Seljuk court. Ibn ʿArabī visited **Konya**, where Sultan **Ghiyāth al-Dīn Kaykhusraw I** reportedly received him.

Ibn ʿArabī tells of being given a house worth **100,000 dirhams** and immediately giving it to a beggar because it was the only property he possessed.

**Status:** first-person anecdote in *Al-Futūḥāt*.

**Caution:** the exact valuation cannot be audited; its literary function is to demonstrate renunciation.

### Jerusalem, Hebron, Cairo, and Mecca, c.1205–1207

Manuscript datings have traditionally been used to place Ibn ʿArabī in Jerusalem for extended periods in **601–602 AH/1204–06**, but archive research has challenged some readings of those colophons. The general presence is credible; exact duration and sequence remain revisable.

He returned to Egypt. A later report says hostile jurists or theologians threatened him in Cairo and that he escaped danger through the protection or intervention of associates.

**Status:** reported fact with hagiographical amplification. No court indictment, prison register, or execution order survives.

He returned to Mecca and continued teaching and writing.

### Aleppo, Damascus, Baghdad, and Anatolia

Between approximately 1207 and 1223, his documented or strongly reconstructed locations include:

- Mecca
- Baghdad
- Aleppo
- Damascus
- Jerusalem
- Konya
- Sivas
- Malatya.

He associated with Ayyubid and Seljuk elites but held no known salaried government office.

### Al-Malik al-Ẓāhir of Aleppo

Ibn ʿArabī had access to **al-Malik al-Ẓāhir Ghāzī**, son of Saladin and ruler of Aleppo. He claimed that al-Ẓāhir granted **118 petitions** presented by him in one session. In another episode he persuaded the ruler to spare a courtier who had criticized him.

**Documented first-person report:** the episodes appear in *Al-Futūḥāt*.

**Political significance:** Ibn ʿArabī functioned at times as an intercessor between subjects and rulers, but this did not constitute formal ministerial office.

### Letter to Kaykāʾūs I

From Malatya, around **609 AH/1212–13**, Ibn ʿArabī wrote to the Seljuk Sultan **ʿIzz al-Dīn Kaykāʾūs I**. He admonished the sultan to:

- administer justice;
- restrain corrupt deputies;
- protect Muslim subjects;
- resist policies favoring or accommodating Christian public power.

The letter contains both general counsel on mercy and justice and harsh prescriptions toward Christians. It is a central primary source against anachronistic portrayals of Ibn ʿArabī as an advocate of equal modern pluralism.

### Al-Tanazzulāt al-Mawṣiliyya and other works

At Mosul and during related travels he wrote works on ritual, divine names, and the inner meanings of worship. His production was mobile: books were begun in one city, continued in another, recited to students elsewhere, and later revised.

### Malatya and family life

Ibn ʿArabī spent substantial time at **Malatya**, where his son Saʿd al-Dīn was born in **618 AH/1221**. His relationship with Majd al-Dīn’s family and with young Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnawī developed in the Anatolian setting.

The claim that he remained continuously in Malatya or Anatolia for twenty years is too simple; dated writings show repeated travel.

## Permanent settlement in Damascus, 1223–1240

Around **620 AH/1223**, Ibn ʿArabī settled in **Damascus**, though short later movements cannot be ruled out.

### Patrons and household

His principal Damascene environment included:

- the learned and politically connected **Banū Zakī** family;
- Qāḍī Muḥyī al-Dīn Ibn al-Zakī;
- Ayyubid rulers and princes;
- students arriving from Syria, Anatolia, Iraq, and elsewhere.

He lived and taught within elite patronage networks, but the evidence does not show luxury, landed power, or an independent financial institution.

### *Fuṣūṣ al-ḥikam*

In **627 AH/1229–30**, Ibn ʿArabī said Muhammad appeared to him in a dream at Damascus holding a book and commanded him to convey it without addition or subtraction. The resulting *Fuṣūṣ al-ḥikam* contains twenty-seven chapters, each organized around a prophet and a particular “wisdom.”

**Documented fact:** this is Ibn ʿArabī’s own account of the book’s origin.

**Visionary claim:** the prophetic delivery is a religious claim not susceptible to ordinary historical verification.

**Authorship controversy:** some defenders later argued that objectionable passages were interpolations or that the whole work was falsely attributed to him. Against wholesale denial stand early manuscript transmission, Ibn ʿArabī’s own *Naqsh al-fuṣūṣ*, citations by close disciples, and the extensive early commentary tradition. The mainstream scholarly conclusion is that *Fuṣūṣ* is genuine, while particular wording remains subject to textual criticism.

### Revision of *Al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya*

Ibn ʿArabī completed a first recension of *Al-Futūḥāt* around **629 AH/1231–32** and then undertook a substantial second recension. The latter was completed in **636 AH/1238**.

A major autograph manuscript, traditionally called the **Konya manuscript**, comprises thirty-seven volumes; most are in Ibn ʿArabī’s hand, with one volume written by another scribe. Its survival makes the *Futūḥāt* one of the best-documented large authorial corpora of the medieval Islamic world.

The second recension was reportedly assigned to or inherited by his son ʿImād al-Dīn and later became associated with Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnawī’s collection.

### Teaching and transmission

His Damascene circle included:

- Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnawī
- Ibn Sawdakīn
- ʿAfīf al-Dīn al-Tilimsānī
- Saʿd al-Dīn and ʿImād al-Dīn, his sons
- members of the Banū Zakī
- transmitters named in hearing certificates on manuscripts.

The “Akbarian school” was not a formally chartered organization founded under that name. It emerged through texts, oral teaching, manuscript transmission, commentaries, family and court networks, and later institutional appropriation.

## Death, Burial, and Estate

Ibn ʿArabī died in Damascus in Rabīʿ II 638 AH/November 1240. Sources disagree over both the Hijrī day and modern conversion:

- 22 Rabīʿ II is the dominant report.
- 28 Rabīʿ II is attributed to al-Maqqarī.
- Modern dates include 8, 9, 10, and 16 November.

He was buried in the Banū Zakī cemetery at al-Ṣāliḥiyya beneath Mount Qāsiyūn. His two sons were reportedly later buried near him.

No reliable source identifies:

- a prolonged terminal illness;
- poisoning or assassination;
- execution;
- imprisonment at death;
- a medically defined cause;
- an estate valuation.

His most consequential “property” was intellectual and manuscript-based: authorial copies, authorized recensions, hearing certificates, and transmission rights.

## Residences and Geographic Movements

A conservative reconstruction includes:

1. **Murcia**, 1165–c.1172/73.
2. **Seville**, principal home from childhood into early adulthood.
3. Repeated travel through **Córdoba, Marchena, Carmona, Morón, Ronda, Algeciras, Almería, Granada, and Murcia**.
4. Crossings to **Ceuta and Fez** and visits to **Tunis**.
5. Permanent departure from the Maghrib, 1201–02.
6. **Alexandria and Cairo**.
7. **Hebron and Jerusalem**.
8. **Medina and Mecca**.
9. **Baghdad and Mosul**.
10. **Aleppo and Damascus**.
11. **Konya, Sivas, and Malatya**.
12. Permanent settlement at **Damascus**, c.1223–1240.

Claims for exact months in Jerusalem and certain Anatolian cities must remain provisional because some depend on disputed readings of manuscript notes.

There is no evidence of travel to Christian western Europe, Persia proper, India, Central Asia, or North Africa after his eastward migration.

## Professions, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Professions and functions

Ibn ʿArabī can be described as:

- Sufi teacher and spiritual director
- Qurʾān interpreter
- hadith transmitter
- jurist
- theologian and metaphysical thinker
- poet
- autobiographer and hagiographer
- letter writer and political counselor
- manuscript author, reviser, reader, and certifier.

“Philosopher” is appropriate in the broad modern sense but was not his preferred disciplinary identity. He criticized the *falāsifa* while engaging many problems shared with philosophy.

### Institutions

He was connected to:

- Andalusian and Maghribi hadith and Sufi circles
- the Almohad social and administrative world through his family
- the Meccan sanctuary’s scholarly community
- Seljuk court networks
- Ayyubid court networks
- the Banū Zakī household in Damascus
- informal teaching circles later called the Akbarian tradition.

No evidence establishes that he founded a named Sufi order, madrasa, corporation, commercial partnership, or endowed institution during his lifetime.

### Finance and property

The financial record is sparse:

- His family was comfortable and politically connected.
- He received hospitality and gifts from rulers and elites.
- His account of giving away a house valued at 100,000 dirhams presents radical detachment from property.
- He interceded with rulers on behalf of petitioners.
- He appears to have relied partly on patronage and household support.
- No ledgers, tax records, investments, commercial ventures, or estate inventory are known.
- No patent, invention, or proprietary technology is associated with him.

His diagrams of cosmology, letters, divine names, and spiritual hierarchy are intellectual and graphic instruments, not patented inventions.

## Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

## Revelation, unveiling, and knowledge

Ibn ʿArabī distinguished discursive learning from **kashf**, unveiling. He did not reject reason outright; he argued that reason is limited when it confines reality to what its own conceptual procedures can establish.

Knowledge comes through:

- revelation
- prophetic tradition
- reason within its domain
- disciplined spiritual practice
- imagination
- dreams and visions
- divine self-disclosure.

His autobiographical presentation makes lived revelation-like experience central without claiming legislative prophethood after Muhammad.

## Being and “unity of existence”

Ibn ʿArabī is routinely called the founder of **waḥdat al-wujūd**, “unity of being/existence.” The exact phrase is not a stable technical slogan in his corpus in the way it became among later followers and critics.

His characteristic positions include:

- only God possesses being in an absolute and independent sense;
- created things are loci of divine self-disclosure;
- the immutable entities or archetypes do not possess independent existence;
- multiplicity is real at the level proper to manifestation but not as an independent rival to God;
- God is both incomparable with creation and disclosed through it.

### Disputed interpretation

- **Akbarian reading:** a rigorous account of divine unity preserving the Creator-creature distinction through levels and relations.
- **Critical Sunni reading:** ontological monism collapsing God into creation and therefore pantheism or incarnationism.
- **Modern comparative reading:** a form of monism, nondualism, panentheism, or “mystical philosophy.”
- **Scholarly caution:** none of these modern labels maps perfectly onto his vocabulary.

## Imagination

The **imaginal realm** (*ʿālam al-khayāl*, later often called *ʿālam al-mithāl*) mediates between purely intelligible and sensory realities. Dreams, visions, resurrection forms, symbols, and divine self-disclosure are understood through this faculty or world.

Imagination is not merely fantasy. It is the domain in which contraries can coincide without ceasing to be distinct.

## The Perfect Human

The **Perfect Human** (*al-insān al-kāmil*) is:

- the comprehensive locus of divine names;
- the mirror in which God and cosmos are disclosed;
- fully realized in Muhammad;
- reflected in prophets and saints according to capacity.

Later authors systematized the doctrine more rigidly than Ibn ʿArabī always did.

## Prophecy and sainthood

He affirmed Muhammad as the final law-bearing prophet while elaborating a hierarchy of sainthood. His claims concerning the **Seal of Muhammadian Sainthood** were among his most controversial assertions.

He associated himself with this seal through symbolic visions. Critics regarded the claim as self-exaltation or encroachment on prophetic status; defenders distinguished sainthood’s seal from Muhammad’s finality as prophet.

## Scripture and prophetic wisdom

His method combines:

- literal adherence to Qurʾānic wording;
- etymological interpretation;
- multiple simultaneous meanings;
- cosmological correspondences;
- visionary confirmation;
- prophetic typology.

The prophets in *Fuṣūṣ* are not merely historical biographies. Each becomes the bearer of a distinctive divine wisdom.

## Religious diversity

The celebrated poem beginning “My heart has become capable of every form” has often been presented as a manifesto of unrestricted religious pluralism. Ibn ʿArabī did recognize divine self-disclosure across forms and warned that a believer who confines God to one conceptual belief worships a limited construction.

At the same time:

- he regarded Muhammad’s revelation as comprehensive and normatively final;
- practiced Islamic law;
- described non-Islamic beliefs as partial or restricted;
- urged Kaykāʾūs to impose discriminatory legal restrictions on Christians.

The evidence supports neither the caricature of simple modern universalism nor the claim that his references to religious plurality were absent.

## Women and gender

Ibn ʿArabī held that women can attain every spiritual station, including sainthood and spiritual leadership. He learned from women and described the feminine as a privileged locus of divine manifestation.

He also wrote within medieval Islamic legal and sexual assumptions. Modern claims that he straightforwardly advocated contemporary gender equality go beyond the evidence.

## Eschatology and divine mercy

His writings emphasize the all-encompassing divine mercy. Passages concerning the final condition of hell’s inhabitants and Pharaoh generated controversy.

Some interpreters conclude that:

- punishment ultimately becomes congenial to its inhabitants or ceases;
- Pharaoh died in faith;
- divine mercy finally encompasses all.

Others argue that these readings ignore distinctions and rhetorical levels. The textual basis for controversy is real; consensus on his final position is not.

## Political Activity

Ibn ʿArabī did not lead a party, army, rebellion, or state. His political action consisted of:

- advising rulers;
- interceding for individuals;
- commenting on justice and taxation;
- urging rulers to restrain abusive deputies;
- advocating enforcement of Islamic public hierarchy;
- interpreting kingship through divine vicegerency;
- maintaining relations with Seljuk and Ayyubid elites.

His letter to Kaykāʾūs demonstrates that metaphysical universalism did not erase concrete medieval confessional politics. The ruler was told to be merciful and just toward subjects while also maintaining legal and symbolic Muslim supremacy.

No evidence connects him with espionage, intelligence organizations, assassination, political violence, or an underground revolutionary society.

## Controversies, Accusations, and Adversarial Findings

A dedicated adversarial search was conducted in English and Arabic using combinations of Ibn Arabi with controversy, criticism, accusation, takfīr, fraud, scandal, conspiracy, crime, lawsuit, and recent 2024–2026 discussions.

The result is that Ibn ʿArabī’s documented controversies are overwhelmingly **theological, textual, political, and reputational**. No credible evidence was found of modern-style financial fraud, money laundering, commercial corruption, patent theft, sexual prosecution, criminal conviction, or civil litigation. Claims in those categories are absent rather than suppressed.

## Accusations of unbelief and heresy

Later critics included, with differences in wording and intensity:

- Ibn Taymiyya
- al-Dhahabī
- Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya
- Saʿd al-Dīn al-Taftāzānī
- ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn al-Bukhārī
- Burhān al-Dīn al-Biqāʿī
- Ibn Ḥajar al-ʿAsqalānī or teachers cited by him
- Ibn Khaldūn
- later Salafi and anti-Sufi jurists.

Charges included:

- *ittiḥād*, union of God and creation;
- *ḥulūl*, divine indwelling;
- pantheism;
- denial of the Creator-creature distinction;
- vindication of Pharaoh;
- praise or exoneration of idol worship;
- relativization of revealed religions;
- elevating saints above prophets;
- esoteric distortion of Qurʾān;
- antinomianism.

### Evidentiary assessment

- **Documented fact:** such condemnations exist.
- **Documented fact:** some disputed passages have a real basis in *Fuṣūṣ* or *Futūḥāt*.
- **Disputed interpretation:** whether they entail the doctrines attributed by critics.
- **Hostile exaggeration:** descriptions of Ibn ʿArabī as rejecting Islamic law conflict with the large legal and ritual content of his authenticated work.
- **Unresolved textual issue:** individual passages require comparison across early manuscripts, but wholesale dismissal of *Fuṣūṣ* as a forgery is not supported by the manuscript and reception history.

## “Unity of being” as pantheism

Modern polemics frequently state that Ibn ʿArabī taught “everything is God.”

That formula is inadequate:

- he repeatedly affirms divine incomparability;
- he distinguishes the Real from created loci;
- he also denies independent being to anything other than God;
- he deliberately uses paradoxical language that permits sharply divergent readings.

“Pantheist” is therefore an interpretive label, not an uncontested historical fact.

## Pharaoh

A notorious controversy concerns whether Pharaoh died as a believer after declaring faith while drowning.

- Critics read Ibn ʿArabī as saving Pharaoh and contradicting Qurʾānic consensus.
- Defenders distinguish legal judgment in this world from hidden divine knowledge, or interpret the passage as describing receipt of faith rather than assured salvation.
- The controversy is grounded in genuine textual language, not fabricated from nothing.
- His precise final judgment remains disputed.

## Noah, idols, and worship

In the Noah chapter of *Fuṣūṣ*, Ibn ʿArabī interprets polytheistic forms through divine manifestation. Critics charge that this praises idolatry or faults Noah for calling people away from idols.

Akbarian commentators answer that:

- every object depends on God and discloses a divine name;
- worship directed to a form remains restricted and therefore deficient;
- recognizing divine disclosure in forms is not identical to permitting idol worship.

The conflict turns on whether metaphysical inclusion cancels normative religious distinction. Ibn ʿArabī’s continued commitment to Islamic law argues against a simple abolition of distinction, but his language invited the hostile reading.

## The “religion of love”

The poem “My heart has become capable of every form” circulates as proof that Ibn ʿArabī held all religions equally true.

- **Documented:** the poem belongs to *Tarjumān al-ashwāq*.
- **Documented:** he supplied esoteric interpretations for its images.
- **Disputed:** whether it establishes equal-validity pluralism.
- **Contrary evidence:** his legal writings and Kaykāʾūs letter retain Islamic supersession and discriminatory medieval rules.

## Erotic poetry and Niẓām

Critics in Aleppo reportedly treated *Tarjumān al-ashwāq* as erotic poetry whose mystical explanation was an afterthought.

- The poems visibly use Arabic love-poetry conventions.
- Niẓām was a real woman known to him.
- He answered by writing a line-by-line spiritual commentary.
- It is not possible to prove that either the erotic or mystical dimension exhausts the poems.

No reliable evidence supports allegations of sexual misconduct.

## Self-proclaimed spiritual rank

Ibn ʿArabī associated himself with the Seal of Muhammadian Sainthood and reported extraordinary visions and divine instructions.

- Admirers treated these as saintly disclosures.
- Critics saw spiritual vanity, delusion, or rivalry with prophetic authority.
- Modern psychological diagnoses cannot responsibly be inferred from medieval visionary writing.
- No clinical evidence exists for psychosis, mania, epilepsy, or drug-induced experience.

## Threats in Egypt

Later biographies report that hostile scholars sought his death in Cairo.

- No preserved court judgment or indictment has been located.
- The possibility of serious theological hostility is historically plausible.
- Detailed escape narratives should be labeled reported or hagiographical.

## Political intolerance

The letter to Kaykāʾūs contains demands concerning Christian dress, churches, public symbols, and government employment.

- **Documented fact:** Ibn ʿArabī did not advocate modern civil equality.
- **Context:** comparable prescriptions belonged to medieval *dhimma* jurisprudence.
- **Interpretive dispute:** scholars differ over how this material fits his metaphysical account of religious diversity.

## Book burning and censorship

Later scholars reportedly called for his books to be burned or withheld from unprepared readers. Ottoman scholars issued opposing rulings defending him and sometimes forbidding public denunciation.

This was not a single legal “trial of Ibn ʿArabī.” It was a centuries-long conflict over textual interpretation, teaching authority, and boundaries of Sunnism.

## Recent controversies, 2024–2026

Recent academic and online discussion repeats older disputes:

- whether he should be called a philosopher;
- whether *waḥdat al-wujūd* is Sunni, pantheist, or a later label;
- whether objectionable *Fuṣūṣ* passages are authentic;
- whether defenders rely too heavily on esoteric reinterpretation;
- whether universalist readings erase his Islamic supersessionism;
- whether hostile readings ignore his law-centered corpus;
- whether popular television transforms him into a politically useful saint.

No credible new evidence from 2024–2026 establishes criminal, financial, or sexual wrongdoing. The recent record concerns reception, authenticity, theology, political appropriation, and historical representation.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

## The prophets’ assembly

**Story:** all prophets assembled spiritually before Ibn ʿArabī at Córdoba around 1190.

**Appearance:** narrated through his autobiographical corpus and reconstructed by later biographers.

**Evidence:** Ibn ʿArabī’s testimony that he experienced the vision.

**Against literal verification:** no independent witness is possible.

**Reputational effect:** supporters read it as recognition of his universal spiritual inheritance; critics see self-authorizing mythology.

## “Yes and no” with Averroes

**Story:** Ibn ʿArabī’s paradoxical answer caused Averroes to turn pale because mystical unveiling both confirmed and exceeded rational philosophy.

**Evidence:** Ibn ʿArabī’s retrospective narrative.

**Historical core:** the meeting is credible because his father knew Ibn Rushd.

**Literary layer:** the polished exchange functions as a symbolic drama.

**Effect:** it became a canonical scene in histories contrasting Sufism and philosophy.

## Ibn Rushd’s coffin and books

**Story:** Ibn Rushd’s coffin was placed on one side of a pack animal and his books on the other; a companion asked whether his aspirations had been fulfilled.

**Evidence:** first-person recollection.

**Effect:** frequently interpreted as the weighing of lived realization against accumulated rational knowledge.

## The Kaʿba appearing as a living interlocutor

**Story:** the Kaʿba appeared or spoke to Ibn ʿArabī, and he experienced himself in relation to it as lover, servant, or cosmic counterpart.

**Status:** visionary-poetic report.

**Effect:** central to Meccan hagiography and interpretation of the *Futūḥāt*.

## The missing brick and seal

**Story:** in a dream of the prophetic edifice, Ibn ʿArabī saw two missing bricks, one gold and one silver, corresponding to the completion or sealing of Muhammadian sainthood.

**Status:** autobiographical visionary claim.

**Effect:** foundation for his status as seal of Muhammadian sainthood and a major focus of criticism.

## *Fuṣūṣ* delivered by Muhammad

**Story:** Muhammad handed him the book in a dream and ordered him to transmit it exactly.

**Status:** documented as Ibn ʿArabī’s claim; supernatural origin unverified.

**Effect:** gives the text exceptional authority for followers and makes its controversial passages more objectionable to critics.

## The 100,000-dirham house

**Story:** a sultan gave Ibn ʿArabī a valuable house; when a beggar asked for alms he immediately gave away the house.

**Evidence:** Ibn ʿArabī’s own moral anecdote.

**Uncertainty:** price and legal transfer cannot be independently checked.

**Effect:** establishes saintly generosity and detachment from rulers’ gifts.

## The hidden tomb and Selim I

**Story:** Ibn ʿArabī predicted that “when sīn enters shīn, my tomb will appear,” interpreted as Sultan Selim entering al-Shām. After conquering Damascus in 1516, Selim I supposedly identified and restored the forgotten grave.

**Evidence:** the tomb complex’s Ottoman patronage and Selim’s promotion of the site are historical. The prophecy belongs to later attribution and is connected with the doubtful *Al-Shajara al-nuʿmāniyya* tradition.

**Against:** no secure lifetime manuscript establishes the famous wording as Ibn ʿArabī’s prophecy.

**Effect:** made Ibn ʿArabī a posthumous legitimizer of Ottoman conquest and sovereignty.

## Hidden treasure beneath the tomb

Variants of the tomb story include discovery of treasure or miraculous signs.

**Status:** shrine legend.

**Effect:** reinforced the site’s sanctity and Selim’s image as the ruler destined to vindicate the saint.

## Meeting Ertuğrul

The television series *Diriliş: Ertuğrul* portrays Ibn ʿArabī as Ertuğrul’s mentor.

**Status:** historical fiction.

**Evidence against:** no historical source documents a meeting. The chronology and uncertain biography of Ertuğrul make it highly improbable. Ibn ʿArabī died in 1240; Ertuğrul’s historically visible period is later.

**Effect:** the series produced one of the most widely recognized twenty-first-century popular images of Ibn ʿArabī.

## Secret founder of Ottoman power

Later Ottoman literature treated Ibn ʿArabī, his Anatolian disciples, and his alleged prophecies as spiritual preconditions of the empire.

**Historical core:** his thought strongly influenced early Ottoman scholars through Qūnawī, Dāwūd al-Qayṣarī, and later commentators.

**Mythic extension:** claims that he consciously designed or foretold the Ottoman state rest on doubtful prophetic texts and retrospective imperial ideology.

## Christian, Neoplatonist, or covert heterodox thinker

Miguel Asín Palacios’s *El Islam cristianizado* emphasized Christian parallels. Other writers have called Ibn ʿArabī a Neoplatonist, Hermeticist, Shiʿi esotericist, perennialist, or precursor of Western nondualism.

These comparisons may reveal structural parallels, but no evidence makes him a covert member of another religion or secret organization. His explicit intellectual world is Qurʾānic, hadith-based, Sufi, and Islamicate, while also engaging philosophical and cosmological materials circulating in that world.

## Manuscripts, Textual Transmission, and Attribution

Osman Yahia’s *Histoire et classification de l’œuvre d’Ibn ʿArabī* recorded roughly **1,395 title entries**, including duplicates and variant titles. After consolidation, the traditional attribution is often summarized as approximately **850 works**, of which perhaps **700** may be authentic and more than **400** extant. These figures are provisional rather than final.

The modern manuscript project uses stronger categories:

- holograph: in Ibn ʿArabī’s hand;
- autograph: signed or certified by him;
- copy explicitly made from an autograph;
- externally or internally verified work;
- doubtful attribution;
- false attribution.

Important manuscript centers include:

- Turkish and Islamic Arts Museum, Istanbul
- Yusuf Ağa Library, Konya
- Süleymaniye Library, Istanbul
- National Library collections
- Manisa
- Cairo
- Damascus collections
- European manuscript libraries
- private collections.

The manuscript record has suffered:

- dispersal;
- cataloguing confusion;
- mistaken author names;
- variant titles;
- fire and earthquake risk;
- theft, including unique manuscripts stolen from Konya collections in the 1990s.

## Works by Ibn ʿArabī

A literal catalogue of every title would require a separate multi-volume bibliography. The following is a substantial list of the principal authenticated, substantially authenticated, disputed, or influentially attributed works. Dates are approximate when the composition extended over years.

### Major authenticated works

1. **Al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya fī maʿrifat al-asrār al-mālikiyya wa-l-mulkiyya**  
   *الفتوحات المكية* — *The Meccan Openings/Revelations*. Begun at Mecca c.598/1202; first recension completed c.629/1231–32; second recension completed 636/1238. 2. **Fuṣūṣ al-ḥikam**  
   *فصوص الحكم* — *The Ringstones/Bezels of Wisdom*. Damascus, 627/1229–30. Twenty-seven prophetic wisdoms. Genuine according to the predominant manuscript and reception evidence, despite later defensive claims of forgery or interpolation.

   *ترجمان الأشواق* — *The Interpreter of Desires/Yearnings*. Poems composed from the Meccan period onward, conventionally c.1202–1211; inspired by Niẓām.

   *ذخائر الأعلاق* — Ibn ʿArabī’s prose commentary giving spiritual interpretations of the love poems.

54. **Dīwān Ibn ʿArabī / al-Dīwān al-kabīr**  
    A very large poetic corpus, transmitted in differing recensions. The major collection contains hundreds of poems; some volumes are lost or were stolen.

55. **Al-Fihrist / Fihrist al-muʾallafāt**  
    Ibn ʿArabī’s own list of writings; essential for attribution.

56. **Al-Ijāza li-l-Malik al-Muẓaffar**  
    An authorization listing works and teachers, prepared for an Ayyubid ruler.

57. **Letters and responsa**  
    Including counsel to Kaykāʾūs and correspondence with disciples and rulers. Collections vary and require manuscript-level authentication.

58. **Awrād al-usbūʿ**  
    Weekly prayers, with day and night recitations.

59. **Al-Dawr al-aʿlā / Ḥizb al-wiqāya**  
    A widely circulated protective litany.

### Works lost, incomplete, or known chiefly through references

Many titles in the *Fihrist*, *Ijāza*, and cross-references have not been located. Some may survive under different titles; others may have been short epistles incorporated into larger collections. “Lost” therefore includes:

- genuinely destroyed works;
- unrecognized manuscripts;
- alternate-title duplicates;
- projected works never completed;
- portions of composite manuscripts.

### Disputed and spurious attributions

Particular caution is required for:

- **Al-Shajara al-nuʿmāniyya fī al-dawla al-ʿuthmāniyya** and related prophetic texts: central to Ottoman legends, but attribution and textual history are deeply problematic.
- **Kitāb māhiyyat al-qalb**: shown by Claude Addas and Michel Chodkiewicz not to be Ibn ʿArabī’s work in the commonly published form.
- Certain versions of **Kitāb al-mabādiʾ wa-l-ghāyāt li-maʿānī al-ḥurūf**.
- Popular occult, talismanic, astrological, letter-magical, and dream-interpretation manuals printed under his name.
- Short treatises titled simply *al-Aḥadiyya*, *al-Huwa*, *al-Rūḥ*, or *al-Insān al-kāmil* without manuscript provenance.
- Aphorism collections assembled posthumously from genuine and non-genuine material.
- Modern books marketed as “the sayings,” “secrets,” or “prophecies” of Ibn ʿArabī without critical apparatus.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

## Immediate disciples

The decisive transmitter was **Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnawī**. He reorganized, taught, and philosophically articulated Ibn ʿArabī’s ideas in Persian and Anatolian scholarly contexts. Through him, the Akbarian tradition intersected with:

- Persian metaphysics;
- Qurʾān commentary;
- Seljuk and early Ottoman learning;
- later Sufi orders;
- philosophical theology.

Ibn Sawdakīn and ʿAfīf al-Dīn al-Tilimsānī also contributed to early interpretation.

## Persianate and Ottoman worlds

Major interpreters included:

- Muʾayyid al-Dīn al-Jandī
- ʿAbd al-Razzāq al-Qāshānī
- Dāwūd al-Qayṣarī
- ʿAbd al-Raḥmān Jāmī
- Ṣāʾin al-Dīn Turka
- Mullā Fanārī
- ʿAbd Allāh Bosnevī
- Ismāʿīl Ḥaqqī Bursevī.

Dāwūd al-Qayṣarī’s role in early Ottoman education helped embed Akbarian metaphysics in Ottoman learned culture. This influence was substantial but never uncontested.

## India

Ibn ʿArabī’s thought entered South Asia through Persian commentaries, Chishti and other Sufi networks, and court intellectual culture. It became central to controversies involving:

- *waḥdat al-wujūd*;
- Ahmad Sirhindī’s *waḥdat al-shuhūd*;
- Mughal religious policy;
- later reformist and traditionalist debates.

It is misleading to reduce Mughal religious experimentation directly to Ibn ʿArabī; the relationship was mediated and contested.

## Shiʿi and Iranian reception

Despite Ibn ʿArabī’s Sunni legal and hadith framework, his metaphysics strongly influenced later Iranian thinkers, including figures in the orbit of:

- Sayyid Ḥaydar Āmulī
- Ibn Turka
- Mullā Ṣadrā
- later *ʿirfān* traditions.

Iranian interpreters debated his references to the caliphs, the Mahdi, sainthood, and spiritual authority. Some assimilated his system to Shiʿi imamology; others criticized his Sunni commitments.

## Arabic-speaking reception

In Syria and Egypt his reputation oscillated between saint, difficult master, dangerous monist, and heretic. Damascus became the geographical center of his cult through his tomb, while Cairo and Mamluk legal culture generated significant opposition.

## Ottoman shrine and imperial appropriation

After the Ottoman conquest of Damascus in **1516**, Sultan Selim I sponsored the rediscovery or monumental reconstruction of Ibn ʿArabī’s tomb. A mosque, tomb, kitchen, and educational complex were developed around it.

The shrine:

- made Ibn ʿArabī an imperial saint;
- linked Ottoman sovereignty with sacred geography;
- encouraged pilgrimage and manuscript culture;
- strengthened legends that he had predicted the Ottoman rise.

Some Damascene scholars disputed whether the Ottoman monument marked the authentic grave. ʿAbd al-Ghanī al-Nābulusī later discussed the tomb’s location. Thus even the physical center of the cult remained contested.

## Europe and comparative mysticism

European scholarship framed Ibn ʿArabī successively as:

- pantheist;
- Neoplatonist;
- Muslim mystic comparable to Christian contemplatives;
- visionary philosopher;
- source or analogue for Dante;
- exponent of creative imagination;
- perennialist metaphysician.

Asín Palacios’s work stimulated debate over Islamic influences on Dante. Direct literary dependence remains debated and is not established merely by structural parallels between ascension narratives and the *Divine Comedy*.

Henry Corbin emphasized the imaginal world and theophanic imagination. Traditionalist and perennialist writers, including René Guénon’s intellectual successors, universalized Ibn ʿArabī as a spokesman for a transcendent unity of religions. Later scholars have criticized these readings for downplaying his Qurʾānic language, Islamic law, and supersessionist commitments.

## Modern Sufi and academic institutions

The Muhyiddin Ibn Arabi Society, founded in 1977, helped establish:

- a specialist journal;
- conferences;
- translations;
- manuscript identification;
- cataloguing and conservation;
- international networks of scholarship and devotion.

Its scholarship is indispensable, though its institutional orientation is generally sympathetic. Critical work by Knysh, Lipton, and others provides a necessary counterweight.

## Contemporary popular image

Today Ibn ʿArabī appears as:

- an orthodox Sunni saint;
- a metaphysical philosopher;
- an esoteric poet;
- a pluralist icon;
- a supposed pantheist or heretic;
- an Ottoman imperial patron-saint;
- a New Age or perennialist sage;
- a television mentor to Ertuğrul.

These images derive from different strata of evidence and should not be collapsed into one biography.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the sources are included. Mere presence in the same century, religion, country, or intellectual tradition is excluded.

### Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani

**Nature:** documented intellectual-spiritual influence, not a physical meeting. Ibn ʿArabī was born approximately one year before ʿAbd al-Qādir al-Jīlānī’s death in Baghdad and did not meet him. Later Akbarian transmission connected Ibn ʿArabī with Jīlānī’s spiritual authority and mantle traditions.

**Date/place:** indirect, chiefly through later Sufi transmission; Baghdad and the wider Islamic East.

**Label:** **reported influence; no documented meeting**.

### Abu Yazid al-Bistami

**Nature:** Ibn ʿArabī discusses and interprets ecstatic sayings associated with Abū Yazīd in his Sufi and metaphysical writings.

**Date/place:** textual and posthumous; no physical meeting was possible.

**Label:** **documented textual influence**.

### Abu al-Qasim al-Junayd al-Baghdadi

**Nature:** Junayd’s model of sober Sufism, terminology, and sayings belonged to the inherited tradition Ibn ʿArabī cited and reinterpreted.

**Date/place:** posthumous textual-spiritual influence.

**Label:** **documented intellectual influence; no meeting**.

### Abu al-Qasim al-Qushayri

**Nature:** Ibn ʿArabī knew the earlier Sufi tradition systematized in al-Qushayrī’s writings. Direct dependence on a particular text requires case-by-case proof.

**Label:** **reported and probable textual influence; no meeting**.

### Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani and Abu Madyan distinction

Abū Madyan, one of Ibn ʿArabī’s most important acknowledged spiritual authorities, is not on the atlas roster. He should not be conflated with ʿAbd al-Qādir al-Jīlānī.

### Mansur al-Hallaj

**Nature:** Ibn ʿArabī knew and discussed sayings and doctrines associated with al-Ḥallāj but did not simply adopt the later image of Hallajian union.

**Date/place:** posthumous textual influence.

**Label:** **documented textual relationship; no meeting**.

### Ibrahim ibn Adham

**Nature:** Ibn ʿArabī’s corpus draws on the inherited ascetic and saintly tradition in which Ibrāhīm ibn Adham was a major exemplar. A specific personal chain was not established in the consulted sources.

**Label:** **reported traditional influence, not a direct relationship**.

### Rabia al-Adawiyya

**Nature:** part of the earlier Sufi tradition of divine love relevant to Ibn ʿArabī’s intellectual environment. No direct, explicitly documented dependence strong enough for a personal connection was located.

**Label:** **no specific atlas connection established beyond shared tradition**.

### Al-Malik al-Kamil

**Nature:** Ibn ʿArabī moved within the wider Ayyubid political network that included al-Malik al-Kāmil. The consulted sources firmly establish relations with al-Kāmil’s Ayyubid relatives—especially al-Ẓāhir, al-ʿĀdil, and al-Ashraf—but did not establish a specific personal meeting with al-Kāmil strongly enough to assert one.

**Label:** **possible political-network connection; direct meeting unresolved**.

### Abraham

**Nature:** Abraham is a foundational prophetic figure throughout Ibn ʿArabī’s writings and has a dedicated wisdom in *Fuṣūṣ al-ḥikam*. Ibn ʿArabī visited Hebron and Abraham’s traditional burial sanctuary during his eastward journey.

**Date/place:** c.1201–02, Hebron; continuing textual influence.

**Label:** **documented devotional and intellectual connection; prophetic history, not contemporaneous contact**.

### Moses

**Nature:** Ibn ʿArabī repeatedly reported visionary encounters with Moses and devoted a chapter of *Fuṣūṣ* to Mosaic wisdom.

**Date/place:** visions associated with his spiritual conversion and later writings.

**Label:** **documented as Ibn ʿArabī’s visionary claim; supernatural encounter unverified**.

### Jesus of Nazareth

**Nature:** Ibn ʿArabī described Jesus as a central initiatory guide in his conversion and developed an extensive Christology of spirit, breath, and revivification.

**Date/place:** visionary experiences from the Andalusian period onward.

**Label:** **documented textual influence and documented visionary claim; supernatural encounter unverified**.

### Muhammad ibn Abdullah

**Nature:** Muhammad was Ibn ʿArabī’s supreme prophetic model. Ibn ʿArabī claimed visions of Muhammad, including the delivery of *Fuṣūṣ al-ḥikam*, and defined his own sainthood as Muhammadian.

**Date/place:** numerous visions; most notably Damascus, 627/1229–30, for *Fuṣūṣ*.

**Label:** **documented intellectual-devotional relationship and documented visionary claim; supernatural encounter unverified**.

### Plato

**Nature:** the later nickname “Ibn Aflāṭūn,” son of Plato, associates Ibn ʿArabī with philosophical wisdom. The epithet does not prove direct study of Plato’s original works.

**Label:** **later symbolic association**.

### Aristotle

**Nature:** Aristotle formed part of the philosophical tradition represented by Ibn Rushd, whom Ibn ʿArabī personally met. No direct reading of an identifiable Aristotelian manuscript was established.

**Label:** **indirect intellectual connection through the Islamicate philosophical tradition**.

### Jalal al-Din Rumi

**Nature:** no documented meeting has been established. Ibn ʿArabī lived in Anatolia before Rumi’s mature career. The significant connection runs through Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnawī, Ibn ʿArabī’s disciple and Rumi’s contemporary in Konya.

**Date/place:** indirect connection in thirteenth-century Konya.

**Label:** **documented shared network through Qūnawī; no documented personal meeting**.

### Baha ud-Din Walad

**Nature:** both were connected with the Seljuk Anatolian world, but the consulted sources did not establish a meeting, correspondence, or explicit influence.

**Label:** **no documented connection found**.

### Shams al-Din Muhammad of Tabriz

**Nature:** no documented meeting or correspondence with Ibn ʿArabī. Later Sufi reception sometimes juxtaposes Shams, Rumi, Qūnawī, and Ibn ʿArabī.

**Label:** **no direct connection found**.

### Farid al-Din Attar

**Nature:** contemporaneous Persian Sufi author, but no securely documented meeting, correspondence, or acknowledged direct influence was established.

**Label:** **no direct connection found**.

### Maimonides

**Nature:** both emerged from the Andalusian intellectual world and migrated east, but no meeting, correspondence, or explicit acknowledged influence was located.

**Label:** **absence of documented connection**.

### Ramon Llull

**Nature:** both were associated with medieval Majorcan/Iberian religious thought, but no meeting or textual relationship is established.

**Label:** **absence of documented connection**.

### Dante Alighieri

**Nature:** later scholars, especially Miguel Asín Palacios, argued that Islamic ascension literature, including material associated with Ibn ʿArabī, helped shape Dante’s *Divine Comedy*.

**Evidence:** structural and thematic parallels and possible channels of transmission.

**Against:** no documentary chain proves Dante read Ibn ʿArabī.

**Label:** **scholarly theory; direct influence unresolved**.

### René Guénon

**Nature:** Guénon and the twentieth-century Traditionalist milieu helped transmit Akbarian concepts in Europe. Guénon’s work contributed to the perennialist reception of Ibn ʿArabī.

**Label:** **documented posthumous intellectual reception**.

### Carl Gustav Jung

**Nature:** both are sometimes compared through symbolism and imagination, but no strong evidence of Jung’s direct, substantial dependence on Ibn ʿArabī was established in the consulted sources.

**Label:** **later comparative association; direct influence unproven**.

### Henry Corbin

Henry Corbin is not included in the supplied atlas roster, although he is one of the most important twentieth-century interpreters connecting Ibn ʿArabī to European philosophy.

### Roster-wide negative finding

For the great majority of the 896-person roster, no documented meeting, correspondence, collaboration, family tie, patronage relation, or explicit acknowledged influence was found. This is expected: most lived in different centuries or intellectual worlds. No connection has been inferred merely from shared geography, Sufism, philosophy, symbolism, or later comparative writing.

| 1946 | Affifi’s influential edition of *Fuṣūṣ al-ḥikam*. |
| 1964 | Osman Yahia publishes his monumental bibliography. |
| 1972–92 | Yahia’s incomplete critical edition of *Futūḥāt* appears. |
| 1977 onward | Muhyiddin Ibn Arabi Society develops conferences, journal, and archives. |
| 1989–93 | Claude Addas’s documentary biography appears in French and English. |
| 1990s | Important manuscripts disappear from Konya collections. |
| 2000s–present | Manuscript projects identify holographs, autographs, doubtful works, and false attributions. |
| 2014–19 | *Diriliş: Ertuğrul* creates a globally popular but fictionalized Ibn ʿArabī. |
| 2024–26 | Scholarship continues on Ottoman, Iranian, theological, political, and manuscript reception; online controversy remains focused on orthodoxy and *waḥdat al-wujūd*. |
