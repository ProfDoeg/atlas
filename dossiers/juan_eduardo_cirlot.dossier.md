# Juan Eduardo Cirlot Laporta: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier reconstructs the life and work of Juan Eduardo Cirlot Laporta from institutional authority records, archival descriptions, contemporary journalism, interviews, bibliographic catalogs, published correspondence, family testimony, and academic scholarship consulted through 4 September 2026.

The following labels are used where evidence is contested:

- **Documented fact:** supported by an archival record, publication, contemporaneous document, or multiple consistent sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a named biographer, relative, associate, or reputable secondary source but not independently confirmed here from the underlying document.
- **Interpretation:** a scholarly or critical judgment rather than a discrete biographical fact.
- **Allegation or disputed characterization:** a consequential claim for which sources disagree or the evidence does not establish the strongest version.
- **Rumor or mythology:** a story important to Cirlot’s reputation or self-presentation but not demonstrable as literal fact.
- **Unresolved:** the accessible evidence does not permit a firm conclusion.

No credible evidence was located of lawsuits, financial fraud, corruption, money laundering, criminal prosecution, espionage, or personal involvement in political violence. His ideological relationship to Falangism, National Socialism, Rudolf Hess, CEDADE, and the Franco regime is addressed separately and without collapsing aesthetic attraction, political belief, organizational contact, and criminal complicity into one category.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full civil name:** Juan Eduardo Cirlot Laporta.
- **Usual published name:** **Juan-Eduardo Cirlot**, generally with a hyphen between the forenames. Library and archival authorities also normalize him as Juan Eduardo Cirlot or Cirlot, Juan-Eduardo.
- **Pseudonyms:** No firmly documented literary pseudonym was found.
- **Birth:** 9 April 1916, Barcelona, Catalonia, Spain.
- **Death:** 11 May 1973, Barcelona, at his home on Carrer Hercegovina.
- **Cause of death:** Pancreatic cancer, diagnosed in 1971 and treated surgically.
- **Nationality:** Spanish.
- **Primary languages:** Spanish was his literary language; he also knew and used Catalan. His relatives and biographers emphasize that he wrote poetry almost entirely in Spanish despite living in Barcelona’s Catalan cultural environment.
- **Occupations:** Poet, art critic, art historian, essayist, symbolist and mythographer, editor and publishing employee, journalist, music critic, composer, translator, lecturer, collector of historic weapons.
- **Principal institutional affiliations:** Banco Hispano Americano; Editorial Argos; Editorial Gustavo Gili; Círculo Manuel de Falla; Dau al Set; *La Vanguardia*; *Destino*; *Goya*; *Papeles de Son Armadans*; Academia del Faro de San Cristóbal.
- **Best-known work:** *Diccionario de símbolos tradicionales* (1958), expanded and subsequently published as *Diccionario de símbolos*.
- **Major poetic achievement:** The extensive *Bronwyn* sequence, written principally between 1967 and 1972.

Cirlot was neither a university-trained art historian nor a professional academic. His career combined salaried publishing work with self-directed study, criticism, poetry, and a short but serious period of musical composition.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and military background

**Documented fact:** His father was **Juan Cirlot Nieto** (1886–1962), a career military man. His mother was **María Laporta Blanco**. Spanish state archival authority records identify two sisters:

- **María Victoria Cirlot Laporta**
- **María de la Paz Cirlot Laporta**

The family belonged to a military milieu extending beyond his father. Recent scholarship on Cirlot’s “arms and letters” symbolism emphasizes his veneration of military ancestors and his tendency to treat the warrior as a metaphysical or spiritual type.

**Reported fact:** Antonio Rivero Taravillo’s biography describes geographically diverse family origins. Cirlot later invoked those origins when explaining what he considered his insufficient or atypical “Catalanness.” The accessible sources do not supply a reliable, generation-by-generation pedigree, so no more precise ancestry should be inferred.

### Economic circumstances

**Reported fact:** The family’s finances were constrained rather than affluent. Cirlot’s father reportedly distrusted books and did not support a prolonged literary or university education. This helps explain Juan Eduardo’s practical training in commerce and his early employment.

There is no evidence that Cirlot inherited substantial wealth or controlled a significant business. His later collection of books, paintings, archaeological-looking objects, and swords was personally important but is not documented as a major financial estate.

### Marriage and children

**Documented fact:** In August 1947 he married **Gloria Valenzuela**. No evidence of divorce or a second marriage was found.

They had two daughters:

- **Lourdes Cirlot Valenzuela**, born in 1949, later an art historian specializing in twentieth-century and contemporary art and a scholar of Dau al Set.
- **Victoria Cirlot Valenzuela**, born in 1955, later a medievalist and scholar of Arthurian literature, mysticism, symbolism, and visionary culture.

Both daughters became principal custodians, editors, interpreters, and depositors of their father’s archive. Their testimony is indispensable but should also be recognized as family testimony, particularly in disputes about his political identity.

No documented extramarital partner or additional child was found. The women who dominate poems such as Susan Lenox, Bronwyn, and Inger were cinematic or imaginative figures, not established lovers.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1916–1935

Cirlot was born and raised in Barcelona. He attended the Jesuit school on Carrer Casp.

**Reported fact:** His Jesuit education stimulated a lasting interest in ancient Rome and Egypt. These were not merely historical interests. Rome, Egypt, Carthage, Sumer, the Celtic world, and the Middle Ages became interior geographies in his writing.

He did not proceed through a conventional university degree. Instead, he studied:

- Commercial correspondence
- Bookkeeping and accounting
- Piano
- Musical theory and composition
- Literature, art, mythology, and religion independently

He began work while still young, first in a customs agency and later at the Banco Hispano Americano.

### Musical education

At the Instituto Musical Academia Ardèvol, under **Fernando/Ferran Ardèvol**, Cirlot reportedly completed approximately eight years of piano study and began composition.

His musical formation was one of the most consequential parts of his education. Music supplied structural principles later transferred into his poetry: serial organization, atonality, repetition, variation, and permutation.

---

## Education and Formation, 1935–1939

Cirlot identified 1936 as a decisive year of “discoveries.” These included:

- Pablo Picasso and modern painting
- Surrealism
- Igor Stravinsky
- Anton Webern
- Alban Berg
- Ernst Krenek
- Alexander Scriabin, who became particularly important
- The possibility of formally radical art as a mode of spiritual knowledge

In 1936 he attended the International Society for Contemporary Music festival in Barcelona, organized substantially through Robert Gerhard. Works by leading modern composers exposed him directly to European atonal and experimental music.

### Spanish Civil War

**Documented fact:** Cirlot was mobilized by the Spanish Republic in 1937.

**Reported fact:** Biographical accounts state that he later crossed or changed sides and spent a short period in a concentration or detention camp before serving in the victorious Nationalist military. The exact date, place, circumstances, voluntariness, and legal status of this transition are not clearly established in the accessible institutional accounts.

The formulation that he “fought for both sides” is broadly accurate but potentially misleading:

- Service in the Republican forces followed mobilization.
- His later Nationalist service occurred after the Republic’s defeat or during his transition from one zone to another.
- The sources consulted do not establish comparable ideological commitment to both armies.
- Later remarks by Cirlot and his fascination with military order suggest that he did not experience military life simply as an imposed trauma. Rivero Taravillo even reports that the war was not remembered by him as traumatic, although this is an interpretive biographical conclusion rather than a clinical finding.

There is no evidence that Cirlot held command, participated in atrocities, or faced a war-crimes investigation.

---

## Zaragoza and Nationalist Military Service, 1940–1943

Cirlot was again mobilized, now by the Francoist army, and lived in Zaragoza from approximately 1940 until 1943.

This period was intellectually formative. Zaragoza’s artistic circle accepted him, and he encountered:

- Pianist **Pilar Bayona**
- Painter and collagist **Alfonso Buñuel**
- Through Alfonso, the work and reputation of his brother **Luis Buñuel**
- Modern French poetry and Surrealism
- Books and avant-garde periodicals available in the Buñuel circle

Cirlot and Alfonso Buñuel reportedly worked with or translated poems by Paul Éluard, André Breton, and Antonin Artaud. The degree to which each translation was jointly authored is not fully itemized in accessible catalogs.

Pilar Bayona became a friend and musical reference. Cirlot dedicated early poems to her, including material later known as *Pájaros tristes y otros poemas a Pilar Bayona*.

### Early music and poetry

In Zaragoza he produced or presented early compositions. A *Vals* was performed there in 1942. His earliest printed poetic books followed shortly afterward:

- *La muerte de Gerión. Ballet* (1943)
- *Seis sonetos y un poema del amor celeste* (1943)

The surviving record suggests that poetry and music initially developed together rather than as separate careers.

---

## Return to Barcelona and Early Career, 1943–1948

Cirlot returned to Barcelona in the summer of 1943 and resumed work at Banco Hispano Americano.

He met novelist and journalist **Benítez de Castro**, who helped introduce him to journalism and art criticism. Cirlot also worked in or around the Argos bookshop and publishing house.

### Poetry of the 1940s

His early books appeared in very small editions, often through minor presses:

- *Oda a Ígor Strawinsky y otros versos* (1944)
- *Árbol agónico* (1945)
- *En la llama* (1945)
- *Canto de la vida muerta* (1946)
- *Cordero del abismo* (1946)
- *Donde las lilas crecen* (1946)
- *Susan Lenox* (1947)

The imagery already included death, ruined civilizations, abyssal landscapes, sacrifice, erotic distance, visionary women, and the transformation of cinematic images into private mythology.

### Composition and the Círculo Manuel de Falla

Cirlot joined the **Círculo Manuel de Falla**, founded in 1947 to reinvigorate contemporary musical culture in Barcelona. Associates included Manuel Valls, Alberto Blancafort, Àngel Cerdà, Joan Comellas, Josep Maria Mestres Quadreny, and singer Anna Ricci.

Surviving or reported compositions include:

- *Vals* (performed 1942)
- *Himno para piano*
- *Preludio para cinco instrumentos de cuerda*
- *Suite atonal*, with more than one version; a 1947 version survives through a copy sent to Carlos Edmundo de Ory
- *Poema de Rilke*
- *Preludio para quinteto y doble violonchelo*
- *Concertino para instrumentos de cuerda* (1948)
- *Sonet*, for soprano and orchestra, setting a text by Joan Brossa, dated 1950

**Reported fact and personal mythology:** Cirlot later destroyed most of his musical manuscripts, exclaiming “Esto es Schönberg”—an act presented as self-criticism or purification after concluding that his work remained derivative. The survival of *Suite atonal* through Ory and the later recovery of *Sonet* partly counteracts the resulting documentary gap.

### Marriage

He married Gloria Valenzuela in August 1947. Their first daughter, Lourdes, was born two years later.

## Dau al Set, Surrealism, Paris, and the Avant-Garde, 1948–1954

### Dau al Set

Dau al Set was formed in Barcelona in 1948 around Joan Brossa, Arnau Puig, Antoni Tàpies, Modest Cuixart, Joan Ponç, and Joan-Josep Tharrats. Cirlot joined its orbit in 1949 and contributed more pieces to its magazine than is sometimes acknowledged.

His exact status is described differently:

- Some accounts list him as a member or even among the group’s effective founders.
- Others treat him as a later associate or the metaphorical “seventh face” attached to an original nucleus.
- The best documentary formulation is that he became an active collaborator and intellectual ally in 1949 but was not part of every account of the group’s initial 1948 formation.

The name *Dau al Set*—“the seventh face of the die”—became especially apt in later representations of Cirlot as a supplementary, anomalous member.

He shared the group’s interests in:

- Surrealism
- Magic and occult traditions
- Alchemy
- Medieval thought
- Antoni Gaudí
- Ramon Llull
- Arnold Schoenberg
- Jazz
- Non-classical or marginal systems of knowledge

### Relationships inside the group

Cirlot became particularly close to **Antoni Tàpies** and **Modest Cuixart**. He met the cousins at or around the Salons d’Octubre in 1947.

Cirlot’s first identified discussion of Tàpies appeared in 1949. Tàpies subsequently asked him to write an exhibition text for the Galeries Laietanes. According to later specialist testimony, they saw one another almost daily, explaining the relative scarcity of letters.

Cirlot became a major early advocate and international interpreter of Tàpies’s informalist work. Their personal relationship broke down in the early 1960s. The evidence consulted does not establish one definitive cause. Political differences, changing artistic alignments, and Cirlot’s difficult relationship with Barcelona cultural circles have all been suggested.

With **Joan Brossa**, relations were more competitive. Rivero Taravillo characterizes them as two poets competing within one group. Their language choices and politics differed: Brossa was a Catalanist and wrote in Catalan; Cirlot, although bilingual, made Spanish his poetic language and was politically conservative or reactionary.

### Paris and André Breton

In October 1949 Cirlot traveled to Paris and met **André Breton**, reportedly at or near the Place Blanche. Cirlot was accompanied on at least part of the journey by associates connected with the Barcelona avant-garde.

He corresponded with Breton and dedicated *Lilith* to him.

The relationship nevertheless contained a basic ideological conflict:

- Cirlot admired Surrealist methods, images, dreams, and theories of the marvelous.
- He rejected the movement’s Communist and atheistic alignments.
- According to Rivero Taravillo, Breton’s circle invited or was prepared to admit him into the international Surrealist movement, but Cirlot refused.
- A dramatic scene in a Paris café, reportedly involving Cirlot’s sincere ideological repudiation and enjoyed by the Surrealists as an authentically disruptive act, entered his biography. The accessible summary does not supply a full contemporaneous transcript; the episode should therefore be treated as a **reported incident**, not reconstructed dialogue.

He also sought admission to Spanish **Postismo**, associated with Carlos Edmundo de Ory, Eduardo Chicharro, and Silvano Sernesi, but was not accepted. Cirlot nevertheless maintained a substantial friendship and correspondence with Ory.

### First books of criticism

Important publications included:

- *Diccionario de los ismos* (1949)
- *Ígor Strawinsky* (1949)
- *Joan Miró* (1949)
- *El arte de Gaudí* (1950)
- *Ferias y atracciones* (1950)
- *La pintura abstracta* (1951)
- *El estilo del siglo XX* (1952)
- *Introducción al surrealismo* (1953)

The *Diccionario de los ismos* surveyed avant-garde movements but also coined or regularized categories of Cirlot’s own. It is therefore not a neutral historical dictionary; it mixes documentation, criticism, taxonomy, and invention.

### Nebiros and Francoist censorship

Cirlot wrote his only known novel, *Nebiros*, in August and September 1950. Publisher **José Janés** submitted it to the Dirección General de Propaganda.

**Documented fact:** Francoist censorship rejected it twice, describing its morality as “grosera” and “repugnante” and its spirit as defeatist. Marked passages concerned prostitution, sexuality, despair, and the book’s metaphysics of evil. Cirlot declined or failed to make the changes necessary for approval.

César González-Ruano reportedly appealed to censorship official Juan Beneyto, emphasizing Cirlot’s literary merit and adhesion or loyalty to the regime. The intervention failed.

The manuscript’s later history has been described in two complementary ways:

- A family copy survived among Cirlot’s papers and was recovered by Victoria Cirlot.
- The censorship copy, with passages marked in red, survived in the Archivo General de la Administración at Alcalá de Henares, where Victoria Cirlot and Enrique Granell located it.

The book was finally published by Siruela in 2016 with an epilogue by Victoria Cirlot.

Its suppression is significant evidence against claims that Cirlot enjoyed uncomplicated ideological or institutional acceptance under Franco. It does not, by itself, disprove his right-wing beliefs.

### Marius Schneider and symbolic thought

Between approximately 1949 and 1954 Cirlot studied with and befriended German musicologist and ethnologist **Marius Schneider**, then working in Barcelona.

Schneider taught him to approach symbols through correspondences among:

- Sound
- Rhythm
- Animals
- Musical modes
- Myth
- Cosmic structures
- Ancient and medieval iconography

This mentorship connected Cirlot’s musical formation to his later theory of symbols.

In 1954 he produced *El ojo en la mitología. Su simbolismo* and joined the **Academia del Faro de San Cristóbal**, a playful or semi-serious cultural association rather than a state academy.

## Editorial Gustavo Gili and Professional Consolidation, 1951–1960

Cirlot began work at Editorial Gustavo Gili in 1951 and remained there until his death. Publishing provided his stable employment while facilitating his work with art books and artists.

His roles are variously described as editorial employee, adviser, editor, writer, and contributor. He was not documented as an owner or equity investor.

### Ghostwriting

**Reported fact:** Rivero Taravillo’s biography and a 2016 profile describe Cirlot as having worked as a literary “negro”—a ghostwriter. The sources consulted do not provide a verified, comprehensive list of books ghostwritten by him. It would therefore be unsafe to attribute specific anonymous publications without manuscript or contract evidence.

### Art-historical production

During the 1950s Cirlot published at a rapid rate:

- Monographs on Gaudí, Miró, Manuel Capdevila, Cuixart, Pablo Serrano, and others
- Surveys of abstraction, Surrealism, expressionism, Cubism, Romanesque art, sculpture, and contemporary art
- Spanish regional art guides
- Exhibition essays and catalog texts
- Articles in *Destino*, *Dau al Set*, and other periodicals

He worked with art historian **José Gudiol Ricart** and developed a reputation as a defender of abstraction and informalism at a time when such art faced conservative, clerical, and political hostility. Abstract art was sometimes attacked in Francoist Spain as fraudulent, decadent, or even “philocommunist.” Cirlot’s criticism argued for its formal, spiritual, and historical legitimacy.

### Abandonment of composition

Cirlot effectively ceased sustained musical composition around 1948–1950. Music nonetheless remained central to his writing. His criticism, poetry, and symbolic theory repeatedly returned to Schoenberg, Stravinsky, Scriabin, Mahler, and serial form.

### Destruction of papers

**Documented through family and archival history:** Cirlot destroyed a substantial portion of his early manuscripts and papers, especially material predating 1958. The destruction created major gaps in reconstructing his juvenilia, compositions, and political development. *Nebiros* was one of the important exceptions.

The surviving personal archive consequently concentrates on 1958–1972.

## The Dictionary of Symbols and International Recognition, 1958–1965

In 1958 Luis Miracle published *Diccionario de símbolos tradicionales*. Later editions expanded the title and contents as *Diccionario de símbolos*.

Cirlot’s prefatory explanation identifies several major intellectual sources:

- Marius Schneider
- Carl Gustav Jung
- René Guénon
- Mircea Eliade
- Traditional emblem books
- Anthropology
- Comparative religion
- Depth psychology
- Alchemy
- Occultist compilations

The dictionary is synthetic rather than narrowly philological. It assembles meanings across cultures and epochs, often prioritizing analogical contemplation over historical proof. Its enduring usefulness and its methodological risks derive from the same feature: it connects traditions boldly but can flatten differences of time, place, and context.

### English publication

In 1962 Routledge published *A Dictionary of Symbols*, with an introduction by **Herbert Read**. This gave Cirlot an international readership far exceeding that of his poetry.

The dictionary has since appeared in repeated Spanish and translated editions. Later editions contain expansions and editorial changes, so readers should distinguish the 1958 first edition, Cirlot’s later revisions, and posthumous editorial forms.

### Journalism and lectures

Beginning in 1958 Cirlot contributed to *Goya* and *Papeles de Son Armadans*. He became an active lecturer and wrote extensively about Spanish and international modern art.

His critical method provoked recurring objections:

- **Documented critical dispute:** Opponents accused him of excessive subjectivity.
- **Documented critical dispute:** His refusal to adopt social realism led to accusations of elitism or withdrawal from mass concerns.
- **Cirlot’s answer:** Poetry answered spiritual or ontological questions and was not journalism, advertising, or political ministry.

## Intellectual and Artistic Development

### Surrealism without Surrealist orthodoxy

Cirlot accepted automatic association, dreams, irrational imagery, cinematic fascination, and the Surrealist object. He rejected compulsory materialism and Communist political commitment.

His position is therefore best described as **heterodox, spiritual, and anti-orthodox Surrealism**, not membership in Breton’s movement after 1949.

### Symbolism and the “imaginal”

For Cirlot, symbols were not merely signs requiring decoding. They could mediate between ordinary experience and another level of reality. His daughter Victoria and later critic Clara Janés often described this as an “imaginal” domain: not imaginary in the sense of false, but a world of meaningful images.

### Religion

Cirlot was raised in a Catholic environment but cannot be classified simply as an orthodox Catholic writer.

His work drew upon:

- Christianity and Christological imagery
- Gnosticism
- Orphism
- Sufism
- Jewish Kabbalah
- Abraham Abulafia’s letter permutations
- The *Zohar*
- Buddhism and Taoism
- Alchemy
- Grail literature
- Celtic, Egyptian, Sumerian, Roman, Persian, and Germanic mythology

Rivero Taravillo describes him as not conventionally believing but intensely nostalgic for belief. His poetry often seeks transcendence while simultaneously affirming negation, nothingness, death, or the impossibility of arrival.

### Nihilism

Cirlot explicitly described himself as a nihilist. His *Del no mundo* and later poems elaborate “no-world,” absence, and ontological negation.

This nihilism was not simply political despair. It functioned as:

- A diagnosis of modern reality
- A poetic method
- A spiritual stripping away
- An attraction to death and non-being
- A refusal of social or literary normality

### Music and permutation

Schoenbergian serialism and Abulafian letter combination converged in Cirlot’s mature poetic technique. Words, names, syllables, and letters were permuted much as a tone row can be transformed.

Representative works include:

- *Bronwyn, permutaciones*
- *Inger, permutaciones*
- *Homenaje a Bécquer*
- *Donde nada lo nunca ni*

### Medievalism and the warrior

His collection of swords, admiration for medieval weapons, and symbolic identification of poet and warrior reflected an antimodern conception of order. He viewed the sword as protection, distinction, sacrifice, spiritual combat, and axis.

The collection was not merely decorative. Photographs and his essay “Mis espadas” made it central to his public image.

## Mature Career, 1961–1966

From 1961 to 1971 Cirlot published more than one hundred articles in *La Vanguardia*, including art criticism, film writing, reflections on history, music, death, myth, and contemporary events.

He also continued publishing major art-historical studies:

- *Pintura catalana contemporánea* (1961)
- *Significación de la pintura de Tàpies* (1962)
- *Pintura contemporánea (1863–1963)* (1963)
- *Nuevas tendencias pictóricas 1955–1965* (1965)
- *El espíritu abstracto desde la Prehistoria a la Edad Media* (1965)
- *Introducción a la arquitectura de Gaudí* (1966)
- *Lucio Fontana* (1966)

### Home and private museum

At different periods Cirlot lived in Barcelona residences including an apartment in **La Pedrera**, Antoni Gaudí’s Casa Milà, and later in Vallcarca and on Carrer Hercegovina.

His workroom became part library, armory, cabinet of curiosities, and imagined observatory. It contained:

- Swords and other weapons
- Books on art, myth, religion, and symbolism
- Paintings and works by contemporary friends
- A Roman-style portrait of Cirlot
- Reproductions of Giorgio de Chirico and ancient art
- Objects interpreted through personal symbolic associations

Later exhibitions reconstructed or evoked this “imaginary room.”

### Carcassonne journey

Cirlot made a brief journey to **Carcassonne**, attracted by its medieval associations.

**Reported incident and self-created mythology:** The journey disappointed his expectation of an initiatory encounter. He injured his hand on a light bulb and interpreted the wound in relation to the wounded Grail king. The physical accident is reported in family and biographical accounts; its Grail significance was Cirlot’s symbolic interpretation.

His travel was generally limited. Compared with his vast imaginary geography, he spent most of his life in Barcelona, apart from Zaragoza, Paris, Carcassonne, and professional journeys within Spain or nearby Europe. Claims that he was a world traveler would be false.

## Bronwyn and the Final Poetic Phase, 1966–1972

In 1966 Cirlot saw Franklin J. Schaffner’s film *The War Lord* (*El señor de la guerra*), starring Charlton Heston and Rosemary Forsyth.

Forsyth played **Bronwyn**, a Welsh woman who emerges from water in a medieval landscape. Cirlot experienced the image as a revelation: woman, water, return, lost beloved, and medieval otherworld converged.

### Nature of the Bronwyn relationship

Bronwyn was:

- A fictional film character
- Embodied on screen by Rosemary Forsyth
- A symbolic woman emerging from water
- A lost or impossible beloved
- A guide toward death, transcendence, and the “other world”
- A phonetic object whose name could be permuted

She was not a documented personal lover. Accounts calling Cirlot “in love with Rosemary Forsyth” describe a cinematic or imaginative fixation, not an established relationship.

Cirlot reportedly wrote to Forsyth and to actor **Laurence Olivier**, but the extent and reciprocity of these contacts require study in the archive.

### The cycle

Beginning in 1967, Cirlot produced an extensive sequence including:

- *Bronwyn*
- *Bronwyn II*
- *Bronwyn III*
- *Bronwyn IV*
- *Bronwyn V*
- *Bronwyn, x*
- *Bronwyn, y*
- *Con Bronwyn*
- *Bronwyn, permutaciones*
- *Bronwyn, n²*
- *Bronwyn, w*
- *La quête de Bronwyn*
- Other associated poems, transformations, and posthumously collected texts

The cycle was often privately printed in extremely small editions. Cirlot’s statement that Spain “hides and denies” him reflected his sense that his most radical poetry had virtually no public.

### Inger Stevens

The death by barbiturate poisoning of Swedish-American actress **Inger Stevens** in 1970 generated another poetic sequence:

- *Inger Stevens. In memoriam*
- *A Inger Stevens*
- *Inger, permutaciones*
- *Inger* / phonovisual variations

Again, this was a symbolic and cinematic relationship, not a documented personal acquaintance. Her name became material for visual, sonic, and permutational poetry.

### Other late work

Late poetry included:

- *Regina tenebrarum*
- *Las oraciones oscuras*
- *Cosmogonía*
- *Hamlet*
- *Orfeo*
- *Los restos negros*
- *V canto de la vida muerta*
- *Donde nada lo nunca ni*
- *44 sonetos de amor*
- *Perséfone*

His late work became more concentrated, fragmented, musical, and eschatological.

## Political Activity and Ideological Position

Cirlot did not hold elected office, direct a political party, or pursue a conventional political career. His politics must be reconstructed from military history, correspondence, journalism, friendships, and publication venues.

### Falangism and Francoism

**Reported fact:** Painter Modest Cuixart later called Cirlot an “orthodox Falangist.” Other sources describe him as Falangist in youth, conservative in maturity, reactionary, anticommunist, or a “Francoist eccentric.”

**Documented complications:**

- He served in the Republican army through mobilization.
- He later served in the Nationalist army.
- An intermediary trying to secure approval for *Nebiros* invoked his loyalty to the regime.
- He published in periodicals associated with Falangist or right-wing cultural networks.
- His art criticism defended Surrealism, abstraction, and artists whom conservative Francoist culture often distrusted.
- Francoist censorship prohibited *Nebiros*.
- His unconventional religious thought did not fit National Catholicism.
- He did not join the major institutional “families” of the regime in any clearly documented official capacity.
- His daughter Victoria states that he was never involved in the Franco regime.
- Luis María Anson later depicted him as hostile to Franco and prepared to sacrifice himself for freedom; this retrospective personal tribute conflicts sharply with descriptions of him as Francoist and is not sufficient by itself to resolve the matter.

**Assessment:** It is documented that Cirlot held strongly anticommunist, hierarchical, militarized, and antimodern attitudes compatible with parts of the radical right. A formal and continuous role within Falange or the Francoist state has not been established from the sources consulted. “Falangist” is supported as an associate’s characterization and possible youthful or ideological identification, but “Franco official” or “regime functionary” is unsupported.

### National Socialism, “Nordism,” and Rudolf Hess

This is the most serious disputed element of Cirlot’s reputation.

#### Documented facts

- Cirlot expressed aesthetic attraction to Germanic or “Nordic” imagery.
- He collected weapons and idealized warrior symbolism.
- He wrote “Destrucción de Rudolf Hess,” published in *La Vanguardia* on 2 June 1967.
- The article supported or sympathized with the campaign for Hess’s release from Spandau.
- He published poems in publications identified by historian Xavier Casals as neo-Nazi.
- CEDADE, the Spanish neo-Nazi organization, mentioned him favorably in its magazine after his death.
- A CEDADE-related “Freedom for Rudolf Hess” campaign operated between 1967 and 1971, and Casals describes Cirlot’s involvement as a “relative approach” or proximity to the organization.
- In correspondence concerning poems he considered unpublishable because of proximity to Nazi aesthetics, Cirlot reportedly wrote that he did not approve Nazi crimes and did not identify politically with Hitlerism, but could not avoid attraction to “Nordism.”

#### Disputed characterizations

- Calling him a **Nazi activist** goes beyond the available evidence.
- Calling his attraction purely antiquarian or apolitical understates his public intervention on behalf of Hess and publication in extremist venues.
- No evidence was found that he belonged formally to the NSDAP, CEDADE, or an equivalent organization.
- No evidence was found that he advocated the Holocaust, racial extermination, or political violence.
- No evidence was found of his participation in Nazi German government or military structures.
- His lifespan and residence make direct participation in German National Socialism unlikely, but this does not settle the nature of his later sympathies.

#### Counter-evidence and family interpretation

Victoria Cirlot emphasizes:

- His enthusiasm for Jewish composers Gustav Mahler and Arnold Schoenberg
- His study of Hebrew Kabbalah, Abraham Abulafia, and the *Zohar*
- His defense of *entartete Kunst*, the modern art condemned by the Nazis
- His attraction to both Rome and Carthage as symbolic opposites
- His habitual *coincidentia oppositorum*, in which apparently contradictory identifications coexisted

This is relevant counter-evidence to a simple antisemitic or doctrinal-Nazi classification, but admiration for Jewish culture would not automatically disprove every possible far-right sympathy.

Historian Ferran Gallego argues that Cirlot was not a fascist in the ordinary political sense but inhabited an antimodern intellectual universe comparable to Ezra Pound, René Guénon, Julius Evola, and Ernst Jünger.

**Best-supported conclusion:** Cirlot entertained and publicly expressed politically consequential admiration for elements of Germanic, militarist, and Hess-related mythology; he came into limited proximity with Spanish neo-Nazi circles. The available evidence does not establish doctrinal Nazi membership, participation in Nazi crimes, or systematic antisemitism. His precise political self-definition remains contested.

### Catalan identity

Cirlot’s Spanish-language writing, military family background, anticommunism, and stated “lack of Catalanness” distanced him from increasingly Catalanist cultural circles.

This contributed to tension with figures including Joan Brossa and possibly to his later marginalization. It would nevertheless be inaccurate to remove him from Catalan cultural history: his professional and artistic life was rooted in Barcelona, Dau al Set, Catalan publishing, and Catalan modern art.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Adversarial Findings

### 1. Falangist identification

- **Claim:** Cirlot was an “orthodox Falangist.”
- **Source and circulation:** Modest Cuixart, quoted in later scholarship.
- **Evidence for:** Anticommunism, militarism, right-wing cultural publications, assertions of loyalty to the regime during the *Nebiros* appeal.
- **Evidence against a simple formulation:** No documented office or sustained party function; censorship of his novel; heterodox spirituality and defense of avant-garde art; family denial of regime involvement.
- **Status:** **Disputed characterization with substantial contextual support but incomplete organizational evidence.**

### 2. Nazi or neo-Nazi affiliation

- **Claim:** Cirlot was a Nazi or neo-Nazi.
- **Evidence for a relationship:** Hess article; poems in neo-Nazi venues; CEDADE’s posthumous notice; Nordic and military aesthetics; reported correspondence about Nazi-adjacent poetic imagery.
- **Evidence against the strongest claim:** Explicit disavowal of Nazi crimes and Hitlerian politics; defense of artists condemned by Nazis; engagement with Jewish music and mysticism; no demonstrated formal membership or operational activity.
- **Status:** **Documented ideological and publishing proximity; formal Nazi affiliation unproven.**

### 3. Rudolf Hess campaign

- **Claim:** Cirlot demanded freedom for Rudolf Hess.
- **Evidence:** His 1967 *La Vanguardia* article and his connection to the campaign discussed by Casals.
- **Status:** **Documented fact.**
- **Interpretive caution:** Advocacy for Hess’s release does not by itself prove agreement with every Nazi policy, but it is not politically neutral.

### 4. Ghostwriting

- **Claim:** Cirlot worked as a literary ghostwriter.
- **Evidence:** Reported by Rivero Taravillo and repeated in the *La Vanguardia* profile.
- **Missing evidence:** A verified title list, contracts, or manuscript comparison.
- **Status:** **Reported fact; specific attributions unresolved.**

### 5. Suppression by Francoism

- **Claim:** The Franco regime persecuted or systematically suppressed Cirlot.
- **Evidence for:** *Nebiros* was banned twice; his heterodox art and spirituality did not fit official orthodoxies.
- **Evidence against the strongest version:** He maintained steady publishing employment, wrote in major newspapers, published many books, and was not documented as imprisoned or banned comprehensively.
- **Status:** **Specific censorship documented; generalized lifelong persecution overstated without further evidence.**

### 6. Literary exclusion

- **Claim:** Political hostility alone kept him out of the canon.
- **Evidence:** His political ambiguity alienated left and Catalanist circles; his heterodoxy also sat uneasily with National Catholic culture.
- **Other factors:** Tiny private editions, extreme formal difficulty, refusal of social realism, destruction of manuscripts, lack of university affiliation, and the dispersal of his work.
- **Status:** **Multicausal scholarly interpretation.**

### 7. Criminality, finance, and intelligence

Dedicated searches combining his name with scandal, allegations, lawsuits, fraud, corruption, conspiracy, crime, and recent 2024–2026 material produced no credible evidence of:

- Criminal charges or conviction
- Financial fraud or corruption
- Money laundering
- Intelligence-service affiliation
- Political violence
- Abuse allegations
- Addiction-related scandal
- Civil litigation of biographical importance

The absence of results is not proof that no private dispute ever occurred, but no significant documented case was found.

### Recent adversarial scholarship

The most relevant new scholarship found in the last two years was Alicia López Latorre’s 2024 study of Cirlot’s antimodern synthesis of “arms and letters.” It does not disclose a new scandal but strengthens the documentary case that militarism and the warrior ideal were structural, not incidental, in his thought.

A 2024–2025 publication of *La muerte de Gerión* and recent museum programming demonstrate continued scholarly recovery rather than newly documented misconduct.

## Illness, Psychological Crises, and Death

### Psychological life

Cirlot’s writing contains despair, death fixation, negation, apocalyptic imagery, erotic obsession, and intense identification with screen figures. These are literary and biographical data, not sufficient grounds for retrospective psychiatric diagnosis.

No reliable evidence was found of:

- Psychiatric hospitalization
- A diagnosed psychotic or mood disorder
- Substance addiction
- Suicide attempt

His recording of dreams and “imaginal” experiences was deliberate intellectual and poetic practice. Describing it as mental illness without clinical evidence would be speculative.

### Cancer

In 1971 Cirlot was diagnosed with pancreatic cancer and underwent surgery.

He continued writing during his illness, including late *Bronwyn* works and *Perséfone*. His preoccupation with death long predated the diagnosis, but the final poems acquired an unavoidable terminal context.

He died at home on Carrer Hercegovina, Barcelona, on 11 May 1973, aged fifty-seven.

No competing cause of death was found in reliable sources.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Self-Created Mythology

### The “seventh face of the die”

- **Story:** Cirlot was the seventh, anomalous member of Dau al Set.
- **Origin:** The group’s name means the seventh face of a die; later writers applied the phrase to Cirlot.
- **Evidence:** He joined the circle and became one of the magazine’s most active contributors, but was not consistently named among the original 1948 nucleus.
- **Effect:** It made his marginality appear predestined and symbolically encoded.
- **Status:** **Symbolic retrospective characterization grounded in a real association.**

### The solitary “poet of the no-world”

- **Story:** Cirlot lived almost wholly in an interior universe, unrecognized by Spain.
- **Evidence for:** Small editions, isolation from dominant poetic movements, letters complaining of neglect, the late recovery of his poetry.
- **Evidence against literal isolation:** He worked for a major publisher, published extensively, maintained artist networks, lectured, and contributed over one hundred newspaper articles.
- **Effect:** Produced the modern cult image of the hidden initiate or “accursed” Spanish poet.
- **Status:** **Reputational mythology with a substantial factual core.**

### The room of swords

- **Story:** His Barcelona room was an armory, observatory, Egyptian chamber, or submarine descending into dreams.
- **Evidence:** Photographs, family testimony, his collection, and his poem “Momento.”
- **Effect:** The room became a physical emblem of his mind and the basis of the 2011 exhibition *La habitación imaginaria*.
- **Status:** **Documented environment transformed by poetic self-description.**

### Carcassonne as failed initiation

- **Story:** Cirlot traveled to Carcassonne seeking medieval revelation, failed to find it, cut his hand on a light bulb, and interpreted the wound as that of the Grail king.
- **Evidence:** Biographical and family accounts; no contemporaneous medical or travel record was consulted.
- **Effect:** Exemplifies his conversion of accident into symbolic destiny.
- **Status:** **Reported incident plus documented-style self-mythologizing.**

### Bronwyn as “the beloved from another life”

- **Story:** On seeing Bronwyn emerge from the water, Cirlot recognized a woman loved in another existence.
- **Evidence:** His writings describe the cinematic image in revelatory and mythic terms.
- **Against literal interpretation:** Bronwyn was fictional; no evidence establishes reincarnational memory or a relationship with Forsyth.
- **Effect:** Generated his principal poetic cycle and the most durable legend around him.
- **Status:** **Self-created spiritual-poetic mythology.**

### The destroyed composer

- **Story:** Cirlot burned or destroyed his music because he suddenly recognized it as imitation of Schoenberg.
- **Evidence:** Repeated by specialists; surviving copies show that destruction was substantial but not total.
- **Effect:** Turns abandonment of composition into a ritual sacrifice preceding poetic rebirth.
- **Status:** **Reported biographical incident, narratively mythologized.**

### “Maldito” or accursed poet

- **Story:** Cirlot was Spain’s hidden twentieth-century *poète maudit*.
- **Origin:** Posthumous journalism, cult readership, rarity of his editions, political ambiguity, and dark imagery.
- **Evidence against the strongest version:** He was not wholly unpublished, outlawed, destitute, or unknown during life.
- **Effect:** Helped revive readership while sometimes simplifying his substantial professional success.
- **Status:** **Posthumous reputational mythology.**

- Aesthetic theory
- Publication materials
- Evidence of his working methods and personal networks

Because he destroyed much earlier material, it is not a complete life archive.

#### Archivo General de la Administración, Alcalá de Henares

Contains the censorship file and marked copy of *Nebiros*, documenting the state’s refusal to permit publication.

#### Carlos Edmundo de Ory archive

Contains correspondence and materials sent by Cirlot, including the surviving copy of *Suite atonal* and evidence concerning their literary relationship.

#### Spanish state authority records

PARES and the Biblioteca Nacional de España maintain authority and bibliographic records. PARES also links Cirlot-related material in the Archivo de la Corona de Aragón.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

At his death in 1973, Cirlot was respected as an art critic and symbolist but remained a marginal poet. His poetry’s small print runs, formal extremity, and refusal of generational categories obstructed circulation.

### Phases of recovery

1. **Immediate memorialization, 1973–1981:** Obituaries, memorial issues, Leopoldo Azancot’s 1974 selection, and Clara Janés’s 1981 edition.
2. **Critical consolidation, 1980s–1990s:** Studies of music, Surrealism, Kabbalah, Bronwyn, and symbolic form; publication of dreams and unpublished prose.
3. **1996 institutional recovery:** IVAM’s *El mundo de Juan Eduardo Cirlot*, centering him within postwar Spanish art and literature.
4. **Collected poetry, 2001–2008:** Siruela’s *Bronwyn*, *En la llama*, and *Del no mundo* made the poetic corpus more accessible.
5. **Archival phase after 2009:** Deposit at the MNAC enabled documentary study.
6. **2011 imaginary-room exhibition:** His domestic symbolic world became an exhibition subject.
7. **2016 centenary:** Rivero Taravillo’s biography and the recovery of *Nebiros* expanded public interest.
8. **2020s:** New studies emphasize his art criticism, agrammatical poetry, antimodernism, militarism, and continuing influence.

### Influence

His legacy lies in several partly independent fields:

- The *Dictionary of Symbols*, read internationally by artists, writers, psychologists, occultists, and general readers
- Early interpretation and promotion of Tàpies, Cuixart, Miró, and Spanish informalism
- A distinctive postwar Spanish Surrealism not subordinate to social realism
- Permutational and phonovisual poetry
- The convergence of Kabbalah, serial music, and poetic form
- Renewed interest in the imaginal and symbolic in modern literature
- Influence on poets and critics including Clara Janés, Jaime D. Parra, and later experimental writers

The dictionary remains much more widely known than his poetry. Specialists increasingly regard this imbalance as historically misleading because Cirlot himself treated symbol studies, criticism, music, and poetry as aspects of one project.

### Critical reinterpretation

Older accounts tended to choose among incompatible images:

- Surrealist
- Fascist or Falangist
- Christian mystic
- Nihilist
- Art historian
- Occultist
- Failed composer
- Hidden major poet

Recent scholarship treats those tensions as structurally related. That approach does not erase political responsibility; it distinguishes his documented acts from totalizing labels.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by consulted sources are included. Acknowledged intellectual influence qualifies; mere contemporaneity does not.

- **Abraham ben Samuel Abulafia — Documented intellectual influence.** Cirlot used Abulafia’s Kabbalistic letter permutation as a model for poetic combinatorics, especially in the *Bronwyn*, *Inger*, and Bécquer works. Jaime D. Parra published a dedicated study, “Juan Eduardo Cirlot y Abraham Abulafia,” in 1996.

- **Achille-Claude Debussy — No specific supported connection found.** Cirlot wrote extensively on music, but the consulted sources did not establish a distinctive acknowledged debt to Debussy.

- **André Breton — Documented personal and intellectual connection.** Cirlot met Breton in Paris in October 1949, corresponded with him, dedicated *Lilith* to him, and engaged deeply with Surrealism. He rejected Breton’s political and atheistic orthodoxy and reportedly declined closer formal incorporation into the movement.

- **Antonin Artaud — Documented literary influence and translation activity.** In Zaragoza, Cirlot and Alfonso Buñuel engaged with or translated poetry by Artaud and other French Surrealists. No personal meeting is documented.

- **Antoni Gaudí i Cornet — Documented intellectual and critical connection, posthumous.** Gaudí died when Cirlot was ten; no meeting is established. Cirlot wrote *El arte de Gaudí* and *Introducción a la arquitectura de Gaudí*, treated Gaudí as a precursor of the avant-garde, and at one point lived in La Pedrera.

- **Carl Gustav Jung — Documented intellectual influence.** Cirlot explicitly cited Jung’s studies of alchemy and depth psychology in explaining the intellectual basis of the *Diccionario de símbolos*.

- **Edgar Allan Poe — Documented literary influence.** Cirlot wrote on Poe, and later scholarship has studied their shared symbolic negativity. No personal connection was possible.

- **Federico García Lorca — No documentary personal connection found.** Both belonged to Spanish literary history, but no supported meeting, correspondence, or explicit formative link appeared in the consulted sources.

- **Friedrich Nietzsche — Documented intellectual influence.** Critics trace *Del no mundo* and Cirlot’s nihilism partly to Nietzsche. This is an intellectual reception, not a personal relationship.

- **Igor Fyodorovich Stravinsky — Documented intellectual and musical influence.** Cirlot dedicated an ode to Stravinsky and published a book-length study, *Ígor Strawinsky*, in 1949. No personal meeting was found.

- **Jorge Luis Borges — No supported personal connection found.** Similar interests in symbols, Kabbalah, and labyrinths do not establish contact.

- **Julius Evola — Reported intellectual affinity; direct relationship unproven.** Ferran Gallego places Cirlot in an antimodern intellectual universe comparable to Evola, Pound, Guénon, and Jünger. The consulted sources do not establish correspondence or a meeting.

- **Mircea Eliade — Documented intellectual influence.** Cirlot named Eliade among the important comparative-religion authorities informing the *Dictionary of Symbols*. No personal contact was found.

- **Pablo Ruiz Picasso — Documented critical connection; personal meeting unresolved.** Cirlot discovered Picasso’s importance in the 1930s, wrote repeatedly about him, and published *Picasso, el nacimiento de un genio* in 1972. The sources consulted do not clearly establish a personal relationship.

- **Ramon Llull — Documented intellectual and cultural influence, posthumous.** Llull was among the medieval and combinatorial figures valued in the Dau al Set environment and Cirlot’s symbolic world. No direct biographical relationship was possible.

- **René Guénon — Documented intellectual influence.** Cirlot explicitly cited Guénon in the conceptual background to his symbol dictionary. The relation was textual; no correspondence was found.

- **William Blake — Documented literary affinity and influence.** Scholars describe a Blakean visionary and liturgical strain in Cirlot’s poetry. This is posthumous reception rather than personal contact.

- **Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart — No distinctive documentary connection found.** General musical knowledge is insufficient for an atlas connection.

- **Wilhelm Richard Wagner — Documented subject of reflection.** Cirlot discussed Wagner, Christianity, Buddhism, and *Parsifal* in a 1968 Holy Thursday article. This is an intellectual reception, not a personal link.

- **Zoroaster — No specific supported connection found.** Persian and dualist materials appear in Cirlot’s broad mythographic range, but no clear, source-supported Zoroastrian influence was established in this research.

No other atlas-roster connection met the documentary threshold. In particular, shared residence in Barcelona, common interest in occultism, or thematic similarity was not treated as proof of a relationship.

## Chronology

- **1916, 9 April:** Born in Barcelona to Juan Cirlot Nieto and María Laporta Blanco.
- **1920s–early 1930s:** Educated by Jesuits on Carrer Casp; develops interests in Rome and Egypt.
- **1930s:** Studies commerce, bookkeeping, piano, and composition; works in a customs agency and Banco Hispano Americano.
- **1936:** Attends the International Society for Contemporary Music festival in Barcelona; identifies Picasso, Surrealism, Stravinsky, Webern, and Scriabin among decisive discoveries.
- **1937:** Mobilized into the Republican army.
- **1938–1939:** Reportedly changes zones or sides; briefly detained before eventual Nationalist service. Exact sequence unresolved.
- **1940:** Mobilized by the Francoist army; stationed in Zaragoza.
- **1940–1943:** Joins Zaragoza intellectual circles; friendships with Pilar Bayona and Alfonso Buñuel; encounters French Surrealism.
- **1942:** *Vals* performed in Zaragoza.
- **1943:** Publishes *La muerte de Gerión* and *Seis sonetos y un poema del amor celeste*; returns to Barcelona.
- **1944:** Publishes *Oda a Ígor Strawinsky y otros versos*.
- **1945:** Publishes *Árbol agónico* and *En la llama*.
- **1946:** Publishes *Canto de la vida muerta*, *Cordero del abismo*, and *Donde las lilas crecen*.
- **1947:** Marries Gloria Valenzuela; joins Círculo Manuel de Falla; composes a version of *Suite atonal*; meets Tàpies and Cuixart around the Salons d’Octubre.
- **1948:** Publishes *El poeta conmemorativo*; composes *Concertino*; Dau al Set formed.
- **1949:** Daughter Lourdes born; joins Dau al Set circle; publishes *Diccionario de los ismos*, *Ígor Strawinsky*, *Joan Miró*, *Lilith*, and other books; travels to Paris and meets André Breton; begins period of study with Marius Schneider.
- **1950:** Writes *Nebiros*; publishes *El arte de Gaudí*, *Ferias y atracciones*, and *Ontología*; dates *Sonet*.
- **1951:** *Nebiros* rejected by Francoist censorship; begins employment at Gustavo Gili; publishes *La pintura abstracta* and *80 sueños*.
- **1952–1955:** Produces major books on modern art, Surrealism, Romanesque art, and symbolic mythology.
- **1954:** Publishes *El ojo en la mitología*; writes “Mis espadas”; joins Academia del Faro de San Cristóbal.
- **1955:** Publishes *La pintura surrealista*, *Del expresionismo a la abstracción*, and *El palacio de plata*.
- **1955:** Daughter Victoria born.
- **1957:** Publishes *El arte otro*, *Cubismo y figuración*, and *La escultura del siglo XX*.
- **1958:** Publishes *Diccionario de símbolos tradicionales* and major writing on Cuixart and contemporary art; begins contributions to *Goya* and *Papeles de Son Armadans*.
- **1960:** Publishes *El informalismo*, *Tàpies*, *Subirachs*, and *Román Vallés*.
- **Early 1960s:** Close friendship with Antoni Tàpies breaks down.
- **1961:** Publishes *Pintura catalana contemporánea*; begins highly productive period at *La Vanguardia*.
- **1962:** Routledge publishes *A Dictionary of Symbols* with introduction by Herbert Read.
- **1965:** Publishes *Nuevas tendencias pictóricas* and *El espíritu abstracto desde la Prehistoria a la Edad Media*.
- **1966:** Sees *The War Lord*; the figure of Bronwyn transforms his poetry.
- **1967:** Begins publishing the *Bronwyn* cycle; publishes “Destrucción de Rudolf Hess.”
- **1968–1969:** Develops permutational poetry and *Del no mundo*; describes his poetic hermeticism publicly.
- **1970:** Publishes numerous privately printed late works, including *Bronwyn, permutaciones*, *Orfeo*, *Los restos negros*, and *Inger Stevens. In memoriam*.
- **1971:** Diagnosed with pancreatic cancer and operated on; continues *Bronwyn*, *Inger*, Bécquer, and “no-world” sequences.
- **1972:** Publishes *Picasso, el nacimiento de un genio* and *La pintura de Montserrat Gudiol*; archive record ends around this year.
- **1973, 11 May:** Dies at his home on Carrer Hercegovina, Barcelona.
- **1974:** Leopoldo Azancot edits *Poesía de J. E. Cirlot (1966–1972)*.
- **1981:** Clara Janés edits *Obra poética*.
- **1986:** *El mundo del objeto bajo la luz del surrealismo* published posthumously.
- **1988:** Complete *88 sueños* published.
- **1996:** IVAM stages *El mundo de Juan Eduardo Cirlot*; renewed publication and controversy over his politics.
- **2001–2008:** Siruela issues the three principal collected-poetry volumes.
- **2009:** Lourdes and Victoria Cirlot deposit the personal archive at the MNAC.
- **2011–2012:** Arts Santa Mònica stages *La habitación imaginaria*.
- **2016:** Centenary; publication of Rivero Taravillo’s biography and the recovered *Nebiros*.
- **2020:** Publication of *Se parece el dolor a un gran espacio*, collecting informalism writings.
- **2023:** Fiftieth anniversary of his death prompts exhibitions, new editions, and reassessment.
- **2024:** New scholarship examines the structural role of militarism and antimodernism in his work.
- **2026:** Cirlot remains internationally known primarily for the symbol dictionary, with his poetry and political complexity receiving continuing specialist reassessment.
