# Clare of Assisi: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 24 August 2026  
**Scope:** Documentary reconstruction of Clare's life, writings, institutions, legends, cult, and reception. Dates before her departure from home are often approximate. Hagiographic claims are identified as such.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Name:** Clare of Assisi; Italian **Chiara d'Assisi**; Latin **Clara Assisiensis** or simply **Clara**.
- **Birth name:** Clare/Chiara, daughter of Favarone of Offreduccio. Medieval usage did not give her a modern hereditary surname. Forms such as *Chiara Offreduccio* are convenient later constructions.
- **Titles during life:** sister; abbess (*abbatissa*) of San Damiano (an office she said she accepted reluctantly); “mother” and “lady” in the community and correspondence.
- **Posthumous titles:** Saint Clare; virgin; foundress or co-foundress of the Poor Ladies/Poor Sisters of San Damiano, subsequently the Order of Saint Clare (Poor Clares); “little plant of blessed Francis” (*plantula beatissimi patris Francisci*), Clare's own expression in her Testament.
- **Birth:** probably **1193 or 1194**, Assisi, Duchy of Spoleto, Papal States. A traditional exact date, **16 July 1194**, is not established by contemporary evidence.
- **Death:** **11 August 1253**, San Damiano outside Assisi, after a prolonged disabling illness; approximately 59–60.
- **Canonization:** proclaimed by Pope Alexander IV at Anagni in **1255** (the bull *Clara claris praeclara* is dated 26 September 1255). Her liturgical feast is now 11 August; older calendars commonly used 12 August.
- **Principal residences:** family house near the cathedral of San Rufino, Assisi; probable family refuge in Perugia during Assisi's civil conflict; brief stays at San Paolo delle Abbadesse near Bastia and Sant'Angelo di Panzo; San Damiano, c. 1212–1253.
- **Core achievement:** she secured papal confirmation on 9 August 1253 of a rule preserving corporate non-possession and Franciscan identity for her community. It is commonly described as the first surviving rule composed by a woman for women to receive papal approval; the precise degree of Clare's sole authorship versus collaborative redaction cannot be measured.

## Evidence Base and Source Criticism

The evidence is unusually rich for a thirteenth-century religious woman but uneven:

1. **Clare's writings:** four letters to Agnes of Prague, her Rule/Form of Life, Testament, and Blessing. The letters and Rule are secure; the Testament and Blessing have generated authenticity debates, although substantial modern scholarship defends them. Clare almost certainly worked with scribes and inherited Franciscan, Benedictine, and curial language.
2. **Papal and cardinalatial documents:** rules, privileges, permissions, and letters from Honorius III, Gregory IX, Innocent IV, Cardinal Hugolino, and Cardinal Rainaldo. These establish institutional conflicts more securely than later narrative does.
3. **Canonization process:** Bishop Bartolomeo of Spoleto examined witnesses at San Damiano and San Paolo on 28–29 November 1253. The surviving Latin is a later translation of an original vernacular record, but it preserves named testimony from sisters, relatives, and townspeople. Questions were designed to establish sanctity and miracles, not neutral biography.
4. **Official Legend of Saint Clare:** commissioned after canonization and completed around 1255–56, traditionally attributed to Thomas of Celano. Its author remains debated. It reshaped testimony into a saintly model and sometimes suppresses female agency or institutional friction.
5. **Clare's Rule versus later order history:** “Poor Clares” is retrospectively convenient. During Clare's life, houses grouped under curial regulation varied widely; not all followed Clare personally or adopted her economic settlement.

## Family and Ancestry

Clare belonged to Assisi's knightly aristocracy, the **maiores**, rather than Francis's merchant class.

- **Father:** **Favarone**, son of Offreduccio. The canonization record and papal *Sicut manifestum* identify Clare as his daughter. Narrative traditions portray the household as wealthy and fortified. The evidence does not support elaborate modern pedigrees with confidence.
- **Mother:** **Ortolana** (Hortulana). The canonization process says that she made pilgrimages overseas, to Rome, and to Monte Sant'Angelo, and eventually entered San Damiano. Accounts that she prophetically heard a voice before Clare's birth belong to hagiography.
- **Sisters:** **Caterina**, renamed **Agnes** after joining Clare in 1212; and **Beatrice**, who later entered San Damiano and testified in 1253. Some reconstructions posit additional siblings, but the evidence is uncertain.
- **Uncle:** **Monaldo**, remembered in the canonization testimony as heading armed male relatives who tried to remove Agnes from religious life.
- **Marriage and children:** none. Clare rejected proposed marriage and professed celibacy.
- **Pica Bourlemont:** no documented familial relationship to Clare. Pica is the traditional name of **Francis of Assisi's mother**. “Pica de Bourlemont,” French/Provençal noblewoman, and “Giovanna” are later genealogical traditions of uncertain foundation. Pica therefore belongs only indirectly to Clare's network, as the mother of Francis; no reliable source documents a Clare–Pica relationship or even a meeting.

## Childhood and Early Life (c. 1193/94–1211)

Clare grew up near San Rufino in a household with servants, property, and the expectations of noble marriage. Witnesses recalled that as a girl she distributed food to the poor, reserved delicacies for them, wore rough cloth beneath fine clothing, prayed, and counted prayers with small stones. These recollections were recorded forty years later in a canonization inquiry and are simultaneously biographical memories and sanctity topoi.

Assisi experienced violent class and factional conflict. The communal uprising of 1198 displaced noble families; Clare's family probably lived for a time in **Perugia**, although the exact dates and Clare's movements are reconstructed rather than directly documented. The later war between Assisi and Perugia (including the 1202 battle at Collestrada, where Francis was captured) formed the political background of her youth.

Her education was domestic and religious. Her sophisticated biblical imagery and capacity to govern do not prove formal Latin schooling. She likely heard Scripture and liturgy in Latin, received instruction from household clergy and women, and later used secretaries. She could participate in aristocratic domestic management, textile work, almsgiving, and patronage.

## Education and Formation

### Religious and textual formation

- Gospels, especially the poverty, humility, Incarnation, and Passion of Christ.
- Psalms, liturgy, Marian and virginity traditions, and the monastic inheritance associated with Benedict.
- Penitential and apostolic movements flourishing in central Italy.
- Francis and his brothers' experimental evangelical life.
- Institutional formation through Cardinal Hugolino/Gregory IX and later papal legislation, which Clare sometimes accepted tactically and sometimes resisted.

### Francis as guide—and limits of the “disciple” model

The canonization process says Francis first sought contact through Brother Philip; Clare then met Francis secretly, accompanied by **Bona di Guelfuccio**. Her Testament says that through Francis God showed her the path and that he gave the women a short form of life and a last will on poverty. Francis was guide, collaborator, and guarantor. Clare was not simply passive: she governed for four decades, negotiated with popes, advised Agnes of Prague, and created a durable female constitutional text.

## Chronological Life History

### Conversion and flight from home (1211–1212)

After meetings with Francis, Clare left the family house on the night following Palm Sunday. The traditional date is **18–19 March 1212**, but chronological calculations also permit 1211; scholarship remains divided. The official Legend dramatizes departure through a blocked “door of the dead,” a detail not independently verifiable.

At the **Portiuncula/Santa Maria degli Angeli**, Francis and the friars cut her hair and clothed her in penitential religious dress. Tonsure was a public juridical and symbolic crossing from marriageable noblewoman to religious. Francis placed her first at the Benedictine house **San Paolo delle Abbadesse**. Male relatives attempted to retrieve her. The Legend reports that she seized the altar cloth and revealed her shorn head, invoking sanctuary and consecration; this is plausible in legal-symbolic terms but survives as hagiographic narrative.

She then moved to **Sant'Angelo di Panzo**. Caterina followed roughly sixteen days later. Relatives violently attempted to carry Caterina away; the canonization process contains eyewitness claims that her body became impossibly heavy and that Clare's prayer weakened Uncle Monaldo's raised arm. Francis later named Caterina **Agnes**. The violence is supported by early testimony; miraculous mechanics remain faith claims.

### San Damiano and the first community (c. 1212–1216)

Francis settled Clare and Agnes at **San Damiano**, the restored church outside Assisi. Women joined, including Pacifica di Guelfuccio, Benvenuta da Perugia, Philippa, Amata, Balvina, and eventually Clare's mother and sister Beatrice. The community called itself Poor Sisters or Poor Ladies and relied on alms collected partly by serving sisters and friars.

Clare resisted becoming abbess but accepted the office around **1215** under pressure from Francis. “Abbess” gave the community canonical legibility but imported Benedictine hierarchy. Testimony emphasizes that she washed sisters' feet, nursed the sick, worked cloth, and avoided privileges of rank.

The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) restricted new religious orders and pushed communities toward approved rules. Clare's house therefore existed in tension between Francis's brief *forma vitae* and Benedictine-curial legal structures.

### Privilege of Poverty and curial regulation (1216–1227)

Clare sought authorization not to be compelled to possess property. Tradition attributes an initial **Privilege of Poverty** to Innocent III in 1216 and says he drafted it personally. No original survives, and its exact historicity/form is debated. A secure version is Gregory IX's *Sicut manifestum* of **17 September 1228**, addressed to “Clare, daughter of Favarone,” and her companions, confirming that no one could force them to receive possessions.

Cardinal **Hugolino of Ostia** (future Gregory IX) became protector of female houses associated with the Franciscan movement. His rule, based on Benedictine profession and rigorous enclosure, treated the houses as a regulated order and allowed landed endowment. Clare used parts of this framework but defended San Damiano's special non-possession.

**Jacques de Vitry's 1216 letter** reports that “lesser sisters” lived together near cities and supported themselves by labor, an important external witness to the broader phenomenon, although whether every detail describes Clare's San Damiano specifically is debated.

### Francis's illness, death, and Clare's independence (1225–1228)

Francis, ill and nearly blind, stayed near San Damiano in 1225; tradition connects this period with the *Canticle of the Creatures*. His surviving *Canticle of Exhortation for the Ladies of San Damiano* addresses Clare's community. Clare is sometimes said to have nursed him personally; proximity is secure, precise actions less so.

Francis died on **3 October 1226**. His body was carried past San Damiano so Clare and the sisters could see and mourn him. The official Legend's emotional scene reflects both historical practice and hagiographic composition.

Without Francis, Clare confronted friars and churchmen over pastoral care and poverty. Gregory IX reportedly urged her to accept property because of dangerous times; Clare replied that she desired absolution from sins, not from following Christ. The exchange is recorded in the Legend, not a transcript. The documented 1228 privilege nevertheless confirms a real negotiated settlement.

### Agnes of Prague correspondence and international influence (1230s–early 1250s)

**Agnes of Prague**, daughter of King Ottokar I of Bohemia, rejected dynastic marriage and established a hospital and women's monastery. Clare wrote four surviving letters to her, conventionally dated **1234, 1235/36, 1238, and 1253**. They combine political tact, affective kinship, bridal mysticism, “mirror” imagery, and concrete advice on fasting.

Clare encouraged Agnes not to abandon chosen poverty under curial pressure. Yet their situations differed: Agnes's Prague foundation was tied to royal wealth, hospital patronage, and papal negotiation. There is no evidence that they met. Their friendship was epistolary, perhaps mediated by friars.

A letter to **Ermentrude of Bruges** was long attributed to Clare, often divided into two letters. Modern critical editions generally reject it as an authentic composition in its transmitted form, though it may preserve Clarissan material.

### Illness and governance (from c. 1224/25)

Clare suffered a serious chronic illness for approximately the last 28–29 years of her life. Sources describe recurrent confinement to bed, weakness, and inability to attend liturgy, but do not permit diagnosis. Suggestions ranging from tuberculosis to malarial or gastrointestinal disease are speculative. She continued governance from her sickbed.

Her austerity included severe fasting, sleeping on bare ground or vine branches, and haircloth; Francis and the bishop reportedly ordered moderation. These practices can be read both as period asceticism and as behavior damaging to health, but no medical causal finding exists.

### Military crisis and Assisi politics (1240–1241)

During the conflict between Emperor **Frederick II** and the papacy, imperial forces, including Muslim soldiers commonly called “Saracens” in Latin Christian sources, threatened the Assisi area.

- **San Damiano attack, 1240:** sisters testified in 1253 that attackers climbed the enclosure and Clare, sick, had herself carried to the doorway and prayed before a container holding the Eucharist. The attackers withdrew. The early sources describe a small ivory/silver pyx or Eucharistic container, not the large sunburst monstrance of later paintings.
- **Threat to Assisi, 1241:** witnesses credited Clare's prayer with the army's retreat on 22 June.

The regional military threats are historically credible; the interpretation of withdrawal as miracle comes from participant testimony and cult. Later art turns Clare into a civic defender holding a conspicuous monstrance. These episodes made her politically significant to Assisi without making her a party official or military actor.

### The rule struggle, 1247–1253

Innocent IV's **1247 rule** brought the female houses closer to the Friars Minor and allowed communal property. Opposition and impracticality led to modification. A 1250 instruction let houses retain earlier observances. Clare's community pursued its own text.

Cardinal **Rainaldo of Ostia** approved Clare's Form of Life on **16 September 1252**. Pope **Innocent IV**, then at Assisi, confirmed it by *Solet annuere* on **9 August 1253**. The document incorporated the Franciscan profession “to observe the holy Gospel,” governance by an elected abbess and chapter, strict enclosure, work, fasting, care of sick sisters, relations with friars, and the central ban on possessions except the small land required for the monastery's seclusion.

The Rule reflects negotiation, not total institutional victory: it applied directly to San Damiano; it accepted enclosure and ecclesiastical visitation; and later Poor Clare houses followed different rules. Nonetheless it preserved a female community's chosen economic vulnerability against repeated endowment pressure.

### Death and funeral (August 1253)

Innocent IV visited the dying Clare. Agnes returned from Monticelli; Brothers Leo, Angelo, and Juniper appear in death narratives. Clare reportedly addressed her soul and blessed God for creating her. These last words come through hagiographic testimony, not a contemporaneous transcript.

Clare died on **11 August 1253**. Civic authorities, fearing loss or theft of a valuable sacred body, helped move it to **San Giorgio** within Assisi. At the funeral Innocent IV reportedly proposed the Office of Virgins rather than the Office of the Dead; Cardinal Rainaldo counseled normal procedure. The story signals immediate sanctity claims and curial management.

## Institutions, Work, Property, and Financial Structure

Clare had no “career,” company, patents, or investments in modern terms. Her institutional labor was governance, constitutional writing, textile production, pastoral correspondence, and care work.

### San Damiano economy

- Renunciation of individual and corporate property was the defining policy.
- Sisters worked, especially in textiles; the Rule calls work a grace and forbids extinguishing prayer.
- External alms and friars' assistance supplied food and necessities.
- A small perimeter of land was allowed for seclusion and cultivation, revealing the practical boundary of “absolute” poverty.
- The sisters produced altar linens; canonization testimony credits cloth made under Clare's direction and sent to churches.
- The community depended structurally on donors, papal protection, local goodwill, and male clerical service. Non-ownership did not mean absence from an economy.

### Governance

Clare was abbess for most of four decades. Her Rule required consultation with all sisters, weekly chapter, election, a council of discreet sisters, and special provision for the sick. It also maintained class distinctions between choir sisters and serving sisters characteristic of medieval communities.

### Enclosure and agency

Strict enclosure could protect women from family coercion and preserve community autonomy, while also restricting movement and making the house dependent on male intermediaries. Clare defended enclosure and simultaneously disputed external control over property and Franciscan pastoral access. Modern descriptions of her simply as either “liberated” or “imprisoned” flatten this mixed legal reality.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

Clare did not hold civic office or formulate a state program. Her politics were institutional and economic:

- She moved from aristocratic household expectations to voluntary identification with the poor.
- She used papal privileges against both family power and papal/episcopal preferences for endowment.
- She navigated the papal–imperial conflict without documented partisan organizing.
- She transformed Franciscan language through a female, enclosed, communal form.

Her spirituality centered on Christ poor and crucified, the Incarnation, Eucharist, contemplation, and transformation. In the letters to Agnes, Christ is a mirror: one looks, considers, contemplates, and desires to imitate. Bridal imagery is intense but belongs to established Christian mystical language, not evidence of a human sexual relationship.

## Controversies, Disputes, and Questions

No credible evidence links Clare to fraud, corruption, sexual abuse, criminal enterprise, intelligence activity, money laundering, or addiction. Relevant disputes are historical and institutional:

### Family violence

Clare's departure defied planned marriage and household authority. Relatives tried to seize Clare and violently abduct Agnes. Early sources favor the women and cast the family as worldly antagonists; nevertheless, coercive retrieval is well attested within the hagiographic record.

### Conflict with church authorities

Popular accounts present a simple heroic struggle of Clare versus oppressive popes. Documents show a more complex relationship: popes protected the community, imposed enclosure and rules, worried about subsistence, and ultimately approved Clare's settlement. Clare practiced persistent legal resistance from within obedience.

### Authorship

Calling Clare the sole author of every word of the Rule is stronger than the evidence. The text borrows extensively from Francis's 1223 Rule, Hugolino's legislation, Benedictine structures, and curial formulae. Its arrangement, women-specific provisions, poverty clause, and first-person community memory justify attributing decisive authorship to Clare and her circle. Testament authenticity was challenged especially in twentieth-century scholarship; linguistic and manuscript work has strengthened the case for authenticity but has not erased all disagreement.

### Ascetic harm

Her fasting and sleep deprivation were extreme enough that male authorities ordered restraint. Sources praise these practices and do not provide clinical evidence. It is reasonable to identify risk and probable bodily harm, but not to diagnose an eating disorder retrospectively.

### “Foundress of the Poor Clares”

Accurate as a statement of spiritual and constitutional descent, but administratively anachronistic if taken to mean Clare founded and controlled one uniform international order. Curial officials organized multiple houses; Urban IV formally unified the Order of Saint Clare in 1263 under a rule that permitted common property.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### Prophecy before birth

**Story:** Ortolana heard that the unborn child would be a light illuminating the world, explaining the name Clara (“bright”).  
**Appearance/circulation:** official Legend and later hagiography.  
**Evidence:** no contemporary independent record; standard name-prophecy motif.  
**Effect:** established Clare as providentially chosen and made light her chief iconographic symbol.

### Door of the dead

**Story:** on Palm Sunday Clare escaped through a door normally blocked and used for carrying out the dead.  
**Evidence:** official Legend; absent from Clare's own account and difficult to verify archaeologically.  
**Effect:** powerful symbolism of dying to aristocratic life.

### Agnes made supernaturally heavy

**Story:** twelve men could not lift Agnes; Monaldo's arm was paralyzed when he tried to strike her.  
**Evidence:** early sworn testimony preserves reported eyewitness memory, but physical miracle is not independently testable.  
**Effect:** dramatized female vocation defeating patriarchal force.

### The Eucharist repels “Saracens”

**Story:** Clare raised a monstrance and an invading army fled.  
**Evidence:** the attack and Clare's Eucharistic prayer appear in 1253 testimony and the Legend; later visual details are anachronistic. The early container was not the later Gothic monstrance.  
**Effect:** made Clare protector of Assisi and an emblem of Eucharistic devotion; later retellings sometimes racialize a complex imperial army into an undifferentiated Muslim menace.

### Multiplication of bread and oil

**Story:** half a loaf fed fifty sisters; an empty oil vessel was found filled.  
**Evidence:** canonization testimony/Legend, modeled partly on biblical miracle forms; no independent material verification.  
**Effect:** cast Clare as provider despite institutional poverty.

### Christmas “broadcast” and patronage of television

**Story:** too sick to attend Christmas offices, Clare saw and heard the liturgy at San Francesco from her cell.  
**Appearance:** official Legend, later devotion; witnesses also described distant hearing.  
**Evidence:** visionary/hagiographic report, not ordinary observation.  
**Effect:** Pius XII named her heavenly patroness of television in **1958**, a technologically creative reuse of medieval remote vision.

### Francis and Clare as lovers or “soulmates”

**Story:** modern novels, films, and popular spirituality sometimes imply romantic attraction.  
**Evidence:** their writings and early records show affection, spiritual kinship, and cooperation but no sexual relationship. Meetings were few and usually socially mediated.  
**Effect:** humanizes the pair for modern audiences but can displace Clare's independent institutional work.

### “Incorrupt body”

**Story:** Clare's body was found incorrupt in 1850 and remains visibly intact.  
**Evidence:** accounts conflict. More sober descriptions state that excavation found skeletal remains, while the present visible effigy is a constructed/wax representation with relics housed behind or within it. It should not be represented as an untouched visible corpse.  
**Effect:** pilgrimage publicity and online miracle claims persist.

## Works by Clare

### Securely authentic or substantially authentic

1. **First Letter to Agnes of Prague** (*Prima epistola ad Agnetem Pragensem*), c. 1234.
2. **Second Letter to Agnes of Prague** (*Secunda epistola*), c. 1235–36.
3. **Third Letter to Agnes of Prague** (*Tertia epistola*), c. 1238.
4. **Fourth Letter to Agnes of Prague** (*Quarta epistola*), c. 1253.
5. **Rule/Form of Life of the Order of Poor Sisters** (*Forma vitae Ordinis Sororum Pauperum*), approved by Cardinal Rainaldo in 1252 and Pope Innocent IV's *Solet annuere*, 9 August 1253.
6. **Testament** (*Testamentum*), probably late in life; authenticity formerly and still occasionally disputed, but widely accepted in current critical scholarship.
7. **Blessing** (*Benedictio*), final-period text, transmitted in Latin and Italian forms; core attribution generally accepted, textual history complex.

### Embedded or indirectly preserved texts

- A short **Form of Life given by Francis** and Francis's **Last Will** to the sisters are quoted in Clare's Rule (chapter 6) and Testament.
- Clare's reported oral sayings and deathbed blessing survive through witnesses and hagiography; they are not stenographic records.

### Disputed or rejected attribution

- **Letters to Ermentrude of Bruges:** medieval/early modern attribution, generally judged inauthentic in received form.
- Later prayers, novenas, and aphorisms published under Clare's name often lack medieval manuscript support.

## Death, Burial, Relics, and Canonization

Innocent IV ordered the canonization inquiry on 18 October 1253. Hearings followed in late November. The process was fast but not instantaneous: Pope Alexander IV canonized Clare in 1255. Miles Pattenden argues that the procedure must be read within early Franciscan and papal politics, not merely as spontaneous acclaim.

Clare was buried at San Giorgio. Construction of the Basilica of Santa Chiara began soon after; her remains were transferred there in **1260**. Her tomb was rediscovered/excavated in **1850**, and relics were installed in the neo-Gothic crypt in **1872**. Claims of a wholly incorrupt visible body conflict with reports of skeletal remains and the documented use of an effigy.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Order and institutional legacy

Urban IV's 1263 legislation gave the **Order of Saint Clare** its name and a common rule permitting property. Communities subsequently divided among Urbanist, Colettine, Capuchin, and other observances. Some followed Clare's 1253 Rule closely; others balanced poverty with endowment. Today Poor Clare federations exist worldwide, but governance is decentralized.

### Cult and iconography

Clare is depicted in a dark or brown habit with cord, book (Rule), lamp, lily, crozier, or Eucharistic vessel. The monstrance became dominant through the attack legend. She is invoked as patron of Assisi, embroiderers, laundry workers, eye disease, and television/telecommunications; patronage lists vary and often reflect puns on clarity/light.

### Historical reinterpretation

- Medieval hagiography emphasized virginity, miracles, obedience, and Clare as a feminine reflection of Francis.
- Observant reforms invoked her absolute poverty against property-owning settlements.
- Nineteenth-century devotion emphasized relics, sentiment, and Eucharistic defense.
- Twentieth- and twenty-first-century scholarship recovered her as author, governor, legal negotiator, and theorist of a specifically female Franciscan life.
- Feminist readings see resistance to patriarchal marriage and clerical property policy; critics warn that enclosure, asceticism, and hierarchical church obedience remain integral rather than incidental.
- Ecological spirituality often pairs Clare with Francis, though Clare left no developed environmental doctrine comparable to modern ecological ethics.

Her most concrete legacy is constitutional: a community of women secured the right to organize life around non-possession, communal discernment, manual labor, enclosure, and Franciscan spiritual care.

## Research Limitations

- No contemporary birth register fixes Clare's birthday or year.
- The original vernacular canonization minutes are lost; a Latin translation survives.
- Internal experiences, precise diagnoses, and many dialogues are known only through retrospective religious testimony.
- The first 1216 Privilege of Poverty is textually less secure than Gregory IX's 1228 confirmation.
- Sources cannot establish a relationship between Clare and Pica, nor the “Bourlemont” genealogy assigned to Francis's mother.
- Institutional terms shifted; “Poor Clare Order” should not be projected backward as a uniform organization under Clare's direct command.

## Atlas Connections

### Francis of Assisi

- **[T5]** **Documented fact:** Clare met Francis repeatedly in and around Assisi, beginning before her departure from home in 1211/12. At the Portiuncula, Francis and his brothers received her, cut her hair, and gave her a penitential habit; he subsequently helped establish her community at San Damiano.
- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** Francis supplied Clare and her sisters with a written Form of Life, conventionally dated 1212–13, promising them the friars’ continuing care. Clare reproduced this text in chapter 6 of her Rule, creating a directly documented textual exchange between them. [Epistolae, Columbia University](https://epistolae.ctl.columbia.edu/letter/578.html)
- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** After Francis’s death in 1226, Clare wrote about his role in her conversion and community in her Rule and Testament, calling herself his “little plant” and recording his teachings and commitment to the sisters’ poverty.

### Jesus of Nazareth

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Clare wrote extensively about Jesus Christ as a past earthly figure and present divine exemplar. Her four letters to Agnes of Prague, Rule, and Testament invoke his birth, poverty, humility, Passion, crucifixion, Eucharistic presence, and imitation; this is a documented textual connection irrespective of the theological truth-status of particular devotional claims. [Digital Collections: Franciscan Tradition](https://digitalcollections.franciscantradition.org/pages/early-sources-table-of-contents)

### Jacobus de Voragine

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Jacobus, writing after Clare’s death, included a substantial life of her in the *Legenda aurea* or *Golden Legend*, compiled during the later thirteenth century. His account retells her conversion under Francis, establishment at San Damiano, defense of Assisi, Eucharistic devotion, death, and miracles; many individual episodes are **reported facts or hagiographic claims**, but Jacobus’s written treatment of Clare is itself documented. [Jacobus de Voragine, “The Life of the Holy Virgin S. Clare”](https://ccel.org/ccel/voragine/goldleg6/goldleg6.xviii.html?queryID=63397509&resultID=182939)

No qualifying personal, textual, prophetic, proximity, or shared-object crossing was found for the remaining roster members. In particular, shared Franciscan identity or contemporaneity does not establish a crossing with Anthony of Padua or Giovanni da Pian del Carpine, and no reliable source documents Clare meeting Pica Bourlémont.
