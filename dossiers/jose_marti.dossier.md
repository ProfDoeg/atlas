# José Martí: Research Dossier

**Subject:** José Julián Martí Pérez (1853–1895)  
**Prepared:** 22 August 2026  
**Method:** Chronological documentary dossier. Dates and claims are attributed where the evidence is contested. Martí’s own texts are primary evidence for what he wrote, not automatic proof that every event he described occurred exactly as represented. Later devotional and hostile traditions are treated as historical evidence about reputation, not silently converted into biography.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and household

- **Father:** Mariano Martí Navarro (1815–1887), born in Valencia, Spain. A lower-level Spanish military/colonial employee, he worked at different times as artilleryman, police sergeant, prison or port guard, and local official. His modest and unstable earnings shaped the household. His political loyalty and stern temperament generated conflict with his independentist son, but the relationship was not simply estrangement: Martí later cared for and wrote tenderly of him.
- **Mother:** Leonor Pérez Cabrera (1828–1907), born in Santa Cruz de Tenerife, Canary Islands. She opposed the mortal risks of her son’s politics and repeatedly sought his safety, including petitions during his imprisonment. Martí’s last letter to her, from Montecristi on 25 March 1895, acknowledged the pain his chosen course caused.
- The parents were Spanish immigrants, making Martí a first-generation Cuban criollo in the colonial legal setting. The family was working or lower-middle class rather than planter elite; Martí did not inherit land, slaves, or substantial capital.
- José was the only surviving son and eldest of eight children. His sisters were **Leonor (“Chata”), Mariana Matilde, María del Carmen, María del Pilar, Rita Amelia, Antonia, and Dolores (“Lolita”)**. Two sisters died young; dates and full forms vary among biographical chronologies.
- The family moved to Valencia when José was about four (1857) and returned to Havana around 1859. In Cuba they changed addresses repeatedly. This was an economically pressured household, not the static bourgeois home later monuments can suggest.

### Marriage, child, and disputed second paternity

- Martí met **Carmen Zayas-Bazán Hidalgo** (1853–1928), from a Cuban family living in Mexico, in the mid-1870s. They married in Mexico City on **20 December 1877**. Their only undisputed child, **José Francisco Martí Zayas-Bazán** (“Pepito,” “Ismaelillo”), was born in Havana on **22 November 1878** and died in 1945. The poetry collection *Ismaelillo* is addressed to him.
- The marriage was repeatedly divided by exile, poverty, political vocation, and incompatible expectations. Carmen wanted a settled family life and did not share Martí’s readiness for renewed war. She and the child made several reunions with him but returned to Cuba. After the 1891 New York reunion failed, Martí never saw his wife or son again. Treating Carmen merely as an obstacle is a feature of Martí hagiography; surviving correspondence shows mutual attachment, grievance, jealousy, economic anxiety, and political disagreement.
- In New York Martí lived for long intervals in the boardinghouse of **Carmen Miyares de Mantilla**, a Venezuelan-born married woman separated in practice from her husband Manuel Mantilla. Martí was emotionally close to her and to her children.
- **Disputed claim—María Mantilla Romero (1880–1962) as Martí’s biological daughter:** María later told her son, actor César Romero, in a 9 February 1935 letter that Martí was her father; Romero publicly repeated the claim. Photographs, Martí’s extraordinary affection, private letters, and the household chronology are circumstantial evidence. Manuel Mantilla was legally and publicly her father; no authenticated DNA comparison has established biological paternity. A Cuban forensic proposal based on photographs and anthropometry has been publicized but is not equivalent to genetic proof and has been criticized as incapable of certainty. The correct status is **plausible, longstanding, and unresolved**, not demonstrated fact. Consequently, descriptions of César Romero as Martí’s grandson should be labeled family claim rather than established genealogy.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1853–1868

- Martí grew up amid Havana’s racial slavery, colonial policing, and widening political division. An often-repeated formative episode places the boy with his father near Hanábana in Matanzas, where he witnessed the treatment of enslaved laborers. His later poetry recalls a hanged enslaved man. The general exposure is credible; the precise scene has been polished by retrospective patriotic narration.
- He attended neighborhood schools and, from 1865, the **Escuela de Instrucción Primaria Superior Municipal de Varones**, directed by poet and educator **Rafael María de Mendive**. Mendive recognized his literary ability, helped finance his education, and became his decisive teacher and moral-political model.
- He studied at the Instituto de Segunda Enseñanza de La Habana. His closest youthful companion was **Fermín Valdés Domínguez**, whose affluent family assisted him. Their friendship endured through prison, exile, and revolutionary work.
- The outbreak of the **Ten Years’ War** on 10 October 1868 transformed school politics. Mendive was arrested and deported. Martí’s early sonnet “10 de Octubre” and dramatic allegory *Abdala* cast resistance to foreign domination as a sacred duty.

## Education and Formation

### Havana

- Mendive’s school joined literary training to reform liberalism and Cuban patriotism. Martí absorbed Spanish Romantic poetry, republican discourse, classical models, and the abolitionist-nationalist atmosphere.
- Formal schooling was interrupted by repression in 1869 and imprisonment in 1870.

### Madrid and Zaragoza, 1871–1874

- Exiled to Spain after imprisonment, Martí registered at the **Universidad Central de Madrid**, then continued at the **Universidad de Zaragoza**.
- In 1874 he completed a licentiate in **Civil and Canon Law** and a degree/licentiate in **Philosophy and Letters**. Accounts differ over ceremonial issuance of diplomas, but the academic work is documented. His thesis treated political theory/classical law.
- His intellectual formation included Spanish republicanism, Krausist ethics and educational reform, liberal constitutionalism, natural law, and direct observation of the First Spanish Republic’s failure to resolve Cuba’s status. He supported republican principles while condemning Spanish republicans who withheld them from Cuba.
- Poverty and chronic pain accompanied study. Friends sometimes paid fees or supplied clothes and food. The shackle injury from Cuba remained recurrent.

## Early Political Prosecution and Imprisonment, 1869–1871

- In January 1869 he helped produce the one-issue papers **El Diablo Cojuelo** and **La Patria Libre**; the latter printed *Abdala*.
- Colonial volunteers searched the Valdés Domínguez home and found a letter addressed to schoolmate **Carlos de Castro y de Castro**, rebuking him for joining the Spanish volunteer corps and using the word “apóstata.” Martí and Fermín were arrested on **21 October 1869**.
- A military court convicted Martí of **infidencia** (disloyalty/treason). He accepted principal responsibility for the letter, apparently attempting to protect Fermín. On **4 March 1870** he received **six years’ imprisonment with hard labor**.
- He worked in the San Lázaro quarries wearing a chain and iron shackle. The metal cut his groin/leg; later descriptions include infection, testicular or scrotal injury, and lifelong pain. Photographs and his tract *El presidio político en Cuba* made the shackle a central icon of the martyr biography.
- Family petitions helped secure transfer in October 1870 to the Isla de Pinos estate of **José María Sardá**, then commutation to deportation. He sailed for Cádiz aboard the *Guipúzcoa* in January 1871.
- The conviction was political prosecution under colonial military law. It was not an ordinary criminal offense, and no evidence supports later insinuations of fraud or personal criminality.

## Chronological Life History

### Spain, 1871–1874

- In Madrid he published **El presidio político en Cuba** (1871), a documentary-polemical indictment built from his and other prisoners’ suffering, and **La República española ante la Revolución cubana** (1873), demanding consistency from Spanish republicans.
- He campaigned over the November 1871 execution in Havana of eight medical students, an event that also radicalized Fermín Valdés Domínguez. Martí did not witness the execution; he used testimony and exile networks.
- He moved to Zaragoza amid political upheaval and completed his university work. He wrote drama and poetry, including *Adúltera* and *Amor con amor se paga* in the larger 1870s creative period.
- Late in 1874 he left Spain via France, passing through Paris (where accounts place meetings with Victor Hugo or prominent writers; many details are thinly documented), then sailed to Mexico by way of England/New York.

### Mexico, 1875–1876

- He reached Mexico City in February 1875, rejoining his parents and sisters; his sister Mariana had recently died during the family’s Mexican period.
- He joined **Revista Universal** as writer, translator, parliamentary chronicler, theatre critic, and editorial polemicist. He translated Victor Hugo’s *Mes fils* and participated in the literary **Sociedad Gorostiza** and **El Porvenir**.
- He formed his lifelong friendship with **Manuel Mercado**, public official and confidant. His last unfinished letter, written on the eve of death, was addressed to Mercado.
- His play **Amor con amor se paga** was staged in Mexico in 1875. He also became entangled in polemics over Mexican art and politics.
- Martí supported the liberal republic but opposed the seizure of power by **Porfirio Díaz** through the Plan of Tuxtepec. Political pressure and the new regime’s climate contributed to his departure in early 1877.

### Guatemala and marriage, 1877–1878

- Traveling under his second surname as **Julián Pérez** to enter Cuba briefly, he proceeded to Guatemala. President **Justo Rufino Barrios** and education minister **José María Izaguirre** gave him teaching posts at the Escuela Normal Central and other institutions. He taught literature, composition, and philosophy and lectured publicly.
- He wrote the laudatory-descriptive booklet **Guatemala** (published 1878). His initial engagement with liberal modernization cooled as he encountered Barrios’s authoritarianism and demands for public conformity.
- **María García Granados**, daughter of former president Miguel García Granados, became attached to him. Martí returned to Mexico and married Carmen Zayas-Bazán. María died in May 1878, probably of respiratory disease. Years later poem IX of *Versos sencillos* created “la niña de Guatemala, la que se murió de amor.” The poem is not a death certificate: the claim that she literally died from love is literary legend; the emotional triangle may have had a factual basis, but Martí’s exact conduct and her diagnosis remain uncertain.
- He resigned after Izaguirre was dismissed and returned with Carmen to Cuba under the post-Zanjón amnesty.

### First return to Cuba and second deportation, 1878–1879

- The Pact of Zanjón ended the Ten Years’ War without independence or immediate abolition. Martí worked as a teacher and sought legal practice; colonial authorities would not recognize or permit the degree in a way that gave him a stable career.
- José Francisco was born in November 1878. Martí spoke at cultural societies and organized secretly with **Juan Gualberto Gómez** and New York’s revolutionary committee.
- During the **Little War** conspiracy, he was arrested on 17 September 1879 and deported again to Spain. Documentary evidence supports revolutionary association; romantic accounts of constant operational command exaggerate his still-limited role.

### Spain, New York, and Venezuela, 1879–1881

- Martí escaped or quickly left Spain for France and reached New York in January 1880. He worked with **Calixto García’s** Cuban Revolutionary Committee, delivered the Steck Hall speech, and contributed to exile papers.
- Strategic tensions arose: Martí rejected both Spanish autonomy and military caudillismo, while veteran generals doubted a young civilian intellectual. After the Little War collapsed, he traveled to Caracas in January 1881.
- In Venezuela he taught and founded **Revista Venezolana**. Only two issues appeared. His praise of the independent-minded Cecilio Acosta and refusal to flatter President **Antonio Guzmán Blanco** provoked official hostility; he left in July 1881. “Expelled” is common shorthand, but the mechanism was political pressure rather than a fully documented judicial deportation order.

### New York base, 1881–1887

- New York remained his principal residence for roughly fourteen years, with extensive travel. He lived precariously in boardinghouses, especially the Mantilla household, and made a living through journalism, translation, teaching, lectures, and later consular fees.
- From 1882 he became a celebrated foreign correspondent for **La Nación** of Buenos Aires; he also wrote for **La Opinión Nacional** (Caracas), **La América** (New York), **El Partido Liberal** (Mexico), and other Latin American papers. His “Escenas norteamericanas” reported elections, labor conflict, technology, art, disasters, racism, lynching, Indigenous dispossession, wealth, and mass culture.
- He admired U.S. constitutional liberties, industry, education, and self-government while increasingly fearing monopoly capitalism, racism, aggressive expansion, and annexationist designs on Cuba. Reducing him either to an uncomplicated admirer or a congenital enemy of the United States falsifies the changing and double-sided record.
- He published **Ismaelillo** (New York, 1882), privately circulated, and drafted much of **Versos libres**, unpublished as a collection in his lifetime. In 1885 the novel **Amistad funesta** appeared serially under “Adelaida Ral” in *El Latino-Americano*.
- In 1884 he joined Máximo Gómez and Antonio Maceo in planning a new war, then broke with them. His 20 October letter to Gómez warned against importing “a people of generals” and military despotism into the republic. This was a major strategic failure in the short term and a defining statement of civilian republican control. Reconciliation came later.
- Translation supported him: works included Hugh Conway’s *Called Back* (*Misterio*), and Helen Hunt Jackson’s **Ramona** (Spanish edition, New York, 1888). His translations were adaptive literary interventions, not mechanically literal versions.

### Diplomatic work, journalism, and continental politics, 1887–1891

- Martí served as New York consul for **Uruguay**, later simultaneously for **Argentina and Paraguay**. The Uruguayan diplomatic archive preserves appointment/resignation documents and his monetary-conference report. Exact starting dates vary by whether honorary or formal appointment is counted.
- He taught Spanish at New York’s Central High School and delivered lectures in Spanish-American societies. He also worked commercially for the publisher D. Appleton & Co. and in newspaper offices; none made him wealthy.
- He covered the 1886 Haymarket affair and anarchist trial. His first accounts reflected mainstream suspicion; later writing became more sympathetic to the condemned men and critical of class justice. This evolution is important evidence against presenting a single timeless ideology.
- He attended the **First International American Conference** (Washington, 1889–90) as journalist-observer and served Uruguay at the **International American Monetary Conference** (1891). He opposed economic arrangements he believed would subordinate Spanish America to U.S. power.
- **La Edad de Oro**, a children’s monthly written and edited by Martí, produced four issues from July to October 1889. Its mix of history, science, literature, anti-racism, and continental pedagogy became canonical after his death. It ceased for financial/editorial reasons; Martí resisted compromises desired by publisher A. Da Costa Gómez.
- In 1890–91 he resigned his consular positions as open Cuban revolutionary work made diplomatic service untenable. He declined to place host governments in conflict with Spain.
- He published **Versos sencillos** in New York in 1891, poems written partly after illness and rest in the Catskills in 1890. “Guantanamera” later drew verses from this book, but Martí did not compose the famous modern song.
- His essay **“Nuestra América”** appeared first in New York’s *Revista Ilustrada* (1 January 1891) and in Mexico’s *El Partido Liberal* (30 January). It argued for governments grounded in local realities, racial inclusion, cultural self-knowledge, and resistance to imperial pressure.

### Creating the Partido Revolucionario Cubano, 1891–1894

- Martí traveled repeatedly to exile centers in **Tampa, Key West, New York, Philadelphia, Ocala, Jacksonville**, and elsewhere. In Tampa in November 1891 he delivered **“Con todos y para el bien de todos”** and **“Los pinos nuevos.”** Cigar workers—Black and white, often organized in mutual-aid societies—were the financial and social base of the movement.
- An illness in Tampa in December 1892 was remembered as an **attempted poisoning** (“glass of Spanish wine” or contaminated drink). Martí recovered in the home of Afro-Cuban activists **Ruperto and Paulina Pedroso**. No identified perpetrator, surviving forensic test, or adjudication proves poisoning. It is best classified as a contemporary suspicion consolidated into patriotic legend.
- Local clubs approved the **Bases** and **Estatutos Secretos** of the Partido Revolucionario Cubano on 5 January 1892. The PRC was proclaimed on **10 April 1892**. Its stated purpose was to achieve Cuban independence and assist Puerto Rican independence, then found a just republic—not operate as a permanent one-party state.
- Martí was elected **Delegado** annually. **Benjamín Guerra** served as treasurer. The weekly **Patria**, first issued 14 March 1892, became the party’s political newspaper.
- Organization depended on small worker contributions (“the republic’s money”), club dues, benefit events, and larger donors. Martí cultivated transparency and sacrifice, but revolutionary secrecy limits a complete modern audit. There is no credible evidence he enriched himself; letters show chronic personal scarcity, dependence on friends, and strict separation in principle between campaign funds and household needs.
- He repaired relations with generals, commissioning **Máximo Gómez** as military chief and negotiating with **Antonio Maceo**, while insisting on civil political purpose. Tensions remained over command, funding, racial constituencies, and the future state.
- Networks included Juan Gualberto Gómez inside Cuba; Gonzalo de Quesada, Sotero Figueroa, Serafín Sánchez, Carlos Roloff, Enrique Collazo, José Dolores Poyo, Rafael Serra, the Pedrosos, and Puerto Rican revolutionaries **Eugenio María de Hostos** and **Ramón Emeterio Betances**.
- His 1893 essay **“Mi raza”** attacked both white racism and political mobilization premised on separate races, insisting that “there are no races” in a moral/political sense. Historians debate the limitation: its universalism delegitimized white supremacy, yet could also obscure specific Black institutions and structural inequality. Martí’s alliances with Black organizers are documented; so are paternalistic and assimilationist formulations in portions of his earlier writing.

### Fernandina Plan and outbreak of war, 1894–February 1895

- Martí, Gómez, and collaborators planned to send men and arms to Cuba aboard three vessels—*Lagonda*, *Amadís*, and *Baracoa*—from Fernandina, Florida. The plan cost a substantial share of exile funds and depended on covert procurement under U.S. neutrality law.
- U.S. authorities seized the ships and arms in January 1895 after the plot was exposed. Whether betrayal came chiefly from Colonel Fernando López de Queralta, loose security, informants, or Spanish surveillance remains disputed. The loss was a severe material and reputational failure, but much of the equipment was later recovered or sold rather than simply forfeited.
- On **29 January 1895**, Martí, Enrique Collazo, and José María Rodríguez signed the order authorizing simultaneous uprising. Revolt began in Cuba on **24 February**; several western risings failed quickly, while Oriente sustained the war.
- Martí went through the Dominican Republic and worked with Gómez at Montecristi. Their **Manifesto de Montecristi**, signed 25 March 1895, defined the war as national rather than racial vengeance, rejected hatred of Spaniards as persons, promised protection of property compatible with military necessity, and opposed annexation.

### Final expedition and campaign, April–May 1895

- Martí and Gómez left Montecristi on 1 April with Ángel Guerra, Francisco Borrero, César Salas, and Marcos del Rosario. After failed transport arrangements and danger in the Caribbean, they landed at **Playitas de Cajobabo** in eastern Cuba on **11 April 1895**.
- Martí kept a campaign diary attentive to plants, food, terrain, and people. Insurgent commanders welcomed him but disputed the relationship between the PRC delegate and military command.
- At **La Mejorana** on 5 May, Martí, Gómez, and Maceo held a famous meeting. No agreed minutes survive. Martí’s diary notes disagreement; later versions variously make it a constitutional clash, personal humiliation, racial/political rupture, or tactical conference. Claims about exact words are reconstructions.
- On 15 April Gómez conferred on Martí the rank of **major general**. Martí’s military experience was minimal; the rank expressed political standing and incorporation into the army.
- On 18 May at Dos Ríos he began his last letter to Manuel Mercado: he wrote that he was daily in danger of giving his life to prevent U.S. expansion through the Antilles and that what he had done and would do was for that purpose. Because the letter is unfinished and private, it is unusually strong evidence of his late strategic thought, though later governments have excerpted it polemically.

## Death, Identification, and Remains

- On **19 May 1895**, Gómez’s force encountered a Spanish column commanded by Colonel **José Ximénez de Sandoval** near Dos Ríos. Gómez ordered an attack and, according to his later account, told the militarily inexperienced Martí to remain behind.
- Martí advanced with the young rider **Ángel de la Guardia**, dressed conspicuously and riding a light-colored horse. Spanish fire struck him. The contemporary medical/death record describes wounds involving chest, neck/jaw region, and right leg; later medical studies disagree on exact trajectories because the original examination was limited and exhumations disturbed the remains.
- Spanish forces recovered the body. Dr. **Valencia Fortún** performed an examination after exhumation at Remanganaguas; identification relied on papers, clothing, physical marks, and persons familiar with Martí. Ximénez de Sandoval rejected an insurgent attempt to recover the corpse and transported it to Santiago.
- He was buried at Santa Ifigenia on 27 May 1895. The remains were moved/inspected more than once (including 1907 and the 1947–51 mausoleum project), complicating modern forensic inference.
- **Cause of death:** gunshot wounds received in combat; whether death was instantaneous cannot be established absolutely from the surviving record.
- **“Suicide by Spanish bullet” thesis:** some biographers infer that despair, marital loss, political conflict, poor health, or fear of marginalization led him deliberately to seek death. Evidence for the inference includes his repeated sacrificial language, disregard of Gómez’s caution, and conspicuous charge. Evidence against: he had concrete political tasks, wrote plans for survival, and combat confusion suffices to explain the action. There is no suicide note or eyewitness proof of intent. Status: **interpretive hypothesis, not documented suicide**.

### Wealth and property

- Martí possessed no known major real estate, company equity, patent portfolio, plantation, or investment fortune. He rented rooms and relied periodically on the Mantillas, Mercado, and political comrades.
- His income combined piece-rate journalism, translations, teaching, lectures, and consular fees. Correspondence repeatedly discusses debts, remittances, clothing, medical bills, and inability to support wife and son to Carmen’s expectations.
- Revolutionary fundraising handled meaningful sums, but surviving evidence supports austerity rather than embezzlement. Claims of perfect accounting are hagiographic unless tied to ledgers; claims of diversion likewise require evidence and have not been substantiated.
- No patents or technical inventions are known. His technological importance is observational: vivid reporting on electricity, railways, bridges, industrial production, communications, and urban spectacle, and strategic use of print networks and steam transport.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Independence and republicanism

- Martí moved from adolescent romantic separatism to a program of organized “necessary war” under civilian-republican purpose. Independence was a means to prevent both renewed Spanish rule and foreign annexation.
- He distrusted autonomous colonial reform and annexationism. His future republic emphasized elected government, civil dignity, education, productive labor, municipal knowledge, and restraints on military caudillos.
- He was neither a Marxist nor a twentieth-century laissez-faire ideologue. His language drew on republican liberalism, radical democracy, Krausist moral philosophy, Christian imagery, social reform, and Latin American anti-imperialism. He admired labor dignity and criticized concentrated wealth while distrusting dogmatic class war.

### Slavery, race, and nation

- He opposed slavery and sought a multiracial independence coalition. The PRC deliberately organized among Afro-Cuban cigar workers, and Martí worked closely with Juan Gualberto Gómez, Rafael Serra, and the Pedrosos.
- His formula of a raceless Cuban nationality challenged scientific racism and white fears of a “Black republic.” Yet “color-blind” rhetoric could demand that Black Cubans subordinate autonomous racial claims to unity. Modern scholarship therefore distinguishes his genuine anti-racism from the limits of his integrationist era.
- The Montecristi Manifesto denied that the war was a race war and praised Black participation in earlier struggle. This answered Spanish propaganda that independence meant racial vengeance, but also reassured property-owning whites.

### United States and empire

- His U.S. chronicle is internally divided by design: admiration for democratic traditions, abolitionists, writers, invention, and civic energy coexists with accounts of lynching, Indigenous dispossession, electoral corruption, plutocracy, and expansion.
- By 1889–95 he regarded U.S. hemispheric predominance as an immediate danger. He did not advocate isolation from U.S. people or culture; he sought sovereign relations and a balance of powers.

### Indigenous peoples, immigration, and gender

- *Nuestra América* attacked imported governance and called for knowledge of Indigenous and mixed populations. Some writings humanized Indigenous resistance; others retain nineteenth-century civilizational vocabulary.
- He condemned anti-Chinese violence and U.S. exclusion in some chronicles, but isolated descriptions of Asian peoples can be exoticizing or stereotyped. The record is mixed and should be read chronologically.
- He praised women’s education, writers, workers, and aspects of suffrage activism. His private and poetic ideal of womanhood could be protective, domestic, and patriarchal. He did not formulate a modern program of full gender equality, and revolutionary organizations remained male-led despite women’s indispensable labor.

### Religion and philosophy

- Baptized Catholic, Martí used Christological sacrifice, providence, soul, and moral transcendence throughout his writing. He opposed clerical authoritarianism and dogma, admired ethical religion, and was receptive to Krausism, Emersonian transcendentalism, and comparative spirituality.
- Labels such as Catholic, deist, spiritualist, freethinker, or Mason each capture fragments. Claims that he was formally a Freemason have documentary supporters and disputes about lodge evidence; Masonic symbolism became part of later commemoration. No single denominational label exhausts his mature thought.

### Literature and modernismo

- Martí transformed Spanish prose through rhythmic syntax, image clusters, neologism, compressed reportage, and a continental point of view. He is conventionally treated as a precursor or founder of **modernismo**, alongside but distinct from Rubén Darío.
- He rejected art detached from ethical and civic life, yet his poetry is not reducible to propaganda. *Versos libres* explores alienation, sexuality, bodily suffering, urban modernity, and poetic vocation; *Versos sencillos* turns apparent simplicity into intricate symbolic autobiography.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Unresolved Questions

### Colonial conviction

- **Established:** military conviction for infidencia based on the 1869 letter; imprisonment at hard labor; commutation and exile.
- **Context:** political repression during an anticolonial war. It is a conviction in the documentary sense, not evidence of corruption or an ordinary offense.

### Revolutionary violence

- Martí knowingly organized armed insurrection, covert arms purchases, clandestine expeditions, and a war expected to cause deaths and property damage. He called it a “necessary” and deliberately brief war and attempted to regulate it through political principles.
- The PRC’s activities risked violating U.S. neutrality law; the Fernandina seizures were law-enforcement action against an expedition being outfitted from U.S. territory. Martí was not tried or convicted in the United States over the plan.
- The Montecristi program rejected indiscriminate vengeance and promised humane treatment, but Martí died before he could govern wartime conduct. Later acts by insurgent forces cannot automatically be assigned to his personal orders.

### Relations with military chiefs

- Hostile accounts portray Martí as a vain civilian who obstructed experienced generals; devotional accounts make him undisputed architect and commander. Correspondence documents real strategic distrust in 1884 and renewed disagreements in 1895. Neither caricature resolves the institutional question of who would control the future republic.

### Family responsibilities and relationships

- Carmen Zayas-Bazán and some contemporaries faulted his inability or unwillingness to provide stable family support. The evidence confirms long separations and poverty; it does not show simple abandonment without contact or remittances.
- The depth and possible sexual nature of his relationship with Carmen Miyares is not fully documented. María Mantilla’s paternity claim raises the possibility of adultery, but biological paternity remains unresolved.
- Martí’s romantic correspondence and verse reveal multiple emotional attachments. Lists that turn every admired woman into a consummated affair are speculative.

### Race

- Admirers cite “Mi raza” as definitive anti-racism; critics note that denying race as a valid political category could suppress Black claims after slavery. Both readings have textual basis. There is no documentary basis for calling Martí a white-supremacist organizer; equally, treating every phrase as identical to twenty-first-century racial politics is anachronistic.

### Finances

- No substantiated evidence of fraud, corruption, money laundering, private enrichment, or secret business ownership emerged in the consulted record. Revolutionary secrecy, cash collections, and the Fernandina loss created opportunities for accusation, but evidence is required transaction by transaction.

### Poisoning in Tampa

- **Story:** Spanish agents poisoned Martí in December 1892; Paulina Pedroso saved him.
- **Evidence:** contemporary illness and recovery in the Pedroso home are well embedded in local memory. No laboratory evidence, named poisoner, prosecution, or conclusive contemporary investigation is known.
- **Status:** suspected attempt/legend, not established assassination attempt.

## Illnesses, Injuries, and Physical Condition

- The 1870 quarry shackle caused deep injury in the groin/leg and recurrent pain; a ring made from the iron became a relic in Martí iconography.
- He suffered episodes described as ulcers, fever, bronchial or pulmonary illness, eye problems, and debilitating weakness. A 1890 illness led him to recuperate in the Catskills.
- Later Cuban medical writing has diagnosed **sarcoidosis** retrospectively, sometimes explaining multi-organ symptoms. Without modern tests this remains a retrospective medical hypothesis, not a diagnosis established in life.
- Heavy workload, poor sleep, irregular meals, tobacco-smoke environments, and chronic financial stress are well documented context. No reliable evidence establishes alcoholism or drug addiction.
- Psychological language in letters and poems includes despair, loneliness, sacrificial longing, and bodily disgust. Retrospective psychiatric diagnosis is not justified by the available record.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. “The Apostle” as flawless secular saint

- **Story:** Martí was morally perfect from childhood, destined to die for Cuba, without contradiction or ordinary desire.
- **Appearance/circulation:** began in independence-era obituaries and Gonzalo de Quesada’s editorial memorialization; strengthened through republican schoolbooks, monuments, civic ritual, and after 1959 state pedagogy.
- **Evidence:** extraordinary early commitment, imprisonment, poverty, organizing, and death are factual. Perfection is a devotional construction that suppresses marital conflict, tactical errors, changing opinions, and political defeat.
- **Effect:** supplied Cuba with a unifying civil saint while making historical criticism appear sacrilegious.

### 2. María García Granados “died of love”

- **Story:** the Guatemalan president’s daughter died because Martí married another woman.
- **Source:** Martí’s poem IX in *Versos sencillos* (1891), written thirteen years after her 1878 death, and later memoir/biographical elaboration.
- **Evidence:** attraction and emotional distress are plausible; physical illness is documented in broad terms. Poetry cannot establish medical causation.
- **Effect:** generated “La Niña de Guatemala,” one of Latin America’s most durable romantic legends, often shifting attention from María’s own social world to Martí’s charisma.

### 3. Martí as biological father of María Mantilla

- **Story:** his New York intimate Carmen Miyares bore his daughter María.
- **Circulation:** family knowledge made explicit in María’s 1935 letter to César Romero; interviews and descendants repeated it; Cuban forensic and journalistic treatments revived it.
- **Evidence for:** María’s own assertion, chronology, intimacy, photographs, letters, resemblance claimed by descendants. **Against/limits:** Manuel Mantilla’s legal paternity, lack of direct acknowledgment by Martí, no conclusive DNA test, unreliable certainty of facial comparison.
- **Effect:** humanizes or scandalizes Martí depending on the narrator; revolutionary Cuba long treated it cautiously because adultery disturbed the family icon.

### 4. Deliberate martyrdom at Dos Ríos

- **Story:** Martí knowingly rode into fire to escape political defeat or fulfill a death wish.
- **Circulation:** biographical interpretation from his sacrificial prose, Gómez’s account of disobedience, and the visual drama of black coat/white horse.
- **Evidence:** risk-taking is certain; suicidal intent is not. His current plans and letters contain future-oriented work.
- **Effect:** reinforces martyr cult in positive versions and incompetence/vanity in hostile versions.

### 5. “José Martí, intellectual author of Moncada”

- **Story:** Martí authored in spirit the 26 July 1953 attack on the Moncada barracks.
- **Source/circulation:** Fidel Castro’s courtroom defense and later pamphlet *La historia me absolverá*; the centennial rebels called themselves the **Generación del Centenario** because 1953 marked Martí’s hundredth birth anniversary.
- **Evidence:** Castro repeatedly quoted Martí and located armed revolt within Cuban nationalist genealogy. Martí could not author an event 58 years after death, and his multiparty republican language cannot simply be equated with the post-1959 state.
- **Effect:** gave Castro’s movement national legitimacy beyond Marxism and made Martí ubiquitous in revolutionary iconography. Anti-Castro Cubans simultaneously claimed a democratic Martí against one-party rule.

### 6. Martí as communist—or as doctrinaire anti-communist

- **Story:** rival camps claim his thought logically culminates in Cuban communism, or categorically condemns it.
- **Evidence:** he criticized monopoly and exploitation, supported workers, and sought social equality; he also defended private initiative, civil pluralism, and warned about authoritarian organization. He died before Leninism and twentieth-century Cuban communism. Selective quotation powers both myths.
- **Effect:** Martí became a contested “usable past” claimed by republicans, liberals, socialists, communists, Catholic thinkers, exiles, and the Cuban state.

### 7. Martí as prophet who foresaw the Spanish-American War and every later U.S. policy

- **Story:** his Mercado letter precisely predicted U.S. occupation and all later Cuban-U.S. conflict.
- **Evidence:** his fear of U.S. expansion and annexation is explicit and prescient; specific later events were not foreknown. The unfinished letter’s canonical status encourages decontextualized quotation.
- **Effect:** central to Latin American anti-imperialist memory.

### 8. The “white rose” and the German White Rose resistance

- Martí’s “Cultivo una rosa blanca” became a universal emblem of friendship and principled generosity. Claims that it directly inspired the Scholl siblings’ **White Rose** group circulate widely, but a clear documentary chain from Martí’s poem to the group’s name is not established in the sources consulted. Status: attractive association requiring stronger primary evidence.

### 9. Poisoned relic, sacred body, and incorruptible memory

- Shackle iron, soil, houses, death-site objects, photographs, and successive tombs have functioned as civic relics. The body was in fact exhumed and transferred; claims of miraculous preservation belong to memorial culture rather than forensic fact.

## Works by José Martí

### Bibliographical cautions

Martí did not create a definitive collected edition. Much of his output appeared in newspapers, was unsigned, republished with alterations, or remained in notebooks. His 1 April 1895 “literary testament” asked Gonzalo de Quesada to organize thematic volumes and notably expressed hesitation about preserving some verse. Quesada’s **Obras** began in 1900; later Cuban *Obras completas* and the ongoing **Edición crítica** greatly expanded and re-dated the corpus. Therefore “publication date” below distinguishes lifetime printing from posthumous collection.

### Major posthumous works and collections

- **Versos libres** — poems largely composed in the 1870s–80s; not issued by Martí as a book. First assembled posthumously (1913 editions are commonly cited); textual order and inclusion depend on editor.
- **Flores del destierro** — posthumous grouping of poems, an editorial category rather than a lifetime book.
- **Obras** — Gonzalo de Quesada y Aróstegui’s editions beginning 1900, following and modifying Martí’s literary testament.
- **Obras completas** — multiple Cuban editions; the 28-volume Editorial Nacional/Editorial de Ciencias Sociales corpus (1963–75 and reprints) became the standard reference, but is not a critical final text.
- **Obras completas. Edición crítica** — Centro de Estudios Martianos, ongoing multi-volume chronological critical edition incorporating manuscripts, variants, attribution, and annotation; the best large-scale scholarly basis, still not reducible to a short list.
- **Epistolario** — critical multi-volume correspondence edited by Luis García Pascual, Enrique H. Moreno Pla, and collaborators; genuine letters with editorial apparatus.
- **Escenas norteamericanas**, **En los Estados Unidos**, **Nuestra América**, **Política de Nuestra América**, **José Martí Reader**, and innumerable “selected writings” are posthumous thematic selections, not books planned in those exact forms by Martí.
- **Granos de oro**, aphorism books, calendars, and quotation anthologies are posthumous extracts. Many internet maxims credited to Martí are paraphrases or lack traceable locations; attribution should be checked against the critical edition.

### Journalism by venue (substantial rather than exhaustive)

Martí wrote or corresponded for *Revista Universal* (Mexico), *El Federalista*, *La Opinión Nacional* (Caracas), *La Nación* (Buenos Aires), *El Partido Liberal* (Mexico), *La República* (Honduras), *La Opinión Pública* (Montevideo), *La América* (New York), *The Hour*, *The Sun*, *El Economista Americano*, *La Juventud*, *Patria*, and other exile or Spanish-American papers. Attribution of anonymous editorial matter remains a specialized textual problem.

### Hostile and polemical traditions

- Spanish colonial newspapers and official reports depicted Martí as filibuster, separatist conspirator, racial agitator, or foreign-supported adventurer. These are primary evidence of counterinsurgency rhetoric and sometimes operational intelligence, requiring corroboration.
- Annexationist and autonomist Cuban writers criticized the PRC’s war as reckless or dictatorial.
- Post-1959 exile writers, prominently Carlos Ripoll, argued that the state falsified a liberal-democratic Martí. Cuban state scholarship responded by emphasizing Martí’s anti-imperialism and social radicalism. Both camps preserve useful documents and both can over-select quotations.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### 1895–1902: martyr and absent founder

- News of his death produced tributes across the Americas. Rubén Darío mourned “Maestro”; Charles A. Dana’s *Sun* praised a writer and revolutionary it had known. The war continued under Gómez, Maceo, and other commanders.
- U.S. intervention in 1898, military occupation, the Platt Amendment, and the 1902 republic transformed Martí’s warning about external domination into retrospective prophecy. Because he never governed, rival groups could project alternative republics onto him.

### Republican Cuba, 1902–1952

- Schools, municipalities, veterans, parties, Masons, labor societies, and intellectuals institutionalized Martí. His birth house became a shrine; statues and busts proliferated; 28 January rituals taught civic nationalism.
- Juan Gualberto Gómez and other veterans invoked Martí against racism, corruption, and subordination. Governments also used his image ceremonially while pursuing policies critics considered contrary to him.
- The **Sociedad La Unión Martí-Maceo** in Tampa joined his name to Antonio Maceo in Afro-Cuban mutual-aid memory, showing that commemoration was never solely state-directed.
- The 1920s–40s produced competing Catholic, liberal, socialist, communist, student, and anti-imperialist interpretations. The 1940 Constitution’s nationalist-social language was frequently connected to Martí.

### Centennial generation and the 1959 Revolution

- **1953**, the centenary of his birth, saw Batista-state commemorations and opposition mobilization. Fidel Castro’s Moncada attackers became the **Generación del Centenario**. In the later published courtroom defense Castro called Martí the “intellectual author” of the attack.
- After 1959 the revolutionary government made Martí a foundational precursor: quotations entered speeches, schools, mass organizations, museums, international diplomacy, and the iconographic pairing Martí–Fidel. The state emphasized anti-imperialism, worker solidarity, and “radical” democracy.
- Tension remained: Martí founded a revolutionary party for a war and future republic, not the Communist Party of Cuba; his civil pluralism, elections, and suspicion of military domination have been invoked by dissidents and exiles. Official scholarship argues that social justice and anti-imperialism connect him to the revolution without claiming he was literally Marxist.
- Cuban exile communities place Martí in Miami, Tampa, New York, and elsewhere as a symbol of independent, democratic Cuba. Thus the same figure legitimates mutually opposed regimes and oppositions.

### Literature, music, and global commemoration

- Verses from *Versos sencillos*, especially “Yo soy un hombre sincero,” entered **“Guantanamera.”** The song’s underlying guajira tradition and popular form predate or exceed Martí; radio performer Joseíto Fernández and later adaptations, including Julián Orbón/Pete Seeger pathways, made Martí’s stanzas internationally famous.
- His prose helped define Latin American modernismo and the Spanish-language chronicle; later writers from Darío and Gabriela Mistral to anti-colonial theorists treated him as a continental author.
- Monuments exist throughout the Americas and beyond. In New York, Anna Hyatt Huntington’s equestrian statue in Central Park shows the fatal moment at Dos Ríos. Havana’s Plaza de la Revolución centers a monumental Martí memorial later overlaid by revolutionary state ritual.
- UNESCO’s recognition of the fonds confirms global archival significance.

### Jardín de los Poetas, Palermo, Buenos Aires

- A bronze bust by Cuban sculptor **Alberto Lescay Merencio** was donated by the Republic of Cuba and installed in **2002** in the Jardín de los Poetas, within the Rosedal/Parque Tres de Febrero, Palermo.
- Buenos Aires legislation authorized the placement. The city reported the bust among artworks stolen from the Rosedal in the 2010s; later accounts describe replacement/restoration and political disputes over plaques or access. Cuban emigrants of differing politics have gathered at it, making the site a diasporic rather than exclusively diplomatic monument.
- The placement is particularly apt because Martí was a major correspondent for Buenos Aires’s *La Nación* and served briefly as Argentine consul in New York. It materializes a genuine Martí–Argentina relationship, not merely generic pan-Latin commemoration.

### Honors and names

- No modern literary prize or state decoration defines his lifetime career; his principal “honors” were offices and movement election. Most honors are posthumous.
- Cuba formally designates him **Héroe Nacional**; the principal national library, airport (José Martí International Airport), university, streets, schools, orders, cultural institutions, and international friendship organizations bear his name.
- The **Order of José Martí** is a high Cuban state decoration created after his death and awarded in his name; it was not an award he received.

## Atlas Connections

### Abraham Lincoln

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Martí repeatedly invoked Lincoln as the exemplary antislavery president and moral standard against which he judged later United States politics. He also attended Walt Whitman’s April 1887 New York lecture commemorating Lincoln, an occasion incorporated into “El poeta Walt Whitman.” Martí’s published assessment that Lincoln, were he alive, would reject the political conduct of his successors is preserved in his United States chronicles. [Library of America](https://www.loa.org/news-and-views/poet-and-revolutionary-jose-marti-on-walt-whitman-the-united-states-and-the-universal-i/), [Journal of Latin American Studies](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-latin-american-studies/article/abs/jose-marti-and-the-united-states-a-further-interpretation/E43C4C23813BDF81890F5D8EE66AC10D)

### Simón Bolívar

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Martí delivered a commemorative address on Bolívar in New York on 24 July 1883 and later placed him among the three independence exemplars in “Tres héroes,” published in the first number of *La Edad de Oro* in July 1889. These were explicit biographical and political interpretations of Bolívar, not evidence of personal contact. [Sociedad Cultural José Martí](https://martianoscuba.wordpress.com/2022/05/09/simon-bolivar-en-la-obra-de-jose-marti-por-wolfgang-r-vicent-vielma/)

### José de San Martín

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Martí presented San Martín, together with Bolívar and Miguel Hidalgo, as one of the liberators of Spanish America in “Tres héroes,” published in *La Edad de Oro* in July 1889. The essay interprets San Martín’s character and renunciation of power for a young readership. [Text and publication context](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/author/7847)

### Charles Darwin

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Following Darwin’s death on 19 April 1882, Martí wrote the extensive obituary-essay conventionally titled “Darwin ha muerto,” dated 17 May 1882 and published in Caracas’s *La Opinión Nacional*. It summarized Darwin’s travels, books, natural-selection theory, and South American observations while registering Martí’s qualified admiration. [Darwin Online transcription and critical notes](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/Ancillary/1882_Marti_A645.html)

### Karl Marx

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** In a New York dispatch dated 29 March 1883 and published by *La Nación* on 13 May, Martí described a labor gathering honoring the recently deceased Marx. The familiar titles “Karl Marx ha muerto” and “A la muerte de Karl Marx” are later editorial shorthand: the passage originally formed part of a broader “Cartas de Martí” chronicle. [Publication-history discussion](https://jovencuba.com/marx-textos-marti/), [contemporary-text reproduction](https://www.yumpu.com/es/document/view/14540183/cartas-de-marti-la-nacion-buenos-aires-portal-jose-marti)

### Giuseppe Garibaldi

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Martí wrote about Garibaldi after the Italian revolutionary’s death in June 1882, portraying him as a transnational hero of liberty. Garibaldi’s earlier sympathy for Cuban independence gave the subject particular relevance to Martí, but there is no evidence that the two men met. [Centro de Estudios Martianos critical-edition material](https://biblioteca-repositorio.clacso.edu.ar/bitstream/CLACSO/15541/1/Tomo29.pdf), [study of Italy in Martí’s work](https://accedacris.ulpgc.es/bitstream/10553/2993/1/0234608_00000_0014.pdf)

### Victor Hugo

- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** Martí translated Hugo’s *Mes fils* as *Mis hijos* and published the translation in Mexico’s *Revista Universal* in March 1875, accompanied by “Traducir *Mes fils*.” This constitutes direct literary engagement with Hugo’s text. [Martí critical-edition volume](https://biblioteca-repositorio.clacso.edu.ar/bitstream/CLACSO/16480/1/JOSE-MARTI_Tomo-05.pdf)
- **[T5]** **Reported fact:** Biographical and Martían scholarly accounts state that Martí was introduced to Hugo and Auguste Vacquerie during his brief Paris stay in late 1874, and that Hugo or his circle placed *Mes fils* in Martí’s hands for translation. The meeting is widely repeated but rests on thinner documentation than the surviving translation and article, so its precise circumstances should not be treated as fully reconstructed. [Centro de Estudios Martianos study](https://biblioteca.clacso.edu.ar/Cuba/cem-cu/20171128045156/Anuario_11.pdf), [critical-edition annotation](https://biblioteca-repositorio.clacso.edu.ar/bitstream/CLACSO/16480/1/JOSE-MARTI_Tomo-05.pdf)

### Oscar Wilde

- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** Martí followed Wilde’s 1882 American tour in the press and produced two substantial portraits: a New York chronicle dated 7 January 1882 and the better-known essay published later that year in Caracas and Buenos Aires. This is one-sided written engagement; no personal meeting between Martí and Wilde has been documented. [Scholarly study of the January chronicle](https://irlandeses.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/Irlanda-y-Cuba-historias-entretejidas-030120-with-cover.pdf), [publication-history study](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13569325.2019.1654441)

### Mark Twain

- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** Martí read and discussed Samuel Clemens’s writings in notebooks and United States chronicles, treating Twain as a humorist and critic of both medieval and modern forms of privilege. The surviving evidence establishes Martí’s one-sided textual engagement; scholarship has not established that Twain read Martí or that the two met. [Centro de Estudios Martianos, “José Martí como lector de Mark Twain”](https://biblioteca.clacso.edu.ar/Cuba/cem-cu/20171215052209/Anuario_30.pdf)

### Thomas Edison

- **[T3]** **Documented fact, with attribution qualification:** A New York chronicle devoted to Edison, his laboratory, telegraphy, and the phonograph appeared without a signature in *El Partido Liberal* on 5 February 1890 and is attributed to Martí by editors on stylistic and documentary grounds. Claims that its detail proves a personal interview with Edison remain an inference; no independently documented meeting is presently established. [Centro de Estudios Martianos analysis](https://www.revistas.una.ac.cr/index.php/repertorio/article/download/9254/11150)

### Domingo Faustino Sarmiento

- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** Sarmiento read Martí’s chronicle on the 1886 dedication of the Statue of Liberty and, in a letter to Paul Groussac dated 4 January 1887, urged that it be translated into French for European readers, praising Martí’s prose in comparison with Victor Hugo’s. Martí learned of the praise and circulated copies of Sarmiento’s letter among friends. This is documented reciprocal literary awareness, although not personal correspondence or a meeting. [Centro de Estudios Martianos study of Martí and *La Nación*](https://biblioteca-repositorio.clacso.edu.ar/bitstream/CLACSO/5336/1/Anuario_27.pdf)
- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** After Sarmiento’s death on 11 September 1888, Martí wrote a critical memorial portrait that praised his energy and educational work while refusing to suppress their differences over Latin American society and Indigenous peoples.

### Fidel Castro

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** In his 16 October 1953 Moncada trial defense, subsequently published as *La historia me absolverá*, Castro repeatedly invoked Martí and identified him as the “intellectual author” of the Moncada uprising. The connection is Castro’s posthumous interpretation and political appropriation of Martí, not personal contact or literal authorship by Martí. [UCF archival edition record](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/prism/363/), [contemporary historical account](https://www.granma.cu/cuba/2015-10-15/16-de-octubre-de-1953)

### Ernesto “Che” Guevara

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Guevara delivered a speech commemorating Martí’s birth on 28 January 1960, subsequently published in *Revolución*, presenting Martí as a revolutionary teacher for Cuban youth. Guevara continued to quote Martí in later political addresses, including his 1961 intervention at the Inter-American Economic and Social Council. This is a posthumous ideological reading, not evidence that Martí anticipated Guevara’s complete political program. [1960 speech transcript](https://www.archivochile.com/America_latina/Doc_paises_al/Cuba/Escritos_del_Che/escritosdelche0013.PDF), [1961 address](https://perio.unlp.edu.ar/catedras/planipoliticasdecom/wp-content/uploads/sites/162/2020/08/4.5-Discurso-CHE-GUEVARA-EN-CIES-1961.pdf)

No T2 prophecy/hyperstition or T9 shared-object connection with a roster member was found that met the stated evidentiary threshold. Mere chronological overlap in nineteenth-century New York, later occupation of the same cities, or appearance in the same commemorative landscape has therefore not been counted.
