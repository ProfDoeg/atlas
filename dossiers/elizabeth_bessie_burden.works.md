## Works by Elizabeth

### Embroidered obras

- **c. 1860–1861:** *St Catherine*. Figurative silk-and-wool embroidery for the projected twelve-panel *Legende of Good Wimmen* hangings at Red House; unlike most panels in the collaborative series, it is recorded as entirely worked by Elizabeth “Bessie” Burden. The panel survives at Kelmscott Manor. ([Hulse, pp. 24–25, 29–30](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/21.1.Winter2014.pdf))

- **c. 1860–1861:** *Aphrodite*. An unfinished embroidered figure from the projected Red House series, jointly catalogued to Jane Morris and Elizabeth Burden. It remains on its linen working ground, so it is both an extant obra and an arrested stage in a larger, unrealized decorative scheme. ([National Trust, no. 60226](https://www.nationaltrustcollections.org.uk/object/60226))

- **c. 1860–1861:** *Penelope*. Figurative embroidery from the Red House series, surviving at Kelmscott Manor and exhibited under “Miss Burden” in 1888. The exhibition catalogue credits Burden as its embroiderer, although production of the original series was generally collaborative. ([National Trust, no. 60226](https://www.nationaltrustcollections.org.uk/object/60226); [1888 exhibition catalogue, no. 280](https://bifmo.furniturehistorysociety.org/research/aces/1888/entrance-hall?page=0))

- **c. 1860–1861:** *Hippolyte* (also *Hippolyta*). A figurative embroidery designed for the Red House hangings and later incorporated into a three-panel screen; scholarship attributes its working jointly to Jane Morris and Elizabeth Burden, while the 1888 exhibition catalogue names Burden alone as exhibitor. It survives at Castle Howard. ([Hulse, pp. 29–30](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/21.1.Winter2014.pdf); [1888 exhibition catalogue, no. 281](https://bifmo.furniturehistorysociety.org/research/aces/1888/entrance-hall?page=0))

- **c. 1860–1861:** *Helen of Troy* (also *Flamma Troiae*). Another Red House figure, subsequently part of the three-panel screen and exhibited under Burden’s name in 1888. The differing titles and the series’ shared manufacture make individual authorship less simple than the exhibition credit suggests; the panel survives at Castle Howard. ([Hulse, pp. 29–30](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/21.1.Winter2014.pdf); [National Trust, no. 60226](https://www.nationaltrustcollections.org.uk/object/60226); [1888 exhibition catalogue, no. 282](https://bifmo.furniturehistorysociety.org/research/aces/1888/entrance-hall?page=0))

- **c. 1860–1861:** *Queen with Sword* (identified in some scholarship as *Lucretia*). A surviving embroidered figure from the Red House scheme, now at Castle Howard. Burden participated in working the series, but the surviving catalogue evidence does not securely isolate her hand in this panel. ([National Trust, no. 60226](https://www.nationaltrustcollections.org.uk/object/60226); [Hulse, pp. 29–30](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/21.1.Winter2014.pdf))

- **c. 1860–1861:** *Guenevere or Isoude*. An unfinished Red House figure now at Kelmscott Manor; even its subject remains unsettled. It belongs to the collaborative series worked principally by Jane Morris with Burden’s assistance, so it resists assignment as Burden’s independent obra. ([National Trust, no. 60226](https://www.nationaltrustcollections.org.uk/object/60226))

- **1868:** *Lamb and Flag altar frontal*. An ecclesiastical embroidery for Llandaff Cathedral, designed by Philip Webb and executed by Burden for Morris, Marshall, Faulkner & Co. It is one of the very few identified survivals from the firm’s embroidery production of the 1860s. ([Hulse, pp. 29–30](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/21.1.Winter2014.pdf); [The Letters of Philip Webb, vol. I](https://s3-euw1-ap-pe-df-pch-content-store-p.s3.eu-west-1.amazonaws.com/9781315642581/f338889b-37a7-4ab7-8c04-80e8388c6916/preview.pdf))

- **c. 1875:** *Grand pianoforte cover for Susan, Lady Wharncliffe*. A linen-and-crewel cover designed by William Morris and prepared by Burden, probably as partially worked needlework for its owner to complete. The identification is explicitly described by Hulse as probable rather than certain. ([Hulse, pp. 26–27 and note 20](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/21.1.Winter2014.pdf))

- **c. 1885:** *Angel Minstrel with Cymbals*. A surviving portion of a chalice cover or pall, embroidered in silk and gold thread after a Morris design and signed by Burden. Its unusually documented maker, technique, function, and provenance make it the most securely individualized work in her known output. ([William Morris Gallery, F454](https://www.wmgallery.org.uk/object/angel-minstrel-with-cymbals-embroidery/))

### Directed and collective making

- **1875–1876:** *Complete Design for Decorating a Room with Hangings*. Walter Crane designed this ensemble of large figurative hangings for the Royal School of Art Needlework’s Philadelphia Centennial display; the figures were executed under Burden’s technical direction, using the stitch she taught. This is a collective manifestation rather than an object made by her hand alone. ([Hulse, pp. 27–29](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/21.1.Winter2014.pdf))

- **1876:** *Royal School of Art Needlework furnishings for the Philadelphia Centennial Exhibition*. A set of furnishings produced under Burden’s direction and awarded a Certificate of Award. The record establishes her supervisory making but does not provide a secure object-by-object division of hands, so the set is best treated as a collective obra. ([Hulse, pp. 27–29](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/21.1.Winter2014.pdf))

### Printmaking

- **c. 1866–1868:** *Cupid Going Away*. A woodblock cut by Burden after an Edward Burne-Jones design for the projected illustrated edition of William Morris’s *The Earthly Paradise*. The book project was abandoned after unsatisfactory trials, but impressions and blocks survive: this is both a finished printing matrix and a fragment of an unrealized publication. ([William Morris Gallery biography and collection evidence summarized by Pascal Theatre](https://www.pascal-theatre.com/biographies/label-elizabeth-burden/))

### Attributed work

- **after 1881:** *Embroidered binding for Dante Gabriel Rossetti’s Ballads and Sonnets*. A floral embroidered binding imitating the book’s gold-stamped trade cover, held by the National Library of New Zealand. Scholarship attributes it to Burden, but the attribution is not documented as certain. ([Hulse, pp. 29–30](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/21.1.Winter2014.pdf))

### Projects and protocols

- **1873 or 1874:** *Independent embroidery school*. A project to establish Burden’s own teaching institution after her first departure from the Royal School of Needlework; Rossetti offered financial support, but no evidence establishes whether the school opened or produced anything. Its intention is documented, while its realization remains unknown. ([Hulse, note 16](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/21.1.Winter2014.pdf))

- **1873–1877:** *Teaching and transmission of the stitch later called “Burden stitch”*. Burden did not invent this medieval cushion or “tapestry” stitch; she recovered, taught, and directed its use for embroidered flesh, after which the Royal School’s 1880 handbook named it for her. It therefore resists classification as her obra: it is better understood as a transmitted technical protocol and pedagogical practice. ([Hulse, pp. 27–29](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/21.1.Winter2014.pdf); [Royal School of Needlework Stitch Bank](https://rsnstitchbank.org/stitch/burden-stitch))

### Surviving documentary texts

- **21 December 1872:** *Letter to Victoria Welby*. A professional letter setting Burden’s fees, class sizes, hours, and conditions for teaching at the School of Art Needlework; it functions partly as a proposed employment protocol. The manuscript remains in the Welby family papers. ([Hulse, pp. 21–22 and note 6](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/21.1.Winter2014.pdf))

- **30 December 1872:** *Letter to Victoria Welby*. A letter accepting a revised initial teaching arrangement and postponing the interview until February 1873. It survives in the Welby family papers. ([Hulse, p. 22 and note 7](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/21.1.Winter2014.pdf))

- **c. 20 February 1873:** *Letter to Victoria Welby concerning exhibition of pupils’ work*. An incompletely dated professional objection to attaching Burden’s name to work she considered insufficiently accomplished. The supplied date is archival inference, not a date written securely on the letter. ([Hulse, pp. 22–23 and note 9](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/21.1.Winter2014.pdf))

- **26 February 1873:** *Letter to Victoria Welby refusing continuation of lessons*. A performative professional text: it terminates or suspends Burden’s teaching rather than merely describing the dispute, and forbids use of her name on the proposed exhibition. The manuscript survives in private papers. ([Hulse, pp. 23–24 and note 10](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/21.1.Winter2014.pdf))

- **22 March 1873:** *Letter to Victoria Welby defending Burden’s teaching*. A final letter repudiating criticism of her instruction and claiming responsibility for introducing a previously unknown class of figurative embroidery to the School. It is simultaneously correspondence, professional self-definition, and a claim of technical authorship. ([Hulse, pp. 24–25 and note 13](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/21.1.Winter2014.pdf))
