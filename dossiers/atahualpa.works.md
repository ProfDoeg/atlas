## Works by Atahualpa

### Architectural obras and projects

- **After c. 1527 and before 1532:** *Royal complex at Inca-Caranqui*. Juan de Betanzos attributes new buildings at Caranqui to Atahualpa, intended to commemorate Huayna Capac and provide a setting for Atahualpa’s marriage and installation as Sapa Inca. Archaeology indicates two construction phases, however, so Tamara Bray and José Echeverría argue that Huayna Capac may have founded the complex and Atahualpa remodeled it—possibly including the raised floor of its water temple—as an unfinished royal-estate project rather than creating the entire complex himself ([Bray 2024, pp. 184–86](https://www.cuadernosdeantropologia-puce.edu.ec/index.php/antropologia/article/download/358/248/); [Bray and Echeverría 2014](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1179/0077629714Z.00000000020)).

- **Date unfixed; during his contested accession:** *Palace project at Tomebamba*. This was an attempted royal palace rather than a securely identified completed building: Susan Niles’s architectural study reports that Atahualpa had difficulty mobilizing sufficient labor for it, making the project’s incompleteness part of its political meaning. Its attribution should not be confused with Tomebamba’s earlier monumental architecture commissioned by Huayna Capac ([Niles 1999, pp. 79–80](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt20q1xfh); [Christie 2004, p. 146](https://ens9004-infd.mendoza.edu.ar/sitio/historia-america-latina/upload/05-%20EVANS%20%26%20PILLSBURY%20-%20LIBRO%20-%20Palaces%20of%20the%20Ancient%20New%20World.pdf)).

### Political-material project

- **November 1532–June 1533:** *The ransom assemblage at Cajamarca*. Atahualpa conceived and directed the gathering of an enormous quantity of gold and silver while imprisoned; it was an assemblage and survival project, not an artwork made by his own hands, because its constituent vessels, ornaments, and other objects had already been produced by many unnamed makers and were subsequently melted into bullion by the Spaniards. Pedro Sancho’s notarized distribution record documents the promise and collection, while modern scholarship emphasizes that Atahualpa continued commanding messengers, courtiers, and armies during the undertaking ([Sancho, “Report on the Distribution of the Ransom”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/reports-on-the-discovery-of-peru/report-on-the-distribution-of-the-ransom-of-atahuallpa-certified-by-the-notary-pedro-sancho/F5900BCA69949C850D44D413886E655D); [Covey 2020, chap. 6](https://academic.oup.com/book/36754/chapter-abstract/321857688)).

### Communications that resist treatment as works

- **1532–1533:** *Messages and commands issued during the civil war and captivity*. Atahualpa made and transmitted consequential communications through messengers and the Inca administrative system, including orders connected with his armies and the ransom; these survive only as reported actions or speech in colonial narratives, not as attributable verbal transcripts or securely associated khipus. They are therefore manifestations of government—and sometimes protocols—but cannot responsibly be catalogued as individually titled texts ([Betanzos, *Suma y narración de los incas*, part II](https://libros.kichwa.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/suma-y-narracion-de-los-incas-1551-Juan-de-Betanzos.pdf); [Covey 2020, chap. 6](https://academic.oup.com/book/36754/chapter-abstract/321857688)).
