# Saint Blaise of Sebastea: Research Dossier

## Research Status and Evidentiary Framework

Saint Blaise is traditionally described as a physician, bishop of Sebaste and martyr under the emperor Licinius, usually dated to approximately 316 CE. His cult is indisputably ancient, but nearly every biographical detail comes from hagiographic texts written or preserved substantially later than his supposed lifetime.

The evidence should be divided into four layers:

1. **Historically supportable context:** Christianity existed at Sebaste; bishops and other Christians were persecuted in the eastern Roman Empire under Licinius; an early cult of a martyr named Blaise or Blasius developed.
2. **Probable but not independently documented biography:** Blaise was a bishop of Sebaste and died as a martyr.
3. **Late hagiographic narrative:** his medical profession, election as bishop, retreat to a cave, friendship with animals, arrest by hunters, miracles, tortures, trial before Agricolaus and execution with two boys.
4. **Later cult, folklore and institutional appropriation:** throat blessings, crossed candles, wool-combers, livestock protection, relic translations, Dubrovnik’s civic patronage and the Fourteen Holy Helpers.

No contemporary inscription, letter, episcopal list, trial record, eyewitness act or securely identified bodily remains establishes Blaise’s biography. The detailed *Passio* therefore cannot be read as a contemporary judicial record.

## Geographic and Political Setting

Sebasteia, Latin *Sebaste*, was the ancient city corresponding to modern Sivas in central-eastern Turkey. In Blaise’s purported lifetime it belonged to the Roman administrative and cultural borderlands conventionally called Armenia Minor. It was not located within the core territory of the Armenian Arsacid kingdom throughout every relevant administrative period; later descriptions calling it simply “Armenia” reflect changing Roman, Byzantine, ecclesiastical and vernacular geography.

Christianity was established in the region by the early fourth century. The much better documented cult of the Forty Martyrs of Sebaste shows that Sebaste became an important center of martyr memory.

Licinius ruled portions of the Roman Empire from 308 to 324. He had participated in the imperial settlement associated with the so-called Edict of Milan in 313, which protected Christian worship. Eusebius of Caesarea nevertheless later accused him of increasingly restrictive measures against Christians and of arranging the deaths of bishops through provincial officials.

That evidence supports a period of anti-Christian pressure in Licinius’s eastern territories. It does not mention Blaise. Eusebius wrote as an ally and panegyrist of Licinius’s victorious rival, Constantine, so his account must also be read in its political setting.

The traditional year 316 is problematic rather than impossible. Accounts of Licinius’s most severe anti-Christian measures are often placed later, amid his conflicts with Constantine and before his defeat in 324. The assignment of Blaise’s death to precisely 316 may represent later chronological harmonization.

## Family and Ancestry

No historically useful information survives concerning Blaise’s:

- parents
- ancestry or ethnicity
- siblings
- spouse or partner
- children
- inherited property
- social class

Hagiographic descriptions of him as noble, wealthy, exceptionally charitable or born to prominent parents occur in later devotional traditions. They lack independent documentary support.

No early source states that he was married or celibate. Later portrayals naturally present him according to the expected ascetic model of a saintly bishop.

---

## Childhood and Early Life

Nothing recoverable is known about Blaise’s childhood.

Later biographies supply a conventional retrospective pattern: piety from youth, learning, moral gentleness, medical ability, charitable treatment of the poor and eventual selection for episcopal office. These motifs establish sanctity narratively but cannot reconstruct his actual upbringing.

Claims sometimes encountered in popular accounts that he was born into a rich Christian family, studied classical medicine or practiced in a particular hospital should be classified as **unverified devotional elaborations** unless supported by a specific early textual witness.

---

## Education, Teachers and Formation

No teacher, school, monastic community or theological mentor is documented.

The claim that Blaise was trained as a physician appears in later hagiography and subsequent tradition. No medical treatise, professional register, inscription or contemporary author confirms it. “Physician” may represent:

- an authentic memory of his secular occupation;
- a later inference from his healing miracles;
- a symbolic extension of episcopal care;
- or a fusion of all three.

No evidence connects him personally to Gregory the Illuminator, Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nyssa, Eusebius of Caesarea or the bishops attending the Council of Nicaea.

## Profession and Ecclesiastical Office

### Physician

**Reported hagiographic tradition:** Blaise practiced medicine before becoming bishop and treated patients generously, sometimes without payment.

**Documentary status:** Unverified. The report belongs to later saints’ lives rather than contemporary evidence.

### Bishop of Sebaste

**Probable traditional core:** Blaise was remembered as bishop of Sebaste and martyr.

His episcopacy is the most stable element of the cult. Greek and Latin traditions consistently call him bishop, and the Roman Martyrology retains that designation. Nevertheless:

- no contemporary episcopal list identifies him;
- no surviving episcopal act bears his signature;
- he is not securely documented at a council;
- no letter by or to him survives;
- his predecessor and successor cannot be identified through reliable evidence.

### Hermit or fugitive

The *Passio* says that persecution led him to withdraw from his see and live in a cave. The narrative combines two plausible historical conditions—flight during persecution and ascetic withdrawal—with the established hagiographic motif of a holy person living peacefully among animals.

The cave’s location is not recoverable. Modern sites called “the cave of Saint Blaise” in several countries are devotional associations rather than established residences.

## Chronological Life History

### Before approximately 300: Unknown early life

No contemporary evidence survives. Later tradition makes him a native or resident of the Sebaste region, a physician and a conspicuously devout Christian.

### Early fourth century: Traditional medical practice

According to later biographies, Blaise used medical knowledge in conjunction with Christian prayer and charity. Some accounts say he supplied medicine and even patients’ expenses.

These details are **hagiographic reports**. They are absent from contemporary medical or ecclesiastical documentation.

### Election or appointment as bishop

The Latin and Greek narrative traditions say that the Christians of Sebaste chose Blaise as bishop because of his gentleness, virtue and reputation.

“Election by the people” is compatible with late-antique episcopal practice, but the particular election is **not independently documented**. Jacobus de Voragine later popularized the story in Western Europe.

### Withdrawal during persecution

The developed legend places Blaise’s withdrawal under Diocletian, Licinius or a narrative combination of their persecutions. Diocletian’s empire-wide persecution began in 303; Licinius’s restrictions were later.

Blaise reportedly entered a mountain cave or wilderness refuge. There he prayed and cared for sick animals. Birds and beasts gathered around him, waited for his blessing and sometimes brought him food.

**Classification:** Hagiographic legend. The animals identify Blaise with the wider desert-saint tradition and explain later patronage of animals and livestock.

### Discovery by hunters

The provincial governor’s servants supposedly entered the wilderness while collecting wild animals for arena executions. They discovered numerous animals standing peacefully around Blaise and found themselves unable to capture them.

They reported this prodigy to the governor, conventionally named Agricolaus or Agricola.

**Classification:** Hagiographic legend. Agricolaus is also associated in tradition with the Forty Martyrs of Sebaste. The reuse of a persecuting official may reflect regional martyr tradition rather than independent evidence about Blaise.

### Arrest and journey to Sebaste

Blaise reportedly welcomed the arresting soldiers, regarding their arrival as fulfillment of a dream or divine summons. On the road he performed cures.

One woman’s pig had allegedly been seized by a wolf. Blaise ordered the wolf to return it unharmed. The wolf obeyed. The grateful widow later brought Blaise food and candles while he was imprisoned.

**Classification:** Legend. The episode helps explain his patronage of swine, livestock and animals and possibly the later offering or blessing of candles.

### The child with a bone in his throat

A mother brought Blaise a child who was choking on a fishbone—or, in some versions, simply a bone. Blaise prayed, blessed the child and restored him.

Some recensions place this miracle during the journey to prison; others effectively place it during imprisonment. Later Latin tradition expanded the story into a general promise of aid to anyone suffering obstruction or disease of the throat.

**Classification:** Hagiographic miracle, not a documented medical case.

**Historical effect:** This became the single most influential story in Blaise’s cult. It produced patronage against choking and throat disease, prayers for humans and animals with obstructions, the blessing of throats and modern associations with otolaryngology.

### Trial before Agricolaus

The governor allegedly demanded sacrifice to the Roman gods. Blaise refused and confessed Christianity. The accounts contain extended speeches defending Christian worship and denouncing idols.

**Classification:** Literary trial narrative. Such speeches often express the theology of the hagiographer’s period rather than verbatim courtroom testimony.

### Imprisonment and the widow’s offerings

The widow whose pig had been restored reportedly killed the animal, cooked its head and feet, and brought the food with bread and a candle to Blaise. He blessed her and instructed her to commemorate him annually.

This episode was used to explain:

- offerings of candles;
- foods blessed on his feast;
- pig imagery;
- domestic prosperity attributed to his intercession.

**Classification:** Cult-foundation legend.

### Torture with iron combs

Blaise was allegedly beaten, suspended from a tree or wooden frame and torn with iron combs or rakes.

Seven women gathered drops of his blood. When challenged, they declared themselves Christians. Their breasts were cut or combed; one recension says milk rather than blood issued from their wounds. They were beheaded.

**Classification:** Hagiographic martyr narrative. The sexualized torture and miraculous milk belong to recognizable martyr-literature motifs. The seven women are not independently attested.

The torture instrument was later interpreted as a wool-comber’s tool. This produced Blaise’s patronage of comb-makers, wool-combers, carders and parts of the textile trade. Medieval and early-modern occupational celebrations sometimes transformed him into a legendary inventor of wool-combing; there is no historical evidence that he invented the technology.

### Lake miracle

One recension says Agricolaus had Blaise thrown into a lake. Blaise stood or walked upon the water and challenged the pagans to approach through the power of their gods. Those who attempted to do so drowned. An angel then instructed Blaise to leave the water and receive martyrdom.

**Classification:** Hagiographic miracle modeled on biblical water narratives and martyr contests.

### Execution

Blaise and two boys—sometimes said to be sons entrusted to him by one of the martyred women—were reportedly beheaded outside Sebaste. A pious woman, named in some forms of the legend as Elissa or a comparable variant, buried them.

**Traditional date:** 3 February 316.  
**Alternative approximate period:** 316–320 or broadly the later reign of Licinius.  
**Historically defensible conclusion:** If the cult preserves an actual person, he was probably an early-fourth-century clerical martyr of Sebaste. The manner, companions and exact date of death are unverified.

## Residences, Movements, Exile and Imprisonment

### Reconstructable movements

- **Sebaste:** traditional place of ministry and episcopal office.
- **Nearby wilderness or mountain cave:** legendary refuge.
- **Road from the cave to Sebaste:** setting of healing stories.
- **Prison at Sebaste:** hagiographic imprisonment.
- **Lake near Sebaste:** legendary attempted execution.
- **Execution site outside or at Sebaste:** traditional place of death.

No credible evidence shows Blaise traveling outside the Sebaste region. Later European movements concern claimed relics, not the living man.

## Religious and Intellectual Development

No writings survive from which a personal theology can be reconstructed.

The developed legend portrays:

- orthodox confession of Christ;
- rejection of sacrifice to the Roman gods;
- ascetic withdrawal;
- compassionate healing;
- harmonious authority over animals;
- willingness to undergo martyrdom;
- intercessory power continuing after death.

These characteristics reveal the ideals of the communities transmitting his cult more clearly than Blaise’s own intellectual development.

The association of episcopal authority, medicine and animal guardianship allowed his cult to cross several social worlds: clerical, medical, agricultural, pastoral and urban craft culture.

### The unnamed mother and choking child

**Nature:** Supplicants in the throat-healing miracle.  
**Status:** Legend.

### The poor widow

**Nature:** Recipient of the returned pig; later provider of food and candles in prison.  
**Status:** Legend.

### Seven women martyrs and two boys

**Nature:** Followers or witnesses converted or encouraged by Blaise’s steadfastness.  
**Status:** Hagiographic tradition; no independent record.

### Animals of the wilderness

**Nature:** Symbolic beneficiaries and companions.  
**Status:** Legend with lasting cultic importance.

### Christian community of Sebaste

**Nature:** Supposed electors and flock.  
**Status:** Plausible contextual relationship, but individual members are unknown.

## Property, Wealth, Employment and Financial Relationships

No reliable source records Blaise’s income, property, inheritance, episcopal finances or medical fees.

Later stories emphasizing free treatment, almsgiving and refusal of worldly reward represent moral characterization rather than financial documentation.

The cult accumulated substantial material interests after his death:

- churches and monasteries dedicated to him;
- offerings at healing shrines;
- occupational guild patronage;
- relic treasuries;
- civic processions;
- endowments and pilgrimage economies.

These belonged to later institutions and cannot be assigned to Blaise personally.

## Relics and Their Movements

### Original burial

The legend says a pious woman buried Blaise and his two young companions at or near Sebaste. No excavated or securely proven grave has been identified.

### Western dispersal

Numerous churches later claimed:

- complete bodies;
- heads or portions of heads;
- jaws;
- arms and hands;
- fingers and teeth;
- ribs and other large bones;
- blood, cloth or contact relics.

Claims occur in Italy, Croatia, France, Germany, Belgium, the Netherlands, Spain, Portugal and elsewhere.

The number and anatomical incompatibility of these claims make collective authenticity impossible. Explanations include:

- division of a genuine body;
- relics of different saints named Blaise;
- contact relics later described as bodily relics;
- mistaken labels;
- duplication through institutional tradition;
- pious manufacture;
- sale or donation without reliable provenance;
- deliberate forgery or simony in some cases.

No published modern genetic or radiocarbon program has established that major collections derive from one fourth-century individual from Anatolia.

### Maratea

Maratea in Basilicata claims important remains, including the saint’s “sacred thorax.” The local translation legend says that in 732 a ship carrying the relics toward Rome was forced ashore by a storm and could not continue.

**Status:** Translation legend. The date and voyage are not contemporary documented facts.

A formal opening of the reliquary in 1941 reportedly found chest bones, a cranial fragment, a femur and an arm bone. This establishes the presence of human-looking skeletal material, not identity as Blaise.

A sixteenth-century effort to open the container reportedly failed when a marble fragment nearly injured the investigating bishop. The episode became part of local relic lore.

### Dubrovnik

Dubrovnik possesses major relic traditions, especially a claimed head. Its cult of Saint Blaise is conventionally dated from 972.

The surviving head reliquary was made in 1694 by the Venetian goldsmith Francesco Ferro, incorporating older enamels. Art historian Ana Munk rejected the entrenched interpretation that its form reproduced a Byzantine imperial *kamelaukion*. She argued that the enamels’ script and iconography point to production in Dubrovnik, approximately 1164–1180, rather than Constantinople.

This critique concerns the reliquary’s artistic origin, not a scientific authentication of the bone.

### Relic criticism

Jacques-Albin-Simon Collin de Plancy mocked the multiplication of Blaise relics in his 1822 critical dictionary, observing in substance that their aggregation would produce a saint with implausibly many limbs.

The criticism identifies a genuine evidentiary problem. It does not prove that every relic is fraudulent; it shows that all competing anatomical claims cannot be literal simultaneously.

## Development of the Cult

### Eastern cult

Blaise’s cult first developed in the Christian East. The Greek *Passio* is catalogued under *Bibliotheca Hagiographica Graeca* 276–276f and survives in numerous medieval manuscripts.

The oldest form of the *Martyrologium Hieronymianum* apparently does not contain him; he occurs in later expanded transmission. That weakens attempts to treat the full biography as an immediately post-martyrdom record.

### Western transmission

His veneration was established in Western Europe by the early Middle Ages. Ninth-century historical martyrologies record him, and Latin versions of the passion circulated extensively. Manuscript research identifies multiple Latin recensions under *Bibliotheca Hagiographica Latina* 1370–1380h.

The cult spread through:

- monasteries;
- episcopal calendars;
- relic translations;
- craft guilds;
- pilgrimage;
- miracle collections;
- liturgical books;
- vernacular saints’ lives.

### Jacobus de Voragine

In the thirteenth century Jacobus de Voragine included Blaise in the *Legenda aurea*. His synthesis circulated the cave, animals, pig, choking child, torture, lake and execution stories throughout Latin Christendom.

William Caxton’s English version, printed in 1483, helped establish the English forms “Blase” and “Blaise.”

### Fourteen Holy Helpers

In late-medieval German-speaking Europe Blaise became one of the Fourteen Holy Helpers, saints invoked collectively against illness and sudden danger, especially amid epidemic anxiety. Within the group he was assigned protection against throat disease.

This grouping was posthumous and devotional; its members did not constitute a historical organization.

### Dubrovnik

Dubrovnik made Sveti Vlaho both heavenly protector and civic emblem. According to local tradition, Blaise appeared in 971 or 972 to warn the priest Stojko of an impending Venetian attack disguised as a friendly visit. The warning supposedly enabled Ragusa to defend itself.

**Classification:** Civic foundation legend. It is not contemporary military documentation.

The saint’s image appeared on gates, seals, flags, coins, public buildings and diplomatic symbolism. The cult therefore served the Republic of Ragusa as a language of sovereignty and communal independence, not merely as private devotion.

The annual Festivity of Saint Blaise includes bells, processions, banners, relics, white doves and throat blessings. UNESCO placed it on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2009.

### Throat blessing

The Western sacramental commonly employs two candles crossed near the throat. The prayer asks that, through the intercession of Saint Blaise, God deliver the recipient from diseases of the throat and every other illness.

The candles connect:

- Candlemas on 2 February;
- Blaise’s feast on 3 February;
- the widow’s legendary candle offering;
- the choking-child miracle.

The developed ritual is medieval or early-modern, not a practice documented from Blaise’s lifetime.

### Occupational patronage

Because his flesh was supposedly torn by iron combs, wool-combers and related textile workers adopted him. His feast was especially prominent in parts of England, Flanders and German-speaking Europe.

Bradford and other English textile centers developed elaborate Saint Blaise processions. Some pageants described him as an inventor of wool-combing. That claim is occupational mythology, not technological history.

### Medical patronage

The 2005 historical study by Ante Skrobonja, Amir Muzur and Stanko Jurdana documented the Croatian *grličanje* throat-blessing ritual and Blaise’s more recent adoption by otolaryngologists and laryngectomized-person associations.

This is a history of medical patronage, not evidence that invoking Blaise prevents or treats disease. No controlled clinical evidence was located showing that the rite has therapeutic efficacy beyond its religious, psychological or communal functions.

## Iconography and Symbolic Associations

Blaise is commonly shown as:

- a bearded bishop wearing mitre and vestments;
- holding a crozier or Gospel book;
- carrying one or two candles;
- with crossed candles at a person’s throat;
- holding an iron comb or rake;
- undergoing comb-torture;
- blessing a choking child;
- accompanied by wolf, pig, birds or wild animals;
- holding a model of Dubrovnik.

The iron comb simultaneously symbolizes torture and wool work. The candles signify feast-day ritual, the widow’s offering and light. The child signifies throat protection. Animals refer to cave legends and pastoral patronage. In Dubrovnik, the city model marks civic guardianship.

## Controversies, Allegations and Evidentiary Problems

### Historicity of the detailed biography

**Documented fact:** Medieval Greek and Latin manuscript traditions contain a *Passio* of Blaise.

**Unresolved question:** Whether the cult preserves a substantially historical bishop-martyr or whether very little beyond name, title and location can be recovered.

**Critical assessment:** The detailed acts are late, formulaic and miraculous. The older Catholic Encyclopedia itself judged the particulars “purely legendary” and without historical value, while retaining the traditional identification as bishop and martyr.

### Chronological conflict

Blaise is sometimes assigned to Diocletian’s persecution, sometimes Licinius’s, and sometimes to a narrative spanning both. The two contexts are not interchangeable. The lack of a contemporary date makes “316” conventional rather than certain.

### Agricolaus

Agricolaus may have been a historical regional official, but the sources for Blaise do not independently establish his office, jurisdiction or encounter with Blaise.

### Physician claim

Repeated as fact in popular summaries, it remains an unverified tradition. It may be early, but presently available evidence does not document training or practice.

### Confusion with other saints named Blaise

Martyrologies contain other martyrs called Blaise, Blasius, Blastus or similar forms. Relics and narratives may consequently have been misassigned. A Roman Blaise or Blastus associated with 17 June and the martyr Diogenes is not demonstrably identical to Blaise of Sebaste.

### Relic authenticity

There are mutually incompatible claims to multiple bodies, heads and limbs. Individual shrines often possess institutional authentications, inventories or episcopal recognitions, but these document permission for veneration or chain of custody within the institution—not scientific identity.

Possible fraud, simony and deliberate fabrication belong to the general history of the medieval relic economy. No court finding or modern investigation reviewed here establishes that a specific major Blaise shrine knowingly committed fraud.

### Health claims

Devotional claims of cures are testimonies of belief unless medically documented. Throat blessing should not be presented as a substitute for treatment of choking, infection, cancer or other disease.

### Adversarial-search result

Dedicated searches combined the subject’s name with *controversy*, *scandal*, *allegations*, *lawsuit*, *fraud*, *accusations*, *conspiracy* and *criticism*, including 2024–2026 material.

No credible evidence was found of personal financial crime, sexual misconduct, political corruption, intelligence activity, litigation or comparable wrongdoing by the purported fourth-century man. The meaningful contested areas are:

- historical existence and recoverability;
- late and legendary Acts;
- inconsistency in persecution chronology;
- confusion with namesake saints;
- anatomically excessive relic claims;
- possible relic misidentification, manufacture or sale;
- civic and ecclesiastical use of the cult;
- scientifically unverified miracle and healing claims;
- disputed interpretation of the Dubrovnik head reliquary.

A 2020 controversy over an inadequately restored nineteenth-century statue at Carosino concerns modern custodians and conservators, not Blaise.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors and Disputed Narratives

### Animals attending him in the cave

**Story:** Wild animals gathered peacefully, sought healing and waited for his blessing.  
**Appearance:** Medieval Greek and Latin passion traditions; later popularized by the *Golden Legend*.  
**Evidence:** No contemporary corroboration.  
**Effect:** Patronage of wild and domestic animals.

### Birds bringing food

**Story:** Birds fed or attended Blaise in his retreat.  
**Status:** Variant ascetic legend.  
**Effect:** Iconography of birds and comparison with desert saints.

### Wolf returning the widow’s pig

**Story:** At Blaise’s command, a wolf returned a poor woman’s only pig unharmed.  
**Evidence:** Hagiographic only.  
**Effect:** Association with pigs, livestock and household prosperity.

### Healing the choking child

**Story:** Blaise saved a child obstructed by a fishbone.  
**Evidence:** Hagiographic only; names and clinical details are absent.  
**Effect:** Throat patronage, ritual blessing and modern medical symbolism.

### Walking or standing on water

**Story:** Blaise survived immersion and stood on a lake while pagan challengers drowned.  
**Evidence:** Hagiographic only.  
**Effect:** Demonstration of sacred power in visual and literary cycles.

### Miraculous milk from wounded women

**Story:** Milk flowed from the mutilated breasts of women who collected Blaise’s blood.  
**Evidence:** Martyr-legend motif; no independent corroboration.  
**Effect:** Intensification of the narrative’s themes of bodily sanctity and supernatural nourishment.

### Inventor of wool-combing

**Story:** Guild pageants transformed Blaise into the inventor or civilizer of the wool-combing trade.  
**Evidence against:** The technology long predates the supposed fourth-century bishop.  
**Effect:** Strengthened his role in English and continental textile identity.

### Warning Dubrovnik of Venetian attack

**Story:** Blaise appeared to the priest Stojko in 971/972 and exposed Venetian plans to seize Ragusa.  
**Evidence:** Later civic tradition, not contemporary documentation.  
**Effect:** Made Blaise the personification of Dubrovnik’s vigilance and independence.

### Storm carrying relics to Maratea

**Story:** A ship bearing Blaise’s relics toward Rome was stopped by a storm at Maratea in 732; the relics remained there by providential choice.  
**Evidence:** Local translation tradition; no contemporary voyage record.  
**Effect:** Established Maratea as a major shrine and pilgrimage center.

### Byzantine imperial crown reliquary

**Story or scholarly assumption:** Dubrovnik’s head reliquary copied a Byzantine imperial crown.  
**Counter-evidence:** Ana Munk’s material, epigraphic and iconographic study rejected that interpretation and assigned the enamels to Dubrovnik, approximately 1164–1180.  
**Effect:** Revision of the reliquary’s artistic and political interpretation.

### Pirate explanation for Maratea’s covered statue

**Story:** The statue was covered in red so pirates could not see its silver glittering from the sea.  
**Documented alternative:** The covering arose from an eighteenth-century jurisdictional conflict between parishes and a 1781 order suppressing a disputed procession.  
**Effect:** A forgotten legal compromise became protective folklore.

## Works by or Attributed to Blaise

No genuine work by Blaise is known.

### Genuine surviving works

None.

### Letters, sermons, medical writings or theological treatises

None securely attributed.

### The *Passio Sancti Blasii*

- Greek repertory: BHG 276–276f
- Latin repertory: BHL 1370–1380h
- Genre: posthumous hagiography
- Authorship: anonymous
- Status: written about Blaise, not by him

### Liturgical prayers and blessings

Prayers invoking Blaise, formulas for throat blessing, offices, hymns and local propers are posthumous cultic compositions. They should not be listed as his authored speech.

### Speeches in the Acts

The trial speeches and prayers placed in Blaise’s mouth are literary attributions. No transcript establishes them as his words.

No major biographical feature film or modern documentary devoted exclusively to the historically reconstructable life of Blaise was identified.

Relevant visual and archival materials include:

- Dubrovnik Museums’ 2012 exhibition **St Blaise in History and the Present**, comprising 339 objects from numerous institutions.
- The Dubrovnik Museums mobile and digital project **St Blaise & Dubrovnik**.
- UNESCO documentation and audiovisual coverage of the annual Dubrovnik festivity.
- Manuscripts of the Greek *Passio* catalogued by Biblissima in collections including the Vatican Library, Bibliothèque nationale de France, National Library of Greece, Grottaferrata, Brussels and the University of Michigan.
- Medieval illuminated manuscripts and stained-glass cycles depicting the arrest, torture and execution.
- Museum catalogues documenting iron-comb torture iconography.
- Local Italian archival inventories, episcopal acts and shrine records concerning claimed relics.

These materials document the cult’s transmission. They are not contemporary records of Blaise’s life.

## Death and Burial

The reconstructable minimum is limited:

- Blaise was remembered as a Christian martyr associated with Sebaste.
- His cult identified him as a bishop.
- Tradition assigned his commemoration to 3 February in the West.
- Later narrative specified torture and beheading under Agricolaus during Licinius’s reign.

No remains can presently be linked to him by a continuous contemporary provenance. His traditional grave at Sebaste has not been securely identified.

---

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Blaise’s importance lies less in reconstructable biography than in the unusually broad cultural life generated by his name.

His cult joined:

- late-antique martyr veneration;
- episcopal ideals;
- ascetic animal legends;
- household and agricultural protection;
- healing ritual;
- textile guild identity;
- urban government;
- relic diplomacy;
- pilgrimage;
- visual art;
- medical professional symbolism.

He remains liturgically commemorated in Catholic, Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox and some Anglican settings. Local devotion is particularly prominent in Croatia, Italy, parts of Germany, France, Spain, Latin America and the Philippines.

The Dubrovnik festivity remains one of the largest continuous public expressions of the cult and functions simultaneously as religious observance, civic identity and intangible cultural heritage.

Modern critical scholarship has not simply “disproved” Blaise. It has narrowed what can responsibly be called biography and redirected attention toward manuscript history, ritual, civic symbolism, material culture and folklore.

## Atlas Connections

The roster was checked for documented personal or posthumous connections. Because Blaise’s lifetime relationships are almost entirely unknown, no direct meeting, correspondence, family tie or collaboration with another roster member can be established.

### Jacobus de Voragine

**Nature:** Major posthumous literary transmitter.  
**Date and place:** *Legenda aurea*, composed in Latin in thirteenth-century Italy, approximately 1259–1266.  
**Label:** **Documented fact.**

Jacobus incorporated Blaise’s legend into the *Legenda aurea*, helping disseminate it through medieval Western Europe. This is a documented influence on Blaise’s posthumous reputation, not a lifetime relationship.

### Constantine the Great

**Nature:** Political context only. Constantine was Licinius’s co-ruler and rival; later Constantinian sources describe Licinius’s anti-Christian policies.  
**Label:** **Reported historical context, not a personal connection.**

No source documents a meeting, correspondence or acknowledged relationship between Blaise and Constantine. Mere contemporaneity is insufficient for an atlas connection.

### Saint Acacius of Byzantium, Saint Barbara of Heliopolis, Saint Catherine of Alexandria, Saint Cyriacus of Rome, Saint Denis of Paris, Saint Erasmus of Formia, Saint Eustace, Saint George of Lydda, Saint Giles, Saint Pantaleon of Nicomedia and Saint Vitus

**Nature:** Posthumous grouping with Blaise among the Fourteen Holy Helpers.  
**Date and place:** Collective devotion developed especially in the Rhineland and German-speaking Europe during the later Middle Ages.  
**Label:** **Documented posthumous cultic association.**

This does not establish that the saints met or belonged to a historical organization.
