# Dalila Puzzovio: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Note

Dalila Puzzovio is a living Argentine visual artist, designer, art director, costume designer, interior designer, and participant in the Buenos Aires avant-garde associated with the Instituto Torcuato Di Tella.

The surviving record is strongest for her artistic career from 1961 through the 1970s. Family history, finances, and the precise chronology of some later commercial work are comparatively thin. Several frequently repeated details conflict, especially her year of birth, the dimensions and number of shoes in *Dalila doble plataforma*, and the precise corner occupied by the 1965 billboard *¿Por qué son tan geniales?* Those conflicts are retained below.

Evidentiary terms used:

- **Documented fact:** supported by an institutional, archival, contemporary, or first-person source.
- **Reported fact:** stated in credible journalism or scholarly secondary literature but not independently confirmed here from an underlying record.
- **Artist’s account:** Puzzovio’s recollection in an interview; valuable primary testimony, but potentially affected by memory and retrospective interpretation.
- **Interpretation:** a curator’s or scholar’s analysis rather than a directly verifiable biographical fact.
- **Unverified or disputed:** conflicting or insufficiently supported.
- **No evidence located:** the searches conducted did not produce substantiation; this does not prove nonexistence.

### Birth-year conflict

The most defensible formulation is **“born in Buenos Aires, probably in 1942 or 1943.”**

- Museo Moderno’s 2025 retrospective, based on extensive work with the artist and her archive, says **1942**.
- Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes also says **1942**.
- Getty’s Union List of Artist Names says **1943**.
- A 23 October 2022 profile called her 79, also pointing to **1943**.
- Fundación IDA’s archival authority record says **1945**.
- Older general-reference texts repeat 1943 or 1945.

Because an official birth certificate or direct statement of the complete date was not located, no exact date should be treated as established.

## Names and Self-Fashioning

A Museo Moderno text reports that she “had been Delia” until Charlie Squirru insisted on “Dalila,” associating the name with the biblical Delilah as reimagined by Hedy Lamarr in Cecil B. DeMille’s *Samson and Delilah*. This is a significant piece of deliberate self-fashioning: the glamorous, potentially dangerous cinematic woman became part of Puzzovio’s public identity.

The name change does not appear to have been documented as a legal change. “Dalila” became her stable professional and public name. The episode belongs partly to biography and partly to the couple’s self-created mythology.

Journalists and institutions have subsequently applied titles such as:

- “icono pop”
- “musa del Di Tella”
- “sacerdotisa del pop en la Argentina”
- “reina del pop”

These are reputational labels, not offices or formal titles.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and immigration

**Documented through Puzzovio’s testimony and profiles:** she was born into an Italian immigrant family originating in Lecce, in the Apulia region of southern Italy. She described herself as a first-generation Argentine.

Her father was named **Armando Puzzovio**. A 2022 profile identifies him as an engineer and draftsman; Puzzovio’s 2025 interview calls him an industrialist who owned a factory with his brother. These descriptions are compatible: he may have been technically trained and also operated an industrial concern.

Puzzovio recalled that her father actively supported her drawing and painting. He supplied art materials, paid for instruction, and did not prevent her from pursuing art, even if he may have preferred a more conventional future. She characterized the family as strict, loving, and financially comfortable enough to make artistic training possible.

The father’s factory, line of business, assets, and subsequent ownership history were not identified in the consulted sources.

Her mother’s name, birthplace, and occupation were not established.

### Siblings

Puzzovio said there were **two sisters** and that she was the younger. Her elder sister became a psychiatrist. The sister’s name and career details were not located.

### Childhood material culture

Puzzovio remembered European ships bringing fabric samples, silks, shirts, ties, and suiting materials to Buenos Aires. Sailors visited Italian families with swatches and merchandise. She recalled these visits as a ritual. This domestic exposure to textiles and imported fashion later became part of her account of the origins of her design sensibility.

### Marriage and extended family

Puzzovio met artist **Carlos María Miguel “Charlie” Squirru** in approximately 1962. Charlie was a younger brother of critic and museum director **Rafael Squirru**, founder and first director of the Museo de Arte Moderno de Buenos Aires.

The couple reportedly married after consulting the astrologer **Horangel**, who selected 14 February—later internationally associated with Valentine’s Day—as an auspicious date. Accounts do not consistently supply the marriage year. By 2016 they were described as having been married for about fifty years.

Puzzovio recalled that her father and Charlie’s mother had reservations about the match because both partners were artists, but neither family stopped it.

Charlie and Dalila remained spouses, collaborators, and close companions until his death on 17 August 2022. Contemporary profiles describe their relationship as artistically intimate but not a merger of individual authorship: they collaborated extensively while maintaining distinct bodies of work.

No reliable evidence of children, other spouses, or publicly documented extramarital partners was found.

## Childhood and Early Life

Puzzovio grew up in Buenos Aires in an observant or conventionally religious educational environment rather than in a documented deeply theological household. She and her sister attended the Catholic girls’ school **Nuestra Señora de la Misericordia**, on Avenida Cabildo.

She recalled emerging from “colegio de monja” with limited experience of the broader social world. This transition—from a protected, disciplined domestic and school environment into the experimental art scene—became an important element in her retrospective life story.

Drawing was present early. She said that she habitually tried to solve school assignments visually, “with a sheet” rather than through books. By her first year of secondary school, her father had enrolled her with painter, sculptor, and draftsman **Gaspar Besares Soraire**. She remembered him as helpful but authoritarian.

Her family bought her outdoor-painting equipment, including an easel. Published biographies sometimes claim that her artistic career began at twelve; this is plausible given her account, but the exact age remains a secondary-source approximation.

No evidence was found of prolonged childhood residence outside Buenos Aires, exile, imprisonment, institutionalization, major childhood illness, or an early family rupture.

## Education and Formation

Puzzovio did not follow a conventional academy-to-degree route in the sources consulted. Her formation was workshop-based.

### Gaspar Besares Soraire

In early adolescence, Besares Soraire guided her drawing and painting. Puzzovio later described his teaching as authoritative. He represents the more traditional phase of her instruction.

### Juan Batlle Planas

Between roughly 1955 and the early 1960s, she spent time in the workshop of surrealist painter **Juan Batlle Planas**. She later explained that she left or limited this association because she did not want to become merely his disciple.

Batlle Planas’s surrealism nevertheless supplied an early proximity to automatism, psychic imagery, and non-naturalistic form.

### Jaime Davidovich

At about seventeen, Puzzovio met **Jaime Davidovich**, an artist associated with Arte Nuevo and informalism. She credited him with giving her “wings” and the freedom to make her own work.

Under this influence she moved toward informalist, nonfigurative painting. She was also attracted to:

- Zen Buddhism
- Chinese poetry
- the compression and economy of haiku
- orientalizing concepts of space and gesture

These were artistic and philosophical influences; no evidence was found that she formally converted to Buddhism.

### Germaine Derbecq and Galería Lirolay

French artist and critic **Germaine Derbecq**, the first artistic director of Galería Lirolay, became an early advocate. Derbecq encouraged experimental young artists and invited Puzzovio to exhibit. Puzzovio’s first solo exhibition was held at Lirolay in 1961.

Derbecq described the early paintings as subtle like Chinese silks suggesting infinite spaces. Her support was critical in transferring Puzzovio from private instruction into the Buenos Aires avant-garde network.

### Rafael Squirru and Jorge Romero Brest

Rafael Squirru supported the new object-based work and included Puzzovio in exhibitions associated with the Museo de Arte Moderno. Later, **Jorge Romero Brest**, director of the Centro de Artes Visuales of the Instituto Torcuato Di Tella, brought her into the Di Tella orbit.

Romero Brest functioned as selector, institutional patron, critic, and gatekeeper. Puzzovio remembered the period as simultaneously collaborative and intensely competitive.

## Early Career, 1961–1964

### 1961: first solo exhibition

Puzzovio’s first individual exhibition, usually identified as *Informalismo*, opened at Galería Lirolay in Buenos Aires in 1961. The work consisted of nonfigurative oils connected to Argentine informalism and to the freedom she associated with Davidovich.

She received a mention in the Salón de la Mutualidad de Estudiantes y Egresados de Bellas Artes in 1961, according to later curricula.

### 1962: prizes and the turn toward objects

Later accounts list a **Faja de Honor** in the Premio Ver y Estimar in 1962. Sources also place her in *El hombre antes del hombre*, an exhibition organized under Rafael Squirru’s direction that brought together experimental objects.

Puzzovio showed an early plaster object alongside, among other works, an early mattress by Marta Minujín and an object by Rubén Santantonín. The exhibition helped establish the shift from painting to “arte de las cosas”—an Argentine object art related to, but not reducible to, US or British pop.

### The hand injury and Hospital Italiano

A problem or accident involving one of Puzzovio’s hands brought her to the Hospital Italiano. There she encountered discarded orthopedic casts. She began collecting and transforming them.

This origin story is supported by institutional and interview sources, although the date and medical nature of the injury are not specified. No lasting disability is documented.

### 1963: *Cáscaras*

Her individual exhibition *Cáscaras* was held at Galería Lirolay in 1963. The works incorporated discarded orthopedic casts and other materials. They evoked absent, injured, or constrained bodies while turning medical debris into autonomous objects.

The title, “Shells” or “Husks,” emphasized the cast as a body’s abandoned exterior. Later curators identify this as the foundation for her lifelong concern with:

- bodily contours
- the relation between body and covering
- clothing as a second skin
- absence and presence
- injury transformed into spectacle

A surviving photograph of Puzzovio among the *Cáscaras*, taken by Rubén Santantonín, is an important contemporary record.

Some short biographies misdate *Cáscaras* to 1964 or say it occurred at the Museo de Arte Moderno. The Galería Lirolay archive and recent Museo Moderno research support **Lirolay, 1963**.

### 1964: object art and international exposure

Puzzovio participated in exhibitions including *Objetos 64* and works dealing with death, funeral wreaths, shoe-shine boxes, corsets, and orthopedic fragments. Her *Coronas para los habitantes no humanos* or related funeral-wreath constructions are dated in different reconstructions to 1964 and later remade or extended through 1998.

She was one of approximately thirty artists in *New Art of Argentina*, organized by the Walker Art Center with the Instituto Torcuato Di Tella. She was also represented in *Buenos Aires 64* at the Pepsi-Cola Exhibition Gallery in New York.

These exhibitions placed her work within an international presentation of new Argentine art before she herself undertook a longer New York stay.

## The Pop and Di Tella Years, 1965–1968

### La Siempre Viva and the *Microsucesos*

In 1965, Puzzovio participated in the performance company **La Siempre Viva**, with Charlie Squirru, Marilú Marini, Edgardo Giménez, and Alfredo Rodríguez Arias.

The group developed *Microsucesos*: short, partly improvised performances in apartments and small theatres. Performers parodied advertising and popular culture, personifying commercial products and logos and involving spectators.

This activity is important because Puzzovio’s subsequent work cannot be reconstructed only as a sequence of objects. It joined:

- performance
- fashion
- domestic staging
- advertising
- publicity
- the creation of the artist as a public persona

### *¿Por qué son tan geniales?* (1965)

In August 1965, Puzzovio, Charlie Squirru, and Edgardo Giménez installed a large commercial-style billboard bearing their faces and the question *¿Por qué son tan geniales?* (“Why are they so brilliant/great?”).

The work advertised the artists themselves. It used the mechanisms of celebrity advertising before the artists possessed celebrity on the scale the billboard pretended.

**Location conflict:** sources variously identify:

- Florida and Viamonte;
- Viamonte and Córdoba;
- or, less precisely, a central Buenos Aires street corner.

The archival consensus generally favors **Florida and Viamonte**, while one recent art article says Córdoba and Viamonte. The exact placement should therefore be treated as disputed pending consultation of the contemporary permit, photographs, or newspapers.

The billboard circulated through contemporary press and provoked ridicule, fascination, and claims of arrogance. Its apparent boast was also self-parody. Later scholarship treats it as an early Argentine intervention into artistic celebrity, publicity, and mass-media validation.

### Competition and opposition

Puzzovio later said that conventional painters “hated us to death.” This should be read as the artist’s compressed description of an aesthetic conflict, not evidence of literal threats.

She recalled the Bar Moderno at Maipú and Paraguay as a volatile meeting ground where artists debated their careers and reputations. The scene combined friendship, competition, patronage, and publicity.

### *Autorretrato* (1966)

For the 1966 Premio Nacional Instituto Torcuato Di Tella, Puzzovio created a monumental illuminated or billboard-like self-portrait.

The image joined her own face to the reclining bikini-clad body of German model **Veruschka von Lehndorff**, whom she resembled. Commercial cinema-billboard painters executed the image, complicating conventional individual authorship.

Accounts describe the work as:

- approximately eight metres wide;
- a poster-panel or illuminated marquee;
- accompanied by inflatable cushions or bodily forms;
- an appropriation of advertising and fashion photography.

It received the **Second National Di Tella Prize**. Some journalistic and curriculum sources call it the national or grand prize without the qualification “second”; the Universidad Torcuato Di Tella and Fundación IDA specify second prize.

A scholarly dissertation reports that the bikini image scandalized conservatives. Puzzovio also recalled that police outside the Di Tella reacted warily to her flamboyant dress. These are reported social reactions, not evidence of a formal obscenity case or arrest.

The original monumental object did not survive intact. Puzzovio reconstructed or remade it in 1997, and Museo Moderno again restored its scale for the 2025 exhibition.

### Fashion as artistic medium

By the middle of the decade Puzzovio designed clothing for herself and wore it at exhibitions and media appearances. Her body and public appearance became extensions of the work.

She developed giant corset forms, dresses with scales, papier-mâché or celluloid accessories, jewelry, hats, and other hybrid fashion-objects. Later feminist and performance-oriented scholarship has interpreted this practice as an early investigation of constructed identity and the female body. Puzzovio did not necessarily use the later vocabulary of “performativity” at the time.

### *Dalila doble plataforma* (1967)

For the 1967 Premio Internacional Instituto Torcuato Di Tella, Puzzovio made *Dalila doble plataforma*, a display of fluorescent patent-leather platform shoes in a metal-and-acrylic structure.

The shoes were functioning commodities, produced with **Grimoldi**, exhibited as art at the Di Tella, and sold through Grimoldi shops. The project erased a stable division between artwork, industrial product, fashion accessory, installation, and advertising.

Puzzovio later summarized the effect: standing on the platforms changed the wearer’s viewpoint.

#### Object-description conflicts

Sources disagree on:

- **Number of pairs:** sixteen in recent Museo Moderno and Hammer accounts; twenty-five in several earlier descriptions.
- **Height:** one account gives fourteen-centimetre platforms.
- **Dimensions:** often approximately 300 × 300 × 30 cm, but later reconstructed versions are smaller.
- **Award:** institutional sources call it the second International Di Tella prize; condensed biographies sometimes omit “second.”

The safest conclusion is that the original installation displayed a series of approximately sixteen to twenty-five wearable double-platform shoes, in black and fluorescent patent leather, inside a purpose-built transparent display.

The work also received international attention and is associated in curricula with a prize at the Lima Biennial in 1967, though some sources date the Lima award to 1966.

### Commercial production

The Grimoldi collaboration was not merely appropriation of an existing shoe. It involved manufacture and retail distribution. No contract, royalty schedule, production count, sales revenue, trademark registration, or patent was located. Claims that Puzzovio “invented” the platform shoe would be too broad; her documented innovation was the particular **double-platform artwork/product**.

No patent for the shoe was found.

### Stage and screen

Around this period she designed clothing for the café-concert revue *Help Valentino*, whose cast included Antonio Gasalla, Edda Díaz, Norah Blay, and Carlos Perciavalle.

She and Charlie designed costumes for Héctor Olivera’s film *Psexoanálisis* (1968), including costumes worn by Libertad Leblanc. Her screen credit should be understood as costume design rather than acting or film direction.

## New York and Textile Design, circa 1969–early 1970s

After the decline or closure of the Di Tella visual-arts operation, Puzzovio and Charlie went to New York around 1969. Some accounts describe the move as a decision following the Di Tella’s closure; because institutional contraction took place over several years, the sequence is approximate.

In New York the couple concentrated on textile and surface design. Fundación IDA records more than **200 textile patterns**, many now preserved in its Puzzovio–Squirru archive.

A 2025 profile reports that designs created by the couple entered collections associated with **Oscar de la Renta** and **Calvin Klein**. This is a reported commercial connection; no surviving contracts or labeled production records were reviewed.

The consulted sources do not establish:

- the exact duration of the New York residence;
- their immigration or visa status;
- their address;
- employers or agents;
- income;
- whether every pattern reached manufacture.

They returned to Buenos Aires and continued to work across fashion, graphics, stage design, and commercial design.

## Fashion, Textiles, Publishing, Film, Theatre, and Television

### Dalila Tricot and knitwear

At the end of the 1960s, Puzzovio experimented at home with a manual **Knittax** knitting machine. This led to an artisanal clothing label, **Dalila Tricot**.

Reported retail outlets included:

- Madame Frou-Frou
- L’Interdit
- La Solderie
- Z
- Harrods Buenos Aires

Her knitwear included organic fibers, landscape patterns, pre-Columbian borders and tassels, transparent vinyl windows, accessories, hats, and aprons.

The evidence supports a brand or label and boutique distribution, but no company incorporation, investor structure, employee count, tax filing, or sales records were located.

### Dior connection

A later profile says she designed hats “for Dior.” The precise entity, season, contract, and whether the work was for the French house, an Argentine licensee, or a local boutique were not established. This should remain a **reported professional credit**, not a fully documented institutional appointment.

### Other costume work

Her documented or reported costume and wardrobe work includes:

- *Psexoanálisis* (film, 1968), directed by Héctor Olivera.
- *Help Valentino* (café-concert), costumes for Antonio Gasalla and other cast members.
- *La vera historia de Salomé*, a musical by Miguel Ángel Rondano based on Oscar Wilde, at Teatro San Martín.
- *Con sabor a Pinky* (television, 1977), with Charlie Squirru.
- Additional theatre, film, and television work through approximately 1985.

A complete credit-by-credit filmographic record was not found. Institutional summaries describe the output collectively and do not always separate Dalila’s individual credit from joint work with Charlie.

### Magazine and graphic work

Puzzovio wrote, illustrated, styled, and/or art-directed productions for fashion and lifestyle magazines through approximately 1990. The known work includes:

- fashion features;
- editorial illustrations;
- set and art direction;
- cover graphics, including later work associated with *Cuisine & Vins*;
- *Mientras unos destruyen, otros construyen* for *Claudia*.

Not every contribution has been bibliographically indexed. She was often responsible for visual conception rather than the bylined prose.

## *Mientras unos destruyen, otros construyen* (1979)

In August 1979, for issue 266 of *Claudia*, Puzzovio conceived a fashion photo-performance amid demolitions undertaken for the widening of Avenida 9 de Julio.

She:

- devised the mise-en-scène;
- designed the clothing and hats;
- staged models as participants in an opulent wedding;
- placed fashion spectacle against demolished houses and urban rubble.

Photographer **Isidoro Rubini** is credited with the images.

The work documented the destruction of parts of Buenos Aires under the military dictatorship, although sources consulted do not show Puzzovio presenting it as an explicit party-political manifesto. Its meanings arise from the juxtaposition of celebration, fashion, rubble, modernization, and erased urban memory.

Rolf Art later exhibited the series under the title *Mientras unos construyen, otros destruyen*. The title’s word order varies between sources.

## Interior Design, Architecture, and Commercial Environments

During the 1980s and 1990s, Puzzovio increasingly worked on interiors and architectural environments. Reported projects include:

- the Alberto Sanders hair salon;
- the men’s boutique **Limbo**;
- offices for **NEC Buenos Aires**;
- **Adrogué Plaza Shopping Center**;
- a triplex on Avenida del Libertador;
- private residences;
- retail and corporate interiors.

A university exhibition record says she received a Museo de la Ciudad award for the Alberto Sanders salon in 1987. Other curricula instead call the award “best recycled house” and variously date it to 1977, 1992, or 1997. These are probably either multiple recognitions or conflated projects; the exact award history requires consultation of Museo de la Ciudad registers.

Although journalism has called Puzzovio an “architect,” no architecture degree or professional registration was identified. “Interior designer,” “environmental designer,” and “architectural designer” are better-supported descriptions.

No property-development equity, NEC ownership interest, or investment in the Adrogué shopping center was found. The documented relationship is design work.

## Return to Gallery-Centered Practice, 1990s–Present

### Reconstruction and digital work

Puzzovio reconstructed major ephemeral works in the 1990s, including versions of *Autorretrato* and *Dalila doble plataforma*. These later objects are not identical to the lost or dismantled 1960s originals and should be catalogued with dual dates when appropriate.

From approximately 1998 she worked digitally with personal photographs, constructing collages and hybrid bodies. The series *Híbridos* grew from this practice.

## Political, Religious, Philosophical, and Artistic Development

### Political activity

No evidence was located that Puzzovio held elected office, belonged to a political party, served in government, financed a political campaign, or joined an armed political organization.

Her work operated within politically charged contexts:

- the 1966 Onganía coup and dictatorship;
- police and conservative hostility toward the Di Tella milieu;
- closure and contraction of avant-garde cultural spaces;
- the 1976–83 military dictatorship;
- the demolition of central Buenos Aires for Avenida 9 de Julio.

Her work has been interpreted politically through consumer culture, gender, public space, and urban destruction. That interpretation should not be converted into an undocumented party affiliation.

### Religion and esotericism

Puzzovio attended Catholic schools. No continuing formal religious affiliation is documented.

Her early painting drew on Zen Buddhism and Chinese poetry. She and Charlie studied or used astrology, numerology, and symbolic color systems. Their circa 1969–70 Hartford zodiac cup designs coded water, fire, earth, and air chromatically.

The story that astrologer Horangel selected their wedding date shows astrology functioning in their private mythology and decision-making. There is no evidence of membership in an occult order or sect.

### Artistic development

Her practice can be reconstructed as a continuum rather than a succession of abandoned careers:

1. informalist painting;
2. casts and shells as absent bodies;
3. funeral and medical objects;
4. the public self as an image;
5. clothing and shoes as extensions of the body;
6. fashion photography as staged performance;
7. interiors as inhabited scenography;
8. digital collage as reconstruction of the personal image.

Later curators emphasize that body, covering, and environment are three layers of one investigation.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Charlie Squirru — spouse and collaborator

Their partnership joined marriage, travel, textile design, graphics, performance, costume, interiors, astrology, and domestic scenography. They made more than 200 textile designs during or around their New York period and jointly created costumes and commercial graphics.

Charlie died on 17 August 2022 after a prolonged illness culminating in cardiac arrest. Puzzovio later described him as spouse, friend, and artistic companion of nearly six decades.

### Rafael Squirru — advocate and brother-in-law

Founder and first director of the Museo de Arte Moderno de Buenos Aires, Rafael helped create institutional space for Puzzovio’s generation. He was not merely connected through marriage; his exhibitions preceded or accompanied her relationship with Charlie.

### Jorge Romero Brest — institutional patron and gatekeeper

Romero Brest selected and promoted Puzzovio within the Di Tella. His patronage provided visibility but also placed her within an intensely competitive system structured by juries, prizes, press, and access.

### Germaine Derbecq — early curator and mentor

Derbecq gave Puzzovio her early Lirolay opportunity and publicly interpreted the paintings. Her role was decisive in the transition from student to exhibiting artist.

### Jaime Davidovich — teacher and liberating influence

Puzzovio explicitly credited Davidovich with allowing her to escape imitation of Batlle Planas and develop her own informalist vocabulary.

### Edgardo Giménez — collaborator

Giménez collaborated with Puzzovio and Charlie on *¿Por qué son tan geniales?* and shared performance, design, and Di Tella networks.

### Marta Minujín — peer, competitor, and friend

The women first exhibited at Lirolay in the same general period. Puzzovio’s 2025 phrase that they were “karma” for one another condenses decades of proximity, competition, mutual recognition, and media comparison. No evidence was found of a lasting personal rupture.

## Illnesses, Injuries, Crises, and Bereavement

- A hand injury or condition led Puzzovio to the Hospital Italiano and indirectly generated *Cáscaras*. The diagnosis and date are not documented.
- No reliable evidence was found of addiction, psychiatric hospitalization, criminal violence, suicide attempt, or disabling later illness.
- Charlie Squirru’s prolonged illness and death in 2022 were a major bereavement. Press interviews show Puzzovio continuing to live and work independently afterward.
- As of the 2025 Museo Moderno retrospective, she was giving interviews and participating in the interpretation and reconstruction of her work.

- linked to an intelligence service;
- involved in a documented plagiarism or provenance scandal;
- subject to a substantiated professional-misconduct allegation.

Search results containing political investigations concerned politicians attending or conducting an official tribute to her, not allegations against Puzzovio.

### Historically documented controversy

The meaningful controversies surrounding her concern artistic legitimacy and public display:

1. **The 1965 billboard:** treated by some contemporaries as arrogant self-advertising; framed by its makers as both promotion and satire.
2. **The 1966 bikini self-portrait:** reportedly scandalized conservative observers.
3. **Commercial manufacture of *Doble plataforma*:** challenged the distinction between artwork and retail commodity.
4. **Use of professional sign painters:** unsettled conventional assumptions that the artist must personally execute the image.
5. **Fashion versus “serious art”:** later scholarship argues that her design career contributed to her being marginalized in art histories that privileged autonomous gallery objects.
6. **Retrospective feminist interpretation:** current institutions read the work through female autonomy, embodiment, and performativity. This is a consequential reinterpretation, though it does not establish that every later theoretical position was explicitly articulated by Puzzovio in the 1960s.

### Factual and cataloguing disputes

- birth year: 1942/1943/1945;
- first name: Delia before Dalila;
- number of platform-shoe pairs: sixteen/twenty-five;
- billboard location: Florida–Viamonte/Córdoba–Viamonte;
- Lima award: 1966/1967;
- recycled-house or interior award: 1977/1987/1992/1997;
- whether certain reconstructed works should carry only their original date or dual original/reconstruction dates.

These are documentary inconsistencies, not allegations of deception.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Why are they so brilliant?”

**Story:** Three unknown or emerging artists placed their giant faces in central Buenos Aires and publicly declared—or asked—why they were geniuses.

**Origins:** The 1965 billboard itself and contemporary newspaper circulation.

**Circulators:** Puzzovio, Squirru, Giménez, journalists, later historians of Argentine pop.

**Evidence:** Photographs and contemporary records establish the work. Its exact location varies in later accounts.

**Meaning and effect:** It created a durable myth of the Di Tella artist as flamboyant, narcissistic, media-savvy, and self-invented. The apparent vanity was also an inquiry into how publicity produces artistic reputation.

### The dangerous “Dalila”

**Story:** Delia became Dalila through Charlie’s invocation of the biblical seductress and Hedy Lamarr’s cinematic incarnation.

**Origins:** Recounted in the Museo Moderno’s 2025 book description.

**Evidence:** Strong as couple lore; legal documentation of a name change was not found.

**Effect:** It joined biblical myth, Hollywood glamour, erotic danger, and personal branding.

### “They were afraid of her”

Puzzovio recalled that police near the Di Tella feared or were unsettled by her extravagant appearance. This is **artist’s testimony**, plausible within the Onganía regime’s policing of appearance, but not an independently documented police incident. There is no record that she was arrested.

### Inventor of the platform shoe

Popular retellings sometimes imply that Puzzovio invented platform footwear. Platforms existed centuries earlier and were already part of twentieth-century fashion.

The documented claim is narrower: she designed an innovative fluorescent **double-platform shoe installation and retail product** in 1967, using height and altered viewpoint as an artistic proposition.

### “Muse of the Di Tella”

The phrase casts Puzzovio as inspirational image rather than producer. It reflects her carefully constructed appearance and press visibility but can obscure her authorship, technical direction, commercial work, and institutional awards.

### Seamless pop marriage

Later profiles often present Dalila and Charlie as an ideal “pop couple.” Evidence supports a durable marriage and extensive collaboration, but the published record is curated and celebratory. It provides little access to ordinary conflict, division of income, domestic labor, or authorship negotiations.

### The forgotten woman restored

Recent exhibitions frame Puzzovio as insufficiently recognized because fashion, domestic craft, and collaborative production were marginalized. There is evidence for uneven visibility: she received major prizes early, then moved partly outside the gallery system, followed by substantial rediscovery after the late 1990s.

The stronger historical formulation is not that she disappeared entirely, but that her canonical status fluctuated and that later gender- and design-oriented scholarship altered the terms on which her work was valued.

## Later Life and Present Status

Charlie Squirru died in Buenos Aires on 17 August 2022 after a long illness that ended in cardiac arrest. Puzzovio continued living in Palermo, Buenos Aires. Press photographs show a home filled with selected design objects, period furniture, a functioning 1960s Siam refrigerator, English tableware, Murano glass, and elements of the couple’s long domestic scenography.

In 2025 the Museo de Arte Moderno de Buenos Aires opened *Dalila Puzzovio: Autorretrato*, the most extensive recent reassessment of her career. Pino Monkes and Patricio Orellana curated the exhibition.

It included:

- early informalist paintings;
- orthopedic-plaster works;
- reconstructed corsets;
- the monumental scale of *Autorretrato*;
- *Dalila doble plataforma*;
- garments and fashion records;
- a reconstruction of the couple’s domestic interior;
- the 1979 Avenida 9 de Julio series;
- previously unpublished archival material.

The exhibition ran from 12 April 2025 to 22 February 2026 according to the museum’s current page. Some initial publicity gave an earlier closing date in November 2025.

Puzzovio participated in interviews, publication preparation, and interpretation of the exhibition. No death report was located; she remains living as of the latest sources consulted.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Because Puzzovio is living, her legacy remains under active construction.

### Art and fashion

Her principal historical contribution lies in making fashion neither a subject represented by art nor a secondary commercial career, but an artistic medium operating in galleries, shops, magazines, streets, and on bodies.

*Doble plataforma* is repeatedly cited as a foundational Argentine example of an object that was simultaneously:

- sculpture;
- installation;
- fashion design;
- industrial product;
- commodity;
- wearable performance device.

### Publicity and artistic identity

*¿Por qué son tan geniales?* anticipated later artist-branding practices by turning publicity itself into the work. *Autorretrato* similarly treated identity as an assembled image made from fashion photography, commercial labor, bodily resemblance, and display technology.

### Gender and feminist reinterpretation

Later curators place Puzzovio within histories of women artists who challenged:

- the separation of art and craft;
- the designation of fashion as a lesser or feminized field;
- the passivity of the female fashion image;
- the expectation that serious art remain autonomous from commerce.

Puzzovio’s historical work supports these readings, but her original statements should not automatically be rewritten in the vocabulary of later feminist theory.

### Design history

The Fundación IDA archive and design exhibitions have expanded her legacy beyond pop-art surveys. Her textile patterns, product graphics, costumes, magazines, and interiors reveal sustained participation in Argentine design history.

### Reconstruction and authenticity

Because important works were billboard-sized, wearable, sold, dismantled, or ephemeral, Puzzovio’s legacy also raises cataloguing questions:

- What is the “original” of a retail artwork?
- Is a reconstructed billboard the work or documentation of it?
- Should dates reflect conception, fabrication, reconstruction, or exhibition?
- How should joint commercial and artistic labor be credited?

Modern displays frequently use dual dates or explicitly label recreations.
