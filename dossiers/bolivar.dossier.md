# Simón Bolívar: Research Dossier

## Scope, Method, and Evidentiary Labels

This dossier reconstructs the life, writings, political career, relationships, controversies, mythology, death, and posthumous uses of Simón Bolívar. It draws on Bolívar’s correspondence and public documents, nineteenth-century documentary collections, archival catalogues, scholarly articles, medical literature, and major modern biographies.

Labels used for contested material:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary documents, official records, or strong convergent evidence.
- **Reported fact:** stated by credible witnesses or historians but not independently established in every detail.
- **Disputed interpretation:** the underlying events are documented, but their meaning, motivation, or responsibility remains contested.
- **Allegation or theory:** a claim advanced without conclusive evidence.
- **Rumor or myth:** folklore, propaganda, literary invention, or an unsupported story significant chiefly for its cultural effects.

Dates follow the Gregorian calendar.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full baptismal name:** Simón José Antonio de la Santísima Trinidad Bolívar Palacios.

Longer genealogical forms—including **Simón José Antonio de la Santísima Trinidad Bolívar Ponte y Palacios Blanco**—appear in later biographical and genealogical literature. The shorter “Bolívar y Palacios” or “Bolívar Palacios” is conventional.

**Common name:** Simón Bolívar.

**Titles and epithets:**

- **El Libertador** (“The Liberator”), formally bestowed by the municipality of Caracas in October 1813 and subsequently confirmed in political usage.
- **Libertador de Colombia**, **Libertador Presidente**, and **Padre de la Patria**, in contemporary or commemorative usage.
- Captain, colonel, brigadier, major general, and general-in-chief at different stages of the independence wars.
- Supreme Chief of the Republic of Venezuela during several revolutionary governments.
- President of the Republic of Colombia—later called Gran Colombia—from 1819 to 1830, though frequently absent on campaign and periodically exercising extraordinary powers.
- Dictator of Peru, 1824–1826.
- The Republic of Bolívar—soon renamed **Bolivia**—was named for him in 1825. He was designated its first president but exercised the office only nominally and briefly, leaving Antonio José de Sucre to govern.

**Birth:** 24 July 1783, Caracas, Captaincy General of Venezuela, Spanish Empire.

**Baptism:** 30 July 1783, Caracas Cathedral.

**Death:** approximately 1:00 p.m., 17 December 1830, Quinta de San Pedro Alejandrino, near Santa Marta, Republic of Colombia, aged 47.

**Recorded cause of death:** a wasting pulmonary illness diagnosed by his physician, Alexandre‑Prosper Révérend, as tuberculosis or “pulmonary phthisis.”

**Modern differential diagnoses:** tuberculosis remains the conventional diagnosis, but histoplasmosis, paracoccidioidomycosis, chronic arsenic exposure, bronchiectasis, and other illnesses have been proposed. No modern diagnosis is conclusive.

**Nationality:** Born a Spanish American subject; subsequently Venezuelan and Colombian in the changing constitutional senses of those terms.

**Religion:** Baptized and raised Roman Catholic. His mature private beliefs were influenced by Enlightenment rationalism and were not consistently orthodox. He nevertheless invoked Providence, supported public religion as a source of social cohesion, received Catholic rites near death, and was buried in consecrated ground.

**Physical descriptions:** Contemporary observers commonly described him as short or of modest height, thin, restless, physically resilient, dark-haired in youth, with penetrating eyes and exceptionally animated speech and movement. Later heroic portraits often idealized and Europeanized his appearance.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal line

The Bolívars originated in the Basque country. An ancestor generally identified as **Simón de Bolívar y de la Rentería**, from the area of Ziortza-Bolibar in Biscay, migrated to Spanish America in the late sixteenth century. He and his descendants entered municipal office, colonial administration, landholding, mining, commerce, and militia service.

Over generations, the family intermarried with other elite Caracas families and accumulated:

- cacao plantations;
- cattle estates;
- urban real estate;
- the Aroa copper properties;
- offices and social privileges;
- enslaved laborers.

The family sought aristocratic distinctions associated with the proposed Marquisate of San Luis and Viscounty of Cocorote, but the titles were never finally secured.

### Parents

**Father:** Juan Vicente Bolívar y Ponte (1726–1786), a wealthy landowner and militia colonel.

**Mother:** María de la Concepción Palacios y Blanco (1758–1792), from another prominent Caracas family.

Juan Vicente died when Simón was two. María de la Concepción died, probably of tuberculosis, when he was nine. Bolívar’s early experience was consequently marked by wealth, orphanhood, disputed guardianship, and emotional separation within an extended household.

A later controversy concerns accusations that Juan Vicente had sexual relations with girls or young women in dependent positions. Such allegations appear in colonial complaints examined by modern biographers. They concern Bolívar’s father, not Simón Bolívar, and their precise circumstances remain dependent on interpretation of surviving legal material.

### Siblings

- **María Antonia Bolívar Palacios** (1777–1842). She remained royalist for much of the independence struggle, lived in exile, and later corresponded extensively with Bolívar about politics, lawsuits, property, and family interests.
- **Juana Nepomucena Bolívar Palacios** (1779–1847).
- **Juan Vicente Bolívar Palacios** (1781–1811), who participated in the revolutionary diplomatic mission to the United States and died in a shipwreck while returning to Venezuela.
- **María del Carmen Bolívar Palacios**, who died in infancy.

Bolívar’s correspondence with María Antonia is an important source for the management of the Aroa mines and other family property.

### Caregivers and household

Bolívar was cared for by enslaved and formerly enslaved women, especially:

- **Hipólita Bolívar**, remembered by him with exceptional affection and described in a later letter as the person whose milk had nourished him and as his mother.
- **Matea Bolívar**, another household caregiver who survived into old age and became a living link in the nineteenth-century Bolívar cult.

The family’s dependence on enslaved labor is documented. Bolívar therefore grew up simultaneously as a beneficiary of slavery and, later, an advocate of legal emancipation.

### Marriage

Bolívar married **María Teresa Rodríguez del Toro y Alayza** in Madrid on 26 May 1802. She belonged to a Venezuelan-Spanish elite family. They returned to Venezuela in July 1802 and lived at or visited Caracas and the Bolívar estates.

María Teresa died in Caracas on 22 January 1803, most probably of yellow fever, though malaria has also been suggested. They had no children.

Bolívar later declared that her death redirected him from domestic life toward politics. That retrospective statement is documentary evidence for his self-understanding, not proof that widowhood alone caused his revolutionary career. He never remarried.

### Children and disputed paternity

No legitimate or securely documented biological children are known.

Claims that Bolívar fathered children with Fanny du Villars, Manuela Sáenz, or other partners are unproven. Some later families asserted descent from him, but no claim has achieved documentary consensus. Assertions that he was sterile are likewise not medically established.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1783–1799

Bolívar was born into the **mantuanos**, Caracas’s white Creole landholding elite. His childhood environments included:

- the family house in Caracas;
- the San Mateo estate in the Aragua valleys;
- other plantations and urban properties;
- households sustained by enslaved domestic and agricultural labor.

Following his mother’s death, guardianship disputes developed within the Palacios family. His maternal grandfather Feliciano Palacios initially supervised the children’s interests. After the grandfather’s death, Simón came under the authority of his uncle **Carlos Palacios**, with whom he had a difficult relationship.

In 1795, the twelve-year-old Bolívar left or fled his uncle’s house and sought to live with his married sister María Antonia. The dispute reached colonial authorities, who ordered him placed in the household of **Simón Rodríguez**, one of his teachers. The episode is documented, although later patriotic narratives enlarged it into a precocious rebellion against every form of authority.

### Early teachers

- **Simón Rodríguez**—born Simón Carreño Rodríguez—was his most celebrated teacher. Rodríguez was an educational reformer influenced by Rousseau and the Enlightenment. His later role in Bolívar’s intellectual formation is unquestionable, though patriotic hagiography probably exaggerated the duration and exclusivity of the childhood instruction.
- **Andrés Bello**, later a major grammarian, jurist, poet, and educator, taught the young Bolívar in some capacity.
- **Fray Francisco de Andújar** instructed him in mathematics and other subjects.
- Other tutors supplied instruction in languages, history, religion, riding, fencing, and elite social accomplishments.

Bolívar was not a systematic scholar in the later university sense. His education combined private tutoring, social formation, extensive travel, conversation, and broad but uneven reading.

In January 1797 he entered a Caracas militia unit associated with his family’s status. This was both aristocratic initiation and practical military exposure.

## Education and Formation in Europe, 1799–1807

### First Atlantic journey

In 1799 Bolívar sailed from La Guaira for Spain. His route included Veracruz and Mexico City, followed by Havana, before he reached the Iberian Peninsula. These stops exposed him to other colonial elites and to political discussion during the crisis-ridden final decades of Spain’s American empire.

In Madrid he lived under the supervision of relatives, especially his maternal uncle **Esteban Palacios**, and entered aristocratic society. He studied languages, history, mathematics, fencing, dancing, and social etiquette.

### Marriage and bereavement

Bolívar met María Teresa Rodríguez del Toro in Madrid. After their 1802 marriage they returned to Venezuela. Her death in January 1803 led him back to Europe.

### Paris, Napoleon, and intellectual formation

From 1804 Bolívar spent significant time in Paris. He moved in expatriate and salon society and had a relationship with **Fanny du Villars**, usually identified as a married cousin or distant relative by marriage. The emotional and sexual character of the relationship is widely reported but incompletely documented.

He read or discussed:

- Rousseau;
- Montesquieu;
- Voltaire;
- classical history;
- modern republicanism;
- British constitutional practice;
- French revolutionary politics.

Simón Rodríguez rejoined him in Europe and became a traveling companion and intellectual interlocutor.

Bolívar witnessed the public ceremonies surrounding Napoleon Bonaparte’s imperial coronation in 1804, though sources differ over whether he was inside Notre-Dame or observed related festivities. He admired Napoleon’s military achievement while later presenting the emperor’s assumption of a crown as a betrayal of republican glory.

### Italy and the Monte Sacro oath

Bolívar and Rodríguez traveled in Italy in 1805, visiting Milan, Venice, Florence, Rome, and other locations.

**The story:** On Rome’s Monte Sacro, Bolívar supposedly swore before Rodríguez that he would not rest until Spanish America was liberated.

**Evidence:** Rodríguez later reported the episode, and Bolívar’s subsequent career made it a powerful retrospective origin story. No contemporary written record of the oath survives.

**Label:** **Reported fact with hagiographic elaboration.**

**Reputational effect:** The scene became the sacred calling narrative of Bolivarian nationalism: an American revolutionary vow pronounced at a site associated with ancient Roman plebeian resistance.

Bolívar returned to Venezuela in 1807. He managed estates, participated in elite social circles, and became increasingly hostile to peninsular political control.

## Early Revolutionary Career, 1808–1812

Napoleon’s invasion of Spain in 1808, the abdications of Charles IV and Ferdinand VII, and the installation of Joseph Bonaparte produced a legitimacy crisis throughout the empire.

On 19 April 1810, Caracas elites displaced Captain General Vicente Emparan and established a governing junta claiming to preserve Ferdinand VII’s rights. Bolívar did not direct the coup but became an active member of the revolutionary movement.

### Mission to Britain

Bolívar, Luis López Méndez, and Andrés Bello traveled to London in 1810 to seek British support. Britain, allied with anti-Napoleonic Spain, would not recognize Venezuelan independence but received the delegation unofficially.

In London Bolívar met **Francisco de Miranda**, the veteran Venezuelan revolutionary. Bolívar helped persuade Miranda to return to Venezuela. Their alliance soon became strained by generational, strategic, and personal differences.

### First Republic

Venezuela declared independence on 5 July 1811. Bolívar served under Miranda and participated in military operations, including responsibility for Puerto Cabello.

In June–July 1812 royalist prisoners at Puerto Cabello revolted and the fortress was lost. The defeat damaged Bolívar’s standing. Combined with military reverses, internal division, the 26 March earthquake, economic dislocation, and royalist mobilization, it contributed to the collapse of the First Republic.

Miranda negotiated capitulation with royalist commander Domingo de Monteverde in July 1812.

### Arrest of Miranda

On the night of 30–31 July 1812, Bolívar, Miguel Peña, Manuel María de las Casas, and others detained Miranda at La Guaira and delivered him to authorities who turned him over to the royalists. Miranda died in Spanish imprisonment in Cádiz in 1816.

**Documented fact:** Bolívar participated in Miranda’s arrest.

**Disputed interpretation:** Bolívar and his associates claimed Miranda had betrayed the republic by capitulating and possibly intended to escape with public funds. Critics characterize the action as Bolívar’s betrayal of his mentor, perhaps undertaken to secure his own passage. Monteverde issued Bolívar a passport, praising his service in Miranda’s arrest, which makes the episode particularly damaging to heroic accounts.

There is no evidence that Bolívar intended Miranda’s eventual death in Spanish captivity. There is equally no basis for removing his direct responsibility for the arrest.

Bolívar escaped to Curaçao and then Cartagena in New Granada.

## Cartagena, the Admirable Campaign, and the Second Republic, 1812–1814

### Cartagena Manifesto

In December 1812 Bolívar issued the **Memoria dirigida a los ciudadanos de la Nueva Granada por un caraqueño**, commonly called the **Cartagena Manifesto**.

He blamed Venezuela’s collapse on:

- weak federal government;
- inadequate military organization;
- clerical and royalist opposition;
- financial disorder;
- political indulgence toward enemies;
- the earthquake’s exploitation as royalist propaganda.

The document established recurring features of his political thought: central authority, disciplined armed force, suspicion of premature federalism, and fear that social fragmentation would destroy republican government.

### Magdalena campaign and invasion of Venezuela

Serving the United Provinces of New Granada, Bolívar conducted operations along the Magdalena River and obtained authorization to invade western Venezuela.

The **Admirable Campaign** of 1813 advanced through Cúcuta, Mérida, Trujillo, and other Andean towns. At Trujillo on 15 June he issued the **Decreto de Guerra a Muerte**: Americans would receive pardon even if culpable, while Spaniards and Canary Islanders who did not actively support independence faced death.

The decree responded to royalist violence and attempted to convert a civil war into a national war between Americans and Spaniards. In practice, both sides committed killings, confiscations, and reprisals. Bolívar’s decree remains one of the clearest documentary cases of his authorization of political violence.

He entered Caracas on 6 August 1813. The municipality awarded him the title **Libertador** in October.

### Second Republic and social war

The republic controlled major cities but lacked stable support in much of the countryside. Royalist caudillo **José Tomás Boves** mobilized llaneros, pardos, enslaved people, and others hostile to Creole landowners or attracted by plunder, advancement, and royalist promises.

Patriot defeats at La Puerta and elsewhere culminated in the 1814 collapse. Bolívar ordered or approved harsh measures, including the execution of Spanish prisoners at La Guaira and Caracas amid fear of internal revolt. Exact chains of command and totals vary by source, but mass killing of prisoners is documented.

The **Emigration to the East** evacuated thousands from Caracas toward Barcelona and Cumaná; many died from hunger, disease, exposure, or royalist attacks.

Bolívar and Santiago Mariño were displaced by rival patriot leaders and left Venezuela for Cartagena.

## New Granada, Jamaica, Haiti, and the Restoration of the War, 1814–1817

### New Granada and Cartagena

Bolívar assisted the federal government against Cundinamarca and entered Bogotá in December 1814. Ordered to operate against royalist Santa Marta, he became embroiled in conflict with Cartagena’s authorities. Rather than intensify a patriot civil war, he resigned and sailed for Jamaica in May 1815.

### Jamaica exile

In Kingston he lived with limited funds and wrote extensively to explain the American struggle and seek foreign aid.

The **Carta de Jamaica**, dated 6 September 1815 and addressed to Henry Cullen, surveyed the collapse of Spanish power and forecast the political future of the Americas. It advocated independence and continental cooperation while expressing doubts that Spanish America possessed the habits required for stable liberal democracy.

A servant named **Pío**, reportedly bribed or politically motivated, attempted to kill Bolívar in December 1815 but murdered Bolívar’s host or sleeping companion, Félix Amestoy, who occupied the hammock Bolívar normally used.

**Documented core:** an assassination attempt occurred and the wrong man was killed.

**Unresolved question:** the ultimate sponsor, if any, has never been conclusively established.

### Haiti and Alexandre Pétion

Bolívar traveled to Haiti, where President **Alexandre Pétion** supplied arms, money, printing resources, and volunteers. Pétion requested or conditioned assistance on emancipation of enslaved people in liberated territories.

Bolívar’s first 1816 expedition landed at Margarita and Carúpano. He proclaimed emancipation at Carúpano on 2 June and again at Ocumare on 6 July. The expedition faltered, and Bolívar returned to Haiti before mounting another attempt late in 1816.

His emancipation policy was both ideological and strategic:

- he increasingly condemned slavery as incompatible with republican liberty;
- Haiti’s assistance was indispensable;
- the patriot cause needed soldiers and support among nonwhite populations;
- royalists had effectively mobilized enslaved and free people of color.

Implementation was incomplete. Freedom was initially tied partly to military service; slaveowners resisted; revolutionary congresses later substituted gradual emancipation; slavery survived in Venezuela, New Granada, Ecuador, and Peru long after Bolívar’s death.

### Piar and consolidation of command

By 1817 patriot forces held positions in Guayana. **Manuel Piar**, a successful general of mixed racial background, won the Battle of San Félix and challenged Bolívar’s leadership. Piar was arrested, court-martialed, convicted of desertion, insubordination, conspiracy, and related charges, and executed at Angostura on 16 October 1817 after Bolívar confirmed the sentence.

**Documented fact:** Bolívar allowed the execution of a major rival following a military trial.

**Bolívar’s position:** Piar endangered unity and was fomenting caste war.

**Critical interpretation:** the proceeding eliminated a political and military rival and demonstrated the racial limits of Creole revolutionary leadership.

Evidence does not reduce the event to race alone: Piar’s insubordination and political maneuvering were real. Nor does that eliminate the racial and class context of his challenge.

Bolívar subsequently secured supreme command. Guayana provided river access, cattle, manpower, trade, and a durable territorial base.

## Angostura and the Liberation of New Granada, 1818–1819

In 1818 Bolívar suffered reverses during operations in central Venezuela, including surprise at Rincón de los Toros. He increasingly combined warfare with institution-building and foreign recruitment.

British, Irish, German, and other European veterans entered patriot service in the **British Legions**. They were motivated by ideology, pay, adventure, unemployment after the Napoleonic Wars, or combinations of these factors. Their discipline was important in later campaigns, though disease and hardship caused heavy losses.

### Congress of Angostura

Bolívar opened the Congress of Angostura on 15 February 1819 with his most important constitutional speech.

He proposed:

- a centralized republic;
- separation of powers;
- a strong executive;
- a hereditary senate, modeled partly on Britain’s House of Lords but without formal nobility;
- a “moral power” responsible for civic education;
- abolition of slavery;
- social equality before law;
- institutional adaptation to Spanish American conditions.

Congress did not accept all these proposals.

### Crossing the Andes

In 1819 Bolívar joined forces with **Francisco de Paula Santander** in the plains and undertook a hazardous wet-season crossing from the Llanos over the Andes through the Páramo de Pisba.

Major actions included:

- Paya, 27 June;
- Gámeza and Tópaga, July;
- Pantano de Vargas, 25 July;
- Boyacá, 7 August.

The victory at Boyacá opened Bogotá and gave the patriot movement New Granada’s treasury, administrative center, population, and strategic depth.

### Creation of Colombia

On 17 December 1819 the Angostura congress proclaimed the Republic of Colombia, joining Venezuela and New Granada and anticipating the incorporation of Quito. Historians later called it **Gran Colombia** to distinguish it from the present Republic of Colombia.

Bolívar became president; Santander became vice president responsible for administration in New Granada.

## Gran Colombia and the Expansion of Independence, 1820–1822

### Armistice and regularization of war

In November 1820 Bolívar and Spanish commander **Pablo Morillo** concluded an armistice and a Treaty for the Regularization of War at Santa Ana de Trujillo. Their personal meeting symbolized the transition from the exterminatory logic of the War to the Death toward recognized rules for prisoners, wounded soldiers, and civilians.

### Carabobo

On 24 June 1821 Bolívar defeated the principal royalist army at **Carabobo**, securing control of most of Venezuela. José Antonio Páez and the llanero cavalry were decisive. Puerto Cabello remained royalist until 1823.

The Congress of Cúcuta adopted a constitution for Colombia in 1821. It established a central republic but rejected several of Bolívar’s more authoritarian or corporatist proposals.

Congress adopted gradual rather than immediate abolition: children born to enslaved mothers after the law’s effective date would eventually be free, subject to service requirements. Bolívar continued to press emancipation but compromised with property interests and political opposition.

Bolívar’s own record was mixed. He had inherited enslaved people and relied on enslaved labor economically. He offered freedom to some who served militarily and later manumitted additional people from his estates. Exact totals vary. His actions did not dissolve the economic system from which his family wealth derived.

### Southern campaign

Royalist resistance continued in Quito and Pasto. Bolívar won at **Bomboná** on 7 April 1822, although the battle was costly and tactically indecisive. Antonio José de Sucre won the decisive Battle of **Pichincha** on 24 May, enabling Quito’s incorporation into Colombia.

Pasto remained fiercely resistant. Patriot forces conducted punitive operations there, especially during the December 1822 **Navidad Negra** under Sucre’s immediate command. Killings, looting, and destruction followed resistance in the city.

**Documented fact:** severe reprisals occurred.

**Command responsibility:** Sucre commanded the operation; Bolívar was supreme political and military authority and supported suppression of Pasto. Exact numbers killed and the extent of Bolívar’s detailed advance direction remain disputed.

## Manuela Sáenz and the Guayaquil Conference, 1822

### Manuela Sáenz

Bolívar met **Manuela Sáenz de Vergara y Aizpuru** in Quito in June 1822. Sáenz, married to the English merchant James Thorne, had already participated in independence politics and had received recognition from José de San Martín’s Peruvian government.

She became Bolívar’s lover, confidante, political ally, intelligence gatherer, archivist, and defender. Their relationship continued intermittently until his death, constrained by campaigns and politics.

Surviving correspondence documents intimacy and political exchange. However, the published corpus contains uncertain, reconstructed, missing, or possibly embellished texts. Romantic collections should therefore be checked against archival provenance.

Later culture often reduced Sáenz to “the Liberator’s mistress” or exalted her solely as **Libertadora del Libertador**. Modern scholarship emphasizes her independent political agency.

### Guayaquil

Bolívar had entered Guayaquil in July 1822 and supported its annexation to Colombia. **José de San Martín**, Protector of Peru, arrived and met privately with him on 26–27 July.

Topics evidently included:

- the future of Guayaquil;
- continued war against Spanish forces in Peru;
- military reinforcement;
- possible political arrangements, including monarchy;
- leadership of the final campaign.

No mutually authenticated transcript exists. Later accounts by participants and partisans conflict.

Soon afterward San Martín resigned his Peruvian authority and withdrew. Bolívar’s supporters presented this as voluntary recognition of Bolívar’s superior capacity. San Martín’s defenders emphasized Bolívar’s refusal to share command or provide sufficient assistance. The private conversations cannot be reconstructed with certainty.

## Peru, Junín, Ayacucho, and Bolivia, 1823–1826

Peru’s congress invited Bolívar in 1823 amid military collapse and factional struggle. He arrived at Callao on 1 September.

In February 1824 Congress granted him dictatorial powers. The term **dictator** was an official emergency office, but the powers were genuinely extensive: military command, executive authority, restrictions on opponents, control of appointments, and suspension or displacement of ordinary constitutional processes.

Bolívar reorganized armies, finance, recruitment, supplies, and regional administration from bases including Pativilca, Trujillo, and the central highlands. He endured a serious illness at Pativilca, reported as fever and extreme wasting.

### Junín and Ayacucho

Bolívar led the campaign culminating in the cavalry Battle of **Junín** on 6 August 1824. The action involved no firearms and was turned by a charge associated with José Andrés Rázuri and the Peruvian cavalry.

Bolívar then left field command to Sucre. At **Ayacucho**, 9 December 1824, Sucre defeated Viceroy José de la Serna. The capitulation effectively ended Spain’s principal continental military power in South America, although Callao held out until January 1826.

Bolívar received honors, money, properties, and ceremonial gifts from grateful governments. He sometimes refused or redirected rewards, but he also maintained an expensive military-political household and continued asserting claims to family property. The common claim that he died literally penniless is an exaggeration: his finances were disordered and much wealth had been consumed or rendered inaccessible by war, confiscation, litigation, and debt, but he retained valuable claims, notably the Aroa mines.

### Indigenous policy in Peru

In 1824–1825 Bolívar issued decrees intended to:

- abolish compulsory personal services;
- end formal caste terminology;
- distribute certain community lands as individual property;
- eliminate hereditary indigenous offices and privileges;
- establish formal equality.

Consequences were contradictory. The attack on corporate protections could expose communal lands to alienation. Fiscal crisis led to restoration or continuation of an indigenous head tax under the name **contribución**, despite the language of equality. Indigenous communities therefore encountered both abolition of colonial disabilities and renewed extractive demands.

### Slavery in Peru

Bolívar requested legal clarification for enslaved soldiers and supported emancipation in principle. He did not impose immediate universal abolition. Owners continued to reclaim individuals, and slavery survived in Peru until 1854.

### Political repression in Peru

Bolívar’s Peruvian government restricted opposition, exiled or marginalized critics, influenced the press and municipalities, and used special powers against suspected conspirators. Patriot figures opposed to Colombian predominance sometimes faced prosecution or exile.

The allegation that every execution or exile was ordered personally by Bolívar is not sustainable without case-specific documentation. The broader fact of authoritarian emergency government is not disputed.

### Creation of Bolivia

Upper Peru convened an assembly and declared independence on 6 August 1825. The new state was initially named the Republic of Bolívar and then Bolivia. Bolívar objected at first to separation from both Peru and Río de la Plata but accepted the assembly’s decision.

He drafted the **Bolivian Constitution of 1826**, proposing:

- a president for life;
- presidential power to nominate a successor;
- a three-chamber legislature;
- formal civil equality;
- abolition of slavery in principle;
- strong centralized administration.

Bolívar argued that the life presidency would provide continuity without hereditary monarchy. Opponents saw a disguised monarchy and a project for permanent personal rule.

He envisioned an Andean federation joining Colombia, Peru, and Bolivia under related constitutions. It failed because of local nationalism, constitutional opposition, military resentment, and suspicion of Colombian domination.

### Congress of Panama

Bolívar called an inter-American congress that met in Panama in 1826. Delegates discussed collective defense, arbitration, and confederation. Attendance was incomplete; governments imposed divergent instructions; ratification failed. The congress later became a foundational symbol of Pan-American and Latin American integration despite meager immediate results.

## Return to Colombia and Political Breakdown, 1826–1828

### La Cosiata

In Venezuela, José Antonio Páez resisted orders from Bogotá in 1826. The movement known as **La Cosiata** combined regional autonomy, elite resistance to central administration, and Páez’s personal power.

Bolívar returned from Peru. Rather than prosecute Páez, he offered an amnesty and confirmed him as civil and military chief in Venezuela. This restored temporary peace but weakened constitutional authority and deepened Bolívar’s rupture with Santander.

### Bolívar and Santander

Santander had been Bolívar’s principal collaborator in building Colombia. Their division concerned:

- strict constitutionalism versus emergency executive power;
- fiscal administration;
- foreign loans;
- military requisitions;
- the Peruvian expedition;
- federalism and regional autonomy;
- Bolívar’s life-presidency project;
- personal and factional rivalry.

Bolivarian accounts often portray Santander as a legalistic intriguer or hidden traitor. Santanderian accounts portray Bolívar as an aspiring monarch. Their surviving correspondence shows a long working partnership that deteriorated gradually rather than an original, simple opposition.

### Convention of Ocaña

A constitutional convention assembled at Ocaña in 1828. Bolívar’s supporters sought stronger central government; Santander’s allies resisted. When the Bolivarian delegation withdrew, the convention lost its quorum.

On 27 August 1828 Bolívar assumed supreme powers under an **Organic Decree**, effectively establishing a dictatorship. He abolished the vice presidency, reorganized government, curtailed constitutional guarantees, and promised a future representative settlement.

**Documented fact:** Bolívar replaced the Cúcuta constitutional order with personal emergency rule.

**Disputed interpretation:** supporters described it as necessary to prevent state dissolution; opponents described it as usurpation.

---

## The September Conspiracy and Repression, 1828

On the night of 25 September 1828 conspirators attacked the presidential palace at Bogotá. Guards were killed. Manuela Sáenz delayed the attackers and persuaded Bolívar to escape through a window. He hid nearby until loyal troops recovered control.

Her role is well supported, though later retellings embroidered dialogue and physical details.

A special proceeding followed. Executions included conspirators and Admiral **José Prudencio Padilla**, who was already imprisoned and whose direct participation in the attack was not established. The initial tribunal had acquitted or treated some defendants more leniently; extraordinary review produced harsher results.

Santander was convicted of complicity and sentenced to death. Evidence showed foreknowledge or political association was suspected, but direct participation remains disputed. Bolívar commuted the sentence to exile.

Bolívar later expressed regret over the deaths of Piar, Padilla, and others. That regret does not erase his responsibility for confirming or permitting their executions.

## War, Rebellion, and Dissolution, 1828–1830

Peru opposed Colombian influence and intervened in Bolivia; war followed between Peru and Colombia. Sucre defeated a Peruvian force at **Tarqui** on 27 February 1829, but the settlement did not restore Bolívar’s continental project.

In Colombia, General **José María Córdova**, a hero of Ayacucho, rebelled against the dictatorship in 1829. He was defeated at El Santuario and killed by a foreign officer, Rupert Hand, serving government forces.

**Documented fact:** Bolívar’s government ordered suppression of Córdova’s revolt.

**Disputed question:** whether Bolívar or his ministers intended Córdova to be killed rather than captured is not conclusively established.

Meanwhile:

- Venezuela moved toward separation under Páez;
- Ecuadorian leaders considered their own political future;
- New Granadan opposition grew;
- Bolívar’s health deteriorated;
- Sucre, his preferred political and military successor, withdrew from high office.

A new Colombian constituent congress met in 1830. Bolívar resigned the presidency on 27 April. Joaquín Mosquera succeeded him amid continuing instability.

### Assassination of Sucre

Antonio José de Sucre was murdered at Berruecos on 4 June 1830.

Responsibility has been attributed to networks associated with José María Obando and regional political interests. Legal and historiographical disputes persisted for generations.

There is no credible evidence that Bolívar caused Sucre’s death. He was devastated by it and regarded Sucre as indispensable to the union.

---

## Final Journey, Illness, and Death, 1830

After resigning, Bolívar left Bogotá intending to travel to the Caribbean and possibly Europe. His route included Honda, the Magdalena River, Mompox, Turbaco, Cartagena, Barranquilla, Soledad, and Santa Marta.

He was politically isolated, short of liquid funds, and increasingly ill. He wrote bitterly that America was ungovernable and that those who had served the revolution had “plowed the sea.” The exact wording and context of several famous last judgments should be checked against individual letters rather than treated as a single final testament.

Spanish merchant **Joaquín de Mier** provided the Quinta de San Pedro Alejandrino near Santa Marta. French physician **Alexandre‑Prosper Révérend** attended Bolívar and kept a medical bulletin.

Symptoms included:

- chronic cough;
- fever;
- weakness;
- emaciation;
- expectoration;
- respiratory distress;
- periods of apparent improvement and relapse.

Bolívar dictated his will on 10 December. He also issued a final proclamation urging Colombians to preserve union and avoid factional destruction.

He received the sacraments and died on 17 December 1830.

### Autopsy

Révérend performed an autopsy. His description reported extensive pulmonary damage consistent with chronic lung disease and interpreted it as tuberculosis.

A modern retrospective study argued that some findings were atypical for tuberculosis and considered fungal disease or arsenic exposure. Venezuelan infectious-disease specialists responded that the historical evidence was too limited and biased for certainty and that tuberculosis remained plausible.

### Murder and poisoning claims

**The theory:** Bolívar was poisoned, often with arsenic, by political enemies.

**Evidence offered:** nonspecific symptoms, possible arsenic-containing contemporary medicines, political motive, and retrospective medical interpretation.

**Evidence against:** chronic respiratory decline, an autopsy compatible with severe pulmonary disease, absence of a documented poisoner or chain of administration, and the inability of historical symptom descriptions to establish toxicological causation.

**Label:** **Unresolved medical theory; no documentary proof of assassination.**

---

## Remains, Burial, and Exhumations

Bolívar was initially buried in Santa Marta Cathedral. In 1842 his remains were transferred to Caracas and placed in the cathedral. In 1876 they were moved to the National Pantheon under President Antonio Guzmán Blanco, a decisive architect of the state-sponsored Bolívar cult.

On 16 July 2010 the Venezuelan government exhumed the remains to investigate identity and cause of death. President Hugo Chávez publicly entertained assassination by poisoning.

The investigation generated facial reconstruction and examinations but did not establish that Bolívar had been murdered. Publicly reported genetic work could not produce a fully conclusive identity comparison because of problems obtaining or matching family reference material.

The remains were later placed in a purpose-built mausoleum adjoining the National Pantheon.

## Residences, Travels, Exiles, and Major Geographical Movements

Bolívar’s movements were unusually extensive:

- Caracas and the Aragua valleys during childhood;
- La Guaira–Veracruz–Mexico City–Havana–Spain, 1799;
- Madrid and Bilbao, 1799–1802;
- Venezuela, 1802–1803;
- Paris and other parts of France, 1803–1805;
- Italy, including Milan and Rome, 1805;
- the United States, probably including Charleston, during his 1806–1807 return route;
- Venezuela and London, 1807–1810;
- Venezuela, Curaçao, and New Granada, 1812;
- New Granada and Venezuela during the Admirable Campaign, 1813;
- eastern Venezuela, New Granada, and Jamaica, 1814–1815;
- Haiti and Caribbean expedition routes, 1815–1816;
- Guayana, the Orinoco, the Llanos, and central Venezuela, 1817–1819;
- across the Andes into New Granada, 1819;
- Venezuela and New Granada, 1820–1821;
- Pasto, Quito, and Guayaquil, 1822;
- Peru and the central Andes, 1823–1826;
- Upper Peru/Bolivia, 1825;
- Colombia, Venezuela, and Ecuador, 1826–1830;
- the Magdalena corridor and Caribbean coast during his final journey.

Claims that he traveled roughly 80,000–90,000 kilometers and participated in hundreds of military actions derive from Bolivarian biographical tabulations. They convey the scale of his mobility but depend on definitions of “combat” and reconstructed routes.

## Wealth, Property, and Financial Relationships

Bolívar inherited a very large but legally complicated fortune. Assets included:

- Caracas houses;
- San Mateo and other agricultural estates;
- cacao and livestock interests;
- the Aroa copper mines;
- enslaved workers;
- debts owed to and by the family;
- litigated inheritances.

War damaged estates, disrupted production, led to confiscations, and separated Bolívar from direct management. He financed political activity from personal resources at several moments, though claims that he single-handedly spent his entire fortune on independence are patriotic simplifications.

His sister María Antonia and agents managed or litigated property during his absence. Surviving family correspondence discusses:

- boundaries and ownership of Aroa;
- tenants and administrators;
- debts;
- lawsuits;
- confiscation and restoration;
- the condition of rural and urban property.

The Aroa mines became entangled with British commercial interests and later litigation. Bolívar’s will attempted to dispose of remaining property and recognized obligations to relatives, servants, and associates.

He received public honors and grants but did not construct a stable private business empire through office. His household and campaigns nevertheless depended on public treasuries, forced loans, requisitions, confiscated property, customs revenue, foreign credit, and elite patronage.

Gran Colombia’s London loans and fiscal crisis were administered chiefly by ministers and agents rather than as Bolívar’s private transactions. Allegations that he personally stole the loans are not supported by established evidence. Corruption, speculation, poor accounting, and patronage existed within revolutionary financial networks, and Bolívar repeatedly complained about them without creating an effective transparent system.

## Political, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### Republicanism

Bolívar consistently rejected restoration of Spanish monarchy. He did not consistently support majoritarian or parliamentary democracy.

His mature model combined:

- republican sovereignty;
- strong centralized executive power;
- civil equality in law;
- abolition of formal caste distinctions;
- public education;
- military guardianship during emergency;
- institutional checks designed to restrain faction;
- continental cooperation.

### Enlightenment and classical influences

Rousseau, Montesquieu, Voltaire, classical republican history, British constitutionalism, the American Revolution, the French Revolution, Napoleon, and direct experience of civil war all influenced him.

He quoted or invoked these traditions selectively. His constitutional projects were not copies of one theorist.

### Democracy and authoritarianism

Bolívar argued that Spanish America lacked institutions, education, and civic habits required for unrestricted democracy. He feared:

- regional fragmentation;
- race and class war;
- military anarchy;
- demagoguery;
- foreign intervention;
- legislative paralysis.

These fears helped produce centralized constitutions, emergency powers, the proposed hereditary senate, life presidency, and 1828 dictatorship.

The documentary record therefore supports both descriptions:

- a revolutionary republican who destroyed imperial government and hereditary caste law;
- an executive centralist who suspended constitutions and suppressed opponents.

### Race, slavery, and social hierarchy

Bolívar described Spanish Americans as a mixed people—not Europeans, Indigenous Americans, or Africans alone. He increasingly regarded slavery as dangerous and incompatible with independence.

Limitations included:

- delayed and conditional manumission;
- accommodation with slaveowners;
- fear of autonomous nonwhite mobilization;
- the execution of Piar;
- failure to secure immediate abolition;
- continued reliance on elite regional alliances.

### Continental politics

Bolívar feared that fragmented American republics would become weak, warlike, and vulnerable to Europe or the United States. His projects included Colombia, the Panama Congress, and an Andean federation.

A widely circulated quotation predicts that the United States “appears destined by Providence to plague America with misery in the name of liberty.” It derives from an authentic 1829 letter to British chargé Patrick Campbell. Modern usage often removes its immediate context: Bolívar was discussing U.S. objections to foreign monarchical arrangements and the geopolitical weakness of Spanish America.

### Religion

Bolívar attacked clerical political resistance when it supported royalism but did not pursue systematic dechristianization. His governments negotiated with ecclesiastical institutions, used religious ceremony, and treated Catholicism as a public institution. His proposed “moral power” was civic rather than conventionally ecclesiastical.

### Education

He repeatedly presented education as the foundation of republican government. He supported schools and universities and appointed intellectuals to public roles, but war, fiscal collapse, and administrative weakness limited implementation.

## Controversies, Political Violence, and Accusations

### Enslavement and inherited privilege

**Documented fact:** Bolívar inherited wealth created partly through enslaved labor.

**Documented fact:** he later promoted emancipation, manumitted enslaved people connected to his estates, and repeatedly urged legislatures to abolish slavery.

**Documented limitation:** abolition remained incomplete and gradual, and his governments protected property compromises.

### War to the Death

The 1813 decree openly authorized lethal discrimination against Spaniards and Canary Islanders who did not support independence. Patriot and royalist forces both committed atrocities. The decree cannot be reduced to later propaganda; it is an authentic public document.

### Miranda’s arrest

Bolívar participated directly. Whether he acted from sincere belief that Miranda had betrayed the republic, self-preservation, ambition, or mixed motives is unresolved.

### Killings of prisoners, 1813–1814

Patriot authorities executed Spanish prisoners during the existential crisis of the Second Republic. Bolívar bore command and political responsibility for policies of reprisal, though exact responsibility for individual prison massacres varies.

### Execution of Piar

A legally constituted military tribunal convicted Piar, but the trial occurred within a struggle for supreme command. Race, class, regional authority, and personal rivalry formed part of the context.

### Pasto repression

Patriot forces inflicted collective punishment and killings in a region that repeatedly resisted republican incorporation. Sucre exercised immediate command; Bolívar’s government authorized suppression.

### Peru and dictatorship

Bolívar accepted dictatorial power granted by Congress, governed through emergency decrees, restricted opposition, and promoted a life-presidency constitution. Whether these were temporary measures required to finish the war or a durable authoritarian design is a historical interpretation; the powers themselves are documented.

### Padilla, Santander, and post-conspiracy justice

Proceedings after September 1828 lacked normal constitutional safeguards. Padilla’s execution remains especially controversial because direct participation in the palace attack was not proven. Santander’s sentence was commuted, but his guilt remains disputed.

### Córdova’s death

Córdova died while rebelling against Bolívar’s government. Claims of a planned assassination remain unproven.

### Alleged monarchy

Bolívar denied seeking a crown. He rejected proposals associated with European princes but proposed a president for life with power to nominate a successor.

**Documented fact:** he did not become a king or formally establish hereditary monarchy.

**Disputed interpretation:** the Bolivian constitutional structure resembled elective or quasi-hereditary monarchy closely enough that opponents’ fears were not invented.

### Personal financial corruption

No strong documentary case establishes that Bolívar used office to create a secret personal fortune. His finances, governmental spending, gifts, property litigation, and revolutionary confiscations were opaque by modern standards. Claims of personal enrichment require case-specific evidence and should not be inferred from governmental corruption generally.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Liberator of five/six nations”

Bolívar is traditionally credited with liberating Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, and sometimes Panama.

This formula compresses collective wars involving local armies, civilians, Indigenous and Afro-descended combatants, foreign volunteers, regional commanders, San Martín’s southern campaign, Haitian support, and Sucre’s victories. Panama declared separation from Spain through its own 1821 movement before joining Colombia.

**Label:** **Commemorative simplification grounded in real leadership.**

### The Monte Sacro oath

A retrospective account with no contemporary transcript became the sacred beginning of Bolívar’s mission.

**Label:** **Reported fact transformed into national legend.**

### The flawless abolitionist

State hagiography sometimes portrays Bolívar as having personally abolished slavery everywhere he governed. He issued emancipation decrees and pressed abolition but did not overcome legislative and property resistance. Slavery persisted.

**Label:** **Mythologized version of a documented but incomplete policy.**

### The aristocratic reactionary

Hostile accounts, notably Karl Marx’s 1858 biographical article, depicted Bolívar as cowardly, vain, dictatorial, and dependent on subordinates.

Marx relied heavily on hostile memoir literature, especially H. L. V. Ducoudray Holstein. Modern historians regard the portrait as polemical and unreliable in major respects. It remains historically important in Marxist debates because later Latin American Marxists either defended Bolívar or reinterpreted him as an anti-imperialist precursor.

**Label:** **Hostile political mythology built on selected factual episodes.**

### The democratic saint

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century official culture often presented Bolívar as the uncomplicated founder of modern liberal democracy. This minimizes his distrust of federalism and mass democracy, hereditary senate proposal, life presidency, and dictatorship.

**Label:** **Civic hagiography.**

### The would-be emperor

Opponents called him “King Simón” or alleged that he sought an imperial crown. His constitutional projects supplied plausible grounds for suspicion, but no conclusive document establishes an accepted plan to crown himself.

**Label:** **Contemporary allegation and disputed interpretation.**

### Manuela as merely the woman who saved him

The palace rescue is documented. The reduction of Sáenz to a romantic accessory obscures her earlier patriot activity, political networks, correspondence, archival work, and direct participation.

**Label:** **Gendered hagiographic simplification.**

### “Plowing the sea”

The phrase summarizes Bolívar’s late despair and appears in accounts of his final political judgments. Multiple pessimistic statements are authentic, but popular versions often combine separate letters and retrospective recollections.

**Label:** **Authentic sentiment, frequently decontextualized.**

### Poisoning

The assassination theory gained renewed state sponsorship during Hugo Chávez’s government and motivated the 2010 exhumation. No toxicological result publicly established murder.

**Label:** **Unproven theory with major political afterlife.**

### Facial appearance and racial representation

Official portraits progressively standardized Bolívar as a pale, neoclassical republican hero. The 2012 Venezuelan forensic reconstruction showed a darker-complexioned face and was promoted as recovering an authentic, more mestizo Bolívar.

Because reconstruction depends on assumptions about remains, tissue depth, pigmentation, and artistic presentation, it is not a photographic recovery.

**Label:** **Forensic-artistic interpretation used in political symbolism.**

### Freemasonry

Bolívar is widely claimed as a Freemason, with reports of initiation in Cádiz or Paris and association with lodges. Some documentary evidence supports participation in Masonic environments and possibly initiation; later lodge traditions greatly magnified the role of Masonry in his career.

He later restricted or prohibited secret societies in Colombia after conspiracy fears.

**Label:** **Probable association; extensive later mythologization.**

## Works by Simón Bolívar

Bolívar did not write a conventional book-length autobiography. His corpus consists primarily of correspondence, proclamations, constitutional drafts, decrees, speeches, military communications, manifestos, and dictated texts. Secretaries often took dictation or prepared fair copies; official texts could be collaboratively drafted.

### Major authentic works and public documents

- **Memoria dirigida a los ciudadanos de la Nueva Granada por un caraqueño** (“Cartagena Manifesto”), 15 December 1812.
- **Decreto de Guerra a Muerte**, Trujillo, 15 June 1813.
- **Manifiesto de Carúpano**, 7 September 1814.
- **Contestación de un americano meridional a un caballero de esta isla**, commonly **Carta de Jamaica**, Kingston, 6 September 1815.
- Letter to the editor of the *Royal Gazette* of Jamaica on Spanish American society, 1815.
- **Proclamación de libertad de los esclavos**, Carúpano, 2 June 1816.
- Emancipation decree at Ocumare, 6 July 1816.
- **Discurso pronunciado ante el Congreso de Angostura**, 15 February 1819.
- Speech and documents establishing Colombia, 1819.
- Armistice and Treaty for the Regularization of War, negotiated with Pablo Morillo, 1820; formally joint diplomatic-military documents rather than sole-authored works.
- Messages to the Congress of Cúcuta and Colombian legislatures, 1821 onward.
- **Mi delirio sobre el Chimborazo**, conventionally dated 1822 and attributed to Bolívar. The text’s transmission and publication history warrant caution; it is treated as authentic in much of the Bolívar canon but has attracted attribution questions.
- Messages and proclamations to Peru, 1823–1826.
- **Convocatoria del Congreso de Panamá** and related diplomatic correspondence, 1824–1826.
- **Proyecto de Constitución para la República de Bolivia**, 1826.
- **Discurso del Libertador al Congreso Constituyente de Bolivia**, 25 May 1826.
- **Organic Decree** establishing the Colombian dictatorship, 27 August 1828; official governmental text.
- Letter to Patrick Campbell, 5 August 1829, containing the often-quoted judgment on the United States.
- Letter to Andrew Jackson, 28 November 1829.
- **Una mirada sobre la América española**, 1829, depending on editorial title and corpus classification.
- **Última proclama**, 10 December 1830.
- **Testamento**, 10 December 1830.

### Correspondence

Bolívar produced thousands of letters, orders, notes, and dispatches. Counts vary because editors distinguish originals, copies, drafts, dictated letters, circulars, and official documents differently.

Important collected editions include:

- *Colección de documentos relativos a la vida pública del Libertador de Colombia y del Perú, Simón Bolívar*, compiled by Francisco Javier Yanes and Cristóbal L. Mendoza, 1826 onward.
- Daniel Florencio O’Leary, *Memorias del general O’Leary*, posthumous documentary collection, 1879–1888 and later editions.
- Vicente Lecuna, editor, *Cartas del Libertador*, multiple volumes and editions.
- Vicente Lecuna, editor, *Obras completas de Simón Bolívar*, twentieth-century editions.
- *Escritos del Libertador*, large documentary edition produced in Venezuela.
- *Doctrina del Libertador*, edited by Manuel Pérez Vila, Biblioteca Ayacucho, 1976 and later editions.
- *Cartas inéditas*, Bogotá, 1899.
- Numerous thematic collections of letters to Santander, Sucre, Páez, María Antonia Bolívar, Manuela Sáenz, and foreign correspondents.

### Attribution cautions

- Many texts survive in secretarial copies rather than autograph manuscripts.
- Official proclamations may have been drafted collaboratively.
- Nineteenth-century editors silently regularized spelling or combined documents.
- Romantic correspondence with Manuela Sáenz includes missing originals and disputed transcriptions.
- Aphorisms circulating online are frequently paraphrased, modernized, misdated, or fabricated.
- *Mi delirio sobre el Chimborazo* requires attention to its textual transmission rather than automatic acceptance of every printed version.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate aftermath

Bolívar died amid the dissolution of Colombia and political repudiation by many former allies. Venezuela, Ecuador, and New Granada became separate states. His centralized continental project had failed.

His reputation recovered rapidly as successor governments sought usable founders. The 1842 transfer of his remains to Caracas transformed him into a principal state ancestor.

### Guzmán Blanco and the official cult

President Antonio Guzmán Blanco institutionalized commemoration through:

- transfer to the National Pantheon;
- statues and civic ritual;
- centennial ceremonies in 1883;
- publication and collection of documents;
- association of national unity with Bolívar’s authority.

Germán Carrera Damas described this process as the **cult of Bolívar**: the conversion of a historical actor into a legitimizing principle available to competing governments.

### Across Latin America

Bolívar became a symbol of:

- independence;
- Latin American unity;
- republican statehood;
- military leadership;
- anti-imperialism;
- centralized authority.

Liberals, conservatives, military governments, socialists, democrats, and revolutionaries all selected different parts of his record.

### Marxist interpretations

Karl Marx’s hostile 1858 article influenced some early Marxist attitudes. Later Latin American Marxists rejected Marx’s judgment or reinterpreted Bolívar through anti-colonial and class analysis.

Cuban and other revolutionary movements treated him as a precursor to Martí, continental solidarity, or anti-imperialism. This genealogy is political interpretation, not evidence that Bolívar held twentieth-century socialist views.

### Chávez and Bolivarianism

Hugo Chávez made Bolívar central to Venezuela’s state ideology:

- Venezuela became the **Bolivarian Republic of Venezuela** under the 1999 constitution;
- Chávez invoked Bolívar’s unity, social equality, military citizenship, and opposition to foreign domination;
- the government sponsored the 2010 exhumation;
- official iconography adopted the reconstructed face;
- Bolívar’s remains received a new mausoleum.

Chavismo’s socialism, petroleum-state policies, participatory institutions, and twenty-first-century geopolitical alignments cannot simply be attributed to Bolívar. “Bolivarianism” in this setting is a modern ideological construction using selected historical texts.

### Names and symbols

Bolívar’s name survives in:

- Bolivia;
- Venezuela’s currency, the bolívar;
- states, provinces, departments, cities, squares, streets, universities, military units, and monuments;
- the former Bolivarian Games and many regional institutions;
- political movements across Latin America;
- Bolívar, Tennessee, and other locations outside Latin America;
- innumerable portraits, equestrian statues, stamps, banknotes, and school rituals.

### Historiographical reinterpretation

Modern scholarship has shifted attention from an individual “great man” narrative toward:

- enslaved and free Afro-descended soldiers;
- Indigenous communities;
- women and political households;
- Haiti’s decisive assistance;
- regional caudillos;
- royalist popular mobilization;
- foreign volunteers;
- fiscal and administrative structures;
- constitutional conflict;
- collective violence.

This does not eliminate Bolívar’s central strategic and political role. It situates it within a continental revolution made by many actors with incompatible goals.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted record are included. Mere contemporaneity has not been treated as a connection.

### Simón Rodríguez — **Documented fact**

Bolívar’s childhood teacher in Caracas and later companion in Europe. Rodríguez’s educational philosophy and engagement with Rousseau influenced Bolívar. They traveled together in France and Italy in 1804–1805; Rodríguez later reported the Monte Sacro oath.

### Francisco de Miranda — **Documented fact**

Bolívar met Miranda in London in 1810 and helped persuade him to return to Venezuela. Bolívar served under him during the First Republic. In July 1812 Bolívar and others arrested Miranda at La Guaira after Miranda capitulated to Monteverde.

### Andrés Bello — **Documented fact**

Though the roster gives Bello only implicitly through other names and does not list him separately, no atlas entry is recorded because he is absent from the supplied roster.

### Alexander von Humboldt — **Documented fact**

Bolívar encountered Humboldt in Parisian intellectual circles in the 1804–1805 period. They discussed Spanish America. Later tradition attributes prophetic dialogue to Humboldt about Bolívar’s possible role; the fact of acquaintance is sound, while exact dialogue is not.

### Napoleon Bonaparte — **Documented observation; no personal meeting established**

Bolívar witnessed ceremonies surrounding Napoleon’s imperial coronation in Paris in 1804 and probably the coronation as king of Italy in Milan in 1805. No reliable evidence establishes a personal meeting. Napoleon’s career became both an inspiration in military glory and a warning about republican leaders becoming monarchs.

### Jean-Jacques Rousseau — **Documented intellectual influence**

Bolívar read and discussed Rousseau, particularly through Simón Rodríguez and European intellectual life. Rousseau influenced his thinking on education, civic formation, and republican society. Bolívar did not simply adopt Rousseau’s political system.

### George Washington — **Documented symbolic influence**

Bolívar was repeatedly compared to Washington and treated him as the model of a liberator who relinquished power. The relationship was posthumous and intellectual; Washington died in 1799, when Bolívar was sixteen, and no meeting occurred.

### Andrew Jackson — **Documented correspondence**

Bolívar wrote to U.S. president Andrew Jackson on 28 November 1829. The manuscript, with an English translation, survives in the Andrew Jackson Papers at the Library of Congress. No personal meeting is documented.

### Antonio José de Sucre — **Documented fact**

Bolívar’s principal lieutenant, collaborator, and intended political successor. Sucre commanded at Pichincha in 1822 and Ayacucho in 1824 and governed Bolivia. His murder at Berruecos on 4 June 1830 was a severe personal and political blow to Bolívar.

### Bernardo O’Higgins Riquelme — **Documented political association**

O’Higgins and Bolívar belonged to the interconnected independence leadership of Spanish South America and corresponded within the diplomatic-military network of liberation. Their relationship was political and indirect compared with Bolívar’s relationship with San Martín; no major sustained personal collaboration is established in the consulted sources.

### Carlos María de Alvear — **Reported political-diplomatic intersection**

Alvear operated within Río de la Plata and later diplomatic networks that intersected with the politics of Colombia and Bolívar’s continental projects. No close personal bond or clearly documented meeting was established in the consulted material.

### José de San Martín — **Documented fact**

Bolívar and San Martín met privately at Guayaquil on 26–27 July 1822. They discussed Guayaquil, Peru, military support, and political organization. The absence of an agreed transcript produced competing national and partisan legends about which liberator yielded to the other.

### Manuela Sáenz de Vergara y Aizpuru — **Documented fact**

They met at Quito in June 1822 and maintained an intimate and political partnership until Bolívar’s death. Sáenz gathered intelligence, handled papers, participated in his political circle, and helped save him during the September 1828 palace attack.

### Juana Azurduy de Padilla — **Documented recognition**

Bolívar met or received the Upper Peruvian guerrilla commander Juana Azurduy during his 1825 progress through the newly independent territory. He recognized her service and is associated with praising her military achievements. Exact wording of famous tributes varies across later retellings.

### Policarpa Salavarrieta — **Documented political-historical association, no meeting**

Salavarrieta was executed by Spanish authorities in Bogotá in 1817 while Bolívar was operating in Venezuela and Guayana. Bolívar later operated within the revolutionary cause for which she became a martyr, but no meeting is documented.

### José Antonio de la Sierra — **No securely established connection**

The consulted sources did not establish a reliable direct connection with Bolívar.

### José Antonio de Sucre / roster duplication

The roster’s **Antonio José de Sucre** is the documented collaborator described above; “José Antonio de Sucre” is not treated as a separate person.

### José de San Martín, Bernardo O’Higgins, and the southern liberation network — **Documented shared political scene**

Bolívar’s northern armies and the southern forces associated with San Martín and O’Higgins converged strategically in Peru and Ecuador. This is a documented institutional and military intersection, though it should not be mistaken for continuous joint command.

### Manuela Sáenz and San Martín — **Documented network connection**

Before her partnership with Bolívar, Sáenz received the Peruvian Order of the Sun associated with San Martín’s government. Her political career therefore crossed both liberation networks.

### Karl Marx — **Documented posthumous hostile interpretation**

Marx wrote the entry “Bolivar y Ponte” for the *New American Cyclopaedia* in 1858. He portrayed Bolívar as cowardly, vain, and dictatorial, relying heavily on hostile sources. There was no lifetime connection; this is a documented posthumous historiographical relationship.

### José Julián Martí Pérez — **Documented posthumous intellectual appropriation**

Martí treated Bolívar as a major precursor of continental American independence and unity. No meeting was possible: Martí was born in 1853. The connection is one of documented influence and commemoration.

### Gabriel García Márquez — **Documented posthumous literary treatment**

García Márquez reconstructed Bolívar’s final journey in the historical novel *El general en su laberinto* (1989). The novel shaped international popular understanding of the aging Bolívar while openly remaining fiction.

### Hugo Rafael Chávez Frías — **Documented posthumous political appropriation**

Chávez made Bolívar the principal historical symbol of his political project, renamed the state the Bolivarian Republic of Venezuela, sponsored the 2010 exhumation, and promoted a new facial reconstruction. Chávez’s Bolivarianism is a modern interpretation, not a direct continuation established by personal transmission.

### Nicolás Maduro Moros — **Documented posthumous political appropriation**

Maduro’s government continued the state’s Bolivarian name, symbolism, mausoleum culture, and invocation of Bolívar. No lifetime connection existed.

### Fidel Alejandro Castro Ruz — **Documented posthumous ideological appropriation**

Castro repeatedly placed Bolívar within a genealogy of Latin American anti-imperialism linked to José Martí and modern revolution. This is a posthumous political interpretation, not evidence of Bolívar’s adherence to Marxism.

### Ernesto “Che” Guevara — **Reported posthumous symbolic influence**

Guevara’s continental revolutionary program has frequently been framed in a Bolivarian tradition. The consulted sources support a broad symbolic genealogy but not a specific, uniquely determinative textual dependence sufficient for a stronger claim.

### Abraham Lincoln — **No direct connection**

Lincoln was born in 1809 and was a young adult when Bolívar died. No documented meeting, correspondence, or direct influence was established.

### Thomas Jefferson — **No direct connection established here**

Although Bolívar traveled through the United States and engaged with U.S. republican examples, the consulted materials did not establish a meeting or correspondence with Jefferson. General intellectual comparison is insufficient for an atlas connection.

### Benjamin Franklin and Alexander Hamilton — **No direct connection established**

Their roles in Atlantic republican history formed part of Bolívar’s broader political environment, but the consulted sources did not establish explicit personal contact or a sufficiently specific acknowledged influence.
