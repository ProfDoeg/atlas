# Franz Anton Mesmer: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier concerns the physician Franz Anton Mesmer (1734–1815), originator of the doctrine he called *animal magnetism*. It distinguishes the following categories:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a primary document, contemporary official record, or strong archival scholarship.
- **Reported fact:** repeated in credible secondary scholarship but not independently verified here from the underlying record.
- **Claim by Mesmer:** found in Mesmer’s own writings; not necessarily independently corroborated.
- **Contemporary allegation:** an accusation made during Mesmer’s lifetime without a demonstrated judicial finding.
- **Later interpretation:** a retrospective medical, psychological, political, or historical explanation.
- **Rumor or myth:** a culturally significant story for which adequate documentary support is absent.
- **Unresolved:** surviving evidence permits more than one defensible reconstruction.

Names, titles, quotations, and dates retain historical forms where useful. “Hypnosis” is not used as a simple synonym for Mesmer’s own practice: that terminology and its associated techniques developed later.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

Mesmer was the third of nine children in a Catholic family of modest but locally connected status.

- **Father:** Anton Mesmer, born in 1701 and still living after 1747 in some genealogical reconstructions. He served as a forester or gamekeeper connected with the Prince-Bishop of Constance.
- **Mother:** Maria Ursula Michel, generally dated 1701–1770, daughter of a locksmith.

These details are reported consistently by German biographical sources, although a complete critical edition of the parish and household records was not available in the sources consulted. The father’s ecclesiastical employment may have facilitated Franz Anton’s Jesuit education.

### Siblings

Mesmer belonged to a family of nine children. Most short biographies do not name all of them. A brother named **Johann** is reported to have entered the priesthood. Another brother, **Joseph Mesmer**, has been described as a writing master associated with the young archduchess Marie Antoinette, but this connection depends on later biographical traditions and requires archival confirmation.

Mesmer must not automatically be identified with every Viennese “Dr. Mesmer” or “Joseph Conrad Mesmer” found in Mozart literature. Confusion among namesakes has materially affected the story of Mozart’s *Bastien und Bastienne*.

### Marriage

On **10 January 1768**, Mesmer married **Maria Anna von Bosch**, née **von Posch**, widow of imperial court councillor Ferdinand Konrad von Bosch. She was older than Mesmer and brought substantial property, including a house or estate in Vienna’s Landstraße suburb and a country property at Rothmühle near Schwechat.

The marriage changed Mesmer’s material circumstances. It placed him in a prosperous household where he could maintain a medical practice, entertain musicians and social guests, acquire expensive instruments, and pursue speculative therapies without depending solely on fees.

The couple had no securely documented children. When Mesmer left Vienna in 1778, he effectively separated from his wife and apparently never resumed married life with her. She died in 1790; breast cancer is given in some later chronologies, but that diagnosis has not been independently confirmed here.

No reliable evidence establishes a later spouse, acknowledged partner, or biological descendants.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1734–1750

Mesmer grew up in Iznang, a fishing and agricultural settlement on Lake Constance. Later romantic biographies made the lake, tides, forests, and changing landscape formative sources for his eventual cosmology of universal fluid and reciprocal influence. That interpretation is plausible but retrospective; it should not be treated as a childhood statement by Mesmer.

From approximately **1746 to 1750**, he attended the Jesuit college at Constance. The curriculum would have emphasized Latin, rhetoric, scholastic philosophy, religion, and classical texts. His education and subsequent Latin dissertation demonstrate a high degree of formal literacy not implied by his family’s limited wealth.

Claims that he was an unusually mystical or visionary child belong largely to later hagiographic biography. No contemporary childhood diary or testimony establishes such a characterization.

## Education and Formation, 1750–1766

### Dillingen and Ingolstadt

From about **1750**, Mesmer studied logic, metaphysics, philosophy, and theology at the Jesuit University of Dillingen. He then continued theological or canon-law studies at Ingolstadt, probably beginning in 1753 or 1754.

Some biographies assign him a doctorate in philosophy. The exact degree history before Vienna varies among sources, and the surviving summaries do not always distinguish completion of a curriculum from formal conferral of a doctorate. He did not enter the priesthood.

### Vienna: law and medicine

Mesmer moved to Vienna by **1759**. Accounts differ on whether he initially studied law and changed to medicine in 1759 or 1760. He entered the medical environment shaped by the First Vienna Medical School.

Teachers and institutional influences included:

- **Gerard van Swieten**, reformer of Austrian medicine and physician to Empress Maria Theresa;
- **Anton de Haen**, professor of clinical medicine;
- **Anton von Störck**, physician and later important institutional opponent in the Paradis affair;
- the mechanistic and Newtonian natural philosophy current in eighteenth-century medicine;
- older doctrines of cosmic influence, tides, electricity, magnetism, nervous fluid, and bodily equilibrium.

### Medical dissertation, 1766

Mesmer received his medical doctorate in **1766** with:

> *Antonii Mesmer Marisburgensis Acron. Suev. Dissertatio physico-medica de planetarum influxu*

Usually shortened to *De planetarum influxu*—“On the influence of the planets.”

The dissertation did not principally advocate astrological prediction. It proposed that celestial bodies exerted physical influences upon the earth and human organisms through a subtle fluid, analogously to tides. This “animal gravitation” became an intellectual precursor of animal magnetism.

#### Dependence on Richard Mead

**Documented scholarly criticism:** historians have shown that the dissertation relied very heavily on Richard Mead’s *De imperio solis ac lunae in corpora humana et morbis inde oriundis* (1704). Some call this plagiarism; others describe it as extensive unacknowledged borrowing consistent with looser eighteenth-century academic conventions. The overlap is real. What remains interpretive is whether it proves deliberate fraud in the modern sense.

The dissertation itself survives in digitized copies, including one held by the Saxon State and University Library Dresden.

## Vienna: Physician, Householder, and Musical Patron, 1766–1773

Mesmer established himself as a physician in Vienna. His marriage in 1768 gave him access to elite social circles and an estate suitable for salons, concerts, and theatrical entertainments. He played the cello, clavichord, and glass harmonica or glass armonica, an instrument closely associated with Benjamin Franklin.

He reportedly knew or entertained composers including Joseph Haydn, Christoph Willibald Gluck, Niccolò Piccinni, and Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart. The evidentiary quality differs for each relationship; “friendship” is sometimes inferred from later musical biography rather than correspondence.

### Mozart and *Bastien und Bastienne*

A durable tradition states that twelve-year-old **Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart** composed or performed *Bastien und Bastienne*, K. 50, for Mesmer’s private theater in 1768.

**Unresolved tradition:**

- Georg Nikolaus Nissen’s nineteenth-century Mozart biography placed an amateur performance at Mesmer’s garden house.
- No contemporary program, account, score annotation, or letter proves that the opera was performed there in 1768.
- Research published in the *Neue Mozart-Ausgabe* noted that Mesmer’s outdoor garden theater was apparently unfinished in 1768; an indoor performance remains possible.
- The Mozarteum’s online Köchel catalogue warns that the “Dr. Mesmer” connected to the work may instead have been the school director Joseph Conrad Mesmer, reportedly not a relative of the magnetizer.
- The first securely documented public performance was in Berlin in 1890.

Mozart nevertheless knew Mesmer’s notoriety. In *Così fan tutte* (1790), Despina revives the supposedly poisoned lovers with a magnetic stone, an unmistakable comic reference to contemporary mesmerism.

## The Development of Animal Magnetism, 1773–1776

### Franziska Österlin and mineral magnets

In **1773–1774**, Mesmer treated **Franziska Österlin**—also rendered Oesterlin—a young woman in his household suffering recurrent attacks described as convulsions, fainting, pain, vomiting, toothache, paralysis, and other shifting symptoms. Modern retrospective labels such as “hysteria,” “conversion disorder,” or psychosomatic illness are hypotheses, not diagnoses established from an examination.

Mesmer initially collaborated with the Jesuit court astronomer **Maximilian Hell**, who fashioned magnetized iron pieces in anatomical shapes. On 28 July 1774, according to Mesmer’s own later account, he applied magnets to Österlin’s stomach and legs, producing sensations, changes in symptoms, and what he interpreted as a therapeutic “crisis.”

### Dispute with Maximilian Hell

Hell believed ordinary mineral magnetism explained the reported effects. Mesmer increasingly concluded that the magnets were only conduits: the operative force resided in the physician or in a universal biological fluid. Public letters produced a priority dispute.

- **Claim by Mesmer:** he had discovered a distinct animal magnetism and Hell wrongly took credit by emphasizing the manufactured magnets.
- **Hell’s position:** his magnetic devices had supplied the effective agency.
- **Historical finding:** neither man demonstrated a curative magnetic force under reliable controlled conditions.

Mesmer abandoned dependence on physical magnets and developed “passes”—slow movements of the hands over the patient’s body—along with touch, fixed gazes, music, magnetized objects, and induced crises.

### Gassner controversy and Munich, 1775

The Catholic priest **Johann Joseph Gassner** had become famous for dramatic exorcisms. Mesmer appeared in Munich during the official controversy over Gassner in 1775. He produced effects resembling Gassner’s apparent exorcistic cures but explained them as natural manifestations of animal magnetism rather than demonic possession.

The Munich Academy of Sciences elected Mesmer a member in **1775**, an important early institutional success. Henri Ellenberger later interpreted this episode as a symbolic transition from supernatural exorcism to secular dynamic psychiatry. That is a historiographic interpretation, not Mesmer’s own terminology.

## The Paradis Affair, 1776–1778

### Patient

**Maria Theresia von Paradis** was a blind pianist and composer, born in Vienna in 1759 and protected by Empress Maria Theresa. Her sight had reportedly disappeared suddenly in childhood. Numerous established physicians had treated her unsuccessfully.

Mesmer began treating her in late 1776 or early 1777, apparently without an initial fee, and eventually housed her in his home. He claimed that she recovered partial sight: she could distinguish movements, colors, figures, and eventually objects. Her new visual impressions were reportedly confusing and unpleasant.

### Competing accounts

**Claim by Mesmer and his defenders:** Paradis genuinely recovered sight under his treatment. Her parents withdrew her because a cure threatened her imperial disability pension, and hostile physicians conspired to suppress the evidence.

**Account attributed to her family and opponents:** the apparent improvement was unreliable; Mesmer isolated her from her family; her behavior and health deteriorated; the parents demanded her return.

**Documented result:** the treatment generated a confrontation involving the Paradis family, Mesmer, Anton von Störck, and possibly court or ecclesiastical authorities. Paradis left Mesmer’s care and remained blind. Mesmer’s standing in Vienna suffered severely.

### Sexual or romantic rumors

Rumors circulated that Paradis had become emotionally attached to Mesmer or that an improper relationship existed. Such stories were strengthened by the intimacy of his treatments and the fact that she lived in his house.

**Evidentiary status: rumor or allegation.** No reliable evidence consulted establishes a sexual relationship. Later fictional treatments have amplified it.

### Medical interpretations

Retrospective explanations have included functional blindness, hysteria or conversion disorder, malingering, neurological disease, ocular illness, autism or savant syndrome, and a partial but temporary recovery. A 2025 medical-biographical article emphasized that the age of onset, persistence, and severity do not neatly fit classic functional visual loss. The cause remains unresolved.

### Departure from Vienna

Mesmer left Vienna for Paris in **1778**; a recent historical study gives **22 June 1778**.

- **Hostile legend:** he was formally expelled as a proven charlatan.
- **Mesmer’s account:** he departed voluntarily and possessed a recommendation from Chancellor Wenzel Anton von Kaunitz to the Austrian ambassador in Paris, Florimond Claude, comte de Mercy-Argenteau.
- **Best reconstruction:** professional hostility and the Paradis collapse made Vienna untenable, but formal banishment has not been demonstrated.

Assertions that he “lost his medical license” are frequently repeated in modern popular journalism. The sources consulted do not document a formal revocation proceeding or judgment.

## Paris, 1778–1781

Mesmer arrived in Paris in early 1778 and initially stayed at the Hôtel de Bouillon. He sought recognition from the Académie royale des sciences, the Société royale de médecine, and the Paris Faculty of Medicine.

### Charles Deslon

His most important early French ally was **Charles Deslon** (also d’Eslon), physician to the comte d’Artois. Deslon observed treatments, became a disciple, defended animal magnetism publicly, and brought socially prominent patients.

The medical faculty threatened or disciplined Deslon for supporting a method it considered unproved. Mesmer himself lacked a Paris medical degree and therefore stood partly outside the faculty’s disciplinary structure.

### The 1779 *Mémoire*

Mesmer published *Mémoire sur la découverte du magnétisme animal* in 1779. Its celebrated twenty-seven propositions asserted, among other things, that:

1. a reciprocal influence exists among celestial bodies, the earth, and living beings;
2. a continuous, subtle fluid conveys that influence;
3. illness results from obstacles to the fluid’s movement;
4. crises can restore equilibrium;
5. animal magnetism can directly cure nervous diseases and indirectly cure others;
6. trained practitioners can perceive and direct the fluid.

The propositions mixed Newtonian vocabulary, older fluid theories, nervous physiology, analogy, and therapeutic assertion. Mesmer offered no independently measurable entity corresponding to his fluid.

### Practice and the *baquet*

By about **1780**, demand exceeded Mesmer’s capacity for individual sessions. He developed group treatments around the *baquet*, an enclosed tub containing bottles, water, iron filings, stones, or related materials, with iron rods projecting toward patients. Participants sat in a circle linked by ropes or by holding hands. Mirrors and music—including Mesmer’s glass harmonica—formed part of the setting.

Some patients cried, laughed, fainted, convulsed, became rigid, or entered altered states. Attendants called *valets toucheurs* carried severely affected patients to padded “crisis rooms.” Poor patients were reportedly treated without charge around a magnetized tree.

The theatrical setting is well documented. Whether Mesmer consciously designed it to manipulate expectation is an interpretation. He understood it as a method for concentrating and transmitting a real physical agency.

### Proposal for official testing

Mesmer sought a formal comparative trial. In 1780 he proposed that patients be divided between conventional treatment and his method. Negotiations failed amid disputes over selection, supervision, diagnostic categories, and whether he would disclose his technique. This complicates the simplified claim that he always refused testing, although his secrecy and insistence upon favorable conditions impeded evaluation.

### Spa and conflict with Deslon

In 1781 Mesmer withdrew to Spa and wrote *Précis historique des faits relatifs au magnétisme animal*. He complained that established institutions had prejudged his discovery. He also came to regard Deslon as a rival who was appropriating his practice and building an independent clientele.

## The Society of Universal Harmony, 1782–1785

### Foundation and structure

Mesmer’s supporters **Nicolas Bergasse**, a lawyer and political thinker, and **Guillaume Kornmann**, a Strasbourg banker, devised a subscription organization to purchase, preserve, teach, and disseminate Mesmer’s secret.

The organization became the **Société de l’harmonie universelle**, founded in 1783–1784. Subscribers generally paid **100 louis d’or**, a large sum. The first hundred members formed a socially distinguished body that included nobles, officers, physicians, financiers, lawyers, and politically active reformers.

Prominent associates or members included:

- Nicolas Bergasse;
- Guillaume Kornmann;
- Charles Deslon, despite his deteriorating relationship with Mesmer;
- the marquis de Lafayette;
- members of the Noailles and Montesquiou families;
- Jean-Jacques Duval d’Eprémesnil;
- Armand-Marie-Jacques de Chastenet, marquis de Puységur;
- other figures later involved in pre-Revolutionary and Revolutionary politics.

Provincial societies spread to Strasbourg, Lyon, Bordeaux and other cities.

### Finance and ownership of the “secret”

Mesmer received substantial subscription income in return for instruction and disclosure. Surviving correspondence documents fees, membership rules, authorized practitioners, and disputes over control.

**Documented controversy:** members complained that Mesmer had not supplied an adequate or reproducible secret. They demanded removal of the 100-louis charge and relaxation of secrecy. Mesmer believed that Bergasse, Kornmann, and associates were taking possession of his intellectual property and manipulating accounts or institutional control.

The quarrel culminated in **1785**, when Bergasse, Kornmann, and d’Eprémesnil were expelled or separated from Mesmer’s faction. Mesmer reportedly accepted a financial settlement of 20,000 livres or francs before leaving, but currency and amount vary in secondary accounts and require checking against the society’s accounts.

There is no known criminal conviction for fraud. The business model, however, provoked contemporary accusations of mercenary secrecy, charlatanism, breach of trust, and sale of an empty secret.

### Puységur and magnetic somnambulism

In 1784, Puységur magnetized a peasant, Victor Race, who entered a calm, sleep-like condition rather than a convulsive crisis. Puységur called it artificial or magnetic somnambulism. Subjects in this state spoke, obeyed suggestions, and sometimes purported to diagnose illnesses.

This innovation shifted mesmerism away from Mesmer’s crisis-centered fluid mechanics toward what became hypnotic suggestion, trance, clairvoyance, and psychotherapy. It was Puységur, rather than Mesmer, who supplied the most direct practical bridge to nineteenth-century hypnotism.

## The Royal Commissions of 1784

### What was actually investigated

Louis XVI appointed two commissions in 1784:

1. A joint group drawn from the Paris Faculty of Medicine and Académie royale des sciences. Members included **Benjamin Franklin**, **Jean-Sylvain Bailly**, **Antoine-Laurent Lavoisier**, **Joseph-Ignace Guillotin**, Jean d’Arcet, Michel-Joseph Majault, Charles-Louis Sallin, and Jean-Baptiste Le Roy.
2. A commission of the Société royale de médecine.

A critical qualification is often omitted: the best-known commission examined **animal magnetism as practiced by Deslon**, not a complete clinical trial personally conducted by Mesmer. Mesmer objected to Deslon’s participation and did not submit his own full practice to the commission.

### Experimental methods

The commissioners:

- observed group treatments and crises;
- magnetized subjects openly;
- blindfolded participants;
- told subjects they were being magnetized when they were not;
- magnetized objects or persons without the subject’s knowledge;
- tested a magnetized tree;
- separated expectation from the supposed physical agent.

Subjects sometimes exhibited symptoms when they believed magnetism was present and failed to respond when it was secretly applied.

### Findings

The principal commission concluded in August 1784 that it could find no evidence for Mesmer’s universal magnetic fluid. It attributed observed effects principally to:

- touch;
- imagination or expectation;
- imitation;
- physiological sensitivity and social contagion.

The report warned that repeated convulsions could themselves be harmful.

It did **not** establish that every patient had consciously faked symptoms, that no patient ever improved, or that Mesmer had been judicially convicted of fraud. It concluded that the hypothesized physical agent was unnecessary to explain the effects observed.

### Secret report on sexual danger

A confidential report to the king addressed the erotic and moral risks of treatment. It described the close bodily position of male magnetizer and female patient, prolonged contact, touching near the breasts, pelvis, and ovaries, mutual gazing, warmth, and escalating crises.

Deslon reportedly acknowledged that abuse was possible. The commissioners warned that vulnerable women might not understand the sensations being induced and that a practitioner could exploit them.

**Evidentiary distinction:** this was an official safety warning about opportunity, arousal, and power imbalance. It was not a finding that Mesmer personally committed sexual assault. No corresponding criminal charge against him is documented.

### Antoine-Laurent de Jussieu’s dissent

Botanist and physician **Antoine-Laurent de Jussieu**, on the Société royale de médecine commission, submitted an individual report. Although unconvinced by Mesmer’s universal fluid, he thought some observed effects could not be reduced completely to imagination and recommended further inquiry into an unknown action involving bodily heat or contact.

Thus “science unanimously proved nothing happened” is inaccurate. The majority rejected the claimed fluid; Jussieu preserved a limited unresolved physiological question.

### Historical importance

Later historians have treated the experiments as early examples of blinded testing, sham intervention, placebo control, and investigation of suggestion. Calling them the “first randomized clinical trial” is anachronistic: subjects were not randomized in the modern statistical sense, clinical endpoints were not standardized, and the inquiry focused primarily on the agent’s existence rather than long-term therapeutic outcomes.

## Collapse of Mesmer’s Parisian Position, 1784–1786

The commission reports were widely printed and translated. Satirical prints, songs, poems, theatrical pieces, and pamphlets portrayed mesmerists as sexual predators, gullible enthusiasts, occultists, or cheats operating around tubs.

Mesmer challenged the inquiry publicly, writing to Franklin, the *Journal de Paris*, Félix Vicq d’Azyr, and officials. A 14 May 1784 letter to Franklin declared that his discovery interested all nations and that he intended to provide both its history and his defense. The Franklin Papers note that Bergasse apparently drafted this and other French texts because Mesmer’s French was limited.

The dispute with Bergasse became public. Bergasse’s *Observations de M. Bergasse, sur un écrit du docteur Mesmer* (1785) accused Mesmer of betraying the society’s arrangements and misrepresenting their relationship.

In 1785 Mesmer left Paris and then France. His departure resulted from several combined pressures:

- official rejection of the physical fluid;
- public ridicule;
- the Society of Harmony schism;
- rivalry with Deslon and independent magnetizers;
- loss of control over a method already spreading across Europe.

The story that a commission simply “exposed him as a fraud and expelled him” compresses these separate events and overstates the commission’s legal authority.

## Travels and Relative Obscurity, 1785–1792

Mesmer’s movements after 1785 are less securely reconstructed than his Vienna and Paris careers. Sources place him at different times in Spa, Strasbourg, Switzerland, southern Germany, Vienna, and possibly England.

Some biographies say he traveled under reduced visibility because his notoriety made settled practice difficult. Claims of a prolonged London practice exist but are poorly documented.

He remained connected to disputes over the use and ownership of his doctrine. Mesmerism itself expanded without him: Puységurian somnambulism, spiritualist elaborations, medical experiments, and provincial harmony societies increasingly displaced Mesmer’s original model.

### French Revolutionary setting

Members of the Harmony societies later appeared in reformist and Revolutionary political networks. Robert Darnton’s influential interpretation presented mesmerism as part of an underground radical culture attacking academies, privilege, and established authority.

More recent research qualifies that model:

- the movement was politically heterogeneous;
- aristocrats, conservative elites, moderate reformers, Freemasons, future revolutionaries, and apolitical healers all participated;
- Mesmer himself was less politically radical than Bergasse’s faction;
- the society’s rhetoric of universal harmony could support competing political programs.

It is therefore unsound to label the whole movement either a revolutionary conspiracy or a politically innocent medical school.

## Vienna Arrest and Swiss Settlement, 1793–1802

### Arrest for suspected Jacobinism

Mesmer returned to Vienna on **14 September 1793**, during a period of intense Habsburg fear of French revolutionary subversion. He was denounced for Jacobin sympathies and taken into preventive custody on **17 November 1793**.

Police investigation reportedly found no operational conspiracy. He was nevertheless considered indiscreet or provocative in political speech and, on **9 December 1793**, was ordered away from Vienna toward his native region and placed under observation.

**Documented fact:** arrest, questioning, and expulsion from Vienna.

**Not established:** membership in a Jacobin organization, involvement in political violence, intelligence work, or a plot against the Habsburg monarchy.

### Thurgau citizenship

Mesmer settled in the Swiss canton of Thurgau and obtained local citizenship or *Landrecht* in **1794**. He lived in or near Wagenhausen and Frauenfeld at different times and practiced selectively.

### Financial losses and French pension

Mesmer had invested substantial money in French state annuities, often described as *rentes viagères*. Revolutionary fiscal disruption greatly reduced or endangered their value. He returned to Paris and Versailles between approximately **1798 and 1802** to pursue his claim.

In **1798**, the French government recognized him as a state creditor and reportedly granted an annual payment of **3,000 francs**. This made his later life financially independent, though not necessarily wealthy by his earlier Paris standard.

He published *Mémoires de F. A. Mesmer, docteur en médecine, sur ses découvertes* in 1799 and letters on smallpox in 1800. By February 1801 he was living in Versailles; in the winter of 1802–1803 he returned toward Lake Constance, eventually residing at Riedetsweiler near Meersburg.

## Final Years, 1803–1815

Mesmer lived quietly in the Lake Constance region, including Frauenfeld and Constance. He continued to receive visitors and correspond about animal magnetism, medicine, cosmology, education, morality, and law.

### Prussian interest

Swiss physician Joseph Anton Zugenbühler publicized Mesmer’s continuing activity. Berlin physicians including **Johann Christian Reil** and **Christoph Wilhelm Hufeland** supported renewed official investigation of magnetism.

Under Prussian state patronage, a commission was organized in **1812**. Its approach was more receptive than the French inquiry, partly because it investigated the expanded phenomena of somnambulism and therapeutic practice rather than only Mesmer’s fluid.

**Karl Christian Wolfart**, physician and commission member, visited Mesmer near Lake Constance in September and October 1812. He recorded Mesmer’s oral teaching and edited it into *Mesmerismus oder System der Wechselwirkungen* (1814). The work is therefore partly dictated or mediated rather than a wholly autograph composition.

Mesmer declined proposals that he move to Berlin to direct a magnetic institution.

### Constance and Meersburg

Reported residential chronology varies slightly:

- Frauenfeld into the early nineteenth century;
- Constance from approximately 1808 or 1812 to 1814;
- Riedetsweiler in summer 1814;
- Meersburg’s Heilig-Geist-Spital from autumn 1814.

In Meersburg, his nephew **Cajetan Strommayer**, an administrator of the hospital, helped arrange his accommodation. Mesmer lived with household attendants and reportedly discussed science with seminarians and students.

### Illness and death

Mesmer became seriously ill around **26 February 1815** and died on **5 March 1815**, aged eighty.

Competing causes appear in the literature:

- **Stroke:** the most commonly repeated local account.
- **Uremia following prostate disease:** proposed in later medical biography.

No modern autopsy evidence exists; the precise cause should therefore be treated as unresolved.

He was buried in Meersburg. A monumental tomb erected in 1830 by admirers or a society of naturalists bears cosmic and geometric symbolism, contributing to later occult readings of his career.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Medical practice

Mesmer maintained private practices in Vienna and Paris. The Vienna practice operated partly through his prosperous marital household. The Paris enterprise combined private consultations, group therapy, assistants, elite clients, poor relief, demonstrations, publications, and paid instruction.

### Therapeutic technologies

- shaped mineral magnets, initially supplied by Maximilian Hell;
- hand “passes” along the body;
- direct touch and pressure;
- fixed gaze;
- magnetized water, trees, ropes, and objects;
- the group *baquet*;
- mirrors;
- music and the glass harmonica;
- “crisis rooms” attended by assistants;
- chains of patients intended to conduct or amplify the fluid.

No patent is known. Mesmer treated the method as proprietary knowledge protected by secrecy and subscription agreements rather than patent law.

### Société de l’harmonie universelle

This was Mesmer’s central institutional and commercial project. It was simultaneously:

- a school;
- a subscription business;
- a licensing or authorization network;
- a therapeutic movement;
- a social club;
- a quasi-initiatory society;
- a vehicle for provincial expansion.

Its 100-louis subscription and secrecy requirement generated both revenue and suspicion. Surviving papers show financial disagreements rather than a simple charitable scientific association.

### Property and investments

- Access to the Landstraße residence and Rothmühle property came through Maria Anna von Bosch.
- Mesmer accumulated substantial French earnings.
- He invested in French state annuities.
- Revolutionary devaluation or repudiation caused serious losses.
- Recognition as a state creditor and the reported 3,000-franc annual payment restored financial security.

No evidence located supports hidden banking structures, money laundering, bribery convictions, or ownership of industrial companies.

## Political, Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### Catholic formation and naturalization of exorcism

Mesmer was educated in Catholic and Jesuit institutions. His intervention against Gassner did not deny that dramatic cures occurred; it reclassified them as natural rather than demonic. Animal magnetism thus participated in an Enlightenment transformation of marvelous phenomena into proposed natural forces.

### Natural philosophy

His thinking combined:

- cosmic reciprocity;
- universal fluid;
- tidal analogy;
- bodily equilibrium;
- nervous sensitivity;
- crisis as a route to cure;
- musical harmony;
- therapeutic rapport;
- a conviction that nature formed one interconnected system.

He rejected the claim that the effect was merely the individual magnetizer’s supernatural gift. In theory, the fluid was universal and technique could be taught.

### Moral and social theory

Later writings expanded “harmony” beyond medicine to morals, education, and legislation. Mesmer proposed that social order should accord with natural reciprocal relations. His unpublished or posthumously serialized *Notions élémentaires sur la morale, l’éducation et la législation* belongs to this phase.

### Republicanism

Recent research describes Mesmer as a moralist and republican in his later life. His 1793 statements were sufficiently anti-monarchical or pro-French to attract Austrian police attention. Yet no evidence makes him a central revolutionary organizer. The political radicalization of mesmerism was driven substantially by disciples such as Bergasse and by later groups.

### Freemasonry, Illuminati, and occultism

The Society of Harmony borrowed forms familiar from initiatory and Masonic culture: degrees of access, secrecy, elite subscription, ritualized meetings, and universalist language. Freemasons and members of the Philalèthes entered the society and competed for influence in 1785.

**Documented:** organizational contact between mesmerist and Masonic networks.

**Unresolved or unsupported:** that Mesmer himself was an initiated Freemason of a particular lodge; that he belonged to Adam Weishaupt’s Bavarian Illuminati; or that he participated in a coordinated occult conspiracy to cause the French Revolution.

Later occultists absorbed animal magnetism into theories of astral fluid, clairvoyance, spiritual healing, magic, and psychic force. Much of that systematization postdates Mesmer and should not be projected backward onto him.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Maria Anna von Bosch

Wife, household partner, and source of the property and wealth that supported his early career. The marriage effectively ended through separation when Mesmer left Vienna.

### Franziska Österlin

Patient whose treatment supplied Mesmer’s first celebrated evidence for magnetic cures. Later accounts say she recovered and married Mesmer’s stepson, but this detail requires stronger documentation.

### Maximilian Hell

Jesuit astronomer, maker of therapeutic magnets, early collaborator, and then priority rival.

### Johann Joseph Gassner

Priest-exorcist whom Mesmer confronted indirectly and experimentally. Mesmer accepted the phenomena while rejecting the supernatural explanation.

### Maria Theresia von Paradis

Patient at the center of Mesmer’s most damaging Viennese controversy. Their relationship became a recurring subject of biography, fiction, film, and sexual rumor.

### Charles Deslon

Parisian medical patron, disciple, spokesman, rival, and the practitioner examined by the 1784 commission.

### Nicolas Bergasse

Patient, theorist, secretary, ghostwriter or drafter of French polemical texts, co-organizer of the Society of Harmony, and later bitter opponent.

### Guillaume Kornmann

Banker, financial patron, society treasurer or organizer, and participant in the 1785 schism.

### Marquis de Puységur

Disciple who developed magnetic somnambulism. His innovations became more consequential for hypnosis than Mesmer’s original crisis method.

### Benjamin Franklin

Commissioner and recipient of Mesmer’s correspondence. Their relationship was official and adversarial in scientific terms, not a friendship.

### Antoine-Laurent Lavoisier and Jean-Sylvain Bailly

Commissioners responsible for the experimental and analytical rejection of the magnetic fluid.

### Lafayette

Subscriber or supporter in the mesmerist network. Surviving society material reportedly authorized him to teach the method in the United States.

### Karl Christian Wolfart

Late-life visitor, student, compiler, and editor of Mesmer’s final systematic work.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### 1. Unacknowledged borrowing in the dissertation

- **Underlying fact:** extensive dependence on Richard Mead.
- **Label:** documented scholarly criticism.
- **Unresolved issue:** whether “plagiarism” fairly describes eighteenth-century doctoral practice.
- **Effect:** weakens the mythology of wholly original discovery.

### 2. Conflict with Maximilian Hell

- **Issue:** who deserved credit for magnetic therapy.
- **Label:** documented priority dispute.
- **No established finding:** neither claimant demonstrated the therapeutic force.

### 3. Paradis affair

- **Documented:** treatment, claimed temporary sight, family conflict, withdrawal, continuing blindness, damage to Mesmer’s reputation.
- **Allegations:** pension-motivated parental sabotage; medical conspiracy; improper emotional or sexual relationship; deliberate fraud.
- **Finding:** no court adjudication or decisive medical evidence resolved these accusations.

### 4. Secrecy and sale of a medical “secret”

- **Documented:** 100-louis subscriptions, secrecy obligations, paid instruction, internal dispute.
- **Contemporary criticism:** Mesmer sold an empty or inadequately disclosed secret.
- **Legal status:** no fraud conviction located.

### 5. The 1784 inquiries

- **Documented:** controlled experiments failed to verify the fluid; imagination, touch, and imitation explained responses.
- **Frequent distortion:** the commission did not put Mesmer personally on criminal trial, revoke his Vienna degree, or prove that every therapeutic improvement was fabricated.

### 6. Sexual danger

- **Documented:** the secret report identified eroticized procedures and opportunity for abuse; Deslon reportedly conceded the risk.
- **Not established:** personal sexual assault by Mesmer.
- **Historical importance:** one of the earliest institutional discussions of clinical power asymmetry and sexual exploitation risk.

### 7. Political arrest

- **Documented:** arrested in Vienna in 1793 after denunciation for Jacobin sympathies; released and expelled under surveillance.
- **Not established:** treason, intelligence activity, violent conspiracy, or conviction.

### 8. Modern characterization as a con man

Historians divide broadly among three interpretations:

- Mesmer as a conscious medical impostor;
- Mesmer as a sincere but self-deceived theorist who commercialized an erroneous system;
- Mesmer as a mistaken natural philosopher who nevertheless discovered reproducible effects of expectation, suggestion, group contagion, and therapeutic relationship.

The documentary record proves false physical claims by modern standards and a profitable secret-selling system. It does not by itself disclose his private intent. “Fraud” is therefore secure as a contemporary accusation, not as a demonstrated state of mind or criminal verdict.

### Dedicated adversarial pass, 2024–2026

Searches combining Mesmer’s name with controversy, scandal, allegations, lawsuit, fraud, accusations, conspiracy, and criticism located no new archival criminal allegation against Mesmer in 2024–2026.

Recent material instead produced:

- Bruno Belhoste’s 2024 archival biography, which rejects both simple charlatan and persecuted-genius caricatures;
- new studies of the political and institutional context of mesmerism;
- a 2025 medical reconsideration of Paradis’s blindness;
- popular journalism using Mesmer as an ancestor of present-day miracle-cure commerce, sometimes inaccurately claiming a formally revoked medical license.

No evidence was found for lawsuits, criminal convictions, corruption findings, money laundering, patents, political violence, or intelligence-service connections.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Mesmer invented hypnosis”

**Story:** Mesmer discovered hypnotism in essentially its modern form.

**Evidence:** he induced crises and altered states using expectation, gaze, passes, touch, music, ritual, and authority. He did not formulate hypnotic suggestion as later understood. Puységur’s somnambulism, James Braid’s nineteenth-century “hypnotism,” and later clinical schools supplied crucial conceptual changes.

**Effect:** it secured Mesmer’s place in histories of psychotherapy while obscuring his literal belief in a fluid.

### “The commissioners tested Mesmer and proved he was a fraud”

**Story:** Franklin personally exposed Mesmer in a decisive trial.

**Evidence against the simplified version:** the commission examined Deslon’s practice; its experiments rejected the fluid but did not adjudicate Mesmer’s honesty or all clinical outcomes.

**Effect:** this remains the dominant skeptical legend and a frequent origin story for placebo-controlled medicine.

### “Paradis was cured, but her parents made her blind again to save a pension”

This was Mesmer’s defensive narrative. The family’s financial incentive was possible because Paradis received court support, but no evidence proves that her parents deliberately restored or pretended blindness. Her later successful career as a blind musician strengthened both hostile and sympathetic retellings.

### “Mesmer seduced Paradis”

This appeared through Viennese malice, later biography, novels, and film. Physical intimacy and residence in his household made the rumor credible to contemporaries, but no primary evidence consulted proves a sexual relationship.

### “Mozart premiered *Bastien und Bastienne* in Mesmer’s garden”

The story derives from later Mozart biography. The garden theater may not have been completed in 1768; confusion with another Dr. Mesmer is possible. It remains a famous but unverified cultural association.

### “Mesmer escaped the guillotine”

Later biographies say revolutionary danger forced him to flee Paris and that he narrowly escaped execution. He did suffer financial losses and left revolutionary France, but a specific arrest warrant or documented near-execution has not been located.

### “Mesmer was an Illuminatus or occult master”

The doctrine entered Masonic and occult networks; initiatory societies competed around the Harmony movement. Direct membership in the Bavarian Illuminati is not established. The later conspiracy narrative confuses network overlap with centralized direction.

### “Animal magnetism was secretly validated in 1831”

Magnetic advocates frequently state that the Paris Academy officially reversed the 1784 verdict. Later committees did report apparently extraordinary somnambulistic phenomena, but institutional decisions were contested and did not establish Mesmer’s universal fluid. The claim functions as movement hagiography.

### “His tomb encodes a secret cosmic doctrine”

Mesmer’s 1830 grave monument bears geometric and celestial imagery. Because it was erected fifteen years after his death, it is evidence of his followers’ commemoration, not necessarily a code designed by Mesmer.

## Works by Franz Anton Mesmer

Authorship and dating are unusually difficult because Mesmer used secret instruction, dictated aphorisms, relied on French-speaking collaborators, and left texts edited after his death.

### Authenticated or substantially authenticated works

1. **1766 — *Antonii Mesmer Marisburgensis Acron. Suev. Dissertatio physico-medica de planetarum influxu*.**  
   Latin medical dissertation, Vienna. Genuine authorship, with extensive dependence on Richard Mead.

2. **1775 — *Sendschreiben über die Magnetkur an einen auswärtigen Arzt*.**  
   Letter on magnetic treatment, dated Vienna, 5 January 1775, addressed to Johann Christoph Unzer.

3. **1775 — *Zweites Schreiben an das Publikum*.**  
   Public defense arising from the Hell controversy.

4. **1775 — *Drittes Schreiben an die Frankfurter*.**  
   Another intervention in the magnetic-treatment controversy.

5. **1776 — *Schreiben über die Magnetkur*.**  
   Collected or expanded German material on the magnetic cure.

6. **1779 — *Mémoire sur la découverte du magnétisme animal*.**  
   Geneva and Paris, P. Fr. Didot le jeune. Principal exposition containing the twenty-seven propositions.

7. **1781 — *Précis historique des faits relatifs au magnétisme animal, jusques en avril 1781*.**  
   Polemical history and defense, published under Mesmer’s name.

8. **1781 — *Lettre à Monsieur Mesmer et autres pièces concernant la maladie de la Dlle Berlancourt de Beauvais*.**  
   Documentary/polemical collection involving a patient; Mesmer’s exact share requires bibliographic examination.

9. **1784 — Letters to Benjamin Franklin, the *Journal de Paris*, Vicq d’Azyr, Breteuil, and others.**  
   Published separately and in newspapers. Some were drafted or polished by Bergasse.

10. **1784 — *Lettre de M. Mesmer à M. le Comte de C***.**  
    Includes an appeal or request to the Parlement of Paris.

11. **1785 — *Lettre de l’auteur de la découverte du magnétisme animal à l’auteur des Réflexions préliminaires*.**  
    Response concerning money paid for the right to publish the discovery.

12. **1799 — *Mémoires de F. A. Mesmer, docteur en médecine, sur ses découvertes*.**  
    Paris, Fuchs, Year VII. Later-life restatement.

13. **1800 — *Lettres de F. A. Mesmer, D.M., sur l’origine de la petite vérole et le moyen de la faire cesser*.**  
    Paris, Imprimerie des sciences et des arts, Fructidor Year VIII.

14. **c. 1803 — *Précis de la découverte du magnétisme animal, avec une lettre sur le flux et le reflux*.**

15. **1814 — “Auszug aus einem das gelbe Fieber betreffenden im Jahr 1804 geschriebenen Brief.”**  
    Published in *Neues Askläpieion*.

16. **1814 — *Mesmerismus oder System der Wechselwirkungen, Theorie und Anwendung des thierischen Magnetismus als die allgemeine Heilkunde zur Erhaltung des Menschen*.**  
    Berlin, Nicolai. Edited by Karl Christian Wolfart from Mesmer’s oral explanations and papers. The title page incorrectly calls him “Friedrich Anton Mesmer.” Best classified as authorized, mediated authorship.

### Dictated, edited, attributed, or posthumous material

17. **1784 — *Théorie du monde et des êtres organisés, suivant les principes de M***.**  
    Generally associated with Bergasse’s systematization of Mesmer’s teaching; not straightforward autograph authorship.

18. **1785 — *Aphorismes de M. Mesmer, dictés à l’assemblée de ses élèves*.**  
    Edited by Caullet de Veaumorel. Purports to record Mesmer’s dictated teaching; several editions appeared in 1785.

19. **1846–1849 — *Notions élémentaires sur la morale, l’éducation et la législation, pour servir à l’instruction publique en France*.**  
    Posthumously serialized in the *Journal du magnétisme*. Connected with the moral-political portion of Mesmer’s later system; publication occurred more than thirty years after his death.

20. **1971 — *Le Magnétisme animal*, edited by Robert Amadou.**  
    Modern collected edition containing a French translation of the dissertation and Mesmer’s principal French works and letters. This is an edited collection, not a lifetime publication.

### Works often confused with Mesmer’s own

- Charles Deslon, *Observations sur le magnétisme animal* (1780): disciple’s work, not Mesmer’s.
- Nicolas Bergasse, *Considérations sur le magnétisme animal* (1784): systematic exposition by Bergasse.
- Puységur’s writings on magnetic somnambulism: independent development.
- Anonymous pro- and anti-magnetic pamphlets: attribution requires case-by-case evidence.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Language

The verb **“to mesmerize”** detached Mesmer’s name from his fluid theory and came to mean to fascinate, enthrall, spellbind, or hold attention completely. This is his most widespread cultural legacy.

### Hypnosis and psychotherapy

A defensible genealogy is:

**Mesmer’s crisis practice → Puységur’s magnetic somnambulism → nineteenth-century animal magnetism → James Braid’s hypnotism → later suggestive therapy and clinical hypnosis.**

Mesmer’s contribution was therefore foundational but indirect. He established a reproducible social-therapeutic setting in which expectation, attention, authority, bodily ritual, and interpersonal rapport produced striking effects. Later practitioners discarded his physical fluid while retaining or reformulating the phenomena.

### Placebo and experimental medicine

The 1784 inquiry became a landmark in the history of blinded and sham-controlled investigation. Its strongest enduring discovery was not simply that “nothing happened,” but that belief and context could produce observable bodily experiences.

### Psychiatry and psychology

Charcot, Bernheim, Janet, Freud, and later psychotherapists worked in traditions partly descended from magnetic somnambulism. Freud did not inherit Mesmer’s fluid doctrine, but hypnosis and the study of unconscious processes entered his professional world through this longer lineage.

### Alternative medicine and occultism

Animal magnetism fed into:

- spiritualism;
- clairvoyance;
- psychical research;
- mental healing;
- magnetic healing;
- occult theories of astral or vital force;
- later “energy medicine.”

Modern practitioners sometimes claim direct scientific validation of Mesmer’s fluid. Established physics and medicine have not identified such a substance.

### Historical reinterpretation

Mesmer has successively been depicted as:

- a charlatan;
- a persecuted medical genius;
- an occult adept;
- the inventor of hypnosis;
- a proto-psychotherapist;
- an entrepreneur of fashionable illness;
- a sincere but mistaken natural philosopher;
- an accidental discoverer of suggestion and placebo effects;
- a figure linking Enlightenment natural philosophy with Revolutionary social thought.

Recent scholarship tends to treat him neither as simply fraudulent nor scientifically vindicated. It reconstructs how an erroneous fluid theory could organize real experiences, profitable institutions, social conflict, experimental innovation, and new forms of psychological practice.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere contemporaneity is excluded.

### Benjamin Franklin — documented fact

Franklin served on the 1784 royal commission examining animal magnetism as practiced by Charles Deslon. Mesmer wrote to him on 14 May 1784. Franklin and Mesmer were thus connected through an official scientific investigation and correspondence, not friendship.

### Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart — reported fact and disputed tradition

Mesmer moved in Mozart’s Viennese musical environment. Later tradition places *Bastien und Bastienne* at Mesmer’s house in 1768, but no contemporary performance documentation survives and another Dr. Mesmer may have been involved. Mozart unquestionably satirized magnetic healing in *Così fan tutte* in 1790.

### Jean-Jacques Rousseau — documented indirect artistic influence

Mozart’s *Bastien und Bastienne*, traditionally connected with Mesmer’s household, ultimately derives from Rousseau’s *Le devin du village* through French theatrical adaptations. No direct Mesmer–Rousseau meeting was found.

### Louis XVI — documented institutional connection

Louis XVI appointed the 1784 commissions that examined animal magnetism. No evidence of a substantive personal relationship with Mesmer was found.

### Marie Adrienne Françoise de Noailles and Lafayette — reported network connection

The Noailles family and the marquis de Lafayette appeared among elite supporters or subscribers associated with the Society of Universal Harmony. Lafayette was reportedly authorized to transmit the practice in the United States.

### Napoleon Bonaparte — reported institutional afterlife, no demonstrated personal meeting

Mesmer received recognition as a French state creditor during the Directory/Consular period and stayed in Paris and Versailles around 1798–1802. Later sources attribute remarks about Mesmer to Napoleon, but no securely documented personal meeting was found.

### Sigmund Freud — documented intellectual lineage, not personal contact

Freud was born decades after Mesmer’s death. Their connection is historical: animal magnetism led through somnambulism and hypnosis to the clinical traditions from which early psychoanalysis emerged. No direct relationship was possible.

### Carl Gustav Jung — documented intellectual afterlife, not personal contact

Jung’s psychology developed in a culture already shaped by hypnosis, dissociation, occult experimentation, and psychical research. This is a mediated intellectual lineage, not a personal connection or an explicitly documented debt to Mesmer in the sources consulted.

### Emanuel Swedenborg, Giuseppe Balsamo (Cagliostro), the Comte de Saint-Germain, and Helena Petrovna Blavatsky — reported cultural association only

These figures are frequently grouped with Mesmer in histories of Western esotericism, secret societies, or occult healing. The research consulted did not establish a direct meeting or correspondence between Mesmer and Swedenborg, Cagliostro, or Saint-Germain. Blavatsky belonged to a later generation and incorporated magnetic concepts into a broader occult environment. These are reception-history connections, not documented personal relationships.

### Immanuel Kant — documented conceptual reception, no personal meeting established

Kant wrote within the same Enlightenment debate over invisible forces, enthusiasm, dreams, and Swedenborgian claims, but no direct contact with Mesmer was found. Later scholarship compares their treatment of imagination and occult claims; comparison is not evidence of a relationship.

### Johann Wolfgang von Goethe — reported shared cultural scene, no direct connection established

Goethe lived through the European vogue for animal magnetism, which entered German literary and scientific culture. The consulted sources did not establish a meeting or correspondence with Mesmer.

### Remaining roster

No reliable direct intersection with the other named atlas figures was identified in the sources actually consulted. The absence of a listed connection should not be interpreted as proof that no archival contact existed; it means none was sufficiently documented for inclusion here.
