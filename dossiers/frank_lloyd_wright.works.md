## Works by Frank Lloyd Wright

### Architectural obra: houses and residential complexes

- **1889:** *Frank Lloyd Wright Home and Studio*. Wright’s own Oak Park house, later enlarged with a studio; it became both a domestic experiment and a workplace, and thus an early Gesamtkunstwerk. [1][3]
- **1890:** *Robert P. Parker House*. A compact Oak Park residence generally catalogued among Wright’s early independent or “bootleg” commissions. [1]
- **1891:** *Thomas H. Gale House*. An Oak Park residence whose authorship belongs to Wright’s disputed period of commissions undertaken while employed by Adler & Sullivan. [1]
- **1892:** *Walter Gale House*. A residence marking Wright’s movement away from historical revival styles toward the Prairie house. [1]
- **1893:** *William H. Winslow House*. A River Forest residence and one of Wright’s first commissions after opening his independent practice. [1][3]
- **1894:** *Robert G. Emmond House*. A La Grange residence catalogued as an early independent work. [1]
- **1894:** *Robert G. Emmond Stable*. A related service building; its separate identity complicates whether the commission is counted as one work or two. [1]
- **1895:** *Nathan G. Moore House*. An Oak Park residence designed in a Tudorizing idiom at the client’s request and substantially reconstructed by Wright after a 1922 fire. [1][3]
- **1895:** *Francis J. Woolley House*. An Oak Park residence transitional between Wright’s early work and the mature Prairie house. [1]
- **1895:** *Chauncey L. Williams House*. A River Forest residence whose low roofs and horizontal organization anticipate the Prairie vocabulary. [1]
- **1896:** *Isidore H. Heller House*. A Chicago residence combining a vertically organized façade with ornament and sculpture integrated into the architecture. [1]
- **1897:** *Rollin Furbeck House*. An Oak Park residence with a conspicuous vertical stair tower, resisting the easy identification of all Wright’s work of this period as horizontal Prairie design. [1]
- **1897:** *George W. Smith House*. An Oak Park residence developed from Wright’s geometric planning experiments. [1]
- **1899:** *William G. Fricke House*. An Oak Park residence with a strongly vertical façade and integrated art glass. [1]
- **1900:** *Arthur Heurtley House*. A major mature Prairie residence in Oak Park, conceived as an integrated composition of plan, masonry, furnishings, glass, and ornament. [1][3]
- **1900:** *Stephen M. B. Hunt House*. A La Grange residence within Wright’s developing Prairie practice. [1]
- **1901:** *Frank W. Thomas House*. An Oak Park residence notable for raising the principal living floor above street level. [1][3]
- **1901:** *William G. Fricke Garage*. A small ancillary structure separately catalogued from the house it served. [1]
- **1902:** *Dana–Thomas House*. A Springfield residence made by transforming an earlier house for Susan Lawrence Dana; architecture, furniture, art glass, lighting, and ornament form a Gesamtkunstwerk. [1][3]
- **1902:** *Ward W. Willits House*. A Highland Park residence organized around a cruciform plan and widely treated as a paradigmatic Prairie house. [1]
- **1903:** *Edwin H. Cheney House*. An Oak Park residence whose compressed entrance and open interior became recurring Wright devices. [1]
- **1903–1905:** *Darwin D. Martin House Complex*. A Buffalo domestic compound comprising the Martin House, Barton House, Gardener’s Cottage, carriage house, conservatory, pergola, furnishings, and art glass; it is more accurately a Gesamtkunstwerk than a single building. [1][3]
- **1904:** *Robert M. Lamp House*. A Madison residence built on a confined urban lot, with rooftop space functioning as part of the architectural composition. [1]
- **1905:** *Thomas P. Hardy House*. A Racine residence placed dramatically above Lake Michigan. [1]
- **1905:** *K. C. DeRhodes House*. A South Bend Prairie residence whose interior and furnishings participated in the commission’s unified design. [1]
- **1905–1908:** *Avery Coonley House*. A Riverside estate incorporating residence, service wings, gardens, furnishings, and art glass; later alterations and subdivision complicate the boundaries of the original obra. [1][3]
- **1906:** *Frederick C. Robie House*. A Chicago Prairie house whose long cantilevers, open plan, art glass, furniture, and site organization make it a canonical Gesamtkunstwerk. [1][3][8]
- **1906:** *A. W. Gridley House*. A Batavia residence that adapts Prairie planning to a comparatively symmetrical exterior. [1]
- **1906:** *Yahara Boat Club*. An unrealized Madison boathouse project; it exists as intention and drawings rather than as a building. [2]
- **1907:** *George Barton House*. The first completed dwelling within the larger Martin complex, sometimes counted independently and sometimes as part of that Gesamtkunstwerk. [1]
- **1907:** *Westcott House*. A Springfield, Ohio, Prairie residence with associated garage and pergola. [1]
- **1908:** *May House*. A Grand Rapids Prairie residence whose later history includes extensive alteration and restoration. [1]
- **1908:** *Edward E. Boynton House*. A Rochester residence for which Wright designed substantial furniture, glazing, and landscape elements. [1]
- **1908:** *Isabel Roberts House*. A River Forest residence produced in Wright’s studio; Isabel Roberts’s later architectural career and studio role make simplistic assumptions about solitary authorship inadequate. [1]
- **1908:** *Seth W. Peterson Cottage project*. Not to be confused with Wright’s much later Seth Peterson Cottage; the catalogues distinguish projects by client, date, and archival number where titles recur. [2]
- **1909:** *Mrs. Thomas H. Gale House*. A comparatively radical Oak Park house composed of interlocking cubic masses. [1]
- **1909:** *Oscar B. Balch House*. An Oak Park residence from the end of Wright’s Prairie period. [1]
- **1909:** *Park Inn Hotel and City National Bank*. A mixed-use Mason City building combining hotel and bank; only portions survive, so the extant object is not identical with Wright’s complete commission. [1]
- **1910:** *Emil Bach House*. A compact Chicago residence balancing Prairie horizontality with a more cubic composition. [1]
- **1911 onward:** *Taliesin*. Wright’s Wisconsin home, studio, school, farm, and continuously revised architectural environment; fires and reconstructions mean that it is a continuing project and Gesamtkunstwerk rather than one stable object. [1][2][8]
- **1913–1915:** *Midway Gardens*. A Chicago entertainment complex integrating architecture, landscape, sculpture, murals, furniture, tableware, and performance spaces; demolished, it survives chiefly through fragments and documentation. [1][2]
- **1915–1922:** *Aline Barnsdall Residence (Hollyhock House)*. The principal realized building of an intended Los Angeles arts complex; the larger project remained incomplete, while the house itself integrates architecture, ornament, furnishings, and landscape. [1][2][8]
- **1916–1922:** *Imperial Hotel*. A Tokyo hotel and Gesamtkunstwerk designed down to furniture, carpets, dishes, and fittings; demolished in 1967, with its entrance wing reconstructed at Meiji-mura. [1][2]
- **1917:** *Frederick C. Bogk House*. A Milwaukee residence combining Prairie principles with the heavier massing of Wright’s 1910s work. [1]
- **1917:** *American System-Built Houses*. A protocol-like system of standardized but variable house designs rather than one building; several examples were constructed, often with disputed or complicated relationships among Wright, dealers, builders, and individual owners. [1][2]
- **1919:** *Henry J. and Alice Millard House (La Miniatura)*. A Pasadena residence and early experiment in patterned concrete-block construction. [1]
- **1923:** *John Storer House*. A Los Angeles textile-block residence whose patterned blocks operate simultaneously as structure, enclosure, and ornament. [1]
- **1923:** *Samuel Freeman House*. A Los Angeles textile-block residence; construction diverged in places from the drawings, and later restoration has become part of the object’s history. [1]
- **1923:** *Charles Ennis House*. A monumental Los Angeles textile-block residence whose fabric has undergone extensive deterioration, repair, and reconstruction. [1]
- **1924:** *Alice Millard Playhouse and Studio project*. An addition to La Miniatura; only parts of Wright’s intention were realized. [2]
- **1927:** *Graycliff*. A lakeside estate for Isabelle and Darwin Martin consisting of several buildings, landscape, furnishings, and successive design revisions. [1]
- **1929:** *Richard Lloyd Jones House (Westhope)*. A Tulsa textile-block residence adapted to a humid climate, not simply a transplant of the California block houses. [1]
- **1929:** *Ocatilla Desert Camp*. An ephemeral Arizona camp of wood and canvas; dismantled, it resists the assumption that architectural obra must be permanent. [1][2]
- **1934:** *Malcolm Willey House*. A Minneapolis residence transitional between the Prairie house and Wright’s later Usonian work. [1]
- **1934:** *Fallingwater*. A house, landscape intervention, interior, furniture ensemble, and inhabitable manifesto of organic architecture designed for the Kaufmann family. [1][2][8]
- **1936:** *Herbert and Katherine Jacobs House*. The first built house Wright explicitly identified as “Usonian,” intended as a model of affordable, spatially integrated American dwelling. [1][8]
- **1936–1939:** *Wingspread (Herbert F. Johnson House)*. A large pinwheel-plan residence at Wind Point, Wisconsin, with integrated furnishings and fittings. [1]
- **1937 onward:** *Taliesin West*. A winter camp, home, studio, school, and collective construction site repeatedly altered by Wright and the Taliesin Fellowship; authorship is therefore real but distributed, and the work remained a process. [1][2][8]
- **1938:** *Ben Rebhuhn House*. A Great Neck Usonian residence using brick, cypress, and a modular plan. [1]
- **1939:** *Lloyd Lewis House*. A Libertyville residence positioned above the Des Plaines River. [1]
- **1939:** *Goetsch–Winckler House*. An East Lansing Usonian house commissioned jointly by Alma Goetsch and Kathrine Winckler. [1]
- **1939:** *Bernard Schwartz House (Still Bend)*. A Wisconsin Usonian house developed from Wright’s “Life” magazine model-house proposal. [1]
- **1940:** *Pope–Leighey House*. A Usonian residence moved twice for preservation; the present building’s site is therefore not the one for which Wright designed it. [1]
- **1940:** *John and Syd Dobkins House project*. An unrealized residential project, distinct from the later built Dobkins House. [2]
- **1941:** *Affleck House*. A Bloomfield Hills Usonian residence set above a sloping site. [1]
- **1941:** *Richard and Berenice Smith House*. A Jefferson, Wisconsin, Usonian residence. [1]
- **1943–1959:** *Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum*. A museum developed through prolonged revisions and completed shortly after Wright’s death; its final realization involved his office and therefore cannot be reduced to a single dated design act. [1][2][8]
- **1944:** *Suntop Homes*. A four-dwelling “quadruple house” prototype; only one of the proposed units was built. [1][2]
- **1945:** *John D. Haynes House*. A Fort Wayne Usonian residence later relocated, materially complicating the identity of building and site. [1]
- **1946:** *David and Gladys Wright House*. A Phoenix residence organized by a spiral ramp, anticipating aspects of the Guggenheim Museum. [1]
- **1947:** *Kenneth and Phyllis Laurent House*. A Rockford Usonian residence designed around the mobility requirements of a wheelchair user. [1]
- **1947:** *V. C. Morris Gift Shop*. A San Francisco commercial interior organized around a spiral ramp; it functioned as an architectural experiment as well as a shop. [1]
- **1948:** *Maynard and Katharine Buehler House*. An Orinda Usonian residence and landscape with furniture and other elements designed as an ensemble. [1]
- **1948:** *Eric and Pat Pratt House*. A Galesburg Country Homes residence produced within a larger cooperative-development project. [1]
- **1948:** *Curtis and Lillian Meyer House*. A hemicycle-plan residence in the Galesburg Country Homes development. [1]
- **1948:** *Robert and Rae Levin House*. Another house within the Galesburg cooperative project; later alterations complicate the integrity of the original work. [1]
- **1948:** *Roland and Ronny Reisley House*. A Usonian cooperative residence at Usonia, New York, later expanded with Wright’s involvement. [1]
- **1949:** *Robert Berger House*. A San Anselmo Usonian house partly constructed by its owner from Wright’s drawings; completion extended beyond Wright’s lifetime. [1]
- **1949:** *Patrick and Margaret Kinney House*. A Lancaster, Wisconsin, Usonian residence with an unusual polygonal module. [1]
- **1949:** *John and Syd Dobkins House*. A Canton, Ohio, Usonian residence, distinct from earlier unbuilt proposals bearing the clients’ names. [1]
- **1950:** *John and Catherine Christian House (Samara)*. A West Lafayette house conceived with furniture, textiles, tableware, and landscape as a Gesamtkunstwerk; portions were realized gradually by the clients. [1][3]
- **1950:** *Toufic H. Kalil House*. A Manchester, New Hampshire, Usonian Automatic house built with modular concrete blocks. [1]
- **1950:** *Zimmerman House*. A Manchester Usonian house with furniture, gardens, and household objects designed as an integrated environment. [1]
- **1950:** *Donald and Virginia Lovness House and Cottage*. A Minnesota residential compound built substantially by its owners; house and cottage form related but separable works. [1]
- **1951:** *Gerald B. Tonkens House*. A Cincinnati Usonian Automatic residence. [1]
- **1952:** *Rayward–Shepherd House (Tirranna)*. A New Canaan residence whose name refers to its setting by water; later expansion and associated landscape form part of its history. [1]
- **1953:** *Seth Peterson Cottage*. A small Wisconsin retreat left unfinished at Wright’s death and completed by others; its posthumous realization preserves a project but is not wholly an object made under Wright’s supervision. [1]
- **1954:** *Kentuck Knob (I. N. and Bernardine Hagan House)*. A Pennsylvania Usonian residence organized on a hexagonal module. [1]
- **1954:** *Bachman–Wilson House*. A New Jersey Usonian house later dismantled and reconstructed in Arkansas; its material continuity coexists with the loss of its original site. [1]
- **1954:** *Dr. Richard Davis House*. A Marion, Indiana, Usonian residence also known as Woodside. [1]
- **1955:** *Kraus House*. A Kirkwood, Missouri, Usonian residence now preserved as the Frank Lloyd Wright House in Ebsworth Park. [1]
- **1955:** *Duncan House*. A prefabrication-related residence originally built in Illinois and later reconstructed in Pennsylvania. [1]
- **1956:** *Price House*. A large Bartlesville residence for Harold C. Price Jr. and Carolyn Price, with integrated interiors and furnishings. [1]
- **1956:** *Marin County Civic Center*. A governmental complex begun from Wright’s project and completed after his death by associated architects; the realized campus exceeds what Wright personally lived to supervise. [1][2]
- **1957:** *Duey and Julia Wright House*. A Wausau residence completed near the end of Wright’s life. [1]
- **1957:** *Annunciation Greek Orthodox Church*. A domed church near Milwaukee, completed after Wright’s death with participation by his successor office. [1]
- **1958:** *Lockridge Medical Clinic*. A Whitefish, Montana, clinic completed shortly before Wright’s death and later demolished despite preservation efforts. [1]
- **1958:** *Pilgrim Congregational Church*. A Redding, California, church project of which only a portion was constructed; the built fragment should not be mistaken for the complete intention. [1][2]
- **1959:** *Norman Lykes House*. Wright’s final recorded residential design, completed posthumously under John Rattenbury; authorship of the realized details is consequently shared and temporally layered. [1]

### Public, commercial, religious, and cultural obra

- **1886:** *Unity Chapel*. Wright assisted Joseph Lyman Silsbee on this Spring Green chapel; Storrer assigns it S.000 but identifies Silsbee as the architect, so it resists inclusion as an autonomous Wright work. [1]
- **1893:** *Chicago World’s Columbian Exposition projects*. Wright produced unrealized competition or study designs associated with the exposition; these are projects embodied in drawings, not constructed buildings. [2]
- **1894:** *Francisco Terrace Apartments*. A Chicago apartment building later demolished. [1]
- **1895:** *Waller Apartments*. A Chicago housing commission for Edward C. Waller. [1]
- **1895:** *Waller Gates*. Entrance structures at River Forest; small in scale but separately catalogued architectural objects. [1]
- **1897:** *Luxfer Prism designs*. Wright devised ornamental patterns for pressed-glass pavement and window prisms; only one design is known to have entered production, making the group both industrial design and unrealized serial project. [2][3]
- **1902:** *Larkin Company Administration Building*. A Buffalo office building integrating architecture, furniture, ventilation, inscriptions, and work organization; demolished in 1950, it survives through fragments and records. [1][2]
- **1905:** *Unity Temple*. An Oak Park Unitarian church complex in reinforced concrete, joining worship space and secular Unity House in one composition. [1][3][8]
- **1908:** *Browne’s Bookstore interior*. A Chicago commercial interior; as with many interiors, demolition makes its documentary afterlife more substantial than its surviving material one. [1][2]
- **1912:** *Coonley Playhouse*. A small school building with celebrated abstract art-glass windows, belonging to the wider Coonley commission yet catalogued independently. [1][3]
- **1913:** *City Club of Chicago competition project*. An unrealized civic project existing through drawings and proposal materials. [2]
- **1914:** *Mamah Borthwick memorial projects*. Unbuilt commemorative designs whose status is intimate project and memorial intention rather than completed obra. [2]
- **1915:** *Aichi Exposition project*. An unrealized Japanese exposition design preserved in Wright’s project archive. [2]
- **1917:** *Jiyu Gakuen Myonichikan*. A Tokyo school designed with Arata Endo’s important participation; attribution should acknowledge the collaborative office and local execution. [1]
- **1921:** *Nakashima Imperial Hotel Annex project*. An unbuilt hospitality project associated with Wright’s Japanese work. [2]
- **1923:** *Lake Tahoe Summer Colony*. An unrealized resort project of cabins, terraces, bridges, and floating or shoreline structures; its elaborate drawings are the principal manifestation. [2]
- **1924:** *National Life Insurance Building*. An unrealized Chicago skyscraper project developed for Albert Johnson. [2]
- **1927:** *San Marcos-in-the-Desert*. An unrealized Arizona resort project whose planning and construction ideas fed later work at Taliesin West. [2]
- **1928:** *Arizona Biltmore consulting designs*. Wright’s precise authorship remains disputed: Albert Chase McArthur was architect of record, while Wright acted as consultant and later asserted a larger role. [2]
- **1928:** *St. Mark’s-in-the-Bouwerie Towers*. An unrealized New York apartment-tower project whose structural and planning ideas reappeared in later towers. [2]
- **1930:** *Richardson Development project*. An unbuilt planning and housing proposal, extant as architectural intention. [2]
- **1931:** *Broadacre City*. A decentralized model of settlement developed through texts, drawings, plans, and a large collaborative model; it is simultaneously manifesto, planning project, and collective manifestation rather than a buildable single building. [2][4]
- **1932:** *Taliesin Fellowship*. An educational, domestic, and labor institution founded by Frank and Olgivanna Lloyd Wright; it is a social project and protocol for architectural life, not solely Wright’s individual “work.” [2][4]
- **1935:** *S. C. Johnson Administration Building*. A Racine workplace and Gesamtkunstwerk integrating structure, light, furniture, graphics, and organizational space. [1][2]
- **1937:** *Johnson Wax Research Tower*. A cantilevered laboratory tower added to the Johnson complex and completed in 1950. [1]
- **1938:** *Monona Terrace Community and Convention Center*. A Madison civic project repeatedly revised and rejected during Wright’s lifetime; the building opened in 1997 after substantial posthumous adaptation, so the realized object is not simply a Wright building. [2]
- **1938:** *Suntop Homes project*. A housing protocol based on four dwellings grouped around a central core, only partly realized. [1][2]
- **1939:** *Florida Southern College campus*. A long-running campus plan and group of buildings, many constructed with student labor; individual buildings and the uncompleted master plan form a distributed architectural obra. [1][2]
- **1942:** *Cooperative Homesteads project*. An unrealized cooperative settlement proposal related to Wright’s Broadacre thinking. [2]
- **1943:** *Beth Sholom Synagogue project*. The congregation’s project developed into the built Elkins Park synagogue of 1954–1959. [2]
- **1945:** *Usonia Cooperative*. Wright supplied the site plan for a cooperative community at Pleasantville, while individual houses were designed by Wright and other architects; it is collaborative planning rather than a wholly Wright-made settlement. [2]
- **1946:** *Rogers Lacy Hotel project*. A tall Dallas hotel project that remained unbuilt. [2]
- **1946:** *Huntington Hartford Sports Club and Play Resort*. An unrealized Los Angeles leisure complex represented by drawings and models. [2]
- **1947:** *Calico Mills Administration Building*. An unrealized Ahmedabad project; its drawings constitute the work’s surviving manifestation. [2]
- **1947:** *V. C. Morris House project*. An unbuilt residential design for the San Francisco shop’s owner. [2]
- **1948:** *First Unitarian Society Meeting House*. A Madison religious building whose congregation assisted construction; later additions are not all Wright’s work. [1]
- **1949:** *Rogers Lacy House project*. An unrealized Dallas residence project. [2]
- **1950:** *Price Tower*. A Bartlesville office-and-apartment tower adapted from Wright’s earlier cantilevered tower studies. [1]
- **1951:** *Anderton Court Shops*. A small Beverly Hills commercial complex organized by a rising central ramp. [1]
- **1952:** *Beth Sholom Synagogue*. A pyramidal, translucent sanctuary near Philadelphia, completed in 1959 and conceived as architecture, symbolic form, lighting apparatus, and liturgical environment. [1][8]
- **1952:** *Doheny Ranch development*. A large unrealized California planning project. [2]
- **1953:** *Nakoma Country Club*. A Wisconsin clubhouse project originating in 1923 and built only in altered posthumous form in 2001; the contemporary building is a realization after Wright, not an untouched lifetime obra. [2]
- **1954:** *Kalita Humphreys Theater*. A Dallas theatre using a rotating stage concept and completed in 1959. [1]
- **1955:** *Public Service Building project*. An unrealized urban office project. [2]
- **1956:** *Mile-High Illinois*. A visionary mile-high Chicago tower project; it is manifesto, publicity image, model, and engineering speculation rather than a construction proposal brought to execution. [2][4]
- **1956:** *Greek Orthodox Church project*. The project became Annunciation Greek Orthodox Church, completed after Wright’s death. [1][2]
- **1956:** *Marin County Civic Center project*. A civic master plan whose principal buildings and landscape were realized posthumously and modified by Wright’s successor office. [1][2]
- **1957:** *Oklahoma State Capitol project*. An unrealized proposal for additions to the state capitol. [2]
- **1957:** *Baghdad cultural plan*. A group of projects—including an opera house, university, museums, gardens, and monuments—commissioned for Baghdad but abandoned after the 1958 revolution; it is a projected cultural landscape, not one building. [2]
- **1958:** *Masieri Memorial*. A proposed Venice student residence and memorial; planning controversy prevented construction, so drawings and model remain its manifestation. [2]
- **1958:** *Arizona State Capitol project*. An unrealized capitol complex commonly called the “Oasis,” surviving as drawings and model. [2]
- **1959:** *Grady Gammage Memorial Auditorium*. An Arizona auditorium designed by Wright and completed posthumously by Taliesin Associated Architects; the realized work embodies his project through successor-office execution. [1][2]

### Designed interiors, furniture, glass, ornament, and useful objects

- **1895–1959:** *Architectural art-glass designs*. Hundreds of window, door, skylight, lamp, and screen patterns were made for particular commissions; they usually resist treatment as autonomous pictures because Wright conceived them as parts of architectural Gesamtkunstwerke. [2][3]
- **1897:** *Luxfer Prism pattern*. A patented ornamental design for industrially produced glass prisms, functioning as both graphic motif and component of a daylighting system. [2][3]
- **1898–1909:** *Oak Park furniture ensembles*. Tables, chairs, settles, cabinets, desks, beds, and lighting were designed for individual Prairie interiors; catalogue boundaries vary because a “design” may survive in several fabricated examples. [2][3]
- **1902–1904:** *Dana–Thomas House furnishings*. A coordinated ensemble of furniture, lamps, art glass, sculpture settings, and ornament inseparable in conception from the house. [3]
- **1903–1905:** *Darwin D. Martin House furnishings and “Tree of Life” glazing*. An extensive decorative program made with studio employees and specialist fabricators; individual execution should not be confused with sole physical manufacture by Wright. [3]
- **1904:** *Larkin Building furniture system*. Desks, chairs, filing units, light fittings, and fittings designed as components of a rationalized workplace. [2][3]
- **1905–1908:** *Unity Temple furnishings and fittings*. Seating, lights, art glass, and interior surfaces form part of the building’s liturgical Gesamtkunstwerk. [3]
- **1907–1912:** *Coonley House and Playhouse art glass*. A group of abstract glazing designs, including the “Kindersymphony” windows; dispersed panels are fragments of larger architectural ensembles. [3]
- **1908–1910:** *Robie House furniture*. Dining furniture, seating, tables, lighting, and fitted elements conceived with the house; surviving movable pieces now circulate as objects although their design was site-specific. [3]
- **1913–1914:** *Midway Gardens ornament and furnishings*. Furniture, murals, lighting, sculptural sprites, tableware, and graphic details formed one entertainment environment, much of it destroyed or dispersed. [2][3]
- **1915–1922:** *Hollyhock House furniture and ornament*. Furniture, carpets, fireplaces, doors, and decorative motifs repeat the house’s abstract hollyhock geometry. [3]
- **1916–1922:** *Imperial Hotel furniture, carpets, silver, china, and fittings*. A large hospitality-design ensemble produced through collaboration with craftspeople and manufacturers; demolition separated these things from their intended total environment. [2][3]
- **1921–1924:** *Textile-block patterns*. Modular relief blocks designed as structure and ornament for the Millard, Storer, Freeman, and Ennis houses; these are architectural components and repeatable protocols as much as independent objects. [2][3]
- **1936–1939:** *Johnson Wax furniture*. Desks, tables, storage, and the celebrated three-legged office chair were designed for the Administration Building; practical instability complicates the chair’s status as a successful useful object. [2][3]
- **1937–1959:** *Taliesin West furniture and fittings*. A changing body of experimental, often site-made objects; workshop production and repeated alteration make individual authorship and final dating difficult. [2][3]
- **1949:** *Taliesin Ensemble furniture*. A manufactured line developed with Henredon and introduced in the 1950s, translating Wright’s architectural vocabulary into serial domestic furniture. [3]
- **1955:** *Taliesin Line decorative textiles and wallpapers*. Machine-produced patterns for F. Schumacher & Co.; unlike site-specific Prairie furnishings, these were intended for broad commercial use. [3]
- **1955:** *Taliesin Line rugs*. Geometric floor coverings designed for commercial production as part of Wright’s late effort to make a coordinated domestic environment available beyond individual commissions. [3]
- **1955:** *Taliesin Line paints*. A coordinated architectural color system; it is a protocol and commercial design apparatus rather than a singular object. [3]
- **1955:** *Tableware for Heritage-Henredon*. Dining objects conceived within Wright’s commercially manufactured domestic-design program. [3]
- **1956:** *Usonian Automatic concrete blocks*. A modular construction system intended to let clients assemble houses from standardized units; it is simultaneously product design, building protocol, and architectural project. [2]

### Printed architectural portfolios and artist’s books

- **1896–1897:** *The House Beautiful*. An essay by William C. Gannett designed and hand-printed by Wright and William Herman Winslow at Wright’s Auvergne Press; Wright made the book’s typography, layout, ornament, and physical form rather than its principal verbal text. [4][5]
- **1910:** *Ausgeführte Bauten und Entwürfe von Frank Lloyd Wright*. The two-volume Wasmuth Portfolio of lithographic plates presenting Wright’s architecture; it is a self-curated graphic obra and international manifesto, not merely documentation of buildings. [2][4]
- **1911:** *Ausgeführte Bauten von Frank Lloyd Wright*. The smaller Wasmuth publication combining photographs and drawings of Wright’s work. [4]
- **1917:** *Japanese Prints: An Interpretation*. A book-length critical and connoisseurial text on Japanese woodblock prints, also reflecting Wright’s activities as collector and dealer. [4][5]
- **1925:** *Wendingen: The Life-Work of the American Architect Frank Lloyd Wright*. A special issue and portfolio-shaped presentation assembled around Wright’s work; it is partly Wright’s self-presentation and partly a publication made by editors and contributors, so it resists sole-author classification. [4]
- **1930:** *Ausgeführte Bauten*. A Berlin portfolio revisiting Wright’s architectural production through plates and commentary. [4]
- **1932:** *The Disappearing City*. A book-manifesto arguing for decentralized settlement and anticipating Broadacre City. [4][5]
- **1932:** *An Autobiography*. Wright’s constructed account of his life and architectural formation; its factual reliability has often been challenged, so it is also a work of self-making. [4][5]
- **1938:** *Architecture and Modern Life*. A book written with Baker Brownell, joining architectural argument to social criticism; authorship is explicitly collaborative. [4][5]
- **1943:** *An Autobiography*, revised edition. A materially revised manifestation of the autobiography rather than a wholly separate life narrative. [4][5]
- **1945:** *When Democracy Builds*. A revised and expanded manifestation of *The Disappearing City*, presenting Broadacre City as a democratic social and spatial program. [4][5]
- **1946:** *Frank Lloyd Wright: In the Nature of Materials*. Henry-Russell Hitchcock’s book includes Wright material but is not a work authored by Wright; it belongs in the reception bibliography and therefore resists inclusion in Wright’s obra. [5]
- **1949:** *Genius and the Mobocracy*. A book on Louis Sullivan, combining memoir, polemic, architectural history, and Wright’s construction of an artistic lineage. [4][5]
- **1953:** *The Future of Architecture*. A collected book assembling Wright’s Princeton lectures and other major statements; it is both a new publication and a manifestation of earlier texts. [4][5]
- **1954:** *The Natural House*. A book-manifesto explaining the Usonian house as architecture, social proposal, and method. [4][5]
- **1955:** *An American Architecture*. A thematic compilation edited by Edgar Kaufmann Jr. from Wright’s writings; Wright is the originating author, but the book’s selection and organization are editorial creations. [4][5]
- **1956:** *The Story of the Tower*. A book about the conception and realization of the Price Tower, combining project history with architectural argument. [4][5]
- **1957:** *A Testament*. A late manifesto joining autobiography, retrospective portfolio, prophecy, and polemic. [4][5]
- **1958:** *The Living City*. A final expanded manifestation of Wright’s Broadacre City argument, combining text, plans, and images into a planning manifesto. [4][5]

### Essays, lectures, manifestos, and protocols

- **1894:** *The Architect and the Machine*. An early lecture or essay addressing architecture’s relation to industrial production; the exact boundaries among delivered lecture, manuscript, and later printed manifestation require bibliographic care. [4]
- **1901:** *The Art and Craft of the Machine*. A Hull House lecture and published manifesto arguing that machine production could become an artistic instrument. [4]
- **1908:** *In the Cause of Architecture*. An essay-manifesto published in *Architectural Record*, setting out principles associated with Wright’s Prairie work. [4]
- **1910:** *In the Cause of Architecture: The Third Dimension*. An installment extending Wright’s architectural argument through spatial rather than stylistic categories. [4]
- **1914:** *In the Cause of Architecture: The Meaning of Materials—Glass*. An essay treating glass as an active architectural material. [4]
- **1914:** *In the Cause of Architecture: The Meaning of Materials—Wood*. An essay on the architectural character and use of wood. [4]
- **1914:** *In the Cause of Architecture: The Meaning of Materials—The Kiln*. An essay connecting fired materials and architectural form. [4]
- **1927:** *In the Cause of Architecture* series. A renewed group of essays in *Architectural Record* covering composition, materials, the plan, the individual, and related themes; whether counted as one serial manifesto or several works depends on bibliographic granularity. [4]
- **1930:** *Modern Architecture: Being the Kahn Lectures for 1930*. A sequence of Princeton lectures later issued as a book; it is both oral event and textual manifestation. [4][5]
- **1931:** *The Tyranny of the Skyscraper*. A polemical essay or lecture criticizing conventional urban towers while distinguishing them from Wright’s own tower projects. [4]
- **1932:** *The Hillside Home School of the Allied Arts prospectus*. A pedagogical and institutional protocol associated with formation of the Taliesin Fellowship. [4]
- **1937:** *Architecture and Peace*. A lecture-text treating organic architecture as a social and ethical program. [4]
- **1939:** *An Organic Architecture: The Architecture of Democracy*. Wright’s London lectures, functioning as both architectural theory and manifesto. [4][5]
- **1940:** *The American Institute of Architects address*. A public polemic delivered on receiving the AIA Gold Medal; its identity lies partly in the performed event and partly in published transcripts. [4]
- **1943:** *Building the New House*. A text presenting the house as the nucleus of social and architectural reconstruction. [4]
- **1947:** *To the Young Man in Architecture*. An advisory text functioning partly as professional protocol and partly as autobiographical manifesto. [4]
- **1952:** *The Destruction of the Box*. A formulation repeatedly used in Wright’s lectures and publications rather than one wholly stable autonomous text; its resistance to fixed-work status is bibliographically significant. [4]
- **1957:** *Sixty Years of Living Architecture*. A retrospective exhibition project and accompanying textual-graphic apparatus; it is a traveling manifestation of an oeuvre rather than a single conventional book. [2][4]

### Drawings, models, photographs, and the architectural archive

- **c. 1886–1959:** *Architectural drawings and presentation renderings*. Tens of thousands of plans, elevations, perspectives, details, and studies survive in the Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation Archives; many are collaborative studio documents, so the archive cannot honestly be redescribed as thousands of individually autographic Wright works. [2][6]
- **c. 1886–1959:** *Architectural models*. Models for built and unbuilt projects served as design instruments, exhibition objects, and persuasive manifestations of intentions; apprentices frequently participated in their fabrication. [2][6]
- **1934–1935:** *Broadacre City model*. A large model built collectively by Taliesin Fellowship apprentices under Wright’s direction; it manifests Wright’s planning project but is not an object made by his hands alone. [2][6]
- **1940–1959:** *Exhibition drawings for “Sixty Years of Living Architecture”*. A changing body of redrawn, recolored, and newly prepared panels; these mediate earlier projects and therefore occupy an unstable position between documentation and new graphic obra. [2][4]
- **Throughout his career:** *Japanese-print arrangements, displays, and connoisseurial installations*. Wright selected and arranged prints as dealer, collector, and exhibition designer; these were curatorial acts and manifestations, not works whose pictured images he made. [3][4]

### Posthumous realizations and works that resist attribution to Wright alone

- **1959–1962:** *Grady Gammage Memorial Auditorium*. Built from Wright’s project after his death by Taliesin Associated Architects; the design is Wright’s, while the completed building is a posthumous collaborative realization. [1][2]
- **1959–1961:** *Annunciation Greek Orthodox Church*. Completed after Wright’s death under William Wesley Peters and the successor office; details of the built object therefore cannot all be assigned to Wright without qualification. [1]
- **1959–1968:** *Norman Lykes House*. Completed and interpreted by John Rattenbury after Wright’s death, then substantially altered; it is a realization of Wright’s final house project rather than an entirely lifetime-supervised work. [1]
- **1957–1966:** *Marin County Civic Center Administration and Hall of Justice buildings*. The scheme originates with Wright, but construction and technical development continued under Aaron Green and Taliesin Associated Architects. [1][2]
- **1938–1997:** *Monona Terrace Community and Convention Center*. The completed Madison building draws from Wright’s successive schemes but was adapted to later requirements by Taliesin Associated Architects; calling it simply “a Frank Lloyd Wright building” suppresses that discontinuity. [2]
- **1923–2001:** *Nakoma Resort clubhouse*. A posthumous construction derived from Wright’s 1923 project and altered for a new site and program; it is a manifestation after Wright, not the lost original intention made material without change. [2]
- **1928–1971:** *King Kamehameha Golf Course Clubhouse*. Built from an altered version of Wright’s unexecuted Arthur Miller house design; reuse, new program, and successor-office development make attribution inherently hybrid. [2]
- **Various dates:** *Taliesin Associated Architects buildings derived from Wright designs*. These should not be silently absorbed into Wright’s oeuvre: archival descent or stylistic fidelity does not make every successor-office building a thing Wright made. [2][6]
