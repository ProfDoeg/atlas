# Giuseppe Garibaldi: Research Dossier

**Scope note.** This dossier reconstructs Garibaldi's life chronologically and separates documented events from recollection, contemporary accusation, later legend, and unresolved questions. Dates follow the Gregorian calendar. Titles and ranks are given as used by the governments or movements concerned; they did not always carry equivalent legal standing across jurisdictions. Garibaldi's own *Memorie* are indispensable but retrospective, repeatedly revised, mediated by editors and translators, and sometimes unreliable on chronology or motive.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and siblings

Garibaldi was the third of six children of **Domenico Garibaldi** (1766–1841), a coastal trader and merchant captain, and **Rosa Raimondi** (1776–1852), married at Nice in 1794. Both families were of Ligurian origin: Domenico's line had moved from Chiavari and Rosa's from Loano. Later genealogy found a remote link between the paternal Garibaldi and Mazzini families, but it had no demonstrated formative role.

The six children were Angelo, Maria Elisabetta, Giuseppe, Michele, Felice, and Teresa; Maria Elisabetta and Teresa died young in accounts derived from family records. Angelo became a merchant and served abroad; Michele was a sea captain; Felice was implicated in the surveillance following the 1834 conspiracy. Exact sibling sequences vary in popular biographies because some count only surviving children.

Rosa was conventionally Catholic and remembered by Giuseppe as the family's moral center. Domenico intended a respectable professional career for his son, then accepted his maritime vocation. Garibaldi's later anticlericalism coexisted with affectionate religious language about his mother and frequent use of providential rhetoric.

### Spouses, partners, and children

1. **Ana Maria de Jesus Ribeiro da Silva (“Anita,” 1821–1849).** Garibaldi met her at Laguna, Santa Catarina, in 1839 during the Farroupilha/Juliana campaign. She was legally married to Manuel Duarte de Aguiar. Sources disagree about whether Duarte had abandoned her, was away in imperial service, or remained a legal obstacle; no sound evidence establishes the romantic legend that Garibaldi simply saw her through a telescope and immediately declared “You must be mine.” Anita joined his mobile military life and sometimes bore arms or endured combat. They married at Montevideo on **26 March 1842**, after representing that Duarte was dead; the precise legal status of the first marriage remains a recurrent biographical question. Their children were:
   - **Domenico Menotti** (usually Menotti), born in Rio Grande do Sul in September 1840; the exact day is disputed (16 or 22 September in different records); later soldier, deputy, and organizer.
   - **Rosa** (1843–1845), born and died at Montevideo.
   - **Teresa** (1845–1903), later wife of Stefano Canzio.
   - **Ricciotti** (1847–1924), soldier and political activist.
   Anita died near Ravenna on **4 August 1849**, pregnant and exhausted during the retreat from the fallen Roman Republic. Later rumors of strangulation, poisoning, or deliberate abandonment lack persuasive documentary support; malaria or another febrile disease compounded by pregnancy and flight is the strongest reconstruction. Her body was hastily buried and later reinterred several times.

2. **Battistina Ravello**, a domestic worker at Caprera, had a relationship with Garibaldi. Their daughter **Anna Maria “Anita” Garibaldi** was born in 1859 and died in 1875. Garibaldi disclosed the child's existence before his second wedding. The relationship illustrates the power imbalance of employer and servant, but surviving evidence does not establish coercion.

3. **Giuseppina Raimondi** (1841–1918), daughter acknowledged by Marquis Giorgio Raimondi. Garibaldi married her at Fino Mornasco on **24 January 1860**. On the wedding day he received information—reportedly by anonymous note and then confirmation—that she had maintained a relationship with the Garibaldian Luigi Caroli and might be pregnant by him. Garibaldi left immediately. The marriage remained legally valid until annulled on **14 January 1880** after protracted proceedings. The lurid wording of many retellings comes from hostile memoir and litigation-era narratives; the separation itself, the Caroli relationship, and the eventual annulment are documented.

4. **Maria Esperance von Schwartz** (pseudonym Elpis Melena), German-speaking writer and traveler. Their close relationship began after her 1857 visit to Caprera. Garibaldi proposed marriage in 1858; she declined, but edited/transmitted versions of his memoirs and remained a correspondent. Whether the relationship was physically sexual cannot be established from published evidence.

5. **Francesca Armosino** (1848–1923), a Piedmontese woman who came to Caprera as caregiver in the extended family. She became Garibaldi's companion and caretaker and married him on **26 January 1880**, after the Raimondi annulment. Their children were **Rosa** (1869–1871), **Clelia** (1867–1959), and **Manlio** (1873–1900). Birth order and the fact that Clelia and Rosa were born before the legal marriage were openly regularized rather than hidden.

Claims of other illegitimate children circulate in local genealogies. None should be accepted without civil, baptismal, correspondence, or estate evidence.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1807–1823

Nice was a multilingual Mediterranean port whose sovereignty shifted during Garibaldi's childhood. He received uneven private instruction from three tutors, two clerics. Accounts name Father Giovanni Giacone among them, but the record is thin. He acquired elementary mathematics, Italian, French, geography, and a strong interest in ancient and Roman history rather than a university education.

His memoirs place dramatic rescues and acts of compassion in childhood. These stories belong to his self-presentation and family tradition; most cannot be independently verified. More securely, he resisted his father's preferred professional path, briefly went to Genoa for further study, and chose the sea.

On **12 November 1821** he was entered in the register of sailors. In January 1824 he sailed aboard the *Costanza*, commanded by Angelo Pesante, for Odessa. Pesante remained an important early professional patron and friend.

## Education and Formation

Garibaldi had no academy or university education. His formation was practical and mobile:

- merchant navigation in the Mediterranean and Black Sea;
- exposure to piracy and maritime violence;
- a 1825 visit to Rome, which fixed Rome as both historical model and future national capital in his imagination;
- an unexplained long stay at Constantinople, roughly 1828–31, sometimes embellished with stories of teaching or illness that cannot be fully documented;
- contact in 1833 with followers of **Saint-Simon**, especially Émile Barrault, whose universalist and associative language affected him;
- an encounter at Taganrog with a Young Italy advocate, traditionally identified as **Giovanni Battista Cuneo**, though that identification is not beyond doubt;
- the political and organizational example of **Giuseppe Mazzini**.

His ideology remained programmatic rather than systematic: national independence, popular sovereignty, civic mobilization, anticlericalism, religious freedom, abolitionism, international fraternity, and later a non-Marxian socialism. He was capable of subordinating republican constitutional preference to monarchy when he believed Victor Emmanuel II could unite Italy.

## Early Maritime Career and Conspiracy, 1824–1834

After the Odessa voyage Garibaldi worked aboard the family vessel *Santa Reparata* and ships of other captains. He sailed to the Levant, the Black Sea, and once into the Atlantic. By 1832 he held a merchant-captain's certificate.

In August 1833 he returned from the eastern voyage politically committed to national revolution and joined Young Italy. Late that year he entered the Royal Sardinian Navy under the conspiratorial name Cleombroto. Mazzini planned to combine an incursion into Savoy with an uprising at Genoa. The February 1834 enterprise collapsed before a genuine Genoese revolt began. Garibaldi deserted or absented himself, hid, and escaped to Marseille on 9 February. A military tribunal condemned him **in absentia to an “ignominious” death** in June 1834 for conspiracy against the state. This was a formal capital sentence, not folklore; it later lapsed or became politically unenforceable.

## Chronological Life History

### First exile and Brazil, 1834–1841

From Marseille Garibaldi moved through a network of Italian political exiles. He sailed for Rio de Janeiro in 1835–36; the exact departure and arrival sequence differs between memoir editions. With Cuneo and other expatriates he entered republican circles and worked as sailor, trader, and eventually privateer.

The rebel **Riograndense Republic**, fighting the Brazilian Empire in the Farroupilha or Ragamuffin War, issued Garibaldi letters of marque. He commanded small craft including the *Mazzini*, seized enemy shipping, was wounded and captured, and was imprisoned and reportedly tortured at Gualeguay in Argentina after entering regional waters. Because imperial Brazil did not recognize the rebel state, it called his operations piracy. Legally, their classification turned on recognition of belligerency: he possessed rebel commissions but not authorization from a universally recognized sovereign. “Pirate” was therefore both an adversary's legal-political claim and propaganda, not a neutral judicial finding.

In 1838–39 he helped create a rebel naval force by transporting boats overland from Lagoa dos Patos to the Atlantic. The expedition against Laguna helped establish the short-lived **Juliana Republic** in Santa Catarina. There he met Anita. Imperial counterattack destroyed most of his flotilla and forced a difficult land retreat. He fought at Santa Vitória and suffered defeat at Curitibanos in January 1840.

Garibaldi's own narratives emphasize freeing enslaved men who entered his service. His later letters are unmistakably abolitionist. A separate allegation that he transported Chinese “coolies” as slaves on a later Pacific voyage has no firm cargo or port documentation and conflicts with his expressed position; it remains an accusation, not an established fact.

The South American forces in which he served used requisition, capture of shipping, and irregular violence. The documentary record supports armed seizures and deaths in battle; it does **not** support attributing to Garibaldi a personally ordered program of massacre. Romantic accounts also sanitize the factional, civil-war character of the conflicts and the material dependence of irregular forces on captured cargo.

### Uruguay and the Italian Legion, 1841–1848

Garibaldi and Anita moved to Montevideo in 1841. They married in 1842. He briefly worked in commerce and teaching before serving the Colorado government of **Fructuoso Rivera** in Uruguay's Guerra Grande against Manuel Oribe, whose forces were supported by Argentine ruler Juan Manuel de Rosas.

He commanded a small Uruguayan squadron. At the Paraná/Costa Brava action of August 1842, Admiral William Brown defeated his flotilla; Garibaldi burned vessels to prevent capture and withdrew. From 1843 he organized the **Italian Legion**, eventually several hundred men, for the defense of besieged Montevideo. The legion used red shirts, probably inexpensive garments obtained from stock intended for slaughterhouse workers; the exact procurement story has been embellished but the South American origin is secure.

At **San Antonio del Salto, 8 February 1846**, the legion resisted a larger force. The engagement's strategic scale was modest, but reports and diplomatic networks converted it into an international reputation. A subscription in Italy funded a sword of honor. Uruguay appointed him commander-in-chief of its forces on 22 June 1847. He declined grants of land or material reward offered to him and his legionaries, strengthening his reputation for personal disinterest.

The charge of piracy reappeared because Garibaldi's naval sorties seized enemy boats and supplies. Montevideo records show that he reported to a recognized government and corresponded in military form; this weakens the claim that he operated as a private robber. It does not erase the coercion inherent in blockade warfare and prize-taking.

In 1847 Garibaldi and Francesco Anzani offered the legion's services to Pope Pius IX, then still seen by many liberals as a possible national reformer. The offer was not accepted. Anita and the children sailed first; Garibaldi followed on 15 April 1848 aboard the *Bifronte*, renamed *Speranza*, purchased by subscription from the Italian community. He landed at Nice on 21 June 1848.

### Revolutions of 1848–49 and the Roman Republic

Garibaldi offered his services to the Sardinian king during the First War of Independence, despite his republican past. Rebuffed or given no effective regular command, he led volunteers in Lombardy, fought at Luino and Morazzone, and retreated into Switzerland after the Piedmontese defeat.

In late 1848 he went to central Italy. The Roman Republic was proclaimed on 9 February 1849 after Pius IX fled. Garibaldi became a deputy in its assembly and a brigadier/general commanding volunteers. The governing triumvirate—Mazzini, Aurelio Saffi, and Carlo Armellini—had to reconcile revolutionary legitimacy, military necessity, and foreign intervention.

Garibaldi defeated Neapolitan forces near Palestrina and Velletri and was central to Rome's defense against the French expedition of General Oudinot. On 30 April his forces repelled the first French assault. He wanted a more aggressive pursuit; Mazzini and political leaders feared diplomatic consequences. French reinforcements eventually overwhelmed the republic after severe fighting around the Janiculum. Rome capitulated on 3 July.

Military critics, notably **Carlo Pisacane**, later argued that Garibaldi's personal bravery exceeded his strategic discipline and that the press made him a general. Other military historians credited his adaptive tactics. The conflict is interpretive, not reducible to a simple verdict.

Garibaldi left Rome with thousands of volunteers, announcing an attempt to reach Venice. Pursued by Austrian, French, Spanish, Papal, and Neapolitan forces, the column disintegrated. The authorities of San Marino negotiated safe passage, but Garibaldi continued with a small group. Anita died at Mandriole near Ravenna on 4 August. Garibaldi escaped via the Adriatic and Tuscan networks.

### Second exile: Mediterranean, New York, Pacific, and return, 1849–1854

Prevented from settling securely in Piedmont, he moved among La Maddalena, Tunis, Tangier, Gibraltar, Liverpool, and the United States. His letters refer to a “cursed melancholy” after defeat and Anita's death; this documents depression-like distress but not a retrospective clinical diagnosis.

He arrived in New York on **30 July 1850**. On Staten Island he worked with inventor **Antonio Meucci**, principally in a candle factory, and attempted other commercial work. Later nationalist memory sometimes turns this into a technical partnership in the invention of the telephone; evidence establishes association and employment, not Garibaldi's co-invention or a patent contribution.

Unable immediately to obtain a US command at sea, he traveled via Central America to Callao, Peru, arriving in October 1851. He commanded the *Carmen*, owned by the Nice shipowner Pietro Denegri, in the guano trade and sailed to Canton in 1852. He then commanded the *Commonwealth* and made voyages touching Australia, the Pacific, South America, Boston, and New York. Port sequences differ among biographies and ship records; the larger circuit is secure.

In 1853 he returned across the Atlantic and met Alexander Herzen in London. By letter to Mazzini on 26 February 1854 he made explicit his tactical conversion: Italian democrats should rally to the Piedmontese monarchy for unification. He returned to Nice in May 1854.

### Caprera and the Piedmontese alliance, 1854–1858

Using an inheritance and earnings, Garibaldi bought part of **Caprera**, an island in the Maddalena archipelago, and gradually enlarged the holding. He designed or expanded the simple Casa Bianca, farmed, raised livestock, planted trees, and used modern or imported equipment where affordable. Friends sent gifts, including a prefabricated iron-clad wooden structure used as a library. Caprera was a working farm, political headquarters, household, and stage on which simplicity and independence were visibly performed.

He joined the **Italian National Society** on 5 July 1856 through Daniele Manin and Giorgio Pallavicino, met Cavour on 13 August, and became a public bridge between revolutionary volunteers and the Savoy state. Republicans accused him of betrayal; Garibaldi presented the monarchy as an instrument, not an abandonment of popular sovereignty.

### Second War of Independence, 1859

The Piedmontese government made Garibaldi a major general and commander of the volunteer **Cacciatori delle Alpi**. He fought Austrian forces at Varese, San Fermo, and in the Alpine-lake region. The campaign showed he could command a substantial corps within a regular strategic framework, though tension with professional officers continued.

The Villafranca armistice halted the war before Venice was gained. Garibaldi later accepted command connected to the Central Italian League but resigned when General Manfredo Fanti blocked an advance into the Papal States. His relationship with Cavour deteriorated sharply.

### Nice, marriage crisis, and the Expedition of the Thousand, 1860

The Treaty of Turin ceded Savoy and Nice to France in exchange for Napoleon III's assistance. Elected deputy for Nice, Garibaldi attacked the transfer and questioned whether the plebiscite was free under French military and administrative pressure. Modern historians generally treat the plebiscite as managed rather than a modern neutral referendum; claims that every result was simply fabricated exceed the surviving evidence.

Garibaldi promoted a “Million Rifles” subscription and the Association of the Armed Nation. Francesco Crispi, Rosolino Pilo, Agostino Bertani, Nino Bixio, and others prepared intervention in Sicily after revolt began there. Cavour publicly disapproved and covertly monitored or partially enabled the operation; Victor Emmanuel II maintained deniable communication. The expedition was neither simply a spontaneous popular rising nor a formally ordered royal invasion.

On the night of **5–6 May 1860**, slightly more than one thousand volunteers sailed from Quarto on the commandeered steamers *Piemonte* and *Lombardo*. They landed at Marsala on 11 May, assisted indirectly by the presence of British warships, which constrained but did not fire on Bourbon forces. The legend that Britain militarily delivered Sicily to Garibaldi is unsupported; British diplomacy, commerce, public sympathy, intelligence, and fundraising did create a favorable environment.

Garibaldi assumed dictatorship of Sicily in Victor Emmanuel's name at Salemi on 14 May. Victories at Calatafimi (15 May), Palermo (27–30 May), and Milazzo (20 July), combined with Bourbon failures, Sicilian insurgency, reinforcements, and political collapse, brought the island under his control. The number “Thousand” is emblematic; the force expanded to tens of thousands.

#### Land, order, and Bronte

Garibaldian decrees offered benefits and encouraged hopes of redistribution, but did not carry out a general agrarian revolution. Rural revolt blended anti-Bourbon, anti-landlord, communal-right, and class grievances. At **Bronte** in August 1860, violence killed local notables and officials. Nino Bixio, sent by Garibaldi's government, imposed martial law; a hurried tribunal condemned five men, including the lawyer Nicolò Lombardo, who were shot on 10 August. Responsibility is layered:

- the killings in Bronte were documented crimes by insurgents;
- Bixio personally directed the repression and executions;
- Garibaldi's dictatorship authorized restoration of order and did not reverse the sentences;
- claims that the repression was ordered primarily to protect British ownership of the Nelson estate are plausible as part of the local property context but not proved as the single determining cause;
- later hagiography minimized the episode, while anti-Risorgimento narratives sometimes inflate casualty figures or attribute every act directly to Garibaldi without command-level evidence.

Crossing the Strait in August, Garibaldi entered Naples on **7 September** before the main Bourbon army was destroyed. His forces prevailed at the Volturno, 1–2 October. Piedmontese troops invaded the Papal States and moved south, preventing a march on Rome and containing democratic possibilities. Plebiscites approved annexation. Garibaldi met Victor Emmanuel II near Teano on 26 October; the exact spot, wording, and whether he first hailed him “King of Italy” are disputed and heavily ritualized. Garibaldi transferred authority and withdrew to Caprera on 9 November, refusing most rewards and titles.

He did not abolish all taxes, solve land tenure, or control the post-annexation state. Conversely, portraying the conquest as achieved by a thousand outsiders alone ignores Sicilian participation, Bourbon disintegration, later volunteer waves, diplomacy, naval factors, and Piedmontese intervention.

### Parliament, Cavour conflict, and Aspromonte, 1861–1863

Garibaldi served repeatedly as deputy, first in the subalpine parliament and then the Italian Chamber, elected in several constituencies. He disliked procedural politics, often resigned, was re-elected, or appeared only for major debates. On **18 April 1861** he attacked Cavour over the dissolution and unequal treatment of his Southern Army, calling him the man who had made him a foreigner in Italy. He wanted volunteer cadres integrated with rank recognized; the state feared an autonomous political army.

In 1861 US diplomats explored recruiting him for the Union. The documentary sequence supports an offer of a **major-general's commission**, not an unconditional offer to replace Lincoln as constitutional commander-in-chief. Garibaldi demanded effective supreme command and authority to make emancipation a war aim. Those conditions were unacceptable and talks failed. His 6 August 1863 letter praised Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation and John Brown. The older claim that there was no approach at all is untenable; the popular version that Lincoln simply offered him command of the entire Union Army is overstated.

In 1862 Garibaldi raised volunteers under the slogan **“Roma o morte”**. Rattazzi's government sent contradictory signals and then stopped him. Italian regulars confronted the volunteers at **Aspromonte on 29 August**. Garibaldi ordered his men not to fire on fellow Italians but an exchange occurred; he was shot in the right foot/ankle and captured. Surgeons disputed whether the bullet remained. After weeks, Professor Ferdinando Zannetti located and extracted it on 23 November using a probe associated with the electromagnetic ideas of Auguste Nélaton. The episode became both medical history and political theater. Garibaldi received amnesty and returned to Caprera.

### London apotheosis, Freemasonry, and the 1866 war

Garibaldi visited Britain in April 1864 and received mass demonstrations, elite receptions, and radical delegations. The government helped shorten the tour, officially on health grounds, amid concern about public order and diplomacy. He reconciled publicly with Mazzini at Herzen's house.

Garibaldi had entered Freemasonry in Montevideo in the 1840s. In 1864 Palermo's Scottish Rite bodies elevated him and he was elected Grand Master of the Grand Orient of Italy/Supreme Council in a period of competing jurisdictions; exact institutional titles vary with Masonic lineage. He resigned from one Palermo supreme command on 8 August 1864 to avoid worsening schism, but remained a powerful Masonic symbol and later honorary leader. Claims of a single, continuous command over all Italian Freemasonry simplify a fragmented institutional history.

In the Third War of Independence (1866), the government appointed him to command roughly 38,000 volunteers in the Trentino sector. Wounded at Monte Suello, he directed operations from a carriage. At Bezzecca on 21 July his forces recovered from near defeat. Ordered to withdraw after the armistice, he replied **“Obbedisco”** (“I obey”) on 9 August. The one-word telegram is documented, although later images turn a reluctant military compliance into an uncomplicated patriotic tableau.

### Mentana and final attempt on Rome, 1867

Garibaldi attended the Geneva Peace Congress in September 1867, then prepared another invasion of the Papal States, expecting a Roman uprising. Italian authorities arrested him at Sinalunga, confined him at Alessandria and Caprera, yet surveillance remained porous. He escaped Caprera and joined volunteers led in part by Menotti.

After taking Monterotondo, the volunteers were defeated at **Mentana on 3 November** by Papal troops and a newly arrived French expedition, whose Chassepot rifles had greater range and rate of fire. Rome did not rise on the expected scale. Garibaldi was detained briefly at La Spezia and returned to Caprera. He blamed Mazzinians harshly; organizational weakness, false expectations, state duplicity, Papal resistance, and French intervention all contributed.

### France, 1870–71

After Napoleon III fell, Garibaldi offered service to the French Republic, despite having fought French troops in 1849 and 1867. He commanded the **Army of the Vosges**, including international volunteers and his sons, against Prussia. Operations around Dijon achieved limited successes; a unit captured the flag of the 61st Pomeranian Regiment, often described as the only Prussian standard lost in the war, though formulations differ according to what counts as a field flag and capture.

Elected to the French National Assembly by multiple departments, Garibaldi went to Bordeaux in February 1871. Monarchist deputies shouted him down and challenged his eligibility; Victor Hugo defended him and resigned in protest. Garibaldi withdrew. The claim that France officially awarded him the Legion of Honour is doubtful; lists of his decorations often reproduce ceremonial or proposed honors without firm acceptance records.

He sympathized with the Paris Communards' republican and social aims but did not command the Commune. Later revolutionary iconography sometimes folds his French service into the Commune itself.

### Last decade, writing, reform campaigns, and material life, 1871–1882

Garibaldi publicly endorsed the **International Working Men's Association** in 1871, but rejected abolition of family, inheritance, all authority, and private property. His socialism was humanitarian, cooperative, anticlerical, and reformist rather than Marxist. Marx and Engels often treated him with contempt as a politically confused celebrity; Bakunin cultivated him as a symbol.

He campaigned for universal suffrage, free compulsory secular primary education, civil liberties, freedom of conscience, abolition of religious corporations, female emancipation in general language, abolition of capital punishment, and international arbitration. His feminism was uneven: he praised women as political actors but retained paternal assumptions common to his era.

He also promoted engineering projects for Rome, especially diversion or control of the Tiber, drainage, and improvements intended to reduce flooding and employ workers. He was an advocate and political sponsor, not the inventor or patent holder of a completed system. Parliamentary and budgetary obstacles prevented most proposals.

#### Finances and property

Garibaldi's public reputation for refusing spoils is well supported, but he was not outside financial networks:

- Caprera was bought and enlarged using inheritance, maritime earnings, subscriptions, and gifts;
- supporters in Britain, Italy, Uruguay, and expatriate communities raised money, weapons, ships, and household aid;
- the Million Rifles Fund was political-military fundraising, not a private company;
- royalties from memoirs and novels became important in old age;
- he used income to assist Menotti and Ricciotti after poor commercial investments;
- the government proposed a “national gift” or pension of 100,000 lire annually. He rejected it in 1874 as an attempted purchase by the Right, then accepted state support after the Left came to power in 1876. This reversal was criticized by opponents and explained by supporters as acceptance from a politically different government amid family debts and disability.

No credible evidence shows corporate embezzlement, money laundering, or secret enrichment. Accounting for semi-clandestine expeditions was inevitably opaque, and donors—especially British sympathizers—sometimes expected geopolitical effects. Opaqueness is not itself proof of personal theft.

In April 1879 he helped found the **Lega della Democrazia**. By then severe arthritis confined him largely to a wheelchair. He married Francesca Armosino in January 1880. In October 1880 he traveled to Genoa and Milan. In March–April 1882 he undertook an exhausting last journey to Naples and Palermo for the sixth centenary of the Sicilian Vespers.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Republicanism and monarchy

Garibaldi entered politics as a Mazzinian republican. From 1854 he accepted the Savoy monarchy as the practical instrument of unity. In 1860 he exercised dictatorial power “in the name of Victor Emmanuel.” He never became a court conservative: he opposed Cavour, the established army, clerical privilege, limited suffrage, and government obstruction. His use of “republic” increasingly meant government legitimated by popular consent, whether headed temporarily by king or president.

### Religion and anticlericalism

He was baptized Catholic and used Christian moral references. He praised Christ while denouncing the Papacy, priestly political power, dogma, and institutional wealth. He described his position as a “religion of truth” grounded in reason. Freemasonry supplied ritual and international fraternity. He supported religious liberty and church-state separation; he was not simply an atheist in every phase, though hostile and friendly contemporaries often labeled him one.

### Nationalism, internationalism, and race

His nationalism was universalist in declared principle: each people should be free and national emancipation should culminate in federation and peace. He backed Poles, Hungarians, Balkan peoples, French republicans, abolition in the United States, and international arbitration. His vocabulary sometimes used nineteenth-century racial and civilizational stereotypes. Evidence of abolitionist action and writing should not be converted into anachronistic proof of fully modern racial egalitarianism.

### Social thought

His later socialism stressed labor, education, cooperation, taxation and relief, and opposition to parasitic privilege. It lacked a theory of class ownership and rejected collectivist or anarchist absolutes. His tactical preference for emergency dictatorship sat uneasily beside democratic rhetoric; he understood dictatorship in the Roman-republican sense of temporary concentrated command.

## Offices, Appointments, Honors, and Dismissals

- Sardinian merchant-captain qualification, early 1830s.
- Young Italy conspirator; sentenced to death in absentia, 1834.
- Riograndense naval privateer and commander, late 1830s.
- Uruguayan naval commander; colonel of the Italian Legion; commander-in-chief of Uruguayan forces, 1847.
- Deputy of the Roman Constituent Assembly and general of the Roman Republic, 1849.
- Major general in Sardinian service and commander, Cacciatori delle Alpi, 1859.
- Commander in Central Italian forces; resigned August/November 1859 amid strategic restrictions.
- Deputy for Nice and later numerous constituencies in the subalpine and Italian parliaments; elections, resignations, and invalidations make a single uninterrupted term misleading.
- Dictator of Sicily and subsequently the Neapolitan provinces, May–November 1860, in Victor Emmanuel II's name.
- Commander of Italian volunteers, 1866.
- Senior Masonic offices and honorary grand masterships, especially 1864 onward.
- Commander, Army of the Vosges, French Republic, 1870–71.
- Elected to French National Assembly, 1871; withdrew after hostile reception and eligibility dispute.
- Numerous swords of honor, municipal citizenships, medals, and honorary memberships were offered. Garibaldi frequently refused dynastic decorations; lists that mechanically assign every proposed order to him are unreliable without an acceptance or brevet.

## Controversies, Allegations, Crimes, and Unresolved Questions

### Documented criminal and coercive acts

- **1834 treason/conspiracy:** formally condemned to death in absentia by the Sardinian military court. Later national victory transformed the condemned rebel into a state founder; the judgment itself was real.
- **Seizure of vessels:** documented under letters of marque in Brazil and government authority in Uruguay. Whether called privateering or piracy depended on recognition of the issuing regime. No court conviction for common piracy is known.
- **Armed invasion and dictatorship in 1860:** the expedition violated Bourbon sovereignty and initially operated without open Sardinian authorization. Garibaldi governed by emergency decrees and military authority. Subsequent annexation legalized outcomes under the new state but does not retroactively make the invasion non-coercive.
- **Bronte:** summary military justice and five executions under his dictatorship; direct command was Bixio's. Garibaldi bore political command responsibility but is not documented as personally selecting the condemned.
- **1862 and 1867 expeditions:** unauthorized armed attempts against the Papal States, followed by arrest/detention. The Italian state alternately tolerated, manipulated, and suppressed them.

### Allegations and disputed claims

- **Chinese slave transport:** alleged by some contemporaries and repeated later. No firm manifest or corroborating port record establishes that Garibaldi transported enslaved Chinese laborers; strongest modern biographical treatment regards it as unsupported.
- **British conspiracy behind the Thousand:** Britain supplied a permissive diplomatic environment, donors, publicity, and material help. British warships were at Marsala. Evidence does not show that British troops won the campaign or that Garibaldi was a controlled British intelligence agent. Claims of direct secret-service command are unverified.
- **Masonic master plan:** Garibaldi was genuinely a high-profile Freemason, and lodges materially supported nationalism. The conclusion that Freemasonry centrally scripted every stage of unification is a conspiracy narrative not demonstrated by lodge or state records.
- **Theft of the Banco di Napoli/Bourbon treasury:** Garibaldi's dictatorship took control of state funds as a conquering government. Assertions that he personally stole the treasury or became secretly rich lack estate or account evidence. Administrative transfers, military spending, and later state absorption require separation from personal enrichment.
- **Massacre/genocide accusations in the South:** Garibaldian conquest involved battle deaths, executions, repression, and helped initiate a state-building process followed by brutal anti-brigandage war. Garibaldi had left power before most post-unification counterinsurgency. Attributing all subsequent deaths or a planned “genocide” personally to him is not supported; minimizing Bronte and coercive annexation is equally inaccurate.
- **Anita's death:** documented during flight; murder, strangulation, and secret autopsy stories emerged from suspicion around the hurried burial. No reliable judicial finding supports homicide.
- **Raimondi marriage:** separation and annulment are documented. Paternity and intimate details were litigated and sensationalized; language about sexual guilt often reflects nineteenth-century honor politics.

### No established record

There is no established record of fraud conviction, corruption conviction, money laundering, addictive disorder, or a formal psychiatric diagnosis. The absence of such a record should not be inflated into proof that every expedition fund was perfectly accounted for.

## Health, Injuries, and Cause of Death

- Recurrent rheumatism began or worsened during South American campaigning.
- Numerous combat wounds included injuries in South America, Rome, Aspromonte (right foot/ankle, 1862), and Monte Suello (1866).
- At Aspromonte the retained projectile produced a famous diagnostic dispute before extraction by Ferdinando Zannetti.
- Chronic arthritis and rheumatic disease progressively impaired mobility; by his final years he used a wheelchair and special bed/chair arrangements.
- Periods of melancholy followed the fall of Rome and Anita's death. Evidence supports grief and depressive language, not a clinical retrospective diagnosis.
- He developed bronchial illness after the 1882 Sicily journey. He died at Caprera on 2 June 1882. Sources commonly describe bronchitis or respiratory decline against chronic disability; no modern autopsy-based single cause is available.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Hero of the Two Worlds”

**Story:** One liberator fought selflessly for freedom in South America and Europe.  
**Appearance/circulation:** Built from Montevideo reports, Mazzinian newspapers, the 1846 sword subscription, European illustrated press, Dumas's publications, and the 1860 campaign.  
**Evidence:** The transatlantic military career is real; the label compresses factional civil wars, privateering, defeats, local actors, and state sponsorship.  
**Effect:** Made Garibaldi a portable liberal-national icon from New York to Buenos Aires and enabled later revolutionary appropriation.

### The red shirt

**Story:** Garibaldi deliberately equipped his men in heroic scarlet; a popular variant says the shirts were intended for slaughterhouse workers so blood would not show.  
**Origin:** Italian Legion in Montevideo, 1840s; later memoir and popular histories.  
**Evidence:** The legion wore red work shirts obtained cheaply in Uruguay. The precise blood-concealment rationale and single purchase story are less securely documented.  
**Effect:** Created one of the nineteenth century's most successful political uniforms; “Redshirts” became the movement's name.

### First sight of Anita

**Story:** From his ship or through a telescope Garibaldi saw Anita, went ashore, and declared “Devi essere mia” (“You must be mine”).  
**Origin:** Garibaldi's romanticized memoir tradition and later hagiography.  
**Evidence:** Meeting at Laguna is secure; exact dialogue and instantaneous destiny are unverifiable. Anita's existing marriage complicates the idyll.  
**Effect:** Converted an irregular wartime relationship into a foundational national romance and made Anita both lover and warrior saint.

### Calatafimi: “Here we make Italy or we die”

**Story:** Garibaldi answered Bixio's call to retreat with “Qui si fa l'Italia o si muore.”  
**Origin:** Veteran recollections and patriotic retelling.  
**Evidence:** The sentiment fits the crisis, but the exact wording lacks a contemporaneous transcript.  
**Effect:** Became a schoolbook formula for voluntarist nation-making.

### British ships saved the landing at Marsala

**Story:** Royal Navy vessels deliberately shielded the Thousand and ensured victory.  
**Origin:** Bourbon complaint, contemporary diplomatic suspicion, later nationalist and anti-Risorgimento writing.  
**Evidence:** British ships were present and Bourbon commanders acted cautiously; they did not open fire for Garibaldi. British political and private aid was important but not equivalent to a naval intervention.  
**Effect:** Supports opposite myths: international liberal solidarity or Italy as a British-engineered conquest.

### Teano handshake

**Story:** Garibaldi met Victor Emmanuel at a precisely identified point, hailed him “King of Italy,” and voluntarily handed over the nation in a harmonious handshake.  
**Origin:** Royal and patriotic iconography after 1860.  
**Evidence:** The meeting on 26 October is secure; exact site, exchange, choreography, and emotional harmony are disputed. Garibaldi accepted transfer but resented his treatment and that of his army.  
**Effect:** Became the canonical reconciliation of popular revolution and monarchy.

### “Obbedisco”

**Story:** One word proves perfect obedience and patriotic self-abnegation.  
**Origin:** Garibaldi's documented telegram of 9 August 1866.  
**Evidence:** The word is genuine; it answered an unwanted strategic withdrawal after political settlement.  
**Effect:** Schoolbook symbol of discipline, often stripped of frustration and civil-military tension.

### Lincoln offered command of the Union Army

**Story:** Lincoln offered Garibaldi supreme command of all Union forces; he refused unless slavery was abolished.  
**Origin:** Wartime press reports, US diplomatic correspondence, later discovery/publication of documents.  
**Evidence:** US representatives offered a major-general's commission. Garibaldi demanded commander-in-chief authority and an emancipation mandate. Lincoln could not delegate his constitutional role; negotiations ended.  
**Effect:** Added abolitionist and global-democratic prestige and generated counterfactual history.

### Garibaldi and Meucci invented the telephone

**Story:** Their Staten Island work made Garibaldi a participant in telephone invention.  
**Evidence:** They knew each other and Garibaldi worked in Meucci's candle factory; no patent, laboratory notebook, or technical paper credits Garibaldi with the telephone.  
**Effect:** Links two Italian immigrant icons but exceeds the record.

### The incorruptible poor man

**Story:** Garibaldi rejected every reward, lived only by manual work, and never touched public money.  
**Evidence:** He repeatedly refused land, titles, and rewards and lived comparatively simply. He also owned Caprera, benefited from international subscriptions and gifts, earned royalties, supported indebted children, and eventually accepted state funding.  
**Effect:** Central to his political credibility; opponents used the 1876 acceptance to allege hypocrisy.

### Competing twentieth-century Garibaldis

Liberals emphasized national unity and constitutional monarchy; socialists the International and labor; nationalists and Fascists the martial leader and emergency dictator; antifascists the volunteer internationalist (the Garibaldi Brigades in Spain and partisan formations in Italy); Catholic critics the anticlerical invader; neo-Bourbon narratives the foreign-backed conqueror; popular culture the pure adventurer. Each selects authentic fragments but becomes myth when presented as the whole person.

## Works by Garibaldi

Authorship is complicated by dictation, lost manuscripts, translation, editing, and posthumous arrangement. The list below distinguishes major original works from collections.

### Memoirs and autobiography

- **Memorie autobiografiche / Memorie** — begun in several recensions from the late 1840s/1850s; circulated through editors and translations. Alexandre Dumas's *Mémoires de Garibaldi* (Paris, 1860) is an adapted literary version based on Garibaldi material, not a verbatim manuscript. An English *Garibaldi: An Autobiography* appeared in 1861, translated by William Robson and mediated by Dumas. The definitive Italian *Memorie autobiografiche* was published posthumously at Florence in **1888**, edited from later text. Genuine autobiographical core; editorial forms differ materially.
- **The Life of General Garibaldi** (New York, 1859), translated/compiled by Theodore Dwight “from his private papers.” Authentic source material, but editorially constructed and extended by the compiler.

### Narrative, fiction, and poetry

- **Poema autobiografico** (composed 1862; later published in collected writings) — autobiographical blank verse.
- **Clelia, ovvero Il governo dei preti** — first issued in English as *The Rule of the Monk; or, Rome in the Nineteenth Century* (London, **1870**), then Italian editions as *Clelia*. Genuine novel, written for income and anticlerical propaganda; heavily dependent on publishing mediation.
- **Cantoni il volontario** (Milan, **1870**) — historical/political novel.
- **I Mille** (Milan, **1874**) — novelized/memoir-like representation of the expedition; polemical toward Mazzini and not interchangeable with a campaign diary.
- **Manlio, romanzo contemporaneo** — completed circa 1879; remained manuscript until publication at Naples in **1982**. Genuine late novel, posthumously edited.
- Poems, hymns, sonnets, and occasional verse appear in collected editions; attribution should be checked against the National Edition.

### Political and military writings

- Proclamations, decrees, military reports, orders of the day, addresses, and speeches from South America, Rome, 1859–67, France, and parliament. These are genuine when preserved in signed originals or government/press transcripts; scribal drafting was routine.
- Programmatic letters on national unity, Rome, Venice, abolition, peace, the International, anticlericalism, suffrage, and education.
- Plans and memorials concerning the Tiber and Roman public works.
- **Epistolario di Giuseppe Garibaldi**, in the *Edizione nazionale degli scritti di Giuseppe Garibaldi* — a modern scholarly collection, not a work conceived by Garibaldi as a single book. Volumes cover correspondence from 1834 to 1882 and continue to be issued/reissued by the Istituto per la storia del Risorgimento italiano.
- **Scritti e discorsi politici e militari** in the National Edition — posthumously edited documentary collection.

No verified patents stand in Garibaldi's name. Works advertised simply “by Garibaldi” in nineteenth-century English and French publishing must be checked for Dumas/Dwight adaptation, translation, anonymous continuation, or publisher compilation.

### Hostile and revisionist traditions

Post-1860 Bourbon loyalist pamphlets, clerical publications, and later neo-Bourbon books depict Garibaldi as pirate, Masonic agent, British puppet, thief, and destroyer of a prosperous South. These works preserve opposition testimony and sometimes identify neglected archival questions, but specific claims require independent treasury, diplomatic, military, and demographic evidence. Fascist-era works conversely appropriated him as a proto-dictatorial warrior; postwar Communist and socialist traditions stressed internationalism. Neither tradition is a neutral whole-life biography.

## Death, Funeral, and Estate

Garibaldi returned to Caprera on 16 April 1882 after the Sicily journey, suffering bronchial decline. He died in the late afternoon of 2 June, surrounded by relatives and a small circle of friends. His 1881 will asked for cremation and placement of ashes beneath a juniper. Political and religious sensitivities, family decisions, and reverence for the intact body prevented compliance.

The funeral took place at Caprera on **8 June 1882** with senior civil and military representatives and veterans' associations. He was buried under a large rough granite stone in the family cemetery. The house and island complex passed into national memorial custody over time. Estate disputes and the competing claims of descendants, state, editors, and relic collectors complicated the posthumous handling of manuscripts and objects; this is one reason provenance matters for attributed texts and relics.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Garibaldi became the most internationally recognizable figure of the Risorgimento. Streets, squares, statues, schools, ships, military units, biscuits, fish, clothing, operas, plays, and political clubs carried his name. Monument campaigns mapped the new nation onto urban space; the Caprera grave became a secular pilgrimage site.

His military legacy was the politically mobilized volunteer army: flexible, charismatic, inexpensive, and capable of exploiting state crisis, but vulnerable to poor discipline, inconsistent logistics, factionalism, and dependence on regular-state diplomacy. Later “Garibaldi” formations included the Lincoln Battalion's Garibaldi-related internationalist culture, Italian Garibaldi Brigades in the Spanish Civil War, Communist-led partisan brigades in the Italian Resistance, and electoral use of Garibaldi's face by the Popular Democratic Front in 1948.

Liberal Italy institutionalized him alongside Mazzini, Cavour, and Victor Emmanuel II while muting their conflicts. Fascism stressed warrior, obedience, empire, and dictatorship. Antifascists stressed republicanism, international volunteers, and popular liberation. Catholics long emphasized aggression against Rome and anticlerical insult, while later national commemoration integrated him more cautiously. Latin American memory treats him variably as republican internationalist, foreign mercenary, privateer, or participant in local state formation.

Recent scholarship has shifted from deciding whether he was simply hero or fraud toward studying networks, media construction, gender, material culture, popular mobilization, regional experience, and transnational revolution. The central documentary tension remains: Garibaldi was both an unusually effective maker of his own legend and a person whose actual actions—voyages, wounds, commands, refusals, failures, marriages, and writings—were extraordinary enough to sustain that legend.
