## Works by Constantine XI Palaiologos

### Chancery texts and diplomatic instruments

- **Early January 1449:** *Letter to Antonio Diedo, Venetian duke of Crete*. An official letter announcing John VIII’s death and Constantine’s succession and requesting passage to Constantinople aboard a Venetian ship; the original is not known to survive, and the work belongs partly to Constantine and partly to his chancery ([Dölger–Wirth, reg. 3520; Nicol, pp. 40–41](https://dokumen.pub/the-immortal-emperor-the-life-and-legend-of-constantine-palaiologos-last-emperor-of-the-romans-9780521467179-9780521414562-9780511098178.html)).

- **February 1449:** *Chrysobull for the sons of George Gemistos Plethon*. A privileges charter granting favors to Plethon’s sons, preserved as Constantine’s earliest known imperial act and bearing his imperial subscription; it is an institutional obra issued in his name rather than a privately composed literary text ([Dölger–Wirth, reg. 3521; text in Zepos, *Jus Graecoromanum* I, pp. 705–707; Nicol, pp. 40–41](https://dokumen.pub/the-immortal-emperor-the-life-and-legend-of-constantine-palaiologos-last-emperor-of-the-romans-9780521467179-9780521414562-9780511098178.html)).

- **1449:** *Order concerning duties on Venetian and other imported goods*. A fiscal protocol imposing increased customs charges in Constantinople. It survives as an act reported through Venetian records rather than as a securely preserved authorial text ([Dölger–Wirth, reg. 3524a; Nicol, p. 48](https://dokumen.pub/the-immortal-emperor-the-life-and-legend-of-constantine-palaiologos-last-emperor-of-the-romans-9780521467179-9780521414562-9780511098178.html)).

- **October 1450:** *Letter to Doge Francesco Foscari concerning the new customs duties*. A diplomatic explanation and defense of Constantine’s fiscal policy, occasioned by Venetian protests; its wording belongs to the imperial chancery, and no autograph is claimed ([Dölger–Wirth, reg. 3526; Nicol, p. 48](https://dokumen.pub/the-immortal-emperor-the-life-and-legend-of-constantine-palaiologos-last-emperor-of-the-romans-9780521467179-9780521414562-9780511098178.html)).

- **Beginning of 1451:** *Appeal to Alfonso V of Aragon and Naples*. A request, delivered through an ambassador, encouraging Alfonso’s projected crusade. The regesta establish a diplomatic communication, but not a surviving independently titled literary composition ([Dölger–Wirth, reg. 3529; Nicol, pp. 49–50](https://dokumen.pub/the-immortal-emperor-the-life-and-legend-of-constantine-palaiologos-last-emperor-of-the-romans-9780521467179-9780521414562-9780511098178.html)).

- **Early April 1451:** *Letter to Borso d’Este, marquis of Ferrara*. A Latin diplomatic letter carried by Andronikos Bryennios Leontaris during Constantine’s appeal for Western aid; the Latin text is edited by Spyridon Lambros, but its degree of direct composition by Constantine cannot be separated from chancery authorship ([Nicol, pp. 49–50](https://dokumen.pub/the-immortal-emperor-the-life-and-legend-of-constantine-palaiologos-last-emperor-of-the-romans-9780521467179-9780521414562-9780511098178.html)).

- **June 1451:** *Chrysobull logos for the Republic of Ragusa*. A commercial-privilege charter offering Ragusan merchants premises, limited tax concessions, and their own consul in Constantinople. The original survives in Dubrovnik with its gold seal; diplomatically it is unusual because it is styled a *chrysobull logos* but lacks the normal proem, so it resists treatment as either a merely formulaic protocol or a personal composition ([Miklosich–Müller III, p. 228; Dölger–Wirth, reg. 3530; Virgilio, pp. 92–94](https://etheses.bham.ac.uk/5738/1/Virgilio15PhD.pdf)).

- **September 1451:** *Instrument for the projected marriage to a Georgian princess*. A signed and gold-sealed agreement, additionally authenticated with three red crosses in accordance with Georgian practice. The project was never completed, and the document is known from George Sphrantzes’ testimony rather than from a surviving instrument; it is therefore the material trace of an abandoned dynastic project ([Nicol, pp. 45–46](https://dokumen.pub/the-immortal-emperor-the-life-and-legend-of-constantine-palaiologos-last-emperor-of-the-romans-9780521467179-9780521414562-9780511098178.html)).

- **1451:** *Communication to Pope Nicholas V accompanying the statement of the anti-unionist Synaxis*. Constantine had the Synaxis formulate and sign its objections to the Union of Florence and sent the resulting statement to Rome through Leontaris. The enclosed statement was made by Constantine’s opponents, not by Constantine; his own covering communication is an imperial diplomatic act whose precise text and independent completeness are uncertain ([Dölger–Wirth, regs. 3532–3535; Nicol, pp. 49–51](https://dokumen.pub/the-immortal-emperor-the-life-and-legend-of-constantine-palaiologos-last-emperor-of-the-romans-9780521467179-9780521414562-9780511098178.html)).

- **1452–1453:** *Appeals to Rome and Venice for the defense of Constantinople*. A series of urgent diplomatic messages requesting a Christian coalition, ships, troops, arms, and supplies. The standard regesta treat these as several acts, but much of their substance survives through replies and narrative witnesses rather than complete authorial texts, so the series is better understood as a continuing political project than as a set of titled works ([Dölger–Wirth, regs. 3545–3549 and 3551; Guilland; Nicol, pp. 61–63](https://www.myriobiblos.gr/texts/english/nicol_fall.html)).

- **Autumn 1452–29 May 1453:** *Chrysobull granting Mesembria to John Hunyadi*. A conditional grant intended to secure Hunyadi’s military assistance: Hunyadi would hold Mesembria as Constantine’s dependent and defend Constantinople against the Ottomans. It is reported only in the much-disputed *Chronicon Maius* attributed to Pseudo-Sphrantzes; Dölger and Wirth accepted the act as historical, but the lost document’s existence and terms remain inseparable from that problematic transmission ([Dölger–Wirth, reg. 3545; discussion and source criticism](https://acta.bibl.u-szeged.hu/56839/1/historica_143.pdf)).

- **Early 1453:** *Final requests to Venice and Alfonso V for aid and provisions*. Messages asking not only for soldiers, arms, and ships but also food for the blockaded capital. Their existence is documented, but they do not survive as a clearly bounded, authorially titled set of texts ([Dölger–Wirth, regs. 3548–3549 and 3551; Nicol, pp. 62–63](https://dokumen.pub/the-immortal-emperor-the-life-and-legend-of-constantine-palaiologos-last-emperor-of-the-romans-9780521467179-9780521414562-9780511098178.html)).

### Coinage

- **1449–1453:** *Silver stavraton*. An imperial monetary manifestation bearing Christ on one face and Constantine’s crowned image and titulature on the other. Individual dies and sigla vary, so “the stavraton” names an issued type rather than one handmade object; its attribution, unknown to older catalogues, was established from later discoveries and the hoard catalogued by Simon Bendall ([Bendall, “The Coinage of Constantine XI”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/numi_0484-8942_1991_num_6_33_1959); [specimen and catalogue references](https://www.numisbids.com/sale/10129/lot/944)).

- **Probably 1449–1453:** *Silver half-stavraton*. A fractional imperial coin type, perhaps including Constantine’s earliest issue, although the proposed dating of particular groups to the beginning of his reign is inferential. Only a small number are known, and attribution rests on legends, dies, and sigla rather than on a documentary mint record ([Bendall](https://www.persee.fr/doc/numi_0484-8942_1991_num_6_33_1959); [Dumbarton Oaks catalogue references summarized in the Newman Numismatic Portal](https://nnp.wustl.edu/Library/AdvancedSearch?contenttype=Auction&fullsearchterm=bank&page=688)).

- **Probably 1453:** *Silver one-eighth stavraton*. A small fractional type, also called a *doukatopoulon* or *aspron*. Bendall associated its fresh, die-linked specimens with the emergency coinage reportedly struck from ecclesiastical silver during the siege, but that precise dating remains an argument rather than an inscriptional fact ([Bendall](https://www.persee.fr/doc/numi_0484-8942_1991_num_6_33_1959); [catalogued specimen and summary of the attribution](https://dev.thenewyorksale.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/nysale40.pdf)).

### Fortification and demolition projects

- **Completed March 1444:** *Reconstruction of the Hexamilion wall*. A large defensive building project undertaken by Constantine and his brother Thomas across the Isthmus of Corinth. Because its making depended on compelled local labor and collective administration, it resists attribution as Constantine’s personal work; Ottoman forces destroyed it in December 1446 ([Nicol, pp. 23–29](https://dokumen.pub/the-immortal-emperor-the-life-and-legend-of-constantine-palaiologos-last-emperor-of-the-romans-9780521467179-9780521414562-9780511098178.html)).

- **c. 1430:** *Demolition of Clarentza*. After recovering the port fortress from Catalan occupiers, Constantine ordered it destroyed to prevent its reuse by pirates. This is an obra of deliberate unmaking rather than a surviving object, and its exact execution date is not securely fixed ([Nicol, pp. 12–13](https://dokumen.pub/the-immortal-emperor-the-life-and-legend-of-constantine-palaiologos-last-emperor-of-the-romans-9780521467179-9780521414562-9780511098178.html)).

- **1452–1453:** *Repair and defensive preparation of Constantinople’s walls*. A continuing collective project—repairing fortifications, gathering weapons, and provisioning the capital—directed by Constantine before the siege. It has no single title or completed form and should not be mistaken for a personally fabricated architectural work ([Nicol, pp. 63–64](https://dokumen.pub/the-immortal-emperor-the-life-and-legend-of-constantine-palaiologos-last-emperor-of-the-romans-9780521467179-9780521414562-9780511098178.html)).

### Reported speech that resists classification as an authored work

- **May 1453:** *Address to the defenders before the final assault*. Several post-conquest witnesses attribute a final exhortation to Constantine, but they preserve materially different versions, and the elaborate Greek text commonly circulated as his “last speech” depends on the later and textually problematic *Chronicon Maius*. A speech was plausibly delivered, yet no recoverable authorial text exists; it is a reported event and later textual manifestation, not securely a work composed by Constantine ([Nicol, discussion of the versions and sources](https://dokumen.pub/the-immortal-emperor-the-life-and-legend-of-constantine-palaiologos-last-emperor-of-the-romans-9780521467179-9780521414562-9780511098178.html)).
