## Works by Meyer Lansky

### Buildings and development projects

- **1956:** *Hotel Monaco*. An unrealized Havana hotel-casino project commissioned by Lansky from Philip Johnson; Johnson’s surviving drawings document the project, but disagreements with Lansky ended the commission. It is a **project**, not a completed building, and its architectural authorship belongs to Johnson rather than Lansky.[^1]

- **1956–1957:** *Hotel Habana Riviera*. Lansky was the developer and principal instigator of this hotel-casino Gesamtkunstwerk, coordinating architecture, interiors, art, entertainment, and gambling; Igor B. Polevitzky and Verner Johnson designed the building, with Cuban collaborators, so Lansky should not be called its architect. It opened on 10 December 1957 and was nationalized after the Cuban Revolution.[^1][^2][^3]

### Gambling enterprises

- **1930s–circa 1950:** *Colonial Inn*. A South Florida casino enterprise owned or controlled by Lansky and operated through associates, including his brother Jake; Lansky later acknowledged ownership, but the concealed partnership structure makes sole authorship impossible to establish.[^4][^5]

- **Late 1930s–early 1940s:** *Oriental Park racetrack and Hotel Nacional casino operation*. A Havana gambling enterprise organized with Frank Erickson and others. Lansky testified that he had interests in the racetrack and the Nacional casino; this was an operating system within existing venues, not a building he designed.[^4][^6]

- **1933–1958:** *The Havana “gangsterismo” arrangement*. A long-running political-business **protocol** initiated through Lansky’s negotiations with Fulgencio Batista: gambling interests shared proceeds and influence with Cuban officials in exchange for protection. Jack Colhoun treats this arrangement as foundational to a larger “gangster state,” but it was collective, mutable, and covert rather than a formally titled work.[^7]

- **1956–1959:** *Riviera de Cuba, S.A., and the Riviera casino operation*. The corporate and managerial mechanism behind the Hotel Habana Riviera, assembled by Lansky with multiple investors, managers, nominees, and Cuban state financing. It resists attribution as a single-person obra because hidden interests and front ownership were intrinsic to its construction.[^2][^4]

### Organizations and collaborative systems

- **Early 1920s:** *The “Bugs and Meyer Mob”*. The conventional name for Lansky and Benjamin Siegel’s Lower East Side partnership and its associates. Its status as a discrete, formally constituted organization is uncertain—the name was largely retrospective, and Lansky later refused to validate the familiar account—so it is better classified as an attributed criminal network than as a securely bounded work.[^8][^9]

- **Circa 1929–1930s:** *The “National Crime Syndicate”*. Lansky is frequently credited, with Charles Luciano and others, as an architect of an interethnic system of criminal cooperation. Robert Lacey’s standard critical biography disputes that any cohesive national organization corresponding to that title existed; accordingly, this is an influential attributed creation whose very existence as a single obra remains contested.[^8][^10]

- **1942–1945:** *Lansky’s intermediary network in Operation Underworld*. Lansky helped connect United States naval intelligence with Luciano and waterfront underworld contacts during the Second World War. The government created the operation, however, and historians dispute its effectiveness and the later heroic narrative, so Lansky’s contribution was an improvised liaison mechanism rather than authorship of the project.[^11]

- **December 1946:** *Havana Conference*. A gathering of American organized-crime figures co-convened by Lansky and Luciano at Havana’s Hotel Nacional. As an orchestrated event it was a collective **manifestation**, not a permanent institution, and subsequent accounts disagree over its decisions and historical importance.[^3][^8]

### Unfinished and private writings

- **Date uncertain; late in Lansky’s life:** *Philosophical notebooks*. Unpublished handwritten notebooks in which Lansky began recording his philosophy and reflections while declining proposals for a criminal “tell-all.” They form an unfinished personal **project**, not a completed memoir; the surviving account does not establish their full number, dates, or present completeness.[^9]

- **1972–1982:** *Letters to Zali de Toledo*. A corpus of slightly more than six hundred private letters discussing their relationship, Lansky’s legal position, Israel, politics, family, business advice, and philosophy. Written for one recipient rather than publication, the correspondence is a textual obra only retrospectively, through its survival and collection as an archive.[^12]

- **Circa 1974–1980:** *Correspondence with Fred E. Weisgal*. Letters preserved with related clippings in the Fred E. Weisgal Papers at the University of Baltimore. The archival description identifies a single file rather than a critically established complete correspondence, so neither its extent nor its independence from other surviving letter groups should be assumed.[^13]

### Recorded and compelled speech

- **14 February 1951:** *Testimony before the Senate Special Committee to Investigate Organized Crime in Interstate Commerce*. A sworn, transcribed performance in which Lansky described some of his Cuban gambling interests. It resists being called a work: the words survive as a government-produced hearing record created under compulsion, although Lansky made the statements recorded in it.[^6]

- **3 September 1971:** *Israeli television interview*. A recorded interview in which Lansky publicly contested allegations about his wealth and criminal authority. It is a jointly produced audiovisual **manifestation**, reportedly his only television interview, rather than a text authored solely by him.[^14][^15]
