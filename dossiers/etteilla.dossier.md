# Jean-Baptiste Alliette: Research Dossier

## Scope, Method, and Evidentiary Labels

Jean-Baptiste Alliette—better known by the reversed surname **Etteilla**—left a substantial printed corpus but a remarkably sparse personal archive. Much of his life has therefore been reconstructed from civil, police, citizenship, and commercial records identified by modern historians; title pages, advertisements, prefaces, and addresses in his publications; surviving cards; and later occult literature.

This dossier uses the following labels:

- **Documented fact:** supported by an archival record, surviving publication, contemporary notice, or physical artifact.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a modern scholarly source that identifies or summarizes documentary research, although the underlying record was not independently inspected here.
- **Self-claim:** stated by Alliette about himself or his system.
- **Contemporary claim:** stated during his lifetime but not independently verified.
- **Disputed claim:** contradicted by documentary evidence or substantial historical scholarship.
- **Later allegation:** an assertion originating after his death.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story transmitted without adequate documentary support.
- **Unresolved:** the presently available evidence does not permit a reliable conclusion.

The exact birthday of 1 March is widely accepted but remains slightly less secure than the year. Thierry Depaulis describes him as born in Paris in 1738, “probably” on 1 March. The death date, 12 December 1791, is more consistently reported.

## Names, Titles, and Identity

**Etteilla** is an anacyclic pseudonym: *Alliette* read backward. It supplied an apparently exotic identity while remaining transparently linked to his civil surname. He used it on manuals, prospectuses, cards, predictions, and organizational material.

He is sometimes called “Etteilla the Younger” because his elder brother was also named Jean-Baptiste Alliette. Modern catalogues occasionally transform him into “Jean-François Alliette.” The archival reconstruction summarized by Depaulis and by Ronald Decker, Thierry Depaulis, and Michael Dummett supports **Jean-Baptiste**, not Jean-François.

Descriptions such as “the first professional tarot reader” are modern historiographical summaries. They are defensible if understood narrowly: he is the earliest documented person known to have constructed a public career around teaching, publishing, and practicing an articulated system of tarot divination. They should not be read as proving that nobody had previously accepted money for card divination.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Father — Jean-Baptiste Alliette**

- **Reported fact:** He was a Parisian master *rôtisseur* or *traiteur*—a licensed cook, caterer, or seller of prepared food—and a bourgeois of Paris.
- A Strasbourg record later summarized in historical research describes the younger Alliette as the legitimate son of Jean-Baptiste Alliette, bourgeois and caterer of Paris.
- No evidence located establishes noble ancestry, a learned family tradition, or membership in an occult lineage.

**Mother — Marie-Anne Bautray**

- **Reported fact:** Her maiden name was **Bautray**.
- She conducted business as a seed or grain merchant after her husband’s death.
- Alliette’s own early occupation as *marchand grainier* probably continued or paralleled the maternal trade.
- She reportedly died in 1769.

### Sibling

**Jean-Baptiste Alliette the Elder**

- **Reported fact:** The occultist was the second son; his elder brother, also Jean-Baptiste, was born around 1737.
- The elder brother was emancipated in 1758 so that he could succeed their father while still legally a minor.
- He worked at different times as a wine merchant, caterer, refreshment seller, or *buvetier* near the Palais de Justice.
- **Documented episode summarized by Decker–Depaulis–Dummett:** Paris police files from 1763 concern the elder brother’s attempted abduction of a young woman with whom he was infatuated. This episode belongs to the brother, not to Etteilla.
- The elder brother experienced commercial failure and obtained arrangements with creditors in 1772 and 1773.
- Confusion between the two same-named brothers has contributed to contradictory accounts of Etteilla’s occupations and marital status.

### Marriage

**Jeanne Vattier**

- **Reported fact:** By 1763 Alliette had married **Jeanne Vattier**.
- The marriage produced at least one child.
- The couple separated in 1767.
- No reliable evidence located establishes a divorce, later reconciliation, or Alliette’s remarriage.
- A later Strasbourg record reportedly called him a bachelor. That description conflicts with the Paris marriage record and may indicate that he presented himself as unmarried after separation, that the official used *garçon* loosely, or that records concerning the same-named brothers have been confused.

### Children

- **Documented minimum:** At least one child was born to Alliette and Jeanne Vattier.
- Publications of 1790 bear the imprint or attribution **“Etteilla fils,”** demonstrating the activity of a son or someone publicly presented as his son.
- A later autobiographical passage is reported to mention a son named **Alexis**, born in Brittany.
- The surviving summaries do not securely establish whether Alexis was the child of Jeanne Vattier, whether “Etteilla fils” was Alexis, or the son’s full civil identity.
- Claims that the son inherited and fully continued Etteilla’s organization require caution. Later disciples and publishers certainly continued the system, but institutional succession is not fully documented.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1738–1762

Alliette was born into the Parisian urban trades rather than the aristocracy, clergy, university, or professional learned classes. His father’s catering status and his mother’s commercial activity place the family among economically active Parisian commoners with guild and bourgeois connections.

Almost nothing reliable is known about his childhood.

No school, college, university, apprenticeship contract, tutor, or formal degree has been identified. His later writing displays broad reading in astrology, divination, alchemy, Hermetic literature, biblical chronology, number symbolism, and popular scientific culture, but it does not demonstrate conventional university training.

### Claimed early formation in cartomancy

Alliette later gave differing retrospective dates for his discovery or instruction:

- **1751:** Some accounts of his tarot writings say that he claimed initiation into card divination in 1751.
- **1753:** Later traditions associate him with a lost or otherwise unverified early version of his card-reading method.
- **1757:** Depaulis reports that Alliette claimed to have received the revelation of card reading in 1757.
- **Italy or Naples:** Later occult narratives sometimes say that he learned from Italians or mysterious Neapolitan masters.

These claims are **self-claims or later traditions**, not established documentary facts. No securely identified publication by him predates 1770, and no independent evidence confirms an Italian journey, a Neapolitan teacher, or an initiation during adolescence.

## Education and Formation

### Formal education

**Unresolved.** No evidence establishes formal higher education.

### “Professor of algebra”

Alliette styled himself **“professeur d’algèbre”** in print, notably in connection with the *Loto des Indiens*.

Possible interpretations include:

1. He taught elementary calculation, combinations, lotteries, or numerical games.
2. He used “algebra” expansively for combinatorial or cabalistic operations.
3. The title was promotional self-fashioning.

No academic appointment, diploma, school affiliation, or recognized mathematical publication has been located. Consequently, “professor of algebra” is a **documented self-designation**, not a documented institutional office.

### Intellectual sources

His work combined several streams:

- Established French and European fortune-telling with ordinary cards.
- Piquet-deck conventions.
- Astrology and zodiacal correspondences.
- The four classical elements and humoral theory.
- Numerological and combinatorial procedures.
- Hermetic and alchemical literature.
- Physiognomy, metoposcopy, palmistry, dream interpretation, and lottery calculation.
- Court de Gébelin’s Egyptian interpretation of tarot.
- The Comte de Mellet’s identification of tarot as the “Book of Thoth.”
- A creation scheme apparently indebted to the Hermetic *Poimandres/Pymander*.
- Contemporary fascination with Egyptian antiquity and hieroglyphic wisdom.

Alliette’s achievement was principally synthetic and procedural. He converted disparate traditions into teachable rules, numbered cards, upright and reversed meanings, layouts, classes, and a purpose-built pack.

## Marriage, Trade, and Early Career, 1763–1769

### 1763: seed and grain merchant

By 1763 Alliette was a **marchand grainier**. This occupation is supported by archival research and accords with his mother’s trade.

The phrase may refer to a seller of seeds, grain, or related agricultural commodities rather than a wholesale grain speculator. No surviving accounts, investments, partnership agreements, or property inventories were located.

### Separation

Alliette and Jeanne Vattier separated in 1767. The reasons are unknown. No litigation or formal separation agreement was identified in the consulted material.

### Transition to print selling

Around 1768–1769 he appears to have become a **marchand d’estampes**, or seller of prints.

This transition is important because it gave him practical access to engravers, printers, illustrated sheets, book distribution, and the commercial visual culture from which his cards emerged. In 1775 he appears as bookseller or vendor in connection with a catalogue for a collection of prints belonging to a Provençal amateur.

### His mother’s death

Marie-Anne Bautray reportedly died in 1769. No detailed inheritance record was located.

## The First Cartomancy Publications, 1770–1773

### *Etteilla, ou manière de se récréer avec un jeu de cartes* (1770)

This is Alliette’s first securely identified book. It concerned fortune-telling with an ordinary 32-card piquet pack, supplemented by an additional blank or “Etteilla” card.

Its historical importance is considerable:

- It is the earliest known printed manual devoted systematically to divination with ordinary playing cards.
- It assigned defined meanings and offered an organized reading procedure.
- It transformed what may previously have circulated through practice, manuscript, or oral instruction into a reproducible commodity.
- Its use of the name Etteilla created a brand covering author, method, cards, and instruction.

This book was not yet the mature “Egyptian tarot” system. Claims that the 1770 work was already a complete tarot manual confuse it with the later *Manière de se récréer avec le jeu de cartes nommées tarots*.

### *Le Zodiaque mystérieux, ou les oracles d’Etteilla* (1772)

Published under an Amsterdam/Paris imprint through Lesclapart, this approximately 200-page work expanded his divinatory enterprise through zodiacal and oracle material.

“Amsterdam” on eighteenth-century French esoteric and controversial books could be a real distribution point, a protective false imprint, or a convention suggesting intellectual freedom. The imprint alone does not establish that Alliette traveled to Amsterdam.

### *Etteilla, ou la seule manière de tirer les cartes* (1773)

A revised and expanded form of his ordinary-card cartomancy appeared in 1773. The title’s claim to be the “only” way is promotional and signals his effort to establish proprietary authority.

A third and purportedly final authorial edition appeared in 1783 under the title *Etteilla, ou instructions sur l’art de tirer les cartes*.

## Print Dealing and Strasbourg, Approximately 1774–1780

### Print trade

In 1775 Alliette participated as a bookseller or vendor in the sale of a print collection. This is direct evidence that his career was not confined to divination.

His print business likely supplied:

- Knowledge of engraving and hand coloring.
- Relationships with printers and booksellers.
- Familiarity with catalogue sales.
- A distribution network for cards and manuals.
- Experience using images as standardized instructional objects.

### Strasbourg residence

**Reported fact:** Between approximately 1777 and 1780 he worked in Strasbourg as a print seller.

A Strasbourg citizenship entry reportedly described him as:

- A print seller.
- From Paris.
- A legitimate son of Jean-Baptiste Alliette, Paris bourgeois and caterer.
- Son of Marie-Anne Bautray.

The record’s description of him as a bachelor conflicts with his known marriage and separation. The discrepancy remains unresolved.

No reliable evidence establishes that Strasbourg was an exile, imprisonment-related move, or political flight. It was probably commercial. Strasbourg’s location also placed him within an active French- and German-language print market, but specific German collaborators from this period have not been securely identified.

## Court de Gébelin and the Turn to Occult Tarot, 1781–1785

### Antoine Court de Gébelin’s intervention

In 1781 Antoine Court de Gébelin published the eighth volume of *Le Monde primitif*. Its tarot material argued that the pack preserved an ancient Egyptian book hidden in plain sight. An associated essay by the Comte de Mellet elaborated divinatory and alphabetic interpretations and associated the pack with Thoth.

Modern historical research places the invention of tarot in fifteenth-century northern Italy as a trick-taking game. No archaeological, textual, or card-historical evidence supports an ancient Egyptian origin.

### Alliette’s response

Alliette rapidly adopted and greatly expanded the Egyptian thesis. Unlike Court de Gébelin, he already possessed a published system for ordinary-card divination. He therefore turned the historical speculation into an operational occult technology.

From 1783 through 1785 he issued four *cahiers* of:

*Manière de se récréer avec le jeu de cartes nommées tarots*

The sequence is bibliographically complicated. Surviving copies bind or title the parts differently, and some catalogues assign inconsistent years. Broadly:

- First, second, and third cahiers appeared around 1783.
- The fourth appeared in 1785.
- The work supplied definitions, spreads, upright and reversed meanings, astrological correspondences, and theoretical claims.
- It presented tarot as the fragmented pages or leaves of an Egyptian “Book of Thoth.”

### Priority dispute

Alliette insisted that his knowledge predated Court de Gébelin. His claims of instruction or discovery in the 1750s would, if true, give him independent priority.

The evidence supports a divided verdict:

- **Documented fact:** Alliette published ordinary-card cartomancy in 1770, before Court de Gébelin’s tarot essay.
- **Documented fact:** Court de Gébelin published the Egyptian-tarot thesis in 1781.
- **Documented fact:** Alliette’s extensive Egyptian tarot publications followed in 1783–1785.
- **Self-claim:** Alliette knew the relevant tarot secrets decades earlier.
- **Historical assessment:** The timing and conceptual overlap make Court de Gébelin and de Mellet the evident printed sources for the specifically Egyptian “Book of Thoth” framework.
- **Unresolved:** Alliette may have experimented privately with tarot before 1781, but no independent evidence establishes his mature Egyptian system before Court’s publication.

### *Épître à M. Court de Gébelin* (1784)

Alliette addressed a printed epistle to Court de Gébelin at the beginning of 1784. This is a documented intellectual contact through publication. The available evidence does not prove a face-to-face meeting or sustained private correspondence.

Court de Gébelin died in May 1784.

### Other publications, 1782–1785

Alliette also pursued numerical games and “high sciences”:

- *Instruction sur le loto des Indiens que nous a donné en 1772 Mr Etteilla, professeur d’algèbre* (1782).
- *Instruction sur la combinaison hislérique, extraite du Loto des Indiens* (1782).
- *L’Homme à projets* (1783).
- *Fragments sur les hautes sciences, suivi d’une note sur les trois sortes de médecines données aux hommes, dont une mal à propos délaissée* (1785).
- *Philosophie des hautes sciences, ou la clef donnée aux enfans de l’art, de la science & de la sagesse* (1785).

These works show that he marketed a comprehensive knowledge system rather than tarot alone.

## Alchemy, Physiognomy, Palmistry, and the “High Sciences,” 1785–1787

### Alchemy

In *Les Sept nuances de l’œuvre philosophique-hermétique*, followed by a treatise on the perfection of metals, Alliette entered alchemical discourse.

The work does not document successful transmutation, metallurgical invention, or laboratory discovery. It belongs to late eighteenth-century Hermetic and “philosophical” alchemy.

### Freemasonry

Alliette has frequently been described as a Freemason.

His own statement argues against membership. In a “Petit avant-propos pour le denier du pauvre,” he said that although he respected “true Masonry” as much as a brother might, the proliferation of lodge and grade names appeared to him more like folly than wisdom.

Accordingly:

- **Documented self-report:** He denied being a Freemason.
- **Later theory:** His symbolism, institutional forms, and milieu have led writers to infer Masonic connections.
- **Historical assessment:** Intellectual proximity or contact with Masons is plausible; formal lodge membership has not been demonstrated.
- **Unverified claim:** Assertions that he occupied a Masonic degree or lodge office.

### Metoposcopy and palmistry

In 1787 he published:

- *L’Art de connoître les hommes par l’inspection du front, ou élémens de métoposcopie suivant les anciens*.
- *L’Art de lire dans les lignes et caractères qui sont dans les mains, ou élémens de chiromance*.

These books packaged traditional physiognomic and chiromantic materials for a commercial audience. They should not be read as evidence of empirical anatomical research.

### *Science. Leçons théoriques et pratiques du livre de Thot. Moyennes classes* (1787)

This work presented the system in graduated lessons and reinforced Alliette’s role as teacher. It also reflects his ambition to classify occult knowledge into elementary, intermediate, and higher levels.

## The Etteilla Tarot and Institutional Expansion, 1788–1790

### Société des Interprètes du Livre de Thot

In or around 1788 Alliette organized the **Société des Interprètes du Livre de Thot**.

Its purposes included:

- Teaching his tarot system.
- Coordinating French-speaking correspondents.
- Standardizing interpretations.
- Circulating tables and instructions.
- Establishing a community of authorized interpreters.

The size and stability of the society are uncertain. Later claims that he trained 500 pupils, including 150 professional readers, are transmitted in posthumous accounts and have not been independently verified. They may preserve a real school tradition while magnifying its scale.

### *Jeu des tarots, ou le Livre de Thot ouvert à la manière des Égyptiens* (1788)

This publication connected tarot with the interpretation of dreams, visions, and nocturnal experiences. Its imprint invoked “Memphis,” reinforcing the system’s Egyptian staging. “Memphis” was symbolic or fictitious; it is not evidence of travel to Egypt.

### The purpose-built tarot pack

Around 1788–1789 Alliette produced or arranged publication of a 78-card pack commonly called the **Livre de Thot** or later the **Grand Etteilla**.

A January 1789 document or application associated with his address at **48 rue de l’Oseille, Paris**, sought authorization or privilege relating to printing the French *Livre de Thot*.

The luxury version was engraved by **Pierre-François Basan** and brush-colored; cheaper uncolored versions were also available.

The pack’s innovations included:

- A fixed numbering from 1 to 78.
- Upright and reversed meanings.
- Astrological assignments.
- Elemental and humoral correspondences.
- A reordered trump sequence.
- A cosmogonic first series representing Creation.
- Explicit divinatory words or interpretive prompts.
- A visual system no longer intended principally for trick-taking play.

It retained recognizable material from Marseille-pattern tarot while altering sequence, imagery, labels, and theoretical function.

The terms “Major Arcana” and “Minor Arcana” were not Alliette’s. Those labels belong to later occult tarot vocabulary.

### Business structure

The Etteilla enterprise joined:

- Paid consultations.
- Lessons and courses.
- Books and pamphlets.
- Tables and interpretive sheets.
- Cards in luxury and inexpensive forms.
- Societies and schools.
- Correspondence.
- Prophetic periodicals.

No reliable balance sheets, income totals, real-estate holdings, investments, or estate valuation were located. Claims that he became wealthy cannot be substantiated. His repeated small-format publications and multiple bookseller relationships suggest entrepreneurial activity, not necessarily financial security.

## French Revolution and Political Activity, 1789–1791

Alliette lived through the opening phase of the French Revolution and adapted his public identity to the new political vocabulary.

### Political posture

His publications used terms such as:

- “French liberty.”
- “Patriotic.”
- “National.”
- Prophecy concerning the nation and political future.

He presented divination as a science available to citizens rather than as a secret belonging exclusively to courtly or priestly elites.

No evidence located shows that he:

- Held elected office.
- Belonged to the Jacobins, Cordeliers, Feuillants, or another political club.
- Served in the National Assembly.
- Participated in revolutionary violence.
- Was imprisoned or investigated as a counterrevolutionary.
- Worked for a government intelligence service.

### Claim to have predicted the Revolution

Alliette later suggested that *L’Homme à projets* or his other calculations had anticipated major upheaval.

This is a **self-claim**. Prophetic texts commonly permit retrospective matching through broad language. No independently verified, precise, pre-1789 prediction by him was located that unambiguously forecasts the Revolution’s principal events and dates.

### Nouvelle École de Magie

On **1 July 1790**, described as the second year of French liberty, Alliette opened or proclaimed a **Nouvelle École de Magie** in Paris.

A surviving announcement and his *Aperçu sur la nouvelle école de magie* support the institution’s existence as a teaching project. A German account of its opening speech appeared in 1792.

The school offered systematic instruction in tarot and associated occult disciplines. Six lessons were apparently projected for the *Cours théorique et pratique du Livre de Thot*, but surviving copies contain four.

### *L’Oracle pour et contre mil sept cent quatre-vingt-onze*

Published in November 1790, this work offered predictions for 1791. It is important as evidence of his increasingly public, topical, and political form of prophecy.

### *Journal projétique et patriotique*

Seventeen issues reportedly appeared between February and July 1791.

“Projétique” relates to projects or forecasts. The journal combined prophecy, patriotism, and commentary. Its short run may reflect limited resources, ill health, political instability, weak sales, or his approaching death; the reason is not documented.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Jeanne Vattier

Wife and mother of at least one child. Their separation in 1767 is documented through archival reconstruction. No later personal correspondence is known.

### Marie-Anne Bautray

Mother and probable commercial model for his early seed-trading occupation.

### Jean-Baptiste Alliette the Elder

Brother with overlapping name and Paris commercial occupations. Confusion between the brothers has distorted Etteilla’s biography.

### Antoine Court de Gébelin

Primary intellectual predecessor and public addressee. Court’s 1781 Egyptian-tarot theory supplied the framework Alliette operationalized. Alliette sent or published an epistle to him in 1784. A face-to-face relationship is unproven.

### Comte de Mellet

His essay in Court de Gébelin’s *Le Monde primitif* helped formulate tarot as the Book of Thoth and described purported divinatory use. No personal meeting with Alliette is documented.

### Pierre-François Basan

Engraver associated with the luxury *Livre de Thot* deck. This is a documented production relationship.

### Lesclapart family

The Lesclapart bookshop and, later, Madame Veuve Lesclapart distributed several works. The relationship was commercial and important to Alliette’s publishing career.

### Other booksellers and publishers

Title pages connect him with:

- Segault or Segaut.
- Legras.
- Mérigot le jeune.
- Nyon l’aîné.
- Durand neveu.
- Petit.
- Samson.
- The Société Typographique.

The nature and duration of each relationship varies. Some were ordinary retail distribution rather than intellectual collaboration.

### Disciples and successors

The named membership of his societies is incompletely reconstructed. Later Etteilla-pattern authors and publishers included Julia Orsini, “Mlle Lemarchand,” Hugand, Joubert de La Salette, and anonymous or pseudonymous compilers. They belong principally to the posthumous Etteilla tradition; direct personal apprenticeship cannot be assumed in every case.

### Marie-Anne Lenormand

Modern summaries frequently say that Etteilla influenced the celebrated cartomancer Marie-Anne Lenormand.

- **Historical influence:** Plausible and likely through the commercial cartomantic tradition he helped establish.
- **Direct relationship:** No reliable evidence located proves that she was his pupil, met him, or worked in his organization.
- Later “Mlle Lenormand” decks were often commercial products published after her death and do not prove a connection to Alliette.

## Religion, Philosophy, and Intellectual Development

Alliette’s religious position cannot be reduced to a clear denominational or philosophical system.

His publications drew upon:

- Biblical Creation.
- Providence and moral order.
- Hermetic revelation.
- Egyptian priestly mythology.
- Astrology.
- Alchemy.
- Cabala or number symbolism.
- Natural-magic language.
- Enlightenment claims to method, classification, instruction, and public utility.

He was neither a conventional academic Enlightenment rationalist nor simply a transmitter of medieval occultism. His work applied the eighteenth century’s appetite for systems, tables, reproducible operations, lessons, and public education to divination.

His “Book of Thoth” was simultaneously:

1. A purported ancient Egyptian book.
2. The tarot pack itself.
3. A symbolic encyclopedia of the cosmos.
4. A commercial divination technology.
5. A curriculum administered by authorized interpreters.

## Technologies, Projects, and Commercial Innovations

Alliette did not invent tarot cards, cartomancy, astrology, alchemy, or the Egyptian myth of tarot. His documented innovations lay in organization and design:

- Printing the earliest known systematic ordinary-card cartomancy manual.
- Assigning standardized meanings to cards.
- Making reversal meaningful as a regular component of interpretation.
- Systematizing spreads and procedural readings.
- Integrating tarot with astrology, elements, humors, and cosmogony.
- Reordering and redesigning a complete tarot for divination.
- Placing interpretive aids directly on or around card images.
- Creating tiered courses.
- Forming societies of interpreters.
- Combining publication, instruction, consultation, correspondence, and card sales.

These practices anticipate later occult orders, correspondence courses, proprietary decks, certification communities, and commercial “schools” of divination.

No patents in the modern sense have been identified. The January 1789 printing application concerned publication authorization or privilege rather than a technology patent.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, and Financial Questions

### Results of the dedicated adversarial search

Searches were conducted using Alliette’s civil name and pseudonym with French and English terms corresponding to controversy, scandal, allegation, lawsuit, fraud, accusation, conspiracy, criticism, imprisonment, police, charlatanism, and recent material from 2024–2026.

No reliable evidence was located that Jean-Baptiste Alliette was:

- Arrested.
- Imprisoned.
- Indicted.
- Convicted.
- Sued for fraud.
- Investigated for financial crime.
- Involved in money laundering.
- Connected to political violence.
- Employed by an intelligence service.
- Formally prosecuted for illegal fortune-telling.
- Subject to a documented censorship or obscenity prosecution.

This negative finding should not be converted into a claim that his business practices were independently validated. It means that no qualifying legal or administrative proceeding was found in the consulted record.

### Confusion with his brother’s police record

The elder Jean-Baptiste Alliette appears in a 1763 police matter involving an attempted abduction. The occultist was the younger, same-named brother. Attributing that incident to Etteilla would be an error.

### Claims of fraud or charlatanism

Later critics portrayed Etteilla as an ignorant charlatan.

These judgments rest mainly on:

- His sale of divinatory services.
- Claims of ancient Egyptian authority.
- Claims to predict future events.
- Unverified assertions of early initiation.
- His self-bestowed title “professor of algebra.”
- His difficult prose and erratic spelling.
- His commercial presentation of occult knowledge.

No known court judgment established fraud. Historically unsupported claims are not the same thing as a legal finding that he knowingly deceived particular clients.

### Egyptian origin of tarot

**Disputed claim:** Tarot was an Egyptian sacred book created by ancient priests and transmitted through cards.

**Evidence against:**

- The earliest documentary and material evidence places tarot in fifteenth-century northern Italy.
- Early tarot was a game using a permanent trump suit.
- No pharaonic card pack, Egyptian text describing tarot, or transmission chain has been found.
- Etteilla’s designs visibly develop European tarot and eighteenth-century occult speculation.

**Status:** Historically unsupported pseudohistory, though foundational to modern occult tarot.

### The “Book of Thoth”

No ancient book matching Alliette’s 78-card system has been identified. “Book of Thoth” was a powerful mythic designation, not a verified Egyptian manuscript.

### Predictive accuracy

Alliette published predictions and claimed successful forecasts. No controlled body of contemporary records permits measurement of accuracy. Later admirers often cite general predictions after the fact; hostile critics dismiss all results without examining individual texts.

**Status:** Unverified. His historical importance does not depend on supernatural accuracy.

### “Professor of algebra”

No institutional appointment has been found. The title may reflect private teaching, calculation, lottery combinations, or promotional inflation.

**Status:** Documented self-title; formal academic status unverified.

### Freemasonry

Formal membership remains unproven, and his own words deny it. The notion that he was a Masonic initiate should therefore be labeled a **theory**, not fact.

### Finances

No detailed financial archive was located. He operated multiple commercial ventures, but:

- Income from consultations is unknown.
- Print and deck sales are unknown.
- Debts are unknown.
- Property ownership is unknown.
- The financial structure of his societies is unknown.
- No bankruptcy has been documented for the younger Alliette; the known creditor arrangements belong to his elder brother.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Myth 1: Etteilla was an illiterate hairdresser

**Story:** He was a former wigmaker or hairdresser who had never learned French or spelling.

**Circulation:** The best-known form appears in Éliphas Lévi’s *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie* in the 1850s. Later occultists and encyclopedias repeated it.

**Evidence:**

- Archival research identifies Alliette as seed merchant and print seller.
- No professional hairdressing record has been located.
- He lived or worked near an address associated with a hairdresser, which may have encouraged confusion.
- His prose is often eccentric, but he authored or supervised a large printed corpus.

**Assessment:** The hairdresser claim is probably false. The attack functioned as a class-coded denunciation of a commercially successful, self-taught predecessor.

**Reputational effect:** It made him the comic or embarrassing ancestor of nineteenth-century occult tarot.

### Myth 2: He possessed an authentic ancient Egyptian tarot

**Story:** The 78 cards were surviving leaves of a book created by Egyptian sages or by Thoth and preserved in symbolic form.

**Evidence for:** Only symbolic parallels, Alliette’s assertions, and eighteenth-century speculative etymology.

**Evidence against:** The documented Italian history of tarot and absence of Egyptian prototypes.

**Assessment:** Myth or pseudohistory.

**Reputational effect:** This became one of the most influential origin stories in modern Western occultism.

### Myth 3: He learned tarot in Naples or Italy in the early 1750s

**Story:** A mysterious Italian or Piedmontese initiated the adolescent Alliette into ancient card divination.

**Evidence:** Later self-referential claims and occult retellings.

**Problems:** No travel record, teacher, diary, correspondence, or contemporary corroboration has been found.

**Assessment:** Unverified self-mythology.

**Effect:** It supplied him with priority over Court de Gébelin and an initiatory pedigree.

### Myth 4: He independently discovered everything before Court de Gébelin

**Story:** Alliette’s entire Egyptian tarot doctrine existed decades before *Le Monde primitif*.

**Evidence for:** His 1770 card-divination book proves an early and genuine interest in cartomancy.

**Evidence against:** His specifically Egyptian tarot corpus appeared after Court de Gébelin and uses the same newly public framework.

**Assessment:** His ordinary-card system was demonstrably independent and earlier; his claim to prior possession of the full Egyptian tarot system is unproven.

### Myth 5: He was a high-ranking Freemason

**Story:** His occult school and symbolism encoded lodge teachings received through formal initiation.

**Evidence:** General resemblance to Masonic and para-Masonic culture; activity in a milieu containing Masons.

**Counterevidence:** His explicit denial of Masonic membership.

**Assessment:** Theory unsupported by membership documentation.

### Myth 6: He founded an enduring occult order with hundreds of initiates

**Story:** The Société des Interprètes and Nouvelle École de Magie were large, formally structured initiatory bodies with hundreds of practitioners.

**Evidence:** His publications and announcements document real organizations or teaching projects. Later accounts report approximately 500 students and 150 professionals.

**Problems:** Membership rolls, minutes, financial records, and full statutes have not survived or have not been identified.

**Assessment:** Organizational activity is documented; numerical scale and institutional continuity are uncertain.

### Myth 7: He predicted the French Revolution

**Story:** He foresaw the collapse of the old regime before 1789.

**Evidence:** His later claims and retrospectively interpreted prophetic works.

**Problems:** No precise and unambiguous forecast has been demonstrated in the consulted material.

**Assessment:** Self-claim, unverified.

### Myth 8: He created tarot itself

**Story:** Etteilla invented tarot cards.

**Evidence against:** Tarot existed in Italy by the fifteenth century.

**Assessment:** False. He created the first known purpose-built occult tarot system, not tarot as a game or card family.

### Myth 9: He was the first person ever to tell fortunes with cards

Earlier divination with cards is documented or reported in Europe, including references predating Alliette.

**Assessment:** Too broad. He was the earliest known publisher of a systematic cartomancy manual and the earliest documented professional system-builder of occult tarot.

## Works by Jean-Baptiste Alliette

Bibliography is difficult because his works appeared in small cahiers, composite volumes, revised editions, separately sold lessons, and later compilations. Imprints reading Amsterdam or Memphis are not always literal places of publication.

### Authenticated or strongly attributed lifetime works

1. **1770 — *Etteilla, ou manière de se récréer avec un jeu de cartes*, par M.***.**  
   Amsterdam and Paris: Lesclapart. Ordinary-card cartomancy using a 32-card piquet pack plus an additional card. Genuine lifetime publication.

2. **1772 — *Le Zodiaque mystérieux, ou les oracles d’Etteilla*.**  
   Amsterdam and Paris: Lesclapart. Zodiacal and divinatory oracle work. Genuine.

3. **1773 — *Etteilla, ou la seule manière de tirer les cartes; revue, corrigée et augmentée par l’auteur sur son premier manuscrit*.**  
   Amsterdam and Paris: Lesclapart. Revised ordinary-card manual. Genuine.

4. **1775 — *Notice d’une collection d’estampes, provenant d’un amateur de Provence…*.**  
   Alliette appears as bookseller or commercial participant rather than necessarily as author. The catalogue is evidence of his print trade.

5. **1782 — *Instruction sur le loto des Indiens que nous a donné en 1772 Mr Etteilla, professeur d’algèbre*.**  
   Place uncertain, probably Paris. Genuine or issued under his authority.

6. **1782 — *Instruction sur la combinaison hislérique, extraite du Loto des Indiens*.**  
   Place uncertain. Genuine or strongly attributed.

7. **1783 — *Etteilla, ou instructions sur l’art de tirer les cartes. Troisième et dernière édition par l’auteur de la Cartonomancie*.**  
   Amsterdam and Paris: Segault and Legras. Authorial revision.

8. **1783 — *L’Homme à projets*.**  
   Paris or no stated place. Genuine, although surviving bibliographic descriptions vary.

9. **1783 — *Manière de se récréer avec le jeu de cartes nommées tarots, pour servir de premier cahier à cet ouvrage*.**  
   Amsterdam and Paris: the author, Mérigot, Legras, Segault. Genuine.

10. **1783 — *Manière de se récréer avec le jeu de cartes nommées tarots, pour servir de second cahier à cet ouvrage*.**  
    Genuine; bibliographic arrangement varies.

11. **1783–1784 — *Manière de se récréer avec le jeu de cartes nommées tarots, pour servir de troisième cahier à cet ouvrage*.**  
    Amsterdam and Paris. Genuine.

12. **1784 — *Épître à M. Court de Gébelin, salut, Etteilla, premier de l’an vulgaire 1784*.**  
    Paris, probably. Genuine printed address to Court de Gébelin.

13. **1785 — *Manière de se récréer avec le jeu de cartes nommées tarots, pour servir de quatrième cahier à cet ouvrage*.**  
    Amsterdam and Paris. Contains extensive astrological material. Genuine.

14. **1785 — *Fragments sur les hautes sciences, suivi d’une note sur les trois sortes de médecines données aux hommes, dont une mal à propos délaissée*.**  
    Amsterdam and Paris. Genuine.

15. **1785 — *Philosophie des hautes sciences, ou la clef donnée aux enfans de l’art, de la science & de la sagesse*.**  
    Amsterdam and Paris: the author and several booksellers. Genuine.

16. **Circa 1786 — *Les Sept nuances de l’œuvre philosophique-hermétique, suivies d’un traité sur la perfection des métaux*, under the preliminary title *L.D.D.P.* or *Le Denier du pauvre*.**  
    Genuine alchemical work.

17. **1787 — *L’Art de connoître les hommes par l’inspection du front, ou élémens de métoposcopie suivant les anciens*.**  
    Amsterdam and Paris. Genuine.

18. **1787 — *L’Art de lire dans les lignes et caractères qui sont dans les mains, ou élémens de chiromance*.**  
    Amsterdam and Paris. Genuine.

19. **1787 — *Science. Leçons théoriques et pratiques du livre de Thot. Moyennes classes*.**  
    Amsterdam and Paris. Genuine instructional work.

20. **1788 — *Jeu des tarots, ou le Livre de Thot ouvert à la manière des Égyptiens, pour servir ici à l’interprétation de tous les rêves, songes et visions diurnes et nocturnes*.**  
    “Memphis,” with distribution by Veuve Lesclapart, Petit, and Samson. Genuine.

21. **Circa 1788–1789 — *Livre de Thot* tarot pack.**  
    Seventy-eight cards, including a luxury hand-colored edition engraved by Pierre-François Basan. Genuine visual work made under Alliette’s direction; the exact division of artistic labor is not fully documented.

22. **1790 — *Cours théorique et pratique du Livre de Thot; pour entendre avec justesse l’art, la science et la sagesse de rendre les oracles*.**  
    Paris: Société Typographique. Four surviving lessons, although six were projected. Genuine.

23. **1790 — *Aperçu sur la nouvelle école de magie établie à Paris, le premier juillet de la seconde année de la Liberté française*.**  
    Paris: Etteilla fils. Genuine organizational prospectus.

24. **November 1790 — *L’Oracle pour et contre mil sept cent quatre-vingt-onze*.**  
    Paris or no stated place. Genuine political-prophetic publication.

25. **February–July 1791 — *Journal projétique et patriotique*.**  
    Seventeen reported issues. Periodical founded and conducted under his name.

26. **1791 — *Tablette catégorique des significations du Livre de Thot, avec des annotations par M. Etteilla, astrologue…*.**  
    Associated with his instructional system. Surviving catalogues differ over exact title, format, and independent-publication status.

### Works requiring caution

- **“Le Petit Etteilla.”** This name commonly denotes the 32-card method or later packs based on it. Many physical “Petit Etteilla” decks are posthumous products, including versions associated with Jacques Grasset de Saint-Sauveur around 1796. They should not automatically be treated as cards personally designed or published by Alliette.
- **“Grand Etteilla.”** Later nineteenth- and twentieth-century packs preserve his system but often alter art, captions, card order, or correspondences.
- **Posthumous manuals titled *L’Art de tirer les cartes*, *Le Grand Etteilla*, or *Le Petit Oracle des dames*.** Some reproduce his teachings; others are anonymous compilations or commercial adaptations.
- **Works attributed to “Julia Orsini,” “Mlle Lemarchand,” or similar names.** These often belong to the Etteilla school but are not writings by Alliette.
- **Modern translations and reconstructions.** These may reorganize or silently harmonize conflicting eighteenth-century texts.

## Books and Major Works About Alliette

### Principal historical studies

- Ronald Decker, Thierry Depaulis, and Michael Dummett, *A Wicked Pack of Cards: The Origins of the Occult Tarot* (1996), especially chapter 4, “Etteilla, the First Professional Cartomancer.” The principal modern archival and critical reconstruction.
- Michael Dummett, *The Game of Tarot: From Ferrara to Salt Lake City* (1980). Foundational card-historical context and critique of occult origin claims.
- Thierry Depaulis, “Etteilla,” *Encyclopædia Universalis* (2009). Compact biography based on archival research.
- Thierry Depaulis, “Le vrai tarot d’Etteilla,” *Le Vieux Papier*, fascicle 368 (April 2003), pp. 448–454. Specialist study of the authentic Etteilla pack and later variants.
- Thierry Depaulis, *Tarot, jeu et magie* (Bibliothèque nationale, 1984). Exhibition catalogue placing Etteilla within the history of tarot and divination.
- Wouter J. Hanegraaff, ed., *Dictionary of Gnosis & Western Esotericism* (2005), entry on Alliette/Etteilla. Bibliographic and intellectual context.
- Helen Farley, *A Cultural History of Tarot: From Entertainment to Esotericism* (2009). Cultural history using the documented game-to-occult transition.
- Paul Huson, *Mystical Origins of the Tarot* (2004). Broad historical study with substantial attention to early cartomancy.
- Leandra Negro Della Torre, *Etteilla e il Libro di Thot*. Italian specialist study cited in modern continental literature.
- Jacques Halbronn, ed., *L’Astrologie du Livre de Toth*, followed by research on the history of astrology and tarot (1993). Edition and commentary drawing on Alliette’s fourth cahier.
- Gérard van Rijnberk, *Le Tarot: histoire, iconographie, ésotérisme* (1947). Influential but now partly superseded historical treatment.
- Giordano Berti, works on the history of tarot, including modern catalogues and surveys that discuss Etteilla’s deck.
- Stuart R. Kaplan, *The Encyclopedia of Tarot*, multiple volumes. Extensive visual documentation of Etteilla-pattern packs, though biographical claims should be checked against archival scholarship.

### Hostile or polemical treatments

- Éliphas Lévi, *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie* (1854–1856). Source of the influential “former hairdresser” attack.
- Alexandre Dumas, *Les Mille et Un Fantômes* and related versions. Contains a literary, mocking discussion of Alliette as a supposed sorcerer and card specialist; not a reliable biography.
- Nineteenth-century bibliographic dictionaries and histories of magic frequently repeat the wigmaker story and criticize his language and claims.

### Sympathetic and practitioner literature

Numerous modern tarot manuals treat the Grand Etteilla as a living divinatory system. Their practical value varies, and many conflate Alliette’s original writings with nineteenth-century alterations. Significant categories include:

- Reproductions of *Grand Etteilla I, II,* and *III*.
- French manuals published by Grimaud.
- Modern reconstructions of the 1789 *Livre de Thot*.
- Caitlin Matthews and Rebecca Birrell’s modern work on the “Original Petit Etteilla,” including an annotated English presentation of the ordinary-card method.
- Belle Bradley, *Etteilla’s Tarot Restored* (2026), a modern practitioner reconstruction rather than an archival biography.

## Death

Jean-Baptiste Alliette died in Paris on **12 December 1791**, aged approximately 53.

No reliable source consulted specifies:

- Cause of death.
- Final illness.
- Attending physician.
- Accident or injury.
- Burial place.
- Funeral arrangements.
- Inventory of possessions.
- Exact estate value.
- Whether Jeanne Vattier or his child was present.

A modern popular account says he was exhausted by intense activity, but this is not a documented medical cause and should not be repeated as fact.

He died before the radicalization of the Revolution into the Terror. His death was therefore not caused by revolutionary execution, imprisonment, or documented political persecution.

## Posthumous Development of the Etteilla Tradition

### Immediate continuation

His disciples, family associates, booksellers, and later compilers continued to publish:

- Interpretive tables.
- Card packs.
- Courses.
- “Grand Etteilla” manuals.
- Ordinary-card “Petit Etteilla” systems.
- Works issued under pseudonymous female authorities.

The Etteilla pack remained a recognizable French commercial tarot family through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

### Nineteenth-century occultism

Later occult authors borrowed his central premise—that tarot was an ancient book of universal wisdom—while often rejecting his particular card order and imagery.

Éliphas Lévi replaced Alliette’s arrangement with a system centered on:

- Twenty-two Hebrew letters.
- Cabalistic paths and correspondences.
- A restored Marseille-like trump sequence.

Lévi’s contemptuous portrait helped make Etteilla simultaneously foundational and disreputable.

Papus, Oswald Wirth, and later occultists inherited the Egyptian and initiatory conception of tarot but generally preferred systems derived through Lévi rather than Alliette’s deck.

### English-language eclipse

The Rider–Waite–Smith tarot, first published in 1909, became dominant in the English-speaking world. Its illustrated minor cards and Golden Dawn correspondences pushed the Etteilla family to the margins outside continental Europe.

Arthur Edward Waite criticized earlier French occult speculation but remained part of a tradition whose conception of tarot as an esoteric book ultimately depended on Court de Gébelin and Alliette.

### Modern reassessment

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century historians have separated three questions:

1. **Was Alliette’s Egyptian history correct?** No supporting historical evidence has been found.
2. **Was he important to occult history?** Yes.
3. **Was he merely an ignorant imitator?** The surviving publications show a more original and technically systematic figure than hostile nineteenth-century accounts allowed.

Modern reassessment credits him with several “firsts” while rejecting his ancient-origin narrative.

## Historical Legacy

Alliette’s principal legacies are:

- The printed systematization of cartomancy.
- Tarot meanings defined for both upright and reversed cards.
- A tarot pack designed for divination rather than play.
- The integration of tarot with astrology, elements, humors, dreams, and cosmology.
- A reproducible curriculum for occult instruction.
- A commercial model combining author, teacher, consultant, publisher, and deck designer.
- The institutionalization of esoteric tarot through societies and schools.
- The transformation of tarot from a European game into a purported ancient book of wisdom.

His historical Egyptian claims are unsupported, but the myth itself became an effective cultural fact. It shaped occult orders, literature, visual art, popular divination, and the global tarot market.

## Atlas Connections

### Antoine Court de Gébelin

- **Nature:** Documented intellectual influence and printed address.
- **Dates:** Court’s tarot essay appeared in 1781; Alliette published his tarot cahiers from 1783 and dated an *Épître à M. Court de Gébelin* to 1 January 1784.
- **Place:** Paris publishing milieu.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Documented fact** for printed influence and epistle; **unresolved** for any face-to-face meeting.
- **Details:** Court’s claim that tarot preserved an ancient Egyptian book supplied the framework that Alliette expanded into an operational divinatory system. Alliette claimed earlier knowledge, but the documented Egyptian tarot corpus follows Court’s publication.

### Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa von Nettesheim

- **Nature:** Reported textual and intellectual association through later Etteilla-school divination literature invoking Agrippa’s physiognomic, astrological, and magical authority.
- **Dates and places:** No personal connection was possible; Agrippa died in 1535.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Reported intellectual influence**, not a personal relationship.
- **Caution:** Some later packs and manuals claiming Agrippan authority are posthumous Etteilla-school products. Direct dependence by Alliette must be established work by work.

### Zoroaster

- **Nature:** Symbolic or legendary authority cited in the learned occult vocabulary surrounding Alliette’s tarot and astrology.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Mythic/intellectual association**, not a historical connection.
- **Caution:** References to “Zoroaster” in occult literature do not identify a recoverable textual transmission from the historical Zarathustra to Alliette.

### Other roster members

No source consulted documents a meeting, correspondence, family relationship, institutional tie, patronage, rivalry, or acknowledged direct influence involving Alliette and the other named atlas figures. Apparent overlaps with figures such as Louis XVI, the Comte de Saint-Germain, Franz Anton Mesmer, Emanuel Swedenborg, Benjamin Franklin, Napoleon Bonaparte, Éliphas Lévi, Papus, Arthur Edward Waite, or Pamela Colman Smith would rest on shared period, later influence chains, or general occult context rather than a documented personal crossing. They are therefore not entered as connections.
