# Wolfgang Ernst Pauli: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier concerns the Austrian-born theoretical physicist Wolfgang Ernst Pauli (1900–1958), sometimes recorded more fully as Wolfgang Ernst Friedrich Pauli.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by archival, institutional, contemporaneous, or primary-source evidence.
- **Reported fact:** stated in a credible biography or scholarly secondary source but not independently verified here from the underlying document.
- **Disputed claim:** sources give materially different accounts.
- **Unverified attribution:** widely repeated, but no secure primary source was located.
- **Anecdote, legend, or myth:** historically significant as a story about Pauli, without establishing the claimed event or causal mechanism.

The documentary base is unusually strong for Pauli’s professional life. CERN holds his scientific papers, manuscripts, correspondence, books, photographs, Nobel diploma, and a sound recording. ETH Zürich holds administrative records and additional correspondence. Large parts of his correspondence have been published in a multivolume scholarly edition.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Principal name:** Wolfgang Ernst Pauli.
- **Fuller recorded form:** Wolfgang Ernst Friedrich Pauli.
- **German pronunciation:** approximately “VOLF-gang POW-lee.”
- **Born:** 25 April 1900, Vienna, Austria-Hungary.
- **Died:** 15 December 1958, Zürich, Switzerland, aged 58.
- **Occupation:** theoretical physicist; university professor; scientific author and editor.
- **Citizenships:**
  - Austrian by birth.
  - His Austrian nationality became entangled with German nationality and Nazi racial law after the 1938 Anschluss.
  - Naturalized as a United States citizen in January 1946.
  - Acquired Swiss citizenship later in 1949.
- **Religion:** baptized and raised Roman Catholic; later left the church and was generally recorded as unaffiliated. His mature intellectual interests nevertheless included Christian symbolism, Jewish mysticism, Greek philosophy, alchemy, Eastern traditions, and Jungian psychology.
- **Major appointments:** University of Göttingen; University of Hamburg; Niels Bohr’s institute in Copenhagen; ETH Zürich; Institute for Advanced Study, Princeton.
- **Best-known achievement:** discovery of the exclusion principle, announced in 1924–1925.
- **Other major work:** relativistic quantum mechanics, Pauli matrices and spin formalism, quantum field theory, the neutrino hypothesis, spin–statistics connection, discrete symmetries, regularization, meson theory, and unpublished early non-Abelian gauge/Kaluza–Klein work.
- **Nobel Prize:** Physics, 1945, “for the discovery of the Exclusion Principle, also called the Pauli Principle.”
- **Nicknames and epithets:**
  - “The conscience of physics,” a retrospective description of his role as a severe internal critic.
  - “Der fürchterliche Pauli” (“the terrible Pauli”).
  - “Die Geißel Gottes” (“the scourge of God”), which he sometimes used jokingly in correspondence.
  - “Mephistopheles,” used in some reminiscences for his sardonic, penetrating style.
- **Not a pseudonym:** “Pauli” was the family surname adopted by his father, who had been born Wolf Pascheles. Wolfgang himself did not maintain a separate literary pseudonym.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal line

**Documented fact:** Pauli’s father was Wolfgang Joseph Pauli (1869–1955), born Wolf Pascheles into a Jewish family in Prague. He studied medicine, changed his surname, converted to Catholicism, and became a physician, biochemist, and professor specializing in colloid chemistry.

The Pascheles family belonged to Prague’s Jewish publishing and bookselling world. Pauli’s paternal great-grandfather Wolf Pascheles was a Jewish publisher. Research on the family has traced connections between Pauli’s father and the circle of physicist and philosopher Ernst Mach.

The father’s work included protein colloids and their electric charge, hydration, swelling, and optical properties. After the Nazi annexation of Austria, he was forced from his Vienna institution and went into exile in Zürich.

### Maternal line

Pauli’s mother was Bertha Camilla Schütz, commonly called Berta or Maria Pauli (29 November 1878–15 November 1927). She was the daughter of Friedrich Schütz, a writer and editor at Vienna’s *Neue Freie Presse*, and Bertha Schütz-Dillner, an opera singer.

**Documented fact:** Bertha Pauli worked as a journalist and wrote theatre criticism, historical essays, and political material. She was active as a feminist, pacifist, and socialist. During Austria’s 1919 election campaign—when women first exercised national suffrage—she urged women to vote for the Social Democratic Party. She converted to Protestantism in 1911.

**Documented fact:** She died by suicide on 15 November 1927 after taking an overdose of Veronal.

**Reported and partly disputed context:** Biographical accounts associate the suicide with anxiety, depression, and the crisis produced by her husband’s relationship with another woman. The relative importance of these factors cannot be established from the sources consulted.

### Sibling

Pauli’s younger sister was Hertha Ernestine Pauli (4 September 1906–9 February 1973), an actress, journalist, anti-fascist activist, biographer, and children’s author. She worked in Max Reinhardt’s theatre circle, lived in Vienna and Berlin, fled Nazi-controlled Europe through France and Portugal, and settled in the United States.

### Godfather and name

Physicist and philosopher Ernst Mach was Pauli’s godfather. Pauli’s middle name Ernst commemorated him. Mach gave the child a baptismal goblet that Pauli reportedly retained throughout his life.

### Marriages and children

1. **Luise Margarete Käthe Deppner**, usually called Käthe Deppner or, in some sources, Käthe Deppert:
   - A Berlin cabaret dancer.
   - Married Pauli in December 1929.
   - The marriage ended in separation and divorce in November 1930.
   - No children.

2. **Franciska “Franca” Bertram** (1901–1987):
   - Married Pauli on 4 April 1934.
   - Remained married to him until his death.
   - Their marriage was childless.
   - After Pauli’s death, Franca played a central role in preserving his papers and transferring his scientific estate to CERN.

No reliably documented biological children, acknowledged illegitimate children, or long-term parallel family relationships were found.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1900–1918

Pauli grew up in an educated upper-middle-class household in Vienna, principally associated with the city’s Cottage district. A documented family address was Anton-Frank-Gasse 18.

The family environment combined science, medicine, journalism, music, literature, political reform, and multiple religious inheritances. His father encouraged mathematics and natural science; a private tutor provided additional mathematical instruction.

Pauli attended the humanistic Döblinger Gymnasium, later Bundesgymnasium XIX, at Gymnasiumstraße 83. The curriculum emphasized Latin and Greek as well as mathematics and science. His class was later called a “class of geniuses”; it included Richard Kuhn, the future Nobel laureate in chemistry.

**Reported fact:** Pauli’s mathematical precocity was already conspicuous at school. An often-repeated classroom story has a teacher, unable to locate an error on the blackboard, finally demanding that Pauli reveal it because everyone knew he had already seen it. This is best treated as an anecdote illustrating his school reputation rather than as a verified verbatim incident.

He completed the *Matura* with distinction in July 1918. Within months he published his first paper, concerning Hermann Weyl’s attempted extension of Einstein’s general relativity.

## Education and Formation, 1918–1923

### University of Munich

In autumn 1918 Pauli entered the University of Munich to study theoretical physics under Arnold Sommerfeld. Sommerfeld recognized that the first-year student already commanded much of contemporary mathematical physics.

Pauli’s principal formative figures included:

- **Arnold Sommerfeld:** doctoral supervisor, mentor, and institutional patron.
- **Albert Einstein:** intellectual authority in relativity; later an admirer and Nobel nominator.
- **Hermann Weyl:** mathematical and conceptual influence.
- **Niels Bohr:** mentor in atomic theory and later close correspondent.
- **Max Born:** employer and collaborator in Göttingen.
- **Ernst Mach:** family connection and early philosophical influence, though Pauli did not remain a simple Machian positivist.

Sommerfeld commissioned Pauli to write the relativity article for the *Encyklopädie der mathematischen Wissenschaften*. Completed while Pauli was barely twenty-one, it surveyed special and general relativity with such authority that Einstein praised it. It remained an important reference work and was later published separately as *Theory of Relativity*.

### Doctorate

Pauli received his doctorate in July 1921. His dissertation, *Über das Modell des Wasserstoffmolekülions* (“On the model of the hydrogen molecular ion”), applied the old quantum theory to the hydrogen molecular ion.

The calculation did not reproduce the molecule’s behavior satisfactorily. Pauli understood this not merely as a personal failure but as evidence of the inadequacy of the old quantum theory. The episode helped form his distrust of half-consistent models and his insistence that quantum theory required a more radical reconstruction.

### Göttingen and Copenhagen

- **Winter 1921–1922:** assistant to Max Born at the University of Göttingen.
- **1922–1923:** worked at Niels Bohr’s Institute for Theoretical Physics in Copenhagen.

At Göttingen and Copenhagen, Pauli entered the central network from which modern quantum mechanics emerged. He interacted with Born, Bohr, Sommerfeld, Heisenberg, Kramers, Ehrenfest, Landé, and other leading theorists. His letters became a major informal channel of theoretical criticism and invention.

## Early Career in Hamburg, 1923–1928

Pauli joined the University of Hamburg in 1923 and completed his *Habilitation* there in 1924. He became *Privatdozent* and received the title of professor in 1926.

### Atomic spectra and the exclusion principle

Pauli worked intensively on the anomalous Zeeman effect, the splitting of spectral lines in magnetic fields. The problem was resistant to the old quantum theory and became, in Pauli’s recollection, an intellectual torment.

On 24 November 1924 he wrote to Alfred Landé describing a new “prescriptive rule.” In early 1925 he published *Über den Zusammenhang des Abschlusses der Elektronengruppen im Atom mit der Komplexstruktur der Spektren*. The rule stated, in its mature form, that no two equivalent electrons in an atom could possess the same complete set of quantum numbers.

Initially Pauli described an electron’s unexplained “two-valuedness” without accepting a mechanical spinning-electron picture. George Uhlenbeck and Samuel Goudsmit soon proposed electron spin. Ralph Kronig had considered a similar idea earlier but did not publish it after discouraging reactions, including Pauli’s skepticism.

**Priority distinction:**

- **Documented fact:** Pauli discovered and published the exclusion rule and the nonclassical two-valued degree of freedom.
- **Documented fact:** Kronig, Uhlenbeck, and Goudsmit contributed to the physical interpretation as electron spin.
- **Contested historical judgment:** Pauli’s negative response helped deter Kronig from publishing his early spin proposal. This is a criticism of Pauli’s influence, not evidence that Pauli appropriated Kronig’s discovery.
- **No evidence of fraud or plagiarism** was found.

### New quantum mechanics

Pauli quickly mastered Heisenberg’s matrix mechanics. In late 1925 he solved the hydrogen atom using matrix methods, demonstrating the power of the new theory.

During 1926 he contributed to establishing the equivalence of matrix mechanics and Schrödinger’s wave mechanics. His letters to Heisenberg and others provided criticism and calculations that were important despite not always appearing as formal publications.

### Pauli spin formalism

In 1927 Pauli formulated the nonrelativistic quantum mechanics of a spin-\(\tfrac12\) particle in an electromagnetic field. The associated \(2\times2\) matrices are now called the Pauli matrices. They became fundamental objects in quantum mechanics, quantum field theory, representation theory, quantum information, and modern particle physics.

### Conferences and travel

Pauli participated in major international physics meetings, including the September 1927 Como conference and the Fifth Solvay Conference in Brussels in October 1927. At Como he was photographed with Werner Heisenberg and Enrico Fermi. At Solvay he participated in the debates involving Einstein, Bohr, Born, Heisenberg, Schrödinger, Dirac, Fermi, and others over the interpretation of quantum mechanics.

## ETH Zürich and the Crisis Years, 1928–1934

### ETH appointment

Pauli took up the chair of theoretical physics at ETH Zürich in April 1928. Experimental physicist Paul Scherrer became a close institutional colleague and handled much of the institute’s administration, allowing Pauli to concentrate on theory.

His early assistants and students included Felix Bloch, Rudolf Peierls, Victor Weisskopf, Markus Fierz, Josef Jauch, Nicholas Kemmer, and later Charles Enz. His teaching could be demanding, and his criticism intimidating, but his institute became a major center of theoretical physics.

### Quantum electrodynamics

In 1928 Pauli and Pascual Jordan developed a relativistically covariant formulation of quantum electrodynamics, showing how field commutators could be constructed consistently with special relativity. He subsequently worked on quantum fields, relativistic wave equations, and regularization.

### Personal breakdown

Pauli’s mother’s 1927 suicide was followed by his father’s remarriage, Pauli’s difficult first marriage, and divorce. By 1930–1931 he was experiencing a severe personal crisis.

**Reported fact:** Biographical accounts describe heavy drinking, nightlife, quarrels, unstable relations with women, depression, and behavior that alarmed his father and friends. The evidence comes principally from correspondence, reminiscence, and later biographical reconstruction. No criminal case or formal institutional disciplinary proceeding was located.

### The neutrino hypothesis

On 4 December 1930 Pauli sent his celebrated open letter to a meeting of experimental physicists in Tübingen, addressing them as “Dear radioactive ladies and gentlemen.” To preserve energy, momentum, angular momentum, and statistics in beta decay, he proposed a neutral, very light, spin-\(\tfrac12\) particle.

He first called it a “neutron.” After James Chadwick discovered the much heavier neutron in 1932, Enrico Fermi adopted the diminutive Italian name *neutrino* for Pauli’s particle and incorporated it into his beta-decay theory.

Pauli was cautious about publishing a particle that might be undetectable. He publicly discussed the hypothesis at the Seventh Solvay Conference in Brussels in October 1933. Cowan and Reines detected the electron antineutrino in 1956 and informed Pauli by telegram.

### Analysis with Jung and Rosenbaum

In early 1932 Pauli consulted Carl Gustav Jung. Jung referred him initially to the young analyst Erna Rosenbaum, partly because Jung wished to observe Pauli’s dream material without imposing his own theoretical framework.

Pauli recorded hundreds of dreams and images. Estimates vary according to what is counted: scholarship refers to roughly 250, approximately 400 used by Jung, or about 1,000 dream reports and associated materials over the broader period.

Rosenbaum conducted the initial analysis; Pauli subsequently worked directly with Jung. He ended regular treatment in October 1934 but maintained a substantial intellectual correspondence with Jung until 1957–1958.

Jung presented anonymized selections from Pauli’s dreams in lectures and publications, including the mandala and “world clock” material. Pauli’s identity as the dreamer was not initially disclosed publicly.

### Second marriage

Pauli married Franca Bertram on 4 April 1934. Contemporary and later accounts describe the marriage as stable and generally happy. They had no children.

## Scientific Maturity and International Work, 1934–1939

Pauli visited the United States repeatedly, including a stay at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton in 1935–1936. His scientific concerns included:

- relativistic wave equations;
- Dirac matrices;
- quantum electrodynamics;
- nuclear and meson physics;
- statistics and the relation between spin and particle type;
- symmetry and invariance;
- philosophical foundations of quantum mechanics.

In 1935 Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen challenged the completeness of quantum mechanics. Pauli remained aligned broadly with Bohr’s complementarity framework, while maintaining his own philosophical reservations and resisting crude positivist summaries.

With Markus Fierz he investigated relativistic fields of arbitrary spin. In 1940 Pauli published his general proof of the spin–statistics connection: under the assumptions of relativistic quantum field theory, half-integer-spin particles obey Fermi–Dirac statistics and integer-spin particles obey Bose–Einstein statistics.

## Nazi Annexation, Citizenship Crisis, and Migration, 1938–1946

### Racial classification

The March 1938 Anschluss converted Pauli’s Austrian situation into one governed by Nazi German law. Although baptized Catholic, his ancestry made him Jewish under Nazi racial classifications. In a 1940 letter to Institute for Advanced Study director Frank Aydelotte, Pauli reportedly described himself as classified under German law as “three-quarters Jewish.”

His ETH position did not immediately disappear, but the prospect of a German invasion of Switzerland and the insecurity of his nationality created a direct threat.

### Failed Swiss naturalization

Pauli applied for Swiss naturalization in Zürich and was rejected in 1938 and again in 1940.

**Documented fact:** ETH and Zürich archival work confirms the two rejected applications.

**Controversial administrative judgment:** The naturalization file treated him as insufficiently assimilated. Accounts refer to official concern about his social behavior, nightlife, drinking, divorce, and limited integration. These were bureaucratic judgments, not criminal findings. Antisemitism and the restrictive climate toward refugees and naturalization in wartime Switzerland form part of the context, though the precise weight of prejudice versus formal residency and conduct criteria differs among interpretations.

### Departure from Europe

In July 1940 Wolfgang and Franca Pauli left Europe. Their route involved travel through France, Spain, and Portugal before sailing to the United States. Pauli took leave from ETH and became a visiting professor/member at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton.

He traveled in the Americas during the war and attended scientific meetings. A frequently circulated photograph said to show Pauli and Samuel Goudsmit in Uruguay in 1942 has generated uncertainty about identification and context; no secure evidence found in this research connects such travel to intelligence or weapons activity.

### Wartime research

At Princeton, Pauli worked on quantum field theory and meson theory. He did not join the Los Alamos weapons laboratory or become a principal Manhattan Project physicist.

**Unverified attribution:** A quotation claiming that Pauli refused the Manhattan Project because it was not science’s business “to engineer mass destruction” circulates online. No primary document establishing that exact statement was located.

**Documented negative finding:** No evidence was found that Pauli engaged in espionage, intelligence work, sabotage, or illicit transfer of nuclear information. The documentary record instead places him in fundamental theoretical research.

### Nobel Prize

On 2 November 1945 the Royal Swedish Academy announced Pauli’s Nobel Prize in Physics for the exclusion principle. Einstein had nominated him.

Because wartime conditions and his status prevented immediate travel, a Princeton ceremony was arranged in December 1945. Einstein gave the principal address and publicly associated Pauli with the succession of modern theoretical physics. Pauli later delivered his Nobel lecture, “Exclusion Principle and Quantum Mechanics,” in Stockholm.

### American citizenship and return

Pauli became a United States citizen in January 1946. He nevertheless returned to Zürich later that year and resumed his ETH chair, retaining strong connections with Princeton and American physics.

## Later Career, 1946–1958

### Meson theory and quantum fields

Pauli’s Princeton lectures became *Meson Theory of Nuclear Forces*, first published in the mid-1940s and revised in 1948. Although meson theory was soon transformed by new particle discoveries and quantum field techniques, the book documented a central stage in nuclear-force research.

He and Felix Villars developed Pauli–Villars regularization, a method for controlling divergences in quantum field calculations through auxiliary regulator fields. It remains historically and technically important.

### Philosophy, Kepler, and Jung

Pauli increasingly sought a framework in which psyche and matter, subject and object, rational theory and symbolic imagination could be treated as complementary aspects of reality.

His mature position was not reducible to positivism, conventional religion, or an endorsement of occult claims. He was drawn to:

- Plato and archetypal forms;
- Bohr’s complementarity;
- Jung’s collective unconscious and archetypes;
- Kepler’s mixture of mathematical astronomy and symbolic cosmology;
- the alchemical controversy between Johannes Kepler and Robert Fludd;
- quaternary structures and the number four;
- connections between symmetry, number, and psychological imagery.

In 1952 Jung and Pauli jointly issued *Naturerklärung und Psyche*. It contained Jung’s essay on synchronicity and Pauli’s historical-psychological study of archetypal ideas in Kepler’s scientific theories. It was a jointly published volume, not a jointly written single theory.

**Documented fact:** Pauli assisted Jung by demanding clearer distinctions and by criticizing careless appeals to physics.

**Disputed interpretation:** Later writers sometimes claim that Jungian psychology directly produced Pauli’s scientific discoveries. Pauli’s letters show reciprocal intellectual influence, but the degree to which dream symbolism generated particular physics results remains interpretive rather than demonstrable.

### Non-Abelian Kaluza–Klein theory

In 1953 Pauli developed, in lectures and correspondence, a mathematically consistent extension of Kaluza–Klein theory using a higher-dimensional internal space and a non-Abelian gauge structure.

He did not publish the result as a foundational new theory, partly because it did not explain gauge-boson masses. Later historians have described this as an unpublished anticipation of Yang–Mills theory.

**Priority qualification:** Pauli derived structures closely resembling non-Abelian gauge theory before the 1954 Yang–Mills paper, but he did not publish a complete physical theory or claim priority in the form later associated with Yang and Mills. “Pauli invented Yang–Mills theory” is therefore too strong without qualification.

### Parity violation and discrete symmetries

Pauli contributed to the postwar analysis of symmetry in particle physics. His 1955 essay “Exclusion Principle, Lorentz Group and Reflection of Space-Time and Charge” formed part of the historical development of what became the CPT theorem.

When parity violation was proposed and confirmed in weak interactions in 1956–1957, Pauli was initially skeptical. He then accepted the experimental result. The episode illustrates both the force and limitation of his reliance on symmetry.

### CERN

Pauli supported the revival and internationalization of European physics and was associated with the early CERN community. His later students and assistants, including Victor Weisskopf, held major CERN roles. CERN ultimately became the principal repository for his scientific estate.

### Final collaboration and rupture with Heisenberg

In 1957–1958 Pauli and Werner Heisenberg worked on a nonlinear spinor-field theory intended as a unified theory of elementary particles.

Heisenberg publicly announced the program in a highly confident manner before its mathematical and physical foundations were secure. A press description reportedly reduced Pauli to Heisenberg’s “assistant,” aggravating Pauli.

Pauli withdrew support and sharply repudiated the claims. In March 1958 he sent George Gamow a drawing of an empty rectangle with the joke that it showed he could paint like Titian; “only the technical details” were missing. The barb targeted grand theoretical claims unsupported by completed calculations.

Their relationship deteriorated. Heisenberg did not attend Pauli’s funeral. Later evaluation generally agreed that the proposed unified field theory was unsuccessful.

## Scientific Contributions and Technologies

Pauli was primarily a theorist. He did not found companies, manufacture devices, or build a patent portfolio.

### Principal contributions

1. **Relativity review, 1921**
   - A comprehensive early synthesis of special and general relativity.
   - Praised by Einstein.
   - Long used as a reference.

2. **Exclusion principle, 1924–1925**
   - Explained electron-shell structure and regularities in atomic spectra.
   - Became fundamental to atomic physics, chemistry, condensed matter physics, and the stability of matter.
   - Later generalized through the spin–statistics theorem to fermions.

3. **Matrix-mechanical hydrogen atom, 1925–1926**
   - One of the first decisive applications of the new quantum mechanics.

4. **Pauli matrices and Pauli equation, 1927**
   - Mathematical representation of spin-\(\tfrac12\).
   - Foundational in quantum mechanics and quantum information.

5. **Jordan–Pauli quantum field formulation, 1928**
   - Important step in relativistic quantum field theory.

6. **Neutrino hypothesis, 1930**
   - Proposed an unseen neutral light particle to preserve conservation laws in beta decay.
   - Incorporated by Fermi into beta-decay theory.
   - Experimentally confirmed in 1956.

7. **Fierz–Pauli work on arbitrary-spin fields, 1939**
   - Important for relativistic field theory, including later studies of massive spin-2 fields.

8. **Spin–statistics theorem, 1940**
   - Established the connection between spin and quantum statistics under relativistic field-theoretic assumptions.

9. **Pauli–Villars regularization, 1949**
   - With Felix Villars.
   - A historically central covariant regularization technique.

10. **CPT development, 1955**
    - Pauli’s formulation was one part of the work by Schwinger, Lüders, Pauli, Bell, Jost, and others that produced the modern CPT theorem.

11. **Non-Abelian Kaluza–Klein construction, 1953**
    - Unpublished work anticipating part of the mathematical structure of non-Abelian gauge theory.

### Patents, businesses, and investments

No significant patents, commercial businesses, corporate directorships, or entrepreneurial ventures were found. His intellectual property consisted chiefly of publications, lecture notes, and correspondence. CERN later inherited copyright interests in his scientific work from Franca Pauli’s estate.

## Wealth, Finances, and Property

The available record depicts Pauli as a salaried senior academic, not a businessman or independently wealthy industrial figure.

- His income derived primarily from university salary, visiting appointments, lecture fees, publications, prizes, and honors.
- His move to Princeton was supported institutionally through the Institute for Advanced Study.
- No evidence was found of substantial corporate holdings, speculative investments, hidden financial structures, tax litigation, bankruptcy, or financial misconduct.
- His Nobel Prize carried a monetary award, but no detailed reconstruction of its disposition was located.
- No major real-estate portfolio is documented in the consulted sources.
- His most consequential estate asset was his scientific archive and associated copyright, preserved by Franca Pauli and transferred to CERN.

## Political, Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### Politics

Pauli was not a party politician and held no elected office.

His family environment included his mother’s socialism, feminism, and pacifism. He opposed Nazism and was personally endangered by Nazi racial policy. His flight to the United States was forced by the European political situation rather than by a public political career.

He supported international scientific exchange after the war. No evidence was found that he belonged to a Communist organization, fascist organization, intelligence service, or political underground.

### Religion

Pauli was baptized Catholic but later became nonreligious in the institutional sense. He was sharply critical of doctrinal monotheism in some private reflections, while remaining fascinated by religious symbols and the history of mystical thought.

His mature outlook is most accurately described as a philosophically and psychologically informed search for unity between mind and matter, not adherence to an organized occult or religious movement.

### Philosophy of science

Pauli rejected both naïve materialism and a simple subjectivism. Central themes were:

- the inseparability of observation and description in quantum physics;
- complementarity;
- symmetry and invariance;
- limits of causal explanation;
- the role of unconscious images in scientific creativity;
- archetypal ordering principles;
- the possibility of a neutral domain underlying psyche and matter.

These ideas were exploratory. They did not become experimentally accepted physical theories of synchronicity.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Arnold Sommerfeld

Doctoral supervisor and mentor. Pauli treated Sommerfeld with unusual deference and gratitude. Sommerfeld’s confidence gave him access to the highest levels of theoretical physics while still a student.

### Niels Bohr

Mentor, close friend, and major correspondent. Pauli spent 1922–1923 in Copenhagen and remained engaged with Bohr’s complementarity program. Their exchanges shaped both atomic physics and interpretations of quantum theory.

### Werner Heisenberg

Friend, correspondent, collaborator, critic, and eventually estranged colleague. Pauli’s letters influenced Heisenberg during the creation of matrix mechanics and the uncertainty principle. Their final unified-field collaboration collapsed publicly in 1958.

### Albert Einstein

Einstein admired Pauli’s relativity article, supported his career, and nominated him for the Nobel Prize. They were colleagues at Princeton during the war but disagreed over quantum theory’s completeness.

### Max Born

Employer at Göttingen and important early professional sponsor. Born later ranked Pauli among the very greatest theoretical physicists.

### Paul Ehrenfest

Friend and joking rival. Their correspondence included a “Witzkrieg,” or war of jokes. Ehrenfest delivered the laudatory address when Pauli received the Lorentz Medal.

### Carl Gustav Jung

Psychiatrist, analyst, correspondent, and intellectual collaborator. Their association extended from Pauli’s crisis treatment to discussions of dreams, symbolism, synchronicity, number, physics, and epistemology.

### Erna Rosenbaum

Pauli’s first analyst in the Jungian circle. She received and discussed a large body of his dream reports. Her importance was often obscured when the material later became known principally through Jung.

### Enrico Fermi

Colleague in the quantum-physics network. Fermi named and incorporated the neutrino into beta-decay theory.

### Paul Dirac

Peer in the development of relativistic quantum theory and quantum fields. Pauli respected Dirac’s precision while criticizing work he considered overly formal or physically unclear.

### Erwin Schrödinger

Peer and interlocutor. Pauli helped establish the relationship between wave mechanics and matrix mechanics and participated in the broader debate over interpretation.

### Ralph Kronig, George Uhlenbeck, and Samuel Goudsmit

Associated with the discovery history of electron spin. Pauli initially rejected the literal spinning-electron hypothesis. His discouragement of Kronig became a recurring case study in the potentially inhibiting effect of elite scientific criticism.

### Students and assistants

Documented members of Pauli’s Zürich circle included Felix Bloch, Rudolf Peierls, Victor Weisskopf, Markus Fierz, Josef Jauch, Nicholas Kemmer, and Charles Enz. Several became prominent physicists and institution builders.

## Successes, Failures, and Abandoned Programs

### Major successes

- Precocious relativity synthesis.
- Exclusion principle.
- Hydrogen atom in matrix mechanics.
- Spin-\(\tfrac12\) formalism.
- Neutrino prediction.
- Spin–statistics theorem.
- Foundational quantum-field work.
- Long-term cultivation of younger theorists.
- Recognition through the Nobel Prize and major medals.

### Scientific failures or incomplete programs

- His doctoral treatment of the hydrogen molecular ion failed quantitatively because the old quantum theory was inadequate.
- He initially rejected electron spin as a classical model.
- His skepticism discouraged Kronig from publishing.
- He did not publish his 1953 non-Abelian gauge construction.
- He initially resisted parity violation.
- The Pauli–Heisenberg nonlinear unified-field theory failed.
- His hoped-for synthesis of psychology and physics remained programmatic and did not yield a testable accepted physical theory.

### Personal failures or crises

- Mother’s suicide and family rupture.
- Brief first marriage and divorce.
- Period of heavy drinking and disturbed behavior.
- Rejected Swiss naturalization applications.
- Final estrangement from Heisenberg.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Contested Material

### Harsh treatment of colleagues

**Documented and widely reported fact:** Pauli’s criticism could be devastating. He attacked unclear theories, interrupted speakers, and sent blunt assessments in letters. This helped produce his reputation as the “conscience of physics.”

**Context:** Colleagues also actively solicited his criticism because it was often technically exact and productive.

**No formal abuse finding:** No employment tribunal, professional misconduct adjudication, or court case concerning his treatment of colleagues was located.

### “Not even wrong”

The phrase “not even wrong” is conventionally attributed to Pauli as a judgment on a theory too incoherent to be falsifiable.

**Unverified attribution:** The general sentiment fits his documented style, but variants circulate without a secure original occasion or verbatim primary document.

### Kronig and electron spin

**Documented historical controversy:** Ralph Kronig considered electron spin before Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit published it. Pauli reacted skeptically, contributing to Kronig’s decision not to publish.

**What is not established:** There is no evidence Pauli stole Kronig’s idea. Pauli’s own exclusion principle and two-valuedness preceded the accepted spin account, but they were not identical to Kronig’s physical proposal.

### Alcohol and nightlife

**Reported fact:** During his crisis years Pauli drank heavily and frequented Zürich nightlife. Biographers connect these patterns to depression, unstable relationships, and his failed first marriage.

**Limits:** No evidence found of a criminal conviction, institutional expulsion, or medically documented addiction diagnosis in the modern clinical sense.

### Swiss naturalization

**Documented fact:** Zürich authorities rejected his applications in 1938 and 1940.

**Contested interpretation:** The official claim that he was insufficiently assimilated reflected assessment of conduct and integration. Later historians emphasize the xenophobic and antisemitic structure of Swiss naturalization policy. Both the personal-file material and the wider political context matter; the refusal was administrative, not a judicial finding of wrongdoing.

### Jung’s use of Pauli’s dreams

**Documented fact:** Jung used anonymized Pauli dream material in seminars and publications.

**Ethical question:** Later commentators have questioned the boundary between treatment and research and the degree of consent attached to the presentation of intimate psychological material. Pauli knew Jung was interested in the dreams and later collaborated intellectually with him, but modern standards of patient confidentiality should not be retroactively assumed to have been satisfied merely because his name was initially concealed.

### Paranormal and occult claims

**Documented fact:** Pauli took synchronicity, dreams, archetypes, and some parapsychological evidence seriously enough to investigate them.

**Not established:** No accepted experiment shows that Pauli possessed psychokinetic powers, caused apparatus failures, or demonstrated an acausal physical mechanism.

### Heisenberg dispute

**Documented fact:** Pauli withdrew from and mocked the 1957–1958 unified-field program after Heisenberg publicized it prematurely. Their relationship fractured.

**Subsequent assessment:** The proposed theory did not become a successful fundamental theory. Pauli’s criticism was therefore not merely personal, though the conflict included resentment over credit and public presentation.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Symbolic Narratives

### The Pauli effect

**Story:** Experimental apparatus supposedly failed whenever Pauli entered a laboratory or even visited the same town.

**Circulation:** The story circulated among physicists during Pauli’s lifetime. Otto Stern was said jokingly to have wanted Pauli kept away from his laboratory. George Gamow later helped popularize it.

**Evidence for:** Numerous anecdotes, including equipment failures timed to Pauli’s presence; Pauli himself enjoyed collecting such stories.

**Evidence against:** No controlled evidence establishes an anomalous causal effect. Equipment failures in early laboratories were common, and selection and retelling favor coincidences.

**Label:** Anecdote and scientific folklore, not an experimentally established phenomenon.

**Reputational effect:** It made Pauli an archetype of the brilliant but dangerous theoretician whose presence disrupts machinery—a mirror image of his intellectual ability to “break” theories.

### Room 137

**Story:** Pauli, fascinated by the inverse fine-structure constant near 137, was hospitalized and discovered that his room number was 137. He interpreted this as ominous and died there.

**Evidence:** Charles Enz and other biographical sources report that Pauli died in room 137 of Zürich’s Red Cross Hospital. The number’s importance to him is extensively documented.

**Uncertain elements:** Exact dialogue attributed to Pauli on noticing the room number varies in retelling. The coincidence has no demonstrated causal significance.

**Label:** Documented coincidence surrounded by later numerological legend.

### Obsession with 137

The inverse fine-structure constant was approximately 137 in the physics of Pauli’s time. Pauli regarded the unexplained dimensionless number as a profound problem connecting relativity, quantum theory, and perhaps psychological symbolism.

Later accounts sometimes portray this as occult numerology replacing physics. His correspondence shows something subtler: he distinguished established calculation from symbolic speculation, although he did pursue both with unusual intensity.

### “Spiritual heir” of Einstein

Einstein’s praise at the Princeton Nobel celebration encouraged the image of Pauli as his “spiritual son” or heir.

**Label:** Reported characterization, meaningful as intellectual genealogy but not a formal appointment or unique succession.

### “The conscience of physics”

This epithet reflects the frequency with which physicists sent Pauli unfinished ideas for judgment. It is reputational rather than an official title. It can function as hagiography when detached from cases in which his criticism was premature or inhibiting.

### Pauli as magician or psychokinetic agent

Later occult and popular-science literature sometimes depicts Pauli as unconsciously producing physical effects through archetypal energy.

**Evidence:** Coincidence narratives and Jungian interpretations.

**Against:** No reproducible evidence and no accepted physical model.

**Label:** Myth and speculative theory.

### The empty Titian painting

The empty rectangle Pauli sent Gamow in 1958 is documented in ETH’s archival presentation. The caption said it showed he could paint like Titian, with only technical details lacking.

The joke became a parable of Pauli’s demand that sweeping physical programs contain actual mathematics and testable results.

## Illness, Psychological Crisis, and Death

### Psychological health

Pauli’s crisis after 1927 included depression, interpersonal instability, heavy drinking, and distressing dreams. Treatment with Rosenbaum and Jung, followed by his marriage to Franca, coincided with greater stability.

The record does not justify retrospective assignment of a precise modern psychiatric diagnosis.

### Final illness

In 1958 Pauli’s health deteriorated. On 5 December he had to abandon a lecture because of severe pain. He was hospitalized at the Red Cross Hospital in Zürich.

Biographical accounts identify pancreatic cancer as the fatal disease. He underwent surgery but died on 15 December 1958.

- **Date:** 15 December 1958.
- **Place:** Red Cross Hospital, Zürich.
- **Reported room:** 137.
- **Cause:** pancreatic cancer and postoperative complications, as reported in the biographical literature.

## Works by Wolfgang Pauli

The list below emphasizes major identifiable works and editions. Pauli’s complete journal output, reviews, letters, lectures, and unpublished manuscripts is much larger and is catalogued through CERN and the *Scientific Correspondence* edition.

### Early and major scientific publications

- **1918 —** “Zur Theorie der Gravitation und der Elektrizität von Hermann Weyl.” Pauli’s first publication, examining Weyl’s extension of general relativity.
- **1921 —** *Über das Modell des Wasserstoffmolekülions*. Doctoral dissertation.
- **1921 —** “Relativitätstheorie,” in *Encyklopädie der mathematischen Wissenschaften*, vol. V, part 2, pp. 539–775.
  - English translation: *Theory of Relativity*, 1958.
  - Genuine sole authorship; later book editions are translations or reprints.
- **1923 —** Work on the periodic system and atomic spectra, including the normal Zeeman effect.
- **1925 —** “Über den Zusammenhang des Abschlusses der Elektronengruppen im Atom mit der Komplexstruktur der Spektren,” *Zeitschrift für Physik* 31, 765–783.
  - Original exclusion-principle paper.
- **1926 —** “Über das Wasserstoffspektrum vom Standpunkt der neuen Quantenmechanik.”
  - Matrix-mechanical solution of hydrogen.
- **1926 —** “Quantentheorie,” in *Handbuch der Physik*.
  - Major survey of the old quantum theory.
- **1927 —** “Zur Quantenmechanik des magnetischen Elektrons,” *Zeitschrift für Physik* 43, 601–623.
  - Pauli equation and matrices.
- **1928 —** With Pascual Jordan, “Zur Quantenelektrodynamik ladungsfreier Felder.”
  - Genuine co-authorship.
- **1933 —** “Die allgemeinen Prinzipien der Wellenmechanik,” in *Handbuch der Physik*.
  - Extensively revised in later editions; English edition published posthumously as *General Principles of Quantum Mechanics*.
- **1939 —** With Markus Fierz, papers on relativistic wave equations and arbitrary-spin fields.
- **1940 —** “The Connection Between Spin and Statistics.”
- **1941 —** With Victor Weisskopf, work on scalar-field self-energy and quantum electrodynamics.
- **1943 —** With Albert Einstein, work concerning generalized Kaluza-type field theory.
- **1946 —** “Remarks on the History of the Exclusion Principle,” *Science* 103, 213–215.
- **1946/1948 —** *Meson Theory of Nuclear Forces*. Revised second edition, 1948.
- **1949 —** With Felix Villars, “On the Invariant Regularization in Relativistic Quantum Theory.”
  - Pauli–Villars regularization.
- **1952 —** “Der Einfluss archetypischer Vorstellungen auf die Bildung naturwissenschaftlicher Theorien bei Kepler,” in C. G. Jung and W. Pauli, *Naturerklärung und Psyche*.
  - Genuine Pauli essay in a jointly published volume.
- **1955 —** “Exclusion Principle, Lorentz Group and Reflection of Space-Time and Charge,” in Pauli, ed., *Niels Bohr and the Development of Physics*.
- **1955 —** With Gunnar Källén, work on the mathematical structure of the Lee model.
- **1957 —** “Zur älteren und neueren Geschichte des Neutrinos.”
- **1957–1958 —** With Werner Heisenberg, papers and communications on a nonlinear spinor-field theory.
  - Genuine collaboration, later repudiated in substantial part by Pauli.
- **1958 —** Work with Bruno Touschek on particle symmetries and transformations.

### Edited volumes

- **1955 —** *Niels Bohr and the Development of Physics: Essays Dedicated to Niels Bohr on the Occasion of His Seventieth Birthday*.
  - Edited by Pauli; individual chapters were written by multiple contributors.

### Posthumous scholarly collections

- **1961 —** *Aufsätze und Vorträge über Physik und Erkenntnistheorie*.
  - Posthumous edited collection.
- **1964 —** *Collected Scientific Papers*, 2 vols., edited by Ralph Kronig and Victor F. Weisskopf.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Pauli’s scientific reputation rests on several achievements independently capable of defining a major career: the exclusion principle, spin formalism, neutrino hypothesis, spin–statistics theorem, and quantum-field contributions.

The exclusion principle explains the electronic organization of atoms and the periodic table. Through fermionic statistics it underlies chemical structure, electron degeneracy pressure, white dwarfs, neutron stars, metals, semiconductors, and much of condensed-matter physics.

The Pauli matrices remain ubiquitous in quantum mechanics, particle physics, quantum computing, and mathematical physics. Pauli–Villars regularization remains a canonical historical method. The Fierz–Pauli framework is still cited in studies of massive higher-spin fields and gravity.

His prediction of the neutrino became a model of theoretical inference: an unseen entity proposed to protect deeply supported conservation laws and later experimentally observed.

His correspondence established a second legacy. Because he often communicated major insights privately, the edited letters are essential sources for the collective creation of quantum mechanics. They also reveal how scientific judgment, rivalry, humor, dreams, symbolism, and personal crisis interacted.

His Jungian work divides his reputation:

- historians treat it as genuine evidence about scientific creativity and the intellectual culture of twentieth-century physics;
- depth psychologists treat Pauli as an important participant in the development of synchronicity;
- some popular accounts overstate the case by implying that quantum mechanics validates paranormal causation;
- skeptics sometimes make the opposite error of treating all of Pauli’s philosophical work as irrelevant occultism.

Institutionally, his legacy is maintained by CERN’s Pauli Archive, the ETH Pauli lectures and archival collections, scholarly editions of his correspondence, and continuing historical research.

A lunar crater and asteroid **13093 Wolfgangpauli** bear his name.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are listed. Mere shared appearance in twentieth-century intellectual history is excluded.

- **Albert Einstein — documented fact.** Einstein admired Pauli’s 1921 relativity survey; they corresponded, overlapped at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton during the Second World War, and disagreed over quantum mechanics. Einstein nominated Pauli for the Nobel Prize and spoke at the Princeton Nobel celebration in December 1945.

- **Niels Bohr — documented fact.** Pauli worked at Bohr’s institute in Copenhagen in 1922–1923, became a close correspondent and ally in quantum theory, attended conferences with him, and edited *Niels Bohr and the Development of Physics* for Bohr’s seventieth birthday in 1955.

- **Carl Gustav Jung — documented fact.** Pauli consulted Jung in Zürich beginning in 1932, underwent analysis first through Erna Rosenbaum and then Jung, corresponded with Jung for roughly twenty-five years, and jointly published *Naturerklärung und Psyche* in 1952.

- **Werner Heisenberg — documented fact.** Friend, correspondent, and collaborator from the early quantum-mechanics period. Pauli’s criticism influenced Heisenberg’s work. They jointly pursued a nonlinear unified-field theory in 1957–1958 before a public rupture.

- **Erwin Rudolf Josef Alexander Schrödinger — documented fact.** Pauli engaged with Schrödinger’s wave mechanics, helped establish its equivalence with matrix mechanics, and participated with him in Copenhagen and Solvay discussions.

- **Paul Adrien Maurice Dirac — documented fact.** Pauli and Dirac were leading members of the same quantum-theory network, met at major conferences including Solvay, corresponded, and contributed independently and interactively to relativistic quantum theory and field quantization.

- **Enrico Fermi — documented fact.** Pauli met Fermi in the interwar conference network, including Como in 1927. Fermi incorporated Pauli’s neutral-particle hypothesis into beta-decay theory and gave the particle the name *neutrino*.

- **J. Robert Oppenheimer — documented fact, limited.** Both belonged to the advanced theoretical-physics network and were associated with Princeton-era American physics. Pauli did not join Oppenheimer’s Los Alamos weapons project. No evidence of a close personal partnership was located.

- **John von Neumann — documented institutional connection, limited.** Both were members of the Institute for Advanced Study community during Pauli’s Princeton period. The consulted sources do not establish a major joint project.

- **Maria Salomea Skłodowska-Curie — documented fact.** Pauli and Curie both attended the Fifth Solvay Conference in Brussels in October 1927.

- **Satyendra Nath Bose — documented intellectual connection.** Bose’s statistics and Pauli’s exclusion principle became the two contrasting statistical classes later linked to integer and half-integer spin. No close personal relationship was established in the consulted sources.

- **J. Robert Oppenheimer, Amalie Emmy Noether, Richard Feynman, Edward Witten, Alan Turing, and other roster figures sometimes placed in broad histories of modern physics:** no sufficiently specific meeting, correspondence, collaboration, or acknowledged personal influence was established here beyond the Oppenheimer institutional overlap stated above.

- **Absence as finding:** No documentary Pauli connection was found with the overwhelming majority of the 896-person atlas roster. Shared period, city, university tradition, philosophical theme, or later comparison was not treated as a connection.
