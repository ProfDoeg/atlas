# Benkos Biohó: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Benkos Biohó is a documented early-seventeenth-century maroon leader, but the surviving record is fragmentary and overwhelmingly produced by Spanish colonial officials who regarded him as an enslaved fugitive, military adversary, and threat to public order. No known autobiographical writing, portrait, birth record, African record, testimony in his own words, or securely identified personal correspondence survives.

His documented biography must therefore be reconstructed from:

- Spanish administrative correspondence and reports, especially letters sent by Cartagena’s governors to the Crown.
- Later transcriptions of those records, notably those assembled by Roberto Arrázola.
- Colonial chronicles, especially Fray Pedro Simón.
- Twentieth- and twenty-first-century historical, anthropological, linguistic, literary, and community-memory scholarship.
- Palenquero oral tradition and later literary elaboration.

This creates several distinct evidentiary layers:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary or near-contemporary colonial records.
- **Reported fact:** stated in reputable later scholarship but not independently verified here in the original manuscript.
- **Probable reconstruction:** consistent with the evidence but not explicitly documented.
- **Disputed claim:** supported by competing interpretations.
- **Oral tradition / legend / literary elaboration:** historically important but not demonstrated by contemporary records.
- **Unverified claim:** repeated in popular biographies without an identified evidentiary foundation.

The most consequential distinction is between Biohó’s documented Palenque de la Matuna, active during his lifetime, and the later Palenque de San Miguel Arcángel—eventually San Basilio de Palenque—whose first unequivocal documentary appearance belongs to the late seventeenth or early eighteenth century. Biohó is securely the founder or principal leader of Matuna and the spiritual and political ancestor of the regional maroon tradition. His literal foundation of the present settlement of San Basilio is disputed and, in the strict chronological form commonly repeated, improbable.

### The name “Benkos Biohó”

“Domingo Biohó” is the form closest to contemporary Spanish usage. “Domingo” was probably a Christian name assigned through baptism, sale, or enslavement. “Biohó” may record a place of origin, an ethnonym, a personal name, or a colonial rendering of a West African designation.

“Benkos” is less securely established in the surviving early documentation than “Domingo” or “Dominguillo.” It became the dominant commemorative name in later Colombian history and Palenquero memory. Modern spellings should not be mistaken for a stable seventeenth-century orthography.

“Arcabuco” in colonial Spanish meant dense, tangled, or heavily wooded country. “King of the Arcabuco” consequently designated sovereignty over the forest refuge, not necessarily a formal hereditary African title.

## Family and Ancestry

### African origin

**Reported fact / probable reconstruction:** Biohó came from the Upper Guinea or Guinea-Bissau region, probably from the Bijagós archipelago. The Banco de la República’s account cautiously places him in a Guinean archipelagic region and connects the name with the Bidyogo/Bijagó population.

The Bijagós were well known in early European descriptions as island navigators and fighters. Such background has been used to explain Biohó’s apparent facility in swamp, forest, and waterborne warfare. That explanation is plausible but remains retrospective: no known contemporary Cartagena document supplies a detailed ethnographic biography of him.

### Claims of royal ancestry

**Claim in colonial discourse:** Biohó said, or was said, to have been a king in Africa.

**Later tradition:** He was a hereditary prince or king captured with his entire royal family.

**Evidence:** Colonial officials unquestionably called him “rey” in the American context, and his political authority among the maroons was real. The stronger proposition—that he belonged to an identifiable royal dynasty in the Bijagós Islands—has not been demonstrated from a surviving African or contemporary commercial record.

The royal-origin story could preserve genuine memory. It could also arise from:

- His own political self-presentation.
- Spanish interpretations of African leadership.
- His adoption of kingship after creating the palenque.
- Later heroic and nationalist narrative.

The sources do not permit a final choice among these possibilities.

### Wife and children

Later accounts name:

- **Wiwa**, described as his wife and queen.
- **Sando** or **Sandro Biohó**, described as a son or heir.
- **Orika**, described as a daughter or princess.

**Evidentiary status:** predominantly **legendary or literary**. Modern retellings frequently present the family as historical fact, but scholarship on the development of the Biohó legend traces the elaborated Wiwa–Sando–Orika family narrative to Camilo Delgado’s early-twentieth-century legend “El Rey del Arcabuco,” written under the pseudonym Doctor Arcos. Delgado reportedly drew upon Cartagena oral traditions, but this is not equivalent to contemporary documentation.

The Museo Nacional’s commemorative account says Biohó’s wife and children continued or repopulated palenques after his death. That is a **reported historical claim**, but the identities and careers of the relatives remain insufficiently established.

### Orika and Francisco de Campos

The most elaborate family story says that Orika fell in love with Francisco de Campos, a white Spanish captive—often made the son of Biohó’s former enslaver or of a governor—helped him escape, and was condemned to death for betraying the palenque.

**Status:** legend and literary narrative, not documented biography. It became especially prominent through Camilo Delgado and Germán Espinosa’s novel *Orika de los palenques*.

## Childhood and Early Life

No reliable information survives about Biohó’s childhood, date of birth, parents, siblings, education, initiation, or exact home community.

Later biographical narratives describe him as growing up within Bijagó aristocratic and military culture and receiving training in navigation and warfare. These accounts may reconstruct general features of Bijagó society rather than facts specifically established for Biohó.

The frequently cited approximate date “c. 1570” is a modern estimate. Published alternatives range from about 1550 to about 1580. None rests on a known birth document.

---

## Education and Formation

No formal teachers or educational institutions are documented.

His later career nevertheless demonstrates several capabilities:

- Organizing and maintaining a fortified settlement.
- Commanding fighters in difficult terrain.
- Conducting negotiations with colonial officials.
- Maintaining intelligence networks between Cartagena, rural estates, and the palenque.
- Managing relations among Africans and Afro-descendants of different origins.
- Using symbolic authority, including kingship.
- Adapting to Spanish urban practices sufficiently to enter Cartagena, dress “a la española,” and carry arms under the truce.

Whether he spoke Portuguese before arriving in Cartagena is unknown. Portuguese and Portuguese-based contact speech were widely used in Atlantic slave-trading networks, while Spanish became essential in Cartagena. The surviving Palenquero language developed later as a Spanish-lexified creole with significant African grammatical influence; it should not be simplistically described as Biohó’s personal language or invention.

No documentary basis exists for claims that he devised a secret intelligence language, although palenque networks undoubtedly depended on clandestine communication.

## Enslavement and Atlantic Passage

### Capture in Africa

Popular biographies say Biohó was seized with Wiwa, Sando, and Orika in the Bijagós Islands and delivered to the Portuguese slave trader Pedro Gomes Reinel.

**Status:** partly plausible Atlantic context combined with unverified individual detail.

Pedro Gomes Reinel was a major Portuguese holder of the Spanish asiento for the supply of enslaved Africans during the 1590s. Biohó could have entered the Cartagena market through networks connected with Reinel, but a securely identified shipping or sale record for him has not been produced in the sources consulted.

The mechanism of capture is also unknown. Possibilities include warfare, raiding, kidnapping, judicial enslavement, or sale through African and Luso-African commercial networks. Statements that European sailors personally kidnapped the entire royal family exceed the evidence.

### Middle Passage and arrival

Biohó was transported involuntarily across the Atlantic and sold in Cartagena, one of Spanish America’s principal slave-trading ports.

Dates commonly given include:

- **1596:** often presented as the year he was sold in Cartagena.
- **1599:** often given as the year of his escape or rebellion.
- **Before 1600:** the safest general formulation.
- **By 1603:** unquestionably active as leader of a large maroon community in the surviving reconstruction.

### Enslavers

Later historical accounts name:

1. **Juan de Palacios**, sometimes described as his first purchaser in Cartagena.
2. **Captain Alonso del Campo** or **Alonso de Campos**, said to have acquired him afterward.

Spellings and roles vary. Some retellings conflate Alonso de Campos with figures in the Orika legend. The ownership sequence is a **reported fact in later historiography**, not independently verified here through a bill of sale.

### First escape

A familiar story says that Biohó escaped while being transported on the Magdalena River, after the boat sank, but was recaptured.

**Status:** reported tradition; documentary basis uncertain.

### Second escape and collective revolt

Most reconstructions place the decisive escape in **1599 or 1600**. Numbers vary:

- Ten or eleven companions in some accounts.
- Thirteen people plus Biohó and his family in the Colombian Truth Commission’s historical synthesis.
- Approximately thirty fugitives in other public and institutional histories.

The variation probably reflects different episodes, source readings, or the later merging of Biohó’s original band with the population that gathered around him.

## Chronological Life History

## Before 1600: From Guinea to Cartagena

Biohó was born somewhere in the Guinea or Guinea-Bissau region and entered the Atlantic slave trade during the late sixteenth century. His age at transportation is unknown.

He arrived at Cartagena, was sold, and received or used the Christian name Domingo. The absence of a verified embarkation record prevents identification of his ship, African port, exact date of arrival, or sale price.

Cartagena was both a slave-trading entrepôt and a fortified imperial port. Enslaved labor was used in domestic service, rural estates, transport, construction, maritime activity, and the regional redistribution of captives. Later tradition associates Biohó with galley or boat labor, but his exact occupation while enslaved is not securely known.

## 1599–1600: Escape and formation of a maroon band

Biohó escaped with a group of enslaved people and moved toward the wetlands and forest south or southeast of Cartagena.

The colonial geographic term **La Matuna** referred to an area of swamp, channels, and dense vegetation. Such terrain obstructed mounted troops and firearms while favoring fighters experienced in movement through water and forest.

The fugitives established a fortified settlement or palenque. “Palenque” referred both to the defensive stockade and, by extension, to a community of escaped enslaved people.

## 1600–1603: Growth of Matuna

By 1603 Biohó’s movement had become a serious military and political problem for Cartagena. One reconstruction describes approximately sixty maroons under “Dominguillo Biohó” who, after an early attack, rebuilt a strong wooden stockade in a swamp surrounded by forest.

The palenque expanded through:

- New fugitives leaving Cartagena and surrounding estates.
- Intelligence supplied by enslaved or free contacts who remained outside.
- Food production, hunting, fishing, and livestock.
- Raids against estates, travelers, settlements, and livestock.
- Possible tribute or “gifts” supplied by neighboring landholders seeking immunity from attack.
- Capture or incorporation of other persons.

This was both a community of refuge and an armed power. It cannot be reduced either to a purely peaceful village or to a band of raiders.

## Military and political organization

Later historical syntheses describe Matuna as having:

- Biohó as king.
- A queen.
- A lieutenant of war.
- An alguacil or law-enforcement officer.
- A treasurer.
- A religious leader.
- Captains and warriors.

The outline is plausible and some offices may derive from documentary description, but modern presentations sometimes combine early evidence with later Palenquero institutions.

Biohó’s use of kingship challenged colonial status ordering. Colonial officials sometimes called him “Dominguillo,” a diminutive that may have expressed contempt, while simultaneously acknowledging the seriousness of his authority.

## Raids and coercion

Colonial and later sources attribute several practices to Biohó’s maroons:

- Stealing or requisitioning cattle.
- Attacking and burning rural estates.
- Robbing travelers or settlements.
- Encouraging enslaved people to flee.
- Maintaining agents inside Cartagena.
- Abducting enslaved African women and, according to later summaries, Indigenous women.

**Evidentiary caution:** The general pattern of armed raiding and liberation of enslaved people is well supported by the nature of the conflict. Particular accusations came from slaveholders and colonial officials with direct interests in destroying the palenques. They should not be discarded, but neither should every hostile allegation be accepted without corroboration.

Abduction of women deserves separate notice. Some scholarship argues that predominantly male fugitive bands seized women to establish demographically sustainable settlements. This is presented as a historical practice, not merely propaganda, but the dossier found no surviving judicial testimony from an identified woman concerning Biohó personally.

## 1602–1604: Colonial expeditions

Spanish authorities organized armed expeditions against Matuna. In one recorded operation, troops entered chest-deep swamp and mud before engaging the maroons. The defenders used thrown lances and arrows. Spanish arquebuses eventually forced a withdrawal.

An African standard-bearer reportedly died holding the palenque’s flag, and Biohó was wounded. The episode demonstrates that Matuna possessed:

- Defensive works.
- Guards or sentinels.
- An organized military command.
- A banner or symbol of collective authority.
- Knowledge of terrain sufficient to impose serious difficulty on Spanish troops.

The expeditions did not permanently eliminate the community.

## 1604: Turbana and regional pressure

A letter associated with Governor Jerónimo de Suazo, dated 25 January 1604 in later transcription, says the maroons had rebuilt a strong palenque and attacked the Indigenous settlement of Turbana, apparently using a maritime or water route.

**Status:** reported from a transcription of colonial correspondence.

The episode indicates that Biohó’s operations reached beyond passive defense. It also complicates later heroic narratives: his forces could prey upon Indigenous communities as well as attack slaveholders and colonial troops.

## 1605: War, negotiation, and provisional peace

After repeated failures to suppress Matuna, Governor **Jerónimo de Suazo y Casasola** negotiated or offered terms. A date of **18 July 1605** is often given for the agreement or offer.

Terms attributed to the settlement include:

- Recognition or toleration of the maroons’ freedom and residence.
- Permission for Biohó and his people to enter Cartagena.
- Permission for Biohó to dress in Spanish fashion and carry a sword or dagger.
- A promise by the maroons to stop attacks.
- A promise not to receive additional fugitives or assist escapes.
- A demand that Biohó cease presenting himself as king.

Some scholarship describes the 1605 arrangement as informal, incomplete, or limited to one year. Other sources say a durable truce was not concluded until 1612 or 1613 under Governor Diego Fernández de Velasco. The disagreement probably reflects several stages of negotiation rather than one final treaty.

Calling the arrangement a “treaty with the Spanish Crown” can overstate its legal form. The evidence more securely supports a negotiated local peace or truce with Cartagena’s government.

## 1605–1612/1613: Uneasy coexistence

During the truce period, Biohó traveled into Cartagena under recognized privileges. He acquired a conspicuous public presence, dressed in Spanish clothing, and carried a blade. Maroons could trade or conduct business in the city.

The arrangement benefited both sides:

- Colonial authorities avoided expensive and ineffective military campaigns.
- Slaveholders gained some protection from raids.
- Biohó’s people enjoyed practical freedom and mobility.
- The palenque preserved internal authority.

The settlement contained an inherent contradiction. Colonial society demanded that the palenque stop taking in fugitives, while the community’s political legitimacy and security were tied to resistance against enslavement. Biohó’s prestige among enslaved Africans inside Cartagena remained threatening even during peace.

## 1612–1613: Consolidated truce

A more formal peace is variously dated to **1612** or **1613**, during the governorship of **Diego Fernández de Velasco**.

The exact relationship between this agreement and the 1605 negotiations is disputed. A cautious reconstruction is:

1. Military stalemate produced an offer or provisional accommodation in 1605.
2. Negotiations and intermittent conflict continued.
3. A more stable settlement was reached approximately 1612–1613.

No evidence shows that the agreement made Matuna an independent state in the modern international-law sense. It did, however, create de facto autonomy and recognized freedom for at least part of Biohó’s community.

## 1613–1619: Biohó in Cartagena

Biohó’s ability to enter Cartagena was both a success and a vulnerability. His clothing and right to bear a weapon made his negotiated rank visible in a city structured by slavery and racial hierarchy.

Governor García Girón later explained that Biohó’s prestige was dangerous because, in the governor’s words, through “embustes y encantos” he drew after him the “nations of Guinea” in the city. The words are colonial accusations, not proof of literal deception or sorcery. They reveal official fear of his charismatic and organizing power.

## 1619: Arrest

Biohó was arrested in Cartagena, apparently during the night. Some accounts date the seizure to 1619; a later commemorative narrative specifies the night of 15 March immediately before his 1621 execution, which may conflate arrest and execution.

The circumstances remain obscure. Explanations include:

- Violation of the peace agreement.
- An altercation with members of the city guard.
- Official suspicion that he continued to encourage escape.
- Political determination by García Girón to remove a rival source of authority.
- A deliberate betrayal of safe-conduct conditions.

The consulted evidence establishes colonial detention and execution but not a transparent criminal proceeding. No complete trial record, defense, witness list, or sentence has been identified.

## 1621: Execution

On **16 March 1621**, Biohó was hanged in Cartagena. Several accounts say he was then drawn and quartered or his body dismembered for exemplary display.

Governor García Girón reported the death to the king in a letter dated **28 March 1621**. The execution was political as well as penal: it removed a leader whose authority survived despite negotiated peace and whose presence in Cartagena demonstrated limits on slaveholding power.

Fray Pedro Simón has sometimes been cited for a date of 1619. Modern documentary histories generally prefer 16 March 1621 because of García Girón’s near-contemporary letter. The date “1630,” occasionally found in commemorative publications, is erroneous.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

Biohó owned no documented colonial company, registered estate, patent, or formal investment.

### Atlantic commercial institutions affecting his life

His enslavement belonged to the Portuguese–Spanish Atlantic trafficking system governed by royal licenses and the **asiento de negros**. Pedro Gomes Reinel, named in later accounts of Biohó’s sale, held an important asiento during the 1590s.

### Palenque de la Matuna as an economic institution

Matuna was simultaneously:

- A fortified settlement.
- A subsistence economy.
- A military base.
- A destination for fugitives.
- An intelligence network.
- A political jurisdiction.
- A participant in exchange with surrounding rural and urban society.

Economic resources reportedly included:

- Crops and garden production.
- Fishing and hunting.
- Cattle and other livestock.
- Goods seized in raids.
- Trade with Cartagena.
- Tribute or gifts from neighboring estates.

There are no reliable figures for Biohó’s personal wealth. Describing palenque resources as his private property would impose an unsupported modern distinction between royal, communal, and personal assets.

### Military technology

The maroons used:

- Wooden stockades.
- Swamp and forest as defensive infrastructure.
- Sentinels.
- Lances, thrown spears, bows, and arrows.
- Water routes and possibly boats.
- Mobile, dispersed, or guerrilla-style tactics.
- Intelligence from sympathizers and enslaved contacts.

Spanish forces possessed firearms, including arquebuses, but were hindered by mud, water, vegetation, disease risk, and poor knowledge of the terrain.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Political system

Biohó constructed authority beyond the categories colonial society assigned to him. His kingship could simultaneously draw upon:

- African political memory.
- A practical wartime command structure.
- Iberian monarchical vocabulary understood by colonial interlocutors.
- A claim to equality in negotiation with the governor.
- Religious or charismatic prestige.

His politics combined armed resistance with negotiated coexistence. He did not simply reject all contact with Cartagena: under favorable terms, his people entered the city, traded, and made use of the colonial economy.

### Political objectives

The securely reconstructable objectives were:

- Freedom from re-enslavement.
- Security of the palenque.
- Recognition of his community’s residence.
- Protection of internal leadership.
- Mobility and exchange with Cartagena.
- Continued capacity to attract or liberate fugitives, despite colonial demands to stop doing so.

There is insufficient evidence for a written program of universal abolition. Biohó’s movement nevertheless directly undermined slavery by creating a durable refuge and encouraging flight.

### Religion

No securely documented personal creed survives.

Later narratives associate him with African spiritual authority or accuse him of “enchantments.” García Girón’s language about “embustes y encantos” may refer to persuasion, ritual prestige, supposed sorcery, or colonial rhetoric used to discredit African leadership.

Claims that Biohó served as a priest, diviner, or founder of specific surviving Palenquero rites are unverified.

### Literacy and intellectual production

No evidence establishes that he could read or write. His diplomacy could have been conducted orally or through intermediaries.

His historical “thought” must be inferred from action rather than texts: self-government, armed defense, negotiated freedom, and the symbolic use of kingship.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Jerónimo de Suazo y Casasola

**Documented political adversary and negotiating counterpart.** As governor of Cartagena, Suazo oversaw unsuccessful military operations and the 1605 peace initiative.

### Diego Fernández de Velasco

**Reported negotiating counterpart.** Associated with the more durable 1612–1613 truce.

### García Girón

**Documented final adversary.** Governor of Cartagena who ordered or defended Biohó’s execution and reported it to the Spanish Crown.

### Alonso del Campo / Alonso de Campos

**Reported enslaver.** Later histories identify him as a purchaser or owner of Biohó before the escape.

### Juan de Palacios

**Reported enslaver or merchant.** Said to have purchased Biohó before transferring him to Alonso de Campos.

### Pedro Gomes Reinel

**Reported commercial connection.** Portuguese slave-trade contractor through whose network Biohó may have entered Spanish America. A direct transaction remains unverified.

### Wiwa, Sando, and Orika

**Legendary family network.** Central to later memory, literature, and visual representation; contemporary proof remains lacking.

### Matuna’s captains, standard-bearer, spies, and residents

**Documented collective network, individual names mostly lost.** Colonial reporting confirms organized defenders, messengers, and supporters. Their anonymity is largely a product of an archive interested in suppressing the movement rather than recording its participants’ biographies.

### Enslaved Africans in Cartagena

**Documented or strongly reported political constituency.** García Girón’s alarm about Biohó drawing the “nations of Guinea” after him indicates that his influence crossed the palenque’s boundary.

### Indigenous communities

Relations were not uniformly allied. The reported attack on Turbana and allegations of abduction demonstrate conflict between some maroon forces and Indigenous communities. No evidence supports a general Biohó–Indigenous alliance.

## Successes and Failures

### Significant successes

- Escaped enslavement after at least one possible failed attempt.
- Built or led a defensible maroon settlement in Matuna.
- Defeated or survived repeated Spanish expeditions.
- Attracted a substantial population and intelligence network.
- Established recognizable political and military institutions.
- Forced Cartagena’s government into negotiations.
- Obtained practical freedom, mobility, and toleration for his people.
- Entered Cartagena visibly armed and dressed as a free political leader.
- Created a model and symbol for later palenques.

### Significant failures or limits

- Did not eliminate slavery in Cartagena or the surrounding province.
- Could not prevent Spanish military penetration of Matuna.
- Was wounded in at least one confrontation.
- Peace terms reportedly required the palenque to refuse new fugitives, contradicting the movement’s liberating function.
- His urban access exposed him to arrest.
- He was captured and executed.
- The documentary record does not demonstrate uninterrupted institutional continuity between his Matuna and present-day San Basilio.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Coercion

## Colonial legal status

Under Spanish colonial law, Biohó was treated as an enslaved fugitive and rebel. His conduct exposed him to severe corporal and capital punishment.

That legal classification is a documented feature of the slave regime; it is not evidence that enslavement itself was legitimate by modern standards, nor does it settle the facts of individual criminal allegations.

## Raiding, robbery, and livestock seizure

**Documented general pattern / particular details sometimes unverified.** Biohó’s forces attacked estates and settlements, seized livestock and supplies, and threatened colonial roads and production.

Colonial documents describe such conduct as robbery, banditry, and damage to property. From the palenque’s position, it supplied a community excluded from lawful ownership and exchange and formed part of the war against slaveholders. Both descriptions concern the same underlying acts but assign them different political meaning.

## Attack on Turbana

**Reported from colonial correspondence.** Biohó’s force reportedly attacked the Indigenous settlement of Turbana around 1603–1604. The incident is significant because it resists a simplified binary story of Africans and Indigenous people acting together against Spaniards.

The exact casualties, property losses, and motives are not established in the consulted material.

## Abduction of women

**Historical allegation repeated in scholarship.** Some accounts say maroon bands kidnapped Indigenous and enslaved African women because women were underrepresented in the fugitive population.

No identified trial record or first-person testimony establishes Biohó’s personal participation in a specific abduction. The allegation should therefore be retained as a serious reported feature of regional maroon warfare, not transformed into a proved individual offense.

## Tribute and protection payments

**Reported practice.** Neighboring estates may have supplied goods to Matuna to avoid raids. This has been described as informal tribute and could also be characterized as coercive protection.

No account book or reliable estimate of amounts survives in the sources reviewed.

## Violation of the peace

Colonial authorities accused the maroons of continuing to attract fugitives and undermine slavery. Conversely, Biohó’s arrest while operating under negotiated privileges can be understood as a colonial violation or repudiation of the settlement.

The surviving record is not a neutral judicial determination of which party first breached the agreement.

## “Embustes y encantos”

García Girón’s allegation that Biohó used “lies and enchantments” to draw Africans after him is a **documented colonial accusation**, not a demonstrated fact.

Possible meanings include:

- Charismatic persuasion.
- African religious authority.
- Oath-taking or ritual.
- Spanish fear of sorcery.
- A rhetorical attempt to portray political organizing as deception.

## Absence of modern financial or personal scandals

The dedicated adversarial search combined Biohó’s name with terms for controversy, scandal, accusation, fraud, corruption, conspiracy, criticism, kidnapping, and crime, including 2024–2026 material.

It produced no evidence of modern revelations concerning the seventeenth-century individual. Many results concerned:

- Israel Alberto Zúñiga Iriarte, a former FARC member and Colombian senator who used **Benkos Biohó** as a nom de guerre.
- An ELN unit called the **Frente Benkos Biohó**.
- A Colombian hospital ship named **Benkos Biohó**.
- Unrelated European businessman René Benko.

Those controversies do not concern the historical Benkos Biohó and must not be attributed to him.

The substantive modern controversy is historiographical: whether public commemoration has conflated Matuna with San Basilio and converted later literary family stories into biography.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## “Founder of San Basilio de Palenque”

### The story

Biohó escaped around 1599–1600, founded a settlement in 1603, negotiated its freedom, and thereby established the present San Basilio de Palenque—the “first free town in the Americas.”

### Appearance and circulation

The narrative is embedded in:

- Palenquero community memory.
- San Basilio’s hymn.
- Public monuments.
- Educational materials.
- Tourism and heritage promotion.
- Roberto Arrázola’s influential 1970 history.
- Anthropological and literary works.
- Colombian state commemoration.

Camilo Delgado’s “El Rey del Arcabuco,” published in the early twentieth century, was important in giving the story a coherent literary form.

### Evidence for

- Biohó unquestionably founded or led an early palenque in the same regional maroon tradition.
- His resistance established political and military precedents followed by later communities.
- Regional palenques moved, dissolved, merged, and reconstituted; population or cultural continuity is possible even where settlement continuity cannot be proved.
- San Basilio’s own historical identity consciously treats him as founder.

### Evidence against a literal founding

- Biohó died in 1621.
- The first unequivocal documentation of the Palenque de San Miguel Arcángel, predecessor of San Basilio, belongs around 1713.
- María Cristina Navarrete and subsequent scholarship distinguish Matuna from San Miguel/San Basilio.
- The 1713 agreement involved leaders and mediators who lived nearly a century after Biohó.
- Documentary work identifies **Domingo Criollo** or **Nicolás de Santa Rosa** more directly with the later settlement’s political formation.

### Assessment

**Disputed claim.** Biohó is securely the founder of Matuna and reasonably described as San Basilio’s spiritual, political, or remembered founder. Literal identity between his settlement and the present town is not established.

## “First free town in the Americas”

### The story

San Basilio was the first free African town in the Americas, created by Biohó in 1603 and legally recognized by Spain.

### Problems

- Maroon settlements existed elsewhere before or contemporaneously.
- “Free,” “town,” “recognized,” and “independent” are not equivalent categories.
- Biohó’s 1605/1613 accommodation and San Miguel’s 1713 settlement were separate episodes.
- The 1713 compact imposed obligations: refusing new fugitives, accepting Christian instruction and a priest, using passes outside the settlement, and providing services to colonial authority.

### Assessment

**Commemorative and heritage designation with a real documentary core, but often chronologically compressed.** UNESCO more cautiously calls San Basilio “one of the first free African towns in the Americas” and emphasizes its survival as a unique cultural space.

## Biohó as African prince or king

### The story

He was born into Bijagó royalty and was already a king before enslavement.

### Evidence

- Reports that he claimed African kingship.
- Colonial acknowledgment of him as “king” in Matuna.
- Possible preservation of African status through oral memory.

### Counterevidence

- No verified African genealogy, royal list, birth record, or contemporary sale document.
- “King” may describe authority acquired after escape.
- Later heroic literature amplified royal motifs.

### Assessment

**Possible but unverified.**

## The entire royal family captured together

### The story

Benkos, Queen Wiwa, Prince Sando, and Princess Orika were captured together and sold in Cartagena.

### Evidence

Later oral and literary tradition.

### Counterevidence

No identified contemporary record naming this complete family.

### Assessment

**Legend / unverified claim.**

## Orika’s forbidden love and execution

### The story

Orika fell in love with the Spanish captive Francisco de Campos, helped him escape, and accepted death rather than renounce her love.

### Origin and circulation

The story is closely associated with Camilo Delgado’s “El Rey del Arcabuco” and was subsequently developed in Germán Espinosa’s *Orika de los palenques*.

### Assessment

**Literary legend.** It profoundly shaped Biohó’s imagined family history but cannot be treated as a documented episode.

## Biohó as inventor of Palenquero

### The story

Biohó created a secret language so his followers could communicate without Spanish understanding.

### Evidence

Palenquero is genuinely a distinctive Spanish-lexified creole with African structural features, developed in a maroon environment.

### Counterevidence

Languages generally develop collectively over generations. The first clear documentation of present San Basilio occurs long after Biohó’s death.

### Assessment

**Symbolic origin story, not demonstrated linguistic fact.**

## Magical powers

### The story

Biohó commanded followers through enchantment, ritual power, or supernatural protection.

### Evidence

García Girón’s hostile reference to “embustes y encantos”; later folklore about African spiritual power.

### Assessment

**Documented accusation and folklore, not verified supernatural fact.** Its historical importance lies in revealing both African charismatic authority and Spanish colonial fear.

## Sole liberator of all Palenqueros

### The story

Biohó single-handedly freed the ancestors of San Basilio.

### Assessment

**Heroic condensation.** Biohó was a central leader, but the movement depended on unnamed women, fighters, sentinels, farmers, messengers, urban informants, and later generations of maroon leaders.

---

## Death

Biohó was executed by hanging in Cartagena on 16 March 1621. His body was reportedly dismembered, a punishment intended to eliminate the physical and political presence of a rebel leader and deter imitation.

The best-known documentary basis is Governor García Girón’s report of 28 March 1621. Because the governor was justifying his conduct to the Crown, the letter is both evidence and an interested political statement.

No burial place is known. If the body was quartered and displayed, there may never have been an intact grave.

---

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

## Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries

Biohó’s death did not end maroon activity around Cartagena. Communities continued to form, relocate, resist attacks, and negotiate. Later leaders included Domingo Criollo, Domingo Padilla, Nicolás de Santa Rosa, and others.

The eventual San Miguel/San Basilio agreement of 1713 belongs to this longer history. Biohó’s influence is best understood as precedent and remembered ancestry rather than proved uninterrupted officeholding or settlement continuity.

## Twentieth-century historical recovery

### Camilo Delgado

Writing as **Doctor Arcos**, Delgado transformed oral and historical material into the legend “El Rey del Arcabuco,” circulating it through *El Porvenir* and later *Historia, leyendas y tradiciones de Cartagena*. His narrative helped fix the names Wiwa, Sando, Orika, and the romantic tragedy of Orika in public memory.

### Aquiles Escalante

Escalante’s anthropological work on Palenque made the community central to Colombian Afro-descendant studies. His presentation strengthened the association between Biohó and San Basilio.

### Roberto Arrázola

Arrázola’s *Palenque, primer pueblo libre de América* (1970) assembled documentary material and gave the “first free town” formula enormous influence. Later scholarship has relied heavily on his transcriptions while revising some of his conclusions.

### Nina S. de Friedemann

Friedemann’s anthropology and public scholarship helped establish Palenque, marronage, and “African traces” as major subjects in Colombian history. Her work contributed to Biohó’s transformation into a national symbol.

### María Cristina Navarrete

Navarrete’s archival research complicated the seamless founder narrative by distinguishing the seventeenth-century Matuna from the later San Miguel/San Basilio formation. This is not a denial of Biohó’s importance; it is a revision of settlement chronology.

## Monumentalization

A prominent statue in San Basilio presents Biohó with broken chains and an outstretched arm. Research on the monument reports that the original sculptural conception still included chains, while later presentation emphasized the act of breaking them.

There is also a bust or monument to Biohó in Cartagena’s Parque Apolo.

Monuments visually convert a sparse archive into a recognizable body. No authentic likeness survives, so every portrait or statue is imaginative.

## UNESCO and heritage status

The Cultural Space of Palenque de San Basilio was proclaimed a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2005 and inscribed on UNESCO’s Representative List in 2008.

UNESCO recognizes:

- Palenquero language.
- Funeral, medical, religious, musical, and oral traditions.
- Ma-kuagro social organization.
- The town’s unique survival among former palenque communities.

UNESCO’s recognition applies to San Basilio’s cultural space, not to the historical verification of every Biohó legend.

## Political uses of the name

Biohó has become a general symbol of Afro-Colombian liberation. His name has been adopted by:

- Schools and cultural organizations.
- Afro-Colombian political and educational initiatives.
- An ELN guerrilla unit.
- Israel Alberto Zúñiga Iriarte as a FARC nom de guerre.
- A Colombian hospital ship.
- Public buildings and commemorative projects.

These appropriations demonstrate the breadth of his symbolic authority but are not continuations of his organization.

In 2024 Colombia’s Ministry of Cultures formalized the renaming of Cartagena’s “Casa del Inquisidor” as **Casa Benkos Biohó**, following a request by President Gustavo Petro. The project framed the renaming as a transformation of colonial memory and a space for Afro-Caribbean and Pacific cultural training.

## Contemporary historiographical position

A defensible synthesis distinguishes three propositions:

1. **Documented:** Domingo/Benkos Biohó led the Palenque de la Matuna, fought Cartagena’s government, negotiated freedom, retained extraordinary prestige, was arrested, and was executed in 1621.
2. **Historically plausible but incomplete:** his movement contributed people, practices, political memory, and institutional precedent to later regional palenques.
3. **Community memory and myth:** he personally founded the present town of San Basilio, accompanied by Queen Wiwa and the royal children Sando and Orika.

The third proposition remains culturally and politically consequential even where archival chronology contradicts a literal reading.

---

## Works by Benkos Biohó

No written, dictated, recorded, or securely attributable work by Biohó is known.

### Agreements and speech acts

The 1605 and 1612–1613 peace arrangements reflect his negotiation, but no surviving text can confidently be classified as authored or signed by him. The agreements are known through colonial administrative reporting.

No genuine manifesto, proclamation, speech transcript, diary, correspondence, technical paper, artwork, patent, or memoir survives.

Statements placed in his mouth in novels, films, school materials, monuments, and dramatic reconstructions are posthumous compositions.

- “Un epígrafe cimarrón para la libertad: monumentalización, silencio y repetición en el archivo de San Basilio de Palenque.” Study of the monument, archival repetition, and the founder narrative.
- Studies of “los acuerdos y la idealización del palenque,” which distinguish Matuna from San Miguel/San Basilio.
- Colombian Truth Commission, historical background volumes on Black, Afro-Colombian, Raizal, and Palenquero peoples.
- ICANH and Colombian Ministry of Culture nomination and safeguarding material for San Basilio de Palenque.
- Studies of Palenque’s printed memory, heritage politics, ethnogenesis, and multicultural public discourse.

### Literature and creative reconstruction

- Camilo Delgado, writing as Doctor Arcos, “El Rey del Arcabuco,” published through *El Porvenir* and collected in *Historia, leyendas y tradiciones de Cartagena* (volumes beginning in 1912; the Biohó legend is commonly dated 1913). Fictionalized legend based partly on oral accounts.
- Germán Espinosa, *Orika de los palenques* (1991). Historical novel; not a documentary biography.
- Antonio Prada Fortul, *Benkos… las alas de un cimarrón* (editions and publication dates vary; later editions appeared in the 2010s and 2020s). Novelistic or creative historical treatment.
- Djemâa Chraïti, *Benkos Biohò: L’esclave libre ou le roi de la Matuna*. French-language literary-historical treatment.
- Creative poems and dramatic monologues in Colombian journals and Afro-Colombian literary collections.

## Films, Documentaries, Interviews, and Archival Material

### Documentaries and audiovisual works

- *El llamado de Benkos*, directed by Diana Kuéllar and Mauricio Prieto. Museum-oriented short documentary linking Biohó’s resistance to San Basilio’s living culture.
- *Palenque: una historia de libertad* (2010), part of *Viajes a la memoria*, produced with Colombia’s Ministry of Culture and Caracol Televisión; hosted by Mábel Lara.
- *Historia Central: San Basilio de Palenque* (RTVC/Señal Memoria, 2011), documenting present community memory and the monument to Biohó.
- *El Beat*, a short documentary by Irene Lema Dimaté and Carolina del Mar Fernández, connecting Biohó’s Atlantic journey and memory with Palenquero music.
- *Benkos Biohó*, a film title appearing in international film references; catalog data are inconsistent and should be verified against a film archive before assigning production details.
- Numerous Colombian educational videos and podcast episodes present simplified biographies; these are useful evidence of modern reception, not independent evidence for seventeenth-century events.

### Archival materials

The most important documentary core consists of:

- Correspondence from Cartagena’s governors concerning the Matuna palenque.
- Reports of military expeditions.
- Negotiation and truce correspondence.
- García Girón’s letter to the Crown dated 28 March 1621.
- Later documents concerning the Palenque de San Miguel Arcángel and the 1713 settlement.
- Archivo General de Indias materials used by Borrego Pla, Arrázola, Navarrete, and other historians.

Researchers should consult the original manuscript images wherever possible. Many public narratives depend upon transcriptions several stages removed from the colonial documents.

## Atlas Connections

The roster was checked for documented personal meetings, correspondence, patronage, conflict, shared institutions, family ties, and acknowledged influence.

### Christopher Columbus

**No personal connection.** Columbus died before Biohó’s documented maroon activity. The two belong to the broader history of Iberian Atlantic expansion, but mere participation in that historical system does not constitute an Atlas connection.

### Pedro de Mendoza, Hernán Cortés, Francisco Pizarro, Hernando Arias de Saavedra, El Inca Garcilaso de la Vega, Blas Valera, Felipe Guamán Poma de Ayala, and Miguel de Cervantes

**No documented connection found.** Some were roughly contemporaneous or belonged to the Spanish imperial world, but no meeting, correspondence, institutional collaboration, rivalry, or explicit influence involving Biohó was identified.

### Gabriel García Márquez, Wifredo Viladrich, Simón Bolívar, Nelson Mandela, Harriet Tubman, Marcus Garvey, Patrice Lumumba, and other later anticolonial or liberation figures on the roster

**No documented personal connection possible.** Later comparisons may place Biohó in a transhistorical genealogy of liberation, but the sources consulted do not establish an explicit, subject-specific influence meeting the atlas standard.

### Result

**Absence is the finding.** No person on the supplied atlas roster has a securely documented direct relationship with Benkos Biohó. His documented network consists principally of figures absent from the roster: Jerónimo de Suazo y Casasola, Diego Fernández de Velasco, García Girón, Alonso de Campos, Juan de Palacios, the people of Matuna, and—at a later and partly legendary level—Wiwa, Sando, and Orika.
