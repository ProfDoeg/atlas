# Alexander Hamilton: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier concerns Alexander Hamilton, the Caribbean-born American soldier, lawyer, constitutional polemicist, first United States secretary of the Treasury, Federalist political organizer, and major general.

Labels used for contested material:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a surviving primary document, official record, or multiple mutually reinforcing contemporary records.
- **Reported fact:** accepted in substantial modern scholarship but dependent on incomplete or retrospective evidence.
- **Allegation:** asserted by an identifiable contemporary or later source without a conclusive adjudication.
- **Disputed claim:** evidence or interpretation remains contested.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story circulated without adequate documentary substantiation.
- **Unresolved question:** surviving evidence does not permit a confident conclusion.

The principal documentary foundation is the 27-volume *Papers of Alexander Hamilton*, now available through the National Archives’ Founders Online project, and the approximately 12,000-item Alexander Hamilton Papers at the Library of Congress.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Alexander Hamilton.

**Birth:** January 11, either **1755 or 1757**, Charlestown, Nevis, British Leeward Islands.

**Birth-year dispute:** Hamilton consistently represented himself in North America as born in 1757. Probate and Danish West Indian records associated with his mother’s estate have been read as making him approximately thirteen in 1768, implying 1755. Most twentieth-century biographies used 1755; many official institutions use 1757. No surviving baptismal record conclusively resolves the question. The Library of Congress therefore describes his birth as “circa 1757.”

**Death:** July 12, 1804, at the Greenwich Village home of William Bayard Jr., New York City, after being shot by Aaron Burr at Weehawken, New Jersey, on July 11.

**Nationality and political status:** Born a British subject; later a citizen of New York and of the United States.

**Major titles and offices:**

- Captain, New York Provincial Company of Artillery, 1776
- Lieutenant colonel and aide-de-camp to General George Washington, 1777–1781
- New York delegate to the Confederation Congress, 1782–1783
- Member, New York State Assembly, 1787
- New York delegate to the Constitutional Convention, 1787
- Delegate to the New York ratifying convention, 1788
- First secretary of the United States Treasury, September 11, 1789–January 31, 1795
- Inspector general of the United States Army, with rank of major general, 1798–1800
- Senior officer exercising day-to-day command of the provisional army during the Quasi-War

**Professions:** Merchant’s clerk, soldier, staff officer, lawyer, legislator, civil servant, political journalist, financial administrator, military planner, and organizational entrepreneur.

**Common descriptions and later honorifics:** Founding Father; “father of American public credit”; “father of the Coast Guard,” referring to his role in creating the Revenue Marine; principal architect of the early federal financial system.

**Documented pseudonyms and collective signatures:** Publius, Phocion, Pacificus, Camillus, Philo Camillus, and No Jacobin. Other signatures attributed to him in bibliographies include Scipio, Cato, Lucius Crassus, An American, An American Citizen, and A Plain Honest Man; attribution and exclusivity vary by series.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and ancestry

Hamilton’s father was **James Hamilton**, a younger son of Alexander Hamilton of Grange in Ayrshire, Scotland. James belonged to a landed Scottish family but possessed little independent wealth. He went to the Caribbean as a merchant.

Hamilton’s mother was **Rachel Faucette**, also found as Rachel Fawcett, Faucett, or Faucette Lavien. She was born on Nevis, the daughter of John Faucette, a physician and planter of French Huguenot ancestry, and Mary Uppington.

Rachel married the Danish or German-born merchant **Johann Michael Lavien** on St. Croix in 1745. They had one surviving son, Peter Lavien. The marriage failed. Lavien had Rachel confined under Danish law for several months on an accusation of adultery or marital misconduct. She left him but could not legally remarry.

Rachel thereafter lived with James Hamilton. Their children Alexander and **James Hamilton Jr.** were consequently born outside lawful marriage. Under the law governing Rachel’s marriage and estate, they were illegitimate and could not automatically inherit her property.

James Hamilton Sr. left Rachel and the boys, probably in 1765. His precise motives and later itinerary are poorly documented. Alexander later corresponded with him and attempted to assist him financially, indicating that the separation did not produce a complete estrangement.

### Mother’s death and estate

Rachel supported herself and her sons through a small store in Christiansted, St. Croix. She and Alexander contracted a severe fever in 1768, conventionally identified as yellow fever. Alexander recovered; Rachel died on February 19, 1768, aged approximately thirty-eight.

A probate inventory included household goods and two enslaved people. Because Rachel’s sons were legally illegitimate, her lawful son Peter Lavien asserted inheritance rights. Alexander and James received little or nothing from the estate. The episode left Alexander dependent on employers, relatives, and patrons.

### Sibling and half-sibling

- **James Hamilton Jr.**, Alexander’s full brother, remained in the Caribbean. The brothers’ later lives diverged and surviving evidence about James is limited.
- **Peter Lavien**, Rachel’s legitimate son by Johann Lavien, was Hamilton’s maternal half-brother.

A cousin or maternal relative, **Peter Lytton**, temporarily assisted the boys. His death by suicide in 1769 further destabilized their circumstances.

### Marriage

Hamilton married **Elizabeth “Eliza” Schuyler** on December 14, 1780, at the Schuyler mansion in Albany, New York.

Eliza was the daughter of General **Philip Schuyler** and Catherine Van Rensselaer Schuyler. The marriage connected Hamilton to two wealthy, politically powerful, landholding New York families. The Schuylers owned enslaved people, and Hamilton’s visits and residence within the extended family placed him in households sustained partly by enslaved labor.

The marriage endured Hamilton’s prolonged military absences, financial insecurity, the public disclosure of his adultery, the deaths of their eldest son and Hamilton himself, and Eliza’s long widowhood. Surviving letters document affection, sexual playfulness, administrative partnership, and periodic strain. The evidence does not disclose Eliza’s private reaction to the Reynolds affair with certainty.

### Children

Alexander and Eliza had eight children:

1. **Philip Hamilton** (1782–1801), killed after a duel with George I. Eacker.
2. **Angelica Hamilton** (1784–1857). Following Philip’s death she experienced a lasting psychological collapse or severe mental illness and remained dependent on relatives.
3. **Alexander Hamilton Jr.** (1786–1875), lawyer and military officer.
4. **James Alexander Hamilton** (1788–1878), lawyer, public official, and later acting secretary of state under Andrew Jackson.
5. **John Church Hamilton** (1792–1882), lawyer, historian, editor, and chief guardian of his father’s documentary reputation.
6. **William Stephen Hamilton** (1797–1850), lawyer, miner, militia officer, and Wisconsin territorial politician.
7. **Eliza Hamilton Holly** (1799–1859).
8. **Philip Hamilton**, called “Little Phil” (1802–1884), named after the dead eldest son.

### Alleged additional children

**Rumor or unresolved claim:** Various genealogical traditions identify men named William Hamilton or George Hamilton as possible extramarital children. No contemporary acknowledgment, reliable chain of documentation, or genetic evidence establishes Alexander Hamilton’s paternity. These claims should not be treated as fact.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1755/1757–1772

Hamilton spent his earliest years on Nevis and moved with Rachel and James Jr. to Christiansted, St. Croix, by approximately 1765.

His childhood environment was a plantation-commercial society dependent on enslaved African labor. Sugar, rum, imported manufactures, credit, bills of exchange, shipping, and the buying and selling of enslaved people formed the economy in which he learned business.

He obtained work with the mercantile firm **Beekman and Cruger**, later principally Nicholas Cruger’s business. The firm handled regional and transatlantic commerce. Its surviving records indicate that its trade included commodities produced by enslaved labor and, at times, enslaved people themselves. Hamilton learned bookkeeping, foreign exchange, inventory control, shipping, insurance, and correspondence.

When Cruger traveled, the adolescent Hamilton was entrusted with substantial responsibility. The often-repeated image of him single-handedly “running an international trading company” is an exaggeration, but his surviving commercial letters show unusual competence for his age.

On August 31, 1772, a hurricane devastated St. Croix. Hamilton described it in a letter to his father. The letter was printed in the *Royal Danish American Gazette*, reportedly at the urging of the Presbyterian minister **Hugh Knox**. Local patrons raised funds to send him to mainland North America for education.

### Formative Caribbean influences

Documentable influences included:

- firsthand exposure to commercial accounting and credit;
- vulnerability produced by illegitimacy and poverty;
- the legal disability imposed on his mother and himself;
- exposure to epidemic disease;
- a slave society’s wealth and violence;
- Presbyterian preaching and providential interpretations of disaster;
- imperial warfare and maritime trade.

Claims that these experiences automatically made Hamilton either a lifelong abolitionist or a defender of planter capitalism go beyond the evidence. Both antislavery language and participation in slave-connected commercial and family networks appear later in his life.

## Education and Formation, 1772–1776

Hamilton sailed to Boston and proceeded to New York and New Jersey in 1772.

He studied at **Francis Barber’s academy** in Elizabethtown, New Jersey, where he lodged within circles connected to William Livingston and Elias Boudinot. Because his preparation was uneven, he undertook intensive study in Latin, Greek, mathematics, history, and literature.

Hamilton sought accelerated admission at the College of New Jersey, now Princeton, but was not permitted to advance at his preferred pace. He entered **King’s College**, now Columbia University, in New York in 1773 or 1774.

Teachers and institutional influences included President **Myles Cooper**, a Loyalist Anglican clergyman; Robert Harpur; and the intellectual life of revolutionary New York. He read classical history, natural law, British political economy, and writers including Plutarch, Cicero, David Hume, Montesquieu, William Blackstone, and later Adam Smith. The precise extent of each influence is debated.

### Jewish education story

**Reported fact with uncertain particulars:** Hamilton attended a school operated by a Jewish woman on Nevis and may have learned to recite the Ten Commandments in Hebrew. Later family recollections and Hamilton’s favorable courtroom remarks about Jewish testimony support a genuine familiarity and sympathy, but claims that he had a formal Jewish tutor or extensive Hebrew education are not securely documented.

### Revolutionary pamphleteer

Hamilton entered the imperial controversy as a teenage pamphleteer. In 1774–1775 he published:

- *A Full Vindication of the Measures of the Congress*
- *The Farmer Refuted*

These defended the Continental Congress against Samuel Seabury’s Loyalist writings. They established Hamilton as an unusually forceful advocate of colonial rights while still invoking British constitutional tradition.

A legend says Hamilton saved his Loyalist college president, Myles Cooper, from a revolutionary mob by addressing the crowd while Cooper escaped. The episode rests substantially on later recollection; some form of intervention is plausible, but polished dramatic versions are not contemporaneously documented.

## Revolutionary War, 1775–1782

Hamilton drilled with a volunteer unit known as the **Hearts of Oak** and participated in removing cannon from the Battery under British fire in August 1775.

In March 1776 he became captain of a New York artillery company. His unit participated in the New York campaign and retreats through New Jersey, including actions associated with White Plains, Trenton, and Princeton.

### Washington’s staff

On March 1, 1777, Hamilton joined George Washington’s military “family” as aide-de-camp with the rank of lieutenant colonel. His duties included:

- drafting Washington’s correspondence and orders;
- communicating with Congress, governors, and generals;
- intelligence evaluation;
- translating and communicating with French officers;
- operational planning;
- arranging supplies and troop movements.

He served through Brandywine, Germantown, Valley Forge, Monmouth, and the war’s central diplomatic and logistical crises.

His colleagues and friends in Washington’s staff circle included **John Laurens**, the Marquis de Lafayette, James McHenry, Tench Tilghman, Robert Troup, and Richard Kidder Meade.

### John Laurens

Hamilton’s letters to Laurens contain intense declarations of love and desire. **Documented fact:** the correspondence was emotionally intimate. **Unresolved question:** whether the relationship was sexual. No surviving evidence proves a physical relationship, while neither the vocabulary of eighteenth-century male friendship nor missing letters allows it to be excluded. Later depictions range from entirely platonic friendship to a concealed romance; certainty in either direction exceeds the record.

Hamilton supported Laurens’s proposal to recruit enslaved men for military service and grant them freedom. Hamilton argued that Black soldiers could fight effectively and that freedom should accompany their service. The plan failed in South Carolina.

### Break with Washington

In February 1781 Washington reprimanded Hamilton for keeping him waiting. Hamilton resigned from the staff after what he considered repeated slights and Washington’s refusal to grant him a field command. He nevertheless continued to assist Washington and did not become a political enemy.

After persistent requests, Hamilton received command of a light-infantry battalion. During the Yorktown campaign he led the nighttime assault on **Redoubt No. 10** on October 14, 1781. The successful attack helped complete the siege lines that forced Cornwallis’s surrender.

Accounts that Hamilton spared all surrendering defenders are part of heroic retelling. The assault was violent and casualties occurred, although contemporary American accounts emphasized restraint after earlier British killing of American troops.

### Military outlook

War convinced Hamilton that the Confederation lacked reliable taxation, credit, executive coordination, supplies, and disciplined armed force. His later nationalism cannot be separated from the administrative collapse he witnessed in the Continental Army.

## Confederation Congress, Law, and Constitutional Politics, 1782–1789

Hamilton studied law rapidly after Yorktown and was admitted to the New York bar in 1782. He opened a practice in New York City and became one of the city’s leading attorneys.

### Confederation Congress

As a New York delegate in 1782–1783, he advocated:

- federal taxing power;
- reliable payment of public debt;
- a permanent revenue system;
- national control of commerce;
- stronger executive administration.

### Newburgh crisis

In 1783 unpaid officers at Newburgh considered collective pressure on Congress. Hamilton corresponded with Washington and Robert Morris and hoped military discontent might force Congress and the states to create dependable national revenue.

**Documented fact:** Hamilton recognized the officers’ grievances as political leverage and encouraged Washington to use his influence.

**Disputed interpretation:** Some historians describe his role as involvement in a threatened military coup. Others find no evidence that he wanted the army to overthrow civilian government; he sought pressure sufficient to produce fiscal reform while expecting Washington to restrain uncontrolled military action. Washington’s intervention ended the immediate crisis. “Hamilton planned a coup” is therefore an interpretation, not an adjudicated fact.

### Law practice and Loyalist cases

Hamilton represented former Loyalists and British subjects whose property or debts were threatened by New York legislation.

In **Rutgers v. Waddington** (1784), he argued that New York’s Trespass Act should be reconciled with the law of nations and the Treaty of Paris. The decision limited the plaintiff’s recovery without voiding the statute. It became an early episode in arguments about judicial review, treaty supremacy, and the legal reintegration of Loyalists.

Hamilton’s willingness to defend unpopular clients reflected professional interest, commercial priorities, treaty obligations, and opposition to retaliatory state legislation.

### Bank of New York

Hamilton helped organize the **Bank of New York** in 1784, drafted its constitution, and served as a director. It became a central institution in New York commerce. He did not “invent banking,” but he helped transplant and adapt British and European banking structures to the new republic.

### Manumission society

In 1785 Hamilton joined John Jay, Robert Troup, and others in the **New-York Society for Promoting the Manumission of Slaves**. The society pursued individual freedom suits, opposed kidnappings, promoted education, and advocated gradual abolition.

Its record was limited and internally compromised: many members were slaveholders, and a proposed rule requiring members to free enslaved people they owned was weakened or defeated. Hamilton’s participation is documented, but descriptions of him as a modern abolitionist obscure the society’s gradualism and his other connections to slavery.

### Annapolis and Constitutional Conventions

Hamilton attended the Annapolis Convention of 1786 and helped draft its call for a broader convention.

At Philadelphia in 1787, New York’s other delegates, John Lansing Jr. and Robert Yates, opposed radical revision and left. Hamilton remained but had little voting power.

On June 18 he proposed an exceptionally centralized plan:

- a powerful national government;
- a president and Senate chosen through electors and serving during good behavior, effectively life tenure absent removal;
- national appointment of state governors;
- national veto authority over state laws.

The convention rejected the plan.

**Documented fact:** Hamilton proposed life tenure and praised aspects of the British constitution.

**Disputed characterization:** Opponents called him a monarchist. His proposal did not establish hereditary succession or formal nobility, and Hamilton later defended the Constitution’s elected executive. “Monarchist” can describe his admiration for monarchical stability but becomes misleading if it implies a documented plan to crown himself or establish a hereditary American king.

Hamilton signed the Constitution on September 17, 1787.

### *The Federalist*

Hamilton organized the newspaper defense of the Constitution under the collective name **Publius**, recruiting James Madison and John Jay.

Of the 85 essays, traditional attribution gives Hamilton 51, Madison 29, and Jay 5, although the authorship of several essays was historically disputed. Modern scholarship generally assigns Hamilton Nos. 1, 6–9, 11–13, 15–17, 21–36, 59–61, and 65–85; the Library of Congress also notes collaborative or disputed treatment of Nos. 18–20, 50–58, and 62–63.

Hamilton led the Federalist cause at the New York ratifying convention in Poughkeepsie. New York ratified on July 26, 1788, by 30 votes to 27.

## Secretary of the Treasury, 1789–1795

Washington nominated Hamilton as the first Treasury secretary. He took office on September 11, 1789.

The department began with responsibility for customs, public accounts, debt, revenue, and economic planning. Hamilton established reporting and administrative systems and selected capable subordinates, including Oliver Wolcott Jr. and William Duer.

### Public credit

Hamilton’s January 1790 *Report Relative to a Provision for the Support of Public Credit* proposed:

- funding federal obligations at close to face value;
- honoring securities held by current owners rather than compensating only original holders;
- federal assumption of state Revolutionary War debts;
- dependable taxation for interest payments;
- consolidation of creditors’ interests around the federal government.

The settlement associated with the Residence and Assumption bargain placed the permanent capital on the Potomac and helped secure assumption.

The program restored market confidence and raised federal securities’ value. It also enriched some speculators who had purchased depreciated certificates from veterans and small holders. Madison proposed compensating original as well as current holders; Hamilton argued that discriminating among legal holders would damage negotiability and credit.

**Documented outcome:** public credit improved substantially.

**Contested interpretation:** Republicans saw a deliberately created “moneyed interest” tying wealthy creditors to federal power. Hamilton regarded that alignment as a stabilizing mechanism rather than corruption.

### First Bank of the United States

Hamilton’s December 1790 bank report proposed a federally chartered corporation capitalized at $10 million, with private shareholders and federal participation.

Jefferson and Attorney General Edmund Randolph argued that Congress lacked enumerated authority to create it. Hamilton’s *Opinion on the Constitutionality of an Act to Establish a Bank* developed a broad doctrine of implied powers: lawful means reasonably related to enumerated ends could be used unless prohibited.

Washington signed the act in February 1791. The bank supplied a national payments mechanism, credit, currency, and a fiscal agent for government.

### Mint, currency, customs, and Revenue Marine

Hamilton’s *Report on the Establishment of a Mint* supported a decimal bimetallic monetary system. Congress established the Mint in 1792, though not within Treasury as he preferred.

He organized federal customs administration and proposed ten armed **revenue cutters** to suppress smuggling and enforce tariffs. The Revenue Marine, authorized August 4, 1790, became an institutional ancestor of the United States Coast Guard.

He also supervised lighthouse federalization and advocated detailed standards for customs officers, urging restraint toward citizens while enforcing federal law.

### Excise tax and Whiskey Rebellion

Congress enacted an excise on distilled spirits in 1791. Western farmers, for whom whiskey functioned as a marketable store of value, regarded the tax and collection system as unequal and intrusive.

Resistance escalated into intimidation, attacks on officials, destruction of property, and organized defiance in western Pennsylvania. In 1794 Washington called out a militia force of approximately 13,000. Hamilton accompanied the expedition and pressed for conspicuous enforcement.

**Documented fact:** the rebellion largely dispersed before major fighting; arrests were made, but few prosecutions succeeded and Washington pardoned two convicted men.

**Contested interpretation:** Supporters saw constitutional enforcement after lawful procedures and failed conciliation. Critics saw a regressive tax and an oversized military demonstration used to discipline agrarian opposition. Claims of “mass indefinite detention” require qualification: coercive arrests and harsh treatment occurred, but later polemical descriptions sometimes merge separate episodes or overstate Hamilton’s personal legal authority.

### Report on Manufactures and industrial planning

The December 1791 *Report on the Subject of Manufactures* argued for economic diversification through:

- protective duties;
- temporary bounties and subsidies;
- encouragement of inventions;
- infrastructure;
- immigration of skilled labor;
- quality inspection;
- expanded markets.

Hamilton drew on British practice, European political economy, and Adam Smith, while departing from simple laissez-faire prescriptions.

He promoted the **Society for Establishing Useful Manufactures**. The corporation planned an industrial city at the Great Falls of the Passaic, later Paterson, New Jersey. Pierre Charles L’Enfant initially designed an ambitious waterpower scheme; Peter Colt replaced him.

The enterprise suffered from speculation, management disputes, William Duer’s collapse, capital shortage, and technical problems. The original manufacturing corporation did not realize Hamilton’s immediate expectations, though Paterson later became a major industrial center.

### Foreign policy and party formation

Hamilton and Jefferson became the leading antagonists within Washington’s cabinet.

Hamilton favored:

- commercial accommodation with Britain;
- neutrality in the European wars;
- federal executive authority;
- public credit, banking, and manufacturing;
- a standing military capacity.

Jefferson and Madison favored:

- greater sympathy for revolutionary France;
- a narrower reading of federal power;
- stronger state and agrarian interests;
- suspicion of financial concentration.

Hamilton’s **Pacificus** essays defended Washington’s Neutrality Proclamation and expansive presidential authority in foreign affairs. Madison answered as **Helvidius**.

Hamilton later wrote the **Camillus** essays defending Jay’s Treaty. He helped create the Federalist Party’s newspaper, patronage, legislative, and correspondence networks, though the party never possessed a modern mass-membership structure.

### Financial investigations and corruption accusations

Republican congressmen, including William Branch Giles, demanded Treasury records in 1792–1793 and alleged:

- unauthorized mixing or shifting of loan funds;
- concealment of accounts;
- favoritism toward speculators;
- improper relations with banks;
- personal enrichment.

Hamilton produced extensive reports, correspondence, and account material. Congress rejected the Giles resolutions. No formal finding established embezzlement, bribery, or personal speculation with public funds.

**Documented fact:** Hamilton exercised broad administrative discretion and sometimes retroactively regularized transactions between loan accounts.

**Documented fact:** Congress investigated and did not sustain the charges.

**Allegation not established:** that Hamilton stole Treasury money or secretly profited from federal securities.

His posthumous financial statement and debts are inconsistent with the image of a secretly enriched official, though personal indebtedness alone cannot prove every administrative decision proper.

### Resignation

Hamilton’s salary was $3,500 annually. Supporting a large family and maintaining elite political and social obligations strained his finances. He resigned effective January 31, 1795, returned to legal practice, and remained Washington’s unofficial adviser.

## The Reynolds Affair and Pamphlet, 1791–1797

In summer 1791 Hamilton began a sexual relationship with **Maria Lewis Reynolds**, wife of James Reynolds.

According to Hamilton, Maria sought financial assistance after claiming abandonment. After the affair began, James Reynolds demanded money and eventually accepted $1,000 in return for permitting or concealing the relationship. Further payments followed.

In 1792 Reynolds and Jacob Clingman were implicated in a scheme involving fraudulent claims for veterans’ back pay. James Monroe, Frederick Muhlenberg, and Abraham Venable confronted Hamilton because the payments suggested possible financial misconduct. Hamilton disclosed the affair and showed them letters. They accepted that the payments concerned adultery rather than Treasury corruption and agreed to confidentiality.

In 1797 the journalist James Thomson Callender published allegations derived from the investigation. Hamilton responded with *Observations on Certain Documents*, the **Reynolds Pamphlet**, publicly admitting the affair and blackmail while denying public corruption.

Evidence categories:

- **Documented fact:** Hamilton admitted and described a prolonged adulterous relationship.
- **Documented fact:** surviving letters and the 1792 inquiry corroborate payments and concealment.
- **Hamilton’s claim:** Maria and James may have jointly entrapped him.
- **Unresolved question:** whether Maria was a willing conspirator, coerced by her husband, independently pursuing Hamilton, or represented unfairly in a narrative controlled by powerful men.
- **Not established:** Callender’s central accusation that the payments proved Treasury speculation.

The pamphlet damaged Hamilton’s family and reputation. It also exposed Maria’s letters and private life without her control. Hamilton framed the choice as sacrificing private honor to protect public integrity.

## Later Law Practice and Legal Significance

Hamilton became one of New York’s most active and highly paid lawyers. Major matters included:

- **Hylton v. United States** (1796), concerning the federal carriage tax and the distinction between direct and indirect taxes.
- **Le Guen v. Gouverneur and Kemble**, a commercially important dispute producing a very large fee and later controversy between lawyers over compensation.
- **People v. Croswell** (1804), in which Hamilton argued that truth should be admissible as a defense in a seditious-libel prosecution and that juries should decide law and fact. He lost under existing doctrine, but New York legislation later adopted core elements of his position.
- Loyalist restitution, maritime insurance, debt, merchant, land-title, and constitutional matters.

The five-volume *Law Practice of Alexander Hamilton*, edited by Julius Goebel Jr., documents more than 150 matters and remains the central scholarly edition.

## Quasi-War and Provisional Army, 1798–1800

During deteriorating relations with France, Washington was recalled as commander in chief. Hamilton became inspector general and effective operational commander of the expanded army.

He devised:

- recruitment and officer systems;
- drill, supply, and fortification plans;
- organizational tables;
- proposals for military education;
- expansive strategic plans involving Spanish possessions.

Hamilton corresponded with **Francisco de Miranda** concerning possible liberation of Spanish America. In August 1798 Hamilton contemplated a British fleet, a United States army, and a post-liberation government agreed upon by the cooperating powers, but said he could participate only with United States government authorization.

**Documented fact:** Hamilton entertained a large interventionist project involving Spanish America.

**Not established:** that he operated a privately controlled invasion or intelligence service independent of government.

President John Adams distrusted Hamilton and feared the army might become an instrument of his faction. Adams’s decision to negotiate with France undercut the military program. Peace made the provisional army unnecessary, and Hamilton left service in 1800.

### Alien and Sedition Acts

Hamilton belonged to the Federalist leadership during enactment of the Alien and Sedition Acts. He expressed concern about immigrant radicalism and supported measures against foreign threats. He did not draft the enacted laws, and at points considered Federalist measures politically excessive.

The Sedition Act was nevertheless enforced by an administration and party with which he was closely identified. Attempts either to make Hamilton its sole architect or to detach him completely from Federalist repression oversimplify the record.

## Election of 1800 and Political Collapse

Hamilton’s relationship with President Adams deteriorated into open hostility. Hamilton considered Adams vain, erratic, and unsuited to party leadership.

In 1800 Hamilton wrote a long confidential letter attacking Adams’s character and conduct while nevertheless concluding that Federalists should support him. Aaron Burr obtained or facilitated publication of the letter. It split the Federalist Party and injured Hamilton’s influence.

When Thomas Jefferson and Burr tied in the Electoral College, Hamilton urged Federalists in the House to prefer Jefferson. He regarded Jefferson as wrong but principled; Burr, in his view, was dangerously opportunistic.

This contributed to Burr’s belief that Hamilton repeatedly blocked his advancement.

## Religion, Philosophy, and Intellectual Development

Hamilton’s religious life changed over time.

As a youth under Hugh Knox’s influence, he wrote in an explicitly providential and Christian idiom. During his public career, his political writings relied more on natural law, classical history, prudential reasoning, and institutional analysis than on theology.

He was associated socially with Anglican and later Episcopal circles but was not a regular communicant for much of adulthood.

After Philip’s death and during his final years, his religious language became more explicit. Before dying he sought communion. Bishop Benjamin Moore initially hesitated because Hamilton had participated in a duel, then administered the sacrament after Hamilton expressed repentance and renunciation of revenge.

Hamilton reportedly proposed a **Christian Constitutional Society** late in life to combine political organization with Christian moral instruction. The plan never became a significant institution.

His intellectual orientation combined:

- classical republican concern with corruption and faction;
- admiration for British fiscal and administrative capacity;
- natural-law constitutionalism;
- commercial nationalism;
- executive “energy”;
- suspicion of direct or unstable popular rule;
- belief that public credit could bind private interest to national durability.

## Wealth, Property, Investments, and Financial Relationships

Hamilton earned money from Treasury service and, more substantially, private legal practice. He invested in or helped organize enterprises including the Bank of New York and the Society for Establishing Useful Manufactures.

He maintained financial relationships with merchants, banks, government contractors, relatives, and clients, including John Barker Church, Philip Schuyler, Robert Morris, William Duer, and New York commercial firms.

His principal residence late in life was **The Grange**, a country house on approximately thirty-two acres in upper Manhattan, completed in 1802. Construction and upkeep contributed to his debt.

At death his accounts indicated debts around **$55,000**, including mortgages, bonds, and personal notes, offset incompletely by receivables and property. Friends secretly raised funds. Benefactors purchased the Grange and resold it to Eliza at a reduced price, permitting the family to remain there.

Hamilton’s precarious estate contradicts claims that he died a great financial magnate. He possessed elite connections, earning power, land, and securities but lacked stable accumulated wealth.

## Slavery and Enslavement

This subject has undergone major reassessment.

### Documented connections

- Hamilton grew up in plantation colonies built on enslaved labor.
- His mother owned two enslaved people at her death.
- Beekman and Cruger participated in slave-connected commerce and transactions.
- The Schuyler family owned numerous enslaved people.
- Hamilton conducted purchases, sales, payments, or financial arrangements involving enslaved people for relatives and clients.
- He joined the New York Manumission Society.
- He supported John Laurens’s proposal to arm and emancipate enslaved soldiers.
- He used antislavery language on several occasions.

### Personal ownership dispute

Hamilton’s cash book records a March 23, 1796, payment of $250 to Philip Schuyler for “2 Negro servants purchased by him for me.” Researcher Jessie Serfilippi identifies this and other entries as evidence that Hamilton personally purchased and owned enslaved people.

Earlier biographers interpreted some entries as agency transactions for John and Angelica Church or other relatives. An 1804 family letter stating that Eliza lacked enslaved assistance has been cited against household ownership at that moment.

**Strongly supported finding:** Hamilton acted as financial intermediary in human-property transactions and benefited from enslaved labor within his family network.

**Disputed but substantial claim:** that the 1796 entry establishes direct personal ownership of two enslaved people. The plain wording favors that reading, but the incomplete accounts and overlapping family agency relationships leave some room for dispute over beneficial ownership.

**Unsupported hagiographic claim:** that Hamilton was an uncompromising abolitionist wholly detached from slavery.

**Equally misleading hostile claim:** that he left no antislavery record. His manumission-society work and Laurens correspondence are genuine, though limited by practice and priority.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### George Washington

Commander, patron, employer, and later political principal. Hamilton drafted Washington’s wartime and presidential documents, including material used in the Farewell Address. Their 1781 rupture did not end the relationship. Washington repeatedly relied on Hamilton’s legal, fiscal, political, and military advice.

### Elizabeth Schuyler Hamilton

Spouse, household partner, mother of his children, posthumous editor and memorializer. Her collection, preservation, and promotion of his papers significantly shaped the historical archive.

### Angelica Schuyler Church

Sister-in-law, intellectual correspondent, and close friend. Their letters contain flirtation and strong affection. **Rumor or unresolved question:** that they had a sexual affair. No surviving document proves one.

### John Laurens

Closest wartime friend, antislavery collaborator, and emotionally intimate correspondent. Laurens’s death in 1782 deeply affected Hamilton.

### Marquis de Lafayette

Military comrade and friend. They served during the Revolution, corresponded, and shared interest in constitutional reform and Atlantic politics.

### James Madison

Collaborator in constitutional reform and *The Federalist*; subsequently principal opponent of Hamilton’s financial system and foreign policy.

### John Jay

Coauthor of *The Federalist*, manumission-society associate, lawyer, diplomat, and Federalist ally.

### Thomas Jefferson

Cabinet colleague, ideological antagonist, and leader of the emerging Republican opposition. Their conflict shaped the first party system.

### John Adams

Federalist president but bitter intraparty rival. Adams’s insults sometimes invoked Hamilton’s illegitimate birth and foreign origin. Hamilton’s 1800 attack helped fracture the party.

### Aaron Burr

Legal colleague, electoral rival, political antagonist, and killer in the 1804 duel.

### Robert Morris and Gouverneur Morris

Financial and constitutional allies, though never without differences. Robert Morris was an important model for national financial administration.

### William Duer

Treasury official and speculator. His collapse during the Panic of 1792 embarrassed Hamiltonian networks. No proof establishes that Hamilton shared Duer’s corrupt speculation.

### Philip Schuyler and John Barker Church

Family patrons and political-commercial associates whose land, credit, slaveholding, and investment networks materially aided Hamilton’s social rise.

### Oliver Wolcott Jr.

Treasury subordinate, successor, confidant, and participant in the private 1792 explanation of the Reynolds payments.

### James Monroe

Initially part of the confidential Reynolds inquiry. Hamilton later believed Monroe had allowed the evidence to leak. Their confrontation nearly produced a duel; Aaron Burr acted as an intermediary.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Political Violence

### Illegitimacy and status

Hamilton’s birth outside lawful marriage was documented and repeatedly weaponized by enemies. John Adams’s descriptions combined class, ethnic, sexual, and political insult. Later heroic narratives turned the same fact into a self-made immigrant origin story.

### Financial corruption

**Allegation:** Republicans charged that Treasury loans, banks, assumption, and securities operations concealed enrichment and executive corruption.

**Investigation:** Congress demanded records in 1792–1793.

**Result:** Hamilton answered at length; formal resolutions failed. No conviction or official finding of theft resulted.

**Continuing historical dispute:** whether a legally administered system can still be called structurally corrupt because it concentrated political and financial power. That is an interpretive dispute distinct from personal embezzlement.

### Reynolds affair

**Documented adultery and blackmail payments; no established Treasury fraud.**

### Slave transactions

**Documented participation as agent or payer; disputed but credible evidence of personal ownership.**

### Newburgh crisis

**Documented use of military discontent as leverage; disputed “coup” characterization.**

### Democratic legitimacy and monarchism

Hamilton’s 1787 proposal for life-tenured national officers and his praise of Britain support descriptions of him as highly conservative and distrustful of popular volatility.

No evidence establishes a plan for hereditary kingship or self-coronation. His subsequent defense and operation of the Constitution are also documented.

### Whiskey Rebellion

Hamilton supported armed enforcement and accompanied the militia expedition. Debate concerns proportionality, class incidence of the excise, arrests, and whether the demonstration protected or narrowed republican dissent.

### Militarism and Spanish America

His Quasi-War army and Miranda correspondence support a picture of ambitious military-national planning. Proposed operations never received full authorization or implementation.

### Press and political warfare

Hamilton sponsored partisan newspapers, wrote anonymously, coordinated attacks, and attempted to shape public perception. He also articulated an important truth defense in *People v. Croswell*. These were not contradictory in his framework: partisan combat was legitimate, while legal punishment for truthful criticism was dangerous.

### Duelling

Hamilton participated in the culture of affairs of honor despite privately condemning the practice. He served as second or adviser in earlier disputes, failed to prevent Philip’s duel, and accepted Burr’s challenge. Dueling was illegal in New York and New Jersey.

## Death: Burr–Hamilton Duel

In the 1804 New York gubernatorial election, Hamilton opposed Burr. Dr. Charles D. Cooper published a letter claiming Hamilton had expressed a “despicable opinion” of Burr and could report something still more dishonorable.

Burr demanded an avowal or denial of the reported statements. Hamilton refused a blanket retraction because he could not answer for every political conversation over fifteen years. Their seconds—William P. Van Ness for Burr and Nathaniel Pendleton for Hamilton—failed to reach a settlement.

Hamilton prepared a written statement saying he had decided to “reserve and throw away” his first fire because of religious scruples, family obligations, and opposition to dueling.

At Weehawken on July 11, 1804, both men fired. Burr’s ball struck Hamilton above the right hip, damaged internal organs and spine, and lodged near the vertebrae. Hamilton’s shot struck a tree above and behind Burr.

**Unresolved first-shot question:** Witnesses differed and seconds deliberately followed conventions that limited their direct observation. Hamilton may have fired first, intentionally wide; Burr may have fired first; or the discharges may have been nearly simultaneous. The tree strike and pre-duel statement support an intention to delope, but cannot establish the exact sequence or exclude an involuntary discharge.

Hamilton was taken across the Hudson to William Bayard’s house. He remained conscious for periods, received family and clergy, and died at approximately 2:00 p.m. on July 12.

Burr was indicted for murder in New York and New Jersey. He was never tried for Hamilton’s death; the charges did not produce a conviction. His political reputation suffered severely, although the duel alone did not immediately end every public role.

Hamilton was buried at Trinity Churchyard in lower Manhattan.

## Psychological Crises, Illness, and Injuries

- Severe fever in St. Croix in 1768; Rachel died during the same outbreak.
- Recurrent illnesses and exhaustion appear in correspondence, but no secure modern diagnosis exists.
- Hamilton contracted yellow fever during the Philadelphia epidemic of 1793 and recovered under treatment by Dr. Edward Stevens.
- The deaths of John Laurens, Philip Hamilton, and George Washington produced documented grief.
- Hamilton’s conduct after Philip’s death and during his political decline has prompted retrospective theories of depression, trauma, or suicidal recklessness. **Unresolved:** contemporary evidence documents grief, pessimism, and religious reflection but cannot support a clinical diagnosis.
- Fatal gunshot wound, July 11–12, 1804.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Born poor and orphaned, he wrote his way off an island”

This modern capsule has a factual core: Hamilton’s family collapsed, his mother died, and patrons funded his education after publication of the hurricane letter. It compresses a more complex merchant apprenticeship and patronage network. He was impoverished relative to planter elites but possessed literacy, commercial training, and access to white mercantile patrons unavailable to most inhabitants.

### The hurricane letter as a miraculous discovery

The letter was real and published. Later accounts transform it into an instantaneous scholarship contest. Fundraising appears to have involved Hugh Knox, Nicholas Cruger, relatives, and merchants; the exact contributors and causal sequence are incompletely documented.

### Hamilton as Washington’s adopted son

Washington trusted and promoted him, but Hamilton explicitly resisted reducing their relationship to filial affection. The “adopted son” motif is retrospective sentimentalization.

### Hamilton as sole author of Washington’s Farewell Address

Hamilton drafted a major version and substantially shaped the language. Washington supplied themes, revisions, and final authority; James Madison had prepared an earlier draft in 1792. Sole authorship is inaccurate.

### Hamilton as the sole creator of American capitalism

He was pivotal in federal credit, banking, taxation, customs, and manufacturing policy. Commercial banking, securities, corporations, slavery-based Atlantic trade, state finance, and private markets predated him. The myth turns institutional synthesis into individual invention.

### Hamilton as an unqualified abolitionist

Promoted in family biography and renewed by the musical era. His antislavery acts were real, but coexist with slave transactions, compromise, political silence, and dependence on slaveholding networks.

### Hamilton as a secret monarchist

The story began in partisan conflict and drew strength from his June 18, 1787 speech. He favored life tenure and admired Britain, but no reliable evidence establishes a secret program for hereditary monarchy. The accusation affected Jeffersonian and later democratic portrayals for generations.

### Hamilton as a foreign British agent

Republican propaganda depicted him as an instrument of British finance. His pro-British commercial strategy and contacts are documented. Payment, control, or direction by British intelligence is not established.

### Hamilton planned a military dictatorship

The Newburgh correspondence, Whiskey expedition, provisional army, and expansive strategic plans supply the material from which this narrative developed. They show comfort with concentrated state and military capacity, but no surviving operational plan to seize the government.

### Hamilton and Angelica Church

Their correspondence is affectionate and flirtatious. Later fiction often depicts an affair. No documentary proof of sexual relations survives.

### Hamilton and John Laurens

Modern representations sometimes present a definite romantic or sexual partnership; older biographies often erased even the emotional intensity. The letters establish intense attachment, not the physical nature of the bond.

### Hamilton deliberately wasted his shot

His pre-duel statement supports that intention. The precise action remains disputed because timing, weapon mechanics, and witness positions prevent certainty.

### “Young, scrappy, and hungry” immigrant democrat

The Broadway characterization captures ambition, immigrant origins, literary energy, and upward mobility. It minimizes his distrust of mass politics, attachment to hierarchy, slave-connected networks, and preference for powerful national institutions.

## Political, Economic, and Institutional Legacy

Hamilton’s durable institutional influence includes:

- federal assumption and funded public debt;
- a permanent Treasury bureaucracy;
- customs-based federal revenue;
- a national fiscal agent and the constitutional case for implied powers;
- decimal currency and mint planning;
- the Revenue Marine;
- federal encouragement of manufacturing;
- organized party journalism;
- constitutional arguments for energetic executive power;
- defenses of judicial independence and broad national authority.

The First Bank’s charter expired in 1811, but the Second Bank and later central-banking debates reused Hamiltonian principles. Chief Justice John Marshall’s reasoning in *McCulloch v. Maryland* paralleled Hamilton’s implied-powers argument.

Competing traditions use “Hamiltonian” to mean:

- developmental government and industrial policy;
- pro-business national administration;
- central banking and public credit;
- executive energy;
- elite insulation from unstable majorities;
- militarized or authoritarian nationalism.

These usages overlap but are not identical.

## Posthumous Reputation

### Immediate memorialization

Hamilton’s death produced sermons, funeral processions, pamphlets, and eulogies. Burr became a symbol of dangerous ambition; Hamilton was recast as a martyr to honor and national union.

### Family construction of the archive

Eliza Hamilton preserved letters, solicited recollections, defended her husband’s authorship, and worked toward publication. John Church Hamilton wrote biographies and histories that presented his father as the central architect of the republic.

This family labor preserved indispensable evidence but also created a hagiographic tendency.

### Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

Jeffersonian and Jacksonian historians often depicted Hamilton as aristocratic, monarchical, and allied with concentrated wealth. Industrializing nationalists and corporate lawyers treated him as a prophet of national development.

Major reinterpretations included:

- Henry Cabot Lodge’s nationalist Hamilton;
- Frederick Scott Oliver’s conservative statesman;
- Broadus Mitchell’s economically grounded biography;
- Clinton Rossiter’s constitutional theorist;
- Forrest McDonald’s administrative and financial architect;
- Stephen Knott’s study of competing myths;
- Ron Chernow’s psychologically rich immigrant-founder narrative.

### Currency and memorials

Hamilton has appeared on several federal notes and has been the principal portrait on the $10 Federal Reserve note since 1929. Treasury proposals in the 2010s to replace or reduce his prominence met resistance amplified by the Broadway musical.

The Grange became a national memorial. It was moved twice and is administered by the National Park Service as Hamilton Grange National Memorial.

### Broadway and twenty-first-century reinterpretation

Lin-Manuel Miranda’s *Hamilton*, inspired principally by Ron Chernow’s biography, premiered off-Broadway in 2015 and on Broadway later that year. Its multiracial casting and hip-hop idiom turned Hamilton into a symbol of immigrant ambition and contested American belonging.

Criticism has focused on:

- marginal treatment of slavery;
- flattering presentation of Hamilton’s democratic credentials;
- disappearance of Indigenous people and much working-class resistance;
- romanticization of elite state-building;
- treatment of Maria Reynolds;
- transformation of political conflict into personal rivalry.

The musical’s influence prompted renewed archival interest and helped stimulate reassessment of Hamilton’s involvement in slavery.

In 2025 the production canceled a planned 2026 Kennedy Center engagement after changes in the institution’s political leadership, showing that Hamilton’s cultural representation remains part of contemporary political conflict.

## Atlas Connections

Only direct, source-supported relationships or documented intellectual influence are included.

### Adam Smith

**Documented intellectual connection.** Hamilton read and used Smith’s political economy, especially *The Wealth of Nations*, while preparing fiscal and manufacturing arguments. Hamilton shared Smith’s attention to division of labor, markets, and productivity but supported more direct federal promotion of infant manufactures than a simplified laissez-faire reading of Smith would suggest. No personal meeting occurred; Smith died in Scotland in 1790.

### Benjamin Franklin

**Documented institutional and political connection, with limited evidence of personal intimacy.** Franklin and Hamilton participated in the broader Revolutionary leadership network and both served as Pennsylvania Society of the Cincinnati figures and American diplomats/political actors, but the surviving Hamilton corpus does not support a close personal relationship comparable to Hamilton’s ties with Washington or Lafayette. Franklin was at the Constitutional Convention with Hamilton in Philadelphia in 1787, establishing a shared documented institutional scene.

### Francisco de Miranda

**Documented meeting, friendship, and correspondence.** Hamilton met the Venezuelan revolutionary in New York between January and July 1784. Miranda’s 1792 letter recalled their New York conversations about the liberation of “Columbia,” meaning Spanish America. They corresponded again in 1797–1799. On August 22, 1798, Hamilton outlined possible cooperation involving a British fleet and a United States army, conditional on American governmental authorization.

### George Washington

**Documented commander-subordinate, patronage, cabinet, and advisory relationship.** Hamilton served as Washington’s aide-de-camp from March 1777 to 1781, as Treasury secretary from 1789 to 1795, and as second-ranking general during the 1798 provisional army. Hamilton drafted major correspondence and contributed substantially to the Farewell Address. Their relationship survived the 1781 quarrel.

### Marie-Joseph Paul Yves Roch Gilbert du Motier, Marquis de Lafayette

**Documented friendship and military collaboration.** Hamilton and Lafayette served in Washington’s military circle, worked together during the Yorktown campaign, and maintained an Atlantic political correspondence. Lafayette was also close to the Schuyler and Washington networks.

### Thomas Jefferson

**Documented cabinet association, ideological conflict, and political rivalry.** Hamilton and Jefferson served together under Washington in New York and Philadelphia. Their disputes over the bank, public debt, France, Britain, executive power, and constitutional construction helped create the Federalist and Republican party systems. Hamilton later helped prevent Aaron Burr from defeating Jefferson in the House election of 1801.

### William Paterson

**Documented shared institutional setting.** Paterson and Hamilton were delegates at the Constitutional Convention in Philadelphia in 1787. Paterson proposed the New Jersey Plan; Hamilton proposed a much more centralized plan. Their presence and positions are documented, although a close personal relationship is not.

### Alexander Baring and Sir Francis Baring

**Reported institutional and financial connection, not a demonstrated close personal relationship.** The Baring banking network participated in the Atlantic financial world through which United States credit and foreign loans operated. Hamilton dealt more directly with Dutch bankers and American financial intermediaries during his Treasury tenure. The evidence consulted does not establish a substantial personal Hamilton–Alexander Baring relationship; Alexander Baring was still a young merchant during Hamilton’s lifetime.

### Napoleon Bonaparte

**Documented political subject, not a personal connection.** Hamilton analyzed revolutionary and Napoleonic France and supported military preparedness against French power. No reliable evidence shows a meeting or correspondence with Napoleon.

### Prince Hall

**No documented direct connection found.** Both belonged to the revolutionary and early-republican Atlantic world and engaged questions of Black freedom, but the consulted evidence does not support a meeting, correspondence, or organizational relationship.

### Alexander Hamilton

The subject himself appears in the supplied atlas roster; this is an identity match, not a separate connection.

### Absence finding

No documentary connection was found between Hamilton and the overwhelming majority of the other rostered persons. Shared themes—banking, industrialization, revolution, centralization, cryptography, mysticism, or later ideological appropriation—were not treated as personal connections without evidence.
