# Elda Neyis Mosquera García: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier reconstructs the available public record through 3 September 2026. The most important primary or quasi-primary materials consulted were:

- The 6 May 2022 Justice and Peace judgment of the Medellín Superior Tribunal.
- The 2026 appellate decision of Colombia’s Supreme Court.
- Reports and audiovisual testimony collected by Colombia’s Truth Commission.
- Mosquera’s judicial statements, public interviews, and 2025 first-person biography.
- Contemporary reporting from 2008, when she left the FARC-EP.

The record contains substantial contradictions, especially concerning her birth date, birthplace, exact age at recruitment, command dates, and responsibility for particular attacks. Those conflicts are retained below.

Evidentiary labels:

- **Documented fact:** established by an official record, judgment, or direct contemporaneous documentation.
- **Reported fact:** consistently reported but not independently established here by a primary record.
- **Self-report:** Mosquera’s account; valuable evidence but not independent corroboration.
- **Allegation:** accusation not shown here to have produced a final finding against her personally.
- **Court finding/conviction:** adjudicated in a criminal or transitional-justice judgment.
- **Disputed claim:** supported and denied by competing sources.
- **Rumor or myth:** culturally or politically circulated story for which reliable proof was not located.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Elda Neyis Mosquera García.
- **Recorded variants and misspellings:** Elda Neyis Mosquera; Elda Nellys Mosquera; Eida Nellys Mosquera; Elda Neyis García Mosquera.
- **Principal nom de guerre:** **Karina**.
- **Other aliases:** **La Negra**, **La Cucha**, and **Nelly Ávila Moreno**. Some secondary sources also associate her with **Janet Mosquera Rentería** and **Rocío Arias**, but the basis and frequency of those identities are unclear.
- **Nationality:** Colombian.
- **Ethnic/family origin:** Her father was reportedly from Istmina, Chocó; her mother came from San Rafael, Antioquia. Public sources often describe Mosquera as an Afro-Colombian or Black woman, although her own available accounts emphasize geography and poverty more than a formal ethnic identification.
- **Occupation:** Former FARC-EP guerrilla commander; later government-designated peace promoter, judicial contributor, reconciliation participant, garment worker, interview subject, and co-author.
- **Status:** Alive as of September 2026.
- **Known organizational affiliations:** Colombian Communist Youth; FARC-EP Fifth Front; Ninth Front according to some sources; Forty-Seventh Front; José María Córdova/Northwestern Bloc; Colombian government peace-promoter program; Justice and Peace process.

### Birth-date conflict

No single birth date can be treated as secure.

- Encyclopedic and several older biographical accounts give **15 August 1963**.
- A 2022 regional report gives **15 October 1967**.
- Mosquera’s 2025 biography and interviews say that she was **sixteen** when she entered the FARC on **3 September 1984**, implying a birth around 1967–1968.
- A September 2025 profile identified her as 58, also implying approximately 1967.
- Other reporting says she entered at seventeen.

The 1967 date aligns better with her recent first-person chronology, but the civil-registration record was not located.

### Birthplace conflict

- Recent first-person-oriented reporting and a long 2013 profile identify **Puerto Boyacá, Boyacá**, as her birthplace.
- Other accounts identify **Nueva Antioquia**, then a rural district of Turbo, Antioquia.
- Mosquera reportedly said that her family moved from Puerto Boyacá to **Currulao, Turbo**, when she was about three.

The strongest reconstructable possibility is birth in Puerto Boyacá followed by early-childhood migration to Urabá, but the matter remains unresolved.

## Family and Ancestry

Mosquera described a household of fourteen: her father, mother, and twelve children. Another judicial-profile summary says she had eight siblings, demonstrating either an error or a narrower count of surviving/immediate siblings.

Her father was born in **Istmina, Chocó**. Her mother came from **San Rafael, Antioquia**, from a Liberal family reportedly displaced during the partisan violence of the 1950s. According to Mosquera’s account, persecution by Conservatives drove the maternal family toward Puerto Boyacá. The family later settled in the Urabá region.

The household was poor and depended on agricultural and informal labor. Mosquera said that her grandmother and a maternal aunt helped her attend primary school in exchange for selling mazamorra, arepas, and fruit. She also worked harvesting maize, rice, beans, sugarcane, and cassava.

Her father’s reported response when she joined the guerrilla was that she should be a “good guerrillera”—which she understood as obedience and proper conduct within the organization. Mosquera later said that this injunction remained psychologically important when she considered leaving.

At least two brothers reportedly entered guerrilla organizations. One was killed and another imprisoned, according to the 2022 judicial-profile reporting. In 2025 Mosquera said that the killing of brothers helped produce her desire for vengeance, but the available sources do not securely identify them or establish dates and circumstances.

### Childhood sexual abuse

**Self-report:** In her 2025 biography and interviews, Mosquera disclosed that her sister’s husband sexually abused her repeatedly when she was approximately twelve or thirteen. She said she had not told her sister before publication. No independent investigation or court finding was located. The disclosure is relevant both as personal history and to her later reassessment of sexual violence inside the FARC.

### Early partner

Mosquera said that at approximately fourteen she sought a boyfriend and attempted to form a household as an escape from exhausting labor. She described the relationship as physically and verbally abusive. The partner’s identity, exact duration of the relationship, and whether it constituted a formal marriage are not established.

### Child

Mosquera has one known daughter. The father’s identity is not securely documented in the consulted public sources.

**Self-report:** She became pregnant while in the FARC. Commanders ordered her to terminate the pregnancy; she refused and gave birth, but was required to leave the child outside the organization and return to guerrilla duty. She feared execution if she did not return. Contact was subsequently limited, sometimes to one telephone call a year. Mosquera said that their relationship improved during 2024 but remained emotionally distant.

She later identified a decisive episode: Iván Ríos allegedly asked her to bring her fourteen-year-old daughter into the guerrilla. Mosquera said that confronting the possibility as a mother altered her understanding of the recruitment she had imposed on other families.

## Childhood and Early Life

Mosquera spent most of her formative years in **Currulao and rural Turbo**, in the banana-growing region of Urabá. This was an area of agrarian conflict, Communist Party organization, union militancy, guerrilla expansion, counterinsurgency, and later extreme competition among the FARC, EPL-derived groups, state forces, and paramilitaries.

Formal education was limited. She dreamed of becoming a physician or a skilled dressmaker but left school early because of poverty and labor demands.

At approximately twelve, according to her recent account, she joined the **Juventud Comunista Colombiana** (JUCO). She later characterized its instruction as teaching that armed struggle was the highest form of revolutionary action. This is her retrospective account; it should not be generalized automatically to every JUCO organization or instructor.

The Communist organizer **Efrén González** reportedly collected or escorted her and five other recruits—three women and three men—to the FARC. She remembered one male recruit receiving the guerrilla name “Enrique.”

## Education and Formation

### Formal education

Mosquera did not complete secondary education before joining the guerrilla. While in state custody, she reportedly completed her bachillerato at or through the Seventeenth Brigade in Apartadó.

No university education, technical degree, or professional credential has been documented.

### Guerrilla formation

On **3 September 1984**, by her recent account, she entered the FARC’s Fifth Front. Earlier reporting variously places her entry in 1981, 1984, or 1988. The exact date supplied by Mosquera and the repeated claim of twenty-four years in the organization make 1984 the best-supported date.

She said that receiving a camouflage shirt made her feel fully realized as a guerrilla. The Fifth Front had a strong military orientation, and its senior commander was **Efraín Guzmán**, also called “El Viejo.” Training included weapons, field discipline, Marxist-Leninist political instruction, obedience, secrecy, and the FARC’s internal disciplinary regulations.

Mosquera later described this formation as progressively suppressing sympathy for outsiders. In 2025 she said that commanders taught her to become a soldier, accept vengeance as legitimate, and regard the guerrilla unit as her family.

### Mentors and commanding officers

- **Efraín Guzmán:** Senior FARC commander who commanded the Fifth Front and later the Northwestern/José María Córdova Bloc. Contemporary reporting associates Mosquera’s advancement with his confidence or patronage.
- **Isaías Trujillo:** Predecessor or senior commander connected with the Forty-Seventh Front and bloc command.
- **Iván Ríos (Manuel de Jesús Muñoz Ortiz):** Secretariat member and head of the Northwestern Bloc. He was Mosquera’s superior during the final phase of her guerrilla career.
- **Hernán Gutiérrez, alias Kadafi/Gadafi:** Described as her second-in-command and, in some periods, as the officer who displaced or superseded her direct command.
- **Efrén González:** Communist organizer who reportedly facilitated her recruitment.

## Early Guerrilla Career, 1984–1993

Mosquera entered the **Fifth Front** in Urabá. Sources disagree about how quickly she advanced. Contemporary 2008 profiles described her as disciplined, militarily capable, and unusually successful in gaining command within a male-dominated organization.

Her unit operated amid violent conflict involving:

- FARC fronts;
- state security forces;
- banana-industry and agricultural interests;
- Communist Party and Unión Patriótica networks;
- the EPL and its demobilized political successor, Esperanza, Paz y Libertad;
- armed “Comandos Populares” associated with conflict against former EPL members;
- emerging paramilitary organizations.

Some reporting places her transfer to the **Ninth Front in 1993**. Subsequent movements and exact ranks are not consistently documented.

## Chronological Life History

### 1993–1996: Urabá conflict and contested atrocity allegations

The FARC’s conflict with demobilized EPL members and supporters of Esperanza, Paz y Libertad escalated into assassinations and massacres.

Mosquera was accused in journalistic and prosecutorial accounts of participation or command responsibility in several events:

- **La Chinita massacre, Apartadó, 23 January 1994:** Thirty-five civilians were killed at a community gathering. Mosquera and Fifth Front commander Danis Daniel Sierra, alias Samir, were among those publicly accused. The 2022 Justice and Peace proceedings addressed the Northwestern Bloc’s extensive crimes, but the consulted summaries do not permit a precise statement that Mosquera personally fired weapons or issued the order at La Chinita. Her relationship to the episode should therefore be described as an **allegation and possible command/organizational responsibility**, not an independently established personal act.
- **Churidó, 1995; Carepa, 1995; Osaka farm, 1996:** Secondary sources repeatedly include these among actions attributed to her. Exact charges, findings, and her operational role were not reconstructed from the available judgment summaries.
- **Campaign against Esperanza, Paz y Libertad:** In 2025 Mosquera acknowledged that the FARC also pursued and “exterminated” members of this demobilized EPL current while the Unión Patriótica was itself being persecuted. That statement is a significant admission concerning the organizational campaign but does not establish her individual participation in every killing.

### Alleged killing of Alberto Uribe Sierra

Alberto Uribe Sierra, father of later president Álvaro Uribe Vélez, was killed in 1983 during an attempted kidnapping at the Guacharacas estate.

Mosquera has frequently been accused in political and press narratives of participating in or ordering the killing. The allegation is chronologically problematic if her own 1984 FARC-entry date is correct, and no conviction or reliable primary evidence connecting her to the death was located. She has denied responsibility.

**Evidentiary status:** **Unproven allegation, expressly denied.** It should not be stated as fact.

### 1998: Serious combat injuries

Around 1998 Mosquera was severely wounded, reportedly at or near **Pavarandó, Antioquia**. Accounts say she suffered a gunshot wound and remained unconscious for approximately eight days.

Reported lasting injuries include:

- loss of the right eye and use of a prosthetic eye;
- facial and cranial scarring;
- impaired hearing in the right ear;
- reduced function in one arm;
- loss of the left breast.

Not every injury has an independently established date, and some may have resulted from separate engagements.

### 1998–2000: Rise to command

Some sources place Mosquera among the Forty-Seventh Front’s leaders by 1998. The Truth Commission’s Eje Cafetero report says she became commander in **2000**. Another account says she took command after Isaías Trujillo moved into bloc leadership.

She became one of the highest-ranking women in the FARC and, according to the Truth Commission, the first woman to serve on the organization’s Estado Mayor. Contemporary descriptions calling her the “only” woman to command a FARC front should be read cautiously because women held important authority elsewhere, although her prominence was exceptional.

### 12 December 1999: Juradó attack

FARC units attacked the Marine infantry base, police station, and government facilities at **Juradó, Chocó**. Reports attributed participation to several fronts and said approximately twenty-five members of the security forces were killed and twelve captured.

Mosquera was accused of commanding one participating group. The attack was investigated after her demobilization. The consulted material establishes the event and investigation but does not isolate every order personally given by her.

### 30 July 1999: Nariño, Antioquia

A major FARC attack on **Nariño, Antioquia**, killed police and civilians and destroyed a large portion of the town. Reporting associates Mosquera with command of the attack and recalls images of her raising a FARC flag and addressing the population afterward.

A car bomb used in the action reportedly killed nine police officers and seven civilians. In 2008 the Fiscalía imposed a detention measure or advanced prosecution for aggravated multiple homicide for terrorist purposes, terrorism, attempted homicide, property damage, aggravated theft, and kidnapping.

Mosquera later acknowledged participation in guerrilla attacks in which civilians died, while disputing that she committed every crime attributed to “Karina.”

### 29–30 June 2000: Arboleda, Pensilvania

The FARC attacked **Arboleda**, a rural district of Pensilvania, Caldas. Thirteen police officers were reported killed.

Mosquera was prosecuted as a commander and in February 2009 received a **33-year ordinary criminal sentence**. This is a **conviction**, not merely an allegation. The ordinary sentence was later incorporated into the Justice and Peace framework.

### 29 April 2000 onward: Forty-Seventh Front in Caldas and Antioquia

A judicial profile records her arrival at the Forty-Seventh Front on **29 April 2000**. Its areas of operation included Argelia, Sonsón, and Nariño in Antioquia; much of eastern and northern Caldas, including Samaná, Pensilvania, Marulanda, Manzanares, Pácora, Salamina, Aguadas, Victoria, La Dorada, and Marquetalia; and parts of Risaralda.

Under Mosquera and associated commanders, the front implemented FARC **Law 002**, demanding payments from wealthy individuals and businesses. Its methods included extortion, kidnapping, roadblocks, attacks on towns, recruitment, forced displacement, disappearances, and killings.

No reliable estimate of Mosquera’s personal wealth was located. The 2022 tribunal examined the FARC’s financing through kidnapping and extortion; reporting on the judgment said kidnapping victims connected with the examined structures paid tens of billions of Colombian pesos. These were organizational criminal revenues, not demonstrated personal assets belonging to Mosquera.

### Kidnapping of Óscar Tulio Lizcano

Former congressman **Óscar Tulio Lizcano** was kidnapped in Caldas on 5 August 2000 and held for more than eight years. Mosquera was repeatedly accused of involvement because the Forty-Seventh Front operated in the region.

The consulted sources do not establish that she personally performed the seizure or continuously controlled his captivity. Attribution should therefore remain at the level supported by the relevant charge or organizational command evidence rather than be presented as an eyewitness-established personal act.

### 2002–2006: Expansion, joint actions, and front command

Reports associate Mosquera or the Forty-Seventh Front with:

- attacks in Caldas during 2002 in which police officers were killed;
- the recruitment of minors and execution of suspected infiltrators;
- kidnappings and extortion along the Medellín–Bogotá corridor;
- a December 2005 action conducted with elements of the ELN and Ejército Revolucionario Guevarista, reportedly killing eight police officers and taking approximately thirty captives;
- the **4 March 2006 attack on Montebonito**, Marulanda, Caldas, in which five people reportedly died.

Judicial findings later treated the bloc’s conduct through patterns of macrocriminality rather than reducing liability to isolated battlefield incidents.

### Internal discipline and the execution of “Omaira”

**Self-report and admission:** Mosquera described a young recruit called **Omaira**, who admitted to her that the Army had used her as an infiltrator. Mosquera promised secrecy but informed a superior. The girl was subjected to a guerrilla “revolutionary council” and executed under FARC disciplinary rules.

Mosquera’s account acknowledges her causal role in disclosing the information. The exact date, unit, legal characterization, and identity of the victim were not independently established in the sources consulted.

### Pregnancy, reproductive coercion, and abortions

Mosquera’s case appears in the Truth Commission’s study of sexual and reproductive violence inside the FARC.

- **Self-report as victim:** She was ordered to abort her pregnancy, refused, and was separated from her daughter.
- **Self-report as commander/perpetrator:** She said that she later made subordinate women undergo abortions and witnessed abortions at advanced stages of pregnancy.
- **Court finding:** The 2022 Justice and Peace judgment documented gender-based violence within the bloc, including four cases connected with personnel under Mosquera’s authority, according to her 2025 interview.
- **Her position:** She denies personally committing sexual assault but accepts command responsibility if investigations establish that subordinates committed it.

Her account conflicts with former FARC leaders’ claims that forced abortion or sexual violence was not institutional or systematic. The existence of coercive reproductive practices is supported by Truth Commission testimony, although the frequency, degree of central policy, and responsibility of particular leaders remain contested.

### 2003–2007: Command ambiguity

A 2022 Truth Commission report says Mosquera commanded the Forty-Seventh Front from 2000 until **2003**, when Hernán Gutiérrez, alias Gadafi/Kadafi, arrived. A 2023 JEP document describes the front in 2003 as commanded by Mosquera, with Gutiérrez second.

Contemporary 2008 government accounts continued to call her the commander. Mosquera herself later claimed that she had not directed the front for roughly two years before surrender. The most careful conclusion is that she remained a senior authority through 2008, but operational command may have been divided, displaced, or altered after 2003.

### 14 November 2007–March 2008: Military pressure and collapse

A 14 November 2007 air strike on a camp at **Los Planes, Sonsón**, reportedly separated Mosquera from subordinates and intensified the front’s deterioration.

On 3 March 2008, **Pedro Pablo Montoya, alias Rojas**, a member of the front and security chief for Iván Ríos, killed Ríos and delivered his severed hand, documents, and computer to Colombian forces. Mosquera had supplied Rojas to Ríos’s security detail and therefore had an indirect organizational connection to the killing, but no evidence located shows that she ordered it.

Rojas reportedly urged Mosquera to demobilize. Numerous Forty-Seventh Front members deserted during the following weeks.

### 18 May 2008: Desertion and surrender

Mosquera left the FARC with her companion **alias Michín** and surrendered in the Sonsón area on **18 May 2008** after negotiations involving relatives, intermediaries, the DAS, military intelligence, and public assurances of safety from President Álvaro Uribe.

The state’s initial presentation was that sustained military pressure, hunger, isolation, organizational collapse, the bounty on her, and fear that her own subordinates might kill her prompted the surrender. Mosquera publicly repeated much of this account in 2008.

In 2025 she revised the emphasis. She said military pressure was secondary and that her principal reasons were disillusionment, conflict with superiors, disgust at commanders’ disregard for life, the treatment of Clara Rojas and her infant, the request that she bring her own daughter into the FARC, and the belief that the organization had no viable future.

These explanations are not mutually exclusive, but the later account suggests that the 2008 presentation was partly shaped by military officials.

### 2008–2009: Prosecution and peace-promoter designation

Following surrender, Mosquera was interrogated and prosecuted in ordinary courts. She accepted or faced charges including rebellion, aggravated homicide, terrorism, kidnapping, recruitment of minors, disappearance, theft, and destruction of property.

In February 2009 she received the 33-year sentence for Arboleda. On **4 March 2009**, the government formally designated her a **gestora de paz**; she and Raúl Agudelo Medina, alias Olivo Saldaña, were released or placed under conditional arrangements to encourage guerrilla demobilization.

The designation did not erase her convictions. She remained under state control and for years lived or worked from a military installation in Urabá. She supplied information used by authorities to prevent alleged kidnappings, recruitment, and other armed actions. A later judicial profile credited her information with helping neutralize nine planned operations.

### Religious conversion

**Self-report:** After surrender, Mosquera experienced intense sadness, pressure, fear, and regret, at times believing that she should have died in the war. A DAS guard named **Gilberto** approached her with an evangelical Christian message. She described crying for the first time since surrender and subsequently becoming an evangelical Christian.

Her later religious outlook differed sharply from the formally Marxist and atheistic culture she associated with the FARC. She read the Bible and framed some reconciliation work in Christian terms. No particular denomination or church office was established.

### 2010: Testimony in the Rito Alejo del Río case

Mosquera testified in proceedings concerning retired general **Rito Alejo del Río**, including claims involving former Apartadó mayor **Gloria Cuartas** and supposed FARC political direction of Unión Patriótica officeholders.

A human-rights lawyers’ organization argued that testimony by Mosquera and Danis Sierra failed to support attempts to associate the Comisión Intereclesial de Justicia y Paz with the FARC. This is an important challenge to her reliability in that litigation, but the advocacy group’s characterization is not itself a judicial finding that every statement she made was false.

In a separate 5 February 2010 statement later cited by the Council of State, Mosquera said that computer messages addressed to “La Negra” were not addressed to her and that she did not know former senator **Piedad Córdoba** or know of her meeting with the FARC.

### 2012–2021: Justice and Peace, imprisonment, and victim encounters

Mosquera entered the Justice and Peace process applicable to demobilized armed actors who supplied truth and reparative cooperation. A 2014 report said she had accumulated approximately ten ordinary convictions totaling more than two hundred years, although ordinary aggregate sentences were subject to statutory limits and transitional treatment.

She spent approximately ten years deprived of liberty by her 2025 account. Exact dates and custodial classifications—prison, military facility, conditional liberty, or supervised peace-promoter status—vary across reports and require a complete penitentiary file to resolve.

During collective truth-telling sessions beginning in the 2010s, she said that testimony from victims and other former combatants restored memories of crimes she had lost or suppressed.

In 2020–2021, facilitator **Leonor** and associated organizations prepared separate processes involving Mosquera and mothers from **Argelia, Antioquia**, whose children had been recruited and disappeared. After approximately five months, Mosquera met the women, answered questions, acknowledged responsibility, and asked forgiveness. The encounter was documented by the Truth Commission.

The reception was divided. Some victims accepted dialogue or forgiveness; others objected, and participants who reconciled with her reportedly faced criticism in their communities.

### 6 May–1 July 2022: Justice and Peace judgment

The Medellín Superior Tribunal’s Justice and Peace Chamber issued a partial judgment on **6 May 2022** against Mosquera and eight other former members of the FARC’s José María Córdova/Northwestern Bloc. Public reading continued through May and June and concluded on 1 July.

The other defendants included:

- Danis Daniel Sierra Martínez, alias Samir;
- Nelson Patiño, alias El Zorro;
- Marco Fidel Giraldo Torres, alias Garganta;
- Pedro Luis Pino, alias Martín;
- Virgilio de Jesús Guzmán, alias Walter;
- Fabio Nelson Aguirre, alias Carlos;
- Jesús Giraldo, alias Miguel;
- Edison Cataño, alias Garra Seca.

The judgment ran nearly 6,000 pages and addressed **3,358 criminal acts** affecting **2,172 recognized victims** across Antioquia, Córdoba, Chocó, Quindío, Caldas, and Risaralda.

The court identified six principal patterns:

- forced disappearance;
- homicide;
- unlawful recruitment, including children;
- gender-based and reproductive violence;
- kidnapping/hostage-taking and associated extortion;
- forced displacement.

It also examined destruction of civilian property, attacks, environmental injury, internal punishment, and financing. The decision recognized affected natural areas as deserving special constitutional protection and ordered ecological reparations.

Mosquera and the others received the Justice and Peace **alternative sentence of eight years**, conditioned on truth, reparative obligations, non-repetition, and compliance. Reporting cited a collective compensation figure of approximately **COP 94.149 billion**. That amount should not be misunderstood as Mosquera’s personal wealth or a demonstrated capacity to pay.

At sentencing, she asked victims for forgiveness and said she had been a victim in childhood but became a perpetrator.

### Relationship to the JEP

Mosquera did not transfer her central case to the Special Jurisdiction for Peace. She said that she had already made a commitment to victims in Justice and Peace and did not want to abandon that process.

She also described hostility from former FARC colleagues, who considered her a traitor because she deserted and because her testimony contradicts senior former commanders on:

- forced recruitment;
- sexual violence;
- forced abortion;
- execution of infiltrators;
- FARC violence against Esperanza, Paz y Libertad.

Her claims are first-person evidence from a former middle commander. They do not automatically resolve disagreements about organization-wide policy, but they are corroborated in part by other testimony collected by the Truth Commission.

### 2024–2025: Family contact, garment work, and memoir

Mosquera said her relationship with her daughter improved in 2024.

By 2025 she was living in or near **Envigado/Medellín** and working in **confecciones**, realizing in a limited form her childhood interest in dressmaking. No business registration, corporate structure, real-estate portfolio, investment holdings, or substantial private fortune was found.

In September 2025, Penguin Random House’s Debate imprint published:

*Volver a ser Elda: Una biografía íntima de alias Karina*, co-authored with political scientist **Gustavo Duncan**.

The book resulted from twenty-nine recorded sessions totaling more than sixty hours, followed by more than two years of writing and editing. Mosquera supplied the life narrative; Duncan transformed transcripts and editing-session material into a first-person past-tense account. It is therefore a collaboratively written oral-history memoir, not an independently written autobiography or conventional third-person biography.

She used interviews promoting the book to admit:

- participation in terrorist attacks;
- recruitment of young people;
- indifference to victims during the war;
- transmitting information that led to Omaira’s execution;
- making some guerrilla women undergo abortions;
- permitting or failing to stop crimes by subordinates.

She continued to deny:

- killing Alberto Uribe Sierra;
- playing football with severed heads;
- castrating men;
- committing sexual assaults herself;
- many crimes mechanically attributed to her because of the “Karina” persona.

### 2026 appellate proceedings and present status

On **6 May 2026**, the Supreme Court of Justice issued its second-instance decision in the Justice and Peace case. The Criminal Cassation Chamber substantially confirmed the conviction of Mosquera and the eight other former FARC members for the 3,358 acts and six patterns of macrocriminality.

The court adjusted aspects of victim recognition and compensation and clarified rules against duplicate recovery between ordinary and transitional jurisdictions. It retained the legal structure of the Justice and Peace sentence.

In May 2026 Mosquera also appeared in proceedings concerning violence against Unión Patriótica members. She said truth-telling remained her contribution to victims and reported security concerns and ostracism by former comrades.

As of 3 September 2026, no reliable report of her death has appeared. She remains alive, engaged in judicial truth processes, public discussion of her memoir, reconciliation activities, and garment work.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

No evidence was located that Mosquera owned major companies, patented technology, made formal inventions, controlled investment vehicles, or accumulated documented personal wealth.

Relevant institutional and financial structures include:

- **FARC-EP:** Her principal institution from 1984 to 2008.
- **Fifth, Ninth, and Forty-Seventh Fronts:** Operational formations with which she was associated.
- **Northwestern/José María Córdova Bloc:** The regional bloc to which the Forty-Seventh Front belonged.
- **FARC Law 002:** Organizational policy imposing payments on wealthy people and enterprises; enforced through extortion and kidnapping.
- **Justice and Peace system:** The transitional process under which her ordinary convictions and organizational crimes were consolidated.
- **Gestora de paz program:** Government mechanism under which she encouraged demobilization and provided intelligence or outreach.
- **Truth Commission reconciliation project:** The five-month process culminating in her encounter with mothers from Argelia.
- **Garment work:** Her reported civilian occupation by 2025; no identifiable company name was located.
- **Book project with Gustavo Duncan:** Based on recorded oral-history sessions and published commercially by Debate.

The Justice and Peace court called for investigation of possible FARC assets in offshore jurisdictions. This concerned alleged organizational finances; no evidence located tied a specific offshore account or asset to Mosquera personally.

## Political, Religious, and Ideological Development

1. **Childhood Liberal family memory:** Her maternal family transmitted stories of anti-Liberal persecution during *La Violencia*.
2. **Communist youth formation:** Around age twelve, she joined JUCO and absorbed revolutionary and pro-armed-struggle ideas.
3. **FARC Marxism-Leninism:** Between 1984 and 2008 she identified the guerrilla as her principal community and accepted its military hierarchy.
4. **Bolivarian-revolutionary discourse:** During public occupations of towns, she reportedly promoted the FARC’s Bolivarian political program.
5. **Disillusionment:** Organizational coercion, treatment of captives and children, internal command conflict, reproductive policies, and the request to recruit her daughter contributed to her departure.
6. **Evangelical Christianity:** Beginning shortly after surrender, she adopted Christian belief and biblical reading.
7. **Reconciliation and individual truth:** Her later position emphasizes confession, direct meetings with victims, personal responsibility, and disagreement with former senior FARC leaders.
8. **Continuing structural claims:** She maintains that the Colombian state, security forces, and paramilitary structures also bear responsibility and argues that children used as military infiltrators remain owed justice.

No evidence was found that she held elected office or became an electoral candidate.

## Important Relationships and Networks

- **Parents:** Poor agricultural workers whose regional migrations shaped her childhood.
- **Grandmother and maternal aunt:** Supported her elementary schooling through food vending and agricultural work.
- **Brothers in armed politics:** At least two reportedly joined guerrillas; one was killed and another imprisoned.
- **Unnamed early partner:** Described by Mosquera as physically and verbally abusive.
- **Daughter:** Separated from Mosquera because of FARC rules; limited contact, improving in 2024.
- **Efrén González:** Communist organizer who reportedly facilitated her entry into the FARC.
- **Efraín Guzmán:** Military-political superior and patron of her advancement.
- **Isaías Trujillo:** Senior Northwestern Bloc commander connected with her assumption of the Forty-Seventh Front.
- **Iván Ríos:** Secretariat-level superior; his killing precipitated the front’s collapse. Mosquera later said he requested that her daughter be brought into the FARC.
- **Hernán Gutiérrez, alias Kadafi/Gadafi:** Deputy, co-commander, or successor, depending on the source.
- **Pedro Pablo Montoya, alias Rojas:** Her subordinate who became Ríos’s security chief, killed Ríos, and encouraged Mosquera to surrender.
- **Alias Michín:** Companion who surrendered with her; the exact personal or romantic status of the relationship is unclear.
- **Álvaro Uribe Vélez:** President during her surrender and earlier governor of Antioquia. He publicly urged her to surrender and guaranteed safety. Their relationship was adversarial and mediated by the unproven allegation concerning his father.
- **Raúl Agudelo Medina, alias Olivo Saldaña:** Fellow former guerrilla designated as a peace promoter.
- **Gilberto:** DAS guard whom she credits with initiating her evangelical conversion.
- **Gloria Cuartas:** Former Apartadó mayor whom Mosquera accused of contacts with FARC figures; the claim emerged in the Rito Alejo del Río proceedings and was contested.
- **Piedad Córdoba:** Mosquera stated under oath that she did not know Córdoba and that messages to “La Negra” were not messages to her.
- **Leonor:** Facilitator in the reconciliation process between Mosquera and mothers from Argelia.
- **Gustavo Duncan:** Political scientist, interviewer, editor, and co-author of her biography.
- **Carlos Álvarez:** Documentarian involved in the interviews that began the biography project.
- **Raúl Hasbún and Rodrigo:** Intermediaries identified by Duncan as helping persuade Mosquera to participate in the book project.
- **Mothers of Argelia:** Victims and interlocutors in the Truth Commission encounter; neither their forgiveness nor their rejection was uniform.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Financial Questions

### Adjudicated responsibility

Mosquera has ordinary and transitional convictions connected with rebellion, terrorism, homicide, kidnapping, recruitment, disappearance, displacement, gender-based violence, and other conflict-related conduct.

The clearest individual ordinary conviction identified here is the 33-year sentence for the Arboleda attack. The 2022 Justice and Peace judgment and 2026 appellate ruling established responsibility within extensive macrocriminal patterns involving her and eight co-defendants.

### Scope of the 3,358 crimes

The figure does **not necessarily mean that Mosquera personally executed each of 3,358 acts**. Transitional command and organizational liability can encompass ordering, contributing, facilitating, tolerating, or bearing responsibility through position and joint criminal structures. The judgment must be consulted incident by incident before attributing any particular physical act to her.

### Recruitment of children

This is both a **court finding** and an area of Mosquera’s own admissions. She acknowledged recruiting young people and participating in a system that retained or punished minors. Victims continue to seek the remains or whereabouts of children who disappeared.

### Sexual and reproductive violence

The bloc’s gender-based violence was judicially documented. Mosquera says four sexual-violence cases in her sentence involved personnel under her command. She denies committing sexual assault personally but accepts potential command responsibility. She separately admits compelling abortions.

### Killings of alleged infiltrators

Mosquera admits reporting Omaira, knowing the likely penalty. She also alleges that state forces used children as infiltrators and that the FARC executed many of them. The first proposition is a self-incriminating account; the broader numerical scope requires further case-specific investigation.

### Alberto Uribe Sierra

The claim that Mosquera killed Álvaro Uribe’s father remains **unproven and denied**. The chronology may exclude it if she joined the FARC in 1984, one year after his death.

### La Chinita and other Urabá massacres

Her organizational environment and public accusations are documented. Her precise personal role in La Chinita, Churidó, Carepa, and Osaka should not be overstated without the complete charging instruments and findings for each case.

### Óscar Tulio Lizcano

The kidnapping is documented; Mosquera’s exact role remains less clear in the sources examined. Command responsibility is plausible given the front and territory, but it is not equivalent to proof that she personally abducted or guarded him.

### Informant and state-cooperation controversy

After surrender, Mosquera supplied operational information and encouraged desertions. Former FARC members regarded this as betrayal; the government framed it as peace promotion. Her evidence was useful in some cases and attacked as unreliable or politically instrumental in others.

### Claims against Gloria Cuartas and human-rights organizations

Mosquera’s testimony was used to allege FARC influence over political and civil-society figures in Urabá. The José Alvear Restrepo Lawyers’ Collective said the testimony failed in court to substantiate accusations against the Comisión Intereclesial de Justicia y Paz. No finding located establishes a conspiracy by Mosquera to fabricate testimony, but the episode shows that her statements require external corroboration.

### Organizational money

The FARC structures with which she served financed themselves through kidnapping, extortion, and other illegal practices. Courts examined large ransom and extortion payments and requested investigation of possible offshore assets. No personal offshore account, money-laundering conviction, property empire, or private enrichment by Mosquera was located.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Playing football with severed heads”

- **Story:** Mosquera supposedly decapitated victims or played football with the heads of dead police officers and soldiers.
- **Appearance and circulation:** Widely repeated in Colombian media and popular political discourse by the time of her 2008 surrender.
- **Circulators:** Press accounts, security-force narratives, political commentary, and word-of-mouth in conflict regions.
- **Evidence:** No court finding, named eyewitness account, forensic report, or contemporaneous primary document proving the story was located.
- **Mosquera’s response:** She categorically denies it.
- **Status:** **Rumor or hostile myth.**
- **Effect:** It made “Karina” a personalized symbol of FARC cruelty and shaped the fear of guards and civilians after her surrender.

### Castration of male captives

- **Story:** She supposedly ordered or personally performed castrations.
- **Evidence:** No substantiating judgment or medical documentation was located.
- **Response:** Denied by Mosquera.
- **Status:** **Unverified hostile mythology.**

### Personal commission of rapes

- **Story:** Some accounts or accusers associated Mosquera personally with sexual violence.
- **Evidence:** The tribunal documented sexual violence by members of her bloc and cases under her command, but available summaries do not show a finding that she physically raped someone.
- **Response:** She denies personal commission while accepting possible superior responsibility.
- **Status:** **Disputed allegation; broader command responsibility judicially supported.**

### “The bloodiest woman in the FARC”

- **Story:** Contemporary reports repeatedly called her the most sanguinary, cruel, or feared female FARC commander.
- **Evidence:** She held senior command in structures responsible for extensive adjudicated crimes. The superlative itself cannot be objectively verified.
- **Status:** **Media epithet based on real command responsibility but not a measurable historical finding.**
- **Effect:** It helped reduce a large organizational system to a recognizable female villain and amplified sensational stories.

### Sole or unique female commander

- **Story:** Mosquera was the only woman ever to command a FARC front.
- **Evidence:** She was unquestionably an unusually senior woman and was reported as the first female member of the FARC Estado Mayor. Absolute claims of uniqueness are difficult to sustain across all units and periods.
- **Status:** **Probably exaggerated shorthand.**

### Surrender caused exclusively by military pressure

- **Story:** The state defeated and cornered her, producing surrender.
- **Evidence for:** Bombardment, hunger, desertion, the collapse of the front, the bounty, and Ríos’s killing are documented.
- **Evidence against exclusivity:** Mosquera later said military officers encouraged that presentation and that her primary motivation was moral and organizational disillusionment.
- **Status:** **Partly documented but disputed as a complete explanation.**

### Heroic redemption narrative

- **Story:** Christianity, truth-telling, and victim encounters wholly transformed “Karina” back into “Elda.”
- **Circulation:** Government peace-promotion material, religious testimony, reconciliation journalism, and publicity surrounding her memoir.
- **Evidence:** Conversion, cooperation, admissions, and victim meetings are documented. Some victims forgave her; others rejected reconciliation or continued demanding information.
- **Status:** **Documented change surrounded by a partly self-created and institutionally promoted redemptive narrative.**

### FARC traitor narrative

- **Story:** Her desertion, cooperation with the state, and divergence from former Secretariat testimony made her a traitor.
- **Circulation:** Former FARC colleagues and organizations that allegedly refused to attend events with her.
- **Evidence:** Mosquera reports direct exclusion and enduring hostility; independent detail is limited.
- **Status:** **Reported fact and political label, not a neutral factual category.**

## Death, Later Life, and Present Status

Mosquera is alive. As of September 2026, she had survived severe wartime injuries, approximately twenty-four years in the FARC, surrender, multiple convictions, a decade or approximately a decade in varying forms of detention, and prolonged security threats.

Her present public roles include:

- garment work;
- testimony in Justice and Peace proceedings;
- victim-reconciliation work;
- promotion and discussion of her memoir;
- evangelical religious practice;
- public criticism of both her former organization and unaddressed state responsibility.

Her precise residential address and security arrangements are appropriately not public. Recent reporting places her in the Medellín/Envigado area.

## Reputation and Legacy

Mosquera’s reputation has passed through several overlapping forms:

1. **FARC military reputation:** Disciplined commander who rose unusually high as a woman.
2. **Regional reputation:** Feared commander associated with kidnappings, extortion, destroyed towns, recruitment, and disappearance in Antioquia, Caldas, Chocó, and Risaralda.
3. **State propaganda symbol:** Her surrender was presented as proof of FARC military collapse in 2008.
4. **Hostile popular mythology:** Stories of severed-head football, castration, and limitless personal cruelty.
5. **FARC betrayal narrative:** Former comrades reportedly regard her as a deserter and state collaborator.
6. **Judicial actor:** First prominent former FARC commander sentenced within Justice and Peace, with responsibility fixed through patterns of macrocriminality.
7. **Truth witness:** Source contradicting former Secretariat members on recruitment, abortion, sexual violence, and violence against demobilized EPL members.
8. **Reconciliation figure:** Participant in carefully mediated encounters with victims, without universal forgiveness.
9. **Memoir subject:** The 2025 book seeks to distinguish the person Elda from the wartime persona “Karina,” while admitting major crimes and disputing sensational allegations.

Her historical value as a witness lies partly in her middle-command position: close enough to central structures to observe policy, but subordinate enough to describe how orders operated at front level. Her evidence remains subject to the ordinary problems of memory, self-exculpation, judicial incentives, trauma, institutional pressure, and contradiction by other witnesses.

## Chronology

- **1963 or 1967:** Birth; date and birthplace disputed between 15 August 1963 and 15 October 1967, and between Nueva Antioquia/Turbo and Puerto Boyacá.
- **Circa 1970:** Family already established in Currulao, Urabá, if her account of moving at age three is accurate.
- **Circa 1979–1980:** Joins Colombian Communist Youth at approximately twelve.
- **Early adolescence:** Works in agriculture and food vending; reports sexual abuse by a brother-in-law and abuse by an early partner.
- **3 September 1984:** Enters FARC Fifth Front, according to her detailed later account.
- **1993:** Reported transfer to Ninth Front.
- **23 January 1994:** La Chinita massacre; later accused of involvement, exact personal role unresolved here.
- **1995–1996:** Associated in secondary reports with Churidó, Carepa, and Osaka attacks.
- **Circa 1998:** Seriously wounded; loses right eye and suffers other lasting injuries.
- **30 July 1999:** FARC attack on Nariño, Antioquia; Mosquera associated with command.
- **12 December 1999:** Juradó attack; accused of commanding a participating group.
- **29 April 2000:** Reported arrival at the Forty-Seventh Front.
- **29–30 June 2000:** Arboleda attack; later receives 33-year conviction.
- **5 August 2000:** Óscar Tulio Lizcano kidnapped; Mosquera’s exact role remains disputed.
- **2000–2003:** Best-documented core period of her direct Forty-Seventh Front command.
- **2003:** Some sources say Gadafi/Kadafi arrived and displaced her; other records still list her as commander.
- **December 2005:** FARC–ELN–ERG joint action in Caldas attributed to her structures.
- **4 March 2006:** Montebonito attack.
- **14 November 2007:** Los Planes bombardment accelerates the front’s collapse.
- **3 March 2008:** Pedro Pablo Montoya kills Iván Ríos.
- **18 May 2008:** Mosquera and alias Michín surrender near Sonsón.
- **19 May 2008:** Public press conference in Medellín.
- **2008:** Accepts or faces extensive charges and begins cooperation with authorities.
- **February 2009:** Sentenced to 33 years for Arboleda.

- **6 May 2022:** Justice and Peace judgment issued against Mosquera and eight co-defendants.

- **September 2025:** Publishes _Volver a ser Elda_ with Gustavo Duncan.
