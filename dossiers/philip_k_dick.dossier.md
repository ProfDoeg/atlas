# Philip Kindred Dick: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier concerns the American writer **Philip Kindred Dick (1928–1982)**, generally published as **Philip K. Dick** or **PKD**.

Labels used for contested material:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary records, surviving correspondence, archival material, or multiple independent accounts.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a named witness or biographer but not independently documented here.
- **Allegation:** an accusation for which evidence is incomplete or disputed.
- **Self-report:** something Dick said or wrote about himself; it establishes that he made the claim, not necessarily that his interpretation was correct.
- **Interpretation:** a later critical, medical, religious, or political explanation.
- **Rumor or myth:** a circulating story not adequately supported by evidence.
- **Unresolved:** the available record does not establish a single conclusion.

The principal documentary bases are Dick’s surviving letters, interviews, speeches, fiction and *Exegesis*; archival descriptions from California State University, Fullerton and the University of California, Riverside; contemporary obituary material; Anne R. Dick’s memoir; Paul Williams’s interviews and recollections; Lawrence Sutin’s biography; Gregg Rickman’s interviews and biographical research; bibliographical databases; and subsequent scholarship.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

Dick’s father was **Joseph Edgar Dick**, generally described as a federal employee who worked for the United States Department of Agriculture and later held other government employment. His mother was **Dorothy Kindred Dick**, née Kindred, who worked in government-related positions and later as an editor or personnel employee.

The middle name “Kindred” came from his mother’s family. Published biographies describe predominantly English, Irish, or Scots-Irish American ancestry, but a fully documented genealogical reconstruction was not located in the sources consulted. Claims that the name concealed aristocratic, occult, or unusual ethnic descent belong to fan speculation rather than established genealogy.

### Twin sister

Philip and his fraternal twin, **Jane Charlotte Dick**, were born prematurely on December 16, 1928. Jane died in infancy, usually dated **January 26, 1929**, although some timelines give January 29. The discrepancy likely reflects differing secondary transcriptions and should not be silently resolved without the underlying death certificate.

Jane was buried at Riverside Cemetery in Fort Morgan, Colorado. A stone carried both children’s names, leaving a blank for Philip’s eventual death date. After his death, his ashes were placed beside her and the inscription completed.

Jane’s death became central to accounts of Dick’s psychology and art:

- **Documented fact:** Jane died weeks after their birth, and Dick repeatedly returned to twins, doubles, alternate selves, missing counterparts, and divided identities.
- **Self-report:** Dick treated the dead twin as a continuing psychic presence and sometimes suggested that his life or identity remained paired with hers.
- **Interpretation:** Biographers and psychoanalytic critics have understood his doubling motifs as consequences of survivor guilt or unresolved mourning.
- **Limitation:** No retrospective psychological interpretation can establish precisely what an infant consciously experienced.

### Parental separation and family movements

When Dick was a child, Joseph’s work reportedly required a move to Reno, Nevada. Dorothy declined to accompany him, and the parents separated and divorced. Philip remained primarily with his mother. His childhood included movement among **Chicago**, the **San Francisco Bay Area**, and **Washington, D.C.**

The absent father, controlling or anxious mother, repeated uprooting, and lost twin became recurrent elements in later biographical interpretations. Dick’s fiction often places an ordinary, insecure man between powerful institutions and emotionally formidable women. That correspondence is real as a literary pattern, but one-to-one diagnoses of fictional characters as portraits of family members remain interpretations.

### Marriages and children

Dick married five times:

1. **Jeanette Marlin**, May–November 1948. The marriage was extremely brief.
2. **Kleo Apostolides**, married June 14, 1950; separated and divorced by 1959.
3. **Anne Williams Rubinstein**, married April 1, 1959; marriage ended in separation and divorce, finalized in 1965.
4. **Nancy Hackett**, married July 6, 1966; divorced in 1972.
5. **Leslie “Tessa” Busby**, married April 18, 1973; separated and divorced in 1977.

Children:

- **Laura Archer Dick**, born February 25, 1960, daughter of Anne.
- **Isolde Freya Dick**, later **Isa Dick Hackett**, born March 15, 1967, daughter of Nancy.
- **Christopher Kenneth Dick**, born July 25, 1973, son of Tessa.

After Dick’s death, Laura, Isa, and Christopher became beneficiaries and later stewards of the literary estate. Isa Dick Hackett has been particularly visible in screen licensing and production.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1928–1945

### Chicago and the death of Jane

Dick’s life began with premature birth, medical fragility, and the death of his twin. Later accounts sometimes blame inadequate nutrition or parental neglect for Jane’s death. These are retrospective accusations; no court finding or definitive medical reconstruction establishes culpability. The event nevertheless became the foundational story of Dick’s life.

### California and Washington

After his parents’ separation, Dick lived with Dorothy. He spent part of his childhood in the Washington, D.C., area and then returned to California. He attended schools in Berkeley and Washington, including **John Eaton Elementary School** in Washington and eventually **Berkeley High School**.

Dick described childhood anxiety, recurrent vertigo, agoraphobic or panic-like symptoms, and difficulty attending school. Biographies report psychotherapy from a young age. Exact diagnoses varied across time and were not made under modern diagnostic standards.

### Early reading, music, and writing

Dick read science fiction, fantasy, history, philosophy, and popular science. He was especially devoted to classical music and opera. Music remained one of the few continuous pleasures across his life; it also supplied structures, names, and theological associations for his fiction.

As a boy he produced a newspaper or periodical sometimes identified as the *Daily Dick*, wrote poetry, and composed an early extended work about Edgar Rice Burroughs. He later recalled reading pulp science fiction and authors including A. E. van Vogt. The ontological traps and unstable realities in van Vogt’s work were formative.

The death of his twin, parental conflict, wartime news, fears about fascism, and his attraction to German music coexisted uneasily. A surviving youthful enthusiasm for Wagner and martial German culture has sometimes been cited as evidence of pro-Nazi sentiment. The stronger record shows an adolescent fascination with German cultural power and authoritarian spectacle, followed by a lifelong literary preoccupation with fascism, Nazism, persecution, and false authority. It does not establish mature adherence to National Socialism.

## Education and Formation, 1945–1950

### Berkeley High School

Dick graduated from Berkeley High School in 1947. Ursula K. Le Guin attended the same school and graduated in the same year, but the two apparently did not know one another then. They later corresponded as professional writers.

### University of California, Berkeley

Dick briefly attended the University of California, Berkeley, commonly dated to 1949. He took courses connected with philosophy, psychology, history, or zoology but did not complete a degree.

Accounts differ on why he left:

- **Reported fact:** anxiety, agoraphobia, and difficulty attending required classes contributed.
- **Reported fact:** he objected to compulsory military-training requirements or the institutional climate.
- **Probable combined explanation:** psychological difficulties, political discomfort, financial pressures, and lack of interest in formal credentialing all played roles.

Dick’s education thereafter was largely autodidactic. He read Plato, the pre-Socratics, Christian scripture, Gnostic sources, Spinoza, Kant, Hegel, Schopenhauer, Nietzsche, Bergson, Whitehead, Heidegger, existentialist literature, comparative religion, psychology, and contemporary science. He stated that he read virtually everything by Carl Jung then available in English.

### Music employment

Dick worked in Berkeley record stores, including **Art Music Company**, and in radio or music sales. His knowledge of classical recordings was extensive. The world of the small shop—clerks, customers, commercial pressure, insecure employment, and intense private taste—became a model for the social scale of his fiction.

### First marriage

He married Jeanette Marlin in 1948. It lasted roughly six months. Surviving summaries give few securely documented details, and later biographical accounts are partly anecdotal.

## Early Career and Second Marriage, 1950–1958

### Kleo Apostolides

Dick married **Kleo Apostolides**, a politically active Berkeley student, in 1950. Their household participated in the Bay Area’s left-wing, bohemian, and student culture.

In 1955 FBI agents visited the couple. The documentary fact is the visit; explanations vary. Dick and Kleo associated it with her socialist or left-wing political associations and with interest in recruiting or questioning Dick. Later recollections sometimes amplify this into a formal attempt to make Dick an informant. Without the complete underlying file, the safest description is that federal agents contacted them in a Cold War context and that Dick remembered the encounter as menacing.

Dick’s mature politics resisted stable labeling. He distrusted communism, fascism, corporate rule, police states, religious authoritarianism, and American security agencies. His early social environment included leftists; later he supplied information to the FBI about people he regarded as communist-connected. He was neither a consistent party activist nor an officeholder.

### Entry into professional writing

Dick began selling short fiction around 1951–1952. His first published story is usually identified as **“Roog”** or **“Beyond Lies the Wub,”** depending on whether “first” means first sale, first acceptance, or first appearance. “Beyond Lies the Wub” appeared in *Planet Stories* in 1952; “Roog” also dates from the opening phase of his career.

His agent **Scott Meredith** became important in placing his work. Editors including **Donald A. Wollheim** helped establish him in paperback science fiction. By June 1953 Dick said he had seven stories simultaneously on newsstands.

Dick wrote rapidly because pulp and paperback rates were low. His method commonly began with an ontological premise, opposing groups or alternate explanations, and characters whose apparent reality progressively collapsed. During productive bursts he used amphetamines or prescription stimulants, a practice that eventually became medically and socially damaging.

### First novels

His first published novels were:

- *Solar Lottery* (1955)
- *The World Jones Made* (1956)
- *The Man Who Japed* (1956)
- *Eye in the Sky* (1957)
- *The Cosmic Puppets* (1957; expanded from an earlier magazine version)
- *Dr. Futurity* (1960; magazine form earlier)
- *Vulcan’s Hammer* (1960; expanded from a novella)

He also wrote realist or “mainstream” novels about marriage, business, status, and failure. Publishers rejected most of them during his lifetime. This was one of his largest professional disappointments: he wanted recognition as a general American novelist but was economically confined to the low-paying science-fiction paperback market.

### Point Reyes and Anne Rubinstein

Dick and Kleo moved from Berkeley to **Point Reyes Station** in Marin County. There he met **Anne Williams Rubinstein**, a widow, jewelry maker, and mother. Their relationship began before both prior marriages were fully disentangled. Dick left Kleo and married Anne in 1959.

## Chronological Life History

## 1958–1965: Anne Dick, Marin County, and the breakthrough years

### Domestic and economic life

Dick moved into Anne’s larger household in Point Reyes. Anne operated a jewelry business and managed property and family responsibilities. Dick wrote under pressure to contribute financially. Their daughter Laura was born in 1960.

The marriage was artistically productive and personally violent.

- **Documented through Dick’s correspondence:** he described “dreadful violent fights,” mutual physical struggle, and smashed objects.
- **Reported by Anne and biographers:** Dick struck Anne and behaved unpredictably.
- **Reported in competing accounts:** Dick portrayed Anne as controlling, manipulative, or dangerous and believed at times that she intended to have him committed or kill him.
- **Assessment:** a seriously conflictual and at times physically violent marriage is well supported. Precise responsibility for individual incidents is disputed. Dick’s paranoid interpretations should not be treated as proof of Anne’s intent.

Dick underwent psychiatric treatment and consumed prescribed medications, including stimulants and tranquilizers. During one crisis he reportedly attempted to arrange Anne’s involuntary psychiatric commitment. The effort failed. Some accounts say clinicians instead focused on Dick’s condition.

### Realist novels

During this period he wrote works including:

- *Confessions of a Crap Artist*
- *The Man Whose Teeth Were All Exactly Alike*
- *Puttering About in a Small Land*
- *In Milton Lumky Territory*
- *Humpty Dumpty in Oakland*
- *The Broken Bubble*
- *Voices from the Street*

Most remained unpublished until after his death. *Confessions of a Crap Artist* was the only realist novel published during his lifetime, in 1975, and then initially outside the United States.

### *Time Out of Joint* and constructed reality

*Time Out of Joint* (1959) moved Dick toward the form that made his reputation: an apparently mundane world revealed as a manufactured environment. It joined Cold War paranoia, advertising, suburban conformity, and ontological uncertainty.

### *The Man in the High Castle*

Published in 1962, *The Man in the High Castle* imagines an Axis victory in the Second World War and a divided, occupied United States. Dick used the *I Ching* during composition, sometimes allowing its hexagrams to guide plot decisions. The novel contains a novel within the novel describing another alternate outcome.

It won the **1963 Hugo Award for Best Novel**, Dick’s major lifetime award. The official Hugo record lists it against finalists including Arthur C. Clarke’s *A Fall of Moondust* and H. Beam Piper’s *Little Fuzzy*.

The award raised his prestige but did not establish financial security. Dick continued producing books quickly for relatively small advances.

### Episcopal affiliation and Bishop James Pike

During the Marin years Dick became involved with the Episcopal Church and knew **Bishop James A. Pike**, a prominent and controversial cleric interested in theology, heresy, psychic phenomena, and survival after death. Pike’s later death in the Judean Desert in 1969 profoundly affected Dick. Pike became a major model for Timothy Archer in Dick’s final completed novel.

### Breakdown of the marriage

Dick and Anne separated amid escalating accusations and psychiatric instability. The divorce became final in 1965. Later portrayals split sharply:

- Anne’s memoir presents Dick as gifted, frightened, manipulative, and unstable.
- Dick’s letters portray Anne as domineering and potentially murderous.
- Biographers generally find elements of mutual hostility but stronger evidence for Dick’s paranoia and violence than for his extreme allegations against Anne.

## 1964–1970: Prolific middle period, Nancy Hackett, and deepening instability

### Major novels

Dick’s most concentrated sequence of major science-fiction novels appeared in the 1960s:

- *Martian Time-Slip* (1964)
- *The Simulacra* (1964)
- *Clans of the Alphane Moon* (1964)
- *The Penultimate Truth* (1964)
- *Dr. Bloodmoney, or How We Got Along After the Bomb* (1965)
- *The Three Stigmata of Palmer Eldritch* (1965)
- *The Crack in Space* (1966)
- *Now Wait for Last Year* (1966)
- *The Zap Gun* (1967)
- *Counter-Clock World* (1967)
- *The Ganymede Takeover* (1967), with Ray Nelson
- *Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?* (1968)
- *Galactic Pot-Healer* (1969)
- *Ubik* (1969)
- *A Maze of Death* (1970)
- *Our Friends from Frolix 8* (1970)

These books examine artificial identity, corporate and governmental manipulation, psychiatric classification, narcotics, precognition, commodified religion, entropy, and counterfeit reality.

### Nancy Hackett

Dick met the much younger **Nancy Hackett**, who had experienced mental-health difficulties. They married in 1966. Their daughter Isa was born in 1967.

The marriage took place during continuing stimulant use, depression, and financial insecurity. Dick’s work habits alternated between extraordinary output and periods of paralysis. Nancy and Dick separated; their divorce was completed in 1972.

### Amphetamines and other drugs

Dick used amphetamines for years, often in a quasi-medical or work-related context, but the pattern became dependency.

Important distinctions:

- **Documented fact:** he consumed large quantities of stimulants and associated with younger drug users in the late 1960s and early 1970s.
- **Self-report:** he sometimes dramatized himself as deeply immersed in drug culture.
- **Reported by acquaintances:** his actual use of some illicit drugs was less extensive than his reputation suggested; amphetamines, prescription drugs, and episodic LSD exposure are better supported than indiscriminate use of every drug around him.
- **Interpretation:** drug use plausibly contributed to sleeplessness, paranoia, cardiovascular risk, and psychiatric crises, but it cannot by itself explain his entire metaphysical system.

### Political atmosphere

The assassinations, Vietnam War, surveillance controversies, radical movements, and Nixon administration reinforced Dick’s fear of police-state power. He opposed authoritarian structures more consistently than he supported any party program.

His fiction was not conventional movement literature. It frequently depicts rebels as compromised, institutions as self-protecting, and ideological certainties as mechanisms of domination.

### James Pike’s death

Pike died in 1969 after becoming lost in the Judean Desert. Dick followed both Pike’s heterodox theology and the controversy surrounding efforts to contact Pike’s dead son. Pike’s death merged in Dick’s imagination with questions about information surviving bodily death.

## 1970–1972: San Rafael commune, break-in, Vancouver, and X-Kalay

### San Rafael household

After Nancy left, Dick’s home in **San Rafael, California**, became a gathering place for younger people, including drug users and people in crisis. The household supplied material for *A Scanner Darkly*.

Accounts differ over whether it was a “commune.” Dick was the leaseholder or principal adult and sometimes played rescuer, host, paternal figure, romantic pursuer, informant, and suspicious observer. His relationships with vulnerable young women have been criticized for age and power imbalances, although no criminal conviction relating to these relationships is documented.

### The November 1971 break-in

On or about November 17, 1971, Dick returned to find his house and locked file cabinet damaged. Papers were disturbed or removed. The physical break-in is a **documented fact**.

Dick proposed numerous perpetrators:

- federal agents;
- local police;
- political radicals or the Black Panthers;
- drug associates;
- religious organizations;
- thieves seeking manuscripts;
- Dick himself, possibly in a dissociative state.

Law-enforcement investigation did not identify a perpetrator. The case remains **unresolved**.

The theory that Dick staged the event appears in biographical discussion but is not established. Likewise, claims that the FBI or CIA conducted the operation are unsupported by a known admission or conclusive record. The incident mattered regardless of authorship: it intensified his fear that he was watched and endangered.

### Vancouver science-fiction convention

In February 1972 Dick traveled to Vancouver as guest of honor at the **Vancouver Science Fiction Convention**. He delivered “The Android and the Human,” an address about humanization, technology, and resistance to mechanical systems.

He became attached to a woman named Janis and announced that he would remain in Vancouver. The relationship did not develop as he expected. Depressed, isolated, and using drugs, he attempted suicide by taking an overdose, cutting his wrist, and entering a car or garage environment where exhaust might kill him. Accounts differ in operational detail, but a serious suicide attempt is well supported.

### X-Kalay

Dick entered **X-Kalay**, a Synanon-influenced drug-rehabilitation community in Vancouver. His stay was short, approximately several weeks.

The center’s authoritarian group methods and population of addicts helped inform *A Scanner Darkly*. Dick later suggested that his Canadian experience allowed him to observe drug rehabilitation from inside. It was not a long-term successful treatment program in the conventional sense, but the move interrupted the San Rafael crisis.

### Move to Orange County

Professor **Willis E. McNelly** of California State University, Fullerton, had corresponded with Dick and helped arrange his relocation to Orange County. Dick moved to **Fullerton**, later living in Santa Ana.

McNelly persuaded him to place manuscripts, correspondence, typescripts, and other materials at the university. The resulting archive is among the principal collections for Dick research.

## 1972–1976: Tessa Busby, recovery, *Flow My Tears*, and 2-3-74

### Tessa Busby

Dick met **Leslie “Tessa” Busby**, then a young woman, in Orange County. They married in April 1973. Their son Christopher was born in July.

Tessa typed manuscripts, maintained the household, and witnessed the 1974 experiences. The marriage later deteriorated, but her testimony is an important source because she was present during Dick’s most famous visionary period.

### *Flow My Tears, the Policeman Said*

Dick drafted *Flow My Tears, the Policeman Said* rapidly. Published in 1974, it depicts a celebrity erased from official existence inside an American police state. It received a Nebula nomination and won the **John W. Campbell Memorial Award** for Best Science Fiction Novel.

### The February–March 1974 experiences

Following dental treatment in February 1974, Dick received pain medication from a delivery woman wearing a Christian fish symbol. He later said sunlight striking the pendant emitted or triggered a beam of pink light. The resulting experiences continued through February and March—hence the shorthand **“2-3-74.”**

What Dick reported included:

- rapid visual imagery and geometric patterns;
- a sense of external information entering his mind;
- apparent memories of first-century Rome;
- the conviction that apostolic Christians and the Roman Empire existed beneath modern California;
- dreams and auditory messages;
- a presence he called Zebra, VALIS, living information, plasmate, Logos, or the Vast Active Living Intelligence System;
- a temporary identification with a first-century personality named Thomas;
- the feeling that his young son had an undiagnosed medical danger.

### Christopher’s illness

Dick told Tessa that Christopher had a dangerous inguinal hernia or related condition and should receive medical attention. A physician subsequently found a condition requiring treatment.

- **Documented core:** Dick urgently raised a medical concern and the child was medically evaluated and treated.
- **Self-report:** the information came from the pink beam or superhuman intelligence.
- **Alternative explanation:** Dick may have unconsciously noticed physical symptoms or absorbed ordinary medical cues.
- **Unresolved:** the event cannot establish the supernatural source Dick proposed, but it became his strongest personal evidence that the experience contained veridical information.

### Interpretations of 2-3-74

Dick considered many competing explanations:

- God, Christ, or the Logos;
- Gnostic revelation;
- extraterrestrial or satellite transmission;
- Soviet psychotronic experimentation;
- a future artificial intelligence communicating backward in time;
- the spirit of Elijah;
- Jungian archetypes;
- a surviving information-organism;
- a neurological event;
- drug effects;
- schizophrenia, temporal-lobe dysfunction, or another psychiatric disorder;
- his own subliminal knowledge.

He did not consistently settle on one.

Later writers often present a false binary—either divine revelation or psychosis. Dick’s notebooks show a repeated process of hypothesis, rejection, recombination, and self-criticism. A medical explanation is plausible but cannot be diagnosed retrospectively from literary documents alone.

### The *Exegesis*

Dick began an enormous handwritten and typed commentary, eventually about 8,000 pages. It became known as the **Exegesis**. It is not a finished systematic book. It combines diary, theology, letter drafts, dream interpretation, textual commentary, diagrams, speculative physics, and self-analysis.

The 2011 edited volume *The Exegesis of Philip K. Dick* contains only a selection—roughly 15 percent, according to the *Encyclopedia of Science Fiction*.

### Nixon and “King Felix”

Dick associated his visions with the fall of Richard Nixon. He interpreted political events as evidence of a hidden struggle between oppressive “Empire” and salvific intelligence. These claims belong to his private theological-political interpretation, not evidence that Dick materially caused or possessed classified foreknowledge of Watergate.

## 1974–1977: FBI correspondence, Lem controversy, France, and marital collapse

### Stanisław Lem and the FBI

Polish writer **Stanisław Lem** admired Dick and regarded him as an important American science-fiction writer. A Polish translation and royalty dispute arose amid the difficulty of Cold War currency transfers and intermediary arrangements.

Dick wrote to the FBI in 1974 alleging that “Lem” might function as a committee or composite operation associated with communist cultural strategy. He also named critics and intermediaries including Peter Fitting, Fredric Jameson, Darko Suvin, and Franz Rottensteiner.

Evidentiary status:

- **Documented fact:** Dick sent such allegations to the FBI.
- **Documented fact:** Lem was a real individual author, not a collective identity.
- **Unsupported allegation:** that “Lem” was a communist committee directing a subversive network.
- **Context:** royalty conflict, Cold War suspicion, Dick’s deteriorating relationship with some Marxist critics, and his recurrent paranoia.
- **Consequence:** the Science Fiction Writers of America rescinded or failed to continue Lem’s honorary membership after controversy over Lem’s criticisms of American science fiction; Dick’s precise causal role is debated, but his denunciatory correspondence is part of the record.

The episode contradicts any simple picture of Dick as consistently aligned with the New Left. It also supplies critics with one of the clearest examples of his willingness to turn private paranoia into an accusation delivered to state authorities.

### Paul Williams

Journalist and critic **Paul Williams**, founder of *Crawdaddy!*, first met Dick in 1968. He became a friend, interviewer, advocate, and eventually literary executor.

Williams’s 1975 *Rolling Stone* profile introduced Dick to a wider countercultural readership. His later book *Only Apparently Real* assembled interviews and biographical material and helped shape the early posthumous image.

### European reputation and Metz

Dick’s reputation in France and elsewhere in Europe often exceeded his American prestige. He visited France in 1977 for the Metz science-fiction festival and delivered **“If You Find This World Bad, You Should See Some of the Others.”**

In the speech he publicly proposed that some of his fictional worlds might be genuine alternate realities and that déjà vu could register changes in a programmed universe. The audience reportedly included fascination, confusion, and laughter.

Modern simulation-culture communities frequently circulate edited video from Metz as though Dick had straightforwardly “proved” the simulation hypothesis or predicted the “Mandela effect.” That is a **later mythologizing interpretation**. The authentic speech contains speculative claims but no empirical demonstration.

### End of marriage to Tessa

Dick and Tessa separated, and their divorce was completed in 1977. Accounts refer to emotional volatility, financial pressure, infidelity, religious obsession, and Dick’s absorption in the *Exegesis*. Later relations were sometimes contentious, but Tessa continued to preserve and interpret aspects of his life.

## 1977–1982: Final relationships, VALIS, *Blade Runner*, illness, and death

### Doris Sauter and Joan Simpson

Dick had important late relationships with **Doris Sauter** and **Joan Simpson**. Joan, a Canadian woman with whom he developed a strong romantic relationship, declined to reorganize her life permanently around him. Her departure or refusal contributed to depression and appears in transformed form in *VALIS*.

Doris Sauter was a close friend, confidante, and later source for biographical research. Dick’s late letters show recurrent efforts to find a companion, rapid idealization, fear of abandonment, and withdrawal.

### Patricia Warrick and academic recognition

Scholar **Patricia S. Warrick** interviewed Dick and wrote extensively about his cybernetic and philosophical imagination. Willis McNelly, Fredric Jameson, Peter Fitting, Darko Suvin, R. D. Mullen, and other academics helped move discussion of his work from fan criticism toward sustained scholarly analysis.

Dick welcomed serious attention but could become suspicious of critics’ motives, especially when Marxist frameworks were involved.

### The VALIS books

Late fiction transformed 2-3-74 into overlapping autobiographical and theological narratives:

- *Radio Free Albemuth*, written first but rejected and published posthumously in 1985.
- *VALIS* (1981), in which “Philip K. Dick” and “Horselover Fat” are both characters.
- *The Divine Invasion* (1981).
- *The Transmigration of Timothy Archer* (1982).

The last three were later marketed together as the “VALIS Trilogy,” although *Timothy Archer* differs sharply in method and does not reproduce the same cosmology. *Radio Free Albemuth* is sometimes called a precursor or alternate first VALIS novel.

### *A Scanner Darkly*

Published in 1977, *A Scanner Darkly* recast the San Rafael drug world as a surveillance tragedy. Its afterword lists friends whom drugs left dead or permanently damaged. Dick wrote that they had been punished excessively for wanting to play and framed himself as one of the injured.

The book should not be treated as literal memoir: characters and events are composites. It remains the most direct fictional record of his drug milieu.

### *Blade Runner*

Ridley Scott’s *Blade Runner* was adapted from *Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?* Dick initially disliked a screenplay he believed discarded the novel’s moral structure and emphasis on animals. After seeing production footage, he praised the film’s visual realization and said film and book could reinforce one another.

He died before the movie’s June 1982 release. The familiar story that he died wholly unknown immediately before sudden Hollywood fame is exaggerated: he had won a Hugo, had significant European and academic recognition, knew about the film, viewed footage, and received adaptation income. Nevertheless, the enormous commercial afterlife of his intellectual property lay ahead.

### Final illness

In February 1982 Dick experienced visual difficulties and symptoms of a stroke. He was found unconscious at his Santa Ana home and hospitalized. He suffered further cerebral injury and became brain-dead. Life support was withdrawn, and he died on **March 2, 1982**.

Contemporary obituary summaries describe death from complications following a stroke. Long-term stimulant use, hypertension, stress, and other health conditions have been proposed as contributing risk factors, but the consulted sources do not establish a single toxicological cause.

His father arranged for burial of the cremated remains beside Jane in Fort Morgan, completing the shared gravestone.

## Employment, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Employment

Before living primarily by writing, Dick worked in:

- record retail;
- classical-music sales;
- radio or electronics-related retail;
- short-lived conventional employment around Berkeley.

He did not hold a stable academic appointment. His principal professional structure consisted of literary agents, paperback publishers, magazine editors, foreign publishers, and later film-option agreements.

### Publishing economics

Dick’s productivity reflected the poor economics of mid-century paperback science fiction. He often wrote rapidly for small advances and lived with tax and cash-flow anxiety. Foreign sales, particularly French and British editions, became increasingly important.

His 1975 interview mentioned an English sale worth approximately $8,000–$9,000, a substantial amount relative to his earlier advances. Exact lifetime earnings remain difficult to reconstruct because of contracts, agent commissions, taxes, foreign-currency issues, and irregular publication.

He was not wealthy at death by later estate standards. The large value of his intellectual property arose primarily through posthumous adaptations and reissues.

### Literary estate

Paul Williams served as literary executor during an important period and helped organize posthumous publication. The author’s children later became the principal beneficiaries and administrators through the **Philip K. Dick Testamentary Trust**.

**Electric Shepherd Productions** serves as the estate’s media-production and development arm. Its activities include licensing and participating in adaptations.

### Estate litigation

A federal case arising from *The Adjustment Bureau* involved **Laura Archer Dick Coelho**, the testamentary trust, and production or distribution entities. The pleadings concerned contract, compensation, copyright, and accounting issues connected with the adaptation of “Adjustment Team.”

This was posthumous commercial litigation involving heirs and rights holders, not evidence that Philip K. Dick personally committed fraud. Estate disputes demonstrate the much greater value his work acquired after death.

### Technologies and inventions

Dick held no significant known engineering patents and did not establish a technology company. His importance to technological culture is conceptual rather than inventive:

- artificial human identity;
- machine-mediated empathy;
- predictive policing;
- memory implantation;
- surveillance;
- corporate control of perception;
- virtual or simulated realities;
- ubiquitous personalized advertising;
- autonomous weapons;
- artificial intelligence and ambiguous personhood.

Claims that he “invented” modern technologies are rhetorical. His fiction anticipated or dramatized social problems around technologies but did not supply patentable implementations.

## Political, Religious, Philosophical, and Artistic Development

### Political development

Dick’s political trajectory cannot be reduced to left or right.

- Berkeley associations and his marriage to Kleo connected him to socialist and progressive circles.
- Cold War repression and FBI attention encouraged distrust of state power.
- He opposed fascism and repeatedly represented Nazi victory or authoritarian rule as catastrophic.
- He distrusted organized communism and denounced Lem and Marxist critics to the FBI.
- He opposed the Vietnam-era security state and Nixonian authoritarianism.
- He was attracted to the dignity of marginal, working-class, mentally ill, addicted, and socially unsuccessful people.
- He sometimes used racial, sexual, and ethnic stereotypes present in mid-century popular fiction.
- His political fiction generally privileges individual empathy over ideological systems.

### Religion

Dick’s religious development included:

1. Childhood exposure to Christianity.
2. Adult Episcopal participation in Marin County.
3. Interest in Bishop Pike’s heterodox theology.
4. Study of the *I Ching* and comparative religion.
5. Jungian psychology and archetypes.
6. Gnosticism, Neoplatonism, early Christianity, Zoroastrian dualism, Jewish mysticism, and Pauline theology.
7. The 2-3-74 experiences and the *Exegesis*.
8. Persistent uncertainty between supernatural and neurological explanations.

His aphorism “The Empire never ended” compresses a central late idea: the oppressive structure of imperial Rome survives beneath changing historical forms.

### Philosophy of reality

Dick repeatedly asked two questions:

- What is real?
- What constitutes an authentic human being?

His provisional answers emphasized empathy, kindness, grief, and resistance to imposed false realities. Androids can be humanely responsive; biological humans can behave mechanically. Reality is not merely subjective fantasy but something that resists manipulation and persists when belief fails.

### Music and artistic influence

Music—especially Beethoven, Bach, Wagner, Schubert, and classical recordings—provided emotional and structural models. Wagner’s combination of myth and total artwork fascinated him despite the political burden of Wagnerian reception.

Important literary influences included A. E. van Vogt, Robert Heinlein, Theodore Sturgeon, Clifford Simak, Flaubert, Kafka, Dostoevsky, Joyce, Proust, Orwell, Huxley, and existentialist and modernist fiction. Dick also read Carl Jung deeply.

- **Brian Aldiss:** British colleague and admirer.
- **Ursula K. Le Guin:** school contemporary and later correspondent.
- **Stanisław Lem:** admirer turned antagonist in the royalty and FBI controversy.
- **James A. Pike:** religious friend and model for Timothy Archer.
- **K. W. Jeter, Tim Powers, and James Blaylock:** younger Orange County writers associated with Dick late in his life.
- **Thomas M. Disch, Norman Spinrad, Harlan Ellison, Robert Silverberg:** members of the broader professional network, with relationships ranging from friendly to argumentative.

### Romantic and domestic network

Dick repeatedly built intimate relationships quickly and then experienced them as rescue, danger, engulfment, or abandonment. His wives and partners provided financial, clerical, emotional, and creative labor. They also became fictional models, sometimes in unflattering or hostile forms.

Women close to him disputed how biographers represented them. The archive should therefore be read as a multi-voiced record rather than as Dick’s testimony alone.

### Adversaries and perceived enemies

Some adversaries were real—estranged spouses, disappointed business contacts, hostile critics, and parties in royalty disputes. Others were products of suspicion:

- imagined federal conspirators in the break-in;
- alleged secret political organizations;
- the supposed “Lem committee”;
- occult or religious agencies;
- Soviet or extraterrestrial transmitters.

Dick frequently revised the identity of the presumed enemy. That instability is evidence against treating any one theory as established.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Other Negative Material

## Domestic violence

**Status: documented in general; disputed in individual details.**

Dick admitted violent fights with Anne. Anne and later biographers described physical aggression by him. Accounts also describe Anne fighting back and a mutually destructive domestic environment.

There is no known criminal conviction arising from this violence. Absence of conviction does not negate the admissions and testimony, but precise incident-level reconstruction remains incomplete.

## Attempted involuntary commitment of Anne

**Status: reported fact.**

Dick sought psychiatric intervention against Anne during their marital conflict, portraying her as dangerous. Later accounts suggest clinicians did not accept his version and instead questioned his stability. The episode is relevant both to domestic coercion and to Dick’s fear of psychiatric confinement.

## Relationships with younger and vulnerable women

**Status: documented age and dependency disparities; interpretation disputed.**

Dick repeatedly entered relationships with substantially younger women, some experiencing addiction or mental illness. Critics interpret his rescuer role as controlling or exploitative. Defenders stress that the relationships were consensual and that Dick also provided housing and assistance. No single label captures all such relationships, and no related criminal conviction was found.

## Drug use

**Status: documented.**

Amphetamine dependency, prescription-drug misuse, and immersion in a drug-using social scene are strongly supported. Claims that every strange experience resulted from LSD or that he was continuously intoxicated are oversimplifications.

## Suicide attempts and self-harm

**Status: documented or strongly reported.**

Dick attempted suicide in Vancouver in 1972. Biographical accounts also report earlier or later suicidal episodes, overdoses, and recurrent ideation. Exact totals differ because some incidents were ambiguous or known only through Dick’s changing accounts.

## Psychiatric diagnosis

**Status: multiple historical assessments; no reliable retrospective single diagnosis.**

Dick received psychotherapy and psychiatric treatment and was variously described as anxious, agoraphobic, depressive, paranoid, schizoid, or potentially schizophrenic. He sometimes diagnosed himself and sometimes rejected clinicians’ labels.

Modern suggestions include bipolar disorder, temporal-lobe epilepsy, psychotic depression, stimulant-induced psychosis, dissociation, and schizotypal traits. These are retrospective interpretations, not settled findings. His sustained employment, self-critical notebooks, and awareness of alternative explanations complicate simplistic descriptions of him as continuously psychotic.

## FBI relationship

**Status: documented contact and correspondence; extent often exaggerated.**

The FBI contacted Dick and Kleo in the 1950s. Dick later corresponded voluntarily with the bureau and supplied allegations about Lem and others. This makes him, at least episodically, an informant in the ordinary sense of giving information to law enforcement.

There is insufficient evidence that he was a controlled, paid, or long-term intelligence asset. Claims that he worked secretly for the CIA, encoded operations in his novels, or was murdered by an agency remain conspiracy narratives.

## The 1971 break-in

**Status: crime documented; perpetrator unresolved.**

No theory—FBI, CIA, police, radicals, associates, burglars, or self-staging—has been conclusively proven. Presenting any as settled falsifies the evidence.

## Lem accusations

**Status: accusations documented; central allegation false or unsupported.**

Lem was not a composite communist committee. Dick’s accusations affected his reputation and relations with international science fiction. The episode is variously interpreted as paranoia, Cold War politics, retaliation over royalties, or all three.

## Racial and gender criticism

**Status: critical interpretation grounded in textual evidence.**

Critics have identified racial stereotypes, racialized language, recurrent “castrating” or manipulative female figures, and narrow female characterization in some novels. Other works expose racism, fascism, antisemitism, slavery, and dehumanization.

These findings are not mutually exclusive: a writer can oppose institutional racism while reproducing stereotypes, and can create important women characters while repeatedly using misogynistic narrative patterns.

## Nazism and antisemitism claims

**Status: youthful material and cultural fascination documented; mature Nazi adherence unsupported.**

Dick’s early attraction to Wagner, German culture, and the theatrical force of Nazi imagery has generated accusations of ideological sympathy. His major fiction, particularly *The Man in the High Castle*, presents genocidal fascist domination as horrifying. He had Jewish friends and family connections and developed an intense interest in Jewish and early Christian history.

The evidence supports fascination and ambivalence, not a settled finding that he was a Nazi. Individual stereotyped passages should nevertheless be assessed rather than excused by the general anti-fascist tendency.

## Plagiarism, fraud, and criminality

No substantial evidence was located that Dick committed literary plagiarism, commercial fraud, money laundering, political violence, or serious profit-driven crime. Drug possession or use occurred in his environment, but no major conviction defines his biography.

His repeated appropriation of acquaintances as fictional models generated resentment but is not normally a legal offense. Posthumous copyright disputes concern licensing and accounting by heirs or companies, not personal criminal conduct by Dick.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

## “The pink beam diagnosed his son”

**Story:** A supernatural pink beam told Dick that Christopher had a life-threatening medical condition.

**Origin and circulation:** Dick’s own accounts, Tessa’s recollections, the *Exegesis*, interviews, and later biographies.

**Evidence:** Dick raised a specific concern, and a medically significant condition was found. Evidence does not establish how he obtained the insight.

**Effect:** It became the most persuasive miracle story in the Dick cult and encouraged readers to treat the rest of 2-3-74 as objectively verified.

## “He remembered ancient Rome”

**Story:** Dick recovered the memories of an early Christian named Thomas and perceived modern California as a disguise laid over Roman imperial reality.

**Evidence:** His written reports document the experience. No independent archaeological or historical evidence verifies literal recovered memory.

**Effect:** It shaped *VALIS*, the *Exegesis*, and the slogan “The Empire never ended.”

## “He proved we live in a simulation”

**Story:** Dick publicly revealed a computer-programmed universe decades before *The Matrix*.

**Evidence:** The 1977 Metz speech genuinely discusses programmed reality, alternate worlds, and déjà vu. It offers speculation and autobiographical testimony, not a reproducible proof.

**Circulation:** Video platforms, simulation-theory communities, “Mandela effect” forums, UFO discourse, and conspiracy media.

**Effect:** Dick is increasingly presented as a technological prophet rather than primarily a novelist.

## “The CIA or FBI stole his papers”

**Story:** Intelligence officers staged the 1971 break-in to recover secret information encoded in his fiction.

**Evidence:** The break-in occurred; no conclusive link to an agency has surfaced. Dick himself named mutually incompatible suspects.

**Effect:** The mystery fused his life with the plots of his books and sustains intelligence-related mythology.

## “Stanisław Lem was not one man”

**Story:** “Lem” was a communist committee producing strategically varied cultural propaganda.

**Evidence against:** Lem’s individual identity, biography, manuscripts, correspondence, and public life are overwhelmingly documented.

**Effect:** The allegation damaged Dick’s relationship with Lem and is now cited as an example of paranoid Cold War behavior.

## “Dick predicted the future”

Frequently cited supposed predictions include predictive policing, personalized advertising, implanted memories, virtual reality, authoritarian data systems, and artificial humans.

The stronger formulation is that Dick extrapolated existing tendencies—Cold War surveillance, behavioral psychology, computers, advertising, psychoactive drugs, and corporate consolidation. Similar ideas appeared in other speculative writing. His distinctive achievement was combining them with unstable identity and moral uncertainty.

## “He died just before becoming famous”

Dick died four months before *Blade Runner* opened, and the film did accelerate his global cultural afterlife. But he was already a Hugo winner, widely translated, the subject of academic study, and an honored convention guest. The myth compresses a real posthumous expansion into an inaccurately total lifetime obscurity.

## The blank gravestone

The image of Jane’s marker waiting for Philip’s name has become a biographical emblem: the dead twin had supposedly reserved his place from infancy.

The gravestone is real. Interpretations of destiny, divided identity, or a life lived in Jane’s shadow are symbolic elaborations.

## VALIS as alien satellite

Some followers read VALIS literally as an extraterrestrial satellite, Soviet experiment, or trans-temporal artificial intelligence. Dick himself considered each possibility but repeatedly changed theories. Treating one discarded hypothesis as his final doctrine misrepresents the *Exegesis*.

## Archival Material

### California State University, Fullerton

The Willis E. McNelly Science Fiction Collection holds the principal **Philip K. Dick Papers, 1959–1985**. The collection contains manuscripts, working papers, correspondence, first editions, critical studies, and ephemera.

McNelly’s direct relationship with Dick gives the collection unusual provenance. Some materials were later reorganized, withdrawn, or distributed, so researchers should consult current finding aids rather than assume the original deposit remains intact in its first arrangement.

### University of California, Riverside

The Eaton Collection holds a separate collection concerning Dick, including publications, clippings, short stories, and manuscripts.

### Other repositories

Related correspondence appears in author, editor, and science-fiction collections elsewhere, including the Merril Collection in Toronto and the Cushing Memorial Library at Texas A&M. These holdings are dispersed because Dick corresponded intensively with writers, editors, scholars, fans, and family members.

### FBI and government records

FBI records relating to Dick, Kleo, and his later communications are central to studying his real contacts with the security state. They must be distinguished from Dick’s theories about what agencies secretly did. A file recording an allegation proves receipt of the allegation, not its truth.

## Death and Estate

Dick suffered a stroke in February 1982 and died after further cerebral injury on March 2. The New York Times obituary described him as the award-winning author of dozens of science-fiction novels and story collections and noted his five marriages.

His death preceded *Blade Runner* by approximately four months. Initial film proceeds were modest compared with the later value of the estate.

Posthumous administration involved:

- publication of unpublished realist novels;
- restoration or expansion of altered texts;
- selected letters;
- publication of portions of the *Exegesis*;
- reissue programs;
- film, television, comics, game, and audio licensing;
- litigation over adaptation revenue and contractual rights.

The children ultimately became central stewards of the estate. Their commercial development transformed a precarious authorial legacy into a durable intellectual-property structure.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Literary canonization

Dick moved from paperback genre writer to a Library of America author and sustained subject of university scholarship. This transition depended on:

- European reception;
- academic science-fiction studies;
- advocacy by Paul Williams, Willis McNelly, Gregg Rickman, Patricia Warrick, and others;
- posthumous publication of realist fiction and letters;
- film adaptations;
- cyberpunk and postmodern criticism.

His unevenness remains part of the record. He wrote rapidly, recycled devices, produced contradictory endings, and published books he considered compromised. Canonization has not eliminated the industrial conditions under which the work was made.

### Influence on science fiction

Dick’s influence is visible in cyberpunk, alternate history, paranoid fiction, theological science fiction, and stories of artificial identity. Writers often compared with or influenced by him include William Gibson, Jonathan Lethem, Kim Stanley Robinson, K. W. Jeter, Tim Powers, James Tiptree Jr., Ursula K. Le Guin, Stanisław Lem, and numerous filmmakers and game designers.

William Gibson has qualified the frequent claim of direct influence, saying he had not read much Dick before beginning to write. This is a useful distinction between direct literary influence and a later shared cultural genealogy.

### Film culture

*Blade Runner*, *Total Recall*, and *Minority Report* made “Philip K. Dick” a shorthand for uncertain reality, artificial memory, surveillance, and unstable humanity. The films often diverge from his politics and plots. Their visual authority can obscure the domestic, economic, religious, and comic dimensions of the books.

### Philosophy and technological culture

Dick is frequently cited in debates about:

- artificial intelligence and personhood;
- deepfakes and counterfeit evidence;
- virtual reality;
- predictive policing;
- algorithmic governance;
- surveillance capitalism;
- simulation hypotheses;
- altered memory;
- disinformation and manufactured consensus.

These appropriations range from careful analysis to slogan. “Dickian” and “PKD-like” now describe situations where institutions, media, and technology make reality difficult to verify.

### Religious and occult reception

Readers have treated Dick as:

- Christian visionary;
- modern Gnostic;
- mystical philosopher;
- alien contactee;
- psychotic autobiographer;
- technological prophet;
- unwilling occultist;
- satirist of all such categories.

The *Exegesis* supports multiple readings because Dick himself continually generated and destroyed explanatory systems.

### Negative reassessment

Recent criticism has examined:

- domestic violence;
- treatment of wives and younger partners;
- misogynistic character patterns;
- racial stereotypes;
- his allegations to the FBI;
- romanticization of psychiatric crisis;
- fandom’s tendency to convert illness and addiction into genius mythology.

No major new criminal revelation from 2024–2026 was located in the adversarial search. Recent discussion primarily revisits established archival controversies or applies contemporary critical frameworks to the fiction.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted record are included. Mere chronological overlap is excluded.

### Carl Gustav Jung

**Evidentiary label: documented intellectual influence.**

Dick stated in an interview with Richard Lupoff that he had read all the Jung available in English at the time. Jung’s archetypes, synchronicity, collective unconscious, and autonomous psychic figures informed Dick’s interpretations of dreams and 2-3-74.

No personal meeting is documented.

### Frank Herbert

**Evidentiary label: documented professional friendship.**

Dick described Herbert as an old and valued friend in material connected with a Vancouver convention and wrote an enthusiastic introduction concerning him. The two belonged to the same professional science-fiction network. Correspondence between them is reported and at least one Dick letter to Herbert survives in published-letter discussions.

No collaboration is documented.

### Richard Wagner

**Evidentiary label: documented artistic influence.**

Dick was a devoted listener to Wagner and defended or discussed Wagner’s music and political burden. Wagner’s mythic structures, redemption themes, and totalizing artistic ambition formed part of Dick’s musical world.

This was influence only; Wagner died before Dick’s birth.

### Carl Gustav Jung the elder / Dr. Carl Jung of Mainz

**Evidentiary label: no supported connection.**

The atlas roster contains several similarly named historical figures. The documentary connection is specifically to the psychologist **Carl Gustav Jung (1875–1961)**, not to other persons bearing the name.

### Plato

**Evidentiary label: documented intellectual influence.**

Plato’s distinction between appearance and reality, especially the cave analogy and forms, is repeatedly relevant to Dick’s fiction and late metaphysics. Dick’s own theological-philosophical writings engage Platonic and Neoplatonic ideas.

No personal connection was possible.

### Jesus of Nazareth and Paul the Apostle

**Evidentiary label: documented religious and textual influence.**

Dick’s late work intensely engages Jesus, Pauline Christianity, Acts, the Logos, early Christian persecution, and the possibility that apostolic time persists beneath modern history. He connected a scene in *Flow My Tears* with an episode in Acts after discussion with an Episcopal priest.

These are devotional, textual, and visionary connections, not historically verifiable meetings.

### William Blake

**Evidentiary label: documented thematic/intellectual affinity; degree of direct influence less certain.**

Dick’s criticism and later reception place him in a visionary tradition associated with Blake: oppressive world-systems, divine imagination, and reality perceived through altered vision. Direct reading is reported in biographical scholarship, but the consulted sources provide less explicit first-person testimony than for Jung.

### William Seward Burroughs II

**Evidentiary label: documented literary interest, not personal association.**

Dick discussed Burroughs’s language-virus idea and read or knew *Naked Lunch* and related work. In interview he resisted simply classifying Burroughs as science fiction. No meeting or correspondence was established.

### George Orwell

**Evidentiary label: documented literary-political influence and comparison.**

Orwell’s police-state fiction belongs to the acknowledged background of Dick’s authoritarian futures. *Radio Free Albemuth* and *Flow My Tears* transform Orwellian surveillance through alternate reality and theological resistance. No personal contact occurred.

### Jorge Luis Borges

**Evidentiary label: documented critical comparison; direct personal connection unestablished.**

Scholars have compared Dick’s branching realities, false books, doubles, and labyrinths with Borges. The sources consulted do not establish a meeting or substantial correspondence, and thematic resemblance alone should not be upgraded to personal influence.

### Steven Allan Spielberg

**Evidentiary label: documented posthumous adaptation.**

Spielberg directed *Minority Report* (2002), based on Dick’s 1956 story. Dick died twenty years before the film, so there was no collaboration or meeting.

### Jean Baudrillard, Gilles Deleuze, Paul-Michel Foucault, Jacques Derrida, Slavoj Žižek

**Evidentiary label: documented later critical association; no established personal relationship.**

Postmodern and critical-theory discussions frequently place Dick beside these thinkers in studies of simulacra, control, unstable subjectivity, and constructed reality. This is posthumous interpretation, not evidence of correspondence or collaboration.
