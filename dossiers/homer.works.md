## Works by Homer

### Monumental oral epics

- **Late eighth or seventh century BCE:** *Iliad*. A monumental hexameter song about Achilles’ wrath during the Trojan War, traditionally assigned to Homer and surviving substantially complete. Its date, the identity and number of its composers, and the stages by which an orally composed and repeatedly performed song became a written text remain disputed; “obra” here may mean a long tradition’s crystallization rather than one documented person’s production.
- **Late eighth or seventh century BCE:** *Odyssey*. A monumental hexameter song of Odysseus’ return, traditionally attributed to Homer and surviving substantially complete. Ancient “Separatists” and many modern scholars have distinguished its poet from the poet of the *Iliad*, while oral-traditional models further complicate the idea of a single author or definitive original text.

### Hymnic performances transmitted as Homeric

- *Hymn 1, To Dionysus*. A fragmentary hexameter hymn reconstructed from separately transmitted pieces. Its unity, date, and authorship are uncertain, and Martin West’s arrangement is itself an editorial hypothesis.
- *Hymn 2, To Demeter*. A substantial narrative hymn or rhapsodic prelude concerning Demeter and Persephone. It is “Homeric” in language and transmission, not securely the creation of the undocumented Homer.
- *Hymn 3, To Apollo*. A substantial hymn combining Delian and Pythian material. Scholars dispute whether its two principal sections began as separate compositions; Thucydides attributed part of it to “Homer,” but that testimony does not settle authorship.
- *Hymn 4, To Hermes*. A substantial comic-narrative hymn about Hermes’ infancy. It belongs to the Homeric tradition but is generally treated as an anonymous composition.
- *Hymn 5, To Aphrodite*. A substantial narrative hymn concerning Aphrodite and Anchises. Its “Homeric” designation marks traditional corpus and style, not demonstrable personal authorship.
- *Hymn 6, To Aphrodite*. A short invocatory prelude, distinct from the longer fifth hymn and anonymously composed within the Homeric hymnic tradition.
- *Hymn 7, To Dionysus*. A narrative hymn about Dionysus and the pirates. It is transmitted in the Homeric collection without secure evidence that Homer made it.
- *Hymn 8, To Ares*. A prayer-like hymn whose language and theology differ conspicuously from early epic; its date and authorship are disputed, and it may be substantially later than most of the collection.
- *Hymn 9, To Artemis*. A short hymnic prelude transmitted under Homer’s name but not securely attributable to an individual Homer.
- *Hymn 10, To Aphrodite*. A short invocatory performance, conventionally Homeric rather than demonstrably authored by Homer.
- *Hymn 11, To Athena*. A brief hymnic prelude belonging to the anonymously developed Homeric corpus.
- *Hymn 12, To Hera*. A brief invocation whose survival as a separate numbered “work” depends upon the manuscript collection and modern editorial division.
- *Hymn 13, To Demeter*. A short hymnic prelude distinct from the long second hymn and of unknown authorship.
- *Hymn 14, To the Mother of the Gods*. A short hymn invoking the Mother of the Gods; its cultic identity and date are debated.
- *Hymn 15, To Heracles the Lionheart*. A brief hymnic synopsis of Heracles’ mortal labours and divine life, anonymously transmitted as Homeric.
- *Hymn 16, To Asclepius*. A short invocation belonging to the Homeric hymnic collection rather than the secure oeuvre of a historical author.
- *Hymn 17, To the Dioscuri*. A brief hymnic prelude, distinct from Hymn 33 and of unknown individual authorship.
- *Hymn 18, To Hermes*. A short invocation distinct from the long narrative Hymn 4.
- *Hymn 19, To Pan*. A narrative hymn celebrating Pan’s birth and music. Its date and composer are unknown.
- *Hymn 20, To Hephaestus*. A short hymn connecting Hephaestus with civilized craft; it is commonly considered later than the supposed lifetime of Homer.
- *Hymn 21, To Apollo*. A very brief invocation whose independent identity is primarily a fact of collection and transmission.
- *Hymn 22, To Poseidon*. A short hymnic prelude transmitted anonymously within the Homeric corpus.
- *Hymn 23, To Zeus*. A very brief invocation, conventionally a Homeric Hymn but not securely Homer’s creation.
- *Hymn 24, To Hestia*. A short ritual invocation transmitted as Homeric.
- *Hymn 25, To the Muses and Apollo*. A brief invocation of the divine sources of song; it functions as a performance prelude rather than as a “book.”
- *Hymn 26, To Dionysus*. A short hymnic prelude distinct from Hymns 1 and 7.
- *Hymn 27, To Artemis*. A short narrative-invocatory hymn transmitted in the anonymous Homeric collection.
- *Hymn 28, To Athena*. A short hymn celebrating Athena’s birth, with no secure personal attribution.
- *Hymn 29, To Hestia*. A short ritual hymn invoking Hestia and Hermes, distinct from Hymn 24.
- *Hymn 30, To Earth, Mother of All*. A hymn to Earth as universal nourisher; “Homeric” describes its inherited poetic medium and corpus.
- *Hymn 31, To Helios*. A hymnic prelude to the sun god, transmitted anonymously under Homer’s name.
- *Hymn 32, To Selene*. A hymnic prelude to the moon goddess, likewise Homeric by convention rather than proven authorship.
- *Hymn 33, To the Dioscuri*. A short hymn distinct from Hymn 17 and of unknown authorship.
- *Hymn to Hosts*. Properly an epigram addressing hosts rather than a hymn to a deity. Its inclusion shows that the transmitted “Homeric Hymns” are a constructed collection, not necessarily a coherent obra by one maker.
- *Unplaced hymn fragment*. A fragment printed with the Homeric Hymns by West but too incomplete to receive a secure subject, title, date, or independent author.

### Lost heroic epics anciently claimed for Homer

- *Thebaid*. A lost hexameter epic about the war of the Seven against Thebes, surviving only in fragments and testimonia. Callinus reportedly called it Homeric, and the *Contest of Homer and Hesiod* likewise assigned it to Homer, but modern scholarship does not treat that attribution as secure.
- *Epigoni*. A lost sequel about the sons of the Seven, represented by scant fragments and ancient notices. Some ancient sources assigned it to Homer, others to Antimachus of Teos, and even antiquity appears to have been less certain about it than about the *Thebaid*.
- *Cypria*. A lost Trojan-cycle epic narrating events before the *Iliad*. Traditions variously named Homer, Stasinus, Hegesias or Hegesinus, and “Cyprias”; Herodotus explicitly argued from its contents that Homer was not its author.
- *Little Iliad*. A lost Trojan-cycle epic covering events after Achilles’ death and before Troy’s fall. The pseudo-Herodotean *Life* says Homer composed it and Thestorides appropriated it, whereas other traditions assigned it to Lesches, Cinaethon, or Diodorus.
- *Phocais*. A lost or unidentified epic which the pseudo-Herodotean *Life* associates with Homer’s supposed stay at Phocaea. Almost nothing securely establishes its contents, extent, date, or author, so it resists classification as more than an ancient report of a work.
- *Capture of Oechalia*. A fragmentary heroic epic about Heracles’ capture of Oechalia. Ancient testimony alternately made it Homer’s work, Creophylus’ work, or a poem Homer gave to Creophylus in exchange for hospitality; Callimachus treated Creophylus as its maker.
- *Expedition of Amphiaraus*. A lost poem about Amphiaraus’ expedition to Thebes, mentioned in the pseudo-Herodotean *Life* as something Homer performed. Its independence from the *Thebaid*, its dimensions, and its authorship are all uncertain.

### Comic and playful poems attributed to Homer

- **Seventh or sixth century BCE, probably:** *Margites*. A largely lost mixed-meter comic narrative about a spectacularly incompetent man. Aristotle treated Homer as its maker and as the founder of comedy through it, but later ancient and modern scholarship disputed the attribution.
- *Cercopes*. A lost comic or mock-epic poem about the mischievous Cercopes, surviving through minimal fragments and testimonia. The pseudo-Herodotean biography placed it among Homer’s playful compositions, but there is no reliable evidence of his authorship.
- *Epikichlides*. A lost playful poem or group of short pieces known only through sparse ancient testimony. Even its title and precise subject are uncertain; its place in a Homeric oeuvre records an attribution, not an identifiable surviving obra.
- *Battle of the Weasel and the Mice*. A fragmentary animal mock-epic represented by a papyrus fragment and included by West among the Homeric Apocrypha. Its title, reconstruction, relationship to other animal battles, date, and authorship remain uncertain.
- *Battle of Frogs and Mice* (*Batrachomyomachia*). A surviving short animal mock-epic long transmitted under Homer’s name. Linguistic and literary evidence makes authorship by the composer of the *Iliad* implausible, and ancient sources also attributed it to Pigres.
- *Psaromachia*. A lost “battle” poem listed among Homer’s playful compositions in the pseudo-Herodotean *Life*. Its subject and even its distinction from similarly titled animal mock-epics are uncertain, so it survives more as an attributed title than as a recoverable work.
- *Heptapaktike*. A lost playful composition named in the pseudo-Herodotean *Life*. No adequate remains establish what kind of thing it was, and treating it as a discrete Homeric obra depends entirely on that late biographical attribution.

### Occasional and inscribed verse

- *Seventeen Homeric Epigrams*. A heterogeneous assemblage preserved principally inside the pseudo-Herodotean *Life of Homer*, including verses connected with places, occupations, hospitality, dedications, and epitaphs. They have no secure collective date or single authorship—some circulate elsewhere under other names—and therefore resist being treated either as one work or as seventeen securely bounded works by Homer.
