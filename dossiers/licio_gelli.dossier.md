# Licio Gelli: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier reconstructs the life, networks, activities, prosecutions, self-representations, and posthumous reputation of Licio Gelli from documentary and published sources consulted through 2 September 2026.

Evidentiary labels used:

- **Documented fact:** established by an archival record, official document, or final judicial ruling.
- **Reported fact:** consistently reported by reputable sources, but not independently established here from a primary document.
- **Allegation or theory:** asserted in testimony, an investigation, scholarship, or journalism but not established by a final judgment against Gelli.
- **Disputed claim:** substantial sources conflict or the evidence permits competing readings.
- **Rumor or myth:** unsupported or weakly supported narrative important principally because of its circulation or symbolic effect.
- **Self-claim:** Gelli’s own account, not necessarily independently verified.

Names appearing on the confiscated P2 membership lists are described as listed or affiliated rather than automatically as knowing participants in every activity attributed to Gelli or the lodge.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and siblings

**Documented or strongly corroborated fact:** Gelli was born in Pistoia to Ettore Gelli and Maria Gori. Ettore came from nearby Montale and was described as a miller, millstone dresser, landowner, or a proprietor possessing both land and a mill. The differences reflect the family’s mixed artisanal and small-property position rather than necessarily contradictory accounts.

Licio was the youngest of four children. Two siblings were sisters. His brother Raffaello went with him to the Spanish Civil War and was killed while fighting with Franco’s forces. The Pistoia state archive contains papers concerning Licio, Raffaello, Ettore, and Maria.

No reliable evidence reviewed establishes aristocratic ancestry. Gelli’s later use of noble titles rested on honors or purported ennoblements he acquired in adulthood, not on a securely demonstrated hereditary lineage.

### Marriages and children

On 16 December 1944 he married Wanda Vannacci of Pistoia. Sources disagree on whether she was born on 31 January 1926 or 31 January 1928; family-oriented sources usually give 1926, while a parliamentary police file reproduced a 1928 date. She died at Villa Wanda on 14 June 1993 after a long illness.

Their four children were:

- Raffaello, born 28 June 1947.
- Maria Rosa, born 22 December 1952.
- Maria Grazia, born 9 September 1956; died 21 June 1988.
- Maurizio, born 25 October 1959.

In 2006 Gelli married Gabriela Vasile, a Romanian woman who had reportedly worked as his carer and was approximately 38 years younger. She survived him.

**Reported allegation:** Nara Lazzerini, his secretary, has been described in later journalism as his lover as well as an administrative confidante. That characterization rests principally on journalistic reconstruction and personal testimony rather than a judicial finding.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1919–1936

The family lived at Via Gorizia 7 in Pistoia. Gelli’s childhood occurred during Fascism’s consolidation and in a social setting that later biographers describe as modest but not destitute.

His formal education appears to have been limited. Biographical accounts describe expulsion or departure from school after he struck a teacher. He did not acquire a university education, legal training, or senior military academy education. The parliamentary P2 report later observed, in discussing the *Piano di rinascita democratica*, that legally sophisticated texts associated with him were almost certainly drafted by someone with more specialized knowledge, though inspired by Gelli.

His practical formation came through:

- Fascist youth culture and local party networks.
- The Spanish Civil War.
- Military and police-administrative work during the Second World War.
- Commercial salesmanship.
- His capacity to accumulate personal dossiers, favors, honors, introductions, and compromising information.

No securely documented teacher or intellectual mentor comparable to his later political patrons emerges from the consulted material.

---

## Spain and Fascist Formation, 1937–1939

At approximately eighteen, Gelli volunteered for the Spanish Civil War on the Nationalist side. His brother Raffaello also served and was killed.

Gelli worked with or alongside the Italian Fascist expeditionary forces supporting Francisco Franco. The episode supplied the subject matter for his first book, *Fuoco! Cronache legionarie della insurrezione antibolscevica di Spagna* (1939). His Spanish service became central to his self-created image: the youthful legionary, anti-communist combatant, loyal comrade, and bearer of dangerous wartime knowledge.

**Documented fact:** His participation is supported by the family papers deposited in Pistoia and by his contemporary publication.

**Qualification:** His later accounts magnified the adventure and his own importance. The surviving evidence confirms participation but does not independently verify every exploit in his memoirs.

## Second World War and the Italian Civil War, 1940–1945

### Royal Army service

Gelli was recalled on 9 June 1940 and assigned to the 127th Infantry Regiment of the “Venezia” Division. Biographical reconstructions place him in military and administrative roles rather than among senior commanders.

### Yugoslavia and the missing royal treasure

Gelli served in occupied Yugoslavia and was involved in the transport or handling of treasure and valuables connected to King Peter II of Yugoslavia.

**Documented core:** Italian forces handled Yugoslav royal property, and Gelli’s wartime activities placed him near related movements of assets.

**Persistent allegation:** Later accounts claimed that about sixty tonnes of royal gold were transported but only forty tonnes restored, and that Gelli somehow retained or diverted the missing twenty tonnes. Gelli denied this.

**Assessment:** No judgment reviewed established that Gelli stole the missing gold. The story became important because gold bars were later found in circumstances connected to his flight and because his postwar wealth seemed difficult to reconcile with his documented origins. The numerical claim should be treated as an unresolved allegation, not an established source of his fortune.

### Italian Social Republic

After the September 1943 armistice, Gelli remained with Fascist institutions and served the German-backed Italian Social Republic. Accounts place him as a liaison or official at the Fascist federation in Pistoia and associate him with anti-partisan work.

### Contacts with the Resistance

**Reported fact with disputed interpretation:** Gelli simultaneously developed contacts with anti-Fascist partisans, including the formation around Silvano Fedi. He supplied passes or information and participated, according to several reconstructions, in the liberation of political prisoners from the Ville Sbertoli prison.

There are three principal interpretations:

1. Gelli genuinely shifted toward the Resistance as the Axis position deteriorated.
2. He operated as a double agent, preserving whichever side might prevail.
3. His aid to partisans was later exaggerated to rehabilitate his Fascist record.

The available material supports contacts and assistance but not a simple conversion narrative.

### Death of Silvano Fedi

Fedi was killed on 29 July 1944 in circumstances that generated suspicions of betrayal.

**Allegation or theory:** Some writers suspected Gelli of responsibility or of supplying information leading to Fedi’s death.

**Assessment:** The allegation was never established judicially. Gelli’s simultaneous relations with Fascists and partisans made it durable, but documentary proof of his authorship or complicity has not emerged in the sources reviewed.

## Postwar Survival and Early Career, 1945–1960

Gelli avoided permanent political marginalization after the war. Claims that he immediately “fled to Argentina and lived there continuously from 1944 to 1960” are repeated in Spanish-language summaries but conflict with records placing his marriage, children, and business activities in Pistoia and Italy during the same period.

The most defensible reconstruction is:

- He remained substantially based in Italy after 1944.
- He undertook commercial activity and may have traveled to, or developed business contacts with, Argentina before the 1960s.
- Later accounts compressed intermittent Latin American connections into a continuous Argentine exile.

He sold books and other goods and developed a reputation as an energetic commercial representative. He worked in or around a bookshop and participated in small trading ventures. In 1952 he published *Nozioni commerciali: settore vendita*, reflecting his identity as a salesman and organizer.

He later joined the Permaflex mattress business. Sources variously describe him as a salesman, personnel manager, commercial director, or factory director. By the late 1950s and 1960s he was an important Permaflex executive, associated especially with its Frosinone operation.

His personnel methods reportedly combined:

- Recruitment of former Fascists and veterans.
- Attention to workers’ personal circumstances.
- Creation of reciprocal obligations.
- Political and ecclesiastical introductions.
- Detailed record-keeping.

These techniques anticipated the patronage and dossier structure of P2.

## Industrial Career and Villa Wanda, 1960–1969

By the 1960s Gelli had moved well beyond his family’s provincial circumstances. He became associated with the Lebole clothing and mattress interests, the Dormire company, Giole, and Socam. He acquired Villa Carla near Arezzo from the Lebole family, probably in 1965–1968; sources differ on the exact year. He renamed it Villa Wanda after his wife.

The property, on the Santa Maria delle Grazie hill, comprised more than thirty rooms and a park of roughly three hectares. It became:

- A family residence.
- A meeting place for politicians, officers, businessmen, diplomats, and Freemasons.
- A repository for papers, memorabilia, honors, and historical documents.
- A principal symbol of Gelli’s power.

Gelli also used rooms at Rome’s Hotel Excelsior—especially the frequently cited suite 127—as a discreet recruitment and meeting venue.

### Wealth and business structure

His expanding wealth was linked publicly to executive work, textile and mattress holdings, and international trade. The exact composition of his beneficial ownership is difficult to reconstruct because:

- Companies and accounts were distributed across jurisdictions.
- Financial relationships overlapped with P2 members.
- Assets were at times held through relatives or corporate structures.
- Banco Ambrosiano litigation later exposed secret accounts and transfers.
- Gelli cultivated stories of hidden wartime wealth.

No complete audited balance sheet of his lifetime fortune has been located.

## Entry into Freemasonry and the Construction of P2

### Initiation

Gelli entered Italian Freemasonry in the 1960s, commonly dated to 1965. Giordano Gamberini, Grand Master of the Grande Oriente d’Italia, was a decisive sponsor. Gelli’s Fascist record initially caused resistance within an institution that possessed a strong anti-Fascist tradition.

He advanced rapidly because he offered what the leadership valued: access to senior military, political, business, diplomatic, and foreign figures reluctant to appear in ordinary lodge records.

### Propaganda lodge

The historic “Propaganda” lodge had long provided privacy for prominent members. Under Gelli it became Propaganda Due or P2.

The parliamentary commission’s central finding was that Gelli did not invent secrecy or covered membership but took control of existing mechanisms and transformed them into a personal instrument. By the second half of the 1970s, according to the commission, he exercised “complete and uncontested control” over P2 and had effectively displaced the ordinary Masonic hierarchy.

Important stages included:

- Recruitment of senior armed-forces, police, intelligence, banking, publishing, political, and industrial personnel.
- Maintenance of membership records primarily under Gelli’s control.
- Expansion in Latin America.
- A nominal dissolution or suspension in 1974–1975 amid internal Masonic controversy.
- Continuation or reconstruction under arrangements that left Gelli’s effective network intact.
- Increasing conflict with Grand Master Lino Salvini and other Masons.
- Formal expulsion of Gelli from the Grande Oriente d’Italia on 31 October 1981, after the scandal had broken.

### Membership and structure

The 1981 seizure produced a list commonly described as containing 962 names, though numbers vary according to whether one counts duplicates, applications, deceased persons, foreign branches, and unnumbered records.

The list included:

- Parliamentarians and party officials.
- All three then-serving intelligence chiefs or the heads of the relevant services.
- Senior military and police officers.
- Magistrates and civil servants.
- Bankers and industrialists.
- Editors, publishers, and journalists.
- Silvio Berlusconi, Roberto Calvi, Michele Sindona associates, Maurizio Costanzo, Franco Di Bella, Angelo Rizzoli, and members of the royal House of Savoy.

**Important qualification:** Presence on the list is documentary evidence of recorded affiliation or application, not proof that every listed person knew the full structure or participated in crimes attributed to other members.

## Latin America and Argentina

Gelli built one of P2’s most important foreign networks in Argentina. His relationships included Juan Domingo Perón, María Estela Martínez de Perón (“Isabel”), José López Rega, Admiral Emilio Massera, and other military, diplomatic, and business figures.

### Perón

**Documented and reported fact:** By the early 1970s Gelli had direct relations with Perón and his entourage. He assisted the development of contacts surrounding Perón’s return from exile and was rewarded with Argentine status and official credentials.

Gelli described himself as:

- Economic adviser accredited through the Argentine embassy in Rome.
- Special commercial delegate.
- Minister plenipotentiary for cultural affairs.

Italian archival descriptions state that he served from 1973 to 1980 as an economic adviser accredited to the Italian government and as Perón’s minister plenipotentiary for cultural affairs.

**Archival complication:** One scholarly inquiry reported that a search of the Argentine embassy archive in Rome did not locate confirming appointment records. His diplomatic passport and access are better supported than every exact formulation of office. The grander titles should therefore be described as reported or self-claimed unless the underlying decree is produced.

### López Rega, Triple A, and repression

López Rega, Perón’s private secretary and later social-welfare minister, became a central Gelli associate. Argentine reporting and scholarship link Gelli’s P2 network with López Rega’s political and business circle.

**Documented fact:** López Rega and numerous senior Argentine figures were linked to P2 or its Argentine networks.

**Allegation or theory:** Gelli or P2 helped shape, sponsor, or internationally connect the Argentine Anticommunist Alliance, the Triple A death squad.

**Assessment:** There is considerable evidence of overlapping personnel, ideology, Masonic relationships, and political protection. Direct operational command by Gelli over specific Triple A murders has not been established in a judgment reviewed here. “Gelli created the Triple A” overstates the demonstrated record.

### Perón’s corpse and the severed hands

David Cox and Damian Nabot’s *The Second Death* argues that the 1987 removal of Perón’s hands was connected to Gelli, P2, financial secrets, or documents linked to Perón.

**Theory:** The mutilation supposedly served to obtain fingerprints, open a safe, authenticate documents, or intimidate people holding evidence about hidden accounts.

**Evidence:** Networks around Gelli and López Rega provided a plausible background; the mutilation occurred; and the case remained unresolved.

**Against:** No judicial finding established Gelli’s direction of the crime, and several variants depend on anonymous testimony and speculative financial reconstruction.

The story is historically important as an Argentine counterpart to Italian “Puppet Master” mythology, but remains unproved.

### Wider Latin American contacts

Gelli cultivated relations with military governments and anti-communist circles in Uruguay, Brazil, and elsewhere. Claims that he hid in Augusto Pinochet’s Chile during his 1983–1987 flight were publicly circulated, including by Chilean nationalist Jorge Vargas, but his full fugitive itinerary remains unresolved.

## Political and Ideological Program

### Core outlook

Gelli’s durable commitments were:

- Anti-communism.
- Executive authority stronger than parliament.
- A reduced role for mass parties and unions.
- Restructuring of the judiciary.
- Professionalization and political reliability of state institutions.
- Concentration and political management of the press.
- An elite of selected technicians, officers, managers, and political intermediaries.

He adapted his presentation to audiences. Fascist comrades heard continuity; Christian Democrats heard anti-communist stabilization; soldiers heard order; businessmen heard liberalization; Latin American military elites heard continental anti-Marxism.

### *Piano di rinascita democratica*

The *Piano di rinascita democratica* proposed changes involving:

- Media ownership and influence over newspapers.
- Reduction or restructuring of parliament.
- Strengthening the prime minister and executive.
- Reform of electoral and party systems.
- Greater control over prosecutorial and judicial careers.
- Alteration of the Superior Council of the Judiciary.
- Weakening of union power.
- Restructuring of broadcasting.
- Selection and financial support of reliable political cadres.

The document contemplated tens of billions of lire for acquiring or influencing key positions.

**Documented fact:** The plan was found in July 1982 in a false bottom in luggage belonging to Gelli’s daughter Maria Grazia.

**Authorship qualification:** The parliamentary commission concluded that Gelli inspired and possessed it but probably did not personally draft its legally technical language. Francesco Cosentino, a senior parliamentary official and P2 member, has often been identified as the drafter, though descriptions of his exact authorship should retain attribution language.

### Was it a coup plan?

Two readings have persisted:

1. **Parliamentary majority and many historians:** the plan documented a secret project to subordinate democratic institutions through infiltration and controlled legal change.
2. **Gelli and sympathetic or revisionist interpreters:** it was a conservative reform memorandum whose individual proposals could be pursued constitutionally.

It was not a conventional blueprint for tanks seizing ministries. Its distinctive danger, according to the parliamentary commission, lay in covert control and coordinated penetration of institutions.

## Publishing, Finance, and the Corriere della Sera

P2 became entangled with the Rizzoli publishing group and *Corriere della Sera*. Gelli sought influence over Italy’s largest newspaper during a financial crisis affecting its owners.

Documented P2-linked figures included publisher Angelo Rizzoli, managing director Bruno Tassan Din, editor Franco Di Bella, and interviewer Maurizio Costanzo. Gelli received prominent treatment, including a major *Corriere* interview on 5 October 1980 in which he publicly discussed political reforms resembling the later-discovered plan.

The network used banking relationships, especially those involving Banco Ambrosiano, to support publishing transactions. Whether each editorial decision was dictated by Gelli is not demonstrable; the financial and personnel penetration itself is documented.

## Michele Sindona, Roberto Calvi, Banco Ambrosiano, and the Vatican

### Michele Sindona

Sindona was a financier with political, Mafia, Vatican, and international banking relationships. Investigations into his activities helped expose Gelli’s network. Judges Gherardo Colombo and Giuliano Turone ordered the March 1981 searches while investigating matters connected to Sindona.

P2 and Sindona shared:

- Anti-communist political contacts.
- Financial intermediaries.
- Intelligence and political patrons.
- Vatican-linked banking circles.
- Interests in media and corporate control.

The precise hierarchy—whether Sindona served Gelli, Gelli served Sindona, or both participated in intersecting networks—remains debated.

### Roberto Calvi and Banco Ambrosiano

Calvi, chairman of Banco Ambrosiano, was on the P2 list and financially connected to Gelli and Umberto Ortolani. Ambrosiano’s offshore system involved Latin American banks, shell companies, the Vatican bank or Istituto per le Opere di Religione, and enormous unsecured transfers.

The bank collapsed in 1982 with a shortfall commonly calculated at well over one billion US dollars. Calvi was found hanging beneath Blackfriars Bridge in London on 18 June 1982. A British inquest’s initial suicide conclusion was later rejected; subsequent proceedings treated the death as homicide.

### Gelli’s financial responsibility

**Court finding:** Gelli was convicted of fraudulent bankruptcy in the Banco Ambrosiano case. The first-instance sentence in 1992 was eighteen years and six months; after appeals, a twelve-year sentence became definitive. The case established his criminal participation in diversion of Ambrosiano assets.

The record involved secret accounts, transfers benefiting Gelli-linked interests, and financial dealings among Calvi, Gelli, Ortolani, Francesco Pazienza, Flavio Carboni, and others.

### Calvi’s death

**Investigation and allegation:** Gelli was repeatedly investigated or suspected as a possible instigator of Calvi’s murder. Motives proposed included silencing Calvi, preventing exposure of P2 and offshore finances, or resolving obligations involving Mafia and Vatican-linked funds.

The later Roman murder trial concerned Pippo Calò, Flavio Carboni, Ernesto Diotallevi, Silvano Vittor, and others. The defendants were ultimately acquitted for insufficient proof. Gelli was not convicted of Calvi’s murder.

At Gelli’s death, a separate inquiry reportedly still considered his possible role; the prosecutor sought closure because participation was considered plausible but evidentially unprovable.

**Conclusion:** Calvi was probably murdered according to the later legal and forensic consensus; Gelli’s responsibility remains an unresolved allegation, not a court finding.

### Paul Marcinkus and the IOR

Archbishop Paul Marcinkus headed the IOR during the Ambrosiano period. Ambrosiano’s offshore companies possessed IOR “letters of patronage,” and the Vatican ultimately made a large payment framed as recognition of moral involvement rather than legal liability.

There is evidence of institutional and financial intersection among Calvi, Ambrosiano, IOR personnel, and P2-associated intermediaries. Claims that Marcinkus was a P2 member or that Gelli directly commanded Vatican policy remain unestablished.

## Discovery of P2, 1981

On 17 March 1981, magistrates Colombo and Turone ordered searches at Villa Wanda and Gelli-linked business premises at Castiglion Fibocchi, including Giole. The Guardia di Finanza discovered membership lists and extensive documentation.

The list’s release precipitated a national crisis. Prime Minister Arnaldo Forlani delayed publication, then released the names in May. His government fell amid the scandal.

The parliamentary response included:

- A bicameral commission of inquiry chaired by Tina Anselmi.
- Law no. 17 of 25 January 1982, implementing the constitutional prohibition on secret associations and dissolving P2.
- Examination of Gelli’s relationships with intelligence, finance, publishing, political parties, the military, and foreign governments.

The commission concluded that P2 under Gelli was a secret association with political purposes operating as an instrument for influencing or controlling public institutions.

### General P2 criminal trial

Gelli and others were prosecuted for political conspiracy and related association charges.

**Court result:** The broad claim that P2 itself constituted a criminal or subversive conspiracy did not produce the sweeping convictions anticipated by the parliamentary findings. Gelli was acquitted of the principal subversive-association charge in that proceeding.

This does not erase his separate final convictions for Ambrosiano bankruptcy and Bologna-related diversion. Parliamentary, constitutional, and criminal-law findings answered different questions under different standards.

## Arrest, Escape, Fugitive Years, and Extradition

### Flight and arrest

Gelli left Italy after the 1981 searches. On 13 September 1982 Swiss authorities arrested him in Geneva while he attempted to access tens of millions of dollars in accounts connected to Banco Ambrosiano.

He was held at Champ-Dollon prison pending extradition.

### Escape, 10 August 1983

Gelli escaped with the assistance of guard Roland Béguelin. Contemporary accounts establish bribery and passage over or through the prison perimeter, followed by movement into France. Later retellings added a helicopter; contemporary reporting more reliably describes transport by car across the border.

The guard stated that he had been bribed and feared threats to his family.

### Fugitive period

From August 1983 to September 1987, Gelli’s location was obscure. Proposed locations included:

- France and the Côte d’Azur.
- Switzerland.
- Uruguay and Argentina.
- Chile.
- Grand Bahama.
- Other Latin American refuges protected by military and Masonic contacts.

FBI material reported in 2025 shows that American authorities could not successfully follow leads placing him in Grand Bahama in 1984. The heavily redacted file also records Gelli’s effort in January 1982 to contact the US secretary of state. It does not by itself establish that he was a CIA asset.

### Surrender and return

Gelli surrendered to Swiss authorities in Geneva on 21 September 1987. He was convicted in Switzerland of offenses connected to illegal entry and bribing the guard.

Swiss extradition was limited by the specialty principle. He returned to Italy in February 1988 and was placed in Parma prison, but was released in April for health reasons.

The extradition limitations repeatedly complicated Italian arrest orders: Italy could not automatically imprison him for offenses outside the set approved by Switzerland.

## Bologna Railway Station Bombing

On 2 August 1980 a bomb at Bologna Centrale killed 85 people and injured more than 200.

### The 1995 final judgment: diversion of the investigation

**Final court finding:** On 23 November 1995 the united criminal sections of the Court of Cassation definitively convicted Gelli and Francesco Pazienza, with SISMI officers Pietro Musumeci and Giuseppe Belmonte, in relation to a deliberate campaign of aggravated calumny and diversion.

The diversion included:

- Fabricated or manipulated foreign and left-wing leads.
- Use of intelligence channels controlled by P2 members.
- The planting of a suitcase containing weapons, explosives, and false documentary clues on the Taranto–Milan train in January 1981.
- Efforts to push investigators toward a fictitious international or “Lebanese” trail.

The court found that Gelli could directly influence SISDE director Giulio Grassini, a P2 affiliate, and that the diversions formed part of a prearranged strategy.

Gelli was acquitted of subversive association in this proceeding but sentenced to ten years for the aggravated calumny/diversion offenses.

### Later “mandants” proceedings, 2020–2025

A new investigation centered on Paolo Bellini and the financing and organization behind the massacre. Prosecutors relied heavily on the “Documento Bologna,” a financial record associated with Gelli, including the notation “Bologna – 525779 – X.S.” and transfers near the bombing date.

The Court of Assizes convicted Bellini in 2022; the Bologna Court of Assizes of Appeal confirmed the central reconstruction in 2024. On 1 July 2025 the Court of Cassation made Bellini’s conviction final.

**Finding in proceedings against surviving defendants:** The courts’ reconstruction identified Gelli as a conscious financier and instigator or mandant of the massacre, alongside Umberto Ortolani, and identified Federico Umberto D’Amato and Mario Tedeschi within the coordinating structure.

**Essential legal qualification:** Gelli had died before this proceeding and therefore was neither tried nor convicted in it. The finding about his role is a judicial factual reconstruction in another defendant’s final case, not a personal conviction entered against Gelli.

The 2025 Cassation judgment summarized the trial court’s conclusion that Gelli was a “mandante-finanziatore”—an instigator-financier—and that P2’s leadership directed or supported a multi-cell neo-Fascist operation coordinated with elements of diverted state services.

This materially changes the evidentiary status from the position at Gelli’s death in 2015. It is no longer accurate to say merely that journalists “suspected” his involvement in financing Bologna. It remains necessary to distinguish the posthumous finding from a conviction of Gelli himself.

## Other Political Violence and Contested Intelligence Connections

### Italicus bombing, 4 August 1974

The Italicus train bombing killed twelve people.

Gelli and P2 appeared in investigations because of:

- Ties to Tuscan neo-Fascists.
- Intelligence reports concerning P2.
- Statements and evidence about financing of the radical right.
- Attempts to obstruct inquiries.

Gelli was convicted at one stage of financing members of the Tuscan extreme right but was not finally convicted as an organizer or perpetrator of the Italicus bombing. The massacre remains without a complete final attribution of all responsibility.

### Borghese coup attempt, 1970

**Allegation supported by substantial circumstantial and testimonial material:** Gelli and P2-linked military personnel were associated with Junio Valerio Borghese’s aborted coup plan. Accounts assign Gelli the role of arranging access to, or control of, strategic institutions and possibly the intended seizure of President Giuseppe Saragat.

The parliamentary commission treated P2 connections as significant. No final conviction established Gelli as commander of the coup attempt.

### “White coup” and Edgardo Sogno

Gelli’s network intersected with military and political circles connected to Edgardo Sogno’s plans for an emergency anti-communist restructuring of the state.

**Assessment:** Personnel and ideological overlap are documented; Gelli’s exact operational role in every scheme attributed to Sogno is not.

### Aldo Moro kidnapping and murder

P2 members occupied important positions in the police, military, and intelligence structures involved in the Moro investigation.

Claims have included:

- P2 deliberately prevented Moro’s rescue.
- Gelli controlled the crisis committees.
- P2 or a stay-behind structure knew the Via Gradoli location.
- Gelli acted for an international power seeking Moro’s death.

**Documented fact:** Several officials involved in the response were P2 affiliates.

**Official limitation:** Parliamentary investigation did not obtain proof that Gelli directed the kidnapping, knew Moro’s location, or controlled a decision to let him die.

This is a major area where structural suspicion should not be converted into an established personal act.

### Mino Pecorelli

Journalist Mino Pecorelli published intelligence-derived material and wrote about P2 before his 1979 murder. Gelli appeared in the surrounding political-intelligence world.

**Unresolved:** No final judicial finding established Gelli’s participation in Pecorelli’s murder.

### Gladio, NATO, and CIA claims

Gelli is often described online as a CIA agent, head of Gladio in Italy, or NATO’s operational commander for a “strategy of tension.”

The evidence supports narrower propositions:

- He was intensely anti-communist.
- His network included senior Italian intelligence and military officers.
- He had American contacts and sought high-level US access.
- P2’s objectives overlapped with Cold War anti-communist priorities.
- US agencies collected information about him.
- Some investigators, witnesses, and scholars have alleged intelligence sponsorship.

Evidence not located in the consulted primary material:

- A verified CIA personnel or agent file naming Gelli as a recruited and controlled asset.
- A NATO order appointing him head of Gladio.
- Proof that P2 and Gladio were institutionally identical.

A CIA Reading Room document describing Gelli and P2 originated as a republished issue of *CovertAction Information Bulletin*. Its presence on a CIA server reflects declassification and archival release, not CIA endorsement of the article’s claims.

The categorical formulation “Gelli was the CIA commander of Gladio” is therefore an allegation or conspiracy narrative, not a demonstrated fact.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Giordano Gamberini

Grand Master of the Grande Oriente d’Italia who facilitated Gelli’s Masonic advancement. Gamberini supplied institutional legitimacy; Gelli supplied elite recruits.

### Lino Salvini

Gamberini’s successor. Salvini and Gelli cooperated and later fought over control, secrecy, money, and the status of P2. The conflict produced nominal dissolution and reconstruction before the 1981 rupture.

### Umberto Ortolani

Businessman, Vatican-linked intermediary, and one of Gelli’s closest P2 associates. Their cooperation extended through Latin America, publishing, international business, and Banco Ambrosiano. The later Bologna proceedings identified both as financing principals.

### Michele Sindona

Financier whose investigations opened the path to P2’s discovery. Their networks overlapped in banking, the Vatican, Sicily, anti-communist politics, and the United States.

### Roberto Calvi

Banco Ambrosiano chairman and P2 affiliate. Calvi’s banking system financed or transferred money for Gelli-linked operations. Their relationship deteriorated as the bank collapsed.

### Francesco Pazienza

Political and intelligence intermediary connected with SISMI and Ambrosiano affairs. He and Gelli were finally convicted for Bologna-investigation diversion, although personal trust between them varied over time.

### Pietro Musumeci and Giuseppe Belmonte

SISMI officers who participated in the Bologna diversion and were convicted with Gelli.

### Giulio Grassini

SISDE director and P2 affiliate. The 1995 judgment found that Gelli could exercise direct influence over him.

### Federico Umberto D’Amato

Powerful Interior Ministry intelligence official. The 2022–2025 Bologna judicial reconstruction placed him in the higher coordinating environment of the massacre with Gelli, though D’Amato was dead and not tried in those proceedings.

### Silvio Berlusconi

Listed as P2 member no. 1816. His documented connection is membership in Gelli’s lodge and participation in the business-political environment targeted by Gelli’s media strategy. Claims that every later Berlusconi policy directly executed Gelli’s commands are interpretive and not proved by membership alone.

### Tina Anselmi

Christian Democratic parliamentarian and chair of the P2 commission. Institutionally, she became Gelli’s most consequential adversary. The commission assembled the public documentary case concerning P2’s secret political character.

### Gherardo Colombo and Giuliano Turone

Magistrates whose Sindona investigation led to the March 1981 searches and discovery of P2 records.

### Nara Lazzerini

Secretary and reported intimate partner. Later accounts credit her testimony or disclosures with illuminating Gelli’s Rome operations. Exact claims require comparison with her formal statements.

## Properties, Collections, and Financial Questions

### Villa Wanda

Gelli’s primary residence and symbolic headquarters. The state attempted to auction it in 2006 to recover approximately €1.5 million in legal expenses arising from the Ambrosiano litigation.

In October 2013 authorities placed the villa under preventive seizure in a tax-evasion investigation involving Gelli and family members. Contemporary reporting described approximately €17 million in unpaid taxes. An investigation and seizure are not equivalent to a final conviction, and the disposition of every tax allegation was not established in the sources consulted.

### Historical collection

Gelli amassed:

- Autographs attributed to Napoleon, Mussolini, Hitler, Gabriele D’Annunzio, Alessandro Manzoni, and others.
- Military and Masonic regalia.
- Medals and honorific documents.
- Correspondence with political, cultural, journalistic, and financial figures.
- Press dossiers.
- His complete literary output and translations.

The State Archive of Pistoia’s description confirms the scale but does not authenticate every collectible.

### Stolen Madonna

A Renaissance statue of the Madonna della Consolazione, stolen from a church in Rotonda, was found in the grounds of Villa Wanda in 1980.

**Documented occurrence; disputed knowledge:** The recovery is reported, but the reviewed sources do not establish that Gelli stole it or knew it was stolen.

## Honors, Titles, and Self-Presentation

Gelli accumulated foreign decorations, academic memberships, literary prizes, and claimed noble distinctions.

His later styles included “Count” and honorific references associated with the former Italian royal house. Listings attribute to him awards or orders from Argentina and other Latin American governments and connections to cultural academies in Italy, Romania, Russia, and elsewhere.

He was associated with or honored by organizations including:

- Accademia Il Richiamo.
- Accademia Oraziana di Lettere, Scienze ed Arti.
- Accademia Il Tetradramma.
- Accademia Internazionale Pontzen.
- Accademia Artisti Europei.

His personal archive records hundreds of literary prizes and a campaign in 1996–1997 for his nomination for the Nobel Prize in Literature.

**Qualification:** Most honors came from small private academies, foreign cultural bodies, dynastic organizations, or institutions within his patronage network. “More than 200 prizes” is principally a self-archived claim, not a standard measure of literary reception. He never received a Nobel Prize or a major canonical Italian literary award.

## Later Life, 1988–2015

After his extradition, Gelli repeatedly benefited from ill health, extradition restrictions, age, and house-arrest provisions. He spent much of his later life at Villa Wanda.

In May 1998, when required to begin serving the definitive Ambrosiano sentence, he disappeared again. He was found or surrendered later that year in Cannes, France. He returned to Italy and ultimately served his sentence principally under house arrest.

From approximately 2001 until death he remained under home detention or judicial restrictions at Villa Wanda, though the exact legal basis changed as sentences, health rulings, and proceedings evolved.

His later activities included:

- Publishing memoirs, stories, religious writings, and large quantities of poetry.
- Corresponding with admirers, academics, journalists, and political figures.
- Giving interviews in which he minimized P2’s criminality.
- Promoting his archive and historical collection.
- Seeking literary recognition.
- Watching political developments and claiming that the *Piano di rinascita democratica* had gradually been realized.

In a 2003 interview he provocatively called Silvio Berlusconi the sort of man who could carry forward parts of his program. This helped sustain the theory of direct continuity from P2 to later Italian politics, although similarity between policy proposals and the plan does not independently prove Gelli’s command.

On 17 April 2013 he gave what Rai News described as his final television interview, at Villa Wanda. He discussed plans for a film about his life and presented his preferred autobiographical narrative.

---

## Health, Death, and Funeral

Sources described Gelli as chronically ill during several extradition and imprisonment proceedings. Courts repeatedly accepted health-based restrictions or release. Skeptics claimed illness was strategically exaggerated, but some decline was genuine in extreme old age.

He died at Villa Wanda in Arezzo on 15 December 2015, aged 96. Public reporting did not identify a violent or suspicious cause. His health had reportedly deteriorated for some time.

His funeral was held in a church in Pistoia. Attendance consisted mainly of relatives and a small number of others; the nationally prominent people once associated with P2 were conspicuously absent. He was buried in the family setting in Pistoia.

He was survived by his second wife Gabriela and three of the four children of his first marriage.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The Puppet Master of Italy”

**Story:** Gelli personally controlled virtually every major scandal, terrorist attack, intelligence operation, financial collapse, and political realignment in postwar Italy.

**Origin and circulation:** Italian journalism after the 1981 P2 discovery; reinforced by the extraordinary membership list, Gelli’s flight, Calvi’s death, parliamentary reports, and criminal proceedings.

**Evidence for:** He possessed an unusually broad elite network; controlled P2; influenced intelligence chiefs; participated in Ambrosiano fraud; and was convicted in the Bologna diversion. The later Bologna judgment identified him as financier and instigator.

**Evidence against the maximal version:** Networks do not prove command of every participant or event. He was acquitted of the broad political-conspiracy charge, and many attributed operations lack direct evidence.

**Reputational effect:** “Gran Burattinaio” displaced his identity as businessman and made him the personal symbol of Italy’s hidden state.

### The stolen Yugoslav gold financed P2

**Story:** Gold missing from King Peter II’s treasure gave Gelli his postwar capital.

**Evidence for:** His proximity to the treasure, later unexplained wealth, and reports of gold connected to his affairs.

**Against:** No demonstrated chain from the wartime treasure to his later assets; no conviction for theft.

**Status:** Rumor or unresolved allegation.

### Gelli as American intelligence’s supreme Italian agent

**Story:** The CIA installed Gelli to operate P2, Gladio, coups, bombings, and political corruption.

**Evidence for:** Cold War convergence, US contacts, P2’s intelligence membership, and later FBI/CIA archival interest.

**Against:** No authenticated recruitment file, handler reports, payroll evidence, or command order reviewed here.

**Status:** Allegation expanded into conspiracy mythology.

### P2 “won” because its plan became modern Italy

**Story:** Judicial reform, media concentration, weakened unions, a stronger executive, and reduced parliamentary representation prove that P2 continued to govern after its dissolution.

**Circulation:** Gelli himself, critics of Berlusconi, journalists, activists, and recent social-media debates.

**Evidence for:** Several later reforms resemble proposals in the plan; Berlusconi was listed in P2; media concentration became a central political fact.

**Against:** Many proposals were already part of wider conservative, liberal, presidentialist, or technocratic debate. Similarity alone cannot prove continuous command.

**Status:** Interpretive political claim. The plan’s influence as a repertoire of ideas is plausible; line-by-line covert implementation is not established.

### Gelli possessed the secrets of the Italian Republic

**Story:** His hidden archive could expose every major political crime.

**Evidence:** He kept extensive files and correspondence; authorities never recovered everything they believed existed; an “Uruguayan archive” was repeatedly sought.

**Against:** Gelli had incentives to exaggerate his archive’s reach, enhancing fear and bargaining power.

**Status:** Part documentary fact, part self-created mythology.

### Aristocrat and internationally celebrated poet

**Story:** Gelli was a hereditary count and major poet acclaimed throughout the world.

**Evidence:** Foreign translations, many awards, academy memberships, claimed dynastic honors, and organized Nobel support.

**Against:** His noble status was acquired or asserted, not securely hereditary; most literary recognition came from small organizations or his own network; mainstream literary histories give him little standing.

**Status:** Self-fashioned hagiography with a documentary infrastructure of real, but often minor, honors.

### Gelli as merely a harmless reformer

**Story:** P2 was no more than a discreet discussion club, and Gelli was persecuted because his constitutional reforms anticipated later developments.

**Circulation:** Gelli’s interviews, memoirs, admirers, and revisionist accounts.

**Evidence for:** The general P2 conspiracy prosecution ended in major acquittals; some plan proposals were constitutional policy ideas.

**Against:** Secret membership, institutional penetration, Ambrosiano conviction, Bologna diversion conviction, and the 2025 judicial findings are incompatible with a wholly harmless club.

**Status:** Apologetic counter-myth.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

Gelli did not construct a systematic philosophy. His ideas were pragmatic and organizational.

### Fascist phase

Youthful Fascism supplied nationalism, hierarchy, anti-communism, martial comradeship, and belief in disciplined minorities.

### Postwar adaptation

He abandoned open party-Fascist identity as a sole political vehicle but retained anti-communism and vertical authority. He integrated himself into:

- Christian Democratic patronage.
- Atlantic military and intelligence environments.
- Conservative business.
- Freemasonry.
- Peronism and Latin American military nationalism.

### Masonic phase

Freemasonry offered ritual fraternity, international recognition, secrecy, and reciprocity. Gelli used it less as speculative philosophy than as institutional technology.

### Late religious-literary phase

After imprisonment and Wanda’s death, his publications increasingly used Christian imagery, forgiveness, suffering, love, and transcendence. Whether this represented conversion, bereavement, reputational reconstruction, or all three cannot be determined.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Gelli’s reputation operates on several levels:

1. **Judicial:** a convicted participant in Banco Ambrosiano’s fraudulent bankruptcy and in the organized diversion of the Bologna investigation.
2. **Posthumous judicial reconstruction:** the 2025 final Bellini judgment accepted a reconstruction identifying him as a conscious financier and instigator of the Bologna massacre.
3. **Parliamentary:** organizer of a secret political association intended to penetrate and condition constitutional institutions.
4. **Masonic:** the figure whose private capture of an elite lodge produced the greatest modern scandal in Italian Freemasonry.
5. **Political:** symbol of covert institutional reform, media capture, intelligence corruption, and elite patronage.
6. **Cultural:** archetype of the “puppet master” or *Grande Vecchio* behind visible politics.
7. **Self-fashioned:** soldier, patriot, persecuted reformer, Christian poet, collector, diplomat, and confidant of statesmen.
8. **Revisionist:** sometimes presented as a showman who exaggerated his reach and whose mythology has obscured the autonomy of more powerful institutions.
9. **International:** in Argentina, linked to Perón, López Rega, military power, Triple A narratives, and the unresolved removal of Perón’s hands.

His most durable institutional legacy is not proof that every item in his plan was centrally executed. It is the documentary demonstration that senior officials in nominally separate institutions could belong to a concealed, personally managed network.

The discovery also produced Law no. 17/1982, the official dissolution of P2, parliamentary scrutiny of secret associations, and a continuing debate over transparency in Italian public life.
