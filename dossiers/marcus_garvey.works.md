## Works by Marcus Mosiah Garvey

### Periodicals and publishing projects

- **1909:** *Garvey’s Watchman*. A small Jamaican newspaper made and published by Garvey; only three issues appeared, so it survives as a short-lived publishing project rather than a sustained periodical. [S4]
- **1911:** *La Nación*. A Costa Rican daily edited—not founded or solely authored—by Garvey while he lived in Limón; it belongs to his editorial labor but resists classification as an individual work. [S4]
- **1911:** *La Prensa*. A Panamanian newspaper reportedly founded or edited by Garvey to publicize the conditions of Black canal workers. Its title, periodicity, and Garvey’s precise role are reported inconsistently, so the attribution remains qualified. [S4]
- **1918–1933:** *The Negro World*. The international weekly organ of the UNIA-ACL, created by Garvey and edited by him until 1932; its unsigned institutional matter and many contributors make it a collective obra, although Garvey regularly supplied its leading editorials. [S5]
- **1922:** *Daily Negro Times*. A projected daily newspaper launched under Garvey’s direction but unable to establish a durable publication run; it is better understood as an incomplete publishing project than as a stable periodical. [S6]
- **1929–1931:** *The Blackman*. A Jamaican daily, subsequently weekly, founded and edited by Garvey as a vehicle for political campaigning and Garveyism. It is distinct from his later magazine *The Black Man*. [S4; S7]
- **1932–1933:** *The New Jamaican*. A Jamaican newspaper published by Garvey in connection with his political and labor campaigns; it is an editorial platform and collective production, not a single-authored text. [S6]
- **1933–1939:** *The Black Man: A Monthly Magazine of Negro Thought and Opinion*. Garvey’s final major periodical: begun in Kingston, continued in London, edited by him, and substantially written by him, but also containing contributions and reprinted material by others. [S7]

### Pamphlets, books, and composite publications

- **1915:** *A Talk with Afro-West Indians: The Negro Race and Its Problems*. A Kingston pamphlet presenting an early formulation of Garvey’s program of racial organization and self-reliance. [S6]
- **1923:** *An Appeal to the Soul of White America*. A pamphlet-length political appeal; surviving bibliographies date it tentatively to 1923, so the date should not be treated as fully secure. [S8]
- **1923:** *Eight Uncle Tom Negroes and W. E. Burghardt Du Bois as a Hater of Dark People*. A polemical pamphlet produced during Garvey’s conflict with Du Bois and other Black political leaders. [S6]
- **1923–1925:** *The Philosophy and Opinions of Marcus Garvey; or, Africa for the Africans*. A two-volume compilation of speeches and writings selected and edited by Amy Jacques Garvey. The words are largely Marcus Garvey’s, but the books as organized objects are her editorial creation and should not be treated as two newly composed monographs by him. [S2; S3]
- **1924:** *Aims and Objects of Movement for Solution of Negro Problem Outlined*. A brief programmatic pamphlet: simultaneously exposition, recruitment instrument, and protocol for the UNIA’s political purpose. [S8]
- **1927:** *The Tragedy of White Injustice*. A long prison poem or verse polemic, issued by Amy Jacques Garvey; some bibliographies consequently give joint or ambiguous responsibility for the publication, although the poetic text is attributed to Marcus Garvey. [S1; S8]
- **1927:** *Selections from the Poetic Meditations of Marcus Garvey*. A booklet of twenty-four dated prison poems, selected and published by Amy Jacques Garvey. It is a genuine Garvey poetic cycle but not his complete poetic production. [S9]
- **1928:** *Speech at Royal Albert Hall, London, England: The Case of the Negro for International Racial Adjustment*. A separately printed speech: an oratorical event converted into a pamphlet, and therefore both performance and documentary manifestation. [S6]
- **1934:** *Universal Negro Improvement Association Convention Hymns*. A convention hymnal associated with Garvey’s leadership and publishing program. Authorship of every hymn should not be assigned to him merely from the imprint; it is an institutional and potentially collaborative compilation. [S6]

### Manifestos, constitutions, and political programs

- **1914 onward:** *Constitution and Book of Laws of the Universal Negro Improvement Association and African Communities’ League*. The governing protocol of Garvey’s movement, repeatedly revised as the organization changed. It is institutional legislation rather than a stable single-authored book, and individual clauses cannot automatically be assigned to Garvey. [S1; S10]
- **1920:** *Declaration of Rights of the Negro Peoples of the World*. A manifesto enacted by the UNIA’s international convention: Garvey was its principal political architect, but convention delegates drafted, debated, and adopted it collectively. Its red-black-green standard, anthem, offices, honors, and claims to African nationhood make it part of a larger political Gesamtkunstwerk rather than merely a declaration on paper. [S10]
- **1925:** *African Fundamentalism*. A creed first printed as a *Negro World* editorial written in Atlanta Penitentiary, then slightly revised and sold as a UNIA poster. It is a manifesto in the literal sense: a text intended to institute racial doctrine and conduct, not simply describe them. [S11]
- **1929:** *People’s Political Party platform*. A Jamaican reform manifesto calling for measures in public health, housing, labor, education, and law. Garvey unveiled it in a public speech, but the platform also functioned as the program of a party and therefore exceeds individual authorship. [S12]
- **1937:** *Lessons from the School of African Philosophy: The New Way to Education*. A twenty-one-lesson course, with an oath, reading instructions, leadership training, economics, diplomacy, propaganda, institutional practice, and a five-year UNIA plan. It is best classified as a protocol for forming Garveyist organizers; the text survived through students’ typescripts and was published posthumously as *Message to the People*, so its exact wording is textually mediated. [S1]
- **1937:** *Five-Year Plan of the UNIA*. An organizational program embedded in the final lesson of the School of African Philosophy; simultaneously a projected institution-building campaign and an unfinished political project. [S1]

### Essays, autobiography, dialogues, and serial forms

- **1923:** *Autobiography* (articles in the *Pittsburgh Courier*). A serial autobiographical account rather than a book conceived in its later collected form. Its boundaries are supplied by periodical publication and modern editors, so it resists being treated as a single finished autobiography. [S1]
- **1925:** *Governing the Ideal State*. A prison-written political-philosophical essay that Garvey compared to utopian state-making exercises such as Plato’s *Republic*. It remained an abstract model rather than a constitution for an existing state. [S13]
- **1920s–1930s:** *His Vanity Fair; or, The Tragedy of White Injustice*. A satirical sequence or framing work associated with Garvey’s prison and periodical writings. Its relationship to the separately issued poem *The Tragedy of White Injustice* is unstable in later editorial presentation, so it should not be silently counted as an entirely independent book. [S1]
- **1930s:** *A Dialogue: What’s the Difference?* A dramatic dialogue published through *The Black Man*, using named speakers and staged exchange rather than expository prose; it is closer to a political sketch than to an essay. [S1]
- **1930s:** *Smiles for the Thoughtful, by “The Imp”*. A sequence of satirical dialogues or comic observations published under a persona. The pseudonymous frame makes “the Imp” part of the made object while complicating straightforward authorial identity. [S1]

### Poetry and song

- **c. 1910s–1939:** *Garvey’s poetic corpus*. The body of poems subsequently assembled as *The Poetical Works of Marcus Garvey* includes political hymns, devotional verse, occasional poems, satires, and prison meditations. The posthumous collection is an editorial reconstruction, not a book Garvey himself made in that form, and dates for individual poems are unevenly documented. [S14]
- **1920:** *A National Hymn*. A political hymn associated with the UNIA’s nation-making ceremonies. It is a performative communal text and not merely a page poem. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Africa for the Africans*. A poem and rallying text built around Garvey’s central slogan; later musical settings are manifestations by other makers and should not be attributed to Garvey as compositions. [S14]
- **1920s:** *The Battle Hymn of Africa*. A ceremonial poem or hymn intended for collective performance within the Garvey movement. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Hail! United States of Africa*. A poetic projection of a future federation: both lyric and unrealized political project. [S14]
- **1920s:** *The Black Mother*. A political poem personifying Africa; it belongs to Garvey’s poetry, not to the later musical works that reuse Garveyite imagery. [S14]
- **1920s:** *A Black Man’s Prayer*. A devotional poem linking religious address to racial emancipation. [S14]
- **1920s:** *A Black Man’s Speech to a White Man in America*. A verse address whose title identifies it as simulated public speech as well as poem. [S14]
- **1920s:** *A Rallying Song*. A poem designed for collective mobilization and therefore poised between lyric, song text, and manifesto. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Get Up and Do*. An exhortatory poem functioning almost as a compact protocol for action. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Get Up and Go!*. An exhortatory poem; despite its title and cadence, evidence of a musical composition by Garvey should not be presumed. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Get Up and Work*. A didactic labor poem within Garvey’s self-reliance program. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Go and Win*. An exhortatory poem or maxim expanded into verse. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Be King of Circumstances*. A didactic poem on self-mastery. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Find Yourself*. A poem of racial and personal self-knowledge. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Have Faith in Self*. A didactic poem, close in function to Garvey’s maxims and educational lessons. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Freedom’s Noble Cause*. A political poem presenting liberation as ethical obligation. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Our Day*. A prophetic political poem anticipating Black emancipation. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Blackman!*. An apostrophic race poem and call to action. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Black and White*. A poem organized around racial contrast. [S14]
- **1920s:** *The Black Woman*. A racial-uplift poem centered on Black womanhood. [S14]
- **1920s:** *The Bearers*. A political or allegorical poem; its precise date is not securely established in the consulted sources. [S14]
- **1920s:** *The Brute of History*. A historical-political poem. [S14]
- **1920s:** *The Brutal Crime*. A protest poem whose precise occasion and date require verification from the collected poetic edition. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Fire in the Flint*. A poem using latent fire as an image of unrealized power. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Man to Man*. A moral and political address in verse. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Man’s Ambition*. A didactic poem on aspiration. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Going Wrong*. A moral poem. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Let Us Know*. An exhortatory poem. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Life’s Procession*. A philosophical poem. [S14]
- **1920s:** *The Bravest Soul*. A moral-exemplary poem. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Broken Hearts*. A lyric poem rather than an organizational text. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Love’s Morning Star*. A lyric or devotional poem. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Music in My Soul*. A lyric poem; the title alone does not establish that Garvey supplied music. [S14]
- **1920s:** *A Summer’s Dream*. A lyric or visionary poem. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Death’s Pleasure*. A meditation on mortality. [S14]
- **1920s:** *The City Storm*. A descriptive or allegorical poem. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Revenge*. A moral-political poem. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Hell’s Back Door*. A religious-moral poem. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Christ the Way*. A Christian devotional poem. [S14]
- **1920s:** *For He Is God*. A devotional poem. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Glory to the Lord*. A hymn-like devotional poem. [S14]
- **1920s:** *The Call of Heaven*. A religious poem. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Jubilee*. A celebratory poem with religious and emancipatory resonances. [S14]
- **1920s:** *Merry Christmas*. An occasional religious poem. [S14]
- **1920s:** *A Recitative Song! (For Julius Winston Garvey)*. A family-addressed poem identified as a recitative song; because Julius Winston Garvey was born in 1933, broad listings that place it among undated “1920s” material are chronologically impossible, and the poem must date **1933 or later**. [S4; S14]
- **1927:** *Keep Cool*. A prison-written song text or poem advertised and performed as a song in Garvey’s lifetime. Contemporary notices attribute the words to Garvey, but the surviving evidence consulted here does not securely identify the composer of its musical setting. [S9; S15]
- **1929:** *Centenary’s Day*. An occasional poem associated with a centenary observance; its subject and exact publication context should be taken from the collected poetic edition rather than inferred from the title. [S14]
- **1930s:** *Africa’s Love*. A poem belonging to Garvey’s later poetic production; precise dating is uncertain. [S14]
- **1930s:** *The Devil in Mussolini*. An anti-fascist political poem responding to Mussolini and the Ethiopian crisis. [S14]
- **1936:** *Ras Nasibu of Ogaden*. A poem honoring the Ethiopian commander Nasibu Emmanual during resistance to the Italian invasion; spellings of both the title and Nasibu’s name vary in later reproductions. [S14]

### Organizations, enterprises, and constructed social forms

- **1914–1940:** *Universal Negro Improvement Association and African Communities’ League*. Garvey’s principal obra: an international self-help, political, commercial, educational, and ceremonial movement founded in Jamaica and reorganized in the United States. Thousands of members and local organizers made it collectively, so “work by Garvey” names initiation and sustained direction, not sole authorship. [S3; S10]
- **1918 onward:** *Liberty Hall system*. A network of meeting halls and movement spaces, beginning with the Harlem headquarters, made for lectures, worship, performance, commerce, and political assembly. No single building exhausts the work; it is an institutional and spatial project. [S10]
- **1919–1922:** *Black Star Line*. A Black-owned steamship corporation intended to join commerce, migration, and African liberation. It operated ships but never realized its full transatlantic project; promotional misrepresentation, disputed management, financial collapse, and Garvey’s mail-fraud conviction are inseparable from its status as both material enterprise and unfinished political symbol. [S3; S10]
- **1919 onward:** *Negro Factories Corporation*. An umbrella commercial project intended to establish Black-owned manufacturing and services, including a laundry, restaurants, grocery stores, garment work, and printing. Its components had unequal and often brief existences, so the corporation’s program was larger than its completed enterprises. [S10]
- **1919 onward:** *Universal Printing House*. The UNIA’s printing enterprise, created to manufacture newspapers, pamphlets, forms, and publicity for the movement. It was infrastructure for other works as much as an independent business. [S10]
- **1919 onward:** *Black Cross Nurses*. A uniformed women’s auxiliary providing health instruction, relief, and public ceremonial service. Garvey helped institute it, but Black women organized and enacted it; assigning the whole creation to him alone would erase its collective makers. [S10]
- **1919 onward:** *Universal African Legion*. A uniformed, disciplined male auxiliary that staged sovereignty and collective defense. Its ranks, drills, insignia, and parades made it a performative political form rather than merely an organization on paper. [S10]
- **1919 onward:** *Universal African Motor Corps*. A women’s auxiliary trained in driving, mechanics, discipline, and ceremonial service. Like the Black Cross Nurses, it was institutionally designed within Garveyism but materially made by its members. [S10]
- **1919 onward:** *UNIA Juvenile Division*. A children’s organization designed to reproduce Garveyist identity through education, uniform, ceremony, and participation. It is a pedagogical institution rather than a text. [S10]
- **1920:** *The red, black, and green standard*. The UNIA convention adopted the horizontal tricolor as the flag of the “Negro race.” It emerged from Garvey’s program and convention, but claims that Garvey personally designed every visual detail are less secure than the collective act of adoption. [S10]
- **1920:** *Provisional Government of Africa*. A government-in-exile or enacted political fiction, with Garvey as provisional president-general and with ministers, offices, honors, uniforms, anthem, flag, and constitutional claims. It resists being called either merely imaginary or an accomplished state: it was a lived ceremonial Gesamtkunstwerk directed toward a sovereignty that did not yet exist. [S10]
- **1920 onward:** *UNIA orders, titles, uniforms, parades, and convention ritual*. A linked ceremonial system—including the Orders of Ethiopia and the Nile—through which Garveyism performed government and historical dignity. Individual designs and practices had many makers, so this is properly a collective body of work under Garvey’s direction. [S10]
- **1924–1926:** *Black Cross Navigation and Trading Company*. Garvey’s attempt to reconstitute the failed Black Star Line as a new shipping and trading concern. It acquired the *S.S. General G. W. Goethals* but did not become the durable international network imagined for it. [S16]
- **1929:** *People’s Political Party*. Jamaica’s first modern mass-oriented political party, created by Garvey to contest elections and promote social and constitutional reform. It was an organizational manifestation of the platform, not simply the platform’s container. [S12]
- **1937:** *School of African Philosophy*. A Toronto training school created to form a cadre of UNIA leaders through Garvey’s lectures and lessons. Its graduates, notes, oath, and projected five-year program make it simultaneously institution, curriculum, and unfinished succession plan. [S1]

### Documentary and oratorical corpus that resists itemization

- **c. 1909–1940:** *Letters, telegrams, petitions, memoranda, legal statements, organizational directives, editorials, interviews, and speeches*. Thousands of such documents survive or are known through copies and reports; the scholarly *Marcus Garvey and Universal Negro Improvement Association Papers* prints a selection rather than a complete corpus because no parent archive survived. Treating every documentary witness as a separately titled “work” would invent boundaries the evidence does not possess, while omitting the corpus would exclude the chief medium of Garvey’s making. [S17]
- **c. 1916–1940:** *Public oratory*. Garvey’s lectures, convention addresses, courtroom speech, campaign speeches, and recorded addresses were made first as performances and often survive only in newspaper reports, stenographic versions, government surveillance copies, or later editing. Dates and titles belong to particular documentary witnesses, not necessarily to authorially fixed texts. [S17]
- **1918–1932:** *Negro World editorials*. A large serial body of political writing, normally led by Garvey’s front-page editorial, but containing unsigned, staff-written, reprinted, and sometimes only tentatively attributed matter. The searchable surviving run permits article-level study, yet the corpus cannot responsibly be converted into a definitive list of Garvey titles without repeating the archive’s question marks. [S5]
- **1927–1940:** *Maxims, proverbs, and “poetic meditations”*. Short sayings appeared on periodical covers and in Garvey’s publications, sometimes adapted from earlier writers—especially Marcus Aurelius—rather than wholly originated by Garvey. This body of work therefore resists both clean enumeration and unqualified claims of authorship. [S17]
