## Works by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz

### Corpus, papers, and things that resist work-status

- **1663–1716:** *Leibniz’s surviving papers (Nachlass)*. This is not a single obra: it comprises more than 80,000 manuscript items—drafts, notes, calculations, excerpts, drawings, official papers, and letters—many untitled or only provisionally dated; the still-unfinished *Akademie-Ausgabe* and its working catalogue are therefore the proper exhaustive inventory rather than any short list of “works.” (Leibniz-Edition, Series I–VIII; *Arbeitskatalog*, more than 70,200 records as of April 2026.)
- **1663–1716:** *Correspondence*. Thousands of letters to and from roughly 1,300 correspondents form a distributed intellectual practice rather than one authored book; individual letters can be works, but the correspondence as a whole resists singular authorship. (Leibniz-Edition, Series I–III; Persons and Correspondence Database.)
- **Throughout his working life:** *Excerpts, reading notes, marginalia, and slips*. These are material instruments for thinking and arranging knowledge, not necessarily texts intended as autonomous works; the *Arbeitskatalog* includes thousands of such pieces and continues to identify their sources. (Leibniz-Edition catalogue; *Leibniz’ historiographischer Zettelkasten*.)
- **Throughout his working life:** *Untitled logical, mathematical, philosophical, technical, and linguistic fragments*. Many survive in several drafts whose boundaries, sequence, and dates are editorial reconstructions; treating every sheet as a separately titled obra would manufacture identities Leibniz did not give them. (Leibniz-Edition, especially Series VI–VIII.)

### Dissertations, books, treatises, and dialogues

- **1663:** *Disputatio metaphysica de principio individui*. A university dissertation on individuation, defended at Leipzig and printed under Leibniz’s name; its scholastic form makes it simultaneously an academic exercise and an early philosophical obra. (A VI,1.)
- **1664:** *Specimen quaestionum philosophicarum ex jure collectarum*. A printed disputation assembling philosophical problems from law, produced for Leibniz’s master’s degree. (A VI,1.)
- **1666:** *Dissertatio de arte combinatoria*. A printed dissertation and program for combinatorial analysis, concept formation, and demonstrative reasoning; Leibniz later distanced himself from parts of this youthful work without disowning the larger project. (A VI,1.)
- **1666:** *De casibus perplexis in jure*. Leibniz’s Altdorf doctoral dissertation in law, a juridical treatise about difficult cases rather than a philosophical book in the later sense. (A VI,1.)
- **1667:** *Nova methodus discendae docendaeque jurisprudentiae*. A printed program for reorganizing the learning and teaching of jurisprudence; it is both educational protocol and reform project. (A VI,1.)
- **1668–1669:** *Confessio naturae contra atheistas*. A two-part apologetic treatise—on bodies and on minds—attempting to direct mechanical natural philosophy against atheism. (A VI,1.)
- **1668–1669:** *Defensio Trinitatis per nova reperta logica*. An unfinished logical-theological defense of the Trinity, transmitted in drafts rather than as a completed publication. (A VI,1.)
- **1668–1671:** *Demonstrationes catholicae*. A projected demonstrative encyclopedia of natural theology and Christian doctrine; the project, not a finished book, is the made thing, surviving through plans, preliminary demonstrations, and related drafts. (A VI,1; A VI,2.)
- **1669–1671:** *Elementa juris naturalis*. Successive, unfinished drafts toward a demonstrative science of natural law; editors distinguish several recensions rather than one final text. (A VI,1.)
- **1671:** *Theoria motus abstracti*. A printed physical-mathematical treatise on abstract motion, issued with a companion work on concrete motion. (A VI,2; A VII,2.)
- **1671:** *Hypothesis physica nova / Theoria motus concreti*. A printed “new physical hypothesis” addressing concrete motion and natural phenomena; its relationship to the separately issued *Theoria motus abstracti* is complementary rather than merely successive. (A VI,2; A VII,2.)
- **1672–1673:** *Confessio philosophi*. A Latin dialogue on divine justice, human freedom, and evil; left unpublished and known through manuscript witnesses whose composition and revision span more than one moment. (A VI,3.)
- **1673:** *Accessio ad arithmeticam infinitorum*. A mathematical supplement on infinite series and quadratures, printed by the Royal Society in connection with Leibniz’s London visit. (A VII,4.)
- **1675–1676:** *De summa rerum*. Not Leibniz’s title for a finished book but an editorially constituted group of Paris-period metaphysical and theological papers; it resists treatment as a single authored obra. (A VI,3.)
- **1676:** *Pacidius Philalethi*. A substantial unfinished dialogue on motion, continuity, and cohesion; its title names a manuscript composition, not a lifetime publication. (A VI,3.)
- **1677:** *Caesarini Fürstenerii de jure suprematus ac legationis principum Germaniae*. A pseudonymously published political-juridical treatise defending the sovereignty and diplomatic standing of German princes. (A IV,2.)
- **1677:** *Entretien de Philarete et d’Eugene*. A French dialogue recasting arguments from *De jure suprematus* for another public and medium. (A IV,2.)
- **c. 1678:** *Quid sit idea*. A short manuscript analysis of what an idea is; the familiar title is its opening phrase rather than evidence of an intended book. (A VI,4.)
- **1678–1679:** *De corporum concursu*. A large, unpublished investigation of collisions and conservation laws, reconstructed from many manuscript sheets; it is better understood as a research complex than a single polished treatise. (A VIII, forthcoming; critical reconstruction by Fichant.)
- **1686:** *Discours de métaphysique*. A thirty-seven-section exposition prepared in connection with the Arnauld exchange but not published in Leibniz’s lifetime; its present title is Leibnizian, while its canonical “book” status is posthumous. (A VI,4.)
- **1686–1690:** *Correspondence with Antoine Arnauld*. A jointly produced philosophical exchange about substance, complete concepts, bodies, and possible worlds; it is not solely Leibniz’s obra even though his letters constitute major works. (A II,2 and later editorial reconstructions.)
- **c. 1686:** *Examen religionis christianae*. A manuscript theological exposition associated with Leibniz’s church-reunion activity; it remained unfinished and unpublished. (A VI,4.)
- **c. 1686:** *Systema theologicum*. A synthetic theological manuscript, later published posthumously under an editorial title; its date, intended audience, and relation to Leibniz’s reunion negotiations have been debated. (A VI,4 and later editions.)
- **1689:** *Primae veritates*. An untitled manuscript conventionally known as *Primary Truths*; once dated around 1686, it was redated to 1689 by the Academy editors from physical evidence including its paper watermark. (A VI,4, N. 324.)
- **1704:** *Nouveaux essais sur l’entendement humain*. A book-length dialogue replying chapter by chapter to Locke; completed but withheld after Locke’s death and first published in 1765, so its lifetime status was a finished manuscript rather than a public book. (A VI,6.)
- **1710:** *Essais de Théodicée sur la bonté de Dieu, la liberté de l’homme et l’origine du mal*. Leibniz’s principal lifetime philosophical book, published anonymously and accompanied by several related preliminary and supplementary texts. (A VI,6, later-edited writings; first edition, Amsterdam, 1710.)
- **1714:** *Principes de la nature et de la grâce fondés en raison*. A concise French exposition written for Prince Eugene or his circle; it circulated in manuscript and was published posthumously. (Robinet, *Principes de la nature et de la grâce*; Akademie catalogue.)
- **1714:** *La Monadologie*. An untitled French manuscript exposition whose now-canonical title was supplied by eighteenth-century editors; different manuscript and translation histories complicate the notion of one definitive original “book.” (Robinet, *Principes de la nature et de la grâce et Monadologie*; Akademie catalogue.)
- **1715–1716:** *Leibniz–Clarke correspondence*. Five papers by Leibniz and five replies by Samuel Clarke form a jointly made controversy about God, space, time, matter, and freedom; the exchange, mediated by Caroline of Ansbach, cannot properly be assigned to Leibniz alone. (Clarke, *A Collection of Papers*, 1717; Akademie catalogue.)

### Essays, journal interventions, and manifestos of method

- **1682:** *De vera proportione circuli ad quadratum circumscriptum in numeris rationalibus*. A mathematical journal article presenting an infinite series for π; it is an intervention in the new medium of the learned periodical. (A VII; *Acta Eruditorum*, 1682.)
- **1684:** *Meditationes de cognitione, veritate et ideis*. A compact philosophical essay distinguishing kinds of cognition and criteria of adequate ideas, published in the *Acta Eruditorum*. (A VI,4.)
- **1684:** *Nova methodus pro maximis et minimis, itemque tangentibus*. The first printed statement of Leibniz’s differential calculus; it functions partly as a protocol, giving symbolic rules with little foundational explanation. (A VII; *Acta Eruditorum*, October 1684.)
- **1686:** *De geometria recondita et analysi indivisibilium atque infinitorum*. A published presentation of integral-calculus notation and techniques. (A VII; *Acta Eruditorum*, June 1686.)
- **1686:** *Brevis demonstratio erroris memorabilis Cartesii et aliorum*. A journal demonstration attacking Cartesian conservation of quantity of motion and advancing what became the vis viva controversy. (A VI,4; *Acta Eruditorum*, 1686.)
- **1687:** *Réplique de M. L. à M. l’Abbé D. C.* A contribution to the Arnauld–Malebranche debate concerning ideas and representation; its abbreviated periodical title and polemical occasion make it an intervention rather than a free-standing book. (A VI,4.)
- **1689:** *Tentamen de motuum coelestium causis*. A published dynamical account of planetary motion, offered in rivalry and partial convergence with Newtonian celestial mechanics. (A VI,4; *Acta Eruditorum*, 1689.)
- **1692:** *Animadversiones in partem generalem Principiorum Cartesianorum*. A critical commentary on the general part of Descartes’s *Principia*, published in the *Acta Eruditorum*. (A VI,4.)
- **1694:** *De primae philosophiae emendatione et de notione substantiae*. A brief manifesto for reforming first philosophy through a concept of substance grounded in force. (Gerhardt, *Philosophische Schriften*, IV; *Acta Eruditorum*, 1694.)
- **1695:** *Système nouveau de la nature et de la communication des substances*. A published programmatic essay introducing the new system of substances and pre-established harmony; replies and clarifications followed, making it the opening move in a public textual exchange. (Gerhardt, IV; *Journal des Sçavans*, 1695.)
- **1695:** *Specimen dynamicum*. A two-part Latin exposition of dynamics; only the first part appeared during Leibniz’s lifetime, while the second remained manuscript, so “the work” is materially incomplete as a publication. (Gerhardt, *Mathematische Schriften*, VI.)
- **1696:** *Tentamen anagogicum*. A manuscript essay linking mechanical explanation to final causes; its conventional title comes from Leibniz’s draft. (Gerhardt, VII.)
- **1697:** *De rerum originatione radicali*. A published metaphysical essay on why this world exists and why it has the order it does. (Gerhardt, VII.)
- **1698:** *De ipsa natura, sive de vi insita actionibusque creaturarum*. A journal essay defending active powers in created things against occasionalist and purely passive conceptions of matter. (Gerhardt, IV; *Acta Eruditorum*, 1698.)
- **1702:** *Considérations sur la doctrine d’un esprit universel unique*. A French essay rejecting the doctrine that individual minds are merely temporary modifications of one universal spirit. (Gerhardt, VI.)
- **1702–1703:** *Méditation sur la notion commune de la justice*. A manuscript meditation defining justice through the wisdom of charity; dating remains approximate. (Mollat, *Rechtsphilosophisches aus Leibnizens ungedruckten Schriften*; Riley.)
- **1703:** *Explication de l’arithmétique binaire*. A published account of binary arithmetic, illustrated through comparison with the hexagrams of the *Yijing*; it is mathematical exposition and cross-cultural manifestation at once. (Gerhardt, *Mathematische Schriften*, VII; *Mémoires de l’Académie royale des sciences*, 1703.)
- **1706:** *Monita quaedam ad Samuelis Pufendorfii principia*. A critical juridical-theological response to Pufendorf’s principles of natural law. (Dutens, IV; Riley.)
- **1707:** *Cum prodiisset*. An unpublished explanation of the differential calculus and its rules; the title is merely its opening phrase. (Gerhardt, *Mathematische Schriften*, V.)
- **1712:** *Judgment of the Works of the Earl of Shaftesbury*. A critical assessment written in response to Shaftesbury, surviving as a manuscript rather than a lifetime publication. (Riley, *Political Writings*.)
- **1715:** *Observations sur le projet d’une paix perpétuelle de l’Abbé de Saint-Pierre*. A critical engagement with Saint-Pierre’s peace project, itself part of Leibniz’s long-running designs for European order. (Riley, *Political Writings*.)

### Logic, signs, universal science, and unfinished projects

- **1666–1716:** *Ars combinatoria*. Beyond the 1666 dissertation, this was a lifelong project for systematically generating and testing combinations of concepts; it never stabilized as one finished work. (A VI–VII; Couturat, *Opuscules et fragments inédits*.)
- **1666–1716:** *Characteristica universalis*. A project for a universal system of signs in which conceptual structure could be represented and transformed; no single surviving text or notation is “the” characteristic. (A VI–VII; Couturat.)
- **1670s–1716:** *Calculus ratiocinator*. A project for making valid reasoning calculable; manuscript logical calculi are manifestations of it, not a completed universal protocol. (A VI; Couturat; Parkinson.)
- **1670s–1716:** *Scientia generalis*. A projected general science of invention, judgment, signs, method, and the ordering of knowledge; its numerous plans resist consolidation into a single book. (A VI,4 and later manuscripts; Couturat.)
- **1678–1686:** *Generales inquisitiones de analysi notionum et veritatum*. A large manuscript investigation of conceptual analysis and formal inference, dated 1686 in its surviving principal form but drawing on earlier logical experiments. (Couturat; A VI,4.)
- **1679:** *Elementa characteristicae universalis*. A cluster of manuscripts assigning numbers to primitive concepts and testing arithmetical representation of propositions; the plural drafts, not one finished calculus, constitute the work. (A VI,4; Couturat.)
- **1679:** *Binary calculating machine*. A paper design using binary states, envisaged in some descriptions through moving balls or marbles; it remained a project rather than a completed apparatus. (A VII; Zacher, *Hauptschriften zur Dyadik*.)
- **c. 1680s–1710s:** *Combinatorial or analytical machine*. Designs for mechanisms that would manipulate letters or general symbols, extending mechanized calculation beyond numbers; surviving descriptions do not establish a completed physical machine. (Leibniz-Edition manuscripts; Leibniz Philosophical Papers Project.)
- **c. 1679–1716:** *Analysis situs*. A recurrent project for a “geometry of situation” concerned with position and connection rather than magnitude; it exists as proposals and specimen problems, not one definitive treatise. (Gerhardt, *Mathematische Schriften*, V; A VII.)
- **c. 1693:** *Integraph*. A projected mechanical instrument for drawing curves and, in principle, performing graphical integration; the surviving object is a design rather than a securely completed machine. (Gerland, *Nachgelassene Schriften physikalischen, mechanischen und technischen Inhalts*.)
- **1680s–1716:** *Encyclopedia and universal library projects*. Plans for arranging all sciences, improving catalogues, abstracting books, and coordinating learned labor; these are infrastructural projects dispersed across memoranda rather than a single encyclopedia authored to completion. (A IV; Couturat.)

### Mathematics and symbolic constructions

- **1673–1676:** *Arithmetical quadrature of the circle and ellipse*. A substantial Latin treatise developed in Paris, demonstrating quadratures through infinite series; completed in manuscript but not published by Leibniz, and critically edited only in modern times. (A VII,6, *De quadratura arithmetica circuli ellipseos et hyperbolae*.)
- **1675:** *Differential and integral notation*. Leibniz’s manuscript calculations introduced forms of \(d\) and the elongated-s integral sign; these signs are made symbolic instruments rather than a book, and priority disputes concern the calculus more broadly, not the existence of these dated manuscripts. (A VII,3; Leibniz manuscript, 29 October 1675.)
- **1670s–1716:** *Differential and integral calculus*. A body of methods, symbols, demonstrations, publications, and correspondence rather than one text; Newton independently developed a different fluxional calculus, and the later priority controversy cannot be reduced to authorship of a single work. (A VII; Gerhardt, *Mathematische Schriften*; *Commercium epistolicum*.)
- **1670s–1716:** *Determinant and elimination methods*. A manuscript body of algebraic techniques for systems of linear equations; their modern classification as determinant theory is retrospective, and Leibniz did not issue them as a single named work. (A VII; Knobloch’s studies of Leibnizian determinants.)
- **1679–1703:** *Dyadic or binary arithmetic*. A sustained mathematical construction expressed through tables, manuscripts, machine designs, and the 1703 printed *Explication*; it is a body of work rather than a single invention-date. (A VII; Zacher.)
- **1680s–1716:** *Infinite-series calculus*. Numerous algorithms, transformations, convergence discussions, and exchanges—especially with the Bernoullis—constitute a collaborative mathematical practice whose individual components cannot all be collapsed into named works. (A III; A VII; Gerhardt, *Mathematische Schriften*.)
- **1690s–1716:** *Calculus of variations and isoperimetric methods*. Problems, solution techniques, and correspondence developed with Jakob and Johann Bernoulli; attribution is distributed across the exchange rather than exclusively Leibnizian. (A III; Gerhardt, *Mathematische Schriften*.)

### Natural philosophy, earth history, and life sciences

- **1670s–1690s:** *Dynamics*. Leibniz’s science of force, expressed in collision studies, the vis viva controversy, *Specimen dynamicum*, and many unpublished drafts; it is a body of work whose terminology and principles changed over time. (A VI; A VIII; Gerhardt, *Mathematische Schriften*, VI.)
- **1691–1693:** *Protogaea*. A manuscript earth history written in connection with the history of the House of Brunswick, treating strata, fossils, and the earth’s formation; only a preliminary notice appeared in 1693, while the full work was published posthumously in 1749. (Leibniz, *Protogaea*; Akademie catalogue.)
- **1690s–1716:** *Living machines and organism*. Not one titled work but a theoretical construction developed across essays and correspondence: organic bodies are machines whose parts remain organized to infinity. (Gerhardt, IV; correspondence with De Volder and Des Bosses.)
- **c. 1700–1716:** *Medical reform plans*. Memoranda proposed public-health administration, medical statistics, professional organization, and coordinated observation; they are protocols and governmental projects rather than medical treatises. (A IV; A VIII.)
- **c. 1700–1716:** *Insurance and mortality projects*. Proposals for annuities, widows’ funds, mortality calculations, and public insurance combine mathematical work with administrative design; several were unrealized or only partially developed. (A IV; A VIII.)

### Histories, documentary monuments, and language projects

- **1680s–1716:** *History of the House of Brunswick*. Leibniz’s enormous commissioned historical project generated archival journeys, transcriptions, genealogies, source editions, *Protogaea*, and annals but was never completed as the promised dynastic history. (A I; A V; Bodemann.)
- **1693:** *Codex juris gentium diplomaticus*. A printed collection of diplomatic and legal documents with a substantial preface on international law; it is both source-edition and juridical obra. (A IV; first edition, Hanover, 1693.)
- **1700:** *Mantissa codicis juris gentium diplomatici*. A printed supplement adding documents to the 1693 *Codex*. (A IV; first edition, Hanover, 1700.)
- **1707–1711:** *Scriptores rerum Brunsvicensium illustrationi inservientes*. A three-volume documentary edition of medieval and early-modern historical sources assembled for Brunswick history; it is editorial scholarship rather than a narrative authored wholly by Leibniz. (Hanover, 1707–1711; A V catalogue.)
- **c. 1690s–1716:** *Annales Imperii Occidentis Brunsvicenses*. Unfinished Latin annals of the Western Empire and Brunswick dynasty, reaching only part of the projected chronological span; published posthumously from the manuscripts. (Pertz, *Annales Imperii Occidentis Brunsvicenses*.)
- **1690s–1716:** *Genealogical tables and proofs for the House of Brunswick-Lüneburg*. Documentary and diagrammatic products supporting dynastic claims, inseparable from Leibniz’s archival labor and court employment. (A I; A V; Bodemann.)
- **1690s–1716:** *Collectanea etymologica*. A posthumously assembled collection of Leibniz’s linguistic and etymological materials, not a book he finalized under that title. (Eckhart, ed., 1717; Leibniz-Edition digital facsimile.)
- **1690s–1716:** *Comparative-language questionnaires and word lists*. Protocols for collecting linguistic evidence from travelers, missionaries, officials, and scholars; the resulting multilingual archive was collaborative and remained open-ended. (A I; A V.)
- **c. 1700–1716:** *Project for a universal comparative linguistics*. A research program using vocabularies, place names, inscriptions, and historical testimony to reconstruct relations among peoples; it produced many fragments but no completed universal language history. (A V; *Collectanea etymologica*.)

### Political texts, manifestos, and state projects

- **1669:** *Specimen demonstrationum politicarum pro eligendo rege Polonorum*. A pseudonymous election pamphlet applying a formally demonstrative structure to the Polish royal succession. (A IV,1.)
- **1669:** *Georgius Ulicovius Lithuanus*. The pseudonymous authorial persona attached to Leibniz’s Polish-election intervention; persona and pamphlet together operate as political manifestation rather than transparent authorship. (A IV,1.)
- **1669–1670:** *Bedenken, welchergestalt Securitas Publica … zu stabilieren*. A substantial memorandum on securing the Holy Roman Empire, commonly shortened to *Securitas publica*. (A IV,1.)
- **1669–1672:** *Bedenken von Aufrichtung einer Akademie oder Societät in Deutschland*. A sequence of memoranda proposing a German learned society dedicated to arts, sciences, and useful practice; dates and textual boundaries differ among drafts. (A IV,1.)
- **1670–1671:** *Consilium Aegyptiacum*. An extensive strategic project urging France toward an Egyptian expedition and away from war in Europe; it survives in several forms rather than as one publicly issued book. (A IV,1.)
- **1671–1672:** *De expeditione Aegyptiaca regi Franciae proponenda justa dissertatio*. The fuller Latin presentation of the Egyptian project, designed to act on royal policy but never formally delivered to Louis XIV in the intended manner. (A IV,1.)
- **1676:** *Consultatio de naturae cognitione ad vitam promovenda*. A memorandum advocating an institution that would coordinate knowledge of nature for practical human benefit. (A IV,1.)
- **1683:** *Mars Christianissimus*. An anonymously published satirical political pamphlet attacking Louis XIV’s expansionism; its irony and polemical agency make it a manifesto-like intervention. (A IV,2.)
- **1684:** *Raisons touchant la guerre ou l’accommodement avec la France*. An unpublished policy memorandum weighing war against settlement with France rather than a public declaration. (A IV,2.)
- **1678–1685:** *Harz mining project*. An engineering-administrative effort to drain mines with wind-powered pumps and improve mining operations; trials were real, but the project failed to achieve the promised durable system and involved craftsmen and miners as co-makers. (A I; A III; Gerland.)
- **1678–1685:** *Horizontal windmill and pump systems for the Harz*. Designs, models, instructions, and installations within the mining project; attribution to Leibniz concerns conception and direction, not solitary fabrication. (A III; Gerland.)
- **c. 1679:** *Portrait of the Prince*. A political-ethical manuscript describing exemplary rulership; it remained unpublished in Leibniz’s lifetime. (Riley, *Political Writings*.)
- **mid-1690s:** *Mémoire pour des personnes éclairées et de bonne intention*. A reform memorandum for enlightened political actors; its dating is approximate and it was not a lifetime publication. (Riley, *Political Writings*.)
- **c. 1694–1698:** *De felicitate*. A group of reflections on happiness and political ethics rather than a securely finalized independent book. (Grua; Riley.)
- **1697–1699:** *Projects for the reunion of Protestant churches*. Negotiating papers aimed at reconciling Lutheran and Reformed communities; they form a political-theological process, not one finished treaty. (A I; A IV.)
- **1670s–1702:** *Catholic–Protestant reunion negotiations*. Draft formulas, letters, theological memoranda, and institutional proposals made with figures including Bossuet, Spinola, and Molanus; this collaborative project failed to produce reunion and cannot be credited to Leibniz alone. (A I; A II; A IV.)
- **1700:** *Brandenburg Society of Sciences*. An institution whose statutes, program, and organization Leibniz substantially designed and whose first president he became; it is a collective creation, not an exclusively authored object. (Berlin-Brandenburg Academy history; A I; A IV.)
- **1703:** *Manifesto for the Defense of the Rights of Charles III*. A political manifesto supporting the Habsburg claimant in the War of the Spanish Succession; unlike a descriptive treatise, it was meant to intervene in an active succession struggle. (Riley, *Political Writings*.)
- **1704–1716:** *Russian academy and reform proposals*. Memoranda and conversations with Peter I proposed an academy, libraries, schools, scientific surveys, and administrative reforms; the later Russian Academy was not simply Leibniz’s creation, and most of his integrated scheme remained a project. (A I; Guerrier.)
- **1711–1716:** *Imperial academy proposals in Vienna*. Plans for a learned society under Habsburg patronage; Leibniz obtained support and titles but did not realize the institution. (A I; A IV.)
- **1712–1716:** *Projects for scientific exploration and mapping of Russia and Asia*. Protocols for geographic, magnetic, linguistic, ethnographic, and natural-historical observation, intended for state-supported expeditions. (A I; A III; Guerrier.)

### Machines, instruments, models, and designs

- **1671–c. 1694:** *Decimal calculating machine (Stepped Reckoner)*. A four-operation mechanical calculator developed through several designs and at least two constructed machines; Leibniz conceived and directed it, but artisans physically fabricated the devices, and the surviving machine was not reliably functional in every operation. (A III; Leibniz University Hannover exhibition; Jones.)
- **1673:** *Wooden calculating-machine model demonstrated in London*. A provisional manifestation presented to the Royal Society; contemporary reports indicate incompleteness, so it should not be silently identified with the later brass machine. (Royal Society records; A III.)
- **c. 1690s:** *Surviving brass-and-steel calculating machine*. A workshop-made embodiment of Leibniz’s design, discovered in the Göttingen attic of the Hanover library in 1879; construction history and artisan contributions make “made by Leibniz” an attribution of design and supervision, not personal manufacture. (Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz Bibliothek; Leibniz University Hannover.)
- **1670s–1716:** *Stepped-drum mechanism (Leibniz wheel)*. A mechanical construction enabling multiplication by repeated addition through a cylinder with teeth of graduated length; it is a component and operative principle rather than a freestanding text. (A III; Jones.)
- **1670s–1680s:** *Mining machinery, drill, cable, pump, and transmission designs*. Drawings and specifications accompanied the Harz work; some were constructed or tested, others remained paper machines, and craftsmen’s contributions complicate individual authorship. (A III; Gerland.)
- **1690s–1716:** *Carriage, clock, hydraulic, and wind-power designs*. A heterogeneous body of technical drawings and memoranda, many unfinished or unbuilt; it resists classification as one obra except at the level of Leibniz’s engineering practice. (A III; A VIII; Gerland.)
- **c. 1700:** *Ship-propulsion and navigation proposals*. Technical projects for improving vessels, including mechanical propulsion and navigational methods; manuscript survival does not imply successful construction. (A III; A VIII; Gerland.)
- **c. 1700–1716:** *Universal measuring, calculating, and observational instruments*. Designs for devices serving geometry, astronomy, timekeeping, and administration; many are known only from sketches or correspondence and must be classified as projects rather than completed artifacts. (A III; A VIII; Gerland.)

### Editorial and curatorial constructions made after Leibniz’s death

- **1717:** *Collectanea etymologica*. Edited by Johann Georg von Eckhart from Leibniz’s papers; it manifests Leibniz’s linguistic labor but is not a volume he himself assembled for publication.
- **1718:** *Otium Hanoveranum sive miscellanea*. Joachim Friedrich Feller’s posthumous compilation from Leibniz’s speech and papers; its selection and title are editorial creations.
- **1749:** *Protogaea*. The first full posthumous printing transformed Leibniz’s manuscript earth history into a public book decades after his death.
- **1765:** *Nouveaux essais sur l’entendement humain*. Raspe’s posthumous publication made the completed 1704 manuscript public; the edition is a manifestation of Leibniz’s obra, not a new work written in 1765.
- **1768:** *Opera omnia*. Louis Dutens’s six-volume collection constructed a printed Leibnizian corpus; it is an editor’s Gesamtkunstwerk-like compilation, not an authored “complete works” set prepared by Leibniz.
- **1849–1890:** *Gerhardt editions of the mathematical and philosophical writings*. These foundational scholarly collections created many familiar textual boundaries and titles but are incomplete and sometimes textually superseded.
- **1923–present:** *Sämtliche Schriften und Briefe (Akademie-Ausgabe)*. The historical-critical edition is the standard catalogue raisonné of Leibniz’s writings and correspondence; still incomplete, it makes visible why “everything Leibniz made” cannot honestly be reduced to a closed list of named books.
