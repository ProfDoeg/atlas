# Hilma af Klint: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier reconstructs Hilma af Klint’s life and posthumous history from institutional chronologies, Swedish biographical records, exhibition documentation, published notebook selections, scholarly studies, contemporary reporting, and recent reporting on disputes surrounding her foundation and artistic legacy.

Terms used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary records, af Klint’s surviving papers, institutional records, or multiple strong secondary sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a credible source but not independently confirmed here from the underlying document.
- **Claim or interpretation:** a scholarly, familial, institutional, or journalistic conclusion open to dispute.
- **Spiritual claim:** an experience recorded or asserted by af Klint or her circle; historically documented as a belief or report, not independently verifiable as supernatural fact.
- **Rumor or myth:** a widely repeated story whose factual basis is weak, misstated, or contradicted by documentary evidence.

Dates and facts vary occasionally among sources. The largest recurrent discrepancies concern when af Klint formally joined the Theosophical Society, precisely which pictures were executed collaboratively, Rudolf Steiner’s response to her work, the legal force of her twenty-year restriction, and the circumstances of the accident preceding her death.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Hilma af Klint.
- **Pronunciation and surname:** “af” is part of the Swedish noble surname, not a middle name. “Klint” alone is an imprecise shortening.
- **Alternative names:** No documented pseudonym or professional alias has been found. Within spiritualist records, she and her companions sometimes used initials, signs, collective designations, or the name **De Fem** (“The Five”), but these were not ordinary pseudonyms.
- **Occupation:** Painter, draughtswoman, botanical and veterinary illustrator, portraitist, landscape painter, spiritualist practitioner, theosophical and anthroposophical student, diarist, compiler of esoteric notebooks.
- **Nationality:** Swedish.
- **Birth:** 26 October 1862, Karlberg Palace, then a military establishment at Solna, immediately outside Stockholm, Sweden.
- **Death:** 21 October 1944, Djursholm area, Sweden, five days before her eighty-second birthday.
- **Burial:** Galärvarvskyrkogården, the naval cemetery on Djurgården in Stockholm, in the family grave headed by her father’s name.
- **Marital status:** Never married.
- **Children:** None documented.
- **Principal affiliations:** Royal Academy of Fine Arts, Stockholm; Swedish Association for Art; Theosophical Society; Association of Swedish Women Artists; Anthroposophical Society; De Fem.
- **Extent of surviving legacy:** Institutional counts vary because they distinguish paintings, drawings, sketchbooks, collaborative sheets, and notebook pages differently. The intact estate contains roughly 1,300–1,600 artistic works, more than 125 notebooks, and approximately 26,000 manuscript pages. The seven-volume catalogue raisonné records nearly 1,600 works.

Af Klint belonged to the Swedish untitled nobility. No inherited personal title equivalent to baroness or countess is documented.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal line

Hilma was born into a family closely associated with the Swedish navy, navigation, hydrography, mathematics, and cartography.

Her paternal grandfather was **Gustaf af Klint** (1771–1840), a Swedish naval officer and cartographer known for nautical charts. Hilma’s father, **Fredrik Victor af Klint**—usually rendered Fredrik Viktor in Swedish sources—was born 19 September 1822 and died in 1898. He attained the rank of commander or commander-captain and taught naval cadets at Karlberg Palace. His interest in mathematics and nature is regularly identified as an influence on Hilma.

The family’s professional culture supplied several elements later conspicuous in her work: classification, diagramming, accurate observation, mapping, signs, measurement, and the idea that an image could chart an otherwise invisible territory.

### Mother

Her mother was **Andrietta Mathilda “Thilda” af Klint, née Sontag**, born 14 November 1830 and died 5 January 1920. Later in life she became blind or severely visually impaired. Hilma reorganized her work and residence to help care for her, assisted by nurse Thomasine Andersson.

### Siblings

Hilma was described as the fourth of five children. The Swedish Women’s Biographical Dictionary states that only Hilma, her elder brother **Gustaf**, and younger sister **Ida** reached adulthood. A younger sister, **Hermina**, died in 1880 at age ten. Another child had died in infancy before Hilma’s birth.

Hermina’s death is often presented as the event that caused Hilma to turn to spiritualism. The evidence supports a more qualified account:

- **Documented fact:** Hermina died in 1880.
- **Documented fact:** Hilma had begun attending séances by approximately 1879, before Hermina’s death.
- **Interpretation:** The bereavement probably intensified an interest already present; it did not necessarily originate it.

Hilma’s brother Gustaf became the father of naval officer **Erik af Klint** (1901–1981), Hilma’s nephew and eventual heir. Erik preserved the bulk of her artistic and manuscript estate and created the Hilma af Klint Foundation in 1972.

### Wealth and social position

Sources describe the family as affluent, upper-middle-class, noble, or aristocratic. They possessed or used the rural family properties Tofta and Hanmora on Adelsö. Hilma enjoyed educational opportunities unavailable to most Swedish women of her generation, but surviving evidence does not establish that she possessed unrestricted personal wealth sufficient to finance every later project.

Her income came partly from conventional landscape and portrait painting and illustration. Anna Cassel and other women in her circle supplied important financial and practical assistance. The construction of the Munsö studio was financed substantially by Cassel and the women’s network.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1862–1879

Hilma was born at Karlberg Palace because her father taught the naval cadets stationed there. In 1868 the family moved to Norrtullsgatan in Stockholm. Summers were spent at Tofta and Hanmora on Adelsö in Lake Mälaren.

The island landscape gave her prolonged exposure to plants, water, animal life, and seasonal change. Her later botanical pictures were not a sudden retreat from abstraction but part of a lifelong observational practice.

In 1872 she entered the **Normalskola för flickor**, a progressive girls’ school in Stockholm. She displayed ability in drawing and an interest in botany and mathematics. The Swedish Women’s Biographical Dictionary identifies her as the first woman in her family to pursue education beyond secondary school.

By 1879 she was participating in séances, probably in the circle of **Bertha Valerius**, a portrait painter, photographer, and medium. Spiritualism was then fashionable among educated European and American circles and offered women unusually authoritative roles as mediums, speakers, writers, and organizers.

---

## Education and Formation, 1879–1887

In 1879–1880 af Klint studied at Stockholm’s **Technical School**, now Konstfack. She also took portrait-painting lessons at the private school of **Kerstin Cardon**.

From 1882 through 1887 she studied at the **Royal Academy of Fine Arts in Stockholm**, which admitted women earlier than many comparable European academies. Her teachers included:

- **Georg von Rosen**, history and portrait painter.
- **August Malmström**, history painter, illustrator, and academy professor.
- Kerstin Cardon, through private instruction.

Her studies included portraiture, landscape, anatomical observation, and botanical drawing. She met **Anna Cassel**, who became her closest enduring artistic and spiritual collaborator.

Af Klint graduated in 1887 with strong marks or honors. As a reward for her academic performance, she received access to an academy-associated studio in the Atelier Building near Kungsträdgården. This was an important professional advantage, placing her in Stockholm’s principal artistic district near exhibition spaces such as Blanch’s Art Salon.

## Early Professional Career, 1887–1905

### Conventional art and employment

Af Klint initially built a recognizable professional career as a naturalistic painter. She produced:

- Landscapes, especially scenes associated with Adelsö and Lake Mälaren.
- Portraits, including commissioned portraits.
- Botanical studies.
- Animal and scientific illustrations.
- Occasional religious, allegorical, or genre compositions.

Between 1886 and 1914 she participated in more than two dozen exhibitions, many organized by **Sveriges Allmänna Konstförening**, the Swedish Association for Art. The later characterization of her as an entirely unknown recluse is therefore incorrect when applied to her conventional work.

In 1900 she worked as a draughtswoman at Stockholm’s Veterinary Institute. With Anna Cassel, she illustrated veterinarian John Vennerholm’s *Grunddragen af hästens operativa speciella kirurgi* (“Fundamentals of the Horse’s Special Operative Surgery”), published in 1901. The experience reinforced her familiarity with anatomical illustration and systematic visual explanation.

### Studios and travel

- **1899:** Following her father’s death, Hilma moved with her mother to Brahegatan 52, Stockholm.
- **Around 1899:** Study travel is documented or reported to Norway, the Netherlands, Belgium, and Germany.
- **1902–1908:** She rented a studio at Hamngatan 9 in Stockholm.
- **1903:** She traveled to Germany with Anna Cassel and probably visited Italy during the same journey. The Italian portion is less firmly documented.

No imprisonment, exile, military service, overseas migration, political office, patent activity, industrial business, or corporate employment is documented.

## Spiritualism and De Fem

### Formation

Af Klint participated in the **Edelweiss Society**, a Stockholm spiritualist circle. Sources differ slightly on whether she formally joined it in 1896 or had already been associated with its members.

In 1896, or by 1897 under its settled name, she formed **De Fem**—“The Five,” also called the Friday Group—with:

- Anna Cassel
- Cornelia Cederberg
- Mathilda Nilsson
- Sigrid Hedman
- Hilma af Klint

A notebook survives from gatherings as early as 1892, indicating that their shared practice predated the formal name.

### Practice

The women met in one another’s homes, prayed, studied the New Testament, held séances, used a psychograph, practiced automatic writing and drawing, and recorded their sessions in collective notebooks. Early sheets were frequently signed collectively rather than attributed to one maker.

Their named spiritual interlocutors included **Gregor, Clemens, Esther, Amaliel, Ananda**, and, in some sources, **Georg**. They called the guiding entities *De Höga*—“The High Ones” or “High Masters.”

Two levels of evidence must be separated:

- **Documented fact:** The notebooks show that the women conducted these practices and recorded messages attributed to named spirits.
- **Spiritual claim:** That incorporeal entities actually dictated the messages and controlled their hands cannot be historically verified.

### Intellectual environment

De Fem’s practice combined Christian prayer with spiritualism, theosophy, Rosicrucianism, comparative religion, contemporary science, and ideas about evolution. Their work belonged to a broad international field that included mediumistic drawing well before modernist abstraction.

The group’s collaborative practice is crucial to current attribution debates. The early automatic drawings were plainly collective. Recent examination of Anna Cassel’s rediscovered notebooks also suggests that the collaborative model extended farther into the celebrated Temple project than twentieth-century accounts acknowledged.

## Theosophy, Anthroposophy, Science, and Artistic Thought

### Theosophy

A major source discrepancy concerns the date of af Klint’s entry into the Theosophical Society:

- Moderna Museet’s recent Swedish chronology places her membership in **1889**, the year the Swedish section formed.
- The Hilma af Klint Foundation chronology states that she joined in **May 1904**.
- Some accounts may be conflating initial association, local membership, and later formal registration.

What is secure is that theosophy deeply affected her by the early 1900s. Important concepts included spiritual evolution, reincarnation, polarities, correspondences between microcosm and macrocosm, and an underlying unity joining matter and spirit.

She read or studied ideas associated with **Helena Petrovna Blavatsky**, **Annie Besant**, Charles Webster Leadbeater, Swedish writer Viktor Rydberg, Christian mysticism, Rosicrucianism, and probably Emanuel Swedenborg. Goethe’s color theory and morphology are also relevant to her later anthroposophical practice.

### Science

Af Klint treated recent scientific discoveries as evidence that invisible forces could have visible effects. Her imagery drew upon or paralleled:

- Electromagnetic waves.
- Atomic and subatomic theories.
- X-rays.
- Evolution and embryology.
- Botanical and anatomical cross-sections.
- Polarities such as positive/negative, male/female, material/spiritual.
- Mathematical and cartographic diagramming.

Her use of scientific language was metaphysical and symbolic rather than experimental science in the modern institutional sense. No scientific invention, patent, controlled experiment, or peer-reviewed technical publication by her is documented.

### Anthroposophy

Af Klint encountered **Rudolf Steiner**, then a leader in German theosophy and later founder of Anthroposophy, during his Swedish visits. Their first substantive encounter is generally dated to 1908. She later attended his Pentecost lectures at Norrköping and repeatedly visited Dornach, Switzerland.

After 1920 she formally joined the Anthroposophical Society and spent extended periods in Dornach. Her working method changed: instead of reporting that a spirit controlled the execution, she used watercolor and allowed forms to emerge from interactions of color, following methods associated with Steiner’s color teaching.

## Chronological Life History

### 1904–1905: The commission

In 1904 De Fem’s records began predicting that Hilma would make “astral paintings” representing what is imperishable in humanity. In 1905 a recorded message from Amaliel instructed her to prepare for a large work, submit to a year of trials, and abstain from other painting.

Af Klint understood this as a commission from a higher plane. She did not describe the resulting project primarily as a bid to reform the contemporary art world.

### 1906–1908: First phase of *The Paintings for the Temple*

In November 1906 she began **Målningarna till templet**—*The Paintings for the Temple*. The cycle ultimately comprised 193 works.

The opening group, **Urkaos** or *Primordial Chaos*, contained twenty-six relatively small pictures. In 1907 came the large-format sequences, including:

- *The Ten Largest*, painted in approximately nine weeks.
- Groups addressing childhood, youth, adulthood, and old age.
- *Eros*.
- *Evolution*.
- The *WUS/Seven-Pointed Star* material.

The paintings used spirals, shells, flowers, letters, crosses, divided circles, biological forms, and geometric diagrams. Colors frequently encoded polarities: blue and yellow could represent female and male principles, though her symbol system was not perfectly stable.

They were created on paper, sometimes laid on canvas, often with tempera. Their unprecedented scale required assistants to prepare supports, mix paint, or work on the floor.

Af Klint initially described the process as mediumistic. A much-repeated translation has her say that the pictures were painted “directly through me” without preliminary drawing and with great force. Her own later records, revisions, planning, and systematic catalogs show that the process also involved sustained conscious organization.

### The widening circle and collaborative production

By the later Temple years, the working circle included more women than the original Five. Names appearing in research include:

- Anna Cassel
- Cornelia Cederberg
- Gusten Andersson
- Inga Jehander
- Thomasine Andersson
- Anna Ljungberg
- Alma Arnell
- Lotten Rönnqvist
- Emilia Giertta

The precise function of each woman varied: medium, scribe, nurse, financier, assistant, co-painter, studio colleague, or organizer.

**Current attribution dispute:** Historian Hedvig Martin argues that Cassel painted parts of the Temple cycle and that several women participated across multiple series. The evidence includes af Klint’s 1914 statement that she and Cassel would complete a series together, stylistic comparison, and notebook entries.

The foundation catalogue and most museums still attribute the cycle to Hilma af Klint. Defenders of sole or principal authorship argue that Hilma designed and directed the project and that ordinary studio assistance does not invalidate authorship. The evidence firmly establishes collaboration; it does not yet produce universal agreement about which individual painted every surface.

### 1908: Rudolf Steiner and interruption

Af Klint showed Steiner her paintings when he visited Stockholm in 1908. The aftermath has accumulated contradictory stories.

What is reasonably secure:

- Steiner saw a substantial body of her esoteric work.
- He did not give the unqualified endorsement for which she hoped.
- He was skeptical of passive mediumship.
- Af Klint entered a hiatus in the Temple project and devoted increasing time to caring for her visually impaired mother.
- She continued to seek Steiner’s interpretation and institutional support.

What remains disputed:

- Popular accounts say Steiner declared that no one would understand the pictures for fifty years or ordered her not to exhibit them. Documentary support for that exact statement is uncertain.
- Some writers describe his reaction as outright rejection; others argue that he offered criticism, discussed the images, or later assisted attempts to place them before anthroposophical audiences.
- The four-year interruption cannot be attributed to Steiner alone; care responsibilities, the reorganization of De Fem, and af Klint’s own reassessment also mattered.

### 1908–1912: Caregiving and reorganization

Af Klint left or reduced work at her Hamngatan studio and used a room at Brahegatan while caring for her mother. She attended the Theosophical Society’s Pentecost conference in Norrköping, where Steiner lectured.

She did not simply cease artistic activity. She cataloged, studied, planned, maintained notebooks, and reoriented the project. Her spiritual circle also changed.

### 1912–1915: Completion of the Temple cycle

Af Klint resumed the Temple paintings in 1912 with a more deliberate rather than wholly automatic method. Major groups included:

- **US Series** (1912).
- **The Tree of Knowledge** (approximately 1913–1915).
- **The Swan** (1914–1915).
- **The Dove** (1915).
- **The Altarpieces** (1915).

The First World War began while she was making *The Swan*. The sequence moves between black-and-white opposition, mirrored forms, increasingly geometric constructions, and resolution or union. *The Dove* introduced Christian and cosmological imagery, including sacrifice, blood, stars, and geometric systems.

The three monumental *Altarpieces* completed the Temple cycle in 1915. They visualize ascent and descent between material and spiritual realms, culminating in a golden disk.

That year af Klint left the Theosophical Society. She rented a studio at Ynglingagatan 21 in Stockholm.

### 1916–1917: *Parsifal*, *The Atom*, and writing

In 1916 she produced the **Parsifal Series**, 144 small works, chiefly watercolor. The spelling varies in the archive and publications between *Parsifal* and *Parcifal*.

In 1917 she created **The Atom Series**, joining contemporary atomic discourse to a spiritual model of matter.

She dictated an extensive work on the life of the soul to **Anna Ljungberg**. The typescript reportedly reached 2,058 pages. It was not conventionally published during her lifetime.

### 1917–1918: Munsö studio

A purpose-built studio was erected at Furuheim on Munsö on land made available by the Giertta family’s Bona fideicommiss estate. Construction began in 1916 or 1917 and was completed in 1917–1918. Anna Cassel and the women’s circle provided much of the financing.

In 1918 Hilma moved there with her mother and Thomasine Andersson, her mother’s nurse and later Hilma’s long-term household companion. The building provided space for the vast paintings but was difficult and expensive to heat and maintain.

### 1919–1920: Nature studies and world religions

Af Klint undertook a large botanical-esoteric project often called **Blumen, Moose und Flechten** or *Flowers, Mosses and Lichens*. She drew and painted plants while assigning spiritual or psychological properties to them. The unfinished project fused precise natural observation with symbolic classification.

On 1 January 1920 she began a series addressing major world religions. Her mother died on 5 January.

In the autumn Hilma and Thomasine Andersson traveled to Dornach, Switzerland. Hilma met Steiner again, entered the Anthroposophical Society, and sought a setting in which her work could be interpreted and eventually displayed.

### 1921–1930: Dornach years

Af Klint repeatedly traveled between Sweden and Dornach. She attended Steiner’s lectures and studied anthroposophical literature. After her initial Dornach visit, she stopped painting for roughly a year.

Her subsequent watercolor practice emphasized forms emerging from washes of color. Important late groups include:

- *On the Viewing of Flowers and Trees* (1922).
- Watercolors concerning elemental beings and spiritual forces.
- Continued botanical and atmospheric studies.

She prepared ten large blue-covered albums containing small painted copies or photographs of the Temple works. These **Blue Books** were portable catalogs intended to explain and circulate a body of work too large to move easily.

In 1925 Steiner died. No dated paintings are known from approximately 1925–1929, although af Klint continued reading, organizing, writing, traveling, and promoting the work.

### Residences and travel, 1926–1934

- **1926:** Af Klint and Thomasine Andersson settled at Skolgatan 17 in Uppsala.
- **1927:** She deposited botanical, moss, and lichen studies with the natural-science archive at the Goetheanum in Dornach.
- **1928:** She traveled by sea to London and exhibited the *Altarpieces* at the **World Conference on Spiritual Science and Its Practical Applications**, organized by the English Anthroposophical Society.
- **1931:** She moved to Helsingborg and occupied two addresses there through 1934.
- **1930–1931:** She drew or recorded plans for a spiral temple, including a possible observatory.
- **1931–1932:** She considered the island of Ven in the Öresund as a location for a temple to house the pictures. Nothing was built.
- **1934–1944:** She lived at three different addresses in Lund.
- **1944:** She moved to the home of her cousin Hedvig af Klint at Ösby in Djursholm.

### The 1928 London exhibition

This event materially revises the old claim that af Klint never exhibited an abstract work in her lifetime.

**Documented fact:** At least some esoteric works, including the *Altarpieces*, were displayed in London in 1928.

**Qualification:** This was an anthroposophical spiritual-science conference, not a conventional modern-art gallery exhibition. The event does show that she wanted selected audiences to encounter the work.

### 1930s: Editing, prediction, and legacy planning

Af Klint systematically reorganized her manuscripts and pictures. Family testimony and scholarship indicate that she removed, rewrote, or destroyed autobiographical passages around 1930, making reconstruction of her intimate life unusually difficult.

In 1932 she recorded that the work should not be shown until at least twenty years after her death. The statement’s wording and legal status require care:

- **Documented fact:** A twenty-year instruction or wish occurs in a notebook.
- **Documented fact:** Her formal will left the artistic estate to Erik af Klint.
- **Correction of myth:** Current research reports that the formal will itself did **not** contain the famous twenty-year exhibition ban.
- **Unresolved question:** Whether the notebook instruction applied to every esoteric work, public exhibition generally, or a particular audience and phase of the project.

She painted two prophetic-looking maps in 1932, later titled or interpreted as **The Blitz** and **The Fight in the Mediterranean**. Admirers claim they foretold the Second World War.

The evidentiary position is:

- The pictures predate the London Blitz and wartime Mediterranean campaigns.
- European war fears, aerial warfare, and Mediterranean tensions already existed.
- Titles, dating, and retrospective interpretation need to be examined work by work.
- No evidence establishes supernatural foreknowledge.

### Final years and death

Anna Cassel died in 1937. Thomasine Andersson died in 1940. These deaths removed two of Hilma’s closest and longest-standing companions.

On 9 October 1944 af Klint made her last notebook entry, ending with a sentence commonly translated as: “You have mystery service before you and will soon realize what is required of you.”

She died on 21 October 1944 after an accident. Sources describe it variously as a streetcar or tram accident, a fall while leaving a tram, or injuries following a traffic mishap. Recent biographies usually say that she was struck or seriously injured in connection with a tram and died afterward. The exact accident report has not been independently examined for this dossier, so the narrowest responsible formulation is:

- **Reported fact:** Af Klint suffered an accident involving or occurring around a tram.
- **Documented outcome:** She died shortly afterward, aged eighty-one.
- **Cause-of-death uncertainty:** The precise medical cause and sequence are not consistently stated in accessible institutional sources.

She was buried with her parents in the naval cemetery at Djurgården. The family stone bears her father’s name and does not visibly identify her.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Anna Cassel

Cassel was af Klint’s academy classmate, lifelong friend, co-founder of De Fem, financial supporter, spiritual collaborator, and probable co-producer of parts of the esoteric oeuvre. Her family wealth helped support the Munsö studio.

Their relationship was close but not free from tension. Recent reporting refers to later financial strain. Newly found Cassel notebooks have transformed scholarship by revealing the independence of Cassel’s spiritual art and the collective character of the women’s work.

Some biographical treatments suggest an intimate or romantic dimension. Surviving evidence establishes emotional closeness and sustained partnership but does not conclusively define their relationship in modern sexual categories.

### Thomasine Andersson

Andersson served as Mathilda af Klint’s nurse and then lived and traveled with Hilma for many years. She accompanied Hilma to Dornach in 1920 and shared the Uppsala household. Some modern writers identify her as Hilma’s companion or partner.

- **Documented fact:** Long-term co-residence, travel, caregiving, and companionship.
- **Unresolved:** Whether the relationship was sexual or romantic. Af Klint’s deliberate removal of personal material limits certainty.

### Rudolf Steiner

Steiner was af Klint’s most important living religious-intellectual authority after 1908. She showed him paintings, attended lectures, traveled to his Goetheanum center, and eventually joined Anthroposophy.

Their relationship was asymmetrical: af Klint sought recognition, interpretation, and practical help; Steiner’s responses were mixed and have been mythologized. There is no strong evidence of a romantic relationship, despite dramatizations describing him as a “soulmate.”

### The women of De Fem and the wider circle

Sigrid Hedman, Cornelia Cederberg, Mathilda Nilsson, Anna Ljungberg, Inga Jehander, Gusten Andersson, Emilia Giertta, Alma Arnell, and Lotten Rönnqvist formed overlapping spiritual, practical, and artistic networks. Their labor ranged from collective automatic drawing to transcription, financing, prayer, painting assistance, studio sharing, and care.

### Erik af Klint

Hilma’s nephew became sole principal heir to the artistic estate. He preserved it despite storage costs and the absence of a commercial market, attempted to interest Moderna Museet, and established the foundation that still owns almost all major esoteric works.

## Employment, Institutions, Property, and Finances

### Income and work

Af Klint supported herself at least partly through professional portraiture, landscape painting, and illustration. She was not economically dependent on sales of the esoteric work, which remained largely outside the art market.

Documented institutional and professional engagements included:

- Exhibitions with the Swedish Association for Art.
- Work for the Veterinary Institute.
- Membership in the Theosophical and later Anthroposophical societies.
- Membership in the Association of Swedish Women Artists.
- Service as that association’s secretary until 22 April 1911.
- Participation in the 1928 London anthroposophical conference.

### Studios and property

- Academy-associated studio near Kungsträdgården after 1887.
- Hamngatan 9, 1902–1908.
- Large-work space associated with Hamngatan 5 in 1907.
- Brahegatan workspace during care of her mother.
- Ynglingagatan 21 from 1915.
- Purpose-built Furuheim studio on leased Munsö land, financed partly by Anna Cassel and friends.

Af Klint did not establish a company or commercial art enterprise. No investment portfolio, debts, bankruptcy, or personal fortune of reliable amount has been documented in the sources consulted.

### Estate structure

Af Klint left her artistic estate to Erik af Klint. He formed **Stiftelsen Hilma af Klints Verk** in January 1972. Its statutes directed the board to safeguard the work and make it available to people seeking spiritual knowledge or able to help fulfill its intended mission. The statutes also gave anthroposophical representatives an institutional role and required family representation in leadership.

Because the foundation retains most major works and its statutes restrict sale, af Klint remains unusually absent from the conventional auction market. Private works and duplicate series do exist outside the foundation.

## Artistic Development

### Naturalism and observation

The early landscapes and portraits demonstrate professional academic training. They supplied technical competence in contour, botanical specificity, tone, and the construction of pictorial space.

### Automatic drawing

De Fem’s automatic drawings introduced nonrepresentational lines and collective authorship. They are a bridge between séance practice and the later Temple project but should not automatically be classified as modernist abstraction; their makers treated them as spiritual communications.

### Temple period

The Temple cycle combines:

- Organic and geometric form.
- Symbolic alphabets.
- Christian, theosophical, and evolutionary narratives.
- Male/female polarity.
- Microcosm and macrocosm.
- Large-scale serial installation.
- Movement from division toward union.

Af Klint’s paintings never consistently abandoned representation. Snails, lilies, roses, swans, doves, crosses, human bodies, words, and diagrams coexist with nonobjective forms.

### Post-mediumistic period

After 1912 she increasingly described herself as consciously responsible rather than passively controlled. After 1920 anthroposophical watercolor practice replaced many hard-edged geometries.

### Temple architecture

Af Klint imagined the pictures in a spiral or ascending temple, sometimes with an observatory. The project changed across decades and never received a definitive architectural realization. The resemblance between her spiral concept and Frank Lloyd Wright’s later Guggenheim Museum has generated symbolic speculation, but no documentary evidence establishes that Wright saw her plans.

## Religious, Philosophical, and Ideological Position

Af Klint’s worldview combined elements that do not fit neatly into one creed:

- Lutheran or broadly Christian formation.
- Spiritualism and séance practice.
- Theosophical reincarnation and spiritual evolution.
- Rosicrucian symbolism.
- Anthroposophy.
- Comparative study of Christianity, Judaism, Islam, Buddhism, and other religions.
- Scientific and mathematical imagery.
- A belief that apparent opposites would ultimately be reconciled.

She was not a conventional theologian or systematic philosopher. Her notebooks are associative, repetitive, coded, and frequently circular. She built dictionaries explaining symbols and abbreviations but used them inconsistently.

### Gender and sexuality in the notebooks

Recent scholarship reports passages concerning:

- The “twin soul.”
- Male and female principles within one being.
- Gender duality.
- Af Klint’s feeling that she was, in some sense, a man in a woman’s body.

These passages have encouraged queer and transgender readings. They are genuine subjects for historical inquiry, but no source establishes that af Klint adopted modern identity terms. Describing her categorically as lesbian, bisexual, transgender, or nonbinary exceeds the documentary evidence. Describing her as a modern queer icon is accurate as a fact about reception.

### Politics

No sustained party membership, electoral activity, public office, revolutionary association, or political campaign by af Klint has been documented. Her work engaged world war, social transformation, sex and gender relations, and hopes for humanity’s spiritual evolution, but it was not organized around a conventional political program.

---

## Successes, Failures, and Uncompleted Projects

### Successes during life

- Completed a demanding academy education with honors.
- Maintained a professional conventional-painting career.
- Exhibited publicly in numerous Swedish group exhibitions.
- Produced an exceptionally large, systematic esoteric oeuvre.
- Created the 193-work Temple cycle.
- Preserved extensive documentation and portable catalogs.
- Exhibited esoteric paintings in Stockholm theosophical contexts and London in 1928.
- Secured an heir willing to preserve the archive.

### Failures and frustrations

- The physical temple was never built.
- Steiner never supplied the unequivocal validation she sought.
- Attempts to obtain broader anthroposophical exhibition or institutional support had limited success.
- The work did not enter mainstream art history during her lifetime.
- Parts of her botanical and encyclopedic projects remained unfinished.
- Her own editing and destruction of personal passages made her biography harder to recover.
- Her estate had no permanent public museum at her death and still lacks a dedicated comprehensive home.

## Health, Bereavement, and Psychological Questions

Documented losses included Hermina in 1880, her father in 1898, her mother in 1920, Steiner in 1925, Anna Cassel in 1937, and Thomasine Andersson in 1940.

No reliable evidence of addiction, psychiatric hospitalization, formal insanity adjudication, or chronic criminal incapacity has been found. Contemporary and later observers sometimes called her insane, a witch, deluded, or merely a medium. Those labels largely reflect reactions to spiritualism rather than a documented clinical diagnosis.

Her reports of voices and spiritual control can be interpreted religiously, psychologically, culturally, or clinically. Historical evidence proves that she reported and systematized such experiences; it cannot retrospectively establish a psychiatric diagnosis or the supernatural source she claimed.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### Was she “the first abstract artist”?

**Popular claim:** Af Klint invented abstract painting in 1906 before Kandinsky, Malevich, Mondrian, or Kupka.

**Evidence supporting a qualified claim:**

- She produced radically nonrepresentational pictures in 1906–1907.
- These predate the canonical abstract works of several famous modernists.
- She was an academically trained professional artist rather than an untrained occasional medium.

**Evidence against the absolute formulation:**

- Nonfigurative art existed in many cultures long before European modernism.
- Spiritualist **Georgiana Houghton** made abstract spirit drawings in the 1860s and exhibited them in 1871.
- Annie Besant and C. W. Leadbeater published abstract color diagrams in *Thought-Forms* in 1905.
- Af Klint did not initially present her work as a formalist revolt against representation or participate in the avant-garde debate that defined modernist abstraction.
- Much of the Temple cycle incorporates words, symbols, plants, animals, human figures, and recognizable religious imagery.
- “First” depends on whether the category means earliest nonrepresentational image, earliest abstract easel painting, earliest trained artist, or earliest participant in modernism.

**Conclusion:** It is documented that af Klint made exceptionally early large-scale nonobjective paintings. The claim that she single-handedly “invented abstraction” is an interpretive and promotional simplification.

### Did she conceal all abstract work during her lifetime?

**Myth:** Nobody saw the work until decades after her death.

**Evidence against it:**

- Friends, collaborators, and spiritual circles saw the work.
- Reproductions circulated through the Blue Books.
- Seventeen spiritual pictures were reportedly shown at a Theosophical Society exhibition in Stockholm.
- The *Altarpieces* were exhibited in London in 1928.

The accurate formulation is that the work remained outside broad mainstream art-world circulation, not that it was absolutely secret.

### Did her will impose a twenty-year embargo?

**Myth:** Her legal will ordered that no painting be shown until twenty years after her death.

**Current documentary distinction:**

- A 1932 notebook records a wish that the work not be shown until at least twenty years after her death.
- Recent researchers state that the formal will contains no such clause.
- The estate nevertheless followed the restriction in practice.

The “twenty-year will” is therefore based on a real instruction but repeatedly assigned to the wrong document.

### Was Steiner responsible for the embargo?

The story that Steiner rejected the work and ordered it hidden for fifty years is widely repeated. Surviving evidence is insufficient for that exact wording. His skepticism toward passive mediumship is documented, and his cool response mattered greatly to af Klint. The leap from those facts to a binding fifty-year command is disputed.

### Authorship and Anna Cassel

**Documented facts:**

- Cassel helped produce De Fem’s collective drawings.
- She financed and practically supported af Klint.
- Af Klint wrote of the two women completing a series together.
- Cassel made an independent body of esoteric art.
- Other women assisted in the Temple undertaking.

**Hedvig Martin’s scholarly claim:** Several paintings traditionally cataloged solely under af Klint’s name were partly or wholly painted by Cassel, with further contributions from other women.

**Counterposition:** Af Klint remained the conceptual director and chief painter; collaborators’ labor resembles a studio practice rather than equal authorship.

**Status:** Active attribution dispute. No finding of forgery or fraud has been made. The issue concerns historical cataloging and the suppression or under-recognition of collective labor.

### Foundation governance litigation

Recent conflict concerns the foundation, not misconduct by Hilma during her life.

Family chairman **Erik af Klint**, other relatives, and anthroposophical board members disputed:

- Who lawfully constituted and represented the board.
- Whether works could be sold.
- Whether museum exhibitions complied with the statutes.
- Whether the work should be directed chiefly toward “spiritual seekers.”
- Commercial licensing, NFTs, virtual reality, publication contracts, and conflicts of interest.
- A proposed relationship with dealer David Zwirner.

Swedish litigation included proceedings over the right of Juhani Selvani, Katarina Kaila De Voto, and Anders Kumlander to act as board members. SVT reported that Erik af Klint could not unilaterally remove the other board.

The foundation later announced a settlement ending the proceedings and an agreement that it would continue to make the works known through exhibitions.

### Proposed sales and market value

The foundation’s major collection has been valued journalistically in the hundreds of millions of dollars, but it is not an ordinary liquid market valuation: most works cannot readily be sold, and very few comparable pictures have been auctioned.

In 2024 the foundation communicated with David Zwirner concerning possible sales or representation. Opposition argued that dispersal would violate the estate’s integrity and spiritual purpose. In January 2026 Swedish reporting stated that the county administrative authority advised the foundation to avoid sales where possible, reduce administrative expenses first, and keep the work together. The foundation said it then had no current sale plan.

### The *Tree of Knowledge* transaction

One of two surviving *Tree of Knowledge* sets had been outside the foundation in an anthroposophical collection in Switzerland. Anders Kumlander acquired it in 2020. It appeared at David Zwirner in New York in 2021 and was sold to the Glenstone Museum.

Representatives of the former Swiss owner later said they had believed the purchase served an anthroposophical institutional purpose and expressed betrayal. Kumlander denied misrepresenting his role. No court finding of fraud concerning the transaction was identified in the sources consulted.

### NFTs

A project with Acute Art and Stolpe Publishing created NFT editions based on af Klint’s public-domain works. Family opponents said the project commercialized and fragmented a spiritual ensemble against her intentions. Organizers argued that the project expanded access and related to the catalogue raisonné.

The underlying images had entered the public domain after the expiry of copyright protection, limiting the family’s intellectual-property control. The dispute was ethical and governance-related rather than a demonstrated infringement finding.

### Broader anthroposophical foundation scrutiny

Swedish authorities criticized governance and conflict-of-interest procedures in other anthroposophical foundations associated with some of the same individuals. Those findings should not automatically be imputed to the Hilma af Klint Foundation or to Hilma herself. They are relevant only as background to contemporary governance concerns.

### Crimes and accusations

No evidence was found that Hilma af Klint was arrested, imprisoned, indicted, convicted, sued, accused of financial fraud, involved in money laundering, connected to an intelligence service, or participated in political violence.

Claims that she consciously deceived the public by inventing spirit messages are speculative. Her decades-long private practice and lack of direct commercial benefit weigh against a simple fraud-for-profit explanation, while the supernatural content remains unverifiable.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### “The forgotten woman who invented modern art”

- **Story:** A lone woman invented abstraction before famous men, was dismissed as mad, hid everything for twenty years, and was rediscovered intact.
- **Circulation:** Especially prominent after the 2013 Moderna Museet retrospective, the 2018 Guggenheim exhibition, the 2019 documentary, and Lasse Hallström’s 2022 biopic.
- **Evidence for:** Early dates, later neglect, male-dominated canonical art history, intact estate, exceptional visual innovation.
- **Evidence against:** She was professionally exhibited; she worked collaboratively; earlier nonobjective spiritualist art existed; selected esoteric works were shown during her life.
- **Effect:** It transformed her into a symbol of women erased from art history and drove enormous exhibition attendance.

### “A completely isolated genius”

- **Story:** Af Klint created the Temple works alone in total secrecy.
- **Evidence against:** De Fem, Anna Cassel, the Thirteen, studio assistants, financial support, theosophical networks, and documented viewings.
- **Effect:** The story magnified heroic individuality while obscuring the women’s collaborative network.

### “Steiner silenced her”

- **Story:** Rudolf Steiner rejected the pictures and commanded that they be hidden for fifty years.
- **Evidence:** His reservations about mediumship and her disappointment are credible; the precise command is inadequately documented.
- **Effect:** It makes Steiner the male authority who suppressed a woman visionary, a compelling narrative used in films and popular accounts.

### “Her will sealed the paintings”

- **Story:** The formal will imposed a twenty-year ban.
- **Evidence:** The twenty-year direction is in a notebook, not apparently the will.
- **Effect:** It created the “paintings for the future” legend and made the posthumous revelation seem deliberately timed.

### Prophecy of the Second World War

- **Story:** Af Klint foresaw the London Blitz and Mediterranean warfare in 1932.
- **Evidence for:** Relevant pictures predate the events and were later read as maps of them.
- **Evidence against:** Europe was already marked by war anxiety; imagery and titles allow retrospective interpretation; no controlled prediction was publicly recorded and verified beforehand.
- **Status:** Unresolved symbolic interpretation, not established prophecy.

### Guggenheim prophecy

- **Story:** Her spiral temple predicted the architecture of the Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum.
- **Evidence for:** Striking formal resemblance and the later record-breaking Guggenheim exhibition.
- **Evidence against:** No documentary chain connects her plans to Frank Lloyd Wright.
- **Effect:** A powerful posthumous symbolic association that made the 2018 installation appear destined.

### Mystic, witch, prophet, feminist, and queer icon

Modern culture alternately presents af Klint as witch, saint, medium, scientist, feminist, queer ancestor, prophetic environmentalist, or founder of modernism. Each label selects genuine elements but can flatten contradictions.

- “Medium” is historically accurate as a role she claimed.
- “Witch” is a later cultural label, not her recorded self-description.
- “Feminist” fits her independent professional life and women-centered network, but no clear organized feminist program by her is established.
- “Queer icon” accurately describes reception; her precise sexuality is undocumented.
- “Scientist” describes her aspirations and methods metaphorically; she did not hold a scientific post beyond illustration work.
- “Prophet” belongs to belief and reception, not independently demonstrated fact.

## Works by Hilma af Klint

Af Klint did not publish a conventional authored monograph during her lifetime. Her primary works comprise paintings, drawings, diagrams, sketchbooks, notebooks, dictionaries, catalogs, and dictated texts.

### Principal artistic cycles

Dates and titles follow standard modern cataloging; some English titles are translations or later catalog conventions.

- **De Fem spiritualist drawings** (1890s–1908), collectively made and signed.
- **Målningarna till templet / The Paintings for the Temple** (1906–1915), 193 works:
  - *Urkaos / Primordial Chaos* (1906–1907)
  - *WUS / The Seven-Pointed Star*
  - *De tio största / The Ten Largest* (1907)
  - *Eros* (1907)
  - *Evolution* (1908)
  - *US Series* (1912)
  - *Kunskapens träd / Tree of Knowledge* (c. 1913–1915)
  - *Svanen / The Swan* (1914–1915)
  - *Duvan / The Dove* (1915)
  - *Altarbilder / Altarpieces* (1915)
- **Parcifal/Parsifal Series** (1916), 144 watercolors.
- **Atomen / The Atom Series** (1917).
- **Geometric Series and Other Works** (principally 1917–1920).
- **Series concerning world religions** (1920).
- **Blumen, Moose und Flechten / Flowers, Mosses and Lichens** (1919–1920; unfinished).
- **On the Viewing of Flowers and Trees** (1922).
- **Late watercolors** (1922–1941).
- **The Blitz** and **The Fight in the Mediterranean** (1932, conventional titles associated with prophetic readings).
- **Naturalistic landscapes, portraits, botanical studies, and miscellaneous works** (approximately 1877–1941).

### Manuscripts, catalogs, and writings

- **De Fem notebooks** (from the 1890s): collective séance records and automatic drawings. Authorship is shared and should not be assigned solely to af Klint.
- **Studier över själslivet / Studies of the Life of the Soul** (dictated 1917 to Anna Ljungberg; approximately 2,058 typed pages): unpublished lifetime manuscript.
- **The Blue Books** (assembled primarily in the 1910s–1920s): ten hand-painted and annotated catalogs of the Temple pictures.
- **Letters and Words Pertaining to Works by Hilma af Klint** (compiled c. 1930s): symbolic lexicon, posthumously translated.
- More than 125 additional notebooks containing spiritual messages, working instructions, diaries, indexes, architectural plans, glossaries, and revisions.
- **Hilma af Klint: Notes and Methods** (2018): posthumous edited selection, edited by Christine Burgin, with introduction and commentary by Iris Müller-Westermann. It is not a book designed or published by af Klint herself.
- **Catalogue Raisonné**, seven volumes (2020–2022): posthumous institutional catalog, not an authored lifetime publication:
  1. *Spiritualistic Drawings 1895–1910*
  2. *The Paintings for the Temple 1906–1915*
  3. *The Blue Books*
  4. *Parsifal and the Atom 1916–1917*
  5. *Geometric Series and Other Works 1917–1920*
  6. *Late Watercolours 1922–1941*
  7. *Landscapes, Portraits and Miscellaneous Works 1877–1941*

### Scientific illustration

- Illustrations, with Anna Cassel, for John Vennerholm, *Grunddragen af hästens operativa speciella kirurgi* (1901). The text was Vennerholm’s; af Klint and Cassel were illustrators, not co-authors of the surgical treatise.

No patents, inventions, recorded compositions, films, sound recordings, manifestos published under a pseudonym, or authenticated ghostwritten books by af Klint have been identified.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### 1944–1972: Preservation without recognition

Erik af Klint inherited the estate. The large pictures were costly and difficult to store. He preserved them rather than breaking up or selling the collection.

Accounts differ over when and how Moderna Museet first rejected or declined the archive. A frequently repeated story places an unsuccessful approach around 1970. Details about whether museum staff refused even to view the pictures require corroboration from museum records.

Erik created the foundation in 1972, eight years after the commonly cited twenty-year embargo had expired.

### 1986–1999: Entry into art history

The major public breakthrough came through **The Spiritual in Art: Abstract Painting 1890–1985**, curated by Maurice Tuchman at the Los Angeles County Museum of Art in 1986.

Further milestones:

- **1988:** *Hilma af Klints hemliga bilder / The Secret Pictures of Hilma af Klint*, Nordic Arts Centre, Helsinki, later traveling to P.S.1 in New York, Reykjavík, and Moss.
- **1989:** *Hilma af Klint—Occult Painter and Abstract Pioneer*, Moderna Museet and touring venues.
- **1999:** Liljevalchs Konsthall displayed the complete 193-work Temple cycle for the first time.

### 2000–2012: Specialist recognition

Exhibitions and scholarship increasingly situated her within spiritualism, theosophy, women’s art history, and abstraction. Her work remained much better known among specialists than the public.

### 2013: International retrospective

Moderna Museet’s *Hilma af Klint—A Pioneer of Abstraction*, curated by Iris Müller-Westermann with Jo Widoff, traveled across Europe and attracted more than one million visitors. The museum helped catalog, conserve, frame, and digitize previously inaccessible portions of the estate.

The subtitle “A Pioneer of Abstraction” was deliberately provocative and became central to her public identity.

### 2018–2019: Guggenheim phenomenon

*Hilma af Klint: Paintings for the Future* opened at New York’s Guggenheim on 12 October 2018 and closed 23 April 2019. It became the museum’s best-attended exhibition to that date, drawing more than 600,000 visitors.

The spiral building produced an unusually persuasive visual dialogue with af Klint’s unbuilt temple. The exhibition made her a mass-cultural figure and generated merchandise, fashion collaborations, reproductions, and intense online circulation.

### 2020–present

Subsequent exhibitions have reframed her as artist, researcher, medium, naturalist, ecological thinker, and member of a collaborative women’s network. The Tate Modern paired her with Piet Mondrian in *Forms of Life* in 2023. MoMA’s 2025 *What Stands Behind the Flowers* foregrounded the botanical work.

Her current reputation has several simultaneous forms:

- Canonical early abstractionist.
- Spiritualist and occult artist.
- Feminist recovery figure.
- Queer cultural icon.
- Model for contemporary installation and serial practice.
- Commercial visual brand.
- Subject of disputes over religious stewardship, authorship, access, and market control.

The catalogue raisonné and digitized notebooks have moved the field away from a simple rediscovery narrative toward questions of collaboration, manuscript editing, institutional power, and the distinction between modernist abstraction and mediumistic image-making.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere contemporaneity is excluded.

### Helena Petrovna Blavatsky — documented intellectual influence

Af Klint studied theosophy, whose central modern texts included Blavatsky’s *The Secret Doctrine* (1888). Moderna Museet explicitly places the book in the intellectual environment of af Klint’s 1889 theosophical involvement. No personal meeting was possible or documented: Blavatsky died in 1891.

### Annie Besant — documented intellectual and institutional connection; no meeting established

Besant was a major Theosophical Society leader, and ideas and diagrams associated with Besant and C. W. Leadbeater’s *Thought-Forms* formed part of the visual-esoteric field in which af Klint worked. Direct personal correspondence or a meeting has not been established in the consulted sources.

### Rudolf Steiner — documented meeting, teacher-authority relationship, and institutional affiliation

Af Klint met Steiner in Stockholm in 1908, attended his lectures in Sweden, met him again at Dornach in 1920, studied his teachings, and joined the Anthroposophical Society. She repeatedly sought his assessment and help. His exact response to the paintings remains disputed.

### Emanuel Swedenborg — reported intellectual background, not a personal connection

Swedenborg’s spiritual cosmology was influential in European and Swedish spiritualism and appears among the earlier thinkers cited in accounts of af Klint’s religious environment. No evidence shows a direct family or documentary link beyond intellectual reception; he died long before her birth.

### Johann Wolfgang von Goethe — documented later intellectual influence

Through Steiner and anthroposophical color practice, af Klint engaged Goethean ideas of color and natural morphology. No personal connection was possible.

### Jacob Boehme — reported intellectual background

Boehme is named among Christian theosophical and mystical precursors relevant to the tradition studied by af Klint and her circles. Direct evidence of a particular annotated edition or sustained textual commentary by her was not established here.

### Robert Fludd — reported intellectual background

Fludd’s cosmological diagrams and Rosicrucian associations belong to the visual-intellectual genealogy identified by curators around af Klint. This is an influence-context connection, not proof of direct copying.

### William Blake — reported comparative or intellectual association

Curatorial accounts place Blake among visionary predecessors familiar to the wider spiritualist and esoteric environment. The sources consulted do not establish that af Klint owned or directly studied a specific Blake work.

### Wassily Kandinsky — documented art-historical comparison; no personal relationship established

Af Klint’s 1906 nonobjective work predates Kandinsky’s canonical abstractions. Both died in 1944. No meeting, correspondence, collaboration, or demonstrated direct influence in either direction was found. Claims that they knowingly exhibited together require venue-specific proof and should not be inferred merely from overlapping theosophical networks.

### Piet Mondrian — documented posthumous pairing; no lifetime relationship established

Af Klint and Mondrian were both shaped by theosophical ideas and were paired in Tate Modern’s 2023 exhibition *Forms of Life*. No meeting or correspondence has been established.

### Carl Gustav Jung — comparative association only

Modern writers compare af Klint’s images and concealed archive with Jung’s *Red Book*. No documentary evidence of meeting, correspondence, or direct influence was found.

### Frank Lloyd Wright — rumor or myth

Af Klint’s spiral temple plans resemble the later Guggenheim Museum, designed by Wright. No evidence establishes transmission of her plans to Wright.

### Other roster names

No well-supported direct biographical crossing was identified with the remainder of the atlas roster. The scarcity of connections is itself significant: af Klint’s documented life centered on Swedish women’s artistic networks, Scandinavian theosophy, Steiner’s anthroposophical movement, and repeated travel to Dornach rather than the mainstream European avant-garde.

- **2023:** Tate Modern pairs af Klint with Mondrian; *Anna Cassel: The Saga of the Rose* intensifies authorship debate.
- **2024–2026:** Foundation governance, sale, NFT, and exhibition disputes receive litigation, regulatory attention, and international reporting.
- **2025:** MoMA presents *What Stands Behind the Flowers*.
- **2026:** Foundation announces settlement of its internal legal dispute; Swedish regulator advises keeping the collection intact and avoiding sales where possible.
