## Works by Leonardo di ser Piero da Vinci

### Paintings and painted surfaces

- **c. 1470–1475:** *Tobias and the Angel*. Workshop painting by Andrea del Verrocchio; Leonardo is often credited with the fish and dog, but the division of hands remains disputed. [Zöllner–Nathan; National Gallery]
- **c. 1472–1475:** *The Baptism of Christ*. Verrocchio workshop panel on which Leonardo painted at least the left-hand angel and parts of the landscape; it is a collaborative obra, not wholly his painting. [Zöllner–Nathan; Uffizi]
- **c. 1472–1476:** *The Annunciation*. Panel generally accepted as an early Leonardo, although its attribution and the extent of workshop participation were formerly disputed. [Zöllner–Nathan; Uffizi]
- **c. 1473–1478:** *Madonna of the Carnation*. Panel usually attributed to Leonardo but not unanimously accepted; its execution may include workshop participation. [Zöllner–Nathan; Alte Pinakothek]
- **c. 1474–1478:** *Ginevra de’ Benci*. Portrait panel, now cut down at the bottom; the reverse bears an emblematic painted device. [Zöllner–Nathan; National Gallery of Art]
- **c. 1475–1480:** *Madonna and Child with a Pomegranate (Dreyfus Madonna)*. Small panel variously assigned to Leonardo, Lorenzo di Credi, or Verrocchio’s workshop; it resists inclusion as an autograph obra. [Zöllner–Nathan; National Gallery of Art]
- **c. 1478–1481:** *Benois Madonna*. Intimate devotional panel generally accepted as Leonardo’s, though unfinished or altered passages complicate its status. [Zöllner–Nathan; Hermitage]
- **1478:** *Madonna and Child altarpiece for the chapel of San Bernardo, Palazzo della Signoria*. Commission documented by payment to Leonardo, but no securely identifiable painting survives; the project may never have been completed. [Zöllner–Nathan]
- **c. 1480–1482:** *Saint Jerome in the Wilderness*. Unfinished panel whose exposed underdrawing and modeled passages make the process of making part of the surviving obra. [Zöllner–Nathan; Vatican Museums]
- **1481–1482:** *The Adoration of the Magi*. Monumental unfinished altarpiece abandoned when Leonardo left Florence; it is simultaneously painting, underdrawing, and incomplete project. [Zöllner–Nathan; Uffizi]
- **1483–c. 1486:** *The Virgin of the Rocks* (Louvre version). Altarpiece panel made for the Confraternity of the Immaculate Conception in Milan, with Leonardo’s collaborators responsible for associated side panels. [Zöllner–Nathan; Louvre]
- **c. 1483–1490:** *Portrait of a Musician*. Unfinished portrait panel generally associated with Leonardo, although both attribution and the sitter’s identity have been debated. [Zöllner–Nathan; Ambrosiana]
- **c. 1489–1491:** *Lady with an Ermine*. Portrait generally identified as Cecilia Gallerani; later repainting altered its background and other details. [Zöllner–Nathan; National Museum in Kraków]
- **c. 1490–1497:** *La Belle Ferronnière*. Portrait panel generally accepted as Leonardo’s, although the sitter and precise date remain uncertain. [Zöllner–Nathan; Louvre]
- **1495–1498:** *The Last Supper*. Experimental wall painting in the refectory of Santa Maria delle Grazie, Milan; Leonardo’s oil-and-tempera technique deteriorated rapidly, so the surviving manifestation includes extensive damage and conservation history. [Zöllner–Nathan; Cenacolo Vinciano]
- **1496–1498:** *Sala delle Asse decoration*. Painted and drawn arboreal canopy for the Castello Sforzesco, conceived under Leonardo and executed with assistants; restoration has revealed unfinished monochrome work as well as later intervention. [Zöllner–Nathan; Castello Sforzesco]
- **c. 1491/1492–1508:** *The Virgin of the Rocks* (London version). Second version of the Milanese altarpiece, produced over an extended period; scholars dispute how much was painted by Leonardo and how much by Ambrogio de Predis or other assistants. [Zöllner–Nathan; National Gallery]
- **c. 1500:** *Madonna of the Yarnwinder*. Leonardo’s documented composition survives through two principal panels and numerous workshop versions, but whether either principal version is substantially autograph—and whether an autograph original survives—is disputed. [Zöllner–Nathan; National Galleries of Scotland]
- **c. 1500–1510:** *Salvator Mundi*. Severely damaged and extensively restored panel attributed by some specialists to Leonardo and by others to his workshop or a follower; its authorship cannot be stated as settled. [National Gallery 2011; Prado; Zöllner]
- **c. 1501–1508:** *Madonna and Child with Saint Anne and a Lamb*. Known through workshop versions and related drawings; whether Leonardo completed a distinct painted version before the Louvre panel is uncertain. [Zöllner–Nathan; Louvre]
- **c. 1501–1519:** *The Virgin and Child with Saint Anne*. Large panel repeatedly revised and apparently still unfinished at Leonardo’s death. [Zöllner–Nathan; Louvre]
- **c. 1503–1519:** *Portrait of Lisa Gherardini, called the Mona Lisa or La Gioconda*. Portrait worked on over many years and apparently retained by Leonardo; the identification as Lisa Gherardini is strongly supported but has not eliminated every alternative proposal. [Zöllner–Nathan; Louvre]
- **1503–1506:** *The Battle of Anghiari*. Mural project for Florence’s Palazzo Vecchio; Leonardo’s experimental wall process failed, the painting was later covered or destroyed, and its composition survives chiefly through preparatory studies and copies. [Zöllner–Nathan; Uffizi]
- **c. 1504–1508:** *Leda and the Swan* (kneeling composition). Painted project known from drawings and derivatives; the existence and extent of a finished autograph panel are uncertain. [Zöllner–Nathan; Royal Collection]
- **c. 1505–1510:** *Leda and the Swan* (standing composition). Lost painting or painting project known from Leonardo’s studies, early descriptions, and workshop copies; a work once at Fontainebleau may have been Leonardo’s original. [Zöllner–Nathan; Louvre; Royal Collection]
- **c. 1508–1519:** *Saint John the Baptist*. Panel generally accepted as a late Leonardo, probably completed with some studio assistance. [Zöllner–Nathan; Louvre]
- **c. 1510–1515:** *Saint John the Baptist/Bacchus*. Panel transformed after Leonardo’s lifetime from a Baptist into Bacchus; usually classified as a workshop painting based on his design rather than an autograph obra. [Zöllner–Nathan; Louvre]
- **c. 1508–1515:** *La Scapigliata (Head of a Woman)*. Small unfinished painting or painted study on wood; attribution to Leonardo is traditional but not universal, and its intended function is unknown. [Zöllner–Nathan; Galleria Nazionale di Parma]

### Lost or textually attested paintings

- **Youthful work; date uncertain:** *Dragon or monster shield*. Vasari describes Leonardo assembling studies of animals into a frightening painted shield; the object is lost, and the anecdote’s factual reliability is uncertain. [Vasari; Zöllner]
- **Before 1500; date uncertain:** *Head of Medusa*. Lost or unverified painted panel described by Vasari and recorded in the Medici collection; it may or may not be identical with the youthful shield. [Vasari; Zöllner]
- **Date uncertain:** *Madonna sent to Pope Clement VII*. A painting mentioned by Vasari but not securely identifiable with a surviving object. [Vasari; Zöllner]
- **c. 1503–1505:** *Angel of the Annunciation*. Lost small painting mentioned by Vasari and sometimes connected with a surviving study; its precise form and authorship history cannot be recovered. [Vasari; Zöllner]
- **Date uncertain:** *Neptune*. Drawing or painted presentation piece reportedly made for Antonio Segni; it is lost and known only through descriptions and possible derivatives. [Vasari; Zöllner]
- **Date uncertain:** *Pomona*. A lost mythological composition attributed to Leonardo by later testimony; its existence as an autograph finished painting is uncertain. [Zöllner]

### Sculpture and monument projects

- **c. 1470s:** *Small clay heads and figures*. Vasari credits Leonardo with modeled heads and figures in youth, but no surviving sculpture is securely attributable to him; this is an attested practice rather than a recoverable catalogue of objects. [Vasari; Zöllner]
- **c. 1482–1499:** *Equestrian monument to Francesco Sforza*. Monumental bronze project developed through numerous drawings and models; a full-size clay horse was displayed but destroyed, while the intended bronze was never cast. [Zöllner–Nathan; Royal Collection]
- **c. 1506–1511:** *Equestrian monument to Gian Giacomo Trivulzio*. Tomb-and-horse project preserved in drawings and notes but never executed; the project, rather than a finished sculpture, is the obra. [Zöllner–Nathan; Royal Collection]
- **c. 1517–1519:** *Equestrian monument for Marshal Gian Giacomo Trivulzio or the French court*. Late horse-and-rider studies have competing identifications and may represent a renewed monument project rather than one fixed commission. [Zöllner–Nathan]
- **Date uncertain:** *Wax or plaster anatomical models*. Leonardo’s notes describe making three-dimensional models for anatomical investigation, including cerebral ventricles and cardiac structures; none is securely known to survive. [Royal Collection, *Leonardo da Vinci: Anatomist*]

### Performances, pageants, and ephemeral machines

- **1490:** *Festa del Paradiso*. Court spectacle for the marriage celebrations of Gian Galeazzo Sforza and Isabella of Aragon, combining stage machinery, costume, music, text, and celestial effects; it is best understood as an ephemeral Gesamtkunstwerk. Bernardo Bellincioni supplied the surviving verse, so the whole cannot be assigned to Leonardo alone. [Zöllner; Museo Galileo]
- **1491:** *Tournament and festivities for Ludovico Sforza and Beatrice d’Este*. Leonardo designed costumes, emblems, and theatrical machinery, but the surviving evidence does not permit a complete list of discrete objects. [Zöllner–Nathan]
- **1496:** *Danaë*. Stage project associated with a play by Baldassare Taccone; Leonardo’s surviving drawings concern scenery and mechanisms, while the performance as a whole was collaborative and ephemeral. [Zöllner–Nathan; Metropolitan Museum]
- **c. 1506–1508:** *Orpheus theatrical project*. Designs for a mountain that opens to reveal Pluto’s realm, probably intended for a staging of *Orpheus*; whether it was performed is uncertain. [Zöllner–Nathan]
- **1515:** *Mechanical lion for the entry of Francis I into Lyon*. Automaton traditionally credited to Leonardo and described as opening its breast to reveal fleurs-de-lis; the device is lost and the exact occasion and mechanism remain debated. [Zöllner; Museo Galileo]
- **1517–1518:** *Court festivities at Amboise*. Leonardo participated in designs for spectacles and architecture under Francis I, but individual responsibility is difficult to separate from collaborative court production. [Zöllner]

### Architectural, hydraulic, urban, and territorial projects

- **c. 1487–1490:** *Tiburio project for Milan Cathedral*. Architectural consultation and model for the cathedral crossing; Leonardo withdrew or lost the commission, and the model does not survive. [Zöllner–Nathan; Veneranda Fabbrica del Duomo]
- **c. 1487–1490:** *Ideal-city studies*. A body of urban designs separating traffic, services, waterways, and habitation onto different levels; these are speculative projects rather than plans for one securely commissioned city. [Zöllner–Nathan]
- **c. 1490s:** *Centralized-church studies*. Numerous related plans and elevations explore domed, centrally planned buildings; they form an experimental architectural corpus, not a single buildable church. [Zöllner–Nathan]
- **c. 1490s:** *Designs for the Castello Sforzesco and ducal estates*. Architectural, hydraulic, garden, pavilion, and service proposals survive in dispersed drawings; attribution to particular commissions is often conjectural. [Zöllner–Nathan]
- **c. 1494–1497:** *Naviglio and Lombard canal projects*. Surveys and designs for locks, channels, water control, and navigation; some record existing works while others are protocols or unrealized interventions, so the corpus resists treatment as one invention. [Zöllner–Nathan; Museo Galileo]
- **1502:** *Bridge over the Golden Horn for Sultan Bayezid II*. Unexecuted proposal documented by Leonardo’s drawing and draft letter; its enormous single-span form was a project, not a constructed bridge. [Zöllner–Nathan; Museo Galileo]
- **1502:** *Map of Imola*. Measured, ichnographic city plan made during Leonardo’s service to Cesare Borgia; it is simultaneously survey, military-intelligence object, and cartographic obra. [Zöllner–Nathan; Royal Collection]
- **1502–1503:** *Maps and surveys for Cesare Borgia*. A body of route maps, fortification studies, and territorial observations produced as military engineer; individual sheets often combine record, diagnosis, and proposed intervention. [Zöllner–Nathan]
- **1503–1504:** *Arno diversion and Florence-to-the-sea canal*. Hydraulic and military project intended partly to deprive Pisa of the river and partly to improve navigation; works begun in the field failed, while Leonardo’s larger scheme remained unrealized. [Zöllner–Nathan; Museo Galileo]
- **c. 1506–1513:** *Lombard waterway and irrigation projects*. Surveys and proposals concerning the Adda, Martesana, locks, mills, and canals; these constitute a shifting technical programme rather than one completed obra. [Zöllner–Nathan]
- **1513–1516:** *Pontine Marshes drainage project*. Maps and channel designs associated with Giuliano de’ Medici’s proposed reclamation; the scheme was not executed in Leonardo’s form. [Zöllner–Nathan; Royal Collection]
- **c. 1516–1519:** *Romorantin palace and ideal town*. Architectural and hydraulic project for Francis I, including palace, canals, stables, and an urban complex; it remained on paper. [Zöllner–Nathan]
- **c. 1517–1519:** *Château and waterworks projects at Amboise/Clos Lucé*. Late architectural and hydraulic studies traditionally connected with Francis I’s court; the link between particular drawings and executed structures is disputed. [Zöllner–Nathan]

### Engineering, instruments, and machine projects

- **c. 1478–1519:** *Engineering and machinery studies*. Catalogue corpus of drawings for gears, bearings, transmissions, mills, pumps, textile machinery, clocks, measuring devices, and automata. Many sheets analyze existing technology rather than claim a new invention, and drawings do not prove that machines were built. [Zöllner–Nathan, graphic-work catalogue, section 13]
- **c. 1480s–1510s:** *Military-equipment studies*. Designs include multi-barrel guns, large crossbows, fortifications, armored vehicles, mobile bridges, and siege devices; they are projects and technical thought experiments, not a surviving arsenal. [Zöllner–Nathan, section 14]
- **c. 1480s–1515:** *Flying-machine studies*. A changing body of projects for human-powered wings, ornithopters, gliding, parachute-like devices, and the so-called aerial screw; no successful full-scale flying machine is documented. [Zöllner–Nathan, section 15; Codex on the Flight of Birds]
- **c. 1490s–1515:** *Water-motion and hydraulic-machine studies*. Drawings and notes examine currents, vortices, pumps, dredges, locks, boats, and water-lifting devices; observation and project continually merge. [Zöllner–Nathan; Codex Leicester]
- **c. 1495:** *Mechanical knight*. Drawings have been interpreted as plans for an articulated automaton, perhaps for court entertainment; its construction in Leonardo’s lifetime is unproved. [Codex Atlanticus; Museo Galileo]
- **c. 1500:** *Diving and underwater-warfare apparatus*. Protocols and designs for breathing tubes, floats, webbed gloves, and attacks on ships; some details were deliberately obscured, and there is no evidence of a complete surviving apparatus. [Codex Atlanticus; Museo Galileo]
- **c. 1504–1508:** *Giant crossbow*. Highly finished design for an enormous wheeled bow; it is a presentation drawing or engineering project, with no evidence that it was built. [Zöllner–Nathan; Codex Atlanticus]
- **c. 1506–1510:** *Self-propelled cart*. Spring-driven vehicle design, possibly intended for theatrical motion rather than transport; no original machine survives. [Codex Atlanticus; Museo Galileo]
- **c. 1510–1515:** *Mechanical lion*. Automaton project associated with court spectacle; documentary testimony supports a manifestation, but its exact construction cannot be recovered from the surviving drawings. [Codex Atlanticus; Museo Galileo]

### Anatomical and natural-philosophical bodies of work

- **c. 1487–1515:** *Anatomical drawings and notes*. A corpus of dissections, diagrams, layered views, measurements, and explanatory writing covering bones, muscles, organs, generation, the nervous system, and comparative anatomy. It was intended at various moments for a treatise, but no finished book was assembled or published by Leonardo. [Clark–Pedretti; Royal Collection, *Leonardo da Vinci: Anatomist*]
- **c. 1508–1513:** *Studies of the heart and circulation*. Drawings, experiments, wax models, and hydraulic analogies investigating valves, ventricles, and blood flow; this is an experimental research project embedded within the anatomical corpus. [Royal Collection]
- **c. 1510–1511:** *Anatomical collaboration with Marcantonio della Torre*. Planned illustrated anatomical work combining Leonardo’s dissections and images with della Torre’s medical learning; the collaborator’s death and Leonardo’s movements left it incomplete. [Royal Collection]
- **c. 1480s–1519:** *Plant studies*. Catalogue corpus of drawings and notes on branching, leaf arrangement, growth, light, and water; individual sheets range from autonomous studies to material for paintings and treatises. [Zöllner–Nathan, section 9]
- **c. 1480s–1519:** *Landscape, water, geology, and deluge studies*. A corpus joining empirical observation, cosmological speculation, and visionary images of catastrophe; the late deluge drawings are finished graphic manifestations but not illustrations to a completed book. [Zöllner–Nathan, section 10; Royal Collection]
- **c. 1490s–1510s:** *Studies of light, optics, and shadow*. Notes, diagrams, and experiments intended for several projected treatises; the surviving material is distributed across notebooks and never reached a final authorial edition. [Zöllner–Nathan, section 16]

### Drawing corpora

- **1473:** *Landscape drawing dated 5 August 1473*. Pen-and-ink view traditionally regarded as Leonardo’s earliest dated drawing; whether it represents a specific Tuscan site remains debated. [Zöllner–Nathan; Uffizi]
- **c. 1470–1519:** *Studies for surviving and documented paintings*. Hundreds of preparatory sheets record alternative compositions, figures, draperies, heads, hands, landscapes, and technical trials; catalogue entries are individual physical sheets, often bearing several otherwise unrelated acts of thought. [Zöllner–Nathan, section 1]
- **c. 1480–1511:** *Horse and equestrian-monument studies*. A large corpus made around the Sforza and Trivulzio projects but also including independent anatomical and motion studies. [Zöllner–Nathan, section 2]
- **c. 1470–1519:** *Human figures, animals, and monsters*. Drawings range from observational studies to inventions and caricatural composites; many have no fixed contemporary title. [Zöllner–Nathan, section 3]
- **c. 1470–1485:** *Drapery studies*. Highly worked brush-and-heightening studies, some made on linen prepared over clay models; attribution within the broader Verrocchio workshop remains disputed for several sheets. [Zöllner–Nathan, section 4]
- **c. 1470–1515:** *Profile studies, character heads, and grotesques*. A corpus spanning ideal beauty, social observation, physiognomic experiment, satire, and fantasy; “caricature” is useful but historically imperfect as a single category. [Zöllner–Nathan, section 5]
- **c. 1487–1492:** *Vitruvian Man*. Pen-and-ink study of bodily proportion based on Vitruvius but revised through Leonardo’s own measurements; it is diagram, research note, and iconic independent drawing at once. [Zöllner–Nathan; Gallerie dell’Accademia]
- **c. 1480s–1510s:** *Proportion studies*. Drawings measuring human and animal bodies and relating motion to geometry; they form research toward projected treatises rather than one completed text. [Zöllner–Nathan, section 6]
- **c. 1480s–1510s:** *Allegories, emblems, pictographs, and rebuses*. Graphic-verbal inventions made for courtly exchange, moral reflection, entertainment, or personal notation; many resist separation into “text” and “image.” [Zöllner–Nathan, section 8]
- **c. 1500:** *Portrait of Isabella d’Este*. Large cartoon made at Mantua, probably in preparation for a painted portrait that was requested but not securely known to have been executed. [Zöllner–Nathan; Louvre]
- **c. 1500–1508:** *The Virgin and Child with Saint Anne and Saint John the Baptist (Burlington House Cartoon)*. Full-scale compositional cartoon, probably a stage in Leonardo’s evolving Saint Anne project rather than a straightforward transfer design for the surviving painting. [Zöllner–Nathan; National Gallery]
- **c. 1512:** *Self-portrait in red chalk*. Celebrated drawing traditionally identified as Leonardo’s self-portrait; both the identification and, less often, the attribution have been questioned. [Zöllner–Nathan; Biblioteca Reale]
- **c. 1517–1518:** *A Deluge*. Series of late black-chalk catastrophe drawings; they are neither simple scientific diagrams nor illustrations to a known finished text. [Zöllner–Nathan; Royal Collection]

### Manuscript objects and notebook assemblages

- **c. 1478–1518:** *Codex Atlanticus*. The largest surviving assemblage of Leonardo’s sheets, covering art, machines, mathematics, architecture, and natural philosophy; Pompeo Leoni created the codex after Leonardo’s death, so it is a later compilation of many obras and fragments, not a book Leonardo made in its present form. [MPIWG catalogue; Ambrosiana]
- **c. 1478–1518:** *Codex Arundel*. Miscellaneous collection of sheets now bound as British Library Arundel MS 263; its present codex form is posthumous and resists treatment as one authored text. [British Library; Metropolitan Museum manuscript concordance]
- **c. 1487–1490:** *Codex Trivulzianus*. Notebook concerned with vocabulary, architecture, military matters, and other studies; leaves are missing, so it is an incomplete physical remnant rather than a finished work. [MPIWG catalogue; Castello Sforzesco]
- **c. 1486–1490:** *Paris Manuscript B*. Notebook especially important for architecture, military engineering, and flying-machine projects; parts were removed in the nineteenth century and later partly reunited. [Metropolitan Museum manuscript concordance; Institut de France]
- **c. 1490–1492:** *Paris Manuscript A*. Notebook on painting, mechanics, and related investigations; stolen and missing folios make its current form incomplete. [Metropolitan Museum manuscript concordance; Institut de France]
- **1490–1491:** *Paris Manuscript C*. Notebook principally concerned with light, shade, and optics, with dated entries. [Metropolitan Museum manuscript concordance; Institut de France]
- **1493–1494:** *Paris Manuscript H*. Three originally distinct small notebooks now treated together, containing geometry, mechanics, vocabulary, and other notes. [Metropolitan Museum manuscript concordance; Institut de France]
- **c. 1490–1497:** *Codex Forster III*. Notebook of geometry, weights, hydraulics, and miscellaneous observations; its chronological layers make it a working instrument rather than a composed book. [Metropolitan Museum manuscript concordance; Victoria and Albert Museum]
- **c. 1494–1497:** *Codex Forster II*. Two notebook units now bound together, largely concerned with mechanics, proportions, and physical problems. [Metropolitan Museum manuscript concordance; Victoria and Albert Museum]
- **c. 1487–1505:** *Codex Forster I*. Two chronologically separate notebook units, including material on geometry and hydraulic measurement; the present binding is not a single continuous authorial text. [Metropolitan Museum manuscript concordance; Victoria and Albert Museum]
- **c. 1491–1505:** *Madrid Codex II*. Composite notebook containing studies for the Sforza monument, fortifications, geometry, maps, and the Battle of Anghiari period; its contents belong to separated phases. [Metropolitan Museum manuscript concordance; Biblioteca Nacional de España]
- **c. 1493–1499:** *Madrid Codex I*. Extensive working manuscript on theoretical and applied mechanics, assembled as a notebook but never converted into the systematic treatise Leonardo envisaged. [Metropolitan Museum manuscript concordance; Biblioteca Nacional de España]
- **c. 1495–1500:** *Paris Manuscript M*. Pocket notebook containing geometry, physics, personal memoranda, and reading notes; most pages date from about 1498–1500, though work may have begun earlier. [Metropolitan Museum manuscript concordance; Institut de France]
- **c. 1497–1499:** *Paris Manuscript I*. Two notebook units containing mechanics, geometry, water studies, and other notes. [Metropolitan Museum manuscript concordance; Institut de France]
- **c. 1497–1502, possibly later:** *Paris Manuscript L*. Pocket notebook associated particularly with military architecture, waterways, and Leonardo’s travels. [Metropolitan Museum manuscript concordance; Institut de France]
- **c. 1503–1508:** *Paris Manuscript K*. Three units containing geometry, motion, hydraulics, and anatomical or optical observations; their dates differ, so the modern manuscript is composite. [Metropolitan Museum manuscript concordance; Institut de France]
- **1505:** *Codex on the Flight of Birds*. Short manuscript analyzing bird flight and mechanical flight; it was originally attached to Paris Manuscript B and was removed in the nineteenth century, making its independence partly an accident of collection history. [Metropolitan Museum manuscript concordance; Biblioteca Reale]
- **c. 1508:** *Paris Manuscript D*. Small notebook devoted chiefly to optics and the eye. [Metropolitan Museum manuscript concordance; Institut de France]
- **1508:** *Paris Manuscript F*. Dated notebook on water, geology, astronomy, optics, and related natural philosophy. [Metropolitan Museum manuscript concordance; Institut de France]
- **c. 1508–1512:** *Codex Leicester*. Notebook principally on water, geology, fossils, light, and cosmology; although unusually coherent, it remains a working manuscript rather than a finalized treatise. [Metropolitan Museum manuscript concordance]
- **c. 1510–1515:** *Paris Manuscript G*. Notebook addressing water, botany, painting, and other subjects, with material accumulated over several years. [Metropolitan Museum manuscript concordance; Institut de France]
- **1513–1514:** *Paris Manuscript E*. Notebook on weights, mechanics, water, and flight; a substantial group of folios is missing. [Metropolitan Museum manuscript concordance; Institut de France]
- **c. 1504–1506:** *Codex Huygens source drawings*. Lost Leonardo material on human proportion and movement appears to underlie a later manuscript by another hand; what survives is a derivative witness, not Leonardo’s own codex. [Zöllner–Nathan]

### Projected books, protocols, and verbal creations

- **c. 1480s–1519:** *Book on Painting*. Repeatedly projected treatise assembled only after Leonardo’s death by Francesco Melzi as the *Libro di pittura* in Codex Urbinas Latinus 1270; the familiar *Treatise on Painting* is therefore a posthumous manifestation of Leonardo’s notes, not a finished book he issued. [Metropolitan Museum manuscript concordance; Vatican Library]
- **c. 1489–1510s:** *Book on the Human Figure and Anatomy*. Planned illustrated treatise announced and reorganized in Leonardo’s notes but never completed as an authorial codex. [Royal Collection]
- **c. 1490s–1510s:** *Books on mechanics, weight, force, percussion, and motion*. Leonardo repeatedly numbered or cross-referenced projected “books,” but their material survives dispersed among notebooks; treating them as completed titles would invent works he did not finalize. [Madrid Codex I; Codex Atlanticus]
- **c. 1490s–1510s:** *Book on Water*. Projected systematic treatment of water’s forms and motions; the notes and diagrams survive, especially in the Codex Leicester and Arundel, but the book itself remained unfinished. [Codex Leicester; Codex Arundel]
- **c. 1490s–1510s:** *Books on light, shade, perspective, and the eye*. Interlocking treatise projects preserved in Manuscripts C and D and other notebooks; their boundaries and proposed titles changed during Leonardo’s work. [Paris Manuscripts C and D]
- **c. 1505–1506:** *Treatise on the Flight of Birds*. Research project most clearly manifested in the codex bearing that modern title; it combines observation, protocol, diagrams, and projected flying-machine design without reaching a final edition. [Codex on the Flight of Birds]
- **c. 1490s:** *Bestiary*. Compilation of animal lore and moralized properties embedded in Leonardo’s notebooks, largely adapted from earlier sources; it is a textual assemblage rather than an independent authored book. [Jean Paul Richter; Paris Manuscript H]
- **c. 1490s:** *Fables*. Short prose narratives scattered through the manuscripts; they form a recognizable literary corpus but were not assembled by Leonardo into a published collection. [Richter; Giunti manuscript editions]
- **c. 1490s:** *Prophecies*. Riddle-like texts describing ordinary events as apocalyptic visions; performative and satirical manifestations, probably intended for oral or courtly use, rather than predictions in the modern sense. [Richter; Codex Atlanticus]
- **c. 1490s:** *Jests, riddles, and rebuses*. Verbal-graphic entertainments dispersed through the notebooks; many are adaptations or records of circulating material, so authorship at the level of each item is uncertain. [Zöllner–Nathan; Richter]
- **c. 1490s–1510s:** *Recipes, workshop procedures, and experimental protocols*. Instructions for pigments, casting, optics, hydraulics, anatomy, and tests of materials occur throughout the manuscripts; they are operative texts, but not one stable work. [Codex Atlanticus; Madrid Codices; Institut de France manuscripts]
- **c. 1503–1504:** *Draft letter to Sultan Bayezid II*. Unsent or unverified proposal offering Leonardo’s engineering services and describing bridge projects; it acts as a professional manifesto as much as correspondence. [Codex Atlanticus]
- **c. 1482:** *Letter of self-recommendation to Ludovico Sforza*. Draft enumerating military, hydraulic, architectural, sculptural, and pictorial capacities; it is a performative professional text, though the surviving manuscript may be a draft rather than the delivered letter. [Codex Atlanticus]
- **1507–1519:** *Personal memoranda, accounts, lists, and letters*. Dispersed working texts concerning money, travel, books, assistants, patrons, and family; they are things Leonardo wrote, but resist being elevated into autonomous “works.” [Richter; manuscript facsimiles]

### Collaborative mathematical illustrations

- **c. 1496–1498:** *Polyhedral illustrations for Luca Pacioli’s De divina proportione*. Drawings of solid and skeletal polyhedra traditionally credited to Leonardo and preserved through manuscript and printed versions; their exact transfer into the 1509 woodcuts and the division of labor remain subjects of study. [Pacioli manuscripts; Metropolitan Museum]
- **c. 1490s:** *Geometrical transformations and “lunar” figures*. Extensive experimental drawings made in dialogue with Pacioli’s mathematics; they are research manifestations spread across notebooks rather than illustrations to one securely completed Leonardo text. [Codex Atlanticus; Madrid Codex I]
