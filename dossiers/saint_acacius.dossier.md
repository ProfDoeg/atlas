# Saint Acacius of Byzantium: Research Dossier

## Scope, Identity, and Method

This dossier concerns the late-antique Christian martyr usually identified as **Acacius the Centurion**, **Acacius of Byzantium**, or **Agathius of Byzantium**. It does **not** concern:

- Acacius of Caesarea, the fourth-century bishop and theologian.
- Acacius of Constantinople, the fifth-century patriarch associated with the Acacian Schism.
- Acacius of Melitene.
- Acacius, leader of the legendary Ten Thousand Martyrs of Mount Ararat.
- Acacius the Younger, the nineteenth-century Greek New Martyr.
- The monk Acacius celebrated in Byzantine monastic literature.

The surviving evidence does not permit a continuous, independently documented biography. It consists chiefly of:

1. Early calendar and martyrological notices.
2. A developed Greek martyr passion, catalogued as **BHG 13**, preserved in a version attributed to Symeon Metaphrastes.
3. Related Oriental versions catalogued as **BHO 20–21**.
4. Late-antique testimony concerning churches or shrines called “of Acacius.”
5. Medieval and modern liturgical traditions.
6. Local traditions concerning relics in Calabria and Spain.
7. Later iconography and the western cult of the Fourteen Holy Helpers.

Statements below are therefore labeled by evidentiary status.

| Western and Italian forms | Agathius, Agazio, Agario |

The name **Ἀκάκιος** derives from the Greek adjective *akakos*, approximately “innocent,” “without malice,” or “not evil.” “Agathius” and “Agazio” are conventional transformations rather than evidence for a different birth name.

## Evidence Map

### Early or relatively early evidence

- **Syriac Breviary/Martyrology:** Preserved in a manuscript copied in 411 and reflecting a calendar associated with Nicomedia. It places an Acacius martyr at **Nicomedia**, apparently on **10 May**. This is the most important evidence against the conventional title “of Byzantium.”
- **Martyrologium Hieronymianum:** Its tradition includes the entry *Constantinopoli Acaci militis et Maximi presbiteri*—“At Constantinople, Acacius the soldier and Maximus the priest”—under 8 May. Although early in substance, the work is composite and survives in later manuscripts.
- **John Chrysostom:** Around 400, sermons were preached in a Constantinopolitan church or shrine identified with the martyr Acacius. These attest an established cult in the capital by the end of the fourth century, but do not preserve a biography.
- **Socrates Scholasticus:** Writing in the fifth century, Socrates knew a Constantinopolitan tradition that Acacius had been hanged from a walnut tree at a place called Carya/Karya. This conflicts with the beheading narrative.
- **Procopius, *On Buildings*:** In the sixth century, Procopius reported that Justinian demolished and rebuilt a ruined Church of Acacius in Constantinople, enlarging it considerably.

### Later narrative evidence

The developed Greek passion gives the fullest “life,” including Cappadocian origin, Christian ancestry, military rank, interrogations, named officials, torture, prison miracles, transport to Byzantium, and beheading. Its elaborate speeches and miracles belong to hagiographic composition. It cannot be treated as a verbatim judicial transcript merely because it incorporates official-sounding letters and sentences.

### Historical minimum

The most cautious reconstruction is:

- A Christian soldier named Acacius was remembered as a martyr of the Great Persecution.
- His cult existed in or was transferred to Constantinople by approximately 400.
- Different early traditions placed his death at Nicomedia or Constantinople.
- The detailed Cappadocian-centurion biography is later than the event and historically insecure.
- A shrine or church bearing the name Acacius existed in Constantinople and was rebuilt under Justinian.
- His cult subsequently spread to Calabria, Iberia, and central Europe.

## Family and Ancestry

### Christian parents and ancestors

**Hagiographic claim:** In the developed passion, Acacius tells the tribune Firmus that he was born a Christian and descended from Christian parents and ancestors.

This is the only substantial statement about his family. No parents, grandparents, siblings, spouse, partner, or children are named. No independent inscription, contemporary letter, legal record, or genealogy confirms his ancestry.

The claim serves a recognizable literary purpose: it makes Acacius’ confession continuous with an inherited Christian identity and emphasizes that pagan officials could not treat his religion as a momentary enthusiasm.

### Ethnicity and regional identity

**Traditional claim:** Acacius was Cappadocian and is sometimes described as a “Cappadocian Greek.”

“Cappadocian” is present in the later tradition. “Greek” is a modern ethno-linguistic specification not established by surviving personal evidence. Cappadocia in the late third and early fourth centuries was a culturally mixed Roman province. His spoken language, citizenship status beyond membership in the Roman army, and precise hometown are unknown.

## Childhood and Early Life

No historically usable account of Acacius’ childhood survives.

The passion’s assertion of Christian parentage implies upbringing within a Christian household, but it supplies:

- No birth year.
- No town or estate of origin.
- No parents’ names.
- No account of baptism.
- No childhood incidents.
- No social class beyond the later military career.
- No explanation of entry into the army.

A late-third-century birth is an inference from the traditional martyrdom date and claimed rank, not an independently recorded fact.

## Education and Formation

No source identifies a school, teacher, bishop, military instructor, or theological mentor.

The speeches assigned to Acacius in the passion present him as capable of extended Christian apologetic argument. He denounces the worship of manufactured images and confesses Christ as creator, healer, and heavenly rewarder. These passages reveal the theology and literary conventions of the passion’s author or redactor more securely than Acacius’ personal education.

No writings, dictated teachings, correspondence, or independently transmitted sayings by the historical Acacius survive.

## Military Career

### Rank

**Hagiographic claim:** Acacius was a Roman **centurion**.

A centurion normally commanded a century-sized unit and occupied a professional officer rank. The title is plausible in a general sense but is not independently confirmed by an inscription, papyrus, military diploma, or contemporary administrative record.

### Unit

The passion describes him as serving under the tribune Firmus in a formation rendered in Latin by the Bollandists as the **Martesii** and in Greek as something resembling **Arieioi**. Modern devotional versions call it the “Martesian regiment.”

The exact historical identity of this unit is uncertain. The terminology may reflect a later military environment or textual corruption. It should not be used to locate Acacius within a securely reconstructed order of battle.

### Command structure in the passion

The principal officials are:

- **Firmus:** Tribune and Acacius’ immediate commander.
- **Bibianus/Vivianus:** A higher military or judicial official who conducts prolonged interrogation and torture.
- **Flaccinus:** Proconsul or senior governor who pronounces the death sentence at Byzantium.

The names and titles belong to the literary dossier. No securely identified contemporary officeholder has been matched to them.

## Religious and Intellectual Development

The sources depict no gradual conversion. Acacius is represented as Christian from birth and as maintaining that identity through military service.

His reported beliefs include:

- Exclusive worship of the Christian God.
- Confession of Jesus Christ.
- Rejection of sacrifice to the traditional gods.
- Condemnation of images made from stone or other material.
- Belief in bodily healing by Christ.
- Expectation of heavenly reward through martyrdom.
- Willingness to accept death rather than perform the commanded cult.

These positions fit the ideology of martyr acts: the tribunal becomes a contest between imperial coercion and Christian confession. The surviving speeches cannot be verified as his actual words.

## Chronological Life History

### Later third century: alleged birth in Cappadocia

**Hagiographic claim:** Acacius was born in Cappadocia to Christian parents and ancestors.

No exact date or locality is recorded. There is no evidence concerning his early residence or the route by which he entered the Roman army.

### Before 303–305: military service in Thrace

**Hagiographic claim:** He attained the rank of centurion in a regiment commanded by Firmus and served in Thrace.

Some retellings place the initial proceedings at **Perinthus**, an important Thracian city on the Propontis. The passion’s administrative geography is not sufficiently secure to establish where Acacius had been stationed before his arrest.

### 303–305: imperial persecution

The narrative is set during the persecution associated with **Diocletian**, **Maximian**, and especially **Galerius**. The Great Persecution began with edicts in 303. Galerius became Augustus only in 305, which helps explain disagreement between traditional dates.

Modern church biographies commonly say either:

- The martyrdom occurred in **303**.
- It occurred “around **304**.”
- It occurred under Galerius’ persecution in **305–311**.
- More critically, it occurred at Nicomedia on **10 May 305**.

The sources do not yield a universally accepted exact date.

### Interrogation by Firmus

**Hagiographic narrative:** Firmus summoned soldiers individually to test their willingness to obey imperial religious orders. Some yielded to persuasion or threats. Acacius openly declared:

> “I was born a Christian; therefore I am, and shall be, a Christian.”

Firmus allegedly offered threats and inducements three times. Acacius remained unmoved, so Firmus bound him and sent him to Bibianus.

The repeated threefold interrogation, alternating threats and promises, is characteristic of martyr literature. No original protocol survives.

### Proceedings before Bibianus/Vivianus

**Hagiographic narrative:** Bibianus attempted to persuade Acacius to sacrifice and then ordered repeated beatings. The instruments vary across retellings and iconography: leather thongs, rods, clubs, or iron bars.

Acacius invoked Christ for assistance. A heavenly voice allegedly told him not to fear and promised reunion with his fathers in heaven. His torturers then became temporarily unable to continue.

**Status:** Miracle story within the passion; not independently corroborated.

### Imprisonment and transport

**Hagiographic narrative:** Acacius was placed in heavy restraints and confined. He and other prisoners were marched rapidly toward Byzantium without adequate food or water. His wounds, chains, hunger, and exhaustion weakened him.

At an overnight stop he prayed and heard the command, “Courage, Acacius, and be strong.” Other prisoners supposedly heard the voice, converted, and requested instruction from him.

No source names these converts or records their subsequent fate. Some western titles refer to Acacius “and seventy-seven companions,” but the developed biography generally treats the accompanying prisoners anonymously. The figure is not securely established.

### Prison in Byzantium

**Hagiographic narrative:** At Byzantium, Acacius was kept under exceptionally severe conditions. Radiant youths—understood as angels—appeared at night, washed his wounds, and brought food. When presented after seven days, he looked healthy.

Bibianus suspected that the jailer had accepted a bribe to feed or release him from his restraints. The guard denied this but was beaten. Acacius then attributed his recovery to Christ. Bibianus ordered blows to his face and the smashing of his teeth.

This episode is important for later iconography and devotion but remains hagiographic. The bribery suspicion is not evidence of an actual corruption investigation.

### Referral to Flaccinus

The passion contains an official-sounding letter in which Bibianus reports that Acacius, sent by Firmus, had resisted interrogation for twenty days and remained attached to the Christian religion.

**Textual fact:** Such a letter appears in the passion.

**Historical status:** It is not an independently preserved government document. Its formal structure may imitate late Roman administrative correspondence.

Flaccinus, according to the story, had a Christian wife who made him swear not to torture Christians at length. He therefore kept Acacius without the harshest restraints and reviewed the case after five days.

The wife is unnamed and unattested outside the passion.

### Condemnation and execution

**Hagiographic narrative:** Flaccinus criticized Bibianus for torturing a military man at excessive length and ordered prompt decapitation outside Byzantium. The formal sentence describes Acacius as a supporter of the Galileans, a deserter from the emperors’ victories, and a man disobedient to the laws.

Before execution Acacius prayed in thanksgiving and submitted his neck to the sword.

**Competing traditions:**

- Developed Greek passion: **beheading outside Byzantium**.
- Roman Martyrology: torture at Perinthus and death by sentence of Flaccinus at Byzantium.
- Martyrologium Hieronymianum: an Acacius the soldier commemorated at Constantinople.
- Syriac calendar: martyrdom at **Nicomedia**.
- Socrates Scholasticus: Acacius was said to have been **hanged from a walnut tree** at Carya in Constantinople.

The disagreement cannot presently be resolved by a contemporary execution record.

### Burial

The Greek tradition places his burial at or near Byzantium/Constantinople. Another brief account says the body was first buried at **Stauropolis** in Asia Minor.

No archaeologically authenticated original grave has been identified.

## Death: Date, Place, and Cause

### Traditional reconstruction

- **Date:** 303 or approximately 304.
- **Place:** Byzantium.
- **Cause:** Judicial beheading following refusal to sacrifice.

### Critical reconstruction proposed by David Woods

David Woods argues that the strongest early calendrical evidence points to:

- **Date:** 10 May 305.
- **Place:** Nicomedia.
- **Cause:** Martyrdom during the Great Persecution.

Woods further argues that the Constantinopolitan Church “of Acacius” was originally named for its founder, an official called Acacius, and was only subsequently reinterpreted as the shrine of the martyr.

This reconstruction is influential but remains a scholarly argument, not a demonstrated consensus based on newly discovered contemporary records.

## The Constantinopolitan Cult

### Church or shrine by approximately 400

John Chrysostom preached at a Constantinopolitan sanctuary described as the church or temple of the holy martyr Acacius. The relevant texts are catalogued among his sermons as **CPG 4441.14** and **CPG 4441.15**.

This evidence shows that a martyr named Acacius was associated with a Constantinopolitan church by Chrysostom’s period. It does not establish that:

- The martyr died there.
- Constantine founded the church.
- The church contained Acacius’ original grave.
- Every later reference to a Church of Acacius concerns the same building.

### The Carya walnut-tree tradition

Socrates Scholasticus reports that a large Constantinopolitan property was called **Carya**, “Walnut,” because a walnut tree stood in its courtyard. Acacius was said to have suffered martyrdom by hanging from that tree, and a chapel had been constructed nearby.

Emperor Arcadius visited and prayed there. The neighborhood’s residents went outside to see him; once the building complex had emptied, it collapsed without killing them. Socrates presents this providential escape as evidence of divine favor toward Arcadius.

This account:

- Attests the existence of a local Acacius tradition by the early fifth century.
- Conflicts with the later beheading account.
- Does not identify Acacius as a centurion.
- May preserve the memory of another Acacius.
- May reflect the reinterpretation of a place originally associated with a non-martyr named Acacius.

### Constantine and the church

**Ecclesiastical tradition:** Constantine the Great built a church honoring Acacius and deposited his relics there.

**Critical alternative:** Woods argues that a comes or official named Acacius founded the building during Constantine’s reign. “Church of Acacius” originally meant the church founded or owned by Acacius, not a shrine dedicated to Saint Acacius. Later generations reinterpreted the name hagiographically.

An Acacius is documented as **comes Macedoniae** in 327, but identifying him as the founder is hypothetical.

### Constantius II and Constantine’s sarcophagus

Socrates reports that Bishop Macedonius transferred Constantine’s sarcophagus from the Church of the Holy Apostles to the Church of Acacius because the original structure was considered unsafe. Violence occurred between rival crowds during the removal.

This is firm evidence that a prominent building called the Church of Acacius existed by the middle of the fourth century. It does not settle whom the church originally commemorated.

### Justinianic rebuilding

**Documented literary report:** Procopius states that the Church of Acacius had become ruinous. Emperor **Justinian I** demolished it and rebuilt it from the foundations, greatly increasing its size.

The church has not survived as an identified standing monument. Its exact plan, final destruction date, and archaeological footprint remain uncertain.

## Relics, Translations, and Geographical Movements

### Constantinople to Stauropolis

**Later tradition:** One account places Acacius’ first burial at Stauropolis. When Muslim invaders threatened the region, inhabitants sealed his body in a lead casket and committed it to the sea.

No contemporary translation record has been located.

### Miraculous voyage to Calabria

**Legend:** Although made of lead, the reliquary floated across the sea and arrived at the shore of Squillace in Calabria. The Latin hymn used at Squillace explicitly celebrates the reversal of nature: lead becomes like a ship and floats.

Variants say:

- The casket came directly to Squillace.
- It first approached the coast near Guardavalle but was rejected because residents feared something diabolical.
- Basilian monks fleeing eastern iconoclasm transported the relics by ordinary human means.

### Dates proposed for the arrival

The traditions are inconsistent:

- Approximately **630** in some modern summaries.
- The eighth century, connected with Byzantine iconoclasm.
- The eleventh century in other Italian accounts.
- An unspecified period “at least thirteen centuries” ago in the diocesan account.

No surviving early translation charter establishes the date. The explanation involving iconodule Basilian monks is historically possible in outline but remains a local tradition rather than a documented itinerary.

### Squillace

The Cathedral of Squillace claims to preserve Acacius’ body in a monumental chapel. He is patron of Squillace and co-patron of the present Archdiocese of Catanzaro-Squillace.

Two principal celebrations continue:

- **16 January:** Feast of the Translation or “of the Bones.”
- **7 May:** Byzantine-derived commemoration of the martyrdom.

The diocesan clergy traditionally assemble at the cathedral on 7 May and renew obedience to the diocesan bishop.

The archival system of the Italian Ministry of Culture lists records concerning the Cathedral of Squillace and its relics, dating from 1523 to 1892, in the diocesan historical archive. These records may document the early modern custody and administration of the claimed relics but do not by themselves authenticate their late-antique identity.

### Guardavalle

In **1584**, Bishop Marcello Sirleto reportedly transferred a relic of Acacius’ arm from Squillace to Guardavalle, his native town. Acacius consequently became Guardavalle’s patron.

### Spain

The Archdiocese of Catanzaro-Squillace reports relics at **Cuenca** and **Ávila**, said to have come from Squillace. The dates and documentary chains of custody were not established in the sources consulted.

## Institutions, Offices, Property, and Financial Interests

### Offices

The only personal office attributed to Acacius is military:

- Centurion in a Roman imperial formation.

No ecclesiastical office is claimed. He was not a bishop, priest, monk, patriarch, or church founder in the developed centurion tradition.

### Property and wealth

No reliable evidence survives concerning:

- Salary.
- Land.
- House ownership.
- Inheritance.
- Slaves or dependants.
- Investments.
- Commercial activity.
- Debts.
- Testamentary dispositions.

His alleged centurion rank would ordinarily imply a status and income above those of common soldiers, but no personal financial data can be reconstructed.

### Posthumous institutional assets

Churches, chapels, altars, fairs, confraternities, relic shrines, and pilgrimage practices were later maintained in his name. These represent the institutional economy of his cult, not property owned by Acacius during life.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Firmus

**Hagiographic relationship:** Immediate commander, interrogator, and accuser. Firmus is portrayed as alternately threatening and enticing Acacius before referring him onward.

### Bibianus or Vivianus

**Hagiographic relationship:** Senior official and principal torturer. The variant forms may reflect manuscript transmission or translation.

### Flaccinus

**Hagiographic relationship:** Final judge or proconsul at Byzantium. He orders execution after criticizing the duration of Bibianus’ proceedings.

### Flaccinus’ wife

**Hagiographic relationship:** Unnamed Christian spouse whose intervention supposedly limited the duration of Christian prisoners’ torture.

### Fellow prisoners

**Hagiographic relationship:** Anonymous prisoners who allegedly heard the heavenly voice, converted, and received instruction from Acacius.

### John Chrysostom

**Posthumous cult relationship:** Chrysostom preached in a Constantinopolitan church associated with the martyr. He was not Acacius’ contemporary and had no personal relationship with him.

### Constantine the Great

**Disputed posthumous relationship:** Later tradition makes Constantine builder of Acacius’ shrine. The critical alternative is that Constantine’s official Acacius founded the building and was later confused with the martyr.

### Arcadius

**Posthumous devotional relationship:** Arcadius prayed at the Carya chapel associated with Acacius shortly before the surrounding complex collapsed harmlessly.

### Justinian I

**Documented posthumous institutional relationship:** Justinian rebuilt the Constantinopolitan Church of Acacius.

## Controversies, Accusations, and Evidentiary Problems

No credible source accuses the historical Acacius of fraud, corruption, abuse, financial crime, sexual misconduct, conspiracy, or political violence. The adversarial search returned no substantive modern scandal concerning this martyr.

The significant controversies are historiographical.

### Was he martyred at Byzantium or Nicomedia?

- **Byzantium:** Greek passion, Hieronymian tradition, Roman Martyrology.
- **Nicomedia:** Early Syriac calendar.
- **Assessment:** Unresolved. The Syriac source may preserve the earlier localization, while Constantinople unquestionably possessed an Acacius cult by approximately 400.

### Was he beheaded or hanged?

- **Beheaded:** Developed passion and later martyrologies.
- **Hanged from a walnut tree:** Socrates’ Carya tradition.
- **Assessment:** The accounts are incompatible unless they concern different Acacii or the hanging tradition refers to torture rather than the ultimate manner of death. The sources do not say the latter.

### Was he a Cappadocian centurion?

- **Claim:** Central to the developed passion.
- **Supporting evidence:** Repeated later Greek, Latin, Orthodox, and Catholic tradition.
- **Against certainty:** No contemporary military or epigraphic record; the earliest short calendars do not supply the full biography.
- **Assessment:** Traditional identification, historically plausible but unverified.

### Did Constantine build his church?

- **Traditional claim:** Yes.
- **Critical theory:** The church was founded by a Constantinian official named Acacius and only later converted in memory into a martyr’s shrine.
- **Assessment:** Disputed. The wording “Church of Acacius” alone cannot determine whether Acacius was patron, founder, owner, or martyr.

### Are the Squillace relics authentic?

- **Ecclesiastical and local claim:** The cathedral contains Acacius’ body.
- **Documented fact:** Relics identified as his have long been venerated there; early modern archival records concerning cathedral relics survive.
- **Problem:** No continuous chain of custody from a fourth-century grave to Calabria has been demonstrated.
- **Assessment:** Historically documented cult object; biological identity unverified.

### Confusion with the Ten Thousand Martyrs

A different Acacius or Achatius appears in a medieval legend as leader of thousands of Roman soldiers converted and crucified on Mount Ararat. That story developed in the twelfth or thirteenth century and is not the biography of the Byzantine centurion.

The two figures were easily conflated because both were military martyrs named Acacius/Achatius, and both entered western devotional art.

### Confusion within the Fourteen Holy Helpers

Some reference works identify the Helper Achatius as the bishop or confessor of Melitene; others identify him as the soldier martyr of Byzantium; still others allow features of the Ten Thousand Martyrs narrative to intrude.

Consequently, an artwork labeled “Saint Achatius” cannot be assigned automatically to Acacius the Centurion without examining attributes and local context.

### Results of the dedicated adversarial pass

Searches combining the name with *controversy, scandal, allegations, lawsuit, fraud, accusations, conspiracy,* and related terms—including material indexed in 2024–2026—found:

- No lawsuit involving the historical person.
- No credible allegation of personal misconduct.
- No modern criminal or financial investigation.
- No significant conspiracy narrative centered on him.
- Continuing uncritical repetition of the relic-translation miracle.
- Continuing confusion with Patriarch Acacius and the Acacian Schism.
- Continuing disagreement over deathplace, date, manner of execution, and the origin of the Constantinopolitan church.

The absence of scandal claims is unsurprising for a poorly documented third- or fourth-century martyr. The proper “negative” material is therefore the instability and late elaboration of his biography, not invented misconduct.

## Myths, Legends, and Disputed Narratives

### Heavenly voice during torture

**Story:** Christ or a heavenly voice encourages Acacius, paralyzing or astonishing the torturers.

**Appearance:** Developed martyr passion and derivative Orthodox lives.

**Evidence:** No independent corroboration.

**Effect:** Establishes Acacius as a divinely recognized athlete of faith.

**Label:** Hagiographic miracle.

### Angelic prison attendants

**Story:** Radiant youths wash his wounds, feed him, and restore his appearance.

**Appearance:** Developed passion.

**Evidence:** Reported through fellow prisoners within the narrative, but no independent witness account survives.

**Effect:** Makes the prison a sacred space and explains his miraculous recovery.

**Label:** Hagiographic miracle.

### Conversion of prisoners

**Story:** Fellow captives hear the heavenly voice, convert, and ask Acacius to teach them.

**Evidence:** No names or separate martyr records securely attached to the episode.

**Effect:** Portrays Acacius as an evangelist whose suffering produces new believers.

**Label:** Hagiographic claim.

### Flaccinus’ Christian wife

**Story:** The final judge’s Christian wife secretly binds him by oath to limit torture of Christians.

**Evidence:** Only the passion.

**Effect:** Explains why the final judge orders rapid execution and creates a Christian presence inside the persecuting administration.

**Label:** Hagiographic claim.

### The floating lead reliquary

**Story:** Acacius’ bones were sealed in lead, cast into the sea, and floated miraculously to Squillace.

**Appearance:** Calabrian local tradition and liturgical hymn.

**Circulation:** Clergy and civic devotees of Squillace and Guardavalle.

**Evidence for:** Long-established cult, claimed physical relics, annual translation feast, local hymn.

**Evidence against or limiting certainty:** Contradictory dates; no contemporary shipping or translation record; the physically paradoxical floating lead casket is presented expressly as a miracle.

**Effect:** Made Acacius a patron whose sacred body chose Squillace.

**Label:** Relic-translation legend.

### Rejection of the casket at Guardavalle

**Story:** Before reaching Squillace, the reliquary approached Guardavalle. Residents feared diabolical activity and sent it away.

**Evidence:** Local folklore; no early documentary confirmation identified.

**Effect:** Explains both the relic’s destination and Guardavalle’s later acquisition of only an arm relic.

**Label:** Local legend.

### Assistance against sexual temptation

**Story:** Acacius especially aids those struggling with temptations of the flesh, reportedly on the authority of Epiphanius, disciple of Andrew the Fool-for-Christ.

**Evidence:** Later spiritual tradition, not evidence about Acacius’ earthly life.

**Effect:** Added a specialized intercessory function to his cult.

**Label:** Devotional tradition.

### Protection from headaches

In western iconography Acacius may carry or wear a crown of thorns, and he became an intercessor against headache. The association likely arose from his torture, crown imagery, or the broader system assigning particular illnesses to the Fourteen Helpers.

**Label:** Medieval devotional association.

### Maniot resistance symbolism

Modern summaries report that Greek Maniots adopted Acacius’ resistance to pagan Roman authority as a symbol of their own resistance to Ottoman domination and conversion pressure.

No strong primary Maniot documentation was located in the consulted material. The assertion should therefore remain provisional.

**Label:** Reported later tradition.

## Iconography and Symbolic Associations

Acacius appears as:

- A Roman centurion or armored knight.
- A soldier holding a military standard and shield.
- A martyr holding a palm branch.
- A figure wearing or holding a thorny branch or crown.
- A bound prisoner beaten with rods or clubs.
- A kneeling martyr awaiting beheading.
- One member of a collective image of the Fourteen Holy Helpers.
- Occasionally beside another military martyr, such as Theodore.

Images involving mass crucifixion or a vast company of soldiers may represent the legendary Achatius of the Ten Thousand Martyrs rather than Acacius the Centurion.

A sixteenth-century Spanish painting traditionally called *The Martyrdom of Saint Agathius*, attributed to an anonymous Toledo artist, illustrates his later Iberian reception. Attribution of particular paintings varies across catalogues.

## Posthumous Cult and Legacy

### Byzantine world

His strongest early legacy was liturgical and architectural:

- A Constantinopolitan cult was established by approximately 400.
- Chrysostom preached at his shrine.
- The Carya chapel generated an imperial miracle narrative.
- Justinian rebuilt the Church of Acacius.
- Byzantine synaxaria fixed the Cappadocian-centurion biography and commemorated him on 7 May.

### Calabria

At Squillace, Acacius became:

- Patron of the city.
- Patron of the historic diocese.
- Co-patron of the modern archdiocese.
- Center of two annual commemorations.
- Subject of a Latin translation hymn.
- Focus of cathedral relic devotion.
- Patron linked to an old civic-religious fair.

The cult constitutes an important survival of Byzantine religious influence in southern Italy.

### Central and northern Europe

Under the name **Achatius**, he was incorporated into the Fourteen Holy Helpers, a collective devotion that expanded markedly in the later Middle Ages, especially in German-speaking territories during and after the Black Death.

The standardized group generally included:

- Acacius/Achatius.
- Barbara.
- Blaise.
- Catherine.
- Christopher.
- Cyriacus.
- Denis.
- Erasmus.
- Eustace.
- George.
- Giles.
- Margaret.
- Pantaleon.
- Vitus.

Regional substitutions occurred. The collective cult culminated institutionally in shrines such as Vierzehnheiligen in Franconia, associated with visions reported by the shepherd Hermann Leicht beginning in 1446.

### Modern churches

Both Orthodox and Catholic calendars continue to commemorate him. Modern devotional biographies normally reproduce the Metaphrastic narrative without sustained discussion of its date or historical reliability. Academic discussion focuses more narrowly on the identity of the Constantinopolitan church and the discrepancies among early calendars.

## Works by or Attributed to Acacius

### Genuine works

None known.

No surviving book, letter, homily, inscription, diary, prayer, military report, or legal petition can be authenticated as Acacius’ work.

### Speeches attributed within the passion

The passion assigns him:

- Confession before Firmus.
- Refutations of traditional cult.
- Prayers during torture.
- Statements before Bibianus.
- Thanksgiving before execution.

These are embedded literary speeches and should not be catalogued as independently transmitted works by Acacius.

### Official documents associated with his case

The passion includes:

- A report or covering letter from Bibianus to Flaccinus.
- A formal death sentence.

These documents are transmitted only inside the hagiographic text. Their genuine derivation from a trial archive is unproven.

### Liturgical works about him

Not authored by Acacius:

- Byzantine troparion and kontakion for 7 May.
- Synaxarial notice.
- Latin hymn used at Squillace, beginning *Iam sol occidit aureus*.
- Medieval and modern offices, collects, litanies, and invocations of the Fourteen Holy Helpers.

## Primary and Major Textual Sources

### Syriac Breviary or Martyrology

An early Syriac calendar preserved in a manuscript dated 411. Its Nicomedian Acacius notice is fundamental to the critical argument for Nicomedia and 10 May 305.

### Martyrologium Hieronymianum

Composite Latin martyrology traditionally but incorrectly attributed to Jerome. The relevant entry associates Acacius the soldier with Constantinople and Maximus the priest.

### John Chrysostom

Two sermons are connected by their headings or manuscript settings with the church of the martyr Acacius:

- *In illud: Quia quod stultum est Dei* — CPG 4441.14.
- *In martyres omnes* — CPG 4441.15.

The manuscript and attributional history requires specialist examination; their importance here lies chiefly in attesting the cult site.

### Socrates Scholasticus, *Ecclesiastical History*

Relevant passages concern:

- The Church of Acacius as temporary resting place of Constantine’s sarcophagus.
- The Carya chapel, walnut tree, alleged hanging of Acacius, visit of Arcadius, and collapse of the surrounding building.

### Procopius of Caesarea, *On Buildings* (*Περὶ κτισμάτων / De aedificiis*)

Book I reports Justinian’s reconstruction and enlargement of the Church of Acacius.

### Greek Passion of Acacius

- Catalogued as **BHG 13**.
- Preserved in the Metaphrastic corpus.
- Printed in the *Patrologia Graeca* material associated with Symeon Metaphrastes.
- Reprinted and discussed by the Bollandists under 8 May.

This is the source of most biographical detail.

### Oriental versions

The *Encyclopedia of Ancient Christianity* identifies related versions as **BHO 20–21**.

### Acta Sanctorum, May 8

The Bollandist dossier assembles short notices and a longer passion, with Latin translation, variants, commentary, and comparison with martyrologies.

### Roman Martyrology

The traditional 8 May entry summarizes Acacius as a centurion denounced by Firmus, tortured at Perinthus by Bibianus, condemned at Byzantium by Flaccinus, and miraculously translated to Squillace.

## Books and Major Studies About Acacius

### Direct specialist study

- David Woods, “The Church of ‘St.’ Acacius at Constantinople,” *Vigiliae Christianae* 55, no. 2 (2001): 201–207.  
  The most important modern critical study located. Woods compares Syriac, Latin, and Greek evidence, proposes Nicomedia and 10 May 305 for the martyrdom, and separates the martyr from the original founder of the Constantinopolitan church.

### Major reference works and dossiers

- *Acta Sanctorum*, May, volume for 8 May. Greek/Latin hagiographic dossier and critical notes.
- *Bibliotheca Hagiographica Graeca*, entry BHG 13.
- *Bibliotheca Hagiographica Orientalis*, entries BHO 20–21.
- *Encyclopedia of Ancient Christianity*, entry “Acacius,” distinguishing the centurion from numerous namesakes.
- Hippolyte Delehaye, *Les origines du culte des martyrs*. Discusses the formation and geography of martyr cults and reportedly treats Acacius.
- Hippolyte Delehaye and Henri Quentin, *Commentarius perpetuus in Martyrologium Hieronymianum*. Essential for the Latin calendar evidence.
- Scholarly manuals on Byzantine hagiography, especially discussions of martyr passions and Metaphrastic rewriting.

## Atlas Connections

The roster contains Acacius himself and several people connected to his posthumous cult. No documented face-to-face meeting, correspondence, family tie, or contemporary collaboration with another roster member has been established.

### Constantine the Great

**Nature:** Alleged founder of Acacius’ Constantinopolitan church and custodian of his relics.

**Date/place:** Traditionally after Constantine’s victory and development of Constantinople, approximately 320s–330s.

**Evidence label:** **Disputed tradition.**

Later church tradition says Constantine built the sanctuary. Woods argues that a Constantinian official named Acacius founded the church and that the martyr association arose later. Constantine’s sarcophagus was subsequently moved temporarily into the Church of Acacius under Constantius II, but this occurred after Constantine’s death.

### Justinian I

**Nature:** Rebuilder of the Church of Acacius.

**Date/place:** Reign of Justinian, 527–565; Constantinople.

**Evidence label:** **Documented literary fact.**

Procopius reports that Justinian demolished the ruined church and reconstructed it from the foundations on a larger scale.

### Jesus of Nazareth

**Nature:** Central object of Acacius’ confession in the passion; alleged source of healing, strength, and heavenly reward.

**Date/place:** Passion narrative, Thrace and Byzantium, traditionally 303–305.

**Evidence label:** **Hagiographic and theological claim, not a documented historical meeting.**

The passion repeatedly places invocations of Christ in Acacius’ mouth and reports heavenly intervention. There is no claim that the two met during Jesus’ earthly life.

### Saint Andrew the Fool-for-Christ

The roster contains no separate entry under that exact name, but the devotional tradition cited by the Orthodox Church in America attributes Acacius’ assistance against carnal temptation to Epiphanius, a disciple of Andrew the Fool-for-Christ. This is a posthumous devotional chain, not a personal connection.
