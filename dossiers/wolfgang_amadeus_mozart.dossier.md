# Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 24 August 2026  
**Scope:** Documentary life history, works, networks, finances, Freemasonry, Mesmer and Nadia Boulanger interlocks, death, myths, and reception.  
**Method note:** Dates and claims are presented at the level supported by the surviving record. “Documented,” “reported later,” “inference,” “disputed,” and “legend” are distinguished. The Digital Mozart Edition (DME), the *Neue Mozart-Ausgabe* (NMA), Mozart-family correspondence, contemporary registers, and specialist scholarship have priority over popular retellings.

## Basic Identifying Information

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal line

Mozart’s father, **Johann Georg Leopold Mozart** (Augsburg, 14 November 1719–Salzburg, 28 May 1787), was the eldest son of Augsburg master bookbinder **Johann Georg Mozart** and **Anna Maria Sulzer**. The wider Augsburg family included builders and craft masons; those occupational “masons” must not be retroactively conflated with speculative Freemasonry. Leopold studied at the Jesuit St Salvator institutions in Augsburg and at the Benedictine University in Salzburg, was expelled for poor attendance, entered aristocratic service, and joined the Salzburg court orchestra in 1743. He became a violin teacher, composer, copyist, networker, impresario, and author of the internationally influential *Versuch einer gründlichen Violinschule* (1756).

Leopold’s management made the prodigy career possible: he trained both surviving children, arranged passports, letters of introduction, lodgings, instruments, publicity, court access, fees, gifts, copying, and publication. He could be affectionate and intellectually stimulating, but also controlling, suspicious, and emotionally coercive. His surviving letters repeatedly bind obedience, family duty, religion, and professional prudence. The adult Wolfgang’s departure from Salzburg, marriage, and freelance choices produced a long conflict without ending affection or professional exchange.

### Maternal line

Mozart’s mother, **Anna Maria Walburga Pertl** (St Gilgen, 25 December 1720–Paris, 3 July 1778), was the daughter of **Wolfgang Nikolaus Pertl**, an official of the Salzburg prince-archbishop, and **Eva Rosina Barbara Altmann**. Her father’s death left the household dependent on a charity pension. She married Leopold on 21 November 1747. The correspondence shows her literacy, humor, participation in household enterprise, and mediation between father and son; her role was larger than the passive “touring mother” of older biographies.

### Siblings

Leopold and Anna Maria had seven children; five died in infancy. The other survivor was **Maria Anna Walburga Ignatia Mozart**, “Nannerl” (30/31 July 1751–29 October 1829). She was an exceptional keyboard player and toured as a co-prodigy. As she entered marriageable age, social convention ended her public career while her brother’s continued. No securely attributable composition by her survives, although Wolfgang’s letters show that she composed. In 1784 she married **Johann Baptist Franz von Berchtold zu Sonnenburg**, moved to St Gilgen, helped raise his children from earlier marriages, and had three children of her own. Her preserved diary and correspondence are major biographical witnesses.

### Wife and Weber family

Mozart met the musical **Weber family** at Mannheim in 1777–78 and first became enamored of singer **Aloysia Weber**. After she rejected or cooled toward him, he later boarded with the family in Vienna and courted her younger sister **Maria Constanze Cäcilia Josepha Johanna Aloysia Weber** (5 January 1762–6 March 1842). They married at St Stephen’s Cathedral on 4 August 1782, one day before Leopold’s reluctant written consent arrived.

Constanze was a trained singer and sang in private performances, including Mozart’s unfinished C-minor Mass. The marriage correspondence documents affection, erotic play, jealousy, household negotiation, spa absences, and financial strain. Older biographies frequently portrayed her as frivolous or incompetent; the post-1791 record instead shows a capable estate manager who organized benefit concerts, pensions, manuscript sales, publication, commemoration, and—with her second husband **Georg Nikolaus Nissen**—a foundational documentary biography. Her promotion also shaped the Requiem and death legends, sometimes by strategic withholding or polishing of facts.

The Weber network included singers **Josepha Hofer** (first Queen of the Night), **Aloysia Lange**, and **Sophie Haibel**, an important later witness to Mozart’s last illness; brother-in-law **Joseph Lange** painted the unfinished portrait now central to Mozart iconography.

### Children

Wolfgang and Constanze had six children; four died young:

1. **Raimund Leopold** (17 June–19 August 1783).
2. **Carl Thomas** (21 September 1784–31 October 1858), later a government accounting official in Milan; no issue.
3. **Johann Thomas Leopold** (18 October–15 November 1786).
4. **Theresia Constanzia Adelheid Friedericke Maria Anna** (27 December 1787–29 June 1788).
5. **Anna Maria** (born and died 16 November 1789).
6. **Franz Xaver Wolfgang** (26 July 1791–29 July 1844), pianist, composer, teacher in Galicia and Lviv; no acknowledged issue.

Both surviving sons died unmarried and childless, ending Mozart’s direct legitimate line.

## Childhood, Education, and Formation, 1756–1762

There was no ordinary school career. Leopold provided intensive education in keyboard, violin, composition, languages, arithmetic, religion, geography, court manners, and the practicalities of professional music. Nannerl’s music notebook records Wolfgang’s earliest pieces, initially notated by Leopold. The child absorbed music through imitation, memory, improvisation, transposition, sight-reading, and encounters with many regional styles.

The prodigy was real, but the public image was managed. Leopold advertised feats—playing covered keyboards, identifying notes, improvising, reading at sight—and sometimes emphasized Wolfgang’s small age. The family’s long tours were commercial-cultural enterprises as well as education: performances brought cash, jewelry, clothing, snuffboxes, and honorific gifts, while disease, delay, transport, and court calendars made returns uncertain.

## Chronological Life History

### 1762: Munich and Vienna

Leopold first took Nannerl and Wolfgang to Munich early in 1762. In September the family went to Vienna, performing at the Habsburg court before **Maria Theresa**, **Francis I**, and the imperial children. Anecdotes of the six-year-old leaping into Maria Theresa’s lap and proposing marriage to Archduchess Marie Antoinette are later-reported court folklore; the performances and court access are documented, the exact dialogue is not.

### 1763–1766: the Grand Tour

The family left Salzburg on 9 June 1763 and returned 29 November 1766. Their route included Munich, Augsburg, Ludwigsburg, Schwetzingen, Mainz, Frankfurt, Koblenz, Brussels, Paris, London, the Low Countries, Paris again, Switzerland, and German cities. They played for Electors, princes, Louis XV’s circle, and George III’s court.

In Paris, Wolfgang’s violin-and-keyboard sonatas K. 6–9 appeared in print with elite dedications. In London he developed a formative bond with **Johann Christian Bach**, heard Italian opera and orchestral music, met **Karl Friedrich Abel**, and wrote early symphonies. Scientific tests by **Daines Barrington** helped create a contemporaneous record of the prodigy. Serious illnesses affected both children; travel was not an uninterrupted triumph. Receipts varied, and ceremonial gifts were not always liquid assets.

### 1767–1769: Vienna, Olomouc, and first operatic struggles

The family returned to Vienna during a smallpox epidemic. Wolfgang contracted the disease at Olomouc and recovered. He composed the Latin intermezzo *Apollo et Hyacinthus* (K. 38), the German Singspiel *Bastien und Bastienne* (K. 50), and the opera buffa *La finta semplice* (K. 51). The last became entangled in theatrical rivalry and doubts—possibly encouraged by competitors—about whether a child had really written it. A projected Vienna production failed; it was later performed in Salzburg.

#### Mesmer interlock

Physician **Franz Anton Mesmer** and his wealthy wife moved in Vienna’s musical-patronage world. Nissen’s posthumous biography says *Bastien und Bastienne* was performed in Mesmer’s garden theater in 1768. No contemporary program, letter, score annotation, or eyewitness record verifies that performance; the earliest securely documented staging is Berlin, 1890. The appropriate status is **plausible early biographical tradition, unproven**. Mozart later made Mesmer culturally immortal in *Così fan tutte* (1790), when Despina, disguised as a doctor, cures the feigned poisoned lovers with a magnet and invokes the famous doctor’s method. Andrew Steptoe’s specialist study treats Mesmer’s place in the biography while retaining the uncertainty: [“Mozart, Mesmer and ‘Così fan tutte’”](https://www.jstor.org/stable/735887); a modern edition discussion likewise notes the lack of proof: [Carus, *Bastien und Bastienne*](https://blog.carus-verlag.com/en/opera/wolfgang-amadeus-mozart-bastien-and-bastienne/).

### 1769–1773: three Italian journeys

Mozart and Leopold made three Italian expeditions: December 1769–March 1771; August–December 1771; October 1772–March 1773. They visited Verona, Milan, Bologna, Florence, Rome, Naples, Turin, Venice, and other centers.

In Bologna Wolfgang studied counterpoint with **Padre Giovanni Battista Martini** and, after an examination whose surviving exercise shows corrections/assistance, was admitted to the Accademia Filarmonica. Pope Clement XIV awarded him the **Order of the Golden Spur** in Rome in 1770. The often-told Sistine Chapel story—that he heard Allegri’s *Miserere* once and wrote it out from memory—originates close to the event in Leopold’s reporting, but later versions exaggerate both papal secrecy and the idea of a single unaided hearing.

Milan commissioned *Mitridate, re di Ponto* (1770), *Ascanio in Alba* (1771), and *Lucio Silla* (1772). *Mitridate* succeeded despite rehearsal resistance. The hoped-for permanent Italian appointment did not materialize. Maria Theresa’s negative advice to her son Ferdinand against hiring itinerant “useless people” likely harmed the Milan prospect.

### 1773–1777: Salzburg service and frustrated advancement

After Prince-Archbishop **Sigismund von Schrattenbach** died, **Hieronymus Colloredo** became ruler. Mozart served as court Konzertmeister (the 1769 appointment had initially been honorary/unpaid; it became salaried). He composed symphonies, serenades, divertimenti, concertos, masses, and liturgical works for local institutions. The family moved in 1773 from Getreidegasse to the Tanzmeisterhaus on Hannibalplatz (now Makartplatz).

Trips to Vienna (1773) exposed Mozart to Haydn’s newer quartet style; Munich brought the successful *La finta giardiniera* (1775). Yet Salzburg offered limited opera and tight discipline. Mozart sought posts in Munich and elsewhere without success. In 1777 Colloredo dismissed him after a petition to travel; Leopold could not leave without risking his own position.

### 1777–1779: Augsburg, Mannheim, Paris, loss, and return

Wolfgang traveled with his mother through Munich and Augsburg to Mannheim. In Augsburg he renewed contact with cousin **Maria Anna Thekla Mozart**, recipient of the famous “Bäsle letters.” Their intimate, punning, sexual and scatological language is documented; a sexual relationship is possible but **unproven**. Diagnostic claims of Tourette syndrome based on the letters are retrospective speculation, weakened by the conventional and family-shared culture of scatological humor. A psychiatric review identifies the evidentiary problem: [Möller, “Do features of Mozart’s letter-writing style indicate the presence of a neuropsychiatric disorder?”](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24337967/).

Mannheim’s orchestra, winds, crescendos, and virtuosi transformed Mozart’s orchestral imagination. Important contacts included **Christian Cannabich**, the Wendling family, the Weber family, and Aloysia Weber. Mozart proposed traveling to Italy with Aloysia; Leopold ordered him toward Paris.

In Paris he obtained commissions but no permanent office. Anna Maria became ill and died on 3 July 1778. Wolfgang delayed telling Leopold directly, first preparing him through a letter to family friend Abbé Bullinger. His Paris works include the Symphony K. 297 (“Paris”), the flute-and-harp concerto K. 299, ballet music *Les petits riens*, and instrumental works; attribution and commissioning disputes surround some flute music. After further Mannheim and Munich stops—and Aloysia’s rejection—he returned to Salzburg in January 1779 as court organist at 450 florins annually.

### 1779–1781: court organist, *Idomeneo*, and rupture

His duties included cathedral, court, and chamber music. A Munich commission produced *Idomeneo, re di Creta* (premièred 29 January 1781), a major synthesis of Italian opera seria, French spectacle, Mannheim orchestral color, and psychologically active ensembles.

Colloredo summoned his household to Vienna. Mozart resented being treated as a servant, restrictions on independent performance, and denial of permission to play lucrative engagements. In May–June 1781 escalating confrontations ended with chief steward **Count Karl Joseph Arco** dismissing him; Mozart’s phrase that Arco delivered a “kick in the arse” is his own angry report, not an independently witnessed literal finding. Leopold feared the destruction of court patronage. Wolfgang stayed in Vienna as a freelancer—an unusually risky but not unique career model.

### 1781–1784: Vienna freelancer, marriage, and ascent

Mozart rented rooms, taught elite pupils, published by subscription, organized academy concerts, improvised, performed new piano concertos, and negotiated opera commissions. *Die Entführung aus dem Serail* (1782) succeeded at the Burgtheater. Emperor Joseph II’s alleged judgment “too many notes” comes from a later source and should be treated as a polished anecdote, not stenographic fact.

He married Constanze in 1782. A 1783 Salzburg visit did not fully reconcile Leopold and Constanze; the unfinished Mass in C minor K. 427 was performed there, with Constanze probably singing. Mozart’s encounter with Baron **Gottfried van Swieten** and manuscripts of Bach and Handel deepened his contrapuntal practice. His friendship with **Joseph Haydn** culminated in six string quartets dedicated to Haydn (published 1785), whose famous praise to Leopold—reported by Leopold—called Wolfgang the greatest composer known to him in person or by name.

### 1784–1786: peak concert economy and Freemasonry

Mozart kept his own thematic catalogue from February 1784. He was at the center of Vienna’s concert season, often supplying a new piano concerto for each subscription academy. Income could be very high: performance receipts, subscriptions, lessons, commissions, gifts, publication, and fees. Expenses—large apartments, servants, clothing, carriage hire, entertaining, medical/spa costs, and uncertain collecting—were also high. “Poor all his life” is false; “financially secure” is also false.

On 14 December 1784 Mozart was initiated as an Entered Apprentice in **Zur Wohltätigkeit** (“Beneficence/Charity”). He advanced to Fellow Craft at **Zur wahren Eintracht** (“True Concord”) on 7 January 1785 and became Master soon afterward. Surviving lodge attendance material places him repeatedly at True Concord, led by mineralogist and Enlightenment intellectual **Ignaz von Born**. Leopold entered Masonry during his 1785 visit. Imperial regulation consolidated lodges in late 1785; Mozart’s affiliation passed into **Zur neugekrönten Hoffnung** (“New Crowned Hope”). The King’s College London Mozart-material-culture database summarizes the lodge record and K. 477 performance: [“True Concord”](https://mmc.kdl.kcl.ac.uk/entities/institution/true-concord-masonic-lodge/index.html).

With librettist **Lorenzo Da Ponte**, Mozart made *Le nozze di Figaro* (Vienna, 1 May 1786), adapted from Beaumarchais. Imperial censorship removed the play’s explicit political speech; Da Ponte reframed social conflict through sexual intrigue, forgiveness, and ensemble action. Claims that the opera was simply “banned by aristocrats” compress a mixed reception: it had nine Vienna performances in 1786, was revived, and became a sensation in Prague.

### 1787: Prague, *Don Giovanni*, death of Leopold, court appointment

Mozart visited Prague, where *Figaro* was popular, and premiered the “Prague” Symphony K. 504. Leopold died on 28 May. Wolfgang did not attend the funeral; distance, work, their strained relationship, and eighteenth-century travel conditions all matter, and no single motive is documented.

*Don Giovanni* premièred at Prague’s Estates Theatre on 29 October 1787. Mozart then received the imperial appointment **k.k. Kammermusicus** (Imperial-Royal chamber composer), succeeding Gluck, with an annual salary of 800 florins, largely for dance music. The title was an honor and base income but not the grand Kapellmeister post he had sought.

### 1788–1790: shrinking public market, loans, and travel

The last three symphonies—E-flat K. 543, G minor K. 550, C major “Jupiter” K. 551—were entered in his catalogue in summer 1788. The old claim that he never heard them performed is not proven; evidence is insufficient to establish the exact occasions.

From 1788 Mozart wrote repeated loan appeals to Masonic brother **Michael Puchberg**—roughly twenty surviving requests. They document immediate cash crises, rent/moving problems, promised repayments, and reliance on fraternal friendship. They do not by themselves prove destitution; incomplete income, repayment, and private-credit records prevent a clean balance sheet. Performance was his most important income source through 1786, after which public opportunities contracted: [Dexter Edge, “Mozart and Finances,” in *Mozart in Context*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/mozart-in-context/mozart-and-finances/3C5E1ABE57A34457F6EFA312BF00573B).

Possible contributors include war-related contraction of Viennese aristocratic spending, fewer subscription concerts, high household costs, Constanze’s treatments at Baden, moves, unstable receivables, and Mozart’s consumption. Gambling losses are often asserted but not quantifiable from surviving evidence. There was no bankruptcy proceeding or criminal financial case.

In 1789 he traveled with Prince **Karl Lichnowsky** through Prague, Dresden, Leipzig, Potsdam, and Berlin, seeking performances, gifts, commissions, and perhaps a post. Documentary returns are smaller than the later legend of lavish Frederick William II commissions. *Così fan tutte* premièred 26 January 1790 but its run was interrupted by Joseph II’s death and theater closure. Mozart went to Frankfurt for Leopold II’s coronation at his own risk; the trip produced performances but limited financial rescue.

### 1791: final resurgence

1791 was productive and may have been financially improving. Mozart wrote the piano concerto K. 595, string quintet K. 614, motet *Ave verum corpus* K. 618, *Die Zauberflöte* K. 620, *La clemenza di Tito* K. 621, clarinet concerto K. 622 for **Anton Stadler**, Masonic cantata K. 623, and the unfinished Requiem K. 626.

*La clemenza di Tito*, commissioned for Leopold II’s Prague coronation as king of Bohemia, was composed under severe time pressure with **Caterino Mazzolà** revising Metastasio. *Die Zauberflöte*, with actor-impresario-librettist **Emanuel Schikaneder**, opened 30 September at the Freihaus-Theater auf der Wieden. Mozart conducted early performances; Josepha Hofer sang the Queen of the Night and Schikaneder Papageno. It became a mounting popular success.

An intermediary commissioned the Requiem for **Count Franz von Walsegg**, who wanted a memorial work for his deceased wife and maintained anonymity. Mozart received an advance. After his death Constanze needed a completed score to collect the balance; Joseph Eybler attempted portions, then Franz Xaver Süssmayr completed the standard version. Mozart fully scored the Introit; left vocal/bass and instrumental indications of varying completeness through the Sequence and Offertory; the Lacrimosa breaks after eight bars. The Sanctus, Benedictus, and Agnus Dei in the received version are Süssmayr’s, although dependence on lost Mozart sketches remains unresolved.

## Employment, Institutions, Projects, and Finances

| Period | Institution/model | Position and economic structure |
|---|---|---|
| 1763–1773 | Family touring enterprise | Leopold managed performances, gifts, fees, publication, travel, and education; Wolfgang had no independent business identity. |
| 1769/1770–1777 | Salzburg prince-archiepiscopal court | Honorary then salaried Konzertmeister; composition and performance duties. |
| 1779–1781 | Salzburg court | Court and cathedral organist, 450 florins yearly; resignation/dismissal in Vienna. |
| 1781–1787 | Vienna freelance market | Lessons, academies, subscriptions, commissions, publication, private performance, gifts; high potential income and high volatility. |
| 1787–1791 | Habsburg court plus freelance work | Imperial-Royal chamber composer, 800 florins annually; dance commissions plus independent teaching, theater, publishing, and concerts. |

Mozart did not own a manufacturing company, patent technology, or long-lived publishing firm. His “enterprise” was a portfolio of intellectual and performance labor. He negotiated copy ownership and exclusivity in a musical market before modern copyright. Manuscript copying and unauthorized circulation reduced control. Publishers included **Artaria**, **Torricella**, **Hoffmeister**, **Breitkopf**, and others.

His instruments were working capital: fortepianos, a clavichord, violins, viola, and billiard table. He understood subscription marketing and programmed himself as both composer and virtuoso. Surviving figures are fragmentary and currencies/values resist modern conversion. Claims of enormous yearly income generally extrapolate good seasons; claims of absolute poverty overread loan letters. At death the estate had liabilities, but also valuable manuscripts, commissions, household goods, receivables, a court salary, and rapidly exploitable reputation. Constanze obtained an imperial pension and raised money through memorial concerts.

## Religious, Intellectual, Artistic, and Masonic Development

### Catholic formation

Mozart grew within Salzburg Catholicism, wrote Masses, litanies, offertories, vespers, church sonatas, and devotional works, attended sacraments, married in church, and received Catholic funeral rites. His letters invoke Providence and moral duty, sometimes conventionally, sometimes intensely. Freemasonry did not simply replace Catholic belief. Vienna’s reform Catholicism, Josephinism, and Enlightenment Masonry overlapped in personnel and ethics even as papal condemnations made the relationship institutionally tense.

### Freemasonic association: documented core

**Documented:** proposal/initiation in Beneficence (14 December 1784); Fellow Craft at True Concord (7 January 1785); Master soon after; attendance at True Concord; Leopold’s initiation; lodge consolidation; Masonic patrons, performers, lenders, and collaborators. Important brothers in Mozart’s orbit included **Ignaz von Born**, **Otto von Gemmingen**, **Michael Puchberg**, **Anton Stadler**, **Joseph Lange**, and **Karl Lichnowsky**. Schikaneder had been a Mason, though his precise active status in 1791 is complex. Haydn was initiated once and did not proceed through the degrees.

Masonry offered sociability across estate boundaries, mutual aid, concert subscribers, intellectual discussion, ritual experience, and commissions. Mozart’s loan rhetoric to Puchberg invoked brotherhood. This network should not be inflated into a secret government or intelligence service: no evidence establishes espionage, revolutionary conspiracy, or an order to kill Mozart.

### Explicit and occasion-specific Masonic music

The secure core includes:

- **K. 468, *Gesellenreise* (“Die ihr einem neuen Grade”)** (1785), for advancement to a new degree.
- **K. 471, *Die Maurerfreude*** (1785), honoring Ignaz von Born.
- **K. 477/479a, *Maurerische Trauermusik*** (1785), performed in memory of Duke Georg August of Mecklenburg-Strelitz and Count Franz Esterházy; dark winds, basset horns, and cantus-firmus associations give it a ritual memorial character.
- **K. 483, “Zerfließet heut, geliebte Brüder”** and **K. 484, “Ihr unsre neuen Leiter”** (1785/86), lodge opening/closing songs tied to reorganization.
- **K. 623, *Laut verkünde unsre Freude* (Kleine Freimaurer-Kantate)** (1791), performed 18 November for dedication of a lodge temple; Mozart’s last completed catalogue entry.
- **K. 623a, “Laßt uns mit geschlungnen Händen”**: traditionally attached as concluding chorus; attribution/history requires caution.
- **K. 429/468a, *Dir, Seele des Weltalls***: fragmentary and chronologically/ritually debated.
- **K. 619, “Die ihr des unermeßlichen Weltalls Schöpfer ehrt”**: Enlightenment/cosmopolitan cantata for Franz Heinrich Ziegenhagen, Masonic-adjacent rather than a simple lodge ritual piece.
- Earlier K. 148/125h is sometimes listed as Masonic, but predates Mozart’s initiation; text/occasion, not membership, governs the classification.

The list and basic lodge history are summarized by the Museum of Freemasonry and the Berliner Philharmoniker: [Museum of Freemasonry](https://museumfreemasonry.org.uk/blog/learn-about-freemasonry-was-mozart-freemason), [Berliner Philharmoniker](https://www.berliner-philharmoniker.de/en/stories/mozart-and-freemasonry/).

### *The Magic Flute*: Masonic themes, evidence, and limits

The opera is not a transcript of a lodge initiation, yet Masonic-Enlightenment structure is pervasive:

- **Initiation:** Tamino and Pamina undergo silence, separation, ordeals of fire and water, then admission to a community of wisdom.
- **Light/darkness:** movement from deceptive appearances toward tested knowledge; solar imagery and the triumph of light.
- **Brotherhood, virtue, constancy, discretion:** explicit ethical vocabulary overlaps lodge ideals.
- **Temple and priests:** Sarastro’s domain, councils, ceremonial marches, and antiphonal responses evoke ritual society.
- **Number three:** three opening chords, three Ladies, three Boys, three temples, repeated triple groupings. In the eighteenth-century Masonic context, three is relevant; it is also a normal operatic, Christian, fairy-tale, and formal number. Each occurrence is not independently proof of a cipher.
- **E-flat major / three flats:** often read as Masonic. The tonal plan is much more complex; C minor, F major, and other keys carry dramatic functions. Key signatures cannot be reduced to secret code.
- **Instrumentation:** trombones, basset horns/clarinets, low male voices, hymn-like chorales, fugue/learned counterpoint, dotted ceremonial rhythms, and march topics construct solemn ritual sound.
- **Gender and hierarchy:** the libretto voices misogynistic maxims and excludes women from the priestly order, yet Pamina proves equal to Tamino in the climactic trials. Scholarship disputes whether the opera confirms, revises, or exposes lodge gender ideology.
- **Politics:** readings range from general Josephinian Enlightenment to portraits of Ignaz von Born, reactions to lodge restriction, anticlericalism, or defense of reformed monarchy. No single one-to-one political allegory is documentarily proven.

Recent scholarship treats Masonic interpretation as indispensable but not exclusive, alongside fairy tale, popular theater, Christianity, Egyptianizing spectacle, Orphic myth, and Enlightenment pedagogy: [*The Cambridge Companion to The Magic Flute*, sources/types/tropes](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-companion-to-the-magic-flute/partial-derivatives-sources-types-and-tropes-in-the-magic-flute/AB9A53DAF6D4B2556B97323ADA8243AC), [music as stagecraft](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-companion-to-the-magic-flute/music-as-stagecraft/3A4681B56F7F4F04A175CD1DAD7CDB80), and [approaches and perspectives](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-companion-to-the-magic-flute/approaches-and-perspectives/F61F8CDD4EA6C1E250C0F9F21B3B962D).

### Artistic development

Mozart’s development was absorptive rather than a march toward isolation: Salzburg sacred and serenade traditions; J. C. Bach’s galant concerto style; Italian opera; Martini’s counterpoint; Mannheim orchestral discipline and winds; Haydn’s thematic and quartet craft; Bach and Handel through van Swieten; French spectacle; Viennese keyboard virtuosity; German Singspiel; and the individual capacities of singers and instrumentalists. He often composed for specific bodies and personalities—Stadler’s clarinet/basset clarinet, Leutgeb’s horn, Nancy Storace’s Susanna, Aloysia Lange’s high soprano, Anton Raaff’s Idomeneo.

## Important Relationships and Networks

- **Leopold Mozart:** teacher, manager, editor, moral authority, critic; later conflict over autonomy and marriage.
- **Nannerl Mozart:** co-prodigy, correspondent, keyboard peer, custodian of family memory.
- **Constanze Mozart:** spouse, singer, household partner, widow-entrepreneur, architect of reception.
- **Joseph Haydn:** admired older composer, quartet interlocutor, chamber-music partner; relationship evidenced beyond later sentimentality.
- **Johann Christian Bach:** London mentor/model; Mozart retained esteem for him.
- **Padre Martini:** Italian counterpoint teacher and credentialing patron.
- **Lorenzo Da Ponte:** librettist of *Figaro*, *Don Giovanni*, *Così*; collaboration joined theatrical pragmatism and musical dramaturgy.
- **Emanuel Schikaneder:** actor-manager, Papageno, librettist/producer of *Zauberflöte*; commercial popular-theater partner and Masonic interlock.
- **Gottfried van Swieten:** patron of Bach/Handel study and arranger commissions.
- **Anton Stadler:** clarinetist and Masonic brother; dedicatee of K. 581 and K. 622. Later claim that Stadler pawned Mozart’s manuscripts/instruments rests partly on hostile or incomplete evidence; debts between them are documented, exact losses disputed.
- **Michael Puchberg:** merchant, Masonic brother, lender; the loan letters are crucial financial documents.
- **Antonio Salieri:** powerful court composer and administrator; sometimes competitor, sometimes participant in performances and supporter of Mozart’s music. Documentary evidence does not sustain murderer or lifelong arch-enemy narratives.
- **Emperor Joseph II:** institutional patron and theater reformer; commissioned or enabled important work but did not make Mozart principal court composer.
- **Archbishop Colloredo / Count Arco:** employer and steward in the decisive 1781 rupture.
- **Prague network:** Duscheks, Bondini company, orchestra, and audiences gave Mozart major operatic success.
- **Women performers/pupils:** Josepha Auernhammer, Barbara Ployer, Nancy Storace, Catarina Cavalieri, Aloysia Lange, Josepha Hofer, and others were not incidental muses; works preserve their technique and professional agency.

### Nadia Boulanger interlock

There can be **no personal association**: **Nadia Boulanger (1887–1979)** was born ninety-six years after Mozart’s death. The interlock is a posthumous pedagogical lineage. Boulanger’s teaching centered on disciplined harmony, counterpoint, score reading, formal proportion, voice leading, and the ability to justify every note. Mozart supplied exemplary solutions within that curriculum, even though Bach, Monteverdi, Fauré, Stravinsky, and others were equally or more publicly emblematic of her canon.

The most concrete circulated Mozart–Boulanger anecdote comes from **Philip Glass**: during a lesson she reached for Mozart to show that, in a comparable voice-leading situation, Mozart resolved an upper note to the third rather than the tonic—an example of rule understood through compositional judgment, not mechanical obedience. This is a pupil’s recollection and pedagogical exemplum, not evidence for a distinctive “Mozart school” of Boulanger. Through pupils such as Glass, Aaron Copland, Elliott Carter, Quincy Jones, and many others, her Mozart-based close reading became part of twentieth-century composition pedagogy. Context on her teaching reach: [American Symphony Orchestra, “Nadia Boulanger: Teacher of the Century”](https://americansymphony.org/concert-notes/nadia-boulanger-teacher-of-the-century/) and [Oxford University Press blog](https://blog.oup.com/2016/02/nadia-boulanger-teaching-stravinsky-david-conte/).

## Controversies, Allegations, and Disputed Questions

### Authorship and prodigy manufacture

Rivals in Vienna questioned whether Leopold wrote the child’s music. Surviving autographs, stylistic development, revisions, independent tests, and the later working record establish Wolfgang’s authorship in general. Leopold sometimes notated, corrected, revised, or collaborated; individual juvenilia have complex hands and attributions. “Entirely fabricated prodigy” is unsupported; “untouched spontaneous genius” is equally misleading.

### Scatology, sexuality, and retrospective diagnosis

The vulgar letters and canons are genuine, though publishers long bowdlerized them and some bawdy canon texts have complicated transmission. Their language belongs to familial wordplay, south-German comic culture, inversion, and possibly erotic intimacy. Claims of Tourette syndrome, mania, autism, ADHD, or specific paraphilia cannot be clinically established from selected letters two centuries later. The scholarship on bawdy canons places them in performance culture rather than treating them as a transparent psychiatric chart: [David J. Buch, “Mozart’s Bawdy Canons”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/eighteenth-century-music/article/mozarts-bawdy-canons-vulgarity-and-debauchery-at-the-wiednertheater/9390E182B74E7809A743E66899F3CA36).

### Women and sexual conduct

Documented: courtship of Aloysia; marriage to Constanze; flirtatious/sexualized letters; the Bäsle correspondence. Alleged affairs—with pupils, singers, or others—remain largely rumor or biographical inference. There is no adjudicated sexual crime. The operas contain gender stereotypes, coercion, class power, and forgiveness plots characteristic of their sources and institutions; these are textual facts, not proof of Mozart’s private conduct.

### Plagiarism and borrowing

Mozart borrowed styles, topics, themes, and procedures in normal eighteenth-century practice; the DME/NMA documents sources and doubtful works. The opening resemblance between *Bastien und Bastienne* and Beethoven’s later “Eroica” is coincidence or indirect common vocabulary, not Beethoven copying a then-unpublished Mozart score. The complete critical edition is available through [NMA Online](https://dme.mozarteum.at/DME/nma/start.php?l=2).

### Politics

Mozart held no elected office, organized party, or documented revolutionary cell. *Figaro* engages estate hierarchy; *Zauberflöte* engages enlightened authority and initiation; the Masonic milieu advocated education, tolerance, fraternity, and reform. Attempts to label him straightforwardly Jacobin, conservative monarchist propagandist, anti-clerical conspirator, or state intelligence actor exceed the evidence.

### No criminal or corruption record

No substantiated record establishes fraud, money laundering, political violence, espionage, criminal conviction, or abuse by Mozart. Ordinary disputes involved employment, fees, loans, theatrical competition, copying, and attribution. Walsegg’s intended presentation of the Requiem under his own name belongs to the patron’s practice, while Constanze’s secret completion arrangements were commercial estate protection, not a formal fraud prosecution.

## Illness, Death, and Burial

Mozart experienced childhood smallpox and intermittent fevers/illnesses recorded during travel. Claims of lifelong rheumatic fever, chronic kidney disease, syphilis, poisoning, or a particular congenital syndrome are reconstructions.

In mid-November 1791 he became seriously ill with fever, swelling/edema, pain, weakness, and later incapacity. Witness accounts were written at different distances from the event and do not perfectly agree. Physicians **Thomas Franz Closset** and **Matthias von Sallaba** attended. Treatments probably included the period’s medicines and bloodletting, but exact regimens are not fully recoverable. No autopsy was performed.

Proposed modern diagnoses number well over a hundred: streptococcal infection with post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, rheumatic fever, epidemic infection, nephritic syndrome, trichinosis, influenza, tuberculosis, and others. A major medical review emphasizes the uncertainty: [Jan Hirschmann, “What Killed Mozart?”](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/article-abstract/648393). The only secure conclusion is a rapidly fatal natural illness with fever and edema; the specific cause remains unresolved.

On 6 December the death register recorded a third-class funeral. Later accounts place the coffin’s blessing at the Crucifix Chapel beside St Stephen’s and transport to St Marx. Regulations and custom limited the procession; the familiar storm, abandoned dog, and lonely pauper-cart imagery developed later. Mozart was buried in an unmarked shaft grave with several other bodies, a normal practice for non-aristocratic Viennese of his social level. “Common grave” is therefore defensible if carefully defined; **mass grave** and **pauper’s grave** are misleading. The Mozarteum’s burial summary is explicit on this distinction: [Mozarteum biography and family portal](https://mozarteum.at/en/wolfgang-amade-mozart/).

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. Salieri poisoned Mozart

**Story:** jealous Antonio Salieri poisoned the divinely gifted rival.  
**Appearance/circulation:** poisoning rumors circulated soon after the death; the named Salieri story gained durable form in Alexander Pushkin’s *Mozart and Salieri* (1830), Rimsky-Korsakov’s opera (1897/98), Peter Shaffer’s *Amadeus* (1979), and Miloš Forman’s film (1984).  
**Evidence:** Mozart reportedly feared poisoning during illness, but late testimony is unstable. No autopsy, toxicological evidence, confession, charge, or reliable eyewitness implicates Salieri. Salieri interacted professionally with Mozart and later taught Franz Xaver Mozart.  
**Status:** **legend unsupported by documentary evidence**.  
**Reputational effect:** transformed Mozart into innocent natural genius and Salieri into the archetype of murderous mediocrity, obscuring both careers. The transmission is summarized by [Handel and Haydn Society](https://handelandhaydn.org/amadeus-fact-fiction/) and [Wisconsin Public Radio](https://www.wpr.org/culture/salieri-poisoning-mozart).

### 2. The gray messenger and Mozart’s own funeral music

**Story:** a supernatural cloaked stranger commissions a Requiem; Mozart realizes he is writing his own death mass.  
**Origin:** the real commissioner, Walsegg, used an intermediary and concealed his identity. Constanze and early biographers helped dramatize the episode; later literature and *Amadeus* fused the messenger with Salieri and the Commendatore.  
**Evidence:** anonymous commission and advance are historical; Walsegg’s memorial motive and attribution practice are recoverable. Mozart’s conviction that the mass was for himself rests on later recollection.  
**Status:** **documented secret commission plus later supernatural/psychological legend**.  
**Effect:** made the unfinished Requiem an autobiographical death artifact and concealed the collaborative completion.

### 3. Pauper’s mass grave

**Story:** Vienna discarded an impoverished, forgotten genius in a lime-filled mass grave.  
**Origin:** nineteenth-century genius-and-neglect narrative, strengthened by visual culture and Forman’s film.  
**Evidence:** ordinary third-class funeral; shared unmarked shaft grave; exact grave subsequently lost. This was not proof of pauper status.  
**Status:** **misleading transformation of normal burial practice**.  
**Effect:** supplies the canonical tragic ending and underwrites accusations that Vienna failed him.

### 4. Constanze and friends abandoned the funeral

**Story:** nobody accompanied Mozart because he was unloved; alternatively a storm drove mourners away.  
**Evidence:** later accounts conflict over who attended and how far; funeral custom and regulation did not require a family procession to the cemetery. Weather reconstructions cannot recover individual motive.  
**Status:** **unresolved attendance details; moralized abandonment story not proved**.

### 5. Mozart’s skull

**Story:** a gravedigger preserved Mozart’s skull, eventually acquired by the Mozarteum.  
**Evidence:** provenance has gaps. DNA comparison in 2006 failed because supposed female-family remains were not mutually related, so it neither authenticated nor definitively excluded the skull. Dental/provenance discrepancies remain.  
**Status:** **unverified relic**.  
**Effect:** created a scientific-relic cult and repeated facial reconstructions. See [Live Science, DNA result](https://www.livescience.com/532-mystery-mozart-skull-unsolved.html) and a recent forensic discussion of the disputed skull: [facial approximation study](https://czasopisma.uni.lodz.pl/ar/article/download/24000/25138?inline=1).

### 6. “Too many notes”

**Story:** Joseph II dismisses *Entführung* with “Too beautiful for our ears, and far too many notes.”  
**Evidence:** transmitted in Niemetschek’s biography, not a contemporary transcript; wording varies. It may preserve court criticism sharpened into anecdote.  
**Status:** **early biographical anecdote, not verbatim secure**.  
**Effect:** emblem of philistine authority confronting genius.

### 7. Childlike idiot versus effortless vessel

The letters show childish games, vulgar humor, impulsiveness, and play; they also show acute negotiation, rehearsal management, memory, stylistic analysis, and long-range compositional planning. Shaffer/Forman’s giggling savant is a brilliant dramatic construction, not a balanced clinical portrait. Autographs contain corrections, sketches, discarded solutions, and planning; the myth that complete works arrived without labor is false.

### 8. Masonic murder or disclosure

**Story:** Masons killed Mozart for revealing lodge secrets in *The Magic Flute*, or Catholic/Jesuit enemies killed him for Masonry.  
**Appearance:** nineteenth- and twentieth-century conspiracy literature.  
**Evidence:** no threat record, investigation, forensic evidence, or credible insider testimony. Masons participated in his support and memorial culture; the opera was performed publicly with Masonic collaborators.  
**Status:** **conspiracy narrative without evidence**.  
**Effect:** recasts symbolism as dangerous encrypted revelation.

### Sacred and choral works (selection)

Masses include K. 49, 65, 66, 139 (“Waisenhaus”), 167, 192, 220 (“Spatzenmesse”), 257 (“Credo”), 262, 275, 317 (“Coronation”), 337; unfinished **Mass in C minor K. 427**; **Requiem in D minor K. 626**, unfinished and completed principally by Süssmayr after an earlier Eybler attempt. ### Symphonies and orchestral music

Modern attribution yields roughly forty-one numbered symphonies but the numbering is obsolete as a complete count. Central works include K. 16 (E-flat, London), K. 183 (G minor), K. 201 (A), K. 297 (“Paris”), K. 318, K. 319, K. 338, K. 385 (“Haffner”), K. 425 (“Linz”), K. 504 (“Prague”), and the 1788 trilogy K. 543, 550, 551 (“Jupiter”). Serenades/divertimenti include K. 239 (“Serenata notturna”), K. 250 (“Haffner”), K. 320 (“Posthorn”), K. 361/370a (“Gran Partita”), K. 525 (*Eine kleine Nachtmusik*).

### Concertos

Twenty-seven conventionally numbered piano concertos include early arrangements and mature originals; central Vienna concertos include K. 449, 450, 451, 453, 456, 459, 466, 467, 482, 488, 491, 503, 537, 595. Five authentic violin concertos K. 207, 211, 216, 218, 219; *Sinfonia concertante* for violin/viola K. 364; horn concertos for Leutgeb (transmission incomplete); flute, oboe, bassoon, flute-and-harp K. 299, and clarinet concerto K. 622. Some traditional wind-concerto attributions and versions remain disputed.

### Chamber and keyboard music

String quartets include the six dedicated to Haydn (K. 387, 421, 428, 458, 464, 465), “Hoffmeister” K. 499, and three “Prussian” quartets K. 575, 589, 590. String quintets K. 174, 406, 515, 516, 593, 614; clarinet quintet K. 581; piano quartets K. 478 and 493; violin sonatas; piano trios; divertimenti; oboe quartet K. 370; horn quintet K. 407. Keyboard works include sonatas K. 279–284, 309–311, 330–333, 457, 533/494, 545, 570, 576; variations, fantasies K. 396/397/475, rondos, fugues, and four-hand/two-piano music.

### Songs, canons, dances, arrangements

Lieder include *Das Veilchen* K. 476, *Abendempfindung* K. 523, *An Chloe* K. 524, and *Sehnsucht nach dem Frühlinge* K. 596. Canons range from devotional/social to bawdy; authentic text and musical attribution must be checked separately. Court dances occupy a significant part of the late catalogue. Mozart arranged Handel’s *Messiah*, *Acis and Galatea*, *Alexander’s Feast*, and *Ode for St Cecilia’s Day* for van Swieten’s circle; these are arrangements, not original compositions.

### Writings

Mozart authored no treatise or memoir. His genuine prose corpus consists chiefly of **letters**, contracts/receipts and inscriptions, and his **Verzeichnüss aller meiner Werke** (autograph thematic catalogue, 1784–1791). Major editions include:

- *Mozart. Briefe und Aufzeichnungen*, ed. Wilhelm A. Bauer, Otto Erich Deutsch, Joseph Heinz Eibl (7 vols., 1962–75 and commentary/index).
- *The Letters of Mozart and His Family*, trans./ed. Emily Anderson (1938; revised editions).
- *Mozart’s Letters, Mozart’s Life*, ed./trans. Robert Spaethling (2000), selected and modernized.

Posthumous edited collections must not be mistaken for books “written” by Mozart. Family letters are often joint documentary products, and older editions silently censored scatology.

### Hostile, revisionist, and myth-critical traditions

Schlichtegroll and Nissen created rival early emphases; nineteenth-century romantic biographies isolated genius from commerce; psychoanalytic lives emphasized father conflict; social histories restored institutions, women, markets, and servants; performance scholarship reexamined instruments and improvisation. “Hostile biography” is less a single genre here than recurring portrayals of Leopold as exploiter, Constanze as spendthrift, Mozart as infantile libertine, or Vienna as murderer-by-neglect. Each rests on selective use of the same letters and legends.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Constanze, Nannerl, the sons, Nissen, publishers, and benefit-concert organizers converted fragile estate materials into a European cult. The first collected edition (Breitkopf & Härtel, 1798 onward) and Köchel catalogue stabilized the corpus. Salzburg monuments and the Mozarteum institutionalized birthplace pilgrimage; Vienna memorialized him despite losing the grave.

The nineteenth century canonized Mozart as divine, effortless, childlike, and apolitical; Romantic culture favored the Requiem, G-minor works, and *Don Giovanni*. The twentieth century alternated Apollonian “perfection” with darker, psychoanalytic, sociological, and performance-historical Mozarts. *Amadeus* massively widened the audience while fixing false images of Salieri, the mass grave, the dictated Requiem, constant vulgarity, and effortless composition.

His technical legacy is not a single “Mozart style.” Beethoven learned from his concertos, chamber works, and opera; Schubert, Chopin, Mendelssohn, Tchaikovsky, Strauss, Stravinsky, and many others constructed different Mozarts. Nadia Boulanger transmitted Mozart as a model of voice leading and formal necessity to twentieth-century students. Opera production continues to reinterpret gender, class, race, empire, Orientalism, brotherhood, and Enlightenment authority in *Entführung*, *Figaro*, *Don Giovanni*, *Così*, and *Zauberflöte*.

The so-called “Mozart effect”—a short-lived spatial-task result from listening to one sonata, inflated in popular culture into generalized infant intelligence—belongs to reception myth and commercial neuroscience, not to Mozart’s biography.

| 1956–2007 | *Neue Mozart-Ausgabe* published, with digital continuation. |

## Research Limitations

The family correspondence is abundant but uneven: Leopold’s management perspective dominates childhood; Constanze and early biographers shaped the death narrative; ordinary servants, performers, and creditors are less audible. Exact lifetime income, gambling, sexual relationships, final medical treatment, funeral attendance, grave location, and the degree of Süssmayr’s reliance on lost Requiem sketches cannot be established. Masonic symbolism is strongest where lodge occasion, text, records, and musical function converge; numerical-key allegories without those controls remain interpretations.

## Atlas Connections

### Franz Anton Mesmer

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Mozart and Lorenzo Da Ponte made Mesmer’s magnetic therapy the subject of a comic topical reference in the Act I finale of *Così fan tutte* (Vienna, 1790): Despina produces “Mesmer’s stone” to revive the supposedly poisoned lovers. The episode unmistakably parodies contemporary mesmerism, although the libretto’s words are Da Ponte’s and the music Mozart’s. [Cambridge Opera Journal](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-opera-journal/article/abs/despina-cupid-and-the-pastoral-mode-of-cosi-fan-tutte/4098BB54CB9842C5BC870F3504DFEDA3), [Social History of Medicine](https://academic.oup.com/shm/article/25/2/271/1739124).
- **[T5]** **Reported fact:** Mozart-family contact with Mesmer’s Viennese household is supported by Mesmer’s surviving encouragement to Leopold concerning Wolfgang’s prospects in Vienna and by accounts that Wolfgang encountered the glass armonica at Mesmer’s house. The familiar claim that *Bastien und Bastienne* was performed in Mesmer’s garden theater in 1768 comes only from later biography and lacks a contemporary program or eyewitness record; that particular event remains **plausible but unverified**, rather than a securely documented première. [Simon P. Keefe, *Mozart in Vienna* excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811071/16719/excerpt/9781107116719_excerpt.pdf), [PubMed historical study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11855437/).

### Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** Fourteen-year-old Goethe attended a Frankfurt concert by seven-year-old Mozart and Nannerl on 25 August 1763. Goethe recalled the spectacle late in life, describing Mozart as a little man in coiffure and sword; the evidence establishes Goethe as an audience witness, not a personal conversation or friendship. [Frankfurter Personenlexikon](https://2021.frankfurter-personenlexikon.de/node/2398), [Freies Deutsches Hochstift/Goethe House](https://freies-deutsches-hochstift.de/en/mediaguide/goethe-house/1-obergeschoss/loeschen/).
- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** Mozart later set Goethe’s poem “Das Veilchen” as K. 476 in Vienna in 1785, creating a one-sided literary-musical engagement; no response by Goethe to this setting is known. Goethe subsequently drafted a sequel to *Die Zauberflöte*, providing a further posthumous engagement with Mozart’s theater. [Goethezeitportal](https://www.goethezeitportal.de/wissen/projektepool/musik-goethe/wirkung-goethes-auf-die-musikgeschichte.html).

### Søren Kierkegaard

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Kierkegaard made Mozart—especially *Don Giovanni*—the central historical subject of “The Immediate Erotic Stages, or the Musical-Erotic,” published pseudonymously in volume I of *Either/Or* (Copenhagen, 1843). The essay also considers *Figaro* and *The Magic Flute* and presents an aesthetic interpretation through the fictional author “A”; its claims should therefore not be flattened into unqualified autobiography. [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/litthe/article-abstract/28/4/411/932845), [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/kierkegaards-eitheror/don-giovanni-and-the-musicalerotic/1172F31D9DEBB2307B25C11F05DEFA3D).

### Richard Wagner

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Wagner repeatedly wrote about Mozart in his theoretical prose, including *Opera and Drama* (1851) and the 1860 “Letter on Music,” using Mozart’s operas to discuss the relationship between poet, drama, and composer. This is a substantial posthumous critical engagement, not a personal crossing. [Wagner text hosted by the University of Texas](https://www.laits.utexas.edu/wagner/selectedessays/pdf/Heise2.pdf), [“Lettre sur la musique”](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Lettre_sur_la_musique_%28Richard_Wagner%29).

### Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Tchaikovsky discussed Mozart extensively in his 1870s criticism and private diaries, repeatedly calling *Don Giovanni* the greatest opera. In 1887 he composed the orchestral Suite No. 4, *Mozartiana*, from four Mozart pieces as an explicit centenary tribute. [Tchaikovsky Research](https://en.tchaikovsky-research.net/pages/Wolfgang_Amadeus_Mozart), [Suite No. 4 documentation](https://en.tchaikovsky-research.net/pages/Suite_No._4).
- **[T9+]** **Documented fact:** Mozart originated the autograph score of *Don Giovanni* in 1787–88; on 12 June 1886 Tchaikovsky examined that manuscript at Pauline Viardot’s Paris home and recorded the encounter in his diary as an experience of being in the presence of divinity. The autograph later entered the Bibliothèque nationale de France. [Tchaikovsky Research](https://en.tchaikovsky-research.net/pages/Pauline_Viardot), [Bibliothèque nationale de France](https://www.bnf.fr/fr/mediatheque/manuscrit-autographe-de-don-giovanni), [*19th-Century Music*](https://online.ucpress.edu/ncm/article-abstract/25/2-3/165/69385/Enshrining-Mozart-Don-Giovanni-and-the-Viardot).

### Friedrich Nietzsche

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Nietzsche repeatedly evaluated Mozart in his philosophical-musical prose. *Human, All Too Human II* includes the aphorism “Beethoven and Mozart” and other comparisons involving Mozart and *Don Giovanni*. This constitutes direct posthumous written engagement, though not a dedicated Mozart study. [Nietzsche text, *Human, All Too Human II*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/37841/37841-h/37841-h.html), [German text of “Beethoven und Mozart”](https://www.textlog.de/nietzsche/schriften/menschliches-allzumenschliches/beethoven-und-mozart).

### Claude Debussy

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Debussy discussed Mozart in his published music criticism, later collected in *Monsieur Croche, antidilettante* (1921), contrasting Mozart’s absorption in music with other composers’ theatrical or ideological preoccupations. The connection is posthumous critical reception only. [Debussy, *Monsieur Croche*](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3ADebussy_-_Monsieur_Croche,_1921.djvu/146), [Bibliothèque nationale de France catalogue](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb35395962p).

### Theodor W. Adorno

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Adorno treated Mozart repeatedly in his musicological and philosophical writings, including the 1933 essay “Vierhändig, noch einmal” and an unfinished fragment specifically concerned with Mozart. His readings addressed convention, freedom, social order, and the individuality of Mozart’s musical characters. [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/modern-intellectual-history/article/jazz-the-wound-negative-identity-culture-and-the-problem-of-weak-subjectivity-in-theodor-adornos-twentieth-century/AD9E3EB4775854BA7B5BA4550F9753C6), [*Law & Literature*](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1525/lal.2008.20.1.47).

### Nadia Boulanger

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Boulanger lectured and taught directly about Mozart. A July 1936 *Le Monde musical* account preserves her Mozart piano class and introductory discussion of his life and works; Mozart scores also served as models in her teaching of voice leading, formal proportion, and compositional judgment. There could be no personal meeting, since Boulanger was born in 1887. [Cambridge University Press, *Nadia Boulanger: Thoughts on Music*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/nadia-boulanger/le-piano-cours-dinterpretation-de-mlle-nadia-boulangermozart-le-monde-musical-47-no-7-july-1936-21112-complete-text/DEC875259CDCFBF9DACE96C73C82DEAE).

No sufficiently supported personal or object-level crossing was found for the remainder of the roster. In particular, shared Enlightenment, Masonic, religious, literary, or musical subject matter alone was not counted; nor were unsupported claims that Cagliostro was a personal model or participant in Mozart’s *Magic Flute* circle.
