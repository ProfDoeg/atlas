# Johan Palmstruch: Research Dossier

## Scope, Method, and Evidentiary Labels

This dossier reconstructs Johan Palmstruch’s life from Swedish archival biography, institutional histories, genealogical compilations, surviving financial instruments, monetary-history research, and modern reassessments of the failure of Stockholms Banco.

The record is uneven. Palmstruch’s banking career is unusually well documented; his childhood, education, Amsterdam business dealings, private beliefs, and final illness are not.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by an archival record, contemporary instrument, official proceeding, or strong institutional reference.
- **Reported fact:** stated in a credible secondary source but not independently verified here against the underlying original record.
- **Allegation:** asserted during a dispute, prosecution, or later controversy without being established as fact.
- **Interpretation:** a historian’s reconstruction or explanation.
- **Rumor or myth:** a recurring story unsupported, contradicted, or not presently verifiable.
- **Unresolved:** surviving evidence permits no firm conclusion.

## Names, Titles, and Orthography

Palmstruch was born into the Witmacker family. Seventeenth-century records and later histories variously spell the name Witmacker, Wittmacher, Witmacher, and Wittmacker. His given name appears as Johan and Hans. “Hans” was not necessarily a pseudonym; it was a normal Germanic/Dutch form related to Johannes/Johan.

After his 1651 ennoblement, he used Palmstruch. The spelling Palmstrüch is common in some modern scholarship, while Palmstruck occurs in genealogical and older sources. Surviving banknotes identify him as publisher or signer and preserve his direct association with Stockholms Banco.

Descriptions such as “father of Swedish banking,” “inventor of European paper money,” “first central banker,” and “copper-money doctor” are retrospective epithets, not offices he formally held.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

Palmstruch’s parents were:

- **Reinholdt Witmacker**, merchant in Riga.
- **Anna Bjelska**, also written Bielska.

The authoritative Swedish biographical register identifies them without assigning a securely established ethnic or religious identity to Anna.

Later Internet biographies describe Anna as Ruthenian, Lithuanian, Jewish, or “of Jewish origin.” No primary evidence establishing those formulations was located in the consulted sources. They should therefore be treated as **unverified genealogical claims**, not documented fact.

### Paternal background

An eighteenth-century family tradition traced the Palmstruch/Witmacker family to Roermond in Limburg. According to that story, the family fled Spanish persecution of Protestants in the sixteenth century, passing through Poland and Lithuania before establishing itself at Riga.

**Evidentiary status: rumor or family tradition.** The Swedish biographical register expressly calls this descent unverified.

The family’s letter of nobility stated that Reinholdt Witmacker lent money for the maintenance of Swedish garrisons in Livonia during Gustav II Adolf’s reign and was promised ennoblement. He reportedly died before the promise could be fulfilled. His sons’ 1651 ennoblement was presented as recognition of the father’s services.

That account is supported by the noble-family record but also served the legal and commemorative purpose of justifying the sons’ elevation. The exact amounts, dates, and contractual terms of Reinholdt’s advances have not been established here.

### Siblings

At least two brothers are securely documented:

- **Reinholdt Wittmacher/Palmstruch**, born 29 September 1612 at Riga; customs and revenue official; died in custody in Stockholm on 9 December 1670.
- **Gerhard Wittmacher/Palmstruch**, born 24 July 1620 at Riga; merchant and later customs notary; died 2 March 1677 at Riga.

All three brothers were ennobled as Palmstruch on 18 June 1651.

Reinholdt Palmstruch’s career has sometimes been conflated with Johan’s. Lithuanian scholarship specifically warns that activities by Reinholdt in Lithuanian taxation and municipal government have been incorrectly attributed to Johan. This conflation can distort both men’s biographies.

Reinholdt was accused of having improperly profited from Crown revenues and was imprisoned. Johan reportedly submitted medical evidence that his brother’s health was endangered in custody. Reinholdt nevertheless remained confined and died there. In 1672, after his death, the Swedish government declared him innocent and freed his widow and children from further proceedings. These events belong primarily to Reinholdt’s biography but show Johan intervening for a close family member while his own legal position was precarious.

### Marriage

Palmstruch married **Margareta van der Busch**, also rendered Margrieta van der Bosch or von der Busch:

- Born **30 December 1617**
- Died **20 March 1677**
- Buried with her husband at Täby Church

Some summaries date the marriage to February 1644 in Amsterdam. A direct marriage-register image or transcription was not independently recovered in this research; the date is therefore best treated as a **reported fact**.

### Children

A noble-family genealogy attributes five children to Johan and Margareta:

1. **Anna Margareta Palmstruch**
   - Born 16 December 1644 in Amsterdam
   - Died 19 March 1721 at Skällnora
   - Married Cornelius Bahn on 11 July 1664

2. **Reinhold Tobias Palmstruch**
   - Born 11 August 1646
   - Died of a stroke on 20 March 1700 at Svarsta in Håbo-Tibble parish
   - Described as living “without service,” meaning without a recorded public or military appointment
   - Married his cousin Maria Palmstruch in 1699 after royal permission
   - Their daughter Christina Margareta died during the plague of 1710

3. **Catharina Elisabet Palmstruch**
   - Born 13 January 1649
   - Died 20 June 1696 in Stockholm
   - Married the wine merchant Here von Santen in 1678

4. **Gustaf Johan Palmstruch**
   - Born 24 November 1650
   - Died 19 December 1652

5. **Christina Maria Palmstruch**
   - Born 9 September 1653
   - Died in 1721
   - Married Johan Cornelius Sintler in 1677

These details come from a later genealogical compilation based substantially on Gustaf Elgenstierna’s noble genealogies. They are useful but not equivalent to inspection of every underlying baptismal, marriage, and burial record.

No illegitimate children, extramarital partners, or additional marriages are documented in the sources consulted.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1611–1634/35

Palmstruch was born in Riga during the city’s early seventeenth-century development as a major Baltic commercial center. Riga had come under Swedish rule in 1621, when Palmstruch was approximately ten.

His father’s position as a merchant and Crown creditor placed the family within the trans-Baltic commercial world linking Livonia, Poland-Lithuania, German ports, Sweden, and the Dutch Republic. That environment is the most plausible context for Palmstruch’s later facility with commerce, currencies, public credit, and Dutch banking institutions.

No reliable source located for this dossier identifies:

- His school
- A university
- An apprenticeship contract
- A named teacher or mentor
- The languages he learned
- The date of his first commercial employment
- His religious upbringing
- His childhood residences within Riga

Claims about formal financial training would therefore be speculative.

## Amsterdam, Continental Travel, Debt, and Imprisonment, c. 1634–1647

### Migration to Amsterdam

Palmstruch left Riga for the Dutch Republic during the first half of the 1630s. The Swedish biographical register states that he became a citizen or burgher of Amsterdam in **1635**.

Amsterdam was then Europe’s leading commercial and financial center. Its Wisselbank, founded in 1609, offered deposit and giro services and became a central influence on Palmstruch’s later bank plans. Hamburg’s public bank was another model.

### Commercial activity

Palmstruch later claimed that he built up a flourishing commercial business in the Netherlands. Details of the enterprise—its legal form, commodities, partners, ships, warehouses, employees, capital, and balance sheet—are not securely reconstructed in the consulted sources.

His later familiarity with Amsterdam banking practices indicates close observation, commercial use, or study of the system, but it does not by itself prove that he had access to secret bank information.

### Continental business journey

According to Palmstruch’s own later account, he departed on a continental business journey in **August 1637**. His itinerary is not established. When he returned, he was imprisoned in **February 1639**.

### Nature of the Dutch imprisonment

This episode has generated two competing narratives.

#### Debt-confinement interpretation

**Documented or strongly supported interpretation:** Palmstruch was held in civil confinement for unpaid debts at the request of creditors.

Dutch banking historian J. G. van Dillen, as reported in the Swedish biographical literature, rejected the espionage story and identified the confinement as debt imprisonment. Palmstruch’s own insistence that he possessed substantial assets is not proof that the debts were paid, liquid, or undisputed.

He apparently remained confined for years, an indication of a serious or unresolved creditor dispute. Some later accounts report a transfer in October 1642 to the Voorpoort at The Hague, but the underlying prison record was not directly examined here.

#### Economic-espionage story

**Allegation or theory:** Palmstruch was imprisoned because he had learned, stolen, or attempted to steal secrets of the Amsterdam Wisselbank.

The theory arose because he later demonstrated unusually detailed knowledge of a bank whose internal procedures were guarded. Aleksandrs Platbārzdis discussed the story, but the Swedish biographical register records its firm rejection by Van Dillen.

No indictment, conviction, interrogation, or prison record proving economic espionage was found. The story remains important because it turns Palmstruch into a financial Prometheus: a man who carried Dutch banking knowledge to Sweden after being punished for acquiring it. Its narrative power has encouraged repetition beyond its evidentiary foundation.

### Marriage and children during confinement

Palmstruch’s daughter Anna Margareta was born in Amsterdam in December 1644, and his son Reinhold Tobias in August 1646. This means his family life continued during or around the long confinement. The precise conditions under which he married, maintained a household, and fathered children while legally confined require further work in Amsterdam municipal and prison records.

### Intervention by Queen Christina

On **18 July 1646**, Queen Christina of Sweden issued a *promotorial* or letter of intercession on Palmstruch’s behalf. A genealogical source gives 15 July, producing a minor date discrepancy. The Swedish biographical register’s 18 July date is preferred here.

The intervention secured or assisted his release. He left the Netherlands and migrated to Sweden in **1647**.

The surviving summaries do not explain:

- Who brought his case to Christina
- Whether Swedish officials negotiated with particular creditors
- Whether debts were settled, guaranteed, compromised, or left outstanding
- Why the Crown considered his release desirable
- Whether family connections or his father’s services played a role

## Establishment in Sweden, Ennoblement, and Public Office, 1647–1655

Palmstruch arrived in Sweden in 1647. The family subsequently had documented associations with Stockholm and estates or residences north of the capital, including the Täby area.

On **18 June 1651**, Queen Christina ennobled Johan and his brothers under the name Palmstruch in recognition of their father’s reported services. The family was formally introduced at the House of Nobility in 1660.

On **16 March 1654**, Palmstruch became an extraordinary commissioner in the National College of Commerce, although genealogical summaries give 1 March and note that the office did not appear on the College’s formal establishment until 1657.

His commission required him to:

- Inspect manufactures throughout the kingdom
- Determine whether enterprises were properly operated
- Compare Swedish products with foreign goods
- Check whether holders of commercial privileges fulfilled their conditions

Nothing substantial is known about his performance in this office. He later became an ordinary commissioner and, according to noble-family records, an extraordinary assessor on 16 March 1665.

His appointment placed him inside Sweden’s mercantilist administrative system, where the Crown granted monopolies and privileges to enterprises expected to strengthen domestic production, trade, and state revenue.

## Formation of the Banking Project

### Swedish monetary problems

Sweden operated with silver and copper monetary standards. Copper plate money could be extraordinarily heavy. Large transactions might require transport by horse, cart, or sled. A bank capable of accepting metal, settling accounts by transfer, and extending credit promised obvious logistical advantages.

Palmstruch’s models were principally:

- The Bank of Amsterdam
- The Hamburg Bank
- Possibly Swedish deposit certificates and the so-called Falun copper notes

He did not invent deposit banking. His innovation lay in combining public-bank mechanisms, a lending operation, and eventually freely transferable credit notes denominated in standard amounts.

### First proposal, 1652

In **1652**, Palmstruch submitted a banking proposal to Queen Christina. It was referred to Stockholm’s mayor and council but produced no institution.

This was not Sweden’s first bank scheme. Axel Oxenstierna had advanced a national banking project in 1619, based on interest-bearing deposits.

### Second proposal, 1655

Palmstruch proposed a municipal bank for Stockholm in **1655**. It was likewise rejected or allowed to lapse.

### Successful proposal, 1656

At the end of **1656**, Palmstruch appealed directly to King Karl X Gustav with proposed charters for:

- A **växelbank**: exchange, deposit, and giro bank
- A **lånebank**: lending bank

The king confirmed the privileges on **30 November 1656**. A genealogical source gives 31 October, but the official biographical chronology supports 30 November.

The charter contemplated a private company with a **thirty-year monopoly** over banking in the Swedish realm.

## Stockholms Banco: Structure, Ownership, and Patronage

Stockholms Banco was often described as Palmstruch’s private bank, but its actual structure was more complicated.

### Profit shares

The contemplated division of profit was:

- Crown: **one-half**
- City of Stockholm: **one-quarter**
- Influential participating patrons: collectively **one-eighth**
- Palmstruch: **one-eighth**

The favored participants were not required to contribute substantial capital or bear corresponding liability. Their position gave the project political backing while reserving profit rights.

### State control

The bank was effectively a semi-public or state institution:

- The king appointed its principal officers.
- Councillor **Gustaf Bonde**, head of the Treasury, served as chief inspector.
- Palmstruch served as director.
- Three commissioners sat with him as the bank’s formal board.
- Soldiers guarded the bank building.

The charter prescribed collegiate governance, but testimony after the collapse alleged that Palmstruch dominated the commissioners and that they followed his decisions. This testimony was politically useful in making him the principal culprit, but it is also evidence that formal checks may not have operated.

### Premises

Palmstruch acquired centrally located premises on **Västerlånggatan** in Stockholm’s old city. The exchange bank opened in **July 1657**.

## Operations and Initial Success, 1657–1660

### Deposit and giro business

The exchange bank accepted non-interest-bearing deposits for a fee. Depositors could withdraw their money or transfer balances by written order—an early check or giro mechanism.

Deposits rose rapidly:

- Approximately **200,000 daler kopparmynt** by October 1657
- More than **400,000 daler kopparmynt** by spring 1660

### Lending business

Although the lending bank was notionally supposed to lend the proprietors’ capital, Stockholms Banco used deposited funds. Palmstruch had disclosed this intention in earlier proposals, and contemporary depositors, government, and parliament initially accepted the practice.

Lending began in spring 1659:

- Gross lending in 1659: approximately **380,000 daler kopparmynt**
- Gross lending in 1660: approximately **830,000 daler kopparmynt**

Borrowers included the Crown, high nobles, and senior officials. Loans were frequently long-term and secured on real property. Deposits, by contrast, could move rapidly as merchants and the Crown used their accounts.

This maturity mismatch—short or mobile liabilities funding long and illiquid assets—was the bank’s fundamental structural vulnerability.

### Economic beneficiaries

The bank offered borrowers an alternative to private lenders. Its lower-than-market interest rate made credit attractive but produced excess demand. Direct lending to ordinary productive businesses appears to have been limited, although noble borrowers may have redirected capital into ironworks and other enterprises.

### Wealth and personal financial interest

Palmstruch was entitled to one-eighth of profit, but the consulted sources do not provide reliable annual personal income figures, a full estate inventory, or evidence that he extracted the bank’s funds for private luxury.

The later portrayal of him as motivated by insatiable greed originated substantially in political explanations of the collapse. It should not be mistaken for a documented inventory of misappropriated personal wealth.

---

## The 1660 Monetary Change and Liquidity Crisis

In 1660, the mint equivalent of Swedish copper money was altered. The copper content of new plate coins was reduced by roughly **17 percent**.

Holders of older, heavier plates could gain by withdrawing them from the bank, melting them, or exporting the copper. Depositors therefore demanded their old metal. The heavier coins disappeared while replacement money was insufficient.

The bank’s lending contracted sharply:

- More than 800,000 daler kopparmynt in 1660
- Less than 230,000 in 1661

The crisis combined:

- Monetary debasement or revaluation
- Withdrawal of valuable old copper plates
- Shortage of circulating coin
- Maturity mismatch
- Inadequate liquid reserves
- The bank’s exposure to large, illiquid borrowers

The institution was not brought down solely by “printing too much money.” That popular summary omits the state’s coinage changes, Crown borrowing, the bank’s public structure, and the decision to require redemption in metal at fixed values.

## The Kreditivsedlar and Europe’s First Modern Banknotes, 1661–1664

### Introduction

In **July 1661**, traditionally dated **16 July**, Palmstruch began issuing *kreditivsedlar*—credit notes.

Their defining features were:

- Printed standard denominations
- Payable to the bearer rather than only to a named individual
- Transferable without a new private contract
- Accepted as a general means of payment
- Redeemable in coin on presentation, at least initially

China had used paper money centuries earlier, and European merchants had long used bills of exchange, promissory notes, receipts, and transferable claims. Palmstruch’s notes are therefore best described as the **first European banknotes in the modern institutional sense**, not the invention of paper money worldwide.

### Were the notes backed by deposits?

A common myth says the notes were simply warehouse receipts for particular copper plates. In fact, the *kreditivsedlar* were not necessarily tied to an individual’s deposit. They could be issued through loans and relied on confidence in the bank’s ability to redeem them.

### Purpose: two interpretations

Historians disagree about Palmstruch’s original intention.

1. **Temporary-emergency interpretation:** The notes were a provisional substitute until sufficient new copper money could be coined and outstanding loans recovered.

2. **Deliberate-monetary-innovation interpretation:** Their standardized form and transferability indicate that Palmstruch intended a permanent, generally accepted payment medium independent of particular deposits.

Palmstruch’s broader plans for payment and credit support the second interpretation, but the surviving evidence does not eliminate the possibility that immediate crisis management accelerated the experiment.

### Claimed Falun precedent

Palmstruch said the notes were inspired by Falun copper certificates or deposit documents circulating in the Bergslagen mining economy.

Most researchers cited by the Swedish biographical register accepted some connection. Karl-Gustaf Hildebrand questioned whether Palmstruch correctly understood the Falun instruments. The issue remains disputed.

### Popularity

The notes were far easier to move than copper plates. They could be sent in an envelope instead of transported in a cart. They initially traded at or near par and may briefly have commanded a small premium.

An economic-history reconstruction estimates:

- Total notes issued, according to Palmstruch: approximately **2.7 million daler kopparmynt**
- Possible share of money supply: roughly **10–20 percent**
- Maximum observed discount against metal: approximately **8–10 percent**

These are estimates, not complete modern balance-sheet figures.

### Surviving notes

A surviving **10-daler silvermynt** note dated **17 April 1666** is catalogued by the Alvin cultural-heritage portal. Palmstruch appears as publisher and signer. Surviving specimens provide direct evidence of the notes’ printed structure, seals, denomination, and authentication.

Some later instruments were heavily endorsed or transferred, demonstrating circulation through multiple holders.

### Palmstruch’s warning

Palmstruch reportedly recognized the ambiguous power of credit creation and wrote:

> “But it is so in this world, that where Our Lord builds a church, the devil gladly sets up a chapel.”

The quotation is preserved in the Swedish biographical tradition. It represents his own moral or rhetorical interpretation of financial innovation, not proof of a sustained theological doctrine.

## Expansion, Inflation, Run, and Collapse, 1663–1667

By 1663, lending and note issuance had expanded sharply. The bank’s lending rate was below the market rate, encouraging borrowers to demand more credit than the institution could safely supply.

Major credits went to the Crown as well as nobles and officials. The bank’s paper began to fall relative to copper coin.

When noteholders demanded redemption:

- The bank lacked a sufficient metallic reserve.
- From October 1663 it increasingly refused or delayed redemption.
- Palmstruch reportedly bought copper at a loss and had it coined in an effort to meet demands.
- The government did not permit a permanent suspension of convertibility or a full paper standard.
- Government and parliament instead ordered loans called in and notes withdrawn.

Modern scholarship differs on the precise causal sequence. One recent interpretation argues that the notes themselves worked acceptably until the fixed copper-silver relationship again created losses, and that the Crown’s 1664 ban on notes destroyed the bank’s ability to continue. Older accounts emphasize overissue and poor management.

Both factors can coexist: Stockholms Banco had serious governance and reserve deficiencies, but governmental monetary rules and the compulsory contraction transformed a liquidity crisis into institutional failure.

### Branches and geographic reach

Later accounts sometimes claim Palmstruch created branches in Göteborg, Falun, and Åbo/Turku.

The Swedish biographical register qualifies this:

- Göteborg had an exchange agent, not necessarily a full branch.
- A planned Falun branch encountered distrust and did not open.
- What occurred at Åbo is unknown.

A nationwide branch network is therefore a **disputed or exaggerated claim**.

### Closure and redemption

The bank effectively ceased ordinary business in **1664**. Its liquidation continued until **1667**, when the last credit note was reportedly redeemed at full value.

This matters when assessing claims that all depositors or noteholders were simply defrauded. Economic disruption and hardship occurred, but the notes were ultimately withdrawn at their nominal value under the liquidation program.

## Investigation and Prosecution, 1664–1669

### Political investigation

A Council commission appointed in **1664** investigated the bank. It completed its work in 1667. On **2 May 1667**, the Council resolved to prosecute Palmstruch in the Svea Court of Appeal.

### Charges and accusations

The proceedings and surrounding political debate attributed to Palmstruch:

- Failure to prepare adequate annual accounts or inventories
- Reckless or careless bookkeeping
- Failure to supervise staff and transactions
- An unexplained cash deficiency
- Improper management of a semi-public institution
- Dominating what was legally supposed to be a collegiate board
- Fraud or embezzlement, in prosecutorial and hostile political language
- Excessive note issuance and lending
- Greed and pursuit of interest income

### The deficit

Modern scholarship reports a deficit of about **88,000 daler silvermynt**. The prosecutor concentrated on accounting failures and the shortage rather than on the abstract legitimacy of banknotes.

The deficit has been attributed to:

- Calculation errors
- Unrecorded expenditures
- Understaffing
- Multiple currencies
- Changing exchange relationships
- Weak internal controls

No clear evidence has been found that Palmstruch deliberately stole the missing amount for personal use.

### Judgment, 22 July 1668

The Svea Court of Appeal delivered judgment on **22 July 1668**.

Palmstruch was sentenced to:

- Removal from office
- Loss of the banking privilege
- Perpetual exile
- An obligation to cover the established cash deficiency
- Death if he did not make good the deficiency within six months

Popular accounts frequently condense this into “executed for printing money” or “sentenced to death because the bank failed.” Neither is exact. He was not executed, and the death provision was conditional on failure to replace the shortage.

Sources differ on whether his noble title itself was formally lost. Some genealogical and popular summaries say he lost his nobility; the authoritative biographical entry more precisely lists dismissal and loss of banking privilege. The exact legal treatment of his noble status should therefore be regarded as **not fully resolved in the consulted material**.

### Council review and commutation

The Council considered the case on **26 February 1669**. Councillors reportedly doubted whether the harsh judgment had an adequate basis.

The death penalty was removed. Palmstruch remained imprisoned, with some modern scholarship describing the substituted sentence as imprisonment and hard labor.

He was released in **1670**.

## Was Palmstruch a Fraudster?

The evidence supports a differentiated finding.

### Documented

- He directed an institution with dangerously weak accounting and internal control.
- Deposits and short-term liabilities were used to fund illiquid lending.
- Note and loan expansion exceeded the bank’s capacity to redeem at the promised metallic value.
- A substantial cash deficit existed.
- He was formally prosecuted and convicted.
- He received a conditional death sentence, later commuted.
- He had previously spent years in Dutch debt confinement.

### Not established

- That he deliberately stole the missing money.
- That he falsified accounts to enrich himself.
- That the bank was created as a planned swindle.
- That he intended to defraud noteholders.
- That his Dutch imprisonment was for proven industrial espionage.
- That all losses resulted from his unilateral decisions.
- That the Crown, Council, inspector, commissioners, and politically connected borrowers were passive outsiders.

The Swedish biographical register concludes that most accusation points were insignificant and identifies his principal faults as indefensibly careless bookkeeping/control and a cash shortage caused by errors and unbooked expenses. Sven Brisman regarded the embezzlement judgment as excessively severe.

A modern study by Patrik Winton and colleagues examining political narrative formation states that no sign of deliberate fraud by Palmstruch can be found in the sources.

## Scapegoating, Political Narrative, and Institutional Interests

Recent scholarship has examined how Swedish elites reconstructed the failure.

Gustaf Bonde combined several roles:

- Councillor
- Head of the Treasury
- Stockholms Banco’s inspector
- Participant in the bank company
- Later critic of Palmstruch

Bonde portrayed Palmstruch as greedy and argued that the “errors of the master” had discredited banking. Other councillors were participants, borrowers, or stakeholders.

The post-collapse narrative separated:

- **Banking as a useful public technology**
- **Palmstruch as the supposedly private, greedy, arbitrary manager who corrupted it**

This distinction made it politically easier to replace Stockholms Banco with a parliamentary bank without discrediting banking itself.

One historical interpretation proposes that heavily indebted councillors wanted to remove a powerful creditor. Modern research notes that their interests created a possible motive but finds **no explicit proof** of a coordinated conspiracy.

The “scapegoat” thesis is stronger in a limited sense:

- Responsibility was institutionally distributed.
- Palmstruch was turned into the singular public embodiment of failure.
- Crown borrowing and state monetary policy were minimized in the dominant account.
- Staff from the old bank could be reused once the institution was reconstructed under new ownership.
- The nickname “Palmstruch’s bank” perpetuated personal responsibility for what had been a semi-public enterprise.

This does not erase his documented failures of control.

## Riksens Ständers Bank and Institutional Succession

In **1668**, the Swedish Estates established **Riksens Ständers Bank**, later Sveriges Riksbank.

Stockholms Banco was its immediate institutional predecessor, but statements that the old bank simply “became” the Riksbank can obscure legal and political discontinuities:

- Stockholms Banco operated under a royal charter.
- The new bank was controlled by the Estates/parliament.
- It was designed to separate bank governance from the Crown.
- Its rules prohibited Palmstruch-style credit notes.
- Banknote issuance did not immediately continue as a normal function.

Sveriges Riksbank identifies itself as the world’s oldest central bank. Whether Stockholms Banco itself should be called a central bank depends on definition. It had state supervision, public functions, Crown participation, and note issuance, but it was chartered through a mixed corporate structure and lacked many features of a modern central bank.

## Other Offices and Projects

Palmstruch served on Swedish coinage commissions:

- November 1663 to March 1664
- September to October 1666

These appointments show that he remained involved in official monetary deliberations even as his bank was failing or liquidating.

No patents in the modern sense have been located. His principal technical-financial projects were:

- A giro and check-payment system
- A lending bank
- Transferable fixed-denomination banknotes
- Plans or proposals for broader geographic banking services
- Monetary and banking memoranda preserved in manuscript

No reliable evidence was found of participation in military intelligence, political violence, colonial enterprises, slave trading, money laundering in the modern sense, or secret societies.

## Property, Residences, and Financial Position

Documented or reported locations associated with Palmstruch include:

- Riga: birthplace and childhood
- Amsterdam: commerce, burgher status, family formation, and confinement
- Continental Europe: unspecified 1637–39 business journey
- The Hague: reported later place of debt confinement
- Stockholm: government career, bank, prosecution, imprisonment, and death
- Täby/Gribbylund area: family property or commemorative association and burial

Stockholms Banco occupied a property on Västerlånggatan. It is not clear from the consulted record whether Palmstruch personally owned the property or obtained it for the bank through another arrangement.

The family had a hereditary grave in Täby Church. A grave letter copied in 1704 calls Palmstruch the “Inventor and Director” of the exchange and lending bank. In return for the family burial right near the altar, Palmstruch reportedly financed or built a gallery for the parish youth. His and Margareta’s grave slab bore both coats of arms.

A complete probate inventory, landholding schedule, or reliable estimate of his net wealth at death was not located. His inability or failure to cover the bank deficit does not by itself establish personal destitution, because the boundary between personal and institutional liability was contested.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Political position

Palmstruch was not a party politician in the modern sense. His political activity consisted of:

- Petitioning Queen Christina and Karl X Gustav
- Obtaining a monopoly charter
- Serving the College of Commerce
- Participating in coinage commissions
- Building alliances among councillors, officials, the Crown, and Stockholm
- Operating an institution deeply involved with state credit

His career depended on monarchical privilege and mercantilist administration.

### Economic ideas

His surviving plans exhibit several recurring ideas:

- Money should be understood as a transferable claim and accounting unit, not only as metal.
- Giro transfers could reduce the physical movement of coin.
- Deposits could support lending.
- Bank credit could expand commerce and lower borrowing costs.
- Notes could solve the logistical problem of heavy copper money.
- Bank credit should correspond to real commercial activity—an outlook modern scholarship relates to the later “real bills” tradition.

At the same time, his system did not adequately control liquidity risk, note redemption, exchange-rate changes, or the political demand for cheap credit.

### Religion

No detailed evidence establishes Palmstruch’s personal denomination, devotional practices, or theology. His family’s alleged Protestant refugee ancestry is unverified. The church-and-devil metaphor shows familiarity with conventional Christian rhetoric but cannot sustain conclusions about his spiritual life.

### Education and intellectual influences

No formal education is documented. His principal formative influence was the Dutch commercial world, especially the Amsterdam and Hamburg public-bank models. The Falun certificate system was a claimed Swedish precedent.

No named teacher or acknowledged philosophical mentor is securely documented.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Queen Christina

**Documented political patron.** She intervened for his release from Dutch confinement in 1646, ennobled him and his brothers in 1651, and received his first banking proposal in 1652.

### King Karl X Gustav

**Documented chartering authority.** He approved Palmstruch’s exchange- and lending-bank privileges on 30 November 1656.

### Gustaf Bonde

**Documented supervisor, stakeholder, and later critic.** Bonde served as bank inspector and Treasury head. After failure, he portrayed Palmstruch as greedy and principally responsible. His overlapping roles create a conflict-of-interest question but do not prove conspiracy.

### Stockholms Banco commissioners

**Documented collaborators and subordinates.** Three commissioners formally shared governance. Commissioner Erik Appelgren later testified that Palmstruch dominated the board. The testimony supported the prosecution’s and Council’s personalization of responsibility.

### Crown and high nobility

**Documented borrowers and political principals.** Crown loans contributed to the bank’s illiquidity. Nobles and senior officials borrowed heavily, frequently against real property.

### Stockholm merchants and depositors

**Documented clients.** They used the exchange accounts more actively than the bank anticipated, making deposits less stable than long-term lending required.

### J. G. van Dillen, Aleksandrs Platbārzdis, Sven Brisman, Eli Heckscher, Anders Ögren, and later historians

These were posthumous interpreters rather than life relationships. Their work shaped competing images of Palmstruch as debtor, spy, innovator, reckless banker, scapegoat, and monetary reformer.

### Friends, enemies, and intimate associates

No securely documented personal friendship circle, mistress, private secretary, or enduring non-family enemy was identified. Political and financial alliances are much better documented than personal attachments.

## Imprisonment, Health, and Final Years, 1668–1671

Palmstruch remained in custody after the 1668 judgment and the 1669 commutation. The exact prison, conditions, and period of hard labor are not consistently identified in the consulted sources.

He was released in **1670**.

During this period his brother Reinhold was also imprisoned in Stockholm. Johan reportedly submitted medical evidence warning that Reinhold’s health was endangered. Reinhold died in custody on 9 December 1670.

Palmstruch himself died in Stockholm on **8 March 1671**, less than a year after his release and approximately three months after his brother’s death.

No consulted source provides:

- A diagnosis
- A deathbed account
- An autopsy
- Evidence of addiction
- Evidence of psychiatric illness
- A documented accident or injury
- Proof that prison conditions caused his death

Any claim that he died “broken,” “of prison hardship,” or “from disgrace” is literary inference unless supported by a presently unidentified medical or parish record.

He was buried at Täby Church with his wife’s later remains. The burial site indicates continuing family status despite his conviction.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, and Financial Questions

### Dutch insolvency

- **Documented fact:** Long confinement beginning in 1639.
- **Strongest explanation:** Civil imprisonment for unpaid debts.
- **Palmstruch’s position:** He claimed substantial assets.
- **Unresolved:** The original debt amounts, creditors, judgment, settlement, and balance sheet.

### Economic espionage

- **Theory or myth:** He was imprisoned for stealing Amsterdam bank secrets.
- **Evidence for:** Later detailed knowledge of Dutch banking.
- **Evidence against:** A specialist Dutch historian identified the case as debt confinement; no espionage charge was located.
- **Finding:** Unproven.

### Misuse of deposits

- **Documented fact:** Deposited funds helped finance lending.
- **Qualification:** Palmstruch did not entirely conceal that intention, and depositors and public authorities initially accepted it.
- **Failure:** The bank lacked sufficient liquidity for simultaneous withdrawals.

### Reckless credit and overissue

- **Documented fact:** Lending and note issuance expanded; the bank could not maintain redemption.
- **Disputed interpretation:** Whether overissue was the primary cause or whether fixed metallic conversion rules and the Crown’s policy transformed a manageable system into collapse.

### Accounting misconduct

- **Documented court finding:** Serious accounting and control failures and a cash deficit.
- **Modern historical assessment:** The failures were real; most other counts were minor; complex currencies and understaffing contributed.

### Fraud and embezzlement

- **Formal allegation/charge and historical conviction:** Fraudulent or criminally improper management was alleged and punished.
- **Not established by surviving evidence reviewed in modern scholarship:** Deliberate theft or intentional fraud for personal gain.
- **Finding:** Conviction is a fact; intentional embezzlement is not independently demonstrated.

### Harm to the public

- **Documented fact:** The contraction of credit and redemption crisis caused economic disruption and hardship.
- **Qualification:** Notes were ultimately withdrawn at full nominal value according to monetary-history research.
- **Unresolved:** A complete social accounting of individual bankruptcies and losses.

### State responsibility

- **Documented context:** The Crown received profit rights, appointed officials, borrowed, changed coinage rules, supervised the bank, and later prohibited notes.
- **Interpretation:** These actions materially contributed to failure.
- **Conspiracy theory:** The collapse was deliberately engineered so the state could seize banking.
- **Evidence:** Political actors had interests and later controlled the narrative, but no direct proof of a planned state operation has been found.

### Recent adversarial material

Searches combining Palmstruch’s name with terms for fraud, scandal, conspiracy, accusation, criticism, and related concepts produced no new 2024–26 allegation based on newly discovered primary evidence.

The most substantive recent contribution is Anders Ögren’s 2024 chapter, which presents Palmstruch as a “copper money doctor,” argues that fixed copper-silver conversion and the Crown’s 1664 prohibition were central to the collapse, and describes Palmstruch as scapegoated.

A conspiratorial Swedish publication claims that the bank run was a commissioned state operation and describes banking itself as fraud or counterfeiting. It supplies suspicions and ideological argument rather than direct documentary proof of an organized plot. Its allegation is therefore classified as an **unverified conspiracy theory**.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Palmstruch invented paper money”

**Story:** Palmstruch invented paper currency.

**Evidence:** He directed issuance of Europe’s first generally circulating banknotes in standardized denominations.

**Against literal version:** Chinese states had issued paper money centuries earlier; European bills and paper credit instruments predated him.

**Effect:** It made him a symbolic founder of modern money while obscuring both non-European precedents and the institutional work of bank staff and the state.

### “The notes were simple copper receipts”

**Story:** Customers deposited copper plates and received paper receipts representing the same metal.

**Evidence against:** The *kreditivsedlar* were also issued as credit and were not tied one-to-one to named deposits.

**Effect:** This myth makes the later overissue appear as a simple act of counterfeiting rather than credit creation by a chartered bank.

### “He stole Amsterdam’s secret banking system”

**Story:** Palmstruch was a financial spy who carried secret Dutch banking techniques to Sweden.

**Origin and circulation:** Later numismatic and popular biographies, encouraged by his long imprisonment and detailed knowledge of the Wisselbank.

**Evidence:** Knowledge of Dutch methods; no located espionage judgment.

**Counterevidence:** Debt confinement is the documented specialist explanation.

**Effect:** The story romanticized him as a daring or criminal transmitter of forbidden knowledge.

### “A lone private banker destroyed his bank through greed”

**Story:** Palmstruch alone ran a private bank, printed excessive notes, enriched himself, and caused its collapse.

**Evidence for:** He exercised strong operational control, failed to impose limits, and maintained inadequate accounts.

**Evidence against:** The Crown shared profits, appointed officers, borrowed from the bank, controlled monetary policy, and supervised the institution. Commissioners and inspector also had formal responsibility.

**Effect:** The story enabled Swedish authorities to preserve the concept of public banking while attributing failure to one individual.

### “He was executed”

**Story:** Sweden executed its first central banker.

**Evidence against:** His death sentence was conditional and later commuted. He was released in 1670 and died naturally or from an undocumented cause in 1671.

**Effect:** The myth provides a dramatic warning about monetary irresponsibility.

### “The bank failed in 1668”

This is convenient institutional shorthand. Ordinary operations had effectively ceased in 1664, liquidation was completed in 1667, the judgment came in 1668, and the new parliamentary bank was founded that year.

### “The Riksbank directly continued Palmstruch’s banknotes”

The new bank was the institutional successor, but it initially prohibited instruments resembling Palmstruch’s credit notes. Sweden did not simply continue the same note regime under a new name.

### Protestant-refugee ancestry

**Story:** The family fled Roermond to Poland-Lithuania to escape Spanish anti-Protestant persecution.

**Evidence:** An eighteenth-century family account.

**Problem:** The Swedish biographical register calls it unverified.

**Effect:** It provided the noble family with a coherent confessional and migration origin story.

### Jewish or Ruthenian maternal ancestry

Modern online retellings assign Anna Bjelska varying Jewish, Ruthenian, Lithuanian, or Dutch identities.

No adequate primary documentation was located. These claims should remain separate from the documented fact of her name and marriage to Reinholdt Witmacker.

## Works by Johan Palmstruch

Palmstruch was not a conventional published author. His surviving corpus consists primarily of institutional plans, memoranda, official submissions, correspondence, bank documents, and signed notes.

### Known or reported writings and documents

1. **First bank proposal to Queen Christina** — 1652  
   Manuscript proposal concerning a Swedish bank. Rejected or shelved. Genuine authorship is attributed to Palmstruch, though secretarial copying is possible.

2. **Proposal for a municipal Stockholm bank** — 1655  
   Manuscript submission. Not implemented.

3. **Proposed charters for the exchange and lending banks** — 1656  
   Submitted to Karl X Gustav and confirmed on 30 November 1656. Institutional/legal text associated with Palmstruch; final language may reflect royal officials.

4. **Banking manuscripts preserved in the Nordinska collection, Uppsala University Library** — seventeenth century  
   Three manuscripts were presented and analyzed by Ernst Nathorst-Böös in “The Advantage of Banking: A Presentation of 3 Manuscripts of Johan Palmstruch” (1968). They concern the utility, theory, and operation of banking. They remain manuscripts rather than books published during Palmstruch’s life.

5. **Letters from Palmstruch** — dates various  
   The Swedish National Archives reports letters by Palmstruch in its collections. A complete calendar or scholarly edition was not located.

6. **Stockholms Banco *kreditivsedlar*** — issued 1661–68  
   Institutional financial instruments published under the bank’s authority and signed or authorized by Palmstruch and other officials. These are not solely authored literary works. A surviving 10-daler silvermynt note dated 17 April 1666 names Palmstruch as publisher and signer.

7. **Official explanations, accounts, and defenses during the bank investigation** — 1660s  
   Palmstruch’s statements about note issuance, the Falun precedent, circulation totals, and his conduct survive through archival proceedings and later quotation. A complete modern edition was not found.

### Works not attributable to him

*Svensk botanik* and *Svensk zoologi* were projects of the much later Johan Wilhelm Palmstruch and must not be included in Johan Palmstruch’s bibliography.

No patents, diary, memoir, autobiography, theological work, printed treatise, or book-length publication under Johan Palmstruch’s name has been securely identified.

## Death and Burial

Palmstruch died in Stockholm on **8 March 1671**, aged fifty-nine if age is calculated exactly from 13 June 1611, although he is often described as sixty according to seventeenth-century age conventions.

He was buried in Täby Church. His wife joined him there after her death in 1677.

A grave-right document recorded in 1704 described him as:

- Noble
- “Banco lord”
- Inventor and director of the exchange and lending bank

The wording shows that his descendants and parish community preserved his inventive and institutional identity rather than commemorating him principally as a convicted offender.

The grave was situated on the south side of the choir near the altar. A gallery constructed for parish youth formed part of the arrangement by which Palmstruch acquired hereditary burial rights. A stone displayed the carved arms of Palmstruch and his wife.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Palmstruch’s reputation has moved among several durable types.

### The failed speculator

Older institutional histories and popular narratives treated him as the banker who created excessive paper credit and caused Sweden’s first bank crash.

### The monetary inventor

Numismatists and central-bank histories emphasized the *kreditivsedlar* as Europe’s first modern banknotes.

### The cautionary criminal

Popular journalism repeatedly invokes the conditional death sentence to contrast seventeenth-century punishment with modern responses to banking crises.

### The scapegoat

Recent research emphasizes that:

- The bank was not simply private.
- The Crown, Council, inspector, commissioners, and politically connected borrowers shared responsibility.
- Monetary revaluation precipitated withdrawals.
- The Crown’s note ban compelled contraction.
- No evidence of deliberate fraud has been found.
- The prosecution helped legitimate a new parliamentary bank.

### Institutional influence

Palmstruch’s experiment demonstrated that bank-issued paper could circulate because of confidence rather than direct attachment to a particular deposit.

Its failure also produced institutional lessons:

- Need for transparent accounts
- Need for liquidity and reserves
- Danger of maturity mismatch
- Political danger of lending heavily to the state and elites
- Importance of governance independent of individual discretion
- Tension between convertibility and flexible credit
- Need to distinguish a bank’s unit of account from the commodity content of coin

The new parliamentary bank was structured partly in reaction against Palmstruch’s institution. Thus both his success and failure contributed to the development of Sveriges Riksbank.

### Limits of the “central-bank founder” title

Palmstruch did not found Sveriges Riksbank in its 1668 parliamentary form and did not direct it. He founded its immediate predecessor. Calling him the “founder of Sweden’s central bank” is acceptable only as institutional shorthand and should not erase the 1668 rupture.
