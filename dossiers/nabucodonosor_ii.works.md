## Works by Nabucodonosor II

### Royal inscriptions

- **605–562 BC:** *Royal inscriptions of Nebuchadnezzar II*. A corpus of at least 136 catalogued inscriptions—principally commemorative building texts on cylinders, bricks, paving stones, tablets, stelae, and rock faces—rather than 136 securely independent literary compositions. They speak in the king’s voice, but their scribal composition and therefore personal authorship are uncertain; the current standard catalogue is the Royal Inscriptions of Babylonia online corpus, building on Da Riva’s catalogue. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005364); [Da Riva 2008](https://www.ugarit-verlag.com/products/the-neo-babylonian-royal-inscriptions-an-introduction))

- **605–562 BC:** *Standard brick inscriptions*. Extremely short, repeatedly stamped texts naming Nebuchadnezzar as king, provider for Esagil and Ezida, and heir of Nabopolassar. These are architectural marks reproduced in many exemplars, so the “work” is better understood as a formula and stamping program than as every surviving brick separately. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 001–002*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/pager); [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/321676))

- **605–562 BC:** *Babylon building inscriptions*. Cylinders, tablets, bricks, and paving inscriptions commemorating work on Babylon’s temples, palaces, fortifications, streets, gates, quays, and canals. Individual witnesses overlap, abbreviate, or combine the same projects and cannot always be treated as separate authored “texts.” ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II inscriptions from Babylon*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/pager); [Da Riva, “The Inscriptions of the Neo-Babylonian Empire”](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781614513551_A21656210/preview-9781614513551_A21656210.pdf))

- **605–562 BC:** *Three-column cylinder inscriptions*. A family of long building accounts surveying numerous works at Babylon and elsewhere. Their shared passages and variant exemplars make them a textual tradition rather than a single fixed work. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 022 and related inscriptions*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005501); [Da Riva, “The Inscriptions of the Neo-Babylonian Empire”](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781614513551_A21656210/preview-9781614513551_A21656210.pdf))

- **605–562 BC:** *East India House Inscription*. A long building inscription on stone surveying Nebuchadnezzar’s religious and civic works in Babylon and Borsippa. It is a royal monumental statement—part record, part ideological self-presentation—not an annalistic history. ([British Museum, BM 129397](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1801-1019-1); [RIBo corpus](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/pager))

- **605–562 BC:** *Stone Tablet inscription*. A monumental summary of the king’s building curriculum, conventionally named from its support rather than by an ancient title. It combines commemoration, royal self-fashioning, and a prayer for dynastic permanence. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 008*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005473); [Da Riva, “The Inscriptions of the Neo-Babylonian Empire”](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781614513551_A21656210/preview-9781614513551_A21656210.pdf))

- **605–562 BC:** *Nebuchadnezzar Prism (“Hofkalender”)*. A fragmentary clay prism combining a broad building résumé with a list of court personnel. Its administrative catalogue and royal commemoration resist classification as a purely literary work. ([Michael Jursa, *Aspects of the Economic History of Babylonia in the First Millennium BC*](https://www.ugarit-verlag.com/products/aspects-of-the-economic-history-of-babylonia-in-the-first-millennium-bc); [Da Riva, “The Inscriptions of the Neo-Babylonian Empire”](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781614513551_A21656210/preview-9781614513551_A21656210.pdf))

- **605–562 BC:** *Ištar Gate Inscription*. A building account incorporated into the gate complex, commemorating its reconstruction and the raising and ornamentation of the adjoining processional route. It is simultaneously text, architectural component, and royal image-making. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 013*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/pager); [German Archaeological Institute](https://publications.dainst.org/journals/zora/article/view/4471))

- **605–562 BC:** *Processional-street paving inscriptions*. Brief inscriptions laid into the paving of Ay-ibūr-šabû and related streets, identifying Nebuchadnezzar’s rebuilding. Because they were designed to be walked over and integrated into a ritual route, they are textual architecture rather than freestanding literature. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 070*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005541/); [German Archaeological Institute](https://publications.dainst.org/journals/zora/article/view/4471))

- **605–562 BC:** *Nahr al-Kalb inscription*. A badly weathered royal rock inscription beside earlier Egyptian and Assyrian monuments in Lebanon. Its surviving text is fragmentary, and its setting makes it as much an imperial manifestation in a monumental landscape as a discrete text. ([Da Riva, “The Nebuchadnezzar Inscription in Nahr el-Kalb”](https://www.academia.edu/1032367/The_Nebuchadnezzar_inscription_in_Nahr_el_Kalb); [RIBo corpus](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/pager))

- **605–562 BC:** *Twin inscriptions at Brisa*. Two extensive rock inscriptions—one in archaizing script and one in contemporary Neo-Babylonian script—accompanied by reliefs in the Wadi esh-Sharbin, Lebanon. They are parallel but not identical monumental compositions and form a combined textual-visual intervention in the landscape. ([Da Riva, *The Twin Inscriptions of Nebuchadnezzar at Brisa*](https://www.oeaw.ac.at/en/publications/the-twin-inscriptions-of-nebuchadnezzar-at-brisa); [RIBo corpus](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/pager))

- **605–562 BC; authenticity disputed:** *Tower of Babel Stele*. A black-stone stele bearing a building inscription, a plan, and a relief representation of Etemenanki with a royal figure. Its modern provenance is insecure and scholars have questioned its authenticity, so it cannot be accepted unreservedly as Nebuchadnezzar’s work. ([Da Riva, “A Stele of Nebuchadnezzar II”](https://doi.org/10.1515/za-2012-0001); [Schøyen Collection, MS 2063](https://www.schoyencollection.com/palaeography-collection-introduction/babylonian-collection/tower-babel-stele-ms-2063))

### Temples and cult buildings

- **605–562 BC:** *Esagil*. A major restoration and embellishment of Marduk’s principal temple at Babylon, including sanctuaries, roofing, doors, precious-metal fittings, and its surrounding precinct. Nebuchadnezzar did not originate the ancient temple, so the obra is a royal reconstruction program rather than the temple’s first creation. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 008*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005473); [Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Babylon”](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/hd/babl/hd_babl.htm))

- **605–562 BC:** *Etemenanki*. The completion or large-scale rebuilding of Babylon’s ancient ziggurat, begun before Nebuchadnezzar and conventionally associated with the later “Tower of Babel” tradition. His inscriptions claim the work, but archaeology and earlier royal texts show that it was restoration and completion, not creation ex nihilo. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 067*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005538); [Andrew George, “The Tower of Babel”](https://www.jstor.org/stable/4200539))

- **605–562 BC:** *Esiskur*. Rebuilding of Marduk’s akītu, or New Year festival, temple outside Babylon’s principal sacred precinct. It was a ritual destination integrated with the processional streets and therefore part of a larger ceremonial project. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 008, commentary*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005473))

- **605–562 BC:** *Emaḫ*. Restoration of the temple of Ninḫursag/Ninmaḫ near the Ištar Gate at Babylon. The extant structure and inscriptions represent renewal of an older sanctuary rather than an independently invented building. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 008, commentary*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005473))

- **605–562 BC:** *Eniggidarkalamma*. Work on the temple of Nabû of the ḫarê at Babylon. Its inclusion in summary inscriptions is secure, although the extent and exact phases of Nebuchadnezzar’s intervention remain incompletely recoverable. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 008*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005473))

- **605–562 BC:** *Eḫursagtila*. Restoration of the temple of Ninurta at Babylon. The building was ancient, and the king’s obra was its reconstructed Neo-Babylonian form. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II building corpus*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/pager))

- **605–562 BC:** *Ekitusḫeĝalla*. Work on the temple of Adad at Babylon, known chiefly through the royal building accounts. Its poor archaeological preservation makes the inscriptional project easier to identify than the finished architecture. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 008*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005473))

- **605–562 BC:** *Ezida*. Reconstruction and lavish adornment of Nabû’s principal temple at Borsippa, including its courts, chambers, doors, and cultic fittings. As with Esagil, this was the remaking of a much older sanctuary rather than an original foundation. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 054 and related texts*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005525); [British Museum, Borsippa cylinder](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1880-0617-1))

- **605–562 BC:** *Eurmeiminanki*. Work on the ziggurat of Borsippa, often called Birs Nimrud and associated with Ezida. Nebuchadnezzar’s inscriptions describe reconstruction, but the building’s long and complicated chronology prevents attribution of the whole monument to him. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II corpus*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/pager); [UNESCO, Babylon](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/278/))

- **605–562 BC:** *Egula*. Restoration of one of three temples of Gula/Ninkarrak at Borsippa recorded together in a royal building résumé. The shared passage does not permit every construction phase to be independently dated. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 008, commentary*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005473))

- **605–562 BC:** *Etila*. Restoration of a temple of Gula/Ninkarrak at Borsippa. It survives primarily as an inscriptionally attested project rather than a fully recoverable building. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 008, commentary*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005473))

- **605–562 BC:** *Ezibatila*. Restoration of another temple of Gula/Ninkarrak at Borsippa. Like Egula and Etila, it resists treatment as an individually preserved architectural work because its physical remains and building sequence are uncertain. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 008, commentary*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005473))

- **605–562 BC:** *Ebabbar at Sippar*. Reconstruction and adornment of Šamaš’s principal temple, including work in its precinct and on associated cult buildings. Multiple inscriptions and earlier construction phases make this a continuing institutional project rather than a single newly made object. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II inscriptions from Sippar*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/pager); [British Museum, Sippar cylinder](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/search?keyword=Nebuchadnezzar&place=Sippar))

- **605–562 BC:** *Eanna at Uruk*. Restoration and embellishment within Ištar’s great temple complex, documented by cylinders and administrative records. Individual components and dates are not always separable, and some work continued projects of earlier kings. ([Da Riva, “A Cylinder of Nebuchadnezzar II from Uruk”](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/94047/7/94047.pdf); [RIBo corpus](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/pager))

- **605–562 BC:** *Lugal-Marada’s temple at Marad*. Rebuilding recorded by foundation cylinders from Marad. The ancient ceremonial name and the precise architectural scope vary in modern treatments, so the securely identifiable work is the royal restoration project itself. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II inscriptions from Marad*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/pager); [Da Riva 2008](https://www.ugarit-verlag.com/products/the-neo-babylonian-royal-inscriptions-an-introduction))

- **605–562 BC:** *Ebabbar at Larsa*. Restoration work on the ancient temple of Šamaš, known from fragmentary royal evidence. The surviving documentation does not support a precise reconstruction of everything Nebuchadnezzar altered. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II inscriptions from Larsa*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/pager))

- **605–562 BC:** *Egišnugal at Ur*. Repairs to the temple complex of the moon god Sîn, attested by building inscriptions. The work belongs to a long succession of restorations and should not be confused with the original construction of the sanctuary or its ziggurat. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II inscriptions from Ur*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/pager))

### Palaces and royal compounds

- **605–562 BC:** *South Palace at Babylon*. Enlargement and reconstruction of the principal royal palace beside the Processional Way, ultimately a vast multi-courtyard complex. Because it incorporated earlier fabric and underwent successive phases, “Nebuchadnezzar’s palace” names a building program more accurately than a single act of creation. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 008*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005473); [UNESCO, Babylon](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/278/))

- **605–562 BC:** *North Palace at Babylon*. A new or substantially new fortified palace built north of the inner city beside the Processional Way. Its role combined residence, treasury, defence, display, and control of the northern approach, making it a compound rather than a single building. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 008*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005473); [Da Riva, “Urban Religion in First Millennium BCE Babylonia”](https://iris.cnr.it/retrieve/7f5010c7-5933-4a33-8e0b-3a8a0adbfc26/10.1515_9783110798432.pdf))

- **605–562 BC:** *Summer Palace at Babylon*. A palace complex on the northern outskirts of Babylon, conventionally associated with Nebuchadnezzar’s expansion of the capital. Its identification and function are less securely documented than those of the South and North Palaces. ([Olof Pedersén, *Babylon: The Great City*](https://www.zaphon.de/epages/83179382.sf/en_GB/?ObjectPath=/Shops/83179382/Products/9783963271366); [UNESCO, Babylon](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/278/))

### Gates, streets, fortifications, and waterworks

- **605–562 BC:** *Ištar-sākipat-tēbîša (Ištar Gate)*. Monumental reconstruction of Babylon’s northern inner-city gate, with molded and glazed brick images of bulls and mušḫuššu-dragons. The surviving monument has several superimposed phases, and modern museum displays reconstruct only selected portions. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 008, commentary*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005473); [Musée du Louvre–French Ministry of Culture, Babylon](https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/babylone/en/ishtar-gate-and-processional-way))

- **605–562 BC:** *Ay-ibūr-šabû*. Rebuilding, raising, paving, and ornamentation of Marduk’s principal processional street through Babylon. Because it joined gates, palaces, temples, relief façades, inscriptions, drainage, and ritual movement, it is best understood as a Gesamtkunstwerk-like urban and ceremonial route. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 040*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005511); [German Archaeological Institute](https://publications.dainst.org/journals/zora/article/view/4471))

- **605–562 BC:** *Nabû’s processional street at Babylon*. A raised and strengthened ceremonial roadway associated with Nabû’s movement during festivals. It was related to, but not necessarily identical in every stretch with, Ay-ibūr-šabû; the ancient nomenclature and overlapping routes complicate modern separation. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 070*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005541/))

- **605–562 BC:** *Imgur-Enlil*. Reconstruction and strengthening of Babylon’s inner defensive wall. The wall was ancient and had been rebuilt by earlier rulers, so Nebuchadnezzar made a new monumental phase, not the original fortification. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II corpus*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/pager); [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/321676))

- **605–562 BC:** *Nēmetti-Enlil*. Reconstruction of Babylon’s outer defensive wall. Fragmentary inscriptions and multiple ancient phases make the exact extent of Nebuchadnezzar’s fabric difficult to isolate. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 017*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005364); [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/321676))

- **605–562 BC:** *Eastern outer wall of Babylon*. A massive additional fortification beyond the traditional double wall, represented by inscribed foundation cylinders. It belongs to a larger defensive landscape rather than functioning as an autonomous architectural work. ([Metropolitan Museum of Art, cylinder 86.11.1](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/321676))

- **605–562 BC:** *Fortification between the Euphrates and the Tigris north of Babylon*. A defensive earthwork or wall intended to protect northern Babylonia, described in royal inscriptions but imperfectly located and reconstructed. Its common identification with the later “Median Wall” is not certain. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 008*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005473); [D. J. Wiseman, *Nebuchadrezzar and Babylon*](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/nebuchadrezzar-and-babylon-9780197261002))

- **605–562 BC:** *Araḫtu quays and embankments*. Reinforcement and rebuilding of quays along the Euphrates/Araḫtu at Babylon to regulate water, protect the city, and support monumental riverfront construction. These interventions were infrastructure and environmental engineering, not a single bounded object. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 008*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005473))

- **605–562 BC:** *Lībil-ḫegalla canal works*. Work on Babylon’s eastern canal, including hydraulic and defensive functions. The canal predated the king, and the evidence concerns his intervention in a continuing water system rather than its first excavation. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II 008, commentary*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/Q005473))

- **605–562 BC:** *Euphrates bridge at Babylon*. Construction or major reconstruction of the bridge linking the eastern and western halves of the city, with masonry piers documented archaeologically. Attribution to Nebuchadnezzar’s urban program is strong, but ancient sources do not preserve a securely dated, self-contained “bridge inscription.” ([Olof Pedersén, *Babylon: The Great City*](https://www.zaphon.de/epages/83179382.sf/en_GB/?ObjectPath=/Shops/83179382/Products/9783963271366); [UNESCO, Babylon](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/278/))

- **605–562 BC:** *Walls of Borsippa*. Restoration and strengthening of the city’s defences as part of Nebuchadnezzar’s northern-Babylonian building policy. The remains include earlier and later phases, so the whole circuit cannot simply be called his work. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II inscriptions from Borsippa*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/pager); [D. J. Wiseman, *Nebuchadrezzar and Babylon*](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/nebuchadrezzar-and-babylon-9780197261002))

- **605–562 BC:** *Walls of Kish*. Royal restoration of the fortifications of Kish. Evidence is limited compared with Babylon, and the precise extent of completed work is uncertain. ([RIBo, *Nebuchadnezzar II inscriptions from Kish*](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon7/pager); [D. J. Wiseman, *Nebuchadrezzar and Babylon*](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/nebuchadrezzar-and-babylon-9780197261002))

### Images and composite monuments

- **605–562 BC:** *Glazed-brick animal relief program of the Ištar Gate*. Repeated molded figures of bulls and mušḫuššu-dragons integrated into the gate façades. Their design was institutional and serial, and no individual artist is known; attribution to the king means royal commission rather than personal fabrication. ([Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Babylon”](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/hd/babl/hd_babl.htm); [French Ministry of Culture, Babylon](https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/babylone/en/ishtar-gate-and-processional-way))

- **605–562 BC:** *Glazed-brick lion relief program of the Processional Way*. A serial architectural image-field of striding lions placed along the ceremonial route. It resists separation into “sculpture,” “decoration,” and “architecture,” since image, wall, street, inscription, and procession formed one designed manifestation. ([Metropolitan Museum of Art, relief panel 31.13.1](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/322585); [French Ministry of Culture, Babylon](https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/babylone/en/ishtar-gate-and-processional-way))

- **605–562 BC:** *Royal reliefs at Brisa*. Two rock-cut images associated with the twin inscriptions, generally understood as representing Nebuchadnezzar in relation to divine symbols and royal building ideology. Weathering and the unusual iconography leave aspects of their interpretation disputed. ([Da Riva, *The Twin Inscriptions of Nebuchadnezzar at Brisa*](https://www.oeaw.ac.at/en/publications/the-twin-inscriptions-of-nebuchadnezzar-at-brisa))

### Projects and attributed creations that resist inclusion as works

- **605–562 BC; attribution disputed:** *Hanging Gardens of Babylon*. A celebrated garden-and-waterworks complex attributed to Nebuchadnezzar by later Greco-Roman writers, but not named in his surviving building inscriptions or securely identified by Babylon’s archaeology. Some scholarship relocates the relevant tradition to Sennacherib’s Nineveh; it therefore cannot be listed as an established work of Nebuchadnezzar. ([Stephanie Dalley, *The Mystery of the Hanging Garden of Babylon*](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-mystery-of-the-hanging-garden-of-babylon-9780199662265); [D. J. Wiseman, *Nebuchadrezzar and Babylon*](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/nebuchadrezzar-and-babylon-9780197261002))

- **605–562 BC:** *Imperial rebuilding of Babylon*. The coordinated transformation of the capital through temples, palaces, walls, gates, streets, canals, quays, inscriptions, and colored architectural imagery. This is a genuine royal project and perhaps the king’s largest obra, but it resists being called one “work” because it was collective, multigenerational, continuously revised, and executed by unnamed administrators, architects, scribes, artisans, and laborers. ([Da Riva, “Urban Religion in First Millennium BCE Babylonia”](https://iris.cnr.it/retrieve/7f5010c7-5933-4a33-8e0b-3a8a0adbfc26/10.1515_9783110798432.pdf); [UNESCO, Babylon](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/278/))
