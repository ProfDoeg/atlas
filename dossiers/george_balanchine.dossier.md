# George Balanchine: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier concerns George Balanchine, born Georgiy Melitonovich Balanchivadze (1904–1983), the Russian-born, Georgian-descended American dancer, choreographer, teacher, and institutional founder.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by institutional records, archival finding aids, court records, contemporary reporting, or multiple independent scholarly sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a participant, biographer, memoirist, or reputable secondary source but not independently verified here.
- **Allegation:** an accusation or adverse claim, identified as such.
- **Rumor or myth:** a circulated story whose factual basis is uncertain, incomplete, or demonstrably embellished.
- **Interpretation:** a critic’s or scholar’s analysis rather than a directly verifiable event.

Dates before Russia’s 1918 calendar change can appear in Old Style and New Style forms. Balanchine’s birthday is therefore variously given as 9 January 1904 Old Style and 22 January 1904 New Style.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full birth name:** Georgiy Melitonovich Balanchivadze.

**Georgian form:** Giorgi Melitonis dze Balanchivadze.

**Russian form:** Георгий Мелитонович Баланчивадзе.

**Professional name:** George Balanchine.

**Other catalog forms:** Georges Balanchine; György Balanchine; Zhorzh Balanchin; Georgi, George, or Gueorgui Balanchivadze.

**Nickname and professional address:** “Mr. B,” especially among his dancers and students.

**Birth:** 22 January 1904 New Style, corresponding to 9 January Old Style, Saint Petersburg, Russian Empire.

**Death:** 30 April 1983, Roosevelt Hospital, New York City.

**Immediate recorded cause:** pneumonia following a prolonged neurological illness.

**Postmortem diagnosis:** Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease, a fatal transmissible spongiform encephalopathy. The National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke’s institutional history reports that neuropathologist Philip Duffy made the diagnosis and that James Goldman later confirmed the characteristic tissue pathology. The underlying examination was not published as a conventional clinical case report.

**Burial:** 3 May 1983, Oakland Cemetery, Sag Harbor, Long Island, New York.

**Citizenship:** Russian imperial subject at birth; Soviet citizen after the Revolution; naturalized United States citizen in 1939.

**Occupations:** ballet dancer, choreographer, ballet master, teacher, company director, stage director, musical-theatre and film choreographer, occasional actor and pianist.

**Principal institutions:**

- Imperial Theater School/State Ballet School, Saint Petersburg–Petrograd
- State Academic Theater of Opera and Ballet, formerly and later the Mariinsky
- Young Ballet
- Serge Diaghilev’s Ballets Russes
- Les Ballets 1933
- School of American Ballet
- American Ballet
- American Ballet Caravan
- Ballet Russe de Monte Carlo
- Ballet Society
- New York City Ballet

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal family

Balanchine’s father, **Meliton Antonovich Balanchivadze** (1862/63–1937), was a Georgian singer, composer, teacher, and cultural organizer associated with the formation of Georgian opera. Georgian sources call him a founder of Georgian national operatic music and sometimes “the Georgian Glinka.”

Meliton came from western Georgia. Elizabeth Kendall’s archival research traces the paternal family to a rural clerical and musical milieu: Balanchine’s grandfather was reportedly a priest and schoolmaster, while a great-grandmother was remembered as a village elder and performer on the Georgian *chonguri*. These particulars are **reported facts** based on family and regional documentation summarized by Kendall.

Meliton had children from an earlier marriage to **Gayane Eristavi** and later formed a household with Balanchine’s mother. The exact legal status of the second union is uncertain. Kendall reports that no marriage certificate has been found and that surviving baptismal documents described Maria’s children as born to an unmarried mother. Other Georgian biographical sources list Maria straightforwardly as Meliton’s wife. The evidence therefore supports a family household but leaves the legal marriage unresolved.

### Maternal family

Balanchine’s mother was **Maria Nikolayevna Vasilyeva**, also identified in some sources through the surname von Almedingen or Allmendingen. She was Russian and reportedly had German ancestry. Accounts variously describe her as educated, musically capable, socially ambitious, a bank employee, a craftswoman, or formerly employed in Meliton’s household. These descriptions cannot all be reconciled from the accessible records.

Maria played the piano, valued ballet as education and social advancement, and was principally responsible for sending Georgiy to audition for the Imperial Theater School.

### Siblings

The children of Meliton and Maria included:

- A first child who reportedly died in infancy.
- **Tamara Balanchivadze**, Georgiy’s elder sister. She shared her mother’s interest in ballet and accompanied him to the school audition.
- **Andria or Andrei Balanchivadze** (1906–1992), Georgiy’s younger brother, who became a prominent Soviet Georgian composer.
- Half-siblings from Meliton’s earlier marriage.

Balanchine had no documented children.

### Family circumstances

The household alternated between Saint Petersburg and a country property at **Lounatjoki**, then in Finland within the Russian Empire. The land was reportedly bought with lottery winnings. Family recollections depict extensive gardening, piano practice, Georgian music, gatherings, food, and periods of unstable finances.

Meliton frequently worked away from the household and appears to have been an intermittent paternal presence. Kendall’s reconstruction portrays Georgiy as quieter and less favored than his siblings; this is a biographical interpretation grounded partly in family testimony rather than a provable psychological fact.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1904–1913

Balanchine’s early childhood passed between cosmopolitan Saint Petersburg and the Finnish countryside. He began piano lessons around 1909. Music was not ancillary to his later choreography: he developed enough keyboard ability, sight-reading skill, and theoretical knowledge to read orchestral scores and prepare reductions.

He later said he had not initially wanted to become a dancer. His mother brought him to the Imperial Theater School audition with Tamara. Georgiy was accepted while his sister was not.

Family accounts remembered Lounatjoki as one of the last stable landscapes of his childhood: forest, snow, vegetable gardens, a lake, and a relatively isolated domestic life before boarding-school discipline, war, revolution, and hunger.

## Education and Formation, 1913–1924

### Imperial Theater School

Balanchine entered the ballet division of the **Imperial Theater School** in Saint Petersburg in 1913 as a day student and became a boarder in 1914.

Teachers named in Russian and institutional sources include:

- Pavel Gerdt
- Samuil Andrianov
- possibly other faculty inherited from the late-imperial Mariinsky system

His curriculum combined ballet, character dancing, mime, academic subjects, music, and theater discipline. He first appeared on the Mariinsky stage in 1915 as a cupid in Marius Petipa’s *The Sleeping Beauty*. Balanchine later treated this encounter with the stage spectacle as a decisive revelation.

### Revolution and hunger

The school closed during the 1917 Revolution and subsequently reopened under Soviet authority as the State Ballet School. Students lost imperial subsidies and food security. Balanchine later recalled severe hunger, scavenging, and performing practical musical work for survival.

He played piano in cabarets, silent-film houses, and other commercial settings, sometimes receiving food rather than reliable currency. These experiences exposed him to popular songs, ragtime, revue structures, jazz-inflected music, and direct audience response.

### Musical education

Around 1920 he entered the **Petrograd Conservatory**, studying piano, theory, counterpoint, harmony, and composition while continuing ballet work. His conservatory training was formative to his score-centered choreography. He did not become a professional composer, although catalog records attribute a *Valse lente* to him.

### Graduation and Mariinsky employment

Balanchine graduated from the ballet school in 1921 and joined the State Academic Theater of Opera and Ballet, the renamed Mariinsky.

He was a capable classical dancer but not generally described as the company’s dominant virtuoso. His historical importance soon shifted toward choreography.

### Young Ballet and experimentation

Balanchine choreographed his first survivingly documented work, *La Nuit*, around 1920, to music by Anton Rubinstein. School authorities disliked or rejected his experimental work.

In 1922–23 he organized **Young Ballet**, a small laboratory of dancers exploring acrobatics, contemporary music, irregular movement, and departures from official academic conventions. Participants included Tamara Geva and other young Petrograd dancers.

The ensemble presented “Evenings of the Young Ballet.” Its activities attracted official disapproval, but claims that Balanchine was formally persecuted require qualification: he continued to work inside Soviet theatrical institutions until his foreign tour.

One 1923 project choreographed Alexander Blok’s revolutionary poem *The Twelve* for a large speaking or choral ensemble. Lincoln Kirstein later cited it as evidence of Balanchine’s early openness to revolutionary artistic culture.

## Marriages, Partners, and Children

Balanchine had four documented legal marriages and one long common-law partnership. All were with dancers.

### Tamara Geva

**Tamara Geva**, born Tamara Zheverzheyeva, was his first wife and a member of the group that left Soviet Russia in 1924. Sources disagree on whether they married in 1922 or 1924 and on her exact age; the strongest accounts place her in her later teens.

The marriage ended in separation during the Ballets Russes years. The legal divorce chronology is inconsistently reported. Geva later became a Broadway performer and memoirist.

A family legend says Geva’s father approved the marriage after Balanchine played Wagner for him. This is a **family story**, not independently documented.

### Alexandra Danilova

Balanchine lived with ballerina **Alexandra Danilova** for approximately seven years during the later Ballets Russes and post-Diaghilev period. They were widely regarded socially as husband and wife but apparently never legally married, in part because Balanchine’s divorce status remained unresolved.

Danilova was both intimate partner and major artistic collaborator. She later described their poverty and professional instability after Diaghilev’s death without presenting herself simply as a passive “muse.”

### Vera Zorina

Balanchine married **Vera Zorina**, born Eva Brigitta Hartwig, on 24 December 1938. A German-Norwegian dancer and film actress, she linked his theatrical and Hollywood periods. They divorced in 1946.

### Maria Tallchief

He married **Maria Tallchief** in 1946. Tallchief, an Osage dancer from Oklahoma, became one of the first internationally prominent American prima ballerinas. Balanchine created or substantially refashioned major roles for her, including the title role in *The Firebird* and the Sugar Plum Fairy in his *Nutcracker*.

Their marriage ended in 1952. Tallchief’s memoir depicts Balanchine as personally reserved, professionally transformative, and unusually domestic in his enthusiasm for cooking. She also recalled that he preferred twin beds. Their partnership was both marital and an employer–star relationship.

### Tanaquil Le Clercq

Balanchine married **Tanaquil Le Clercq** in 1952. She had entered the School of American Ballet as a child and embodied the elongated, fast, off-axis American classicism associated with his mature style.

During New York City Ballet’s 1956 European tour, Le Clercq contracted poliomyelitis in Copenhagen and was permanently paralyzed in her legs. Balanchine suspended work, cared for her, and attempted rehabilitation exercises. Their marriage later deteriorated, and they divorced in 1969.

A frequently repeated “prophecy” story concerns *Resurgence*, a 1944 March of Dimes benefit in which the teenage Le Clercq portrayed a polio victim who rose from a wheelchair and danced. The choreography and benefit are documented; the later interpretation as supernatural premonition is **retrospective myth**.

### Suzanne Farrell

Balanchine became intensely attached to ballerina **Suzanne Farrell**, born Roberta Sue Ficker. He created important roles for her and proposed marriage while still connected to Le Clercq. Farrell declined and married fellow dancer **Paul Mejia** in 1969.

Farrell and Mejia subsequently left, or were forced from, New York City Ballet under circumstances variously described:

- Farrell’s memoir says the working situation became untenable after her marriage.
- Contemporary observers and later biographers describe Balanchine’s behavior as obsessive and retaliatory.
- Some accounts say Farrell resigned; others describe both dancers as effectively dismissed.

The documentary record supports an employer’s romantic pursuit of a much younger subordinate followed by adverse professional consequences after she married someone else. Whether every company decision was consciously retaliatory remains partly interpretive.

Farrell joined Maurice Béjart’s Ballet of the Twentieth Century and returned to New York City Ballet in 1975. Balanchine again created roles for her, including in *Chaconne*, *Tzigane*, *Robert Schumann’s “Davidsbündlertänze”*, and *Mozartiana*.

## Departure from the Soviet Union and Ballets Russes, 1924–1929

### The 1924 tour

In 1924 Balanchine, Geva, Danilova, and Nikolai Efimov received permission to tour Germany and western Europe as the Principal Dancers of the Russian State Ballet. They did not return.

Later Cold War accounts called Balanchine a “defector.” The initial departure was legally authorized, but the decision to remain abroad converted the tour into permanent emigration.

### Serge Diaghilev

The group auditioned for **Serge Diaghilev**, who incorporated them into the Ballets Russes. Diaghilev simplified Balanchivadze to “Balanchine,” although exact stories about the renaming vary.

Balanchine became dancer, ballet master, and increasingly principal choreographer. Diaghilev directed him toward museums, literature, painting, and music and demanded that choreography function inside a total theatrical work.

### Illness and shift to choreography

Balanchine suffered a serious knee injury or illness during the mid-1920s. Accounts sometimes identify tuberculosis or other complications, but the precise medical record is unclear. Reduced dancing capacity accelerated his movement toward choreography.

### Important works

His Ballets Russes works included:

- *Le Chant du rossignol* / *The Song of the Nightingale* (1925), Stravinsky
- dances for Ravel’s *L’Enfant et les sortilèges* (1925)
- *Jack in the Box* (1926), Erik Satie
- *La Chatte* (1927), Henri Sauguet, designs by Naum Gabo and Antoine Pevsner
- *Apollon musagète* / *Apollo* (1928), Stravinsky
- *The Gods Go A-Begging* (1928), Handel arrangements
- *Le Bal* (1929), Vittorio Rieti, designs associated with Giorgio de Chirico
- *Le Fils prodigue* / *The Prodigal Son* (1929), Sergei Prokofiev, designs by Georges Rouault

*Apollo* began the defining Balanchine–Stravinsky collaboration. Balanchine progressively stripped the work of décor and narrative detail, treating classical steps, spatial counterpoint, and music as the principal dramatic materials.

### Diaghilev’s death

Diaghilev died in Venice in August 1929. The Ballets Russes dissolved, leaving Balanchine and many colleagues without a stable institutional base.

---

## Europe After Diaghilev, 1929–1933

Balanchine worked across Paris, London, Copenhagen, Monte Carlo, and other European centers.

His employment included:

- Paris and London music-hall and revue choreography
- work for Cochran’s Revue
- guest ballet master duties at the Royal Danish Ballet
- staging or revising ballets by Michel Fokine and Léonide Massine
- choreography for companies formed around former Ballets Russes dancers
- opera and film work
- an unsuccessful attempt to establish a durable company of his own

### Les Ballets 1933

With Boris Kochno and financial support from British patron **Edward James**, Balanchine formed **Les Ballets 1933**. Its only season included:

- *The Seven Deadly Sins*, Bertolt Brecht and Kurt Weill
- *Errante*, Schubert arrangements
- *Les Songes*, Darius Milhaud
- *Fastes*, Henri Sauguet
- *Mozartiana*, using Tchaikovsky’s music
- other short works

The company was artistically ambitious but financially short-lived.

### Lincoln Kirstein meeting

In London in 1933 Balanchine met **Lincoln Kirstein**, a wealthy American writer, editor, arts organizer, and patron. Kirstein proposed building a permanent American classical company with American-trained dancers. Balanchine accepted.

The celebrated reply “But first, a school” is consistent with their institutional decisions and appears throughout Balanchine history, but its precise wording and occasion should be treated as a **reported quotation**, not a stenographic record.

Balanchine arrived in New York in October 1933.

---

## Building American Ballet, 1934–1941

### School of American Ballet

Balanchine, Kirstein, and Edward M. M. Warburg founded the **School of American Ballet** in January 1934. Balanchine served as principal artistic and pedagogical authority; Kirstein organized finance and administration.

The school trained dancers for a projected company and developed the style later called “Balanchine technique”: speed, musical attack, deep plié, open lines, articulate feet, displaced hips, expansive arabesques, flexible use of classical positions, and movement designed for large theatrical space.

Balanchine himself did not publish a fixed technical manual. Later formulations are reconstructions by dancers and teachers; claims that any single contemporary syllabus reproduces his teaching exactly are therefore disputable.

### Serenade

In 1934 he made *Serenade* to Tchaikovsky’s *Serenade for Strings* for students. Its outdoor premiere took place at Warburg’s estate near White Plains, New York. Accidents of rehearsal—a late dancer, a fall—were reportedly incorporated into the ballet. The extent to which each detail originated accidentally has become part of the work’s mythology.

### American Ballet and Metropolitan Opera

The professional **American Ballet** was founded in 1935. It became the Metropolitan Opera’s resident ballet ensemble, with Balanchine serving as ballet master and choreographing dances for operas and independent programs.

The institutional relationship failed. The Metropolitan Opera primarily wanted serviceable opera ballets, while Balanchine and Kirstein wanted an autonomous repertory company. The engagement ended in 1938.

### Broadway and Hollywood

Balanchine treated commercial theater as legitimate theatrical practice, not merely a financial detour. Broadway projects included:

- *On Your Toes* (1936), Rodgers and Hart — especially *Slaughter on Tenth Avenue*
- *Babes in Arms* (1937)
- *I Married an Angel* (1938)
- *The Boys from Syracuse* (1938)
- *Louisiana Purchase* (1940)
- *Cabin in the Sky* (1940), staged with Katherine Dunham
- later *Where’s Charley?* (1948) and other projects

Film work included choreography or dance direction for:

- *The Goldwyn Follies* (1938)
- *On Your Toes* (1939)
- *I Was an Adventuress* (1940)
- *Star Spangled Rhythm* (1942)

His Broadway work helped integrate extended ballet sequences into musical drama. *Slaughter on Tenth Avenue* made dance the engine of the plot rather than a decorative interruption.

### Naturalization

Balanchine became a United States citizen in 1939. His later works drew overtly on American popular culture and patriotic iconography, including Westerns, marching bands, Broadway, jazz rhythm, and the Fourth of July.

---

## American Ballet Caravan and Wartime Work, 1941–1946

### South American tour

Balanchine and Kirstein organized **American Ballet Caravan**, which toured South America in 1941 under cultural-diplomatic auspices.

The repertory included:

- *Concerto Barocco*, Bach
- *Ballet Imperial*, later titled *Tchaikovsky Piano Concerto No. 2*
- works by Lew Christensen and other American choreographers

The tour functioned both as performance enterprise and as United States cultural diplomacy during wartime hemispheric policy.

### Ballet Russe de Monte Carlo

From 1944 to 1946 Balanchine worked for Sergei Denham’s Ballet Russe de Monte Carlo, touring extensively in the United States. Works included:

- *Danses concertantes*, Stravinsky
- *Raymonda*
- *Night Shadow*, later *La Sonnambula*
- revivals of *Serenade* and other repertory

This employment gave him a large touring company but not the permanent institution he and Kirstein wanted.

## Ballet Society and New York City Ballet, 1946–1964

### Ballet Society

In 1946 Balanchine and Kirstein founded **Ballet Society**, initially a subscription organization. Major works included:

- *The Four Temperaments* (1946), Paul Hindemith
- *The Spellbound Child*
- *Symphonie concertante*
- *Orpheus* (1948), Stravinsky, designs by Isamu Noguchi

After seeing *Orpheus*, City Center official **Morton Baum** invited the organization to become a resident company.

### New York City Ballet

On 11 October 1948, a program of *Concerto Barocco*, *Orpheus*, and *Symphony in C* inaugurated **New York City Ballet**.

Balanchine was ballet master and chief choreographer; Kirstein was the principal administrator and institutional strategist. Their partnership was complementary but unequal in artistic mythology: Kirstein raised funds, negotiated, wrote, recruited patrons, and built administrative continuity, while Balanchine controlled choreography, repertory, training, and casting.

### Jerome Robbins

Jerome Robbins joined the company in 1949 as associate artistic director and choreographer. Their relationship combined cooperation, competition, and stylistic difference. Robbins supplied psychologically and theatrically detailed works; Balanchine retained ultimate artistic primacy during his lifetime.

### Major works, 1946–1964

Among the central ballets of this period:

- *The Four Temperaments* (1946)
- *Theme and Variations* (1947)
- *Symphony in C* (1947)
- *Orpheus* (1948)
- *Bourrée fantasque* (1949)
- *La Valse* (1951)
- *Scotch Symphony* (1952)
- *Western Symphony* (1954)
- *The Nutcracker* (1954)
- *Ivesiana* (1954)
- *Agon* (1957)
- *Square Dance* (1957)
- *Stars and Stripes* (1958)
- *Episodes* (1959), joint program with Martha Graham
- *Monumentum pro Gesualdo* (1960)
- *Liebeslieder Walzer* (1960)
- *A Midsummer Night’s Dream* (1962)
- *Movements for Piano and Orchestra* (1963)

### The Nutcracker

Balanchine’s 1954 *Nutcracker* became a durable New York and American Christmas institution. It generated annual box-office income, a large children’s cast, and a model widely imitated by regional companies.

Claims that Balanchine single-handedly created the American *Nutcracker* tradition are exaggerated: other American productions preceded his. His version nevertheless proved institutionally decisive in making the ballet an annual financial anchor.

### Agon and Arthur Mitchell

Balanchine admitted **Arthur Mitchell** to New York City Ballet in 1955 and created the central *Agon* pas de deux for Mitchell and white ballerina Diana Adams in 1957.

Mitchell recalled that, when a presenter objected to his presence, Balanchine said that if Mitchell did not dance, the company would not dance. This is a **reported fact from Mitchell’s testimony**.

The interracial casting was unusually conspicuous in 1950s American ballet. Later scholarship complicates the heroic version: Mitchell remained exceptional in a predominantly white institution, and later all-white casting sometimes removed the interracial charge from *Agon*. Balanchine’s action was materially important but did not create a consistently integrated company.

### Episodes with Martha Graham

Balanchine and **Martha Graham** jointly presented *Episodes* in 1959 to music by Anton Webern. They choreographed separate halves for their respective companies, with several crossover performers. It was a documented collaboration between leading representatives of American modern dance and neoclassical ballet, but not an integrated co-choreography in which they made the same dances together.

### Ford Foundation finance

On 16 December 1963 the Ford Foundation announced a ten-year ballet program totaling approximately **$7.756 million**. About $2 million went to New York City Ballet and $2.425 million to the School of American Ballet, with other funds supporting associated companies and schools.

This funding consolidated Balanchine’s institutional influence throughout American ballet. It also generated criticism that one aesthetic network had received disproportionate power over training and repertory.

## Lincoln Center and the Mature Company, 1964–1974

### New York State Theater

New York City Ballet formally opened the **New York State Theater** at Lincoln Center on 23 April 1964. The building, costing approximately $19 million, incorporated Balanchine’s preferences for stage dimensions, sightlines, backstage movement, and orchestra–dance relations.

Balanchine thereby became one of the rare choreographers whose institutional authority extended into the architecture of a major purpose-built theater.

### Don Quixote and Farrell

Balanchine’s *Don Quixote* premiered in 1965, with Balanchine himself as the Don and Suzanne Farrell as Dulcinea. Contemporary and later accounts interpret the ballet as an unusually personal dramatization of idealized, impossible love. The biographical parallel is strong but remains an **interpretation**, not proof that every scene was autobiographical.

### Jewels

*Jewels* premiered in 1967:

- *Emeralds*, to Gabriel Fauré
- *Rubies*, to Stravinsky
- *Diamonds*, to Tchaikovsky

Often called the first full-length “abstract” ballet, it has no continuous plot but uses costume, music, and style to evoke French, American, and Russian traditions. Balanchine himself frequently resisted the label “abstract,” emphasizing that bodies, music, and stage relationships inevitably created meaning.

### Farrell crisis

During 1968–69 Balanchine divorced Le Clercq and sought to marry Farrell. Farrell instead married Paul Mejia. Their departure from New York City Ballet intensified the perception that personal loyalty and casting were inseparable under Balanchine’s leadership.

### Stravinsky Festival

After Stravinsky’s death in 1971, Balanchine and Robbins organized the 1972 Stravinsky Festival. Over eight days the company performed thirty-one ballets to Stravinsky, twenty-two newly created by seven choreographers.

Balanchine’s festival works included *Stravinsky Violin Concerto*, *Duo Concertant*, *Symphony in Three Movements*, and *Divertimento from “Le Baiser de la fée.”* The festival reinforced the Balanchine–Stravinsky partnership as a central monument of twentieth-century music and dance.

## Final Creative Decade, 1975–1983

Farrell returned in 1975, restoring an artistically productive if personally altered relationship.

Major late works included:

- *Chaconne* (1976), Gluck
- *Union Jack* (1976), British music and pageantry
- *Vienna Waltzes* (1977), Johann Strauss II, Lehár, Richard Strauss
- *Ballade* (1980), Fauré
- *Robert Schumann’s “Davidsbündlertänze”* (1980)
- *Mozartiana* (1981), Tchaikovsky
- *Variations for Orchestra* (1982), Stravinsky

### Festivals

Balanchine used festival programming to reorganize the company around composers and national traditions:

- Stravinsky Festival, 1972
- Ravel Festival, 1975
- Tchaikovsky Festival, 1981

Festivals encouraged new choreography, concentrated publicity, and treated a ballet company as an institution capable of sustained musical interpretation.

### Illness

Balanchine suffered angina and a heart attack or serious cardiac episode in the late 1970s, followed by coronary bypass surgery. After initial recovery, his memory, balance, vision, coordination, and general neurological function deteriorated.

By 1982 he was substantially incapacitated. The illness was initially described publicly as a progressive neurological disorder. On 16 March 1983 he was named ballet master emeritus; Peter Martins and Jerome Robbins became ballet masters-in-chief.

He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in absentia in March 1983.

Balanchine died on 30 April. The company performed its scheduled program that night.

## Artistic and Intellectual Development

### Music first

Balanchine’s most durable principle was that choreography should make musical structure visible without merely illustrating it. His conservatory background allowed him to work from scores, identify inner voices, and structure ensembles contrapuntally.

“See the music, hear the dance” is widely attributed to him. Its exact first formulation is uncertain and it functions partly as a condensed institutional motto.

### Classical vocabulary transformed

He retained pointe work, turnout, steps, partnering, and compositional principles inherited from Petipa and the Imperial school while altering:

- speed and rhythmic attack
- degree of extension
- balance and off-balance movement
- flexed hands and feet when musically useful
- asymmetry
- parallel and turned-in positions
- visibility of effort and muscular articulation
- sparse décor and practice clothing
- hierarchy between plot and pure movement

### “Ballet is woman”

Balanchine repeatedly described ballet as centered on women. His repertory gave ballerinas extraordinary choreographic complexity and stage authority. It also placed their bodies under a male director’s close aesthetic, professional, and personal control.

The slogan can therefore support two documented readings: women as the generative center of his art, and women as selected, shaped, and ranked by a powerful male choreographer.

### Orthodoxy

Balanchine remained a practicing Russian Orthodox Christian. Kirstein described his faith as habitual and unswerving. Religious imagery appears in *Prodigal Son*, *Orpheus*, *Mozartiana*, *Davidsbündlertänze*, and the Tchaikovsky Festival finale.

He requested Mozart’s *Requiem* for his memorial observance and received Orthodox burial rites.

### Politics

Balanchine was not a party politician. His political trajectory included:

- youthful participation in experimental post-revolutionary Soviet culture
- permanent emigration from the Soviet Union
- American naturalization
- cultural-diplomatic touring
- patriotic works such as *Stars and Stripes*
- 1962 New York City Ballet tour of the Soviet Union
- public identification with the United States during the Cold War

Scholarship on the 1962 tour indicates that Balanchine and the company participated in State Department cultural diplomacy. This does not establish intelligence work. No credible evidence located for this dossier identifies Balanchine as an intelligence officer or covert operative.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Lincoln Kirstein

Co-founder, patron, fundraiser, writer, and administrator. Their partnership from 1933 until Balanchine’s death created the School of American Ballet and New York City Ballet. Kirstein’s wealth, social networks, and institutional persistence made Balanchine’s American project possible.

### Igor Stravinsky

Composer, friend, artistic partner, and model of neoclassical organization. Their work extended from *Apollo* through *Agon*, *Orpheus*, *Movements*, *Stravinsky Violin Concerto*, and numerous other ballets.

### Serge Diaghilev

Employer and mentor. Diaghilev gave Balanchine international access, renamed him professionally, and trained him in collaborative theatrical production.

### Boris Kochno and Edward James

Kochno, Diaghilev’s former secretary, helped organize Les Ballets 1933; James financed it.

### Jerome Robbins

Resident choreographer, colleague, and partial rival at New York City Ballet. Robbins shared leadership after Balanchine’s incapacitation.

### Ballerinas and dancers

Principal creative relationships included:

- Alexandra Danilova
- Tamara Geva
- Vera Zorina
- Maria Tallchief
- Tanaquil Le Clercq
- Suzanne Farrell
- Alicia Markova
- Tamara Toumanova
- Diana Adams
- Allegra Kent
- Violette Verdy
- Patricia McBride
- Karin von Aroldingen
- Gelsey Kirkland
- Merrill Ashley
- Maria Calegari
- Arthur Mitchell
- Jacques d’Amboise
- Edward Villella
- Peter Martins
- Paul Mejia
- Nicholas Magallanes

The term “muse” is historically common but can obscure the dancers’ own technical, interpretive, and creative labor.

### Designers and visual artists

Collaborators included Isamu Noguchi, Pavel Tchelitchew, Georges Rouault, Karinska, Barbara Karinska, Naum Gabo, Antoine Pevsner, and others.

## Companies, Projects, Finance, Property, and Intellectual Property

Balanchine was not chiefly an entrepreneur in the conventional commercial sense. His durable “businesses” were nonprofit artistic institutions and the repertory they controlled.

### School of American Ballet

Founded in 1934 and incorporated as a nonprofit in 1940. Its institutional purpose was both educational and strategic: it supplied dancers trained for Balanchine’s company.

### New York City Ballet

Founded through Ballet Society’s transformation in 1948. Balanchine’s authority encompassed repertory, casting, teaching, rehearsal, design, and musical choices.

### Personal finances

Accessible public sources do not provide a reliable comprehensive account of Balanchine’s personal wealth. He experienced instability in the early European years but later received salary, theatrical fees, film and Broadway income, licensing payments, and royalties.

He maintained a summer home in Sag Harbor. No significant investment portfolio, patents, or proprietary technologies are documented.

### Estate and copyrights

Balanchine licensed ballets during his lifetime. His will distributed rights among several individuals rather than creating a single hereditary succession.

In **Horgan v. Macmillan** (1986), executrix Barbara Horgan sought to prevent publication of a photographic book reproducing the sequence of Balanchine’s *Nutcracker*. The federal appellate court held that photographs could potentially infringe choreographic copyright and reversed the lower court’s categorical treatment sufficiently to permit further consideration. The case became an important American precedent concerning copyright in choreography.

The **George Balanchine Trust** was established in 1987 by rights holders including Barbara Horgan and Karin von Aroldingen. It licenses productions, approves répétiteurs, collects fees and royalties, and regulates use of Balanchine’s name and choreography.

In 2019 former ballerina **Gelsey Kirkland** initiated legal action questioning management of the rights and alleging that assets had been moved from the trust into private Delaware entities controlled by Horgan and associates. These were **civil allegations concerning governance and control**, not criminal charges or findings of fraud. Public reporting described disagreement over whether the restructuring endangered the repertory or represented legitimate administration. No criminal conviction, corruption finding, or money-laundering evidence was located.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Criticism

### Sexual and professional power

**Documented structure:** Balanchine repeatedly formed romantic or marital relationships with dancers whom he taught, employed, cast, and promoted.

**Reported pattern:** Biographers, memoirists, and Kirstein’s diaries describe additional affairs with dancers. Jennifer Homans reports a 1930s rumor, preserved in Kirstein’s diary, that dancer Holly Howard underwent several abortions while associated with Balanchine’s company. This remains a **historical rumor**, not a verified medical fact.

**Farrell case:** The strongest documented controversy involves Balanchine’s pursuit of Suzanne Farrell and the professional consequences following her marriage to Paul Mejia. The available evidence supports a serious conflict of interest and an abuse-prone concentration of personal and employment authority. Sources disagree about whether Farrell formally resigned or was dismissed.

No court charge or criminal finding of sexual assault against Balanchine was located.

### Le Clercq after polio

Balanchine initially devoted substantial time to Le Clercq’s care and rehabilitation. Later accounts accuse him of emotional coldness, infidelity, or abandonment as the marriage failed.

These adverse accounts derive principally from biographies, recollections, and interpretation of their divorce. They are not judicial findings. Orel Protopopescu’s biography of Le Clercq presents a more complicated relationship and disputes a simple tyranny-and-abandonment narrative.

### Body type and eating disorders

Balanchine preferred long-legged, highly flexible, visibly linear dancers and made direct comments about weight and breasts. Former dancers recall remarks such as being “fat” in particular areas and an institutional preference for extreme thinness.

Gelsey Kirkland’s memoir connected starvation, body alteration, plastic surgery, drug use, and psychological distress to ballet culture and Balanchine’s authority. Other dancers rejected exclusive causal blame, noting that eating disorders have multiple medical and cultural determinants.

The evidence supports:

- a documented aesthetic preference for unusually thin, elongated dancers;
- documented humiliating or intrusive comments about dancers’ bodies;
- reported eating disorders among dancers in the Balanchine-era institution.

It does **not** establish that Balanchine alone caused a particular dancer’s illness or that every dancer experienced the environment identically.

### Gender and the “muse” system

Scholars criticize the system in which women appeared visually supreme while men retained choreographic, directorial, and financial authority. Defenders emphasize the complexity, agency, and centrality of female roles.

Both are interpretations supported by different parts of the record. The repertory simultaneously enlarged female technical possibility and emerged from an unequal employer–performer hierarchy.

### Race

Balanchine’s engagement of Arthur Mitchell and interracial casting in *Agon* were concrete challenges to racial exclusion. Mitchell credited Balanchine with defending him against discriminatory presenters.

Critics nevertheless identify limits:

- New York City Ballet remained overwhelmingly white.
- Mitchell was treated as exceptional rather than inaugurating broad integration.
- Later white casting in *Agon* erased the work’s original interracial encounter.
- Balanchine’s “color-blind” or abstract rhetoric could obscure how audiences assigned racial meanings to bodies.

No evidence supports calling either a wholly egalitarian institution or a simple program of deliberate racial exclusion. The record is mixed and historically specific.

### Centralization of American ballet

The Ford Foundation grants, School of American Ballet pipeline, touring network, and Balanchine licensing system concentrated funding and prestige around one style. Critics saw aesthetic monopoly; supporters saw the creation of durable professional standards.

This was an institutional controversy, not evidence of illegal financial conduct.

### Cold War cultural diplomacy

New York City Ballet’s foreign tours served American diplomacy, especially during the 1962 Soviet tour. Balanchine’s emigration made him symbolically useful as an artist who had left the USSR and flourished in the United States.

Claims of secret intelligence connections remain unsupported by the sources consulted. Participation in State Department cultural programs is documented; espionage is not.

### Adversarial search, including 2024–2026 material

Dedicated searches combined Balanchine’s name with *controversy, scandal, allegations, lawsuit, fraud, accusations, conspiracy,* and *criticism*, including 2024–2026.

The recent material located principally revisits:

- body-image and eating-disorder legacies;
- sexual and employment power over female dancers;
- racial meanings in *Agon*;
- intellectual-property control;
- continued institutional authority of the “Balanchine aesthetic.”

No newly reported 2024–2026 criminal investigation, indictment, substantiated fraud scheme, corruption case, money-laundering allegation, or credible conspiracy evidence involving Balanchine personally was found. Recent claims largely reinterpret previously available memoirs, diaries, and institutional history.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### “Father of American ballet”

**Story:** Balanchine created American ballet.

**Evidence:** He decisively built its most influential school and company and developed a distinctive repertory. Ballet, however, existed in the United States before 1933 through touring European artists, immigrant teachers, vaudeville, opera ballet, and earlier companies.

**Effect:** The phrase strengthens his founder mythology while minimizing predecessor traditions and collaborators, especially Kirstein, teachers, dancers, regional schools, and women choreographers.

### “But first, a school”

**Story:** When Kirstein proposed an American company, Balanchine immediately answered, “But first, a school.”

**Evidence:** The school did precede the stable company, making the saying structurally true. The exact exchange is not securely documented as a verbatim contemporary record.

**Effect:** It condenses a complex institutional strategy into a founder’s aphorism.

### “See the music, hear the dance”

**Story:** Balanchine’s entire method is contained in this sentence.

**Evidence:** It accurately summarizes his musical approach but circulates as an attributed maxim with uncertain original occasion.

**Effect:** It supports a near-mystical image of Balanchine as music’s visual translator.

### Le Clercq’s polio prophecy

**Story:** Balanchine foretold Le Clercq’s paralysis by choreographing her as a polio victim in *Resurgence*.

**Evidence:** The March of Dimes ballet and wheelchair scenario predated her illness. There is no evidence of supernatural prediction; polio benefits were widespread because the disease was a major contemporary danger.

**Effect:** It turned Le Clercq’s illness into tragic theatrical fate and reinforced Balanchine’s reputation as an uncanny visionary.

### Serenade’s accidents

**Story:** Its famous gestures came entirely from rehearsal accidents, including a late dancer and a fall.

**Evidence:** Balanchine did incorporate rehearsal circumstances, but repeated retellings vary and can exaggerate improvisation at the expense of deliberate composition and revision.

**Effect:** The story presents him as a magician transforming contingency into timeless form.

### Five wives

**Story:** Balanchine married five ballerinas.

**Evidence:** Four legal wives are documented: Geva, Zorina, Tallchief, and Le Clercq. Alexandra Danilova was a long-term common-law partner, not demonstrably a legal wife.

**Effect:** Counting Danilova dramatizes the repeated fusion of erotic and choreographic relationships but obscures legal distinctions.

### Balanchine as benevolent “Mr. B”

**Story:** A paternal genius intuitively created ideal roles for devoted dancers.

**Evidence:** Many dancers described love, gratitude, and artistic transformation. Others recorded favoritism, fear, body criticism, dependence, and retaliation.

**Effect:** The affectionate title helped produce institutional cohesion while masking unequal power.

### Balanchine as controlling monster

**Story:** Every dancer was an interchangeable instrument subjected to total personal domination.

**Evidence:** His authority was unusually concentrated, and documented abuses of power existed. Yet testimony varies greatly; dancers also exercised interpretive agency, resisted him, left, returned, and materially shaped the repertory.

**Effect:** This hostile counter-myth emerged more strongly through memoir, feminist criticism, body politics, and post-#MeToo institutional reassessment.

## Death and Funeral

Balanchine’s final neurological decline became visible through memory loss, poor coordination, visual disturbance, and inability to work. Contemporary public notices identified pneumonia as the immediate cause and described the underlying illness nonspecifically.

Postmortem neuropathology established Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease. Later review reported severe spongiform changes in the neocortex and thalamic nuclei and kuru-type plaques in the cerebellum. Institutional medical history states that tissue from Balanchine transmitted disease experimentally to a chimpanzee.

He received Russian Orthodox rites. He was buried in Sag Harbor, where he had kept a summer residence. Alexandra Danilova was later buried nearby.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Balanchine’s repertory remains central to New York City Ballet and is licensed to companies internationally. The School of American Ballet continues as NYCB’s principal training institution.

His effects include:

- establishment of a durable American classical-ballet company and school;
- expansion of plotless and symphonic ballet;
- integration of American vernacular rhythm with imperial Russian technique;
- transformation of ballet’s relationship to Broadway, film, and television;
- a score-centered model of choreography;
- internationally disseminated Balanchine staging and pedagogy;
- choreographic copyright and preservation practices centered on authorized répétiteurs.

Posthumous reinterpretation increasingly examines:

- the creative agency of dancers previously called “muses”;
- body ideals and eating disorders;
- romantic pursuit within employer–employee hierarchies;
- race and the original casting of *Agon*;
- the concentration of philanthropic funding;
- copyright control and institutional ownership;
- the gap between affectionate memory of “Mr. B” and testimony about fear or dependency.

His legacy is consequently neither static nor uniformly celebratory. The same institutional record supports admiration for the repertory, criticism of power structures, and continuing dispute about how faithfully choreography can—or should—be preserved.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by consulted sources are included. Mere contemporaneity is excluded.

- **Igor Fyodorovich Stravinsky — documented fact.** Balanchine met Stravinsky through the Ballets Russes in the 1920s. Their collaboration began decisively with *Apollo* (1928) and continued through *The Card Party*, *Orpheus*, *Agon*, *Movements*, and the 1972 Stravinsky Festival. Locations included Paris/Monte Carlo and New York.

- **Sergei Pavlovich Diaghilev — documented fact.** Diaghilev hired Balanchine and his fellow émigré dancers in 1924–25, gave him his professional surname, and made him a principal Ballets Russes choreographer. Their association continued until Diaghilev’s death in Venice in 1929.

- **Martha Graham — documented fact.** Graham and Balanchine participated in the 1959 *Episodes* project at New York City Center. They choreographed separate sections to Webern for the Martha Graham Dance Company and New York City Ballet.

- **Arthur Mitchell — documented fact.** Balanchine admitted Mitchell to New York City Ballet in 1955, created major roles for him, and paired him with Diana Adams in the interracial *Agon* pas de deux in 1957. Mitchell later reported that Balanchine defended his casting against racist objections.

- **Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky — documented artistic influence, not a personal meeting.** Tchaikovsky died before Balanchine’s birth. His music was central to *Serenade*, *Ballet Imperial*, *Theme and Variations*, *The Nutcracker*, *Diamonds*, *Mozartiana*, and the 1981 Tchaikovsky Festival. Balanchine explicitly discussed Tchaikovsky in interviews with Solomon Volkov.

- **Achille-Claude Debussy — documented artistic use, no personal meeting established.** Balanchine choreographed works to Debussy, including versions associated with *La Mer* and other concert or theatrical projects recorded in the catalogue. Debussy died in 1918; no meeting has been established.

- **Erik Satie — documented artistic use, no personal meeting established.** Balanchine choreographed *Jack in the Box* to Satie in 1926 within the Ballets Russes environment. Evidence of a direct meeting was not located.

- **Pablo Ruiz Picasso — documented shared Ballets Russes artistic network; direct collaboration requires qualification.** Both worked in Diaghilev’s Ballets Russes world. Picasso designed for Ballets Russes productions, while Balanchine became choreographer in its final phase. The consulted sources support institutional overlap but do not establish that Picasso personally designed a Balanchine premiere.

- **Gabrielle Bonheur “Coco” Chanel — documented shared Ballets Russes network; personal connection not established here.** Chanel supported and designed for Diaghilev’s enterprise. Balanchine later worked as its choreographer. No specific direct Balanchine–Chanel collaboration was securely established from the consulted material.

- **Vaslav Nijinsky — documented institutional lineage, no meeting established.** Both were major Ballets Russes choreographers in different phases. Nijinsky’s active career had effectively ended before Balanchine joined Diaghilev. Sources treat them as successive innovators rather than documented collaborators.

- **Anna Matveyevna Pavlova — documented shared Imperial/Ballets Russes lineage, no personal relationship established.** Both emerged from the Saint Petersburg imperial ballet system and were connected historically to Diaghilev-era Russian ballet. No sourced meeting or collaboration was established.

- **Isadora Duncan — documented indirect influence on Russian modernist ballet; direct Balanchine relationship unestablished.** Scholarship shows Duncan’s impact on the Russian theatrical environment inherited by Fokine, Diaghilev, Nijinsky, and later modernists. A specific acknowledged Balanchine–Duncan meeting was not found.

- **Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart — documented artistic influence, not a personal meeting.** Balanchine choreographed Mozart scores and treated Mozart as a supreme musical model. His final memorial included Mozart’s *Requiem*.

- **Wilhelm Richard Wagner — reported family-story connection, no meeting possible.** The young Balanchine reportedly won Tamara Geva’s father’s approval by playing Wagner at the piano. The story derives from family recollection.

No supported direct connection was found between Balanchine and the great majority of the 896-person atlas roster. Their absence from this section is intentional.
