# Francis Julius Bellamy: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier reconstructs the documented life of Francis Julius Bellamy (1855–1931), Baptist minister, Christian socialist, editor, advertising practitioner, organizer of the 1892 National Public-School Celebration of Columbus Day, and traditionally recognized author of the Pledge of Allegiance.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a primary document, institutional archive, government record, contemporaneous publication, or closely corroborated records.
- **Reported fact:** stated in a reputable secondary history but not independently confirmed here from the underlying primary document.
- **Allegation or theory:** a claim advanced by an identifiable person or organization but not conclusively established.
- **Rumor or myth:** a circulating story unsupported by adequate documentary evidence.
- **Unresolved:** surviving evidence permits more than one reasonable conclusion.

A dedicated adversarial search was conducted for controversy, scandal, allegations, litigation, fraud, accusations, conspiracy claims, criticism, racism, plagiarism, and recent discussion from September 2024 through September 2026. No credible evidence was found that Bellamy was charged with a crime, sued for fraud, formally investigated for corruption, imprisoned, or connected to an intelligence service. The substantive adverse material concerns racial and immigration views, support for disfranchisement, coercive nationalism, the commercial function of the school-flag campaign, and contested authorship of the Pledge.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Documented or strongly corroborated facts**

- Father: **Rev. David Bellamy** (1806–1864), a Baptist minister.
- Mother: generally identified as **Lucy Ann Clark Bellamy** (about 1819/1820–1898). One detailed secondary history calls her **Lucy Eells**; genealogical summaries usually call her Lucy Ann Clark. The discrepancy may involve a middle, maiden, or ancestral surname, but the exact explanation was not established from a primary vital record.
- Francis appears to have been their only surviving child; a history of the First Baptist Church of Manlius calls him David Bellamy’s “only child.”

David Bellamy preached at Mount Morris, then accepted a call to the First Baptist Church of Rome, New York, in 1859. He died in 1864, when Francis was nine. Francis consequently grew up under the combined influence of a ministerial household, early paternal loss, and the Baptist culture of central and western New York.

### Wider family and ancestry

Bellamy’s ancestry is less securely documented in readily accessible records than his immediate family. Published accounts describe the family as long established in New York and New England Protestant life. Claims of a particularly distinguished or uniformly clerical ancestry should be treated cautiously unless supported by a complete genealogical chain.

His best-known relative was his first cousin:

- **Edward Bellamy** (1850–1898), author of *Looking Backward: 2000–1887* and *Equality*. Edward’s industrial-nationalist ideas and the Nationalist Club movement formed part of Francis Bellamy’s intellectual and organizational environment.

### Marriages and children

#### Harriet Benton Bellamy

Bellamy married **Harriet Benton** (1857–1918) in **Newark, New York, in 1881**.

Their documented or consistently reported children were:

1. **John Benton Bellamy** (1882–1962), later resident in California.
2. **David Bellamy** (1888–1960), born October 30, 1888, in Roxbury, Massachusetts; later resident in Rochester, New York.
3. **Brewster Bellamy**, born February 17, 1896, in Newton, Massachusetts, and died February 19, 1896.

One commercial genealogical index also lists a **Francis Julian Bellamy**, born about 1881. That entry conflicts with the usual family reconstruction, which identifies three sons. Without an independently inspected birth record, this possible fourth child remains **unresolved** and may reflect duplication or conflation.

Harriet died in 1918.

#### Marie Morin Bellamy

Bellamy married **Marie Morin** in 1920. She is described in later family histories as a widowed or previously married New York businesswoman who had operated successfully in the women’s hat or millinery trade. They had no documented children together.

### Descendants

Reported descendants include:

- David Bellamy’s wife, **Rachael**, and sons **David Jr.** and **Peter Bellamy** (1929–2021).
- John Benton Bellamy’s wife, **Ruth “Polly” Edwards Bellamy**, and children **Harriet**, **Barbara**, and **John Benton Bellamy Jr.**

These later descendants participated, directly or indirectly, in preserving Francis Bellamy’s papers and defending his authorship claim.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1855–1872

Bellamy was born in Mount Morris during the late phase of the evangelical culture commonly associated with western New York’s “burned-over district.” His father’s ministry placed him within organized Baptist life from infancy.

In **1859**, when Francis was about four, the family moved to **Rome, New York**, where David Bellamy became pastor of First Baptist Church. Rome stood near the site of Fort Stanwix, a location later incorporated into patriotic accounts of Bellamy’s childhood because an early American flag had flown there during the Revolutionary War. That connection is geographically real; claims that it predestined his later flag writing belong to retrospective hagiography.

After his father’s death in 1864, Francis remained associated with First Baptist Church. He attended **Rome Free Academy**. No surviving evidence reviewed here establishes prolonged foreign travel, military service, imprisonment, exile, serious juvenile illness, or inherited wealth.

## Education and Formation, 1872–1879

### University of Rochester

Bellamy entered the University of Rochester in **1872**. The university was then closely associated with Baptist education.

He graduated in **1876**, in a class of 34 men, and belonged to **Alpha Delta Phi**.

University records and the university’s later historical review report that he:

- won the **Sophomore Latin Prize**;
- won the **Senior Essay Prize**;
- delivered a prize essay titled **“Wordsworth’s Place in Literature”**;
- delivered a second commencement address, **“The Poetry of Human Brotherhood.”**

The second title anticipates the language of fraternity and social solidarity that later appeared in his religious socialism and patriotic writing.

### Rochester Theological Seminary

Bellamy entered **Rochester Theological Seminary** after college, with authorization from his home church. Sources disagree slightly about whether he completed his course in **1879** or **1880**. Local church history gives 1879; some general accounts give 1880. His call to Little Falls and ordination in December 1879 favor substantial completion by 1879.

### Formative influences

The principal documented or reported influences were:

- Baptist theology and pastoral culture.
- The Social Gospel tendency to apply Christian ethics to industrial and economic relations.
- The Golden Rule as an economic principle.
- French social thinker **Henri de Saint-Simon**, especially the idea that modern organization and knowledge should be used to overcome poverty.
- **Edward Bellamy’s** industrial Nationalism.
- English Christian socialism and Fabian discussion, encountered through reform literature and contributors to *The Dawn*.
- Literary training in rhetoric, compact prose, Romantic poetry, and the language of human brotherhood.
- **Daniel Sharp Ford**, later publisher-employer at *The Youth’s Companion*, whom Bellamy remembered as an exacting master of English style.

Bellamy was not a Marxist revolutionary. His socialism favored moral conversion, public organization, nationalization or collective administration of major economic functions, and a strong state capable of subordinating private competition to social ends.

## Early Career: Baptist Ministry, 1879–1891

### First Baptist Church, Little Falls, New York

Bellamy accepted the pastorate of First Baptist Church in **Little Falls** on **December 1, 1879**. He was ordained there on **December 14, 1879**, with delegates from First Baptist Church of Rome participating.

A surviving local account says the Rome church notified him later that month that his membership there had terminated, apparently as the administrative consequence of his transfer rather than a disciplinary expulsion.

His first Little Falls residence was the **Girvan House**, at what is now 2–8 West Main Street.

Bellamy developed a reputation as an educated and rhetorically capable minister. He also wrote for newspapers or periodicals, though a complete bibliography of his early journalism has not been recovered.

He remained at Little Falls until **February 1, 1885**.

### Dearborn Street Baptist Church, Boston

Bellamy moved to Boston to pastor **Dearborn Street Baptist Church**. The neighborhood was changing through immigration, particularly Irish Catholic settlement. A detailed history reports that Bellamy expanded the congregation but eventually moved or reorganized it in another district.

At Dearborn Street he met **Daniel Sharp Ford**, a congregant sympathetic to Bellamy’s preaching and proprietor of *The Youth’s Companion* through the Perry Mason Company. Ford later became Bellamy’s employer and an important professional patron.

### Bethany Baptist Church

In approximately **1890**, Bellamy became pastor of **Bethany Baptist Church**, also in the Boston area.

During this period he preached or lectured on subjects including:

- “Jesus the Socialist”
- “The Socialism of the Primitive Church”
- “The Socialism of the Bible”
- “What Is Christian Socialism?”
- “Socialism versus Anarchy”

### Society of Christian Socialists

On **February 18, 1889**, William Dwight Porter Bliss, Bellamy, and associates organized the **Society of Christian Socialists** in Boston. Bellamy became a vice-president and chaired or participated in its educational work.

The society published *The Dawn*, edited by Bliss. Bellamy contributed articles, educational notes, and arguments linking the Golden Rule with economic reorganization. Its contributors and associates included reformers from different Protestant and socialist tendencies; material by Edward Bellamy and the British Fabian Sidney Webb appeared in its orbit.

Bellamy urged ministers to preach on the relationship between Christianity and current economics even when congregations were resistant. In a May 1890 educational column, he acknowledged that a pastor who openly declared himself a Christian socialist might be unable to carry his congregation with him.

### Departure from the pastorate

Many later accounts say Bellamy was “fired” or “forced from his pulpit” because of socialist preaching. The more cautious reconstruction is:

- **Documented fact:** his increasingly explicit Christian socialism produced tension with conventional church life, and he left the active pastorate in 1891.
- **Reported fact:** congregational resistance to sermons attacking capitalism precipitated his departure.
- **Unresolved detail:** the records consulted do not establish the exact form of dismissal, whether there was a formal congregational vote, or whether he resigned under pressure.

Daniel Sharp Ford offered him work at *The Youth’s Companion*. This shifted Bellamy from pulpit ministry to mass communication.

## The Youth’s Companion and the Schoolhouse-Flag Campaign, 1891–1895

### The magazine and the premium department

*The Youth’s Companion* was a nationally circulated Boston family and children’s weekly published by the Perry Mason Company. Ford employed Bellamy in **1891**, assigning him to work with **James Bailey Upham**, Ford’s nephew by marriage and a manager in the magazine’s premium or promotional operation.

The magazine used premiums to build subscriptions. Children or local organizers could sell subscriptions and receive school flags. Beginning in 1888, the company promoted the installation of an American flag over every public schoolhouse.

By 1892, contemporary and later accounts estimate that approximately **26,000 schools** had acquired flags through the campaign.

The project combined:

- commercial subscription promotion;
- sale or distribution of flags;
- public-school advocacy;
- patriotic ritual;
- assimilation of immigrant children;
- post–Civil War national reunification;
- organizational expansion of the magazine.

It is misleading to describe it as either purely commercial or purely civic. The surviving evidence supports both purposes.

### National Public-School Celebration of Columbus Day

Upham conceived a nationwide school celebration for the four-hundredth anniversary of Columbus’s Atlantic voyage. Bellamy became the campaign’s principal writer, lecturer, and organizer.

Bellamy promoted the proposal before education officials. A committee of state school superintendents and educators endorsed the celebration, and Bellamy served as chairman or executive organizer.

Congress approved a joint resolution on **June 29, 1892**, authorizing the president to recommend a national observance. President **Benjamin Harrison’s Proclamation 335**, dated **July 21, 1892**, designated **Friday, October 21, 1892**, as a general holiday and explicitly asked that schools form the center of the observance and that the flag fly over every schoolhouse.

The celebration therefore bore the marks of a private-public partnership: a commercial magazine designed and promoted it; educators institutionalized it; Congress and the president supplied official sanction.

## Composition and Publication of the Pledge

### Bellamy’s retrospective account

Bellamy later said Upham considered George T. Balch’s existing school pledge—“We give our heads and hearts to God and our country; one country, one language, one flag”—too juvenile or inadequate for the national ceremony. After discussion, Upham asked Bellamy to produce a new text centered on “allegiance.”

Bellamy recalled composing it in the Boston office during August 1892. His stated reasoning was:

- **“Pledge”** was preferable to “vow” or “swear” for schoolchildren.
- The first-person singular gave individual responsibility.
- **“My flag”** created a personal bond and accommodated children whose parents had come from other countries.
- **“Republic”** identified the American constitutional form.
- **“One nation indivisible”** invoked Civil War reunion and the survival of national unity.
- **“Liberty and justice for all”** compressed the nation’s professed political ideals.

### Original publication

The pledge appeared without an individual byline in *The Youth’s Companion*, volume 65, on **September 8, 1892**, as part of “National School Celebration of Columbus Day: The Official Programme.”

The printed text was:

> I pledge allegiance to my Flag and the Republic for which it stands: one Nation indivisible, with Liberty and Justice for all.

Accounts sometimes call this a 22-word pledge and sometimes a 23-word pledge. The difference turns on whether the later-added second “to” in “and to the Republic” is counted. Bellamy reportedly added that “to” after hearing the pledge spoken during the October observance, producing the version he later called his 23 words.

### The accompanying gesture

The official program instructed pupils to begin with a military salute—right hand near the forehead—and, at the words “to my Flag,” extend the right hand gracefully toward the flag, palm upward, holding it there until the end.

The extended-arm gesture was principally devised or demonstrated by Upham, although it later became known as the **Bellamy salute** because of its association with the pledge.

The gesture predated European Fascism and Nazism. Claims that Bellamy copied Hitler, Mussolini, or a Nazi ritual are chronologically impossible. Its visual resemblance to later Fascist salutes nevertheless became politically consequential.

### October 21, 1892

Schools across the United States used the official program on October 21. Exact participation totals are difficult to verify, and claims of universal or nearly universal compliance are promotional estimates.

Bellamy attended the ceremony at **Malden, Massachusetts**, Upham’s home city, and gave a speech. He described the public-school system as, “under God,” a central support of the national future. This is important evidence against the myth that omission of the words “under God” from the pledge meant that Bellamy was an atheist.

## Intellectual and Political Content of the Pledge

Bellamy’s pledge joined several elements that were not entirely harmonious:

- republican constitutionalism;
- centralized national unity;
- Social Gospel universalism;
- Civil War reunion;
- public-school assimilation;
- opposition to anarchism and revolutionary disorder;
- a promise of liberty and justice;
- contemporary racial and immigration hierarchy.

Bellamy later recalled considering “equality,” associated with the French revolutionary triad “liberty, equality, fraternity,” but not including it. A widely circulated explanation says education officials warned that equality would provoke opposition because women and African Americans did not possess equal political and civil standing. The evidence supports that equality was considered and rejected; the exact wording and weight of the officials’ warning rests largely on Bellamy’s later narrative and subsequent biographies.

“Liberty and justice for all” therefore should not be treated as proof that Bellamy personally supported equal citizenship in every sphere. His later editorials show that he did not.

## New York Editorial and Advertising Career, 1895–1922

Bellamy left *The Youth’s Companion* at the end of **1895**. He later said loyalty to the magazine’s convention of anonymous editorial authorship prevented him from attaching his name publicly to the pledge.

He relocated to **New York City** and spent approximately nineteen years there in publishing, publicity, and advertising, although a complete employer-by-employer chronology remains unavailable.

### The Illustrated American

Bellamy became an editor of *The Illustrated American*, a pioneering illustrated weekly. Bibliographic histories identify him as editor in approximately **1897–1898**.

Known or indexed writings from this period include:

- **“A Man of the People”** (February 13, 1897), concerning Abraham Lincoln.
- An editorial commonly cited as **“The New Democracy”** or identified by its discussion of democracy, heredity, and immigration.
- **“Taking Away the Negro’s Ballot”** (January 15, 1898).

### Advertising philosophy

Later accounts characterize Bellamy as an advocate of intensive or “high-pressure” advertising that could remain truthful. He regarded advertising as an economic mechanism for creating demand, maintaining industrial production, and organizing consumption.

No patents, manufacturing inventions, incorporated business empire, major stockholdings, or substantial personal investments have been documented. He appears to have worked as an employee, consultant, editor, and publicity specialist rather than as a large-scale proprietor.

The available record contains no reliable valuation of his income or estate. His continued need for part-time employment in his seventies and rapid search for another position after dismissal in 1931 do not suggest great independent wealth.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Christian socialism

Bellamy described himself as a Christian first and a socialist afterward. His central propositions included:

- the teachings of Jesus condemned greed and economic exploitation;
- private competition prevented both employer and worker from consistently practicing the Golden Rule;
- major economic processes should be coordinated for social rather than merely private benefit;
- public education could form solidaristic citizens;
- a strong democratic state was necessary to restrain private power and anarchic conflict.

This placed him within American Christian socialism and the Social Gospel, but not cleanly within later Marxist, social-democratic, communist, or libertarian-socialist categories.

### Nationalism

“Nationalism” in Bellamy’s 1890s environment often meant the program associated with Edward Bellamy: national organization of industry and public ownership or administration. It also meant patriotic integration under a single republic.

Francis Bellamy treated national cohesion as a prerequisite for social reform. He feared class war, anarchism, sectional division, and foreign radicalism. Public-school patriotic ritual was intended to create emotional loyalty before children entered adult political and industrial conflict.

### Religion and church separation

Bellamy was a devout Christian during the period in which he composed the pledge. He did not put “God” in the text, apparently because:

- the pledge was a concise civil statement to a flag and republic;
- it was intended for public schools;
- Bellamy supported institutional separation of church and state;
- religious language already appeared elsewhere in the Columbus Day program and in Harrison’s proclamation.

The later claim that Bellamy would certainly have opposed the 1954 addition of “under God” is **disputed**. His son David said that he would have objected to alteration of the text and to church-state entanglement. Bellamy’s own speeches, however, used providential national language. The strongest conclusion is that he opposed unnecessary alteration and wrote a deliberately civil pledge; his precise response to the Cold War amendment cannot be known.

### Later church participation

A secondary tradition says that after moving to Florida Bellamy ceased regular church attendance because of racism he encountered in local churches. This is a **reported fact**, not fully substantiated here from Bellamy’s own surviving statement. It also requires careful treatment because his own published racial views were exclusionary.

## Race, Immigration, Suffrage, and Hierarchy

Bellamy’s writings contain documented racial and nativist positions.

### Immigration and heredity

In an 1897 editorial in *The Illustrated American*, Bellamy rejected natural human equality and argued that democratic government depended on the biological or inherited “stuff” of the population. He wrote that democracy could not safely admit every immigrant and referred to “every alien immigrant of inferior race” as potentially corrupting national “stock.” He distinguished races supposedly capable of contributing “wholesome blood” from those he thought would lower the American racial standard.

**Evidentiary status: documented published position.**

These statements are not later paraphrases invented by opponents; substantial portions are reproduced from his periodical writing and discussed by historians of the pledge.

The language resembles the racial hereditarianism and immigration restrictionism that helped prepare the ground for twentieth-century eugenics. No evidence reviewed here proves that Bellamy belonged to an organized eugenics society or advocated compulsory sterilization. It is therefore more exact to call the passage racial-hereditarian and nativist than to assign him a specific institutional role in the later eugenics movement.

### Black suffrage

In **“Taking Away the Negro’s Ballot”** Bellamy discussed Louisiana’s constitutional efforts to restrict voting. A contemporaneous newspaper reproduced his description of the Black electorate as ignorant, shiftless, “depraved,” and politically menacing. He supported educational or property requirements that would predictably disfranchise large numbers of Black citizens.

**Evidentiary status: documented published advocacy.**

This directly contradicts modern hagiographic descriptions of Bellamy as uniformly committed to racial equality. It also limits claims that “justice for all” embodied an intention to secure equal voting rights for Black Americans.

### Women’s equality

Bellamy considered but omitted “equality” from the pledge. Surviving accounts connect the omission partly to the politically contentious status of women’s suffrage and racial equality. The evidence does not establish that he conducted a major campaign either for or against women’s suffrage.

### Apparent contradiction

Bellamy combined universal moral language with hierarchical judgments about political competence, immigration, race, property, education, and assimilation. This was not merely an inconsistency attributed by later critics: both strands occur in his documented public work.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Daniel Sharp Ford

Publisher of *The Youth’s Companion*, congregant under Bellamy in Boston, stylistic mentor, and professional patron. Ford hired Bellamy after his departure from active ministry.

### James Bailey Upham

Advertising and premium-department executive at *The Youth’s Companion*. Upham conceived and managed much of the schoolhouse-flag campaign, worked closely with Bellamy on the Columbus Day celebration, and devised or demonstrated the accompanying salute.

Their relationship later became central to the authorship dispute. Bellamy credited Upham with proposing that a new pledge be written and with important organizational work, but denied that Upham wrote any part of the final 23 words.

### William Dwight Porter Bliss

Episcopal clergyman, reformer, editor of *The Dawn*, and organizing leader of the Society of Christian Socialists. Bellamy served with him as an officer and educational advocate.

### Edward Bellamy

First cousin, novelist, and leader-symbol of the Nationalist movement. Francis shared significant assumptions with Edward about industrial coordination, solidarity, and the ethical failure of competitive capitalism. Francis was not simply Edward’s spokesman; his Baptist theology and patriotic educational work gave his program a distinct form.

### George T. Balch

Former Union officer and author of an earlier school pledge. Bellamy and Upham treated Balch’s formula as a predecessor but rejected it as too juvenile and insufficiently republican in historical content. There is no proof of a close personal relationship.

### John Winfield Scott and Florence Scott

Participants in the Companion’s advertising and 1892 celebration work. Bellamy encountered them in New York in 1923 and sought their memories as evidence for his authorship.

### C. E. Kelsey

Later proprietor or representative of *The Youth’s Companion*. Bellamy corresponded with Kelsey in 1923 in an unsuccessful attempt to obtain recognition from the magazine.

### Margarette S. Miller

Posthumous advocate and biographer. Miller initially encountered competing claims, investigated family papers, and helped bring the authorship question before historical committees and Congress.

## The Authorship Disputes

### Why the dispute arose

The pledge was printed as anonymous editorial matter. Bellamy did not publicly claim authorship in 1892, and relevant office drafts were not preserved in an unambiguous institutional file. These conditions allowed rival claims to grow.

### The James B. Upham claim

In **1917**, *The Youth’s Companion* answered a question about authorship by crediting Upham or describing the pledge as originating with him. Upham had died in 1905.

Bellamy objected. On **June 19, 1923**, he wrote to C. E. Kelsey that he had written “every one of the 23 words” without alteration by Upham or anyone else. The magazine did not give him the recognition he wanted and eventually stopped engaging the dispute.

**Assessment:** Upham unquestionably originated or co-developed the campaign, requested a new salute or pledge, and designed the gesture. The surviving evidence historically led review committees to credit the final wording to Bellamy. The possibility that Upham suggested individual concepts or phrases cannot be excluded.

### Bellamy’s documentary campaign

From 1923 onward Bellamy assembled:

- personal recollections;
- correspondence;
- statements from surviving colleagues;
- a detailed narrative of composition;
- explanations of his word choices.

He did not obtain universally accepted recognition during his lifetime.

### The 1939 historical review

A committee of three academic historians convened through the United States Flag Association reviewed the competing Bellamy and Upham evidence. It concluded that Francis Bellamy wrote the pledge. The association reversed its earlier support for Upham.

**Evidentiary status:** documented institutional historical finding, not a judicial judgment.

### The 1957 Library of Congress review

At the request of Bellamy’s family and through congressional involvement, the Library of Congress Legislative Reference Service examined the dispute. Congressman Kenneth Keating presented the conclusion in 1957: the evidence was overwhelmingly in favor of Francis Bellamy.

**Evidentiary status:** official research conclusion, not a court verdict and not legally binding adjudication.

### Frank E. Bellamy of Kansas

A separate claim concerns **Frank E. Bellamy**, an unrelated Cherryvale, Kansas, schoolboy.

Versions of the story include:

1. Frank submitted the wording to an 1890 *Youth’s Companion* contest.
2. The magazine appropriated his entry.
3. He later used the wording in a school contest and was accused of copying the already published pledge.
4. A Kansas school ceremony used almost identical wording before Francis Bellamy’s claimed August 1892 composition.

For many years, the claim was weakened by inconsistent dates and by evidence that Frank won a later contest after the pledge had circulated nationally.

In **2022**, researcher Barry Popik located a report in the **May 21, 1892, Ellis County News Republican** describing an April 30 Kansas school flag-raising with a nearly identical pledge. Fred Shapiro, editor of *The Yale Book of Quotations*, consequently said the discovery favored reconsideration of Frank Bellamy’s priority.

This evidence is significant because April 30 predates Francis Bellamy’s reported August composition.

Outstanding questions remain:

- Was the newspaper text printed contemporaneously or retrospectively altered in digitization or later transcription?
- Who supplied the wording used at the April ceremony?
- Was it Frank Bellamy’s composition, an earlier uncredited Companion formula, a regional borrowing, or wording circulating orally?
- Did Francis Bellamy see a submission or report before August?
- Is the 1890 contest date independently documented?

**Assessment: unresolved authorship challenge.** The traditional attribution to Francis Bellamy rests on his detailed narrative, testimony from colleagues, the 1939 review, and the 1957 Library of Congress conclusion. The Kansas newspaper creates a genuine priority problem that those earlier reviews could not have considered. It does not by itself prove plagiarism by Francis Bellamy.

### Fraud and plagiarism labels

- Calling Francis Bellamy a proven plagiarist is unsupported.
- Calling Frank Bellamy’s later contest entry a proven fraud may also be too categorical without the original contest documentation.
- The historically accurate formulation is that the pledge’s precise textual origin is now **contested**, while Francis Bellamy remains its congressionally and institutionally recognized author.

## Later Recognition and Revisions During Bellamy’s Life

### 1923 National Flag Conference

The words **“my Flag”** were changed to **“the Flag of the United States.”** The purpose was to prevent ambiguity among immigrant children who might understand “my flag” as the flag of their country of origin.

Bellamy opposed the change as unnecessary and damaging to the composition’s rhythm and personal force.

### 1924 National Flag Conference

The wording was expanded to **“the Flag of the United States of America.”**

Bellamy again objected. The official text had now moved away from his compact original.

### Final public position

Shortly before his death, Bellamy described the pledge as an easily remembered patriotic symbol that had encouraged love of country and flag in millions of people. Recognition of his authorship remained incomplete, but he considered the text his principal contribution.

---

## Later Life in Florida, 1922–1931

Bellamy and Marie moved to **Tampa, Florida, in 1922**.

The **1930 federal census** placed them at **2926 Wallcraft Avenue**, with Bellamy’s occupation recorded in public-utility advertising.

In **1926**, he reportedly persuaded **Tampa Electric Company** that it needed systematic publicity and became its part-time advertising manager.

On **July 15, 1931**, Tampa Electric dismissed him, reportedly preferring a younger employee. On the same day, Bellamy sought and obtained similar work with the **Tampa Gas Company**. The episode was later used to portray him as energetic and professionally active in old age.

No reliable record reviewed here establishes large property holdings, insolvency, bankruptcy, financial misconduct, or dependence on public relief.

---

## Death and Burial

Bellamy died in Tampa on **August 28, 1931**, aged 76.

A New York obituary described him as having been “stricken” in Florida, but the accessible sources consulted do not establish a precise medical cause of death. No documented evidence was found of addiction, suicide, violent death, psychiatric hospitalization, or fatal accident.

His body was cremated. The ashes were returned to **Rome, New York**, and placed in the family plot in Rome Cemetery. His grave bears the pledge.

A Tampa newspaper editorial predicted that Bellamy’s pledge would continue through the centuries. The quotation subsequently appeared on a local commemorative marker.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### Documented controversies

1. **Racial hierarchy and immigration restriction:** Bellamy published arguments about inferior races, racial stock, and selective immigration.

2. **Black disfranchisement:** he supported educational or property voting tests in Louisiana and characterized Black voters in derogatory racial and class terms.

3. **Commercial patriotism:** the 1892 ceremony promoted civic education and national unity while also expanding subscriptions and the school-flag market.

4. **Assimilation and coercion:** Bellamy intended school ritual to form loyal national citizens, especially among immigrant children. Later compulsory recitation raised constitutional issues beyond his lifetime.

5. **Authorship:** Upham and Frank E. Bellamy rival claims complicate exclusive attribution.

6. **Textual alteration:** Bellamy objected to the 1923–1924 changes. His response to the 1954 addition cannot be known because it occurred 23 years after his death.

### No substantiated evidence found

The search found no credible evidence that Francis Julius Bellamy:

- was indicted or convicted of a crime;
- committed documented financial fraud;
- laundered money;
- held corrupt public office;
- participated in political violence;
- worked for an intelligence service;
- was imprisoned or exiled;
- owned slaves;
- belonged to the Ku Klux Klan;
- collaborated with Fascist or Nazi organizations;
- invented the extended-arm salute as a Fascist gesture;
- held a patent on the pledge, flag, or salute.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “A socialist wrote America’s loyalty oath”

**Story:** The pledge is portrayed as a hidden socialist project.

**Evidence:** Bellamy was unquestionably a Christian socialist. His pledge nevertheless contains no explicit economic program. It fused his commitment to solidarity with republican nationalism and the magazine’s commercial-patriotic campaign.

**Reputational effect:** The fact is used both by socialists reclaiming patriotic history and by anti-socialists portraying the pledge as collectivist indoctrination.

### “The pledge was only an advertising gimmick”

**Story:** Bellamy wrote it solely to sell flags and subscriptions.

**Evidence:** The campaign had a commercial structure and helped market magazine subscriptions and school flags. Bellamy’s sermons, organizing, speeches, and later recollections also demonstrate sincere ideological commitments.

**Assessment:** reductive myth built around a documented commercial component.

### “Bellamy was fired simply for saying Jesus was a socialist”

**Story:** A single sermon resulted in immediate dismissal.

**Evidence:** His socialism created pastoral conflict and he left the ministry. The exact administrative process and causal sequence are insufficiently documented.

**Assessment:** plausible simplification, not fully established in its dramatic form.

### “Bellamy deliberately omitted God because he was an atheist”

**Story:** The original secular wording proves atheism.

**Evidence against:** Bellamy was a Baptist minister and Christian socialist who used providential language. His public-school pledge was civil rather than theological.

**Assessment:** false or seriously misleading.

### “Bellamy would have supported ‘under God’”

**Story:** His Christianity proves that he would approve the 1954 amendment.

**Evidence:** He was Christian but opposed earlier alterations and supported church-state separation. His son said he would have opposed the addition.

**Assessment:** counterfactual and unresolved.

### “Bellamy would have opposed ‘under God’”

The opposite prediction also exceeds the evidence. It is better supported than the pro-amendment claim by his defense of the original text and his son’s statement, but remains counterfactual.

### “Bellamy invented the Nazi salute”

**Story:** Photographs of American pupils prove a direct ideological connection to Nazism.

**Evidence against:** the American school gesture was specified in 1892, decades before the Nazi Party. Upham appears to have devised the physical action.

**Historical significance:** Once European Fascists adopted a visually similar salute, the resemblance became intolerable. Congress changed the American practice in December 1942.

### “Francis Bellamy stole the pledge from a Kansas boy”

**Story:** *The Youth’s Companion* appropriated Frank E. Bellamy’s contest entry.

**Evidence for:** the newly highlighted May 1892 Kansas report contains nearly identical language before Francis’s stated August composition; local testimony and tradition support Frank.

**Evidence against or limiting:** the supposed 1890 submission lacks a fully verified primary trail; later Frank Bellamy contest activity may postdate national publication; there is no direct document showing Francis received or copied Frank’s text.

**Assessment:** serious unresolved theory, not established plagiarism.

### “Bellamy was an uncomplicated egalitarian”

**Story:** “Liberty and justice for all” represented full racial, political, and gender equality.

**Evidence against:** his 1897–1898 editorials advocated racial hierarchy, selective immigration, and voting restrictions.

**Assessment:** hagiographic distortion.

### “Bellamy was simply a Fascist before Fascism”

**Story:** nationalism, socialism, mass ritual, and the extended-arm salute make him a proto-Fascist.

**Evidence:** those ingredients invite retrospective comparison, but Bellamy operated within Christian socialism, republican public-school reform, and nineteenth-century Nationalism. There is no evidence of membership in a Fascist movement, which had not yet formed.

**Assessment:** anachronistic polemical classification.

## Legal and Institutional Afterlife of the Pledge

Bellamy did not live to see the federal government adopt or substantially litigate the pledge.

- **1940 – *Minersville School District v. Gobitis*:** the Supreme Court upheld compulsory school flag salute requirements against Jehovah’s Witness objections.
- **June 22, 1942:** Congress incorporated flag etiquette and a version of the pledge into the Flag Code.
- **December 22, 1942:** Congress prescribed the right-hand-over-heart gesture, replacing the extended-arm salute.
- **1943 – *West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette*:** the Supreme Court reversed the constitutional principle of *Gobitis*, holding that public authorities could not compel pupils to affirm prescribed political orthodoxy.
- **December 28, 1945:** Congress formally used the title **“Pledge of Allegiance.”**
- **June 14, 1954:** Congress and President Dwight D. Eisenhower added **“under God”** after “one Nation.”
- **1957:** the Library of Congress review endorsed Francis Bellamy’s authorship.

These developments transformed a private magazine’s ceremonial text into a federal patriotic formula while limiting the government’s power to compel its recitation.

## Works by Francis Julius Bellamy

No definitive scholarly bibliography of Bellamy’s writings is readily available. Because much magazine material was anonymous or unsigned, the following is a reconstructable rather than exhaustive list.

### Securely attributed or officially recognized

1. **“Wordsworth’s Place in Literature”** (1876)  
   University of Rochester senior prize essay. Survival or publication status not established.

2. **“The Poetry of Human Brotherhood”** (1876)  
   University of Rochester commencement address. Publication status not established.

3. **“Education Department”** (*The Dawn*, May 1890)  
   Article or department note advocating Christian-socialist education and advising ministers how to introduce economic themes.

4. **“Socialism versus Anarchy”** (lecture, about 1890–1891)  
   Delivered and reportedly prepared in written form. Bellamy argued for organized public authority and distinguished Christian socialism from anarchism. Exact publication details require archival verification.

5. **“National School Celebration of Columbus Day: The Official Programme”** (*The Youth’s Companion*, September 8, 1892, pp. 446–447)  
   Institutional campaign document prepared under Bellamy’s chairmanship. It contains the first conventionally recognized printing of his pledge. The article was unbylined as magazine copy.

6. **Pledge of Allegiance** (1892)  
   Original form: “I pledge allegiance to my Flag and the Republic for which it stands: one Nation indivisible, with Liberty and Justice for all.”  
   Later 1892 recitation form added “to” before “the Republic.”

7. **Columbus Day address at Malden, Massachusetts** (October 21, 1892)  
   Patriotic public-school address. Portions survive in quotation or later reproduction.

8. **“A Man of the People”** (*The Illustrated American*, February 13, 1897, p. 232)  
   Essay on Abraham Lincoln.

9. **Editorial on democracy, heredity, race, and immigration** (*The Illustrated American*, volume XXII, no. 394, 1897, p. 258)  
   Frequently titled or discussed as part of “The New Democracy.” Contains the racial-stock and immigration passages cited above.

10. **“Taking Away the Negro’s Ballot”** (*The Illustrated American*, volume 23, January 15, 1898, p. 72)  
    Commentary supporting suffrage restrictions in Louisiana.

11. **“A Brief Synopsis of the Story of the Origin of the Pledge”** (prepared during the 1920s)  
    Bellamy’s retrospective account of composing the pledge.

12. **“The Story of the Pledge of Allegiance to the Flag”**  
    Bellamy’s detailed narrative, published posthumously in the *University of Rochester Library Bulletin*, volume VIII, winter 1953. This is an edited posthumous publication of his account, not a work first issued during his lifetime.

### Reported sermon and lecture titles

The following are documented as titles or topics but may not survive as formally published works:

- “Jesus the Socialist”
- “The Socialism of the Bible”
- “The Socialism of the Primitive Church”
- “What Is Christian Socialism?”
- “Socialism versus Anarchy”

### Correspondence and manuscripts

The University of Rochester’s Bellamy-related collections contain or have been reported to contain:

- correspondence about Pledge authorship;
- Bellamy’s composition narrative;
- affidavits and recollections;
- family papers;
- material assembled by his son David;
- later documents concerning the Upham and Kansas claims.

No diary edition, collected letters, autobiography, patent corpus, novel, or book-length theological treatise by Bellamy has been identified.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Bellamy’s posthumous reputation developed in several stages.

### Neglected author

At his death in 1931 he had not secured universal recognition. Later admirers called him “the most neglected patriot in American history.” This became a central hagiographic motif.

### Officially recognized author

The 1939 historians’ inquiry, congressional references, and 1957 Library of Congress report consolidated the attribution to Bellamy. Congress printed histories naming him, and institutional exhibits routinely identify him as author.

### Civic commemoration

Memorials include:

- his inscribed grave at Rome Cemetery;
- historical markers in Tampa;
- **Bellamy Elementary School** in Tampa, built in 1974, dedicated in 1975, and later rededicated;
- local centennial observances in 1992;
- University of Rochester alumni commemoration;
- recurring Flag Day and school-history programs.

### Constitutional symbol

The pledge became inseparable from debates over:

- freedom of speech and conscience;
- compulsory patriotic ritual;
- religion in public schools;
- immigration and assimilation;
- national unity;
- dissent during war;
- the boundary between patriotism and state orthodoxy.

The most influential constitutional statement connected to its compulsory use is Justice Robert H. Jackson’s 1943 declaration in *Barnette* that no official may prescribe orthodoxy in politics, nationalism, religion, or opinion.

### Socialist recovery

Modern socialists emphasize Bellamy’s opposition to capitalism and his Christian-socialist office. This corrects sanitized patriotic biographies but can obscure his strong nationalism, racial hierarchy, and rejection of anarchism.

### Racial reassessment

More recent scholarship and journalism emphasize his writings on “inferior race,” racial stock, immigration restriction, and Black disfranchisement. These are documented aspects of his record, not merely guilt by association with his era.

### Authorship reopened

The 2022 rediscovery of the May 1892 Kansas newspaper report reopened a question many institutions had treated as settled. Francis Bellamy remains the recognized author, but the exact textual genealogy of the sentence is again a legitimate research problem.

## Atlas Connections

### Edward Bellamy — documented fact

Francis Julius Bellamy and novelist **Edward Bellamy** were first cousins. Francis participated in the Boston Christian-socialist and Nationalist milieu shaped partly by Edward’s *Looking Backward*. Their relationship was familial and intellectual, although Francis developed his own explicitly Baptist and public-school-centered synthesis.

### Christopher Columbus — documented symbolic and institutional connection

Bellamy did not meet Columbus, who died centuries before his birth. The documented connection is symbolic: Bellamy organized the National Public-School Celebration of the four-hundredth anniversary of Columbus’s 1492 voyage and placed the pledge at the center of its official program. Columbus functioned as the campaign’s national-origin symbol.

### Abraham Lincoln — reported intellectual and literary connection

Bellamy wrote “A Man of the People” about Lincoln in 1897. His phrase “one Nation indivisible” has also been interpreted as invoking the Union preserved by the Civil War and the national language associated with Lincoln. Direct verbal borrowing from the Gettysburg Address is not established.

### Francis Julius Bellamy

The subject himself appears in the atlas roster. No separate self-connection is recorded.

### Absence finding

No source consulted documented a personal meeting, correspondence, organizational partnership, rivalry, patronage relationship, or family tie between Bellamy and any other person on the supplied roster. Mere contemporaneity, shared American residence, or broad ideological similarity was not counted.
