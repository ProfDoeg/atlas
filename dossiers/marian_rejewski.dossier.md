# Marian Rejewski: Research Dossier

**Subject:** Marian Adam Rejewski (1905–1980)  
**Scope:** Reconstructable life history, work, networks, writings, disputes, myths, reception, and legacy  
**Prepared:** 30 August 2026  

## Research and Evidence Note

This dossier distinguishes among contemporaneous or archival evidence, Rejewski's later recollections, institutional histories, scholarly reconstruction, and popular retelling. Intelligence history presents special problems: much of the work was secret; files were dispersed or destroyed in the 1939 evacuation; participants wrote decades later; and British, French, Polish, German, and later Cold War narratives emphasized different actors. Dates that are secure are stated directly. Where sources conflict, the competing versions are given.

Rejewski's 1967 memoir and 1974 supplement are indispensable first-person sources, but they were written twenty-two to thirty-five years after many of the events described. The modern Polish-English edition was prepared from family typescripts carrying his corrections and errata; the publisher describes it as the first book publication of those texts. His 1940/42 technical-history report, his postwar mathematical papers, and the July 1978 interview with Richard A. Woytak provide additional first-person evidence. Polish security-police records document postwar surveillance, though such records record the state's suspicions and informants' claims, not necessarily verified conduct. Official commemorative pages are used mainly for institutional facts and reception, not as substitutes for critical history.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Marian Adam Rejewski.
- **Polish pronunciation:** approximately *MAH-ryan reh-YEF-skee*.
- **Alternative forms:** Marian Rejewski; in German geographical records his birthplace appears as Bromberg. No authenticated pseudonym, nom de guerre, or habitual nickname has been found in the consulted record.
- **Titles and ranks:** mathematician, cryptologist and intelligence employee; later a Polish Army officer. He entered the wartime Polish service with the formal status of an educated private, became second lieutenant on 10 October 1943, and wartime lieutenant on 1 January 1945.
- **Born:** 16 August 1905, Bydgoszcz (then Bromberg, Province of Posen, German Empire; today Bydgoszcz, Poland).
- **Died:** 13 February 1980, Warsaw, Polish People's Republic, aged seventy-four.
- **Burial:** Powązki Military Cemetery, Warsaw, quarter B39; he received a military funeral.
- **Nationality/citizenship context:** born a subject of the German Empire in the Prussian partition; resident of reborn Poland after Bydgoszcz's incorporation into the Polish state in 1920; served the Polish Republic and the Polish armed forces in exile; returned to communist-governed Poland in 1946.
- **Principal professions:** university assistant in mathematics; civilian cryptanalyst of the Polish General Staff's Cipher Bureau; wartime cryptologist in France and Britain; postwar commercial and cooperative-sector accounting/economic employee.
- **Central historical distinction:** in late 1932, using permutation theory and intelligence supplied through French-Polish cooperation, he reconstructed the secret wiring and operation of the German military Enigma. With Jerzy Różycki, Henryk Zygalski, military supervisors and AVA engineers, he then developed machines and methods that made recurring decryption possible. The trio disclosed the achievement and transferred reconstructed Enigmas and their methods to British and French representatives in July 1939. This preceded, enabled, and was technically distinct from the much larger British wartime Enigma program.

The National Security Agency inducted him into its Cryptologic Hall of Honor in 2014, describing him as responsible for the initial analysis that enabled later Enigma exploitation. The page is useful evidence of institutional recognition, although its compressed statement that he used “statistical analysis” understates the specifically algebraic/permutation-theoretic method and its phrase about escaping to Britain before conquest collapses a much longer route through France, Algeria, Vichy France and Spain. [NSA, “Marian Rejewski”](https://www.nsa.gov/History/Cryptologic-History/Historical-Figures/Historical-Figures-View/Article/1621548/marian-rejewski/)

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and siblings

Rejewski was the youngest of seven children. His father, **Józef Rejewski**, was a tobacco merchant; his mother, **Matylda Rejewska, née Thoms**, was the daughter of a brewer from Toruń. The official Polish biographical material and specialist chronology agree on the parents and the size of the household. Józef died on 1 June 1921, when Marian was fifteen. [Institute of National Remembrance, biographical infographic](https://archiwum.ipn.gov.pl/pl/historia-z-ipn/157145,Giganci-nauki-infografiki-historyczne-Marian-Rejewski.html) [Muzeum Wojsk Lądowych, chronology](https://marian-rejewski.pl/kalendarium-2/)

The family is described as Polish and Catholic in some popular biographies, but the consulted primary and official sources do not provide enough evidence to reconstruct religious practice or a deep genealogy. Later publications disagree over some collateral family names and relationships. A specialist review of a recent popular biography identifies multiple genealogical errors; therefore this dossier does not repeat unsupported sibling names or extended pedigrees as fact. [Jerzy Lelwic, “Biograficzny galimatias, czyli jak skrzywdzić Mariana Rejewskiego”](https://marian-rejewski.pl/biograficzny-galimatias-czyli-jak-skrzywdzic-mariana-rejewskiego/)

### Marriage and children

On **20 June 1934**, Rejewski married **Irena Maria Lewandowska**, the daughter of a well-to-do dentist. They had two children:

- **Andrzej Rejewski**, born 22 June 1936. He died of poliomyelitis on 9 July 1947, after an illness reported as lasting only days. The death occurred less than eight months after his father returned from Britain and was the best-documented private catastrophe of Rejewski's postwar life.
- **Janina Rejewska**, born 16 February 1939, later **Janina Sylwestrzak**. She trained as a mathematician and represented the family in later commemorations.

Irena and the children remained in Poland during the war. After the 1944 Warsaw Uprising, German authorities expelled them through the Pruszków transit camp. They spent roughly nine months at Reczul near Skierniewice, then returned to Bydgoszcz in June 1945. Rejewski did not see them between his September 1939 evacuation and November 1946 return. Irena died in 1986. No other spouse, partner, or child is documented in the sources consulted. [MacTutor History of Mathematics, “Marian Rejewski”](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Rejewski/) [Muzeum Wojsk Lądowych, chronology](https://marian-rejewski.pl/kalendarium-2/)

## Childhood and Early Life, 1905–1923

Rejewski was born at **6 Wileńska Street, Bydgoszcz**, in a Polish household living under Prussian rule. From 1912 he attended a German-language elementary school, identified more precisely in the local chronology as the preparatory school of the municipal *Oberrealschule*. From 1914 he studied at the *Königliches Gymnasium*—later the Friedrich-Wilhelm Gymnasium—which became the Polish State Classical Gymnasium after sovereignty changed. He graduated in 1923. [Muzeum Wojsk Lądowych, chronology](https://marian-rejewski.pl/kalendarium-2/) [Institute of National Remembrance, biographical infographic](https://archiwum.ipn.gov.pl/pl/historia-z-ipn/157145,Giganci-nauki-infografiki-historyczne-Marian-Rejewski.html)

His childhood therefore crossed three political regimes without a family migration: Imperial Germany, the revolutionary postwar transition, and the Second Polish Republic. Bydgoszcz formally passed to Poland in January 1920. His facility in German, acquired in school and in the local environment, later complemented his mathematics and made him especially suitable for work on German communications.

The evidentiary record for his childhood temperament, friendships and religious life is thin. Later reminiscences depict him as reserved, precise and self-effacing, but these are retrospective character sketches rather than contemporaneous diaries. There is no documented juvenile political activity, scandal, addiction, serious illness or criminal conduct.

## Education and Formation, 1923–1932

### University of Poznań

In 1923 Rejewski entered the Faculty of Mathematics and Natural Sciences at the **University of Poznań**. He studied pure and applied mathematics, completing the master's thesis **“Opracować teorię funkcji podwójnie periodycznych drugiego i trzeciego rodzaju oraz wskazać jej zastosowanie”** (“Develop the theory of doubly periodic functions of the second and third kinds and indicate its application”). On **1 March 1929** he received the degree *magister filozofii* in mathematics. [Institute of National Remembrance, Osiński biography](https://ipn.gov.pl/pl/historia-z-ipn/archiwum/213688,Krzysztof-Osinski-Tajemniczy-jak-Enigma-czyli-slow-kilka-o-pewnym-kryptologu-z-B.html) [Muzeum Wojsk Lądowych, chronology](https://marian-rejewski.pl/kalendarium-2/)

Two professors mattered directly to his later work:

- **Zdzisław Krygowski**, a mathematician who helped the General Staff identify mathematically capable, German-speaking students and later offered Rejewski a university assistantship.
- **Mieczysław Abramowicz**, another teacher whom Rejewski later credited, with Krygowski, for the basic mathematics he used. In a letter quoted by the editors of his memoir, Rejewski resisted heroic exaggeration: the cryptologists had done nothing beyond applying knowledge acquired in their first university year from Krygowski and Abramowicz. [Adam Mickiewicz University Press, memoir edition](https://press.amu.edu.pl/pl/wspomnienia-z-mej-pracy-w-biurze-szyfrow-oddzialu-ii-sztabu-glownego-w-latach-1930-1945-memories-of-my-work-at-the-cipher-bureau-of-the-general-staff-second-department-1930-1945.html)

### Secret cryptology course

In early 1929 the Polish General Staff's Cipher Bureau and University of Poznań organized a secret cryptology course for selected German-speaking mathematics students. Sources differ slightly on duration: the Institute of National Remembrance gives **15 January–22 March 1929**, while broader accounts say January–May. Twenty-six students were initially selected. The instructors included Cipher Bureau officers, notably **Maksymilian Ciężki**, and practical work emphasized German systems. Rejewski left before the course's end because of the next stage of his mathematical training; noncompletion did not prevent later recruitment. Jerzy Różycki and Henryk Zygalski emerged from the same recruitment pool. [Institute of National Remembrance, Osiński biography](https://ipn.gov.pl/pl/historia-z-ipn/archiwum/213688,Krzysztof-Osinski-Tajemniczy-jak-Enigma-czyli-slow-kilka-o-pewnym-kryptologu-z-B.html)

### Göttingen and university employment

From the summer of 1929 to summer 1930, Rejewski studied **actuarial statistics at the University of Göttingen**. Göttingen was one of Europe's great mathematical centers, but later accounts report that he found the actuarial level disappointing and disliked the party life of some fellow students. He returned when Krygowski offered him an assistantship. On 1 September 1930 he became an assistant at the University of Poznań, a post he retained until the summer of 1932. [Cipher Centre, “Marian Rejewski—student, mathematician, cryptologist”](https://csenigma.pl/enigma/marian-rejewski-student-matematyk-kryptolog/) [Muzeum Wojsk Lądowych, chronology](https://marian-rejewski.pl/kalendarium-2/)

At the same time, from September 1930, he worked approximately twelve hours a week at a Cipher Bureau outpost in Poznań, deciphering ordinary German military and administrative hand ciphers. This dual university/intelligence employment was his transition from academic mathematics to professional cryptanalysis.

## Early Career and the First Enigma Break, 1930–1933

### Transfer to Warsaw

The General Staff closed the Poznań branch and brought its best recruits to Warsaw. Rejewski joined the Cipher Bureau's German section, **BS-4**, on **1 September 1932**. The office was then in the Saxon Palace complex. His supervisors included Bureau chief **Major Gwido Langer** and German-section chief **Captain Maksymilian Ciężki**. Rejewski gave up his university post for full-time secret state employment.

### The assignment and French intelligence contribution

In October 1932 Ciężki assigned Rejewski the military Enigma problem. For an initial period he worked alone and apart from the routine German-cipher section. The problem was not simply reading messages: the Bureau did not possess the secret military machine wiring or current key system. It had commercial Enigma documentation and intercepts, but the military plugboard and changed internal connections made direct comparison insufficient.

The decisive external contribution came from French intelligence. The French recruited **Hans-Thilo Schmidt**, a financially distressed employee in the German Cipher Office and brother of a German general, under the codename **Asché**. Schmidt supplied photographs of operating instructions and monthly/daily key materials. French cryptanalysts did not themselves exploit the material against the machine; Captain **Gustave Bertrand** passed copies to Poland under the Polish-French arrangement. Rejewski later said that the first batch reached him on **8 December 1932**. The documents did not hand over the rotor wirings: they supplied keys and procedures against which a mathematical reconstruction could be tested. [Foreign Intelligence Agency of Poland, “Enigma decryption”](https://aw.gov.pl/en/history/enigma-decryption/183,Enigma-decryption.html) [Jean Stengers, “Enigma, the French, the Poles and the British, 1931–1940”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/rbph_0035-0818_2004_num_82_1_4836)

### Mathematical reconstruction

The German procedure then required an operator to choose a three-letter message key and type it twice under a daily setting, creating six-letter indicators. Rejewski represented the machine's components as permutations. By grouping the first and fourth, second and fifth, and third and sixth encrypted letters across many messages, he could derive cycle structures. The plugboard relabeled letters but did not change the lengths of those cycles—a conjugacy invariant. From the intercepted indicators, knowledge of daily settings supplied through France, the commercial machine, and the correct guess that the German military entry wheel connected letters alphabetically, he constructed and solved equations for the rotors and reflector.

This was an unusual and historically important move from language-frequency cryptanalysis to algebraic structure. The Institute of Mathematics of the Polish Academy of Sciences describes his method as an application of permutation theory; his later paper provides the first-person mathematical derivation. [Marian Rejewski, “An Application of the Theory of Permutations in Breaking the Enigma Cipher”](https://www.impan.pl/en/publishing-house/journals-and-series/applicationes-mathematicae/all/en/publishing-house/journals-and-series/applicationes-mathematicae/all/16/4/102945/an-application-of-the-theory-of-permutations-in-breaking-the-enigma-cipher)

### Date and meaning of “the break”

The strongest Polish institutional chronologies place the first successful reading **between Christmas 1932 and New Year's Day 1933**; the Foreign Intelligence Agency gives **31 December 1932**. Exact retrospective dates depend on whether “break” means reconstruction of wiring, recovery of one daily key, or the first readable traffic. It is safest to state that Rejewski reconstructed the military Enigma and produced the first decrypts in the last days of December 1932, with routine team exploitation beginning in 1933. [Foreign Intelligence Agency of Poland, “Enigma decryption”](https://aw.gov.pl/en/history/enigma-decryption/183,Enigma-decryption.html) [Muzeum Wojsk Lądowych, chronology](https://marian-rejewski.pl/kalendarium-2/)

The phrase “Rejewski broke Enigma alone” is therefore true only in a bounded sense: he made the central initial mathematical reconstruction while working alone on the assignment. It is false if it erases French agent material, Polish intercept and command infrastructure, later work by Różycki and Zygalski, or the AVA engineers who built replicas and devices.

## Chronological Life History

### 1933–1938: From breakthrough to a working Enigma system

Once the wiring had been reconstructed, **Jerzy Różycki** and **Henryk Zygalski** joined Rejewski in the sustained attack. Their work was a continuous contest with German procedural changes rather than a single permanent solution.

#### Replicas and the AVA network

The radio-engineering firm **AVA** built Polish replicas from the mathematical reconstruction. Its principal relevant figures were **Antoni Palluth**, **Edward Fokczyński**, **Leonard Danilewicz** and **Ludomir Danilewicz**. The machines were not copied from stolen engineering drawings; the Polish team had inferred the internal connections. By the middle 1930s, the Bureau possessed a small family of reconstructed Enigmas for operational work and allied transfer. [Jack Copeland, “Enigma,” in *The Essential Turing*](https://cryptocellar.org/turing/0-19-825080-0.pdf) [IEEE Milestone record](https://ethw.org/Milestones%3AFirst_Breaking_of_Enigma_Code_by_the_Team_of_Polish_Cipher_Bureau%2C_1932-1939)

#### Methods and devices

- **Grill method (Rejewski):** an early method for recovering daily settings while the plugboard remained relatively sparse.
- **Clock method (Różycki):** exploited the different turnover points of the rotors to identify which rotor occupied the right-hand position.
- **Cyclometer and characteristic catalogue (Rejewski, built with AVA):** the cyclometer linked two Enigma sets and allowed the team to precompute the cycle characteristics for six rotor orders and 26³ starting positions—**105,456 cases**. Building the catalogue took more than a year. Once completed, a sufficient set of daily indicators could be matched in minutes rather than solved afresh. Germany's reflector change on 1 November 1937 forced the catalogue to be rebuilt.
- **Zygalski perforated sheets (Zygalski):** sheets whose superimposed holes located repeated-letter patterns while being substantially independent of the plugboard.
- **Bomba kryptologiczna (Rejewski's logical design, AVA construction):** an electromechanical device using linked Enigma mechanisms to test rotor positions against patterns created by the doubly enciphered message key. Six devices—one per possible order of three rotors—were in operation by about November 1938. A favorable solution could be found in roughly two hours.

The cyclometer and bomba were special-purpose electromechanical cryptanalytic machines, not programmable general-purpose computers. Descriptions of the bomba as “the first computer” are commemorative analogy, not a defensible classification. The Science Museum's reconstruction study is especially useful because no original Polish bomba is known to survive and every modern physical visualization therefore contains interpretive choices. [Chris Christensen, “Polish Mathematicians Finding Patterns in Enigma Messages”](https://www.matematiksider.dk/enigma/MAA%20article%20about%20Enigma%20.pdf) [Science Museum Group Journal, “Zygalski sheets”](https://journal.sciencemuseum.ac.uk/article/zygalski-sheets-polish-codebreaking-and-the-role-of-reconstruction-in-the-top-secret-exhibition-at-the-science-museum/)

#### German changes in 1938–1939

On 15 September 1938, the German Army and Air Force changed the indicator procedure, rendering the cyclometer catalogue and older routines ineffective. The bomba and Zygalski sheets were responses. On 15 December, two additional rotors increased the possible three-rotor orders from six to sixty. Replacing six bombas with sixty and multiplying the sheet sets tenfold exceeded the Bureau's budget and manufacturing capacity. A January 1939 increase to as many as ten plugboard pairs further reduced the bomba's effectiveness.

This was a severe operational setback, not proof that Polish work had become worthless. A later-recovered Polish-French report indicates that some Security Service traffic was read through 31 July 1939 and that a military key was recovered on 25 August 1939. The record therefore contradicts the blanket statement that the Poles read nothing after September 1938. [Marek Grajek, “An Inventory of Early Inter-Allied Enigma Cooperation”](https://ep.liu.se/ecp/149/017/ecp18149017.pdf)

#### Home life and finances

Rejewski's marriage and the births of Andrzej and Janina occurred during these secret years. A 1938 military-intelligence contract records monthly pay of **515 złoty**. Archival research also identifies earlier salary increases and special cash awards, including a substantial 1934 reward for theoretical work. In 1938 he received the **Gold Cross of Merit**, while Różycki and Zygalski received Silver Crosses. The reason could not then be public. A significant reward helped Rejewski buy an apartment in Warsaw's Żoliborz district, where the family lived before the war. These are the only material assets of consequence securely documented; no business ownership, investment portfolio, royalty stream or private commercialization is known. [Wojciech Polak, “Marian Rejewski w optyce władz bezpieczeństwa PRL”](https://bazhum.muzhp.pl/media/texts/czasy-nowozytne-periodyk-poswiecony-dziejom-polskim-i-powszechnym-od-xv-do-xx-wieku/2005-tom-18-19/czasy_nowozytne_periodyk_poswiecony-dziejom-polskim-i-powszechnym-od-xv-do-xx-wieku-r2005-t18_19-s115-127.pdf)

### January–August 1939: Allied disclosure

An inconclusive tripartite meeting in Paris in January 1939 left Britain and France unaware of the full Polish achievement. With war approaching, Langer's Bureau chose full disclosure at a second conference in Warsaw and the Cipher Bureau installation in the **Kabaty/Pyry forest**.

The event is best dated at three levels:

- **24–27 July 1939:** the full conference interval, including arrivals and related sessions, in the Polish specialist chronology.
- **25–26 July:** the core forest meeting in GCHQ's dedicated historical account.
- **26 July:** the day emphasized in Alastair Denniston's later report and some British commemorations.

A separate GCHQ speech once gave 15–16 July while elsewhere giving 25–26; that appears to be an internal error, not a distinct conference. [GCHQ, “The Pyry Forest Meeting”](https://www.gchq.gov.uk/information/the-pyry-forest-meeting) [UK National Archives blog, “Polish cryptologists reveal they have cracked the Enigma code”](https://history.blog.gov.uk/2019/07/26/whats-the-context-polish-cryptologists-reveal-they-have-cracked-the-enigma-code-26-july-1939/)

The delegations included Langer, Ciężki, Rejewski, Różycki and Zygalski for Poland; Gustave Bertrand and colleagues for France; and **Alastair Denniston** and **Alfred Dillwyn “Dilly” Knox** for Britain. Alan Turing was **not** at Pyry. The Poles revealed the rotor wiring, algebraic reconstruction, Enigma replica, bomba principle, perforated sheets, and operational methods. Contemporary and later accounts say they also provided a detailed German-language explanation. Poland undertook to give one reconstructed Enigma to Britain and one to France. Bertrand and a British diplomatic courier brought the British replica to London, where it reached intelligence chief Stewart Menzies on 16 August. [UK National Archives, HW 25/12 catalogue](https://discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk/details/r/C10924824) [Marek Grajek, “An Inventory”](https://ep.liu.se/ecp/149/017/ecp18149017.pdf)

No consulted record proves that a physical Polish bomba was handed over. A recovered evacuation record instead says Polish devices were buried or destroyed in September. “Handed the bomba to the Allies” is therefore defensible only as shorthand for disclosure of its design and method; “supplied reconstructed Enigmas and the complete Polish solution” is more exact.

The meeting also began a warm technical relationship between Rejewski and Dilly Knox. Knox had spent years on the military Enigma entry-wheel problem—his “QWERTZU”—and reacted strongly when he learned that the German military connections were alphabetic. Soon after, he sent the Poles silk scarves decorated with a horse-race motif, a personal token often repeated in the literature. GCHQ's later institutional account credits the Polish disclosure with transforming Britain's position. [GCHQ Director's 2014 commemorative speech](https://www.gchq.gov.uk/news/director-gchq-commemorates-crucial-pre-war-enigma-information-sharing-meeting-poland)

### September 1939: Invasion and flight from Poland

Germany invaded Poland on 1 September. Cipher Bureau personnel began leaving Warsaw around 5–6 September. Rejewski's route, reconstructed in the Polish chronology, ran through Mińsk Mazowiecki, Mrozy, Siedlce, Brest, Kowel, Volodymyr, Lutsk, Dubno, Kremenets, Ternopil, Chortkiv and Kuty. The Soviet invasion from the east on 17 September made the escape urgent; the group crossed into Romania and reached Bucharest around 21 September.

The evacuation was not orderly. Personnel destroyed two cyclometers and substantial records after the crossing and buried at least two bombas, Lacida machines and lesser material. The surviving record explicitly accounts for two, not necessarily every one of the reported six operational bombas. Rejewski, Różycki and Zygalski left Bucharest on 22 September, traveling through Yugoslavia and Italy into France, and reached Paris on or about 25 September. [Mariusz Borowiak and Marek Grajek, “Enigma History and an Unexpected Treasure Trove”](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/391/349/292) [Muzeum Wojsk Lądowych, chronology](https://marian-rejewski.pl/kalendarium-2/)

He left his wife and children behind without knowing when he would see them again. The family apartment and most of his settled prewar life were lost to him for the next seven years.

### 1939–1940: P.C. Bruno and cooperation with Bletchley Park

French intelligence reassembled the Polish group at **P.C. Bruno**, Château de Vignolles at Gretz-Armainvilliers, east of Paris. The center formally began in late September; the Polish **équipe Z** was operating there by 20 October. The French paid Rejewski at a captain's rate while his formal Polish military status was regularized separately.

Bruno and Bletchley Park exchanged keys and methods by teleprinter. British staff produced complete sets of Zygalski sheets. **Alan Turing** visited Bruno in January 1940 and spoke directly with Rejewski; he carried part of the British sheet set and discussed plugboard connections. On **17 January 1940**, in Turing's presence according to the Polish chronology, Bruno recovered the first wartime Enigma key, for messages transmitted on 28 October 1939. This phrase means the first key recovered during the war, not that the traffic had been read in real time in October. In later months the much smaller Bruno team recovered a significant minority of allied daily keys—one scholarly estimate gives about seventeen percent. [Jean Stengers, “Enigma, the French, the Poles and the British”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/rbph_0035-0818_2004_num_82_1_4836) [Jack Copeland, “Enigma”](https://cryptocellar.org/turing/0-19-825080-0.pdf)

Rejewski wrote a secret training text, **`Metodyka rozwiązywania szyfrów`** (“Methodology of Solving Ciphers”), for Bruno personnel. On 22 January 1940 his service examination listed him as fit, an educated private; the French requisitioned his cryptologic labor rather than release him for normal troop service.

The German change of 1 May 1940 abolished the doubled message-key procedure and made both the Polish bomba and Zygalski-sheet method obsolete for normal Army/Air Force traffic. British work did not stop: it shifted to cribs and the new Turing–Welchman machine.

### The Polish bomba and the British Bombe: lineage without identity

The British **Bombe** inherited the proof that Enigma could be mechanized, the reconstructed wiring, Polish experience, and probably its name from the Polish **bomba**. It was nevertheless a different attack:

| Feature | Polish bomba, 1938 | British Bombe, 1940 onward |
|---|---|---|
| Primary basis | Doubly enciphered message-key patterns | Guessed plaintext “cribs” aligned with ciphertext |
| Logical design | Marian Rejewski | Alan Turing; generalized by Gordon Welchman's diagonal board |
| Engineering | AVA engineers | Harold “Doc” Keen and British Tabulating Machine Company |
| Original scale | Six devices for six three-rotor orders | Industrial wartime network, expanded and repeatedly modified |
| Dependency | German indicator procedure retained through April 1940 | Message-specific crib structure and menu logic |
| Relationship | Foundational Polish precedent | New design adapted to new procedures and wartime volume |

A 1 November 1939 British note described the proposed machine as resembling but being much larger than the Polish Bombe and “adapted to use for cribs.” That near-contemporary formulation captures both continuity and difference. [Jack Copeland, “Enigma”](https://cryptocellar.org/turing/0-19-825080-0.pdf) [GCHQ, “The Pyry Forest Meeting”](https://www.gchq.gov.uk/information/the-pyry-forest-meeting)

### June 1940–November 1942: Algeria and Cadix

As France collapsed, Bruno personnel flew to Algeria on 24 June 1940. Rejewski spent the summer in or near Oran. Under the Vichy regime, Bertrand secretly re-established the operation in southern France as **P.C. Cadix**, at the Château des Fouzes near Uzès, under the counterintelligence cover *Travaux Ruraux*. The Polish section, also called **Ekspozytura 300** or équipe Z, resumed in October 1940. Rejewski used the cover identity **Pierre Ranaud**, presented in surviving accounts as a schoolteacher or mathematics teacher from Nantes. [Marek Grajek, “Mysteries of P.C. Cadix and its Evacuation in 1942/43”](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/695/601/712)

Enigma itself was only part of Cadix's work and perhaps a small part of Rejewski's daily work because the center lacked a regular supply of relevant intercepts. Surviving documents associate him with German agent transposition/Playfair systems, reconstruction of the Swiss commercial Enigma, a probable-word method, and other systems. Bertrand later credited Cadix with thousands of decrypts, but the exact division between Cadix and the Algerian intercept outstation is unclear.

Important episodes included:

- **1940 or 1942 technical history:** Rejewski and probably Zygalski prepared the thirty-two-page typescript **`“Enigma” 1930–1940. Metoda i historia rozwiązania niemieckiego szyfru maszynowego (w zarysie)`**. One scholarly bibliography dates it 1940; an archival catalogue and accounts of Bertrand's commission date it 1942. Unless the original is inspected, both dates should be retained.
- **3 July 1941 Lacida test:** Rejewski and Zygalski broke a test message made with Poland's own Lacida cipher machine in approximately two hours, exposing serious weaknesses. Langer suspended the machine's use. This was a technical success and an institutional embarrassment for Polish designers, but not a scandal involving Rejewski.
- **9 January 1942:** Różycki, Jan Graliński and Piotr Smoleński died when the French passenger ship *Lamoricière* sank in the Mediterranean while returning from the Algerian detachment. Rejewski lost a close colleague; two Polish Enigma replicas may also have been lost, though the equipment detail is less secure than the deaths.

Cadix personnel lived under persistent risk from German and Italian security services, radio direction finding, and an ambiguous Vichy environment. Later Polish and French accounts traded accusations over the evacuation: Polish officers alleged obstruction or negligence; Bertrand in turn alleged indiscipline, including alcohol abuse and refusals to cooperate. These hostile retrospective claims do not establish that Rejewski himself abused alcohol or that Bertrand intentionally betrayed the team. The documentary reconstruction shows mistrust and failed coordination, not a proven conspiracy. [Marek Grajek, “Mysteries of P.C. Cadix”](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/695/601/712)

### November 1942–August 1943: Escape, arrest and Spanish imprisonment

Allied landings in North Africa and the German occupation of Vichy France forced Cadix to disperse. Sources give 6, 9 or 10 November for closure, depending on whether they mean cessation of work, evacuation order or final abandonment. Rejewski and Zygalski remained in hiding and attempted several escape routes.

On the night of **29–30 January 1943**, they crossed the Pyrenees. Their guide robbed them at gunpoint, and Spanish gendarmes arrested them near Puigcerdà. They were held successively at Belver/Bellver, La Seu d'Urgell and Lleida/Lérida. The Polish Red Cross and diplomatic representatives intervened. Most sources give **4 May 1943** for release; a competing secondary chronology gives 24 May. They then stayed in Madrid, entered Portugal around 21–22 July, transferred by sea to a British vessel, reached Gibraltar, and flew to Britain at the end of July or beginning of August. The most defensible arrival interval is **2–3 August 1943**. [Marek Grajek, “Mysteries of P.C. Cadix”](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/695/601/712) [MacTutor biography](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Rejewski/)

Rejewski reportedly developed rheumatism in the damp Spanish prisons. No allegation of espionage for Spain or Germany emerged from the detention; he and Zygalski had concealed their cryptologic role.

### August 1943–November 1946: Britain and demobilization

Rejewski and Zygalski joined the Polish Signals/intelligence establishment at Stanmore Park and Boxmoor/Felden. Despite their expertise, they initially served as privates and were not placed in Bletchley Park's Enigma sections. Rejewski became second lieutenant on 10 October 1943 and lieutenant on 1 January 1945. He headed or helped head the German section designated **N**, working principally on manual German SS and Security Service double-Playfair systems. The unit also attacked Soviet ciphers, but no surviving document proves Rejewski personally handled Soviet traffic; recovered examples concern his German work.

Why Britain did not return the two Poles to front-line Enigma work remains unresolved. Security concern after travel through occupied territory, strict compartmentation, late arrival, different skills needed after procedural changes, and Polish deployment priorities are all plausible. No document located proves the popular allegation that British jealousy or a plan to monopolize credit caused their exclusion.

During this period Rejewski:

- met courier and future broadcaster **Jan Nowak-Jeziorański** in early 1944;
- wrote a critical memorandum on 1 October 1944, **`Kilka uwag na temat trudności, w jakich znajduje się w chwili obecnej dział szyfrów niemieckich`**, about the German section's operational problems;
- moved to general studies and possibly Russian-cipher preparation after section N closed in November 1944;
- received the **Silver Cross of Merit with Swords** and promotion to lieutenant on 1 January 1945;
- traveled to France from 21 April to 12 May 1945 on official work;
- received the **Medal Wojska** later in 1945;
- joined Zygalski and Sylwester Palluth on 8 October 1945 in a committee that destroyed twenty-four of thirty-six sensitive files. Twelve retained files, entrusted to Edmund Piechowiak, were rediscovered decades later.

He declined transfer to the Polish Resettlement Corps on **11 September 1946**. A personal questionnaire dated 6 September had said he hoped eventually to settle with his family in France and work in actuarial mathematics, evidence that his plans were not always a simple binary choice between Britain and communist Poland. Homesickness, separation from Irena and the children, and uncertainty about their circumstances drove the immediate decision. He requested return in October, was demobilized in November, and arrived through Gdynia on **20–21 November 1946**. [Mariusz Borowiak and Marek Grajek, “Unexpected Treasure Trove”](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/391/349/292) [Wojciech Polak, security-file study](https://bazhum.muzhp.pl/media/texts/czasy-nowozytne-periodyk-poswiecony-dziejom-polskim-i-powszechnym-od-xv-do-xx-wieku/2005-tom-18-19/czasy_nowozytne_periodyk_poswiecony-dziejom-polskim-i-powszechnym-od-xv-do-xx-wieku-r2005-t18_19-s115-127.pdf)

### 1946–1950: Return, family loss and removal from strategic employment

Rejewski returned to Irena, Andrzej and Janina in Bydgoszcz. They were then living with Irena's mother at **10/2 Dworcowa Street**. He explored an academic return at the University of Poznań and the new technical institution in Szczecin. Professor **Władysław Orlicz** offered assistance, but Rejewski lacked a doctorate, postwar housing was scarce, and no appointment materialized.

On **1 February 1947**, he became head of the invoicing section at the cable factory **Kabel Polski** in Bydgoszcz. On **9 July 1947**, his eleven-year-old son Andrzej died from complications of poliomyelitis. This loss is repeatedly documented; no equally strong evidence supports speculative psychological diagnoses sometimes attached to Rejewski's later reserve.

The communist security service became interested in him because he had worked for the prewar General Staff's Second Department and the wartime Polish command in the West. A workplace personnel clerk copied military documents carried in his wallet. The Provincial Office of Public Security in Bydgoszcz issued an initial report on 12 March 1949. Because Kabel Polski was designated strategically sensitive, security pressure brought his removal: notice was dated **28 January 1950**; sources give 21 March or 30 April as the effective final day. The distinction between notice and completed departure likely explains part of the conflict. [Wojciech Polak, security-file study](https://bazhum.muzhp.pl/media/texts/czasy-nowozytne-periodyk-poswiecony-dziejom-polskim-i-powszechnym-od-xv-do-xx-wieku/2005-tom-18-19/czasy_nowozytne_periodyk_poswiecony-dziejom-polskim-i-powszechnym-od-xv-do-xx-wieku-r2005-t18_19-s115-127.pdf)

### 1950–1967: Civilian accounting career and state surveillance

His postwar employment sequence, with institutional reorganizations reflected in changing names, was:

- **1950–1951:** State Surveying Enterprise and/or the Surveyors' Union, in accounting/economic work.
- **15 June 1951–30 September 1954:** craft and woodworking cooperative organizations, as financial clerk, cashier, and senior accountant.
- **1 October 1954–31 January 1967:** Provincial Association of Work Cooperatives, progressing through senior accountant, economic instructor and senior accounting instructor to head of the Costs and Prices Inspectorate.

The assignments were steady but far below his mathematical and intelligence expertise. They demonstrate occupational marginality, not unemployment or destitution. No evidence has been found that he owned the enterprises, received Enigma royalties, held patents, or accumulated substantial wealth.

#### The security cases

Security-service records refer to inquiries and operations with codenames including **`Sztab`**, **`Zachód`**, **`Targowica`**, and finally the individual observation case **`Kryptolog`**. The IPN catalogue identifies file **AIPN By 044/200** and dates opening of `Kryptolog` to **13 February 1956**; a local chronology says 14 February, and one derivative text appears to misprint the year as 1955. The primary catalogue date should control. [IPN archival catalogue, AIPN By 044/200](https://www.katalog.bip.ipn.gov.pl/informacje/582954) [IPN presentation of the file](https://przystanekhistoria.pl/pa2/tematy/nauka-i-technika/105592,Marian-Rejewski-Czlowiek-ktory-zlamal-Enigme.html)

Methods included:

- workplace informants and copying of his papers;
- neighborhood and employment inquiries;
- interception and inspection of correspondence;
- attempts to map former Second Department and Western-army contacts;
- review of a 1948 handwritten curriculum vitae and personnel questionnaires;
- a security opinion after he applied on 29 April 1956 for employment in the Ministry of National Defence Cipher Bureau.

The reports portrayed him as quiet, without addictions, on good terms with neighbors, and unwilling to discuss politics. Six months of mail monitoring produced personal correspondence, not evidence of espionage. The service rejected or frustrated his 1956 attempt to re-enter cipher work, but no surviving record states a technical reason. On **12 June 1958**, it closed `Kryptolog` because it had not established hostile activity or a foreign-intelligence connection.

These facts support the formulation **documented surveillance, suspicion and professional harm**. They do not support claims that Rejewski was charged, tried, convicted, imprisoned after the war, or proved to be an agent. Nor do they establish that senior authorities knew his Enigma role and centrally directed a campaign to erase it; several security reports misunderstood his history, at one point confusing Zdzisław Krygowski with another academic and inventing a captivity at Murnau.

#### Political caution and private life

No record located shows party membership, public opposition organizing, or political office. In a 22 July 1957 letter to university friend **Stanisław Dobrzycki**, he wrote that he had quickly understood “which way the wind was blowing” and had therefore limited contact with old acquaintances. This is evidence of self-protective caution, not a comprehensive ideology. [Wojciech Polak, security-file study](https://bazhum.muzhp.pl/media/texts/czasy-nowozytne-periodyk-poswiecony-dziejom-polskim-i-powszechnym-od-xv-do-xx-wieku/2005-tom-18-19/czasy_nowozytne_periodyk_poswiecony-dziejom-polskim-i-powszechnym-od-xv-do-xx-wieku-r2005-t18_19-s115-127.pdf)

The marriage ceremony of 1934 is documented as Catholic, at the Church of the Sacred Heart of Jesus in Bydgoszcz. No substantial source establishes Rejewski's later religious observance, theology, conversion, clerical association, or public religious activity. Claims beyond the rite should remain unasserted.

### 1966–1973: Disability pension, memoir and move to Warsaw

Rejewski was classified in **disability group III** on 13 October 1966. He ended employment on 31 January 1967 and began a disability pension on 1 February. In spring 1967 he wrote a typescript of more than one hundred pages, **`Wspomnienia z mej pracy w Biurze Szyfrów Oddziału II Sztabu Głównego w latach 1930–1945`**. The text went to the Military Historical Institute; sources variously say he deposited, submitted or sold it, and the acquisition instrument has not been consulted. The manuscript did not immediately create public fame.

He moved within Bydgoszcz in 1967 and returned to Warsaw in 1969, recovering or reoccupying a family apartment and later living with Janina, her husband and their son at **2 Gdańska Street**. One local chronology gives retirement age on 16 August 1969; another IPN synthesis gives 1970. The disability pension from February 1967 is secure; the administrative conversion to ordinary old-age retirement is not.

### 1973–1980: Public emergence, late writings and recognition

For three decades the Enigma story had been protected by wartime secrecy and then fragmented by national memory. **Gustave Bertrand's** 1973 book *Enigma ou la plus grande énigme de la guerre 1939–1945* was the first major insider account to disclose the Polish achievement publicly, though it did not initially give every individual full prominence. A Warsaw newspaper appeal, **“Szukamy ludzi związanych z Enigmą”** (“We are looking for people connected with Enigma”), helped reconnect the story to living participants. Rejewski contacted Bertrand, Zygalski, Tadeusz Lisicki and historian Władysław Kozaczuk. In 1974 he wrote a supplement to his 1967 memoir.

**F. W. Winterbotham's** *The Ultra Secret* (1974) popularized Ultra in English but sharply minimized the Polish prehistory. On 29 December 1974, **David Kahn** named Rejewski, Różycki and Zygalski in *The New York Times*, helping international recognition. Rejewski then cooperated with Polish historians and filmmakers while also correcting accounts he considered inaccurate.

Late projects included:

- a 1976 cryptanalytic examination of encoded early socialist correspondence associated with Józef Piłsudski, at the request of the Józef Piłsudski Institute of America;
- consultation on Andrzej Trzos-Rastawiecki's documentary *Enigma* and Roman Wionczek's feature/television dramatization;
- an unfinished or unpublished Polish translation of Bertrand's book;
- technical articles explaining the permutation attack, Polish daily-key methods and the bomba;
- a substantial interview with historian **Richard A. Woytak** on 24 July 1978;
- a written rebuttal to the inaccurate Polish section of the first volume of the British official intelligence history.

His state and professional honors finally became public. He received the **Officer's Cross of the Order of Polonia Restituta** in 1978, with the decoration ceremony on 12 December; source dates for the conferral instrument differ among 12 August, 30 August and 29 November. In 1979 he received the **Silver Medal “For Merit to National Defence”**—again, exact award date varies—and was elected an honorary member of the **Polish Mathematical Society** on 15 September.

### Technologies and projects

Rejewski's securely attributed technical contributions are the algebraic reconstruction of military Enigma; the grill; cyclometer and characteristic catalogue; bomba logic; Enigma replicas specified through reconstructed wiring; wartime methods and training texts; double-Playfair work; and the Lacida security test. Zygalski sheets belong to Henryk Zygalski; the clock method to Jerzy Różycki; fabrication to AVA's engineers; the British Bombe to Turing, Keen and Welchman within a broader Bletchley organization.

### Patents, businesses, property and money

No patent in Rejewski's name has been found. The absence is unsurprising: the devices were classified work for military intelligence, implemented by a contractor. No evidence shows that he owned AVA shares, licensed a device, received royalties, operated a private company, traded on intelligence, or held substantial investments. His known financial history consists of salary, military rewards and decorations, purchase of a Żoliborz apartment with award money, postwar wages, and disability/retirement pensions. The cable-factory removal appears politically and security driven, not a financial dispute. No bankruptcy, tax case, lawsuit, inheritance contest or suspicious transaction was located.

## Political, Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

- **Political:** State service tied him institutionally to the Second Polish Republic and the Polish government-in-exile. He was a soldier and intelligence employee, not a documented party politician. No evidence was found of fascist, communist, socialist, nationalist-party or organized opposition membership. His 1976 work on Piłsudski-era correspondence was a cryptanalytic commission, not proof of a party allegiance.
- **Under communist rule:** He limited old contacts and avoided politics under surveillance. This behavior is documented; the precise private beliefs behind it are not.
- **Religious:** A Catholic marriage ceremony is established. Personal practice and doctrine are not reconstructable from the consulted record.
- **Intellectual:** His formation joined pure mathematics, German language, actuarial training and practical cryptanalysis. The distinctive intellectual move was to model the cipher as permutations and exploit structural invariants rather than begin with word frequencies. His later writings became both technical exposition and a contest over documentary credit.
- **Self-presentation:** His surviving remarks are notably anti-mythic. He emphasized ordinary university mathematics, the contributions of collaborators and French intelligence, and the difference between Poland's reasons for allied disclosure and later claims that technical defeat forced it. This modest style itself became part of his posthumous image, but it should not be mistaken for a complete absence of priority disputes: he wrote a firm rebuttal when the British official history misdescribed Polish work.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, and Financial Questions

### Documented investigation: Polish state-security surveillance

The postwar surveillance described above is the only substantial investigation found. Its evidentiary status is precise:

- **Documented fact:** security organs investigated him, recruited or used informants, inspected correspondence, affected his employment at Kabel Polski, and opened `Kryptolog` in 1956.
- **Suspicion/allegation in state files:** possible connections arising from prewar Second Department and Western service.
- **Outcome:** no hostile activity or foreign-intelligence relationship established; case closed in 1958.
- **Not found:** indictment, formal criminal charge, trial, conviction, postwar imprisonment, confiscation judgment, or proof of spying against Poland.

### Cadix accusations

Postwar accounts contain French-Polish accusations about the 1942 evacuation, ranging from incompetence and obstruction to alcohol-related indiscipline. The surviving documentation supports institutional mistrust and a badly managed escape. It does not establish intentional betrayal by Bertrand, a criminal conspiracy, or an addiction on Rejewski's part. These remain allegations within partisan retrospective accounts. [Marek Grajek, “Mysteries of P.C. Cadix”](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/695/601/712)

### Credit disputes

The 1979 first volume of *British Intelligence in the Second World War* included an Appendix 1 that understated or misstated Polish and French contributions and repeated an unsupported story involving a Pole who supposedly worked in an Enigma factory. Rejewski prepared **`Remarks on Appendix 1...`**, published posthumously in 1982. The official history later replaced the account with a revised Appendix 30 in volume 3, part 2 (1988). This is a documented historiographical correction. The broader claim that British authorities conspired for decades to steal Rejewski's personal patent, money or fame has no supporting evidence. [Marian Rejewski, “Remarks on Appendix 1”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/0161-118291856867) [GCHQ 2014 commemorative speech](https://www.gchq.gov.uk/news/director-gchq-commemorates-crucial-pre-war-enigma-information-sharing-meeting-poland)

### Negative findings

No credible source consulted identified Rejewski as a perpetrator or subject of a substantiated case involving fraud, corruption, money laundering, theft, violence, abuse, collaboration with Nazi Germany, illicit business, sexual scandal, or falsification of credentials. No lawsuit or civil judgment was located. These negative findings do not prove that no dispute ever existed; they define the limits of the currently reconstructable record.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. “Alan Turing broke Enigma first and alone”

1. **Story:** Turing single-handedly broke Enigma at Bletchley Park.
2. **Appearance and circulation:** British wartime secrecy, English-language Ultra memoirs after 1974, and dramatizations centered on Bletchley—especially *The Imitation Game* (2014)—made Turing the dominant public face.
3. **Evidence:** Rejewski reconstructed German military Enigma in late 1932. Różycki, Zygalski, French intelligence and AVA were essential to sustained prewar success; Turing, Welchman, Keen and the Bletchley organization then created new methods at wartime scale.
4. **Assessment:** false as an exclusive priority claim; valid only if narrowed to particular British wartime innovations.
5. **Effect:** delayed English-language recognition of the Polish and French foundations; later corrective campaigns sometimes produced an inverse Polish-only myth. [GCHQ, “The Pyry Forest Meeting”](https://www.gchq.gov.uk/information/the-pyry-forest-meeting)

### 2. “Rejewski alone broke Enigma”

1. **Story:** a solitary Polish genius solved the entire problem without outside help.
2. **Circulation:** biographical headlines, national commemoration and the rhetorical correction to Turing-centered narratives.
3. **Evidence for:** he alone conducted the concentrated initial mathematical assignment and made the decisive reconstruction.
4. **Evidence against:** Schmidt's material, Bertrand's transfer, Cipher Bureau direction, intercepted traffic, Różycki and Zygalski's methods, and AVA hardware were indispensable to the larger program.
5. **Effect:** restores Rejewski's priority but can erase the intelligence and engineering network that made it operational.

### 3. “Poland handed the bomba machine to Britain”

1. **Story:** at Pyry, Rejewski physically handed over a bomba which Britain copied.
2. **Circulation:** compressed commemorative language, journalism and the similarity of *bomba/Bombe*.
3. **Evidence:** Poland demonstrated or explained the bomba and disclosed its method, wiring, sheets and complete solution; Britain and France received reconstructed Enigma machines. A recovered evacuation report places Polish bombas among equipment buried or destroyed in September.
4. **Assessment:** no good evidence of physical bomba transfer; strong evidence of intellectual/design disclosure and Enigma-replica delivery.
5. **Effect:** emphasizes Polish priority but obscures the distinct Turing–Welchman crib-based design. [Mariusz Borowiak and Marek Grajek, “Unexpected Treasure Trove”](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/391/349/292)

### 4. The ice-cream origin of *bomba*

1. **Story:** while Rejewski, Różycki and Zygalski ate an ice-cream dessert called a *bomba*, Różycki proposed the name for the machine.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** attributed in postwar retellings to Colonel **Tadeusz Lisicki** and repeated in books, lectures and museum lore.
3. **Evidence for:** Lisicki's recollection is a named oral tradition.
4. **Evidence against:** Lisicki was not a member of the prewar Cipher Bureau and had not known Różycki, who died in 1942. Rejewski's own published formulation was that they called the devices bombs “for lack of a better name.” Other unverified stories invoke a falling weight, ticking noise, or bomb-like form. No original machine survives to test mechanical anecdotes.
5. **Effect:** supplies a memorable human origin story and has become the device's principal folklore, despite weak provenance. [Christopher Christensen, “The Evolution of the Cryptologic Bombe”](https://websites.nku.edu/~christensen/091hnr304%20Enigma%20and%20the%20bombe.pdf)

### 5. “Poland disclosed only because its methods had failed”

1. **Story:** the Poles shared Enigma because the five-rotor system had defeated them and they had nothing left to contribute.
2. **Appearance:** Harry Hinsley and derivative British accounts framed the July 1939 transfer in relation to technical exhaustion.
3. **Evidence:** the German changes had severely reduced capacity and made a tenfold equipment expansion unaffordable.
4. **Counterevidence:** Rejewski explicitly rejected technical defeat as the motive, saying the worsening political situation prompted an allied contribution and that Poland would have shared even without difficulty. Some traffic continued to be read in 1939.
5. **Effect:** in its strongest form, the story diminishes strategic generosity and continuing Polish capability; the inverse claim that difficulties played no practical role is also too absolute.

### 6. “Poles did nothing after September 1938”

The main Army/Air Force attack was drastically restricted, but later-discovered records show continuing Security Service reads through July 1939 and a recovered military key on 25 August. The categorical story is false; the underlying resource crisis is documented. [Marek Grajek, “An Inventory”](https://ep.liu.se/ecp/149/017/ecp18149017.pdf)

### 7. “The bomba was the first computer” or led directly to Colossus

The bomba was a specialized electromechanical search device. Colossus was an electronic machine for Lorenz/Tunny teleprinter traffic, not Enigma, and did not descend directly from Rejewski's machine. Calling the bomba a “computer” can be a loose historical analogy; calling it the first programmable electronic computer or the direct parent of Colossus is false.

### 8. “British jealousy deliberately excluded Rejewski from Bletchley Park”

Rejewski and Zygalski were indeed kept outside the principal Enigma sections after arriving in 1943, and their talent was underused. Intentional credit theft is an allegation without a located assignment order or policy memorandum. Security after passage through occupied territory, compartmentation, changed techniques, late arrival and Polish command priorities are documented or plausible alternatives. The motive remains unresolved.

### 9. “Communist Poland erased him because officials knew he had broken Enigma”

The regime surveilled him and caused professional harm; those are facts. His importance remained secret and poorly understood, and the security files themselves contain basic errors. A centrally directed Enigma-specific suppression campaign is unproved. His silence was a combination of official secrecy, self-protection and the limited public record. He was not wholly silent: he wrote the memoir in 1967, corresponded with historians and published late technical work.

### 10. “The break shortened the war by exactly two/four years and saved a fixed number of lives”

British official historian **Harry Hinsley** offered a retrospective counterfactual estimate that Ultra as a whole shortened the European war by not less than two years and probably four. Ultra encompassed far more than Rejewski, Poland, or even Enigma. Exact claims—fourteen, twenty-one, or thirty million lives—are later extrapolations without a reproducible documentary calculation. They should be labeled unverified counterfactuals, not body counts attributable to one person. [Hinsley lecture, “The Influence of ULTRA in the Second World War”](https://www.cdpa.co.uk/UoP/HoC/Lectures/HoC_08e.PDF)

### 11. “France contributed nothing”

French cryptanalysts did not make Rejewski's mathematical reconstruction, but French intelligence recruited Schmidt, acquired the decisive manuals and keys, delivered them to Poland, and hosted Bruno and Cadix. Erasing France is as inaccurate as erasing Poland. [Jean Stengers, “Enigma, the French, the Poles and the British”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/rbph_0035-0818_2004_num_82_1_4836)

### 12. Modern biographical dispute

Robert Gawłowski's 2022 Polish biography and 2023 English adaptation revived interest but received a sharply hostile review from Bydgoszcz historian **Hanka Sowińska**, who catalogued errors in dates, family relationships, churches and addresses and accused the book of fabrication. A museum-associated genealogical critique made similar objections. These criticisms are documented scholarly/public disputes; every allegation in the reviews has not been independently adjudicated here. They justify preferring civil, university and archival evidence where the popular biography conflicts. [Hanka Sowińska, review](https://czasopisma.ukw.edu.pl/index.php/kronika-bydgoska/article/download/858/859) [Jerzy Lelwic, genealogical critique](https://marian-rejewski.pl/biograficzny-galimatias-czyli-jak-skrzywdzic-mariana-rejewskiego/)

### Work attributed incorrectly or needing caution

- Władysław Kozaczuk's *Enigma* is not a Rejewski-authored book, although it contains his appendices.
- `Enigma 1930–1940` should not be assigned to Rejewski alone without noting probable Zygalski co-authorship and the 1940/1942 date conflict.
- The Woytak conversation is an interview; its wording passed through transcription and translation.
- Posthumous English titles in 1981–1984 often translate or rework overlapping Polish texts and should not be counted as wholly independent compositions.
- No patent, commercial manual, manifesto, diary, novel, artwork, film, recording or political speech under Rejewski's name has been located.

## Death and Health

Rejewski suffered rheumatism reportedly acquired or aggravated in Spanish imprisonment. By 1966 his health qualified him for disability group III, but the accessible record does not specify every diagnosis underlying the classification. On **13 February 1980**, after going out in Warsaw, he suffered a massive heart attack and died, described in some accounts as at home. No accident, suicide, addiction or foul play is alleged in reliable sources.

He was buried with military honors in **Powązki Military Cemetery**, quarter B39. Irena was later buried beside him after her death in 1986. [Muzeum Wojsk Lądowych, chronology](https://marian-rejewski.pl/kalendarium-2/)

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Rejewski died just as his technical explanations were reaching print. The next four decades brought an institutional reversal from secrecy and marginal recognition to state, military, mathematical and popular commemoration.

### Principal phases

1. **1980s—documentary correction:** posthumous *Cryptologia* papers, Woytak's interview, Kozaczuk's English edition and the revised British official history established the documentary core internationally.
2. **2000s—Polish civic commemoration:** the 2000 Grand Cross, bilingual biographical volumes, monuments in Bydgoszcz and Poznań, plaques and named schools placed him in national public memory.
3. **2010s—international institutional recognition:** the Knowlton Award, IEEE Milestone and NSA Hall of Honor formally recognized both Rejewski and the Polish team.
4. **2020s—museum and media recovery:** the Enigma Cipher Centre in Poznań opened in 2021; Bydgoszcz Główna station took his name in 2022; digitized interview clips circulated internationally in 2024.

Representative memorials include the **Bydgoszcz bench monument** by Michał Kubiak (2005); the **Poznań Cryptologists Monument** to Rejewski, Różycki and Zygalski (2007); the **Enigma Cipher Centre** at Adam Mickiewicz University (opened 24 September 2021); and naming of **Bydgoszcz Główna railway station** for Rejewski on 2 February 2022. [AMU, opening of the Enigma Cipher Centre](https://amu.edu.pl/dla-mediow/komunikaty-prasowe/uniwersyteccy-kryptolodzy-uhonorowani-w-collegium-martineum-uam-otwarto-centrum-szyfrow-enigma3) [Government of Poland, station naming](https://www.gov.pl/web/uw-kujawsko-pomorski/kryptolog-marian-rejewski-patronem-dworca-kolejowego-bydgoszcz-glowna)

His name also designates streets, schools, plaques, a mural, cryptologic competitions and an honorary badge of Poland's Military Counterintelligence Service. [Military Counterintelligence Service, Rejewski badge](https://skw.gov.pl/o-nas/tradycja/odznaka-skw-im-mariana-rejewskiego-hold-dla-zaslug-wybitnego-kryptologa/)

### Historical reinterpretation

The durable scholarly interpretation is collaborative and sequential rather than national and zero-sum:

- France supplied human intelligence and sustained the liaison.
- Rejewski made the first mathematical reconstruction of the German military Enigma.
- Różycki, Zygalski, Polish commanders and AVA created an operational prewar system.
- Poland disclosed the achievement in July 1939 and delivered reconstructed machines.
- French, Polish and British teams cooperated at Bruno.
- Turing, Welchman, Keen and thousands of Bletchley and allied personnel built distinct wartime attacks and industrial capacity.

This formulation neither dilutes Rejewski's priority nor attributes later allied successes to him personally. The 2014 IEEE plaque is significant because it recognizes the **team of the Polish Cipher Bureau**, while the NSA Hall of Honor singles out Rejewski's mathematical breakthrough. Together they express the two scales of the history. [IEEE Milestone](https://ethw.org/Milestones%3AFirst_Breaking_of_Enigma_Code_by_the_Team_of_Polish_Cipher_Bureau%2C_1932-1939) [NSA Hall of Honor](https://www.nsa.gov/History/Cryptologic-History/Historical-Figures/Historical-Figures-View/Article/1621548/marian-rejewski/)
