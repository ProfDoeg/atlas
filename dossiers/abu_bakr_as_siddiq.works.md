## Works by Abu Bakr as-Siddiq

### Compilation project

- **632–634:** *The Ṣuḥuf of Abū Bakr*. A project, proposed by ʿUmar and commissioned by Abū Bakr after the battle of al-Yamāma, in which Zayd ibn Thābit gathered Qurʾanic material into a single official collection. It is not properly an authored book by Abū Bakr: its text was the Qurʾan, its principal compiler was Zayd, and no manuscript securely identifiable as these ṣuḥuf survives; even the project’s historicity and precise relation to the later ʿUthmānic codex remain subjects of scholarship. [1–3]

### Manifestos and proclamations

- **632:** *Speech after the death of Muḥammad*. A brief public proclamation directing the community’s allegiance from the deceased prophet to the living God; transmitted in several early narratives but preserved only through later textual witnesses. [4–5]

- **632:** *Speech at the Saqīfa of the Banū Sāʿida*. An improvised political speech advocating Qurashī leadership and offering ʿUmar or Abū ʿUbayda as candidates. The surviving versions are retrospective reports rather than a contemporaneous transcript, so neither their exact wording nor their boundaries constitute a securely fixed text. [4–5]

- **632:** *Inaugural address after the general oath of allegiance*. A manifesto of conditional government: the caliph presents himself as accountable, commands obedience only while he obeys God and Muḥammad, and defines justice through protection of the weak. Its famous compact form is multiply transmitted, but the verbal form varies among the chronicles and sermon anthologies. [4–6]

- **632:** *Address defending the expedition of Usāma ibn Zayd*. A policy speech insisting that the army organized by Muḥammad should depart despite the crisis following his death. It survives as reported speech in later historical narratives rather than as an independently transmitted document. [4–5]

- **632:** *Address advocating war against the withholding tribes*. A spoken manifesto refusing to separate ritual prayer from the alms obligation and announcing armed enforcement. The best-attested core survives in canonical ḥadīth, while expanded orations in historical works should not automatically be treated as verbatim records. [4, 7]

- **632:** *Letter to the apostate tribes*. A circular proclamation sent with Abū Bakr’s commanders during the Ridda campaigns, combining theological argument, an account of Muḥammad’s mission, and notice of military consequences. Al-Ṭabarī says copies sent to different tribes were identical, but his text comes through much later transmitters—prominently Sayf ibn ʿUmar—so authorship of the precise wording is uncertain. [4]

- **632:** *Instructions to the commanders of the Ridda armies*. A military protocol accompanying the circular letter and prescribing proclamation, testing of allegiance, and action against resistance. It represents an order issued under Abū Bakr’s authority; the surviving literary formulation cannot be shown to be his personal composition. [4]

### Administrative letters and protocols

- **632–634:** *Correspondence with Khālid ibn al-Walīd*. Orders concerning Khālid’s Ridda and Iraqi campaigns survive dispersed through the chronicles. They resist treatment as one stable work: individual letters have no authorial collection, many are undated, and their wording depends on later historical transmission. [4, 8]

- **632–634:** *Correspondence with the commanders in Iraq, Syria, Yemen, Oman, Bahrain, and Hadramawt*. A dispersed administrative corpus of appointments, reinforcements, strategic orders, rebukes, and replies made in Abū Bakr’s name. Modern “collected works” editions assemble these notices from chronicles, but neither completeness nor personal dictation by Abū Bakr can be established. [6, 8]

- **633–634:** *Instructions to Yazīd ibn Abī Sufyān*. A protocol of conduct traditionally delivered when an army was dispatched toward Syria, including rules concerning noncombatants, cultivated land, animals, and religious recluses. Its several recensions differ, and some clauses are also attached to other commanders; it should therefore be catalogued as an attributed instruction, not a recoverable autograph. [8–9]

- **634:** *Appointment covenant naming ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb as successor*. A performative state document dictated during Abū Bakr’s final illness and written down—according to the historical tradition—by ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān. It acts rather than merely describes, but surviving versions differ and the material document is lost. [5–6]

### Counsel and testament

- **632–634:** *Counsel to ʿUmar*. A group of moral and governmental instructions represented in later adab and historical literature as Abū Bakr’s advice to ʿUmar. The pieces have no securely recoverable original order and sometimes absorb maxims transmitted elsewhere without a named occasion. [6, 10]

- **634:** *Deathbed instructions concerning burial and the caliphal estate*. An oral testament directing his burial near Muḥammad and the return to the public treasury of property used in the caliphate. These are remembered acts of instruction rather than a surviving written will, and reports disagree over particulars. [5, 10]

### Sermons, prayers, and sayings

- **632–634:** *Caliphal sermons*. A scattered body of Friday addresses, exhortations, campaign speeches, and short homilies collected from chronicles, ḥadīth books, and anthologies. They are an attributed oral corpus rather than a book made by Abū Bakr; most lack fixed titles and exact dates, and their wording and authenticity vary by transmission. [6, 10–11]

- **Before 634:** *Supplications and aphoristic sayings*. Brief prayers, moral counsels, legal opinions, and maxims attributed to Abū Bakr throughout the transmitted literature. These resist enumeration as autonomous works because later collectors excerpted them from reports of actions and conversations, often without independent chains or stable textual limits. [6, 10]

- **Before 634:** *Narrations from Muḥammad*. Later musnads arrange traditions transmitted through Abū Bakr as a “Musnad of Abū Bakr,” but this is not his authored compilation: it is a posthumous arrangement by ḥadīth collectors of material for which he is a transmitter. [11–12]

- **Before 634, attribution disputed:** *Collection of five hundred traditions*. A late report says that Abū Bakr gathered five hundred reports from Muḥammad and then burned them because he distrusted their accuracy. Al-Dhahabī expressly questioned the report’s soundness; if it existed, it was a destroyed private compilation or project, not an extant work. [13]

### Poetry attributed to Abu Bakr

- **Before 632, attribution disputed:** *Occasional verses*. Medieval historical, biographical, and lexicographical works attribute isolated lines and short pieces to Abū Bakr, but he was not known in the early tradition as the author of a formal dīwān. The modern *Dīwān Abī Bakr al-Ṣiddīq* is therefore a collector’s reconstruction from scattered attributions, not a book demonstrably assembled by him. [6]

- **632, attribution disputed:** *Elegies for Muḥammad*. Several laments are attributed to Abū Bakr through al-Wāqidī and later compilers. Their variant openings, late collection, and uncertain transmission make it safer to treat them as an attributed elegiac cluster rather than as securely established individual poems. [6, 14]
