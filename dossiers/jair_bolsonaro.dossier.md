# Jair Messias Bolsonaro: Research Dossier

## Scope, Method, and Evidentiary Labels

This dossier reconstructs Jair Bolsonaro’s life through **3 September 2026**. Bolsonaro is living.

Sources consulted include Brazilian presidential, congressional, electoral, judicial, prosecutorial, statistical, and archival records; foreign-government records; contemporary reporting; investigative journalism; academic studies; books; interviews; and audiovisual material. Wikipedia was used only for orientation where stronger documentation was unavailable or to identify disputed biographical details.

Labels used:

- **Documented fact:** supported by official records, authenticated documents, or direct audiovisual evidence.
- **Reported fact:** consistently reported by reputable sources but not independently established here from a primary record.
- **Allegation:** asserted by a complainant, investigator, prosecutor, journalist, or political opponent without a final judicial finding.
- **Indictment / formal charge:** an investigating authority recommended prosecution or prosecutors formally charged the subject.
- **Court finding / conviction:** adjudicated by a competent court.
- **Disputed claim:** material accounts conflict.
- **Rumor or myth:** circulated without adequate substantiation.
- **Unresolved:** proceedings or factual questions remained open at the cutoff date.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Jair Messias Bolsonaro.
- **Common name:** Jair Bolsonaro.
- **Political sobriquets:** “Capitão Bolsonaro,” “Capitão,” and, among supporters, “Mito” (“the Myth”).
- **Hostile or journalistic sobriquets:** “Trump dos Trópicos” / “Trump of the Tropics.” This is an analogy, not a self-adopted title.
- **Birth:** 21 March 1955.
- **Birthplace:** **Disputed in official and family accounts.** The presidential library formerly listed Campinas, São Paulo; the Brazilian National Archives and family accounts identify Glicério, São Paulo, with registration later occurring in Campinas.
- **Nationality:** Brazilian.
- **Religion:** Raised Roman Catholic and continued publicly to identify as Catholic. Baptized by evangelical pastor Everaldo Pereira in the Jordan River on 12 May 2016; he did not formally announce abandonment of Catholicism. His third wife, Michelle, is evangelical.
- **Professions:** Army artillery officer and parachutist; politician.
- **Military rank:** Captain, Brazilian Army reserve.
- **Principal offices:**
  - Municipal councillor, Rio de Janeiro, 1989–1991.
  - Federal deputy for Rio de Janeiro, 1991–2018, seven consecutive terms.
  - President of the Federative Republic of Brazil, 1 January 2019–31 December 2022.
- **Party at cutoff:** Partido Liberal (PL).
- **Present legal status, 3 September 2026:** Living; convicted in 2025 of five crimes connected with the post-election coup attempt and sentenced to 27 years and three months. After imprisonment in federal-police and Brasília penitentiary facilities, he was placed under temporary humanitarian house arrest in March 2026 because of broncho-pneumonia and continuing post-stabbing medical problems; that arrangement was extended in July 2026.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and ancestry

Bolsonaro was the son of:

- **Percy/Perci Geraldo Bolsonaro**, reportedly a dentist without a completed formal dentistry degree who practiced in interior São Paulo.
- **Olinda Bonturi Bolsonaro**, homemaker, who died in January 2022.

He was one of six children. Public accounts name siblings including Renato Bolsonaro and Vânia Bolsonaro; complete authoritative biographical documentation for every sibling is limited.

The family was principally of Italian descent, with ancestors from Veneto and Calabria. Bolsonaro also had German ancestry through Carl “Carlos” Hintze, reportedly born near Hamburg around 1876 and brought to Brazil in 1883.

A presidential biography claimed that his Italian ancestors arrived after the Second World War. Genealogical and immigration evidence contradicts that compression: multiple ancestors arrived substantially earlier, during Brazil’s nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century immigration waves.

His father reportedly selected “Jair” in honor of footballer Jair Rosa Pinto, whose birthday also fell on 21 March and who had played for Palmeiras, Percy’s favored team. This is a family story rather than a civil-registration fact.

### Marriages, partners, and children

Bolsonaro married three times.

1. **Rogéria Nantes Nunes Braga**
   - First wife.
   - Children: **Flávio**, **Carlos**, and **Eduardo Bolsonaro**.
   - Bolsonaro supported Rogéria’s successful campaigns for the Rio de Janeiro City Council in the 1990s.
   - Their sons became leading members of the family’s political organization:
     - Flávio: Rio state deputy and later senator.
     - Carlos: Rio de Janeiro councillor.
     - Eduardo: federal deputy for São Paulo.

2. **Ana Cristina Siqueira Valle**
   - Married Bolsonaro approximately 1997–2007.
   - Child: **Jair Renan Bolsonaro**.
   - She later entered electoral politics and became associated in reporting with investigations of the family’s former legislative offices. Allegations concerning her do not by themselves establish misconduct by Jair Bolsonaro.

3. **Michelle de Paula Firmo Reinaldo Bolsonaro**
   - Met Bolsonaro while working at the Chamber of Deputies.
   - Civil marriage reported in 2007; a public religious celebration was held in Rio de Janeiro on 21 March 2013.
   - Child: **Laura Bolsonaro**, born in 2010.
   - Bolsonaro is also stepfather to Michelle’s daughter **Letícia Aguiar**.
   - Michelle became a prominent evangelical and political surrogate, particularly after Bolsonaro left office.

Bolsonaro repeatedly described his four sons numerically in public—“01” Flávio, “02” Carlos, “03” Eduardo, and “04” Jair Renan. Laura was sometimes called “05.” The numbered vocabulary became part of the family’s political branding and hostile satire.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1955–1972

The Bolsonaro family moved among towns in São Paulo state, reportedly including Glicério, Ribeira, Jundiaí, Sete Barras, and eventually Eldorado in the Vale do Ribeira, where Jair spent much of his adolescence.

Eldorado was a small, economically limited town amid rural communities, quilombola settlements, and the region in which the military dictatorship pursued members of the Vanguarda Popular Revolucionária guerrilla organization led by Carlos Lamarca.

Bolsonaro later said that, as a teenager in 1970, he supplied soldiers with information about local geography during the hunt for Lamarca. Versions of the story differ on whether he merely drew directions, served informally as a guide, or had only a passing encounter. The broad setting is credible, but claims that the adolescent Bolsonaro played a decisive operational role are **self-created mythology or unverified family lore**.

Reported youthful work included fishing, gathering and selling passion fruit, unloading fertilizer trucks, and searching streams for gold. These accounts come principally from family members and Eldorado acquaintances.

He was raised in a socially conservative Catholic household. The Army offered stable income, status, mobility, and technical education. The dictatorship’s anti-communist political culture and his proximity to the Lamarca search became enduring components of his worldview.

## Education and Military Formation, 1973–1988

### Military education

- **1973:** Entered the Escola Preparatória de Cadetes do Exército in Campinas.
- **1974–1977:** Attended the Academia Militar das Agulhas Negras (AMAN), Resende, graduating as an artillery officer in 1977.
- Completed military parachuting training in 1977.
- Completed physical-education studies at the Army Physical Education School.
- Became a parachute jumpmaster in 1983.
- Completed autonomous-diving training through the Rio de Janeiro Fire Brigade in 1985.
- Attended the Escola de Aperfeiçoamento de Oficiais in 1987.

He served in units including the 9th Field Artillery Group in Nioaque, Mato Grosso do Sul, and in the Army Parachutist Brigade in Rio de Janeiro. Military evaluations reproduced in the later disciplinary record praised his physical vigor, courage, initiative, confidence, and combativeness while also describing financial ambition and friction with discipline.

### “O salário está baixo” and disciplinary imprisonment

In September 1986, while a captain, Bolsonaro published the signed article **“O salário está baixo”** in *Veja*. It complained that poor military pay was driving officers out of the service.

- **Documented fact:** He acknowledged authorship.
- **Military consequence:** Fifteen days’ disciplinary imprisonment for unauthorized public political expression.
- **Historical importance:** The episode made him publicly known as an advocate for military salaries and helped establish his first political constituency.

### “Beco Sem Saída” bomb allegations

In October 1987, *Veja* reported that Bolsonaro and fellow officer Fábio Passos da Silva had discussed detonating low-powered bombs at military facilities and possibly at the Guandu water-supply installation. The purported purpose was to protest military pay without causing casualties. A sketch attributed to Bolsonaro was published.

The evidentiary history is unusually contested:

- **Documented:** *Veja* journalists reported meetings and produced a sketch they attributed to Bolsonaro.
- **Army administrative finding:** A Council of Justification concluded that the officers had engaged in conduct incompatible with officership.
- **Bolsonaro’s position:** He denied planning bombings and disputed authorship or authentication of the sketch.
- **Final military adjudication:** In 1988, the Superior Military Court overturned the adverse conclusion and acquitted him, finding the evidence insufficient or technically inconclusive.
- **Correct classification:** A serious, formally investigated allegation followed by acquittal—not an established bombing conspiracy.

The episode nevertheless made conventional advancement difficult. Bolsonaro left active service in 1988 and entered politics, retaining the rank and pension status of an Army captain.

---

## Entry into Politics and Rio City Council, 1988–1991

Bolsonaro won election to Rio de Janeiro’s City Council in November 1988 on the Christian Democratic Party ticket and took office in 1989.

His base consisted disproportionately of military personnel, police, firefighters, veterans, and their families. His early political identity centered on:

- military salaries and pensions;
- defense of the armed forces;
- opposition to left-wing parties;
- public-order politics;
- resistance to civilian criticism of the 1964–1985 military regime.

His council tenure was brief because he won election to the Chamber of Deputies in 1990.

## Federal Deputy, 1991–2018

### Terms and party migrations

Bolsonaro represented Rio de Janeiro in seven consecutive legislatures. His principal party affiliations were:

- PDC, 1989–1993;
- PP/PPR, 1993–1995;
- PPB, 1995–2003;
- PTB, 2003–2005;
- brief affiliation or attempted affiliation with PFL, 2005;
- PP, 2005–2016;
- PSC, 2016–2018;
- PSL, 2018–2019;
- no party, 2019–2021;
- PL, from 2021.

The frequency of movement was common in Brazil’s fragmented party system but later sat uneasily with his anti-establishment image.

### Legislative work

Chamber records preserve hundreds of speeches and 171 proposals of various kinds attributed to him between 1991 and 2017. Only a small number became law. His parliamentary significance came less from committee craftsmanship than from media visibility and constituency advocacy.

Issues included:

- military remuneration, benefits, pensions, and amnesties;
- broader civilian access to firearms;
- lower age of criminal responsibility;
- harsher sentencing and greater legal protection for police;
- opposition to LGBTQ-rights and gender-education initiatives;
- opposition to abortion;
- printed confirmation of electronic votes;
- nationalist control of strategic resources in his earlier career;
- later, market liberalization under the influence of Paulo Guedes.

Two measures frequently credited in official summaries were provisions connected with tax benefits for information technology and authorization for phosphoethanolamine, the unproven “cancer pill.” The latter was suspended by the Supreme Court because efficacy and safety had not been established.

### Defense of the military dictatorship

Bolsonaro consistently praised the 1964 coup and military regime. He denied or minimized the regime’s systematic torture and political repression, praised Colonel Carlos Alberto Brilhante Ustra, and said in a 1999 televised interview that the dictatorship’s error had been to torture rather than kill.

During the 17 April 2016 Chamber vote to authorize Dilma Rousseff’s impeachment proceedings, he dedicated his vote to Ustra, who had commanded the DOI-CODI intelligence and repression center where Rousseff said she was tortured.

- **Documented fact:** The dedication and numerous pro-dictatorship statements are in audiovisual and congressional records.
- **Historical dispute:** Bolsonaro and supporters described 1964 as a revolution preventing communism. Brazil’s National Truth Commission and a large documentary literature identify it as a coup followed by dictatorship, torture, disappearances, censorship, and political killing.

### Statements about political violence

In 1999 he stated that Brazil would change only through civil war and that work the military regime had not completed would have to be done, including killing large numbers. In other appearances he endorsed closing Congress or institutional rupture.

These statements were usually political speech rather than operational plans. Their later relevance arose when courts examined whether his post-2021 conduct moved from authoritarian rhetoric into criminal organization and attempted coup activity.

### Maria do Rosário cases

In 2003, and again in December 2014, Bolsonaro told deputy Maria do Rosário that she did not “deserve” to be raped. He later said she was “very ugly” and “not my type.”

- **Documented fact:** Statements were recorded and repeated in an interview.
- **Civil court finding:** He was ordered to pay R$10,000 in moral damages and publish an apology; the Supreme Court declined to disturb the judgment in 2019.
- **Criminal proceeding:** The Supreme Court accepted accusations of incitement and insult in 2016. Procedural developments and his presidential immunity prevented a final criminal merits judgment during the period reviewed.
- Bolsonaro said the remarks responded to Rosário calling him a rapist and were not literal incitement.

### Hebraica speech and discrimination case

At Rio’s Hebraica club on 3 April 2017, Bolsonaro made derogatory remarks about quilombolas, using the livestock-weight measure “arroba,” and spoke disparagingly about Indigenous land, refugees, women, and LGBTQ people.

- **Documented fact:** The speech was recorded.
- **Formal accusation:** Prosecutors alleged racism.
- **Court disposition:** The Supreme Court rejected the criminal complaint by a divided vote, finding that, however crude or offensive, the statements did not satisfy the statutory offense.
- **Separate civil proceedings:** Courts and public institutions pursued other remedies over discriminatory speech in different contexts.

Thus, the statements are established; criminal racism was not established in that case.

### Relationship to militias and police networks

Bolsonaro built his Rio base among soldiers, military police, civil police, firefighters, and security-family networks. He publicly praised some police officers later accused or convicted of violent crimes.

A 2003 Chamber speech defended militias as a form of community self-defense; later he denied supporting criminal militias. His family had political and social proximity to individuals connected with Rio militia circles, including former police officers. Michelle Bolsonaro at one point received payments from Fabrício Queiroz, a former police officer and Flávio Bolsonaro aide, which Bolsonaro said were repayments of a personal loan.

- **Documented:** Political praise, employment connections, and payments exist in records and reporting.
- **Not established:** No final court judgment reviewed here found Jair Bolsonaro to be a member or commander of a militia organization.

## Ideological and Religious Development

Bolsonaro’s ideology evolved through overlapping stages rather than a formal intellectual system.

### Military corporatism and anti-communism

His earliest politics emphasized military pay and status, nationalism, the armed forces’ guardianship role, law-and-order policies, and opposition to communism. He admired the Brazilian dictatorship and Augusto Pinochet’s Chile, although he later adopted economic policies closer to Pinochet-era market liberalism than those he advocated during his early congressional career.

### Social conservatism

From the 2000s he became nationally identified with opposition to:

- same-sex marriage;
- LGBTQ-rights teaching in schools;
- abortion;
- affirmative-action policies;
- recognition and expansion of Indigenous and quilombola territories;
- gun restrictions.

The expression “kit gay,” used by Bolsonaro and allies for proposed anti-homophobia educational material, became a powerful campaign myth. The underlying educational project existed but was never distributed in the form or with the purpose Bolsonaro’s rhetoric suggested.

### Catholic–evangelical coalition

Bolsonaro remained nominally Catholic while forging strong relationships with evangelical pastors, broadcasters, and churches. Pastor Everaldo baptized him in the Jordan River in 2016. Silas Malafaia became an important ally. Michelle Bolsonaro strengthened links to evangelical women and church networks.

The campaign phrase “Brasil acima de tudo, Deus acima de todos” combined the parachutist-associated nationalist expression “Brasil acima de tudo” with explicit religious politics.

### Economic conversion

For much of his congressional career Bolsonaro favored protectionism, state control, and military-national-development themes. Preparing for the presidency, he adopted a market-liberal platform under economist **Paulo Guedes**, promising privatization, fiscal restraint, pension reform, and deregulation.

In government this produced pension reform, central-bank autonomy, privatizations or concessions, and sanitation and business-regulation changes, but the program was moderated by Congress, the pandemic, military-nationalist factions, and Bolsonaro’s alliance with the congressional *Centrão*.

### International conservative alignment

He cultivated ties with Donald Trump, Benjamin Netanyahu, Viktor Orbán, conservative evangelical Zionism, CPAC networks, and later Javier Milei. Foreign policy combined:

- early alignment with the United States under Trump;
- strong symbolic alignment with Israel;
- hostility to Nicolás Maduro’s government;
- skepticism about China among ideological advisers, tempered by trade dependence;
- continued participation in BRICS;
- ambiguous accommodation of Vladimir Putin, including a Moscow visit immediately before Russia’s 2022 invasion of Ukraine.

## Presidential Campaign of 2018

### Rise

The impeachment of Dilma Rousseff, the Lava Jato anti-corruption environment, recession, violent crime, distrust of established parties, evangelical organization, and social-media mobilization created favorable conditions.

Bolsonaro presented himself as an outsider despite nearly three decades in elected office. His campaign centered on:

- anti-corruption;
- public security and gun rights;
- opposition to the Workers’ Party;
- conservative family and religious themes;
- nostalgia for military order;
- market reforms through Paulo Guedes.

He joined the small PSL and selected retired general Hamilton Mourão as vice-presidential candidate.

### Stabbing in Juiz de Fora

On 6 September 2018, Adélio Bispo de Oliveira stabbed Bolsonaro in the abdomen during a campaign procession in Juiz de Fora, Minas Gerais.

- **Documented:** The attack was filmed and witnessed. Bolsonaro suffered life-threatening intestinal and vascular injuries and underwent emergency surgery.
- **Criminal disposition:** Bispo was found responsible but legally not punishable because of severe mental illness and confined for treatment.
- **Federal Police conclusion:** Investigations concluded that Bispo acted alone; a further inquiry examining possible assistance did not substantiate a sponsor.
- **Myth:** Supporters circulated theories implicating the Workers’ Party, left-wing organizations, Freemasons, lawyers, or foreign financiers. No credible evidence established those narratives.
- **Political effect:** Bolsonaro withdrew from conventional debates, received extensive coverage, and campaigned heavily through social media. The injury created a durable martyr-survivor image.

### Election

Bolsonaro led the first round and defeated Fernando Haddad in the second round on 28 October 2018 with **57,797,847 votes, 55.13% of valid votes**. He took office on 1 January 2019.

## Presidency, 2019–2022

## Government Formation and Networks

The administration initially combined four clusters:

1. Military officers, including Hamilton Mourão, Augusto Heleno, Luiz Eduardo Ramos, and Walter Braga Netto.
2. Market liberals led by Paulo Guedes.
3. Ideological conservatives influenced by Olavo de Carvalho and evangelical leaders.
4. Politicians and family advisers, especially Carlos, Eduardo, and Flávio Bolsonaro.

Sérgio Moro, the Lava Jato judge who had convicted Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, became justice minister. He resigned in April 2020, accusing Bolsonaro of seeking political influence over Federal Police leadership. Bolsonaro denied improper interference. The dispute ended their alliance and weakened Bolsonaro’s anti-corruption positioning.

The administration experienced high turnover in Education, Health, Foreign Affairs, Justice, and the presidential secretariat. Bolsonaro eventually relied on the *Centrão*, the bloc of transactional congressional parties he had long denounced.

### Institutional and administrative changes

The government reduced and reorganized ministries, abolished the standalone Ministry of Culture, altered responsibilities involving FUNAI and Indigenous-land demarcation, expanded military participation in civilian posts, and used decrees extensively.

Bolsonaro appointed two Supreme Court justices:

- Kassio Nunes Marques, 2020.
- André Mendonça, 2021, whom Bolsonaro had described as a “terribly evangelical” nominee.

## Economy and Material Policy

### Reforms and projects

Major enacted or advanced policies included:

- 2019 pension reform, negotiated principally through Congress;
- Economic Freedom Law;
- revised sanitation framework;
- autonomy for the Central Bank;
- privatization or capitalization of Eletrobras;
- concessions in transport infrastructure;
- sale of state assets and Petrobras subsidiaries;
- Pix, introduced by the autonomous Central Bank project during his presidency, though not Bolsonaro’s personal invention;
- emergency income transfers during COVID-19;
- Auxílio Brasil, replacing Bolsa Família;
- reductions in some federal taxes and fuel-tax measures before the 2022 election.

No significant invention or patent is attributable to Bolsonaro.

### Economic record

Official IBGE figures show:

- modest growth in 2019;
- sharp contraction in 2020 during the pandemic;
- recovery in 2021;
- GDP growth of 2.9% in 2022 under the then-current calculation.

Unemployment rose sharply during the pandemic and fell by the end of the term. Inflation, particularly food and fuel prices, became politically damaging in 2021–2022. Emergency transfers reduced immediate hardship but substantially increased fiscal expenditure.

Claims that Bolsonaro alone caused either the recession or recovery are interpretive: the pandemic, commodity prices, congressional legislation, the Central Bank, state governments, and global recovery all materially affected outcomes.

## Public Security and Firearms

Bolsonaro issued decrees and supported legislation expanding access to firearms and ammunition, particularly for collectors, sport shooters, and hunters—CACs.

The legally registered civilian arsenal expanded markedly. Supporters argued that armed citizens could defend themselves and deter crime. Critics and later research linked looser controls to diversion of weapons to organized crime.

Homicide declined during parts of his presidency, continuing a trend that began before he took office and varying significantly by state. Attribution to federal policy alone is not supported.

---

## Environment, Amazon, and Indigenous Peoples

Bolsonaro favored mining, agriculture, infrastructure, and settlement over conservation restrictions and Indigenous territorial expansion. He repeatedly criticized IBAMA enforcement and FUNAI demarcation.

During his term:

- enforcement institutions lost personnel, authority, or political support;
- environmental fines and collection weakened;
- Amazon deforestation rose to its highest annual levels since 2006;
- illegal mining expanded in Indigenous territories;
- fires in 2019 generated international conflict;
- INPE director Ricardo Galvão was dismissed after defending satellite deforestation data Bolsonaro had challenged.

The government said environmental criticism exaggerated Brazil’s responsibility, threatened sovereignty, or served foreign commercial interests. It deployed the armed forces in Amazon enforcement operations, with disputed effectiveness.

### Yanomami crisis

Illegal mining, malaria, malnutrition, violence, and health-service disruption worsened in Yanomami territory. Indigenous organizations, prosecutors, and human-rights bodies repeatedly sought federal action.

- **Documented:** Severe conditions, illegal-mining expansion, government warnings, judicial orders, and later emergency findings.
- **Allegation:** Indigenous advocates and some legal submissions described the policy as genocidal.
- **No final personal conviction:** No court judgment reviewed here convicted Bolsonaro of genocide against Indigenous peoples.
- **Open historical question:** The extent to which deliberate policy, administrative incapacity, criminal mining networks, and pandemic conditions respectively caused mortality remains the subject of investigation and scholarship.

## COVID-19 Pandemic

Brazil recorded one of the world’s largest absolute COVID-19 death totals.

Bolsonaro:

- minimized the disease as a “gripezinha” in March 2020;
- opposed lockdowns and frequently criticized governors and mayors;
- promoted chloroquine or hydroxychloroquine and “early treatment” despite lack of demonstrated benefit;
- appeared publicly without a mask and generated crowds;
- opposed vaccine mandates and expressed vaccine skepticism;
- changed health ministers during the crisis;
- personally tested positive in July 2020;
- delayed publicly acknowledging vaccination and repeatedly suggested he had not been vaccinated.

Health ministers Luiz Henrique Mandetta and Nelson Teich left after conflicts over distancing and unproven treatments. General Eduardo Pazuello led the ministry during the Manaus oxygen collapse and early vaccine procurement.

### Vaccine procurement

Evidence before the Senate CPI showed delayed or unanswered approaches from Pfizer while the government prioritized other negotiations and disputed CoronaVac politically with São Paulo governor João Doria. The government ultimately purchased and distributed vaccines through Brazil’s established public-health system.

Bolsonaro’s defense maintained that regulatory caution was required, states possessed autonomous authority, and the federal government financed vaccine procurement and emergency care.

### Senate Pandemic CPI

In October 2021, the Senate CPI approved a 1,279-page report recommending Bolsonaro’s indictment for nine offenses, including epidemic-related offenses, misuse of public funds, incitement, falsification, crimes of responsibility, and crimes against humanity.

- **Correct status:** A legislative investigative recommendation, not a criminal conviction.
- The Prosecutor-General’s Office later sought archival or narrower treatment of several referrals.
- The Covaxin prevarication inquiry was formally archived after police and prosecutors said the required criminal elements were not established.
- Legal and historical assessment of Bolsonaro’s personal responsibility for excess deaths remains disputed.

## Foreign Policy and Major Journeys

Bolsonaro made official visits to the United States, Israel, Argentina, Japan, China, India, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Russia, and other states.

Key episodes:

- Benjamin Netanyahu attended Bolsonaro’s inauguration; Bolsonaro visited Israel in March–April 2019 and visited the Western Wall with Netanyahu.
- He did not fulfill the campaign promise to move Brazil’s embassy from Tel Aviv to Jerusalem, settling for a trade office.
- He aligned closely with Donald Trump but did not obtain all the bilateral concessions promised by supporters.
- He maintained Brazilian agricultural and mineral trade with China despite ideological conflict inside his government.
- In February 2022 he visited Vladimir Putin in Moscow, saying Brazil stood in solidarity with Russia shortly before the invasion of Ukraine; Brazilian diplomacy later voted at the United Nations to condemn Russian aggression while Bolsonaro maintained a posture he called neutral.
- He spoke by telephone with Volodymyr Zelenskyy in 2020 and again during the war period.
- He refused to recognize Joe Biden’s victory promptly and was among the last G20 leaders to congratulate him.
- He stayed in Florida from 30 December 2022 into March 2023, missing Lula’s inauguration.

## The 2022 Election and Defeat

Bolsonaro ran for reelection with Braga Netto as vice-presidential candidate.

He repeatedly alleged, without producing proof of outcome-changing fraud, that electronic voting was unreliable. On 18 July 2022 he summoned foreign ambassadors to the Alvorada Palace and presented claims against the electoral system.

Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva defeated him on 30 October 2022:

- Lula: approximately 50.90%.
- Bolsonaro: approximately 49.10%.

Bolsonaro did not deliver a conventional concession speech, although his administration authorized the transition. Supporters blocked roads and established camps outside military installations demanding intervention.

The PL challenged votes from older voting machines while seeking to retain results favorable to its own legislative candidates. Electoral authorities rejected the filing and imposed a fine for bad-faith litigation.

---

## Post-Election Coup Plot and 8 January 2023

### Established sequence

Investigators and later the Supreme Court found that a network of civilian officials and military officers had, from 2021 onward, attacked the voting system, considered emergency decrees, pressured military commanders, and sought to prevent or reverse the transfer of power.

Key documentary elements included:

- draft decrees contemplating emergency intervention and arrests;
- meetings between Bolsonaro and senior commanders;
- pressure on commanders to support institutional rupture;
- organized encampments and financing;
- the “Punhal Verde e Amarelo” plan contemplating the killing of Lula, vice-president-elect Geraldo Alckmin, and Justice Alexandre de Moraes;
- the 8 January 2023 invasion and destruction of Congress, the presidential palace, and the Supreme Court.

Bolsonaro was in Florida on 8 January. Physical absence did not prevent prosecutors from alleging leadership of the broader organization.

### Bolsonaro’s defense

He denied ordering or approving a coup, denied responsibility for the 8 January riot, said drafts were discussed only within constitutional bounds, attacked Mauro Cid’s credibility, and characterized the prosecution as political persecution. Defense lawyers argued that criminal preparation had been confused with attempt and that the First Panel of the Supreme Court was not the proper original forum after he left office.

### Criminal adjudication

- **November 2024:** Federal Police formally accused Bolsonaro and 36 others.
- **18 February 2025:** Prosecutor-General Paulo Gonet charged Bolsonaro and 33 others.
- **26 March 2025:** The Supreme Court’s First Panel unanimously accepted the charges against Bolsonaro’s core group.
- **September 2025:** After trial, the First Panel convicted Bolsonaro of:
  - leading or participating in an armed criminal organization;
  - attempted violent abolition of democratic rule of law;
  - attempted coup d’état;
  - aggravated damage;
  - deterioration of protected heritage.
- **Sentence:** 27 years and three months, including prison terms and fines; the defendants were also jointly liable for collective moral damages.
- **November 2025:** The panel rejected his principal appeal.
- **Status:** This is a **court conviction**, not merely an accusation. Bolsonaro continued to deny wrongdoing and sought further relief.

---

## Electoral Disqualification

On 30 June 2023, the Superior Electoral Court found, by 5–2, that Bolsonaro abused political power and misused state media by using the July 2022 ambassadors’ meeting for electoral purposes while presenting unsupported accusations against the voting system.

He was declared ineligible for eight years calculated from the 2022 election, effectively through 2030 under that judgment.

A second TSE judgment in October 2023 also found abuse connected with use of the Independence Day bicentenary celebrations for campaigning.

These are electoral-law findings, distinct from the later criminal conviction.

---

## Imprisonment and Present Status, 2025–2026

In July 2025, while the coup case was pending, the Supreme Court imposed restrictions including an ankle monitor, nighttime confinement, prohibitions on certain contacts and foreign diplomatic approaches, and limitations on social media.

On 4 August 2025, Justice Alexandre de Moraes ordered house arrest after finding that Bolsonaro had circumvented restrictions by producing material disseminated through allies’ accounts.

After his conviction became executable in November 2025, Bolsonaro began serving the 27-year sentence in federal custody. He was initially held in an individual room at Federal Police facilities in Brasília and in January 2026 transferred to a state-of-major room in the PM-DF facility at the Papuda penitentiary complex.

A first humanitarian-house-arrest request was denied in March 2026, with the court citing intensive medical care available in custody. After hospitalization for broncho-pneumonia beginning 13 March, Moraes granted 90 days’ humanitarian house arrest from discharge. Bolsonaro left hospital for his home in Brasília’s Jardim Botânico neighborhood on 27 March. The arrangement included electronic monitoring, limits on communications and visitors, and police surveillance.

It was extended in July 2026 on health grounds. As of 3 September 2026, he remained alive, convicted, politically ineligible, and serving his sentence at home subject to judicial conditions.

## Health, Injuries, and Medical History

### 2018 stabbing and sequelae

The Juiz de Fora stabbing damaged Bolsonaro’s small and large intestines and caused major blood loss. He underwent multiple operations, including:

- emergency abdominal surgery in Juiz de Fora;
- later reconstruction and colostomy reversal;
- hernia surgery;
- repeated treatment for intestinal obstruction or adhesions.

Long-term consequences included abdominal pain, digestive difficulty, recurrent obstruction, hiccups, hospitalizations, and risk associated with further surgery.

### COVID-19

Bolsonaro announced a positive SARS-CoV-2 test in July 2020. He publicly took hydroxychloroquine. No evidence establishes that the drug caused his recovery.

### Later episodes

He was hospitalized repeatedly for abdominal obstruction, pain, and related complications. In 2025 he underwent a lengthy operation connected with intestinal adhesions and abdominal-wall reconstruction. In March 2026 he developed broncho-pneumonia while imprisoned, leading to hospitalization and humanitarian house arrest.

No reliable public evidence establishes addiction, a diagnosed psychiatric disorder, or a psychological breakdown. Political characterizations of him as insane, psychopathic, or cognitively impaired are not medical findings.

## Finances, Property, and Institutional Interests

### Income and pensions

Bolsonaro’s documented income streams have included:

- military reserve remuneration;
- legislative salary and benefits;
- presidential salary;
- party remuneration after leaving office;
- possible pensions or benefits governed by applicable law.

He did not build or manage a major private company and has no known patent portfolio.

### Property holdings

Investigative reporting by UOL examined 107 property transactions involving Bolsonaro, his children, former wives, mother, and siblings over roughly three decades. It reported that at least 51 were described in deeds or related records as paid wholly or partly in cash, with inflation-adjusted values exceeding R$25 million.

Important qualification:

- “Moeda corrente” in a deed can mean payment in lawful Brazilian currency and does not always prove physical banknotes.
- UOL published transaction-by-transaction evidence arguing that additional wording and records did show cash use in many cases.
- Cash purchase is not itself illegal.
- The reporting raised questions about source of funds and compatibility with declared income but did not itself prove money laundering.
- Bolsonaro denied wrongdoing and said the reporting manipulated terminology.

### Pix fundraising

After electoral penalties and investigations, supporters sent Bolsonaro large sums through Pix. These transfers were politically presented as voluntary assistance with fines and legal costs. Their existence is documented; characterizations as laundering or unlawful enrichment require separate proof.

### Corporate structures

The family’s central enterprise was political rather than a unified documented corporation: elected offices, party structures, staff appointments, social-media operations, campaign committees, and real estate held by different relatives.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Adjudicated Cases

## 1. Military bomb plan, 1987–1988

- **Status:** Formally investigated allegation; adverse Army administrative finding; final Superior Military Court acquittal for insufficient evidence.
- **Not appropriate to state as fact:** That Bolsonaro conclusively planned to bomb barracks.

## 2. Pro-dictatorship and torture rhetoric

- **Status:** Documented speech.
- **No ambiguity about occurrence:** Praise for Ustra, Pinochet, military intervention, and political killing is recorded.
- **Dispute:** Supporters treat it as anti-communist polemic; critics treat it as endorsement of state terror.

## 3. Maria do Rosário

- **Status:** Statements documented; civil liability established; criminal accusation accepted but not finally adjudicated on the merits in the cited record.

## 4. Discriminatory speech

Bolsonaro made documented statements demeaning women, Black and quilombola communities, Indigenous people, refugees, and LGBTQ people.

- Some generated civil findings or political condemnation.
- The 2017 criminal racism complaint was rejected.
- Describing every offensive statement as a criminal conviction would be inaccurate.

## 5. “Rachadinha” and Fabrício Queiroz

The Rio prosecutor investigated an alleged scheme in Flávio Bolsonaro’s state-deputy office in which employees returned portions of salaries. Fabrício Queiroz was described as the financial operator.

Relevant links to Jair Bolsonaro included:

- longstanding friendship with Queiroz;
- Queiroz’s employment with Flávio;
- Queiroz family members’ employment in Bolsonaro-family offices;
- payments from Queiroz to Michelle, explained by Bolsonaro as repayment of a loan;
- recorded or reported efforts by political allies concerning the investigation.

- **Status for Flávio/Queiroz:** Serious criminal allegations subject to extensive procedural litigation, including annulment or exclusion of evidence.
- **Status for Jair:** No final conviction establishing that he directed the Flávio-office scheme.
- **Broader allegation:** Investigative journalist Juliana Dal Piva argued that analogous salary-return practices existed across earlier family offices. This remains an investigative claim rather than a final personal conviction of Jair Bolsonaro.

## 6. PSL campaign-candidate funding

The 2018 PSL campaign faced allegations that nominal female candidates were used to divert public campaign funds.

- Bolsonaro was not personally convicted of organizing the schemes.
- Party president Luciano Bivar and other regional organizers became focal points.
- The controversy contributed to Bolsonaro’s break with PSL.

## 7. Federal Police interference allegation

Sérgio Moro alleged in April 2020 that Bolsonaro sought to change Federal Police command to obtain information or protect family interests.

- **Documented:** Moro’s resignation statement; messages and a cabinet-meeting recording; Bolsonaro’s desire to change leadership.
- **Bolsonaro’s defense:** He claimed lawful presidential authority and denied corrupt purpose.
- **Disposition:** The investigation did not yield a final criminal conviction of Bolsonaro in the sources reviewed.

## 8. Covaxin

Deputy Luís Miranda said he warned Bolsonaro about irregularities in a proposed vaccine contract, prompting an investigation into possible prevarication.

- **Disposition:** Federal Police reported insufficient evidence of the offense; the Supreme Court archived the inquiry at the Prosecutor-General’s request.
- **Correct classification:** Investigated and archived, not proved corruption.

## 9. Pandemic conduct

- **Status:** Extensive documentary record and a Senate CPI recommendation for indictment.
- **No criminal conviction identified:** The CPI’s recommendations did not themselves establish guilt.
- **Separate fact:** Courts repeatedly required federal measures to protect Indigenous groups and address public-health failures.

## 10. Vaccination-card records

Federal Police alleged that false COVID-vaccination entries were inserted for Bolsonaro and others, potentially to facilitate travel.

- **March 2024:** Police recommended indictment.
- **Bolsonaro’s position:** He denied ordering falsification and continued to imply he had not been vaccinated.
- **March 2025 disposition:** The Supreme Court archived the investigation at the PGR’s request because Mauro Cid’s cooperating testimony lacked sufficient independent corroboration for prosecution.
- **Legal qualification:** Archival could permit reopening if new evidence emerged; there was no conviction.

## 11. Saudi jewelry and official gifts

High-value jewelry sets from Saudi Arabia and other Gulf sources entered or were attempted to be brought into Brazil. Some items were treated as personal gifts, transported abroad, offered for sale or auction, and later repurchased or returned after scrutiny.

- Customs intercepted a set reportedly intended for Michelle Bolsonaro.
- The Federal Police concluded in 2024 that Bolsonaro and associates had participated in appropriation, sale, and conversion of official gifts and recommended indictment for offenses including embezzlement, money laundering, and criminal association.
- Bolsonaro denied criminal intent and argued that rules separating personal from public presidential gifts were unclear.
- In early 2026, PGR sought archival on the ground that Brazilian law had not clearly defined ownership of such gifts and that criminal punishment would violate legality.
- In March 2026 Moraes sought a further PGR opinion before deciding.
- **Status at cutoff:** Police indictment, no conviction; final archival decision not established in the consulted record.

## 12. “Abin paralela”

Federal Police investigated alleged unauthorized use of the FirstMile geolocation system and ABIN structures to monitor judges, legislators, journalists, tax officials, and political opponents and to assist Bolsonaro-family interests.

- Investigators found evidence that a clandestine political-intelligence structure operated during Bolsonaro’s administration.
- Bolsonaro was not separately indicted because authorities considered overlapping organizational conduct already included in the coup proceeding.
- In July 2026 the investigation as to Bolsonaro was archived because the overlapping conduct had already been addressed in the coup case, not because the FirstMile activity was found never to have existed.
- Cases involving Carlos Bolsonaro and others were sent to the federal courts or continued separately.

## 13. Electronic-voting attacks

- **Documented:** Repeated unsupported claims; ambassadors’ presentation; attacks on the TSE.
- **Electoral court finding:** Abuse of political power and misuse of media; ineligibility.
- **Criminal relevance:** Treated in the 2025 coup judgment as part of the organization’s preparation and delegitimization strategy.

## 14. Coup attempt

- **Status:** Finality sufficient for sentence execution after conviction and appeal rejection in 2025.
- **Not merely allegation:** Bolsonaro was convicted after trial.
- **Continuing dispute:** He and supporters reject legitimacy, jurisdiction, evidence evaluation, and proportionality of sentence.

## 15. International Criminal Court submissions

Indigenous organizations, lawyers, and activists submitted communications to the ICC alleging crimes against humanity or genocide connected with Amazon and pandemic policies.

- **Status:** Communications or requests for investigation, not ICC indictments or convictions of Bolsonaro in the sources reviewed.

## 16. Political violence and January 8

Bolsonaro condemned property destruction after January 8 but had spent years depicting courts and elections as illegitimate.

- **Court finding:** The 2025 judgment treated the riot as part of the criminal organization’s course of conduct.
- **Disputed:** The exact chain of command between Bolsonaro, encampments, financiers, operational planners, and individual rioters.

## Successes and Failures

### Documented political successes

- Built a durable national movement from a marginal congressional career.
- Won seven consecutive federal-deputy terms.
- Received 464,565 votes in Rio in 2014.
- Won the 2018 presidency with nearly 58 million votes.
- Consolidated evangelical, police, military-family, agribusiness, anti-PT, and gun-rights constituencies.
- Obtained pension reform and several deregulatory and infrastructure measures.
- Preserved a large personal following after defeat, legal disqualification, and conviction.
- Helped elect sons and numerous ideological allies.
- Shifted Brazil’s political vocabulary and party competition sharply rightward.

### Documented failures or reversals

- Limited legislative production during 27 years in Congress.
- High ministerial turnover and internal factional conflict.
- Failed to create the proposed Aliança pelo Brasil party.
- Broke with PSL and governed without a party for two years.
- Failed to move Brazil’s embassy to Jerusalem.
- Failed to win reelection—the first incumbent Brazilian president to lose a reelection contest under the post-1997 reelection system.
- Failed to obtain military support sufficient to prevent Lula’s inauguration.
- Electoral disqualification through 2030.
- Criminal conviction and imprisonment.
- Several signature decrees, especially firearms measures, were later reversed or judicially limited.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The Myth” / “Mito”

**Story:** Bolsonaro was a uniquely incorruptible outsider who alone could rescue Brazil.

**Origin and circulation:** Grass-roots conservative internet culture, pro-gun groups, military and police networks, campaign rallies, and Bolsonaro-family social media before and during 2018.

**Evidence for:** He was not implicated as a defendant in the central Petrobras/Lava Jato bribery cases that destroyed several rival parties; he communicated directly and rejected conventional elite etiquette.

**Evidence against:** He had served in elected office since 1989, changed parties repeatedly, joined the *Centrão*, and became subject to substantial financial, electoral, and criminal investigations.

**Effect:** “Mito” became a chant, identity marker, marketing device, and quasi-messianic political symbol.

### The teenage hunter of Carlos Lamarca

**Story:** Young Bolsonaro personally helped the Army locate the guerrilla leader.

**Evidence:** Bolsonaro lived in the region and may have given soldiers geographic information.

**Against exaggeration:** No strong record establishes that he played a decisive intelligence or combat role.

**Effect:** Retrospectively linked his childhood to anti-communism and the armed forces.

### The stabbing was staged

**Story:** Bolsonaro’s 2018 stabbing was fake, theatrically arranged, or medically fabricated.

**Evidence against:** Multiple videos, eyewitnesses, immediate surgery, medical records, continuing operations, and judicial proceedings establish a real, grave wound.

**Status:** False conspiracy narrative.

### The stabbing was ordered by the Workers’ Party or a hidden network

**Story:** Adélio Bispo acted for leftists, organized crime, lawyers, or international sponsors.

**Evidence for:** Bispo had once been a PSOL member; questions arose about legal representation and his movements.

**Evidence against:** Federal Police investigations found that he acted alone and did not substantiate sponsorship.

**Effect:** Reinforced martyrdom and distrust of institutions among supporters.

### “Kit gay”

**Story:** The Workers’ Party planned to distribute a “gay kit” sexualizing children.

**Underlying fact:** Educational materials against homophobic bullying were prepared.

**Evidence against campaign version:** The material was not distributed as described, and the sensational label distorted its content and status.

**Effect:** One of the most effective moral-panic narratives of the 2018 campaign.

### Electronic machines secretly stole the 2022 election

**Story:** Voting machines changed or fabricated enough votes to defeat Bolsonaro.

**Evidence:** Bolsonaro and allies offered anomalies, statistical suspicions, and criticism of source-code access.

**Evidence against:** Audits, military inspection, electoral-court testing, parallel records, and the PL’s submission did not demonstrate outcome-changing fraud. Bolsonaro and allied legislators accepted victories produced by the same machines.

**Legal effect:** The unsupported allegations produced electoral sanctions and became evidence in the coup case.

### “He never attempted a coup because no tanks entered the streets”

**Story:** Discussions and draft decrees were constitutionally protected contingency planning, and the failure to deploy forces means there was no attempt.

**Evidence for:** Some commanders refused participation; not all proposed acts were implemented; Bolsonaro was outside Brazil on January 8.

**Evidence against:** Testimony, drafts, meetings, pressure on commanders, operational plans, and coordinated institutional attacks were accepted by the Supreme Court as acts exceeding preparation.

**Status:** Defense theory rejected by the trial court but still central to political propaganda and appeals.

### Communist takeover narrative

**Story:** Lula, the Supreme Court, media, universities, China, and international organizations formed a communist project to destroy Brazil.

**Evidence:** Brazilian left parties maintained international relationships and state-development policies.

**Evidence against broader conspiracy:** No credible documentary evidence supports a unified communist command over the institutions identified.

**Effect:** Supplied an overarching explanation for elections, prosecutions, public-health measures, and environmental enforcement.

### Bolsonaro as divine instrument

Evangelical and charismatic supporters described the survival of the stabbing and election victory as providential. Michelle and pastors used biblical language about spiritual warfare, deliverance, and national destiny.

This is religious-political interpretation, not empirically testable biography. It materially shaped mobilization.

### Prisoner and martyr mythology

After disqualification and conviction, supporters portrayed Bolsonaro as Brazil’s Nelson Mandela, a political prisoner, or a victim of judicial dictatorship. Opponents compared him to failed coup leaders or fascist strongmen.

- **Documented reality:** He was prosecuted and convicted by the Supreme Court under Brazilian criminal law.
- **Interpretive dispute:** Whether procedure was fair, jurisdiction proper, and sentence proportionate remains debated domestically and internationally.
- Analogies to Mandela, Hitler, Mussolini, Pinochet, or Trump are rhetorical comparisons, not evidence of identity or direct equivalence.

## Later Reputation and Legacy

Bolsonaro’s historical position remains unsettled because he is living and politically influential.

Documentable components of his legacy include:

- creation or consolidation of **bolsonarismo** as a movement larger than his formal party;
- normalization of explicit praise for the military dictatorship in national electoral politics;
- durable alliance among evangelical activism, police and military constituencies, agribusiness, digital influencers, gun-rights groups, and anti-left voters;
- transformation of social media into a central channel of presidential rule;
- intensified political role of his sons and spouse;
- weakened environmental enforcement and increased Amazon deforestation during his term;
- one of the world’s most controversial national COVID-19 responses;
- institutional confrontation with the Supreme Court and electoral judiciary;
- the first failed presidential reelection by an incumbent under Brazil’s present reelection system;
- a judicially established role in the 2022–2023 attempted coup;
- continuing debate about Supreme Court power, speech regulation, military accountability, and procedural protections.

Supporters remember lower crime during parts of his term, firearms liberalization, Pix-era modernization, emergency pandemic payments, social conservatism, opposition to the Workers’ Party, and resistance to elite institutions.

Opponents emphasize pandemic deaths, environmental degradation, discriminatory rhetoric, corruption questions surrounding the family, militarization of government, electoral disinformation, and the coup conviction.

Both reputational blocs produce selective mythology. The archival record requires separating genuine policy achievements, external economic conditions, congressional authorship, administrative failures, allegations, archived cases, electoral findings, and criminal convictions.
