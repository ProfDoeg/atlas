## Works by Odysseus

### Material obra

- **Before the Trojan War; no fixed date:** *The rooted olive-wood marriage bed and its chamber*. Odysseus says that he built the room around a living olive tree, fashioned its trunk into a bedpost, and completed the bed with an inlaid frame and hide webbing; because architecture, living tree, furniture, and marriage-token are inseparable, it approaches a domestic *Gesamtkunstwerk*. Its existence depends entirely on Odysseus’s own account, although Penelope accepts his technical knowledge as proof of identity ([*Odyssey* 23.181–230](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D23%3Acard%3D181); [Grethlein, “Odysseus and His Bed”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/classical-quarterly/article/abs/odysseus-and-his-bed-from-significant-objects-to-thing-theory-in-homer/CCC22E64A7158507DDFE91BA50827D58)).

- **During the return from Troy; no fixed date:** *The olive-wood stake used against Polyphemus*. Odysseus selected and cut down the Cyclops’s club, while his companions helped trim, sharpen, and fire-harden it: this is therefore a collectively fabricated weapon made under his direction, not securely his sole obra ([*Odyssey* 9.318–335](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D9%3Acard%3D307)).

- **Late in the return journey, traditionally c. 1174 BC:** *The vessel built on Calypso’s island*. Odysseus felled and squared timber, bored and joined the planks, fitted decking and bulwarks, and made the sail and steering gear from materials and tools supplied by Calypso. Usually called a “raft,” it is described with enough shipbuilding detail to resist that simple category; Poseidon destroyed it soon after launch ([*Odyssey* 5.228–261, 282–387](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D5%3Acard%3D228); Mark, “Odyssey 5.234–53 and Homeric Ship Construction”](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.2307/505490); Dougherty, *The Raft of Odysseus*](https://academic.oup.com/book/47126/chapter-abstract/422337402)).

### Protocols and strategic projects

- **Before the Trojan War; no fixed date:** *The oath of Helen’s suitors*. In the later mythographic tradition, Odysseus proposed the protocol by which every suitor would swear to defend Helen’s eventual husband; Tyndareus imposed it. This is not a written work and arguably not Odysseus’s obra at all, but an attributed institutional design absent from Homer and preserved chiefly in later sources ([Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Library* 3.10.9](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Apollod.%203.10.9); Sommerstein and Torrance, *Oaths and Swearing in Ancient Greece*, ch. 3](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/gdc/gdcebookspublic/20/20/71/88/90/2020718890/2020718890.pdf)).

- **Final year of the Trojan War, traditionally c. 1184 BC:** *The Wooden Horse project*. One tradition makes Odysseus inventor or designer of the stratagem but assigns the actual construction to Epeius; another credits Athena with inspiring Epeius. It must therefore be catalogued as a disputed strategic project rather than as a sculpture demonstrably made by Odysseus ([*Odyssey* 8.492–520](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D8%3Acard%3D482); [Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Epitome* 5.14](https://beta.perseus.tufts.edu/urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0548.tlg002.perseus-eng2:5/); [*The Cambridge Guide to Homer*, “Trojan Horse,” pp. 233–34](https://eclass.upatras.gr/modules/document/file.php/LIT2211/%CE%8C%CE%BC%CE%B7%CF%81%CE%BF%CF%82,%20%CE%9F%CE%B4.%20%CE%99%CE%A7-%CE%A7%CE%99%CE%99/2%20The%20Cambridge%20Guide%20to%20Homer-Cambridge%20University%20Press%20(2020).pdf)).

### Narrative manifestations

- **During the return from Troy; no fixed date:** *The Apologoi*. The first-person account of the Cicones, Lotus-Eaters, Cyclopes, Aeolus, Laestrygonians, Circe, the dead, Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, and the cattle of Helios is an oral narrative performed by Odysseus for the Phaeacians, not a book he wrote. It resists attribution as an independent work because it is simultaneously Odysseus’s embedded performance and part of the Homeric poet’s *Odyssey*; scholarship also disputes how far audiences should trust its otherwise uncorroborated marvels ([*Odyssey* 9–12](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D9); [de Jong, *Narratology and Classics*, “The First Person”](https://academic.oup.com/book/2779/chapter-abstract/143298307)).

- **Near the end of the return, traditionally c. 1174 BC:** *The Ithacan false tales*. This is a body of improvised oral pseudobiographies told in disguise: principally the stories addressed to Athena, Eumaeus, Antinous, Penelope, and Laertes. They have no ancient collective title or fixed independent text; the “Cretan tales” overlap but do not perfectly coincide with the group, and scholarship treats them as deliberate recombinations of alternative epic traditions rather than simple lies ([*Odyssey* 13.256–286](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D13%3Acard%3D256), [14.192–359](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D14%3Acard%3D185), [17.415–444](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D17%3Acard%3D401), [19.165–307](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D19%3Acard%3D165), [24.303–314](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D24%3Acard%3D303); [Emanuel, “Cretan Lie and Historical Truth”](https://www-current.chs.harvard.edu/jeffrey-p-emanuel-cretan-lie-and-historical-truth-examining-odysseus-raid-on-egypt-in-its-late-bronze-age-context/)).
