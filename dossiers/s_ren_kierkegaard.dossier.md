# Søren Kierkegaard: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 24 August 2026  
**Scope:** Reconstructable life history, writings, documentary controversies, myths, and reception  
**Method note:** Dates and titles follow the critical edition *Søren Kierkegaards Skrifter* (SKS), the Søren Kierkegaard Research Centre, Kierkegaard’s surviving journals and correspondence, and modern specialist scholarship. Later recollections are identified as such. Assertions inferred from Kierkegaard’s pseudonymous fiction are not treated as direct autobiography.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Søren Aabye Kierkegaard. “Aabye” is also modernized as “Åby” in occasional references, but Kierkegaard used the older spelling.
- **Born:** 5 May 1813, Copenhagen, Denmark, at the family house on Nytorv.
- **Baptized:** 3 June 1813, Church of the Holy Ghost (*Helligåndskirken*), Copenhagen.
- **Died:** 11 November 1855, Frederiks Hospital, Copenhagen, aged forty-two.
- **Burial:** 18 November 1855, Assistens Cemetery, Copenhagen, after a service at the Church of Our Lady. His grave is in the Kierkegaard family plot.
- **Nationality and legal status:** Danish subject of the absolute monarchy and, after the Constitution of 1849, the constitutional Danish state.
- **Languages:** Danish; reading and working command of German, Latin, Greek, and Hebrew; some French.
- **Occupations:** Independent author, theologian, philosophical writer, cultural critic, newspaper polemicist. He completed the theological degree but never accepted ordination, a pastorate, a university chair, or salaried public office.
- **Common designations:** philosopher, theologian, religious author, poet or “religious poet,” psychologist, social critic; retrospectively “father of existentialism” or “first existentialist.” He did not use the twentieth-century movement label *existentialist*.
- **Physical descriptions:** Contemporaries described a slight, angular man with an uneven gait, distinctive clothes, intense eyes, quick speech, and prodigious conversational energy. Corsair caricatures exaggerated his trouser legs, coat, umbrella, hat, thin legs, and spinal asymmetry; these are satirical evidence, not neutral portraits.
- **Marital/parental status:** Never married; no documented children. Engaged to Regine Olsen from September 1840 until October 1841.

### Authorial names and personae

Kierkegaard’s pseudonyms were constructed standpoints, not ordinary concealment. They must not automatically be quoted as Kierkegaard’s own settled opinion.

- **Victor Eremita** — editor of *Either/Or*.
- **A** — papers of the aesthete in *Either/Or*; **William Afham** later narrates the banquet in *Stages on Life’s Way*.
- **Judge William / Wilhelm** — ethical-marital correspondent in *Either/Or* and *Stages*.
- **Johannes de silentio** — author of *Fear and Trembling*.
- **Constantin Constantius** — author of *Repetition*.
- **Vigilius Haufniensis** (“the watchman of Copenhagen”) — author of *The Concept of Anxiety*.
- **Nicolaus Notabene** — author of *Prefaces*.
- **Hilarius Bookbinder** — editor of *Stages on Life’s Way*.
- **Frater Taciturnus** — author of the “Guilty?/Not Guilty?” section of *Stages*.
- **Johannes Climacus** — author of *Philosophical Fragments* and *Concluding Unscientific Postscript*; also of the unpublished *Johannes Climacus, or De omnibus dubitandum est*.
- **Anti-Climacus** — stated by Kierkegaard to represent a Christian standpoint higher than his own; author of *The Sickness unto Death* and *Practice in Christianity*, with Kierkegaard named as editor.
- **Inter et Inter** — author of *The Crisis and a Crisis in the Life of an Actress*.
- **H.H.** — author of *Two Ethical-Religious Essays*.
- Additional signatures and masks include **A**, **B**, **A.F.**, **Procul**, and anonymous or unsigned newspaper pieces. “S. Kierkegaard” appeared on his signed religious discourses.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal line

Kierkegaard’s father, **Michael Pedersen Kierkegaard** (1756–1838), was born in Sædding in west Jutland. The family name referred to a church farm (*kirkegård*, literally church-yard/farm; today also “cemetery”), not originally to aristocratic ancestry. As a poor shepherd boy Michael went to Copenhagen, entered his maternal uncle Niels Andersen Seding’s dry-goods concern, became a merchant, and accumulated a substantial fortune in hosiery and colonial/dry goods. He retired relatively early and invested conservatively, including in government securities and property. His financial caution reportedly preserved much of the fortune through Denmark’s 1813 state bankruptcy.

Michael first married **Kirstine Nielsdatter Røyen** (1757–1796). The marriage was childless. After her death he married his household servant **Ane Sørensdatter Lund** (1768–1834), also from Jutland. Their first child arrived less than five months after the marriage. The chronology strongly indicates premarital conception; claims of coercion or “rape” go beyond the surviving evidence.

Michael combined commercial intelligence, argumentative power, patriarchal control, Lutheran orthodoxy, and Moravian/Pietist influence. Søren later represented him as both intellectual benefactor and source of inherited melancholy, guilt, and an impossibly demanding Christianity.

### Mother and siblings

Ane Sørensdatter Lund is far less visible in Kierkegaard’s papers than his father. Her relative absence has generated speculation, but it is not proof of emotional indifference. She bore seven children:

1. **Maren Kirstine** (1797–1822).
2. **Nicoline Christine** (1799–1832), married Peter Wilhelm Lund.
3. **Petrea Severine** (1801–1834), married Henrik Ferdinand Lund.
4. **Peter Christian Kierkegaard** (1805–1888), theologian, pastor, later bishop of Aalborg and briefly minister for ecclesiastical and educational affairs.
5. **Søren Michael** (1807–1819).
6. **Niels Andreas** (1809–1833), who emigrated or travelled to North America and died at Paterson, New Jersey.
7. **Søren Aabye** (1813–1855).

By 1834 only Peter Christian and Søren survived among the seven children. The repeated deaths, and Michael’s belief that divine punishment hung over the household, shaped the “family curse” narrative. Peter and Søren were affectionate, competitive, and frequently estranged. Peter became an ordained churchman within the institution Søren attacked. He nonetheless handled parts of Søren’s estate and published *The Point of View for My Work as an Author* in 1859.

### Nephews, estate, and archive

Kierkegaard’s nephew **Henrik Lund** publicly protested at the funeral that burial by the established church violated the dead man’s position. The protest caused a disturbance and legal proceedings resulting in a fine. Family members preserved and disputed access to the papers. The Nachlass eventually entered the Royal Library; the Søren Kierkegaard Archive contains manuscripts, drafts, proofs, journals, notebooks, loose sheets, letters, biographical documents, presentation copies, and books from his library.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1813–1830

Kierkegaard grew up chiefly at Nytorv 2 in central Copenhagen in a wealthy but emotionally severe household. Michael staged philosophical walks indoors, inviting the boy to imagine streets and landscapes, and trained him in dialectical argument. Søren later said that he had been a child disguised as an old man and that his father’s melancholy had deprived him of ordinary childhood.

He entered the **School of Civic Virtue** (*Borgerdydskolen*) in 1821. School recollections portray him as physically vulnerable, verbally sharp, observant, and capable of retaliating through wit. He acquired the nickname **“the fork”** in some later accounts because of his ability to stick opponents with remarks. The documentary status of colorful school anecdotes varies; many derive from later memoirs collected in Bruce Kirmmse’s *Encounters with Kierkegaard*.

The household was religiously serious. Bishop **Jacob Peter Mynster**, once pastor to the family and later primate of the Danish church, was a trusted authority. Michael’s contacts with Moravian circles intensified an inward, penitential form of Christianity. Søren’s later attack on “Christendom” arose from within, not outside, Lutheran belief.

## Education and Formation, 1830–1841

### University years

- **30 October 1830:** matriculated at the University of Copenhagen to study theology, the expected route to clerical employment.
- Studied theology, philosophy, classics, literature, history, and aesthetics. Important teachers included philosopher **Frederik Christian Sibbern**, theologian **Hans Lassen Martensen**, and classicist **Poul Martin Møller**. Møller became the most personally admired academic mentor; Kierkegaard dedicated *The Concept of Anxiety* to him after Møller’s death.
- Absorbed Socrates and Plato, Aristotle, Augustine, Luther, Pascal, Hamann, Lessing, Goethe, Romantic literature, and German idealism. Hegel was simultaneously indispensable and adversarial. Danish Hegelianism reached him through Martensen and **Johan Ludvig Heiberg**.
- He attended Copenhagen theater and opera obsessively. Mozart’s *Don Giovanni*, especially the sensuous immediacy of music and the Don Juan figure, became central to *Either/Or*. Actress **Johanne Luise Heiberg** became the subject of his later analysis of theatrical repetition and maturity.

His student career lasted unusually long. He accumulated debts, dressed fashionably, frequented cafés and theaters, and experimented with a dandified persona. It is misleading to describe the entire period as dissipated: his journals show disciplined reading alongside restlessness, grief, and vocational uncertainty.

### Journals and the Gilleleje episode

The first surviving journal entry dates from 15 April 1834. In summer 1835 he stayed at **Gilleleje** on Zealand’s north coast. The famous entry about finding “the idea for which I am willing to live and die” belongs to this search for vocation; it is not a secular manifesto detached from his theological problem.

The so-called **“great earthquake”** in his inner life is conventionally placed around 1835. An undated journal passage describes learning something about his father that transformed old age from blessing into curse. Kierkegaard never plainly identifies the revelation. Later reconstructions propose Michael’s boyhood curse against God, the premarital conception of the first child, sexual guilt, infidelity, or—as Joakim Garff speculatively proposed—syphilis. No surviving document establishes the syphilis theory.

### Deaths and reconciliation with his father

His mother died 31 July 1834. Several siblings had already died; Petrea died later in 1834. The concentration of deaths fed Michael’s prediction that none of his children would survive Christ’s age of thirty-three. Søren temporarily pulled away from his father, then reconciled with him before Michael died on 9 August 1838.

The father’s death gave Søren both a religious obligation and financial independence. He returned seriously to theology, passed the theological examination on **3 July 1840**, and completed seminary preaching requirements. He preached a trial sermon but did not pursue ordination.

### First publications and dissertation

Kierkegaard published early newspaper pieces from 1834 onward, often on politics, journalism, women, and student culture. *From the Papers of One Still Living* (1838), his critique of Hans Christian Andersen’s novelistic treatment of genius, was his first book.

His dissertation, *Om Begrebet Ironi med stadigt Hensyn til Socrates* (*On the Concept of Irony with Continual Reference to Socrates*), was accepted in 1841. He defended it publicly on **29 September 1841** and received the magister degree. The dissertation analyzed Xenophon, Plato, Aristophanes, Socratic irony, Hegel, and Romantic irony; it already joined scholarship to an indirect, ironic authorial practice.

## Regine Olsen and the Broken Engagement, 1837–1841

Kierkegaard met **Regine Frederikke Olsen** (1822–1904), daughter of civil servant Terkild Olsen, in May 1837 in the Rørdam household. He courted her after a prolonged period of observation and friendship. On 8 September 1840 he declared himself; her father consented, and the engagement became formal on 10 September.

Kierkegaard later claimed that almost immediately he recognized a marriage would be impossible. His explanations vary:

- lifelong “melancholy” and fear that intimate disclosure would burden her;
- a hidden paternal/family secret he felt unable to reveal;
- an authorial or religious vocation incompatible, in his mind, with domestic responsibility;
- sexual anxiety, physical frailty, or a conviction that he would die young;
- a wish to preserve Regine as a poetic and religious catalyst.

These are documented self-interpretations, not mutually exclusive diagnoses. Claims that impotence, homosexuality, congenital deformity, or venereal disease caused the break remain speculative.

On **11 August 1841** he returned the ring with a letter. Regine resisted the dissolution and her father pleaded with him. Kierkegaard deliberately cultivated the appearance of a callous seducer so she would release him. On **11 October 1841** the engagement was definitively broken. The episode caused real distress and Copenhagen gossip. Regine later married **Johan Frederik Schlegel** in 1847 and accompanied him to the Danish West Indies in 1855. Kierkegaard continued to monitor chance encounters with her, addressed journals and unpublished papers to her as his “reader,” and in 1849 unsuccessfully sought Schlegel’s permission to communicate.

Regine’s later dictated recollections are indispensable but retrospective and shaped by decades of public interpretation. She preserved her own agency: her subsequent marriage was not merely an afterlife of Kierkegaard’s story.

## Chronological Life History and Authorship

### First Berlin journey and Schelling’s lecture, 1841–1842

Kierkegaard left Copenhagen on **25 October 1841** and remained in Berlin until **6 March 1842**. He attended **Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling’s** lectures on the philosophy of revelation at the University of Berlin. The course was a European intellectual event organized in part as a conservative response to Hegel’s legacy.

The audience included, at various sessions, **Mikhail Bakunin, Friedrich Engels, Jacob Burckhardt, Alexander von Humboldt, Philip Schaff**, and others. This establishes co-presence in the same celebrated lecture milieu, not a documented friendship or conversation between Kierkegaard and Bakunin. Bakunin and Kierkegaard both began interested and became disappointed, though from different intellectual and political positions. Kierkegaard’s surviving lecture notes and letters chart his movement from excitement at Schelling’s distinction between essence and actuality to impatience with mythology and prolixity.

In Berlin he lived near Gendarmenmarkt, took German lessons, attended theater and opera, and worked on material that entered *Either/Or*. “Rarely left Copenhagen” is broadly correct: his foreign travel comprised four Berlin visits and one short Swedish excursion, while Danish journeys included Jutland, Gilleleje, and Zealand carriage trips.

### The publishing eruption, 1843

On 20 February 1843 C.A. Reitzel published the two-volume *Either/Or*, “edited” by Victor Eremita. The papers of A explore boredom, seduction, music, tragedy, and aesthetic existence; Judge William argues for ethical choice and marriage. Kierkegaard simultaneously published signed upbuilding discourses so the pseudonymous experiment would be accompanied by an explicit religious voice.

In May he travelled to Berlin again, revisiting the breakup through *Repetition*. On 16 October he published both *Fear and Trembling* (Johannes de silentio) and *Repetition* (Constantin Constantius), alongside *Three Upbuilding Discourses*. *Fear and Trembling*’s Abraham and Isaac has often been read as a transfigured account of sacrificing Regine. The parallel is plausible and supported by journals, but the book cannot be reduced to romantic autobiography.

By year’s end he had issued four collections totaling nine signed discourses. The dual production—pseudonymous aesthetic/ethical works and signed religious addresses—was deliberate “indirect communication.”

### 1844–1845: philosophy, psychology, and stages

In 1844 he published *Two Upbuilding Discourses*, *Three Upbuilding Discourses*, *Four Upbuilding Discourses*, *Philosophical Fragments* (Johannes Climacus), *The Concept of Anxiety* (Vigilius Haufniensis), and *Prefaces* (Nicolaus Notabene). Anxiety was treated as the “dizziness of freedom” and as the psychological presupposition of hereditary sin; *Fragments* contrasted Socratic recollection with the Christian claim that truth enters time.

He made a third Berlin journey in May 1845. *Stages on Life’s Way* appeared 30 April 1845, dramatizing aesthetic, ethical, and religious spheres through multiple narrators. *Eighteen Upbuilding Discourses* collected the signed 1843–44 series. *Three Discourses on Imagined Occasions* examined confession, marriage, and death.

### The Corsair affair, 1845–1846

Peder Ludvig Møller reviewed *Stages* in his 1846 annual *Gæa*, praising Kierkegaard while attacking his personal and authorial coherence. Møller secretly contributed to the satirical weekly **Corsaren** (*The Corsair*), edited by **Meïr Aron Goldschmidt**. Kierkegaard exposed the association and, with calculated sarcasm, complained that he alone had not been abused by the paper.

The Corsair obliged. In early 1846 it printed repeated caricatures and gossip about his body, clothes, voice, gait, habits, and relationship with Regine. Street boys and acquaintances reportedly mocked him. The episode was not state persecution, criminal prosecution, or an intelligence operation; it was an escalating press feud and mass campaign of ridicule that Kierkegaard deliberately provoked but did not control. It altered his daily experience and deepened his analysis of “the public,” leveling, envy, anonymity, and press power. Goldschmidt later expressed regret and credited Kierkegaard with moral influence.

On 27 February 1846 Kierkegaard published *Concluding Unscientific Postscript* under Johannes Climacus, naming himself responsible for the pseudonymous books while insisting on the personae’s independence. He initially considered ending his authorship.

### 1846–1848: second authorship and revolution

*A Literary Review* (1846), ostensibly about Thomasine Gyllembourg’s *Two Ages*, became his diagnosis of a passionless “present age,” leveling, publicity, and the abstract public.

In 1847 he published *Upbuilding Discourses in Various Spirits* and *Works of Love*. The latter treats neighbor-love as commanded, anti-preferential, and enacted rather than sentimental. Regine married Schlegel on 3 November 1847.

Kierkegaard visited Berlin for the fourth and last time in May–June 1846 according to the standard travel chronology; some popular accounts blur dates among his 1843, 1845, and 1846 stays. He also made a brief Swedish trip in 1851.

The European revolutions of **1848** and Denmark’s transition from absolutism to constitutional monarchy formed the background to a burst of journals and retrospective writings. Kierkegaard distrusted mass politics, parties, “the public,” numerical sovereignty, nationalism, and both liberal and socialist abstractions. Yet he criticized privilege, wealth, clerical comfort, and established rank, and emphasized equal individual responsibility before God. He was neither a conventional reactionary party activist nor a democratic organizer. He held no political office, attended no revolutionary conspiracy, and had no documented intelligence connection.

He wrote *The Point of View for My Work as an Author* in 1848 but withheld it. It retrospectively claimed that the whole authorship had been religious and designed to introduce Christianity into Christendom. Scholars treat this as crucial evidence and as a later self-construction, not a transparent blueprint proving every earlier intention.

### 1848–1851: explicit Christianity

- *Christian Discourses* (1848) and *The Crisis and a Crisis in the Life of an Actress* (Inter et Inter, newspaper serialization) appeared.
- *The Lily of the Field and the Bird of the Air*, *Two Ethical-Religious Essays* (H.H.), and *The Sickness unto Death* (Anti-Climacus) appeared in 1849. The last defined despair as a misrelation in the self’s relation to itself and to the power that established it.
- *Practice in Christianity* (Anti-Climacus, 1850) presented Christ as contemporaneous offense rather than cultural reassurance. *An Upbuilding Discourse* and *Two Discourses at the Communion on Fridays* also appeared.
- *For Self-Examination* and additional communion discourses appeared in 1851. *Judge for Yourself!* was written but withheld until 1876.

The “second authorship” reduced elaborate pseudonymous polyphony and made the demand of Christianity more explicit. Kierkegaard still declined to call himself an extraordinary Christian; Anti-Climacus was deliberately placed above him.

### Silence and attack on the established church, 1852–1855

For roughly three years Kierkegaard published little, while his capital diminished and his hostility to official Christianity sharpened. Bishop Mynster died on 30 January 1854. In a memorial sermon **H.L. Martensen** called Mynster one of the authentic “witnesses to the truth.” Kierkegaard regarded this as a falsification: an affluent, honored state bishop had not suffered like an apostolic witness.

On **18 December 1854** *Fædrelandet* printed Kierkegaard’s attack (dated in the paper’s issue sequence around 20 December), beginning a series of articles against Martensen, the clergy, paid Christianity, infant baptism as social registration, and the identification of Danish citizenship with Christianity. In May 1855 he issued pamphlets and then his own periodical **Øieblikket** (*The Moment*). Nine numbers appeared during his life; the tenth was prepared but published posthumously.

His attack was theological and institutional, not a renunciation of Christ. He argued that New Testament Christianity demanded imitation, suffering, and costly discipleship, whereas the state church sold reassurance. His rhetoric could be sweeping and personally destructive; he described clergy as officials whose livelihoods depended on softening Christianity. There was no formal heresy trial, censorship proceeding, imprisonment, or dismissal because he held no church office.

## Residences, Movements, and Daily Life

Kierkegaard’s geographical world was unusually concentrated:

- Family home at Nytorv, later Copenhagen addresses including Nørregade and Rosenborg quarter apartments.
- University, Church of Our Lady, Royal Theatre, cafés, booksellers, and the streets inside Copenhagen’s ramparts formed his working landscape.
- Regular walks were a method of composition and social observation. He spoke with servants, tradesmen, students, officials, and children, then wrote standing at a high desk.
- Danish travel: Gilleleje (1835), family pilgrimage to Sædding/Jutland (1840), visits around Zealand and Copenhagen’s environs.
- Foreign travel: Berlin, 1841–42, 1843, 1845, and 1846; a short Sweden trip in 1851. Exact departure/return dates vary in secondary chronologies for the shorter trips.

The cliché that he was a total recluse is false. He avoided professional institutions and increasingly experienced public ridicule, but remained a conspicuous walker, theatergoer, café observer, conversationalist, and participant in Copenhagen print culture.

## Financial Interests, Property, Publishing, and Work Structure

### Inheritance

Michael Kierkegaard left a large estate in 1838. Division figures differ according to whether gross estate, securities, property, and later adjustments are counted. Biographies commonly estimate Søren’s portion at roughly **31,000–34,000 rigsdaler**, enough to live independently for years. The exact amount should not be stated without the probate accounts and currency convention.

Kierkegaard’s occupation was therefore structurally financed by mercantile capital accumulated by his father. He had no company, patent, invention, state salary, or university income. He held government bonds and drew down capital. He sold inherited property interests and securities as needed.

### Publishing business

Kierkegaard often acted as his own financial risk-bearer: he arranged publication with Copenhagen booksellers and printers, paid production costs or guaranteed editions, revised proofs intensely, and received sales revenue. **C.A. Reitzel** was the principal publisher/bookseller for major works; **Bianco Luno** and other Copenhagen printers produced volumes. Print runs were generally small by modern standards. The signed discourses and pseudonymous books were coordinated commodities as well as components of a conceptual project.

The authorship was expensive and not commercially self-supporting. Some books sold slowly; unsold sheets could be remaindered. He sometimes purchased copies for presentation. By 1855 much of the inheritance had been consumed through living expenses, travel, servants, clothes, carriage rides, books, and especially publication. He died with a much reduced but not necessarily literally zero estate; popular claims that he spent “his last cent” are dramatic simplifications.

### Institutions and appointments

- University of Copenhagen: student and degree recipient, never faculty.
- Danish Lutheran state church: baptized member and trained theologian, never ordained or employed.
- Royal Theatre and Copenhagen literary culture: audience member and critic, not employee.
- No businesses, patents, investments in industrial technologies, decorations, public awards, or state offices are documented.

## Intellectual, Religious, Artistic, and Political Development

### Socrates and indirect communication

Socrates supplied the model of irony, midwifery, and unsettling an illusion from within. Kierkegaard did not simply state doctrines; he arranged authors, genres, and choices that force the reader to occupy a position. The pseudonyms disagree, and Kierkegaard explicitly asked readers not to attribute their words directly to him.

### Against “the system”

His target was less Hegel personally than the pretense that a completed speculative system could absorb existence, decision, temporality, sin, and faith. A logical system may be possible; a system of existence is impossible for an existing thinker who is still becoming. “Subjectivity is truth” does not mean any belief becomes fact through sincerity; it concerns the mode of appropriation in ethical-religious truth.

### Existence, choice, anxiety, despair

The familiar “aesthetic–ethical–religious stages” are heuristic spheres, not a simple developmental staircase. Choice constitutes the ethical self; anxiety discloses freedom’s possibility; despair is often unconscious and concerns the self’s grounding; faith involves a paradoxical relation to God that cannot be reduced to public ethics or objective proof.

### Christianity and Lutheranism

Kierkegaard remained a Christian shaped by Luther, Pietism, Scripture, and sacramental practice, but opposed established “Christendom.” He attacked cheap grace, cultural baptism, clerical careerism, and the confusion of national membership with discipleship. His final refusal to receive communion from a priest was directed at the official mediation of clergy, not necessarily at communion or Christ.

### Art and literature

Mozart’s *Don Giovanni*, Goethe’s *Faust*, Shakespeare, the Heiberg circle, Romantic irony, fairy tales, sermons, and Copenhagen theater formed his aesthetic vocabulary. His own books mix criticism, fiction, dialogue, diary, imaginary speeches, sermons, satire, and philosophical analysis.

### Politics and society

Kierkegaard wrote amid the fall of absolutism, liberal-national agitation, 1848 revolution, the First Schleswig War, and the 1849 Constitution. He opposed rule by anonymous “public opinion” and doubted that numerical equality created spiritual equality. He also attacked hierarchy before God and the fusion of wealth and religious authority. Attempts to enlist him straightforwardly as conservative, liberal, socialist, anarchist, or proto-fascist omit contrary evidence. His politics was an anti-party ethics of the single individual, with socially radical and politically anti-democratic implications held together.

## Important Relationships and Networks

- **Michael Pedersen Kierkegaard:** father, educator, financier, religious and psychological determinant; also the object of resentment, reconciliation, and repeated dedication.
- **Ane Lund Kierkegaard:** mother; documentary silence has made her life disproportionately obscure.
- **Peter Christian Kierkegaard:** brother, pastor, bishop, executor/publisher; familial affection and theological divergence.
- **Regine Olsen / Regine Schlegel:** fiancée, lost partner, reader, muse, and independent historical actor. Kierkegaard turned the relationship into authorial mythology, sometimes without her consent.
- **Emil Boesen:** childhood friend, theologian and pastor; recorded Kierkegaard’s hospital conversations.
- **Poul Martin Møller:** teacher and admired writer, not to be confused with Peder Ludvig Møller.
- **Peder Ludvig Møller:** critic and Corsair contributor; principal literary antagonist in 1845–46.
- **Meïr Aron Goldschmidt:** Corsair editor; admirer turned antagonist, later reflective about the affair.
- **J.P. Mynster:** family pastor and bishop; respected paternal figure whose posthumous elevation provoked Kierkegaard’s church attack.
- **H.L. Martensen:** professor and later bishop; Danish Hegelian rival and final polemical target.
- **J.L. and Johanne Luise Heiberg:** cultural arbiters; objects of attraction, criticism, and artistic analysis.
- **Hans Christian Andersen:** target of Kierkegaard’s 1838 book; not an intimate enemy in the sensational sense.
- **F.W.J. Schelling:** lecturer heard in Berlin; initial promise, eventual disappointment.
- **Mikhail Bakunin and Friedrich Engels:** co-auditors in the celebrated Schelling lecture milieu. No evidence consulted establishes a direct meeting or relationship with Kierkegaard.
- **Rasmus Nielsen:** theologian who tried to systematize or promote Kierkegaard’s religious position; their relationship cooled.

## Illness, Psychology, Disability, and Death

### Lifelong health

Kierkegaard repeatedly described **melancholy** (*Tungsind*), anxiety, bodily weakness, and suffering. Retrospective diagnoses have included depression, bipolar spectrum disorder, obsessive personality, epilepsy, spinal disease, kyphosis, and psychosomatic illness. None can be securely diagnosed from literary texts alone. His productivity, sociability, and strategic self-dramatization complicate one-dimensional psychiatric portraits.

He had an uneven gait and possible spinal curvature. A childhood fall from a tree appears in later explanatory traditions. Military examiners deemed him unfit for service, but the precise medical basis is uncertain. There is no strong evidence of alcoholism or narcotic addiction. Coffee and tobacco were ordinary habits.

### Final illness

On **2 October 1855** Kierkegaard collapsed in the street and was taken to Frederiks Hospital. His legs progressively failed; he experienced weakness and urinary difficulties but remained mentally lucid and conversed with visitors. Emil Boesen recorded important exchanges. Kierkegaard declined communion from a state-church pastor and did not want an autopsy.

He died on 11 November. The cover of the clinical record contains “tubercul.?”—a tentative notation, not a confirmed autopsy diagnosis. Proposed explanations include:

- tuberculosis affecting the spine or spinal cord (Pott disease);
- acute ascending paralysis or polyradiculitis, sometimes compared with Guillain–Barré syndrome;
- consequences of an old spinal injury;
- another neurological disease.

Modern medical articles dispute the tuberculous-spondylitis conclusion. Because there was no autopsy and nineteenth-century records are limited, the cause remains unresolved. “Tuberculosis of the spine” should be reported as a hypothesis, not established fact.

### Funeral controversy

Official clergy conducted the funeral despite Kierkegaard’s campaign against the church. At the graveside Henrik Lund protested this appropriation and the church’s involvement. The event became the last enactment of the conflict between the individual and Christendom. It was a documented disturbance, later romanticized as a final Kierkegaardian scene.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Documentary Assessment

### Treatment of Regine

**Documented:** Kierkegaard proposed, broke the engagement, used deceptive coldness to make Regine reject him, continued to write about and symbolically address her, and attempted contact through her husband. Regine suffered acutely during the rupture.  
**Interpretive dispute:** whether the act was sacrificial care, avoidance, psychological incapacity, religious vocation, aesthetic exploitation, or a mixture. Kierkegaard’s own explanation is interested evidence, not a neutral verdict.  
**Unsupported extensions:** claims of a secret child, proven impotence, a specific sexual orientation, or a proven venereal infection.

### Corsair provocation

**Documented:** Kierkegaard publicly challenged a powerful satirical paper and exposed P.L. Møller; the paper retaliated with sustained ridicule.  
**Responsibility:** he initiated the immediate confrontation, while the editors chose the scale and bodily character of harassment.  
**Afterlife:** modern analogies to “cancel culture” can illuminate public shaming but are anachronistic if substituted for the 1840s press economy.

### Elitism and democracy

**Documented:** hostile statements about crowds, the numerical principle, mass politics, women’s emancipation in some journal contexts, and the press; also emphatic equality before God and attacks on privilege.  
**Dispute:** democratic, socialist, conservative, and post-liberal readers select different strands. He did not create a party program.

### Antisemitic language

Kierkegaard sometimes repeated negative Christian stereotypes about “the Jews,” Judaism, money, or legalism typical of his setting, while also using Israel and Jewish scripture as central religious categories and rejecting race-based politics unknown in their later form. Scholarship debates whether his rhetoric constitutes theological anti-Judaism, social antisemitism, or contextual polemic. The offensive passages should neither be erased nor converted into evidence of twentieth-century racial ideology without argument.

### Women, marriage, and gender

His pseudonymous works contain misogynistic commonplaces, aesthetic objectification, defenses of marriage, and accounts of women constructed largely by male narrators. His signed religious work can stress spiritual equality while retaining nineteenth-century gender roles. Pseudonymity complicates but does not make the cultural effects disappear.

### Criminality, corruption, and state coercion

No evidence was found of fraud, money laundering, corruption, violent crime, imprisonment, political violence, intelligence work, patents disputes, or criminal conviction. The funeral protest involved his nephew, not Kierkegaard. His major conflicts were literary, religious, familial, and reputational.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. “The family curse”

1. **Story:** Michael cursed God as a starving boy on the Jutland heath; God therefore doomed his children to die young, and Michael expected to outlive them all.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** grounded in Søren’s journals and family recollections, amplified by early biographies and popular retellings.
3. **Evidence:** Michael’s guilt and Søren’s “earthquake” are well attested; the precise disclosure and causal belief are not fully explicit in a single contemporary record.
4. **Against literal truth:** mortality has natural causes; Peter Christian lived to eighty-two and Søren passed the feared age of thirty-three.
5. **Effect:** became a master key for reading anxiety, despair, the engagement, and early death, sometimes flattening a complex authorship into hereditary trauma.

### 2. The “great earthquake” was syphilis

1. **Story:** Michael revealed that he had syphilis and transmitted a hereditary taint.
2. **Circulation:** a modern biographical hypothesis, especially associated with Joakim Garff’s reading of obscure journal imagery.
3. **Evidence for:** sexual guilt, premature deaths, metaphors of inherited corruption.
4. **Evidence against:** no medical record or explicit confession identifies syphilis; imagery is not diagnosis; Peter’s longevity weighs against a simple congenital account.
5. **Effect:** sensationalized the family secret and generated criticism of biographical overreach.

### 3. Kierkegaard sacrificed Regine exactly as Abraham sacrificed Isaac

1. **Story:** the engagement is the hidden referent of *Fear and Trembling*.
2. **Circulation:** began with Kierkegaard’s own journals and the book’s temporal proximity; repeated by biographers and literary culture.
3. **Evidence for:** his language of sacrifice, renunciation, and receiving back; recurring encrypted references to Regine.
4. **Against reduction:** Johannes de silentio is a pseudonym; the work has theological, ethical, and philosophical arguments exceeding biography; Kierkegaard did not receive Regine back.
5. **Effect:** made their romance the dominant popular gateway to his philosophy and risked turning Regine into a symbol rather than a person.

### 4. The “leap of faith” quotation

1. **Story:** Kierkegaard coined and constantly used the exact English phrase “leap of faith.”
2. **Circulation:** twentieth-century introductions and popular philosophy.
3. **Evidence:** he discusses leaps, qualitative transition, faith, and Lessing’s “ditch”; related Danish expressions occur.
4. **Qualification:** the standardized slogan is largely a reception formula, not a single canonical sentence that summarizes his position.
5. **Effect:** made him accessible but encouraged irrationalist caricatures.

### 5. “Life can only be understood backwards…”

This is substantially genuine: an 1843 journal entry says life must be understood backward but lived forward. Online versions often detach it from its continuation about the difficulty of living temporally. Exact English wording depends on translation.

### 6. Total recluse who never left Copenhagen

1. **Story:** he lived alone in a room and never travelled.
2. **Evidence against:** four Berlin stays, Sweden, Jutland, Gilleleje, country excursions, theatergoing, cafés, and daily street conversations.
3. **Kernel:** he lived almost his entire life in Copenhagen and never built a conventional career, household, or international network.
4. **Effect:** supported the image of a disembodied philosopher while hiding his urban ethnography.

### 7. Kierkegaard, Bakunin, and Engels as a personal circle

1. **Story:** the future existentialist, anarchist, and communist met together under Schelling.
2. **Evidence:** credible scholarship places all three in the famous Berlin lecture audience.
3. **Limit:** co-attendance does not prove they met, spoke, or influenced one another personally. Their dates of attendance may not coincide at every session.
4. **Effect:** the lecture hall has become a symbolic crossroads of nineteenth-century thought.

### 8. Death from a childhood fall or definitively from spinal tuberculosis

Both stories appear in biographies. Neither is proved. The hospital record is compatible with serious neurological/spinal illness; “tubercul.?” was tentative, there was no autopsy, and modern physicians disagree.

### 9. “Father of existentialism”

The label accurately marks profound influence on twentieth-century philosophies of existence, but it is retrospective. Kierkegaard’s project was Christian and polemical; Sartrean atheistic existentialism is not simply his doctrine continued.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### 1855–1900: Danish controversy and first biography

At death Kierkegaard was notorious locally but not a European philosophical celebrity. Peter Christian and editors released withheld works and papers. Danish clergy, theologians, and literary critics argued over whether he was saint, pathological exception, comic eccentric, or destroyer of the church. **Georg Brandes’s** 1877 biography secularized him and helped transmit him beyond theology. **Harald Høffding** and **Georg/Edvard Brandes** placed him in modern intellectual history.

### 1900–1945: translation and existential/dialectical appropriation

German translations by Christoph Schrempf, Hermann Gottsched, Emanuel Hirsch, and others opened continental reception, though editorial choices sometimes distorted pseudonymity. **Karl Barth** found in Kierkegaard’s infinite qualitative distinction a weapon against liberal Protestantism; **Emil Brunner, Rudolf Bultmann**, and dialectical theology followed different paths. **Karl Jaspers** and **Martin Heidegger** transformed anxiety, possibility, decision, and existence. Heidegger acknowledged Kierkegaard selectively, and scholars debate uncredited depth of dependence.

Jewish dialogical thinkers **Martin Buber** and **Franz Rosenzweig** engaged the single individual, relation, and revelation. **Miguel de Unamuno** developed a parallel Christian existentialism. **Kafka** read Kierkegaard intensely. **Wittgenstein** called him a profound thinker and religious writer.

### After 1945: “father of existentialism”

Jean-Paul Sartre, Simone de Beauvoir, Albert Camus, Gabriel Marcel, and later existentialists made Kierkegaard central to histories of freedom, anxiety, absurdity, authenticity, and choice. The label widened readership while frequently secularizing a specifically Christian authorship. Psychology and psychotherapy appropriated anxiety, despair, selfhood, and commitment; existential therapy often separates these categories from sin and grace.

### Continuing disputes

- **Religious versus philosophical Kierkegaard:** whether the philosophy is autonomous or subordinate to Christian communication.
- **Pseudonyms:** whether a unified doctrine can be reconstructed without silencing distinct voices.
- **Politics:** radical egalitarian before God, conservative anti-democrat, critic of capitalism, proto-anarchic individualist, or non-programmatic religious author.
- **Gender and antisemitism:** newer scholarship reopens passages marginalized by older admiring reception.
- **Biography:** Regine-centered psychological readings versus textual/contextual approaches; dispute over Garff’s imaginative reconstructions.
- **Canonization:** statues, anniversaries, pilgrimage sites, and quotations have made a critic of cultural Christianity into a cultural emblem of Denmark.

The archive’s inclusion in major Danish heritage work and completion of SKS have shifted study from quotation anthologies toward manuscripts, publication history, and exact authorial sequencing.

## Research Limitations

- Kierkegaard’s journals are both records and literary self-fashioning; late retrospective entries frequently reorganize earlier motives.
- Pseudonymous texts cannot be read as depositions.
- Regine’s testimony was recorded decades later after she had read parts of the posthumous papers.
- Exact financial totals depend on probate and historical currency accounting; published estimates vary.
- No autopsy fixes the cause of death.
- Co-attendance at Schelling’s lectures does not establish interpersonal contact among Kierkegaard, Bakunin, and Engels.
- The works list is substantial and chronologically comprehensive at book/series level, but individual unsigned newspaper attributions and every separate discourse title require the SKS electronic concordance.

## Atlas Connections

### Abraham

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** Kierkegaard, writing as Johannes de silentio, made the biblical Abraham the central figure of *Fear and Trembling* (Copenhagen, 1843), examining the binding of Isaac, faith, sacrifice, and the “teleological suspension of the ethical.” This is a textual engagement with the scriptural figure, not evidence for a historical encounter.

### Paul the Apostle

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** Kierkegaard repeatedly interpreted Paul’s letters throughout his signed discourses, journals, and final attack on Christendom. Especially important were Paul’s treatments of faith, offense, suffering, love, the “thorn in the flesh,” and Christianity as foolishness before the world.

### Aristotle

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** Kierkegaard discussed Aristotle in the 1841 dissertation *On the Concept of Irony*, *The Concept of Anxiety* (1844), *Philosophical Fragments* (1844), *Concluding Unscientific Postscript* (1846), and his journals. Aristotle’s accounts of movement, possibility, actuality, virtue, and classical drama supplied recurring conceptual and literary reference points. Specialist source-and-reception scholarship identifies the Greek tradition—especially Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle—as fundamental to Kierkegaard’s authorship. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/)

### Cicero

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** Kierkegaard cited and discussed Cicero in his dissertation, journals, and classical notes, particularly as a transmitter of ancient accounts of Socrates and irony. The connection is textual and scholarly.

### Plato

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** Plato’s dialogues were primary evidence for Kierkegaard’s reconstruction of Socrates in *On the Concept of Irony* (1841), and Platonic recollection became the principal ancient position against which Johannes Climacus constructed *Philosophical Fragments* (1844). Kierkegaard’s extensive engagement with Plato is textual, not personal. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/)

### Saint Augustine of Hippo

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** Kierkegaard engaged Augustine in his journals and religious writings on sin, inwardness, time, temptation, conversion, and the will. The relationship is one of documented textual reception; claims that Kierkegaard simply reproduced an Augustinian system would overstate it.

### René Descartes

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** Kierkegaard examined Cartesian doubt and the maxim *de omnibus dubitandum est* in the unfinished 1842–43 work *Johannes Climacus, or De omnibus dubitandum est* and returned to modern methodological doubt in *Philosophical Fragments* and *Concluding Unscientific Postscript*. His treatment was critical and often mediated through German idealism.

### Johann Gottlieb Fichte

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** Kierkegaard discussed Fichte’s theory of the self and Romantic irony in *On the Concept of Irony* (1841) and related notes. Fichte belonged to the German-idealist background against which Kierkegaard developed his accounts of selfhood, reflection, and ethical existence.

### Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** Kierkegaard wrote repeatedly about Goethe’s *Faust*, *Wilhelm Meister*, lyric poetry, and conceptions of genius and personality. Goethean figures and quotations appear throughout the journals and pseudonymous works, especially *Either/Or* and *Stages on Life’s Way*.

### Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** Kierkegaard’s dissertation and most of the major pseudonymous philosophical works engage Hegelian logic, history, mediation, ethics, religion, and claims to systematic completeness. His relation to Hegel was both appropriative and polemical, often mediated by Danish Hegelians; it cannot accurately be reduced to simple rejection. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/)

### Immanuel Kant

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** Kierkegaard engaged Kantian ethics, autonomy, radical evil, religion, and the limits of reason in his dissertation, journals, *Either/Or*, *Fear and Trembling*, *The Concept of Anxiety*, and later religious writings. Kant’s universal moral law forms part of the philosophical background to the Abraham problem in *Fear and Trembling*.

### Martin Luther

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** Kierkegaard read and repeatedly wrote about Luther, sometimes invoking him against complacent Lutheran Christendom and sometimes accusing later Protestants of converting Luther’s corrective into an excuse for avoiding imitation and works of love. The engagement became especially pointed in his journals and anti-church publications of 1854–55.

### Jesus of Nazareth

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** Jesus Christ is the central historical and theological figure of Kierkegaard’s signed discourses, *Practice in Christianity* (1850), *For Self-Examination* (1851), and the 1854–55 attack on official Christendom. Kierkegaard wrote about Christ as the paradoxical God-man, the “sign of contradiction,” and a contemporary demand for imitation rather than merely an object of historical knowledge.

### Novalis

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** Kierkegaard treated Novalis as a representative of Romantic irony in *On the Concept of Irony* (1841), criticizing the Romantic ironist’s aesthetic dissolution of actuality while also absorbing elements of Romantic literary practice.

### Friedrich Schiller

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** Kierkegaard cited and discussed Schiller’s drama, poetry, and aesthetics in his journals and pseudonymous writings. Schiller belonged to the German literary canon through which Kierkegaard formulated contrasts among aesthetic immediacy, ethical formation, and religious existence.

### Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** The “Immediate Erotic Stages” essay in *Either/Or* (1843) offers an extended interpretation of Mozart and *Don Giovanni*, which Kierkegaard regarded as a uniquely perfect conjunction of musical medium and sensuous-erotic subject. Kierkegaard also repeatedly attended productions of the opera in Copenhagen, but this was engagement with Mozart’s posthumously performed work, not a meeting with Mozart. [Royal Ballet and Opera overview of the work’s reception](https://www.rbo.org.uk/production/don-giovanni-kasper-holten?cast=aigul-akhmetshina)

### Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling

- **[T3] Documented fact.** Kierkegaard attended Schelling’s University of Berlin lectures on the philosophy of revelation during the winter semester of 1841–42, took extensive surviving notes, and assessed the lectures in letters and journals. He initially welcomed Schelling’s distinction between essence and actuality but became sharply disappointed with the course. [Edition record for Kierkegaard’s lecture notes](https://iris.univr.it/handle/11562/346786)
- **[T4] Documented fact.** Lecturer and auditor occupied the same Berlin lecture hall beginning with Schelling’s inaugural lecture on 15 November 1841. This establishes direct pedagogical proximity, although no private meeting between them is securely documented. [De Gruyter overview](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9780791479940/html)

### Mikhail Bakunin

- **[T4] Documented fact.** Bakunin and Kierkegaard were among the auditors at Schelling’s inaugural Berlin lecture in November 1841. The evidence supports shared lecture-hall presence, not friendship, conversation, correspondence, or mutual intellectual influence. [Cambridge history preview](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781316169193_A23896542/preview-9781316169193_A23896542.pdf)

### Friedrich Engels

- **[T4] Documented fact.** Engels and Kierkegaard attended Schelling’s inaugural Berlin lecture in November 1841 as intellectually opposed but independently motivated auditors. No documented personal exchange between them is known. [Study of Schelling’s Berlin audience](https://publicatt.unicatt.it/handle/10807/69424)

### Alexander von Humboldt

- **[T4] Reported fact.** Contemporary and later scholarly accounts place Humboldt and Kierkegaard among the distinguished audience at Schelling’s inaugural Berlin lecture in November 1841. This supports co-presence only; no personal interaction is documented. [Survey of Schelling’s 1841 audience](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/21692327.2019.1571806)

### Hans Christian Andersen

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** Kierkegaard’s first book, *From the Papers of One Still Living* (Copenhagen, 1838), was an extended critique of Andersen’s novel *Only a Fiddler* and of what Kierkegaard regarded as Andersen’s insufficiently developed conception of personality. [Hans Christian Andersen Centre](https://andersen.sdu.dk/forskning/anmeldelser/kritik_e.html)
- **[T5] Reported fact.** Andersen’s autobiography recalls encountering Kierkegaard in a Copenhagen street before the review appeared and receiving the impression that Kierkegaard would write favorably. Kierkegaard himself said that he scarcely knew Andersen personally; the safest conclusion is a documented acquaintance or encounter, not friendship. [Museum Odense study](https://museumodense.dk/udgivelser/h-c-andersen-og-soeren-kierkegaard/)

### Theodor W. Adorno

- **[T1+] Documented fact.** Adorno made Kierkegaard the subject of his habilitation, published in 1933 as *Kierkegaard: Construction of the Aesthetic*. It offered a sustained Marxian-critical interpretation of Kierkegaard’s aesthetics, interiority, and social setting rather than straightforward discipleship. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/)

### Martin Heidegger

- **[T1+] Documented fact.** Heidegger discussed Kierkegaard from his early Freiburg and Marburg work through *Being and Time* (1927) and later writings, selectively crediting him for penetrating analyses of anxiety and existence while confining him chiefly to the religious sphere. In 1963 Heidegger also prepared a paper for the Paris conference marking Kierkegaard’s 150th anniversary. [Cambridge, *Heidegger and Kierkegaard*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/abs/heidegger-and-kierkegaard/959809F81EF9C70A74CAFBF322EC1C42)

### Martin Buber

- **[T1+] Documented fact.** Buber explicitly examined Kierkegaard’s “single one” in the essay “The Question to the Single One” (*Die Frage an den Einzelnen*, 1936), accepting the importance of personal responsibility while criticizing a conception of relation to God that could eclipse relation to other human beings.

### Albert Camus

- **[T1+] Documented fact.** Camus directly analyzed Kierkegaard in *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942), grouping him among thinkers who recognized the absurd but then, in Camus’s judgment, made a religious “leap” that escaped its consequences. This is a documented but contestable interpretation of Kierkegaard. [Harvard Theological Review discussion](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/harvard-theological-review/article/faith-and-the-absurd-kierkegaard-camus-and-jobs-religious-protest/9EB583ED29F743B3FCA6B4BCDB100A73)

### Jean-Paul Sartre

- **[T1+] Documented fact.** Sartre drew on Kierkegaard in his accounts of freedom, anxiety, choice, and singular existence, then addressed him explicitly in “The Singular Universal,” delivered at the UNESCO Kierkegaard colloquium in Paris in April 1964. Sartre recast Kierkegaard’s Christian individual in existentialist and Marxian terms. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/)

### Jacques Derrida

- **[T1+] Documented fact.** Derrida devoted sustained attention to Kierkegaard—especially Abraham, secrecy, decision, responsibility, and sacrifice—in *The Gift of Death* (*Donner la mort*, developed from lectures and published in French in 1992). His engagement centers principally on *Fear and Trembling*.

### Gilles Deleuze

- **[T1+] Documented fact.** Deleuze explicitly invoked Kierkegaard, alongside Nietzsche, in *Difference and Repetition* (1968) as a thinker who made repetition a fundamental philosophical problem and created conceptual personae capable of enacting movement within thought.

### Jorge Luis Borges

- **[T1+] Documented fact.** Borges read and wrote about Kierkegaard, including in the essay commonly translated as “Kierkegaard,” and referred to him elsewhere when treating theology, paradox, repetition, and the relation between literary authorship and religious thought. This was a posthumous literary-intellectual engagement; the two men’s lifetimes did not overlap.

No T2 prophecy/hyperstition or T9 shared-object transmission meeting the stated evidentiary threshold was found among the roster. In particular, thematic similarity, later influence without identifiable engagement, and simple contemporaneity were not counted as connections.
