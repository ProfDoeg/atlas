## Works by Christian Dior

### Fashion illustrations and work for other couture houses

- **1935–1938:** *Fashion illustrations for Le Figaro, Jardin des Modes, and couture houses*. A dispersed corpus of drawings sold to periodicals, milliners, and houses including Jean Patou, Schiaparelli, Maggy Rouff, Worth, Balenciaga, Molyneux, Paquin, and Piguet; no complete catalogue of the surviving sheets has been published. [1]
- **1938–1940:** *Collections for Robert Piguet*. Three couture collections designed while Dior was Piguet’s salaried house designer; they are collaborative, corporate obras rather than independently signed Dior collections, and their individual contents are incompletely recoverable. [2]
- **1941–1946:** *Designs for Lucien Lelong*. A large, incompletely enumerated corpus produced within Lelong’s studio alongside other designers, notably Pierre Balmain; surviving sketches can be attributed to Dior, but finished garments generally bear Lelong’s name, so individual authorship is not always securely separable. [1]

### Theatre, ballet, and film costumes

- **1939:** *The School for Scandal*. Costume project for Marcel Herrand’s production of Sheridan’s play, undertaken while Dior worked anonymously at Piguet; Dior later identified it as his own theatre work. [3]
- **Date uncertain, before 1947:** *Treize femmes* (*Thirteen Women*). Ballet costumes designed at the request of Boris Kochno and Christian Bérard; Dior later repudiated the result, making it an unusually resistant obra whose existence is clearer than its date and production history. [3]
- **1942:** *Le Lit à colonnes*. Film-costume designs for Roland Tual’s production, part of Dior’s wartime work before his own house existed. [4]
- **1942:** *Lettres d’amour*. Film-costume designs for Claude Autant-Lara’s film; the credit belongs to a production workshop and should not be mistaken for an autonomous couture collection. [4]
- **1943:** *Le Baron fantôme*. Film-costume work associated with Jean Cocteau and Serge de Poligny’s film; surviving filmographies vary over whether Dior designed the entire wardrobe or selected women’s costumes. [5]
- **1946:** *Sylvie et le Fantôme*. Costume designs for Claude Autant-Lara’s film, documented in Dior filmographies but not preserved as a complete independent collection. [5]
- **1946:** *Bedelia*. Clothes for Margaret Lockwood in Lance Comfort’s film; this was star wardrobe rather than comprehensive costume design for the whole production. [6]
- **1947:** *Le Silence est d’or*. Costume contribution to René Clair’s film; scholarship describes Dior as lending designs to the production rather than serving as its sole costume author. [7]
- **1950:** *Les Enfants terribles*. Some costumes for Jean-Pierre Melville’s film; the deliberately partial credit makes this a collaboration rather than a self-contained Dior obra. [7]
- **1950:** *Stage Fright*. Marlene Dietrich’s screen wardrobe for Alfred Hitchcock’s film, including couture models adapted for the character; AFI credits Dior specifically for Dietrich’s costumes, not the entire film. [6]
- **1953:** *Stazione Termini / Indiscretion of an American Wife*. Jennifer Jones’s costumes for Vittorio De Sica’s film; the work received an Academy Award nomination, although Dior’s contribution was essentially a star wardrobe rather than total costume design. [6]
- **1955:** *Gentlemen Marry Brunettes*. Gowns for the “loot” sequence; AFI records a narrowly delimited contribution rather than authorship of all costumes. [6]
- **1956:** *The Ambassador’s Daughter*. Clothes for Olivia de Havilland and other prominently featured Dior fashions; AFI’s “clothes by” credit again denotes couture supplied to a film, not sole costume design. [6]
- **1957:** *The Little Hut*. Clothes made for Ava Gardner; sources disagree in the breadth of Dior’s responsibility, so it is safest to treat the work as Gardner’s wardrobe rather than the film’s complete costume scheme. [8]

### Haute-couture collections and lines

- **Spring–Summer 1947:** *Corolle and En 8*. Dior’s first collection for his own house, comprising ninety designs and quickly nicknamed the “New Look”; it is a collective couture obra made by Dior’s studio and ateliers, not ninety objects fabricated by Dior’s hands alone. [1][9]
- **Autumn–Winter 1947–1948:** *Corolle*. Dior’s second seasonal collection developed the rounded hips, narrow waist, and lengthened skirt of the debut; references disagree over whether “Corolle” is a renewed official line-name or simply continuity with the first collection. [9][10]
- **Spring–Summer 1948:** *Zig-Zag and Envol*. A single seasonal collection articulated through related line-names; contemporary English-language coverage commonly foregrounds *Envol*. [9][10]
- **Autumn–Winter 1948–1949:** *Ailée*. The collection is also called the *Cloche* line in contemporary American Vogue, demonstrating that collection-names circulated differently across markets. [9][10]
- **Spring–Summer 1949:** *Trompe-l’œil*. A seasonal collection characterized in contemporary English coverage by conspicuous pockets and panels; “Pockets” is descriptive rather than a securely universal French title. [9][10]
- **Autumn–Winter 1949–1950:** *Milieu du siècle*. A complete seasonal couture collection organized around geometric cutting; contemporary Vogue also described its dominant silhouette as the “Blused Line.” [10][11]
- **Spring–Summer 1950:** *Verticale*. A couture collection organized around narrow, lengthening vertical effects. [9][10]
- **Autumn–Winter 1950–1951:** *Oblique*. A couture collection structured by slanting seams, closures, wrapping, and asymmetric visual movement. [9][10]
- **Spring–Summer 1951:** *Naturelle*. A couture collection also described in English-language commentary as the “Oval” line; the two terms should not automatically be treated as separate collections. [9][10]
- **Autumn–Winter 1951–1952:** *Longue*. A seasonal collection whose “Long Line” reorganized bodily proportion through an elongated torso and lowered visual emphasis. [9][10]
- **Spring–Summer 1952:** *Sinueuse*. A softer couture collection emphasizing suppleness and curved movement after the preceding season’s rigidity. [9][12]
- **Autumn–Winter 1952–1953:** *Profilée*. A couture collection conceived strongly in side view, with engineered projections at hip and skirt; contemporary English sources call it the “Profile Line.” [9][12]
- **Spring–Summer 1953:** *Tulipe*. A seasonal couture collection whose silhouette treated the body as a narrow stalk opening into fullness above. [9][10]
- **Autumn–Winter 1953–1954:** *Vivante*. A couture collection also encountered as *Coupole* or “Cupola”; the varying terminology appears to name emphases within the same season, not securely distinct obras. [9][10]
- **Spring–Summer 1954:** *Muguet*. A couture collection dedicated to lily-of-the-valley forms and motifs. [9][13]
- **Autumn–Winter 1954–1955:** *H*. A seasonal collection built around a relatively straight, flattened silhouette; it was also informally nicknamed *Haricot vert*, but that sobriquet is not a separate collection. [10][14]
- **Spring–Summer 1955:** *A*. A couture collection whose widening silhouette was diagrammed through the capital letter A. [9][10]
- **Autumn–Winter 1955–1956:** *Y*. A couture collection concentrating visual breadth above a narrow lower body. [9][10]
- **Spring–Summer 1956:** *Flèche*. A seasonal couture collection associated with arrow-like lines and the short *caraco* jacket. [9][10]
- **Autumn–Winter 1956–1957:** *Aimant*. A couture collection whose French title means “magnet”; surviving archival sketches identify it explicitly as Dior’s *Aimant* line. [15]
- **Spring–Summer 1957:** *Libre*. Dior’s penultimate collection, also discussed through its continuous “seven-eighths” silhouette. [9][10]
- **Autumn–Winter 1957–1958:** *Fuseau*. Dior’s final collection, presented shortly before his death and characterized by an elongated, tapered line. Later examination of the collection charts has raised the possibility that assistant Yves Saint Laurent drew substantial portions of the last two collections, so undivided personal authorship is disputed. [10][16]

### Named garments within the couture corpus

- **1947:** *Bar*. Jacket-and-skirt ensemble from the first *Corolle/En 8* collection and the canonical material manifestation of the “New Look”; manufacture belonged to Dior’s atelier system, and Pierre Cardin’s tailoring role has sometimes complicated the shorthand “made by Dior.” [9][17]
- **1949:** *Junon*. Evening dress from *Milieu du siècle*, with an engineered, petal-like skirt; it is one named model within a collection, not a collection itself. [11]
- **1949:** *Vénus*. Evening dress from *Milieu du siècle*, conceived as a companion in theme to *Junon*. [18]
- **1952:** *La Cigale*. Architecturally constructed evening dress from the *Profilée* collection; its emphatic structure makes it both garment and sculptural manifestation of the season’s project. [19]
- **1952:** *Palmyre*. Evening gown from *Profilée*, produced with specialized embroidery and textile collaborators; attribution to “Dior” therefore names the directing house and designer as well as a distributed act of making. [20]
- **1956:** *Flamme*. Dress from the *Flèche* collection, notably worn by Grace Kelly; it remains a couture model rather than a unique costume made solely for her. [21]
- **1957:** *Muguet*. Dress from the *Libre* collection, distinct from the 1954 collection also titled *Muguet*. [13]

### Perfumes and cosmetics

- **1947:** *Miss Dior*. The first Parfums Christian Dior fragrance, launched in December as part of Dior’s ambition to surround the dressed body with scent; it is a collaborative creation of the perfume company and perfumers, not a formula personally composed by the couturier. [1]
- **1949:** *Diorama*. House fragrance whose olfactory authorship belongs to perfumer Edmond Roudnitska; Dior’s role was artistic and institutional direction, so “made by Christian Dior” requires that qualification. [22]
- **1953:** *Eau Fraîche*. Fragrance created for Parfums Christian Dior by Edmond Roudnitska; it is a house creation under Dior’s direction rather than his sole material composition. [22]
- **1955:** *Christian Dior lipstick collection*. Boxed sets in twenty-two shades, the house’s first lipsticks; this is a cosmetic range and packaging project, not a single fixed object or personally compounded product. [1]
- **1956:** *Diorissimo*. Lily-of-the-valley fragrance created with Edmond Roudnitska; Dior supplied the governing floral idea and house context, while the perfume formula was Roudnitska’s specialist work. [1][13][22]

### Publications, lectures, and operative fashion texts

- **1951:** *Je suis couturier*. An illustrated account of couture and Dior’s working life, assembled from statements recorded by Alice Chavane and Élie Rabourdin and first serialized in *Elle*; its mediated authorship makes it neither a wholly ghost-free autobiography nor merely a book about Dior. [23][24]
- **1954:** *The Little Dictionary of Fashion*. An alphabetically organized protocol of dress: a book whose entries instruct readers how to choose, combine, and wear clothes rather than simply describing fashion. It first appeared in English; the later French *Petit dictionnaire de la mode* is a translation, not another work. [1][25]
- **1954:** *Preface to Cecil Beaton’s The Glass of Fashion*. A short paratextual work contributed to another author’s book; it should not be expanded into a separate Dior volume. [26]
- **1955:** *Sorbonne lecture on fashion*. A lecture-text delivered with an accompanying fashion show in the Sorbonne’s Grand Amphithéâtre; as a spoken event joined to displayed garments, it approaches a Gesamtkunstwerk more closely than an ordinary essay. [1][27]
- **1956:** *Christian Dior et moi* (*Dior by Dior / Christian Dior and I*). Autobiography of the couturier and the corporate “Dior,” published in French in 1956; some house chronologies confusingly illustrate or date it through the 1958 edition, issued after his death. [1][28]
- **1957:** *“Les révolutions dans la couture: Comment on fait la mode”*. Newspaper essay published in *Le Figaro littéraire* on 8 June 1957, a compact explanatory text on how fashion is produced. [24]
- **1955–1957:** *Conférences écrites pour la Sorbonne*. Two lecture-texts on fashion history, the couturier’s role, contemporary taste, and clients; preserved and published together only posthumously, they are completed texts but not lifetime books. [27]

### Institutional and spatial projects

- **1928–1931:** *Galerie Jacques Bonjean*. Art-gallery project founded by Dior and Jacques Bonjean to exhibit modern and contemporary artists; it is an entrepreneurial-curatorial obra rather than an artwork authored by Dior alone. [1]
- **1932–1934:** *Galerie Pierre Colle partnership*. Curatorial and commercial gallery project at rue Cambacérès, including a 1933 Surrealist exhibition; the displayed artworks were not Dior’s creations, while the program and selection were collaborative manifestations. [1]
- **1946–1957:** *Maison Christian Dior*. Couture house founded with Marcel Boussac and opened at 30 Avenue Montaigne: an institutional Gesamtkunstwerk encompassing garments, salons, shows, perfume, graphics, retail, licensing, and labor by many named and unnamed collaborators. It strongly resists classification as Dior’s personal “work,” yet it was his governing project and the structure through which most later obras appeared. [1][29]
