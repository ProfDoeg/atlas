# Sophie Taeuber-Arp: Research Dossier

## Scope and Source Note

This dossier reconstructs Sophie Taeuber-Arp’s life and work from currently accessible archival, museum, foundation, scholarly, and contemporaneous sources. It gives priority to the Sophie Taeuber-Arp Research Project (STARP), the archives and chronologies of the Stiftung Arp and Fondazione Marguerite Arp, the 2021 *Living Abstraction* research project, Taeuber-Arp’s published correspondence, and specialist scholarship on Dada dance, the Aubette, and the Arp–Taeuber collaborations.

The surviving record is uneven. Taeuber-Arp rarely signed or titled her visual work. Hans Arp and Hugo Weber assigned many dates and titles after her death for the 1948 catalogue raisonné. Her live dances left programs and written recollections but no securely provenanced performance photograph or film. Collaborative objects are sometimes catalogued inconsistently. Exact earnings, salary, commission fees, and the police or medical file concerning her death are not available in the sources consulted. These limitations are identified where they matter.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full birth name:** Sophie Henriette Gertrud Taeuber. “Gertrude” occurs as a spelling variant in some records and later reference works.
- **Professional and married name:** Sophie Taeuber-Arp; also printed as Sophie H. Taeuber-Arp, S. H. Taeuber-Arp, Sophie Taeuber, and, in French contexts, Sophie Taeuber Arp.
- **Dada pseudonym:** **G. Thauber**. She used the altered name in the Zurich Dada milieu, including as a signatory of the 1918 Dada manifesto, to reduce the risk that her avant-garde activities would compromise her municipal teaching position. The extent to which every anonymous dance appearance can be assigned to her remains uncertain.
- **Spouse’s names:** Her husband was born Hans Peter Wilhelm Arp and used both Hans Arp and Jean Arp. Contemporary French and later international sources accordingly describe the couple as Sophie and Hans Arp or Sophie and Jean Arp.
- **Birth:** 19 January 1889, Davos-Platz, Graubünden, Switzerland.
- **Death:** Zurich-Höngg, Switzerland, in January 1943, aged fifty-three. The documentary sources disagree over whether the fatal night was 12–13 January or 13–14 January. The Fondazione Marguerite Arp chronology gives the night of 13–14 January; STARP gives 12–13 January; many reference works give 13 January as the date of death. The cause is generally reported as accidental carbon-monoxide poisoning from a stove at Max and Binia Bill’s house, but STARP states that the precise circumstances remain largely unexplained.
- **Citizenship:** Born a German citizen through her Prussian/German father; reacquired Swiss citizenship with her mother and a sibling in 1905; acquired German citizenship again through marriage to Hans Arp in 1922; obtained French citizenship in Strasbourg in 1926–28. Sources commonly call her Swiss-born and French-naturalized. The sequence matters because it shaped residence, employment, and wartime mobility.
- **Occupations:** Artist, textile designer, embroiderer, weaver, painter, draughtswoman, collagist, sculptor and wood-turning designer, marionette and stage designer, costume designer, dancer and choreographer, interior and furniture designer, architect, teacher, editor, and artistic network organizer.
- **Movements and associations:** Schweizerischer Werkbund; Zurich Dada; Das Neue Leben; Cercle et Carré; Abstraction-Création; Allianz; international Concrete and Constructive art networks.
- **Languages and working contexts:** German was her principal written language; she worked within German-, French-, and multilingual Swiss and Parisian circles. *Plastique/Plastic* was deliberately international and multilingual.
- **Children:** No children are documented.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

Taeuber was the youngest of four surviving children in the account maintained by the Fondazione Marguerite Arp, although some older summaries call her the “fifth child,” presumably counting a child who did not survive or using a different family reckoning. The discrepancy should not be silently resolved without a civil-register or church-register reconstruction.

Her father, **Karl Emil or Emil Taeuber** (born about 1855), was a German or Prussian pharmacist. Her mother, **Sophie Taeuber-Krüsi**, was Swiss, from Gais in Appenzell Ausserrhoden. The parents operated a pharmacy in Davos. Emil died of tuberculosis in 1891, when Sophie was two. The loss removed the principal male income and led to a major household reorganization.

Taeuber-Krüsi was capable in architecture, needlework, and household design. After a period with relatives, she designed or helped design the family’s Villa Taeuber in Trogen and operated it as **Pension Taeuber** from about 1902. The pension was an economic enterprise as well as the setting of Sophie’s childhood. Her mother taught or encouraged sewing, embroidery, knitting, lace, and other textile practices. The later artist’s refusal to separate “fine” art from useful objects had an immediate family and economic foundation rather than being only an avant-garde theory acquired in adulthood.

### Siblings and extended family

The family moved with four children to Trogen in 1895–96. The eldest brother was a seafarer. According to Gabriele Mahn’s STARP biography, he brought home photographs of Indigenous North American leaders and objects identified within the family as Indigenous art. Sophie decorated her room with these objects. Walburga Krupp has studied their trace in her early motifs. Precise provenance and cultural identification of the objects have not been established in the public sources; later claims that directly identify particular tribal traditions should therefore be treated cautiously.

Her sister **Erika Taeuber Schlegel** (1884–1973) was especially important. Sophie lived with her in Zurich after returning from Germany in 1914 and again after escaping France in 1942. Erika later worked as a librarian for C. G. Jung. During the Arps’ wartime privation in Grasse she sent food parcels. Other siblings are less visible in the readily accessible record and are not securely named in the principal foundation chronologies.

Taeuber’s maternal aunt **Mathilde Zellweger-Krüsi**, married to a physician, sheltered the widowed mother and children at Trogen. The maternal family network therefore helped make the move from Davos possible.

### Marriage and later estate family

Taeuber married Hans Arp on 20 October 1922 at Pura in Ticino. Their marriage was childless and lasted until her death. It was also an artistic, domestic, and financial partnership, with recurrent joint works and mutual influence but unequal later attribution.

After Sophie’s death, Arp eventually married the Basel collector **Marguerite Hagenbach** in 1959. Hagenbach had been Sophie’s friend and collector from the 1930s, had bought *Cercles mouvementés* in 1937, and sent assistance during the war. She later became a major custodian of both artists’ estates. Sophie, Hans/Jean, and Marguerite share a grave at Locarno. This unusual posthumous arrangement became part of the symbolic story of the Arp household and estate.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1889–1906

Sophie was born in the alpine health-resort economy of Davos. Her father’s pharmacy belonged to a town shaped by tuberculosis treatment, and tuberculosis also caused his death. In 1895–96 the family settled in Trogen, near St. Gallen, after a transitional period with relatives.

The household combined financial necessity, tourism, craft, and design. Her mother’s pension required management, decoration, clothing and linens, service work, and contact with travelers. Surviving photographs show Sophie in embroidered clothing and engaging with traditional textile techniques. Nature and Trogen’s historic architecture were later invoked by biographers as formative surroundings, but the stronger documentary point is the practical continuum between family craft, commercial hospitality, and visual design.

In 1904–05 she attended the **Stauffacher School** in St. Gallen. In 1905–06 she attended the **Mesdames van Muyden boarding school in Vevey**, an episode omitted from many concise biographies. In 1905 her mother and the two youngest children reacquired Swiss citizenship.

Two adolescent essays survive through later republication: *Eine Stunde im Hofbräuhaus in München* (22 April 1905) and *Etwas aus dem Tierleben (Ein Menageriebesuch)* (1907). They are genuine early writings, not posthumous texts composed in her name.

## Education and Formation, 1906–1915

### St. Gallen

From approximately 1906 or 1907 to 1910, Taeuber was a guest student in the drawing/textile school of the **Industrie- und Gewerbemuseum St. Gallen**. The chronology varies slightly: Stiftung Arp gives attendance beginning in 1904 at the Stauffacher and drawing schools; the Fondazione chronology distinguishes the Stauffacher School in 1904–05 and formal guest study at the museum school in 1907–10.

Her teachers included **Johannes Stauffacher**, who taught stylization and composition for industrial needs. St. Gallen was a major textile and embroidery center, so training joined design to production. She learned textile design, embroidery, lace, and the disciplined transformation of motifs for repeat and manufacture.

### Munich: the Debschitz School

In October 1910 she entered the **Lehr- und Versuch-Ateliers für angewandte und freie Kunst** in Munich, known as the Debschitz School after cofounder **Wilhelm von Debschitz**. **Hermann Obrist** was the other founding figure. The school followed reform-art ideas associated with the Arts and Crafts movement, Vienna, and Henry van de Velde. It rejected a rigid hierarchy between painting, craft, furniture, architecture, and ornament.

Taeuber’s curriculum included art history, weaving, lace, ornament, applied-art design, woodworking, wood turning, representation techniques, projection and design drawing, and aspects of architecture. The breadth was decisive: later shifts from beadwork to marionettes, turned heads, furniture, and rooms were not sudden changes of profession but uses of a common training.

### Hamburg

In 1912–13 she studied weaving at the **Kunstgewerbeschule Hamburg**, where **Maria Brinckmann** is identified as an important teacher. She returned to Munich for the winter semester of 1913–14. A 1914 photograph by Eduard Wasow shows her wearing a costume she designed and made, evidence that clothing, self-presentation, and movement already intersected before Dada.

### Diploma and return to Switzerland

She graduated from the Debschitz School in summer 1914 with a diploma qualifying her to teach. With the outbreak of the First World War she returned to neutral Switzerland in November and established herself in Zurich with Erika.

### Movement education

Beginning in summer 1915, she studied **Bewegungskunst**—movement art—with **Rudolf von Laban** in Zurich and at Monte Verità above Ascona. **Mary Wigman**, then a close Laban collaborator, was another teacher. Laban treated spatial pathways, rhythm, group formation, and notation as compositional problems. Taeuber participated in early work toward Laban notation. Her fellow dancers included Wigman, **Suzanne Perrottet**, **Katja Wulff**, and others.

The Laban milieu mixed pedagogical reform, communal experiment, Naturkultur, expressive dance, and an attraction to ritual and non-European forms. It was not a single doctrine. Taeuber’s later geometric movement, marionette joints, rhythmic grids, and room plans can be compared with this spatial education, but direct one-to-one derivations should not be claimed without evidence.

## Early Career and Economic Base, 1914–1916

Taeuber returned with professional qualifications and worked in painting, portrait and still-life studies, textile design, beadwork, bags, cushion covers, table linens, necklaces, and other objects. Some were sold through the Zurich branch of the **Wiener Werkstätte**, led by Dagobert Peche from 1917 to 1919. Her early craft objects were not merely preparatory juvenilia: they entered contemporary applied-art commerce and helped earn her teaching appointment.

She joined the **Schweizerischer Werkbund** in 1915 and remained a member until 1931 or 1932, depending on the chronology used. In November 1915 she met Hans Arp at an exhibition, commonly identified as one at the Galerie Tanner. Arp was a Strasbourg-born poet and artist who had come to Zurich during the war. Their shared interest in abstraction and unconventional materials quickly became personal and collaborative.

Her earliest dated geometric colored-pencil **vertical-horizontal compositions** are from 1915–17. They translate the warp-weft structure of textile into autonomous arrangements of rectangular color. Later historians placed them beside contemporaneous developments by Mondrian and Malevich. Their historical status does not require a claim that she “invented” all geometric abstraction; they independently demonstrate that geometry preceded Arp’s influence and arose from her own education.

On 5 May 1916 she took charge of textile design at the Zurich **Kunstgewerbeschule/Gewerbeschule**, teaching in the applied-arts and embroidery division. From 1916 to 1929 she taught composition, design, embroidery, weaving, and later ornamental design. Her students included **Elsi Giauque** and, later, **Max Bill**. The municipal salary gave the couple their most reliable pre-1929 income. Contemporary and later sources agree that Taeuber financially supported herself and, for long periods, Arp.

## Zurich Dada, Dance, and Constructive Work, 1916–1920

### Cabaret Voltaire and Galerie Dada

The Cabaret Voltaire opened on 5 February 1916 under Hugo Ball and Emmy Hennings, with Tristan Tzara, Marcel Janco, Arp, Richard Huelsenbeck, and others. Taeuber brought Laban-trained dancers into contact with the Dada group. Dance became a recurring part of its mixed evenings of poetry, music, masks, noise, and visual art.

She performed under **G. Thauber** or anonymously because the Kunstgewerbeschule was a conventional municipal institution and public association with Dada could threaten her position. The surviving sources establish that she danced in Dada contexts, but they do not support a complete performance list.

The most securely documented solo occurred at the opening of the **Galerie Dada, Bahnhofstrasse 19, Zurich, on 29 March 1917**. She danced abstract pieces to Hugo Ball’s phonetic poetry, in a costume and/or mask attributed in contemporary Dada sources to Arp. Ball described dances of flashes, fishbones, dazzling light, and penetrating intensity; another often quoted recollection compares her to a lark lifting into the sky. These are contemporaneous poetic testimonies, not technical notation.

In August 1917 she took part in Laban’s large **Sun Festival** at Monte Verità with Wigman, Perrottet, Wulff, and others. Dada performances and Laban performances must not be conflated, although the same dancers participated in both networks.

### The famous photograph: documented image, disputed event

An anonymous photograph shows a masked figure generally identified as Taeuber in a rigid, collage-like Cubo-Dada costume. It is the only widely accepted visual trace of her as a dancer. No surviving print has original documentation or a secure provenance. Captions have assigned it to the Cabaret Voltaire in 1916, the Galerie Dada opening in 1917, or simply Zurich in 1916–17. The mask and costume have been attributed variously to Arp and Marcel Janco.

Nell Andrew’s archival study shows that the photograph was probably posed rather than taken during a dark live performance. The Galerie Dada in 1917 has the strongest event-specific written documentation for Taeuber dancing formally in an Arp costume. The Fondation Arp in Clamart has sometimes maintained the Cabaret Voltaire/1916 identification; Walburga Krupp and the Rolandswerth archive favor a cautious Galerie Dada connection. The historically responsible caption is therefore **“figure thought to be Sophie Taeuber, Zurich, c. 1916–17; event, photographer, and costume attribution uncertain.”**

The popular story “Sophie Taeuber danced at the opening of the Cabaret Voltaire in this costume” is not proven. It emerged through retrospective captioning and became powerful because one image could embody the origin myth of Dada.

### Visual and applied work

Between 1916 and 1920 Taeuber created turned wooden bowls, chalices, containers, and other eccentric vessels, among them *Coupe Dada*, *Puderdose*, *Kelch*, and *Amphore*. She also made textiles, embroideries, beadwork, gouaches, and collages. The porous boundary between utilitarian object and autonomous sculpture was intrinsic to her practice.

In 1918 Alfred Altherr, director of her school, commissioned her to create stage designs, props, and marionettes for a modern adaptation of Carlo Gozzi’s *Il re cervo/König Hirsch* for the new Zurich puppet theatre during the Werkbund exhibition. The adaptation placed the story in a satirical modern setting and included psychoanalysis, a Dr. Ödipus figure, and the conflict between Freud and Jung. Approximately seventeen marionettes probably existed. Their turned and jointed geometric components produced both mechanical and expressive movement. The production ran only a few times, partly because of the influenza epidemic and limited theatre operation, but the surviving figures became among her best-known works.

From about 1918 to 1920 she produced a small series of painted turned-wood **Dada Heads**, including a 1918 portrait of Arp, an apparent 1919 self-portrait, and *Tête Dada* (1920). Their basis in hat stands and craft turning collapses portrait, sculpture, useful support, and toy-like object.

Her monumental 1918 *Triptyque I, II, III: Composition verticale-horizontale à triangles réciproques* combined geometric order, color, and a scale suggestive of religious painting. It complicates the later stereotype of Dada as purely nihilistic.

### Manifesto and groups

In 1918 she signed the Zurich Dada manifesto as G. Thauber. In 1918–20 she joined **Das Neue Leben**, alongside Janco, Arp, and others. Its program aimed to integrate abstract art with education and everyday life. She participated in its four exhibitions.

### Illness

A lung complaint required months of treatment at a sanatorium in Arosa around 1919–20. It is sometimes described simply as “lung disease”; the sources consulted do not provide a secure diagnosis of tuberculosis. Later work-related exhaustion also brought periods of medical care. There is no evidence in the consulted record of addiction, psychiatric hospitalization, or a sustained psychological disorder.

## Partnership with Hans/Jean Arp and the Authorship Problem

Taeuber and Arp made collages, textiles, drawings, reliefs, and sculptures together. The five large **Duo-Collages** of 1916–18 use equal rectangular units and chance arrangements of colored paper. Their authorship was deliberately shared, matching Dada’s suspicion of the solitary genius.

The later record is less equal. Arp claimed one Duo-Collage as his alone, and his posthumous writings sometimes presented geometric discovery as his own. Yet Taeuber’s dated vertical-horizontal studies preceded their meeting. STARP identifies this as a source of lasting attribution confusion.

Many works were unsigned. In 1948 Arp helped shape the first monograph and Hugo Weber catalogue, assigning titles and dates to much of her oeuvre. This preserved the work but also filtered it through the surviving husband. The current STARP catalogue records about 1,024 objects; a new SIK-ISEA catalogue raisonné estimates at least 1,200 and is reexamining provenance and collaboration.

The attribution problem is especially acute for turned wood sculptures of the late 1920s and 1930s. Taeuber made design drawings that craftspeople executed. More than ten sculptures were exhibited under Arp’s name at the Kunstmuseum/Kunsthalle Basel in 1932. *Un grand et deux petits* (1931) was long treated as a duo work; according to STARP, Taeuber’s co-authorship was removed around 2010 without a published explanation. *Sculpture conjugale* (1937) and *Jalon* (1938) are explicitly collaborative.

The evidence does not support either a simple story of Arp stealing everything or a frictionless “perfect couple.” It supports genuine collaboration, economic interdependence, mutual influence, and later cataloguing practices that disproportionately favored the already better-known husband.

## Travel, Marriage, and the 1920s

Taeuber and Arp traveled frequently in Switzerland, Germany, Italy, Spain, France, and the Baltic. In summer 1922 they met Max Ernst, Paul and Gala Éluard, and Tristan Tzara in the Tyrol. They married at Pura on 20 October. In 1923 they vacationed on Rügen with **Kurt Schwitters** and **Hannah Höch**. Such journeys were working networks as much as leisure.

Taeuber gradually withdrew from live dance; the reasons include the demands of teaching, health, and visual work. Movement remained evident in gouaches and later curved and circular compositions.

In 1922 she published **“Bemerkungen über den Unterricht im ornamentalen Entwerfen”** in the journal of the Swiss association of vocational and domestic-economy women teachers. She rejected fixed formulas for form and color and argued for attentive experience, internal necessity, and freedom within design education. It is her principal surviving theoretical statement.

In 1925 she represented Switzerland with **Johannes Itten** on the jury or Swiss section of the **Exposition Internationale des Arts Décoratifs et Industriels Modernes** in Paris. She exhibited tapestries and other works and received a prize. The sources consulted do not consistently specify the formal class or medal name, so it should not be inflated into a precisely named grand prize.

## Strasbourg, the Aubette, and Financial Independence, 1926–1929

### Citizenship and first commissions

The Arps established a Strasbourg base and obtained French citizenship. Through developers **André and Paul Horn**, Taeuber received interior commissions. She designed murals and stained glass for Paul Horn’s apartment, then the entrance and restaurant at **Hotel Hannong**. The Hannong mural was later destroyed by fire.

She also designed a mural for the entrance and staircase of the villa of Dr. Heimendinger, an ear, nose, and throat specialist treating her. Its repeated angular motif derived from stylized dancing figures. Other interiors included the Werner apartment in Paris and later work in Berlin.

### The Aubette

In 1926 the Horn brothers commissioned the transformation of the eighteenth-century **Aubette** building on Place Kléber into a modern entertainment complex containing cinema, dance spaces, restaurant, tearoom, bars, and billiards. Taeuber was the original artistic contact and brought in Arp and then **Theo van Doesburg**, whose architectural experience and De Stijl network were useful for the enormous commission.

Van Doesburg sought and partly obtained control over the project. Surviving publications and later histories often foregrounded his dramatic Ciné-Dancing room and reduced Taeuber to a secondary collaborator. Archival reconstruction credits her with the Five O’Clock tea room, Aubette Bar, billiard room, large foyer-bar, lighting, furniture, surfaces, and participation in the entrance, staircase, stained glass, and arcade floor. The floor’s geometric pattern has been compared to Laban notation.

The complex opened in 1928. Patrons and operators soon altered or covered its severe abstract interiors. Much was destroyed. Later descriptions called it a “Sistine Chapel of modern art,” a retrospective myth of a unified masterpiece. The actual project involved divided authorship, client compromise, commercial failure of some spaces, and rapid modification. Major rooms were reconstructed and reopened in Strasbourg in the twenty-first century, notably in 2006.

### Money and property

The exact fees are not publicly established in the consulted sources. Foundation records state that the Strasbourg commissions gave the Arps financial independence. In 1927 they used the earnings to buy land at **Clamart-Meudon/Meudon-Val-Fleury**, southwest of Paris. Taeuber designed a limestone studio-house, garden, built-in storage, and modular furniture. The house provided separate studios on different levels without doors that would impede exchange.

She continued exhausting commutes between Zurich, Strasbourg, and Paris while still teaching. Illness and overwork led to treatment, including a stay at Arosa with the Éluards. In 1929, once commissions and the house made independence possible, she resigned from the Zurich school and moved permanently to France.

## Paris and Clamart: International Abstraction, 1929–1936

The Clamart house became a center of the international avant-garde. Documented or reported visitors included Kurt Schwitters, Hans Richter, Alexander Calder, Theo and Nelly van Doesburg, Robert and Sonia Delaunay, Wassily and Nina Kandinsky, Piet Mondrian, Florence Henri, Max Ernst, Meret Oppenheim, Marcel Duchamp, Joan Miró, Gabrielle Buffet-Picabia, and Michel Seuphor. James Joyce and André Breton are also named in later foundation accounts, though individual visit dates are not always given.

**Gabrielle Buffet-Picabia** was a close friend and advocate. **Michel Seuphor**, impressed by work in Taeuber’s upstairs studio, invited her into **Cercle et Carré**. She exhibited with the group in 1930 and then worked with **Abstraction-Création** from 1931 to 1934, exhibiting and designing catalogues and invitations. Her role was not only that of member but of graphic organizer.

She contributed works to the international modern-art collection created by Polish artists for the **Muzeum Sztuki in Łódź** and traveled to the opening without Arp. She became friends with **Katarzyna Kobro, Władysław Strzemiński,** and **Henryk Stażewski**. The Łódź connection places her within a trans-European artist-built museum network, not simply a Paris school.

Her painting expanded from vertical-horizontal grids to diagonals, stepped forms, bars, broken crosses, circles, arcs, shells, and irregular rhythms. Important series and works include *Quatre espaces à croix brisée* (1932), *Échelonnement désaxé* (1934), *Cercles mouvementés* (1934), and the so-called “ping pictures.” She described herself in Paris as **artiste peintre**, but continued design, architecture, relief, and collaborative work.

In 1933 she designed the cover of the February issue of Eugene Jolas’s *transition*. In 1934 Anatole Jakovski published *Cinq artistes suisses*—Hans Erni, Hans Schiess, Kurt Seligmann, S. H. Taeuber-Arp, and Gérard Vulliamy—with Taeuber responsible for the layout.

### The Müller-Widmann patrons

**Oskar and Annie Müller-Widmann**, Basel collectors, became crucial patrons. In 1932 they asked Taeuber to design a house. A local architect subsequently incorporated her ideas into separate plans without permission and offered her only an advisory role. She rejected the arrangement and withdrew in 1933. No lawsuit is documented.

The Müller-Widmanns then provided a monthly stipend principally intended to support Arp. Taeuber handled bills, correspondence, social access, purchases, and much household administration. Thirty-five surviving letters from 1932–42 show a relationship that moved from formal patronage to friendship while retaining a financial basis. During the war, the stipend and requested food parcels became a lifeline.

## Mature Reliefs, Networks, and *Plastique/Plastic*, 1936–1939

From 1936 Taeuber made layered round and rectangular wooden reliefs, sometimes pierced so that the wall became part of the composition and sometimes projecting with cones, cylinders, and blocks. These works converted color and line into actual depth while retaining the logic of design and carpentry.

In 1937 she joined **Allianz**, founded by Max Bill and Leo Leuppi, and worked with **Richard Paul Lohse** and other Swiss concrete artists. The major **Konstruktivisten** exhibition at the Kunsthalle Basel in 1937 included twenty-four of her works. Marguerite Hagenbach bought *Cercles mouvementés*, beginning or consolidating a collection that later shaped the estate.

From 1937 to 1939 Taeuber directed the journal **Plastique/Plastic**, issued in Paris and New York. Early collaborators included Arp, **César Domela**, and American artists/collectors **A. E. Gallatin** and **George L. K. Morris**, whose money helped finance it. Five issues appeared. Taeuber managed editorial correspondence, solicited international contributions, designed or supervised layout and printing, distributed copies, and ultimately functioned as sole editor and distributor. Contributors and subjects connected European and American abstraction at a time when dictatorship, exile, and impending war were breaking artist networks apart.

The journal was not a commercial business in the usual sense. It was a subsidized intellectual and logistical project. Her letters include the self-aware complaint that she was always eager to undertake things that brought in no money. Plans for further issues with Bill and others ended with the war.

## War, Flight, and Grasse, 1939–1942

After Germany invaded France, the Arps left Clamart in June 1940, abandoning studios and much of fifteen years’ work. They stayed first with Gabrielle Buffet-Picabia at **Nérac**, then with **Peggy Guggenheim** at **Veyrier**, and finally, through Alberto and Susi Magnelli, at the **Château Folie in Grasse**.

At Grasse they formed an improvised working community with **Alberto Magnelli** and **Sonia Delaunay**; after Robert Delaunay died in 1941, Sonia stayed with them. Two or three artists worked successively on shared sheets. The collaborative gouaches were later issued as the 1950 portfolio *Dix originaux*; that publication is posthumous and should not be mistaken for a lifetime edition by Taeuber.

Material conditions deteriorated. Paint, canvas, and food became scarce. Taeuber worked with pencil, colored pencil, and paper, creating *Lignes géométriques et ondoyantes* and other linear constructions. Letters to Erika and Annie Müller-Widmann document refugee crowds, fear, Arp’s despair, the search for papers, hunger, and the daily work of obtaining food. Food parcels came from Erika, Marguerite Hagenbach, and patrons.

The Arps planned to emigrate to the United States but failed to secure all necessary documents. The sources do not indicate political-party membership, intelligence work, resistance operations, arrest, or collaboration. Their documented political situation was that of avant-garde artists and refugees fleeing Nazi occupation. Some Grasse works have been interpreted as anti-fascist, and Dada itself arose in an antiwar milieu, but Taeuber did not leave a conventional political manifesto or party program.

She and Nelly van Doesburg planned an encyclopedia of Concrete artists; war prevented it. When German forces occupied southern France in November 1942, the Arps escaped to Switzerland. Sophie stayed with Erika in Zurich; Hans stayed with Max and Binia Bill. Former colleagues, including Johannes Itten, welcomed and honored her at an exhibition opening.

## Death, January 1943

The usual reconstruction says that Taeuber visited or celebrated with friends at the home of **Max and Binia Bill** in Zurich-Höngg, missed the last tram, and slept in a garden or summer house heated by a stove. Carbon monoxide accumulated and killed her in her sleep.

What is documented across the foundation traditions is accidental carbon-monoxide poisoning at the Bills’ home. What remains uncertain is the exact night, the type and handling of the stove, who last attended it, and the detailed official findings. STARP expressly says the circumstances remain largely unexplained. No evidence located for this dossier supports murder, suicide, or criminal negligence. Those possibilities should not be inferred merely from the incomplete file.

The death was less than a week before her fifty-fourth birthday. Her final series, nine geometric ink-and-pencil drawings, was made only weeks earlier and published in Zurich later in 1943 as *Les derniers 9 dessins de Sophie Taeuber-Arp*. Arp entered a severe creative crisis and did not resume sculpture in the round for about four years. He processed her death in poems and memorial texts.

She was ultimately buried with Arp and Marguerite Arp-Hagenbach at Locarno.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Employment and institutional offices

- Head/instructor of textile design, embroidery, weaving, composition, and ornamental design at the Zurich Kunstgewerbeschule/Gewerbeschule, 1916–29.
- Member, Schweizerischer Werkbund, 1915–31/32.
- Member, Das Neue Leben, 1918–20.
- Swiss juror/representative, Paris decorative-arts exposition, 1925.
- Member and organizer, Cercle et Carré and Abstraction-Création, 1930–34.
- Member, Allianz, from 1937.
- Founder/director/editor/distributor, *Plastique/Plastic*, 1937–39.

### Major commissions and properties

- Textile and applied-art products sold in Zurich, including through the Wiener Werkstätte branch.
- *König Hirsch* marionettes, scenery, and props, Zurich, 1918.
- Horn apartment, Hotel Hannong, Heimendinger villa, and other Strasbourg interiors, 1926–28.
- Aubette entertainment complex, Strasbourg, 1926–28, with Arp and van Doesburg.
- Arp house and studios, 21 rue des Châtaigniers, Clamart/Meudon-Val-Fleury, designed 1927–28 and occupied from 1929; now the Fondation Arp.
- Werner apartment and other Paris and Berlin interiors, 1929–35.
- Unexecuted Müller-Widmann house project, Basel, 1932–33.

### Technical practices

Her “technologies” were material and spatial rather than patented inventions: textile grid and repeat, embroidery and weaving, beadwork, wood turning, modular furniture, layered relief, cut-out wall integration, stained glass, mural systems, marionette joints, Laban-related spatial notation, offset/lithographic magazine production, and collaborative chance procedures. No patents or incorporated businesses have been found.

### Finances

The salary from Zurich sustained the household through 1929. Strasbourg fees financed the Clamart land and house and created temporary independence. Patrons supplied stipends and purchases in the 1930s; American artists financed *Plastique*. Wartime letters document hunger and dependency on stipends and parcels. No complete asset statement, salary table, bank record, inheritance inventory, or exact commission ledger was located. Claims that she was either independently wealthy throughout or destitute throughout are both inaccurate.

Her posthumous market is unrelated to her lifetime income. In April 2025 Christie’s Paris sold the 1936 *Relief rond en quatre hauteurs, éléments courbes, coupant(s), cassant(s)* for approximately €3.06 million/US$3.34 million, then an auction record; a *Dada Head* had set a prior reported record in 2003. These prices accrued to later owners, not to Taeuber.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Politics

Taeuber’s most documentable political contexts were wartime exile, antiwar Zurich Dada, the egalitarian program of applied art, and the preservation of international abstract networks against fascist cultural repression. She signed the 1918 Dada manifesto, but no evidence places her in a political party or armed movement. Labels such as anarchist, communist, pacifist, or anti-fascist may describe aspects of circles around her or later interpretations; they should not be turned into a formal affiliation without a document.

### Religion and spirituality

No stable denominational or occult affiliation is established in the consulted record. Her 1918 triptych, the ritual atmosphere of Laban’s Monte Verità festivals, Ball’s mystical language, and her attention to inner necessity have encouraged spiritual readings. These concern artistic form and milieu, not proof of a particular creed. Later descriptions of her masked dance as “shamanic” are interpretive and often depend on uncertain costume attribution.

### Artistic ideas

Her durable principles were:

1. No absolute hierarchy between useful and autonomous art.
2. Form and color cannot be reduced to fixed rules.
3. Geometry can be generated directly rather than abstracted from visible nature.
4. Rhythm joins textile, dance, graphic composition, sculpture, and architecture.
5. Beauty in everyday objects is not merely a way to raise monetary value.
6. System and intuition, precision and deviation, can coexist.

Her development was cumulative rather than a march from “craft” to “pure art.” Textiles supplied the grid; dance supplied spatial and rhythmic knowledge; marionettes made geometry move; rooms enlarged composition to architectural scale; reliefs brought the wall and viewer into the work; wartime drawings reduced the vocabulary to mobile lines.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Financial Questions

No evidence was found of criminal charges, convictions, fraud, corruption, money laundering, political violence, intelligence connections, abuse allegations, imprisonment, or addiction. The meaningful controversies are art-historical and documentary:

### 1. Attribution between Taeuber and Arp

**Documented:** unsigned works, shared methods, collaborative objects, posthumous titles, and sculptures shown under Arp’s name despite Taeuber designs.

**Disputed:** which partner originated or executed particular Duo-Collages and wood sculptures. Current catalogue projects are reassessing them.

**Unproven simplification:** that Arp intentionally stole the entire oeuvre. The record shows some self-serving claims and structural bias, but also extensive consensual collaboration and his preservation of her work.

### 2. Aubette authorship

**Documented:** Taeuber obtained the connection, designed multiple rooms, and invited van Doesburg; van Doesburg sought overall control.

**Later distortion:** histories centered van Doesburg and treated her as assistant. Restorations and new scholarship have reconstructed her independent rooms.

### 3. Müller-Widmann house

**Documented through letters:** a local architect used or incorporated her designs without permission and offered her a reduced role; she quit.

**Not documented:** lawsuit, court decision, or monetary settlement.

### 4. Death

**Established at the level of consistent contemporary tradition:** death by carbon monoxide at the Bills’ property.

**Unresolved:** exact date-night sequence and stove circumstances.

**No evidence found:** homicide, suicide, prosecution, or official finding of criminal culpability.

### 5. Market and estate

Multiple foundations and later heirs have controlled works, copyright, casts, and archives. The Stiftung Arp, Fondation Arp, and Fondazione Marguerite Arp preserve different holdings. Foundation history records unresolved French taxes as one reason for creating a French foundation. No evidence connects Sophie herself to estate misconduct.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Myth 1: “She danced Dada at the Cabaret Voltaire in the famous photograph”

1. **Story:** Taeuber performed at the Cabaret Voltaire in 1916 wearing the photographed mask and Cubist costume.
2. **Appearance and circulation:** captions from later dance and art scholarship, foundation reproductions, museum displays, Google Arts & Culture, press accounts, and popular Dada histories.
3. **Evidence for:** she was connected to the Cabaret milieu and danced in Dada events; the costume resembles other Zurich Dada constructions.
4. **Evidence against/limits:** no print has event provenance; no contemporary source attaches the photograph to a specific Cabaret night; the best-documented costumed solo is Galerie Dada, 29 March 1917; the image was probably posed.
5. **Effect:** it made her body an icon of Dada while paradoxically obscuring her choreography and allowing the image to be discussed mainly as a Janco or Arp costume.

### Myth 2: “She was only Jean Arp’s wife or muse”

1. **Story:** her significance derives from Arp and Dada.
2. **Origin:** male-centered movement histories, fine-art hierarchies that discounted textile and design, and the husband-shaped 1948 catalogue.
3. **Evidence against:** professional training, salaried leadership, geometric work before meeting Arp, major independent architecture, international groups, teaching, and editorial direction.
4. **Complication:** Arp did advocate her work, collaborate with her, and preserve it; erasing him reverses rather than solves the distortion.
5. **Effect:** delayed museum recognition and made recent recovery narratives unusually dramatic.

### Myth 3: “Perfect egalitarian artist-couple”

1. **Story:** Sophie and Hans dissolved authorship into a harmonious “community of two.”
2. **Circulation:** Arp memorials, couple exhibitions, the shared grave, and the language of *Zweiklang* (“duet”).
3. **Evidence for:** genuine shared works, open studios, long marriage, mutual stylistic exchange.
4. **Evidence against/limits:** she carried stable income and household paperwork; works migrated toward his name; her independent contribution was later obscured.
5. **Effect:** preserved a moving love-and-art legend but sometimes concealed labor and power asymmetries.

### Myth 4: “Pure abstraction from mathematics alone”

1. **Story:** Taeuber arrived at immaculate geometry through detached rational calculation.
2. **Evidence:** careful grids, circles, modular systems, and Concrete-art associations.
3. **Counterevidence:** textile technique, dance, humor, shells, flowers, Indigenous objects, chance, bodily movement, asymmetry, and intuitive variation all entered the work.
4. **Effect:** enabled her canonization as a precursor of Swiss Concrete art while narrowing her material and cultural sources.

### Myth 5: “Joyful Dada, without politics”

Her bright color and play have been contrasted with aggressive male Dada. This is a critical construction, not a neutral fact. Her work was made amid industrial war, exile, antiwar cabaret, and later fascist repression. Yet there is also no warrant for turning every grid into a coded partisan statement. The political importance lies in institutions, conditions, and practice as much as explicit iconography.

### Myth 6: Exotic or “shamanic” dancer

Masks, Monte Verità ritual, and early Indigenous objects have encouraged descriptions of the dance as shamanic or primitivist. Such language comes largely from European avant-garde projection. The photographed mask’s maker is disputed, its cultural source is not securely identified, and Taeuber left no claim to be performing an Indigenous rite. The myth is historically significant as evidence of Dada’s primitivist imagination, not as ethnographic fact.

### Myth 7: Mysterious death

The conflicting date and incomplete public circumstances invite speculation. The responsible conclusion is a poorly documented domestic accident, not a solved conspiracy. “Mystery” accurately describes missing detail, not evidence of foul play.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Arp, Emmy Hennings, Max Bill, Kandinsky, Duchamp, Alberto Magnelli, Gabrielle Buffet-Picabia, and others wrote memorial appreciations. The 1948 monograph preserved a corpus but framed it through Arp and categorized a highly interdisciplinary oeuvre within conventional media.

For decades, she occupied an unstable position: famous enough to appear in Dada histories and museum collections, but described as Arp’s wife, a craft artist, or a minor geometric modernist. The causes included gender, the low status assigned to textiles and design, destroyed interiors, live performance’s disappearance, small-scale works, unsigned objects, and attribution to male collaborators.

Recovery proceeded through the 1954 Bern exhibition, documenta 1 in 1955, the Paris exhibition of 1964, Staber’s 1970 monograph, the 1981 MoMA traveling retrospective, centenary exhibitions in 1989, architectural reconstruction of the Aubette, textile and feminist art history, and the opening of foundation archives.

Her image on the Swiss 50-franc note from 1995 made her one of the country’s most widely circulated artist portraits. The Arp Museum opened in 2007, while the Clamart house and Locarno foundation preserved domestic and estate contexts. The 2021–22 Basel–London–New York retrospective assembled more than 300, and in some counts more than 400, objects across media and decisively re-situated her within international modernism.

Her influence is visible in later Concrete art, Op art, textile abstraction, modular and participatory design, postwar relief, interdisciplinary performance, artist-designed interiors, and contemporary efforts to undo the art/craft hierarchy. Specific artists often associated with her legacy include Max Bill, Elsi Giauque, Swiss concrete artists, textile modernists such as Anni Albers, and later geometric and feminist practitioners. Direct influence must be distinguished from later affinity unless documentary contact exists.

The current historical reinterpretation replaces a linear story—craftswoman becomes painter—with a networked one: textiles, dance, teaching, objects, typography, architecture, and painting were simultaneous manifestations of rhythmic construction. It also replaces the myth of an isolated female genius with evidence of a materially skilled organizer embedded in patronage, marriage, schools, magazines, and refugee networks.

## Atlas Connections

### André Breton

- **[T5]** **Reported fact:** Breton was among the avant-garde writers received by Taeuber-Arp and Jean Arp at their Clamart studio-house during the 1930s. Available institutional accounts do not supply a date or describe a specific conversation, so the connection should be treated as a reported personal visit rather than a documented collaboration. [Fondation Arp site guide](https://litterature-lieux.com/fr/guide/lieu/19-fondation-jean-arp-l-atelier-de-jean-arp-et-sophie-taeuber)

### James Joyce

- **[T5]** **Documented fact:** Joyce visited the Arps’ studio-house at Clamart and appears with Taeuber-Arp, Jean Arp, Meret Oppenheim, Max Ernst, and others in a garden photograph dated approximately 1933–34. The evidence establishes personal proximity at the house but not a sustained friendship or artistic collaboration. [Fondation Arp house study](https://blogs.mediapart.fr/guillaume-lasserre/blog/101224/habiter-latelier-sophie-taeuber-et-jean-arp)

### Johannes Itten

- **[T5]** **Documented fact:** Taeuber-Arp and Itten jointly represented Switzerland on the jury or Swiss section of the 1925 Exposition Internationale des Arts Décoratifs et Industriels Modernes in Paris. After her return to Zurich in late 1942, Itten was also among the former colleagues and friends who personally welcomed and honored her at an exhibition opening at the Kunstgewerbemuseum. [STARP biography](https://sophietaeuberarp.org/english/biografie/)

### Katja Wulff

- **[T5]** **Documented fact:** Wulff and Taeuber-Arp belonged to Rudolf von Laban’s Zurich movement school. From 1915 onward Taeuber-Arp developed dances in direct exchange with Wulff and Mary Wigman, establishing personal pedagogical and artistic contact rather than merely parallel membership in expressionist-dance circles. [Recent scholarly exhibition publication](https://www.wienand-verlag.de/out/media/9783868326345.pdf)

### Mary Wigman

- **[T5]** **Documented fact:** Taeuber-Arp studied movement in Zurich and at Ascona/Monte Verità with Laban and Wigman beginning in 1915 and developed her own dances through exchanges with Wigman and Katja Wulff. Wigman and Taeuber-Arp also participated in Laban’s August 1917 Sun Festival at Monte Verità. [STARP biography](https://sophietaeuberarp.org/english/biografie/), [scholarly exhibition publication](https://www.wienand-verlag.de/out/media/9783868326345.pdf)

### Rudolf Laban

- **[T5]** **Documented fact:** Beginning in summer 1915, Taeuber-Arp attended Laban’s movement courses in Zurich and Ascona/Monte Verità and participated in early work toward his dance-notation system. She later took part in Laban’s Sun Festival at Monte Verità in August 1917. This was a direct teacher–student and performance relationship. [STARP biography](https://sophietaeuberarp.org/english/biografie/)

### Suzanne Perrottet

- **[T4]** **Documented fact:** Perrottet and Taeuber-Arp trained and performed within Laban’s Zurich–Monte Verità movement school and were both participants in the August 1917 Sun Festival at Monte Verità. The evidence securely places them in the same working and performance milieu; the sources consulted do not establish correspondence or a separately documented close friendship.

### Wassily Kandinsky

- **[T5]** **Documented fact:** After Kandinsky and Nina Kandinsky settled in France in 1933, they became friends with Taeuber-Arp and Jean Arp and visited the couple’s Clamart studio-house. Taeuber-Arp and Kandinsky also exhibited within the same international abstraction networks during the 1930s. [Arp Museum](https://arpmuseum.org/en/museum/museum/the-arps.html), [STARP exhibition archive](https://sophietaeuberarp.org/english/exhibitions/)
- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** After Taeuber-Arp’s death in 1943, Kandinsky wrote a memorial assessment of her geometric reliefs for the 1948 monograph *Sophie Taeuber-Arp*. On Taeuber-Arp’s dossier she receives the plus sign as the earlier, written-about figure. [MoMA’s digitized 1948 catalogue](https://www.moma.org/documents/moma_catalogue_235_300062660.pdf)

No evidence sufficient for the specified Atlas typology was located for the remaining roster members. In particular, later comparisons with Anni Albers, references to Freud and Jung in the *König Hirsch* production, and participation by Taeuber-Arp and other roster artists in broad group exhibitions do not by themselves establish a personal, discursive, prophetic, or shared-object connection.
