# Emanuel Swedenborg: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 24 August 2026  
**Scope:** Life, work, networks, controversies, visions, documentary afterlife, and reception of Emanuel Swedenborg (1688–1772).  
**Evidence note:** Dates before Sweden adopted the Gregorian calendar in 1753 can appear in Old Style and New Style forms. Swedenborg's birth is conventionally given as **29 January 1688 Old Style** (9 February New Style). Visionary events are reported as Swedenborg's claims unless independently documented. Later diagnoses are retrospective hypotheses, not clinical findings.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name at birth:** Emanuel Swedberg (also Svedberg in modernized or variant spelling).
- **Later name:** Emanuel Swedenborg, after the ennoblement of his father and siblings in 1719; the family was introduced at the Swedish House of Nobility as noble family no. 1598.
- **Latinized forms:** Emanuel Swedenborgius; in Latin title pages usually *Emanuel Swedenborg*.
- **Self-description in the late theological period:** servant of the Lord Jesus Christ; he did not claim priestly office and did not found a church during his lifetime.
- **Born:** 29 January 1688 Old Style (9 February Gregorian), Stockholm, Sweden.
- **Died:** 29 March 1772, London, England, aged 84.
- **Burial:** Swedish Church, Princes Square, Shadwell, London, 5 April 1772; remains removed in 1908 and reinterred in Uppsala Cathedral in 1910. Some popular accounts give 1912/13, but contemporary transfer history and cathedral records favor 1908 removal/1910 reinterment; the discrepancy should be retained as a bibliographic caution.
- **Nationality/status:** Swedish subject; nobleman; civil servant; member of the House of Nobility; natural philosopher, mining and metallurgical administrator, engineer, mathematician, anatomist, physiologist, biblical exegete, visionary, and religious author.
- **Principal residences:** Stockholm; Uppsala (childhood and university); intermittent residence with family at Brunsbo near Skara; repeated long stays in London, Amsterdam, The Hague, Paris and other European cities; later a house and extensive garden on Hornsgatan, Södermalm, Stockholm.
- **Marriage and children:** never married; no documented children.

The basic documentary outline is supported by Swedenborg's letters and official memorials, collected in Alfred Acton's *Letters and Memorials*, and by contemporary testimony assembled by R. L. Tafel. The [Swedenborg Society biography](https://www.swedenborg.org.uk/about-us/about-swedenborg/) and [Swedenborg Foundation life chronology](https://swedenborg.com/emanuel-swedenborg/about-life/) provide accessible institutional summaries; both are affiliated with Swedenborgian organizations and are used here alongside independent and primary material.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Jesper Swedberg (1653–1735)**, Emanuel's father, came from the Falun mining region. He became court chaplain, professor of theology and rector at Uppsala University, and from 1702 bishop of Skara. Jesper's preaching stressed lived piety, providence, angels and spirits. He was accused by opponents of Pietist tendencies but remained within the Lutheran establishment. Emanuel later differed sharply from orthodox Lutheran doctrine, yet the father's supernatural realism and insistence on an active spiritual world were formative.

**Sara Behm (1666–1696)**, Emanuel's mother, was the daughter of Albrecht Behm, an assessor in the Board of Mines, and belonged to a prosperous mining and official family. Her property and kin connections linked Emanuel early to Sweden's metallurgical-administrative elite. She died when he was eight. Jesper married **Sara Bergia** in 1697; she became Emanuel's stepmother and brought additional wealth. Her estate later produced difficult inheritance and property arrangements among the siblings.

### Siblings and extended network

Emanuel was one of several surviving Swedberg children. The most consequential sibling relationship was with **Anna Swedberg**, who married **Erik Benzelius the Younger** (1675–1743), librarian, scholar, later archbishop of Uppsala. Emanuel lived in the Benzelius household during his student years. Benzelius became patron, correspondent and conduit to European learned networks. Other siblings included Albert, Hedvig, Daniel, Eliezer and Catharina; exact enumerations vary because some children died young and older sources number Emanuel differently among births versus surviving children.

The family's ennoblement in 1719 was a reward for Bishop Swedberg's service rather than for Emanuel alone. It gave Emanuel a hereditary seat in the Riddarhuset and changed the siblings' surname to Swedenborg. The noble line through Emanuel ended because he never married; collateral branches continued through siblings.

### Courtship and celibacy

The best-known marriage episode concerns **Emerentia Polhem (1703–1760)**, daughter of inventor Christopher Polhem. A recollection recorded decades later by her brother Gabriel says Swedenborg became strongly attached to the teenage Emerentia while working with Polhem at Trollhättan. Polhem allegedly induced her to sign a promise of future marriage; distressed, she resisted, and Swedenborg eventually returned or relinquished the document. This is not evidence of a consummated engagement in the modern sense. The story comes from late family testimony and was amplified by biographers. Swedenborg never married. Later admirers interpreted his celibacy through his doctrine of eternal conjugial love; hostile writers used it to speculate about repression. His 1743–44 dream journal contains candid erotic imagery and struggles over desire, but establishes neither a sexual partnership nor a diagnosis. See [Sigstedt's documentary discussion](https://swedenborgdigitallibrary.org/ES/epic7.htm) and the [Swedenborg Society essay on the erotic dreams](https://swedenborgsociety.wordpress.com/2013/03/20/the-erotic-dreams-of-emanuel-swedenborg-by-richard-lines/).

## Childhood and Early Life, 1688–1709

The family moved from Stockholm to Uppsala in 1692/93 when Jesper took his professorship. Emanuel grew up inside the university-clerical world. At eleven he matriculated at Uppsala University, a normal early enrollment for a boy of his class rather than proof that he immediately followed an adult curriculum. After Jesper left for Skara, Emanuel remained with Anna and Erik Benzelius.

His education embraced Latin, Greek, mathematics, astronomy, mechanics, natural philosophy, poetry and some medicine. Professors associated with his formation include **Johan Upmarck Rosenadler** in rhetoric, **Petrus Elvius** in mathematics, and **Harald Vallerius** in astronomy; Benzelius was the decisive mentor. Swedenborg's dissertation, *Selectae sententiae L. Annaei Senecae et Publilii Syri mimi* (1709), edited moral sayings from Seneca and Publilius Syrus. A collection of Latin verse, *Ludus Heliconius*, followed in 1710.

The Great Northern War, plague, and Sweden's fiscal-military state formed the background. His later engineering was never merely amateur invention: it was tied to mines, canals, fortifications, transport, currency, and state survival.

## Education, Grand Tour, and Formation, 1710–1715

With family support, Swedenborg left Sweden in 1710. He survived a hazardous voyage to England during war and quarantine. London became his most important intellectual school. He lodged with artisans, learned instrument-making and practical crafts, studied Newtonian astronomy and mathematics, visited the Royal Society's orbit, and sought access to John Flamsteed's Greenwich observations. Claims that he personally studied under Newton are unsupported; he absorbed Newtonian and post-Newtonian science through books, instrument makers and learned contacts.

He moved through Oxford and perhaps Cambridge, then the Netherlands, France and German territories. His correspondence with Benzelius is full of proposed devices: a submarine, flying machine, universal musical instrument, air pump, pumps and sluices, a rapid-fire weapon, and methods for determining longitude. Most remained designs, not constructed inventions or patents. The journey taught him printing, publishing, mechanics and the organization of European science.

His long absence was partly involuntary: war and finances complicated return. He came home in 1715 with manuscripts, scientific ambitions and a need for patronage.

## Early Career: Polhem, Charles XII, and the Board of Mines, 1715–1721

Swedenborg allied with **Christopher Polhem (1661–1751)**, Sweden's leading mechanical engineer. From 1716 to 1718 he edited and financed six issues of *Daedalus Hyperboreus*, commonly called Sweden's first scientific periodical. It publicized Polhem's machines and Swedenborg's mathematical, mechanical and astronomical projects. He also issued the first Swedish-language algebra text and worked on lunar methods for longitude.

In 1716 Charles XII appointed him **assessor extraordinary** in the Royal Board of Mines (*Bergskollegium*). The office at first carried status more reliably than salary, and senior officials resisted an irregular royal appointment. Swedenborg inspected mines and works, learned ore testing, smelting and mining law, and eventually became a salaried ordinary assessor. His expertise grew from administrative fieldwork as well as reading.

During Charles XII's Norwegian campaigns, Swedenborg and Polhem worked on the Trollhättan canal and military transport. The celebrated claim that Swedenborg moved ships overland for the 1718 siege of Fredriksten is broadly grounded in engineering operations but later heroic accounts exaggerate his sole responsibility. He was one organizer within a state project using rollers, capstans, manpower and prepared roads.

Charles XII's death in November 1718 ended the absolutist phase. Queen Ulrika Eleonora ennobled the Swedberg siblings in 1719. Swedenborg thereafter sat in the House of Nobility during Riksdags of the Age of Liberty.

## Scientific and Administrative Career, 1721–1743

### Publication and travel, 1721–22

In Amsterdam and Leipzig he published a cluster of works in Latin: chemistry and corpuscular physics, metallurgy, algebra and geometry, geological observations, and a new method for longitude. The *Prodromus principiorum rerum naturalium* tried to explain chemical processes geometrically through particles and motion. *Miscellanea de rebus naturalibus* assembled geological and mineral observations. These were ambitious syntheses, sometimes ingenious and often speculative.

### Board of Mines and practical expertise

Swedenborg traveled to Swedish and European mines, furnaces and mints, produced reports, judged technical disputes and advised the state. He did not own a great mining company and no major patent portfolio is documented. His economic base combined official salary, inherited property, investment income, later pension and the ability to self-finance expensive books. His maternal/stepmaternal estates and noble-professional network matter more than a modern entrepreneurial narrative.

In 1724 Uppsala offered him a mathematics professorship. He declined, citing the broad and practical character of his work and, according to later reports, a speech impediment or stammer that made lecturing unattractive. He continued at the Board of Mines.

### *Opera philosophica et mineralia* (1734)

His scientific monument was the three-volume *Opera philosophica et mineralia*, printed in Dresden and Leipzig:

1. *Principia rerum naturalium* proposed a dynamic, vortical cosmology in which finite reality develops through ordered stages from a first natural point.
2. *Regnum subterraneum sive minerale de ferro* treated iron.
3. *Regnum subterraneum sive minerale de cupro et orichalco* treated copper and brass.

The metallurgical volumes compiled processes from Sweden and Europe and became useful reference works. The cosmology contains a nebular-like account of solar and planetary formation, but claims that Swedenborg straightforwardly “discovered” the modern nebular hypothesis or anticipated Kant–Laplace should be qualified: resemblance exists, yet the conceptual and mathematical settings differ. The [Foundation survey of his scientific writings](https://swedenborg.com/emanuel-swedenborg/scientific-writings/) is useful for titles; modern historical assessment is less triumphalist.

### Search for the soul

After cosmology and metals, Swedenborg turned to anatomy and physiology. He studied current literature and attended dissections during European travel. *Oeconomia regni animalis* (1740–41) and *Regnum animale* (1744–45) sought the soul through the organization of blood, brain, nerves and organs. He proposed functional hierarchies in the nervous system, emphasized the cerebral cortex, and made suggestive remarks later compared to neuron theory, cortical localization and cerebrospinal circulation.

Modern neuroscientists have credited genuine anticipations while noting that he did not experimentally establish the neuron doctrine and that much of his anatomy was derivative or speculative. The most defensible claim is that he was an unusually synthetic eighteenth-century neuroanatomical theorist whose unpublished manuscripts were rediscovered after modern neuroscience had developed independently. See [Tubbs et al., *Child's Nervous System*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21424557/) and the Royal Academy archive history summarized by [Riksbankens Jubileumsfond](https://www.rj.se/en/grants/2006/the-swedenborg-archives-at-the-royal-swedish-academy-of-sciences-a-cataloguing-project/).

## Political Activity and Economic Thought

As a nobleman, Swedenborg participated in Riksdags and submitted memorials on currency, exchange, mining, trade, taxation and foreign policy. He was neither party chief nor minister. Older Swedenborgian biographies call him democratic; more precisely, he worked within the estate system while arguing that public welfare, productive industry and stable money should constrain private and factional advantage.

His most sustained economic interventions came during the inflation and exchange crises around 1760–62. He blamed unfavorable exchange partly on excessive paper credit, imports and banking practices, advocated metallic discipline and policies to restore confidence, and criticized secretive financial manipulation. Some proposals were prescient, others reflected mercantilist assumptions. The [study of his House of Nobility memorials](https://www.newchurchhistory.org/articles/awa1942.php) reproduces the structure of his November 1760 memorial; the primary corpus is in [*Letters and Memorials*](https://newchristianbiblestudy.org/bundles/ncbsw/on-deck/english/books/Letters%20and%20Memorials.html).

He favored cautious foreign policy after Sweden's military disasters and opposed policies that risked renewed war with Russia. Schuchard's later claim that he operated through Jacobite, Masonic and diplomatic intelligence networks is discussed below; ordinary parliamentary and official activity is documented independently of that thesis.

## The Crisis and Transformation, 1743–1747

### Dream journal

On 21 July 1743 Swedenborg left Stockholm for another research and publishing journey through Ystad, Stralsund, Hamburg, Bremen and the Netherlands, then London. His private journal, rediscovered in the Royal Library in the 1850s and first published in 1859, records travel, dreams, prayer, bodily sensations, erotic images, terror, exaltation, humiliation and repeated efforts to subordinate intellectual ambition to God. It is the closest primary record of his transformation. A scan of the early English edition is available through the [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/download/emanuelswedenbor00swed/emanuelswedenbor00swed.pdf), and a text presentation begins with the dated itinerary at the [New Christian Bible Study archive](https://newchristianbiblestudy.org/nl/exposition/translation/swedenborgs-journal-of-dreams-wilkinson/).

The crisis peaked around Easter 1744. He described trembling, prostration, luminous and auditory experiences, temptation, sexual symbolism and a sense of direct divine presence. The diary does not provide a single clean “conversion date.” Swedenborg's later retrospective accounts variously locate divine appearances or the opening of spiritual sight in 1743, 1744 or 1745.

### The London commission story

The canonical later narrative says that in 1745, while dining alone at a London inn, he experienced darkness, saw a figure who told him not to eat too much, and later saw the same figure identified as the Lord, commissioning him to disclose Scripture's spiritual sense. The fullest early source is not a contemporaneous diary entry but **Carl Robsahm's** memoir, based on conversations with Swedenborg and written/published after Swedenborg's death. Therefore the encounter is securely part of Swedenborg's narrated self-understanding, not independently verified apparition.

He abandoned the projected seventeen-volume anatomical system, left *De cultu et amore Dei* incomplete, studied Hebrew anew, and began large biblical indexes and verse-by-verse exegesis. In 1747 he requested release from the Board of Mines. The crown accepted his resignation honorably and granted half salary as pension. He was not dismissed for incompetence or madness.

## Visionary-Theological Career, 1747–1771

### Working method and publication

Swedenborg maintained disciplined routines: Hebrew and Bible study, vast indexes, Latin composition, revisions, and self-financed printing in London or the Netherlands, partly beyond Swedish censorship. He claimed that his spiritual senses were continuously opened and that he conversed with angels, devils and the dead while remaining active in ordinary society. The documentary fact is the production of thousands of manuscript pages and printed volumes; the ontological status of the claimed encounters is unresolved by historical method.

*Arcana Coelestia* (London, 1749–56), issued anonymously in eight quarto volumes, interprets Genesis and Exodus through correspondences and inserts extensive reports of heaven, hell and the soul. Sales were poor. Swedenborg distributed copies and bore the cost. In 1758 he published five concise syntheses, including *De caelo et ejus mirabilibus et de inferno* (*Heaven and Hell*), which became his best-known work.

### Core doctrines

- **One God in Jesus Christ:** he rejected a Trinity of three divine persons, teaching a trinity of soul, body and proceeding activity within one divine-human Lord.
- **Scripture and correspondences:** biblical language contains a spiritual sense systematically corresponding to realities of mind and heaven. He did not treat every biblical book as equally part of the inspired “Word.”
- **Human freedom and regeneration:** salvation involves freely turning from evils as sins and living charity; he opposed an imputed salvation by faith alone.
- **Heaven and hell:** people enter communities matching their ruling loves; God does not arbitrarily sentence them. Angels are once-human beings, not a separately created species.
- **Marriage:** the union of love and wisdom has an eternal conjugial form. His *Delitiae sapientiae de amore conjugiali* (1768) also contains hierarchical eighteenth-century sexual assumptions and a controversial regulated-toleration discussion of concubinage under limited conditions.
- **Last Judgment and Second Coming:** he claimed the Last Judgment occurred in the spiritual world in 1757 and that the Second Coming was a disclosure of spiritual truth, not a bodily return in the clouds.
- **Other planets:** he reported spirits from the Moon, planets and worlds beyond the solar system. These passages were presented as experiential reports, not astronomical observation, and are contradicted by modern planetary science where they imply inhabited nearby planets.
- **Africa and slavery:** Swedenborg condemned slavery and represented Africans as spiritually receptive, positions that influenced some abolitionist followers. Yet he also essentialized “Africans” through hierarchical racial-spiritual generalizations. A modern study, [“Swedenborg on African superiority, evangelization, and slavery”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17496977.2023.2192585), reconstructs both the anti-slavery implications and the racial typology.

## Important Relationships and Networks

- **Erik Benzelius the Younger:** brother-in-law, university mentor, correspondent, and early scientific patron.
- **Christopher Polhem:** engineering mentor, collaborator and at times frustrated senior partner; their relationship combined admiration, technical work, publishing disputes and the failed Emerentia courtship.
- **Charles XII:** royal patron who appointed Swedenborg to the Board of Mines; not an intimate friend.
- **Royal Swedish Board of Mines:** core employer, 1716–47; colleagues initially resisted his extraordinary appointment but later recognized his service.
- **Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences:** Swedenborg became a member in 1740. The Academy later received his manuscripts.
- **Carl Robsahm:** banker/official, friend and memoirist; important but retrospective source for habits, visions and personality.
- **Count Anders Johan von Höpken:** statesman and Academy figure who defended Swedenborg's learning while not endorsing all supernatural claims.
- **Gabriel Andersson Beyer and Johan Rosén:** Gothenburg academics whose teaching of Swedenborgian ideas triggered ecclesiastical proceedings.
- **Friedrich Christoph Oetinger:** German Pietist theologian and early interpreter; promoted translations and drew censorship.
- **Thomas Hartley:** Anglican clergyman, translator and English supporter; recipient of Swedenborg's important 1769 autobiographical letter.
- **John Clowes:** English translator and later organizer of Swedenborgian reading.
- **John Wesley:** Methodist leader. The story that Swedenborg supernaturally knew Wesley wanted a meeting and predicted his own death rests on later Wesleyan and Swedenborgian testimony; correspondence establishes interest but the paranormal inference remains unverified.
- **Immanuel Kant:** investigated reports about Swedenborg, initially expressed guarded interest in a private letter, bought *Arcana Coelestia*, then satirically attacked visionary metaphysics in *Träume eines Geistersehers* (1766). Scholarship disputes whether the book masks continued philosophical debt.
- **Queen Louisa Ulrika:** central to the “queen's secret” story; her own later recollection circulated through courtiers and German literary testimony.

## Property, Finances, Institutions, Technologies, and Projects

### Income and wealth

No complete modern balance sheet exists. Swedenborg was comfortably situated but not a great magnate. Resources included family inheritances, a Board of Mines salary, noble property interests, investments/claims associated with family estates, and from 1747 a half-salary pension. He repeatedly paid for large Latin books that sold poorly, indicating substantial liquidity and willingness to spend capital on publication. There is no evidence of bankruptcy, embezzlement, money laundering or commercial fraud.

### Hornsgatan estate

His Stockholm property on Hornsgatan included a main house, workshops/outbuildings and a large garden with fruit and vegetables, clipped hedges, a maze or winding walks, and a small summerhouse where he worked and received visitors. After his death it passed to heirs and later owners. The summerhouse was moved to Skansen and survives as a memorial; the [garden history](https://swedenborg.com/a-peek-inside-swedenborgs-garden/) and a [material-history study](https://swedenborg-philosophy.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/01/Hjern_Article-New_Philosophy_January-June_2012.pdf) separate surviving fabric from later reconstruction.

### Inventions and technical projects

Documented proposals included:

- a fixed-wing flying-machine sketch (unbuilt and incapable of sustained powered flight as drawn);
- submarine and diving concepts;
- pumps, locks, cranes, furnaces and ore-processing improvements;
- ship/boat transport overland during the Norwegian campaign;
- methods for determining longitude by lunar/stellar observations;
- a decimal monetary system and administrative-economic reforms;
- anatomical models of blood circulation, brain and nervous system.

“Inventor” is accurate for conceptual designs, but patent-era language can mislead. The dossier found no verified Swedenborg patent and no evidence that most spectacular devices were built.

## Controversies, Investigations, and Disputed Claims

### Gothenburg heresy proceedings, 1768–70

The major formal controversy was ecclesiastical rather than criminal. Professors **Gabriel Beyer** and **Johan Rosén** were investigated by the Gothenburg Consistory after using or defending Swedenborg's theology. Charges centered on conflict with Lutheran orthodoxy: Trinity, atonement, justification and scriptural authority. Swedenborg defended his writings in letters and appealed to royal protection. A 1770 decision characterized the doctrines as erroneous and restricted teaching; the proceeding was directed primarily at the professors, not a criminal indictment of Swedenborg. Later investigation lost momentum and was not a conviction of Swedenborg. His correspondence with Beyer is preserved in [Tafel's documentary collection](https://runeberg.org/tafeldoces/1847/0129.html); a narrative based on those documents appears in [*The Swedenborg Epic*, chapter 41](https://swedenborgdigitallibrary.org/ES/epic41.htm).

### Psychiatric and neurological interpretations

From contemporaries who called him mad to nineteenth-century alienists and modern clinicians, commentators have retrospectively proposed monomania, schizophrenia-spectrum psychosis, mania, temporal-lobe epilepsy, sleep paralysis, migraine, or a nonpathological voice-hearing/visionary state. Henry Maudsley's “messianic psychosis” interpretation is summarized in [Johnson's historical study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7866688/). A later interdisciplinary paper argues that schizophrenia and epilepsy do not adequately fit the record and proposes “hallucinations in the sane” as a less reductive description; see [Jones and Fernyhough](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0952695107086138).

Documented symptoms include vivid dreams, auditory and visual experiences, altered bodily sensations, at least one reported collapse/prostration, ecstatic conviction, and late-life stroke. Missing are clinical examination, reliable seizure chronology, progressive disorganization, or clear functional deterioration during the visionary decades. Retrospective diagnosis is therefore unresolved. The visions can be described phenomenologically; neither divine revelation nor disease is established by the historical record.

### Secret-agent, Jacobite and Masonic thesis

Marsha Keith Schuchard's *Emanuel Swedenborg, Secret Agent on Earth and in Heaven* (2012) argues that Swedenborg used Jacobite, Jewish, diplomatic and Masonic networks and performed intelligence work, even connecting his visionary techniques to esoteric training. The thesis draws on wide archival research and highlights real Swedish diplomatic intrigue, encrypted correspondence and overlapping networks. Reviewers have criticized conjectural identifications, associative chains and the conversion of contextual possibility into biography. The [Institute of Historical Research review](https://reviews.history.ac.uk/review/1236/) describes the claim and its evidence problems; a [Scandinavian review record](https://septentrio.uit.no/index.php/1700/article/view/3304) marks continuing scholarly debate.

**Finding:** Swedenborg certainly moved through politically sensitive European networks and carried information in an age when gentlemen, diplomats and scientists overlapped. A sustained formal career as a Jacobite spy, French agent or Freemason is a disputed scholarly reconstruction, not established consensus. No criminal intelligence prosecution is documented.

### Censorship and anonymous publication

Swedenborg printed abroad and initially anonymously, reducing exposure to Swedish religious censorship. Anonymity also reflected his claim that content, not personality, mattered. Once authorship became known, critics could target his status and sanity. There is no evidence that he used ghostwriters; translators and posthumous editors must be distinguished from his Latin originals.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. The Stockholm fire vision

**Story:** On 19 July 1759, while dining in Gothenburg roughly 400 km from Stockholm, Swedenborg became agitated, described a fire consuming Stockholm's Södermalm, and around 8 p.m. announced it had stopped three doors from his house. Couriers later confirmed details.

**Appearance and circulation:** The fire itself is documented. The paranormal story circulated orally and was investigated by Kant several years later; Kant recounted it in his letter to Charlotte von Knobloch and in material associated with *Dreams of a Spirit-Seer*. Later witnesses and Swedenborgian compilations elaborated it.

**Evidence:** Kant said he commissioned inquiry and treated witness testimony as impressive. However, no surviving same-evening written record by a dinner guest has been identified; versions differ over venue, host, timings, courier schedule and exact distance of the fire from Swedenborg's property. Ordinary information leakage and retrospective compression cannot be excluded.

**Reputation:** It became the signature proof for believers in clairvoyance and a canonical case in psychical research; skeptics use the evidentiary delay as a model of legend formation. Kant's text is accessible in [*Dreams of a Spirit-Seer*, Appendix 2](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Dreams_of_a_Spirit-Seer/Appendix_2); C. D. Broad's [analysis of Kant and psychical research](https://www.newdualism.org/papers/C.Broad/Kant-and-Psychical-Research.htm) lays out the transmission problems.

### 2. Madame de Marteville's lost receipt

**Story:** The widow of the Dutch ambassador (often incorrectly called French in retellings) faced a claim for payment for a silver service. Swedenborg supposedly contacted her deceased husband, after which the receipt was found in a concealed compartment—some versions say she herself dreamed the hiding place.

**Appearance and circulation:** Reported through Stockholm society and Kant's inquiries; versions differ materially on who received the revelation and how.

**Evidence:** A real widow, debt dispute and recovered receipt appear to underlie the story. The differing dream/seance mechanisms and lack of an immediate documentary statement prevent treating spirit communication as proven.

**Reputation:** The story cast Swedenborg as helpful domestic clairvoyant and was repeatedly paired with the fire and queen anecdotes.

### 3. Queen Louisa Ulrika's secret

**Story:** The queen asked Swedenborg to communicate with her dead brother, Prince Augustus William of Prussia. At a later court encounter Swedenborg whispered something that made her pale and declare only her brother could have known it.

**Appearance and circulation:** Court testimony circulated through Count Scheffer, the queen's own later remarks, Thiebault's anecdotes of Frederick the Great's court, Kantian discussion and Tafel's nineteenth-century compilation.

**Evidence:** The audience and startled response have multiple lines of later testimony, but the content of the “secret” was never recorded. Possibilities include privileged intelligence, a court joke, retrospective embellishment, or what Swedenborg claimed. The secrecy makes the supernatural proposition unfalsifiable.

**Reputation:** It gave royal validation to the seer persona and fueled both hagiography and secret-agent interpretations. Tafel's relevant testimony is digitized in [Project Runeberg](https://runeberg.org/tafeldoces/1841/0146.html).

### 4. Precise prediction of his death and John Wesley

**Story:** Swedenborg told acquaintances he would die on 29 March 1772 and told Wesley that a delayed meeting would be too late; he died on that date.

**Evidence:** Landlord-family and clerical testimony supports his expectation of death after a serious stroke. The Wesley episode was recorded later and has textual variants. A dying elderly man naming a date is not equivalent to a securely preregistered prophecy. That the date matched became important hagiographically.

### 5. “First modern airplane,” “discoverer of the neuron,” and universal scientific priority

These claims grew from genuine sketches and suggestive passages. The aircraft was not built or tested; the neuroanatomical ideas did not establish modern cell theory; the cosmology was not the Kant–Laplace theory in modern form. Priority legends helped nineteenth- and twentieth-century followers answer ridicule of the visionary by presenting him as centuries ahead of science. His real technical and synthetic achievements do not require those maximal claims.

### 6. Founder of the Swedenborgian Church

Swedenborg supplied the writings but created no denomination, ordained no clergy, and left no church constitution. Organized New Church bodies arose after his death, especially in Britain from 1787. Calling him “founder” is genealogically understandable but institutionally imprecise.

## Later Life, Illness, Death, and Remains

Swedenborg continued to travel in his eighties. He went to Amsterdam in 1770 to publish *Vera Christiana Religio* under his own name, then to London in 1771. He lodged with **Richard Shearsmith**, a peruke-maker, and family in Great Bath Street, Coldbath Fields. In December 1771 he suffered a stroke, with partial paralysis and impaired speech. He improved enough to communicate and receive visitors but did not recover fully.

The Swedish pastor **Arvid Ferelius** visited him and administered communion. Later polemics claimed Swedenborg recanted; Ferelius's testimony does not establish a repudiation of his theology, and Swedenborg reportedly affirmed that he had written truth. He died on Sunday 29 March 1772. No autopsy is documented; stroke complications are the probable cause.

He was buried in the Swedish Lutheran church at Princes Square. Sweden acquired the remains in the early twentieth century; after exhumation and identification they were transported aboard a Swedish naval vessel and placed in Uppsala Cathedral. The skull had previously been removed and changed hands; a skull believed to be Swedenborg's was returned from Britain to Sweden in 1978, an episode that generated its own relic history. The larger fact—state-sponsored repatriation and cathedral entombment—converted a once-suspect visionary into a national cultural figure.

## Works by Emanuel Swedenborg

This is a substantial, not item-level exhaustive, bibliography. Dates are original publication dates; manuscripts published later are labeled. Swedenborg wrote primarily in Swedish early and Latin later. English titles vary by translator.

### Early literary, mathematical, scientific, and technical works published in his lifetime

1. *Selectae sententiae L. Annaei Senecae et Publilii Syri mimi* (1709) — university dissertation/edition of maxims.
2. *Ludus Heliconius, sive Carmina miscellanea* (1710) — Latin poems.
3. *Daedalus Hyperboreus, eller några nya mathematiska och physicaliska försök* (1716–18), six issues — editor, publisher and contributor; included Polhem's work.
4. *Förslag til wårt mynts och måls indelning* (1719) — proposal on decimal division of measures/money.
5. *Om watnens högd och förra werldens starcka ebb och flod* (1719) — water levels and ancient tides/geology.
6. *En ny theorie om jordens afstannande* (1719) — earth's motion/rotation.
7. *Prodromus principiorum rerum naturalium, sive novorum tentaminum chymiam et physicam experimenta geometrice explicandi* (1721).
8. *Nova observata et inventa circa ferrum et ignem* (1721).
9. *Methodus nova inveniendi longitudines locorum terra marique* (1721).
10. *Miscellanea de rebus naturalibus* (1722).
11. *Opera philosophica et mineralia* (1734), 3 vols.: *Principia rerum naturalium*; *Regnum subterraneum sive minerale de ferro*; *Regnum subterraneum sive minerale de cupro et orichalco*.
12. *Prodromus philosophiae ratiocinantis de infinito, et causa finali creationis; deque mechanismo operationis animae et corporis* (1734) — commonly *The Infinite*.
13. *Oeconomia regni animalis in transactiones divisa* (1740–41), 2 vols. — *The Economy of the Animal Kingdom*.
14. *Regnum animale, anatomice, physice et philosophice perlustratum* (1744–45), 3 published parts — *The Animal Kingdom*; projected larger series unfinished.
15. *De cultu et amore Dei* (1745), parts I–II — *The Worship and Love of God*; unfinished philosophical-poetic bridge work.

### Major manuscripts and posthumous editions

- *Drömboken: Journalanteckningar, 1743–1744* — private dream/travel journal; discovered in the 1850s; first published 1859. Genuine autograph, not prepared for publication.
- *Diarium Spirituale* / *Spiritual Diary* (entries chiefly 1747–65) — thousands of private memoranda; posthumously edited, not a lifetime book.
- *Adversaria* (1740s) — extensive biblical notes and exegetical drafts; posthumous.
- *Apocalypsis Explicata* / *Apocalypse Explained* (c. 1757–59) — enormous unfinished exposition; posthumous.
- *De Athanasii Symbolo*, *De Domino*, *De Verbo*, *De Fide Athanasiana*, *Canones Novae Ecclesiae*, *Coronis* and other theological drafts — posthumous editions from manuscripts.
- *The Cerebrum*, *The Brain*, *The Fibre*, *Generation*, and other anatomical manuscripts — written in the scientific period, edited and translated long afterward.
- Letters, parliamentary memorials, travel diaries and scientific notebooks — genuine documents, published in edited collections; editorial dating and translation can vary.

The Royal Swedish Academy collection comprises roughly 20,000 manuscript pages in about 80 bound volumes. It entered UNESCO's Memory of the World Register in 2005; see the [official UNESCO record](https://www.unesco.org/en/memory-world/emanuel-swedenborg-collection) and [nomination dossier](https://media.unesco.org/sites/default/files/webform/mow001/emanuel_swedenborg_collection_form.pdf).

## Posthumous Reputation, Cult, Influence, and Legacy

### Institutional religion

Readers in England organized the **New Church** or **Church of the New Jerusalem** in 1787. Later divisions produced the General Conference of the New Church (Britain), Swedenborgian Church of North America, General Church of the New Jerusalem, and Lord's New Church, among others. These bodies differ on the authority of unpublished manuscripts, church government and whether Swedenborg's writings constitute the “Third Testament.” Swedenborg himself remains revelator rather than object of worship in mainstream New Church theology, though manuscripts and relics have acquired devotional aura.

### Literature, philosophy, art and reform

- **William Blake** attended the 1789 New Jerusalem conference, admired Swedenborg, then satirized systematic heaven and hell in *The Marriage of Heaven and Hell* while retaining correspondential thinking.
- **Kant** used Swedenborg to police the border between metaphysics and enthusiasm, yet scholars continue to debate conceptual influence on Kant's moral-spiritual community.
- **Ralph Waldo Emerson** made him one of the “representative men,” praising immense classificatory power while criticizing literalism.
- **Honoré de Balzac**, **Charles Baudelaire**, **Gérard de Nerval**, **Victor Hugo** and later Symbolists drew on correspondences, angelology and spiritual worlds.
- **Henry James Sr.** underwent a spiritual crisis interpreted through Swedenborg; the family atmosphere affected William and Henry James.
- **Helen Keller** credited Swedenborgian theology with giving intelligible spiritual form to her world.
- **Johnny Appleseed (John Chapman)** distributed Swedenborgian tracts on the American frontier.
- **George Inness** and other artists translated correspondential theology into landscape aesthetics.
- **August Strindberg**, **W. B. Yeats**, **Arthur Conan Doyle**, **Carl Jung**, **Jorge Luis Borges**, **Czesław Miłosz** and many others engaged him selectively, not necessarily as believers.
- Swedenborgian abolitionists such as **Carl Bernhard Wadström** linked his favorable theology of Africans to antislavery action; this influence coexisted with essentialist racial ideas in the source texts.

### Scientific reputation

His metallurgy was respected in his century, while his later visionary fame eclipsed it. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century followers rediscovered anatomical manuscripts and sometimes overstated priority. Historians now treat him as a revealing integrator of mechanics, vitalism, anatomy, biblical philology and Enlightenment metaphysics—not as the secret originator of every later discovery.

### National and archival commemoration

Reburial in Uppsala Cathedral, preservation of his summerhouse at Skansen, streets and societies bearing his name, and UNESCO registration mark transition from sectarian embarrassment or curiosity to national and world documentary heritage. UNESCO does not certify his revelations; it recognizes the authenticity, scale, survival and historical influence of the archive.

## Chronology

- **1688, 29 January OS / 9 February NS:** born in Stockholm to Jesper Swedberg and Sara Behm.
- **1692/93:** family moves to Uppsala.
- **1696:** mother dies.
- **1697:** father marries Sara Bergia.
- **1699:** matriculates at Uppsala University.
- **1702:** Jesper becomes bishop of Skara; Emanuel remains in the Benzelius household.
- **1709:** completes university work; publishes dissertation on Seneca and Publilius Syrus.
- **1710:** publishes Latin poetry; leaves on European grand tour.
- **1710–15:** studies and works in England, Netherlands, France and German lands.
- **1715:** returns to Sweden.
- **1716:** meets Charles XII at Lund; appointed assessor extraordinary, Board of Mines; begins *Daedalus Hyperboreus*.
- **1716–18:** works with Christopher Polhem; canal, transport and military-engineering projects.
- **1718:** Charles XII killed; scientific journal ceases.
- **1719:** family ennobled as Swedenborg; publishes Swedish scientific/economic tracts.
- **1721–22:** publishes scientific works in Amsterdam/Leipzig; mine and industrial travel.
- **1724:** declines Uppsala mathematics chair.
- **1720s–30s:** Board of Mines administration, field inspections, parliamentary activity.
- **1733–34:** major European journey; publishes *Opera philosophica et mineralia* and *The Infinite*.
- **1740:** elected to Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences; first volume of *Oeconomia regni animalis*.
- **1740–41:** *Economy of the Animal Kingdom*.
- **1743, 21 July:** leaves Stockholm; dream/travel journal begins.
- **1744, Easter season:** acute dreams, visions and religious crisis; *Regnum animale* volumes appear.
- **1745:** later-reported London vision and divine commission; publishes unfinished *Worship and Love of God*.
- **1747:** resigns Board of Mines with honorable half-pay pension; begins intensive biblical-spiritual work.
- **1749–56:** anonymous publication of *Arcana Coelestia*.
- **1757:** claims a Last Judgment occurs in the spiritual world.
- **1758:** publishes *Heaven and Hell* and four companion theological works.
- **1759, 19 July:** Stockholm fire; Gothenburg clairvoyance story begins circulating.
- **1760–61:** lost-receipt and queen's-secret stories circulate; major monetary memorials.
- **1763–64:** doctrinal works, *Divine Love and Wisdom*, *Divine Providence*.
- **1766:** *Apocalypse Revealed*; Kant publishes *Dreams of a Spirit-Seer*.
- **1768:** *Conjugial Love*; Gothenburg controversy develops.
- **1769:** *Brief Exposition* and *Interaction of Soul and Body*; correspondence defending Beyer and Rosén.
- **1770:** appeals to Swedish crown amid doctrinal restrictions; leaves for Amsterdam.
- **1771:** publishes *True Christian Religion* under his name; travels to London; stroke in December.
- **1772, 29 March:** dies in London.
- **1772, 5 April:** buried at Swedish Church, Shadwell.
- **1782:** Robsahm memoir published.
- **1787:** organized New Church formed in Britain.
- **1850s/1859:** dream journal rediscovered and first published.
- **1908–10:** remains exhumed, repatriated and reinterred in Uppsala Cathedral.
- **1978:** skull attributed to Swedenborg returned from Britain to Sweden.
- **2005:** archive inscribed in UNESCO Memory of the World Register.
- **2010:** Royal Swedish Academy hosts major scholarly conference on the archive and influence.
- **2012:** Schuchard's secret-agent reinterpretation published; extensive debate follows.
- **2026:** Swedenborg remains simultaneously an object of confessional study, history of science, literary reception, esotericism, psychology of voice-hearing and archival heritage.

## Research Limitations and Evidentiary Conclusions

- No complete modern financial reconstruction or probate inventory was accessible in the consulted digital sources; wealth descriptions remain qualitative.
- Several canonical anecdotes are preserved through delayed testimony. Their social circulation is documented more securely than their paranormal explanation.
- Original Swedish official records and Latin first editions are unevenly digitized; edited documentary collections mediate many quotations.
- The bibliography lists major standalone works and major manuscript complexes, not every article in *Daedalus*, parliamentary memorial, notebook fragment or posthumous translation.
- Searches reached diminishing returns when further results repeated confessional summaries or nineteenth-century compilations without new primary evidence. Consequential disputed claims have therefore been bounded rather than resolved beyond the record.

## Atlas Connections

### Abraham

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Swedenborg made Abraham a recurring subject of *Arcana Coelestia* (1749–56), his verse-by-verse spiritual interpretation of Genesis and Exodus. He treated the patriarchal narrative correspondentially rather than merely as biography. [Swedenborg Foundation](https://swedenborg.com/emanuel-swedenborg/writings/)

### Aristotle

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Swedenborg read and excerpted Aristotle while developing his philosophical and psychological vocabulary; Aristotle appears among the historical authorities engaged in Swedenborg’s scientific notebooks and pre-theological works. This was textual engagement, not evidence of unmediated intellectual descent. [Scholarly overview of Swedenborg’s philosophical context](https://swedenborg-philosophy.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/10/Williams-Hogan_Article-New_Philosophy_January-June_2013.pdf)

### Honoré de Balzac

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Balzac studied Swedenborgian teaching and made Swedenborg and his doctrines explicit subjects in novels including *Louis Lambert* (1832) and *Séraphîta* (1835). The precise channels and extent of doctrinal dependence remain subjects of scholarship, but the written engagement itself is unmistakable. [Scholarly survey](https://swedenborg.com/scholars-swedenborgs-influence-on-balzac-and-strindberg/), [Persée study](https://www.persee.fr/doc/caief_0571-5865_1963_num_15_1_2263)

### Jorge Luis Borges

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Borges repeatedly wrote and lectured about Swedenborg. His works include “Los ángeles de Swedenborg” in *El libro de los seres imaginarios* (1967), an essay used to open *Testimony to the Invisible*, and a lecture delivered at the University of Belgrano in Buenos Aires on 16 June 1978. [Bibliographic discussion](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/s3/BVMC_OBRAS/ffb/d32/648/2b1/11d/fac/c70/021/85c/e60/64/mimes/ffbd3264-82b1-11df-acc7-002185ce6064_37.html), [book record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Testimony_to_the_Invisible.html?hl=en&id=rm9wNAAACAAJ&output=html_text)

### William Blake

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Blake read and annotated Swedenborg’s works during the 1780s, attended the New Jerusalem Church’s General Conference in London in 1789, and then subjected Swedenborg’s system to admiring but pointed criticism in *The Marriage of Heaven and Hell* (c. 1790–93). Surviving marginalia establish direct textual engagement. [Swedenborg Foundation](https://swedenborg.com/emanuel-swedenborg/influence/), [discussion of the textual evidence](https://swedenborg.com/swedenborg-blake-the-everlasting-gospel/)

### Sir Arthur Conan Doyle

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Conan Doyle devoted the opening chapter of *The History of Spiritualism* (1926) to “The Story of Swedenborg,” portraying him as a major precursor of modern Spiritualism. This was Doyle’s retrospective interpretation, not evidence that Swedenborg belonged to the nineteenth-century Spiritualist movement. [Cambridge University Press contents](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/history-of-spiritualism/frontmatter/27B697363574428952AABE8EEA86A512), [1926 text](https://www.arthur-conan-doyle.com/images/4/4d/The-history-of-spiritualism-vol1-cassell-1926.pdf)

### Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Goethe read Swedenborgian texts shortly after 1770 and made a small number of explicit written references to Swedenborg. Scholarship regards Goethe’s knowledge of Swedenborg as established but treats claims of extensive doctrinal influence more cautiously. [*Scribe of Heaven*, reception chapter](https://swedenborg.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/NCE_ScribeofHeaven.pdf)

### Jesus of Nazareth

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Jesus Christ was the central historical and theological figure of Swedenborg’s later writings. Works such as *Doctrine of the Lord* (1763) and *True Christian Religion* (1771) argue that the divine and human are united in Jesus and reject a Trinity of three divine persons. [Swedenborg Foundation writings guide](https://swedenborg.com/emanuel-swedenborg/writings/)

### Carl Gustav Jung

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Jung recalled reading seven volumes of Swedenborg while studying occult and paranormal literature during his university years, and he referred to Swedenborg in later autobiographical and psychological discussions. Claims that Jung’s mature psychology was simply derived from Swedenborg go beyond this evidence. [Jung, *Memories, Dreams, Reflections* text](https://ratical.org/co-globalize/MemoriesDreamsReflections-Jung.pdf), [Swedenborg Society reception summary](https://www.swedenborg.org.uk/events/swedenborg-and-the-english-romantics-items-from-the-swedenborg-collection/)

### Immanuel Kant

- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** Kant investigated reports of Swedenborg’s clairvoyance, obtained and read *Arcana Coelestia*, and made Swedenborg the explicit target and foil of *Träume eines Geistersehers* (*Dreams of a Spirit-Seer*, 1766). The surviving record supports one-sided written engagement rather than a personal meeting or correspondence between the two men. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-development/)

### Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Swedenborg took extensive notes from Leibniz’s *Theodicy* and engaged Leibnizian questions concerning mind, body, pre-established harmony, freedom and divine order in his philosophical notebooks and mature works. Direct borrowing and disagreement can be documented; claims that Swedenborg’s entire system was Leibnizian are interpretive. [Philosophical-context study](https://swedenborg-philosophy.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/10/Williams-Hogan_Article-New_Philosophy_January-June_2013.pdf), [study of Leibniz and Swedenborg](https://swedenborg-philosophy.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/1991-Vol-94-No-1-2-January-June_Part2.pdf)

### Martin Luther

- **[T1-]** **Reported fact:** Swedenborg wrote about Luther both historically and as a person he claimed to encounter in the spiritual world, particularly when attacking justification by faith alone. The writing is documentary fact; the meetings are Swedenborg’s reported visionary experiences and are not independently verifiable.

### Moses

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Moses and the books attributed to him occupy a foundational place in Swedenborg’s exegesis. *Arcana Coelestia* (1749–56) interprets Genesis and Exodus verse by verse, while Swedenborg’s later doctrinal works repeatedly discuss Mosaic law, revelation and biblical correspondences. [Swedenborg Foundation writings guide](https://swedenborg.com/emanuel-swedenborg/writings/)

### Isaac Newton

- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** During his 1710–15 stay in England, Swedenborg studied Newtonian astronomy and mechanics and subsequently engaged Newtonian natural philosophy in his scientific program. No reliable evidence shows that Swedenborg studied personally under or met Newton; the supported crossing is textual and intellectual. [Swedenborg Foundation scientific-writings survey](https://swedenborg.com/emanuel-swedenborg/scientific-writings/)

### Noah

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Swedenborg wrote at length about Noah in *Arcana Coelestia*, interpreting him correspondentially as representing the people of an ancient spiritual church rather than treating the flood account only as literal history. [Swedenborg Foundation description](https://swedenborg.com/emanuel-swedenborg/writings/)

### Rudolf Steiner

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Steiner repeatedly discussed Swedenborg in lectures, including the Penmaenmawr lecture of 25 August 1923, where he classified and evaluated Swedenborg’s mode of supersensible perception. Steiner’s account belongs to his own anthroposophical framework and is not independent verification of Swedenborg’s visions. [Rudolf Steiner Archive](https://rsarchive.org/Lectures/GA227/English/SOL/19230825p01.html)

### Victor Hugo

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Hugo explicitly invoked Swedenborg in his poetry and prose. His poem *Ce que dit la bouche d’ombre* (1854) assigns a speaking passage to Swedenborg, while *Les Misérables* (1862) mentions him alongside Pascal in a remark about genius passing into madness. Broader claims that Hugo was a committed Swedenborgian remain unproven. [Documentary discussion](https://www.swedenborgstudy.com/articles/history-of-art/hugo.htm)

### William Butler Yeats

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Yeats read, cited and acknowledged Swedenborg as one of his principal mystical authorities, grouping him with Jacob Boehme and William Blake. Swedenborgian accounts of spirits, correspondences and the afterlife informed Yeats’s essays and occult thought, though usually through a synthesis with other traditions. [*Scribe of Heaven*](https://swedenborg.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/NCE_ScribeofHeaven.pdf), [Swedenborg Society reception summary](https://www.swedenborg.org.uk/events/swedenborg-and-the-english-romantics-items-from-the-swedenborg-collection/)

### Johnny Appleseed (John Chapman)

- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** Chapman became a devoted reader and itinerant distributor of Swedenborgian/New Church publications in the early nineteenth-century United States. He and Swedenborg never met—Chapman was born in 1774, two years after Swedenborg’s death—but Chapman’s circulation of the writings constitutes direct, one-sided textual engagement. [Swedenborg Foundation](https://swedenborg.com/interview-with-author-ray-silverman-on-the-core-of-johnny-appleseed/)
