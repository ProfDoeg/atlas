## Works by Saint George of Lydda

### Securely attributable obra

- **No securely attributable date:** No text, object, building, image, protocol, manifesto, or other made thing can be assigned to George of Lydda. This is an empty corpus, not a lost-work catalogue: even the historical person behind the cult is recoverable only conjecturally, and scholarship cannot securely establish his birthplace, life, occupation, or death date.

### Works attributed to others, not to George

- **Before or around 494; subsequently revised:** *Passio Georgii* / *Acts of Saint George*. This is an anonymous and extensively variant hagiographic manifestation about George, not a work made by him; the *Decretum Gelasianum* already treated George’s Passion as apocryphal and its author was unknown.
- **Late antique to medieval periods:** *Lives, Passions, Miracles, Encomia, and Hymns of Saint George* (BHG 670–691 and related traditions). These constitute a multilingual dossier created by anonymous or named devotees, redactors, translators, and liturgical communities—not George’s oeuvre. Their speeches placed in George’s mouth are legendary compositions and cannot be catalogued as his texts.
- **Probably eleventh century or later in its specifically Georgean form:** *Saint George and the Dragon*. This is a developing narrative and iconographic tradition, not an attributable action, project, or Gesamtkunstwerk made by the historical George.
- **Fourth century onward:** *Cult, shrine, relic traditions, images, and dedications of Saint George*. These are collective manifestations produced around a martyr’s remembered or legendary identity. They resist classification as George’s works because he did not make them; some evidence may also conflate relics or memories of more than one martyr named George.

### Sources

- Christopher Walter, “[The Origins of the Cult of Saint George](https://doi.org/10.3406/rebyz.1995.1911),” *Revue des études byzantines* 53 (1995), pp. 295–326. Walter concludes that analysis of the Lives cannot recover a historical biography, notes possible conflation of martyrs, and treats the surviving narratives as stories about a “meta-historical” George.
- François Halkin, *Bibliotheca Hagiographica Graeca*, 3rd ed., Subsidia Hagiographica 8a, Brussels: Société des Bollandistes, 1957; George of Diospolis dossier, BHG 670–691. This is the standard repertory of Greek hagiographic texts about George; it supplies no authorial corpus by him.
- *Acta Sanctorum*, April III, 23 April, dossier on George; digital contents at “[23 April](https://actasanctorum.org/april/day-23/index.html).” The critical dossier collects traditions concerning George rather than works authored or made by him.
- Vatican Apostolic Library, “[April 23: Martyrdom of St. George](https://www.vaticanlibrary.va/moduli/202007EN.pdf).” This identifies the *Passio Georgii* as an apocryphal work under the *Decretum Gelasianum* and distinguishes the later narrative tradition from the sparse evidence for the martyr.
- E. A. Wallis Budge, *The Martyrdom and Miracles of Saint George of Cappadocia*, London, 1888, digitized [introduction and texts](https://wikisource.org/wiki/The_Martyrdom_and_Miracles_of_Saint_George). This comparative edition documents Coptic and related recensions and their disputed, generally pseudonymous attributions.
