# Jacques Marie Émile Lacan: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier reconstructs the life, work, institutions, relationships, controversies, and posthumous reception of Jacques Lacan from documentary, bibliographical, scholarly, archival, and contemporaneous press sources consulted through September 4, 2026.

Labels used:

- **Documented fact:** supported by an institutional record, primary document, contemporaneous report, or strong agreement among scholarly sources.
- **Reported fact:** found in a biography, memoir, interview, or secondary reconstruction but not independently established here from a primary record.
- **Allegation or disputed claim:** asserted by an identifiable source and contested, unadjudicated, or dependent on testimony.
- **Rumor or myth:** a circulating story whose factual basis is weak, uncertain, or symbolic.
- **Interpretation:** a scholarly or partisan judgment rather than a directly verifiable event.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Jacques Marie Émile Lacan; frequently styled Jacques-Marie Lacan or Jacques Lacan.
- **Profession:** French physician, psychiatrist, forensic psychiatrist, psychoanalyst, lecturer, institutional founder, and author.
- **Birth:** April 13, 1901, at 95 boulevard Beaumarchais, Paris, France.
- **Death:** September 9, 1981, aged eighty.
- **Death place:** Sources conflict in formulation. Contemporary and biographical accounts place his final treatment at the Clinique Hartmann in Neuilly-sur-Seine; his civil death certificate reportedly records death at 74 rue d’Assas, Paris, at 11:45 p.m.
- **Burial:** Cemetery of Guitrancourt, Yvelines, near his country property La Prévôté.
- **Nationality:** French.
- **Religious background:** Raised in a strict bourgeois Catholic family. His adult relationship to Catholicism was intellectually persistent but personally heterodox; he was neither a conventional Catholic thinker nor an uncomplicated secularist.
- **Common epithets:** “The French Freud”; “the most controversial psychoanalyst since Freud”; “Maître” among some followers. These are reputational descriptions, not formal titles.
- **No documented pseudonym:** He used pseudonyms for patients—notably “Aimée”—but normally published under his own name.

The authoritative EPHE prosopographical record confirms the principal dates, medical career, family origins, and his appointment as *chargé de conférences*. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy supplies a reliable synthetic intellectual chronology.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and commercial background

**Documented fact:** Lacan’s father was **Alfred Lacan** (1873–1960), from an Orléans Catholic bourgeois family. He served first as a commercial representative and then managed financial interests connected with the Dessaux vinegar concern. Lacan’s mother was **Émilie Philippine Marie Baudry** (1876–1948), from an affluent Parisian Catholic family. Her father, Charles-Honoré Baudry, had worked as a goldbeater before becoming a rentier.

The Lacans lived in the same boulevard Beaumarchais building as paternal relatives. Biographical reconstructions describe conflict produced by this multigenerational arrangement. Jacques was expected by his father to enter the family commercial sphere, but instead chose medicine.

### Siblings

The couple had four children:

- **Jacques Marie Émile Lacan**, born 1901.
- **Raymond Lacan**, born 1902. Sources disagree on whether he survived eleven days or roughly two years; the strongest detailed genealogical account says that he died in infancy from hepatitis.
- **Madeleine-Marie Lacan**, born 1903, died 1995. She married engineer and First World War veteran Jacques Houlon and lived for a period in Indochina.
- **Marc-Marie Lacan**, born 1908, died 1994. He entered the Benedictine abbey of Hautecombe in 1929 and took the religious name **Marc-François**. He later participated in Jacques’s funeral observances.

### First marriage and children

Lacan married **Marie-Louise “Malou” Blondin** (1906–1983) in Paris on January 29, 1934. Their children were:

- **Caroline Lacan**, born 1937, later Caroline Roger.
- **Thibaut Lacan**, born 1939.
- **Sibylle Lacan**, born November 1940, later author of *Un père: puzzle*.

Lacan’s relationship with actress **Sylvia Maklès Bataille** began while he remained married to Malou. Sylvia was still legally connected to writer Georges Bataille, though their marriage had broken down. Lacan and Malou divorced on December 15, 1941.

Sibylle later described an emotionally fragmented family and her difficult relation to an often absent father. Her memoir is important testimony but should not be treated as a neutral family chronicle.

### Sylvia Bataille and Judith

Sylvia Maklès (1908–1993), professionally known as **Sylvia Bataille**, was an actress associated with Jean Renoir and the French avant-garde. She and Georges Bataille had a daughter, Laurence Bataille.

Sylvia and Lacan’s daughter, **Judith**, was born in 1941. Because Lacan’s divorce and Sylvia’s marital status created legal complications, she initially bore the name Bataille. Lacan and Sylvia married in 1953; Judith later used the name Judith Lacan and, after marriage, **Judith Miller**.

Judith married philosopher and psychoanalyst **Jacques-Alain Miller**, who became a central organizer of the Lacanian movement, editor of the authorized seminar series, and Lacan’s literary executor.

### Other intimate relationships

Biographical literature reports relationships before and outside Lacan’s marriages, including an attachment to **Olesia Sienkiewicz**, formerly associated with Pierre Drieu la Rochelle.

Psychoanalyst and writer **Catherine Millot** has documented that she lived with Lacan for several years while simultaneously undergoing analysis with him. This dual erotic and analytic relationship is relevant to later ethical criticism. It is based on Millot’s own memoir and is therefore a **documented autobiographical report**, although the details remain her retrospective account.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1901–1919

Lacan grew up in a prosperous but emotionally and religiously restrictive Catholic household. From 1907 through 1918 or 1919 he attended the private Catholic **Collège Stanislas** in Paris. Sources describe him as physically delicate, frequently absent, and nevertheless academically accomplished.

During adolescence he read **Spinoza** and **Nietzsche**, reportedly reading Nietzsche in German. In 1917–1918 he studied philosophy under **Jean Baruzi**, later known for scholarship on John of the Cross, Angelus Silesius, and Christian mysticism. Baruzi’s attention to mystical language and negative theology remained relevant to Lacan’s later engagement with religious discourse, feminine jouissance, and the limits of speech.

The First World War unfolded during his school years. Unlike slightly older intellectuals, Lacan did not serve in its principal combat phase.

## Education and Formation, 1920–1932

### Medical studies

Against his father’s commercial plans for him, Lacan began medical studies at the University of Paris in 1920. He completed the usual hospital externship and gravitated toward neurology and psychiatry.

Around 1923–1925 he considered leaving for Senegal. He sought introductions through military and right-wing networks, met nationalist journalist **Léon Daudet**, and obtained a brief audience with **Charles Maurras**.

### Action française episode

**Reported fact:** Lacan attended some Action française meetings and for a period found Maurras’s literary and political thought attractive. There is no good evidence that he became a durable party militant or adopted Action française antisemitism. Later portrayals range from minimizing the episode to treating it as evidence of a lifelong authoritarian temperament. The defensible conclusion is narrower: he underwent a real but limited Maurrassian-monarchist phase in the mid-1920s.

### Psychiatric training

From 1926 or 1927 Lacan specialized in psychiatry at **Sainte-Anne Hospital**, particularly in the clinic headed by **Henri Claude**. His clinical appointments also included:

- The **Infirmerie spéciale des aliénés de la préfecture de police**, under **Gaëtan Gatian de Clérambault**.
- The **Henri-Rousselle Hospital**.
- Neurological and psychiatric professional societies in Paris.

Lacan later called Clérambault his “only master in psychiatry,” while also criticizing him. Clérambault’s concept of *automatisme mental*—mental phenomena experienced as imposed from outside—strongly influenced Lacan’s thinking about psychosis and the subject’s lack of mastery.

In 1931 Lacan obtained the *Diplôme de médecin légiste*, qualifying in forensic medicine and psychiatry.

### Early medical publications

His early papers were conventional clinical works, some co-authored:

- “Abasie chez une traumatisée de guerre” (with Maurice Trénel), 1928.
- Studies of hallucination, neurological syndromes, and psychosis with Jean Lévy-Valensi and other hospital colleagues.
- “Structures des psychoses paranoïaques,” 1931.
- A French translation of Freud’s “Über einige neurotische Mechanismen bei Eifersucht, Paranoia und Homosexualität,” published in 1932 as “De quelques mécanismes névrotiques dans la jalousie, la paranoïa et l’homosexualité.”

### The Aimée case

In April 1931, **Marguerite Pantaine Anzieu** attacked actress **Huguette Duflos** with a knife outside a theatre. She was committed to Sainte-Anne, where Lacan interviewed her intensively beginning in June.

Lacan called her “Aimée” or “Mme A.” and used her history, writings, and crime as the major case in his doctoral thesis:

*De la psychose paranoïaque dans ses rapports avec la personnalité*  
(*On Paranoid Psychosis in Its Relations to the Personality*)

He defended the thesis on September 7, 1932. It proposed “self-punitive paranoia,” connecting psychosis to personality, social relations, identification, and an attack on an idealized image of the self. The work attracted Surrealist interest and separated Lacan from narrowly constitutional psychiatry.

#### Later ethical dispute

- **Documented fact:** “Aimée” was Marguerite Pantaine Anzieu, mother of future psychoanalyst Didier Anzieu.
- **Documented fact:** After the war, Didier entered analysis with Lacan without initially knowing that Lacan had made his mother the subject of the thesis.
- **Reported fact:** Marguerite later worked for Lacan’s father.
- **Reported fact:** Didier discovered the connection during or around his analysis and subsequently confronted Lacan.
- **Disputed ethical question:** Lacan used a pseudonym, but later critics argue that he instrumentalized Marguerite’s life and writings, did not seek a therapeutic alliance, and failed to manage the conflict created by treating her son. No court or professional disciplinary finding against Lacan concerning this case was located.
- **Patient’s position:** Roudinesco reports that Marguerite resented being turned into a “case.” Jean Allouch’s later reconstruction re-examined the file and challenged Lacan’s interpretation.

## Entry into Psychoanalysis and the Avant-Garde, 1932–1939

### Training analysis

In June 1932 Lacan began a training analysis with **Rudolph Loewenstein**, a prominent member of the Société psychanalytique de Paris (SPP). The analysis continued until approximately 1938.

Their accounts diverged:

- Lacan treated the analysis as completed when he was elected a full member of the SPP.
- Loewenstein reportedly considered it unfinished.
- The disagreement became part of the institutional argument over Lacan’s qualification and personal independence.

Lacan joined the SPP in 1934 and became a full member in 1938.

### Surrealism and literary circles

Lacan moved among Surrealist and dissident-Surrealist circles without formally becoming a canonical Surrealist. His psychiatric work on paranoia was read by **Salvador Dalí**, Georges Bataille, and others interested in madness, crime, and involuntary creation.

He published in *Minotaure*, including:

- “Le problème du style et la conception psychiatrique des formes paranoïaques” (1933).
- “Motifs du crime paranoïaque: le crime des sœurs Papin” (1933).

The Papin sisters’ 1933 murder case became a meeting point between psychiatry, class politics, crime journalism, and Surrealist myth. Lacan’s treatment emphasized paranoid structure and the sisters’ closed imaginary relation; it was an interpretive intervention, not a forensic determination of guilt.

He associated with **Georges Bataille**, **André Breton**, André Masson, Pierre Drieu la Rochelle, and other interwar figures. Accounts report that his residence hosted early meetings connected with **Contre-Attaque**, the short-lived anti-fascist formation linking Bataille and Breton in 1935. Claims that Lacan was an initiate of Bataille’s secret society **Acéphale** remain less securely documented and should be treated as **reported association**, not proven membership.

### Kojève, Hegel, and philosophy

From 1933 to 1939 Lacan attended **Alexandre Kojève’s** celebrated EPHE lectures on Hegel’s *Phenomenology of Spirit*. Other auditors included Raymond Queneau, Raymond Aron, Georges Bataille, and Maurice Merleau-Ponty.

Kojève’s account of desire as desire for recognition, the master–slave dialectic, and the historicity of subjectivity profoundly affected Lacan. He also drew on:

- Hegel through Kojève and Jean Hyppolite.
- Heidegger.
- Spinoza.
- Freud.
- Clérambault.
- Karl Jaspers and phenomenological psychiatry.
- Surrealist theories of paranoia and artistic production.

### Mirror stage

Lacan first presented the “mirror stage” at the International Psychoanalytical Association congress at Marienbad in 1936. IPA president **Ernest Jones** stopped the presentation when Lacan exceeded his allotted time. The original text is lost.

He reformulated the paper for the 1949 IPA congress in Zurich as “Le stade du miroir comme formateur de la fonction du Je.”

The theory describes an infant’s identification with a unified visual image as a formative but alienating basis of the ego. The body is experienced as uncoordinated, while the image promises unity; ego identity therefore begins in misrecognition.

#### Wallon priority dispute

Psychologist **Henri Wallon** had already discussed children’s mirror recognition and used closely related terminology. Later scholarship shows that Lacan drew materially on Wallon while failing to acknowledge him adequately in the canonical 1949 paper.

- **Documented intellectual dependence:** Wallon’s empirical work preceded Lacan’s version.
- **Disputed characterization:** Critics call Lacan’s silence appropriation or plagiarism; defenders argue that Lacan transformed developmental psychology into a distinct psychoanalytic theory of identification.
- No formal plagiarism adjudication occurred.

### “The Family”

In 1938 Lacan contributed “Les complexes familiaux dans la formation de l’individu” to Henri Wallon’s section of the *Encyclopédie française*. It treated the family as a cultural rather than merely biological structure and elaborated weaning, intrusion, and Oedipal complexes.

## War Years, 1939–1945

The Second World War interrupted French psychoanalytic institutions and Lacan’s formal analytic activities.

Lacan served in French military medicine, including work connected with the Val-de-Grâce military hospital. He remained largely in France during the occupation. His private and medical activity continued, but his publication record was sparse.

No reliable evidence located in this research establishes that Lacan was:

- an active collaborator with the German occupation;
- a formal Resistance operative;
- imprisoned or exiled;
- connected to an intelligence service.

Accounts of the period emphasize personal and family complications: his marriage collapsed, Sylvia became pregnant, Judith was born, and anti-Jewish laws endangered Sylvia’s Jewish family and social network. Reports exist that Lacan helped or sheltered threatened acquaintances. These episodes require case-specific archival corroboration and should not be inflated into a general claim of Resistance membership.

In 1945 he published “Le temps logique et l’assertion de certitude anticipée,” an important essay using a prison puzzle to formulate intersubjective logical time.

## Postwar Psychiatry and Psychoanalysis, 1945–1952

After liberation, Lacan resumed activity in the SPP and developed an increasingly distinct theoretical position.

He traveled to England for approximately five weeks in 1945, studying wartime British psychiatry and group methods. His observations included the work of **Wilfred Bion** and **John Rickman** at Northfield. He reported on this in “La psychiatrie anglaise et la guerre” (1947). The journey influenced his interest in groups and institutions, though he did not simply adopt British object-relations theory.

Major postwar presentations included:

- “Propos sur la causalité psychique” (1946).
- “L’agressivité en psychanalyse” (1948).
- The revised “Stade du miroir” paper (1949).

In 1951 he began holding private seminars, initially in the apartment of Sylvia Bataille. These meetings moved to Sainte-Anne and developed into the annual seminar that would dominate his public career.

## Institutional Ascendancy and the First Split, 1953–1963

### The 1953 crisis

Conflict inside the SPP concerned training standards, control of its new institute, medical authority, and Lacan’s use of sessions shorter than the conventional fifty minutes.

Lacan was elected president of the SPP in January 1953 but resigned amid the dispute. In June, Daniel Lagache, Françoise Dolto, Juliette Favez-Boutonier, Lacan, and others left to form the **Société française de psychanalyse** (SFP).

Because the SFP was not recognized by the IPA, its members lost automatic international standing. A decade of negotiations followed.

### Rome Discourse

In September 1953 Lacan delivered “Fonction et champ de la parole et du langage en psychanalyse” in Rome. It became the manifesto of his “return to Freud.”

His core claim was not merely that words matter but that the unconscious operates through signifying relations. Psychoanalysis had to return from ego adaptation to speech, desire, repression, and the Freudian unconscious.

### Sainte-Anne seminars

From 1953 to 1963 his annual seminars at Sainte-Anne covered:

1. Freud’s technical papers.
2. The ego in Freud’s theory and technique.
3. Psychoses.
4. The object relation.
5. Formations of the unconscious.
6. Desire and its interpretation.
7. The ethics of psychoanalysis.
8. Transference.
9. Identification.
10. Anxiety.

The seminar was oral, improvisatory, and collective. Participants supplied questions, clinical material, linguistic expertise, mathematical concepts, and transcriptions. Later printed books are edited reconstructions, principally by Jacques-Alain Miller, not verbatim author-written manuscripts.

### Structuralism

During the 1950s Lacan engaged closely with:

- **Ferdinand de Saussure:** signifier/signified.
- **Roman Jakobson:** metaphor and metonymy.
- **Claude Lévi-Strauss:** symbolic structures, kinship, exchange, and the prohibition of incest.
- **Freud:** dreams, jokes, parapraxes, repression, sexuality, and transference.

Lacan’s formulation “the unconscious is structured like a language” became the best-known summary of this synthesis. He altered Saussure’s model by emphasizing the signifier’s priority and the instability of signification.

### Imaginary, Symbolic, and Real

He developed three interdependent registers:

- **Imaginary:** images, ego identifications, rivalry, and misrecognition.
- **Symbolic:** language, law, kinship, difference, and the “big Other.”
- **Real:** what resists symbolization and returns as impossibility or rupture.

Their relative importance changed over his career. Early work privileged the Imaginary; the 1950s emphasized the Symbolic; later work increasingly explored the Real and the knotting of all three.

### Heidegger

Lacan met **Martin Heidegger** and hosted him at Guitrancourt in 1955. He obtained permission to translate Heidegger’s essay “Logos” and published his French translation in *La Psychanalyse* in 1956. Sources report several meetings, the last in 1975.

The relationship was intellectual and social, not one of formal discipleship. Lacan drew on Heideggerian language while adapting it to psychoanalysis. Heidegger’s Nazi past makes the association historically significant, but there is no evidence that their contact constituted political collaboration.

### Acquisition of *L’Origine du monde*

In 1955 Lacan and Sylvia acquired Gustave Courbet’s 1866 painting *L’Origine du monde*. They installed it at their Guitrancourt house behind a sliding cover painted by Sylvia’s brother-in-law **André Masson**.

This is a documented art-ownership history, not merely legend. After Lacan’s and Sylvia’s deaths, the painting entered the Musée d’Orsay in 1995 through a tax settlement (*dation*).

### IPA recognition dispute and “excommunication”

The IPA investigated the SFP’s training practices. Lacan’s variable-length sessions were central. In 1963 recognition was made conditional on his removal from the list of training analysts.

His followers called this an “excommunication,” borrowing a religious-political metaphor. Strictly:

- **Documented fact:** Lacan was excluded from training functions recognized by the IPA.
- **Not literally true:** He was not prohibited by the state from practicing medicine or analysis, nor was he expelled from all psychoanalytic life.
- **Mythic function:** “Excommunication” cast the institutional dispute as Freud’s heterodox heir confronting an orthodoxy.

## The École Freudienne de Paris and Public Celebrity, 1964–1970

### Founding of the EFP

On June 21, 1964, Lacan founded the **École française de psychanalyse**, soon called the **École freudienne de Paris** (EFP). The BnF records its foundation in 1964 and dissolution in 1980.

His “Acte de fondation” organized work through small groups called **cartels**, generally four participants plus one, intended to frustrate hierarchy and passive discipleship.

In practice, the EFP became strongly centered on Lacan’s personal authority. This contradiction—anti-bureaucratic structure combined with charismatic leadership—was a recurring institutional problem.

### École normale supérieure

With support from **Louis Althusser**, Lacan moved the seminar to the École normale supérieure on rue d’Ulm. His 1964 seminar, later published as *Les quatre concepts fondamentaux de la psychanalyse*, reformulated the unconscious, repetition, transference, and drive.

The audience expanded beyond clinicians to philosophers, literary theorists, anthropologists, artists, and political intellectuals.

### EPHE appointment

Lacan was appointed *chargé de conférences* at the École pratique des hautes études from January 1964 through June 1972. This gave his teaching an academic framework without making him a conventional university professor.

### Baltimore, 1966

In October 1966 Lacan attended the Johns Hopkins conference “The Languages of Criticism and the Sciences of Man” in Baltimore, alongside Roland Barthes, Jacques Derrida, René Girard, Jean Hyppolite, and others.

Derrida later recalled that he and Lacan met and exchanged words only twice; their personal relationship was therefore far more limited than their later intellectual entanglement suggests.

### *Écrits*

Éditions du Seuil published *Écrits* in 1966. The nearly nine-hundred-page collection transformed Lacan from a Parisian seminar leader into an international intellectual figure.

The book was not a systematic treatise. It assembled rewritten lectures and essays whose style was compressed, allusive, punning, and deliberately difficult. Its success made Lacan part of the French theoretical constellation associated—sometimes too loosely—with structuralism and post-structuralism.

### Political position and May 1968

By the late 1960s Lacan was publicly associated with the intellectual left, but he never became a standard Marxist party theorist. His trajectory had moved from youthful Maurrassian interest through Surrealist anti-fascist circles to a nonparty, often skeptical politics.

During May 1968:

- Students and militants attended or challenged his teaching.
- Lacan did not present spontaneous revolt as liberation from authority.
- He famously told revolutionary students that they were seeking a new master and would obtain one. The exact wording and occasion vary in retellings; the sentiment is grounded in his 1969–1970 seminar but frequently detached from context.
- He analyzed the university, capitalism, mastery, and hysterical protest through his theory of four discourses.

## Vincennes, the Pass, and Institutional Conflict, 1967–1975

### The pass

In 1967 Lacan proposed **la passe**, a procedure meant to investigate how an analysand becomes an analyst. A candidate conveyed testimony through “passers” to a jury or institutional body.

The pass sought to replace bureaucratic credentialism with testimony about subjective transformation. It generated factional disputes:

- Some regarded it as Lacan’s major institutional innovation.
- Others considered it opaque, vulnerable to favoritism, or structurally incapable of escaping the master’s authority.
- The process contributed to resignations and internal division.

### Scilicet and unsigned publication

The EFP journal *Scilicet* adopted a system in which most contributions were unsigned, while Lacan retained the right to sign his own. Supporters saw this as resistance to prestige and proprietary authorship; critics saw a conspicuous exception that reinforced Lacan’s status.

### Vincennes

After 1968 a psychoanalysis department developed at the experimental University of Paris VIII at Vincennes. Lacanian associates, including Serge Leclaire and later Jacques-Alain Miller, played major roles.

In the mid-1970s Miller, with Lacan’s authority, reorganized or “took control” of the department. The intervention produced conflict with Gilles Deleuze, Jean-François Lyotard, and others. Foucault and Barthes reportedly defended the department against external threats, although they were not simply Lacanian adherents.

### Luce Irigaray

**Documented institutional conflict, disputed agency:** Feminist philosopher and linguist **Luce Irigaray**, a participant in Lacanian circles, published *Speculum de l’autre femme* in 1974. She subsequently lost her teaching position at Vincennes and was ostracized or expelled from Lacanian institutional life.

Later accounts often state that “Lacan expelled Irigaray.” The broad rupture is established; the precise administrative chain and Lacan’s direct personal role require care. Her work became one of the strongest feminist critiques of Freud’s and Lacan’s phallocentrism.

## International Expansion and Late Theory, 1970–1979

### Seminar locations and audiences

After the ENS period, Lacan lectured at the Faculté de droit opposite the Panthéon and other Paris venues. His audience could number in the hundreds.

### Four discourses

In *L’envers de la psychanalyse* (1969–1970) he formalized the discourses of:

- the Master;
- the University;
- the Hysteric;
- the Analyst.

He later added the capitalist discourse. These were formal arrangements of subject, knowledge, master signifier, and object *a*, not descriptions of ordinary conversation alone.

### Sexuation and feminine jouissance

In the early 1970s, particularly *Encore* (1972–1973), Lacan developed formulae of sexuation. “Woman does not exist” meant that no universal signifier exhaustively defines the feminine position; it did not literally deny the existence of women.

The formulation became both influential and notorious:

- Feminist readers including Irigaray criticized the continued centrality of the phallus and the positioning of woman as other or “not-all.”
- Other feminist theorists used Lacan to distinguish anatomy, social identity, symbolic position, and desire.
- The disagreement remains theoretical and political; no consensus interpretation exists.

### Mathematics, topology, and knots

Late Lacan increasingly employed:

- set theory;
- logic;
- topology;
- Möbius strips;
- cross-caps;
- Borromean knots;
- quasi-algebraic “mathemes.”

His aim was to transmit relations resistant to intuitive psychological paraphrase. Critics, especially scientists and analytic philosophers, argued that he used mathematics metaphorically, incorrectly, or without explanatory gain. Defenders reply that the objects were formalizations within psychoanalysis, not contributions to mathematics or physics.

### Joyce and the sinthome

In 1975–1976 Lacan’s Seminar XXIII treated **James Joyce** through the archaic spelling *sinthome*. He proposed that a fourth ring—the sinthome—could stabilize the Real, Symbolic, and Imaginary.

Lacan said that he had encountered Joyce in Paris during his youth. This is a **reported recollection**, not evidence of a substantial relationship. Jacques Aubert supplied significant Joyce expertise to the seminar.

### China and François Cheng

Lacan worked with writer and sinologist **François Cheng** on classical Chinese poetry, writing, and thought. These exchanges influenced his late interest in the written mark, emptiness, and the relation between speech and script.

### Travel

Documented major travel included:

- Marienbad, 1936, for the IPA congress.
- England, 1945, to observe wartime psychiatry.
- Zurich, 1949, for the IPA congress.
- Rome, 1953, for the Rome Discourse.
- Germany and France-based encounters with Heidegger.
- Baltimore, 1966, for the structuralism conference.
- Italy repeatedly for lectures and intellectual contacts.
- Japan, where his interest in writing and Japanese language informed “Lituraterre” and later reflections.
- Caracas, July 1980, for his final international intervention.

A full itinerary cannot be reconstructed from the presently consulted sources.

## Clinical Practice, Money, and Professional Controversies

### Variable-length and very short sessions

This was the central documented professional controversy of Lacan’s lifetime.

The normal IPA practice was approximately fifty minutes. Lacan ended sessions at variable moments, sometimes after only a few minutes. His rationale was that an interpretive cut or **scansion** could punctuate significant speech and prevent the clock from becoming a ritual that encouraged patients to defer important material.

Critics argued that:

- extremely short sessions violated the analytic frame;
- Lacan could see more patients and collect more fees;
- abrupt endings were humiliating or abusive;
- the practice intensified dependency on his authority;
- it contributed to the IPA’s refusal to recognize him as a training analyst.

Defenders argued that:

- sessions did not have a natural therapeutic duration;
- the cut acted as interpretation;
- variable time disrupted rehearsed speech and resistance;
- the method should not be reduced to every session being short.

**Documented fact:** Lacan practiced variable-length sessions and this influenced the IPA dispute.  
**Reported fact:** Some sessions lasted only a few minutes or less.  
**Unresolved question:** The frequency, fees, clinical outcomes, and degree of informed patient agreement cannot be reconstructed comprehensively. No systematic patient register or controlled outcome evidence was located.

### High fees and wealth

Memoirs and biographies describe Lacan as charging unusually high fees, sometimes requiring payment in cash and sometimes adjusting fees according to what he believed a patient could pay. A large and rapid patient flow, combined with short sessions, produced substantial income.

He owned or maintained:

- a major Paris residence and consulting rooms, notably at 5 rue de Lille and later connections with 74 rue d’Assas;
- the Guitrancourt country estate La Prévôté;
- an art collection including Courbet’s *L’Origine du monde*.

Exact income, assets, tax arrangements, investment vehicles, and estate value were not established by the sources consulted. Claims that he became rich through abbreviated sessions are plausible biographical interpretations, not audited financial findings.

### Conduct toward patients

Biographical testimony alleges that Lacan could:

- shout at patients;
- strike or physically propel them;
- receive them while dressing or moving between other activities;
- hold sessions in unconventional circumstances;
- sleep with women who had been or were connected to his analytic practice.

The strongest clearly attributable evidence concerning an analyst–partner overlap is Catherine Millot’s account that she lived with Lacan while continuing analysis with him.

Other anecdotes—kicking a woman out, striking analysands, charging for a virtually nonexistent encounter—come from memoirs and hostile or retrospective testimony. They should be labeled **reported allegations**, not established as a general clinical pattern. No criminal charge, civil judgment, or professional disciplinary ruling concerning assault or sexual abuse by Lacan was located.

By current clinical standards, a simultaneous sexual and analytic relationship would ordinarily constitute a serious boundary violation. This is a present-day professional comparison, not a retrospective court finding.

### Aimée and Didier Anzieu

The sequence involving Marguerite Anzieu and her son presents an independently documented conflict of roles. The ethical questions concern disclosure, confidentiality, use of a patient’s material, and the later analysis of her son. No evidence was found of adjudication during Lacan’s lifetime.

### Absence of conventional outcome evidence

Lacan’s clinical reputation depends heavily on testimony, institutional lineage, and theoretical influence. There are no modern randomized trials establishing the efficacy of “Lacan’s treatment,” and Lacan’s heterogeneous, highly individualized technique would be difficult to treat as a standardized intervention.

That absence neither proves ineffectiveness nor validates the reported cures. It limits factual claims about clinical success.

## Personal Style, Authority, and the “Lacanian Scene”

Witnesses describe a carefully constructed appearance: tailored clothing, unusual shirts or collars, cigars, theatrical pauses, elaborate automobiles, sudden gestures, and an oracular mode of speech. His lectures mixed close textual reading, jokes, puns, diagrams, provocation, silence, and improvised displays.

Two interpretations coexist:

- **Sympathetic testimony:** The style activated listeners and prevented theory from becoming a fixed doctrine.
- **Hostile testimony:** The obscurity and theatricality concealed weak arguments and fostered cultic submission.

Both are interpretations. The observable basis—dense prose, performance, charismatic centrality, and competing schools—is well documented.

## Intellectual Development

### 1920s–early 1930s: psychiatry and personality

Principal concerns:

- paranoia;
- hallucination;
- personality;
- forensic psychiatry;
- phenomenological description;
- psychosis as connected to social meaning.

### 1930s–1940s: image, identification, aggression

Principal concepts:

- mirror stage;
- ideal ego and ego ideal;
- imaginary identification;
- aggression as structurally linked to rivalry;
- social and familial complexes.

### 1950s: “return to Freud” and the Symbolic

Principal concepts:

- primacy of the signifier;
- unconscious structured like a language;
- Name-of-the-Father;
- paternal metaphor;
- foreclosure in psychosis;
- desire as desire of the Other;
- distinction between need, demand, and desire.

### Late 1950s–1960s: the Real, ethics, and object *a*

He moved beyond a predominantly linguistic structuralism toward:

- the Thing (*das Ding*);
- jouissance;
- anxiety;
- gaze and voice as partial objects;
- **objet petit a**, cause rather than obtainable object of desire;
- the barred or divided subject.

### Late 1960s–1970s: discourse, sexuation, writing, topology

Principal themes:

- social bonds as discourses;
- surplus-jouissance;
- sexuation;
- “there is no sexual relation,” meaning no complete symbolic formula guarantees complementarity;
- *lalangue*, emphasizing the material and affective effects of language;
- knots and the sinthome;
- the written trace as distinct from speech.

## The Dissolution, 1979–1981

Internal strains in the EFP concerned bureaucracy, succession, the pass, competing factions, and the widening gap between Lacan’s anti-institutional rhetoric and a large organization centered upon him.

On January 5, 1980, Lacan issued a letter announcing the dissolution of the EFP. *Le Monde* reported it on January 9.

Members challenged whether he could legally dissolve an association unilaterally. Contemporary reports noted litigation and disagreement over whether the required majority had been obtained. The association’s formal dissolution was ultimately registered on October 18, 1980.

Lacan asked those wishing to continue with him to write. More than a thousand responses were reportedly received. He initiated **La Cause freudienne**, which developed into the **École de la Cause freudienne**, with Jacques-Alain Miller becoming decisive in its organization.

In March 1980 Lacan addressed approximately eight hundred people at the Hôtel PLM Saint-Jacques. In July he traveled to Caracas for an international gathering and declared, in substance: “It is up to you to be Lacanians if you wish. I am Freudian.”

This became a foundational Lacanian saying. Its documentary basis is strong, although exact translations differ.

## Illness, Decline, and Death

Lacan was injured or substantially shaken in a 1978 automobile accident. Thereafter witnesses found him increasingly fatigued and silent; his late seminars sometimes consisted largely of diagrams or manipulations of knots.

He developed colon cancer and delayed surgery. Sources report aphasia after a stroke during his final period. He spent time at Judith and Jacques-Alain Miller’s home.

On September 9, 1981, urgent surgery was performed for an abdominal or colonic tumor at the Clinique Hartmann. He died from postoperative renal failure or complications of colon cancer.

The words “Je suis obstiné… Je disparais” (“I am obstinate… I disappear”) are widely reported as final words. They are **reported testimony**, not an independently recorded utterance.

### Dispute over the circumstances of death and burial

Élisabeth Roudinesco later wrote that Lacan died at the clinic under a false name, had resisted treating his cancer, wished to die in Italy, and wanted Catholic funeral rites. Judith Miller sued her for defamation.

The defense maintained that the burial at Guitrancourt in privacy and without a full public ceremony was factual. This litigation concerned Roudinesco’s later account, not wrongdoing by Lacan. The exact accuracy of every asserted final wish remains disputed.

Lacan was buried at Guitrancourt. His brother Marc-François participated in religious observances and reportedly summarized the life with: “Jacques Lacan spoke.”

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### Findings from the dedicated adversarial search

Searches combining Lacan’s name with *controversy, scandal, allegations, lawsuit, fraud, accusations, conspiracy,* and *criticism*, including material published or indexed in 2024–2026, produced no substantiated evidence that Lacan was:

- convicted of a crime;
- formally charged with fraud;
- investigated for corruption or money laundering;
- connected to an intelligence organization;
- a participant in political violence;
- the subject of a proven financial fraud;
- accused in a newly documented 2024–2026 criminal case.

Recent material mostly revisits older controversies: short sessions, patient boundaries, institutional authoritarianism, feminism, scientific validity, and control of the seminar editions.

### Professional-ethical disputes

1. **Short sessions — documented practice and institutional dispute.**  
   The IPA regarded the technique as incompatible with recognized training. Whether it was clinically innovative or financially exploitative remains contested.

2. **Sexual/analytic boundary crossing — partly documented, incompletely reconstructed.**  
   Catherine Millot’s memoir establishes her dual position as partner and analysand. Broader claims that Lacan “often slept with patients” exceed the securely documented evidence reviewed here.

3. **Physical aggression — reported allegation.**  
   Biographical anecdotes say he struck or pushed analysands and once kicked a woman out. No contemporaneous complaint or adjudication was located.

4. **Fees and throughput — reported fact with incomplete financial evidence.**  
   High fees and numerous brief appointments are repeatedly attested. Exact revenues and whether particular payments were exploitative cannot be verified from published anecdotes alone.

5. **Aimée/Anzieu conflict — documented sequence, disputed ethics.**  
   The underlying relationships are established; the adequacy of consent and disclosure remains debated.

6. **Charismatic domination — interpretation grounded in institutional history.**  
   Lacan repeatedly attacked bureaucracy while retaining exceptional authority over admission, recognition, publication, and dissolution.

### Scientific and intellectual criticism

#### Chomsky’s “charlatan” judgment

Noam Chomsky, recalling an encounter with Lacan, called him a conscious charlatan. This is a hostile personal and intellectual judgment, not evidence of legal fraud. Peter D. Mathews’s *Lacan the Charlatan* systematically tests the accusation rather than simply accepting it.

#### Sokal and Bricmont

Alan Sokal and Jean Bricmont criticized Lacan’s deployment of mathematics and science, especially topology and imaginary numbers, as confused or abusive.

- **Documented fact:** Lacan used mathematical terminology and objects.
- **Critical interpretation:** The uses do not meet ordinary standards of mathematical exposition or empirical science.
- **Defense:** Lacan was constructing psychoanalytic mathemes and analogical formalizations, not offering new mathematical theorems.
- **Unresolved issue:** The value of these formalizations depends on philosophical and psychoanalytic criteria for which no shared adjudicating standard exists.

#### Obscurantism

Critics argue that Lacan’s syntax, neologisms, and equivocations prevent falsification and create prestige through incomprehensibility. Defenders argue that ordinary transparent prose would erase the linguistic phenomena his work stages. Difficulty is observable; whether it is necessary or fraudulent is interpretive.

#### Feminist criticism

Irigaray and other feminist thinkers identified phallocentrism in Lacan’s account of the Symbolic, castration, and sexuation. Other feminists—among them Juliet Mitchell, Jacqueline Rose, Joan Copjec, and later theorists—used Lacan to argue that sexual identity is not reducible to biology. Lacan therefore occupies opposed positions within feminist history.

### Publication litigation after his death

Lacan appointed Jacques-Alain Miller to establish the text of the seminars. The published volumes are editorial reconstructions from stenotypes, recordings, and notes.

Critics charged Miller with:

- publishing too slowly;
- suppressing variants and contextual material;
- presenting edited texts without full critical apparatus;
- centralizing control over Lacan’s intellectual estate.

In 2007 the Association des amis de Jacques Lacan sued Miller seeking faster publication. The Paris court rejected the demands and ordered the association to pay nominal damages for abusive procedure and publish the judgment. This was not a ruling that every editorial decision was beyond criticism; it rejected the attempt to compel Miller’s pace of work.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The French Freud”

- **Story:** Lacan was Freud’s unique French successor.
- **Origin and circulation:** Publishers, journalists, adherents, and introductory works.
- **Evidence:** He unquestionably reorganized French psychoanalysis and made an influential return to Freud.
- **Against:** He departed substantially from Freud through structural linguistics, Hegel, topology, and his own institutional inventions.
- **Effect:** It gave him international visibility while obscuring rival French traditions.

### The excommunicated heretic

- **Story:** An orthodox IPA expelled Lacan because it feared his truth.
- **Evidence:** IPA recognition was indeed made conditional on excluding him as a training analyst.
- **Qualification:** The dispute also concerned concrete training standards and his session technique, not doctrine alone.
- **Effect:** It provided Lacanianism with a foundational drama of persecution and return.

### The miraculous clinician

- **Story:** A gesture, pun, or seconds-long session could transform a life.
- **Circulation:** Patient memoirs, oral tradition, documentary testimony, and institutional teaching.
- **Evidence:** Some former patients report decisive benefit.
- **Against:** Other witnesses report humiliation, dependency, rage, or harm; systematic outcomes are unavailable.
- **Effect:** It sustained both hagiography and hostile counter-legend.

### The avaricious analyst

- **Story:** Lacan shortened sessions solely to maximize income.
- **Evidence:** Brief sessions increased possible daily throughput, and high fees are attested.
- **Against:** He articulated a clinical theory of scansion and did not reduce every encounter to the same duration.
- **Status:** A plausible motive attributed by critics, not a demonstrated exclusive explanation.

### The deliberate charlatan

- **Story:** Lacan knowingly produced nonsense to deceive intellectuals.
- **Major circulator:** Noam Chomsky; later scientific and anti-theory critics.
- **Evidence offered:** obscurity, mathematical language, personal theatricality, and lack of conventional empirical validation.
- **Against:** Decades of internally connected concepts, sustained clinical teaching, identifiable sources, and serious critical scholarship.
- **Status:** Hostile interpretation, not proven fraud.

### Lacan as an Acéphale initiate

- **Story:** He belonged to Bataille’s secret, quasi-sacral society.
- **Evidence:** Lacan moved within Bataille’s circle and may have attended associated gatherings.
- **Against:** Clear membership documentation is weak.
- **Status:** Reported association shading into legend.

### “There is no sexual relationship” and “Woman does not exist”

- **Story in popular circulation:** Lacan denied sex or women.
- **Documentary meaning:** The claims concern the absence of a complete symbolic formula guaranteeing sexual complementarity and the inability of a universal signifier to totalize “Woman.”
- **Effect:** Detached quotations made Lacan appear either radically anti-essentialist or straightforwardly misogynistic.
- **Status:** The popular literal readings are distortions, though feminist objections to the underlying theory remain substantive.

### The inaccessible hidden masterwork

- **Story:** Miller or the Lacan family has withheld a definitive, unedited Lacan whose release would transform interpretation.
- **Evidence:** Numerous seminars remained unpublished for decades, and variants exist.
- **Against:** Oral seminars do not yield a single uncontested “original”; transcription necessarily involves editorial choices.
- **Effect:** Publication delays generated lawsuits, rival transcriptions, unauthorized editions, and conspiracy-inflected factional claims.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Psychoanalytic institutions

Lacan’s death did not produce a unified succession. Instead, French and international Lacanianism divided into multiple schools:

- École de la Cause freudienne and the World Association of Psychoanalysis, associated with Jacques-Alain Miller.
- Association lacanienne internationale.
- École lacanienne de psychanalyse.
- Numerous national and regional societies in Latin America, Europe, Israel, Australia, North America, and elsewhere.

The fragmentation reflects doctrinal disagreements, personal rivalries, publication control, and competing interpretations of the cartel and pass.

### Philosophy and critical theory

Lacan influenced:

- Louis Althusser’s account of ideology.
- Slavoj Žižek’s philosophy and political theory.
- Alain Badiou.
- Ernesto Laclau and Chantal Mouffe.
- Film theory, especially theories of gaze, identification, and spectatorship.
- Literary criticism and deconstruction.
- Feminist, queer, and gender theory—both positively and as a principal object of critique.
- Cultural studies, political theory, architecture, and visual art.

### Psychiatry and clinical psychoanalysis

His continuing clinical influence is uneven:

- Strong in France, Argentina, Brazil, Spain, Belgium, and parts of Latin America.
- Institutionally weaker in mainstream Anglophone psychiatry and clinical psychology.
- Frequently practiced outside medical psychiatry.
- Criticized for limited empirical outcome research and obscure transmission.
- Defended as a theory of subjectivity and speech not reducible to symptom-management protocols.

### Historical reinterpretation

Broad phases of reception include:

1. **1950s–1960s:** heterodox Freudian reformer.
2. **Late 1960s–1970s:** French intellectual celebrity associated with structuralism.
3. **1980s–1990s:** institutional fragmentation, feminist debate, English-language expansion.
4. **1990s–2000s:** science-war criticism, deconstruction debates, growth of Žižekian Lacanianism.
5. **2010s–2020s:** renewed biographical scrutiny, discussion of clinical boundaries, estate politics, gender and trans debates, and continuing publication of seminars.

No major new criminal or financial revelation concerning Lacan himself emerged in the 2024–2026 adversarial search. Recent disputes continue to concern the interpretation and institutional use of his legacy.

## Atlas Connections

Only documented or strongly reported intersections with names on the supplied atlas roster are included.

- **Sigmund Freud — documented intellectual connection, no personal meeting established.** Lacan translated Freud in 1932 and organized his mature project as a “return to Freud.” Freud was the principal declared authority of Lacan’s psychoanalysis. Lacan’s line “I am Freudian” at Caracas in 1980 distinguished Freud from later “Lacanians.”

- **Carl Gustav Jung — documented intellectual reference, no substantial personal connection established.** Lacan discussed Jung within the history of psychoanalysis but did not belong to Jung’s school. Claims presenting Lacan as simply a peer or disciple of Jung are misleading.

- **André Breton — reported personal and documented milieu connection, Paris, 1930s.** Lacan circulated in Surrealist networks and was associated with the Bataille–Breton *Contre-Attaque* milieu. The evidence supports shared circles and meetings, not a continuous close friendship.

- **Antonin Artaud — documented shared psychiatric/Surrealist milieu; direct relationship not firmly established.** Artaud formed part of the avant-garde and psychiatric world in which Lacan’s early work circulated. No adequately sourced sustained personal relationship was established in this research.

- **Albert Camus — no supported personal crossing found.** Both occupied postwar French intellectual life, but contemporaneity alone is insufficient.

- **Claude Lévi-Strauss — documented intellectual and social connection, Paris, especially 1950s onward.** Lacan repeatedly acknowledged a major debt to Lévi-Strauss’s structural anthropology. They met socially and participated in overlapping Parisian intellectual networks.

- **Martin Heidegger — documented meeting, translation, and correspondence connection.** Lacan hosted Heidegger at Guitrancourt in 1955, received authorization to translate “Logos,” and met him on several occasions, reportedly last in 1975.

- **Jacques Derrida — documented meetings and intellectual dispute.** They both attended the 1966 Johns Hopkins conference in Baltimore. Derrida later said they exchanged words on only two occasions. Derrida’s “Le facteur de la vérité” criticized Lacan’s reading of Poe; “Pour l’amour de Lacan” revisited their relation.

- **René Girard — documented conference connection.** Girard participated in organizing or mediating the 1966 Baltimore conference and, according to Derrida’s recollection, introduced him to Lacan there.

- **Roland Barthes — documented shared intellectual scene and conference connection.** Barthes and Lacan appeared at the 1966 Baltimore meeting and in overlapping Paris structuralist and Tel Quel networks. Evidence reviewed here does not justify calling them intimate friends.

- **Paul-Michel Foucault — documented intellectual and institutional connection.** Foucault engaged with Lacan’s place in psychoanalysis and, according to later testimony, helped protect the Vincennes psychoanalysis department. Their relation was one of intermittent alliance and theoretical distance, not discipleship.

- **Gilles Deleuze — documented intellectual relationship and later conflict.** Lacan responded favorably to some of Deleuze’s 1960s work. The relationship became antagonistic around Deleuze and Guattari’s *Anti-Oedipus* (1972) and the mid-1970s Vincennes struggle.

- **Pierre-Félix Guattari — documented analyst–analysand and teacher–student connection.** Guattari underwent analysis with Lacan and participated in Lacanian institutions before developing schizoanalysis with Deleuze. *Anti-Oedipus* marked a major intellectual and institutional break.

- **Jean-Paul Sartre — documented shared postwar intellectual scene; limited direct relation.** Lacan engaged Sartrean philosophy and became, like Sartre, a Paris public intellectual. The sources reviewed do not establish a close collaboration.

- **Simone de Beauvoir — no securely documented personal connection found.** Shared milieu and indirect theoretical relevance do not prove contact.

- **Pablo Picasso — reported social connection.** Picasso visited or moved within the artistic circle around Lacan, Sylvia Bataille, André Masson, and Guitrancourt. Reports specifically place Picasso among visitors who saw Courbet’s concealed *L’Origine du monde*.

- **James Joyce — reported brief encounter; documented intellectual influence.** Lacan recalled encountering Joyce in Paris in his youth, but no substantial relationship is established. Joyce became the central subject of Seminar XXIII in 1975–1976.

- **Edgar Allan Poe — documented textual influence, no possible personal contact.** Lacan’s “Seminar on ‘The Purloined Letter’” made Poe’s tale a major model of signifier circulation. Derrida later disputed Lacan’s reading.

- **Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel — documented intellectual influence.** Lacan’s engagement was mediated principally by Kojève and Hyppolite. Desire, recognition, and master–slave dialectic became central to his early mature theory.

- **Karl Marx — documented intellectual influence.** Lacan used Marx’s commodity and surplus-value analyses in developing surplus-jouissance and capitalist discourse. He was not a conventional Marxist party intellectual.

- **Friedrich Nietzsche — documented formative influence.** Lacan read Nietzsche in German as a young man and wrote or delivered a school-period tribute according to biographical testimony.

- **René Descartes — documented intellectual influence.** Lacan repeatedly reformulated the Cartesian cogito to theorize the divided subject and the subject of the unconscious.

- **Immanuel Kant — documented intellectual influence.** “Kant avec Sade” aligned Kantian moral formalism with Sadean enjoyment in a deliberately provocative structural comparison.

- **Plato and Aristotle — documented intellectual references.** Seminar VIII uses Plato’s *Symposium* extensively; Aristotle is central to Lacan’s discussions of ethics, causation, rhetoric, and logic.

- **Aurelius Augustinus — documented intellectual reference.** Augustine’s account of infant jealousy appears in Lacan’s discussions of aggression and identification.

- **Marcel Proust — documented literary reference, no personal contact.** Proust’s writing informed Lacan’s examples concerning desire, memory, and love.

- **Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling, Søren Kierkegaard, and Giambattista Vico — intellectual references occur, but no biographical connection.** They should not be represented as personal relations.

- **Slavoj Žižek — documented posthumous influence only.** Žižek never met Lacan in the sources reviewed but became one of the most internationally visible philosophical interpreters of Lacan.

For the many hundreds of other atlas names, no documented crossing was established. Absence from this section means that the research found no support beyond possible intellectual analogy, geographic overlap, or contemporaneity.

## Chronology

- **1901, April 13:** Born at 95 boulevard Beaumarchais, Paris.
- **1902:** Brother Raymond born; dies in infancy.
- **1903:** Sister Madeleine-Marie born.
- **1907–1918/1919:** Studies at Collège Stanislas.
- **1908:** Brother Marc-Marie born.
- **1917–1918:** Studies philosophy with Jean Baruzi.
- **1920:** Begins medicine at the University of Paris.
- **1923–1925:** Reads Maurras, meets Léon Daudet and briefly Maurras, attends Action française circles; considers Senegal.
- **1926–1927:** Enters psychiatric specialization at Sainte-Anne.
- **1928:** Works at the police prefecture infirmary under Clérambault; publishes early medical work.
- **1929:** Brother Marc enters the Benedictines; Lacan’s relationship with Olesia Sienkiewicz belongs approximately to this period.
- **1931:** Qualifies in forensic medicine; begins examining Marguerite Pantaine after the Duflos attack.
- **1932, June:** Begins analysis with Rudolph Loewenstein.
- **1932, September 7:** Defends doctoral thesis on paranoid psychosis and “Aimée.”
- **1933:** Publishes in *Minotaure* on paranoia, style, and the Papin sisters.
- **1933–1939:** Attends Kojève’s Hegel seminars.
- **1934, January 29:** Marries Marie-Louise Blondin; joins SPP.
- **1935:** Associated with the Contre-Attaque milieu.
- **1936:** Presents first mirror-stage paper at Marienbad; Ernest Jones cuts the presentation short.
- **1937:** Daughter Caroline born.
- **1938:** Becomes full SPP member; publishes “Les complexes familiaux.”
- **1938–1939:** Relationship with Sylvia Bataille begins.
- **1939:** Son Thibaut born.
- **1940:** Daughter Sibylle born.
- **1941:** Daughter Judith born; divorce from Malou finalized December 15.
- **1945:** Visits England to observe wartime psychiatry; publishes “Logical Time.”
- **1946:** Presents “Psychical Causality.”
- **1947:** Publishes “British Psychiatry and the War.”
- **1948:** Presents “Aggressiveness in Psychoanalysis”; mother Émilie dies.
- **1949:** Presents revised mirror-stage paper in Zurich.
- **1951:** Begins private seminars.
- **1952:** Seminar activity moves toward Sainte-Anne.
- **1953:** Marries Sylvia; leaves SPP with the SFP group; delivers Rome Discourse; begins numbered annual seminars.
- **1955:** Acquires Courbet’s *L’Origine du monde*; hosts Heidegger at Guitrancourt.
- **1956:** Publishes translation of Heidegger’s “Logos.”
- **1960:** Father Alfred dies.
- **1963:** IPA recognition made conditional on Lacan’s exclusion as training analyst; seminar at Sainte-Anne ends.
- **1964, January:** Begins EPHE appointment and ENS seminar.
- **1964, June 21:** Founds the École freudienne de Paris.
- **1966:** Publishes *Écrits*; attends Johns Hopkins conference in Baltimore.
- **1967:** Introduces the pass.
- **1968:** May uprising; Vincennes institutional developments begin.
- **1969–1970:** Delivers Seminar XVII on the four discourses.
- **1971:** Publishes “Lituraterre”; develops *lalangue* and writing themes.
- **1972:** Deleuze and Guattari publish *Anti-Oedipus*; major rupture with Guattari’s Lacanian formation.
- **1972–1973:** Delivers *Encore*.
- **1973–1974:** Benoît Jacquot films the interviews associated with *Télévision*.
- **1974:** Irigaray rupture; Lacan presents “La troisième.”
- **1975:** Reorganization conflict at Vincennes; later meeting with Heidegger reported.
- **1975–1976:** Seminar on Joyce and the sinthome.
- **1978:** Automobile accident; visible physical decline.
- **1979:** Last regular annual seminar, *La topologie et le temps*.
- **1980, January 5:** Announces dissolution of the EFP.
- **1980, March:** Public “Dissolution” address at PLM Saint-Jacques.
- **1980, July:** Caracas conference, his last major international appearance.
- **1980, October 18:** Formal dissolution of EFP recorded.
- **1980–1981:** Formation of the Cause freudienne and École de la Cause freudienne.
- **1981, September 9:** Dies following emergency surgery for colon cancer/abdominal tumor.
- **1981, September 10:** Private funeral observances; burial at Guitrancourt.
- **1993:** Sylvia Bataille dies; Roudinesco’s major biography appears.
- **1995:** *L’Origine du monde* enters the Musée d’Orsay.
- **2001:** *Autres écrits* published.
- **2007:** Court rejects an attempt to compel Jacques-Alain Miller to publish the seminars faster.
- **2011–2012:** Litigation and public conflict over Roudinesco’s account of Lacan’s death.
- **2014:** Nathalie Jaudel publishes a polemical attack on Roudinesco’s biographical method.
- **2016:** Catherine Millot publishes *La Vie avec Lacan*.
- **2024–2026:** Continued seminar publication, archival circulation, and debate; no newly substantiated criminal or financial case against Lacan found in the dedicated adversarial search.
