# Hernando Arias de Saavedra: Research Dossier

## Scope, method, and evidentiary conventions

This dossier reconstructs the life of Hernando Arias de Saavedra from surviving correspondence, colonial administrative records, archival catalogues, modern academic studies, genealogical research, and later historiography.

The evidence is uneven. Hernandarias lived before systematic civil registration, newspapers, or modern personal archives. Exact dates differ among historians, official chronologies sometimes conflate interim and substantive appointments, and much biographical writing about him is commemorative. The following labels are therefore used:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a contemporary document, archival description, or judicial/administrative record.
- **Reported fact:** stated by reputable historical scholarship but not independently confirmed here from the original manuscript.
- **Allegation:** accusation made by an identified contemporary or later source without a surviving final judgment establishing it.
- **Court or administrative finding:** conclusion of a judicial, residency, or administrative proceeding.
- **Disputed claim:** materially different versions exist.
- **Legend or myth:** story repeated in commemorative or popular history without adequate contemporary proof.
- **Unresolved:** the surviving or accessible evidence does not permit a firm conclusion.

The principal modern critical source used is Darío G. Barriera’s “Hernandarias de Saavedra en la historiografía rioplatense, o las raíces coloniales de un nacionalismo criollo” (2018), based on material from the Archivo General de Indias, the Gaspar García Viñas collection of the Biblioteca Nacional Argentina, and civil files in Santa Fe. It is especially useful because it separates the documentary Hernandarias from the apologetic and hostile traditions constructed around him.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Principal name:** Hernando Arias de Saavedra.

**Common shortened name or sobriquet:** Hernandarias.

**Other forms found in documents and historiography:**

- Hernán Arias de Saavedra.
- Hernando Arias de Saavedra.
- Hernandarias de Saavedra.
- Fernando Arias de Saavedra, an occasional modernization or error.
- Hernando Suárez de Toledo Saavedra, or expanded genealogical versions such as Hernando Suárez de Toledo Saavedra y Sanabria Calderón. These extended constructions are largely genealogical; surviving public records normally identify him as Hernando or Hernán Arias de Saavedra.
- “Hijo de la tierra,” a descriptive epithet popularized above all by Raúl A. Molina’s 1948 biography.
- “First criollo governor,” a durable historical designation requiring qualification below.

**Status and titles:**

- Hidalgo and member of an established Spanish-conquest family.
- Soldier and militia captain.
- Encomendero, landholder, rancher, and livestock proprietor.
- Municipal and provincial officer.
- Lieutenant governor and chief justice in Asunción and other jurisdictions.
- Governor and captain general of the united Río de la Plata–Paraguay jurisdiction during several periods.
- Protector of Indigenous people or *protector de los naturales*, according to later records and royal recognition.
- Royal commissioner or judicial investigator in later disputes.

**Birth:** Probably Asunción, in the Governorate of the Río de la Plata and Paraguay, in **1561**.

Alternative years include **1560** and **1564**. Barriera, relying on genealogical and archival research, gives 1561. The frequently repeated exact date **10 September 1561** appears in modern genealogical databases but was not confirmed here from a contemporary baptismal record. The safest formulation is therefore: **born in Asunción, probably in 1561**.

**Death:** Santa Fe la Vieja, Governorate of the Río de la Plata, in **1634**. The exact day is not securely established in the sources consulted. Depending on which birth year is used, he was approximately seventy-three or seventy-four.

**Burial:** Reported in commemorative literature as having been buried in Santa Fe, sometimes said to be near or with his wife in a church associated with the Franciscan establishment. The exact original grave and the history of any transfer during the relocation of Santa Fe require archival or archaeological confirmation.

**Nationality in modern terms:** None. He was a subject of the Spanish monarchy, born in the American territory governed from Asunción. Later Paraguayan, Argentine, and Uruguayan historiographies all incorporated him into their national prehistories.

## Family and Ancestry

### Maternal family

Hernandarias’s mother was **María de Sanabria Calderón**.

She was a daughter of **Juan de Sanabria**, who capitulated with the Crown in 1547 as the prospective third adelantado of the Río de la Plata, and **Mencía Calderón**. Juan de Sanabria died in 1549 before taking possession of the government.

Mencía Calderón participated in one of the most notable early migrations of Spanish women to southern South America. Her expedition departed Sanlúcar de Barrameda on 19 April 1550, reached the Brazilian coast, and eventually proceeded overland toward Asunción. Barriera reports approximately 300 participants, including about fifty marriageable women and Mencía’s daughters. The exact numbers vary in the literature.

María de Sanabria first married **Hernando de Trejo or Trejo y Carvajal**. Their children included:

- **Hernando de Trejo y Sanabria**, Franciscan and later bishop of Tucumán, an important religious and political supporter of Hernandarias.
- **María de Trejo y Sanabria**.

After Trejo’s death, María married Hernandarias’s father.

### Paternal family

Hernandarias’s father was **Martín Suárez de Toledo**, a conquistador, encomendero, municipal officer, and political figure in Asunción. Sources associate him with Álvar Núñez Cabeza de Vaca’s expedition and later with the faction of Felipe de Cáceres. He served as alcalde of first vote in Asunción in 1568 and subsequently exercised high provincial authority.

The paternal family claimed descent from the Spanish Arias de Saavedra lineage. Hernandarias reportedly took the surname of his paternal grandfather, also named Hernando Arias de Saavedra, who was associated with the office of *correo mayor* of Seville. Later genealogies extend this ancestry through aristocratic and royal lines, but many such remote pedigrees rely on early-modern status claims and should not be accepted without separate verification.

### Siblings and half-siblings

Barriera states that Martín Suárez de Toledo and María de Sanabria had seven legitimate children, all born in Asunción. Securely identified members include:

- **Hernando Arias de Saavedra**.
- **Juana de Sanabria y Saavedra**, who married Juan de Garay y Becerra.
- **Martín Suárez de Toledo the younger**, who married Francisca de Moscoso y de la Vega.
- Other children are listed in genealogical sources as Ana, Francisca, Inés, and Beatriz, although their order and exact names vary.

His maternal half-siblings from María de Sanabria’s first marriage included Bishop **Hernando de Trejo y Sanabria** and María de Trejo y Sanabria.

The family formed part of the interconnected conquest elite of Asunción, Santa Fe, Córdoba, and Buenos Aires. Public office, military participation, encomiendas, marriage, and landholding reinforced one another.

### Connection claimed to Hernán Cortés

Barriera reports that Juan de Sanabria, Hernandarias’s maternal grandfather, was a relative of **Hernán Cortés**. The precise degree is not established in the cited passage. This is therefore a **reported genealogical connection**, not a demonstrated close personal relationship; Cortés died before Hernandarias was born.

### Marriage

In **1582**, at Santa Fe, Hernandarias married **Jerónima de Contreras**, also called **Jerónima de Garay y Becerra**. She was a daughter of:

- **Juan de Garay**, founder of Santa Fe in 1573 and refounder of Buenos Aires in 1580.
- **Isabel de Becerra or Isabel de Becerra y Contreras**.

The marriage joined the Saavedra–Sanabria family to the Garay network. It was not merely domestic: it placed Hernandarias at the center of the dominant familial and political bloc of Santa Fe and connected his career to Garay’s settlers, descendants, encomiendas, and territorial projects.

Jerónima reportedly lived until 1649.

### Children

Hernandarias and Jerónima had three daughters:

1. **Jerónima de Saavedra**, probably born around 1584; reported to have died unmarried before 1643.
2. **Isabel de Saavedra y Garay**, probably born around 1590; married her maternal cousin **Jerónimo Luis de Cabrera y Garay**, later governor of the Río de la Plata, Chucuito, and Tucumán.
3. **María de Sanabria Saavedra y Garay**, probably born around 1596; married **Miguel Jerónimo de Cabrera y Villarroel**.

The marriages of Isabel and María further joined the Garay–Saavedra house to the descendants of **Jerónimo Luis de Cabrera**, founder of Córdoba. The network consequently linked several of the most powerful founding families of the interior Río de la Plata.

No legitimate son is securely documented. References to “sons” in polemical summaries of seventeenth-century governors appear to concern other officeholders or use “children” generically.

## Childhood and Early Life

Hernandarias grew up in Asunción, the political center of a vast and thinly governed jurisdiction extending toward the Atlantic, the Paraná basin, Guayrá, and the southern plains.

No school record or detailed childhood memoir survives. His formation was probably domestic, military, religious, and administrative rather than university-based. His family gave him access to:

- Literacy and scribal culture.
- Horses, arms, livestock management, and river navigation.
- Indigenous labor through encomienda relationships.
- The municipal politics of Asunción.
- Networks of conquistadors, clerics, and provincial officers.
- Familiarity with Guaraní-speaking populations and frontier diplomacy.

Barriera reports that at approximately **fourteen** he entered the Asunción militia. At about **fifteen**, he joined the forces of **Gonzalo de Abreu** in a 1578 expedition connected with the search for the legendary City of the Caesars.

The evidence shows a youth spent inside the machinery of conquest. Later heroic biographies often present this as a precocious school of courage and public service. A less celebratory reconstruction must add that such expeditions involved territorial penetration, punitive warfare, coerced Indigenous labor, and the pursuit of settlements and populations subject to Spanish authority.

## Education and Formation

No university, college, or formal teacher can be securely assigned to Hernandarias.

His correspondence and legislative activity show practical literacy and familiarity with Spanish imperial law, petitions, royal rhetoric, Catholic doctrine, and administrative procedure. Secretaries and notaries drafted many official texts, as was normal; their polished legal language cannot automatically be treated as his unaided composition.

Important formative influences included:

- **Martín Suárez de Toledo**, his father: military, encomendero, and political model.
- **Juan de Garay**, later his father-in-law: organizer of settlements, livestock movements, and territorial occupation.
- The political culture of Asunción’s cabildo.
- Catholic clergy, especially Franciscans and later Jesuits.
- The experience of frontier war and diplomacy with Guaraní, Guaycurú, Charrúa, Querandí, and other peoples.
- The imperial ideology of “pacification,” settlement, conversion, and royal service.
- Competition between locally born descendants of conquerors, metropolitan officials, and newer Portuguese and Atlantic commercial networks.

He became markedly hard of hearing. A contemporary critic described him as “sordo de entrambos oídos.” Later accounts attribute this to severe otitis and sometimes add facial paralysis, but a precise medical diagnosis cannot be retrospectively established. His deafness was both a real disability and a political weapon: opponents argued that it made his tribunal absurd because he could not properly “hear justice.”

## Early Military and Political Career, 1575–1591

### Militia service

**Reported fact:** Around 1575 Hernandarias entered the Asunción militia.

**1578:** He joined Gonzalo de Abreu’s expedition associated with the City of the Caesars. The expedition belongs to the overlap between actual military exploration and the persistent legend of a rich hidden settlement in the south.

### Buenos Aires expedition

**1580:** Hernandarias participated in Juan de Garay’s refoundation expedition to Buenos Aires. Barriera states that he was responsible for driving or caring for the expedition’s livestock. He also participated in exploration south of the Salado River.

Popular biographies sometimes say he “founded Buenos Aires.” The documented formulation should be narrower: he participated in Garay’s enterprise and livestock movement but was not its commanding founder.

### Marriage and Santa Fe base

**1582:** He married Jerónima de Contreras/Garay at Santa Fe. Santa Fe became his family base and later his principal residence in retirement.

### Military campaigns

Later chronicles place him in multiple expeditions against Guaycurú and other Indigenous groups in the 1580s and early 1590s. Alejandro Nieto’s biographical narrative assigns him an expedition against Guaycurú groups in 1591 and another against the people called Pasanco in 1592.

These were described in colonial language as “entries,” “punishments,” “pacifications,” or responses to attacks. Such terminology should not obscure that they were armed campaigns intended to impose Spanish security and jurisdiction. Exact casualty figures and the circumstances of individual expeditions remain uncertain.

### Concepción del Bermejo and regional office

Hernandarias is frequently credited with participating in or supporting the occupation of Concepción del Bermejo and with holding offices in Asunción, Santa Fe, and possibly Corrientes. The sequence of early subordinate appointments varies among chronologies.

## Chronological Life History

## 1592–1594: First exercise of high authority

On **13 July 1592**, the Cabildo of Asunción appointed Hernandarias lieutenant governor and chief justice. Some lists treat this as his first term as governor; others distinguish it as an interim or locally elected lieutenant governorship.

This distinction explains the recurrent statements that he governed three, four, or even six times:

- “Three terms” usually counts the principal governorships of approximately 1597–1599, 1602–1609, and 1615–1618.
- “Four terms” usually includes the 1592 local or interim exercise.
- Higher counts divide periods when he governed through separate commissions or lieutenants.

The 1592 appointment is the basis of claims that he was the first American-born or first criollo governor. It was unquestionably an unusually early accession of an American-born Spaniard to high provincial authority. The universal claim—“the first person born in the Americas to govern any European colony”—is too broad to sustain without a comparative survey of all Iberian jurisdictions. The safer conclusion is that he was the **first locally born criollo to govern the Río de la Plata–Paraguay jurisdiction and one of the earliest American-born governors in Spanish America**.

His rise consolidated the “benemérito” network formed by the older conquest families and Garay’s descendants.

## 1595–1599: Lieutenant and governor

On **6 November 1595**, Governor Juan Ramírez de Velasco reportedly appointed him lieutenant governor of Asunción. By 1596 or 1597 Hernandarias was exercising the substantive provincial government.

Different sources give the first principal term as:

- 1596–1599.
- 1597–1599.
- 1598–1599.

The discrepancy reflects when acting authority, local recognition, and formal succession are counted.

### Ordinances concerning Indigenous communities, 1598

On **12 November 1598**, Hernandarias issued regulations concerning Indigenous communities and encomiendas. A surviving thirteen-page manuscript written and signed in 1598 was identified in the United States in 2025 and recovered through cooperation between Paraguayan institutions and the Manhattan Antiquities Trafficking Unit.

The ordinances addressed:

- Concentration or “reduction” of dispersed Indigenous populations into settlements.
- Construction and maintenance of churches.
- Catholic instruction and attendance at Mass.
- Limits on removing Indigenous people from one encomienda or locality to another.
- Protection of fishing and hunting.
- Restrictions on separating families.
- Limits on corporal punishment, shackles, and abusive detention.
- Regulation of the number and ages of workers taken to towns.
- Time for Indigenous households to cultivate their own fields.
- Limits on domestic service and on the labor demanded from women.
- Restrictions on encomenderos describing Indigenous people as personal property.

These measures were regulatory and paternalistic. They did not end Spanish sovereignty, tribute, compulsory resettlement, or the encomienda. Claims that Hernandarias “abolished slavery” or abolished the entire encomienda system in 1598 or 1603 are therefore misleading.

## 1600–1602: Succession conflict

After the death of Governor Diego Rodríguez de Valdés y de la Banda, **Francés de Beaumont y Navarra** exercised authority. At the end of 1601 local electors reportedly chose Hernandarias, but Beaumont resisted surrendering power.

A royal appointment issued on **6 November 1601** was presented in Buenos Aires in August 1602. Sources variously date his assumption to 1 or 15 August 1602.

The episode illustrates the interaction of local election, royal confirmation, family power, and personal rivalry.

## 1602–1609: Major governorship

This was his longest and most consequential continuous administration.

### Government and urban works

Reported policies included:

- Repairing or strengthening the defenses of Buenos Aires.
- Building a tower or defensive work near the Riachuelo after an English corsair attack on ships in March 1607.
- Encouraging brick and tile production to replace more fragile adobe construction.
- Promoting elementary instruction. He is often credited with “founding the first schools” in Buenos Aires or Paraguay, but the precise institutional continuity of these schools is uncertain.
- Attempting to improve communications and navigation among Asunción, Santa Fe, Corrientes, and Buenos Aires.
- Reporting natural resources, settlement possibilities, and defensive needs to the Crown.

### Ordinances of 1603

In 1603 Hernandarias promulgated a further body of ordinances on Indigenous labor. Their preamble complained that encomenderos had neglected Christian instruction, used Indigenous people without proper limits, and contributed to deaths without baptism or confession.

Regulations attributed to this body included:

- Prohibiting unauthorized transfers of Indigenous workers.
- Restricting compulsory labor.
- Protecting caciques and their children from some forms of service.
- Limiting labor to four days per week in specified circumstances.
- Reserving days for Indigenous families’ own fields.
- Prohibiting or limiting work in dangerous yerba-mate forests.
- Forbidding the breaking up of families.
- Restricting maltreatment, chains, and whipping.
- Regulating wages or subsistence.
- Requiring churches and Christian instruction in settlements.

### What the ordinances did not do

Modern commemorative summaries often call them the “abolition of the encomienda.” Academic research instead shows that they regulated and preserved a modified system of tribute and compelled labor.

A UNAM study describes Hernandarias’s approach as an attempt to “acriollar” Indigenous people—incorporating them into the colonial order—rather than granting political autonomy. The ordinances regarded organized Indigenous settlement and tribute as components of the Spanish polity.

Branislava Sušnik interpreted the protective language partly as a response to catastrophic population loss and the colonial economy’s need to conserve labor. Protective measures and colonial economic interest were not mutually exclusive.

### Contemporary accusations by Ruy Díaz de Guzmán

On **8 May 1604**, the chronicler and political actor **Ruy Díaz de Guzmán** sent the king a memorial containing seventeen accusations against Hernandarias.

The accusations included:

- Illegitimate or unsuitable exercise of government by a locally born man.
- Partiality to relatives and allies.
- Appointment of relatives and brothers-in-law as lieutenants.
- Control of municipal councils through patronage.
- Maladministration of justice.
- Intimidation of inhabitants.
- Maltreatment of Indigenous people.
- Unauthorized resettlement or “reduction” of Indigenous communities.
- Use of Indigenous labor for personal purposes.
- Abandonment of an office or jurisdiction.
- Deafness that allegedly prevented him from hearing litigants.

**Evidentiary status:** These are documented contemporary allegations, not a collective conviction. Barriera situates them in a factional struggle. Some have independent plausibility or corroboration—especially nepotistic appointments and personal use of Indigenous labor—but the memorial was also a political attack.

### Patagonia and the City of the Caesars

In 1604 Hernandarias led an expedition southward from Buenos Aires. Popular accounts state that he:

- Crossed deep into Patagonia.
- Reached the Río Negro or perhaps farther south.
- Searched for the legendary City of the Caesars.
- Was surrounded or captured by Tehuelche people and narrowly escaped.

The secure core is that he organized a major southern reconnaissance connected with defense, territorial knowledge, and the Caesars tradition.

The maximum southern extent is disputed. Some historians place the expedition only in what is now Buenos Aires Province; others say it reached the Río Negro, and popular accounts extend it farther. The claim that he was personally captured is repeated but lacks sufficient confirmation in the sources consulted. It should be treated as **reported tradition or unresolved legend**, not established fact.

### Banda Oriental expeditions

Hernandarias explored territory east of the Uruguay River during the first decade of the seventeenth century. Dates vary between 1604 and 1607–1608 because several journeys are sometimes merged.

A letter of **2 July 1608** described the region’s timber, waterways, fertile soils, and potential for agriculture, livestock, mills, and maritime shipment. He recommended settlement and economic use.

He named or described waterways including the Santa Lucía in later accounts. His movements probably included the Uruguay and Negro river systems and coastal sections of what is now Uruguay.

### Corsair defense

On **18 March 1607**, English corsairs reportedly attacked or robbed ships near Buenos Aires. Hernandarias ordered additional defenses, including a tower near the mouth of the Riachuelo.

### Mission policy and Guayrá

Royal orders of 1608 and Hernandarias’s own correspondence supported a change from direct armed conquest toward missionary “reduction” in Guayrá, Paraná, and Guaycurú territory.

In 1609:

- Hernandarias and Bishop Reginaldo de Lizárraga urged the Jesuit provincial Diego de Torres Bollo to send missionaries.
- José Cataldino and Simón Mazeta/Maceta were dispatched to Guayrá.
- Other Jesuits were assigned to the Paraná and Guaycurú regions.
- The foundations that followed included Nuestra Señora de Loreto and San Ignacio in Guayrá in 1610.

This did not mean an end to colonial domination. Julia Ossanna describes the reductions as evolving structures of alliance and domination amid conflict among Indigenous groups, Jesuits, Spaniards, and Portuguese slavers. Missionary protection could reduce direct encomendero exploitation while simultaneously concentrating populations, imposing Catholic discipline, and enlarging Spanish control.

## 1609–1614: Out of the principal governorship

Diego Marín de Negrón succeeded Hernandarias around late 1609 or 1610.

### Conflict over the Alfaro ordinances

Royal visitor **Francisco de Alfaro** inspected the region and issued ordinances in 1611–1612 abolishing or restricting personal service, recognizing Indigenous legal freedom, and reforming tribute.

A Jesuit-derived narrative states that Hernandarias initially became a leader of encomendero opposition to Alfaro and Governor Negrón, speaking in terms that Father Diego González Holguín warned sounded like rebellion. According to this account, Hernandarias later repented, resigned as the encomenderos’ procurator, compensated workers from his own encomienda, and accepted the reforms.

**Evidentiary status:** The political opposition is reported from an early ecclesiastical narrative. Its conversion-and-repentance ending has an evident edifying structure and should be read as both possible history and Catholic moral narrative.

### Cattle in the Banda Oriental

Around **1610 or 1611**, Negrón reportedly granted Hernandarias two islands near the confluence of the Uruguay and Negro rivers, usually identified as Lobos and Vizcaíno.

In a 1627 statement attributed to Hernandarias, he said that around 1611 he released cattle on one island and approximately ten years later added fifty females and goats brought from Córdoba and Tucumán.

A second introduction is conventionally dated to **1617**, involving approximately fifty or one hundred heifers and several bulls, some obtained from Melchor Maciel del Águila’s Santa Fe ranch.

The broad fact that Hernandarias introduced cattle into the western Banda Oriental is well supported. The national myth requires qualification:

- He was not the first person to bring cattle to the greater Río de la Plata basin.
- Cattle already existed in Asunción, Tucumán, Buenos Aires, and Santa Fe.
- The massive herds of the eastern *Vaquería del Mar* may have descended mainly from animals moving south from Corrientes and the Jesuit missions rather than exclusively from his island herds.
- The exact numbers and release sites vary.

He was therefore an important early promoter of cattle in what became Uruguay, but the statement that “all Uruguayan cattle descended from Hernandarias’s herd” is a **national origin myth**, not a demonstrated biological fact.

### Yerba mate policy

Hernandarias opposed aspects of the yerba-mate economy and consumption. A letter of **8 June 1616** called its widespread use a “vice” and complained of its spread as far as Peru.

Policies attributed to him included confiscation or burning of yerba, fines, and short imprisonment. His motives were mixed:

- Moral suspicion of a stimulating social drink.
- Concern over uncontrolled commerce.
- Opposition to the exploitation and mortality of Indigenous workers sent into distant yerbales.
- A desire to regulate labor and trade.

The claim that he permanently banned mate is false. Consumption and commerce expanded dramatically during and after the seventeenth century.

## 1615–1618: Final governorship and war on contraband

Hernandarias returned to office under a royal appointment made around 1614, taking power in 1615. The Crown expected him to confront the commercial and fiscal network dominating Buenos Aires.

### Structure of contraband

Buenos Aires’s legally restricted commerce encouraged an extensive system involving:

- Portuguese merchants.
- Unlicensed or falsely distressed ships.
- Enslaved Africans imported outside authorized channels.
- Undeclared silver from Potosí.
- Local merchants and officeholders.
- Cabildo members.
- Royal treasury officials.
- Clerical patrons and institutional clients.

Modern historians sometimes call the network the **“confederados”** or the **“contrabando ejemplar.”** Hernandarias’s own supporters belonged to the older *benemérito* conquest families. The conflict was consequently both an anti-smuggling prosecution and a struggle between elite coalitions.

### Principal opponents

The most important figures prosecuted or denounced by Hernandarias included:

- **Juan de Vergara**, powerful cabildo politician and notary of the Holy Office.
- **Diego de Vega**, Portuguese merchant and financier.
- **Simón de Valdés**, royal treasurer.
- **Mateo Leal de Ayala**, justice official.
- **Mateo de Grado**, associated with Vergara’s faction.
- Other merchants, officers, shipmasters, and intermediaries.

Hernandarias described Vergara and Vega as the origin and cause of the port’s contraband. He arrested leading members and attempted to transfer cases beyond the local network’s control.

### The alleged killing of an alguacil

In a letter of **25 May 1616**, Hernandarias told the king that Vergara and Vega had arranged the murder of an alguacil who had been the first witness against them. A suspect allegedly confessed under torture that he had been offered 500 pesos and paid 100.

Important qualifications:

- The confession was obtained under torture.
- The accused witness or perpetrator escaped before ratifying it.
- The allegation came from Hernandarias, who was prosecuting the faction.
- No final conviction for ordering the killing was established in the material consulted.

This is a **documented allegation**, not an established murder finding.

### Use of torture

Hernandarias explicitly reported ordering torture of the suspected participant in the alguacil’s killing.

**Evidentiary status:** Documented by his own official correspondence.

Torture was legally available under specified early-modern procedures, but the fact remains relevant to any account of his administration and coercive practices.

### Ecclesiastical conflict

Hernandarias complained that the accused merchants cultivated religious orders through donations and induced Jesuits to defend a prisoner’s clerical immunity. His opponents in turn used excommunication and ecclesiastical privilege against royal officers.

The religious orders were not a single political bloc. Jesuits collaborated with Hernandarias in missionary policy but could oppose him in particular jurisdictional cases.

### Division of the province

Hernandarias repeatedly argued that the enormous united jurisdiction could not be effectively governed. A royal decree dated **16 December 1617** divided it into:

- The **Governorate of Paraguay**, centered on Asunción and including Guayrá and associated northern territories.
- The **Governorate of the Río de la Plata**, centered on Buenos Aires and including Santa Fe, Corrientes, and the Banda Oriental.

The administrative implementation occurred over the following years, commonly dated 1618–1620.

Later national histories portray him as the architect of either Paraguay or Argentina. In documentary terms, his proposal addressed distance, defense, justice, and governability within the Spanish monarchy, not the creation of independent nations.

## 1618–1623: Removal, residency proceeding, confiscation, and imprisonment

**Diego de Góngora** replaced Hernandarias in the new Río de la Plata government.

Góngora subjected him to a **juicio de residencia**, the standard but politically potent review of an outgoing governor. According to Barriera:

- Góngora’s report repeated accusations against Hernandarias.
- Hernandarias was imprisoned.
- His personal property was confiscated and publicly auctioned.
- His status as a native of Paraguay was used as evidence of supposed local partiality.

This was not merely rumor. Arrest, confiscation, auction, and residency proceedings are documented. The exact final disposition of every charge is less clear.

Supporters later portrayed Góngora as an ally of smugglers exacting revenge. That interpretation has a substantial political basis—Góngora himself was later associated with contraband—but can become overly schematic when it turns Hernandarias into pure legality and every opponent into pure corruption.

Góngora died in May 1623. Hernandarias’s relationship with the royal judge **Alonso Pérez de Salazar** helped reverse or mitigate his position. He left prison and settled in Santa Fe.

### Litigation involving Mateo de Grado

The jurist **Juan de Solórzano Pereira** participated in an appellate determination favorable to Hernandarias in his dispute with Mateo de Grado. The judgment described Hernandarias as a man of knowledge and experience and regarded the charges he had made against Grado as justified and proved.

This establishes that at least one major branch of litigation ended favorably to him. It does not amount to a blanket acquittal on every accusation ever brought against him.

## 1623–1634: Later life in Santa Fe

Hernandarias spent his later years primarily in Santa Fe, managing family interests, land, livestock, and litigation.

He was not politically irrelevant. In the 1620s he remained sufficiently influential to be used in royal or Audiencia commissions.

### The Céspedes–Vergara crisis

Governor **Francisco de Céspedes** attempted to imprison Juan de Vergara in 1627. Bishop **Pedro Carranza**, a relative and ally of Vergara, led resistance, broke into the jail, freed Vergara, and excommunicated or threatened officials. The conflict caused military and political disorder.

The Audiencia of Charcas assigned Hernandarias a role in reviewing the dispute. Accounts state that his inquiry helped restore Céspedes to office.

This episode demonstrates that Hernandarias’s long contest with the Vergara network continued after his own governorship.

### Economic position

Hernandarias was not an impoverished disinterested official. He was:

- A landholder.
- Rancher and livestock owner.
- Encomendero.
- Owner or beneficiary of skilled Indigenous and enslaved labor.
- Member of a family with urban houses, agricultural lands, ranches, and political office.
- Holder or recipient of island and territorial grants.

His estate’s exact net value has not been established here. His capacity to move herds, equip expeditions, arrange marriages, and survive confiscation indicates substantial resources, although frontier wealth was often held in livestock, land claims, labor rights, tools, and debts rather than cash.

### Final illness and death

No securely documented terminal illness has been identified. His deafness remained part of his public image. Claims of facial paralysis from otitis are plausible but rest on later medical reconstruction.

He died at Santa Fe in 1634. No reliable source consulted establishes a violent death, accident, poisoning, or epidemic as the cause. The cause of death is therefore **unknown**.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

Modern corporations did not exist in his world, but his activities can be reconstructed through institutional and property structures.

### Institutions

- Cabildo of Asunción.
- Cabildos and subordinate governments of Santa Fe and Buenos Aires.
- Government and captaincy-general of Río de la Plata and Paraguay.
- Spanish Crown and Council of the Indies.
- Royal Treasury at Buenos Aires.
- Real Audiencia of Charcas.
- Franciscan and Jesuit missions.
- Encomienda settlements and Indigenous reductions.
- Family networks of Garay, Saavedra, Sanabria, Trejo, and Cabrera.

### Land and livestock

Hernandarias possessed or claimed:

- Ranching interests near Santa Fe.
- Livestock herds capable of supplying new settlements.
- Two islands near the Uruguay–Negro confluence, according to later testimony.
- Encomienda rights and Indigenous labor.
- Urban and rural property subjected to confiscation and auction after 1618.

The exact boundaries and titles of all properties require examination of Santa Fe protocols, land-grant books, and litigation files.

### Livestock technology and logistics

His projects used the principal productive technologies of the region:

- Long-distance cattle drives.
- River barges or rafts for moving animals.
- Open-range reproduction of cattle.
- Horse-based military and ranching systems.
- Leather production.
- Brick and tile kilns.
- Mills proposed for the Banda Oriental.
- Fortifications and river defenses.

There are no patents in the modern sense and no documented personal invention. His significance was administrative and logistical, not inventorship.

### Trade policy

Hernandarias simultaneously:

- Defended legal colonial commerce.
- Sought Crown permission for limited exports of local products.
- Opposed illicit importation and silver export.
- Tried to regulate or suppress yerba trade.
- Benefited personally from the livestock-and-land economy.
- Represented an older landholding elite challenged by a newer Atlantic mercantile coalition.

The conflict cannot be reduced to “honest protectionist versus corrupt free trader,” although later nationalist historians often did so.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Political position

Hernandarias consistently presented himself as a servant of the king. His governing philosophy emphasized:

- Royal jurisdiction.
- Municipal order under controlled local participation.
- Defense of the territory.
- Catholic evangelization.
- Regulation of Indigenous labor.
- Settlement and population concentration.
- Suppression of unauthorized commerce.
- Rewards and offices for locally born descendants of conquerors.

His career also reveals tension between those principles and personal/familial interest. He appointed relatives and allies, used family networks to control offices, and defended the political position of the *beneméritos*.

### Criollo identity

He did not advocate independence from Spain. Nevertheless, his American birth became politically significant.

Opponents used his birth to suggest partiality, inferiority, or unfitness. Supporters invoked royal preferences for rewarding descendants of conquerors and “children of the land.” Twentieth-century historians then turned him into a precursor of criollo nationalism.

Calling him an Argentine, Paraguayan, or Uruguayan nationalist is anachronistic. His operative community was the Spanish monarchy, the provincial jurisdiction, the city, and the conquest-family network.

### Religion

Hernandarias was a publicly orthodox Catholic ruler.

Documented or reported associations included:

- Support for Franciscan missions.
- Collaboration with Jesuit provincial Diego de Torres Bollo.
- Support from Bishops Hernando de Trejo y Sanabria and Martín Ignacio de Loyola.
- Promotion of churches and Christian instruction in Indigenous settlements.
- Reliance on Catholic “civilization” as a justification for reduction policy.
- Jurisdictional conflict with clergy who protected his political enemies.

The claim that he became nearly saintly after repenting his opposition to the Alfaro ordinances belongs partly to hagiographic Jesuit historiography.

### Indigenous policy

His policy combined several elements:

- Genuine attempts to limit certain abuses.
- Concern about demographic collapse.
- Economic need to preserve a labor force.
- Catholic missionary paternalism.
- Compulsory resettlement.
- Tribute and regulated labor.
- Armed “pacification” of communities resisting Spanish intrusion.
- Personal ownership or control of encomienda labor.

It is therefore inaccurate either to depict him simply as an emancipator or simply as an undifferentiated enslaver.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Martín Suárez de Toledo

Father, political model, and source of status in Asunción. Opponents accused the father of usurping royal jurisdiction and used that charge against the son.

### María de Sanabria Calderón and Mencía Calderón

Mother and maternal grandmother. Their family brought the failed Sanabria adelantado claim, migration history, and connections to the earliest Spanish settlement elite.

### Juan de Garay

Father-in-law, military associate, and central influence. Hernandarias helped with the livestock component of Garay’s Buenos Aires expedition. Marriage to Garay’s daughter made him a leader of the extended Garay political network.

### Jerónima de Contreras/Garay

Wife and dynastic partner. Through her, Hernandarias acquired connections across Santa Fe and Buenos Aires. Direct evidence about emotional life within the marriage is absent.

### Bishop Hernando de Trejo y Sanabria

Maternal half-brother, Franciscan, bishop of Tucumán, and reported political supporter. He was important in the religious-institutional expansion of the region.

### Bishop Martín Ignacio de Loyola

One of Hernandarias’s strongest contemporary defenders. In a 1603 letter to the king, Loyola praised him as a distinguished servant with a lifetime of military service and no self-interested motive.

### Ruy Díaz de Guzmán

Political enemy and principal early accuser. His 1604 memorial attacked Hernandarias’s legitimacy, patronage, justice, Indigenous policy, and personal fitness.

### Juan de Vergara

Long-term political and judicial enemy. Vergara represented or coordinated the powerful Buenos Aires cabildo-commercial faction opposed by Hernandarias.

### Diego de Vega

Portuguese merchant and alleged organizer of contraband, closely associated with Vergara. Hernandarias identified him as a central target of the 1615–1616 investigations.

### Simón de Valdés

Royal treasurer accused by Hernandarias of fiscal fraud and complicity in contraband.

### Francisco de Alfaro

Royal visitor and legal reformer. Hernandarias reportedly first opposed Alfaro’s stronger curtailment of personal service, then accepted it.

### Diego Marín de Negrón

Successor and, at times, political opponent. He nevertheless reportedly granted Hernandarias the islands used for cattle.

### Diego de Góngora

Successor, adversary, and judge in the 1618 residency. He imprisoned Hernandarias and confiscated and auctioned his assets.

### Alonso Pérez de Salazar

Royal judge whose relationship with Hernandarias helped rehabilitate him after Góngora’s death.

### Juan de Solórzano Pereira

Influential jurist who issued or signed a favorable appellate ruling in Hernandarias’s case against Mateo de Grado.

### Jesuits

Collaborators in mission policy but not unconditional allies. Diego de Torres Bollo, José Cataldino, Simón Mazeta, and Diego González Holguín intersected with his policies. Jesuits could also invoke ecclesiastical privilege against his prosecutions.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Corruption, and Financial Questions

## 1. Nepotism and control of municipal government

**Allegation with substantial structural support:** Ruy Díaz de Guzmán accused Hernandarias of appointing relatives and brothers-in-law as lieutenants and using reciprocal favors to dominate cabildos.

His marriage network did occupy numerous offices, and Barriera treats his rise as consolidation of the Garay-centered *benemérito* group. What remains unresolved is whether particular appointments violated law or merely reflected normal early-modern patronage.

## 2. Maltreatment and exploitation of Indigenous people

**Documented allegations:** The 1604 memorial accused him of maltreatment, forced resettlement, and misuse of labor.

**Additional documentary support:** Santa Fe records cited by Barriera indicate complaints that:

- Indigenous people assigned to the city were used to repair Hernandarias’s own house.
- Indigenous artisans from his encomienda worked in town.
- The services of an Indigenous blacksmith named Pedro were charged at allegedly excessive rates.

These records complicate his image as a disinterested protector. They do not negate his ordinances but show that he participated personally in the coercive colonial labor economy he regulated.

## 3. Encomienda “abolition”

**Disputed public claim:** Modern summaries repeatedly say Hernandarias abolished mita and encomienda.

**Finding:** His 1598 and 1603 ordinances restricted abuses, working time, removal, punishment, and some forms of service. They did not abolish the underlying system. The more radical Alfaro ordinances followed in 1611–1612 and also encountered resistance and evasion.

## 4. Opposition to the Alfaro reforms

**Reported fact:** An early Jesuit narrative portrays Hernandarias as briefly leading encomendero resistance and speaking of possible rebellion.

**Counter-narrative:** The same account says he withdrew, confessed, compensated Indigenous workers, and supported reform.

Because the narrative follows a fall-repentance-restoration pattern, the conversion episode should be regarded as partly hagiographic until checked against secular administrative records.

## 5. Punitive campaigns and colonial violence

**Documented general fact; details variable:** Hernandarias took part in military campaigns against Indigenous groups from youth onward. Expeditions against Guaycurú and Charrúa groups were represented as defense or pacification.

Later celebratory histories euphemize these actions. Salaberry acknowledged that Hernandarias and allied families defended private and regional interests through these wars. The campaigns furthered Spanish territorial occupation and exposed communities to killing, capture, displacement, missionization, and coerced labor.

## 6. Use of torture

**Documented fact:** In his 1616 report to the king, Hernandarias acknowledged ordering torture of a suspect in the alleged alguacil murder conspiracy. The resulting confession was not ratified because the prisoner escaped.

## 7. Household slavery and an abuse narrative

Hernandarias’s social world included enslaved Africans. A Santa Fe public-history account recounts testimony by an enslaved woman named **Francisca**, who allegedly suffered repeated flight, capture, whipping, salt applied to wounds, shackling, and forced labor and named members of the Garay–Saavedra household as responsible.

**Evidentiary status:** Reported allegation in local historical presentation; the underlying judicial manuscript was not independently examined for this dossier. It should not be treated as a final court finding, but it warrants inclusion and further archival investigation.

## 8. Contraband prosecutions

**Documented fact:** Hernandarias investigated, arrested, and prosecuted officials and merchants connected to illicit trade.

**His allegations:** Vergara, Vega, Valdés, and associates had diverted royal revenue, controlled port society, bribed or patronized clergy, arranged false imports, and attempted to kill a witness.

**Qualification:** His opponents were politically and commercially interested, but Hernandarias likewise represented a landholding family faction. His enforcement actions were not politically neutral merely because they supported royal law.

## 9. His own alleged corruption

Góngora’s 1618 residency and earlier hostile memorials accused Hernandarias of partiality, abuses, and improper enrichment or use of office.

**Documented administrative action:** Arrest, confiscation, and auction of property occurred.

**Unresolved legal result:** The accessible evidence does not establish a final comprehensive conviction for personal contraband or embezzlement. Later favorable judicial decisions and his rehabilitation undermine claims that all accusations were proved. Conversely, later hagiography’s assertion that every charge was fabricated is also too broad.

## 10. Hearing disability and capacity for office

His deafness was real or widely recognized. Opponents used it to claim that litigants could not receive justice. No neutral medical or administrative determination of incapacity survives in the consulted sources.

## 11. Yerba-mate suppression

His attempt to suppress or regulate yerba was criticized as economically unrealistic. He called consumption a vice while also invoking Indigenous deaths and exploitation in the yerbales.

The policy failed in the long term. Yerba became one of the central commodities of Paraguay and the missions.

## 12. Confiscation under Góngora

Góngora’s imprisonment and sale of Hernandarias’s goods were legal-administrative actions under the form of a residency review, but their political neutrality is doubtful. Góngora was connected in later investigations with the commercial network Hernandarias opposed.

The episode was later converted into a martyr story: incorruptible criollo punished by corrupt foreign merchants. The documentary reality supports retaliation and factional conflict but is more complex than the legend.

## Dedicated 2024–2026 adversarial-search findings

Searches pairing his full name and “Hernandarias” with Spanish and English terms for scandal, allegation, fraud, corruption, lawsuit, conspiracy, criticism, abuse, and controversy produced no credible newly discovered accusation about the historical person during 2024–2026.

Most recent results concerned:

- Crimes or corruption in the modern Paraguayan city of Hernandarias, not the colonial governor.
- The 2025 recovery of the stolen “Hernandarias Manuscript.”
- Republishing of older historical scholarship.
- Continuing debate over encomienda, colonial violence, and heroic historiography.

The absence of a recent scandal is not evidence that older allegations were false. The main contested corpus remains the 1604 memorial, the Vergara litigation, the 1618 residency, Indigenous-labor records, and twentieth-century nationalist reinterpretation.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## “The first criollo governor of America”

**Story:** Hernandarias was the first American-born person to govern anywhere in the European Americas.

**Appearance and circulation:** Repeated in Argentine, Paraguayan, Uruguayan, and general reference works; reinforced by twentieth-century nationalist biography.

**Evidence:** He was born in Asunción and exercised high provincial authority from 1592, later receiving royal appointment.

**Against the strongest version:** The phrase depends on how “governor,” “criollo,” “America,” and locally elected interim authority are defined. Comparable cases across the entire Spanish and Portuguese empires are rarely examined.

**Assessment:** Secure as the first or earliest locally born governor of the Río de la Plata–Paraguay jurisdiction; universal primacy remains a **disputed superlative**.

## The flawless “protector of the Indians”

**Story:** Hernandarias ended Indigenous enslavement and consistently defended Indigenous people against encomenderos.

**Circulators:** Catholic, nationalist, educational, and commemorative literature, especially Salaberry, Molina, Tiscornia, and popular biographies.

**Supporting evidence:** The 1598 and 1603 ordinances restricted abuse; he promoted missionary reductions and criticized deadly yerbal labor.

**Contrary evidence:** He was an encomendero, used Indigenous labor, participated in punitive campaigns, supported compulsory reduction, and was accused of personal labor abuses. His policy conserved colonial rule and tribute.

**Assessment:** Protective legislation is factual; emancipation and spotless humanitarianism are **hagiographic exaggerations**.

## Founder of Uruguay’s livestock economy

**Story:** Hernandarias personally introduced the cattle from which all Uruguayan herds descended.

**Circulators:** Uruguayan school histories, livestock histories, monuments, and civic commemoration.

**Evidence:** He introduced cattle in approximately 1611 and 1617 and advocated the region’s pastoral development.

**Counter-evidence:** Other herds entered from Corrientes, Río Grande, and Jesuit mission zones. Scholarship cited in later summaries argues that the great *Vaquería del Mar* may not have descended principally from his releases.

**Assessment:** Important pioneer, not securely the sole biological founder. The totalizing version is a **national economic origin myth**.

## Captured by the Tehuelche in Patagonia

**Story:** During his 1604 expedition he penetrated nearly 1,000 kilometers south of Buenos Aires, was captured, and escaped alive.

**Evidence:** A southern expedition occurred.

**Problems:** Routes and southern limits differ widely; readily accessible accounts do not supply a decisive contemporary narrative for the capture.

**Assessment:** **Unresolved popular tradition**.

## Search for the City of the Caesars

**Story:** Hernandarias searched for a hidden wealthy city in Patagonia.

**Context:** The City of the Caesars was a long-lived South American colonial legend, imagined as a settlement of shipwrecked Spaniards, Incas, or survivors rich in precious metals.

**Evidence:** His 1578 service and later southern expedition were connected in historical sources to “the Caesars.”

**Impact:** The story made him part of Patagonia’s exploration mythology and dramatized his later image as a tireless frontier adventurer.

**Assessment:** The expeditionary association is reported fact; the city itself is myth.

## The incorruptible anti-imperialist

**Story:** Hernandarias was an early Argentine economic nationalist who defended domestic production against foreign capital and imperial finance.

**Circulators:** Twentieth-century revisionists, notably José María Rosa and María Elena Tiscornia, with antecedents in Molina and Salaberry.

**Evidence:** He fought Portuguese-led contraband and defended local legal production.

**Against the interpretation:** He enforced Spanish imperial restrictions and royal revenue, not national independence. His interests also aligned with the older landholding faction.

**Impact:** The narrative allowed writers of very different politics to project modern protectionism, nationalism, Peronism, anti-imperialism, or Catholic corporatism into the seventeenth century.

**Assessment:** **Anachronistic political mythology built on real conflicts**.

## “Hijo de la tierra” as a proto-national identity

**Story:** Because he was born in Asunción, Hernandarias embodied an already existing Argentine or Paraguayan nation.

**Evidence:** His American birth mattered politically, and adversaries explicitly attacked it.

**Against:** His territorial and institutional loyalties belonged to the Spanish monarchy, local cabildos, family, and a province that predated modern borders.

**Assessment:** Criollo identity is documented; modern national identity is retrospective.

## Saintly repentance after near rebellion

**Story:** After opposing Alfaro’s ordinances, Hernandarias accepted a priest’s correction, renounced leadership of the opposition, made general confession, paid his Indigenous workers, and lived with exemplary Christian discipline.

**Source tradition:** Jesuit or ecclesiastical narrative reproduced in later collections.

**Evidence for:** Named clerical actors and a plausible political conflict.

**Evidence against or limiting confidence:** Literary structure resembles religious exemplum and moral rehabilitation.

**Assessment:** Possible historical core with **hagiographic elaboration**.

## Works by Hernando Arias de Saavedra

Hernandarias was not an author of books in the modern literary sense. His surviving corpus consists principally of official letters, ordinances, memorials, judicial interventions, instructions, reports, appointments, and administrative acts. Many were written by scribes under his authority and signed by him.

### Authentic or institutionally attributable works

1. **Ordenanzas concerning Indigenous communities and encomiendas**, Asunción, 12 November 1598.

   - A thirteen-page signed manuscript was recovered in New York in April 2025 after appearing in an American auction.
   - It regulates Indigenous labor, movement, settlement, subsistence, and religious instruction.
   - Genuine signed administrative authorship; drafting may have involved legal advisers or scribes.

2. **Ordenanzas de Hernandarias**, 1603.

   - A wider regulatory code addressing Indigenous labor, reduction, doctrine, encomendero conduct, punishment, family separation, and work in yerbales.
   - Preserved through manuscript copies, editions, quotations, and later legal compilations.
   - Genuine governmental authorship in the institutional sense.

3. **Letters to the king**, 1603.

   - The Museo Mitre catalogue identifies four letters to the Council of the Indies and king from Buenos Aires.
   - Subjects include provincial administration, defense, and Indigenous affairs.

4. **Letter or letters to the king**, 1604.

   - Reports on governance and frontier conditions.
   - Letters dated 5 April 1604 are cited in mission scholarship.

5. **Reports on the Banda Oriental**, especially 2 July 1608.

   - Description of fertility, rivers, timber, agriculture, mills, livestock, and potential settlement.

6. **Four letters to the king**, 1608.

   - Archival copies are catalogued by the Museo Mitre.
   - Include requests for assistance to Buenos Aires and defense against Indigenous attacks.

7. **Letters concerning Guayrá and missionary policy**, July 1608 and later.

   - Advocated evangelical rather than purely military incorporation and requested missionaries.

8. **Correspondence and instructions concerning Jesuit missions**, 1609–1610.

   - Connected to the dispatch of Cataldino and Mazeta to Guayrá and missionaries to Paraná and Guaycurú territory.

9. **Letters and reports concerning Indigenous labor and reductions**, 1615–1618.

   - A 25 May 1616 letter describes his inspection of reductions.
   - A 13 May 1618 letter reports three reductions near Buenos Aires.

10. **Letter to the king concerning contraband and prosecution**, 25 May 1616.

    - Describes arrests of Simón de Valdés, Juan de Vergara, Diego de Vega, and others.
    - Contains the allegation about the murder of an alguacil and the confession under torture.

11. **Letter to the king**, 6 July 1616.

    - Cited under Archivo General de Indias, Charcas 27, R.10, N.99 in modern scholarship.

12. **Letter concerning yerba mate**, 8 June 1616.

    - Condemns its extensive use and discusses effects on the provinces and Indigenous labor.

13. **Letters concerning division of the government**, various dates before the royal decree of 16 December 1617.

    - Proposed administrative separation because of distance and difficulty of government.

14. **Statement concerning cattle and island grants**, 1627.

    - Retrospectively describes livestock introductions around 1611 and 1617.
    - Known through later historical citation; original manuscript location should be verified.

15. **Judicial decisions, commissions, appointments, and cabildo communications**, 1592–1630s.

    - Numerous documents survive or are referenced in the Archivo General de Indias, Archivo Nacional de Asunción, Museo Mitre, Biblioteca Nacional Argentina, and Santa Fe collections.
    - These are official products rather than literary works personally penned in full.

### Posthumous collections and editions

- **Cartas y memoriales de Hernandarias**, published in historical and archival journals and later compilations. Such volumes are edited documentary collections, not books assembled by Hernandarias.
- Documents reproduced by **Pablo Pastells**, *Historia de la Compañía de Jesús en la Provincia del Paraguay*.
- Documents in the **Colección Gaspar García Viñas**.
- Manuscript and copied material catalogued in **Documentos del Archivo Colonial**, Museo Mitre.
- Extracts printed in *Anales de la Biblioteca* and historical academy publications.

### Disputed, attributed, or misleading categories

- No verified diary, autobiography, memoir, philosophical treatise, literary work, patent, map personally drawn by him, or private commonplace book was located.
- Speeches assigned to him in later narrative histories are usually literary reconstructions unless tied to a dated proceeding.
- “Hernandarias Manuscript” is a modern archival label for the 1598 regulations, not a title he gave a book.
- Highly expanded signatures or aristocratic forms of his name in modern genealogy should not automatically be treated as the form he personally used.

### Major biographies and focused studies

- **Leoncio Gianello, *Historia de Santa Fe* and *Historia de Entre Ríos*.** Provincial narratives that present him as a leading early criollo.
- **Beatriz Bosch, *Historia de Entre Ríos* (1978).**

- **Pacho O’Donnell, José Ignacio García Hamilton, and Felipe Pigna, *Historia confidencial* (2003).** Popular revisionist reuse of early colonial conflict.
- **Darío G. Barriera, *Abrir puertas a la tierra: microanálisis de la construcción de un espacio político. Santa Fe, 1573–1640* (2013).** Places family, jurisdiction, labor, and politics in regional context.
- **Darío G. Barriera, *Justicias y fronteras* (2009).**
- **Zacarías Moutoukias, *Contrabando y control colonial en el siglo XVII* (1988).** Fundamental for the Buenos Aires commercial network and the limits of the simple hero-versus-smuggler narrative.

- **Pablo Pastells, *Historia de la Compañía de Jesús en la Provincia del Paraguay*, vol. I (Madrid, 1912).** Documentary compilation with letters relevant to Hernandarias and early missions.
- **Pedro Lozano, *Historia de la conquista del Paraguay, Río de la Plata y Tucumán*.** Early Jesuit history and source of influential moral and heroic narratives.

- **Emilio A. Coni, *Historia de las vaquerías de Río de la Plata, 1555–1750*.** Relevant to livestock history and the later cattle legend.

### Hostile or adverse contemporary texts

- **Ruy Díaz de Guzmán, memorial to the king, Tucumán, 8 May 1604.** Seventeen complaints against Hernandarias; preserved in the Gaspar García Viñas collection.
- **Reports and juicio de residencia conducted by Diego de Góngora, beginning 1618.**
- **Letters and statements from Hernando de Vargas and other metropolitan or mercantile opponents.**
- **Proceedings involving Juan de Vergara, Diego de Vega, Mateo de Grado, Simón de Valdés, and associated parties.**

These should be read as interested adversarial testimony, not discarded. They are indispensable for reconstructing patronage, labor use, justice, disability, and factional politics.

## Death, Burial, and Estate

Hernandarias died in Santa Fe in 1634 after approximately four decades of intermittent public office and more than half a century of military, ranching, and political activity.

Documented or strongly reported circumstances:

- He had been out of the governorship since 1618.
- He recovered political standing after imprisonment and confiscation.
- He continued to manage family property and undertake official commissions.
- His wife survived him.
- His three daughters or their descendants inherited family claims and property.

Unknown or unresolved:

- Exact day and month of death.
- Immediate cause of death.
- Complete probate inventory.
- Exact value of his estate.
- Final disposition of every confiscated item.
- Exact original burial location and survival of remains.

No credible evidence was found for assassination, poisoning, suicide, execution, or death in combat.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries

His immediate reputation was divided:

- Admirers described a distinguished soldier, experienced governor, opponent of contraband, and defender of the Crown.
- Enemies described a deaf, partisan local oligarch who controlled offices through relatives and abused Indigenous labor.
- Jesuit histories incorporated him into the providential development of the missions and sometimes narrated his repentance.
- Litigation preserved both favorable and hostile testimony.

### Nineteenth-century appropriation

Emerging national and provincial histories sought antecedents before independence. Hernandarias could be claimed by:

- Paraguay, because he was born in Asunción.
- Argentina, because he governed Buenos Aires and Santa Fe and became a leading criollo public figure.
- Uruguay, because of his explorations and cattle introductions.
- Santa Fe and Entre Ríos, because of his residence, family, land, and campaigns.

### Twentieth-century heroic construction

Writers such as Cervera, Salaberry, Molina, Gianello, and Tiscornia created overlapping images:

- First great criollo.
- Protector of Indigenous people.
- Founder of productive livestock society.
- Honest ruler persecuted by smugglers.
- Proto-national protectionist.
- Catholic statesman.
- Precursor of later Argentine, Paraguayan, or Uruguayan identity.

Barriera shows that these interpretations often import modern political categories into a prenational colonial conflict. Hernandarias could be made compatible with Catholic nationalism, provincialism, revisionism, anti-imperialism, and state-centered developmentalism.

### Critical reinterpretation

Recent scholarship emphasizes:

- Jurisdiction rather than retrospective nation.
- Family and patronage networks rather than isolated heroism.
- The coexistence of protective law and coerced labor.
- Indigenous agency within and against reductions.
- Contraband as a structural Atlantic economy, not merely individual vice.
- Competition between old conquest families and newer merchant networks.
- The interested nature of both accusations and defenses.

### Commemoration

His name survives in:

- **Hernandarias, Paraguay**, formerly Tacurú Pucú.
- **Villa Hernandarias, Entre Ríos, Argentina**.
- Streets, schools, institutes, public buildings, and monuments across the Río de la Plata region.
- The former official name **Túnel Subfluvial Hernandarias**, connecting Santa Fe and Paraná; the tunnel is now officially associated with Raúl Uranga and Carlos Sylvestre Begnis, although “Hernandarias” remains historically familiar.
- Monuments and reliefs in Uruguay commemorating the introduction of cattle.
- Municipal and regional heraldry.
- Textbooks presenting him as the first criollo governor.

### The recovered manuscript

The 2025 recovery of the 1598 manuscript added a new layer to his legacy:

- It confirmed the continued material survival of an ordinance personally signed by him.
- It exposed the illicit international circulation of Paraguayan archival material.
- It renewed official presentation of Hernandarias as a pioneer of Indigenous protection.
- It also makes closer textual study possible, potentially correcting the oversimplified claim that he abolished encomienda.

### Hernán Cortés

**Nature:** Genealogical relationship through Hernandarias’s maternal grandfather, Juan de Sanabria.

**Date and place:** The relationship predates Hernandarias’s birth; no meeting was possible because Cortés died in 1547.

**Evidentiary label:** **Reported fact, exact degree unresolved.** Barriera describes Juan de Sanabria as a relative of Cortés but does not establish the precise line in the cited passage.

### Hernando Arias de Saavedra

The subject himself appears in the atlas roster. This is an identity match, not a separate connection.

### Pedro de Mendoza

Both belonged to successive phases of the Río de la Plata conquest, and Hernandarias later participated in the refounded Buenos Aires after Mendoza’s first settlement had failed. No meeting, correspondence, family tie, acknowledged influence, or other direct personal crossing was found. Mendoza died before Hernandarias was born.

**Evidentiary label:** **No documented personal connection.**

### El Inca Garcilaso de la Vega

A possible extended kinship network appears through Hernandarias’s brother Martín Suárez de Toledo the younger, who married Francisca de Moscoso y de la Vega, described by Barriera as the daughter of a mestizo and granddaughter of the Inca princess Isabel Chimpu Ocllo. Garcilaso was also a son of Isabel Chimpu Ocllo.

This would make Francisca closely related to Garcilaso, but the exact parentage and degree require specialist genealogical confirmation. No direct meeting or correspondence between Hernandarias and Garcilaso was found.

**Evidentiary label:** **Genealogical theory or reported extended family connection; direct relationship unresolved.**

### Miguel de Cervantes Saavedra

The shared surname “Saavedra” does not establish kinship. No meeting, correspondence, collaboration, family connection, or acknowledged influence was found.

**Evidentiary label:** **No documented connection.**
