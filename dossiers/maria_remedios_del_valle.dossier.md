# María Remedios del Valle: Research Dossier

## Research scope and evidentiary method

María Remedios del Valle left no known memoir, correspondence archive, parish dossier, portrait from life, or personally written narrative. Her life is reconstructable principally from:

1. her 1826–1828 petition for compensation and military recognition;
2. certificates and testimony supplied by officers who had served with or known her;
3. the 1828 debates of the Buenos Aires House of Representatives;
4. military payroll and pension records extending to 1847;
5. nineteenth-century military memoirs, especially those of Gregorio Aráoz de Lamadrid;
6. later historiography, iconography, commemorative legislation, and public-memory projects.

The evidentiary labels used below mean:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a contemporary official document, payroll, legislative proceeding, or direct contemporaneous testimony.
- **Reported fact:** stated in a later institutional or scholarly reconstruction, but not independently established by a surviving contemporary record located for this dossier.
- **Claim in her petition:** an assertion made on her behalf in the 1826 compensation proceeding. Such claims are evidence, but not automatically independent proof of every detail.
- **Corroborated claim:** a petition claim substantially confirmed by a named witness with relevant personal knowledge.
- **Disputed identification:** scholars disagree about whether two descriptions refer to the same person.
- **Later tradition:** a detail introduced or elaborated after her lifetime.
- **Myth or unsupported embellishment:** a recurring story for which no adequate documentary foundation was found.
- **Unresolved:** the surviving evidence does not permit a secure conclusion.

Because much of the biographical literature repeats the same small documentary base, the apparent abundance of sources should not be mistaken for independent confirmation of every episode.

### Name and title problems

Her 1826 petition opened under the name “Doña María Remedios del Valle, capitana del Ejército.” Officers and legislators also called her “María de los Remedios,” “María del Valle,” and “la Capitana.” The title “Capitana” was reportedly conferred by Manuel Belgrano, but the original commission has not been found.

By November 1836, military pension lists reportedly carried her as **Remedios Rosas**. Later authors interpreted this as gratitude to Governor Juan Manuel de Rosas for improving her military status and income. That motive is plausible but is an inference, not a surviving declaration by María Remedios. Some popular accounts call her “Mercedes Rosas”; that form should be treated as a likely transmission error unless an original document is produced.

“Madre de la Patria” is attested in nineteenth-century testimony and memory. It was not merely a twenty-first-century invention, although the precise date and person who first bestowed it are unknown.

## Family and Ancestry

### African ancestry and legal status

**Documented fact:** Bernardo de Anzoátegui called her “la parda María del Valle” in evidence submitted during her pension proceeding. The term places her within the colonial racial taxonomy and supports African or mixed African ancestry.

**Unresolved:** No parents, grandparents, place of ancestral origin in Africa, sibling group, parish, confraternity, or household of childhood has been securely identified. No surviving document consulted establishes whether she or an immediate ancestor had been enslaved.

Modern accounts sometimes call her a **liberta**, meaning a freed woman. Florencia Guzmán notes that this description circulates in revisionist retellings, but the documents quoted in the pension file identify her as “parda,” not expressly as formerly enslaved. “Liberta” should therefore be treated as **reported but not established**.

### Husband and children

Her 1826 petition stated that she had lost:

- her husband;
- one biological son;
- one adopted son or stepson, described in different transcriptions as an *hijo adoptivo*, *entenado*, or “otro” son.

Bernardo de Anzoátegui’s certification placed María Remedios and her family in the 6th Company of Flying Artillery that left Buenos Aires in July 1810. This supports the presence of a husband and children during the campaign.

**Documented claim with partial corroboration:** Her husband and two sons died during the campaigns. The petition treated their deaths as part of her claim for compensation, and Anzoátegui confirmed that the family traveled with the force. Exact names, units, dates, places, and causes of death have not been recovered.

### The alleged daughters Lucía and Blanca

Gregorio Aráoz de Lamadrid remembered a “morena” called Tía María or Madre de la Patria carrying water at Ayohuma with one or two daughters. A 1935 *Caras y Caretas* article, “Las Samaritanas de Ayohuma,” named the daughters **Lucía**, aged approximately twenty-five, and **Blanca**, approximately twenty.

These names and ages are a **later tradition**, not a detail found in the pension record. The pension petition instead mentions one biological son and one adopted son or stepson. Possible explanations include:

- Lamadrid’s Tía María was María Remedios, but he misremembered or generalized her companions as daughters;
- the women were unrelated assistants;
- María Remedios had daughters omitted from the compensation petition;
- two separate female figures were later fused.

No source located resolves the conflict.

### Other alleged relatives

Some modern accounts claim that she fought with her mother, also called Tía María, and a sister. No documentary evidence for this family arrangement was located. It should be labeled **unsupported later tradition**.

## Childhood and Early Life

### Birth and childhood

The conventional reconstruction places her birth in Buenos Aires around **1766 or 1767**; some modern institutional sources give 1767, 1768, or even 1776. The basis for a precise year is unclear.

The strongest age indication comes from Juan José Viamonte’s 1828 statement that she was “unos 60 o más años.” This supports a birth before approximately 1768 but does not establish a particular year.

Nothing reliable is known about:

- her exact birth date;
- baptism;
- parents or siblings;
- childhood residence;
- whether she was born free;
- literacy;
- schooling;
- occupation before military service;
- religious affiliation beyond participation in the broadly Catholic society of colonial Buenos Aires.

### Social and urban setting

She grew up, if the conventional birthplace is correct, in late-colonial Buenos Aires, where African-descended people formed a substantial part of the laboring population. Women classified as Black, mulatta, or parda worked in domestic service, markets, food preparation, laundering, sewing, street vending, and household production. This context helps explain her later documented work washing and repairing soldiers’ clothing, but it does not prove a specific childhood occupation.

## Education and Formation

No formal education, school, teacher, intellectual mentor, or apprenticeship is documented.

Her petition was signed **“a ruego de la parte” by Manuel Rico**, indicating that he signed on her behalf. This may suggest that she could not sign, was not sufficiently comfortable writing a formal legal petition, was physically unable to do so, or simply followed a common legal practice. It is not conclusive proof of complete illiteracy.

Her formation appears instead to have been practical:

- care of sick and wounded soldiers;
- washing, mending, provisioning, and camp organization;
- survival during long military marches;
- courier and intelligence-related work;
- guerrilla participation;
- firearms or other combat service;
- navigation of military patronage and later administrative petitioning.

## Early Career: British Invasions, 1806–1807

Modern governmental and biographical accounts state that during the second British invasion of Buenos Aires in 1807 she aided the **Tercio de Andaluces**.

A frequently reproduced military report says that during the Barracas campaign she “asistió y guardó las mochilas para aligerar su marcha a los Corrales de Miserere.”

This is best labeled **reported documentary fact**: the quotation is repeatedly attributed to a contemporary unit report, but a direct image or complete archival transcription of the original report was not located during this research.

The task was logistically important. Removing and guarding packs allowed troops to move more rapidly, while her presence in the campaign indicates an association with organized defensive forces before the May Revolution.

No evidence establishes that she held a military rank in 1807.

## Chronological Life History

## 1810: Departure for the Alto Perú

After the May Revolution, the Buenos Aires government organized the First Auxiliary Expedition to the interior and Upper Peru.

**Documented fact:** Bernardo de Anzoátegui testified that María Remedios traveled in his division, associated with the **6th Company of the Flying Artillery Battalion**, which left Buenos Aires on **7 July 1810**. Modern accounts sometimes give 6 July, probably distinguishing assembly or incorporation from departure.

She traveled with her husband and children. The expedition passed northwest through the former viceregal interior toward Jujuy, the Desaguadero frontier, and Potosí.

Anzoátegui stated that, on an order from Juan José Viamonte received in Jujuy, he conducted her with his division to Potosí. In Potosí she was paid **twenty national pesos**, on the order of Sergeant Major Carreras. This is the earliest specific payment connected to her services found in the record.

Anzoátegui described her conduct:

- she cared for soldiers, corporals, and sergeants without distinction;
- mended and washed their clothing;
- also served officers;
- behaved honestly;
- was so widely known that virtually anyone who had served in the Army of Peru knew of her services.

These functions placed her within the indispensable but poorly formalized labor system that kept early revolutionary armies operating.

## 1811: Upper Peru, Huaqui, and retreat

The revolutionary army occupied parts of Upper Peru but was defeated by royalist forces at **Huaqui/Desaguadero on 20 June 1811**.

Viamonte later said María Remedios followed the Army of Peru during the entire period in which he commanded and that he left her in **Jujuy after the disaster at the Desaguadero**. This provides direct evidence of her presence during or immediately after the campaign and retreat.

Modern biographies usually list her as a participant at Huaqui. The safer formulation is:

- **Documented:** she was with the army through the Desaguadero campaign and retreat.
- **Probable but not individually documented:** she was physically present in the battle line at Huaqui.

No secure record locates the deaths of her husband or sons at Huaqui. Claims that all three died in one particular battle are unsupported unless tied to a new primary document.

## 1812: Reorganization under Manuel Belgrano

Manuel Belgrano assumed command of the Army of the North in 1812. María Remedios remained associated with it.

Hipólito Videla testified that he knew her in the Army of Peru from **1812** onward. Eustoquio Díaz Vélez stated that her services were consistently notable from 1810 to 1814.

Modern biographies place her in:

- the **Jujuy Exodus**, 23 August 1812;
- the **Battle of Tucumán**, 24 September 1812.

Her continuous service makes participation highly plausible. The pension testimony says there was no action in Peru in which she was not found, but it is general rather than a battle-by-battle muster.

### Belgrano and the title “Capitana”

During the 1828 legislative debate, Tomás Manuel de Anchorena, who had served as Belgrano’s secretary and knew María Remedios, stated that Belgrano normally refused to permit women to follow the army but made an exception for her. Anchorena described her as the “paño de lágrimas” of officers and soldiers and said she fought in competition with the bravest soldier.

He recalled that, after one of the victories—Tucumán or Salta, he could not remember which—Belgrano granted her the rank or title of **Capitana**.

This is **strong reported testimony by a knowledgeable contemporary**, but the original military commission or promotion order has not been found. The uncertainty is over when and through what administrative form the rank was conferred, not over whether contemporaries treated her as “Capitana.”

## 1813: Salta, Vilcapugio, Ayohuma

### Battle of Salta, 20 February 1813

Modern accounts place her at the patriot victory at Salta. Her continuous service and Anchorena’s uncertainty over whether Belgrano promoted her after Tucumán or Salta support her presence.

**Reported/corroborated in general testimony:** present with the army and active in the campaign.

### Battle of Vilcapugio, 1 October 1813

She is routinely listed among the participants in the patriot defeat at Vilcapugio. The claim is consistent with the officers’ description of continuous service, though no separate personal action report was located.

### Battle of Ayohuma, 14 November 1813

Ayohuma is the most famous and most source-confused episode of her life.

Hipólito Videla supplied direct evidence:

- María Remedios served in hospitals;
- she encouraged troops in the lines during combat;
- she was shot;
- she fell prisoner at Ayohuma, as Videla himself did.

This is a **corroborated documented fact** based on testimony by a fellow prisoner.

Her 1826 petition further claimed that she:

- fed, preserved, and relieved patriot officers and soldiers held by royalists;
- helped many escape;
- carried correspondence;
- encouraged people to take arms against royalist authorities;
- fought with weapons and without them;
- was publicly flogged for nine days on the orders or under the authority of royalist commanders Pezuela, Ramírez, and Tacón;
- was placed “in chapel” seven times—meaning prepared for execution;
- received six serious bullet wounds over her service.

Videla directly corroborated capture and a bullet wound at Ayohuma. Viamonte later saw and invoked her bullet and flogging scars. Díaz Vélez confirmed her work with the wounded and her service in guerrillas. The precise numbers—nine days, seven threatened executions, six bullet wounds—derive principally from her petition and should remain labeled **claims supported in general nature but not independently verified count by count**.

### The water carriers at Ayohuma

Lamadrid’s memoir describes a Buenos Aires-born “morena” called **Tía María** and **Madre de la Patria**, accompanied by two young women described as her daughters. During the midday cannonade, they repeatedly carried water in jars from a spring between the lines to the thirsty patriot troops.

In a later correction or discussion of José María Paz’s memoirs, Lamadrid remembered “una célebre parda, creo llamada María,” with one or two daughters, known as Madre de la Patria, who had died in Buenos Aires not many years earlier.

Bartolomé Mitre incorporated this account into his *Historia de Belgrano y de la independencia argentina*, making it a canonical patriotic scene. Later schoolbooks named the women collectively the **Niñas de Ayohuma**.

Florencia Guzmán argues that Lamadrid’s later reference to the celebrated parda who had recently died in Buenos Aires strongly identifies Tía María with María Remedios del Valle. Dora Barrancos, however, treated Tía María and María Remedios as separate figures.

The evidence permits the following distinction:

- **Documented:** María Remedios was at Ayohuma, served in hospitals and the battle lines, was shot, and was captured.
- **Contemporary-memory identification, probable but disputed:** she was Lamadrid’s water-carrying Tía María/Madre de la Patria.
- **Unresolved:** whether the accompanying women were her daughters, relatives, fellow camp workers, or products of later narrative fusion.
- **Later label:** “Niñas de Ayohuma” was not the known name of an organized wartime unit.

## 1814 and the unknown return

Díaz Vélez said her important services continued through 1814. The petition states that she made the entire Upper Peru campaign.

The circumstances of her escape or release from royalist captivity are not documented in the sources consulted. Popular versions say she escaped and rejoined patriot forces, but no detailed contemporary account was found.

Her movements between approximately **1814 and 1826** are largely unknown. Possible later service is implied by the petition’s broad language, but dates and units cannot be securely reconstructed.

## Poverty and Survival in Postwar Buenos Aires

By the mid-1820s she was back in Buenos Aires, impoverished, physically impaired, and seeking help.

Viamonte testified in 1828 that:

- he knew her from the Upper Peru campaign;
- he knew her in Buenos Aires;
- she begged for subsistence;
- years of wounds and mistreatment left her naturally unable to work;
- her body displayed bullet wounds and flogging scars.

Carlos Ibarguren’s 1932 reconstruction placed her around:

- the Recova or Plaza de la Victoria, now Plaza de Mayo;
- the atria of San Francisco, San Ignacio, Santo Domingo, and other central churches;
- a poor dwelling or hut in the outskirts or market-garden zone of Buenos Aires.

Stories that she sold cakes, pastries, or fried bread and received leftovers from convents are common in later biographies. These are **later reported details** rather than statements found in the quoted pension testimony. The securely documented point is that she begged and lacked adequate means of support.

The popular story that Viamonte suddenly recognized her while she was begging in the street or at the Recova—exclaiming that she was the Capitana or Madre de la Patria—is dramatically plausible but is not how the strongest documentary sequence is recorded. Viamonte said she had presented herself at his home “the first time,” and he then became a major advocate. The theatrical street-recognition scene should be classified as **later legend**.

## The Pension and Recognition Campaign, 1826–1830

### Initial petition, 23 October 1826

On **23 October 1826**, a formal petition was submitted in her name. Manuel Rico signed “a ruego de la parte.”

The petition requested monetary settlement and recognition for:

- years of military service;
- her rank as captain;
- her wounds and mistreatment;
- the loss of her husband and sons;
- her destitution.

Some transcriptions say she requested **6,000 pesos “para acabar su vida cansada.”** The exact accounting basis requires consultation of the original expediente.

The petition’s closing appeal explicitly introduced racial discrimination:

> she asked that justice and compensation be granted “si su color no le hace indigna al derecho que le otorga al mérito y a las virtudes.”

This is among the most important contemporary statements in the record. It shows that her representative anticipated her color could obstruct recognition.

### Supporting witnesses

Her claim received support from a substantial military network:

- **Juan Martín de Pueyrredón:** said he could not certify the events in detail because he had not belonged to the army at the relevant time, but their notoriety among commanders led him to believe them and to regard her as deserving reward if direct witnesses confirmed them.
- **Bernardo de Anzoátegui:** documented her movement from Jujuy to Potosí, the twenty-peso payment, care for troops, laundering and mending, honesty, and wide recognition.
- **Juan José Viamonte:** confirmed her long attachment to the army, presence after the Desaguadero disaster, wounds, destitution, and general notoriety.
- **Eustoquio Díaz Vélez:** confirmed her valuable service from 1810 to 1814 in care of wounded and sick soldiers and in guerrillas.
- **Hipólito Videla:** confirmed hospital service, encouragement in the battle lines, shooting, and capture at Ayohuma.
- **Manuel Ramírez:** endorsed her as deserving government and national consideration for distinguished patriotism and blood shed.
- **Miguel Rebelo:** later represented her in asking that accounts and back pay be settled.

Officials reportedly could not locate the relevant old muster rolls. Her representatives therefore pointed to bodily scars and the loss of her family as evidence, while officers supplied testimonial proof.

### Delay and legislative debate

The matter entered the Buenos Aires House of Representatives on **25 September 1827** and was debated on **18 July 1828**.

Viamonte, by then a representative for Ensenada, Quilmes, and Magdalena, argued forcefully that:

- she had followed the army since 1810;
- there was no action in Peru in which she had not been present;
- everyone from generals to junior officers knew her;
- she bore bullet and flogging scars;
- the state should not leave her begging.

He estimated her age at “sixty or more” and noted the urgency of assistance.

Representative Silveira called her a heroine and said that, but for her humble condition, she would have become celebrated throughout the world. García Valdez emphasized the contradiction between her service and her return to beg before those who should admire her. Anchorena described her unusual permission to follow Belgrano and her courage in combat.

There was jurisdictional debate over whether Buenos Aires Province or the national government owed the compensation. The House nevertheless approved assistance.

### Pension and proposed biography/monument

The legislature awarded her the pay of a **captain of infantry**, retroactive to **15 March 1827**. Modern reconstructions state that this amounted to **thirty pesos per month**, or approximately one peso per day.

Representative Ceferino Lagos proposed:

- preparing and publishing a biography;
- publishing it in newspapers;
- building a monument;
- having a commission prepare a design and budget.

The proposals were approved unanimously but were not carried out during her lifetime.

### Promotions and military establishment

Subsequent reported administrative milestones are:

- **28 July 1828:** the file passed to the General Accounting Office.
- **21 November 1829:** promotion to **Sargento Mayor de Caballería**.
- **29 January 1830:** inclusion in the staff of the **Cuerpo de Inválidos**, with the full salary of her class.

The Cuerpo de Inválidos accommodated veterans whose wounds, illness, or age limited active duty. The designation confirms that the state eventually recognized her as a military veteran rather than merely a charitable pensioner.

## Later Life Under Juan Manuel de Rosas, 1830–1847

Reported military payroll evidence indicates:

- she appeared with double pay during parts of 1832–1835;
- on **16 April 1835**, Governor Juan Manuel de Rosas assigned her to the active general staff at the rank of sargento mayor;
- by November 1836, she appeared in lists as **Remedios Rosas**;
- her last located receipt, dated **28 October 1847**, recorded a payment of **216 pesos**.

Modern sources often say Rosas increased her pension by more than 600 percent. Nominal peso comparisons in this inflationary and institutionally changing period require caution. The administrative status and reported amount are more secure than claims about their real purchasing power.

No evidence was found that María Remedios:

- held property of consequence;
- owned a business;
- made investments;
- maintained a patronage network;
- participated in Rosas-era repression;
- held a political party office;
- engaged in commercial or financial wrongdoing.

Her adoption or acceptance of the name Rosas may have signaled gratitude, patronage, political loyalty, an administrative renaming, or some combination. No statement in her own words establishes the motive.

---

## Death and Burial

A military-list notation dated **8 November 1847** states:

> “El mayor de caballería Dña. Remedios Rosas falleció.”

This is the basis for the accepted death date.

Her precise time and place of death within Buenos Aires, terminal illness, cause of death, funeral, church, cemetery, grave, and estate proceedings are unknown.

There is no evidence that she died wholly unrecognized: by then she held a recognized military rank and received pay. The more precise formulation is that recognition came late, after years of poverty and administrative struggle, and that her public historical reputation remained fragmentary.

### Personal finances

Known or reported amounts include:

- **20 pesos:** payment in Potosí during the 1810 campaign;
- **6,000 pesos:** lump-sum compensation reportedly requested in the 1826 petition;
- **30 pesos monthly:** pension/pay awarded at captain’s level, retroactive to 15 March 1827;
- **216 pesos:** amount on her last reported receipt, 28 October 1847.

Comparisons between these amounts are complicated by currency changes, fiscal disorder, inflation, and the distinction between pension, salary, arrears, and nominal military pay. No secure valuation of her wealth can be made.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Political activity

Her political commitment was practical and military rather than theoretical. The petition depicts her as supporting independence “desde el primer grito de la Revolución.”

Documented or corroborated activity included:

- accompanying revolutionary armies;
- tending their wounded;
- fighting in guerrillas;
- encouraging troops in battle;
- carrying correspondence;
- encouraging resistance to royalist power;
- aiding patriot prisoners and allegedly facilitating escapes.

No political manifesto, party membership, election activity, public office beyond military status, or postwar ideological statement survives.

### Religion

No personal devotional writings or formal religious affiliations are known. The petition and legislative rhetoric used conventional Christian and patriotic language. Her reported presence around church atria in later life likely reflects the role of churches in urban almsgiving rather than proof of a particular devotional practice.

Mitre compared the women carrying water at Ayohuma with the biblical Samaritan. That was Mitre’s literary framing, not a recorded self-description by María Remedios.

### Intellectual or philosophical development

No direct evidence survives. Claims about feminism, nationalism in the modern sense, abolitionism, or later political ideologies should not be projected backward without evidence. Her petition does, however, contain a clear contemporary challenge to exclusion based on color.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Manuel Belgrano

Belgrano was her commanding general from 1812. Anchorena testified that he made an exception to his rule excluding women from the marching army and allowed María Remedios to accompany it. He reportedly awarded her the title of captain after Tucumán or Salta.

The relationship was military rather than demonstrably personal. No correspondence between them survives.

### Juan José Viamonte

Viamonte knew her during the Upper Peru campaign, ordered Anzoátegui to conduct her divisionally from Jujuy, saw her again in Buenos Aires, and became the principal legislative advocate for her pension. His testimony was central to her recognition.

### Bernardo de Anzoátegui

Her immediate divisional commander or sponsoring officer during the early campaign. He documented her movement, payment, labor, honesty, and service to troops of all ranks.

### Eustoquio Díaz Vélez

A senior officer who corroborated her hospital and guerrilla service from 1810 through 1814.

### Hipólito Videla

A fellow member of the army and fellow prisoner after Ayohuma. His statement is the strongest direct corroboration of her combat-line service, wounding, and capture.

### Tomás Manuel de Anchorena

Belgrano’s former secretary, fellow campaign participant, legislator, and defender of her claim. His recollection supports the Belgrano promotion tradition.

### Juan Martín de Pueyrredón

Supported her petition cautiously. He explicitly admitted that he lacked direct detailed knowledge but considered her services publicly notorious and deserving of compensation if direct witnesses confirmed them.

### Manuel Rico and Miguel Rebelo

Legal or administrative representatives who advanced her claim. Rico signed the 1826 petition for her; Rebelo later sought back pay and adjustment of accounts.

### Juan Manuel de Rosas

As governor of Buenos Aires, Rosas’s administration placed her on the active staff in 1835 and improved her recognized military status or pay. The later payroll name Remedios Rosas suggests an association, but there is no evidence of a close personal relationship.

### Royalist adversaries

Her petition named:

- Joaquín de la Pezuela;
- a commander Ramírez;
- a commander Tacón.

They were identified as authorities responsible for or associated with her punishment for correspondence, recruitment, combat, and assistance to prisoners. Exact command responsibility for every flogging or death sentence was not independently established.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Financial Questions

## No personal criminal or corruption case found

The dedicated adversarial searches combined her name with Spanish and English terms for controversy, scandal, fraud, accusation, lawsuit, conspiracy, and criticism, including 2024–2026 results.

No credible evidence was found that María Remedios herself was accused of:

- fraud;
- corruption;
- theft;
- financial misconduct;
- a civil lawsuit;
- common crime;
- abuse of authority;
- intelligence work for a foreign power;
- postwar political violence.

From the royalist perspective, her alleged conduct—carrying insurgent correspondence, inducing people to take arms, aiding prisoners, and facilitating escapes—was criminal rebellion or espionage-related activity. From the revolutionary government’s perspective, it was patriotic service. The conduct is reported in her compensation petition; the precise royalist trial records or sentences have not been located.

## Was she an invented person?

Following her proposed appearance on Argentine banknotes, social-media users reportedly called her fictitious, an invention of a gender agenda, or a construction connected to George Soros.

**Classification: conspiracy narrative / false historical claim.**

Evidence against the claim includes:

- the 1826 petition;
- named testimony from multiple senior officers;
- the 1828 legislative debate;
- nineteenth-century military payrolls;
- the death notation in the 1847 list;
- nineteenth-century memoir and historiography.

The real source-critical problem is not her existence. It is the later fusion or embellishment of particular episodes.

## Was her rank fabricated retrospectively?

**Disputed in administrative detail, not in substance.**

No original Belgrano commission has been found, and old muster lists were missing when she sought compensation. That documentary gap delayed her case. Nevertheless:

- her 1826 petition identified her as captain;
- officers and legislators treated the rank as credible;
- Anchorena attributed the promotion to Belgrano;
- the legislature awarded captain’s pay;
- she was later formally promoted to sargento mayor and entered military establishments.

Thus, “Belgrano named her captain” is supported by contemporary recollection but lacks the original commission. It is not a modern fabrication.

## Pension jurisdiction and state neglect

The legislature debated whether Buenos Aires Province or the national authority bore responsibility. The institutional confusion reflected the collapse of the central government in 1820 and the accumulated unpaid obligations owed to independence veterans.

The documentary record supports a pattern of delayed compensation. It does not support a claim that María Remedios was uniquely denied while all male veterans were promptly paid; many veterans and their families faced arrears and failed promises. Her color, sex, poverty, and inability to produce missing muster records nevertheless created additional obstacles explicitly recognized in her petition and the debate.

## Numbers of wounds, floggings, and threatened executions

The figures of:

- six serious bullet wounds;
- nine days of public flogging;
- seven occasions “in chapel” awaiting execution,

come primarily from her petition.

Viamonte confirmed seeing bullet and flogging scars; Videla confirmed one combat wound and capture; Díaz Vélez confirmed guerrilla service. The exact counts remain **petition claims with substantial general corroboration**, not independently verified numerical totals.

## The destroyed 2022 monument

A monument by Alexis Minkiewicz, developed with Gisella/Gisela Kraisman and Louis Youpanqui, was inaugurated in the Plazoleta Alfonso Castelao, Buenos Aires, on 8 November 2022. It was made from combustible synthetic materials including resin.

In the early hours of **1 September 2023**, it was burned and destroyed.

**Documented fact:** the monument was deliberately or at least externally burned and left destroyed.

**Unresolved:** no perpetrator, organizational responsibility, or judicial finding was identified in the sources consulted.

Government officials, activists, and scholars interpreted the destruction as a racist or politically motivated attack. That interpretation is plausible given the object and public context but remains an **interpretation**, not a proven motive attributable to an identified offender.

A separate criticism concerned the use of flammable and potentially fragile materials for a permanent outdoor monument. That design criticism is documented in later scholarship and does not establish who destroyed it.

## Political instrumentalization

Critics of her inclusion on banknotes argued that:

- she was less historically important than Belgrano;
- other figures had greater claims to commemoration;
- her rediscovery served contemporary gender or racial politics;
- the monument and currency image were government propaganda.

These are political assessments rather than factual disproof of her service. Academic studies by Florencia Guzmán, María de Lourdes Ghidoli, Ana Bugnone, and others agree that her image has been used in changing projects of national memory. The evidence also shows that her rediscovery was not created by a single modern administration: it passed through nineteenth-century memoir, Mitre, 1930s nationalism, military biography, late twentieth-century scholarship, Afro-Argentine activism, feminist research, revisionist popular history, legislation, monuments, and currency design.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## “Madre de la Patria”

### Story

Soldiers allegedly called her “Madre de la Patria” because she cared for them, fought beside them, and carried water under fire.

### Appearance and circulation

Lamadrid used the title in his nineteenth-century recollections. Mitre repeated it in 1857. Carlos Ibarguren, Jacinto Yaben, later schoolbooks, revisionist writers, Afro-Argentine organizations, and the Argentine state subsequently amplified it.

### Evidence

The sobriquet is historically attested. The identity of the first person to use it and the exact occasion remain unknown.

### Effect

It supplied a female and Afro-Argentine counterpart to the male “Father of the Nation” model and became central to later public commemoration.

## “Niña de Ayohuma”

### Story

María Remedios was one of three heroic female water carriers who crossed the battlefield at Ayohuma.

### Appearance

The canonical seed is Lamadrid’s account of Tía María and two daughters. Mitre retold it. The collective phrase “Niñas de Ayohuma” emerged later in patriotic pedagogy and iconography.

### Evidence

Her presence, wounding, and capture at Ayohuma are documented. Her identification with Lamadrid’s Tía María is probable to some scholars but disputed by others.

### Effect

The story made her visible but also simplified her career into a maternal water-carrying role, obscuring guerrilla, courier, hospital, and armed-combat service.

## Lucía and Blanca

### Story

Her daughters Lucía and Blanca helped her carry water.

### Appearance

Eros Nicola Siri’s 1935 *Caras y Caretas* article supplied names, ages, and an elaborate family story.

### Evidence

No earlier source located supplies these names. Her pension instead mentions sons. The account is **unverified later narrative**.

### Effect

It helped establish a vivid trio for school and popular imagery.

## The street recognition by Viamonte

### Story

Viamonte encountered an old beggar near the Recova, suddenly recognized her as the Capitana, and exclaimed publicly that she was the Madre de la Patria.

### Evidence

Viamonte undeniably knew her, advocated for her, and said she begged. He referred to her visiting his home. The dramatic chance encounter and exclamation are not established by the quoted primary record.

### Classification

**Legend built around documented relationships.**

## Belgrano’s exclusive female follower

### Story

Belgrano excluded all women except María Remedios.

### Evidence

Anchorena testified that Belgrano did not allow women to follow and made an exception for her. Military armies nevertheless contained many women in camp, supply, family, and informal support roles.

### Interpretation

The statement likely refers to formal permission to remain with the force or close to operational units, not the literal absence of every other woman.

## “Forgotten until the twenty-first century”

### Story

Argentina entirely erased her until recent feminist or Afro-Argentine activism restored her.

### Evidence against a total-erasure claim

She appeared in:

- 1820s official proceedings;
- military lists through 1847;
- Lamadrid’s memoir tradition;
- Mitre’s 1857 history;
- Ibarguren’s 1932 work;
- Yaben’s 1940 military biography;
- *Caras y Caretas* and school narratives;
- twentieth-century military-historical publications.

### More accurate description

Her memory was intermittent, fragmented, often racialized or deracialized, and rarely central to the national pantheon. Twenty-first-century recognition was a major expansion, not creation from nothing.

## Authentic portrait

No verified portrait made from life is known. Images commonly presented as her portrait are later artistic constructions.

Important modern examples include:

- Miriam Gomes portraying her for *Veintitrés* in 2008;
- Gisela Banzer’s painting *La Capitana*;
- portraits selected through government competitions;
- Alexis Minkiewicz’s monument;
- the image derived from Banzer’s work on the 10,000-peso banknote.

These are commemorative images, not documentary likenesses.

## Works by María Remedios del Valle

No books, essays, letters, diaries, speeches, artworks, or memoirs securely authored in her hand are known.

### Attributable documentary work

1. **Petition beginning “Doña María Remedios del Valle, capitana del Ejército…”**  
   Date: 23 October 1826.  
   Form: dictated or lawyer-drafted legal petition; signed on her behalf by Manuel Rico, “a ruego de la parte.”  
   Status: authentic document submitted in her name, but not demonstrably written personally by her.  
   Contents: military service, wounds, capture and punishment, aid to prisoners, family losses, rank, destitution, and request for compensation.

2. **Subsequent compensation and back-pay representations**  
   Dates: 1827–1828.  
   Form: administrative submissions advanced by representatives including Miguel Rebelo.  
   Status: claims made for her, not independent authored works.

No separate corpus should be attributed to her merely because later books, plays, or educational materials carry her name.

## Books and Major Works About María Remedios del Valle

### Primary and near-primary sources

- **María Remedios del Valle / Manuel Rico.** Pension and recognition petition, 23 October 1826. Preserved through the General Accounting/National archival record and reproduced by later historians.
- **Bernardo de Anzoátegui, Juan José Viamonte, Eustoquio Díaz Vélez, Hipólito Videla, Juan Martín de Pueyrredón, Manuel Ramírez.** Certifications and statements in support of her claim, 1826–1827.
- **Buenos Aires House of Representatives.** Debate of 18 July 1828 concerning her pension and military recognition.
- **Gregorio Aráoz de Lamadrid.** *Memorias*, written substantially by the early 1840s, published posthumously; and *Sobre las memorias póstumas del Brigadier General D. José M. Paz…* (1855). Important for Tía María/Madre de la Patria and Ayohuma.
- **Military payroll and pension lists, 1829–1847.** Basis for rank, Cuerpo de Inválidos status, Remedios Rosas name, final payment, and death notation.

### Foundational nineteenth- and twentieth-century treatments

- **Bartolomé Mitre.** *Historia de Belgrano y de la independencia argentina*. First edition, 1857; expanded later editions. Canonized the water-carrying Madre de la Patria episode.
- **Carlos Ibarguren.** *En la penumbra de la historia argentina*. Buenos Aires, 1932. Includes “La Capitana María Remedios del Valle, Madre de la Patria,” organized under “Una mendiga,” “Heroína ignorada,” and “Triste final.”

- **Jacinto R. Yaben.** Entry on María Remedios del Valle in *Biografías argentinas y sudamericanas*, 1940. Military-biographical reconstruction based on accounting records.
- **Aníbal Jorge Luzuriaga and Julio Arturo Benencia.** “Sargenta Mayor María Remedios del Valle.” *Revista del Instituto Belgraniano Central*, 1980. Reproduced the expediente more fully.
- **Oscar Beltrán.** *Episodios argentinos*, vol. 1, 1947.

- **Pacho O’Donnell.** “Las mujeres de Mayo” and later biographical treatments. Popular revisionist account.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### 1847–1890: Military memory and national history

After her death, Lamadrid’s memories and Mitre’s national history preserved the Madre de la Patria/Ayohuma story. Mitre’s version emphasized humanitarian courage and maternal service more than her formal military career or petition.

### 1930s–1980s: Nationalist, military, and school recovery

Carlos Ibarguren recovered the pension debate in 1932. *Caras y Caretas* dramatized the Ayohuma women in 1935. Yaben reconstructed her military status in 1940. Schoolbooks and patriotic images circulated the Niñas de Ayohuma, sometimes depicting the women with light skin and thereby obscuring their African descent.

In 1980, Luzuriaga and Benencia published the pension expediente through the Instituto Belgraniano, strengthening the documentary basis for later scholarship.

A Buenos Aires street and schools were named for her. Exact dates of every naming were not established here, although street-name advocacy is associated with educator and official Octavio Sergio Pico.

### 2000s–2010s: Afro-Argentine and feminist reappropriation

Studies of women, slavery, race, and military participation reframed her as an Afro-Argentine historical actor rather than only a sentimental battlefield figure.

The 16 October 2008 issue of *Veintitrés* featured activist and scholar Miriam Gomes portraying her. The cover and accompanying articles helped trigger a new cycle of monument proposals, journalism, and Afro-Argentine public-memory claims.

Revisionist popular historians Felipe Pigna and Pacho O’Donnell brought her to larger audiences around the Bicentenary.

### Law 26,852

On **24 April 2013**, Congress enacted Law 26,852, published on 22 May 2013. It established **8 November as the National Day of Afro-Argentines and Afro Culture**, expressly commemorating María Remedios del Valle and stating that Belgrano granted her the rank of captain for courage in battle.

The law:

- entered the date in the school calendar;
- directed educational authorities to incorporate the commemoration and promotion of Afro culture;
- directed cultural authorities to support public visibility.

### Portraits and official imagery

In 2020–2021, national cultural authorities held portrait and sculpture competitions. Because no authentic portrait survives, the process necessarily created symbolic likenesses.

The painting **La Capitana** by Gisela Banzer became especially influential. It depicts a dark-skinned military woman facing forward with a violet cloth, joining military, Afro-Argentine, and feminist visual languages.

A portrait was installed in the Argentine Chamber of Deputies in 2021, described institutionally as the first portrait of a woman placed in that building’s relevant display area.

### Monument

The monument resulting from a national competition was inaugurated on **8 November 2022** in Plazoleta Alfonso Castelao. Alexis Minkiewicz led the work in collaboration with Gisella/Gisela Kraisman and Louis Youpanqui.

Its destruction by fire on 1 September 2023 turned the sculpture into a new site of political memory. Scholars and activists interpreted the episode through debates over race, gender, national identity, monument materials, and control of public space.

A separate commemorative monument had been inaugurated at a school in Santa Rosa, La Pampa, in December 2018.

### Currency

In May 2022 the Central Bank announced a “Heroines and Heroes of the Homeland” banknote family, initially assigning María Remedios and Manuel Belgrano to a proposed 500-peso note.

On **7 May 2024**, the Central Bank placed a **10,000-peso banknote** into circulation bearing:

- Manuel Belgrano and María Remedios del Valle on the front;
- a reconstruction of the 1812 flag oath on the reverse.

Her image derives from Gisela Banzer’s *La Capitana*, not an authentic lifetime portrait.

The banknote generated public dispute over:

- her historical importance;
- unfamiliarity with her life;
- representation of women and Afro-Argentines;
- political use of historical images;
- continuity between governments with sharply different ideological positions.

### Present historical position

Her current significance rests on several distinct, sometimes competing identities:

- independence veteran;
- military officer;
- Afro-Argentine historical figure;
- woman combatant;
- caregiver and hospital worker;
- victim of royalist punishment;
- impoverished veteran;
- symbol of delayed state recognition;
- emblem of Afro-Argentine visibility;
- figure in feminist public memory;
- subject of revisionist nationalism;
- contested image in contemporary party politics.

These roles are historically consequential even when they simplify different parts of her life.

## Atlas Connections

The atlas roster was checked for documented personal, institutional, political, or posthumous intersections. Mere contemporaneity or common geography was not counted.

### Manuel Belgrano — documented/reported fact

- **Nature:** commanding general; reportedly granted her the rank of Capitana.
- **Dates and places:** Army of the North, principally 1812–1814; Tucumán, Salta, and the Upper Peru campaigns.
- **Evidence:** Tomás Manuel de Anchorena, who served as Belgrano’s secretary and knew María Remedios, testified in 1828 that Belgrano uniquely permitted her to accompany the army and promoted her after Tucumán or Salta. The original commission has not been found.
- **Label:** **Documented relationship; promotion reported by knowledgeable contemporary testimony.**

### Juan Martín de Pueyrredón — documented fact

- **Nature:** supporter of her compensation claim.
- **Dates and places:** Buenos Aires, 1826–1827 pension proceeding.
- **Evidence:** Pueyrredón submitted a statement saying he lacked detailed firsthand knowledge of the relevant campaigns but regarded her service as publicly notorious and believed her deserving of reward if direct witnesses confirmed it.
- **Label:** **Documented administrative/political support, not documented close personal association.**

### Juan Manuel José Domingo Ortiz de Rozas y López de Osornio — documented fact

- **Nature:** governor under whose administration she was assigned to active general staff and received improved military recognition/pay; this is Juan Manuel de Rosas under his full atlas-roster name.
- **Dates and places:** Buenos Aires, particularly 16 April 1835 onward.
- **Evidence:** reported decree and military lists; later lists call her Remedios Rosas.
- **Label:** **Documented institutional patronage; the claim that she personally adopted his surname from gratitude is an inference.**

### José de San Martín — no documented personal crossing found

San Martín replaced Belgrano in northern command and belongs to the same independence history, but no source consulted documents a meeting, correspondence, command relationship, or acknowledged influence involving María Remedios.

- **Label:** **No supported connection beyond broader institutional succession.**

### Carlos María de Alvear — no documented connection found

Alvear was a revolutionary and military contemporary, but no source consulted places him in a personal or administrative relationship with her.

- **Label:** **No supported connection.**

### Juan Martín de Pueyrredón and Manuel Belgrano versus broader atlas roster

Other rostered independence figures—Juana Azurduy, Martín Miguel de Güemes, Bernardo O’Higgins, José de San Martín, Simón Bolívar, Antonio José de Sucre, Manuela Sáenz, Manuel Belgrano, and others—shared the broader independence era. No additional direct relationship with María Remedios was documented in the consulted sources.

**Absence is the finding:** apart from Belgrano, Pueyrredón, and Rosas, the research did not establish further roster connections without relying on inference from common wars, institutions, or geography.
