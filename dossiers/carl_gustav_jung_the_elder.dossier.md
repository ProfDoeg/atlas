# Carl Gustav Jung the elder: Research Dossier

## Scope and identification

This dossier concerns the German-Swiss physician, anatomist, surgeon, obstetrician, university reformer, politician, philanthropist, dramatist, and Freemason usually catalogued as **Karl Gustav Jung** or **Carl Gustav Jung** (1794/95–1864).

He must be distinguished from:

- his grandson, the psychiatrist **Carl Gustav Jung** (1875–1961), founder of analytical psychology;
- other authors catalogued as “C. G. Jung”;
- the unrelated German physician and writer Johann Heinrich Jung-Stilling.

The label **“the elder”**, **“Jung the Elder,”** or **“Carl Gustav Jung I”** is retrospective, used principally to distinguish him from his famous grandson.

### Birth-year problem

The date is consistently given as **7 September**, but the year varies:

- German and Swiss archival authority records, LEO-BW, the Deutsche Digitale Bibliothek, and much specialist literature give **1794**.
- Several derivative biographies and encyclopedic records give **1795**.
- His son Ernst’s privately printed diary extracts have appeared with both “1794–1864” and “1795–1864” in bibliographical descriptions.

The strongest institutional authority-file evidence presently favors **7 September 1794**, while **1795** should remain visible as a persistent competing cataloguing tradition. The atlas’s “1794/95” formulation is therefore justified.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Documented fact:** Jung was the legally recognized son of:

- **Franz Ignaz Jung** (1759–1831), a physician, medical councillor, and court physician associated with Mainz and Baden;
- **Maria Josepha/Sophie Maria Josepha Ziegler** (born 1770; later death date not securely established in the consulted material).

The family was Roman Catholic. Franz Ignaz appears to have been prosperous enough to support his son during portions of his advanced education and Paris residence.

### Siblings and extended family

The available online archival descriptions do not yield a complete verified sibling list. Later biographical literature mentions relatives linked to the wider Protestant-Romantic circle around Friedrich Schleiermacher, but a full reconstruction would require examination of Mannheim parish registers, Jung family papers, and the Basel Nachlass.

### Marriages

Jung was married three times and outlived all three wives.

1. **Virginie de Lassaulx / de Lassault** (usually 1804–1828; one later biography gives 1830).

   She belonged to a Rhineland/French-connected family. Jung married her during or shortly before the move from Paris to Basel. Sources report three children from this marriage, but the complete names and vital dates were not recovered from the accessible records.

2. **Katharina Elisabeth Reyenthaler** (usually 1813–1835), also rendered **Elisabeth Catharina Reyenthaler**.

   Later family narrative portrays her as a Basel tavern waitress whom Jung abruptly proposed to after Sophie Frey or her father initially rejected him. Two children are reported from the marriage. The marriage itself is documented; the theatrical proposal story is family legend and is treated separately below.

3. **Sophie Frey** (approximately 1812–1855), daughter of Basel mayor **Johann Rudolf Frey**.

   She had reportedly been the object of Jung’s courtship before his marriage to Reyenthaler. After Reyenthaler’s death, Jung and Sophie married. The marriage tied the formerly suspect German political refugee to an established Basel political family.

### Children

Older accounts sometimes gave inconsistent totals. Albrecht Burckhardt’s history of the Basel medical faculty and later Basel local-history work support **thirteen children**, of whom **seven died before their father**.

Identifiable children include:

- **Emma Jung** (1823–1844), represented in the Basel manuscript catalogue through biographical and correspondence material;
- **Ernst Georg Constantin Jung** (1841–1912), architect and later editor of extracts from his father’s diaries;
- **Johann Paul Achilles Jung** (1842–1896), Protestant pastor and father of psychiatrist Carl Gustav Jung.

Not all thirteen children’s names, maternity, and dates can be responsibly reconstructed from the online material consulted. Statements that Jung had “at least thirteen” children reflect this incomplete documentation rather than evidence for more than thirteen.

### Grandchildren and later family

Through Paul Jung and Emilie Preiswerk, Jung became the paternal grandfather of **Carl Gustav Jung** (1875–1961). The elder Jung died eleven years before the psychiatrist’s birth, so there was no personal meeting.

Architect Ernst Georg Jung preserved part of the elder Jung’s family memory and issued diary selections, helping shape the later picture of him as refugee, reformer, patriarch, and philanthropist.

## Childhood and Early Life

Jung was born in Mannheim while the Rhineland and southwestern German lands were being transformed by the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic wars. His father’s medical position placed him within an educated professional household.

Detailed, independently documented information about his earliest schooling is scarce in accessible online sources. Later biographies characterize him as intellectually energetic, literary, musically inclined, religiously questioning, and receptive to the liberal-national ideals that spread among German students after the wars against Napoleon.

The young Jung’s adult identity developed at the intersection of:

- professional medicine;
- Romantic literature;
- Protestant theology;
- liberal constitutionalism;
- German national unity;
- the associative culture of student fraternities.

---

## Education and Formation

### Heidelberg

**Documented fact:** Jung began medical studies at the University of Heidelberg in or about **1813**. He received his medical doctorate in **1816**.

In **1815**, he joined the Heidelberg **Burschenschaft Teutonia**. The early Burschenschaften mixed student reform, national unification, constitutional liberalism, patriotic ritual, and hostility to the post-Napoleonic order of separate German monarchies.

Jung attended the **Wartburg Festival of 18 October 1817**. He later regarded it, according to Burschenschaft and family traditions, as one of the purest and most meaningful experiences of his youth.

### Religious conversion

**Reported fact:** Jung was raised Catholic but converted to Protestantism around **1817**. Later accounts associate the change with his attraction to the theology and intellectual atmosphere of **Friedrich Schleiermacher** and philosopher **Jakob Friedrich Fries**.

A later family tradition states that Schleiermacher signed or participated in the conversion/baptismal documentation. This is plausible within Jung’s Berlin network but was not independently verified from a digitized original in the present research.

### Berlin

After Heidelberg, Jung went to Berlin for advanced work in:

- surgery;
- ophthalmology;
- anatomy;
- clinical medicine.

He worked or trained in the orbit of the **Charité** and the royal military medical school. Later narratives connect him with the surgeon **Johann Nepomuk Rust** and the household of publisher **Georg Andreas Reimer**, a center of liberal and Romantic intellectual society.

Berlin brought Jung into contact with leading medical, theological, literary, and political networks, but it also placed him under surveillance during the Prussian campaign against alleged “demagogues.”

## Political Persecution, Arrest, and Exile

### Karl Ludwig Sand and the geological hammer

On 23 March 1819, Burschenschaft member **Karl Ludwig Sand** murdered the conservative dramatist and Russian state councillor August von Kotzebue. The killing accelerated the German governments’ repression of student organizations and liberal-national activists.

**Documented fact:** Jung was arrested in Berlin on **7 July 1819**.

Police found in his possession a **geological hammer given to him by Sand**. The hammer was treated as incriminating or at least suspicious evidence of association. In later retellings it became a symbol of the absurdity of the investigation: an ordinary scientific instrument construed as a dangerous weapon.

**Documented fact:** Jung spent approximately **thirteen months** in Berlin’s Hausvogtei detention facility.

**Important legal distinction:** Koelbing’s archive-based reconstruction states that Jung was held and then released **without a judicial verdict**. Some derivative accounts incorrectly say he was “convicted to thirteen months.” The stronger evidence supports **investigative/political detention without conviction**, followed by expulsion from Prussia.

### Consequences

On release, Jung was expelled from Prussia and found German university employment effectively closed to him because of his political file. His first promising academic career had failed for political rather than scholarly reasons.

He was therefore:

- a documented political detainee;
- a person punished administratively without a demonstrated criminal conviction;
- subsequently a liberal political refugee or forced professional migrant.

No evidence was found that he participated in Sand’s murder, planned political violence, or committed a prosecutable offense.

## Paris and the Road to Switzerland, 1821–1822

Jung went to **Paris in June 1821**, then Europe’s leading center for clinical medicine, anatomy, and surgery. He studied or worked in the surgical environment of the **Hôtel-Dieu**, associated in later accounts with **Guillaume Dupuytren**.

### Alexander von Humboldt’s patronage

In Paris, Jung encountered **Alexander von Humboldt**. The surviving documentation, as reconstructed by Huldrych Koelbing, establishes that Humboldt supported Jung’s search for an academic appointment.

The appointment process had at least two stages:

1. Jung applied for a chair of chemistry at the Academy of Bern.
2. After that attempt failed, he applied for the chair of anatomy, surgery, and midwifery at Basel.

Humboldt supplied recommendations, including correspondence directed to Basel’s governing circle. This patronage was decisive or at minimum highly influential.

Later family versions embellished the meeting:

- in one account Jung was seated unknowingly beside Humboldt at a banquet and impressed him;
- another says Humboldt wished to compensate for the injustices inflicted by the Prussian state;
- the most documentary version places the introduction within Jung’s already existing network around Reimer, Schleiermacher, Rust, and German émigré intellectuals.

The indisputable core is that Humboldt knew Jung, recommended him, and helped open the Basel route.

### Arrival in Basel

Jung left Paris in mid-March 1822 and reportedly arrived in Basel on **18 March 1822**. He was twenty-seven or twenty-eight.

Basel then had fewer than 25,000 inhabitants, a small patrician commercial republic with an ancient but weakened university. Its medical faculty had recently faced possible abolition or reduction to a preparatory school.

---

## Early Basel Career, 1822–1828

### Appointment

**Documented fact:** In 1822 Jung was appointed to the University of Basel in anatomy, surgery, and obstetrics/midwifery. Sources differ in describing the initial rank—lecturer, extraordinary professor, or immediate professor—but agree that he soon held the ordinary professorial chair.

His remit was unusually broad. He was responsible for subjects that later became separate specialties.

### Reform program

Jung argued that medicine could not be taught adequately without:

- systematic anatomical dissection;
- prepared specimens and models;
- an anatomical theatre;
- a dissector;
- proper clinical institutions;
- closer integration of theoretical instruction and bedside experience.

His 1823 pamphlet, *Können in Basel die nöthigsten Hülfsanstalten zur Förderung medizinischer Studien gegründet werden?*, publicly framed the institutional problem.

### Anatomical Cabinet and Museum

In **1824**, after securing municipal credit and authorization, Jung established an **Anatomical Cabinet** in the Lower College at Rheinsprung. It became the foundation of the present Anatomical Museum of the University of Basel.

Under Jung:

- dissections were institutionalized;
- anatomical preparations and wax models were manufactured or acquired;
- the collection became available for medical instruction;
- it was eventually opened to public viewing on Sundays;
- between 1824 and 1844 it reportedly grew to approximately 3,000 objects.

Some older specimens in the present museum predate Jung, including Basel’s early modern anatomical holdings. He should therefore be described as founder of the modern institutional museum and organizer of the expanded collection, not creator of every object it contains.

### Citizenship

In **1824**, Jung received Basel citizenship. Sources sometimes call this both Swiss naturalization and the “freedom of the city.” In the political structure of the period, Basel citizenship was the decisive civic incorporation.

---

## University Rector and Medical-Faculty Builder

Jung became **Rector of the University of Basel in 1828**. His rectoral address of 26 September 1828 treated the relationship of anatomy to medical science and the historical contribution of Basel anatomists.

His work in the 1820s and 1830s is credited with helping rescue the medical faculty from institutional marginality. He:

- expanded the curriculum;
- joined anatomical, surgical, obstetric, and clinical teaching;
- pressed authorities for staff, space, and equipment;
- linked the faculty to the hospital;
- maintained instruction through severe financial retrenchment;
- helped make Basel more attractive to medical students.

The 1833 division of the canton into Basel-Stadt and Basel-Landschaft produced a financial crisis. The 1835 university law assigned the medical faculty a restricted, primarily theoretical preparatory role. Jung nevertheless continued to teach a wide range of fields, at times accepting reduced resources and salary.

Descriptions of him as the sole “savior” of the faculty are commemorative simplifications. Basel politicians, fellow professors, hospital officials, and municipal financing were also necessary. The documentary record nonetheless supports his central role.

---

## Hospital Career and Clinical Practice

Jung became a hospital physician at the **Basel Bürgerspital** in **1828**. With the opening of the rebuilt or reorganized hospital in the **Markgräflerhof, Hebelstrasse 7, in 1842**, he served as senior physician of the medical department.

He remained closely associated with the hospital for nearly thirty-six years, until his death.

### Clinical records

The Basel hospital’s medical case records survive continuously from about 1840. Modern researchers consider their unusually systematic form likely indebted to Jung’s scientific and administrative discipline.

The files recorded:

- patient identity and occupation;
- diagnosis and symptoms;
- treatment;
- ward placement;
- responsibility for fees;
- course and outcome.

These records are major primary sources for the social history of illness, poverty, migration, and medical practice in nineteenth-century Basel.

### Private practice and income

Jung also maintained a private medical practice. Later biographies describe him as one of Basel’s most sought-after physicians.

No reliable total of his income, assets, debts, or estate was located. The evidence suggests professional success but also the substantial expense of a very large household, repeated bereavement, and philanthropic commitments. Claims that he “made a fortune” are not supported; later accounts of his son Paul’s limited means suggest that no large durable fortune passed through the family.

### Medical approach

Jung’s career preceded bacteriology, antiseptic surgery, modern anesthesia, and scientific psychiatry in their mature forms. His treatments necessarily belonged to early nineteenth-century medicine. No patient lawsuit, malpractice judgment, formal disciplinary proceeding, or documented medical fraud was found.

---

## Political and Civic Activity in Basel

Jung’s youthful liberal nationalism evolved into civic participation within Basel.

Documented or strongly reported offices include:

- work in the **Sanitätskollegium**, Basel’s medical governing body;
- membership of the **Grossrat** from **1838**;
- leadership in or membership of the governing board of the **Akademische Zunft** from **1841**;
- university rector and repeated faculty administrative service.

His integration is notable: within roughly two decades, a politically expelled German outsider became a Basel citizen, professor, hospital leader, legislator, guild officer, and member of a mayoral family.

No evidence was found that he resumed clandestine revolutionary activity after migration, supported political violence, or faced further prosecution in Switzerland.

---

## Freemasonry

Jung was initiated into the Basel lodge **Zur Freundschaft und Beständigkeit** in **1825**.

Institutional Masonic histories report that he served:

- as **Master of the Chair** of the Basel lodge from **1838 to 1845**;
- as a significant participant in the formation or consolidation of the **Swiss Grand Lodge Alpina**, founded in 1844;
- as **Grand Master of Alpina from 1850 to 1856**.

His Masonic activity was public enough to enter institutional histories and later biographies. It aligned with his liberal, associational, philanthropic, and educational commitments.

There is no evidence that his lodge activity constituted criminal conspiracy, occult fraud, political subversion, or an intelligence connection. Later occult and anti-Masonic writing sometimes projects the grandson’s symbolic psychology backward onto the grandfather. The grandfather’s membership and senior offices are fact; claims that this made him a secret Rosicrucian, “high initiate,” or architect of an occult bloodline are unsupported.

---

## The Anstalt “Zur Hoffnung”

Jung was the principal medical and philanthropic initiator of an institution for children then described with terms now regarded as obsolete, including “schwachsinnig” and “geistig zurückgeblieben.”

An early attempt encountered organizational difficulties. The durable foundation came in **1857**, when Jung and educator **Johann Böpple** rented premises on Grenzacherstrasse in Basel.

A frequently repeated foundation story says Jung placed a **five-franc coin** on the table as the first donation and challenged others to join him. Whether preserved in the earliest minutes or later institutional memory, the story accurately expresses his role as initiator, fundraiser, and patron.

The institution was named **Zur Hoffnung** (“To Hope” or “Hope”). It later moved to Riehen and evolved into a modern residential, educational, and therapeutic organization.

Historical analysis should distinguish:

- **documented philanthropy:** Jung materially and organizationally supported a specialized institution;
- **period practice:** nineteenth-century disability institutions could combine education and care with segregation and paternalism;
- **unsupported inference:** no evidence found in the consulted material establishes abuse by Jung, personal enrichment, or fraudulent use of the institution.

## Intellectual, Religious, and Literary Development

### Liberal nationalism

The dominant ideology of Jung’s student years was a blend of:

- constitutional freedom;
- opposition to arbitrary princely government;
- German cultural and political unity;
- student fraternity solidarity;
- Romantic patriotism.

His detention and expulsion intensified his identity as a victim of post-Napoleonic reaction.

### Protestant Romanticism

His conversion from Catholicism to Protestantism appears connected not merely to convenience but to a serious engagement with Protestant theology, Schleiermacher’s religious thought, and German Idealist/Romantic culture.

He subsequently integrated into Protestant Basel, though Freemasonry gave his religious identity a liberal-humanitarian rather than narrowly confessional form.

### Poetry and drama

Jung wrote poetry and drama as well as medicine. A later Burschenschaft historian called him a “forgotten poet from the early period of the Burschenschaft.”

His most identifiable literary publication is:

- *Die Revolution. Originaldrama in drei Acten*, published under **Demius** in Tübingen in 1831.

The work’s title and pseudonymous publication reflected his continuing engagement with revolution and the political experiences of his generation. A complete modern critical edition was not located.

### Scientific orientation

His surviving medical works focus on:

- the institutional requirements of medical teaching;
- anatomy’s foundational role;
- the anatomy of the cerebral ventricles;
- the fornix or “vault” of the human brain.

He was primarily an institution-builder and teacher rather than the originator of a major named medical discovery.

## Major Journeys and Geographical Movements

### Documented or strongly supported movements

- **1794/95:** born in Mannheim.
- **From 1813:** Heidelberg for medical study.
- **1817:** Wartburg for the Wartburg Festival; travel elsewhere in Germany followed his doctorate.
- **After 1816:** Berlin for surgical and ophthalmological training and medical employment.
- **1819–1820:** confined in Berlin’s Hausvogtei.
- **June 1821:** Paris.
- **March 1822:** Paris to Basel.
- **1822–1864:** Basel as permanent professional and civic base.
- **1844–1860:** journeys documented in surviving travel diaries in the Basel University Library.

The online catalogue identifies the travel diaries but does not supply a complete itinerary. It would be speculative to convert them into a city-by-city chronology without examining the manuscripts.

### Proposed emigration beyond Switzerland

Research on the network around **Karl Follen** and **Friedrich List** indicates that Jung considered or participated in discussions about overseas or alternative emigration schemes. He withdrew after resistance from family connections in Paris. He did not emigrate to the United States.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Alexander von Humboldt

Patron and recommender. Humboldt helped Jung pursue the Bern and Basel appointments. This is the best-documented prestigious relationship of Jung’s early career.

### Karl Ludwig Sand

Student-era associate or friend. Sand gave Jung the geological hammer later seized by police. No evidence implicates Jung in Sand’s assassination of Kotzebue.

### Friedrich Schleiermacher

Formative Protestant intellectual influence and probable network contact. Some family accounts attach Schleiermacher directly to Jung’s conversion. The precise documentary degree of personal intimacy remains uncertain.

### Jakob Friedrich Fries

Philosophical and theological influence associated with liberal Protestant and Burschenschaft culture.

### Georg Andreas Reimer

Berlin publisher whose household connected liberal intellectuals, Schleiermacher, and Humboldt. Later biographies regard Reimer as a probable intermediary in Jung’s access to Humboldt.

### Johann Nepomuk Rust and Guillaume Dupuytren

Medical figures associated respectively with Jung’s Berlin and Paris training. The exact formal titles Jung held under them differ among narratives.

### Johann Adam von Itzstein

A surviving letter dated **10 April 1841**, Mannheim, from liberal politician Johann Adam von Itzstein to Jung is catalogued in the Basel Nachlass. It documents Jung’s continuing connection to the southwestern German liberal network.

### Johann Böpple

Collaborator in founding and operating Zur Hoffnung in 1857.

### Basel political and academic elite

Jung worked with municipal officials, hospital boards, faculty colleagues, the Academic Guild, and the Grossrat. Marriage to Sophie Frey integrated him into Mayor Johann Rudolf Frey’s family.

### Family relationships

A son later described Jung as despotic within the household, while other recollections emphasized humor, warmth, generosity, music, and participation in his children’s games. Both may describe different aspects of a forceful nineteenth-century patriarch.

---

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

No commercial company, patent, investment syndicate, or industrial invention is securely associated with Jung.

His principal institutional projects were:

1. **University of Basel medical faculty reform**

   Curriculum expansion, preservation of the faculty, and closer linkage between theoretical and clinical medicine.

2. **Anatomical Cabinet/Anatomical Museum**

   Established in 1824 with municipal support; supplied with preparations, models, dissection facilities, and staff.

3. **Basel Bürgerspital modernization**

   Advocacy for enlarged, more modern hospital premises; long service as medical-department physician and senior physician.

4. **Clinical documentation**

   Probable responsibility for the systematic case-record culture visible in surviving hospital files.

5. **Zur Hoffnung**

   Philanthropic institution for children with intellectual and developmental disabilities, founded in durable form in 1857.

6. **Swiss Grand Lodge Alpina**

   Organizational and executive activity culminating in the grand mastership.

### Property and wealth

No verified inventory of Jung’s real property, investments, securities, or estate value was found. Available sources do not document bankruptcy, insolvency, embezzlement, secret business interests, or accumulation of major inherited wealth.

## Successes and Failures

### Significant successes

- Completed medical doctorate at a young age.
- Obtained advanced surgical and ophthalmological training.
- Survived political detention and rebuilt his career abroad.
- Secured a Basel professorship through merit, networking, and Humboldt’s patronage.
- Became a Basel citizen within two years.
- Helped restore a declining medical faculty.
- Founded the modern anatomical teaching collection.
- Served as university rector.
- Led the Bürgerspital’s medical department.
- Entered the Basel legislature and guild establishment.
- Became Grand Master of Swiss Freemasonry.
- Founded a durable disability-care institution.

### Significant failures and reversals

- Political activity destroyed his initial German academic career.
- A chemistry appointment in Bern did not materialize.
- Basel’s medical faculty remained financially fragile despite his reforms.
- An initial attempt to establish the children’s institution appears to have failed before the successful 1857 foundation.
- He suffered the deaths of three wives and at least seven children.
- His medical publications did not establish a lasting international research school under his name.
- His literary career largely disappeared from public memory.

---

## Illness, Bereavement, and Death

Jung experienced repeated domestic bereavement:

- death of Virginie de Lassaulx;
- death of Elisabeth Reyenthaler;
- death of Sophie Frey;
- death of seven of his thirteen children before 1864.

Later biographical summaries state that he died after a **short illness** on **12 June 1864** in Basel. A precise diagnosis or medically documented cause of death was not located in the accessible sources.

He was buried in Basel’s **Wolfgottesacker cemetery**.

No reliable evidence was found of addiction, chronic psychiatric illness, suicide attempt, disabling accident, or violent death.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### 1. Prussian investigation and imprisonment

**Status: documented arrest and detention; no established conviction.**

Jung was arrested on 7 July 1819 during the “demagogue persecution.” The material evidence included a geological hammer received from Karl Ludwig Sand. He was held for thirteen months, released without a court judgment, and expelled.

Later accounts calling this a “conviction” appear to confuse time served in investigative detention with a sentence.

### 2. Association with Karl Ludwig Sand

**Status: documented association; unsupported implication of complicity.**

Sand assassinated Kotzebue. Jung knew Sand and possessed his gift. No evidence found shows that Jung knew of the murder plan, assisted it, approved it in advance, or participated in political violence.

### 3. Burschenschaft radicalism

**Status: documented political activity; degree of revolutionary intent uncertain.**

Jung belonged to Teutonia and attended the Wartburg Festival. He advocated liberal and national unity ideals. Whether he privately favored republican revolution at particular moments is less certain. His drama *Die Revolution* shows continuing literary engagement with revolutionary questions but is not proof of criminal conspiracy.

### 4. Alleged illegitimate paternity by Goethe

**Status: family rumor/myth; contradicted by available chronology and documentary research.**

Discussed fully below. Franz Ignaz Jung remains the documented father.

### 5. Abrupt marriage proposal to a waitress

**Status: family legend built around a documented marriage.**

The marriage to Elisabeth Reyenthaler is factual. The claim that he proposed to the first waitress he encountered immediately after rejection by Sophie Frey is colorful but unverified.

### 6. Despotic father

**Status: reported family testimony.**

One son reportedly characterized him as despotic. Other family memories portray him as generous and playful. No independent evidence supports stronger accusations of domestic violence or abuse.

### 7. Freemasonry and alleged occult conspiracy

**Status: membership and leadership documented; conspiratorial extensions unsupported.**

He was a senior Freemason. Searches found no documentary basis for claims of Rosicrucian command, Illuminati membership, ritual criminality, political espionage, or an occult program transmitted to his grandson.

### 8. Institutional treatment of disabled children

**Status: institution documented; no specific abuse allegation found against Jung.**

Zur Hoffnung emerged from nineteenth-century custodial and educational practice. Modern disability history properly scrutinizes segregation, terminology, coercion, and power within such institutions. The consulted record does not identify a lawsuit, investigation, abuse finding, or financial misconduct involving Jung personally.

### 9. Medical ethics and anatomical collections

**Status: historical question; no individualized accusation established.**

Nineteenth-century anatomical collections depended on human remains acquired under legal and social regimes that gave the poor, prisoners, and hospital dead limited control. Jung expanded Basel’s anatomical collection and used dissection in teaching. The accessible museum history does not provide case-level provenance sufficient to determine consent or acquisition circumstances for every specimen. No specific grave-robbing, illegal procurement, or commercial trafficking charge against Jung was found.

### 10. Adversarial-search findings, including 2024–2026 material

Dedicated searches pairing his name with scandal, fraud, accusations, lawsuits, conspiracy, criticism, corruption, and related terms produced:

- repeated confusion with controversies involving his psychiatrist grandson;
- anti-Masonic or occult speculation;
- repetition of the Goethe-paternity legend;
- inaccurate descriptions of his 1819 detention as a criminal conviction;
- no newly documented lawsuit, financial fraud, sexual scandal, corruption case, malpractice finding, or criminal conviction;
- no credible new allegations published in the most recent two years concerning the elder Jung himself.

Absence from online search results is not proof that no local dispute ever existed, but no major omitted scandal emerged from the adversarial pass.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The Goethe paternity legend

#### What the story says

The story claims that Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, not Franz Ignaz Jung, fathered Karl Gustav Jung through an affair with Sophie Maria Josepha Ziegler.

#### Appearance and circulation

The claim survived as a Jung family legend and became widely known through biographies and the younger Jung’s posthumously edited *Memories, Dreams, Reflections*. The psychiatrist investigated and reflected upon it, reportedly regarding it as annoying while also recognizing its psychological appeal.

#### Purported evidence

Supporters cited:

- alleged connections between Sophie Ziegler and the Mannheim theatre;
- supposed friendship with a member of the Kestner/Buff circle;
- a perceived physical resemblance between the elder Jung and Goethe;
- the younger Jung’s deep identification with Goethe and *Faust*.

#### Evidence against

Modern examination identifies serious problems:

- Goethe was not in Mannheim during the relevant conception period;
- stories about Sophie Ziegler’s movements and contacts are unsupported;
- the frequently invoked “Lotte Kestner” relationships are genealogically confused;
- no letter, diary entry, parish record, financial record, or contemporary testimony connects Goethe sexually with Sophie Ziegler;
- the elder Jung’s own diary reportedly recorded only seeing Goethe in Weimar **from behind**.

#### Historical effect

The legend supplied the younger Jung with a symbolic literary ancestry and helped later writers frame analytical psychology as an extension of German Romanticism. It also displaced attention from the documented father, Franz Ignaz Jung.

**Assessment:** historically influential family myth; no credible evidence of biological paternity.

### Humboldt’s surprise banquet

One version says Jung unknowingly sat beside Humboldt at a Paris banquet, impressed him, and was unexpectedly offered the Basel position.

**Evidence:** Humboldt’s recommendation is documented; the dramatic banquet revelation is not. The appointment required applications and Basel institutional deliberation, not an instantaneous offer.

**Assessment:** embellished patronage story built around a documented relationship.

### “The hammer as weapon”

Later narratives emphasize the comedy of Prussian police treating a geological hammer as proof of revolutionary danger.

The hammer and detention are documented. Whether officials literally classified it as an “offensive weapon” in the precise language repeated by later writers requires consultation of the original investigation file.

**Assessment:** factual core with commemorative dramatization; symbol of arbitrary repression.

### The tavern proposal

Family legend says that after rejection by Sophie Frey or her father, Jung entered a tavern and proposed to the first waitress he saw, Elisabeth Reyenthaler, who accepted immediately.

The sequence of marriages and later marriage to Sophie Frey are documented. The impulsive “first woman he saw” scene is not independently established.

**Assessment:** family folklore expressing Jung’s impulsiveness, pride, social defiance, and upward integration.

### Heroic savior of Basel medicine

Commemorative accounts depict Jung as almost single-handedly saving the University of Basel medical faculty.

He was unquestionably central. Nevertheless, municipal politicians, hospital boards, colleagues, public funding, and later successors were necessary.

**Assessment:** strong factual foundation expanded into institutional hagiography.

### Masonic/occult inheritance

Some later writers imply a direct esoteric lineage from the grandfather’s Freemasonry to the grandson’s analytical psychology.

The grandfather’s Masonic career is documented. The grandson was not thereby automatically a Mason, and no evidence shows transmission of secret lodge doctrines from a man who died eleven years before the grandson was born.

**Assessment:** symbolic association and retrospective theory, not documented personal transmission.

## Works by Carl/Karl Gustav Jung the Elder

Because catalogues frequently merge the elder and younger Jungs, attribution requires particular caution.

### Authenticated or strongly attributed publications

1. **Medical doctoral dissertation**, Heidelberg, 1816.

   The exact title was not securely recovered from the consulted online records. Its existence is established through university and biographical evidence.

2. **Können in Basel die nöthigsten Hülfsanstalten zur Förderung medizinischer Studien gegründet werden?** Basel, 1823.

   Institutional reform pamphlet asking whether Basel could create the indispensable auxiliary institutions required for medical education.

3. **Über das Verhältniss der Anatomie zu der medicinischen Wissenschaft und über die Leistungen der Anatomen an der Baseler Hochschule: Rectoratsrede gehalten den 26. September 1828.** Basel, 1828.

   Rectoral address on anatomy’s relationship to medicine and the achievements of Basel anatomists.

4. **Die Revolution. Originaldrama in drei Acten.** Tübingen: Druck und Verlag von Ernst Traugott Eisert, 1831, 131 pages.

   Published under the pseudonym **Demius**. Literary rather than medical work.

5. **Über die seitliche Erhabenheit in dem Lateral-Ventrikel des menschlichen Gehirnes.** 1843.

   Anatomical paper on the lateral prominence in the human brain’s lateral ventricle.

6. **Über das Gewölbe in dem menschlichen Gehirne.** 1845.

   Anatomical work on the cerebral fornix.

### Manuscripts and archival works

7. **Reisetagebücher**, 1844–1860.

   Manuscript travel diaries preserved in the University Library Basel, Nachlass Carl Gustav Jung, UBH NL 34:5.

8. **Catalog der Sammlung für normale menschliche Anatomie.**

   Manuscript catalogue connected with Jung and later anatomist Wilhelm His; present catalogue dating is broad and indicates later continuation or revision.

9. **Correspondence.**

   The Basel Nachlass includes incoming and outgoing correspondence. A digitized example is Johann Adam von Itzstein’s letter to Jung, Mannheim, 10 April 1841, UBH NL 34:7,19.

10. **Clinical records and administrative papers.**

    Jung’s hospital work is represented indirectly in Bürgerspital case files and administrative records. These are institutional documents and should not automatically be treated as individually authored publications.

### Pseudonymous and uncertain work

- **Demius:** securely associated with *Die Revolution* in bibliographical records.
- **Mathias Nusser:** recorded as a pseudonym in German authority files, but the consulted catalogues did not identify specific titles that can confidently be assigned to Jung under this name.
- General statements that he published multiple plays or extensive poetry under “various pseudonyms” remain plausible but incompletely documented. They should not be converted into invented titles.

### Misattributed catalogue records

Automated library aggregators sometimes attach works by the psychiatrist Carl Gustav Jung—such as the *Zofingia Lectures* or twentieth-century psychological collections—to the elder physician’s authority record. These are cataloguing collisions, not works by the 1794/95–1864 subject.

## Death and Immediate Commemoration

Jung died in Basel on 12 June 1864 after what contemporary-derived summaries call a short illness.

Obituary tradition emphasized:

- his large physical presence;
- humor and charm;
- openness;
- generosity;
- energy in institutional affairs;
- endurance through repeated family deaths.

These character descriptions come from commemorative sources and should not be mistaken for neutral psychological assessment.

His immediate institutional legacy included:

- a functioning medical faculty;
- the anatomical collection;
- a modernized hospital structure;
- a disability-care institution;
- extensive clinical and administrative records;
- a prominent family embedded in Basel society.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Medical legacy

Jung is remembered less for a single discovery than for rebuilding the infrastructure of medical education in Basel. His most durable achievements were organizational:

- anatomical teaching;
- preservation and expansion of collections;
- clinical documentation;
- linkage of university and hospital;
- defense of the medical faculty during financial crisis.

### Anatomical Museum

The Anatomical Museum remains the clearest material continuation of his work. Later division and relocation of the collection—to pathology, the Vesalianum, zoology, and the present museum—changed its form but preserved the institutional lineage begun in 1824.

### Hospital history

The Bürgerspital case records provide modern historians with unusually rich evidence about urban patients. Jung’s reputation for systematic recordkeeping has therefore acquired new significance in social, epidemiological, and bioarchaeological research.

### Disability care

Zur Hoffnung survived and evolved through changing concepts of disability, education, residential care, and social inclusion. Its later history cannot be attributed entirely to Jung, but it preserves him as its founding medical patron.

### Urban memory

Basel officially named **Jungstrasse** after him in **1897**. The street commemorates the physician, not his later-famous grandson.

### Masonic memory

Swiss Masonic reference works remember him as a major nineteenth-century Grand Master and institutional builder.

### Family and Jungian memory

The worldwide fame of his grandson simultaneously preserved and distorted the elder Jung’s memory:

- it kept his name in biographies;
- it encouraged psychoanalytic interpretation of family anecdotes;
- it amplified the Goethe legend;
- it led catalogues to merge the two men’s publications;
- it sometimes turned an independently important Basel physician into a mere “ancestor of C. G. Jung.”

### Historical reinterpretation

Recent scholarship has shifted attention from heroic biography toward:

- political policing in Restoration Germany;
- refugee and patronage networks;
- institutional medical history;
- provenance of anatomical collections;
- social history of hospital patients;
- disability institutions;
- construction of Jung family mythology.

No comprehensive modern book-length scholarly biography devoted solely to the elder Jung was identified. Koelbing’s article, the Basel institutional histories, the Nachlass, and his son’s diary extracts remain the essential starting points.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by consulted sources are included. Mere contemporaneity has been excluded.

### Alexander von Humboldt

**Evidentiary label: documented fact.**

Jung met or entered Humboldt’s circle in Paris in 1821–1822. Humboldt recommended him first in connection with a possible Bern position and then for the Basel chair of anatomy, surgery, and midwifery. Letters examined by Koelbing establish Humboldt’s intervention. The relationship was patronage and scientific-professional sponsorship.

### Carl Gustav Jung

**Evidentiary label: documented family relationship; posthumous influence.**

The psychiatrist Carl Gustav Jung (1875–1961) was the elder Jung’s paternal grandson through Johann Paul Achilles Jung. They never met: the grandfather died in 1864, eleven years before the grandson’s birth. The younger Jung inherited his name, investigated his diaries, discussed the Goethe family legend, and incorporated the ancestral narrative into his autobiographical self-understanding.

### Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

**Evidentiary label: rumor or myth; one sighting reported.**

Family legend claimed Goethe was the elder Jung’s biological father. Documentary and chronological research weighs strongly against the story. The elder Jung’s diary reportedly says only that he saw Goethe in Weimar from behind. The myth nevertheless affected the younger Jung’s identification with Goethe and *Faust*.

### Friedrich Schiller

**Evidentiary label: no documented personal connection found.**

Schiller died while Jung was a child. Although Jung’s intellectual world was shaped by German Classicism and Romanticism, the consulted evidence does not document a direct connection.

### Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel

**Evidentiary label: no documented personal connection found.**

Jung lived and studied in Berlin during Hegel’s professorship, but no consulted source establishes attendance at Hegel’s lectures, correspondence, or a meeting. Shared presence in Berlin is insufficient.

### Friedrich Nietzsche

**Evidentiary label: no connection possible during Jung’s lifetime.**

Nietzsche was born in 1844 and was still a student when Jung died. No documented meeting or influence was found.

### Sigmund Freud

**Evidentiary label: no personal connection.**

Freud was eight years old when Jung died. The association exists only through Jung’s grandson.

### Samuel Preiswerk

**Evidentiary label: indirect family-network connection; direct personal contact not established.**

Preiswerk later became the maternal grandfather of the psychiatrist through the marriage of his daughter Emilie to Jung’s son Paul. Preiswerk and the elder Jung belonged to Basel’s learned Protestant world, but the consulted sources did not document a specific meeting or collaboration. No stronger claim is made.

### Other roster members

No securely documented meeting, correspondence, collaboration, rivalry, teacher-student relationship, family tie, patronage, or acknowledged influence involving other listed atlas figures was found. The limited number of connections is itself a finding.
