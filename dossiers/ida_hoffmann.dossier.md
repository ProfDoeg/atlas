# Ida Hofmann: Research Dossier

## Scope, Method, and Evidentiary Note

Ida Hofmann left no known consolidated personal archive. Her life is reconstructed principally from:

- her own publications, especially the retrospective *Monte Verità. Wahrheit ohne Dichtung*;
- surviving correspondence and institutional records associated with Monte Verità;
- contemporary publicity, press reports, and a September 1926 obituary;
- later scholarship on Lebensreform, natural healing, vegetarianism, modern dance, anarchism, and Monte Verità;
- fragmentary Portuguese-language research on her Brazilian colony, Monte Sol.

The Swiss period is comparatively well documented. Her childhood, court employment, finances, separation from Henri Oedenkoven, Brazilian activities, illness, and death contain unresolved points. Her autobiographical account is indispensable but partisan: it presents the founders’ disputes from Hofmann and Oedenkoven’s side and should not be treated as neutral institutional history.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a primary document, contemporary record, or unusually strong convergent evidence.
- **Reported fact:** stated in reputable secondary scholarship but not independently confirmed here from a primary record.
- **Disputed claim:** materially conflicting versions exist.
- **Allegation or theory:** asserted without sufficient documentary confirmation.
- **Rumor or myth:** folklore, propaganda, imaginative amplification, or demonstrably anachronistic association.

Hofmann and Oedenkoven deliberately rejected conventional marriage, and publisher Karl Rohm printed her name as “Ida Hofmann-Oedenkoven.” “Oedenkoven-Hofmann” also appeared in institutional publicity. It signified partnership and joint enterprise more than a documented legal marriage.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Raphael Hofmann (1829–1899), father — reported fact.** He was a mining engineer associated with the Banat mining and metallurgical region. He studied at the Bergakademie Freiberg, explaining Ida’s birth in that Saxon mining town. Later accounts also describe him as a singer and composer of Hungarian national songs. These descriptions are not incompatible: music appears to have been an important avocation alongside engineering.

**Luise Hofmann, née Orges, mother — reported fact.** She came from Braunschweig. Her dates have not been securely established in the consulted sources.

Several summaries describe the family as cultivated and reasonably affluent. Hofmann’s later access to capital, musical education, elite employment, and ability to travel support that general characterization, although no probate inventory or complete accounting of the family wealth was located.

### Claimed maternal connections

Later biographies connect Hofmann’s maternal family with historian and writer **Justus Möser** and geologist **Bernhard von Cotta**. The frequently repeated description of Möser as her “grandfather” is chronologically impossible: Möser died in 1794, seventy years before Hofmann’s birth. If the connection was genuine, he must have been a more remote ancestor. Von Cotta is described as a relative by marriage, sometimes “brother-in-law,” but the exact genealogical link remains unverified.

### Siblings

Four children are reported:

- **Justus Hofmann**, older brother. He apparently remained outside the Monte Verità project.
- **Eugenie Hofmann, called Jenny**, older sister, a trained singer or operatic singer. She joined the founding group and entered a non-state-sanctioned “reform marriage” with Karl Gräser.
- **Ida Hofmann**.
- **Julia Ferdinandine Hofmann, called Lilly or Lilli**, younger sister. She became associated with Monte Verità and later married Protestant pastor and writer **Friedrich Wilhelm Brepohl**. She emigrated to Brazil and lived at Lapa, Paraná.

A later oral-history-based dissertation says the brother remained permanently distant from the settlement while both sisters participated.

### Marriage, partnership, and children

Hofmann never entered a documented conventional marriage. From approximately 1899–1900 until 1918 she lived with **Henri Oedenkoven (1875–1935)**, eleven years her junior, in what they called a “reform marriage,” “vegetarian marriage,” or free union.

No children of Hofmann are documented.

During the First World War Oedenkoven became involved with **Isabelle Adderley**, an Englishwoman born in India in 1890. He married Adderley and had children with her; sources differ between one and three children. Hofmann and Oedenkoven nevertheless retained a personal and practical association, and she later traveled to Brazil with or near his new family.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1864–1879

Ida Hofmann was born on 5 October 1864 in Freiberg. Her father’s training and employment connected the household to the mining world of Saxony and the Banat. Although some later accounts call her “Transylvanian-born,” the more precise reconstruction is that she was **born in Saxony and grew up in or became closely identified with Transylvania**.

Details of her earliest residence are sparse. Her father’s career reportedly took the family into the Banat and Transylvanian cultural sphere. Hofmann grew up in a German-speaking, musically active household and later became a passionate Wagnerian. One secondary source reports that she spoke seven languages, but no contemporary curriculum vitae or examination record confirming the number has been located.

In **1879**, when Hofmann was about fifteen, the family moved to Vienna.

---

## Education and Formation

### Musical education in Vienna

Hofmann studied piano and music pedagogy under **Wilhelm Dörr** and **Julius Epstein**, the latter a prominent pianist and professor at the Conservatory of the Gesellschaft der Musikfreunde. She qualified as a professional music teacher and pianist.

Her education provided both employment and entry into elite households. Before going to Montenegro she taught piano in aristocratic Viennese families. A later notice says she was summoned to Vienna by a “Prince Batthyány” and became a celebrated member of society, but the patron’s exact identity and documentary basis are unclear.

### Wagner and the artistic ideal

Hofmann was an ardent admirer of Richard Wagner. Wagnerian ideas of total artwork, cultural regeneration, mythic landscape, and renewal through art formed part of the vocabulary later used at Monte Verità. The founders assigned Wagnerian or legendary names to features of the property, including places associated with Parsifal, the Valkyries, and Lorelei.

This did not make Monte Verità a Wagnerian institution in a formal sense. Wagner was one influence within a mixture of natural healing, vegetarianism, feminism, cooperative economics, anticlericalism, theosophy, and aesthetic reform.

### Employment in Montenegro

In the later 1890s Hofmann accepted a position in **Cetinje**, capital of the Principality of Montenegro. Accounts describe her as:

- a piano and music teacher;
- an educator at a school for girls of prominent families reportedly established under Russian patronage;
- a music teacher or governess to Montenegrin princesses;
- a figure connected through the Montenegrin court to Mecklenburg-Strelitz.

The broad fact of employment in Cetinje is well attested. The precise pupils and formal title are not. She was reportedly in her second year of Montenegrin service in 1899.

The reference to Mecklenburg-Strelitz may relate to **Duchess Jutta of Mecklenburg-Strelitz**, who married Crown Prince Danilo of Montenegro in Cetinje in July 1899, rather than proving that Hofmann had held a separate appointment at the Mecklenburg-Strelitz court.

## Crisis, Natural Healing, and the Meeting at Veldes, 1899

In 1899 Hofmann went to **Veldes**—now Bled, Slovenia—where her seriously ill father was staying or receiving treatment. She lodged at or visited **Arnold Rikli’s** natural-healing establishment at Mallnerbrunn.

Rikli, known as the “sun doctor” or heliopath, prescribed exposure to light and air, hydrotherapy, exercise, simple diet, and reduced clothing. His establishment became the immediate institutional model against which Monte Verità developed.

At Veldes Hofmann met:

- **Henri Oedenkoven**, son of a wealthy Antwerp industrial family, recovering from a serious illness;
- **Karl Gräser**, a Habsburg officer contemplating resignation from military service.

Hofmann later wrote that years of severe sorrow and “dull resignation” had darkened her life and that, like many women, she suffered under the pressure of the *Versorgungsfrage*: the expectation that a woman’s security depended on marriage or family support. A later study interprets this as depression or a psychological crisis. That diagnosis should be understood as a secondary characterization, not a clinical record.

Oedenkoven’s plans for a radically different life—health reform, vegetarianism, freedom from convention, and cooperative settlement—gave Hofmann a new practical direction. Their intimate relationship began around this time.

Her father died in 1899. Hofmann reportedly inherited money and later invested much of it in Monte Verità, though the amount is unknown.

---

## The Search for a Settlement, 1900

In 1900 the reformers regrouped at the Hofmann family apartment in **Munich-Schwabing**. The enlarged party included:

- Ida Hofmann;
- Henri Oedenkoven;
- Karl Gräser;
- Gustav “Gusto” Gräser, Karl’s brother;
- Jenny Hofmann;
- Lotte Hattemer;
- at some stages Ferdinand Brune, a theosophically inclined associate from Graz.

They sought land south of the Alps for a vegetarian cooperative. The group traveled, partly on foot, through **Oberammergau**, across the Alpine region, and into northern Italy and southern Switzerland. Accounts mention Cadenabbia, the Italian lake district, and the Lugano area.

Near Ascona they found land on **Monte Monescia**, approximately 320 metres above Lake Maggiore. They purchased property from **Alfredo Pioda**, a Ticinese politician and theosophically interested landowner.

The acquisition date is variously given as autumn 1900 or early 1901. The initial parcel is sometimes described as roughly three hectares; later sales material described a combined estate of approximately **94,000 square metres**. These figures probably refer to different phases of acquisition.

Oedenkoven paid most of the original price. Ida and Jenny Hofmann also contributed capital. No surviving complete partnership agreement was located.

They renamed the hill **Monte Verità**, “Mountain of Truth.” Hofmann later explained that the name did not claim possession of truth; it announced an intention to live truthfully in speech and action against commercial deception and social convention.

## Founding Principles

The project passed through several descriptions:

1. vegetarian or “vegetabile” settlement;
2. “vegetabile individualistic cooperative”;
3. natural-healing establishment;
4. *Sonnen-Kuranstalt* or sun-cure institution;
5. Sanatorium Monte Verità;
6. commercial pension, hotel, and cultural venue.

The founders proposed:

- a plant-centred diet;
- abstinence from alcohol, tobacco, coffee, strong tea, and other stimulants;
- simple, loose clothing and sandals;
- light, air, sun, water, mud, exercise, and gardening as therapies;
- opposition to corsets and restrictive dress;
- sexual and marital autonomy;
- equality or equal opportunity between women and men;
- individual freedom within cooperative arrangements;
- internationalism and pacifist tendencies;
- simplified or phonetic spelling;
- intellectual discussion and artistic practice.

Their positions were not uniform. The Gräser brothers favored radical simplicity, manual labor, primitivism, non-possession, and opposition to machinery. Hofmann and Oedenkoven accepted modern technology if it reduced human or animal labor and tried to build a financially viable institution.

---

## Breakdown of the Founding Group, 1901–1902

Conflict emerged almost immediately over property, work, authority, machinery, money, and the meaning of community.

### Gusto Gräser’s exclusion

**Documented core, disputed interpretation:** In 1901 Hofmann and Oedenkoven caused or supported Gusto Gräser’s exclusion from the core enterprise. In her later chronicle Hofmann called him an “absonderliche Erscheinung,” an odd or peculiar apparition, and claimed he was unsuitable for the undertaking and merely tolerated.

Supporters of the Gräsers argue that Gusto was a principal spiritual originator and that Hofmann and Oedenkoven later wrote him out of the institutional story. Hofmann’s own account gives Oedenkoven a dominant creative role and minimizes Gusto.

### Karl Gräser and Jenny Hofmann

Karl Gräser and Jenny Hofmann also separated from the central operation. They obtained nearby land and lived in a rudimentary vineyard hut, deliberately rejecting the increasingly organized sanatorium. Their mode of life was described, sometimes admiringly and sometimes mockingly, as “prehistoric.”

One concrete dispute concerned Karl’s donkey. Hofmann, holding that machinery was preferable to exploiting draft animals, reportedly insisted that Karl and Jenny take the animal when they left.

### Two competing models

The quarrel was not merely personal:

| Hofmann–Oedenkoven model | Gräser model |
|---|---|
| Organized health institution | Decentralized settlement |
| Paying guests | Voluntary simplicity |
| Administrative leadership | Minimal authority |
| Selective use of machinery | Rejection of machinery |
| Buildings and professional services | Primitive shelter and manual labor |
| Reform of modernity | Withdrawal from industrial modernity |

The split generated a historiographical conflict that continues. Institutional histories often center Hofmann and Oedenkoven; Gräser-oriented accounts describe their narrative as monopolizing the name and legacy of Monte Verità.

## Feminist Writing and Sexual Reform, 1902

In 1902 Hofmann published:

*Wie gelangen wir Frauen zu harmonischen und gesunden Daseinsbedingungen? Offener Brief an die Verfasserin von “Eine Mutter für Viele” [Christine Thaler].*

It responded to the controversial book *Eine für Viele*, a set of fictional diary writings published under the name **Vera** and associated with Christine Thaler. Hofmann defended the author and used the controversy to discuss women’s health, sexuality, marriage, education, and social dependence.

Her program joined feminism to natural healing:

- young people should not be forced to suppress sexuality indefinitely;
- marriage should not function primarily as female economic provision;
- women required education, meaningful work, bodily autonomy, and access to knowledge;
- conventional clothing damaged women’s bodies;
- children should receive a more natural education;
- coeducation and wider professional opportunities were desirable;
- marital property rules and divorce laws required reform.

Some arguments were radical for their context; others were biologically essentialist. Hofmann often treated motherhood, heterosexual union, and reproductive health as natural destinies. A recent academic study argues that her work oscillated between concrete emancipatory demands and the reconstruction of fixed ideas about female nature.

Her discussions of masturbation, hysteria, “impure” sexual thought, hereditary degeneration, and “natural instinct” belong to turn-of-the-century sexual-hygiene discourse. They should not be translated uncritically into present medical categories.

## Development of the Sanatorium, 1901–1910

Construction began with small wooden **Lichtlufthütten**—light-and-air cabins—designed for simple individual living. Later development included:

- a central house with dining room, music room, and library;
- Casa Anatta, the founders’ residence;
- guest houses and three larger residences;
- approximately eleven light-and-air cabins;
- utility buildings;
- open-air showers;
- separate sunbathing areas;
- mud-bath and water-treatment installations;
- a glass-roofed winter sunbathing facility;
- gymnastic equipment;
- a swimming pool;
- gardens, orchards, and cultivated fields;
- a tennis court;
- Villa Semiramis, built around 1909.

A later sale advertisement described an estate of about 94,000 square metres. One scholarly reconstruction states that the sanatorium accommodated at most thirty-six guests.

### Diet

The early food included bread, butter, milk, nuts, dried and fresh fruit, and simple cooked meals. Under the influence of Dutch reformer **Joseph Salomonson**, who arrived around 1903, Hofmann and the institution moved toward stricter exclusion of eggs, milk products, wool, and other animal products.

The modern word “vegan” had not yet been coined. “Strict plant-based” or Hofmann’s *vegetabilisch* is historically safer.

Salt, alcohol, tobacco, coffee, tea, meat, and stimulating spices were prohibited at various times. Guests did not always obey. Later recollections describe visitors secretly eating salami, cheese, and drinking Ticinese wine in Ascona. Rules gradually relaxed, and meat eventually appeared at the establishment.

### Therapy and daily life

Treatments included:

- exposure to sunlight and open air;
- water and mud applications;
- gymnastics and sport;
- mountain walking;
- gardening and work therapy;
- dietary regulation;
- music, reading, lectures, discussion, visual arts, and dance.

The enterprise advertised itself principally to exhausted urban people seeking recovery, not necessarily to seriously ill patients. At periods it employed a physician and nurse. Oedenkoven offered daily consultations on hygiene and diet despite lacking a standard medical qualification.

### Hofmann’s practical role

Hofmann’s activities included administration, publicity, teaching, writing, cultural programming, guest relations, and music. Contemporary and later descriptions call her the intellectual “head” or “mind” of the colony. Such formulas risk undercounting Oedenkoven’s capital and administration, the labor of employees, and the Gräsers’ formative contribution.

## Vegetarianism and Natural Healing Publications, 1905–1906

### *Vegetabilismus! Vegetarismus!* (1905)

Hofmann distinguished between:

- **Vegetabilismus:** a plant-based diet;
- **Vegetarismus:** a complete vigorous, joyful, natural manner of living, derived—according to her—from Latin *vegetus*.

The pamphlet treated diet as ethical, hygienic, social, and cultural reform. It rejected the idea that vegetarianism merely meant abstention from meat.

Some health claims were experiential or ideological rather than products of controlled medical research. Hofmann did not invent a patented treatment or technology, and no patents have been found.

### *Monte Verità. Wahrheit ohne Dichtung* (1906)

Her principal autobiographical work narrated the origins and first years of Monte Verità. It combined:

- chronological memoir;
- defense of the sanatorium;
- founder mythology;
- sketches of visitors and associates;
- polemic against opponents, anarchists, sensational journalists, and “nature-person” stereotypes;
- statements on religion, society, health, and sexuality.

The title—“Truth without Poetry/Fiction”—itself made a claim to documentary authority. The book remains a primary source for events Hofmann witnessed but is also an institutional apologia.

She sometimes used **reform orthography**, following a “write as you speak” principle associated with Oedenkoven.

## Visitors, Networks, and Cultural Milieu

Monte Verità became a node connecting several overlapping milieus:

- Lebensreform;
- natural medicine;
- vegetarianism;
- theosophy and occultism;
- anarchism and socialism;
- pacifism and wartime exile;
- psychoanalysis and sexual reform;
- expressive dance;
- literary and artistic modernism.

Persons associated with the hill or surrounding Ascona scene included Erich Mühsam, Hermann Hesse, Otto Gross, Raphael Friedeberg, Max Weber, Franziska zu Reventlow, Rudolf von Laban, Mary Wigman, Suzanne Perrottet, Isadora Duncan, Hans Arp, Sophie Taeuber, Marianne von Werefkin, and many others.

Association with Monte Verità does not by itself prove a personal friendship with Hofmann. Some prominent visitors were connected principally to neighboring properties, the Gräser circle, Laban’s school, or Ascona’s wider expatriate community.

### Erich Mühsam

Mühsam initially stayed at or participated in the Oedenkoven establishment but sharply criticized its commercial and disciplinary character. He called it a capitalist enterprise and preferred the more anarchic outsiders. His 1905 *Ascona* helped fix the public image of eccentric naturists and anarchists, which Hofmann’s 1906 book tried to correct.

### Hermann Hesse

Hesse visited the Monte Verità milieu and underwent treatment associated with alcohol withdrawal and natural healing. His presence is documented, but he later wrote that he shared little spiritually with many sanatorium “seekers.” Claims that Hofmann directly caused his later literary development are interpretive, not documented personal influence.

### Otto Gross and Lotte Hattemer

Psychoanalyst and anarchist **Otto Gross** moved within the Ascona circle. Founder **Lotte Hattemer**, who was psychologically distressed and practiced extreme asceticism, died in 1906 after taking poison supplied by Gross. This death became part of Monte Verità’s scandal history.

No evidence found implicates Hofmann in supplying the poison or causing Hattemer’s death. Later narratives sometimes merge the event with the sanatorium’s institutional history simply because Hattemer had been a founder.

### Rudolf von Laban and expressive dance

Laban established summer courses and a school for movement art at Monte Verità from about 1913. Mary Wigman, Suzanne Perrottet, and other dancers participated. Hofmann’s admiration for Isadora Duncan and interest in freeing the body helped make dance congenial to the site, but Laban operated with considerable independence.

A 1917 sun festival and occult congress produced tensions between the sanatorium’s proprietors, Laban’s group, and Theodor Reuss.

---

## Travel and Contacts Beyond Ascona

Hofmann and Oedenkoven studied other reform and natural-healing establishments. Sources report visits or contact with:

- Arnold Rikli at Veldes;
- Adolf Just’s Jungborn;
- Louis Kuhne’s natural-healing practice;
- magnetists and prayer-healing circles influenced by Mary Baker Eddy;
- other European sanatoria.

In early summer **1908**, Hofmann and Oedenkoven visited artist and Lebensreform prophet **Karl Wilhelm Diefenbach** on Capri. Hofmann later sent him her Monte Verità book.

Her relationship to theosophy deepened. She wrote the undated Italian-language—or Italian-titled—tract *L’Importanza della Teosofia vera* (“The Importance of True Theosophy”).

## Financial and Organizational Structure

### Capital

Monte Verità was not a moneyless commune:

- Oedenkoven supplied the largest share of the purchase money.
- Ida and Jenny Hofmann contributed funds.
- Oedenkoven’s parents later provided substantial financial assistance.
- Paying guests financed ongoing operation.
- Land, buildings, treatment services, and publicity formed an increasingly conventional enterprise.

One later account says Oedenkoven’s mother disliked the project and threatened to withdraw support, hastening conversion from a cooperative settlement into a sanatorium.

### Labor and class

The founders rhetorically rejected bourgeois convention, yet depended on inherited wealth, guest payments, employees, professional services, and property ownership. Manual labor was unevenly distributed. Critics accused the institution of reproducing hierarchy while selling an image of liberation.

### From cooperative to business

A useful institutional sequence is:

- communal settlement;
- “individualistic cooperative”;
- health pension;
- sanatorium and hotel;
- leased establishment;
- property offered for sale.

This evolution was both a success and a failure: it created durable buildings and international visibility but abandoned much of the original cooperative ideal.

### No patents or identifiable formal corporate holdings

No patents, registered inventions, stock portfolio, or diversified business empire attributable to Hofmann were located. Her principal property and financial interest was the Monte Verità estate and later the Monte Sol land.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Feminism

Hofmann advocated women’s education, economic independence, reform of marriage and property law, freer divorce, bodily autonomy, dress reform, and public discussion of sexuality.

Her feminism was individualist and hygienic rather than party-organized. No candidacy, party office, or suffrage-organization leadership has been found.

### Anarchism

She lived among anarchists and shared opposition to state, church, militarism, and bourgeois marriage, but she explicitly criticized doctrinaire anarchism and materialist revolutionary politics. Labeling her simply an “anarchist” obscures her preference for personal transformation, natural healing, and controlled institutional practice.

### Technology

Unlike the Gräsers, Hofmann did not reject machinery. She argued that technology should relieve exhausting human labor and reduce exploitation of animals. Her hostility to draft-animal labor helps explain the donkey dispute.

### Religion and theosophy

Monte Verità was religiously plural. Hofmann rejected conventional dogma but pursued a spiritualized idea of truth and ethical self-reformation. She became interested in theosophy and distinguished “true theosophy” from what she regarded as distortion.

### Occult-Masonic activity

A surviving report reproduced in the Gusto Gräser archive states that in April 1919 Hofmann participated with **Theodor Reuss**, head of the Ordo Templi Orientis, and was identified with high-degree numbers “33, 90, 95, IX°.” This is documentary evidence of involvement in Reuss’s esoteric organizational milieu.

It does not establish criminal conspiracy, occult political control, or the later sensational claims attached to the O.T.O.

## The First World War and Decline, 1910–1920

The sanatorium was leased in **1910**. In 1912–1913 it was medically directed by Dresden physician **Anna Fischer-Dückelmann**.

The First World War sharply reduced international travel and guest numbers. At the same time Monte Verità and Ascona attracted pacifists, deserters, expatriates, artists, and refugees. Laban’s school gave the site renewed artistic significance but also created organizational conflict.

Hofmann and Oedenkoven’s personal union ended around **1918**, after Oedenkoven’s relationship with Isabelle Adderley. Sources describe the separation as amicable or at least followed by continued friendship. Hofmann nevertheless lost her position as his exclusive companion and institutional partner.

The sanatorium’s institutional momentum had weakened by 1918–1920. Hofmann’s *Beiträge zur Frauenfrage* appeared around 1920, returning to the subject that had first made her a public writer.

The property’s disposition is described inconsistently:

- one account says the sanatorium was sold in 1920;
- others say it was offered for sale in 1922 and managed as a hotel by Werner Ackermann, Max Bethke, and Hugo Wilkens between 1923 and 1926;
- the estate was ultimately acquired by banker and art collector **Eduard von der Heydt** in 1926 or 1927.

The apparent reconciliation is that Hofmann and Oedenkoven relinquished operational control around 1920, while final transfer of the property took several years.

---

## Spain and Emigration to Brazil, circa 1920–1923

One secondary reconstruction says Hofmann went first to **Spain** in 1920. Details of the Spanish residence, its length, and purpose remain unclear.

She then emigrated to Brazil, associated with Oedenkoven, Isabelle Adderley, and their children. Accounts differ on whether they traveled together continuously or reunited there.

The aim was to establish another community, **Monte Sol**, in the Brazilian south.

---

## Monte Sol, Palmital, Brazil

### Location and property

Portuguese-language research places Monte Sol at **Palmital**, then in the Joinville region of Santa Catarina and now associated with the municipality of Garuva, roughly thirty to forty kilometres from Joinville.

Hofmann reportedly bought approximately **120 hectares** from Empresa Industrial Agrícola Palmital Ltda., a company founded in 1919 whose administration was in Joinville.

The precise deed has not been reproduced in the sources consulted, although later heritage research treats the acquisition as historical fact.

### Associates

Hofmann corresponded with **Albert Raymond Costet de Mascheville**, esoteric name **Cedaior**, a musician, Martinist, and occult organizer then in Argentina. Cedaior came to Brazil in 1923 to help establish the colony.

Other associated figures included:

- **Emma/Peregrina Costet de Mascheville**, described as Hofmann’s niece and a pianist;
- **Leo Alvarez Costet de Mascheville**, also known as Jehel or Swami Sevananda;
- a young German identified as **Tuitenhof**, reportedly assigned to supervise rice planting and timber work;
- Henri Oedenkoven and his family, at least during part of the Brazilian period.

The exact kinship by which Emma was Hofmann’s niece is not securely established.

### Economy and failure

The settlers attempted rice cultivation, timber cutting, teaching, and musical employment. Esoteric movement histories say Cedaior and Peregrina gave lessons and played in café orchestras to support the settlement.

Monte Sol failed by approximately **1924–1925**. Reported causes were:

- difficult access;
- rough terrain;
- subtropical heat;
- mosquitoes;
- malaria;
- weak agricultural supervision;
- insufficient working capital;
- small numbers and the founders’ age and health.

One later account says Hofmann had been deceived into buying swampy land and invested the remainder of her fortune there. This is a **reported allegation**, not a proven fraud: no seller’s prosecution, civil judgment, or contemporary investigative record has been located.

After Palmital, the group dispersed toward Paraná and elsewhere.

---

## Later Life, Illness, and Death

Hofmann reportedly contracted **malaria** during the Monte Sol period. Later accounts describe a serious or prolonged illness and unsuccessful consultations with specialists. The exact diagnosis and whether malaria was the immediate or underlying cause of death are not established.

She worked briefly in **radio**, but the station, city, position, and dates remain unidentified.

In poor health she stayed with her younger sister Lilly and Lilly’s husband, Friedrich Wilhelm Brepohl, in **Lapa, Paraná**. She later went to São Paulo seeking medical help.

### Death-date dispute

The strongest reconstruction is:

- **Death:** 12 July 1926, São Paulo.
- **Contemporary notice:** *Die Südschweiz*, 15 September 1926, announced her death.
- **Modern authority usage:** 1864–1926.
- **Competing version:** 4 July 1930, São Paulo, in Julia Schiff’s 1998 account and a later dissertation following it.

The existence of a 1926 obituary makes a 1930 death very unlikely unless the newspaper notice has been misidentified. No civil death certificate was found in the accessible sources, so 12 July 1926 should be described as best supported rather than absolutely proven.

Hofmann was sixty-one if she died on 12 July 1926, not sixty-two as some accounts state.

No reliable evidence establishes her burial place or the precise certified cause of death.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Henri Oedenkoven

**Documented fact.** Partner, co-founder, principal financial collaborator, and co-administrator from 1899/1900 to roughly 1918. Their relationship joined intimacy, finance, property, ideology, and business. After his marriage to Isabelle Adderley, Hofmann remained connected to him and participated in the Brazilian migration.

### Jenny Hofmann and Karl Gräser

**Documented fact.** Sister and co-founder; Karl was a founding associate and Jenny’s partner. Their withdrawal exposed the split between institutional and primitivist approaches.

### Gusto Gräser

**Documented conflict.** Co-founder or early participant, subsequently excluded at Hofmann and Oedenkoven’s initiative. Hofmann portrayed him as unsuitable; his defenders accuse her of minimizing his foundational importance.

### Lotte Hattemer

**Documented association.** Fellow founder and early companion. Hattemer later separated from the institutional group and died by poison in 1906.

### Arnold Rikli

**Documented formative influence.** His Veldes natural-healing establishment was the site where Hofmann, Oedenkoven, and Karl Gräser met and a model for light-and-air therapy.

### Joseph Salomonson

**Reported direct influence.** Dutch reformer who visited in 1903 and persuaded Hofmann toward strict avoidance of animal foods and products.

### Karl Wilhelm Diefenbach

**Documented visit/correspondence.** Hofmann and Oedenkoven visited him on Capri in 1908; Hofmann subsequently sent him her book.

### Rudolf von Laban

**Documented institutional relationship.** Ran movement-art summer schools at Monte Verità from 1913. Their relationship included cooperation and later tension over the 1917 festival and the role of occult organizations.

### Theodor Reuss

**Documented organizational association.** O.T.O. leader who held a 1917 congress at Monte Verità; a 1919 notice lists Hofmann as assisting him in degree work.

### Albert “Cedaior” Costet de Mascheville

**Reported and partly document-supported relationship.** Correspondent and collaborator in establishing Monte Sol in Brazil.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### Commercialization of a utopian settlement

**Documented controversy.** Anarchists and independent settlers accused Hofmann and Oedenkoven of turning a cooperative ideal into a commercial sanatorium for paying bourgeois guests. Erich Mühsam called it a capitalist enterprise.

**Evidence:** fee-paying operation, concentrated property ownership, employees, advertising, expansion, and family capital.

**Counter-position:** the sanatorium provided the income and infrastructure necessary for the experiment to survive and attracted the public that made Monte Verità influential.

No fraud or illegal commercial practice has been demonstrated.

### Exclusion and historiographical control

**Documented conflict; disputed interpretation.** Hofmann and Oedenkoven excluded Gusto Gräser and later marginalized the Gräsers’ contribution in their account. Gräser-oriented historians accuse them of attempting to monopolize both the property and the name “Monte Verità.”

Hofmann’s language about Gusto could be hostile, but the characterization of her writings as “hate tirades” comes from a partisan Gräser memorial archive and is an interpretation, not a neutral archival finding.

### Medical claims and unlicensed authority

**Documented practice; no located prosecution.** Monte Verità marketed natural treatments and dietary cures. Oedenkoven offered hygiene consultations without conventional medical credentials. Hofmann promoted health claims grounded in contemporary natural-healing belief.

No lawsuit, conviction, regulatory investigation, or proven patient injury involving Hofmann was found.

### Hattemer’s death

**Documented death; Hofmann’s culpability unsupported.** Lotte Hattemer died after obtaining poison from Otto Gross. The event contributed to the hill’s scandalous reputation. No source located establishes that Hofmann participated in the act.

### Occult organizations

**Documented affiliation, later sensationalized.** Hofmann participated in Theodor Reuss’s esoteric network. Later occult literature places her within elaborate Rosicrucian, Martinist, Masonic, and O.T.O. genealogies.

Membership claims based on contemporary degree notices are evidence of organizational involvement. Claims of hidden political control, sexual-magical conspiracy, or institutional continuity between Monte Verità, Monte Sol, and later occult orders are not established.

### The Brazilian land purchase

**Unresolved financial question.** Hofmann reportedly spent her remaining capital on unsuitable, mosquito-infested land. The claim that she was “conned” appears in later biography, but no contract litigation, criminal complaint, or court determination was located.

### Dedicated adversarial-search result

Searches in English, German, Italian, and Portuguese using Hofmann’s name with equivalents of controversy, scandal, accusation, lawsuit, fraud, criminality, and conspiracy—including 2024–2026 results—found:

- strong criticism over commercialization and exclusion of the Gräsers;
- polemical accusations that her self-history distorted Monte Verità’s origins;
- unresolved allegations concerning the Brazilian land;
- criticism of natural-healing and essentialist sexual-health theories;
- occult affiliation and later conspiracy narratives.

They did **not** reveal a documented criminal charge, civil judgment, formal fraud investigation, political-violence case, intelligence connection, money-laundering allegation, imprisonment, or conviction attributable to Ida Hofmann.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Founder of the first hippie commune”

**Story:** Hofmann and her companions founded a hippie commune decades before the hippies.

**Appearance and circulation:** post-1960s journalism, tourism promotion, documentaries, and museum publicity.

**Evidence:** Monte Verità did anticipate later countercultural practices—communal living, nudism, plant-based eating, free relationships, ecological building, alternative medicine.

**Against:** “hippie” is an anachronistic category; the project arose from German-speaking Lebensreform, theosophy, bourgeois reform, and sanatorium culture.

**Effect:** greatly increased popular recognition while flattening ideological differences among founders.

### “The whole European avant-garde lived there”

**Story:** virtually every modernist, anarchist, dancer, occultist, and psychologist resided at Hofmann’s colony.

**Evidence:** many important figures did visit, stay, work, or own property in the Ascona–Monte Verità region.

**Against:** later lists merge paying sanatorium guests, nearby residents, post-Hofmann visitors, Eranos participants, and people with no verified presence. Some names—Bakunin, Trotsky, Kafka, James Joyce, Carl Jung during Hofmann’s proprietorship—are frequently included without adequate evidence of direct contact with Hofmann.

**Effect:** transformed a complex geographical milieu into a single legendary commune.

### Hofmann as sole “inventor” or “mind” of Monte Verità

**Story:** Hofmann conceived and intellectually directed the whole project.

**Evidence:** she was a founder, writer, publicist, administrator, and major investor.

**Against:** Oedenkoven supplied major capital and organizational leadership; Karl and Gusto Gräser supplied important communitarian and ascetic ideas; Jenny Hofmann, Lotte Hattemer, workers, guests, and local contacts also shaped the settlement.

**Effect:** recent rediscovery has corrected earlier neglect of Hofmann but can reproduce the founder-monopoly criticized in her own narrative.

### Oedenkoven as sole founder

The opposite legend makes the wealthy male investor the decisive founder and Hofmann merely his companion. Her publications, financing, feminist program, and administrative role contradict this.

### “Free love” as unrestricted sexual libertinism

Monte Verità’s rejection of church and state marriage is frequently converted into stories of continuous promiscuity or organized sexual ritual. Relationships outside marriage occurred, and Otto Gross advocated radical sexual liberation. Hofmann herself discussed sexual autonomy. Evidence does not show that her sanatorium maintained an institutional program of compulsory or ritualized promiscuity.

### The magnetic Lorelei/Corelai rock

Later accounts describe a rock named by Hofmann as the site of exceptional magnetic or spiritual energy. The naming is consistent with her Wagnerian symbolism. Claims of measurable unusual energy remain **legend or spiritual belief**, not established physical fact.

### Monte Verità as direct parent of Eranos

Some recent writing says Hofmann founded Eranos. This is false if taken literally: **Olga Fröbe-Kapteyn founded the Eranos conferences in 1933**, seven years after Hofmann’s death, at Casa Gabriella near Ascona. Monte Verità and the earlier Ascona spiritual milieu formed part of the local cultural prehistory; Hofmann did not found the institution.

### Brazilian occult continuity

Martinist and Rosicrucian histories portray Monte Sol as a decisive bridge from European avant-garde occultism to Brazilian esoteric orders. Hofmann’s collaboration with Cedaior supports a genuine bridge. Claims of an unbroken secret lineage or unified order should be labeled internal tradition rather than independently verified history.

### Incorrect aristocratic origin

Some esoteric sources call Hofmann the daughter of a Transylvanian baroness or wealthy Hungarian mine owner. Better-supported accounts identify her parents as mining engineer Raphael Hofmann and Luise Orges. No reliable evidence of a baronial title has been found.

## Works by Ida Hofmann

### Authenticated books and pamphlets

1. ** *Wie gelangen wir Frauen zu harmonischen und gesunden Daseinsbedingungen? Offener Brief an die Verfasserin von “Eine Mutter für Viele” [Christine Thaler]* **. Ascona, Lago Maggiore: self-published; distributed by Reformverlag C. von Schmidtz, Haimhausen, **1902**.  
   Genuine authorship. Feminist, sexual-hygiene, natural-healing, and educational polemic.

2. ** *Blätter zur Verbreitung vegetarischer Lebensweise. Vegetabilismus! Vegetarismus!* ** Monte Verità bei Ascona: self-published by Ida Hofmann-Oedenkoven; printed by El. Em. Colombi & Co., Bellinzona, **1905**. Thirty-one pages.  
   Genuine authorship. A digitized public-domain copy survives.

3. ** *Monte Verità. Wahrheit ohne Dichtung. Aus dem Leben erzählt* **. Lorch, Württemberg: Druck und Verlag von Karl Rohm, **1906**.  
   Genuine authorship, published as Ida Hofmann-Oedenkoven. Autobiographical and institutional chronicle.

4. ** *Beiträge zur Frauenfrage* **. Winnenden: Zentralstelle zur Verbreitung guter deutscher Literatur, **circa 1920**.  
   Genuine attribution; exact publication date requires bibliographical confirmation.

5. ** *L’Importanza della Teosofia vera* **. No place or date identified.  
   Attributed to Hofmann in specialist bibliographies. A complete verified edition was not located.

### Joint or institutional publications

6. **Prospectus of the “Vegetabilische Gesellschaft des Monte Verità,” circa 1905.**  
   Reported as prepared by Henri Oedenkoven and Ida Hofmann. Institutional/joint authorship.

7. **Sanatorium Monte Verità prospectuses and advertisements, circa 1903–1910.**  
   Hofmann contributed to the institution’s publicity, but precise individual authorship of every text is not established.

### Correspondence and archival writings

- Letters to Fritz Brupbacher and other reform figures survive in scattered archives or reproductions.
- Correspondence with Karl Wilhelm Diefenbach is reported.
- A letter to Cedaior concerning the Brazilian settlement is quoted in esoteric movement histories.
- No complete critical edition of her correspondence has been published.
- No diary or consolidated Nachlass is presently known.

### Modern editions and translations

- *Monte Verità. Verità senza poesia*, Italian translation/edition, Bellinzona: Edizioni Casagrande, **2023**, with a preface by Edgardo Franzosini and afterword by Nicoletta Mongini.
- *Monte Verità. Vérité sans poésie*, French edition, Éditions de la Baconnière, **2025**.

These are translations or modern editions of the 1906 work, not newly discovered separate compositions.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Hofmann’s reputation passed through several phases.

### 1926–1960s: eclipse behind the place

After her death, Monte Verità survived as a geographical and cultural name. Eduard von der Heydt transformed the property into a modern hotel and art-oriented venue. Hofmann became one founder among many and was often subordinated to Oedenkoven, famous visitors, or the later Eranos circle.

### 1970s: Szeemann and countercultural rediscovery

Harald Szeemann’s 1978 exhibition recast Monte Verità as a genealogy of alternative modernity. Hofmann re-emerged as a founder of ecological living, feminism, body reform, vegetarianism, and communal experimentation.

This rediscovery also encouraged mythic compression: distinct movements and decades were assembled into a single “Monte Verità” constellation.

### Feminist reassessment

Later researchers emphasized that Hofmann was not merely Oedenkoven’s companion. She supplied money, writing, administration, ideological definition, and public defense. Her feminist work is now examined both for emancipatory demands and for its naturalized conceptions of sex and motherhood.

### Vegetarian and vegan history

Her 1905 pamphlet has made her a significant figure in histories of European vegetarianism and precursors of veganism. Her “vegetabilism” extended beyond diet to clothing, animal labor, and daily practice.

### Architecture, ecology, and body culture

The surviving light-and-air cabins, Casa Anatta, landscape design, and treatment installations are interpreted as early ecological or reform architecture. Monte Verità’s integration of housing, diet, exercise, sun, nudity, art, and collective discussion anticipated later holistic-health institutions.

### Dance and modernism

Hofmann created or maintained the institutional environment in which Laban’s movement school could work, though she did not originate Laban’s choreographic system. Monte Verità became a major site in the genealogy of Ausdruckstanz.

### Centenary reassessment, 2023–2026

The Italian edition of her memoir in 2023 and French edition in 2025 made her own voice more widely accessible. Events around the centenary of her 1926 death have increasingly presented her as a central author and organizer rather than a picturesque supporting character.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. A person’s mere presence in a later list of Monte Verità celebrities is insufficient.

### Henri Oedenkoven — documented fact

Met Hofmann at Arnold Rikli’s establishment in Veldes in 1899. Became her partner in a free or “reform” union, principal financial collaborator, and co-founder/co-director of Monte Verità from 1900. Their intimate partnership ended around 1918, but they remained associated and both migrated to Brazil.

### Arnold Rikli — documented fact

Hofmann stayed at or used Rikli’s natural-healing establishment at Veldes in 1899. Rikli’s light-air-water regimen was a direct formative model for Monte Verità.

### Karl Gräser — documented fact

Met Hofmann at Veldes in 1899; co-founded the settlement in 1900–1901. Later separated from the central institution amid ideological and property conflict. Became partner of Hofmann’s sister Jenny.

### Gustav Arthur “Gusto” Gräser — documented fact

Joined the founding expedition in 1900. Hofmann and Oedenkoven regarded him as unsuitable and excluded him in 1901. Subsequent controversy concerns whether Hofmann’s chronicle minimized his role in originating the Monte Verità ideal.

### Henri Oedenkoven, Karl Gräser, and Gusto Gräser together — documented fact

They formed, with Hofmann and others, the central network from which Monte Verità emerged. Their subsequent split produced the two enduring models of the hill: organized sanatorium and anti-institutional spiritual colony.

### Erich Mühsam — documented fact

Participated in the early Monte Verità/Ascona milieu and had direct institutional contact with Oedenkoven’s establishment. His 1905 *Ascona* criticized its commercial character; Hofmann’s 1906 book responded to the wider sensational and anarchist image of the hill. Their relationship was antagonistic rather than collaborative.

### Hermann Hesse — reported fact

Stayed in the Monte Verità environment and underwent natural-healing treatment. He encountered the institution Hofmann co-directed, but the consulted sources do not establish a close personal friendship. Later claims of direct literary influence should remain interpretive.

### Otto Gross — documented shared milieu

Gross was active in the Ascona–Monte Verità scene during Hofmann’s proprietorship. His supply of poison to Lotte Hattemer connected him to a major scandal affecting the colony’s reputation. No close collaborative relationship with Hofmann was established.

### Isadora Duncan — reported fact

Hofmann admired Duncan and reportedly went to see her; modern accounts say the connection helped draw Rudolf von Laban toward Monte Verità. Direct sustained collaboration is not demonstrated.

### Rudolf Laban — documented fact

Laban established his summer school for movement art on Monte Verità from 1913. Hofmann and Oedenkoven provided or controlled the site. Cooperation later turned to conflict around the 1917 festival and Theodor Reuss’s congress.

### Mary Wigman — documented institutional connection

Wigman studied and worked within Laban’s Monte Verità school while Hofmann remained associated with the estate. No separately documented close friendship with Hofmann was found.

### Suzanne Perrottet — documented institutional connection

Participated in Laban’s movement-school circle on Monte Verità during Hofmann’s proprietorship. The evidence supports shared institution and scene, not a separately documented intimate relationship.

### Theodor Reuss — documented fact

Reuss held an O.T.O.-associated congress and “gnostic mass” at Monte Verità in 1917. A 1919 organizational notice lists Hofmann as assisting him and assigns her esoteric degree designations.

### Hermann Graf von Keyserling — no verified direct connection found

Both belonged to the broader early twentieth-century spiritual-reform world, but the consulted sources did not document a meeting, correspondence, or collaboration.

### Carl Gustav Jung — unverified direct connection

Jung is frequently named in generalized lists of Monte Verità or Ascona visitors. His major documented Ascona association belongs to the Eranos conferences founded after Hofmann’s death. No reliable evidence of a personal Hofmann–Jung encounter was found.

### Olga Fröbe-Kapteyn — no verified personal connection found

Fröbe-Kapteyn founded Eranos near Ascona in 1933. The local spiritual culture Hofmann helped create was a precursor, but no meeting or correspondence between the two women was established.

### Annie Besant — no verified direct connection found

A woman identified as “Frau Paulus,” a friend of Besant, lived in the wider Monte Verità community. This is an indirect network link, not evidence that Hofmann met or corresponded with Besant.

### Rudolf Steiner — unverified direct connection

Steiner is often included in expansive Monte Verità visitor lists, and a patron associated with him lived nearby. The consulted evidence does not securely establish direct contact with Hofmann.

### Friedrich Nietzsche — documented intellectual background, not personal connection

Nietzsche’s ideas and earlier plans for philosophical communal life formed part of the intellectual prehistory later associated with Monte Verità. He died in 1900, and no meeting or correspondence with Hofmann is documented.

### Richard Wagner — documented influence, no meeting

Hofmann was a committed Wagnerian and used Wagnerian names and imagery in shaping Monte Verità’s symbolic landscape. Wagner died in 1883; no personal meeting is documented.

### Émile Jaques-Dalcroze — reported presence, direct Hofmann relationship unverified

Sources connect Jaques-Dalcroze with Monte Verità’s movement and educational milieu. No specific meeting or correspondence with Hofmann was confirmed.

### Friedrich Fröbel — intellectual-background connection only

Hofmann’s educational and child-reform ideas belonged to a wider pedagogical world influenced by Fröbel, but no source located records an explicit acknowledgment by Hofmann or a direct institutional relationship.

### Remaining roster

No evidence was found for genuine direct connections between Hofmann and the overwhelming majority of the atlas roster. Famous-person lists attached to Monte Verità are especially unreliable because they combine different institutions, properties, and periods.
