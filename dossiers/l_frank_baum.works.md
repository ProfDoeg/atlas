## Works by Lyman Frank Baum

### Books and book-length literary obras under Baum’s name

- **1873:** *Baum’s Complete Stamp Dealer’s Directory*. A reference directory made by the teenage Baum for stamp collectors; it is a protocol-like working tool rather than a narrative book. [1][3]
- **1886:** *The Book of the Hamburgs: A Brief Treatise upon the Mating, Rearing and Management of the Different Varieties of Hamburgs*. A practical poultry-breeding manual, not children’s literature. [1][3]
- **1897:** *Mother Goose in Prose*. A collection transforming nursery rhymes into prose narratives; its illustrated book form was collaborative with Maxfield Parrish. [1][2]
- **1898:** *By the Candelabra’s Glare*. A privately printed collection of Baum’s poems, accompanied by photographs made by his son Harry Neal Baum. [1][3]
- **1899:** *Father Goose: His Book*. A collaborative illustrated book of nonsense verse, made with W. W. Denslow; its typographic and pictorial form is integral enough to approach a small Gesamtkunstwerk. [1][2]
- **1900:** *A New Wonderland*. A children’s fantasy subsequently reissued, with textual changes, as *The Magical Monarch of Mo*; these are manifestations of substantially the same obra, not two independent novels. [1][2]
- **1900:** *The Army Alphabet*. An illustrated alphabet in verse, made with illustrator Harry Kennedy. [1][3]
- **1900:** *The Art of Decorating Dry Goods Windows and Interiors*. A professional handbook and protocol for window dressers, developed from Baum’s trade-journal work. [1][3]
- **1900:** *The Navy Alphabet*. An illustrated alphabet in verse, companion to *The Army Alphabet*. [1][3]
- **1900:** *The Songs of Father Goose: For the Kindergarten, the Nursery, and the Home*. A songbook in which Baum’s verses were set to music by Alberta Neiswanger Hall; it is a collaborative musical manifestation of the *Father Goose* material. [1][4]
- **1900:** *The Wonderful Wizard of Oz*. An illustrated fantasy novel made with W. W. Denslow; the unusually integrated relation of narrative, typography, color and image makes the first book more than a text alone. [1][2]
- **1901:** *American Fairy Tales*. A collection of modern American fairy stories, several first published separately in newspapers. [1][3]
- **1901:** *Dot and Tot of Merryland*. An illustrated children’s fantasy, made with W. W. Denslow, about a country adjoining Baum’s developing fairyland geography. [1][3]
- **1901:** *The Master Key: An Electrical Fairy Tale*. A technological fantasy novel whose subtitle deliberately joins invention narrative and fairy tale. [1][3]
- **1902:** *The Life and Adventures of Santa Claus*. A myth-making fantasy novel connecting Santa Claus with the Forest of Burzee. [1][3]
- **1903:** *The Enchanted Island of Yew*. A fantasy novel organized as a sequence of adventures in the island’s several kingdoms. [1][3]
- **1903:** *The Magical Monarch of Mo*. The revised manifestation of *A New Wonderland*, not an entirely new obra. [1][2]
- **1904:** *The Marvelous Land of Oz*. The second Oz novel; early copies called it *The Marvelous Land of Oz*, while later manifestations often use *The Land of Oz*. [1][2]
- **1905:** *Queen Zixi of Ix; or, The Story of the Magic Cloak*. A fantasy novel first serialized in *St. Nicholas* and then manifested as a book. [1][3]
- **1905:** *The Woggle-Bug Book*. A picture-book/comic narrative using characters from Oz; its relation to the contemporary stage musical makes it an extension across media rather than an ordinary sequel. [1][3]
- **1906:** *John Dough and the Cherub*. An illustrated fantasy novel; its protagonist and world later crossed into Baum’s Oz-related geography. [1][3]
- **1907:** *Father Goose’s Year Book: Quaint Quacks and Feathered Shafts for Mature Children*. A collection of topical nonsense and satirical verse aimed principally at adults. [1][3]
- **1907:** *Ozma of Oz*. The third Oz novel and the first whose first edition was illustrated by John R. Neill. [1][2]
- **1908:** *Dorothy and the Wizard in Oz*. The fourth Oz novel, partly assembled from situations Baum had used in other manifestations. [1][2]
- **1909:** *The Road to Oz*. The fifth Oz novel; its first edition used successively tinted paper gatherings as part of the book-object. [1][2]
- **1910:** *The Emerald City of Oz*. The sixth Oz novel, initially presented as Baum’s farewell to the series. [1][2]
- **1910:** *L. Frank Baum’s Juvenile Speaker*. A performance-oriented anthology of dialogues, monologues and recitations, many revised from earlier writings; *Baum’s Own Book for Children* was an alternate title, while *The Snuggle Tales* and *Oz-Man Tales* were later publisher-made rearrangements rather than new Baum obras. [1][3]
- **1911:** *The Daring Twins: A Story for Young Folk*. A nonfantastic juvenile novel; *The Secret of the Lost Fortune* is a modern retitling, not Baum’s title. [1][3]
- **1911:** *The Sea Fairies*. A fantasy novel introducing Trot and Cap’n Bill. [1][3]
- **1912:** *Phoebe Daring: A Story for Young Folk*. A juvenile mystery and sequel to *The Daring Twins*. [1][3]
- **1912:** *Sky Island: Being the Further Exciting Adventures of Trot and Cap’n Bill*. A fantasy novel linking the Trot stories with characters from Oz. [1][3]
- **1913:** *Little Wizard Stories of Oz*. A collection of six short Oz stories originally issued as separate small books; the collection and the six pamphlet manifestations should not be confused with six new narratives. [1][2]
- **1913:** *The Patchwork Girl of Oz*. The seventh Oz novel, related to but distinct from Baum’s stage project and film scenario of the same title. [1][2]
- **1914:** *Tik-Tok of Oz*. The eighth Oz novel, substantially refashioned from the stage project *The Tik-Tok Man of Oz* rather than simply adapted from it. [1][2]
- **1915:** *The Scarecrow of Oz*. The ninth Oz novel, drawing on the film *His Majesty, the Scarecrow of Oz* and incorporating Trot and Cap’n Bill. [1][2]
- **1916:** *Rinkitink in Oz*. The tenth Oz novel; most of its narrative originated as a non-Oz manuscript before Baum supplied an Oz ending. [1][2]
- **1917:** *The Lost Princess of Oz*. The eleventh Oz novel. [1][2]
- **1918:** *The Tin Woodman of Oz*. The twelfth Oz novel. [1][2]
- **1919:** *The Magic of Oz*. The thirteenth Oz novel, published posthumously but completed by Baum. [1][2]
- **1920:** *Glinda of Oz*. The fourteenth Oz novel, published posthumously; its manuscript was edited for publication, so the printed text is a posthumous manifestation of Baum’s obra rather than an untouched authorial document. [1][2]
- **1969:** *Animal Fairy Tales*. A posthumous collected-book manifestation of nine animal stories first published as a 1905 newspaper series; Baum made the stories, but not this 1969 book as such. [1][3]

### Novels made under pseudonyms or anonymously

- **1905:** *The Fate of a Crown*. An adult historical-adventure novel published as by “Schuyler Staunton.” [1][3]
- **1906:** *Annabel: A Novel for Young Folk*. A juvenile novel published as by “Suzanne Metcalf.” [1][3]
- **1906:** *Aunt Jane’s Nieces*. A girls’ juvenile novel published as by “Edith Van Dyne”; the pseudonym was constructed as a continuing authorial identity. [1][3]
- **1906:** *Daughters of Destiny*. An adult adventure novel published as by “Schuyler Staunton.” [1][3]
- **1906:** *Sam Steele’s Adventures on Land and Sea*. A boys’ adventure novel published as by “Capt. Hugh Fitzgerald”; its 1908 recasting as *The Boy Fortune Hunters in Alaska* under “Floyd Akers” is another manifestation of the same obra. [1][3]
- **1906:** *The Twinkle Tales*. Six small fantasy books published as by “Laura Bancroft”; they are separate physical manifestations of a story cycle later gathered as *Twinkle and Chubbins*. [1][3]
- **1907:** *Aunt Jane’s Nieces Abroad*. The second “Edith Van Dyne” novel. [1][3]
- **1907:** *Policeman Bluejay*. An animal fantasy published as by “Laura Bancroft,” later retitled *Babes in Birdland* and issued under Baum’s name. [1][3]
- **1907:** *Sam Steele’s Adventures in Panama*. A boys’ adventure novel published as by “Capt. Hugh Fitzgerald,” then reissued as *The Boy Fortune Hunters in Panama* under “Floyd Akers.” [1][3]
- **1907:** *Tamawaca Folks: A Summer Comedy*. A satirical roman-à-clef published as by “John Estes Cooke.” [1][3]
- **1908:** *Aunt Jane’s Nieces at Millville*. The third “Edith Van Dyne” novel. [1][3]
- **1908:** *The Boy Fortune Hunters in Egypt*. A boys’ adventure novel published as by “Floyd Akers.” [1][3]
- **1908:** *The Last Egyptian: A Romance of the Nile*. An adult novel published anonymously; contemporary promotion concealed Baum’s authorship. [1][3]
- **1909:** *Aunt Jane’s Nieces at Work*. The fourth “Edith Van Dyne” novel. [1][3]
- **1909:** *The Boy Fortune Hunters in China*. A boys’ adventure novel published as by “Floyd Akers.” [1][3]
- **1910:** *Aunt Jane’s Nieces in Society*. The fifth “Edith Van Dyne” novel. [1][3]
- **1910:** *The Boy Fortune Hunters in Yucatan*. A boys’ adventure novel published as by “Floyd Akers.” [1][3]
- **1911:** *Aunt Jane’s Nieces and Uncle John*. The sixth “Edith Van Dyne” novel. [1][3]
- **1911:** *The Boy Fortune Hunters in the South Seas*. The sixth and last original “Floyd Akers” novel in the cycle. [1][3]
- **1911:** *The Flying Girl*. An aviation novel for girls published as by “Edith Van Dyne.” [1][3]
- **1912:** *Aunt Jane’s Nieces on Vacation*. The seventh “Edith Van Dyne” novel. [1][3]
- **1912:** *The Flying Girl and Her Chum*. The second “Edith Van Dyne” aviation novel. [1][3]
- **1913:** *Aunt Jane’s Nieces on the Ranch*. The eighth “Edith Van Dyne” novel. [1][3]
- **1914:** *Aunt Jane’s Nieces Out West*. The ninth “Edith Van Dyne” novel. [1][3]
- **1915/1918:** *Aunt Jane’s Nieces in the Red Cross*. A topical “Edith Van Dyne” war novel, revised and reissued after the United States entered the First World War; the two states are materially and politically distinct manifestations of one obra. [1][3]
- **1916:** *Mary Louise*. A girls’ mystery novel published as by “Edith Van Dyne.” [1][3]
- **1916:** *Mary Louise in the Country*. The second “Edith Van Dyne” Mary Louise novel. [1][3]
- **1917:** *Mary Louise Solves a Mystery*. The third “Edith Van Dyne” Mary Louise novel. [1][3]
- **1918:** *Mary Louise and the Liberty Girls*. The fourth “Edith Van Dyne” Mary Louise novel and a wartime work. [1][3]
- **1919:** *Mary Louise Adopts a Soldier*. Published as by “Edith Van Dyne,” but reportedly completed largely by an unidentified ghostwriter from a fragment or outline by the ill Baum; it resists classification as an undivided Baum obra. [1][3]

### Periodicals, editorial bodies and photographic contributions

- **c. 1870–1872:** *The Rose Lawn Home Journal*. An amateur family newspaper printed by Baum at Rose Lawn; individual issues and contributions survive unevenly, so the periodical is better treated as an editorial-printing project than as one stable text. [3]
- **1873:** *The Stamp Collector*. An amateur philatelic periodical printed and edited by Baum; its serial and partly ephemeral nature resists reduction to a single “book.” [3]
- **1880–1882:** *The Poultry Record*. A specialist poultry periodical founded and edited by Baum; surviving evidence is incomplete. [3]
- **1890–1891:** *The Aberdeen Saturday Pioneer*. A South Dakota newspaper owned and edited by Baum, containing editorials, fiction and local writing; authorship of every unsigned item cannot safely be assigned to him. [3]
- **1897–1902:** *The Show Window*. A trade periodical founded and edited by Baum for professional window dressers; it combined instruction, advocacy and trade organization and thus functioned partly as a protocol and partly as a manifesto for a new profession. [3]
- **1907:** *In Other Lands Than Ours*. Maud Gage Baum’s travel letters, with photographs and an introduction by Frank Baum; he has sometimes been called its editor, but it is not properly a book authored wholly by him. [1][3]

### Stage works, musical works and dramatic projects

- **1882:** *The Mackrummins*. A supposed early play registered or reported under “Louis F. Baum”; no text is known, and scholars question whether it was actually completed. [4][5]
- **1882:** *The Maid of Arran: An Irish Idyl*. A musical play adapted from William Black’s *A Princess of Thule*; Baum wrote the book, lyrics and music and performed in it, making it his clearest personally composed theatrical Gesamtkunstwerk. [4][5]
- **1882:** *Matches*. A drawing-room comedy performed in repertory and now lost. [4][5]
- **1883:** *Kilmourne; or, O’Connor’s Dream*. A performed but apparently lost play; its surviving status is uncertain. [4][5]
- **1883 or 1885:** *The Queen of Killarney*. A reported early play whose date varies in the sources and which may never have been written; it is more securely a claimed project than a demonstrable obra. [4][5]
- **1901:** *King Midas*. An unproduced Baum–Paul Tietjens musical project, possibly incomplete. [4][5]
- **1901:** *The Octopus; or, The Title Trust*. An unproduced topical musical project with Paul Tietjens; material from it migrated into *The Wizard of Oz*. [4][5]
- **1901:** *The Wonderful Wizard of Oz*. Baum’s closer stage adaptation of his novel, distinct from the heavily rewritten 1902 musical; the script survives but was not the version that became the long-running commercial production. [4][5]
- **1902:** *The Wizard of Oz*. A musical extravaganza whose produced text was extensively rewritten by other hands; Baum originated the adaptation and supplied material, but the stage manifestation resists attribution to him alone. [4][5]
- **1902:** *Montezuma; or, The Son of the Sun*. A completed musical script made with Emerson Hough from Hough’s spoken play, with music planned or partly composed by Nathaniel D. Mann; it remains in private hands. [4][5]
- **1903:** *King Jonah XIII*. A projected musical with Nathaniel D. Mann; its completion and survival are uncertain. [4][5]
- **1903:** *The Maid of Athens: A College Fantasy*, also called *Spartacus*. A collaborative Baum–Emerson Hough dramatic scenario; no music is known. [4][5]
- **1903–1915:** *Prince Silverwings*. A long-running musical-extravaganza project with Edith Ogden Harrison, based on her book; a completed draft and Baum’s song “Down Among the Marshes” survive, but the production project failed. [4][5]
- **1903:** *The Whatnexters*. A disputed attribution: Michael Patrick Hearn placed it among the plays, while the underlying evidence describes only the first chapter of an unfinished book planned with Isidore Witmark; the manuscript has not been found. [4][5]
- **1904:** *Father Goose*. A proposed musical collaboration with Paul Tietjens; neither its intended relation to the verse book nor its degree of completion is known. [4][5]
- **1904:** *The Pagan Potentate*. A theatrical title or project associated with Baum and Tietjens; little evidence of a completed work survives. [4][5]
- **1905:** *The King of Gee-Whiz*. A copyrighted dramatic scenario made with Emerson Hough; it is related to *Montezuma* but is a distinct treatment, and its intended music by Paul Tietjens was probably never composed. [4][5]
- **1905:** *The Woggle-Bug*. A musical extravaganza adapted from *The Marvelous Land of Oz*, with music by Frederic Chapin; it was produced briefly but failed commercially. [4][5]
- **1906:** *Untitled play set in Egypt*. A proposed dramatic project mentioned after Baum’s Egyptian journey; it may never have progressed beyond intention, and therefore resists being called a completed work. [4]
- **1907:** *Down Missouri Way*. A theatrical project known principally by title; completion is uncertain. [4][5]
- **1907:** *Our Mary*. A theatrical project known principally by title; completion is uncertain. [4][5]
- **1909:** *Mortal for an Hour*, also called *The Fairy Prince* or *Prince Marvel*. A short play loosely drawing on *The Enchanted Island of Yew* and published in more than one textual manifestation. [4][5]
- **1909:** *The Koran of the Prophet*. A theatrical project known from Baum’s dramatic bibliography; no complete surviving text is established. [4][5]
- **1909:** *The Pipes O’ Pan*. An unfinished musical play: Baum wrote one first act and then another with George Scarborough, but the later acts were never completed. [4][5]
- **1909:** *Peter and Paul*. A projected “opera” with Arthur Pryor, intended for Montgomery and Stone; no script or score has been found, although scholars have not established that it was merely imaginary. [4][5]
- **1909:** *The Girl from Oz*, also called *The Girl of Tomorrow*. A non-Oz theatrical work despite its title; its surviving status is uncertain. [4][5]
- **1910:** *The Clock Shop*. A dramatic project for which little beyond the title is known. [4][5]
- **1910:** *The Pea-Green Poodle*. A projected dramatization of Baum’s animal story; the extent of completion is unknown. [4][5]
- **1913:** *The Tik-Tok Man of Oz*. A produced musical with music by Louis F. Gottschalk, developed through earlier titles including *Ozma of Oz* and *The Rainbow’s Daughter; or, The Magnet of Love*; these titles largely mark stages of one evolving project. [4][5]
- **1913:** *The Patchwork Girl of Oz*. A musical play with Louis F. Gottschalk, subsequently revised into a film scenario; the stage and screen versions are related manifestations, not interchangeable objects. [4][5]
- **1913:** *King Bud of Noland; or, The Magic Cloak*. A musical play with Gottschalk, based on *Queen Zixi of Ix* and later refashioned as the scenario for *The Magic Cloak of Oz*. [4][5]
- **1914:** *Stagecraft; or, The Adventures of a Strictly Moral Man*. A musical play for the Uplifters, with music by Gottschalk. [4][5]
- **1914:** *High Jinks*. A private Uplifters theatrical entertainment; “high jinks” also named the club’s participatory event, so the boundary between script, performance and social ritual is porous. [4][5]
- **1915:** *The Corrugated Giant*. An Uplifters play; surviving evidence is limited. [4][5]
- **1915:** *The Uplift of Lucifer; or, Raising Hell: An Allegorical Squazosh*. A private musical play with Gottschalk; it was published only posthumously in 1963. [4][5]
- **1915:** *The Birth of the New Year*. An Uplifters performance piece. [4][5]
- **1916:** *Blackbird Cottages: The Uplifters’ Minstrels*. A musical minstrel show with music by Byron Gay; its blackface form is essential historical information about the object and prevents neutral classification as merely a “musical.” [4][5]
- **1916:** *Snow White*. An Uplifters stage work, not to be confused with unrelated adaptations of the fairy tale. [4][5]
- **1917:** *The Orpheus Road Show: A Paraphrastic Compendium of Mirth*. An Uplifters musical entertainment with Gottschalk, combining revue, club ritual and private performance. [4][5]

### Film, slides, performance and cross-media obras

- **1908:** *The Fairylogue and Radio-Plays*. A touring Gesamtkunstwerk combining Baum’s live narration, hand-colored lantern slides, motion pictures, actors and an orchestral score by Nathaniel D. Mann; it cannot adequately be classified as either a film or a lecture alone. [3][6]
- **1910:** *The Wonderful Wizard of Oz*. A silent film derived from Baum’s work; Baum’s precise creative role is less secure than his authorship of the source and should not be inflated into sole filmmaking authorship. [6]
- **1910:** *The Land of Oz*. A silent short derived from Baum’s Oz material; its survival status and exact production credits are uncertain. [6]
- **1910:** *John Dough and the Cherub*. A silent short derived from Baum’s novel; its survival status is uncertain. [6]
- **1914:** *The Patchwork Girl of Oz*. A feature-length silent fantasy for the Oz Film Manufacturing Company; Baum wrote the scenario and served as producer, while J. Farrell MacDonald directed. [6]
- **1914:** *The Magic Cloak of Oz*. A silent fantasy developed from Baum’s *King Bud of Noland* project; Baum produced it, but surviving catalogues do not credit him as its sole maker. [6]
- **1914:** *His Majesty, the Scarecrow of Oz*. A silent feature for which Baum supplied the story and served as producer; it was later mined for *The Scarecrow of Oz*. [6]
- **1914:** *The Last Egyptian*. A silent feature based on Baum’s anonymous novel and credited by the AFI Catalog as directed by him; the film is lost, and some production details remain difficult to verify. [6]

### Unfinished, lost or documentary-only book projects

- **1912:** *Our Married Life*. A lost or unpublished novel project; without a recoverable text, it survives chiefly as bibliographical evidence of intention. [1][3]
- **1912:** *Johnson*. A lost or unpublished novel project. [1][3]
- **1914:** *The Mystery of Bonita*. A lost novel mentioned in legal records connected with the Oz Film Manufacturing Company; its completion cannot be demonstrated. [1][3]
- **1915:** *Molly Oodle*. A lost or unpublished novel project. [1][3]
