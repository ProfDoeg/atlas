# Dr Carl Jung of Mainz: Research Dossier

## Scope and Identification Note

This dossier concerns the obscure seventeenth-century person whom the Swiss psychiatrist Carl Gustav Jung identified in *Erinnerungen, Träume, Gedanken* (*Memories, Dreams, Reflections*) as:

> Dr. med. et jur. Carl Jung, of Mainz, died 1654.

He must not be confused with:

- Carl Gustav Jung (1875–1961), the Swiss psychiatrist and founder of analytical psychology.
- Karl Gustav Jung (1795–1864), the psychiatrist’s paternal grandfather, a German-Swiss physician, anatomist, university administrator, and Freemason.
- Carl Jung (1868–1944), a German dentist and university professor.
- Other artists, jurists, politicians, and physicians named Carl or Karl Jung.

The seventeenth-century Mainz doctor is known principally through a passage in the psychiatrist’s partly autobiographical *Memories, Dreams, Reflections* and through later scholarship analyzing that passage. No independently verified, full contemporary biography has been found.

The limitations are unusually severe. No securely attributable birth record, matriculation, degree record, marriage record, testament, publication, portrait, correspondence, property record, or contemporary narrative biography was located in the consulted online material. Some later accounts add claims—most notably that he was a Catholic rector of the University of Mainz—but the publicly accessible sources consulted did not independently substantiate them.

Accordingly, this dossier distinguishes:

- the small biographical core reported by C. G. Jung;
- later family tradition;
- modern scholarly interpretation;
- unsupported or unverified expansions of the story.

### The Carl/Simon problem

The principal published form is “Carl Jung.” An editorial or biographical note associated with Aniela Jaffé’s edition of *Memories, Dreams, Reflections* records a competing Jung-family tradition according to which the “learned doctor of medicine and law” at Mainz was named **Simon**, not Carl.

No primary record located in this research resolves the discrepancy. “Dr Carl Jung of Mainz” should therefore be treated as the conventional identification created or transmitted through C. G. Jung’s memoir, not as a name independently confirmed here from a seventeenth-century register.

### The 1654/1645 problem

C. G. Jung’s account, in German and in the principal English translation, gives **1654**.

Hereward Tilton’s scholarly monograph repeatedly prints **1645**. Websites and conspiracy-oriented publications drawing on Tilton reproduce 1645. No documentary explanation for reversing the final two digits was found.

The balance of the transmission favors 1654 because:

1. It is the date given in the underlying Jung/Jaffé autobiographical text.
2. Multiple translations reproduce 1654.
3. Tilton cites the same memoir passage but prints 1645, suggesting a transposition or secondary error.
4. No separately identified death record supporting 1645 was located.

**Working conclusion:** 1654 is the better-attested reported year, but neither date has been independently verified from a contemporary civil, ecclesiastical, or university document.

## Family and Ancestry

### Claimed relationship to Carl Gustav Jung

Carl Gustav Jung presented the Mainz doctor in a discussion of his paternal ancestors. Later authors ordinarily call the doctor Jung’s ancestor, sometimes specifically his great-great-grandfather or a more remote forebear.

The memoir itself simultaneously acknowledges a genealogical break. Jung wrote that his demonstrable family tree began with **Sigmund Jung**, described as a *civis Moguntinus*—citizen of Mainz—living at the beginning of the eighteenth century. The seventeenth-century doctor stood on the far side of that documentary hiatus.

Consequently:

- **Family tradition:** Dr Carl or Simon Jung belonged to the paternal Jung line.
- **C. G. Jung’s belief:** He treated the physician as an ancestor whose intellectual world might illuminate his own family destiny.
- **Documentary status:** The continuous parent-to-child chain from the seventeenth-century doctor to Sigmund Jung and then to the later family was not demonstrated in the sources consulted.
- **Historical conclusion:** An ancestral relationship is plausible as family tradition but remains genealogically unproved in the accessible evidence.

### Alleged descendants

The later documented paternal line associated with the psychiatrist includes:

- Sigmund Jung, an early-eighteenth-century citizen of Mainz.
- Franz Ignaz Jung (1759–1831), a physician associated with Mainz and Mannheim.
- Karl Gustav Jung (1795–1864), physician and professor at Basel.
- Johann Paul Achilles Jung (1842–1896), Reformed pastor.
- Carl Gustav Jung (1875–1961), psychiatrist.

These persons cannot be inserted as confirmed direct descendants of the Mainz doctor without the missing intermediate records.

### Coat of arms and the phoenix

C. G. Jung connected his family history to a heraldic tradition. He said that the Jung arms originally included a **phoenix**, but that his grandfather Karl Gustav Jung modified them. The revised arms displayed:

- a blue cross;
- blue grapes on a gold field;
- a blue horizontal band;
- a gold star.

The psychiatrist interpreted the revised design as Masonic or Rosicrucian: cross and grapes represented heavenly and chthonic spirits, and the gold star represented their union or *aurum philosophorum*.

The original phoenix has been associated retrospectively with the seventeenth-century doctor and with Michael Maier’s alchemical phoenix symbolism. No seventeenth-century seal, armorial grant, grave marker, bookplate, or autograph belonging to the Mainz doctor was found. The application of this heraldry to him is therefore **family mythology or retrospective interpretation**, not established contemporary fact.

### Possible University of Mainz affiliation

A later French-language biographical summary claims that Carl Jung was:

- doctor of medicine and law;
- Catholic;
- rector of the University of Mainz;
- a learned man.

The University Archive of Mainz identifies a surviving or reconstructed source titled *Aula honoris primorum ab origine sua philosophorum Trophaeis*, containing a chronology of rectors, deans, promoters, and doctoral recipients from **1618 to 1712**. This is precisely the type of source capable of testing the rector and degree claims.

However, the publicly indexed description does not name Jung, and no searchable transcription conclusively identifying Carl or Simon Jung was located. The rector claim therefore remains **unverified**.

It should not be repeated as fact until the relevant folios or an authoritative transcription are examined.

### Intellectual formation

C. G. Jung assumed that a learned physician of this period would have known pharmacological literature influenced by Paracelsus. This is a contextual inference, not evidence concerning the individual’s actual reading.

No personal library, lecture notes, citations, correspondence, or authored work has been found to demonstrate his intellectual formation.

## Chronological Life History

## Before approximately 1600

No established events.

A birth in the later sixteenth century would be chronologically compatible with holding advanced degrees and practicing during the first half of the seventeenth century, but no birth year can be calculated responsibly.

## Early seventeenth century

According to C. G. Jung, Dr Carl Jung lived in Mainz “at that time,” referring generally to the age of Michael Maier and Gerhard Dorn.

Gerhard Dorn’s principal activity belonged largely to the later sixteenth century, whereas Michael Maier lived from 1568 to 1622 and was particularly associated with Frankfurt during the 1610s. If Carl Jung was professionally active during Maier’s Frankfurt years, he would have lived roughly forty kilometers away in Mainz.

That geographical proximity establishes only possibility, not contact.

## 1618–1648: Thirty Years’ War

If the reported death year is correct, Jung’s adult life overlapped substantially with the Thirty Years’ War.

Mainz experienced:

- military pressure and occupation;
- Swedish control beginning in the early 1630s;
- disruption of civic and university life;
- impoverishment and physical damage;
- interruption or weakening of university teaching.

No record found establishes Jung’s political allegiance, migration, military activity, exile, arrest, loss of property, or service to any army or court.

If he remained in Mainz, the war necessarily formed the setting of his later life. Its particular consequences for him are unknown.

## 1631–1636: Swedish period at Mainz

Mainz was occupied by Swedish forces during the Thirty Years’ War. University activity suffered, and professors and students dispersed.

No evidence was found showing whether Jung:

- remained in the city;
- fled;
- practiced medicine during an epidemic or siege;
- held municipal or university office;
- collaborated with or resisted the occupying administration.

## 1654: reported death

C. G. Jung gives 1654 as the year of death. No day, month, burial place, cause of death, will, inventory, or obituary was identified.

The reported location is Mainz, but the memoir’s wording primarily establishes residence there rather than a fully cited death record.

## Alternative: 1645

Tilton’s *The Quest for the Phoenix* prints 1645. No independent corroboration was located. Until such evidence is produced, this is best classified as a **secondary-source variant, probably a transposition**, rather than an equally documented competing date.

### Religion

Later secondary literature describes him as Catholic. This has not been traced to a baptismal, marriage, burial, university, or ecclesiastical record.

Residence and possible university employment in Mainz make Catholic affiliation plausible, but confession must not be inferred solely from geography.

### Paracelsianism

C. G. Jung reasoned that the Mainz doctor was probably familiar with Michael Maier and Gerhard Dorn because seventeenth-century pharmacology remained influenced by Paracelsus.

This contains several separate propositions:

- Paracelsian ideas influenced early modern medicine: historically documented context.
- A learned Mainz physician might encounter Paracelsian literature: reasonable possibility.
- Carl Jung actually read Paracelsus, Dorn, or Maier: unverified.
- Carl Jung was himself a Paracelsian: allegation or theory.
- He practiced alchemy: unverified.
- His medical or legal thought anticipated analytical psychology: retrospective mythology.

### Rosicrucianism

C. G. Jung placed the doctor near an intellectual world associated with Rosicrucian and Hermetic literature. Tilton interprets this passage as part of the psychiatrist’s effort to locate Rosicrucianism and alchemy in his spiritual ancestry.

No evidence was found that the seventeenth-century doctor:

- used Rosicrucian emblems;

The existence of a formal Rosicrucian “order” operating as later fraternal mythology imagined it is itself historically contested. Describing Jung as a member is unsupported.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## Myth 1: The Rosicrucian ancestor

### Story

The story presents Dr Carl Jung of Mainz as a learned seventeenth-century physician-jurist who stood in the intellectual lineage of Paracelsus, Gerhard Dorn, and Michael Maier and transmitted an esoteric inheritance eventually fulfilled in Carl Gustav Jung’s analytical psychology.

### Earliest identified appearance

The principal source is C. G. Jung’s late autobiographical recollection, prepared with Aniela Jaffé and first published in German in 1962, after the psychiatrist’s death.

### Circulation

The story was subsequently circulated by:

- editions and translations of *Memories, Dreams, Reflections*;
- Hereward Tilton’s scholarship on Michael Maier;
- Jungian and esoteric websites;
- Illuminati and secret-society literature;
- synarchist and conspiracy compilations.

### Evidence for

- Family tradition named a seventeenth-century learned doctor in Mainz.
- Mainz was geographically near Frankfurt.
- Physicians of the period could encounter Paracelsian and alchemical medicine.
- Maier’s and Dorn’s works circulated in learned settings.

### Evidence against or absent

- No correspondence between Jung and Maier or Dorn.
- No library inventory.
- No book ownership marks.
- No Rosicrucian confession or membership record.
- No securely attributed alchemical writing.
- No continuous genealogy proving descent.
- No contemporary use of the later family heraldic interpretation.

### Reputational effect

The story supplied the psychiatrist with an ancestral precedent for his interest in alchemy. Later esoteric writers converted his tentative “surmise” into stronger claims of membership, initiation, or secret transmission.

## Myth 2: A direct hereditary transmission of analytical psychology

### Story

The Mainz doctor left unresolved spiritual or intellectual questions that reappeared in Carl Gustav Jung and were completed through analytical psychology.

### Source

The psychiatrist wrote of “fateful links” and questions left unanswered by ancestors. The doctor appeared in the same autobiographical meditation.

### Status

This is a personal theory of ancestral destiny or family karma, not a demonstrable historical mechanism.

### Historical significance

It shows how Carl Gustav Jung organized his self-understanding. It does not establish anything about the seventeenth-century doctor’s psychology.

## Myth 3: The phoenix coat of arms

### Story

The original Jung arms contained a phoenix, joining the family to alchemical rebirth and to Michael Maier’s phoenix symbolism.

### Evidence

C. G. Jung reported a family armorial tradition and said his grandfather altered the arms.

### Missing evidence

No seventeenth-century armorial object belonging to Dr Carl/Simon Jung was found.

### Status

Reported family heraldic tradition; its application to the Mainz doctor and interpretation as Rosicrucian are unverified.

## Myth 4: The doctor was an Illuminatus

### Story

Modern conspiracy writing links the Mainz doctor to a continuous Illuminati or occult lineage.

### Evidence

None located.

### Chronological objection

The historical Bavarian Illuminati postdated him by more than a century.

### Status

Rumor or myth; anachronistic if understood as membership in the Bavarian order.

## Myth 5: The archives made proof impossible

### Story

C. G. Jung wrote that the family genealogy was interrupted because Mainz’s municipal archives burned during a siege in the War of the Spanish Succession.

### Documentary problem

Mainz was besieged in **1689**, but that siege belonged to the **War of the Palatine Succession**, not the War of the Spanish Succession. Mainz suffered other wars, archival dispersals, the destructive 1793 siege, and later bombing.

The modern Mainz City Archive still holds medieval and early-modern materials, while the University Archive lists a chronology of academic officeholders and doctoral recipients for 1618–1712.

### Assessment

The family explanation may preserve a real memory of documentary destruction or dispersal, but its identification of the war is historically imprecise. It cannot be assumed that every potentially relevant record was destroyed.

### Reputational effect

The claim helps explain why later writers treated the genealogy as permanently untestable. Surviving university, church, civic, and regional sources indicate that further archival investigation remains possible.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

For approximately three centuries after his reported death, no individual reputation can be reconstructed from the sources consulted.

His modern significance is almost entirely derivative of Carl Gustav Jung’s fame. The psychiatrist transformed a faint family tradition into part of a larger meditation on:

- ancestry;
- unfinished family questions;
- alchemy;
- the union of opposites;
- Rosicrucian symbolism;
- personal destiny.

Thereafter, the Mainz doctor acquired several overlapping posthumous identities:

1. **The learned ancestor:** a physician-jurist in the paternal Jung family.
2. **The presumed Paracelsian:** a doctor assumed to have known the medical-alchemical literature of his period.
3. **The Rosicrucian forebear:** a stronger, insufficiently documented version of Jung’s speculation.
4. **The occult transmitter:** an esoteric figure imagined as conveying a symbolic inheritance to analytical psychology.
5. **The Illuminatus:** an anachronistic conspiracy version lacking evidence.
6. **The archival ghost:** a person whose uncertain name, date, offices, and genealogy illustrate the hazards of building history from family memoir.

His actual medical or legal legacy cannot be assessed because no securely attributable work or documented institutional activity has been recovered.

## Research Assessment

### Strongest reconstructable core

The strongest available formulation is:

> A Jung-family tradition, published through Carl Gustav Jung’s late autobiographical recollections, held that a learned man conventionally called Carl Jung—possibly Simon Jung—lived in Mainz and held doctorates in medicine and law. He reportedly died in 1654. The psychiatrist believed or entertained the possibility that this man was his ancestor and speculated that, as an early-seventeenth-century physician, he knew Paracelsian and alchemical literature associated with Michael Maier and Gerhard Dorn.

### What cannot presently be established

The available evidence does not establish:

- exact name;
- birth;
- continuous ancestry;
- university;
- degree dates;
- rectorship;
- medical office;
- legal office;
- publications;
- marriage or children;
- property;
- direct relationship with Maier or Dorn;
- Rosicrucian membership;
- cause or exact date of death.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported or explicitly proposed by the consulted sources are included. Mere contemporaneity or geographical proximity has not been converted into a connection.

### Carl Gustav Jung

**Nature:** Claimed descendant and principal transmitter of the doctor’s modern reputation.

**Dates and places:** Carl Gustav Jung discussed the ancestor while reflecting on his family history and his Bollingen tower in the 1950s; published posthumously in 1962.

**Evidentiary label:** **Reported family tradition / unresolved genealogical claim.**

C. G. Jung treated Dr Carl Jung of Mainz as a paternal ancestor but admitted that the documented family tree broke off with the early-eighteenth-century Sigmund Jung. A continuous descent was not demonstrated in the sources consulted.

### Carl Gustav Jung the elder

**Nature:** Later member of the claimed family line; the psychiatrist’s paternal grandfather. His modification of the family arms provided the immediate setting for discussion of the Mainz doctor.

**Dates and places:** Karl Gustav Jung, 1795–1864; Mannheim, Heidelberg, Berlin, and Basel.

**Evidentiary label:** **Documented later family member; connection to the Mainz doctor remains dependent on the unresolved genealogy.**

The elder Jung was a physician and Freemason. His documented Freemasonry is not evidence that the seventeenth-century doctor was a Freemason or Rosicrucian.

### Paracelsus

**Nature:** Alleged or inferred intellectual influence through early-seventeenth-century pharmacology.

**Dates and places:** No meeting possible on the available chronology; Paracelsus died in 1541. Any connection would have been textual.

**Evidentiary label:** **Allegation or theory.**

C. G. Jung inferred that the doctor probably knew Paracelsian medicine. No personally owned text, citation, manuscript, or authored work confirms this.

### Absence of other Atlas connections

No documented meeting, correspondence, collaboration, rivalry, patronage, institutional association, or acknowledged influence was found between Dr Carl/Simon Jung of Mainz and the remaining atlas-roster figures.

Michael Maier and Gerhard Dorn are central to the subject’s later mythology but do not appear in the supplied atlas roster. Their alleged connection to him is also unverified.
