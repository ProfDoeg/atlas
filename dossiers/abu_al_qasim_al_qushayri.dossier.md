# Abu al-Qasim Abd al-Karim ibn Hawazin al-Qushayri: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Abū al-Qāsim al-Qushayrī lived in fifth/eleventh-century Khurasan. The surviving record is uneven: substantial information exists about his education, writings, public teaching, doctrinal politics, imprisonment, travels, and descendants, but almost nothing securely documents his everyday domestic life, personal finances, health, or final illness.

The principal biographical tradition derives from medieval Nishapuri and Shāfiʿī biographical literature, especially material preserved by ʿAbd al-Ghāfir al-Fārisī, al-Samʿānī, Ibn Khallikān, al-Dhahabī, al-Subkī, and later compilers. These writers often used honorific and hagiographical language. Modern critical reconstruction is particularly indebted to Martin Nguyen and to Francesco Chiabotti and Nguyen’s bibliographical work.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** directly stated in a work by al-Qushayrī, an early biographical record, manuscript evidence, or several mutually reinforcing sources.
- **Reported fact:** found in the biographical tradition but not independently verifiable.
- **Disputed attribution:** a work or claim assigned to al-Qushayrī in some sources but questioned by manuscript or bibliographical research.
- **Hagiographical tradition / legend:** an edifying narrative whose historical core cannot be established.
- **Modern interpretation:** a conclusion proposed by modern scholarship rather than an explicit contemporary statement.

Dates are given in Hijrī and Common Era forms where reasonably secure. Medieval Islamic dates do not always convert to one unique Gregorian day.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Abū al-Qāsim ʿAbd al-Karīm ibn Hawāzin ibn ʿAbd al-Malik ibn Ṭalḥa ibn Muḥammad al-Qushayrī al-Naysābūrī al-Istiwāʾī.

**Arabic:** أبو القاسم عبد الكريم بن هوازن بن عبد الملك بن طلحة بن محمد القشيري النيسابوري الأُستوائي

**Common short forms:**

- Abū al-Qāsim al-Qushayrī
- ʿAbd al-Karīm al-Qushayrī
- al-Ustādh Abū al-Qāsim
- Imam al-Qushayrī
- al-Qushayrī al-Naysābūrī
- al-Qushayrī al-Istiwāʾī

**Honorifics appearing in later literature:**

- Zayn al-Islām, “Ornament of Islam”
- al-Ustādh, “the Master/Professor”
- Ustādh al-Jamāʿa, “Master of the Community”
- Shaykh al-mashāyikh, “Master of the Masters”
- Imām al-aʾimma, “Imam of the Imams”
- Shaykh Khurāsān, “Master of Khurasan”

These are encomiastic titles, not government offices.

**Religious and intellectual affiliations:**

- Sunni Muslim
- Shāfiʿī jurisprudence
- Ashʿarī scholastic theology
- Sufism, especially the sober or “Junaydian” current transmitted through Abū ʿAlī al-Daqqāq
- Qurʾanic exegesis, hadith, legal theory, Arabic language, preaching, and devotional literature

**Primary occupations:** jurist, theologian, Sufi teacher, preacher, Qurʾan commentator, hadith transmitter, author, madrasa leader, and head of a Sufi teaching lodge.

**Birth:** Rabīʿ I 376 AH, conventionally 986 CE.

**Birthplace:** Ustuwā or Ustuwā district, north of Nishapur in Khurasan, usually localized in the region of modern Quchan in northeastern Iran. Statements that he was born “in Nishapur” use the name of the principal regional city rather than the more precise district.

**Death:** Sunday, 16 Rabīʿ II 465 AH. The most commonly accepted conversion is 30 December 1072 CE. Some older reference works give 1073 or even 1074 because of conversion or copying errors.

**Death place:** Nishapur, Khurasan.

**Burial:** near the Qushayriyya madrasa, beside or near the tomb of his teacher and father-in-law Abū ʿAlī al-Daqqāq.

## Family and Ancestry

### Tribal and regional ancestry

**Documented or strongly reported fact:** His nisba *al-Qushayrī* identified him with Banū Qushayr, an Arab tribe belonging to the larger ʿĀmir ibn Ṣaʿṣaʿa grouping. His ancestors were described as Arab settlers who entered Khurasan during or after the Muslim conquests and received agricultural property for military service.

By his lifetime, the family had been established in Iran for generations. Modern scholarship considers it probable that Persian was used in the household even though he received a high-level Arabic literary education.

His secondary nisba, *al-Istiwāʾī*, refers to Ustuwā, while *al-Naysābūrī* reflects his long residence and public career in Nishapur.

### Parents and childhood guardianship

His father was Hawāzin ibn ʿAbd al-Malik. Medieval sources state that Hawāzin died while ʿAbd al-Karīm was still a child. His mother’s personal name is not securely preserved. A later report identifies her as the sister of Abū ʿAqīl al-Sulamī, but this is less firmly documented than his paternal genealogy.

Following his father’s death, the boy reportedly came under the care or educational supervision of Abū al-Qāsim al-Yamānī or al-Ilmānī, from whom he learned Arabic letters and *adab*. Variants in the name reflect manuscript transmission.

### Inherited land

**Reported fact:** He inherited an estate or village property in Ustuwā. The tradition that his original journey to Nishapur was prompted by a desire to obtain tax relief for this property is plausible but not independently verifiable. It portrays him initially as a rural landholder concerned with revenue and taxation rather than as a religious aspirant.

No reliable valuation of the property survives. There is no evidence for commercial companies, financial partnerships, banking, or investment structures in the modern sense.

### First wife: Fāṭima bint Abī ʿAlī al-Daqqāq

Al-Qushayrī married Fāṭima, daughter of his Sufi master Abū ʿAlī al-Ḥasan ibn ʿAlī al-Daqqāq. The marriage probably occurred before 414/1023, though an exact date is unavailable.

Fāṭima was born around 390/1000 and died in 480/1087. She was remembered as a learned and devout woman and a transmitter of hadith. Reports that she heard Abū Nuʿaym al-Iṣfahānī during his 400/1009 visit to Nishapur are chronologically possible. Her intellectual identity should not be reduced to her roles as daughter and wife.

### Second wife

Sources report a second wife, described as a daughter of a man named Aḥmad ibn—after which surviving summaries differ or become incomplete. Her full identity has not been securely reconstructed. Some accounts assign three sons to Fāṭima and three to the second wife; others attribute six sons to Fāṭima and three additional sons to the second wife. This numerical conflict remains unresolved.

### Children

Names associated with al-Qushayrī’s immediate family include:

- Abū Naṣr ʿAbd al-Raḥīm, born approximately 430/1038–39, died 514/1120. The most prominent son; theologian, preacher, grammarian, and Shāfiʿī scholar.
- Abū Saʿīd ʿAbd Allāh.
- ʿUbayd Allāh.
- ʿAbd al-Wāḥid.
- ʿAbd al-Raḥmān.
- ʿAbd al-Munʿim, also called Abū al-Muṭahhar in transmission literature.
- A daughter variously named Amat al-Raḥīm or Umm/Amat al-Karīm in secondary records.
- Other daughters are mentioned generically but not securely named.

The oft-repeated statement that he left “six sons” is therefore not the whole tradition: scholarly introductions also report three sons by a second wife. The discrepancy may result from counting only the prominent sons of Fāṭima, confusion between children and surviving scholarly descendants, or divergent medieval genealogies.

His maternal grandson ʿAbd al-Ghāfir al-Fārisī, an important Nishapuri historian, grammarian, and hadith scholar, transmitted material from al-Qushayrī and Fāṭima and became an important source for the family’s history.

## Childhood and Early Life, 376/986–c. 390s/1000s

His father’s early death left him an heir to rural property. His first education consisted of writing, Arabic grammar, literature, poetry, and some arithmetic. Arithmetic would have been useful in administering agricultural revenues and taxes.

Sources also emphasize his training in:

- horsemanship;
- archery;
- weapons;
- the courtly and martial accomplishments expected of a provincial landholder’s son.

This education distinguishes him from retrospective images of a child destined exclusively for monastic or scholarly life.

**Reported turning point:** He traveled to Nishapur, perhaps at about fifteen years of age, either to pursue education or to seek a reduction in taxes imposed on his property. The tax-relief motive is explicitly presented as a report rather than a securely dated fact in modern scholarship.

At Nishapur he attended a sermon or teaching session of Abū ʿAlī al-Daqqāq. The encounter redirected his life toward Sufi discipline and formal religious study.

## Education and Formation

### Abū ʿAlī al-Daqqāq

Abū ʿAlī al-Ḥasan ibn ʿAlī al-Daqqāq, who died around 405/1015 or somewhat later, became al-Qushayrī’s principal Sufi teacher. Al-Daqqāq accepted him as a disciple, directed him to study the formal religious sciences, later gave him his daughter Fāṭima in marriage, and was eventually succeeded by him.

Al-Qushayrī quotes al-Daqqāq hundreds of times in his writings, frequently using the formula “I heard the Master Abū ʿAlī say.” This is direct evidence of an unusually strong intellectual and spiritual dependence.

A first-person passage preserved in the *Risāla* says that, early in his discipleship, al-Qushayrī would approach al-Daqqāq only while fasting and after ritual washing; he sometimes retreated from the door in awe. The emotional experience is his own testimony, although later retellings amplify its hagiographical quality.

### Sufi lineage

Al-Daqqāq had studied with Abū ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Sulamī and/or Ibrāhīm al-Naṣrābādhī. The lineage presented in al-Qushayrī’s intellectual environment ran through the sober Baghdad school:

al-Qushayrī → al-Daqqāq → al-Naṣrābādhī → al-Shiblī → al-Junayd and the Baghdad tradition.

Exact initiation chains differ in later sources, but al-Junayd’s doctrinal and symbolic authority is unmistakable.

### Jurisprudence

He studied Shāfiʿī law with Abū Bakr Muḥammad ibn Bakr al-Ṭūsī, who died in 420/1029. Sources say he completed a formal set of legal notes or a *taʿlīqa* under him.

### Theology and legal theory

His principal theological teachers were:

- Abū Bakr Ibn Fūrak, died 406/1015;
- Abū Isḥāq al-Isfarāʾīnī, died 418/1027.

The biographical tradition says al-Isfarāʾīnī was so impressed by al-Qushayrī’s ability to reproduce and organize his lessons that he told him to study the master’s books independently and bring forward only difficult questions.

Al-Qushayrī also studied the writings of al-Bāqillānī. His mature theology combined the approaches of Ibn Fūrak and al-Isfarāʾīnī rather than simply repeating one teacher.

### Hadith

Modern synthesis estimates that he heard hadith from at least seventeen authorities and transmitted to approximately sixty-six students. Named teachers in the tradition include Abū al-Ḥasan ibn Bishrān and Abū al-Ḥasan al-Khaffāf.

His career included public hadith dictation sessions (*majālis al-imlāʾ*), including sessions at the Abbasid court in Baghdad.

### Abū ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Sulamī

Al-Daqqāq introduced him to al-Sulamī, the Nishapuri Sufi author who died in 412/1021. Al-Sulamī’s *Ṭabaqāt al-ṣūfiyya* and exegetical traditions became major sources for the *Risāla* and *Laṭāʾif al-ishārāt*. Al-Sulamī is cited throughout al-Qushayrī’s work.

## Early Career and Institutional Base

After al-Daqqāq’s death, al-Qushayrī succeeded him as the leading teacher associated with his teaching house or madrasa. The institution, originally connected with al-Daqqāq and reportedly established around 391/1001, became known as the Madrasa al-Qushayriyya.

The word *madrasa* should not automatically suggest a later university with standardized departments. It probably began as a teacher’s household, lecture circle, prayer space, and residence for students. Al-Qushayrī also maintained a Sufi lodge or training establishment.

His activities included:

- Shāfiʿī legal teaching;
- Ashʿarī theology;
- Sufi instruction;
- sermons and devotional exhortation;
- Qurʾanic exegesis;
- hadith dictation;
- writing and poetry.

Early biographies praise his eloquence, refined Arabic, handwriting, and ability to move audiences. Those descriptions are partly conventional, but his surviving prose demonstrates considerable literary skill.

## Chronological Life History

### Before 410/1019: first Qurʾanic commentary

Al-Qushayrī reportedly completed *al-Taysīr fī ʿilm al-tafsīr*, a conventional, philological-legal Qurʾan commentary, before 410/1019. Its survival and authorship require caution because a similarly titled commentary is also connected with his son Abū Naṣr.

### 410/1019: *Laṭāʾif al-ishārāt*

He completed or substantially composed *Laṭāʾif al-ishārāt*, his full mystical Qurʾan commentary, around 410/1019.

The work advances through literal, ethical, theological, and increasingly inward readings. It joins Ashʿarī positions on divine attributes and human action to Sufi conceptions of the heart, spirit, secret, and divine intimacy. Its exegetical sources include Qurʾan, hadith, Arabic usage, legal reasoning, earlier Nishapuri exegesis, and sayings of Sufi authorities.

### Pilgrimage and western travel

At an uncertain date, but after his establishment as a scholar, al-Qushayrī performed the pilgrimage with:

- Abū Muḥammad al-Juwaynī, father of Imām al-Ḥaramayn;
- Aḥmad al-Bayhaqī;
- other scholars.

The group traveled through Baghdad and the Hijaz. Al-Qushayrī heard and transmitted hadith on the journey. Some sources imply more than one western journey, but the exact itinerary and dates cannot be reconstructed.

### 429/1038: Seljuk conquest of Nishapur

The transfer of Nishapur from Ghaznavid to Seljuk control altered the city’s factional balance. Competition among Shāfiʿī-Ashʿarī, Ḥanafī, Muʿtazilī, Karrāmī, and other constituencies involved access to pulpits, judgeships, teaching institutions, patrons, and government favor—not theology alone.

Al-Qushayrī became a major spokesman for the Shāfiʿī-Ashʿarī party.

### 436/1044–45: defense of al-Ashʿarī

He issued a declaration or fatwa defending Abū al-Ḥasan al-Ashʿarī and identifying Ashʿarī teaching with Sunni orthodoxy. The exact title and textual boundaries are not uniformly reported, but this intervention preceded or accompanied the broader persecution.

### 437/1045–46: *al-Risāla al-Qushayriyya*

Al-Qushayrī states that he addressed the *Risāla* to Sufis throughout Islamic lands in 437/1045–46. It was simultaneously:

- a history of recognized Sufi masters;
- an exposition of technical vocabulary;
- a guide to spiritual stations and states;
- a manual of conduct;
- an Ashʿarī theological statement;
- an apology for disciplined Sufism;
- a complaint about contemporary pseudo-Sufis.

The author alleges that, in his time, genuine Sufi practice had nearly disappeared and that impostors used mystical claims to excuse appetite, laxity, antinomian behavior, or pursuit of status. This is a partisan diagnosis, not a neutral census of eleventh-century Sufism.

### 445–446/1053–55: the Ashʿarī-Shāfiʿī persecution

Seljuk vizier ʿAmīd al-Mulk al-Kundurī persuaded or used authorization from Sultan Ṭughril Beg to condemn “innovators” from Khurasani pulpits. In practice, Ashʿarīs were included among the targets. Leading Shāfiʿī-Ashʿarī scholars were removed from pulpits, prevented from teaching, exiled, or arrested.

Al-Qushayrī wrote *Shikāyat ahl al-sunna bi-ḥikāyat mā nālahum min al-miḥna* (“Complaint of the People of the Sunna concerning the ordeal that befell them”). The document defended al-Ashʿarī and portrayed the campaign as persecution of Sunni scholars. Its text survives through later transmission, notably al-Subkī.

### 446/1054–55: arrest and imprisonment

**Documented core:** Al-Qushayrī was arrested and confined in the citadel of Nishapur.

A modern reconstruction based on the medieval sources places his imprisonment at approximately one week. His supporters, together with allies of the prominent Shāfiʿī Abū Sahl ibn al-Muwaffaq, reportedly mobilized and threatened or initiated armed action. Al-Qushayrī was released.

The episode was not a modern criminal prosecution: no ordinary crime, evidentiary hearing, or judicial conviction is recorded. It was coercive detention within a political-theological factional struggle.

### 448/1056–57: Baghdad

Caliph al-Qāʾim invited or authorized al-Qushayrī to hold hadith sessions in the Abbasid palace. Sources report an honorable reception and public teaching.

This was an important symbolic success for a scholar marginalized by Seljuk administration in Khurasan. It also indicates that al-Qushayrī’s authority extended beyond Nishapur.

### Return to Khurasan and residence in Tus

Because Nishapur remained controlled by hostile factions, he reportedly settled temporarily in Tus, near modern Mashhad. The duration is uncertain. Accounts sometimes describe this as exile; “enforced displacement” or “residence outside Nishapur under hostile conditions” is more precise because no surviving formal banishment decree is known.

### 455–456/1063–64: political reversal

Ṭughril Beg died in 455/1063. Alp Arslan succeeded him, and Niẓām al-Mulk replaced al-Kundurī. Al-Kundurī was arrested and later executed.

Niẓām al-Mulk reversed the anti-Ashʿarī policy, patronized Shāfiʿī institutions, and enabled displaced scholars—including al-Qushayrī and Imām al-Ḥaramayn al-Juwaynī—to return.

Al-Qushayrī returned to Nishapur around 455–456/1063–64. The sources describe Niẓām al-Mulk as honoring him, but no salary, grant amount, or direct financial contract has survived.

### Final years, 456–465/1064–72

He resumed teaching, preaching, writing, and leadership of the Qushayriyya establishment. By then he was approximately seventy-nine lunar years old.

His disciples were fewer than might be expected from his fame. The best-known was Abū ʿAlī al-Fārmadhī, later a teacher of Abū Ḥāmid al-Ghazālī. Al-Qushayrī’s strongest institutional succession came through his sons and family madrasa rather than a large personally initiated order.

---

## Death

Al-Qushayrī died in Nishapur on Sunday, 16 Rabīʿ II 465 AH, normally converted as 30 December 1072.

No reliable account identifies:

- a terminal illness;
- an accident or injury;
- poisoning;
- a psychological crisis;
- addiction;
- medical treatment;
- a violent cause of death.

He was buried close to al-Daqqāq. Fāṭima survived him until 480/1087.

Later writers give his age as approximately eighty-seven to eighty-nine lunar years. Claims that he died at seventy result from erroneous or variant chronological reporting.

## Institutions, Projects, Property, and Financial Interests

### Madrasa al-Qushayriyya

The school associated first with al-Daqqāq and then al-Qushayrī became a multigenerational family institution. It provided a base for:

- public sermons;
- Shāfiʿī legal study;
- Ashʿarī theology;
- hadith transmission;
- Sufi training;
- family scholarly succession.

The Qushayrīs subsequently became one of Nishapur’s prominent Shāfiʿī scholarly families.

### Sufi lodge and teaching circle

He maintained a lodge or organized circle for aspirants. There is no evidence that he founded a later-style mass *ṭarīqa* with standardized branches, uniforms, or hereditary global administration. Describing him as founder of a “Qushayriyya order” would be misleading.

### Property and wealth

The only consistently reported property is the inherited rural estate in Ustuwā. There is no documentary basis for estimating his adult wealth.

His family’s capacity to maintain a school suggests access to property, donations, patronage, or endowment income, but surviving sources do not securely itemize these arrangements. Assertions that he was either extremely wealthy or completely propertyless are not demonstrated.

### Technologies, inventions, patents, or commercial businesses

None are documented. His “projects” were literary, pedagogical, and institutional rather than technological or industrial.

## Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### From rural notable to Sufi scholar

The standard life narrative contrasts his early identity as a landholding, martially trained youth with his transformation under al-Daqqāq. It should not be read as a complete rejection of formal learning: al-Daqqāq explicitly directed him into law and theology.

### Synthesis of disciplines

Al-Qushayrī’s distinctive contribution was not simply “reconciling Sufism with Islam,” since earlier authors such as al-Sarrāj, al-Kalābādhī, al-Makkī, and al-Sulamī had already presented Sufism within Islamic scholarship. His particular synthesis joined:

- Shāfiʿī jurisprudence;
- Ashʿarī theology;
- hadith;
- Qurʾanic exegesis;
- Arabic linguistic analysis;
- a lineage of disciplined Sufi practice.

### Theology

He affirmed divine attributes while rejecting crude anthropomorphism. In interpreting references to God’s hand, eye, or establishment on the throne, he sometimes used figurative explanation: power, protection, sight, or domination.

Modern scholarship disagrees over how conventional his Ashʿarism was. Older accounts often cast him as an uncomplicated spokesman for established Ashʿarī doctrine. Recent research argues that his theology is more varied, especially where Sufi apophatic language and teleological reasoning reshape school positions.

### Sufism and law

His recurring formula is that spiritual reality (*ḥaqīqa*) cannot dispense with revealed law (*sharīʿa*). He opposed claims that spiritual attainment cancelled prayer, fasting, moral responsibility, or legal obligations.

His target was not mystical experience as such but people who claimed mystical privilege without discipline.

### Spiritual psychology

The *Risāla* systematizes:

- repentance;
- struggle against the self;
- seclusion;
- fear and hope;
- patience;
- gratitude;
- trust;
- contentment;
- remembrance;
- love;
- longing;
- annihilation and subsistence;
- sobriety and intoxication;
- audition or *samāʿ*;
- dreams, visions, and saintly miracles.

The treatment joins quotations from earlier Sufis to al-Qushayrī’s own analytic commentary.

### Qurʾanic hermeneutics

*Laṭāʾif al-ishārāt* presents inward meanings without formally rejecting the outward text. It maps exegetical understanding onto a hierarchy of faculties: intellect, heart, spirit, secret, and “secret of secrets.”

The commentary sometimes contains ecstatic or monistic-sounding formulations. These coexist with an insistence on divine transcendence and the authority of revelation.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Teachers and mentors

- **Abū ʿAlī al-Daqqāq:** chief Sufi teacher, father-in-law, institutional predecessor.
- **Abū ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Sulamī:** Sufi and exegetical authority; frequent source.
- **Abū Bakr Ibn Fūrak:** Ashʿarī theology and legal theory.
- **Abū Isḥāq al-Isfarāʾīnī:** theology.
- **Muḥammad ibn Bakr al-Ṭūsī:** Shāfiʿī law.
- **Abū al-Ḥasan ibn Bishrān and al-Khaffāf:** hadith transmission.
- **Al-Bāqillānī:** textual influence rather than demonstrated personal teacher.

### Friends and collaborators

- **Abū Muḥammad al-Juwaynī:** pilgrimage companion and fellow Shāfiʿī scholar.
- **Aḥmad al-Bayhaqī:** pilgrimage companion, hadith scholar, and Ashʿarī-Shāfiʿī associate.
- **Abū Sahl ibn al-Muwaffaq:** prominent local Shāfiʿī ally during the Nishapur conflict.
- **Imām al-Ḥaramayn al-Juwaynī:** younger contemporary and fellow victim of anti-Ashʿarī policy; direct intimacy is less documented than shared faction and institutional environment.

### Disciples and transmitters

- **Abū ʿAlī al-Fārmadhī:** best-known Sufi disciple; later taught al-Ghazālī.
- His sons, especially **Abū Naṣr ʿAbd al-Raḥīm**, transmitted and developed the family’s teaching.
- His grandson **ʿAbd al-Ghāfir al-Fārisī** transmitted hadith and biographical information.

### Political patrons and adversaries

- **Caliph al-Qāʾim:** received him and facilitated palace hadith sessions.
- **Niẓām al-Mulk:** reversed hostile policy and supported his return.
- **ʿAmīd al-Mulk al-Kundurī:** principal political adversary in the persecution.
- **Sultan Ṭughril Beg:** ruler under whose authorization the pulpit condemnations occurred.
- **Alp Arslan:** ruler during the policy reversal.

## Controversies, Accusations, Investigations, and Contested Material

### The Nishapur *miḥna*

**Documented fact:** Al-Qushayrī was imprisoned during an officially supported campaign against Ashʿarī-Shāfiʿī scholars.

**Partisan point:** His *Shikāya* presents the conflict as persecution of orthodox Sunnis by sectarian opponents. Other reconstructions emphasize competition for pulpits, patronage, and control of urban institutions.

**Unresolved question:** Al-Kundurī is variously labeled Ḥanafī, Muʿtazilī, or Shiʿi in polemical sources. These descriptions are not mutually equivalent and may reflect hostile retrospective classification. His administrative role in the anti-Ashʿarī campaign is better established than every theological label attached to him.

### Threatened or actual factional violence

Supporters reportedly freed al-Qushayrī or forced his release by threatening revolt or armed confrontation. The scale of violence is unclear. No reliable casualty total has been recovered.

This episode complicates purely quietist depictions of his network, although no source securely shows al-Qushayrī personally ordering an attack.

### Polemic against contemporary Sufis

In the *Risāla*, he accused many contemporary claimants to Sufism of hypocrisy, appetite, disregard for religious law, and pursuit of reputation. These were generalized religious accusations, not adjudicated criminal findings.

His presentation helped construct a distinction between “authentic” sober Sufism and illegitimate imitation. Modern scholars therefore read the *Risāla* as an active attempt to define and police Sufi identity, not merely to record it.

### Selective construction of Sufi history

The *Risāla* gives biographies and sayings of earlier masters but is selective. Comparison with al-Sulamī’s *Ṭabaqāt* shows purposeful inclusion and omission.

Most notably, al-Ḥallāj does not receive an independent biography, even though sayings attributed to him occur in the work. The likely explanation is reputational and theological caution: al-Ḥallāj’s execution and disputed ecstatic claims made him difficult to place in a defense of legally observant Sufism. This is a modern scholarly inference, not an admission by al-Qushayrī.

### Ibn Taymiyya’s later criticism

**Documented later criticism:** Ibn Taymiyya argued that al-Qushayrī’s account retrojected Ashʿarī theology onto early Sufi masters by assembling selected, fragmentary sayings. He disputed the claim that early Sufi belief coincided neatly with later Ashʿarism.

This is criticism of method and doctrinal genealogy, not an accusation that al-Qushayrī fabricated manuscripts or committed fraud.

### Anthropomorphism and allegorical interpretation

Traditionalist and later Salafi critics objected to Ashʿarī figurative interpretation of divine attributes and sometimes classified al-Qushayrī’s positions as theological innovation. Ashʿarī and Sufi traditions, conversely, treated the same interpretation as protection of divine transcendence.

These are competing doctrinal judgments; there was no neutral tribunal that resolved them as questions of historical fact.

### Gender and the Joseph narrative

Modern criticism has examined al-Qushayrī’s treatment of Zulaykhā’s attempted seduction of Joseph. Sufia Uddin framed the interpretive problem as “mystical journey or misogynist assault,” drawing attention to the spiritualization of a narrative involving coercion and gendered blame.

This is a modern ethical and hermeneutical critique, not evidence of misconduct by al-Qushayrī in his personal life.

### Financial or criminal allegations

The dedicated adversarial search located no credible evidence that al-Qushayrī was accused of:

- fraud or forgery for financial gain;
- embezzlement;
- bribery;
- money laundering;
- sexual abuse;
- ordinary homicide;
- theft;
- commercial deception;
- espionage;
- covert intelligence activity.

The historically relevant adverse material concerns factional religious politics, imprisonment, doctrinal polemic, selective historiography, and the possibility of supporters using force.

No meaningful “latest two years” scandal exists for a person who died in 1072. Recent publications revisit his theology and hermeneutics rather than disclose newly documented personal crimes.

## Myths, Legends, Hagiography, and Disputed Narratives

### Journey to obtain tax relief

**Story:** The young landowner went to Nishapur to ask officials to reduce taxes on his village. Hearing al-Daqqāq transformed his purpose and life.

**Appearance and circulation:** Medieval biographical tradition, repeated in modern introductions.

**Evidence:** Consistent with his inherited estate and arithmetic training, but no tax document survives.

**Label:** Reported fact with hagiographical narrative structure.

**Reputational effect:** Presents spiritual vocation interrupting worldly estate management.

### Paralysis from awe before al-Daqqāq

**Story:** Al-Qushayrī approached al-Daqqāq only while fasting and ritually purified; on entering the school he felt a numbness so intense that a needle prick might have gone unnoticed.

**Evidence:** Rooted in al-Qushayrī’s own reported testimony, though later devotional retellings intensify it.

**Label:** First-person devotional report; physiological details unverifiable.

**Effect:** Became a model story of reverence for a spiritual teacher.

### Al-Isfarāʾīnī dismisses him because he has mastered the subject

**Story:** After hearing al-Qushayrī accurately reproduce his teaching, al-Isfarāʾīnī told him he no longer needed ordinary lessons.

**Evidence:** Early biographical report, but shaped as a conventional demonstration of precocious mastery.

**Label:** Reported fact / encomiastic anecdote.

### Sainthood and miracles

Later authors describe al-Qushayrī as a saint, master of inward states, and possessor of spiritual unveiling. No single miracle dominates his biography in the manner found in later shrine cults.

**Label:** Hagiographical interpretation. Such claims belong to religious memory and cannot be established by ordinary historical method.

### “Founder of orthodox Sufism”

**Modern myth or simplification:** Al-Qushayrī is sometimes said to have made Sufism orthodox or single-handedly rescued it.

Earlier authors had already linked Sufi practice with Qurʾan, hadith, and law. Al-Qushayrī’s work was exceptionally influential, but it belonged to a pre-existing literary and institutional movement.

### A direct teacher of al-Ghazālī

Some popular accounts compress the transmission into a personal teacher-student relationship. The better-supported chain is:

al-Qushayrī → al-Fārmadhī → al-Ghazālī.

A direct sustained discipleship of al-Ghazālī under al-Qushayrī is not established and would have been chronologically difficult: al-Ghazālī was a young teenager when al-Qushayrī died.

### Age and death-year variants

Dates of 1073 and 1074 and an age of seventy appear in secondary literature. The internally coherent chronology is birth in 376/986 and death in 465/1072, making him approximately eighty-six solar or eighty-eight lunar years old.

## Works by or Attributed to Al-Qushayri

The authoritative modern inventory is Martin Nguyen and Francesco Chiabotti, “The Textual Legacy of Abū l-Qāsim al-Qušayrī: A Bibliographic Record,” which records every located attribution, including lost, fragmentary, and doubtful titles. Medieval title forms and modern editions vary.

### Securely authentic and substantially surviving

1. **الرسالة القشيرية في علم التصوف — *al-Risāla al-Qushayriyya fī ʿilm al-taṣawwuf***  
   Written 437/1045–46. Usually called *al-Risāla* or *The Qushayri Epistle*. Contains doctrinal preliminaries, biographies of Sufis, terminology, stations, states, ethics, miracles, dreams, audition, and instruction for novices. Numerous manuscripts, commentaries, Arabic editions, and Persian, Turkish, Urdu, German, French, and English translations survive.

2. **لطائف الإشارات — *Laṭāʾif al-ishārāt***  
   Approximately 410/1019. A complete allusive or mystical Qurʾan commentary. Published in several modern Arabic editions, commonly in three or four volumes.

3. **التحبير في التذكير — *al-Taḥbīr fī al-tadhkīr***  
   A theological and devotional treatment of the divine names. Survives and has been edited.

4. **نحو القلوب الصغير — *Naḥw al-qulūb al-ṣaghīr***  
   “The Lesser Grammar of Hearts.” Uses Arabic grammatical categories as analogies for spiritual discipline.

5. **نحو القلوب الكبير — *Naḥw al-qulūb al-kabīr***  
   “The Greater Grammar of Hearts.” Once considered lost; manuscripts were subsequently identified and the work edited.

6. **ترتيب السلوك في طريق الله — *Tartīb al-sulūk fī ṭarīq Allāh***  
   A short practical work on spiritual training and remembrance. Edited by Fritz Meier with German translation.

7. **كتاب المعراج — *Kitāb al-Miʿrāj***  
   A treatment of the Prophet’s ascension and its mystical meanings. Survives in manuscript and edition; an English translation has been an announced scholarly project.

8. **عيون الأجوبة في فنون الأسئلة — *ʿUyūn al-ajwiba fī funūn al-asʾila***  
   “Choice Answers to Various Questions.” A literary-ethical work attributed to him in early sources. Manuscript evidence and quotations by the near-contemporary al-Bākharzī strongly support authenticity.

9. **شكوى/شكاية أهل السنة بحكاية ما نالهم من المحنة — *Shakwā/Shikāyat ahl al-sunna bi-ḥikāyat mā nālahum min al-miḥna***  
   Polemical complaint concerning the anti-Ashʿarī persecution. Preserved within later biographical literature rather than as an independent autograph.

10. **الأربعون في الحديث — *al-Arbaʿūn fī al-ḥadīth***  
    A forty-hadith collection. Listed and transmitted under his name; edition and manuscript traditions require differentiation from similarly titled works.

### Reported, fragmentary, lost, or bibliographically uncertain works

The following titles occur in medieval bibliographies, manuscript catalogs, quotations, or later lists. Some may be alternative titles or sections of larger works:

- *Ādāb al-ṣūfiyya* — “Conduct of the Sufis.”
- *Aḥkām al-samāʿ* — on Sufi audition or spiritual music.
- *al-Munājāt* — devotional conversations or supplications.
- *Ḥayāt al-arwāḥ wa-l-dalīl ilā ṭarīq al-ṣalāḥ wa-l-falāḥ*.
- *al-Damʿ* — “The Tear” or a work on weeping.
- *al-Fuṣūl*.
- *al-Fatra*.
- *al-Maqāmāt al-thalātha* — “The Three Stations.”
- *al-Qaṣīda al-ṣūfiyya* — a Sufi poem.
- *al-Tawḥīd al-nabawī*.
- *al-Murdādāt* or *al-Mardūdāt*, title uncertain in transmission.
- *Nāsikh al-ḥadīth wa-mansūkhuhu* — on abrogating and abrogated hadith.
- *al-Taysīr fī ʿilm al-tafsīr* — a conventional Qurʾan commentary, reportedly his earliest work.
- *al-Tafsīr al-kabīr* — perhaps the same as *al-Taysīr*, a descriptive label, or another commentary.
- Short dogmatic or creedal epistles transmitted independently or within later compilations.
- Poems quoted by al-Bākharzī and other anthologists.
- Hadith dictations and responses preserved through students.

### The *Taysīr* attribution problem

A commentary called *al-Taysīr fī ʿilm al-tafsīr* is traditionally assigned to Abū al-Qāsim, but some surviving material or catalog descriptions may belong to his son Abū Naṣr ʿAbd al-Raḥīm. The title should therefore not be treated as bibliographically settled without identifying the manuscript and incipit.

### Posthumous and edited collections

Modern books called “works,” “letters,” “prayers,” or “sayings” of al-Qushayrī may be:

- extracts from the *Risāla*;
- selections from *Laṭāʾif*;
- later compilations;
- translations;
- devotional rearrangements;
- collections edited from manuscripts.

They are not necessarily independent works issued by al-Qushayrī under those modern titles.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Family school

His sons and descendants sustained the Madrasa al-Qushayriyya and helped establish the family as one of Nishapur’s major Shāfiʿī scholarly houses.

Abū Naṣr ʿAbd al-Raḥīm became a prominent but more combative Ashʿarī preacher. In Baghdad in 469/1077, his preaching against anthropomorphism contributed to violent Ashʿarī–Ḥanbalī disturbances. That event occurred after his father’s death and should not be assigned to Abū al-Qāsim personally.

### Influence through al-Fārmadhī and al-Ghazālī

Al-Qushayrī’s disciple al-Fārmadhī became one of al-Ghazālī’s teachers. The *Risāla* also anticipated elements of al-Ghazālī’s project: presenting disciplined spiritual practice as an indispensable component of Sunni religious learning.

### Canonical standing of the *Risāla*

The *Risāla* became one of the most widely copied, taught, translated, and commented-upon Sufi manuals. Its biographical section provided a usable genealogy of Sufi authority; its terminology became a reference system for later authors.

Its continuing authority is visible in:

- early Persian translation;
- Ottoman and Arabic commentaries;
- modern seminary teaching;
- European-language translations;
- modern Sufi instruction.

### Qurʾanic exegesis

*Laṭāʾif al-ishārāt* remains one of the earliest complete surviving Sufi commentaries on the Qurʾan. Modern scholarship has revised the older tendency to view it as merely mystical, emphasizing its integration of theology, law, hadith, grammar, and Nishapuri exegetical traditions.

### Modern reinterpretation

Recent scholarship increasingly treats al-Qushayrī as:

- a theologian in his own right, not merely a transmitter of Ashʿarism;
- an institutional and factional actor in Seljuk Nishapur;
- a selective historian who helped manufacture a normative Sufi canon;
- a literary stylist and theorist of religious knowledge;
- a representative of intellectual exchange between Arabic scholarship and Persian-speaking Khurasan.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections directly supported by the consulted evidence are included. Mere membership in a shared religious tradition is excluded.

### Abu al-Qasim al-Junayd ibn Muhammad al-Baghdadi

**Nature:** Documented intellectual and spiritual-lineage connection.

Al-Junayd died decades before al-Qushayrī’s birth, so no meeting occurred. Al-Qushayrī’s teacher al-Daqqāq belonged to a transmission line connecting the Nishapuri circle through al-Naṣrābādhī and al-Shiblī to the Baghdad school. Al-Qushayrī devoted an individual notice to al-Junayd in the *Risāla*, called him a leader and imam of the Sufi community, and made sober Junaydian spirituality central to his normative presentation.

**Dates and places:** al-Junayd, Baghdad, died 297/910; al-Qushayrī’s literary use, Nishapur, especially 437/1045–46.

**Label:** Documented textual influence; reported spiritual lineage.

### Abu Yazid Tayfur ibn Isa al-Bistami

**Nature:** Documented textual and doctrinal connection.

Abū Yazīd al-Bisṭāmī died before al-Qushayrī’s birth. Al-Qushayrī included him among the early Sufi masters in the *Risāla* and repeatedly cited sayings attributed to him, especially concerning annihilation, mystical knowledge, and the disappearance of selfhood.

**Label:** Documented literary reception; no personal contact.

### Ibrahim ibn Adham

**Nature:** Documented textual connection.

Ibrāhīm ibn Adham appears as an early ascetic authority in the tradition assembled by al-Qushayrī. His sayings and life stories contributed to the *Risāla*’s genealogy of renunciation, trust, and spiritual discipline.

**Label:** Documented literary reception; no personal contact.

### Rabia al-Adawiyya

**Nature:** Documented textual and symbolic connection.

Rābiʿa preceded al-Qushayrī by more than two centuries. Sayings and stories associated with early ascetic women, including Rābiʿa, formed part of the devotional heritage he transmitted, though women did not receive equal structural prominence in the main male biographical sequence.

**Label:** Reported textual reception; no personal contact.

### Mansur al-Hallaj

**Nature:** Documented but contested textual relationship.

Al-Ḥallāj died in 309/922. Al-Qushayrī did not give him an independent biography in the *Risāla*, although sayings attributed to al-Ḥallāj appear in the text. Modern researchers interpret the omission as deliberate caution caused by al-Ḥallāj’s controversial execution and reputation.

**Label:** Documented inclusion of sayings and omission of a formal biography; explanation is a scholarly inference.

### Muhammad ibn Abdullah

**Nature:** Foundational textual and religious authority.

Al-Qushayrī continually used the Qurʾan and reports attributed to Muhammad to validate Sufi doctrine and practice. His *Kitāb al-Miʿrāj* interprets Muhammad’s ascension, and the *Risāla* presents Sufi masters as heirs to prophetic knowledge.

**Label:** Documented textual and doctrinal relationship; necessarily posthumous.

### Abu Bakr as-Siddiq and Aishah bint Abi Bakr

**Nature:** Textual and hadith-transmission connection only.

Reports attributed to Abū Bakr and ʿĀʾisha appear within the wider hadith and ascetic tradition used by al-Qushayrī. No special personal or genealogical link is established.

**Label:** Documented or reported textual authority; no direct relationship.

### Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani

**Nature:** Chronological succession and later comparative tradition, not a demonstrated personal relationship.

ʿAbd al-Qādir al-Jīlānī was born around the time of al-Qushayrī’s death. Later scholarship compares their approaches to Sufi exegesis and legal observance, but the consulted evidence does not establish a meeting, correspondence, or direct discipleship.

**Label:** Documented later comparison; direct connection absent.

### Ibn Arabi

**Nature:** Later reception context only.

Ibn ʿArabī lived in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. Although he inherited a Sufi literary world to which the *Risāla* contributed, the consulted evidence did not securely demonstrate a specific, explicitly acknowledged dependence on al-Qushayrī sufficient to claim a direct atlas connection.

**Label:** Possible broad intellectual reception; specific connection unproven.

### Jalal al-Din Rumi and Farid al-Din Attar

**Nature:** No direct connection established in the consulted sources.

Both belonged to later Persian Sufi culture in which Qushayrian terminology was known, but no documented meeting was possible and no explicit direct dependence sufficient for this atlas was located.

**Label:** Absence of demonstrated direct connection.

For the remainder of the atlas roster, no documented meeting, correspondence, family tie, collaboration, rivalry, patronage, shared institution, or explicit acknowledged influence was found. Given the roster’s geographical and chronological range, absence is the expected result.
