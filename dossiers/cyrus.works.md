## Works by Cyrus the Great

### Royal inscriptions and inscribed objects

- **539–538 BCE:** *Cyrus Cylinder* (RIBo Cyrus II 01; formerly CB). An Akkadian royal building inscription deposited in Babylon, presenting Cyrus’s accession, cult restorations, repatriations, and work on the city wall; it is a manifesto of legitimate Babylonian kingship as well as a foundation object, not a modern charter of human rights. ([RIBo corpus](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon8/corpus/); [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/cyrus-iv/))

- **After 539 BCE:** *Cyrus Ur Brick* (RIBo Cyrus II 02). A six-line Akkadian brick inscription naming Cyrus and declaring that he established peace; it is simultaneously a building component and a compact royal manifesto. The surviving brick came from the temenos wall at Ur, but the precise construction campaign to which it belonged is not securely reconstructed. ([RIBo corpus](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon8/corpus/); [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1923-1110-221))

- **After 539 BCE:** *Cyrus Uruk Brick* (RIBo Cyrus II 03; sometimes “Cyrus A”). A short Akkadian stamped-brick inscription from Uruk, conventionally catalogued as a royal inscription of Cyrus; the associated building work is insufficiently known to name it as a separate monument. ([RIBo corpus](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/ribo/babylon8/corpus/))

- **Probably c. 519 BCE, after Cyrus’s death:** *CMa*. A trilingual label formerly repeated at Gate R and Palaces S and P at Pasargadae, identifying Cyrus as king and Achaemenid. Older editions treated it as Cyrus’s foundation inscription, but the dating of Old Persian writing and the site’s building sequence have led much recent scholarship to attribute its production to Darius I; it therefore resists inclusion as something Cyrus made. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/pasargadae/); [royal-inscription catalogue](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/achaemenid-royal-inscriptions/))

- **Probably c. 519 BCE, after Cyrus’s death:** *CMb*. A fragmentary trilingual inscription once placed above royal doorway reliefs in Palace P. The detection of Darius’s name in the fragments makes Cyrus’s authorship especially unlikely, although the object represented and commemorated Cyrus. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/pasargadae/); [royal-inscription catalogue](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/achaemenid-royal-inscriptions/))

- **Probably c. 519 BCE, after Cyrus’s death:** *CMc*. A trilingual label carved on the robe of the royal figures in Palace P, naming Cyrus as “Great King” and Achaemenid. Traditionally catalogued under Cyrus, it is now commonly understood as a Darius-period addition to Cyrus’s monument and thus as a posthumous manifestation of Cyrus rather than his own text. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/pasargadae/); [royal-inscription catalogue](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/achaemenid-royal-inscriptions/))

### Architecture and designed landscape at Pasargadae

- **After c. 547–530 BCE:** *Pasargadae*. Cyrus’s new capital was an architectural and political Gesamtkunstwerk: palaces, gatehouse, garden, hydraulic works, ceremonial structures, fortification, and tomb distributed across an extensively planned landscape. Construction remained unfinished when Cyrus died and continued under his successors, so “Cyrus’s capital” does not mean that every surviving element was completed by him. ([standard-site synthesis and bibliography](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/pasargadae/); [Achaemenica](https://achaemenica.org/articles/pasargadae))

- **After c. 547–530 BCE:** *Gate R*. A monumental freestanding gatehouse with winged-bull doorways and a surviving four-winged, crowned figure. Its initial project belongs to Cyrus’s capital, but its decoration may have been completed or altered under Cambyses II or Darius I, and the winged figure cannot securely be called a portrait of Cyrus. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/pasargadae/); [Achaemenica](https://achaemenica.org/articles/pasargadae))

- **After c. 547–530 BCE:** *Palace S*. A columned audience palace whose multiple porticoes opened toward the surrounding park. It formed part of Cyrus’s reception architecture, although its later inscriptions and some finishing work belong to the post-Cyrus building phase. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/pasargadae/); [Achaemenid Dynasty: the empire](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/achaemenid-dynasty/achaemenid-dynasty-ii-the-empire/))

- **Late in Cyrus’s reign; completed or altered under Darius I:** *Palace P*. A residential palace with a central hypostyle hall and long porticoes. Cyrus initiated the project, but its final form, doorway reliefs, and “Cyrus” inscriptions are disputed or assigned to Darius’s reign, making it an inherited project rather than a securely finished Cyrus work. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/pasargadae/); [Achaemenica](https://achaemenica.org/articles/pasargadae))

- **After c. 547–530 BCE:** *Royal garden and watercourse system*. A designed, irrigated palace landscape articulated by stone channels and basins, probably intended to bind the widely separated buildings into one ceremonial environment. The familiar reconstruction as a fully realized four-part *čahārbāḡ* is plausible but not demonstrated by all recent survey evidence. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/pasargadae/); [Achaemenica](https://achaemenica.org/articles/pasargadae))

- **Before 530 BCE, unfinished:** *Tall-e Takht stone platform*. A vast, finely built terrace begun for an unidentified monumental purpose, perhaps a palace or citadel. Work stopped abruptly at Cyrus’s death; Darius later converted the unfinished project into a fortified treasury, so its incompleteness is integral to the obra. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/pasargadae/); [Fārs: Monuments](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/fars-v/))

- **Sixth century BCE:** *Tall-e Takht mud-brick fortification*. Defensive construction adjoining and enclosing the great stone platform. Its surviving form reflects more than one phase, including substantial post-Cyrus adaptation, and cannot be assigned wholly to Cyrus. ([Fārs: Monuments](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/fars-v/); [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/pasargadae/))

- **Sixth century BCE:** *Zendān-e Soleymān*. A tall, windowed stone tower within the Pasargadae complex. Its original function—tomb, shrine, repository, or another ceremonial use—remains unresolved, and its precise position within Cyrus’s and his successors’ building phases is disputed. ([Achaemenica](https://achaemenica.org/articles/pasargadae); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, Achaemenid art and architecture](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/art-in-iran-iii-achemenian/))

- **Sixth century BCE:** *Sacred Precinct stone plinths*. Two monumental stone platforms, often interpreted as supports for open-air fire installations or ritual hearths. Their attribution to Cyrus’s program is archaeological rather than epigraphic, while their exact ritual purpose is unknown. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, Achaemenid art and architecture](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/art-in-iran-iii-achemenian/); [Achaemenica](https://achaemenica.org/articles/pasargadae))

- **Before or around 530 BCE:** *Tomb of Cyrus*. A gabled stone chamber raised on a six-tiered plinth and generally identified as Cyrus’s burial monument. The identification closely matches ancient descriptions but is not secured by an extant inscription; whether Cyrus personally commissioned it, rather than his court completing it at his death, is also unknown. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/cyrus-v-tomb/); [Achaemenica](https://achaemenica.org/articles/pasargadae))

### Restoration projects in Babylonia

- **After 539 BCE:** *Restoration of Imgur-Enlil*. Repairs to Babylon’s great inner wall are claimed in the Cyrus Cylinder, which itself was deposited in connection with the work. The surviving text establishes a royal building project but does not permit a complete architectural reconstruction of Cyrus’s intervention. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/cyrus-iv/); [Mainz University](https://www.ao.altertumswissenschaften.uni-mainz.de/bringing-the-past-to-light/cyrus-cylinder/))

- **After 539 BCE:** *Babylonian shrine and divine-image restorations*. The Cylinder presents Cyrus as returning cult images and their communities to sanctuaries and repairing neglected sacred places. This was a distributed program of royal restoration rather than one readily bounded work, and the document’s legitimating rhetoric does not independently prove every claimed intervention. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/cyrus-iv/); [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/listings/2013/cyrus-cylinder))

### Protocols and policies preserved only through later texts

- **First regnal year in Babylon, conventionally 538 BCE:** *Decree concerning the Jerusalem temple*. Ezra preserves a Hebrew proclamation and an Aramaic memorandum authorizing reconstruction and the return of temple vessels. Scholars disagree over how much authentic Persian administrative material survives beneath the later biblical composition; the extant wording is therefore not safely classifiable as a verbatim text made by Cyrus. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Book of Ezra”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ezra-book-of/); [Oxford study of Persian temple renovations](https://academic.oup.com/book/32365/chapter-abstract/268639346))

### Cities, institutions, and political creations

- **c. 550–530 BCE:** *Achaemenid imperial polity*. Cyrus combined Media, Lydia, Babylonia, Iranian territories, and other conquered regions into the first Persian empire while retaining many local administrative structures. This is a political obra made collectively through conquest, negotiation, and inherited institutions—not a single object and not something attributable to Cyrus’s labor alone. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, Cyrus II](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/cyrus-iii/); [Achaemenid Dynasty: the empire](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/achaemenid-dynasty/achaemenid-dynasty-ii-the-empire/))

- **During Cyrus’s reign:** *Cyropolis* or *Cyreschata*. A fortified settlement on the northeastern frontier, probably founded by Cyrus and remembered by Greek authors as his city. Its precise location, archaeological identity, and even the historical circumstances of its foundation remain uncertain. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Cyropolis”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/cyropolis-latin-form-of-gr/); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, Cyrus II](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/cyrus-iii/))

### Monetary production

- **After the conquest of Sardis, c. 547/546–530 BCE:** *Persian-period Croeseid coinage*. Gold and silver coins bearing the inherited Lydian lion-and-bull design continued to be struck at Sardis under Persian government. They are products of Cyrus’s administration rather than personally designed works, and individual specimens cannot always be separated securely from issues of Croesus or Darius; the distinct royal-archer darics and sigloi belong to Darius’s reforms. ([Oxford Handbook of Greek and Roman Coinage](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/27945/chapter-abstract/211878026); [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/listings/2013/cyrus-cylinder); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Daric”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/daric/))
