# Katja Wulff: Research Dossier

**Scope note.** This dossier reconstructs the life of the German-Swiss dancer, choreographer, school director, and movement educator Katja (Käthe) Wulff. It is based principally on the detailed finding aid for her papers at the Deutsches Tanzarchiv Köln; the *Historisches Lexikon der Schweiz* (HLS); the *Theaterlexikon der Schweiz* record preserved by the Swiss Performing Arts Platform; Basel city-history research; archival and biographical material on Mariette von Meyenburg; and scholarship on the Laban school, Monte Verità, and Zürich Dada. The contents of archival folders are not assumed to prove more than the finding aid states. Where the public record supplies only the existence of a document—an account, legal file, letter, or diary—the dossier says so rather than inventing its contents.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Primary name:** Katja Wulff.
- **Other recorded names:** Käthe Wulff (the form used by friends and in some Swiss records); the archive also preserves a 1926 poem by Friedrich Glauser dedicated “für Kathia,” an intimate spelling rather than an established professional pseudonym.
- **Married name:** She married Charles Ferdinand Vaucher, but the consulted records consistently identify her professionally as Wulff. No sustained use of “Katja Vaucher” has been established.
- **Born:** 31 August 1890, Hamburg, German Empire.
- **Died:** 11 June 1992, Basel, Switzerland, in the Felix Platter Hospital.
- **Age at death:** 101. Some later notices call her 102, evidently counting the calendar years 1890–1992; she died about eleven weeks before her 102nd birthday.
- **Nationality/citizenship:** German by birth; naturalized in Basel/Swiss in 1936. The Cologne archive lists Swiss naturalization documents dated 1936 and 1947, but the second document’s exact administrative significance is not stated in the online finding aid.
- **Municipal citizenship:** Basel, from 1936 according to HLS.
- **Religion:** HLS describes her as *konfessionslos* (without denominational affiliation). Her archive nonetheless preserves baptism and confirmation certificates, showing at least a conventional Christian documentary background; these do not by themselves establish adult belief.
- **Occupations:** drawing teacher; gymnastics teacher; expressionist/free dancer; choreographer; dance and movement pedagogue; lecturer; writer on movement education; founder and director of a school; director of a performing ensemble.
- **Principal institutions:** Rudolf von Laban’s Schule für Bewegungskunst/Labanschule in Zürich; the Zürich Schule für Eurythmie established with Suzanne Perrottet; the Basler Labanschule/Wulffschule; Tanzstudio Wulff; associations of Laban schools and dance congresses.
- **Principal residences and work centers:** Hamburg; Monte Verità/Ascona and the Ticino in summer 1914 and the Laban years; Zürich, 1916–approximately 1919/20; the Amalfi coast and Capri, approximately 1920–1923; Basel from 1923 until death. From 1947 she lived and taught at Augustinergasse 3 in Basel.
- **Burial:** Her urn was interred at Friedhof am Hörnli, Basel. Later local accounts state that the grave was removed in 2022.
- **Archival identities:** GND 12398355X; VIAF 45078944. Her principal fonds is Deutsches Tanzarchiv Köln, Bestand Nr. 014, catalogue object 111.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and siblings

The HLS identifies Wulff as the daughter of **Hugo Friedrich Wulff** and **Marie Elisabetha Amalia Wulff, née Döring**. The public finding aid confirms sustained correspondence with her mother, indexed as Marie Wulff, in 1916–1918. It also identifies a sister, **Cläre Wulff**, to whom Katja wrote in 1917–1919, and an otherwise unidentified “Tante Maria,” recipient of letters in 1916.

The fonds contains birth, baptism, confirmation, marriage, and ancestral baptismal records. Those files could permit a fuller genealogy, but the online catalogue does not transcribe them. No reliable public source consulted gives her parents’ occupations, wealth, politics, or the complete sibling group. No evidence found supports claims of aristocratic ancestry or a relation to other prominent Wulff families.

### Marriage, partnership, and children

Wulff’s long-term partner and eventual husband was **Charles Ferdinand Vaucher** (1902–1972), a Basel actor, dancer, director, writer, and later cabaret artist. Basel city-history research describes their pre-marital arrangement as a long *wilde Ehe*—an unmarried cohabitation that challenged prevailing bourgeois convention—and identifies Vaucher as a member of the Communist Party. They moved within Basel’s left-wing and antifascist artistic milieu, especially the Club 33 circle.

The best-supported marriage year is **1939**: the HLS entry for Vaucher and the *Deutsches Theater-Lexikon* say he married Wulff in that year. Some secondary summaries give **1937**. This discrepancy should remain visible; no consulted digital reproduction of the civil marriage certificate settles it. They divorced in **1947**. Vaucher married actress Edith Charlotte Herta Wernecke in 1949.

No children of Wulff and Vaucher, or of Wulff by another partner, are documented in the consulted sources. No evidence was found for another marriage.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1890–1912

Katja Wulff was born in imperial Hamburg on 31 August 1890. The public record is thin for her first two decades. Her later archive contains school material, including geometry notes from a Hamburg trade or applied-arts school (*Gewerbeschule*), and the personal certificates that ordinarily anchor childhood chronology. Published summaries say she trained in Hamburg as a teacher of drawing and gymnastics. The archive holds diplomas dated **1912** and **1914**, and teaching certificates dated **1914** and **1916**.

Her choice of drawing and physical education placed her near the early twentieth-century German intersection of art education, gymnastics reform, women’s vocational training, *Körperkultur*, and the emerging free-dance movement. It gave her a livelihood before she committed herself fully to dance. The consulted sources do not identify her elementary or secondary schools or establish university study.

From **1912 to 1915**, according to biographical summaries, she worked as a drawing and gymnastics teacher. From about **1913 or 1914**, she studied dance with **Gertrud Falke**, one of the Hamburg dancer sisters Gertrud and Ursula Falke. Both remained in Wulff’s personal orbit; her archive includes an album with the sisters and correspondence from them.

## Education and Formation

### Hamburg training

Wulff’s formal pre-dance qualifications were practical teaching credentials rather than a conservatory ballet education. The 1912 and 1914 diplomas qualify her as a drawing and gymnastics teacher; the later records do not describe her as classically trained. Her work emerged from modern movement education and Ausdruckstanz, which treated the body’s spatial, dynamic, and expressive capacities as primary.

### Monte Verità, 1914

In summer **1914**, she attended Rudolf von Laban’s course in “Tanz, Ton, Wort” at Monte Verità above Ascona. Her archive preserves the student card. Photographs by Johann Adam Meisenbach show her dancing with members of the Laban group near Ascona/Lago Maggiore; one widely reproduced image identifies Wulff in an orange dress. A later caption has sometimes misplaced the scene at Lake Lugano. The Kunsthaus Zürich/Perrottet material and Basel city-history account locate it by Lago Maggiore/Ascona.

A repeated narrative says that Wulff walked to Monte Verità with a backpack and no money, made a primitive shelter in or from a large storage box, and survived the season there. The account appears in later scholarship based on Wulff material and has the character of autobiographical memory. The student card and photographs establish her presence; the trek, cashlessness, and box shelter have not been independently corroborated by a contemporaneous document available online. They are plausible but should be classified as **recollected anecdote**, not fully verified fact.

The Monte Verità setting combined life-reform practices, vegetarianism, outdoor work, loose clothing, sandal-making, artistic communalism, nudity, esoteric and utopian currents, and movement experimentation. In Laban’s school, everyday collective labor and artistic study interpenetrated. Suzanne Perrottet later recalled mornings of gardening and sandal-making before outdoor dancing. Wulff met or worked among **Rudolf von Laban, Mary Wigman, Suzanne Perrottet, Maja Lederer/Laban, Sophie Taeuber**, the Falke sisters, and others who formed the early Ausdruckstanz network.

### Zürich apprenticeship, 1916–1918/19

Wulff left her salaried teaching career and entered Laban’s Zürich school in **1916**. Her fonds preserves the training contract specifying preparation as a **“Lehrerin in Tanz–Ton–Wort”** at Laban’s Schule für Bewegungskunst. She studied under Laban and Mary Wigman and worked closely with Suzanne Perrottet. A **1918 diploma** certifies pedagogy in *tänzerischem Turnen und Kunsttanz*—movement/dance gymnastics and art dance.

This was not merely attendance at a course. Letters, drawings, notes, a model icosahedron, material on Laban’s 1926 *Choreographie: erstes Heft*, and correspondence with Laban lasting to 1957 show a sustained intellectual and pedagogical relationship. Wulff drew portraits and caricatures of Laban and Wigman and later wrote recollections of Laban in 1958. Her archive also preserves an unrealized or conceptual “dance temple” design by Laban.

Wulff’s 1917 letter to her sister about cutting her hair records the bodily and psychological significance she gave to everyday form: she felt “as if liberated” after it was cut and said she moved better, adding that short hair suited a large part of her nature. The passage is direct personal evidence, though known through a later Basel historical transcription rather than a full facsimile.

## Early Career: Zürich, Dada, and the School for Eurythmy, 1916–1920

### Laban performances and movement experimentation

The archive preserves program material for a Zürich Labanschule performance in **1917**, the text *Nordische Runen* spoken and embodied by the school in 1916/17, and material surrounding the performance *Tanz und Melodie im Wort*. Wulff belonged to the generation for whom dance, speech, tone, geometric spatial analysis, improvisation, and communal movement chorus were related practices rather than separate arts.

Whether Wulff danced in Laban’s August 1917 all-night **Sonnenfest** at Monte Verità is represented in modern scholarship and group descriptions as likely or established, and she was certainly in the Laban circle. The strongest easily accessible image captions and secondary studies name her among Laban’s Monte Verità dancers. A role-by-role contemporaneous cast document was not located online, so claims about her exact part should be made cautiously.

### Zürich Dada

Wulff performed in the Zürich Dada environment and knew **Hans (Jean) Arp** and **Sophie Taeuber(-Arp)**. This did not make her a Dada theorist or a permanent member of a tightly organized party; Dada itself functioned as a volatile constellation. Her involvement is nonetheless documented through programs, Arp’s memoir, correspondence, and a 1982 interview.

The best-known episode occurred at a large Dada evening at the Kaufleuten hall. Wulff read Arp’s poems while concealed beneath a large fiery-red paper bag. When hostile or agitated “art connoisseurs” moved against her, she continued reading. Arp later called her the **“Heldin Dadas”** (“heroine of Dada”). The exact imagery and wording derive from Arp’s retrospective memoir *Dadaland*, later collected in *Unsern täglichen Traum* (1955), not from a neutral police report. It is eyewitness self-mythologizing but anchored to a real performance and became Wulff’s principal Dada legend.

In a **1982 interview with Raimund Meyer**, Wulff resisted later efforts to construct a unified Laban–Dada school. She said, in substance, that Zürich’s artistic circle was small, people inevitably encountered one another and occasionally worked together, but the circles were not unified schools; after she went to Italy, she did not write to or see them again. “It was something fleeting. And it was very good. We took part.” This retrospective testimony corrects the stronger myth of a durable organizational synthesis while confirming participation.

### Schule für Eurythmie

In **1918**, Wulff and **Suzanne Perrottet** founded a Zürich **Schule für Eurythmie**. They later changed the name to distinguish their work from Rudolf Steiner’s anthroposophical Eurythmy. The Wulff archive contains the school’s educational concept, rules, diplomas, course and demonstration material, and lecture drafts, roughly 1918–1920.

The record therefore supports a distinction: the word *Eurythmie* was initially used, but Wulff’s school was not thereby an Anthroposophical institution. No evidence found makes Wulff a member of the Anthroposophical Society. Nor does evidence of her participation in Laban’s milieu automatically make her a member of the Ordo Templi Orientis, Freemasonry, or another esoteric order associated with figures or events at Monte Verità.

## Italy: Amalfi Coast and Capri, approximately 1920–1923

After Zürich, Wulff spent about **three years** teaching dance on the **Amalfi coast and Capri**. The archive includes Italian residence certificates dated **1921 and 1923**, independently anchoring this phase. Public summaries do not identify every residence, employer, school, patron, or student, and they differ slightly in describing Capri alone versus the wider Amalfi coast.

The Italy period was not exile in a legal or political sense. It was a self-directed professional migration after World War I. No imprisonment, deportation, or refugee proceeding is documented. The available record also does not support claims that she founded a durable Italian institution. By 1923 she was looking for a Swiss city in which free dance was not already institutionally occupied.

## Basel: School Founder and Cultural Entrepreneur, 1923–1926

Wulff settled in **Basel in 1923** and founded a school for Ausdruckstanz, known in sources as the **Basler Labanschule**, **Wulffschule**, and in promotional language as a school for lay pupils and professional training. Basel city-history research says she chose Basel because comparable free-dance schools already operated in other Swiss cities and the local field remained open.

Her project served multiple constituencies:

- lay adults seeking movement and body education;
- children;
- professional teacher trainees;
- dancers and choreographers;
- movement-chorus participants;
- audiences reached by demonstrations and school performances.

The archive documents school advertising from **1924 through 1961**, examination plans for **1928, 1936, and 1948**, student work, teacher authorizations, annual reports to the Basel education department from **1933–1961**, and handwritten school diaries from **1925–1979**. This continuity makes the school her central life work, not an adjunct to performance.

Early pupils included **Mariette von Meyenburg**, **Marie-Eve Kreis**, **Els Havrlik**, **Fritz Stebler**, **Lisa Mutschelknaus**, **Trix Gutekunst-Tschumi**, and **Annemarie Nadolny**. The archive follows some graduates into their subsequent professions. Meyenburg, trained in Berlin in direction and classical ballet before coming to Wulff in 1923, became her master pupil and the principal choreographic and directorial force in Tanzstudio Wulff.

The 1926 school performance repertoire included **Altfranzösische Tänze und Gesänge** and **Der Tod zu Basel**. A 1926 lease survives. A proposed **new school building in 1929** generated plans, costings, and correspondence; the available finding aid does not show that it was built.

## Tanzstudio Wulff and the Performing Years, 1926–mid-1930s

### Formation and division of labor

From **1926**, Wulff directed **Tanzstudio Wulff**, the performance group attached to her school. Wulff and Mariette von Meyenburg worked in close partnership. The sources frequently call Meyenburg the choreographer or director of most repertory pieces, while Wulff supplied the institution, pedagogy, performers, organization, and joint artistic direction. Attributions should therefore not collapse the company’s repertory into sole choreography by Wulff.

### Basel Theater breakthrough, 1927

On the morning of **27 February 1927**, the company made its first major appearance at the Stadttheater Basel. It performed group and solo dances to music by **Alfredo Casella**, with **Paul Sacher and his chamber orchestra**, and the ballet **Barabau** to music by Vittorio Rieti. Program evidence and photographs survive. The performance introduced a nonclassical, barefoot, expressionist movement vocabulary into Basel’s institutional theater.

The production network extended beyond dancers. Wulff’s group collaborated at different times with Sacher, **Max Bill**, **Max Sulzbachner**, **Meret Oppenheim**, and others from Basel’s progressive visual-art and music circles. Sulzbachner designed for *Ariadne*; the archive includes stage designs. Precise credits vary by production and should be verified from individual programs before attributing a specific costume or setting.

### Repertoire documented in the archive

The Cologne fonds’ photographic and program series documents the following repertory or study works. Dates identify surviving material and do not always prove premiere dates:

- *Alt-französische Tänze und Gesänge* (1926)
- *Der Tod zu Basel* (school production, 1926)
- *Tanzsuite* to Casella (1927)
- *Barabau* (1927; music Vittorio Rieti)
- *Studienabende* I, II, IV, and V (1927–1928; other records may establish the missing III)
- *Formeln*, including Mariette von Meyenburg’s *Stummer Tanz* at the second study evening (23 November 1927)
- *Les Bergers et les dieux* (associated with 1929; music Rameau)
- *Dissonancen* (1930)
- *Parade* (1930/31 in differing archival descriptions; music Erik Satie)
- *Relâche* (1929/30; music Erik Satie)
- *Die Liebe zu den drei Orangen / L’amour des trois oranges* (1930)
- *Ariadne* (1931)
- *Dido and Aeneas* (school production, 1931)
- *Mercure* (1932 and 1935; collaboration with the dancer/writer Edwin Denby)
- *Ouverture* (about 1934)
- *Ballo* (about 1934)
- *Höfische Tänze* (1936; fifteenth- and sixteenth-century dances, with Schola Cantorum Basiliensis under Sacher)
- *La tragédie d’une jeune-fille* (1936)
- an undated *Krippenspiel* and unidentified dances.

Meyenburg’s record also attributes to her *Les Bergers et les dieux*, *Relâche*, *Ariadne*, and *Parade*, confirming that repertory ownership was collaborative but choreographic credit often belonged primarily to her.

### Dance congresses and international network

Wulff participated in the German dancers’ congress movement:

- **Magdeburg, 1927:** a congress card and an image of the Wulffschule exhibition stand survive.
- **Essen, 1928:** the archive contains Wulff’s lecture manuscript on dance pedagogy and Tanzstudio Wulff program material. The company performed within the congress/festival context.
- **Munich, 1930:** a participant card and school-director questionnaire survive.

Her correspondence connects her to **Kurt Jooss, Fritz Böhme, Martin Gleisner, Rudolf Schulz-Dornburg, Albrecht Knust, Lisa Ullmann, Gertrud Snell**, and the Laban Art of Movement Center; to **Edouard Szamba and Pierre Tugal** of Les Archives Internationales de la Danse; and to the Javanese dancer **Raden Mas Jodjana**. The documentary network shows engagement in debates over pedagogy, notation, school certification, and international dance organization.

### Other exhibitions and public projects

- **SAFFA, Bern, 1928:** Wulff took part in the First Swiss Exhibition for Women’s Work, which publicly displayed women’s work in domestic, professional, scientific, and artistic life. Her participation certificate survives.
- **Workers’ movement project, 1930:** she collaborated on speech and movement choruses of the **Arbeiter-Bildungs- und Sportkartell**. This is direct evidence of work with a labor education/sport organization; it is not by itself evidence that Wulff belonged to the Communist Party.
- **Hyspa, Bern, 1931:** the Wulffschule participated in the Swiss hygiene and sport exhibition; related records survive.
- **Zürich guest engagement, 1931/32:** the archive contains a Stadttheater Zürich guest-performance contract.
- **Swiss National Exhibition, Zürich, 1939:** Wulff’s school/company presented a dance performance.

By the **mid-1930s**, Wulff increasingly withdrew from dancing onstage and turned to teaching, administration, and the organization of pupil performances. This was a change in function, not retirement.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### The Wulffschule as a business

The school was a cultural enterprise dependent on tuition, professional training, advertising, rentals, permissions, and performance income. The archive preserves:

- a 1926 lease;
- school advertisements and prospectuses over nearly four decades;
- cost plans and correspondence for the proposed 1929 new building;
- a formal financial concept for school and company;
- lists of tuition income and costs;
- performance accounts for 1927–28, 1930–31, and 1936–37;
- school-performance accounts;
- police permits for performances, 1928–1934;
- annual reports to the Basel education authorities, 1933–1961;
- student files, examinations, diplomas, and teaching authorizations.

The existence of these records supports a picture of continuous, disciplined small-institution management. It does **not** disclose her net worth, salary, profitability, property ownership, investments, or debts without examination of the underlying files. Published Basel history says she repeatedly accepted financial hardship to avoid artistic compromise. That generalization accords with the Monte Verità anecdote and the school’s difficult economics, but no quantified personal balance sheet is publicly available.

### Recognition disputes

The archive explicitly catalogs correspondence and legal material concerning official recognition of the Wulffschule in **1932 and 1938**. This was an administrative/legal matter over recognition of a vocational training institution, not a criminal case. The online finding aid does not state the arguments, judgment, or outcome. A **1928 specialist-school certificate for professional training in movement art** survives, suggesting partial or earlier recognition, but it should not be conflated with the later disputes.

### Successor institution

During World War II, the performance side evolved into or was succeeded by the **Basler Kammerballett**, associated especially with Mariette von Meyenburg. Its productions included Monteverdi’s *Il combattimento di Tancredi e Clorinda* (1942), *Ein Totentanz zu Basel im Jahre 1943* on Münsterplatz with music by Frank Martin, and in 1945 *Daphne*, *Traum*, and *Jeux*. These later works belong chiefly to Meyenburg’s directorial biography and should not automatically be attributed to Wulff, though they grew from the company she had founded.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Life reform and the “new human”

Wulff’s formative years belonged to the *Lebensreform* and Körperkultur environment that sought changed bodies, clothing, diet, labor, community, education, and art. The Monte Verità school cultivated outdoor dancing, manual work, spatial experiment, and freedom from academic ballet conventions. Wulff carried its pedagogical rather than sectarian core to Basel.

Her adult philosophical position is most securely expressed in the statement used in the later memorial publication: **“Tanz ist die Auseinandersetzung zwischen dem Ich und der Umwelt. Wer tanzt, spürt mehr von sich selbst.”** (“Dance is the engagement between the I and the environment. Whoever dances feels more of themself.”) This joins self-awareness to spatial and social relation.

### Labanian method and independence

Wulff retained Labanian tools—movement analysis, group choruses, spatial geometry, the icosahedron, dance/tone/word integration—but established her own school and survived the dispersal of Laban’s wartime Zürich circle. Her archive’s notes on Laban’s *Choreographie*, her lectures, and the decades of school diaries document a pedagogue adapting a system rather than merely repeating a master.

### Dada

Her Dada involvement was performative and relational. Arp and Taeuber brought her into the scene; she read poetry, participated in evenings, and embodied Dada presentation. She later minimized any idea of institutional fusion between Dada and the Labanschule. Both propositions can be true: the contact was artistically significant and organizationally brief.

### Left-wing and antifascist network

In Basel, Wulff and Vaucher frequented **Club 33**, associated with **Gruppe 33**, an antifascist artists’ organization including Max Sulzbachner, Meret Oppenheim, Paul Artaria, and Hans Schmidt. Wulff’s labor movement-chorus project in 1930 provides an additional left-cultural connection. Vaucher was a Communist Party member. No consulted evidence establishes Wulff’s own party membership, intelligence role, involvement in political violence, or clandestine activity. Her circle was political; her exact formal affiliation remains undocumented.

### Religion and esotericism

Wulff is documented as confessionless. Monte Verità hosted occult, Masonic, O.T.O., nature-religious, and utopian currents, and Laban participated in an O.T.O.-connected 1917 congress and solar festival. Wulff’s proximity to that milieu is historically relevant. Proximity is not evidence of initiation. No membership card, ritual office, or self-identification ties her personally to O.T.O., Freemasonry, Anthroposophy, or a cult.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Rudolf von Laban

Teacher, institutional model, correspondent, and continuing intellectual reference. Letters between them span 1916–1957; Wulff’s relationship was durable despite professional independence.

### Mary Wigman

Teacher and fellow pioneer of expressionist dance. Their correspondence spans 1915–1954. A 1916 letter from Wulff to Wigman also survives. Public sources do not document a later rivalry.

### Suzanne Perrottet

Fellow Laban collaborator and co-founder of the Zürich School for Eurythmy. Wulff’s Italy departure ended the close institutional collaboration; her 1982 recollection says the wartime circle did not remain in contact as a unified school.

### Sophie Taeuber-Arp and Hans/Jean Arp

Taeuber was a fellow Laban dancer and link to Dada; Arp supplied the “heroine of Dada” story. Wulff later corresponded with Hans and Marguerite Arp in the period 1935–1959 and attended an Arp exhibition opening in Basel in 1958.

### Mariette von Meyenburg

Pupil, master pupil, dancer, choreographer, director, and Wulff’s most important artistic collaborator in Basel. Meyenburg shaped most Tanzstudio Wulff repertory from 1926, later led the Basler Kammerballett, and remained close to “Käthe” Wulff even after leaving theater work. The archive does not support a rivalry narrative.

### Charles Ferdinand Vaucher

Long-term partner, husband, collaborator, and political-cultural link. He wrote about the Tanzgruppe Wulff around 1931. Their divorce in 1947 is documented; the reason is not established in the consulted material.

### Paul and Maja Sacher

Paul Sacher and his Basler Kammerorchester accompanied or collaborated on major productions. Maja Sacher was an important supporter of Wulff’s free-dance and lay-education work. The surviving correspondence includes Paul Sacher.

### Max Bill, Binia Bill, Max Sulzbachner, Meret Oppenheim

These visual artists and designers connected the Wulff enterprise to Basel and Swiss modernism. Correspondence with Max and Binia Bill survives; Sulzbachner designed for *Ariadne*; Oppenheim belonged to the Club 33 circle and collaborated in the wider project network. Exact production credits require program-level verification.

### Friedrich Glauser and Rudolf Jakob Humm

Wulff’s archive includes a handwritten 1926 Glauser poem “für Kathia.” She was a friend of writer Rudolf Jakob Humm, with whom correspondence survives. These contacts place her within the Swiss German literary as well as dance avant-garde.

### Students and professional descendants

Documented pupils or younger collaborators include Mariette von Meyenburg, Marie-Eve Kreis, Els Havrlik, Fritz Stebler, Lisa Mutschelknaus, Trix Gutekunst-Tschumi, Annemarie Nadolny, and others recorded in the school files. **Mary Delpy**, a later Basel dancer, left a vivid recollection of Wulff’s regal posture and continued classroom authority beyond age ninety.

## Marriage, War Years, and Postwar Teaching, 1936–1979

Wulff became Swiss/Basel in **1936**, a consequential protection and civic status three years before World War II. She married Vaucher probably in **1939**, after years of cohabitation. Their milieu was openly antifascist, but Wulff remained in neutral Switzerland rather than undergoing a second exile. No Nazi persecution file, wartime arrest, or proscription has been found in the consulted sources.

She appeared at the **1939 Landesausstellung** in Zürich. During and after the war, school prospectuses, advertisements, demonstrations, and reports show continuing work. The school held or planned demonstration evenings in 1939 and 1944/45; its advertising sequence continues through the 1940s and 1950s.

The marriage ended in **1947**. That year Wulff began living and teaching at **Augustinergasse 3**, on Basel’s Münsterhügel. A second 1947 naturalization-related document exists in the archive. It may reflect a status change associated with divorce or civil documentation, but its meaning cannot be inferred safely from the catalogue alone.

Her school diaries continue to **1979**, and reports to the education department to **1961**. A school performance is documented in **1959**. She did not create a large commercial company or university department; her long influence operated through a private studio, repeated teacher formation, lay education, and the persistence of embodied practice.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Financial Questions

### Documented issues

1. **Official recognition disputes (1932, 1938):** Archival files concern the administrative/legal recognition of her school. No evidence found characterizes these as fraud or criminal wrongdoing; the outcome is not stated online.
2. **Financial precarity:** Basel historical writing says Wulff accepted recurrent shortage rather than compromise. Archive files contain costs, tuition income, performance accounts, and a financial plan. No public evidence supports embezzlement, bankruptcy, tax evasion, or investor loss.
3. **Police permits (1928–1934):** Performance permissions survive. Their existence reflects normal public-performance regulation and does not indicate arrest or censorship unless the individual files reveal it.
4. **Communist and antifascist associations:** Her husband belonged to the Communist Party, and the couple frequented the antifascist Gruppe 33/Club 33 milieu. Wulff worked on a labor education and sport cartel movement chorus. No evidence found establishes party membership, espionage, Soviet intelligence work, or political violence by Wulff.
5. **Conventional scandal:** Their long unmarried cohabitation and Wulff’s bohemian appearance conflicted with bourgeois convention. This is social nonconformity, not crime.

### Matters for which no evidence was found

No consulted source documents lawsuits for personal misconduct; criminal charges or convictions; fraud, corruption, money laundering, abuse, addiction, intelligence connections, imprisonment, or political violence. The responsible conclusion is absence of evidence in the available record, not proof that every private event is known.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. The penniless pilgrimage and the box shelter

1. **Story:** In summer 1914 Wulff walked with a backpack to Monte Verità without money, improvised shelter in a large box, and survived the season there.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Repeated in later scholarship on Monte Verità and Wulff; apparently based on retrospective biographical testimony.
3. **Evidence for:** Her 1914 Monte Verità student card and photographs prove attendance; later memories fit the material austerity of the Laban group.
4. **Evidence against/limits:** No contemporaneous diary entry or photograph of the shelter was found online.
5. **Reputational effect:** It became a compressed origin myth of total artistic commitment and rejection of bourgeois security.

### 2. “Heroine of Dada”

1. **Story:** Hidden under a fiery-red paper bag, Wulff calmly finished reading Arp’s poems as hostile spectators advanced, earning Arp’s title “Heldin Dadas.”
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Arp’s retrospective *Dadaland*, later collected in 1955; repeated in Dada scholarship and Basel commemoration.
3. **Evidence for:** Arp was a participant; Wulff’s Dada activity is independently documented.
4. **Evidence against/limits:** The theatrical wording is memoir and self-mythology; exact crowd behavior is not independently reconstructed here.
5. **Reputational effect:** It made an otherwise pedagogy-centered life legible within the canonical Dada story.

### 3. A unified Laban–Dada movement

1. **Story:** Laban’s dancers and Zürich Dada formed an integrated avant-garde school.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Later exhibitions and studies emphasize their exchange because dancers appeared at Dada events and artists shared stages, friendships, and spaces.
3. **Evidence for:** Numerous contacts and performances are real; Arp, Taeuber, Wulff, Perrottet, Wigman, and Laban intersected.
4. **Evidence against/limits:** Wulff explicitly rejected the claim of unified schools in 1982, describing the connection as occasional and ephemeral.
5. **Reputational effect:** The synthesis has raised Wulff’s visibility but can subordinate her autonomous, decades-long Basel pedagogy to a brief Zürich episode.

### 4. Monte Verità esoteric initiation

1. **Story:** Because Wulff danced in Laban’s Monte Verità circle, she is sometimes absorbed into narratives of Masonic/O.T.O. ritual, solar cult, nudism, and occult initiation.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Modern accounts of Laban’s 1917 solar festival and the O.T.O. congress encourage the association.
3. **Evidence for:** The milieu and festival are documented; Wulff belonged to the dancer network.
4. **Evidence against/limits:** No evidence found that Wulff was initiated into O.T.O., Freemasonry, or another order. HLS describes her as confessionless.
5. **Reputational effect:** It lends her life an esoteric aura, but converting shared artistic space into personal membership exceeds the evidence.

### 5. “Carried the school to Basel”

1. **Story:** Wulff transplanted Laban’s Monte Verità/Zürich school intact to Basel.
2. **Evidence:** She used the Labanschule name, Laban credentials, spatial method, movement choruses, and teacher training.
3. **Qualification:** Her Basel institution was independent, economically local, collaborative with Basel modernists, focused strongly on lay pupils, and increasingly shaped by Wulff’s own decades of practice. “Carried the school” is accurate as lineage, misleading if taken to mean a branch controlled by Laban.

### 6. Marriage year and age at death

- **Marriage:** 1939 in HLS and theater reference material; 1937 in some derivative summaries. The stronger version is 1939, pending civil-certificate inspection.
- **Age:** Exact dates yield 101; “102” is a recurrent calendar-year miscount.

## Works by Katja Wulff

Wulff was not primarily a book author. Much of her work exists as choreography, pedagogy, unpublished manuscripts, lectures, journalism, drawings, poems, and school documentation. The following is the fullest defensible catalogue from the public finding aid.

### Published and journalistic writing

- Newspaper articles by Wulff dated **1916**, **1932–1934**, **1938**, and **1958** survive in her archive. Titles and publication venues are not listed in the online catalogue and should not be invented.
- A **1958 recollection of Rudolf von Laban** survives, whether as article or manuscript depending on the item-level record.
- Statements and recollections in the **1982 Raimund Meyer interview** on Dada in Zürich; later used in Meyer’s *Dada in Zürich: Die Akteure, die Schauplätze* (1990).

### Unpublished manuscripts, notebooks, and pedagogical works

- Writings on dance, movement, body formation, pedagogy, and Rudolf von Laban.
- Account of her training under Laban and Mary Wigman in Zürich, 1916–1918.
- Writing on the idea of the Labanschule and her cooperation with Laban.
- Lecture manuscripts on body formation, movement training, gymnastics schools, and teaching methods.
- Lecture on dance pedagogy for the 1928 Essen dancers’ congress.
- Concept and lecture drafts for the Zürich School for Eurythmy, approximately 1918–1920.
- Demonstration-evening lecture drafts for the Wulffschule: 1923, 1925–26, 1928–29, 1931, 1933–34, 1936–37, 1939, 1944/45, and undated.
- Handwritten school diaries, 1925–1979.
- Annual reports to the Basel education department, 1933–1961.
- Examination plans, 1928, 1936, 1948.
- A 1927 movement-chorus design.
- Choreographic notes and designs for dance works.
- Explanations and sketches, partly with pupils, responding to Laban’s *Choreographie: erstes Heft* (1926).
- Repertoire lists, press releases, advertisements, prospectuses, school regulations, and curricular/administrative texts.
- A financial concept for her school and dance group.
- Travel diary for the Netherlands and travel notes for England, undated.

### Creative works on paper

- Poems by Wulff, unpublished in the identified catalogue.
- Drawings, including dance sketches and portraits of Mary Wigman and Rudolf von Laban.
- Probable drawings in the fonds whose authorship is not fully certain.

### Choreography and performance works

Wulff’s name is institutionally attached to the Tanzstudio Wulff repertory listed above, but Mariette von Meyenburg choreographed or directed most major company works after 1926. A precise scholarly catalogue should distinguish:

- works choreographed by Wulff;
- works jointly conceived by Wulff and Meyenburg;
- works choreographed by Meyenburg for Wulff’s company;
- school demonstrations directed pedagogically by Wulff;
- roles Wulff personally danced.

The public catalogue is not granular enough to make that division for every title. No commercial film, sound recording, patent, or published technical invention by Wulff was found. The Laban icosahedron in her archive is a pedagogical model, not her patented invention.

### Correspondence

Substantial surviving correspondence includes letters to family (1914, 1916–1919), Laban, Wigman, the Arps, and business correspondents, and incoming letters from leading dance figures. These are genuine primary works by or to Wulff but, so far as established here, not published as a complete scholarly edition.

## Illness, Later Life, and Death

Wulff continued to teach into her **nineties**. Mary Delpy remembered her as tall, beautifully and discreetly dressed, with a nearly regal walk, straight back, and perfect posture. Even when former pupils taught, Wulff sat beside them, intervened, and commented. This memory emphasizes a transfer from demonstration to supervisory authority.

Her final years were reportedly spent **confined to bed**. Public sources do not identify the illness, injury, diagnosis, medication, addiction, dementia, or psychological crisis responsible. She died in Basel’s **Felix Platter Hospital** on 11 June 1992. No consulted source supplies a medical cause of death. She was cremated and her urn buried at Friedhof am Hörnli.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Wulff’s historical position rests on four linked but distinct achievements:

1. **Witness and participant in the Monte Verità/Laban experiment.** She belonged to the small cohort that transformed prewar movement reform into modern European dance practice.
2. **Performer within Zürich Dada.** The red-bag reading and Arp’s “heroine” phrase preserve her in Dada memory, though she rejected claims of a unified Dada–Laban school.
3. **Institution builder in Basel.** From 1923 she created the local infrastructure for free dance, teacher training, lay movement education, children’s classes, and experimental performance.
4. **Pedagogical longevity.** Her diaries run to 1979 and testimony places her teaching beyond ninety. Her influence passed through bodies and classrooms more than through a canonical book.

Her reputation was long overshadowed by Laban, Wigman, Taeuber-Arp, and the better-documented choreographic work of Meyenburg. Later recovery has taken several forms:

- the preservation of her extensive estate in Cologne;
- biographical work by Frank-Manuel Peter, Bettina Zeugin, Simon Baur, and Swiss theater historians;
- the 2001 Basel initiative **“Wer hat Katja Wulff tanzen gesehen?”**, with performances outside Augustinergasse 3;
- a memorial plaque at **Augustinergasse 3**, stating that Wulff lived and taught there and recalling her pioneering role in Basel free dance;
- renewed Monte Verità and Dada exhibitions and scholarship;
- inclusion in Basel city-history accounts of women’s work, artistic nonconformity, and antifascist networks.

Her archive also revises the romantic picture of the solitary free dancer. It shows a school director producing budgets, permits, curricula, examinations, legal correspondence, advertisements, and annual reports for decades. The “heroine of Dada” and the austere Monte Verità wanderer are real parts of the record, but the institutional labor is the larger historical achievement.

## Chronology

- **31 Aug. 1890:** Born in Hamburg to Hugo Friedrich Wulff and Marie Elisabetha Amalia Döring Wulff.
- **1912:** Receives a drawing/gymnastics teaching diploma; begins or is already engaged in teaching.
- **1913/14:** Studies dance with Gertrud Falke.
- **Summer 1914:** Attends Laban’s Tanz–Ton–Wort course at Monte Verità; appears in documented group photographs.
- **1914:** Additional teacher diploma/certificate; correspondence from the Monte Verità period begins.
- **1915:** End of the usual published date range for her Hamburg teaching work; earliest preserved Wigman correspondence dates from this year.
- **1916:** Moves to Zürich; signs training contract with Laban’s Schule für Bewegungskunst; begins intensive study under Laban and Mary Wigman; writes to family, Laban, and Wigman.
- **1916–17:** Participates in Tanz–Ton–Wort/Labanschule activities including *Nordische Runen*; intersects with Zürich Dada.
- **1917:** Writes to sister Cläre about the liberating effect of cutting her hair; program records document the Zürich Laban school.
- **1918:** Receives diploma in *tänzerischem Turnen und Kunsttanz*; co-founds the Schule für Eurythmie with Suzanne Perrottet.
- **1918–20:** Develops Zürich school curriculum, rules, courses, diplomas, and demonstrations; school later changes name to avoid confusion with Steiner Eurythmy.
- **1919:** Final phase of Zürich Dada/Laban interaction; Wulff later describes it as valuable but ephemeral.
- **c. 1920–23:** Teaches on Capri and the Amalfi coast.
- **1921, 1923:** Italian residence certificates survive.
- **1923:** Settles in Basel; founds her expressionist-dance/Laban school.
- **1924:** Earliest surviving Basel school prospectus; correspondence with Laban resumes/continues.
- **1925:** School diaries begin; early school demonstrations.
- **1926:** Founds/directs Tanzstudio Wulff with Mariette von Meyenburg; *Altfranzösische Tänze und Gesänge* and *Der Tod zu Basel*; signs a lease; receives Glauser’s poem “für Kathia.”
- **27 Feb. 1927:** Major Stadttheater Basel debut with Casella dances and *Barabau*, accompanied by Paul Sacher’s chamber orchestra.
- **1927:** Magdeburg dancers’ congress; movement-chorus design; study evenings begin.
- **1928:** Essen dancers’ congress and Wulff lecture on dance pedagogy; SAFFA participation; specialist-school certificate; company production accounts and police permits begin.
- **1929:** Plans and costs prepared for a new school building; repertory includes works associated with Rameau and Satie.
- **1930:** Munich dancers’ congress; *Dissonancen*, *Parade*, *Relâche*, and *The Love for Three Oranges* in repertory; collaboration with Arbeiter-Bildungs- und Sportkartell movement choruses.
- **1931:** *Ariadne* and *Dido and Aeneas*; Wulffschule participates in Hyspa; Zürich theater guest contract for 1931/32.
- **1932:** *Mercure* with Edwin Denby; first documented official-recognition dispute concerning the school.
- **1933:** Annual reports to Basel education authorities begin.
- **1934:** *Ouverture* and *Ballo* material; school/company publicity continues.
- **Mid-1930s:** Wulff increasingly leaves performing to pupils and concentrates on pedagogy and organization.
- **1935:** Revival/new documentation of *Mercure*; correspondence with the Arps in the later 1930s.
- **1936:** Naturalized in Basel/Switzerland; examination cycle; *Höfische Tänze* with Schola Cantorum Basiliensis and *La tragédie d’une jeune-fille*.
- **1938:** Second documented recognition-related legal/administrative matter.
- **1939:** Probable marriage to Charles Ferdinand Vaucher; dance presentation at the Swiss National Exhibition in Zürich.
- **1942–45:** Tanzstudio Wulff’s successor environment becomes Basler Kammerballett under Meyenburg; Wulffschool activity continues.
- **1947:** Divorce from Vaucher; Wulff begins living and teaching at Augustinergasse 3; a second naturalization-related document survives.
- **1948:** School examination plans; school administration continues.
- **1957:** Last dated Laban-to-Wulff correspondence in the fonds.
- **1958:** Writes recollections of Laban; photographed at Hans Arp exhibition opening in Basel.
- **1959:** Documented school performance; letters to the Arps continue to about this year.
- **1961:** Last annual school report/promotion dates represented in specified archive series.
- **1972:** End of some indexed professional correspondence; death of former husband Charles Ferdinand Vaucher.
- **1979:** Last year of the surviving handwritten school diaries.
- **1982:** Interviewed by Raimund Meyer about Zürich Dada and the Laban school.
- **1980s:** Still teaches/supervises students beyond age ninety.
- **1986:** Included in Vera Isler’s portraits of people over eighty.
- **1990:** Frank-Manuel Peter publishes centenary article.
- **11 June 1992:** Dies in Felix Platter Hospital, Basel, aged 101.
- **2001:** Bettina Zeugin/IG Tanz Basel publication; “Wer hat Katja Wulff tanzen gesehen?” performances and memorial activity at Augustinergasse 3.
- **2021:** Basel city-history project publishes a substantial public reconstruction.
- **2022:** Her grave at Friedhof am Hörnli is reported removed.
