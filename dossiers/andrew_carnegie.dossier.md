# Andrew Carnegie — Research Dossier

**Subject:** Andrew Carnegie (1835–1919), Scottish-American industrialist, steel manufacturer, investor, writer, philanthropist, political activist, and peace campaigner  
**Prepared:** 19 August 2026  
**Method:** chronological reconstruction using Carnegie's own writings; manuscript and institutional finding aids; surviving business correspondence; government and court records; contemporaneous newspapers; institutional histories; documentary transcripts; and major historical scholarship. Encyclopedic summaries were used only for orientation where noted and not as the principal evidentiary basis.

## Evidentiary conventions

This dossier uses the following labels where controversy requires them:

- **Documented fact:** supported by primary records, contemporaneous documentation, or convergent strong secondary sources.
- **Self-report:** a claim made by Carnegie in his autobiography, correspondence, speeches, or reminiscences; valuable evidence but not automatically independent confirmation.
- **Contemporaneous allegation:** a claim published or formally alleged at the time, without implying proof.
- **Investigation finding:** a conclusion or factual determination made by an investigating body; distinct from a criminal conviction or civil judgment.
- **Pleading/allegation in litigation:** an assertion made by a litigant, not a judicial finding unless identified as such.
- **Disputed interpretation:** historians or sources differ materially about meaning, responsibility, motive, or scale.
- **Myth / legend / later anecdote:** a story important to reputation whose factual basis is incomplete, exaggerated, or dependent on retrospective retelling.

Amounts are nominal historical dollars unless otherwise stated. Modern-dollar conversions are avoided because different inflation, income-share, and GDP-share measures produce radically different equivalents.

### Name and pronunciation

The surname is the Scottish **Carnegie**. Carnegie himself belonged to a Dunfermline family of handloom weavers and radicals, not to the titled Carnegie line sometimes imagined from the name. His own autobiographical mythology stressed pride in descent from “honest weavers,” contrasting that ancestry with hereditary aristocracy.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Father — William Carnegie (born c. 1804 in secondary genealogies; died 1855).** William was a skilled handloom damask weaver in Dunfermline. Andrew's autobiography and later biographical sources describe him as politically radical, active in the local weavers' movement, sympathetic to Chartism, and a contributor of letters to radical periodicals. The mechanization of weaving and arrival of steam-powered looms undermined the household craft on which the family depended. After migration to Pennsylvania, William found factory work but never achieved the economic security his son later did. He died in 1855.

**Mother — Margaret Morrison Carnegie (c. 1810–1886).** Margaret was one of the decisive figures in Carnegie's life. In Dunfermline she supplemented family income by running a small shop and binding or mending shoes. She pushed for emigration when the household's economic position collapsed. In Pennsylvania she continued earning money and helped support her sons. Andrew remained exceptionally close to her; for years they shared hotel accommodations in New York and traveled together. Modern biographies and PBS's documentary materials describe her as an obstacle to Andrew's long courtship of Louise Whitfield. Carnegie and Louise became secretly engaged in 1883 but did not marry until after Margaret's death on 10 November 1886.

### Grandparents and radical inheritance

Carnegie's maternal grandfather, **Thomas Morrison**, was a leather merchant/tanner and a prominent local radical. Carnegie remembered him as a publisher of *The Precursor*, a contributor to William Cobbett's *Political Register*, and an advocate of popular education. Morrison's phrase and essay theme “Head-ication versus Hand-ication” became part of Carnegie's family memory of intellectual self-improvement. Carnegie's maternal grandmother came from the Hodge family of Edinburgh.

His paternal grandfather, **Andrew Carnegie**, was remembered within the family by the joking title “Professor,” associated with the local Patiemuir social world. Carnegie's autobiography transforms both grandfathers into representatives of a vernacular Scottish culture of argument, song, storytelling, democracy, and skepticism toward aristocratic privilege.

### Siblings

Carnegie also had an **infant sister, Anne/Ann Carnegie**, who died in childhood before the family emigrated. The exact birth/death dating is inconsistently reproduced in secondary genealogical sources (often 1840–1841), so those exact years should be treated cautiously. Her existence is independently reflected in the Tiffany memorial window Carnegie commissioned in 1913 for his parents, brother Thomas, and “sister Anne,” documented by the Carnegie Hero Fund.

His younger brother, **Thomas Morrison Carnegie** (2 October 1843 – 19 October 1886), emigrated with the family and later became an important partner in the Carnegie enterprises. National Park Service material reconstructs Thomas's role in the family companies and his marriage to **Lucy Coleman**, daughter of iron manufacturer William Coleman. Thomas and Lucy had nine children. In the 1881 Carnegie Brothers & Company ownership structure summarized by the NPS, Andrew held roughly 54.5 percent while Thomas and Henry Phipps each held about 17.6 percent, with smaller holdings distributed to other partners. Thomas also participated in the expanding H. C. Frick coke interests. He died of pneumonia in 1886, shortly before his mother. Some biographical sources describe heavy drinking as a serious problem in Thomas's adult life; this is a claim about Thomas, not Andrew, and is not evidence that Andrew had an alcohol addiction.

### Cousins and extended kin

**George Lauder (“Dod”)**, Carnegie's cousin and childhood companion in Dunfermline, remained close throughout life and became a significant business associate. The two boys' shared childhood stories—Dunfermline Abbey, Pittencrieff Glen, Scottish history, mechanical experiments, and family radicalism—became recurring material in Carnegie's autobiography. George Lauder later participated in Carnegie's industrial enterprises and technological development.

An uncle **George Lauder**, father of Carnegie's cousin, was another formative figure. Carnegie remembered him as a teacher of Scottish history, mechanics, poetry, and patriotic memory. The **Lauder Technical College** in Dunfermline was later named in his honor.

### Wife: Louise Whitfield Carnegie

**Louise Whitfield** (1857–1946) was the daughter of a prosperous New York merchant family. She met Carnegie when he was about forty-five and she was in her early twenties. Their courtship began around 1880 and became a secret engagement in 1883. They married on **22 April 1887** at the Whitfield family home in New York in a small private ceremony.

The marriage was accompanied by formal financial arrangements. Contemporary and later accounts describe a prenuptial or marriage settlement under which Louise relinquished claims to the bulk of Carnegie's fortune while receiving property and securities establishing an independent income. This arrangement is relevant to Carnegie's personal financial structure: his philanthropic doctrine did not mean that his wife and later daughter were simply left without provision.

Louise was not merely a passive spouse. She traveled with Carnegie, participated in the management and social life of Skibo, supported philanthropic activities, preserved his manuscript papers, and wrote the preface to the 1920 posthumous *Autobiography*. Her preface is an important first-hand source for Carnegie's condition after the outbreak of the First World War.

### Daughter

**Margaret Cameron Carnegie**, born **30 March 1897** in New York, was Andrew and Louise's only child. She married **Roswell Miller Jr.** on 22 April 1919 at the Carnegie mansion, only months before her father's death. She later served for decades as a trustee of Carnegie Corporation of New York. A 1980 recorded interview with Margaret survives in Columbia University's Carnegie Corporation archives.

## Childhood and Early Life

### 1835–1847: Dunfermline

Carnegie was born into a skilled-artisan household, not into destitution at birth. The family's position deteriorated as industrialized textile production displaced handloom weaving. Dunfermline's damask-weaving tradition had supported independent craftsmen, but power looms transformed costs and labor relations. Carnegie's later “rags-to-riches” story is therefore most accurately understood as **downward mobility followed by migration and upward mobility** rather than unchanging childhood poverty.

The family environment was intensely political. Carnegie's father and maternal relatives participated in the culture around Chartism, working-class enfranchisement, republicanism, and popular education. PBS's biographical reconstruction identifies William Carnegie and Andrew's uncle Tom Morrison with Dunfermline Chartism and notes the family's experience of the 1842 general strike. In Carnegie's own retrospective account, aristocratic titles were objects of suspicion while the United States represented democratic possibility.

The cultural inheritance was equally important. Burns, Scottish songs, local history, the Abbey, stories of Wallace and Bruce, and debates among working men furnished a kind of informal curriculum. Carnegie later turned Pittencrieff Glen—the private estate from which members of the Morrison family had symbolically been excluded—into a public park, consciously converting a childhood memory of exclusion into civic benefaction.

### Schooling

Carnegie attended local schooling in Dunfermline, identified by his autobiography's editor as the **Rolland School**. His formal education ended with emigration at age twelve. He later took limited evening instruction, including a winter of night school and lessons in French and public speaking, but he never attended college.

This fact became central to his self-presentation. Carnegie repeatedly emphasized self-education: reading, telegraphy, conversation with older men, literary clubs, travel, statistics, and association with intellectuals. The gap between minimal formal schooling and later patronage of universities and libraries was deliberately incorporated into his public philosophy.

### 1848: collapse of the weaving economy and emigration

By the winter of 1847–48, the family's finances had become precarious. Margaret favored joining relatives already in America. The household auctioned belongings but still needed outside help. Carnegie's own account and PBS's reconstruction state that the family borrowed **£20** to complete the passage.

They left Dunfermline on **17 May 1848**, traveled to Glasgow, and crossed the Atlantic aboard the sailing ship **Wiscasset**. PBS estimates the passage at roughly fifty days. After New York they traveled north and west through the Hudson/Erie Canal system, Buffalo, and onward toward western Pennsylvania. They settled in **Allegheny**, then a separate municipality across the river from Pittsburgh.

The migration itself became one of Carnegie's foundational stories: technologically displaced Scottish artisan family, transatlantic passage, grim industrial Pittsburgh, then ascent through literacy and technology.

## Education and Formation

### Work as education

Carnegie's first American work was industrial. His father obtained employment in a cotton factory, and Andrew became a **bobbin boy** at about **$1.20 per week**. The work involved long hours and tasks he later remembered as physically and emotionally difficult. Short biographies sometimes present this as the whole of his “factory childhood,” but he moved relatively quickly into telegraph work.

He subsequently worked as a **telegraph messenger**, learning Pittsburgh's streets, businesses, and influential residents. Telegraphy placed him at an information crossroads. He learned to operate the equipment and became unusually adept at reading signals by sound. That technical skill transformed a messenger's job into an operator's position and helped bring him to the attention of railroad executive **Thomas A. Scott**.

### Colonel James Anderson's library

A local benefactor, **Colonel James Anderson**, opened his private library—about four hundred volumes in Carnegie's recollection—to working boys on Saturdays. Carnegie later credited access to those books as a decisive experience behind his public-library program.

The story has a second component that illustrates his early appetite for public argument. When access rules appeared to exclude messenger boys because they lacked a recognized “trade,” Carnegie wrote to the newspaper under the name **“Working Boy,”** then replied again as **“A Working Boy, though without a Trade.”** The rule was changed or clarified. Carnegie's autobiography's editor traced the story to telegraph historian David Homer Bates.

This episode is simultaneously documentary history and self-created mythology: it became the ideal origin story for the future library benefactor—working boy, denied access, argues publicly, gains books, later gives libraries to others.

### Reading and intellectual self-education

Among the authors Carnegie remembered reading were **Thomas Babington Macaulay, George Bancroft, Charles Lamb**, and the major English and Scottish literary canon. Telegraph deliveries to theaters also let him see Shakespeare and other drama. Later he cultivated literary salons and clubs in New York, especially the **Nineteenth Century Club**, where public debate became additional training.

### Mentors

**Thomas A. Scott** was the crucial early patron. Scott hired Carnegie in 1853 as clerk/private telegrapher at **$35 per month** and taught him railroad management, finance, and investment. Scott's combination of railroad administration and private business ventures gave Carnegie a model of how managerial information, corporate relationships, and personal investment could reinforce one another.

**J. Edgar Thomson**, president of the Pennsylvania Railroad, became another major business relationship and investor. Carnegie later named the Edgar Thomson steelworks after him.

**James D. Reid**, a telegraph figure, helped teach Carnegie telegraphy. **David McCargo**, **Robert Pitcairn**, and other railroad colleagues appear repeatedly in his reminiscences.

**Andrew Dickson White**, Cornell's founding president and later diplomat, became a lifelong friend and adviser after meeting Carnegie in New York social-intellectual circles. White was especially important to Carnegie's later international arbitration and Peace Palace projects.

### Herbert Spencer and Darwinian development

Carnegie read **Herbert Spencer** before he knew him personally and explicitly called himself Spencer's “disciple.” He met Spencer in Britain and traveled with him aboard the *Servia* in 1882. Spencer's evolutionary social philosophy and anti-militarism profoundly shaped Carnegie's worldview. Darwinian evolution and Spencerian synthesis helped Carnegie move away from inherited Christian theology toward a more naturalistic, non-dogmatic outlook.

Carnegie did not simply become an atheist polemicist. His later language continued to invoke God, moral duty, service, immortality as a cultural question, and Protestant moral vocabulary, while rejecting conventional doctrines he regarded as incompatible with science. His mature position is best described as religiously heterodox, evolutionary, and ethically theistic/agnostic rather than easily assigned to one creed.

## Early Career

### Telegraph and railroad: 1849–1865

Carnegie's movement from cotton work to telegraphy was the first major occupational break. Telegraph service gave him speed, informational advantage, and social access. In **1853**, Thomas A. Scott recruited him to the Pennsylvania Railroad. Carnegie became Scott's secretary and telegrapher, then advanced through operations.

By **1859**, he succeeded Scott as superintendent of the Pennsylvania Railroad's Pittsburgh Division. The role gave him responsibility for traffic, personnel, accident response, scheduling, and large-scale infrastructure. It also exposed him to the economics of bridges, rails, rolling stock, fuel, and finance—the industries he would later integrate into his own enterprises.

### First investments: Adams Express and sleeping cars

Carnegie's autobiographical account identifies an investment in **Adams Express Company** as a psychological turning point. Scott offered him ten shares; the family raised money against its house, and Carnegie remembered the first dividend as revelation—capital could generate income without direct labor. His later “goose that lays the golden eggs” story is self-report, but the underlying investment is well documented in the biographical record.

He also invested in **Theodore T. Woodruff's sleeping-car enterprise**. Carnegie borrowed to make his initial payment and helped connect the sleeping-car technology to the Pennsylvania Railroad. Sleeping-car income became one of his early significant revenue streams before steel.

### Insider relationships and modern conflict-of-interest questions

Carnegie, Scott, and Thomson invested in enterprises that did business with the Pennsylvania Railroad. Carnegie's later institutional biography published by the Carnegie organization itself acknowledges that Scott and Thomson were secret partners in companies receiving railroad contracts. Carnegie's Keystone bridge and iron enterprises also benefited from Pennsylvania Railroad business.

**Documented fact:** Carnegie's railroad management position, personal investments, and supplier relationships overlapped.  
**Historical/legal qualification:** nineteenth-century corporate-governance rules and disclosure norms differed from modern standards. The record supports describing these arrangements as insider dealing or conflicts by modern governance standards; it does **not** by itself establish a criminal offense by Carnegie under the law of the period.

### Oil

Carnegie and associates acquired an interest in the **Storey Farm** oil property in western Pennsylvania during the early petroleum boom. His autobiography describes a roughly **$40,000** purchase/option and later enormous returns; editor John C. Van Dyke noted that the wells at one point paid about **$1 million** in cash and dividends in a year and that the property was later valued on a stock basis at several million dollars. Carnegie also describes a failed attempt to capture still greater profits by storing petroleum while prices moved against him. The episode demonstrates that his pre-steel fortune was diversified across oil, rail-related investments, bridge building, and securities.

### Civil War service, 1861

When Scott was placed in charge of federal military railroads and telegraphs, Carnegie accompanied him to Washington and helped organize transportation and communications. His autobiography describes work repairing rail and telegraph lines, rebuilding connections toward Alexandria, and assisting the movement of wounded men after First Bull Run. He met or observed senior political figures including **Abraham Lincoln, William H. Seward, and Simon Cameron**.

Carnegie liked to recount that a telegraph wire cut his cheek while he was restoring a line, making his blood the first shed in the Civil War. This belongs in the **self-created legend** category. The cut may well have occurred; the claim to “first blood” or “first casualty” is rhetorical, not a recognized military-historical finding.

### Exit from the Pennsylvania Railroad

By the mid-1860s Carnegie's outside income exceeded his salary. He resigned from the Pennsylvania Railroad in **1865** to concentrate on manufacturing and finance. This was not a sudden leap from employee to steel king: he already possessed partnerships, securities, industrial knowledge, capital access, and a powerful network of railroad executives and financiers.

## Chronological Life History

### 1861–1867: forge, bridges, iron, and the transition from railroad manager to industrialist

Carnegie's first sustained industrial interests predated the Civil War's end. In **1861** he was associated with the **Sun City Forge Company**. In 1862 he and partners organized a bridge enterprise identified in his autobiography as **Piper & Schiffler**; in **1863** it developed into the **Keystone Bridge Company**. Carnegie claimed to have supplied the name. The central technological proposition was simple and consequential: replace combustible and failure-prone wooden railroad bridges with iron structures.

His bridge interests connected directly with his railroad knowledge and relationships. Keystone supplied railroad projects and participated in technically ambitious bridge work. Carnegie's network included engineer **James B. Eads**, whose St. Louis bridge across the Mississippi became a major demonstration of large-scale metal construction. The Eads Bridge, completed in 1874, is sometimes folded into the “Carnegie invented steel” story; more accurately, Carnegie's companies and financing participated in a larger engineering system involving Eads and many specialists.

Carnegie invested in **Cyclops Iron Works** in 1864. In **1867**, Cyclops and another set of interests were reorganized as **Union Iron Mills**. His brother Thomas and Henry Phipps became increasingly important in Pittsburgh operations while Andrew specialized in policy, financing, sales, and the cultivation of eastern and European capital.

### 1867: New York becomes headquarters

In **1867** Carnegie left Pittsburgh as his primary residence and moved with his mother to New York City. They first stayed at the **St. Nicholas Hotel**; Carnegie opened an office on Broad Street. They later moved to the **Windsor Hotel**, which remained Carnegie's New York home until his 1887 marriage.

The move gave him direct access to Wall Street, transatlantic finance, publishers, political figures, writers, and the city's intellectual clubs. He traveled back to Pittsburgh frequently, while Thomas Carnegie and Henry Phipps handled much of the manufacturing detail.

Carnegie claimed that he deliberately rejected ordinary stock-market speculation even as he became an aggressive industrial financier. His autobiography recounts refusing an approach from **Jay Gould** to join an effort to control the Pennsylvania Railroad. This is a self-report, but it fits a genuine long-term distinction in Carnegie's business philosophy between productive manufacturing investment and speculative trading.

### December 1868: the “retire at thirty-five” memorandum

At the St. Nicholas Hotel in December 1868, Carnegie wrote a private memorandum that later became one of the most revealing documents in his mythology. At thirty-three and with an income he put at about **$50,000 per year**, he declared that he should never seek more than that annual income, should spend surplus on benevolent purposes, should leave business at thirty-five, study at Oxford, enter public affairs, and perhaps acquire a newspaper.

The memo condemns wealth accumulation as a form of idolatry and worries that the struggle to make money will degrade him. The documentary point is not that Carnegie followed the plan—he emphatically did not. For roughly three more decades he pursued scale and profit with extraordinary intensity. Its importance lies in showing that his later “Gospel of Wealth” did not appear suddenly after retirement. The ethical unease about accumulation and the ambition to become a public intellectual were already explicit by 1868.

### 1869–1872: finance, bonds, and European reconnaissance

Carnegie became increasingly important as a **bond salesman and international intermediary**. In financing bridge and railroad ventures, he cultivated London capital. His dealings brought him into contact with **Junius Spencer Morgan**, father of J. P. Morgan. Carnegie learned that large industrial projects could be financed through transatlantic securities markets, a competence that distinguished him from a manufacturer confined to the shop floor.

He also traveled repeatedly in Britain and Europe, combining business reconnaissance with literary and political socializing. Britain was simultaneously ancestral homeland, technological competitor, capital market, and political laboratory.

### 1872–1875: the Bessemer decision and Edgar Thomson Works

Carnegie did **not** invent the Bessemer process. **Henry Bessemer** in Britain developed and patented the process that bears his name, while **William Kelly** pursued a related pneumatic method in the United States. Carnegie's contribution was entrepreneurial adoption, financing, organization, scale, and continuous improvement.

During British travel Carnegie observed the rapid expansion of Bessemer steel and concluded that steel rails would displace iron. Engineer and steelmaker **John A. Wright** was among those who reinforced the opportunity. On **1 January 1873**, Carnegie and associates organized a steel-rail enterprise whose partners included **William Coleman, David McCandless, John Scott, David A. Stewart, J. Edgar Thomson**, and Thomas A. Scott, alongside Carnegie interests.

Construction of the **Edgar Thomson Steel Works** at Braddock, Pennsylvania, began in the 1870s, with production beginning in **1875**. The works were named for J. Edgar Thomson, both a mentor and a powerful railroad customer. The plant embodied Carnegie's emerging operating system:

1. adopt the most productive proven technology rather than defend sunk capital;
2. build at large scale;
3. measure costs intensely;
4. reinvest earnings rather than distribute all profits;
5. recruit exceptionally capable technical managers;
6. bargain aggressively for freight, fuel, and raw materials;
7. integrate upstream and downstream operations where advantageous.

### The engineers and managers behind “Carnegie steel”

The popular singular noun “Carnegie” obscures a technical collective. Important figures included:

- **Alexander Lyman Holley**, America's leading Bessemer engineer, whose work was critical to the transfer and adaptation of Bessemer technology;
- **Captain William R. “Bill” Jones**, superintendent at Edgar Thomson and one of the era's most celebrated practical steelmakers;
- **Julian Kennedy**, mechanical engineer and designer of mill equipment;
- **Charles M. Schwab**, who rose from the works to executive leadership;
- **George Lauder**, Carnegie's cousin and partner, involved in technological and organizational development;
- **Henry Phipps**, financial and administrative partner;
- **Thomas M. Carnegie**, operational partner until his death;
- **Henry Clay Frick**, whose coke empire and administrative force became essential to vertical integration.

Carnegie's own role was less that of inventor than of **capital allocator, recruiter, negotiator, cost disciplinarian, salesman, strategist, and controlling partner**.

### Cost accounting and continuous rebuilding

Carnegie's famous business maxim was to watch costs and allow profits to follow. The mills developed detailed accounting systems that compared departments, heats, tonnage, inputs, and unit costs. Managers were pushed to rebuild functioning facilities when new machinery promised lower costs. This willingness to make yesterday's investment obsolete helped the Carnegie companies survive falling steel prices.

The cost regime also had a labor dimension. Productivity gains, mechanization, work-rule disputes, workforce reductions, wage scales tied to prices, long shifts, and antiunion strategy were not separate from the low-cost system; they were part of it.

### 1870s–1880s: consolidation and vertical integration

Carnegie reorganized partnerships repeatedly rather than building a single modern corporation from the outset. Important entities included **Carnegie, McCandless & Company**, **Carnegie, Phipps & Company**, **Carnegie Brothers & Company**, Keystone Bridge, Union Iron Mills, and related coke, ore, transport, and steel interests.

**Carnegie Brothers & Company**, organized in **1881**, concentrated family and partner ownership. The same year Carnegie deepened his relationship with **H. C. Frick & Company**, the dominant Connellsville coke producer. Coke was indispensable for blast furnaces. Carnegie capital gave Frick expansion resources; the Carnegie interests progressively acquired control. NPS reconstruction of Thomas Carnegie's business life shows the Carnegie share of the coke company rising markedly during the 1880s.

This was vertical integration in practice: control not only rolling mills, but ore, coke, blast furnaces, transport links, and sales. Later Carnegie Steel also controlled ore resources and shipping/rail connections through relationships and acquisitions.

### 1881: first Carnegie library

Carnegie's first famous library gift went to his birthplace, **Dunfermline**, in **1881**. The location mattered symbolically: the working-class Scottish town from which his family had emigrated became the first major theater of his public benefaction. The gift established the pattern later repeated on a massive scale: capital for a civic building, with the local community responsible for sustaining it.

### Travel writing and the making of a public persona

Carnegie increasingly appeared not merely as a businessman but as a writer and transatlantic commentator. His travel books include *Notes of a Trip Round the World* (1880), *Our Coaching Trip: Brighton to Inverness* (1882), the related/expanded *An American Four-in-Hand in Britain* (1883; later editions often dated 1884 or 1886), and *Round the World* (1884).

These books mixed travel observation, humor, politics, technological comparison, Scottish sentiment, and republican commentary. They also enlarged his network among British politicians and writers.

### 1882: Herbert Spencer and the intellectual network

Carnegie traveled with Herbert Spencer aboard the **Servia** from Liverpool to New York in 1882. Spencer was already an intellectual authority for him. Carnegie's later recollections present the philosopher familiarly—irritable over cheese, anxious over speeches, witty in conversation—while simultaneously crediting him as a teacher.

Through New York and British salons Carnegie developed relationships with **Matthew Arnold, John Morley, William Ewart Gladstone, Andrew D. White**, and others. His social network became unusually wide for an industrialist, spanning engineers, financiers, presidents, prime ministers, writers, university leaders, and reformers.

### 1883–1887: Louise Whitfield, secret engagement, family deaths, marriage

Carnegie and Louise Whitfield's relationship deepened while he remained intensely attached to his mother. They became secretly engaged in **September 1883**. In **1886** Carnegie experienced a cluster of personal and physical crises. He became seriously ill with **typhoid fever** at Cresson, Pennsylvania. His brother Thomas died in October, and his mother Margaret died on **10 November 1886**. Accounts of the episode say news of Thomas's death was initially withheld from the ill Andrew and from their mother.

Carnegie and Louise married on **22 April 1887**. He was fifty-one; she was thirty. They honeymooned in Europe, including the Isle of Wight, and thereafter Louise became the principal partner in his domestic life.

### 1886: *Triumphant Democracy*

Carnegie published *Triumphant Democracy; or, Fifty Years' March of the Republic* in **1886**, after years of statistical work. He had begun compiling material in 1882. The book celebrated the productive power, republican institutions, mass education, and social mobility of the United States in comparison with aristocratic Europe.

It is also a source for the limits and assumptions of his political thought. Its developmental language shared nineteenth-century hierarchies of “civilization” and race. Carnegie's later internationalism repeatedly privileged an imagined English-speaking or Anglo-Saxon community as an engine of global peace. Scholars including Duncan Bell and Srdjan Vucetic have analyzed the racial structure of that peace vision. These categories were not incidental vocabulary; they formed part of the era's imperial and liberal internationalist thought.

### 1886–1890: Allegheny library and the conditional-grant model

Carnegie offered **$250,000** for a library and related civic facilities in Allegheny in **1886**, conditional on public support for maintenance. It opened in 1890. This evolved into the standard Carnegie library mechanism: he typically supplied construction money after a community demonstrated need, provided a site, and committed tax revenue for continuing operation.

The system mattered organizationally. Carnegie did not personally run thousands of libraries. He used a grant formula to induce local governments to create durable public institutions.

### 1888–1889: Homestead acquisition, labor conflict, and Frick's rise

The Carnegie interests acquired the **Homestead Steel Works** in the 1880s. Homestead contained skilled unionized workers represented by the **Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers**. Labor conflict there predated the famous 1892 confrontation. An **1889 dispute** ended with a negotiated arrangement that left the union in place for another contract period. Pinkerton guards had already been considered or used by Carnegie interests around labor disputes in the late 1880s.

In **1889**, Carnegie elevated **Henry Clay Frick** to the top operating leadership/chairmanship of the steel interests. Carnegie admired Frick's executive capacity and his willingness to make hard decisions. That delegation would become central to Homestead.

### 31 May 1889: Johnstown Flood and the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club

The **South Fork Dam** failed on 31 May 1889, releasing water that destroyed much of Johnstown and neighboring communities. The traditional official death count was **2,209**; the National Park Service now notes that the actual toll may have exceeded 3,000.

Carnegie was a **documented member** of the private **South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club**, whose membership included other Pittsburgh industrialists such as Henry Clay Frick, Henry Phipps, and Thomas Carnegie. The club owned the dam and resort property above Johnstown.

Important distinctions:

- **Fact:** Carnegie belonged to the club.
- **Fact:** the club owned the property and dam when it failed.
- **NPS assessment:** Carnegie appears to have spent little time there, if any, and was in Paris when the flood occurred.
- **Legal result:** the club and its members were not successfully held civilly liable for the catastrophe; contemporary litigation against club members failed under the law then applied.
- **Unresolved/interpretive question:** the extent to which club alterations, maintenance failures, institutional negligence, and legal doctrines of the period contributed to the disaster has been debated extensively. Membership alone does not establish that Carnegie personally designed, ordered, or supervised a dangerous modification.

Carnegie participated in American relief activity, visited Johnstown on **28 November 1889**, and ultimately contributed approximately **$55,000** toward a public library there (an initial $10,000 followed by a larger additional gift, according to the NPS account).

### June 1889: “Wealth” / the “Gospel of Wealth”

Carnegie's essay **“Wealth”** appeared in the *North American Review* in 1889. British republication helped attach the enduring title **“The Gospel of Wealth.”** Its core argument was that modern industrial concentration inevitably generated very large fortunes; the wealthy person should treat surplus fortune as a trust to be administered during life for public benefit rather than simply consumed or handed undivided to heirs.

The essay opposed indiscriminate almsgiving and favored institutions that enabled self-development: libraries, universities, parks, concert halls, scientific institutions, and similar civic infrastructure. Its central formula—commonly condensed as “the man who dies rich dies disgraced”—became inseparable from Carnegie's public identity.

The essay generated immediate debate. Respondents included **William E. Gladstone, Cardinal Manning, Hugh Price Hughes**, and **Chief Rabbi Hermann Adler**, among others, and Carnegie answered critics. The essays were later collected in *The Gospel of Wealth and Other Timely Essays* (1900).

### 1890–1891: Carnegie Hall and cultural philanthropy

Carnegie financed a major New York concert venue designed by **William Burnet Tuthill**. The Music Hall opened on **5 May 1891**, with **Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky** participating in the inaugural festival during his American visit. The building later became universally known as **Carnegie Hall**.

The project combined several Carnegie themes: public cultural infrastructure, private capital, elite artistic networks, and long-term institutional visibility.

## 1892: Carnegie Steel and the Homestead Crisis

### Formation of Carnegie Steel Company

In **1892**, Andrew Carnegie, Henry Clay Frick, George Lauder, Henry Phipps, and associated partners consolidated major iron and steel interests as **Carnegie Steel Company**. It was organized as a partnership rather than a widely held public corporation. The private structure enabled concentrated control and aggressive reinvestment.

### Economic background

Steel prices were falling. PBS's reconstruction gives a decline in rolled-steel products from roughly **$35 per gross ton in 1890 to $22 early in 1892**. The union contract at Homestead was expiring. Management proposed a wage scale and changes that threatened the Amalgamated Association's position. Frick was determined to eliminate the union as an independent power in the works.

### Carnegie's public labor rhetoric before Homestead

Carnegie had publicly defended workers' right to organize and often advocated arbitration rather than violent confrontation. This record is real; it was not invented after Homestead. It is also why the 1892 correspondence matters so much: the conflict between published principle and management action can be reconstructed without assuming either side of the record is imaginary.

### Carnegie's role before leaving for Scotland

The strongest evidence against the later story that Carnegie was merely an uninformed absentee is his own correspondence.

On **4 May 1892**, he wrote Frick that the partners approved a contest if necessary and were with him “to the end.” On **10 June**, he cabled that Frick should refuse renewed conferences and predicted that he would win. Carnegie supported building inventory before the contract expiration and accepted a shutdown rather than capitulation to the union.

These documents establish **strategic approval and continuing involvement**. They do not establish that Carnegie personally designed every tactical measure later used at Homestead.

### Lockout

Frick announced on **25 June** that he would no longer negotiate with the union as such. On **29 June**, management shut parts of the works and locked out approximately **3,800 workers**. Only about **750** were union members, but thousands of nonmembers supported the strike. Workers took control of approaches to the plant and prevented normal replacement hiring.

Frick surrounded the works with a high fence topped by barbed wire—“Fort Frick”—and prepared to bring in nonunion labor.

### Pinkertons

Frick contracted the **Pinkerton National Detective Agency** to provide armed guards. This was not the first Carnegie/Frick use of Pinkertons in labor disputes. The agency was a common but highly controversial private security force in nineteenth-century labor conflict.

On **6 July 1892**, roughly **300 Pinkerton men** approached the Homestead works by barge on the Monongahela River. Armed workers and townspeople confronted them. Shooting began during an attempted landing. **Who fired the first shot remains unresolved** in the surviving conflicting accounts.

A battle lasting many hours followed. Workers used rifles, an improvised cannon, dynamite, burning materials, and attempts to ignite the river; the Pinkertons fired from the barges. Casualty totals vary by the boundaries used. A frequently cited immediate count is **seven workers/strike supporters and three Pinkertons killed**, with many wounded; some narratives include later deaths and therefore give higher totals. It is safer to say that **at least ten people died in the battle and immediate aftermath** rather than flatten all sources into one count.

The Pinkertons surrendered. Members of the crowd beat and humiliated some surrendering agents as they were marched away. Contemporary reporting condemned both the use of private armed guards and violence committed against the captives.

### What Carnegie did and did not order

**Documented:** Carnegie supported Frick's antiunion strategy; urged firmness; monitored the struggle by cable; backed refusal to rehire men he classified as rioters; and celebrated ultimate victory.

**Documented:** Frick arranged the Pinkerton force and directed operations in Pennsylvania.

**Not established in the consulted evidence:** that Carnegie personally issued an operational order to Pinkerton agents to fire on workers, or personally planned the exact river landing and battle tactics.

Thus two popular simplifications are both defective: **“Frick did it all and Carnegie knew nothing”** is contradicted by correspondence, while **“Carnegie personally ordered the Pinkertons to shoot the strikers”** exceeds the evidence.

### July 7 and July 17 correspondence

After the battle, Carnegie told Frick by cable to stand firm and not reemploy rioters. In a **17 July** letter to a cousin he criticized the tactical “fiasco” of landing guards by boat while emphasizing that the partners had to support Frick and remain publicly quiet. This is unusually direct evidence that his silence was a strategic public posture rather than lack of information.

### State militia

At Frick's request and amid breakdown of local control, Pennsylvania Governor **Robert E. Pattison** sent the state militia. Roughly **8,500 troops** occupied Homestead and secured the mill. Nonunion replacement workers entered under military protection. By mid-August the works were operating with roughly 1,700 replacement workers.

### Alexander Berkman's attempted assassination of Frick

On **23 July 1892**, anarchist **Alexander Berkman**, associated personally and politically with **Emma Goldman**, entered Frick's Pittsburgh office under a pretext and attempted to assassinate him, shooting and stabbing him. Frick survived.

The evidentiary distinction is essential:

- Berkman acted from anarchist revolutionary motives and interpreted Homestead as an opportunity for “propaganda by deed.”
- He was **not acting on behalf of the Amalgamated Association**, and the union did not commission the attack.
- Goldman shared Berkman's anarchist politics and had discussed revolutionary violence with him; authorities suspected her after the attack, but she was **not charged as a participant in the attempted killing**.
- Berkman was convicted and received a sentence totaling roughly twenty-two years; he served about fourteen.

The attack damaged public sympathy for the strikers and strengthened Frick's determination.

### Black strikebreakers and racial violence

A frequently omitted part of Homestead is the role of **Black replacement workers**. PBS's historical reconstruction identifies the replacements as including some of Pennsylvania's first Black steelworkers. In **November 1892**, about **2,000 white workers** attacked the town's roughly fifty Black families; gunfire was exchanged and multiple people were seriously injured.

This violence belongs in the Homestead history independently of the Carnegie-Frick labor dispute. It shows that the strike community was not politically or racially homogeneous and that the category “workers” concealed conflicts among white union supporters, immigrant groups, Black strikebreakers, and nonunion labor.

### Legal proceedings against strikers

Authorities charged strike leaders with murder, brought lesser charges against large numbers of workers, and charged members of the strike committee with **treason against Pennsylvania**. Juries ultimately convicted none of the principal defendants in these prosecutions. This means the indictments and charges should not be recited as convictions.

### Defeat of the union

The strike ended in **November 1892**. The Amalgamated Association's position at Homestead was destroyed. Some former workers were rehired, many leaders were blacklisted, wages and work rules were restructured, and management eliminated hundreds of jobs. The twelve-hour day became emblematic of the steel industry's labor regime.

Carnegie cabled a jubilant response to Frick after victory. Privately and publicly, however, he also expressed anguish over the bloodshed and later called Homestead the only wound from his business career that remained painful.

### Reputation after Homestead

Contemporary criticism was severe. American newspapers accused him of hypocrisy and cowardice for remaining in Scotland while the conflict unfolded. The **Glasgow Trades Council** denounced him as a “new Judas Iscariot.” He faced criticism within Britain's National Liberal Club. Protests also complicated acceptance of Carnegie cultural gifts in Pittsburgh.

The controversy was not a later invention imposed by twentieth-century historians; it was immediate, transatlantic, and rooted in the contradiction between his published labor rhetoric and documented business correspondence.

## 1893–1900: empire at full scale, armor-plate scandal, family life, Frick rupture

### Technological expansion after Homestead

Carnegie Steel continued expanding despite the reputational crisis. It adopted and refined **open-hearth steelmaking**, improved blast-furnace and rolling technology, expanded coke and ore control, and drove unit costs downward. Charles M. Schwab rose as a central executive. Carnegie's private partnership system generated profits that were heavily reinvested.

By the end of the 1890s, Carnegie Steel was among the world's most formidable industrial enterprises. Claims that it produced “more steel than Britain” around 1900 appear frequently in biographies and depend on what products and years are compared; the broader point—that the enterprise rivaled national-scale output and cost levels—is well supported.

### 1894 armor-plate scandal

Carnegie Steel held important U.S. Navy contracts for armor plate. In **1894**, former workers and congressional investigators brought forward evidence that armor plates had been manipulated, defects concealed, and testing/inspection records falsified or evaded at company facilities.

**Documented/investigated:** Congress and the Navy investigated substantial irregularities in armor-plate manufacture and inspection. Contemporaneous newspapers reported testimony about altered test samples, concealed defects, and supervisory practices designed to get plates accepted despite contract specifications. A government financial penalty was imposed; President Grover Cleveland reduced the amount, with later historical accounts giving the final assessment as approximately **$140,485**.

**Responsibility:** the evidence demonstrates misconduct within Carnegie's industrial organization and makes this more than a rumor. The materials consulted do **not** establish that Andrew Carnegie personally ordered workers to falsify a particular armor test. Claims of personal knowledge must therefore be separated from established company-level misconduct. Charles M. Schwab and other managers figure in the testimony and later accounts; the historical argument centers on how far responsibility should travel upward through a tightly controlled partnership.

This was a commercial/government-contract scandal, not a criminal conviction of Carnegie personally.

### Tariffs and political economy

Steel's expansion occurred behind high U.S. protective tariffs. Carnegie defended protection in earlier stages of industrialization and benefited from the protected domestic market. Over time he argued that mature American industry no longer required extreme protection and publicly criticized excessive tariff shelter.

This evolution made him a useful symbol to both sides. Protectionists could point to American industrial growth; critics charged that tariff-protected manufacturers demanded market shelter while suppressing labor costs. Homestead intensified this critique. It is misleading either to credit tariffs alone for Carnegie's fortune or to treat them as irrelevant: technology, cost control, railroad demand, vertical integration, finance, natural resources, and tariff policy all formed the environment in which the company expanded.

### 1895: Carnegie Institute in Pittsburgh

Carnegie's cultural and educational giving in Pittsburgh coalesced in the **Carnegie Institute**, including library, museum, art, and music functions. The institution eventually developed into distinct organizations including Carnegie Museums and Carnegie Library of Pittsburgh.

### 1897: birth of Margaret and the search for a Scottish home

Louise gave birth to **Margaret** in New York on 30 March 1897. Carnegie's autobiography links fatherhood to Louise's wish for a permanent Scottish summer home. The family leased and then acquired **Skibo Castle** in Sutherland in the late 1890s, conventionally dated to lease in 1897 and purchase in 1898. Carnegie transformed the estate through extensive renovation and used it as both family retreat and international salon.

Skibo became a stage for bagpipes, golf, formal hospitality, political conversation, and the projection of a deliberately Scottish identity by an American plutocrat. Guests ranged across politics, letters, universities, finance, and diplomacy.

### 1898: Spanish-American War and anti-imperialism

Carnegie opposed the United States' acquisition of overseas colonies after the Spanish-American War, especially annexation of the **Philippines**. In **August 1898** he published **“Distant Possessions—The Parting of the Ways”** in the *North American Review*, warning that an American colonial empire contradicted republican principles.

He supported the **Anti-Imperialist League** and related campaigns. One of his more theatrical proposals was to provide money equivalent to the U.S. purchase payment to Spain if the Philippines could thereby obtain independence; the proposal never became policy.

His anti-imperialism should be distinguished from modern anticolonial theory. Carnegie could oppose formal U.S. colonial possession while simultaneously imagining the English-speaking peoples—and often an explicitly Anglo-Saxon civilizational bloc—as the leading agents of global progress and peace.

### British radical newspapers

Carnegie invested in British newspaper ventures intended to advance radical/Liberal causes. The editor of his autobiography states that he acquired interests in **no fewer than eighteen British newspapers**, saying the political results disappointed him while the financial results were satisfactory. The precise ownership form varied by title and venture; it should not be imagined as eighteen wholly owned personal newspapers. The **Express & Star** archive in Wolverhampton identifies Carnegie among the founders/investors alongside radical Liberal figures.

### Dinosaur philanthropy and *Diplodocus carnegii*

In the late 1890s Carnegie sought spectacular scientific exhibits for his Pittsburgh museum. After reading reports of giant dinosaur finds in the American West, he financed collecting expeditions in Wyoming. In **1899**, a Carnegie Museum team uncovered the specimen that became the basis for **Diplodocus carnegii** (modern spelling often *D. carnegii*).

The naming honored Carnegie as patron, not discoverer. Casts of the mounted dinosaur—“Dippy”—were later sent to museums and monarchs in Europe and beyond. This turned paleontology into a form of diplomatic gift exchange and gave Carnegie an unusually durable association with natural history.

### 1899–1900: break with Henry Clay Frick

The Carnegie-Frick relationship deteriorated after Homestead through disputes over expansion, coke interests, executive authority, and personal insults. On **5 December 1899**, Frick resigned from the Carnegie Steel board.

Carnegie then attempted to invoke the partnership's so-called **“iron-clad agreement”** to acquire Frick's minority interest at a formula price far below what Frick believed to be its actual value. Frick sued in Pennsylvania equity court.

**Frick's pleading alleged** that Carnegie was using partnership machinery oppressively and that the enterprise's assets were worth vastly more than the price offered for his shares. Modern legal commentary treats the case as an early, vivid example of minority-shareholder oppression.

**No final judicial finding on those allegations was entered:** the parties settled in **March 1900**. Frick received securities and property whose value is commonly given at roughly **$30 million**. The two men never reconciled and apparently never met again.

The breakup matters beyond personality. It exposed how Carnegie's supposedly harmonious partnership system depended on private agreements, concentrated control, and the continued consent of very wealthy minority partners.

## 1900–1901: the sale of Carnegie Steel and creation of U.S. Steel

### Why Carnegie sold

By 1900 Carnegie was in his mid-sixties, increasingly interested in philanthropy, writing, family life, and peace work. The industrial landscape was also changing. Financier **J. Pierpont Morgan** was consolidating steel-related companies. Carnegie could threaten Morgan's combinations by entering tubes, wire, and other downstream markets, while Morgan could organize competitors and capital against Carnegie.

**Charles M. Schwab**, then Carnegie's leading executive, became an intermediary. After a celebrated dinner speech laying out the logic of steel consolidation, Schwab helped bridge Morgan and Carnegie. Negotiations in early 1901 ended with Carnegie agreeing to sell.

### The price problem: several legitimate numbers

There is no single number that should be called “the price Carnegie got” without specifying the denominator.

- Contemporary and later institutional histories commonly describe the acquisition/consolidation of the **Carnegie interests** at roughly **$480 million**; some accounts give about **$492 million**, depending on which securities and related interests are included.
- A frequently cited accounting figure for the Carnegie Steel interests is about **$303.45 million**.
- Carnegie's **personal** share is commonly given at approximately **$225.6 million**, substantially in long-term U.S. Steel gold bonds.
- PBS gives a rounded personal figure of about **$250 million**.

These figures are not necessarily contradictions; they describe different scopes of the transaction and different valuations of securities. The dossier therefore rejects the familiar but misleading sentence “Morgan paid Carnegie exactly $X.”

A surviving financial artifact in an Illinois digital collection is a **$100,000 United States Steel Corporation gold bond issued to Andrew Carnegie on 14 June 1901**, illustrating the security form in which part of the consideration was held.

### U.S. Steel

Morgan combined Carnegie Steel with **Federal Steel**, **National Steel**, and other enterprises to form **United States Steel Corporation** in 1901, widely identified as the first corporation capitalized above **$1 billion**. Carnegie withdrew from active steel management. Schwab became the first president of U.S. Steel.

Carnegie had spent roughly three decades violating his 1868 promise to stop accumulating at thirty-five. The sale finally made possible the second half of the program: systematic distribution on an institutional scale.

## 1901–1914: philanthropy as an operating system

Carnegie's giving was not simply episodic generosity. He designed **endowed organizations**, conditional grants, pension systems, trusts, and international institutions intended to outlive him. By his death he had distributed approximately **$350 million**, according to the Carnegie organizations' own historical accounting—most of his fortune, though not literally every dollar.

### Public libraries

Carnegie's best-known program ultimately supported **2,509 library buildings worldwide**, according to Carnegie institutional history, at a cost of more than **$56 million**. The familiar phrase “Carnegie built 2,509 libraries” is shorthand. The actual system was cooperative:

- Carnegie or his agents supplied capital for construction.
- The local authority generally supplied the site.
- The community committed recurring tax revenue for books, staff, maintenance, heat, and operations—often benchmarked at around 10 percent of the building grant annually.
- Local architects and authorities built and ran the institutions.

The formula reduced the risk of creating empty monuments without operating funds. Critics nevertheless complained that Carnegie could give a town a building while leaving taxpayers to fund it and that some grants produced “bookless libraries” until collections caught up.

Library access also reflected local racial and social systems. Carnegie's program did not impose a universal desegregation condition. In the Jim Crow South, public institutions receiving Carnegie capital could remain segregated or unequal. This is a feature of the grant system and local governance, not evidence that every individual segregation decision was ordered by Carnegie personally.

### Carnegie Institute / Carnegie Museums and Library of Pittsburgh

Beginning in the 1890s Carnegie created in Pittsburgh a complex of library, museum, art, music, and educational institutions. The **Carnegie Institute** became a permanent cultural anchor and eventually differentiated into organizations now known as Carnegie Museums of Pittsburgh and Carnegie Library of Pittsburgh.

The natural-history program financed major scientific collecting; *Diplodocus carnegii* became its most internationally visible object.

### Carnegie Technical Schools / Carnegie Mellon University

In **1900**, Carnegie committed an initial **$2 million** to establish the **Carnegie Technical Schools** in Pittsburgh, conceived as practical and technical education for industrial society. The schools developed into the Carnegie Institute of Technology. In **1967**, long after Carnegie's death, Carnegie Tech merged with the Mellon Institute to form **Carnegie Mellon University**.

### Carnegie Trust for the Universities of Scotland, 1901

Carnegie endowed the **Carnegie Trust for the Universities of Scotland** with approximately **$10 million** in U.S. securities. The trust supported university fees, research, buildings, and institutional development. University of St Andrews archival material emphasizes the extraordinary scale: the endowment was roughly two hundred times the annual government funding then reaching Scotland's four ancient universities.

St Andrews became especially important to Carnegie. He received the **Freedom of the City** and was elected **Rector of the University of St Andrews** twice, in **1903 and 1905**. His specific St Andrews benefactions included support for facilities, recreation, library expansion, women students, and an organ.

### Carnegie Institution of Washington, 1902

On **29 January 1902**, Carnegie founded the **Carnegie Institution of Washington** with an initial **$10 million** endowment, later increased so that his total commitment reached roughly **$22 million**. The institution supported fundamental scientific research rather than only classroom instruction. Its descendants and programs contributed to astronomy, genetics, geophysics, embryology, ecology, and other sciences.

This was a different philanthropic model from the library: endow expert researchers for long-horizon inquiry that ordinary markets or universities might underfund.

### New York mansion, 1899–1902

Carnegie commissioned a sixty-four-room mansion at **2 East 91st Street** in Manhattan, designed by **Babb, Cook & Willard** and built from 1899 to 1902. The house used then-advanced systems including central heating and a passenger elevator. The family moved in around the end of 1902.

Its location well north of the traditional Fifth Avenue mansion district helped give the neighborhood the later name **Carnegie Hill**. The building now houses Cooper Hewitt, Smithsonian Design Museum.

### Pittencrieff Glen and Dunfermline Trust, 1902–1903

Carnegie purchased **Pittencrieff Estate** in Dunfermline and transferred it for public use. In his autobiography, this gift is treated as almost uniquely emotional: the private glen associated with childhood exclusion was converted into a public park.

The **Carnegie Dunfermline Trust**, endowed in **1903** with roughly $4 million in contemporary dollar terms, was intended to improve the quality of civic life in his birthplace through parks, recreation, culture, and public amenities.

### Peace Palace, The Hague, 1903

At the urging of internationalists including **Andrew D. White**, Carnegie provided **$1.5 million** for a “Temple of Peace” at The Hague. He insisted on formal Dutch governmental involvement; in his autobiography he took pride in having the Dutch government draw on him rather than presenting himself as spontaneously imposing a monument.

The **Peace Palace**, opened in **1913**, became the home of the Permanent Court of Arbitration and later international judicial institutions. It embodied Carnegie's conviction that interstate disputes could be juridified and arbitrated rather than settled by war.

### Carnegie Hero Fund, 1904

After a mine disaster and other celebrated acts of civilian rescue, Carnegie established the **Carnegie Hero Fund Commission** in **1904**, initially endowing it with about **$5 million**. The fund recognized civilians who risked their lives to save others and often provided financial support to heroes or dependents.

Carnegie's concept of heroism was deliberately redirected from military killing to civilian lifesaving.

### Carnegie Foundation for the Advancement of Teaching, 1905

Carnegie founded the **Carnegie Foundation for the Advancement of Teaching** with an initial endowment of about **$10 million**, initially focusing on pensions for college professors and later on educational research and standards.

The foundation sponsored **Abraham Flexner's 1910 report on medical education**, which transformed American medical-school standards. Its later effects are contested: higher standards and laboratory-based education improved scientific training, while school closures and institutional exclusion disproportionately harmed medical education for Black Americans and women. The important attribution point is that **Carnegie founded and endowed the institution; he did not personally author the Flexner Report**.

### *James Watt*, 1905

Carnegie published a biography of Scottish engineer **James Watt** in 1905. The subject suited his personal mythology: Scottish origins, engineering transformation, useful invention, and modern industry. It also reveals Carnegie's habit of embedding industrial history in heroic biography.

### Rectorial address and “League of Peace,” 1905–1906

As Rector of St Andrews, Carnegie used his address to advocate a **League of Peace**—an association of powerful nations committed to arbitration and collective opposition to war. The address was published in 1906. Later commentators often treat it as one precursor among many to twentieth-century League of Nations ideas; it was not a fully developed constitutional blueprint for the later League.

### Simplified Spelling Board, 1906–1915

Carnegie funded the **Simplified Spelling Board** beginning in **1906** and continued support until **1915**. He believed irregular English spelling imposed unnecessary educational burdens and that simplifying it could help English function more easily as a global language.

The campaign attracted prominent intellectuals and briefly obtained support from President **Theodore Roosevelt**, whose attempt to introduce selected spellings into federal printing produced political ridicule and congressional resistance. Carnegie eventually withdrew financial support when the movement failed to achieve the hoped-for adoption.

This project connects philanthropy, language politics, efficiency, and Carnegie's larger belief in an English-speaking international civilization.

### Peace Society of New York, 1907

Carnegie accepted the presidency of the **Peace Society of New York** in 1907 after initially declining. His autobiography describes his conscience driving him to reverse that refusal. He presided over a national peace gathering drawing delegates from many states and foreign guests.

### French Legion of Honor

At the peace gathering Carnegie received the **Legion of Honor**, in the rank his autobiography renders **“Knight Commander”** (i.e. a commander-grade decoration in its English wording), for international peace activity. His memoir describes this as his first “decoration.” He accumulated many civic freedoms and honorary degrees—his autobiography's editor claimed **fifty-four Freedoms of cities in Britain and Ireland**, a figure worth treating as an editorial compilation rather than independently re-counted here.

### *The Negro in America*, 1907; Booker T. Washington and Tuskegee

Carnegie delivered **“The Negro in America”** before the Philosophical Institution of Edinburgh on **16 October 1907**; it was published as a pamphlet.

His relationship with **Booker T. Washington** and **Tuskegee Institute** was financially substantial but evolved over time. The Andrew Carnegie Birthplace Museum's reconstruction notes that Washington first solicited Carnegie unsuccessfully in 1890. By **1900**, Carnegie gave about **$20,000** for a Tuskegee library. He visited Tuskegee in **1906** and later contributed a much larger block of U.S. Steel securities—reported at approximately **$600,000**—as well as personal provision for Washington.

Carnegie admired Washington's emphasis on education, industrial training, and self-help. This relationship also placed him on one side of a major African-American political debate, as **W. E. B. Du Bois** and others criticized Washington's accommodationist strategy.

Carnegie's racial thought should not be reduced either to this support or to later celebratory institutional accounts. His writings could be paternalistic, and his global peace theory often used hierarchical Anglo-Saxon or “civilizational” categories. At the same time, he materially financed Black education at Tuskegee and spoke publicly about African-American advancement. Both belong in the record.

### *Problems of To-Day*, 1908

Carnegie's 1908 collection **Problems of To-Day: Wealth—Labor—Socialism** brought together arguments on economic organization, labor, wealth, and social policy. It is useful evidence for his late attempt to reconcile private industrial capitalism, labor arbitration, progressive social reform, and philanthropic redistribution.

### Nobel Peace Prize nominations

The official Nobel nomination archive records Carnegie as a nominee for the **Nobel Peace Prize**, including in **1908** and **1913**. He did **not** receive the prize. The nomination rationale stressed his financing of peace institutions, advocacy of arbitration, presidency of peace organizations, and construction of international “temples of peace.”

The fact of nomination is documentary; later claims that Carnegie “won” or was formally promised the Nobel Peace Prize are incorrect.

### Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, 1910

In **1910**, Carnegie endowed the **Carnegie Endowment for International Peace** with **$10 million**. He recruited the statesman and lawyer **Elihu Root** as a central leader. The endowment supported international law, diplomacy, research, education, and peace advocacy.

### Carnegie Corporation of New York, 1911

Carnegie created **Carnegie Corporation of New York** in **1911**, transferring a large block of remaining philanthropic capital to a permanent grantmaking corporation. This was the institutional solution to a practical problem: at his age and with thousands of requests arriving, he could no longer personally distribute wealth intelligently at the scale his doctrine required.

The corporation's records, now at Columbia University, preserve extensive grant, administrative, and financial documentation. Andrew Carnegie served as its first president from **1911 to 1919**. In 2026 the corporation began doing business under the name **Andrew Carnegie Foundation**; that current name change is posthumous institutional history, not a change to Carnegie's original entity.

### Carnegie United Kingdom Trust, 1913

Carnegie created the **Carnegie United Kingdom Trust** in **1913**, endowed with approximately **$10 million**, to support libraries, community development, culture, and public welfare in Britain and Ireland.

### Church Peace Union, 1914

In **1914** Carnegie supplied about **$2 million** to establish the **Church Peace Union**, designed to mobilize religious leadership for international peace. It later evolved into what is now **Carnegie Council for Ethics in International Affairs**.

## 1914–1919: war, industrial-relations reckoning, old age, and death

### August 1914: collapse of the peace expectation

Carnegie was in Scotland when European mobilization produced the First World War. Louise Carnegie's 1920 preface states that the news of **4 August 1914** ended the autobiographical manuscript and that he was never again able to return to it successfully. She wrote that his “heart was broken”—a family interpretation of psychological decline rather than a medical diagnosis.

The outbreak of war was a direct intellectual defeat. Carnegie had spent years arguing that economic interdependence, arbitration, international law, and elite Anglo-American/German connections made large-scale war increasingly irrational. He had personally met **Kaiser Wilhelm II** and cultivated heads of government and diplomats. The war demonstrated that those networks did not control state mobilization.

### Physical decline

Louise records a **severe attack of influenza followed by two serious attacks of pneumonia**, which she believed accelerated his aging. Before 1914 Carnegie had remained physically active, golfing, fishing, and swimming. There is no strong evidence in the sources consulted of drug addiction, chronic alcoholism, or a diagnosed psychiatric illness.

### 5 February 1915: Commission on Industrial Relations testimony

Carnegie testified before the **United States Commission on Industrial Relations** on **5 February 1915**. The testimony was separately reprinted from the *New York Herald* and survives in library collections. Its very existence is significant: more than two decades after Homestead, federal investigators examining labor-capital relations questioned the retired steelmaster about industrial responsibility, labor policy, and his former enterprises.

Carnegie continued to present arbitration and communication as the proper model for labor relations and insisted that his own ideals had favored consultation. Critics viewed such late statements through the documentary record of Homestead. The testimony should therefore be read alongside, not instead of, the 1892 correspondence.

### 1915: end of major spelling subsidies; archival self-fashioning

By 1915 Carnegie was withdrawing from some experimental philanthropic campaigns, including continued subsidy of simplified spelling. **Margaret Barclay Wilson's privately printed *A Carnegie Anthology*** appeared that year, collecting writings and material useful for a bibliography of Carnegie's publications.

### Final American residences

Carnegie's family continued to use the New York mansion and the Massachusetts estate **Shadowbrook** in Lenox. The First World War curtailed the regular transatlantic rhythm that had taken the family to Skibo almost every year.

### Daughter's marriage, April 1919

On **22 April 1919**, Margaret Carnegie married **Roswell Miller Jr.** at the family mansion in New York. Andrew gave his daughter away. The wedding linked the final months of his life to a family succession quite different from dynastic inheritance on the scale of the Vanderbilts: Carnegie had deliberately transferred much of his fortune to institutions rather than to a single heir.

### Death, 11 August 1919

Carnegie died at **Shadowbrook, Lenox, Massachusetts, on 11 August 1919**, aged **83** (in his eighty-fourth year). Contemporaneous reports identified **bronchial pneumonia** as the immediate cause. He was buried at **Sleepy Hollow Cemetery** in New York.

His wife Louise survived until 1946; their daughter Margaret until 1990.

### Estate and amount given away

Carnegie institutional histories put his lifetime giving at about **$350 million**. The amount remaining at death is often rounded to approximately **$30 million**, much of it committed or provided for family, pensions, and residual estate purposes. Consequently, the statement that he “gave away his entire fortune” is inaccurate if taken literally. He gave away the great majority of it and established institutions to continue distribution.

### Technologies and inventions: what belongs to Carnegie and what does not

Carnegie was a technological entrepreneur, **not a major inventor-patentee in the Edison sense**.

He did not invent:

- the Bessemer converter;
- open-hearth steelmaking;
- the railroad sleeping car;
- structural iron bridges;
- the Jones hot-metal mixing process.

His firms **licensed, purchased, commissioned, improved, integrated, and industrialized** such technologies. U.S. Supreme Court litigation in **Carnegie Steel Co. v. Cambria Iron Co., 185 U.S. 403 (1902)** concerned a patent issued to **William R. Jones** for a method involving molten pig metal. The case illustrates the technological property of Carnegie's organization while also showing why attributing every Carnegie-company patent to Andrew Carnegie personally is wrong.

### Vertical integration

Carnegie's system came to include or control:

- iron and steel mills;
- blast furnaces;
- coke production;
- iron ore interests;
- bridge and structural work;
- transportation relationships and dedicated rail connections;
- lake/ore logistics through associated arrangements;
- sales and financing networks.

The goal was to reduce dependence on suppliers, secure throughput, and push unit cost below competitors. “Vertical integration” is a later analytic label, but accurately describes the structure.

### Financial philosophy

Carnegie distinguished between **industrial reinvestment** and **stock speculation** in his memoirs. He preferred private partnerships, retained earnings, and bonds over widely traded equity for much of his career. His partners could become extremely wealthy, but he maintained dominant control through ownership percentages and contractual arrangements.

This system generated repeated tensions: a partner could possess enormous paper wealth while still remain vulnerable to Carnegie's control over valuation or buyout terms, as Frick's 1899–1900 litigation demonstrated.

## Wealth, Property, and Financial Relationships

### Sources of wealth by phase

1. **Wages and savings:** cotton, telegraph, railroad.
2. **Railroad-connected investment:** express, sleeping cars, suppliers.
3. **Oil:** Storey Farm and related petroleum profits.
4. **Iron and bridges:** Keystone, Cyclops, Union Iron.
5. **Bond sales and transatlantic finance:** commissions, network access, project finance.
6. **Steel:** Edgar Thomson, Homestead, Carnegie Brothers, Carnegie Steel.
7. **Coke/raw materials:** Frick company and vertical integration.
8. **1901 conversion:** industrial ownership exchanged for U.S. Steel securities.

### Private ownership and partners

Carnegie's fortune was created through partnerships rather than solitary ownership. **Henry Phipps, Thomas Carnegie, George Lauder, Frick, Schwab**, and many smaller partners/managers shared in the enterprise. The exact percentages changed across reorganizations.

The model created strong incentives for managers who became partners, but it also concentrated strategic authority in Carnegie. He was famous for identifying capable younger men, rewarding them with partnership stakes, and expecting relentless performance.

### Property

Principal properties connected with his adult life included:

- the Homewood-area Pittsburgh family residence, later given to Thomas Carnegie;
- long-term hotel residences in New York;
- a summer residence at **Cresson, Pennsylvania**;
- the Whitfield/Carnegie household after marriage;
- **Skibo Castle and estate** in Scotland;
- the **Andrew Carnegie Mansion**, 2 East 91st Street, New York;
- **Shadowbrook**, Lenox, Massachusetts.

### Scale of philanthropic distribution

The often-cited total of about **$350 million** distributed during his lifetime comes from Carnegie institutional accounting and is broadly accepted in major biographies. Because his wealth was held in businesses and securities whose market and income values changed, any claim that this represented exactly a fixed percentage of his “net worth” requires a specified date and valuation method.

## Political / Religious / Intellectual / Artistic Development

### Radical Scottish inheritance

Carnegie's earliest politics came from Dunfermline's artisan radicalism: Chartism, working-class suffrage, skepticism toward hereditary privilege, civic education, and republican admiration for the United States. His mature political identity remained transatlantic. He could be an American industrial capitalist, a British Liberal-radical social figure, a Scottish civic patriot, and an internationalist simultaneously.

### Republicanism and anti-aristocracy

Carnegie repeatedly mocked inherited rank and monarchy in his writing. His American nationalism centered on the absence of titled hereditary classes, mass schooling, industrial expansion, and political citizenship. This was one reason *Triumphant Democracy* mattered to him as more than a statistics book: it was a comparative argument that republican institutions were producing a more powerful society than European aristocratic systems.

Yet his own lifestyle at Skibo—castle, servants, elite guests, ceremonial hospitality—made the anti-aristocratic pose a ready target for critics. Carnegie treated the difference as one between **social ceremony** and **hereditary political privilege**; critics often saw plutocratic imitation of aristocracy.

### British Liberal and Home Rule networks

Carnegie cultivated **William Ewart Gladstone, John Morley**, and other British Liberals and sympathized with reform causes including **Irish Home Rule**. His newspaper investments were intended to strengthen radical/Liberal opinion. He was not merely buying social access: correspondence and memoirs show sustained engagement with British political debate.

### United States politics

Carnegie held no elected office and was never a cabinet member. He had direct access, however, to senior political leaders through business, social, and philanthropic networks. He knew or corresponded with figures including **James G. Blaine, Benjamin Harrison, William McKinley, John Hay, Theodore Roosevelt, William Howard Taft, Woodrow Wilson**, and others.

His political activity typically took the form of published argument, private correspondence, elite lobbying, organizational financing, and public addresses rather than candidacy.

### Tariffs

Carnegie's tariff position evolved. His industry benefited heavily from nineteenth-century protection, and he defended protection as a mechanism for nurturing developing American industry. When U.S. steel became globally competitive, he increasingly argued that industries should not remain permanently sheltered. In later tariff debates he could therefore appear as a manufacturer who had benefited from protection but now advocated reduction.

### Anti-imperialism

After the 1898 war with Spain, Carnegie opposed annexation of the Philippines and the creation of a permanent American colonial empire. He contributed to Anti-Imperialist League activity and published “Distant Possessions—The Parting of the Ways.” His position rested on republican constitutionalism, concern about militarism, and fear that colonial commitments would corrupt American political development.

He nevertheless accepted a hierarchy of “civilized” peoples common to his period and sometimes imagined less-developed peoples in paternalistic terms. Anti-imperialism did not erase racialized developmental thinking.

### Peace, arbitration, and international organization

Carnegie's late political project was the abolition or radical reduction of war through:

- permanent courts of arbitration;
- international law;
- treaties requiring cooling-off periods;
- elite diplomacy;
- educational propaganda;
- an association or “league” of powerful states capable of enforcing peace;
- Anglo-American cooperation, frequently imagined as the nucleus of a wider peace order.

He financed institutions to operationalize these ideas rather than limiting himself to speeches. The Peace Palace, Carnegie Endowment, Church Peace Union, peace congresses, and dozens of pamphlets belong to this campaign.

### Racialized internationalism

Modern scholarship has emphasized that Carnegie's peace universalism sometimes rested on a racialized hierarchy. His preferred agents of order were often the **English-speaking peoples**, framed in nineteenth-century “Anglo-Saxon” language. Duncan Bell's work on Carnegie's “racial utopianism” analyzes how visions of imperial federation, Anglo-American unity, and the abolition of war could coexist.

This is not an allegation imported from a single hostile biography; it is an interpretation grounded in Carnegie's own published vocabulary and the wider imperial discourse of his age. The same Carnegie could oppose U.S. colonial annexation, support Black educational institutions, and employ hierarchical racial categories in global politics.

### Religion

Carnegie grew up amid Scottish Protestant culture but became increasingly skeptical of orthodox doctrine. Darwin and Spencer helped dissolve his fear of inherited Calvinist concepts. His writings reject the image of a punitive supernatural order and emphasize natural law, moral progress, service to humanity, and ethical conduct.

His language remained religiously resonant. He could invoke God and quote Luther while redefining worship as service to people. He supported church organs and religious peace organizations even though he did not subscribe straightforwardly to confessional orthodoxy. The label **agnostic** captures part of his mature metaphysics; **ethical religious humanism shaped by evolutionary thought** captures another part.

### Literature, art, music, and public culture

Carnegie read widely, cultivated writers, financed concert halls and organs, and built museum and library systems. He was personally fond of Scottish song and bagpipe ceremony. His relationships included **Matthew Arnold**, **Mark Twain**, and many literary figures represented in his papers. Unlike a collector such as Frick, whose legacy centered on a private art collection, Carnegie tended to channel artistic capital into public institutions and performance spaces.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### 1. Railroad-era insider investments and contracting

**Status:** documented overlapping interests; ethical characterization depends on historical standard; no criminal conviction identified.

While a Pennsylvania Railroad manager, Carnegie invested with railroad superiors in businesses that supplied the railroad. The modern Carnegie institutional biography acknowledges secret partner relationships involving Scott and Thomson. Carnegie's supplier companies benefited from railroad contracts and his access to decision-makers.

This is a substantive financial conflict by contemporary corporate-governance standards. It should not be converted without evidence into a claim that Carnegie was convicted of bribery or fraud. No such personal criminal conviction has been identified in the sources consulted.

### 2. Labor policy before 1892

**Status:** documented tension between rhetoric and practice.

Carnegie published unusually conciliatory statements about unions and arbitration, but his companies used aggressive wage bargaining, long hours, strikebreakers, and Pinkerton security before 1892. The 1889 Homestead settlement preserved the union temporarily; it did not establish a stable pro-union regime.

### 3. Homestead lockout and armed conflict, 1892

**Status:** strategic Carnegie involvement documented; operational responsibility shared within management; battlefield first shot unresolved; no evidence of a personal Carnegie order to kill.

The decisive evidence is the Carnegie-Frick correspondence. Carnegie backed a confrontation if needed, expected Frick to refuse further conferences after taking his stand, supported shutting the works, and after the battle told him not to rehire “rioters.” He remained in Scotland but was not uninformed.

Frick contracted the Pinkertons and managed the local operation. The battle on 6 July killed at least ten people in commonly used immediate counts and wounded many more. The crowd's abuse of surrendered Pinkertons is also documented.

### 4. Use of state force and strikebreakers

**Status:** documented.

Pennsylvania militia occupied Homestead and enabled replacement labor to enter. Management ultimately broke the union. This was legal state intervention in a labor crisis, not a private army under Carnegie's personal command, though the intervention materially served the company's effort to reopen the plant.

### 5. Racial violence during Homestead

**Status:** documented historical episode, usually omitted from simplified retellings.

Black workers were brought into the mills as replacements. In November 1892 a large white crowd attacked Black families in Homestead. The racial violence cannot be attributed simply to “Carnegie” or “the union” without distinguishing actors, but it formed part of the social aftermath of management's strikebreaking labor market.

### 6. Berkman assassination attempt

**Status:** documented criminal act by Alexander Berkman; not a union plot; not an act by Carnegie.

Berkman shot and stabbed Frick. He was convicted and imprisoned. Emma Goldman was investigated/suspected in the political aftermath but was not convicted of participation in the attempted assassination. The event is relevant because it changed public perceptions of Homestead and supplied both pro-management and anarchist propaganda.

### 7. Johnstown Flood / South Fork Club

**Status:** Carnegie membership documented; personal causation unproven; club liability claims unsuccessful.

Carnegie's membership in the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club is not a conspiracy theory. It is documented by the National Park Service and club records. The stronger claim that **Carnegie personally caused the dam to fail** is not supported. NPS material suggests he spent little if any time at the club and was abroad at the time of the disaster.

The broader question of elite-club responsibility is real. The club owned the dam and property, repairs and alterations were historically consequential, and victims failed to obtain damages from club members under contemporary law. Later historians have debated negligence, maintenance, spillway changes, engineering knowledge, and the legal protection enjoyed by wealthy defendants.

### 8. Armor-plate fraud, 1894

**Status:** government investigation found serious company irregularities; financial penalty imposed; personal Carnegie criminal culpability not established.

Workers and investigators described concealed defects, altered testing, and acceptance of armor plate contrary to specifications. Congressional and Navy investigation treated the charges as substantive. The company was penalized. Later retellings differ over precisely which managers knew what and when.

It is justified to call this the **Carnegie armor-plate scandal** and to say fraud was investigated and company misconduct substantiated. It is not justified, on the evidence consulted, to state as established fact that Andrew Carnegie personally ordered a fraudulent plate test or was criminally convicted for it.

### 9. Frick minority-shareholder litigation, 1899–1900

**Status:** civil allegations documented; settlement, not final adjudication.

Frick accused Carnegie of trying to force his minority stake out at an artificially low valuation under the partnership agreement. The complaint painted Carnegie as an absentee controller manipulating the arrangement to appropriate value. Carnegie disputed the characterization. Settlement terminated the litigation before a definitive merits judgment.

Thus phrases such as “Carnegie stole Frick's shares” are argumentative shorthand, not a court finding.

### 10. Protective tariff benefits

**Status:** documented economic/political relationship, not corruption by itself.

Carnegie Steel profited within a strongly protected U.S. market. Critics argued that manufacturers captured tariff rents while resisting labor demands. Carnegie later criticized continued high protection for mature industries. No evidence consulted supports reducing his industrial success to a secret tariff “fraud”; tariffs were public law, though shaped by intense political lobbying from many industries.

### 11. Philanthropy and labor reputation

**Status:** historical controversy, not a question of whether the gifts were real.

Critics from the 1890s onward argued that libraries and universities could not erase labor conditions under which Carnegie's fortune was accumulated. Sympathetic interpreters emphasized the unprecedented scale and institutional durability of his giving. Both facts can coexist: the philanthropy was materially enormous and institutionally consequential, and Homestead remained part of its public reception from the beginning.

### 12. Race, segregation, and education

**Status:** mixed record requiring institutional attribution.

Carnegie funded Tuskegee heavily and worked with Booker T. Washington. He also used paternalistic and racial-hierarchical language characteristic of his intellectual milieu. Carnegie library grants generally deferred access rules to local authorities, including segregated systems.

The Carnegie Foundation for the Advancement of Teaching sponsored the Flexner Report, whose restructuring of medical education had exclusionary effects for Black medical institutions and women. The institutional connection is documented; claims that Carnegie personally designed each closure or racial consequence go beyond that record.

### 13. No substantiated money laundering, intelligence connection, or personal criminal conviction found

The broad search conducted for this dossier did **not** produce credible evidence that Carnegie engaged in money laundering in the modern criminal sense, served as an intelligence operative, was imprisoned, or was personally convicted of a crime. His world included political influence, private security, government contracts, and high-level diplomacy, but those facts should not be converted into intelligence or criminal claims without specific evidence.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Myth 1: “The penniless boy who arrived in America with nothing and single-handedly became a steel king”

**Story:** Carnegie is presented as the purest American self-made man: starving immigrant child, no advantages, fortune created by individual genius.

**Appearance/circulation:** Carnegie's own autobiography, public speeches, schoolbook biographies, philanthropic institutional memory, and twentieth-century “rags-to-riches” culture.

**Evidence:** the family's poverty by 1848 was real, as were Andrew's factory wages and minimal formal schooling. But the family borrowed migration money; his mother and father worked; family property was mortgaged to finance an early investment; Scott and Thomson supplied mentorship, access, and investment opportunities; partners and engineers were indispensable.

**Reputational effect:** became a foundational American mobility myth and made his libraries seem like a return of opportunity to boys resembling his younger self.

### Myth 2: “Carnegie invented steel” or “invented the Bessemer process”

**Story:** Carnegie personally invented modern steelmaking.

**Appearance:** simplified popular history and educational shorthand.

**Evidence against:** Henry Bessemer and William Kelly developed the key pneumatic processes; Holley, Jones, Kennedy, and many engineers adapted technologies. Carnegie was an organizer, financier, adopter, integrator, and scale builder.

**Effect:** concentrates an industrial system into one heroic inventor figure.

### Myth 3: “Carnegie had nothing to do with Homestead; Frick betrayed him while he was in Scotland”

**Story:** Carnegie, the labor-friendly owner, went on vacation; Frick unexpectedly hired Pinkertons and caused a massacre contrary to Carnegie's wishes.

**Circulation:** encouraged by Carnegie's later autobiography and defensive public memory; repeated in some simplified biographies.

**Evidence against:** May–July 1892 letters and cables show Carnegie approved a decisive contest, expected refusal of conferences, supported shutdown, and backed Frick after the battle.

**Effect:** protected the philanthropic Carnegie image by isolating coercion in Frick.

### Myth 4: “Carnegie personally ordered Pinkertons to shoot the workers”

**Story:** Carnegie directly commanded the armed attack.

**Circulation:** labor polemic, compressed popular retellings, social-media summaries.

**Evidence:** Carnegie supported the antiunion confrontation and knew private guards were part of management's labor repertoire; Frick arranged the Pinkerton deployment. The consulted record does not show a Carnegie order to open fire or establish who fired first at the landing.

**Effect:** turns structural and managerial responsibility into a single tactical command claim that is easier to narrate but harder to prove.

### Myth 5: “The first blood of the Civil War was Andrew Carnegie's”

**Story:** while repairing telegraph wire, Carnegie cut his face and became the first casualty/first blood of the Civil War.

**Source:** Carnegie's own Civil War reminiscence and later retellings.

**Evidence:** the wire-cut anecdote is a first-person self-report. The superlative “first blood” is rhetorical and not accepted as a formal casualty record.

**Effect:** inserts Carnegie bodily into the Union cause and dramatizes telegraph technology as war service.

### Myth 6: “Carnegie caused the Johnstown Flood”

**Story:** as a millionaire member of the South Fork club, Carnegie personally altered the dam and bears direct individual responsibility for the catastrophe.

**Circulation:** later polemic, internet summaries, broader “robber baron” mythology.

**Evidence:** club membership is true; individual engineering control by Carnegie is not established. NPS research suggests he barely, if ever, used the club and was in Paris at the time. Club ownership and possible institutional negligence remain legitimate historical subjects.

**Effect:** converts diffuse engineering, corporate, class, and legal responsibility into one famous name.

### Myth 7: “Carnegie gave away every penny”

**Story:** after selling his company, he disposed of literally all personal wealth before death.

**Circulation:** philanthropic hagiography and the slogan about dying rich.

**Evidence:** he gave away about $350 million—most of his fortune—but retained family provision and an estate commonly estimated around $30 million.

**Effect:** makes his own “Gospel” appear perfectly fulfilled rather than substantially but incompletely realized.

### Myth 8: “2,509 libraries were wholly built and operated by Carnegie”

**Story:** Carnegie directly constructed, stocked, staffed, and ran thousands of libraries.

**Evidence:** his grants financed buildings; communities usually supplied sites and permanent tax-funded operation. Local authorities chose books, staff, access rules, and architecture within broad grant requirements.

**Effect:** magnifies personal agency while erasing municipal co-financing and local governance.

### Myth 9: “The man who dies rich dies disgraced” as an isolated quip

**Story:** a catchy personal aphorism explains all his philanthropy.

**Evidence:** the idea is genuine and central to the 1889 “Wealth” essay, but it belongs to a larger argument: inequality is an effect of industrial competition; fortunes should be administered during life as trusts for community benefit; indiscriminate charity is often harmful.

**Effect:** the slogan survives while the controversial defense of wealth concentration around it is forgotten.

### Myth 10: “Carnegie was simply anti-union” / “Carnegie was simply a friend of labor”

Both are reductions. He published defenses of organized labor and arbitration, and those texts influenced contemporaries. His companies also moved decisively to eliminate union power at Homestead and used coercive labor methods. The contradiction is historical evidence, not a problem that can be solved by selecting only one archive.

### Myth 11: “Carnegie was the richest man in the world”

The phrase was widely circulated and attached to the 1901 Morgan transaction. Carnegie was unquestionably among the world's richest private individuals. Exact ranking against **John D. Rockefeller**, dynastic fortunes, landowners, monarchs, and privately held business interests is not susceptible to a single audited global list. Treat the phrase as a contemporaneous status claim rather than a precise statistical title.

### Myth 12: Frick's alleged deathbed “meet you in hell” response

Later popular histories dramatize the permanent Carnegie-Frick breach with stories that, when Carnegie sought reconciliation late in life, Frick refused and effectively answered that he would meet Carnegie in hell. Versions vary in wording and chain of transmission. The estrangement itself is documented; the theatrical wording should be treated as **later anecdote unless traced to a contemporaneous first-hand record**. Its endurance reflects the public need to personify the conflict between the two former partners.

## Works by Andrew Carnegie

Carnegie was a much more prolific author than the handful of books normally associated with him. John C. Van Dyke's bibliography in the 1920 *Autobiography* lists the principal volumes but explicitly says Carnegie also produced many magazine articles, newspaper pieces, speeches, dedicatory addresses, founders' day addresses, and peace pamphlets. J. E. Higgins's bibliographical study, “Andrew Carnegie, Author,” likewise stresses the range. **Margaret Barclay Wilson's *A Carnegie Anthology* (1915)** was compiled during Carnegie's lifetime as a fuller guide.

Dates below distinguish first appearance where it can reasonably be reconstructed from the sources consulted from later collected editions.

### Books and substantial volumes published during his lifetime

1. **_Notes of a Trip Round the World_ (1880).** Early travel narrative based on Carnegie's global journey. Genuine authorship.
2. **_Our Coaching Trip: Brighton to Inverness_ (1882).** Privately/limitedly circulated or early form of the British coaching narrative; later reworked under the better-known *American Four-in-Hand* title. Genuine authorship.
3. **_An American Four-in-Hand in Britain_ (1883).** Travel/coaching book; bibliographies also list New York editions dated 1884, 1886, and later, so publication dates in secondary lists vary by edition rather than necessarily indicating error. Genuine authorship.
4. **_Round the World_ (1884).** Expanded or differently issued travel narrative connected to the earlier world-trip material. Genuine authorship.
5. **_Triumphant Democracy; or, Fifty Years' March of the Republic_ (1886).** Statistical-political celebration of U.S. republican development; revised/expanded editions followed, including late-1880s/1890s issues. Genuine authorship.
6. **_The Bugaboo of Trusts_ (1889).** Essay/pamphlet on industrial combinations and trusts. Genuine authorship.
7. **_The Gospel of Wealth and Other Timely Essays_ (1900).** Collection of articles written principally between 1886 and 1899. Genuine authorship of component essays; the famous “Wealth” essay first appeared in 1889.
8. **_Britain's Appeal to the Gods_ (1901).** Political/international pamphlet or address. Genuine authorship.
9. **_The Empire of Business_ (1902).** Collection of essays/addresses on business and social questions. Some bibliographies list a New York edition as 1903; 1902 is the standard first-edition date. Genuine authorship, collected form.
10. **_Industrial Peace_ (1904).** Labor/arbitration address or pamphlet. Genuine authorship.
11. **_James Watt_ (1905).** Biography in the “Famous Scots”/heroic industrial tradition. Genuine authorship.
12. **_A League of Peace: A Rectorial Address Delivered at the University of St. Andrews, 17th October 1905_ (1906).** Published address. Genuine authorship.
13. **_Edwin M. Stanton: An Address by Andrew Carnegie on Stanton Memorial Day at Kenyon College_ (1906).** Published commemorative address. Genuine authorship.
14. **_The Negro in America: An Address Delivered before the Philosophical Institution of Edinburgh, 16th October 1907_ (1907).** Published address. Genuine authorship.
15. **_Problems of To-Day: Wealth—Labor—Socialism_ (1908).** Collection of economic and social writings. Genuine authorship.
16. **_The Path to Peace Upon the Seas_ (1909).** Peace/naval-policy pamphlet. Genuine authorship.
17. **_Supplement to The Path to Peace_ (1909).** Supplementary peace argument. Genuine authorship.
18. **_War as the Mother of Valor and Civilization_ (1910).** Peace pamphlet challenging the martial theory of civilization. Genuine authorship.
19. **_Address at the Unveiling of a Statue to Burns_ (1912).** Published commemorative address. Genuine authorship.
20. **_The Baseless Fear of War_ (1913).** Peace/international-relations pamphlet. Genuine authorship.

### Major essays and articles

The following are among the most historically significant separate pieces; the list is substantial but not exhaustive of every newspaper contribution:

- **“Wealth”** — *North American Review*, 1889. Later universally associated with the title “The Gospel of Wealth.”
- **“The Best Fields for Philanthropy”** — companion/related philanthropy essay, later collected with the “Gospel” writings.
- **“An Employer's View of the Labor Question”** / related labor essays — Carnegie published several pieces in the 1880s defending arbitration, organization, and industrial conciliation; titles varied across magazine and collected editions.
- **“The Bugaboo of Trusts”** — 1889.
- **“How I Served My Apprenticeship as a Business Man”** — *Youth's Companion*, **23 April 1896**, later reprinted separately.
- **“Distant Possessions—The Parting of the Ways”** — *North American Review*, **August 1898**, anti-imperialist argument against colonial annexation.
- Numerous writings in the *North American Review*, *Century Magazine*, *Forum*, *Contemporary Review*, *Fortnightly Review*, *Nineteenth Century*, *Scottish Leader*, and other British and American newspapers and periodicals.

### Speeches and addresses published separately or in pamphlet form

Carnegie's published-address corpus includes:

- labor arbitration and industrial peace speeches;
- addresses on **Ezra Cornell** and higher education;
- **William Chambers** commemorative material;
- **Edwin M. Stanton** address at Kenyon College (1906);
- St Andrews rectorial addresses;
- peace congress addresses;
- library dedications and founders' day speeches;
- Burns commemorative speeches;
- speeches on business organization, wealth, democracy, education, and international arbitration.

The 1920 autobiography bibliography points researchers to *A Carnegie Anthology* for a fuller contemporary list.

### Testimony

- **_Mr. Carnegie's Testimony Before the United States Commission on Industrial Relations, February 5th, 1915_** (1915). Reprinted from the *New York Herald*, 6 February 1915. Genuine testimony; not a memoir.

### Posthumous works and edited collections

1. **_Autobiography of Andrew Carnegie_ (1920).** Genuine autobiographical manuscript by Carnegie, **posthumously edited and arranged by John C. Van Dyke**. Louise Whitfield Carnegie held the copyright and wrote the preface. Carnegie had stopped substantive work on the memoir around the outbreak of the First World War. It is a primary source but not an unmediated diary: Van Dyke arranged material, and Louise selected him after Carnegie's death.
2. **_Andrew Carnegie's Own Story for Boys and Girls_ (1920).** Posthumous adaptation/abridgment based on Carnegie's autobiographical material; not a separately written late-life children's autobiography.
3. **_The Andrew Carnegie Reader_**, ed. Joseph Frazier Wall (1992). Modern edited selection of Carnegie's writings; Carnegie is author of the original texts, Wall the editor/selector.
4. Numerous later books titled **_The Gospel of Wealth_** are reprints or edited selections of the 1889 essay and related writings, not new Carnegie-authored books.

### No major patent corpus under Andrew Carnegie personally

Carnegie's name appears constantly in histories of patented industrial technology because his firms used and litigated patents. The available record does not support portraying him as a prolific personal patentee. *Carnegie Steel Co. v. Cambria Iron Co.* concerned a patent of **William R. Jones**, not an invention patented by Andrew Carnegie.

## Books and Major Works About Andrew Carnegie

### Major biographies

- **Burton J. Hendrick, _The Life of Andrew Carnegie_, 2 vols. (1932).** Early major posthumous biography; important for access to people and sources relatively close to Carnegie's lifetime.
- **Joseph Frazier Wall, _Andrew Carnegie_ (1970; later editions/reprints).** For decades the standard scholarly biography and still essential.
- **Harold C. Livesay, _Andrew Carnegie and the Rise of Big Business_ (1975; later editions).** Concise analytical biography emphasizing entrepreneurship and organizational development.
- **Peter Krass, _Carnegie_ (2002).** Full modern biography, especially useful on business and personal life.
- **David Nasaw, _Andrew Carnegie_ (2006).** Major modern scholarly biography based on extensive archival research; particularly important on Carnegie's family, politics, Homestead, philanthropy, and international activity.
- **Samuel Bostaph, _Andrew Carnegie: An Economic Biography_ (2015).** Emphasizes economic organization and entrepreneurship.
- **Bernard Alderson, _Andrew Carnegie: The Man and His Work_ (1905).** Lifetime-era biography; valuable as evidence of Carnegie's contemporary public image but close to its subject.
- **Elbert Hubbard, _Andrew Carnegie_ (1909).** Contemporary celebratory/personal portrait. John C. Van Dyke's 1920 bibliography labels it “amusing, but inaccurate,” itself a useful indication of early reputation management.

### Family, correspondence, and close-circle works

- **Burton J. Hendrick and Daniel Henderson, _Louise Whitfield Carnegie: The Life of Mrs. Andrew Carnegie_ (1950).** Essential companion for Louise and domestic life.
- **John A. Poynton, _A Millionaire's Mail Bag_ (1915).** By Carnegie's secretary; close-circle view and document-oriented source.
- **Margaret Barclay Wilson, _A Carnegie Anthology_ (1915).** Contemporary compilation of Carnegie materials and bibliography.
- **_Memorial Addresses on the Life and Work of Andrew Carnegie_ (1920).** Posthumous commemorative collection.
- **_Memorial Service in Honor of Andrew Carnegie on his Birthday, Tuesday, November 25, 1919_** (Pittsburgh, 1919). Contemporary memorial source.

### Business and U.S. Steel

- **James Howard Bridge, _The Inside History of the Carnegie Steel Company: A Romance of Millions_ (1903).** Early detailed insider/near-contemporary narrative of the steel company; indispensable but should be checked against partnership papers.
- **Abraham Berglund, _The United States Steel Corporation_ (1907).** Early analysis of the consolidation.
- **Arundel Cotter, _The Authentic History of the United States Steel Corporation_ (1916).** Early U.S. Steel history.
- **Joseph Frazier Wall, ed., _The Andrew Carnegie Reader_ (1992).** Primary-text selections contextualized by a major biographer.

### Homestead and labor

- **Paul Krause, _The Battle for Homestead, 1880–1892: Politics, Culture, and Steel_ (1992).** Major scholarly study placing the strike in local political and social context.
- **William Serrin, _Homestead: The Glory and Tragedy of an American Steel Town_ (1992).** Long history of the town and labor community.
- **David P. Demarest Jr., ed., _“The River Ran Red”: Homestead, 1892_ (1992).** Documentary/history collection tied to the centennial and film.
- **Les Standiford, _Meet You in Hell: Andrew Carnegie, Henry Clay Frick, and the Bitter Partnership That Changed America_ (2005).** Popular dual history emphasizing the partnership and rupture; source of some widely repeated dramatic anecdotes that should be checked against primary records.
- **Jonathan Rees, “Homestead in Context: Andrew Carnegie and the Decline of the Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers.”** Scholarly article situating 1892 within longer antiunion restructuring.

### Historiography, philanthropy, and ideology

- **Matthew Josephson, _The Robber Barons_ (1934).** Highly influential critical synthesis that helped cement the “robber baron” framework. Important as historiography as well as interpretation.
- **Milton Goldin, “Andrew Carnegie and the Robber Baron Myth.”** Revisionist response examining how Carnegie's image was constructed.
- **J. E. Higgins, “Andrew Carnegie, Author,” _Pennsylvania Magazine of History and Biography_ (1964).** Bibliographic/intellectual study of Carnegie as writer.
- **John White, “Andrew Carnegie and Herbert Spencer” (1979).** Study of the Carnegie-Spencer intellectual relationship.
- **Charles Harvey and collaborators, “Andrew Carnegie and the foundations of contemporary entrepreneurial philanthropy,” _Business History_ 53:3 (2011), 425–450.** Analysis of philanthropy as an entrepreneurial system.
- **Duncan Bell, “Racial Utopianism, Empire and the Abolition of War” (2014).** Scholarly analysis of Carnegie's racialized internationalist peace vision.
- **Srdjan Vucetic, “A Racialized Peace?”** Scholarship situating Anglo-American peace ideas within race and international hierarchy.
- Scholarship on Carnegie libraries, educational philanthropy, medical-school reform, and segregation is extensive; the institutional consequences should be distinguished from decisions personally made by Carnegie.

### Johnstown

The National Park Service's **Johnstown Flood National Memorial** research and interpretive materials are among the most useful current documentary guides because they separate club membership, engineering history, casualty figures, and the legal aftermath. Broader Johnstown literature should be read alongside those records rather than using Carnegie's fame as a substitute for dam-history evidence.

## Films, Documentaries, Interviews, and Archival Material

### Major films and documentaries

- **_Andrew Carnegie: The Richest Man in the World_**, *American Experience* (PBS/WGBH), **1997**. Major television documentary with extensive supporting essays and interview-based historical interpretation.
- **_The River Ran Red_**. Documentary on the 1892 Homestead conflict; a full catalog record/transcript is preserved by the American Archive of Public Broadcasting. Particularly useful for labor-community memory and the battle narrative.
- **_The Johnstown Flood_** (Charles Guggenheim, 1989). Academy Award-winning documentary short shown in the Johnstown museum context; Carnegie is relevant through the South Fork Club story rather than as sole subject.

### Recorded talks and interviews about Carnegie

- **David Nasaw on Andrew Carnegie**, Carnegie Council for Ethics in International Affairs. Recorded talk/interview material associated with publication of Nasaw's biography; useful for Carnegie's peace movement, First World War disillusionment, and the problems of reconciling business and philanthropy.
- **Margaret Carnegie Miller interview (1980)**, Columbia University Rare Book & Manuscript Library, Carnegie Corporation of New York Records. Recorded testimony by Carnegie's daughter.

### Principal archival collections

#### Library of Congress — Andrew Carnegie Papers

The **Andrew Carnegie Papers** are the principal personal archive: approximately **67,400 items**, **304 containers**, about **72 linear feet**, dated **1803–1935** with bulk material from roughly 1890–1919. The collection contains correspondence, speeches, articles, financial and philanthropic material, organizational records, and personal files.

The finding aid identifies major subject areas including labor, investments, education, anti-imperialism, peace, race/African-American affairs, philanthropy, and the steel industry. Correspondents include Frick, Phipps, Schwab, Morgan, Rockefeller, Roosevelt, Wilson, Twain, Booker T. Washington, Gladstone, Morley, Spencer, and many others.

#### University of Pittsburgh — Henry Clay Frick Business Records / Homestead archives

Pitt's archival collections preserve **Henry Clay Frick Business Records** and a dedicated Homestead Strike research portal. These holdings include correspondence surrounding the 1892 lockout, Pinkerton arrangements, business records, legal/inquest material, and other documentation critical for testing Carnegie's retrospective account against contemporaneous management communications.

#### University of Pittsburgh — Carnegie Steel Company records

The **Records of the Carnegie Steel Company** include Andrew Carnegie material, corporate records, and business records. They are essential for reconstructing organization, partnership structure, production, and the transition to U.S. Steel.

#### Columbia University — Carnegie Corporation of New York Records

Columbia's Rare Book & Manuscript Library holds extensive **Carnegie Corporation of New York records**, including digitized philanthropic records, grant ledgers, journals, oral histories, and audiovisual material. Columbia explicitly notes that Carnegie's main personal papers are at the Library of Congress; its collections are strongest for institutional philanthropy.

#### Carnegie Birthplace Museum, Dunfermline

The museum preserves the birthplace context, family history, objects, and educational material on Carnegie's Scottish origins, migration, Booker T. Washington relationship, and philanthropy.

#### University of St Andrews

University collections contain substantial material on Carnegie's rectorship, donations, Scottish university trust, ceremonial relationship with the university, and the family's repeated travel between the United States and Scotland.

#### Georgia Archives / Carnegie family album

Digitized Carnegie family photographs include Louise, Margaret, and European/Mexican travel material, useful for reconstructing family geography and the visual self-presentation of the household.

#### National Park Service — Johnstown Flood National Memorial

NPS records and interpretive research are central for the South Fork Club, Thomas Carnegie, Andrew Carnegie's membership, dam history, casualty estimates, and legal aftermath.

#### Cooper Hewitt / Andrew Carnegie Mansion

Cooper Hewitt preserves the Carnegie mansion and provides architectural history for the 2 East 91st Street residence.

## Honors, Offices, Decorations, and Public Appointments

Carnegie never held elected political office, but accumulated an unusual number of civic and honorary distinctions. The most important documented examples include:

- **Rector, University of St Andrews**, elected in 1903 and again in 1905; this was a prestigious representative university office rather than an academic teaching post.
- **President, Peace Society of New York**, accepted in 1907 after initially declining.
- **French Legion of Honor**, received in connection with his international peace activity; Carnegie's autobiography describes it as his first decoration and uses the English rendering “Knight Commander.”
- **Freedoms of cities and burghs:** the editor of the 1920 autobiography reported that Carnegie had received fifty-four civic freedoms in Britain and Ireland. The figure is useful as a contemporary compilation but has not been independently re-counted for this dossier.
- **Honorary degrees and university distinctions:** conferred by numerous institutions in the United States and Britain in connection with education, philanthropy, literature, and peace work.
- **Nobel Peace Prize nominations:** official Nobel records document nominations, including 1908 and 1913; **he never won the Nobel Peace Prize**.
- **Taxonomic honor:** *Diplodocus carnegii* was named for him as patron of the Carnegie Museum expedition, not because he discovered or scientifically described the dinosaur.

No evidence was found of an elected legislative, gubernatorial, diplomatic, military-command, or cabinet office. His influence operated primarily through money, publishing, private correspondence, civic institutions, and access to officeholders.

## Health, Illness, and Psychological Crises

- **1886 typhoid fever:** Carnegie became seriously ill at Cresson, Pennsylvania, during the same year in which his brother Thomas and mother Margaret died. The clustering of illness and bereavement was one of the most severe personal crises of his middle life.
- **Statistical overwork while preparing _Triumphant Democracy_:** in his autobiography Carnegie described a serious illness associated with the strain of simultaneously running businesses and immersing himself in statistical research. This is a self-report rather than a modern diagnosis.
- **Post-1914 decline:** Louise Carnegie wrote that the First World War destroyed the peace project that had dominated his later life and that his “heart was broken.” This is an intimate psychological description, **not a clinical diagnosis of depression**.
- **Influenza and pneumonia:** Louise records a severe influenza attack followed by two serious bouts of pneumonia after 1914, which she believed accelerated old age.
- **Final illness:** contemporaneous reports identified bronchial pneumonia as the immediate cause of death in August 1919.
- **Addiction:** no credible evidence found in the sources consulted establishes a substance addiction in Andrew Carnegie. Reports of heavy drinking concern his brother Thomas in some biographies and should not be transferred to Andrew.

## Death / Later Life / Present Status

Andrew Carnegie is deceased. He died on **11 August 1919** at Shadowbrook in Lenox, Massachusetts, of bronchial pneumonia as reported contemporaneously, after years that included influenza and recurrent pneumonia. He was buried at Sleepy Hollow Cemetery.

His principal institutional creations continued independently of the family. The Carnegie organizations multiplied, merged, changed names, or specialized over the twentieth and twenty-first centuries. Carnegie Corporation of New York, founded in 1911, began doing business as **Andrew Carnegie Foundation** in 2026. Carnegie Mellon University, Carnegie Hall, Carnegie Museums, Carnegie Science, the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, the Carnegie Foundation for the Advancement of Teaching, Carnegie Hero Fund, Carnegie Trusts in Britain and Scotland, and the Peace Palace survive in different institutional forms.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Industrial legacy

Carnegie became one of the canonical figures in the creation of large-scale American industrial capitalism. His organization demonstrated how technological adoption, managerial accounting, vertical integration, private partnerships, and relentless reinvestment could create cost leadership in a capital-intensive industry.

His company became a core component of **U.S. Steel**, whose creation marked a new era of giant corporate consolidation. The transaction also shifted symbolic economic power from the individual owner-industrialist toward Morgan-style finance and publicly capitalized corporate organization.

### Labor legacy

**Homestead** is inseparable from Carnegie's name. It is studied as:

- a turning point in the decline of craft unionism in steel;
- a case study in private security and industrial violence;
- a conflict over property rights and workers' control of production;
- a test of state power during labor disputes;
- an example of contradiction between public industrial-reform rhetoric and private antiunion strategy;
- a site of racial division as well as class conflict.

The Homestead archive has made it increasingly difficult to sustain the older apologetic narrative that Frick acted entirely against Carnegie's wishes.

### Philanthropic legacy

Carnegie helped institutionalize a particular model of **large-scale strategic philanthropy**:

- give during life;
- endow permanent organizations;
- use expert administration;
- prefer institutions over direct alms;
- require matching/local responsibility where possible;
- invest in education, information, science, culture, and civic capacity.

Modern foundations from Rockefeller onward developed in dialogue with this model, even when they rejected elements of Carnegie's paternalism.

### Library legacy

The physical Carnegie library remains one of the most geographically dispersed monuments ever attached to one private donor. Thousands of towns inherited recognizable civic buildings, but their endurance depended on local taxpayers and librarians. The libraries therefore represent both private philanthropy and the expansion of municipal public-service capacity.

### Science and education

Carnegie-funded organizations became major actors in astrophysics, genetics, biology, geophysics, higher education, teacher pensions, technical education, museums, and professional standards. *Diplodocus carnegii* turned his name into a taxonomic and museum-cultural artifact independent of steel.

### Peace legacy

The Peace Palace and Carnegie Endowment gave durable institutional form to his arbitration project. The First World War discredited his expectation that elite rationality and interdependence were sufficient to stop war, but international adjudication, arbitration, and peace research continued through organizations he funded.

### Reputation cycles

Carnegie's posthumous image has oscillated rather than moving in a single direction:

1. **Self-made benefactor:** dominant in memorial literature and schoolbook biography.
2. **Robber baron:** emphasized by Progressive/labor memory and works such as Josephson's *The Robber Barons*.
3. **Managerial entrepreneur:** postwar business history stressed organization, cost accounting, technology, and strategy.
4. **Contradictory reform capitalist:** later biographies placed Homestead and “Gospel of Wealth” in the same frame.
5. **Philanthropic-system founder:** twenty-first-century philanthropy scholarship treats him as an early architect of strategic/entrepreneurial giving.
6. **Imperial/racial internationalist:** recent scholarship has examined the racial assumptions inside his visions of Anglo-American-led peace and global order.

No one of these interpretations exhausts the documentary record.

## Research Notes on Uncertainty and Source Conflict

- **1901 sale price:** different figures represent the Carnegie complex, Carnegie Steel interests, broader U.S. Steel consolidation, and Carnegie's personal proceeds. This dossier preserves the distinction rather than selecting one figure as universally “the price.”
- **Homestead death count:** sources vary according to immediate deaths versus later deaths from wounds. At least ten is a conservative standard for the battle/immediate toll.
- **First shot at Homestead:** unresolved.
- **Johnstown responsibility:** Carnegie's club membership is established; individual engineering causation is not.
- **Armor-plate scandal:** company-level misconduct/investigative findings are well documented; evidence for Andrew Carnegie personally ordering falsification is insufficient in the consulted record.
- **Frick litigation:** allegations were serious but settlement prevented final adjudication on the merits.
- **Religious label:** “agnostic,” “freethinking,” “heterodox Protestant,” and “religious humanist” each capture different features; Carnegie's own language changed over time.
- **“Richest man in world”:** valid as a historical epithet, not a globally audited ranking.
- **Newspaper holdings:** the autobiography editor's figure of eighteen British newspapers records the scale of the political press venture, but ownership and control were not identical across every title.
- **Bibliography:** Carnegie produced hundreds of shorter pieces. The works section includes principal books and historically significant pamphlets/articles; *A Carnegie Anthology* (1915), the Library of Congress papers, and periodical databases are the appropriate starting points for an item-by-item exhaustive bibliography.
