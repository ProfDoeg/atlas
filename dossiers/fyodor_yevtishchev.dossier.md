# Fyodor Adrianovich Yevtishchev: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Fyodor Yevtishchev’s public identity was constructed within nineteenth-century circus, panopticon, dime-museum, medical, and newspaper cultures. Many surviving “biographical” details originated in advertising copy designed to sell admission tickets. His birth name, parentage, birth date, and birthplace are consequently disputed.

This dossier uses the following labels:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a contemporary scientific report, archival record, photograph, advertisement, or independently corroborating contemporary newspapers.
- **Reported fact:** stated in contemporary reporting or later scholarship but not independently documented here.
- **Publicity claim:** circulated by managers or promotional publications and not reliable as literal biography.
- **Allegation or theory:** proposed without sufficient documentary confirmation.
- **Rumor or myth:** demonstrably fabulous, chronologically impossible, or unsupported.
- **Unresolved:** surviving evidence permits no secure conclusion.

The name requested by the user—**Fyodor Adrianovich Yevtishchev**—is retained in the title, but the strongest available Russian local-history reconstruction identifies the performer originally as **Fedor Petrov**, the biological son of Petr Ivanov and Mavra, rather than Adrian Yevtishchev’s biological son.

## The Identity and Birth Dispute

### Version A: “Fedor Adrianovich Jeftichew,” born in 1868

**Reported fact, not securely documented.** English-language reference works commonly state that he was born in Saint Petersburg in 1868 and was Adrian Yevtishchev’s son. A frequently repeated exact date is 14 January 1868.

This version underlies most modern biographies and the familiar patronymic “Adrianovich.” It accords with his presentation as Adrian’s son in European exhibitions and with a reported age of sixteen upon reaching the United States in 1884.

No birth certificate, baptismal entry, or other primary civil record supporting the Saint Petersburg/1868 version was located in the consulted material.

### Version B: Fedor Petrov, born in Berezniki in January 1870

**Reported archival reconstruction and strongest available local account.** Kostroma-region researcher Sergei N. Toropov identifies the boy as Fedor Petrov, born in January 1870 in Berezniki, Spasskaya volost, Kologriv district, Kostroma province. Toropov names his parents as the peasants **Petr Ivanov** and **Mavra**, formerly serfs or “temporarily obligated” peasants on the estate of N. D. Pushchina, previously Fonvizina.

This reconstruction fits Rudolf Virchow’s 1873 description of the boy as three years old and from Kostroma province. It also explains the alternate professional name **Theodore Petroff** and indicates that “Adrianovich Yevtishchev” was an assumed public identity created when Fedor and Adrian were marketed as father and son.

Toropov states that archival documents for the reported Moscow University examination have not yet been located. His account is therefore more specific and locally grounded than the received circus biography, but the underlying birth entry is not reproduced on the consulted webpage.

### Version C: Finland

**Erroneous or unresolved report.** Several 1904 obituary notices called Jo-Jo a native of Finland. A modern sideshow website likewise claims he was born in Finland to Adrian Maximovich Jefticheff and a woman named Nadia Petrova.

No documentary support for this version was found. The Kostroma provenance is older, appears in Virchow’s medical description, and is repeated by contemporary show publicity. “Finland” may reflect journalistic confusion within the geography of the Russian Empire.

### Assessment

The most internally consistent reconstruction is:

1. Fedor was born in January 1870 in Berezniki, Kostroma province.
2. His biological parents were probably Petr Ivanov and Mavra.
3. He was paired professionally with the older hypertrichotic performer Adrian Yevtishchev.
4. Their managers represented them as father and son.
5. That commercial identity became his durable public and historical name.

This remains a reasoned reconstruction, not a conclusively proven civil identity.

## Family and Ancestry

### Probable biological family

- **Petr Ivanov:** identified by Toropov as Fedor’s biological father.
- **Mavra:** identified as his biological mother.
- Their surname arrangements are unclear. “Petrov” could function either as a surname or as a patronymic-derived public form.
- No siblings or wider ancestry are documented in the consulted sources.
- No reliable information was found concerning the parents’ later lives, deaths, property, or relationship with Fedor after he entered exhibition work.

### Adrian Yevtishchev

Adrian—also rendered Andrian, Adrien, Adrian Maximovich Jefticheff, or Andrian Evtikhiev—was an older Kostroma peasant affected by comparable extensive hypertrichosis.

Rudolf Virchow described Adrian in 1873 as approximately fifty-five years old and the accompanying Fedor as three. The pair were publicly presented as father and son. Their shared condition made that claim commercially persuasive, although Toropov’s reconstruction indicates that the relationship was adoptive, contractual, or wholly theatrical.

Adrian reportedly came from **Korovino**, near Fedor’s village of Berezniki in Kostroma province. Toropov describes the villages as neighboring settlements near present-day Manturovo.

Contemporary and later reports portray Adrian as poorly educated and heavily dependent on alcohol. Virchow reportedly remarked that while traveling he subsisted largely on sauerkraut and vodka. Later accounts say that after returning to Russia he drank heavily and died soon afterward. These reports are plausible but do not establish a precise death date or medical cause.

Claims that Adrian was violent, feral, incapable of civilization, or captured in a cave are advertising mythology.

### Alleged mother in publicity traditions

Some promotional or derivative accounts claim that Fedor was Adrian’s illegitimate child by an unnamed peasant woman who died. A much later sideshow website names “Nadia Petrova” and describes her as a professional mistress.

No primary evidence for either story was found. The named “Nadia Petrova” tradition conflicts with Toropov’s identification of Mavra and should be treated as **unverified claim**.

### Adult family

No reliable source located in this research identifies:

- a spouse;
- a long-term partner;
- children;
- legally adopted relatives;
- an heir;
- probate proceedings;
- or family members present at his death.

## Childhood and Early Life, circa 1870–1873

Fedor’s visible hypertrichosis developed in early childhood. Virchow’s 1873 medical descriptions establish that a very young Fedor and the much older Adrian were already being examined and exhibited together.

Toropov reports that at about three years old Fedor’s appearance attracted the attention of scholars from Moscow University, who examined both Fedor and Adrian. He cautions that documentation of this particular examination has not been recovered.

Nothing dependable is known about Fedor’s domestic childhood, religious upbringing, games, early education, or relationship with his biological parents. Descriptions of a childhood spent wild in a forest or cave belong to later commercial mythology.

---

## Education and Formation

No school enrollment, tutor, or formal qualification is documented.

Evidence of his later abilities nevertheless contradicts the “wild child” persona:

- American arrival reporting described him as able to sign his name.
- Contemporary reports state that he spoke Russian and German.
- English-language sources initially describe limited English, followed by later accounts of greater competence.
- New Zealand reporting in 1890 called him “fairly intelligent” and said he spoke several languages.
- Photographs show him conventionally dressed and carefully groomed.
- Accounts of his act describe deliberate timing: he conversed or submitted to inspection before barking or growling on cue.

His practical education was probably acquired through travel, managers, interpreters, and repeated public performance. Nicholas A. Forster appears to have been a decisive organizer and intermediary, although he is better understood as a manager than a teacher.

## Early Career: Russian and European Exhibition

### 1873 scientific examinations

In June 1873 Rudolf Virchow presented **“Die russischen Haarmenschen”** (“The Russian Hairy People”) to the Berlin Medical Society. The paper appeared in *Berliner klinische Wochenschrift*, volume 10, no. 29, pages 337–339. Virchow also discussed “Andrian, 55 years, and his son Fedor, 3 years old, from the Government of Kostroma” in the *Zeitschrift für Ethnologie*.

These medical notices are among the earliest and strongest chronological anchors in Fedor’s life. They establish:

- the presence of Adrian and Fedor in Berlin by 1873;
- their Kostroma provenance;
- the approximate ages fifty-five and three;
- extensive hair growth;
- and their public presentation as father and son.

The phrase “his son” documents the identity being presented to observers, but does not independently prove biological paternity.

Virchow associated their hair growth with deficient dental development. Later medical literature cited the Russian “hairy men” as examples of congenital hypertrichosis with dental anomalies.

### Paris, 1873

On 17 October 1873 *Le Figaro* published “Les Coulisses de l’Homme-Chien,” an account of the “dog-man” exhibition. Fedor and Adrian were shown in Paris under animalizing labels that translated their medical condition into theatrical spectacle.

The impresario or intermediary was **Nicholas A. Forster**—variously rendered N. A. Ferster or Forster—who became an important figure in Fedor’s career. Toropov dates Forster’s association with the performers to this period.

### European route

Sources agree that Adrian and Fedor traveled through major European cities, particularly in France and Germany. Exact itineraries for 1873–1883 are incomplete. Claims that they visited “nearly all Europe” are plausible publicity generalizations but not fully reconstructable stop by stop.

Accounts of a single 1872–1874 European tour and accounts of a renewed 1883 Berlin-panopticon appearance may refer to separate circuits or may reflect confused retrospective chronology.

### Separation from Adrian

Toropov reports that Adrian returned to Russia after the European tour, drank heavily, and died. Other biographies place Adrian’s death before Fedor’s American career. No exact date, place, burial record, or estate record was found.

Fedor continued performing without him, retaining the constructed surname and filial identity.

---

## Recruitment for the United States, 1883–1884

Fedor was reportedly exhibited again as **Theodore Petroff** before an agent associated with P. T. Barnum recruited him.

He reached New York on or about **12 October 1884**, reportedly aboard the *City of Chicago*. A New York newspaper account described him the following day. He was accompanied by Nicholas Forster and Forster’s wife, presented as custodians responsible to the Russian government.

Publicity claimed:

- that the czar had personally authorized the journey;
- that substantial bonds guaranteed Fedor’s safe return;
- that a representative of the czar would supervise him;
- and that the Russian government regarded him as a royal protégé.

These claims helped manufacture rarity, diplomatic importance, and exotic authenticity. No Russian imperial permit, bond, or official guardianship record was located.

One syndicated account later asserted that S. H. Barrett’s “money and diplomacy” had secured Russian permission and named Nicholas Forster as the czar’s agent. This should be treated as **contemporary publicity claim**, not verified diplomacy.

## Career with Barnum and American Managers, 1884–1888

### Creation of “Jo-Jo”

Barnum’s organization billed Fedor as:

- **Jo-Jo the Dog-Faced Boy**;
- **the Human Skye Terrier**;
- a “bright boy with a dog’s face”;
- and a supreme contradiction or mystery of nature.

His appearance was staged in tension with his behavior. He wore respectable clothing, sometimes described as a Russian cavalry uniform, while publicity insisted on an animal origin. During inspection or performance he growled, barked, snarled, and exposed his few teeth on cue.

The act did not require him to claim continuously that he was nonhuman. Its commercial structure depended on oscillation: an apparently educated youth was framed as the partially civilized remnant of a wild canine creature.

### The official life story

A circa-1885 Barnum courier announced that Adrian and Fedor had been found living in a remote cave in the Kostroma forests. Hunters supposedly tracked them down; Adrian fought “with all the fury of an enraged mastiff”; the pair were captured; Adrian could never be civilized; and the younger Fedor was taught to wear clothes and speak.

This is a **documented publicity narrative** and a **biographical falsehood**. Fedor had been publicly examined in European cities since early childhood. Adrian was a Russian peasant and performer, not an unknown cave-dweller.

### 1885 Barnum tour

Barnum’s Great Barnum–London Show opened at Madison Square Garden on 16 March 1885 and closed at Newburgh, New York, on 24 October. It traveled through ten northeastern states and made two Canadian stops. Jo-Jo received prominent billing alongside Jumbo and a heterogeneous program advertised as an “ethnological” or living-curiosity congress.

An advertisement for Brattleboro, Vermont, on 21 July 1885 placed Jo-Jo among the show’s leading attractions.

### April 1885 medical inspection

A special reception for journalists and physicians occurred at Madison Square Garden in April 1885. The *New York Herald* described him as about five feet tall, with most of his body covered in long, tow-colored hair.

As physicians handled and inspected him, he reportedly snarled, displayed three canine teeth, and asked his guardian whether he could bite them. That incident may have been spontaneous irritation, staged behavior, or both. The source shows how medical inspection itself became part of the entertainment.

The physicians reportedly declined to speculate publicly about his ancestry. Modern medicine does not support the human–dog hybridity implied by the publicity.

### Work intensity

Later scholarship, drawing on Robert Bogdan’s history of the freak show, reports that a New York museum manager scheduled **twenty-three Jo-Jo performances in a twelve- to fourteen-hour day**. The figure illustrates the repetitive and physically demanding structure of dime-museum labor, although the exact venue and date are not clear in the accessible sources.

### Income

A University of Michigan student exhibit claims he earned **$500 per week in 1886** and calls him one of Barnum’s best-paid performers. A 1904 obituary said his “freak salary” reached several hundred dollars weekly.

These are **reported figures**, not verified through a surviving contract or ledger. They indicate high gross remuneration by contemporary labor standards, but do not disclose:

- Forster’s or other managers’ commission;
- travel and lodging deductions;
- currency arrangements;
- access to his earnings;
- taxes;
- savings;
- or the value of any estate.

### Character and private conduct

Contemporary reports commonly described him as amiable, playful, intelligent, and approachable when not performing anger. A report stated that he disliked tobacco, chewed gum, and enjoyed gumdrops. The same anecdote said a reporter tricked him with a banana peel filled with tobacco, provoking a flash of anger.

Such details are minor but useful because they contradict the “savage” script. They remain journalistic observations rather than intimate testimony.

## Australia and New Zealand, 1889–1890

### Melbourne, 1889

By July 1889 Jo-Jo was appearing at a Melbourne waxworks. A *Melbourne Punch* notice, reprinted in New Zealand, described:

- formal evening dress;
- a spotless white shirt;
- long silky facial hair;
- and only his eyes clearly visible through it.

The writer joked that he would not look out of place among fashionably dressed young men on Bourke Street. The notice said his American manager had made “a barrel of money” from him, while doubting whether the Australian enterprise would do as well.

The show also included a pianist, a performer billed as a Circassian youth, a fortune-telling “Gypsy Queen,” and the conjuror Professor Tregaski. The manager or promoter was identified as **Clark**.

### New Zealand, 1890

On 25 February 1890 Jo-Jo opened or continued an engagement at the **Exchange Hall, Wellington**, in association with an Australian waxworks collection of more than 160 figures and other attractions. Advertisements and notices described him as the “celebrated and only Russian dog-faced boy.”

The exhibition was still being patronized on 4 March, when its last five nights were announced.

By 15 March he was advertised in Auckland. These notices establish a New Zealand route from Wellington to Auckland, although further stops have not been reconstructed.

The New Zealand press repeated the cave story while simultaneously acknowledging that he was an ordinarily formed Russian youth with abnormal hair growth, intelligence, and several languages.

## Later Career, 1890–1903

Fedor continued touring internationally during the 1890s. Academic reconstruction indicates appearances in the United States, Europe, and Australia, but the accessible evidence becomes fragmentary after the Australasian tour.

Photographic studios associated with his image include:

- **Charles Eisenmann**, 229 Bowery, New York;
- **Fred Park Swasey**;
- studios or surviving cards associated with Boston, Chicago, Wooster, Seville, Waterville, Burlington, and Indianapolis.

A surviving Indianapolis photograph bears wording equivalent to “The Original and Only Living Man with a Dog’s Head.” Studio locations may indicate tour stops, although photographs could also have been distributed far from where they were taken.

Barnum died in 1891. Fedor’s later career continued within the broader Barnum & Bailey organization and through museums, waxworks, and independent managers. It is unsafe to describe every post-1891 engagement as a personal Barnum contract.

A modern exhibit states that he stayed with Barnum’s circus until 1900. The more careful academic assessment is that newspaper coverage declines around 1900. No termination agreement or precise retirement date has been found.

A University of Michigan exhibit also claims that in 1898 Fedor and other performers successfully protested the word “freak” and obtained the designation “prodigies.” This is a **reported fact requiring primary verification**; no petition, contract, or contemporary news report confirming Fedor’s individual role was located in this research.

## Medical History

### Hypertrichosis

Fedor’s condition is conventionally identified as congenital generalized hypertrichosis. Nineteenth-century observers described long, fine, wavy hair across the face and much of the body, reportedly excluding the palms and soles.

Toropov reproduces a description attributed to physicians Le Double and Broca:

- the forehead, eyelids, ears, cheeks, lips, chin, margins of the nostrils, ear openings, neck, trunk, and limbs were covered in long, fine, wavy, predominantly light hair;
- body hair was thinner than the facial and scalp hair;
- thicker tufts appeared near the neck and upper back.

The exact modern genetic subtype cannot be assigned securely without clinical or genetic evidence.

### Dental anomalies

Virchow and later medical writers associated the Russian hairy men with defective dentition. At the 1885 inspection Fedor was reportedly seen displaying only three canine teeth. Congenital hypertrichosis syndromes may coexist with dental abnormalities, but the available evidence does not provide a complete dental chart.

### General health

Later sources describe intermittent poor health or dissatisfaction with his career, but no clinical records have surfaced. No reliable documentation was found for:

- psychiatric illness;
- addiction;
- surgery;
- major injury;
- hospitalization before the terminal illness;
- or a chronic internal disease.

### Pneumonia and death

Contemporary international notices agree that he died of pneumonia while in Salonica. Some say pneumonia followed illness contracted during a Greek tour. No attending physician’s report or death certificate was located.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

No evidence was found that Fedor held public office, joined a political party, published political opinions, participated in a religious organization, or developed a documented philosophical program.

His career nevertheless intersected with contemporary intellectual currents:

- evolutionary debate;
- medical teratology;
- anthropology;
- comparative anatomy;
- popular fascination with “missing links”;
- and the commercial classification of disabled or physically unusual people as racial, animal, or evolutionary types.

These frameworks were imposed by physicians, journalists, showmen, and audiences. They should not be attributed to Fedor as his own beliefs.

His performance itself was a practiced theatrical work. The contrast between formal dress, multilingual speech, docility, sudden snarling, and canine billing formed a repeatable act. No scripts, diaries, or first-person artistic statements survive in the consulted sources.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Adrian Yevtishchev

**Documented professional relationship; biological relationship disputed.** Adrian was Fedor’s early co-performer and publicly represented father. The two were examined and exhibited together in 1873. The shared surname and patronymic probably derive from this pairing.

### Nicholas A. Forster and Mrs. Forster

**Documented or strongly reported managerial relationship.** Forster organized or accompanied European exhibitions and brought Fedor to the United States. The couple traveled with him in 1884 and were presented as official guardians. The claim that Forster represented the czar is unverified promotional material.

### P. T. Barnum

**Documented commercial association.** Barnum’s enterprise transformed Fedor into “Jo-Jo” in the United States and distributed the cave-capture biography. Whether Barnum personally negotiated with Fedor, rather than through Bailey, Forster, or another agent, is less clear.

### James Anthony Bailey

**Reported commercial association.** Later scholarship states that Bailey secured permission or posted bonds to exhibit Fedor. Bailey was central to the enterprise in which Jo-Jo toured.

### Rudolf Virchow

**Documented examining physician and scientific observer.** Virchow examined or presented Adrian and Fedor in Berlin in 1873 and published two accounts. No evidence indicates an enduring personal relationship.

### Physicians Le Double and Broca

**Reported medical observers.** Later Russian material attributes detailed descriptions of Fedor and Adrian to these physicians. The exact examination dates and original French publications require further bibliographical verification.

### Charles Eisenmann

**Documented photographer–subject relationship.** Eisenmann produced multiple studio portraits of Jo-Jo in New York. These cabinet cards became central to the performer’s advertising and later historical image.

### S. H. Barrett

**Reported promoter.** An 1887 advertisement billed Jo-Jo with Barrett’s New United Monster Shows and attributed his American acquisition to Barrett’s diplomacy. The latter was probably advertising exaggeration.

### Clark and Professor Tregaski

**Documented shared Australian exhibition network.** Clark managed or promoted the Melbourne waxworks engagement in 1889; Tregaski appeared on the same program as a conjuror. No closer personal relationship is known.

### Friends, enemies, patrons, and rivals

No private friendship, romantic relationship, personal enemy, or artistic rivalry is securely documented. Other unusual-body performers shared bills and institutions with him, but co-billing alone does not establish friendship or rivalry.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Financial Questions

### Fabricated biography

**Documented commercial deception.** The cave-capture story was false. It concealed Fedor’s Russian peasant background and long history of organized European exhibition.

Its function was to:

- animalize him;
- present his body as a zoological mystery;
- associate him with the Russian wilderness;
- suggest scientific uncertainty;
- and make barking or snarling appear natural rather than performed.

### Imperial authorization

**Unverified publicity claim.** Advertisements invoked a special permit from the czar, a bond guaranteeing Fedor’s return, and a czarist guardian. No official documentation has been found.

Later repetitions sometimes name **Nicholas II**, which is chronologically impossible for 1884 because Alexander III was then emperor. This is a clear sign of retrospective corruption of the publicity story.

### Labor exploitation

**Documented context; individual contractual conditions unresolved.** Reports of as many as twenty-three daily performances demonstrate an intense schedule. Fedor began exhibition work as a small child and crossed the Atlantic as a minor under managerial supervision.

Whether he freely controlled his contracts and income is unknown. Substantial salaries and professional success can coexist with exploitative presentation and working conditions. No court case, labor complaint, guardianship suit, or wage action by Fedor was found.

### Dehumanizing medical and theatrical display

**Documented fact.** Fedor was handled and scrutinized by physicians before paying or publicity-oriented audiences. Advertisements compared him to dogs and described him as an “incarnate paradox.” His staged request to bite inspectors in 1885 may register irritation, performance, or both.

### “Freak” terminology protest

**Reported but not adequately verified.** A modern educational exhibit says Fedor joined other performers in a successful 1898 objection to the label “freak,” replacing it with “prodigies.” This should not be treated as established until contemporary evidence is located.

### Criminality, litigation, and scandal

No credible evidence was found that Fedor was:

- arrested;
- imprisoned;
- charged with a crime;
- convicted;
- sued;
- investigated for fraud;
- involved in corruption or money laundering;
- associated with intelligence work;
- implicated in political violence;
- or accused of personal abuse.

The principal fraud connected with his name was the promotional fiction created around him, not a crime established against him.

### Adversarial search, including 2024–2026 material

Dedicated searches paired his names with controversy, scandal, allegation, lawsuit, fraud, accusation, conspiracy, criticism, exploitation, and hoax. They produced no evidence of a newly disclosed personal scandal, criminal proceeding, or financial fraud.

The principal contested issues remain:

1. his true name and parentage;
2. false place and date of birth;
3. fabricated cave-capture narrative;
4. questionable czarist guardianship claims;
5. labor exploitation;
6. the Jacko/Bigfoot identification;
7. and modern reuse of his image without adequate distinction between history and publicity.

Recent academic work continues to examine him as an example of staged human–animal hybridity and disability spectacle, rather than uncovering new misconduct by him.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The cave capture

**Story:** Adrian and Fedor lived in a remote Kostroma cave, were hunted down after a violent struggle, and were dragged into civilization.

**Appearance and circulation:** Distributed in Barnum promotional couriers around 1884–1885 and repeated internationally in newspaper advertisements, including New Zealand reporting in 1890.

**Evidence against:** Both performers were known Russian peasants and had undergone public medical inspection and European exhibition since 1873. Their manager arranged their appearances.

**Reputational effect:** The story fixed Jo-Jo in popular memory as a semi-canine wild child and largely displaced his actual rural and professional background.

### Adrian as an untamable animal

**Story:** Fedor’s father could not be civilized and behaved like an enraged mastiff.

**Status:** Publicity fiction. Adrian’s reported alcoholism and peasant manners may have been selectively incorporated, but his international travel and professional exhibition contradict literal wildness.

**Effect:** It allowed promoters to present Fedor as a safer, younger, partially civilized version of the same supposed species.

### Biological father and son

**Story:** Adrian’s hypertrichosis passed directly to his son Fedor.

**Evidence for:** They were publicly presented together; Virchow called Fedor Adrian’s son; both had similar extensive hair growth.

**Evidence against:** Toropov identifies Fedor as the son of Petr Ivanov and Mavra and explains that the father–son identity was created for exhibition.

**Assessment:** Professional/adoptive relationship is better supported by the local reconstruction; biological paternity remains disputed rather than conclusively disproved by an accessible civil record.

### Protégé or ward of the czar

**Story:** The Russian emperor personally protected Fedor, authorized his American journey, demanded a bond, and sent Forster to supervise him.

**Evidence:** Only promotional and newspaper repetition was found.

**Against:** No imperial documentation; later versions anachronistically invoke Nicholas II.

**Effect:** The claim conferred prestige and reassured audiences that a foreign minor had been lawfully transported.

### A human–dog hybrid or evolutionary “missing link”

**Story:** His appearance might reveal descent from, mixture with, or proximity to another species.

**Status:** Pseudoscientific theatrical framing. Fedor was human and had a recognized congenital hair-growth condition. Contemporary physicians’ reluctance to pronounce on “ancestry” was turned into advertising ambiguity.

**Effect:** This framing made his body part of popular debates over evolution and human classification.

### Barking as instinct

**Story:** He barked because canine nature emerged when he became angry.

**Evidence:** He did bark and growl during performances and inspections.

**Interpretation:** The behavior was an element of the act. Contemporary reporting also records ordinary speech, literacy, humor, preferences, and multilingual ability.

### “Jacko,” the Canadian wild man or Bigfoot

**Story:** A supposed apelike creature called Jacko, reported captured near Yale, British Columbia, in 1884, was acquired by Barnum and exhibited as Jo-Jo.

**Circulation:** Proposed in later Bigfoot and cryptozoological literature, including work by Grover Krantz.

**Evidence against:**

- Canadian newspapers quickly exposed the Jacko capture as a likely hoax.
- Fedor had a traceable Russian and European exhibition history before 1884.
- He spoke and wrote, while Jacko was described as an unknown nonhuman creature.
- No acquisition record joins the two stories.

**Assessment:** **Conspiracy theory/cryptozoological myth, unsupported and contradicted by chronology.**

**Effect:** It extended Jo-Jo’s afterlife from circus folklore into North American Bigfoot mythology.

### Substitution by another Jo-Jo

Claims that photographs show Jo-Jo being replaced between 1884 and 1885 occur in Jacko-related summaries. No persuasive photographic or contractual proof was found. Variations in age, grooming, pose, retouching, and image reproduction provide simpler explanations.

**Assessment:** Unverified theory.

### Immense fortune

Obituaries and later articles say his face made his fortune or that he amassed substantial wealth.

High weekly earnings and strong attendance are credible. Retained wealth, ownership, and estate value are undocumented.

**Assessment:** Possibly based on real earnings but exaggerated or unprovable as net worth.

- No autograph manuscript was located in the consulted digital collections.

### Works published under his name or persona

The following are commercial representations, not authored works:

- *Jo-Jo the Dog-Faced Boy* or *Human Skye Terrier* souvenir biography/courier, circa 1884–1885. Authored by Barnum’s publicity apparatus; false first-life narrative.
- Cabinet-card photographs by Charles Eisenmann, circa 1880s.
- Photographs by Fred Park Swasey, approximately 1888–1896.
- Barnum & Bailey posters bearing the Jo-Jo name, including a French Faria/Bourgerie lithograph dated broadly to the 1880s–1890s.
- Advertisements, trade cards, and wax representations using the Jo-Jo persona.

### Source-critical observations

No full-length, archivally exhaustive biography devoted solely to Fedor was identified. Most popular profiles recycle one another and combine:

- Barnum publicity;
- Virchow’s medical account;
- uncertain birth information;
- modern diagnostic terminology;
- and unsourced anecdotes.

Toropov’s local research and Virchow’s contemporary papers are particularly important for correcting the standard biography.

## Death

Fedor died in Salonica while touring or exhibiting in the region.

The strongest contemporary date is **31 January 1904**:

- an Oregon newspaper dated 4 February reported by Vienna cable that he had died in Salonica;
- a New Zealand theatrical column later specified 31 January;
- other obituaries repeated pneumonia as the cause.

Some later sources give **1 February 1904**, possibly owing to dateline, calendar, or transcription differences.

Salonica was then an Ottoman city in Macedonia. Describing it simply as “Greece” is geographically understandable today but politically anachronistic for 1904.

No reliable account was found of:

- the exact venue of his last performance;
- the duration of the illness;
- a hospital;
- attending physicians;
- companions at death;
- burial place;
- funeral;
- repatriation;
- will;
- or estate administration.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Medical legacy

Fedor and Adrian became standard historical examples in medical discussions of congenital generalized hypertrichosis and its association with dental anomalies. Their images circulated far beyond the original examinations.

### Circus history

Jo-Jo remained one of the most recognizable products of Barnum-era human exhibition. His career demonstrates how a real medical condition could be packaged through:

- fabricated geography;
- invented capture;
- imperial authorization;
- zoological comparison;
- scientific inspection;
- studio photography;
- and repeated international syndication.

### Visual legacy

Eisenmann’s portraits have become the dominant surviving representation. Detached from captions and dates, the photographs are frequently misidentified, incorrectly dated, or confused with later hairy-faced performers.

The continued commercial value of original cabinet cards and posters—sometimes estimated in the thousands of dollars at auction—contrasts with the absence of evidence concerning Fedor’s own estate.

### Disability-history reinterpretation

Recent scholarship treats Fedor less as an inexplicable “freak” and more as a working performer whose body was narratively constructed as animal, foreign, and evolutionarily ambiguous. This scholarship also emphasizes his literacy, languages, costuming, and theatrical agency while acknowledging the exploitative conditions of childhood exhibition and relentless scheduling.

### Popular and folkloric afterlife

His name persisted in:

- novelty music;
- carnival jokes and barkers’ cries;
- film references;
- television sideshow imagery;
- wax figures;
- listicles;
- cryptozoological claims;
- and internet circulation of public-domain portraits.

The phrase “Jo-Jo the Dog-Faced Boy” became more durable than the details of the historical individual.

## Atlas Connections

The supplied atlas roster was checked for documented meetings, correspondence, collaboration, rivalry, family relationships, shared institutions, and explicitly acknowledged influence.

### Charles Robert Darwin

**No direct connection established.** Fedor was exhibited during intense public debate about Darwinian evolution, and later scholarship places his publicity within “missing-link” culture. No source consulted shows that Darwin met, corresponded with, examined, mentioned, or was acknowledged by Fedor. Contextual association is not an atlas connection.

### Charles John Huffam Dickens

**No direct connection established.** Dickens was connected in circus historiography with an earlier “What Is It?” performer, not with Fedor. No meeting or influence involving Fedor was found.

### P. T. Barnum

Barnum is central to Fedor’s career but does not appear in the supplied atlas roster under his own name; accordingly he is not entered as an atlas-roster connection.

### Result

**Absence is the finding.** No source-supported connection between Fedor and any named member of the atlas roster was established. Mere contemporaneity, common placement in later freak-show studies, or participation in the same broad intellectual climate was excluded.
