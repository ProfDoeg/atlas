# Arthur Ben: Research Dossier

## Scope, Identity, and Evidentiary Method

This dossier concerns **Arthur Benjamin Chitty III (1947–2024)**, the American librarian, Vietnam veteran, labor organizer, antiwar activist, and coauthor of *From the Folks Who Brought You the Weekend*.

He must be distinguished from his father, **Arthur Benjamin “Ben” Chitty Jr. (1914–2003)**, a University of the South historiographer and writer associated with theories that John Wilkes Booth escaped death in 1865. Search results, bibliographic databases, and even some biographical aggregators regularly conflate father and son. The Booth-exhumation litigation, writings on Sewanee history, and most references to an “Arthur Ben Chitty” before 1947 concern the father, not the subject of this dossier.

Evidence is labeled as follows:

- **Documented fact:** supported by an institutional record, contemporaneous publication, identifiable first-person document, or mutually corroborating sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated by an obituary, memorial account, or other credible secondary source but not independently established here.
- **First-person account:** Chitty’s own recollection, preserved without assuming that every detail has been independently verified.
- **Family testimony:** recollection supplied by his wife or another relative.
- **Disputed or unresolved:** credible sources conflict or the surviving documentation is insufficient.
- **Unsupported/conflated claim:** material apparently referring to another person or not supported by evidence found.

### Death-date discrepancy

Two strong institutional sources give **February 11, 2024**:

- Queens College’s memorial notice says he died on Sunday, February 11.
- VVAW’s obituary says he died February 11 at Calvary Hospital.

The *Sewanee Mountain Messenger* obituary instead gives **February 12, 2024**. Because the conflict cannot be resolved from the consulted material, February 11 is the better-corroborated date, while February 12 remains a published competing date. The *Messenger* also calls him 77, although a person born August 28, 1947, was 76 in February 2024.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Documented/reported fact:** Chitty was the eldest of four children of:

- **Arthur Benjamin Chitty Jr.** (June 15, 1914–February 11, 2003), commonly Arthur Ben Chitty, a University of the South administrator, publicist, historiographer, local historian, and writer.
- **Mary Elizabeth Nickinson Chitty**, commonly **Betty Nick Chitty** or Betty Chitty.

The younger Chitty therefore inherited both his father’s full name and the everyday name “Ben.” This naming continuity is the principal source of later bibliographic confusion.

The family lived in Sewanee’s historic **Chitty House** from approximately 1948 to 2002. The house stood on South Carolina Avenue near the University of the South campus. A Sewanee digital-history project says Arthur Jr. and Betty hosted numerous students there. Ben III thus grew up in a household embedded in university, Episcopal, archival, and historical culture.

### Siblings

VVAW’s obituary identifies three younger siblings:

- **John Abercrombie Merritt Chitty**, who predeceased Ben.
- **Em Turner Chitty**.
- **Nathan Harsh Brown Chitty**.

Ben and John became Eagle Scouts, following their father.

### Ancestry

The consulted sources establish a multigenerational Chitty family rooted in the American South, especially Florida and Tennessee, but they do not provide a fully documented genealogical chain for Ben III’s maternal and paternal ancestry. A privately compiled Chitty genealogy contains earlier family material, but its exact chain to the subject was not independently verified and should not substitute for civil or church records.

### Marriages, partner, and children

**Documented fact:** On **June 24, 1971**, Chitty married **Mary Glen Kuhl** in All Saints’ Chapel at Sewanee. The Rev. James W. Brettmann officiated. A contemporary alumni report described Chitty as a Swarthmore senior majoring in medieval studies and Kuhl as a Yale graduate in anthropology. They spent that summer in Sewanee before returning to Swarthmore.

No consulted source establishes when or under what circumstances this marriage ended. Later memorial notices omit it. No biological children from the marriage were identified.

**Documented fact/family testimony:** Chitty first met historian **Priscilla Murolo** in **1962**, when they were adolescents. They married in **1994**. Murolo became his intellectual collaborator and coauthor.

Through Murolo, Chitty had two stepsons:

- **Max Schultz**
- **Tony Schultz**

Queens College’s obituary also records two grandchildren but does not name them.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1947–1964

Arthur Benjamin Chitty III was born in Sewanee on August 28, 1947. VVAW’s obituary calls him the first of four children.

He attended **Sewanee Public School**. His childhood unfolded within the University of the South’s small, socially interconnected community. His father’s work as a university official and historiographer placed books, institutional memory, church history, and visitors near the center of family life.

He and his brother John participated in scouting and became Eagle Scouts.

Chitty subsequently attended **Indian Springs School**, near Birmingham, Alabama. He did not complete the conventional final year: VVAW’s obituary says he **skipped his senior year** and entered the University of the South in 1964.

Priscilla Murolo later described him as having grown up with “an enticingly comfortable spot in a very small world,” while feeling discontent and wanting something beyond it. That is family interpretation rather than an independently verifiable psychological diagnosis, but it helps explain the movement from Sewanee’s protected academic environment into civil-rights activity and then military service.

## Education and Formation

### University of the South, 1964–1965

Chitty entered the University of the South in **1964**, apparently at about seventeen. He became a member of **Sigma Nu** fraternity.

His university attendance was interrupted by military service. The available sources do not indicate that he completed a degree at Sewanee.

### Selma-to-Montgomery march, 1965

**Reported fact:** In 1965, Chitty joined the Selma-to-Montgomery civil-rights march led by Martin Luther King Jr. VVAW’s obituary says he did so despite his father’s cautions.

The source does not specify which stage or day he joined. Accordingly, it is safer to say that he participated in the broader march rather than claim that he was present at a particular confrontation or at every stage from Selma to Montgomery.

This was an early documented crossing between his family’s establishment Southern environment and mass political activism.

### Naval technical training

After receiving a draft notice or becoming subject to imminent induction in 1965, Chitty chose to enlist in the Navy. He later explained that military service, particularly naval service, had a family precedent. He received advanced training in **electronics** and served as an electronics technician.

### Swarthmore College

Following his 1969 discharge, Chitty enrolled at **Swarthmore College** and studied medieval subjects. A 1971 alumni report described him as a senior majoring in **medieval studies**. He graduated in **1972**.

Chitty later explained his intellectual choice in political terms: amid dismay at contemporary American events, he hoped that studying pre-capitalist society might help explain how the modern world had developed. His widow added that medieval scholarship also offered psychological distance from wartime responsibility and memory.

### Keble College, Oxford

Chitty studied at **Keble College, University of Oxford**, receiving a **B.A. in 1974**, later converted to or followed by an Oxford **M.A.**, according to the obituaries. Oxford’s traditional degree-conversion procedures may explain the phrasing “B.A. (later M.A.),” but no Oxford registry record was consulted.

His field remained medieval literature or medieval studies.

### University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill

By **1978**, Chitty had completed substantial graduate study in English at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. VVAW’s obituary calls him **ABD in English**, meaning that he completed doctoral coursework and requirements short of a dissertation.

He also earned an **M.L.S.**, or Master of Library Science, from UNC in 1978.

Chitty later supplied two reasons for leaving medieval literary scholarship:

1. Academic employment prospects in the mid-1970s were poor.
2. He believed serious medieval scholarship was difficult without direct access to manuscripts and original documentary material.

The library degree provided an occupational transition from medievalist to professional librarian.

## Military Service and Vietnam, 1965–1969

### Entry into the Navy

Chitty’s later accounts use both “drafted” and “enlisted” language. The most precise reconstruction is:

- He expected conscription and was reportedly drafted or called.
- He elected to **enlist in the Navy** rather than enter another service through ordinary induction.

He served from **1965 to 1969**.

### Ship and deployments

VVAW sources consistently identify his ship as the **USS *Richmond K. Turner*** and describe two deployments:

- **1966–1967**
- **1968**

A Sewanee obituary misnames the vessel “Richard K. Turner.” The Navy vessel was the guided-missile destroyer leader/cruiser named for Admiral Richmond Kelly Turner.

Chitty described himself later as a **petty officer**, electronics technician, “shellback,” and veteran of two Vietnam deployments. The available sources do not provide his precise rating, rank progression, duty station for every month, or a complete service record.

### Political change during service

**First-person account:** Chitty said that during his first deployment he accepted the official explanation that the United States was defending democracy against communism and believed the war was being won.

His view changed by the second deployment in 1968. He pointed to:

- The Tet Offensive, which he interpreted as demonstrating that the United States would not win.
- The assassinations of Martin Luther King Jr. and Robert F. Kennedy, which led him to connect the war’s failures with domestic American conditions.
- His own responsibility as a petty officer guiding other sailors into a war he no longer believed in.

### Injury and disability

**Family testimony:** Chitty had injured a knee in high school. During service in Vietnam, an explosion caused a large radio to fall on him, seriously reinjuring it.

Murolo said the damage produced lifelong pain, required regular analgesics, and caused him to walk with a cane for the rest of his life. VVAW publications later identified him as a **disabled Navy veteran**.

No medical, Department of Veterans Affairs, or shipboard casualty record was found in the consulted sources, so the mechanics and severity of the incident rest principally on family testimony and his later public designation as disabled.

### Discharge and American Legion story

Chitty was on terminal leave in August 1969 before discharge. He later recounted visiting a local American Legion hall and asking for a beer. According to him, an older veteran initially refused service because he did not recognize Chitty as a veteran; after Chitty identified Vietnam as his war, the bartender replied, “You lost your war, didn’t you, boy?”

This is a **first-person anecdote** preserved in a 2019 interview. No independent witness or contemporaneous record was found. Its historical importance lies in how Chitty used it: as an example of hostility to Vietnam veterans coming from older veterans, complicating the later popular myth that rejection came exclusively from the antiwar left.

## Antiwar Radicalization and First VVAW Period, 1969–1970s

After discharge and enrollment at Swarthmore, Chitty entered antiwar activity. He joined **Vietnam Veterans Against the War** and counseled young men about avoiding or exiting military service and the draft.

A later VVAW author note says he had been a member since **1968**, while Chitty’s own 2019 account says he joined after discharge and college enrollment, which would place the beginning in late 1969. These versions cannot both be exact unless his first contact or membership preceded formal post-discharge activity. The safest range is **1968–1969**, with active campus organizing clearly underway after August 1969.

Chitty was not a participant in VVAW’s **1971 Winter Soldier Investigation**. A 2019 issue of the PSC newspaper *Clarion* mistakenly identified him as one; the following issue published a correction. He was a VVAW member and antiwar veteran, but this specific participation claim is false.

His early work reportedly included draft counseling. The available record does not establish arrests, prosecutions, or imprisonment arising from that work.

The war ended while he was in North Carolina. In a later speech, Chitty remembered becoming extremely drunk when Saigon fell in 1975. He said the ending was “bittersweet”: the fighting had stopped, but the official acknowledgment, reparations, and political transformation that he considered “peace with honor” had not occurred.

At some point he allowed active involvement with VVAW to lapse. His widow characterized this as an attempt to set Vietnam aside and retreat into scholarship and private life.

## Early Library and Labor Career, 1978–1984

After completing his M.L.S., Chitty entered librarianship.

### Hamden Public Library

In **1980**, he became **computer systems librarian** at the main branch of the **Hamden Public Library** in Connecticut.

He was elected president of the library’s **American Federation of State, County and Municipal Employees** local or chapter. He subsequently became an AFSCME representative to the **Greater New Haven Central Labor Council**.

This period established the combination that defined his later career:

- Technical library systems work.
- Employee representation.
- Cross-union activity.
- Labor history and politics.

His widow described him as an experienced labor activist by age thirty-seven.

## Return to VVAW, 1985

Murolo’s memorial testimony gives a detailed account of Chitty’s return to antiwar veterans’ work.

In spring **1985**, while she was researching how Americans remembered the Vietnam War, Murolo arranged to interview or meet VVAW counter-recruiters **Clarence Fitch** and **Dave Cline**. Chitty accompanied her. According to Murolo, Fitch and Cline became more interested in drawing Chitty back into organizing than in her article.

After returning home, Chitty reportedly took a bottle of whiskey into the bedroom and remained there for at least eighteen hours, confronting memories he had avoided. Within days he rejoined VVAW and volunteered for counter-recruitment work.

This episode is **family testimony**, not medical documentation. It indicates an acute emotional crisis and use of alcohol on that occasion, but it does not establish chronic alcoholism, alcohol dependence, or a psychiatric diagnosis.

Chitty’s stepsons, Max and Tony, also played a formative role. Murolo said their desire to hear and learn from his wartime experience pushed him toward speaking rather than suppressing it.

## Queens College and Library-Systems Career, 1984–2023

### Appointment and transition

Chitty joined **Queens College, City University of New York**, in **1984** as an assistant professor in the Paul Klapper Library.

When the **Benjamin S. Rosenthal Library** opened in 1988, he moved from the professorial line into an administrative **Higher Education Officer** position and became the library’s computer or library systems officer.

His responsibilities over nearly four decades included:

- Library systems administration.
- Acting acquisitions librarian.
- Acting head of technical services.
- Assistant to the chief librarian for special projects.
- Assessment of Queens College’s Federal Depository Library Program.
- Adjunct teaching in the Queens College Graduate School of Library and Information Studies.

### Technical publication

In December **1987**, *Information Technology and Libraries*, volume 6, issue 4, published Chitty’s article **“Indexing for the Online Catalog.”**

The article proposed evaluating online public-access catalogues through three dimensions:

1. The varieties of bibliographic data processed.
2. The indexes constructed.
3. The ways users searched those indexes.

Chitty argued that no single configuration worked equally well for every research purpose. The article is preserved in CUNY Academic Works.

No patents, proprietary inventions, technology companies, or private investments attributable to him were identified.

### Retirement

Chitty retired in **summer 2023**, after approximately thirty-nine years at Queens College. Shortly before retirement he was diagnosed with pancreatic cancer.

## Union Activity at CUNY

### Initial involvement and withdrawal

Because Chitty had been an AFSCME local president, the Queens College chapter of the **Professional Staff Congress** recruited him to train as a grievance counselor.

He later moved from a faculty appointment to a Higher Education Officer line. With little local HEO union activity at the time, he reduced his involvement.

### Conflict over the 1994–1995 fiscal crisis

In his 2019 interview, Chitty recalled attending an HEO chapter meeting during CUNY’s 1994–95 fiscal-emergency period. He said PSC president **Irwin Polishook** told those present that the union would not oppose HEO layoffs because protecting tenured professors, the union’s core constituency, took priority.

This is Chitty’s **first-person account**. No minutes or response from Polishook were located. Chitty said the experience extinguished his interest in union work for a time.

### Renewed union activity

Chitty later returned through:

- The PSC Welfare Fund Advisory Council.
- Workplace health-and-safety work.
- Contract enforcement.
- Representation of concerns crossing formal bargaining-unit boundaries.

Although formally part of the PSC’s HEO chapter, he became an ad hoc health-and-safety advocate for faculty, professional staff, and workers represented by **District Council 37** and building-trades unions.

### Queens College Unions Joint Committee on Quality of Work Life

His principal organizing project was the **Queens College Unions Joint Committee on Quality of Work Life**, created or consolidated around **2006**.

Queens College and VVAW described it as the only sustained multi-union committee of its kind in CUNY. It met monthly for more than a decade.

Participating organizations or crafts included:

- Professional Staff Congress.
- AFSCME District Council 37.
- Teamsters Local 237.
- Plumbers Local 1.
- Painters Local 1969.
- Steamfitters Local 638.
- New York City District Council of Carpenters.
- International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers Local 3.
- International Union of Operating Engineers/Stationary Engineers Local 30.
- New York State Nurses Association.

Issues Chitty identified included:

- Recurrent mold associated with leaks.
- Asbestos abatement.
- Toxic-chemical releases.
- Legionella in cooling-tower water.
- Ventilation failures.
- Poor lighting.
- Buckled sidewalks.
- Workplace violence prevention.
- Inadequate maintenance.
- Defective or corrupt supervision of construction and renovation projects.

His public allegation of “poor or corrupt supervision” concerned institutional construction oversight generally. The consulted sources do not identify a specific accused person, investigation, criminal charge, or adjudicated corruption case involving Chitty.

### Labor politics

Chitty advocated:

- Grassroots workplace organization.
- Cross-craft and cross-bargaining-unit solidarity.
- Contract enforcement.
- Public-sector unions acting as defenders of the common good.
- “Militant solidarity” in response to anti-union legal and political pressures.

Queens College Academic Senate minutes record him in 2016 as a member of the PSC chapter executive committee, reporting on efforts to secure state funding for retroactive CUNY labor settlements.

## Antiwar and Veterans’ Organizing, 1985–2024

### Clarence Fitch Chapter and regional leadership

After 1985 Chitty became a central member of VVAW’s **Clarence Fitch Chapter** in the New York metropolitan area.

Published role descriptions include:

- Chapter co-coordinator.
- New York City contact.
- East Coast coordinator.
- East Coast regional coordinator.
- Member of the New York/New Jersey coordinating committee.

His collaborators included **Clarence Fitch**, **Dave Cline**, **Greg Payton**, **Mike Gold**, **Steve Somerstein**, **Dayl Wise**, **Per-Olof Odman**, and other VVAW and Veterans for Peace organizers.

### Counter-recruitment

Chitty spoke in high schools about military service and challenged recruiting claims. A surviving film clip shown at his memorial reportedly depicts Bronx high-school counter-recruitment work in the late 1980s with Fitch, Cline, and Payton.

He later argued that schools’ legal facilitation of recruitment was ethically incompatible with recruiters’ incomplete presentation of military risks, sexual abuse, disability, veterans’ care, and the political purposes of war.

### VVAW versus VVAW–Anti-Imperialist

Chitty co-signed a New York/New Jersey VVAW open letter warning organizations against **VVAW–Anti-Imperialist**, a rival formation associated with the Revolutionary Communist Party.

The letter alleged that VVAW-AI misrepresented itself as the authentic VVAW, engaged in disruption, and used the older organization’s name dishonestly. It referred to litigation initiated by VVAW and an out-of-court settlement restricting the rival group’s use of the name and insignia.

This was an **organizational polemic and reported legal dispute**, not an accusation against Chitty. It shows him taking a side in a longstanding factional struggle within the antiwar left.

### School of the Americas protest reporting

Chitty reported on protests against the U.S. Army’s School of the Americas at Fort Benning, Georgia, including arrests of activists who chained doors, distributed leaflets, or used blood in symbolic protest.

The located article identifies him as reporter or commentator, not as one of the arrested participants. No evidence was found that Chitty himself was imprisoned for these actions.

### Nicaragua and international solidarity

VVAW materials link Chitty’s chapter to:

- The **Veterans Peace Convoy to Nicaragua**.
- Support for Nicaraguan veterans and communities.
- Opposition to Reagan-administration Central America policy.

A VVAW appeal directed donations through Barry Romo or Chitty. This establishes an organizational financial-handling role for aid, but no accusation of financial misconduct was located.

### Antiwar demonstrations and veterans’ public ritual

Documented appearances include:

- Anti–George W. Bush demonstration during Bush’s United Nations appearance, New York, September 2006.
- Manhattan Veterans Day parade participation in 2008.
- Antiwar veterans’ contingent in the 2011 New York City Veterans Day parade.
- Armistice Day reclamation work.
- Vigil at the Isaiah Wall near the United Nations.
- Opposition to the Iraq and Afghanistan wars.
- Agent Orange advocacy.
- Support for counter-recruitment and veterans’ counseling.
- Advocacy for recognition of Hugh Thompson, who intervened at Mỹ Lai.
- Support for replacing Andrew Jackson with Harriet Tubman on the U.S. twenty-dollar bill.

### Veterans Peace Council

Chitty worked in the **Veterans Peace Council of Metropolitan New York**, a joint project involving local Veterans for Peace and VVAW members.

With Wendy Fisher, he drafted the report **“Peace at Home, Peace Abroad”** following a June 6, 2015 conference in New York. The report linked racism, materialism, militarism, economic power, domestic inequality, and foreign war, drawing explicitly on Martin Luther King Jr.’s 1967 Riverside Church address.

### Later-life membership

Near the end of his life, Chitty purchased or requested **lifetime membership in VVAW** as an explicit sign of continuing solidarity.

## Intellectual, Political, and Religious Development

### From establishment Southern setting to civil-rights participation

Chitty grew up in a prominent Sewanee family with deep University of the South and Episcopal connections. Participation in the 1965 Selma-to-Montgomery march marked an early political break from caution within that environment.

### From military acceptance to anti-imperial analysis

His first deployment began with acceptance of Cold War language about democracy and communism. The Tet Offensive, political assassinations, and his experience of returning to Vietnam as a petty officer altered his interpretation.

His mature writings described Vietnam not as an isolated mistake but as part of an American imperial pattern. He applied the Vietnam analogy to Central America, Grenada, Panama, the Persian Gulf, Somalia, Haiti, the Balkans, Afghanistan, and Iraq.

### Medievalism

Medieval study was both an intellectual investigation of pre-capitalist society and, according to his widow, a refuge from war memory. He abandoned an academic medievalist career because of the labor market and the difficulty of research without manuscript access.

### Labor history

His union experience and Murolo’s training as a labor historian redirected his historical work toward workers, workplaces, race, sex, gender, migration, slavery, class organization, and political economy.

Chitty said the labor-history book was initially “not really my choice.” Murolo’s dissertation director had been invited to write a new general labor history, suggested Murolo instead, and Murolo asked Chitty to help. The result became their major joint work.

### Historical memory

Chitty repeatedly examined how national myths reorganized war memory:

- The claim that the United States could have won in Vietnam.
- The claim that it fought primarily for democracy.
- The idea that antiwar civilians uniformly abused returning veterans.
- The POW/MIA narrative.
- The political use of “support the troops.”
- Military and governmental avoidance of responsibility for war crimes.
- The conversion of Veterans Day from an armistice observance into a celebration of military power.

### Religion

Chitty’s language was principally historical and political rather than confessional. In reviewing Daniel Hallock’s *Hell, Healing and Resistance*, he treated Christian interpretations of guilt and forgiveness respectfully but stopped short of embracing Hallock’s spiritual diagnosis.

VVAW’s obituary reports that he attended **Christ Church Bronxville**. The evidence supports an Episcopal affiliation or practice late in life, but not a detailed account of his private theology.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Priscilla Murolo

Wife, historian, memorial witness, and principal intellectual collaborator. They met in 1962 and married in 1994. Their joint labor history combined her academic formation with his library research, union experience, and historical analysis.

### Mary Glen Kuhl

First documented wife. They married in 1971. The subsequent history and termination of that marriage remain undocumented in the consulted record.

### Max and Tony Schultz

Stepsons. Murolo credited their attention to Chitty’s stories of war and peace with encouraging him to make public use of his experience.

### Clarence Fitch

Black Marine veteran, counter-recruiter, and VVAW organizer. Fitch helped bring Chitty back into VVAW in 1985. Chitty’s New York chapter was later named for him. Chitty also advised the documentary project *Another Brother*, about Fitch’s war service, addiction, recovery, organizing, and AIDS.

### Dave Cline

Vietnam veteran and major VVAW/Veterans for Peace organizer. Cline helped recruit Chitty back into the movement in 1985. Chitty delivered a eulogy at Cline’s funeral in Jersey City on September 19, 2007, and later participated in projects bearing Cline’s name.

### Greg Payton

VVAW collaborator in counter-recruitment and an adviser, with Chitty, to *Another Brother*.

### Steve Somerstein

VVAW organizer and lawyer associated with the 1988 Veterans Peace Convoy to Nicaragua. Chitty wrote his memorial tribute.

### Joe Sacco

Comics journalist who illustrated both major editions of Chitty and Murolo’s labor history. The evidence establishes a publication collaboration; it does not disclose the details of their personal relationship or contractual arrangements.

### Wendy Fisher

Veterans for Peace associate who co-drafted the 2015 “Peace at Home, Peace Abroad” report with Chitty.

### Irwin Polishook

PSC president whom Chitty accused, in retrospect, of prioritizing tenured faculty over HEO employees during CUNY’s 1994–95 crisis. This was a political and union disagreement documented only from Chitty’s account in the sources consulted.

### Arthur Benjamin Chitty Jr.

Father and namesake. The elder Chitty’s academic and archival world helped shape the son’s early environment. The younger man nevertheless departed significantly from his father’s institutional career through naval service, antiwar organizing, public-sector unionism, and labor history.

## Significant Successes and Failures

### Successes

- Maintained a library-technology career at Queens College for nearly four decades.
- Helped manage the transition into the Rosenthal Library and online library systems.
- Built a durable multi-union workplace committee at Queens College.
- Coauthored a labor history that received a starred *Publishers Weekly* review and remained in publication through a substantially revised 2018 edition.
- Sustained counter-recruitment, veterans’ peace, and antiwar work from the mid-1980s to the end of his life.
- Helped preserve VVAW institutional memory through writing, memorials, photography, and documentary advising.

### Abandoned or incomplete paths

- Left his initial Sewanee education for military service.
- Completed doctoral work in English only to ABD status; no dissertation or Ph.D. was identified.
- Abandoned medieval literary scholarship as a profession.
- Temporarily withdrew from both VVAW and union involvement.
- His efforts did not achieve the larger political outcomes he advocated: full reparative settlement with Vietnam, demilitarization of foreign policy, an end to military recruiting in schools, or the remaking of Veterans Day as primarily an armistice observance.

These are documentary outcomes rather than value judgments.

## Illnesses, Injuries, Alcohol, and Death

### Knee injury and chronic pain

Murolo reported an initial high-school knee injury and severe reinjury in Vietnam when an explosion caused a radio to fall on him. The consequences reportedly included:

- Lifelong cane use.
- Chronic pain.
- Analgesic use at least twice daily.
- Recognition in VVAW publications as a disabled veteran.

### Psychological effects of war

Murolo described avoidance, withdrawal, loss of self-assurance, and an intense 1985 confrontation with suppressed wartime memory.

Chitty himself described difficulty sleeping and facing the war’s meaning within the broader VVAW community. He also wrote about guilt for participating in a military system whose firepower killed civilians even when individual personnel did not see or personally identify victims.

No formal diagnosis of post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, or another psychiatric condition was found.

### Alcohol

Two episodes are documented in retrospective testimony:

- Chitty said he became “drunk as a skunk” when the Vietnam War ended in 1975.
- Murolo said he secluded himself with whiskey for approximately eighteen hours after meeting Fitch and Cline in 1985.

These incidents support episodic heavy alcohol use during moments of wartime memory. They do **not**, by themselves, establish addiction or alcohol-use disorder.

### Pancreatic cancer

Queens College reports that Chitty was diagnosed with **pancreatic cancer** shortly before retiring in summer 2023.

He died at **Calvary Hospital in the Bronx** on February 11 or 12, 2024. Pancreatic cancer is the clearly documented terminal illness, though the consulted sources do not reproduce a death certificate or give a more technical immediate cause of death.

His ashes were to be interred in the University Cemetery at Sewanee beside his parents and brother John.

### False association with John Wilkes Booth litigation

Search engines return a Maryland appellate case involving “Arthur Ben Chitty,” a petition to exhume John Wilkes Booth, and a theory that Booth escaped.

**Unsupported as to Chitty III / documented conflation:** Those materials concern his father, Arthur Ben Chitty Jr., identified in the litigation as a historiographer who had researched Booth since the 1950s. They cannot be attributed to Ben Chitty III.

The same warning applies to modern criticism of the elder Chitty’s editorial handling of Black writer Ely Green’s autobiography. That criticism concerns the father’s archival and editorial work, not the son.

### Winter Soldier misidentification

**Published error, formally corrected:** *Clarion* initially described Chitty as a participant in the Winter Soldier Investigation. Its May 2019 correction states that he protested the war but did not participate in that investigation.

### VVAW factional controversy

Chitty coauthored a hostile open letter against VVAW–Anti-Imperialist and the Revolutionary Communist Party. The letter called the rival group dishonest, disruptive, and fraudulent in its use of the VVAW identity.

These were **political allegations made by Chitty and fellow organizers**, not findings against him. The letter says an earlier trademark/name dispute ended in an out-of-court settlement, but the underlying court papers were not located.

### Claims about Vietnam veterans being spat upon

Chitty argued that the only contemporary photograph known to him of someone spitting on a Vietnam veteran showed a person wearing a Veterans of Foreign Wars cap at VVAW’s 1970 Valley Forge march.

This is a **qualified historical claim stated by Chitty**, not established here as an exhaustive survey of all surviving photographic evidence. He used it to challenge a generalized cultural narrative, not to claim that no antiwar civilian ever mistreated a veteran.

### Political accusations in his writings

Chitty’s articles used strong language about presidents, military policy, war crimes, imperialism, military recruitment, and official deception. These writings sometimes restated allegations concerning:

- The USS *Iowa* explosion and Navy safety practices.
- Tiger Force atrocities and alleged suppression of accountability.
- POW/MIA political mythology.
- Abuse and rape in the military.
- The School of the Americas.
- U.S. policy toward Haiti and Central America.
- George W. Bush’s National Guard service.
- Government manipulation of the Vietnam narrative.

Those are political-historical arguments by Chitty. They are not evidence of personal wrongdoing on his part, and assessing every underlying allegation is beyond the biographical scope of this dossier.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Arthur Ben Chitty” as a single historical person

**Story:** Online results can create the impression that the labor librarian and Vietnam veteran also founded Episcopal historical organizations, wrote histories of Sewanee, edited Ely Green, and litigated to exhume John Wilkes Booth.

**Origin and circulation:** The confusion arises from identical family names, both men’s use of “Ben,” their Sewanee connections, and databases that omit generational suffixes.

**Evidence:** The elder man was born in 1914 and conducted historical work before the younger man’s birth. Court documents describe the Booth researcher as a longtime historiographer. The younger man’s documented career was in librarianship, labor, and veterans’ activism.

**Effect:** The conflation can assign the father’s controversial Booth-survival theory and editorial history to the son. It is false.

### The “drafted sailor” formulation

**Story:** Chitty was a drafted sailor.

**Evidence:** VVAW’s obituary says he was drafted and enlisted in the Navy; Chitty himself said he expected to be drafted and enlisted instead. The likely reality is that draft pressure or notification caused him to choose Navy enlistment.

**Status:** Simplified but not wholly false. “Draft-motivated Navy enlistment” is more exact.

### The transformed veteran

**Story:** Chitty moved from believing in the Vietnam mission to lifelong antiwar militancy.

**Evidence:** This is supported by his own repeated accounts, his widow’s testimony, continuous organizational records, and his published writings. It is not merely posthumous hagiography, although memorial sources naturally emphasize coherence and purpose.

### The bedroom-and-whiskey crisis

**Story:** After reconnecting with VVAW organizers in 1985, Chitty shut himself in a bedroom with whiskey for eighteen hours and emerged ready to rejoin counter-recruitment work.

**Origin:** Priscilla Murolo’s memorial address.

**Evidence:** Family testimony; no independent corroboration.

**Effect:** The episode became a compact origin story for Chitty’s mature activism: suppressed war memory converted into collective political work.

### “Pain made useful”

**Story:** Chitty transformed physical and emotional pain from Vietnam into service to younger people and peace organizing.

**Origin:** Murolo’s memorial tribute.

**Status:** Interpretive family testimony, supported by his counter-recruitment, writing, and veterans’ work but not an objectively testable proposition.

### The antiwar movement and the spat-upon veteran

**Story contested by Chitty:** Popular memory holds that hostile antiwar demonstrators commonly spat on returning Vietnam veterans.

**Chitty’s counter-story:** A documented photograph of spitting at the 1970 Valley Forge VVAW march instead showed an older veteran in a VFW cap spitting on an antiwar Vietnam veteran.

**Status:** Chitty’s historically significant intervention in veterans’ memory. It should not be expanded into an absolute assertion that no other spitting incident occurred.

### Memorial reputation

VVAW tributes described him as exceptionally sincere, knowledgeable, precise with language, committed to veterans’ rights, and devoted to “peace with justice.” These are **memorial judgments**, useful evidence of organizational reputation but not neutral measurements.

## Later Life and Death

Chitty continued working at Queens College into 2023 and remained publicly identified with VVAW, Veterans for Peace, union health-and-safety work, and labor history.

The 2018 revision of *From the Folks Who Brought You the Weekend* extended the book’s narrative into globalization, immigrant organizing, worker centers, the 2016 election, and the Trump period.

He retired in summer 2023 after nearly four decades at Queens College. Pancreatic cancer was diagnosed shortly before retirement.

In January 2024, as his condition worsened, Chitty reportedly sent VVAW a suggested obituary format: solicit one-, two-, or three-sentence tributes from comrades and combine them. The resulting memorial pages reflect that request.

He died at Calvary Hospital in the Bronx on February 11 according to VVAW and Queens College, or February 12 according to the *Sewanee Mountain Messenger*.

Survivors included Priscilla Murolo, Max and Tony Schultz, two grandchildren, Em Turner Chitty, Nathan Harsh Brown Chitty, and their families.

His ashes were designated for interment in Sewanee’s University Cemetery beside his parents and brother John.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Library and campus legacy

Queens College remembered Chitty as a long-serving systems officer and administrator whose work extended beyond his job description into campus-wide health, safety, and contract enforcement.

The Academic Senate’s March 2024 memorial emphasized service to the entire campus, not only the library.

### Labor legacy

The Queens College Unions Joint Committee on Quality of Work Life was described as his proudest institutional achievement. Its significance lay in bringing faculty, professional staff, civil-service employees, nurses, and building-trades workers into the same continuing forum.

### Historiographical legacy

*From the Folks Who Brought You the Weekend* remained his most visible public work. Reviews praised its broad coverage, accessibility, attention to race and gender, and combination of historical synthesis with Joe Sacco’s illustrations.

The book is often catalogued primarily under Murolo’s name, and publisher descriptions may give Chitty less biographical space. Nevertheless, title pages and bibliographic records consistently identify genuine coauthorship.

### Veterans’ movement legacy

VVAW memorials present Chitty as:

- A carrier of organizational memory.
- A counter-recruiter.
- A writer capable of connecting veteran experience to wider political history.
- A bridge between antiwar and labor movements.
- An advocate of “peace with justice” rather than patriotic reconciliation without accountability.

These characterizations originate in an organization to which he devoted much of his life and should be read as informed but sympathetic memorial testimony.

### Limits of public recognition

Chitty was not a nationally famous officeholder, military commander, celebrity, or movement founder. Much of his work was infrastructural: systems maintenance, meeting organization, workplace monitoring, handouts, memorial writing, committee-building, counter-recruitment, and preservation of movement continuity.

That limited celebrity helps explain why his life is documented more thoroughly in organizational archives than in national newspapers or commercial biography.

### Martin Luther King Jr.

**Evidentiary label: documented/reported fact and explicitly acknowledged influence.**

### Avram Noam Chomsky

**Evidentiary label: documented publication connection; personal relationship unestablished.**

This documents Chomsky’s endorsement of Chitty and Murolo’s work. It does not establish that Chomsky and Chitty met, corresponded directly, or collaborated.

### George Walker Bush

**Evidentiary label: documented political opposition; no personal meeting established.**

Chitty protested Bush’s appearance at the United Nations in September 2006 and criticized the Bush administration’s Iraq policy, constitutional claims, military recruiting, veterans’ policy, and use of intelligence.

This was an activist-to-officeholder political relationship, not a documented personal encounter.

### Donald Trump

**Evidentiary label: documented subject of analysis; no personal encounter established.**

The 2018 revised edition of Chitty and Murolo’s labor history added material on the 2016 election and unions’ relationships with Trump. In 2019 Chitty also discussed organized labor “in the age of Trump and *Janus*.”

No meeting, correspondence, employment link, or other direct relationship was found.

### Harriet Tubman

**Evidentiary label: documented symbolic advocacy; not a personal-life crossing.**

In the 2015 “Peace at Home, Peace Abroad” report, Chitty and collaborators proposed replacing Andrew Jackson on the twenty-dollar bill with Union veteran Harriet Tubman.

This is a symbolic political association, not a biographical meeting or historical contemporaneity.

Chitty’s 2015 report discussed replacing Jackson with Tubman. Searches for “Arthur Ben Chitty” and Lincoln also produce the elder Arthur Ben Chitty’s John Wilkes Booth research. No legitimate personal connection between Chitty III and Lincoln or the Booth investigation exists.
