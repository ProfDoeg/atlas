## Works by Ibn Battuta

Ibn Battuta left one work. He did not write it himself in the ordinary sense: he dictated his recollections late in life to a professional editor, who shaped them into a finished book. There is no second composition, no collection of letters, and no independent diary to set alongside it.

### The dictation

After nearly a quarter-century of travel across the Islamic world, East Africa, Central Asia, India, and (by his own disputed account) China and the Mali empire, Ibn Battuta returned to Morocco in the early 1350s. The Marinid sultan Abu Inan Faris commissioned a record of the journeys and assigned a young Andalusi man of letters, Ibn Juzayy al-Kalbi, to produce it. Ibn Juzayy had met Ibn Battuta a few years earlier in Granada. Working from Ibn Battuta's spoken testimony, he organized the material, supplied ornamented prose and verse, and incorporated descriptive passages lifted or adapted from earlier Arabic travel writers, especially Ibn Jubayr, to flesh out regions Ibn Battuta covered only briefly. A colophon dates the completed text to December 1355. The result carries the long title usually rendered A Gift to Those Who Contemplate the Wonders of Cities and the Marvels of Travelling, and is known simply as the Rihla, "the journey."

Because of this process, the book is best understood as a joint production: Ibn Battuta's memory and Ibn Juzayy's literary craftsmanship. Modern scholars disagree over how much of the geography and incident is firsthand observation, how much is borrowed convention, and how much is outright invention, particularly for the most contested stretches of the itinerary in Anatolia, the northern steppe, and inland China.

### Survival and the Paris manuscripts

No version close to the original dictation survives. What exists instead is a single coherent manuscript tradition, now held at the Bibliothèque nationale de France, acquired through nineteenth-century French activity in Algeria. Among these volumes is a copy regarded by specialists as Ibn Juzayy's own autograph or very close to it. Outside this Paris group there is no independent medieval witness of comparable completeness: unlike many classical Arabic texts that circulated in multiple competing recensions, the Rihla survived chiefly through this one line of transmission, later supplemented by an abridged epitome that reached Europe earlier and through a separate line of manuscripts in Morocco.

### Print history

European knowledge of the text began indirectly, through an abridged Arabic epitome. Samuel Lee translated this shortened version into English in 1829, long before anyone had worked from the full Paris manuscripts. The foundational edition came only in the mid-nineteenth century: Charles Defremery and Beniamino Raffaello Sanguinetti produced a four-volume Arabic text with facing French translation, Voyages d'Ibn Batoutah, published in Paris between 1853 and 1859, based directly on the Paris manuscripts. This remains the base text nearly every later translation relies on.

The standard English version took more than a century to complete. H. A. R. Gibb began translating the full Defremery-Sanguinetti text for the Hakluyt Society; the first volume appeared in 1958, and Gibb continued the project until his death left it unfinished. C. F. Beckingham completed the remaining material, with the full set of volumes, including an index volume, appearing in stages through the 1990s and reaching completion around 2000. A separate modern Arabic edition, assembled by the Moroccan scholar Abd al-Hadi al-Tazi in five volumes, was published in Rabat in 1997. Tim Mackintosh-Smith produced a widely read abridged English edition drawn from the Gibb-Beckingham translation in 2002.
