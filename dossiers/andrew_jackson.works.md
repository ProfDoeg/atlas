## Works by Andrew Jackson

### Surviving papers: an incomplete obra

- **1780s–1845:** *Andrew Jackson’s surviving papers*. This is the nearest defensible “complete works”: a dispersed, partly lost body of correspondence, legal and financial papers, military records, speeches, presidential documents, and memoranda rather than a single authored book. The ongoing scholarly edition, *The Papers of Andrew Jackson*, has published twelve of seventeen projected volumes, through 1834; the Library of Congress holds about 26,000 Jackson-related items but explicitly records dispersal, destruction, later additions, and documents written by others, so neither collection can be equated with everything Jackson made. ([Papers of Andrew Jackson](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/); [Library of Congress finding aid](https://findingaids.loc.gov/exist_collections/ead3pdf/mss/2009/ms009180.pdf))

### Letters and memoranda

- **1780s–1845:** *Correspondence*. Thousands of public, military, political, commercial, plantation, and family letters survive in drafts, letter-book copies, recipient copies, transcripts, fragments, and printed versions. This is a body of work without a title fixed by Jackson; attribution must be assessed document by document because secretaries frequently drafted or copied texts that he signed or authorized. ([Papers of Andrew Jackson](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/); [Library of Congress finding aid](https://findingaids.loc.gov/exist_collections/ead3pdf/mss/2009/ms009180.pdf))

- **1798–1820:** *Letters to Rachel Donelson Jackson*. At least twenty substantial letters remained together long enough to be described as a group, though they are now part of the wider correspondence rather than a book conceived by Jackson. Their survival is accidental and incomplete. ([Library of Congress finding aid, p. 29](https://findingaids.loc.gov/exist_collections/ead3pdf/mss/2009/ms009180.pdf))

- **1820s–1845:** *Letters to Francis Preston Blair, Amos Kendall, Martin Van Buren, and other political allies*. These overlapping sequences functioned as political counsel, editorial instruction, patronage negotiation, and retrospective self-construction. They resist treatment as wholly private authorship: Jackson’s political circle copied, edited, circulated, and sometimes drafted material in his name, while substantial portions became separated from his papers. ([Library of Congress finding aid, pp. 11–19](https://findingaids.loc.gov/exist_collections/ead3pdf/mss/2009/ms009180.pdf))

- **1843–1845:** *Recollections supplied for an intended life of Andrew Jackson*. These conversations, corrections, notes, and manuscript annotations were contributions to Amos Kendall’s unfinished biographical project, not an autobiography written by Jackson. Jackson reviewed at least five installments, possibly all seven, but Kendall’s work remained incomplete and authorship belongs principally to Kendall. ([Library of Congress finding aid, pp. 11–12](https://findingaids.loc.gov/exist_collections/ead3pdf/mss/2009/ms009180.pdf))

### Legal, commercial, plantation, and household records

- **1787–1804:** *Legal papers*. Pleadings, agreements, notes, judicial records, and related documents survive from Jackson’s work as prosecutor, attorney, and Tennessee Superior Court judge. They are operative texts—closer to legal instruments and protocols than literary works—and clerical hands or formulaic language often complicate personal authorship. ([*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vols. 1–2](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/); [Library of Congress finding aid](https://findingaids.loc.gov/exist_collections/ead3pdf/mss/2009/ms009180.pdf))

- **1790s–1845:** *Accounts, contracts, land memoranda, and mercantile records*. This dispersed documentary obra records Jackson’s stores, land speculation, debts, horse breeding, crops, household, and plantation economy. Many items are jointly made records rather than compositions, and the archive preserves only a fraction of the transactions in which he participated. ([Library of Congress finding aid, pp. 18, 30–43](https://findingaids.loc.gov/exist_collections/ead3pdf/mss/2009/ms009180.pdf))

- **1804–1845:** *The Hermitage plantation as a project*. Jackson acquired the property in 1804 and directed its development as residence, farm, political headquarters, and slave plantation. It is not solely his “work”: its buildings and landscape were materially made by architects, craftsmen, laborers, and people Jackson enslaved, so it is better classified as a coercively organized, collaborative project than as an individually authored creation. ([Library of Congress finding aid, p. 4](https://findingaids.loc.gov/exist_collections/ead3pdf/mss/2009/ms009180.pdf); [The Hermitage history](https://thehermitage.com/learn/mansion-grounds/mansion/))

### Military protocols, orders, and proclamations

- **1812–1821:** *Military correspondence and orders*. These include commands, general orders, courts-martial papers, returns, supply instructions, negotiations, and reports from the southern expedition, Creek War, defense of New Orleans, First Seminole War, and Florida governorship. They are collective command instruments rather than a sequence of autonomous “works”; aides commonly drafted and copied them under Jackson’s authority. ([*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vols. 2–5](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/); [Library of Congress finding aid, pp. 35–37](https://findingaids.loc.gov/exist_collections/ead3pdf/mss/2009/ms009180.pdf))

- **1813–1814:** *Creek Campaign orderly book*. A working military protocol containing orders and administrative records, not a retrospective narrative of the campaign. Its several hands make it an institutional object maintained for Jackson’s command rather than a wholly autograph composition. ([Library of Congress finding aid, pp. 18, 35](https://findingaids.loc.gov/exist_collections/ead3pdf/mss/2009/ms009180.pdf))

- **1814:** *Treaty of Fort Jackson*. Jackson negotiated and signed this coercive treaty after the Creek War; it ceded an enormous territory, including land belonging to Creek people who had fought beside the United States. It is a jointly executed legal instrument and manifestation of federal power, not Jackson’s sole-authored text. ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/treaty-of-fort-jackson); [*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vol. 3](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/))

- **1814–1815:** *Addresses and proclamations at New Orleans*. These public texts recruited inhabitants, imposed military authority, praised troops, and addressed Louisiana’s multilingual population; some circulated in English and French. Edward Livingston and other aides helped compose or translate them, making “Jackson” an authorizing voice rather than necessarily the writer of every sentence. ([*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vol. 3](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/))

- **1818:** *Orders and reports of the Seminole campaign*. These command texts authorized movements, occupations, arrests, trials, and executions during Jackson’s invasion of Spanish Florida. They are consequential protocols produced through a headquarters staff, and their documentary authorship should not be collapsed into Jackson’s handwriting or personal composition. ([*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vol. 4](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/); [Library of Congress finding aid, pp. 35–37](https://findingaids.loc.gov/exist_collections/ead3pdf/mss/2009/ms009180.pdf))

- **1821:** *Florida gubernatorial papers*. Proclamations, ordinances, appointments, instructions, and correspondence made during Jackson’s brief governorship constitute an administrative corpus rather than a single work. Secretarial preparation and the authority of office make these collaborative state acts. ([*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vol. 5](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/); [Library of Congress finding aid, pp. 37–38](https://findingaids.loc.gov/exist_collections/ead3pdf/mss/2009/ms009180.pdf))

### Political manifestos and campaign texts

- **1824–1828:** *Campaign letters and statements*. Jackson’s published letters, private instructions, and authorized statements helped construct the political identity later called “Jacksonian Democracy.” They form a campaign project rather than a fixed manifesto, and newspapers, advisers, committees, and editors participated in their wording and dissemination. ([*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vol. 6](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/))

- **1825:** *Letter to Dr. L. H. Coleman*. Commonly known as Jackson’s “national nomination” or “judicious tariff” letter, it was a private letter deliberately made public and therefore operated as a campaign manifesto. Its identity lies partly in that change of function from correspondence to printed political declaration. ([*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vol. 6](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/))

- **1826:** *Letter to the Tennessee legislature declining reelection to the Senate*. A resignation and political declaration that presented rotation in office as a republican principle. It is both an administrative act and an early manifesto of the reform program attached to Jackson’s presidential candidacy. ([*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vol. 6](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/))

### Presidential state papers

- **1829-03-04:** *First Inaugural Address*. A ceremonial manifesto announcing the intended principles of Jackson’s administration. As with his other presidential state papers, the signature and delivery establish official responsibility, but advisers’ drafting prevents a simple equation between responsibility and sole verbal authorship. ([Miller Center](https://millercenter.org/the-presidency/presidential-speeches/march-4-1829-first-inaugural-address); [*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vol. 7](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/))

- **1829-12-08:** *First Annual Message to Congress*. A programmatic state paper addressing administrative reform, Native removal, finance, internal improvements, and constitutional change. It is an administration document assembled with departmental and secretarial assistance, though issued under Jackson’s constitutional authority. ([American Presidency Project](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/first-annual-message-3); [*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vol. 7](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/))

- **1830-05-27:** *Maysville Road Veto Message*. A veto and constitutional manifesto rejecting federal support for a road lying wholly within Kentucky. The text acts by returning the bill unsigned while simultaneously defining Jackson’s view of federal internal-improvement power. ([U.S. Senate](https://www.senate.gov/artandhistory/history/resources/pdf/JacksonMaysville.pdf); [*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vol. 8](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/))

- **1830-12-06:** *Second Annual Message to Congress*. An administrative survey and policy manifesto, especially notorious for presenting forced Native removal as benevolent policy. Its status is institutional and collaborative even though Jackson assumed presidential responsibility for it. ([American Presidency Project](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/second-annual-message-2); [*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vol. 8](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/))

- **1831-12-06:** *Third Annual Message to Congress*. A state paper treating finance, foreign relations, Native removal, and limits on federal public works. It belongs to the collaboratively produced presidential record rather than to an individually composed literary oeuvre. ([American Presidency Project](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/third-annual-message-2); [*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vol. 9](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/))

- **1832-07-10:** *Bank Veto Message*. A veto that also functions as a political manifesto against renewal of the Second Bank of the United States. Amos Kendall, Roger B. Taney, and other advisers contributed to its preparation; Jackson made it his act and argument, but sole verbal authorship is not tenable. ([U.S. Senate](https://www.senate.gov/artandhistory/history/common/generic/BankVeto.htm); [*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vol. 10](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/))

- **1832-12-04:** *Fourth Annual Message to Congress*. A state paper reporting on the Bank veto, tariff, federal finances, foreign relations, and Native removal. It is one installment in an annual constitutional protocol, not an independently conceived book. ([American Presidency Project](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/fourth-annual-message-2); [*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vol. 10](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/))

- **1832-12-10:** *Proclamation to the People of South Carolina*. Usually called the Nullification Proclamation, this is simultaneously proclamation, constitutional argument, warning, and manifesto against nullification and secession. Secretary of State Edward Livingston prepared the principal draft under Jackson’s direction, so the document is Jackson’s official act but a collaborative composition. ([Library of Congress guide](https://guides.loc.gov/nullification-proclamation); [Miller Center](https://millercenter.org/the-presidency/presidential-speeches/december-10-1832-nullification-proclamation); [*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vol. 10](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/))

- **1833-03-04:** *Second Inaugural Address*. A short ceremonial manifesto centered on preservation of the Union and the obligations of majority government. It is an official performance as much as a manuscript text. ([Miller Center](https://millercenter.org/the-presidency/presidential-speeches/march-4-1833-second-inaugural-address); [*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vol. 11](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/))

- **1833-09-18:** *Paper Read to the Cabinet on the Removal of the Deposits*. A directive and justificatory manifesto ordering the administration to remove federal deposits from the Bank of the United States. Roger B. Taney helped formulate the administration’s bank arguments; the document records Jackson’s decision while resisting the category of solitary authorship. ([*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vol. 11](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/); [Library of Congress finding aid](https://findingaids.loc.gov/exist_collections/ead3pdf/mss/2009/ms009180.pdf))

- **1833-12-03:** *Fifth Annual Message to Congress*. A state paper defending the removal of federal deposits and reviewing foreign, fiscal, military, postal, and Native affairs. It belongs to the machinery of executive government and was compiled from many officers’ reports. ([American Presidency Project](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/fifth-annual-message-2); [*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vol. 11](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/))

- **1834-04-15:** *Protest to the Senate*. A constitutional argument contesting the Senate’s censure of Jackson for removing the deposits. It is a manifesto of executive authority and a formal intervention in a dispute between institutions, not merely a descriptive message. ([U.S. Senate](https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/censure/President_AJ.htm); [*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vol. 12](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/))

- **1834-12-01:** *Sixth Annual Message to Congress*. A state paper reporting on foreign relations, revenue, the national debt, the Bank, the Post Office, Native removal, and defense. Like the other annual messages, it is presidentially authorized but administratively assembled. ([American Presidency Project](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/sixth-annual-message-2); [*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vol. 12](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/))

- **1835-12-07:** *Seventh Annual Message to Congress*. An annual state paper issued after extinguishment of the national debt and amid conflict with France over indemnity payments. The modern scholarly edition has not yet reached this year, so its textual history is less fully settled there than that of the documents through 1834. ([American Presidency Project](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/seventh-annual-message-2); [Library of Congress, Messages and Speeches series](https://www.loc.gov/item/maj025358/))

- **1836-12-05:** *Eighth Annual Message to Congress*. Jackson’s final annual message, surveying finance, foreign affairs, Native removal, slavery-related postal controversy, and the approaching transfer of power. It is a collective state paper bearing Jackson’s ultimate official responsibility. ([American Presidency Project](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/eighth-annual-message-2); [Library of Congress finding aid, pp. 38–39](https://findingaids.loc.gov/exist_collections/ead3pdf/mss/2009/ms009180.pdf))

- **1829–1837:** *Presidential special messages, veto messages, proclamations, executive orders, nominations, commissions, pardons, and instructions*. This large official corpus contains hundreds of individually dated acts, many without distinctive titles beyond their documentary form. Listing later printed headings as if they were Jackson’s titles would manufacture works; moreover, departmental clerks and advisers drafted much of the language, while Jackson’s signature or authorization made each instrument operative. ([Library of Congress finding aid, pp. 38–39](https://findingaids.loc.gov/exist_collections/ead3pdf/mss/2009/ms009180.pdf); [*A Compilation of the Messages and Papers of the Presidents*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/11202))

- **1837-03-04:** *Farewell Address*. A valedictory manifesto reviewing Jackson’s administration and warning against concentrated financial power, sectionalism, and disunion. Roger B. Taney drafted much of it, making it a paradigmatic case in which Jackson’s adopted political voice and responsibility do not amount to sole textual composition. ([American Presidency Project](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/farewell-address-0); [Library of Congress finding aid](https://findingaids.loc.gov/exist_collections/ead3pdf/mss/2009/ms009180.pdf))

### Post-presidential political interventions

- **1837–1845:** *Hermitage letters and public statements*. Jackson continued to make endorsements, denunciations, policy arguments, electoral instructions, and advice for Democratic leaders after leaving office. This late political corpus has no fixed title and remains incompletely published because the scholarly edition has not yet reached these years. ([Library of Congress finding aid, pp. 33–39](https://findingaids.loc.gov/exist_collections/ead3pdf/mss/2009/ms009180.pdf))

- **1844:** *Letters advocating the annexation of Texas*. Private letters were deliberately circulated or published to influence national policy and the presidential election, turning correspondence into political manifesto. Their public identity was made collaboratively by Jackson, recipients, editors, and Democratic organizers. ([Library of Congress finding aid, pp. 33–34](https://findingaids.loc.gov/exist_collections/ead3pdf/mss/2009/ms009180.pdf))

### Projects and formations that resist classification as authored works

- **1820s–1830s:** *Jacksonian political organization*. Jackson helped create the coalition that became the Democratic Party through candidacies, patronage, correspondence, newspapers, local committees, and presidential action. It is a collective political formation, not a text or an individually authored obra, and naming Jackson as its sole maker would erase the work of Martin Van Buren, editors, officeholders, organizers, and voters. ([*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vols. 6–12](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/); [U.S. Senate historical overview](https://www.senate.gov/artandhistory/history/common/generic/Origins_Development.htm))

- **1829–1837:** *Indian-removal regime*. Jackson’s messages, negotiations, appointments, military preparations, treaty enforcement, and refusal to protect Native sovereignty helped make a governmental project culminating in mass dispossession and forced migration. It resists being called a “work”: it was a coercive process carried out by Congress, states, negotiators, soldiers, contractors, and successor administrations, and Native nations contested both its legality and its imposed agreements. ([National Archives, Indian Removal Act](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/indian-removal-act); [*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vols. 7–12](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/))

- **1830s:** *Destruction of the Second Bank of the United States as a political project*. The veto, removal of deposits, cabinet paper, appointments, and public campaign together formed a sustained project rather than one text. Jackson directed it, but Congress, advisers, state banks, voters, and Bank president Nicholas Biddle jointly produced its course and consequences. ([U.S. Senate, “The Senate Censures President Andrew Jackson”](https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/censure/President_AJ.htm); [*Papers of Andrew Jackson*, vols. 10–12](https://thepapersofandrewjackson.utk.edu/volumes/))
