## Works by Benjamin of Tudela

Benjamin left essentially one work: the account of his own journey. There is no second book to round out a bibliography, and padding this note with minor items would misrepresent him. What makes the entry worth more than a single line is that the text itself resists a clean "written in year X, published in year Y" treatment: what survives is not an original manuscript but a chain of copies and print editions separated from Benjamin by centuries, and the modern critical edition did not exist until 1907.

### The journey itself

Benjamin, a merchant of Tudela in the Kingdom of Navarre, traveled roughly 1165-1173 through southern France, Italy, Greece, Constantinople, the Levant, Mesopotamia (he reached Baghdad), Persia, and Egypt, recording the Jewish communities, their populations, leaders, and conditions, alongside notes on trade, geography, and local wonders. The work that resulted is known in Hebrew as *Sefer ha-Masa'ot* (Book of Travels) and in English usually as *The Itinerary of Benjamin of Tudela*. It is the single most-cited Jewish travel account of the Middle Ages and a working source for historians of the 12th-century Mediterranean and Near East generally, Jewish and non-Jewish alike.

The text as we have it opens with a short preface written about Benjamin in the third person, vouching for him as "a trustworthy man" whose statements, "wherever we have tested" them, were found "accurate, true to fact and consistent," and stating that he kept notes as he traveled and "brought this book with him on his return to the country of Castile" in 1173. That framing device, an editorial hand speaking about Benjamin rather than Benjamin speaking in his own voice, sits in front of a travel narrative that then proceeds in the first person. Whether that preface is Benjamin's own device or the work of a later compiler is exactly the kind of question the manuscript evidence cannot fully settle (Project Gutenberg edition of M. N. Adler's translation, https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/14981).

### Transmission and editions

No autograph survives, and the gap between composition and the earliest evidence of the text is large. The manuscript Adler took as his base text, British Museum (now British Library) MS 27,089, has been assigned by paleographers to the 13th century, already a copy once or more removed from anything Benjamin himself might have written. Other substantial witnesses are later still: a manuscript in Rome's Biblioteca Casanatense (no. 216), dated by a scribal colophon to 1429-1430; a Vienna manuscript written at the end of the 15th or beginning of the 16th century; and two fragmentary manuscripts in Oxford's Bodleian Library (the Oppenheim collection). The work's first appearance in print came centuries after Benjamin's travels: an editio princeps at Constantinople in 1543, reprinted at Ferrara in 1556 from Abraham Usque's press, neither grounded in manuscript collation as modern editing would require.

The first major scholarly edition, by Abraham Asher (London, 1840-41, two volumes with English translation and extensive annotation), was built entirely from those two printed editions; Asher himself, by his own account, "deplored the fact that he had not a single MS. to resort to." It took until 1907 for a manuscript-based critical edition to appear: Marcus Nathan Adler's *The Itinerary of Benjamin of Tudela: Critical Text, Translation and Commentary* (Oxford University Press), which established the British Museum manuscript as its base text and collated it against the Rome and Vienna manuscripts and the Bodleian fragments. Adler's remains the standard scholarly edition and translation today. So the usual single "publication" line does not apply here: the work was composed around 1173, survives only in copies written one to three and a half centuries later, reached print in 1543, and did not receive a real critical edition until 1907, 734 years after it was written.
